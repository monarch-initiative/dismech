# Development of an exon 27-skipping antisense oligonucleotide as a targeted therapy for refractory skin ulcers in Werner syndrome.
**Authors:** Man Z, Asano S, Kakutani T, Kiyoshi A, Hino K, Ikeda A
**Journal:** Mol Ther Nucleic Acids (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.omtn.2026.102930](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omtn.2026.102930)

## Content

Werner syndrome (WS) is a rare autosomal recessive progeroid disorder caused by biallelic mutations in WRN and is frequently complicated by refractory skin ulcers for which no effective therapy exists. We developed WRN-108, a splice-switching antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) designed to induce exon 27 skipping and restore the open reading frame (ORF) disrupted by the most common WRN mutation in Japanese WS patients, c.3139-1G>C, which leads to exon 26 skipping. In WS patient-derived fibroblasts, WRN-108 efficiently induced exon 27 skipping, restored WRN protein expression, and re-established its nuclear localization. Treatment improved cell proliferation and reduced senescence-associated markers, G-quadruplex accumulation, and γH2AX signaling, consistent with partial restoration of WRN-dependent genome maintenance functions. Topical administration in a rat skin wound model resulted in effective dermal penetration and sustained tissue retention. In a cynomolgus monkey wound model, WRN-108 induced exon 27 skipping in the skin and achieved dermal exposure without local toxicity. Short-term toxicity studies in mice and miniature pigs further confirmed its favorable tolerability. These findings provide preclinical evidence that ASO-mediated exon skipping can restore WRN function, highlighting the translational potential of WRN-108 as a therapeutic approach for refractory skin ulcers in WS patients harboring the c.3139-1G>C mutation.

Werner syndrome (WS) is a rare autosomal recessive progeroid disorder characterized by features of premature aging, including skin atrophy, early graying and hair loss, bilateral cataracts, type 2 diabetes mellitus, atherosclerosis, osteoporosis, and a markedly increased risk of malignancies—particularly sarcomas and thyroid carcinomas.1,2,3 Among these manifestations, refractory skin ulcers—typically located on the lower extremities—severely impair quality of life by causing chronic pain, recurrent infections, delayed wound healing, and reduced mobility.4 Despite their clinical burden, no pharmacological therapies are currently approved for WS-associated ulcers. Management currently relies on conservative approaches such as topical agents, wound dressings, or surgical procedures (e.g., debridement or skin grafting).5 However, these interventions are often insufficient due to intrinsic defects in skin integrity, wound repair capacity, and vascular function in WS,4 underscoring the urgent need for disease-modifying therapies.

WS is caused by biallelic pathogenic mutations in the WRN gene (also known as RECQL2 or RECQ3), located on chromosome 8p12. The gene comprises 34 coding exons and encodes a 1,432-amino acid nuclear protein of the RecQ family of DNA helicases. WRN harbors an N-terminal exonuclease domain,6 a central RecQ-type helicase domain,7 and two conserved regions—the RecQ helicase conserved region (RQC) and the helicase RNase D C-terminal conserved region (HRDC)—positioned between the helicase domain and the C-terminal nuclear localization signal (NLS).8 Functionally, WRN plays a critical role in maintaining genome stability9 and participates in DNA replication, repair, recombination, transcription, and telomere maintenance.7

Pathogenic variants in WRN often include nonsense or frameshift mutations that introduce premature termination codons (PTCs). Previous studies have shown that many such variants are associated with reduced WRN mRNA levels,10 consistent with nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), thereby limiting WRN protein production. Even when translated, the resulting truncated proteins are likely unstable and subject to rapid degradation. As WRN contains a C-terminal NLS that is essential for its function, loss of this signal results in the absence of functional WRN in the nucleus.11,12,13,14 Consequently, depletion of nuclear WRN protein is considered a central pathogenic mechanism of WS, as it disrupts critical nuclear processes, including DNA replication and repair, ultimately leading to genomic instability and premature cellular senescence. These cellular defects are thought to underlie the multisystem clinical manifestations of WS.7

Approximately 60% of patients with WS are of Japanese origin, and in this population, the most prevalent WRN mutation is the splice-site variant, c.3139-1G>C, which accounts for approximately 70% of WS cases.15,16 This mutation disrupts the canonical splice acceptor site of intron 25, leading to exon 26 skipping. The resulting aberrant splicing causes a frameshift and introduces a PTC in exon 27, ultimately resulting in loss of WRN function.17 Another recurrent WRN mutation, c.1105C>T (p.Arg369∗), which introduces a PTC in exon 9, has also been reported in both Japanese and non-Japanese populations.16,18

Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO)-mediated splicing modulation has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for genetic disorders caused by aberrant splicing. FDA-approved ASO therapies such as eteplirsen (EXONDYS 51) for Duchenne muscular dystrophy and nusinersen (Spinraza) for spinal muscular atrophy exemplify the clinical utility of splicing-targeted approaches.19,20 Although splicing-affecting mutations in WRN have been well documented, no ASO-based therapies for WS have been developed to date. Given the well-defined splicing defect in a subset of patients and the loss-of-function mechanism underlying WS, even partial restoration of WRN expression may provide meaningful clinical benefit, particularly for complications such as refractory skin ulcers.

Because exon 26 skipping results in a frameshift that introduces a PTC in exon 27, we hypothesized that additional skipping of exon 27 could restore the reading frame by realigning the downstream coding sequence. In this study, we developed and characterized WRN-108, a splice-switching ASO designed to induce exon 27 skipping in WRN transcripts carrying the c.3139-1G>C mutation. This approach is expected to restore the reading frame and enable expression of a partially functional WRN protein. Exon-skipping approaches are particularly suitable for correcting frame-disrupting mutations when the targeted exon does not encode an essential functional domain.21,22,23 Notably, exon 27 does not contain any known catalytic or structurally critical region, supporting its suitability as a therapeutic target for exon skipping.8

We demonstrate that WRN-108 induces exon 27 skipping in WS patient-derived fibroblasts (hereafter, WS fibroblasts) homozygous for the c.3139-1G>C mutation, thereby restoring WRN expression with appropriate nuclear localization. This molecular correction was associated with restoration of genome maintenance capacity in WS fibroblasts, along with reduced cellular senescence and improved proliferative capacity—phenotypes known to contribute to impaired wound healing in WS.5 Furthermore, WRN-108 showed favorable dermal penetration and retention in a rat skin wound model mimicking ulcerative lesions and exhibited exon 27-skipping activity in the cynomolgus monkey (Macaca fascicularis). Preliminary safety studies in mice and miniature pigs demonstrated that WRN-108 was well tolerated across species.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that ASO-mediated splicing modulation can restore WRN expression and improve key WS-associated cellular phenotypes. WRN-108 therefore represents a promising therapeutic approach for refractory skin ulcers, a severely debilitating and currently unmet medical need in WS.

To address the disrupted open reading frame (ORF) caused by exon 26 skipping (c.3139-1G>C), the most prevalent pathogenic variant in WS, we developed a splice-modulating strategy targeting exon 27. Skipping of exon 26 alone induces a frameshift and introduces a PTC within exon 27, resulting in loss of functional WRN protein (Figures 1A and S1). In contrast, concurrent skipping of exon 27 enables in-frame splicing from exon 25 to exon 28, restoring the ORF and allowing production of a WRN protein that retains the NLS. Because exon 27 is relatively short (76 nucleotides), limiting the number of potential ASO binding sites, we adopted an empirical screening approach rather than relying solely on in silico predictions. A library of 20-mer ASOs tiling exon 27 with offsets of 1, 4, 5, or 10 nucleotides was designed (Figure 1B; Table S1). These ASOs were transfected into WS fibroblasts, and exon 27 skipping was assessed 48 h post-transfection. Among the candidates, WRN-108 showed the most potent and consistent activity, with a low half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) of 2.6 nM (Figures 1C and 1D; Table S1). WRN-108 induced exon 27 skipping in a robust, dose-dependent manner, reaching 50.7%, 79.8%, and 100% at 1, 3, and 10 nM, respectively (Table S1). These results establish WRN-108 as the lead candidate for further preclinical development.

Because exon 26-skipped (Δ26) transcripts contain a PTC, we assessed their susceptibility to NMD. In WS fibroblasts, NMD inhibition modestly increased Δ26 transcript levels under vehicle-treated conditions (Figure S3). However, under WRN-108 treatment, Δ26 transcript levels were not further increased by NMD inhibition. These findings suggest that Δ26 transcripts are partially degraded via NMD and that WRN-108 suppresses their degradation by modulating splicing.

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of WRN-108 under physiologically relevant, transfection-free conditions, it was directly added to the culture medium of WS fibroblasts at final concentrations of 1 or 3 μM. Notably, skipping efficiencies approached 100% at both concentrations. We next assessed whether this molecular correction led to the restoration of WRN protein expression. Western blot analysis showed dose-dependent restoration of WRN protein in WRN-108-treated WS fibroblasts, whereas WRN was undetectable in vehicle-treated cells. WRN protein levels were partially restored toward those observed in healthy fibroblasts (Figures 2C and 2D). Immunocytochemistry further demonstrated nuclear localization of WRN in WRN-108-treated cells, approaching levels observed in healthy fibroblasts (Figure 2E). Quantification revealed a significant increase in the proportion of WRN-positive nuclei following WRN-108 treatment, reaching approximately 50% (Figure 2F). Notably, this restoration occurred in the absence of transfection reagents, consistent with efficient cellular uptake of WRN-108.

Together, these data demonstrate that WRN-108 efficiently induces exon 27 skipping and restores the reading frame disrupted by the pathogenic mutation, resulting in robust re-expression of WRN protein with nuclear localization under transfection-free conditions.

We next investigated whether the exon 27-skipped WRN protein retains functional capacity in maintaining genome integrity. To assess restoration of helicase-related activity, we examined nuclear accumulation of G-quadruplex (G4) DNA structures,24 known substrates of RecQ helicases. WS fibroblasts displayed elevated G4 signal intensity compared with healthy fibroblasts (Figures 3A and 3B). WRN-108 treatment significantly reduced G4 accumulation, suggesting improved resolution of G4 structures. We next evaluated DNA damage using γH2AX as a marker of double-strand break signaling.25 WS fibroblasts exhibited elevated basal γH2AX nuclear intensity, which further increased following treatment with the topoisomerase I inhibitor topotecan (TPT) (Figures 3C and 3D). WRN-108 treatment significantly reduced γH2AX levels under both basal conditions and after TPT exposure. Given the established link between WRN deficiency and premature senescence in WS fibroblasts,26,27,28 we next evaluated the expression of senescence-associated markers. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis demonstrated dose-dependent downregulation of senescence-associated secretory phenotype factors (CD44, IL-6, and IL-8) and the cell cycle inhibitor CDKN1A (encoding p21∧Cip1/Waf1) (Figure 4D).

Together, these results indicate that WRN-108 promotes a more proliferative and less senescent cellular state following correction of the exon 26-skipping-induced frameshift.

Topical delivery of ASOs to intact skin is limited by the stratum corneum, particularly for hydrophilic and high-molecular-weight compounds.29,30 In contrast, WS-associated refractory ulcers are characterized by loss of epidermal integrity, potentially permitting direct access to the dermal compartment. To evaluate the feasibility of dermal delivery and retention under such conditions, we employed a full-thickness excisional wound model in rats (Figure 5A). WRN-108 (1 mg per wound) was topically applied at 2 days post-injury, when the dermis remains exposed during early regeneration. Dermal concentrations rapidly reached 85.2 μM at 6 h and declined modestly to 57.9 μM at 24 h. Substantial levels were maintained at 72 h (53.9 μM), and concentrations remained elevated at 120 h (106.1 μM) and 168 h (88.9 μM) without repeated dosing (Figure 5B). These data indicate sustained dermal exposure of WRN-108 for more than 7 days following a single administration. Systemic exposure was minimal, with plasma levels peaking at 4 h and declining below the limit of detection by 6 h (Figure 5C), indicating rapid clearance and limited systemic distribution.33,34 Consistently, WRN-108 levels in major organs were low—1.1–2.5 μM in the kidney and 0.8–1.3 μM in the liver—both markedly lower than dermal concentrations (Figure 5B). These measurements reflect tissue-level ASO concentrations. Together, these findings indicate a tissue-restricted pharmacokinetic profile of WRN-108, with minimal systemic exposure.35,36

Collectively, these results demonstrate that WRN-108 achieves high dermal exposure, is retained at the application site, and exhibits pharmacokinetic properties favorable for local therapy. Importantly, the loss of epidermal barrier function characteristic of WS-associated ulcers may support the feasibility of dermal delivery in patients.

Given the potential safety concerns associated with ASOs,36 we evaluated the safety profile of WRN-108. Cytotoxicity was assessed in WS fibroblasts using an MTT assay at 72 h post-treatment. WRN-108 did not affect cell viability at the tested concentrations, which remained comparable to vehicle controls (Table S2).

To assess specificity, potential off-target effects were evaluated using in silico prediction and in vitro validation. An in silico homology search identified 99 genes with ≤3 mismatches to the WRN-108 sequence. Among these, three genes (HHLA2, GRHL2, and DPP10) with ≤2 mismatches were selected for further evaluation. WRN-108 was administered to HDLM-2 and RT-4 cells expressing these genes, and gene expression levels were assessed. As expected, WRN transcript levels were markedly reduced, consistent with exon 27 skipping-induced disruption of the reading frame in the wild-type context. In contrast, expression levels of HHLA2, GRHL2, and DPP10 were unchanged compared with vehicle-treated controls (Table S3), indicating no detectable off-target modulation under the conditions tested.

Preliminary in vivo safety studies were conducted by subcutaneous administration of WRN-108 every 3–4 days for 2 weeks in male mice (12, 60, or 120 mg/kg/week) and in male and female miniature pigs (4, 12, or 36 mg/kg/week). WRN-108 was well tolerated in both species up to the highest doses tested. Minor findings were observed at higher dose levels, including injection-site reactions and slight increases in selected organ weights or clinical chemistry parameters (Table S4). These changes were mild and consistent with the established safety profiles of approved ASO therapeutics.35,36,37

Toxicokinetic (TK) analysis demonstrated dose-proportional plasma exposure without systemic accumulation (Tables S5 and S6). Plasma levels returned to near baseline within 24 h, whereas skin concentrations remained elevated. The absence of systemic accumulation together with sustained skin retention supports further preclinical development of WRN-108 as a topically deliverable therapeutic agent.

WRN-108 shows seven mismatches with the rat Wrn gene but only a single mismatch with the cynomolgus monkey WRN ortholog (Table S7), suggesting potential applicability in non-human primates. In vitro studies confirmed exon 27-skipping activity in cynomolgus monkey dermal fibroblast (NCMDF) cells(Figure 6A). To evaluate in vivo activity, we conducted a pilot study using a single cynomolgus monkey (Figure 6B). Two full-thickness skin wounds were created on the dorsum, and WRN-108 (2 mg per site) was applied topically by dropwise administration directly to the wound surface. Skin biopsies collected 24 h post-application revealed exon 27-skipped transcripts, with skipping efficiencies of 26.4% and 29.1% at the two sites (Figure 6C). Subsequent DNA sequencing confirmed exon 26–28 junctions (Figure 6D), indicating successful exon 27 exclusion in vivo. Dermal WRN-108 concentrations at the treated sites were 272 and 576 μM, respectively, indicating substantial local retention following topical delivery. Although direct visualization of intracellular uptake in defined dermal cell populations was not performed, the detection of exon-skipped transcripts demonstrates pharmacodynamic activity and supports functional target engagement in vivo. No signs of erythema, ulceration, or edema were observed, suggesting favorable local tolerability. This study provides preclinical evidence supporting a splice-switching ASO, WRN-108, as a therapeutic strategy for WS caused by splicing-disruptive mutations. WRN-108 was rationally designed to restore the disrupted ORF resulting from the prevalent WRN c.3139-1G>C mutation via exon 27 skipping, thereby enabling expression of a partially functional WRN protein. Our data demonstrate that WRN-108 efficiently induces exon 27 skipping and restores WRN protein expression with appropriate nuclear localization. This molecular correction was associated with improved WRN-dependent genome maintenance functions and amelioration of cellular phenotypes relevant to WS pathology.

From a therapeutic design perspective, exon 27 represents a rational target for skipping as it does not encode catalytically essential domains. This strategy is consistent with exon-skipping approaches used in other genetic diseases, such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy, where restoring a partial yet functional transcript yields clinically meaningful benefit.38 WRN-108 exerted its activity under transfection-free conditions, supporting its feasibility for local therapeutic administration.

Although WRN-108 was originally developed to address the c.3139-1G>C mutation, its exon 27-skipping mechanism may also be applicable to other splicing-disruptive variants that result in exon 26 or exon 28 skipping, such as c.3233 + 1G>T,9 and IVS28 + 2T>C,39 respectively. In these contexts, additional exon 27 skipping may restore the downstream ORF and allow generation of in-frame, functional transcripts. While this possibility remains to be experimentally validated, it suggests that exon 27-targeted splice modulation may have broader relevance for selected WRN splice-site mutations.

WRN-108 induced exon 27 skipping in WRN wild-type cells, including cynomolgus monkey-derived fibroblasts (Figure 6A) and healthy human fibroblasts (data not shown). As exon 27 skipping is predicted to generate an out-of-frame transcript in the wild-type context, this could potentially result in reduced functional WRN expression. However, the intended clinical application of WRN-108 is restricted to patients with biallelic WRN mutations, and administration to healthy individuals is not anticipated, consistent with other ASO therapies.40,41

To explore its translational potential, we evaluated WRN-108 in a relevant non-human primate model. Topical administration of WRN-108 resulted in detectable exon 27-skipped transcripts in vivo, providing evidence of target engagement in primate skin. Although this evaluation was limited to a single animal, the detection of exon-skipped transcripts supports pharmacodynamic activity within treated tissue. While dermal exposure and exon-skipping activity were confirmed, direct visualization of intracellular uptake in defined dermal cell populations was not performed. Nevertheless, detection of exon-skipped transcripts supports intracellular target engagement in vivo, although direct confirmation of cellular internalization will require further studies employing labeled ASOs or cell-specific localization analyses. Pharmacokinetic analysis in a rat full-thickness skin wound model demonstrated sustained dermal retention with minimal systemic exposure, indicating that topical administration enables localized tissue exposure while limiting systemic distribution. These findings suggest that WRN-108 may reduce the risk of off-target effects and systemic toxicity through tissue-restricted exposure. Consistent with these observations, short-term toxicity studies in miniature pigs further support the feasibility of achieving substantial dermal exposure within tolerated dose ranges. While cross-species comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, the combined pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and tolerability data support the translational feasibility of localized topical administration.42,43

Beyond its therapeutic application, WRN-108 exemplifies a precision splice-switching strategy targeting a defined pathogenic mutation. This study highlights the feasibility of RNA-based therapeutics for genetically stratified rare disorders and may inform futrue approaches for other diseases associated with genome instability.

Despite these promising findings, several challenges remain. Notably, no animal model fully recapitulates both the human exon 27 sequence and the WS clinical phenotype, including chronic ulceration. Existing Wrn-deficient mouse models incompletely reflect WS manifestations, likely due to species-specific differences such as telomere length.44 Humanized WRN-expressing models, particularly in telomerase-deficient backgrounds,45 may provide improved platforms for future efficacy studies.

Long-term dosing studies will also be necessary, as sustained exon skipping may require repeated administration. For cutaneous indications, prolonged treatment is expected due to extended re-epithelialization and remodeling phases. Evaluating wound healing endpoints—such as closure rate, inflammation resolution, and tissue remodeling—will be essential. Pharmacodynamic biomarkers, such as WRN protein restoration or modulation of senescence-associated secretory phenotype factors, may further assist in optimizing dose selection.

ASO delivery remains a major limitation in the field, particularly for systemic applications, where limited tissue penetration and rapid clearance often constrain therapeutic efficacy.46 In this context, the topical administration strategy evaluated here provides a practical approach for achieving localized exposure while minimizing systemic distribution. The favorable dermal retention and limited plasma exposure observed in preclinical models suggest that cutaneous delivery may be well suited for WS-associated skin lesions. These findings highlight the importance of aligning therapeutic strategies with anatomically accessible disease sites. Disorders affecting the skin or mucosal surfaces may represent particularly suitable targets for locally administered ASOs. Further optimization of topical formulations—such as incorporation into thermoresponsive gels, lipid nanoparticles, or microneedles,47,48,49—may potentially improve tissue penetration and pharmacodynamic efficacy. Evaluation of WRN-108 in combination with such delivery technologies represents a rational direction for future investigation.

WS-associated chronic ulcers are multifactorial and likely involve vascular and angiogenic dysfunction in addition to impaired fibroblast proliferation. In this study, we focused on correcting WRN deficiency in dermal fibroblasts, as their proliferative impairment is a key driver of defective wound repair. The effects of WRN-108 on endothelial function and angiogenic responses warrant further investigation.

In conclusion, WRN-108 represents a mechanism-driven candidate therapeutic for a genetically defined subset of WS patients. By correcting a splicing defect at the RNA level, WRN-108 restores WRN protein expression and improves cellular function in vitro, while demonstrating favorable delivery properties in vivo. These findings provide a strong foundation for further preclinical development. Given the absence of effective treatments for WS-associated skin ulcers, WRN-108 may address a critical unmet need and improve patient quality of life.

Chemically modified ASOs targeting exon 27 of human WRN pre-mRNA were designed to induce exon skipping. Each ASO was 20 nucleotides in length and uniformly modified at the 2′-position with 2′-O-methoxyethyl (2′-MOE) groups. All cytosine residues were 5-methylated, and phosphorothioate (PS) linkages were incorporated throughout the backbone to enhance nuclease resistance and stability. A non-targeting control ASO (Ctrl ASO; 5′-TGTTGCGTCGTTTCTGGAGA-3′) was used as a negative control. The Ctrl ASO was designed to match WRN-108 in length, backbone chemistry, and chemical modification pattern. Sequence specificity was evaluated by BLAST analysis against the human transcriptome, confirming the absence of significant complementarity. The Ctrl ASO was administered under the same conditions as WRN-108. All ASOs were synthesized by GeneDesign, Inc. (Osaka, Japan).
