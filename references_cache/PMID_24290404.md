# Individualised pelvic floor muscle training in women with pelvic organ prolapse (POPPY): a multicentre randomised controlled trial.
**Authors:** Hagen S, Stark D, Glazener C, Dickson S, Barry S, Elders A, Frawley H, Galea MP, Logan J, McDonald A, McPherson G, Moore KH, Norrie J, Walker A, Wilson D, POPPY Trial Collaborators
**Journal:** Lancet (2014)
**DOI:** [10.1016/S0140-6736(13)61977-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(13)61977-7)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Pelvic organ prolapse is common and is strongly associated with childbirth and increasing age. Women with prolapse are often advised to do pelvic floor muscle exercises, but evidence supporting the benefits of such exercises is scarce. We aimed to establish the effectiveness of one-to-one individualised pelvic floor muscle training for reducing prolapse symptoms.

METHODS: We did a parallel-group, multicentre, randomised controlled trial at 23 centres in the UK, one in New Zealand, and one in Australia, between June 22, 2007, and April 9, 2010. Female outpatients with newly-diagnosed, symptomatic stage I, II, or III prolapse were randomly assigned (1:1), by remote computer allocation with minimsation, to receive an individualised programme of pelvic floor muscle training or a prolapse lifestyle advice leaflet and no muscle training (control group). Outcome assessors, and investigators who were gynaecologists at trial sites, were masked to group allocation; the statistician was masked until after data analysis. Our primary endpoint was participants' self-report of prolapse symptoms at 12 months. Analysis was by intention-to-treat analysis. This trial is registered, number ISRCTN35911035.

FINDINGS: 447 eligible patients were randomised to the intervention group (n=225) or the control group (n=222). 377 (84%) participants completed follow-up for questionnaires at 6 months and 295 (66%) for questionnaires at 12 months. Women in the intervention group reported fewer prolapse symptoms (ie, a significantly greater reduction in the pelvic organ prolapse symptom score [POP-SS]) at 12 months than those in the control group (mean reduction in POP-SS from baseline 3.77 [SD 5.62] vs 2.09 [5.39]; adjusted difference 1.52, 95% CI 0.46-2.59; p=0.0053). Findings were robust to missing data. Eight adverse events (six vaginal symptoms, one case of back pain, and one case of abdominal pain) and one unexpected serious adverse event, all in women from the intervention group, were regarded as unrelated to the intervention or to participation in the study.

INTERPRETATION: One-to-one pelvic floor muscle training for prolapse is effective for improvement of prolapse symptoms. Long-term benefits should be investigated, as should the effects in specific subgroups.

Introduction 3 
Pelvic organ prolapse is a common female condition,  with 40% of women over t he age of 50 years having 4 
some degree of prolapse on examination .1 Approximately 11% of all women undergo surgery for urinary 5 
incontinence or prolapse during their lifetime, and 7% for prolapse alone. 2 In England around 29,000 prolapse 6 
repairs were performed in 2010/20113 at a cost of around £60m, and surgery numbers are likely to increase 7 
substantially as the population ages. 4 Increasing age and parity, and family history of prolapse have been 8 
reported as the main risk factors for prolapse, although factor s such as obesity, heavy lifting and constipation 9 
may also play a role. 5 One study reported a total population-attributable risk for prolapse of 46% associated 10 
with having prolapse symptoms during pregnancy, a mother with prolapse, and undertaking heavy physical 11 
work.6 Prolapse is characterised by symptomatic descent of the vaginal walls, apex or vault from the normal 12 
anatomical position.7 Women with prolapse may present with vaginal, bladder, bowel, back, abdominal and 13 
sexual symptoms. The condition can affect daily activities and quality of life.  Current treatment options include 14 
surgery and conservative management, the latter being considered if the prolapse is less severe or the woman 15 
is a poor candidate for surgery. Conservative interventions include: physical interventions which aim to improve 16 
pelvic floor muscle function and support via pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT); mechanical interventions 17 
which aim to support the prolapse (e.g. us e of vaginal pessaries); and lifestyle interventions which seek to 18 
avoid exacerbation of the prolapse by decreasing intra- abdominal pressure (e.g. weight loss , avoiding heavy 19 
lifting). 20 
 21 
Many physiotherapists who specialise in women’s health offer women with prolapse individualised PFMT.8 The 22 
aim of PFMT is to improve pelvic floor muscle function (strength, endurance and coordination) and ultimately 23 
increase the structural support for the pelvic organs. There is evidence that PFMT is effective in the treatment 24 
of urinary incontinence 9 but the evidence for PFMT in the management  of prolapse is less clear. The 25 
Cochrane systematic review updated in 201110 identified four trials (including two pilot trials) comparing PFMT 26 
with control, two of which were at significant risk of bias. Symptoms , although measured differently in different 27 
studies, were improved in the short -term in three trials, and pooled data on severity from two trials indicated 28 
an improvement post -treatment in prolapse stage due to PFMT. The review concluded that reliable evidence 29 
4 
 

relating to effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of PFMT for symptomatic prolapse in the medium and long 3 
term is needed. 4 
 5 
We report findings of the P elvic Organ Prolapse PhysiotherapY (P OPPY) trial which compared an 6 
individualised PFMT programme compatible with UK NHS practice ( five one -to-one appointments over 16 7 
weeks), with a control group allocated to a prolapse lifestyle advice leaflet and no PFMT. Our hypothesis was 8 
that, in women with stage I to III prolapse of any type, one-to-one pelvic floor muscle training, as compared to 9 
a lifestyle advice leaflet, w ould reduce the symptoms of prolapse and the need for further prolapse treatment, 10 
and that it would be a cost-effective treatment for prolapse. 11

Methods 13 
Participants 14 
Between September 2007 and February 2010 we identified new atten dees at outpatient gynaecology clinics 15 
presenting with symptomatic prolapse  in 25 centres ( 23 UK; 1 Dunedin, New Zealand; 1 Sydney, Australia). 16 
Centres were a mix of university teaching hospitals and district general hospitals , all offering similar special ist 17 
pelvic floor physiotherapy services . Women were asked to take part if stage I, II or III prolapse of any type 18 
(anterior, posterior, apical, or a combination) was confirmed by their gynaecologist on vaginal examination  19 
using the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP -Q) measurement system,11 and if prolapse was their 20 
main presenting complaint.  21 
 22 
We excluded women if they had had previous prolapse treatment including surgery, if they were pregnant or 23 
less than six months post-natal, or if they were unable to comply with the intervention  (i.e. if they were not 24 
able to attend the clinic for appointments with the physiotherapist) . Women w ho, on examination,  were 25 
deemed to need treatment for vaginal atrophy were eligible after completing a course of local oestrogens.  26 
 27 
The trial methods were based on our  pilot trial findings. 12 Women gave signed informed consent to being 28 
randomised and to long- term follow-up. O ur trial was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of 29 
Helsinki. It was approved by : Scotland A Research Ethics Committee, Edinburgh, Scotland; Lower South 30

Procedures 8 
Women allocated to the intervention were invited to attend five one-to-one PFMT appointments over 16 weeks 9 
(at weeks 0, 2, 6, 11 and 16 ) with a women’s health physiotherapist. The intervention duration of 16 weeks 10 
was chosen on the basis of both muscle physiology (15 weeks specific muscle training is required to gain 11 
muscle hypertrophy 13) and UK clinical guidel ines for the management of urinary incontinence recommend  12 
PFMT for “at least 3 months”.14 Appointment frequency was based on current practice within the UK NHS; first 13 
appointments close together to allow reinforcement of correct exercise technique and understanding of all 14 
advice given, later appointments becoming further apart to encourage independent home exercise.  15 
 16 
At the first appointment an explanation of types of prolapse, pelvic floor muscle anatomy and function were 17 
given using diagrams and a model pelvis. Internal pelvic floor muscle assessment to correct exercise technique 18 
and assess muscles ( using the PERFECT Scheme) 15 was completed. An individualised home ex ercise 19 
programme was prescribed based on examination findings. Women were encouraged to pr ogress exercises, 20 
aiming for ten times ten second maximal holds and up to fifty fast contractions, three times per day and to 21 
record all exercises in a diary. W omen were also taught how to pre- contract the pelvic floor muscles against 22 
increases in intra- abdominal pressure (“the knack”)  and encouraged to use this technique daily. Th e home 23 
exercise programme was modified at each appointment as indicated by examination findings  and diary 24 
recordings. The use of electromyography biofeedback, pressure biofeedback and electrical stimulation were not 25 
permitted. Trial physiotherapists attended training prior to their involvement in intervention delivery within the 26 
trial. No additional training was given to physiotherapists during intervention delivery. 27

Participants received a lifestyle advice leaflet that gave advice about weight loss, constipation, avoidance of 3 
heavy lifting, coughing and high impact exercise: control women received this by post, whilst intervention group 4 
women received it at their first appointment . The leaflet contained no information about pelvic floor muscle 5 
exercises or techniques . Women attended a review appointment with their gynaecologist at six months post -6 
trial entry, at which time they could be referred for further prolapse treatment if desired. 7 
 8 
We used postal questionnaires to collect data at the time of  trial entry (baseline) , and at 6 and 12 months 9 
after trial entry. Our primary clinical endpoint was prolapse symptoms at 12 months as measured by the Pelvic 10 
Organ Prolapse Symptom Score ( POP-SS),16 a validated,  patient-completed instrument with seven items , 11 
relating to frequency of prolapse symptoms over the previous four weeks, each scored from 0 (never) to 4 (all 12 
of the time) (total score 0 to 28). Secondary outcomes included: women’s perceived change in prolapse since 13 
the start of the study (same, better, worse); quality of life measured as interference of prolapse symptoms with 14 
everyday life (scored 0 ‘not at all’ to 10  ‘a great deal’ ); number of days with prolapse symptoms in the 15 
previous four weeks; uptake of further prolapse treatment  (surgery, ring pessary, referral to physiotherapy, 16 
referral to dietician, oestrogen cream/tablets or HRT ); impact of incontinence (International Consultation on 17 
Incontinence Questionnaire Urinary Incontinence S hort Form - ICIQ UI SF –  scored 0 to 21 , higher values 18 
indicating greater severity)17; bowel symptoms (early short form version of ICIQ bowel symptom questionnaire  19 
provided by the developers ); sexual symptoms ( Pelvic Organ Prolapse/Urinary Incontinence Sexual 20 
Questionnaire, PISQ-12)18; general health (SF -12)19; use of health services in primary and secondary care;  21 
and frequency of the practice of pelvic floor muscle exercises in last 4 weeks (a few times only, once a week, 22 
a few times a week, once a day, a few times a day, and contractions per day: <5, 5–10, 11–20, 21–30, 31–23 
60, >60). Intervention adherence was measured in terms of attendance at appointments and the amount of 24 
exercise w omen recorded in their daily exercise diary. In addition, the phys iotherapists delivering the 25 
intervention collected data at each appointment about women’s adherence to the prescribed exercise. 26 
 27 
Assessment of prolapse type and stage by the gynaecologist in clinic was carried out in all women before 28 
group allocation and at the 6-month review appointment using the POP -Q system.11 Formal POP-Q training 29

POP-Q examination (e.g. examination position, bladder empty ing, equipment use); use of the recording form 5 
and a question and answer session. Each centre was provided with a copy of the DVD and the publication 6 
describing the POP-Q.11 Centres were encouraged to carry out further in- house training, and additional centre 7 
visits were offered if necessary. 8

Group allocation and masking 11 
Women were allocated to groups using the remote-computer determined randomisation application at the 12 
Centre for Healthcare Randomised Trials , Health Services Research Unit, University of Aberdeen, UK . 13 
Treatment allocation used minimisation to balance group sizes on key prognostic factors at baseline. These 14 
were centre, stage of prolapse (I, II or III), and the woman’s motivation for prolapse surgery (not considering 15 
surgery/considering surgery). The latter is potentially an important factor influencing how adherent women will 16 
be to PFMT. The university-based trial coordinator accessed the web-based application and then informed the 17 
woman, and the physiotherapist as necessary, of the allocated group. The intervention could not be masked 18 
from women or treating physiotherapists. Outcome assessment was by participant -completed questionnaires, 19 
thus avoiding assessor bias : data entry was carried out blinded to group allocation. The gynaecologist 20 
undertaking the POP -Q assessment at 6  months was blinded to women’s trial group until after the 21 
examination.  22 
 23 
Sample size 24 
We estimated a difference betwe en groups in mean POP -SS of 2.5 as our effect size, based on the pilot 25 
trial.12 With 253 women per group the trial had 80% power at the 5% significance level to detect a different of 26 
2.5 points in the primary outcome measure,  assuming a common standard dev iation (SD) of 8 points.20 This 27 
calculation allowed for 10% loss to follow -up overall, and 15% of the control group receiving all of the benefit 28 
of PFMT by undertaking exercises under their own initiative. 29

Statistical analysis 4 
We tabulated descriptive statistics, reporting baseline demographics and clinical characteristics with means and 5 
SDs, or median and inter-quartile range (IQR) as appropriate. We used intention-to-treat analyses to compare 6 
the primary outcome at 12 months by fitting a linear mixed eff ects model to change from baseline in POP-SS 7 
at 6 and 12 months , with a random intercept for subject within centre and a random slope for time within 8 
subject, and adjusted for baseline POP-SS score and the minimisation variables. Such models implicitly adjust 9 
the model estimates where there are missing data, assumed them to be missing at random, according to 10 
observed values .21 Women who had observations at baseline and at least one follow -up time-point were 11 
included in the model. The difference between the intervention and control groups in estimated mean change 12 
from baseline was presented for 6 and 12 months  with 95% confidence intervals and p- values. We also 13 
assessed the assumption of missing at random and corresponding impact of missing responses on the primary 14 
outcome using multiple imputation.22 Model assumptions were checked using residual plots and found to hold. 15 
 16 
POP-Q stage was compared between groups  in an ordinal regression model with 6 month POP-Q stage as 17 
the dependent variable, and baseline POP-Q stage and minimisation variables as covariates. The pooled odds 18 
ratio from the ordinal model was calculated with a 95% confidence interval and p- value. Stage II prolapse was 19 
subdivided depending on whether the prolapse was above the hymen, or at the hymen or below . Change in 20 
POP-Q stage between baseline and six months was also presented. Other secondary outcomes were 21 
compared between groups using the Mann- Whitney (M-W) test for continuous and ordinal variables and the 22 
chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. 23 
 24 
Through planned subgroup analyses we explored the effect on the primary outcome of prolapse stage and 25 
type, age and motivation for surgery, using stricter levels of statistical significance (two-sided p<0·01). 26 
 27

Analyses were conducted according to a pre- specified Statistical Analysis Plan using the R programming 3 
package23 and the mi package in R 24 for post-hoc multiple imputation analysis. The analyst was independent 4 
of the research team and was blinded to group allocation until after the main analysis had been undertaken. 5

Economic analysis 7 
Our economic assessment was a within- trial analysis at 12 months after recruitment  taking an NHS cost 8 
perspective. Direct health-service costs were used to generate the total cost for each participant. Based on the 9 
number of trial physiotherapy appointments attended, we estimated the amount of physiotherapy time which 10 
was involved in the delivery of the intervention and the associated costs of clinic space. All women were 11 
asked in follow -up quest ionnaires about their use of health services ( general practitioner, practice nurse 12 
consultations) and any further prolapse treatment they had received. Costs were attributed to these items  13 
using UK data from: Personal Social Services Research Unit, Unit Costs of Health and Social Care; Scottish 14 
Health Service Costs; British National Formulary; and C&G Medicare Limited. 25–28 The costs were balanced 15 
against changes in the primary clinical outcome. We assumed that where we observed a difference between 16 
the trial groups in rates of subsequent treatments such as surgery at the end of the trial follow-up period, 17 
these represented savings. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the possible impacts of varying the 18 
intervention effect size and the uptake of subsequent prolapse treatment. 19

Prolapse outcomes 4 
Women in the intervention group reported more improvement in prolapse symptoms (a significantly greater 5 
reduction in POP-SS) compared to the control group both at 6 months (difference between groups in change 6

3.67], 12 months: 1.66, 95% CI [0.74, 2.58]). 10

abdominal heaviness” this was true at 12 months also (Table 3). Women in the intervention group were also 15 
less likely to report having prolapse symptoms in the last 4 weeks both at 6 and 12 months (Table 3). 16

(52%) versus 32/189 (17%), M-W p<0.0001) and 12 months (83/145 (57%) versus 63/141 (45%), M-W 20

Discussion 17 
We found a greater reduction in prolapse symptoms in the PFMT group at 12 months when compared to the 18 
control group. The difference was both statistically significant, and of a magnitude that would be important to 19 
women, as it exceeded the minimally important change for the POP -SS.29 This finding was  supported by a 20 
higher uptake of supplementary treatments (principally PFMT) in the control group after 6 months indicating 21 
residual need; a lower prevalence of each individual prolapse symptom, as well as in bladder, bowel and 22 
sexual symptoms, and better quality of life in the intervention group after 6 months of PFMT. Women in the 23 
intervention group were also more likely to say their prolapse was “better” at both 6 and 12 months. While 24 
more women in the intervention group demonstrated improvement in prolapse  stage, this was not significantly 25 
different between the groups. Subgroup analyses indicated that these findings of effectiveness held regardless 26 
of the woman’s prolapse stage or type, her age or her attitude towards having surgery. 27 
 28 
14 
 

Since there was a h igh degree of uptake of some form  of PFMT in the control group before the primary 3 
outcome assessment at 12 months, and no evidence of differential use of other non -PFMT interventions, it 4 
seems plausible that the intention- to-treat treatment effect estimate is an underestimate of the benefit 5 
associated with PFMT at 12 months. We are confident therefore that the significant treatment effects reported 6 
represent real effects that are of importance to women and clinicians. 7 
 8 
By 12 months a greater proportion of c ontrol women than intervention women had received further prolapse 9 
treatment (49% versus 23%), predominantly  PFMT between 6 and 12 months : a quarter of the control group 10 
women sought a referral to physiotherapy, giving them access to PFMT , indicating residual need for treatment. 11 
However, similar proportions of women in both groups had undergone prolapse repair surgery by 12 months 12 
(11% intervention and 10% control), or received a pessary.  13 
 14 
The observation that at 12 months the control group were as likely to be exercising as the intervention group 15 
may be explained partly by the uptake of physiotherapy in the control group. It is encouraging that almost 80% 16 
of intervention women were still exercising at 12 months, as long-term adherence is an important consideration 17 
for the effectiveness of this intervention. 18 
 19 
The net cost of the intervention was £1 31.61 per woman. The main determinant of the cost is of providing the 20 
intervention. The main area of uncertainty is the longer -term impact of PFMT on the need for subsequent 21 
treatments such as pessaries, physiotherapy and surgery: our results are based on the trial follow-up period 22 
and we cannot exclude the possibility that treatments have been delayed rather than avoided. The sensitivity 23 
analyses show the plausible ranges around our results, however there are reasons for believing that the higher 24 
costs are unlikely, notably that the expenditure on the intervention is a one- off so all the costs have been 25 
incurred and it is plausible that the benefits in terms of reduced symptoms and treatments avoided will 26 
continue to accrue over time.  27 
 28 
15 
 

Assuming that intervention women gained 10% on their quality of life for a year as a result of the intervention, 3 
the cost per quality adjusted life year (QALY) gained is around £16 ,000. This level of cost per QALY is 4 
commonly accepted as worthwhile by organisations such as the National Institute for Health and Clinical 5 
Excellence in the UK.  6 
 7 
The main strengths of the trial were its size, rigour and pragmatic design, with the intervent ion being relevant 8 
to UK NHS practice , and potentially to other similar health systems worldwide, and the outcomes being 9 
woman-centred. Participants’ compliance with trial processes and the intervention were generally high. Unlike 10 
other trials in this area, our main focus was the prolapse symptoms which led the women to seek treatment, 11 
and which we used to measure treatment success.  12 
 13 
In terms of limitations, we achieved 88% of our target sample size of 506 , and experienced a lower 14 
questionnaire response rate at 12 months  (66%) than expected, despite postal and telephone reminders. 15 
However, as  the observed SD of the POP -SS was smaller than originally assumed, we nevertheless had 16 
sufficient power to identify important differences. There was no evidence of differential dropout as the response 17 
rate was similar in both trial groups, and results were also found to be robust to missing data. Not all women 18 
had a prolapse assessment at 6 months; therefore there was also attrition in the POP -Q responses (75% 19 
intervention, 77% control). This may have contributed to the non- significant POP -Q finding. There was 20 
significant crossover of control women to the intervention due to their uptake of PFMT after 6 months, and this 21 
makes interpretation of the findings more challenging. A further limitation is the short follow -up period of 12 22 
months: due to natural fluctuation in prolapse symptoms and the effect of different treatment modalities, clinical 23 
and cost differences between the groups might be expected to change with time. W omen included in this trial 24 
were treatment -naïve, presenting for treatment for the first time. However, PFMT may also be effective in 25 
enhancing surgical or pessary treatment, or for use after surgical failure, or shortly after childbirth and these 26 
situations need further research. In the economic analysis we did not estimate QALYs gained since we found 27 
in our pilot work that the SF-12 was insensitive to meaningful changes in prolapse symptoms  in this 28 
population. Decision-makers therefore must interpret the results based on a careful reading of the symptoms 29 
16 
 

women suffered and the extent to which these were relieved. The lack of other economic studies in this field 3 
makes it difficult to compare results, and we look forward to future studies that provide comparisons for these 4 
results. 5 
 6 
There are six other randomised studies published to date comparing PFMT with a control.12,30–34 Three of these 7 
are pilot trials making it problematic to draw conclusions from their findings due to their developmental nature 8 
and small sample sizes.12,30,31
 Three other full-sized trials have been published. 32–34 The Piya-Anant trial32 had 9 
methodological limitations and high risk of bias and cannot reliably contribute to the evidence- base. No 10 
information was provided on the processes of random sequence generation or allocation concealment; there 11 
was no reporting of attrition, selective reporting of only a subgroup of the women randomised, and uncertainty 12 
as to whether the analysis was an intention to treat analysis.  13 
 14 
Of the remaining two trials , the Brækken single-centre trial33 of PFMT versus control randomised 109 women 15 
with stage I to III prolapse, of which a subgroup of only 69 women were symptomatic and hence comparable 16 
to our population. The very intensive PFMT regimen consisted of weekly appointments for 3 months, followed 17 
by bi-weekly appointments for 3 months : a model of treatment that it would not be possible to deliver in the 18 
UK and many other countries. Kashyap34 recently reported on a single-centre trial in women with stage I to III 19 
prolapse which compared taught PFMT plus a self instruction manual (n=70) with the  self instruction manual 20 
alone as control (n=70). A single person delivered the PFMT intervention to all women. The content of the 21 
manual was not described and therefore it is unclear what written instruction the control group received. More 22 
importantly, four women transferred from the control group to the PFMT plus manual group and it is not clear 23 
in which group these women were analysed: until this is clarified the results have limited utility. 24 
 25 
Symptom benefit from PFMT was reported by both Braekken33 and Kashyap34. Braekken analysed women with 26 
symptoms at baseline, and found that those who had received PFMT compared to controls were more likely to 27 
have reduced frequency of symptoms (74% versus 31%) and reduced bother (67% versus 42%). Kashyap 28 
reported a significantly greater mean reduction in POP -SS score post-intervention for the PFMT  plus manual 29 
17 
 

group compared to the control group (2.99 versus 1.25). Neither trial sought evidence about longer term 3 
outcomes or effect on the uptake of other treatments. 4 
  5 
Braekken33 also reported that PFMT improved POP -Q stage: 19% had an improved stage in the intervention 6 
group versus 8% in the control group (11% risk difference). O ur finding for POP -Q was marginally non-7 
significant but of a similar size (risk difference 7.5%, 95% CI [-1.4%, 16.4%]). The most likely reason for the 8 
non-significant finding in our trial is that the study was not powered to show a difference for this out come. 9 
Data on change in the POP -Q or prolapse stage in the Kashyap trial were not adequately reported to allow 10 
comparison.34  11 
 12 
We chose our primary outcome measure to be symptom change: this is usually the driver for seeking 13 
treatment for prolapse, and hence the most important outcome for women. It is increasingly recognised that 14 
there is little correlation between ‘stage’ of prolapse and the prolapse symptoms ascribed to it.35,36 Therefore it 15 
is not surprising that , as we found, an improvement in symptoms does not necessarily correspond to an 16 
improvement in stage.  17 
 18 
We found that 45% of women in the control group reported that their prolapse was better at 12 months. This 19 
is in part due to the fact that around half ( 49%) of these women had received further t reatment for prolapse 20 
by this time-point. Although significantly more women in the intervention group compared to the control group 21 
reported their prolapse was better  (57% versus 45%), the remaining 43%  reported no change or worse 22 
prolapse. Thus we conclude that a substantial group of women did not benefit. One potential reason is that a 23 
more intensive intervention might be required for some women . Another is that some types  or stages of 24 
prolapse do not respond to PFMT as well as others and hence better selection of women for PFMT might be 25 
required. Although our subgroup analyses (for prolapse stage and type, age and motivation for surgery) did 26 
not support these hypotheses, the analyses were exploratory and under-powered to draw firm conclusions. 27 
 28 
18 
 

It is reco gnised that prolapse can regress with time, and this could partially explain the improvement we 3 
observed. Three studies of the epidemiology of prolapse have concluded that prolapse can both progress and 4 
regress.37–39 The studies by Handa37 and Bradley38 looked at change in severity of prolapse, but in populations 5 
older than our own. The study by Miedel39 is most relevant for comparison as it examined both symptoms and 6 
stage of prolapse over time in women with a mean age of 56. They found that 44% of stage I  prolapses had 7 
regressed (improved) to stage 0, 24% of stage II showed regression, and 64% (95% CI [56%, 72%]) of 8 
women had a red uction in symptoms by 5 years. However the study population was mainly non- consulting 9 
women identified by a positive questionna ire response to “a feeling of a vaginal bulge” , rather than women 10 
who were actively seeking treatment for prolapse. As the authors point ed out, results cannot automatically be 11 
generalised to patients who present to health care. Thus we do not know to what extent women in our trial 12 
naturally improved. However, we would expect that any natural regression or progression would occur equally 13 
in both groups by virtue of the group allocation, and hence the observed significant differences between the 14 
groups must be due to the intervention. 15 
 16 
Our trial constitutes the largest, rigorous, pragmatic multicentre trial of PFMT for prolapse, with the longest 17 
follow-up, and as such provides the necessary evidence to support changes in clinical practice. However the 18 
resource implications of implementing these findings need to be considered. The physiotherapists delivering the 19 
trial intervention were specialists in women’s health; their numbers are limited and workload is large, currently 20 
consisting mainly of the management of urinary incontinence. With the establishment of an evidence- base for 21 
PFMT in the management of prolapse, health care providers will need to invest in extra resources to ensure 22 
that a similar service can be provided for women with prolapse.  In addition, outwith the clinical arena, the role 23 
of pelvic floor muscle exercises in alleviating prolapse symptoms is an important public health message, which 24 
needs to be shared widely with females of all ages. 25 
 26 
In summary, we found individualised PFMT was effective, leading to greater reduction in prolapse symptoms in 27 
the PFMT group. The net cost of the intervention was £1 31.61 per woman, and under plausible conditions this 28 
would prove cost-effective. 29 
19 
 

 3 
We conclude that PFMT should be recommended for the conservative management of prolapse. Effectiveness 4 
of PFMT in the long-term, in women who have had previous prolapse surgery, in conjunction with pessary use 5 
and within populations of women with different types or combinations of prolapse should be investigated 6 
further. 7

Systematic review 11 
The Cochrane review on the topic of conservative management of prolapse was updated in 2011 by two of the 12 
authors, prior to the completion of the analysis of  the current trial. Four trials compared PFMT with a 13

prolapse stage compared to no PFMT. 18 
Interpretation 19 
Our trial represents the largest, rigorous, pragmatic trial of PFMT versus control for prolapse, and as such 20 
provides important robust evidence to inform clinical practice. Its findings confirm the findings of other smaller 21 
or less rigorous studies that PFMT is beneficial in terms of reducing women’s prolapse symptoms. The findings 22 
have implications for a range of healthcare professionals who care for women with prolapse ( general 23 
practitioners, gynaecologists, physiotherapists, nurses, healthcare managers) and for women themselves. 24
