Galloway-Mowat syndrome (GAMOS) is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder
that is underrecognized. The phenotype is heterogeneous, but it is now widely
accepted that early-onset nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) and microcephaly with brain
malformation are characteristic features of Galloway-Mowat syndrome. Although
the five subunits that encode the KEOPS complex, OSGEP/TP53RK/TPRKB/LAGE3/GON7,
are known to cause Galloway-Mowat syndrome, the mutation of the WDR73, WDR4,
NUP107, NUP133, and PRDM15 genes can lead to Galloway-Mowat syndrome, which
makes the diagnosis more challenging. This review outlines current knowledge
regarding Galloway-Mowat syndrome from another perspective. Starting from the
history of Galloway-Mowat syndrome and reviewing the clinical details of
patients with and without genetic traits, we discuss the phenotypic and genetic
heterogeneity of the disease. We pay particular attention to all confounding
clinical signs and symptoms that may lead to misdiagnosis. Indeed, some patients
with Galloway-Mowat syndrome have a clinical condition of nephrotic range
proteinuria, with or without hematuria, such as glomerular disease or chronic
kidney disease of unknown origin. Although glomerular injury is frequently
documented in biopsies of patients with Galloway-Mowat syndrome, there is
currently no reliable evidence that renal biopsy has diagnostic or prognostic
value. We reviewed published histopathological reports of renal tubule and
glomerular injury in these patients and discussed the current knowledge on the
role of genes that contribute to the onset of Galloway-Mowat syndrome in
glomerular function.

GAMOS, a distinct renal–neurological disease, is characterized by early-onset nephrotic syndrome associated with microcephaly, brain anomalies, and developmental delays [1, 2]. Some patients present with retinal dysfunction, hiatal hernia, psychomotor disorders, and hypotonia, with or without chronic kidney disease (CKD). The prevalence of Galloway–Mowat syndrome is estimated to be less than 1 in 1 million, and like most genetic disorders, onset usually occurs in childhood [3, 4]. Most often, five genes encode the KEOPS complex subunits OSGEP TP53RK/TPRKB/LAGE3/GON7, and one random genetic mutation can lead to Galloway–Mowat syndrome [5]. WDR73, WDR4, NUP107 NUP133, and PRDM15 gene mutations can lead to the occurrence of this disease [6–9]. While the discovery of these genetic mutations would undoubtedly lead to a diagnosis of Galloway–Mowat syndrome, it does not reveal the phenotypic heterogeneity of the disease. The Galloway–Mowat syndrome phenotype was defined as the presence of primary microcephaly, developmental delay, seizure tendency, and early-onset nephrotic syndrome. That is, an increasing number of reports describe subjects with mutations in the above genes that present an incomplete phenotype. This phenotypic diversity is the dark side of Galloway–Mowat syndrome, as it can lead to it being missed diagnosis or misdiagnosed and poorly treated or even leading to pointless kidney biopsies.

In this review, we discuss the heterogeneity of Ggalloway–Mowat syndrome, starting with its first validated gene and focusing on its diverse genetic and phenotypic characteristics. We also highlight the complexity of the clinical phenotype of Gallowy–Mowat syndrome to highlight the clinical manifestations that lead to misdiagnosis.

In 1968, Galloway and Mowat reported the simultaneous occurrence of three symptoms in two siblings from a nonblood family [10]. Siblings presented with microcephaly, hiatal hernia, and nephrotic syndrome. This is the first report of these conditions occurring in siblings at the same time, discussing the possible genetic basis of these disorders.

Since then, more patients with clinical features very similar to those described by Galloway and Mowat have been reported [11–17], The Galloway and Mowat triad is present in all of these individuals, but other eye, nervous system, and ear abnormalities have also been documented at a sufficient frequency to indicate the presence of a syndrome with a range of clinical manifestations. Analyzing these families expands the range of clinical features originally described in Galloway–Mowat syndrome, which is now known as Galloway–Mowat syndrome. Unfortunately, despite a growing number of genes associated with syndromic and nonsyndromic inherited NS, the molecular basis of Galloway–Mowat syndrome is remained unknown, and despite efforts to characterize proteins that may be involved in its pathogenesis, the genetics of Galloway–Mowat syndrome remained a mystery, with the authors unable to determine whether this disorder, called idiopathic, is X-linked or autosomal inherited. Further research is needed to characterize the pathogenesis of this disease.

In 2014, Estelle et al. used whole-exome sequencing (WES) to identify WDR73 as a gene and finally cloned and characterized the WDR73 gene, suggesting that it may be the cause of Galloway–Mowat syndrome. Mutations in the gene caused Galloway–Mowat syndrome in two unrelated families, and Galloway–Mowat syndrome caused by this gene was identified as an autosomal recessive disorder. Since then, 50 additional cases have been reported as having this diagnosis: Julia Vodopiutz et al., 2015 [1], Tawfeg Ben-Omran et al., 2015 [18], Robert N. Jinks 2015 [2], Leo C.K. Wanet al., 2016 [19], and Chen Jianget al., 2017 [20].

It took until 2017 that another milestone, namely, the genetic heterogeneity of Galloway–Mowat syndrome, was reached. Daniela A Braun et al. described individuals from 32 GAMOS families with the Galloway–Mowat syndrome phenotype (primary microcephaly, developmental delay, seizure tendency, and early-onset nephrotic syndrome) without any WDR73 gene mutation. Using WES and high-throughput exon sequencing, mutations in the OSGEP, TP53RK, TPRKB, and LAGE3 genes coding for four subunits of the KEOPS complex were identified as the cause of Galloway–Mowat syndrome [5].Four novel single-gene causes of Galloway–Mowat syndrome were identified, the link between KEOPS function and human disease was described, and the underlying pathogenic mechanisms were described. In the same year, Leo C.K. Wan et al. identified C14ORF142 as the fifth core subunit of the human KEOPS complex through proteomic analysis. This further confirms the view that Galloway–Mowat syndrome is a genetically heterogeneous disease.

In addition to KEOPS genes, other unrelated genes were subsequently identified, and in 2018, Daniela A. Braun et al. identified WDR4 as a new cause of Galloway–Mowat syndrome [7]. In the same year, Atsushi Fujita proposed that the homozygous mutation NUP133 could also cause Galloway–Mowat syndrome [8]. Hye Sun Hyun et al. reported that Galloway–Mowat syndrome is caused by TP53RK mutations [21]. In 2019, Christelle Arrondel reported that YRDC mutations can also cause Galloway–Mowat syndrome [22]. In 2021, Nina Mann proposed that PRDM15 mutation was a new cause of Galloway–Mowat syndrome [9].

The first gene associated with GAMOS syndrome was WDR. The WDR gene (MIM#618586, reference sequence NM_014023.4) is a WD40 repeat sequence (WDR) containing a protein of unknown function that was cloned in 2014 [6]. A homozygous mutation, c. 129T > G (p.Tyr43*) and a homozygous frameshift mutation, c.766dupC (p.Arg256Profs*18), were identified from two unrelated families [6]. WDR73 is expected to encode proteins containing six WD40 motifs, and WDR represents one of the most abundant domains in the human proteome [23], which is also present in other eukaryotes [24]. Proteins with WDR domains form a ring-shaped structure surrounded by beta, a “doughnut hole”, where the propeller normally interacts with other proteins. The buckeye beta propeller structure is the most common and is expected to be highly stable [25]. p.Tyr43* alteration truncates the protein precociously and is located upstream of all of the WD40 repeats, whereas the p.Arg256Profs*18 mutant is expected.

to contain four WD40 repeats.

The WDR73 gene is located in the 15q25.2 chromosome band with 9 exons, and the ATG initiation site is located on exon 1. Five WDR73 transcripts encoding the classic 8 amino acid protein were produced. Nearly 15 different pathogenic variants of WDR have been described [1, 2, 4, 6, 18–20].Most reported mutations were missense (58%) or frameshift (16%), followed by truncation (16%), nonsense (8%), or large deletions (2%).

WDR73 encodes a protein containing WD40 repeats, which are motifs of approximately 40–60 amino acid residues that usually end with Trp-Asp (WD) [26]. Each WD40 repeat folds into a four-stranded antiparallel b sheet blade. These blades often assemble into domains containing a seven-bladed propeller called the WD40 domain. These domains act as platforms for protein assembly, making them hubs in many cell signaling networks [26]. Several genes encoding WD40-domain proteins have already been implicated in human genetic disorders [27–29]. Experiments by Estelle et al. in various cell models clearly revealed the role of WDR73 in cell survival and microtubule regulation [6]. Human WDR73 plays a crucial role in the regulation of microtubules in glomerular podocytes [30]. However, its expression is not limited to these cells. WDR73 is also involved in the organization of neuronal and axonal microtubule networks [31]. Although podocytes and neurons are highly differentiated cells, which are very dissimilar in terms of structure and function, both cell types share many common biological features [32, 33]. Neurons and podocytes are both organized into the main cell body, giving rise to multiple projections that subsequently form axons and dendrites in neurons and primary foot processes in podocytes with the same atypical microtubule polarity [34].

Many in vitro studies have been conducted to elucidate the role of WDR73 in GAMOS. In 2014, when the WDR73 protein structure was first described, Estelle reported that cells with frameshift mutations in WER73 presented dense and disorganized aggregates of microtubules around the nucleus [6] In 2015, Robert et al. provided the first description of microtubules, which are critical for normal cell cycle progression, proliferation and survival of human fibroblasts, podocytes and neuronal subtypes [2].

Many studies have demonstrated that differentiated podocytes are fragile cells and that maintaining their structure requires fine regulation of microtubule and actin dynamics [35, 36]. These findings in the kidney support the idea that WDR73 plays a role in microtubule network regulation, which is consistent with recent data on inherited NS and FSGS. The integrity of the slit membrane depends on microtubules, which are essential for maintaining cell structure and protein transport. The different immunostaining patterns of WDR73 and the actin-associated protein synaptopodin indicate that WDR73 preferentially localizes to microtubule-rich primary foot processes rather than actin-rich secondary foot processes. Both neurons and podocytes organize into one major cell body, producing multiple protrusions, followed by the formation of axons and dendrites in neurons and primary podocytes, with the same atypical microtubule polarity [34]. WDR73 is an example of a gene that mutates to cause this dual phenotype, providing additional evidence for the presence of common developmental and maintenance molecular processes in the glomeruli and nervous system [37]. They have shown that WDR73 is localized in the cytoplasm during interphase and then accumulates at the spindle poles and microtubule asters during mitosis. This behavior resembles that of proteins encoded by genes mutated in primary microcephaly, namely CENPJ [38], and WDR62 [29]. Thus, it is easy to speculate that WDR73, like WDR62, is involved in the mitosis of neuroprecursors during embryonic life. The key functions of WDR73 in vivo remain to be defined, but the consistent and severe NCS phenotype reported by Colin et al. [6] and Robert et al. [2] indicates that WDR73 has specific and indispensable roles in the generation, proliferation, and/or viability of cortical neuroblasts; development of the cerebellar granule cell layer; survival of cholinergic striatal interneurons; central visual pathway development; and podocyte biology. Robert et al. suggested that WDR73 interacts in a complex with the mTORC1-regulated p70 S6 kinase and its natural substrate, CAD [2]. Tilley et al. demonstrated the association of WDR73 with the INTS9 and INTS11 components of the Integrator complex. Researchers have shown that in cells in which WDR73 expression is either absent or reduced, cellular processes known to be regulated by the Integrator complex, including UsnRNA 3′ processing and EGF responsiveness, are perturbed [39]. This study provides insight into the pathogenesis of disease in GAMOS patients.

Although many mutations have been identified, the correlation between the type of mutation or protein domain affecting, on the one hand, protein function/expression, on the other hand, has not been determined. All of these studies have demonstrated a link between specific types of mutations and different types of cellular dysfunction, and despite these advances, the precise cellular function of WDR73 and how WDR73 mutations lead to the development of GAMOS remain poorly understood. However, these effects appear to be independent of the phenotypic heterogeneity observed in GAMOS patients.

In 2015, a WDR73 knockout zebrafish model was generated to reveal the molecular mechanism of GAMOS. In a zebrafish model, WDR73 knockdown by morpholino was linked to disrupted brain morphogenesis, perturbed neuronal differentiation and a reduction in the number of mitotic neural progenitor cells (NPCs) [18]. These models provide new insights into the functional role of WDR73 in brain development. Unfortunately, there is no mouse model at present, which needs to be further studied.

KEOPS was first discovered in two separate genetic screens in the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae [40, 41]. The KEOPS/EKC complex is a tRNA modification complex involved in the biosynthesis of N6-threonylcarbamoyladenosine (t6A), a universally conserved tRNA modification found on ANN codons that recognizes tRNAs. In archaea and eukaryotes, KEOPS is composed of OSGEP/Kae1, PRPK/Bud32, TPRKB/Cgi121 and LAGE3/Pcc1. Wan et al. performed a proteomic analysis of human KEOPS in 2016 [42]. This work identified 152 protein interactors, one of which, C14ORF142, interacted strongly with all four KEOPS subunits, suggesting that it may be a core component of human KEOPS. Each KEOPS subunit OSGEP (Kae1 ortholog), PRPK (Bud32 ortholog), TPRKB (Cgi121 ortholog), and LAGE3 (Pcc1 ortholog) was strongly interacting with each other. It was first proposed that C14ORF142 is the fifth core subunit of the complex (also known as the GON7 subunit). In 2017, Braun et al. conducted whole-exon sequencing from 32 GAMOS families and reported recessive mutations in OSGEP, TP53RK, TPRKB, and LAGE3, which encode four subunits of the KEOPS complex. Four novel single-gene etiologies of GAMOS were identified, the associations between KEOPS function and human disease were described, and impaired cell proliferation, reduced translation, ER stress, DDR signaling activation, increased apoptosis, and deficient actin regulation were described as possible pathogenic features [5]. Inheritance was autosomal recessive or X chromosome linked (LAGE3). Phenotypically, individuals with mutations in any of the four genes that code for the KEOPS subunit have primary microcephaly, developmental delays, a tendency toward seizures, and early-onset nephrotic syndrome. In 2019, Christelle et al. confirmed the structural and cellular characterization of GON7, which encodes the fifth KEOPS subunit, suggesting that it is involved in the cellular stability and quaternary arrangement of the KEOPS complex, with mutations that lead to milder forms of disease [22].GAMOS is clinically heterogeneous, reflecting genetic heterogeneity [1–8, 10, 16–21, 43–47] (Table 1).

Knocking out osgep or tprkb in juvenile zebra fish summarizes the primary microcephaly phenotype, as found in humans with mutations in the gene encoding the KEOPS subunit [5]. Compared with wild-type embryos, mouse embryos in which Lage3, Osgep, or Tprkb was knocked out via CRISPR-Cas9 had significantly shorter cortical length, cortic-midbrain line length, and cortical width, thus recreating the human phenotype of primary microcephaly [5]. However, no kidney phenotypes were observed in the knockout mice or fish, possibly because early lethality masks possible kidney involvement in older animals.

In fact, disease-causing mutations have been found in 10 genes to date: five in the KEOPS gene and five in other unrelated genes, namely, WDR73, NUP107, WDR4, NUP133, and YRDC.

In 2017, Rosti et al. first reported that homozygous mutations in NUP107 cause microcephaly with steroid-resistant nephropathy, similar to Ggalloway–Mowat syndrome [45]. NUP107 belongs to the family of nuclear pore complex components involved in dual functions, regulating bidirectional trafficking at the nucleus‒cytoplasm interface and the mitotic apparatus during cytokinesis [48]. The clinical overlap of mutations in NUP107 and WDR73 leading to GAMOS suggests potential shared mechanisms. WDR73 encodes a six-bladed propeller WD40 repeat protein, which likely functions as a scaffold for protein complex assembly [6].

An increasing number of mutations have been found in genes encoding tRNA-modifying enzymes associated with neurodevelopmental disorders in humans. The mutation in WDR4 was originally described as causing a unique form of microcephalic primordial dwarfism and brain malformation [49]. In 2018, Daniela et al. reported that patients with the GAMOS phenotype presented with homozygous splicing site mutations in WDR4 [7]. WDR4 is a component of the METTL1/WDR4 holoenzyme, an N7-methylguanosine (M7G) responsible for the G modification of a specific subgroup of tRNAs by the highly conserved m methyltransferase 7 [50, 51]. WDR4 is an additional example of a gene that encodes a tRNA-modifying enzyme and gives rise to GAMOS if mutated. These findings support the recent observation that, similar to neurons, podocytes of the renal glomerulus are particularly vulnerable to cellular defects resulting from altered tRNA modifications. The absence of m7G tRNA modification leads to impaired cell proliferation, neural differentiation, and a decrease in global translation with a less efficient translation of mRNAs involved in cell division and brain development, which is consistent with the microcephaly and brain anomalies found in individuals with WDR4 mutations [52]. (Deletion of the 7G tRNA modification of leads to impaired cell proliferation, reduced neural differentiation and global translation, and less efficient mRNA translation involved in cell division and brain development, which is consistent with microcephaly and brain abnormalities found in individuals with WDR4 mutations). Genes encoding components of the t6A biosynthesis pathway have to be added to the growing list of translation-associated proteins whose loss of function is responsible for rare genetic disorders.

In the same year, Atsushi et al. identified the homozygous nup133 mutation via whole-exon sequencing [8]. The zebrafish model of NUP133-knockdown presented microcephaly, fewer neurons, glomerular hypoplasia, and podocyte process fusion, which is characteristic of human GAMOS. This finding suggests that the loss-of-function mutation in the biallelic gene NUP133 causes GAMOS [8].

The YRDC gene mutation identified by Christelle et al. in 2019 can also cause GAMOS [22]. Threonyl carbamylation (t6A) of adenosine of N6-ANN-type tRNA is a general-purpose modification that is critical for the accuracy and efficiency of translation. This t6 pathway uses two sequentially acting enzymes, YRDC and OSGEP, the latter of which is a subunit of the polyprotein KEOPS complex. tRNA modifications have been shown to play a role in the development of the brain and nervous system, and a growing number of defects in these modifications are now associated with various human neurodevelopmental disorders [53]. A study revealed that mutations in YRDC lead to extremely severe forms of GAMOS [22].

Since the clinical presentation of GAMOS was first described in 1968, the best feature of the disease is the simultaneous occurrence of various neurological symptoms and glomerular kidney disease [10]. Neurological symptoms include primary or postnatal microcephaly, often accompanied by brain abnormalities such as cerebral atrophy/hypoplasia and neural migration defects [44]. However, the significant heterogeneity of the clinical manifestations of this disease means that it is not uncommon for patients to be undetected or be misdiagnosed. The renal manifestations range from proteinuria to steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS), which rapidly progresses to end-stage renal disease [2, 6]. However, the significant heterogeneity of the clinical manifestations of this disease means that it is not uncommon for cases to be undetected or misdiagnosed.

GAMOS is a type of glomerular disease, but steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), and minimal change disease (MCD) are typical consequences of glomerular dysfunction and are most commonly diagnosed clinically before the molecular diagnosis of GAMOS in childhood. SRNS is usually detected in the first few months after birth and rapidly progresses to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) within a few months; however, in rare cases, patients with preserved renal function in adulthood [1]. Most affected individuals die in early childhood. GAMOS patients with WDR73 mutations are affected by specific subgroups of the disease, typically presenting with progressive microcephaly, ataxia with cerebellar degeneration after birth, and sometimes with epilepsy and optic nerve atrophy [19]. Compared with patients with coding gene mutations (such as the KEOPS complex), these patients tend to have kidney disease later and greater age variation at onset [5]. Several individuals with mutations in any one of the four genes encoding the KEOPS subunit have facial deformities, including small and narrow forehead, large/soft ear, deep congealed eye, excessive eye distance, and small jaw deformities, as well as skeletal abnormalities such as arachnoids [5]. The results of brain imaging examinations include brain atrophy and cerebellar atrophy, as well as defects in the gyrus and/or myelin sheath formation. These abnormalities are associated with neurological deficits, such as psychomotor disorders, hypotonia, epileptic seizures, and even rarer sensorineural blindness and deafness. Arrondel et al. reported that mutations in YRDC lead to extremely severe forms of GAMOS, whereas mutations in GON7, which encodes the fifth KEOPS subunit, result in milder forms of the disease [22]. The GAMOS phenotype spectrum was expanded by describing congenital hypothyroidism associated with YRDC mutations [22]. They presented 14 GAMOS-affected individuals from seven families, with mutations in GON7 (alias C14orf142) and YRDC, both of which are genes encoding proteins involved in the biosynthesis of the t6A modification. These reports indicate that individuals with GAMOS have a genetic background and clinical signs.

SRNS is usually detected in the first few months after birth and rapidly progresses to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) within a few months; however, in rare cases where adult kidney function is preserved, the range of renal lesions ranges from minor lesions to potentially collapsed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) or diffuse mesangial sclerosis (DMS). The results of brain imaging examinations include brain atrophy and cerebellar atrophy, as well as defects in the gyrus and/or myelin sheath formation. These abnormalities are associated with neurological deficits, such as psychomotor disorders, hypotonia, epileptic seizures, and even rarer sensorineural blindness and deafness. Affected children may also exhibit facial and/or skeletal deformities. The prognosis of GAMOS is poor, with most affected children dying before the age of 6 [5].

The renal clinical signs of GAMOS mainly reflect the expression of OSGEP, WDR73, NUP107, WDR4, NUP133, and YRDC in glomerular podocytes. The renal lesions associated with GAMOS syndrome are considered to be the result of glomerular injury. Mollet et al. used antibodies against TP53RK in rat kidney sections and reported that TP53RK colocalized with the DNA repair protein PARP1 in the glomeruli. The colocalization of OSGEP and PARP1 has been observed in human podocyte lines [5]. Podocyte loss has been described as an important pathological mechanism of glomerulosclerosis [54]. Therefore, in individuals with gene mutations encoding the KEOPS subunit, the induction of the telomere-related DNA damage response and increased apoptosis may be indispensable parts of the pathogenesis of GAMOS. Proteinuria is common in kidney disease, sometimes even within the scope of kidney disease, reflecting glomerular involvement. Most patients with GAMOS are diagnosed during childhood. When patients are diagnosed in adulthood, this usually occurs when they are referred to a nephrologist for treatment of idiopathic CKD. Jinks reported ‘striped fibrosis’ and tubular atrophy resembling that caused by calcineurin inhibitors, cyclosporine and tacrolimus [55], although GAMOS is a glomerular disease.

Microcephaly, a feature common to various syndromes of defective DDR [56], is likely due to increased apoptosis of neuronal progenitor cells. Microcephaly is a common feature of various defective telomere-related DNA damage response syndromes [53].This may be caused by increased apoptosis of neuronal progenitor cells. In the human cerebellum, intensive cell proliferation occurs within the external granular layer between the 28th and 34th gestational weeks and is still active by the fifth postnatal month [57]. The number of mature granule cells exceeds 50 billion, accounting for 75% of the neurons in the human brain. The hypoplasia and degeneration of cerebellar tissue observed in NCS reflect massive aplasia and/or depletion of this cerebellar granule cell population and its associated neuropil due to reduced proliferation, accelerated death, or both [58]. Selective depletion of striatal cholinergic interneurons alters signaling through cortico-basal-thalamic circuits to produce an array of extrapyramidal movements (i.e., dystonia, chorea, restlessness) (Table 1).

It is recommended to screen for genetic mutations in all patients with severe renal cerebellar syndrome, regardless of any other clinical features. Reverse phenotype analysis can be described as an inverted diagnostic workflow, starting from genetic screening and ending with clinical diagnosis. It can help clinical doctors review patients’ phenotypes and make correct diagnoses. Reverse phenotype analysis helps identify several misdiagnosed cases of GAMOS and expands the scope of atypical GAMOS phenotypes. This process may result in more unexpected outcomes in the future.

Like many other genetic diseases, the clinical management of GAMOS patients focuses on symptoms rather than any specific treatment method. The main purpose of drug intervention is to reduce proteinuria and neurological symptoms. The treatment plans described thus far require further research, especially considering the rarity of this disease, and most of the data available to us are obtained from retrospective studies. In addition, we must consider that we face a vulnerable population in terms of age and clinical presentation, where treatment options must be fully evaluated to avoid the adverse side effects already described.

Currently, there is no doubt that the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of GAMOS hinders its timely identification, and genetic screening is the only tool that can establish a diagnosis. For children with glomerular signs (such as SRNS or idiopathic FSGS) or neurological symptoms, GAMOS should be considered in differential diagnosis.
