Abstract
The indispensable roles of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), including chondroitin sulfate, dermatan sulfate, and heparan sulfate, 
have been demonstrated in various biological events such as construction of the extracellular matrix, tissue development, and cell 
signaling through interactions with extracellular matrix components, morphogens, and growth factors by analyses using the follow -
ing model organisms: nematodes, fruit ﬂies, frogs, zebraﬁsh, and mice. A large number of human genetic diseases including heart 
defects, immune deﬁciencies, and neurological abnormalities in addition to connective tissue diseases such as hereditary multiple 
exostoses and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome were recently reported to be caused by mutations in the genes encoding glycosyltransferases, 
epimerases, and sulfotransferases that are responsible for the biosynthesis of GAGs. Glycobiological approaches revealed that muta -
tions in GAG-biosynthetic enzymes led to reductions in their enzymatic activities as well as in the levels of GAGs. This review pro -
vides an overview of the growing number of glycobiological studies on recently characterized genetic disorders caused by the faulty 
biosynthesis of GAGs.
A. Introduction
Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), including chondroitin sulfate 
(CS), dermatan sulfate (DS), and heparan sulfate (HS), are linear 
polysaccharides and are covalently attached to the core proteins 
that form proteoglycans (PGs), which are ubiquitously distributed 
in the extracellular matrix and on the cell surface (1–6). GAGs are 
crucially involved in a wide range of biological processes includ -
ing cell adhesion, the regulation of cellular signaling, and assembly 
of the extracellular matrix (1–6). A number of functional analyses 
using cells from various tissues and model organisms, such as 
nematodes, fruit flies, zebrafish, chicks, and mice, revealed the 
biological signiﬁcance of GAG chains, which are essential for de-
velopment as well as the retention of biological activity (5, 7–9). 
In the late 1990 s, achondrogenesis and spondyloepimetaphyseal 
dysplasia were shown to be caused by mutations in the sulfate 
transporter (diastrophic dysplasia sulfate transporter, DTDST) and 
3/uni2032-phosphoadenosine 5/uni2032-phosphosulfate synthase-2 (PAPSS2), re-
spectively (12, 13). In addition, mutations in B4GALT7 and EXT1 
as well as EXT2, which encode the β4galactosyltransferase-7 and 
HS polymerases (exostosin-1 and -2, respectively) responsible 
for the biosynthesis of GAGs, caused Ehlers–Danlos syndrome 
(EDS) progeroid form and hereditary multiple exostoses, respec-
tively (14–18). Advances in genome analyses since 2000 revealed 
that hereditary disorders are caused by mutations in genes cod-
ing GAG-biosynthetic enzymes including glycosyltransferases, 
epimerases, and sulfotransferases (9–11). This review provides an 
overview of genetic diseases, including bone, skin, heart, immune, 
and neurological abnormalities, caused by disturbances in the bio-
synthesis of GAGs
B. Biosynthetic Pathways of CS, DS, and HS
CS chains consist of alternating disaccharide units compris-
ing N-acetyl- D-galactosamine (GalNAc) and D-glucuronic acid 
(GlcUA) residues with 100–200 repeats (Fig. 1A) (1). DS is a ste-
reoisomer of CS and is composed of GalNAc and L-iduronic acid 
(IdoUA) instead of GlcUA (Fig. 1A) (1). CS and DS are often dis-
tributed as CS-DS hybrid chains in mammalian cells. HS consists 
of 
D-glucosamine (GlcN) and GlcUA or IdoUA (Fig. 1B) (1). CS, 
DS, and HS chains are modiﬁed by sulfation at various hydroxy 
as well as amino group positions during the biosynthetic process, 
resulting in structural diversity, which plays an important role in a 
wide range of biological processes through interactions with vari-
ous proteins (3, 5, 6). These GAG chains are covalently attached to 
speciﬁc core proteins through the common linker region tetrasac -
charide, (xylose-galactose-galactose-glucuronic acid: Xyl-Gal-Gal-
GlcUA) (Fig. 1, linker region tetrasaccharide) (1).
The biosynthesis of the linker region tetrasaccharide is initiat -
ed by the transfer of a Xyl residue from uridine diphosphate (UDP) 
–Xyl to the serine residue on the speciﬁc core proteins of PGs by 
β-xylosyltransferase (XylT) (Fig. 1A, Table 1) (19, 20). β 1,4-Ga-
lactosyltransferase-I (GalT-I), β 1,3-galactosyltransferase-II (GalT-
II), and β1,3-glucuronosyltransferase-I (GlcAT-I) then sequentially 
transfer two Gals and a GlcUA from UDP-Gal and UDP-GlcUA to 
serine-O-Xyl in the core proteins (Fig. 1A, B) (15, 16, 21, 22).

C. Proteoglycan Linkeropathy Caused by Defects in 
the Biosynthesis of the GAG-Linker Region Tetrasac -
charide (Table 1)
Mutations in genes encoding glycosyltransferases, which 
are responsible for the biosynthesis of the common linker region 
tetrasaccharide, cause the hereditary disorder, “Proteoglycan link-
eropathy”, which is characterized by bone, skin, connective tissue, 
and heart defects (Table 1). Proteoglycan linkeropathy, which was 
proposed by Dr. Shiro IKEGAWA (Riken), is a collective term 
for diverse connective tissue disorders caused by mutations in the 
glycosyltransferases responsible for the biosynthesis of the linker 
region (41).
C-1. XYLT1 Mutations in Desbuquois Dysplasia Type 2
XYLT1 transfers a Xyl residue from UDP-Xyl to the serine 
residue on the specific core proteins of PGs (19). Desbuquois 
dysplasia type 2 is caused by single nucleotide substitutions, de-
letions, and duplications in XYLT1 (42–45), which result in 1) 
missense mutations (p.Arg551Cys and p.Arg598Cys); 2) a non-
sense mutation (p.Arg147 *); 3) frame shifts (p.Pro93Alafs *69, 
p.Val724Serfs*10, and p.R598Afs *7), in which “fs *69 represents 
the stop codon of the 69th reading frame, in the open reading

C-3. B4GALT7/GalT-I Mutations in Spondylodysplas -
tic EDS and Larsen Syndrome Reunion Island Type
EDS is a heterogenous group of heritable connective tissue 
disorders characterized by skin hyperextensibility, joint hypermo-
bility, and tissue fragility. EDS was previously classiﬁed into seven 
types: the classical type, hypermobility type, vascular type, kypho-
scoliosis type, arthrochalasia type, dermatospraxis type, and other 
types, which are caused by mutations in the genes coding collagen 
α-chains and its modifying enzymes characterized by biochemical 
as well as genetic analyses (54). Recent studies reported various 
new types of EDS including GAG-deﬁciency types (55, 56).
GalT-I transfers a second Gal residue in the linker region tet -
rasaccharide, Xyl-Gal-Gal-GlcUA, from UDP-Gal to the serine-
O-Xyl (Fig. 1A) (15, 16). A compound heterozygous mutation 
(p.Leu206Pro/p.Ala186Asp) in B4GALT7 encoding GalT-I causes 
EDS-progeroid type 1 (15, 16). These patients show various clini -
cal symptoms including an aged appearance, craniofacial dysmor -
phism, hypermobile joints, generalized osteopenia, a short stature, 
hypotonic muscles, and defective wound healing (15). Skin ﬁbro-
blasts of a patient (p.Leu206Pro/p.Ala186Asp) were shown to be 
defective for DS (14). Furthermore, patients with homozygous mu-
tations (p.Arg270Cys and pGlu277 *) or compound heterozygous 
mutations (p.Arg270Cys/p.Leu41Pro) exhibited a short stature and 
muscle hypotonia in addition to EDS-like phenotypes without the 
progeroid facial appearance (57, 58). Fibroblasts from a patient 
with a homozygous mutation in B4GALT7, p.Arg270Cys, exhib-
ited marked reductions in GalT-I activity in vitro and a decrease 
in the content of IdoUA, resulting in an increase in the content of 
GlcUA in the side chains of DS-PGs, decorin and biglycan (59). 
Moreover, ﬁbroblasts from the patient showed reduced levels of 
the sulfation of HS chains and the retardation of wound closure 
in vitro (60). Therefore, various clinical manifestations in EDS-
progeroid type 1 may be partially caused by changes in not only 
DS, but also CS and HS.
Salter et al. reported two affected individuals with com-
pound heterozygous mutations, p.His93Profs *73/p.Cys214Tyr 
or p.Arg270Cys/p.Arg141Trp, in B4GALT7 (61). These patients 
exhibited distinct clinical symptoms including a short stature, joint 
hypermobility, radioulnar synostosis, and severe hypermetropia, 
but not progeroid features or skin hyperextension (61). Arun -
rut et al. reported that a patient with the homozygous mutation, 
p.Cys324Ser, presented with global developmental delays, scolio-
sis, and prominent joint laxity with soft skin (62). This patient also 
showed various phenotypes including triangular face, a prominent 
forehead, proptosis, a small nose, small jaw, and ocular abnor -
malities such as corneal clouding, colobomas of the iris and optic 
nerve, and posterior subcapsular cataracts. Colobomas were a nov-
el phenotypic ﬁnding, indicating a different genetic etiology in this 
patient. The reasons why these patients with the same B4GALT7  
gene mutations showed distinct manifestations may be due to the 
different mutational positions in B4GALT7, which may result in a 
difference in the diversity of the inﬂuence of enzymatic activity or 
intracellular localization of the mutant B4GALT7, a change in the 
GAGs produced by the mutant enzymes, and their manifestations. 
EDS caused by mutations in B4GALT7 was classiﬁed into “Spon-
dylodysplastic EDS” in the 2017 International Classiﬁcation of the 
Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (56).
Larsen syndrome, Reunion Island type, is caused by the 
homozygous mutation, p.Arg270Cys, in B4GALT7 (63). The 
characteristic manifestations of Larsen syndrome are congenital 
large-joint dislocations including the hip, elbow, and knee, cranio-
facial abnormalities, and foot deformities (64). Patients exhibited 
similar phenotypes to EDS, caused by a mutation in B4GALT7  
(p.Arg270Cys), such as hyperlaxity, distinctive facial features, 
and dwarﬁsm in addition to the classical manifestations of Lasen 
syndrome (63), indicating that spondylodysplastic EDS and Larsen 
syndrome, Reunion Island type, share clinical spectra. Although 
the reason why the two disorders are caused by the same mutation, 
p.Arg270Cys, in B4GALT7  currently remains unclear, it may be 
due to another genetic etiology of the Reunion Island.

GlcAT-I transfers the 4th GlcUA residue in the linker region 
tetrasaccharide, Xyl-Gal-Gal-GlcUA, from UDP-GlcUA to the 
serine-O-Xyl-Gal-Gal (Fig. 1A) (22). A previous study reported

in B3GAT3 encoding GlcAT-I exhibited elbow dislocation with 
a bicuspid aortic valve in the heart as well as the characteristic 
manifestations of Larsen syndrome (70). von Oettingen et al.  
also described a patient from a second family with the homozy-
gous mutation, p.Arg277Gln, in the B3GAT3 gene (71). Probands 
with another homozygous mutation, p.Pro140Leu, in B3GAT3  
presented with skeletal dysplasia including a short stature, ky-
phoscoliosis, and joint dislocation, but no heart phenotype in the 
family (72). GlcUA-transferase activities as well as the levels of 
CS, DS, and HS in ﬁbroblasts from these patients, p.Arg277Gln 
and p.Pro140Leu, were signiﬁcantly lower than those in the ﬁbro-
blasts of healthy controls (70, 72). Furthermore, a proband with 
the compound heterozygous mutation, p.Met1?/p.Leu224Gln, in 
B3GAT3 presented with a severe phenotype of osteoporosis, hy-
potonia, joint laxity, fractures, scoliosis, a biscuspid aortic valve, 
and myopia (73). The mutation of the start codon, c.A>G led to 
the initiation of other ATGs in B3GAT3, resulting in a frame shift 
and the production of unrelated proteins (73). Thus, this muta-
tion is considered to produce an abnormal B3GAT3 protein and 
be functionally null. The recombinant GlcAT-I-p.Leu224Gln and

weaker GlcUA-transferase activity than that in the controls (73). 
Furthermore, the levels of CS and DS were lower in the patient’s 
ﬁbroblasts than that in normal ﬁbroblasts (73).
Alazami et al. found that the homozygous mutation, 
p.Pro82Leu, in B3GAT3 caused diverse manifestations such as 
geroderma osteodysplasticum, which is characterized by cutis laxa 
and osteoporosis, SEMD, fractures, multiple bony chondromas, 
and a short stature (69). Jones et al. reported a proband with the 
homozygous mutation, p.Gly223Ser, in B3GAT3 who presented 
with multiple fractures, blue sclerae, and glaucoma (74). Further -
more, the patient exhibited bilateral radio-ulnar synostosis, severe 
osteopenia, bilateral clubfeet, atrial and ventricular septal defects, 
hypertelorism, a small chest, diaphragmatic hernia, arachnodac-
tyly, hypotonia, hearing loss, and perinatal cerebral infarction 
with bilateral supra- and infratentorial subdural hematomas (74).

fractures, a diaphragmatic hernia, and small thorax have not 
been previously reported in patients with a mutation in B3GAT3 
(p.Met1?/p.Leu224Gln, p.Pro82Leu, p.Pro140Leu, p.Leu224Gln, 
and p.Arg277Gln). Yauy et al. also described six probands in 
four unrelated consanguineous families with the same mutation, 
p.Gly223Ser, in B3GAT3 (75). They presented a number of mani -
festations including craniosynostosis, bone fragility, radioulnar 
or radiohumeral synostosis, midface hypoplasia, and joint con-
tracture (75). These manifestations are similar to those of Antley–
Bixler syndrome and Shprintzen–Goldberg syndrome, which are 
characterized by radioulnar or radiohumeral synostosis and cra-
niosynostosis in the prenatal and postnatal periods, respectively 
(75). Yauy et al. proposed the classiﬁcation for B3GAT3-related

patients with different mutations in B3GAT3 (75): 1) “B3GAT3-
related disorder with joint dislocation and congenital heart defect” 
(p.Pro140Leu and p.Arg277Gln), which is similar to Larsen syn-
drome; 2) “B3GAT3-related disorder with craniosynostosis and 
bone fragility” (p.Gly223Ser), which is similar to Antley–Bixler

mutation, p.Met1?/p.Leu224Gln), which is similar to Larsen syn-
drome and Antley–Bixler/Shprintzen–Goldberg syndromes (75).

clinical symptoms in B3GAT3-related disorders, which have been 
recognized as differential diagnoses such as Larsen, Antley–Bixler, 
Shprintzen–Goldberg, and geroderma osteodysplastica syndromes.

levels of PG as well as GAGs, and signal transduction involved in 
the differentiation of chondrocytes and bone development using 
the cells patients with four types of mutations in B3GAT3 are re-
quired in order to obtain a deeper understanding of the underlying 
pathogenic mechanisms.

Dysplasia with Congenital Joint Dislocations, Larsen

Chondroitin 6-O -sulfotransferase-1 (C6ST1) transfers a sul-
fate group from 3/uni2032-phosphoadenosine 5/uni2032-phosphosulfate (PAPS) 
to the C-6 hydroxy group of GalNAc residues in CS chains (Fig. 
2) (85, 86). Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia Omani type is caused 
by any of following homozygous mutations: p.Arg304Gln, 
p.Leu286Pro, and p.Thr141Met, in CHST3 encoding C6ST1 (87–
89). Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia Omani type is characterized by 
a short stature, severe kyphoscoliosis, mild brachydactyly, fusion 
of the carpal bones, metacarpal shortening, osteoarthritis in the el-
bow, wrist, and knee joints, deafness, and ventricular septal, mitral, 
and/or tricuspid defects (87–90). Skin ﬁbroblasts from two patients 
with the mutations, p.Arg304Gln or p.Leu286Pro, exhibited the 
loss of C6ST activity, which resulted in a defect in 6-O -sulfated 
CS chains (87, 88). Furthermore, no 6-O-sulfated CS chains were 
observed in the urine of patients (87, 88). These ﬁndings indicate 
that the 6- O-sulfation of CS side chains on PGs is required for 
the normal development of the heart and speciﬁc bones, and that 
chondroitin 4-O -sulfotransferase (C4ST) and/or the 4-O -sulfation 
of CS do not compensate for the loss-of-function in C6ST1. The 
identiﬁcation of the core protein with 6-O-sulfated CS chain(s) as 
well as the bone morphogenetic factors interacting with CS chains 
is necessary for a clearer understanding of the underlying patho-
genic mechanisms. To date, 20 probands of spondyloepiphyseal

D-4. 6q25.1 Deletion Including UST  in Heart Defects, 
Underdeveloped Cerebellar Vermis, Abnormal Cutane -

Uronyl 2-O -sulfotransferase (UST) transfers a sulfate group 
from PAPS to the C-2 hydroxyl group of GlcUA and IdoUA resi-
dues in CS and DS chains, respectively (Fig. 2) (98, 99). A hetero-
zygous deletion of 0.63 Mb in chromosome 6q25.1 causes multiple 
congenital anomalies including pre- and postnatal growth failure, 
mild dysmorphic facial features, abnormal cutaneous elasticity, 
joint laxity, a ventricular septal defect, and an underdeveloped 
cerebellar vermis (100). The deletion region on the chromosome 
of the patient contained eight proteins encoded by UST, TAB2, 
SUMO4, ZC3H12D, PPIL4, KATNA1, and LATS1. TAB2 and 
LATS1 are necessary for normal cardiac development and growth, 
respectively (101, 102). Hence, manifestations such as heart de -
fects and growth failures in the patient may be caused by the dele -
tion of TAB2 and LATS1, respectively. Furthermore, the patient 
exhibited clinical symptoms including joint laxity as well as abnor-
mal cutaneous elasticity, which are characteristic features of EDS. 
Thus, EDS-like features may be caused by the heterozygous dele -
tion of UST encoding the sulfotransferase responsible for the bio-
synthesis of CS and DS. However, the possibility of the etiology 
being caused by deletion of ﬁve other genes cannot be excluded.

synthesis of the DS-Disaccharide Region  (Table 2)
E-1. DSE Mutations in EDS Musculocontractural 
Type 2
Dermatan sulfate epimerase (DSE) converts GlcUA into Id-
oUA by epimerizing the C-5 carboxy group of GlcUA residues 
in the repeating disaccharide region of the chondroitin precursor 
chain, [GlcUA-GalNAc] n, resulting in the formation of the di-
saccharide region of DS, [IdoUA-GalNAc] n (Fig. 1A) (29). The 
homozygous mutation, p.Ser268Leu, in DSE causes EDS musculo-
contractural type 2 (Table 2) (103). Syx et al. also reported the two 
cases of EDS musculocontractural type 2 caused by the mutation, 
p.Arg267Gly, in DSE (104). The clinical features of these patients 
showed characteristic facial features, contracture of the thumbs 
and feet, hypermobility of the ﬁnger, elbow, and knee joints, and 
atrophic scars on skin (103). The epimerase activity of recombi -
nant p.Ser268Leu-DSE was markedly weaker than that of wild-
type DSE (103). Signiﬁcant decreases in epimerase activity as well 
as the level of DS were also observed in ﬁbroblasts from a patient 
with the mutation, p.Ser268Leu, in DSE, and were accompanied 

© 2018 FCCA (Forum: Carbohydrates Coming of Age)E79
by an increase in the level of CS, compared with those in the ﬁ-
broblasts of healthy subjects (103). These ﬁndings suggest that a 
mutation in DSE may cause a decrease in DS, resulting in EDS 
musculocontractural type 2. Further analyses including the identi -
ﬁcation of the core proteins of DS-PGs, signaling pathway(s), and 
molecules interacting with DS chains will be required in order to 
elucidate the underlying pathogenic mechanism.
E-2. DSEL Mutations and SNPs in Bipolar Disorder 
and Diaphragmatic Hernia
DSE2, encoded by dermatan sulfate epimerase-like (DSEL), 
similar to DSE, converts GlcUA into IdoUA (30). Although DSE2 
contains a predicted sulfotransferase domain at the carboxy ter -
minus in addition to the epimerase motif, similar to DSE, there 
have been no studies on the sulfotransferase activity of DSE2 (30). 
Various SNPs, −546 A>G (a substitution of adenine into guanine 
in the 5/uni2032-non-coding region 546 bp upstream of the coding re -
gion), p.Val287Ile, p.Pro673Ser, p.Tyr730Cys, p.Pro942Ser, and 
p.Ile1113Met, in DSEL have been identiﬁed among the 113 pro-
bands with bipolar disorder characterized by alternating episodes 
of depression (105). In addition, Shi et al. reported that 20 recur -
rent early-onset major depressive disorder-related SNPs were iden-
tiﬁed by a genome-wide association study using 1020 case subjects 
with recurrent early-onset major depressive disorder and 1636 
controls (106). The SNP, rs17077540, which is the most closely as-
sociated with the disease, was found 75 kb upstream of the DSEL 
gene (106). Zayed et al. reported probands with diaphragmatic 
hernia caused by the heterozygous mutation, p.Met14Ile, in DSEL 
(107). These ﬁndings indicate that DSEL and/or DS chains con-
structed by DSEL are needed for the maintenance of the nervous 
system and development of the diaphragm. Further analyses are 
required because the functions of DSEL in these tissues currently 
remain unclear.
E-3. CHST14/D4ST1 Mutations in EDS Musculocon -
tractural Type 1
Dermatan 4-O -sulfotransferase-1 (D4ST1) transfers a sulfate 
group from PAPS to the C-4 hydroxy group of GalNAc residues 
in DS chains (Fig. 2) (108, 109). Mutations in CHST14 encoding 
D4ST1 cause EDS musculocontractural type 1 (Table 2) (110–
112). Kosho et al. described six unrelated Japanese probands with 
EDS characterized by characteristic craniofacial features, multiple 
congenital contracture of the joints, progressive skin and joint 
laxities, recurrent dislocations, spinal deformities, and progres-
sive multisystem fragility (113, 114). The homozygous mutation, 
p.Pro281Leu, or compound heterozygous mutations, p.Lys69 */
p.Pro281Leu, p.Pro281Leu/p.Cys289Ser, and p.Pro281Leu/
p.Tyr293Cys, have been identiﬁed in these patients (110). Not only 
the recombinant CHST14 mutants, p.Pro281Leu, p.Cys289Ser, and 
p.Tyr293Cys, but also ﬁbroblasts from patients with the mutations, 
p.Pro281Leu or p.Pro281Leu/p.Tyr293Cys, showed markedly 
weaker D4ST activity than that of the respective control (110). CS 
side chains, but not non-sulfated DS (dermatan), was generated 
on the decorin of skin ﬁbroblasts from probands (110). A previ-
ous study demonstrated that DSE did not act on the uronic acids 
ﬂanked by 4-O-sulfated GalNAc in CS and DS chains, and that the 
enzymatic activity of DSE for converting IdoUA to GlcUA was 
stronger than that for forward epimerization, GlcUA to IdoUA, in 
vitro (115). Hence, the defect in 4-O-sulfation in DS caused by the 
loss-of-function of D4ST1 in patients allows for a back-epimer -
ization reaction converting IdoUA to GlcUA in dermatan in order 
to form chondroitin by DSE, followed by the 4-O -sulfation of 
GalNAc residues in chondroitin by C4ST. Although regularly and 
tightly assembled collagen fibrils were observed in the reticular 
dermis of normal subjects in an analysis with electron microscopy, 
the collagen ﬁbrils of patients were dispersed (110). These ﬁndings 
imply that mutations in CHST14 causes distinct EDS with a defect 
in the assembly of collagen ﬁbrils mediated by decorin-PG with 
CS side chains.
Dünder et al. showed that the homozygous mutations,

blasts of a patient (p.Arg213Pro) than that those of control subjects 
(111).

hyperextensible, thin and bruisable skin, atrophic scarring, joint

F-1. EXT1 and EXT2 Mutations in Multiple Osteo -
chondromas (Exostoses)
Hereditary multiple exostoses are autosomal dominant dis-
orders that are characterized by cartilaginous capped bony out -
growths (osteochondromas) located at the growth plates of long 
bones, such as femora, and malignant transformation in approxi-
mately 1% of patients (126–129). Furthermore, probands presented 
with clinical manifestations such as a short stature, camptomelic 
dysplasia including the forearm and crus, and early-onset osteoar -
thritis (126–129). The term osteochondromas is now used instead 
of exostoses because these lesions are caused by cartilaginous 
processes, including ossiﬁcation, and not the outgrowth of bone. 
Hereditary multiple osteochondromas were found to be caused 
by mutations in EXT1 or EXT2 in 1995 and 1996, respectively 
(17, 18). Furthermore, EXT1 and EXT2 were shown to encode the 
glycosyltransferase responsible for HS biosynthesis in 1998 (31, 
32). The hetero-dimeric complex of EXT1 and EXT2 exhibits the 
alternating addition of GlcUA and GlcNAc from UDP-GlcUA 
and UDP-GlcNAc, respectively, on the linkage region tetrasac-
charide acceptor in vitro, which results in the biosynthesis of the 
HS-repeating disaccharide region (Fig. 1B) (33). A total of 436 
and 223 mutations in EXT1 and EXT2, respectively, have been 
identified to date (http://medgen.ua.ac.be/LOVDv.2.0/home.php) 
(130–139). There have been 75 Japanease patients with mutations 
in EXT1 or EXT2 (140, 141).
Langer–Giedion syndrome (trichorhinophalangeal syndrome 
type II) is an autosomal dominant disorders caused by a deletion 
in chromosome 8q23.3-q24.11, which contains EXT1 and TRPS1  
(142). Langer–Giedion syndrome is characterized by multiple os-
teochondromas, a short stature, coned-shaped epiphyses, a bulbous 
nose, long philtrum, sparse scalp hair, and bushy eyebrows (142). 
A deletion in chromosome 8q24.11 or 8q23.3-q24.13, which in-
clude EXT1, but not TRPS1, also causes Langer–Giedion syndrome 
(143–145). Other deletions in genes in both regions may also cause 
the syndrome, except for osteochondromas.
A heterozygous deletion of 2.3 Mb in chromosome 11p11.21 
containing EXT2 causes Potocki–Shaffer-Syndrome, which is 
characterized by craniofacial abnormalities, developmental delays, 
intellectual disability, multiple osteochondromas, and biparietal 
foramina (146, 147). These manifestations, except for multiple os-
teochondromas, may be caused by eight other genes contained in 
the chromosome 11p11.21.
F-2. EXT2 Mutations in Seizures–Scoliosis–Macro -
cephaly Syndrome
The complex homozygous mutations, p.Met87Arg and 
p.Arg95Cys, in EXT2 cause seizures–scoliosis–macrocephaly 
syndrome, but not multiple osteochondromas (148). Probands 
exhibited various clinical features including seizures, intellectual 
disability, hypotonia, scoliosis, macrocephaly, hypertelorism, and 
renal dysfunction (148). The protein level of the mutant EXT2, 
p.Met87Arg/p.Arg95Cys, in fibroblasts from patients was sig-
nificantly lower than that from control subjects (148). Although 
the mRNA level of GlcNAc- N-deacetylase/N-sulfotransferase  
(NDST1) was normal, the protein level of NDST1, which inter -
acts with EXT2 (38), was also abolished in the cells of patients 
(148). These findings suggest that complex homozygous muta-
tions in EXT2 (p.Met87Arg and p.Arg95Cys) cause dysfunctions 
in NDST1. Mutations in NDST1 cause autosomal recessive intel -
lectual disability (see section F-4). Hence, the clinical features of 
patients with seizures–scoliosis–macrocephaly syndrome caused 
by mutations in EXT2 (p.Met87Arg and p.Arg95Cys) appear to be 
dependent on a dysfunction and/or reductions in NDST1. Further 
studies on the glycosyltransferase activity of EXT2, sulfotrans-
ferase activity of NDST1, and level of HS chains using the cells 
of patients may provide a clearer understanding of the molecular 
mechanisms responsible for seizures–scoliosis–macrocephaly syn-
drome.
F-3. EXTL3 Mutations in Neuro-Immuno-Skeletal 
Dysplasia Syndrome
The protein encoded by exostosin-like-3 (EXTL3) exhibits 
dual glycosyltransferase activities, GlcNAcT-I and GlcNAcT-II, 
which transfer a GlcNAc residue from UDP-GlcNAc to the GlcUA 
residue at the non-reducing end of the common GAG-linker region 

© 2018 FCCA (Forum: Carbohydrates Coming of Age)E81
tetrasaccharide and to the GlcUA residue in the disaccharide region 
of HS chains, respectively (Fig. 1B, Table 3) (35). The homozy-
gous mutations, p.Pro461Leu, p.Arg513Cys, p.Asn657Ser, and 
p.Tyr670Asp, in EXTL3 cause neuro-immuno-skeletal dysplasia 
syndrome (149). The clinical features of patients include severe 
combined immunodeﬁciency with a complete absence of T cells, 
severe platyspondyly, kyphoscoliosis, brachydactyly, and intel-
lectual disability (149). A patient with the mutation, p.Pro461Leu, 
presented with skeletal abnormalities, but not immunodeﬁciency 
or intellectual disability (149). These findings indicate that the 
mutation, p.Pro461Leu, does not affect the nervous or immune 
system. Although wild-type and p.Tyr670Asp-EXTL3 proteins 
from a healthy subject and the patient, respectively, localized to the 
Golgi, the p.Arg513Cys-EXTL3 protein from another patient did 
not (149). Hence, the mislocalization of the mutant, p.Arg513Cys-
EXTL3, may result in the loss-of-function of EXTL3. Further -
more, HS levels in the ﬁbroblasts of the patients, p.Arg513Cys, 
p.Asn657Ser, and p.Tyr670Asp, except for the affected individual, 
p.Pro461Leu, were markedly lower than those in control fibro-
blasts (149). HS levels in the serum and urine of the affected 
individual, p.Tyr670Asp, were also decreased and accompanied 
by an increase in CS and DS levels (149). On the other hand, HS 
concentrations were normal in the serum and urine of the affected 
individual, p.Arg513Cys (149). These ﬁndings suggest that muta -
tions in EXTL3 cause a decrease in HS and increase in CS and DS, 
which may result in neuro-immuno-skeletal dysplasia syndrome.
V olpi et al. also reported that probands with the homozygous 
mutations, p.Arg339Trp and p.Pro461Leu, in EXTL3, exhibited 
severe skeletal dysplasia, immune deficiency, and developmen-
tal delay (150). Their clinical features at birth were short-limb 
skeletal dysplasia, a severe T cell immunodeﬁciency, generalized 
platyspondyly with an increased intervertebral space, short and 
plump limb bones, metacarpals, and phalanges, premature cra-
niosynostosis, neurological abnormalities including generalized 
seizures, opisthotonus, and hyperreflexia, developmental delay, 
nystagmus, and muscular hypotonia (150). Increases in the 2-O - 
and 6-O-sulfation of HS and the up-regulation of ﬁbroblast growth 
factor signaling were observed in the fibroblasts of the patient, 
p.Arg339Trp, over those in normal ﬁbroblasts (150). The induced 
pluripotent stem (iPS) cells of the patient, p.Arg339Trp, showed 
the reduced expansion of lymphohematopoietic progenitor cells 
and defects in thymic epithelial progenitor cell differentiation 
(150). Thus, these ﬁndings imply that the mutation in EXTL3 and/
or an aberrant sulfation pattern in HS chains inhibit T cell differen-
tiation, leading to an immune deﬁciency.
Guo et al. reported that patients with the homozygous muta -
tion, p.Pro318Leu, in EXTL3 presented with a novel type of SEMD 
(151). The clinical features of the patients were severe platyspon-
dyly, kyphoscoliosis, pelvic distortion, constriction of the proxi-
mal femora and brachydactyly, and a decrease in T-lymphocyte 
subsets as well as immunoglobulins. The recombinant mutant 
p.Pro318Leu-EXTL3 showed a marked reduction in GlcNAcT-II 
activity from that of wild-type-EXTL3 (151). These ﬁndings indi-
cate that the decrease in glycosyltransferase activity caused by the 
mutation, p.Pro318Leu, in EXTL3 results in defects in the biosyn-
thesis of HS, which may lead to SEMD.
These three studies imply that EXTL3, HS chains, and/or 
HS-PGs play roles in not only skeletal development, but also cell 
differentiation and the development of the immune and nervous 
systems. The roles of EXTL3 and HS as well as HS-PGs synthe-
sized by EXTL3 in the immune and nervous systems currently 
remain unclear. Hence, further studies using the cells of patients 
and knockout mice are needed in order to elucidate the underlying 
pathogenic mechanisms.
F-4. NDST1 Mutations Lead to Intellectual Disability 
and Developmental Delay
NDST1 has bifunctional domains including an N-deacetylase 
and N-sulfotransferase, which act on the GlcNAc residues in HS 
chains (152, 153). Following the formation of the repeating disac-
charide region, [GlcNAc-GlcUA] n, of HS by EXT and EXTL 
proteins, the ﬁrst modiﬁcation is the hydrolytic release of acetyl-
groups in GlcNAc by the N -deacetylase domain of NDST1, fol -
lowed by the transfer of sulfate groups to the resultant GlcN resi-
dues from PAPS by the N-sulfotransferase domain of NDSTs (Fig. 
2) (152). The homozygous mutations, p.Arg709Gln, p.Glu642Asp, 
p.Phe640Leu, and p.Gly611Ser, in the N -sulfotransferase domain 
of NDST1 cause autosomal recessive intellectual disability (154, 
155). Probands presented with clinical features such as intellectual 
disability, muscular hypotonia, epilepsy, and postnatal growth deﬁ-
ciency (155). Furthermore, Armstrong et al. reported a patient with 
the compound heterozygous mutation, p.Ala372Ser/p.Ala736Val, 
in NDST1 who showed developmental delay, a short stature, sei-
zures, cranial nerve palsies, ataxia, severe respiratory difﬁculties 
in infancy, and distinctive facial features including a long nose 
and biﬁd uvula (156). These ﬁndings indicate that NDST1 and/or 
N-sulfation in HS chains play roles in the development as well as 
maintenance of the brain, lungs, and body. Further analyses includ-
ing the identiﬁcation of core protein(s) with HS chains, which are 
biosynthesized by NDST1, and its interacting molecules may lead 
to a clearer understanding of the underlying pathogenic mecha-
nisms.
F-5. HS6ST1 Mutations in Kallmann Syndrome and 
Idiopathic Hypogonadotrophic Hypogonadism
Heparan sulfate 6-O-sulfotransferase-1 (HS6ST1) transfers a

p.Arg313Gln, p.Arg372Trp, and p.Met394Val, cause idiopathic 
hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism and Kallmann syndrome (Table 
3) (159). Hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism is characterized by

gonadotropin-releasing hormone neuron development or function.

in addition to the manifestations of hypogonadotrophic hypogo-

tions with KAL1/Anosmin-1 and/or FGF, thereby allowing the 
regulation of their signaling pathways as well as of neurons yield -
ing the gonadotropin-releasing hormone.

The sugar donor substrate, UDP-sugar, is converted into UDP 
after a reaction with a glycosyltransferase. Mutations in CANT1  
encoding a calcium-dependent nucleoside diphosphatase that 
converts UDP into uridine 5 /uni2032-monophosphate, cause Desbuquois 
dysplasia type 1 (Table 4) (183–190). This disorder is common to 
Desbuquois dysplasia type 2 caused by mutations in XYLT1, which 
indicates that the accumulation of UDP functions as an inhibi-
tor for XYLT1, resulting in defects in the biosynthesis of GAGs 
as well as Desbuquois dysplasia. Furthermore, the sulfate donor 
substrate, PAPS, is converted into adenosine-3/uni2032,5/uni2032-bisphosphate 
(PAP) after a reaction with a sulfotransferase. Mutations in inositol 
monophosphatase domain-containing protein 1/Golgi 3/uni2032-phospho-
adenosine 5/uni2032-phosphate 3/uni2032-phosphatase (IMP AD1/GP APP) encod-

the phosphate from PAP to adenosine 5/uni2032-phosphate, cause chondro-
dysplasia with joint dislocations (Table 4) (191, 192). Hence, these 
metabolic abnormalities in the reaction products of donor sub-
strates including UDP and PAP, which may function as an inhibitor 
for glycosyltransferase as well as sulfotransferase, respectively, 
cause skeletal disorders similar to that of faulty GAGs. Further 
studies are required in order to elucidate the underlying pathogenic 
mechanisms in more detail.
H. Conclusions and Perspectives
Various manifestations, including skeletal, skin, immune, 
heart, and neuronal abnormalities, are clearly caused by mutations 
in the glycosyltransferases, epimerases, and sulfotransferases re -
sponsible for the biosynthesis of the three kinds of polysaccharide 
chains, CS, DS, and HS, which are constructed on the core proteins 
of PGs. The clinical manifestations of PG linkeropathies do not 
always agree among the distinct mutations of a glycosyltransferase 
nor the four different glycosyltransferases, XYLT1, B4GALT7, 
B3GALT6, and B3GAT3. This range of clinical features may par -
tially be due to distinct residual enzymatic activities, the cellular 
mislocalization of mutant proteins, or partial compensation for the 
loss-of-function in each glycosyltransferase by other homologue 
(s) such as β 4GalTs, β3GalTs, and β 3GlcATs family members, 
which may affect the quality of the GAGs yielded, i.e., different 
lengths, numbers, and sulfation patterns of GAGs in the affected 
individuals. Moreover, the GAG-linker region tetrasaccharide is 
modiﬁed by phosphorylation and sulfation on the Xyl and Gal resi-
dues, respectively, which may regulate the biosynthesis of GAGs 
(165–176). Fam20B and ACPL2 exhibit kinase and phosphatase 
activities at the hydroxy group C-2 position on the Xyl residue in 
vitro, respectively (Table 1) (170, 174). Hence, further studies on 
their modiﬁcations are necessary for understanding PG inkeropa-
thies in more detail.
More than 20 years ago, it was revealed that hereditary multi -
ple osteochondromas were caused by mutations in EXT1 as well as 
EXT2. Recent studies reported that neuro-immuno-skeletal dyspla-
sia syndrome and intellectual disabilities were caused by mutations 
in EXTL3 and EXT2 as well as NDST1, respectively, which lead to 
further investigations on the roles of HS and its biosynthetic en-
zymes in the nervous systems.
In order to further elucidate molecular pathogeneses and de-
velop new therapeutics for these diseases, analyses of the chain 
lengths and levels of GAGs and the core proteins of PGs in each 
patient with defects in the biosynthesis of GAGs, the functional 
proteins that interact with GAGs and are involved in the develop -
ment of cartilage and skin, the functional domains (speciﬁc sulfa-
tion sequences) in GAGs, signaling pathways, and GAGosomes 
will be required.
