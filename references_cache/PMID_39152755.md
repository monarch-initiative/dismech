Cobalamin C (Cbl-C) defect causes methylmalonic acidemia, homocystinuria,
intellectual disability and visual impairment, despite treatment adherence.
While international guidelines recommend parenteral hydroxocobalamin (OH-Cbl) as
effective treatment, dose adjustments remain unclear. We assessed OH-Cbl therapy
impact on biochemical, neurocognitive and visual outcomes in early-onset Cbl-C
patients treated with different OH-Cbl doses over 3 years. Group A (n = 5),
diagnosed via newborn screening (NBS), received high-dose OH-Cbl (median
0.55 mg/kg/day); Group B1 (n = 3), NBS-diagnosed, received low-dose OH-Cbl
(median 0.09 mg/kg/day); Group B2 (n = 12), diagnosed on clinical bases,
received low-dose OH-Cbl (median 0.06 mg/kg/day). Biochemical analyses revealed
better values of homocysteine, methionine and methylmalonic acid in Group A
compared to Group B1 (p < 0.01, p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively) and B2
(p < 0.001, p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively). Neurodevelopmental assessment
showed better outcome in Group A compared to low-dose treated Groups B1 and B2,
especially in Developmental Quotient, Hearing and Speech and Performance
subscales without significant differences between Group B2 and Group B1.
Maculopathy was detected in 100%, 66% and 83% of patients in the three groups,
respectively. This study showed that "high-dose" OH-Cbl treatment in
NBS-diagnosed children with severe early-onset Cbl-C defect led to a significant
improvement in the metabolic profile and in neurocognitive outcome, compared to
age-matched patients treated with a "low-dose" regimen. Effects on maculopathy
seem unaffected by OH-Cbl dosage. Our findings, although observed in a limited
number of patients, may contribute to improve the long-term outcome of Cbl-C
patients.

Cobalamin C (Cbl‐C) defect (OMIM #277400) is the most frequent disease involving intracellular cobalamin (vitamin B12) metabolism.
1
 Biochemically, the disease impairs the conversion of dietary cobalamin into its metabolically active forms, methyl‐cobalamin and adenosyl‐cobalamin, which act as coenzymes for methionine synthase and methylmalonyl‐CoA mutase. As a result, methionine (Met) synthesis decreases, and a combined elevation of total homocysteine (Hcy) and of methylmalonic acid (MMA) occurs in plasma and urine.

Clinically, Cbl‐C defect represents a paradigm of multisystem metabolic disease in which the central nervous system (CNS) and the eye are primarily affected. To a lesser and variable extent, other organs and tissues can be involved, with renal, cardiac, and hematological manifestations, and sometimes mild facial dysmorphisms.
1
, 
2



On the basis of the age of symptoms appearance, an early‐onset (<1 year) and a late‐onset (>1 year) form are distinguishable.
3

, 
5
 In early‐onset form, which represents the most frequent mode of appearance of the disease, symptoms manifest acutely or sub‐acutely with metabolic acidosis, anemia/cytopenia, hypotonus, lethargy, neurological deterioration, epilepsy and failure to thrive.
4
 Severe brain injury may cause cerebral atrophy, basal ganglia lesions or slowly progressive white matter changes.
6
 In most severe cases, life‐threatening manifestations may occur as atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, pulmonary hypertension and hydrocephalus.
4
, 
7

As for disease treatment, current guidelines indicate hydroxocobalamin (OH‐Cbl) and betaine as the therapeutic molecules with proven biochemical and clinical efficacy.
1
 However, beside a recommended parenteral OH‐Cbl starting dose of 1 mg/day at diagnosis, regardless of patients' age and/or weight, there is no consensus on dose adjustments throughout the course of the disease.
1
 Once OH‐Cbl therapy is initiated, patients undergo a relative clinical stabilization and do not experience further acute and life‐threatening events.
4
, 
5
 Nevertheless, in the majority of early‐onset cases with severe genotypic variants, treatment allows only a partial improvement of metabolic profile, without leading to its normalization.
5

 Overall, these reports have shown improved biomarker trends in patients treated with higher doses of OH‐Cbl. However, these reports did not provide evidence for the optimal OH‐Cbl regimen, since they were conducted on small and heterogeneous case series and used different, non‐standardized OH‐Cbl dose protocols. With the aim of providing a uniform definition of the different therapeutic regimens, in this study we have arbitrarily distinct three types of OH‐Cbl dosages: “low dose” (<0.3 mg/kg/day), “high‐dose” (≥0.30 to <2.0 mg/kg/day), and “ultra high‐dose” (≥2.0 mg/kg/day).

To answer the question whether the use higher dose OH‐Cbl therapy may allow biochemical and clinical advantages, we performed a retrospective analysis of data collected from genetically homogeneous, age‐matched early‐onset Cbl‐C patients treated with different OH‐Cbl regimens.

A case series of 20 patients affected by severe early‐onset Cbl‐C defect have been evaluated. Inclusion criteria comprise the presence of mutations in MMACHC and/or PRDX1 in individuals diagnosed by NBS or on clinical bases, presenting combined elevation of Hcy and MMA. Main clinical and genetic features of patients' sample are reported in Table 1. According to the administered OH‐Cbl dose, patients have been divided into three groups: a “high‐dose” group (Group A) and two “low‐dose” groups (Group B1 and B2). Group A consisted of five patients, all diagnosed by NBS and treated with high‐dose parenteral OH‐Cbl (median 0.55 mg/kg/day; range 0.30–0.90). Group B1 consisted of three patients diagnosed by NBS and treated with low‐dose parenteral OH‐Cbl (median 0.09 mg/kg/day; range 0.06–0.19). Group B2 consisted of 12 patients, belonging to a historical patients' group identified on clinical bases and treated with low parenteral OH‐Cbl (median 0.06 mg/kg/day; range 0.02–0.20).

All patients were also treated with betaine [Group A, median 88 mg/kg/day (range 36–153); Group B1, median 192 mg/kg/day (range 97–238); Group B2, median 162 mg/kg/day (range 78–287)], carnitine, folic/folinic acid and all were on a free diet, with no restrictions on protein intake. Genetically, 19/20 patients harbored homozygous or compound heterozygous MMACHC mutations, and 1/20 patient has a PRDX1 mutation in compound heterozygosity with a MMACHC mutation (Table 1).

For each patient, the retrospective analysis of the first months of life included the recording of clinical events, classified as major, if consisted of conditions requiring NICU admission, hemolytic uremic syndrome/acute kidney disease, hydrocephalus, metabolic acidosis, cytopenia, macrophage activation syndrome, or minor, if consisted of vomiting, hypotonia, lethargy, poor sucking and/or mild acidosis. Patients diagnosed through NBS, belonging to Groups A and B1, did not show clinical events at diagnosis, while 10/12 patients in Group B2 presented clinical events leading to diagnosis, classified as major in 7 and minor in 3 (Table 1).

During the follow up, each patient underwent periodic metabolic, clinical, neurocognitive and ophthalmological evaluations. All diagnostic procedures and pharmacological treatments involving human participants were conducted in full observation of Helsinki Declaration after obtaining written informed consent, approved by the Bambino Gesù Children's Hospital ethical committee (OPBG n. 3347/2024), from patients' parents or their legal guardians. All procedures and treatments were carried out within standard clinical practice. All retrospective data analysis were conducted on anonymized databases.

Biochemical data‐set includes periodic evaluations over 3 years of follow‐up, performed during scheduled outpatient visits. To increase uniformity among groups, a common age window corresponding to neurocognitive evaluation time points, has been considered for data collection throughout the follow‐up period. Therefore, only biochemical parameters collected within this age window were included into statistical analyses for each patient. The assessed biomarker profile in plasma included Hcy and Met, determined by HPLC, MMA and propionyl‐carnitine determined by tandem mass spectrometry.

All patients underwent an initial assessment of neurodevelopment around the age of 2 years, and were subsequently retested 1 year later. Neurodevelopment was assessed by Griffith's Mental Developmental Scales‐R (GMDS‐R), a test designed to measure the rate of development of infants and young children from birth to 8 years of age. All test required a limited administration time (60 min maximum) and were conducted by the same operators. Values are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR).

Patients were classified as presenting normal (DQ > 85) or border (DQ 70–84) developmental profile, or mild (DQ 55–69), moderate (DQ 40–54), severe (DQ < 40) and profound (untestable) developmental delay.

All patients underwent periodic comprehensive ophthalmological and orthoptic assessments every 6 months, within 3 months of the neurodevelopmental assessment. Ophthalmological assessment included anterior segment examination by slit lamp, cycloplegic refraction, and retinal examination by indirect ophthalmoscopy. In younger patients born since 2016 onwards, retinal imaging was integrated by Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), by the Envisu 2300 portable hand‐held SD‐OCT (Bioptigen Incorporated, Durham, NC). Scan size was 8 × 8 mm and scan density was 1000 A scans/100 B scans. OCT scans were included and reviewed only if the entire foveal region was visible and image quality was adequate to assess presence and severity of any retinal abnormalities. Different degrees of central retinal dystrophies were previously classified as “bull's eye” pattern. Nowadays OCT imaging allows a better definition of different dystrophies patterns.

The orthoptic assessment included ocular motility assessment, describing abnormal ocular movements such as nystagmus and strabismus whenever present, and visual acuity evaluation by means of different methods according to level of collaboration (Durand Acuity Cards, Teller Acuity Cards and LEA Symbols).

All statistical analyses and graphs were performed using Graph‐Pad Prism 10 (Graph‐Pad software Inc., San Diego, CA). Demographic data and biochemical parameters were analyzed using descriptive statistics. For this study no formal power calculation was made. Mean ± SD or median with range (minimum‐maximum value) were used for normally distributed and not‐normally distributed measurements, respectively. Kruskal–Wallis was used as nonparametric test to compare more than three groups followed by Dunn's Multiple Comparison as post‐test. Spearman correlation r was used to correlate nonparametric variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed in order to explore the relationship among continuous variable (Hcy, Met, MMA, propionyl‐carnitine, OH‐Cbl and betaine dose, body weight, Total DQ score) and categorical variable (NBS presence). Correlation matrix was also included to show the linear correlation between two variables: X and Y. The matric has a value range between +1 and −1, where +1 indicates total positive linear correlation, 0 no linear correlation and −1 total negative linear correlation. The correlation coefficient ranges from −1 to +1, where +1 implies the existence of a linear equation establishing a relationship between two factors (X and Y) that increase simultaneously. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to the correlation matrix according to Benjamini, Krieger and Yekutieli method. The fdr is indicated as asterisks (*<20%, **<10%, ***<5%). The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

At first assessment, Kruskal Wallis test detected a significant difference in the median developmental quotients (DQ) within the three groups (Figure 3). Group B2 showed the worst neurodevelopmental functioning compared to Group A (71 vs. 96, p = 0.007) and B1 (71 vs. 97, p = 0.034). More specifically, in Group B2 one patient presented a normal DQ, four presented a borderline functioning, four a mild developmental delay and three a profound developmental delay, being too severely impaired to undergo standardized tests. Subscales analysis showed significant impairments in most of subdomains: Locomotor (B2 vs. A: 82 vs. 96, p = 0.025; B2 vs. B1: 82 vs. 105, p = 0.042), Personal‐Social (B2 vs. A: 69 vs. 111, p = 0.017), Eye and Hand Coordination (B2 vs. A: 69 vs. 96, p = 0.007; B2 vs. B1: 69 vs. 93, p = 0.033), Performance (B2 vs. A: 73 vs. 96, p = 0.017; B2 vs. B1: 73 vs. 100, p = 0.034) (Figure 3).

At follow‐up retesting, evaluations confirmed significantly worse DQ outcomes for Group B2 compared to Group A (74 vs. 98, p = 0.05), with lowering scores in Hearing and Speech (53 vs. 91, p = 0.011) and Performance (76 vs. 93, p = 0.025) scales. Differently from the first assessment, no significant differences were detected between Group B2 and Group B1. Regarding the two NBS‐diagnosed groups, Group A showed a better DQ outcome compared to Group B1, with higher scores in all the subscales (Figure 3). Moreover, Group A patients maintained a normal developmental functioning (first assessment vs. retest, DQ: 96 vs. 98), while those belonging to Group B1 showed a worsening trend over time (first assessment vs. retest, DQ: 97 vs. 83), with 2/3 patients moving from normal to borderline DQ, and a lower score in Hearing and Speech subscale (95 vs. 83).

In order to assess the influence of OH‐Cbl dose and different biochemical parameters on the neurodevelopmental outcome, we have performed a multiple linear regression analysis including the last Total DQ assessment. As shown in patients' clusterization (Figure 4A), OH‐Cbl dose was identified as a significant predictor of Hcy outcome (p = 0.003). Moreover, Hcy showed a significant predictive effect on Total DQ Score (p = 0.045). Accordingly, “high‐dose” treated patients belonging to Group A showed lower Hcy levels (median 22 μM/L, range 11–37) and higher DQ Score (median 98, range 75–100) compared to “low‐dose” treated B1 and B2 groups, with better DQ outcome in B1 patients. Correlation matrix showed that Hcy was negatively associated with OH‐Cbl dose (r = −0.72, p < 0.001) and Total DQ Score (r = −0.70, p < 0.001), and positively with MMA (r = 0.48, p = 0.036) (Figure 4B,C). Similarly, Total DQ Score was positively correlated with OH‐Cbl dose (r = 0.49, p = 0.027) (Figure 4B,D).

At ophthalmological examination, anterior segment was normal in all Group A patients. Cycloplegic refraction was normal in 3/5, with evidence of hyperopia in 1/5 and astigmatism in 1/5. At first evaluation, retinal examination evidenced atrophic maculopathy in 4/5 and no appreciable alterations in 1/5 patients. At follow‐up, 3/5 patients presented a progression of retinal involvement. The OCT scans showed from the first assessment abnormal results in all Group A patients, with individual variations ranging from slight reduction of global retinal thickness with sparing of the foveal depression and ERP‐photoreceptor layer in 1/5, to atrophic maculopathy with loss of retinal layers in 4/5 cases. In 3/5 patients, maculopathy worsened at follow‐up evaluations. Orthoptic assessment showed normal ocular motility in 1/5 patients, isolated strabismus in 1/5, and nystagmus + strabismus in 3/5. Visual acuity was reduced in all patients with results ranging between 1 and 3/10 (Logmar 1 to 0.5).

Among Group B1 e B2 patients, retinal examination evidenced no appreciable ophthalmoscopic alterations in 3/15 patients and atrophic maculopathy in 12/15. Given the retrospective nature of the study, OCT scan was performed only in one patient of Group B1 and in one of Group B2, and in both showed atrophic maculopathy. Orthoptic assessment evidenced isolated strabismus in 3/15 patients and nystagmus + strabismus in 12/15 patients. Visual acuity resulted normal in 3/15 patients and reduced in 12/15, with results ranging between 1 and 5/10 (Logmar 1 to 0.3).

To evaluate the biochemical and clinical impact of intensified OH‐Cbl regimen, we performed a retrospective study in three groups of Cbl‐C patients with different diagnostic and treatment modalities.

In terms of disease sub‐type characteristics (i.e. early‐onset vs. late onset phenotype), the three patients' groups were comparable, and presented similar genetic backgrounds with homozygosity for the severe early‐onset MMACHC variant c.271dupA in 4/5 patients in Group A, in 2/3 in Group B1 and in 7/12 in Group B2. All other mutations are known as pathogenetic and associated with the early‐onset phenotype; the c.394 C>T variant, which is usually linked with a late‐onset phenotype, has also been reported in a homozygous early‐onset patient.
45
 This latter variant was found in compound heterozygosity with the c.515‐1G>T PRDX1 mutation in one patient from Group A, to whom we attributed the early‐onset phenotype due to the presence of maculopathy, a typical manifestation of this disease sub‐group.
4



The study showed that “high‐dose” OH‐Cbl treatment in children with severe early‐onset Cbl‐C defect led to a significant improvement in the metabolic profile and in neurocognitive outcome, compared to age‐matched patients treated with a “low‐dose” regimen.

Biochemically, “high‐dose” treated patients reached significantly lower levels of Hcy and MMA and higher values of methionine compared to those receiving a “low‐dose” regimen. The improved biomarker profile was found to be significantly correlated with OH‐Cbl dose. Since the small number of patients may represent a limitation for data interpretation, to overcome this issue we used more than one measure per patient in biomarker evaluations. Moreover, to increase uniformity among groups, we adopted a common age window corresponding to neurocognitive evaluation time points for follow‐up data collection.

The achievement of borderline‐normal Hcy levels in Group A did not require to increase betaine dosage, as attempted in Group B1 and B2.
46
 As demonstrated by multiple linear regression analysis, the higher Met levels in Group A were not dependent on betaine dose but reflect the higher OH‐Cbl dosage.

Clinically, patients treated with “high‐dose” OH‐Cbl and diagnosed by NBS, maintained a normal cognitive profile over the 3 years follow‐up. Conversely, patients treated with “low‐dose”, including those identified by NBS, showed a lower DQ. Analysis of the subscales showed substantially stable developmental profile trajectories in the “high‐dose” patients and a significant drop of verbal skills in both “low‐dose” groups, regardless of the mode of diagnosis (i.e. NBS vs. clinically driven diagnosis). The worst outcome observed in B2 patients may results from the impact of a late diagnosis combined with low OH‐Cbl regimen. These findings are similar to those reported in our previous study, showing a selective decline of the verbal subscale, between the second and the third year of life, in non‐screened “low‐dose” treated patients.
14
 Similar to what observed in Group B1, impaired neurodevelopmental and adaptive functioning has been reported also in NBS diagnosed patients, treated with “low‐dose” OH‐Cbl and maintaining circulating levels of Hcy well above the normal range.
37
, 
38
 Consistently, our study showed that Hcy significantly predicted the neurocognitive outcome and that OH‐Cbl predicted the Hcy levels.

Differently from cognitive outcome, maculopathy resulted equally represented in our patients' population treated with different OH‐Cbl regimens, confirming that the pathogenesis of eye disease moves in a field less dependent from Hcy values and possibly involving other mechanisms, such as those related to redox alterations.

Despite a potential favorable impact, a real obstacle to use “ultra‐high” OH‐Cbl regimens is represented, at least in Europe, by the lack of pharmaceutical products containing highly concentrated OH‐Cbl solutions. With the exception of a very‐high concentrated preparation, approved only for intravenous treatment of cyanide intoxication (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/cyanokit), all the other commercial formulations available in the European market contain a maximum of 5 mg/mL OH‐Cbl, making not achievable the long‐term maintenance of an “ultra‐high” therapeutic regimen. If we consider that the daily parenteral administration of the therapy has a strong impact on the quality of life, this issue represents a real unmet need for Cbl‐C patients and their families, hampering to adjust the OH‐Cbl dose when patients, as they grow, reach a weight that would require too large volumes to be tolerated for daily parenteral administration.

In conclusion, our study highlights that early initiation of “high‐dose” OH‐Cbl therapy, in association with the implementation of large‐scale NBS programs, may allow normal neurodevelopment in children with severe early‐onset Cbl‐C defect, and that long‐term metabolic monitoring is required to optimize the OH‐Cbl regimen over follow‐up. In our patients the “high‐dose” regimen did not prevent macular disease and, to this regard, further studies are needed to demonstrate whether the early initiation of “ultra‐high” dose therapy may provide a protective effect on visual function. Finally, we believe that our findings, although observed in a limited number of patients, may contribute to improve the long‐term outcome of Cbl‐C patients.
