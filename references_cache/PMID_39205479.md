# Deletions in the CDKL5 5' untranslated region lead to CDKL5 deficiency disorder.
**Authors:** Haviland I, Hector RD, Swanson LC, Verran AS, Sherrill E, Frazier Z, Denny AM, Lucash J, Zhang B, Dubbs HA, Marsh ED, Weisenberg JL, Leonard H, Crippa M, Cogliati F, Russo S, Suter B, Rajaraman R, Percy AK, Schreiber JM, Demarest S, Benke TA, Chopra M, Yu TW, Olson HE
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.a.63843](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.a.63843)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in the cyclin-dependent kinase-like 5 (CDKL5) gene are associated with CDKL5 deficiency disorder (CDD), a severe X-linked developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. Deletions affecting the 5' untranslated region (UTR) of CDKL5, which involve the noncoding exon 1 and/or alternatively spliced first exons (exons 1a-e), are uncommonly reported. We describe genetic and phenotypic characteristics for 15 individuals with CDKL5 partial gene deletions affecting the 5' UTR. All individuals presented characteristic features of CDD, including medically refractory infantile-onset epilepsy, global developmental delay, and visual impairment. We performed RNA sequencing on fibroblast samples from three individuals with small deletions involving exons 1 and/or 1a/1b only. Results demonstrated reduced CDKL5 mRNA expression with no evidence of expression from alternatively spliced first exons. Our study broadens the genotypic spectrum for CDD by adding to existing evidence that deletions affecting the 5' UTR of the CDKL5 gene are associated with the disorder. We propose that smaller 5' UTR deletions may require additional molecular testing approaches such as RNA sequencing to determine pathogenicity.

Cyclin-dependent kinase-like 5 (CDKL5) deficiency disorder (CDD; OMIM 300203, 300672) is a rare, severe X-linked developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) characterized by medically refractory infantile-onset epilepsy, global developmental delay, and cerebral visual impairment (CVI)(Brock et al., 2021; S. T. Demarest et al., 2019; Leonard et al., 2022; Olson et al., 2019, 2021). Other common features of CDD include generalized hypotonia, gastrointestinal symptoms such as reflux, constipation, and dysphagia, hand stereotypies with limited functional hand use, and sleep disturbance (Benke et al., 2024; Leonard et al., 2022; Olson et al., 2019).

CDKL5 is located on the X-chromosome (Xp22.13) and disease-causing variants are de novo in the majority of reported individuals (Hector et al., 2016). CDKL5 expresses five protein-coding transcript isoforms, and although all contain the critical catalytic domain (spanning exons 2–11), isoform hCDKL5_1 is the predominant functional isoform expressed in the brain and is required for normal neurodevelopment (Hector et al., 2016; Hector et al., 2017). In the 5’ untranslated region (UTR), canonical hCDKL5 transcripts splice from exon 1 to exon 2, however; several alternatively spliced exons in the 5’ UTR have been identified: exons 1a, 1b, 1c/d, and 1e (Hector et al., 2016).

Disease-causing deletions, early truncations and splice variants occur throughout the CDKL5 gene, whereas missense variants are restricted to the catalytic domain; missense variants outside the catalytic domain are unlikely to be pathogenic (Olson et al., 2019). Deletions affecting the 5’ UTR are less commonly described and have variably been reported as (likely) pathogenic or of uncertain significance (Bahi-Buisson et al., 2010; Liang et al., 2011; Mei et al., 2014; Nemos et al., 2009) (Table 1). Through our involvement in the CDKL5 network, we identified 15 females with deletions in the CDKL5 5’ UTR who have clinical features consistent with CDD. In this study, we describe the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of this cohort. We also used RNA sequencing to aid in determination of pathogenicity of smaller deletions in this region and to explore whether there was evidence of compensatory use of alternatively spliced first exons for CDKL5 expression.

This study includes 15 individuals with deletions in the 5’ UTR of the CDKL5 gene who underwent informed consent and were enrolled in Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved protocols. Participants were enrolled at six clinical CDKL5 Centers of Excellence throughout the United States, as well as in the International CDKL5 Disorder Database, which is approved by the University of Western Australia Human Research Ethics Committee. Six individuals were co-enrolled in the International CDKL5 Clinical Research Network (ICCRN), approved by the Colorado Multiple IRB. Informed consent for RNA sequencing studies for 3 individuals was obtained through an IRB-approved protocol at Boston Children’s Hospital.

We describe genetic and phenotypic characteristics for 15 individuals with CDKL5 partial gene deletions affecting the 5’ UTR. We reviewed clinical genetic test results and medical history. We obtained clinical severity scores from the ICCRN for 6 individuals in our sample who were co-enrolled in the ICCRN as well as for a broader cohort of ICCRN participants for comparison (excluding these 6 individuals). The Caregiver and Clinician CDKL5 Severity Assessments have sound psychometric properties and inter and intra rater reliability (S. Demarest et al., 2019; Saldaris et al., 2021; Ziniel et al., 2023). Further detail and individual scores for our participants are available in Supporting Information File S1.

Reported deletion coordinates were converted to genome assembly GRCh38/hg38 (Dec. 2013) for consistency, using the open access UCSC Genome Browser Lift Genome Annotations tool (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgLiftOver)(Nassar et al., 2023). Variants were reclassified using the most recent American College of Medical Genetics /ClinGen guidelines for copy number variant classification, which is applicable to scoring of CNVs within the 5’ UTR of a single gene (Riggs et al., 2020). For the phenotype specificity criterion, the phenotype of each participant was evaluated utilizing the CDKL5 clinical phenotype guidelines developed by the ClinGen Rett and Angelman-like Disorders Variant Curation Expert Panel (McKnight et al., 2022). Specifically, the criterion for phenotype specificity was applied in probands with documented infantile-onset seizures, global developmental delay or intellectual disability, impaired gross motor function, and CVI with onset prior to age 12 months. Scoring details may be found in Supporting Information File S2. CVI is defined as visual dysfunction in the absence of ocular or anterior visual pathway abnormalities (Brock et al., 2021; S. T. Demarest et al., 2019; Leonard et al., 2022; Olson et al., 2019, 2021; Sakki et al., 2018)

RNA sequencing was performed on fibroblast samples from 3 individuals with small deletions involving exons 1 and/or 1a/1b only, with the goal of determining pathogenicity and potential use of alternatively spliced first exons for CDKL5 expression. RNA extracted from patient-derived fibroblasts was sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq platform (sequencing performed the Harvard Medical School Biopolymers Facility) to a minimum read coverage of 100–200 MM raw reads per sample (paired-end 150 base pair reads). Alignment and quantification were performed with the Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) open-source software. Interactive genomics viewer was used to visualize read coverage of the CDKL5 locus.

We identified 15 females with deletions affecting the 5’ UTR region of CDKL5. Variants were de novo (with confirmed parentage by trio exome sequencing or genome sequencing in 2/15 individuals). In 7/15 individuals, parents were tested and were negative, but parentage was not confirmed. In 6 families, parental testing was not performed or not available for review. The deletion was identified as likely mosaic in blood in one individual (Individual 6). CDKL5 deletion coordinates were known for 13/15 individuals, with deletion sizes ranging from 1,222 to 1,652,229 base pairs, all affecting the 5’ UTR of CDKL5 and spanning the exon 1 region (Table 1; Figure 1). Two individuals were diagnosed by multiplex-ligation dependent probe amplification testing, which identified deletions in the exon 1 region but did not identify precise coordinates. Of those with known deletion coordinates, 5 individuals had relatively large deletions (>40 kb) encompassing all isoforms of exon 1, 6 individuals had deletions encompassing exons 1, 1a, and 1b, one individual had a deletion encompassing only the main exon 1 (Individual 2), and one individual had a deletion encompassing only exons 1a and 1b (Individual 3) (Table 1; Figure 2).

All 15 individuals in the cohort were female, with an age at last follow-up between 2 and 19 years (Table 2). All had global developmental delay. Fourteen of 15 individuals had onset of epilepsy at ≤3 months (range 1–12 weeks), a single individual had onset of epilepsy at 32 weeks. Epilepsy was refractory to multiple anti-seizure treatments for all participants, and 11/15 individuals reported daily seizures at last follow-up. Visual impairment was reported in 12/15 individuals, with either a formal diagnosis of CVI or features consistent with CVI in those lacking a formally documented diagnosis, such as roving gaze and inconsistent fixation in the absence of structural ocular or anterior visual pathway abnormalities. A majority of individuals had additional common features of CDD, including hypotonia (10/15), constipation (12/15), dysphagia (10/15), and sleep disturbance (9/15). Other medical issues that are known to co-occur in CDD were also reported in our cohort, including behavioral dysregulation, movement disorders (chorea), scoliosis, and respiratory issues.

We obtained clinical severity scores from the ICCRN for 6 individuals and a comparison cohort for two measures: the CCSA-Clinician (clinician-completed), and the CCSA-Caregiver (caregiver-completed). Both assessments are graded on a scale of 0–100 (100 being the most severe) (Saldaris et al., 2021; Ziniel et al., 2023). The median severity score on the CCSA-Clinician for the 6 individuals in our study was 66.29 (IQR 53.19–81.69), compared to a median score of 59.25 in a broader ICCRN cohort of 142 participants (IQR 38.79–73.31). The median severity score on the CCSA-Caregiver for our sample was 34.13 (IQR 22.19–49.84), compared to a median score of 29.97 in a broader ICCRN cohort of 192 participants (IQR 19.46–44.65). Thus, these individuals are similar to a larger cohort with CDD. Further detail and individual scores for our participants are available in Supporting Information File S1.

The phenotypic presentation of individuals in this study was consistent with CDD according to clinical diagnostic criteria (Olson et al., 2019), although one individual had resolution of seizures (Individual 3). We describe clinical vignettes and molecular investigations for 3 individuals who had smaller deletions restricted to exons 1 and/or 1a/1b only (Fig. 1), to explore whether there was evidence of compensatory use of alternatively spliced first exons for CDKL5 expression and/or to aid in determination of pathogenicity. Detailed clinical vignettes for individuals not included in the main manuscript are provided in Supporting Information File S3.

A female infant presented with seizures at age 3 weeks. Initial seizures were bilateral tonic followed by a cluster of myoclonic jerks. Seizures then evolved to bilateral tonic with head deviation, followed by non-rhythmic hypermotor activity. She developed infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS) with hypsarrhythmia at age 6 months. Seizures were refractory to multiple anti-seizure medications, including prednisolone. Initiation of ketogenic diet led to a 3-month seizure-free period, after which seizures recurred and increased in frequency, reaching up to 50 tonic-clonic seizures per day. At age 5 years, seizure control varied from 6–8 seizures daily to once weekly (generalized tonic-clonic, at times followed by a cluster of myoclonic jerks).

She had global developmental delay with developmental regression at age 3 years. She lost the ability to sit and stand independently (as well as to walk with assistance), the ability to use a raking grasp, and the purposeful use of words (previously used 2–3 words). At age 3 years 9 months, she had some head control and could bear weight in quadruped position, could bat at objects but could not grasp, and had some non-purposeful vocalizations but no use of words. At age 5 years and 9 months, she had started using eye-gaze communication and simple gesturing (waving, smacking her lips when prompted). Other medical issues included generalized hypotonia, CVI, dysphagia (requiring thickening agents for liquids), and constipation.

No abnormalities were identified on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). No diagnostic findings were identified on metabolic workup (plasma amino acids, acylcarnitine profile, lactate/pyruvate) or karyotype. At 11 months, an epilepsy gene panel test and a whole genome oligonucleotide array identified a heterozygous partial deletion including exon 1 of the CDKL5 gene, which was later confirmed to be de novo through parental testing. This deletion was confirmed, and breakpoints more finely specified, on genome sequencingat age 3 years (deletion size 10,199 base pairs), spanning exons 1, 1a and 1b (Table 1; Fig. 1).

A female infant presented with seizures at age 3 weeks and then developed IESS with hypsarrhythmia present prior to age 3. Ketogenic diet was initiated at 3 years of age, which she continued for 3.5 years with some stabilization of seizures, though overall seizures remained refractory to multiple anti-seizure medications. At age 18 years, she was taking three anti-seizure medications and seizures followed a cyclic pattern, with 4–5 weeks with no seizures followed by 3 days with 4–5 generalized tonic-clonic seizures every day. At age 19 years, seizure types were predominantly tonic asymmetric (with the right arm extended above the head and lasting 45 seconds to 1 minute often in clusters) followed by myoclonic seizures lasting less than 5 minutes.

She demonstrated profound developmental impairments. At age 19 years, she had some head control and could only sit briefly with significant support; she could not stand or take steps. She could bat at objects but could not grasp. She also had intermittent hand stereotypies with frequent hand mouthing. She had some non-purposeful vocalizations but no use of words. She had a gastrostomy tube placed at 12 years of age due to a history of failure to thrive and to maximize fluid intake, though this was not her primary source of nutrition. Other medical issues include severe generalized hypotonia, constipation, scoliosis, and CVI.

No abnormalities were identified on MRI. She had metabolic workup at 1 month of age after onset of seizures, which included normal lactate/ pyruvate, urine organic acids, and plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) amino acids. Repeat studies at 8 months of age were also normal with the exception of an elevation of plasma alanine that was not present in CSF. Acylcarnitine profile and CSF lactate/pyruvate were also sent at that time and normal. CDKL5 deletion/duplication analysis was performed by multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, which identified a de novo deletion in the 5’ UTR of CDKL5. An epilepsy panel test later confirmed the presence of a de novo minimum 1,222 base pair deletion encompassing the primary exon 1 of CDKL5 but none of the alternatively spliced first exons (Table 1; Fig. 1).

A female infant presented with seizures at age 4 weeks (generalized tonic to clonic/myoclonic), which later evolved to generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Seizures were refractory to multiple anti-seizure medications and occurred multiple times daily during the first year of life. She initiated the ketogenic diet at 10 months of age. She was seizure-free by age 12 months. She tapered off the ketogenic diet and all anti-seizure medications at 3 years of age with no further seizure recurrence.

She had global developmental delay and intellectual disability with no history of developmental regression. At age 17 years, she was able to walk with minimal support with a wide-based stance and unstable gait pattern, and she was able to stand from sitting without support. She could use both hands to handle toys and reach toward choices but did not have a well-developed pincer grasp or use of utensils. She made non-purposeful vocalizations and occasionally used a communication book to make choices as well as an eye-gaze assisted communication system for basic needs such as hunger and thirst. Other medical issues included behavioral dysregulation, with periods of anxiety and irritability, frequent hand stereotypies, bruxism, intermittent breath holding and hyperventilation, air swallowing, sialorrhea, sleep disturbance (difficulty with sleep initiation and maintenance with frequent nighttime awakenings), decreased oral intake requiring gastrostomy tube placement for feeding at age 12 years, constipation with stool retention behavior, frequent loose stools, gastroesophageal reflux, and delayed puberty.

No abnormalities were identified on MRI. No diagnostic findings were identified on metabolic workup (cerebral folate metabolism, adenyl succinate lyase metabolism, neurotransmitters/lactic acid in CSF). Chromosomal microarray, sequencing of MECP2, ARX, NLGN3 and NLGN4, and UBE3A, and Prader-Willi fluorescence in situ hybridization were all non-diagnostic. Autism gene panel with deletion/duplication analysis identified a 2,354 base pair heterozygous deletion in the 5’ UTR of CDKL5 classified as a variant of uncertain significance; later parental testing confirmed that this variant was absent in parents. The deletion encompassed CDKL5 alternatively spliced exons 1a and 1b but did not affect the primary exon 1 (Table 1; Fig. 2). A maternally inherited CDKL5 variant was identified, c.2388C>T p. Ser796Ser (synonymous, no predicted splicing impact, maximum allele frequency 0.00346%) and classified as likely benign (Karczewski et al., 2020). The previously identified CDKL5 deletion was not found on exome sequencing likely due to lack of sensitivity of ES for capturing deletions during that time period. Mitochondrial DNA testing was non-diagnostic.

We performed RNA sequencing on fibroblast samples from 3 individuals with small 5’ UTR deletions to evaluate their pathogenicity. These deletions encompassed exons 1 and/or 1a/1b but in theory might have allowed residual expression from alternate first exons not encompassed by the individual’s deletion (such as 1c, 1d or 1e). RNA was analyzed from samples that were cultured with or without treatment with cycloheximide, an inhibitor of nonsense-mediated decay.

RNA sequencing showed that all three variants were associated with reductions in CDKL5 mRNA levels (normalized expression levels of 25.6%, 17.5% and 56.7% for individuals 1, 2 and 3, respectively, relative to controls), demonstrating their pathogenicity (Figure 3A). No significant usage of alternative exon 1s was detected in cycloheximide-untreated cell lines (Figure 3B). Cycloheximide-treatment revealed a very low level of alternative exon 1 usage in individual 3 (splice junctions between exons 1 and 1e and exons 1e and 2 were detected in 3.8% of reads from individual 3) (Supporting Information File S4). Since these alternative splicing events were only visible with cycloheximide treatment, we concluded that they are creating out-of-frame transcripts and being subject to nonsense-mediated decay.

In this study, we report 15 individuals with chromosome X deletions affecting the 5’ UTR of CDKL5. This is the first large case series of individuals with deletions in this region. While there have been prior reports of individuals with deletions in this region (Bahi-Buisson et al., 2010; Liang et al., 2011; Mei et al., 2014; Nemos et al., 2009) (Tables 1 and 2), the pathogenicity of these variants and their association with CDD had not been further explored. Furthermore, the possibility of compensation with expression from alternatively spliced first exons had not been measured. This clinical series data in combination with prior literature support an association of deletions in the 5’ UTR including the non-coding first exon of CDKL5 with CDD. RNA sequencing suggestions that 5’ UTR deletions result in lack of CDKL5 RNA production, resulting in haploinsufficiency in females or lack of protein in males with germ-line deletions.

Key features of CDD include infantile onset, medically refractory epilepsy, and global developmental delay, which were present in all 15 individuals in our study. CVI is common in genetic developmental and epileptic encephalopathies but occurs at a higher rate in CDD (Daniels et al., 2023; S. T. Demarest et al., 2019; Olson et al., 2021). While not all individuals in our study had a formal diagnosis of CVI, 12 of 15 had features consistent with CVI.

Ninety percent of individuals with CDD have onset of seizures, mostly refractory, by age 12 months (Mangatt et al., 2016). Fourteen of 15 individuals in our cohort had onset of seizures by age 12 weeks. There have been few reports of individuals with CDD who have never had seizures or who are seizure-free for more than 5 years (Aznar-Laín et al., 2023; Fehr et al., 2016; MacKay et al., 2020; Weaving et al., 2004). Fourteen of 15 individuals in our cohort had onset of seizures by 12 weeks, and all but one presented with medically refractory epilepsy; patient 3 later became seizure-free and has remained so for the last 17 years. This individual has a small de novo deletion that encompasses only exons 1a and 1b of the CDKL5 gene, sparing the major first exon. We hypothesized that the sparing of the major first exon could have resulted in a level of CDKL5 expression on the mutant allele that would not cause CDD, but results did not support this hypothesis. Genotype-phenotype correlations in CDD are not yet fully understood. While specific variants have been identified as associated with a milder clinical presentation either with regard to epilepsy or functional abilities (Bahi-Buisson et al., 2012; MacKay et al., 2020, 2021; Olson et al., 2019), molecular patterns related to milder phenotype remain to be identified. Random X-inactivation can additionally play a role in confounding genotype-phenotype relationships.

Individual 3’s intronic deletion and RNA sequencing results could be explained by a few hypotheses. First, the deletion may include regulatory regions in intron 1 critical to the expression of the major hCDKL5_1 isoform, such as the complete promoter-associated CpG island, putative cis-regulatory elements, and histone methylation marks, as shown in Fig. 2. Second, while exon 1-containing isoforms may be most abundant in the central nervous system (Hector et al., 2016), exons 1a- and 1b-containing isoforms may be of special relevance to disease pathogenesis. Third, the deletion may affect splicing from exon 1 to exon 2 and is therefore an intronic splicing mutation. However, in silico analysis performed using SpliceAI (Jaganathan et al., 2019) did not suggest any change in splicing, predicting that the exon 1 splice donor site was not compromised by the deletion.

A limitation of this study is the variability in genetic testing approaches among participants in different healthcare settings and time points. While trio exome or genome sequencing (inclusive of both biological parents) provides the best evidence for variant pathogenicity, as it 1) rules out an alternative or co-occurring genetic condition, 2) confirms the de novo status, and 3) confirms parentage, it is uncommon for such testing to be ordered after a diagnostic chromosomal microarray. On internal variant reclassification, 5 of the 15 variants identified in this cohort only reached American College of Medical Genetics classification of variant of uncertain significance. Confirmed de novo status and/or specificity of phenotype according to the CDKL5 disease-specific modifications (McKnight et al., 2022) would have allowed the variant to reach the threshold for (likely) pathogenic. Variant classification was limited by the available clinical and phenotypic information available for this international cohort. In the absence of details, we erred on the side of conservative classification. We also note that the clinical criteria described in McKnight et al. are highly specific, but may lack sensitivity at a younger age range due to challenges in ascertaining ophthalmologic features and intellectual disability. Notably, for each proband, the treating physician reported a diagnosis of CDD based on co-interpretation of genetic results and clinical presentation. We highlight the distinction between variant classification and clinical interpretation, as reflected in real-world genetic medicine. While males with CDD can have a more severe phenotype (Leonard et al., 2022), the limited data available for two males known to have deletions in this region (Bahi-Buisson et al., 2010; Liang et al., 2011) make it difficult to draw conclusions about male-female differences.

Deletions over 1 kb in size residing upstream of the first translated region of CDKL5, exon 2, are extremely rare in reference population datasets. In version 4.1 of gnomAD (Karczewski et al., 2020), only 11 alleles with such deletions are reported. The most frequent of these reported deletions is 5.43kb in size and has an allele frequency of 8.34e-5 (Karczewski et al., 2020), in the intronic region downstream of exon 1 and all alternative exon 1s. None of the deletions in gnomAD include primary exon 1. Furthermore, no homozygous deletions larger than 1 kb in this region have been recorded in reference population datasets. This intolerance to copy number variation in the general population in the 5’ UTR, particularly involving main exon 1, supports the pathogenicity of deletions in this region. Clinical management of individuals with pathogenic deletions in the 5’ UTR including exon 1 or with evidence of lack of CDKL5 mRNA would be the same as for CDD broadly (Amin et al., 2022; Hong et al., 2022; Leonard et al., 2022).

The present study contributes to the existing body of evidence supporting the pathogenicity of deletions in the 5’ UTR of the CDKL5 gene. We were not able to demonstrate use of alternatively spliced first exons for expression of CDKL5 in patients with smaller deletions in the region, though prior work interrogating RNA sequencing data and analysis of CDKL5 Transcription Start Sites has demonstrated that some minor CDKL5 transcript isoforms can originate in the brain using alternatively spliced first exons 1a, 1b, or 1e (Hector et al., 2016). Although these are very low abundance transcripts and do not appear to compensate when the predominant first exon (exon 1) is missing, methods of upregulating these alternative, low abundance isoforms may offer an avenue for future research and therapeutic development.

Our findings emphasize the importance of testing for copy number variants in non-coding regions when the suspicion for a CDD diagnosis remains strong despite inconclusive genetic testing. We also highlight the potential utility of RNA sequencing to determine pathogenicity of deletions of uncertain significance in the exon 1 region of CDKL5, though this testing is not currently available clinically due to low expression in blood and fibroblasts. Furthermore, due to the possibility of differential expression patterns between fibroblasts and neurons, RNA sequencing of induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons could potentially provide further insights.
