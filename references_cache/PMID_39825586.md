Many genes in the human genome encode proteins that are dosage sensitive, 
meaning they require protein levels within a narrow range to properly execute 
function. To investigate if clinically relevant variation in protein levels 
impacts the same downstream pathways in human disease, we generated cell models 
of two SETBP1 syndromes: Schinzel-Giedion Syndrome (SGS) and SETBP1 
haploinsufficiency disease (SHD), where SGS is caused by too much protein, and 
SHD is caused by not enough SETBP1. Using patient and sex-matched healthy 
first-degree relatives from both SGS and SHD SETBP1 cases, we assessed how 
SETBP1 protein dosage affects downstream pathways in human forebrain progenitor 
cells. We find that extremes of SETBP1 protein dose reciprocally influence 
important signalling molecules such as AKT, suggesting that the SETBP1 protein 
operates within a narrow dosage range and that extreme doses are detrimental. We 
identified SETBP1 nuclear bodies as interacting with the nuclear lamina and 
suggest that SETBP1 may organize higher order chromatin structure via links to 
the nuclear envelope. SETBP1 protein doses may exert significant influence on 
global gene expression patterns via these SETBP1 nuclear bodies. This work 
provides evidence for the importance of SETBP1 protein dose in human brain 
development, with implications for two neurodevelopmental disorders.

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a group of conditions that occur due to the impaired development of the nervous system [1]. Mutations in several genes have been associated with NDDs, impacting a broad range of cellular processes such as the spatio-temporal regulation of cell proliferation, cell migration, and cell fate determination [2]. Particular mutations in specific genes differentially affect neurodevelopmental phenotypes depending on their expression levels during brain development, the cell types they are expressed in, and the genetic networks they contribute to. Brain development is highly orchestrated, and the amount of protein made and when it is made is often important for many but not all genes. These genes are called dosage sensitive, and this concept can reflect genetic dosage (the number of copies in the genome), transcriptomic dosage (the level of steady state mRNA), and protein dosage.

With the increased use of genome-wide analysis technologies, the high frequency by which copy number (CN) changes (ie. genetic dosage disorders) cause disease has become better understood [3]. With SNP arrays [4], comparative genomic hybridization, and massively parallel sequencing technologies, many more copy number deletion and duplication syndromes have been discovered. Several disorders are caused by recurrent CN changes and suggest that specific genes at these locations are dosage sensitive [5, 6].

Dosage sensitive syndromes are also caused by mutations that do not delete or duplicate whole genes. For example, frameshift or stop mutations can trigger nonsense mediated decay of mRNA from the mutant allele and lead to haploinsufficiency. Mutations can also occur in specific regions of a gene encoding critical domains that influence the protein’s stability [7]. Mutations in degron regions for example, which are short linear motifs acting as degradation signals, can disrupt protein degradation pathways, causing protein accumulation [8]. Degrons are recognized by the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), conferring instability and a short half-life to proteins [9, 10].

This paper aims to assess cellular effects of dosage by investigating two syndromes caused by mutations in the same gene, SETBP1, which are thought to cause disease by producing too much or too little SETBP1 protein. While the reciprocal protein doses of these two syndromes are not expected to be equivalent, our hope is to empirically test how clinically relevant protein doses arising from a single gene affect downstream pathways in a relevant cell type for disease. Ultimately the question we are interested in testing is whether protein doses can affect the same pathway in opposite ways. This may have implications for disease mechanism and treatment for other protein dosage syndromes.

Schinzel-Giedion Syndrome (SGS) (OMIM # 269150) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline heterozygous missense mutations in SETBP1 [11–14]. All SGS mutations reside in a region of about 12 base pairs coding for a degron motif [12], recognized by a β-TrCP E3 ubiquitin ligase [12, 15]. Mutations within this region impair the ligase’s ability to associate with SETBP1 protein leading to less ubiquitination, less breakdown of SETBP1 in the proteasome, and accumulation of SETBP1 [12]. Symptoms of SGS include severe developmental and growth delays, progressive brain atrophy, distorted neuronal layering, delayed myelination, and white matter atrophy. Additional manifestations encompass hydronephrosis, cystic fibrosis-like lung anomalies, midface retraction, severe seizures, neuroepithelial tumors [16], bone abnormalities, and congenital malformations [14, 17, 18]. Neoplastic tumors (sacrococcygeal teratomas) may also develop around the spinal cord [19]. The severity of symptoms leads to early mortality in affected children, almost always in the first decade of life [16].

SETBP1 haploinsufficiency disorder (SHD) (OMIM # 616078) is caused by heterozygous deletions or frameshift mutations in SETBP1, meaning that SHD is a loss-of-function (LOF) disorder [18, 20–23]. The characteristic features of this syndrome are mild to severe developmental delay, distinctive facial features, seizures, hypoplastic corpus callosum, autistic behaviours, early life hypotonia, heightened sociability and expressive speech delay [18, 20].

We reasoned that investigation of the SETBP1 protein dosage in these two syndromes would allow us to determine if a downstream pathway of SETBP1 shows reciprocal effects due to differential SETBP1 protein doses. While we do not expect protein dose of SETBP1 to be equal and opposite between SGS and SHD, the dynamic range of SETBP1 protein levels in these two disorders might nevertheless be informative for the effects on a known downstream pathway of SETBP1.

Diverse roles for SETBP1 have been reported, any of which could be reasonable targets with which to assess our dosage hypothesis. SETBP1 acts as a repressor of RUNX1 within the NuRD complex and as a transcription factor that upregulates HOXA9 and HOXA10, thereby enhancing the self-renewal capacity of hematopoietic stem cells in myeloid malignancies [24, 25]. Additionally, both wild-type and missense-mutant forms of SETBP1 directly activate Myb transcription, which is crucial for the immortalization of myeloid progenitors in myeloid leukemia [13]. SETBP1 also stabilizes the SET protein thereby inhibiting PP2A phosphatase activity in myeloid leukemias [26, 27]. SETBP1 may also be a DNA-binding protein that recruits the HCF1/KMT2A/PHF8 epigenetic complex to activate and regulate genes involved in cell differentiation and organ development [11]. SETBP1 also appears to interact with p53, via SET [28].

We chose to study the PP2A-AKT axis because of the well-studied role of the SET oncoprotein on PP2A inhibition [29]. Protein Phosphatase 2A functions to cleave phosphate groups from target proteins and is an important tumour suppressor in most cell types. We used induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to model SETBP1 dosage disorders, given their capacity to replicate various neurodevelopmental processes within the patient’s specific genetic background. This approach allows us to examine in detail the developmental and molecular mechanisms influenced by SETBP1 variation in a controlled and patient-specific context.

IPSC lines were generated from clinically diagnosed SGS and SHD cases and sex-matched familial controls (n = 8 different individuals from 4 families, n = 4 controls and n = 4 cases; Fig. 1A). We generated iPSCs from patient somatic cells, verifying the presence of pluripotency markers and confirming that cells were free of chromosomal anomalies (Fig. S1A, Fig. S2A). Our goal is to better understand the role of SETBP1 in the brain since this organ is so detrimentally affected in both SGS and SHD, so we made forebrain neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and confirmed expression of neural specific markers (Fig. S1B, Fig. S2B). Induction to forebrain progenitor cells was normal suggesting forebrain induction is not impacted in either SGS or SHD, at least on gross analysis of relevant markers. In support of the importance of SETBP1 in the brain, we found that SETBP1 mRNA expression in human NPCs is significantly increased compared to fibroblasts and iPSCs, using a publicly accessible database (Fig. 1B), and consistent with SETBP1 protein levels from our own cells (Fig. S3A). The two participants with de novo SGS mutations occurring inside the four amino acid degron sequence DSGI were SGS1 (AA change: I871S) and SGS2 (AA change: G870S) (Fig. 1C,D,E). We confirmed reported mutations in DNA by Sanger Sequencing (Fig. 1F) and assayed cell lysates from SGS and control NPCs for protein expression. We found that in SGS1 and SGS2, there is a ~ 5–10-fold increase in SETBP1 protein levels compared to sex-matched biological controls (Fig. 1G). For SHD, we recruited two participants with single base pair deletion mutations, causing a frameshift and inducing a loss of function from one allele, SHD1 (S608Afs*22) and SHD2 (H523Lfs*32) (Fig. 1H). Mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1I) and SETBP1 protein levels were approximately 50% of sex-matched biological controls (Fig. 1J), however RNA levels were inconsistent showing extreme loss of mRNA in one patient and no loss of mRNA in the other (Fig. S3B). It is not clear why mRNA levels would differ so much between patients, so a second primer set was used which confirmed the first findings.

In an attempt to increase the dynamic range of SETBP1 protein loss, we made SETBP1 Knock-out (KO) cells in a healthy control cell line using CRISPR-Cas9 through a simultaneous reprogramming and genetic engineering technique as we have previously described [30] (Fig. 1K). We designed guide RNAs (gRNAs) to target exon 2 of SETBP1 (Fig. S3C, Table S3) to induce a frameshift mutation. We then screened clones via Western Blot (WB) to identify any potential KOs that showed loss of SETBP1. We selected two clones that showed a range of SETBP1 loss (Fig. S3D) and Sanger-sequenced these to confirm a mutation in SETBP1 (Fig. 1L). KO1 has a heterozygous frameshift mutation caused by a 2-base pair insertion while KO2 is a compound heterozygous KO (Fig. S3E). KO2 shows almost complete loss of SETBP1 putting it at the extreme end of SETBP1 protein dose, while KO1 showed an approximate 50% loss of SETBP1 (Fig. 1M). Both KO1 and KO2 cell lines were consistent for iPSC markers (Fig. S2A). We were again able to successfully induce NPCs from KO cell lines, suggesting that loss of SETBP1 protein levels doesn’t interfere with forebrain induction.

Given the range and severity of the phenotypes presented in the SETBP1 disorders, we also performed gross characterizations of the differentiation process from neural progenitors to mature cell types including oligodendrocytes, astrocytes and neurons from cases andcontrols. We were able to successfully differentiate cells to oligodendrocytes (Fig. S4), astrocytes (Fig. S5) and forebrain cortical neurons (Fig. S6), suggesting that terminal differentiation of SGS or SHD iPSCs is not impaired with a range of pathological SETBP1 protein doses, at least with respect to commonly used markers of these cell types. Immunostaining of mature forebrain neuronal cultures with GFAP appeared to show an increased ratio of GFAP+ cells in both SGS and SHD, possibly indicating that a proportion of cells remains in an immature state in both diseases.

Previous reports [12, 31–33] have indicated that mutations occurring within a mutational hotspot located in the degron motif of SETBP1 decrease the efficiency of degradation mediated by the SCF-βTrCP1 E3 ligase, a component of the ubiquitin-proteasome system. Consequently, this reduced probability of degradation changes the steady state balance of SETBP1 between synthesis and breakdown, leading to the accumulation of SETBP1 protein (Fig. 2A). As SGS-causing mutations alter the sequence of the degron motif, we aimed to explore the stability and half-life differences between control and SGS-associated SETBP1 proteins. Control A1 and SGS1 NPCs were treated with protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) for up to 144 h and lysates were collected every 24 h (Fig. S7A). SETBP1 protein from Control A1 was quickly degraded, with very low detectable signal after 24 h, indicating that its half-life is well under 24 h. SETBP1 expressed from SGS1 is detectable even after exposure to CHX for 144 h, indicating that it has a much longer half-life. To better assess the half-life of WT and SGS SETBP1, all control lines were treated with CHX for up to 6 h with hourly lysate collection, while SGS lines were treated for up to 144 h with daily lysate collection. WT SETBP1 assessed from CHX treatment on control lines indicates that WT SETBP1 half-life is relatively short (Control A1 t1/2 = 3.35 ± 1.41 h and Control A2 t1/2 = 2.69 ± 1.45 h) (Fig. 2B–D). This is supported by the fact that SETBP1 contains 6 PEST sequences, which characterize proteins with short half-lives [17, 34]. SGS SETBP1 cells had a much longer half-life, albeit with wide variation (SGS1 t1/2 = 86.94 ± 8.69 h and SGS2 t1/2 = 39.07 ± 11.24 h) (Fig. 2E–G). The slow but progressive reduction of SETBP1 observed in CHX-treated SGS samples indicates that SETBP1 SGS mutations are capable of being degraded via the proteasome but that this process is very inefficient compared to controls. CHX assessment of Control B1 and SHD1 indicates that there are no differences in the half-life of Control B1 (t1/2 = 2.10 ± 0.32 h) and SHD1 (t1/2 = 2.11 ± 0.10 h) SETBP1 (Fig. 2H–J), as expected since SHD cells have an intact degron.

SETBP1 inhibits PP2A activity in non-CNS cancer cells [12, 27, 29, 35, 36]. To provide independent validation of endogenous SETBP1 interacting with SET and PP2A in a neural context, we performed an IP in healthy forebrain NPCs followed by Mass Spectrometry (MS; Fig. 3A). We screened all proteins that had a ratio to the IgG control of ≥ 3:0 or ≥ 10:1, resulting in 275 proteins that may interact with SETBP1 (Table S5). The IP resulted in an endogenous SETBP1 pullout ratio of 3:0 compared to the negative control, indicating a successful IP. We detected a robust signal for SET (13:0), PP2P2R1A (5:0), as well as other proteins previously known to associate with SET or the PP2A complex. These include ANP32A and ANP32B (8:0 and 5:0 respectively) which are potent PP2A inhibitors similar to SET [37–39], and part of the SET complex. We detected several members of the SET complex for DNA damage [40] including XRCC proteins Ku80/Ku70 family members (XRCC5/6) at 35:1 and 18:1, and APEX1 at 4:0. The detection of these known interactors of SET suggests that SETBP1 interacts with similar complexes in NPCs as it does in myeloid progenitors. Since our IP was for SETBP1 and not SET, these data support a model whereby SETBP1 acts as a scaffold for SET to associate with several interacting proteins. This may support a model whereby SETBP1 functions as a platform to speed reactions of proteins with acidic amino acid-rich protein tails (D and E amino acids) such as ANP32 and SET.

To confirm that SETBP1 associates with PP2A, we performed co-IPs for SETBP1 and the PP2A catalytic subunit PP2AC (PPP2CA gene) since SETBP1 protein has three predicted PP2A binding sites (LxxZxE; Z = hydrophobic AA such as I, L, V) at AAs 853–859, 954–960, and 1197–1203 (UniProt: Q9Y6X0). We also verified if there is an interaction with ANP32A (aka I1PP2A) and SET (aka I2PP2A), which are also components of the INHAT complex [41] and the SET repair complex [29]. Using tagged expression constructs in HEK293 cells, we carried out co-IPs between SETBP1-Flag and Myc-tagged SET, ANP32A, and PP2AC. Our analysis revealed a robust binding between SETBP1-Flag/SET-Myc and SETBP1-Flag/ANP32A-Myc, and a weaker interaction between SETBP1-Flag/PP2A-Myc (Fig. 3B). This data confirms the known interaction between SET and SETBP1 and provides empirical support that SETBP1 will likely also be an important regulator of PP2A function.

Christobal et al. previously reported that the mechanism of action of SETBP1 on SET is to stabilize SET and prevent its degradation [27]. To assess this model, we first attempted to validate commercially available SET antibodies to determine the effects of SETBP1 dosage on SET. We know already that our SETBP1 antibody is valid for WB given that it detects band intensities consistent with gain and loss of SETBP1 protein levels in different individuals (Fig. 1). We conducted co-transfections involving Myc-tagged SET and Flag-tagged SETBP1 in HEK293 cells to facilitate the detection of these proteins using independent and verified protein-tag antibodies. Verification of SETBP1-Flag overexpression was achieved through the detection of a band at approximately 170 kDa following Flag immunoblotting, alongside specific detection of SETBP1 using a Proteintech antibody (Fig. 3C). For SET, the Abcam SET antibody is capable of detecting both endogenous SET at 37 KDa and the slightly heavier SET-Myc under transfected conditions. Over-expression reflects transfection efficiency, which is somewhat low in these experiments, where ~ 40%–50% of cells are transfected. The Santa Cruz SET antibody also demonstrates specific detection of SET-Myc and endogenous SET at the expected molecular weights. Co-transfection of both SET and SETBP1 leads to a decrease in their expression levels compared to individual transfections as evidenced by lower band intensities in co-transfected lanes, likely an artifact of transfection using identical plasmids with different constructs.

We assessed the levels of endogenous SET protein within the SHD and SGS cohort of NPCs, aiming to investigate whether increased and decreased levels of SETBP1 is reflected in SET protein levels, where a positive correlation is expected. Tests with Abcam and Santa Cruz SET antibodies showed highly variable expression of SET protein, inconsistent with the expected SETBP1 stabilization of SET in NPCs (Fig. 3D, Fig. S8A and B). SET levels may be highly variable in NPCs and might be regulated by multiple mechanisms. This might reflect protein stability of SET itself. Still, the lack of correlation between SETBP1 levels and SET in SGS and SHD samples argues against a simple stabilization model of SET by SETBP1, given that SETBP1 levels are detectable in SGS and SHD.

These findings prompted us to repeat as closely as possible the experiments conducted by Christobal et al. [27] to understand SETBP1 stabilization of SET. We replicated the design as closely as possible using the same cell line, experimental conditions, and vector, albeit employing a Flag-tag instead of a GFP-tag. The SET antibody used by Christobal et al. [27] was discontinued (sc-5655) and the antibody available to replace it (sc-133 138) was used instead. We did not observe cleavage of SET in our assay (Fig. 3E). Interestingly, we have been able to observe SET fragmentation in one SGS NPC cell line in triplicate experiments (Fig. S8C). In this single experiment, a SET cleavage pattern similar to Christobal et al.’s finding was observed, where increased SETBP1 protein appears to stabilize SET (37 kDa form) and reduce the levels of the low molecular weight form of SET (25 kDa). However, we cannot rule out that these lower weight bands are non-specific or appear only in certain cell states. Further research needs to be performed to ascertain the relationship between SETBP1 and SET.

To visualize SETBP1 in cells we performed immunocytochemistry (ICC) in NPCs. Repeated attempts to investigate SETBP1 endogenous location in the cells failed due to the lack of specificity of commercial SETBP1 antibodies to assess endogenous SETBP1 localization in the cell by ICC (Fig. S9). We detected no change in signal intensity in SGS or KO NPCs using three anti-SETBP1 antibodies even though these are drastically different and consistent with patient genotype when measured on WB. To circumvent this problem, we engineered tagged SETBP1 constructs. Co-labelling of tagged SETBP1 (eGFP-P2A-SETBP1-HA) with ANP32A (Fig. 3F, Fig. S10A) or with SET using a different SETBP1 construct (HA-SETBP1-miniGFP) (Fig. 3G, Fig. S10B), revealed that SETBP1 exhibited a punctate-like staining exclusively in the nucleus reminiscent of nuclear bodies or lamina-associated domains, while ANP32A and SET revealed exclusively nuclear staining but not as puncta.

Both SET [14, 26, 27, 31, 36] and ANP32A [39] are inhibitors of PP2A, indicating the potential for SETBP1 to inhibit PP2A through two different proteins. We hypothesized that increased SETBP1 dose (SGS) would cause hyper inhibition of PP2A activity (amplification of wild-type function), leading to hyperphosphorylation of a known target downstream of PP2A, phospho-AKT (Fig. 4A). Decreased SETBP1 dose (SHD) might then result in reduced PP2A inhibition (loss of wild-type SETBP1 function), leading to increased activity of PP2A and so less phospho-AKT (Fig. 4B). PP2A cleaves a phosphate group from AKT at Thr308 [42, 43] and influences AKT dephosphorylation at Ser473 which is cleaved by PHLPP [42, 44, 45]. We observed a significant ~ 5-fold increase in AKT pThr308 (P = 0.0007) and a 1.5-fold increase in AKT pSer473 (P = 0.1275) in SGS cases compared to controls in NPCs (Fig. 4A, Fig. S11). This is consistent with a more direct effect of PP2A on Thr308 than Ser473. When looking at a commonly activated downstream target of AKT, rpS6 [46, 47], we found a significant ~ 1.5-fold increase in its phosphorylation at Ser240/244 (P = 0.0126), supporting a model whereby increased SETBP1 increases the AKT cascade generally. Upon examining AKT pThr308 in SHD lysates compared to controls, we noted a trend towards decreased pThr308 and pSer473 which was similar for KO1 (Fig. 4B. Fig. S11). However, in KO2, where SETBP1 is almost completely absent, an absence of both AKT pThr308 and AKT pSer473 was observed. For phosphorylation of rpS6, we observe no difference in SHD but decreases in both KO1 and KO2. The almost complete absence of phospho-AKT when SETBP1 is absent suggests that the protein dose of SETBP1 in SHD is not low enough to deactivate AKT via PP2A. This may be consistent with a non-linear, or threshold, model of SETBP1 protein dose on AKT activation, in that too high or too low extremes are enough to influence its (de)activation but a range of SETBP1 levels around a mean is tolerated with respect to AKT activation, including 50% loss of SETBP1. RpS6 phosphorylation dynamics appear to mimic expectations in high and very low SETBP1 at the extreme doses, though the effect is less pronounced than for phospho-AKT. This suggests other layers of control of phosphorylation and dephosphorylation of rpS6 that do not solely rely on AKT activation state.

To confirm that SETBP1 was operating through PP2A to affect AKT activation, we aimed to reverse the effects of SGS AKT activation using a PP2A activator drug that operates through SET. We selected FTY720 because it activates PP2A by binding to amino acids Arg71 and Glu111 in SET, thereby disrupting SET’s dimerization/oligomerization and capacity to inhibit PP2A [27, 48–50]. We administered increasing doses of FTY720 (1, 2.5, and 5 μM for 24 h) to both control (Fig. 4C) and SGS NPCs (Fig. 4D, Fig. S12). We observed a dose-dependent reduction of AKT pThr308, which was further validated in all SGS NPCs at 5 μM, demonstrating a significant reduction in both AKT pThr308 and AKT pSer473 (Fig. 4E, Fig. S12). Some changes in SETBP1 dose occasionally occurred following FTY720 treatment, but these weren’t statistically significant (Fig. S12C). This finding indicates that SETBP1 effects on AKT phosphorylation levels are operating at least in part via PP2A. We did not observe differences in pSer240/244 levels of rpS6 following FTY720 treatment. This discrepancy in AKT and rpS6 response to the drug suggests that SETBP1 may exert additional effects within the cell that influence rpS6 phosphorylation, unrelated to its role in PP2A regulation.

Lysates obtained from SGS, SHD and their respective matched control NPCs were also assayed for phosphorylation at Tyr307 within PP2A itself, a PP2A inactivation mark. We suspected that SGS cells might show increased levels of this mark, and SHD cells would show lower levels (Fig. S13, Fig. S14). No consistent differences were observed in the levels of PP2A pTyr307 in SGS or SHD lysates compared to their respective controls. Additionally, treatment with FTY720 did not alter PP2A pTyr307 levels; however, antibodies used to detect phospho-PP2A are known to be unsuitable to study PP2A modifications or assessing PP2A activity or holoenzyme composition [51, 52], providing an explanation for these data. Finally, we assayed PP2A activity, but this did not reveal any changes relative to SETBP1 dosage (Fig. S15). This activity assay involves an IP for PP2A which does not IP SET or SETBP1, so we suspect that activity may look normal in the assays that do not contain SET or SETBP1 but may still be abnormal in SGS or SHD NPCs. Changes in protein dose are not expected to alter enzymatic activity per se and thus are not valid for this type of assay.

AKT activation is known to drive cell proliferation, and so we next asked whether SGS and SHD cells showed opposite effects on cell proliferation. We assayed NPC proliferation over 3 days and observed an increase in proliferation in SGS cells but no changes in proliferation patterns of SHD or KO cells (Fig. 4F, Fig. S16, Fig. S17). FTY720 treatment reduced the increased proliferation phenotype seen in SGS1 cells and reduced proliferation in Control A1 cells (Fig. 4F, Fig. S16B), again suggesting that the effect is due to a PP2A-based mechanism. These findings suggest that increased inhibition of PP2A signaling likely contributes to an increased growth rate in SGS NPCs due at least in part to increased phospho-AKT, while their downregulation does not necessarily produce the opposite effect. This discrepancy could be attributed to the 5–10-fold greater impact of increased SETBP1 protein on downstream signaling and functional cellular outcomes, such as proliferation rate, which may not be fully mirrored when SETBP1 is reduced by half or nearly absent. It is reasonable to imagine that increased inhibition of a phosphatase like PP2A (SGS case) might have very different effects on its activation than losing inhibition of that same phosphatase.

To further validate the involvement of the PP2A and/or AKT signaling pathway in SGS and SHD disease models, we investigated additional potential PP2A or AKT targets. We chose FOXO3A [53] and β-Catenin [54] because they are known to play roles in neurodevelopment [55–60]; and Lamin A/C [61] because it is reportedly a direct target of PP2A action by a SET-mediated mechanism [61]. While our intention was to evaluate phosphorylation levels of these proteins at sites reported to be PP2A or AKT targets, we quickly discovered that the total protein levels of these proteins were affected in a mostly reciprocal way. It is difficult to evaluate phosphorylation status when total protein levels change, though we did attempt this (Fig. S18); however, it was immediately obvious to us that a more fundamental mechanism with respect to SETBP1 protein dosage may be operating. We note that the protein levels of common endogenous controls such as β-Actin and HSP90 are essentially balanced across samples, and at the very least their minimal variation cannot explain the large effect sizes observed for the other protein targets (Fig. S18, Fig. S19). SGS NPCs show increased protein levels of β-Catenin, and decreased levels of LMNA/C, while levels of FOXO3A were more variable. (Fig. 5A, Fig. S18A). SHD cells largely show the opposite effect, where levels of β-Catenin were decreased, and LMNA/C and FOXO3A levels were increased (Fig. 5B, Fig. S18B and C). To ensure LMNA/C results were not a more global problem related to nuclear envelope (NE) dynamics, we also included the lamin protein LMNB1. We found that LMNB1 levels don’t substantially change between SGS and SHD conditions, indicating that the effects are not indiscriminately occurring for all lamins (Fig. S19A and B).

We wanted to manipulate one other pathway in attempt to see if we could alter protein levels of targets initially chosen as AKT/PP2A targets. There is growing evidence of an association between SET and the tumor suppressor p53 [62], though we did not detect p53 in our Mass Spec following IP of endogenous SETBP1 (Table S5). SET [28, 62, 63] and ANP32 [64] have been shown to inhibit p53 by preventing its acetylation, an activating mark of p53 [65], so in this sense may form an inhibitory matrix with SETBP1 similar to the case with PP2A. P53 has been shown to increase FOXO3A [66], stabilize Lamin A/C [67] and decrease β-Catenin [68], so we hypothesized that this could occur via SET/SETBP1. P53 is important in stem cell maintenance and brain development [69] and it has been shown to regulate neural differentiation [70]. Thus, it is possible that the inhibitory effect of SETBP1/SET on p53 might contribute to the observed protein changes in FOXO3A, β-Catenin, and LMNA/C in SGS and SHD NPCs.

To investigate whether the inhibition of p53 by SETBP1-SET underlies these protein changes, we conducted an IP to confirm the interaction between SET and p53, which we observe in human NPCs (Fig. 5C) and HEK293 cells (Fig. S19C). To identify an output marker of p53 inhibition in human NPCs, we treated NPCs with Pifithrin-α hydrobromide (PFT), a p53 inhibitor, and measured the response of CDKN1A, a well-known responder to p53 [71, 72]. We observed that CDKN1A levels were reduced in response to PFT treatment confirming that CDKN1A is an output marker of p53 in NPC cells (Fig. 5D). We then evaluated the levels of CDKN1A in SGS, SHD and KO cells to determine if CDKN1A levels reflect SETBP1 dosage changes. We reasoned that if CDKN1A levels show reciprocal changes in SHD and SGS, this might suggest that β-Catenin, FOXO3A, and LMNA/C may be regulated via p53. We found no differences in CDKN1A levels in SHD cells but found a strong increase in CDKN1A levels in SGS cells and KO2 cells (Fig. 5E, Fig. S19A and B). This might suggest that increased levels of SETBP1 in SGS are having an effect on CDKN1A independent of p53 action. This could be occurring through the reported direct action of SET binding to CDKN1A [73]. These data suggest that the reciprocal protein changes observed for LMNA/C, β-Catenin and FOXO3A in SETBP1 protein dosage models are unlikely to be operating through p53. Observing reciprocal effects for SGS and SHD on proteins that we had chosen as PP2A and P53 targets suggests a more fundamental cellular property is at play.

Our investigation of reciprocal effects of SETBP1 began by an assessment of PP2A activity, yet our results have revealed a more general dysfunction than was originally anticipated. To identify other pathways that may be affected by SETBP1 in NPCs, we performed a Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on SETBP1 interacting proteins from the Mass Spec IP (Table S5) and found enrichment of several biological processes, including nuclear/cytoplasmic transport and nuclear membrane, (Fig. 6A, Table S6). Using STRING DB network analysis, we visualized interactions between SETBP1 interacting proteins, highlighting pathways of interest (Fig. S20). One pathway of interest, ‘Nucleocytoplasmic transport’ (Fig. 6B), included proteins such as XPO1, LMNA, ELAVL1, IPO9, NUP210, RAN, and NPM1.

The strong IP signal for LMNA (11:0) and LMNB1 (26:0) as well as other nuclear envelope proteins including IPO9, XPO, NPM1 together with DNA structural proteins NAP1L1, NAP1L4, HIST1H4, HIFX, HIST1H3A, and H2AFZ from the endogenous SETBP1 IP in NPCs suggest that SETBP1 may have a structural role connecting the nuclear lamina to chromatin perhaps similar to lamina-associated domains [74]. SETBP1 distribution in ICC images suggested a punctate-like staining reminiscent of nuclear bodies or multimeric SETBP1 bodies, ie. clusters of SETBP1 together. After ruling out SETBP1 association with PML bodies (Fig. 6C) [75], where we did not observe overlap, we then assessed whether SETBP1 interacts with nuclear-envelope proteins predicted from the IP-Mass Spec. We chose to first validate one target, XPO1, since XPO1 and SETBP1 are already reported to interact [76]. We performed an IP in HEK293 cells between Flag-tagged SETBP1 and Myc-tagged XPO1 and validated our endogenous MS result (Fig. 6D). Next, to determine if SETBP1 interacts with the nuclear lamina, we conducted an IP between SETBP1 and Lamin A/C, which we observed (Fig. 6E). Indeed, transfection of SETBP1-HA into HEK293 cells and stain for LMNA/C revealed SETBP1 punctate though these appeared distributed in the nucleus (Fig. 6F, Fig. S21A).

The nuclear lamina is composed of lamins and it is a matrix of these proteins adjacent to and on the inside of the nuclear envelope. As such, the nuclear lamina is a 3D structure. To visually assess whether SETBP1 punctate were near the lamina, we took confocal images to capture SETBP1 and LMNA in the same plane. In one representative video we demonstrate that SETBP1 has a distributed pattern in the top and the bottom of cells consistent with association near the lamina, while it is near or within LMNA in the middle of cells (Supplemental Media file 1). This is consistent with SETBP1 punctate associating with the nuclear lamina around the periphery of a cell. We selected one confocal plane to demonstrate this in 2D (Fig. 6G), where SETBP1 punctate preferentially associate with the LMNA-containing nuclear periphery. Engineering GFP into endogenous LMNB1 in SGS and control cells revealed no gross distortions in nuclear envelope size or shape in patient NPCs during induction from iPSCs (Fig. S22A and B), suggesting that SETBP1 is not causing major nuclear envelope structural disruption.

In this study, we characterized two SETBP1-associated neurodevelopmental disorders as protein dosage disorders by comparing and contrasting their effects on a known downstream pathway in NPCs. The SETBP1 syndromes, caused by too much or too little SETBP1 represent a reasonable model with which to evaluate how different but clinically relevant protein doses might influence downstream pathways. We first assessed a PP2A-AKT axis and found reciprocal dosage effects but soon realized that there were major increases and decreases in other proteins reciprocally consistent between SGS and SHD, pointing to some more global function of SETBP1. This prompted us to assess SETBP1 interaction proteins and to visualize SETBP1.

We characterized SETBP1 in the nucleus and discovered that it has a punctate-like appearance and associates with the nuclear lamina. The punctate-like appearance may suggest SETBP1 forms oligomers with itself, associates with DNA through its three AT-hook domains, and associates with the nuclear lamina, reminiscent of lamina-associated domain proteins (LADs) [77]; however, SETBP1 does not have a typical LEM domain, which LAD proteins must have. Furthermore, LAD proteins are associated with gene repression, which is not what has been reported with respect to SETBP1/chromatin interactions [11].

We favour a model where SETBP1 is an organizer of higher order chromatin structure and does this in cooperation with the nuclear lamina. SETBP1 may have an important role in nuclear envelope assembly and breakdown, where PP2A is also important, including how chromatin gets re-organized before and after cell division. There are several domains in SETBP1 for cell cycle regulators, which might suggest SETBP1 is degraded and resynthesized during the cell cycle, possibly explaining its critical need to be degraded and its short half-life. For example, SETBP1 has two RXXL domains at AAs 625–633 and 947–955 (UniProt Q9Y6X0), which are important for APC/C degradation. SETBP1 also has several CDK docking sites (AAs 39–44, 503–508, 563–568, and 1545–1550). Additionally, SETBP1 has an HCF1 docking site at AAs 991–994, where HCF1 is a cell cycle regulator. We propose that SETBP1 is a hub for nuclear envelope re-assembly and 3D chromatin organization. It may nucleate re-assembly of the nuclear envelope after cell division and structurally associate with both DNA and the nuclear periphery to re-establish appropriate 3D chromatin states. The robust and reciprocal effects of SGS and SHD SETBP1 mutations on LMNA/C protein levels might support a model where SETBP1 has direct influence on nuclear envelope breakdown and reassembly, or stabilisation of LMNA/C, perhaps by regulating PP2A action on LMNA/C.

Protein dosage and degradation in our SETBP1 model can be contextualized for SGS and SHD. If SETBP1 does oligomerize (the reason we see the punctate like appearance) and attach to chromatin at particular regions in relation to the nuclear lamina, total amount of SETBP1 may be important. Loss of SETBP1 might lead to not enough SETBP1 bodies forming on chromatin which might affect gene expression in all these locations. Conversely, not enough breakdown of SETBP1, in the SGS case, might lead to too many SETBP1 bodies forming on chromatin and not breaking down at precise times during the cell cycle. This might have profound effects on gene expression such as the observations here, but also reported by others [28]. We don’t view these SETBP1 bodies as exclusive from other SETBP1 interactions including those with the SET or INHAT complexes. Indeed, we suspect our discovery adds meaning to those interactions in that they may occur at the SETBP1 nuclear bodies. Proteins such as SET, for example, might function to block SETBP1/DNA interactions given that two of the SETBP1 AT-hooks are in the SET-binding domain. In short, rather than being seen as a mere ‘SET-binding protein’, SETBP1 might be a critical driver of nuclear assembly and/or higher order chromatin dynamics that allows other complexes such as INHAT or SET to associate with DNA via a SETBP1 scaffold.

There are some limitations to our work that warrant follow up in future studies. First, we were unable to visualize endogenous SETBP1, so visualization was done with a transfected and tagged SETBP1 without using live cell imaging. Since we tagged other relevant proteins in the way described in this work and do not get similar nuclear staining, we think this pattern is specific to SETBP1. Still, new antibodies that can unambiguously detect SETBP1 via ICC and show the protein differences that define SHD and SGS are needed. Second, it is necessary to use live cell imaging to observe cell division in real time to understand whether SETBP1 has a role in nucleating reformation of the nuclear envelope and re-establishing 3D chromatin structure. Finally, a role for SETBP1 in higher order chromatin structure can be ascertained via HiC experiments involving SETBP1, chromatin, and the nuclear lamina.
