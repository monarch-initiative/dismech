OBJECTIVES: One-third of patients diagnosed with dermatomyositis harbour an 
occult cancer, a rare condition named cancer-associated dermatomyositis (CAD). 
Emerging evidence suggests that genetic alterations in autoantibody-related 
genes give rise to neoantigens and specific autoantibodies that initiate an 
autoimmune response characteristic of CAD. This study evaluated the prevalence 
of such genetic alterations and elucidated molecular mechanisms that may 
underlie their role in triggering autoimmunity.
METHODS: Fifteen patients with CAD were included in the study. We conducted 
genomic and transcriptomic analyses of available leukocyte (germline), muscle, 
skin, and tumour samples. T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoire profiling and 
multiplex immunophenotyping were performed to characterise immune responses in 
tumoural and nontumoural tissues.
RESULTS: Somatic mutations and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in 
autoantibody-related genes as tripartite motif containing 33, tripartite motif 
containing 66, MORC family CW-type zinc finger 3 (MORC3), Chromodomain Helicase 
DNA Binding Protein 4, and IFIH1, interferon-induced helicase C 
domain-containing protein 1-corresponding to known myositis-specific 
autoantibodies Anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1 gamma, anti-nuclear 
matrix protein 2, (anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1 gamma 
(anti-TIF1γ), anti-nuclear matrix protein 2 (anti-NXP2), anti-Mi2, antimelanoma 
differentiation-associated gene 5 protein), were detected in the majority of 
tumours from patients with CAD (75%). Tumour-specific missense mutations gave 
rise to highly immunogenic neoantigens, while LOH resulted in enriched allelic 
expression not observed in nontumoural lesions (skin and muscle). 
Immunophenotyping revealed a clonally expanded lymphocyte population within 
tumours, but not in nontumoural lesions, suggesting a distinct cellular immune 
response.
CONCLUSIONS: We detected highly frequent genetic alterations in 
autoantibody-related genes, supporting their role in the CAD pathogenic 
mechanisms. Moreover, our findings suggest that distinct TCR repertoire and 
immune signatures between tumoural and nontumoural tissues may underlie 
divergent cancer and dermatomyositis outcomes.

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) encompass a heterogeneous group of autoimmune disorders impacting multiple organs and systems, including muscles, skin, lungs, and joints [1]. Dermatomyositis (DM), a prominent phenotype within IIM, is notably associated with cancer. Epidemiological studies define cancer-associated myositis as the occurrence of both cancer and myositis within a 3-year period [2]. The exact mechanisms linking cancer and myositis remain unclear; however, previous research in DM and related autoimmune diseases, such as scleroderma, suggests that somatic mutations in the tumours of these patients may contribute to disease pathogenesis [3–5].

Early studies on patients with paraneoplastic systemic sclerosis, characterised by inflammation in the skin and other organs driven by DNA-directed RNA polymerase III subunit RPC1 (anti-RPC1) autoantibodies, suggest a molecular mechanism whereby tumour cells somatic mutations in the POLR3A gene, which encodes the RPC1 protein, lead to the formation of highly immunogenic neoantigens [3]. These neoantigens trigger the production of antibodies that cross-react with nontumoural tissues, leading to autoimmune manifestations characteristic of the disease. In addition to POLR3A mutations, other tumours in patients with anti-RPC1-related scleroderma, however, did not exhibit somatic mutations; instead, they showed loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in the POLR3A locus [3], a well-known phenomenon that results in the loss of the mutated allele—and consequently, the loss of expression of the associated neoantigen—thereby promoting immune evasion. Since this foundational study, advancements in understanding the genetic and immunological mechanisms underlying paraneoplastic diseases have remained limited. We recently reported that tumours from patients with cancer-associated dermatomyositis (CAD) and anti-TIF1γ autoantibodies harboured somatic mutations and LOH in the TIF1γ-related gene, tripartite motif containing 33 (TRIM33) [4]. However, the prevalence of such alterations in TRIM33—as well as in other genes encoding known myositis autoantigens, including TRIM66, MORC3, chromodomain helicase DNA binding protein 4 (CHD4), and IFIH1 (corresponding to the anti-NXP2, anti-Mi-2, and antimelanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 protein [MDA5] autoantibodies, respectively)—is still poorly characterised. Moreover, no comprehensive multiomics studies have been conducted in CAD, and the underlying molecular mechanisms driving disease pathogenesis remain largely unexplored.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have changed the land-scape of cancer, mainly in melanoma and nonsmall cell lung cancer among other tumours. These monoclonal antibodies activate the immune system increasing the immune reaction against tumour cells. Patients with autoimmune disorders are currently excluded from receiving such therapies to avoid potential immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and worsening of the preexisting autoimmune condition. However, clinical data show ICIs as efficacious antitumor therapy to patients with common autoimmune disorders while causing frequent but only mild and controllable irAEs [6,7]. Despite the extensive review of clinical and therapeutic data and outcome, the molecular basis of these clinically relevant questions remains largely unexplored and an in-depth investigation into the underlying molecular features that could drive response and/or irAEs remain lacking.

In this study, we aim to build upon our previous findings by performing integrated genomic, transcriptomic, and immunophenotypic analyses of both tumoural and nontumoural tissues from patients with CAD.

Fifteen patients diagnosed with CAD at Vall d’Hebron University Hospital were included in the study. The diagnosis of DM was based on both the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology / American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) criteria (score >95) [8] and the European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) criteria [9]. Serum samples were tested for anti-MDA5, anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme (antiSAE), anti-Mi2, and anti-NXP2 autoantibodies using a commercial immunoblot assay (Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany), and for anti-TIF1γ using an in-house blot/Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) previously validated by immunoprecipitation [10].

A total of 38 biological samples from the 15 patients with CAD were available for multi-OMICs profiling and immunophenotyping. These included 15 tumour samples, 6 muscle lesions, and 2 skin lesions and 15 germline DNA. From these specimens, we generated whole-exome sequencing data (N = 33). Additionally, a subset of samples underwent whole-genome sequencing (N = 8), RNA sequencing (N = 6), and immunophenotyping (N = 12) for comprehensive immunogenomic profiling (Fig 1; Supplementary Table S1).

Whole-exome library preparation and sequencing were performed as described previously [11,12] using the ImmunoID NeXT platform. DNA was sequenced in paired-end format using NovaSeq instrumentation (Illumina) to a coverage depth of ≥300×. Alignment to the human genome (hs37d5) was performed using Burrow-Wheeler Aligner [13] (BWA v. 0.6.2) and Picard (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard, v.1.74), with quality score recalibration via the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v3.1).

WGS data were analysed using the SAREK pipeline (v3.3.2) as part of the nf-core framework [14]. Briefly, alignment was performed against the GRCh38 reference genome using BWA (v0.7.17-r1188) and variant calling was performed using GATK (v4.4.0.0).

Core single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and SNV quality metrics were generated using GATK’s HaplotypeCaller6. SNVs were stratified by their false-positive likelihood using the variant quality score recalibration module (GATK), in addition to inhouse SNV accuracy software. Miscalled variants were omitted using in-house SNV accuracy software, which employs a model informed by genomic context and sequence alignment data. MuTect [15] was used to call somatic SNVs and indels, and Var-dict [16] was used to call small somatic insertions or deletions (indels) less than 50 bp. Combined somatic SNV and indel calls were then analysed through a set of rigorously validated filters incorporating coverage, read quality, and positional features, along with the likelihood of presence in normal tissue.

We calculated the P value for observing the number of somatic mutations detected or a higher count using a Poisson-binomial distribution Discrete Fourier Transform and Convolutional Fast Fourier Transform (DFT-CFFTDFT-CFFT), with individual mutation probabilities derived from the somatic genetic variants identified in the 10,967 tumour samples of the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) PanCancer Atlas Study [17].

Allele-specific copy number analysis of tumors (ASCAT) (v3.1.3) was used to identify which regions of the tumour genome have gained or lost gene copies, while adjusting for tumour purity and ploidy. This algorithm accurately dissects allele-specific copy number profiles in solid tumours by accounting for tumour cellularity and nonaberrant (normal) cell admixture [18]. As a result, it allows for precise detection of genomic gains, losses, copy number-neutral events, and LOH.

Clonality of somatic mutations was assessed by calculating the cancer cell fraction (CCF), which indicates whether a given mutation is present in all tumour cells (clonal) or only in a subset (subclonal). This distinction provides valuable insights into the evolutionary dynamics and potential aggressiveness of the tumour. CCF was estimated using the formula:

CCF = VAF/(P·CT + (1—P)·CN), where VAF is the variant allele frequency, P is tumour purity, CT is the copy number at the mutation site in the tumour, and CN is the corresponding copy number in normal tissue (typically assumed to be 2 for autosomes). Variants with CCF > 0.9 were considered clonal, whereas those below this threshold were classified as subclonal.

Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) typing up to 4 places was carried out via the ImmunoID NeXT Platform, which employs probes targeting the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) reference sequences in the GRCh38 human reference genome. HLA LOH was called using DASH (Deletion of Allele-Specific HLAs), a machine-learning-based algorithm [19].

Nonsynonymous somatic DNA variants (SNVs, indels, and fusions) were used to call putative neoantigens, which were then verified using transcriptomic data. For each putative neoantigen, all peptides containing the event were generated; binding was assessed for each Class I HLA using NetMHC [20] and NetMHCpan [21], and immunogenecity scores were calculated as described in Calis et al [22].

Whole transcriptome sequencing of tumour RNA was performed as described in Abbott et al. Briefly, RNA sequencing was performed to a coverage depth of ≥192 million reads, with augmentation of T-cell receptor (TCR) regions (100-fold). Ribosomal reads were excluded, and transcripts were aligned using STAR [23]. Transcript expression was calculated and normalised with the aid of Salmon [24], and gene expression was derived using a proprietary tool, Expressionist (Personalis, Inc).

TCRβ clones were profiled using the ImmunoID NeXT RepertoireID. RepertoireID captures CDR3 regions and boosts TCR region transcriptome sequencing to over 100 times the standard ImmunoID NeXT transcriptome. TCR clones were identified using MiXCR. Clones containing frameshift mutations or premature stop codons in the CDR3 sequence were omitted, along with clones with alignment scores below 70 for V or J sequences.

Next-generation immunohistochemistry was used for the analysis and quantification of different T cell populations (regulatory, helper, cytotoxic, and total T cells) as previously published [25]. Antibodies and protocols are shared in Supplementary Table S1. Visiopharm was used to quantify each subpopulation, and ImageJ was used to assign virtual colours to the different stainings and merge them, providing images of the different biomarkers and their coexpressions (cyan/green = CD3+ FOXP3-CD8+; Blue = CD3+FOXP3-CD4+; Red/Pink = CD3+ FOXP3+).

The median age of the cohort was 63 years (ranging from 35 to 88), and 10 out of 15 patients were female (66.7%). Regarding tumour type, 6 patients (40%) were diagnosed with thoracic malignancies (5 small-cell lung cancer [SCLC], and 1 thymic carcinoma), 3 patients with gynaecological malignancies (1 squamous cervix carcinoma, 1 cervix adenocarcinoma, and 1 ovarian serous carcinoma), and 3 patients with gastrointestinal malignancies (1 colonic neuroendocrine tumour-NET, 1 rectum adenocarcinoma, and 1 stomach adenocarcinoma). Two patients presented with breast cancer, and 1 patient presented nasopharyngeal carcinoma (Supplementary Table S1). Two patients had localised tumours, and 1 patient was diagnosed with DM in a malignancy relapse. Data on patients’ cancer diagnoses, immunological profiles, and the timing of DM onset in relation to cancer diagnosis and treatment are summarised in Table 1.

Among the 12 patients with available genetic analysis, 5 patients were diagnosed with SCLC and the other tumour types were thymic carcinoma, squamous cervix carcinoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, colonic neuroendocrine tumour, stomach adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, and ovarian serous carcinoma (Table 1). Our analyses revealed a spectrum of mutations, copy number alterations (CNA), and gene fusions (Supplementary Fig S2) in cancer-associated genes listed on the OncoKB platform [26]. Mutations in the TP53 tumour suppressor gene were the most frequently observed genetic events, followed by alterations in genes such as CSMD3, NPIPB15, EP400, KMT2C, and KMT2D, among others (Supplementary Fig S1A). tumour mutation burden exhibited substantial variability, with a mean of 229.3 ± 86.86 nonsynonymous somatic mutations.

Genetic alterations in members of the C-VI subgroup of tripartite motif (TRIM) genes, specifically TRIM24 (TIF1α), TRIM28 (TIF1β/KAP1), TRIM33 (TIF1γ), and TRIM66 (TIF1δ), which all feature a C-terminal tandem plant homeodomain and bromodomain module [27], were the most common, as follows. The tumour sample from patient DM8, who was diagnosed with SCLC and detectable TIF1γ circulating antibodies, had a somatic mutation in TRIM33 (p. Y1072), a mutation previously linked to CAM [4] (Fig 2B). Also, patient DM16, who was diagnosed with stomach adenocarcinoma and detectable TIF1γ circulating antibodies, had another somatic mutation in TRIM33 (p.R440C). Tumours from other patients with anti-TIF1γ autoantibodies (DM1, DM12, DM17, DM18, and DM19) presented evidence of LOH in the TRIM33, TRIM24, and TRIM66 genes (Fig 3, Table 2), which together with the TRIM33, TRIM24, and TRIM28 forms the transcriptional intermediary factor 1 (TIF1) family proteins [28] (Supplementary Fig S3).

Patient DM15, who presented anti-NXP2 autoantibodies, was found to have a somatic mutation in MORC3, the gene encoding the NXP2 protein, and LOH in Microrchidia family CW-type zinc finger 2 (MORC2) (Fig 4), a gene closely related structurally to MORC3 (Fig 3, Table 1). Similarly, patient DM4, with anti-Mi2 autoantibodies presented focal amplifications in chromosome 12 that contains the CHD4 gene, coding for Mi2b (Fig 3). Patient DM3, with anti-SAE autoantibodies, presented a gain in the whole chromosome 19, the genomic region that contains the gene UMO1 Activating Enzyme Subunit 1 (SAE1) (Fig 3).

Lastly, patient DM2, who tested positive for antibodies against MDA5, was diagnosed with nasopharyngeal carcinoma that exhibited a previously unreported missense somatic mutation (p.D408H) in the gene encoding interferon-induced helicase C domain-containing protein 1 (IFIH1), which is responsible for producing MDA5 (Fig 2B).

In stark contrast, the frequency of somatic mutations in TRIM33, IFIH1, and MORC3 was approximately 10-fold higher in DM-associated tumours compared to large, publicly available pan-cancer datasets that are not enriched for DM. Specifically, mutations in these genes were present in 33.3% of DM-related tumours vs 3.4% of non-DM-associated tumours (368 of 10,967 samples) across 32 genomic studies in the 2Portal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/). Both Fisher’s exact test and binomial statistical analyses demonstrated a significant enrichment of these mutations in the DM-associated cohort relative to the pan-cancer background (odds ratio = 14, P < .001).

Consistently, the frequency of these DM-related mutations remained low across multiple tumour types in additional pan-cancer datasets not enriched for patients with DM. For example, in the very large Cosmic catalogue of somatic mutations in cancer (COSMIC) database, their combined mutation frequency was only 0.96% (4001 out of 416,935 tumour samples). Notably, mutation of the CHD4 gene, which encodes the Mi-2 autoantigen, showed a specific enrichment in uterine carcinoma in the cBioPortal dataset. In contrast, no somatic mutation enrichment signal was detected for any of the other DM-associated genes across non-DM-related tumour types.

Similar patterns were seen for somatic CNA; however, data on the frequency of allele-specific LOH were not available in large pan-cancer datasets. As an indirect assessment of LOH in DM-associated genes, we compared the mutant allele frequencies (MAF) between the 2 cohorts. A high somatic MAF is indicative of LOH. The average MAF of tumours from the DM cohort (Figs 2–4) was 0.75 (75%), suggestive of frequent LOH, whereas the average MAF in 1128 non-DM tumours was significantly lower (31%, t-test P < .001), suggesting that LOH is rare in this context. Specifically, only 2.5% of non-DM tumours exhibited a MAF higher than 0.59, which was the minimum observed among DM-associated tumours (Supplementary Table S2).

To further assess the likelihood of observing somatic mutations in autoantigen-encoding genes among patients with CAD, we compared the observed mutation frequency with the expected background mutation risk. Across the analysed cases, somatic mutations were identified in 4 out of 12 tumours in genes corresponding to known autoantibodies. Using reported baseline mutation frequencies for each gene, we calculated a cumulative P value of 1.3 × 10−5.

To determine when the IFIH1 and TRIM33 mutations occurred during tumorigenesis, we calculated the CCF based on variant allele frequency, copy number, and ploidy, as detailed in the Methods section. The CCF values for both IFIH1 and TRIM33 mutations were 1, strongly indicating that these mutations arose early, likely during tumour initiation. These findings are consistent with the co-occurrence of common driver mutations such as TP53, CSM3, and KMT2D, all of which also exhibited CCF values greater than 0.9, in line with their role in early tumorigenesis (Supplementary Table S1).

We conducted whole-exome sequencing (WES) on genomic DNA (gDNA) and paired tumour and muscle paraneoplastic lesions in 6 patients with CAD. Employing a stringent MAF cutoff of 5%, we detected somatic mutations in all sequenced muscle samples. The median number of mutations was 9.2 (ranging from 5 to 15) and a MAF ranging from 5% to 44% (Supplementary Fig S4A). The number of mutations observed in affected muscle lesions was significantly lower than that in tumours, with the number of mutations in tumours ranging from 297 to 2309 and MAFs from 5% to 98% (Supplementary Fig S4A). Importantly, no genetic events at the mutation or gene level were shared between paired tumour and muscle lesions from the same patient, except for a single instance where the FAM47B, a gene with largely unknown functions, carried mutations in both the tumour (p.P25L, MAF = 32%) and muscle (p.H246P, MAF = 6%) (Supplementary Fig S4B).

HLA typing at the germinal level was performed on all patients, and there was no enrichment of a specific typing in our cohort (Supplementary Fig S5A–B). Somatic HLA allele-specific copy number was also analysed, and one tumour showed evidence of LOH in the tumour sample from patient DM3 (Supplementary Fig S5C).

We characterised tumour-infiltration lymphocytes in 10 tumours by sequential multiplexed immunohistochemistry and grouped lesions into different categories according to the density of immune cells and the pattern of infiltration (Fig 5A, Supplementary Table S1). Two tumours were defined as ‘hot/inflamed’ based on a substantial intratumoural and peritumoral infiltration of T cells; 4 tumours were classified as ‘excluded’, indicating the presence of a considerable number of T cells at the interface between the tumour and peritumoral stroma but relatively few within the tumours themselves; another 4 tumours, all SCLCs, were categorised as ‘desert’, revealing a very low or complete absence of T cells both outside and inside the tumours (Fig 5B–D).

Immunophenotyping and RNAseq deconvolution analyses unveiled elevated infiltration of immune cells in the 2 skin lesions available for the study, from patients DM5 and DM10 (Fig 5B,C and 6A–F).

In our RNAseq data analysis, we observed significant variability in the TCR repertoire across both skin lesions and tumours. Approximately, half of the samples exhibited increased clonality, while the other half displayed a multitude of clones with relatively low frequencies (Fig 6A). Particularly striking was the divergent TCR repertoire in the paired skin lesion and tumour of patient DM5. The skin lesion exhibited a plethora of TCR clones (N = 106) with notably low frequencies (average 0.009, range 8e-5-0.049), whereas the tumour demonstrated a markedly lower number of TCR clones (N = 50) but with considerably higher frequencies (average 0.02, range 0.001-0.284). Importantly, 6 TCRs were shared between the skin and the tumour. Remarkably, all 6 of these TCRs were substantially enriched in the tumour, ranging from 4.16 to 45.35 times (Fig 6B–D).

This study reveals a high prevalence of genetic alterations in tumour cells affecting genes that encode DM-specific autoantigens in patients with CAD. These genetic alterations are exceedingly rare in databases like TCGA [17] and GENIE [29], suggesting that they could be specific to CAD. These findings suggest that such tumour-specific alterations may play a central role in initiating the autoimmune response, potentially through the generation of neoantigens that drive the production of myositis-specific autoantibodies. In addition, the identification of distinct immunogenomic profiles and tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte patterns provides further insight into the immunopathogenesis of CAD and supports a model of paraneoplastic autoimmunity driven by early oncogenic events.

In total, genetic alterations in CAD-related genes were highly frequent (75%), strongly suggesting that such genetic alterations may serve as the initiating pathogenic basis of the disease. This finding parallels the high frequency of POLR3A mutations and LOH observed in tumours from patients with scleroderma paraneoplastic disease and anti-RPC1 antibodies (75%) [3].

Interestingly, we identified somatic mutations in autoantigens corresponding to the detected autoantibodies in patient groups not typically associated with cancer, with anti-MDA5 serving as a notable example. Although most patients with anti-MDA5 DM are not paraneoplastic and our cohort overall did not demonstrate a strong association between anti-MDA5 autoantibodies and cancer, this particular patient exhibited a clear cancer association and harboured a mutation in the relevant autoantigen.

These findings suggest that distinct sources of molecular mimicry may give rise to the same autoantibody. We propose that in most patients from cancer-unrelated groups, such as anti-MDA5, autoantibodies emerge through molecular mimicry triggered by noncancer sources, including viral or bacterial antigens. By contrast, in a minority of patients—such as the case described here—they may instead result from tumour-derived mutations that generate neoantigens. For autoantibodies strongly associated with malignancy, such as anti-TIF1γ, the balance of mechanisms may be shifted towards tumour-derived sources, translating into an epidemiological association with cancer.

Based on the results in this study and previous research from our group and others, the current model of CAD disease suggests that tumour neoantigens induce myositis-specific antibodies (MSA) against the mutated neoantigen that then cross-react with the unmutated antigens found in other tissues, such as skin or muscle [10]. Consistent with this model, we identified somatic genetic events in CAD-specific genes such as TRIM33 and TRIM66 within the tumours. Importantly, our study extends this paradigm beyond anti-TIF1γ-positive patients by reporting, for the first time, a somatic mutation in the IFIH1 gene, which encodes the MDA5 protein, in a patient positive for anti-MDA5 antibodies (patient DM2, Table 1), as well as a somatic mutation in MORC3 in a patient with anti-NXP2 autoantibodies. The new mutation in anti-MDA5 is predicted to result in the overproduction of IFN-gamma according to conformational and functional studies of MDA5 protein. It is worth noting that gain-of-function mutations in the IFIH1MDA5 gene cause type 1 interferonopathies [30]. Our findings underscore the potential significance of IFIH1MDA5 mutations in triggering autoimmunity and in the development of CAD.

Moreover, recently published findings indicate that certain autoantibodies in myositis can be internalised into living cells [31,32], where they disrupt the function of their respective autoantigens, providing further evidence for a self-perpetuating cycle of the disease, particularly in patients with CAD. In this cycle, somatic mutations in tumours generate neoantigens, which trigger the production of myositis-associated autoantibodies by the immune system. These autoantibodies subsequently are internalised, targeting the corresponding wild-type antigens in affected tissues and disrupting the function of theirrespective autoantigens, thereby contributing to the characteristic autoimmune manifestations of the disease.

Our immunophenotyping analysis revealed heterogeneous immune infiltration patterns in tumours from patients with CAD, using T-cell (CD4, CD8, FOXP3), B-cell (CD20), and Programmed death-ligand 1 (PDL1) markers. As typical of autoimmune contexts, paraneoplastic lesions predominantly exhibited an ‘inflamed’ phenotype with dense immune infiltration (Fig 5B). TCR sequencing further demonstrated minimal clonal overlap between tumours and paraneoplastic lesions, suggesting a more selective T-cell infiltration in the tumour than in areas affected with paraneoplastic autoimmunity (Fig 6A–D and 7).

SCLC is an aggressively progressing cancer that is frequently associated with various paraneoplastic syndromes [33,34]. ICIs have gained approval for use in both first- and second-line treatments for SCLC [35,36]. Distinct immune profiles have been observed depending on the type of paraneoplastic syndrome. Notably, neurologic paraneoplastic syndromes were associated with higher CD3+ T-cell infiltration (88% vs 33%) and improved overall survival compared to endocrinologic cases, even under similar treatment regimens [37]. In our small cohort of 4 patients with SCLC with CAD, tumours showed ‘cold’ immune phenotypes, lacking immune infiltration and PDL1 expression.

Despite the risk of flare and irAEs, patients with preexisting common autoimmune disease can experience clinical benefit from ICI [38,39]. However, it is currently unclear whether patients with rare autoimmune diseases, such as CAD, could benefit from these treatments in a safe manner. Notwithstanding the limited number of patients included in our study, our immunophenotyping, RNAseq, and TCRseq data reveal that the immu- nogenomic profiles of the paraneoplastic lesions and tumours in the patients do not seem to greatly overlap but rather are composed of mutually exclusive somatic mutations and diverse TCR clones (Figs 5 and 6). This indicates that the autoimmune response to the muscle and skin, while likely triggered by genetic events at the tumour site, has evolved separately over time (Fig 7). Taking into consideration the distinct immune profiles, we observed in tumour and nontumoural tissues, patients with CAD could possibly benefit from the anticancer benefits of ICI without necessarily eliciting a major flare in the paraneoplastic lesions at least in some cases.

The present study, while offering valuable insights, must be interpreted within the context of its inherent limitations. Although this represents the largest cohort analysed at the molecular level to date, the modest sample size of 15 patients limits statistical power and constrains the generalisability of the findings. In addition, the expected mutation rates were derived from large publicly available datasets rather than sequencing data drawn from an identical epidemiologic background. Furthermore, the heterogeneous nature of the cancer types associated with CAD presents another natural limitation. The diverse range of cancer types, each with distinct molecular profiles and clinical characteristics, may introduce substantial variability in the results. Autoantibodies against TRIM24, TRIM28, and TRIM66—all members of the TIF1 family sharing relevant structural homology with TRIM33—were not tested, and these specificities were therefore excluded from the analysis assessing the association between cancer and myositis. The significance of these variants in relation to immune responses remains undetermined. Future research with larger independent cohorts of patients will be needed to validate our findings and generalise the observed outcomes.

In conclusion, the current study reports the largest cohort of patients with CAM with available multiomics data described to date. The results define tumour mutations and/or genetic imbalance in genes that codify for antigenic proteins, leading to MSA as the genetic basis of CAD. Furthermore, our molecular findings demonstrating distinct immune profiles in tumour and nontumoural tissues may support future investigations into the use of ICI in patients with CAD. The comprehensive data analysis presented here reveals that tumours and paraneoplastic lesions in patients with CAD may harbour divergent genetic and immunological characteristics.

These findings provide novel insights into the interplay between autoimmunity and tumour immunity in CAD, suggesting that while specific antigens may act as common triggers for the autoimmune response, the overall immunogenomic landscapes of these conditions remain largely distinct.

This growing body of evidence supports the role of somatic mutations as potential initiators of autoimmune disease and may guide future investigations into the use of ICIs as a therapeutic strategy for patients with autoimmune disorders.
