# Novel Compound Heterozygous Variants of DSP Causing Skin Fragility-Woolly Hair Syndrome: A Rare Case Report and Literature Review.
**Authors:** Yang M, Zhang G, Luo S
**Journal:** Case Rep Dermatol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000548001](https://doi.org/10.1159/000548001)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Desmoplakin is constitutive component of desmosome which plays a critical part in keratinocyte adhesion. Skin fragility-woolly hair syndrome (SFWHS) is a very rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by variants in desmoplakin (DSP).

CASE PRESENTATION: Herein, we described a 2-year-old Chinese boy who presented with palmoplantar keratoderma, short curly hair, and thickening of nails since birth. Histopathological examination showed hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, hypergranulosis, and prominent intercellular fissures. Two heterozygous variants in exon 24 of DSP, c.7642C>T (p.R2548X) and c.6449delC (p.R2151Gfs*3), were identified by whole-exome sequencing and further proven to be a novel compound heterozygous variant by Sanger sequencing. Both variants have not been identified and reported previously.

CONCLUSION: To the best of our knowledge, this is the second case of SFWHS with compound heterozygous variants in a Chinese population. This case adds new variant spectrum in DSP of SFWHS and highlights the need for genetic testing understanding in patients with characteristic cutaneous findings.

Skin fragility-woolly hair syndrome (SFWHS) characterized by palmoplantar keratoderma, woolly hair, and skin fragility is a very rare autosomal recessive syndrome linked to variants in desmoplakin (DSP) [ 1– 3 ]. Herein, we described a 2-year-old Chinese boy of SFWHS caused by two new compound heterozygous pathogenic variants of the DSP gene. The CARE Checklist has been completed by the authors for this case report, attached as online supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000548001).

A 2-year-old Chinese boy presented to our hospital with keratoderma and short curly hair since birth. The skin blistering occurred more during the hot days and frequently over the hands and feet. On physical examination, he had extensive woolly hair on scalp and yellowish hyperkeratotic plaques prominently on the palms, cubital fossa, soles, knee, and sacrococcygeal region (Fig. 1). On palms and soles, there was striate palmar keratoderma and diffuse thickened horniness over metacarpophalangeal joints, as well as nail plates of all fingernails and toenails (Fig. 1). There was not family history of sudden death. His parents and elder sister were unaffected. Electrocardiogram and echocardiogram findings in all family members were within normal limits.

Clinical features of this patient. a, b Tightly curled, slow-growing hair. Diffuse hyperkeratotic papules of the knee (c), cubital fossa (d), sacrococcygeal region (e), palm (f), and soles (g). h Thickening of the nail plates of the toe.

A skin biopsy from his palm showed hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, hypergranulosis, and prominent intercellular fissures. After obtaining informed consent, whole-exome sequencing was performed. The results revealed two heterozygous variants in exon 24 of DSP, which were identified by Sanger sequencing to be a combination of nonsense and frame shift variants, c.7642C>T (p.R2548X) and c.6449delC (p. R2151Gfs*3) (Fig. 2). Then, his father was identified to be a heterozygous carrier of c.7642C>T and his mother was a heterozygous carrier of c.6449delC. Both variants have not been identified and reported previously. Thus, a diagnosis of SFWHS was made based on above clinical features and genetic analysis. Subsequently, he was treated with skin moisturizers and keratolytics. The possibility that this syndrome may develop into cardiomyopathy is a potential risk, so he will continue to be monitored regularly.

Desmosomes are disc-like structures in cell-cell adhesion comprising two distinct domains predominantly expressed in epidermis and heart [ 4 , 5 ]. The extracellular domain consists of the heads of desmosomal cadherins which provide the intercellular connection, and the intracellular domain is composed of the tails of desmosomal cadherins and a group of cytoplasmic plaque proteins including desmoplakin, plakoglobin, and plakophilins [ 6– 8 ]. Desmoplakin is constitutive desmosomal plaque protein which plays a structural role in bridging cytoplasmic intermediate filament network and desmosomal cadherins [ 9 ]. Desmoplakin has three isoforms (DP-I, DP-II, and DP-Ia) derived from alternative spliced mRNA and have differential expression in keratinocytes and cardiomyocytes [ 10– 12 ]. Variants of desmoplakin are responsible for several congenital disorders affecting skin, nails, hair, and heart as either autosomal recessive or dominant inheritance such as Carvajal syndrome [ 13 , 14 ], lethal acantholytic epidermolysis bullosa [ 15 ], and type 8 arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy [ 16 , 17 ].

Variants in desmoplakin have already been proved to be associated with several distinct syndromes affecting skin, nails, hair, and heart as either autosomal recessive or dominant inheritance [ 8 , 18 ]. The heterogeneous desmoplakin-related phenotypes are due to the position and type of variant together with different expression and functional properties of the desmoplakin isoforms [ 1 , 9 , 16 ]. SFWHS usually presents with woolly hair, recurrent blisters, palmoplantar keratoderma, and nail dystrophy in the absence of cardiomyopathy [ 10 ]. The DSP variant c.7642C>T (p.Arg2548Ter; rs146296069) has already been reported as a pathogenic variant associated with arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia [ 11 , 19 ]. Although cardiac abnormalities are not be present in SFWHS, these patients may be also at an increased risk of cardiomyopathy liking other desmoplakin variant disorders. Reviewing most case reports of desmoplakin variant, the predilection age of cardiomyopathy ranges between 15 and 30 years [ 1 , 20 ]. Therefore, patients should be monitored regularly and genetic testing is necessary to confirm variant pattern and determine the risk for themselves and future offspring.

Either homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in DSP can cause SFWHS. Among 6 cases described in the literature (Table 1), four are compound heterozygous variants and two are homozygous variants. Interestingly, there are ten variant loci in total and six variant loci are in exon 24, suggesting this exon may be a variant hotspot of SFWHS. Among variants linked to SFWHS described in the literature, the most common locus located in exon 24 which is the inner desmosomal plaque region at the C-terminal of DSP [ 1– 3 , 21 ]. In our case, we found both nonsense variants in one allele and the frame shift variant in the other can form stop codon, which result in premature termination of protein translation in C-terminal tail, leading to truncated protein. The C-terminal tail is essential in the function of desmoplakin as it interacts with intermediate filament network. Consequently, structurally impaired desmoplakin causes the presentation of palmoplantar keratoderma, woolly hair, and other phenotypes seen in this patient.

In conclusion, the clinical evaluation of SFWHS should cover multiple aspects of patients and all family members. Recommended diagnostic approaches include skin biopsy and direct sequencing of all potential patients and carriers. To the best of our knowledge, this is the second case of SFWHS with compound heterozygous variants in Chinese population. Our case adds new variant spectrum in DSP of SFWHS and highlights the importance for gene examination in patients with similar findings.
