# Novel COL4A3-COL4A5 variants and digenic inheritance in pediatric Alport syndrome from Southwestern China.
**Authors:** Chen JY, Jiang XM, Liao YB, Zhang YH, Yang MF, Cui JJ, Wang J, Zhang J, Wang HY, Zhao B
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-025-17027-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-17027-9)

## Content

Alport syndrome is a hereditary glomerular disease driven by pathogenic variants in COL4A3-COL4A5 that compromise the α3-α4-α5 type IV collagen scaffold, manifesting as persistent hematuria, proteinuria, and ultimately end-stage renal disease. Its pronounced phenotypic variability, low sensitivity of renal biopsy, and limited response to ACE inhibitors complicate accurate diagnosis and therapy. In a cohort of 40 pedigrees from southwest China, we discovered 21 novel COL4A3-COL4A5 mutations. Notably, two families carried rare digenic COL4A4/COL4A5 variants, providing strong evidence for dual-locus pathogenicity. Immunofluorescence of five mutation-positive patients revealed allele-specific depletion of α3 and α5 chains in the glomerular basement membrane, with frameshift and splice-site mutations eliciting more severe collagen loss than missense. Complementary AlphaFold modeling predicted that digenic variants induce greater destabilization of Gly-X-Y triple helices domain than monogenic lesions. Over three years, ACEI therapy achieved only sporadic partial remissions, underscoring the need for precision-guided treatment strategies. This study not only expands the genetic spectrum of Alport syndrome in China's ethnic minority populations but also elucidates the mechanistic impact of digenic inheritance.

Alport syndrome (AS) is an inherited glomerulopathy resulting from mutations in type IV collagen genes encoding the α3-4-5 heterotrimer, leading to progressive kidney disease. Clinically, AS is characterized by persistent glomerular hematuria, proteinuria, and progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), with potential extrarenal manifestations1. As the second most prevalent monogenic nephropathy after autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, AS accounts for over 1% of kidney transplant recipients and exhibits age and ethnic disparities2. It contributes to 1–2% of ESRD cases in Europe and 2.3% in the United States3,4. Recent advancements in genetic sequencing have identified digenic mutations in type IV collagen and potential modifier genes in AS pathogenesis5–7. Current therapeutic strategies primarily involve angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) to delay ESRD progression8. However, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal, with limited treatment responsiveness and reduced life expectancy observed in ACEI-treated cohorts9.

AS exhibits clinical heterogeneity due to complex gene-environment interactions and unclear molecular mechanisms. Although X-linked Alport syndrome (XLAS) was historically considered the primary cause of ESRD risk, autosomal recessive AS (ARAS) also leads to complete ESRD penetrance in both sexes, but with differing age-dependent progression10. XLAS patients often present with early, nephrotic-range proteinuria and a high ESRD incidence (90% by age 40, median onset: 25 years)11,12. ARAS typically involves milder urinary abnormalities (microscopic hematuria or subnephrotic proteinuria) and later ESRD onset, especially in heterozygous carriers13,14. Digenic AS exhibits unique phenotypes intermediate between autosomal dominant AS (ADAS) and ARAS15,16. Notably, significant phenotypic differences exist among genotypes as the disease progresses, but the mechanisms behind these temporal disparities are unclear.

To date, hundreds of pathogenic variants causing AS have been identified. With the advancement of Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, rare digenic mutations and the role of modifier genes have been increasingly recognized in AS17–19. However, AS remains underreported in southwestern ethnic minorities, with few documented cases of digenic inheritance20,21. In our earlier studies, we focused on ARAS caused by overlooked compound heterozygous variants in COL4A3 or COL4A4, employing comprehensive diagnostic approaches22. In this study, we investigated patients presenting with hematuria, proteinuria, and steroid-sensitive or -resistant nephrotic syndrome, and identified 40 AS families through integrated clinical and genetic analysis. We applied genomics, clinical diagnostics, and in silico structural modeling to improve diagnostic precision, define genotype-phenotype correlations, and characterize underlying mutations. These findings contribute to expanding the mutational spectrum of AS and support the development of precision medicine approaches for underrepresented populations.

All sample collection procedures were approved by the Kunming Children’s Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No.2022-03-02-K01) and conducted according to institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Medical records of pediatric patients diagnosed with Alport syndrome between September 2018 and December 2024 were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic system. All data were anonymized to ensure patient confidentiality. Blood samples from children and parents, clinical leftover specimens, and biological samples from Kunming Children’s Hospital were sent to Beijing MyGenostics for whole-exome sequencing (WES) sequencing. Residual renal biopsy specimens from diagnostic procedures and archived samples from the Children’s Research Institute Biobank, with prior ethical approval, were also used. All samples were collected with informed consent from patients and their families.

Clinical parameters were retrospectively obtained via medical records and telephone interviews. Data included demographics, family history (3-generation pedigree analysis), renal function, audiology/ophthalmologic evaluations, and urinalysis (hematuria/proteinuria severity).

Genomic DNA was fragmented and ligated to Illumina PE adapters for library preparation. Target regions were enriched via hybridization to capture probes and amplified by linker-mediated PCR. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform. Raw data underwent image processing and sample demultiplexing.

Genomic DNA extracted from peripheral blood using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). One to three micrograms of DNA was sheared to approximately 150 bp fragments using a Covaris S220 Focused-ultrasonicator. Libraries were constructed using the MyGenostics DNA Sample Prep Reagent Set and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

Targeted enrichment was performed using the GenCap skeletal system gene panel (MyGenostics). Biotinylated 100 bp probes spanning coding exons and ± 50 bp flanking regions were hybridized with 500 ng of DNA library. Target regions were captured with streptavidin beads, washed, then PCR-amplified (13 cycles) and purified using SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter) prior to sequencing.

Sequencing reads were adapter-trimmed and filtered with Cutadapt, then aligned to the UCSC hg19 reference genome using BWA (Sentieon). Duplicates were removed and base qualities recalibrated. SNPs and InDels were called with Sentieon and annotated with ANNOVAR using 1000 Genomes, dbSNP, ExAC, and HGMD databases26. Copy number variations (CNVs) were detected with CNVkit.

All mutations detected by DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing were validated by Sanger sequencing. Genomic DNA from all available family members was collected for Sanger sequencing. Two pairs of primers (F: 5’-XXXXXXXXXX-3’; R: 5’-XXXXXXXXXX-3’) were designed to amplify the XXXXX region. PCR amplification was conducted using the following protocol: an initial denaturation step at 98 °C for 2 min, followed by 10 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 65 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 10 s. This was succeeded by 25 cycles at 98 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 10 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The purified PCR products (approximately XX bp) were sequenced using Sanger sequencing (Biosune), and the results were analyzed with DNASTAR (Madison) software (Supplementary Table S1).

Primary antibodies: mouse anti-human collagen IV α1/α3 [MAB1, MAB3] and rabbit anti-human α5 [MAB5] (Wieslab). Also: mouse anti-human α4 polyclonal [AB756P] (Sigma) at 1:50. Secondary antibodies: fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-goat anti-rabbit IgG and FITC-rabbit anti-mouse IgG at 1:100. Other reagents: pepsin K solution, H₂O₂, Phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBST), and anti-fade mounting medium.

Frozen kidney sections (4–6 μm) were thawed and fixed in acetone for five minutes. Sections were washed in PBST three times for five minutes each. Sections were digested with pepsin K for ten minutes. After PBST washes, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 1:50 for two hours or overnight at 4 °C. Sections were washed and incubated with secondary antibodies at 1:100 for one hour in the dark. Sections were washed, mounted with anti-fade medium, and coverslipped. Fluorescence was observed, and images were captured by a microscope.

Longitudinal monitoring included: urinalysis (proteinuria/RBC quantification), renal function (creatinine/eGFR-Schwartz), ophthalmic/auditory evaluations, with efficacy defined by proteinuria remission (complete: <150 mg/24 h + normal albumin; partial: ≥50% reduction or dipstick < 1+) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression (KDIGO 2024)27.

We analyzed 40 Alport syndrome families and characterized their clinical and molecular features. We identified 21 novel pathogenic COL4A variants, comprising 12 in COL4A5, 5 in COL4A3, and 4 in COL4A4. These included 26 XLAS, 12 autosomal AS, and two COL4A5/COL4A4 digenic families. XLAS phenotypes were more severe than ARAS and ADAS. Structural effects of novel mutations were predicted by AlphaFold and confirmed by immunofluorescence.

Among 40 probands, 26 were male and 14 were female. Mean onset age was 5.74 ± 3.72 years for males and 4.98 ± 3.47 years for females. No significant age difference was found (P = 0.52). Age at onset ranged from one to fourteen years. Pre-school onset (1–6 years) accounted for 65% of cases. Ethnically, 77.5% were Han Chinese and 22.5% were ethnic minorities (mainly Yi, Hui, Miao, and Hani). Hematuria with proteinuria was the main phenotype in 34 cases (85%). Pure hematuria occurred in six cases (15%). Six cases had gross hematuria, and ten had nephrotic-range proteinuria. One case presented with chronic renal failure. Extrarenal manifestations occurred in eight cases (20%). Seven had sensorineural hearing loss, and one had congenital cataract. Positive family history was observed in 22 families (55%) (Table 1).

Sequencing of probands and parents identified 21 novel COL4A3-COL4A5 pathogenic variants. Variants comprised eleven missense, five frameshift, three splice, and two nonsense mutations (Fig. 1). Two novel digenic variant combinations were discovered. These variants were absent from the HGMD, ClinVar, LOVD and PubMed databases (Table 2).

We compared XLAS (n = 25) with ADAS/ARAS (n = 12) groups, after excluding three families (P1, P2, and P14) with suspected digenic involvement. No significant differences in onset age or eGFR were observed (Mann-Whitney Test, P > 0.05). Proteinuria levels did not differ significantly between groups (Mann-Whitney Test, P = 0.59). Within XLAS (n = 28), patients were stratified by missense versus non-missense variants. Age at onset did not differ (Mann-Whitney Test, P = 0.21). The non-missense variant group had higher proteinuria than the missense group. Proteinuria was 1.21 ± 1.24 g/24 h versus 0.27 ± 0.16 g/24 h (Mann-Whitney Test, P = 0.01 < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

AlphaFold predicted structural impacts of digenic COL4A3-COL4A5 mutations. In the wild-type model, COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5 chains formed a stable triple-helix structure, with well-aligned Gly-X-Y repeats domain (Fig. 3A,B). Among the identified variants, COL4A5 p.P1314S and COL4A4 p.R1226C were located in close spatial proximity within the triple-helix domain (Fig. 3C,D). Structural predictions revealed that this digenic combination reduced local interchain hydrogen bonding and altered backbone geometry, leading to marked conformational destabilization. In contrast, COL4A5 p.G475D introduced a bulky aspartate residue, disturbing local helix packing via steric hindrance. The COL4A4 p.A832T variant had a relatively mild effect, introducing a polar residue that increased local flexibility but did not disrupt the overall helical configuration (Fig. 3E,F). Predicted structural defects correlate with severe clinical phenotypes.

All 40 AS patients received ACEI therapy; some also received prednisone or immunosuppressants and were followed for over three years. Only a few patients achieved laboratory remission after ACEI, steroids, or immunosuppressants. Of 21 patients receiving ACEI alone (captopril 1–2 mg/kg d), only three (P6, P12, P19) had partial remission (urinalysis <+). The remaining 18 patients had persistent hematuria or proteinuria. Among 12 patients on ACEI plus prednisone (1–2 mg/kg d), only one (P5) achieved partial remission in the first year. None of the other 11 patients achieved remission over a three-year follow-up. In ten patients on ACEI, prednisone, and tacrolimus (0.05–0.1 mg/kg d), two (P9, P32) had partial remission after one year. Their proteinuria remained < 0.5 g/24 h. However, P32 experienced proteinuria recurrence in the third year. Patient P33 (COL4A3-mutant ADAS, FSGS) treated with prednisone and cyclophosphamide, followed by prednisone and tacrolimus, had persistent proteinuria and frequent relapses. Rituximab (375 mg/m2) reduced relapse frequency in year five, but proteinuria persisted. Notably, among early-diagnosed AS patients without baseline CKD, 39/40 (97.5%) maintained preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2) throughout the 3-year follow-up, with only one patient (P28, male, COL4A5 c.81 + 2T>C) progressed to ESRD, demonstrating rapid functional decline from diagnosis at age 14 years (baseline creatinine 300 µmol/L, eGFR 32 mL/min/1.73 m2) to hemodialysis dependence within 3 years (pre-dialysis creatinine 857 µmol/L, eGFR 5.94 mL/min/1.73 m2), with post-dialysis stabilization at creatinine 281 µmol/L (eGFR 18.2 mL/min/1.73 m2).

In this study, we performed a retrospective analysis of 40 genetically confirmed pedigrees of AS from Southwest China. We identified 21 novel mutations in the COL4A3-COL4A5 genes, including two previously unreported cases of digenic inheritance involving type IV collagen genes. Immunofluorescence assays showed that the newly discovered mutations in COL4A5 affect the expression of type IV collagen. This research is one of the few studies to explore the clinical manifestations, pathological features, and molecular genetics of AS in children from ethnic minority regions in Southwest China. It expands the known mutational spectrum of AS and uncovers rare digenic mutations.

Our center found that patients with AS in Southwest China exhibited early disease onset, with a predominance of male cases. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the age of onset between genders. The clinical phenotypes varied widely, typically presenting as edema, microscopic hematuria accompanied by proteinuria, and gross hematuria. Extrarenal manifestations were uncommon, and the diagnostic rates using electron microscopy remained low. The study revealed that approximately 38.46% of male AS patients developed nephrotic-range proteinuria, compared to only 7.14% of females. Current research indicates that in XLAS, females possess two homologous X chromosomes, leading to heterozygous variants due to single-point mutations with differential expression29–31. This is further complicated by random X-chromosome inactivation. In contrast, males have hemizygous mutations that cause disease regardless of whether the mutations are dominant or recessive. Reports indicate that female XLAS patients develop significant proteinuria and reach ESRD much later than males11. Additionally, single-center cohort studies on XLAS genotype-phenotype relationships show that nonsense, splicing, frameshift, duplication, and digenic mutations are associated with more severe clinical phenotypes compared to missense mutations. These findings highlight the importance of early monitoring, routine urinalysis, and genetic screening for male patients and those with severe genotypes in AS families, enabling prompt diagnosis and intervention.

Different types of XLAS mutations affect type IV collagen expression in varying degrees. Through immunofluorescence staining of five novel AS patients, we found that COL4A5 mutations—specifically c.2557delC, c.3091delG, c.3790 + 1G > T, c.3535G > T, and c.1045 C > A—significantly influence the expression of type IV collagen. Frameshift and splicing mutations in XLAS exhibit more pronounced deficiencies in α5 chain expression in the GBM than missense mutations. These mutations are also associated with more severe clinical phenotypes. For example, members of the c.2557delC pedigree developed hematuria and proteinuria at younger ages. During embryogenesis, type IV collagen forms a triple helix composed of α1, α1, and α2 chains in developing capillaries32–34. In glomeruli and other tissues rich in collagen IV, this structure is subsequently replaced by an α3, α4, and α5 network in the capillary walls, and an α5, α5, and α6 network in Bowman’s capsule, which is crucial for kidney maturation35. In cases of X-linked Alport syndrome, mutations in the COL4A5 genes disrupt the post-translational processing of collagen, preventing the maturation of the GBM1. The persistence of the α1, α1, and α2 networks makes it susceptible to cleavage by endopeptidases under the flow of protein-rich plasma33,34. This results in GBM thickening, stratification, and microtears, which can present as hematuria, proteinuria, and ultimately lead to ESRD36. As previously reported, 29% of male XLAS cases and 20% of autosomal recessive ARAS cases display atypical α5 expression in the GBM13,37. Males with α5-positive mutations in XLAS typically carry non-truncating or somatic mosaic variants in COL4A5 and tend to present with later-onset ESRD. In contrast, ARAS cases with α5 chain positivity usually have missense variants in at least one allele of the COL4A3 or COL4A4 genes, and they generally display milder phenotypes38. When comparing our findings with previous studies on XLAS, immunofluorescence tests on XLAS non-truncating groups with missense mutations revealed α5(IV) positive staining in three out of seven patients (43%)38. Our research thus confirms genotype-phenotype correlations in XLAS, indicating that splicing and frameshift mutations lead to truncated transcripts, which are potentially associated with a poorer prognosis.

Digenic inheritance has intrigued geneticists since the early 20th century, but only with the advent of high-throughput sequencing (HTS) has identifying one or multiple pathogenic genes for monogenic disorders become more straightforward, similarly simplifying digenic inheritance verification5,30,39. This study describes rare instances of two digenic mutation AS families (P2, P14), providing new evidence of digenic inheritance. Current research on AS digenic mutations typically divides into combinations between two type IV collagen-encoding genes40. Here, we describe two AS families with COL4A4 and COL4A5 digenic mutation combinations. Using the AlphaFold deep learning prediction model, we systematically analyzed the structural impact of COL4A5 and COL4A4 digenic mutations (P2, P14) on highly conserved triple-helical Gly-X-Y domains and NC1 domains in type IV collagen α3, α4, and α5 chains. This revealed that, compared to single gene mutations, digenic mutations cause multiple structural defects—including premature termination of triple helices, disruption of disulfide bond networks, and decreased local stability from glycine/alanine residue substitutions—resulting in more severe AS clinical phenotypes. All probands inherited digenic mutations from their mothers, featuring co-inheritance of two variants. The P14 proband’s mother had already progressed to CKD stage III relatively early at age 30. This aligns with previous European AS clinical research indicating correlations between digenic mutations and clinical phenotypes24. For instance, a European AS patient data center study of COL4A3/COL4A4 mutation combination pedigrees demonstrated that digenic autosomal mutations on homologous chromosomes present more severe phenotypes than autosomal dominant patterns41. This potentially suggests that kidney involvement severity correlates with the number of mutant type IV collagen molecules, though validation requires additional cases.

In conclusion, our analysis of 40 Alport syndrome pedigrees from Southwest China uncovered 21 novel mutations, including two rare COL4A4–COL4A5 digenic variants. Immunofluorescence showed that COL4A5 mutations differentially alter type IV collagen α3 and α5 expression, while deep learning–based modeling revealed distinct structural changes in both single-gene and digenic mutations. These findings broaden the AS mutation spectrum and enhance diagnostic strategies.
