Glycine encephalopathy, also known as nonketotic hyperglycinemia (NHK), is a 
rare inherited disease caused by an inborn error of glycine metabolism, 
resulting in elevated glycine concentration in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid. 
Clinical manifestations mainly include varying degrees of hypotonia, apneic 
episodes, epilepsy, psychomotor delay during the neonatal period or early 
infancy. Biallelic variants in GLDC account for up to 80 % of classical NKH 
cases. Here we describe the clinical, biochemical, and molecular characteristics 
of two Chinese siblings with severe NHK. Their phenotypes included severe 
symptoms in neonatal period, seizures, and psychomotor delay. The siblings carry 
novel compound heterozygous variants in GLDC, c.1740C > G (p.His580Gln) and 
c.1023G > A (p.Val341=). Based on previous literature reports and pathogenicity 
prediction, the c.1740C > G (p.His580Gln) variant is classified as likely 
pathogenic. By minigene analysis, we confirmed the synonymous mutation 
c.1023G > A (p.Val341=) led to abnormal splicing, with 38 bp missing in exon 7 
in the GLDC gene. These findings highlight the pathogenic nature of a novel 
synonymous mutation c.1023G > A, expand the genetic spectrum of GLDC and provide 
crucial guidance for both the patient's clinical management and family's 
reproductive genetic counseling.

Glycine encephalopathy (GCE), previously known as nonketotic hyperglycinemia (NKH), is a rare inherited disease caused by an inborn error in glycine cleavage system (GCS). This defect leads to the accumulation of glycine in body fluids, particularly in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), resulting in an elevated CSF/plasma glycine ratio [1]. The GCS is a mitochondrial multienzyme system comprising four distinctive protein components: glycine decarboxylase (P protein), aminomethyltransferase (T protein), hydrogen carrier protein (H protein), and dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (L protein), which are encoded by GLDC, AMT, GCSH, and DLD, respectively [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. The vast majority of NKH-causing variations have been detected in the GLDC or AMT genes. Previous studies showcased biallelic variants in GLDC (MIM# 238300) account for up to 80 % of classical NKH cases, AMT (MIM# 238310) mutations for about 20 %, whereas mutations in the GCSH (MIM# 238330) gene are responsible for <1 % of NKH cases [7,8].

In central nervous system, glycine plays a crucial role in synaptic transmission, acting as either an inhibitory or excitatory neurotransmitter depending on its localization. At the level of the spinal cord and brainstem, glycine acts as an inhibitory neurotransmitter, primarily regulating motor, pain, visual, and auditory functions. However, in the cerebral cortex, cerebellum and basal ganglia, glycine functions as an excitatory neurotransmitter to activate N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) type glutamate receptor, which can cause intractable seizures [9]. Consequently, patients with NKH, who have elevated glycine levels in body fluids, present varying degrees of hypotonia, respiratory depression, epilepsy, neurodevelopmental disorders and other clinical manifestations during the neonatal period or early infancy. Based on clinical severity and developmental outcomes, classic NKH is categorized into severe NKH and attenuated NKH. Some patients with NKH die during the newborn period due to respiratory and neurological distress. The majority of survivors exhibit progressive lethargy, severe mental retardation and intractable seizures typical of severe NKH [10].

The diagnosis of NKH is based on the elevated glycine concentration in CSF, along with an increased CSF/plasma glycine ratio, brain imaging, and molecular genetic detection of pathogenic mutations in the genes encoding the protein subunits of GCS or deficient activity of the GCS. Gene sequencing is crucial in diagnosing NKH. Rapid genomic sequencing analysis has revealed a spectrum of GLDC and AMT variants, including an extensive number of missenses, splicing, and insertion deletion (indel) variants [[11], [12], [13]]; however, no pathogenic synonymous mutations have been reported in the GLDC gene.

Here we reported two patients with severe NKH from a Chinese family carrying novel compound heterozygous variants in GLDC, c.1740C > G (p.His580Gln) and c.1023G > A (p.Val341=). We confirmed that the synonymous mutation c.1023G > A (p.Val341=) led to abnormal splicing of the GLDC gene in exon 7 through minigene analysis. This finding enriches the mutation spectrum of GLDC. Additionally, current therapy predominantly aims to decrease glycine concentration and control seizures. We describe our pharmacological therapy directed by molecular diagnosis results, and highlight its potential therapeutic benefit for severe NKH, particularly in seizures management.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children's Hospital (2020R007-F01). Peripheral blood samples were collected from participants for molecular analysis. Informed consent was diligently obtained from patients' parents for the use of their clinical and genetic data in this research.

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood samples using QIAGEN kits (Germany). Reanalysis of trio-whole-exome sequencing (TWES) data from other testing institution, and Sanger sequencing were performed to confirm the disease-causing variants on four family members. The pathogenicity of the identified variations was determined following the guidelines set forth by American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) [14].

A synonymous variant, c.1023G > A (p.Val341=), in exon 7 of the GLDC gene was predicted by Varseak (https://varseak.bio) and SpliceAI (https://github.com/ Illumina/SpliceAI) to affect the splicing effect at a noncanonical splice site. The expression of GLDC in peripheral blood is extremely low, and it is difficult for us to obtain other samples other than peripheral blood of patients. Therefore, to validate its functionality, we performed an in vitro minigene splicing study. Amplicons containing either wild type or mutant GLDC minigene [Exon 6 (148 bp)-Intron 6 (346 bp)-Exon 7 (197 bp)-a portion of Intron 7 (837 bp)] were generated by standard PCR and cloned into pcMINI-N vector, which contained the universal MCS-IntronB-ExonB. The recombinant vectors were transfected into 293 T and HeLa cell lines respectively. Total RNA was extracted 48 h after transfection, and RT-PCR and Sanger sequencing were used to verify whether Exon6-Exon7-ExonB underwent normal splicing. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Patient 1, the firstborn child in the family, was admitted to our hospital at 3 months of age. She was delivered at 38 weeks gestation following an uncomplicated pregnancy, weighing 2400 g at birth with Apgar scores of 9 at both 1 and 5 min. However, she developed lethargy, poor feeding, hiccups, sniffling breathing, and hypotonia on the third day postpartum. This rapidly progressed respiratory failure, necessitating mechanical ventilation for 3 weeks. Persistent feeding difficulties, hypotonia, and seizures emerged around 1 month of age. Initially, epileptic seizures were characterized by left eye deviation, bilateral hands stiffening and shaking, and loss of consciousness, lasting dozens of seconds. Later, seizures manifested as upturned eyes, nodding and hugging in clusters. Each cluster comprised 10–20 seizures, occurring 7–8 times daily. EEG revealed hypsarrhythmia (Fig. 1), and the patient exhibited severe developmental delay. Initially diagnosed with West syndrome she was treated with levetiracetam, topiramate, and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) before glycine determination. During the treatment, she experienced a sudden-onset status epilepticus and respiratory failure. Blood tandem mass spectrometry revealed significantly elevated serum glycine concentration (1090.59 μmol/L), prompting cerebrospinal fluid glycine testing, which also elevated levels (Table 1). Brain MRI demonstrated extensive symmetrical abnormal signals in the cerebral white matter, cerebellar dentate nuclei, and brainstem (Fig. 2).

Patient 2 is the second child of the same family, born after a pregnancy without definite genetic diagnosis of the first patient and adequate genetic counseling. She was born at 39 + 4 weeks of gestation via spontaneous vaginal delivery with a birth weight of 2960 g. Her Apgar scores were 9 at both 1 and 5 min. She presented with a weak cry and poor muscle tone at birth. After 2 days of poor feeding and tachypnea, she rapidly developed type II respiratory failure, requiring intubation on day 3. She experienced frequent seizures throughout her illness. EEG showed burst suppression and epileptic activity, and her brain MRI showed diffuse hypomyelination. Despite two weeks of treatment, she remained ventilator-dependent. According to the poor prognosis of the first child, the guardians ceased treatment and she died 2 days after discharge.

In order to confirm the diagnosis, genetic testing is conducted in addition to clinical and biochemical analyses. TWES and Sanger sequencing revealed novel compound heterozygous variants in GLDC (NM_000170.3) in patient 1: c.1740C > G (p.His580Gln) in exon 15 and c.1023G > A (p.Val341=) in exon 7. No other likely pathogenic or pathogenic (LP/P) variants were identified. Genetic testing confirmed that patient 2 carried the same GLDC mutations (Fig. 3A-B). Sanger sequencing also confirmed that the two mutations inherited from the father and the mother respectively (Fig. 3A-B). The position of p.His580 is highly conserved among a variety of species (Fig. 3C). Although p.His580Gln variant has not been previously reported in the publication, however, the same variant has been previously reported as likely pathogenic from Labcorp Genetics in 2021 (ClinVar Variation ID: 1485831), and three other amino acid changes (p.His580Asp, p.His580Tyr and p.His580Pro) at the same coding position have been identified as likely pathogenic in severe patients with NKH [7,8,13]. The molecular dynamic structure modeling demonstrated variation at p.His580 affecting important junctions between structural domains [13]. The c.1740C > G mutation was absent from controls in the gnomAD. Bioinformatic prediction analysis using REVEL (0.93) indicated this variant is deleterious. The synonymous variant c.1023G > A (p.Val341=) is absent from both the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) and the gnomAD. In silico analyses using Varseak and SpliceAI prediction software suggested that c.1023G > A variant may affect normal splicing and generate an aberrant splicing site. According to the variant classification guidelines of ACMG and ClinGen, the c.1023G > A variant is classified as variant of uncertain significance (VUS) (PM3 + PM2_supporting + PP3 + PP4) based on current evidence. However, VUS is not sufficient for molecular diagnosis and genetic counseling, so we conducted further splicing analysis.

The expression of GLDC in peripheral blood is extremely low, and it is difficult for us to obtain other samples other than peripheral blood of patients. In vitro minigene assays were then performed to determine the effect of the synonymous variant c.1023G > A on GLDC mRNA splicing. Amplicons containing either wild type or variant GLDC minigene [Exon 6 (148 bp)-Intron 6 (346 bp)-Exon 7 (197 bp)-a portion of Intron 7 (837 bp)] were generated by standard PCR and cloned into pcMINI-N vector, which contained the universal MCS-IntronB-ExonB (Fig. 4A). Recombinant vector sequencing showed the successful insertion of both wt and mutant GLDC minigene into the pcMINI-N vector (Fig. 4B). The minigene assays showed that pcMINI-N-GLDC-wt produced an expected band of approximately 550 bp in both 293 T cells and Hela cells. In contrast, pcMINI-N-GLDC-mut produced a smaller band than pcMINI-N-GLDC-wt (Fig. 4C), suggesting that c.1023G > A variant may have caused an abnormal splicing. Sequencing and online alignment of the PCR product showed that band ‘a’ was a normal splicing band Exon 6 (148 bp)-Exon 7 (197 bp)-Exon B (57 bp), while band ‘b’ was abnormal, missing 38 bp from the right side of Exon 7, with a splicing pattern of Exon 6 (148 bp)- △Exon 7 (159 bp)-Exon B (57 bp) (Fig. 4D). According to the clinical presentations, glycine concentration, and precise molecular diagnosis, the siblings were diagnosed with nonketotic hyperglycinemia. The treatment was subsequently adjusted to include dextromethorphan (5 mg/kg/d), sodium benzoate (250 mg/kg/d), and tetrahydrofolic acid (10 mg/d). The seizures of patient 1 were controlled and the blood glycine level decreased to 761.96 μmol/L after one month of treatment.

The patients described in this study presented typical clinical manifestations of NKH, including hiccups, hypotonia, and seizures within the first week after birth. Glycine concentrations elevated in CSF and plasma, along with an increased CSF/plasma glycine ratio. Combined with the genetic findings and variation functional analysis results, a diagnosis of classic NKH caused by compound heterozygous mutations in GLDC was established. In this study, a novel synonymous variant c.1023G > A was found to affect splicing, leading to 38 bp deletion in Exon 7. This is the first instance of a synonymous mutation in the GLDC gene confirmed as a disease-causing mutation. Compound heterozygosity with another novel pathogenic mutation c.1740C > G forms the pathogenic basis of the two NKH patients in this family, further enriching the variation spectrum of the GLDC gene.

NKH is a rare genetic metabolic disease caused by biallelic mutation in GLDC, AMT, or GSCH genes. Classic NKH typically manifests lethargy, poor feeding, hiccups, hypotonia, and seizures during the first week after birth, potentially progressing to central apnea requiring ventilator assistance. The mortality rate at this stage can be as high as 50 % [15]. Laboratory abnormalities such as an elevation of glycine level in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid are found in patients with NKH, and the ratio of CSF to serum glycine is higher than 0.08. Ketoacidosis and urine organic acid abnormalities are absent. It is worth noting that glycine concentration detection is recommended using venous blood and CSF instead of dried blood spots. In this study, tandem mass spectrometry screening of a dried blood spot from patient 1 in hospitalization showed no obvious abnormality (375.06 μmol/L). However, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis of serum and CSF revealed that significantly increased glycine concentrations: 1090.59 μmol/L in serum (reference range 125-450 μmol/L) and 140.45 μmol/L in CSF (reference range < 20 μmol/L) respectively. These findings underscore the importance of accurate detection methods for early diagnosis of NKH.

So far, there is no standardized treatment for NKH patients, and no effective way to underlying etiology. While low glycine diet does not improve the symptoms and prognosis, sodium benzoate can combine with glycine to form hippurate, which is excreted in urine, thereby reducing the level of blood glycine. The current treatment for severe cases primarily focuses on the use of sodium benzoate to reduce plasma glycine levels and N-methyl d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) antagonists such as dextromethorphan or ketamine [16]. Although the combination of sodium benzoate and dextromethorphan can improve the clinical seizures and abnormal discharges in children with NKH, antiepileptic drugs are still necessary for patients with refractory epilepsy. Commonly used antiepileptic drugs include clopazone, diazepam, phenobarbital, levetiracetam, carbamazepine and pirampanide. Although valproic acid is an effective antiepileptic drug, it is generally not recommended for children with NKH because it may increase glycine levels [17,18]. In recent years, some researchers have suggested that NKH, a monogenic disorder with a well-established genetic etiology, could be a promising candidate for gene therapy. Kit-Yi Leung et al. designed an AAV9 (Adeno-Associated Virus 9)-based expression vector to target the sites of GCS activity. In their study, GLDC-deficient mice treated with the vector demonstrated a significant reduction in glycine levels in both plasma and brain tissues, providing strong evidence for the potential of AAV-mediated gene therapy as a therapeutic approach for NKH [19].

Most patients with classic NKH have biallelic mutations in GLDC. Missense mutations were the most common type identified in GLDC, followed by nonsense mutations, splice mutations, and small insertion/deletions [8]. In this study, a missense mutation c.1740C > G (p.His580Gln) and a novel synonymous mutation c.1023G > A (p.Val341=) in the GLDC gene were identified in our patients. The mother was heterozygous for c.1023G > A (p.Val341=) and the father was heterozygous for c.1740C > G (p.His580Gln). Structural analysis, based on molecular modeling of the 3D structure, showed p.His580 is located in an internal loop of the P-protein. The corresponding change such as p.His580Tyr was predicted to impact the structural stability of the monomer by affecting the junction between several structural domains [13]. The c.1023G > A (p.Val341=) variant is a synonymous mutation, presenting a challenge in interpreting its clinical significance. Splicing experiment analysis addressed this question through a minigene assay, which verify that c.1023G > A affects splicing, leading to 38 bp deletion in Exon 7 and finally resulting in the loss of gene function. Given the well-defined genetic basis of NKH, early molecular diagnose can avoid unnecessary drug-induced damage and provide guidance for future family planning.

It is crucial to emphasize the importance of valuable genetic counseling for families with a history of genetic disease carrying VUS. VUS are commonly identified in genetic testing and their reclassification rate depends on multiple factors. However, due to the variable time frame for VUS reclassification, some families whose first child with a genetic disease carries a VUS might have a second affected child before VUS reclassification, especially without prenatal counseling. Such an outcome would place a great economic and emotional burden on family and society. The c.1023G > A variant was classified as VUS before splicing analysis but was reclassified as P (PM3 + PM2_supporting + PVS1 + PP4) after splicing analysis, highlighting the value for prenatal genetic counseling.

In summary, we described two novel mutations in the GLDC gene identified in two patients with classical NKH from the same family. To our best knowledge, this is the first report of a synonymous mutation in the GLDC gene verified to result in abnormal splicing by in vitro analysis and cause NKH. The identification of the causative mutations in this family assisted in confirming a clinical diagnosis, providing an important reference for further management, and supporting valuable genetic counseling.
