# A phase 1 trial of CD123-directed chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy in adults with relapsed/refractory acute myeloid leukemia or blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm.
**Authors:** Budde LE, Del Real MM, Song J, Stiller T, Wang Y, Aribi A, Sandhu K, Nakamura R, Marcucci EC, Wu JJ, Ng K, Clark MC, Wood B, Wagner JR, Paul J, Brown CE, Stein A, Marcucci G, Blanchard MS, Palmer J, Forman SJ
**Journal:** J Hematol Oncol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13045-026-01833-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13045-026-01833-3)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Patients with relapsed acute myeloid leukemia (AML), particularly following allogeneic stem cell transplant (alloHCT), have extremely limited therapeutic options. CD123 is an AML-associated antigen and represents an attractive target for immunotherapy. We report outcomes of a phase 1 trial evaluating CD123-targeting chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells in patients with relapsed/refractory (r/r) AML or blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm (BPDCN).

METHODS: This was a single center, open-label, phase 1 dose escalation study enrolling patients with either CD123-positive r/r AML (Arm 1) or BPDCN (Arm 2). Patients received autologous or donor-derived allogeneic CD123 CAR T cells following lymphodepletion. The co-primary objectives were to examine safety and anti-tumor activity using an activity-constrained for toxicity design and to determine the recommended phase 2 dose. Secondary objectives included assessments of progression-free and overall survival.

RESULTS: We enrolled 41 patients, of whom 21 patients (n = 19 on Arm 1 and n = 2 on Arm 2) received CD123 CAR T-cell infusion. The median age of treated AML patients was 45 years (range: 20-71); the two BPDCN patients were aged 24 and 75 years. Among AML patients, 17 (89%) had undergone prior alloHCT. AML patients received 50 × 106 (n = 2), 200 × 106 (n = 6), or 500 × 106 (n = 11) CAR T cells; BPDCN patients received 100 × 106 CAR T cells. There was no dose-limiting toxicity, prolonged myelosuppression, or graft-versus-host disease. Fourteen (74%) AML patients developed grade ≤ 3 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and 11 (58%) experienced grade ≤ 2 neurotoxicity. Fifteen of 19 treated AML patients were evaluable for disease response for overall best response, of whom 4 (26.7%) had best response of complete remission, including 2 with incomplete count recovery. Neither patient with BPDCN developed CRS but both experienced grade 1 neurotoxicity. One of 2 BPDCN patients achieved CR and relapsed at 3 months. CAR T cell expansion was dose-dependent, but persistence was limited.

CONCLUSIONS: CD123-directed CAR T-cell therapy is feasible and demonstrates a favorable safety profile in patients with r/r AML and BPDCN, including those with prior alloHCT. Although the estimated overall complete remission rate was modest, these findings provide a foundation for further optimization of CD123 CAR T-cell design, patient selection, and combination strategies.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: This trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT02159495.

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most common adult acute leukemia and has a poor prognosis with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of approximately 30% [1]. Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (alloHCT) plays a central role in managing AML in complete remission (CR) with 3-year disease-free survival in 33% of patients [2]. The efficacy of alloHCT in AML is mainly driven by the T cell-mediated graft-versus-leukemia effect, highlighting the potential of immune-based therapy in this disease [3]. The prognosis of patients who relapse after alloHCT is dismal, with a 2-year OS of ≤ 15% [4, 5]. Therefore, development of novel and more effective therapies are urgently needed.

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has led to practice change in treating patients with B cell malignancies and offers a potential strategy to improve outcomes for patients with AML. CAR T cells targeting several AML-associated antigens, including CLL-1 [6], CD33 [7–10], NKG2D ligand [11–13], and CD371 [14] have been developed and tested in clinical trials. However, successes at the level of B cell malignancies have not yet been achieved for AML, due in part to the lack of a highly specific, pan-myeloid-associated antigen for AML. Furthermore, AML cells, including leukemic stem cells (LSCs), share similar immunophenotypic features with normal hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), posing challenges to specifically target these cells without affecting normal hematopoiesis ultimately causing unacceptable hematologic toxicity.

CD123, the interleukin-3 receptor alpha subunit (IL3RA), is expressed in 70% of patients with AML and is also expressed on LSCs [15]. Of note, high CD123 expression is also a pathognomonic marker for blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm (BPDCN), a rare but highly aggressive hematologic malignancy. CD123 signaling is associated with cell proliferation and survival, increased treatment resistance, and rapid disease progression [16, 17]. Its expression levels are significantly higher on AML blasts and LSCs than on normal HSCs, making it an attractive target antigen for immunotherapy. Several CD123-targeted, antibody-based immunotherapies have been developed, and have displayed manageable safety profiles. However, these therapies have had limited efficacy in AML [15, 18], suggesting that alternative, more potent therapies targeting CD123 may be required to effectively eradicate AML. Several CD123 CAR T cell products have been tested in early phase clinical trials for AML and have demonstrated promising early anti-AML activity but significant toxicity including myelosuppression [19].

We previously developed and preclinically demonstrated effective CD123-dependent anti-AML activity of CD123 CAR T cells in vitro and in vivo [20]. Our CD123 CAR construct is second generation with a CD28 co-stimulatory domain and includes truncated epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFRt) for detection. Notably, our CD123 CAR T cells exhibited minimal impact on normal hematopoiesis from cord blood-derived CD34 + stem cells [20], supporting its favorable safety profile. Here we reported the feasibility of generating patient or donor-derived CD123 CAR T cells for patients with AML or BPDCN and the results of a phase 1, single center, clinical trial evaluating CD123 CAR T cells in relapsed or refractory (r/r) AML and BPDCN.

This was a single-center, open-label, two arm, phase 1, dose escalation study approved by the City of Hope (COH) Institutional Review Board (IRB #13272) under BB-IND 16,457 and registered under the ClinicalTrials.gov number NCT02159495. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) Guideline for Good Clinical Practice (ICH E6). All patients provided informed consent. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 12 years old, Karnofsky performance status ≥ 70%, adequate organ function, with a diagnosis of CD123 + r/r disease after prior treatment including r/r AML (Arm 1) defined as disease relapsed after first CR or refractory following 2 cycles of induction therapy or r/r BPDCN (Arm 2) after at least one line of systemic therapy. Patients who were at a high risk for disease recurrence [i.e., CR with positive minimal residual disease (MRD)] were also eligible for study enrollment, apheresis, and product manufacturing, but were required to have relapsed with active disease and an identified donor or stem cell source for alloHCT prior to treatment with the start of lymphodepletion (LD). CD123 expression was measured by flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry; there was no prespecified threshold of expression intensity to be eligible for this study.

A total of 4 dose levels (DL) of CD123 CAR T cells were proposed, including a de-escalation DL [12.5 million (M)] for Arm 1. The starting dose (DL0) for Arm 1 was 50 M (allowable dose range was 32-50 M), DL1 was 200 M (150-200 M), and DL2 was 500 M (320-500 M) CAR + T cells. For Arm 2, the de-escalation dose was 50 M (32-50 M), the starting DL (DL0) was 100 M (80-100 M), DL1 was 200 M (150-200 M), and DL2 was 500 M (320-500 M); all participants on Arm 2 received 100 M (DL0). The predefined sample size for each arm was 21 evaluable patients.

The treatment schema is depicted in Fig. 1A. Patients or the original donors of patients who received prior alloHCT underwent leukapheresis to collect cells for CAR T cell manufacturing; product source was determined by donor availability and whether the patient achieved donor T cell engraftment of > 50% chimerism in the T cell compartment. Salvage chemotherapy prior CAR T cell treatment was allowed per physician discretion. If clinically indicated, patients were restaged following salvage therapy; patients without measurable disease were not eligible for treatment. Following recovery from toxicity related to salvage therapy, patients received LD with 25–30 m/m2 fludarabine and 300 mg/m2 cyclophosphamide (Flu/Cy) on days − 5, -4 and − 3 prior to a single intravenous infusion of CAR T cells. Decitabine 20 mg/m2/day on days − 7 to day − 3 in combination with Flu/Cy was allowed as an alternative LD regimen. Patients were followed for activity and dose limiting toxicity (DLT) for the first 28 days following CAR T cell infusion and had a disease assessment on day 28 post infusion. Patients could receive an optional second CAR T cell infusion on the study protocol if they continued to meet safety and eligibility criteria and still had CD123 + disease.

This trial tested T cells transduced with a second generation CD123-targeted CAR [21] that was hinge-optimized to reduce FcR binding [22] and included a CD28 costimulatory domain and a truncated human EGFR [23] (CD123R(EQ)28ζ/EGFRt + T cells). The primary objectives were to examine safety and the anti-tumor activity of the administered CD123 CAR T cells and to determine the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) for each arm. This study employed an activity constrained for toxicity design, a variation on the toxicity equivalence range design of Blanchard and Longmate [24], to guide dose escalation/de-escalation and to determine the RP2D for each arm. Briefly, the dose escalated for lack of activity [defined as CR or CR with incomplete hematological recovery (CRi) as defined below], with the lowest acceptable rate of activity of 66% and de-escalated for toxicity occurring within 28 days following CAR T cell infusion (DLT monitoring period), with the highest acceptable DLT rate of 51%. Thus, the dose escalated if the rate of activity was below 66% and de-escalated if the cumulative DLT rate was ≥ 51%. The RP2D was the highest dose closest to the target activity rate of 100% using isotonic regression with a DLT rate < 51%. All dose-escalation decisions were made in cohorts of 3 participants; dose escalation or continuation at a dose level was guided by the cumulative proportion of participants demonstrating activity. Specifically, if the observed activity rate was less than 66%, escalation to the next dose was considered, whereas if the activity rate was at least 66%, enrollment continued at the current dose level. Dose de-escalation occurred if the observed DLT rate at a given dose level was ≥ 51%.

The co-primary endpoints were toxicity and disease response. Disease response served as a measure of activity for both dose decision (day + 28) and overall best response (any response assessment after CAR T cell infusion and prior to initiating off protocol therapy). Dose limiting toxicity (DLT) was defined as: (1) any grade 3 or higher organ toxicity (cardiac, dermatologic, gastrointestinal, hepatic, pulmonary, genitourinary or neurologic), with the exception of those listed in the protocol as expected adverse events associated with the in vivo expansion of T cells, and designated as definitely or probably related (level of attribution) to the infusion of the T cells and occurring within 28 days of CAR T cell infusion; (2) any grade 3 or greater cytokine release syndrome (CRS) lasting more than 72 h with intervention; (3) any grade 3 or greater allergic reaction related to CAR T cell infusion; (4) any grade 3 or greater autoimmune toxicity, and occurring within 28 days of CAR T cell infusion; or (5) any grade 5 toxicity with an attribution of possibly, probably or definitely related to the infusion of the CAR T cells, and occurring within 28 days of CAR T cell infusion. CRS was graded according to Lee’s Criteria [25]. All other adverse events, including neurologic toxicities, were graded using the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v.4.0. Secondary endpoints were OS and the expansion and persistence of CAR T cells in the peripheral blood (PB) after infusion.

For Arm 1, disease assessment was based on the modified International Working Group [26]. CR was defined as restoration of normal hematopoiesis with < 5% blasts in the post-treatment bone marrow (BM) and absence of extramedullary disease (EMD). CRi was defined as CR with insufficient hematological blood count recovery (either neutrophils or platelets). Morphologic leukemic-free state (MLFS) was defined as < 5% BM blasts, absence of blasts with Auer rods, absence of extramedullary disease and with no requirement of hematologic recovery. Treatment failure (TF) designation is used for patients with a response less than CR or CRi. MRD was measured by multiparameter flow cytometry and/or AML mutational panel. For Arm 2, disease assessment was based on published recommendations [26, 27].

Patient or donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were collected and transduced with a lentivirus to express the CD123R(EQ)28ζ/EGFRt + CAR [20], expanded in vitro to achieve cell numbers sufficient for the planned dose, and cryopreserved until the time of infusion in a Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)–compliant facility at COH in Duarte, CA. During the enrollment on DL2, we modified our manufacturing platform to include a T cell selection process prior to transduction (Tselect). Briefly, patient or donor PBMC were depleted of monocytes and T-regulatory cells using anti-CD14 and anti-CD25 microbeads, respectively, and enriched for CD4 + and CD8 + T cells using anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 microbeads, respectively. Depletion and enrichment steps were performed on a CliniMACS (Miltenyi Biotech).

PB and BM samples were collected before and after CAR T cell infusion (Fig. 1A). Presence of CAR T cells was assessed by flow cytometry using cetuximab, which detects the truncated EGFR in the CAR construct, and/or quantitative PCR to estimate lentiviral vector transgene copy number by measuring the woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element (WPRE) in the CAR lentiviral vector. AML blasts in the BM or PB were evaluated for CD123 expression by flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry. Quantification of CD123 on AML blasts in BM or PB was determined by multiparameter flow cytometry at the University of Washington Medical Center Hematopathology Laboratory [28]. Exploratory cytokine analysis of patient serum samples was performed using the Human 30-Plex panel (Invitrogen) for the Luminex platform. Additional sample processing and storage for correlatives were done as previously described [29].

Single-cell DNA and antibody library preparation was performed according to the Mission Bio Tapestri manufacturer’s protocol (Mission Bio, San Francisco, CA). Briefly, cryopreserved BM or PB samples were thawed and stained with TotalSeq™-D Human Heme Oncology Cocktail (BioLegend, 399906). After encapsulation, lysis, barcoding, and targeted amplification of a customized target panel containing 127 amplicons for 20 AML-related genes (Mission Bio), final libraries were amplified, purified and sequenced on either an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 system or NextSeq 2000 P2 system. Analysis of single-cell DNA and protein data was completed using the Mosaic Python library (Mission Bio) available at the Mission Bio website: https://missionbio.github.io/mosaic/.

Standard descriptive statistics were presented for patient characteristics. Rates were assessed for DLTs, and Activity/ Response. Two sample t tests were used to compare responders and non-responders for max WPRE, and OS were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier methods.

We enrolled a total of 41 patients (Fig. 1B), 35 with AML (Arm 1) and 6 with BPDCN (Arm 2) who underwent T cell collection between 12/15/2015 and 02/11/2021, including 26 allogenic donor collections. We successfully manufactured products for 37 (90%) patients. On Arm 1, 14 patients were off study prior to LD due to manufacturing failure (n = 2), death (n = 8), and ineligibility due to MRD-negative CR (n = 1), CD123-negative disease (n = 1), and inadequate organ function (n = 2). Among 21 patients on Arm 1 who underwent LD, 19 received CAR T cell infusion and 2 did not (n = 1, rapid disease progression; n = 1, LD-related toxicity). On Arm 2, two patients received LD and CAR T cells, 2 received cells on compassionate use protocols due to ineligibility (1 did not meet the weight requirement for patients aged 12–17 years and 1 did not have measurable disease), and 2 did not have a successfully manufactured product and had rapid disease progression. Arm 2 closed due to the opening of an industry sponsored trial using the same CD123-CAR vector in the same patient population.

Table 1 summarizes the patient characteristics of the 19 patients with AML and 2 patients with BPDCN who received CAR T cell infusion. Briefly, the median age at infusion of AML patients on Arm 1 was 45 years (range, 20–71), 11 (58%) were female, and 14 (74%) were non-Hispanic. The median number of prior lines of therapy (excluding alloHCT) was 4 (range, 1–9), with 17 patients (89%) having received at least one prior alloHCT. The ages at infusion of BPDCN patients on Arm 2 were 24 and 75, both patients were male, and 1 (50%) was non-Hispanic. Both patients had received 1 prior line of therapy; neither had received prior alloHCT. Fourteen (74%) AML patients and both BPDCN patients received at least one line of bridging therapy while CAR T cells were being manufactured; delay between apheresis and LD was due to recovery from salvage therapy. Two, 6, and 11 AML patients on Arm 1 were treated on DL0, DL1, and DL2, respectively. Three patients on Arm 1 received a second CAR T cell infusion, 3 months (n = 2) and 20 months (n = 1) after their first CAR T cell infusion. Both BPDCN patients were treated on DL0.

There were no DLTs associated with CAR T cell infusion on either arm. On Arm 1, all 19 patients experienced grade ≥ 3 toxicities post the first CAR T cell infusion regardless of attribution (Table 2), the most common of which were platelet count decreased (n = 19), anemia (n = 19), white blood cell count decreased (n = 17), and febrile neutropenia (n = 12). Seventeen patients (89%) had grade ≥ 3 toxicities that were at least possibly attributed to CAR T cells. On Arm 1, a total of 11 serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred in 8 patients (42%) including two grade 5 SAEs on day 24 and day 28, respectively, post CAR T cell infusion due to infection (sepsis, n = 1) and intracranial hemorrhage (n = 1) that were unlikely to be attributed to CAR T cells, as determined by the treating physician. No patients developed either treatment-related prolonged grade ≥ 3 neutropenia over day 42 or graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). Fourteen patients (74%) experienced CRS at least possibly attributed to CAR T cell infusion, including 6 patients with grade 1 (n = 1, DL0; n = 2, DL1; n = 3, DL2), 7 patients with grade 2 (n = 1, DL1; n = 6, DL2), and 1 patient with grade 3 CRS (DL1). No patients experienced grade ≥ 4 CRS (Table 2). The median time to onset of CRS from start of CAR T cell infusion was 1 day (range, 0–10 days) and the median duration of the CRS event was 2 days (range, 0–10 days). Eleven patients (58%) experienced grade ≤ 2 nervous system disorders (indicating immune effector cell associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) (Table 2), all of which resolved. The median time from CAR T cell infusion to onset of nervous system disorders was 2 days (range, 0–15 days). CRS and ICANS were treated per institutional SOPs, which included corticosteroids and/or tocilizumab. There were no additional toxicities experienced by patients who received decitabine as part of their LD regimen. Neither patient treated on Arm 2 experienced CRS, infection, or SAE. Both patients experienced reversible CAR T cell related grade 1 neurologic adverse events (Table 2).

Among the 15 participants treated on Arm 1 who were evaluable for overall response, 4 patients proceeded to a subsequent alloHCT at a mean of 64 days (range: 43–83 days) after CAR T cell infusion; the best responses post alloHCT of 2 MRD-negative CR, 1 MRD-negative CRi, and 1 disease recurrence. With a median length of follow up of 11 months (range: 3–73 months), the median OS was 11.3 (95% CI [6.9, 14.5]) months post CAR T cell infusion. Fourteen of 15 (93.3%) participants on Arm 1 evaluable for overall best response have died. Causes of death were disease progression (n = 10), and infection (n = 4).

Both participants treated on Arm 2 were evaluable for dose-decision. Of the 2 patients treated on Arm 2, 1 patient achieved CR with complete resolution of an extramedullary mass (Fig. 2A). Both patients had disease progression on day 100 and day 29 post infusion, respectively, proceeded to an alloHCT at 154 and 99 days post infusion, and received additional treatment. Both patients subsequently died from disease progression 17 and 34 months after T cell infusion, respectively.

CD123 CAR T cells expanded in the PB, and expansion peaked between days 7 and 12 post infusion as detected by quantitative PCR (Fig. 2B) and flow cytometry (Supplemental Fig. 1). For patients on Arm 1, there was greater expansion with increasing CAR T cell dose, with mean maximum WPRE log10 (copies/ug of DNA) of 1.8 (95% CI [-14.4,18.1]), 3.5 (95% CI [2.5,4.6]), and 4.7 (95% CI [4.0,5.3]) for DL0, DL1, and DL2, respectively (Fig. 2B). There was no difference in mean maximum WPRE log10 between responders (n = 4) and non-responders (n = 14) using a two sample T test (diff = 1.1, 95% CI [-1.43, 3.55]). Serum cytokine levels were measured during the first 28 days post CAR T cell infusion (Supplemental Fig. 2). Notably, levels of serum GM-CSF which others found to contribute to resistance to therapy [30], were detectable but not quantifiable post CD123 CAR T cell infusion in most (90%) patients.

CD123 expression levels on AML blasts varied by patient at baseline and during the course of CD123 CAR T cell therapy. CD123 quantitation by antibody bound per cell (ABC) analysis of the pre-infusion white blood cells or CD34 + cells from 12 patients with available data showed a range of 467 to 10,209 CD123 ABC (Supplemental Fig. 3A). Lowered or lost expression of CD123 occurred in 5 of 7 (71%) patients with available data at day 28 after receiving CD123 CAR T cell infusion; none of these patients responded to treatment by evaluation on day 28 (Supplemental Fig. 3B). One patient had an extramedullary muscle leukemia-infiltrating mass in the left forearm that subsided by day 10 post CD123 CAR T cell infusion but recurred as a predominantly CD123-negative mass by day 25 (Supplemental Fig. 3C).

Cell products of patients who received their CD123 CAR T cells were either autologous (Arm 1, n = 6; Arm 2, n = 2) or allogeneic generated from cells collected from the original alloHCT donor (Arm 1, n = 12 related donors; n = 1 unrelated donor; Arm 2, n = 0). Eleven (58%) and 2 (100%) received PBMC-derived CAR T cell products while 8 (42%) and 0 (0%) received T-select-derived CAR T cell products on Arm 1 and Arm 2, respectively. The median manufacturing time was similar across product types: 13.5 days (range, 12–17) for autologous products and 13 days (range, 12–16) for allogeneic products, and 14 days (range, 12–17) for PBMC products compared with 12.5 days (range, 12–14) for T-select products. CAR percentage ranged from 28% to 98%, which was significantly different for allogeneic compared to autologous products (65% vs. 83%, p = 0.0393) Supplemental Fig. 4A). Allogeneic products had significantly higher fold expansion with a median of 12.1 (range, 3.3–24.0) than that of the autologous products which had an average of 6.3-fold expansion (range, 3.0–11.0) during the manufacturing process (p = 0.0262; Supplemental Fig. 4B). Allogeneic CAR T cell products had more T-central memory cells (CD45RA-CD62L+CCR7+) than autologous products (Supplemental Fig. 4C), as well as higher levels of Tim-3, while levels of PD-1 and Lag-3 were similar between autologous and allogeneic products (Supplemental Fig. 4D).

UPN 517 was a patient with AML who achieved MRD-negative CR following an initial infusion of 500 M CD123 CAR T cells, relapsed, and achieved MRD-negative CR following a second infusion of 500 M CD123 CAR T cells (Fig. 3A). This patient was diagnosed with AML at age 55 and subsequently received 2 alloHCT. Following relapse post second alloHCT, this patient was enrolled on this study. Prior to CAR T cell therapy, next generation sequencing revealed mutations in Runx1, DNMT3A, and SRSF2 in the patient’s AML blasts. Quantitative flow cytometry analysis performed pre-LD showed abnormal myeloid blasts (22.9% of white blood cells) expressing markedly high levels of CD123 (10,209 CD123 molecules per cell; Supplemental Fig. 3A) on 99.9% of the leukemic blasts. The allogeneic donor of the most recent alloHCT underwent apheresis for CAR T cell product manufacturing using the T-select platform. The CAR T cell product had the highest fold expansion of CD3 + cells at the end of CAR T manufacturing but otherwise had a comparable phenotype to other CAR T cell products (Supplemental Fig. 4B). The patient was enrolled on DL2, received 500 M CAR T cells and experienced grade 1 CRS on day 1 post infusion; peak expansion of CAR T cells occurred on day 5 post infusion. At day 28, the patient achieved MRD-negative CR and remained in remission for 20 months. Upon leukemia relapse, he received a second infusion of 500 M CAR T cells. The patient experienced persistent grade 1 CRS days 1 through 8 and achieved MRD-negative CR on day 28. He remained in remission for 7 months from the second infusion.

We investigated the presence and phenotype of AML clones (or clonal abundance) and immune cell subtypes in BM aspirates taken before LD and 28 days post both first and second CAR T cell infusions utilizing a single cell multi-omics. We found one independent genetically identified clone in the BM aspirate that harbored mutations in DNMT3A and SRSF2 and likely passenger mutations in DNMT3A, EZH2, and WT1 (Fig. 3B). By immunophenotypic analysis, we identified B cell, T cell, NK cell, myeloid cell clusters and 3 major AML clusters (Fig. 3C; Supplemental Fig. 5A) and showed the relative abundance at different timepoints. The 3 identified AML clusters (clusters 1 to 3) included CD34+, CD123+/lo, CD33+/hi/-, CD117+/-. Overlay of the genetically defined AML clone onto the protein-based Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) demonstrated that the dominant AML clone was distributed across multiple phenotypic AML clusters, including clusters 1, 2, and 3 (Fig. 3D). AML clusters 1 (CD123loCD33hic-Kit⁻) and 2 (CD123⁺CD33⁺CD117⁺) predominated in pretreatment samples, collectively accounting for 33% of the defined populations, but together comprised only 3% of cells at relapse (Supplemental Fig. 5B). In contrast, the predominant AML population at relapse, cluster 3 (CD123⁺CD33⁻CD117⁺), representing 41% of AML cells was rare at the pre-LD timepoint, accounting for less than 1% of defined AML cells (Fig. 3C). Single-cell genotyping of relapsed BM samples did not identify the emergence of new mutational clones but rather relapse was characterized by a shift in surface protein phenotype within the existing genetic clone (Fig. 3D–E).

This phase 1 trial demonstrates the feasibility and safety of targeting CD123 using CAR T cells in patients with r/r AML and BPDCN, including a challenging to treat patient population with a high prevalence of prior alloHCT. We successfully manufactured CD123 CAR T cell products for 90% of the enrolled patients and safely administered both autologous and donor-derived products without DLTs, prolonged myelosuppression, or GVHD, even at the highest dose level evaluated.

A notable finding of the study is the favorable tolerability of this CD123 CAR T cell therapy. Due to the relatively low number of responders, it is difficult to comment on the need for stem cell rescue due to treatment related irreversible cytopenia or myeloablation in responders, a worrisome potential on-target, off-leukemia effect. These results are in line with our preclinical data and suggest that our CD123 CAR T product have minimal impact on normal HSC function and hematopoietic regeneration. There are several autologous and allogeneic CD123 CAR T cell products in clinical development for patients with AML [30–33], which have demonstrated variable safety and efficacy outcomes in small studies of 5–19 treated patients. Gill et al. reported marked myeloablation using their CD123 CAR T cells in the preclinical setting [34], a discrepancy which may be explained by differences in CAR design, reagent use, and manufacturing. Furthermore, we did not observe high grade CRS, reported in other CD123 CAR T cell clinical trials [30, 33, 35], nor did we observe excessive production of GM-CSF post infusion (Supplemental Fig. 2), which others found to be a major contributor to cytokine driven resistance mechanism in AML CAR T-cell therapy [30]. We also did not observe other on-target, off-tumor toxicities such as capillary leak syndrome due to endothelial damage following the infusion of our CD123 CAR T cells, which was reported to be associated with CD123 expression on endothelial cells and observed in the UCAR123 study [36].

The observed clinical activity (overall best response rate), while modest compared to the CAR T cell remission rate in B cell lymphoid malignancy, is nonetheless meaningful in the context of this challenging-to-treat population. Overall clinical activity, including best responses of CR and CRi, were seen in 26.7%, 4 of 15 AML patients evaluable for overall best response on Arm 1 and 1 of 2 evaluable patients on Arm 2 (Fig. 2A). This level of activity is in line with Gill et al., who showed a CR rate of 25% [34]. The RP2D of CD123 CAR T-cells was not determined for either arm as we did not achieve the desired level of activity. Responses were observed without excess toxicity, validating CD123 as a clinically relevant and actionable antigen in myeloid malignancies. Overcoming the negative impact of the leukemic bone marrow niche, which can rapidly transition toward an immunosuppressive, pre-leukemogenic microenvironment that fosters disease persistence and therapeutic resistance, in future studies may enhance antileukemic activity of the CAR T approach.

Several factors likely contributed to the limited durability and remission rate observed, reflecting a complex, multifactorial resistance landscape, including limitations of target selection, antigen heterogeneity and escape, and the emergence of both intrinsic (e.g., upregulation of inhibitory T-cell checkpoints) and extrinsic (e.g., expansion of regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor populations) immunosuppressive mechanisms that have limited the development of CAR T cell approaches in AML [37, 38]. Peak expansion of CAR T cells correlated with CAR T cell dose, but this correlation did not translate into better clinical activity induced by CAR T cells or CAR T cell persistence on day 28 (Fig. 2B). It is possible that the dose-dependent expansion was confounded by the change in manufacturing platform during the enrollment of DL2 as well as the use of both autologous and allogeneic products. A more effective bridging therapy and/or a novel LD regimen might be necessary to reduce target burden and improve T cell function. Limited persistence may also result from the use of a murine scFv in the CD123 CAR construct. A humanized version of the CD123 CAR and fine tuning the target affinity and its association with CAR activity are important steps toward a more potent CD123 CAR T cell therapy. Additionally, antigen modulation has emerged as a recurrent resistance mechanism. Loss or downregulation of CD123 expression was observed in the majority of evaluable non-responders in this study, including a case of extramedullary relapse characterized by predominantly CD123-negative disease. These findings highlight the phenotypic plasticity of AML and the heterogeneity of AML blasts.

The case of an exceptional responder, while a singular patient, provides a signal of efficacy and important biological insight as well as supports further refinement of patient selection strategies. This patient achieved two separate MRD-negative complete remissions following sequential CD123 CAR T-cell infusions, with each remission lasting at least 7 months in the absence of additional therapy. Notably, this leukemia exhibited exceptionally high CD123 expression at baseline and harbored a RUNX1 mutation, a genomic feature associated with high levels of CD123 in subsets of AML [39, 40]. Single-cell multi-omic analysis revealed that relapse occurred through phenotypic diversification within an existing genetic clone rather than clonal evolution, emphasizing the importance of dynamic antigen expression in shaping response and resistance. While anecdotal, this case supports the hypothesis that subsets of AML characterized by high and stable CD123 expression may derive particular benefit from CD123-directed cellular therapy.

Conducting CAR T-cell trials in AML presents substantial logistical and clinical challenges, including rapid disease progression and high risk for serious infectious complications due to the nature of the disease as well as manufacturing constraints in the setting of profound cytopenia or high circulating blast burden. Tambaro et al. terminated a phase 1 study using autologous CD33-targeted CAR T cells for patients with r/r AML due to high CAR T cell manufacturing failure rate of 50% and high drop off rate between apheresis and CAR T cell infusion [8]. For both arms on our study, the top two reasons that patients who underwent apheresis did not receive CAR T cell infusion on study were death from disease progression or infectious complication (8 of 18) and manufacturing failure (4 of 18). In response, we subsequently implemented workflow optimization by shortening the screening time, prioritizing aphesis slots for patients with AML, and minimizing the delay in starting bridging therapy. Additionally, we introduced a T cell selection from the apheresis product step to the AML manufacturing platform to enrich T cells and mitigate the impact of leukemic contamination, which is a bottleneck for autologous CAR T cell production from AML patients [41]. Donor-derived allogeneic products demonstrated superior expansion during manufacturing without increased toxicity post infusion, supporting the feasibility of this approach for patients with prior alloHCT, although there was no difference in response in patients who received autologous or donor-derived products. Additional optimization of this therapeutic platform will be necessary to reduce pre-infusion attrition in context of aggressive relapsed AML, which was a limitation of this study.

In summary, this study demonstrated that CD123-directed CAR T-cell therapy can be delivered safely to patients with r/r AML and BPDCN, including those with extensive prior therapy and prior alloHCT. Although overall clinical activity was limited, the absence of prohibitive toxicity, evidence of on-target antileukemic activity, and identification of biologically informative responder subsets collectively justify continued development of this platform. Future strategies should focus on optimizing CAR design and manufacturing methodology to enhance persistence, refining patient selection based on antigen diversity, density and molecular features, and exploring rational combination approaches to overcome microenvironment-mediated resistance. Together, these findings support CD123 as a relevant therapeutic target in myeloid malignancies and provide a foundation for next-generation CD123 CAR T-cell approaches.
