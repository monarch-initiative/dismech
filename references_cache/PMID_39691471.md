# Conditional ablation of MCU exacerbated cardiac pathology in a genetic arrhythmic model of CPVT.
**Authors:** Deb A, Tow BD, Hao J, Nguyen BL, Gomez V, Stewart JA Jr, Smuder AJ, Knollmann BC, Wang Y, Liu B
**Journal:** J Mol Cell Cardiol Plus (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jmccpl.2024.100093](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmccpl.2024.100093)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is a genetic arrhythmic syndrome caused by mutations in the calcium (Ca2+) release channel ryanodine receptor (RyR2) and its accessory proteins. These mutations make the channel leaky, resulting in Ca2+-dependent arrhythmias. Besides arrhythmias, CPVT hearts typically lack structural cardiac remodeling, a characteristic often observed in other cardiac conditions (heart failure, prediabetes) also marked by RyR2 leak. Recent studies suggest that mitochondria are able to accommodate more Ca2+ influx to inhibit arrhythmias in CPVT. Thus, we hypothesize that CPVT mitochondria can absorb diastolic Ca2+ to protect the heart from cardiac remodeling.

METHODS AND RESULTS: The Mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (MCU), the main mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake protein, was conditionally knocked out in a CPVT model of calsequestrin 2 (CASQ2) KO. In vivo cardiac function was impaired in the CASQ2-/--MCUCKO model as assessed by echocardiography. Cardiac dilation and cellular hypertrophy were also observed in the CASQ2-/--MCUCKO hearts. Live-cell imaging identified altered Ca2+ handling and increased oxidative stress in CASQ2-/--MCUCKO myocytes. The activation status of Ca2+-dependent remodeling pathways (CaMKII, Calcineurin) was not altered in the CASQ2-/--MCUCKO model. RNAseq identified changes in the transcriptome of the CASQ2-/--MCUCKO hearts, distinct from the classic cardiac remodeling program of fetal gene re-expression.

CONCLUSIONS: We present genetic evidence that mitochondria play a protective role in CPVT. MCU-dependent Ca2+ uptake is crucial for preventing pathological cardiac remodeling in CPVT.

The cardiac ryanodine receptor (RyR2) interacts with its multiple accessory proteins to form a calcium (Ca2+) release channel complex in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) membranes. Following depolarization, RyR2-mediated SR Ca2+ release triggers the heartbeat by activating the myofilaments. Genetic defects in the RyR2 channel complex lead to an arrhythmic syndrome called catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) [1]. CPVT mutations have been identified in the RyR2 and its accessory proteins from both the cytosolic and SR luminal sides [1]. Several of these mutations are found in calsequestrin 2 (CASQ2), a Ca2+ buffering protein that stabilizes the channel from the SR luminal side. Ablation of CASQ2 in a mouse model leads to Ca2+-dependent arrhythmias and recapitulates phenotypes of CPVT [2].

Most CPVT mutations cause dysfunction of the RyR2 channel complex by making it hyperactive or leaky, thus giving rise to aberrant Ca2+ release (ACR) during diastole [3]. When large enough, ACR activates the electrogenic sodium‑calcium exchanger (NCX), resulting in delayed afterdepolarization and triggered arrhythmias [4,5]. Additionally, acquired defects in the RyR2 channel complex due to post-translational modifications also results in leaky RyR2 channels. For instance, ACR due to excessive oxidation/phosphorylation of RyR2 has been implicated in a spectrum of cardiac pathologies including heart failure and metabolic heart diseases [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Intriguingly, ACR only leads to Ca2+-dependent arrhythmias in CPVT, and there are no obvious abnormalities observed in the cardiac structure of CPVT hearts [3]. However, in other pathological settings such as heart failure and metabolic heart diseases [[10], [11], [12], [13]], ACR is linked to not only arrhythmias, but also cell death and pathological cardiac remodeling. Thus, adaptive changes may occur in CPVT to protect the heart from cardiac remodeling.

Mitochondria take up Ca2+ to regulate energy production and also cell death. The mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (MCU) is considered as the primary mechanism for Ca2+ to enter mitochondria [14]. Under physiological conditions, mitochondria are not considered as Ca2+ stores, and thus are not expected to buffer a significant amount of Ca2+ to impact global Ca2+ dynamics in cardiomyocytes [15]. However, under pathological conditions such as CPVT, when challenged with sustained ACR, mitochondria's capacity to accumulate diastolic Ca2+ may be important and can impact the pathophenotype [15]. Recent studies propose to enhance mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake as a therapeutic strategy to treat arrhythmias. Indeed, pharmacological approaches targeting MCU or other mitochondrial Ca2+ transporters to promote mitochondrial calcium (mCa2+) uptake have been shown effective in reducing arrhythmogenic ACR and inhibiting arrhythmias in CPVT [16,17]. These studies provide evidence supporting that mitochondria play a protective role by absorbing diastolic Ca2+, mitigating detrimental consequences of ACR in CPVT. Indeed, as reported in our previous study, conditional ablation of MCU in the CASQ2−/− model (CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO) led to premature death and exacerbated arrhythmias [17].

In this study, we thoroughly evaluated the phenotype of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model. We observed pathological cardiac remodeling and reduced cardiac contractility, which may have contributed to the premature death of the animals. Ablation of MCU in CASQ2−/− hearts also altered intracellular Ca2+ dynamics, impacting mainly diastolic Ca2+. Increased oxidative stress was observed in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO cells. We examined Ca2+-dependent cardiac remodeling pathways but did not detect activation of CaMKII or Calcineurin pathways. RNAseq was performed to further investigate potential contributors of cardiac remodeling in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model and we found an altered gene expression profile distinct from the re-expression of fetal genes.

MCUCKO mice were obtained by crossbreeding MCUfl/fl mice (Stock No: 029817, Jackson lab) with α-MHC MCM mice (stock number: 005657, Jackson lab) that express Cre recombinase to allow for tamoxifen-inducible MCU knockout (MCUfl/fl-MCM or MCUCKO) [18,19]. The genetic model of CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO was created by crossbreeding CASQ2−/− and the MCUCKO.[17]. Genetic changes were induced by a tamoxifen-containing diet (TD.130859, Envigo). MCUCKO and CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO were fed with the diet for three weeks, followed by another three weeks on a regular diet before conducting any experiments. The genotypes of the crossbred mice were confirmed with polymerase chain reactions (PCR; for CASQ2 KO, MCUfl/fl, and Cre) using tail DNA. Age- and litter-controlled mice of both genders (aged 2–8 months) were utilized for experiments.

All animal procedures were approved by the University of Florida and Mississippi State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conformed with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH publication no. 85–23, revised 2011).

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed to examine cardiac contractile function (LOGIQ e NextGen, SOUND Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Adult mice were lightly anesthetized using isoflurane during echocardiography measurement. M-mode tracings of the left ventricle (LV) were obtained in the parasternal short-axis view at the level of the papillary muscles and pulse-wave Doppler was assessed at the level of the mitral valve leaflet tips. Measurements were performed as previously described [20]. Data analysis (diameters, thickness, and time intervals) was performed in ImageJ (NIH) on 8–10 cardiac cycles and averaged for each animal.

Ventricular myocytes were isolated from mice hearts using Liberase TH (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Mice hearts were excised quickly following full anesthesia. The hearts were attached to a Langendorff apparatus via a cannulating needle. After perfusing with a 37 °C Tyrode solution (140 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 5.6 mM Glucose, 5.4 mM KCl, 0.5 mM MgCl2, pH 7.30 with NaOH) for 3 min, the hearts were digested using the Tyrode solution with the addition of Liberase for 10–15 min. After isolation, ventricular myocytes were plated on laminin-coated coverslips for imaging experiments.

Live cell imaging experiments were performed using an inverted epifluorescent microscope (Olympus IX83, Breinigsville, PA, USA) and recorded using a Hamamatsu ORCA-Flash4.0 V3 Digital CMOS camera (maximum speed: 100 frames/s). Ca2+ imaging was conducted using Fluo-3 Am as described previously [17,21]. ROS imaging was conducted using the CM-H2DCFDA. All live cell imaging data were collected with a 40× objective.

Immunofluorescence was performed on myocytes fixed by 2 % formalin. Fixed samples were washed with PBS (3 × 10 min at room temperature or RT) followed by blocking/permeabilization (3 % fetal bovine serum + 0.2 % triton X-100 in PBS; 45 min at RT). The samples were then incubated in a solution containing the primary antibody (in PBS + 10 % BSA) overnight at 4 °C. After that, samples were washed with PBS (5 × 5 min at RT), incubated with fluorescently-labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT and washed again with PBS (3 × 5 min at RT). The images were captured using a Leica TCS SPE-II inverted confocal microscope, using a 40× objective.

Whole heart lysate was extracted from cardiac tissue after homogenization using an extraction buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were probed using primary antibodies against CaMKII (GTX111401, Genetex, RRID:AB_1949809), CaMKII (Phospho-Thr286) (29253, Cayman chemical), and GAPDH (104941AP150UL, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2263076) followed by secondary horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibodies. SuperSignal chemiluminescence (Pierce Biotechnology Inc., Rockford, IL, USA) was utilized to detect the protein bands. Images were captured by a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). ImageJ was utilized to quantify the protein expression levels.

Mouse hearts were fixed in 10 % phosphate-buffered formaldehyde (pH 7.4), paraffin-embedded, and sectioned longitudinally at 5 μm thickness. Cardiac tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) as previously described [21]. After staining, H&E slides were scanned using a PrimeHisto XE histology slide scanner (Carolina Biological). WGA slides were imaged with an Olympus IX83 inverted fluorescent microscope, using a 40× objective.

RNA was extracted from freshly harvested mouse heart tissue with RNAzol reagent, following the manufacturer's protocol (Molecular Research Center, Inc. Cincinnati, OH, USA). Libraries were constructed following an established protocol as described previously [22]. All the libraries were analyzed and quantified with a bioanalyzer and sequenced on an Illumina NEXTSeq 2000 system using a fee-based service at the Weill Cornell Genomic Core Facility. All the sequencing data were deposited in the NCBI sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under the Bioproject acession number PRJNA916203 (WT and CASQ2-/- samples) and PRJNA623119 (MCUCKO and CASQ2-/--MCUCKO samples) mRNA Sequence Processing was performed as previously described [21]. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package Version 1.30.1 [23]. The p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg method. A corrected p-value of 0.05 and log2FC (fold change) of 1 were set as the thresholds to determine the significantly changed genes.

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess if the data were normally distributed (N > 10). If the data were normally distributed, parametric tests (unpaired Student t-test or ANOVA) were employed; otherwise, nonparametric tests (unpaired Mann-Whitney test or Kruskal-Wallis test) were used. Since normality cannot be assessed for data with a small sample size (N < 10), we performed nonparametric tests for experiments with a small sample size (N < 10), using the nonparametric unpaired Mann-Whitney test (for comparison between two groups) or the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test (for comparison among more than two groups), as appropriate.

For experiments utilizing isolated myocytes, nested ANOVA were employed to determine statistical difference. All values were reported as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise noted. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

By crossbreeding the CASQ2−/− with the MCUCKO mice, we generated the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model. The conditional ablation of MCU was induced by tamoxifen. Previously, we reported that the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice suffer from early mortality [17]. Compared with the CPVT model of CASQ2−/−, ventricular arrhythmias were further exacerbated in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice, which may partially contribute to the early mortality observed with this model [17]. In the current study, we performed echocardiography to examine cardiac contractile function in vivo. Both CASQ2−/− and MCUCKO mice have been shown to have a normal contractile function similar to wildtype (WT) previously by multiple studies [18,19,21,24]. Therefore, we did not include an additional WT control for this measurement.

The in vivo cardiac function of CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice exhibited a considerable degree of heterogeneity. The mean fractional shortening of this group was not significantly different from the control groups of CASQ2−/− and MCUCKO (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, ∼50 % of CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice (N = 12) developed cardiac remodeling. Specifically, two CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice exhibited severe systolic dysfunction, as evidenced by a markedly low fractional shortening (approximately 20 %, compared to the normal value of around 40 %) (Fig. 1B), indicating dilated cardiomyopathy (Fig. 1A). In addition, four CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice had higher than usual fractional shortening (Fig. 1B), which may be explained by the compensatory pathological hypertrophy occurring to these animals. Indeed, the posterior wall of the heart was found to be thicker in these mice when looking at individual data points. Moreover, the mean of the posterior wall of CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO was significantly thicker than the MCU group (Fig. 1B), consistent with the cardiac hypertrophy as reported by our previous study [17]. The myocardial performance index (MPI) is used as a measurement of combined LV diastolic and systolic function, with a higher MPI indicating impaired LV function. Our results showed that MPI had a trend to be higher in CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO than the control groups (Fig. 1B). Indeed, animals in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO with impaired fractional shortening also had higher MPI (Fig. 1B). Of note, ablation of MCU led to a remarkably high mortality in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice. Only 20 % of the mice survived after 3 weeks of tamoxifen-induced genetic change [17]. Unfortunately, with the high mortality rates of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice, the surviving mice that we could include in the study were likely more resistant to the cardiomyopathy/cardiac remodeling phenotype. Therefore, our results for this group were skewed to the less severe phenotype. Yet, for the twelve CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice included in the echocardiography study, we were still able to detect two exhibiting severe cardiac remodeling (dilation) and contractile dysfunction which was not observed in any other control groups. In addition, another four mice seemed to be undergoing compensated hypertrophy. Thus, despite the survivorship bias, half of the mice developed cardiac remodeling that can be detected by echocardiography.

The frequency of SCWs serves as a good indicator of cellular arrhythmic potential. When stimulated at 0.5 Hz, CASQ2−/− cells had a significantly higher frequency of SCWs than that of WT and the MCUCKO cells (Fig. 3A and B), consistent with a CPVT phenotype at the cellular level. The ablation of MCU in CASQ2−/− did not further increase the frequency of SCWs at 0.5 Hz (Fig. 3A and B). However, when stimulated at 2 Hz, CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO cells had a significantly higher frequency to develop SCWs during the diastolic phase following the stimulation (Fig. 3D and E).

SR Ca2+ content was estimated by measuring the amplitude of caffeine-induced Ca2+ transient. Under baseline conditions, no significant difference in SR Ca2+ content was detected among the three groups (Fig. 3C). With ISO perfusion, WT and MCUCKO had a similar level of SR Ca2+ content. CASQ2−/− cells had significantly lower SR Ca2+ content than WT and MCUCKO cells (Fig. 3C). The reduced SR Ca2+ content in CASQ2−/− is likely due to the depletion of the SR Ca2+ store caused by leaky RyR2. Similar to CASQ2−/−, CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO cells also had lower SR Ca2+ content than WT and MCUCKO.

A generic ROS indicator CM-H2DCFDA was employed to assess cellular ROS production. At baseline conditions, CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO cells had significantly higher ROS production rate than WT (Fig. 4A and B), while the other control groups (MCUCKO and CASQ2−/−) only showed a trend of higher ROS production rate than WT. With ISO perfusion, ROS production was further accelerated in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO cells, which was considerably higher than WT, MCUCKO, and CASQ2−/− cells (Fig. 4A and B). Thus, oxidative stress was significantly higher in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO group. Previous studies have reported the activation of Ca2+-dependent signaling pathways (CaMKII/HDAC or CN/NFAT) in several cardiac pathologies [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Thus, we examined the activation status of these pathways in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model. To assess the activation of the CaMKII pathway, we measured the autophosphorylation status of CaMKII at Thr 286. No significant difference in CaMKII autophosphorylation was detected in CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO compared with the control groups (Fig. 5A and B). We also evaluated the CN/NFAT pathway by performing immunofluorescence in isolated myocytes. When CN is activated, it induces the nuclear translocation of NFATc3 [30,31], which subsequently activates the transcription of genes associated with cardiac hypertrophy, such as fetal genes. We observed the NFATc3 nuclear translocation in the positive control group (Fig. 6A), where there was an enrichment of NFATc3-related fluorescent signal in the nuclear region when cells were treated with a K+ free solution to induce Ca2+ overload [30]. However, we did not detect any nuclear translocation of NFATc3 in CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO or the other two control groups (Fig. 6B and C). To further understand the factors contributing to the phenotype of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice, we conducted deep sequencing on RNA samples extracted from whole hearts. The initial quality control of principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated the segregation of the four groups (Supplemental Fig. 1). Comparisons were made between CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO and the three control groups (WT, CASQ2−/−, and MCUCKO). Compared with WT, 170 genes were upregulated and 55 genes were downregulated in CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO. Compared with CASQ2−/−, 56 genes were upregulated and 21 genes were downregulated in CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO. Compared with MCUCKO, 25 genes were upregulated and 24 genes were downregulated in CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO. We also compared MCUCKO and WT and found 176 genes were upregulated and 67 genes were downregulated (Fig. 7). Pathway analysis was conducted and mapped these altered genes into several pathways (Supplemental Table 3). However, it remains unclear how these pathways may contribute to the phenotype of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model.

Previously, the re-expression of fetal genes has been reported in cardiac remodeling [32,33]. Thus, we looked into the expression of several fetal genes (Nppa, Nppb, Myh7, and Acta1) and found no difference between the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO and the control groups (Supplemental Fig. 2). This suggests that the remodeling of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO hearts may involve different molecular pathways. Ablation of MCU may induce compensatory changes in genes involved in mCa2+ homeostasis. Therefore, we examined the expression of several genes related to mCa2+ homeostasis, including the several modulatory proteins of MCU, as well as RyR1, mNCX, and Letm1. In this study, we comprehensively characterized the phenotype of the novel mouse model of CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO and investigated the mechanism underlying the exacerbation of cardiac pathology. Previously we reported that the ablation of MCU in the CPVT model of CASQ2−/− caused early mortality and more severe ventricular arrhythmias [17]. Here, we report that impaired cardiac contractile function may also contribute to the lethal phenotype of this model. Severe cardiac remodeling was detected in a subset of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice, including cardiac dilation and hypertrophy. Unfortunately, we did not detect significant differences between the groups when evaluating the mean parameters of echocardiography. However, our in vivo echocardiography studies may be skewed to a group of mice that were more resistant to cardiac remodeling due to survivorship bias. After all, 80 % of mice from this group had died before reaching the time point of performing echocardiography [17]. Nevertheless, myocyte Ca2+ handling was altered in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO ventricular myocytes, which exhibited more Ca2+ waves when stimulated at 2 Hz. Additionally, higher cellular oxidative stress was detected in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO group. The phenotype of this new model did not appear to be induced by the activation of the CaMKII or CN-dependent cardiac remodeling pathways. RNAseq identified global changes in the transcriptome of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model.

Mitochondria are involved in Ca2+ signaling to regulate energy production and cell death. Surprisingly, the cardiomyocyte-specific KO of MCU did not lead to obvious phenotype in the WT mouse models [18,19]. In contrast, cardiomyocyte-specific KO of MCU leads to a lethal phenotype in the CPVT hearts [17], highlighting the importance of MCU-mediated Ca2+ uptake in protecting the CPVT hearts from pathological remodeling. RyR2-mediated ACR is implicated in a range of cardiac pathologies, including heart failure, metabolic heart disease (pre-diabetic and diabetic cardiomyopathies), and various arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation, CPVT, etc.) [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Intriguingly, although CPVT is marked by ACR, CPVT hearts are usually anatomically normal and lack cardiac remodeling. It's been hypothesized that the severity of RyR2 leak is not sufficient to cause cardiac remodeling in CPVT hearts. Indeed, when SERCA was overexpressed in CPVT hearts to induce a more severe leak, cardiac remodeling and cardiomyopathy developed in genetic mouse models [35,36]. Here, we present new evidence that even with a similar level of RyR2 leak, by preventing MCU-mediated Ca2+ uptake, cardiac remodeling and cardiomyopathy can be induced in the CPVT hearts. Of note, with these RyR2 leak-mediated cardiomyopathy models (CPVT-SERCAox and CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO in this study), the cytosolic Ca2+ transient exhibited unaltered or even higher amplitude, which contrasts with the typically reduced amplitude found in heart failure. These results suggest that altered myocyte Ca2+ homeostasis may manifest in different forms under different settings of cardiac remodeling. While enhanced diastolic Ca2+ release (RyR2 leak) is implicated in various heart disease settings, systolic Ca2+ release may not necessarily be depressed as presented in this and our previous studies [35,36].

Although involved in Ca2+ signaling, mitochondria are not known as intracellular Ca2+ stores. It's been suggested that mitochondria do not play significant roles in buffering systolic Ca2+ under physiological conditions, because their Ca2+ uptake is limited compared to the large amplitude of systolic Ca2+ release. Indeed, our data on systolic Ca2+ transient amplitude and SR Ca2+ content does not support mitochondria's role in buffering systolic Ca2+.

However, mitochondria seem to play a crucial role in absorbing diastolic Ca2+, thereby protecting CPVT hearts from pathological remodeling. Although RyR2 leak is a common defect across various cardiac pathologies—such as heart failure, different forms of arrhythmias including atrial fibrillation and CPVT, as well as in prediabetes and diabetes—the role of mitochondria in handling diastolic Ca2+ (leaked Ca2+) appears to be context-dependent. Our previous study demonstrated that while mitochondria promote ROS production in prediabetes, they absorb diastolic Ca2+ in CPVT to protect the heart [17]. Both conditions are characterized by RyR2 leak. The mechanism behind the differing roles of mitochondria across these disease contexts remains unclear, but it seems that their protective role is unique to CPVT. Notably, RyR2 leak is the primary cause of CPVT, whereas in other cardiac pathologies, it is more likely a secondary defect or consequence. This difference may drive the distinct mitochondrial responses observed in various settings, warranting further investigation.

We attempted to further explore the mechanism underlying the cardiomyopathy phenotype of CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO mice. Our data suggest that in the settings of CPVT, mitochondria seem to be adapted to absorb or buffer SR-derived ACR to protect the heart from cardiac remodeling. Thus, when MCU-mediated mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake is abolished in this model, Ca2+-dependent cardiac remodeling pathways may get activated to cause Ca2+-dependent cardiomyopathy. We examined the activation status of the CaMKII/HDAC and CN/NFAT pathways which are classic signaling pathways known to be involved in Ca2+-dependent cardiac remodeling [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. However, these pathways do not seem to contribute to the exacerbation of cardiac pathology as observed in this model. We did not detect significant autophosphorylation of CaMKII. The validity of the autophosphorylation assay has been verified in our previous study, which detected activation of the CaMKII pathway in a different genetic model [21]. We also did not detect CN activation-dependent nuclear translocation of NFAT, the validity of this cellular assay was demonstrated by our positive control which successfully induced NFAT nuclear translocation (Fig. 6A). Additionally, our RNAseq results indicate that the transcripts of fetal genes were not up-regulated in the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model (Supplemental Fig. 2), further supporting that CaMKII and CN dependent pathways were not activated. Other Ca2+-dependent signaling pathways include the activation of the protease calpain and its downstream effectors [37]. Additionally, direct interaction between Ca2+ and certain DNA sequences may change the local structure of DNA, thus impacting transcription and remodeling program [37]. The contribution of these potential mechanisms in the Ca2+-dependent remodeling of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model awaits future studies.

Due to the high early mortality (∼80 %) of the CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model, we were limited to study a relatively healthy group of the animals of this genotype. The CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO model also exhibited a high degree of heterogeneity as detected by the in vivo echocardiography measurements. Fortunately, this heterogeneity did not seem to have a major impact on some of the cellular and biochemical assays that were conducted on this model. We were able to detect altered Ca2+ homeostasis and oxidative stress in this model. Additionally, tamoxifen-activated cardiac Cre is known to cause transient cardiac toxicity, detectable by echocardiography [38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the impaired fractional shortening due to Cre-toxicity is reversible after tamoxifen withdrawal. We therefore ensured that all experiments were performed after a 3-week washout period following tamoxifen treatment so that we could study the effect due to MCU KO, without being confounded by the Cre-dependent cardiac toxicity. The tamoxifen-treated MCUCKO had normal fractional shortening when assessed by echocardiography, confirming the adequacy of the 3-week washout period. Moreover, the comparison made between CASQ2−/−-MCUCKO and MCUCKO already accounts for the potential effects of tamoxifen and/or Cre-toxicity. Multiple assays detected differences between these two groups, both of which were treated with tamoxifen.

Our study provides genetic evidence that mitochondria play a protective role in CPVT. MCU dependent Ca2+ uptake is crucial for preventing pathological remodeling in CPVT.
