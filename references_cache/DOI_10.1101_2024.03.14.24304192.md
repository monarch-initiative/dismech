# Ischemia promotes hypertrophic nerve trunk formation and enteric neuron cell death in Hirschsprung’s disease
**Authors:** Deshu Xu, Weiwei Liang, Chaoting Lan, Lanying Li, Weiyong Zhong, Meng Yao, Xiaoyu Zuo, Jixiao Zeng, Wei Zhong, Qiang Wu, Andrew M Lew, Wenhao Zhou, Huimin Xia, Fan Bai, Yuxia Zhang, Yan Zhang

ABSTRACT

Objective
Enteric nervous system dysfunction is linked to digestive and neurological disorders. Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR) is characterized by the loss of enteric neuron cells (ENCs) in the distal colon. Embryonic enteric neural crest cell (ENCC) migration defects contribute to HSCR development in some infants, but postnatal factors that regulate ENC fate are undetermined. We sought to establish how postnatal changes contribute to HSCR by profiling the colonic microenvironment of HSCR infants.


Design

                    In this study, we recruited infants with HSCR, infants with anorectal malformations but normal ENC development (CT), and a group of age-matched healthy control subjects. Laboratory findings and clinical manifestations were recorded. Single-cell and spatial transcriptome sequencing were applied to colonic tissues from a sub-cohort of CT and HSCR infants. Patient specimens, a mouse model of neonatal ischemic enterocolitis, and
                    Sox10
                    knockdown mouse (
                    Sox10
WT/MUT
                    ) were used to reveal the factors that lead to ENC loss in HSCR infants.
                  


Results

                    We discover that intestinal ischemia promotes CLDN1
                    +
                    hypertrophic nerve trunk formation and ENC death. Mechanistically, ischemia leads to defective nitric oxide (NO) signaling in ENCs, which aggravates mitochondria damage and caspase-mediated apoptosis that can be ameliorated by a NO donor drug.
                  


Conclusion
We show that ischemia contributes to postnatal ENC loss in HSCR infants and suggest that NO donor drugs may alleviate ischemia-related ENC death.

INTRODUCTION 84 
The enteric nervous system (ENS) is the largest component of the autonomic nervous 85 
system and orchestrates gastrointestinal homeostasis and motility. ENS dysfunction is 86 
linked to a vast range of digestive diseases and neurological disorders including 87 
inflammatory bowel disease and autism1 2. During embryonic stage, the enteric neural 88 
crest cells (ENCCs) migrate from the foregut toward the distal hindgut, colonize, and 89 
differentiate into enteric nerve cells (ENCs) and glial cells 3. Defective ENCC 90

leading to severe intestinal obstruction in infancy. The disease is characterized by the 92 
absence of ENCs in the distal colon 4. Previous studies have shown that genetic 93 
predispositions (such as variants of RET, SOX10, and EDNRB) and nutritional factors 94 
(such as retinoic acid deficiency) contribute to HSCR by impairing ENCC 95 
migration4-10. However, HSCR does not follow simple mendelian inheritance 5 10, and 96 
extensive ENCC-derived enteric glial cells have been observed in aganglionic colons 97 
in HSCR11 12. Altogether, the specific mechanism of HSCR remains elusive. 98 
 99 
Remodeling of ENCs can occur postnatally. ENC loss and injury have been observed 100 
in patients with necrotizing enterocolitis 13 and inflammatory bowel disease 14 15 . 101 
Postnatal herpes simplex virus 1 infection promotes neutrophil-mediated destruction 102 
of the ENCs and HSCR-like megacolon in mice 16. Given that the intestinal 103 
microenvironment changes dramatically after birth 4, it is essential to determine 104 
whether abnormal intestinal factors contribute to disease aggregation postnatally. 105 
Currently, the cellular heterogeneity of human ENCs have been studied by spatial and 106 
single-cell transcriptomic sequencing 17-20, but definitive characterization of ENCs in 107 
HSCR is lacking. Here, we present the cellular landscape of the intestinal ganglia and 108 
hypertrophic nerve trunks (HNTs) in patients with HSCR at single cell resolution and 109 
spatially. We show that intestinal ischemia is an important pathogenic mechanism 110 
(and potential therapeutic target) for infants with HSCR.  111

Identification of differentially enriched ST-clusters and cell types in HSCR. 260 
By comparing HSCR with control subjects, we identified 3 unique ST-clusters (figure 261 
2D). ST-cluster C12, which contained ENC-related signatures, were absent in the 262 
aganglionic HSCR (HA) colons. ST-cluster C13, encompassing glial cells, CLDN1+ 263 
fibroblast-like cells (FLCs) and APOD + FLCs, was only identified in HA colons. 264 
ST-cluster C10, a muscular-associated cluster enriched with cellular stress-related 265 
pathways, was present in 5 HG and 1 HA colons but was absent in CT colons. (figure 266 
2A-2D). At the single cell level, our data showed that many immune cells, including 267 
Th17, macrophages and plasma cells, were significantly increased in the colons of 268 
patients with HSCR compared with control subjects (online supplemental figure 1F). 269 
This potentially contributes to enterocolitis risk in patients with HSCR38. 270

Analysis of muscular associated spatial cluster C10 reveals prevalence of 272 
ischemic stress in HSCR. 273 
We first analysed C10, a muscular ST-cluster identified only in infants with HSCR 274 
(figure 2D). Bulk RNA-seq data deconvolution confirmed that C10 was significantly 275 
increased in HG and HA colons compared with the control subjects ( figure 3A). To 276 
examine whether C10 may represent a transitional or intermediate disease state, we 277 
compared C10 with the rest of the muscular regions (C8, C9) ( figure 3B, 3C, and 278 
online supplemental figure 3A). The upregulated genes in C10 included myocardial 279 
infarction-related transcription factors (such as FOSL1  and  THBS1 )39, genes that 280 
mediate misfolded protein responses, chemotaxis, and response to zinc ion. The 281 
downregulated genes encode collagens ( COL1A1, COL1A2, COL4A1 ) and regulate 282 
muscular contraction ( ACTG2, CALD1, MYH11). These changes resemble cellular 283 
responses to ischemic stress 39. Indeed, analysis of the ischemic response gene set 284 
showed that C10 had the highest ischemia score compared with the rest of the 285 
muscular regions (figure 3D). At the cellular level, C10 contained increased numbers 286 
of vascular smooth muscle cells (vSMC) and ischemia-related pericytes ( CCL2+ 287 
Pericyte) but had decreased myofibroblast ( ACTA2+ FLCs) cells ( figure 3D and 288 
online supplemental figure 3B-3E). 289

DISCUSSION 440 
In this study, we generated single-cell and spatial transcriptomes for the ganglionic 441 
and aganglionic colons of infants with HSCR. Analysis of a muscular-associated C10 442 
cluster that present exclusively in HSCR infants reveal extensive ischemia-related 443 
stress responses in HSCR. The intestinal vasculatures were clogged by aggregated 444 
platelets and collagen. Ischemia, inflammation and neuron-derived signals promote 445 
the differentiation of CLDN1+FLCs, which lie at the periphery of the HNTs and 446 
support neuron regeneration and angiogenesis. However, under persistent oxygen 447 
deprivation, NO becomes a limiting factor for the ENCs, which succumb to 448 
mitochondria damage, neuron inflammation and Caspase-mediated apoptosis. Finally, 449 
the pathophysiological significance of ischemia-related neuropathy in HSCR was 450 
confirmed by the amelioration of neuron death in vitro and in the NIE mouse model of 451 
human HSCR in vivo by SNP, a NO donor drug.  452 
 453 
Defective ENCC migration has been suggested as the underlying cause of HSCR 5 64 65. 454 
This is consistent with the fact that most HSCR patients in our cohort presented 455 
symptoms (delayed meconium passage) immediately after birth. However, glial cells 456 
derived from ENCCs 3 are broadly present in most of the HA colons 11 12 , which 457 
indicates success colonization of ENCCs in the patients. Interestingly, glial 458 
cell-containing HNTs are absent in HSCR patients whose entire colons are 459 
aganglionic66 67. Consistent with the above clinical observations, glial cells and HNTs 460 
are absent in the aganglionic colons in Sox10WT/MUT mice, which are known to have 461 
ENCC migration defects 53. By analyzing colonic tissues of sporadic HSCR infants, 462 
we show for the first time that ischemia contributes to ENC loss in HSCR colons. 463

Given that intestinal obstruction leads to intestinal ischemia 68, our results support a 466 
pathophysiological cascade of HSCR development, which may involve obstruction, 467

 470 
Our findings highlight the importance of preventing ischemia-related ENC loss in 471 
infants with HSCR. As a vasodilator and an inhibitor of platelet activation, NO has 472 
been used to treat ischemia for decades61. Moreover, our previous work had identified 473 
a novel function of NO, which removes damaged mitochondria by inducing 474 
mitophagy62. By culturing a neuron cell line under ischemic conditions, we observed 475 
significantly increased mitochondria depolarization, mitochondria damage and 476 
caspase-dependent neuron cell death. The therapeutic effects of NO donors as 477 
candidate treatments for HSCR are supported by SNP restoring neonatal growth and 478 
preventing ENC loss in the NIE model. Altogether, our study reveals that 479 
ischemia-induced postnatal ENC loss is a novel pathogenic mechanism of HSCR, and 480 
suggest that NO donor drugs may be useful in treating ischemia-related ENC death. 481
