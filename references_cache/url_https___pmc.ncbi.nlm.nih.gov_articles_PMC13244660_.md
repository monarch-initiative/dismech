</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P4">Auto-brewery Syndrome (ABS) is a rarely diagnosed disorder of alcohol intoxication due to gut microbial ethanol production. Despite case reports and a small cohort study, the microbiological profiles of patients remain poorly understood. Here, we conducted an observational study of 22 patients with ABS and 21 unaffected household partners. Fecal samples from individuals with ABS during a flare produced more ethanol in vitro, which could be reduced by antibiotic treatment. Gut microbiome analysis using metagenomics revealed an enrichment of Proteobacteria, including <em>Escherichia coli</em> and <em>Klebsiella pneumoniae</em>. Genes in metabolic pathways associated with ethanol production were enriched, including the mixed acid fermentation pathway, heterolactic fermentation pathway and ethanolamine utilization pathway. Fecal metabolomics revealed increased acetate levels associated with ABS, which correlated with blood alcohol concentrations. Finally, one patient was treated with fecal microbiota transplantation, with positive correlations between gut microbiota composition and function, and symptoms. These findings can inform future clinical interventions for ABS.</p></section><section class="abstract" id="ABS2"><h2>Graphical Abstract</h2>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/4e56/13244660/b25958389c4d/nihms-2165678-f0001.jpg" loading="lazy" height="604" width="640" alt="graphic file with name nihms-2165678-f0001.jpg"></p></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="P6">Auto-brewery Syndrome (ABS), also known as gut fermentation syndrome, is a rarely diagnosed condition where patients exhibit symptoms of intoxication due to systemic absorption of pathologic levels of ethanol production by dysregulated gut microbiota<sup><a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a></sup>. Currently this condition has only been described in case reports and case-control studies<sup><a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a>,<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a></sup>, as the gold standard for diagnosis consists of a monitored rise in blood alcohol concentration while the patient is in a supervised clinical setting and is typically facilitated with administration of an oral glucose load<sup><a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a></sup>. These diagnostic methods are resource-intensive and are often not readily accessible to patients or clinicians or covered by insurance. Consequently, many patients will visit multiple medical centers only to be dismissed as surreptitious drinkers and leave without a diagnosis. These patients often experience significant complications similar to those associated with alcohol use disorder, including serious family, social, and legal problems<sup><a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="P7">The etiology and triggers of ABS are poorly understood. ABS has been associated with gastrointestinal illnesses such as short gut syndrome and Crohn’s disease and several early reports indicated that yeast overgrowth was the culprit, hence the name<sup><a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a>,<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>,<a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a></sup>. More recent case reports have identified ethanol-producing strains of <em>Klebsiella</em> species in patients with ABS and liver disease, with recovery facilitated by antibiotic treatment<sup><a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a>,<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup>. In these cases, the <em>Klebsiella pneumoniae</em> strains produced high levels of ethanol via the 2,3-butanediol fermentation pathway<sup><a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a></sup>. However, bacteria can synthesize ethanol via multiple metabolic pathways, primarily involving acetyl-CoA or pyruvate intermediates, with alcohol dehydrogenase catalyzing the final step<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup>. These pathways, including the 6-phosphogluconate, hexitol, mixed acid fermentation, and ethanolamine utilization pathways, in addition to the 2,3-butanediol fermentation pathway, vary in substrate preference and byproducts, allowing bacteria metabolic flexibility in diverse environmental conditions.</p>
<p id="P8">Here, we investigate the microbial mechanisms underlying ABS using an <em>in vitro</em> culture approach combined with metabolomic and metagenomic taxonomic and functional analysis of fecal samples. We also report successful treatment of one patient with ABS using fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). This cohort study comprehensively characterizes patients with clinically documented Auto-brewery Syndrome, comparing their gut microbiomes to those of household partners to better control for environmental and dietary factors that significantly influence gut microbiomes.</p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Patient characteristics</h3>
<p id="P9">A total of 91 subjects (48 patients who reported symptoms of ABS and 43 household partners) were screened. 22 individuals with clinically documented ABS diagnosed by spontaneous rise in blood alcohol level after oral glucose challenge and 21 unaffected household partners were enrolled in our study (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1a</a>; <a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). One patient lived alone. Four pairs of individuals were subsequently re-enrolled under new household IDs for repeat biospecimen collection after undergoing significant changes to their gut microbiome (i.e. receiving antibacterial and antifungal medications or fecal microbiota transplantation), as described in the <a href="#S9" class="usa-link">Methods</a>. During diagnostic testing, the median peak serum alcohol concentration after oral glucose load was 73 mg/dL (IQR 41.75 – 142 mg/dL) and occurred 240 minutes after oral glucose load (IQR 120 – 420 minutes). Patients with ABS were more likely to be male and exhibited serum markers suggestive of liver injury, such as elevations in alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (AP), and total bilirubin, compared with household partners. One patient had cirrhosis. Patients with ABS were also more likely to consume three or more alcoholic drinks per week prior to diagnosis, and to have a prior history of antifungal use, and less likely to consume foods rich in starch after diagnosis (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table 1</a>). 17 patients provided stool samples during remission and flare. “Flare” was defined as periods when patients had symptoms of intoxication or had a detectable blood alcohol level. “Remission” was defined as having no symptoms nor detectable blood ethanol levels for at least one week. Five patients did not develop a flare during the duration of their enrollment (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1b</a>). The average blood alcohol concentration measured by breathalyzer during collection of stool samples during a flare was 136 +/− 82 mg/dL, significantly above the US legal driving limit of 80 mg/dl.</p>

<p id="P10">Stool samples from patients with ABS during a flare produced more ethanol in cultures, with a median of 14.47 mg/dL (IQR 11.18, 17.37), compared with stool samples from patients in remission (median 8.76 mg/dL, IQR 7.03, 11.55) or from household partners (median 5.00 mg/dL, IQR 2.89, 6.79) at 24 hours of growth (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1c</a>, <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">d</a>). Blood alcohol concentration of flaring ABS subjects at the time of stool collection positively correlated with ethanol production in cultures at 24 hours (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1e</a>).</p>
<p id="P11">Treatment of cultures with the broad-spectrum antibiotic chloramphenicol significantly decreased ethanol production in samples from patients with ABS, while treatment with the broad-spectrum antifungal amphotericin B did not have a significant effect (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1f</a>) when compared with the DMSO vehicle control. However, ethanol production was partially reduced by amphotericin B in three ABS patient samples during a flare (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1f</a>). No significant difference in ethanol production was observed in cultures of household partner samples treated with amphotericin B or chloramphenicol (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1f</a>).</p></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Patients with ABS exhibit gut dysbiosis</h3>
<p id="P12">Shotgun metagenomic sequencing (HC, n = 22; HHP, n = 21; Remission, n = 22; Flare, n = 17) revealed that patients with ABS during a flare had a higher relative abundance of Proteobacteria, particularly Enterobacteriaceae (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2a</a>, <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">b</a>, <a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 1a</a>). Blood alcohol concentration of subjects at the time of sample collection positively correlated with Proteobacteria abundance (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2c</a>). Patients with ABS had lower alpha diversity at the species level, quantified by the inverse Simpson index (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2d</a>), and distinct beta diversity profiles compared with household partners and healthy controls (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2e</a>). These differences were predominantly driven by relative abundance of <em>Escherichia coli</em> and <em>Klebsiella pneumoniae</em> (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 1b</a>). MaAsLin2 analysis identified significant species differences (q-value &lt; 0.05) identified between groups while controlling for the random effects of each household (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 1c</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table 2</a>). <em>Ruminococcus gnavus</em>, <em>Escherichia coli</em>, and <em>Klebsiella pneumoniae</em> were the top differentially over-abundant species during flares (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 1c</a>-<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">f</a>). <em>Ruminococcus gnavus</em> is thought to be a pathobiont associated with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and liver cancer<sup><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a></sup>, while <em>Escherichia coli</em> and <em>Klebsiella pneumoniae</em> are members of the Proteobacteria phylum with known ethanol fermentation capacity<sup><a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a>,<a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a></sup>. Also notably, several beneficial obligate anaerobes within the Clostridiales order, such as <em>Ruminococcus bromii</em> and <em>Coprococcus eutactus</em><sup><a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a></sup>, were less abundant during flares (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 1c</a>).</p>

<p id="P15">We next examined microbial metabolic function by analyzing gene counts and metabolic pathways from the metagenomic sequencing data. Using MaAsLin2 to identify significant differences in metabolic pathway abundance between the different conditions while controlling for the random effects of each household, 18 metabolic pathways were significantly differentially abundant between “Flare” and “Remission” samples and 133 microbial pathways were significantly differentially abundant between “Flare” and household partner samples (q &lt; 0.05; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table 4</a>). Three functional groups (biosynthesis, degradation, and energy metabolism) and their respective subgroups illustrate the differentially abundant microbial metabolic pathways that distinguish between disease states (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3a</a>).</p>

In the heterolactic fermentation pathway, glucose is metabolized through the phosphoketolase pathway to acetyl phosphate, which is subsequently converted to ethanol through oxidation<sup><a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a></sup>. The mixed acid fermentation pathway allows for the conversion of glucose into ethanol and succinate<sup><a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a></sup>. Nearly every Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthology-anotated enzyme in these two pathways is significantly over-represented in the flare microbiome samples compared with the remission and household partner samples from within the same household (<a href="#F10" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 5</a>). The mixed acid fermentation and heterolactic fermentation pathway gene content increases in the patients with ABS were largely attributed to two species within the Proteobacteria phylum, <em>Escherichia coli</em> and <em>Klebsiella pneumoniae</em> (<a href="#F12" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 7b</a>), with a minimal contribution of <em>Clostridia</em> and <em>Desulfovibrionaceae</em> species. Several pathways important for menaquinol and demethylmenaquinol biosynthesis were also increased in samples from patients in a flare compared with household partners and healthy controls (<a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 4e</a>). Facultative anaerobes from the Gammaproteobacteria class such as <em>E. coli</em> utilize low-potential quinones such as menaquinone and demethylmenaquinone as electron acceptors mainly in anaerobic respiration <sup><a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a></sup>, which is also the setting for alcohol fermentation. In contrast, the TCA cycle VII, used by acetate-producing bacteria to oxidize ethanol to acetate, was significantly under-represented in flare samples compared with remission samples (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3a</a>, <a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 4f</a>).</p>

<p id="P19">One subject A20 with clinically documented ABS was treated with FMT twice (<a href="#F14" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 9a</a>) as described in the <a href="#S9" class="usa-link">Methods</a> section. After initial FMT, the patient experienced significant improvement in symptoms and no detectable blood alcohol levels for 3 months. The patient experienced greater wakefulness, less somnolent periods, and less belligerence, and family members noted that his pre-morbid behavior patterns had essentially returned. Unfortunately, his symptoms relapsed after this initial period of remission. Nine months after initial FMT, the patient received a second FMT. This FMT was preceded by a more aggressive antibacterial pretreatment regimen with well-absorbed antibiotics that could target not only intraluminal organisms but also have the potential to reach extraluminal sites, including the villous brush border and intestinal crypts, where microbes may persist in biofilms or less metabolically active states, and included monthly maintenance dosing of FMT for six months. Additional complementary approaches were added because the patient was so severely affected and wished to do anything possible to avoid relapse. The patient remained asymptomatic/in remission for over 16 months after the second FMT, and advanced his diet to include more complex carbohydrates. Metagenomic analyses of the stool samples collected before (Remission, n = 1; Flare, n = 5) and after the first (Remission, n = 6; Flare, n = 1) and second FMT (Remission, n = 6) correlate closely with the patient’s symptoms. In particular, principal coordinate analysis demonstrated that after the first FMT, the composition of the patient’s gut microbiota during remission clustered closely with the FMT donor gut microbiota, but after his symptoms relapsed during a flare (yellow square), his gut microbial composition returned to clustering with his pre-FMT gut microbiota (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5a</a>). After the second FMT, the composition of the patient’s gut microbiota remained closely clustered together and distinctly different from pre-FMT and household partner gut microbiota. The bacterial strains that contributed most to the first and second principal components were <em>Escherichia coli_SGB10068, Firmicutes bacterium_CAG176, Alistipes putredinis_SGB2318,</em> and <em>Ruminococcus bromii_SGB4285</em> (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5b</a>). During flares of symptoms, the patient’s gut microbiota contained a high relative abundance of <em>E. coli,</em> without significantly increased relative abundance of <em>Klebsiella pneumoniae</em> or <em>Ruminococcus gnavus</em> (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5c</a>). Strain-level metagenomic profiling of <em>E. coli</em> by PanPhlAn demonstrated an increase in mean <em>E. coli</em> gene coverage across the <em>E. coli</em> genome during flares that diminished with remission (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5c</a>). The phylogenetic relationship of patient samples longitudinally over the course of two FMTs along with multiple sequence alignment of the genomes of different strains of <em>E. coli</em>, determined using StrainPhlAn, demonstrates clustering of samples based on timing of FMT (<a href="#F14" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 9b</a>). These strain-level differences correlated well with the patient’s blood alcohol concentration, serum AST and ALT, and abundance of metabolic pathways associated with fermentation of alcohol (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5c</a>). The patient’s symptoms correlated inversely with the fraction of post-FMT strains shared with pre-FMT, and correlated positively with the fraction of post-FMT strains shared with the donor and percent engraftment (<a href="#F14" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 9c</a>)<sup><a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a></sup>. These metagenomic sequencing results were confirmed by single colony isolation of ethanol-producing strains of <em>E. coli</em> from this patient’s stool samples, which was more resistant to ethanol than an <em>E. coli</em> strain isolated from the donor (<a href="#F15" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 10</a>).</p>

<p id="P20">In this study, we comprehensively characterize a cohort of patients with rigorously documented ABS and their household partners, including untargeted gut microbiome analysis. We demonstrate that fecal microbiota from patients with ABS produce significantly more ethanol in culture compared with asymptomatic household partners. We also demonstrate that the predominant pathobionts responsible for ABS in most patients are bacteria in the Proteobacteria phylum rather than fungi, as previously reported<sup><a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup>. Additionally, known fermentation pathways are overabundant in the microbiomes of the majority of patients with ABS, providing a molecularly consistent explanation for pathologic ethanol production by these patients’ gut microbiota during symptomatic flares.</p>
<p id="P21">Our study found that flare microbiome samples had a notable enrichment of genes involved in menaquinol and demethylmenaquinol biosynthesis, which are essential for anaerobic ATP-generating redox reactions<sup><a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a></sup>. This increased abundance, coupled with the elevated availability of electron acceptors, may contribute to the pathologic production of ethanol<sup><a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a></sup>. In contrast, remission samples showed a significant enrichment of genes involved in the tricarboxylic (TCA) cycle from acetate-producing bacteria, suggesting that enhanced ethanol metabolism by acetate-producing bacteria may underlie spontaneous symptom resolution. These results imply that targeting ethanol metabolism may offer an alternative or synergistic therapeutic approach for managing ABS. An important area of further study will be to elucidate exact genomic differences that lead to pathogenicity and alcohol tolerance in pathogenic bacterial strains from patients with ABS, and the effect of these pathogenic strains on the larger gut microbial community.</p>
<p id="P22">There is currently no consensus or standard therapy for ABS. Case reports have previously documented successful use of FMT <sup><a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a>,<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a></sup>. Here, we demonstrate the successful treatment of a patient with ABS using FMT and identified that the treatment is associated with a significant reduction in bacteria of the Proteobacteria phylum and fermentation pathway gene enrichment. We hypothesize that the longer, ongoing period of remission after the second FMT could be related to a more vigorous, systemic antibacterial treatment, including antibiotics with higher tissue levels. The initial antibiotic regimen was chosen to have primarily luminal effects and minimize systemic side effects. Additionally, monthly maintenance dosing and administration of a resistant starch may have contributed.</p>
<p id="P23">Although the sample size of this study is small, a major strength of this study is that all enrolled patients had rigorously documented ABS. An additional strength is the use of patients’ household partners, with similar environment and diets, as controls, in addition to the use of healthy controls from unaffected households. The sex differences observed in our study are consistent with existing case reports (out of the 24 previously reported cases where patient sex was identified, 19 (79%) were male).</p>
<p id="P24">While our study includes only patients with ABS with extreme levels of gut microbial dysbiosis and pathologic gut microbial ethanol production sufficient to result in detectable peripheral blood alcohol concentrations after hepatic metabolism, other studies have reported low levels of gut microbial ethanol production in patients with diabetes, and have implicated pathologic gut microbial ethanol production in the pathogenesis of metabolic-dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)<sup><a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a>,<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>-<a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a></sup>. The levels of gut microbial ethanol production in these cases are low enough to be completely metabolized by the liver and not cause systemic alcohol toxicity<sup><a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a>,<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a></sup>. This raises a broader question of how prevalent gut microbial ethanol production is in the general population and how widespread the pathological implications could be. The approach used in the experiments described may be applicable to diseases beyond ABS, and therapies targeting gut microbial ethanol production warrant further investigation. Additionally, our work highlights the importance of the gut microbiome and microbial metabolites to human health.</p></section><section id="S9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">METHODS</h2>
<section id="S10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study design and study populations</h3>
<section id="S11"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Auto-Brewery Syndrome cohort</h4>
<p id="P25">We performed a prospective, observational, study of patients with Auto-brewery Syndrome including serial stool sampling. Patients were recruited via the Auto-Brewery Syndrome Information and Research, Inc. non-profit 501(c)3 organization and their support groups. Inclusion criteria for patients with Auto-brewery Syndrome include age over 18 years and measured increased blood alcohol concentration, either spontaneously or after exposure to oral glucose challenge, while the patient is supervised in a clinical setting without alcohol consumption. Inclusion criteria for household partners (HHP) were that they lived with a confirmed patient with ABS in the same household. Eligible persons were at least 18 years of age. Patients with Auto-brewery Syndrome included in our study all had documentation of serial rise in blood alcohol concentration as measured by serum ethanol levels (86%) or breathalyzer (14%) while in a supervised clinical setting (or in their home in the presence of a medical professional for one participant) in the documented absence of alcohol consumption. Patients who had obtained a diagnosis of Auto-brewery Syndrome by other diagnostic methods such as culture of duodenal aspirate or stool culture but did not have documented rise of blood alcohol concentration in a supervised clinical setting in the absence of alcohol consumption were excluded from the study. Patients’ household partners (HHPs), who did not exhibit any symptoms of Auto-brewery Syndrome and blood alcohol concentrations were negative, were also enrolled and dyads were linked by a household ID. Within the recruited patient pool, four household pairs were studied at baseline and then re-enrolled under new household IDs for repeat biospecimen collection after the patients received significant microbiome-altering agents that affected their symptoms (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Extended Fig. 1b</a>). Specifically, one of these four patients (household 4) underwent fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) as treatment as part of this study, was studied for over 4 years, and re-enrolled after FMT as household 19. Another (household 5) underwent FMT from a private source, went into remission for 10 months, was re-enrolled as household 20, then developed a relapse of symptoms. A third (household 6) was initially in remission for several years in our study, then subsequently moved and re-developed symptoms after 26 months, and was re-enrolled as household 21. The last (household 8) received broad spectrum antibiotics and antifungals, went into remission for three months, was re-enrolled as household 22, then subsequently developed a flare.</p>
<p id="P26">Baseline demographic data and laboratory test results were collected and a comprehensive clinical history was obtained from each participant. Fasting blood samples were collected; serum and plasma samples were frozen and stored at −80°C until use. At least 10 grams of stool were collected from each patient during remissions and flares of clinical intoxication, defined by the presence of both symptoms of intoxication and a positive alcohol breath test. Samples were collected from household partners within 24 hours of patient samples. Concurrent blood alcohol concentrations were measured by an FDA-approved portable breathalyzer (BACtrack, San Francisco, CA), and results were recorded by the patient. Stool samples were immediately frozen in the patient’s freezer and shipped within 24-48 hours on dry ice to UC San Diego and stored at −80°C until use. The Auto-brewery Syndrome Registry was approved by the UCSD Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.</p></section><section id="S12"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)</h4>
<p id="P27">Subject A20 and his household partner connected the research groups requesting consideration of fecal transplant, as his Auto-brewery Syndrome had begun after multiple courses of ciprofloxacin for chronic prostatitis secondary to prostate enlargement which was ultimately surgically corrected. Empiric antifungal therapy, virtual total elimination of dietary carbohydrates and alternative/ complementary medical treatments had failed to durably suppress this subject’s ongoing flares of documented intoxication with serious medical and social consequences. IRB and FDA approval (Single Patient Expanded Access IND 28390/PI Hohmann) was obtained for single donor, capsule-based fecal transplant, performed after written informed consent was obtained. The subject received oral antibiotic pre-treatment (rifaximin, erythromycin and neomycin for 3 days) and a standard pre-colonoscopy purge with polyethylene glycol solution to empty the GI tract of food and stool, prior to administering 5 doses of 15 FMT capsules over a week’s time. The subject did well for 3 months with marked improvement in health status (by both self and family reports), and absence of intoxication flares, but relapsed thereafter. Repeat FMT was proposed to regulators, this time pre-treating with vancomycin, metronidazole and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole orally for 3 days, followed by a purge. Whereas rifaximin and neomycin, used in the first regimen, are primarily intraluminal antibiotics with minimal systemic absorption, metronidazole and trimethoprim/ sulfamethoxazole are systemically absorbed antibiotics aimed to target both intraluminal organisms and reach extraluminal sites, including the villous brush border and intestinal crypts, where microbes may persist in biofilms or less metabolically active states. On repeat treatment, the subject received 3 doses of 15 capsules after pre-treatment, followed by monthly maintenance dosing of 15 capsules (derived from the same donor) without pre-treatment for 6 months. FMT capsules were generated as previously described<sup><a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a></sup> from the same healthy unrelated donor, and the subject was NPO for 3 hours before and one hour after dosing. The patient was also directed to take one heaping tablespoon daily of potato starch (Bob’s Red Mill, Milwaukie, OR), comprised of approximately 60% resistant starch<sup><a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a>,<a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">29</a></sup>, dissolved in water, and a commercially available combination probiotic, one capsule daily, containing <em>Bifidobacteria infantis, Clostridium butyricum, Clostridium beijerinckii, Anaerobutyricum hallii</em>, and <em>Akkermansia muciniphilia</em> (Metabolic Daily, Pendulum, San Francisco, CA) in an effort to promote persistence of the transplanted microbiome<sup><a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">30</a></sup>.</p></section><section id="S13"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Inflammatory bowel disease cohort</h4>
<p id="P28">Fecal samples were acquired from a cohort of patients with inflammatory bowel disease, including 20 patients with ulcerative colitis and 20 patients with Crohn's disease, identified from a convenience sampling biobank at the University of California, San Diego<sup><a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a></sup>. Data on demographics, disease characteristics, treatment history, and clinical disease activity were collected longitudinally using standardized tools, including the partial Mayo score and Crohn’s disease activity index. Fecal samples were also acquired from a cohort of 15 non-obese controls without any known disease who consumed less than 10 grams of alcohol per day, as published previously<sup><a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a></sup>.</p></section></section><section id="S14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bacterial cultures</h3>
<p id="P29">100 mg of stool collected from patients during flare and remission and from their household partners was cultured, which consisted of 10 mL of Luria Broth (LB) media supplemented with 2 mM glucose and 2 mM cysteine in anaerobic conditions using hungate tubes. This concentration of glucose was chosen to emulate physiologic glucose concentrations in the intestine<sup><a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a></sup>. Cultures were untreated (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig 1a</a>-<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">c</a>, <a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Extended Data Fig. 4</a>) or treated with 0.05% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, vehicle), 10 μg/mL amphotericin B in DMSO, or 100 μg/mL chloramphenicol in DMSO (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1d</a>). Samples were collected at 0, 6, and 24 hours of culturing, centrifuged, and filtered. Ethanol concentrations were assessed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a 1260 Infinity II LC system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an HPX-87H (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA) with a lower limit of detection of 5 mg/dL or by using an enzyme-based kit (Abcam) with a lower limit of detection of 50 ng/dL.</p></section><section id="S15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bacterial strain isolation and culture</h3>
<p id="P30">To isolate high ethanol-producing bacterial strains, fecal samples from patients with Auto-brewery Syndrome were cultured in Luria Broth medium supplemented with 5% ethanol to select for bacteria capable of growth in high ethanol concentrations, under anaerobic and aerobic conditions at 37°C for 24 hours, then plated onto agar plates. 100 single colonies were isolated at random, grown in 5mL of LB media for another 24 hours, and DNA was isolated using the QIAamp<sup>®</sup> Fast DNA Stool Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For gel analysis, the purified DNA was mixed with 1 volume of Loading Dye and 5 volumes of purified DNA, and the solution was loaded onto the gel after mixing. Purified PCR amplicons were submitted for Sanger sequencing and bacterial identities were determined by comparing 16S sequences to two public databases, NCBI Microbial Blast and the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP).</p>
<p id="P31">To isolate <em>E. coli</em> strains from the FMT donor used in the FMT trial, their fecal sample was cultured in LB media without ethanol for 6 hours, then plated on <em>E. coli</em>-selective agar plates (CHROMagar, Saint-Denis, France), and single colonies were isolated at random. Individual <em>E. coli</em> strains from subject A20 and the FMT donor were grown in cultures containing 0, 1, 5, and 10% ethanol in LB media supplemented with 2 mM glucose and 2 mM cysteine under anaerobic conditions and growth was measured longitudinally over 24 hours using OD600.</p></section><section id="S16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Fungal colony isolation</h3>
<p id="P32">To assess the presence of fungal colonies in stool microbiota, 30–50 mg of stool from patients and HHPs were resuspended in Yeast Extract Peptone Dextrose (YPD) media at a 1% (w/v) concentration in culture tubes, vortexed to ensure thorough homogenization, incubated at 30°C for 4 hours, and plated onto YPD agar plates supplemented with 100 μg/mL chloramphenicol and 100 μg/mL gentamicin to inhibit bacterial growth. The plates were incubated for 48 hours at 30°C and fungal colonies were assessed. <em>Candida albicans</em> was separately plated as a positive control.</p></section><section id="S17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metagenomic sequencing and analysis</h3>
<p id="P33">50 mg of stool from patients with Auto-brewery Syndrome in remission and flare, their household partners, and healthy controls were submitted for metagenomic sequencing (minimum of 1 million reads per sample) by the UC San Diego Microbiome Core. The Microbiome Core performed nucleic acid extractions utilizing previously published protocols<sup><a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a></sup>. Briefly, samples were purified using the MagMAX Microbiome Ultra Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and automated on KingFisher Flex robots (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Blank controls and mock communities (Zymo Research Corporation, USA), which included <em>Listeria monocytogenes, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, Salmonella enterica, Lactobacillus fermentum, Enterococcus faecalis, Staphylococcus aureus, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Cryptococcus neoformans</em>, were included per extraction plate, which were carried through all downstream processing steps. DNA was quantified using a PicoGreen fluorescence assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and metagenomic libraries were prepared with KAPA HyperPlus kits (Roche Diagnostics, USA) in a miniaturized 1/5th reaction volume format, and automated on EpMotion automated liquid handlers (Eppendorf, Germany). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus sequencing platform with paired-end 150 bp cycles at the Institute for Genomic Medicine (IGM), UC San Diego. A total of 87 samples were sequenced, including 21 samples from household partners, 22 samples from patients with ABS in remission, 26 samples from patients during a flare, and an additional 18 samples from our FMT patient and household partner during and after FMTs. 1-5 million reads were generated per sample after processing with kneaddata. Three patients with ABS provided more than one sample during the flare condition and these samples were averaged for each patient. Samples were not averaged for patients who provided only one sample during remission and one sample during flare. All household partners contributed one sample each and their samples were not averaged.</p>
<p id="P34">To account for the potential contribution of sex-differences between patients with Auto-brewery Syndrome and their household partners, published gut microbiome metagenomic sequencing data from age, sex, and BMI-matched healthy controls from the United States was downloaded and analyzed as an additional comparator for microbial taxonomic and functional analyses. Briefly, the metadata for healthy controls enrolled through the iMSMS Consortium<sup><a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">34</a></sup> was downloaded through the NCBI SRA (accession number PRJEB32762) and optimal pair matching was performed using the “MatchIt” package in R for the variables of age, sex, and BMI. The published metagenomic sequencing data from these 22 matched individuals were downloaded and analyzed as below.</p>
<p id="P35">Raw metagenomic reads were pre-processed with KneadData (KneadData version 0.12.0, Trimmomatic version 0.33, Bowtie version 2.5.3), for quality control, filtering, and removal of contaminating host (human) and viral reads<sup><a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a></sup>. Taxonomic and functional profiling was performed using the HUMAnN 3 (version 3.8) pipeline<sup><a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a></sup> with MetaPhlAn 4<sup><a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">36</a></sup> (version 4.0.6, database version mpa_vOct22_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202212) and Diamond (version 2.1.9, full UniRef90 database). Mapping to MetaCyc reactions, KEGG orthogroups (KOs), Pfam domains, and Gene Ontology (GO) databases was performed.</p></section><section id="S18"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Strain level analyses</h3>
<p id="P36">Strains of <em>Escherichia coli</em> were identified in the metagenomes and compared across samples using StrainPhlan<sup><a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">36</a>,<a href="#R37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R37">37</a></sup>, a program within MetaPhlan (v4.1.1), and PanPhlan (v3.1)<sup><a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a>,<a href="#R38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R38">38</a></sup>, respectively. StrainPhlan was used to align metagenome reads to <em>E. coli</em> consensus marker gene sequences from the marker database ChocoPhlan version mpa vJune23CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202403 (provided by MetaPhlan) and a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was generated. The MSA was used to create a phylogenetic tree of samples containing the <em>E. coli</em> marker genes based on their phylogenetic similarity to the marker genes. The MSA and the phylogenetic tree were visualized using the ggtree (v3.14.0) package in R (v4.4.3). The length of the MSA was 2,996 nucleotides.</p>
<p id="P37">PanPhlan was used to determine the depth of coverage for metagenome reads mapped to 10,834 <em>E. coli</em> reference genomes representing the <em>E. coli</em> pangenome from the UniProt Reference Clusters (UniRef) database. A presence/absence table was also generated based on the depth of coverage of the samples’ alignment to the <em>E. coli</em> reference genomes.</p>
<p id="P38">SameStr (v1.2024.08)<sup><a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">39</a></sup> was used to determine the presence of bacterial strains in our samples, using the marker database ChocoPhlan version mpa vJune23CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202403 from MetaPhlan. Specifically, SameStr compared the samples in a pair-wise fashion to determine if two samples shared specific clades and/or strains with one another. SameStr was used to examine engraftment in our FMT patient. Clades at the species genome bin (i.e., SGB) level were compared between pre-FMT samples, post-FMT samples and the donor. The fraction of clades found to be shared between the pre-FMT and post-FMT samples (i.e., the number of shared clades found in the post-FMT sample divided by the total number of clades in the pre-FMT sample) and the fraction of clades shared between the donor and the post-FMT samples (i.e., the number of shared clades found in the post-FMT sample divided by total the number of clades in the donor) were determined. Percent engraftment was calculated by considering the number of shared strain events between post-FMT samples and the donor, such that each shared strain event was attributed to a clade not found in the pre-FMT sample.</p></section><section id="S19"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) rRNA sequencing and analysis</h3>
<p id="P39">The UC San Diego Microbiome Core performed nucleic acid extractions on stool samples from patients with Auto-brewery Syndrome in remission and flare and their household partners using the same protocol as for metagenomic sequencing. The ITS2 region was amplified using primers ITS3-2024F (5’-GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC-3’) and ITS4-2409R (5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’), the product was purified, the libraries were prepared and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and ITS2 amplicon sequences were demultiplexed and adapters were removed (Novogene). The quality of the sequences was assessed via FastQC<sup><a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">40</a></sup>. The FastQC reports were used to determine where reads should be trimmed, and the reads were trimmed and filtered with BBduk<sup><a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a></sup>. After trimming, the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2 version 1.26) pipeline<sup><a href="#R42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R42">42</a></sup> was used via the RStudio environment to assign reads to amplicon sequence variants using the UNITE fungal ITS database (version 10, published on 04-04-2024) as the reference. The compositional relative abundance across samples was calculated and fungal genera associations were compared across the patient groups using Maaslin2<sup><a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">43</a></sup>. The arcsine square root transformation was used by Maaslin2 to transform the fungal genera relative abundances before running a linear mixed effects model. A minimum abundance threshold of 0.00001 and a minimum prevalence of 20% was used. To analyze beta diversity, Aitchison distances were calculated by transforming amplicon sequence variant counts with a center-log ratio transformation, then calculating the Euclidean distance. Aitchison distance was input for principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Permutational multivariate analyses of variance (PERMANOVA) were performed to determine if there were significant differences in microbial composition across groups.</p></section><section id="S20"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolomics analysis</h3>
<p id="P40">Untargeted metabolomics and targeted short chain fatty acid quantitative analysis was performed by MtoZ Biolabs using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Briefly, for untargeted metabolomics, 50 mg of stool was homogenized in methanol, cleaned, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. LC-MS was conducted using a Thermo Fisher Scientific U3000 capillary high-performance liquid chromatography system and a Q Exactive mass spectrometer. Data analysis was performed using Progenesis QI software for peak alignment, baseline correction, noise removal, and compound identification and quantification.</p>
<p id="P41">For short-chain fatty acid analysis, 50 mg of stool was homogenized and solvent extracted using ultrapure water and methanol. The extracted samples were then derivatized using 3-nitrophenylhydrazine (3-NPH) and 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC). Chromatographic separation was performed using Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-CLASS ultra performance liquid chromatography. Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted using a Waters XEVO TQ-S Micro tandem quadrupole mass spectrometer. Data processing was performed using Masslynx quantitative software, with standard curves generated for quantification.</p>
<p id="P42">Metabolites detected in less than 95% of samples were excluded. A centered log-ratio transformation was performed on metabolomics data prior to calculating spearman correlations between metabolites and differentially abundant microbial metabolic pathways. P-values were adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control false discovery rate.</p></section><section id="S21"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="P43">All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R statistical software (R version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) within the RStudio integrated development environment. Continuous patient characteristics are presented as median and interquartile range. The Fisher exact test and Kruskal-Wallis test were used to compare groups for binary and continuous variables, respectively. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value equal to or less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For analysis of metagenomic sequencing results, PCoA and PERMANOVA were performed to determine if there were significant differences in microbial composition across groups, after confirmation of homogenous dispersion between groups. MaAsLin2 was used to identify associations between host phenotype and gut microbial taxonomic and metagenomic features<sup><a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">43</a></sup>. Patients and their household partners were grouped into households and the households’ random effects were controlled for. To identify gut microbial species associated with disease state, MaAsLin2 was used with a minimum abundance threshold of 0.00001 and a minimum prevalence of 10%. To identify microbial metabolic pathways associated with disease state, MaAsLin2 was used with a minimum abundance threshold of 0.00001 and a minimum prevalence of 20%. P-values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control for the false discovery rate and q-values &lt; 0.05 were considered statistically significant.</p></section></section><section id="S25"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Extended Data</h2>
