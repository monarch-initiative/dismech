Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are single stranded nucleic acids that target 
RNA. The US Food and Drug Administration has approved ASOs for several diseases. 
ASOs utilize three principal modes of action (MOA). The first MOA is initiated 
by base-pairing between the ASO and its target mRNA, followed by RNase 
H-dependent mRNA degradation. The second MOA is triggered by ASOs that occlude 
splice acceptor sites in pre-mRNAs leading to skipping of a mutation-bearing 
exon. The third MOA involves ASOs that sterically hinder mRNA function, often 
inhibiting translation. ASOs contain a variety of modifications to the 
sugar-phosphate backbone and bases that stabilize the ASO or render them 
resistant to RNase activity. RNase H-dependent ASOs include inotersen and 
eplontersen (for hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis), fomiversen (for 
opportunistic cytomegalovirus infection), mipomersen (for familial 
hypercholesterolemia), and tofersen [for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)]. 
Splice modulating ASOs include nursinersen (for spinal muscular atrophy) and 
eteplirsen, golodirsen, viltolarsen, and casimersen (all for the treatment of 
Duchenne muscular dystrophy). In addition, a designer ASO, milasen, was used to 
treat a single individual afflicted with Batten disease. Since ASO design relies 
principally upon knowledge of mRNA sequence, the bench to bedside pipeline for 
ASOs is expedient compared with protein-directed drugs. [Graphical abstract 
available.].

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are short single-stranded deoxyribonucleotide sequences that base-pair with targeted cellular mRNA molecules to achieve pharmacological effects at the level of mRNA function [1]. ASOs are a subgroup of a broader category of “transcript-targeted” drugs that bind to RNA, which include small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), small molecules that bind RNA, guide RNAs (gRNAs), and RNA editors [2–4]. All ASOs initiate their action by binding to their target RNAs to form an RNA/DNA heteroduplex. ASO/RNA heteroduplexes then trigger mRNA degradation, alter pre-mRNA splicing, or sterically hinder translation, each a distinct mode of action (MOA). ASOs can also target the destruction of viral RNAs. Ultimately, ASOs can reduce protein production, restore expression of functional proteins (via splicing modulation), or eliminate viral RNAs.

To date, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved ten ASO drugs and several more are in the research and development pipeline. This review provides an overview of the general MOAs and design features of ASO drugs, as well as how they target pre-mRNAs, mRNAs, and viral RNAs. Additionally, we present specific information on the MOAs of each of the FDA-approved ASO drugs and discuss likely future applications of ASO drugs.

There are three major MOAs for ASO action: (1) destruction of the target RNA via ASO-mediated recruitment of endogenous RNase H activity, (2) modulation of splicing events in the conversion of pre-mRNAs to mature mRNAs, and (3) steric hindrance of mRNA regions, such as initiator methionine codons (Fig. 1). ASOs can affect target RNAs in the cytoplasm but can also enter the nucleus and bind to pre-mRNA to perturb mRNA maturation by inhibiting 5′ cap formation, inhibiting mRNA splicing, or activating nuclear RNase H. These mechanisms can prevent the maturation of a pre-mRNA to a mature mRNA, thus modulating protein formation [1]. ASOs can be designed to block splice acceptor sites in pre-mRNAs and splice out exons containing disease-causing mutations.

The first of these principal MOAs involves the recruitment of endogenous RNase activities to the ASO/RNA heteroduplex, which then triggers the RNase H enzyme to cleave the targeted RNA within the heteroduplex region. ASOs are taken up by cellular endocytosis, and they then hybridize with the target mRNA in the cytoplasm or nucleus. The formation of an ASO/mRNA heteroduplex recruits RNase H, and this results in the selective degradation of the bound mRNA. After internal cleavage, other nucleases (typically exonucleases) act upon the RNA resulting in the complete and specific degradation of the targeted transcript [5]. RNase H enzymes are present in all mammalian cells. RNase H enzymes are members of the broader family of endonucleases that degrade RNA through internal cleavage mechanisms [5]. Human cells have two types of RNase H enzymes, RNase H1 and RNase H2 [5]. RNase H1 is expressed in all stages of the cell cycle, while RNase H2 is expressed exclusively in G2/M [6]. RNase H1 is a single protein present in both nuclei and the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. Its function is to cleave RNA within regions of RNA/DNA heteroduplexes [7]. RNase H2 is a heterotrimeric enzyme specialized in the cleavage of single ribonucleotides erroneously incorporated into DNA [7]. During DNA replication, transcription, and repair, RNA/DNA hybrids (Okazaki fragments) form to prime DNA synthesis, especially on the lagging strand. However, RNA primers at the 5′ end of Okazaki fragments are removed by exonuclease action and FEN1 during DNA synthesis [8]. The mechanism of ASOs involves the recruitment of RNase H1, which mediates the cleavage of an ASO-mRNA heteroduplex [7]. Therefore, for this review, only RNase H1 will be considered since it is the species of RNase H that participates directly in ASO mechanisms of action. In the presence of an RNase H1-dependent ASO, the ASO severely reduces the level of mRNAs in the cytoplasm and the levels of their corresponding pre-mRNAs inside the nucleus [9]. This reduction of specific mRNA levels occurs after the initial formation of the ASO-mRNA heteroduplex triggers RNase H activity leading to mRNA degradation.

The second major MOA of ASOs is the inhibition or alteration of pre-mRNA splicing, which alters the inclusion of exons that are spliced into the mature mRNA. In normal gene expression, transcription, intron removal (splicing), and protein expression (translation) occur [1]. In some genetic diseases caused by splice-site mutations, ASOs can induce the production of mRNAs that restore the expression of normal functional proteins. Certain rare human genetic disorders are caused by mutations in splice donor or splice acceptor sites, leading to the expression of mutant (and often truncated) proteins. In some cases, ASOs cause restoration of normal splicing or the skipping of exons that contain mutations, resulting in a therapeutic effect.

Transcription is a process that produces mRNAs on the basis of the sequence of the sense strand of DNA via the action of RNA polymerase II. The initial transcript (the pre-mRNA) is then subjected to splicing (intron removal) leading to the production of mature mRNA. The spliceosome is a ribonucleoprotein complex that contains spliceosomal proteins and functional RNAs, including U1, U2, U3, U4, and U6, which facilitate the removal of introns and ligation of exons. In many cases, multiple mRNA splice forms can be produced from a single pre-mRNA via alternative splicing. mRNAs are also subject to posttranscriptional modifications that regulate their function, often at the level of RNA stability [10].

Mature mRNAs contain defined segments, including the 5′-untranslated region (UTR), an open reading frame (ORF), and the 3′-UTR. Typically, the first methionine codon encountered by the ribosome (scanning from 5′ to 3′) defines the site of translational initiation and the boundary between the 5′-UTR and the ORF. In some cases, translation can be initiated internally at an internal ribosome entry site (IRE). The 5′-UTR controls ribosomal access to the initiator codon via secondary structures, which serve to modulate the activity of ribosomes during the initiation phase of translation. Some mRNA secondary structures can lead to premature transcriptional termination [11]. ORFs consist of nucleotide sequences between the start codon (AUG) and a stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UAG). 3′-UTRs are often longer than 5′-UTRs in humans and often contain regulatory elements, such as microRNA target recognition sequences and sites that specify polyadenylation. Overall, the 3′-UTR regulates the stability and translational efficiency of the mRNA.

Knowledge of the composition and position of key mRNA features can be used to rationally design ASO drugs. Regions in or near the 5′-methyguanosine cap and the 3′ poly-A tails are also attractive regions within an mRNA for targeting. Binding of ASOs near the 5′ cap can interfere with ribosomal recruitment while targeting initiator methionine codons can affect translational initiation. In general, such approaches are effective owing to steric hindrance of ribosomal association with mRNAs or the ability of the ribosome to initiate translation.

The modulation of splicing of pre-mRNAs is also an attractive approach. In fact, this MOA is utilized by the majority of FDA-approved ASO drugs, which target pre-mRNAs as opposed to mature mRNAs. This approach is made more effective because pre-mRNAs are less abundant compared with mature mRNAs in mammalian cells, which effectively increases the ratio of ASO to target when pre-mRNAs (as opposed to mature mRNAs) are targeted. Splice-modulating ASOs are typically designed to occlude splice donor or acceptor sites, thus leading to ASO-induced alternative splicing events. Using such approaches, it is possible to prevent inclusion of an exon containing a disease-causing mutation into the final mRNA.

ASOs are designed to be 13–30 nucleotides in length and bind to a targeted mRNA or pre-mRNA via Watson–Crick base pairing. ASO length can impact both efficacy and safety. Although shorter oligonucleotides have sufficient affinity to stably pair with their targets, shorter ASOs are prone to off-target effects. For oligonucleotides shorter than 12-mers, there is a theoretical chance of over 97% that another human mRNA sequence will contain a perfect match. For ASOs in the 13–16 nucleotide range, this probability decreases to less than 57%, and for those longer than 16 nucleotides, the probability is less than 5% [12]. Although ASOs are designed to be perfect complements for their target mRNA, mismatches can sometimes be tolerated without severely compromising ASO efficacy [12]. However, mismatches increase the likelihood that an ASO can also bind to a similar but unintended RNA target [12, 13].

The efficacy of an ASO depends in part upon its ability to hybridize to the target sequence on its complementary mRNA. Single-stranded mRNAs fold into a thermodynamically favorable secondary structure, which may hinder the ability of an ASO to bind [14]. Regions accessible for ASO binding typically include the terminal ends of target RNAs, internal loops, joint sequences, and bulges of ten or more consecutive nucleotides, and these are promising sites for the design of ASOs [13]. Many ASOs are also designed to modulate exon-intron splicing of pre-mRNAs. For these purposes, ASOs are often designed to bind near splice acceptor sites present within pre-mRNAs. Splice site modulating ASOs often contain high guanine-cytosine content and there is a significant positive correlation between binding efficacy and the presence of conserved local motifs containing sequences, such CCAC and TCCC [15, 16].

Once present in the cell, oligonucleotides are readily degraded by intracellular nucleases prior to reaching their targets, conferring an intrinsic impediment to ASO therapies, which must be overcome [17]. Xeno-nucleic acids (XNAs) exhibit improved chemical and biological stability through various modifications to the backbones, sugars, or bases of oligonucleotides. Therefore, ASOs typically contain chemically modified sugars and bases to improve their intracellular stability and ability to reach their targets. The broad functional potential of XNAs expands the therapeutic capabilities of ASOs, making XNA design features useful for the optimization of ASO pharmacodynamic action. The chemical modifications of ASOs can be broadly classified by those altering the backbone, such as phosphorothioate (PS), phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomer (PMO), and peptide nucleic acid (PNA), the 2′-sugar position, including locked nucleic acid (LNA), 2′-O-methyl nucleotide (2′-OMe), 2′-O-methoxyethyl nucleotide (2′-MOE), and 2′-fluoro nucleotides or the nucleobase (5′-methylcytosine). 2′-O-sugar modifications are used to increase the stability of RNA duplexes in ASO therapies [18]. Substitutions in the 2′-O-position often impede their ability to activate RNase H cleavage of the target RNA, so these are commonly inter-spersed with PS nucleotides that lack the 2′-O-sugar modification [18].

Chemical modifications used in the FDA-approved ASO therapies include PS backbones, 2′-MOE substitutions, 5′-methylcytosine base substitutions, and PMO oligomer substitutions. Each substitution confers an advantage for the ASO, usually increasing intracellular stability.

These are organized into two themes: MOAs that involve recruitment of RNase H leading to target RNA degradation (Sect. 5.1) and those that alter pre-mRNA splicing (Sect. 5.2). Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (hATTR) is a fatal autosomal dominant genetic disorder caused by heterozygous mutations in the TTR gene, which encodes the protein transthyretin. An inherited disease allele of the TTR gene gives rise to a mutant form of transthyretin that aggregates and accumulates as amyloid deposits in the nervous system, heart, kidneys, and eyes of affected individual [19]. It is estimated that nearly 50,000 people are affected, and symptoms include peripheral neuropathy, numbness, lower extremity weakness, and loss of sensation of pain or temperature [20]. The disease is more prevalent in the age group > 30 years old and primarily occurs in adulthood. Death occurs 3–15 years after disease onset and often occurs owing to malnutrition or cardiac dysfunction [21].

Transthyretin is a transport protein for vitamin A and thyroxine (T4). There are four exons within the TTR gene that encode a 127 amino acid polypeptide. Four identical monomers make up the TTR protein complex. Point mutations in the TTR gene result in mutant monomers, which can destroy the TTR complex’s homotetrameric structure, leading to amyloid formation and hATTR. Until recently, the only treatment option for hATTR was liver transplantation since this is the primary organ that produces TTR [21].

Inotersen (brand name Tegsedi, Ionis Pharmaceuticals, Carlsbad, CA, USA) is a second generation ASO that binds the TTR mRNA, leading to its degradation and thereby reducing transthyretin production. Its MOA is to bind to the TTR mRNA within the 3′-UTR by complementary base pairing (Fig. 2). The inotersen/DNA heteroduplex then recruits RNase H1 to degrade TTR mRNA, which slows the progression of the hATTR disease [21]. The sequence of Inotersen is 5′-dT*-dMeC*-dT*-dT*-dG*-dG-dT-dT-dA-dMeC-dA-dT-dG-dA-dA-dA*-dT*-dMeC*-dMeC*-dMeC*−3′ (where * = 2′-MOE; Me = 5-methyl, and d = deoxy; all linkages are PS) [22]. Inotersen reduces or destroys both mutant and wild type TTR mRNAs, which is beneficial for hATTR patients. However, this treatment lowers levels of the normal protein, leading to a reduced ability of treated patients to transport retinol (Vitamin A). Therefore, patients receiving inotersen are treated with Vitamin A supplementation [22]. The drug is administered subcutaneously and is given at a dose of 248 mg once weekly [23].

Recently, a second drug has been developed. This drug, eplontersen (brand name Wainua, Ionis Pharmaceuticals, Carlsbad, CA, USA and AstraZeneca, Cambridge, UK), was approved by the US FDA in December 2023, and as with inoterson, targets the TTR mRNA for degradation [24]. However, eplontersen recognizes a slightly different sequence within the TTR mRNA compared with inotersen, though the targeted sequences of both drugs partially overlap. Eplontersen is conjugated to GalNac (triantennary N-acetyl galactosamine), a ligand that causes targeted uptake of this drug into hepatocytes. Hepatocytes are the primary producers of TTR protein production. The 5′ GalNac moiety within eplontersen causes this ASO to become concentrated in hepatocytes, thus destroying TTR mRNAs at their source, resulting in a reduction of TTR levels by about 90%. [25] The GalNac conjugation allows eplontersen to be dosed at 45 mg once monthly using subcutaneous injection, a significantly reduced dose in amount and frequency compared with the unconjugated drug inotersen.

Half of the general population is latently infected by human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), a Herpesvirus. HCMV evades the immune system by downregulating human major histo-compatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules on the host cell’s surface, thus eliciting a degree of immune evasion. However, Natural Killer (NK) cells typically eliminate cells with downregulated human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I molecules; but HCMV also expresses a viral homologue of MHC molecules, which confers some protection against NK action as well [26].

In immunocompetent patients, primary HCMV infection manifests as mild flu-like illness followed by latent infection of myeloid progenitor cells. Latency persists throughout the patient’s life; however, periodic shedding may occur during illness [27]. Reactivation is inhibited by CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses. Acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) causes diminished T cell response, rendering patients immunocompromised and susceptible to infection, and leading to reactivation and dissemination of HCMV to target organs. When HCMV reaches retinal blood vessels, it enters the eye, infects vascular endothelial cells and produced full-thickness retinal necrosis and progressive blindness [28]. This pathology is termed HCMV retinitis and manifested in up to 80% of patients infected with both AIDS and HCMV [29].

The first stage of HCMV genome expression and replication involves the expression of viral RNA encoding the immediate-early gene products (IE) [30]. The major IE gene is expressed within hours and has a role in primary infection and viral reactivation [31]. IE2 is an 86 kDa nuclear phosphoprotein that binds in a sequence-specific manner to DNA in the host cell nucleus. IE2 acts as the principal transcriptional activator of the CMV early genes and inhibits apoptosis and inflammatory responses of the immune system [31].

Fomivirsen (brand name Vitravene, Novartis, Basel, Switzerland) is a 21-base synthetic PS oligonucleotide with the sequence: 5′-dG-dC-dG-dT-dT-dT-dG-dC-dT-dC-dT-dT-dC-dT-dT-dC-dT-dT-dG-dC-dG-3′ [32]. It inhibits HCMV replication and is not complementary to the HCMV DNA polymerase or any known mRNA sequence in humans [32]. Fomivirsen hybridizes to the IE2 protein’s target mRNA by Watson–Crick base-pairing. The DNA/RNA hybrid complex is then recognized by RNase H, resulting in cleavage, mRNA degradation, and reduced IE2 protein levels [30], thus blocking viral replication. By this means, fomivirsen acts as an effective therapeutic against retinal HCMV. Additionally, fomivirsen has a secondary and less potent sequence-independent MOA involving interaction with CMV virions and inhibition of viral absorption [32]. Fomivirsen’s PS modification substitutes sulfur for nonbridging oxygen in the backbone, conferring greater resistance to nuclease degradation.

In 1998, fomivirsen was the first ASO drug approved by the FDA. It was an intravitreal injection brought to market with an indication of HCMV retinitis in patients with AIDS who are intolerant to other treatments and who were insufficiently responsive to previous HCMV retinitis treatments (FDA.gov). Around the same time, antiretroviral combination therapies began to yield highly effective results, significantly reducing the incidence of opportunistic infections, such as HCMV in patients with AIDS [28]. Owing to the steep decrease in demand, fomivirsen was withdrawn from the market in 2001, just 3 years after its approval.

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an inherited disorder that affects apolipoprotein profiles typified by increased levels of low-density apolipoprotein particles (LDL), leading to early onset cardiovascular events [33]. Most cases of FH are caused by a mutation in the gene encoding the LDL receptor. There are two forms of FH, heterozygous FH, and homozygous FH. The homozygous form of FH is more severe. In untreated populations of heterozygotes, LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) levels range between 155 and 500 mg/dL, while LDL-C levels for homozygotes is greater than 500mg/dL [33]. Homozygous patients with FH should be treated with pharmaceuticals upon diagnosis. This includes the use of statin drugs that work by decreasing cholesterol biosynthesis by inhibiting the activity of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase. The dosage of mipomersen [brand name Kynamro, Genzyme (Sanofi), Cambridge, MA, USA] is 200 mg subcutaneously once per week, and it is estimated to reduce the LDL-C by 24–28% [33].

Mipomersen is a second-generation ASO drug that inhibits apolipoprotein B-100 synthesis and directly decreases levels of very low density lipoprotein (VLDL) and LDL [34]. Structurally, mipomersen is a single-stranded synthetic ASO that is 20 nucleotides in length. The sequence of mipomersen is 5′-dG*-dMeC*-dMeC*-dT*-dMeC*-dA-dG-dT-dMeC-dT-dG-dMeC-dT-dT-dMeC-dG*-dMeC*-dA*-dMeC*-dMeC*−3′ (where * = 2′-MOE; Me = 5-methyl; and d = deoxy; all linkages are PS) with a molecular weight of 7,954.8 Da. Mipomersen base-pairs to the ApoB mRNA in the conjoined region of exon 20 and 21 and inhibits the production of apolipoprotein B-100, which decreases LDL levels and improves FH. The binding of mipomersen to the apolipoprotein B-100 mRNA creates a substrate for RNase H (an RNA:DNA heteroduplex), which drives hydrolysis of the apolipoprotein B-100 mRNA, thus decreasing apolipoprotein B-100 levels in the cytoplasm and nucleus [35]. Since LDL particles ordinarily contain abundant ApoB-100, treatment with mipomersen reduces LDL levels.

The active metabolites of mipomersen are excreted through the urine and drug–drug interactions are negligible since mipomersen and its breakdown products do not interact with traditional metabolic enzymes, such as cytochrome P450s [36]. The most common side effects associated with the drug are a risk of hepatotoxicity and flu-like symptoms. However, mipomersen was correlated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, liver disease, injection site reactions, and flu-like symptoms [37]. Mipomersen is no longer marketed on the basis of concerns of hepatoxicity.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a severe and progressive neurological disease that causes degenerative changes in neurons of the central nervous system, including both the brain and spinal cord. Although ALS causes are multifactorial, a small subset (1–2%) of ALS patients harbor mutations in their superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene [38, 39]. In these patients, mutant SOD1 protein acts as a gain-of-function protein that results in neural toxicity, though the mechanism of neural toxicity is poorly understood. The ASO drug tofersen (brand name Qalsody, Ionis Pharmaceuticals, Carlsbad, CA, USA and Biogen, Cambridge, MA, USA) was developed by Biogen Pharmaceuticals to target mutant SOD1 mRNA in these individuals. Tofersen was provisionally approved by the FDA for treatment of these patients early in 2023, after a phase 3 trial showed possible slowing of progression of ALS in treated patients (though the results are not statistically significant owing to a very low patient enrollment in the study) [40].

Tofersen is an RNA-DNA-RNA gapmer ASO with a sequence of 5′-dMeC*PS-dA*-dG*PS-dG*-dA*PS-dTPS-dAPS-dMeCPS-dAPS-dTPS-dTPS-dTPS-dMeCPS-dTPS-dAPS-dMeC*-dA*PS-dG*-dMeC*PS-dT*−3′ (where * = 2′-MOE; Me = 5-methyl; and d = deoxy; PS = phosphorothioate backbone). Tofersen’s molecular formula is C230H317N72O123P19S15 with a molecular weight of 7127.86 Da. Tofersen base pairs with mutated SOD1 mRNA resulting in recruitment of RNase H and degradation of mutant SOD1 mRNA. A small study of SOD1-mutant ALS sufferers treated with tofersen showed a reduced expression of neurofilament light chain (NfL), a biomarker of ALS. Though this study and clinical trials are not yet conclusive, accelerated approval of tofersen by the FDA was granted owing to the severity of ALS and the possibility that tofersen might have some beneficial action for patients with the SOD1-mutant form of ALS. Tofersen is dosed at 100 mg contained in a 15 mL solution. Administration is by intrathecal injection via a lumbar puncture needle. Tofersen is administered as three initial loading doses (at 14-day intervals) followed by maintenance doses every 28 days thereafter. Further studies are warranted to determine if tofersen will prove efficacious in post-approval patient cohorts who are prescribed this drug.

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a genetic disorder that is characterized by progressive muscle degeneration and weakness owing to a lack of dystrophin protein [41]. This condition is an X-linked inherited neuromuscular disorder [42]. DMD presents during early childhood. Muscle weakness is progressive and generally leads to the patient becoming wheelchair-bound. DMD also increases risk of restrictive lung disease, cardiomyopathy, scoliosis, and educational and psychosocial issues [41].

DMD is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the dystrophin gene, DMD, located at Xp21.2. DMD encodes a cytoskeletal protein called dystrophin that keeps muscle cells intact by forming a component of the dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex that stabilizes the plasma membrane of muscle fibers [41]. Dystrophin protein provides a mechanical link between the cytoskeleton and extracellular matrix [43]. Without dystrophin, the stability of muscle cells and fibers is disrupted [43]. DMD has many causative mutations, including frameshift mutations, nucleotide deletions, duplications, and substitutions. Splice site mutations are known in DMD, and these alleles perturb the recognition of intron and exon junctions by the spliceosome leading to errors during the splicing process [44]. In DMD, these splice site mutations lead to altered reading frames containing premature stop codons that result in truncated nonfunctional dystrophin protein [45].

Research in DMD patients and mouse models indicates that acceptor splice site mutations can cause DMD. In one example, a causative mutation was found in the first two nucleotides of DMD intron 26, which causes splicing into an ectopic site and a resulting transcript with a premature stop codon [46]. Whether the mutation occurs in a splice donor or splice acceptor site, the spliceosome cannot recognize a proper intron-exon junction and the resulting mis-splicing yields a mutant mRNA. In such cases, the spliceosome will use alternative acceptor splice sites [47]. This is problematic because the spliceosome will exclude critical exons from the mature mRNA transcript that are needed for production of a fully functional dystrophin protein. Another problem occurs when a mis-spliced mRNA brings an out-of-frame stop codon into register, causing premature termination of translation and production of a mutant truncated protein. Overall, extensive sequencing of patient DNA has revealed that truncated dystrophin proteins are typically formed owing to splice site mutations [47].

The dystrophin gene is especially susceptible to mutations owing to its large size and the presence of 79 exons. Not all patients with DMD have the same mutation, but the majority of mutations, occurring in 65–70% of patients, are intragenic deletions that occur in a hotspot region spanning exons 45–53 of the dystrophin gene [41]. These deletions often cause frame shifts in the ORF [48]. Other dystrophin gene mutations that result in DMD are duplications and point mutations. Duplication and point mutations are less common than intragenic deletions [41]. Frame-shift mutations in DMD alter the reading frame of the primary transcript and bring premature stop codons into register, disrupting translation and dystrophin protein synthesis [49]. Such mutations in DMD result in shortened, unstable, and non-functional dystrophin proteins [41].

ASOs are emerging therapies for the treatment of DMD that can restore dystrophin protein function [50]. ASO therapy is the preferred treatment method owing to the challenges of gene-replacement therapy. The size of the dystrophin gene is prohibitively large such that inclusion of a full-length cDNA corresponding to the entire dystrophin mRNA into a viral vector is problematic [51]. ASOs currently approved for the treatment of DMD modulate the splicing of pre-mRNA. In DMD, ASO therapy focuses on exon skipping. In this strategy the ASO binds to the target pre-mRNA through conventional nucleotide base pairing and induces the skipping of an exon that contains a mutation [15]. Mechanistically, splice-modulating ASOs work via steric hindrance to block a specific splice donor/acceptor interaction in the spliceosome. This forces the spliceosome to utilize an alternative downstream splice acceptor site, thereby skipping a mutated exon containing a premature stop codon or other mutatio [15]. When the causative lesion is a frame-shift mutation, ASO-induced skipping of the mutant exon can restore the proper reading frame (though typically at the expense of a short exon) and thus allows translation to produce a functional dystrophin protein.

Eteplirsen (brand name Exondys 51, Sarepta Therapeutics, Cambridge, MA, USA), golodirsen (brand name Vyvondys 53, Sarepta Therapeutics, Cambridge, MA, USA), viltolarsen (brand name Viltepso, NS Pharma, Paramus, NJ, USA), and casimersen (brand name Amondys 45, Sarepta Therapeutics, Cambridge, MA, USA) are four ASOs currently approved for DMD treatment, and all have similar MOAs [51]. These ASOs have PMO backbones and bind to splice sites that cause the skipping of exons that contain frame shift mutations [51, 52] (Fig. 3). The PMO modification prevents the initiation of the RNase H pathway, and this allows the splice-modulating activity of these ASOs to proceed [43, 53]. Eteplirsen, golodirsen, viltolarsen, and casimersem all work by targeting specific exons in DMD pre-mRNA to modulate splicing.

These four DMD ASOs target the hotspot region containing exons 45–53 on the dystrophin gene [51], a region where the majority of DMD mutations occur. The exon skipping caused by these four ASOs restores the reading frame of the dystrophin primary transcript to allow translation of a readable pre-mRNA into a functioning dystrophin protein [54]. These four DMD ASOs will recognize their targeted splice junctions in the DMD pre-mRNA, and cause the splicing machinery to skip the targeted exon from the mature mRNA transcript [43]. The goal of this approach is to induce skipping of a specific exon containing a mutation, and restoration of reading frame to yield functional dystrophin protein. The general mechanism of ASOs for DMD treatment is to render mutant DMD alleles less harmful, thus reducing the severity of DMD [43]. The therapeutic outcome of ASO treatment is a dystrophin protein that is shorter and partially deleted owing to exon skipping but functional owing to reading frame restoration [55].

Eteplirsen was developed by Sarepta Therapeutics, approved in 2016, and is the first FDA-approved drug that modulates splicing for the treatment for DMD [56]. Eteplirsen is a 30-nucleotide PMO. Its sequence is as follows: CTC CAA CAT CAA GGA AGA TGG CAT TTCT [43]. Eteplirsen was granted accelerated approval because it increased dystrophin expression in patients with DMD with deletions ending at exon 50 and starting at exon 52. Eteplirsen targets exon 51 in the hotspot region of the pre-mRNA and is beneficial in 21% of patients with DMD with deletion mutations or 14% of all patients with DMD [43]. The road to approval for eteplirsen was prolonged. The FDA was initially reluctant to approve eteplirsen owing to small numbers of patient samples in clinical trials and wanted evidence of increased dystrophin expression by western blot from more patients. Eteplirsen ended up showing only modest increases in dystrophin restoration. However, the increases in dystrophin levels did provide evidence for the intended mechanism of action for eteplirsen, which led to FDA approval. Eteplirsen is dosed at 30 mg/kg of patient weight, which is administered once weekly by intravenous infusion. The FDA had concerns about eteplirsen because the drug had not yet shown a clinical benefit, such as improved motor function, and mandated that Sarepta Therapeutics perform confirmatory studies to establish that eteplirsen can slow down disease progression [57]. The results of confirmatory studies are expected in 2024.

Golodirsen was also developed by Sarepta Therapeutics, approved in 2019, and is the second FDA-approved ASO for treatment of DMD [58]. Golodirsen is a 25-nucleotide PMO. Its sequence is GTT GCC TCC GGT TCT GAA GGT GTTC (FDA label). Golodirsen targets the skipping of exon 53 in the hotspot region during mRNA processing and is appropriate for patients with confirmed mutations amenable to the exon 53 skipping mechanism [59]. It can treat 8% of patients with DMD [60].

Golodirsen was granted accelerated approval because it could increase dystrophin levels more dramatically than eteplirsen. However, the FDA did not initially approve golodirsen owing to concerns of infusion infection risks and renal toxicity. Sarepta Therapeutics addressed these issues and will continue to monitor kidney function in patients treated with golodirsen. Golodirsen is dosed at 35 mg/kg of patient weight by intravenous infusion once per week. With the accelerated approval, the FDA required Sarepta Therapeutics to provide evidence through clinical trials to confirm that the golodirsen results in slower DMD disease progression and improvement in motor function [61]. Recently, a more comprehensive clinical trial was completed that shows the long-term efficacy and safety of golodirsen [62].

Viltolarsen was developed by Nippon Shinyaku Pharma, and the FDA approved it for the treatment of DMD in 2020 [52]. Viltolarsen is a 21-nucleotide PMO. Its sequence is CCT CCG GTT CTG AAG GTG TTC [63]. Similar to golodirsen, viltolarsen targets the skipping of exon 53 of the hotspot region from the DMD primary transcript and aids patients with confirmed mutations amenable to exon 53 skipping. It can treat 8–10% of patients with DMD [49]. Viltolarsen was approved because it caused a significant increase in dystrophin levels in skeletal muscle [61]. There was also evidence in a preclinical study in a large animal model and clinical trials showing that it improved muscle function and had clinical benefits [64–66]. Viltolarsen is dosed at 80 mg/kg of patient weight once weekly by intravenous infusion. There are still ongoing trials to provide more evidence that viltolarsen slows disease progression and improves muscle function [52].

Casimersen was developed by Sarepta Therapeutics, and the FDA approved it for the treatment of DMD in 2021 as the newest DMD ASO. Casimersen is a 22-nucleotide PMO. Its sequence is as follows: CAATGCCATCCTGGAGTTCCTG (FDA label). Casimersen targets the skipping of exon 45 of the hotspot region on the DMD gene pre-mRNA and is applicable to patients with confirmed mutations amenable to exon 45 skipping [52]. This ASO works in 9% of all patients with DMD [49]. Like the rest of the ASOs used to treat DMD, casimersen was granted accelerated approval on the basis of an observed increase in dystrophin levels in skeletal muscle after administration [67]. Casimersen is dosed at 30 mg/kg of patient weight once weekly by intravenous infusion. There are still ongoing clinical trials to show evidence that casimersen has a clinical benefit on patients with DMD by improving muscle function or slowing disease progression [67].

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a genetic neuromuscular disease resulting from the destruction of the anterior horn in the spinal cord, motor neurons, and brain stem leading to muscular atrophy and muscle weakness [68]. SMA is an autosomal recessive genetic disorder that occurs in 1/11,000 births. The symptoms are present in patients from birth to early childhood and may lead to other health complications, such as weight gain, lung disease, and scoliosis. However, cognitive function is not affected. Several forms of SMA exist (SMAI/II/III and IV) and these range in severity [69]. In Type I, symptoms are observed within 6 months after birth. Type II has a disease onset after 6 months, and Type III patients experience onset after 3 years of age. Type IV is the least severe form of SMA because it only affects proximal muscle weakness and loss of tendon reflexes in adult patients [68].

In normal individuals, the survival of motor neuron 1 (SMN1) pre-mRNA splicing produces functional SMN1 mRNA and SMN1 protein. A second related gene, SMN2, skips exon 7 in all individuals (though exon 7 is still present in the gene), resulting in a nonfunctional SMN2 protein, but there are no ill consequences as SMN function is derived from the wild-type SMN1 gene. However, a mutation in the SMN1 gene (at chromosome 5q11.2–5q13.3) causes mis-splicing leading to SMN1 mRNAs that lack exon 7. This has the effect of producing a mutant, nonfunctional form of SMN1 protein resulting in SMA. The most common mutation is the homozygous deletion of exon 7 of the SMN1 mRNA [68].

Nusinersen (brand name Spinraza, Ionis Pharmaceuticals, Carlsbad, CA, USA, and Biogen, Cambridge, MA, USA) was developed in 2010 by Adrian Krainer and coworkers and was approved by the FDA for the treatment of SMA in 2016. This ASO has the sequence 5′-TCACTTTCATAATGCTGG-3′ with all ribonucleotides modified with 2′-MOE and PS linkages and targets intron 7 splice sites in SMN2 pre-mRNA. In patients with SMA treated with nusinersen, the ASO induces the inclusion of a cryptic exon 7 into the SMN2 mRNA; this creates a full length and functional SMN2 protein, which compensates for the lack of functional SMN1 protein [70]. A single nusinersen dose consists of a solution containing 12 mg in 5 mL. Nusinersen is administered intrathecally with three initial loading doses (at 14-day intervals) followed by a fourth loading dose 30 days after the third dose. Maintenance doses are administered every 4 months thereafter. In clinical trials, 93% of patients treated with nusinersen exhibited favorable motor neuron response scores compared with 23% positive responses in sham-treated individuals [71].

Batten disease is the general name for several forms of rare, inherited autosomal recessive neurological disorders [72]. Each form is due to a different lysosomal gene mutation. They all have the effect or rendering the lysosome unable to carry out essential waste degradation and recycling functions, resulting in neurodegeneration and premature death. Batten disease is typically diagnosed when an apparently healthy child begins experiencing symptoms of vision loss or seizures [73, 74].

In 2019, the potential of personalized ASO therapeutics became a reality when milasen, an “n-of-1” ASO therapy, was FDA-approved in under a year to treat a 6-year-old girl with Batten disease. After this patient presented with blindness, ataxia, seizures, and developmental regression, genetic panel testing revealed that she was heterozygous for a mutation in the ceroid lipofuscinosis, neuronal 7 (CLN7) gene. One form of Batten disease can be caused by a mutation in the CLN7 gene on chromosome 4 [72]. CLN7 encodes a reduced major facilitator superfamily domain containing 8 protein (MFSD8), a ubiquitous integral membrane protein with transporter activity. The child’s heterozygous status prompted whole-genome sequencing which revealed the insertion of a SINE–VNTR–Alu retrotransposon [72]. It was further determined that the inserted element was inducing mis-splicing upstream from its insertion site at exon 6 by acting as a cryptic splice-acceptor site, leading to premature translation termination [72]. Using the ASO for SMA, nusinersen (described above), as a guide for backbone modifications, his team began crafting an individualized medication, which came to be known as milasen.

Milasen is a 22-nucleotide ASO with the same backbone and modifications as nusinersen [72]. Its sequence is 5′-AATGTTAGTGCTTGTTGAGGGC-3′ [72] (Fig. 4). Milasen exerts its function by altering the pre-mRNA splicing. It base-pairs with the pre-mRNA transcript within the cryptic splice-acceptor site present in the SINE-VINTR-Alu element [72]. Because the regulation of alternative splicing is under the modulation of these cis-acting sequences in the pre-mRNA transcript, milasen acts by causing a steric block directed against splicing factors, causing an alteration in the capacity for the spliceosome to recognize the splice site [75]. Milasen has 2′-MOE and 5Me-C chemical modifications allowing it to work in an RNase H resistant mechanism so that the abundance of the corrected mRNA transcript is maintained [72, 75].

Milasen-transfected fibroblast experiments showed that the drug was effective for reversing abnormal splicing and restoration of lysosome function [72]. Upon testing, milasen was able to triple the ratio of normal to mutant splicing [72]. The ASO was then used in the patient, who responded well [72]. Milasen was given to the patient by intrathecal injection starting with 3.5 mg (initial bolus dose) followed by increasing weekly doses every 2 weeks up to 42 mg. A 42 mg dose was then delivered approximately twice again at 3-week intervals, followed by maintenance dosing at this level every 3 months thereafter. Since only a single patient was treated with milasen, it is difficult to draw broad conclusions on efficacy. However, the patient did experience demonstrably reduced rates of seizures and adaptive behavioral scores, in decline prior to treatment, seemed to stabilize.

The drug design pipeline for ASOs is vastly different from that of traditional small molecule drugs that primarily target proteins. In the case of conventional small molecule drug development, a protein (often a receptor) is identified as a suitable therapeutic target; then in vitro assays are designed to detect the chemical inhibition or activation of the target protein. Subsequently, a large number of compounds (often numbering in the tens or hundreds of thousands) are tested using high-throughput approaches to find candidate molecules that can modulate the activity of the target protein. By this method, each drug discovery process is uniquely tailored to the specific target protein of choice. This approach to drug discovery and development is time consuming, costly and lengthy.

In contrast, ASOs target the mRNAs and pre-mRNAs that encode target proteins, and successful ASOs elicit the RNase H-mediated degradation of the target mRNA (thus effectively acting much like an antagonist), or ASOs can modulate the splicing of the pre-mRNA that encodes the protein of interest. In either case, the only knowledge required to begin this process is the complete mRNA sequence and an understanding of the pre-mRNA splicing of the initial pre-mRNA transcript. Thus, all ASO design considerations are based on sequence knowledge, whereas the identification of small molecule drugs is always a specialized process. Since all ASO design processes are similar, and the human genome sequence and corresponding cell-specific transcriptomes are widely available, ASOs can be designed and tested easily. This procedure alleviates the time-consuming and laborious journey of small molecule chemical screens. Therefore, ASO-based treatments have been of keen interest for rare genetic diseases, though in principle, they should work for any condition, in which the reduction in the amount of a target protein or restoration of abnornal protein expression and function is therapeutically desirable. For instance, the net effect of reducing receptor numbers in a cell can have a very similar effect as the inhibition of a receptor by a chemical antagonist. In addition, each of the thousands of receptor proteins differ from others owing to the complexity of protein structure. Still, all of these are encoded by mRNAs, all susceptible to ASOs, based primarily on the knowledge of RNA sequences. Therefore, the future of ASO drugs is promising, and their growing use may offer a paradigm shift compared with a century of protein-directed drugs.

Rare human diseases (often rare monogenic disorders) have been frequently ignored by the pharmaceutical industry as, although they are potentially treatable with small molecule drugs, the cost of screening and developing small molecule treatments does not offer significant returns on investment. However, as ASO design is based simply on knowledge of mRNA sequence, many rare disorders might now be treated with ASO approaches since this approach is, in principle, less costly, and more rapid. Thus, it may be that ASO treatments are a method of choice for rare diseases, and this notion is already being borne out by the progress with DMD subtypes, in which a variety of individual mutations have been treated by using four DMD ASO treatments. In addition, the recent success of the treatment of a single patient diagnosed with Batten disease with milasen (a custom-designed ASO developed for the treatment of a single patient) [72] demonstrates that ASOs may be a method of choice as a treatment modality for personalized medicine.

Since human viruses all express viral RNA, whose sequence differs from human RNA, ASOs can provide useful and specific treatments of viral infections. Indeed, one ASO (fomivirsen) has already been used for this purpose, though it was subsequently withdrawn from approval after combination therapy for HIV supplanted the need for treatment of opportunistic infections. However, the use of ASOs as antiviral agents is still attractive, especially with viruses that mutate quickly, resulting in variants that can evade vaccines.

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) disease is caused by the severe acute respiratory distress syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) novel coronavirus, a positive strand RNA virus related to SARS and Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS) viruses. The disease often progresses to acute respiratory syndrome (ARS). SARS-CoV-2 infects cells by first binding to the ACE2 receptor and enters via endosomes. The spike protein (S) is proteolytically cleaved, releasing the viral RNA genome into the cytoplasm. The 29.9 kilobases (Kb) viral RNA genome [76] is protected by a 5′ 7me-G cap and a 3′ poly-A tail. The viral genome is then recruited to the endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes, and the replicase proteins (R) are translated as early polyproteins. An RNA pseudoknot structure causes a −1 frameshift resulting in two polyproteins (pp1a and pp1b) being produced from ORF1a and ORF1b. Processed proteins encoded by ORF1a and ORF1b include RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDRP), RNA helicases, and RNA-modifying proteins. Subsequently, negative stand intermediate RNAs are produced from the 3′ structural gene region by the action of RDRP, from which a series of nested positive-strand RNAs are produced leading to the production of structural proteins, including the RNA-binding nucleocapsid protein (N) and spike protein (S). Finally, virions are assembled and include M (an envelope protein), N, and the E protein (involved in viral shedding).

Because the SARS-CoV-2 genome is composed of RNA and highly resembles an endogenous messenger RNA, an attractive antiviral pharmacological strategy is to develop ASOs that target SARS-CoV-2 genomic RNA that can elicit RNaseH-mediated cleavage and destruction of the viral genome. One recent study identified several ASOs that successfully target SARS-CoV-2 RNA genome in a cell culture model [77]. This study also showed that cells can be made resistant to SARS-CoV-2 infection in vitro by ASO-mediated destruction of ACE-2, the SARS-CoV-2 receptor. Furthermore, as SARS-CoV-2 variants arise repeatedly and unexpectedly, ASOs might be rapidly designed to target variants.

The problem of emergent viruses is a grave concern, especially as the modern world encroaches upon indigenous biota at levels not seen before. Since the sequence of emergent viruses can be obtained very rapidly, ASOs might be designed and tested quickly after an emergent virus appears. Furthermore, vaccines are usually designed to present antigens corresponding to viral coat proteins (and these are often highly mutable). In contrast, ASOs can be designed to target any region of the viral genome, including sequences that are much less prone to mutational changes.

ASOs, though originally conceived decades ago, are now becoming a more widely used therapeutic approach. Together with mRNA-based medical approaches, such as the SARS-CoV2 mRNA vaccines and siRNA drugs, ASOs can be viewed as a part of a new and emerging pharmaceutical approach of nucleic acid therapeutics that targets and uses RNA to achieve desirable effects. Like these other approaches, the only basic information needed for initial ASO design is knowledge of the sequence and function of the mRNA target. Since design of all ASOs share this basic approach, development of ASOs as pharmaceuticals is relatively expedient compared with conventional small molecule-based pharmacology, in which the target is typically a protein. In addition, with full knowledge of the human genome and its transcriptome, it may be possible to target key mRNAs for reduction (via RNase H) or altered splicing. Artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches may aid in the identification of key mRNA targets through identifying keystone mRNA targets from complex and multiple pathways that impact on disease processes. Overall, the promise of nucleic acid-based pharmacology is still in its infancy, but should these approaches be generally successful, then this approach may be both disruptive and transformative.
