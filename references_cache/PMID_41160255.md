# NUT Carcinoma of the Head and Neck: Clinicopathologic and Molecular Analysis of 18 Cases.
**Authors:** Liu Y, Yuan C, Sun J, Zhang J, Lin L, Gu J, Hu C
**Journal:** Head Neck Pathol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s12105-025-01853-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12105-025-01853-4)

## Content

PURPOSE: NUT carcinoma (NC) is a rare, aggressive neoplasm with limited therapeutic options. This study aimed to delineate the clinicopathologic and molecular characteristics of head and neck NC (HNNC) to refine diagnostic strategies and identify prognostic factors.

METHODS AND RESULTS: We conducted a retrospective analysis of 18 HNNC cases from a single institution (2015-2024). Our cohort consisted of 7 males and 11 females, aged 14-72 years (mean: 45 years), with the predominant site of involvement being the sinonasal tract (77.8%), followed by the larynx (16.7%) and the nasopharynx (5.5%). Microscopically, HNNCs typically grew as nests and sheets of poorly differentiated cells with focal or extensive abrupt keratinization. Several cases also displayed distinct morphological variants, including spindle cell components (4/18), basaloid (1/18), ameloblastic-like (1/18), papillary structures (1/18), monomorphic cell clusters (1/18), and concentric pattern around blood vessels (2/18). All cases were moderately to strongly positive for NUT protein (H-score: 80 to 300). PD-L1 was expressed in 43.8% of tumors (7/16). FISH confirmed NUTM1 rearrangement in 15 cases (15/16, 93.8%), and RNA sequencing analysis of 14 cases revealed the following fusion patterns: NUTM1::BRD4 (10/14, 71.4%), NUTM1::BRD3 (3/14, 21.4%), and NUTM1::NSD3 (1/14, 7.1%). Follow-up data were available for 17 patients (range: 2-52 months), among whom 10 patients died of the disease. The median overall survival was 12 months.

CONCLUSIONS: This large single-center series confirms that HNNC exhibits marked morphological heterogeneity and poor prognosis. Accurate diagnosis relies on immunohistochemistry, with FISH and RNA sequencing as needed, offering molecular insights to guide prognosis and clinical management.

Abstract

Introduction
NUT carcinoma (NC) is a poorly differentiated carcinoma characterized by rearrangements involving the NUTM1 (also known as NUT) gene [1]. It was first described in 1991 as a mediastinal/thymic malignancy with a t (15;19) chromosomal translocation in two case reports [2, 3]. Subsequently, in 2003, French et al. [4] identified the BRD4::NUTM1 fusion oncogene resulting from this chromosomal abnormality. As most early cases involved midline anatomical structures, the disease was initially termed “NUT midline carcinoma” (NMC). Over time, NC has been reported in various organs, including the thyroid, salivary glands, pancreas, kidneys, bladder, endometrium, ovaries, bone, and other soft tissues [5]. Accordingly, the tumor was renamed “NUT carcinoma” in the 4th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Head and Neck Tumors [6]. Meanwhile, several novel fusion partners for NUTM1, such as BRD3, NSD3, and other NUTM1 variant rearrangements, have been identified [7].
NC is generally a highly lethal disease with a poor response to standard treatments, and the median survival time is as short as 6.5 months [8]. It shows no gender predilection and can affect individuals of any age, ranging from the neonatal period to the elderly [9]. The unclear etiology and nonspecific histological features of NC contribute to frequent misdiagnoses, despite the availability of ancillary tests such as anti-NUT immunostaining and molecular analyses. Head and neck NUT carcinoma (HNNC) has been increasingly reported [10] and accounts for approximately 40% of NC cases [8]. However, limited awareness of this rare tumor among pathologists has led to an underestimation of its true incidence. Therefore, it is crucial to raise awareness among head and neck pathologists and otolaryngologists to improve diagnostic accuracy.
Herein, we present the largest single-center series of HNNC, analyzing the clinicopathological features and molecular alterations to summarize diagnostic strategies, prognostic factors, and therapeutic approaches for this disease.
Materials and Methods
Case Selection and Clinical Data
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples were collected from 18 patients diagnosed with HNNC in the Department of Pathology, Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University, between January 2015 and April 2024. The samples comprised both biopsy and resection specimens, including 15 pre-treatment cases and 3 post-treatment cases (Cases 5, 8 and 9). All pathological slides were independently reviewed and confirmed by two experienced pathologists. Clinical data, including demographic information and clinical characteristics, were retrieved from medical records; however, complete protocols for chemotherapy were not available for a subset of cases. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University and was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.
Immunohistochemistry, in situ Hybridization, and HPV Genotyping PCR
IHC staining was performed on 4-µm-thick sections obtained from FFPE tissues using a BenchMark Auto-stainer (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. IHC was performed using the following antibodies: NUT (Cell Signaling Technologies Inc., C52B1, 1:100), CK (Maxim, AEI/AE3, 1:200), VIM (Gene Tech, V9, 1:1000), Ki67 (Gene Tech, GM027, 1:300), P40 (Gene Tech, GR006, ready-to-use), P63 (Gene Tech, GM724729, 1:100), Syn (Gene Tech, GT206529, 1:300), CHG (Gene Tech, GT211404, ready-to-use), INSM-1 (ZSGB-BIO, MRQ-70, ready-to-use), INI-1 (Gene Tech, GT225702, ready-to-use), BRG1 (Gene Tech, GT219902, ready-to-use), PD-L1 (Agilent, 22C3, 1:50). The extent of IHC expression was quantified in quartiles as previously reported [11]: 0, negative; 1+ , 1–25% positive cells; 2+ , 26–50% positive cells; 3+, 51–75% positive cells; and 4+ , 76–100% positive cells. NUT staining was interpreted as positive when most (> 50%) tumor cells showed diffuse nuclear reactivity in a speckled pattern [12]. To achieve greater standardization and reproducibility, we evaluated NUT IHC by H-scores ranging from 0 to 300 [13]. The evaluation of PD-L1 was performed according to previously published methods [14]. EBER in situ hybridization (EBV-ISH) (CS1-4, Roche) and the HPV23 detection kit (YaNeng Bio) were used according to the manufacturer’s protocol as previously described [15].
Fluorescence in situ Hybridization
Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed on FFPE sections using a commercially available NUTM1 (15q14) break-apart probe (Guangzhou LBP Medicine Science & Technology Co., Ltd) to assess NUTM1 gene translocation. 200 nuclei were counted in four different areas of each tumor by two pathologists. Separate green and orange signals in the nucleus were interpreted as indicative of gene rearrangement, and the result was considered positive if more than 15–20% of tumor cell nuclei exhibited break-apart signals [16].
Targeted RNA Sequencing (RNA-seq)
Total RNA was extracted from FFPE tissues using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CaptureSeq libraries were prepared by the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit for Illumina (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA), and targeted custom capture of all coding sequences and intron/exon boundaries of coding exons from 630 genes (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA,USA), following previously described protocols [17]. The cDNA libraries were generated and sequenced on the Illumina Next500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with paired-end 150 bp reads (2 × 150 bp). Sequencing reads were aligned to the GRCh37 reference genome and analyzed with STAR-Fusion. All candidate fusion transcripts were manually validated using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).
Statistical Analysis
All data analyses were performed using Stata statistical software. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or the last follow-up. Analyses were performed to identify factors associated with OS using Kaplan–Meier plots and log-rank tests. One- and two-year survival rates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using the Greenwood formula. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Results
Clinical Findings
The clinical features of the 18 patients are summarized in Table 1. The sex ratio (male: female) was 7:11, with a median age of 43 years (mean 45 years; range 14–72 years). Two patients (2/18, 11.1%) were pediatric cases. It predominantly involved the sinonasal tract (14/18, 77.8%), followed by the larynx (3/18, 16.7%) and nasopharynx (1/18, 5.5%). Most patients presented with facial pain and nasal congestion lasting from 2 weeks to 11 months (average 2.2 months). Other symptoms included dysphagia, hoarseness, and cervical swelling. Preoperative endoscopic findings showed that most patients (10/18, 55.6%) presented with an exophytic, ulcerated mass (Fig. 1A–C). CT/MR imaging displayed a diffuse soft tissue mass invading adjacent structure, frequently involving the orbit or anterior cranial base (Fig. 1D–F). The lesions ranged in size from 2.1 to 6.6 cm (mean 4.3 cm, median 4.5 cm). According to the 8th AJCC staging system, 12 patients presented with advanced-stage disease (IVA, n = 10; IVB, n = 2), mainly in the sinonasal tract (11/12, 91.7%).

Histopathology Findings
Microscopically, the tumors were characterized by sheets and nests of monomorphic small to medium-sized undifferentiated cells with round to oval nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and variable eosinophilic to amphophilic cytoplasm (Fig. 2A, B). Focal to extensive necrosis and a mixed inflammatory infiltrate of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils were frequently present, along with scattered microabscesses (Fig. 2A). Characteristic abrupt keratinization was found in 5 cases (5/18, 27.8%), and focal or single-cell keratinization was noted in 10 cases (10/18, 55.6%) (Fig. 2C, D). However, keratinization was absent in 3 cases (3/18, 16.7%). Another common morphologic feature was separation artifacts and clear cytoplasm imparting a “fried-egg” appearance (16/18, 88.9%) (Fig. 2E). In addition, 38.9% (7/18) exhibited other morphological variations, either alone or admixed with classical undifferentiated morphology. One case showed basaloid features with peripheral palisading and irregular borders (Fig. 3A). Four cases (22.2%) contained spindle cell components with undifferentiated round cell areas (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the tumor cells were observed to be arranged in an ameloblastic-like pattern (1/18, 5.6%), papillary-like structure (1/18, 5.6%), concentric circles around fibrotic thick-walled blood vessels (2/18, 11.1%), and scattered small clusters (1/18, 5.6%) (Fig. 3C–F). Another case (case 8) exhibited undifferentiated round-to-plasmacytoid cells with clear cell changes, lacking keratinization or expression of epithelial markers (Fig. 3G). Review of the patient’s medical history revealed prior chemoradiotherapy, and the tumor initially displayed classic NC morphology. The histological patterns for each case are detailed in Table 2.

Immunohistochemistry Findings and HPV Status
The immunohistochemical features are summarized in Table 2. NUT protein demonstrated moderate to strong expression in all tumors (Fig. 3I). The H-score of NUT was ≥ 100 in 17 cases (17/18, 94.4%) (median 225; average 211; range 80–300). Tumor cells variably expressed CK (18/18), P40 (16/18, 88.9%), and P63 (16/18, 88.9%). Seven (7/18, 38.9%) cases focally expressed neuroendocrine markers (Syn, CHG, INSM-1). The Ki67 index ranged from 5 to 80% (often > 50%). PD-L1 was detected in 7 of 16 cases, with TPS and CPS ranging from 1 to 10% and 2 to 12, respectively. All cases were negative for EBER-ISH. HPV genotyping revealed that only case 4 tested positive for the HPV16 subtype, whereas P16 immunohistochemistry and high-risk HPV RNA in situ hybridization were negative.
Molecular Findings
FISH and RNA-seq analyses were performed on 16 and 14 tissue samples, respectively. NUTM1 rearrangement was detected in 15 cases by FISH, with case 11 showing a negative result (break-apart signals in 5% of nuclei). However, RNA-seq analysis of this case revealed a NUTM1::BRD3 fusion. Among the 14 RNA–seq–analyzed samples, fusion patterns were identified as follows: NUTM1::BRD4 in 10 cases (10/14, 71.4%), NUTM1::BRD3 in 3 cases (3/14, 21.4%), and NUTM1::NSD3 in 1 case (1/14, 7.1%) (Fig. 4).

Treatment and Follow-up
Treatment and follow-up data were available for 18 patients, as detailed in Table 1. Six patients received chemoradiotherapy alone, while 12 underwent surgery combined with chemoradiotherapy (n = 9) or radiotherapy (n = 3). Additionally, three patients (Cases 2, 6, and 12) received immunotherapy. Complete follow-up information, ranging from 2 to 52 months (mean 14 months; median 12 months), was available in 17 patients. At the last follow-up, ten patients (10/17, 58.8%) had died of the disease (range: 2 to 32 months), including 6 with NUTM1::BRD4 fusion. Seven patients (7/17, 41.2%) were alive without evidence of disease, and one patient was lost to follow-up after 7 months. The locoregional recurrence rate was 33.3% (6/18), and 38.9% (7/18) of patients developed distant metastasis, most commonly to the lungs. The overall median survival was 12 months (95% CI: 7–32), one-year OS rate was 48% (95% CI: 22.6–70.3%), and two-year OS rate was 32% (95% CI: 7.2–61.6%) (Fig. 5). No statistically significant associations were found between OS and clinicopathological features, including age (p = 0.14), sex (p = 0.39), tumor size (p = 0.65), AJCC stage (p = 0.55), treatment modality (p = 0.47), and NUTM1 fusion partner (p = 0.16). Notably, PD-L1 expression showed a trend toward improved survival (p = 0.084).

Discussion
HNNC is a rare and highly aggressive malignancy with markedly greater malignant potential than conventional squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the head and neck. Traditional treatment approaches, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, have shown limited efficacy, underscoring the critical need for accurate identification of this entity to improve prognostic assessment and guide therapeutic strategies.
In our cohort, the peak age of onset was around 45 years, with only two pediatric patients (< 18 years), indicating that HNNC is not confined to adolescents, as initially reported, but also affects middle-aged and elderly individuals. This aligns with the findings of Chau et al. [8], who described a bimodal age distribution with peaks in the second and fifth decades, distinct from the typical demographic profile of most head and neck cancers. These observations underscore the need for heightened clinical awareness of HNNC across all age groups, particularly among middle-aged and older adults. Consistent with previous studies [10], the sinonasal tract was the most frequently affected site, followed by the larynx and nasopharynx. Clinical symptoms varied by anatomical location but were generally nonspecific. The etiology of HNNC remains unclear [18].
The histopathology of HNNC is both distinctive and heterogeneous. It typically presents as a poorly differentiated or undifferentiated neoplasm composed of small- to medium-sized round or oval cells with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio [19]. Apoptosis and necrosis are frequently observed, along with characteristic features such as “fried-egg”-like cells, high mitotic activity, mucoid stroma, and neutrophil infiltration [20]. Morphologically, NC overlaps with several other poorly differentiated tumors, including poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, DEK::AFF2-associated squamous cell carcinoma, undifferentiated sinonasal carcinoma, Ewing sarcoma, and neuroendocrine carcinoma. Although abrupt keratinization may aid in diagnosis, it is not specific and has been reported in approximately one-third of NUT carcinomas in the literature [21]. In contrast, this feature was observed in 83.3% of cases in our cohort. However, similar keratinization patterns can also be seen in poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma and HPV-associated subtypes.
In addition, we identified several unusual histological patterns, including spindle cell components, basaloid morphology, ameloblastic features, papillary-like structure, monomorphic cell clusters, and concentric arrangements around blood vessels. Similarly, Viswanathan et al. [22], reported that 53% of cases exhibited non-classical features such as basaloid features, squamous component, clear cell changes, glandular differentiation, and papillary structures. This broad histological spectrum significantly complicates diagnosis, and most NCs are initially misdiagnosed. Therefore, NUT immunohistochemistry should be routinely included in the differential diagnosis of poorly differentiated or undifferentiated head and neck tumors. According to Haack et al. [10], when more than 50% of tumor cells show the characteristic speckled nuclear staining pattern, the specificity for NUT carcinoma reaches 100%, with a sensitivity of 87%. In this study, all cases demonstrated varying proportions and intensities of NUT staining, with 94.4% showing an H-score > 100; only one case exhibited weak staining with an H-score of 80. For tumors demonstrating focal or equivocal NUT expression, molecular confirmation is essential to ensure diagnostic accuracy and avoid misclassification. In addition, most NUT carcinomas express markers such as CK and P63. In our study, case 8 was negative for these markers because the sample tested was obtained after chemotherapy. Upon re-evaluation of the original biopsy specimen, the tumor exhibited typical NUT morphology and strong expression of CK and P63. In case 10, CK was focally expressed while Vim was partially positive. In combination with the morphological features, these findings support a diagnosis of NUT carcinoma.
At the molecular level, NUTM1 rearrangements are not consistently detected by FISH. In our study, one case tested negative by FISH, consistent with previous reports by McLean-Holden et al. [23] and Haack et al [12]. This discrepancy may be attributed to complex chromosomal rearrangements that are not readily identifiable by standard FISH probes. Among fusion partners, BRD4 is the most common, while BRD3 and NSD3 are less frequently observed. The NUTM1::BRD4 fusion plays a key role in the oncogenesis and progression of NC. Although NCs are defined by the presence of NUTM1 gene rearrangements, advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have led to the identification of a broader range of NUTM1 fusions across various tumor types. YAP1::NUTM1 and WWTR1::NUTM1 fusions have been detected in approximately 64% of porocarcinomas and a small subset of poromas. These tumors are appendageal tumors, and their histological characteristics have been described [24, 25]. Additionally, NUTM1-rearranged sarcoma has emerged as a distinct entity characterized by heterogeneous morphology, including small round, epithelioid, plasmacytoid, and spindle cell components. These tumors harbor a wide range of fusion partners such as MGA, MXD4, MXI1, MXD2, BRD4, BRD3, and BCORL1, [16, 26] as comprehensively summarized by Zhu et al [27]. Furthermore, NUTM1 rearrangements have also been reported in cases of leukemia [28]. Given this expanding spectrum, the diagnosis of NUT carcinoma should be based on an integrated approach that incorporates clinical presentation, histomorphology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular detection.
NC is a highly aggressive malignancy with no standardized treatment protocol. While platinum-based chemotherapy is widely used as a first-line approach, as was the case for most patients in our cohort, responses are often transient and the overall prognosis remains poor. Viswanathan et al. [22] reported 3-year disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free survival (DFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) rates of 38%, 35% and 48%, respectively, in a cohort of 30 patients with HNNC. Similarly, Contrera et al. [29] analyzed 12 patients with sinonasal NC and found that the median OS and DSS were both 14.6 months, with no significant differences in outcomes across treatment modalities. In our cohort, the median survival time was 12 months, and no correlation was observed between clinical or pathological parameters and prognosis. A study by Luo et al. [30] analyzing a large international cohort of 118 NC patients, clarified the role of ifosfamide in NUT carcinoma, demonstrating a remarkably high objective response rate (75%) in the metastatic setting that surpasses platinum-based chemotherapy. However, this potent initial activity demonstrates limited durability and does not improve overall survival. These findings highlight the urgent need to explore novel therapeutic strategies. Targeted therapies such as BET inhibitors (BETi) and Histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi), which act on the underlying gene fusion, have been investigated either alone or in combination with chemotherapy [31]. However, the efficacy of HDACi and BETi has been limited by drug toxicity and resistance, and novel target drugs such as dual HDAC/PI3K inhibitors (CUDC-907) and CDK9 inhibitors have shown significant effects on inhibiting NC cell proliferation in vitro compared to HDACi or BETi [32, 33]. In parallel, given the recent advancements in understanding the immune microenvironment of NC, immune checkpoint inhibitors are emerging as promising candidates [34]. Notably, in our series, three patients received PD-1 inhibitors—two with PD-L1–negative tumors (CPS = 0) and one with low expression (CPS = 2)—but this did not result in improved survival. Additionally, PD-L1 expression showed a trend toward longer OS, which needs to be further validated in large-scale studies.
Conclusion
In conclusion, HNNC is a highly aggressive tumor with diverse histomorphology, and approximately two-thirds of patients present with locally advanced disease; the median OS was 12 months. NUT immunohistochemistry is recommended for the evaluation of poorly differentiated or undifferentiated head and neck tumors. The development of targeted therapies has offered new therapeutic opportunities, while immunotherapy is also being explored.
