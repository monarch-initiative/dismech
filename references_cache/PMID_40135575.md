# α Protein Kinase 3 Is Essential for Neonatal and Adult Cardiac Function.
**Authors:** Feng W, Wang L, Bogomolovas J, Zhang Z, Huang T, Chang CW, Shain A, Gu Y, Cho Y, Zhou X, Chen J
**Journal:** J Am Heart Assoc (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1161/JAHA.124.039464](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.124.039464)

## Content

BACKGROUND: ALPK3 (α protein kinase 3) is an atypical kinase highly expressed in human and murine hearts. Biallelic loss-of-function mutations in ALPK3 lead to pediatric cardiomyopathy. The specific stages at which ALPK3 is essential for cardiac function and the mechanisms by which it regulates cardiac function require further exploration.

METHODS AND RESULTS: We generated ALPK3 global knockout and inducible cardiac-specific knockout mice. We performed time-course physiological and morphological assessments to determine ALPK3's role in neonatal and adult hearts. We also generated an Alpk3-3xFLAG-HA knock-in mouse model to determine endogenous ALPK3 localization. To investigate mechanisms of ALPK3 regulation, we performed biochemical assays and RNA sequencing experiments in global knockout mice. ALPK3 is critical for both neonatal and adult cardiac function. Loss of ALPK3 at germline and adult stages leads to dilated cardiomyopathy. Approximately 75% of germline ALPK3 mice die within 1 month, while surviving mutant mice develop dilated cardiomyopathy that transitions to left ventricular hypertrophy, mirroring clinical manifestations in human patients with biallelic ALPK3 mutations. We found that ALPK3 localizes to the M-band in both neonatal and adult cardiomyocytes and interacts with muscle RING-finger proteins, which may regulate thick filament protein turnover.

CONCLUSIONS: Our study highlights the necessity of ALPK3 in neonatal and adult cardiac function. Our data support a model in which ALPK3 serves as a scaffold protein to recruit machineries essential for regulating thick filament protein turnover.

Heart disease is a major cause of morbidity in the United States.
1
 While relatively rare compared with adult cardiomyopathy, pediatric cardiomyopathy is a serious condition that frequently necessitates heart transplant.
2
 Understanding the pathogenesis of pediatric cardiomyopathy is essential for the development of effective treatments.

An increasing number of studies have revealed the genetic basis of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM).
1
 Recently, a series of clinical studies have reported that biallelic loss of function mutations in ALPK3 (α protein kinase 3) lead to pediatric cardiomyopathy in human patients.
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
 Those patients develop progressively worsened cardiac function, which leads to lethality at an early age or necessitates heart transplant. Interestingly, among the patients who have been initially diagnosed with DCM and survive without heart transplant, most of them develop DCM to left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) transition. Additional studies further show that heterozygous ALPK3 variants are associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in human adult patients.
7
,
8
 Together, those studies highlight the significance of ALPK3 in regulating cardiac function at different stages.

ALPK3 belongs to the αprotein kinase family, a subfamily of the atypical protein kinase family distinguished by its unique α kinase domain.
9
 Based on the transcriptional data on human tissues, the expression of ALPK3 is highest in the heart,
10
 suggesting it has a specific role in regulating cardiac function. Interestingly, while possessing a kinase domain, we and others have previously shown that ALPK3 lacks kinase activity in vitro.
11
,
12
 We have further shown that mutating the catalytic lysine residue in ALPK3 does not change cardiac function in mice, suggesting that ALPK3 functions as a pseudokinase.
12
 While studies in human induced pluripotent stem cell derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC‐CMs) suggest that ALPK3 localizes to the M band of cardiomyocytes, and that ALPK3 potentially plays a role in maintaining sarcomere protein turnover as well as sarcomere structure,
11
,
13
 mechanisms by which ALPK3 regulates cardiac function in vivo remain unknown.

In the present study, we generated Alpk3 global knockout (gKO) and inducible cardiac‐specific knockout (icKO) mouse models. We showed that ALPK3 is essential for cardiac function in both the neonatal and adult stages. Our gKO mouse model also recapitulates the DCM to LVH transition observed in human patients carrying biallelic loss of function mutations in ALPK3, providing a model to study the molecular changes leading to this unique clinical manifestation. To investigate the mechanisms by which ALPK3 regulates cardiac function in vivo, we generated an Alpk3‐3xFLAG‐HA knock‐in mouse model without affecting the function of ALPK3. We showed that ALPK3 localizes to the M band in cardiomyocytes and interacts with MuRFs (muscle RING‐finger proteins) to potentially regulate thick filament protein turnover. Our study delineates the requirement for ALPK3 in cardiac function in neonatal and adult stages, provides in vivo models for studying ALPK3‐related cardiomyopathies, and sheds light on mechanisms by which ALPK3 regulates cardiac function.

To investigate the role of ALPK3 in cardiac function in vivo, we first generated an Alpk3 germline gKO mouse model. Alpk3
flox/flox mice, in which exon 3 of Alpk3 gene is flanked by LoxP sites, were crossed with Sox2‐Cre deleter
15
 mice to generate gKO mice. Compared with their littermate controls, gKO mice exhibited premature lethality, starting from 1 week after birth. Fifty percent of gKO mice died within 3 weeks after birth, with only 25% of the gKO mice surviving beyond 1 month after birth (Figure 1A). To examine the change in cardiac function in gKO mice, we performed time‐series echocardiography studies across gKO and control mice starting from postnatal day 3 to 8 weeks after birth. gKO mice exhibited a progressive decrease in cardiac contractile function as measured by fraction shortening starting from postnatal day 3 (Figure 1B). gKO mice also exhibited an increase in left ventricular dimension starting from postnatal day 10 (Figure 1C and 1D), suggesting that gKO mice developed DCM. Interestingly, gKO mice exhibited similar left ventricular wall thickness compared with controls until 4 weeks, after which they developed a thicker left ventricular wall (Figure 1E). Histological studies also revealed a markedly dilated heart at postnatal day 10 and a dilated heart with a thicker left ventricular wall at 8 weeks in gKO mice (Figure 1F). While gKO hearts underwent dramatic functional and morphological changes shortly after birth, expressions of cardiac remodeling markers, Nppa (atrial natriuretic peptide) and Nppb (brain natriuretic factor), were not significantly different between gKO and controls at 2 weeks (Figure 1G). Those data support that Alpk3 is essential for neonatal cardiac function in mice. Loss of Alpk3 in germline results in the development of DCM and premature lethality in majority of the mice within a month after birth, while a small fraction of the mice undergoes DCM‐to‐LVH transition at 4 weeks and survives. To note, the DCM‐to‐LVH transition in our gKO mouse model recapitulates the cardiac pathogenesis in human patients carrying biallelic ALPK3 loss‐of‐function mutations, in which most of the patients who have been initially diagnosed with pediatric DCM undergo transition to LVH.

29

 This prompted us to investigate if loss of ALPK3 leads to dysregulation of M band proteins as well as other sarcomeric proteins essential for cardiac contractile function in vivo.

To assess if loss of ALPK3 leads to the dysregulation of sarcomeric structural proteins in vivo, we extracted the sarcomeric protein from adult gKO and control hearts. SDS‐PAGE analysis on the sarcomeric protein fraction revealed a significant increase in thick filament proteins, including MYH, myosin‐binding protein C, myosin light chain 1, and MLC2 in gKO hearts compared with controls (Figure 4A and 4B). In contrast, thin filament protein TNNI did not increase in gKO hearts compared with controls.

To additionally validate the changes in protein abundance observed in the SDS‐PAGE experiment, we performed Western blots using antibodies specific to pan‐MYH, MYH6, TNNI, MLC2, and actin, ACTC1 from total protein samples of adult mouse hearts. Consistent with the SDS‐PAGE results, we found increased levels of MYH, MYH6, and MLC2 in gKO hearts (Figure 4C and 4D), while there were no changes in TNNI and ACTC1 protein levels. We also assessed the total protein levels of the M‐band proteins myomesin‐1 and ‐2, which showed significant increases in gKO hearts compared with controls (Figure 4C and 4D). These data suggest that the loss of ALPK3 leads to selective dysregulation of thick filament protein homeostasis. Interestingly, while MYH, MYH6, MLC2, myomesin‐1, and myomesin‐2 showed significantly increased protein levels in gKO hearts (Figure 4A through 4D), the transcript levels of Myh6, Myl2, Myom1, and Myom2 did not increase significantly compared with control hearts (see below). This suggests that the elevated levels of thick filament proteins in gKO hearts are at least partly due to posttranscriptional regulation.

Studies have revealed that protein turnover by the ubiquitin proteosome system is a critical posttranscriptional mechanism for maintaining sarcomere function.
33
 In ubiquitin proteasome system–mediated protein degradation, a ubiquitin E3 ligase transfers ubiquitin to a protein of interest, which is subsequently degraded by the 26S proteosome. MuRFs are E3‐ubiquitin ligases localized to the M band of the sarcomere and are critical in regulating thick filament protein turnover.

 leading us to hypothesize that ALPK3 could potentially interact with MuRF1 to regulate sarcomere protein turnover. To test this, we performed transient transfection of MuRF1 and ALPK3 into HEK293T cells. Immunofluorescence on cotransfected cells show that ALPK3 alone localized to the cytoplasm, yet in the presence of MuRF1, ALPK3 relocalized to the MuRF1 puncta (Figure 4E, top). To note, neither ALPK3‐FLAG transfection alone nor ALPK3‐FLAG cotransfected with mCherry led to the formation of puncta as seen in ALPK3‐FLAG cotransfected with MuRF1–mCherry, suggesting that the relocalizatiion of ALPK3 is due to the presence of MuRF1 instead of being an artifact of mCherry overexpression (Figure 4E, bottom). These findings are consistent with data published in a recent study by McNamara et al.

 In that study, McNamara et al performed a coimmunoprecipitation experiment in hiPSC‐CMs with endogenous FLAG‐tagged ALPK3 and identified MuRF2 as an interacting partner, supporting the interaction between these proteins in cardiomyocytes. Additionally, it has been reported that, similar to ALPK3, which is localized at the M‐line in adult mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes as shown in our article, MuRF1 is also localized at the M‐line in these cells.
38
 Taken together, these data suggest that ALPK3 interacts with MuRFs and may facilitate the scaffolding of MuRF substrates, contributing to the regulation of sarcomeric protein homeostasis. Consequently, in the absence of ALPK3 in cardiomyocytes, the turnover of sarcomeric proteins is impaired, leading to their accumulation.

The transition from DCM to LVH is a unique clinical feature in human patients carrying biallelic ALPK3 loss of function mutations. To investigate the molecular changes associated with the DCM to LVH transition, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on the left ventricular tissue of Alpk3 gKO and control mice at the 4‐week time point, at which the increase of ventricular wall thickness in gKO hearts was first observed (Figure 1E). Principal component analysis revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between gKO and control hearts (Figure 5A). Differential gene expression analysis revealed 158 significantly downregulated genes and 250 significantly upregulated genes (P
adj<0.05, |log2[fold change]|>log2[0.8]) (Figure 5B, Table S1). To determine potential pathways dysregulated in gKO hearts at DCM‐to‐LVH transition, we performed GO analysis on downregulated genes. GO analysis showed that the processes related to muscle contraction (GO:0006936), actin filament organization (GO:0007015), actin‐myosin filament sliding (GO:0033275), and myofibril assembly (GO:0030239) were significantly enriched in downregulated gene list (Figure 5C) (Table S2). To determine if the increased levels of thick filament proteins shown previously (Figure 4A) was due to their transcriptional upregulation, we performed differential expression analysis on their transcripts in our RNA sequencing data set. None of the transcriptional changes reached statistical significance (data not shown), suggesting that the upregulation of those thick filament proteins is regulated at the posttranscriptional level. To validate those findings from the RNA sequencing experiment, we performed quantitative real‐time PCR assessment of the transcript levels for selective genes encoding for thin and thick filament proteins, including Tnni3, Tnnt2, Myom1, Myom2, and Myh6 at the 6‐week time point. None of the other transcripts had different levels between gKO and controls (Figure 5D). Those data suggest that DCM‐to‐LVH remodeling is associated with significant sarcomere remodeling, which is regulated at the transcriptional level as well as at the posttranscriptional level.

While multiple clinical studies show that biallelic loss of function mutations in ALPK3 lead to pediatric cardiomyopathy in human patients,

,

,

,

 it remained unknown whether ALPK3 is essential for cardiac function only during a specific developmental period. In our study, using Alpk3 gKO and icKO mouse models, we unequivocally showed that Alpk3 is essential for cardiac function during the neonatal and the adult stages. gKO mice developed DCM, leading to premature lethality within 1 month after birth in ≈75% of the mice. Interestingly, gKO mice that survived all developed DCM‐to‐LVH transition, recapitulating the clinical manifestation of human patients carrying biallelic ALPK3 loss of function mutations.
3
 In contrast, upon the loss of ALPK3 in the adult stage, icKO mice developed DCM without transitioning into LVH. Those data suggest that while ALPK3 is essential in both the neonatal and the adult stages, it may regulate cardiac function via distinct mechanisms, dependent on the specific developmental stage. Consequently, loss ALPK3 in germline or adult cardiomyocytes leads to different cardiac phenotypes in gKO and icKO mice. As icKO mice did not exhibit DCM‐to‐LVH transition, our work suggests that the transition from DCM to LVH in human patients carrying ALPK3 mutations and germline ALPK3 knockout mice results from the loss of ALPK3 before adulthood. Since postnatal cardiac development is a dynamic process regulated by different pathways,
39
 loss of ALPK3 could impact such pathways indirectly in a temporal fashion, leading to the difference in cardiac phenotypes between gKO and icKO mice. Those data warrant the further mechanistic investigation by which ALPK3 regulates cardiac function at specific developmental stages. While 2 previous studies using an ALPK3 gene trap mouse model
40
 and an ALPK3 knockout mouse model
11
 show that the loss of ALPK3 impacts cardiac function in vivo, they did not report that these models exhibit the unique transition from DCM to LVH observed in human patients with biallelic ALPK3 loss‐of‐function mutations. Our ALPK3 gKO model thus provides an invaluable tool for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying this unique DCM‐to‐LVH clinical manifestation.

As no commercially available antibodies could detect the endogenous ALPK3 specifically, we used CRISPR‐Cas9 to knock in a 3x‐FLAG‐HA tag to the C‐terminus of Alpk3 without impairing its function in vivo. Using our knock‐in mouse model, we showed that ALPK3 localizes to the M band in cardiomyocytes, in agreement with previous studies showing the M‐band localization of ALPK3 in hiPSC‐CM.

,
13
 Our 3x‐FLAG‐HA knock in mouse model provides an additional valuable tool for the research community to further investigate the role of ALPK3.

Our study reveals a significant upregulation of thick filament proteins in the hearts of gKO animals, highlighting a novel aspect of ALPK3's function in cardiac muscle. While similar observations have been reported in independent studies using hiPSC‐CMs and murine models carrying null or hypomorphic ALPK3 alleles using proteomic methods,
11
,
13
 our findings provide direct evidence that ALPK3 deficiency leads to the increased levels of thick filament components in the heart. By using Western blot analysis and direct quantification of protein bands in SDS‐PAGE gels, we were able to quantify levels of key thick filament proteins, confirming their increased levels due to ALPK3 deficiency.

The increased levels of thick filament proteins in ALPK3‐deficient hearts suggest a potential link between ALPK3 and sarcomeric protein turnover mechanisms. The physiological and molecular parallels observed between genetic murine models of ALPK3 and MuRF deficiency extend to structural similarities as well. The primary MuRF docking platform in the sarcomere is located in the M‐line, immediately upstream of the titin pseudokinase domain.

Collectively, our findings not only establish a regulatory axis involving ALPK3 and MuRFs in cardiomyocytes but also provide a mechanistic framework for understanding the role of ALPK3 in maintaining sarcomeric protein homeostasis and cardiac function.
