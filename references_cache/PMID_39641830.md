# Loss of GATAD1 in cardiomyocyte does not cause cardiomyopathy in mice.
**Authors:** Pang J, Zhu S, Shyy M, Duong J, Tran T, Sanchez-Garcia E, Chen C, Gu Y, Fang X
**Journal:** J Mol Histol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10735-024-10297-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10735-024-10297-z)

## Content

GATA zinc finger domain containing 1 (GATAD1) is an as-yet uncharacterized zinc finger domain protein, which was initially identified as a histone 3 trimethylated at lysine 4 (H3K4me3) interactor. A recessive mutation in GATAD1 is associated with adult-onset dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure, suggesting that GATAD1 is critical for maintaining normal cardiac structure and function. However, little is known as to the specific role of GATAD1 in cardiomyocytes. A mammalian Gatad1 knockout model has yet to be generated for investigating its specific role in the heart. To address this, we generated a Gatad1 cardiomyocyte-specific knockout (cKO) mouse model. Gatad1 cKO mutants exhibited normal cardiac function during the aging process up to 18 months of age. Unlike the abnormal nuclei shape observed in patients carrying GATAD1 mutations, the nuclei shape of cardiomyocytes remained unaffected by the loss of Gatad1. Furthermore, Gatad1 cKO mice responded normally to pressure overload induced by transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery. Together, these observations suggest that deletion of Gatad1 in cardiomyocytes does not induce cardiomyopathy during aging or affect the response to pressure overload stress in mice.

The cardiomyocyte transcriptome is tightly controlled by complex and well-orchestrated transcriptional programs comprised of specific transcription factors, cofactors, and chromatin regulators (Lee and Young 2013; Bruneau 2002). Mutations in key components of cardiac gene expression programs have been identified in patients with congenital heart disease and cardiomyopathy (McCulley and Black 2012; Li et al. 2014; Kohli et al. 2011; Akazawa and Komuro 2003; Bang et al. 2022), revealing major pathways in cardiac transcriptional regulation and the maintenance of cardiac physiology. Transcriptional regulation has also been implicated in the regulation of cardiac remodeling by driving re-expression of a fetal gene program during stress (Akazawa and Komuro 2003).

GATA zinc finger domain containing 1 (GATAD1) is an as-yet uncharacterized zinc finger domain protein, which was initially identified as a histone 3 trimethylated at lysine 4 (H3K4me3) interactor (Vermeulen et al. 2010). Genome-wide homozygosity mapping and exome sequencing identified a GATAD1 mutation in a large four-generation family with autosomal recessive dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) (Theis et al. 2011). Three individual carriers that were homozygous for a single amino acid substitution of Serine 102 to Proline (S102P) in GATAD1 presented with full penetrance adult-onset DCM and heart failure in their fifties, being 50, 53 and 57 years of age (Theis et al. 2011). In GATAD1 mutant patients, cardiomyocyte nuclei showed an abnormal globular shape, contrasting with the elongated spindle shape seen in the controls (Theis et al. 2011). However, thirteen relatives were heterozygous mutation carriers with no evidence of myocardial disease, even at advanced ages (Theis et al. 2011). The unique DCM phenotype observed in patients carrying GATAD1 S102P homozygous mutation suggests that GATAD1 is critical for maintaining normal cardiac structure and function. However, little is known as to the specific role of GATAD1 in cardiomyocytes, or whether GATAD1 deficiency results in cardiomyopathy.

GATAD1 is ubiquitously expressed among various tissue types and is highly expressed in human, mouse and zebrafish hearts at all stages of development (Theis et al. 2011; Yang et al. 2016). A previous study reported that Gatad1 knockout zebrafish display a heart failure-like phenotype and lethality at 7 months of age when exposed to ethanol and high cholesterol stress (Yang et al. 2016). Despite previous suggestions of the importance of GATAD1 in maintaining normal cardiac physiology and in response to stress, a mammalian Gatad1 knockout model has yet to be generated for investigating its specific role in the heart. To address this knowledge gap and determine the role of GATAD1 in cardiomyocytes, we generated Gatad1 global knockout (gKO) and cardiomyocyte-specific knockout (cKO) mouse models. We found that deletion of GATAD1 in cardiomyocytes slightly induced cardiac stress markers. However, our analyses for Gatad1 cKO and control mice revealed no defects in cardiac function up to 18 months of age, and Gatad1 cKO mice responded normally to pressure overload induced by transverse aortic constriction (TAC). We also observed that cardiomyocytes isolated from Gatad1 cKO mice displayed normal nuclear shapes, compared to the controls. Taken together, our data suggest that the deletion of Gatad1 in cardiomyocytes does not cause cardiomyopathy during aging or affects the response to pressure overload stress in mice.

Gatad1 floxed mouse line was generated by inserting to LoxP sites to flank exons 2–4 using CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Stroud et al. 2018a, b). We utilized Gatad1 floxed mice to generate Gatad1 cardiomyocyte-specific knockout (cKO) mouse models utilizing Xenopus laevis myosin light chain 2 (Xmlc2)-Cre transgenic mice (Breckenridge et al. 2007; Liu et al. 2019). The corresponding littermate controls were Gatad1f/f; Xmlc2-Cre negative mice. Genotyping primers were listed in Supplemental Methods Table 1.

Mice were anesthetized with 1% isoflurane and underwent echocardiography using a FUJIFILM VisualSonics SonoSite Vevo 2100 ultrasound system with a 32- to 55-MHz linear transducer. The percentage of fractional shortening (FS) was used as an indicator of systolic cardiac function. Measurements of left ventricular internal dimensions at the end of diastole and systole (LVIDd, LVIDs, respectively) and LV posterior wall thickness (LVPWd) were determined from the LV M-mode tracing. For TAC, male Gatad1 cKO and control mice at the age of 12 weeks were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine and had their aortas banded with a 27.5-gauge needle (for body weight less than 25 g mice) as previously described (Stroud et al. 2018a, b; Fang et al. 2016; Fan et al. 2020).

Total RNA was isolated from ventricular heart tissues with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and cDNA was synthesized with SuperScript™ III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) in 96-well PCR plates. The relative gene expression was normalized according to 18S expression level. Primers for qPCR analysis were listed in Supplemental Methods Table 1.

Hearts were isolated from age- and sex-matched littermates, washed in PBS, and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The hearts were then transferred to 70% dehydrant alcohol for another 24 h. Afterwards, the hearts were processed, embedded in paraffin, and cut into sections using a microtome (Leica Biosystems). Sections were cut at 8-μm thickness and stained with hematoxylin and eosin.

Adult cardiomyocytes were isolated at 6 months, 12 months, and 18 months of age using enzymatic digestion. The hearts were isolated using isoflurane anesthesia and rinsed in Krebs-Henseleit buffer B (KHB62 B) (118 mM NaCl, 4.8 mM KCl, 25 mM HEPES, 1.25 mM K2HPO4, 1.25 mM MgSO4, 11 mM glucose, pH 7.4). The heart was cannulated through the aorta, perfused on a Langendorff apparatus with KHB solution (3 ~ 5 min, 37 °C), and then incubated with KHB enzyme solution (0.5 mg/ml Collagenase Type 2, 0.5 mg/ml Collagenase Type 4, and 25 μM Blebbistatin) for 25 min at 37 °C. After digestion, the heart was perfused with 5 ml KHB solution to wash out the collagenase. The hearts were then minced in KHB solution with 2% BSA, gently agitated, and filtered through a 100 μm polyethylene mesh. After settling, cells were centrifuged at 500 rpm at 4 °C for 1 min. The pellet was resuspended in KHB solution and collected after centrifuging at 500 rpm at 4 °C for another 1 min.

Cardiomyocytes were isolated from mice at 6 months, 12 months, and 18 months of age. Cells were then fixed with 4% formaldehyde solution and preserved for immunostaining later. After permeabilization with washing buffer (PBS with 0.1% TX-100), the CMs were incubated with the indicated antibodies overnight with blocking buffer (PBS with 2% normal donkey serum, 3% bovine serum albumin) in a humidified chamber at 4 °C. CMs were rinsed in wash buffer and mounted in mounting buffer (Dako, Santa Clara, CA). The nucleus were stained with DAPI in blue color, and the nucleus envelopes were stained with Lamin B receptor (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat No. sc-160482) and Lamin B1 (Abcam, Cat No. ab16048).

Isolated cardiomyocytes were stained with DAPI and imaged. The form factor (nuclear circularity) is calculated as the area inside the nucleus over its perimeter squared. The solidity (nuclear curvature) is calculated as the ratio of the nucleus’s measured area to the area of its convex hull shape. The eccentricity (nuclear elongation) is calculated as the length-to-width ratio (Janssen et al. 2022).

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 and SPSS software, with a two-tailed Student’s t-test or two-way ANOVA used for comparisons among groups. The survival data was calculated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with a log-rank statistical method. P values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Published single-cell RNA sequencing data (Tabula Muris et al. 2018) revealed that Gatad1 is ubiquitously expressed in all tested organs (Figure. S1A) and in all cell types of the heart (Figure. S1B–C). To investigate the role of Gatad1, we generated a Gatad1 global knockout (gKO) mouse model. Gatad1 heterozygous (+/−) mice were viable and born at expected Mendelian ratios, with no observed defects. However, Gatad1 gKO mutants displayed early embryonic lethality at embryonic day (E) 8.5 (Supplemental Table 2), indicating the essential role of Gatad1 in early embryonic development. To overcome the early lethality of Gatad1 gKO mice and determine the role of GATAD1 in cardiomyocytes, we have generated a floxed Gatad1 mouse line, in which exons 2–4 of the Gatad1 gene were flanked by two LoxP sites (Fig. 1A). We utilized Gatad1 floxed mice to generate cKO mice by utilizing Xmlc2-Cre transgenic mice (Breckenridge et al. 2007), which have been widely used to ablate genes in early developing CMs from E7.5 with no cardiac toxicity (Breckenridge et al. 2007; Liu et al. 2019). Gatad1 cKO mice were viable and survived to adulthood. Our qPCR analysis confirmed effective deletion of Gatad1 in cKO hearts at three months of age (Fig. 1B). As cardiac stress leads to the re-expression of cardiac fetal genes, atrial natriuretic factor (Nppa) and B-type natriuretic peptide (Nppb) are typically elevated in the hearts under pathological conditions of stress (Heineke and Molkentin 2006). We performed qPCR to analyze the mRNA levels of Nppa and Nppb in Gatad1 cKO and control hearts at 3 months of age. As shown in Fig. 1B, the expression of Nppa and Nppb were increased in the hearts of cKO mice, compared to those of Cre negative controls, suggesting that deletion of Gatad1 in CMs induced cardiac stress. However, our echocardiographic analysis of a pilot group of Gatad1 cKO and control mice showed similar cardiac structure and function at 3 months of age (Fig. 1 C–F). We speculate that loss of Gatad1 might result in slowly developing late-onset cardiomyopathy during aging, as observed in the patients carrying GATAD1 mutations.

To investigate whether Gatad1 is required to maintain normal cardiac function during aging, we subjected a large group of Gatad1 cKO and control mice to the aging process. Most of the Gatad1 cKO mice survived up to 18 months of age, the longest time point in our study, which is comparable to the survival of control mice (Fig. 2A). To assess the effects of Gatad1 ablation in cardiomyocytes on cardiac function, we performed echocardiographic analysis for Gatad1 cKO mice and littermate control mice over a time course of 18 month (Fig. 2B–F). Surprisingly, the cardiac function of Gatad1 cKO was comparable with control littermates (FS: 36.3% vs. 36.2%), as were LVIDd (3.79 vs. 3.81 mm), LVIDs (2.44 vs. 2.43 mm), LVPWd (0.79 vs. 0.77 mm), and IVSd (0.78 vs. 0.76 mm) at 18 months of age (Fig. 2B–F). We observed no significant change in any of these parameters irrespective of sex. In addition, morphological and histological analyses revealed no evidence of morphological defects in Gatad1 cKO mice at 6, 12, and 18 months respectively (Fig. 3A). There were also no changes in the ratio of ventricular weight to body weight (Fig. 3B) and ventricular weight to tibia length (Fig. 3C) in Gatad1 cKO mice, compared to littermate controls, at 18 months of age. Interestingly, we observed an increase in the mRNA levels of Nppa and Nppb at 15 months of age (Fig. 3D), consistent with our observations at 3 months of age (Fig. 1B). These results suggest that loss of Gatad1 in cardiomyocytes leads to cardiac stress but not cardiac dysfunction during aging.

In GATAD1 mutant patients, cardiomyocyte nuclei showed an abnormal globular shape, contrasting with the elongated spindle shape seen in the controls (Theis et al. 2011). To determine whether deletion of Gatad1 leads to abnormal cardiomyocyte nuclei shape as seen in patients, we performed immunostaining with Lamin B receptor and Lamin B1 antibodies to visualize the nuclear envelope in cardiomyocytes isolated from Gatad1 cKO and control mice at 6, 12, and 18 months of age, respectively. Our results revealed no evidence of defects in cardiomyocyte nucleus envelopes in Gatad1 cKO cardiomyocytes from the staining of Lamin B receptor and Lamin B1 at all stages of our analyses (Fig. 4A–C). We further quantified the circularity, curvature, and elongation for the DAPI stained nuclei (Fig. 5A). The form factor and solidity represent the nuclear circularity and curvature respectively, while the eccentricity measures the nucleus elongation. Our results showed no significant differences between Gatad1 cKO and control cardiomyocytes in all the parameters we quantified (Fig. 5B–D), suggesting that nuclei shape remains normal in Gatad1 cKO cardiomyocytes up to 18 months of age.

Mutations in GATAD1 have been associated with human cardiomyopathy (Theis et al. 2011), highlighting the importance of GATAD1 in the heart. While we observed the increase of cardiac stress markers due to the loss of Gatad1 in cardiomyocytes (Figs. 1B and 3D), we did not observe any cardiac dysfunction (Fig. 2B–F) in Gatad1 cKO mice up to 18 months of age. A previous study reported that gatad1 knockout zebrafish display a heart failure-like phenotype and lethality at 7 months of age when exposed to ethanol and high cholesterol stress (Yang et al. 2016), suggesting the critical role of GATAD1 in response to stress. Interestingly, our qPCR analysis revealed that the mRNA level of Gatad1 was increased in hearts one week after transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery, a commonly used model for pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy and failure (Fig. 6A). However, the potential role of GATAD1 during cardiac remodeling and pressure-overload induced heart failure remains unknown. To address this question, we performed TAC on 12-week-old mice followed by serial echocardiography measurements at 1-, 5-, and 8-week post-TAC. As expected, when compared to Sham-operated animals, both Gatad1 cKO and control mice subjected to TAC underwent remodeling, as evidenced by a progressive decrease in cardiac function, and increasing dimensions of the left ventricular chambers and wall thickness (Fig. 6B–E). However, there were no significant differences in any of these parameters between Gatad1 cKO and control littermates. The ratios of ventricular weight to body weight at 8 weeks post-TAC were comparable between Gatad1 cKO and control mice (Fig. 6F). These results suggest that Gatad1 cKO hearts exhibit a normal hypertrophic response to pressure overload.

GATAD1 is an as-yet uncharacterized zinc finger domain protein and highly expressed in hearts (Vermeulen et al. 2010; Theis et al. 2011; Yang et al. 2016). Previous human genetic studies have revealed that an autosomal recessive mutation in GATAD1 is associated with adult-onset dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure, suggesting that GATAD1 is critical for maintaining normal cardiac structure and function. Furthermore, gatad1 knockout zebrafish display a heart failure-like phenotype and lethality at 7 months of age when exposed to ethanol and high cholesterol stress (Yang et al. 2016). However, little is known as to the specific role of GATAD1 in cardiomyocytes. To investigate the role of Gatad1 in the mammalian heart, we have generated a mouse model in which GATAD1 is specifically ablated in cardiomyocytes. Our results revealed that, while cardiac stress markers increased at 3 and 15 months of age, Gatad1 cKO mice did not develop cardiomyopathy by the time they reached 18 months of age at baseline condition. Unlike GATAD1 mutant patients, cardiomyocytes from Gatad1 cKO mice displayed normal nuclear morphology. Given the upregulation of Gatad1 mRNA in response to pressure overload induced by TAC surgery, we explored whether pressure overload could expedite the development of cardiac dysfunction in Gatad1 cKO mice. However, the analysis of large groups of mice revealed a comparable hypertrophic response to pressure overload in Gatad1 cKO and control mice. Thus, we conclude that deletion of Gatad1 in cardiomyocytes does not induce cardiomyopathy during aging or affect the response to pressure overload stress in mice.

These results surprise us, as the patients carrying S102P homozygous mutations in GATAD1 exhibit dilated cardiomyopathy, but no other associated diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, or coronary artery disease, suggesting that the importance of GATAD1 in maintaining proper cardiac function. However, our Gatad1 cKO mice did not display any phenotype even at 18 months of age at baseline, or exhibit an exacerbated response to pressure overload-induced heart failure. Notably, the S102P heterozygous mutation carriers with no evidence of myocardial disease, even at advanced ages (8). While the recessive mutation is often considered a loss-of-function mutation, it remains possible that the S102P mutation could be a gain-of-function mutation with dose-dependent effects that impair cellular functions. This possibility could be tested using an S102P knock-in mutant in future studies.

It is also possible that Gatad1 cKO mice may develop cardiomyopathy at an older age, beyond the 18 months that we analyzed, or over a longer time period following TAC. Although homozygous GATAD1 mutations lead to cardiomyopathy in humans in their fifties (Theis et al. 2011), which is roughly equivalent to 15–18 months of age in mice (Dutta and Sengupta 2016), it is important to recognize that the function of GATAD1 in humans and mice may not be directly comparable. There could be unknown genetic modifiers necessary to manifest a cardiomyopathy phenotype. Future research could involve studying Gatad1 cKO mice in different inbred mouse strains to explore this possibility further. Additionally, future studies should consider potential non-cardiomyocyte contributions to the cardiomyopathy associated with the GATAD1 S102P mutation, given that Gatad1 is highly expressed across various tissue types. Gatad1 global knockout (gKO) mice exhibit early embryonic lethality at E8.5, suggesting that Gatad1 is essential for early embryonic development. While the Xmlc2-Cre used in this study specifically expresses in cardiomyocytes from E7.5, it is possible that Gatad1 influences the differentiation of cardiomyocyte progenitor cells or has other essential functions prior to E7.5. Utilizing human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes with GATAD1 knockout or mutation could be an excellent approach to assess the function of human GATAD1 in cardiomyocyte differentiation.
