INTRODUCTION: Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) is a highly curable 
malignancy arising from placental trophoblasts, yet a small subset of patients 
develops multidrug resistance with limited therapeutic options. The discovery of 
high programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression across trophoblastic tumors 
has provided a compelling biological rationale for immunotherapy, particularly 
immune checkpoint blockade targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis.
OBJECTIVE: To summarize current evidence on immunotherapy in GTN, integrating 
biological foundations, clinical experiences, and ongoing clinical trials, and 
to discuss future perspectives toward individualized, fertility-preserving 
management.
METHODS: A narrative review was conducted according to structured PRISMA-based 
principles. Literature was retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science 
from 2000 to 2025 using predefined descriptors related to GTN and immunotherapy. 
Eligible studies included clinical trials, case series, case reports, and 
translational research addressing immune checkpoint inhibitors in GTN.
RESULTS: GTN exhibits high PD-L1 expression, mirroring the immune-privileged 
nature of the placenta. Checkpoint inhibitors alone, such as pembrolizumab, 
avelumab, or the combination of camrelizumab plus apatinib (that potently 
suppresses the kinase activity of vascular endothelial growth factor 2), have 
demonstrated complete and durable responses in approximately 70-80% of patients 
with multidrug-resistant GTN, with acceptable safety and preserved fertility.
CONCLUSIONS: Immunotherapy has expanded therapeutic GTN, transforming refractory 
disease as a result of its immune responsiveness. Checkpoint inhibition not only 
achieves high remission rates but also offers fertility preservation and 
long-term survivorship. The future challenge lies in optimizing combination 
strategies, refining biomarkers, and ensuring equitable global access to these 
emerging treatments.

INTRODUCTION
Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) rep‑
resents a unique group of human malignancies 
arising from the trophoblast, a tissue that nor‑
mally supports fetal development [1 ]. Unlike 
most solid tumors, GTN is characterized by 
exceptional chemosensitivity and a high likeli‑
hood of cure even in metastatic settings. The 
establishment of quantitative human chorionic 
gonadotropin (hCG) monitoring, the develop‑
ment of multi‑agent chemotherapy, and the 
refinement of the International Federation of 
Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging sys‑
tems have transformed GTN into one of the 
most curable cancers in oncology [ 2], particu ‑
larly when managed in specialized reference 
centers (RC) [3].
Yet, this success conceals important chal‑
lenges. A minority of patients develop resist‑
ance or relapse after standard therapy, facing 
cumulative toxicity, psychological burden, and, 
in a few cases, infertility [1 , 4]. The search for 
less aggressive, targeted, and fertility‑preserving 
treatments has therefore become a new frontier 
in trophoblastic disease management.
GTN treatment has long relied on chemothera‑
peutic regimens, including methotrexate (MTX) 
and actinomycin D (Act ‑D) for low ‑risk disease 
[5], and multi‑agent chemotherapy, such as 
etoposide, methotrexate, actinomycin D, cyclo‑
phosphamide, and  Oncovin®/vincristine (EMA‑
CO), for high‑risk cases, achieving survival rates 
exceeding 90% [6 , 7]. Despite the remarkable 
curability of GTN with chemotherapy, resist‑
ance and relapse still occur and vary according 
to prognostic category. Among low‑risk GTN, 
primary resistance to single‑agent MTX or Act‑D 
occurs in approximately 20–30% of cases. How‑
ever, nearly all achieve remission after switch‑
ing to an alternative single agent or an EMA/CO 
combination, with relapse rates of less than 3–5% 
[5]. In contrast, high‑risk GTN presents primary 
resistance to first‑line EMA/CO in about 20–25% 
of patients, and relapse occurs in 5–8%, usually 
within 12 months after remission. A small subset, 
defined as ultra‑high‑risk GTN (FIGO score ≥ 13 
or with liver/brain metastases, hCG  >  106 IU/L, 
or prior multidrug exposure), shows resistance 
or relapse in up to 40–50% of cases, with sur‑
vival rates declining to 60–70% even in reference 
centers [4]. In these situations, cumulative toxic‑
ity becomes dose‑limiting, and new therapeutic 
strategies, such as targeted agents and immuno‑
therapy, are urgently needed.
Over the last decade, the emergence of immu‑
notherapy has transformed the landscape of 
oncology, offering durable responses through 
immune checkpoint blockade [8 ]. In GTN, this 
approach is biologically appealing given the 
tumor’s origin from semi‑allogeneic placental 
tissue and its high expression of programmed 
death‑ligand 1 (PD‑L1) [9]. Early clinical experi‑
ences with pembrolizumab, avelumab, and, to a 
lesser extent, the combination of camrelizumab 
plus  apatinib have shown promising results, 
including complete and durable remissions in 
multidrug‑resistant disease [10– 12]. These find‑
ings have improved the enthusiasm for integrat‑
ing immunotherapy, alone or in combination 
with chemotherapy or targeted therapy, into 
future GTN management [13–17].
The objective of this paper is to review the cur‑
rent evidence regarding immunotherapy in GTN, 
contextualized within the historical and biologi‑
cal framework of trophoblastic disease. We sum‑
marize conventional management, discuss the 
rationale for immune checkpoint inhibition, 
review clinical data of published cases and trials, 
and explore ongoing research and future perspec‑
tives toward manageable toxicities in most cases 
and more individualized treatment strategies.

tions of the Preferred Reporting Items for System‑

ment, adapted for descriptive synthesis, to ensure 
comprehensive retrieval and transparent report‑
ing of the literature [18].

Relevant publications were identified through 
comprehensive searches in PubMed/MED‑
LINE, Scopus, and Web of Science, covering the 
period from January 2000 to September 2025.

RESULTS
Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia: 
Concepts and Clinical Overview
GTN comprises a rare spectrum of malignant 
disorders, which includes invasive mole, chori‑
ocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumor 
(PSTT), and epithelioid trophoblastic tumor 
(ETT) [1 , 2]. These entities are distinguished 
from the benign forms of gestational tropho ‑
blastic disease (GTD), namely complete and 
partial hydatidiform moles, by their potential 
for invasion and metastasis [19].
Epidemiology and Prevalence
The global incidence of GTN varies consider‑
ably, ranging from 0.5 to 10 cases per 10,000 
pregnancies, influenced by geographic, genetic, 
and socioeconomic factors. Although individu‑
ally uncommon, GTN collectively accounts for 
an estimated 20,000 new cases worldwide each 
year, with incidence varying markedly across 
regions and higher rates observed in parts of 
Asia, Africa, and South America [20– 22].
Despite its rarity, GTN carries substantial 
public health importance given the young 
age of affected women, the psychological and 
reproductive consequences of treatment, and 
the need for centralized care in RC [3 , 23].
Diagnosis and Staging
Diagnosis of GTN is based primarily on abnor‑
mal patterns of quantitative serum hCG fol‑
lowing molar evacuation. FIGO criteria define 
persistent disease through plateauing or rising 
hCG levels or by histologic confirmation of 
choriocarcinoma. Imaging modalities, includ‑
ing pelvic ultrasound and CT or MRI when 
needed, are used to assess tumor burden and 
metastatic spread. Prognostic stratification 
follows the FIGO 2000 scoring system, which 
guides treatment intensity and predicts the 
likelihood of chemoresistance [1 , 2].
Current Management and Outcomes
Uterine Evacuation and Second Curettage
Second curettage may be considered in selected 
cases of post‑molar GTN with low hCG levels 
and localized uterine disease. Although remis‑
sion can be achieved in a subset of patients, the 
benefit is limited, and the procedure carries risks 
such as perforation and hemorrhage [24].
Chemotherapy for Low‑Risk GTN
Low‑risk GTN is highly chemosensitive and is 
treated with single‑agent methotrexate or actin‑
omycin D, achieving overall cure rates above 
95%. Most patients respond promptly, and tox‑
icity is usually mild [1, 2, 25].
Chemotherapy for High‑ and Ultra‑High‑Risk 
GTN
High‑risk GTN requires multi ‑agent chemo ‑
therapy, with EMA‑CO remaining the standard 
regimen and yielding high cure rates [1 , 2, 7]. 
Outcomes are poorer in ultra‑high‑risk disease, 
where early mortality and chemoresistance are 
more common. Induction low‑dose EP may be 
used to stabilize patients with extensive disease 
[26, 27], while alternative regimens (e.g., etopo‑
side methotrexate, and actinomycin D/ etopo‑
side and cisplatin, EP/EMA; or paclitaxel, cispl‑
atin, paclitaxel, etoposide, TP/TE) are reserved 
for refractory or relapsed cases [4, 6].
GTN Resistance and Relapse
GTN Resistance is defined as failure of hCG to 
decline after two consecutive cycles of chemo‑
therapy or a rise in hCG > 10% on two successive 
assays [1, 2]. Relapse is defined by the increase of 
hCG after achieving normal levels.
Both conditions require prompt reassess‑
ment of metastatic sites and therapeutic esca‑
lation, frequently involving regimen switch 
or, in selected cases, surgical resection of local‑
ized lesions. Despite aggressive management,

survival in multidrug‑resistant GTN remains 
inferior, prompting the search for novel, biologi‑
cally targeted therapies.
Chemotherapy Toxicity
Toxicities in GTN therapy are generally grouped 
into early (acute or subacute adverse events 
occurring during or within weeks after treat‑
ment) and late (delayed complications appear‑
ing months to years later).
Overall, early toxicities of GTN chemotherapy 
are common but reversible, while late sequelae 
are rare, especially with modern dose optimiza‑
tion and supportive care. The main long‑term 
concerns involve gonadal toxicity (risk increas‑
ing with age and alkylating agents), etoposide‑
related secondary leukemia (incidence < 0.5%), 
and cisplatin nephrotoxicity or ototoxicity. Sur‑
vivorship studies consistently show excellent 
reproductive outcomes and minimal chronic 
morbidity in women cured of GTN [5, 28].
Unmet Needs and Therapeutic Gap
Although cure rates for GTN are among the 
highest in oncology, treatment remains cyto‑
toxic and non‑selective. For the small but criti‑
cal subset of patients with multidrug resistance, 
the prognosis worsens sharply.
These cases highlight a pressing need for 
novel therapies capable of achieving a cure with 
reduced toxicity and preserved reproductive 
potential [14–17].
Emergence of Immunotherapy in GTN
The discovery that GTN originates from fetal 
trophoblastic tissue, a naturally immune‑toler‑
ant environment, provides a strong biological 
rationale for immunotherapeutic approaches [8, 
29, 30]. Historical reports of spontaneous remis‑
sion, along with early observations of tumor 
lymphocytic infiltration, suggested that the 
host immune system can recognize and control 
trophoblastic tumors [9, 31].
With the advent of immune checkpoint 
inhibitors, these insights gained practical rel‑
evance: pembrolizumab, avelumab, and, more 
recently, camrelizumab plus apatinib have dem‑
onstrated clinically relevant responses in resist‑
ant GTN, often inducing complete and durable 
remissions after failure of multiple chemother‑
apy lines. These breakthroughs mark the begin‑
ning of a new era in trophoblastic oncology, one 
that shifts focus from cytotoxic destruction to 
immune reactivation [10, 11, 13, 32]. Figure  2 
presents a timeline outlining the historical evo‑
lution of GTN treatments, from chemotherapy 
to immunotherapy.
PD‑L1 Expression in Trophoblastic Tumors: 
Biological Rationale for Immunotherapy
A distinctive feature of GTN is its remarkably 
high expression of PD‑L1, which mirrors the 
physiological immune privilege of normal pla ‑
cental tissue [33, 34]. In the healthy placenta, 
PD‑L1 expression on syncytiotrophoblasts and 
cytotrophoblasts suppresses maternal T cell acti‑
vation and maintains fetomaternal tolerance 
[35, 36]. This immune‑evasive phenotype is 
co‑opted by trophoblastic tumors, which retain 
and often amplify PD‑L1 expression to evade 
immune surveillance.
Immunohistochemical studies have consist‑
ently demonstrated uniform and intense PD‑L1 
staining in nearly all cases of choriocarcinoma 
and invasive mole, with positive rates approach‑
ing 100% of tumor cells and strong membra‑
nous localization. In contrast, PSTT and ETT 
exhibit lower and more heterogeneous PD‑L1 
expression, correlating with their monomor‑
phic morphology, reduced proliferative index, 
and lower hCG production [34, 37, 38]. These 
biological differences parallel clinical observa‑
tions: classical post‑molar GTN, dominated by 
syncytiotrophoblastic and cytotrophoblastic ele‑
ments, shows dramatic responses to checkpoint 
blockade, whereas PSTT and ETT, of intermediate 
trophoblastic differentiation, tend to respond 
partially or more slowly.
When compared with other gynecologic 
malignancies, this pattern is unique. In cervi‑
cal and endometrial cancers, PD‑L1 positivity 
is typically limited to tumor cells and is often 
confined to the tumor–stroma interface. Even 
in these tumors, modest PD‑L1 expression has

been sufficient to justify regulatory approval of 
pembrolizumab for PD‑L1‑positive, demonstrat‑
ing that checkpoint inhibition can be effective 
even in weakly immunogenic contexts [39, 40].
In GTN, however, the combination of near ‑
universal PD‑L1 expression, the immune‑toler‑
ant placental microenvironment, and high sys‑
temic antigenicity through circulating hCG and 
trophoblastic antigens creates an ideal setting 
for checkpoint inhibition. Ultimately, GTN is a 
unique human cancer where the tissue is at least 
a partial allograft due to paternal genetic mate‑
rial. These biological conditions likely explain 
the favorable outcomes in preliminary studies 
(70–80%) observed with anti‑ PD‑1/PD‑L1 anti‑
bodies in multidrug‑resistant GTN, contrasting 
with the more modest outcomes seen in other 
gynecologic cancers [32, 41, 42].
Together, these findings establish a robust 
mechanistic basis for immunotherapy in GTN: 
a malignancy derived from an immune‑privi‑
leged tissue that remains exquisitely sensitive to 
immune reactivation once inhibitory signaling 
is disrupted.

Inhibitors in GTN
The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibi‑
tors has introduced a transformative para‑
digm in the management of GTN, particularly

for patients with multidrug‑resistant disease. 
Although conventional chemotherapy achieves 
cure rates exceeding 95% overall, a small subset, 
especially those with ultra‑high‑risk or refractory 
GTN, exhibit resistance to all established regi‑

sion of immune regulatory molecules such as 
PD‑L1 and abundant immune cell infiltration, 
providing a compelling biological rationale for 
immune checkpoint blockade. Since 2017, sev‑

induce rapid and durable hCG normalization 
even in heavily pretreated patients, with favora‑

following subsections summarize the accumu‑

avelumab, and camrelizumab plus apatinib, 
which together define the evolving landscape 
of immunotherapy in GTN.
Pembrolizumab (Anti‑PD‑1)
Beyond classical PD‑1 blockade, pembrolizumab 
exerts distinctive immunologic effects in GTN 
due to the unique biology of trophoblastic tis ‑
sue [36, 37]. The drug promotes reactivation of 
previously exhausted  CD8+ T cells and increases 
interferon‑γ  secretion, which in turn upregu‑
lates major histocompatibility complex expres‑
sion and enhances tumor antigen presentation. 
In GTN, this immune re‑engagement is rapidly 
reflected by declining serum β‑hCG levels, serv‑
ing as a functional biomarker of tumor clear‑
ance. Pembrolizumab also modulates the tumor 
microenvironment by reducing regulatory 
T cell density and restoring the cytotoxic‑to‑
regulatory lymphocyte ratio, thereby reversing 
the state of immune tolerance that characterizes 
trophoblastic tumors (Fig.  3A). When adminis‑
tered after chemotherapy, it may further exploit 
chemotherapy‑induced neoantigen release and 
transient PD‑L1 upregulation, producing the 
accelerated and durable remissions observed 
clinically [10]. Therefore, by reversing tropho‑
blastic immune tolerance and reactivating cyto‑
toxic pathways, pembrolizumab translates its 
mechanistic potential into meaningful clinical 
responses.
Taken together, this mechanistic knowl‑
edge explains the remarkable clinical activity 
observed with pembrolizumab in chemoresist‑
ant GTN, making pembrolizumab the most 
extensively studied immune checkpoint inhibi‑
tor in GTN. Initial reports from Ghorani et al. 
[10] described complete and durable responses 
in patients with multidrug‑resistant choriocarci‑
noma and invasive mole who had failed several 
prior regimens, establishing the biologic plausi‑
bility of PD‑1 blockade in trophoblastic disease.
Subsequent multicenter experiences consoli‑
dated these findings: approximately 70–80% of 
patients with resistant or relapsed GTN achieved 
sustained remission, most discontinuing therapy 
after hCG normalization [10, 43–63]. Median 
response time ranged from 4 to 8 weeks, and 
treatment duration was typically between 3 and 
6 months. Toxicities were generally mild and 
manageable, most frequently fatigue, rash, and 
thyroid dysfunction, without immune‑related 
fatalities. There are reports of successful concep‑
tion following pembrolizumab therapy for GTN 
and other malignancies, although available data 
remain scarce [50, 56, 64, 65].
According to NHS England guidance (2017), 
pembrolizumab is indicated for patients with 
high‑risk GTN (FIGO score ≥ 7) who have failed 
both first‑line multi‑agent chemotherapy (EMA/
CO) and second‑line salvage regimens (such as 
EP/EMA or TE/TP). Pembrolizumab has been 
administered in GTN predominantly at a dose of 
2 mg/kg every 3 weeks, as described in the NHS 
England guidance [ 66], although several more 
recent studies have adopted the fixed dose of 
200 mg every 3 weeks, consistent with its use 
in other solid tumors [56]. Treatment response 
should be evaluated through serial quantitative 
β‑hCG measurements combined with radio‑
logic follow ‑up to confirm tumor regression 
and exclude new lesions. Case reports and small 
series suggest that, in the absence of biochemical 
or radiologic improvement after approximately 
five cycles, discontinuation should be consid‑
ered. Conversely, in patients achieving complete 
remission—defined by sustained β‑hCG normali‑
zation and imaging response—some authors 
have administered five additional consolidation 
cycles to minimize the risk of relapse [66]. This 
policy formalized the use of pembrolizumab as

a therapeutic option for drug‑resistant GTN, 
reflecting the shift toward immunotherapy in 
patients unresponsive to conventional cytotoxic 
treatment.
Nevertheless, contrasting this original salvage 
indication, emerging studies are now evaluat‑
ing pembrolizumab as part of first‑line regimens 
for high‑risk GTN, either as monotherapy or 
combined with cytotoxic agents [47, 50, 67]. 
Furthermore, a 2025 report described first‑line 
pembrolizumab administered concurrently 
with multi‑agent chemotherapy and inhaled 
tranexamic acid in a patient with stage III mixed 
trophoblastic tumor complicated by pulmonary 
hemorrhage, resulting in prompt hemostasis and 
complete disease remission [68]. These emerg‑
ing experiences suggest that PD‑1 blockade may 
not only rescue patients with drug‑resistant dis‑
ease but could also enhance early tumor control 
and reduce chemotherapy burden in selected 
high‑risk presentations. Further research is still 
required to assess the cost‑effectiveness and 
head‑to‑head comparison of remission rates 
and toxicity profiles between chemotherapy and 
immunotherapy in GTN.
Overall, pembrolizumab exhibits a rapid, 
durable, and well‑tolerated immune effect across 
the GTN spectrum, supporting its integration 
into front‑line or consolidation regimens, par‑
ticularly in centers with immunotherapy exper‑
tise and access to close monitoring [32].

antibody that targets PD ‑L1, preventing its 
interaction with PD‑1 on activated T cells and 
thereby restoring cytotoxic antitumor immu‑
nity. In contrast to pembrolizumab, which 
blocks the PD ‑1 receptor, avelumab binds 
directly to PD‑L1 expressed on tumor cells 
and immune cells within the microenviron‑
ment. In addition to reversing PD ‑1‑mediated 
T cell exhaustion, avelumab retains an intact

maintain immune tolerance, thus aligning with 
the biological rationale of checkpoint inhibition 
in GTN.
Avelumab has been evaluated in the TRO‑
PHIMMUN trial, a multicenter phase II study 
conducted in France. The first cohort included 
patients with low‑risk GTN resistant to MTX. In 
this trial, avelumab was administered at a fixed 
dose of 10 mg/kg intravenously every 2 weeks 
until complete remission or unacceptable toxic‑
ity. Treatment monitoring included serial β‑hCG 
measurement and imaging surveillance, with 
discontinuation recommended after five to six 
cycles if no biochemical response was achieved 
[11].
Following this treatment protocol, eight out 
of 15 women (53.3%) achieved complete remis‑
sion after a median of nine cycles, all without 
severe toxicity and with preserved ovarian func‑
tion [11]. Although a comparative analysis of 
remission rates and cost‑effectiveness has not 
been performed, it seems evident that the use 
of avelumab as a second‑line regimen in low‑
risk GTN resistant to methotrexate is associated 
with lower complete response rates compared to 
actinomycin D (approximately 50% vs. 80%), a 
similar toxicity profile, but substantially higher 
costs [70]. Therefore, until robust data demon‑
strate otherwise, the use of avelumab instead of 
actinomycin D should be restricted to research 
settings rather than routine clinical practice.
The second cohort, which included patients 
with multi‑agent‑resistant high‑risk GTN, dem‑
onstrated only a modest benefit, with responses 
observed in 1/7 (14.2%) cases [12]. These find‑
ings suggest that checkpoint inhibition may be 
most effective when introduced early in the dis‑
ease course or in tumors with stronger tropho‑
blastic differentiation. Accordingly, based on the 
limited efficacy observed in the avelumab high‑
risk GTN cohort, the use of this immune check‑
point inhibitor cannot be recommended for this 
group of patients. Overall, avelumab’s excellent 
tolerability profile, predominantly grade 1–2 
infusion reactions, supports its exploration as 
a front‑line or maintenance option in selected 
low‑risk patients.
Building on these findings, the TROPHAMET 
trial (NCT04396223) [71], presented at the 
ESGO/SGO 2025 Annual Meeting, explored a 
novel first‑line strategy aimed at minimizing 
chemoresistance in low‑risk GTN, thereby reduc‑
ing the need for subsequent multi‑agent chemo‑
therapy. In this academic multicenter phase I/II 
study led by the French Gestational Trophoblas‑
tic Center, avelumab (800 mg IV on day 1) was 
combined with the standard 8‑day MTX regi‑
men (1 mg/kg/day on days 1, 3, 5, 7, alternating 
with folinic acid), administered every 2 weeks 
until hCG normalization plus three consolida ‑
tion cycles [72].
Following this protocol, a high efficacy rate 
was observed: among 26 assessable patients, 
96.2% (25/26) achieved complete hCG nor‑
malization after a median of 3.3 months (IQR 
2.5–4.0), with no grade 4–5 adverse events and 
only rare grade 3 toxicities (stomatitis, tran ‑
sient ALT/AST elevation, or catheter infection) 
[73]. After a median follow‑up of 30 months, 
no relapses were reported. Fertility outcomes 
were encouraging: among 17 women eligible 
for conception, 12 (70.6%) conceived, resulting 
in seven live births (41.2%), three miscarriages, 
one ectopic pregnancy, and one ongoing preg ‑
nancy [72].
Together, these findings demonstrate that 
avelumab plus methotrexate markedly enhances 
cure rates compared with historical MTX mono‑
therapy (approx. 70%), while maintaining excel‑
lent tolerability and fertility preservation. This 
combined approach represents a promising first‑
line option designed to reduce chemoresistance 
and prevent escalation to polychemotherapy in 
patients with low‑risk GTN.

(Anti‑PD‑1 + Anti‑VEGFR2)
Camrelizumab is a fully humanized IgG4 mon‑
oclonal antibody that targets the PD‑1 recep‑
tor on activated T lymphocytes, blocking its 
interaction with PD‑L1 and PD‑L2 expressed by 
trophoblastic and immune cells in the tumor

microenvironment. This inhibition reverses 
T cell exhaustion and re‑establishes cytotoxic 
immune surveillance against chemoresist‑
ant trophoblastic cells [73]. Apatinib, an oral 
tyrosine kinase inhibitor that selectively targets 
vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 
(VEGFR2), complements this effect by nor‑
malizing abnormal tumor vasculature, reduc‑
ing VEGF ‑mediated immunosuppression, and 
enhancing immune‑ cell infiltration, thereby 
creating a microenvironment more permissive 
to checkpoint blockade (Fig.  3C) [74].
In the CAP‑01 multicenter phase II trial 
conducted in China, patients received camre‑
lizumab 200 mg intravenously every 3 weeks 
combined with apatinib 250 mg orally once 
daily until complete remission or unaccepta‑
ble toxicity. Among women with multidrug‑
resistant GTN refractory to at least two prior 
chemotherapy regimens, complete remission 
was achieved in 50% (10/20) of cases [ 13]. 
The main adverse events were hypertension, 
hand–foot syndrome, and fatigue, all manage‑
able with dose adjustment. Notably, several 
patients conceived spontaneously after treat‑
ment discontinuation, underscoring the fertil‑
ity‑sparing potential of this combination.
Although early data demonstrate strong 
synergy between PD‑1 blockade and VEGFR2 
inhibition, camrelizumab–apatinib remains 
available only in China, and global clinical 
experience is still limited [75]. Broader inter‑
national studies are needed to confirm its long‑
term efficacy and to define its role as a poten‑
tial alternative to pembrolizumab for refractory 
GTN.

encouraging results [76], further studies and 
broader access will be required before its poten‑

refractory GTN can be fully assessed.
Integration of Immunotherapy and 
Chemotherapy
The integration of immune checkpoint inhibi‑
tors with traditional chemotherapy represents a 
promising frontier in the management of GTN. 
Wang et al. conducted a multicenter retrospec‑
tive study comparing anti‑PD‑1 therapy alone 
versus anti‑PD‑1 therapy combined with chem‑
otherapy in patients with high‑risk chemore‑
fractory or relapsed GTN [67 ]. In this cohort of 
66 patients treated across three Chinese tertiary 
hospitals, the combination of immunotherapy 
and chemotherapy significantly increased the 
objective response rate from 62.9% (22/35) 
to 96.8% (30/31) (p  < 0.001), with a marked 
improvement in progression‑free survival [haz‑
ard ratio of 0.06 (95% CI 0.02–0.16), p < 0.001], 
while maintaining comparable toxicity profiles 
between groups. Although overall survival did 
not differ significantly, the combined strategy 
provided sustained antitumor activity and high 
complete‑response rates, even among patients 
previously refractory to PD‑1 inhibitors, who 
achieved 84.6% remission with subsequent sal‑
vage chemotherapy.
Complementary evidence was reported by 
Liu et al. [76 ], who described four patients 
with recurrent or drug‑resistant GTN treated 
with PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitors combined with 
chemotherapy. The mean age at recurrence was 
45.8 years (range 35–56), including three cases 
of choriocarcinoma and one of invasive mole. 
According to FIGO prognostic scoring, one 
case was low‑risk (≤ 6), one high‑risk (7–12), 
and two very high ‑risk (≥  13). Metastatic sites 
included the lungs (two cases) and vulvar or 
inguinal lymph nodes (one case). Among the 
four patients, one underwent hysterectomy 
and another lung metastasectomy. After receiv‑
ing combined chemo‑immunotherapy, one 
achieved a complete response (CR), two had 
partial responses (PR), and one developed pro‑
gressive disease (PD). Notably, three patients 
with PR or CR were maintained on single‑agent 
immunotherapy without recurrence during 
follow‑up, while the patient with PD attained 
CR after salvage chemotherapy, also remain‑
ing disease‑free thereafter. All immune‑related 

1071Adv Ther (2026) 43:1060–1081 
adverse events were grade I–II, and no severe 
toxicity was observed.
These findings highlight a synergistic inter‑
action between cytotoxic and immune‑medi‑
ated mechanisms, supporting the rationale for 
integrated chemo‑immunotherapeutic regi‑
mens in advanced GTN. Cytotoxic agents can 
induce tumor ‑cell apoptosis and neoantigen 
release, thereby enhancing tumor immuno‑
genicity, while checkpoint inhibitors reinvig‑
orate exhausted T cells and restore antitumor 
activity. This interplay likely underlies the 
durable remissions observed in heavily pre‑
treated patients with GTN exposed to PD‑1/
PD‑L1 blockade after chemotherapy failure.
Preclinical evidence indicates that chemo‑
therapy can modulate the tumor microen‑
vironment [77], reducing myeloid‑derived 
suppressor cells and regulatory T cells while 
enhancing antigen presentation and effector 
T cell infiltration [78, 79]. In trophoblastic 
tumors, where immune evasion mechanisms 
are robust, this immunogenic remodeling may 
be particularly relevant [ 29, 34, 80]. Moreover, 
cytotoxic therapy may promote upregulation 
of PD‑L1 expression, further sensitizing tumors 
to PD‑1/PD‑L1 blockade [67, 76].
Clinical observations support these bio‑
logical hypotheses. Sequential or concurrent 
administration of pembrolizumab after multi‑
agent chemotherapy failure has yielded com‑
plete responses in cases previously refractory to 
all cytotoxic combinations. Similarly, ongoing 
studies are evaluating combinations such as 
avelumab plus methotrexate for low‑risk GTN 
and pembrolizumab with EMA/CO or EP/EMA 
for high‑risk or relapsed disease. Early data 
suggest that combining cytotoxic and immu‑
nomodulatory therapy may enhance efficacy 
while allowing de‑escalation of chemotherapy 
intensity in responsive cases [67, 76].
Potential biomarkers of response remain 
under investigation. Tumor PD‑L1 expression, 
tumor‑infiltrating lymphocyte density, micro‑
satellite instability status, and serum hCG 
kinetics during treatment are being explored 
as predictive indicators. Integrative approaches 
that combine immune and molecular profil‑
ing could eventually refine patient selection, 
identifying those most likely to benefit from 
early checkpoint inhibition [81, 82].
Together, these findings indicate that immu‑
notherapy is not merely a salvage option but 
may, in the near future, become an integral 
component of multimodal GTN management, 
potentially reducing chemotherapy exposure, 
minimizing toxicity, and preserving fertility 
without compromising cure rates.
DISCUSSION
The emergence of immunotherapy represents 
a promising strategy under investigation in the 
management of GTN, transforming a disease 
once defined by its exquisite chemosensitiv‑
ity into a model for immune responsiveness. 
Beyond the historical success of cytotoxic ther‑
apy, the encouraging response rates reported in 
small cohorts observed with checkpoint block‑
ade in multidrug‑resistant GTN challenge long‑
held assumptions about the limits of curability 
and open a new era of biologically guided treat‑
ment [14–17]
Biological and Translational Perspectives
The trophoblast is a naturally immune‑priv‑
ileged tissue, characterized by intense PD‑L1 
expression, low MHC class I presentation, and 
an abundance of regulatory immune cells that 
protect the semi‑allogeneic fetus from mater‑
nal rejection [37, 83]. Malignant trophoblastic 
cells preserve and amplify these mechanisms, 
establishing a profoundly immunosuppressive 
microenvironment. When the PD‑1/PD‑L1 axis 
is inhibited, previously silenced cytotoxic T cells 
are reactivated, triggering rapid tumor regres‑
sion—a phenomenon uniquely pronounced in 
GTN compared with most solid tumors [84].
Chemotherapy itself may augment this 
immune susceptibility by inducing immuno‑
genic cell death and neoantigen release, thus 
enhancing checkpoint inhibitor efficacy. This 
dual effect—cytotoxic priming followed by 
immune disinhibition—underpins the dura‑
ble remissions reported after pembrolizumab,

avelumab, and camrelizumab–apatinib regimens 
[67, 76].
Clinical Advances and Remaining Gaps
Accumulated evidence across case series, reg‑
istries, and phase II trials consistently shows 
objective response rates exceeding 70% in 
multidrug‑resistant GTN treated with immune 
checkpoint inhibitors. Pembrolizumab, the most 
widely studied agent, induces rapid hCG decline 
and long‑term remission, often after multiple 
chemotherapy failures. Avelumab, evaluated 
in the TROPHIMMUN and TROPHAMET clini‑
cal studies, demonstrated efficacy in low‑risk 
methotrexate‑resistant GTN with preserved fer‑
tility and minimal toxicity [11, 72]. The Chinese 
experience with camrelizumab plus apatinib 
extended these findings, revealing synergistic 
immunoangiogenic modulation and sustained 
remissions in heavily pretreated disease [13].
Nevertheless, knowledge gaps remain. Most 
published data originate from small, uncon ‑
trolled studies. Optimal treatment duration after 
hCG normalization, the timing of immunother‑
apy initiation (salvage vs. front‑line), manage‑
ment of partial responders, and the number of 
consolidation cycles of immunotherapy are yet 
to be standardized.
Finally, although most immune‑related 
adverse events associated with checkpoint 
inhibitors are mild and reversible, some tox‑
icities—particularly endocrinopathies such as 
hypothyroidism and insulin‑dependent dia‑
betes, as well as inflammatory arthritis—may 
be permanent [85]. These risks require special 
consideration given the very small number of 
GTN cases treated worldwide and the limita‑
tions of the current evidence base, which relies 
predominantly on small cohorts, is vulnerable 
to publication bias, and lacks randomized tri‑
als capable of defining true incidence rates. This 
uncertainty is especially relevant in low ‑risk 
GTN, where conventional sequential single‑
agent chemotherapy achieves cure rates exceed‑
ing 70–90% [1 , 2], underscoring the need for a 
careful risk–benefit assessment before consider‑
ing earlier use of immunotherapy.
Reproductive Outcomes After 
Immunotherapy
Fertility preservation is a central concern in the 
management of GTN, given the young age and 
reproductive aspirations of most patients. One 
of the most encouraging aspects of checkpoint 
inhibitor therapy has been its apparent absence 
of long‑term gonadotoxicity. Across published 
case series and multicenter cohorts, including 
patients treated with pembrolizumab, ave‑
lumab, or camrelizumab plus apatinib, men ‑
strual function typically resumed within weeks 
to months after hCG normalization, and spon‑
taneous conception occurred in several women 
without increased rates of miscarriage or con‑
genital anomalies.
Fertility and obstetric outcomes after immu‑
notherapy in GTN have been reported, particu ‑
larly with PD‑1/PD‑L1 blockade (e.g., avelumab 
in TROPHIMMUN/TROPHAMET and pembroli‑
zumab case series), with patients maintain‑
ing regular cycles and achieving subsequent 
uncomplicated pregnancies and healthy live 
births [56, 72]. These data, however, were not 
reported in the CAP‑01 camrelizumab–apatinib 
study.
Importantly, a recent Brazilian report by 
Braga et al. [56] described a woman with mul‑
tidrug‑resistant choriocarcinoma who achieved 
complete and durable remission following 
pembrolizumab therapy and subsequently 
experienced a normal term pregnancy with 
no maternal or fetal complications. This case 
adds to the growing evidence that checkpoint 
inhibitors do not impair ovarian reserve, uter‑
ine receptivity, or long‑term fertility potential.
In clinical practice, once achieved GTN 
remission after chemotherapy, pregnancy is 
deferred for 12 months after completion of 
therapy, including immune checkpoint inhibi‑
tors, to allow adequate hCG surveillance dur‑
ing the first year of remission, when the risk 
of GTN relapse is highest [23]. By contrast, 
outside the GTN context, many oncology 
programs consider conception ≥ 4–5 months 
after the last immunotherapy dose, provided 
immune‑related adverse events have resolved 
[86, 87]. Thus, in GTN, the 12‑month interval

remains standard, mirroring post ‑chemother ‑
apy guidance and driven primarily by sur‑
veillance needs rather than drug washout, 
notwithstanding the reassuring reproductive 
outcomes reported to date [1 , 2, 23].
Collectively, these data suggest that immu‑
notherapy represents a fertility‑sparing and 
pregnancy‑compatible approach for women 
with refractory GTN. Although long‑ term fol ‑
low‑up remains limited, reproductive outcomes 
appear comparable to those of patients cured 
with standard chemotherapy, an encouraging 
perspective in a disease that primarily affects 
women of reproductive age.
Immunotherapy in PSTT and ETT
PSTT and ETT are relatively chemoresistant var‑
iants of GTN. Their indolent growth, delayed 
presentation, and low hCG secretion often 
result in late diagnosis and limited response to 
standard multi‑agent chemotherapy. Surgical 
resection remains the cornerstone for localized 
disease; however, metastatic or unresectable 
PSTT/ETT carries a poor prognosis, with histori‑
cal survival rates below 50% despite aggressive 
cytotoxic regimens [88].
PSTT and ETT show lower and more heteroge‑
neous PD‑L1 expression than choriocarcinoma. 
Although immunobiology appears less overtly 
inflamed than in choriocarcinoma, case reports 
and small series still document durable complete 
responses to pembrolizumab in patients with 
metastatic PSTT, ETT, or mixed choriocarcinoma‑
ETT refractory to multiple prior regimens, sup‑
porting selective use of checkpoint inhibition in 
these histologies [34, 37, 38].
Crucially, the meta‑analysis by Barcellos et al. 
[32], which synthesized global data on immuno‑
therapy for GTN, demonstrated that remission 
rates did not significantly differ between chorio‑
carcinoma and PSTT/ETT, supporting the view 
that checkpoint inhibition can be effective even 
in these chemoresistant histotypes. This repre‑
sents a paradigm shift, suggesting that immuno‑
therapy may overcome the inherent biological 
resistance of intermediate trophoblastic tumors.
Although the total number of reported PSTT/
ETT cases remains limited (< 25 worldwide), 
cumulative evidence indicates that PD‑1/PD‑L1 
blockade can achieve clinically meaningful and 
sometimes curative responses in refractory dis‑
ease, with favorable safety profiles. Ongoing 
studies are exploring whether early incorpora‑
tion of immunotherapy, alone or combined with 
anti‑angiogenic agents such as apatinib, might 
improve survival in advanced or relapsed cases. 
Until larger trials confirm these results, check‑
point inhibitors should be regarded as a valid 
salvage option for metastatic or recurrent PSTT/
ETT, preferably within expert reference centers.
Registered Clinical Studies
Recent years have witnessed a paradigm shift in 
the management of GTN, driven by the inte‑
gration of immune checkpoint inhibitors into 
clinical research frameworks. Beyond the initial 
success of pembrolizumab and avelumab in mul‑
tidrug‑resistant GTN, new studies are expanding 
the field to include camrelizumab, toripalimab, 
domvanalimab  + zimberelimab, and combina ‑
tion regimens pairing anti‑PD‑1 or anti‑PD‑L1 
antibodies with targeted agents such as apatinib 
or with cytotoxic backbones like Act‑D or MTX 
[11–13, 71–73, 89–95].
Several ongoing and recently completed pro‑
spective trials are now exploring earlier thera‑
peutic integration, combinatorial strategies, 
and reproductive safety endpoints. Collec‑
tively, these studies aim to refine patient selec‑
tion across FIGO risk categories, identify pre‑
dictive biomarkers of immune responsiveness, 
and define the optimal timing and duration of 
treatment. Table 1 summarizes the main clinical

Looking ahead, several priorities emerge:
1. Biomarker refinement—integrating PD‑ L1 
expression, tumor‑infiltrating lymphocytes, 
MSI status, and hCG kinetics into predictive 
algorithms.
2. Rational combinations—checkpoint inhibi ‑
tors with low‑dose chemotherapy or targeted 
agents to enhance efficacy and minimize tox‑
icity.
3. Reproductive outcomes—systematic assess‑
ment of ovarian function, pregnancy rates, 
and offspring health after immune therapy.
4. Global accessibility—partnerships and tiered 
pricing to extend access in low‑ and mid‑
dle‑income countries, where GTN burden 
remains highest.
Ultimately, checkpoint blockade in GTN rep‑
resents not merely a therapeutic innovation but 
a proof of concept for immune reawakening 
in human malignancy—a model of curability 
through biological understanding rather than 
therapeutic escalation.
CONCLUSION
GTN remains one of the most curable human 
malignancies owing to its remarkable chemo‑
sensitivity, and multi‑agent or sequential single‑
agent chemotherapy continues to represent the 
standard of care across risk groups. Nevertheless, 
a small subset of patients develops drug‑resist‑
ant disease, highlighting the need for alterna‑
tive therapeutic strategies capable of achieving 
remission while minimizing cumulative toxicity.
Checkpoint inhibition has emerged as a 
promising option in this context, with pem‑
brolizumab, avelumab, and camrelizumab–apat‑
inib demonstrating meaningful clinical activity 
in small, heterogeneous cohorts of patients with 
multidrug‑resistant GTN. These early findings 
suggest that immunotherapy may complement 
existing treatment algorithms in highly selected 
scenarios, although long‑term safety, fertility 
outcomes, and comparative effectiveness remain 
to be fully defined.
Looking ahead, the integration of immuno‑
therapy into GTN management will depend 
on prospective trials that clarify optimal 
sequencing, combination strategies, biomark‑
ers of response, and patient selection. Ensuring 
equitable access to innovative therapies and 
maintaining the central role of evidence‑based 
chemotherapy remain essential. Ultimately, 
while immunotherapy may contribute to future 
refinements in GTN care, the current evidence 
base warrants cautious optimism and continued 
rigorous evaluation.
