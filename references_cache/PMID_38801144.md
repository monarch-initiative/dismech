# BCL11B-Related Dystonia: Further Evidence of an Emerging Cause of Childhood-Onset Generalized Dystonia.
**Authors:** Garone G, Capuano A, Amodio D, Nicita F, Travaglini L, Graziola F, De Benedictis A, Frascarelli F, Parisi P, Pizzi S, Tartaglia M, Marras CE, Niceta M
**Journal:** Mov Disord Clin Pract (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mdc3.14084](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.14084)

## Content

BCL11B encodes for a bifunctional protein which acts both as a transcriptional repressor and transactivator, involved in dermis, dentition, immune and nervous system development.
1

Heterozygous BCL11B mutations are associated with a neurodevelopmental syndrome featuring intellectual disability (ID), dysmorphic facies, and T cell abnormalities.
2

Recent case reports suggested that a complex movement disorder (MD) including spasticity and dystonia can be part of the phenotypic spectrum.
3

4
,

,

Here, we report on a young girl with childhood‐onset generalized dystonia and ID carrying a de novo, likely pathogenic BCL11B variant.

Our proband is a 23‐year old woman of European ancestry, born at term after an uneventful pregnancy by non‐consanguineous parents. Her motor development was normal, with head control at 3 months, trunk control at 7 months, and autonomous walking at 15 months. First words were pronounced around 1 year of age, but from the age of 2 dysarthric speech was noticed.

At the age of 5 years, dystonic posturing of the left foot was noticed. In the following years, a slow spreading of dystonia involving the left arm (6–7 years), the right arm (8 years), the trunk and the right leg (10 years) was observed. Between 10 and 14‐years of age, a progressive and more rapid worsening of dystonia occurred, with loss of ambulation, further deterioration of manual skills and speech, leading to almost complete anarthria and hypophonia (Video 1).

Non‐verbal cognitive assessment unraveled a moderate ID (intellectual quotient 40, Leiter‐R scale).

Extensive work‐up for metabolic disorders [plasma, urinary and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) amino acids, urinary and CSF organic acids, plasma acylcarnitines, transferrin isoforms, ceruloplasmin and CSF neurotransmitters] was unremarkable. Genetic testing with SNP‐array and a targeted NGS panel for MD were uninformative. A trio‐based whole‐exome sequencing unraveled a de novo missense variant in BCL11B, c.2507G > A p.(Ser836Asn) [NM_138576.3, rs2139752892], annotated in population databases (dbSNP: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp; gnomAD_v.4.0: https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/variant) but with no frequency data, and classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines
7
 (criteria PS4, PM2, PM6, see Table S1 and S2).

Oral treatment with levodopa, tetrabenazine, baclofen and trihexyphenidyl combined with regular BoNT‐A injections only partially improved the symptoms. At the age of 19, she underwent bilateral globus pallidus internus deep brain stimulation (DBS). Despite DBS, dystonia progressively worsened. A mild benefit on appendicular dystonia was noticed (with recovery of limited manual functions, including ability to feed herself alone and to use a tablet to communicate), but axial symptoms worsened. Two years after starting DBS, unintended interruption of the stimulation caused a deterioration of upper limb dystonia which recovered after its restoration, confirming the partial benefit of DBS (see Table S1 and S2).

In‐depth immunological evaluation (see Table S1 and S2) showed a marked reduction in CD4+ Recent Thymic Emigrants cells, increased levels of natural killer cells, and expansion of γδ T cell lineage. Despite immune abnormalities, the patient did not show increased infection susceptibility. Screening for common autoimmune disorders unraveled chronic autoimmune thyroiditis.

Three BCL11B patients have been previously reported to present with complex MD featuring spasticity, dyskinesia and/or dystonia, with onset in infancy in the context of an early neurodevelopmental condition with absent or very limited motor development (Fig. 1B, Table 1).

Unlike those previous reports, our patient showed normal motor milestones up to preschool age. Speech disorder was likely the first dystonic symptom, followed by lower limb dystonia and subsequent generalized spreading with prominent bulbar involvement. In our case, body distribution and temporal evolution of dystonia, cooccurrence of ID and slight bipallidal hypointensities on MRI, made the clinical syndrome reminiscent of KMT2B‐related dystonia.
8
 However, unlike several other monogenic dystonias including KMT2B, DBS response was overall disappointing. Information about phenomenology and response to DBS in other patients with BCL11B‐related dystonia are needed to better delineate this phenotype.

BCL11B is mainly expressed in thymic lymphocytes and brain, and it is essential for nervous and immune systems development.
1
 Specifically, it is critical for striatal development and for medium spiny neurons (MSNs) survival and function. BCL11B deficiency disrupts striatal matrix organization and dysregulates the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)/protein kinase A (PKA) pathway in MSNs, with abnormal phosphorylation of several PKA targets, ultimately leading to impaired synaptic signaling in response to dopamine and glutamate stimulation.
9
 Disruption of cAMP/PKA signaling is the primary molecular alteration in several genetic hyperkinetic MD (such ADCY5‐, GNAO1‐, PDE10A‐, PDE2A‐, GNAL‐related MD), but secondary dysregulation of the same pathway has been found in levodopa‐induced dyskinesia and Huntington's disease.
10

The variant identified in our case falls in the fifth zinc finger domain. This region is highly conserved across evolution, and critical for protein function (Fig. 1C). Accordingly, immunological abnormalities—including increased innate immunity (with increased natural killer and γδ T cell expansion) and reduced recent thymic emigrants—are consistently described in BCL11B mutation carriers,
2
 and represent useful diagnostic biomarkers.

In conclusion, our report expands BCL11B phenotypic spectrum, suggesting that it should be added to the differential diagnosis of genetic causes of generalized dystonia with childhood onset, prominent bulbar involvement and combined ID.
