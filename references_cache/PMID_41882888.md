# An Autopsy Case of Beare-Stevenson Cutis Gyrata Syndrome Presenting with an Extremely Short Small Intestine.
**Authors:** Uraki R, Yamamoto M, Miura N, Irie M, Uemura T, Nakashima J, Araki M, Matsushita K, Kaname T, Fujieda M

## Content

Beare-Stevenson cutis gyrata syndrome (BSS) is a craniosynostosis caused by an FGFR2 pathogenic variant. Detailed reports of autopsy results are scarce. We herein present an autopsy case of BSS characterized by a disproportionate organ size and severe failure to thrive. Upon autopsy, the weights of the heart, liver, and lungs corresponded to the patient's body weight, whereas the brain, pancreas, and spleen were enlarged. Notably, the small intestine was much shorter in our patient than in infants with a similar body size, which could explain the poor weight gain. This case is the first comprehensive autopsy report of organ findings in BSS.

Introduction
Beare-Stevenson cutis gyrata syndrome (BSS) is an ex-
tremely rare autosomal dominant craniosynostosis caused by
a pathogenic variant ofFGFR2 (1). Owing to the sparsity of
reported cases (2-26), detailed symptoms and organ abnor-
malities remain unknown. We herein report an autopsy case
of BSS characterized by severe weight loss and developmen-
tal abnormalities in visceral organs.
Case Report
The patient was delivered via emergency cesarean section
at 36 weeks and 5 days of gestation because of a breech
presentation observed at another hospital. At birth, the pa-
tient’s weight, height, and head circumference were 2,286 g
[-0.9 standard deviation (SD)], 44.0 cm (-1.3 SD), and 30.2
cm (-1.5 SD), respectively. The Apgar scores were 5 at 1
min and 6 at 5 min.
The patient was immediately intubated owing to severe
respiratory distress. Physical examination revealed cranio-
synostosis, characterized by a clover-leaf skull, ocular hy-
pertelorism, and ocular proptosis. Additional findings in-
cluded low-set ears, a prominent umbilical stump, and broad
fingers and toes (Fig. 1). The patient’s family history was
unremarkable. Head CT performed on the first day of life
revealed craniosynostosis (Fig. 2). Due to progressive
ultrasound-detected hydrocephalus, the patient underwent
ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement and subsequent tra-
cheostomy for worsening apnea.
Despite appropriate enteral nutrition, the patient failed to
gain weight. No clinical symptoms suggestive of malabsorp-
tion, such as diarrhea or steatorrhea, were observed. In addi-
tion, even with total parenteral nutrition (TPN) of up to 136
kcal/kg/day, adequate weight gain was not achieved. Recur-
rent sepsis and pneumonia occurred, presumably associated
with malnutrition and intestinal bacterial translocation. Addi-

and skin tags (25). Table 2 presents 28 reported cases of

Previous case reports on BSS have primarily focused on the
prognosis, airway management, neural development, and
craniofacial, skeletal, and limb abnormalities. In the 28 pre-
viously reported cases, the mean birth weight was 3.3 kg,
and most newborns were born full-term, as in our case.
BSS has a poor prognosis, the median age at death is 2
months, and the most common causes are sudden death and
respiratory failure. Biopsies were performed in three previ-
ous cases, and autopsies were performed in only one
case (15) (Table 2). In cases in which autopsy was per-
formed, diffuse anoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, respiratory
failure from diffuse lobar pneumonia, and acute hepatocellu-
lar necrosis were observed.
The first reported case described vomiting due to gastric
dilatation (2); however, other reports only mentioned the
presence of an umbilical hernia (4). Intestinal dysplasia, in-
cluding malrotation, congenita l diaphragmatic hernia, and
esophageal atresia, have been observed in patients with Ap-
ert syndrome, another FGFR-related craniosynostosis (30).
However, unlike Apert syndrome, intestinal dysplasia and
visceral organ abnormalities, aside from pyloric stenosis, are
not prominent in BSS (30).
Although failure to thrive in BSS has been re-
ported (5, 6), the underlying cause remains poorly under-
stood. We herein present an autopsy case of BSS character-
ized by severe failure to thrive and developmental anomalies
in the visceral organs, including the novel finding of an un-
usually short small intestine.
Our patient survived for two years, longer than most pre-
viously reported cases, but exhibited persistent growth im-
pairment despite receiving appropriate TPN. No previous re-
ports of BSS requiring TPN have been identified in the lit-
erature. During life, there were no overt clinical signs of
malabsorption, such as watery diarrhea or steatorrhea, leav-
ing the etiology of her poor weight gain unclear. The
autopsy finding of a markedly shortened small intestine pro-
vides a plausible anatomical explanation for the failure to
thrive and represents a unique contribution of this report.
The growth impairment in this case may have resulted
from malabsorption due to the extremely short small intes-
tine. Despite sufficient nutritional support via TPN, the exact
etiology of growth failure remains uncertain. Previous stud-
ies have suggested that growth impairment in patients with
short bowel syndrome (SBS) receiving TPN can result from
multifactorial mechanisms (31, 32). TPN cannot replace key
intestinal functions such as hormone secretion and enzyme
production, both of which are essential for nutrient assimila-
tion. Nutrient imbalances within TPN formulations, reduced
secretion of growth factors (e.g., insulin-like growth factor),
chronic intestinal inflammation, and TPN-associated compli-
cations, including hepatic dys function and infections, may
all contribute (31, 32). Such underlying disturbances in nu-
trient processing may account for the poor weight gain ob-
served, despite adequate TPN. Together with the marked in-
testinal abnormalities, these factors likely contributed to the
patient’s failure to thrive.
Histologically, fibroblast proliferation was observed from
the submucosa to the muscularis propria, suggesting that im-
paired peristalsis may have caused abdominal distension and
bacteremia secondary to bacterial translocation (33). Fibro-
blast proliferation within the gastrointestinal tract may be
secondary to recurrent infections, malnutrition, or impaired
intestinal perfusion. In addition, the prolonged absence of
enteral nutrition may have resulted in intestinal dysbio-
sis (34), which could have contributed to fibroblast prolif-
eration and subsequent fibrosis via cytokine-mediated
mechanisms (35).
Thymic hypoplasia or atrophy can occur because of con-
genital and acquired factors. Congenital causes include de-
velopmental disorders, such as DiGeorge syndrome, whereas
acquired atrophy may result from chronic stress, infection,
inflammation, or malnutrition. Chronic illness, inflammation,
and nutritional deficiencies may contribute to functional thy-
mic atrophy in patients with SBS. Consequently, repeated
physical stress from increased infection susceptibility secon-
dary to thymic atrophy may have also contributed to the
failure to thrive and the poor prognosis in this case (36).
FGFR proteins can be classified as types 1-4. All FGFRs
are transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinases that comprise
extracellular immunoglobulin-like domains. FGFRs play im-
portant roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, and the de-
velopment of bone and nerve tissues (37). The exact reason
for organ heterogeneity in our case is unclear. However,
FGFR2 is also known to contribute to organ development.
Thus, patients with BSS may have abnormalities in the ex-
pression and function of FGFR2 in various organs (38),
which could affect the growth of these organs. However,
further detailed investigations of these issues are required.
Conclusion
We presented a case of BSS with poor weight gain and
disproportionate organ development; most notably, as a
novel finding, the small intestine was extremely short. We
could not determine the cause of insufficient body weight
gain; however, our autopsy findings might provide new
knowledge about BSS.
