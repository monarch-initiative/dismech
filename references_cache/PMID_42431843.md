# Evaluation of Mutation Risk Using Patient-Derived Organoids in Patients With Lynch Syndrome.
**Authors:** Shin Y, Kim DK, Seo Y, Lee HH, Park J, Park SJ, Park JJ, Cheon JH, Oh J, Lee S, Kim TI
**Journal:** Int J Cancer (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ijc.70646](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.70646)

## Content

Lynch syndrome (LS) is a hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline mutation of DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes, most notably associated with colorectal cancer. Although LS patients face high risk of CRC, risk can vary even among those with the same pathogenic MMR germline mutations. We suggest a functional assay platform for assessing mutation risk using patient-derived organoid (PDOs). We measured organoid response to the cytotoxic effects of a methylating agent, N-methyl-N'-nitro-N-nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) based on DNA damage-induced apoptosis. When normal (non-LS) colon organoids were used, treatment with MNNG and four passages of organoid culture led to decreased total growth (organoid area), but there was no significant change in number. To increase the effect of MNNG and induce apoptosis in normal colon organoids, we added O6BG (an MGMT inhibitor) and an ATR inhibitor. The cytotoxic effects of combined treatment with MNNG, O6BG and ATR inhibitor on non-LS-PDOs were significantly higher than in PDOs of LS patients. To detect the difference at an earlier phase, we examined DNA damage response by analyzing γH2AX expression after treatment with MNNG and O6BG for 24 h. Then, we found higher expression of γH2AX in non-LS-PDOs than in PDOs of LS patients, suggesting continued cell survival with mutation accumulation in LS-PDOs. In conclusion, treatment with MNNG and O6BG using PDOs and measurement of resulting DNA damage response could be used as a preclinical platform for risk stratification in LS patients.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide [1]. Approximately 65% of CRC cases are sporadic with no family history or apparent genetic predisposition. The majority of CRCs (~85%) show chromosomal instability, with microsatellite stable status, involving gain or loss of chromosome segments and loss of heterozygosity [2]. The remaining cases (~10%) shows microsatellite instability‐high (MSI‐H) status caused by non‐functioning mismatch repair (MMR) genes. The majority of sporadic MSI‐H CRC is caused by hypermethylation of the promoter of the MLH1 gene and about 20% of MSI‐H CRC results from germline mutation in MMR genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and EPCAM), which is known as Lynch syndrome (LS) or hereditary non‐polyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC) [3].

Lynch syndrome patients have a 60%–80% lifetime risk of developing CRC and other tumors, including endometrial and ovarian cancer, urologic cancer and gastric cancer; these typically develop around the fifth decade of life [3]. Mutations in the MLH1 or MSH2 gene tend to lead to a higher risk (70%–80%) of developing cancer in a person's lifetime, while mutations in the MSH6 or PMS2 gene have relatively lower risk (25%–60%) of cancer development [4, 5]. In addition, MMR‐deficient dysplastic lesions in individuals with LS tend to progress to cancers more rapidly than in individuals with sporadic adenomas [6]. Therefore, careful endoscopic surveillance every 1–2 years is important for early detection of precancerous colon lesions in LS patients [7].

Although LS patients face high risk of CRC, this risk varies, and there has been no way to evaluate the difference in tumor risk among the LS patients. Even among those with the same pathogenic germline mutations of MMR gene, individualized risk of cancer development differs, which suggests the necessity of individualized cancer prediction model [8, 9, 10].

Functional assay of MMR genes and their variants using classical cell lines and animal models in biomedical research has successfully identified some different molecular mechanisms and their biologic significance [11]. However, these results could not explain individual differences in cancer risk among LS patients. To establish a functional risk assessment platform, we focused on cellular response to DNA damage, a key function of the MMR pathway. In a functional MMR system, DNA damage activates apoptosis to eliminate compromised cells as a critical tumor‐suppressive mechanism [12, 13]. Paradoxically, we hypothesized that MMR‐deficient cells from LS patients would fail to trigger this apoptotic response, thereby tolerating DNA damage and accumulating mutations. This differential response, known as damage tolerance, represents not only a mechanistic hallmark of MMR deficiency but also a potential functional biomarker for individualized mutation risk prediction. Recently, with the advent of human organoids, it is possible to recreate the architecture and physiology of patient‐specific organs in remarkable detail. Patient‐derived organoids (PDOs) offer a unique advantage for testing this hypothesis: by preserving each patient's genetic background, they overcome the inherent limitations of traditional cell lines and animal models [14]. Therefore, we developed a PDO model of LS patient colon tissue to measure the biologic significance of MMR gene variants and predict mutation risk in individual LS patients.

Human colon organoids were established from histologically normal colonic mucosa obtained by endoscopic biopsy. Two distinct groups of organoids were used in this study: (1) PDOs from normal mucosal of patients without Lynch syndrome (non‐LS‐PDOs), and (2) PDOs of normal mucosal from individuals with pathogenic germline mutations in mismatch repair (MMR) genes associated with Lynch syndrome (LS‐PDOs). Among the non‐LS and LS patients included in this study, some had a prior diagnosis of colorectal cancer (CRC) or polyps, whereas others were cancer‐free carriers. For patients with a history of CRC, the normal mucosal tissues were strictly obtained from areas at least 20 cm away from the tumor margin. All biopsied tissues were confirmed to be normal mucosa without any evidence of dysplasia or neoplastic lesions. Detailed clinical characteristics, including the individual CRC histology of the LS patients, are summarized in Table S2. The tissues from patients were washed three times with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and cut into pieces. Subsequently, the fragments were incubated in a digestion solution (DMEM with collagenase). After incubation for 30–40 min at 37°C, they were pipetted to liberate the crypts; then PBS was added and samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min. Isolated crypts were pelleted and cultured in domes of Matrigel (Corning, USA) with organoid culture medium. The composition of organoid culture medium was as follows: Basal culture medium with 50% Wnt/R‐spondin conditioned medium, 10% Noggin (Sigma, USA), B27 and N2 supplements (Gibco, USA), n‐acetyl cysteine (Sigma‐Aldrich), nicotinamide (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), human EGF, gastrin (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), A83‐01 (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), SB202190 (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), and Primocin (InvivoGen, France). Organoid culture medium was refreshed every 2 days. To passage the organoids, Matrigel was broken up by pipetting and organoids were collected in a tube, then centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min, and the medium was removed. Then, after adding 400 μL TrypLE Express (Invitrogen, USA), the organoids were incubated at 37°C for approximately 5 min. After seeding the organoids with Matrigel, the same medium was used with 10 μM Y27632 (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) added for the first 2 days. All experiments were performed with mycoplasma‐free cells. The absence of contamination was confirmed using the e‐Myco Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (Intron Biotechnology Inc., South Korea). For evaluation of organoid growth, the number of organoids was counted, 10 organoids with a size over 50 μm on the initial day were marked and changes of organoid area in these 10 organoids were measured.

The organoids were exposed to N‐methyl‐N′‐nitro‐N‐nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Japan). To exclude differences in the efficacy of MNNG related to O6‐methylguanine‐DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) activity, organoids were pretreated with 25 μM O6‐benzylguanine (O6BG) (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), MGMT inhibitor, and 10 μM VE‐821 (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), ATR inhibitor, that was also used to increase cell death, as shown in Figure 1A. All chemicals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide and diluted in deionized water.

The organoid culture media was removed, and the organoid was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min at room temperature. Then they were washed extensively at least 3 times with PBS for removal of the fixing solution. For staining, organoids were permeabilized with 5% Triton‐X for 20 min at room temperature. The organoids were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Then, they were incubated with rabbit anti‐γH2AX antibody (Cell Signaling, USA) overnight at 4°C in 1:500 dilution. Afterward, the organoids were washed several times with PBS and incubated with secondary anti‐rabbit antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark and washed again with PBS. After DAPI staining and washing, the organoids were analyzed with an LSM700 laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) and Operetta CLS (PerkinElmer, USA). Immunofluorescence images were analyzed using ImageJ software. To precisely quantify the nuclear expression of γH2AX, regions of interest (ROIs) were strictly defined by applying a threshold to the DAPI channel to outline the nuclei. These DAPI‐derived ROIs were then transferred to the corresponding target protein channel, and the mean fluorescence intensity within these specific nuclear regions was measured.

Four‐micrometer sections of 4% PFA fixed, paraffin‐embedded organoids were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in gradually decreasing concentrations of ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) in a heated pressure cooker for 5 or 7 min. After incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were incubated in 3% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with anti‐γH2AX antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by the secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. After sections had been developed with a Vectastain ABC kit (Vector Laboratories, USA), immunodetection was performed using DAB solution (Dako, USA). After counterstaining with hematoxylin, IHC staining was evaluated by light microscopy and immunoactivity was assessed based on the proportion of immunostained organoids.

The organoids were lysed using lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP‐40) containing protease inhibitors. Protein content was quantified using standard BCA assay (BioRad, USA), and equal amounts of protein were run on polyacrylamide gels (15%) and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 10% skim milk in 0.1% TBST (Tween20 in TBS solution) for 60 min, membranes were probed with anti‐γH2AX antibodies and incubated at 4°C overnight. Bound antibodies were detected with secondary antibody and visualized using West‐Q Femto clean ECL solutions (GenDEPOT, USA). For a loading control, the monoclonal anti‐⍺‐tubulin antibody (Genetex, USA) at a dilution of 1:2000 was used. The intensity of the protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software. The relative expression levels of γH2AX were normalized to the expression of ⍺‐tubulin.

Samples were prepared according to either the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA library preparation guide or the TruSeq DNA PCR‐free library preparation guide. Briefly, libraries were constructed using either 1 μg (TruSeq DNA PCR‐free library) or 100–200 ng (TruSeq Nano DNA library) genomic DNA, fragmented via Covaris to achieve insert sizes of ~350 or ~550 bp, followed by bead‐based size selection, end repair, A‐tailing, and adapter ligation with indexed adapters (“A”‐overhang ligated to “T” on adapters). For the TruSeq Nano protocol, PCR was employed post‐ligation to enrich the library. Final libraries were validated by quantification and quality assessment. All sequencing library preparation and sequencing was performed by Macrogen (South Korea). Paired‐end sequencing was performed on Illumina platforms. Real‐time base calling was conducted using RTA, and raw BCL data were converted to FASTQ via bcl2fastq2 (v2.20.0) with stringent demultiplexing settings. Sequencing coverage and quality statistics for all samples are summarized in Table S1.

Somatic variants were identified using GATK Mutect2 pipeline (v4.6.2.0) in a tumor‐only mode, as matched germline DNA from blood was not available. To distinguish MNNG‐induced somatic mutations from germline variants and preexisting background mutations, we implemented a rigorous filtering strategy. First, known germline variants were filtered using the gnomAD (v2.1.1) AF‐only germline resource and small ExAC common sites. Second, we utilized the untreated PDOs from the same patient as a baseline reference. Any variants detected in the MNNG‐treated organoids that were already present in their respective untreated counterparts were systematically subtracted. Only variants meeting the ‘PASS’ filter criteria were retained for subsequent functional annotation with Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP, v115, GRCh37 cache). To further ensure the retention of high‐confidence somatic variants and to minimize residual technical artifacts, additional post hoc variant filtering was applied following initial PASS‐based filtering. Specifically, variants were required to meet all of the following criteria: (1) a tumor variant allele frequency (VAF) of ≥ 3% supported by a minimum of five alternate reads; (2) a VAF of ≤ 1% in the matched untreated PDO, used here as a paired baseline reference to eliminate preexisting background mutations not captured during the subtraction step; and (3) a population allele frequency of ≤ 1% in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v2.1.1) to exclude common germline polymorphisms. Annotated VCF files were converted into mutation annotation format (MAF) using vcf2maf (v1.6.21). All downstream analyses were performed in R (v4.3.2) with the maftools and sigminer packages.

Gene‐level mutation frequencies were compared between normal and MLH1‐mutant organoids. For each gene, a 2 × 2 contingency table (mutated vs. non‐mutated across the two groups) was constructed and tested using Fisher's exact test [15]. Resulting p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR), and genes with adjusted p values (padj) < 0.05 were defined as significantly mutated and retained for downstream analyses.

Mutational signatures were profiled in R using sigminer with COSMIC v3.4 reference matrices (GRCh37). Variants from the significantly mutated genes were categorized into single‐base substitutions (SBS; 96 trinucleotide contexts) and short insertions/deletions (ID; 83 indel categories). Sample‐wise mutation catalogs were aligned to the COSMIC references, and signature exposures were estimated by non‐negative least squares (NNLS) fitting. Both absolute exposures (counts) and proportionally normalized exposures were computed.

To compare mutational patterns between groups, SBS and ID signature exposures were summarized and visualized using bar plots, and differences in mutation spectra were further assessed by separately enumerating single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (INDELs) among significantly mutated genes.

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software (v2.4, Sydney, Australia) [16]. Statistical differences between the control and experimental groups were evaluated using the unpaired Student's two‐tailed t‐test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

The MMR process causes death of cells with methylation‐damaged DNA bases [17]. Therefore, MNNG induces cell death through DNA damage [18]. To determine the appropriate dose and treatment time of MNNG for normal response as a control, we measured organoid growth after treatment with a methylating agent, MNNG, in normal colon organoids (see Supporting Information: Methods). We observed organoid growth at every passage after treatment with MNNG, and compared the area and number of organoids after four passages. Organoid growth was significantly decreased in MNNG‐treated non‐LS‐PDOs (p < 0.05), but there was no significant difference in organoid number with either high‐dose/short‐term treatment or low‐dose/long‐term treatment (Figure S1). To increase sensitivity to MNNG and induce apoptosis in normal colon organoids, we additionally treated them with O6BG and ATR inhibitor (Figure 1A) [19]. Although an O6BG‐alone treatment arm was not included in our current experimental design, it is well established in prior literature that O6BG acts exclusively as a specific MGMT inhibitor without inherently inducing cytotoxicity or DNA damage [20, 21, 22]. Indeed, we found that the growth of normal colon organoids significantly decreased after combined treatment with MNNG, O6BG and ATR inhibitor (p < 0.001) (Figure 1B). However, LS‐PDOs with MLH1 or MSH2 mutations did not show significant change in organoid growth (Figure 1C,D).

Although we found a significant difference in the growth of organoids between non‐LS‐PDOs and LS‐PDOs after combined treatment with MNNG, O6BG, and ATR inhibitor, we could only measure this difference after several passages of organoid culture. Therefore, to detect this difference earlier, we used γH2AX, which is a key marker in the repair process of damaged DNA, as a marker of early DNA damage response (DDR) (Figure S2) [23]. To evaluate the expression of γH2AX in non‐LS‐PDOs as well as MLH1‐mutated and MSH2‐mutated PDOs, we used a low and high dose of MNNG or a combination of MNNG and O6BG for a 24‐h period. This duration was chosen to ensure the detection of sustained DNA damage signaling [24, 25, 26, 27]. Meanwhile, in DDR experiments, ATR inhibitor was not added because too much cell death could disturb measurement of DDR. We performed immunohistochemistry to evaluate the effect of MNNG treatment. In non‐LS‐PDOs, MNNG induced strong γH2AX expression, whereas only faint expression was detected in LS‐PDOs (MLH1‐/MSH2‐mutated) (Figure 2A). To increase the DNA‐damaging effect of MNNG, we then treated PDOs with a combination of MNNG and O6BG, an MGMT inhibitor. Under these conditions, non‐LS‐PDOs showed a clear increase in γH2AX, while MLH1‐ and MSH2‐mutated PDOs exhibited less induction. These differences were robustly supported by the quantitative analyses of both western blotting (Figure 2B) and immunofluorescence (Figure 2C). Specifically, the quantification of immunofluorescence demonstrated a statistically significant increase in nuclear γH2AX intensity in non‐LS‐PDOs following treatment with either MNNG alone or MNNG with O6BG compared to the untreated control (p = 0.002). In contrast, this DNA damage response was notably blunted in the MMR‐deficient LS‐PDOs.

To confirm mutation accumulation in LS‐PDOs compared to non‐LS‐PDOs, we performed whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) analysis on both normal and MLH1‐mutated PDOs following treatment with MNNG. Organoids were treated with 25 μM MNNG for 24 h, and then cultured for 48 h without further MNNG treatment to allow organoids to repair mutations or recover DDR (Figure 4A). Both MNNG‐treated and untreated organoids were subjected to WGS analyses, and comparative assessments were conducted after significance filtering of the mutation profiles to pinpoint mutations accruing in both normal and MLH1‐mutated PDOs. Overall, the total somatic mutation burden was higher in MLH1‐mutated PDOs (n = 742) than in non‐LS‐PDOs (n = 600), consistent with impaired mutation surveillance in MLH1‐deficient cells. In non‐LS‐PDOs, the overall mutation burden remained relatively low following MNNG exposure. According to the variant classification, somatic mutations were predominantly distributed across non‐coding genomic regions, including intergenic regions (IGR; n = 241) and intronic sequences (n = 226), while coding variants were comparatively limited, with only four missense mutations identified. However, in terms of overall variant type, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were the most frequent. Furthermore, their single nucleotide variant (SNV) class spectrum was relatively restricted, with C>T (n = 143) and T>C (n = 121) substitutions being the most frequently observed base changes. In contrast, this baseline profile was significantly altered in MLH1‐mutated PDOs, which exhibited a substantial increase in mutation burden. Their variant classification similarly demonstrated a predominance of non‐coding mutations, with intergenic (IGR; n = 293) and intronic variants (n = 286) constituting many somatic alterations. Accordingly, the variant type analysis revealed a marked enrichment of insertions (INS; 65 vs. 31, approximately 2.1‐fold increase) and deletions (DEL; 121 vs. 110) in MLH1‐mutated PDOs relative to non‐LS‐PDOs, along with an elevated SNP count (537 vs. 443) and a slightly increased frequency of dinucleotide polymorphisms (DNP; 19 vs. 16). Finally, their SNV class distribution was remarkably expanded, with C>T (n = 181) and T>C (n = 137) transitions predominating, alongside elevated frequencies across a broader range of base substitution types compared to non‐LS‐PDOs (Figure 4B). Furthermore, when comparing mutation profiles across the same gene set, MLH1‐mutated PDOs harbored a greater number of alterations—encompassing missense, nonsense, frameshift, splice‐site variants, and in‐frame indels (Figure 4C). To dissect the mutational processes underlying these differences, we compared SBS and ID spectra against COSMIC reference signatures (Figure S3A,B). Focusing on MMR deficiency and DNA damage repair associated signatures, MLH1‐deficient PDOs exhibited strong contributions from SBS1, SBS16, whereas these were minimal or absent in non‐LS‐PDOs. In MLH1‐mutated organoids, SBS1 may reflect the increased fixation of 5‐methylcytosine deamination events, and SBS16 may indicate secondary cellular or metabolic perturbations associated with MLH1 loss. Interestingly, SBS32, a signature associated with alkylating agent exposure, was prominently observed in non‐LS organoids (Figure 4D), whereas it was markedly lower in MLH1‐mutated PDOs. This likely reflected a transient mutational trace generated during active, albeit potentially futile, DNA mismatch repair processing of MNNG‐induced lesions in MMR‐proficient cells. In contrast, the blunted SBS32 response in MLH1‐deficient PDOs underscored their damage tolerance phenotype. On the other hand, the detection of SBS6 in non‐LS‐PDOs suggested that excessive lesion load might have transiently exceed the repair capacity of an intact MMR system. Furthermore, the significant enrichment of indel signatures, specifically ID14, ID21, and ID23, in MLH1‐mutated PDOs (Figure 4E) aligned with the experimental compendium of mismatch repair deficiency [28]. These signatures represent the failure to correct polymerase slippage at homopolymer tracts, confirming that our PDO model functionally recapitulated the microsatellite instability (MSI) hallmark of Lynch syndrome. These findings supported that pathogenic mutation of the MLH1 gene promoted the accumulation of mutations through dysfunction of mismatch repair.

LS is caused by inherited mutations in genes of the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) pathway, which is remarkably important for correcting DNA replication errors. Although LS patients have a high risk of CRC, this risk varies [29]. However, individualized risk assessment tools that evaluate the cancer or mutation risk of LS patients have not been established. Therefore, further detailed molecular information about the MMR pathway is essential for estimating mutation risk in LS. Recently, it has been reported that MMR‐deficient cells are more resistant to the cytotoxic effects of certain DNA damage agents. In particular, Karran et al. demonstrated that MMR‐deficient
Escherichia coli
 were resistant to the cytotoxic effects of the DNA alkylating agent N‐methyl‐N′‐nitro‐N‐nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) [30]. MNNG has been used to induce DNA methylation damage that is directly recognized by MMR proteins, which results in recruitment of MMR genes. A critical cytotoxic lesion created by MNNG is the O6‐methylguanine, which is commonly mispaired with thymine during replication, resulting in a MeG‐T mispair. MMR‐related genes recognize and remove these lesions, and methylguanine methyltransferase (MGMT) also directly repairs methylguanine. Thus, the processing of MeG‐T mispairs by the MMR pathway creates secondary DNA damage that ultimately causes cell death. Many reports have demonstrated molecular interactions between MMR proteins and key DNA damage signaling molecules such as ATR, ATM, CHK1, and CHK2 [31]. These mechanisms indicate that MMR plays a protective role in repairing and removing damaged cells to reduce the risk of mutation accumulation.

As for the biological significance of MMR genes, some researchers showed functional assays in different experimental systems. However, most had intrinsic limitations because they were developed based on bacteria, yeast, or mice [11]. Recently, Bouvet et al. reported that an assay for measuring cell response to the cytotoxic effect of a methylating agent can determine the effects of VOUS in MMR genes. In total, 30% of LS patients have a variant of unknown significance (VOUS) [32], and VOUS represent a major clinical challenge for diagnosis and proper surveillance of carriers and their relatives due to lack of satisfying significant evidence. Therefore, based on the DNA damage‐induced apoptosis function of MMR genes, Bouvet et al. evaluated the significance of VOUS of MMR genes [32]. However, in individualized risk assessment, this assay also has limitation due to its restrictive cell line system (the MMR‐deficient HCT116 cell line).

Human organoids can provide valuable information about the mechanisms of specific diseases, and patient‐specific in vitro disease models. Therefore, we used LS‐PDOs to recapitulate the patient‐specific molecular background, including other factors that interact with the MMR pathway and its functional consequence. Then, to maximize the difference in MNNG‐induced cytotoxicity in these organoids, we used an ATR inhibitor. In addition, to suppress the functional activity of MGMT to repair methylguanine and to show more MMR‐dependent pathways, O6BG, an MGMT inhibitor, was used in our models. We compared non‐LS‐PDOs and LS‐PDOs at four passages after treatment with ATR inhibitor and O6BG to maximize the difference in MNNG‐induced cytotoxicity. From our results, we demonstrated that LS‐PDOs could survive MNNG‐induced cytotoxicity, which suggests high risk of mutation accumulation and cancer.

However, to confirm this difference in MNNG‐induced cytotoxicity in PDOs, long‐term culture of organoids for several passages was needed. Therefore, to detect DNA damage in an earlier phase, we utilized a DNA damage recognition marker, γH2AX. Organoids were treated with MNNG alone or MNNG/O6BG without ATR inhibitor to detect DDR in live organoids. Non‐LS‐PDOs showed high DDR after MNNG and O6BG treatment. However, MMR gene‐mutated organoids showed much less DDR. Our results enabled the comparison of early‐phase DDR resulting from MNNG and O6BG between normal and LS patients. Then, we confirmed differences in mutation accumulation between normal and LS‐PDOs by WGS using the same treatment protocols. In addition, our analysis of sequencing data allowed us to define a mutational signature of MMR deficiency. Notably, Drost et al. reported that MLH1‐knockout organoids accumulate mutations predominantly due to replication errors and recapitulate mutation profiles similar to MMR‐deficient colorectal cancers, supporting our observations of signature shifts in MLH1‐mutant PDOs [33]. Building upon this, our data further revealed the distinct enrichment of specific indel signatures (e.g., ID14, ID21, and ID23) in MLH1‐mutated PDOs, which represent unchecked polymerase slippage at homopolymer tracts. Moreover, the blunted SBS32 response in these MMR‐deficient PDOs, contrasted with the prominent mutational trace generated during active repair attempts in normal cells, profoundly underscores their damage tolerance mechanism. Capturing these delicate signature shifts highlights the value of our PDO model not just as a mechanistic study tool, but as a robust functional surrogate for evaluating patient‐specific MMR competence.

A recent study showed substantial inter‐individual variation in MLH1 protein expression in MLH1+/− mice [34]. They also observed heterogenous expression of MLH1, showing MLH1‐negative cells in the villi rather than in the crypt. This heterogeneity underscores the advantage of PDO models in capturing patient‐level variation in MMR competence [35]. These findings also support the usefulness of PDO models for precise risk stratification in LS patients.

However, recently, Bohaumilitzky et al. demonstrated that healthy LS carriers showed elevated mucosal T‐cell infiltration even without CRC, and the immune milieu of normal colon mucosa of cancer‐free LS carriers varied significantly from that of LS patients with CRC [36]. These results suggest that we have to consider immune rejection as a protective factor against CRC risk in LS patients in future studies.

Further research using larger sample sizes with long‐term prognostic data and various genetic mutations associated with LS is needed to confirm that our PDO model is suitable for mutation and cancer risk prediction in LS patients.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that MNNG‐induced DNA damage and cell death varied between non‐LS‐PDOs and LS‐PDOs, suggesting that our PDO model could serve as a useful functional surrogate for individualized mutation and CRC risk stratification in LS patients.
