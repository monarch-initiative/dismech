BACKGROUND: Renal agenesis (RA), a rare congenital kidney defect, varies in 
clinical severity. Unilateral renal agenesis (URA) is more prevalent and may 
cause long-term renal complications, whereas bilateral renal agenesis (BRA) is 
typically fatal. While epidemiological data on RA is limited in China, 
understanding its prevalence and associated factors is crucial for public health 
interventions.
METHODS: We conducted a prevalence analysis using data from the Chinese Birth 
Defects Monitoring Network between 2007 and 2020. Prevalence rates of RA, URA, 
and BRA were calculated and stratified by birth year, maternal age, residence 
area, geographical region, and infant sex. Temporal trends in prevalence were 
examined. We also investigated pregnancy outcomes among affected infants, 
including preterm births, low birth weight, early neonatal deaths, and 
termination of pregnancy for fetal anomaly (TOPFA). Furthermore, co-occurring 
congenital anomalies were assessed. Poisson regression analysis was used to 
assess the distribution of URA and BRA across residential areas and geographic 
regions and to identify factors associated with their prevalence.
RESULTS: Among 25,909,000 births between 2007 and 2020, a total of 5,906 cases 
of RA were identified (5,020 URA and 780 BRA). The prevalence rates per 10,000 
live and still births were 2.28 for RA, 1.94 for URA, and 0.30 for BRA, all 
showing an increasing trend over the study period. Compared to URA, BRA was 
significantly associated with a higher proportion of preterm birth, low birth 
weight, early neonatal death, and TOPFA. Poisson regression analysis revealed a 
consistent distribution of URA and BRA across residential areas and geographic 
regions, with higher prevalence observed in urban areas and the eastern and 
central regions of China. Maternal age < 35 years and female sex were associated 
with a higher prevalence of URA, while male sex was associated with a higher 
prevalence of BRA. Both URA and BRA frequently co-occurred with congenital 
malformations of the circulatory, urinary, and musculoskeletal systems.
CONCLUSIONS: The study provides the first comprehensive epidemiological 
assessment of RA in China. The increasing prevalence and distinct differences in 
the pregnancy outcomes between URA and BRA underscore the importance of timely 
prenatal diagnosis and appropriate counseling for affected families.

Renal agenesis (RA) is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by the complete absence of one (unilateral renal agenesis, URA) or both kidneys (bilateral renal agenesis, BRA). This developmental defect arises from the failure of ureteric bud to form or to appropriately interact with the metanephric mesenchyme, leading to the absence of kidney development, often followed by apoptosis during early embryogenesis [1, 2]. URA, the more frequent form, is often asymptomatic due to compensatory hypertrophy of the contralateral kidney [3]. However, individuals with URA face an increased long-term risk of developing hypertension, proteinuria, and chronic kidney disease later in life [4, 5]. BRA, also known as Potter’s syndrome, is typically associated with severe oligohydramnios, pulmonary hypoplasia, and characteristic craniofacial anomalies, which are generally incompatible with postnatal survival [6, 7]. Current research underscores the complex and multifactorial origin of renal agenesis, where genetic susceptibility, potentially through mutations in genes such as EYA1, SIX1, SIX4, and SIX5, combines with various environmental exposures—including maternal diabetes, obesity, parental age, alcohol/tobacco exposure, caffeine intake, environmental chemicals, certain drugs, assisted reproductive technologies, and prenatal infections—and the interactions between these factors to impede normal renal development [2, 8–13]. Both unilateral and bilateral renal agenesis are known to be associated with other congenital anomalies [1].

Similar to China, numerous countries have established national birth defect surveillance systems that include renal agenesis as a monitored malformation, such as the European Platform on Rare Disease Registration (EUROCAT), and the National Birth Defects Prevention Network (NBDPN) in the United States. Renal agenesis prevalence varies considerably across geographic regions and ethnic groups. According to the NBDPN’s major birth defects data report, the average prevalence of renal agenesis/dysplasia across states in the United States ranged from 1.4 to 10.2 per 10,000 live births between 2016 and 2020 [14]. Consistent with global observations, URA is more prevalent than BRA. The EUROCAT report, encompassing data from 2005 to 2022, documented an average prevalence of 3.72 per 10,000 births for URA, significantly higher than the 1.24 per 10,000 births for BRA. Furthermore, EUROCAT data indicated an increasing trend in the prevalence of URA during this period, while the prevalence of BRA remained relatively stable [15]. However, national studies on RA remain limited in China. To address this gap and elucidate the first epidemiological profile of renal agenesis in China, we performed a 14-year (2007–2020) nationwide analysis of hospital-based data from the Chinese Birth Defects Monitoring Network (CBDMN). Our study examined the prevalence, long-term trends, associated pregnancy outcomes, and concurrent congenital anomalies.

The study population comprised perinatal infants and renal agenesis cases reported to the CBDMN from surveillance hospitals between 2007 and 2020, including both live births and stillbirths. The perinatal infant data represent monthly aggregate counts of infants from 28 weeks of gestation through the first 7 days after birth. RA cases were collected as individual case records, regardless of gestational age. Stillbirths were categorized as fetal deaths or terminations of pregnancy for fetal anomalies (TOPFA). The CBDMN, founded in 1986, is a nationwide hospital-based birth defects surveillance system comprising 763 member hospitals. With their combined annual deliveries representing over 10% of the country’s total births, the network demonstrates strong national representativeness [16]. Comprehensive protocols for case identification, data acquisition, and quality assurance in this surveillance network have been published elsewhere [17–19]. Congenital anomaly diagnoses are confirmed by neonatologists, obstetricians, or sonographers in participating healthcare facilities during pregnancy or within the first week postpartum. Aggregate monthly statistics and all registered birth defect cases are recorded and submitted by certified staff through a secure web-based reporting platform. All documented malformations in this system are assigned codes by an expert panel based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). In accordance with EUROCAT Guide, cases with ICD-10 codes for URA (Q60.0) and BRA including Potter sequence (Q60.1, Q60.6), were retrieved for analysis [20].

Fetal kidneys are typically detectable via ultrasound examination around 12 weeks of gestational age, located inferior to the adrenal glands [11]. The CBDMN adheres to the definition and criterion of renal agenesis as specified by EUROCAT. Specifically, URA is defined as “unilateral absence of kidney”, and BRA is defined as “bilateral absence, agenesis, dysplasia, or hypoplasia of kidneys, including Potter’s syndrome - a condition incompatible with life " [21]. Within the monitoring network, congenital anomaly identification adheres to the CBDMN monitoring handbook and relevant clinical guidelines. Following the 2003 Administrative Method on Antenatal Diagnostic Techniques Regulation issued by National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China, fetal anomalies detected during pregnancy require verification by at least two licensed physicians at an accredited prenatal diagnostic center. Specifically, suspected RA observed during prenatal ultrasound necessitates definitive diagnosis through post-termination evaluation or postnatal examination.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University. This study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, as well as the declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of this study and the use of de-identified data, a waiver of informed consent was granted.

The prevalence rate (PR, per 10,000 births) of renal agenesis was calculated for URA and BRA subtypes, stratified by pregnancy outcome (live birth, fetal death, and TOPFA). Prevalence was analyzed by fetal/infant sex, year of birth (2007–2020), maternal age (< 35 and ≥ 35 years), residence (urban or rural), and geographic region (eastern, central, western China). Maternal residence and geographic region were determined according to the mother’s address maintained for at least one year prior to delivery, with urban area defined as cities or urbanized regions and rural area as villages or countryside. Geographic regions followed provincial-level administrative divisions, reflecting both geography and socioeconomic factors. Birth weight (< 2,500 g, 2,500-3,999 g, and ≥ 4,000 g), gestational age (< 37, 37–41, and ≥ 42 weeks), and pregnancy outcomes (fetal death, TOPFA, early neonatal death, and alive more than 7 days) were also analyzed. Group comparisons used Chi-square tests, secular trends were assessed via linear Chi-square tests, and Poisson regression models generated adjusted prevalence rate ratios (aPRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1, with statistical significance set at α = 0.05.

As detailed in Table 1, between 2007 and 2020, the CBDMN identified a total of 5,906 cases of renal agenesis, comprising 5,020 URA cases, 780 BRA cases, and 106 cases with unknown laterality. The incidence of preterm birth and low birth weight was notably elevated among infants with BRA (96.79% and 97.53%, respectively) compared to those with URA (62.72% and 60.98%, respectively). Similarly, TOPFA was occurred in 63.99% of all cases, with a significant higher proportion in BRA (96.59%) compared to URA (59.08%) cases. Postnatal survival within the first seven days also differed significantly by laterality. The survival rate during this period was 39.29% for infants with URA, while infants with BRA experienced a considerably lower survival rate. The cases was predominantly Han Chinese (94.38%) and involved mostly singleton pregnancies (97.38%), with the majority of mothers being under 35 years of age (89.72%). Notably, BRA showed a male excess (59.08%), while URA was more common in urban (66.31%) and eastern regions (43.61%). Furthermore, the proportion of cases associated with other congenital malformations was higher in infants with BRA (40.77%) than in those with URA (31.14%). Finally, the diagnosis of BRA was more frequently made prenatally (98.72%) compared to URA (85.57%).

During the study period, the average prevalence rate for overall RA was 2.28 per 10,000 births (95% CI: 2.22–2.34), with rates of 1.94 (95% CI: 1.88–1.99) and 0.30 (95% CI: 0.28–0.32) per 10,000 births for unilateral and bilateral renal agenesis, respectively. Additionally, URA cases comprised 2,010 live births, 44 fetal deaths, and 2,965 TOPFA. The corresponding prevalence rates per 10,000 births for live birth, fetal death, and TOPFA among URA cases were 0.78 (95% CI: 0.74–0.81), 0.017 (95% CI: 0.012–0.023), and 1.14 (95% CI: 1.10–1.19), respectively. For BRA cases, the prevalence rates for live birth, fetal death, and TOPFA were notably lower. These rates were 0.013 (95% CI: 0.009–0.018), 0.006 (95% CI: 0.004–0.010), and 0.28 (95% CI: 0.26–0.30) per 10,000 births, respectively. The prevalence rates increased from 1.08 to 3.17 per 10,000 births for overall cases, from 0.87 to 2.70 per 10,000 births for URA cases, and from 0.21 to 0.44 per 10,000 births for BRA cases (Table 2). These increasing trends were consistently observed across various subgroups for URA, including maternal age, fetal/infant sex, residential area, and geographic region. For BRA, statistically significant upward trends in prevalence were also evident across most subgroups, with the exception of female infants, mothers residing in the eastern region, and mothers aged ≥ 35 years. However, the magnitude of these trends was less pronounced compared to those observed for URA, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Table 2 shows the results of the multivariable Poisson modeling analysis, revealing statistically significant prevalence differences across urban–rural areas, maternal age, geographic region, and fetal/infant sex. For URA, offspring of women aged ≥ 35 years showed a lower PR relative to those born to younger mothers (aPRR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.97). In contrast, no statistically significant association with maternal age was identified for BRA. Regarding fetal/infant sex, a significantly lower prevalence was observed in males for URA (aPRR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.71–0.80). Conversely, a male excess was noted for BRA (aPRR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.10–1.49). Urban-rural disparities were evident for both URA and BRA, with higher prevalence rates observed in urban areas. The aPRR for urban areas was 2.63 (95% CI: 2.48–2.79) for URA and 1.96 (95% CI: 1.68–2.28) for BRA. Both unilateral and bilateral renal agenesis exhibited similar patterns of significant geographic variation, with the highest PRs in eastern China, followed by central and then western regions.

During the study, a notable proportion of RA cases co-occurred with other congenital malformations. Specifically, 1,563 (31.14%) URA cases and 318 (40.77%) BRA cases presented with concomitant congenital anomalies. Both unilateral and bilateral renal agenesis most frequently co-occurred with congenital anomalies of the circulatory, urinary, and musculoskeletal systems. However, the proportion of BRA cases associated with congenital anomalies of the respiratory, digestive, and genital systems was higher compared to URA. Chromosomal syndromes were observed in 2.35% of non-isolated URA cases, whereas no such association was found in cases of bilateral renal agenesis (Table 3).

Concomitant anomalies in non-isolated cases of renal agenesis

Abbreviation: URA, unilateral renal agenesis; BRA, bilateral renal agenesis

Leveraging data from over 25 million births in the CBDMN between 2007 and 2020, our study estimated the average prevalence rates of overall, unilateral, and bilateral renal agenesis to be 2.28, 1.94, and 0.30 per 10,000 births, respectively. Previously reported prevalence rates exceeded our overall prevalence estimate in the majority (95%) of U.S. states [14], South Korea [22], Japan [23] and in areas of Taiwan [24]. However, it was higher than the rate reported in British Columbia [25], although the data from that study may be outdated. The prevalence of URA was lower than rates reported in France [26], Australia, Canada [23], and the EUROCAT data [15]. Similarly, the prevalence of BRA was also lower than the EUROCAT rate for the corresponding period [15]. Overall, the prevalence of renal agenesis in this Chinese population appears to be lower than that observed in many developed countries. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in diagnostic capabilities and potentially to racial or ethnic variations. For instance, a study conducted in Hawaii reported that the prevalence of renal agenesis among individuals of White ancestry was 2.3 times higher than that among individuals of Japanese ancestry [27]. Direct comparison of RA prevalence rates is constrained by variations in surveillance methodologies, diagnostic, and inclusion criteria across studies.

Our large-scale, nationally representative study revealed a significant increase in the prevalence of overall, unilateral and bilateral renal agenesis during the study period, with observed increases of 194%, 210%, and 109%, respectively. This upward trend in RA prevalence aligns with observations in several other countries and regions. The U.S. CDC’s Birth Defects Monitoring Program documented an average annual increase of 9.7% in the incidence rate of renal agenesis between 1970 and 1977 [28]. Similarly, rising trends have been reported in national monitoring systems in Japan [23] and Korea [22]. Regarding URA, the prevalence in Australia rose 2.4-fold from 1977–1984 to 2007–2011, a trend consistent with findings in Canada [23]. However, in contrast to the observed increase in our study, EUROCAT reported a relatively stable prevalence of BRA between 2007 and 2020, fluctuating between 0.99 and 1.57 per 10,000 births [15]. Several factors may account for the observed rising prevalence in renal agenesis in our study. These include potential improvements in screening and diagnostic capabilities over time, enhanced data reporting quality, and possibly increased exposure to environmental hazardous substances. A review suggests that maternal exposure to various environmental chemicals during pregnancy, such as dioxins, bisphenol, phthalates, heavy metals, and air pollution, can have adverse effects on kidney development [13]. These chemicals are often associated with the consumption of high-fat animal products, pesticide manufacturing, the use of plastic containers, shampoos, soil, drinking water, and fossil fuel combustion, all of which are linked to daily human activities. Given that BRA is often more accurately diagnosed prenatally compared to URA, and the increase in BRA prevalence was less pronounced than that of URA, the observed overall rise in prevalence is likely, at least in part, a reflection of improved detection rates.

Statistically significant differences in RA prevalence were observed across specific maternal and infant demographic characteristics. Regarding maternal age, the association with offspring renal agenesis remains somewhat debated in the literature. Our study indicates that younger maternal age was associated with a higher risk of URA compared to advanced maternal age. This aligns with findings by Parikh et al., who reported younger age as a risk factor when considered alone [29]. However, their study also indicated that this association lost significance after adjusting for confounding factors such as race, diabetes, and smoking [29]. Conversely, another cohort study in Taiwan found a positive association between higher maternal age and an increased risk of congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract in offspring [24]. Importantly, our current surveillance system does not capture data on potential confounders and known maternal risk factors for renal agenesis, including maternal diabetes, obesity, smoking, alcohol exposure, environmental chemicals, and certain maternal drugs [10, 12, 30]. This limitation may affect our ability to definitively establish the independent effect of maternal age.

Our analysis also revealed differences in RA prevalence by infant sex. We found a lower prevalence of URA in male infants compared to female infants, consistent with findings from the Korea National Health Insurance Service [22]. While most case series have reported a male predominance in URA, a large meta-analysis of over 15 million RA cases globally found no statistically significant sex difference [30]. In contrast to URA, our study indicated a higher BRA prevalence in males, which aligns with the findings of other studies [31]. Potential biological mechanisms for a higher susceptibility in males may involve the earlier differentiation of the Wolffian duct, crucial for the development of both the male urinary and reproductive systems [29]. The Wolffian duct is also implicated in inducing the growth of the ureteric bud; thus, abnormalities in this structure could impact ureteric bud development and potentially lead to renal agenesis [31, 32]. However, given the inconsistent findings on sex differences in large population-based studies, further research is needed to clarify this association.

We observed significant urban-rural and geographic disparities in RA prevalence. Offspring of mothers residing in urban and eastern/central regions had a lower risk of all RA types compared to those in rural and western regions. This observation may be linked to the relatively poor economic conditions and health care accessibility in rural areas and the western region [33–35]. Moreover, regions with higher levels of economic development possess more advanced diagnostic capabilities. This regional disparity further supports the earlier discussion regarding the potential for increased detection with advancements in screening and diagnostic techniques. The observed associations between these socio-demographic factors and congenital renal agenesis likely reflect complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors, necessitating future pathological and analytical epidemiological researches to clearly illuminate these disparities.

Our results demonstrate that the vast majority of RA cases were diagnosed prenatally. The prenatal detection rate for URA (85.57%) in our study was lower than the 95.8% reported by Laurichesse et al. [26], but higher than the 72% by Chi Le-Ha et al. [36], and the 62% observed in a study across 12 European countries [37]. The prenatal detection rate of BRA in our study (98.72%) was notably higher than the 74% reported by Chi Le-Ha [36] and comparable to the 91% reported in the European multi-country study [37]. The higher prenatal detection rate for BRA compared to URA is widely attributed to its characteristic ultrasonographic features, which often manifest as severe oligohydramnios or anhydramnios. An empty renal fossa in cases of URA can be challenging to identify, potentially being missed due to a prominent adrenal gland or misdiagnosed as renal aplasia or dysplasia. In contrast, URA often presents with normal amniotic fluid volume as a result of compensatory function of the contralateral kidney. Furthermore, an empty renal fossa in cases of URA can be challenging to identify, potentially being missed due to prominent adrenal gland or misdiagnosed as renal aplasia/dysplasia [38].

The prognoses of URA and BRA differs significantly. URA typically carries a favorable prognosis with normal amniotic fluid, though long-term risks such as renal dysfunction and hypertension can occur [39, 40]. In contrast, BRA is associated with a nearly 100% neonatal mortality rate without prenatal intervention, primarily due to severe oligohydramnios leading to pulmonary hypoplasia, which is generally incompatible with survival [6]. In our study, the rate of TOPFA was approximately 60% for URA cases, which was higher than the 15% reported by Laurichesse [26], the 3% by Wiesel, A [37]., and the 28% by Chi Le-Ha [36]. In contrast, the proportion of TOPFA in BRA cases reached 94%, exceeding rates reported in other studies [36, 37]. In China, the decision for TOPFA is primarily the pregnant woman’s and is legally permitted. This decision is influenced by a complex interplay of individual choices and circumstances. Among our live-born infants, the early neonatal mortality rate (within the first 7 days) was 1.8% for URA cases, while it was 71% for BRA cases. While BRA is typically fatal, a non-randomized controlled trial has suggested that serial amnioinfusions may mitigate the lethal effects of pulmonary hypoplasia. In this study, 82% of live-born infants survived for at least 14 days and underwent dialysis access placement [41]. Therefore, early diagnosis of BRA is crucial for facilitating comprehensive prenatal counseling, enabling patients to make informed decisions regarding either safe pregnancy termination or the consideration of interventions like serial amnioinfusion, which may potentially improve survival outcomes [6].

In our study, a notable proportion of URA (31.14%) and BRA (40.77%) cases presented with additional anomalies. The proportion of non-isolated URA was lower than the 45% reported by Osnat Zmora et al. [42], though that study had a relative small sample size. While specific data on the exact proportion of BRA associated with other congenital anomalies is limited in the literature, a large study analyzing over 625,000 births documented 92 cases of BRA with multisystem anomalies, indicating a high frequency of associated malformations [25]. Overall, the spectrum of associated anomalies appeared relatively similar for URA and BRA, with both commonly linked to congenital cardiovascular anomalies [38, 43]. However, URA was more frequently linked to urinary anomalies, whereas BRA more commonly involved the musculoskeletal system, aligning with prior observations reported by Wilson et al. [25]. Notably, no BRA cases with chromosomal abnormalities were detected in our research. However, other studies have reported associations between BRA and conditions such as 47, XXY [44], 47, XXX [45], and partial trisomy 13 and partial trisomy 16 [46]. This discrepancy in our findings may be partially attributed to the fact that many pregnant women in our study population may have opted against further genetic testing following the confirmation of BRA via ultrasound.

The primary strength of this study stems from its utilization of comprehensive and robust data from the CBDMN spanning the period from 2007 to 2020. This provides, for the first time, a reliable national estimate of RA prevalence across diverse socio-demographic characteristics within China. The CBDMN demonstrates that its perinatal data - including urban-rural distribution, sex ratio, and proportion of advanced maternal age - shows good comparability with national statistics. In 2020, urban perinatal infants in our study comprised 59.14% of births, closely aligning with the 60.84% reported in the 2020 China Population Census Yearbook [47]. Similarly, the male-to-female ratio (1.11:1) in our 2020 perinatal data matched the national birth sex ratio, and the proportion of births to advanced-age mothers (≥ 35 years, 14.42%) was consistent with the 2019 national obstetric data (12.12%) [48]. Also, the CBDMN employs standardized procedures for case ascertainment, data collection, and quality control, overseen by well-trained experts, which contributes to data quality and reduces potential for misdiagnosis. However, this study has certain limitations. One potential limitation is the possibility of referral bias inherent in hospital-based samples. Nevertheless, the high rate of hospital deliveries in China (approximately 99%) may partially mitigate this bias. The associations observed in our study might be biased by unmeasured confounding factors. Future research with comprehensive data collection and rigorous epidemiological design is necessary to further explore RA risk factors and validate our findings. Moreover, regional variations in diagnostic capabilities could lead to the misclassification of other congenital renal anomalies, such as renal hypoplasia, ectopic kidney, or fused kidney, as unilateral renal agenesis.

This study provides valuable insights into the evolving epidemiology of all types of renal agenesis in China over a 14-year period. Our analysis reveals a notable upward trend in RA prevalence, with distinct pregnancy outcomes for unilateral and bilateral cases. These findings emphasize the need for improved prenatal diagnosis, family support, effective intervention, and comprehensive care.
