This transtracheal technique for cricopharyngeal botulinum toxin injection is a 
safe and effective option for chemodenervation of this muscle in the awake 
patient. Its advantages include reduced risk of toxin spread to intrinsic 
laryngeal muscles, the ability to administer higher doses of botulinum toxin, 
improved access to the entire length of the cricopharyngeus, and avoidance of 
general anesthesia.

Cricopharyngeal botulinum toxin injection (CPBTI) has existed for many years as a treatment for hyperfunction of the cricopharyngeus (CP), usually manifesting in the form of symptomatic dysphagia [1]. Given its location deep within the neck, adjacent to many intrinsic muscles of the larynx, this injection is most often performed under per‐oral endoscopic visualization in the operating room.

To obviate the need for general anesthesia, various techniques have been developed for performing these injections in the awake patient using local anesthesia and electromyographic (EMG) guidance. The main drawback to each of these techniques is the proximity with which the needle must pass to the intrinsic laryngeal musculature, where inadvertent deposition or migration of botulinum toxin (BTX) can lead to potentially significant dyspnea, dysphonia, or dysphagia. For this reason, unilateral injection with a smaller dose is often preferred with this approach [2], which may amount to insufficient treatment in some patients.

With the recent increase in patients presenting with retrograde cricopharyngeus dysfunction (RCPD), many patients are now seeking an awake, in‐office option for CPBTI that maximizes their chances for a successful result. The transtracheal technique presented here was developed as a means of delivering high doses of BTX to the CP muscle across its entire length with minimal risk of spreading to the posterior cricoarytenoids (PCA) and other intrinsic laryngeal muscles.

The steps of the procedure are reviewed with the patient before beginning. This includes the various steps of anesthetic administration which occupy most of the total time needed for the procedure. From initial counseling to procedure completion, the entire experience should last less than 15 min.

With the patient in a seated position, the nose is sprayed with topical anesthetic and decongestant. The anterior neck is then cleaned before injecting local anesthetic into the skin and subcutaneous tissue overlying both the cricothyroid membrane and the anterior aspect of the superior trachea, 1–2 cm inferior to the cricothyroid membrane. Use of lidocaine with epinephrine is preferred to minimize bleeding when entering the airway.

Topical anesthesia for the larynx, pharynx, and trachea is then administered via intratracheal injection through the previously anesthetized area overlying the cricothyroid membrane. 2–3 mL of 4% lidocaine is administered in a series of brief pulses, allowing the patient to cough and distribute the medication throughout the upper aerodigestive tract.

A 1 mL syringe is prefilled with the total dosage of BTX to be delivered at a concentration of 10 units/0.1 mL. For RCPD patients in our practice, this dose is generally 90 units, allowing for an even split of 30 units across three sites in the muscle. However, this dose can be tailored based on physician preference or the specific type of pathology being treated.

A 3 in., 22‐Gauge monopolar electrode needle (Natus, Middleton, WI, USA) is then attached to the syringe and EMG machine. Of note, the needle must be bent with a broad distal curve of about 70 degrees to access the CP. This is generally accomplished with 3–4 gentle bends in close succession starting about 4 cm beyond the hub (Figure 1). The lower gauge of the needle helps ensure structural rigidity during placement and the 3‐in. length helps ensure adequate length to reach the muscle from below.

Three inch, 22‐Gauge monopolar electrode needle (Natus, Middleton, WI, USA). Note the broad curve in the needle (about 70°) that is required to rotate underneath the cricoid cartilage and achieve placement within the cricopharyngeus muscle.

Surface electrodes are attached to the patient in the usual fashion for EMG‐guided laryngeal BTX injection. A flexible laryngoscope is then passed by an assistant through the nose and into the subglottis, which additionally ensures adequate anesthesia from laryngeal mucosal contact along the way.

With the laryngoscope situated just past the glottis, the needle is inserted through the previously anesthetized area 1–2 cm inferior to the cricothyroid membrane. Care is taken to visualize the needle entering the lumen of the trachea at midline, usually above or below the second tracheal ring. Entering much lower than this may make it difficult to reach the CP. The needle is then fully inserted past the bend to allow for full maneuverability of the tip within the airway. The assistant operating the laryngoscope may need to slightly withdraw the scope during this maneuver to avoid any unintended damage to the scope itself from the needle, particularly in the case of an unanticipated swallow where the needle is elevated with the trachea directly toward the camera.

With the needle fully advanced into the airway, the tip is directed towards the midline posterior tracheal wall just below the inferior border of the posterior cricoid plate (Figure 2). The needle is then advanced in a posterior‐superior direction towards the CP. The baseline, rhythmic signal of the tonically constricted CP muscle on EMG helps confirm position. With the needle in place, the patient is asked to sniff to ensure that no PCA activity is appreciated on EMG. The patient is then asked to swallow. The cricopharyngeal signal should drop out during the swallow before returning with a brief burst of enhanced activity that rapidly transitions to the original baseline level of tonic activity (Figure 3). A third of the total BTX dose is then injected before withdrawing the needle back into the airway. The same steps are repeated for both the right and left sides of the muscle, entering the lateral aspect of the posterior tracheal wall just medial to the junction with the posterior aspect of the first tracheal ring for each side.

Flexible endoscopic view of the subglottis. Blue arrows delineate the inferior border of the cricoid cartilage. The injection needle is seen passing through the posterior tracheal wall just below this.

Characteristic EMG tracing for the cricopharyngeus muscle. Baseline tonic contractile activity is seen at rest which drops out during swallowing. This drop is followed by a rapid burst of increased activity that quickly fades back to baseline levels. The absence of any increase in activity while sniffing helps confirm that the needle is not within the posterior cricoarytenoid muscle of the larynx.

NSAIDs and/or acetaminophen are recommended as needed for any post‐procedure discomfort. While the potential for spread to the adjacent PCA muscles is substantially reduced with this technique, the risk is not absent. The patient is counseled on dyspnea‐specific precautions that should prompt urgent medical evaluation following their procedure.

Ten patients with a clinical diagnosis of RCPD were treated with in‐office transtracheal CPBTI. Average patient age was 34.2 (range 20–56) and 7 out of 10 (70%) were female. Two patients were only able to complete 1 of the 3 injection sites (midline only) due to anxiety and discomfort with the injection process, though both patients still reported complete symptomatic relief at the initial follow‐up 6 weeks later. These two patients, along with four others, were repeat injection cases after previously successful, operative transoral injections whose effect had worn off within 3 months.

Regarding dosing, the two patients who only tolerated midline injections received a total of 30 units each. One patient received a total dose of 50 units as he was uninsured and requested a lower dose for financial reasons. This was divided into a 20‐unit aliquot and two 15‐unit aliquots for the midline and lateral injections, respectively. The remaining seven patients received a total of 90 units that were evenly split into 30‐unit aliquots across all three injection sites. All patients reported the ability to belch with significant relief of their presenting symptoms at initial follow‐up. Long‐term follow‐up data (≥ 6 months after treatment) were only available for 5 patients at the time this manuscript was prepared, with two patients noting a decrease in symptom relief that warranted repeat intervention. One of these patients had a complete response that faded after 3 months. The other patient was the individual who received 50 units, who only achieved partial symptom relief after their first injection. A repeat injection with 90 units was performed which resulted in a complete response at initial follow‐up.

Postinjection dysphagia and intermittent reflux/regurgitation were noted by all patients. However, these were reported to be mild and easily managed, lasting no more than a few weeks without additional intervention. One patient reported transient dyspnea on exertion with noisy breathing, as well as mild dysphonia with some dyspnea while speaking. This started 2 days after treatment and subsided to a minimal level after 1 week without intervention. No other patients reported any form of dyspnea or dysphonia following their procedure.

While multiple strategies exist for obtaining adequate laryngotracheal anesthesia, the main advantage of the intratracheal lidocaine injection for this procedure is that some of the liquid lidocaine will be coughed into the pharynx and swallowed, helping to anesthetize the mucosa overlying the CP that the injection needle must traverse. Furthermore, this method allows for the use of a standard flexible laryngoscope to complete the procedure without requiring a channeled endoscope. A ‘drip’ or ‘laryngeal gargle’ method through a channeled endoscope would likely also achieve a similar degree of anesthesia for all necessary areas, though the larger scope may be challenging for some patients with tight nasal passages, and more labor‐intensive endoscope reprocessing would also be required.

An important element in this process is the “verbal anesthesia” that is provided concurrently, particularly with RCPD patients who frequently have co‐morbid anxiety [3]. Patients should be told before administration, and again after administration, that it may feel as if they are unable to swallow or that their breathing is “tighter” than before. Actively reminding the patient that these sensations are merely a temporary sensory trick can greatly help to put the anxious patient at ease.

As a final consideration, especially for those patients who show signs of anxiety at their initial pre‐procedure visit, a 5 mg dose of oral diazepam both the night before and 2–3 h before the procedure can significantly help with patient comfort throughout their experience. This was not done for the two patients in our cohort who were unable to complete all three injections, and in hindsight, may have improved their experience enough to do so.

The concentration of BTX should be kept high during reconstitution (10 units/0.1 mL for onabotulinumtoxinA) so that the total volume of injectate is as low as possible. This helps minimize the spread of the toxin to the adjacent pharyngeal constrictors or esophageal musculature, which can potentially worsen the dysphagia that is expected as a transient side effect following CPBTI.

The specific dose to be injected can be tailored based on physician preference or the type of pathology being treated. In contrast to standard practices for patients with cricopharyngeal dysphagia, where smaller doses are typically preferred, the majority of currently available RCPD literature focuses on much higher doses ranging from 50 to 100 units [4]. While no studies currently exist comparing specific doses, there is a trend in the RCPD literature towards higher doses in an effort to maximize chances for a successful therapeutic outcome. Our practice has chosen 90 units as a standard dose for RCPD patients primarily to ensure that the injected dose is as successful as possible. The choice of 90 units additionally makes the quantity easily divisible into 30‐unit aliquots for each of the three injection sites.

The decision to inject three sites along the midline and bilateral aspects of the CP is based upon anatomic studies on the distribution of motor end plates in this muscle. While the central aspect of the muscle contains a random distribution of small and scattered motor end plates, the lateral aspects of the muscle's oblique fibers generally contain much more densely populated and vertically oriented bands of motor end plates that lie 1 cm distal from the insertion site of the muscle on each side [5]. By injecting near these lateral bands in addition to the central belly of the muscle, a more complete chemodenervation is likely achieved.

When advancing through the posterior tracheal wall, the needle should pass readily, and any immediate resistance beyond the mucosa would suggest a position too high within the cricoid cartilage itself. Reinserting the needle from a lower entry point should help address this issue. Once the tip of the needle has entered the CP, a characteristic EMG signal will be heard, though it may be faint. Advancing the needle even further from this point should place the tip deeper within the belly of the muscle, making the signal louder and clearer. At the same time, care should also be taken to avoid advancing too far posteriorly, as disruption of the prevertebral tissues and/or vertebral bodies can lead to unwanted post‐procedure pain and increased risk of infection.

In‐office CPBTI is often considered for cost reasons since it is less expensive than a traditional transoral injection under general anesthesia. It is also often preferred by patients seeking a repeat injection after failing to get a complete or durable response from a prior injection. A waiting period of at least 6 months between injections is advised, as the residual effect of BTX at these high doses can greatly impair EMG signal interpretation before this time, even when no perceptible therapeutic effect of the medication is felt by the patient.

The transtracheal approach is both a feasible and safe option for CPBTI in the awake patient. The primary advantages of this method are the increased distance from the PCA muscles during injection and the ability to inject larger doses of BTX across a greater span of the muscle. While the procedure is both quick and technically straightforward, patient selection and pre‐procedure preparation are essential to ensuring an ideal experience.
