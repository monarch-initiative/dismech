INTRODUCTION: Relapsing polychondritis is a rare, systemic autoimmune condition 
characterized by recurrent inflammation of cartilaginous tissues. Its symptoms 
are often nonspecific and can lead to misdiagnosis or delayed treatment. This 
case report emphasizes the need for clinicians to recognize the potential for 
relapsing polychondritis in patients presenting with recurrent episodes of 
cartilaginous inflammation and sudden hearing loss, as early intervention is 
crucial in preventing irreversible damage.
CASE PRESENTATION: The patient, a 24-year-old Ethiopian male, presented with a 
history of recurrent ocular symptoms, including red eyes and nasal issues such 
as bloody tinged discharge and septal perforation. Accompanying symptoms 
included a dry, intermittent cough, shallow breathing, and chest pain. Each 
episode was managed effectively with a short course of oral or topical 
corticosteroids, alongside antihistamines and antibiotics. Despite these 
interventions, the patient later developed bilateral aural perichondritis, 
followed by a sudden onset of severe bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. A 
comprehensive clinical evaluation and laboratory investigations were conducted 
to confirm the diagnosis of relapsing polychondritis.
CONCLUSION: This case underscores the critical importance of considering 
relapsing polychondritis in patients with recurrent cartilaginous inflammation 
and sudden hearing loss. The patient's delayed diagnosis led to permanent 
hearing impairment, highlighting the necessity for heightened awareness and 
prompt treatment to mitigate the potential for lasting consequences. Early 
recognition and management of relapsing polychondritis can significantly improve 
patient outcomes and quality of life.

Relapsing polychondritis is an uncommon connective tissue disease. This severe, episodic, and progressive inflammatory illness affects cartilaginous and proteoglycan-rich tissues, including the nose, ears, larynx, trachea, blood vessels, heart, cornea, sclera, kidney, and joints [1, 2]. Pearson et al. provided the current definition of relapsing polychondritis in 1960, after the first case was recorded in 1923 under the term “polychondropathia” [2, 3]. Auricular chondritis and polyarthritis lead to RP in more than 80% of patients; however, several organs may be affected. The onset of this condition is typically gradual, with an abrupt painful inflammatory crisis followed by a temporary remission. This may make early diagnosis challenging, resulting in therapy delays and an increased risk of permanent or life-threatening consequences. Patients with RP may experience hearing and vision difficulties, as well as pulmonary complications (30–50%) [4, 5]. The eyeball and ocular adnexa are important target tissues for RP-induced inflammation, and a wide range of ocular manifestations are observed in patients with RP [6]. This case is distinctive owing to the initial presentation mimicking common allergic conditions, the sequential development of multi-site cartilaginous inflammation culminating in bilateral sudden deafness, and the diagnostic journey that ruled out other granulomatous diseases in a young male—a demographic less typical for RP.

After 1 month, he developed hyperemia, swelling, and a painful, tender right pinna that spared the earlobe, alongside chest pain with shallow breathing. He was diagnosed with right ear perichondritis with an upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) and treated with ciprofloxacin and prednisolone by his general practitioner, which improved his condition.

After another month, he reported occasional chest pain and a dry, intermittent cough. Physical examination revealed a tender chest wall with normal breath sounds. His ears were normal, but an anterior septal perforation with crusting was observed. Given these findings, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) was suspected. However, cytoplasmic antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (cANCA) were negative. A punch biopsy of the nasal septum revealed inflammatory infiltration with varying numbers of T-lymphocytes (primarily CD4+ T cells), macrophages, plasma cells, and immunological deposits, but no evidence of granulomas or vasculitis, arguing against GPA.

At 2 weeks following this, the patient developed painful, tender, swollen, and erythematous auricles bilaterally (Fig. 1), accompanied by the acute onset of bilateral hearing loss and tinnitus. A diagnosis of relapsing polychondritis with septal perforation and bilateral sudden sensorineural hearing loss was made. The patient met the McAdam diagnostic criteria, fulfilling four features: (1) bilateral auricular chondritis, (2) nasal chondritis with septal perforation, (3) ocular inflammation (conjunctivitis), and (4) audiovestibular damage (sensorineural hearing loss). He also met the Damiani and Levine criteria by having two McAdam features (auricular and nasal chondritis) with a positive histologic finding from the nasal septum and a positive response to corticosteroid therapy.

He was started on intravenous methylprednisolone 250 mg daily for 3 days, followed by oral prednisolone 50 mg daily, which was tapered over 6 months and then discontinued. His auricular swelling and pain subsided promptly. Follow-up audiometry performed 3 months after initiating therapy showed significant improvement, with residual mild bilateral sensorineural hearing loss (Figs. 3, 4). Laboratory investigations at diagnosis revealed leukocytosis (15.2 × 109/L; normal range: 4–11 × 109/L) and a markedly elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 116 mm/h (normal: < 15 mm/h). Rheumatoid factor (RF) and antinuclear antibody (ANA) were positive. Complement C3 was low at 26 mg/dL (normal range 90–180 mg/dL). cANCA, serology for syphilis (VDRL/TPHA), and tuberculin tests were negative. Urinalysis and renal function tests were normal. Chest computed tomography (CT) scan and echocardiography showed no evidence of tracheobronchial or cardiac involvement.

Relapsing polychondritis is a multisystemic illness characterized by relapses and progressive cartilage degradation. The average age at diagnosis ranges from 44 to 51 years, with a female-to-male ratio of 3:1 [3, 4]. Our case is notable for its occurrence in a young male. There is no specific laboratory test for RP, but elevated ESR and CRP levels are common. In 50% of instances, thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, and normochromic normocytic anemia occur [3, 7].

The diagnostic journey in this case involved careful differential diagnosis. The initial nasal and auricular symptoms raised suspicion for infectious perichondritis and granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA). GPA was systematically ruled out by negative cANCA serology and the absence of granulomatous vasculitis on nasal biopsy. The biopsy finding of nonspecific chronic inflammation with immunological deposits, while not pathognomonic, was consistent with RP in the appropriate clinical context [8]. The sequential involvement of multiple cartilaginous sites (ocular, nasal, auricular, costal) over a few months, culminating in bilateral sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL), was pivotal in steering the diagnosis towards RP.

Auricular chondritis is the most common clinical manifestation and was a key feature in our patient. Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) is a less frequent but well-documented complication, occurring in approximately 30% of patients [5, 8, 9]. The mechanism of hearing loss in RP remains uncertain but is thought to be immune-mediated. Autoantibodies against type II collagen—a major component of both articular and cochlear cartilage—may trigger an inflammatory response or apoptosis in the inner ear, particularly affecting the spiral ligament and modiolus [1, 3]. In our patient, the hearing loss was sudden, severe, symmetrical, and concurrent with active auricular chondritis, supporting this pathogenic link. The partial but incomplete recovery with high-dose corticosteroids is consistent with the variable auditory prognosis described in the literature, where delayed treatment often leads to permanent deficits [1, 9].

The management of RP revolves around immunosuppression to control inflammation and prevent damage. Systemic corticosteroids are the first-line therapy for acute flares and for hearing loss believed to be immune-mediated [1]. The positive, albeit partial, audiometric response in our patient underscores the importance of prompt initiation of high-dose steroids during an acute SSNHL episode. Earlier and potentially more aggressive immunosuppression at the onset of his cartilaginous symptoms might have prevented the progression to severe hearing loss, a point that highlights the critical need for early diagnosis.

This case illustrates that RP, though rare, should be a key consideration in patients presenting with recurrent or multi-site cartilaginous inflammation, especially when accompanied by acute auditory symptoms. The young age and male gender of our patient add to its distinctive nature. A high index of suspicion, systematic exclusion of mimics including GPA, and application of established diagnostic criteria (McAdam or Damiani-Levine) are essential for timely diagnosis. Early and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy is crucial to mitigate irreversible complications, such as permanent hearing loss, and to improve long-term prognosis.
