Hypomyelinating leukodystrophies (HLDs) are a heterogeneous group of inherited white matter disorders characterized by a predominant absence of myelin deposits in the central nervous system (CNS)1. The disorder is caused by alterations in the PYCR2 gene (OMIM *616406), which encodes the mitochondrial enzyme, pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase 2, which converts pyrroline-5-carboxylate to proline. Proline is an essential amino acid for the CNS and connective tissue function2.

These diseases are rare, affecting one out of every 250 000 to 500 000 people. HLD1, also known as Pelizaeus-Merzbacher disease, is the prototype of all HLDs3. The gene responsible for HLD1 encodes proteolipid protein 1. Proteolipid protein 1 is the major myelin structural protein produced by oligodendrocytes in the CNS 4,5. Most patients with severe hypomyelination present in infancy or early childhood with severe neurologic deficits, but the clinical presentation may also be milder, with the onset of symptoms in adolescence or adulthood6.

Over the last 10 years, comprehensive genetic testing such as whole exome sequencing (WES) or whole genome sequencing has helped to identify novel genes associated with HLDs and reduce the number of unsolved cases7–9. To date, more than 20 genes have been linked to HLDs, which encode proteins involved in mRNA translation, heat-shock response, transcription factors, and proteins that are localized in the lysosomes and plasma membrane10. In this study, we report that a recurrent de novo mutation in the gene of PYCR2 also causes a hypomyelinating leukodystrophy type 10.

The patient was a one-year-old girl child, that living in a village near Gorgan (one of the cities of Iran). The first child of the family, born with normal vaginal delivery and full term (39 weeks, birth weight: 3700 g, WT: 4800, HT: 64 cm, HC: 40 cm), complete vaccination, in terms of growth and development, she also rolls over, cannot sit, and grabs objects with her hands. There was no history of hereditary, neurological, psychological, or congenital disease in the family members. The parents were relatives and cousins and the daughter’s cousins.

She at the age of 6 months was hospitalized due to loose, muscle weakness, and upward gaze for 7–8 min with complaints of fever and convulsions. It has had a postictal phase and diarrhea, but she did not have diarrhea or a cough. She has a history of hospitalization due to fever and pneumonia before the age of 6 months. Also, a history of severe reflux with barium swallow into the thoracic esophagus, there is no filling defect or dilatation in the esophagus in hospitalization. Finally, according to the symptoms and laboratory results (Table 1), provisional and differential diagnosis before a WES was sent was failure to thrive and simple febrile convulsions. The weight and height of the child at presentation was 6 kg and 62 cm, respectively.

According to Table 2, using the test of WES, a nonsense homozygous mutation was found in the PYCR2 gene, which causes the conversion of the amino acid arginine into a stop codon and creates an incomplete protein product. This mutation is based on InterVar, VarSome, and guidelines ACMG is pathogenic and pathogenic. A mutation in the PYCR2 gene causes hypomyelinating leukodystrophy type 10 disease. This syndrome has an autosomal recessive inheritance. It is characterized by symptoms of growth deficiency, progressive microcephaly, psychomotor problems, mental retardation, muscle weakness, and specific facial features. In these patients, the forehead is narrow, the upper lip is narrow, the philtrum is long, the ears are malformed with prominent helixes, and the nose is round and upturned. Considering that the symptoms of this disease are consistent with the symptoms of this one-year-old patient, this is the final diagnosis of hypomyelinating leukodystrophy type 10 disease. The child give the antiepileptic drugs and/or muscle relaxants.

This study reported that the patient having HLD10 and a mutation in the PYCR2 gene. The patient showed symptoms of growth deficiency, progressive microcephaly, psychomotor problems, mental retardation, muscle weakness, and specific facial features.

Although this condition has been classified as a hypomyelinating leukodystrophy, we found that the imaging features were variable, ranging from subtle white matter T2 hyperintensities to progressive brain atrophy. HLDs represent a genetically heterogeneous group of neurological disorders characterized by impaired myelin formation in the brain. To date, 11 different genetic forms of HLD have been described with mutations in genes involved in myelin formation and oligodendrocyte maturation11.

Nakayama et al., in 2015, described four children from two consanguineous families with microcephaly and HLD with PYCR2 mutations. This study demonstrates that PYCR2-deficient cells exhibit increased apoptosis under oxidative stress, and therefore, postnatal microcephaly in affected children is likely due to increased CNS cell death12–14.

The prognosis of myelinating leukodystrophy 10 appears to be poor, with most children dying within the first decade of life. Treatment is primarily supportive and requires a multidisciplinary team to address issues such as cramps, feeding difficulties, constipation, reduced mobility, nutritional deficiencies, and more related associated with this debilitating disease15. Genetic testing of parents should be recommended to determine if they are heterozygous carriers of the mutated gene and, if so, genetic counseling before the next pregnancy is needed to prevent a recurrence.

There is a wide range of etiologies for debilitating neurological disorders, which have common, overlapping clinical presentations. The causes of global developmental delay, microcephaly, upper motor neuron signs, and developmental delay may vary. A detailed history, comprehensive clinical examination, neuroimaging, and relevant laboratory tests help narrow the differential diagnosis. Advances in the field of genetics have increased awareness, and the increasing availability of genetic testing in small cities in developing countries are helping to better assess complex neurological disorders and establish a complete diagnosis. An accurate diagnosis helps to outline the disease course, treatment (if available), and prognosis to parents and plays an important role in planning for future pregnancies.
