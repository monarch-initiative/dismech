# Final Analysis of a Study of Etranacogene Dezaparvovec for Hemophilia B.
**Authors:** Pipe SW, Miesbach W, Recht M, Leebeek FWG, Key NS, Castaman G, Lattimore S, Coppens M, Le Quellec S, Mahajan V, Gill S, Drelich D, Monahan PE, HOPE-B Study Group Investigators
**Journal:** N Engl J Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1056/NEJMoa2514332](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2514332)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Prophylactic treatment for hemophilia B necessitates lifelong, regular intravenous factor IX infusions. Gene therapy offers the possibility of a single-dose treatment that produces durable endogenous factor IX expression and disease control. Etranacogene dezaparvovec comprises an adeno-associated virus serotype 5 (AAV5) vector and the highly active Padua factor IX variant. The primary analysis of this study showed that etranacogene dezaparvovec reduced annualized bleeding rates and adverse events were mainly of low-grade severity. Final data from 5 years of follow-up are now available.

METHODS: In this open-label, phase 3 study, after a lead-in period (≥6 months) of factor IX prophylaxis, we administered a single infusion of etranacogene dezaparvovec in men with hemophilia B (factor IX activity level, ≤2 IU per deciliter), regardless of preexisting AAV5 neutralizing antibodies. The prespecified 5-year analyses included adjusted annualized bleeding rates (difference between the post-treatment period of months 7 through 60 after gene therapy and the lead-in period), factor IX expression, and safety outcomes.

RESULTS: In the full analysis population (54 participants), the adjusted annualized bleeding rate for all bleeding events was 4.16 during the lead-in period and 1.52 during months 7 through 60 after gene therapy, a reduction of 63% (95% confidence interval, 24 to 82). During the 5-year follow-up period, endogenous factor IX expression remained stable; at 5 years, the mean (±SD) factor IX activity level was 36.1±15.7 IU per deciliter and the mean exogenous factor IX consumption for routine prophylaxis and treatment of bleeding events had decreased by 96%, from 257,339 IU per year during the lead-in period to 10,924 IU per year during months 7 through 60 after gene therapy. Efficacy did not differ substantially between participants with and those without AAV5 neutralizing antibodies at baseline. Adverse events that were possibly related to treatment were rare after month 6.

CONCLUSIONS: Sustained endogenous factor IX expression and low annualized bleeding rates over a 5-year period were observed after an infusion of etranacogene dezaparvovec. (Funded by uniQure and CSL Behring; HOPE-B ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT03569891.).

G
ene therapy affords opportuni -
ties to treat and possibly provide long-
term correction for an increasing range
of inherited disorders. For persons with hemo -
philia B, gene therapy offers the potential of a
single-dose treatment through delivery of the
functional gene encoding coagulation factor IX
(F9), which results in endogenous production of
factor IX in the liver. Such therapy replaces the
need for lifelong regular continuous administra-
tion of factor IX replacement, which is a burden-
some treatment approach, from both a patient and
economic perspective.1 Moreover, despite factor
IX prophylaxis, breakthrough bleeding events do
occur and are associated with complications, in-
cluding joint arthropathy.2
Small clinical studies of hemophilia B gene
therapy have shown durable factor IX expres -
sion.3 Long-term post-treatment data of a liver-
directed, self-complementary adeno-associated vi-
rus (AAV) gene therapy that systemically delivered
a wild-type factor IX expression cassette packaged
in an AAV serotype 8 vector were recently reported
in 10 participants with severe hemophilia B. The
mean factor IX activity level remained stable and
annualized bleeding rates remained substantially
reduced over a median follow-up of 13.0 years.3
Similar findings were reported for AMT-060, an
AAV serotype 5 (AAV5)–based factor IX wild-type
gene therapy over a period of 8 years. 4 These
reports of sustained long-term benefit are rele -
vant to and possibly predictive of the efficacy of
etranacogene dezaparvovec because the same fac-
tor IX expression cassette (including the same
regulatory elements and codon optimization) is
delivered through etranacogene dezaparvovec with
the use of an AAV5 vector to target hepatocytes;
the only difference is a two-nucleotide change
in the factor IX complementary DNA to direct the
expression of the highly active factor IX Padua
(R338L) variant.5
A phase 2b study6,7 of etranacogene dezapar-
vovec in 3 participants with severe or moder -
ately severe hemophilia B (factor IX activity level,
≤2 IU per deciliter) showed that substituting
wild-type factor IX with the factor IX Padua vari-
ant generated endogenous factor IX activity lev -
els of at least 5 IU per deciliter at 6 weeks
postinfusion (the primary efficacy end point).
The mean factor IX activity level was 40.8 IU per
deciliter at 1 year after gene therapy and was
sustained at 45.7 IU per deciliter at 5 years.6,7
On the basis of the initial phase 2b data, a
pivotal phase 3 study, Health Outcomes with Padua
Gene; Evaluation in Hemophilia B (HOPE-B), of
etranacogene dezaparvovec was conducted in 54
participants with severe or moderately severe
hemophilia B.5 Earlier data from preclinical and
clinical studies suggested that preexisting neu -
tralizing antibodies to the AAV5 vector capsid of
etranacogene dezaparvovec did not preclude he-
patocyte transduction. 6-8 Consequently, and in
contrast to the majority of other hemophilia gene
therapy trials, the HOPE-B study did not exclude
persons with preexisting AAV5 neutralizing anti-
bodies.5
The primary analysis of the HOPE-B study
showed that treatment with etranacogene deza -
parvovec, assessed during months 7 through 18,
was noninferior to standard care (factor IX pro-
phylaxis for ≥6 months) with regard to the pre-
specified primary efficacy end point (the annual-
ized bleeding rate), which supported the approval
of etranacogene dezaparvovec for the treatment
of hemophilia B. 5 Further observation showed
stability of endogenous factor IX activity levels.9
Here, we report the results of the final analysis
of the HOPE-B study after 5 years of follow-up.
Methods
Study Participants
Eligible participants were men (≥18 years of age)
with hemophilia B that was classified as severe
(plasma factor IX activity level, <1 IU per decili-
ter) or moderately severe (plasma factor IX activ-
ity level, 1 to ≤2 IU per deciliter). Participants had
to have been receiving stable factor IX prophy -
laxis for at least 2 months before screening and
continued to receive standard care with factor IX
prophylaxis for at least 6 months (the lead-in
period) before administration of etranacogene
dez aparvovec. Full eligibility criteria have been
reported previously, and key exclusion criteria are
provided in the Supplementary Appendix, avail -
able with the full text of this article at NEJM.org.5
AAV5 neutralizing antibody positivity was not an
exclusion criterion.
Study Design
In this phase 3, open-label, international study,
participants received a single intravenous dose of
etranacogene dezaparvovec (Hemgenix) at 2×1013
genome copies per kilogram of body weight. The

Statistical Analysis
The demographic and clinical characteristics of
the participants at baseline were summarized
descriptively; continuous variables were reported
as numbers of participants, means with standard
deviations, ranges, and medians with interquar-
tile ranges, and categorical variables were reported
as frequency and percentages. The number of re-
ported bleeding events was analyzed with the use
of a negative binomial regression model involv-
ing repeated-measures generalized estimating
equations; the model accounted for the paired de-
sign of the study with an offset variable to ac -
count for the differential collection periods (ad-
justed annualized bleeding rate). The 95%
confidence intervals were calculated with the
Wald method. The widths of the confidence in-
tervals have not been adjusted for multiplicity and
should not be used in place of hypothesis test -
ing. Details of the statistical methods have been
published previously5 and are available in the
Supplementary Appendix.
Results
Participants
The study began on June 27, 2018, and the final
database lock was on April 30, 2025. The mean
(±SD) lead-in period was 233.8±65.8 days. The
full analysis population comprised 54 participants;
53 participants received the full dose of etrana -
cogene dezaparvovec, and 1 received approximately
10% of the dose owing to an infusion-related
hypersensitivity reaction. A total of 50 partici -
pants (93%) completed 5 years of follow-up. Of
the 4 participants who did not complete the study,
2 died, 1 underwent liver transplantation, and 1
withdrew consent (Fig. S1 in the Supplementary
Appendix). The mean age of the participants at
baseline (screening) was 41.5±15.8 years, and
most participants (82%) had severe hemophilia;
more than half the participants had had viral
hepatitis (hepatitis B in 9 participants [17%] and
hepatitis C in 28 [52%]). Additional information
about the demographic characteristics of the par-
ticipants at baseline and the diversity of the study
population have been described previously.5 On
the day of infusion, before treatment with etra -
nacogene dezaparvovec, AAV5 neutralizing anti-
bodies were detected in 21 participants (39%), of
whom 1 had a titer of 3212.3. AAV5 total bind -
ing IgG antibodies were detected in 13 partici -
pants (24%).

Endogenous Factor IX Activity
After gene therapy, 52 participants (96%) had
endogenous transgene-derived factor IX expres-
sion and were considered to have had a response
to treatment; 80% of the participants had a fac-
tor IX activity level of at least 12 IU per deciliter

level remained stable (>30 IU per deciliter) dur-
ing years 1 through 5 after gene therapy (Fig. 2B);
the mean level at year 5 was 36.1±15.7 IU per
deciliter. Factor IX activity during years 1 through
5 was somewhat lower among participants with
AAV5 neutralizing antibodies than among those
without AAV5 neutralizing antibodies (Fig. S4B).
However, these data should be interpreted cau -
tiously, given that no inferential statistical testing
was conducted between the groups.

Sustained undiminished factor IX expression
in the HOPE-B study contrasts with the progres-
sive decrease or complete loss of factor VIII ex -
pression observed in several trials of gene therapy
for hemophilia A.21-23 Possible factors contributing
to differences in hepatocyte-targeted expression
of factor IX and the larger, more complex, and in-
trinsically immunogenic factor VIII in a heterolo-
gous cell type have been extensively reviewed.22,24-26
Transgene expression from recombinant AAV
vectors, which lack the DNA integrase activity of
the wild-type AAV gene rep , is primarily epi -
somal.22 However, an estimated 0.1 to 3.0% of
liver-targeted recombinant AAV vector may inte-
grate into hepatocyte DNA, which potentially
equates to many millions of liver-related integra-
tion events, given the dose of 2×1013 genome cop-
ies per kilogram.27 Since hepatocytes in the devel-
opmentally mature liver undergo slow but regular
turnover,28 the resulting dilution of episomal DNA
has conventionally been expected to result in pro-
gressive loss of disease correction. Instead, the
stable transgene expression that was observed in
the phase 2b and HOPE-B studies is consistent
with the persistent transgene expression report-
ed over the course of 8 years in the AMT-060
study29 and 13 years in the scAAV2/8-LP1-hFIXco
study.3
Mechanisms to explain stable correction may
include transgene expression primarily from the
small population of hepatocytes with integration,30
which could also possibly involve clonal expansion
of AAV-integrated hepatocytes,31 or persistent epi-
somal expression from nonproliferating hepato-
cytes. Results of a recent study28 suggest that only
a small percentage of stem-cell–like hepatocytes
drive liver regeneration, whereas most hepatocytes
remain quiescent. Moreover, diploid and polyploid
hepatocytes have different roles (liver regenera -
tion and protective functions, respectively) and
rates of turnover, with polyploid hepatocytes hav-
ing a slower turnover rate.32,33 Episomal transgene
persistence was observed in liver biopsy samples
2.6 through 4.1 years after administration of an
AAV5-based gene therapy in persons with hemo-
philia A, and from 8.2 to 12.0 years in dogs with
hemophilia A.34,35 These data suggest the durable
endogenous factor IX expression associated with
etranacogene dezaparvovec may be explained by
stable persistence of primarily episomal factor IX
expression. Elucidation of these hypotheses falls
outside the scope of the HOPE-B study.
Possible complications, including factor IX in-
hibitors and thrombotic events, were not observed.
Specifically, thrombotic microangiopathy, which
has complicated some studies that used high doses
of systemically administered non-AAV5 serotype
vectors,36 was not observed in this study. This
favorable safety profile is notable considering the
presence of AAV5 total binding IgG (which is
potentially capable of activating complement) in
24% of the treated participants and AAV5 neu -
tralizing antibodies in 39%.5
Liver-related safety concerns associated with
systemically delivered AAV gene therapy include
late (≥6 months after infusion) liver functional
deterioration and failure or subacute liver inflam-
mation (or both). No late liver dysfunction or per-
sistent elevations in liver aminotransferase levels
were observed after treatment with etranacogene
dezaparvovec in the phase 2b or HOPE-B studies.7,9
The incidence of elevations in alanine amino -
transferase levels above baseline within 12 weeks
after vector infusion was lower in the HOPE-B
study than previous trials of hemophilia gene
therapy vectors. 3,16-18,37,38 Elevations in alanine
aminotransferase levels were successfully managed
with the use of glucocorticoid treatment, and en-
dogenous factor IX activity remained at clinically
functional levels after alanine aminotransferase
levels normalized in most of the participants who
received glucocorticoids; one participant resumed
factor IX prophylaxis. However, the onset of el -
evated alanine aminotransferase levels in the
first 12 weeks after infusion was the factor that
contributed most consistently to variability in fac-
tor IX activity; therefore, it is important to moni-
tor liver aminotransferase levels closely to ensure
that glucocorticoids are started immediately if
elevations occur.

at very high doses in the neonatal period. 39 Al-

cogene dezaparvovec, a sustained clinical bene -
fit and durable endogenous factor IX expression,
with levels in the mild hemophilia or no hemo-

90% of the participants over a 5-year period. The
increased factor IX expression was associated with
reductions in annualized bleeding rates and exog-

fects was observed after the first 6 months.
