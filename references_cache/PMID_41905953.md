# Congenital aldosterone deficiency and its resistance.
**Authors:** Tajima T, Amano N, Ishii T, Otuski M, Katabami T, Kashimada K, Hasegawa T, Mukai T, Yoshida Y, Shibata H
**Journal:** Endocr J (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1507/endocrj.EJ25-0630](https://doi.org/10.1507/endocrj.EJ25-0630)

## Content

Aldosterone synthase deficiency (ASD), which is caused by a genetic defect in CYP11B2, involves a deficiency in aldosterone alone. Newborn and early childhood ASD patients can present with salt-wasting symptoms. In severe cases, this can lead to shock and be life-threatening. ASD must also be differentiated from another disease, pseudohypoaldosteronism type 1 (PHA1), which involves resistance to aldosterone. PHA1 can be classified into PHA1a, PHA1b, and secondary PHA1. PHA1a is caused by a heterozygous defect in NR3C2, which encodes the mineralocorticoid receptor. PHA1b is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by defects in the 3 epithelial sodium channel subunits α, β, and γ, encoded by SCNN1A, SCNN1B, and SCNN1G. Since ASD is a very rare disorder, the "The Intractable Adrenal Disorders Research by the Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare" developed the "Diagnostic criteria and severity classification for aldosterone synthase deficiency" to better understand the disorder. The criteria are as follows: Clinical symptoms: patient meets criteria 1 and 2. 1) Presents with salt-wasting symptoms (poor feeding, vomiting, dehydration, poor weight gain). 2) No skin pigmentation. Laboratory findings: patient meets criteria 1 through 3. 1) Low serum sodium and high serum potassium. 2) Low to normal plasma aldosterone and high plasma renin activity or high plasma active renin concentration. 3) No low blood cortisol level. Diagnoses of exclusion include PHA1, 21-hydroxylase deficiency, congenital lipoid adrenal hyperplasia, and congenital adrenal hypoplasia. We believe that the diagnostic criteria of ASD will enable clinicians and researchers to better understand congenital aldosterone deficiency.

Children with congenital adrenal insufficiency, including congenital adrenal hyperplasia and congenital adrenal hypoplasia, usually present with deficiencies in both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid secretion. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia is mainly caused by genetic abnormalities in steroidogenic enzymes. Aldosterone synthase deficiency (ASD) is not accompanied by glucocorticoid deficiency or adrenal hyperplasia, but can lead to severe, life-threatening symptoms in the neonatal period, such as vomiting, poor feeding, and dehydration, due to aldosterone deficiency [1, 2]. However, it can be difficult to differentiate ASD from other conditions that cause adrenal insufficiency in neonates and infants [3, 4]. Thus, “The Intractable Adrenal Disorders Research by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare” analyzed the current status of ASD in Japan and the relevant literature, and developed the “Diagnostic criteria and severity classification for aldosterone synthase deficiency” [5].

This review aims to explain the mechanism of sodium reabsorption in the kidney by aldosterone as well as the pathology and clinical symptoms of ASD and pseudohypoaldosteronism type I (PHA1), an important differential diagnosis that involves aldosterone resistance. Our goal is to raise awareness of diseases involving aldosterone dysfunction/resistance, which may lead to earlier diagnosis and treatment of ASD.

Aldosterone is the most important mineralocorticoid that is synthesized in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal gland. The aldosterone synthase CYP11B2, present exclusively in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex, possesses 11β-hydroxylase, 18-hydroxylase, and 18-oxidase activities, and performs the final 3 steps of aldosterone biosynthesis [1, 2]: 11β-hydroxylation of 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC) to synthesize corticosterone, further 18β-hydroxylation of corticosterone to synthesize 18-hydroxycorticosterone, and 18-oxidation of 18-hydroxycorticosterone to finally synthesize aldosterone (Fig. 1).

The primary function of aldosterone is to maintain intravascular volume through sodium reabsorption. Aldosterone exerts its renal effects on the aldosterone-sensitive distal nephron (ASDN) [6, 7]. The distal nephron is composed of the distal convoluted tubule (DCT), connecting tubule (CNT), and cortical collecting duct (CCD). The late DCT (DCT2), CNT, and CCD form the ASDN (Fig. 2). There are 2 cell types in these segments: principal cells (PC) and intercalated cells (IC) [6, 7]. Only PC cells in the DCT2 are involved in Na and K transport, while ICs predominantly regulate the acid-base balance [6, 7].

The distal tubular segments are shown, including the early portion of the distal convoluted tubule (DCT1), late portion (DCT2), connecting tubule (CNT), and cortical collecting duct (CCD). The DCT2, CNT, and CCD are aldosterone-sensitive.

Aldosterone sensitivity is also determined by the cellular expression of both the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) and 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11βHSD2) enzyme [8]. The MR has similarly high binding affinity for aldosterone and cortisol. 11βHSD2 protects the MR from cortisol activation by converting cortisol to cortisone, which has low binding affinity for the MR. Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption in the ASDN by regulating transcription and activity of the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC), as detailed in the next section. Aldosterone also regulates the expression of the renal-outer-medullary potassium channel, which contributes to serum potassium homeostasis [7, 9].

ENaC is widely expressed in organs throughout the body, including the kidney (DCT, CNT, and CCD), colon, lung (alveolar cells, airway epithelial cells), and skin (keratinocytes and sweat glands). It reabsorbs sodium and maintains sodium homeostasis [6, 7, 10, 11]. Since ENaC is specifically blocked by amiloride, it is also known as the amiloride-sensitive sodium channel. ENaC activity is primarily regulated by aldosterone, but insulin, vasopressin, and antidiuretic hormones also regulate ENaC expression [6, 7, 11]. Sodium itself provides negative feedback as well [11]. ENaC is composed of heterotrimers of α, β, and γ subunits [6, 7, 10, 11]. In tissues other than the kidney, some α subunits are replaced by δ subunits [10]. The α, β, and γ subunits share 30–40% amino acid identity, but each subunit consists of 2 short intracellular N- and C-termini, 2 transmembrane domains, and a large extracellular loop [11-13] (Fig. 3A). The transmembrane domain contains a Na+-sensing site [11]. The extracellular domains of the α and γ subunits contain sites for furin cleavage, and the conformational changes induced by this cleavage activate ENaC [11, 12]. The C-termini of the α, β, and γ subunits contain a proline-tyrosine rich motif named PY [12, 13] (Fig. 3A). This PY motif binds to neural precursor cell-expressed developmentally downregulated 4-2 (Nedd4-2) from the ubiquitin ligase family (Fig. 3B). This binding then leads to ubiquitination of the N-terminal lysines of the α and γ subunits, resulting in endocytosis and degradation (Fig. 3B) [12-18]. Missense variants or deletions of this PY motif increase the membrane expression of ENaC and cause Liddle syndrome, a genetic hypertension disorder [19].

Neural precursor cell-expressed developmentally downregulated 4-2 (Nedd4-2) consists of a calcium/lipid binding (C2) domain, 4 tryptophan-rich (WW) domains, and an E6-AP-C-terminal (HECT) domain, similar to human papillomavirus oncoprotein E6-associated protein (E6-AP).

The C2 domain is believed to regulate the function of proteins by translocating them to phospholipid membranes. The WW domains interact with the PY motifs of ENaC. Finally, the HECT domain transfers ubiquitin to a lysine residue of ENaC by binding Nedd4-2 to the PY motif of ENaC, resulting in degradation and endocytosis.

The regulation of ENaC by aldosterone is summarized in Fig. 4. Aldosterone rapidly increases the number of ENaCs on the apical membranes of ASDN cells, often within 2 hours. Aldosterone binds to the cytoplasmic MR and translocates into the nucleus. Although the α subunit has lower transcriptional activity than the β and γ subunits under steady-state conditions, where sodium absorption is not required, aldosterone induces a 2- to 4-fold increase in SCNN1A expression but not SCNN1B or SCNN1G expression [12, 17]. A similar phenomenon is observed in the lung, but this effect is mediated by glucocorticoids. This response is important for postnatal lung maturation and fluid clearance [17]. In contrast, in the colon, aldosterone induces a 10-fold increase in SCNN1B and SCNN1G mRNA expression but not SCNN1A mRNA expression. This difference in the tissue effects of aldosterone may be attributed to differences in the speed of cell renewal between the colon and kidney [17].

After binding to the aldosterone receptor, aldosterone translocates into the nucleus and promotes the transcription of SCCN1A, SGK1, and GILZ. SGK1 phosphorylates Nedd4-2, and phosphorylated Nedd4-2 no longer binds to ENaC, thereby inhibiting ENaC degradation. SGK1 also directly increases ENaC trafficking to the plasma membrane. Furthermore, SGK1 phosphorylates WNK4, inhibiting the proteolytic activity of WNK4 on ENaC. In the absence of aldosterone, SGK1 is transported to the ER and degraded (ERAD). Aldosterone upregulates GILZ, increasing its stability and inhibiting ERAD.

ENaC, epithelial sodium channel; SGK1, serum glucocorticoid-induced kinase 1; GILZ1, glucocorticoid-induced leucine zipper 1; WNK4, with-no-lysine kinase 4; Nedd4-2, neural precursor cell-expressed developmentally downregulated 4-2; ER, endoplasmic reticulum; ERAD, endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation.

Increased expression of SCNN1A alone cannot explain the aldosterone-induced increase in membrane surface expression of ENaC. Importantly, aldosterone binding to MR increases the expression of serum glucocorticoid-induced kinase 1 (SGK1) in the nucleus [6, 7, 12-16]. SGK1 phosphorylates Nedd4-2, inhibiting its binding to ENaC and its degradation, thereby increasing its membrane surface expression [6, 7, 12-16]. SGK1 also increases ENaC trafficking to the membrane surface, although the precise mechanism remains unclear [7]. Additionally, SGK1 phosphorylates with-no-lysine kinase 4, inhibiting its proteolytic activity on ENaC [11, 20]. SGK1 also contains a protein motif that targets it to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where it is degraded. However, in the presence of aldosterone, the expression of glucocorticoid-induced leucine zipper 1 is increased, which inhibits the movement of SGK1 to the ER. Thus, SGK1 continues to be expressed, and ENaC expression is maintained [21].

ASD is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by pathogenic variants in CYP11B2 [1, 2, 22]. Compared to other adrenal enzyme deficiencies, it is a rare disorder and its prevalence is unknown. ASD is commonly reported in the Iranian Jewish community, Mediterranean regions, and Turkey due to founder effects [1, 2, 23, 24]. Historically, ASD has been classified into ASD type 1 (ASD1) and ASD type 2 (ASD2) based on metabolite differences in the aldosterone synthesis pathway [1, 2]. Both ASD1 and ASD2 patients exhibit elevated blood DOC levels and increased urinary excretion of corticosterone metabolites. However, blood 18-hydroxycorticosterone and urinary 18-hydroxycorticosterone metabolites are decreased in ASD1, whereas these levels are elevated in ASD2. Blood aldosterone and urinary aldosterone metabolites cannot be measured in ASD1, but they are mildly reduced in ASD2 [1, 2]. It is speculated that ASD1 is caused by pathogenic variants that completely inactivate the enzyme, while ASD2 is caused by pathogenic variants that retain enzyme activity. However, correlations of genotype and phenotype do not always coincide [2, 3]. Furthermore, some cases show the steroid metabolite findings of ASD2 even with pathogenic variants that completely abolish enzyme activity [2]. Therefore, it is appropriate to consider ASD1 and ASD2 as a continuous spectrum. Although some reports have pointed to other genetic factors that may contribute to the differences between ASD1 and ASD2, no genetic abnormalities, other than those in CYP11B2, have been reported yet for ASD. Future whole-genome sequencing may identify pathogenic variants outside CYP11B2 exons.

To date, more than 50 abnormalities have been reported, including gene deletions, stop codons, frameshifts, amino acid substitutions, and intronic variants [2, 4, 22-64]. Homozygous p.Arg181Trp and p.Val368Ala variants have been frequently found in Iranian Jewish families [2, 22]. Another common variant in the southern Balkans is p.Thr181Ile [23, 35]. Additionally, p.Ile263Asn is a common finding in Turkey [24, 49].

Clinical symptoms of this disease occur in neonates and early infancy due to aldosterone insufficiency, poor feeding, and decreased vitality. In severe cases, shock can occur and may be life-threatening. Table 1 provides a summary of biochemical data for 83 subjects who were identified through a literature search [4, 22-64]. Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hyperreninemia, and hypoaldosteronism are commonly reported. The number of cases with elevated renin levels is low relative to the total number of cases due to the lack of available renin measurements in a substantial proportion of patients. There are no documented blood renin levels within the normal range, but some studies have reported normal blood aldosterone levels. Even in these cases, despite the presence of hyponatremia and elevated blood renin, aldosterone elevation should be considered to be insufficient.

A rare infantile case of ASD initially mimicked renal PHA1a, after presenting with hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, markedly elevated aldosterone, and hyperreninemia [53]. Although symptoms improved with salt supplementation, aldosterone and renin levels remained high. Three years later, the patient’s younger brother showed similar electrolyte abnormalities but with low aldosterone, prompting reconsideration of the ASD diagnosis. Reevaluation of the older brother also revealed low aldosterone. Both siblings were found to carry compound heterozygous CYP11B2 variants (p.Thr185Ile and 2802bpE1–E2del). Since neonatal PHA1a patients can exhibit extremely high aldosterone levels [65], it can be challenging to differentiate ASD from PHA1a in early infancy.

Two Japanese patients with ASD have also been reported. The first patient (a 3-month-old boy) carried CYP11B2 variants p.Trp56Ter and p.Arg384Ter. The second patient (a 26-day-old girl) carried p.Pro108Leu and p.Arg181Trp. The p.Arg181Trp variant is frequently found in Iranian Jewish patients with ASD. Although p.Pro108Leu is predicted to be pathogenic based on in silico analyses, loss of enzymatic function has not been evaluated in vitro. Therefore, according to ACMG/AMP criteria, p.Pro108Leu is classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). Both patients responded to fludrocortisone and NaCl, which was discontinued at one year of age. The first patient remains stable on fludrocortisone at age 12. In the second patient, discontinuation of fludrocortisone at age 6 resulted in a marked reduction in growth velocity, which improved after treatment was resumed (personal communication), underscoring the importance of continued mineralocorticoid replacement for normal growth.

Treatment for ASD consists of NaCl supplementation and fludrocortisone. NaCl can usually be discontinued after one year of age, particularly when the plasma renin activity or concentration is within the normal to low range. In contrast, fludrocortisone therapy is generally continued throughout childhood, since no biochemical or endocrinological markers are available to guide its discontinuation. However, fludrocortisone is often considered to be unnecessary in adulthood [1, 2].

This is because aldosterone receptor expression in the renal tubules is low during neonatal and infancy, resulting in an aldosterone-refractory state. However, expression increases with maturation, resulting in improved aldosterone sensitivity, increased renal tubular sodium absorption, and increased dietary sodium intake [66, 67]. As previously mentioned in the Japanese report, discontinuing fludrocortisone administration has reportedly led to growth retardation in children. In a family case report by Turan et al. [24], the proband was diagnosed with ASD after undergoing a detailed examination at 6 months old due to poor weight gain. The proband’s younger brother was also examined when he was 2 years old due to his short stature (–1.99 SD). Findings revealed normal serum sodium and potassium levels but elevated blood renin and low blood aldosterone levels, leading to a diagnosis of ASD. In this case, the growth rate improved after fludrocortisone administration. Another report described a one-month-old child diagnosed with ASD who was treated with fludrocortisone until one year of age, after which treatment was discontinued [63]. The child was reexamined when he was 7 years old, and had low weight (–5.28 SD) and short stature (–4.32 SD). Another study analyzed the growth and timing of treatment discontinuation in 16 patients with ASD [56]. Six patients discontinued treatment at an average age of 6 years. One patient experienced a relapse of low serum sodium (130 mEq/L) 3 months after treatment discontinuation due to gastroenteritis, leading to resumption of fludrocortisone administration. There is no information available about the growth of the 5 patients who discontinued treatment, and their treatment details are unknown. Given that inadequate mineralocorticoid replacement can lead to measurable growth impairment [67, 68], patients should continue fludrocortisone therapy until they have reached their adult height in order to ensure normal growth.

Notably, there was one case where the condition was diagnosed in adulthood [38]. The patient was a 47-year-old man who developed progressive fatigue after taking indapamide and laxatives. He was found to have hyperkalemia, hypoaldosteronism, and hyperreninemia, which was consistent with ASD, and his symptoms improved with fludrocortisone administration. Poor weight gain and vomiting during his childhood had resolved spontaneously. Genetic testing identified the CYP11B2 p.Arg143_144Leudup variant, and in vitro studies showed complete loss of enzymatic activity. Another adult case was reported where hypotension was observed during orthostatic testing after treatment discontinuation [43]. A study examining aldosterone levels after treatment discontinuation showed that the aldosterone levels and corticosterone/aldosterone ratio remained low compared to normal controls, while sodium and potassium levels were reportedly within the normal range in these cases as well [56]. This suggests that aldosterone synthesis does not recover, even if sodium levels are normal. Thus, if treatment is discontinued after childhood and the patient is asymptomatic, they should be informed about the condition, possible symptoms, and hereditary pattern during the transition period.

The nationwide survey was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jichi Medical University (permission number: 24-042). The first questionnaire was distributed to council members of the Japan Endocrine Society and Japanese Society for Pediatric Endocrinology in May 2024. A second questionnaire was subsequently sent to council members who had examined 2 ASD patients, and their clinical data were analyzed. In addition, we reviewed published reports on ASD since 1992, when the genetic cause of the disorder was first identified. The accumulation of these clinical data provided an opportunity to establish a working group within the Intractable Adrenal Disorders Research project of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, aimed at developing the “Diagnostic criteria and severity classification for aldosterone synthase deficiency” (Table 2) [5].

Clinical symptoms include: 1) Salt-wasting symptoms reflecting aldosterone deficiency (poor feeding, vomiting, dehydration, and poor weight gain); and 2) Absence of skin pigmentation so that the condition can be differentiated from other congenital enzyme deficiencies. Laboratory tests include: 1) Low serum sodium and high serum potassium; 2) Low to normal plasma aldosterone, high plasma renin activity or plasma active renin concentration; and 3) No low blood cortisol.

Excluded conditions are as follows: PHA1, 21-hydroxylase deficiency, congenital lipoid adrenal hyperplasia, and congenital adrenal hypoplasia. Symptoms for 21-hydroxylase deficiency, congenital lipoid hyperplasia, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia are similar to those of ASD. In females with 21-hydroxylase deficiency, differentiation of the disorders is possible based on external genital findings. Males with 21-hydroxylase deficiency, congenital lipoid adrenal hyperplasia, and congenital adrenal hypoplasia also exhibit cortisol deficiency, resulting in elevated adrenocorticotropic hormone levels and skin pigmentation. Differentiation from PHA1 is discussed in the next section. Since 18-hydroxycorticosterone measurements are not possible in clinical practice, the current diagnostic criteria do not classify ASD1 or ASD2. Severity classification follows the same criteria as other congenital adrenal enzyme deficiencies admitted as intractable diseases by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (Table 2).

PHA1 is a disorder characterized by organ insensitivity to aldosterone, and can be classified into 3 types [69, 70]. PHA1a is a renal form of the disorder exhibiting aldosterone insensitivity in only the kidneys. PHA1b is a systemic form of the disorder. The third type, secondary PHA1, is characterized by renal aldosterone insensitivity in neonates and infants, and is often accompanied by underlying urinary tract infections and/or congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) [69, 71].

PHA1a is caused by a heterozygous defect in NR3C2 encoding the MR and is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner [69, 70, 72]. To date, more than 100 pathogenic variants have been reported, including nonsense, frameshifts, missense, and microdeletions [70, 72-79]. There is no particular hotspot, and the disorder is caused by MR haploinsufficiency.

Clinical symptoms of PHA1a are similar to those of ASD, including poor feeding, poor weight gain, and dehydration in neonates and infants [69, 70]. Severe cases of shock have also been reported [75, 79]. There is no clear genotype-phenotype correlation, and onset and severity often vary within the same family [70, 76]. Endocrine testing typically reveals significantly elevated aldosterone and elevated serum renin levels. Treatment for PHA1a involves salt supplementation. Since renal aldosterone insensitivity improves with maturation, most patients no longer require salt supplementation after age 3. The disease remains asymptomatic thereafter, without growth retardation, and continues this way into adulthood [80-82], although elevated aldosterone and renin levels may persist. Walket et al. [81] investigated the effects of persistent aldosterone on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis in adulthood. Their report found increased early morning cortisol, increased urinary cortisol excretion, low HDL cholesterol, and increased central lipids. In a study by Escoubert et al. [82], echocardiography and cardiac MRI revealed no differences in left ventricular morphology or function compared with control groups. They also reported no differences in aortic stiffness. Furthermore, they analyzed the cause of death and found no differences compared with the general population. This suggests that cardiovascular complications caused by persistent aldosterone do not develop in MR haploinsufficiency but require instead the expression of both MR alleles. There are 2 distinct pathways for aldosterone: genomic and non-genomic action. Genomic action is accomplished via MR, an aldosterone or cortisol-dependent transcription factor, whereas non-genomic action is mediated by the putative G-protein-coupled receptor GPR30. In PHA1a, plasma renin and aldosterone levels are elevated due to MR gene mutations, but patients do not exhibit any significant cardiovascular disease or hypertension. The clinical significance of the non-genomic MR-independent action of aldosterone has not been established yet, so its role in cardiovascular function may be mainly mediated by classical MR.

PHA1b is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by genetic defects of SCNN1A, SCNN1B, and SCNN1G that encode the 3 ENaC subunits α, β, and γ, respectively [65, 69, 70, 83-92]. SCNN1A is located on chromosome 12p13.31, and SCNN1B and SCNN1G are located on chromosome 16p12.2. Pathogenic variants of SCCN1A are the most frequently reported.

As previously mentioned, ENaC is expressed in the DCT2, CNT, and CCD in the kidney, distal colon, salivary and sweat glands, and lung, so PHA1b has systemic effects. During the neonatal period, patients with severe cases present with dehydration, poor feeding, skin infections, dermatitis, and respiratory symptoms due to malabsorption of alveolar fluid. They often require repeated hospitalization for respiratory infections and electrolyte abnormalities [70, 83-92]. However, in some cases, salt supplementation alone (5–8 g/day) during infancy eliminated salt-wasting and respiratory symptoms [89, 90]. Such milder phenotypes are often associated with missense variants, but as with PHA1a, the genotype-phenotype correlation is not always clear.

The case of a Japanese patient with an SCNN1G defect has already been reported [91]. The patient experienced frequent hospitalizations due to salt-wasting symptoms during childhood despite salt supplementation [91]. However, his symptoms gradually improved, and he was able to discontinue salt supplementation by the age of 11 [92]. Researchers found increased expression of the thiazide-sensitive sodium chloride cotransporter in the patient’s urine, leading them to speculate that this compensation led to the alleviation of salt-wasting symptoms [92]. It is possible that the same compensatory mechanism may be occurring in other milder cases.

Secondary PHA1, like the renal form of the disorder, is characterized by renal aldosterone insensitivity. It commonly occurs in newborns and infants with underlying urinary tract infections and/or CAKUT [71, 93]. While the exact incidence is unknown, a report from Ireland estimated the incidence to be 1/13,200, with male predominance [94]. The etiology of secondary PHA1 may involve a combination of underlying aldosterone insensitivity due to immaturity of the renal tubules, amplified by paracytic damage caused by urinary tract infections or CAKUT, and inflammatory cytokines (transforming factors, tumor necrosis factor, interleukins 1 and 16) that downregulate aldosterone receptors, thus exacerbating the insensitivity [95, 96]. Symptoms are similar to those of PHA1a, with severe cases including shock and cardiac arrest due to hyperkalemia in the neonatal period [97, 98]. Symptoms can improve with salt supplementation and treatment of the urinary tract infection. Bowden et al. [79] reported a case of PHA1a that developed on the 14th day of life. The patient exhibited severe dehydration, hyperkalemia, urinary tract infection, and posterior urethral valve disease, and was initially diagnosed with secondary PHA1. However, elevated aldosterone levels persisted, and genetic analysis revealed a heterozygous deletion of exons 3 to 5 of NR3C2. Another case that was believed to have secondary PHA1 due to a urinary tract infection was not genetically diagnosed, but family analysis revealed elevated blood aldosterone and renin levels in the mother and 2 siblings, leading to a diagnosis of PHA1a [99]. Therefore, it is plausible that some cases of secondary PHA1 may have pathogenic variants in NR3C2.

In Japan, a study of PHA1 was conducted in infants within 30 days of birth [100]. Although genetic analysis was not performed for all cases, 3 patients had NR3C2 variants, 1 had an SCNN1B defect, 4 patients had associated renal complications, and 7 patients were unclassified. In particular, preterm infants present with symptoms early after birth, even without renal complications. This may be attributed to the immaturity of the renal tubules due to preterm birth and aldosterone insensitivity related to fetal stress [101].

Our manuscript reviews the mechanism of sodium reabsorption by aldosterone and provides a detailed overview of ASD and PHA1, which is important for the differential diagnosis for ASD. The diagnosis of isolated aldosterone deficiency in newborns and early childhood is challenging in actual clinical practice. Poor feeding and poor weight gain are nonspecific symptoms, which makes it difficult to immediately identify them as symptoms related to aldosterone deficiency. Furthermore, in emergency settings, administration of glucocorticoids for adrenal insufficiency may be prioritized over a diagnosis of isolated aldosterone deficiency. Clinicians should consider ASD as a possibility when diagnosing and treating these symptoms. We ultimately hope that the diagnostic criteria we have developed as well as this review will be helpful during clinical practice.

In Japan, where salt intake is high, it is thought that many patients remain asymptomatic without fludrocortisone supplementation after adulthood. However, the long-term prognosis in Japan is unclear. |

Notes on the application of diagnostic criteria and severity classification 1. Unless otherwise specified in the diagnostic criteria, clinical symptoms and test findings that are used to diagnose a disease may be used at any time point, as long as clinical symptoms indicating disease progression are present and can be confirmed. 2. Regarding severity classification after the start of treatment, the physician will determine the patient’s worst condition over the past 6 months while treatment is being carried out under appropriate medical management. 3. In addition, patients whose symptoms do not fall under a certain level of the above severity classification but who require continued expensive medical treatment are eligible for medical expense subsidies.
