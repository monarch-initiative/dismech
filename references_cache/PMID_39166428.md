# Recessive loss-of-function variants in DPH1 identified as the molecular cause in a sibling pair previously diagnosed with Fine-Lubinsky syndrome.
**Authors:** Waskow ER, Undiagnosed Diseases Network, Emrick LT, Rosenfeld JA, Ketkar S, Burrage LC, Scott DA
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.a.63845](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.a.63845)

## Content

Fine-Lubinsky syndrome is a rare clinically defined syndrome sometimes referred to as brachycephaly, deafness, cataract, microstomia, and impaired intellectual development syndrome. Here we provide a clinical and molecular update for a sibling pair diagnosed with Fine-Lubinsky syndrome. An extensive genetic work-up, including chromosomal microarray analysis and quad exome sequencing, was nondiagnostic. However, a research reanalysis of their exome sequencing data revealed that both were homozygous for an intronic c.749+39G>A [NM_001383.6] variant in DPH1. RNAseq analysis performed on RNA from fibroblasts revealed significantly reduced expression of DPH1 transcripts suggestive of abnormal splicing followed by nonsense mediated mRNA decay. Since the phenotypes of this sibling pair were consistent with those associated with the inheritance of biallelic pathogenic variants in DPH1, they were given a diagnosis of developmental delay with short stature, dysmorphic facial features, and sparse hair 1 (DEDSSH1). This leads us to recommend that all individuals with a clinical diagnosis of Fine-Lubinsky syndrome be screened for variants in DPH1. The clinical histories of this sibling pair emphasize that hearing loss associated with DEDSSH1 may remit over time and that individuals with DEDSSH1 should be monitored for the development of cardiomyopathy. This case also demonstrates the clinical utility of RNAseq as a means of functionally validating the effects of intronic variants that may affect splicing.

Fine-Lubinsky syndrome is an extremely rare clinically-defined genetic condition that is alternatively described as brachycephaly, deafness, cataract, microstomia, and impaired intellectual development (MIM# 601353) (Fine & Lubinsky, 1983). Corona-Rivera et al. suggested that the key diagnostic features of Fine-Lubinsky syndrome are non-synostosis brachycephaly or plagiocephaly, structural brain anomalies, abnormal EEG, intellectual disability, deafness, ocular abnormalities (cataracts or glaucoma), a high/wide forehead, shallow orbits, a flat/round face, low-set posteriorly rotated ears, microstomia, and body asymmetry (Corona-Rivera et al., 2009). Limb anomalies were also found to be common.

The majority of individuals used by Corona-Rivera et al. to define the features of Fine-Lubinsky syndrome represented sporadic cases born to asymptomatic parents (Corona-Rivera et al., 2009). Hence, it was hypothesized that Fine-Lubinsky syndrome was caused by de novo autosomal dominant variants in a single causative gene. The exception to the sporadic/autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of Fine-Lubinsky syndrome was a brother-sister sibling pair reported by Holder et al., which suggested the possibility of an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (Holder et al., 2007).

Here, we provide a clinical and molecular update for this sibling pair in which we demonstrate that they carry homozygous loss-of-function variants in DPH1, whose protein product functions in the posttranslational modification of a histidine residue in elongation factor-2 (EEF2) to diphthamide (Liu et al., 2004). They were subsequently given the diagnosis of developmental delay with short stature, dysmorphic facial features, and sparse hair 1 (DEDSSH1; MIM# 616901), an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental syndrome (Alazami et al., 2015; Loucks et al., 2015; Urreizti et al., 2020).

The parents and children were enrolled in the Baylor College of Medicine (BCM) Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN), and informed consent was obtained prior to initiation of any research studies. This research study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) at Baylor College of Medicine and the National Institutes of Health.

Quad exome sequencing was performed clinically at GeneDx. Briefly, DNA was enriched for the complete coding regions and splice site junctions for most genes of the human genome using a proprietary capture system developed by GeneDx for next-generation sequencing with CNV calling (NGS-CNV). The enriched targets were simultaneously sequenced with paired-end reads on an Illumina platform. Bi-directional sequence reads were assembled and aligned to reference sequences based on NCBI RefSeq transcripts and human genome build GRCh37/UCSC hg19. Using a custom-developed analysis tool (XomeAnalyzer), data were filtered and analyzed to identify sequence variants and most deletions and duplications involving three or more coding exons (Retterer et al., 2015).

Sequencing data were transferred to the BCM UDN site for research reanalysis. Variants were reviewed using Codified Genomics software. Biallelic coding and non-coding variants that were shared between the two affected siblings and with minor allele frequency < 0.01 in gnomAD and in our local BCM UDN database of > 1200 exomes and genomes were prioritized for further review (Chen et al., 2024).

The putative pathogenicity of variants was evaluated using Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD v1.7), a framework that integrates multiple annotations into one metric by contrasting variants that survived natural selection with simulated mutations (Schubach et al., 2024). Scaled CADD scores of 10, 20, and 30 correspond to a variant being predicted to be amongst the top 10%, 1%, and 0.1% of deleterious variants in the human genome, respectively.

Variants that were predicted to affect splicing were further analyzed by SpliceAI, a deep residual neural network that predicts whether each position in a pre-mRNA transcript is a splice donor, splice acceptor, or neither (Jaganathan et al., 2019). SpliceAI scores range from 0 to 1 and can be interpreted as the probability of the variant being splice-altering.

Allele frequencies were determined using the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) version 4.1.0. (Chen et al., 2024). This database catalogs variants detected on genetic testing from 807,162 individuals, including 730,947 exomes and 76,215 genomes.

A skin biopsy was collected from Subjects 2. Fibroblast cell lines were cultured using standard protocols. RNAseq was performed as described by Murdock et al. (Murdock et al., 2021). Briefly, RNA was quantified and processed using a stranded, polyA-tailed kit (Illumina) before being multiplexed and subjected to 150 bp paired-end sequencing at the Baylor College of Medicine (BCM) Laboratory for Translational Genomics. Approximately 30–50 million reads were generated per sample. Sequencing data were processed with a pipeline adapted from one developed by the GTEx Consortium in which fastq files were aligned to the GRCh37/ hg19 reference sequence using STAR-2.6.1b in 2-pass mode, and duplicates were marked with Picard (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard) (Consortium, 2013; Dobin et al., 2013). Gene expression was quantified using RSEM to generate transcript per million (TPM) values for expressed genes (Li & Dewey, 2011). Processed alignment files were then used as input for the outlier detection step in which we used the Detection of RNA Outlier Pipeline (DROP) with default, recommended settings for aberrant expression and splicing detection (Yepez et al., 2021). This workflow analyzes RNA-seq data sets to identify genes with aberrant expression levels using OUTRIDER and aberrant splicing using FRASER (Brechtmann et al., 2018; Mertes et al., 2021).

Quantitative visualization of RNA-Seq reads alignments from individual samples was accomplished by generating color coded Sashimi plots using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) (Katz et al., 2015; Robinson et al., 2011). Briefly, read densities across exons were quantified in reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) units and junction reads were plotted as arcs that are annotated with the raw number of junction reads present in each sample (Mortazavi et al., 2008).

When reported by Holder et al., Subject 1 was an 11-year-old African American male. Key features of his medical history included global developmental delay, postaxial polydactyly of the left hand, an occipital encephalocele surgically repaired at 3 months of age, bicoronal craniosynostosis requiring a craniotomy at 11 months of age, and a club foot treated with serial casting in early childhood. He had a history of dental caries, abscesses, and extractions, and early hearing loss suspected to be secondary to stenotic external auditory canals, which was treated with hearing aids until 9 years of age when reevaluation indicated that he had normal hearing.

Subject 1 is now 28 years old. He graduated from high school in special education classes and is currently in a day habilitation program where he takes life skills classes. He is able to sight read some words and is very sociable and communicative. At 26 years of age, he developed uncompensated congestive heart failure which required multiple hospitalizations. He had biatrial and biventricular dilation with an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 20%. He was persistently tachycardic and had difficulty tolerating guideline-directed medical therapy due to hypotension. He ultimately underwent dual chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placement. Several months after placement of his ICD, he had a single tonic-clonic seizure with brief loss of consciousness. Both a head CT and a brain MRI revealed postsurgical changes consistent with his prior surgeries without evidence of intracranial bleeding or other structural abnormality. An EEG showed mild asymmetry with decreased voltage in the left temporal region. He was placed on levetiracetam and has not had further seizure activity.

His most recent physical examination was at 27 years of age. His height was 162.5 cm (2nd centile, −2.0 SD), his weight was 69.7 kg (51st centile, +0.04 SD) and his head circumference was 50 cm (<1st centile, −3.4 SD). He has a narrow forehead with bitemporal narrowing, sparse hair, bilateral epicanthal folds, hypoplastic alai nasi, a thin upper lip, and widely spaced teeth (Fig. 1A-F, Fig. S1A,B). His fingers are small and tapered with hypermobile joints. He has contractures of his second fingers bilaterally. He has a single palmar crease on the left and normal palmar creases on the right. He has 1st toe clinodactyly and brachydactyly of his second to fifth toes bilaterally with the toes on his left foot being particularly short with the second toe overriding the first and third toes. He has dystrophic toenails. His most recent ophthalmologic examinations revealed alternating exotropia, right vertical strabismus, bilateral dry eye syndrome, bilateral myopia, irregular astigmatism, and mild bilateral optic nerve pallor. Although prescribed glasses, he refuses to wear them.

Subject 2 was a 9-month-old African American female when reported by Holder et al. She is the full biological sibling of Subject 1. Key features of her medical history included global developmental delay, a neurogenic bladder, and bilateral mild to moderate hearing loss that was described as sensorineural but was noted to be concomitant with auditory canal stenosis.

She is now 18 years old. She began walking independently at 3 years of age and said her first word between 3 and 4 years of age. She wore hearing aids until she was 8 years of age when hearing examinations demonstrated normal hearing function. She had several dental caries, delayed exfoliation of deciduous teeth, congenitally missing lateral incisors, left eye exotropia, and horizontal nystagmus. At age 15, she was diagnosed with epilepsy after having multiple seizures of different types including generalized tonic-clonic seizures, staring spells with vocalizations, and atonic seizures. Her epilepsy has since been classified as Lennox-Gastaut syndrome with EEG findings of multifocal and irregular generalized spike and sharp waves frequently seen as 2-2.5 Hz spike-wave complexes and generalized background slowing. Her brain MRI showed tonsillar ectopia. Her current anti-epileptic regimen consists of valproic acid, clobazam, and felbamate. Of note, her seizure burden has decreased from over 10 per day to 3 times per month after initiating treatment with felbamate.

Developmentally, she has required help to complete many activities of daily living. Neuropsychological evaluation performed at 16 years of age estimated her cognitive skills to be approximately that of a 2.5- to 3.5-year-old with social and adaptive skills up to an 8-year-old equivalent.

Following her brother’s onset of cardiac failure, she also underwent cardiac evaluation. An echocardiogram revealed an ejection fraction of 58% and cardiac MRI showed mildly reduced left ventricular systolic function with normal left ventricular size and structure. She has been started on lisinopril.

At 17 years, 10 months of age, her height was 140.9 cm (0.03rd centile, −3.4 SD), and her weight was 36.3 kg (<0.01st centile, −4.2 SD). At 16 years, 11 months of age, her head circumference was 50 cm (<1st centile, −4.0 SD). She has sparse lateral eyebrows, up-slanting palpebral fissures, a bulbous nasal tip, hypoplastic alae nasi, a short philtrum, widely spaced teeth, prognathism, and a prominent chin (Fig. 1G-L, Fig. S1C,D). Her ears are normally formed but are low-set, posteriorly rotated, and asymmetric with the right being longer than the left. Her fingers are tapered with hypermobile joints. She has a single palmar crease on the right and normal palmar creases on the left. She has first toe clinodactyly and brachydactyly of her second to fifth toes bilaterally with the fourth toe of the left foot being especially short and malformed. Her toenails are hyperconvex. She has been prescribed glasses but refuses to wear them.

A research-based reanalysis of Subjects 1 and Subjects 2’s exome sequencing data identified a homozygous intronic c.749+39G>A [NM_001383.6] variant in DPH1 in both siblings. Each parent was found to be heterozygous for this variant. These findings were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and an unaffected sibling was found not to carry this variant. This variant is located in intron 7 of 12 and has a CADD score of 20.6 (Schubach et al., 2024). In the gnomAD database v4.1.0, this variant was seen in heterozygous state in 1/74930 African American alleles (0.0013%) and in 5/1179710 non-Finish European alleles (0.00042%) but never in the homozygous state (Chen et al., 2024). SpliceAI reported a donor gain score of 0.63, consistent with a predicted effect on splicing (Jaganathan et al., 2019).

RNAseq performed on skin fibroblasts revealed use of an alternative splice donor site 40 bases into the intron 7 [NM_001383.6] in Subject 2. Use of this alternative splice donor site was not seen in three control samples (Fig. 1M). Use of this alternative splice site leads to an extended exon 7 that includes an TGA stop codon (Fig. 1N). Of the 22 transcripts detected between exon 7 and 8 in Subject 2, 10 (45%) used the wild-type splice donor site and 12 (55%) used the alternative splice donor site. However, the total number of DPH1 transcripts seen in Subject 2 were also dramatically reduced with control samples having 304, 355, and 378 normally spliced transcripts detected between exons 7 and 8 (Fig. 1M). This reduction in expression is consistent with the majority of DPH1 transcripts in Subject 2 using the alternative splice donor site and subsequently undergoing nonsense mediated mRNA decay.

As summarized in Table 1 and Supplemental Table 1, Subject 1 and 2’s phenotypes are consistent with those seen in individuals with developmental delay with short stature, dysmorphic facial features, and sparse hair 1 (DEDSSH1; MIM# 616901), an autosomal recessive disorder caused by variants in DPH1 (Alazami et al., 2015; Loucks et al., 2015; Urreizti et al., 2020).

Here, we report an update to a previously published sibling pair diagnosed with Fine-Lubinsky syndrome for whom an autosomal recessive inheritance was hypothesized (Holder et al., 2007). A research reanalysis of clinically-based quad exome sequencing studies, and RNAseq on fibroblasts, revealed that both siblings were homozygous for a c.749+39G>A [NM_001383.6] intronic variant in DPH1 that resulted in abnormal splicing and dramatically reduced expression of DPH1 transcripts most likely due to altered splicing and triggering of nonsense mediated mRNA decay. Since the clinical presentation of Subjects 1 and 2 were consistent with phenotypes associated with the inheritance of biallelic pathogenic variants in DPH1 (Table 1, Supplemental Table 1), they were given a diagnosis of DEDSSH1 (MIM# 616901) (Alazami et al., 2015; Loucks et al., 2015; Urreizti et al., 2020).

Our findings lead us to recommend that all individuals with a clinical diagnosis of Fine-Lubinsky syndrome be screened for variants in DPH1. We note that other individuals with phenotypes suggestive of Fine Lubinski syndrome have been shown to carry de novo variants in MAF. These individuals were ultimately given a molecular diagnosis of Ayme-Gripp syndrome (Ayme & Philip, 1996; Niceta et al., 2015). It is possible that pathogenic variants in other genes may ultimately be found to cause the phenotypes seen in other individuals who carry a diagnosis of Fine Lubinski syndrome.

The medical histories of Subjects 1 and 2 provide some important insights into DEDSSH1. Both were diagnosed with hearing loss with concomitant auditory canal stenosis when they were young and were treated with hearing aids. However, subsequent hearing screens performed at 8 and 9 years of age revealed normal hearing. This suggests that the hearing loss seen in DEDSSH1 may remit over time. Congenital heart defects and cardiomyopathy are known features of DEDSSH1. The development of uncompensated congestive heart failure in Subject 1 and reduced left ventricular systolic function in Subject 2 emphasize the need to monitor for cardiomyopathy in individuals with DEDSSH1.

The homozygous c.749+39G>A [NM_001383.6] intronic variants in DPH1 in Subjects 1 and 2 were detected by clinically-based exome sequencing but was not reported. This is not surprising given its location relatively deep into an intron. Indeed, it was only after careful review of the overlapping phenotypes between the Subjects and individuals with DEDSSH1, and a review of the in-silico prediction regarding this variant, that it was considered for further study. Confirmation of the diagnosis, however, still required demonstration of a significant effect on DPH1 mRNA levels through RNAseq analysis on fibroblasts. With the increased use of clinically-based whole genome sequencing, the laboratory geneticist will be faced with the task of interpreting an increasing number of intronic variants. To effectively identify intronic variants that could be causative will require rapid matching of an individual’s phenotypes to those of known diseases. Perhaps this ability will come through the expanded use of artificial intelligence (Aradhya et al., 2023). However, to confirm the effects of most intronic variants will require additional functional analyses. Hence, the usefulness of the genomic data available from an individual will ultimately depend on the clinical availability of functional studies such as RNAseq (Ketkar et al., 2023).
