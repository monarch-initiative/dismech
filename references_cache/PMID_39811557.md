# Repeat expansions in RFC1 gene in refractory chronic cough.
**Authors:** Hirons B, Cho PSP, Rhatigan K, Shaw J, Curro R, Rugginini B, Dominik N, Turner RD, Mackay E, Hull JH, Abubakar-Waziri H, Kesavan H, Jolley CJ, Hadden RD, Cortese A, Birring SS
**Journal:** ERJ Open Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1183/23120541.00584-2024](https://doi.org/10.1183/23120541.00584-2024)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Refractory chronic cough (RCC), persisting despite addressing contributory diagnoses, is likely underpinned by neurally mediated cough hypersensitivity. RFC1 disorders are genetic neurodegenerative conditions caused by biallelic RFC1 repeat expansion sequences, commonly presenting with cough, followed by neurological features including cerebellar ataxia with neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS). The prevalence and identifying clinical characteristics of RFC1 repeat-expansion disorders in patients with RCC are unknown.

METHODS: Consecutive patients with RCC underwent RFC1 genotyping, cough severity visual analogue scale (VAS) and cough-specific health status assessment (Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ)). Participants with biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions (RFC1++) also underwent nerve conduction studies, brain imaging (MRI) and cough reflex sensitivity testing.

RESULTS: 51 participants with RCC were recruited; 36 (71%) female, median (IQR) age 65 (56-70) years, duration of cough 12.8 (6.9-20.0) years. Four (8%) were RFC1++, five (10%) monoallelic carriers (RFC1+-) and 42 (82%) of wild-type genotype (RFC1--). No difference was observed in age, sex, cough duration, spirometry, VAS or LCQ scores between RFC1++ and RFC1-- subjects (p>0.05). The symptom of pins and needles was more frequent in RFC1++ (n=4, 100%) compared to RFC1-- (n=12, 33%) (p=0.01). RFC1++ participants had impaired sensory action potentials, and one had cerebellar atrophy. RFC1++ participants had heightened cough reflex sensitivity to capsaicin, similar to previous CANVAS and RCC studies.

CONCLUSION: Biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions (RFC1++) were present in 8% of RCC patients. RFC1++ participants demonstrated features of cough reflex hypersensitivity. RFC1++ chronic cough had few identifying features, although symptoms of pins and needles were more common.

Chronic cough, a persistent cough lasting >8 weeks, affects around 10% of the global population and is associated with significant impairment in quality of life [1, 2]. In up to >40% of patients with chronic cough, cough persists despite addressing potentially contributory associated disorders and is termed unexplained or refractory (RCC) [3]. Chronic cough is considered a neuropathic disorder, suggested clinically by features of cough hypersensitivity. This concept is supported by mechanistic studies [4–6] and clinical trials which demonstrate a response to treatments that target both peripheral and central neural cough pathways [7, 8]. Since the late 1990s, chronic cough has been reported as a manifestation of genetic neurological conditions that affect the sensory nervous system [9, 10]. In 2019, biallelic AAGGG repeat expansions in intron 2 of the replication factor C subunit 1 (RFC1) gene were identified as the causative factor for the most common aetiology of hereditary ataxia: cerebellar ataxia with neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) [11]. Patients with RFC1 disorders usually present with chronic cough, often decades before the development of neurological features that range from the full spectrum of CANVAS to isolated peripheral neuropathy [12, 13]. The prevalence of monoallelic repeat expansions in the RFC1 gene (asymptomatic carriers) is estimated at 1–6% of the population, and biallelic repeat expansions (RFC1 disorders) at 1:625 to 1:20 000 [11, 14]. Heightened cough reflex sensitivity to capsaicin, comparable to RCC, has recently been reported in CANVAS [15]. However, few studies have investigated the prevalence of RFC1 repeat expansions in RCC, which is estimated to be between 7% and 16% of patients [16, 17]. We investigated the prevalence of biallelic repeat expansions in the RFC1 gene (RFC++) in unselected patients with RCC without a known diagnosis of CANVAS or RFC1 disorder, and evaluated clinical characteristics to aid identification of patients with RCC and RFC1++ from RCC without RFC1 repeat expansions (RFC1−−).

Consecutive patients with RCC from a tertiary care specialist cough clinic (King's College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust) were evaluated prospectively between December 2022 and March 2023. Participants were investigated and managed according to European guidelines [2, 3]. RCC was defined as chronic cough (>8 weeks’ duration) that remained intractable despite investigation or management of treatable traits [2, 3]. Participants with RCC and biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions (RFC1++) were compared to RCC and no RFC1 repeat expansions (RFC1−−). RCC with monoallelic RFC1 repeat expansions (carriers, RFC1+−) were analysed separately. National Health Service (NHS) research ethics committee approval was granted as part of the Integrating Mental & Physical Healthcare: Research, Training & Services (IMPARTS) program (Health Research Authority South Central – Oxford C: 18/SC/0039), and East London and the City Research Ethics Committee (REC Reference: 10/H0703/6). All elements of the study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent for participation in the study.

Demographic and anthropometric data were collected at cough clinic consultation. Participants were invited to complete subjective assessments of cough severity, cough-specific quality of life, depression and anxiety. Participants underwent objective cough frequency (CF) assessment, spirometry and thoracic imaging as part of routine clinical assessment. Consecutive participants were offered RFC1 genetic testing to investigate for a cause of RCC. Only participants having genetic testing were included for analysis, and were screened for typical symptoms of CANVAS or RFC1 disorders. Participants with biallelic pathogenic RFC1 repeat expansions were invited back for further characterisation of their cough with objective measurement of cough reflex sensitivity with capsaicin tussive challenge, nerve conduction studies and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

All participants with RCC underwent RFC1 PCR testing. Pathogenic AAGGG and non-pathogenic AAAGG or AAAAG repeat expansions in RFC1 were detected utilising flanking and repeat-primed (RP) PCRs, as previously described [11]. Positive cases were defined as negative flanking PCR, negative RP-PCR for (AAAGG)exp and (AAAAG)exp configurations, and the presence of a decremental saw-tooth pattern in RP-PCR for the pathogenic AAGGG repeat expansion [11].

Nerve conduction studies were performed as previously described [18] to measure motor and sensory nerve action potentials. Brain MRIs were conducted following established clinical protocols. Axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery, coronal T2-weighted, and 3D T1 sequences were employed to assess cerebellar atrophy. Participants were screened for common symptoms of RFC1 disorders. Seven questions, developed by a specialist neurology multidisciplinary team, recorded the presence of pins and needles (paraesthesia), numbness and pain in the limbs (not explained by non-neurological conditions, e.g. arthritis), unsteadiness, speech or swallowing problems, and a question intended to detect oscillopsia (the illusion that stationary objects or the surrounding environment are oscillating with head movement) (supplementary table E1).

Cough reflex sensitivity was assessed as per European Respiratory Society (ERS) recommendations [19]. RFC1++ participants underwent inhaled capsaicin cough challenge testing; no participant had upper respiratory tract infection within the last 6 weeks. Diluted capsaicin was delivered via 10-μL single-breath inhalations in increasing doubling doses (0.49–1000 μmol·L−1) with an air-powered digital dosimeter (KoKo Digidoser; nSpire Health Inc., Longmont, CO, USA) at 1-min intervals. The anticipation effect was attenuated by use of randomly interspersed 0.9% saline solution inhalation [19]. A single characterised nebuliser (Model 646; DeVilbiss Healthcare, Port Washington, NY, USA) with an output of 1.205 mL·min−1 was used for all participants. Inspiratory flow was restricted to 0.5 L·s−1 [19] by use of a valve. At least three respiratory cycles occurred prior to the administration of nebulised solution, with simultaneous inspection of flow-volume signals to ensure a consistent and maximal inspiratory effort (0.5 L·s−1) throughout administration. The test was repeated if observed inhalation was sub-maximal. After administration, coughs were counted for a period of 15 s with the aid of an MP3 recorder (LFH0662; Philips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) [19]. Capsaicin challenge test was terminated once ≥5 coughs were elicited by a single dose administration. The capsaicin concentrations required to elicit two (C2) and five (C5) coughs were calculated by interpolation with lower levels indicating higher cough reflex sensitivity [20]. Utilising the same methodology and equipment as this study, normal ranges for C2 and C5 have been previously reported in women and men [21]; C2 range 4.44–18.2 μmol·L−1 and 7.63–35.9 μmol·L−1, and C5 range >37.8 μmol·L−1 and >158 μmol·L−1, respectively.

CF was recorded for 24 h with the validated Leicester Cough Monitor [22], an ambulatory system comprising a wearable digital audio recorder (LFH0662, Philips), free-field lapel microphone (LFH9173, Philips) and cough detection software [22]. Coughs were detected as single events regardless if they occurred in isolation or within bouts [22]. Objective CF (coughs per hour) over 24 h was documented.

Cough severity was assessed with the cough severity visual analogue scale (VAS) (range 0–100 mm) [19]. Cough-specific health status was assessed with the Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ) (range 3–21, lower scores indicating worse impairment) [23]. Screening for anxiety was performed with the General Anxiety Disorder assessment (GAD-7) and for depression with the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8); scores ≥10 in GAD-7 or PHQ-8 indicate moderate to severe anxiety or depression [24–26]. Profiling of cough triggers and sensations was assessed with the Cough Hypersensitivity Questionnaire (CHQ) (range 0–22, higher score indicates more cough triggers and sensations) [27].

Spirometry (Jaeger MS-PFT Analyser Unit with Sentry Suite software version 2.19.96) was measured according to guidelines by the ERS and the American Thoracic Society [28]. Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO) was measured with the NIOX VERO analyser (Aerocrine AB, Solna, Sweden).

We aimed to recruit 50 consecutive patients with RCC; estimated to detect eight patients (16%) with biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions, based on previous reports [16]. All data were expressed as median (interquartile range (IQR)), except CF and capsaicin threshold concentrations, which were presented as geometric mean (geometric standard deviation). Mann–Whitney U test was performed for comparison of unpaired continuous data, and Fisher's exact test for categorical data. Associations between data were analysed with Spearman's correlation coefficient (ρ). All analyses were performed on Prism® Version 10.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

51 consecutive patients with RCC were included in the analysis: 36 (71%) female, median (IQR) age 65 (56–70) years, body mass index (BMI) 26.4 (24.2–32.7) kg·m−2, duration of cough 12.8 (6.9–20.0) years (table 1). Spirometry and FENO were within normal range: median (IQR) forced expiratory volume in 1 s 94 (84–103) % predicted, forced vital capacity 98 (89–110) % predicted, FENO 16 (12–26) ppb. Four (8%) participants had biallelic repeat RFC1 expansions (RFC++): female sex 75%, median (IQR) age 69 (59–74) years, duration of cough 21.5 (16.6–28.0) years. Five (10%) participants had monoallelic RFC1 repeat expansions (RFC1+−, carrier status): female sex 40%, median (IQR) age 60 (52–64) years, duration of cough 8.0 (4.5–12.0) years. Comparing RFC1++ and RFC1−−, there was no significant difference in current age, sex, BMI, spirometry or CF (all p>0.05, table 1); cough duration was nominally longer in RFC1++ (median (IQR) 21.5 (16.6–28.0) versus 12.4 (5.9–20.0) years, p=0.06) (figure 1).

All participants with RFC1++ had nerve conduction studies, demonstrating mild to severely impaired sensory action potentials (table 2). MRI brain scans were performed in all RFC1++ participants; one demonstrated evidence of mild cerebellar atrophy consistent with CANVAS, and one had incidental small vessel disease. In RFC1++ participants, prior to genetic testing, one had fibromyalgia, and one had essential tremor treated with deep brain stimulation (unrelated to RFC1 disorder) (table 2).

Neurological symptoms were common across all groups of participants, regardless of RFC1 status (table 1); the most frequently reported were pains/cramps in limbs (60%), followed by distal pins and needles (38%), and unsteadiness (30%). Symptoms of distal pins and needles were more common in RFC1++ participants compared to RFC1−− and RFC+− (100% versus 33% and 20% respectively, all p<0.05), but no significant difference was observed in other neurological symptoms.

Moderate to severe cough severity and health status impairment were observed in all groups (table 1). There was no significant difference in cough severity and health status in participants with RFC1++ compared to RFC1−−; median (IQR) severity VAS 77 (72–80) versus 69 (52–75) (p=0.06) and LCQ score 8.2 (7.3–8.9) versus 9.7 (7.8–12.6) (p=0.18), respectively. Objective CF was similar between RFC1++ and RFC1−−: geometric mean±sd 25±2.0 and 19.2±1.7 coughs·h−1, respectively (p=0.8). Participants with RFC1++ and RFC1−− had similar depression and anxiety scores; median (IQR) PHQ-8 5 (1–9) versus 6 (2–9) and GAD-7 5 (3–8) versus 3 (1–7), respectively (all p>0.05). RFC1+− (carriers) had moderate to severe cough severity and health status impairment; median (IQR) VAS 80 (32–81) and LCQ score 9.8 (8.3–13.2), comparable to RFC1++ and RFC1−− (table 1).

RFC1++ participants reported multiple cough triggers: median (IQR) CHQ triggers 8 (6–12), abnormal laryngeal sensations 4 (2–5), total score 11 (8–17). Cough reflex sensitivity to capsaicin was heightened in RFC1++ participants (geometric mean±sd C2: 1.1±4.4; and C5: 1.9±3.6 μmol·L−1) compared to what we have previously reported in healthy controls (geometric mean±sd C5: 159±0.6 μmol·L−1), but similar to our observations in RCC (geometric mean±sd C5: 15.3±0.7 μmol·L−1) [20].

Our study investigated the prevalence of biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions (RFC1++) in consecutive patients attending a specialist chronic cough clinic. We compared cough and neurological clinical characteristics in RFC1++ with participants with monoallelic (RFC1+−) and no (RFC1−−) RFC1 repeat expansions. The prevalence of biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions was 8% (1:13), much higher than the estimated population prevalence of 1:625 to 1:20 000 [13, 14]. The prevalence of monoallelic RFC1 repeat expansions was 10%, not significantly different from the estimated population prevalence of up to 6.8% [13, 14]. Demographics, anthropometrics and cough characteristics were broadly similar between RFC1++, RFC1−− and RFC1+−, although there was a trend towards a longer duration of cough in RFC1++. Neurological symptoms were commonly reported in all participants, regardless of presence of RFC1 repeat expansions; however, the symptom of pins and needles was more common in participants with RFC1++ compared to RFC1−− and RFC1+−.

Two studies have investigated the prevalence of RFC1 repeat expansions in patients with refractory chronic cough (RCC) [16]. Palones
et al. [17] and Guilleminault
et al. [16] reported rates of biallelic repeat expansions in 7% and 16%, respectively. Our finding of a longer duration of cough in those with RFC1 repeat expansions and high rates of reported neuropathy is consistent with their studies [16, 17]. Combining these studies confirms that a significant minority of patients with RCC have underlying RFC1 disorders; however, accurate identification of such patients remains elusive. Guilleminault
et al. [16] suggested that monoallelic RFC1 repeat expansions may be relevant in chronic cough. However, the monoallelic prevalence in each of these studies was not significantly higher than the general population; therefore, the significance of monoallelic expansions needs to be interpreted with caution [29].

Consistent with previous studies, our patients with RFC1 repeat expansions reported multiple cough triggers and sensations suggestive of cough hypersensitivity [16, 17]. Furthermore, cough reflex sensitivity was heightened compared to historical healthy controls [20, 21], and comparable to historical patients with RCC [20, 21] and known CANVAS [15]. That RFC1 disorders are associated with a depletion of central and peripheral neurons and, as in our study, impaired sensory action potentials [11, 30] means the neural mechanisms of heightened reflex sensitivity need careful consideration. Broadly, neural pathways involved in heightened nerve sensitivity are categorised as peripheral or central, such as in neuropathic pain conditions [4, 31]. Indeed, the similarities between neuropathic pain and chronic cough have been frequently described [32–34]. Neuropathic pain syndromes are often associated with both denervation and hypersensitivity, such as in diabetic or post-herpetic neuropathy [35–37]. In RFC1 disorders, nerve and skin biopsies have revealed a depletion of small and large fibres, which include C-fibres and A delta fibres [11, 38, 39]. C-fibres may play both a stimulatory and inhibitory role in cough, and loss of inhibitory fibres can increase pain or cough sensitivity [31, 36, 40]. Severely depleted nerve fibres which continue to activate central mechanisms can lead to central sensitisation in neuropathic pain syndromes [31]. Indeed, in chronic cough, increased activation of cough centres, and corresponding reduced activation of cough suppression centres, have been reported in functional MRI studies, although the association with peripheral nerves was not explored [4]. Increased airway innervation has been reported in RCC compared to healthy controls [5]. However, airway hyperinnervation is unlikely in RFC1 disorders based on the known pathophysiology, and studies with airway biopsy are needed. It is likely, therefore, that the drivers of cough hypersensitivity in RFC1 disorders and RCC may differ.

Symptoms of peripheral neuropathy and unsteadiness were common in all patients. This may be partially explained by our patient demographics, typical for chronic cough with a median age of 65 years [2]. Symptoms of polyneuropathy and unsteadiness in the general population increase with age, reported in up to 7% and 20% of patients aged over 65 years, respectively [41, 42]. We report rates of numbness, paraesthesia and pain in patients without RFC1 repeat expansions in 21–60%. Whether this finding is representative of our patient demographics, or represents an underlying presence of neuropathy, genetic or otherwise, needs further investigation. A limitation of our neurology symptom screening questions was that they were perhaps not sufficiently specific for symptoms of CANVAS, potentially leading to overreporting. The screening questions were designed by a multidisciplinary team to be brief and practical in a respiratory clinic; future studies should develop validated questionnaires for neuropathic and CANVAS symptoms and include a full neurological examination. Indeed, Palones
et al. [17] recently reported a higher incidence of abnormal Rydel–Seiffer tuning fork testing in patients with RCC and RFC1 disorders compared to no RFC1 disorder (33% versus 7.1%). Symptoms or examination findings of peripheral neuropathy in patients with RCC should prompt clinicians to consider RFC1 disorders.

There are limitations with our study. As an observational study, causality cannot be inferred. As it is a rare disease, and because our prevalence was lower than a previous comparable study, the number of patients with biallelic repeat expansions was low (n=4), meaning slight changes in patient outcomes could affect the overall results; therefore, group comparisons need to be made cautiously. Capsaicin cough challenge test and neurological assessment (including examination and investigations) were only performed in patients with known biallelic RFC1, therefore introducing the possibility of observer bias. There was a minority of patients who declined genetic testing, although we did not formally capture this. Future studies of genetic testing in chronic cough should explore patients’ opinion on this, and testing should be done with appropriate counselling. Older patients and patients with neuropathy might accept genetic testing more readily, compared to younger patients without such symptoms, potentially causing a selection bias in favour of higher rates of neurological symptoms and a higher prevalence of repeat RFC1 expansions. Gastro-oesophageal reflux and dysmotility occur commonly in CANVAS; however, several studies have shown limited or no benefit to cough from anti-reflux treatment [15, 16, 43]. We did not systematically evaluate gastro-oesophageal dysfunction in all our patients, and this should be evaluated in future studies. Nerve conduction studies, brain MRI scans and cough reflex sensitivity were only performed in participants with biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions, precluding the direct comparison with patients without biallelic expansions.

That few discriminating features are available to differentiate patients with and without RFC1 disorders makes the decision of when to offer RFC1 genetic testing difficult. Careful consideration for neurological referral, nerve conduction studies and RFC1 testing should be made in patients with RCC with cough hypersensitivity features and neurological symptoms or abnormal examination findings, especially if there is a long duration of cough and family history. Ultimately, genetic screening of RFC1 spectrum disease and other neurological disorders may be needed to identify the cause of chronic cough in some patients.

Future studies are needed to confirm the prevalence of RFC1 disorders in chronic cough, and to identify discriminating characteristics, such as with the use of validated neurological questionnaires, examination or biomarkers. Increased availability of RFC1 genetic testing is required to assist development of such studies. Longitudinal studies to evaluate the life course of patients with chronic cough and RFC1 repeat expansions, biallelic and monoallelic, are needed. Mechanistic studies utilising airway biopsies and functional MRI, which have borne fruit in RCC, will help ascertain the driver of cough reflex hypersensitivity in RFC1 disorders [4, 5], and may aid development of targeted pharmacotherapy. The frequent presence of symptoms suggesting polyneuropathy in RCC needs corroborating with further study involving validated tools and objective investigations.

Biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions represent an important aetiology in patients with RCC, with a prevalence of ∼8%, and is associated with cough hypersensitivity. Neurological symptoms and examination findings in RCC need further study. Currently, the lack of clear discriminating features may indicate a need for increased availability of RFC1 genetic testing in patients with chronic cough.
