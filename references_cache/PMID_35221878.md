ALG1-congenital disorder of glycosylation (ALGI-CDG) is among the 5 most common CDG, resulting in a deficiency of β-1,4 mannosyltransferase.

The most common phenotypic manifestations of ALG1-CDG are failure to thrive, intellectual disability, developmental delay, hypotonia, and epilepsy.

The most common variant of the humanALG1gene is c.773C>T (28.8%) and results in a severe-disease phenotype that leads to death in the first year of life. The second most common variant is c.1076C>T; it may be considered a mild-disease phenotype.

Congenital disorders of glycosylation (CDG) are a genetically and clinically heterogeneous group of about 150 diseases caused by defects in protein and lipid glycosylation. ALG1-CDG is among the 5 most common CDG. It is an autosomal recessive disease due to β-1,4 mannosyltransferase [Jaeken et al., 2015]. Since its first description in 2004, at least 58 patients have been reported. The most common phenotypic manifestations are developmental delay, intellectual disability, failure to thrive, hypotonia, and epilepsy [Ng et al., 2016]. This report presents a phenotypic, molecular, and biochemical analysis of a young patient with a mild form of ALG1-CDG, along with a review of the literature.

A 15-month-old girl was referred to our clinic with hypotonia, failure to thrive, developmental delay and seizures. She was born at 37 weeks' gestation by caesarean section as the first child of healthy consanguineous parents with an unremarkable family history (Fig.1a). The course of pregnancy was uneventful, but was interrupted due to the premature rupture of membranes. Her birth weight was 2,700 g (between the 25th and 50th percentiles), body length was 47.5 cm (50th percentile) and occipital-frontal circumference (OFC) was 33 cm (50th percentile; percentiles according to Fenton, 2013 growth charts). At 8 months of age, failure to thrive, feeding difficulties, and developmental delay became apparent. An epileptic seizure was observed, and an anticonvulsant treatment (phenobarbital 5 mg/kg/day) was initiated when she was 11 months old. Due to progressive deterioration and swallowing difficulty, a nasogastric feeding tube was placed to provide nutritional support.

Her physical examination revealed evident facial dysmorphism, including large and everted ears with helix hypoplasia, a large forehead, a turned-up nose, thin lips, a small mouth, and small almond-shaped eyes with a wide nasal bridge (Fig.1b). At the age of 18 months, her height was 75 cm (−2.3 SDS), weight 7.8 kg (−2.05 SDS), and OFC 45 cm (between 3rd and 10th percentiles; percentiles according to Turkish child growth standards). She was hypotonic, and developmental delay was seen in all domains. She was unable to sit unassisted and make discrete sounds. Other system examinations were unremarkable.

Routine laboratory investigations revealed a normal complete blood count (hemoglobin 10.5 g/dL, white blood cell 11,140/mm3, platelet 337,000/mm3), blood glucose and renal function test. Serum transaminase levels were mildly elevated [aspartate transaminase 52 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 39 U/L (N < 35 U/L)], while γ-glutamyl transferase 15 U/L (N < 38 U/L), alkaline phosphatase 177 U/L (N 135–320 U/L), total bilirubin 0.19 mg/dL (N 0.13–1.2 mg/dL), direct bilirubin 0.02 mg/dL (N < 0.2 mg/dL), creatine kinase 118 U/L (N < 145U/L), and lactate (10.06 mg/dL; N 10–14 mg/dL) were normal. Thyroid function test, plasma amino acids, urine organic acids, and plasma acylcarnitines were normal. Anticlotting factors protein C (32%; N 70–140%), protein S (61.5%; N63.5–149%) and antithrombin III (33%; N 83–128%) were decreased. The urine protein/creatinine ratio and the tubular phosphate reabsorption were normal. All serum immunoglobulin levels were within the normal range.

Brainstem auditory evoked response were normal, while visual evoked potentials were mildly decreased (right 139 ms, left 136 ms; normal <120 ms). Abdomen sonography and echocardiography were normal. At 3 years of age, brain MRI revealed a widening of cerebrospinal fluid spaces and the ventricular system, with a normal brain stem and cerebellum.

Isoelectric focusing showed a CDG-I-pattern with increased disialotransferrin (11.79%; normal range 2–2.5%), decreased tetrasialotransferrin (52.81%; normal range 64–80%) and decreased pentasialotransferrin (10.79%; normal range 12–18%).

These findings suggested CDG. Whole-exome sequencing showed homozygosity for the known ALG1 c.1076C>T (p.Ser359Leu) variant.

During infancy, she was admitted several times to a local hospital with recurrent bacterial infections (pneumonia, urinary tract infections). Otherwise, her overall condition was stable. Immunological workup (serum immunoglobulins, isohemagglutinins, T-cell, B-cell, and neutrophil phenotyping by flow cytometry, and respiratory burst activity) was normal.

At the time of her last examination, at the age of 3.5 years, her height was 87 cm (−2.7 SDS), weight 9.5 kg (−3.59 SDS) and OFC 45 cm (−2.98 SDS). She was microcephalic, but brain MRI was normal. She is able to generate 10 words, to walk with assistance, and to feed orally. She was myopic. Her seizures are kept under control with phenobarbital (5 mg/kg per day) and levetiracetam (60 mg/kg per day) treatments.

ALG1-CDG is an autosomal recessive disease with varying multisystem signs and symptoms that typically develops during infancy [Jaeken et al., 2015]. Here, we report on a 3.5-year-old Turkish girl with a mild clinical progression of ALG1-CDG.

To the best of our knowledge, 43 variants of theALG1gene have been identified to date in 58 affected individuals, making this to be the 59th case to be reported. The most common variant is c.773C>T (28.8%); it is a severe-disease phenotype that leads to death in the first year of life. The second most common variant is c.1076C>T (11.7%) (Table1) [Dupré et al., 2010; Lei et al., 2021]. Considering the limited data, the ALG1 c.1076C>T homozygous variant may result in a milder form, as seen in our patient.

Of the 57 patients (prenatal diagnosis was established in one patient and pregnancy was aborted), 19 (33.3%) died in the first year of life, indicating that ALG1-CDG is associated with a severe clinical profile (Table2). The most common presentation, including that of our patient, is neurological namely psychomotor delay. Of the 58 previously reported cases, data was available on 56, and the main neurological symptoms were psychomotor developmental delay, intellectual disability, and hypotonia (Table2). Nearly, all patients experienced epileptic seizures. Also, facial dysmorphism has been reported in most patients (39 of 56; about 70%) (Table2).

Renal manifestations, most commonly nephrotic syndrome, were reported in about 16% (Table2). ALG1-CDG seems to be the CDG that is most frequently associated with nephrotic syndrome, and there is an association between nephrotic syndrome and the c.773C>T (p.Ser258Leu) variant [Harshman et al., 2016].

Gastrointestinal manifestations such as feeding difficulties, chronic diarrhea, and protein-losing enteropathies have been reported in about 50% (Table2). Some patients had visual symptoms (strabismus and nystagmus), skeletal anomalies (scoliosis, kyphosis, contractures), cardiac manifestations and immunological involvement, resulting in recurrent serious infections.

Similar to previously reported cases, our patient experienced neurological manifestations, facial dysmorphism, epilepsy, feeding difficulties, type 1 isoelectric focusing pattern, coagulation abnormalities, and recurrent severe infections without any immunological abnormalities. In conclusion, we report another patient with mild ALG1-CDG as has been described in 58 other ALG1-CDG patients.
