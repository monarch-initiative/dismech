# A Case Report and Literature Review on Osteo-Oto-Hepato-Enteric Syndrome in Premature Infants Caused by UNC45A Deficiency.
**Authors:** Sun Z, Song Q, Zhang Z, Liu S, Yu R, Chen Y, Liu H, Wang L
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.70149](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.70149)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To report the clinical manifestations, treatment, and genetic diagnosis of a patient with osteo-oto-hepato-enteric (O2HE) syndrome.

CASE PRESENTATION: A retrospective analysis was performed on a Chinese premature infant born at 36 + 5 weeks of gestation. The analysis included maternal pregnancy and delivery history, prenatal ultrasound findings, clinical manifestations, diagnosis and treatment process, and UNC45A gene mutation results for the infant and parents. A literature review was also conducted. The mother had a history of six spontaneous abortions. A late-pregnancy prenatal ultrasound revealed polyhydramnios and diffuse intestinal dilation. The infant developed watery stools and diarrhea shortly after birth and was clinically suspected to have congenital chloride diarrhea. Supportive treatment was administered, and whole-exome sequencing was performed for the family. Two heterozygous mutations were identified in the UNC45A gene, including c.2455C > T (p.Arg819Ter), a novel, previously unreported variant. The infant was ultimately diagnosed with osteo-oto-hepatoenteric syndrome.

CONCLUSION: Genetic analysis of the UNC45A gene is valuable for diagnosing O2HE syndrome. The novel mutation c.2455C > T (p.Arg819Ter) enriches the mutation spectrum of UNC45A, providing further theoretical support for diagnosis and genetic counseling of O2HE.

Osteo‐oto‐hepato‐enteric syndrome (O2HE) is a rare autosomal recessive inherited disease recently discovered due to mutations or deletions in the UNC‐45 myosin partner A (UNC45A) gene. Its typical manifestations are different combinations of bone fragility, hearing loss, cholestasis, and congenital diarrhea. Some patients also exhibit mild developmental delay and intellectual impairment (Wang et al. 2022) (Kong et al. 2023). The rarity of O2HE makes its diagnosis very difficult. Due to limited previous reports and insufficient clinical understanding, misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis of this disease often occur, such as being misdiagnosed as congenital chloride diarrhea (CCD) for the similar intestinal symptom. CCD was first reported by Gamble and Darrow in 1945, and its clinical manifestations include watery stools that begin to appear in the neonatal period, hypochloremia, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis. It is also a familial autosomal recessive hereditary disease (Kere et al. 1999). Its pathogenic gene is the SLC26A3 gene located on 7q31, which mainly participates in the absorption of intestinal chloride. Up to now, more than 100 gene mutations of SLC26A3 have been discovered (Sadagah et al. 2022) (Konishi et al. 2020). Diarrhea is the most important clinical manifestation that can appear from the fetal stage, manifested as excessive amniotic fluid and dilation of the fetal intestinal tract in prenatal ultrasound, which often leads to miscarriage or premature birth as a result (Alrefai et al. 2007). Diarrhea often occurs on the first day after birth, with yellow watery stools accompanied by abdominal distension. Also, diarrhea is very stubborn, which doesn't respond to various treatments such as antibiotics, gut microbiota, and mucosal protectors (Cendal et al. 2021).

This present paper reports a case of a premature infant from China with prenatal examination and postnatal manifestations suspected to be CCD; afterwards, genetic testing confirmed it to be a case of O2HE which was caused by a novel compound heterozygous mutation in the UNC45A gene. We also conducted a literature review to provide a basis for the diagnosis and treatment of O2HE.

The premature infant is a girl from a 39‐year‐old primipara. The parents are both in good health and deny incest and family genetic history. Her mother had 6 adverse pregnancies in the past, all of which were embryos damaged about 50 days into pregnancy and required medication or artificial abortion. Routine prenatal examination was carried out in the local hospital during this pregnancy. During the second trimester of pregnancy, the patient was diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus and hypothyroidism, for which appropriate treatment was administered by the obstetrician. Beginning at 33 weeks of gestational age, prenatal ultrasound revealed progressive fetal intestinal dilation, polyhydramnios, and fetal growth restriction (FGR). At 36 weeks GA, ultrasound showed that the whole abdominal bowel of the fetus appeared to have diffuse dilation with a maximum inner diameter of about 2.3 cm and an amniotic fluid index of 25 cm (Figure 1). Based on ultrasound imaging, the fetus is suspected to be suffering from CCD disease. At that time, amniocentesis for genetic testing was recommended, but the pregnant mother and her family declined. At 36 + 5 weeks of gestation, the mother experienced premature rupture of membranes and regular uterine contractions. The infant was delivered with a birth weight of 2160 g, which corresponds to approximately the 10th percentile (Z ≈ −1.3) for gestational age based on the Fenton growth chart, indicating mild fetal growth restriction. The Apgar score was 9 at 1 min, with cyanosis of the lips and face. After birth, the infant received immediate interventions, including drying, thermal support, and airway clearance. However, by the second minute of life, the infant's heart rate dropped to 40 beats per minute, and breathing became irregular. Immediate 30% FiO2 T combination resuscitation airbag pressure‐assisted ventilation is given to her, and the 5th minute Apgar Score was 6 scores (shallow breathing, heart rate 108 times/min, facial skin cyanosis, hypotonia). Continue to provide warmth and 30% FiO2 nasal catheter for oxygen inhalation; the baby's heart rate gradually increased to 130 times per minute. The Apgar Score at the 10th minute was 9 scores (lip cyanosis). There were no obvious abnormalities in the amniotic fluid, umbilical cord, and placenta. The infant was admitted to the NICU ward due to premature birth, low birth weight, and a doubtful diagnosis of CCD disease. After admission, the baby began to develop watery stools and diarrhea, with more than 10 times a day and separation of water and feces. With parental consent, 3 mL of peripheral venous blood was collected from both the infant and her parents in anticoagulant tubes (EDTA) for genetic testing to confirm the diagnosis of congenital chloride diarrhea (CCD). Simultaneously, intravenous nutrition support, additional fluid replacement, sodium chloride supplementation, and potassium chloride treatment were provided to the baby. The child developed hyperkalemia at 12 days, and after discontinuing additional supplementation of potassium and sodium, there was no significant hypochloremia or metabolic alkalosis to appear. This also led us to doubt the diagnosis of CCD. We continued to strengthen enteral and parenteral nutrition support treatment and provided montmorillonite powder, probiotics, and low osmotic pressure hydrolyzed formula milk to take orally for the baby. The diarrhea situation of the baby gradually improved, and her weight began to gain from 3 weeks after birth. Trio‐based whole‐exome sequencing revealed that the child carried compound heterozygous mutations in the UNC45A gene: c.2455C > T (p.Arg819Ter) and c.710 T > C (p.Leu237Pro) (Figure 3). Pedigree analysis confirmed that these variants were inherited from the parents, supporting the diagnosis of the rare disorder osteo‐oto‐hepato‐enteric (O2HE) syndrome. Among the mutations in the UNC45A gene, c.710 T > C is a reported mutation, and c.2455C > T is a new mutation. According to the ACMG‐AMP guidelines, the UNC45A variant c.2455C > T (p.Arg819Ter) is classified as likely pathogenic. By the above result, we confirmed the diagnosis of O2HE for this baby. At 18 days after birth, the baby's cranial ultrasound showed signs of brain injury (Figure 4). Osteo‐oto‐hepato‐enteric (O2HE) syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. In 2018, Clothilde Esteve et al., identified biallelic UNC45A mutations in four patients from three families presenting with congenital cholestasis, diarrhea, recurrent fractures, and deafness, and formally proposed the term O2HE (Esteve et al. 2018). UNC45A encodes a myosin co‐chaperone, and mutations or deletions in this gene can impair myosin function. In our patient, the predominant postnatal manifestation was isolated congenital refractory diarrhea, prompting us to focus on the gastrointestinal consequences of UNC45A dysfunction. Previous studies have demonstrated a unidirectional link between UNC45A and myosin Vb expression, suggesting that intestinal symptoms in O2HE may result from disrupted myosin‐Vb–dependent processes (Goulet et al. 2022) (Li et al. 2022). Furthermore, as a regulator of myosin Vb, impaired UNC45A expression may contribute to the variable and complex clinical features seen in microvillus inclusion disease and related conditions (Banal et al. 2023).

The patient in our case was a premature infant born to a mother with a history of multiple unexplained miscarriages. Prenatal ultrasound revealed polyhydramnios and diffuse bowel dilation, initially suggesting a diagnosis of congenital chloride diarrhea (CCD). However, serum electrolyte levels, including potassium, sodium, and chloride, remained within normal limits throughout the clinical course. Brain imaging also revealed signs of injury, and cholestasis gradually emerged, raising doubts about the initial diagnosis. Genetic testing ultimately confirmed O2HE. CCD is caused by autosomal recessive mutations in the SLC26A3 gene and typically presents with watery diarrhea, hypochloremia, and metabolic alkalosis (Doğan et al. 2020), Prenatal ultrasound in CCD often shows polyhydramnios and intestinal dilation, which may lead to premature delivery and significant postnatal abdominal distension (Husu et al. 2001). Therefore, CCD is frequently considered when such prenatal findings are present (Kawamura and Nishiguchi 2017).

In clinical settings, diagnostic reasoning often prioritizes common disorders, and rare conditions like O2HE are easily overlooked. In this case, the mother declined prenatal genetic testing due to concerns about the risks associated with amniocentesis or cord blood sampling, which delayed definitive diagnosis. This highlights the importance of early and effective communication between healthcare providers and families. Improved prenatal genetic evaluation may facilitate timely differentiation between O2HE and CCD in neonates presenting with severe diarrhea, enabling more accurate and individualized management.

To date, only 12 cases of O2HE associated with loss‐of‐function UNC45A mutations have been reported (Table 2), with severe diarrhea being the most consistent feature, followed by neonatal cholestasis. Currently, no standardized treatment guidelines exist; management typically involves a combination of parenteral and enteral nutrition. Our patient gradually transitioned to oral formula feeding, with subsequent improvement in diarrhea and weight gain. Literature review indicates that nearly half of the reported cases exhibited bone fragility and hearing loss, with additional manifestations developing during growth (Esteve et al. 2018). The presence of cholestasis in our patient before discharge further supports this disease profile. Brain injury has also been documented in neonatal O2HE cases (Kong et al. 2023), and our patient showed complex ultrasound features consistent with this. Among the 12 published cases, four were premature, suggesting that UNC45A mutations may contribute to preterm birth and even recurrent miscarriage.

Early fetal genetic testing is thus crucial for timely diagnosis and can help avoid mismanagement of affected neonates. As our findings are based on a single case, further research and case accumulation are needed to expand the genetic understanding of O2HE and to inform clinical decision‐making.

In conclusion, the diagnosis of O2HE requires a high index of suspicion, particularly in the presence of early‐onset refractory diarrhea, atypical laboratory findings, and prenatal ultrasound abnormalities. This case underscores the critical role of genetic testing in distinguishing O2HE from similar neonatal disorders such as CCD. Strengthening prenatal diagnostic approaches and accumulating clinical experience are key to improving the recognition and management of this rare syndrome.
