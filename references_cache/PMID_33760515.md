# Kleine-Levin Syndrome (KLS).
**Authors:** Sharma A, Sankari A

## Content

Kleine-Levin syndrome (KLS) represents a rare neuropsychiatric disorder that primarily affects teenagers and manifests as relapsing-remitting episodes of hypersomnia accompanied by cognitive and behavioral symptoms. The disorder classically presents with hypersomnolence and cognitive and behavioral dysregulation, which may include hyperphagia and hypersexuality. During symptomatic periods, patients may sleep for the majority of the day and exhibit marked mental slowing, impaired attention and memory, derealization, irritability, apathy, or disinhibition. Furthermore, perceptual disturbances, confusion, and impaired executive functioning have also been reported. Although clinicians traditionally describe hyperphagia and hypersexuality as components of the classic symptom triad with hypersomnolence, neither feature is required for diagnosis. The episodic course represents the most informative clinical characteristic, with somnolent attacks lasting from days to weeks followed by near-complete or complete recovery between episodes. This relapsing-remitting pattern can help distinguish KLS from idiopathic hypersomnia, narcolepsy, and primary mood disorders. See "Sleep Disorder," "Idiopathic Hypersomnia," and "Narcolepsy" for further information. However, the same episodic pattern can contribute to delayed recognition and misdiagnosis, particularly when early episodes are attributed to psychiatric illness. Most KLS cases occur sporadically, although familial cases with similar clinical phenotypes have been described. The median age of onset is 15 years, with reported cases ranging from 4 to 82 years and the majority presenting during the second decade of life. Adolescence remains the prototypical period of onset and should heighten clinical suspicion in teenagers and young adults who develop recurrent hypersomnolence accompanied by behavioral changes. Males represent the predominant affected population, with an approximately 2:1 ratio relative to females. Although apathy and derealization occur frequently across patients, reported sex-based differences include a longer disease course and lower frequency of hypersexuality among women, along with a higher frequency of depressed mood compared with men. The etiology and pathophysiology of KLS remain incompletely elucidated. The typical age of onset, relapsing-remitting disease course, and infectious prodrome reported in many patients have prompted hypotheses involving autoimmune or postinfectious mechanisms. Reported preceding infections have included flu-like illnesses, nonspecific fever, upper respiratory infections, and gastroenteritis. Diagnosis requires a detailed clinical history documenting recurrent episodes of excessive daytime sleepiness lasting days to weeks, recovery that reaches a complete or near-complete baseline between episodes, and associated neuropsychiatric symptoms. KLS represents a diagnosis of exclusion, requiring clinicians to evaluate alternative causes, including other central disorders of hypersomnolence, metabolic and neurologic conditions, substance-related causes, and primary psychiatric disorders. A comprehensive clinical review of KLS, therefore, encompasses current knowledge of its proposed etiology, clinical presentation, diagnostic evaluation, and treatment, based on the available literature.
