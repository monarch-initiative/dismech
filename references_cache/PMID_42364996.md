# Structural basis for multivitamin recognition and transport by human SMVT.
**Authors:** Zhen Q, Wang M, Zhang Z
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-74948-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-74948-3)

## Content

The human sodium-dependent multivitamin transporter (SMVT, SLC5A6) mediates cellular uptake of essential metabolic cofactors, including biotin, pantothenate, and lipoate. Its dysfunction is associated with neurological disorders, metabolic abnormalities, and cancer. However, the molecular mechanism underlying its multi-substrate transport has remained elusive. Here, we present cryo-electron microscopy structures of human SMVT in three conformational states: occluded, outward-open, and inward-open. These structural snapshots capture the complete transport cycle and reveal a conserved substrate-binding pocket near a kinked transmembrane helix (TM1). Within this pocket, substrate carboxyl groups are electrostatically anchored, while distinct chemical moieties interact with specific polar and hydrophobic residues. Functional assays identify key binding residues and elucidate the pathogenic effects of disease-associated mutations. Further structural analysis delineates the principles of substrate discrimination within the SLC5 transporter family. Together, our work provides a structural framework for SMVT's polyspecificity and lays a foundation for understanding related diseases and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

Solute carrier (SLC) transporters are integral membrane proteins that facilitate the movement of diverse ions, nutrients, metabolites, and drugs across cellular membranes1,2. Among these, the SLC5 family, also known as the sodium/solute symporter family, utilizes the electrochemical sodium gradient to drive substrate uptake and plays a critical role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis3. A prominent member of this family, SLC5A6, commonly referred to as the sodium-dependent multivitamin transporter (SMVT), is distinguished by its capacity to transport several essential water-soluble micronutrients: biotin (vitamin B7), pantothenic acid (vitamin B5), and lipoic acid4,5. These substrates serve as indispensable cofactors in central metabolic pathways. Biotin acts as a prosthetic group for carboxylases involved in gluconeogenesis, fatty acid synthesis, and amino acid metabolism6. D-pantothenic acid (D-PA) is a precursor of coenzyme A, essential for energy metabolism and biosynthesis7. α-lipoic acid (α-LA) functions both as a critical mitochondrial coenzyme in oxidative decarboxylation reactions and as a potent antioxidant8. Given the pleiotropic roles of these micronutrients, knockout of the Slc5a6 gene leads to premature death in mice9,10, and dysregulation of human SMVT has been implicated in a range of pathological conditions. Specifically, impaired transport function is associated with neurological deficits, developmental abnormalities, metabolic acidosis, and inflammatory conditions11; whereas its overexpression contributes to the progression of multiple cancers12–15. These associations highlight the physiological and clinical significance of SMVT.

Previous structural studies of other SLC5 transporters, such as the sodium-glucose cotransporters SGLT1 (SLC5A1)16–18 and SGLT2 (SLC5A2)18–21, the sodium-iodide symporter NIS (SLC5A5)22, the choline transporter CHT1 (SLC5A7)23–25, and the sodium-coupled monocarboxylate transporter SMCT1 (SLC5A8)16, have revealed conserved mechanisms of sodium-coupled transport and alternating access within the family. Although three decades of functional studies have delineated the transport kinetics, substrate specificity, and regulatory mechanisms of SMVT4,26–30, the specific structural features that enable SMVT to recognize and transport multiple chemically distinct micronutrients remain unclear, hindering the rational development of SMVT-targeted therapeutics for applications such as drug delivery31 or the treatment of metabolic diseases.

Here, we report cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of human SMVT in three conformational states: an occluded state in complex with each of its three primary substrates (biotin, D-PA, or oxidized α-LA), an outward-open state, and an inward-open state (both without bound vitamin substrates). Our structural data, validated by complementary functional assays, reveal the precise coordination chemistry of the substrate-binding pocket and illuminate the molecular mechanism underlying multi-substrate recognition by SMVT via a shared binding site. Furthermore, comparative analyses with other SLC5 family members provide insight into the structural determinants of substrate specificity and preference within the subfamily. Together, these findings establish a mechanistic framework for understanding SMVT function and offer a foundation for developing targeted modulators of its transport activity.

To address the challenges in determining the structure of SMVT, a relatively small membrane protein with a molecular weight of approximately 70 kDa, we implemented an ALFA-tag-based strategy32,33. Specifically, we replaced the extracellular loop EL3 (residues 484-516) between TM11 and TM12 with a stable, short α-helix known as the ALFA tag (Fig. 1a). The functional integrity of SMVTEM was first verified through the [3H]-biotin uptake assays in HEK293T cells (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figs. 1a, b and 2a, b). The results demonstrated that the engineered construct retained significant transport activity, albeit with moderately reduced efficiency compared to the wild-type (WT) protein: SMVTEM exhibited a Kt (Michaelis constant) of 48.4 μM and a Vmax of 1.3 pmol/mg/min, versus the WT’s Kt of 72.3 μM and Vmax of 3.8 pmol/mg/min. These kinetic parameters align with previously reported values for SMVT4,26,27,30, confirming that the modification resulted in a functional construct suitable for structural studies.

We subsequently purified the SMVTEM/NbALFA complex (Supplementary Fig. 1c) in NaCl-based buffer and determined the cryo-EM structure of its biotin-bound form at a resolution of 3.4 Å (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). Although critical for particle alignment in early data processing, the flexibility of the engineered ALFA tag and bound NbALFA relative to the SMVT core limited the overall resolution of the EM map. These regions were therefore masked out during the final refinement to improve the map quality of the transporter (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). The structure reveals that SMVT adopts the conserved LeuT fold34,35 characteristic of the SLC5 transporter family, featuring 13 transmembrane helices (TM0-TM12) with an extracellular N-terminus and an intracellular C-terminus (Fig. 1d). The core domain, formed by TM1-TM10, is organized into two inverted pseudo-symmetric repeats (TM1-TM5 and TM6-TM10). Within these repeats, TM1 and TM6 each contain a pronounced kink, dividing them into two shorter helical segments (TM1a/1b and TM6a/6b) (Fig. 1a, d), a structural motif essential for substrate binding and transport activity34. The auxiliary helices TM0, TM11, and TM12 are situated peripherally: TM0 packs against TM4, TM5, and TM8, while TM11 and TM12 interact with TM3, TM6, and TM10 (Fig. 1d). Biotin is bound within the central cavity of SMVT (Fig. 1d, e). Consistent with most other substrate-bound SLC5 family members18,22,24, biotin binding stabilizes SMVT in an occluded conformation that is sealed from both sides of the membrane (Fig. 1e).

While the core region of biotin is well defined in the cryo-EM map, the density for its terminal carboxyl group is weaker (Fig. 2b), indicative of either greater local conformational flexibility or potential radiation damage to this acidic moiety under the electron beam. Structurally, SMVT recognizes biotin through specialized chemical environments that accommodate different regions of the substrate (Fig. 2c). The carboxyl group inserts into the kink of TM1, engaging in electrostatic interactions with the main-chain amino groups of Ser81 and Ala84, and forming a hydrogen bond with the phenolic hydroxyl group of Tyr156. Its negative charge is further stabilized by the electric dipole of TM1b. The break in TM1 exposes the partially positively charged N-terminus of TM1b within the membrane, creating an ideal binding site for the carboxyl group and underscoring the functional importance of the kinked TM1 architecture. The valeric acid substituent and the sulfur-containing tetrahydrothiophene ring of biotin are nestled within a hydrophobic pocket lined by Val83, Met266, Met267, Leu270, Leu424, Ala427, Ile428, and Phe431. Meanwhile, the polar ureido group of the heterobicyclic ring participates in multiple stabilizing interactions, including hydrogen bonds with Gln80, Tyr99, Tyr106, and Gln301, as well as van der Waals contacts with Leu102 and Leu305 (Fig. 2c). Collectively, these structural observations reveal a finely tuned mechanism for biotin recognition by SMVT: the negatively charged carboxyl group appears to be anchored within an electrostatically complementary region, the hydrophobic core is enveloped in a nonpolar niche, and the ureido group is stabilized through specific polar interactions—each moiety is accommodated by matching surrounding residues.

We next performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to verify the binding pose of biotin (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). In three independent 300-ns replicates, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) values of both the protein and the substrate remained below 2.0 Å (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Key interactions were stably maintained throughout the simulations: the carboxylate group of biotin formed electrostatic interactions with the main chains of Ser81 and Ala84, as well as hydrogen bond to the side chain of Tyr156 (Supplementary Figs. 4d, g and 5). Furthermore, the surrounding polar interactions (e.g., with Gln80, Tyr99, Tyr106) and hydrophobic contacts (e.g., with Phe79, Val83, Leu270, Ala427, Ile428, Phe431) also remained largely stable (Supplementary Fig. 4d, g). These findings support the specificity and reliability of the biotin-binding pose in our structural model.

To functionally validate the biotin-binding site identified in our structural model, we conducted alanine-scanning mutagenesis on key residues and evaluated the effects using [3H]-biotin transport assays (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Activity profiling revealed varying degrees of transport impairment among the mutants. Notably, the F79A, Y156A, M267A, and I428A mutations resulted in the most pronounced functional defects, reducing transport activity to levels comparable to the negative control (Fig. 2d). Similarly, alanine substitutions at Met266, Leu270, Gln301, and Leu424 also severely compromised SMVT activity (Fig. 2d). Thus, the broad functional disruption caused by mutations across various residues supports the conclusion that substrate recognition relies on a cooperative interaction network. Taken together, these mutagenesis results strongly corroborate the biotin-binding site identified in our cryo-EM structure, thereby establishing a robust structure-function correlation for SMVT.

To understand how SMVT accommodates structurally diverse substrates, we next investigated its interactions with D-PA and α-LA. Although both oxidized and reduced α-LA are known substrates of SMVT, the oxidized form is approximately 3-fold more potent26,27. Given that the oxidized form is also more stable extracellularly and likely represents the physiologically relevant uptake species, we specifically used oxidized α-LA in our study. Both D-PA and oxidized α-LA effectively inhibited [3H]-biotin transport mediated by SMVT, with IC50 values of 89.7 μM and 9.1 μM, respectively (Fig. 3a, b), confirming their role as authentic substrates. Using the SMVTEM/NbALFA strategy, we determined cryo-EM structures of SMVT in complex with D-PA or oxidized α-LA at resolutions of 3.7 Å and 4.1 Å, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7 and Supplementary Table 1). Both substrates bound in an occluded conformation closely resembling the biotin-bound state (Fig. 3c, d), with a global backbone RMSD below 1.0 Å (Fig. 3e). The observed densities for each substrate within the central binding cavity substantially overlapped with the biotin site (Fig. 3c–e). Given the conserved location of the binding site, D-PA and oxidized α-LA share many interactions with residues previously implicated in biotin recognition (Fig. 3f, g). Their carboxyl groups form electrostatic interactions with the main-chain amino groups of Ser81 and Ala84 and a hydrogen bond with Tyr156. Their head groups are coordinated by a polar triad consisting of Gln80, Tyr106, and Gln301. In particular, the terminal hydroxyl group of the pantoic acid moiety in D-PA forms hydrogen bonds with these residues (Fig. 3f), while the dithiolane ring of oxidized α-LA engages mainly through van der Waals interactions (Fig. 3g). The core region of D-PA packs against a hydrophobic residue set similar to that of biotin. Its more branched structure permits additional contacts with residues including Tyr271 and Thr366 (Fig. 3f). Furthermore, its amide nitrogen establishes a hydrogen bond with the main-chain carbonyl group of Phe79. In contrast, oxidized α-LA appears to interact with fewer residues, likely owing to its smaller molecular size (206 Da, compared to 244 Da for biotin and 219 Da for D-PA) and more linear conformation (Fig. 3g).

Overall, our structures reveal a conserved binding mode across all three substrates (Fig. 3e): the negatively charged carboxyl group is oriented toward one side of the pocket, and a characteristic head group occupies the opposite end, with both termini adopting nearly identical positions. Flexible carbon chains bridge these moieties, allowing sufficient adaptability to accommodate structural variations. Thus, SMVT recognizes each substrate through a combination of shared molecular shape and chemical properties—including an acidic carboxyl terminus and a complementary arrangement of hydrophobic and polar regions.

Given that the three substrates occupy the same binding site, we sought to verify that the corresponding EM densities indeed originate from the supplemented substrates rather than from endogenous molecules or purification reagents. To this end, we attempted to determine a cryo-EM structure of SMVT prepared in a NaCl-based buffer without any of the three substrates. However, the resulting structure could only be determined at a low resolution of 4.3 Å (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 1), suggesting that substrate binding likely stabilizes the transporter; in its absence, the protein exhibits conformational flexibility that limits high-resolution reconstruction. Nevertheless, only a small, isolated patch of density was observed in the substrate-binding site, which cannot accommodate substrate (Supplementary Fig. 9a), confirming that the densities in the substrate-bound structures are specific and reliable.

We note that the EM map exhibited blurred densities in the extracellular region of TM10, which forms part of the extracellular gate (Supplementary Fig. 9b), indicating possible conformational flexibility in this area. To further explore this, we performed 3D variability analysis in cryoSPARC36. The results suggest that the extracellular portion of TM10 is likely dynamic, with two representative conformations observed at the extremes of the motion (Supplementary Fig. 8a). One conformation closely resembles the occluded state (Fig. 4a), while the other appears to feature a more open extracellular gate, consistent with an outward-open arrangement (Fig. 4b, c). In this latter conformation, the overall structure remains highly similar to the occluded state (RMSD of 1.0 Å), except that the extracellular end of TM10 shifts outward by approximately 10 Å (Fig. 4d), thereby maintaining extracellular access to the substrate-binding site (Fig. 4c). The poorly defined EM densities in this region raise the possibility that sodium binding alone may allow TM10 to sample both outward-open and occluded states. This inherent flexibility might facilitate substrate entry, and once substrates occupy the binding site, TM10—and the entire SMVT structure—becomes stabilized in the occluded conformation. The observed motion of TM10 recapitulates the conformational changes seen in CHT1 and SGLT1/2 during transport16–18,20,23,24. Although 150 mM sodium was present during sample preparation, our cryo-EM maps did not reveal clear density features corresponding to sodium ions (Fig. 5b, c), likely due to the limited resolution of the reconstructions. To functionally validate these predicted sodium-binding sites, we mutated critical coordinating residues to alanine and evaluated their effects on [3H]-biotin uptake. The results showed that all mutants significantly impaired SMVT transport activity, with the S365A mutation exhibiting the most drastic functional deficiencies (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 2b), consistent with its dual role in coordinating both sodium-binding sites. These results confirm the presence of two functional sodium-binding sites in SMVT that are essential for substrate transport.

Our previous structures indicate that sodium binding enables SMVT to access both outward-open and occluded conformations, with subsequent substrate entry stabilizing the occluded state. Mechanistically, release of sodium and substrate is expected to trigger a conformational change towards an inward-open state, corresponding to a post-translocation condition. To capture this state, we purified SMVT in a KCl-based buffer in the absence of substrate and determined its cryo-EM structure at 3.7 Å resolution (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 1). The resulting structure adopted a stable inward-open conformation (Fig. 6b, c). Compared to the occluded state, the intracellular gate—formed by TM0, TM1, TM4, TM5, TM8, and TM9—underwent pronounced conformational rearrangements upon opening (Fig. 6d–f). Near the substrate-binding site, the intracellular half of the kinked TM1 (TM1a) moved outward by 5–6 Å, accompanied by a 3–4 Å outward shift of TM8, collectively creating an exit pathway for substrate release (Fig. 6c, f). Toward the intracellular side, TM0, TM4, and TM5 also shifted outward by about 5–7 Å. As a result, the intracellular loop IL1H (between TM0 and TM1), which normally acts as a plug to seal the gate through extensive interactions, became disordered (Fig. 6g). Together, these structural changes fully open the intracellular vestibule. The substantial movements of TM1, TM5, and TM8 also disrupted the local geometry of residues coordinating the two putative sodium-binding sites (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 11b), likely facilitating sodium release. This release may serve as the initial trigger for the conformational transition from the occluded to the inward-open state. Overall, the intracellular gating mechanism echoes that reported for CHT1 and SGLT1/216–18,20,23,24, underscoring a conserved functional architecture in the SLC5 family.

The other six mutants were expressed normally but exhibited varying degrees of reduced biotin transport activity (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Specifically, C443Y and Q214H showed the most profound functional deficits, while the other four mutations led to moderate impairment. Notably, Ser74 lies near the Na3-binding site and adjacent to Asp203 (Fig. 7c); mutation to phenylalanine may disrupt the local architecture, leading to structural destabilization and misfolding. Our study elucidates the structural and mechanistic basis of multivitamin transport by SMVT. The transport cycle commences with sodium binding, which allows SMVT to equilibrate between outward-open and occluded conformations, thereby facilitating substrate uptake from the extracellular environment. Substrate binding then stabilizes the occluded state and promotes the subsequent transition to an inward-open conformation. Beyond this conserved transport mechanism shared across the SLC5 family, our findings also provide important insights into the broad substrate specificity exhibited by its members. The family comprises 12 transporters (SLC5A1-A12) with remarkable functional diversity: the SGLT branch (SLC5A1, A2, A9, A10) primarily transports monosaccharides such as glucose, mannose, or fructose; SMITs (SLC5A3, A11) are dedicated to myo-inositol transport; NIS (SLC5A5) mediates iodide uptake; SMVT (SLC5A6) transports vitamins; CHT1 (SLC5A7) is specific for choline; and SMCTs (SLC5A8, A12) carry short-chain fatty acids3. Despite their evolutionarily conserved architecture, the molecular determinants underlying such diverse substrate selectivity within the SLC5 family have remained an open question.

Comparative analysis of substrate-bound structures across SLC5 members reveals that all substrates bind within a conserved pocket near the kinks in TM1 and TM6 (Fig. 9a). Although this general location is preserved, the specific chemical environment of each transporter dictates how distinct substrates are accommodated (Fig. 9a, b). For instance, SGLT1/2 utilize a hydrophilic pocket to engage polar sugars18,21, whereas CHT1 employs aromatic residues to coordinate positively charged choline via cation-π interactions23–25. Even among transporters of anionic substrates—SMVT, NIS, and SMCT1—recognition mechanisms differ. In SMVT, vitamins bind parallel to the membrane, with their carboxyl group inserted into the TM1 kink and stabilized by electrostatic interactions with main-chain amino groups and the electric dipole of TM1b. In contrast, iodide in NIS binds within a hydrophobic pocket closer to the extracellular side22. Butyrate bound to SMCT1 adopts a vertical orientation relative to the membrane, with its carboxyl group facing the intracellular side and forming a hydrogen bond with Gln26316. Notably, the SMCT1-butyrate structure was solved in an inward-facing state16—distinct from the occluded conformations observed in other members—potentially representing a transient release intermediate. Butyrate may adopt a different pose in outward-facing or occluded states. Strikingly, the TM1 kink in SLC5 family members is generally positioned closer to the substrate-binding site than the TM6 kink. This region also displays relatively low sequence conservation across the family, particularly at the two terminal residues, whose distinct physicochemical properties are likely major determinants of substrate selectivity (Fig. 9c). For example, SMVT and SMCT1 feature a phenylalanine paired with a small side-chain residue (Ala84 or Thr70)16, which may facilitate the accommodation of bulky carboxyl groups within the kink. In NIS, a polar glutamine (Gln72) replaces the small residue at the onset of TM1b—a substitution critical for coordinating iodide22. CHT1 contains two aromatic residues at these positions, optimizing the site for choline binding via cation-π interactions23–25. In SGLT transporters, two polar residues (Asn78/His83 in SGLT1 and Asn75/His80 in SGLT2) directly interact with sugar substrates18,21 (Fig. 9b, c). Furthermore, these residues undergo conformational changes during transport: in proteins such as CHT1, SMVT, and NIS, they contribute to regulating intracellular gating access22–25, whereas in SGLTs, they participate in modulating substrate binding16,18,21 (Fig. 9b). Together, these findings underscore the pivotal role of the TM1 kink in governing substrate specificity and transport mechanisms throughout the SLC5 family.

Biotin conjugation has been widely employed to improve drug delivery by exploiting efficient cellular biotin uptake systems31,46. As the principal membrane transporter for biotin, SMVT represents a promising target for delivering biotin-drug conjugates. However, both previous studies26,30,46 and our data indicate that a free carboxylic acid group in biotin is a critical feature for its recognition by SMVT. Conjugation via the carboxylic acid moiety—for example, through formation of an amide or ester linkage—would therefore likely diminish transport activity. To harness SMVT for effective delivery, biotin-drug conjugates should instead be modified at alternative sites, such as the valeric acid side chain or the heterocyclic ring, to preserve molecular recognition and transport efficiency. The high-resolution structure of the SMVT-biotin complex provides a crucial foundation for the rational design of such conjugates in future therapeutic applications.

The initial coordinates for the simulations were taken from the cryo‑EM structure of SMVTEM solved in this work, which includes both the protein and the bound ligand. Two sodium ions were placed into the putative binding sites. To complete the missing loop (residues 478-521) not resolved in the density map, the structure was supplemented with the AlphaFold 3‑predicted model54. Specifically, the predicted structure was aligned to the experimental model using the conserved regions flanking the gap as a guide, and the coordinates of the corresponding loop were then transferred to the experimental coordinates. The protein-membrane complex was built with the Membrane Builder module of CHARMM‑GUI60,61. The transmembrane orientation of the protein was assigned by the PPM server62. To recapitulate the native lipid environment, a symmetric POPC bilayer was used, with the same number of lipids in the two leaflets; the lateral box dimensions were set to extend ~30 Å beyond the protein in the membrane plane. The CHARMM36m force field was applied to protein63, ligand parameters were obtained from CGenFF, and the CHARMM all‑atom lipid force field was used for the bilayer. The system was solvated with TIP3P water molecules on both sides of the membrane64, and ions (sodium or chloride) were added to neutralize the net charge of the system. Final topology and parameter files for Amber were generated with CHARMM‑GUI65,66. The simulation system for the SMVT-biotin complex was constructed in a tetragonal box with dimensions of 96.12 × 96.12 × 135.93 Å3. The system comprised 116,432 atoms, including 26,785 TIP3P water molecules and 206 POPC lipid molecules. The SMVT-D-PA and SMVT-α-LA complexes were constructed using an identical protocol, with minor variations in atom counts and box dimensions resulting from the specific protein-ligand conformations.

A conventional MD equilibration and production workflow was employed. The system was first energy‑minimized while position restraints were applied to protein heavy atoms, the ligand, and lipid molecules, with force constants of 10, 10, and 2.5 kcal/mol/Å2, respectively. The temperature was then raised from 0 to 300 K over 250 ps. Subsequent density equilibration was carried out under constant pressure (1 atm) while the restraints were gradually released. After equilibration, a 300 ns production run was performed in the NPT ensemble. The Langevin thermostat (collision frequency 1/ps) maintained the temperature, and the pressure was controlled with an anisotropic Monte Carlo barostat. A 1 fs time step was used during heating and equilibration, and 2 fs during production. Non‑bonded interactions were cut off at 12 Å; van der Waals forces were smoothly switched off between 10 and 12 Å, and long‑range electrostatics were treated with the particle‑mesh Ewald (PME) method. Bonds involving hydrogen were constrained with the SHAKE algorithm67. All simulations were run with Amber2268.

Trajectories were processed and analyzed with the cpptraj module of AmberTools. System stability and convergence were monitored by calculating the RMSD of the protein backbone and the ligand as a function of time. Following the initial 150 ns, all systems reached equilibrium, as evidenced by stable RMSD plateaus (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c), and the protein-ligand complexes maintained structurally homogeneous conformations for the remainder of each simulation. The snapshots at 150 ns were therefore selected as representative structures for subsequent analysis. To evaluate the stability of substrate binding within the SMVT pocket, we performed time-dependent distance analyses of the interactions between the substrate carboxylate group and key residues (Ser81, Ala84, and Tyr156). The distances between the two carboxylate oxygen atoms and the functional groups, specifically the backbone amide (N) and side-chain hydroxyl oxygen (OG) of Ser81, the backbone amide (N) of Ala84, and the phenolic hydroxyl oxygen (OH) of Tyr156, were calculated throughout the 300 ns MD trajectories using cpptraj. To quantify ligand‑binding affinities, MM/GBSA calculations were performed on the equilibrated 150–200 ns window of each 300 ns trajectory69. The calculations used the igb = 5 implicit solvent model and a salt concentration of 0.1 M; per‑residue energy decomposition was also carried out to identify key interaction sites. For each protein-ligand combination, three independent replicate simulations were run, and the resulting binding energies are reported as mean ± s.d. It should be noted that conformational entropy contributions were omitted in the MM/GBSA calculations. Therefore, the reported values represent effective binding energies rather than absolute thermodynamic free energies and were used primarily for per-residue energy decomposition to identify key structural hotspots.
