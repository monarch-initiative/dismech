Dear Editor,

Human monkeypox (Mpox) is a zoonotic infection, caused by the Mpox virus, a double-stranded DNA virus belonging to theOrthopoxgenus of the Poxviridae family. It was first reported in humans in the Democratic Republic of Congo in 1970 and outbreaks have been described mainly in African countries with isolated cases and limited outbreaks in nonendemic countries linked to travel to endemic regions.[1]Nevertheless, a multicountry Mpox outbreak in more than 80 nonendemic regions began in May 2022, mostly affecting men who have sex with men (MSM). The World Health Organization declared this outbreak to be an international public health emergency on July 23, 2022.[1]

The typical clinical syndrome of Mpox is characterized by fever, rash, skin lesions, and lymphadenopathy. However, available evidence also describes the occurrence of several respiratory manifestations and complications. Respiratory symptoms, including cough, shortness of breath, chest pain, and dyspnea, have been reported in 12%–78% of Mpox cases.[1-3]Given the increasing number of cases being reported in the current outbreak, health-care workers, particularly in the inpatient setting, should be aware of these symptoms as possible indicators of infection with Mpox.

Acute tonsillitis is a common symptom in the early stage of Mpox, and cough with or without sputum production is frequently an associated symptom.[4]In some cases of Mpox acute tonsillitis, bulky cervical lymphadenopathy has been associated with respiratory distress and a worsened clinical course.

A poorly characterized, but potentially severe, complication of Mpox infection is bronchopneumonia. The development of focal pulmonary necrosis, diffuse pulmonary consolidation, and fulminant bronchopneumonia in nonhuman primates exposed to Mpox has been repeatedly demonstrated.[3]Jezeket al. reported that bronchopneumonia with respiratory distress occurred in 11.7% and 11% of animal and human infections, respectively, occurring primarily in the late stage of the illness. In humans, the incidence of (bronchopneumonia/respiratory distress/both) was higher among unvaccinated patients.[4,5]In the current outbreak in nonendemic countries, data from a multicenter study in the US reported pneumonitis in 11% of Mpox cases among people living with HIV (PLWH) as opposed to the population without HIV where there were no reported cases of pneumonitis, suggesting that respiratory complications could be more frequent in those with HIV.[6]

Although the impact of secondary respiratory infection in the current outbreak is still unclear, animal studies suggest that bacterial superinfection can exacerbate pulmonary manifestations of illness and result in severe outcomes, including death.[3]In a descriptive case series of Mpox reported from London, a superimposed bacterial lower respiratory tract infection was reported in one immunocompromised patient.[7]Coinfection with respiratory viruses, including SARS-CoV-2 and influenza, has been reported, especially in immunocompromised patients.[8]Such coinfections can aggravate respiratory symptoms and predispose patients to secondary bacterial infections.

Given that Mpox infection has been associated with potentially severe respiratory complications, close attention to respiratory status is essential. Monitoring should include heart rate, respiratory rate, and pulse oximetry measurements. In some cases, incentive spirometry, chest physiotherapy, supplemental oxygen, and respiratory support may be indicated. Antibiotic therapy is indicated if a superimposed bacterial infection is proven or suspected.

Tecovirimat, an Food and Drug Administration-approved antiviral drug for the treatment of human smallpox disease, is currently the recommended treatment for respiratory complications of Mpox infection, including bronchopneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome.[9]This mediation inhibits the VP37 envelope-wrapping protein ofOrthopoxviruses. It is available as an oral capsule (200 mg) and an injection for intravenous presentations and should be administrated early in the course of illness along with supportive care and pain control. Other therapies, such as cidofovir and vaccinia immune globulin, have more concerning safety profiles and are not recommended at this time.[9]

Mpox is typically self-limited, but respiratory complications can occur, especially in nonvaccinated and immunocompromised individuals. Given the ongoing worldwide outbreak, awareness of these manifestations is critical to help identify potential severe Mpox infection. Early recognition may provide an opportunity to intervene and begin appropriate therapy before the onset of severe respiratory compromise. Long-term sequelae also should be considered in Mpox cases, but follow-up studies are needed to define the occurrence, duration, and severity of pulmonary and other potential sequelae of illness. In addition, high-risk groups, such as MSM and PLWH, should be prioritized for Mpox vaccination, in an effort to prevent the occurrence of severe cases and deaths.
