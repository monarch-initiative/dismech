# Subtypes of dementia with Lewy bodies are associated with α-synuclein and tau distribution.
**Authors:** Ferman TJ, Aoki N, Boeve BF, Aakre JA, Kantarci K, Graff-Radford J, Parisi JE, Van Gerpen JA, Graff-Radford NR, Uitti RJ, Pedraza O, Murray ME, Wszolek ZK, Reichard RR, Fields JA, Ross OA, Knopman DS, Petersen RC, Dickson DW
**Journal:** Neurology (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1212/WNL.0000000000009763](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000009763)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To determine whether Lewy body disease subgroups have different clinical profiles.

METHODS: Participants had dementia, autopsy-confirmed transitional or diffuse Lewy body disease (TLBD or DLBD) (n = 244), or Alzheimer disease (AD) (n = 210), and were seen at least twice (mean follow-up 6.2 ± 3.8 years). TLBD and DLBD groups were partitioned based on the presence or absence of neocortical neurofibrillary tangles using Braak staging. Four Lewy body disease subgroups and AD were compared on clinical features, dementia trajectory, and onset latency of probable dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) or a DLB syndrome defined as probable DLB or dementia with one core feature of parkinsonism or probable REM sleep behavior disorder.

RESULTS: In TLBD and DLBD without neocortical tangles, diagnostic sensitivity was strong for probable DLB (87% TLBD, 96% DLBD) and the DLB syndrome (97% TLBD, 98% DLBD) with median latencies <1 year from cognitive onset, and worse baseline attention-visual processing but better memory-naming scores than AD. In DLBD with neocortical tangles, diagnostic sensitivity was 70% for probable DLB and 77% for the DLB syndrome with respective median latencies of 3.7 years and 2.7 years from cognitive onset, each associated with tangle distribution. This group had worse baseline attention-visual processing than AD, but comparable memory-naming impairment. TLBD with neocortical tangles had 48% diagnostic sensitivity for probable DLB and 52% for the DLB syndrome, with median latencies >6 years from cognitive onset, and were cognitively similar to AD. Dementia trajectory was slowest for TLBD without neocortical tangles, and fastest for DLBD with neocortical tangles.

CONCLUSIONS: The phenotypic expression of DLB was associated with the distribution of α-synuclein and tau pathology.

The primary pathologic feature of dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) consists of aggregated α-synuclein in neurons and cell processes that is either confined to brainstem and limbic regions or extends to neocortical regions. Adding to this pathologic heterogeneity, it is estimated that over 50% of those with DLB also have neocortical tau and β-amyloid deposition.1–3 Although it is well-established that DLB is part of a spectrum, it is not known whether there is a clinical signature or pattern associated with pathologic subgroups. Early studies showed the likelihood of parkinsonism and visual hallucinations (VH) to be inversely related to Braak neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) stage and directly related to the distribution of Lewy body disease.4–7 This formed the basis for the DLB pathologic criteria8,9 and introduced the idea that Alzheimer disease (AD)–related pathology may diminish or “Alzheimerize” the clinical expression of DLB.10 This is supported by evidence that DLB core features are more common in so-called pure Lewy body disease than in patients with coexisting AD,11,12 but countered by findings that core DLB features frequently occur in diffuse Lewy body disease with coexisting AD.12–14 Moreover, even though the most common misdiagnosis of DLB is AD dementia,15 a recent meta-analysis showed improved diagnostic sensitivity of probable DLB to 85% using the 2005 clinical criteria in dementia care settings where coexisting AD-related pathology is more likely.15 Based on evidence that α-synuclein pathology subserves the dementia and the core features of DLB,14,16,17 and evidence that DLB with coexisting AD-related pathology is associated with diminished hippocampal integrity,18 memory impairment,19 and faster disease progression,2 differences in phenotypic expression of DLB may depend on the distribution and extent of each pathology.

Given that DLB represents a continuum of Lewy body disease and AD, we sought to determine whether pathologic subgroups differ clinically from each other and from AD. We hypothesized that DLB clinical features would be associated with more widespread α-synuclein relative to tau, while those with both α-synuclein and tau extending to the neocortex would exhibit a combination of DLB and AD features and a more rapid dementia trajectory.

Participants had an antemortem diagnosis of dementia, were seen at least twice through the Mayo Alzheimer's Disease Research Center, and had an autopsy between August 1999 and February 2018 with a pathologic diagnosis of one of the following: (1) transitional Lewy body disease (TLBD) with α-synuclein deposition limited to brainstem and limbic regions, (2) diffuse Lewy body disease (DLBD) with α-synuclein deposition in brainstem, limbic, and neocortex, or (3) AD with no α-synuclein deposition. All AD cases were high likelihood AD using National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer’s Association criteria.20 Individuals with brainstem-only or amygdala-only Lewy body disease were excluded. The sample included 244 patients with a pathologic diagnosis of Lewy body disease (93 TLBD, 151 DLBD) and 210 with a pathologic diagnosis of AD.

The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board. Participants and their proxies provided written informed consent.

Brains were sampled using a systematic and standardized protocol, and all cases underwent macroscopic and microscopic neuropathologic evaluation. Tissue sections were embedded in paraffin, and 5-mm-thick sections were mounted on glass slides for histologic examination and immunohistochemistry. All cases underwent immunohistochemistry for α-synuclein (LB509, 1:1,000, mouse monoclonal antibody [Invitrogen] or NACP, 1:3,000, rabbit polyclonal [Mayo Clinic's antibody]). Counts of Lewy bodies (at 200× magnification) were assessed in middle frontal, superior temporal, inferior parietal, cingulate, and parahippocampal cortices and the amygdala. Semiquantitative assessments of neuronal loss and Lewy-related pathology were also made in nucleus basalis of Meynert, substantia nigra, locus coeruleus, and dorsal motor nucleus of vagus. Semiquantitative Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD) scores of neuritic plaques (none, sparse, moderate, frequent) were obtained. Neuritic plaques and NFTs were identified using thioflavin-S immunohistochemistry or Bielschowsky silver stain. Braak NFT stage was determined on the basis of the distribution of NFTs and operationally defined based on the following: stage 0 had no NFTs, stage I had NFTs confined to the transentorhinal cortex, stage II had NFTs in the entorhinal cortex, stage III had NFTs in the hippocampal region (CA1 and subiculum), stage IV had NFTs in isocortex of the temporal lobe, stage V had NFTs in the temporal, frontal, or parietal association cortices, and stage VI had NFTs in the primary visual or primary motor cortices.

The Lewy body disease subgroups were sorted using a 2 × 2 matrix approach based on TLBD or DLBD and the absence or presence of neocortical NFTs. The absence of neocortical tangles was represented by a Braak NFT stage of 0, I, II, or III and categorized as low (L). The presence of neocortical tangles was represented by a Braak NFT stage of IV, V, or VI and categorized as high (H). As such, the 4 possible combinations derived from this categorical assignment included TLBD-L, DLBD-L, TLBD-H, and DLBD-H. An AD group without Lewy-related pathology served as a control group.

Each patient underwent at least 2 annual evaluations and had an informant who completed questionnaires and participated in clinical interviews. Ratings of dementia severity were based on the Global Deterioration Scale (GLDS), the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and the Dementia Rating Scale (DRS), with GLDS available for all cases, MMSE in 92% of the sample, and DRS in 88% of the sample. A diagnosis of parkinsonism was based on neurologic examination. Parkinsonism severity was quantified using the version of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale that rates 13 signs from a scale of 0–4.21 Estimated onset of cognitive impairment, visual hallucinations, and probable REM sleep behavior disorder (pRBD) were based on clinician interviews with the patient and informant. Fluctuations were based on clinician interview or a Mayo Fluctuations Scale score of 3 or 4 (latter available for 73% of TLBD-L, DLBD-L, and DLBD-H, and 40% of TLBD-H and AD).

A clinical diagnosis of probable DLB consisted of dementia and 2 or more of the following core features: VH, fluctuations, parkinsonism, or pRBD.9 Because dementia in the context of Lewy body disease is strongly associated with Parkinson disease17,22 and with RBD,23,24 we also present data for the DLB syndrome that consists of either probable DLB or dementia with one core feature of parkinsonism or pRBD.

Neuropsychological tests were selected based on their established value at distinguishing DLB from AD.25,26 We calculated a baseline composite memory and naming score using published Mayo norms to derive an average of the age-adjusted scaled scores (mean 10 ± 3) from the Logical Memory delayed recall from the Wechsler Memory Scale–Revised, Auditory Verbal Learning Test delayed recall, and the Boston Naming Test. Data from one test were available for 98% of the sample and 95% had all 3 tests. A baseline composite attention and visual processing score was calculated using published Mayo norms to derive an average of the age-adjusted scaled scores from Block design (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale–Revised), Trail-Making Test A, Rey-Osterrieth complex figure copy, and the Word subtest from the Stroop Color-Word Test. Data from one test were available for 98% of the sample (Block design for 91% of sample), and from 2 or more tests in 80% of TLBD-L, DLBD-L, and DLBD-H and 52% of AD and TLBD-H.

Categorical and continuous variables were summarized using percentages and mean ± SD, respectively. Group comparisons of categorical variables used χ2 tests or the Fisher exact test, and group comparisons of continuous variables used one-way analysis of variance and Tukey honestly significant difference post hoc comparison with p < 0.05 representing a significant difference. To determine whether the latency of probable DLB differed across pathologic subgroups, the cumulative incidence method was used with the sentinel event representing the point in time after the estimated onset of cognitive impairment that a clinical diagnosis of probable DLB could be made. The onset year of cognitive symptoms served as the index value, cause-specific failure probabilities were determined, and the Gray K-sample test was used to compare group differences. When the onset of a core feature preceded the onset of cognitive symptoms, a standard latency value of 0.001 years was assigned instead of the negative value, to indicate that the core feature was present when the cognitive difficulties began. This cumulative incidence method was also used to determine whether the latency of the DLB syndrome differed across pathologic subgroups. To examine rates of decline for global measures of dementia (GLDS, MMSE, DRS), a linear mixed effects model was conducted with fixed effects for age, education, group, time from baseline, and a group by time interaction, and with individual random intercepts and slopes to account for within-person correlation. This type of model adjusts for differences in baseline dementia severity and the number of follow-up visits, providing a more precise estimate for rate of change.27 Models allowing for time-varying slope terms were examined, but did not differ from the linear (constant slope) estimates, and therefore, only fixed-slope models are reported. Exploratory analyses examined the covariate effects on baseline scores and rates of change, and likelihood ratio tests compared a fuller model (all terms) to a model excluding each term. This revealed no significant effects on rates of change for age and education, and therefore, the final model dropped the interactions of age by time and education by time. We also tested whether group membership predicted rates of change compared to a model without using a 4 df likelihood ratio test, and since this was significant for each outcome (GLDS, MMSE, and DRS), we followed up with pairwise comparisons between the pathologic subgroups. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 or SPSS version 25.

Anonymized data will be shared by request from qualified investigators in accordance with the Mayo Alzheimer Disease Research Center data-sharing protocol.

Demographic, pathologic, and clinical characteristics of the subgroups are presented in table 1. Patients were followed for an average of 6.2 ± 3.8 years. Moderate to frequent CERAD neuritic plaques and an APOE ε4 genotype were more likely in patients with neocortical tangles. Duration of illness was shorter for DLBD compared to TLBD and AD (DLBD 8.7 ± 2.7 years vs TLBD 10.3 ± 4.4 years vs AD 10.1 ± 4.2 years, p = 0.001). Of those with a pathologic diagnosis of AD, the diagnostic specificity was 84% for probable DLB and 78% for the DLB syndrome, indicating the percentage correctly identified to not meet these clinical criteria for DLB.

In DLBD-L, diagnostic sensitivity was 96% for probable DLB and 98% for the DLB syndrome. Patients with DLBD-L were disproportionately male. There was clinical overlap among TLBD-L, DLBD-L, and DLBD-H, with earlier development of core DLB features compared to AD (table 1). Using the 1-year rule22 deemed to represent PDD, 13% (5/40) of the DLBD-L group developed motor symptoms more than a year before the estimated onset of cognitive symptoms. Those considered to have PDD in the TLBD-L and DLBD-L groups did not differ in demographics, onset of other core features, cognitive measures, baseline dementia severity, or parkinsonism severity from their counterparts without the PDD designation. Probable DLB emerged earlier in TLBD-L and DLBD-L compared to the other groups (figure 1), which was also the case for the DLB syndrome. Probable DLB was evident a median latency of 0.4 years following the estimated onset of cognitive impairment (95% CI, 0.1–1.9 years) and the DLB syndrome was coincident with estimated cognitive onset. There was no difference in onset latencies across Braak NFT stages 0 to III. In DLBD-L, the lifetime likelihood of probable DLB or the DLB syndrome did not differ across Braak NFT stages 0 to III (table 2).

Patients with DLBD-L had worse baseline attention and visual processing scores and better memory and naming scores than AD, a pattern also seen in TLBD-L. Linear mixed modeling showed no difference in GLDS rate of decline between DLBD-L and the other groups, but DLBD-L showed an annual change of −2 MMSE points, which was faster than TLBD-L (p < 0.02) and slower than DLBD-H (p < 0.01), and an annual change of −9 DRS points, which was faster than TLBD-L (p < 0.03) (table 3 and figure 2).

In DLBD-H, diagnostic sensitivity was 70% for probable DLB and 77% for the DLB syndrome. DLBD-H, TLBD-L, and DLBD-L groups were more likely to have DLB core features and an earlier onset of those features relative to the estimated onset of cognitive impairment than AD (table 1). Women and men were equally represented in the DLBD-H group, but pRBD was less commonly reported in women (29% women vs 62% men, p = 0.001), which contributed to lower rates of pRBD in DLBD-H than in TLBD-L or DLBD-L. A diagnosis of parkinsonism was also less common in DLBD-H (about a third did not have a diagnosis of parkinsonism) and only 6% (4/68) met the 1-year rule for PDD. The presence and severity of parkinsonism did not differ between men and women in the DLBD-H group. Parkinsonism severity was worse for DLBD-H than AD, but milder for DLBD-H than TLBD-L or DLBD-L (table 1).

Probable DLB was identified a median latency of 3.7 years (95% CI, 2.5–4.8 years) from estimated cognitive onset (figure 1), while the median latency for the DLB syndrome was 2.7 years (95% CI, 0.6–4.0 years). These latencies for DLBD-H differed from the other pathologic subgroups (p < 0.001). In DLBD-H, probable DLB emerged earlier for those with Braak NFT stage IV than for stages V or VI, and earlier for stage V than VI. The same pattern was observed for the DLB syndrome (figure 3). The lifetime likelihood of probable DLB did not differ between Braak NFT stages IV and V (p = 0.63), showed a trend to be more common in stage V than VI (p = 0.08), and was more likely in stage IV than VI (p = 0.03). The lifetime likelihood of the DLB syndrome did not differ between Braak NFT stages IV and V (p = 0.68), was more likely in stage V than VI (p = 0.03), and was more likely in stage IV than VI (p = 0.02) (table 2).

The DLBD-H, TLBD-L, and DLBD-L groups each demonstrated worse baseline attention and visual processing scores than AD (table 1). In contrast, baseline memory and naming scores were more impaired for DLBD-H, AD, and TLBD-H compared to TLBD-L and DLBD-L. Linear mixed models of rates of decline (figure 2) showed the annual GLDS change and −12 DRS point change were faster for DLBD-H than the other groups (p < 0.05) except DLBD-L, which had a −9 DRS point change (p = 0.11). The annual rate of −3 MMSE point change was faster for DLBD-H than the other groups (p < 0.01) (table 3).

In TLBD-H, diagnostic sensitivity was 43% for probable DLB and 52% for the DLB syndrome. Patients with TLBD-H were more likely than those with AD to eventually develop VH, parkinsonism, and fluctuations (table 1). Both TLBD-H and AD groups had low rates of pRBD, and none met the 1-year rule for PDD. Probable DLB emerged later in the disease compared to the other Lewy body disease subgroups (p < 0.001), but earlier than AD (p = 0.001) (figure 1), with the same pattern for the DLB syndrome (95% CI, 6.1–16.8 years). Only 2 patients with TLBD had a Braak NFT stage of IV (both met criteria for probable DLB), but there was no difference between Braak NFT stages V and VI in the onset latency of probable DLB or the DLB syndrome. When the entire disease course was considered, Braak NFT stages V and VI did not differ in the lifetime likelihood of probable DLB (p = 0.17) or the DLB syndrome (p = 0.24) (table 2).

The TLBD-H and AD groups had more women, older death age, later onset of core features, and worse baseline memory and naming scores than the TLBD-L and DLBD-L groups. Baseline cognitive scores did not differ between TLBD-H and AD (table 1). Rates of change for GLDS, MMSE, and DRS were slower for TLBD-H and AD than DLBD-H. MMSE rates of change were faster for TLBD-H and AD than TLBD-L (table 3 and figure 2).

Patients with transitional or diffuse Lewy body disease without neocortical neurofibrillary tangles (TLBD-L, DLBD-L) were highly likely to develop DLB core features. The diagnostic sensitivity of probable DLB was 87% for TLBD-L and 96% for DLBD-L. This increased to 97% for TLBD-L and 98% for DLBD-L when the DLB syndrome was represented by either probable DLB or dementia with one core feature of parkinsonism or pRBD. The TLBD-L and DLBD-L groups were disproportionately male, which is consistent with other autopsy23,27 and clinical studies of parkinsonism28 and pRBD.29 TLBD-L and DLBD-L also demonstrated the well-established cognitive pattern of worse attention and visual processing scores and better memory and naming scores than AD.9,25,26 Although 22% of TLBD-L and 13% of DLBD-L met the 1-year rule for PDD,22 these patients did not differ in parkinsonism severity, cognitive performance, or other core features from those without this designation, consistent with other studies.30,31 Overall, patients with TLBD-L and DLBD-L showed the same clinical profile. This confirms that α-synuclein pathology is sufficient for the development of dementia and core features of DLB, even without diffuse cortical involvement.

The group with DLBD and neocortical tangles (DLBD-H) demonstrated clinical overlap with TLBD-L and DLBD-L in terms of the presence and onset of core features, and worse baseline attention and visual processing scores compared to AD (table 1). In DLBD-H, diagnostic sensitivity was 70% for probable DLB and 77% for the DLB syndrome. This more moderate diagnostic sensitivity was predominantly related to milder parkinsonism and fewer patients with pRBD than TLBD-L and DLBD-L, though parkinsonism and pRBD were still far greater in DLBD-H than in AD. Women represented half the DLBD-H group, and although we did not find a sex difference for parkinsonism,28 we did find that women were less likely to have pRBD.23,29,32 To some extent, RBD may be underreported because it tends to be less vigorous in women than men,33 but this does not fully account for the lower rates of pRBD in DLBD-H and does not address the milder parkinsonism. The more modest diagnostic sensitivity in DLBD-H appears attributable to those with a Braak NFT stage of VI, who were less likely to develop the DLB syndrome (64%) than their counterparts with stage IV (89%) or stage V (85%) (table 2). It is possible that a subset of patients with DLBD-H may have less brainstem burden of α-synuclein pathology and associated cell loss known to be associated with parkinsonism and pRBD,27,34 but this needs further investigation.

The DLBD-H group also shared several characteristics with the groups harboring neocortical tangles (AD and TLBD-H). This included greater female representation, more extensive neuritic plaque burden, a higher frequency of the APOE ε4 allele, and worse baseline memory and naming performance than TLBD-L or DLBD-L. This is consistent with postmortem studies11,26,35–37 and with imaging studies that show a relationship between hippocampal atrophy and NFT deposition in DLB18,38 and associated memory impairment.37,39,40 The clinical overlap between DLBD-H, TLBD-L, and DLBD-L and between DLBD-H and AD provide evidence that α-synuclein and neocortical tau pathology independently contribute to the phenotypic expression of DLB.

The TLBD-H group had widespread neocortical tangles with α-synuclein pathology essentially restricted to brainstem and limbic regions. This group's diagnostic sensitivity was 43% for probable DLB and 52% for the DLB syndrome, which was lower than the other Lewy body disease subgroups, but still higher than AD. Patients with TLBD-H were cognitively similar to AD at baseline, but were more likely to develop probable DLB or the DLB syndrome in the later stages of the disease (figure 1). Little is known about TLBD-H, but these findings suggest that patients with a clinical diagnosis of AD who develop DLB in the advanced stages of the disease may have coexisting Lewy body disease.

For TLBD-L and DLBD-L, probable DLB was evident within a year from the estimated onset of cognitive impairment (figure 1), with the same pattern for the DLB syndrome. These short intervals were attributable, in large part, to pRBD, which was common in TLBD-L and DLBD-L and typically preceded cognitive impairment.41 For DLBD-H, probable DLB was evident a median latency of 3.7 years from estimated cognitive onset, and the DLB syndrome emerged a median latency of 2.7 years from estimated cognitive onset. This demonstrates the benefit of detecting DLB sooner when the diagnosis of DLB also considers those with dementia and one core feature of parkinsonism or pRBD. In addition, the onset latencies in DLBD-H were related to the distribution of neocortical tangle pathology, with probable DLB and the DLB syndrome developing earlier in Braak NFT stage IV than V or VI, and earlier in stage V than VI (figure 3). Taken together, these data highlight the importance of monitoring patients over time and indicate that DLB may take longer to declare itself in DLBD-H than in TLBD-L or DLBD-L, particularly when the neocortical tangle distribution is more widespread.

We examined dementia trajectory using GLDS, MMSE, and DRS as indicators of dementia severity. The TLBD-L and DLBD-H groups exhibited the slowest and fastest rates of decline, respectively (figure 2). Whether the slower dementia progression in TLBD-L is related to lower overall levels of pathologic burden is not yet known. The accelerated dementia progression in DLBD-H has been demonstrated in several autopsy studies,42–44 and in clinical cohorts using imaging-based hippocampal atrophy45 and CSF markers of tau and β-amyloid.46 Faster disease progression has been attributed to greater overall pathology and the synergistic effects of α-synuclein, tau, and β-amyloid,2,16,47 with speculation that regional overlap of α-synuclein and tau16,35,48 may promote mutual propagation and contribute to a steeper disease trajectory.49 In addition, faster disease progression has also been attributed to the α-synuclein pathology itself, which typically exceeds the tau burden in DLB2,16,35,47 and has been shown to independently contribute to a more rapid rate of decline42 and lower rates of survival.3,16,47

When the entire disease course was considered, a clinical diagnosis of DLB was highly likely when the distribution of α-synuclein pathology was greater than tau (TLBD-L, DLBD-L), and less likely when the distribution of tau pathology was greater than α-synuclein (TLBD-H). This broadly supports the inverse relationship from which the high and low likelihood classifications of the current DLB pathologic criteria19 are based, but there are 2 caveats pertaining to the intermediate likelihood DLB classifications. In TLBD with Braak NFT stage III (intermediate likelihood DLB), 72% had probable DLB, but 94% (all but one) had the DLB syndrome consisting of probable DLB or dementia with one core feature of parkinsonism or pRBD (table 2). The second caveat pertains to those with DLBD and Braak NFT stage V (intermediate likelihood DLB), who were just as likely as DLBD with Braak NFT stage IV (high likelihood DLB) to develop probable DLB or the DLB syndrome, as previously demonstrated.12

This study is limited by a reliance on distribution rather than burden of α-synuclein and tau pathology, and confirmation is needed using quantitative methods. CERAD neuritic plaque scores were related to Braak NFT stage but the role of β-amyloid in Lewy body disease needs further study. Investigations of amygdala-predominant and brainstem-predominant Lewy body disease, as well as the role of cerebrovascular comorbidity, are needed for a more comprehensive representation of the Lewy body disease spectrum. It is unclear why only 2 patients in our sample had TLBD-H with a Braak NFT stage IV, and further study of this subgroup is clearly warranted. The TLBD-H and AD groups were limited by a greater use of clinical interview to determine fluctuations and by a narrower breadth of attention and visual processing tests. The 2 × 2 classification approach has the advantage of 4 mutually exclusive categories with low and high levels of α-synuclein and tau pathology, but more work is needed to understand the clinical heterogeneity of DLBD-H and cross-validation is needed.

In sum, the TLBD-L and DLBD-L groups were highly likely to develop the DLB syndrome within a short interval from estimated cognitive onset. In DLBD-H, there was a high likelihood for the DLB syndrome in those with Braak NFT stages IV and V but not for stage VI, and a longer latency to diagnosis associated with more widespread neocortical tangle distribution. The TLBD-H and AD groups were less likely to exhibit the DLB syndrome, but those with TLBD-H were still more likely than patients with AD to develop it later in the disease course. The TLBD-L, DLBD-L, and DLBD-H groups showed worse baseline attention and visual processing than AD. The groups with neocortical tangles (DLBD-H, TLBD-H, AD) showed worse baseline memory and naming scores than those without neocortical tangles (TLBD-L, DLBD-L). Dementia trajectory was fastest for DLBD-H (the subgroup with the most diffuse distribution of α-synuclein and tau pathology) and slowest for TLBD-L (the subgroup with the most limited distribution of pathology). Overall, these findings provide evidence for clinical and pathologic subgroups of Lewy body disease. Understanding the spectrum of DLB as it relates to its underlying pathology is important in our efforts to improve the early detection of DLB and to prepare for targeted protein-specific therapies.
