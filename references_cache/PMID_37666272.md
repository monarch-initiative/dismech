Roifman syndrome is a rare congenital disorder characterized by growth
retardation, cognitive delay, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, immunodeficiency,
and retinal dystrophy. However, very rarely, with only one case reported to
date, a patient with Roifman syndrome may develop cardiomyopathy in their
lifetime. We reported a case with underdiagnosed Roifman syndrome confirmed by
whole genome sequencing, manifested as non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy, which has
broadened the association between non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy and the genetic
disorder Roifman syndrome. We also underscored that cardiomyopathy might be part
of the clinical manifestations of Roifman syndrome and the importance of whole
genome sequencing for diagnosis, as RNU4ATAC is not targeted by many
commercially available exome capture kits.

 We hereby report a case with underdiagnosed Roifman syndrome manifested as non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathy.

This is a 20‐year‐old male who was born with microcephaly and later, as a child, developed retinitis pigmentosa and recurrent ear infection. When he turned 11 years old, he was found to be adrenal and thyroid insufficient [autoimmune polyglandular syndrome (APS) type 2] for which he started taking hydrocortisone and levothyroxine. Levothyroxine was slowly tapered down and discontinued when he was 18 years old. He has one younger sister with microcephaly, adrenal insufficiency, and type 1 diabetes mellitus, and two elder unaffected sisters. The parents are both healthy and the medical history of relatives is unremarkable. He is a non‐smoker without history of alcohol or illicit drugs use.

On this occasion, he presented with worsening exertional dyspnoea. Physical examination revealed hypotension (81/40 mmHg), tachycardia (112 b.p.m.), and hypoxia (85% on room air). He appeared in acute respiratory distress and was not able to tolerate decubitus. Jugular venous pressure was elevated. Auscultation revealed diffuse rales over the lower lung fields bilaterally but no gallops or murmurs. There was no significant peripheral oedema.

On admission, thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH) was elevated at 70 mU/L. N‐terminal pro‐hormone B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) was 5340 pg/mL, and chest X‐ray (CXR) showed diffuse pulmonary oedema. A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 20% with severe LV dilatation. Cardiac catheterization ruled out obstructive coronary artery disease, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed non‐specific non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathy with LVEF of 32% without infiltration or inflammation. Cardiac endomyocardial biopsy showed no histopathological abnormalities (no lymphoid infiltrates, deposits, necrosis, granulomas, or multinucleated giant cells, stains for C3b and C4b negative).

He was managed in the intensive care unit (ICU) with diuresis and inotropes (dobutamine) along with mechanical cardiac support using Impella (CP and RP) for cardiogenic shock. He was then slowly weaned off Impella and intravenous inotropes, followed by introduction of guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT). He also received thyroid hormone replacement. Repeat TTE revealed improved LVEF of 30%. He was discharged home with oral medications (metoprolol, spironolactone, digoxin, and furosemide), which were titrated during outpatient follow‐up.

Unfortunately, he was readmitted around 3 months later due to worsening cardiomyopathy. TTE showed LVEF of 17% with severe mitral valve regurgitation (MR). Milrinone infusion was started. He underwent MitraClip NTW (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA) placement for severe MR (Figure 
1
) and a single‐chamber implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) placement due to persistent low LVEF despite optimal medical therapy and thyroid function normalization. He was then slowly weaned off milrinone and tolerated GDMT with metoprolol, spironolactone, sacubitril–valsartan, dapagliflozin, and digoxin. Ivabradine was also initiated for better heart rate control. He showed significant clinical improvement and was discharged home with cardiac outpatient rehabilitation.

Echocardiogram status post‐MitraClip implantation four‐chamber view showed dilated left ventricle (left ventricular internal diameter in diastole of 6.4 cm), severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction of 21%), and residual moderate mitral regurgitation per Doppler flow view (right side).

Genetics were consulted given his cardiomyopathy/congestive heart failure with unclear aetiology, microcephaly, APS type 2, retinitis pigmentosa, recurrent ear infection during childhood, and family history of a younger sister with similar features (microcephaly and autoimmune endocrinopathy). The initial cardiomyopathy comprehensive panel (Invitae, San Francisco, CA, USA), which we commonly use for screening cardiomyopathy‐related genetic disorders, was positive for one likely pathogenic variant identified in aminoglycosides (AGL), c294‐aA>T (splice receptor), heterozygous. However, this variant was not consistent with patient's clinical manifestations. Subsequently, whole genome sequencing from Baylor Genetics for Undiagnosed Disease Network (UDN) was performed and revealed a compound heterozygous pathogenic variant in RNU4ATAC [NR_023343.1:n.118T>C, autosomal recessive (AR) inheritance, heterozygous, mother is heterozygous and father is negative, pathogenic] (Figure 
2
), which was considered to be pathogenic for Roifman syndrome and would explain this patient's clinical manifestations. The whole genome sequencing report for his younger sister revealed the same results.

During the subsequent 2 years of follow‐up, he has been tolerating GDMT well, has been experiencing improved clinical symptoms (New York Heart Association class II), and has had no further admissions for congestive heart failure. LVEF has improved to 33% per a recent TTE.

RNU4ATAC pathogenic variants have recently been known to cause Roifman syndrome (RF), Lowry‐Wood syndrome (LWS), and microcephalic osteodysplastic primordial dwarfism (MOPD), three distinct conditions that share some skeletal and neurological anomalies but with variable disrupting function.

 Roifman syndrome is a rare congenital disorder that was first clinically identified in 1999 and recessive RNU4ATAC pathogenic variants were identified later in 2015.
1
,
5
 It is characterized by growth retardation, cognitive delay, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, immunodeficiency, and retinal dystrophy.
1
,
2
 Very rarely, cardiac defect or cardiomyopathy might also be part of the manifestations. A case was reported of a patient with Roifman syndrome, diagnosed in childhood, who subsequently developed cardiomyopathy due to cardiac non‐compaction during adolescence.
4
 Similarly, our patient, with underdiagnosed Roifman syndrome, presented with non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathy.

The diagnosis of Roifman syndrome is based on typical clinical manifestations combined with positive genetic findings of RNU4ATAC pathogenic variants.
3
 However, sometimes, the diagnosis can be challenging due to its rarity or atypical clinical presentations, as in the case of our patient, who presented with severe non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathy without diagnostic cardiac MRI or pathogenic findings. He was hypothyroid, but his heart function did not improve with the normalization of thyroid function, indicating that hypothyroidism alone would not completely explain his condition. Genetics were then involved, and the patient and his younger sister were finally diagnosed with Roifman syndrome through whole genome sequencing. It is worth noticing that the routine cardiomyopathy comprehensive panel was not able to detect the pathogenic variants unless whole genome sequencing was performed.

The underlying mechanism of cardiomyopathy in Roifman syndrome is still unclear. The RNU4ATAC gene is located in intron 2 on chromosome 2q14.2 and encodes the small nuclear RNA U4atac (RefSeq NR_023343.1), a component of the minor spliceosome for U12‐dependent class of intron splicing.
8
 Variant n.118T>C is located in the Sm protein‐binding site, which is an element of major importance for splicing
5
 (Figure 
2
). Merico et al.
5
 have noticed higher minor intron retention in patients with Roifman syndrome, leading to reduced levels of correctly spliced transcripts for minor intron genes and eventually affecting exosome functionality. Additionally, Mandel et al.
4
 reported a case with Roifman syndrome presenting with left ventricular non‐compaction as a teenager, which is a rare cause of heart failure. Overall, the management of non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathy associated with Roifman syndrome follows the same guidelines as other non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathies.
9



Our patient has broadened the association between non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathy and the genetic disorder Roifman syndrome. Thus, we underscore that cardiomyopathy might be part of the clinical manifestations of Roifman syndrome. In patients with non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathy but without a clear aetiology, if associated with other clinical features such as spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, immunodeficiency, growth retardation, or retinal anomalies, genetics should be involved, and Roifman syndrome should be considered as a part of the differential diagnoses as other manifestations might be subtle and undiagnosed. For diagnosis confirmation, whole genome sequencing should be performed instead of the traditional cardiomyopathy comprehensive panel alone, as RNU4ATAC is not targeted by many commercially available exome capture kits. Meanwhile, in patients with Roifman syndrome, cardiac manifestations should be monitored during their lifetime, as they may present later. Annual cardiac evaluation, along with lifestyle modification and screening and management of risk factors and comorbid conditions, is recommended per guidelines.
