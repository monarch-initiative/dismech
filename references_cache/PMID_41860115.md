# Risk Factors for Airway Infantile Hemangioma.
**Authors:** Castrén E, Salminen P, Kyrklund K
**Journal:** Acta Paediatr (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/apa.70513](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.70513)

## Content

AIM: An infantile hemangioma (IH) affecting the airway is rare. Identifying infants at risk for an airway IH is crucial to avoid potential airway compromise. Our objective was to identify risk factors for an airway IH and to study its incidence and treatments.

METHODS: This study included 798 children with an IH visiting our paediatric vascular anomaly unit between 1.1.2008 and 31.12.2017. Patient demographics and IH-related data were retrospectively reviewed to identify factors predisposing to an underlying airway IH.

RESULTS: An airway IH was diagnosed in 1.6% of the cohort. When a segmental beard distribution hemangioma was present, the risk for an airway IH was 33.3%, and when a non-beard distribution segmental hemangioma on the face or neck was observed, the risk was 11.5%. Focal hemangiomas were seen in 38.5% of infants with an airway IH, mostly in the perioral or nasal area. Propranolol was sufficient treatment for 80.0% of infants with an airway IH.

CONCLUSIONS: The presence of a segmental beard distribution hemangioma involved the highest risk for an underlying airway IH. Children with other types of segmental IHs on the face or neck, or those with focal perioral and nasal hemangiomas were also at risk for airway involvement.

Infantile hemangioma (IH) is the most frequent benign tumour in infants, with an incidence of 2%–5% of the population [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ]. Most IHs are usually not fully present at birth, and they proliferate during the first months of life [ 2 ]. The head and neck are the most common locations for IHs [ 1 ]. Fortunately, IHs are rarely located in the airway [ 1 , 5 ]. However, the rapid proliferation phase of an airway IH may lead to life‐threatening airway obstruction. Infants with a segmental beard distribution IH are known to be at the highest risk for airway involvement, but airway IHs have also been occasionally reported in the presence of other types of cutaneous IHs, or even without any cutaneous hemangioma [ 5 , 6 , 7 ].

Propranolol therapy has revolutionized the treatment of IHs, and studies have shown that most airway IHs also respond well to oral propranolol [ 5 , 8 , 9 ]. Results of propranolol therapy were considered the best when the treatment was initiated early enough to suppress the proliferation phase [ 8 , 10 , 11 ]. It is therefore crucial to identify those children at risk of an airway IH in order to establish the diagnosis and initiate the treatment early, to suppress the airway IH's aggressive proliferation and to prevent airway obstruction.

The aim of this study was to analyse how common airway IHs are, whether perinatal risk factors for an airway IH could be identified, and what the cutaneous hemangioma manifestations of an underlying airway IH. Moreover, we studied the treatments for airway IHs, how often second‐line treatments or surgery were necessary, and what the long‐term outcomes among these patients were.

We identified from electronic hospital records all patients aged 0–16 years who had visited Helsinki University Children's Hospital's Vascular Anomaly unit between 1.1.2008 and 31.12.2017 with the International Classification of Diseases—10th edition (ICD‐10) diagnosis D18.0 (hemangioma), or Q82.5 (birthmark). The diagnosis of an IH was based on the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classification, and only patients with a confirmed IH diagnosis based on clinical characteristics, typical clinical courses, and imaging studies were included in the study. Patient charts were retrospectively reviewed, and demographic and perinatal data concerning gender, birth weight, gestational age, multiple gestations, and maternal age were recorded. We documented IH‐related data concerning location, and morphological subtype as either focal, segmental, indeterminate, or multifocal. All imaging studies and photographs related to the IH and all IH‐related interventions, including office‐based and operation theatre laryngoscopy were recorded. The airway involvement of the IH was classified as supra‐ or subglottic. We registered all medical treatments and their durations and other interventions related to the IH. We recorded the follow‐up due to the IH, and any IH‐related symptoms at the end of the follow‐up.

The data was analysed using the SPSS Statistical Package for Social Sciences and Microsoft Excel. The Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to compare continuous variables between two independent groups. We used the chi‐square test for categorical variables and Fisher's exact test when the preconditions of the chi‐square test were not met.

The study followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Helsinki University Hospital's ethics committee and received institutional approval (HUS/2924/2021). Consent to publish patient photographs was given by the patient's caregiver.

Of the total of 798 IH patients identified, 13 (1.6%) children had an airway IH. Most infants with an airway IH were female (69.2%), corresponding to the female–male ratio of 71.6% in the total cohort. Perinatal data showed that average birthweight was 3600 g (range 800–4630) for infants with an airway IH, and 3129 g (range 580–5300) for the rest of the infants within this IH cohort. Gestational age was on average 36 weeks (range 27–42) for infants with an airway IH, and 37 weeks (range 24–42) for other infants with an IH. Multiple gestation was seen in 7.7% of the infants with an airway IH, and in 8.6% of other infants with an IH. Maternal age was on average 33.0 years in the airway IH group, and 31.4 years in the rest of the cohort. Statistical analyses comparing these perinatal factors showed no significant differences between the groups. Of the 13 infants with an airway IH, two (15.4%) were diagnosed with posterior fossa abnormalities, hemangiomas, arterial anomalies, cardiac abnormalities, eye anomalies and sternal defect (PHACES) syndrome.

Airway examinations were performed on 66 children (8.3%) of the cohort. The diagnosis of an airway IH was based primarily on office‐based flexible transnasal laryngoscopy. If further examination of the lower airway was required, rigid and flexible laryngoscopy were performed under general anaesthesia. Of the infants with an airway IH, five underwent airway examination under general anaesthesia only, five underwent airway examination both office‐based and under general anaesthesia and three infants needed only office‐based examination. Only 43.9% of the children (18/41) with any type of segmental IH on the face or neck underwent airway examination. Therefore, it is possible that some infants with asymptomatic airway IHs remained undiagnosed.

More than half of infants with an airway IH (53.8%) presented with facial or neck segmental hemangioma, segmental beard distribution being the most common location. Notably, six out of seven of these infants had an airway IH with subglottic involvement. The non‐segmental cutaneous hemangiomas in infants with an airway IH were seen in nasal and perioral areas, and in the orbit; in all of these cases, the airway IH involved only the supraglottic area. Then, we calculated the risk ratio for each of the cutaneous patterns susceptible to an underlying airway IH, as we had comprehensive data on the entire cohort of 798 children with an IH. Table 2 shows the risk ratios for an airway IH for each cutaneous pattern. The highest risk ratio for an airway IH involved segmental beard distribution of the hemangioma (33.3%). Among the seven patients with PHACES syndrome in the total cohort, an airway IH was present in two (28.6%). The risk ratios for an airway IH among infants with non‐segmental hemangiomas were much lower.

Almost all infants with an airway IH (10/13) received propranolol with mainly excellent response. Three infants with an airway IH were born prior to the propranolol era. The average age at the initiation of propranolol therapy was 2.8 months (range 0–4 months). Only one infant required re‐admission on propranolol because the symptoms recurred. The mean duration of the propranolol therapy due to an airway IH was 17 months (range, 4–39 months). In comparison, for infants with an IH not involving the airway, propranolol was used for a mean of 7 months (p = 0.11).

In this cohort, 20.0% of the propranolol‐treated infants with an airway IH (2/10) also received oral corticosteroids, of whom all had a subglottic airway IH. Despite medical treatment with oral propranolol and corticosteroids, one infant required tracheotomy due to difficult airway obstruction (Image 2). Decannulation was achieved after 62 days, during which the child received local corticosteroid injections for the subglottic component.

For infants with an airway IH, the mean duration of follow‐up was 8 years (range 3.3–15 years). All children were asymptomatic regarding voice and airway at the end of their follow‐up.

This was one of the largest studies analysing the frequency and risk factors of airway IHs. As IHs are very common among infants, it is important to identify infants at risk for an airway IH. This calls for awareness among the primary practitioners and paediatricians as they play a key role in referring these infants to specialised care. Gradual onset of stridor during the first few weeks of life and the presence of other cutaneous hemangiomas, particularly in risk locations such as the beard area or mouth, should alert clinicians to the possibility of an airway IH.

The incidence of 1.6% for airway infantile hemangioma was similar to those in previous studies (0.2%–1.7%) [ 1 , 5 , 7 ]. However, the true incidence of an airway IH among all infants with an IH in our population must be much lower, as our study cohort consisted only of those infants with an IH referred to specialised care. Most of the uncomplicated hemangiomas were surveilled at primary healthcare and remained outside our patient registries. The national recommendations in Finland for hemangiomas and vascular anomalies advise primary health care providers to refer infants with hemangiomas in risk areas to specialised care [ 12 ]. It is therefore very likely that our unit received all those infants with an IH who were at risk of airway involvement.

Office‐based flexible transnasal laryngoscopy is currently widely available at paediatric otorhinolaryngology units and can be used to screen infants with an IH on a risk location for potential airway involvement [ 8 , 13 ]. The office‐based setting allows early screening of these infants without general anaesthesia. For symptomatic infants, flexible and rigid laryngoscopy under general anaesthesia is often also needed when subglottic lesions are suspected [ 8 , 13 ].

The segmental beard distribution hemangioma has long been known to be a hallmark for an underlying airway IH [ 6 , 7 , 14 ]. We found that every third infant with a segmental beard distribution hemangioma had an airway IH. However, we also found that the risk ratio for an airway IH was also relevant for those infants with a non‐beard distribution segmental IH. Infants diagnosed with PHACES syndrome were also at high risk for an airway IH in this study, corresponding to previous studies reporting that up to 52% of infants with PHACES had an airway IH [ 14 ]. Moreover, the segmental hemangioma pattern was particularly linked to the subglottic involvement, which is crucial regarding potential airway obstruction. When we looked at those infants with an airway IH with non‐segmental cutaneous IHs in the beard area or nose, only supraglottic involvement occurred, suggesting that these children are at lower risk for a relevant airway compromise. Uthurriague et al. also reported that infants with airway IHs may have more variable cutaneous hemangiomas than the typical segmental beard distribution pattern, but they did not specify whether there was a link between the subglottic involvement and segmental skin pattern [ 7 ]. Interestingly, we found only one case (7.7%) of an airway IH without cutaneous hemangioma; previous studies have reported that up to 18%–55% of airway IH cases can occur without cutaneous lesions [ 5 , 7 ].

Studies on propranolol for IH have shown that it is safe and effective for young infants at doses of 2–3 mg/kg/day, and when given at feeding times to avoid hypoglycemia. For young infants, treatment is often initiated under hospital monitoring for cardiovascular parameters and blood glucose levels. Early treatment initiation has been associated with a better response to the therapy [ 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ], most likely due to suppression of the most active proliferation phase of a hemangioma during the first months of life. For an airway IH, the indication for treatment is avoidance or treatment of clinically relevant airway obstruction. In our study, the only child requiring tracheotomy was first diagnosed with a large periorbital hemangioma and was treated with both propranolol and oral corticosteroids due to severe visual compromise. The medication was insufficient to resolve the visual compromise, and therefore the child underwent surgery and oral medications were discontinued. It was only after propranolol cessation that the child presented respiratory symptoms and the airway IH was diagnosed. Here, the subglottic IH severely compressed the airway, and tracheotomy was necessary (Figure 2). Speculatively, if the airway IH had been diagnosed beforehand, propranolol would not have been discontinued and perhaps the severe airway compromise and the tracheotomy could have been avoided. This emphasizes the need for early recognition of an airway IH, although such scenarios are fortunately unusual.

Despite the efficacy of propranolol, other studies have also reported that propranolol monotherapy was not always sufficient to treat airway IHs [ 5 , 8 , 15 ]. We observed that in 20.0% of propranolol‐treated airway IH cases, oral corticosteroids were also used in addition to propranolol. Our result corresponded to a previous Italian multicentre study reporting that 27.8% of the infants with an airway IH needed oral corticosteroids in addition to propranolol, but without the need for surgical airway management [ 8 ]. In contrast, Elluru et al. reported that 59% of infants with an airway IH received corticosteroid injections in addition to propranolol, whereas in McCormick's study, only 7.7% of infants with an airway IH needed oral steroids but 15.4% required surgical resection in addition to propranolol. The differences in the treatment modalities required reflect the fact that airway IHs are rare, and most infants with an airway IH were treated case‐by‐case in a multidisciplinary setting.

A key strength of this study was the fact that our unit was the only referral centre for paediatric vascular anomalies in Helsinki University Hospital's catchment area, and therefore it was very likely that we received all infants with an IH needing evaluation at specialised care. The main limitation of our study was its retrospective nature. However, airway IHs are so rare that a long retrospective setting was needed to obtain a large enough patient cohort. Another limitation was that the study period extended from 2008 to 2017, during which knowledge and treatment of IHs had evolved, and therefore the clinical criteria for referrals and medical treatments have varied throughout the study period.

Based on our findings, the presence of a segmental beard distribution IH showed the highest risk for an underlying airway IH, and this manifestation should always lead to airway evaluation in these infants. However, the presence of other segmental hemangiomas on the face or neck as well as non‐segmental nasal or perioral hemangiomas also involved the risk for an airway IH. No specific perinatal risk factor for an airway IH was identified. We found that early diagnosis was essential because propranolol treatment, when started early, was sufficient for most infants with an airway IH to avoid relevant airway compromise. Our findings underline the importance of close collaboration between primary health care providers, vascular anomaly units, and pediatric otorhinolaryngologists in identifying and treating these infants.
