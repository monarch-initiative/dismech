Fibroblasts constitute a heterogeneous population of cells. In this study, we 
integrated single-cell RNA-sequencing and bulk RNA-sequencing data as well as 
clinical information to study the role of individual fibroblast populations in 
systemic sclerosis (SSc). SSc skin demonstrated an increased abundance of COMP+, 
COL11A1+, MYOC+, CCL19+, SFRP4/SFRP2+, and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblasts signatures 
and decreased proportions of CXCL12+ and PI16+ fibroblast signatures in the 
Prospective Registry of Early Systemic Sclerosis and Genetics versus Environment 
in Scleroderma Outcome Study cohorts. Numerical differences were confirmed by 
multicolor immunofluorescence for selected fibroblast populations. COMP+, 
COL11A1+, SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+, and PI16+ fibroblasts were similarly 
altered between normal wound healing and patients with SSc. The proportions of 
profibrotic COMP+, COL11A1+, SFRP4/SFRP2+, and PRSS23/SFRP2+ and proinflammatory 
CCL19+ fibroblast signatures were positively correlated with clinical and 
histopathological parameters of skin fibrosis, whereas signatures of CXCL12+ and 
PI16+ fibroblasts were inversely correlated. Incorporating the proportions of 
COMP+, COL11A1+, SFRP4/SFRP2+, and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblast signatures into 
machine learning models improved the classification of patients with SSc into 
those with progressive versus stable skin fibrosis. In summary, the profound 
imbalance of fibroblast subpopulations in SSc may drive the progression of skin 
fibrosis. Specific targeting of disease-relevant fibroblast populations may 
offer opportunities for the treatment of SSc and other fibrotic diseases.

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a connective disease with vascular alterations, inflammation and fibrotic remodeling of the skin and multiple internal organs as histopathological hallmarks (Distler et al., 2019). Fibroblasts are the predominant source of extracellular matrix and key effector cells of fibrotic tissue remodeling (Distler et al., 2019, Garrett et al., 2017, Hinz and Lagares, 2020). However, fibroblasts also modulate inflammatory processes and angiogenesis. With the emergence of single cell technologies over the last years, we learned that fibroblasts are a heterogeneous population of cells (Buechler et al., 2021, Deng et al., 2021, Zhang et al., 2019).

In SSc skin, single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) identified ten subpopulations of fibroblasts (Tabib et al., 2021, Tabib et al., 2018). Although these fibroblast subpopulations show altered gene expression in SSc, they are also detected in the skin of healthy individuals. These data also indicated an imbalance in pre-existing fibroblast subpopulations. Although scRNA-seq provided important insights into fibroblast biology, it has several limitations: (I) often, a relatively small number of patients is analyzed. The low number of samples analyzed may fail to detect important associations with clinical features, particularly important in view of the highly heterogeneous pathology seen in SSc skin. (II) The cellular dissociation of tissue samples required for scRNA-seq may lead to distinct recovery rates for different cell populations and may thus introduce systemic biases. (III) The depth of transcript detection for scRNA-seq is lower than for bulk RNA-seq, and thus scRNA-seq may not detect rare transcripts (Chen et al., 2019, Hwang et al., 2018).

CIBERSORTx is an analytical tool based on machine learning methods to infer individual cell type proportions from RNA profiles of intact tissues (Newman et al., 2019, Steen et al., 2020). CIBERSORTx thus offers opportunity to digitally ‘purify’ the transcriptomes of individual cell types from bulk RNA admixtures without physical isolation. By leveraging cell type expression signatures from single-cell experiments or sorted cell subsets, CIBERSORTx has been proven to provide detailed portraits of tissue composition (Newman et al., 2015), and thus has been referred to as “digital cytometry” (Belote et al., 2021, Cascini and Chiodoni, 2021, Chen et al., 2018, Wang et al., 2021).

In the present study, we utilized CIBERSORTx to determine the proportions of individual fibroblast subpopulations in SSc skin. Moreover, we characterize the core signaling pathways and functional roles of individual fibroblast subpopulations and explore correlations between fibroblast subpopulations and disease outcome.

Using fibroblast expression signatures from scRNA-seq datasets, CIBERSORTx detected all previously identified subpopulations of fibroblasts from a total of 182 SSc and 69 healthy control skin tissue samples. SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+, COMP+, COL11A1+, MYOC+, CCL19+, CXCL12+, PI16+, ANGPTL7+ and COCH+ fibroblasts were present in SSc and healthy skin (Supplementary Figure 1a, b). Amongst those, SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblasts revealed less distinct expression patterns from COMP+ fibroblasts both in SSc patients and healthy donors as compared to other populations which showed more overlap in gene expression (Supplementary Figure 1c), indicating that SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+ and COMP+ clusters may thus represent a differentiation continuum, as previously reported (Tabib et al., 2021).

Compared with the previously reported ten fibroblast subtypes from 16 steady-state mouse tissues (Buechler et al., 2021), five human fibroblast subtypes, namely COMP, PI16, COCH, CCL19 and CXCL12, matched to murine fibroblast subpopulations based on the analysis of marker genes (Supplementary Figure 1d).

We deconvoluted individual fibroblast subpopulations in the PRESS cohort (33 healthy controls (HC) and 48 diffuse cutaneous SSc (dSSc)) as a multicentric North-American cohort of patients with early dSSc (mean disease duration 1.3 years)(Skaug et al., 2020). CIBERSORTx revealed altered proportions of several fibroblast subpopulations in SSc skin compared to HC. The abundance of COMP+, COL11A1+, MYOC+ and CCL19+ fibroblast signatures were increased in SSc patients of the PRESS cohort compared to HC. In contrast, the abundance of CXCL12+, PI16+ and ANGPTL7+ fibroblast signatures were decreased in SSc skin. No difference was observed for COCH+ fibroblasts (Figure 1a).

We next used the GENISOS cohort (36 HC, 18 limited cutaneous SSc (lSSc) and 43 dSSc) as a confirmatory SSc cohort (Assassi et al., 2015). GENISOS is a non-enriched, cross-sectional single tertiary referral center cohort with lSSc and dSSc. Most fibroblast subpopulations showed changes consistent to those in the PRESS cohort with higher proportions of COMP+, COL11A1+, MYOC+ and CCL19+ fibroblast signatures and decreases for CXCL12+ and PI16+ subpopulation signatures in patients with dSSc. For patients with lSSc in the GENISOS cohort, statistically significant differences to HC were observed for COMP+, COL11A1+ and MYOC+ fibroblast signatures. Of those, the proportions of COMP+ and COL11A1+ signatures were higher in dSSc compared to lSSc. CXCL12+ and PI16+ fibroblast signatures were suppressed exclusively in dSSc, but not in lSSc (Figure 1b).

More pronounced signatures for SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblast were observed in SSc patients compared with HC in the PRESS and in the GENISOS cohort (Figure 1c). In GENISOS cohort, significant enrichment scores for both populations were observed in patients with dSSc as compared to lSSc (Figure 1c).

Further, we confirmed the numerical differences of three key fibroblast populations, COL11A1+, PI16+ and SFRP4/SFRP2+ fibroblasts, by multicolor immunofluorescence (IF) staining (Supplementary Figure 2). All subsets were stained with specific markers plus prolyl-4-hydroxylase-beta (P4Hβ) as a pan-fibroblast marker. COL11A1+ fibroblasts were identified as POSTN+COL11A1+P4Hβ+ cells, PI16+ fibroblasts as PI16+P4Hβ+ cells, and SFRP4/SFRP2+ fibroblasts as SFRP4+P4Hβ+ cells. Consistent with the results of CIBERSORTx, IF showed a significant increase in the number of COL11A1+ fibroblasts and SFRP4/SFRP2+ fibroblasts and a decrease in the number of PI16+ fibroblasts compared to HC (Supplementary Figure 2). Thus, multicolor IF staining confirmed the validity of CIBERSORTx as an approach for quantification of fibroblast subsets.

We next evaluated potential differences in fibroblast subpopulations between clinically affected (local skin score of 1, 2, or 3) and non-affected skin (local skin score of 0). Proportions of COMP+, COL11A1+, and CCL19+ fibroblasts were higher in clinically affected than in non-affected skin, while PI16+ fibroblasts were lower, although the differences tended to be less pronounced than in SSc with local skin involvement when compared to HC. Similar findings were also detected in the enrichment of SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblast signatures (Supplementary Figure 3a,b). Further, we compared the abundance of fibroblast signatures in SSc patients with different skin scores at the biopsy site (local skin score of 0, 1, 2, or 3) and higher versus lower total modified Rodnan skin scores (mRSS) (stratified into mRSS ≤ 14 or >14). The proportion of SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+, COMP+, COL11A1+, CCL19+, CXCL12+ and PI16+ fibroblast signatures changed in a skin score-dependent manner with positive correlations for SFRP4/SFRP2+,PRSS23/SFRP2+, COMP+, COL11A1+ and CCL19+ fibroblast subpopulation signatures with the local and total mRSS and negative correlations for CXCL12+ and PI16+ fibroblast signatures (Supplementary Figure 4–5).

SSc patients can be categorized into intrinsic molecular subsets based on the gene expression profile (Milano et al., 2008, Pendergrass et al., 2012). SSc patients with a “normal-like” signature showed rather similar ratios of fibroblast subpopulations compared to HC (Figure 1d). SSc patients with an “inflammatory” signature demonstrated most pronounced changes in COMP+, CCL19+, and CXCL12+ fibroblast signatures amongst all molecular subsets, whereas patients with a “fibroproliferative” signature demonstrated more pronounced differences in COL11A1+, MYOC+ and COCH+ fibroblasts. Thus, the molecular signatures are associated with specific changes in individual fibroblast subpopulations (Figure 1d). In all intrinsic molecular subsets, SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ signatures were significantly enriched compared with HC (Figure 1e). We also compared the proportion of fibroblast subsets from the reference scRNA-seq dataset with our results obtained by CIBERSORTx. Although the relative proportions demonstrated differences for certain populations, the changes between SSc and HC were consistent in all populations across both techniques (Figure 1f, Supplementary Figure 6a).

Of note, however, despite a significant upregulation compared to HC, the proportional increases of CCL19+ fibroblast signatures was less pronounced in dSSc patients from the GENISOS cohort than in patients from PRESS patients, indicating a potential loss of CCL19+ fibroblast signatures in later disease stages (Figure 1f). We further confirmed our hypothesis by analyzing the relationship between CCL19+ fibroblast proportions with disease duration. The proportion of CCL19 fibroblasts was negatively correlated with disease duration (Supplementary Figure 6b).

To uncover the specific functions of the different fibroblast subpopulations, we performed gene ontology (GO) analyses (Figure 2a). SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+ and COMP+ fibroblast populations demonstrated multiple terms associated with tissue remodeling and collagen fibril organization. SFRP4/SFRP2+ fibroblasts also expressed genes required for actin crosslinking, whereas IL10 signaling was prominent in COMP+ fibroblasts. COL11A1+ fibroblasts also displayed multiple terms relevant for fibrotic tissue remodeling such as cellular response to transforming growth factor beta stimulus, regulation of WNT signaling pathway, cellular response to platelet-derived growth factor stimulus and also demonstrated activation of the coagulation-related pathways.

Consistent with the functional allocation of SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+ COL11A1+ and COMP+ fibroblast signatures as profibrotic fibroblast subpopulations, these subpopulations expressed the highest levels of COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1. Moreover, SFRP4/SFRP2+ and COL11A1+ fibroblasts express very high levels of myofibroblasts marker genes such as ACTA2, TAGLN, CTGF, ASPN, ADAM12, FNDC1 and PDGFRA (Figure 2b). CCL19+ fibroblasts are a proinflammatory population with positive regulation on macrophages/monocytes and B cells. MYOC+ fibroblast signatures showed prominent regulation of processes relevant to vascular homeostasis such as vascular permeability and vasculogenesis, and also prominent alterations in lipid metabolism. Another population with major regulatory effects on vasculature are ANGPTL7+ fibroblasts. ANGPTL7+ fibroblasts displayed significant regulation in gene sets for biological processes such as the establishment of endothelial barrier and regulation of vascular permeability. COCH+ fibroblasts also displayed controls in the processes of endothelium development, vasculogenesis and angiogenesis. CXCL12+ fibroblasts expressed genes related to immature cells with terms such as stem cell division and proliferation, as well as regulation apoptotic signaling pathway, and also showed prominent regulation of IL10 signaling. PI16+ fibroblasts were characterized by alterations in cholesterol metabolism, and multiple terms related to cell apoptosis or death (Figure 2a).

Based on the analysis of GO functional terms, CCL19+ fibroblasts represented the major proinflammatory subpopulation. ANGPTL7+ fibroblasts displayed the strongest potential to regulate vascular processes. These two fibroblast populations were thus used to further study the interactions of fibroblasts with inflammatory cell populations (for CCL19+ fibroblasts) and endothelial cells (for ANGPTL7+ fibroblasts). Specifically, we analyzed which ligands released from CCL19+ and ANGPTL7+ fibroblasts may interact with receptors on immune cells and endothelial cells, respectively.

CellChat predicted multiple interactions of CCL19+ fibroblasts with CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, FCN1+ moDC and FCGR3A+ macrophages. Key ligands-receptors interactions of CCL19+ fibroblasts with immune cells included CXCL12→CXCR4, PTN→NCL and MDK→NCL (Figure 3a,b).

ANGPTL7+ fibroblasts were predicted to have multiple interactions with endothelial cells. Amongst the key ligand receptor interactions were CCL2→ACKR1, CXCL2→ACKR1 and PGF→VEGFR1 (Figure 3c,d).

The proportions of COMP+, COL11A1+ and CCL19+ fibroblast signatures, and the gene signatures score of SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblasts were positively associated with clinical metrics of skin fibrosis such as the local skin score, the total mRSS and the skin thickness progression rate (STPR). In contrast, the proportion of PI16+ and CXCL12+ fibroblast signatures were inversely correlated with the local skin score, the total mRSS and the STPR, indicating potential antifibrotic effects of PI16+ and CXCL12+ cells. The observed clinical associations were comparable for most fibroblast subset signatures between the PRESS and the GENISOS cohorts (Figure 4a).

Several fibroblast subpopulation signatures demonstrated significant correlations with histopathological markers of fibrosis such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) positive myofibroblasts and relative collagen thickness (as assessed in a previous study (Skaug et al., 2020)). The abundances of many fibroblast subsets also correlated with the presence of previously defined classical markers of fibroblast activation (fibroblast signature) (Skaug et al., 2020). Consistent with the associations with clinical readouts of fibrosis, the proportions of COMP+, COL11A1+ and CCL19+ fibroblast signatures, and the gene signatures score of SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ were positively correlated with these markers of fibrosis. In addition, positive correlations with histopathologic parameters were also observed for MYOC+ fibroblast. In contrast, the signatures of PI16+, CXCL12+ and ANGPTL7+ fibroblasts were inversely correlated with the markers of fibroblast activation, α-SMA expression, and collagen thickness (Figure 4b), again suggesting an antifibrotic role of PI16+ and CXCL12+ fibroblasts.

We next determined whether the proportions of fibroblast subpopulation signatures at baseline (time of biopsy) can predict the future course of skin fibrosis. We applied ten commonly used ML models. In detail, we trained the ML models to classify SSc patients of the GENISOS cohort (as follow-up clinical information was only available in this cohort) into those with progression of skin fibrosis or absence of skin progression. Variables used in the ML models included the abundance of the fibroblast signatures and clinical indices such as age, race, gender, disease duration, antibody subpopulations, disease subtype (dSSc or lSSc), mRSS, presence or absence of ILD, baseline FVC in %, baseline DLco in %, and categorization according to SSc intrinsic subsets (normal-like, inflammatory, and fibroproliferative subsets (Franks et al., 2019, Pendergrass et al., 2012). Based on each model’s accuracy score, the random forest model performed best in classifying SSc patients into skin progressors and non-progressors (Figure 5a). Thus, we utilized the random forest model to determine the contribution of the individual variables. The variable importance was determined through the Mean Decrease Gini coefficient. The variables are listed in order of importance. Of note, eight out of ten variables that contributed most to the model were fibroblast subpopulation variables. To accurately predict the skin progression in SSc patients, it is necessary to combine mRSS with other factors such as COL11A1+, COMP+, SFRP4/SFRP2+, and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblasts. (Figure 5b). We further tested the predictive power of random forest model by classifying SSc patients into skin progressors and non-skin progressors based on the baseline variables (mRSS, the abundance of the fibroblast signatures and etc.). MDS plots displayed that random forest model assigned the patients into two clear separation clusters, one of which rather exclusively composed of patients without progression of skin fibrosis (Figure 5c). However, fibroblast subpopulation signatures in the skin did not enable stratification into progressive and non-progressive ILD (data not shown).

In the present study, we used CIBERSORTx to infer the proportions of different fibroblast population signatures in the skin of SSc patients. Using two large SSc cohorts of patients (PRESS and GENISOS), we demonstrate profound differences in the abundance of individual fibroblast subpopulation signatures in the skin of SSc patients compared to matched healthy individuals (Figure 6). The PRESS cohort includes only patients with early dSSc, whereas the GENISOS cohort is rather a non-enriched, cross-sectional population with early as well as late patients, including lSSc and dSSc. The PRESS cohort includes patients typically recruited for clinical trials with skin fibrosis as primary outcome, whereas the patients in the GENISOS cohort are rather representative for all patients taking care of in a tertiary center. Despite these differences, SSc patients in both cohorts demonstrated increased abundance of COMP+, COL11A1+, MYOC+, CCL19+, SFRP4/SFRP2+, and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblast signatures, and decreased levels of CXCL12+ and PI16+ fibroblast signatures. Two subpopulations, ANGPTL7+ and COCH+ fibroblasts, demonstrated differences between both cohorts. Multicolor IF staining for COL11A1+, PI16+ and SFRP4/PRSS23+ fibroblast confirmed the numerical differences obtained by CIBERSORTx and the validity of CIBERSORTx-based quantification of fibroblast subsets.

CCL19+ fibroblasts are a proinflammatory fibroblast population that has also been described in other chronic inflammatory diseases (Buechler et al., 2021, Davidson et al., 2021, Zhang et al., 2019). CCL19+ fibroblasts express multiple chemokines and cytokines, highlighting their potential to promote immune cell recruitment and activation. Consistently, CellChat analysis predicted multiple interactions of CCL19+ fibroblasts with multiple disease-relevant immune cell populations such.

COMP+, COL11A1+, SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ populations are fibroblast populations with high capability for ECM deposition as shown by high expression levels of COL1A1, COL1A2 and COL3A1 and regulation of numerous GO-functional processes relevant to the pathogenesis of fibrotic diseases. SFRP4/SFRP2+ and COL11A1+ fibroblasts express the highest levels of myofibroblasts marker genes amongst all subpopulations. These cells may be responsive to multiple profibrotic mediators as evidenced by the GO-terms “cellular response to transforming growth factor beta stimulus”, “regulation of WNT signaling pathway”, “cellular response to platelet-derived growth factor stimulus”.

We further demonstrate that the changes in the proportion of certain fibroblast population signatures are associated with clinical markers of skin fibrosis in SSc. The abundance of the profibrotic SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+, COMP+, COL11A1+ population signatures and proinflammatory CCL19+ fibroblast signatures were positively associated with the local skin fibrosis score, the total mRSS and the STPR. These fibroblast subsets also correlated positively with α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) positive myofibroblasts. In contrast, the proportion of CXCL12+ and PI16+ fibroblasts was inversely correlated with those clinical and histopathological parameters of fibrosis, suggesting anti-fibrotic effects of these two populations. We also provide evidence that changes in the abundance of SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+, COL11A1+ and COMP+ fibroblast signatures at the time of biopsy may support and eventually improve the stratification of SSc patients into those with progressive versus stable skin fibrosis. Indeed, our approach could also be applied to transcriptome analyses from future clinical trials for prospective validation as a prognostic marker in additional cohorts. However, in contrast to these correlations with different readouts of skin fibrosis, we also demonstrate that the abundance of fibroblast subset signatures in the skin does not correlate with or predict clinical changes in other organs such as the lungs.

We compared our fibroblast subtypes with the results of the recent study by Gur et al (Gur et al., 2022). The vast majority of the fibroblast subtypes demonstrated good correlations. The LGR5 fibroblasts defined by Gur et al. can be labeled by our PI16 cluster. However, in our dataset, in the dataset of Buechler et al. (Buechler et al., 2021) and the data by Deng et al.(Deng et al., 2021), only around 1.1%–4.9% of fibroblasts express LGR5 gene. However, if we use the top3 marker genes of the LGR5 fibroblasts to define this cluster (and thus follow the approach of Gur et al. with a definition of this population by multiple genes), the proportion increased to 13.9%–42.1%.

Our study has limitations: Determination of cellular proportions by CIBERSORTx is based on gene sets, which have previously been demonstrated to be specific for the cellular population of interest. Theoretically, cellular activation may affect a subset of these population-specific genes. To minimize any theoretical risk, we used data from single cell transcriptome profiling to define subpopulation-specific signature matrices for the different fibroblast subpopulation, all of which consist of hundreds of marker genes. These signature matrices have previously been shown to discriminate cellular subpopulations, independent of their state of activation, as proven by comparison of CIBERSORTx-based quantification with flow-cytometry or IF-staining-based experimental quantification (Newman et al., 2019).

In summary, we identify profound shifts in fibroblast populations in the skin of SSc patients with increased abundance of profibrotic and proinflammatory population signatures and downregulation of immature precursor populations. These findings may have implications for drug development strategies in SSc and other fibrotic diseases. Rather than targeting fibroblasts in general, a more selective approach with specific targeting of disease-relevant profibrotic and/or proinflammatory fibroblast population signatures such as SFRP4/SFRP2+, PRSS23/SFRP2+, COMP+, COL11A1+ and CCL19+ fibroblast signatures may not only improve efficacy, but may in particular reduce adverse events caused by unwanted effects on homeostatic fibroblast population signatures. An alternative approach could be to therapeutically activate CXCL12+ and PI16+ fibroblast signatures as antifibrotic populations to facilitate return to tissue homeostasis and eventually induce regeneration.

We first used scRNA-seq data from fibroblast subpopulations in normal skin to generate gene signature matrixes by CIBERSORTx algorithm. We then applied S-mode batch correction and 1000 permutations without quantile normalization to analyze the relative cell type fractions and to calculate the relative proportion of each fibroblast subtype from skin bulk transcriptomes.

Amongst the different populations, SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblasts were not as clearly separated from COMP+ fibroblasts as compared to other populations. To avoid biases and to better deconvolute the individual fibroblast subpopulations from bulk transcriptome data, we thus focused on the eight fibroblast subpopulations with clear separation in both SSc and healthy (COMP+, COL11A1, MYOC+, CCL19+, CXCL12+, PI16+, ANGPTL7+, COCH+) to generate a signature matrix for the CIBERSORTx analysis, and got the relative proportions of the 8 individual fibroblast subtypes. To analyze SFRP4/SFRP2+ and PRSS23/SFRP2+ fibroblasts, we used an alternative strategy by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using R package clusterProfiler (3.18.0) with the top50 marker genes as signature genes to get the enrichment score. The fibroblast signature matrix and the marker genes lists are provided in supplementary data 1.

The skin thickness progression rate (STPR) was calculated as described (Domsic et al., 2011) by dividing the modified Rodnan skin score (mRSS) at the first visit by the duration of skin thickening (in years) by patient report. Progression of skin fibrosis was defined as an increase of the overall mRSS score of more than 20% from baseline per year. Progressive ILD was defined as decreases in FVC of more than >10% from baseline per year.

To evaluate the potential of the different subpopulations of fibroblasts to predict diverse outcomes, such as the progression of skin or lung fibrosis in SSc patients, we used machine learning (ML) techniques. We applied ten commonly used ML models and choose the most effective one, namely Linear SVM (Support Vector Machines), RBF (Radial Basis Function) SVM, random forests, nearest neighbors, Gaussian processes, decision trees, neural networks, AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting), Gaussian naive Bayes, and QDA (Quadratic Discriminant Analysis). To enhance the robustness of the model and reduce the interference of randomness, we conducted stratified 3-fold cross-validation. SSc patients were partitioned into a training set that consisted of two-thirds of the observations and a test set that comprised the remaining third. The training and validation tests were carried out thrice, each time with arbitrary allocation of patients into the groups. The ML models were constructed utilizing the training set, and the accuracy of the models was assessed using the testing set. Finally, accuracy scores were calculated for each ML model and the models were ranked according to their respective scores.

In detail, we trained the ML models to classify SSc patients into those with progression of skin fibrosis or absence of skin progression. The same approach was also carried out for the progression of ILD. Variables used in the ML models included the abundance of the fibroblast signatures and clinical indices such as age, race, gender, disease duration, antibody subpopulations, disease subtype (dSSc or lSSc), mRSS, presence or absence of ILD, baseline FVC in %, baseline DLco in %, and categorization according to SSc intrinsic subsets (normal-like, inflammatory, and fibroproliferative subsets (Franks et al., 2019, Pendergrass et al., 2012).

Based on the accuracy score, the random forest model performed best in classifying SSc patients into skin progressors and non-progressors. Thus, we utilized the random forest model to determine the contribution of the individual variables. The variable importance was determined through the Mean Decrease Gini coefficient, which measures the effect of each variable on the homogeneity of nodes and leaves in the resulting random forest. The higher the value of the mean decrease Gini score, the greater the importance of the variable in the model. Then we used the varImpPlot to display the importance of variables and the MDS plot to identify patient similarities based on random forest proximities.

All steps were performed by Python V.3.9.1, scikit-learn V.0.24.2, NumPy V.1.19.5 and pandas V.1.2.1. and R package randomForest V. 4.7–1.1.
