# Propofol directly binds to and inhibits TLR7.
**Authors:** Koutsogiannaki S, Bu W, Maisat W, Manzor M, Zhang Z, Ohto U, Eckenhoff RG, Yuki K
**Journal:** FASEB J (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1096/fj.202200312R](https://doi.org/10.1096/fj.202200312R)

## Content

Sedatives/anesthetics are important medical tools to facilitate medical care and increase patients' comfort. Increasingly, there is recognition that sedatives/anesthetics can modulate immune functions. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are major pattern recognition receptors involved in the recognition of microbial components. TLR7 recognizes single-strand RNA virus such as influenza and SARS-CoV2 viruses and initiates interferon (IFN) responses. IFN production triggered by TLR7 stimulation is a critical anti-viral response. For example, patients with TLR7 variants including loss-of- function variants were associated with severe COVID-19. Taken together, it is important to determine if sedatives/anesthetics mitigate TLR7 function. We have previously showed that TLR7-mediated activation was not affected by volatile anesthetics. However, we found that propofol attenuated TLR7 activation among intravenous sedatives in the reporter assay. TLR7 agonist R837 stimulation increased TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-β mRNA levels in bone marrow-derived dendritic cells, while these levels were attenuated by propofol. Our murine lung slice experiments showed that propofol attenuated IFN production. R837 increased IFN-β expression in the lungs, and propofol attenuated IFN-β expression in an in vivo model of R837 intranasal instillation. We also found that propofol directly bound to and hindered its association of TLR7 with MyD88. Our analysis using fropofol, propofol derivative showed that the hydroxyl group in propofol was important for propofol-TLR7 interaction.

Toll like receptors (TLRs) are major pattern recognition receptors that recognize a wide range of microbial components for host immunity (1). Recognition of microbial components via these receptors triggers signaling cascades to augment our host immunity. So far 10 human TLRs (TLR1-TLR10) and 12 mouse TLRs (TLR1–9, TLR11–13) are identified (2, 3).

Increasingly we start appreciating the immunomodulatory effect of anesthetics/sedatives. We have previously reported that a subset of anesthetics we use in surgical arena and intensive care unit settings directly bound to and inhibited some TLRs. Specifically, volatile anesthetics isoflurane and sevoflurane directly inhibited TLR4, a major receptor to recognize Gram-negative bacteria constituent lipopolysaccharide (4). Sevoflurane also directly inhibited TLR2, a critical receptor for the recognition of Gram-positive bacterial component (5). Conversely, TLR2 and TLR4 were minimally impacted by intravenous anesthetics/sedatives.

TLR7 recognizes single strand RNA (ssRNA) from viruses including influenza virus, hepatitis B/C virus, and SARS-CoV2 virus, and stimulates interferon responses (6). Interferon (IFN) is a critical anti-viral cytokine to promote host defense. For example, TLR7 variants including loss of function variants are associated with a significantly higher susceptibility to severe COVID-19 due to an impairment in IFN responses (7–9). Thus, the attenuation of TLR7 functions might have a significant clinical impact on the outcome of patients. We previously found that volatile anesthetics isoflurane or sevoflurane did not block TLR7 activation (10). In this study, we examined the role of common intravenous sedatives in TLR7 function and found that propofol, a common intravenous sedative used in intensive care setting and perioperative period, attenuated TLR7 activation by its direct interaction.

Human TLR7 reporter cell line HEK-TLR7 cells containing a nuclear factor κ-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NFκB)-inducible secreted embryonic alkaline phosphate (SEAP) reporter (InvivoGen; San Diego, CA, USA) were used to assess the effect of intravenous sedatives on TLR7-mediated NFκB activation, as we previously reported (4, 5, 10, 11). The cells were cultured per the company’s protocol. They were stimulated with TLR7 agonist R837 (10 μg/mL) for 24 hours. Sedatives (propofol, dexmedetomidine, midazolam, and fentanyl) or propofol derivative fropofol were added to corresponding wells for 24 hours. The degree of NFkB activation was assessed by quantitating SEAP in the medium per the company protocol. Samples were subjected to a spectrophotometer analysis at 655 nm. We also tested the effect of propofol on TLR3 activation using human TLR3 reporter cell lines (HEK-TLR3 cells) (InvivoGen).

The concentration of propofol in culture medium was examined by the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) gold 126 solvent modules (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) using the method previously described by Yarbrough et al. (12). 10 μL cell culture supernatant was injected into a C18 reverse phase column (Eclipse Plus C18 250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The mobile phase, a mixture of acetonitrile, water, and trifluoroacetic acid (650:350:0.1, v/v) was eluted at 1.0 mL/min. Propofol was detected by fluorescence (Excitation 276 nm, Emission 310 nm). Propofol was assayed by measuring the chromatographic height (mAu), and the amount detected from the standard calibration curve.

All the experimental procedures complied with Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines regarding the use of animals in research (13) and the National Institute of Health guideline for the care and use of Laboratory animals and were approved by Boston Children’s Hospital animal care and use committee. Mice (C57BL/6) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and inbred in our animal facilities under specific pathogen-free conditions, with 12-hour light and dark cycles. Male mice at 8–10 weeks of age were used for the experiments. Mouse bone marrow cells were obtained and cultured in 6 well plates in RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% heat inactivated FBS, glutamine, penicillin, streptomycin, GM-CSF (20 ng/mL) and IL-4 (5 ng/mL). At day 3, the culture medium was entirely discarded and replaced with freshly warmed medium containing GM-CSF and IL-4. At day 7, cells were stimulated with R837 (10 μg/mL) in the presence or absence of propofol (100 μM) for 8 hours. At the end of incubation, cells were subjected to RNA purification and cDNA creation.

Mouse precision cut lung slices were obtained as previously described (14). After each mouse was euthanized, the abdomen was open and the inferior vena cava (IVC) was nicked. Then, a tracheostomy was placed using 20G angiocatheter, and low melting agarose (37°C) was instilled through the tracheostomy. Then, the trachea was ligated, and the lungs were obtained on ice. The lungs were subjected to 200 μm thick lung slicing using a Krumdieck MD6000 Tissue Slicer (Alabama Specialty Products, Inc., Munford, AL). All the lung slices were collected and transferred into a 24 well plate, washed four times and cultured in 1 mL media consisting of HEPES (25.2 mM), NaHCO3 (26.1 mM), CaCl2 (1.8 mM), MgSO4 (0.8 mM), KCl (5.4 mM), NaCl (116.4 mM), NaH2PO4 (1.2 mM), glucose, 1% Na-pyruvate, 2% nonessential amino acids, 1% MEM-vitamin solution, 1% L-glutamine and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Lung slices were cultures with R837 (10 μg/mL) in the presence or absence of propofol (100 μM) for 8 hours. Then, lung slices were subjected to RNA purification and cDNA creation.

10 mg/kg of R837 was intranasally given to mice anesthetized by ketamine and xylazine. Then, some of the mice were given 10 mg/kg/h propofol as we did previously (15, 16). At 8 hours after instillation, mice were euthanized and lungs were explanted for RNA purification and cDNA creation.

We examined TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-β, and GAPDH mRNA expression. Primer sequences are TNF-α forward primer; CCCTCACACTCAGATCATCTTCT, TNF-α reverse primer; GCTACGACGTGGGCTACAG, Il-1β forward primer; GCAACTGTTCCTGAACTCAACT, IL-1β reverse primer; ATCTTTTGGGGTCCGTCAACT

IL-6 forward primer; TAGTCCTTCCTACCCCAATTTCC, IL-6 reverse primer; TTGGTCCTTAGCCACTCCTTC, IL-10 forward primer; GCTCTTACTGACTGGCATGAG

IL-10 reverse primer; CGCAGCTCTAGGAGCATGTG, IFN-β forward primer AAGAGTTACACTGCCTTTGCCATC, IFN-β reverse primer; CACTGTCTGCTGGTGGAGTTCATC, GAPDH forward primer; TGGATTTGGACGCATTGGTC, GAPDH reverse primer; TTTGCACTGGTACGTGTTGAT.

Recombinant monkey (Macaca mulatta) TLR7 ectodomain containing four N-glycosylation site mutations (N167Q, N399Q, N488Q, N799Q) was cloned into Drosophila secretion expression vector pMT-BiP-V5-His (Thermo Fischer Scientific). Residues 440–445 (SEVGFC) were replaced with a thrombin-cleavage sequence (LVPRGS) for artificial cleavage of Z-loop and fused to a C-terminal sequence followed by a protein A tag. Stably transfected Drosophia S2 cells were cultured in EXPRESS FIVE SFM medium (Thermo Fischer Scientific) and stimulated with 0.5 mM CuSO4 to induce protein expression. The protein was purified using IgG affinity purification column assay followed by thrombin cleavage and gel-filtration in 10 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl.

m-azipropofol (aziPm) and azi-fropofol are photoactivatable analogues of propofol and fropofol, respectively as we previously stated (17). We equilibrated aziPm (final concentration 10 μM) or azi-fropofol (final concentration 50 μM) with TLR7 protein (1 mg/mL) for 10 min, followed by exposure to 350-nm light using a Rayonet RPR-3500 Lamp (Southern New England Ultraviolet Co., Branford, CT, USA). Then, the protein was subjected to electrophoresis on an SDS-polyacrylamide gel and Commassie staining. The excised protein gel band was subjected to trypsin digestion. Samples were injected into a nano-LC column with online electrospray into a LTQ linear ion trap (Thermo Fischer Scientific). Xcalibur (Thermo Fischer Scientific) was used for data acquisition, and Sequest (Scripps Research Institute, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used to search for the sequence of TLR7 with an aziPm or azi-fropofol mass modification. To confirm the specificity of aziPm or azi-fropofol, competition assay was done by photolabeling TLR7 protein with aziPm or azi-fropofol in the presence of propofol (200 μM) or fropofol (100 μM), respectively.

We used the protein structure that we deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB; http://www.pdb.org/) (PDB 5ZSF) for rigid docking analysis. We performed a rigid docking simulation of propofol and fropofol on TLR7 in the vicinity of the adducted residues using Autodock Vina (Scripps Laboratories, San Diego, CA). Propofol or fropofol binding site was sought within the grid size of 40 × 40 × 40 cubic angstroms using each adducted residue from photolabeling experiment as a grid center. No positional constraint was applied. We selected the docking position with the highest predicted affinity.

Glutaraldehyde crosslinking assay was done as we previously performed (18). TLR7 protein concentration was adjusted to 25 μM using buffer containing 10 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl. Propofol was added to the final concentration of 50 μM followed by R837 (10 μg/mL). The reaction was started by adding glutaraldehyde to a final concentration of 5 mM, incubated at room temperature for 25 min and quenched by adding Tris-HCl pH 7.5 to a final concentration of 100 mM. Crosslinked samples were analyzed by non-reducing SDS-PAGE. Quantitative analysis of the SDS-PAGE gel image was performed with image J.

The interaction between TLR7 and its downstream protein myeloid differentiation 88 (MyD88) was examined by immunoprecipitation. After HEK-TLR7 cells were stimulated with R837 for 6 hours with or without propofol (100 μM), they were subjected to lysis with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail Complete. Following centrifugation, supernatants were subjected to immunoprecipitation using rabbit anti-human TLR7 antibody (Cell Signaling technology; Danvers, MA) and Dynabeads Protein A immunoprecipitation kit (Thermofischer Scientific). Bead pellets were subjected to Western blot analysis and probed with mouse anti-human MyD88 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc.; Dallas, TX) followed by anti-mouse IgG-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) antibody (Cell Signaling). Immunoreactive proteins were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence.

Data were analyzed as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05. All the statistical calculations were performed using PRISM5 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Propofol and dexmedetomidine are common sedatives utilized extensively in intensive care unit (ICU) settings and surgical settings, usually as a continuous infusion. Midazolam and fentanyl are also used as intensive care sedatives. We examined the effect of these drugs on TLR7 activation. R837 is a TLR7 small molecule agonist. Only propofol attenuated R837-induced TLR7 activation (Fig. 1a). Propofol (1–100 μM) attenuated TLR7 activation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1b). We previously examined the effect of propofol on TLR2 and TLR4. Propofol at 10 μM did not affect TLR2 or TLR4 activation (4, 5). Propofol only at higher doses (50–100 μM) partially decreased TLR2 or TLR4 activation. We also examined TLR3, which recognizes double-stranded RNA viruses. Even at high dose (100 μM), propofol did not attenuate TLR3 activation (Fig. 1c). So among the TLRs tested, TLR7 was most sensitive to propofol. We also examined how long the effect of propofol on TLR7 function lasted. We found that TLR7 activation by R837 was completely back to the baseline state at 6 hours after propofol removal, but TLR7 function was in part attenuated at least for 4 hours (Fig. 1d).

Although we started propofol incubation at the indicated concentrations, we did not know if propofol sustained the same concentration during incubation. We found that propofol concentrations had decreased over time (Fig. 1e). Interestingly, propofol concentration was also reduced without cells, indicating that propofol was absorbed onto the culture well. In the presence of cells, propofol concentration was reduced further. Propofol is metabolized mainly by two main mechanisms (19). One is through conjugation by uridine 5’- diphosphate (UDP) glucuronosyltransferase to become propofol glucuronide. Another is via hydroxylation by cytochrome P450 to 2,6,-diisopropyl-1,4-quinol (4-hydroxypropofol), followed by conjugation to become 4-(2,6,diisopropyl-1,4-quinol)-sulphate, 1–2(2,6-diisopropyl-1,4-quinol)-glucuronide, and 4-(2,6-diiopropyl-1,5-quinol)-glucuronide (19, 20). Given HEK cells have UDP glucuronosyltransferase and cytochrome P450 (21, 22), it is likely that they metabolized propofol. Thus, the effect of propofol at the indicated concentrations on TLR7 functions could be more profound than our data in Fig. 1b if the concentrations were maintained at the same level.

Dendritic cells (DCs) are one of the major immune cells expressing TLR7 (23). Here, we examined the role of propofol in cytokine production from R837 stimulated bone marrow derived DCs. R837 stimulation increased TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-β mRNA levels (Fig. 2a). Their levels were attenuated by propofol. IFN is a critical anti-viral cytokine to increase host defense. First, we focused on the role of propofol in TLR7-mediated IFN production in the lungs, particularly because lung is a major organ affected by influenza and SARS-CoV2 viruses. Precision cut lung slice culture is a novel tool to examine the host response to external stimuli in a controlled fashion. Taking advantage of the lung slice culture system, we examined IFN production from the cultured lungs upon R837 stimulation. R837 stimulation upregulated IFN-β mRNA expression (Fig. 2b). However, propofol attenuated IFN-β expression responses (Fig. 2b). We also examined the role of propofol in IFN-β production in vivo. R837 was nasally instilled to mice. R837 increased IFN-β expression in the lungs, and propofol attenuated IFN-β expression (Fig. 2c).

Because propofol attenuated TLR7 activation, we examined if propofol directly bound to TLR7 by photolabeling experiment. TLR7 exists as a monomer in the absence of ligands. R837 binds to TLR7 and directly induces the formation of an activated TLR7 dimer. The binding site of R837 on TLR7 was previously called “site 1”, located in the dimerization interface (18). R837 binds at the two different sites in the dimerization interface (named site 1–1 and site 1–2) (Fig. 3a). Residues that R837 interacts with, obtained from PDB 5ZSF, are shown in Supplemental Table 1. Here we write two TLR7 that forms a dimer as TLR7 and TLR7*. D555 (D*555) and T586 (T*586) form hydrogen bonds with R837 (24). Other residues in the vicinity of R837 are involved in van der Waals interactions.

The photolabeling experiments using azi-propofol showed five adducted residues, indicating that propofol directly bound to TLR7 (Fig. 3b, Supplemental Table 2). Rigid docking showed five propofol binding pockets (Fig. 3c). Nearby amino acid residues for each docked propofol are shown in Supplemental Table 3. Among them, residues that interact with R837 are underlined in Supplemental Table 3. Y356, F408, T*532, D*555, L*557 and T*586 in pocket 2 are residues that interact with R837 at site 1–2. Y356, F408 and L*557 in pocket 3 are residues that interact with R837 at site 1–2. D555, L557, I585, T586, F*408, and K*432 are residues that interact with R837 at site 1–1. Propofol also bound to TLR7 at pocket 1 and pocket 5. Overall, our binding site analysis showed that propofol directly bound to TLR7 but did not compete with R837 for TLR7 (Fig. 3d).

Although propofol did not compete for the TLR7 pocket, it remains to be determined if propofol lessens TLR7 dimerization by R837. To determine whether propofol favors monomer or dimer formation, we performed glutaraldehyde crosslinking assay. R837 induced dimerization as expected (Fig. 4a). However, in the presence of propofol, TLR7 dimerization did not change (Fig. 4b). TLR7 antagonists can allow TLR7 dimerization in a conformation that is not favorable for TLR7 activation (18). Because TLR7-MyD88 binding is required for TLR7 activation, we determined if propofol would affect TLR7-MyD88 interaction. Our immunoprecipitation showed an increased association between TLR7 and MyD88 in the presence of R837, which was significantly attenuated by propofol (Fig. 4c). This supports the idea that propofol directly bound to TLR7, kept TLR7 in a dimer, but impaired the transduction of its activation signal. This also suggested that propofol altered the conformation of TLR7 dimer in a way not ideal for MyD88 binding as the reported TLR7 antagonists (18).

We previously delineated that hydroxyl (–OH) group in the propofol was critical for the interaction between propofol and its target molecule 5-lipoxygenase (17). The loss of hydroxyl group in the propofol decreased the number of binding sites and lessened the effect of propofol on 5-lipoxygenase (17). Whether or not this is applicable to TLR7-propofol interaction is not known. Fropofol also attenuated R837-mediated TLR7 activation, but the degree of attenuation was much less than propofol (Fig. 5a), which suggested that the hydroxyl group in the propofol would be important for TLR7-propofol interaction. We also performed photolabeling experiment using azi-fropofol. The adducted residues are shown in Supplemental Table 2 and Fig. 5b. Rigid docking showed two fropofol binding pockets (Fig. 5c), which was less than the number of propofol binding sites (five sites). Nearby amino acid residues for each docked fropofol are shown in Supplemental Table 4. Among them, residues that interact with R837 are underlined in Supplemental Table 4. Y356, F408, T*532, D*555, L*557, I*585 and T*586 in the pocket 1 are residues that interact with R837 at the site 1–2. F408 and I*585 in the pocket 2 are residues that also interact with R837 at the site 1–2. However, fropofol did not compete for TLR7 with R837 as in the case of propofol (Fig. 5d). Pocket 1 and Pocket 2 for fropofol are close to Pocket 2 and Pocket 3 for propofol. In contrast, pocket 4 for propofol binding site is unique to propofol, likely due to the hydroxyl group. Overall, these structural data are in line with our functional data and support the role of hydroxyl group in propofol in target molecule interaction.

Here we showed that intravenous sedative/anesthetic propofol directly bound to TLR7 and attenuated TLR7 activation and IFN production in vitro and in vivo. These findings are of great significance since virus illness can pose a significant harm to health including mortality, as seen in infections by influenza and SARS-CoV2 virus, both of which are recognized by TLR7.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, we have experienced a significant number of patients undergoing mechanical ventilation under sedation. In influenza virus infection, a subset of patients also requires mechanical ventilation (26). Sedation is an important component of medical care in critically ill patients in ICU. Sedation not only helps to reduce pain and anxiety but also allows patients to tolerate mechanical ventilation and invasive procedures (27). The use of short-acting opioids, propofol and dexmedetomidine is associated with shorter duration of mechanical ventilation with less side effects including delirium compared to drugs such as lorazepam (27–29). Therefore, propofol is a relatively common sedative in ICU. Propofol can be given as a bolus or a continuous infusion. In ICU setting, a continuous infusion is commonly used. The plasma concentration of patients receiving propofol for sedation was approximately 2 μg/mL (11.3 μM) (31). TLR7 activation was attenuated even at 1 μM, suggesting that TLR7 function could be affected by propofol at sedative concentrations. Propofol is also used for anesthesia. A typical plasma concentration of propofol is 3–11 μg/mL (17–62 μM) to maintain anesthesia (32, 33), although much higher doses may be used. TLR3 function was not affected by propofol at 100 μM. TLR2 and TLR4 was not affected by 10 μM, but affected at high concentration (100 μM). Therefore, TLR2 and TLR4 may be affected by propofol when it is used for anesthesia at relatively higher doses. Our study of popofol on TLR7 function certainly suggests the potential importance of sedative choice for patients with illness from these viruses. We also found that propofol attenuated TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-β mRNA production in R837-stimulated DCs derived from the bone marrow. We also used an in vitro model by utilizing a lung slice culture system and an in vivo mouse model of intranasal R837 instillation that showed increased IFN- β expression in the lungs upon R837 stimulation and attenuation of IFN- β expression by propofol. Examining those patients’ immune cells under propofol sedation compared to other sedatives will be informative to determine clinical translation.

In the present study, we first used a reporter assay system using HEK cells to examine the effect of propofol on TLR7 activation. By measuring propofol concentration in the culture supernatant, we found that propofol concentrations were significantly reduced over time. Although it was largely due to the absorption of propofol onto the culture well, propofol might have been metabolized as well; HEK cells possesses UDP glucuronosyltransferase and cytochrome P450, both of which are responsible for propofol metabolism (21, 22). Regardless, propofol might attenuateTLR7 activation more than demonstrated in the reporter assay when propofol concentration is maintained throughout the incubation. In addition, the effect of propofol on TLR7 activation lasted more than 4 hours after the removal of propofol from the culture supernatant. This may indicate that propofol remains to bind to TLR7 for a while after removal from the culture supernatant. From clinical standpoint, a little over 4 hours of residual effects may not be a significant issue. The duration and dosage of propofol infused continuously in ICU will be a primary issue from TLR7 activation perspective. TLRs are one of the major pattern recognition receptors to recognize a wide range of microbes. TLR7 is critical to detect ssRNA viruses. It has been shown that lack of TLR7 worsened disease progression in influenza infection (30). TLR7 functional attenuation was also associated with more severe COVID-19 (8). IFN is produced as a result of viral infection upon TLR activation and serves as an antiviral cytokine (25), along with other inflammatory cytokines.

From TLR7 antagonist standpoint, our data suggested that propofol altered R837-induced TLR7 dimer conformation in a way to disfavor its interaction with MyD88. Fropofol experiment suggested that fropofol bound to fewer sites on TLR7 compared to propofol. Certainly the presence of the –OH residue would be critical to develop TLR7 antagonists when use propofol as a prototype. The previously reported TLR7 antagonists Cpd-6 and Cpd-7 bind to TLR7 at the same pocket where R837 binds (PDB: 6LW0, 6LW1) (18). This is in contrast to propofol, which did not bind to the pocket. One of the limitations in this study was that our structure analysis was based on the already reported TLR7 structure. Evaluating the detailed TLR7 structure in the presence of propofol using X-ray co-crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy (34) will be a future direction to refine the structural interaction and further understand this unique interaction of propofol with TLR7.

In conclusion, our data showed that propofol would act as direct TLR7 antagonist. The clinical implication of propofol-mediated attenuation of TLR7 activity is critical for future investigation.
