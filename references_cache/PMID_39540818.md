# Atypical Mycobacterial Pneumonia in 2 Siblings with a Novel Hypomorphic NEMO/IKBKG Mutation.
**Authors:** Meric Z, Aydemir S, Kilic Baskan A, Gemici Karaaslan B, Kendir Demirkol Y, Kilinc Sakalli AA, Kiykim A, Cokugras H
**Journal:** Turk Arch Pediatr (2024)
**DOI:** [10.5152/TurkArchPediatr.2024.24087](https://doi.org/10.5152/TurkArchPediatr.2024.24087)

## Content

Dear Editor,

The NEMO protein is encoded by the IKBKG gene that controls the intracellular signal transmission of the NF-kB (nuclear factor kappa light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells) pathway, which plays a fundamental role in the regulation of genes involved in inflammation and cell survival. NEMO syndrome impairs both innate and adaptive immunity, leading to an impaired cellular response and antibody production. This may result in severe bacterial infections early in life, as well as a susceptibility to atypical mycobacterial infections.1-3

Null mutations are typically lethal in males, while female carriers present with X-linked dominant disease. On the other hand, hypomorphic mutations in males are often associated with EDA-ID (anhidrotic ectodermal dysplasia with immunodeficiency). This is characterized by sparse hair, hypodontia, and an inability to sweat leading to episodes of hyperthermia.1 Also, some mutations can cause X-linked recessive Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) without ectodermal dysplasia.4


The World Health Organization estimates that approximately 1.1 million children under the age of 15 develop tuberculosis each year, with many more carrying a latent form of the infection. Children with primary immunodeficiencies associated with MSMD are particularly susceptible to severe infections with atypical mycobacteria.5 Here we report the mutation of IKBKG (NEMO) in 2 siblings with atypical mycobacterial pneumonia without evidence of ectodermal dysplasia.

A 17-year-old boy (Patient 1 (P1)) was referred to our department for evaluation of primary immunodeficiency in the setting of Mycobacterium bovis pneumonia. He was the first child of non-consanguineous parents (Figure 1). He had recurrent sinopulmonary infections since the age of 6 and had undergone surgery for perforated otitis media. He was vaccinated with Bacille de Calmette-Guérin (BCG). At the age of 14, he developed polyarthritis and uveitis with negative autoantibodies but did not receive immunosuppressive treatment. At the age of 16 years, he presented with cervical lymphadenitis, cough, and fever and was subsequently diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis and treated with rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for 9 months. The patient’s physical examination revealed failure to thrive. His growth parameters showed weight in the 3rd percentile and length in the 10th-25th percentile for his age. On auscultation, vesicular breath sounds were heard bilaterally along with crepitations, especially in the left lung areas. He had enlarged lymph nodes in the cervical chain, which were painless. The edge of the liver was palpable 1-2 cm below the right costal margin. A BCG scar was present. Laboratory investigations revealed (Table 1) lymphopenia, decreased serum IgM levels, and increased IgA levels for his age. Flow cytometric immunophenotyping revealed decreased T cells and increased B cells, predominantly naive, with a decreased switched memory subset. Chest CT documented bilateral bronchiectasis.

P2 was a 10-year-old boy who underwent screening after his brother (P1) was diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis. M. bovis was detected in diagnostic specimens, and he was started on antimycobacterial therapy at the age of 5. He was born full-term with a normal birth history. His medical history also included recurrent sinopulmonary infections starting at the age of 2 years. He had been hospitalized for pneumonia twice. Laboratory investigations revealed decreased serum IgM levels and increased IgA for his age. His class-switched B cell subset was decreased, with normal T and B cell counts.

Both patients were started on Ig replacement therapy and antibacterial prophylaxis. A targeted panel for MSMD was performed. DNA sequencing of the coding region of IKBKG revealed a novel c.268delG (p.Ala90GInfs*93) hemizygous variation. The diagnosis was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both patients carried the same mutation, as did the mother. Furthermore, we have been screening the mother’s sisters for carrier status and testing the patients’ male cousins for the disease. The results were shared with the parents for future pregnancies. However, this study has some limitations. We were unable to assess the NFKB signaling pathway in patients.

MSMD is a well-established clinical entity observed in NEMO protein deficiency and several other genetic disorders.6 In 2004, Orange et al. reported 7 boys with mutations leading to NEMO immunodeficiency, most of whom had experienced mycobacterial disease.2 Infections with several atypical mycobacteria, such as Mycobacterium avium complex infections, have been reported in patients with reduced levels of NEMO protein due to splice site mutations in the IKBKG gene.2,6,7

The immunological profile of NEMO deficiency is variable but may include hypogammaglobulinemia with elevated IgM and IgA levels and reduced specific antibody responses. This is attributed to impaired CD40-mediated B cell class switch recombination. While most cases in the literature show elevated IgM levels, Orange et al. reported that 2 cases did not show a hyper-IgM phenotype, similar to ours.8 Ohnishi et al9 described 2 female patients with incontinentia pigmenti and heterozygous NEMO mutations, who had normal and low IgM levels. Although the pathophysiology remains unclear, normal and low IgM levels can be observed in patients with IKBKG mutations. In addition, there may be reduced toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling and NK cell cytotoxicity, while T cell numbers and proliferation may show variability.2,3,10,11

Besides immune deficiency, various autoinflammatory disorders are also observed in patients.12 Inflammatory conditions or autoimmunity were reported in 25% of affected patients, with the most frequent being inflammatory colitis (21%). While inflammatory colitis was not observed in either patient, one of our patients had chronic arthritis, which was reported at a frequency of 3% in affected patients.10 The mechanisms causing autoinflammation remain unclear, but Yilmaz et al. have identified that an expanded population of neutrophil and low-density granulocyte populations and activated neutrophils represent potential cellular sources underlying the clinical autoinflammatory pathophysiology in NEMO deficiency.13

NEMO deficiency, which is often associated with mycobacterial infections, requires continued antimicrobial prophylaxis and Ig replacement therapy. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) has been tried for hypomorphic NEMO mutations, with varying success. Although HSCT is an effective treatment to correct the immune disorder in patients with NEMO deficiency, it is only preferred in severe cases due to the risk of complications. Other recommendations include regular follow-ups to enable early identification of any infectious diseases and autoinflammatory disorders.14,15

This case shows the importance of considering IKBKG mutations in children with atypical mycobacterial infections, regardless of the presence of ectodermal dysplasia. Early detection and diagnosis are essential for protecting them from severe infections and providing their families with genetic counseling for future pregnancies.
