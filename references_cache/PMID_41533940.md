# FGF10/IGSF3 Variants in Bony Congenital Nasolacrimal Duct Obstruction: A Genotype-Phenotype Study of Syndromic Versus Isolated Disease.
**Authors:** Li Y, Feng ZX, Cui Y, Sun J, Liu W, Xiao X, Quan X, Zhang C, Li L
**Journal:** Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1167/iovs.66.15.69](https://doi.org/10.1167/iovs.66.15.69)

## Content

PURPOSE: The purpose of this study was to identify pathogenic variants associated with congenital bony nasolacrimal duct obstruction (bony CNLDO) and to clarify genotype-phenotype correlations.

METHODS: In this single-center retrospective case study, children with clinically confirmed bony CNLDO and their relatives underwent detailed ophthalmic, systemic examinations, orbital computed tomography (CT), and maxillofacial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on peripheral blood samples. Bioinformatics tools were utilized to predict variant pathogenicity, and classifications were performed according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.

RESULTS: Thirty-two participants were included in this study, comprising six families and seven sporadic cases. WES identified 8 novel FGF10 variants, including 6 missense mutations (c.598C>G, c.316T>G, c.395T>C, c.316T>C, c.327C>G, and c.332T>G), one intronic splicing mutation (c.429+2T>A), and one heterozygous deletion at chromosome 5p12 encompassing the FGF10 gene. Two sporadic cases had no identifiable pathogenic variants. All patients with FGF10 variants exhibited syndromic aplasia of lacrimal and salivary glands (ALSGs) phenotype characterized by bony CNLDO, punctal anomalies, and hypoplasia of lacrimal, parotid, and submandibular glands. Three novel IGSF3 variants were also identified including two missense mutations (c.2872G>C and c.2531G>A) and one nonsense mutation (c.2416G>T). In contrast, individuals carrying IGSF3 variants showed isolated bony CNLDO with normal glandular morphology.

CONCLUSIONS: Bony CNLDO is predominantly a monogenic disorder involving FGF10 or IGSF3. FGF10 mutations are associated with syndromic, multi-gland hypoplasia, whereas IGSF3 mutations result in isolated bony CNLDO. These findings enhance understanding of the genetic heterogeneity and genotype-phenotype correlations underlying bony CNLDO, with real-world implications for genetic diagnosis and counseling.

Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction (CNLDO) is one of the most common ophthalmologic conditions in pediatric ophthalmology, with an incidence of up to 20% of newborns.1,2 Bony CNLDO (bony CNLDO) is a rare subtype caused by aplasia or hypoplasia of the bony nasolacrimal duct rather than a membranous obstruction.3 Orbital computed tomography (CT) is the primary imaging modality for diagnosing bony CNLDO.4 Due to the rigid bony blockage, conventional treatments such as lacrimal probing and intubation are ineffective. However, endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) has shown a nearly 100% success rate and is now the preferred surgical approach.5,6

Our team previously reported seven patients from three families who presented with both bony CNLDO and aplasia of the lacrimal, parotid, and submandibular glands. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) of peripheral blood identified novel fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10) gene mutations in all 3 families, implicating bony CNLDO as a novel phenotype of aplasia of lacrimal and salivary glands (ALSGs).7 ALSG is a rare autosomal dominant disorder characterized by underdevelopment or absence of lacrimal and salivary glands. Its ocular manifestations include lacrimal gland aplasia, hypoplasia or agenesis,8–10 lacrimal punctum agenesis,11 lacrimal sac mucocele,12 and membranous or bony CNLDO.7,13 The disease is linked to FGF10 haploinsufficiency.9,14

Although FGF10 mutations have been linked to familial bony CNLDO, the genetic basis of sporadic cases, those without a family history, remains largely unexplored. To address this gap, we analyzed clinical phenotypes and genetic testing results in a consecutive cohort of hereditary and sporadic cases. In addition to FGF10, we also assessed other candidate genes that may contribute to sporadic bony CNLDO. By comprehensively evaluating all affected patients, this study seeks to elucidate the genetic underpinnings of bony CNLDO and improve genetic counseling for this rare condition.

This single-center retrospective study included patients diagnosed with bony CNLDO at the Department of Ophthalmology, Beijing Children's Hospital, from 2020 to 2025. Two groups were enrolled: (1) sporadic cases without known family history, and (2) probands and their family members from families with a history of bony CNLDO. Inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of bony CNLDO through ophthalmic examination and imaging evaluation, and family members of probands with positive family history. Exclusion criteria included bony CNLDO secondary to trauma, surgery, or infection, or any participants (including sporadic patients, probands, or relatives) who failed to complete the required examinations.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital (Approval Number: [2024]-Y-240-D) and adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. All participants or their legal guardians signed written informed consent after a full explanation of the study's purpose and potential risks.

All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmic, systemic, and imaging evaluations. Ophthalmic examinations included slit-lamp examination, fundus examination, and lacrimal irrigation. Systemic physical examinations included evaluation of facial features, external ears, oral cavity, finger and toes, as well as pure-tone audiometry. Audiometry and oral examinations were performed by ear, nose, and throat (ENT) specialists and stomatology specialists. Imaging examinations included orbital CT and maxillofacial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). As this study was conducted at a pediatric specialty hospital, audiometry, oral examinations, and MRI scans were performed on pediatric patients only.

Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients with sporadic bony CNLDO, probands, and their family members. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Blood Midi Kit (QIAGEN). Library construction was performed using the MyGenostics GenCap Whole Exome Capture Kit and 150 bp paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform.

After quality control of raw data, sequences were aligned to the UCSC hg19 (GRCh37) human reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner. The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) HaplotypeCaller was used to detect single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (Indels), followed by GATK variant filtering to optimize call accuracy. All candidate variants were subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing.

Based on the WES data, ExomeDepth version 1.1.10 algorithm was applied to detect copy number variations (CNVs) across the exome by analyzing sequencing coverage depth across target exon regions. All candidate variants were validated by array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH).

All identified variants were functionally annotated using ANNOVAR and cross-referenced against population databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project (1000 Genomes), Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC) database, the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database (dbSNP), and the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD).

For CNV interpretation, results were cross-referenced with the DECIPHER database to exclude non-pathogenic CNV regions, validate CNV loci, and assess the disease association and relevant gene functions. Gene-level clinical associations were explored using the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database.

The potential functional impact of missense variants was predicted using multiple in silico tools: Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner (REVEL), Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT), Polymorphism Phenotyping version 2 (PolyPhen-2), and MutationTaster. Effects of splice variants on transcript processing were evaluated using the Rare Disease Data Center (RDDC) tool (https://rddc.tsinghua-gd.org/) and SpliceAI (https://spliceailookup.broadinstitute.org/). The structural domain affiliation of mutation sites was analyzed via the Universal Protein Resource (UniProt) database. Variants were classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, or benign according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG).

Thirty-two Han Chinese individuals were recruited, comprising 3 newly identified bony CNLDO probands and 6 relatives from families 1 to 3, with 7 newly diagnosed sporadic cases, and 16 previously reported members from families 4 to 6 (reported in our prior study7; Fig. 1). No consanguineous was identified within three generations. Age at diagnosis ranged from 2 to 70 years. The familial probands were diagnosed at 3, 13, 7, 8, 10, and 5 years of age; the sporadic cases at 10, 2, 4, 8, 7, 5, and 12 years. Seventeen patients had clinically confirmed bony CNLDO (10 bilateral and 7 unilateral), whereas the remaining participants had normal lacrimal anatomy and were either asymptomatic FGF10/IGSF3 variant carriers or non-carriers. All affected patients had persistent epiphora, and purulent discharge was noted in the proband of family 1 and sporadic case S4. Twelve patients underwent DCR (7 bilateral and 5 unilateral), achieving complete symptomatic resolution with postoperative lacrimal patency. Surgery was declined in the proband of family 1 due to financial constraints. No reflux was observed in other subjects. Orbital CT confirmed corresponding unilateral or bilateral bony nasolacrimal duct atresia in all affected patients. MRI showed variable degrees of lacrimal and salivary gland abnormalities. Parenchymal thinning of the lacrimal and parotid glands, accompanied by submandibular gland hypoplasia, was observed in the proband of family 1, in patients from families 4 to 6, and in sporadic cases S2 to S4 (Fig. 2). Sporadic case S1 showed thinning of the lacrimal glands and complete absence of signal in the parotid and submandibular glands, consistent with multi-gland aplasia. MRI findings were normal in the probands of families 2 and 3 and in sporadic cases S5 to S7.

Systemic examinations of all probands revealed no ear or facial deformities, normal number and structure of fingers and toes, and normal hearing on pure-tone audiometry. Only the family 1 proband had multiple dental caries.

No disease-related gene mutations were detected in sporadic casea S6 and S7. Among these, 4 FGF10 variants and 1 large deletion were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to ACMG criteria. Most IGSF3 variants were inherited and interpreted as variants of uncertain significance or likely benign.

In family 1, both the proband and his mother carried a novel splice site variant, c.429+2T>A, located in intron 2 of FGF10. The mother was an asymptomatic carrier, whereas the father was unaffected (Fig. 3A). Predictive modeling using RDDC suggest that this mutation created a new splice donor site, introducing a 4-base-pair insertion that resulted in a frameshift (Fig. 4A). Supporting this, SpliceAI analysis predicted decreased splicing efficiency and likely caused exon 2 skipping. Pathogenicity analysis based on the ACMG guidelines classified this mutation as pathogenic.

Three de novo missense mutations of FGF10 were identified in sporadic cases S1, S2, and S3, respectively: c.598C>G in exon 3 (p.His200Asp), c.316T>G in exon 1 (p.Cys106Gly), and c.395T>C in exon 2 (p.Leu132Ser). None of the parents carried the corresponding mutations (Figs. 3G–I). Protein function prediction models including REVEL, SIFT, and PolyPhen-2 predicted a benign effect for c.316T>G, whereas MutationTaster suggested pathogenicity, potentially causing disease or impairing protein function. All 4 protein function prediction models predicted deleterious effects for both c.598C>G and c.395T>C. According to ACMG guidelines, c.598C>G was classified as a variant of uncertain significance, whereas c.316T>G and c.395T>C were both categorized as likely pathogenic variants.

In sporadic case S4, WES showed a heterozygous deletion on chromosome 5p12, which was confirmed by CNV analysis spanning approximately 2.61 Mb (Fig. 4B). The deletion encompassed multiple genes, including FGF10, HCN1, and NNT. OMIM analysis supported its clinical relevance, and ACMG guidelines classified this variant as pathogenic. Notably, all identified FGF10 mutations and the 5p12 deletion were absent from public databases, confirming their status as novel variants.

Additional genetic variants were found in the IGSF3 gene in families 2 and 3 and sporadic patient S5. In family 2, both the proband and his asymptomatic father carried a missense mutation c.2872G>C in exon 10 (p.Val958Leu), whereas the mother did not carry the variant (Fig. 3B). Although REVEL predicted the amino acid change as likely benign, and other predictive models including SIFT, PolyPhen-2, and MutationTaster also supported a benign interpretation, the variant was ultimately classified as a variant of uncertain significance according to ACMG guidelines. In family 3, the proband and his mother both carried a missense mutation c.2531G>A in exon 10 (p.Gly844Glu), whereas the father was a non-carrier (Fig. 3C). The minor allele frequency (MAF) of this mutation in normal populations was 0.016. REVEL, SIFT, and PolyPhen-2 all predicted a benign effect, although MutationTaster suggested the variant may be disease-causing. ACMG guidelines classified it as a variant of uncertain significance.

In sporadic case S5, sequencing identified a nonsense mutation, c.2416G>T, in exon 9 of IGSF3, leading to a premature stop codon at position 806 (p.Glu806X; Fig. 3J). This variant was absent from public databases, and although REVEL, SIFT, and PolyPhen-2 models could not predict its effect, MutationTaster classified it as disease-causing. ACMG guidelines indicated this mutation as likely pathogenic. A comprehensive summary of all genetic variants, their predicted functional impact, and ACMG classification are presented in Table 2.

This study demonstrates that the rare bony CNLDO is predominantly a monogenic disorder with variable penetrance, most often attributable to FGF10 or IGSF3 variants. This conclusion is supported by the pathogenic variants detected among affected individuals in our cohort. FGF10 mutations were associated with syndromic presentation characterized by hypoplasia or aplasia of the lacrimal and major salivary glands (ALSG phenotype), whereas IGSF3 mutations were linked to isolated bony CNLDO in the setting of otherwise normal glandular anatomy. Altogether, we identified 8 novel FGF10 variants and 3 novel IGSF3 variants, expanding the known mutation spectrum and reinforcing distinct genotype-phenotype correlations.


FGF10, located on chromosomal region 5p13-p12, encodes a 208-amino-acid paracrine growth factor that interacts with FGFR2b and heparan sulfate to regulate epithelial-mesenchymal signaling during morphogenesis.15,16 Functional studies have shown that FGF10 knockout mice exhibit agenesis of the lungs, limbs, lacrimal glands, and nasolacrimal duct.17 Consistent with this, patients in our cohort with FGF10 mutations presented with features of aplasia of the lacrimal and salivary glands (ALSG phenotype), punctal agenesis, and bony CNLDO.

A novel splice-site mutation (c.429+2T>A) in family 1 was predicted to cause exon 2 skipping and significantly reduced splicing efficiency. Consistent with our findings, Freund et al. discovered a c.429 + 1G>T mutation in patients with ALSG, and in vitro splicing experiments confirmed exon 2 skipping that resulted in functional haploinsufficiency.18,19 Interestingly, the variant was inherited from an asymptomatic carrier, highlighting incomplete penetrance, which may be attributable to epigenetic or environmental modulation of FGF10 expression.20

Moreover, we reported the first case of bony CNLDO caused by large heterozygous deletion of 5p12 (approximately 2.61 Mb) encompassing FGF10, HCN1, and NNT. This CNV was classified as pathogenic according to the joint consensus standards of ACMG and the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen).21 No additional neurologic or endocrine abnormalities typically related to NNT deletion was seen.22

In contrast, IGSF3, located on chromosomal 1p13.1, encodes a 1194-amino-acid transmembrane protein involved in epithelial cell junction formation via interaction with tetraspanins through its Glu-Trp-Ile (EWI) motif. IGSF3 is essential for maintaining epithelial polarity and promoting lumenogenesis that are integral to lacrimal duct morphogenesis.23,24 Disrupted IGSF3 activity has been shown to impair wound repair, cytoskeletal organization, and cellular migration.25 Foster et al. identified a homozygous frameshift mutation of IGSF3 in patients with CNLDO and lacrimal sac fistula.26 Wang et al. detected 10 IGSF3 variants, including 4 synonymous and 6 missense mutations in 3 families with congenital absence of lacrimal puncta and canaliculi. One family also exhibited bony CNLDO and harbored three specific mutations.27 We now reported two novel missense mutations (c.2872G>C, c.2531G>A) and one nonsense mutation (c.2416G>T) in patients with isolated bony CNLDO and preserved salivary gland architecture.

Notably, no pathogenic variants in FGF10 or IGSF3 were identified in sporadic cases S6 and S7. Whereas the vast majority of cases in our consecutive series were attributable to monogenic mutations in FGF10 and IGSF3, these exceptions suggest that alternative mechanisms may underlie bony CNLDO in a subset of patients. Deep intronic variants, epigenetic modifications, or environmental influences may contribute both to cases without an identifiable mutation and to asymptomatic carriers harboring pathogenic variants.

A recent study by Ru et al. demonstrated that Prickle1, a core component of the Wnt/Planar Cell Polarity (Wnt/PCP) pathway, is essential for tear duct morphogenesis in mice. Prickle1 mutant mice exhibit malformations of the nasolacrimal ducts and lacrimal canaliculi, leading to epiphora,28 phenotypically resembling human bony CNLDO. These findings suggest that disruptions in non-FGF signaling pathways, such as Wnt/PCP, may also contribute to tear drainage anomalies. No variants in Wnt/PCP pathway genes were detected in our cohort, which may reflect the targeted scope of WES or the rarity of such mutations in humans.

This study is limited by its modest sample size of six families and seven sporadic cases, which may restrict generalizability. In addition, WES cannot reliably detect deep intronic, regulatory, or structural variants outside coding regions, and may therefore miss pathogenic variants in noncoding or unsequenced loci. Such limitations could account for the absence of identifiable mutations in sporadic cases S6 and S7. Finally, the inferred pathogenicity of identified variants was based primarily on in silico predictions. Future research incorporating whole-genome sequencing, epigenomic profiling, and functional validation will be critical to define the full genetic spectrum of bony CNLDO, elucidate the mechanisms of FGF10 and IGSF3 dysfunction, and clarify the biological basis of incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity.

We identified 8 novel FGF10 variants and 3 novel IGSF3 variants in patients with bony CNLDO. FGF10 mutations were associated with syndromic disease characterized by bony CNLDO and hypoplasia of the lacrimal and major salivary glands, consistent with the ALSG phenotype, whereas IGSF3 mutations were linked to isolated bony CNLDO without systemic involvement. These findings reinforce that bony CNLDO is predominantly a monogenic disorder, whereas expanding the known mutational spectrum and refining genotype-phenotype correlations. Our findings improve the clinical distinction between syndromic and non-syndromic forms and provide a foundation for future genetic counseling and research.
