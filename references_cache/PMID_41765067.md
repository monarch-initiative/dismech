# A subclinical cytokine storm is present before the clinical diagnosis of amniotic fluid embolism: implications for the prediction and prevention of maternal death.
**Authors:** Romero R, Meyyazhagan A, Gudicha DW, Chaiworspongsa T, Awonuga AO, Tarca AL, Gomez-Lopez N, Hassan SS
**Journal:** Am J Obstet Gynecol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ajog.2026.02.039](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajog.2026.02.039)

## Content

A cytokine storm preceded amniotic fluid embolism and measurement of cytokines can make the condition predictable and treatable

Amniotic fluid embolism (AFE) is a catastrophic complication of pregnancy and the second leading cause of maternal death within the first 24 hours after delivery, following in frequency postpartum hemorrhage. The condition was first described 100 years ago1 and subsequently attributed to embolization of amniotic fluid into the maternal pulmonary circulation2. This mechanical explanation has been questioned, because the amount of amniotic material found in the lungs is considered insufficient to explain abrupt cardiovascular collapse, experimental infusion of filtered amniotic fluid in animals has not reliably reproduced the syndrome, and an anaphylactoid mechanism lacks an identified allergen.3,4

We previously reported that two women who died with a clinical diagnosis of AFE had markedly increased concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) at admission, before the onset of symptoms,5 and other investigators have described cytokine elevations at the time of cardiovascular collapse in four additional lethal cases.6 TNF-α is a central mediator of septic shock, capable of inducing myocardial depression, vasodilation, and activation of coagulation through tissue factor expression, thereby providing a plausible link between inflammation, cardiovascular collapse, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).5 These observations suggested that, at least in some patients, AFE may involve a cytokine storm.

The objective of this communication is to determine whether evidence of hyperinflammation was present before the onset of clinical symptoms in a patient who developed lethal AFE.

We previously reported a patient at 41 5/7 weeks who was in spontaneous labor with meconium stained amniotic fluid, developed a fever after epidural analgesia, was delivered by vacuum extraction, and later experienced postpartum disseminated intravascular coagulation and cardiopulmonary collapse, dying 19 hours after delivery (the family declined an autopsy).5 The TNF-α concentration was extremely high at 1 ng/mL (normal range is <2-15 pg/mL). That observation motivated examination of whether broader cytokine activation might also be detectable before the onset of AFE (the original report referred to two patients. However, we could only find samples in our Bank in one patient).

The present analysis involved two plasma specimens from this patient: (1) an admission sample obtained before the onset of symptoms/diagnosis and (2) a sample collected at the time of AFE diagnosis. Both were retrieved from the biobank of the Perinatology Research Branch (PRB) housed at Wayne State University and Detroit Medical Center (Protocol: Repository of Biological Samples from Normal and Complicated Pregnancies; WSU IRB# 031318MP2F). The only cytokine previously quantified was TNF-α.5 In contrast, the current analysis employed a multiplex bead-based assay to quantify multiple cytokines simultaneously using the Meso Scale Discovery Assay Platform (MESO QuickPlex SQ 120). Control plasma samples from healthy women at term before (n=6) and during labor (n=6) were processed under similar conditions. The admission plasma sample, obtained when the patient appeared completely asymptomatic, showed extraordinary elevations of key inflammatory cytokines relative to women in labor. IL-6 measured 51,735 pg/mL, corresponding to a ~103,000-fold increase over the term-labor reference group. IL-8 measured 5,042 pg/mL, approximately a 2,800-fold elevation. IL-10 measured 269 pg/mL, representing a ~700-fold increase. These concentrations were far above the ranges observed in physiologic labor and exceeded values previously reported in clinical chorioamnionitis,7 fetal death,8 or severe postpartum hemorrhage.9

The sample obtained at the time of AFE diagnosis also demonstrated marked abnormalities, with IL-6 at 16,340 pg/mL (a ~32,500-fold increase over controls), IL-8 at 1,599 pg/mL (an ~884-fold increase), and IL-10 at 298 pg/mL (an ~784-fold increase). Although the magnitude varied across analytes, the data confirms persistent and profound inflammatory activation at both time points.

The present report shows that a cytokine storm was present before the clinical diagnosis of AFE in this patient and has several important implications: 1) a subset of patients who develop AFE may have a cytokine storm detectable in maternal blood before the onset of symptoms of AFE; 2) the concentrations of IL-6 and other cytokines observed at admission greatly exceeded those seen in normal labor and in clinical chorioamnionitis,7 indicating that the inflammatory response was not simply physiologic; 3) the trigger of this cytokine storm remains unknown and may involve microbial or sterile inflammation, both of which are recognized mechanisms in obstetric complications;10 4) the postpartum decompensation may be related, in part, to the loss of placental immunoregulatory functions after delivery, consistent with prior evidence that trophoblast contact can deactivate maternal neutrophils;11 5) admission blood samples may provide an opportunity to identify a subset of patients at risk for AFE before catastrophic deterioration occurs, raising the possibility of prediction and, ultimately, prevention; 6) therapies aimed at modulating hyperinflammation, such as those used in other cytokine storm syndromes including severe COVID-19,12,13 may merit cautious exploration in selected patients with evidence of hyperinflammation;14 and 7) Reduced C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH) activity, reported in some patients with amniotic fluid embolism, may contribute to the hyperinflammatory state by permitting unchecked activation of the complement and contact systems, and kallikrein–kinin systems, leading to vascular permeability, hypotension, and amplification of inflammatory cascades characteristic of a cytokine storm.15,16 It is not known whether C1-INH dysregulation precedes clinical deterioration or reflects downstream consumption, these observations suggest that restoring C1-INH activity may have therapeutic value in a subset of patients with AFE and marked inflammatory activation or immune-paralysis.14,17 Comprehensive prospective studies are urgently needed to define the frequency, mechanisms, and predictive value of preclinical cytokine activation in AFE and to determine whether early recognition and targeted intervention can reduce maternal deaths from a catastrophic condition first described almost a century ago and still poorly understood. This is now feasible because assays for cytokines such as IL-6 can now take 1-3 hours using widely available automated chemiluminescent immunoassays (e.g., Roche, Siemens, Beckman Coulter platform).
