# Pathogenic mechanism of the PLS1 gene variant in hearing loss and functional validation in a zebrafish model.
**Authors:** Xu T, Yang T, Wang H, Xu L
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-47079-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-47079-4)

## Content

PLS1, encoding an actin-bundling protein Plastin 1, is one of the pathogenic genes responsible for autosomal dominant non-syndromic hearing loss. The cellular damage caused by mutant plastin proteins may arise from loss of function and/or dominant-negative effects. In this study, we investigated the pathogenic mechanism of PLS1 mutations by establishing both in vitro cellular models and in vivo zebrafish models. Immunoprecipitation demonstrated that the ΔExon8 mutation disrupts the intramolecular interaction between the ABD1 and ABD2 domains, while immunofluorescence colocalization revealed that the mutation alters the binding pattern of PLS1 to actin bundles and disturbs cytoskeletal organization. Through zebrafish FM1-43 uptake assays, we showed that the mutant protein impaired the functional opening and permeability of the stereociliary mechanoelectrical transduction (MET) channel. Although both PLS1-knockout zebrafish and zebrafish expressing ΔExon8-PLS1 exhibited behavioral signs of hearing impairment, defects in MET channel uptake were observed only in the mutant-expressing fish. Notably, neither PLS1 deficiency nor re-expression of human wild-type PLS1 in knockout zebrafish reproduces this MET defect. Thus, the mutation not only reduces native PLS1 function but also interferes with normal MET channel activity. In summary, our findings indicate that the DFNA76 phenotype caused by the PLS1ΔExon8 mutation is likely due to a dominant-negative effect with partial loss-of-function.

Hearing loss is one of the most common hereditary sensory disorders, affecting approximately one in every 500 newborns1. More than 100 causative genes have been identified, with non-syndromic hearing loss accounting for the majority of cases2. Among the non-syndromic forms, 128 autosomal recessive genes have been characterized, which comprise approximately 20% of all cases; the remaining cases follow X-linked or mitochondrial inheritance3. Autosomal recessive hearing loss typically presents early with rapid progression, whereas autosomal dominant forms usually progress more slowly and are often influenced by environmental or peripheral factors4.

Although many pathogenic variants associated with autosomal dominant non-syndromic hearing loss (ADNSHL) have been reported, most studies have focused on clinical phenotypes. Many animal studies rely primarily on gene knockout models, with limited in-depth functional evaluation of the mutant proteins themselves5 With the rapid development of adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapy, new therapeutic avenues have emerged for patients carrying loss-of-function variants6,7. Therefore, it is essential to define the pathogenic mechanism of each mutation, particularly distinguishing between loss-of-function and dominant-negative mutations.

PLS1 encodes Plastin-1, an actin-binding protein belonging to the plastin family that includes PLS1, PLS2, and PLS38. PLS1 is predominantly expressed in intestinal epithelial microvilli and stereocilia of cochlear and vestibular hair cells9–12. Plastin proteins contain two N-terminal EF-hand calcium-binding domains and two C-terminal actin-binding domains (ABD1 and ABD2), each consisting of a pair of calponin homology (CH) domains13,14. ABD2 has a higher affinity for actin than ABD1, but ABD1 imposes an autoinhibitory constraint on ABD2. When ABD1 first engages actin bundles, autoinhibition is relieved, allowing ABD2 to initiate effective cross-linking15.

PLS1 is a well-known ADNSHL protein. The first reported PLS1 pathogenic variants were three missense mutations—c.805G > A (p.E269K), c.713G > T (p.L238R), and c.383T > C (p.F128S)—identified in three unrelated European families with autosomal dominant deafness16. Schrauwen et al. later identified a p.L363F variant in a Hungarian Roma family17, and the p.E269K mutation was also detected in a Turkish family18. In animal models, Pls1-knockout mice displayed shorter and thinner stereocilia and developed moderate progressive hearing loss, suggesting that PLS1 contributes to mild or progressive hearing impairment in humans14,19,20.

We recently identified a Chinese family with ADNSHL carrying a splice-altering PLS1 variant that leads to exon 8 skipping21. Affected individuals exhibit progressive, unilateral or bilateral, moderate-to-severe, high-frequency hearing loss that begins in childhood and worsens with age. However, the mechanism by which this PLS1 mutation causes severe hearing impairment in humans remains unclear. In this study, we further investigated the pathogenic effects of the previously reported PLS1 c.981G > A (ΔExon8) variant. Using cellular and zebrafish models, we found that exon-8 deletion disrupts the ABD1 domain and impairs the ABD1–ABD2 interaction. Given prior studies showing that ABD1 exerts autoinhibitory control over ABD2, our results are consistent with the possibility that ΔExon8 weakens this intramolecular restraint. This may perturb PLS1–actin organization and contribute to abnormal localization of actin- and myosin-associated structures, ultimately impairing MET channel function.

Together, these findings provide important insights into the pathogenic mechanism of PLS1ΔExon8-associated hearing loss and offer potential clues for therapeutic intervention.

Based on the findings of Schwebach et al.15, PLS1 contains two actin-binding domains, ABD1 and ABD2. ABD2 exhibited a substantially higher affinity for actin than ABD1 in vitro, and the two domains interact intramolecularly such that ABD1 suppresses ABD2-mediated actin binding. Exon 8 encodes the α18 and α19 helices within the ABD1 domain (Fig. 1A), leading us to hypothesize that exon-8 deletion disrupts ABD1 structural integrity.

To test this, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with Flag-WT-ABD1, HA-ABD2, or Flag-ΔExon8-ABD1 and HA-ABD2 (Fig. 1E,F). Lysates were immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag magnetic beads, followed by western blotting with anti-Flag and anti-HA antibodies to assess the ABD1–ABD2 interaction. Only WT-ABD1 successfully pulled down HA-ABD2, whereas ΔExon8-ABD1 failed to capture HA-ABD2 (Fig. 1C).

Based on these findings, we generated stable lentiviral cell lines overexpressing human WT-PLS1 or ΔExon8-PLS1 in HEK293T (Supplementary Fig. 1). Pls1-KO HEI-OC1 cells were used to establish WT-PLS1 and ΔExon8-PLS1 stable overexpression lines, providing a PLS1-free background that enables direct assessment of the functional differences between wild-type and mutant PLS1.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed using anti-FLAG magnetic beads, followed by IP-MS analysis to identify proteins that differentially interacted with WT-PLS1 and ΔExon8-PLS1. The heatmap (Fig. 1D) shows the enrichment patterns of significantly altered interactors. Red represents an increased interaction (higher Z-score), whereas blue represents a reduced interaction. Cluster analysis revealed that, compared with WT-PLS1, ΔExon8-PLS1 exhibited markedly enhanced interactions with multiple cytoskeleton-associated proteins, including MYO1C, ACTG1, MYO5A, ACTBL2, TMOD3, MYH9, and MYL12B. These results suggest that the mutation may strengthen the association of PLS1 with the actin–myosin network, thereby remodeling the cytoskeleton and potentially affecting cell motility or mechanosensory properties.

Among the differentially interacting proteins, we selected MYO1C and ACTG1, both of which have been implicated in non-syndromic hearing loss, for further studies. Consistent with the IP-MS results, our Co-IP (Fig. 2E) and immunofluorescence (Fig. 2A–C) analyses confirmed that the interaction of PLS1 with MYO1C and ACTG1 was enhanced in HEK293T cells expressing the mutant protein.

To further validate these findings, co-IP experiments were performed in four groups: NC, Pls1-KO, WT-PLS1, and ΔExon8-PLS1—using ACTG1 antibody-coated magnetic beads. Western blot analysis showed that ACTG1 and MYO1C intrinsically interact with each other, and this interaction is not affected by the presence or absence of PLS1 (Fig. 2H). Quantitative immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) for ACTG1–MYO1C colocalization were similar across the four groups, except for the difference observed between WT-PLS1 and ΔExon8-PLS1 cells (Fig. 2D,G). Based on these findings, we performed ACTG1 knockdown in the ΔExon8-PLS1 cell group and then examined the relationship between ΔExon8-PLS1, ACTG1, and MYO1C using Co-IP analysis. The results showed that ΔExon8-PLS1 was unable to capture MYO1C after ACTG1 depletion, in contrast to the control group. (Fig. 2F). These results indicate that the mutation creates a tighter functional association among PLS1, ACTG1, and MYO1C, and that ΔExon8-PLS1 most likely interacts with MYO1C indirectly through ACTG1.

We used the Pls1-KO rescue system to parallel the genetic design of our zebrafish model, which also compares a PLS1-null background with wild-type and ΔExon8-mutant conditions. Aligning the cellular and in vivo systems ensures mechanistic consistency and enables direct cross-validation of ΔExon8-PLS1 pathogenic effects. We performed whole-cell cytoskeletal staining and ACTG1 labeling in the NC, Pls1 knockout (Pls1-KO), WT-PLS1 rescue (WT-HOMO), and ΔExon8-PLS1 rescue (ΔExon8-HOMO) HEI-OC1 cell lines to examine the changes in cellular morphology (Fig. 3A). Compared with NC and WT-HOMO cells, both Pls1-KO and ΔExon8-HOMO cells displayed significant alterations in cell volume and sphericity (Fig. 3M–N), indicating that the mutant protein profoundly disrupted the actin structures responsible for maintaining cellular shape.

Next, we performed quantitative analysis of actin cytoskeletal bundle architecture in HEI-OC1 cells using the imaging processing software Imaris by generating 3D surfaces based on ACTG1 and phalloidin-labeled F-actin signals. ACTG1 staining revealed that the total actin bundle volume in Pls1-KO and ΔExon8-PLS1-expressing cells was reduced by 33% compared with wild-type and WT-PLS1–expressing cells (Fig. 3H). Cells expressing the ΔExon8 mutant also showed a 27% reduction in total actin bundle length (Fig. 3I), a 30% decrease in the number of bundle branch points (Fig. 3J), and a 12% decrease in average bundle thickness (Fig. 3K), whereas the maximum 3D projection length of actin bundles remained unchanged (Fig. 3L). Phalloidin-based F-actin quantification showed that ΔExon8-PLS1 expression selectively affected the total bundle length and average thickness of F-actin structures (Fig. 3D–E). At the resolution of confocal microscopy, the analyzed structures correspond to actin bundle—like stress fibers rather than individual actin filaments.

We found that in our Western blot validation of the overexpression models, the protein level of ΔExon8-PLS1 was markedly lower than that of WT-PLS1 (Fig. 4D).

To assess whether ΔExon8-PLS1 undergoes altered ubiquitination, stable lentiviral HEK293T cells expressing WT-PLS1, ΔExon8-PLS1, or an empty vector were co-transfected with an HA-tagged ubiquitin (HA-Ub) expression plasmid. Because the lentivirally overexpressed PLS1 proteins carry a C-terminal Flag tag, cell lysates were immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag magnetic beads. The immunoprecipitates were then immunoblotted with anti-HA antibodies to detect HA-ubiquitin conjugates on Flag-PLS1, revealing increased ubiquitination of ΔExon8-PLS1 compared with WT-PLS1 (Fig. 4A).

 Given the increased ubiquitination of PLS1ΔExon8, we further examined its stability in cycloheximide (CHX)-treated HEK293T cells. Whole-cell lysates harvested at different CHX treatment time points were analyzed by western blotting and the degradation rates of WT-PLS1 and ΔExon8-PLS1 were compared. ΔExon8-PLS1 exhibited a markedly faster decline in protein abundance than WT-PLS1 (Fig. 4B), and after 24 h of CHX treatment, ΔExon8-PLS1 showed approximately 50% degradation (Fig. 4C).

PLS1 is evolutionarily conserved from zebrafish to humans (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 3). We initially generated a ZF (zebrafish)_Pls1_KO model via CRISPR/Cas9 targeting exon 1 of the zebrafish Pls1 gene. Germline screening identified a stable knockout allele carrying a 26-bp deletion that caused a frameshift and is predicted to introduce a premature stop codon. We then performed in vivo rescue by microinjecting WT-PLS1 or ΔExon8-PLS1 mRNA into one-cell–stage embryos, thereby establishing corresponding overexpression rescue models to evaluate the functional consequences of the mutant protein. In the zebrafish model, we compared the otic structural morphology among the control larvae, PLS1 knockout (ZF_PLS1_KO), PLS1-WT rescue, and ΔExon8-PLS1 rescue groups (Fig. 5A,B). Gross morphological observations showed that both PLS1 deficiency and the ΔExon8 mutation caused mild abnormalities in otic structures (Fig. 5A,B). Quantitative measurements revealed that the otic vesicle area significantly increased in Pls1-KO larvae, and this phenotype was partially rescued by PLS1-WT expression, whereas ΔExon8-PLS1–expressing larvae continued to exhibit enlarged otic vesicles (Fig. 5C).

For anterior otolith diameter, Pls1-KO larvae showed a marked increase, which was fully rescued by PLS1-WT expression; however, ΔExon8-PLS1 larvae displayed a reduction similar to that of the knockout group (Fig. 5D). No significant differences were found in the inter-otic distance across all groups, suggesting that the global otic positioning was not affected (Fig. 5E). The posterior (saccular) otolith diameter was significantly enlarged in the Pls1-KO group, restored by PLS1-WT rescue, and remained similar to the knockout levels in the ΔExon8-PLS1 larvae (Fig. 5F). We also quantified body length and eye diameter at 5 dpf (Fig. S2D,E). Body length was significantly reduced in the ZF_PLS1_KO group, whereas eye diameter was significantly reduced in the PLS1ΔExon8 group.

We evaluated the auditory-sensory behavioral responses of zebrafish larvae. Figure 6A shows the full 15-minute swimming trajectory of one representative larva from each group, recorded over the entire experimental period, whereas the quantitative analyses in Fig. 6B,C include all 15 larvae per group, each treated as one biological replicate. Compared with control and WT-PLS1 embryos, both Pls1-KO and ΔExon8-PLS1 embryos exhibited noticeably shorter swimming trajectories under acoustic stimulation (Fig. 6A,B) and reduced swimming velocities (Fig. 6C). In contrast, the swimming activity of WT-PLS1 larvae was comparable to that of the control group.

Previous studies have shown that MYO1C regulates MET function in stereocilia in cochlear hair cells, and this phenotype can be assessed using FM1-43 dye uptake. In light of our Co-IP results that implicates MYO1C’s association with PLS1, we examined whether the ΔExon8-PLS1 mutation affects MET channel activity in hair bundles using FM1-43 labeling. FM1-43 is a vital dye that enters hair cells through non-selective cation-permeable MET channels and emits fluorescence upon internalization, thereby serving as an indicator of MET channel activity22.

Quantification of FM1-43 fluorescence intensity based on the mean fluorescence intensity of the neuromast region showed that ΔExon8-PLS1 larvae exhibited significantly reduced fluorescence compared with wild-type larvae (Fig. 6D,E), indicating impaired MET channel function. In contrast, FM1-43 uptake was increased in Pls1-KO larvae. This pattern suggests that the MET channels in Pls1-KO fish tend to remain abnormally open, whereas the mutant protein impairs the normal opening of the MET channels, resulting in decreased permeability.

This study elucidates the pathogenic mechanism by which exon 8 deletion in the PLS1 gene leads to non-syndromic hearing loss. PLS1 localizes predominantly to the stereociliary shafts of auditory and vestibular hair cells, where it plays a critical role in bundling and stabilizing actin filaments23. Wild-type PLS1 maintains a dynamic regulatory balance through an intramolecular interaction, in which ABD1 suppresses the high actin-binding affinity of ABD224. When ABD1 first engages actin and undergoes conformational changes, this inhibition is relieved, enabling ABD2 to bind with high affinity and promote actin cross-linking13,15. This tightly regulated process ensures proper dynamic remodeling of actin filaments25. However, our findings demonstrate that the deletion of exon 8 generates a mutant PLS1 protein that disrupts this regulatory mechanism.

IP-MS analysis revealed that ΔExon8-PLS1 captured markedly more ACTG1 and MYO1C than WT-PLS1. We hypothesize that loss of exon 8 disrupts the structural integrity of ABD1 and weakens its interaction with ABD2. In light of previous studies showing ABD1-mediated autoinhibition of ABD2, this finding is consistent with a model in which ΔExon8 reduces intramolecular restraint and thereby perturbs PLS1–actin organization. However, direct measurements of ABD2 actin-binding affinity or full-length PLS1 conformational changes were not performed in the present study. PLS1 is primarily known as an actin-binding protein, and no direct biochemical evidence supports its direct interaction with myosin. Therefore, we examined the relationship between PLS1, ACTG1, and MYO1C. We found that MYO1C and ACTG1 exhibit a strong physical interaction, and this interaction remains intact in Pls1-KO cells. However, immunofluorescence colocalization revealed that their spatial correlation was markedly reduced compared with the WT-PLS1 rescue group. Consistent with our 3D quantitative analysis of γ-actin bundles architecture, ΔExon8-PLS1 expression caused substantial reductions in actin bundle volume, bundle length, and bundle branch points, indicating that the actin tracks used by MYO1C26 become fragmented. Under these disrupted cytoskeletal conditions, MYO1C binds to abnormally reorganized actin bundles. Together, these results suggest that ΔExon8-PLS1 may alter the organization of actin-associated structures in a manner that affects the apparent spatial relationship between ACTG1 and MYO1C under the rescue conditions tested, while the overall ACTG1–MYO1C interaction remained detectable by Co-IP.

These findings suggest that mutant PLS1 shows enhanced association with ACTG1 and that its association with MYO1C is likely ACTG1-dependent, as depletion of ACTG1 reduced MYO1C co-precipitation with ΔExon8-PLS1. Therefore, our data support the interpretation that ΔExon8-PLS1 likely exerts a dominant-negative effect, while also showing features consistent with partial loss of function.

Quantitative confocal analyses revealed significant abnormalities in cell morphology in both Pls1-KO and ΔExon8-PLS1–rescued cells. However, ΔExon8-PLS1–expressing cells exhibited smaller and shorter F-actin bundles with fewer branch points, which may contribute to the weakened MYO1C–ACTG1 interaction. Although MYO1C normally interacts with ACTG1, the excessively strong binding of ΔExon8-PLS1 to ACTG1 likely creates a competitive effect on F-actin, thereby disrupting the mechanical coupling interface normally mediated by MYO1C and ACTG1. This raises the possibility that ΔExon8-PLS1 alters the actin scaffold in a way that may affect MYO1C positioning or function. Notably, unlike the pronounced structural defects induced by ΔExon8-PLS1, Pls1-KO cells showed only a reduction in ACTG1-positive volume of actin bundles but no major defects in the length of actin bundles or bundle branch points. This may reflect compensation by other actin-bundling proteins (Espin, Fascin, Villin)20, which exhibit overlapping functions and can partially substitute one another in the event of loss14,27,28.

MYO1C is a single-headed class I myosin that acts as a tension regulator at stereocilia tips, maintaining optimal sensitivity of MET channels29,30. As an ATP-dependent molecular motor, MYO1C specifically binds to actin and generates force via ATP hydrolysis, contributing to cellular contractility, vesicle trafficking, and motility31–33. Its functions extend across diverse cellular contexts by linking membrane structures to the actin cytoskeleton34–36. MYO1C’s involvement in MET has been demonstrated in mouse cochlear hair cells using FM1-43 uptake assays37.

Notably, our FM1-43 uptake assays revealed distinct effects of PLS1 deficiency and ΔExon8-PLS1 expression on hair-cell dye uptake. FM1-43 uptake was slightly increased in Pls1-knockout larvae but reduced in the ΔExon8-PLS1 group. These contrasting patterns suggest that PLS1 deficiency and ΔExon8-PLS1 expression may perturb MET channel function through different mechanisms. However, whether these differences reflect altered bundle mechanics, tip-link tension, or channel gating cannot be determined from the present data and will require direct physiological and ultrastructural analyses. Because Espin and Fascin are also expressed in zebrafish stereocilia and can partially compensate for the absence of PLS138–40, FM1-43 uptake increases only modestly. In contrast, ΔExon8-PLS1 makes the actin bundles stiffer and abnormally arranged, hindering proper MET channel opening and resulting in a pronounced reduction in FM1-43 uptake.

Biochemical analyses further showed that ΔExon8-PLS1 had a shorter protein half-life than WT-PLS1. Previous studies have reported a ~ 24-h half-life for PLS341. CHX-chase experiments revealed rapid degradation of ΔExon8-PLS1 within 12 h, whereas WT-PLS1 remained stable, indicating that the mutation substantially reduced protein stability. According to UniProt, the ubiquitination site (K333) is located only six amino acids downstream of the ΔExon8 deletion region42. Together with our ubiquitination assays showing a marked increase in ΔExon8-PLS1 ubiquitination, these findings indicate that the mutant protein is more susceptible to ubiquitin-mediated degradation and therefore accumulates at lower levels in cells. Unlike the dominant-negative effects described above, this aspect of the phenotype is more consistent with a dosage-insufficient, partial loss-of-function mechanism.

In zebrafish embryos, the human ΔExon8-PLS1 variant results in clear auditory deficits, including abnormal otic morphology and reduced acoustic–evoked swimming responses. Pls1-KO larvae exhibited enlarged otic vesicles and altered otolith diameters, both of which were rescued by WT-PLS1 expression. In contrast, ΔExon8-PLS1 failed to rescue these abnormalities and produced phenotypes comparable to those of the knockout, suggesting that the mutation disrupted the essential functions of PLS1 in otolith formation and cytoskeletal homeostasis. Behavioral assays further demonstrated that both Pls1-KO and ΔExon8-PLS1 larvae exhibited reduced sound-evoked locomotor responses, whereas WT-PLS1 partially restored auditory sensitivity. We did not directly assess the expression of other plastin isoforms (e.g., pls3) in the Pls1-KO lines. Although genetic compensation has been reported in some stable CRISPR/Cas9 zebrafish models43,44, the robust phenotypes observed in hair-cell morphology and FM1-43 uptake suggest that any compensatory expression of other plastin isoforms did not obscure the Pls1-KO phenotypes. Future studies could investigate potential transcriptional adaptation in more detail. These findings indicate that the PLS1 mutation not only disrupts structural integrity but also causes functional impairment of hearing, with effects comparable to those of the null mutant. Although our results suggest that ΔExon8-PLS1 exerts a dominant-negative effect, this conclusion is inferred from the functional divergence between Pls1-KO and ΔExon8-PLS1 rescue models. We did not directly assess the co-expression of wild-type and mutant PLS1, and future studies are needed to confirm the dominant-negative mechanism under physiologically relevant conditions.

Taken together, our results provide new insights into how PLS1 mutations disrupt cytoskeletal dynamics and actin–myosin complex assembly. Further investigation of hair cell electrophysiology and stereociliary ultrastructure in PLS1-mutant zebrafish will deepen our understanding of the mechanism of PLS1-associated hearing loss.

Locomotor responses were quantified by comparing the distance moved during the 30-s stimulus period versus the 5-min rest intervals.
