# Examining the association between synaptic density and neurofibrillary tau among cognitively impaired and unimpaired older adults with and without Alzheimer's disease pathology.
**Authors:** DiFilippo AH, Jonaitis EM, Ennis GE, McVea AK, McLachlan M, Bettcher B, Schulz N, Pasquesi ME, Davenport-Sis NJ, Thor Y, Grover E, Chin N, Barnhart TE, Asthana S, Betthauser TJ, Engle JW, Johnson SC, Bendlin BB, Christian BT
**Journal:** Alzheimers Dement (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/alz.70321](https://doi.org/10.1002/alz.70321)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Synapse loss is a key driver of cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease (AD), yet its direct relationship with neurofibrillary tau tangle (NFT) burden remains unclear. This study leveraged positron emission tomography (PET) imaging to investigate the link between NFT accumulation and synaptic density in older adults with and without AD pathology.

METHODS: Older adults (N = 94) underwent PET imaging to quantify synaptic density ([11C]UCB-J distribution volume ratio [DVR]), Aβ plaque burden ([11C]PiB DVR), and NFT burden ([18F]MK-6240 standardized uptake value ratio). Analyses focused on NFT-synaptic density correlations in the hippocampus (Hp) and entorhinal cortex (ERC), with additional subgroup analyses based on cognitive and Aβ status.

RESULTS: Hp NFT burden strongly correlated with synaptic density, while the ERC showed weaker effects. Subgroup analyses found robust Hp associations in unimpaired AD participants.

CONCLUSION: Hippocampal synaptic density is highly vulnerable to early NFT accumulation. SV2A PET imaging enables early detection of synaptic loss and may also identify resilience to AD pathology.

HIGHLIGHTS: Hippocampal synaptic density is similar in controls and unimpaired biologic AD. Hp synaptic density particularly vulnerable to Hp, ERC NFT burden. NFT-synaptic density relationship may vary between unimpaired and impaired biologic AD.

The central goal of this work was to better understand the effect of regional NFT burden on synaptic density across older adults with and without cognitive impairment using PET imaging of NFTs and synaptic vesicle protein SV2A. Primary analyses examined the local effect of NFT burden on synaptic density within the ERC and Hp, regions expected to first exhibit NFT burden in AD. This study also explored the potential non‐local effects of ERC NFT burden on Hp synaptic density due to potential degradation of inter‐regional neuronal connections. While participants were recruited from cohorts enriched for AD, as the goal was to investigate the effect of neurofibrillary tau on synaptic density independent of disease status, these analyses will not be restricted to those on the Alzheimer's continuum. These correlations were repeated within subgroups categorizing participants by both cognitive and global amyloid beta (Aβ) plaque burden status. The effect of NFT on local synaptic density was also investigated outside these structures; however, these analyses are expected to have limitations in NFT burden range.

Data were collected as part of an ongoing study investigating AD‐related changes in synaptic density conducted at the University of Wisconsin‐Madison. Participants were recruited from the Wisconsin Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) Clinical Core, the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer's Prevention (WRAP)

Participants underwent comprehensive clinical and cognitive evaluation to determine cognitive status according to National Institute on Aging (NIA)‐Alzheimer's Association (AA) criteria,

Partial volume effects (PVEs) refer to the blurring of PET signal between brain structures and tissue types due to resolution limitations of the PET scanner, resulting in the reduction of PET image quantitative accuracy. The degree of PVE is influenced by several factors, including structure size, with atrophy worsening PVEs and causing low radiotracer binding estimates. Previous studies comparing the calculated activity concentration in a phantom applying different PVC methods to the known activity have encouraged the use of a RBV algorithm.
55
 This is a voxelwise method that accounts for spillover between tissue types (white matter, gray matter [GM], cerebrospinal fluid) as well as between parcellated volumes. Performing RBV PVC begins with using the geometric transfer matrix PVC method (FreeSurfer's gtmseg package) to correct for spillover between ROIs and calculate the corrected activities for each parcellated ROI. Next, each voxel in the raw PET image is assigned a probability of being part of a particular region (FreeSurfer's mri_gtmpvc package). The PVC correction is then calculated for each voxel, correcting for both spillover between regions and tissue types (FreeSurfer's mri_gtmpvc package).

AD participants were identified by a positive Aβ plaque burden status, and clinical AD was identified by a cognitive status of “MCI” (mild cognitive impairment) or “Dementia.” After dividing the participants into subgroups based on cognitive and Aβ plaque burden status, the Hp and ERC NFT burden and synaptic density associations were re‐evaluated. Separating cognitively impaired (CI) and cognitively unimpaired (CU) participants aided in identifying potential differences in the response of synapses to NFT burden by clinical disease stage. Subgroup differences in the Hp and ERC synaptic density in each ROI were performed using ANOVA with Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) for pairwise comparisons. Effect sizes in synaptic density differences between subgroups were calculated using Cohen's d.

Synaptic loss plays a pivotal role in driving cognitive decline in AD, underscoring the need to identify the key pathological contributors for effective prevention of cognitive impairment. The process of neurodegeneration in AD is most likely driven by the complex cellular phase of the disease, where the accumulation of NFT induces proteopathic stress and functions as a risk factor in inducing neuron death.
66
 While the degenerative effect of AD pathology on synaptic density may have several contributors, including increased neuroinflammation and the destruction of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, it was expected that NFT burden would have a particularly robust effect on synaptic density due to its well‐known neurotoxic effects and the overlap in temporospatial patterns beginning in the Hp and ERC. To explore this, we used PET imaging of NFTs and the synaptic vesicle protein SV2A in older adults with and without Aβ plaque pathology. The results from these analyses suggested a complex interplay between NFT burden, synaptic density, compensatory processes, and inter‐regional neuronal pathways, emphasizing the challenges in identifying the primary contributors to synaptic loss in AD while also pointing to the potential of SV2A PET imaging as a tool for understanding resilience to neurotoxic pathology.

In this work, investigation into the effect of NFT on synaptic density was not restricted to those with AD pathology. While ROIs were selected  based  on where NFT typically accumulates in AD, this is due to the enrichment of the cohort for the disease. However, the goal of this work was to determine the effect of NFT on synaptic density in older adults, not only those on the Alzheimer's continuum. Results and conclusions about the effect of NFT burden on synaptic density within individuals on the Alzheimer's continuum may differ from those presented.

The brain is an interconnected network of neurons that communicate with each other within, as well as across, regions. Neurons in the outer layer of the Hp dentate gyrus receive excitatory signals from neurons originating in the ERC through the perforant pathway. These ERC neurons are not spared from NFT infiltration,
28
, 
67
, 
68
 causing reduced synaptic transmission in the Hp. As NFT accumulation begins in the ERC before extending into the Hp, the degeneration of neurons in the perforant pathway could lead to reduced synaptic density in the Hp both before and during Hp NFT penetration. The moderate relationship between Hp synaptic density and ERC NFT burden observed in this work is consistent with this hypothesis as well as with results from a previous PET imaging study.
34
 The complex interplay between local and network‐wide effects of ERC NFT burden makes it challenging to identify local NFT‐induced synaptic and neuronal degeneration early in the progression of NFT accumulation. The relatively weak local relationship between NFT burden and synaptic density observed here and the lack of a relationship observed in post mortem analyses
25
, 
26
, 
27
 could reflect the broader influence of NFTs on synaptic loss across interconnected regions.

Another potential explanation for the lack of an expected robust effect of NFT burden on synaptic density across and within regions, as well as a lack of a substantial differences in synaptic density in the unimpaired AD group (CU A+) compared to unimpaired controls (CU A−) despite significant pathology, is the presence of compensatory mechanisms. These mechanisms may provide resilience to NFT‐related pathology by offsetting reductions in synaptic transmission, as shown in several post mortem studies of individuals with Alzheimer's pathology. This would also explain the relatively small difference in synaptic density between unimpaired control and impaired AD groups observed here and in other groups. One such observed compensatory mechanism is axonal sprouting, where neurons extend new axons to replace those that have degenerated.
69
, 
70
, 
71
 Additionally, synaptic transmission can be preserved through the enlargement of presynaptic terminals and an increase in synaptic vesicle numbers, enabling continued neuronal communication despite structural decline.
17
, 
19
, 
21
, 
24
, 
72
 As a result, synaptic density reflects the contribution of both the neurotoxic effects of disease pathology and regenerative efforts of the brain, potentially explaining why unimpaired individuals with biologic AD did not show a significant difference in synaptic density relative to non‐AD individuals.

A recent publication
73
 provided results from an analysis into potential explanations for unexpectedly higher [3H]UCB‐J in AD relative to unimpaired controls using both brain homogenates and large frozen brain‐section autoradiography. When the brain homogenate was separated into nuclear and synaptosomal components, it was expected that most of the [3H]UCB‐J would be present in the synaptosomal portion, where SV2A is present. However, more binding was observed in the nuclear fraction, with further analyses finding UCB‐J to be blocked by NFT PET radiotracers AV‐1451 and MK‐6240 in this fraction. The binding of [11C]UCB‐J to NFT would cause the reductions in synaptic density to be underestimated in individuals with NFT. This may explain why these analyses found moderate associations between synaptic density and NFT burden across individuals. However, the autoradiography and homogenate analyses from this study do not agree with other post mortem analyses using autoradiography
74
 and immunolabeling that observed reduced synaptic density in AD. More post mortem autoradiography with [3H]UCB‐J in AD is needed to understand the discrepancies in findings between groups as well as the potential elevated binding of [3H]UCB‐J in the nuclear fraction of brain homogenate.

While the subgroup correlation analyses suggest there may be a difference in the relationship between NFT and synaptic density for participants on the AD continuum based on cognitive status, the sample sizes were small, so analyses with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings.

A limitation with this analysis is the calculation of [11C]UCB‐J‐specific binding without performing arterial sampling using the reference region method. However, previous studies
36
, 
37
 validated the suitability of the cerebellum as a reference region for the use of [11C]UCB‐J quantification in an AD cohort. This region was found to have minimal difference in radioligand concentration compared to that in plasma at equilibrium (VT) between AD and CU groups. While the DVR calculated with the cerebellum as a reference region would not reflect specific binding, it is suitable for studies evaluating differences in synaptic density between CU and AD groups.

This work has demonstrated that, while NFTs are well‐established contributors to neurodegeneration, the relationship between NFT burden and synaptic density in regions of early NFT accumulation was weaker than anticipated. Synaptic density and NFT imaging via PET may offer insights into individual resilience to NFT pathology, driven by compensatory mechanisms that help maintain synaptic transmission. Understanding the primary pathological drivers of synaptic loss is crucial for the development of cognition‐preserving therapies. However, compensatory processes that maintain synaptic transmission may lead to an underestimation of the true degenerative effects of NFTs when relying on synaptic density quantification. To gain a more comprehensive understanding, further research is necessary to explore the longitudinal trajectories of synaptic loss in relation to the progression and duration of Aβ plaque and tau pathology, as well as the severity of cognitive decline. These studies will be vital in refining therapeutic strategies targeting synaptic resilience and neuronal preservation.
