# Genetic Disorders of Surfactant Metabolism.
**Authors:** Nevel RJ, Brennan SK, Wambach JA
**Journal:** Neoreviews (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1542/neo.26-5-014](https://doi.org/10.1542/neo.26-5-014)

## Content

Genetic disorders affecting surfactant protein production and function can result in respiratory distress and severe respiratory failure in late-preterm and term neonates. Pathogenic variants in surfactant pulmonary-associated protein B (SFTPB) are typically loss-of-function and disrupt surfactant protein B (SP-B) production and surfactant function. Dominant variants in surfactant pulmonary-associated protein C (SFTPC) generally result in a toxic gain-of-function with disruption of surfactant protein C (SP-C) processing and trafficking in the alveolar epithelial type 2 cells. Adenosine triphosphate binding cassette transporter A3 (ABCA3) variants include loss-of-function or "null" variants in which no ABCA3 protein is made or missense variants that disrupt intracellular trafficking of ABCA3 or impair phospholipid transport. Pathogenic variants and deletions of the NK2 homeobox 1 gene (NKX2-1) result in haploinsufficiency and alter transcription of surfactant-associated genes as well as genes for brain and thyroid development. Diagnosis of these disorders requires a high index of clinical suspicion because presentations may vary between and within diseases. Prognosis is highly variable, ranging from requiring supportive care with improvement in respiratory status over time to severe disease with early mortality without lung transplantation. Neonatologists and pulmonologists alike should recognize early presentations of these rare genetic disorders of surfactant metabolism to identify and care for affected infants and to counsel families regarding prognosis, treatment options, recurrence risk, and risk assessment for other family members.

Surfactant is essential to healthy function of the lung and acts by lowering alveolar surface tension, preventing atelectasis of the alveoli at the end of exhalation.1 Surfactant is composed of 90% phospholipids (primarily dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) and 10% protein. Production occurs in the alveolar epithelial type 2 cells (AEC2s) and is initially produced and secreted into the alveoli beginning at approximately 24 weeks’ gestation. Surfactant-associated proteins are synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and undergo modifications in the Golgi apparatus, namely glycosylation. Surfactant protein B (SP-B) and surfactant protein C (SP-C) are synthesized as proproteins and undergo proteolytic processing in the multivesicular and lamellar bodies.2,3 Adenosine triphosphate–binding cassette A3 (ABCA3) is modified in the Golgi and multivesicular bodies and localizes to the lamellar body outer membrane.1,2,4–6 ABCA3 transports phospholipids, which assemble with surfactant proteins to make surfactant.1,2,4–7 NK2 homeobox 1 (NKX2–1) encodes a nuclear transcription factor (thyroid transcription factor 1 [TTF1]) that regulates transcription of multiple genes, including pulmonary-associated protein B (SFTPB) and C (SFTPC) and ABCA3.1,8,9 Surfactant is stored in lamellar bodies, which then undergo exocytosis and secrete surfactant into the alveolar space.1

Several rare disorders in infants, children, and adults are caused by genetic variants that affect surfactant protein production, processing, and function. The American Thoracic Society has classified these disorders of surfactant metabolism as types of childhood interstitial and diffuse lung diseases (chILD).10 Pathogenic variants in SFTPB, SFTPC, ABCA3, and NKX2–1 may present as neonatal respiratory distress in late-preterm or term infants, and these disorders are important for the neonatologist and pulmonologist alike to recognize. In this review, we will summarize the pathogenesis of disease from variants in these genes, the epidemiology, and the clinical aspects including diagnostic evaluation, management, prognosis, and recurrence risk.

There are 4 surfactant proteins: SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, and surfactant protein D (SP-D).1 SP-A and SP-D are hydrophilic and function primarily as components of innate immunity, although some SFTPA variants may contribute to pulmonary fibrosis and lung cancer in adults.11–13 SP-B and SP-C are small and extremely hydrophobic proteins, undergo processing in the multivesicular and lamellar bodies, and contribute to the surface tension–lowering properties of surfactant.1 ABCA3 is a member of the adenosine triphosphate (ATP)–binding cassette transporter protein family, which uses ATP to move substances across biologic membranes. ABCA3 is required for lamellar body biogenesis, is located on the lamellar body outer membrane, and transports phospholipids, namely dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, into the lamellar bodies, which then combine with surfactant proteins to make surfactant.1
NKX2–1 encodes a nuclear transcription factor TTF1 that regulates transcription of several surfactant genes, including SFTPB, SFTPC, and ABCA3.1,4,9,14,15

Located on chromosome 2, SFTPB is composed of 11 exons; however, the last exon is untranslated.16,17
SFTPB encodes a proprotein (381 amino acids) that undergoes proteolytic processing in the multivesicular and lamellar bodies to yield the mature SP-B peptide (79 amino acids).16,17 Specifically, pro–SP-B is processed at the amino terminus and the carboxy terminus, and the mature peptide is encoded by exons 6 and 7.18 SP-B combines with SP-C and phospholipids in the lamellar bodies. The lamellar bodies undergo exocytosis by fusing with the plasma membrane, resulting in secretion of their contents into the alveolar lumen. The lamellar bodies then unravel and interact with surfactant proteins A and D to form tubular myelin, a highly structured lattice of surfactant lipids and proteins, and multilayered surface films at the air-liquid interface.3,19 Characterization of lung tissue from infants with SP-B deficiency due to biallelic loss-of-function variants has demonstrated that mature SP-B is required for complete processing of pro–SP-C.16,17

The most common pathogenic SFTPB variant—c.361delCinsGAA; p.Pro121GlnfsTer95 (also referred to “121ins2”)—results in a frameshift and subsequent premature stop codon, leading to an unstable messenger RNA transcript and nonsense-mediated decay with no mature SP-B protein produced.16,17,20 This loss-of-function variant has been identified in approximately two-thirds of infants with SP-B deficiency and is present in approximately 1 in 1400 adults in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v4.1.0, accessed November 2024) of approximately 800 000 adults,21 with increased frequency identified among individuals of European descent. On the basis of SFTPB loss-of-function variants in gnomAD, the estimated incidence of SP-B deficiency is approximately 1 per 4 million births annually.18 However, this is likely an underestimate because some SFTPB missense variants are also pathogenic.18 Individuals with SFTPB missense variants that permit some residual SP-B protein function may have prolonged survival.22

Lung tissue samples from infants with SP-B deficiency demonstrate absence of mature SP-B as well as marked accumulation of pro–SP-C and misprocessed SP-C in the AEC2s and the alveolar space.16,17,23,24 The mechanism by which SP-C processing is disrupted in infants with SP-B deficiency is not known but may be that mature SP-B and/or properly folded pro–SP-B are required for the formation of lamellar bodies in which the final processing steps of SP-B and SP-C occur.3 Electron microscopy of AEC2s from infants with SP-B deficiency demonstrate fewer lamellar bodies that appear as large, membrane-bound structures that frequently contain smaller membranous vesicles.24,25 Multivesicular and composite bodies may be present. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from infants with SP-B deficiency contains foamy, eosinophilic, lipoproteinaceous material and exhibits increased surface tension compared with that of healthy infants.26 Accumulation of dysfunctional surfactant in the alveolus results in alveolar collapse and impaired gas exchange.

SP-B–deficient mice (sftpbnull/null) demonstrate normal embryonic development but die shortly after birth from atelectatic respiratory failure.27 A conditional mouse model in which SP-B complementary DNA was expressed under control of exogenous doxycycline demonstrated decreased alveolar content of SP-B, resulting in respiratory failure when SP-B concentrations were less than 25% of wild-type levels.27 Similar to results from human infants with SP-B deficiency, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from these animals demonstrated increased protein content, accumulation of misprocessed SP-C proprotein in the alveolar space, increased surface tenson, and decreased maximal tidal and end-expiratory volumes.27 Recently, intratracheal administration of adeno-associated viral gene therapy was performed in a conditional mouse model of SP-B deficiency, resulting in AEC2 transduction, increased SP-B expression, restoration of surfactant homeostasis, improved lung physiology, and prolonged survival.28 Clinical translation of these findings to human infants with SP-B deficiency represents a potential therapy for this typically lethal disease.29,30

SFTPC encodes SP-C and is located on chromosome 8. Similar to SFTPB, as described in the previous section, SFTPC also encodes a proprotein that is synthesized in the ER and undergoes sequential processing at the amino terminus and carboxy terminus to yield the mature SP-C protein. SP-C assembles with SP-B and phospholipids in the lamellar bodies.

Pathogenic variants in SFTPC act in an autosomal dominant manner with variable penetrance. Approximately half of pathogenic SFTPC variants are inherited, and the other half arise de novo.1,7,31 Even among families with the same inherited SFTPC variant, the pulmonary phenotypes are variable and include neonatal respiratory failure, chILD, adult pulmonary fibrosis, and lung cancer, while some individuals remain relatively asymptomatic even as adults.31–35 The reasons for this variable penetrance are unknown but may include other genetic modifiers, infectious or environmental (including tobacco) exposures, or epigenetic mechanisms. Pathogenic variants in SFTPC are grouped by location in the gene/proprotein. The BRICHOS domain refers to the last approximately 100 amino acids of the SP-C proprotein. This domain shares homology to a family of proteins that are implicated in familial dementias, chondrosarcoma, and stomach cancer and is required for proper folding and trafficking of the SP-C proprotein in the AEC2.36–38
SFTPC variants within the BRICHOS domain (near the carboxy terminus) cause misfolding of the SP-C proprotein with subsequent aggregation and ER retention, resulting in ER stress, activation of the unfolded protein response, AEC2 injury, and cell death.39–41 Non–BRICHOS domain variants, including the most common pathogenic variant (p.Ile73Thr, identified in approximately 25% to 30% of affected individuals), result in misrouting of the mutant pro–SP-C to the plasma membrane and endosomes, disruption of posttranslational processing, a block in macroautophagy, and subsequent AEC2 injury.42–44 Interestingly, very few pathogenic variants have been identified in the region of SFTPC that encodes the mature SP-C protein. The incidence of SP-C–related lung disease is difficult to estimate given the variable disease penetrance. Four individuals with p.Ile73Thr variant are listed in the gnomAD database, suggesting a minimum prevalence of 1 per 200 000 adults.18 However, other SFTPC missense variants are listed in gnomAD, some of which are probably pathogenic, so this estimated prevalence is most likely an underestimate.

ABCA3 is encoded by the ABCA3 gene located on chromosome 16, a key protein in the surfactant pathway, and highly expressed in AEC2s.1,5,26,45 ABCA3 is a large transmembrane protein located on the outer membranes of lamellar bodies and serves an important role in lamellar body biogenesis and the transport of phospholipids (primarily phosphatidylcholine) into lamellar bodies for surfactant assembly.5,26,45–47 ABCA3 deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder, requiring pathogenic variants on both alleles (biallelic). The pulmonary phenotypes associated with ABCA3 deficiency included severe neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), chILD, and adult pulmonary fibrosis.48–52 Term and late-preterm infants with monoallelic ABCA3 variants are at increased risk for transient neonatal RDS that improves with neonatal intensive care.53,54 Some preterm infants with extreme RDS or severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia and monoallelic ABCA3 variants have also been reported.55

Pathogenic variants in ABCA3 encode for mutant proteins that either disrupt intracellular trafficking (type I mutant) or phospholipid transport into the lamellar body (type II mutant).56–60 Lamellar bodies from infants and children with pathogenic ABCA3 variants often appear small with electron-dense inclusions and are described as having a “fried egg appearance.”24,49,61,62 Infants with biallelic loss-of-function variants in ABCA3 present with severe neonatal RDS and die within the first months after birth without lung transplantation.38,50,63,64 However, the age of presentation, disease severity, and progression are more difficult to predict for infants with ABCA3 genotypes that include missense, splicing, or in-frame insertion/deletions.48,50,51,64 The cystic fibrosis transmembrane receptor is also a member of the ATP-binding cassette transporter family of proteins and shares a similar protein structure with ABCA3.65 The development of modulators of the cystic fibrosis transmembrane receptor have revolutionized treatment of individuals with cystic fibrosis improving lung function, mortality, and quality of life66 and may serve as a model for the development of therapies for ABCA3 deficiency.29,59,67–69

The incidence of ABCA3 deficiency is difficult to determine from large databases of adult individuals such as gnomAD because most ABCA3 pathogenic variants are missense and unique to individuals and their families (ie, private). On the basis of loss-of-function ABCA3 variants in gnomAD and the frequency of the most common ABCA3 pathogenic variant (p.Glu292Val) associated with chILD, the estimated incidence of ABCA3 deficiency is approximately 1 per 40 000 births, affecting approximately 100 infants per year in the United States, which makes ABCA3 deficiency the most common genetic disorder of surfactant metabolism.18,21 Neonates with ABCA3 deficiency may be underrecognized, especially if born preterm or if cared for in an institution with limited genetic testing capabilities.

The NKX2–1 gene, located on chromosome 14, encodes nuclear transcription factor TTF1, which regulates the transcription of several surfactant-associated genes, including SFTPB, SFTPC, and ABCA3, as well as genes critical for brain and thyroid development.9,70 Pathogenic variants in NKX2–1 are either inherited via an autosomal dominant pattern or arise de novo and result in inactivation of 1 allele (haploinsufficiency).70 Pathogenic single-nucleotide variants in NKX2–1 and deletions involving the NKX2–1 locus have been reported.70 Similar to pathogenic variants in SFTPC, NKX2–1 pathogenic variants also demonstrate variable penetrance. NKX2–1 variants may reduce DNA binding and transcription of TTF1, thus affecting airway, alveolar, brain, and thyroid development. Interestingly, genome-wide analysis in mice identified more than 1300 DNA binding sites for TTF1, including genes involved in lung development, ion transport, RAS signaling, cell cycle regulation, and injury response.9 An extended pedigree with an inherited missense variant in NKX2–1 was associated with neuroendocrine hyperplasia of infancy in multiple family members.71,72

Diagnostic evaluation for an infant with a suspected genetic disorder of surfactant metabolism should include evaluation for other causes of respiratory distress, such as infection, congenital heart disease, pulmonary hypertension, or recurrent/chronic aspiration.10 Initial laboratory and radiographic evaluations include chest radiograph (Figure 2), blood gas analysis, evaluation for infection, and echocardiogram. If these evaluations do not identify the etiology for the clinical presentation, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is recommended. In the setting of genetic disorders of surfactant metabolism, these radiologic evaluations often demonstrate diffuse lung disease findings, including ground-glass opacification, thickening of the inter- and intralobular septae, and over time, the development of parenchymal cysts and increasing fibrotic features (Figure 3).38,73 Significant overlap exists between HRCT findings of the genetic surfactant disorders, and these HRCT findings may change over time.8,33,73

For the infant with diffuse lung disease and a suspected genetic disorder of surfactant metabolism, genetic testing is recommended. In the neonatal intensive care unit, this is most commonly performed with exome or whole genome sequencing (WGS).74 Parental samples are helpful to determine whether a variant arose de novo or if 2 variants are present on 1 (cis) or both alleles (trans).50,61 Clinicians should be aware of the limitations of genetic testing. For example, exome sequencing or panel testing may miss deletions or duplications (ie, copy number variants [CNVs]), intronic variants, or complex structural variants.25,75,76 Partial or whole gene deletions of NKX2–1 have been reported in symptomatic individuals.70 To detect CNVs, WGS or chromosomal microarray should be performed, with WGS detecting smaller CNVs than a microarray. WGS offers the added benefit of identifying intronic and noncoding variants; however, our ability to determine the functional consequences of noncoding variants remains limited.

While most diagnoses are made via genetic testing, bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage could be considered to evaluate for other causes of diffuse lung diseases and respiratory distress, such as pulmonary hemorrhage, chronic aspiration, congenital airway anomalies, and infectious etiologies. While lung biopsy was previously often pursued in the diagnostic evaluation of infants and children suspected of having a genetic disorder of surfactant metabolism, the availability and improved turnaround time of genetic testing have altered this approach. Additionally, the histopathologic findings, which include AEC2 hyperplasia, alveolar proteinosis, inter-alveolar septal thickening, fibrosis, inflammation, foamy macrophages, and proteinaceous material, do not distinguish between the individual genetic surfactant disorders.77 Further, infants with these diseases can have severe respiratory failure; therefore, undergoing open lung biopsy can result in significant morbidities, including hemorrhage, pneumothorax, and worsening of oxygenation, ventilation, or pulmonary hypertension crisis.78

The clinical presentation and the prognosis of infants diagnosed with genetic disorders of surfactant metabolism have overlapping clinical features (Table 1). Outcomes for individual infants and children are also influenced by clinical factors that affect disease course, including maternal health, prenatal care, congenital and nosocomial infections, and nutritional status, as well as access to a specialty center for prolonged mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal support, or even lung transplantation.63 Although the specific gene and genotype may confer prognostic information in terms of morbidity or mortality, clinicians must also consider the many factors that influence the health of infants and children with chronic diseases.

As noted in the Table 1, infants homozygous or compound heterozygous for SFTPB pathogenic variants exhibit severe respiratory failure at birth, requiring intensive care and consideration of referral for lung transplantation.1,7 In contrast to infants with SP-B deficiency, who die in the first few months after birth without lung transplantation,64 the clinical presentation and prognosis for infants and children with SFTPC pathogenic variants is highly variable, and there is not a clear genotype-phenotype correlation.38 Additionally, there is variable penetrance in that some individuals with SFTPC pathogenic variants present in infancy with progressive respiratory failure, others present with chILD or adult pulmonary fibrosis, and some remain relatively asymptomatic.79,80 From a clinical standpoint, most infants with biallelic pathogenic variants in SFTPB present with severe neonatal RDS, while infants with pathogenic variants in SFTPC present beyond the neonatal period with symptoms of chILD, including tachypnea, hypoxemia, and failure to gain weight; however, neonatal presentations of SP-C–related lung disease have been reported.31 While infants with pathogenic SFTPC variants may ultimately progress toward respiratory failure, this process may take months or years, if it occurs at all, and several studies have demonstrated infants successfully managed with chronic invasive or noninvasive respiratory support.33,81

A strong genotype-phenotype correlation is noted among infants with biallelic ABCA3 loss-of-function or “null” variants (eg, nonsense, frameshift) presenting with severe neonatal RDS and death within the first year of age without lung transplantation.50 Infants with missense, in-frame insertion/deletions, or intronic ABCA3 variants have a more variable age of presentation and disease severity, with some having severe neonatal RDS and others presenting with ILDs during childhood, adolescence, or even adulthood.50,82,83 This phenotypic variability may be due to residual protein function, variants in other surfactant-associated genes, environmental or infectious exposures, or epigenetic modifiers.56,60,84

Individuals with pathogenic NKX2–1 variants may have pulmonary manifestations including neonatal RDS or chILD, as well as neurologic and thyroid abnormalities, and this syndrome is termed “brain-lung-thyroid” disease.63,70 The neurologic manifestations may include chorea, seizures, hypotonia, ataxia, or developmental delay. The most common thyroid manifestation is hypothyroidism.1,7,63,70 Like SP-C–related lung disease, pathogenic variants in NKX2–1 have variable clinical presentations and penetrance. Many infants with NKX2–1 pathogenic single-nucleotide variants or deletions have some respiratory symptoms at birth; however, this may range from requiring a small amount of oxygen to severe respiratory failure requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support.70 Long-term respiratory outcomes can also vary and include progressive respiratory failure with death in the first year of age, requiring lung transplantation, chronic oxygen support, or even weaning off oxygen after a few years.70 Other affected individuals may present with thyroid and neurologic abnormalities but minimal to no respiratory symptoms. There is not a clear genotype-phenotype association for infants and children with NKX2–1 variants.70,85

Currently, there are no specific treatments for these disorders.46 Exogenous surfactant replacement is used in preterm infants with a developmental insufficiency of surfactant production. However, in term infants with diffuse lung disease secondary to genetic disorders of surfactant metabolism, surfactant administration may result in a transient (hours) response with improved oxygenation and ventilation.86 These effects dampen over time, and respiratory failure continues to progress.86 Initial management remains ventilation and oxygenation support for infants with RDS. In severe cases, management may include ECMO pending diagnostic studies. Some infants and children have demonstrated improvement with therapies like azithromycin, hydroxychloroquine, and/or systemic steroids such as intravenous pulse methylprednisolone, although outcome data supporting these approaches are variable and limited to small case reports.4,87 Lung transplantation remains the definitive treatment for progressive respiratory failure and is offered in a few centers in the United States, Canada, and Europe.
