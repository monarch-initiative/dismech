# Palmoplantar Keratoderma: A Mechanism-Based Disease Classification.
**Authors:** Liu JW, Habulieti X, Qian YT, Ma X, Han Y, Ma DL
**Journal:** Clin Cosmet Investig Dermatol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.2147/CCID.S613425](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCID.S613425)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK) represents a diagnostically challenging group of genodermatoses due to its extensive genetic heterogeneity. Although multiple causative genes have been identified, the clinical utility of these discoveries remains limited, because few studies directly compare how distinct molecular mechanisms translate into divergent clinical phenotypes.

METHODS: We describe and analyze three paradigmatic PPK cases with genetically confirmed diagnoses involving SERPINB7-related Nagashima-type PPK, TRPV3-related Olmsted spectrum disorder, and KRT1-associated PPK. Clinical features, disease severity, and genetic mutations were systematically compared. These observations generated a novel classification framework and were then integrated with a comprehensive review of contemporary literature to contextualize and support the comparative findings.

RESULTS: Three patients with palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK) harboring distinct pathogenic variants were analyzed and comparatively evaluated. Despite overlapping clinical features, these cases corresponded to three distinct pathogenic mechanisms: defective barrier homeostasis, overactive signaling, and structural collapse. A mechanism-based classification framework was established accordingly. Through comprehensive literature review, 56 PPK-associated genes were identified, the majority of which could be systematically assigned to the three mechanistic categories based on their primary molecular functions.

CONCLUSION: This study highlights that clinically overlapping PPK phenotypes arise from distinct molecular mechanisms. Our mechanism-based classification provides a framework linking genotype to pathogenesis, which may improve diagnostic precision, support the development of mechanism-targeted therapeutic strategies, and thereby advancing personalized management in hereditary PPK.

Hereditary palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK) is frequently encountered in clinical practice. Clinically, PPK is defined by abnormal hyperkeratotic thickening affecting the palmar and plantar surfaces.1 In most cases, PPK can be diagnosed based on clinical presentation alone. Based on the clinical manifestations arising from the underlying genetic abnormalities, hereditary PPK can be categorized into three broad groups: (i) isolated, non-syndromic forms in which hyperkeratosis predominantly affects the palms and soles; (ii) non-syndromic forms accompanied by additional characteristic cutaneous or adnexal features, referred as complex PPK; and (iii) syndromic forms, in which palmoplantar keratoderma occurs in conjunction with defined extracutaneous involvement.2,3 However, a precise molecular diagnosis usually requires more extensive genetic and molecular analyses. PPK exhibits complex genetic heterogeneity and inheritance patterns, including autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked recessive, and, in rare cases, mitochondrial transmission.4,5 Certain subtypes show a geographically restricted distribution and are predominantly observed within specific population isolates.4 Up to 56 genes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of PPK. Although functionally heterogeneous, pathogenic variants in these genes lead to a shared biological outcome—abnormal hyperkeratosis of the stratum corneum—leading to the common clinical phenotype of palmoplantar hyperkeratosis.4,6 For clinicians, translating this genetic heterogeneity into definitive diagnoses and tailored management strategies can be challenging.

In previous studies, the pathogenic genes in PPK are usually studied in isolation, without systematic comparative analysis across distinct pathogenic mechanisms, thereby limiting a mechanistic understanding of how different molecular processes contribute to phenotypic heterogeneity in PPK. In this study, we aim to elucidate the mechanism spectrum of hereditary PPK from a new perspective. First, we presented a comparative case series of three patients, with pathogenic variants in SERPINB7, TRPV3, and KRT1, respectively. Then, expanding from these representative cases, we conduct systematic literature review, relate the genetic heterogeneity with disease pathogenesis, and propose a new mechanism-based framework for PPK. By proposing this novel framework, we aim to provide new insights into the classification of hereditary PPK, as well as improved strategies for diagnosis, prognostication, and patient management in hereditary PPK.

Patients with a clinical diagnosis of palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK) who attended the Dermatology Outpatient Clinic of Peking Union Medical College Hospital were recruited. All participants (and the legal guardian of Case 2) have provided written informed consent for their case details and images to be published. Comprehensive clinical evaluation was performed for each patient, including detailed medical history and physical examination. Family history was systematically collected, and pedigrees were constructed to assess inheritance patterns. Peripheral blood samples were obtained from affected individuals for genetic analysis. Whole-exome sequencing was performed by the Public Welfare Project for Rare Disease Service Improvement Team.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (JS-3362D). All participants (and the legal guardian of Case 2) provided written informed consent.

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from three unrelated patients clinically diagnosed with hereditary palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK). Genomic DNA was extracted using a standard DNA extraction kit Whole-exome sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform with a mean coverage depth exceeding 100×. Sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19), and variant calling was conducted using a validated bioinformatics pipeline. Variants were filtered based on minor allele frequency (<0.01) in public population databases (eg, gnomAD), predicted functional impact (missense, nonsense, frameshift, splice-site), and known association with keratinization disorders. Candidate variants were interpreted in accordance with the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). Sanger sequencing was performed to validate pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants and, when possible, to assess segregation within the family. The three index cases harbored pathogenic variants in SERPINB7, TRPV3, and KRT1, respectively, each representing a distinct biological mechanism relevant to epidermal homeostasis.

To construct a comprehensive mechanism-oriented classification framework for hereditary PPK, a structured literature search was conducted in PubMed and Embase up to January, 2026. We used both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and non-MeSH keywords in the literature search to maximize retrieval of relevant articles. An exhaustive list of all search terms was shown in the Supplementary Material S1, and selective flowchart was presented in Supplementary Figure 1. Studies were eligible if they described genetically confirmed hereditary PPK with available clinical or functional characterization data. Acquired keratoderma, syndromes without molecular confirmation, and review articles without primary data were excluded. All identified PPK-associated genes were extracted and systematically reviewed. Functional annotation and mechanism classification were performed based on published evidence regarding molecular function, protein localization, biological pathway involvement, and experimental validation (Supplementary Material–Supplementary Table 1). In addition, established human genetic variation databases, including Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD), ClinVar, and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), were systematically searched to ensure comprehensive identification of disease-associated genes and to support functional interpretation and disease classification. To minimize subjectivity, gene assignment was independently reviewed by two investigators with expertise in dermatogenetics and molecular biology. PPK-associated genes were then categorized according to their predominant pathogenic mechanism into three major groups: (1) defects protease function and barrier homostasis; (2) dysregulation of inflammatory or signaling pathways influencing keratinization; and (3) abnormalities in structural keratins or cytoskeletal components. The three index cases were used as representative mechanistic archetypes to illustrate these categories, whereas the overall classification was derived from integrative gene-function synthesis. Genes were assigned to mechanistic categories according to the primary biological function of the encoded protein and the predominant pathogenic mechanism. This framework was constructed as a evidence-generated model aimed at providing a mechanistically grounded alternative to morphology-based PPK classification.

The first patient was a 22-year-old female who presented with symmetrical hyperkeratosis on the palms and soles. The lesions initially appeared as reddish plaques on the soles at the age of six and gradually became more prominent, later involving the palms with age. The patient reported no significant pain or pruritus but noted slight scaling and maceration during the winter. Physical examination revealed diffuse reddish plaques on the palms and soles, with proximal extension to the wrists and ankles. Genetic analysis revealed compound heterozygosity for two previously reported pathogenic mutations in the SERPINB7 gene: c.796C>T (p.Arg266*),7 and c.650_653delCTGT (p.Ser217Leufs*7)8 (Figure 1A). Both mutations are predicted to result in premature termination codons, leading to complete loss of SERPINB7 protein function. SERPINB7, located on chromosome 18q21.3, belongs to the clade B serine protease inhibitor (serpin) family, which consists of intracellular serpins that share a highly conserved tertiary structure. In human skin, SERPINB7 is predominantly expressed in the upper epidermal layers, particularly the stratum granulosum and stratum corneum, suggesting a role in regulating epidermal protease activity and maintaining stratum corneum integrity. Loss of functional SERPINB7 results in increased activity of intracorneocyte proteases in the affected epidermis, which subsequently disrupts the integrity of the proteinaceous framework of corneocytes.9 The identified compound heterozygous mutations c.796C>T (p.Arg266*) and c.650_653delCTGT (p.Ser217Leufs*7) lead to complete loss of Legumain inhibitory activity.10 Overactivation of legumain may modulate transglutaminase processing indirectly through cathepsin-dependent mechanisms, leading to impaired terminal differentiation and enhanced breakdown of cytoskeletal components, thereby leading to skin barrier impairment.10

This genetic finding confirms the diagnosis of autosomal recessive Nagashima-type PPK. Management primarily focuses on symptom relief, including the topical use of emollients and keratolytic agents such as tretinoin cream. In our patient, topical 0.1% tretinoin cream markedly softened the hyperkeratosis and achieved a satisfactory therapeutic effect.

The second patient was a 9-year-old boy who presented with an 8-year history of plantar hyperkeratosis. Physical examination revealed scattered hyperkeratosis plaques confined to the soles, predominantly affecting weight-bearing areas. The patient reported occasional pain after physical activity. No plantar fissuring, periorificial keratotic plaques, alopecia, or digital flexion contractures were found. Genetic analysis identified a previously reported heterozygous TRPV3 mutation (c.1703G>A; p.Gly568Asp) (Figure 1B).11
TRPV3 encodes a heat-activated non-selective cation channel that forms a tetramer complex through six transmembrane domains, expressed predominantly in epidermal keratinocytes. Pathogenic gain-of-function mutations in TRPV3 result in constitutive channel activation and excessive Ca2⁺ influx. The elevation of intracellular calcium disrupts normal keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation pathways, and promotes inflammatory signaling within the skin. Persistent activation of TRPV3 has also been shown to stimulate epidermal growth factor receptor–related pathways, further contributing to abnormal epidermal thickening.12,13 The c.1703G>A (p.Gly568Asp) mutation replaces a small glycine with a negatively charged aspartate, altering the pore’s electrostatic environment, leading to constitutive Ca2⁺ influx. Localized PPK phenotype causes by TRPV3 mutation is refractory to multiple conventional treatments, including topical keratolytics and oral acitretin. The patient did not respond to topical treatment, including 0.1% tretinoin ointment and mometasone furoate ointment.

The third patient was a 34-year-old male who presented with hyperkeratotic plaques on the palms and soles. The lesions first appeared when he was 4-year-old. The lesions gradually expanded and became more hyperkeratotic. The patient reported symptoms of dryness and fissuring that worsened during winter. He also experienced mild pain associated with functional impairment, including difficulty wearing shoes and limitation of physical activity. The disease followed an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern in his family, with affected members spanning four generations, including his maternal grandmother, aunts, mother, and son. Physical examination revealed diffuse involvement of the palmar surfaces, with focal hyperkeratosis over the forefoot and heels, accompanied by prominent scaling. No generalized ichthyosiform changes were observed. Genetic analysis identified a heterozygous splice-site mutation in the KRT1 gene (c.1255–2A>G) (Figure 1C). Keratin 1 forms obligate heterodimers with keratin 10, and these dimers further assemble into keratin intermediate filaments that constitute the cytoskeletal network of differentiated keratinocytes and provide mechanical stability to the epidermis, maintaining cell integrity under mechanical stress.14 The identified mutation is predicted to disrupt normal RNA splicing and impair the integrity of the keratin intermediate filament structure. The patient was prescribed oral acitretin 20 mg once daily, as well as topical 0.1% tretinoin ointment. The symptom was partially relieved in 2 months.

The loss of SERPINB7 function leads to uncontrolled protease activity, which in turn disrupts the expression of the desmosomal proteins (eg, Desmoglein-1, Desmocollin-1). This disruption impairs the keratinocyte adhesion and skin barrier homeostasis, resulting in the hyperkeratosis in Nagashima-type PPK15,16(Figure 2A).

In contrast, the pathology in our second patient results from active, dysregulated cellular signaling. TRPV3 encodes a cation channel mostly found in keratinocytes, which plays a role in temperature perception, pain transduction, skin physiology, inflammation, cancer and other diseases.17 The heterozygous mutation (c.1703G>A; p.Gly568Asp) identified in our patient is a previously reported gain-of-function variant that results in constitutive channel activation. The constant influx of calcium prompts sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, resulting in elevated apoptosis of keratinocytes and consequent skin hyperkeratosis13 (Figure 2B). Targeted treatments, such as EGFR inhibitors (eg, erlotinib), have shown promise in the treatment of TRPV3-related PPK.18

The third model showed defected structural integrity. KRT1 encodes a type II keratin that pairs with keratin 10 to form the strong intermediate filament network, providing mechanical strength to epidermal keratinocytes.14 The mutant protein results in mechanical fragility. The identified heterozygous mutation in the KRT1 gene (c.1255–2A>G) is a novel splice-site mutation that affects mRNA splicing and the resultant protein translation. Systemic retinoids target progressive hyperkeratosis by normalizing keratinocyte differentiation and interrupting the damage-proliferation cycle19 (Figure 2C).

The genetic landscape of palmoplantar keratoderma is highly heterogeneous, with pathogenic variants in up to 56 genes currently associated with the condition. In our work, SERPINB7, TRPV3, and KRT1 each represents a distinct pathogenic mechanism: defective skin-barrier homeostasis, dysregulated signaling, and structural fragility. A mechanism-based classification framework was proposed based on the three pathogenic archetypes (Figure 3). This “mechanism-first” perspective shifts the focus from listing variants to understanding the primary dysfunction that links genotype to phenotype.

The remaining 14 genes are excluded because their pathogenic mechanisms involve broader cellular processes that do not align exclusively with structural, barrier, or signaling functions. Genetic defects in MT-TS1 and ENPP1 affect mitochondrial or nucleic‑acid metabolism,4 while KDSR41 and SDR9C742 are involved in intracellular sphingolipid and retinoid metabolism. In these cases, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis typically presents as part of broader phenotypes such as progressive symmetric erythrokeratoderma, congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma, or systemic autoinflammatory/metabolic conditions. Additionally, SMARCAD143 and HOXA1344 function as upstream regulators of chromatin remodeling and developmental patterning, respectively, while RHBDF2,45WNT10A and FAM83G involve pleiotropic effects across multiple pathways.4
PERP is also excluded due to its dual involvement in desmosomal integrity and p53-mediated apoptosis.4 These exclusions suggest that PPK can arise not only from localized defects in the skin’s structural or barrier components, but also from broader disruptions in cellular physiology that indirectly affect the palmoplantar region.

Palmoplantar keratodermas (PPKs) comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by persistent hyperkeratosis of the palmar and plantar skin. It can be congenital or acquired. Previous studies indicate that hereditary palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK) involves genes participating in multiple molecular pathways, including gap junction communication mediated by connexins, intercellular adhesion through desmosomes, and mechanical stability provided by the keratin cytoskeleton.2 Despite their distinct molecular functions, mutations in these genes ultimately converge on mechanisms that drive epidermal hyperplasia, resulting in the shared clinical phenotype of palmoplantar hyperkeratosis. The clinical presentation of PPK is highly variable and is influenced by both genetic background and environmental factors. The distribution of hereditary PPK also shows geographic variation, and some specific diseases have been reported only in certain populations or geographic regions. For example, Nagashima type PPK has been reported only in Chinese and Japanese patients.

Hereditary PPKs can be classified according to different criteria. These classifications are primarily based on the inheritance pattern, morphology, histopathology findings, as well as the presence of additional dermatological or systemic manifestations.2 Based on the morphology of the keratoderma, PPKs are clinically classified into diffuse, focal or striate, and papular or punctate types. Some types are restricted to the palms and soles, whereas others occur as part of syndromic disorders and may be accompanied by abnormalities affecting the hair, teeth, nails, hearing, or cardiovascular system. The increasing use of next-generation sequencing technologies has led to the identification of mutations in numerous genes, and has significantly improved the understanding of the molecular basis and biological mechanisms of this heterogeneous group of disorders and has enabled their reclassification. In this study, we reclassified palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK) based on genotype–mechanism relationships, providing new insights into disease classification. This new framework also improves our understanding of PPK pathogenesis and may facilitate clinical decision making and guide therapeutic strategies.

In our study, we presented 3 PPK cases with identified gene mutation: SERPINB7, TRPV3 and KRT1. Nagashima-type palmoplantar keratoderma caused by SERPINB7 in this patient resulted from compound heterozygous mutations, c.796C>T (p.Arg266*) and c.650_653delCTGT (p.Ser217Leufs*7). The nonsense variant c.796C>T is a well-known founder mutation in East Asian populations,46 whereas the frameshift variant c.650_653delCTGT has been reported in patients with NPPK but appears to be less frequent. The SERPINB7 protein is composed of approximately 380 amino acids and adopts the canonical serpin fold, characterized by a central β-sheet A, two additional β-sheets (β-sheet B and C), and 8–9 surrounding α-helices. Serpins are characterized by a reactive center loop (RCL) positioned close to the C-terminal region. The RCL serves as a substrate mimic for proteases. Proteolytic cleavage at this site allows the loop to insert into β-sheet A, producing a substantial structural rearrangement that traps the protease and permanently blocks its enzymatic activity. SERPINA12 is another serpin-family gene implicated in palmoplantar keratoderma, and emerging evidence suggests that it may participate in epidermal barrier regulation and skin inflammatory pathways, monoallelic SERPINB7 and SERPINA12 variants was identified to cause NPPK phenotypes in a digenic manner.22,47
TRPV3 encodes a calcium-permeable transient receptor potential channel expressed in keratinocytes, where it contributes to epidermal differentiation, barrier formation, and inflammatory signaling. Gain-of-function TRPV3 variants can increase channel activity and calcium influx, thereby promoting keratinocyte hyperproliferation and painful hyperkeratosis.48 The heterozygous TRPV3 variant c.1703G>A (p.Gly568Asp) has been previously reported in a female patient with Olmsted syndrome.49 Similar to our patient, the previously reported patient also exhibited a relatively mild phenotype characterized by localized painful keratoderma. However, that patient additionally showed hyperkeratosis of the distal fingers and subungual hyperkeratosis, which were absent in our patient. The identified heterozygous mutation in the KRT1 gene (c.1255–2A>G) is a novel splice-site variant that resulted in focal palmoplantar keratoderma. The KRT1 gene is located on chromosome 12q13, within the type II keratin gene cluster, and encodes a protein of approximately 644 amino acids. KRT1 contains a central α-helical rod domain flanked by non-helical head and tail domains. The rod domain is highly conserved and includes the helix initiation and helix termination motifs, which are critical for keratin filament assembly. This variant targets the highly conserved 3’ acceptor splice site of intron 6. Its pathogenic mechanism shares the same pattern as the previously reported c.1255–1G>C mutation,14 which is recorded in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD). Both variants affect the same splice junction and are predicted to cause identical functional defects in the KRT1 protein through aberrant splicing, which weakens the keratin cytoskeleton.

Treatment of Nagashima-type palmoplantar keratoderma (NPPK) is mainly symptomatic and includes topical emollients and keratolytic agents. Topical benzoyl peroxide and antiperspirants may also be used to alleviate plantar hyperhidrosis and foot odor. Recently, topical gentamicin has emerged as a promising mechanism-based therapy for patients carrying nonsense mutations. Aminoglycosides promote premature termination codon (PTC) readthrough by binding to ribosomal RNA and disrupting codon–anticodon recognition at the aminoacyl-transfer RNA (tRNA) acceptor A site. This process allows incorporation of near-cognate tRNAs, thereby reducing translational fidelity and enabling insertion of alternative amino acids at the premature stop codon. Consequently, the ribosome can bypass the PTC and produce a full-length protein with partially restored function. Clinical studies have demonstrated that topical gentamicin treatment leads to significant improvement in hyperkeratosis, erythema, maceration, and desquamation after 1–3 months of therapy in patients with NPPK.50

Conventional treatments, including topical keratolytics, emollients, and systemic retinoids, often provide limited benefit in Olmsted syndrome.51 The mechanism of TRPV3 activation may explain why PPK patients with TRPV3 mutations often fail to respond to conventional therapies, such as topical corticosteroids and antihistamines, which do not target the underlying overactive ion channel. Administration of oral sirolimus or erlotinib resulted in significant improvement in both clinical signs and symptoms in patients with Olmsted Syndrome.52

Keratodermas caused by structural keratin defects, such as mutations in KRT1, are often aggravated by mechanical stress. Beside the conventional treatment including systemic and topical use of acetrtin to alleviate hyperkeratosis, systemic treatment with actretin and isotretin can significantly relieve the symptom. New therapies, such as mutant-specific shRNA therapy, have shown their effect in mice models.53 It is also necessary to reduce friction and mechanical stresses on affected areas. The adaptation of tailored orthotic insoles or cushioned mattress overlays can be useful to decrease focal pressure and prevent further skin thickening.1

Based on the underlying genetic function, we conducted a comprehensive review of the literature and genetic databases and classified PPK-associated genes into three major categories: those that affect protease function and epidermal barrier homeostasis, those that affect ion transport and signaling pathways, and those that affect the epidermal cytoskeleton. This tripartite taxonomy aligns with and helps to explain broad clinical patterns observed in the literature. The “defected barrier homeostasis” type, comprising genes involved in epidermal barrier formation, protease regulation, lipid metabolism, cornification, and corneocyte adhesion, tends to follow a milder, non-progressive course and often responds to emollients and keratolytics. The “Overactive Signaling” disorders, comprising genes involved in ion-channel function, inflammatory signaling, growth-factor signaling, or intracellular regulatory pathways, are more severe, painful, progressive, and refractory to conventional therapies, highlighting the need for targeted approaches. Conditions arising from “Structural Collapse” type, comprising genes encoding keratins, desmosomal proteins, cytoskeletal linkers, or extracellular matrix components that maintain epidermal mechanical integrity, present with epidermal fragility and are exacerbated by mechanical stress. For example, individuals with KRT6C-related focal palmoplantar keratoderma may develop diffuse hyperkeratosis of the soles when exposed to increased occupational mechanical stress.54 Clinical presentations of PPK were largely similar; however, understanding these mechanistic classifications can better guide rational therapeutic choice.

Although this new framework clearly elucidates pathogenic mechanisms and correlates genotype with clinical prognosis and treatment, serving as a practical tool in clinical practice, it does not encompass all PPK genes. For instance, AAGAB, associated with punctate PPK, operates through vesicular trafficking, a mechanism that has not yet been fully integrated and cannot be categorized within this framework.

In this study, we report three genetically different cases of PPK, presenting different gene mutations that disrupt epidermal hyplerplasa and lead to a similar keratoderma phenotype through different underlying mechanisms. Based on these observations, we propose three genetic disease models of PPK and introduce a mechanism-based classification framework. The new framework correlates the genetic findings with treatment choices, allowing the clinician to achieve a precise treatment strategy.

Mechanism-driven therapeutic innovation is increasingly emphasized as the future of medical genetics, and adopting mechanism-based frameworks across a broader range of diseases may facilitate clinical decision-making, especially therapeutic selection. A precise genetic diagnosis may help eliminate clinical ambiguity and enable clinicians to quickly identify the underlying disease mechanism and tailor appropriate treatment for each patient. Thus, it is important to ensure patients’ access to affordable genetic testing. Furthermore, clinicians should provide clear explanations of the clinical significance and value of genetic testing, enabling patients to have a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic diagnostic approach.
