Pleuropulmonary blastoma is a rare pediatric lung tumor of mesenchymal origin, 
with prognosis varying by subtype. While types II and III are usually 
identifiable on imaging due to their solid tumor components, the purely cystic 
type I represents a particular diagnostic challenge. In imaging, it is often 
difficult to distinguish from benign congenital lung malformations such as 
congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM, formerly congenital cystic 
adenomatoid malformation or CCAM), sequestration, or bronchogenic cyst. Accurate 
differentiation, however, is clinically relevant, as misclassification may lead 
to inadequate treatment or an increased risk of disease progression. This 
article highlights the role of imaging modalities (computed tomography, magnetic 
resonance imaging, including ultrashort echo time sequences), as well as 
histopathology and genetics-especially DICER1 mutation-in the diagnosis and 
therapeutic decision-making of pleuropulmonary blastoma. A modified flowchart 
based on Feinberg et al. provides practical guidance. For patients with 
pleuropulmonary blastoma carrying a DICER1 mutation, specific organ-targeted 
follow-up recommendations are provided to enable early detection of associated 
tumors.

sequestration, or bronchogenic cyst. Accurate differentiation, however, is clinically relevant, as misclassification may lead

Introduction
Pleuropulmonary blastoma is a rare tumor of early childhood 
(<1/1,000,000 million children [1 ]), with varying aggres-
siveness depending on its subtype. Approximately two-thirds 
of pleuropulmonary blastomas arise from the pulmonary 
mesenchyme, while one-third originate from the pleura [2]. 
They may extend into the diaphragm and mediastinum.
The cystic form of pleuropulmonary blastoma (type I) 
is radiologically difficult to distinguish from the various 
congenital benign cystic malformations of the lung [3 ]. 
Differentiating typical congenital lung malformation from 
histologically confirmed pleuropulmonary blastoma type I 
is therefore the central focus of this review. In contrast, the

Types of pleuropulmonary blastoma 
(radiology)
In 1994, Dehner established the current disease concept [6]:
– Pleuropulmonary blastoma type I presents exclusively as 
a cystic lesion without solid components (Fig.  1).
– Pleuropulmonary blastoma type II demonstrates both 
cystic and solid components on imaging. The extent of 
the solid component may vary, but its presence usually 
leaves little doubt regarding the diagnosis (Fig. 2).
– Pleuropulmonary blastoma type III appears exclusively 
as a solid lesion on imaging (Fig. 3).

Ambiguity in the radiological differentiation between 
pleuropulmonary blastoma types II and III may arise because 
pleuropulmonary blastoma type III frequently contains cen-
tral or peripheral regions with absent or minimal contrast 
enhancement. Such areas cannot be reliably distinguished 
from cysts on imaging, which may hinder differentiation 
from pleuropulmonary blastoma type II [7 , 8]. Ultimately, 
however, the distinction between the two more aggressive 
pleuropulmonary blastoma subtypes (II and III) is of limited 
clinical relevance, since therapeutic management does not 
fundamentally differ [9].
Types of pleuropulmonary blastoma 
(pathology)
In many cases, final differentiation is only possible based on 
histopathological findings.
Pleuropulmonary blastoma type I
Macroscopically, these tumors present as thin-walled cystic 
structures. Histologically, type I can be distinguished from 
type Ir (“regressed”). In the former, the thin-walled cysts 
are lined by benign respiratory epithelium, with malignant 
mesenchymal cells located within the cyst walls (Fig.  4). 
These so-called primitive cells may exhibit rhabdomyosar -
comatous differentiation. Immunohistochemical staining—
such as with CD56, desmin, or myogenin—can be helpful in 
identifying them. In the regressed type (Ir), these malignant 
cell populations are entirely absent [10, 11].
The main differential diagnoses are classical congeni -
tal lung malformations, such as the much more common 
CPAM, which lack any immature (primitive) cell clusters. 
Depending on origin and location within the bronchiolo-
alveolar system, CPAM are histologically classified into 
types 1–3. Type 1 often, though not invariably, presents mac-
roscopically with cysts larger than 2 cm and, histologically, 
shows irregularly shaped (“wavy”) thick-walled cysts lined 
by papillary, ciliated epithelium of predominantly bronchi-
olar character. In addition to smooth muscle, the cyst wall 
may also contain cartilage.
Macroscopically, the cysts of type 2 CPAM are often 
smaller than 2 cm. Microscopically, they typically display 
round cystic contours with a characteristic “punched-out” 
pattern, sometimes resembling an alveolar architecture but 
without marked structural complexity. The epithelium is flat-
tened, and smooth muscle is poorly developed or absent.
The cysts of type 3 CPAM are solid to spongy in archi-
tecture and typically measure only a few millimeters in size 
(“microcystic”). They are interspersed with small, irregular 
airspaces lined by ciliated or columnar epithelium, along -
side epithelium showing bronchiolar differentiation with-
out alveolar maturation. Notably, so-called mucinous cell 
clusters may occur in both types 1 and 3. These mucinous 
cell clusters, as well as the surrounding cyst epithelium, 
harbor mutations in the KRAS gene. Less frequently, altera-
tions in TP53,  GNAS, or FGFR have been reported. This 
observation may point toward a mutation-driven subtype of 
these hamartomatous lesions. Nevertheless, mucinous cell 
clusters are regarded as potentially premalignant, possibly 
representing precursors of mucinous adenocarcinoma. The 
debate remains controversial, but it supports the rationale 
for early surgical intervention [12, 13].
Pleuropulmonary blastoma type II
Macroscopically, solid foci may be visible within the thin 
cyst walls [14]. Histologically, the cysts are lined by respira-
tory epithelium, while the solid areas consist of malignant 
mesenchymal cells with a sarcomatoid growth pattern [4 ].

areas of necrosis. Histologically, it is composed of immature,

Choice of imaging modality
The choice of imaging modality depends, among other fac-
tors, on local practice and the availability of appropriate 
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences. In infants 
with cystic lung lesions, computed tomography (CT) is 
generally performed following an initial chest radiograph. 
Increasingly, however, MRI is also being used for the ini-
tial evaluation of congenital cystic lung abnormalities [15]. 
A prerequisite for this approach is that the MRI sequences 
employed can reliably identify cystic and hyperinflated lung 
areas. Conventional MRI sequences (in the lung, typically 
T2-weighted) are not sufficient for the detection of air-filled 
cysts. Only novel, often not yet commercially available, 
sequences with ultrashort echo time (UTE) provide reli-
able information in this respect [16, 17]. UTE sequences 
should be regarded as complementary to conventional 
MRI sequences. They achieve their short acquisition times 
by sampling the free induction decay directly, rather than 
waiting for a gradient-echo or spin-echo formation [18]. An 
alternative MRI approach for visualizing larger hyperinflated 
or cystic regions is provided by experimental functional 
ventilation/perfusion MRI sequences, which are capable of 
depicting larger cystic lesions [19]. For this specific ques-
tion, however, these sequences have not yet been validated 
and are likely inferior to UTE sequences, presumably due to 
the required slice thickness of more than 10 mm.
Where sufficient expertise in lung MRI is available and 
UTE sequences are accessible, MRI may serve as a viable 
alternative to CT as the initial cross-sectional modality in 
suspected congenital lung malformation or pleuropulmonary 
blastoma type I. Solid components of pleuropulmonary blas-
toma type II, as well as pleuropulmonary blastoma type III, 
can be well visualized using conventional respiratory-trig-
gered, T2-weighted MRI [20]. Yet, risks of sedation, often 
necessary for MRI in infants above 3 months of age, must be 
weighed against risk of ionizing radiation by CT.
Ultrasound, by contrast, plays no relevant role in the diag-
nostic workup of cystic lung lesions. It may, however, be 
useful in differentiating a radiographically solid-appearing 
opacity caused by a mass lesion from one due to large atelec-
tasis or pneumonia [21] (Fig. 6).
Incidence and mortality of pleuropulmonary 
blastoma
The incidence of pleuropulmonary blastoma is very low. 
In the USA, approximately 25–50 new pediatric cases are 
diagnosed each year [4 ], corresponding to an incidence of 
0.07–0.14 cases per 10,000 live births annually.
According to the International PPB/DICER1 Registry, 
the relative frequency of cystic pleuropulmonary blastoma 
(type I) was 33% [5 ]. While this registry represents a large 
dataset, it is by no means comprehensive, as reporting is 
not mandatory for clinical centers. Estimates of incidence 
and mortality therefore vary considerably depending on the 
underlying data source. In particular, reported incidence 
rates of pleuropulmonary blastoma type I in older publi-
cations (without centralized histological review) are highly 
uncertain, as some cases were likely misclassified as con-
genital lung malformations. As a result, the true incidence 
of pleuropulmonary blastoma type I may be underestimated 
[22, 23].

Reliable epidemiological data on the frequency of malignant 
transformation from congenital lung malformation into pleu-
ropulmonary blastoma or other malignancies are lacking. 
Nonetheless, concern about this risk remains the principal 
justification for surgical resection of nearly all congenital 
lung malformations (apart from congenital lobar overinfla-
tion and bronchial atresia). The debate regarding the neces-
sity of prophylactic resection continues.
The relative frequency of pleuropulmonary blastoma type 
II is approximately 35%. Treatment is guided by pediatric 
sarcoma protocols and consists of initial neoadjuvant chemo-
therapy followed by complete surgical resection whenever 
possible. In selected cases, radiotherapy or high-dose chem-
otherapy with stem-cell rescue may be considered.
The relative frequency of pleuropulmonary blastoma type 
III is approximately 32%. Its treatment follows the same 
general principles as pleuropulmonary blastoma type II, 
but the therapeutic intensity usually increases with tumor 
size and is typically higher for type III. The overall 5-year 
survival rate of pleuropulmonary blastoma types II and III 
combined is approximately 70%. However, survival varies 
widely depending on disease extent (localized, locoregional, 
or metastatic). Reported survival rates range from 80% in 
localized pleuropulmonary blastoma type II to only 29% in 
metastatic pleuropulmonary blastoma type III [24].
Radiological differential diagnosis
In total, five benign cystic malformations must always be 
considered in the differential diagnosis of pleuropulmonary 
blastoma type I [22, 26–28].
– CPAM types 1–3 (according to Stocker)
– Hybrid sequestration (a combination of CPAM and pul-
monary sequestration)
– Bronchogenic cyst
– Congenital lobar overinflation
– Bronchial atresia

Histologically, type Ir is reportedly distinguished from 
conventional pleuropulmonary blastoma type I by certain 
morphological features [31]. Radiologically, however,

histopathological examination or observation of spontane-

Isolated case reports have described metastatic adenocar-
cinomas arising in the context of CPAM, occasionally with 
fatal outcomes, typically in cases of incomplete, delayed, 
or absent surgical resection [34]. Remarkably, no cases of 
metachronous metastatic disease have been reported to date 
following complete surgical resection [35 ]. In CPAM, the 
underlying alterations are usually somatic—more precisely, 
mosaic mutations—arising later in embryonic development. 
Depending on the timing and location of the mutation, dif-
ferent tissues or organs may be affected in a segmental or 
localized fashion [13].
By contrast, pleuropulmonary blastoma is associated with 
germline mutations within the DICER1 gene in approximately 
70–80% of cases. These represent the first “hit,” resulting in 
monoallelic loss of function. A subsequent somatic “second 
hit,” often a hotspot mutation in the RNase IIIb domain, leads 
to biallelic inactivation and tumorigenesis. This two-hit model 
forms the basis of DICER1 tumor predisposition syndrome, 
with pleuropulmonary blastoma being its hallmark neoplasm. 
The syndrome is also associated with other tumors such as 
ovarian Sertoli–Leydig cell tumors, cystic nephroma, and 
thyroid neoplasms (e.g., multinodular goiter, papillary carci-
noma), as well as tumors of the central nervous system. In rare 
cases, purely somatic DICER1 mutations without germline 
involvement have been reported.
Therefore, in every diagnosis of pleuropulmonary blas-
toma, molecular testing of tumor tissue for DICER1 should 
be complemented by germline (blood-based) genetic analy-
sis, both to assess for DICER1 syndrome and to inform fam-
ily counseling [14].
Clinical recommendations
The likelihood criteria listed in Table  1 represent the only 
radiological tools available to help differentiate benign CPAM 
from malignant pleuropulmonary blastoma type I. The result-
ing flowchart assists in deciding whether surgical resection is 
warranted. In clinical practice, however, cases are encoun-
tered that do not fit into this scheme; even after appropriate 
cross-sectional imaging, it may remain uncertain whether the 
lesion represents a congenital lung malformation or pleuro-
pulmonary blastoma type I. The overall accuracy rate for dis-
tinguishing benign from malignant lesions is only 81% [23].

infants and young children is surgically resected, irrespective 
of the estimated likelihood of malignancy. This practice is

Summary and outlook
Pleuropulmonary blastoma type I is radiologically dif-
ficult to distinguish from benign congenital lung malfor -
mations, leading to diagnostic uncertainty and potentially 
inappropriate treatment. Since definitive differentiation 
is often only possible histologically, many centers cur -
rently recommend surgical resection of all cystic lung 
lesions in infants and young children. Genetic testing for 
DICER1 mutations is mandatory in confirmed pleuropul-
monary blastoma, as it provides the basis for organ-spe-
cific, lifelong tumor surveillance. A modified flowchart 
may serve as a practical aid in clinical decision-making, 
but it cannot replace comprehensive interdisciplinary 
evaluation.
