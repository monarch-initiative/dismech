Abstract

                  Cohesin is a fundamental genome-organizing complex that orchestrates three-dimensional chromosome folding and gene expression via DNA loop extrusion. Alterations to genes encoding cohesin subunits and cohesin loaders cause Mendelian disorders, including Cornelia de Lange syndrome (CdLS). By contrast, disruption of factors that remove cohesin from DNA, including
                  WAPL
                  and its binding partners
                  PDS5A
                  and
                  PDS5B
                  , have not yet been associated with human disease. Here, we explored the relevance of these cohesin release factors in Mendelian disease by establishing a rare disease cohort of deeply phenotyped individuals with heterozygous, predicted damaging variants in
                  WAPL
                  (n=27),
                  PDS5A
                  (n=8), and
                  PDS5B
                  (n=8), by modeling
                  WAPL
                  deficiency in human cell lines and mice, and by aggregating rare disease association statistics from consortia studies. We identified a
                  WAPL
                  -related disorder characterized by developmental delay, intellectual disability, and risk of other developmental anomalies including clubfoot. Similarities between individuals with damaging
                  WAPL
                  variants and those with large, recurrent 10q22.3q23.2 (10q) deletions (which encompass
                  WAPL
                  ) nominate
                  WAPL
                  as a driver gene within this genomic disorder region. While carriers of
                  PDS5A
                  or
                  PDS5B
                  variants exhibited features of developmental disorders, neither cohort-based statistics nor case phenotyping associated these genes with specific phenotypes. We used CRISPR engineering to generate truncating variants in
                  WAPL
                  , as well the 7.8 Mb 10q deletion or duplication in human iPSCs and induced neurons. Transcriptomic analyses identified differentially expressed genes in both models, with highly significant overlap between
                  WAPL
                  haploinsufficiency and 10q deletion signatures. Mice with 50% residual
                  Wapl
                  expression exhibited mild deficits of growth and learning/memory, whereas those with 25% residual
                  Wapl
                  expression displayed birth defects and postnatal lethality, revealing a dosage liability threshold below the level of heterozygosity. In summary, we delineated a novel genetic condition caused by cohesin release factor deficiency, nominated
                  WAPL
                  as a driver gene within a genomic disorder region, and further illuminated dosage sensitivity of human cohesin.

(Chan et al., 2012; Gandhi et al., 2006; Huis in 't Veld et al., 2014; Kueng et al., 2006; Silva et al., 44 
2020; Tedeschi et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2021) . These opposing forces render genome 45 
organization a dynamic and cyclical process (Gabriele et al., 2022; Mach et al., 2022).  46 
Data from a very large -scale dosage sensitivity prediction analysis of rare copy number 47 
variants (rCNVs) across human phenotypes in almost 1M individuals (Collins et al., 2022)  48 
demonstrate that autosomal cohesinopathy genes, including the cohesin loader NIPBL, have both 49 
high predicted haploinsufficiency (pHaplo) scores and high triplosensitivity (pTriplo) scores, 50 
suggesting that these loci are bidirectionally dosage sensitive (Table S1). This is corroborated by 51 
the existence of known human phenotypes resulting from both heterozygous copy loss of NIPBL 52 
in CdLS and duplication of this gene in the 5p13 duplication syndrome (OMIM #613174) (Yan et 53 
al., 2009). These data, and the known dynamism between TAD formation and resetting, suggests 54 
that a disruption of cohesin balance in either direction may be pathological. Thus, it is surprising 55 
that neither WAPL nor its binding partners PDS5A and PDS5B – the three cohesin release factors 56 
– have been systematically assessed as potential disease genes (Yuan et al., 2019). 57

a novel class of cohesinopathies, and that their clinical and molecular features could provide 59

WAPL as a driver of phenotypes in the 10q22.3q23.2 genomic disorder  108 
WAPL is contained within chromosome 10q22.3q23.2, which is subject to recurrent 109 
deletions and duplications of ~7.8 Mb between flanking low-copy repeats termed LCR3 and LCR4 110 
(Balciuniene et al., 2007)  (Fig. 1e). Case reports have associated these deletions with NDD, 111 
craniofacial dysmorphism, and other features including congenital heart disease and limb 112 
abnormalities, while duplications present with milder developmental effects. However, a 113 
consensus phenotype has not been delineated, and driver genes have been proposed but not 114 
confirmed (Azidane et al., 2024; Breckpot et al., 2012; Coelho Molck et al., 2017; Saito et al., 115 
2012) (Table S5).  116 
To inform on WAPL dosage sensitivity and to make phenotypic comparisons between 10q 117 
dels and WAPL single nucleotide variants (SNVs), we sought to define the 10q22.3q23.2 deletion 118 
phenotype. We screened our cross-disorder rare copy number variant (rCNV) microarray dataset 119 
composed of 458,326 cases (i.e., samples with at least one diagnosed condition) and 491,952 120 
control individuals (Collins et al., 2022) . Our analysis identified 41 carriers of the canonical 121 
10q22.3q23.2 deletion, which were strongly associated with developmental phenotypes (OR = 122 
20.9; two -sided Fisher test of proportions p = 1.8e -10). The most common HPO terms were 123 
abnormality of the nervous system (n = 24; 59%), neurodevelopmental abnormality (n = 21; 51%), 124 
abnormality of head or neck (n = 9; 22%), abnormality of the face (n = 8; 20%), abnormality of 125 
the cardiovascular system (n = 5; 12%), growth abnormality (n = 5), and atypical behavior (n = 5) 126 
(Fig. S17). We also identified 24 carriers of the canonical duplication. In line with the previously 127 
purported milder effect of the duplication, the enrichment of duplication carriers among cases was 128 
more modest (OR = 3.2; p -value = 0.013), although a similar pattern of affected organ systems 129 
with the deletion carriers was noted (Fig. S17). This suggests that the dosage of the region is under 130 
tight control. Next, we performed a literature review and identified 27 published cases of the 131 
deletion. The most common phenotypic features among these 27 cases are NDDs, facial 132

Episignature of cohesin release factors 141 
Global DNA methylation aberrancies have been found in peripheral blood of patients with 142 
CdLS (Aref-Eshghi et al., 2020) , which are both biologically informative (i.e., evidence of 143 
downstream effects of cohesin loss on the epigenome) and clinically useful (i.e., as a diagnostic 144 
aid in cases of cohesin gene variants of uncertain significance). We applied EpiSignTM analysis to 145 
subjects’ peripheral blood DNA (Table S6) to establish a genome -wide DNA methylation 146 
episignature. Three distinct analyses were conducted. Analysis 1 combined truncating variants 147 
affecting WAPL (including 10q dels), PDS5A, and PDS5B. Analysis 2 combined WAPL missense 148 
SNV and 10q del cases. Analysis 3 assessed only WAPL SNV (protein-truncating and missense) 149 
cases. Informative and variably methylated sites were selected, and their ability to distinguish case 150 
samples from controls was confirmed using hierarchical clustering and multidimensional scaling 151 
(MDS) (Fig. S1). The reproducibility of the identified profiles was assessed using leave -one-out 152 
cross-validation (LOOCV) (Fig. S2). The left -out sample clustered with the other cases in 11 of 153 
14 LOOCV iterations of Analysis 1, in 8 of 10 iterations of Analysis 2, and 0 of 8 iterations of 154 
Analysis 3 ( i.e., no methylation signature for WAPL SNVs alone was found). A support vector 155 
machine (SVM) classifier was developed by training case samples against their matched controls, 156 
along with 75% of control samples from the EpiSign Knowledge Database (EKD) and thousands 157 
of samples from individuals with other rare genetic disorders included in the EpiSign v5 clinical 158 
classifier. This was done for Analyses 1 (Fig. S3a) and 2 (Fig. S3b). The remaining 25% of control 159 
and other episignature disorder samples were used to test the model. Both models demonstrated 160 
high specificity, as the majority of testing samples from other episignature disorders (including 161 
CdLS) received very low methylation variant pathogenicity (MVP) scores.  162 
In summary, we identified an overall cohesin release factor deficiency -associated DNA 163 
methylation signature and a DNA methylation signature combining WAPL SNV and 10q del 164 
samples. We did not identify a DNA methylation signature for WAPL SNVs alone. These DNA 165 
methylation patterns do not mimic the overall CdLS methylation pattern (Fig. 2c).  166

Discussion 
Genome organization is an essential nuclear property with implications across biological 352 
functions and scale (Misteli, 2020). The cohesin complex exemplifies both mechanistic control of 353 
chromosome topology and – via cataloged mutations in its component genes – the phenomenon of 354 
genome disorganization as a potential cause of disease (Piche et al., 2019). Cohesin is loaded and 355 
removed from chromatin in a cyclical manner. Yet, the relevance to human disease of cohesin 356 
release factors is unknown. 357 
 
Variants in WAPL define a syndrome of cohesin release factor deficiency 358 
We investigated whether damaging variation in the genes encoding cohesin release factors, 359 
which play an opposing role to the cohesin loader/extrusion factor (and quintessential 360 
cohesinopathy gene) NIPBL, may also be disease genes. Phenotyping individuals with 361 
heterozygous predicted damaging variants in WAPL revealed a WAPL-related syndrome, which 362 
we propose to provisionally label “WAPL deficiency syndrome” (WDS). This condition is 363 
characterized principally by mild-moderate developmental delay/intellectual disability, but also in 364 
some individuals by craniofacial dysmorphism, organ malformations including clubfoot and mild 365 
congenital heart lesions, and other variable features. WAPL variants underlying the above cases 366 
were concentrated in C -terminal regions of the protein, suggesting the relative importance of C -367 
terminal WAPL isoforms and the C-terminal WAPL domain. This is interesting to consider given 368 
differing reported roles for C - versus N -terminal regions of WAPL (Hara et al., 2014)  and 369 
coincides with the C-terminal portion being more deeply conserved and apparently more structured 370 
(Brandes et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2013). Supporting the above, we found that 371 
heterozygous, predicted damaging variants in WAPL are statistically enriched among individuals 372 
with developmental disorders and neurodevelopmental delay.  373 
WAPL participates in meiotic chromosome structuring/segregation (Crawley et al., 2016), 374 
however WAPL heterozygosity appears compatible with human reproduction given observed 375 
paternal and maternal inheritance in at least one case each. Immune defects and cancer did not 376 
seem to be enriched despite WAPL having roles in DNA damage repair (Benedict et al., 2020; 377 
Misulovin et al., 2018) and V(D)J recombination (Dai et al., 2021; Hill et al., 2020) . Despite the 378 
opposing biological roles of their protein products, WDS is milder than and neither a mirror nor a 379

Phenotypes in subjects with PDS5A and PDS5B variants 386 
While WAPL, PDS5A, and PDS5B are all involved in cohesin release, their specific roles 387 
are more nuanced. WAPL removes interphase cohesin from chromatin, acting to decrease 388 
cohesin’s residence time on chromatin, limit loop length, and guide cell -type specific gene 389 
regulation (Busslinger et al., 2017; Gassler et al., 2017; Haarhuis et al., 2017; Kiefer et al., 2023; 390 
Kueng et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2021; Tedeschi et al., 2013; Wutz et al., 2017). PDS5A and PDS5B 391 
(which in some species are a single protein, PDS5) serve as adaptors between WAPL and cohesin, 392 
yet they are not solely cohesin release factors (Zhang et al., 2021) . PDS5 also inhibits cohesin 393 
ATPase activity (Murayama and Uhlmann, 2014; Petela et al., 2018) , is involved in recognizing 394 
convergent CTCF proteins as boundary elements (Wutz et al., 2017) , and recruits ESCO1 and 395 
ESCO2 which promote acetylation of the core cohesin protein SMC3 (Chan et al., 2013; Deardorff 396 
et al., 2012)  to induce cohesin pausing and loop size restriction. Finally, PDS5 can be bound 397 
competitively by sororin (CDCA5), and PDS5 -sororin has the opposite effect of PDS5 -WAPL: 398 
maintenance of cohesin on chromatin (Morales et al., 2020; Nishiyama et al., 2010; Wutz et al., 399 
2017). The net effect of PDS5 loss on genome organization, and whether redundant and/or 400 
differential roles exist for PDS5A versus PDS5B, are still being established (Al-Jomah et al., 2020; 401 
Carretero et al., 2013; Couturier et al., 2016; Cuadrado et al., 2022; Losada et al., 2005; Meisenberg 402 
et al., 2019; Ouyang et al., 2016; van Ruiten et al., 2022; Waizenegger et al., 2000; Wutz et al., 403 
2017; Zhang et al., 2021). 404 
In our study, subjects with variants in PDS5A or PDS5B possessed considerable morbidity, 405 
and no healthy individual has been described in the literature with a predicted damaging variant in 406 
these genes. Some trends emerged in our cases including neurodevelopmental and neurological 407 
features. Yet, the number of PDS5A and PDS5B cases was smaller than that of WAPL cases, and 408 
their features less convincingly c onverged into a unified Mendelian syndrome. Although 409

Differential gene expression in CRISPR disease models  416 
Various knockdown or knockout models of cohesin genes and studies of patient -derived 417 
cell lines or tissue have been undertaken. However, no engineered cellular models exist with the 418 
appropriate zygosity (heterozygous), species (human), and cell type (neuronal) to replicate 419 
mechanisms of brain disease in human cohesinopathies. Each of these aspects may be critically 420 
important for modeling human disease; for example, knockdown experiments where the target 421 
gene is not at heterozygous ploidy may yield different results if they fall at a different point along 422 
a dosage sensitivity inflection curve for that gene (Nadig et al., 2025). We thus used CRISPR to 423 
create heterozygous models in human iPSCs and iNs for WAPL+/- and 10q dels and dups.  424 
RNA-seq demonstrated fewer DEGs in WAPL models than in published models of NIPBL 425 
loss, which is in line with WAPL deficiency syndrome being milder than classic CdLS . We 426 
determined pathways enriched among WAPL and 10q D EGs that could be relevant to disease 427 
biology, for example cell -cell adhesion in neuronal models . Finally, we found that t he 10q del 428 
expression signature appeared to be partially driven by WAPL.  429 
 We found no evidence of expression buffering (i.e. dosage compensation) of any gene in 430 
the 10q22.3q23.2 interval, in the context of deletion or duplication of this region. Of note, LoF 431 
variants in BMPR1A, a gene which falls within the recurrent 10q region, cause a dominant 432 
polyposis syndrome (OMIM *601299). Yet, patients with 10q deletions are not reported to have 433 
polyposis unless they house larger deletions also eliminating the tumor suppressor gene PTEN, a 434 
gene beyond the recurrent deletion’s boundaries (OMIM #612242). A potential explanation for 435 
this phenomenon could be that BMPR1A exhibits dosage compensation when deleted; yet, our data 436 
argue against this hypothesis, at least in the cell lines we modeled (average BMPR1A expression 437 
in 10q del iPSCs decreased by 48% of wt and in iNs by 47%). 438

Limitations and future possibilities 452 
 The penetrance and expressivity of WAPL variants will be clarified by future studies. 453 
Additional studies are needed to confirm disease associations of variants in cohesin release factor 454 
genes, and human cell models of PDS5A and PDS5B genes are needed to further elucidate these 455 
genes’ shared vs disparate and redundant vs independent role s. Further mechanistic studies of 456 
cohesin balance, cycling, and effect on the 3D genome in our models are needed to confirm 457 
pathomechanistic connections linking mutation, genome disorganization, gene misexpression, and 458 
disease. The roles of cohesin and WAPL during human neurodevelopment are of particular 459 
interest, both for informing on brain development and critical temporal windows for treating 460 
cohesinopathies.  461

of WAPL,  are found in patients with intellectual disability and/or other neurodevelopmental 466 
features, but we did not find convincing evidence that these genes underlie clinically homogeneous 467

We applied EpiSignTM analysis to establish a genome-wide DNA methylation episignature. 538 
All case subjects were de -identified and the research was conducted in accordance with relevant 539

by Illumina for poor technical performance. Samples were also excluded if > 5% of their probes 551 
failed or if they were previously identified in the EpiSign Knowledge Database (EKD, 552 
https://episign.lhsc.on.ca/index.html) as being prone to batch effects. All remaining samples 553

Selection of matched control samples: Using the MatchIt package (version 4.5.5; Ho, Imai 563

Methylation profiling: For each probe, the methylation level (β -value) was calculated by 570 
dividing the intensity of the methylated signal by the combined intensity of both methylated and 571 
unmethylated signals. This β -value ranges from 0 (indicating no methylation) to 1 (indicating 572 
complete methylation). These β -values were then converted into M -values using a logit 573 
transformation prior to conducting linear regression analysis with the limma package (v3.58.1) 574 
(Ritchie et al., 2015). The analysis accounted for variations in blood cell type composition, which 575 
were estimated using Houseman’s algorithm (Houseman et al., 2012). Estimated cell proportions 576 
were included as covariates in the model matrix for the linear model. The p-values obtained from 577 
the model were moderated using the eBayes function in limma. For Analysis 2, multiple testing 578 
correction was performed using the Benjamini -Hochberg (BH) procedure, while for Analysis 1 579 
and 3, non-adjusted p-values were utilized.  580

Evidence for the association between genetic variation in a gene and a phenotype was 621 
obtained through the Bayesian transmission and de novo association (TADA) framework that 622 
incorporates evidence from different study designs and variant classes (Fu et al., 2022; He et al., 623 
2013). To do so, SNV/indels were stratified into five classes: i) protein-truncating variants (PTVs) 624 
identified as high -confidence loss -of-function or “other splice” (OS) variants by LOFTEE  625 
(Karczewski et al., 2020) ; ii -iv) three classes of missense variants depending on whether the 626

discovery rate (FDR), as previously described (Fu et al., 2022). Significance threshold for evidence 635 
of an association between damaging variants in a gene and a phenotype was defined at FDR < 636 
0.001, which has previously been shown to approximate exome-wide Bonferroni significance 637 
threshold (P < 2.8 × 10−6) (Fu et al., 2022). 638
