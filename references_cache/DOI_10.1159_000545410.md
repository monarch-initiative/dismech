# Genotype-Phenotype Analysis and New Clinical Findings in a Series of 24 Patients Presenting with Noonan Syndrome and Related Disorders
**Authors:** Bruna Miranda Corso, Luiza de Oliveira Simões, Karina Montemor Klegen de Oliveira, Ana Mondadori dos Santos, Luise Longo Angeloni, Ruy Pires de Oliveira Sobrinho, Vera Lúcia Gil-da-Silva-Lopes, Antonia Paula Marques-de-Faria, Andréa Trevas Maciel Guerra, Gil Guerra-Junior, Alexander Augusto de Lima Jorge, Elisângela Pereira de Souza Quedas, Henrique Garcia Silveira, Giovana Toccoli, Maria Isabel Melaragno, Mara Sanches Guaragna, Carlos Eduardo Steiner
**Journal:** Molecular Syndromology (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000545410](https://doi.org/10.1159/000545410)

## Content

Introduction: RASopathies are a heterogeneous group of conditions of the RAS/mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway presenting with overlapping features such as growth deficiency, neurodevelopmental disorders, cardiac defects, craniofacial dysmorphisms, cutaneous and ocular abnormalities, and increased cancer risk. Methods: This retrospective study analyzed the medical records regarding clinical and molecular data from 2018 to 2024 in a single center for rare diseases of individuals diagnosed with Noonan syndrome and related disorders previously submitted to diagnostic molecular analysis through next-generation sequencing techniques. Results: Twenty-four patients were enrolled with an even sex ratio distribution and ages ranging from 1 month to 16 years at first evaluation. The main reason for referral was diagnostic assessment due to a combination of dysmorphic features (24/24; 100%), growth deficiency (18/24; 75%), neurodevelopmental disorders (15/24; 62.5%), and/or heart disease (13/24; 54.1%). Final diagnoses included 15 individuals with Noonan syndrome (nine with variants in PTPN11, two in SOS1, and one each in LZTR1, A2ML1, and MRAS, besides one with variants in both LZTR1 and SOS1), two with Noonan syndrome with multiple lentigines (both with variants in PTPN11), two with Neurofibromatosis-Noonan (NF1), two with cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome (BRAF), and one each with Noonan syndrome-like with loose anagen hair (PPP1CB), Noonan syndrome-like (CBL), and Costello syndrome (HRAS); one individual presented with a double diagnosis of Noonan and Klinefelter syndromes. Conclusion: Three pairs of unrelated patients presented recurrent variants in the PTPN11 gene, partially concordant in phenotypic correlation among the pairs but not fully concordant compared to previously described cases in the literature. Undescribed features in this group included myopathy and megacolon in a patient with Noonan syndrome-like, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and azoospermia in a patient with Noonan syndrome-like with loose anagen hair, and schizophrenia in a patient with Costello syndrome. One patient with Noonan syndrome had a novel variant of the A2ML1 gene (c.1829G>A), but the variant was strictly of uncertain significance, while c.2033G>A in the LZTR1 gene and c.1A>G in the NF1 gene are variants for the first time associated with features of Noonan syndrome.

Keywords:RASopathies, Noonan syndrome, Cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome, Costello syndrome, Neurofibromatosis-Noonan phenotype, Next-generation sequencing

The term RASopathies refers to a heterogeneous group of genetic conditions caused by germline mutations in genes that encode components or regulators of the RAS/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, which plays an essential role in regulating the cell cycle and cellular growth, differentiation, and senescence, all of which are critical to normal development [1]. Due to this common pathway dysregulation, they exhibit numerous overlapping phenotypic features, including growth deficiency, neurodevelopmental disorders, dysmorphic craniofacial features, cardiac malformations, cutaneous and ocular abnormalities, and increased cancer risk [1,2].

They are one of the largest known groups of malformation syndromes, with a prevalence of up to 1:1,000 individuals [2] and the second most common cause of syndromic congenital heart defects after Down syndrome [3]. Well-established disorders include Noonan syndrome (NS), Noonan syndrome with multiple lentigines (NSML, formerly LEOPARD syndrome), Noonan syndrome-like (NSL) with or without juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia, Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair (NSLAH), Costello syndrome (CS), cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome (CFCS), Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), neurofibromatosis-Noonan (NFN) phenotype, and Legius syndrome (LS), besides capillary malformation-arteriovenous malformation syndrome [1,2,4].

More than 20 genes with a role in the RAS-MAPK signaling network have been established to underlie RASopathies and includeBRAF,CBL,HRAS,KRAS,LZTR1,MAP2KA1,MAP2K2,MAPK1,MRAS,NF1,PPP1CB,PTPN11,RAF1,RASA2,RIT1,RRAS2,SHOC2,SOS1,SOS2,SPRED1, andSPRED2, each one causing a single phenotype or different phenotypes indicating genetic heterogeneity among the RASopathies [2]. Additionally, variants in theA2ML1gene have been described in individuals with an NS phenotype, although its role in RASopathies remains controversial [5–7]. All express autosomal dominant inheritance, but biallelic variants in theLZTR1or theSPRED2genes cause autosomal recessive forms [2,8–10]. Variants in thePTPN11gene are responsible for about half of the cases of NS, followed bySOS1andLZTR1, while other genes account for 5% or less of the remaining cases [1,2].

This is a retrospective case series study of individuals diagnosed with RASopathies in a single reference center for rare diseases. Clinical and laboratory data were collected from medical records from 2018 to 2024 in a cohort of patients previously submitted to diagnostic molecular analysis through gene panel sequencing (GPS), whole exome sequencing (WES), or whole genome sequencing (WGS), according to availability. Patients were included in the study only when they presented with variants classified as pathogenic and likely pathogenic or of uncertain significance (VUS) with a positive phenotypic correlation. Data included sex, age of first assessment in the hospital, family history, clinical and laboratory findings description, and molecular results.

Chromosomal analysis was performed on G-banded metaphase cells according to standard procedures. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) tests were conducted on DNA extracted from peripheral blood samples using different strategies according to availability.

GPS comprised a customized targeted panel with interest genes [11]. Alternatively, some individuals in this sample were analyzed in the cardiomyopathy comprehensive panel by Invitae Corporation (San Francisco, CA, USA).

WES was performed at Mendelics Genomic Analysis (São Paulo, SP, Brazil) facilities using Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The sequencing library was built with Illumina Nextera Flex, and a Customized Exome Kit from Twist Biosciences was used to capture target regions. Sequencing of samples resulted in paired 101 bp sequences mapped to the hg38 reference using BWA MEM software version 1.1.4 (http://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/) accessed on 12 March 2024. The resulting VCF files were processed using Mendelics’ in-house pipeline for annotation and filtering. Quality metrics were minimum coverage over 10× and at least 90% depth.

WGS was performed on an Illumina platform, and data were processed to detect point mutations, copy numbers, and structural variants as per best practices for the bioinformatics pipeline [12]. Quality metrics were minimum coverage over 20× and at least 90% depth above 15×. The reference genome was GRCh38/hg38. Variants nomenclature and classification of all NGS tests (GPS, WES, and WGS) followed the ACMG recommendations [13] and the ClinGen’s RASopathy Expert Panel consensus [14].

Twenty-four individuals were enrolled, with an even sex ratio distribution. The age in their first assessment in the hospital where this study was conducted ranged from 1 month to 16 years. The main reason for referral was diagnostic evaluation due to a combination of dysmorphic features (24/24; 100%), growth deficiency (18/24; 75%), neurodevelopmental disorders (15/24; 62.5%), and/or heart disease (13/24; 54.1%).

The dysmorphic features were mainly craniofacial, and the most frequent findings were low-set and/or dysmorphic ears (17/24; 70.8%), blepharoptosis (15/24; 62.5%), triangular face (11/24; 45.8%), epicanthal folds (10/24; 41.6%), webbed neck (10/24; 41.6%), and hair abnormalities (9/24; 37.5%) (Fig. 1). Growth disorders included isolated prenatal growth deficiency (intrauterine growth retardation or low birth length) in 2/24 (8.3%), postnatal short stature in 10/24 (41.6%), or a combination of pre- and postnatal growth deficiency as seen in 6/24 (25%) patients; one individual (patient 19) presented with postnatal tall stature. Neurodevelopmental disorders comprised developmental delay and/or learning disabilities; intellectual deficiency was confirmed by IQ tests in three individuals, ranging from mild to severe, and one individual (patient 19) presented with borderline intelligence. Isolated or combined heart disease was present in over half of the patients in this cohort and most frequently was congenital, including pulmonary valve stenosis (9/24; 37.5%), persistence of ductus arteriosus and ventricular septal defect (4/24; 16.6%, each), atrial septal defect (3/24; 12.5%), patent foramen ovale (3/24; 12.5%), and persistent left superior vena cava (1/24; 4.1%). Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) was seen in seven (29.1%) individuals, three of them presenting as congenital and four of postnatal onset.

The craniofacial features variability among individuals with RASopathies in this group: Noonan syndrome due to variants inPTNP11(P6),SOS1(11),A2ML1(P14),MRAS(P15), and Noonan syndrome with multiple lentigines (P16), Neurofibromatosis-Noonan (P20), cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome (P22 and P23), and Costello syndrome (P24).

Concerning diagnostic suspicion, most individuals were clinically diagnosed with NS, two with NFN, two with CS, and three with NSML, one of which (patient 18) was reclassified as presenting with NSLAH (Fig. 2) by clinical reevaluation after molecular testing; patient 19 (Fig. 3) initially had no clinical suspicion of RASopathy and was later diagnosed with NSL. All individuals had previous routine chromosomal analysis with normal results except for patient 8, who presented a 47,XXY karyotype compatible with Klinefelter syndrome.

Regarding the molecular investigation, variants were identified in thePTPN11gene (11/24; 45.8%), followed bySOS1(3/24; 12.5%),BRAF,LZTR1, andNF1(2/24; 8.3%, each), as well asA2ML1,CBL,HRAS,MRAS, andPPP1CBgenes (1/24; 4.1%, each). Individual 12 (Fig. 4) presented variants in two genes,LZTR1andSOS1. All variants were in heterozygosity, and most were missense and classified as pathogenic; segregation analysis was not performed due to resource limitations. Three individuals presenting with VUS were considered to have a positive phenotypic correlation. The variant in theA2ML1gene was the only novel in this series, while the remaining have been previously reported in the literature and/or the ClinVar database. All patients in this study were referred due to a combination of dysmorphic features, growth deficiency, neurodevelopmental disorders, and/or heart disease. None of them presented with malignancies, but they were all evaluated during childhood or early adulthood, and a longer clinical follow-up would be necessary to determine the occurrence of tumors.

Variants in thePTPN11gene were present in 45.8% of the cohort, in a similar frequency in individuals with NS, which was the first one to be discovered in association with this condition in nearly 40–50% of the cases, usually presenting with missense variants [1,2,39]. Three pairs of unrelated patients in the present cohort presented with recurrent variants in thePTPN11gene.

The first recurrent variant in thePTPN11gene was c.188A>G p.Tyr63Cys found in individuals 1 and 2, concordant for postnatal growth deficiency and typical craniofacial dysmorphisms but divergent for neurodevelopmental disorder and heart disease. The same was seen in 2 other patients of Japanese origin with the same variant reported by Kosaki et al. [16], concordant for craniofacial dysmorphisms but divergent for growth, neurodevelopmental disorders, and heart disease. Joyce et al. [17] reported an adult male with NS and severe lymphatic dysplasia, and another additional report was provided by Sarkozy et al. [18] in three generations of a family with this variant.

The second one was c.215C>G p.(Ala72Gly), found in patients 3 and 4. Both were concordant for some craniofacial dysmorphisms and heart disease (septal defect, PVS) but discordant for short stature, neurodevelopmental disorder, and other facial features. Yang et al. [40] previously identified the same variant in a patient of Chinese origin who presented with short stature, language delay, unilateral hearing loss, ASD, cryptorchidism, and dysmorphic features.

There are few detailed clinical descriptions regarding the variant identified in individual 5, c.417G>C in thePTNP11gene. Nevertheless, Musante et al. [21] described short stature and PVS in 1 sporadic patient. On the other hand, the variant in individual 6, c.854T>C, was previously described in a patient by Kosaki et al. [16], also identified as case 6 by these authors, and overlapped features include the postnatal onset of short stature, mild neurodevelopmental disorders, some craniofacial dysmorphisms, and heart disease; however, individual 6 in our study presents with PFO and left ventricular hypertrophy, while their patient presented with PVS.

The third recurrent variant in thePTPN11gene was c.922A>G p.Asn308Asp seen in individuals 7 and 8, presenting with craniofacial dysmorphisms but divergent for short stature, neurodevelopmental disorders, and heart disease. It has also been previously reported by Kosaki et al. [16] in a male patient and her mother, both presenting with normal neurologic development, short stature, dysmorphic features, and PVS only in the boy. This variant in exon 8 was first described by Tartaglia et al. [15] and is now considered the most common variant in NS; its de novo mutation frequency exceeds the genome average A>G mutation frequency by more than 2,400-fold [20]. Jongmans et al. [19] also reported 13 other individuals with the same variant but did not provide detailed clinical data, although they support the reputation of this nucleotide as usually associated with normal intelligence, as seen in patient 7 of the present study, but not in patient 8 who has a concomitant diagnosis of Klinefelter syndrome.

The co-occurrence of NS and Klinefelter syndrome, as seen in patient 8, is known since the pre-molecular routine testing era. In 1987, Verloes et al. [41] reported a boy with both conditions, referring to probable other cases from the 1960s.

The c.923A>G variant in thePTPN11gene found in individual 9 was previously reported by Sarkozy et al. [18] in a familial case also presenting with PVS, but these authors provided no further clinical details. On the other hand, the present patient overlaps with the description of patient 4 reported by El Bouchikhi et al. [42], regarding heart disease (PVS and HCM), craniofacial dysmorphisms, and learning disabilities, but not short stature, absent in the latter.

BesidesPTPN11, three individuals presented with variants in theSOS1gene, and pathogenic variants inSOS1are NS’s second most common molecular cause, mainly due to missense mutations and small in-dels. There is no strong genotype-phenotype correlation; nevertheless, such individuals usually present with typical NS craniofacial features and cardiac abnormalities, with PVS and septal defects being the most recurrent findings, as presented by individual 11 of the present study. On the other hand, there is a lower prevalence of short stature and cognitive deficits compared withPTPN11-positive individuals, such as seen in patient 10 of this cohort. Ectodermal involvement includes curly hair, sparse eyebrows, hyperkeratosis pilaris, and ulerythema ophryogenes, a hallmark ofSOS1patients among NS [28].

Heterozygous variants in theLZTR1gene were found in two individuals in this cohort. The role of theLZTR1gene in RASopathies was first described in 2015 by Yamamoto et al. [28] in three familial and 2 sporadic patients presenting with NS; the gene was identified the year before as predisposing to schwannomatosis, and similar missense variants had been previously reported in two additional subjects with NS but were not considered clinically relevant [43].

In 2018, biallelic variants in theLZTR1gene were also reported to cause a recessive form of NS in 12 families [8], and the occurrence of both dominant and recessive forms of NS was confirmed the following year [9,10]. Subjects with pathogenic variants in theLZTR1gene generally show a “classical” NS phenotype, having short stature, typical facies, short/webbed neck, pectus deformity, cardiac involvement, and intellectual disability as recurrent features. The most common cardiac defects include HCM, PVS, and ASD [2].

One of the individuals in this study, patient 12 (shown inFig. 4), presented with the likely pathogenic variant c.850C>T p.Arg284Cys in theLZTR1gene and the c.3347-1G>A p.? variant classified as a VUS in theSOS1gene; the first has been previously reported [26–28], and the second one is registered several times in the ClinVar database as Nr. 40726, including individuals with NS phenotype. Patient 12 was first seen in the first year of life due to a massive cystic hygroma associated with PVS, typical facial features, and unilateral cryptorchidism. Clinical follow-up revealed normal growth and cognitive development. No segregation analysis was possible for this family, but his parents were clinically unaffected.

Compound heterozygous variants in two different genes of the RASopathies pathway have been previously reported, such as patient 1 from Longoni et al. [44] with variants inSOS1andRAF1. As in the present case, in their patient, the c.1490G>A p.(Arg497Gln) inherited variant inSOS1is currently also classified as a VUS, while the c.781C>T p.(Pro261Ser) de novo variant in theRAF1is classified as pathogenic. Brasil et al. [45] also identified one individual presenting with compound heterozygosity for the pathogenic variants c.922A>G p.Asn308Asp inPTPN11and c.1654A>G p.(Arg552Gly) inSOS1. The boy had typical clinical features like the ones with NS and variants in only one gene, and a more severe or atypical phenotype was not observed, suggesting that these mutations do not exhibit an additive effect.

However, more recently, Ferrari et al. [46] reported 2 patients with co-inherited variants inLRP1andLZTR1orLRP1andSOS1genes, besides another patient who carried a compound heterozygote ofRASAL3gene variants and anA2ML1variant. While these subclinical variants were singularly present in the healthy parents, they co-segregated in patients, suggesting their addictive effect and supporting a digenic inheritance as an alternative model to a more common monogenic transmission. Additionally, Chen et al. [47] identified two compound heterozygous likely pathogenic missense variants in theLZTR1gene in a patient presenting with NS and HCM. In this case, family screening revealed that each of the parents was the carrier of one of these variants, a brother was a heterozygote carrier for one of the variants, and a maternal half-sister was wild-type for both variants. Neither the parents nor the siblings were clinically affected.

Patient 13 was identified with the c.2033G>A p.(Arg678Gln), also classified as a VUS and registered in the ClinVar database as Nr. 1397848 three times to date, one without clinical details, another in a hereditary cancer-predisposing or cardiovascular investigation, and the third case associated with Schwannomatosis type 2. Still, no one specifies a clinical diagnosis of NS as the patient here described.

Patient 14 (shown inFig. 1) had the c.1829G>A p.(Arg610His) novel variant in theA2ML1gene, classified as a VUS. Her clinical picture is compatible with the diagnosis of NS, comprising short stature, typical craniofacial dysmorphisms, and mild intellectual deficiency. TheA2ML1gene has been implicated in malformation conditions, including NS [5,6], although the fundamental role of this gene in NS was later questioned [7].

Concerning patient 15 (also shown inFig. 1), the c.68G>T p.Gly23Val pathogenic variant was found in theMRASgene. This is the very same variant as in the work that first described the role of MRAS in individuals with NS when Higgins et al. [29] described a girl presenting with NS and concomitant HCM. Posteriorly, the same group of authors demonstrated the role of this specific variant in causing hypertrophy in induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte lines [48]. However, in 2 large cohorts of patients systematically assessed for MRAS mutations, only a single patient was identified among 288 individuals with a clinical diagnosis of RASopathy, and no mutation was identified in an unselected cohort of 1,840 subjects with a clinical diagnosis of RASopathy or with features suggestive of these disorders; these data suggest that the frequency ofMRAS-related NS is relatively rare, possibly due to a severe, often neonatal lethal phenotype. Thus, variants inMRASshould rather be considered as an exceptionally high-risk factor for the development of early-onset and severe HCM with poor prognosis in most cases, like what has been reported for mutations inRIT1,PTPN11, andRAF1(limited to specific classes) andLZTR1(limited to biallelic events) [2].

Individuals 16 and 17 were clinically diagnosed with NSML, and both presented with variants in thePTPN11gene. The variant in patient 16, c.836A>G, is one of the most frequent in this gene, exhibiting panethnicity and high inter- and intrafamilial genotype-phenotype correlation, being more typically associated with short stature, learning disabilities, craniofacial dysmorphisms, and early onset of multiple lentigines and HCM, but less chance of deafness [49–51], as seen in her case. The variant in individual 17, c.1493G>T, is less frequent than the former but also in a hot spot for NSML due to the association with multiple early-onset lentigines and HCM, being additionally a predictor of adverse cardiac outcomes [30,52,53].

Patient 18 (shown inFig. 2) presented with the recurrent c.146C>G p.Pro49Arg variant in thePPP1CBgene, seen in individuals from diverse ethnicities, suggesting a mutational hotspot for NSLAH [32,33]. The most frequent features are short stature, craniofacial dysmorphisms, scoliosis, hair abnormalities, pigmented lesions, congenital heart disease, cryptorchidism, and developmental delay, present in more than 50% of the reported individuals [33]; however, patient 18 does not present with congenital heart disease. Still, besides a unilateral retractile testicle, he was also diagnosed with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and azoospermia, which are undescribed features in this condition to date.

In patient 19 (shown inFig. 3), the c.1111T>G p.(Tyr371Asp) variant was found in theCBLgene; this is a previously reported variant and is one of the particular hot spots ofCBLmutations [3,34]. TheCBLmutation-associated syndrome is characterized by phenotypic heterogeneity and variable expressivity, but compared to the general NS population, it exhibits a lower prevalence of growth deficiency, cryptorchidism, and cardiac defects, and a higher frequency of neurological features, including psychomotor delay, cognitive impairment, and muscular hypotonia, besides increased predisposition to juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia [3]. Patient 19 has no prenatal growth deficiency but presents with postnatal tall stature, craniofacial dysmorphic features, and several neurological features, such as developmental delay, borderline intelligence with learning difficulties (total IQ of 78 according to WISC-IV testing), anxiety, hypotonia, and myopathic gait, including abnormal electroneuromyography study; WGS was negative for variants in genes associated with neuromuscular disorders. He also presented a functional megacolon at 6 months, an unreported feature in NS and NSL individuals. Until the last evaluation at the age of 13 years, he developed no malignancies.

Two patients (20 and 21) presented with an NFN phenotype and have previously reported pathogenic variants in theNF1gene. The c.1A>G p.(Met1Val) pathogenic variant in patient 20 is a recurrent variant, but none of the previous reports described NFN features [35,54,55]. On the other hand, the c.5606G>T p.(Gly1869Val) likely pathogenic variant in patient 21 was described in a boy presenting with craniofacial dysmorphisms that included proptosis, midface hypoplasia, frontal upsweep of the hair, cryptorchidism, and mild intellectual deficiency [36].

Two other patients in this cohort (22 and 23) exhibited pathogenic variants in theBRAFgene, responsible for 75% of CFCS [56]. Both variants had been reported before [37], and both patients in this series showed standard features of CFCS, such as short stature, HCM, craniofacial dysmorphisms, hair abnormalities, and developmental delay.

At least, patient 24 (shown inFig. 1) presented a pathogenic variant in theHRASgene. In a review of 81 unrelated individuals with variants inHRAS, the missense variant c.34G>A p.(Gly12Ser) was the most frequently described, presenting in nearly 80% of the cases [57–59], and the c.37G>T p.(Gly13Cys), the same present in patient 24, has also been identified in over 10 individuals with CS [38]. Besides the severe HCM with the need for septal myomectomy and infundibulectomy at age 13 years, the patient 24 started to present mood and behavioral disorders at the age of 14 years, including visual hallucinations, with a diagnosis of schizophrenia. This might be a coincidental finding since a maternal uncle has the same psychiatric disease. Still, it is an undescribed symptom in individuals with CS to date, although neuropsychiatric features such as autism spectrum disorders and attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder have been reported in children and adults with CS [60–62].

In conclusion, this case series reinforces the clinical and molecular heterogeneity of the RASopathies and presents undescribed features in this group of disorders, including myopathy and megacolon in a patient with NSL, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and azoospermia in a patient with NSLAH, and schizophrenia in a patient with CS. A novel VUS, c.1829G>A, is described in theA2ML1gene, while the c.2033G>A in theLZTR1gene and c.1A>G in theNF1gene are variants for the first time associated with features of NS. A genotype-phenotype analysis shows a partial positive correlation within patients from the present study but not when compared to cases previously described in the literature.
