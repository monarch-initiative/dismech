# Adult-Onset Dystonia-Parkinsonism: Do Not Forget SERAC1.
**Authors:** Scacciatella G, Masetti C, Yahya V, Treddenti M, Oggioni G, Bocci T, Campiglio L, Zardoni M, Rosci C, Pengo M, Manfredi C, Del Bo R, Monfrini E, Di Fonzo A, Inghilleri M, Priori A
**Journal:** Mov Disord Clin Pract (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mdc3.70051](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.70051)

## Content

Biallelic SERAC1 variants cause 3‐methylglutaconic aciduria, dystonia‐deafness, encephalopathy, and Leigh‐like syndrome (MEGDEL), a rare autosomal recessive mitochondrial disease.
1
 Generally, MEGDEL has neonatal onset and short life expectancy. Neurologically patients present hypotonia, spasticity, epilepsy, and intellectual disability. Disease course is often complicated by non‐neurological involvement (eg, hepatopathy, nephropathy).
2
 Recently, SERAC1 variants were linked to milder phenotypes with minimal systemic manifestations, ranging from juvenile‐onset complicated hereditary spastic paraparesis
3
 to early‐onset dystonia‐parkinsonism.

We aim to further widen the phenotypic spectrum of SERAC1‐related disease reporting an exceptionally mild form presenting with adult‐onset dystonia‐parkinsonism associated with a novel rare homozygous variant.

The proband is a 55‐year‐old female, born to non‐consanguineous healthy parents from Abruzzo, region of central Italy, without family history of neurological disorders. She reported unremarkable medical history until the age of 38 years, when action and rest tremor appeared in her right hand. At 41 years, tremor spread to both upper limbs with progressive bradykinesia and oromandibular dystonia. Dystonia progressively extended to limbs and neck with stiffness and painful spasms.

Over the years, the patient underwent instrumental examinations that resulted normal, including a brain MRI with angiography at 41 years, blink reflex, 123I‐Ioflupane SPECT, multiple vitamin dosage, screening for Wilson's and celiac disease, and direct sequencing for TOR1A and THAP1 genes. Levodopa, amantadine, trihexyphenidyl, tetrabenazine, alcohol test resulted ineffective.

At 53 years, the patient developed severe and progressive dysarthria and dysphagia with marked weight loss (BMI 13.33 kg/m2). At 55 years she came to our attention and was admitted for a thorough evaluation. Neurological examination showed oromandibular and cervical dystonia (jaw tremor, repetitive tongue protrusion, anterocollis, right tilt, and mild left turn), severe dysarthria, dystonic and rest tremor of upper limbs, four‐limb bradykinesia and rigidity, brisk tendon reflexes, and autonomous gait characterized by reduced arm swinging and foot dystonia, configurating generalized dystonia‐parkinsonism with a mild prevalence in left hemisome (Video 1). Neuropsychological evaluation showed normal global cognitive efficiency. Brain MRI showed subtle anterior putaminal bilateral T2‐hyperintensity (Figure S1). Brain FDG‐PET, ophthalmologic evaluation, chest‐abdomen CT, and four‐limb electroneuromyography resulted normal. Polygraphic evaluation of cervical dystonia revealed abnormal activation of right splenius capitis, right levator scapulae, and left sternocleidomastoideus muscles during left‐oriented head rotation. Blood tests displayed a mild transaminase and gamma‐glutamyl‐transferase elevation. Clozapine 25 mg/day was effective on cervical stiffness, but due to neutropenia appearance after 2 weeks, the drug was switched into olanzapine 2.5 mg/day. Dystonia slightly improved with onabotulinumtoxin A injection in oromandibular and cervical districts (Data S1). Eventually, percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy was placed and the patient reached BMI 17.90 kg/m2 in 6 months.

Neurological examination at 55 years, displaying left‐prevalent dystonia‐parkinsonism, recorded while patient was on therapy with Levodopa 800 mg/day.

Whole‐exome sequencing revealed a homozygous SERAC1 missense variant (NM_032861.4): c.1231C>T (p.Arg411Cys), affecting the lipase domain, predicted pathogenic by most in silico prediction tools (metaRNN score 0.8082, ACMG‐PP3), rare (gnomAD AF. 0000031, ACMG‐PM2), and overall classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). No other significant variants in genes associated with dystonia, parkinsonism, or 3‐methylglutaconic aciduria were identified (Data S1).

Biochemical work‐up demonstrated elevated urinary 3‐methylglutaconic acid levels. Because previous studies
8
 reported that SERAC1 mutations are often associated with 3‐MGA‐uria, the increase in our patient is likely of clinical relevance. Lactate was normal.

Among other mild SERAC1‐related phenotypes (Table 1), five patients from a large Iraqi family displayed hereditary spastic paraparesis with developmental delay at onset (2–7 years).
3
 One patient presented developmental delay and generalized hypotonia, with no movement disorder associated and onset at 3 years.
2
 Also, four patients with dystonia were reported with onset between 12 and 24 years. Most of them presented dysarthria and 3‐methylglutaconic aciduria, while other features were inconstant, including psychomotor delay, parkinsonism, brisk stretch reflexes, sensorineural hearing loss, hyperbilirubinemia, and bilateral striatal atrophy at brain MRI.
4

 had blepharospasm and orofacial dyskinesia, our patient had orobuccal, lingual and mandibular involuntary movements and was initially diagnosed as Meige Syndrome. Overall, it can be concluded that patients with SERAC1 mutation, often present oral involvement and dysarthria.

Our patient is the mildest SERAC1‐related phenotype reported to date with late onset, generalized dystonia mainly involving cranial and cervical muscles, mild parkinsonian features, and dysphagia without cognitive or systemic involvement. Regarding SERAC1‐related parkinsonism, poor levodopa response and negative 123I‐Ioflupane‐SPECT are compatible with striatal dysfunction without nigrostriatal degeneration, similarly to juvenile Huntington's disease, glutaric aciduria type 1, propionic acidemia, and dystonia caused by VAC14, PRKRA, COASY variants.
9



The review of all SERAC1‐mutated patients reported so far revealed low frequency of missense variants, suggesting that hypomorphic mutations may be associated with underdiagnosed phenotypes.
2
 All eleven patients with milder phenotypes carried two variants compatible with partially conserved SERAC1 enzymatic activity (missense, in‐frame deletion, splice‐disrupt), with a single exception.
5
 In conclusion, the wide phenotypic spectrum related to biallelic SERAC1 variants ranges from severe syndromic forms (ie, MEGDEL), mostly associated with null variants, to adult‐onset dystonia‐parkinsonism, mostly associated with hypomorphic variants. Our case further points out that SERAC1 testing should be included in diagnostic work‐up of adult‐onset dystonia‐parkinsonism. Previously reported hypomorphic SERAC1 variants are mostly classified as VUSs according to ACMG criteria.
1
,
2
 Although no correlation between neurological manifestations and 3‐methylglutaconic aciduria was documented, this suggestive biochemical finding may represent a useful tool to support the disease‐causing role of SERAC1 VUSs in dystonia.
