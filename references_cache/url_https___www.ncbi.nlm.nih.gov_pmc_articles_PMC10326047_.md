</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p><em>DFNA68</em> is a rare subtype of autosomal dominant nonsyndromic hearing impairment caused by heterozygous alterations in the <em>HOMER2</em> gene. To date, only 5 pathogenic or likely pathogenic coding variants, including two missense substitutions (c.188 C &gt; T and c.587 G &gt; C), a single base pair duplication (c.840dupC) and two short deletions (c.592_597delACCACA and c.832_836delCCTCA) have been described in 5 families. In this study, we report a novel <em>HOMER2</em> variation, identified by massively parallel sequencing, in a Sicilian family suffering from progressive dominant hearing loss over 3 generations. This novel alteration is a nonstop substitution (c.1064 A &gt; G) that converts the translational termination codon (TAG) of the gene into a tryptophan codon (TGG) and is predicted to extend the HOMER2 protein by 10 amino acids. RNA analyses from the proband suggested that <em>HOMER2</em> transcripts carrying the nonstop variant escaped the non-stop decay pathway. Finally, in vivo studies using a zebrafish animal model and behavioral tests clearly established the deleterious impact of this novel <em>HOMER2</em> alteration on hearing function. This study identifies the fourth causal variation responsible for <em>DFNA68</em> and describes a simple in vivo approach to assess the pathogenicity of candidate <em>HOMER2</em> variants.</p>
<section><p><strong>Subject terms:</strong> Genetic testing, Diseases, Medical genetics</p></section></section><section><h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>Approximately 20–30% of hereditary nonsyndromic hearing loss (NSHL) cases diagnosed in infancy and early childhood present an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern (ADNSHL) [<a>1</a>]. For the vast majority of these cases, the HL is described as postlingual, bilateral and progressive. To date at least 51 causal genes have been identified (Hereditary Hearing Loss Homepage; <a>https://hereditaryhearingloss.org/</a>; accessed in May, 2022) and one of them is <em>HOMER2</em> (OMIM*604799).</p>
<p>The <em>HOMER2</em> gene is located on chromosome 15, spans 103.7 kb (UCSC Genome browser; <a>http://genome.ucsc.edu/</a>; accessed in May, 2022), and encodes several isoforms of the HOMER homolog 2 protein, including two long isoforms (HOMER2a: 343 aa, <a>NP_004830.2</a> and HOMER2b: 354 aa, <a>NP_955362.1</a>) due to alternative splicing (<a>NM_004839.4</a> and NM_199330.2, respectively). These HOMER2 isoforms belongs to the HOMER family of scaffolded proteins (encoded as short and long isoforms by alternative splicing of the <em>HOMER1</em>, <em>HOMER2</em> and <em>HOMER3</em> genes), which are involved in Ca2+ homeostasis, cytoskeletal organization and synaptic plasticity (for review see [<a>2</a>]). The long HOMER proteins are all characterized by the presence of two main domains: (i) a conserved N-terminal Enabled Vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein Homology 1 (EVH1) domain that interacts with proline-rich sequences, (ii) a C-terminal domain, comprising a coiled-coiled region with a CDC42-binding domain and two leucine zipper motifs, responsible for multimerization between HOMER family members and interaction with the CDC42 small GTPase [<a>3</a>].</p>
<p>Azaiez et al. [<a>4</a>] were the first to establish the causality of a heterozygous missense <em>HOMER2</em> variation, NM_199330.2:c.587 G &gt; C, p.(Arg196Pro) (published as <a>NM_004839.4</a>:c.554 G &gt; C, p.(Arg185Pro)) in a dominantly inherited form of NSHL (<em>DFNA68</em>). Thereafter, three other <em>HOMER2</em> pathogenic heterozygous variants were identified in three families suffering from ADNSHL: the NM_199330.2:c.840dupC, p.(Met281HisfsTer9) variant (published as c.840_841insC) in a large Chinese family [<a>5</a>], the NM_199330.2:c.592_597delACCACA, p.(Thr198_Thr199del) variant in an Italian family [<a>6</a>], and the NM_199330.2:c.832_836delCCTCA, p.(Pro278AlafsTer10) variant in a Spanish family [<a>7</a>]. Another variant (NM_199330.2:c.188 C &gt; T, p.(Pro63Leu)) reported in an Iranian family with dominant deafness was also described [<a>8</a>] and assumed as likely pathogenic by the authors although the variation was of uncertain significance when applying the ACMG criteria.</p>
<p>In this article, we have identified a new <em>HOMER2</em> variant segregating in a large Sicilian family suffering from ADNSHL. This alteration, which was not reported in databases, consists of a nonstop substitution, a very rare variant class. RNA analysis from the proband’s peripheral blood sample demonstrated that it results in the production of abnormal RNA transcripts with a 3′ coding sequence extension. Finally, its deleterious effect on hearing was established using zebrafish behavioral tests. With this study we expanded the genotype spectrum associated with <em>DFNA68</em> and developed a simple zebrafish experiment to test future candidate pathogenic <em>HOMER2</em> variants.</p></section><section><h2>Materials and methods</h2>
<section><h3>Patients</h3>
<p>A forty-eight-year-old female (index case) and her fifteen-year-old daughter, both suffering from progressive bilateral nonsyndromic hearing loss, were referred to our laboratory for genetic analyses. For each individual, pure tone audiometry, in a frequency range of 250-8000 Hz, was performed to evaluate the severity of their hearing loss in accordance with the recommendations of the International Bureau for Audiophonology (<a>https://www.biap.org/en/recommandations/recommendations/tc-02-classification/213-rec-02-1-en-audiometric-classification-of-hearing-impairments/file</a>). In addition, a family history questionnaire was completed by the clinicians. Informed consent for genetic analyses was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives. Approval was obtained by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of CHU de Montpellier: (2018_IRB-MTP_05-05 obtained on the 15th June 2018).</p></section><section><h3>Sequencing and validation</h3>
<p>DNA from each participant was isolated from peripheral blood samples using standard procedures. Massively parallel sequencing (MPS) was performed with a hearing loss gene panel on an Illumina® MiniSeq sequencer (Illumina,San Diego, CA, USA). The workflow used to identify candidate pathogenic variations has been already described [<a>9</a>] and the 84 screened genes are listed in Supplementary Table <a>1</a>. This approach included a copy-number variant analysis using the MobiCNV algorithm (<a>https://github.com/mobidic/MobiCNV</a>) and a direct visualization of the sequenced reads with the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) software (v2.7.2) [<a>10</a>]. Whole exome sequencing (WES) libraries were prepared using the KAPA® HyperPlus kit (Roche, Madison, WI, USA) according to the KAPA® HyperCap v3.0 workflow. Whole exome enrichment was then performed using the KAPA® HyperExome probes (~43 Mb capture target size) and an Illumina® NextSeq 500 instrument (Illumina) was used for sequencing. Samples were analyzed using an in-house pipeline (MobiDL, <a>https://github.com/mobidic/MobiDL</a>), which performs the secondary and tertiary analyses, and uses BWA [<a>11</a>] as aligner and both GATK HaplotypeCaller [<a>12</a>] and Google DeepVariant [<a>13</a>] as variant callers. Furthermore, copy-number variation (CNV) was assessed using the GATK4 CNV calling module and a pipeline (<a>https://gitlab.univ-nantes.fr/benjamin.co/sv-exome/-/tree/add_vcf_support</a>) based on the GATK recommendations. Variant filtering was based on: (i) a dominant mode of inheritance, (ii) a genotype/phenotype correlation and (iii) a minor allele frequency (MAF) threshold of 0.1%.</p>
<p>Sanger sequencing or MPS were performed in order to validate identified variants and build a comprehensive familial genetic profile. Primer sequences used for PCR-Sanger sequencing are listed in Supplementary Table <a>2</a>.</p></section><section><h3>In silico analysis and data availability</h3>
<p>The reference sequences used in this study were NM_199330.2, NM_153676.3 and NM_194248.2. for the <em>HOMER2</em>, <em>USH1C</em> and <em>OTOF</em> genes, respectively. Functional consequences of variants of interest were evaluated using the online variant interpretation platform Mobidetails [<a>14</a>] (<a>https://mobidetails-iurc-montp-inserm.fr/MD/</a>; accessed in March, 2023). The Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD® Professional 2020.3; <a>https://portal.biobase-international.com</a>) and the Deafness Variation Database (DVD) (<a>https://deafnessvariationdatabase.org/</a>) were also consulted (accessed in March, 2023). The Human Genome Variation Society recommendations [<a>15</a>] v20.05 (<a>http://varnomen.hgvs.org/</a>) were followed for the description of the variants.</p>
<p>The newly identified variant was classified using the Intervar web server (<a>http://wintervar.wglab.org/</a>) in accordance with the adapted ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation in the context of hearing loss [<a>16</a>] and deposited in the Leiden Open Variation Database Global Variome Shared Instance (LOVD GVShared, individuals #00411547 to #00411552).</p></section><section><h3>cDNA analysis of <em>HOMER2</em>
</h3>
<p>Total RNA from the proband and a control was isolated from peripheral blood samples collected in PAXgene<sup>TM</sup> Blood RNA Tubes using the Nucleo Spin® RNA II isolation kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany). RNA quality and concentration were estimated with a 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). RNA samples were used as templates for cDNA synthesis using the SuperScript<sup>TM</sup> III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Cergy-Pontoise, France) and PCR amplifications were performed with <em>HOMER2</em>-specific primers (Supplementary Table <a>2</a>). PCR products were size-separated on agarose gel and Sanger sequenced.</p></section><section><h3>Zebrafish husbandry</h3>
<p>This study was conducted in accordance with the recommendations of INSERM, Montpellier University and the European Convention for the Protection of Animals used for Experimental and Scientific Purposes. The zebrafish Tg(pou4f3:gap43-GFP)s356t transgenic line, re-defined later as <em>Brn3c:mGFP</em> [<a>17</a>], was maintained according to recommended Federation of European Laboratory Animal Science Associations (FELASA) procedures on a 14:10 h light/dark photoperiod at 28 °C [<a>18</a>]. Embryos were obtained from natural spawning and incubated in a water tank at 28 °C. Embryos and larvae were staged by days post-fertilization (dpf).</p></section><section><h3>mRNA synthesis and injection</h3>
<p>The pShuttle<sup>TM</sup> Gateway® PLUS ORF clone for <em>HOMER2</em> (GC-H5096) and its mutated version (<em>HOMER2</em>, c.1064 A &gt; G, 30 bp 5′-GGCTGGCCGAGGCCCAGGCCCCGCCCGTGA-3′ extension) were purchased from GeneCopoeia<sup>TM</sup> (GeneCopoeia, Rockville, MD, USA). The ORF sequences were subcloned into the expression vector pCS2 + (gift from Marc Kirschner (Addgene plasmid # 17095; <a>http://n2t.net/addgene:17095</a>; RRID: Addgene_17095)) using the Gateway<sup>TM</sup> LR Clonase<sup>TM</sup> II Enzyme Mix (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The pCS2+ mCherry expression vector [<a>19</a>] (gift from Georges Lutfalla; DIMNP, CNRS, University of Montpellier, Montpellier, France) was also used.</p>
<p>Transcription was carried out using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE<sup>TM</sup> T7 or SP6 transcription kit (Invitrogen) for <em>HOMER2</em> wild type, <em>HOMER2</em> mutated or mCherry RNA synthesis, respectively. RNA quality and concentration were assessed by electrophoresis and spectrophotometry (NanoDrop One/One<sup>C</sup>, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A dilution range was performed to determine the optimal mRNA quantity that allowed a good ratio of toxicity/phenotype analysis. Therefore, 80 pg of <em>HOMER2</em> RNA (wild type sequence: A1064-RNA or mutated sequence: G1064-RNA) were co-injected with 50 pg of mCherry RNA, 1 nL at the one- to two-cell stage. Twenty-four hours after microinjection, mCherry fluorescence was used as a quality control of injection and translation; all negative embryos were discarded.</p></section><section><h3>Acoustic startle response (ASR) test</h3>
<p>At 5 dpf, zebrafish larvae with normal morphology were transferred into a 96-well plate with 300 μl of water per well and placed into a Zebrabox® (ViewPoint, Lissieu, France). In the device, larvae in the plate were isolated from environmental surrounding noise. After a 30 min period of adaptation, larvae were submitted to 3 noise stimulations (70 dB, 400 Hz, 1 s per stimulus, 5 min apart). The study was conducted in light condition and zebrafish activity was quantified using the quantization mode of Zebralab® software (ViewPoint), as previously described [<a>20</a>]. The quantity of movements during the entire experiment was measured for each larva. Baseline activity levels were subtracted from the activity levels during the sound stimulations (10 s pre-stimulus period before each stimulation) in order to normalize the values. The activity for the 3 stimuli were pooled into 1 s time to assess ASR.</p></section><section><h3>Visual motor response (VMR) assay</h3>
<p>Following the ASR test, each 96-well plate was then used to assess the locomotor activity of the larvae in response to visual stimuli. This test quantifies the activity of zebrafish larvae in response to light changes using infrared tracking system. The VMR protocol consisted of a 30 min dark-adaptation period followed by two periods, each composed of 10 min of brightness (100% of light intensity) and 10 min of darkness, the duration of the whole protocol was 70 min. Zebrafish activity was quantified with the Zebralab® software and data were analyzed using 1 min time bins to assess the VMR.</p></section><section><h3>Statistical analyses</h3>
<p>Statistical analyses were performed using Prism<sup>TM</sup> v8.3 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). A chi-square contingency test was performed to analyze the ASR results. Other data were analyzed with non-parametric Mann–Whitney test and were represented as mean ± SEM. Asterisks indicate statistical significance: *<em>p</em> value &lt; 0.05, **<em>p</em> value &lt; 0.01, ***<em>p</em> value &lt; 0.001 and ****<em>p</em> value &lt; 0.0001.</p></section></section><section><h2>Results</h2>
<section><h3>Clinical findings</h3>
<p>The proband of this study (III.5) was a 48-year-old female suffering from progressive sensorineural nonsyndromic hearing loss (NSHL). The clinical diagnosis of its deafness was made at the age of 7 years, but an earlier onset was presumed by the patient in the light of her educational difficulties. At the age of 28 years, audiometric assessment revealed a bilateral downward-sloping severe 1st degree HL according to the BIAP recommendations (Fig. <a>1a</a>). When she was 47 years old, she was fitted with hearing aids. Her daughter (IV.5) was diagnosed at the age of 3 years and an air conduction test performed at age 13 years showed a bilateral downward-sloping mild 1st degree HL (Fig. <a>1a</a>). The family anamnesis revealed 6 additional affected members over 4 generations (I.1, II.3, II.4, III.2, III.6 and IV), consistent with an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of the pathology (Fig. <a>1b</a>).</p>

<p>Following our laboratory diagnostic approach, massive parallel sequencing (MPS) analysis using a panel of 84 deafness-causing genes was firstly performed in the proband (III.5) to identify candidate pathogenic variants. This screening strategy highlighted the presence of a <em>HOMER2</em> A &gt; G transition (c.1064 A &gt; G) in the heterozygous state (Fig. <a>2</a>). This variant occurred within the translational termination (stop) codon (TAG) of the gene and changed it to a tryptophan codon (TGG). According to the <em>HOMER2</em> human reference genome sequence, a downstream in-frame stop codon was detected, leading to a predicted 10 amino acids C-terminal extension of the protein (Fig. <a>2</a>). This substitution was absent from all the interrogated databases and was considered as a variant of uncertain significance according to the ACMG/AMP hearing loss guidelines. Sanger sequencing validated the genotype of the proband and revealed that her affected daughter was also a heterozygous carrier (Fig. <a>1b</a>).</p>

The 10 amino-acid residues extension of the expected protein is also shown.</p></figcaption></figure><p>In addition, MPS data analysis highlighted two other variants, both in the heterozygous state, in two genes implicated in hearing loss (Supplementary Table S<a>3</a>). The first one was a previously described pathogenic variation (c.778 G &gt; T, p.(Glu260Ter)) in <em>USH1C</em> [<a>21</a>], a gene responsible for both nonsyndromic (OMIM#602092) and syndromic (OMIM#276904) recessive deafness. The second one was an intronic transversion (c.327 + 5 G &gt; T) in <em>OTOF</em>, a gene associated with recessive NSHL (OMIM#601071). This last substitution was present in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v2.1.1) with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.13% in European (Finnish) and was referred in ClinVar (Variation ID: 335462) as a variant of uncertain significance or a likely benign variation. Splicing predictions obtained with the MaxEntScan and SPiP tools were in favor of a deleterious effect of the variant via an alteration of the donor consensus splice site of exon 4. By contrast, the deep neural network SpliceAI did not predict this adverse event but indicated a moderate increase in the probability that a cryptic donor splice site, located 48-bp upstream of the natural donor splice site, could be used. These two <em>USH1C</em> and <em>OTOF</em> variants were validated by Sanger sequencing in the proband. The <em>OTOF</em> variation was also present in the proband’s daughter (Fig. <a>1b</a>).</p>
<p>In order to exclude the implication of a non-explored DFNA gene in the familial hearing loss, whole exome sequencing (WES) was then carried out with DNA samples from four affected (II.4, III.5, IV.1 and IV.5) and one unaffected (III.4) individuals. After filtering for MAF, genotype/phenotype correlation and a dominant mode of inheritance, the only selected variant was the <em>HOMER2</em> nonstop substitution. Further segregation of this variant in the family showed it was only detected in the affected members (Fig. <a>1b</a>).</p>
<p>As the <em>USH1C</em> and <em>OTOF</em> variants identified in the proband could be important for the other family members in a context of genetic counselling, a segregation analysis was additionally performed with Sanger sequencing or MPS. Unexpectedly, this analysis highlighted the presence of another well-known <em>USH1C</em> pathogenic variant (c.496 + 1 G &gt; A; ClinVar variation ID: 371732) in the unaffected IV.2 and IV.3 individuals, presumably inherited from their father III.1 (Fig. <a>1b</a> and Supplementary Table S<a>3</a>).</p>
<p>Finally, the consequence of the c.1064 A &gt; G <em>HOMER2</em> variation at the RNA level was assessed by RT-PCR study on total RNA extracted from proband’s blood sample. Sanger sequencing revealed that <em>HOMER2</em> transcripts carrying the c.1064 A &gt; G were present and not subject to a major degradation by the non-stop decay pathway (Fig. <a>3</a>).</p>

<p>Single-nucleotide pathogenic substitutions that change stop codons into sense codons, termed nonstop variants (also known as no-stop, stop-loss or readthrough variations), are an extremely rare type of alteration (387 nonstop variants among 278,993 single pathogenic nucleotide substitutions reported in the HGMD database (accessed in March, 2023)). In this study we identified a <em>HOMER2</em> nonstop variation segregating with the hearing loss (HL) phenotype described in a large Sicilian family. This result was obtained by massively parallel sequencing of a HL gene panel, confirmed by a whole exome sequencing analysis and validated by Sanger sequencing.</p>
<p>To date only four pathogenic <em>HOMER2</em> variants have been clearly associated with <em>DFNA68</em>, a missense and a two small deletions in three European families [<a>4</a>, <a>6</a>, <a>7</a>] and a single nucleotide duplication in a Chinese family [<a>5</a>]. In these families, all affected individuals suffering from bilateral progressive nonsyndromic hearing loss (NSHL) affecting preferentially the high frequencies with an onset (when indicated) in the first decade of life (7–9 years old), which is consistent with the clinical data collected from the family described herein. It was reported that the severity of the hearing impairment could be variant-dependent [<a>5</a>, <a>7</a>], but due to the limited number of cases a fine genotype/phenotype correlation remains difficult to establish. Concerning the fifth reported variant, c.188 C &gt; T, no family history or clinical data was reported [<a>8</a>].</p>
<p>Nonstop pathogenic alterations can lead to: (i) mRNA degradation due to activation of the non-stop decay (NSD) pathway in response to the presence of stalled or collided ribosomes [<a>22</a>], (ii) degradation of the mutated protein by protein quality control mechanisms [<a>23</a>, <a>24</a>] or (iii) functional alteration of the mutated protein [<a>25</a>, <a>26</a>]. As results obtained from studies on animal models [<a>4</a>] strongly suggest that the pathogenicity of <em>HOMER2</em> variants implicated in <em>DFNA68</em> is not due to haploinsufficiency, a massive <em>HOMER2</em> mRNA degradation by NSD linked to the nonstop variant appeared unlikely. To test this hypothesis, RT-PCR analyses were performed and confirmed that transcripts carrying the <em>HOMER2</em> nonstop variation were not a major target of the NSD system. This result was in accordance with data obtained from a meta-analysis [<a>27</a>], which suggested that alternative stop codons in close proximity to the mutated stop codons (0–49 nucleotides downstream) were not subject to NSD.</p>
<p>In order to validate the pathogenicity of the c.1064 A &gt; G <em>HOMER2</em> substitution, we then performed in vivo behavioral tests on zebrafish larvae injected with wild type or mutated <em>HOMER2</em> RNA. The zebrafish model has been already used by Azaiez et al. [<a>4</a>] to study the impact of the c.587 G &gt; C <em>HOMER2</em> variant. In this previous publication, the effect of the variant was determined by measurements of the otic capsule area in 3 dpf larvae while in vivo behavioral tests were conducted in mice. In our study, we performed the behavioral assays on zebrafish larvae at 5 dpf, using the ASR and VMR conventional tests, which is a much more rapid and straightforward model as we were able to directly compare wild type and variant phenotypes. The ASR test revealed a clear decrease in larval activity when injected with the mutated <em>HOMER2</em> RNA, whereas the VMR assay did not revealed any change in their locomotor capacity, highlighting a hearing impairment. This result was also validated by a western blot experiment detecting human HOMER2 proteins in injected embryos and a qRT-PCR study of the <em>HOMER2</em> RNA that confirmed the integrity of the injected RNAs in larvae at 3dpf. In order to compare our results to those obtained by Azaiez et al. [<a>4</a>], we also measured the ear diameters of 3 dpf larvae injected with wild type or mutated <em>HOMER2</em> RNAs, and showed that contrary to the c.587 G &gt; C variant, the nonstop alteration did not impair the morphology of the ears.</p>
<p>Taking into consideration all these results, and according to ACMG/AMP guidelines, we classified the c.1064 A &gt; G <em>HOMER2</em> transition as a class V pathogenic variant.</p>
<p>The exact molecular mechanism involved in the pathogenicity of the <em>HOMER2</em> nonstop variant has not been determined. A dominant-negative effect on the wild type protein or a gain-of-function have been discussed for the previously identified pathogenic alterations. In the case of the nonstop variant, the 10 amino-acids protein extension could inhibit the multimerization process, which occurs via the C-terminal domains of the proteins or could compete for other partner proteins resulting in a dominant-negative effect.</p>
<p>In summary, in this article, we identified a new <em>HOMER2</em> variation associated with progressive dominant NSHL. This variant consisting of a nonstop substitution, a very rare type of pathogenic variation, expands the mutational spectrum of the <em>HOMER2</em> gene. Identification of this pathogenic variant and 3 other alterations in 2 deafness-causing genes will have important consequences for the proband’s family concerning medical care and genetic counseling. Furthermore, this study highlights the major importance of developing functional tests to unambiguously characterize the pathogenicity of a variants of uncertain significance. Finally, we believe that using zebrafish animal model and simple behavioral assays, such as ASR and VMR, should be considered and encouraged in a molecular diagnostic strategy when necessary.</p></section><section><h2>Supplementary information</h2>
