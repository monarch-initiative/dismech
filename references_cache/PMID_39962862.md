# Amitriptyline use in individuals with KCNQ2/3 gain-of-function variants: A retrospective cohort study.
**Authors:** De Wachter M, Millevert C, Nicolai J, Cats E, Kluger G, Milh M, Cloarec R, Syrbe S, Arts K, Jansen K, Krygier M, Smigiel R, Auvin S, Olofson K, Gjerulfsen CE, Ceulemans B, Møller RS, Bayat A, Weckhuysen S
**Journal:** Epilepsia (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/epi.18310](https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.18310)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Heterozygous gain-of-function (GOF) variants in KCNQ2 and KCNQ3, encoding the voltage-gated potassium channel subunits Kv7.2 and Kv7.3, lead to neurodevelopmental disorders for which no established treatments are available. Amitriptyline, an antidepressant, blocks Kv7.2/Kv7.3 and has previously been reported to be effective in a single individual with a KCNQ2 GOF variant. We designed a retrospective, single-arm, multicenter study to investigate the effects of amitriptyline in a real-world setting.

METHODS: We used a 7-point Likert scale to measure seizure frequency, clinical examination, motor function, alertness, skill acquisition, communication, mood, behavior, self-care, sleep, tiredness, and electroencephalogram at baseline, after a minimum of 6 weeks of intervention, and, if applicable, after discontinuation. Adverse events were assessed in all participants, and the effectiveness of the treatment was evaluated in 11 individuals who received a minimum dosage of .5 mg/kg/day for at least 6 weeks. Data were collected from October 2023 to August 2024.

RESULTS: Thirteen individuals, eight with a pathogenic KCNQ2 GOF variant and five with a pathogenic KCNQ3 GOF variant, were included. Nine were female, and the median age at start of amitriptyline was 7.1 years (range = 1.5-20 years). Eleven individuals received a minimum dosage of .5 mg/kg/day for at least 6 weeks. The median dosage of amitriptyline administered was 1 mg/kg/day, with a median treatment duration of 29 weeks. Although amitriptyline was ineffective in two individuals (18%), eight (72%) demonstrated at least minimal improvement in two or more domains, with improvements in alertness and communication being the most frequently reported. In those with reported improvements, amitriptyline was discontinued in four individuals, but continued improvements were seen, to the same or greater extent compared to treatment. The remaining five individuals are on continued treatment because of perceived benefits.

SIGNIFICANCE: Overall, the effect of amitriptyline remains unclear, and formal n-of-1 trials are needed to investigate the precise effects of amitriptyline in KCNQ GOF-related neurodevelopmental disorders.

Key points AMT has Kv7.2/Kv7.3 blocking properties and has shown promise in a single individual with a KCNQ2 GOF variant. AMT appears to be a safe treatment option for KCNQ2/3 GOF‐related neurodevelopmental disorders, even in young children. Improvement in multiple domains, particularly alertness and communication, was observed in 72% of individuals with KCNQ GOF‐related neurodevelopmental disorders. Continued improvement in those who discontinued AMT highlights the need for cautious interpretation of its effect. Formal n‐of‐1 trials are essential to determine the precise impact of AMT in this population.

KCNQ‐related developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs) are a group of severe neurodevelopmental disorders caused by heterozygous de novo pathogenic variants in KCNQ2 or KCNQ3. 1 KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 encode the voltage‐gated potassium channel subunits Kv7.2 and Kv7.3, respectively, which are widely expressed in the central nervous system. The channel subunits assemble into homo‐ or heteromeric channels that mediate a subthreshold potassium current, called the M‐current. This M‐current plays a major role in controlling neuronal excitability by opposing membrane depolarization. 2

Whereas KCNQ2 loss‐of‐function (LOF) variants are most common and lead to a spectrum of syndromes characterized by neonatal seizures, 3 KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 gain‐of‐function (GOF) variants are rare and distinguish themselves by the absence of neonatal seizures. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Strong GOF KCNQ2 variants are associated with symptoms ranging from neonatal encephalopathy with nonepileptic myoclonia to neurodevelopmental delay with infantile onset epileptic spasms or childhood onset epilepsy. 6 Mild GOF KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 variants generally lead to neurodevelopmental delay and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) without seizures. 5 , 7 Children with milder GOF phenotypes often have a strong activation of epileptic activity during sleep on electroencephalogram (EEG). 5 , 6 The functional effect of many KCNQ2/3 variants has been experimentally assessed, 4 , 6 and reported pathogenic KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 variants, along with their functional consequences, are summarized in a publicly accessible database (www.rikee.org). 8 Knowledge of the functional effect of a variant is crucial for the selection of appropriate treatment options, as individuals with KCNQ2/3 GOF versus LOF variants might require different treatment approaches. 1

Punke and Friederich 9 have shown a reversible and concentration‐dependent inhibition of Kv7.2/7.3 channels by amitriptyline (AMT). AMT is a tricyclic antidepressant with analgesic properties due to the reuptake inhibition of serotonin and norepinephrine in combination with alterations of voltage‐gated ion channels and various receptors. 9 It is a commonly used drug to treat conditions such as depression, obsessive–compulsive disorder, nocturnal enuresis, and neuropathic or functional pain, including in children. 10 , 11 In a heterologous in vitro cell system, AMT has been shown to reverse the mutational effect of pathogenic KCNQ2 GOF variants on the M‐current at clinically relevant concentrations. 5 Recently, the first results of the use of AMT in one individual carrying a mild GOF KCNQ2 variant were published. 12 The authors reported improvements in sensory profile, level of adaptive behavioral functioning, communication, and social and motor skills. In this study, we retrospectively assessed the clinical response and safety profile of off‐label use of AMT in individuals with KCNQ2/3 GOF variants.

Individuals were recruited through international collaborating (child) neurologists, KCNQ2 patient organizations, and the NETRE network. 13 Data were collected from October 2023 to August 2024. Inclusion criteria were carriership of a known pathogenic heterozygous GOF KCNQ2/3 variant; use of AMT as a form of precision therapy for the disorder; and availability of data on the clinical condition before, during, and if relevant, after the intervention. Given the retrospective nature of the study, treatment protocols were not standardized but were dependent on the treating physician's clinical judgment. Clinicians provided a written clinical summary and completed a worksheet focusing on the individual's clinical presentation at baseline, during, and, if relevant, after treatment with AMT. If the clinician was unable to provide sufficient information, details were obtained from online interviews with the parents. The worksheet included information on gene variant, AMT dosage scheme, baseline clinical examination, EEG, seizure types and frequency, antiseizure medication (ASM), neuropsychological testing, degree of developmental delay (DD) or intellectual disability (ID), school performance, communication skills, level of self‐care, mobility, gait, mood, behavior, sleep, and general functioning. Changes in concomitant medication or other environmental factors were specifically requested to be documented. Results of standardized questionnaires or test batteries (if performed following instructions of the treating physician), laboratory results, and electrocardiographic (ECG) results were collected. ID was defined according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)‐4 and DSM‐5, using formal intelligence quotient tests when available (normal: ≥85, borderline: 70–84, mild: 55–69, moderate: 40–54, severe or profound: <39). 14 , 15 If these data were not available, classification was based on clinical records and the level of support in daily life required.

For the assessment of AMT effectiveness, only individuals who used AMT for a minimum of 6 weeks at a minimum dose of .5 mg/kg/day were included. Changes in different domains (seizure frequency, clinical examination, motor function, alertness, skill acquisition [ability of learning new skills or improved use of these skills in daily life], communication, mood, behavior, self‐care, sleep, tiredness, and EEG) were retrospectively scored based on clinical information provided by the clinicians (or the parents if the clinicians were not available), using a 7‐point Likert scale (very much worse, much worse, minimally worse, no change, minimally improved, much improved, and very much improved), as used in the Clinical Global Impression of Change scale and the Caregiver Global Impression of Change scale. 16 These scores were assigned for two different timepoints: (1) the “change during the intervention” scores assessed the changes between last evaluation before start of AMT and last evaluation on AMT if the individual was still continuing treatment, or last evaluation before stopping AMT; and (2) the “change after discontinuation” score assessed the changes between last evaluation before stopping AMT and first evaluation after stopping AMT (minimally 3 months after stop). We used the term adverse events to define any untoward medical occurrence that does not necessarily have a causal relationship with AMT treatment, as routinely done in clinical trials. 17

AMT and nortriptyline levels in blood were analyzed using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry.

All data collected were treated in accordance with the Directive on the Protection of Individuals With Regard to the Processing of Personal Data and the applicable national legislation, as required by the European General Data Protection Regulation (General Data Protection Regulation [AVG/GDPR]–EU2016/679) and the Belgian legislation elaborating on this regulation. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Antwerp University Hospital on November 21, 2022 (Project ID 3867‐EDGE 002766). Site‐specific informed consent was obtained from the caregivers.

This study was designed according to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, as outlined in Checklist Version 4: Cohort Studies. 18

Descriptive statistics were computed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 29. Variables were tested for normal distribution using Shapiro–Wilk test. Nonnormally distributed data were reported as medians with their respective ranges. Fisher exact test of independence was performed to compare treatment response for KCNQ2 versus KCNQ3 variants. Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to assess correlation between treatment response and AMT dosage or treatment duration.

Thirteen individuals with KCNQ2/3 GOF variants, of whom nine were female, were eligible for inclusion in the study (Table 1, Figure 1). The median age at inclusion was 7.8 years, ranging from 1.8 years to 23 years. Clinical and genetic information on five individuals has previously been reported (I1, I3, I4, I5, and I7). 5 , 6 , 12

Eight individuals (I1–I8) carried six different pathogenic variants in KCNQ2; p.(Tyr141Asn), p.(Arg144Trp), p.(Arg144Gln), p.(Arg198Gln), p.(Arg201His), and p.(Arg201Cys). Five individuals (I9–I13) carried three different pathogenic variants in KCNQ3: p.(Arg227Gln), p.(Arg230His), and p.(Arg230Cys).

At baseline, all individuals had DD, noticed from infancy, and had delayed language development. Behavior was challenging in six of 13 (46%). ASD features were observed in five of 13 (38%) and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder features in four of 13 (31%). Sleep problems were present in three of 13 (23%). All were reported to be highly dependent on others for their daily life activities. Twelve of 13 (92%) had never experienced any seizures, and 11 of 13 (85%) were not taking any ASMs at baseline. EEGs were recorded in all individuals at baseline and showed an abnormal slow background for age in six of 13 (46%) individuals, and (multi‐)focal epileptic discharges in nine of 13 (70%). In all but one, sleep EEG was recorded, and seven of 13 (54%) had activation of epileptic activity during sleep. Brain magnetic resonance imaging was normal in 10 of 13 (77%) individuals. In I7 and I10, cerebral atrophy and delayed myelination were seen, and in I13, an increased signal in the central tegmental pathways and discrete cerebellar atrophy were noticed.

AMT was started at a median age of 7.3 years (range = 1.5–21 years) and a median weight of 21 kg (range = 9–36 kg). Each individual followed a different treatment regimen as decided by their treating physician. The median starting dose was .3 mg/kg/day (range = .1–.7 mg/kg/day). The median maximum dose was 22 mg/day or 1.0 mg/kg/day (range = .5–3.0 mg/kg/day). The median titration period to reach .5 mg/kg/day was 3 weeks (range = 0–17 weeks). In I9, the dose was reduced from .96 to .7 mg/kg/day after 3 months due to mood swings and behavior problems. In I3 and I7, treatment was discontinued early due to adverse events (see below) after a few days and 5 weeks, respectively. The dosing schemes are summarized in Table 2.

The serum concentration of AMT was measured in one individual (I13) and was below the reported therapeutic range (nortriptyline 44 ng/mL [reference = 70–170], AMT 32.0 ng/mL [reference = 80–200]) at a dosage of 3 mg/kg/day.

Two individuals were excluded from further effectiveness analysis because of a treatment duration of less than 6 weeks (I3 and I7). The clinical effects of AMT in the remaining 11 individuals were evaluated after a medium treatment duration of 29 weeks (range = 8–86 weeks). Median age at evaluation was 7.4 years (range = 1.8–21 years). Change scores on the Likert scale were based on clinical information provided by the clinician in all individuals except I2 and I4, for whom change scores were provided by the parents. Table 3 gives an overview of the change scores on the Likert scale for all 11 individuals. A summary of the descriptions of the observed positive and negative clinical changes can be found in Table 4. I9, I10, I11, I12, and I13 underwent standardized evaluations using different sets of scales, and the results of these evaluations can be found in Table S1. An overview of the EEG changes can be found in Table S2.

Positive effects in at least two domains were observed in eight of 11 (72%) individuals (I1, I2, I4, I8, I9, I11, I12, and I13). The most common reported positive change was an improvement in alertness (I1, I2, I4, I6, I8, I11, I12, and I13) in eight individuals. Communication improved in seven individuals (I1, I2, I4, I8, I9, I11, and I12). Skill acquisition (I1, I4, I8, I11, and I12) and self‐care (I2, I4, I9, I12, and I13) improved in five individuals. Behavior improvements were seen in three (I11, I12, and I13) and in combination with improvements in mood in another two. Motor abilities improved in four individuals (I1, I4, I8, and I13). None of the individuals developed seizures during AMT treatment. EEG was only recorded in four individuals (I1, I8, I12, and I13); it improved in I8 and did not change in I12 and I13.

There were no positive effects reported in two individuals (I5 and I10). There was no difference in treatment response in individuals with a KCNQ2 variant versus individuals with a KCNQ3 variant (5/6 vs. 4/5, Fisher exact test, p = .73). Treatment groups did not differ in AMT dosage used (Mann–Whitney U, p = .72) nor in treatment duration (Mann–Whitney U, p = .48).

I3 and I7 were excluded from the evaluation of effectiveness because of early discontinuation of AMT. I3 experienced recurrent vasovagal syncopes immediately after initiation of AMT. Blood pressure measurements and ECG Holter monitoring were all normal, and there was no clinical suspicion of seizures. I7 discontinued AMT after 5 weeks because of deterioration of mood and alertness.

Of the nine individuals who experienced positive effects, five also experienced worsening in other domains (I1, I6, I9, I12, and I13). The two individuals who experienced no positive effects had worsening in behavior or mood.

ECGs were recorded during treatment in seven individuals and were all normal. Laboratory workup during treatment was available for three individuals and showed a borderline red blood cell count in I10 (which was concluded to be unrelated to AMT treatment).

The EEG of I1 deteriorated during treatment, lacking an age‐appropriate sleep architecture, and was reported as much worse.

Five of 11 (45%) individuals were still receiving AMT treatment at the last evaluation, with a medium treatment duration of 13 weeks (range = 9–29 weeks). In the remaining six individuals (55%), including four in whom positive changes had been seen during the treatment period, AMT was discontinued after a median treatment duration of 45.5 weeks (range = 8–86 weeks). Reasons for discontinuation are listed in Table 2. The median follow‐up period after AMT discontinuation was 39 weeks (Q1: 24, Q3: 97.5). Median age at last follow‐up was 9.2 years (Q1: 5.6, Q3: 19.7).

None of the six individuals who discontinued AMT experienced any worsening or loss of the improvements seen during treatment with AMT, apart from an increase in epileptic discharges on EEG in I8 and I13 (Table S2). In all four (I8, I9, I12, and I13) in whom improvements were seen during treatment, continued improvements were seen, to the same or greater extent compared to treatment (Table 4).

KCNQ GOF‐related neurodevelopmental disorders differ from their much more common LOF counterparts in the absence of neonatal seizures. 5 For these individuals and their families, seizures are often not the primary concern. Instead, developmental delay, behavioral difficulties, and lack of communication skills can be particularly challenging, as demonstrated in this cohort. AMT has been reported as a potential precision medicine for individuals with KCNQ GOF variants, and one individual with a KCNQ2 p.Gly239Ser variant has been reported to show improvements in cognition, concentration, fine motor skills, speech, and behavior. 5 , 12 In this study, we retrospectively reported the effects of AMT in a larger cohort of individuals with pathogenic KCNQ2/3 GOF variants.

Although the positive effects reported in the published case report by Bayat et al. 12 could not be unequivocally confirmed in this cohort, eight of 13 (72%) demonstrated improvement in at least two domains as evaluated using a Likert scale. Five continued AMT treatment because of the perceived benefits. The other four individuals discontinued AMT; however, clinical improvements persisted in all, despite an increase in epileptic discharges on EEG in two. Based on these observations, several hypotheses can be proposed. First, the (temporary) AMT treatment did have a beneficial effect and might have created a time window where additional (permanent) skills could be acquired, or where educational activities had more impact due to increased alertness. Alternatively, the reported improvements might not be related to AMT but rather reflect the natural disease course. Detailed data on the natural history of KCNQ GOF are scarce, and a more in‐depth understanding of the natural disease course at different ages will be crucial for future treatment evaluations. 4 , 5 , 6 , 19 Finally, a more intensive follow‐up by the clinician and caregivers during this experimental trial with AMT and/or a placebo effect may also have influenced the reportage of clinical effects. The use of subjective outcome measures and the high expectations of a particular treatment, especially relevant in this rare disease without current treatment options, can potentially lead to a more pronounced placebo effect. 20

No serious adverse events related to AMT were reported, and those that were seen were similar to those previously reported in the literature. 11 , 21 The observed increase in behavioral and mood problems seems paradoxical, because AMT is often used to treat such issues in children with ASD. However, behavioral activation has been observed in a subset of children. 22 Behavior and mood should be monitored closely when starting AMT, and titration should be adapted accordingly.

Our study has several important limitations, mainly related to the retrospective, single‐arm, unblinded study design. First, because of the lack of a standardized treatment protocol, the AMT dosing regimen was variable. In this study, eight individuals reached a dose of at least 1 mg/kg/day, which is the recommended dose in children for other indications. 12 , 23 The serum concentration of AMT, measured only in I13 at a dosage of 3 mg/kg/day, was low. However, in individuals with depression, plasma levels or cerebrospinal fluid levels were shown not to correlate with treatment response. 24 Therapeutic levels might also differ between indications, as different target pathways are involved. Second, the lack of standardized outcome measures hampers comparability between individuals. Definition of standardized outcome measures is especially challenging for neurodevelopmental disorders, due to the interindividual variability, range of (age‐related) developmental level and comorbidities, and lack of quantitative neurodevelopmental biomarkers. 25 Therefore, seizures are often the only objective outcome measure when conducting clinical trials in DEEs, 26 but these are only rarely a problem in KCNQ GOF neurodevelopmental disorders. 1 In this study, we used Likert scales to evaluate changes across various domains. Although these scales rely on subjective interpretations by clinicians or parents, they offer a quick, consistent, and systematic approach to monitoring symptom progression. In the absence of more objective outcome measures, Likert scales provide valuable insights and are commonly used in clinical trials for rare genetic diseases. 26 , 27 The variability in response across different domains within a single individual, however, also poses a challenge in drawing an overarching conclusion. This illustrates the inherent complexity of defining appropriate and clinically meaningful outcome measures in rare neurodevelopmental disorders. Lastly, the limited size of our cohort makes it impossible to define whether there are variant specific or age‐dependent effects of AMT. Earlier start of appropriate therapies has been shown to lead to better neurodevelopmental outcome in other channelopathies. 28 The earliest age at initiation of treatment was 1.5 years (I6), which falls within early childhood, a critical period of neurodevelopment. 29 This boy continues to take AMT, and it will be crucial to follow his further neurodevelopment and compare it to individuals carrying the same variant who have never taken AMT, or those who started it at a later age. Future prospective double‐blind, placebo‐controlled clinical trials with larger sample sizes are needed to address all the limitations of this study, and to investigate the window of therapeutic opportunity. In rare diseases, such as KCNQ‐related disorders, such a trial is difficult to conduct, and robustly designed n‐of‐1 trials can be used as an equivalent alternative. 30 , 31

In conclusion, although an at least minimal improvement in at least two domains was seen in 72% of the individuals, the therapeutic effect of AMT remains unclear. None of the individuals who discontinued AMT demonstrated subsequent clinically relevant regression in the observed domains. We cannot exclude the possibility that the positive effects observed were due to the natural progression of the disease or the influence of confounding factors, such as more intensive medical and paramedical care. Our study does indicate that AMT is safe to use, even in young children. However, behavioral and mood disturbances may pose a challenge and should be closely monitored. This retrospective study involves a small cohort, and our results should be interpreted with caution. Further studies with standardized protocols are necessary to determine whether there are subgroups of individuals who may benefit from AMT treatment. Until then, this study can serve as guidance for clinicians considering the initiation of AMT.
