# Suppression of Kv3.3 channels by antisense oligonucleotides reverses biochemical effects and motor impairment in spinocerebellar ataxia type 13 mice.
**Authors:** Zhang Y, Quraishi IH, McClure H, Williams LA, Cheng Y, Kale S, Dempsey GT, Agrawal S, Gerber DJ, McManus OB, Kaczmarek LK
**Journal:** FASEB J (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1096/fj.202101356R](https://doi.org/10.1096/fj.202101356R)

## Content

Mutations in KCNC3, the gene that encodes the Kv3.3 voltage dependent potassium channel, cause Spinocerebellar Ataxia type 13 (SCA13), a disease associated with disrupted motor behaviors, progressive cerebellar degeneration, and abnormal auditory processing. The Kv3.3 channel directly binds Hax-1, a cell survival protein. A disease-causing mutation, Kv3.3-G592R, causes overstimulation of Tank Binding Kinase 1 (Tbk1) in the cerebellum, resulting in the degradation of Hax-1 by promoting its trafficking into multivesicular bodies and then to lysosomes. We have now tested the effects of antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) directed against the Kv3.3 channel on both wild type mice and those bearing the Kv3.3-G592R-encoding mutation. Intracerebroventricular infusion of the Kcnc3-specific ASO suppressed both mRNA and protein levels of the Kv3.3 channel. In wild-type animals, this produced no change in levels of activated Tbk1, Hax-1 or Cd63, a tetraspanin marker for late endosomes/multivesicular bodies. In contrast, in mice homozygous for the Kv3.3-G592R-encoding mutation, the same ASO reduced Tbk1 activation and levels of Cd63, while restoring the expression of Hax-1 in the cerebellum. The motor behavior of the mice was tested using a rotarod assay. Surprisingly, the active ASO had no effects on the motor behavior of wild type mice but restored the behavior of the mutant mice to those of age-matched wild type animals. Our findings indicate that, in mature intact animals, suppression of Kv3.3 expression can reverse the deleterious effects of a SCA13 mutation while having little effect on wild type animals. Thus, targeting Kv3.3 expression may prove a viable therapeutic approach for SCA13.

The Kv3.3 voltage-dependent potassium channel has a dual role in neurons. It is expressed in rapidly firing neurons in several brain regions including Purkinje cells of the cerebellum and the principal neurons of several auditory brainstem nuclei (1). It is also expressed in hippocampal neurons in cell culture. In many of these structures, it is present in the plasma membrane of presynaptic boutons (2). By activating rapidly at positive potentials, Kv3.3 contributes to the rapid repolarization of individual action potentials, allowing neurons to fire at rates of several hundred Hz (3). The Kv3.3 channel also has an extended cytoplasmic C-terminal domain that allows it to interact with several intracellular signaling components, including Hax-1, an antiapoptotic protein required for the survival of cerebellar neurons, and Tbk1 (Tank-Binding Kinase 1), a multifunctional enzyme that is known to regulate the trafficking of intracellular organelles (4, 5). In presynaptic terminals, the interaction of Kv3.3 with these components leads to the formation of a network of actin filaments that are tethered to the channel (2).

Human mutations in KCNC3, the gene that encodes the Kv3.3 channel subunit, give rise to spinocerebellar ataxia type 13 (SCA13) (6–12). Some of the mutations produce an early onset disease in which the cerebellum degenerates within weeks of birth. The effects of other Kv3.3 mutations do not, however, become evident until middle age, and produce a progressive deficit in balance and motor control that is associated with loss of cerebellar tissue (13). Outcomes are poor and there are currently no available treatments for SCA13.

One experimental model of SCA13 consists of mice in which the wild type Kv3.3 channel has been replaced by the Kv3.3-G592R mutant form, containing a single amino acid substitution in the cytoplasmic C-terminal domain that in humans results in the late onset disease (4). The ion conducting properties of Kv3.3-G592R channels are very similar to those of wild type channels, having very similar voltage-dependence and activation kinetics to those of the wild type (5). The interactions of the C-terminal domain with its cytoplasmic partners are, however, substantially modified. Specifically, the Kv3.3-G592R mutant fails to recruit a Hax-1 dependent actin cytoskeleton at presynaptic terminals, resulting in impairment of normal synaptic transmission (2). Although Hax-1 remains bound to the mutant channel, it becomes targeted for destruction via its trafficking through multivesicular bodies into lysosomes. This is mediated by Tbk1, which normally binds to the channel complex, but which becomes overactivated by the Kv3.3-G592R mutant (4). As a result, levels of phospho-Tbk1, the activated form of the enzyme, and of Cd63, a marker for multivesicular bodies are markedly elevated in the cerebellum of Kv3.3-G592R mice, while levels of Hax-1 are substantially reduced (4). Simple behavioral assays show that motor function is impaired in adult Kv3.3-G592R mice (4).

Because the deficits in Kv3.3-G592R mice appear be a result of a gain-of-function in the ability of the channel to activate TBK1, we have now tested effects of intracerebroventricular (ICV) infusion of antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) that target Kcnc3 mRNA and suppress Kv3.3 channel synthesis on biochemical changes in the cerebellum and on the behavior of both wild type and Kv3.3-G592R mice. ASOs are short synthetic sequences that bind to target sequences via complementary base pairing and can modulate target expression via different mechanisms including splice modulation, knockdown and boosting (https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/ebook/978-1-78801-209-6). ASOs represent an emerging therapeutic approach to address neurological disorders (14) and have, for example, demonstrated clinical success in patients with spinal muscular atrophy (15–17). We find that a knockdown of Kv3.3 expression using a gapmer ASO has little effect on the levels of activated Tbk1, Hax-1 or Cd63 in wild type animals but substantially reverses the changes brought about by the disease-causing mutation. These results highlight the potential therapeutic applications of ASOs towards SCA13-mediated disease.

Bioinformatics analysis of Kcnc3 mRNA (RefSeq NM-008422.3) identified all possible exonic target sequences containing 20 nucleotides that afforded selectivity for Kcnc3 over other sequences in the mouse genome. A set of 25 exonic target sequences spread across the entire Kcnc3 mRNA transcript were selected for small scale (100 nmole scale) synthesis at Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). All ASOs used in the study contained a phosphorothioate (PS) backbone. ASOs followed a 5-10-5 gapmer design, with a central core of 10 DNA nucleotides flanked by 5’ and 3’ wings with 5 RNA-like nucleotides each. The initial set of 25 ASOs contained a 2’-O-methyl chemical modification in the ribose sugar moiety. Once top ASO candidates were selected, four ASOs were designed and synthesized at IDT using medium scale synthesis (5 μmole scale) to support in vivo studies. This latter set of 4 ASOs (ASO-006, ASO-012, GFP-ASO-002, and GFP-ASO-003) included 2’-O-methoxyethyl chemistry in the 5’ and 3’ wings and 5-methyl cytosines throughout the ASO, two chemical modifications more commonly used for in vivo studies and in oligonucleotide drugs (18, 19). Quality control of 2’MOE ASOs by IDT showed >90% purity and Endotoxin levels <0.100 EU/mg.

All ASOs were initially tested in vitro using cultured mouse hippocampal neurons. E18 mouse hippocampal tissue was obtained from BrainBits and neurons were obtained after dissociation following BrainBits’ recommendations. Dissociated cells were resuspended using Neurobasal A medium supplemented with 1x N2, 1xB27, 1x GlutaMax (ThermoFisher Scientific) and with 10 ng/mL of recombinant neurotrophic factors BDNF and GDND (R&D Systems). Neurons were plated at a density of 60,000 cells/cm2 onto 96-well plates pre-treated with 100 μg/mL Poly-D-Lysine (Sigma) and 20 μg/mL Laminin (ThermoFisher Scientific). 1–2 hours after plating, ASOs were delivered gymnotically (no transfection) using the same plating medium as vehicle. Medium was not exchanged until two days after, at DIV2 (day in vitro 2), when dissociated mouse primary astrocytes were added to each well at a density of 50,000 cells/cm2 using the same Neuronal Basal medium formulation described above. Mouse glia preparations were carried out as described previously (20). ASOs were not replenished during this medium exchange. Neurons and glia co-cultures were maintained for 8 additional days until DIV10, when total RNA was harvested, and cDNA samples were prepared using a Cells-to-CT kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Kcnc3 transcript levels were assessed using SYBR green (BioRad Laboratories) qPCR assays on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR instrument (BioRad Laboratories). qPCR primers were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) qPCR ready assays (Kcnc3: Mm.PT.58.16518188 (5’-CACAATGCTGCTCAGGCT-3’ and 5’-GAAGACAAGAGCCCAATCACT-3’) and Gapdh: Mm.PT.39a.1 (5’-GTGGAGTCATACTGGAACATGTAG-3’ and 5’-AATGGTGAAGGTCGGTGTG-3’). Cq values were converted into relative normalized expression values using the CFX Software application (BioRad Laboratories).

Wild type and homozygous Kv3.3-G592R knock-in mice were used in the study. The generation of these mice has been described previously (4). DNA was extracted with DNeasy Blood & TissueKit (Qiagen). PCR was performed using Platinum Tag DNA Polymerase High Fidelity (Qiagen) followed by a PCR purification (Qiagen). DNA concentration was measured and sent for sequencing analysis (together with forward and reverse primers 5’-TGATCCTGACGACATCCTGG-’3 and CATGGAAGGAGGGGTGATGG, respectively). Results were analyzed with Finch Tv Software. A breeding colony was established at the Yale University Animal Resources Center. Mice were kept on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Food and water were freely available. All experiments were done in accordance with the Yale University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

ASOs were delivered to mice by intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection. Seven month old wild type mice (male, n =6) and same age mutant Kv3.3-G592R knock- in mice (male, n = 8) were anesthetized with vaporized isoflurane (VetEquip Incorporated). Pre-operative analgesia was provided with buprenorphine 0.1 mg/kg. After skull landmarks were exposed with an anterior-posterior scalp incision, a 0.6 mm burr hole was made, through which the tip of a 35-gauge beveled needle connected to 10 mL glass syringe (NanoFil, World Precision Instruments) was positioned using a stereotaxic apparatus (Kopf Instruments) in the left lateral ventricle at the following coordinates identified from a mouse brain atlas: AP, −0.48 mm; ML, 1.0 mm; DV, −2.25 mm from bregma. 105 μg of either control or Kv3.3-specific ASOs, dissolved in 3 μL PBS without Ca/Mg (Gibco, 14190), were delivered via a micro-syringe pump at 15 nL/s. After waiting 2 minutes for equilibration, the needle was withdrawn at a rate of 1 mm/s, and, as soon as the needle was out of the skull, a cotton swab was rolled over the burr hole and held for an additional 1–2 mins to prevent any ASO from leaking out. The scalp was sutured closed, and postoperative analgesia was provided immediately with meloxicam 5 mg/kg and hydration with subcutaneous delivery of 250 μL normal saline. Animal survival rate was 100% after ASO injections.

For measurements of Kv3.3 mRNA expression in mice, one half of each brain region from each mouse was homogenized, and RNA isolation was done according to the instructions of RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, cat# 74104). cDNA was synthesized using 200 ng RNA using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis system for RT-PCR (Thermo Fisher, cat# is 18080-051). The target gene, Kcnc3 forward primer sequence was ggcgacagcggtaagatcgtg, reverse primer sequence was: ggtagtagttgagcacgtaggcga. The reference gene is Gapdh, for which the forward primer sequence was: gttgtctcctgcgacttca, the reverse primer sequence was: ggtggtccagggtttctta. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed in Bio-Rad CFX96 (Bio-Rad Inc., USA) with the Power SYBR Green PCR MasterMix (Applied Biosystems Inc, cat#25742). PCR amplification was carried out according to the instruction of Manufacturer. Gene expression levels were normalized to the Gapdh levels.

A rotarod test (AccuScan Instruments) was used to evaluate motor coordination in WT and Kv3.3-G592R mice. Before the test, mice were trained with 6 consecutive trials on each of 3 consecutive days. Mice were habituated to the testing room 30 mins prior to the start of testing on each day. The rotarod was accelerated from 0 to 40 rpm over 200 seconds. To ensure enough recovery time between trials, animals were given 10 mins between the end of a trial and the following trial. Trials ended whenever an animal failed to stay on the rotarod or if they made 2 consecutive rotations clinging to the rod and not ambulating. Durations on the rotarod were evaluated as the average of multiple trials.

One half of each brain region from each mouse was homogenized using a tissue grinder and 600uL T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher, cat# 78510) that included a Roche complete Mini Protease inhibitor tablet (Roche, cat# 11836170001) and PhosSTOP Easypack (Roche, cat# 04906837001). Homogenized samples were shaken at 4 °C for 30 mins and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 mins (at 4 °C) to remove large cell debris. The supernatants were then aliquoted, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until use. Protein estimation was done using Bradford’s reagent (Bio-Rad). For Western blot, samples were suspended in 4× sample buffer for electrophoresis and incubated at room temperature for 30 min before loading on a SDS-PAGE gel. After electrophoresis, the protein was transferred onto polyvinylidenedifluoride membranes (Bio-Rad). Blots were then blocked in TBST (a mixture of tris-buffered saline and Tween-20) containing 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature with shaking. Blots were then incubated with the respective primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with TBST, blots were incubated for 1 h with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, followed by extensive washes in TBST. Labeled proteins were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence. Polyclonal rabbit anti-Kv3.3 antibody (Alomone Labs) was used at 1:200; monoclonal rabbit anti-phospho TBK1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) was used at 1:500; polyclonal anti-rabbit Hax-1 antibody (Proteintech) was used at 1:200; monoclonal mouse anti-GAPDH antibody (Santa Cruz) was used at 1:1000; anti- CD63 antibody (Santa Cruz) was used at 1:200.

A series of ASOs were designed to test the hypothesis that selective knockdown of expression of mouse Kv3.3 (Kcnc3) channels would reverse the biochemical and behavioral changes reported in Kv3.3-G592R knock-in mice. The mice are homozygous for the Kv3.3-G592R-encoding mutation, providing a rationale for targeting any locus on the channel to knockdown expression towards reversing the behavioral pathology. 25 ASO sequences were designed to selectively target the mouse Kcnc3 mRNA transcript, RefSeq NM_008422.3, with ASO targeting regions spread across the entire transcript (Supplementary Fig 1A, 1B). ASO sequences followed a 5-10-5 gapmer design, with a central core of 10 DNA nucleotides flanked on the 5’ and 3’ ends by wings of 5 2’-O-methyl (2’-O-Me) RNA nucleotides for enhanced target affinity and stability (19, 21). This gapmer approach is designed to engage RNase H activity leading to the degradation of Kcnc3 transcripts encoding the Kv3.3 channel.

In order to test Kcnc3 knockdown activity, the initial set of 25 ASOs were applied gymnotically (no transfection) at 500 nM and 1 μM to dissociated E18 mouse hippocampal neurons during plating (Fig. 1A). Neurons were maintained in culture for 10 days (DIV10), at which point total RNA was isolated and relative Kcnc3 transcript levels were measured using quantitative PCR (Fig. 1A). Compared to control untreated neurons, cells treated with each of six ASOs (ASO-006, ASO-011, ASO-012, ASO-014, ASO-026, ASO-028) displayed ~65–70% knockdown of Kcnc3 at both 500 nM and 1μM (Fig. 1B, 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1C). Two ASOs (ASO-010 and ASO-020) exhibited significant effects on the housekeeping gene (Gapdh) used to normalize the qPCR data, suggesting potential off-target effects (data not shown). ASO-006 and ASO-0012 were selected for additional analyses based on significant knockdown of the Kcnc3 target gene and no effects on the control housekeeping gene. In addition, ASO-030 and ASO-002 were selected as reference sequences for additional experiments due to lack of significant effect on Kcnc3 or Gapdh. These four ASOs were tested in concentration-response assays, where ASO-006 and ASO-012 showed clear concentration dependent knockdown of Kcnc3 transcript levels, with ~85–90% knockdown observed at 1 μM (Fig. 1D).

ASO-006 and ASO-012 sequences were selected for larger scale synthesis for in vivo experiments, replacing 2’-O-methyl with 2’-O-methoxyethyl (2’-MOE) chemistry and incorporating 5-methyl cytosines (Supplementary Fig. 1D). ASOs with 2’-MOE chemical modifications in the ribose sugar moiety and with 5-methyl cytosines can be well tolerated in vivo, and these modifications have been commonly used, including in several oligonucleotide drugs (18, 19). In parallel to the two Kcnc3-targeting 2’-MOE gapmers, two additional 2’-OMOE gapmers were also synthesized, GFP-ASO-002 and GFP-ASO-003 (Supplementary Fig. 1D). These latter ASOs were designed to selectively target synthetic eGFP sequences and to avoid sequence complementarity with the mouse genome, therefore serving as negative control ASOs. The four ASOs with 2’-MOE chemistry were tested at different concentrations in cultured mouse hippocampal neurons, where treatment with ASO-006 and ASO-012 yielded concentration dependent knockdown of Kcnc3 transcript levels, with ASO-006 achieving >80% knockdown and ASO-012 achieving >60% knockdown at 1 μM. In contrast, GFP-ASO-002 and GFP-ASO-003 exhibited no effects on Kcnc3 transcript levels (Fig. 1E). Based on these data, ASO-006 and GFP-ASO-002 were selected for in vivo studies, hereafter referred to as Kv3.3 ASO and Control ASO, respectively.

Six wild type C57/B6 mice (7 months of age) were injected ICV with the negative control ASO. A second set of six wild type animals of the same age were injected with ASO-006 (Kv3.3 ASO), which had been found to produce strong knockdown of levels of Kcnc3 mRNA in the assay described above. ICV injection procedure and treatment was well tolerated. Five weeks after the injections, both sets of animals were euthanized and levels of Kv3.3 mRNA were measured by pPCR (Fig. 2A, B). Levels of Kv3.3 transcript were found to be reduced by over 75% in the animals treated with the Kv3.3 ASO compared to those treated with the control ASO (Fig. 2A).

The same experiment was also carried out using 7-month-old mice carrying the homozygous Kv3.3-G592R mutation (4), using the same ICV injection parameters. Six mutant animals were injected with the control ASO, and eight with the Kv3.3 ASO. As with the wild type mice, levels of Kv3.3 mRNA transcript were reduced by over 75% in Kv3.3ASO-treateed animals compared to those injected with control ASO (Fig. 2B).

We also measured levels of Kv3.3 protein by immunoblotting assays in wild type and Kv3.3-G592R mutant mice injected with control ASO or Kv3.3 ASO. In wild type mice, levels of the channel protein were found to be reduced by approximately 50% in the animals treated with the Kv3.3 ASO compared to those treated with the control ASO (Fig. 2C). A slightly greater reduction in Kv3.3 protein was found in 7-month-old mice carrying the homozygous Kv3.3-G592R mutation (4), using the same ICV injection parameters. Three animals were injected with the Control ASO, and five with the Kv3.3 ASO. Levels of Kv3.3 protein in the cerebellum measured five weeks after the injections were found to be reduced by approximately 70% in the mutant animals treated with the Kv3.3 ASO compared to those treated with the control ASO (Fig. 2D).

Previous work has shown that activation of Kv3.3 channels by depolarization stimulates the activity of TBK1 (4). This effect is specific to Kv3.3 in that it is not observed in cells expressing the closely related Kv3.1 channel, or large-conductance calcium-activated BK potassium channels. In cells transfected with Kv3.3-G592R, the activity of TBK1 is markedly elevated over that in cells expressing wild type Kv3.3. Accordingly, basal levels of TBK1 activity are increased in the cerebellum of mice expressing this mutation over those in wild type mice (4). To confirm that this process is not disrupted by ICV injections, we compared levels of phosphorylated (activated) TBK1, pTBK1, in the cerebella of wild type and Kv3.3-G592R mice 4 weeks after injection of the control ASO. As previously found for uninjected animals, pTBK1 was significantly increased by the mutation in Kcnc3 (Fig. 3A).

We next compared TBK1 activation in wild type animals or Kv3.3-G592R mice treated with the control and the Kv3.3 ASO. There was no difference in cerebellar levels of pTBK1 between wild type mice treated with the control or the specific Kv3.3 ASO (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the Kv3.3 ASO produced an ~50% reduction in levels of pTBK1 in the Kv3.3-G592R animals compared to that in the cerebellum of these mutant mice treated with the control ASO (Fig. 3C). These findings clearly attribute the increase in pTBK1 activity to the expression of the mutant channel.

Previous work using immunoelectron microscopy and biochemical markers has demonstrated that levels of multivesicular bodies are markedly increased in the cerebellum of mice with the Kv3.3-G592R mutation (4). This is associated with increased secretion of exosomes into the extracellular fluid and increased trafficking of Hax-1 from multivesicular bodies into lysosomes. As a result, Hax-1 levels in the cerebellum of these mutant mice are reduced compared to those in wild type animals.

We therefore next compared the levels of Cd63, a specific marker for multivesicular bodies, in wild type and Kv3.3-G592R animals injected with the ASOs. As was the case for TBK1, no statistical difference was detected in levels of Cd63 between wild type mice treated with the control ASO or with the specific Kv3.3 ASO, as determined by immunoblotting assays of cerebellar tissue extracted 5 weeks after the ICV injections (Fig. 4A). Similarly, no difference could be detected in the levels of Hax-1 in the cerebellum of wild type mice treated with the control ASO or the Kv3.3 ASO (Fig. 4B).

In contrast to the results with wild type animals, the Kv3.3 ASO produced a marked change in levels of both Cd63 and Hax-1 in Kv3.3-G592R animals. Levels of the multivesicular marker Cd63 were reduced by close to 70% by the Kv3.3 ASO compared to mutant animals treated with the control ASO (Fig. 4C). Consistent with this finding, levels of the survival protein Hax-1 were increased by ~ 75% in the cerebellum of Kv3.3-G592R mice injected with the Kv3.3 ASO compared to those treated with the control ASO (Fig. 4D).

Previous work found that the motor behavior of 7-month-old Kv3.3-G592R mice, assayed by their ability to balance on a rotating rod, as well as their ability to walk along a dowel, is impaired compared to that of age-matched wild type animals (4). We therefore assayed motor behavior wild type and mutant mice injected with control and Kv3.3 ASOs. To minimize the time between these behavioral tests and biochemical assays, rotarod assays were carried out 4 weeks after the injections (5 days prior to the biochemical measurements). Consistent with the previous study (4), the ability of the Kv3.3-G592R mice to stay on the rotating rods was significantly impaired in control-ASO-treated Kv3.3-G592R animals compared to wild type animals treated with the same ASO (Fig. 5). As with the biochemical measures, treatment of wild type animals with the Kv3.3 ASO had no detectable effect on their performance on the rotarod. In contrast, injections of the Kv3.3 ASO into the mutant mice restored their ability to remain on the rotating rod to a level that was not significantly different from that of wild type animals (Fig. 5).

We have found that partial suppression of the expression of Kv3.3 channels using antisense oligonucleotides in wild type mice has little or no effect on rotarod motor behavior or on TBK1 activity or Hax-1 levels, two biochemical parameters linked to cerebellar function and integrity. In contrast, reduction of channel levels in mice expressing the Kv3.3-G592R mutant, reverses both the overactivity of TBK1 and the reduction of Hax-1 levels that are produced by the mutant channel. Measurement of the tetraspanin marker Cd63 revealed that Kv3.3 ASO treatment also reverses the enhanced formation of multivesicular bodies through which Hax-1 has previously been shown to be targeted to lysosomes (4). Finally, ASO-mediated reduction of levels of Kv3.3-G592R channel restored motor performance on the rotarod to that of wild type animals.

Complete knockout of the Kv3.3 gene in mice has been shown to reduce the firing rate of cerebellar Purkinje neurons (22), alter motor behaviors (23) and impair synaptic transmission in neuronal pathways that fire at high rates (2). It is not known, however, if neuronal survival is affected by total loss of Kv3.3 channels. It has been shown that in some cell types, deletion of the Kv3.3 gene results in compensation through increases in expression of other channel subunits (24).

The effects of mutations in Kv3.3 appear to be more severe than those expected simply from the loss of channel expression. Different mutations that lead to SCA13 and cerebellar degeneration produce different effects on channel function. Some of the mutations result in failure of the channel subunits to traffic to the plasma membrane, while others alter the kinetic or gating parameters of the channel (6, 8, 12, 13, 25–27). Some of these mutations are likely to alter the firing patterns of neurons, and also alter parameters such as handling of intracellular calcium (28). The introduction of mutant Kv3.3 channels, however, also differs from complete elimination of channel expression in that the mutant proteins may modify cellular signaling by modifying the normal interactions between the channel and its cytoplasmic partners, as is the case for the Kv3.3-G592R channel (5). These factors are likely to play a major role in the neurodegeneration that is seen in humans as well as in animal models of SCA13 (29).

Our findings indicate that the effects of Kv3.3 ASO treatment on channel levels in wild type neurons may differ from that in neurons expressing Kv3.3-G592R channels. Specifically, we found a greater reduction of Kv3.3 levels in Kv3.3-G592R mice. One likely explanation for this finding is that wild type Kv3.3 channels are normally linked to a dense actin cytoskeleton under the plasma membrane that is mediated by its auxiliary Hax-1 proteins (2, 5). This may limit the rate at which they become internalized by catabolic pathways. The Kv3.3-G592R channels fail to generate this actin network, potentially increasing their turnover rate by making them more susceptible to internalization and degradation (4).

In addition to promoting cerebellar degeneration, spinocerebellar ataxias caused by mutations in genes other than KCNC3 are also known to have a major impact on expression of ion channels and on the intrinsic excitability of neurons (30). It has been shown that some of the electrophysiological changes in SCA1 mice can be countered by potassium channel openers (31). It is not yet clear, however, whether simply normalizing the excitability of neurons will counteract the problems caused by altered interaction with cellular signaling pathways.

As reported by others using different ataxia mouse models (32), we found the effects of a single ASO treatment on both target mRNA levels and on other biochemical parameters were evident many weeks after treatment. Indeed, a changes in levels of Cd63, a markers for multivesicular bodies, and of Hax-1 itself, likely require remodeling of the pathways for internalization and degradation of cellular components, Such remodeling is likely to be slower than a simple change in neuronal excitability resulting from altered Kv3.3 currents. Moreover, in contrast to our studies with homozygous G592R Kv3.3 mice, individuals with SCA13 are typically heterozygous for the mutant allele, and, in the late-onset disease, most symptoms take decades to develop (13). Taken together, our findings on the SCA13 mutation Kv3.3- G592R, as well as work on mutations in Ataxin-1, Ataxin-2, Ataxin-3 and Ataxin-7 which give rise to SCA1 (33, 34), SCA2 (35), SCA3 (32, 36, 37) and SCA7 (38) respectively, suggest that genetic manipulations such as use of ASOs or other approaches that suppress expression of mutant alleles may prove efficacious in the treatment of these conditions.
