# Somatic mutation in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease revealed by deep sequencing human kidney cysts.
**Authors:** Mallawaarachchi AC, Hort Y, Wedd L, Lo K, Senum S, Toumari M, Chen W, Utsiwegota M, Mawson J, Leslie S, Laurence J, Anderson L, Snelling P, Salomon R, Rangan GK, Furlong T, Shine J, Cowley MJ
**Journal:** NPJ Genom Med (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41525-024-00452-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41525-024-00452-6)

## Content

Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD) results in progressive cysts that lead to kidney failure, and is caused by heterozygous germline variants in PKD1 or PKD2. Cyst pathogenesis is not definitively understood. Somatic second-hit mutations have been implicated in cyst pathogenesis, though technical sequencing challenges have limited investigation. We used unique molecular identifiers, high-depth massively parallel sequencing and custom analysis techniques to identify somatic second-hit mutations in 24 whole cysts from disparate regions of six human ADPKD kidneys, utilising replicate samples and orthogonal confirmation. Average depth of coverage of 1166 error-corrected reads for PKD1 and 539 reads for PKD2 was obtained. 58% (14/24) of cysts had a detectable PKD1 somatic variant, with 5/6 participants having at least one cyst with a somatic variant. We demonstrate that low-frequency somatic mutations are detectable in a proportion of cysts from end-stage ADPKD human kidneys. Further studies are required to understand the drivers of this somatic mutation.

Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD) is the most common monogenic cause of kidney failure and affects approximately 1 in 1000 people1. The condition is associated with significant morbidity, with approximately 50% of patients with the condition reaching kidney failure by the age of 50 years2. Typical ADPKD is caused by germline disease-causing variants in one allele of either the PKD1 or PKD2 genes3. The disease is characterised by progressive development of kidney cysts that eventually destroy the kidney tissue, leading to kidney failure. ADPKD patients with end-stage disease typically have markedly enlarged kidneys, often double or more in size, that are overwhelmed with cysts throughout the kidney, leaving little to no normal kidney tissue remaining. Remarkably for a common and morbid disease, the definitive pathogenesis of cyst initiation and proliferation in ADPKD is not clearly understood.

The PKD1 and PKD2 genes encode for the proteins polycystin-1 and polycystin-2, respectively3. It is thought that the kidney is sensitive to levels of polycystin-1 and polycystin-2, such that reduced levels results in cyst initiation4. There are hypotheses that haploinsufficiency alone (loss of one allele) is enough to account for the initiation of kidney cysts5. Other data supports a ‘two-hit’ hypothesis, in which a somatic variant is required in the alternate PKD1 or PKD2 allele of a patient with a germline pathogenic variant, such that ADPKD is actually recessive at the level of the kidney epithelial cell6,7.

Previous studies have explored the two-hit hypothesis, including early studies that demonstrated loss of heterozygosity in some kidney cysts from patients with ADPKD6–10. These initial studies investigating the two-hit hypothesis identified potential somatic variants in only a proportion of tested cysts. Investigation of the two-hit hypothesis in ADPKD has been hampered by significant challenges in sequencing PKD1 due to highly homologous pseudogenes that hamper unique sequencing and accurate read alignment3. These pseudogenes are homologous to the first two-thirds of PKD1 (exons 1–33). The challenges of sequencing PKD1 in cyst tissue left it unclear whether this was due to technical limitations or a true absence of somatic variation in some cysts, that suggests that somatic variants are not universally required for cyst initiation. More recently Tan and Zhang et al. demonstrated somatic variants at remarkably high variant allele frequency (12–80%) in the majority of cysts of patients with ADPKD (90% and 93% of cysts), identified with long-range PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing, or standard-depth exome or genome sequencing11,12. These somatic variants were detected in cells from larger cysts (1.0–15 cm), collected from the external surface of end-stage kidneys11,12. This group utilised specific collection techniques that aimed to extract cells only from the epithelial cell lining of intact cysts. As the cysts investigated were relatively large, the question remains about whether somatic variants occurred at the initiation of the cyst or later4.

The field of somatic mutation detection and the contribution of somatic variants to disease pathogenesis has been primarily driven by the cancer research community, where dedicated techniques have been developed to detect somatic variants at low frequency. These include high-depth sequencing and the use of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), which molecularly barcode each DNA fragment prior to amplification, in order to mitigate DNA amplification errors leading to false positive results13,14. The use of UMIs to tag each DNA molecule and therefore identify PCR-error has been shown to correct approximately 99% of sequencing errors15. In both the oncology and mitochondrial disease space, specific analysis pipelines have also been developed to detect true variants at very low allele frequency16.

Understanding the basis of cyst formation and progression is key to understanding disease initiation and progression, and developing effective treatments for ADPKD. Robust investigation of the two-hit hypothesis in ADPKD has met many technical challenges, including early studies that were inconclusive due to limitations of the sequencing technology available at that time and the significant technical challenge of collecting tissue only from a thin epithelial cell lining of an organ in the end stages of a disease process, resulting in very limited amounts of DNA and hence potential amplification or selection bias during sequencing6,7,9,17. In this study, we aimed to overcome these ADPKD-cyst specific technical limitations by utilising low frequency variant detection techniques robustly developed in oncology research. We used UMIs and high-depth massively parallel sequencing with custom analysis techniques to investigate for somatic mutation in smaller, whole cysts, from disparate (including internal) regions of the kidney, utilising replicate samples, with the aim of investigating the prevalence of somatic loss of function mutations in polycystic kidney cysts, and if present, whether they are present in all cysts from the same patient. Through this, we show that 58% of cysts from consecutively collected samples had a detectable somatic variant in PKD1 in addition to their inherited pathogenic variant.

Tissue samples from disparate areas of the kidney were collected in all participants. Only cysts containing clear fluid were selected for dissection and collection. In total, 24 whole cyst samples, from six kidneys, were collected intact and available for genomic sequencing (2–6 whole cysts per participant). Replicates were available for all samples included in the study (Fig. 1). With replicates, deep sequencing was performed in 48 samples. All samples were sequenced to an average depth of 1166 error-corrected reads for PKD1 and 539 reads for PKD2, with the majority of regions covered to a minimum depth of coverage of 1000 reads for PKD1 and 500 reads for PKD2 (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Sequencing coverage for PKD1 exon 1 was poor, likely due to its high GC content. Sequencing coverage for exons 2–33 which have sequence homology to the six PKD1 pseudogenes was similar to exons 34–46 which have no sequence homology to pseudogenes (Supplementary Data 1). After manual review and curation, 27 candidate variants met criteria for pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, from 21 different cyst samples, and were flagged for further statistical analysis (Supplementary Data 2). After this analysis, 10 variants were excluded as there was insufficient evidence for these variants to meet the criteria for a high likelihood of being a true variant call (p > 2.2 × 10−16) (Supplementary Data 2, Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4).

To rule out the possibility that these somatic variants were sequencing artefacts, we re-sequenced the DNA from 10 cysts that had somatic variants identified by the initial method and sufficient material available, using duplex UMI-based sequencing libraries. Duplex UMI’s have been reported to have a theoretical sequencing error as low as 1 in a billion, because any variant needs to be on both strands of the DNA15. By this approach we confirmed the presence of 12 of the 13 candidate somatic variants looked for, with similar VAF to the original method (Supplementary Data 2). The remaining variant that was not detected on orthogonal sequencing had very low VAF (0.26% & 0.10%), with only four supporting reads across both replicates in the original data, that had depth to 1149× and 1020×. In the revised duplex sequencing data, the depth at this location was 871x so it is plausible that we did not sample this fragment. There was insufficient sample available to perform orthogonal studies for four of the original variants identified – samples RPA011KIc, RPA012KH and RPA005KL6.

Finally, to rule out the possibility that the candidate somatic variants were germline mosaic variants, we conducted deep sequencing of DNA extracted from matched peripheral blood DNA from that patient. In all cases the candidate somatic variant was absent from matched blood (Supplementary Fig. 5).

There were 17 pathogenic or likely pathogenic somatic PKD1 variants identified in the study overall – this included three cyst samples that had two, unique pathogenic somatic variants identified in each cyst (Fig. 2). Thus a pathogenic or likely pathogenic somatic variant was identified in PKD1 in 58% (14/24) of cysts (Table 1). Five of six participants (83%) had at least one cyst with a somatic PKD1 variant identified. Variants were identified across the PKD1 gene and no pathogenic or likely pathogenic somatic variants were identified in the PKD2 gene. All reported variants were identified in two replicates from each cyst sample and met position-specific noise threshold criteria for having a high likelihood of being a true variant call (p < 2.2 × 10−16) (Fig. 2). For 12/17 (71%) variants, the variants were in a position where there was no variation observed at that position in any of the other 46 samples reviewed. The remaining five variants also met the criteria of p < 2.2 × 10−16, though there was minimal sequencing noise at the reported position with the samples with the reported variant having significantly more unique calls to support it than the baseline noise at that region (Supplementary Fig. 3). Four out of five of these variants have been previously reported as disease-causing in ADPKD families (Table 1). The underlying reason why germline heterozygous variants in PKD1 or PKD2 lead to kidney cyst development and progression is not clearly understood. This limitation in current understanding is reflected in the lack of effective treatments for this common and highly morbid monogenic disease. Reduction in the amount of polycystin-1 and polycystin-2 within the kidney epithelial cell is linked to cyst development and there is experimental evidence that supports both the theory that haploinsufficiency alone is sufficient for cyst development and alternate data that supports that somatic mutation of the alternate allele is also required5,6. None of the available evidence is however definitive. In particular, studies to date investigating for the presence of somatic mutations in kidney cysts have not been able to demonstrate somatic variants in every cyst studied7,11,12. It is unclear whether this is due to technical limitation or that somatic mutation is not required for cyst initiation or progression in every cyst. Our study, which utilised methodologies well-established in the oncology somatic variant-detection field, identified somatic, pathogenic PKD1 variants in 58% of consecutive cysts studied.

In contrast to previously reported studies, we detected somatic variants at significantly lower variant allele frequency. This low VAF may reflect the different cyst tissue collection technique that we utilised, that allowed collection of cells from much smaller cysts (<1 cm) and allowed adequate cell number, and therefore DNA quantity, for systematic use of replicate samples. A concern in interpreting variants detected at very low VAF is in distinguishing true variants from the known error profiles of the sequencing method utilised. To account for this, we used conservative statistical techniques, and orthogonally validated all but one variant, to determine the likelihood that the variants reported were true and specifically accounted for the baseline error-profile at each site with a variant detected18.

The low VAF of the variants detected in our study are consistent with data from a recent single-cell RNA sequencing study that collected the ‘cups’ of cysts from ADPKD patients in order to characterise the cellular makeup of a cyst19. The authors found significant cellular heterogeneity in the cyst lining cells studied, with a range of cell types identified. In this study, fibroblasts and leukocytes made up more than 50% of the cells identified within a cyst, with less than approximately 25% of cells being attributable to any type of kidney tubular cell in eight different ADPKD cyst samples19. In contrast to this, the studies by Tan and Zhang et al. detected somatic variants from cyst samples at VAFs of 12–80%, with many of the cysts studied having somatic variants approaching or greater than 50% VAF, reflecting that the investigators achieved remarkable sample purity through their collection technique of extracting cells from the inner cyst lining11,12. Replicate samples were not systematically applied in these studies and UMIs were not utilised11,12. The vast majority of cysts reported in these two related studies had somatic variants detected. It was described that not all cysts studied were able to have adequate cells extracted for minimum DNA quantity requirements to be met. It is therefore unclear from the data whether the variants detected reflect unique circumstances of the larger cysts studied, and to what extent any recurrent sequencing artefacts may have been identified. If the main difference between the variant detection rates of these previous studies and our study is the collection technique, it would have been expected that the significantly higher depth of coverage achieved in our study (average PKD1 depth of 1166 reads vs 79 reads) would have overcome differences in sample purity.

Our data and previous studies have not demonstrated somatic variants in every ADPKD cyst studied. There are studies that describe a ‘snowball effect’ in cyst formation, in which a cyst appears to then stimulate growth of cysts in adjacent nephrons, that cluster near the original cyst20. There is also increasing evidence of the role of inflammatory processes in this disease progression19,21–23. The data could also suggest that haploinsufficiency alone is sufficient in some cells to initiate cysts, though this needs further exploration. If there are therapeutic opportunities to intervene in the development of these somatic “second hits” this could potentially be used to delay the onset of disease progression, ideally enough to avoid kidney failure. DNA Damage Response (DDR) pathways, which are initiated by DNA damage, are overexpressed in human ADPKD tissue24,25. There is also experimental evidence that the human-unique polypyrimidine tracts in PKD1 result in the gene being prone to replication stress and DNA damage, through replication fork stalling triggering DNA double-strand breaks and then activation of DNA repair signalling26. Hypertonicity, such as physiologically occurs in the kidney collecting duct where cysts originate, has also been associated with generation of DNA double strand breaks27. Homologous recombination is a highly conserved DNA repair process that is involved in reactivation of stalled replication forks and repair of DNA double-strand breaks28. Errors in homologous recombination are a known mechanism of somatic mutation, notably in breast and ovarian cancer, in which BRCA1 and BRCA2 are key components of homologous recombination pathways28. Interestingly, the majority of pathogenic mutations observed in our study are short deletions, which could be suggestive of errors in homologous recombination29. The sequence context of a number of the variants reported in our study is consistent with microhomology mediated end-joining, which is an alternative DNA repair mechanism that can be triggered in situations of defective homologous recombination28. Interventions that reduce exposure to factors that promote DNA damage may reduce somatic mutation burden and disease progression24.

Recent literature investigating somatic mutation rates in normal tissue across lifespans and species suggests that normal somatic cells accumulate somatic mutations pervasively, at similar rates in both dividing and terminally differentiated cells30. These somatic patterns are conserved across mammals31 and in many cell types32–34, and the somatic mutation patterns observed in individual lymphocytes resembles those from leukaemia35. These studies highlight the importance of endogenous mutation processes that are independent to those incurred during cell division, which further lends evidence to explore the potential of exogenous mutagens driving somatic mutation in ADPKD.

Unique to ADPKD is the presence of six pseudogenes that are highly homologous to PKD1. This remarkable sequence homology has added significant challenges to sequencing the PKD1 gene and also opens up the possibility of gene-conversion events being another mechanism by which mutational rates could be increased in ADPKD36.

The unique aspects of our study include a sample collection technique that allowed systematic application of replicate studies and use of unique molecular barcodes to reduce likelihood of PCR-error impacting results. Compared with other studies, we also collected samples from smaller cysts from external and internal surfaces of the kidney. This adds evidence that somatic mutation is detectable in smaller cysts, which is consistent with the mutation being present at the time of cyst initiation, rather than a secondary driver of cyst progression12. A technical limitation of our study is that the sequencing coverage in PKD1 exon 1 was poor, and that our capture-based sequencing approach may miss structural variants3, low VAF loss of heterozygosity events and deep intronic splice altering variants31, suggesting that there may be additional second hits that we did not identify in our study. The short-read sequencing technique also does not allow for phasing of variants to confirm that the somatic variant is on the alternate allele to the germline-line disease-causing variant. Another major limitation is interpreting data from end-stage kidney, in which it is not possible to determine whether a somatic variant was present at cyst initiation or is a result of secondary end-stage disease processes. Our results add to the evidence that somatic variants are present in over half of cysts, suggesting that they may play a role in cyst pathogenesis. Future research into the causes and consequences of these somatic variants will be essential in developing an improved understanding of cyst pathogenesis in ADPKD.

The pathogenesis of cyst development in ADPKD is complex and reflected by the current gaps in disease understanding despite consistent worldwide research efforts. Our data reflects this complexity, in identifying somatic variants in only a proportion of kidney cysts, despite robust sequencing methods that are developed for low frequency somatic variant detection. Further understanding of disease pathogenesis and the role of somatic variation in ADPKD would be progressed by replicating this study in non-end stage human disease tissue. Further developments in methods for accurate sequencing of DNA from small amounts of epithelial cells will help further delineate the role of somatic mutation in cyst pathogenesis30. Our data shows that somatic mutation has a role in a significant proportion of ADPKD cystic tissue and therapies that can reduce this mutation burden may allow clinically meaningful reduction in disease progression.

Participants were recruited who fulfilled standard clinical criteria for a diagnosis of ADPKD and were planned for a clinically indicated nephrectomy of one or both native kidneys37. All participants provided informed, written consent prior to their procedure. Institutional ethical approval was obtained from the Royal Prince Alfred Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/18/RPAH/726) and this study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Whole kidneys were collected during the operative procedure, immediately after the kidney was removed (Fig. 1 inset A). The kidney was transected and tissue samples were collected from different regions of the kidney. These samples were stored on ice and transferred to the research laboratory. The collected tissue samples were dissected to a discrete, single, whole cyst with the cyst kept intact (Fig. 1 inset B). Cysts with clear fluid, rather than blood-stained fluid, were selected for dissection, to reduce likelihood of contamination from blood-derived cells. The tissue surface was washed with Ca/Mg-free PBS solution prior to dissection and prior to storage. Each whole cyst sample was separately transferred to a storage tube, minced with scissors in the tube so the tissue and cyst fluid was retained, and cryopreserved in Fetal Calf Serum 10% DMSO. The sample was cooled slowly to −70 degrees Celsius.

DNA was extracted from each whole cyst sample using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit. Library preparation was performed using the Agilent SureSelect XT kit (SureSelect XT HS Target Enrichment System for Illumina Paired-End Multiplexed Sequencing Library Version C2 using Enzymatic Fragmentation) with custom capture probes designed to avoid the PKD1 pseudogenes (acknowledgment to Dr Peter Harris, Mayo Clinic, Agilent Design ID 3104241)38,39. As part of the library preparation, a 10-nucleotide single-plex Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) was ligated to each DNA fragment. Two duplicate libraries were independently prepared from each DNA sample, and the prepared libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq instrument using 150 bp paired-end reads at the University of New South Wales Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics, UNSW Sydney, Australia.

Raw data was processed via a custom bioinformatics pipeline as follows. UMI sequences were extracted from the raw FASTQ files and appended to the read name using UMI-tools Version 1.0.114). 3’ adaptor sequences were then removed from the sequencing reads using Cutadapt V3.3. Adaptor-trimmed FASTQ files were then aligned to the hs37d5 reference sequencing using BWA-MEM (V0.7.10-r789) and Novosort (V1.03.01). PCR duplicates were then grouped and deduplicated to one read per group via UMI-tools. Deduped BAM files were then realigned and recalibrated using GATK IndelRealign (V3.3) and GATK BQSR V3.3. Variant calling for single nucleotide variants and indels was performed using FreeBayes due to its strengths in calling low-frequency variants16,40. Variant calling was set to be deliberately permissive, with variants called with minimum base quality of 20, minimum mapping quality 0 (because of the PKD1 pseudogenes, as discussed in ref3) and minimum number of alternate supporting reads of 1. The resultant Variant Call File (VCF) was annotated using VEP v87. Variant interpretation was then performed using the Seave variant interpretation platform41. Initial analyses indicated that variant quality parameters alone were not able to distinguish likely true variants from likely false-positive variants. Thus all predicted high impact coding variants (predicted loss of function variants) in PKD1 and PKD2 were manually reviewed in IGV42 by a variant analyst, with each sample compared to the replicate data and multiple controls. Medium impact variants (predicted non-truncating) in PKD1 and PKD2 which were absent in population databases, had damaging in silico scores and were supported by >/=3 reads were also manually reviewed. Manual review sought to filter out variants called in regions of strand bias, with low base quality, when the variant called was enriched towards the ends of reads or in regions with clusters of low base quality calls or low mapping quality reads, particularly in repetitive or low-complexity genomic regions43. All potential variants were manually reviewed by two analysts who had to come to agreement. Variants that passed manual review, that were present in both replicates and absent in controls were marked as ‘candidate variants’ for further consideration. The type and rate of sequencing errors are highly influenced by DNA extraction, library preparation and the sequence context, fluctuating from 0.01 to 0.001%18. Thus, for all ‘candidate variants’ a position specific noise-threshold was calculated in order to account for the site-specific error profile at each specific genomic site where each variant was called. An error profile was calculated for each genomic site with a potential variant, to account for the local sequence context of that specific site. For example, the C > T deamination artefact is more pronounced in the CpG sequence context, and there may be other subtle biases that are revealed with very deep sequencing (despite our use of UMIs to mitigate this risk). We reasoned that these subtle biases would result in the same error being observed in other cysts and other patients, thus we used the other cyst samples as negative controls. This position-specific error profile was calculated by using the remaining cyst samples as aggregate controls44. The region was interrogated in IGV across all 48 samples and the read depth and number of unique alternate reads was recorded at the site for each sample (Supplementary Fig. 3). This data was used to calculate an error rate estimate for each sample at each variant site, and the average error rate across all samples for each site with a ‘candidate variant’ was calculated (error rate estimate = number of unique alternate read at position/total number of reads at position). A binomial test was then performed to calculate the likelihood of a base calling error accounting for the ‘candidate variant’ using Rstudio statistical software. The p value for a call that was present only in the sample and replicate and absent in all other samples was calculated - only variants less than this value (p < 2.2 × 10−16) were considered true somatic variants.

The library preparation, sequencing and bioinformatic analysis was performed in replicate for each cyst sample (Fig. 1). Variant pathogenicity was classified according to ACMG-AMP guidelines45.

To orthogonally validate these candidate somatic variants, the DNA extracted from each patient’s cyst samples were also sequenced using an alternate library preparation method. There was insufficient DNA remaining for cyst samples RPA011KIc, RPA012KH and RPA005KL6. Twist Library Preparation Kit with duplex unique molecular identifiers (Enzymatic Fragmentation and Twist Universal Adapter System 101058 and Twist Adapter System 101311) was used to process DNA extracted from the kidney cysts. The same Agilent custom capture probes as described above were used given the issue of homology to the PKD1 pseudogenes. Samples were also sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq as described above. UMI de-duplication was performed using an in-house pipeline. Raw sequencing data were first processed with fastp v0.23.446 to extract UMIs (5 bp index with a 2 bp spacer). The processed reads were then mapped to the GRCh37 reference genome using bwa-mem v0.7.1747 with -Y settings. Finally, UMI de-duplication was carried out using mark-dups v1.1.4 from the HMF Tools suite (https://github.com/hartwigmedical/hmftools/releases/tag/mark-dups-v1.1.4) with settings for UMI duplex sequencing, -umi_enabled and -umi_duplex. We manually inspected each candidate somatic variant (identified through method above) in IGV to determine their VAF.

Short-read whole genome sequencing on DNA extracted from peripheral blood was performed on all patients to identify their germline heterozygous disease-causing variant, as per previously published methods48. Only patients with a disease-causing, germline variant were included in the study.

To ensure any identified somatic variants in the cysts were not actually consistent with low-level germline mosaic variants, the peripheral blood DNA was also processed via the deep-sequencing pipeline outlined above (Agilent library preparation with Agilent custom capture probes, and deep-sequencing via Illumina MiSeq) in order to obtain high-depth sequencing data. All regions where somatic variants were identified in the cyst tissue were interrogated in the germline DNA data, to ensure low frequency variants consistent with germline mosaicism were not present.

In developing the method employed in this study, previously published methods for sample collection were trialled. This included insertion of a needle into a single cyst cavity, aspirating its contents, washing the cyst out and then incubating the cyst with PBS/EDTA solution in order to remove the epithelial cells from the basement membrane, while keeping the cyst intact throughout the entire process7,11. We encountered challenges in reliable reproducibility of this method in consecutive cyst samples, with success of the method highly dependent on the position of the cysts. In our hands, the method often failed due to the coalescing nature of cysts in an end-stage kidney and therefore introducing a needle into the target cyst and inadvertently entering a second, neighbouring cyst.

The previously published method of dissecting the cyst ‘tops’ and scraping the inside surface of the cyst with a scalpel in order to collect the internal cyst cells was also trialled7, however adequate cell number for DNA extraction and subsequent library preparation was not reproducibly able to be obtained.

The method described in this study was subsequently developed in order to utilise a more reproducible method that provided consistently adequate DNA quantity for downstream sequencing (including replicate samples).

Custom capture probes were designed to avoid the PKD1 pseudogenes (acknowledgment to Dr Peter Harris, Mayo Clinic, Agilent Design ID 3104241)(Supplementary Fig. 6). These probes were targeted to all exonic and some intronic regions of PKD1 and PKD2. The bed file for these target regions is also included in Supplementary Table 2.

Per-exon depth of coverage was generated for each sample and replicate sequenced after the Agilent library preparation technique, using GATK DepthOfCoverage tool v1.0.1.
