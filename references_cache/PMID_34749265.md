Zaire Ebola virus (EBOV) is a member of the Filoviridae family. Infection with 
EBOV causes Ebola virus disease (EVD) characterized by excessive inflammation, 
lymphocyte death, coagulopathy, and multi-organ failure. In 2019, the 
FDA-approved the first anti-EBOV vaccine, rVSV-EBOV-GP (Ervebo® by Merck). This 
live-recombinant vaccine confers both prophylactic and therapeutic protection to 
nonhuman primates and humans. While mechanisms conferring prophylactic 
protection are well-investigated, those underlying protection conferred shortly 
before and after exposure to EBOV remain poorly understood. In this review, we 
review data from in vitro and in vivo studies analyzing early immune responses 
to rVSV-EBOV-GP and discuss the role of innate immune activation in therapeutic 
protection.

Zaire Ebola virus (EBOV) is a negative-single stranded RNA virus belonging to the Filoviridae family[1,2]. Infection with EBOV virus causes Ebola virus disease (EVD), which is characterized by aberrant activation of myeloid cells resulting in dysregulated inflammatory responses, lymphopenia, hemorrhaging, and multi-organ failure in both humans and nonhuman primates (NHPs)[2–5]. Several strains of EBOV have been identified since the original discovery of the Mayinga strain in 1976[6–8]. This initial outbreak incurred over 300 cases with a case fatality rate (CFR) of 88%. The Kikwit strain later identified in 1995 exhibited a similar CFR (79%) while the recent Makona strain responsible for the largest EBOV outbreak in history (2013-2016) caused over 28,000 cases at a remarkably low ~40% CFR[7,8]. Smaller outbreaks of EBOV Makona in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) vary in CFR from 42% to 66%[7]. CFRs range from 40-90%, for other species of Ebola virus including Sudan (SUDV) and Bundibugyo (BDBV) Ebola viruses[6,7]. In contrast, Tai Forest (TAFV), Reston (RESTV) and Bombali (BOMV) Ebola viruses are not associated with significant or any human disease.

The 2013-2016 West Africa epidemic of EBOV Makona accelerated the development and evaluation of numerous anti-EBOV vaccines[9,10]. Efforts have been further propelled by the second largest EBOV outbreaks in the DRC between 2018-2020. The threat of EBOV is underscored by the fact that the two most recent outbreaks in February-March 2021 in the DRC and Guinea were most likely caused by a persistent source of EBOV infection an survivor of the 2013-2016 epidemic[11,12]. rVSV-EBOV-GP (Ervebo™ by Merck), a live attenuated recombinant vaccine where the G protein of vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) is replaced by the EBOV glycoprotein (GP), is the only FDA-approved anti-EBOV vaccine to date[13–15]. rVSV-EBOV-GP shows complete protection for NHPs—the gold standard for modeling EBOV infection—when administered as little as 7 days prior to lethal challenge with EBOV[16–20]. Importantly, this vaccine provides partial (~50-67%) protection to NHPs when administered a few days before or hours after EBOV exposure, presenting a unique, dual function as both a therapeutic and prophylactic vaccine[21–24]. rVSV-EBOV-GP received FDA approval in December 2019 after demonstrating 100% protection in phase III clinical and ring vaccination trials without severe adverse events[13,25–29].

Mechanisms underlying protection provided by rVSV-EBOV-GP when administered shortly before or after challenge remain poorly defined. Prophylactic vaccination studies (> 1 week before exposure) show strong evidence for humoral immunity in mediating protection. In contrast, both in vitro and in vivo studies of therapeutically administered rVSV-EBOV-GP suggest a role for innate immunity and NK cell-mediated immunity. Here we provide an integrated overview of studies that examined rapid (i.e., therapeutic) protection provided by rVSV-EBOV-GP and discuss putative mechanism behind the robust induction of innate immunity.

rVSV-EBOV-GP is a live attenuated, replication-competent vaccine expressing membrane-bound EBOV glycoprotein (mGP). mGP is the only protein expressed on the surface of EBOV and plays a critical role in viral attachment, membrane fusion and release in target cells (e.g., endothelial cells, monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs))[5,30]. Originally used as a model system for studying GP and antibody neutralization, this construct was later optimized as a vaccine and tested in murine models[31–33]. The VSV vector has several advantages including 1) ease of genetic manipulation that permits replacement of the VSV glycoprotein (G) with EBOV mGP, 2) high replication capacity, and 3) ability to propagate in almost all mammalian cells without diminished replication capacity[34–37]. The replacement of VSV-G with EBOV mGP further avoids issues with pre-existing immunity and potential neurovirulence[38–40]. This vaccine was proven safe in simian-human immunodeficient virus (SHIV)-infected NHPs and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)[36,41–44]. The FDA-approved rVSV-EBOV-GP encodes GP from EBOV Kikwit[13].

Preclinical and clinical trials leading up to licensure, as well as the prophylactic protection and efficacy conferred by rVSV-EBOV-GP, have been extensively reviewed elsewhere[6,45,46]. In brief, preclinical studies in NHPs show complete protection against multiple strains of EBOV when 107 PFU of rVSV-EBOV-GP is administered 28 days prior to infection via the aerosol, mucosal or intramuscular route[1,16–20,47]. Due to high within-species conservation, rVSV-EBOV-GP exhibits efficacious cross-protection against other strains of EBOV, including Makona and Mayinga, but not other species[48,49]. Previous constructs of rVSV-EBOV-GP expressing the EBOV Mayinga GP also conferred complete protection against EBOV Kikwit in NHPs[50]. This protection is accompanied by the detection of anti-EBOV-GP antibodies and proliferating memory T and B cell subsets, although CD8 T cells appear to be dispensable for protection[50]. More recent studies show that protection can be achieved with a dose as little as 10 PFU, although vaccine doses ≤104 PFU are associated with transient EBOV viremia upon challenge[16].

To date, over one dozen clinical trials have been conducted to evaluate immunogenicity of rVSV-EBOV-GP[6,46]. Most trials were initiated during the 2013-2016 West Africa epidemic. Phase I and II clinical trials in the United States, Africa and Europe determined immunogenicity and safety in children, adolescents, non/pregnant adults, and immunocompromised individuals[42,51–57]. A single vaccination of rVSV-EBOV-GP with a dose ranging from 300,000 PFU to 10 million PFU induced high levels of anti-EBOV-GP IgG and neutralizing antibodies that persisted in the majority (>88%) of subjects for two years[42,51–57]. Antibody titers and diversity were positively associated with vaccine dose[56–59]. EBOV-GP-specific CD8 T cells and Th1 cytokines (e.g., interferon (IFN)-γ, IL-2) were also induced early after vaccination in a dose-dependent manner[60]. Vaccination was frequently associated with arthralgia, myalgia, fatigue and fever, suggesting robust innate immune response to vaccination; however, these symptoms typically resolved within a week[42,56,57,60].

Two phase II/III open-label, randomized controlled clinical trials were conducted in West Africa during the epidemic with cohorts of high-risk individuals, including healthcare workers (HCWs) and close-contacts (CC) of laboratory-confirmed EVD patients (children, adults)[25–29,61,62]. Using a ring vaccination strategy, groups of CCs or HCWs at high risk were either vaccinated immediately with 2 × 107 PFU of rVSV-EBOV-GP or 3 weeks later[25–29]. Vaccine efficacy was 100% in HCWs and immediately vaccinated CCs while several cases were reported in the delayed group of CCs. The final phase III clinical trial conducted in Canada, the U.S. and Spain confirmed the safety, immunogenicity and durability of rVSV-EBOV-GP in over 1,000 individuals followed for 24 months[63]. During the 2018-2020 EBOV outbreak in the DRC, rVSV-EBOV-GP was administered to over 100,000 high-risk individuals and HCWs[64]. Efficacy was estimated to be ~97.5% with most cases occurring in unvaccinated individuals[64]. These data led to the approval of rVSV-EBOV-GP by the FDA in December 2019 and is currently manufactured by Merck under the name of Ervebo®[13].

The correlate of protection associated with prophylactic administration of rVSV-EBOV-GP is believed to be primarily humoral immunity[6,50]. Studies in NHPs demonstrated that antibodies are necessary for rVSV-EBOV-GP-mediated protection since animals who failed to generate antibodies because of CD4 T cells depletion prior to vaccination succumb to wild-type EBOV challenge. These observations agree with observations that CD4 T cell loss and low EBOV-specific antibody levels are associated with fatality while durable antibodies and T cell responses are associated with survival in humans[65–71]. Furthermore, monoclonal antibodies targeting EBOV GP, including the trivalent Zmapp cocktail and FDA-approved mAb114 (Ebanga™, Ridgeback Biotherapeutics®) are highly efficacious in preventative and post-exposure settings for NHPs and humans in a clinical trials[14,15,72–75]. A detailed review of adaptive correlates of protection conferred by rVSV-EBOV-GP can be obtained from previous publications[6,76].

Vaccination with rVSV-EBOV-GP also provides rapid and post-exposure protection to vaccinated NHPs in the absence of detectable antibody responses[21–24,77]. Marzi et. al. showed that 67% (2 of 3) of NHPs vaccinated 3 days before challenge survived, with the one of the survivor becoming transiently viremic[23]. Anti-EBOV-GP IgG were not detectable at appreciable levels until 7 DPC. Similarly, a recent study reported 40% protection in NHPs vaccinated with rVSV-EBOV-GP one day before lethal EBOV Kikwit challenge. In these animals, anti-EBOV antibodies were not detected until 6 DPC[24]. Vaccine-mediated rapid protection was complemented by consecutive doses of the MIL77E monoclonal antibody cocktail at days 3, 6, and 9 DPV, which provided complete protection to vaccinated animals[24,78]. In terms of post-exposure, rVSV-EBOV-GP expressing the EBOV Mayinga GP provides 50% protection in rhesus macaques vaccinated 20-30 minutes after challenge with a lethal dose of EBOV Kikwit[22]. Survivors exhibited transient viremia and anti-GP IgM as earlier as 6 days post challenge (DPC). Anti-GP IgG and neutralizing antibodies appeared between 10-14 DPC. Interestingly, both survivors and nonsurvivors experienced T cell lymphopenia and increases in the levels of NK cells and B cells. Subsequent studies demonstrated that the FDA-approved construct of rVSV-EBOV-GP bearing the Kikwit GP could protect 33% and 67% of NHPs vaccinated either 1 or 24 hours after exposure to EBOV Makona, respectively[21]. Survivors lacked the highly elevated and sustained levels of types I and II interferons seen in fatal counterparts. Anti-GP antibodies did not appear until 7-10 DPC. rVSV-EBOV-GP has also provided protection to humans with accidental needle-sticks or high-risk exposure to EBOV in clinical settings when administered roughly 48 hours post-exposure[79,80].

Additional studies showed that rVSV-SUDV-GP provided 100% protection to NHPs 20-30 minutes after lethal SUDV challenge[81]. Moreover, rVSV-MARV-GP protects 67% of EBOV-challenged animals[21]. These data suggest that innate immune responses induced by the VSV backbone and GP likely provide a limited but critical window of time (the “therapeutic window”) for vaccinees to develop adaptive responses. Indeed, VSV is known to induce strong innate responses as a prophylactic and therapeutic vaccine for a number of viral infections including Marburg, influenza A, Andes, Zika, and SARS-CoV-2 viruses[77,82–88]. Replication is needed for a robust response. For instance, a single round of rVSV replication (ΔG) is sufficient to induce innate responses sufficient for B cell and cytotoxic T cell responses comparable to a replication-competent vector bearing viral antigens (e.g., HIV envelope)[89,90]. However, guinea pigs vaccinated with a replication incompetent version of rVSV-EBOV-GP induce similar interferon responses but lower levels of protective neutralizing antibodies compared to counterparts receiving replication-competent rVSV-EBOV-GP[91]. These findings suggest that viral vector replication is important for inducing cellular and humoral immune responses to the engineered antigen[92,93].

VSV infection leads to abundant antigen presentation and type I IFN signaling following detection by RNA sensors (e.g., RIG-I-like receptor, RLR). Ex vivo infection of human PBMCs with VSV induces robust upregulation of genes encoding RLRs (e.g., DDX58, IFIH1) and canonical ISGs (e.g., BST2, MXA, OAS2) implicated in viral control[94–97]. Likewise, high levels of IFN-α, as well as IL-15, TNF-α and inflammatory cytokines, are produced. This agrees with the robust antiviral signatures seen in vaccinated humans and NHPs following the first week of vaccination, as well as transient rVSV viremia and side effects [20,21,24,50,51,55,57,60,79,80,95,98,p.8,99–101]. Transcriptional signatures is punctuated by upregulated ISGs that detect cytosolic viral RNA (e.g., DDX58, DDX60, TLR8) and restrict viral replication (e.g., EIF2AK2, IFIT2, MX1, OAS2)[98,99]. These innate responses may also be independent of the surface antigen presented. For instance, a previous study in mice found that although an rVSV-HBV-MS construct was unable to express large quantities of HBV middle envelope surface glycoprotein (MS) on the vector surface, it induced comparable innate and adaptive responses in mice[92].

Early induction of inflammatory and antiviral responses by VSV (e.g., IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-15, TNF-α) vaccination also suggests activation of cytotoxic NK cells as indicated by increased expression of [21,23,60,95,100,101]. NK cells eliminate virally infected cells through cell lysis following activation by type I IFN, IL-15 and TNF-α produced by activated APCS (e.g., macrophages, DCs)[102–108]. NK cells subsequently produce IFN-γ to augment the antiviral response. In EBOV infection, significant increase in activated NK cells are associated with survival in NHPs and humans [22,p.,109,110]. Although the role of NK cells in vaccine-mediated protection is unclear, studies in mice, NHPs and humans point towards a critical role for these innate cells. Mice depleted of NK cells prior to vaccination with Ebola virus like particles (eVLP) bearing GP and VP40 succumb to infection[111]. This role of NK cells agrees with previous in vitro studies showing that stimulation of NK cells with eVLPs leads to clearance of infected dendritic cells in a NKp30-dependent manner and release of TNF-α, IFN-γ and IL-12[112]. Furthermore, NK-depleted and rVSV-EBOV-GP-vaccinated mice exhibit an accelerated time to death compared to NK-competent counterparts[113]. Examination of rVSV-EBOV-GP-stimulated murine NK cells revealed cytotoxic capacity and robust IFN-γ secretion.

Humans vaccinated with rVSV-EBOV-GP also show evidence of robust innate immune responses highlighted by NK cell activation and DC mobilization. Recent studies in human vaccinated with 3M or 20M PFU of rVSV-EBOV-GP indicated a strong induction of IFN-α, IFN-γ, TNF-α and CXCL10 (IP-10) as well as increased frequency of activated (NKG2D+CD56dim, CD38+CD56dim) NK cells within 1-3 DPV[55,60,100]. CXCL10 is a chemoattractant for CXCR3+ cells like DCs, macrophages and NK cells[114]. Indeed, frequency of activated IFN-producing plasmacytoid DCs and inflammatory CD16+ monocytes as well as IFN-α levels were significantly elevated by 1-3 DPV and accompanied by an antiviral transcriptional signature (e.g., RSAD2, USP18, ISG15, IFI6)[100,101]. This innate immune activation signature persisted for 2-4 weeks post vaccination in a dose-dependent manner[55,60,100] and correlated with vaccines dose [115]. Anti-EBOV-GP IgG responses correlated with this transcriptional signature, notably CXCL10 levels at 3 DPV and CD56dim NK cell levels at 1 DPV, suggesting a role for early NK cell activation in inducing humoral immunity[100]. Similar transcriptional antiviral responses are seen in NHPs vaccinated with 20M PFU at 3-7 DPV[6,98,p.8,99].

Inflammatory and antiviral responses may be modulated in an antigen-specific manner by mGP, which is a potent activator of non/immune cells, to induce NK cell activity[116–121]. This is distinct from sGP, which disrupts endothelial integrity, antagonizes APC activation & migration, and acts as a molecular decoy for anti-GP antibodies[122–128]. In vitro, mGP induces the release and transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12p70, TNF-α) and type I IFN in activated APCs in a TLR4-dependent pathway that activates NFKB and ERK signaling[120,121,129–132]. This agrees with transcriptional studies demonstrating upregulation of TLR4 and genes involved in NFKB-mediated inflammation (e.g., NFKBIA, MYD88) and antiviral defense (e.g., IRF3, STAT1/2) at 3-7 DPV in vaccinated NHPs[99]. The mGP-TLR4 interaction in monocytes and macrophages further facilitates viral infection by upregulating host surface receptors, which could lead to increased antigen processing and presentation [133]. Interaction of mGP with TLR4 or TIM-1 expressed on CD4 T cells also leads to inflammatory cytokine release (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-12p70, TNF-α)[134–137]. These data are supported by data from mice where immunization with purified and solubilized mGP result in significant increase in the levels of type I and II IFN, inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF α, IL1B) and chemokines (e.g., MCP1) within 24 hours[138]. This occurs in parallel with activation and migration of macrophages to draining lymph nodes prior to lethal EBOV challenge. The efficacy of this regime has not been assessed in NHPs but supports an antigenic role for GP in priming innate responses through TLR4-mediated activation of APCs. Similarly, humans vaccinated with adjuvanted eVLPs produce robust responses to GP[139].

rVSV-EBOV-GP is a safe and effective vaccine with both prophylactic and therapeutic uses. Rapid therapeutic protection relies on innate immunity engendered by antigen-specific and -nonspecific immunity. Data from several studies suggest a key role for VSV replication in inducing the antiviral and inflammatory cytokine milieu (e.g., type I IFN, IFN-γ, IL-12p70, IL-15, TNF-α) necessary for APC and NK cell activation. Activated NK cells may serve as potent cytotoxic cells for the removal of virally infected cells, providing the “therapeutic window” for adaptive immune activation. GP may also contribute to antiviral and inflammatory responses through TLR4 signaling and mobilizing APCs for later activation of antibody-producing B cells. However, additional studies are necessary to understand the rapid-protection mediated by rVSV-EBOV-GP. Questions that remain to be answer include understanding 1) the role of rVSV replication in therapeutic and prophylactic protection; 2) the influence of NK cells in early control of EBOV replication and anti-EBOV memory; 3) dissection of rVSV-mediated and GP-mediated innate responses; 4) vectored-GP vs native GP responses; and 5) immune signatures associated with successful outcomes from rapid therapeutic protection. Characterization of this vaccine’s rapid therapeutic and prophylactic protection will be critical for furthering our understanding of anti-EBOV immunity and developing the rVSV platform for other viral diseases.
