# Rescue of stereocilia architecture and hearing function by AAV-CIB2 and AAV-CIB3 in a mouse model of recessive deafness DFNB48.
**Authors:** Rehman S, Giese APJ, Dragich AK, Riazuddin S, Frolenkov GI, Ahmed ZM
**Journal:** Mol Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymthe.2026.06.041](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymthe.2026.06.041)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in the calcium and integrin-binding protein 2 (CIB2) are among the leading causes of nonsyndromic prelingual hearing loss (DFNB48). Loss of Cib2 in mice causes severe stereocilia bundle defects, impaired mechanoelectrical transduction (MET), and profound hearing loss. To determine whether a critical window of opportunity exists to restore the stereocilia architecture and to rescue hearing function, we developed and investigated the effect of adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene delivery in Cib2 mutant mice. A single administration of the AAV-Cib2 vector was performed via semicircular canal injections in Cib2ko/ko mice at several different ages within the early postnatal period. We observed sustained, but partial, recovery of hearing function and restoration of stereocilia architecture in the apical to middle cochlear turns in mice having received AAV-Cib2 between postnatal day 0 (P0) and P4. Both CIB2 and a closely related protein family member, CIB3, are known to bind to TMC1/2 proteins and regulate MET function. Thus, we also investigated the impact of human CIB3 (hCIB3) overexpression on hearing in Cib2ko/ko mice. Intriguingly, administration of AAV-CIB3 gene via semicircular canal injections at P0-P1 also restored hearing function at frequencies corresponding to the apical to middle cochlear turns in Cib2ko/ko mice. Taken together, our data provide a critical timeline in which stereocilia architecture and hearing function can be restored in CIB2-deficient mice and lay the foundation for future gene therapy trials for DFNB48 patients.

Hearing loss is the most prevalent sensory deficit and affects 25% of the population worldwide1–3. In addition to the underlying hearing loss, affected individuals often suffer from additional impairments like cognitive deficits, diminished quality of life, social isolation, and dementia4,5. Although hearing aids and cochlear implants restore some hearing functions in many cases, there is currently only one FDA-approved therapy that treats the underlying biological cause of a subtype of hereditary hearing loss, DFNB9. The AAV-based OTOF gene therapies, including the recently approved lunsotogene parvec-cwha®, have been shown to produce significant and sustainable hearing improvement in children with DFNB9-associated deafness6–8. Among the known genetic causes of autosomal recessively inherited hearing loss, pathogenic variants in CIB2, encoding a calcium and integrin-binding protein 2, are among the common causes of deafness (DFNB48) in certain populations3,9–11. The CIB2 gene encodes a calcium and integrin-binding protein 2 (CIB2), which is expressed in the mammalian auditory hair cells and binds to the pore-forming subunits of the mechanoelectric transduction (MET) complex, TMC1 and TMC2. We have previously shown that deafness-causing CIB2 missense variants disrupt the interactions between CIB2 and TMCs12. In the auditory hair cells, CIB2 is required for proper localization of TMC1/2 at the stereocilia tips13. Interestingly, over-expression of CIB3, another CIB family member with similar structural domains as CIB2, can restore MET currents in the early postnatal mouse cochlear hair cells lacking CIB213. Structural analysis studies revealed that both CIB2 and CIB3 are likely auxiliary subunits of hair cell MET channels13,14.

Besides MET function, our studies also established that CIB2 deficiency results in abnormal growth of the transducing shorter row stereocilia in the hair bundle, while sparing the non-transducing tallest row stereocilia of cochlear hair cells9,12,15. The staircase pattern of the stereocilia bundle is highly conserved across all vertebrate hair cells and is crucial for effective transmission of force across tip links between stereocilia of neighboring rows, which in turn is essential for normal mechanotransduction and sound perception16. Stereocilia staircase patterning is a multi-step process and is orchestrated through spatiotemporal regulation of several protein complexes17–24. During development, nascent stereocilia in rodent auditory hair cells elongate sequentially to form the graded bundle by approximately P4 in outer hair cells (OHCs) and by ~P18 in inner hair cells (IHCs)25,26. Although the precise mechanism and spatiotemporal regulation of CIB2-mediated stereocilia patterning is still elusive, CIB2 represents an important molecule with a role in multiple aspects of hair cell development and function.

With the well-recognized role of CIB2 in hearing function and capitalizing on the advancements in developing potential biological treatments targeting pathogenic variants27–29, in the present study, we seek to determine the critical window of time amenable to intervention to restore the stereocilia architecture, MET, and hearing function in Cib2 mutant mice (Cib2ko/ko and Cib2KI/KI), preclinical animal models for human DFNB48 deafness. Homozygous Cib2 knockout mice are reported to have no detectable hearing nor conventional MET response in the auditory hair cells but have intact vestibular function9,12,15. We also previously reported a mouse model carrying the human deafness-related Cib2 variant (Cib2F91S knock-in)12. Similar to Cib2ko, the Cib2F91S/F91S mice (a.k.a. Cib2KI/KI) are profoundly deaf, and their auditory hair cells generate no MET responses to sound while maintaining appropriate localization of mutant CIB2 within the stereocilia12. Here, we also further investigate the functional redundancy between CIB2 and CIB3 proteins in modulating auditory function. For these purposes, we performed unilateral posterior semicircular canal injection of either Cib2 or CIB3 encapsulated in adeno-associated virus (AAV) capsids in mice lacking functional CIB2 (Cib2ko/ko and Cib2F91S/F91S) at various early postnatal ages. AAVs are the preferred vector of choice for inner ear gene delivery due to a favorable safety profile, high transduction capacity, excellent tropism for hair cells, and long-term gene expression of the transgene30.

We chose to use the AAV2.Anc80L65 viral capsid for delivery to evaluate gene therapy in Cib2 mutant mice, as this capsid transduces IHCs and OHCs with high efficiency at early postnatal age10,31. The size of the longest mouse Cib2 transcript (NM#019686.6) is less than 1 kb, making it ideal for an AAV-mediated delivery approach. We designed an AAV2-Anc80L65 vector containing murine full-length Cib2 transcript along with a GFP reporter (Anc80.CIB2.GFP) driven by a CMV promoter (Figure 1A). In a separate construct, we also inserted WPRE woodchuck hepatitis virus regulatory element downstream to enhance transgenic expression (Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE)15. Finally, as a control, we also generated an AAV vector containing only a GFP reporter (Anc80.GFP; Figure 1A; Table S1).

To evaluate the efficiency of these constructs to induce expression of full-length gene from the delivered exogenous cDNA, we transduced HEK293T cells and evaluated the expression of CIB2 using RT-PCR and Western blot analyses (Figure 1B–D). Although transduction with Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE resulted in a significantly higher amount of Cib2 mRNA as compared with Anc80.CIB2.GFP construct (Figure 1D), comparable steady-state levels of CIB2 protein were observed from both constructs (Figure 1B–C). To confirm the functional activity of the CIB2 protein encoded by the exogenous Cib2 gene, we transduced (dose: ~7E+12 gc/ml) organ of Corti explant cultures generated from control and Cib2ko/ko mice (Figure 1E). Cib2ko/ko mice completely lack mechanotransduction (MET) function and do not uptake channel-permeable FM1–43 dye32. Organ of Corti tissues from wild-type and Cib2ko/ko mice were dissected and explanted at postnatal day 2 (P2) and transduced with either control or Cib2 gene-containing virus constructs 24 hours later. Two days after transduction, these explants were briefly exposed to FM1–43 dye to check for functional MET channels. As anticipated, Cib2ko/ko explant culture transduced with Anc80.GFP control did not uptake FM1–43 dye (Figure 1E), consistent with no recovery of the MET apparatus. As more sensory hair cells were positive for FM1–43 dye after infection with Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE as compared to Anc80.CIB2.GFP-transduced cultures (Figure 1E), therefore, we selected Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE for in vivo studies.

To evaluate the potential functional ototoxicity of Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE, we injected the construct (4E+12 gc/ml) into the inner ear of P0–P1 wild-type mice through the posterior semicircular canal. Hearing tests were conducted by auditory brainstem response (ABR) measurements at multiple ages (P16, P30, and P45), followed by assessment of transduction efficiency and cochlear structure preservation by immunostaining and high-resolution confocal imaging at P45 (Figure 2A). We observed comparable ABR thresholds in injected mice and uninjected littermates at all evaluated ages (Figure 2B). To assess cellular ototoxicity conferred by Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE, we quantified the number of OHCs and IHCs by whole-mount labeling of the organ of Corti and observed no differences between injected and control mice at P45 (Figure S1). Finally, to determine transduction efficiency, we quantified the percentage of hair cells (identified by anti-MYO7A antibody) with GFP expression (Figure 2C–D). Similar to prior studies31, almost 100% of IHCs in all three cochlear turns were transduced, while on average 20–40% OHCs were GFP positive (Figure 2D). These results demonstrate that Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE induced no ototoxicity and thus is suitable for efficacy studies in Cib2ko/ko mice.

To define the temporal requirements of CIB2 expression postnatally to orchestrate organization of the stereocilia staircase pattern, we injected Cib2ko/ko mice with Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE or control (Anc80.GFP) capsids at three different postnatal ages followed by ABR analysis and high-resolution imaging of cochlear stereocilia bundles (Figure 3A). We were able to detect the expression of the transgene two days after injection (Figure S2A). When administered at P0, the Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE construct significantly improved the ABR thresholds of Cib2ko/ko mice at frequencies corresponding to the apical and middle cochlear turns (Figure 3B), as well as improved the stereocilia staircase pattern in both IHCs and OHCs at P16, as compared to control injected mice (Figures 3C–3E and S2B). In mice injected at P0, restoration of stereocilia bundle architecture in the apical and middle cochlear turns was apparent as early as P7 (Figure 3F). In Cib2ko/ko mice injected with Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE at P4, we observed improved ABR thresholds consistent with those injected at P0 (Figure 3B), though there was less consistent restoration of stereocilia bundle architecture in IHCs and OHCs at P16 in these animals (Figures 3C–3E and S3). In contrast, no improvement in ABR thresholds (Figure 3B) or stereocilia bundle morphology was observed in Cib2ko/ko mice injected with Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE at P6 (data not shown). These results suggest a short window for effective treatment in young, postnatal Cib2ko/ko mice to restore stereocilia architecture and hearing function.

Prior studies report that CIB2 localizes to the tips of transducing stereocilia in auditory hair cells, and mice lacking CIB2 (Cib2ko/ko) show rapid degeneration of both inner and outer cochlear hair cells32. Therefore, we examined if Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE administration at P0 can prevent cochlear hair cell degeneration and, consequently, sustain hearing function in adult Cib2ko/ko mice. We performed inner ear Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE vector injection via the posterior semicircular canal in Cib2ko/ko mice at P0–P1 and assessed hair cell survival and hearing at multiple ages (Figures 4, 5, and S4). Although we injected the viral particles unilaterally, expression in the contralateral uninjected ear has been reported after administration with the Anc80.L65 capsid31. As a control group, we injected an Anc80.GFP vector in different Cib2ko/ko mice. We observed expression of GFP in all three cochlear turns at P45, with transduction of nearly 100% IHCs and 20–40% OHCs (Figure 4A). Importantly, transduction with Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE vector significantly improved survival of both IHCs and OHCs in Cib2ko/ko mice at P45 when compared to mice injected with the control construct (Anc80.GFP; Figure 4B).

To investigate the longevity of benefit of Cib2 gene therapy in Cib2ko/ko mice, we assessed their auditory function using ABRs from P16 through 6 months of age (Figure 5A–F). Representative ABR waveforms were recorded from a WT, an Anc80.GFP (control)-treated Cib2ko/ko, and an Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE-treated Cib2ko/ko mouse at P16 using 8 kHz tone bursts at incrementally decreasing SPLs from 100 to 10 dB (Figure 5B, right panel). As expected, Cib2ko/ko mice injected with Anc80.GFP (control) did not show any improvement in hearing thresholds (Figure 5B–F). Significant and sustainable rescue of click and pure tone ABR was observed in Cib2ko/ko treated with the Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE vector, which persisted out to the latest time point tested, 24 weeks after injection (Figure 5B–F). Improvement of hearing function varied between individual mice, shown as thin lines representing ABR thresholds of individual injected mice (Figure 5B, left panel). At the 8 kHz pure-tone frequency, Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE-treated Cib2ko/ko mice exhibited ABR thresholds as low as 40 dB (Figure 5B). Partial rescue was observed at 16 kHz, and minimal but temporary improvement in ABR thresholds were observed at 24 and 32 kHz frequencies (Figure 5D–F). As anticipated, expression of the Anc80.L65 capsid was also found in the contralateral ear, corresponding to improved ABR thresholds in the uninjected ear of Cib2ko/ko mice as well (Figure S5). Overall, these results demonstrate significant and persistent rescue of hearing in the Cib2ko/ko mice. Based on the data, we conclude that a single administration of Cib2 gene therapy by the Anc80.L65 viral capsid results in long-term hearing restoration in the apical and middle cochlear turns of Cib2ko/ko mice.

Among the known deafness-causing variants, the CIB2 p.Phe91Ser (p.F91S) founder allele accounts for the majority of cases11. We previously generated a mouse model carrying the human deafness-related Cib2 variant (Cib2F91S knock-in; Cib2KI) and reported that these mice have no detectable sound perception nor conventional MET responses in auditory hair cells despite the presence of tip links and appropriate localization of mutant CIB2 within the stereocilia12. We also reported that the p.F91S pathogenic variant disrupts CIB2 interaction with the components of the hair cell MET complex, TMC1 and TMC232. In the present study, we also tested the impact of Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE on the restoration of hearing function in Cib2F91S/F91S (Cib2KI/KI) mice. We injected Cib2KI/KI pups at P0 with Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE or control vector (Anc80.GFP) through posterior semicircular canal, followed by ABR analyses at P16, P30, and P60 (Figure 6A) and histology (Figure S6). Similar to results obtained in Cib2ko mice, we observed partial but significant improvements in the ABR thresholds at lower frequencies as early as P16, which persisted until P60, the last time point tested (Figure 6A).

Prior studies have shown that both CIB2 and CIB3 proteins have a conserved hydrophobic groove with highly similar structure and amino acid identity and interact with TMC1/2 proteins12,13. In auditory hair cells, endogenous expression of Cib3 is relatively low12,14; however, when overexpressed, CIB3 can restore MET currents in the early postnatal cochlear explant cultures lacking CIB213. Based on these observations, we reasoned that overexpression of human CIB3 (hCIB3) may also restore hearing function in mice lacking functional CIB2. To test this, we developed Anc80.CIB3.GFP.WPRE constructs to overexpress hCIB3 in Cib2ko/ko mouse cochlea (Figure 6B–E). Intriguingly, overexpression of CIB3 via Anc80.CIB3.GFP.WPRE injection at P0–P1 restored persistent hearing function in Cib2ko/ko mice to the same degree as Cib2ko/ko mice treated with the Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE virus (Figure 6B). Also, similar to Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE treatment, administration of Anc80.CIB3.GFP.WPRE significantly improved survival of both IHCs and OHCs in Cib2ko/ko mice at P45 when compared with the control construct (Anc80.GFP)-injected mice (Figure 6C–E). These results demonstrate that overexpression of CIB3 has the ability to compensate for the loss of CIB2, which could be developed as an alternative to restore hearing in subjects with CIB2-related deafness.

Here, we developed and evaluated an AAV-based in vivo gene therapy in Cib2 mutant mice with the goals of i) determining the “point-of-no-return” in restoring stereocilia bundle morphology, ii) demonstrating proof of concept for gene augmentation rescue of Cib2-related deafness in mice, mimicking DFNB48 in humans, and iii) determining the impact of CIB3 overexpression in CIB2-null auditory hair cells on hearing function. We observed that a single administration of the Anc80L65.CIB2.GFP.WPRE vector resulted in robust restoration of stereocilia architecture and sustained recovery of hearing function in Cib2ko/ko mice injected at P0–P1. In contrast, no rescue of stereocilia length, shape, or hearing was observed in Cib2ko/ko mice injected at P6. Intriguingly, Anc80L65.CIB3.GFP.WPRE gene therapy also restored hearing function in Cib2ko/ko mice when injected at P0–P1. Our study provides a tentative timeline in which stereocilia architecture and hearing function can be restored in CIB2-deficient mice and thus lays the foundation for further investigation and development of an AAV-based gene therapy approach for DFNB48 patients.

AAV vectors are a clinically favorable modality for inner ear gene delivery30 and are currently being studied in several clinical trials in humans as potential treatments for hearing loss7,33–35. We utilized the Anc80L65 serotype to package and deliver the full-length Cib2 gene in the inner ear through posterior semicircular canal injections. Similar to prior studies31,36, our AAV constructs efficiently transduced IHCs (nearly 100%) but were less efficient in transducing OHCs (20–40%). Consistent with prior studies37, we did not observe gross adverse impact of our posterior semicircular canal injections to the integrity of cochlear hair cells (Figure 2C). When assessing for potential side effects or ototoxicity caused by the inherently invasive administration procedure, high dosage, or immune response, we observed no overall adverse effect on ABR thresholds in control mice injected with our gene therapy vector (Figure 2B). Additionally, we did not observe any obvious change in mice behavior, overall health, or survival rate. Furthermore, the Cib2-deficient mice that received the gene therapy vectors had improved ABR thresholds persisting throughout 24 weeks post-injection, further ruling out significant adverse impact of Anc80L65 capsid exposure. These observations are in agreement with the prior studies on the use of the Anc80L65 capsid in the inner ear37,38.

Assembly of the auditory hair cell’s stereocilia bundles depends on differential growth of actin, controlled by several regulatory networks during development17–20. The precise stereocilia staircase arrangement facilitates directional and transduction sensitivity of MET channels that are located at the tips of shorter rows of stereocilia39–41. In mice, this stereotyped staircase pattern develops through dynamic and controlled growth of various rows of stereocilia in discrete stages and time intervals during development26. The first two stages of stereocilia development take place prior to birth, establishing the hair bundle staircase in auditory hair bundles of P0.5 mice. In stage III, stereocilia stop lengthening and begin to widen; the final stage 4 occurs as row 1 stereocilia elongate to their terminal heights, while row 2 stereocilia shorten26. In contrast to wild type mice, the stereocilia in the second and third rows of auditory hair cells of Cib2 mutant mice do not shorten and thus fail to develop this precise staircase pattern12. Prior studies have proposed an important role of actin, as well as actin crosslinking proteins like ESPN, FSCN2, and PLS1, in determining the length of stereocilia during early postnatal days26,42–47. Local regulatory mechanisms controlling the actin dynamics, growth, and patterning of stereocilia may require CIB2 at precise stages during development. To define the essential temporal window when CIB2 function is critical for proper stereocilia growth, we injected Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE virus in the inner ear of Cib2 mutant mice at three different early post-natal ages (P0, P4, P6). Results from these studies revealed that functional CIB2 is required before hearing onset in mice (~P12) to restore stereocilia staircase patterning, and intervention at a later age might not be therapeutic. However, further studies using AAV capsids with more efficient transduction rates across all cochlear turns and more developmental ages are needed to precisely define the “point-of-no-return” in restoring stereocilia bundle morphology. In conjunction with the rescue of stereocilia architecture in Cib2ko/ko mice, we also sought to test a gene therapy approach using Cib2ko/ko and Cib2KI/KI models and AAV2.Anc80L65 as a delivery vector could be used to restore hearing function. Consistent with restoration of stereocilia morphology, we also observed significant improvement in ABR thresholds in Cib2ko/ko mice injected with Anc80.CIB2.WPRE construct either at P0 or P4, but not in mice injected at P6. Gene replacement with Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE in Cib2ko/ko mice resulted in significant improvement of ABR thresholds at low frequencies (Figure 5B). However, in some mice, ABR thresholds at higher frequencies deteriorated over time (Figure 5D–F). The variation observed in ABR thresholds in treated mice could be attributed to human error during injection, variability in local titer within the cochlea, or variability in transduction efficiency across all injected animals. These observations indicate the need for further optimization of several parameters, including the use of other viral capsids like PHP.B that are reported to have better transduction rates of both IHCs and OHC48, alternate gene delivery routes (e.g., surgical approach, double fenestration, cisterna magna)49, and viral titer are necessary prior to translating these results to humans. With the current strategy, we did not observe restoration of either hair cell survival or function in mice injected at P6, which implies that human gene therapy for CIB2 mutations likely would have to be conducted prior to birth.

Both CIB2 and CIB3 are known to interact with TMC1 and TMC2 and are essential for the trafficking and retention of these MET channel components at the tips of transducing stereocilia14,50. Although the very low endogenous expression of CIB3 in the auditory hair cells cannot compensate for the lack of CIB2 in DFNB4832, we show here that overexpression of CIB3 in the cochlear hair cells significantly improved ABR thresholds in Cib2ko/ko mice. This observation suggests an interesting and novel potential therapeutic strategy to restore hearing in DFNB48 individuals with non-functional CIB2. For example, using techniques like CRISPR-based transcriptional activation (CRISPRa)51,52, small molecules53,54, or enhancers, one could target promoters to enhance the expression of endogenous CIB3 in cochlear hair cells and restore hearing in DFNB48 individuals without the need for exogenous gene therapy. Enhancing expression of an endogenous gene that is expressed normally at low levels (CIB3) may result in a decreased immunogenic response in humans compared to exogenous gene delivery to CIB2-naive DFNB48 individuals. However, further studies are needed to determine the impact of long-term CIB3 overexpression on hearing, cochlear hair cell physiology, and stereocilia architecture in order to establish this translational approach. In summary, our results suggest that early intervention by AAV-based CIB2/3 gene delivery may be used as a standalone therapy to rescue stereocilia architecture, hair cells’ survival, and hearing function in DFNB48 individuals with biallelic variants in CIB2.

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines, and after the approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Maryland. For gene augmentation studies, we used Cib2ko (MGI:4432101) and Cib2KI (MGI:6721389) mice that were previously reported32. Mice were housed in a facility with 12 hr light on/off cycle and fed a standard mouse diet (after weaning) with water available ad libitum. In vivo, gene delivery was performed in three genotypes (Cib2ko/ko, Cib2K/KI, and Cib2+/+) and at three different ages (P0–1, P4, and P6). We used CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation to euthanize mice.

For exogenous gene delivery purpose, the mouse Cib2 (Ensembl ID: ENSMUST00000041901.7, NCBI accession: CCDS40642) and human CIB3 (Ensembl ID: ENST00000269878.8, NCBI accession: CCDS12340) cDNAs were subcloned into Adeno-associated virus (AAV) backbone plasmid (Table S1). The GFP cDNA was added downstream to the transgene as a reporter to track transduction efficiency and was linked through an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) sequence for independent expression. In some constructs, we also inserted WPRE sequence for stabilization of transgene expression. Sub-cloned genes’ expression was driven by the ubiquitous cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter. After accuracy verification through Sanger sequencing, these constructs were packaged into AAV2.Anc80L65 capsid using University of Maryland Baltimore Viral Vector Core Facility, and titration of the capsids was performed using Addgene standard qPCR method. As control, we also packaged CMV-GFP-WPRE construct. All viral stock titers were as follows: Anc80.GFP: 2.69E+12; Anc80.CIB2.GFP: 7.71E+12; Anc80.CIB2.GFP.WPRE: 4.41E+12; and Anc80.CIB3.GFP.WPRE: 4.71E+12.

HEK 293T cells were cultured in a 100 cm2 culture dish at 37°C in 5% CO2 using 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1x penicillin-streptomycin (Pen-strep) supplemented Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). For transduction, the cell culture media was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and infected with 1 μl of AAV preps. After transduction, cells were incubated in 5% CO2 at 37°C for two days before harvesting protein and RNA extractions.

For protein extraction, cells were washed with 1X PBS and sonicated for 10s in RIPA lysis buffer with protease inhibitor. Proteins were separated on 4–20% Tris-glycine polyacrylamide gel and transferred to methanol-activated polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane for immunoblotting. Primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-CIB2 antibody (Cat# H00010518-A01; Abnova Inc. Atlanta, GA), mouse anti-GFP monoclonal antibody (Cat# G6539; MilliporeSigma Inc. Burlington, MA) and mouse anti-GAPDH monoclonal antibody (Cat# 60004–1-Ig; Proteintech Inc. Rosemont, IL). Secondary antibodies were HRP labeled and the substrate ECL was used to visualize the protein bands.

For realtime semiquantitative PCR (RT-PCR), total RNA was isolated from infected cells using TRIzol reagent and Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kits (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA). A SMARTScribe Reverse Transcriptase kit (Clontech) and oligo-dT primers were used to synthesis cDNA from mRNA. RT-PCR reactions in triplicates were performed using SYBR Green master mix (Qiagen Inc, Germantown, MD) and gene specific primers. Following primers were used to amplify Cib2, GFP and GAPDH: Cib2_F:5’-cgctgagtacgtcgtggagt-3’; Cib2_R: 5’-gttcagctcagggatgacctt-3’; GFP_F:5’-gacgacgggcaactacaagac-3’; GFP_R:5’-tccttgaagtcgatgccctt; GAPDH_F:5’-gcaaactatgccttcaagat; GAPDH_R:5’-atccagctctgacttagtga-3’.

Cochlear explants from Cib2ko/ko and wild-type control mice were dissected at postnatal day 2 (P2), cultured in a glass-bottom Petri dish (MatTek, Ashland, MA), and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Life Technologies) for 2 days at 37°C and 5% CO2. Explant cultures were infected with 1 μl of AAV (conc. ~7E+12 gc/ml) mixed in 200 μl of DMEM without FBS. Forty-eight hours post-transduction, explants were incubated for 10 s with 3 μM FM1–43, washed three times with Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS), and imaged at a 590 nm wavelength using a fluorescence microscope.

Neonatal (P0–1, P4 or P6) mice were anesthetized by hypothermia for 3–5 minutes and were placed on a 4°C ice pack for surgery. Using sterile scissors, a small post-auricular incision was made, and tissue was dissected deep enough to expose the posterior semicircular canal while avoiding the facial nerve. A Nanoliter 2020 injection system (World Precision Instruments) was used to load the virus into a glass micropipette. A total of 1 μl of virus (titers provided above) was injected at the rate of 13 nl/sec. After injection, the wound was closed using 5-0 sutures and disinfected with povidone iodine. Mice were placed on a heating pad at 37°C for recovery. Injected pups were placed in some bedding from the parent cage for 15 mins to minimize the smell of the apparatus used and to avoid rejection by the dam.

Hearing functions of Cib2ko, Cib2KI and wild-type mice at various ages (e.g., P30, 4, 12, and 24 weeks) were evaluated by recording ABR. Mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100mg/kg) and xylazine (10mg/kg) solution in sterile PBS. All ABR recordings were performed in a sound-attenuated chamber using an auditory-evoked potential RZ6-based auditory workstation (Tucker-Davis Technologies, Alachua, FL) with high frequency transducer. Responses to 50-μs duration clicks and 8-, 16-, and 32-kHz tone-bursts were recorded with maximum 100 dB sound pressure level (SPL) sound intensity for each frequency. Thresholds were determined in 5-dB steps of decreasing stimulus intensity until waveforms lost reproducible morphology. TDT system III hardware and BioSigRZ software (Tucker Davis Technology) were used for stimulus presentation and response averaging, starting at the highest level (100 dB SPL) and reducing in 5 or 10 dB increments until no response trace could be observed. ABR thresholds were visually defined by the investigator blinded to the genotypes as the lowest decibel level at which ABR-wave I peak is detectable.

Temporal bones were collected at the ages indicated in figure legends and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C overnight, followed by decalcification with 0.25M EDTA. Cochleae were then sub-dissected by fine trimming with a blade, and the tectorial membrane was removed using fine forceps. For hair cell count and assessment of transduction efficiency, samples were subjected to blocking in 10% normal goat serum in 0.25% Triton X-100 for 1 hr at room temperature. Then samples were immunolabeled with a polyclonal rabbit primary antibody against mouse Myosin VIIA (dilution 1:200; Cat# 25-6790; Proteus Biosciences, Ramona, CA), and the actin cytoskeleton was decorated with phalloidin.

All images were acquired using an LSM 700 laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) using a 63×1.4 NA or 100×1.4 NA oil immersion objectives. Stacks of confocal images were acquired with a Z step of 0.5μm and processed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). Experiments were repeated at least 3 times, using at least 3 different animals.

Temporal bones from Cib2ko, Cib2KI (Cib2F91S), and wild-type mice were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer supplemented with 2 mM CaCl2 overnight at 4°C. Sensory epithelium from each sample was dissected in distilled water, followed by dehydration in a gradient series of ethanol concentrations. Explants were then critical point-dried using liquid CO2, followed by sputter coating with platinum (5 nm, controlled by a film thickness monitor). A field-emission scanning electron microscope (Helios Nanolab 660, FEI) was used to image the hair bundles.

All data shown are as mean, mean±SD, or mean±SEM and all experiments were repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility. We performed a two-tailed unpaired student’s t test to analyze ABR results, and all analysis was done either on Microsoft Excel or GraphPad Prism. Error bars and n values are demonstrated in respective figure panels and their ligands. p< 0.05 is considered statistically significant.
