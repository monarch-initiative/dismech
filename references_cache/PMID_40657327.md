Type 5 diabetes mellitus (T5DM), recently redefined as malnutrition-related diabetes, represents a distinct form of severe insulin-deficient diabetes that arises from chronic undernutrition, particularly during childhood and adolescence. Unlike type 1 and type 2 diabetes, T5DM is neither autoimmune-mediated nor primarily driven by insulin resistance. Instead, it results from impaired pancreatic development and disrupted insulin signaling pathways due to prolonged nutritional deficiencies. This commentary proposes the formal adoption of “malnutrition-related diabetes” as the nomenclature for T5DM to more accurately reflect its etiology and clinical features. The condition is disproportionately prevalent in low and middle income countries, where malnutrition remains widespread and access to advanced diabetes care is limited. Accurate recognition and classification of T5DM are critical for developing appropriate diagnostic criteria, management strategies, and public health policies. Treatment typically focuses on nutritional rehabilitation and cost-effective oral antidiabetic therapies, rather than insulin dependence. Establishing malnutrition-related diabetes as a distinct category will support targeted research, improve patient outcomes, and raise awareness of its growing burden in vulnerable populations. This commentary urges the global health community to recognize malnutrition-related diabetes as a pressing metabolic disorder requiring urgent attention in low resource settings.

Keywords:Diabetes mellitus, Malnutrition, Metabolic disease, Type 5 diabetes, Low and middle income (developing) countries

In April 2025, the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) launched a dedicated working group focused on Type 5 diabetes mellitus (T5DM), a condition primarily induced by malnutrition. This form of diabetes, recognized as severe insulin-deficient diabetes (SIDD), is characterized by significant insulin deficiency resulting from chronic undernutrition, particularly in childhood or adolescence [1]. Unlike Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), which involves “autoimmune” destruction of insulin-producing cells, and Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which is linked to insulin resistance, T5DM arises from impaired pancreatic development due to long-term nutritional deficiencies [2,3]. The condition predominantly affects malnourished adolescents and young adults in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), with 20 to 25 million individuals estimated to be impacted globally, particularly in regions like Asia and Africa [1]. The primary aim of this commentary is to introduce the reclassification of T5DM as malnutrition-related diabetes, emphasizing its distinct pathophysiology and clinical profile. It seeks to raise awareness of this underrecognized condition, which disproportionately affects populations in LMICs, and to highlight the urgent need for tailored diagnostic criteria, management strategies, and public health responses.

Chronic malnutrition induces insulin deficiency through a network of interrelated molecular pathways that impair insulin synthesis, secretion, and action at both the receptor and post-receptor levels. Specifically, chronic malnutrition results in impaired pancreatic β-cell insulin synthesis, reduced insulin receptor binding affinity, disrupted insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1)/phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling, and hepatic insulin resistance. First, protein-calorie malnutrition directly compromises pancreatic β-cell function by reducing the availability of essential amino acids and cofactors required for insulin biosynthesis [4]. In malnourished animal models, pancreatic islets exhibit a markedly blunted insulin secretory response to glucose stimulation [5]. The dose-response curve for glucose-induced insulin release is shifted to the right, indicating that higher glucose concentrations are necessary to trigger insulin secretion. This defect arises primarily from impaired glucose sensing and intracellular signaling within β-cells, rather than from β-cell loss. Specifically, reduced glucose metabolism within the β-cells limits adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, which is essential for insulin granule exocytosis. Under normal conditions, glucose enters β-cells via glucose transporter type 2 (GLUT2) and undergoes glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, increasing the ATP/adenosine diphosphate (ADP) ratio. This rise in ATP leads to closure of ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channels, causing membrane depolarization and subsequent calcium influx through voltage-gated calcium channels, which triggers insulin vesicle release. In protein-deficient states, however, this cascade is disrupted early due to limited substrate availability and mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in reduced calcium influx and diminished insulin secretion [6]. Second, chronic malnutrition alters the insulin receptor (IR) signaling pathway, which is critical for insulin action in peripheral tissues. Normally, insulin binds to IR, a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor, inducing autophosphorylation of its β-subunit and recruitment of insulin receptor substrates (IRS), primarily IRS-1 and IRS-2. These substrates are phosphorylated on tyrosine residues, activating downstream effectors including PI3K and protein kinase B (Akt). In malnourished animals, insulin binding affinity is significantly decreased at both high- and low-affinity receptor sites, thereby impairing receptor activation [7].

Third, beyond receptor binding, chronic malnutrition impairs downstream insulin signaling. A key defect lies in the phosphorylation and activation of IRS-1 and its interaction with PI3K, which is essential for the translocation of glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) to the plasma membrane in muscle and adipose tissues. In malnourished states, despite increased phosphorylation of the IR and IRS-1—possibly due to compensatory peripheral insulin sensitivity—defective PI3K-Akt signaling hinders effective glucose uptake and utilization [8,9]. Fourth, chronic malnutrition disrupts hepatic glucose metabolism. The liver develops resistance to both insulin and glucagon, leading to dysregulation of glucose production and utilization. This hepatic resistance stems from post-receptor defects, rather than changes in receptor number or affinity, and may serve as an adaptive mechanism to preserve energy and protein under nutrient-deficient conditions. The defect likely occurs at the level of IRS-2 or downstream elements such as forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1), a transcription factor that regulates gluconeogenic enzymes. Impaired insulin signaling allows continued expression of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase, promoting gluconeogenesis and contributing to hyperglycemia despite low circulating insulin. Although this adaptation may transiently support metabolic needs, it ultimately reduces insulin-mediated glucose clearance, resulting in systemic glucose intolerance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia in early stages. Over time, sustained metabolic stress leads to β-cell exhaustion and progressive insulinopenia [10,11].

Historically underrecognized and often clinically misclassified as either T1DM or T2DM, T5DM presents a distinct metabolic profile that requires a unique and tailored diagnostic approach. Unlike T1DM, which is characterized by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells, and T2DM, which is defined by insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction, T5DM is primarily driven by severe insulin deficiency resulting from chronic malnutrition. A key diagnostic feature of T5DM is the presence of significant insulin deficiency, but without the autoimmune markers typically found in T1DM, such as anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase (anti-GAD65), anti-insulin antibodies, and islet cell antibodies (anti-ICA). Blood tests in T5DM will show low insulin levels, often accompanied by low or normal C-peptide levels, which reflect an impaired ability of the pancreas to produce insulin in response to glucose stimuli [12]. The diagnosis of T5DM is not solely reliant on biochemical tests but also requires a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s nutritional history and growth patterns, particularly during critical developmental periods such as childhood and adolescence. A history of chronic undernutrition, especially during these key growth windows, is a hallmark of T5DM. In addition to assessing nutritional history, clinicians must evaluate the patient’s body weight, growth velocity, and any evidence of stunted development or delayed milestones, which are commonly observed in malnourished individuals.

Importantly, unlike T2DM, where insulin resistance is the predominant mechanism, patients with T5DM typically present with normal or slightly reduced insulin receptor numbers, rather than the markedly reduced receptor sensitivity seen in T2DM. This lack of insulin resistance, along with the absence of autoimmunity, helps differentiate T5DM from both T1DM and T2DM. Furthermore, the pathophysiology of T5DM is closely linked to a prolonged deficiency of essential amino acids and micronutrients, which are crucial for the proper development and function of β-cells. This nutritional deficiency impairs β-cell maturation, leading to insufficient insulin production and secretion [4]. In this context, clinicians should consider not only the patient’s weight status and growth patterns but also the broader socio-economic context, particularly in regions where malnutrition is prevalent, such as certain parts of Asia and Africa. In these areas, the prevalence of T5DM may be higher due to dietary limitations, poverty, and limited access to healthcare, further emphasizing the need for region-specific diagnostic criteria and management strategies [13,14].

Unlike individuals with T1DM who require insulin therapy, many individuals with T5DM can effectively manage their condition with oral medications, making treatment more accessible and cost‑effective, especially in resource‑limited settings. The cornerstone of T5DM management is nutritional rehabilitation, which addresses the underlying etiology of chronic undernutrition. Initial interventions focus on gradual repletion of macronutrients—especially high‑quality proteins to support pancreatic β‑cell function—and correction of caloric deficits. Simultaneously, micronutrient supplementation (e.g., zinc, magnesium, and B‑vitamins) is critical to restore enzymatic cofactor pools necessary for insulin synthesis and secretion. A phased feeding approach—beginning with easily digestible, low‑glycemic index carbohydrates and advancing to more complex nutrition—is recommended to prevent refeeding syndrome and minimize postprandial glycemic excursions [15]. Pharmacotherapy for T5DM typically begins with oral antidiabetic agents tailored to the patient’s degree of insulin deficiency and residual β‑cell function. Metformin, a biguanide that improves hepatic insulin sensitivity and suppresses gluconeogenesis, is often the first‑line agent due to its safety profile, low cost, and weight‑neutral effect [16]. For patients with persistent hyperglycemia despite metformin, low‑dose sulfonylureas (e.g., glibenclamide), which enhance endogenous insulin secretion by closing ATP‑sensitive potassium (KATP) channels on β‑cells, may be added cautiously—monitoring for hypoglycemia in the context of variable caloric intake [17]. Emerging evidence also supports consideration of glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs), which potentiate glucose‑dependent insulin release, and sodium‑glucose co‑transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), which promote glucosuria, although cost and accessibility may limit their use in LMICs. Insulin therapy in T5DM is reserved for patients with profound β-cell exhaustion or those presenting with severe hyperglycemia and catabolic features at diagnosis. When indicated, a basal insulin analogue (e.g., insulin glargine) can be initiated at low doses to stabilize fasting glucose levels, with careful titration based on self monitored blood glucose (SMBG) readings. Rapid acting insulin analogues may be introduced if postprandial glucose control remains inadequate; however, ongoing assessment of nutritional intake is essential to adjust prandial dosing and mitigate hypoglycemia risk. Transitioning between oral agents and insulin requires a multidisciplinary team—including dietitians and diabetes educators—to ensure that medication regimens align with evolving nutritional status and lifestyle factors.

Long-term management of T5DM demands regular monitoring of glycemic control and nutritional parameters. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) should be measured quarterly until target levels (< 7.0%) are achieved, then biannually thereafter. In parallel, anthropometric assessments—body mass index (BMI), mid-upper arm circumference, and growth velocity in adolescents—provide objective measures of nutritional recovery. Education on SMBG techniques, recognition of hypo and hyperglycemia symptoms, and culturally appropriate dietary counseling are integral to patient empowerment. Finally, integration of community health workers and support groups can enhance adherence, provide psychosocial support, and facilitate early recognition of complications, thereby optimizing outcomes in populations disproportionately affected by malnutrition-induced diabetes [18].

The recognition of malnutrition-related diabetes as a distinct entity necessitates a paradigm shift in public health policy, especially in LMICs where undernutrition remains prevalent. Integrating T5DM screening into existing nutritional surveillance programs represents a crucial first step. Current nutrition initiatives (e.g., child growth monitoring, school feeding programs, and maternal-child health services) offer strategic entry points for early identification of at-risk individuals [19,20]. For example, adolescents and young adults with documented histories of chronic undernutrition, stunted growth, or delayed developmental milestones should be flagged for metabolic assessment, including fasting blood glucose, C-peptide levels, and absence of autoantibodies typically seen in T1DM [21]. To improve detection, national diabetes strategies in LMICs must incorporate T5DM-specific risk criteria into diabetes registries and primary care screening protocols. The widespread misclassification of T5DM as T1DM or T2DM not only leads to suboptimal treatment (e.g., unnecessary lifelong insulin therapy) but also impairs accurate epidemiological surveillance. Policymakers should mandate the inclusion of nutritional history and anthropometric measurements (e.g., BMI-for-age, mid-upper arm circumference) in all new diabetes diagnoses in regions with high malnutrition prevalence [22]. Additionally, workforce training is essential. Primary healthcare providers must be educated on the unique presentation and pathophysiology of T5DM, including its nutritional underpinnings and non-autoimmune etiology [12]. Community health workers who are already embedded in rural and underserved areas can play a pivotal role in early case identification, ongoing dietary counseling, and monitoring of treatment adherence. Task-shifting models that empower these workers to perform basic glucose testing and refer high-risk individuals for further evaluation could significantly enhance coverage and cost-efficiency in resource-limited settings [23]. Policy frameworks should also address the economic implications of treatment. Given that many individuals with T5DM can be managed with oral agents and nutritional support rather than insulin, revising national essential medicines lists to prioritize affordable drugs such as metformin and sulfonylureas is both clinically appropriate and financially sustainable [24]. This also aligns with Universal Health Coverage (UHC) goals by reducing treatment costs and improving accessibility. Finally, long-term planning should incorporate T5DM into global malnutrition and non-communicable disease (NCD) agendas. The World Health Organization (WHO), the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF), and regional health authorities must explicitly recognize malnutrition-related diabetes in their NCD frameworks and fund research on its prevalence, outcomes, and interventions. Cross-sectoral collaboration between nutrition, diabetes, and primary care programs will be essential to break the intergenerational cycle of undernutrition and metabolic disease [25].

This commentary highlights T5DM, also termed malnutrition-related diabetes, as a distinct and underrecognized form of severe insulin-deficient diabetes caused by chronic undernutrition, particularly during childhood and adolescence (Table1). Unlike T1DM and T2DM, T5DM is neither autoimmune nor driven by insulin resistance but arises from impaired pancreatic development and disrupted insulin signaling. It is especially prevalent in LMICs, affecting millions. The authors advocate for formal recognition of T5DM as a unique diabetes subtype, emphasizing the need for tailored diagnostic criteria, management strategies focused on nutritional rehabilitation and accessible oral therapies, and integration into global health policies to improve outcomes in vulnerable populations. Given its association with under-resourced settings, the commentary underscores the urgent need for the development of mass screening tools that are practical, low-cost, and feasible to deploy in remote areas with limited access to healthcare. Advanced diagnostic technologies are likely impractical for this context; therefore, simplified instruments such as structured questionnaires and field-friendly screening protocols are essential. In parallel, the creation of context-specific clinical guidelines is critical to ensure appropriate diagnosis and treatment. These efforts must be grounded in the realities of healthcare delivery in underserved communities where malnutrition is endemic.

Key takeaways of type 5 diabetes mellitus (T5DM)

- Impaired β-cell insulin synthesis due to amino acid deficiency

- Decreased insulin receptor binding affinity

- Defective IRS-1/PI3K-Akt signaling

- Hepatic resistance to insulin and glucagon, promoting gluconeogenesis and hyperglycemia

- Severe insulin deficiency without autoantibodies (e.g., anti-GAD65, anti-ICA)

- Low/normal C-peptide levels

- No insulin resistance typical of T2DM

- History of chronic undernutrition and stunted growth

- Occurs in adolescents/young adults

- Signs of malnutrition (low BMI, stunting)

- Glucose intolerance and early-onset hyperglycemia

- Nutritional rehabilitation (protein and micronutrient repletion)

- First-line: Metformin

- Add-on: Low-dose sulfonylureas or GLP-1 RAs/SGLT2i (if accessible)

- Insulin reserved for advanced cases

- Continuous nutritional monitoring and patient education

- Integrate T5DM screening in nutrition programs

- Use anthropometry and nutritional history in diagnosis

- Train healthcare workers on T5DM recognition and management

- Include cost-effective oral antidiabetics in essential drug lists

- Recognize T5DM in global NCD and malnutrition policies
