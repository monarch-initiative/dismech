Metabolic byproducts have conventionally been disregarded as waste products 
without functions. In this opinion article, we bring to light the multifaceted 
role of methylmalonic acid (MMA), a byproduct of the propionate metabolism 
pathway mostly commonly known as a clinical biomarker of vitamin B12 deficiency. 
MMA is normally present at low levels in the body, but increased levels can come 
from different sources, such as vitamin B12 deficiency, genetic mutations in 
enzymes related to the propionate pathway, the gut microbiota, and aggressive 
cancers. Here, we describe the diverse metabolic and signaling functions of MMA 
and discuss the consequences of increased MMA levels, such as during the aging 
process, for several age-related human pathologies.

Byproducts of metabolism have often been disregarded or viewed simply as dead-end waste products. However, metabolic byproducts have important functions within the body. A well-known example of these byproducts that was overlooked upon discovery, only to later be revealed as important, is lactate. Lactate was initially thought to simply be a toxic waste product of anaerobic metabolism, which when allowed to build up in the muscles, leads to soreness and muscle fatigue. However, over the years lactate has gained an appreciation for its role in energy production, modulation of signaling pathways, immune regulation, and more [1]. Importantly, it was found that the brain can utilize lactate for energy production in situations of glucose starvation [2,3]. Additionally, lactate plays a role in immune suppression, by regulating both macrophages and T cells [2], which can in turn promote tumor growth [2]. More recently, lactate has been shown to form a novel post-translation modification, lysine lactoylation which has been shown to regulate glycolytic enzymes, epigenetics, as well as other signaling regulators (e.g. KEAP1) [4–7]. Lactate also regulates whole-body energy through the exercise-induced biosynthesis of N-lactoyl-phenylalanine (Lac-Phe), a molecule which curbs feeding behavior and prevents obesity [8]. The discoveries surrounding lactate over the years illustrate why metabolic byproducts warrant further study. Methylmalonic acid (MMA), a metabolite first identified in 1967 as the culprit of the metabolic disorder methylmalonic acidemia [9], is an emerging bioactive metabolite that falls under this category. MMA is a byproduct of the propionate metabolism pathway and most commonly known as a clinical biomarker of vitamin B12 deficiency. Despite its clinical relevance, the functions of MMA and its role in pathology outside of inborn errors of metabolism are only now starting to be addressed. For instance, elevation of circulatory MMA has been shown in multiple studies to be a feature of the aging process [10–12] and found that higher MMA levels predict all-cause mortality in the general human population, independent of vitamin B12 [13,14]. Here, we describe the numerous metabolic and signaling functions of MMA, and discuss their consequences to human pathologies, putting forward MMA as an important and overlooked component of age-related disease development.

Methylmalonic acid is a byproduct of the propionate metabolism pathway (Figure 1), where branched chain amino acids, fatty acids, and cholesterol are catabolized into propionyl-CoA in the mitochondria. Propionyl-CoA is then enzymatically converted to D-methylmalonyl-CoA by propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC). Methylmalonyl-CoA epimerase (MCEE) is responsible for converting the D-isomer to the L-isomer, and L-methylmalonyl-CoA is then converted to succinyl-CoA, which enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, by methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MMUT) in a manner that depends on vitamin B12, specifically adenosylcobalamin. In efforts to identify the biochemical source of MMA, purification from rat liver revealed an enzyme that hydrolyzes D-methylmalonyl-CoA, but not the L-isomer, to form MMA and coenzyme A [15], which is now known as D-methylmalonyl-CoA hydrolase.

Vitamin B12, also known as cobalamin, is a water-soluble diet-derived vitamin that plays a key role in metabolism through its role in powering flux through the folate/methionine cycles and through the propionate metabolism pathway [16]. Upon uptake, vitamin B12 is transported and metabolized through several processes involving the lysosomal degradation of transcobalamin (TC), and the transcobalamin receptor CD320 (TC-R) [17]. Chaperone and adenosyltransferase enzymes, MMAA and MMAB respectively, then convert intracellular vitamin B12 into its propionate metabolism cofactor form, adenosylcobalamin [17]. Adenosylcobalamin serves as a coenzyme required for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase activity [17]. Therefore, a deficiency in vitamin B12, dysregulation of the factors involved in its uptake, transport, and processing, as well as increased flux through the propionate pathway beyond its enzymatic capacity, lead to a subsequent elevation of MMA. Clinically, MMA levels serve as a functional indicator of vitamin B12 status [18]. Normal blood levels of MMA are below 270 nmol/L, while levels above this upper range are suggestive of vitamin B12 deficiency [19]. Although the mechanism behind the age-induced accumulation of MMA remains unknown, it is reasonable to conceive that this may be, at least in part, due to nutritional vitamin B12 deficiency caused by malabsorption [20,21]. Additionally, itaconate-induced B12 depletion may also be a possible mechanism for the age-induced increase in circulatory MMA. Itaconate is an immunomodulatory metabolite released by activated macrophages that negatively regulates B12 levels [22,23]. Aging is associated with increased low-grade chronic inflammation, termed “inflammaging”, as well as metabolic remodeling of immune cells [22,24]. It is plausible that age-related inflammation leads to increased itaconate release from immune cells [22], thus leading to B12 depletion and a consequent increase in MMA. Future studies are needed to precisely pinpoint how aging drives the accumulation of MMA in circulation and its relationship with vitamin B12 availability.

As mentioned above, isolated methylmalonic acidemia is a known autosomal recessive metabolic disorder associated with elevated MMA [25] caused by mutations in genes that encode the enzymes of the propionate pathway, including but not restricted to the MMUT gene. MMUT mutations are divided into two categories: mut0 which has complete loss of enzymatic activity and no responsiveness to excess vitamin B12, and mut− which has partial enzymatic activity and is responsive to excess vitamin B12 [26]. Mut0 mutations are much more common than mut− and are associated with worse morbidity and higher mortality [27]. Although less common than MMUT mutations, methylmalonic acidemia can also be caused by bi-allelic pathogenic variants in the genes MMAA, MMAB, MMADHC, MCEE, SUCLG1, and SUCLA2 [25]. Affected individuals present with a pathological accumulation of MMA, as well as methylmalonyl-CoA, 2-methylcitrate, 3-hydroxypropionate, propionyl-carnitine, ammonia, with a lack of succinyl-CoA production. This is accompanied by a host of functional abnormalities in the mitochondria and drives toxicity in the brain, liver, kidney as well as other organs. A more detailed overview of the clinical presentation of methylmalonic acidemia is described by Forny, et al. [28]. Importantly, the existence of methylmalonic acidemias caused by mutations that impair the function of the propionate metabolic enzymes, highlights the possibility that epigenetic or post-translational regulation of the enzymes in this pathway might play a role in pathological upregulation of MMA in individuals without inborn errors of metabolism. In support of this idea, highly aggressive cancers, such as metastatic triple negative breast cancer, were recently shown to increase the production of MMA through transcriptional inhibition of MCEE, the enzyme directly downstream of D-methylmalonyl-CoA in the propionate pathway [29].

Given that the propionate pathway is evolutionarily conserved between prokaryotes and eukaryotes [30,31], gut microbiota themselves can produce propionate and MMA. Therefore, the gut microbiome serves as another source of MMA and contributes to circulating MMA levels [32]. This is supported by case studies of patients with small bowel bacterial overgrowth, whose elevated levels of MMA were normalized by treatment with metronidazole, a broad-spectrum antibiotic that can target gut bacteria producing propionate [33].

Together these reports support a paradigm where MMA is present at low levels in the body under normal physiological conditions; however, in conditions where propionate metabolism is disrupted, such as in vitamin B12 deficiency, suppression of key enzyme activity, or upon microbiome alterations that lead to the overproduction of propionate, MMA levels rise to supraphysiological levels. Below, we argue that this excess in MMA has profound effects that contribute to disease by impacting various metabolic and signaling processes.

Traditionally, the MMA shunt has been seen as an escape valve to limit the intracellular levels of methylmalonyl-CoA in pathological conditions [34]. However, a mounting body of work over the last decades has demonstrated an important role for MMA in regulating several cellular processes and pathways [10,35–37]. Chief among these is the effect of MMA on metabolism (Figure 2). The most understood effect of MMA is its ability to competitively inhibit succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) [38,39]. SDH is a mitochondrial enzyme that participates in both the TCA cycle and the electron transport chain (ETC), specifically as respiratory complex II. Accordingly, in vitro treatment of rat cerebral cortex homogenates MMA inhibited complex II activity [40]. Because of the competitive nature of this regulation, MMA is only able to inhibit SDH when succinate was not added in excess, highlighting the importance of the MMA/succinate ratio, rather than absolute levels, for SDH regulation [40]. Moreover, evaluation of liver mitochondria samples isolated from a mouse model of methylmalonic acidemia (MMUT-knock out/knock in) has revealed more nuanced effects on TCA cycle flux with protein levels of 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase, fumarate hydratase, and malate dehydrogenase found to be decreased compared to their littermate controls [38].

In line with MMA having inhibitory effects on mitochondrial activity, MMA was also found to inhibit complex I of the ETC in a succinate-independent manner pointing out to ETC inhibition by MMA as an important contributor to MMA-induced pathological effects [40]. Moreover, it has also been suggested that MMA plays a negative role in mitophagy, the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria to preserve healthy mitochondria [41]. A well-defined pathway that regulates mitophagy is the PINK1 kinase activation in response to damaged mitochondria. PINK1 in turn recruits parkin, an E3 ubiquitin protein ligase, to degrade damaged mitochondria. Kidney cell lines with MMUT deficiency displayed a decrease in parkin translocation to damaged mitochondria [42]. This suggests that MMA, which increases in conditions of MMUT deficiency, may contribute to an inability to clear dysfunctional mitochondria and thus contribute to mitochondrial damage propagation.

Hypoglycemia is a common manifestation seen in methylmalonic acidemia. Gluconeogenesis is the main metabolic pathway that generates and maintains blood glucose levels in conditions where glucose availability from diet is low. Malate is an intermediate metabolite in both the TCA cycle and gluconeogenesis. Malate is converted to oxaloacetate by malate dehydrogenase using the reduction of NAD(P)+ to NAD(P)H. Malate can be shuttled to the mitochondrial matrix from the cytosol via the malate-aspartate shuttle which generates oxaloacetate to be used as a substrate for gluconeogenesis. Interestingly, MMA has been shown to have inhibitory effects on malate by inhibiting its transport across rat liver mitochondrial membranes [43]. MMA also inhibited the reduction of NAD(P)+ into NAD(P)H in the presence of malate [43]. Given that the mitochondrial transport of malate is a regulatory site for gluconeogenesis and that MMA inhibits malate transport, these findings suggest that MMA can inhibit gluconeogenesis. This is supported by another study which found MMA to inhibit propionate-fueled gluconeogenesis in rat kidney-cortex slices [44]. Like malate, lactate is another metabolite that plays an important role in gluconeogenesis by serving as a non-carbohydrate carbon source. Lactate accumulation is seen in patients with methylmalonic acidemia [28]. MMA was found to extensively inhibit lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)-catalyzed conversion of lactate to pyruvate in a dose-dependent manner, more so than the conversion of pyruvate to lactate [45]. The decreased ability to convert lactate to pyruvate in the presence of supraphysiological levels of MMA may not only account for the decrease in gluconeogenic ability but also contribute to the accumulation of lactate in circulation seen in methylmalonic acidemias.

In addition to having inhibitory effects on enzymes related to TCA cycle and gluconeogenesis, MMA was shown to play a role in fatty acid synthesis. Studies have suggested that methylmalonyl-CoA reduces fatty acid synthesis through its ability to compete with malonyl-CoA, whose structure only differs by one methyl group, as a substrate for fatty acid synthase (FAS) [46–49]. Interestingly, while the activity of FAS is greatly diminished in the presence of supraphysiological level of MMA, MMA has been shown to be used by FAS, giving rise to fatty acids in multiple animal models, including mouse adipose tissue [50], rat liver [47], chicken liver and sheep adipose tissue [51]. Importantly, analysis of the fatty acids derived from methylmalonyl-CoA show that several types including odd chain fatty acids (OCFAs), sphingolipids, and branched chain fatty acids (BCFAs) can be produced, of which BCFAs are the most abundant [47,51,52]. BCFAs are a type of fatty acid with one or more methyl branches in their carbon chain which contributes to their non-linear structure. While the metabolic significance of BCFAs remains largely unknown, the abnormal incorporation of BCFAs has been linked to disease abnormalities. For example, hepatic BCFAs were significantly higher in subjects with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), a disease caused by an accumulation of fat cells in the liver, compared to those without NAFLD [53]. In line with a potential role for MMA-derived BCFAs in NAFLD, MMA levels were also significantly higher in subjects with NAFLD with advanced liver fibrosis risk [54]. Liver disease and liver fibrosis were found to be secondary complications in some isolated methylmalonic acidemia patients [25].

Beyond central carbon and lipid metabolism, nitrogen metabolism is also modulated in methylmalonic acidemia. In the urea cycle, N-acetylglutamate synthase converts glutamate and acetyl-CoA to N-acetylglutamate. In rat liver mitochondria samples, methylmalonyl-CoA caused about 30% inhibition of N-acetylglutamate synthase, as indicated by a reduced incorporation of radioactivity from labeled glutamate into N-acetylglutamate [55]. A decreased level of N-acetylglutamate limits the activation of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, the rate-limiting enzyme of the urea cycle, and hence decreases clearance of ammonia. Severe hyperammonemia has been shown to occur in infants with methylmalonic acidemia [56,57].

While the studies outlined above point for a key role of methylmalonic acid in regulating key metabolic pathways, it is possible that they only skim the surface of MMA’s role in metabolism. Indeed, a study performed in the 1980s utilizing radioactive 14C-labeled MMA showed that while a small proportion of MMA is secreted, the remaining is metabolized intracellularly into a vast number of unknown products [15], supporting the need for more in-depth metabolic studies to fully map MMA’s metabolic fate.

In addition to its direct metabolic effects, MMA has also been shown to fine-tune cellular functions through its ability to regulate signaling pathways and transcriptional reprogramming (Figure 3). Similar to some of its metabolic effects, which are derived from MMA’s structural similarity with succinate (e.g., SDH inhibition), MMA has been shown to also function as a ligand for the succinate receptor (SUCNR1) [58]. The SUCNR1 is a G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR), that is expressed in various tissues, notably the kidney, adipose tissue, and immune cells [59–61]. As the name indicates, the canonical substrate for this receptor is succinate [62]. MMA was found to be a succinate receptor agonist, likely due to their similar dicarboxylic acid structure [58]. Upon activation, SUCNR1 elicits the activation of canonical GPCR signaling, activating important signaling cascades such as MAPK/ERK, PKA and PKC. The MAPK/ERK and PKC pathways in particular lead to pro-inflammatory cascades, such as downstream NF-κB and p38 signaling. Accordingly, the succinate-SUCNR1 axis has been shown to drive inflammation. For example, in peripheral blood mononuclear cell-derived macrophages exhibiting upregulated SUCNR1 expression, succinate treatment upregulated the expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α and downregulated the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 [63]. Because MMA binds to SUCNR1 with about 80% efficiency as succinate when present at supraphysiological concentrations [58], MMA-induced activation of SUCNR1 may constitute an important axis that links high levels of extracellular levels of MMA to major regulatory networks. If proven correct, MMA-mediated SUCNR1 activation might have important implications to diseases linked to SUCNR1, including diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular disease, and aberrant immune activation [61,64,65].

Another important signaling function related to MMA and methylmalonic acidemia is the role of methylmalonyl-CoA as a mediator of a novel post-translational modification (PTM). It was recently found that the buildup of methylmalonyl-CoA, as seen in patients with methylmalonic acidemia, leads to the formation of a novel lysine PTM called methylmalonylation [66]. Since acyl-CoA synthetase family member 3 (ACSF3) can not only convert malonic acid into malonyl-CoA but also convert MMA into methylmalonyl-CoA [67,68], it is possible that MMA buildup itself can also indirectly lead to the accumulation of methylmalonylation, although this requires further study. Importantly, methylmalonylation was found on various metabolic enzymes and mitochondrial proteins, including several components of the TCA cycle, the urea cycle, and glutathione production [66]. While the systems responsible for methylmalonylation remain unknown, the mitochondrial deacetylase SIRT5 has been shown to be responsible for its removal from mitochondrial proteins [66]. Strikingly, and highlighting the intricacies of MMA-mediated regulation of cellular function, SIRT5 itself was also found to be methylmalonylated. This reduces SIRT5 enzymatic activity, creating a positive feedback loop whereby increased methylmalonylation leads to its inability to demethylmalonylate, leading to further spread of this PTM [66]. While this discovery carries important consequences for the regulation of mitochondria by MMA, it remains to be explored whether these effects are locally driven by the production of MMA in the mitochondria or can also be mediated upon exposure to extracellular MMA.

Mitochondrial metabolites and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are also important components of the signaling networks that fine-tune cellular function in response to the metabolic and redox state of the cells [69,70]. This occurs through communication of mitochondria-derived metabolites to the nucleus to induce a transcriptional response, a process known as retrograde signaling [71]. A key component of retrograde signaling is ROS, which can oxidize key cysteines in several cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins and thereby regulate their functions. A well-established consequence of methylmalonic acidemia is an elevation of oxidative stress, implying that there may be a causal relationship between high MMA levels and ROS [72,73]. Direct evidence for this paradigm has been recently shown in cancer cells, whose short-term treatment with MMA caused an increase in ROS [35]. Supporting a role for MMA-induced ROS-mediated fine-tuning of cellular functions, MMA-induced ROS generation in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) was shown to promote the release of endosomal vesicles containing high levels of IL-6, which in turn exert paracrine effects on other components of the tumor ecosystem [35]. Despite this, the mechanism by which MMA promotes a rapid increase in ROS levels remains unknown. Interestingly, mitochondrial superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), which reduces superoxide into hydrogen peroxide that can be detoxified by catalase, has been found to be methylmalonylated in mice with methylmalonic acidemia compared to controls, through mass spectrometry [66]. Although more work is necessary to evaluate the significance of this methylmalonylation event, it brings forth the idea that MMA might regulate ROS through direct regulation of SOD2 activity.

Succinate is also an important component of retrograde signaling through its ability to inhibit α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases [69]. These include the ten-eleven translocation (TET) methylcytosine dioxygenases, which regulate DNA methylation, the Jumonji C (JmjC) Domain-Containing family of proteins, which regulate histone lysine methylation, and the EGLN prolyl-hydroxylase family of proteins, which among others regulate hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) levels [69]. Considering that MMA can inhibit SDH [39] and activate SUCNR1 due to its structural similarity with succinate [58], it is reasonable to conceive that supraphysiological levels of MMA might bind to α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, thereby resulting in their inhibition and consequently in alterations in DNA and histone methylation as well as the induction of the HIF transcription factors. If proven correct, regulation of α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases could, at least in part, explain the transcriptional reprogramming that exposure to MMA causes in cancer cells, which leads to the expression of various cell-identity genetic programs, signaling ligands, extracellular matrix remodeling factors, and cytokines in cancer cells, pushing them towards a more aggressive gene expression profile [10,74]. Together, these findings put forward MMA as a novel regulator of cellular signaling with implications for abnormal activation/inhibition of these pathways in pathological conditions that associate with elevated MMA production.

Isolated methylmalonic acidemia causes an array of symptoms ranging from neurodegeneration to kidney failure among others. Importantly, as discussed above, supraphysiological levels of MMA can also occur in the absence of inborn errors of metabolism, such as the age-induced increase in MMA levels [10–12]. However, while circulatory levels of MMA in methylmalonic acidemia patients can range from 5 μM to 1 mM depending on the mutational subtype [25,75], MMA levels raise to 0.125-0.4 μM in the elderly compared to approximately 0.075-0.2 μM in young healthy individuals [12,76]. This is in line with a progressive decline of organ function, such as what happens during the aging process or the development of chronic disease, as opposed to the severe pathological effects observed in isolated methylmalonic acidemia, which in its most severe form leads to childhood death [77].

One of the main commonalities between the symptoms of isolated methylmalonic acidemia and age-related pathologies is neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment [25]. A direct connection between high levels of MMA and neurodegeneration has been observed in mice injected with MMA, which display a neuroinflammatory phenotype and altered development in certain brain regions [36]. In accordance with the aforementioned in vivo work, MMA treatment was shown to induce cell death in cultured rat embryonic striatal cells [78]. Mechanistically, these neurogenerative effects of MMA have been ascribed to MMA’s ability to increase ROS [36,79]. Circulatory levels of MMA have been positively associated with pro-inflammatory cytokines, namely TNF-α and IL-6 [56], which have been shown to induce ROS formation in immune cells [56]. Beyond the effects caused by increasing ROS in the brain, MMA’s pro-neurodegenerative ability has also been ascribed to its ability to impair mitochondrial respiration [80], a hallmark of neurodegeneration. In isolated brain mitochondria and rat brain homogenates, MMA has been shown to not only be capable of inhibiting complex II (SDH [39,81]), when present at supraphysiological levels, but also complex I-mediated respiration thereby impairing mitochondrial oxidative metabolism [81]. The astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle is a homeostatic mechanism whereby astrocytes respond to glutamatergic activation by increasing their rate of glucose utilization and release of lactate to the extracellular space, making lactate available for neurons to sustain their energetic demands [82]. As mentioned above, MMA potently inhibits the conversion of lactate to pyruvate and consequently it is reasonable to conceive that high levels of MMA in the brain will interfere with this homeostatic mechanism and cause an energetic crisis in neurons. Additionally, since canonical activation of the succinate receptor is tightly linked to the inflammatory response within the CNS [63], it is possible that MMA-induced activation of the succinate receptor is a contributor to the neuroinflammatory phenotype induced by MMA.

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), usually linked to obesity, diabetes, and aging [83], affects between a quarter to a third of people worldwide [84,85], posing significant challenges to healthcare systems. A key component of NAFLD development is the presence of chronic liver fibrosis [86]. Considering that MMA has been shown to promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in cancer cells [10,29], and that liver fibrosis is thought to be caused in part by EMT of resident hepatic cells [87,88], it is plausible that supraphysiological levels of MMA might contribute to NAFLD development through induction of liver fibrosis. In support of this idea, MMA abundance positively correlated with advanced liver fibrosis risk, independently from levels of vitamin B12 [54]. Further supporting a role for MMA in the development of NAFLD, increased hepatic levels of BCFAs, for which methymalonyl-CoA is an important substrate, have been shown to be a feature of NAFLD [89].

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is another condition linked to MMA that is ubiquitous throughout the population and the main cause of death in western countries [90–92]. CVD is a well-known complication of organic acidemias [93], which is becoming increasingly recognized in patients with isolated methylmalonic acidemia [93–95]. While CVD affects fewer methylmalonic acidemia patients than other organic acidemias, cardiac symptoms affect 10-20% of individuals with isolated methylmalonic acidemia as shown by abnormal electrocardiograms (ECG), and symptoms such as arrythmias or cardiomyopathy [25,95]. Not only is CVD becoming a more appreciated complication associated with methylmalonic acidemia [93,94], but high MMA levels are also positively and independently associated with increased risk of heart failure in the general population unrelated to vitamin B12 levels [37,96–98]. Moreover, elevated levels of succinate were linked to hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, all pathologies associated with increased risk for CVD, both in mice and humans [63,64]. Chronic inflammation is an important component of CVD development, for which elevated MMA levels might contribute to via its ability to activate SUCNR1, which as described above is a well-known driver of inflammation [63,64]. Additionally, abnormal activation of SUCNR1 driven by elevated MMA also has the potential to contribute to CVD by SUCNR1-induced release of renin in the kidney [99,100], which regulates blood pressure through its role in angiotensin production.

Isolated methylmalonic acidemia is also associated with a high risk of kidney disease and renal failure [101,102]. Supporting a direct role of MMA in promoting kidney disease, chronic administration of MMA to rats has shown to be nephrotoxic, as evidenced by injury to the renal tube and mitochondrial swelling [103]. While the mechanisms by which MMA contributes to kidney disease remain to be discovered, it has been shown that the PTM methylmalonylation inhibits enzymes within the urea cycle [66], possibly leading to the accumulation of excess ammonia. Increased ammonia levels in the kidney promotes activation of the alternative complement pathway, which triggers an immune response and causes renal tube injury [104]. Moreover, disruption of the urea cycle has also been shown to induce the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) leading to protein degradation [105], which has consequences for the development of kidney disease.

Another complication of methylmalonic acidemia is hypotonia [25,79] and reduced muscle strength [106]. Skeletal muscles are multinuclear structures that have many mitochondria, which are essential for the energetic demands of force production and contraction [107]. As such, any perturbation in mitochondrial function is likely to drastically impact muscle function [108]. Since MMA has been shown to inhibit the TCA cycle, it is possible that circulatory MMA can impede cellular respiration and thus reduce ATP production within the muscle. Muscle atrophy is caused by an imbalance between protein catabolism and anabolism and may be caused by the increased activation of the UPS or the autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP) [109]. These pathways can be triggered by oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial damage [109,110]. The evidence that MMA induces ROS and inflammation provides a direct association and potential mechanistic links between this metabolite and muscle wasting. Interestingly, aging and cancer are both situations linked to elevated MMA levels [10,29], while also being associated with characteristic muscle wasting disease, namely sarcopenia and cachexia, respectively. Increased serum MMA levels are seen in aging [10], which may be one of the contributing factors to sarcopenia. Similarly, aggressive cancers have been shown to hijack the propionate metabolism pathway [29], which boosts MMA production and thus may also have a hand in cachexia, possibly occurring by similar avenues as hypotonia in patients with methylmalonic acidemia. Although the link between muscle wasting and MMA elevation requires further study, the potential signaling functions of MMA bring us to suspect that MMA may contribute, at least in part, to age and cancer-related muscle atrophy.

While the symptoms of methylmalonic acidemia have been well-established over the years, a previously unknown pathological effect of MMA has been recently reported: its ability to promote cancer progression [10,29,111]. This direct effect of MMA on cancer cells is two-fold: not only does circulating extrinsic MMA promote tumor growth and invasion [10,111], but intrinsic MMA produced by the tumor itself encourages metastasis [29]. More specifically, MMA was shown to directly induce SOX4 expression [10], which is a well-established marker of tumor progression and subsequently poor prognosis [112,113]. Furthermore, metastatic breast cancer cells were shown to downregulate an enzymatic component of the propionate metabolism pathway, namely MCEE, compared to non-metastatic cancer cell lines [29]. MCEE downregulation in turn led to an accumulation of MMA within cancer cells and within tumors, demonstrating that cancer cells hijack the propionate metabolism pathway to generate MMA to fuel metastasis [29]. Highlighting the importance of MMA for tumor progression, in addition to its direct effect on tumor cells, MMA has been shown to affect non-cancer cells in the tumor microenvironment, namely fibroblasts [35]. MMA was shown to induce ROS in fibroblasts, leading to their conversion towards a cancer-associated-fibroblast (CAF) phenotype. The CAFs in turn were shown to secrete IL-6 to communicate back to the tumor cells, leading to increased cancer aggressiveness. In sum, besides its role in inborn errors of metabolism, a case can be made for MMA as a significant contributor for many age-related diseases spanning from neurodegeneration to cancer, putting forward MMA as an important factor to consider in disease pathology.

MMA has several roles in metabolic reprogramming and cellular signaling, which directly link this molecule to several human pathologies. Interestingly, the symptoms of methylmalonic acidemia in which MMA is elevated, such as cardiovascular disease, neurodegeneration, liver disease, and cancer are also common signs of aging, a process linked to increased circulatory levels of MMA [10,12,76]. This raises an important question: is MMA itself a cause of aging? Pertinent to this question, circulating MMA levels, but not vitamin B12 levels, were found to be directly correlated with risk of all-cause mortality [114]. It remains to be seen if MMA treatment alone could induce premature aging and/or the onset of age-related diseases (see Outstanding Questions). However, given the association between MMA and the numerous pathologies reviewed here as well as the age-related increase in circulatory MMA, the possibility that MMA accumulation is a direct cause of aging is a viable hypothesis still to be explored.
