BACKGROUND: Though follicular thyroid carcinoma can be confirmed postoperatively 
by the histological findings of capsular or vascular invasion, preoperative 
diagnosis of follicular-patterned lesions has long been a diagnostic challenge. 
This study seeks to establish a machine-learning (ML) model based on clinical, 
US, and CEUS features for the differential diagnosis of thyroid 
follicular-patterned lesions (TFPLs).
METHODS: Patients with surgical pathologically confirmed TFPLs who underwent 
preoperative US and CEUS between January 2013 to April 2023 were enrolled in 
this retrospective study. We utilized five ML algorithms (logistic regression, 
random forest [RF], k-nearest neighbor [KNN], support vector machine [SVM], and 
elastic net [EN]) to construct an optimized model via US, CEUS and clinical data 
for the differential diagnosis of TFPLs. Model performance was evaluated with 
sensitivity, area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), and area under the 
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).
RESULTS: 114 patients were finally included. The sensitivities of the five ML 
algorithms (Logistic, RF, KNN, SVM, EN) were 0.93, 0.97, 0.93, 0.97, 0.93 for 
the training set, and 0.40, 1.00, 0.80, 1.00, 0.60 for the test set. The AUPRCs 
were 0.91, 0.97, 0.99, 0.91, 0.91 for the training set, and 0.65, 0.71, 0.38, 
0.57, 0.65 for the test set. The AUROCs were 0.95, 0.98, 0.99, 0.89, 0.93 for 
the training set. When applied to the test set, the RF model had a significantly 
higher AUROC value (0.92; 95% CI: 0.88, 0.96) than other ML algorithms (0.91, 
0.89, 0.91, 0.91, P < .05) with significant features including peripheral halo 
sign, thyroglobulin, rim enhancement, peak intensity and wash-out time.
CONCLUSIONS: Our ML model integrating clinical, US, and CEUS features achieved 
high sensitivity for preoperative differentiation of TFPLs, potentially guiding 
surgical planning-a step toward clinical use that requires CEUS standardization 
and external validation.

Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) arises from follicular cells without the diagnostic nuclear features of papillary thyroid carcinoma, accounting for approximately 10% of all thyroid malignancies [1]. Though lymph node metastasis is uncommon, FTC is prone to relapse and metastasize to distant locations such as bone and lung [2]. Other than the indolent nature of papillary carcinoma, invasion is a hallmark in the diagnosis of FTC, which have relatively good prognosis with 20-year survival rates close to 70% when appropriately treated [3]. The decision on the extent of surgery should be made after the greatest consideration as total thyroidectomy requires lifelong pharmacotherapy and higher risk of injury to adjacent structures while lobectomy poses higher risk of recurrence [4]. It has been found frozen-section analysis could not provide useful information for intraoperative decision-making of FTC [5]. Although FTC could be confirmed postoperatively by histologic demonstration of capsular or vascular invasion, preoperative accurate diagnosis is still important for clinical decision-making.

Previous studies have found that FTC has similar sonographic and cytological features to other thyroid follicular-patterned lesions (TTFPLs) including follicular adenoma and adenomatoid hyperplasia nodule [6–8]. In such cases, it seems difficult for the main preoperative diagnostic tools - fine needle aspiration and conventional ultrasound to distinguish benign from malignant TFPLs. Classical sonographic features including hypoechogenicity, irregular margin, taller-than-wide shape, and microcalcification are thought to have close relevance to the malignancy, especially in papillary carcinoma rather than FTC [9, 10]. Currently, more and more researchers have tried to find the sonographic difference between benign and malignant TFPLs but there is still no universal agreement [11–13]. Through qualitative and quantitative assessments of blood perfusion within the lesion, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has been widely applied in the differentiation of benign and malignant thyroid nodules [14, 15]. However, there is currently a lack of evidence about the diagnostic performance of CEUS in the malignancy prediction of TFPL cases.

Recently, various diagnostic models and risk stratification systems based on conventional US for the differentiation of benign and malignant TFPLs have been reported with the area under the curve values of 0.64–0.86 [16–18]. And no machine learning (ML) model has been developed to improve the diagnostic efficacy integrating a combination of US, CEUS and other clinical data. Thus, the purpose of our study was to construct an optimized ML model based on clinical, US, and CEUS features to distinguish FTC from benign TFPLs. More recently, studies have begun to incorporate contrast-enhanced ultrasound features into multimodal models [19, 20]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no machine learning model has yet integrated quantitative CEUS parameters—such as peak intensity and wash-out time—together with clinical and conventional US features for the preoperative diagnosis of TFPLs. The present study represents an independent effort to fill this gap by constructing and validating such a multi‑parametric ML model.

All included patients undergoing CEUS or surgery signed related informed consent forms for these medical examinations or procedures from January 2013 to April 2023. The study was approved by the local ethics committee, and individual consent for this retrospective study was waived. However, all including patients undergoing CEUS or surgery signed related informed consent forms for these medical examinations or procedures from January 2013 to April 2023. In the end, a total of 114 follicular-patterned lesions were recruited for the study. Besides, we added the preoperative serum thyroglobulin (TG) level which has been reported as a useful predictive marker to differentiate follicular thyroid cancer from benign nodules for the following analyses [18].

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) surgical pathologically confirmed follicular-patterned lesions including follicular thyroid carcinoma, follicular adenoma and adenomatoid hyperplasia nodule; (2) US examinations within 1 month before the pathologic examination. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) lack of CEUS examinations within 1 month before surgery; (2) patients with incomplete data; (3) contrast imaging video could not be recognized by the offline analysis software.

A total of 114 patients with TFPLs were included in this study, comprising 34 malignant FTC and 80 benign (follicular adenoma and adenomatoid hyperplasia nodule) cases. This resulted in an imbalanced class distribution (malignant: 29.8%, benign: 70.2%). To address this imbalance, we employed the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) during model training, as described below.

All sonographic examinations were performed with a linear probe of 2–18 MHz of ultrasound system (ESAOTE MyLab 90 Xvision / Mylab Twice, Italy). For patients with multiple nodules, the largest one with definite pathology of TFPL was selected as the target lesion. The following sonographic features were recorded for each TFPL: maximum diameter, echogenicity (hyperechoic, isoechoic / hypoechoic [with respect to the background thyroid]), margin (regular, irregular), peripheral halo sign (thin, absent / unevenly thick), cystic changes (absent, present), calcifications (absent, present [microcalcifications / macrocalcifications / peripheral calcifications]).

CEUS was performed with a linear probe of 2–18 MHz of ultrasound system (ESAOTE MyLab 90Xvision / Mylab Twice, Italy) after conventional US, and the sampling frame was adjusted to include the whole lesion. The focus was placed slightly deeper than target nodule at low mechanical index (0.05–0.07) to avoid destruction of microbubbles. Sulphur hexafluoride (SonoVue®, Bracco International, Milan, Italy) was injected intravenously as a bolus at a dose of 1.2 mL via a 20-gauge cannula, followed by a 10-ml saline flush. Each contrast imaging acquisition was recorded in real time for at least 120 s.

Qualitative enhancement parameters were recorded as follows: enhancement direction (centripetal, diffuse / centrifugal); peak enhancement (hypo-enhancement, iso-enhancement / hyper-enhancement); enhancement homogeneity at peak (homogeneous, heterogeneous); perfusion defects (present, absent); range of perfusion defects (< 50%, ≥ 50%); wash-out style (rapid, slow); rim enhancement (present [standard rim enhancement pattern should be thin and regular], absent / irregular). Quantitative parameters, including the wash-in time, peak intensity (PI), time to peak, wash-out time (WoT), were calculated. Time-intensity curve of the whole follicular-patterned lesion was obtained, and a CNUCA workstation was used for the offline CEUS analysis (Fig. 1).

All ultrasound examinations (including conventional US and CEUS) were performed by a single, highly experienced ultrasound specialist (Dr. Yan Wang) with over 20 years of experience in thyroid ultrasound and more than 10 years of experience in thyroid CEUS. And all the preoperative sonograms were then reviewed in random order by two skilled physicians with over 5 years of experience in both ultrasound techniques without knowing the clinical and pathological information. Each reader independently analyzed sonograms first, and then reviewed the cases with discrepancy together until final consensus was reached.

We utilized five machine learning algorithms: random forest (RF), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), elastic net (EN), and logistic regression (LR). The input dataset consisted of 20 features in total: 3 clinical variables (sex, age, preoperative serum thyroglobulin level), 6 conventional ultrasound features (maximum diameter, echogenicity, margin, peripheral halo sign, cystic changes, calcifications), and 11 contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) features. The CEUS features included 7 qualitative parameters (enhancement direction, peak enhancement, enhancement homogeneity, perfusion defects, range of perfusion defects, wash-out style, rim enhancement) and 4 quantitative parameters derived from the time-intensity curve (wash-in time, time to peak, peak intensity, wash-out time). All features were extracted as described in the corresponding sections above.

The dataset (n = 114) was randomly split into training (80%, n = 91) and test (20%, n = 23) sets using the holdout method, while maintaining the original class proportion via stratified sampling. To mitigate the class imbalance in the training set, we applied SMOTE, which generates synthetic samples of the minority class (malignant) to create a balanced training distribution. For each machine learning algorithm, hyperparameter tuning was performed via a grid search with 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. The hyperparameter search spaces were defined as follows:

RF: number of trees (ntree) in {100, 200, 500}, maximum depth of trees (max_depth) in {5, 10, 15, 20}, minimum samples to split a node (min_samples_split) in {2, 5, 10}.

KNN: number of neighbors (k) in {3, 5, 7, 9, 11}, distance metric (Euclidean, Manhattan).

SVM: kernel (linear, radial basis function), cost parameter (C) in {0.1, 1, 10, 100}, gamma (for RBF kernel) in {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1}.

EN: alpha (mixing parameter) in {0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1} (where 0 corresponds to ridge and 1 to lasso), lambda (regularization strength) automatically selected via cross-validation.

LR: penalty (L2), regularization strength (C) in {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100}, solver = ‘lbfgs’.

The combination of hyperparameters yielding the highest average area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) across the 5 folds was selected for each algorithm. The final models were then retrained on the entire training set using the optimal hyperparameters before evaluation on the test set.

Feature selection was conducted via recursive feature elimination based on variable importance from the RF algorithm. Model performance was evaluated on the held-out test set using sensitivity, specificity, precision, accuracy, area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), and AUROC. Given the clinical importance of correctly identifying malignant cases, we prioritized sensitivity, AUPRC, and AUROC as primary metrics. All of these procedures were performed using the “mlr3” package.

To make the optimal model interpretable, we rebuilt the model using the entire dataset and the tuned hyperparameters and used a bar chart to display the importance of the features. For dataset level explanation, we generated partial dependence plots to illustrate the model’s predictions as a function of each selected feature using all observations. For instance-level interpretation, we randomly selected two patients with benign and malignant TFPLs and created break-down plots to demonstrate the contribution of each selected feature to the predictions. All of these procedures were performed using the “DALEX” package.

The continuous data was presented as either mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on the distribution. Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney test were used to compare the mean and median values, respectively, between the two groups. Categorical data was presented as number and percentage. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the proportions between those two groups. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0. In all cases, two-tailed P values lower than 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

In accordance with the aforementioned inclusion and exclusion criteria, 114 eligible patients were actually seen in Fig. 2 (median age, 49 years [interquartile range, 36–62 years]; 80 women). The histopathological diagnoses of all FTC, follicular adenoma and adenomatoid hyperplasia nodule were determined surgically as follows: 34 cases of FTC (29.82%), 58 cases of follicular adenomas (50.88%), and 22 cases of adenomatoid hyperplasia nodules (19.30%). As follicular adenomas and adenomatoid hyperplasia nodules were both benign TFPLs, it was assigned those two as the benign group and FTC as the malignant group.

Taken together, all the five ML algorithms displayed favorable diagnostic potential in the malignancy prediction of TFPL cases. From the precision-recall curves and receiver operating characteristic curve of the five ML algorithms, it could be seen that the RF model presented the best diagnostic performance in terms of fairly high sensitivity, AUPRC, and AUROC (training set: 97%, 97%, 98% / test set: 100%, 71%, 92%) (Fig. 3C and D). When applied to the test data set, the RF model had a significantly higher AUROC value (0.92; 95% CI: 0.88, 0.96) than the Logistic (0.91; 95% CI: 0.84, 0.97; P < .05), k-nearest neighbor (0.89; 95% CI: 0.81, 0.96; P < .05), and support vector machine (0.91; 95% CI: 0.86, 0.96; P < .05), and elastic net (0.91; 95% CI: 0.85, 0.97; P < .05).

As previously noted, FTC rarely exhibits malignant features on ultrasound, such as taller than wide lesion, irregular margins, and microcalcifications [21, 22], and preoperative diagnosis of FTC is still challenging. Based on 20 studies of 10,215 nodules, Martyna found the most crucial features of FTC were microcalcifications or mixed type of calcifications, irregular margins, and et al. [23]. While Yang argued that there were no significant differences in calcification and shape between follicular adenoma and FTC [12]. Our study developed and validated an optimized machine-learning model based on US, CEUS and clinical data for the malignancy prediction of TFPL cases, and concluded that peripheral halo sign, PI, WoT, and TG were important features of FTC.

Peripheral halo sign is a hypoechoic or anechoic area surrounding a nodule, and the presence may be attributed to the preserved capsule. Some scholars found that thin halo was an important sign of benignancy [6, 24], while others hypothesized that thick and uneven halo was more common in FTC [11]. Our study showed absent / unevenly thick halo was an independent risk factor for FTC (Odds Ratio=66.76, 95% CI༝7.48-595.67) in the logistic regression model, and besides, it was also the most important contributor from both the feature importance chart and partial dependence plots. Previous studies have found FTC has a capsule composed of parallel collagenous fibers, containing vessels with irregular thickness and edema of muscular walls [25, 26]. Due to the local breakthrough of tumor cells hyperplasia and fibrosis, subsequent fibrous tissue reaction might result in the irregular and thick halo appearance on sonographic images [12, 27].

CEUS, as a sensitive method for detection of microvascularization, has uncertain clinical value in the diagnosis of TFPL. Zhang [14] concluded that follicular adenomas exhibited thin and regular rim enhancement, which is associated with peripheral capsular and compressed vessels while our previous qualitive study found FTC showed absent or irregular rim enhancement [28]. which was further verified in the current research (Odds Ratio=0.16, 95% CI༝0.03–0.72), and it may refer to the vessel or capsule penetration of the tumor. Besides, with the improvement of ultrasound software technology, quantitative parameters derived from dynamic clips are believed to provide more valuable information of hemodynamics than visualization [29]. Through the partial dependence plots, higher value of PI and WoT were mainly manifested in FTC, which might have more rich and chaotic vascularity than benignancy. It might be partly explained by the higher perfusion intensity and longer wash-out time. However, due to the complexity and overlapping of the angiographic manifestations in TFPL, more studies are still needed to validate the findings in the future.

Preoperative TG is another factor included in our model, and its levels could be affected by several factors, including hyperthyroidism caused by Graves’ disease, and glandular and nodular size [30]. TG is a useful prognostic marker for predicting recurrence and residual disease after surgery for differentiated thyroid cancer currently [31], but it cannot be used as a diagnostic marker of malignancy as it’s also elevated in various benign diseases. In our study, TG level in the FTC group was higher than the benign group with significant difference, and obvious increase was also seen in the prevalence of malignancy from the partial dependence plots. Actually, preoperative TG concentration has been reported as a predictive factor of FTC [18, 32], which is consistent with our findings. Therefore, we should pay more attention to the preoperative TG level of TFPLs for subsequent clinical practice.

Recent researches on diagnostic models and risk stratification systems based on conventional US for the diagnosis of TFPLs have been reported with a medium-to-high diagnostic efficacy (AUROCs of 0.64–0.86) [16–18], and no ML model has been developed to improve the efficacy using the combination of clinical, US, and CEUS features. As both AUPRC and sensitivity focus on the predictive performance of positive instances, while AUROC provides an overall performance evaluation and can serve as a benchmark in most scenarios. Thus, we prioritized the three as the primary metrics given the clinical challenge of the malignancy prediction of TFPLs. In the final result, we actually explored that RF model presented the best diagnostic performance in terms of fairly high sensitivity, AUPRC, and AUROC among five ML algorithms, especially when applied to the test data set, the RF model had a significantly higher AUROC value (0.92; 95% CI: 0.88, 0.96) than other models. Hence it would be attractive to apply RF model as an optimized and sensitive tool for the malignancy prediction of TFPLs. The high sensitivity (100%) achieved by our RF model on the test set must be interpreted with caution. While prioritizing sensitivity aligns with the clinical imperative to minimize missed malignancies—given that undiagnosed FTC may lead to incomplete surgical resection and risk of distant metastasis—the small test set (n = 23) means this perfect sensitivity may overestimate true performance. In clinical practice, such a high-sensitivity model could potentially lead to overtreatment if applied without appropriate threshold adjustment, as benign nodules might be subjected to unnecessary diagnostic surgery. The optimal operating threshold should balance the consequences of missed malignancy against those of unnecessary intervention, taking into account patient preferences, surgical risk, and healthcare costs. A formal decision curve analysis incorporating these trade-offs would strengthen clinical utility assessment for future multi-center validation studies.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size (n = 114), selection bias is inevitable and the findings should be considered preliminary. The low prevalence of FTC and the selective use of CEUS in clinical practice make the accumulation of large, multi-modal datasets challenging. Second, the lack of external validation limits the generalizability of our model, and future multi-center prospective studies are warranted to confirm its performance. Third, the long-term retrospective nature of this study (2013–2023) may introduce potential variability in image acquisition and quality over time. However, the use of consistent equipment, standardized imaging protocols, experienced operators, and automated quantitative CEUS analysis (via the CNUCA workstation) helps mitigate such concerns. Fourth, although quantitative CEUS parameters offer objective hemodynamic assessment, their reproducibility across different ultrasound platforms and software remains to be established. Further standardization of CEUS acquisition and analysis protocols is needed before widespread clinical application.

The preoperative differentiation of FTC from benign TFPLs remains a formidable challenge. Our proposed RF model, integrating clinical, US, and quantitative CEUS features, demonstrated a high sensitivity (100%) in the test set. In clinical practice, a model with such high sensitivity could serve as a rule-out tool to reduce unnecessary diagnostic surgeries for benign nodules, while also aiding in surgical planning (lobectomy vs. total thyroidectomy) for patients with suspected FTC. The inclusion of quantitative CEUS parameters offers a more objective assessment of tumor vascularity, potentially reducing inter-observer variability associated with qualitative image interpretation. Although the model requires external validation, it represents a step towards a more precise, multi-parametric approach for managing this diagnostically challenging entity.
