# Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell hierarchy is established by thrombopoietin-driven neonatal hematopoiesis.
**Authors:** Mochizuki-Kashio M, Yahagi A, Yokomizo T, Umemoto T, Suda T, Nakamura-Ishizu A
**Journal:** Stem Cell Reports (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.stemcr.2026.102865](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.stemcr.2026.102865)

## Content

The cytokine thrombopoietin (THPO) promotes both self-renewal and cell cycle quiescence of adult bone marrow (BM) hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). It remains unclear how THPO differentially regulates HSC expansion versus quiescence and whether it influences the broader BM hematopoietic hierarchy, particularly multipotent progenitor cells (MPPs), which express MPL, the receptor for THPO. Adult constitutional Thpo-knockout mice exhibited significant decrease in both HSC and MPP numbers, yet single-cell RNA sequence-based genetic changes were prominent only in HSCs. Induced deletion of Thpo in adult mice showed that THPO primarily maintains adult BM HSC numbers by inhibiting apoptosis. Thpo deficiency-driven reduction of adult BM MPPs was attributed to the shortage of expansion and differentiation of HSCs during neonatal BM hematopoiesis. Drug-mediated enhancement of THPO signaling in neonatal Thpo-deficient mice rescued adult BM progenitor cell numbers but not HSC apoptosis. THPO function is thus limited during adult hematopoiesis but is critical during neonatal development to establish a structured HSC and MPP hierarchy.

Through proliferation and differentiation, stem cells establish a cellular hierarchy to sustain the homeostasis of an organ system. Hematopoietic cell hierarchy consists of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) at its apex followed by multipotent progenitor cells (MPPs) that further produce progenitor cells that are committed to various lineages (Kapadia et al., 2025; Kucinski et al., 2024). Uniquely, the hematopoietic hierarchy is established in various anatomical sites through which hematopoiesis shifts during development. Hematopoiesis occurs in the yolk sac, aorta-gonad-mesonephros (AGM) region in the dorsal aorta and fetal liver of the early embryo and shifts to the bone marrow (BM) before birth (Yokomizo and Suda, 2024). Thereon, HSCs expand and differentiate in the BM during neonatal development and eventually form a quiescent cell pool (Li et al., 2020). Although physiological HSCs rarely divide and are cell cycle quiescent, their proliferation and multilineage differentiation also substantiate native adult hematopoiesis (Morcos et al., 2020). Differentiation and expansion of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in the hematopoietic hierarchy is essential to supplement mature hematopoietic cells circulating the peripheral blood.

The formation of the hematopoietic cell hierarchy is governed by extrinsic and intrinsic cues that change in a life-stage-dependent manner. Thrombopoietin (THPO) is produced by hepatocytes and stimulates through its receptor MPL. MPL is chiefly expressed on BM HSCs and megakaryocyte (MK) progenitor cells (Alexander et al., 1996), and thus, THPO/MPL regulates hematopoietic hierarchy bi-modally by controlling MK/platelet production and HSC self-renewal and maintenance (Qian et al., 2007; Yoshihara et al., 2007). However, Thpo/Mpl-deficient mice also present a loss in progenitor cells in the adult BM and THPO can stimulate the propagation of MPPs in vitro (Tezuka et al., 2024). THPO/MPL regulation on the hematopoietic hierarchy is complex, as deletion of Mpl specifically on MK-lineage cells results in the expansion of HSPCs due to an increase in the availability of THPO in the BM (Ng et al., 2014). Moreover, the expression of MPL on BM progenitor cells and whether and how THPO/MPL signaling affects the overall hematopoietic hierarchy is not fully understood. In addition, while being essential for HSC self-renewal, THPO/MPL signaling has been paradoxically characterized as a factor to maintain HSC quiescence (Nakamura-Ishizu et al., 2021). Unveiling the functional scope of THPO/MPL signaling on hematopoiesis is necessary as the signaling pathway has become a key target for the treatment of BM failure disorders and its stimulation is indispensable for the ex vivo expansion of HSCs for therapeutic applications (Sakurai et al., 2023).

A gradual transcriptional and epigenomic change underlies the transition of embryonic to neonatal to adult HSPCs (Li et al., 2020). Embryonically, Thpo deficiency affects fetal liver (FL) HSC number beginning at embryonic day (E)16.5 (Lee et al., 2022) and circulating platelet numbers at E14.5 (Potts et al., 2015), MPL expression has been noted on hematopoietic cells earlier in the AGM and FL (Harada et al., 2017). Yet, THPO/MPL signaling has been implicated in regulating the self-renewal proliferation of early embryonic MPP as well as HSCs in vitro (Morino-Koga et al., 2024). Discrepancy between MPL expression level and in vivo/in vitro effect of THPO signaling questions the true role of THPO/MPL during the transition from embryonic to adult hematopoietic hierarchy. It remains unclear how progenitor cells are affected by THPO/MPL deficiency during embryo/neonatal development.

In this study, we uncover that THPO/MPL signaling preferentially affects HSCs within the adult BM non-lineage committed HSPC population. Lineage tracing of Mpl expression and inducible Thpo deletion indicates the immediate function of THPO in adult mice as preserving BM HSC number by preventing apoptosis. THPO/MPL signaling was dispensable for both HSC and MPPs in the FL before E14.5, yet it was critical for expanding HSPCs and establishing a quiescent HSC pool during neonatal hematopoiesis. The physiological role of THPO/MPL signaling in structuring the adult BM hematopoietic hierarchy is thus specific to THPO/MPL-driven neonatal HSPC proliferation and adult HSC survival by inhibiting apoptosis.

Changes in progenitor cell populations in adult constitutional Thpo-knockout (Thpo−/−) mice have not been reported in detail. We examined the changes in cell numbers in adult Thpo−/− and constitutional Mpl-knockout (Mpl−/−) mice by utilizing a detailed panel of HSPCs and committed progenitor cell subsets (Figure S1A). Mpl expression may corelate to and explain the changes in MPP1–5 numbers observed in Thpo−/− and Mpl−/− mice. Tracing of Mpl expression in Rosa-TdTomato+/−;Mpl-CreERT2+/− mice exhibited significantly high frequency of TdTomato+-labeled HSCs and MPP1 cells compared to other HSPC subsets, including MkPs, at an early time point (day7) after tamoxifen injection (Figure 1B). All HSPC subsets exhibited high percentage of TdTomato+ cells 3 months after tamoxifen injection, indicating that the differentiation and proliferation of HSCs and MPP1 cells expressing Mpl contribute to form BM cell hierarchy in steady-state adult hematopoiesis. In agreement with the traced expression of Mpl in HSCs and MPP1s in day7 Rosa-TdTomato+/−;Mpl-CreERT2+/− mice, adult Mpl-GFP mice exhibited the highest percentage of GFP+ cells in HSCs and MPP1 cells compared to other HSPC subsets including MkP cells (Figure 1C). However, protein expression of MPL, as assessed by antibody staining, differed from Mpl expression pattern and was significantly high on HSCs compared to other progenitor cells including MPP1 and MkP cells from adult wild-type mice (Figure 1D). These results suggest that post-transcriptional regulation of MPL expression may play a critical role; HSCs expressing the highest levels of MPL protein are the most dependent on THPO/MPL signaling among BM cells.

In order to further clarify the effect of THPO/MPL signaling on the HSPC hierarchy, single-cell genome (single-cell RA sequencing [scRNA-seq]) analysis was conducted on HSPCs from adult Thpo+/+ and Thpo−/− mice. Single-cell gene expressions of Lin-cKit+Sca1+ (LSK) cells from 8-week-old murine BM formed 15 clusters upon dimension reduction by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) (Figure S2A). These cell clusters were annotated as HSC, MPP1–5 populations according to the corresponding scores, which are based on gene sets previously reported by Sommerkamp et al. (2021) (Figures 1E and S2B). Distribution of Thpo+/+ to Thpo−/− clusters for MPP1–5 cells were similar yet differed for HSCs; two clusters, HSC-1 and HSC-2, were annotated as BM HSCs, yet cluster HSC-1 was nearly absent in Thpo−/− mice (Figures 1F and 1G).

To understand how Thpo deficiency affects adult HSCs, differences in the genetic signatures of the adult HSC clusters (HSC-1 and HSC-2) from the scRNA-seq were further analyzed (Figure 1H). Gene ontology analysis exhibited enrichment of programmed cell death, cell proliferation, and lymphocyte-related gene sets in HSC-1 compared to HSC-2 (Figure S2C). Cells in HSC-1, which mapped both of Thpo+/+ and Thpo−/− HSCs, exhibited high expression of apoptosis (Bcl2l11, Bcl2, Stk17b, and Cd47)-, cell proliferation (Flt3, Notch1, Cdk6, Phf14, and Lims1)-, and lymphoid (Satb1, Cd69, Flt3, Ighm, Cd37, Clnk, Mef2c, Syk, Pou2f2, Samsn1, Pag1, Adgrg3, and Ikzf1)-related genes compared to cells in HSC-2, which primarily mapped Thpo+/+ HSCs (Figures 1I–1N). When cells in HSC-1 and HSC-2 clusters were evaluated by a previously developed HSC score to detect quiescent HSCs, the Molecular Overlapping Population (MolO) Score (Wilson et al., 2015), cells in HSC-1 cluster expressed a slightly higher score enrichment compared to those in HSC-2 (Figures S3A and S3B). Conversely, the expression of the no molecular overlap (NoMO) score, which identifies HSCs with cell cycle-progressive properties, was slightly more enriched in HSC-2 (Figures S3C and S3D). Within MolO score genes, cells in HSC-1 expressed high levels of Vwf, Mllt3, and Pdzk1ip1 genes, which label highly potent HSCs or are functionally essential for the preservation and self-renewal of HSCs (Figure S4E) (Busch et al., 2015; Calvanese et al., 2019; Sanjuan-Pla et al., 2013). Thus, HSCs that survive Thpo deficiency through developmental stages to adulthood (HSC-2) are genetically apoptotic, less quiescent, and lymphoid lineage biased.

To further assess the response of various BM HSPC populations to THPO/MPL signaling, we analyzed changes in hematopoiesis in conditional Thpo-knockout adult mice (Thpofl/fl;CreERT2+/−) (Figure 2A). Thpofl/fl;CreERT2+/− mice exhibited significant decrease in plasma THPO levels (Figure 2B) and peripheral blood (PB) platelet number (Figure 2C) as early as 7 days after inducing Thpo deletion with tamoxifen injection. Thpo deletion did not alter total BM cellularity (figure not shown). Within the BM, HSC number rapidly and significantly decreased in Thpofl/fl;CreERT2+/− mice at 7 days after inducing Thpo deletion (Figure 2D). MPP1–5 as well as lineage-committed progenitor cell (GMP, CLP, MkP and CFUE) number were intact in Thpofl/fl;CreERT2+/− mice at 7 days after inducing Thpo deletion (Figures 2E, 2F, and S4A–S4G). Noticeable decrease in the number of BM cell populations other than HSCs was detected in MPP1 (at days 14 and 28), MPP2 (day 28), MPP3 (day 28), MPP4 (day 28), and MPP5 (day 28) at time points later than 7 days after inducing Thpo deletion. Despite early changes in PB platelet numbers, BM MkP number significantly declined only at day 21 after inducing Thpo deletion (Figure S4F). Furthermore, decrease in HSC, MPP, and MkP numbers persisted after 3 months of Thpo deletion, yet GMP, CLP, and CFUE numbers remained intact (Figure S4H). THPO/MPL signaling prevents apoptosis by stimulating AKT (Kozuma et al., 2007) and STAT3 and STAT5 (Qian et al., 2021). Compared to wild-type controls, Thpofl/fl;CreERT2+/− HSCs exhibited significant decrease of phosphorylated-Akt (pAkt) and phosphorylated-STAT3 (pSTAT3), but not phosphorylated-STAT5 (pSTAT5), suggesting that AKT and STAT3 may be the main mediators by which Thpo prevents apoptosis (Figures S6A–S6C). Except for a decrease in pSTAT5 expression in MKPs, the three downstream signals were not suppressed in MKP and MPP cells from Thpofl/fl;CreERT2+/− mice (Figures S6A–S6C). THPO/MPL signaling has also been implicated to promote HSC self-renewal and maintenance through activation of mTOR and CREB signaling pathways (Lee et al., 2022; Tsutsumi et al., 2023). Activation of mTOR and CREB was intact in Thpofl/fl;CreERT2+/− HSCs as well as MKPs and MPPs compared to wild-type controls (Figures S6D and S6E). Collectively, induced Thpo depletion in adult mice primarily affects HSC number by enhancing apoptosis, does not immediately influence cell cycle state, and enhances reconstitution potential of HSCs.

Mpl expression was traced by labeling E10.5 Rosa-TdTomato+/−;Mpl-CreERT2+/− embryos and analyzing them at E14.5. Highest percentage of TdTomato+ was detected on MkP cells compared to other HSPC subsets (Figure S7D). In E14.5 Mpl-Gfp FL cells, Mpl was expressed significantly highly in HSC and MkP cells compared to other progenitor cells (Figure 4E). MPL protein expression was similar in HSCs and CD150+ MPPs but significantly lower in short-term HSCs (ST-HSCs), CD150− MPPs, and LKS− cells compared to HSCs (Figure 4F). Unlike adult HSPCs, FL HSPC numbers do not change in correlation to Mpl expression pattern upon Thpo deficiency, suggesting that sensitivity toward THPO/MPL signaling may differ per cell population and developmental phase. This indicates that FL HSPCs rely less on THPO/MPL signaling.

HSC numbers reportedly start to decline in Thpo−/− mice after day E16.5 of embryogenesis (Lee et al., 2022), yet detailed changes in HSPC populations of Thpo-deficient hematopoiesis during embryonic and neonatal development have not been documented. Furthermore, HSCs transition to quiescence around 3 weeks after birth (Bowie et al., 2006), yet whether THPO affects this transition is not known. We thus analyzed whether HSPC populations changed during fetal (E16.5) and neonatal (postnatal day (P) 6 [P6], P14, and P21) development (Figure S8A) and whether these changes inherently altered the BM cell population numbers observed in adult Thpo−/− mice. In agreement with previous reports (Lee et al., 2022), Thpo−/− mice exhibited decrease in the frequency of HSCs from E16.5 onward (Figure 5A). The frequencies of MPP and ST-HSCs were intact at E16.5 and P6 but significantly decreased postnatally at P14 and P21 in Thpo−/− mice (Figure 5A). Expression of Mpl in E16.5 FL and BM (P6 and P14) cells from Mpl-GFP mice were analyzed (Figures 5B–5D). Mpl expression on HSCs was high, yet did not show significant difference when compared to MkPs and was lower than MPP3/4 populations in E16.5 FL cells (Figure 5B). However, postnatally, Mpl expression in HSCs became significantly high compared to other HSPC populations including MEP and MPP3/4 (Figures 5C and 5D). We here show that THPO/MPL signaling is essential for structuring lifelong BM hematopoietic hierarchy during neonatal hematopoiesis but exclusively protects HSCs from apoptosis in adulthood. Whole-body THPO/MPL knockout affected the number of a wide range of HSPC populations, yet genetic and epigenetic changes were confined to HSCs within adult BM non-lineage committed HSPCs. Induced Thpo deletion in adult mice revealed the immediate function of THPO/MPL as preserving BM HSC number by preventing apoptosis. While Thpo-deficient HSCs and MPPs were intact in the FL before E14.5, Thpo-deficient HSCs could not expand in number and generate MPPs and a quiescent HSC pool during neonatal hematopoiesis. These observations imply that the physiological, in vivo function of THPO/MPL signaling is to structure and establish the BM hematopoietic hierarchy in the neonate and maintain adult HSC numbers by inhibiting apoptosis during adulthood.

THPO is among the few cytokines that directly regulate adult HSCs, yet it has been paradoxically characterized to promote both cell cycle quiescence and self-renewal proliferation in HSCs. Both Thpo−/− and Mpl−/− mice exhibit a significant decline in quiescent HSC number, which implies that THPO/MPL maintains HSCs in cell cycle quiescence (Qian et al., 2007; Yoshihara et al., 2007). While past studies demonstrated loss of repopulation potential of THPO/MPL-deficient BMs using competitive transplantation where a fraction of whole BM mononuclear cells (MNCs) were tested, we have shown that an equal number of isolated individual Thpo−/− BM HSCs exhibit comparable repopulation potential to Thpo+/+ HSCs upon serial BM transplantation (Nakamura-Ishizu et al., 2021). As quiescence is regarded as a requisite of highly potent HSCs, our report questioned the previously accepted context of THPO/MPL signaling regulation on HSCs in vivo. Likewise, in this article, conditional deletion of Thpo in adult mice did not alter HSC cell cycle state or lower individual stem cell potential. Instead, we observed that the loss of HSC numbers after induced Thpo deletion was due to increased apoptosis, which clarifies that physiologically THPO/MPL signaling sustains HSC number by inhibiting apoptosis. Indeed, stem cell potential of HSCs from adult mice with conditional deletion of Thpo exhibited a slightly higher long-term repopulation potential compared to control HSCs, which may indicate that low levels of THPO may protect HSCs from proliferative exhaustion during stress. Our data support that THPO-driven HSC self-renewal plays a key role, not during adult steady-state hematopoiesis, but during neonatal HSC expansion, which ultimately is essential to accumulate HSC and MPP numbers for adult hematopoiesis. Furthermore, it has been reported (Lee et al., 2022) and we have confirmed that liver Thpo transcript gradually increases during neonatal development (data not shown), which also supports the fact that THPO/MPL signaling is crucial during neonatal hematopoiesis. As THPO stimulates HSC self-renewal, THPO receptor agonists are essential for ex vivo HSC expansion (Kobayashi et al., 2019; Sakurai et al., 2023) and are implicated for the treatment of BM failure disorders such as aplastic anemia (Yang et al., 2025; Young, 2018). In relation, our study may provide a rationale for treating congenital BM failure syndromes with THPO receptor agonists at earlier time of disease (Agarwal, 2018; Dufour and Pierri, 2022).

MKs serve as a niche that regulates BM HSC numbers, making the physiological regulation of THPO on BM hematopoiesis complex. We and other groups have shown that MK depletion in adult mice results in loss of HSC quiescence (Nakamura-Ishizu et al., 2014) and enhanced proliferation of HSCs (Bruns et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2014). The number of MPL-expressing cells can also affect HSC numbers by determining the availability of THPO in PB and BM; increased MK/platelet mass results in decline of HSPC number (de Graaf et al., 2010). Furthermore, deletion of Mpl specifically on MK lineage cells using Pf4-Cre mice promotes megakaryopoiesis through enhanced proliferation of HSPCs due to the increase of THPO concentration in the BM (Ng et al., 2014). Our study pinpoints the effect of induced Thpo deletion in adult mice as a decline in HSC cell number, albeit a decrease in platelet and MK lineage cells. In relation, induced deletion of Thpo in adult mice decreased the number PB platelets as well as the number of vWF+ HSCs and CD41mid/high HSCs, which are known to rapidly and directly differentiate to MKs (data not shown) (Haas et al., 2015; Sanjuan-Pla et al., 2013). This clarifies and emphasizes the niche cell-independent role of THPO/MPL signaling in regulating HSCs during steady-state hematopoiesis.

Insufficient THPO/MPL signaling can be the underlying pathophysiology of pediatric and adult diseases. Patients with loss-of-function mutations in Mpl develop congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia (CAMT), a disease that is characterized by severe thrombocytopenia and BM failure in the first decade of life (Geddis, 2011). The course of CAMT can vary depending on the type of Mpl mutation; Mpl mutations involving premature stop codon or frameshift that completely eliminates receptor signaling present with earlier BM failure (Geddis, 2011). However, neither Thpo−/− nor Mpl−/− mice develop pancytopenia or BM failure with age (Figure S3) (Lee and Zhan, 2024), indicating that HSC response to THPO/MPL deficiency may differ by species. Nonetheless, our observation that THPO-mediated HSC regulation is essential for the expansion of HSPC pools during neonatal hematopoiesis may indicate this period of development as the critical window for CAMT treatment. Furthermore, our study may provide a rationale for using THPO receptor agonists of congenital BM failure syndromes (Koker and Polat, 2022). In adults, THPO deficiency frequently complicates acute liver failure or cirrhosis by stimulating thrombocytopenia (Schiødt et al., 2003). BM failure is not a frequent complication of liver failure (Koschade et al., 2023), and our data support these findings as acute THPO deficiency in adults exclusively influences HSCs and not progenitor cells. However, while it may not be apparent in a clinical setting, attention must be given to the chronic effect of THPO-driven loss of HSC number, especially in the context of how it may affect the progression of the original hepatic disease (Nautiyal et al., 2024). In summary, our findings add insight into how cytokine signaling acts differently on various HSPC populations to regulate the transition from fetal to neonate to adult hematopoiesis.
