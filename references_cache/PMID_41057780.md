# Use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in idiopathic intracranial hypertension : a systematic review.
**Authors:** Ognard J, Alipour Khabir S, Ghozy S, El Hajj G, Kallmes KM, Chen JJ, Kadirvel R, Kallmes DF, Brinjikji W
**Journal:** J Headache Pain (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s10194-025-02148-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-025-02148-3)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a debilitating neurologic condition marked by elevated intracranial pressure (ICP), typically affecting obese women of reproductive age. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), widely used for obesity and diabetes, have recently emerged as potential therapeutic agents for IIH. OBJECTIVE: To systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of GLP-1 RAs in IIH, based on clinical studies reporting outcomes related to ICP, headache, vision, cognition, weight loss, and safety. METHODS: A systematic search was conducted across MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science up to June 15, 2025, using PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Studies involving adult IIH patients treated with GLP-1 RAs and reporting relevant clinical outcomes were included. Data extraction and bias assessment were performed independently by multiple reviewers. RESULTS: Nine clinical studies (one RCT, eight observational studies) with IIH patients receiving GLP-1 RAs were included. GLP-1 RA use was consistently associated with improvements in headache frequency, weight reduction, and reduced need for acetazolamide or surgical interventions. In a small RCT, exenatide significantly lowered ICP within hours of administration. Several studies reported papilledema resolution and a lower risk of visual deterioration. Cognitive function either improved or remained stable. Adverse effects were mostly mild gastrointestinal symptoms; no serious safety concerns emerged. Interpretation is limited by the predominance of potentially overlapped retrospective data and minimal randomized evidence. CONCLUSION: GLP-1 RAs show promise as a disease-modifying therapy for IIH, with potential benefits beyond weight loss. The consistency of findings across multiple studies and data sources provides justification of a shift in research focus to conduct well-powered phase 3 randomized controlled trials to validate these preliminary findings and define optimal treatment strategies.

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a neuro-ophtalmological and metabolic condition that is characterized by elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) and papilledema, in the absence of an alternate etiology [1]. The incidence and the prevalence of IIH have been rising in parallel with the global obesity epidemic, reaching about 22 per 100,000 in obese young women [2, 3]. Symptoms include chronic headaches, pulsatile tinnitus; IIH can lead to permanent visual impairment or even blindness if left untreated [4]. Headaches are a major contributor to reduced quality of life in IIH patients, and cognitive complaints have also been reported to improve when ICP is lowered. First-line therapies include weight loss interventions and the carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide, which reduce CSF production. Even a modest 6–10% weight reduction can significantly improve IIH outcomes [5]. However, achieving and maintaining substantial weight loss through diet and lifestyle is challenging, and acetazolamide often provides only partial headache relief while causing dose-limiting side effects such as paresthesia, fatigue, nausea and cognitive impairment. Additional medications including topiramate, diuretics like furosemide, and other off-label ICP-lowering agents have been used; however, their efficacy is limited [6, 7]. Furthermore, these medications can have significant side effects, with cognitive impairment particularly reported in the case of topiramate. In cases refractory to medical management, surgical interventions such as venous sinus stenting [8], optic nerve sheath fenestration (ONSF), and CSF shunting procedures may be necessary [9]. There remains a clear unmet need for effective medical therapies that can reduce ICP and symptom burden in IIH patients without exacerbating morbidities.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have recently gained attention for treating multiple medical conditions, including potential to treat IIH. GLP-1 RAs are established treatments for type 2 diabetes and obesity due to their potent glucose-lowering, appetite-suppressing, and weight-loss effects. Beyond metabolic actions, studies showed central regulatory and neuroprotective effects [10, 11] and GLP-1 receptors are expressed in the choroid plexus of the brain [12, 13]. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT) involving 16 patients with IIH, exenatide significantly lowered ICP at multiple time points and no serious safety concerns were reported [14].

Given the limitations of existing treatments and the multifaceted benefits of GLP-1 RAs, repurposing these agents for IIH management represents an attractive alternative. Early clinical data and recent large-scale real-world studies have begun to explore their efficacy and safety in IIH, suggesting benefits beyond just weight loss.

The primary objective of this systematic review is to provide an updated and comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and safety of GLP-1 RAs in the management of IIH.

No ethical approval was required as the current study involved analyzing and synthesizing previously published data and information from secondary use of deidentified electronic databases (Code of Federal Regulations, 45 CFR 46.102). No protocol was registered. The study was not funded. All data to conduct or reproduce the study is available online. It was conducted and reported per the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 recommendations [15] (see Additional File - Supplementary Material 1).

We included clinical studies evaluating the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists for treatment of idiopathic intracranial hypertension in adults. To be included, studies had to involve patients with a diagnosis of IIH according to established diagnostic (including modified Dandy or Friedman criteria) [16], administer a GLP-1 RA as an intervention, and report outcomes of interest such as ICP/CSF opening pressure, headache frequency/severity, visual function, weight/BMI, cognitive measures, and/or safety outcomes. Studies were included regardless of publication status or language, but we excluded any preclinical studies (animal or in vitro), case reports or small case series (sample size < 5), conference abstracts or posters, and review articles. We included both peer-reviewed articles and preprints if available on the initial databases searched. We assessed and reported any overlapping populations.

The literature search, duplicate removal, and article screening and evaluation processes were conducted using the Nested Knowledge® (NK) AutoLit® software, version 1.89(NK, St Paul, Minnesota, USA). A systematic literature search was performed in databases: MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, Web Of Science, covering all entries from database inception to June 15, 2025. The search strategy combined terms for GLP-1 receptor agonists with terms for idiopathic intracranial hypertension. The full detailed search algorithms for each database are provided in Additional File - Supplementary Material 2. To ensure literature saturation, we also hand-searched the reference lists of all included studies and relevant review articles for any additional eligible studies not captured by the database search. We also searched for information for ongoing clinical trials.

The study selection process was also conducted using NK AutoLit® software. Initially, full titles and abstracts were screened independently by two authors (JO, SAK) to identify potentially eligible studies. We excluded obviously irrelevant records that did not address IIH, GLP-1 RA, and for potentially relevant, we obtained full-text articles, and the same reviewers independently assessed each full text against the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Any disagreements in study selection were resolved reaching a consensus.

Using a standardized data extraction form, the following information was collected (JO): first author, publication year, study design, sample size, patient demographic and baseline characteristics, details of the intervention and comparison, and outcomes. Efficacy outcomes extracted included changes in: ICP or opening pressure, headache frequency, headache severity scores, visual acuity, visual field mean deviation, papilledema grade, retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness on optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the optic disc, and any measures of cognitive function. Safety outcomes were also recorded, including incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs), serious adverse events (SAEs), any adverse events leading to drug discontinuation, changes in concomitant IIH medications requirement of surgical, and mortality during follow-up. When outcomes were reported at multiple time points, we chose to display all of them. If outcomes were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), we estimated the corresponding means and standard deviations (SD) using established methods [17].Specifically, the median was used as an estimate of the mean under the assumption of approximate symmetry of the underlying distribution, and the IQR was converted into an SD accounting for sample size and distributional assumptions. In cases where only ranges were reported, similar transformation methods were applied. If only confidence intervals (CIs) around the mean were available, SDs were back-calculated from the standard errors of the mean. From these derived values, 95% CIs were calculated when not directly reported in the source article. All extracted data were cross-checked by a second reviewer for accuracy (GEH) and confirmed by consensus.

We evaluated the methodological quality and risk of bias of included studies using tools appropriate for each study design. For the single randomized controlled trial, we used the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool [18]. For non-randomized intervention studies, we used the ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions) V2 tool [19, 20]. Two reviewers (JO, GEH) independently judged each bias domain for each study as low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias, per tool guidelines. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion to reach a consensus rating. We summarized the risk of bias results in text and in a “traffic light” plot (see Additional File - Supplementary Material 3).

The level of evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) [21, 22].Two reviewers (JO, GEH) applied GRADE per outcome (RCT evidence starts “high,” observational “low”), considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Without a meta-analysis, imprecision was judged from sample sizes and CI width; indirectness from use of coded outcomes or short follow-up. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. This is reported in Additional File - Supplementary Material 4.

The authors employed simple prompts such as “refine”, “scientific tone”, or “translate” and retain full responsibility for the accuracy and integrity of the scientific content and originality of the work.

The database search yielded a total of 584 records after removal of duplicates. Of these, 572 were excluded at full title/abstract screening as they did not meet inclusion criteria. Eighteen full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ten studies were excluded for the following reasons: Two were reviews [23, 24]; one concerned ocular hypertension and GLP-1 RA [25]; one was a previous conference abstract [26] of a published included article [27]; one was a previous preprint [28] of a published included article [29]; two were animal studies on the effect of GLP-1 RA on ICP of rodent models [12, 30]; one was a case report [31] but describing presence of IIH after cessation of GLP-1 RA; one was an opinion paragraph [32]; one did not use GLP-1 RA but instead assessed GLP-1 levels in IIH patients after bariatric surgery [33]. Eight studies, including one preprint [34], met al.l criteria and were included in the systematic review [14, 27, 29, 34–38]. Subsequently, during the manuscript preparation phase, one additional eligible study was published and was also included in the final synthesis [39]. Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics of the 9 included studies. The publication years ranged from 2023 to 2025. Study sample sizes varied widely: the smallest was an RCT with 16 participants [14] and the largest was a multi-center study using TriNetX platform with ~ 11,500 participants (5750 IIH patient treated with GLP-1 RA and 5750 matched controls [37]). Collectively, the studies included at least (considering the risk of overlap) a total of 1074 IIH patients that received a GLP-1 RA as part of the study intervention. Five studies [29, 34, 36, 37, 39] relied on the same large-scale TriNetX platform [40]. Three studies by the same group, Azzam et al. [29, 34, 36], stratified analyses by specific GLP-1 receptor agonists (tirzepatide, liraglutide, and semaglutide), suggesting non-overlapping treatment groups. However, all three studies employed propensity score matching, which may have led to an overlap in the controls. Additionally, the other two studies using the TriNetX platform (O’Leary et al. [37] and Sioutas et al. [39]) included all GLP-1 RAs assessed in the three prior studies, further increasing the risk of substantial overlap in both exposure and control arms. Therefore, these five reports using the TriNetX platform are not independent and likely include a high degree of patient duplication. This is described in Additional File - Supplementary material 5. The RCT by Mitchell et al. [14], the “IIH: Pressure” trial, and the nested ophthalmic analysis by Grech et al. [35] also describe outcomes from the same cohort of 16 participants and studied exenatide, albeit with different endpoints. The remaining seven studies were non-randomized: one was a prospective open-label pilot study [27] in which 13 IIH patients chose to receive GLP-1 RAs (semaglutide, liraglutide) added to usual care, compared to 26 matched controls (usual care alone). The other six were retrospective controlled studies. Only two studies [27, 38] represent independent clinical datasets, though both are limited in size.

Participants were predominantly women (> 85%), with mean ages in the 20–40 s and mean BMI in the mid‑30s to low‑40s kg/m². All studies included patients diagnosed with IIH; however, TriNetX-derived cohorts identified cases via coded data rather than formal adjudication against the modified Dandy or Friedman criteria. Patient were predominantly female (> 90% in most cohorts) and obese (mean BMI ~ 35–42 kg.m−2). Baseline IIH severity varied: in the RCT [14, 35], all patients had active papilledema and elevated ICP > 25 cm H₂O by inclusion criteria. In a prospective open-label pilot study by Krajnc et al., patients had chronic IIH under treatment (some had persistent papilledema ~ 70% at baseline) [27]. In the studies using TriNetX platform, baseline is inferred from of a diagnosis code rather than a graded measure of disease severity. O’Leary et al. noted that they did not exclude patients with mild visual field defect, making their sample more representative of typical IIH, though they performed matching on baseline comorbidities. Importantly, their cohort had slightly higher baseline BMI in the GLP-1 RA group (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, mean ~ 42 vs. 36, standardized difference ~ 1.08 kg.m−2) even after matching [37]. Similarly, Azzam et al. studies each performed propensity score matching on multiple factors, achieving baseline balance except for a residual higher BMI in the GLP-1 RA groups (tirzepatide, liraglutide, semaglutide). Sioutas et al. also employed propensity score matching across several factors and achieved balanced baseline characteristics including severity and BMI [39]. The studied GLP-1 RAs included exenatide [14, 35, 37, 39], liraglutide [27, 36–39], semaglutide [27, 34, 37–39], lixisenatide [37, 39], dulaglutide [37, 39], albiglutide [39] and the dual agonist (GLP-1 and Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)) tirzepatide [29, 37]. In all observational studies, GLP-1 RAs were used as adjuncts to standard IIH care with nearly all patients also receiving acetazolamide. In Kravetz et al. single center retrospective study, all patients were on acetazolamide, and the GLP-1 RA group (liraglutide, semaglutide) consisted of those who had a GLP-1 RA added to their regimen [38]. Similarly, in Krajnc et al. prospective open-label study, the GLP-1 RA group (liraglutide, semaglutide) continued acetazolamide at a mean dose of approximately 1000 mg, with the option to taper if headaches improved [27]. In the RCT (exenatide), patients on acetazolamide were excluded to better isolate the effect of exenatide during the 12-week trial period [14, 35]. O’Leary et al. TriNetX platform study specifically excluded patients with prior acetazolamide use at baseline to capture new prescriptions during follow-up. They found significantly fewer new acetazolamide prescriptions in the GLP-1 RA group (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide) compared to controls [37]. Sioutas et al. [39] defined treatment (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, albiglutide) and control groups based on GLP-1 RA exposure: the treatment group included patients who initiated a GLP-1 RA within 6 months of IIH diagnosis, while the control group consisted of patients who never initiated a GLP-1 RA but were managed with conventional therapies such as acetazolamide, topiramate and furosemide.

The duration of follow-up/intervention ranged from 3 months to 24 months across studies. Generally, outcomes were reported at interim points (3, 6, 12, 24 months).

All studies measured some form of headache and body weight change as an outcome. Eight studies evaluated papilledema or visual outcomes, though methods varied (clinical papilledema grade vs. OCT RNFL vs. visual field exams) [14, 27, 29, 34, 36–39]. Four studies included direct ICP measurements or ICP-related outcomes [14, 29, 37, 39]. Grech et al. nested ophthalmic analysis of the RCT (exenatide) was the only study to conduct formal neuropsychological testing for cognitive function assessment, whereas O’Leary et al. TriNetX platform study examined ‘cognitive deficits’ based on ICD-10 coding. Risk of bias was rated low for the RCT in most domains [14]. Observational studies had moderate risk of bias; strengths included use of matching to address confounding, but limitations were mainly the reliance on coding. Krajnc et al. prospective study had bias due to non-random patient self-selection into treatment vs. control, despite matching for baseline BMI/age [27]. No studies were excluded for quality reasons. One TriNetX study was a preprint, and caution is warranted given the absence of peer review [34]. GRADE was rated very low to moderate, across the reported outcomes.

Headache-related disability was captured using the Headache Impact Test (HIT-6); this was collected in the exenatide RCT, whereas the remaining cohorts assessed coded headache diagnoses. 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) was also reported in this study to evaluate the quality of life [41]. Reduction in headache was a consistent finding across the studies using GLP-1 RAs. Figure 2 illustrates the effect. In the RCT by Mitchell et al. [14], patients kept 28-day headache diaries. By 3 months, those on exenatide had a mean reduction of − 7.7 monthly headache days (from a baseline of ~ 22 days/month) compared to a −1.5 day change in the placebo arm, although the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance (exenatide: p = 0.069 within-group; placebo: p = 0.404). At baseline, HIT-6 scores were significantly higher in the exenatide arm, and neither HIT-6 nor SF-36 showed significant changes over the course of the trial. In the open-label study by Krajnc et al. [27], headache days per month significantly improved with semaglutide or liraglutide: the median change at 6 months was − 4 days in the GLP-1 group versus 0 days in the control group (p = 0.02). Furthermore, 77% of GLP-1–treated patients achieved a ≥ 50% reduction in headache frequency, compared to 40% of controls (p = 0.04). Notably, 31% of patients on GLP-1 RAs became completely headache-free at 6 months, versus only 8% of those on usual care (this difference did not remain significant after adjusting for weight loss). They also reported that the reduction in headache days correlated with the amount of weight lost.

Azzam et al. [29] found a modest but significant reduction in “headache risk” with tirzepatide: by 24 months, the risk of having headaches was ~ 20% lower in the tirzepatide group compared to controls (RR 0.803, 95% CI 0.668–0.966). In contrast, the same group observed improvements in headache-related outcomes with liraglutide that did not reach statistical significance [36]. Importantly, none of the studies reported any worsening of headaches with GLP-1 RAs.

Direct assessment of ICP was limited to the single included RCT by Mitchell et al. [14], in which telemetric intracranial pressure monitors allowed for continuous ICP recording. Exenatide led to a rapid ICP reduction: mean opening pressure decreased by 5.7 cmCSF at 2.5 h post-dose (placebo-corrected difference; p = 0.048) and by 6.4 cmCSF at 24 h (p = 0.030). At 3 months, the exenatide group maintained a 5.6 cmCSF reduction relative to placebo (between-group difference: − 4.1 mmHg, equivalent to − 5.6 cmCSF), p = 0.058). Notably, all participants started with very high ICP (mean ~ 30.6 cmCSF), so a 5–6 cmCSF reduction was felt to be clinically meaningful (roughly 15–20% reduction).

No other study directly measured CSF pressure; instead, several coded outcomes have been declared to be used as indirect proxies for ICP control. For example, the occurrence of a therapeutic CSF shunt procedure may reflect disease escalation.disease. In O’Leary et al. analysis [37], patients on GLP-1 RAs (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide) had a significantly lower 1-year shunt placement rate (OR 0.375, 95% CI 0.171–0.753, p = 0.047). Sioutas et al. [39] likewise found significantly lower rates of CSF shunt procedures in the GLP-1 RA group (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, albiglutide) compared to controls (≤ 1.8% vs. 5.2%; RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.17–0.70; p = 0.002). Similarly, Azzam et al. [29] examined “treatment resistance” in IIH (defined by authors as composite of indicators including requirement for escalation of medical therapy, progression to surgical intervention, need for CSF diversion procedures, or optic nerve decompression or sheath fenestration)) and noted a trend toward fewer refractory cases with tirzepatide (20.5% risk reduction, RR 0.795, p = 0.019 at 24 months).

No significant change in visual acuity attributable to GLP-1 RAs was detected in the short term. In the “IIH: Pressure” RCT, both exenatide and placebo groups had normal baseline acuity; there was a slight trend toward better acuity in the exenatide arm at 3 months (mean improvement ~ 0.08 logMAR vs. baseline, p = 0.07) [14]. Similarly Krajnc et al. open-label study reported no significant change in best-corrected visual acuity from baseline to 6 months in either GLP-1 RAs (semaglutide, liraglutide) or control groups [27]. Kravetz et al. single center retrospective study found no difference in visual acuity outcomes over 1 year between those on acetazolamide alone vs. acetazolamide plus GLP-1 RAs (semaglutide, liraglutide) [38].

In the “IIH: Pressure” RCT, by 3 months, both groups improved slightly and no significant between-group difference in perimetric visual field mean deviation PMD change was reported [14]. In Krajnc et al. open-label study, there were no significant changes in visual field or OCT measures from baseline to six months in either group. Only a minority of participants had substantial baseline visual field deficits; by six months, the rates of “visual worsening” (defined as any deterioration in acuity or field) were essentially identical (8.3% in the GLP-1 RA group versus 8.0% in controls) [27]. Kravetz et al. observed no differences in visual field improvement over 12 months between the GLP-1 group and controls – both groups improved comparably under acetazolamide, reflecting that adding a GLP-1 RA did not yield an extra short-term visual field gain beyond standard care [38]. However, the larger and longer-term big data studies suggest possible protective effects of GLP-1 RAs on vision. O’Leary et al., using diagnostic codes, found that GLP-1 RA (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide) use was associated with a reduction in the odds of visual loss: at 6 months, OR 0.423 (95% CI 0.324–0.546), and at 1 year, OR 0.606 (0.489–0.747), compared to matched controls [37].

Azzam et al. specifically looked at papilledema and visual disturbance risks [29]. By 24 months, the proportion of patients with ongoing papilledema was lower in the tirzepatide group (RR 0.32, 95% CI 0.189–0.542). Concurrently, the risk of visual disturbances or blindness was reduced to ~ 26% of that in controls (RR 0.261, 95% CI 0.14–0.48). Their liraglutide study [36], showed similar findings, with a reduced papilledema risk at 3 months (RR ~ 0.33) that persisted at 24 months (RR ~ 0.52). Krajnc et al. reported higher rates of papilledema resolution by 6 months in the GLP-1 RA group. Importantly, patients treated with GLP-1 RAs required less acetazolamide, yet showed no worsening of papilledema [27]. Kravetz et al. single center retrospective study specifically measured OCT RNFL thickness changes over 12 months; they found no significant difference between GLP-1 RA combination therapy vs. acetazolamide monotherapy; both groups showed similar RNFL improvement [38]. Sioutas et al. further support these findings, reporting significantly lower rates of papilledema (2.2% vs. 11.5%; RR 0.19, 95% CI 0.10–0.34; p < 0.001) and visual disturbances or blindness (7.0% vs. 11.7%; RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.41–0.88; p = 0.007) in GLP-1 RA users (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, albiglutide) compared to controls at 1 year [39].

By design, GLP-1 RAs induce weight loss, though the magnitude depends on duration and the specific agent. In the RCT, no significant weight difference emerged between exenatide and placebo [14]. Both groups had very modest weight changes (exenatide: −0.1 kg, placebo: −1.3 kg on average, no significant difference). In contrast, the 6-month open-label pilot study by Krajnc et al. showed weight loss. The GLP-1 RAs group (semaglutide, liraglutide) lost a mean of 12.0% (± 3.3%) of their body weight, versus 2.8% (± 4.7%) in the control group (p < 0.001) [27]. In absolute terms, this corresponded to a decrease in BMI of − 3.4 kg/m2 in the GLP-1 group vs. − 1.7 kg/m2 in controls (p = 0.029). Additionally, 69% of GLP-1 patients achieved ≥ 10% weight loss in 6 months, compared to only 4% of controls. The observational cohorts similarly demonstrated greater weight/BMI reduction with GLP-1 RAs, though the absolute differences were smaller than in the controlled pilot. O’Leary et al. reported that at 6 and 12 months, the GLP-1 RAs cohort (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide) had a significantly larger BMI drop (–1.64 kg/m2 vs. − 0.76 kg/m2, respectively, at 12 months) [37]. Azzam et al. found that tirzepatide produced a sustained weight/BMI reduction: by 24 months, patients had lost on average 1.15 kg/m2 more than matched controls (95% CI − 1.415 to − 0.879, p < 0.001) [29]. This was observed with liraglutide as well [29, 36]. However, in the large retrospective study by Sioutas et al., BMI at 1 year was not significantly different between groups (40.6 ± 9.2 in GLP-1 RA users (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, albiglutide) vs. 39.5 ± 8.7 in controls; p = 0.10) [39]. The effects on BMI change across studies are summarized in Additional File - Supplementary material 7.

In the exenatide RCT [14], measures as blood pressure, lipids, HbA1c/fasting glucose/insulin, and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry body composition showed no significant between-arm differences (all p ≥ 0.088). The other studies do not report metabolic data as outcome measures; they appear only as baseline comorbidities used for adjustment/matching.

Chronic IIH has been associated with cognitive difficulties. Grech et al. [35] specifically examined cognition in IIH patients from the “IIH: Pressure” RCT [14]. At baseline, they confirmed that IIH patients had impaired cognitive performance in domains of fluid intelligence, attention, and executive function (mean T-scores ~ 33–38, where 50 is population mean). After 3 months of treatment, both exenatide and placebo groups showed improvements in cognitive outcomes, with no significant difference between the groups. Importantly, exenatide did not impair cognition in any domain. Within the exenatide group, however, there were significant improvements from baseline in several cognitive measures, for example, fluid intelligence improved from 38.4 to 52.9 (p = 0.0005), processing speed from 43.7 to 58.4 (p = 0.0058), and episodic memory from 49.4 to 62.1 (p = 0.03). In O’Leary et al. [37], they defined “cognitive deficits” as documented cognitive impairment in the records. They found the GLP-1 RA group (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide) had significantly lower odds of new or ongoing cognitive deficits at 6 months (OR 0.368, 95% CI 0.246–0.539). At 1 year, this protective association persisted but was attenuated (OR ~ 0.59, p = 0.006). The effects on cognitive outcomes across studies are summarized in Additional File - Supplementary material 8.

Across all studies, GLP-1 RAs were generally well tolerated in the IIH population, consistent with their known safety profile in other indications (diabetes/obesity). No study reported any serious adverse event (SAE) related to GLP-1 RAs use, such as pancreatitis, thyroid cancer, or severe hypoglycemia. In the exenatide RCT [14] eight adverse events occurred in the exenatide arm, seven of which were nausea related to exenatide initiation. No patients withdrew due to adverse events. All participants remained compliant with assigned treatment.

Similarly, Krajnc et al. reported no serious adverse events; all adverse effects were classified as mild or moderate [27] using semaglutide or liraglutide. Gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms were by far the most common side effects. Nine out of 13 patients (69%) on GLP-1 RAs experienced transient GI AEs (mostly nausea and some vomiting or diarrhea), but importantly none of these led to treatment discontinuation.

O’Leary et al. interestingly found significantly lower mortality in GLP-1 RAs users (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide) at 6 and 12 months [37] (0.8% vs. ~ 7%, OR 0.115). Sioutas et al. [39] similarly found lower 1-year mortality among GLP-1 RA users (exenatide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, albiglutide) compared to controls (0.5% vs. 2.6%; RR 0.20, 95% CI 0.06–0.66; p = 0.006). The effects on mortality across studies are summarized in Additional File - Supplementary material 9.

A notable quality-of-life outcome is that GLP-1 RA treatment allowed for lower doses of acetazolamide to be used. Krajnc et al. found the median acetazolamide dosage in the GLP-1 group decreased by 16.5% over 6 months, whereas it stayed roughly the same in controls (0% change, p = 0.04 for difference) [27]. In some GLP-1 patients, acetazolamide was stopped without relapsing of papilledema or headache. O’Leary et al. reported the odds of needing acetazolamide at 6 months were 70% lower in those on GLP-1 RAs (OR 0.295), and still 63% lower at 1 year (OR 0.374) [37]. Sioutas et al. [39] also reported significantly lower 1-year use of several IIH-related medications in the GLP-1 RA group compared to controls: tricyclic antidepressants (6.8% vs. 12.3%; p = 0.002), topiramate (11.2% vs. 19.6%; p < 0.001), furosemide (6.8% vs. 14.4%; p < 0.001), and acetazolamide (8.6% vs. 24.0%; p < 0.001). The effects on medication and disease control across studies are summarized in Additional File - Supplementary material 10 and 11, respectively.

One external reference described a case of IIH developing after abrupt cessation of GLP-1 RA therapy [31]. However, none of the included studies reported paradoxical worsening of IIH symptoms upon GLP-1 RA initiation.

Two prospective trials are testing semaglutide in IIH. IIH: DUAL (NCT06027567, phase 4) is a randomized study investigating whether an initial very low-calorie diet for 8 weeks following the diagnosis of IIH combined with GLP1-RA treatment throughout 10 months is tolerated and more efficient in achieving substantial weight loss and reduction of intracranial pressure than standard weight management. An exploratory study in China (NCT06361823) was last listed as not yet recruiting. By contrast, the phase-III exenatide trial (IIH EVOLVE, NCT05347147) was terminated in 2023 after a major downscaling of its target sample. A phase-III tirzepatide program (IIH Advance [42]) has been announced but is not yet open or registered.

In this systematic review, we gathered the therapeutic role of GLP-1 RAs in IIH across multiple domains. The literature tends to demonstrate that GLP-1 RAs may confer significant benefits in IIH patients: they reduce intracranial pressure and headache frequency, promote papilledema resolution with consequent visual stabilization, facilitate weight loss, and potentially improve cognitive symptoms. These benefits come with a tolerable safety profile, mainly limited to manageable gastrointestinal side effects. To our knowledge, this represents the most comprehensive synthesis to date of clinical outcomes with GLP-1 RAs in IIH.

The mechanisms by which GLP-1 RAs may improve IIH outcomes are likely multifactorial. GLP-1 RAs have demonstrated neuroprotective effects [10] and their receptors are expressed in the choroid plexus of the brain [12, 13]. Preclinical data on GLP-1R signaling and CSF dynamics are mixed. In a rat model, GLP-1R agonism (exendin-4) lowered intracranial pressure and reduced Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity in choroid plexus [12]. By contrast, a recent study found that intracerebroventricular GLP-1R activation increased CSF secretion and raised ICP, whereas GLP-1R inhibition reduced CSF secretion and ICP; importantly, inhibition reduced choroid-plexus Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Na⁺,K⁺,2Cl⁻ cotransporter 1 (NKCC1) activity, and peripheral administration had no detectable effect on CSF flow [30]. Overall, GLP-1R signaling clearly modulates choroid-plexus transport, but the direction of effect appears context-dependent, so the mechanism in humans remains unresolved. Additionally, by acting on hypothalamic satiety centers and peripheral adipose tissue, GLP-1 RAs promote sustained weight loss. Weight loss is associated with lower intracranial pressure in IIH [43]. Mechanistically, obesity can raise intra-abdominal and central venous pressures, which may secondarily elevate intracranial venous pressures [44]; BMI also correlates with dural venous sinus pressures in IIH [45]. Nevertheless, no study has directly shown that reducing abdominal adiposity lowers intra-abdominal or venous sinus pressures in IIH.These agents may also attenuate central inflammation and improve hypothalamic autonomic tone, further stabilizing optic nerve function and raising headache thresholds [46]. Tirzepatide, through dual GLP-1 and GIP receptor activation, further enhances metabolic effects, which may explain its reported superior and more durable improvements in papilledema and visual outcomes compared to single-receptor GLP-1 RAs [29].

A particularly important and consistent finding across studies was reduction in headache frequency. IIH headaches can be debilitating; thus, a decrease of even 3–5 days per month may improve quality of life, given that IIH patients often experience 15–20 days of headache per month. This reduction is comparable to what has been observed with acetazolamide in IIH: the landmark IIHTT trial found a ~ 2 days per month difference vs. placebo, albeit in the context of weight management [47]. Notably, some patients receiving GLP-1 RAs achieved complete headache resolution, an outcome rarely seen with standard treatments. Improved headache control in GLP-1 RA patients likely stems from lower ICP fluctuations and possibly from direct neuromodulatory effects of GLP-1 in pain pathways.

Weight loss itself remains a cornerstone of IIH management, and GLP-1 RAs help patients achieve a ~ 10–12% weight loss within 6 months, which is a major advantage, given that even 6–10% weight loss can improve IIH outcomes. Notably, weight regain is a common trigger for IIH relapse [48]. Sustained weight loss has been reported with ongoing GLP-1 RA therapy, including up to 24 months in tirzepatide users. This raises the possibility that long-term treatment could help maintain weight reduction, which may in turn contribute to prolonged improvements in IIH, such as ICP normalization and papilledema resolution. In Krajncet al. open-label study, adjusting for weight loss attenuated the headache reduction benefit of semaglutide/liraglutide [27]. However, GLP-1 RAs may confer additional benefits beyond weight reduction alone. Evidence from the “IIH: Pressure” RCT demonstrating a rapid intracranial pressure (ICP) reduction within hours of exenatide administration supports a direct pharmacologic effect [14]. This rapid action could be leveraged in acute IIH management; for instance, exenatide injections might serve as a temporizing measure to lower ICP in fulminant IIH while preparing for definitive therapy. More research is needed on acute use.Future studies should clarify the relative contributions of weight loss versus direct drug effects, though both mechanisms are likely beneficial in practice [49].

An important consideration in long-term GLP-1 RA therapy is cost. GLP-1 RAs are expensive, and loss of insurance coverage can lead to abrupt treatment discontinuation [50]. After stopping treatment, patients may need careful monitoring and continued management.

Independent of obesity, women with IIH a have higher cardiovascular event risk when matched to controls by BMI and age [51]. Accordingly, GLP-1 receptor have been shown to reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events across a broad range of patients, both with and without type 2 diabetes mellitus and/or established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [52]. A similar rationale may apply to obstructive sleep apnea, which is highly prevalent in IIH [53] and has recently been shown to improve with tirzepatide [54]. Whether these benefits extend to the IIH population remains to be determined.

To improve treatment adherence, it is also crucial to counsel patients about the potential adverse effects. Patients should be counseled about the high likelihood of nausea and GI upset, especially in the first 4–8 weeks of therapy [55]. Clinical trials have shown that semaglutide and liraglutide are generally well tolerated, with gastrointestinal side effects being the most commonly reported AEs, including nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, and constipation, leading to dose reduction or temporary treatment interruption [56]. GLP-1 RAs alter gastrointestinal motility at all levels of the GI tract, with delayed gastric emptying being the most well established [55]. Slow titration of dose can help, as can dietary adjustments. The absence of any serious adverse events in the studies is encouraging, but continued vigilance is needed, monitoring for rare events like pancreatitis or gallstones.

The closest parallel to GLP-1 RAs in terms of effect size is perhaps bariatric surgery, which has been shown to induce reduction or normalization in ICP below 25 cmCSF, resolution of papilledema, and favorable headache outcomes in both case series and a randomized controlled trial. GLP-1 RAs can achieve surgical-level weight loss, with studies in obesity reporting an average of 15–20% weight loss over 1–2 years [5].

The eventuality of a reduction in acetazolamide dosage is particularly important given that acetazolamide is associated with a variety of side effects, including cognition and memory impairment, that can negatively impact patient adherence to treatment [57]. With the available data, we could not determine whether patients with moderate or greater visual loss at baseline derived additional benefit from GLP-1 RAs. Future prospective studies should address this by stratifying outcomes according to baseline visual severity.

Despite promising findings, several limitations should be noted. A key limitation of this review is the potential overlap between studies, which prevented meta-analytic pooling. Although study quality was assessed, inherent biases such as unmeasured confounding and selection bias, persist. This is particularly relevant for findings derived from repeated use of the TriNetX platform, which should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive evidence of efficacy.

Sioutas et al. [39] report strikingly low papilledema in patients labeled as IIH which raise concern that many patients may have been misclassified on a potential under‑coding of papilledema (and IIH itself). Headache and visual symptoms are likewise unlikely to be recorded systematically, which also adds uncertainty. TriNetX relies chiefly on provider-entered diagnoses and visual-loss codes may be deferred until permanence is confirmed, leading to under-ascertainment and potential skew in visual outcome estimates. More broadly, retrospective electronic health record case‑control designs are prone to unmeasured confounding and treatment‑dependent documentation biases, and propensity‑score matching can still yield suboptimal balance by collapsing complex covariates into a single score. There was also few differences in methodology in these TriNetX cohorts : first, headache was captured differently: Sioutas et al. [39] used the code R51 “Headache,” whereas Azzam et al. [29, 34, 36] used G44.-. Second, drug exposure was defined at different granularities: Sioutas et al. [39] identified medication classes using RxNorm with VA/ATC classification (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs drug classification system/Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System, class-level GLP-1 RA), while Azzam et al. flagged specific agents via RxNorm concept identifiers (Normalized Names for Clinical Drugs : U.S. National Library of Medicine standard terminology for medications, tirzepatide/semaglutide). Third, Sioutas et al. published explicit ICD/CPT (Current Procedural Terminology : American Medical Association coding system for procedures and services) code lists for outcomes and procedures (papilledema (H47.1), visual dysfunction (H53-H54), venous-sinus stenting (CPT 37238/37239), ONSF (67570), and multiple CSF-shunt codes) whereas Azzam et al. [29, 34, 36] used a composite “treatment resistance” endpoint that encompassed escalation to surgery/CSF diversion without enumerating the underlying CPT sets. Finally, outcome timing differed: Azzam et al. [29, 34, 36] reported results at prespecified 3, 6, 12, and 24-month intervals, while Sioutas et al. [39] assessed events occurring from 1 day up to 1 year after index therapy.

Additionally, observational studies are also susceptible to treatment allocation bias. For instance, patients who receive GLP-1 RAs may be more motivated or closely followed up, which itself could lead to better outcomes independent of the drug. Moreover, the only included RCT was small (N = 16 completed), although well-conducted, is underpowered for some outcomes. Thus, the highest-level evidence for some outcomes is still lacking. We mitigated this by looking at concordant evidence across studies.

Another limitation is the lack of long-term data beyond two years. IIH is often a chronic, relapsing condition, and it remains unclear whether GLP-1 RAs provide sustained effects after discontinuation or primarily control the disease during active treatment. Also, there is scant discussion of IIH in patients without obesity, where GLP-1 RAs may be less effective given their primarily metabolic mechanism of action. None of the included studies targeted non-obese IIH specifically (almost all patients had BMI > 30).

The consistency of findings across multiple retrospective cohorts and data sources provides a compelling signal of efficacy on clinical endpoints, justifying a shift in research focus: the field now needs adequately powered phase 3 randomized controlled trials to validate these preliminary findings [58], define optimal treatment strategies such as dosing and treatment duration, and establish the role of GLP-1 RAs in both acute and long-term management of IIH. It would also be informative to compare GLP-1 RAs head-to-head with acetazolamide.

In conclusion, GLP-1 RAs represent a promising advancement in the treatment of IIH. Our systematic review found that GLP-1 RAs (such as exenatide, liraglutide, semaglutide, and the dual agonist tirzepatide) may significantly improve multiple facets of IIH: headaches, visual function, and induce substantial weight loss. These benefits were achieved without serious adverse effects, and GLP-1 RA use was associated with reduced reliance on acetazolamide and avoidance of surgical interventions. Positive outcomes were consistent across the evidence base we reviewed, however, some of the included studies showed a potentially significant overlap, which limits the overall level of evidence. Given the strength of these preliminary signals, the field must now shift focus toward conducting well-powered phase 3 RCTs to validate these associations. Ongoing research is needed to clarify optimal dosing strategies, appropriate duration of therapy, and long-term outcomes, including the potential for sustained ICP reduction to below 25 cmCSF, resolution of papilledema, and improvement in headache outcomes.
