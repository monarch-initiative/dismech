# Negative pressure wound therapy versus conventional wound care following surgical debridement of fournier's gangrene: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies.
**Authors:** Farooq T, Asif A, Abufatima IO, Riaz A, Manzoor I, Rehman R, Ullah B, Mehmood MS, Ali ME, Amin MHJ
**Journal:** Langenbecks Arch Surg (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00423-026-04213-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00423-026-04213-0)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Fournier's gangrene is associated with high morbidity and mortality, and optimal postoperative wound management remains uncertain. This study compared negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) with conventional wound care following surgical debridement.

METHODS: A systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Embase, and Scopus were searched to September 2025. Outcomes were pooled using random-effects models and reported as mean differences (MD) or risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals.

RESULTS: Eighteen studies (691 patients) were included. NPWT significantly reduced postoperative analgesic use (MD - 2.16; 95% CI - 3.63 to - 0.70; p = 0.004) and time to wound closure (MD - 10.17; 95% CI - 24.98 to 4.29; p = 0.17). There were no significant differences in mortality (RR 0.66; 95% CI 0.44-1.31), number of debridements (MD - 0.83; 95% CI - 2.42 to 0.76), or length of hospital stay (MD - 1.03; 95% CI - 4.28 to 2.21). Heterogeneity was high across outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS: NPWT is associated with reduced postoperative analgesic consumption following debridement for Fournier's gangrene. However, based on low-to-moderate certainty observational evidence, it does not significantly reduce mortality, surgical debridement frequency, length of stay, or overall time to wound closure. Consequently, NPWT should be considered a supportive adjunct rather than a replacement for standard surgical management. Further high-quality, prospective comparative studies are required to define its cost-effectiveness and optimal clinical indications.

Fournier’s gangrene (FG) is a fulminant necrotizing fasciitis of the perineal, perianal, and genital regions [ 1 ]. While rare, its annual incidence of 1.6–3.3 cases per 100,000 population is rising globally, predominantly affecting males, though female presentations are often characterized by wider tissue destruction and higher mortality [ 2– 4 ].

FG is strongly associated with comorbidities that compromise host immunity and tissue perfusion [ 5 ]. Diabetes mellitus, occurring in as many as 60% of patients, remains the single most important predisposing condition [ 6 , 7 ].

Other contributing conditions include obesity, chronic alcohol use, HIV infection, peripheral vascular disease, renal insufficiency, malignancy, and corticosteroid use [ 8 ]. These risk factors set up a permissive environment for infection by altering microvascular circulation, compromising cellular immunity, and facilitating the growth of anaerobic and aerobic organisms [ 9 ].

The microbiology of FG is usually polymicrobial, involving synergistic interactions between gram-positive cocci, gram-negative bacilli, and anaerobes that together promote rapid tissue necrosis, thrombosis of subcutaneous vessels, and systemic toxicity [ 10 ].

Despite advances in surgical and critical care, mortality remains alarmingly high, variously reported between 20 and 40%, although some series report rates above 50% in cases of severity or late presentation [ 11 ]. Morbidity is similarly significant. Patients frequently suffer from septic shock, multiple organ dysfunction, and prolonged intensive care [ 12 ].

The surgical treatment of FG requires urgent, and frequently repeated, debridement, which invariably leads to extensive soft-tissue losses that necessitate prolonged hospitalization, sophisticated wound care, and often reconstructive surgery [ 13 ]. Large wounds are fraught with several challenges, including persistent infection risk, fluid loss, complex topology, and disturbed granulation [ 14 ].

Optimal postoperative wound management is thus a very crucial component of FG care [ 15 ]. Conventionally, CWC approaches have included saline gauze dressings, wet-to-dry dressings, sitz baths, or dressings soaked in antiseptics as the mainstay following surgical debridement [ 16 ].

These conventional techniques, while widely accessible, have obvious limitations in that they often fail to manage exudate effectively, require numerous frequent dressing changes, are painful, do not ensure consistent moisture balance, and provide little support for angiogenesis or tissue regeneration [ 17 ]. Consequently, healing times are long, with significant risks of secondary infections.

Over the past two decades, increasing interest in NPWT has promoted the rising use of NPWT in the management of FG [ 18 ]. NPWT applies controlled subatmospheric pressure to the wound bed by means of a sealed system that generates mechanical forces to promote wound closure. Its benefits include enhanced tissue perfusion, promotion of granulation tissue, reduction in wound edema, removal of infectious exudate, and potential reduction of bacterial bioburden [ 19 ].

Additionally, NPWT can decrease dressing-change frequency, reduce patient discomfort, and serve as a bridge to definitive reconstructive procedures such as skin grafting or flap coverage [ 20 ].

Given the acuity of Fournier’s gangrene, the complex nature of postoperative wounds, and clinical and economic burdens associated with prolonged lengths of stay and eventual reconstructive needs, determining the efficacy of NPWT compared with traditional methods of wound care is of critical importance.

The purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to analyze Negative Pressure Wound Therapy versus conventional wound care following surgical debridement in Fournier’s gangrene. This review aims to provide the most comprehensive evidence-based assessment to date on whether NPWT has significant benefits compared with conventional wound care in the postoperative management of Fournier’s gangrene.

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [ 21 ] and were prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251176451 [ 22 ].

A comprehensive electronic literature search was performed across PubMed, Scopus, and Embase to identify all relevant published studies. No restrictions were applied regarding geographic location or publication date to capture the broadest possible body of evidence. The search covered studies published from database inception up to October 2025.

The following search strategy was used: (“Fournier Gangrene“[Mesh] OR “Fournier’s gangrene“[tiab] OR “fournier gangrene“[tiab] OR “perineal necrotizing fasciitis“[tiab]) AND (“Negative-Pressure Wound Therapy“[Mesh] OR “negative pressure wound therapy“[tiab] OR “vacuum assisted closure“[tiab] OR “vacuum therapy“[tiab] OR “vacuum sealing drainage“[tiab] OR NPWT[tiab] OR VAC[tiab])

Reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to identify additional eligible articles that may have been missed during the electronic search. All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan software for duplicate removal and title/abstract screening [ 23 ].

Study selection followed a two-stage process. First, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant studies. Second, full texts of shortlisted articles were assessed for eligibility according to predefined inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Eligible studies met the following criteria:

Observational study design (cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional studies); Adult patients diagnosed with Fournier’s gangrene undergoing surgical debridement; Use of negative pressure wound therapy/vacuum-assisted closure as a postoperative wound management strategy; Direct comparison between NPWT and conventional wound care.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex distribution, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, alcohol use, smoking), and wound management strategies, were extracted from all included studies. Detailed baseline characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: insufficient outcome data, paediatric populations, non-surgical cases, necrotizing soft tissue infections outside the perineal/Fournier’s region without separate analysis, review articles, editorials, case reports, conference abstracts, or publications not written in English.

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized data collection form. Extracted data included study characteristics (author, year of publication, country, and sample size), primary outcomes (mortality and number of surgical debridements), and secondary outcomes (length of hospital stay, time to wound closure, and postoperative analgesic use).

Methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool, which evaluates seven domains including bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. The ROBINS-I tool was selected over traditional scales (e.g., Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) because ROBINS-I explicitly evaluates domain-specific confounding and selection bias inherent to observational surgical interventions.

For studies reporting medians and ranges or interquartile ranges, mean and standard deviation values were estimated using the method described by Hozo et al. [ 25 ]. Missing standard deviations for length of hospital stay in one study (Ozkan et al., 2016) were imputed using the precision-weighted mean SD from the remaining 17 studies reporting this outcome, consistent with Cochrane Handbook guidance [ 26 , 27 ]. Sensitivity analyses excluding imputed continuous data confirmed no material change in pooled effect estimates.

For outcomes with substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%), subgroup analyses were conducted across prespecified factors (geographic region, publication period, sample size, diabetes prevalence, and NPWT pressure setting). We also conducted a univariable random-effects meta-regression using publication year, total sample size, diabetes prevalence, and NPWT pressure setting as candidate moderators where feasible. However, per Cochrane Handbook guidelines, which recommend a minimum of 10 studies to ensure adequate statistical power and reliable estimation of moderator effects, this meta-regression was restricted exclusively to the length of hospital stay outcome (k = 18).

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 2025.05.1 + 513) with R (version 4.5.1; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2025) [ 28 ]. Meta-analyses were conducted using the meta and dmetar packages.

For dichotomous outcomes, pooled risk ratios were calculated using the inverse-variance method within a random-effects model, applying the DerSimonian-Laird estimator for between-study variance (τ²). The Mantel-Haenszel method was used to calculate Cochran’s Q statistic, with statistical heterogeneity quantified using the I² statistic. A continuity correction of 0.5 was applied in studies with zero-event cells. For continuous outcomes, pooled mean differences were calculated using the inverse-variance method under a random-effects model, again employing the DerSimonian-Laird estimator for τ². Confidence intervals for τ and τ² were calculated using the Jackson method. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Pooled estimates were derived using the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model. We acknowledge that DerSimonian-Laird can underestimate between-study variance when study numbers are small (k < 10) and heterogeneity is high; thus, random-effects prediction intervals were calculated.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test [ 24 ]; however, per Cochrane recommendations, Egger’s test is underpowered when k < 10, and funnel plot symmetry for outcomes with few studies was interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive. Where evidence of publication bias was detected, the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method was used to estimate the impact of potentially missing studies. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of pooled estimates and the influence of individual studies.

The systematic search across PubMed, Scopus, and Embase identified 353 records. After removal of duplicates and initial screening, 233 records were reviewed in full text for eligibility. Twenty-seven full-text articles were assessed, with nine excluded primarily due to lack of a comparator group or insufficient data. Eighteen studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

The included studies comprised adult patients (≥ 18 years) with Fournier’s gangrene or perineal necrotizing fasciitis undergoing surgical debridement. The intervention group received vacuum-assisted/negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT/VAC), compared against conventional wound dressings. Study designs were observational cohort and case-control studies with variable follow-up periods and heterogeneous outcome reporting. Across the included studies, patients were predominantly male, with a high prevalence of diabetes mellitus and other cardiometabolic comorbidities. Negative pressure wound therapy protocols varied with respect to pressure settings and dressing change intervals. Full baseline characteristics of individual studies are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Figures 2 and 3 summarize the individual quality assessment of each observational study included in the meta-analysis. Overall risk of bias was rated as serious in most included studies. Bias due to confounding was rated as serious in the majority of studies, reflecting the inherent limitations of observational design. Classification of interventions was generally rated as low risk, whereas participant selection and outcome measurement showed variable ratings across studies. Overall, the ROB was primarily driven by confounding and participant selection concerns. These findings were considered in interpreting the pooled estimates.

Eight studies involving 304 patients (125 receiving NPWT and 179 controls) reported all-cause mortality (Fig. 4). Meta-analysis using a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird estimator) demonstrated a pooled risk ratio (RR) of 0.64 (95% CI 0.33 to 1.27; p = 0.24), indicating a non-significant relative reduction in mortality associated with NPWT. No statistical heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 0.0%, p = 0.88). The calculated 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.28 to 1.46.

Subgroup analyses were not performed for mortality due to the absence of between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%) and the limited number of contributing studies (k = 8). Meta-regression was not undertaken, adhering to Cochrane Handbook guidelines requiring a minimum of 10 studies.

Visual inspection of the funnel plot demonstrated minor asymmetry (Fig. 5). Egger’s regression test yielded a p-value of 0.09. However, with only eight included studies, statistical power to detect genuine publication bias or small-study effects is low; thus, Egger’s test cannot conclusively rule out publication bias, and results should be interpreted as exploratory.

Nine studies comprising 387 patients reported the number of postoperative surgical debridements required. The pooled mean difference (MD) was − 0.84 (95% CI −2.44 to 0.77; p = 0.31), indicating a non-significant trend toward fewer debridements in the NPWT group (Fig. 6). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 97.4%, p < 0.0001), and the calculated 95% prediction interval was − 6.32 to 4.65. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed overall stability of the effect size, with pooled MDs ranging from − 0.61 (omitting He et al., 2023) to −1.11 (omitting Yucel et al., 2017) (Fig. 7). Omission of Aktaş et al. (2024) substantially reduced heterogeneity from 97.4% to 57.5%, identifying it as a primary source of statistical variability.

Subgroup analyses demonstrated a statistically significant difference based on publication period (Q = 3.96, df = 1, p = 0.047) (Fig. 8; Supplementary Table S2). Studies published between 2009 and 2020 showed virtually no difference in debridement frequency between groups (MD = 0.69; 95% CI −0.11 to 1.48; k = 2, I² = 43.0%), whereas studies published between 2021 and 2025 demonstrated a non-significant trend favoring NPWT (MD = −1.37; 95% CI −3.23 to 0.49; k = 7, I² = 97.4%). Geographic region (p = 0.600) and diabetes prevalence (p = 0.206) did not significantly account for between-study heterogeneity. Meta-regression was not performed due to insufficient study numbers (k = 9).

Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed mild asymmetry (Fig. 9). Egger’s regression test did not indicate statistically significant small-study effects (p = 0.29). Nevertheless, because fewer than 10 studies were included (k = 9), the test remains underpowered, and potential publication bias cannot be definitively excluded.

Eighteen studies including 722 patients evaluated length of hospital stay (Fig. 10). NPWT was associated with a non-significant reduction in hospital stay of approximately 1 day (MD = −1.06 days; 95% CI −4.33 to 2.20; p = 0.52). Substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed (I² = 83.6%, p < 0.0001). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the pooled estimate, with MDs ranging from − 1.88 days (excluding Yucel et al., 2017) to 0.57 days (excluding Kutsal et al., 2023), while heterogeneity remained moderate to high across all iterations (I² = 76.6–84.6%) (Fig. 11).

Fig. 10 Forest Plot Comparing Length of Hospital Stay between NPWT and Conventional Care

Subgroup analyses identified statistically significant moderator effects for publication period (p = 0.016) and diabetes prevalence (p = 0.021) (Fig. 12; Supplementary Table S2). Studies published in 2009–2020 showed a non-significant increase in length of stay with NPWT (MD = 3.62 days; 95% CI −0.95 to 8.20; k = 7), whereas studies from 2021 to 2025 demonstrated a non-significant reduction (MD = −3.77 days; 95% CI −7.67 to 0.12; k = 11). NPWT significantly reduced hospital stay in studies where diabetes prevalence was under 50% (MD = −6.36 days; 95% CI −13.51 to 0.79; k = 6), compared to studies with a diabetes prevalence of 50% or higher (MD = 4.76 days; 95% CI −1.39 to 10.91; k = 10). Furthermore, standard pressure settings (≤ 125 mmHg) yielded a statistically significant reduction in hospital stay (MD = −5.33 days; 95% CI −9.23 to −1.42; p = 0.007; k = 8), whereas higher or variable pressure protocols showed no significant effect (p = 0.326). Geographic region was not a significant subgroup moderator (p = 0.111).

Univariable random-effects meta-regression (k = 18) confirmed that total sample size (slope = −0.19 days per patient; 95% CI −0.33 to −0.06; p = 0.006) and diabetes prevalence (slope = + 0.40 days per percentage point increase; 95% CI 0.07 to 0.74; p = 0.019) were significant moderators of length of stay (Fig. 13; Supplementary Table S3). Publication year (p = 0.096) and NPWT pressure setting (p = 0.170) were not statistically significant in meta-regression models.

Visual inspection of the funnel plot demonstrated broad symmetry (Fig. 14). Egger’s regression test showed no statistically significant publication bias (p = 0.11). However, given high statistical heterogeneity (I² = 83.6%) and the imputation of an unavailable standard deviation in one study (Ozkan et al., 2016), funnel plot symmetry should be interpreted with caution.

Seven studies involving 347 patients reported time to wound closure. NPWT was associated with a non-significant mean reduction of approximately 10 days compared to conventional dressings (MD = −10.17 days; 95% CI −24.98 to 4.29; p = 0.17) (Fig. 15). Heterogeneity was high (I² = 97.7%, p < 0.0001). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed pooled MDs ranging from − 0.60 days (omitting Ozturk et al., 2009) to −3.43 days (omitting Aktaş et al., 2024), with heterogeneity remaining consistently elevated (I² > 96.0%) (Fig. 16).

Subgroup analyses by publication period (p = 0.165), geographic region (p = 0.257), and diabetes prevalence (p = 0.373) showed no statistically significant differences (Fig. 17; Supplementary Table S2). However, evaluation of NPWT pressure settings demonstrated that higher pressure systems (> 125 mmHg) achieved a statistically significant reduction in closure time (MD = −17.30 days; 95% CI −30.61 to −3.99; p = 0.011; k = 1), whereas standard pressure settings (≤ 125 mmHg) did not (MD = −11.11 days; 95% CI −32.70 to 10.47; p = 0.313; k = 4). Meta-regression was not performed due to the small number of studies (k = 7).

Funnel plot inspection revealed no marked visual asymmetry (Fig. 18), and Egger’s regression test was not statistically significant (p = 0.46). Nonetheless, because k = 7, statistical power to detect small-study effects is low, making publication bias assessment exploratory.

Four studies comprising 207 patients (78 NPWT, 129 controls) reported postoperative analgesic consumption. NPWT demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in analgesic requirement compared with conventional care (MD = −2.16; 95% CI −3.63 to −0.70; p = 0.004) (Fig. 19). Between-study heterogeneity was high (I² = 98.0%, p < 0.0001). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the significant overall effect, with MDs ranging from − 0.60 to −3.43 while heterogeneity remained high (I² = 96.9–98.6%) (Fig. 20).

Subgroup analysis by publication period showed no statistically significant subgroup difference (p = 0.201; 2009–2020: MD = −6.41; 95% CI −15.93 to 3.12; k = 2 vs. 2021–2025: MD = −0.09; 95% CI −1.82 to 1.64; k = 2) (Fig. 21; Supplementary Table S2). Geographic region, diabetes prevalence, and pressure settings could not be analyzed as subgroup moderators because all four contributing studies originated from Turkey and shared similar baseline methodology. Meta-regression was not performed (k = 4).

Visual inspection of the funnel plot showed general symmetry (Fig. 22), and Egger’s regression test did not indicate significant asymmetry (p = 0.90). However, testing for publication bias with only four studies is severely underpowered, and findings must be interpreted with caution.

Data for this systematic review and meta-analysis were collated from 18 observational studies comparing negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) with conventional wound care following initial surgical debridement in patients with Fournier’s gangrene. Pooled analyses demonstrated no statistically significant differences in mortality, frequency of surgical debridement, length of hospital stay, or overall time to wound closure between the two treatment strategies. However, NPWT was associated with a statistically significant reduction in postoperative analgesic requirements, suggesting a tangible benefit in patient comfort and pain management [ 29 ]. Methodological evaluation using the ROBINS-I tool revealed a moderate-to-serious risk of bias across included studies, predominantly driven by unadjusted domain-specific confounding and participant selection bias inherent to non-randomized surgical designs.

The absence of statistically significant differences in primary survival or gross healing metrics should not be interpreted as clinical equivalence between NPWT and conventional dressings [ 30 ]. Fournier’s gangrene represents a life-threatening, rapidly progressive necrotizing soft-tissue infection where disease prognosis is primarily dictated by acute systemic factors—including baseline comorbid state, degree of septic shock, timeliness and adequacy of primary surgical source control, and aggressive resuscitation rather than the choice of postoperative wound dressing alone [ 31 , 32 ]. Although non-significant, the observed direction of effect toward lower mortality and fewer debridements in NPWT-treated patients aligns with established physiological mechanisms, such as enhanced microvascular perfusion, accelerated granulation tissue formation, continuous evacuation of exudate, and bioburden reduction [ 33– 35 ]. In the anatomically challenging perineal region, continuous negative pressure maintains wound bed stability and manages high-volume infectious fluid, serving as an effective bridge to definitive reconstructive procedures (e.g., split-thickness skin grafting or local flap coverage). Nevertheless, localized wound stabilization during the subacute phase is insufficient to alter acute systemic mortality driven by early multi-organ failure.

Postoperative analgesic consumption was the sole secondary outcome demonstrating a statistically significant reduction with NPWT [ 29 ]. This reduction is clinically coherent: NPWT dressings typically remain in situ for 48 to 72 h, substantially reducing the frequency of painful dressing changes compared to conventional wet-to-dry dressings, which require manipulation multiple times daily [ 33 ]. Minimizing mechanical trauma to exposed, highly sensitive perineal nerve endings and fragile granulating tissue directly mitigates procedural and background pain.

Subgroup analyses and meta-regression yielded important clinical nuances regarding healthcare resource utilization. While overall length of hospital stay did not differ significantly between groups, meta-regression identified diabetes mellitus prevalence and total sample size as significant moderating factors. NPWT demonstrated a marked reduction in hospital stay in cohorts with lower diabetes prevalence (< 50%) and when standardized pressure settings (≤ 125 mmHg) were applied. Conversely, high diabetes prevalence (≥ 50%) attenuated this effect, underscoring how systemic microvascular disease and impaired wound healing in severe diabetic states can override the localized benefits of negative pressure. Furthermore, higher pressure settings (> 125 mmHg) significantly accelerated time to wound closure in specific subgroups, while studies published in the modern era (2021–2025) demonstrated greater trends toward reduced debridement requirements, likely reflecting modern standardization of NPWT protocols and earlier application relative to older historical cohorts (2009–2020) [ 36 ].

Previous literature on NPWT in Fournier’s gangrene consisted predominantly of descriptive case series and single-center cohorts. Uncontrolled narrative reviews by Czymek et al. (2009) and Sørensen et al. (2020) suggested marked reductions in hospital stay and healing times; however, these conclusions lacked formal comparative controls [ 37 , 38 ]. Comparative evaluations in non-Fournier populations such as diabetic foot ulcers (Seidel et al., 2022) and sternal wound infections (Ashraf et al., 2021) similarly demonstrated no survival benefit with NPWT but reported reduced debridement frequency and faster granulation [ 39 , 40 ]. By focusing exclusively on perineal necrotizing fasciitis, this analysis advances the current literature by demonstrating that while survival and overall hospitalization duration remain unchanged, analgesic reduction represents the most consistent and demonstrably quantifiable advantage of NPWT in this patient population [ 41 ].

Current major clinical practice guidelines, including those from the European Association of Urology (EAU), the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES), and the Surviving Sepsis Campaign, emphasize emergency radical debridement as the non-negotiable cornerstone of Fournier’s gangrene management [ 47– 50 ]. Adjuvant wound care techniques are left to clinician discretion [ 46 ]. The findings of this meta-analysis directly align with these guideline positions: NPWT represents a safe, useful adjunctive modality for postoperative wound management but should not be regarded as a mandatory standard of care or a substitute for complete surgical debridement [ 51 , 52 ].

This review represents the most comprehensive quantitative synthesis to date on NPWT in Fournier’s gangrene, incorporating 18 comparative studies, pre-registered methodology, rigorous risk of bias assessment via ROBINS-I, random-effects prediction intervals, and detailed subgroup and meta-regression analyses.

Several limitations must be acknowledged, such as all included studies were observational, introducing inherent susceptibility to selection bias and confounding by indication (e.g., clinicians selecting NPWT for larger, complex, or more heavily exuding wounds). Also, the lack of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) limits definitive causal inferences.

Furthermore, crucial clinical variables such as initial wound surface area, depth of tissue loss, exact timing of NPWT initiation following primary debridement, and specific pressure settings were inconsistently reported, contributing to substantial unexplained statistical heterogeneity (I² > 90% across several continuous outcomes). Statistical power for publication bias testing was limited for outcomes with few contributing studies (k < 10), rendering Egger’s test exploratory rather than definitive.

Finally, long-term outcomes, including late wound recurrence, reconstructive failure rates, cosmetic/functional sequelae, quality of life, and formal cost-effectiveness, were rarely reported across the primary literature.

From a practical standpoint, NPWT is a safe and effective supportive adjunct in the postoperative management of Fournier’s gangrene. Its primary clinical utility lies in improving patient comfort, reducing procedural pain associated with frequent dressing changes, and managing complex, highly exuding perineal wounds prior to definitive reconstruction.

Given the absence of clear survival or hospitalization advantages, NPWT should not be universally applied as a mandatory replacement for conventional dressings. Instead, its application should be tailored to individual clinical scenarios, specifically reserved for large, irregular, exudative wounds or patients who experience severe distress during routine dressing changes.

Economic considerations are paramount, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where Fournier’s gangrene carries high incidence and mortality, yet access to specialized NPWT consoles and consumables is severely constrained by cost. Future research must prioritize cost-effectiveness analyses comparing commercial NPWT, simplified/modified negative pressure setups, and standard dressing regimens.

High-quality, prospective multicenter comparative studies or pragmatic randomized trials utilizing standardized reporting of wound dimensions, pressure settings, validated pain scales, and functional quality-of-life metrics are necessary to establish definitive clinical practice pathways.

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicates that NPWT following surgical debridement for Fournier’s gangrene significantly reduces postoperative analgesic requirements compared to conventional wound care, offering meaningful benefits in patient comfort and pain control. However, based on low-to-moderate certainty observational evidence, NPWT does not significantly reduce all-cause mortality, surgical debridement frequency, length of hospital stay, or time to wound closure. Consequently, NPWT should be utilized as a supportive adjunct rather than a replacement for standard surgical and supportive management. Selection of NPWT should be individualized based on wound complexity, exudate volume, patient comfort, and resource availability until high-quality prospective comparative studies provide further guidance.
