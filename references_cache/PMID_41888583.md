# PACS1 syndrome mutation disrupts dynein-mediated cargo transport via HDAC6 and BICD2.
**Authors:** Yang Y, Thomas L, Chen K, Villar-Pazos S, Haffey WD, D'Agostino A, Fanelli K, Choi YJ, Rathi V, Matlapudi MS, Greis KD, Thomas G
**Journal:** Commun Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s42003-026-09924-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-026-09924-0)

## Content

PACS1 syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by a recurrent heterozygous missense mutation in PACS1 (p.R203W). We previously showed that PACS1R203W aberrantly potentiates HDAC6 activity, leading to Golgi fragmentation and neuronal deficits through an unresolved mechanism (Villar-Pazos and Thomas et al., Nature Commun. 14:6547-6564 (2023)). Here, we identify cytoplasmic dynein-1 heavy chain (DHC1) as a PACS1 interactor essential for maintaining furin localization at the trans-Golgi network. PACS1R203W induces a dynein loss-of-function phenotype that disrupts furin trafficking and Golgi organization. We demonstrate that PACS1R203W-HDAC6 recruits the adaptor BICD2, forming a complex that disperses the Golgi and impairs dynein function. Cargo motility assays revealed that PACS1R203W reduces dynein initiation frequency and velocity, defects rescued by HDAC6 inhibition or by expressing the dynein regulator Lis1. These findings uncover a pathogenic mechanism in which PACS1R203W-HDAC6-BICD2 disrupts dynein-driven transport, placing PACS1 syndrome within the broad spectrum of microtubule trafficking disorders.

Precise spatial organization and long-distance transport of organelles and proteins are crucial for cell function and tissue integrity. These processes are carried out along microtubules by kinesin and dynein motors, which drive anterograde and retrograde transport, respectively. The balance of these motors determines cargo flow and is further regulated by posttranslational modifications of the tubulin lattice, such as α-tubulin Lys40 acetylation, which modulate motor attachment and velocity1,2. Neurons, with their extreme polarity, impose exceptional trafficking demands and are particularly vulnerable to disruptions in this system. Mutations that perturb this finely tuned machinery compromise organelle positioning and neuronal structure, giving rise to a growing class of neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative conditions known as microtubule trafficking disorders (MTDs)2–5.

PACS1 syndrome is one such neurodevelopmental disorder, caused by a recurrent de novo missense mutation, c.607 C > T (p.Arg203Trp; PACS1R203W). Affected individuals present with intellectual and developmental delays, seizures, autism, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, and craniofacial dysmorphisms6. PACS1 and its paralog PACS2 encode multifunctional adaptor proteins that act in both the nucleus—interacting with class I and III histone deacetylases to maintain chromatin integrity, assist DNA repair, regulate transcription, and mediate RNA export7–10—and the cytoplasm, where they regulate ER calcium signaling, ER–mitochondria communication, and retrograde trafficking among endosomes, the trans-Golgi network (TGN), and the primary cilium11–22.

Recent studies implicate PACS1 as a regulator of α-tubulin acetylation and retrograde transport16. PACS1 interacts directly with HDAC6, the cytoplasmic class II histone deacetylase responsible for most α-tubulin and cortactin deacetylation and is found in brain-derived microtubule fractions enriched for dynein and the adaptor BICD216,23. PACS1R203W hyperactivates HDAC6, causing hypoacetylation of α-tubulin and cortactin, Golgi fragmentation, and dispersal into overgrown dendrites—a phenotype commonly observed with mutations or loss-of-function (LOF) in microtubule trafficking genes5,24–27. Elevated HDAC6 activity further reduces spine density and impairs synaptic transmission16. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting PACS1 or HDAC6 restore neuronal structure and function in PACS1 syndrome animal models, highlighting PACS1’s central role in coordinating HDAC6 activity, microtubule acetylation, and neuronal integrity16. Yet it remains unclear whether the PACS1R203W–HDAC6 axis alone explains the transport defects observed in PACS1 syndrome.

A key gap in understanding PACS1 syndrome lies in the regulation of dynein, the sole motor for long-range retrograde transport. While over 40 kinesins drive anterograde transport, all retrograde transport relies on Dynein-1, a 1.4-MDa motor built from two 530-kDa DHC1 subunits and associated cofactors. Dynein is autoinhibited in its basal state and requires activation by cofactors such as Lis1, which stabilizes dynein’s interaction with dynactin, enhancing microtubule binding and motility28–32. Cargo-specific coiled-coil adaptors link dynein–dynactin to cargo to initiate long-range transport33–35, and an expanding set of atypical adaptors lacking extended coiled-coil domains reveals additional regulatory layers36–38.

BICD2 illustrates how coiled-coil scaffolds control dynein activity in a cell-cycle-dependent manner. In G1, BICD2 organizes dynein–dynactin–BICD2 (DDB) complexes to drive minus-end transport of ER–Golgi carriers, peroxisomes, and mitochondria. In G2, CDK1-mediated phosphorylation redirects DDB to the nuclear envelope to support interkinetic nuclear migration in radial glial progenitors, a critical process in corticogenesis39–42. Mutations in DYNC1H1 or BICD2 cause the motor neuron disorder, SMA-LED, highlighting neuronal vulnerability to impaired dynein regulation26,43.

Here, we show that PACS1 functions as a dynein effector, connecting cargo selection to microtubule acetylation–dependent retrograde transport. The R203W substitution increases the PACS1–DHC1 interaction while aberrantly engaging HDAC6 and BICD2, reducing dynein motility during G1. This leads to Golgi fragmentation, a hallmark of faulty retrograde trafficking. Restoring dynein activity by HDAC6 inhibition or by increasing Lis1 rescues transport activity and Golgi positioning in patient cells. Together, these findings establish PACS1 as a regulator of dynein-dependent transport and reveal that the PACS1R203W–HDAC6–BICD2 axis disrupts retrograde trafficking, providing a mechanistic explanation for PACS1 syndrome and linking it to the broader spectrum of MTDs.

Given dynein’s essential role in retrograde trafficking, our subsequent analyses focused on the functional link between DHC1 and PACS1, and how the disease-causing R203W substitution impacts this relationship. We next examined whether PACS1R203W-HDAC6-induced Golgi fragmentation correlates with dynein dysfunction by using siRNA knockdowns in dermal fibroblasts from a patient with PACS1 syndrome (159) and the healthy parental control (160). Endogenous PACS1 was confirmed to interact with DHC1 in both cell lines, consistent with our observations in mouse brain (Fig. S5). Antibodies used to detect endogenous PACS1 do not distinguish between WT PACS1 and PACS1R203W, which are heterozygously expressed in patient cells. Consequently, DHC1 coIP reflects total PACS1 and does not permit quantitative comparison of interaction with endogenous WT versus mutant PACS1, unlike side-by-side co-expression assays using tagged proteins (Fig. 1b).

As reported previously16, confocal analysis revealed that in control cells, the Golgi (Giantin) and the associated TGN (furin) concentrated in the paranuclear region. In contrast, patient cells expressing PACS1R203W showed that the Golgi and TGN were largely fragmented into dispersed mini-stacks, with furin also moving to endosomal compartments (Fig. 1d and S6). Control (160) cells treated with siRNAs targeting either PACS1 or DHC1 showed efficient knockdown by Western blot, without affecting the level of the PACS1 paralog PACS2 or the μ1A subunit of the clathrin adaptor AP-1, which also regulates TGN localization of furin19,44 (Fig. 1d, top left). Confocal analysis showed that PACS1 knockdown mirrored Pacs1 knockout (Pacs1KO), redistributing furin to endosomes without altering Golgi positioning12,16. In contrast, DHC1 knockdown induced fragmentation of the entire Golgi/TGN into dispersed ministacks, phenocopying PACS1R203W cells. These findings suggest that the interaction between PACS1R203W and DHC1 produces a dynein LOF phenotype.

In contrast, no interaction was observed between DHC1 and either the C-terminal region (CTR) or the N-terminal atrophin-1-related region (ARR) of PACS1. Compared with full-length PACS1, the R203W substitution reduced DHC1 interaction with the truncated PACS1 FBR (Fig. 2b, compare with Fig. 1b). To understand this difference, we examined PACS1 dimerization. Full-length PACS1 forms homodimers, and R203W marginally increased dimerization by ~1.2-fold (Fig. S7). In contrast, the truncated PACS1 (aa 1–266), which lacks the autoregulatory MR, showed a much stronger effect: R203W boosted homodimer formation by nearly 10-fold (Fig. 2c). These findings support the previously reported model in which the MR regulates access to the FBR18,46. In full-length PACS1, R203W weakens this regulation, exposing the FBR and increasing client binding (e.g., HDAC6 and DHC1, see Fig. 1 and ref. 16). However, in the isolated FBR—where the inhibitory MR is absent—R203W causes excessive dimerization, likely blocking FBR cargo-binding and contributing to the reduced interactions with DHC1 or HDAC6.

To identify the regions within the PACS1 FBR necessary and sufficient for interacting with DHC1, we first tested a series of FBR C-terminal truncation mutants. However, this approach was unsuccessful, likely because the truncations destabilized the FBR fold. Interestingly, the PACS2 FBR (aa 38-187) shares ~80% sequence identity with the PACS1 FBR (aa 117-266) and is predicted to adopt a similar 3D structure; it interacts with DHC1 only weakly (Fig. 2d and refs. 19,47). This finding prompted us to use a chimera-based strategy to identify the sequence features that enable the PACS1-DHC1 interaction.

AlphaFold2 predicts that the FBRs of PACS1 and PACS2 contain multiple β-strands connected by loops, forming two apposed faces within the folded domain. The “traffic” face, composed of strands 1, 3, and 7, engages components of the cellular trafficking machinery, whereas the “cargo” face, formed by strands 2, 4, 5, and 6, binds to furin, HDAC6, and other cargo molecules (Fig. 2e, S8, and see Discussion). Swapping β-strands 1-3 in the PACS2 FBR with the corresponding PACS1 sequence (aa 117-186) markedly increased the DHC1 interaction, comparable to the PACS1 FBR (Fig. 2f). Incremental incorporation of shorter PACS1 segments revealed that β-strand 1 (aa 117-130) was sufficient to maximize the DHC1 interaction.

To evaluate the functional importance of the PACS1-DHC1 interaction, we engineered a PACS1 mutant, PACS1Dynmut, with a 5-Ala substitution targeting β-strand 1. This mutant decreased the PACS1-DHC1 interaction while leaving its interaction with furin unaffected, as confirmed by co-IP (Fig. 3a, b). U20S cells expressing PACS1, PACS1Dynmut, or PACS1R203W were analyzed using confocal microscopy (Fig. 3c and S9). PACS1Dynmut caused furin to redistribute from the TGN to an endosome population without disturbing the Golgi ribbon, consistent with Pacs1 knockout and PACS1 knockdown cells (compare Fig. 3c with 1d and see ref. 16). In contrast, PACS1R203W induced significant Golgi/TGN fragmentation, similar to that observed in patient (159) cells and in DHC1-depleted control (160) cells. To determine whether PACS1 interacts only with DHC1 or also with other components of the dynein motor complex, we examined the FLAG-PACS1 co-IPs shown in Fig. 1a by Western blot (Fig. S10). PACS1 interacted with several dynein subunits, including the dynein intermediate chain (DIC), the dynactin subunit p150Glued, and various adaptors and regulatory proteins, notably BICD2. Sucrose density gradients of postnuclear supernatants from patient (159) and control (160) fibroblasts showed PACS1 and PACS1R203W migrated into lighter density fractions separate from the dynein/dynactin holoenzyme, indicating that PACS1 is not a stoichiometric component of dynein or dynactin and that PACS1R203W does not disrupt the dynein/dynactin complex integrity (Fig. S11).

Given PACS1’s interaction with multiple adaptors, we then examined whether PACS1R203W affects dynein‒adaptor interactions, focusing on BICD2. Co-IP assays confirmed interactions between endogenous PACS1 and BICD2 in patient cells, and the R203W substitution does not affect these interactions (Fig. 4a, b). The disinhibited BICD2 CC1 + CC2 domains strongly interact with DHC1, kinesin, and PACS1, providing a platform to explore how PACS1R203W and HDAC6 influence BICD2–motor interactions. Co-IP assays revealed that PACS1R203W and HDAC6 displaced BICD2 CC1 + CC2 from DHC1 without affecting its interaction with kinesin (Fig. 4e). Conversely, PACS1 or HDAC6 alone did not alter BICD2 CC1 + CC2 interactions with dynein or kinesin. Deleting CC3 also did not change BICD2’s interaction with PACS1 or PACS1R203W (Fig. S12). These findings suggest that PACS1R203W and HDAC6 cooperate to inhibit dynein-dependent trafficking, in part by displacing dynein from kinesin-containing complexes.

To directly evaluate how PACS1R203W affects dynein activity, we used an inducible dynein-dependent peroxisome processivity assay28,49. U2OS cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding eYFP–PEX3–FKBP, FRB–BICD2 CC1 + CC2, and either PACS1 (control) or PACS1R203W (see Fig. 4f). Addition of rapalog triggered FKBP–FRB dimerization, initiating processive DDB-dependent peroxisome transport. Kymograph analysis revealed that PACS1R203W reduced peroxisome velocity by ~15% compared to the PACS1 control, aligning with its ability to reduce Ac-Lys40–α-tubulin (Fig. 4g and S116). PACS1R203W also reduced the percentage of motile peroxisomes, consistent with its capacity, along with HDAC6, to displace dynein from kinesin-containing motor complexes (see Fig. 4e). HDAC6 inhibition with tubacin restored both peroxisome velocity and the number of motile vesicles to control (PACS1) levels. These findings suggest that PACS1R203W–HDAC6 disrupts dynein initiation frequency and transport velocity, consistent with their role in dispersing the Golgi in patient (159) cells16.

Notably, Golgi repositioning varied among BICD2-depleted patient (159) cells: some reestablished a paranuclear Golgi, whereas others accumulated Golgi around distally positioned MTOCs. One explanation for this heterogeneity is the cell cycle-dependent function of BICD2, which mediates dynein-driven trafficking during G1 and centrosome-nuclear envelope tethering during G242. To test this, we repeated BICD2 siRNA knockdown in patient (159) cells arrested in G1 with the Cdk4/6 inhibitor ribociclib (LEE001) or in G2/M with the CDK1 inhibitor Ro-330651,52. Confocal analysis showed that BICD2 knockdown in G1-arrested patient (159) cells restored Golgi positioning, phenocopying PACS1 or HDAC6 knockdown16. Conversely, BICD2 knockdown in G2-arrested cells failed to re-establish the paranuclear Golgi localization and instead caused distal Golgi clustering, consistent with its role in centrosome-nuclear envelope attachment during G242. These findings support BICD2’s phase-specific roles in dynein-dependent transport and suggest that PACS1R203W and HDAC6 aberrantly recruit BICD2 during G1, resulting in dynein dysfunction and Golgi dispersal.

We next tested whether PACS1R203W broadly disrupts dynein-dependent organelle trafficking by analyzing lysosome positioning. In control (160) cells, lysosomes clustered in the perinuclear region, whereas in patient (159) cells they dispersed throughout the cytoplasm (Fig. 6b). Treating patient (159) cells with the HDAC6 inhibitor tubacin restored perinuclear lysosome clustering, consistent with our Golgi findings (Fig. 6c and ref. 16). Lis1 expression similarly rescued lysosomal organization, indicating that the PACS1R203W–HDAC6–BICD2 complex interferes with dynein-mediated transport.

Because Lis1 enhances dynein activation and motility28,29,54, we tested whether it could restore motility in the presence of PACS1R203W using the inducible peroxisome processivity assay (Fig. 6d). Kymograph analysis showed that PACS1R203W reduced both the frequency and velocity of motile peroxisomes relative to PACS1-expressing cells (see also Fig. 4g). As reported, Lis1 increased the frequency but not the velocity of retrograde transport events in control (160) cells30,55. In PACS1R203W-expressing cells, however, exogenous Lis1 expression restored both parameters to levels seen in cells co-expressing WT PACS1 and Lis1. These findings suggest that the PACS1R203W–HDAC6–BICD2 axis impairs dynein-dynactin activation and microtubule-based transport, causing widespread organelle mispositioning—defects reversed by Lis1 expression.

We identify PACS1 as a previously unrecognized DHC1-interacting protein and show that the R203W disease substitution causes a dynein LOF phenotype (Fig. 1). In patient cells and mouse models, PACS1R203W causes Golgi fragmentation and dispersal (ref. 16 and Fig. S1). Expressing PACS1R203W or depleting DHC1 similarly disrupts Golgi/TGN positioning, demonstrating that R203W impairs dynein-dependent transport (Figs. 1 and 3). DHC1 interacts with the PACS1 FBR and disrupting this interface (PACS1Dynmut) mislocalizes the PACS1 client furin from the TGN, phenocopying PACS1 LOF (Figs. 2 and 316;). PACS1R203W and HDAC6 act through BICD2 to drive Golgi dispersal (Figs. 4 and 5). Rapalog-inducible motility assays support this mechanism: the reduced dynein initiation frequency and velocity in PACS1R203W cells are restored by HDAC6 inhibition or Lis1 expression (Figs. 4 and 6). Together with prior studies11,16,23, these findings establish PACS1 as a unique regulator of microtubule trafficking that links the tubulin code to dynein motor function, ensuring that cargoes, such as furin, are correctly targeted to the TGN. We propose that the PACS1R203W–HDAC6–BICD2 axis disrupts this machinery, causing the trafficking defects characteristic of PACS1 syndrome (Fig. 7a). Systematic mutational analyses identify β-strand 1 of the PACS1 FBR (Val117–Met130) as essential for the PACS1–DHC1 interaction (Figs. 2 and 3). β-strand 1 is predicted to form hydrogen bonds with strands 3 and 7, creating the domain’s “traffic” face, where each strand interacts with distinct trafficking components (Fig. 7b). β-strand 3 is necessary and sufficient for PACS1 binding to AP-1 and AP-3, while β-strand 7 contains KIY251, which mediates interaction with GGA344,45. A Ser252 → Phe substitution adjacent to KIY251 weakens GGA3 binding and causes a neurodevelopmental disorder resembling PACS1 syndrome56. Extensive hydrogen bonding among β-strands 1, 3, and 7 suggests that mutations within the FBR traffic face could indirectly disrupt PACS1–dynein association and contribute to disease. The “traffic” face lies opposite the “cargo” face formed by β-strands 2 and 4-6, which bind client proteins, including furin and other acidic cluster–containing cargo, as well as class I/II HDACs and pathogen proteins like HIV-1 Nef9,11,45,57. Although PACS2 weakly interacts with DHC1 and cannot replace PACS1 in cargo trafficking (Figs. 1 and 2, and ref. 58), it interacts with other dynein components to support endosome-to-TGN transport of HIV-1 Nef to downregulate MHC-I11,16,58. Since PACS1–DHC1 coupling operates within a broader adaptor network, we then explored how PACS1R203W and HDAC6 influence BICD2.

PACS1R203W and HDAC6 require BICD2 to disrupt Golgi positioning in patient (159) cells; silencing any of the three restores normal Golgi organization (Fig. 5 and ref. 16). BICD2’s role changes with the cell cycle. In G1, the DDB complex drives minus-end cargo transport33,59, whereas in G2, CDK1 redirects it for neuronal migration40,42,60,61. In ribociclib-treated (G1-arrested) patient cells, BICD2 knockdown prevents PACS1R203W-induced Golgi dispersal. Conversely, in Ro-3306–treated (G2-arrested) cells, Golgi positioning remains abnormal despite BICD2 loss (Fig. 5). This aligns with our in vivo results that PACS1R203W disperses the Golgi specifically in pyramidal neurons, which arrest in G116,62. Because BICD2 deletion causes severe neurodevelopmental defects, we could not evaluate its role in PACS1R203W-induced Golgi disruption in vivo63.

Our findings indicate that PACS1R203W and HDAC6 recruit BICD2 to inhibit retrograde transport, analogous to the dynein-inhibitory function of BICD2-N, a constitutively active BICD2 truncation lacking the CC3 autoregulatory domain39. While BICD2-N forms a stable dynein–dynactin complex in vitro34, it generates an inactive motor in cells that cannot bind microtubules or cargo, preventing access by other adaptors, such as Golgin160, and resulting in Golgi dispersal25,53,64,65. Lis1 counteracts these effects, consistent with its ability to rescue PACS1R203W-induced organelle mispositioning29,32,53. Collectively, PACS1R203W–HDAC6 appears to stabilize a semi-active, BICD2-N–like state that disrupts dynein–dynactin–dependent transport essential for Golgi organization25,66. The observation that SMA-LED2–linked BICD2 missense variants produce similar Golgi defects suggests that these variants may act through PACS1 or HDAC616,26. Restoration of dynein–dynactin velocity by HDAC6 inhibition or Lis1 expression in PACS1R203W cells further implicates HDAC6 in deacetylating factors critical for retrograde transport, potentially including motor-proximal α-tubulin (as supported by rescue with acetyl-mimic α-tubulin K40Q) or motor-associated regulators, in line with HDAC6’s established control of Miro-dependent mitochondrial transport16,67. These findings are fully consistent with established PACS1 localization and function.

Despite extensive evidence obtained across multiple cell types and genetic models, Mekzine et al. did not detect Golgi fragmentation or reduced α-tubulin acetylation in their experimental system68. Although the basis for this discrepancy is unclear, it likely reflects technical or contextual differences, particularly given PACS1’s role in genome stability and the dynamic, condition-sensitive regulation of tubulin acetylation (ref. 9 and see Methods). Notably, these limited negative findings contrast with the broad, convergent, and orthogonal body of positive evidence demonstrating PACS1R203W-dependent disruption of retrograde transport. This conclusion is supported by independent biochemical assays, genetic gain- and loss-of-function approaches, patient-derived cells, and in vivo mouse models, which together provide a robust and internally consistent mechanistic framework for our findings16,69.

Across metazoans, PACS1 localizes to microtubules, the Golgi, and Rab5+, Rab7+, and SNX18+ endosomes, where it colocalizes with AP-115,16,22,23,70,71. Although PACS1 and AP-1 share acidic cluster cargos and directly interact, they perform distinct roles in endosomal–TGN transport (refs. 11,44,72,73 and Fig. 1). Robinson and colleagues nevertheless concluded that AP-1—not PACS1—directs trafficking of furin and other acidic-cluster cargos, based solely on negative data74, a fragile conclusion vulnerable to technical variability. Unsurprisingly, Robinson’s model collapses against the broader evidence reported by multiple labs: retrograde trafficking of acidic-cluster cargos, including furin and HIV-1 Nef, is disrupted in Pacs1KO cells; in cells or tissues depleted of PACS1, AP-1μ1 A, or DHC1; and when PACS1–AP-1 or PACS1–DHC1 interactions are blocked (refs. 11–16,18,20,22,44,73; Figs. 1 and 3). PACS1 is thus a central organizer of retrograde transport, coordinating HDAC6, dynein, cargo, and AP-1, and R203W dominantly derails this machinery—exposing flaws in Robinson’s oversimplified model.

Because dynein interacts with PACS1 but apparently not with AP-172,75, the overall data support a cohesive model in which endosomal AP-1 marks cargoes for handoff to PACS1, which then orchestrates dynein and HDAC6 for long-range retrograde transport. The recent discovery of additional dynein adaptors, including HEATR5B and kazrin, that can also bind AP-1, suggests an extra regulatory layer that may help explain how the single dynein motor achieves exquisite cargo specificity36,38,44. Therefore, defining the AP-1 → PACS1 handoff is essential for understanding retrograde transport and developing therapies to correct R203W-driven defects in PACS1 syndrome.

Treating PACS1 syndrome mice with ASOs that reduce PACS1 or HDAC6 levels in the brain reverses neural deficits caused by PACS1R203W, including overgrown dendrites with dispersed Golgi, decreased spine density, and reduced synaptic transmission to WT levels16. These findings, along with the viability of Pacs1KO mice, have gratifyingly led to the first use of ASO therapy in patients with PACS1 syndrome76. However, neither BICD2 ASO knockdown nor Lis1 overexpression is a feasible treatment; BICD2KO mice develop hydrocephalus and die early, and Lis1 overexpression causes structural brain abnormalities and widespread developmental issues63,77. While ASO therapy shows promise, it is not a cure, and advances in gene editing offer hope for a more definitive therapy for PACS1 syndrome and other diseases caused by dominant mutations78. Despite this therapeutic progress, key mechanistic questions remain unanswered.

The limited number of PACS1-interacting proteins identified here likely reflects the autoinhibited conformation of full-length PACS1, in which intramolecular MR–FBR interactions restrict partner access (Fig. 1a18,46). Identification of DNA-PKcs further supports a role for PACS1 in genome stability and raises the possibility that PACS1 contributes to DNA damage–induced Golgi remodeling9,79. Future studies should define how PACS1 coordinates cargo selection with HDAC6, dynein–dynactin, and Lis1 to couple α-tubulin acetylation to retrograde transport and how AP-1 interfaces with PACS1 or related regulators to support endosomal trafficking. Testing these mechanisms in cell-free systems and neurons will also be important for achieving greater mechanistic resolution and for establishing their physiological relevance to the growing number of PACS1-associated neurologic and developmental disorders6,69,80–82.

In summary, our findings establish PACS1 as a key regulator of retrograde trafficking, facilitating cargo selection, engaging dynein motors, and modulating tubulin acetylation—likely in response to cargo and signaling cues—to fine-tune the velocity and selectivity of microtubule-based transport. We further propose that the PACS1R203W–HDAC6–BICD2 complex destabilizes this trafficking machinery, driving the cellular pathology of PACS1 syndrome and aligning the disorder with features of MTDs.

Immunoprecipitated protein samples were resolved using SDS-PAGE. The gels were fixed and stained with a Pierce Silver Stain for Mass Spectrometry Kit (Thermo Fisher 24600). This software employs the Paragon algorithm (version 4.5.0.0, revision 1654), which searches against a Human UniProt Fasta database (captured on March 15, 2019), supplemented with common contaminating proteins (e.g., human keratins, porcine trypsin, and bovine albumin). The ProteinPilot search parameters included typical biological modifications (e.g., Met oxidation, N-terminal Gln conversion to pyroGlu, and acetylation) as variable modifications, with carboxyamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification to account for the reduction and alkylation steps prior to trypsin digestion. Protein identifications are reported for proteins that matched at least two peptides at >99% confidence (<1% FDR) compared to an inverse decoy database.
