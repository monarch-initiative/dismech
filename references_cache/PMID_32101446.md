# Endothelial Responses in Sepsis.
**Authors:** Joffre J, Hellman J, Ince C, Ait-Oufella H
**Journal:** Am J Respir Crit Care Med (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1164/rccm.201910-1911TR](https://doi.org/10.1164/rccm.201910-1911TR)

## Content

Endothelial cells (ECs) are vascular, nonconventional immune cells that play a major role in the systemic response after bacterial infection to limit its dissemination. Triggered by exposure to pathogens, microbial toxins, or endogenous danger signals, EC responses are polymorphous, heterogeneous, and multifaceted. During sepsis, ECs shift toward a proapoptotic, proinflammatory, proadhesive, and procoagulant phenotype. In addition, glycocalyx damage and vascular tone dysfunction impair microcirculatory blood flow, leading to organ injury and, potentially, life-threatening organ failure. This review aims to cover the current understanding of the EC adaptive or maladaptive response to acute inflammation or bacterial infection based on compelling recent basic research and therapeutic clinical trials targeting microvascular and endothelial alterations during septic shock.

Abstract
Endothelial cells (ECs) are vascular, nonconventional immune
cells that play a major role in the systemic response after
bacterial infection to limit its dissemination. Triggered by exposure
to pathogens, microbial toxins, or endogenous danger signals,
EC responses are polymorphous, heterogeneous, and multifaceted.
During sepsis, ECs shift toward a proapoptotic, proin ﬂammatory,
proadhesive, and procoagulant phenotype. In addition, glycocalyx
damage and vascular tone dysfunction impair microcirculatory
blood ﬂow, leading to organ injury and, potentially, life-
threatening organ failure. This review aims to cover the
current understanding of the EC adaptive or maladaptive
response to acute in ﬂammation or bacterial infection based
on compelling recent basic research and therapeutic clinical
trials targeting microvascular and endothelial alterations
during septic shock.
Keywords: endothelium; sepsis; in ﬂammation; cytokines;
glycocalyx
Sepsis, de ﬁned as “life-threatening organ
dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host
response to infection, ” is a common
condition that is responsible for high
morbidity and mortality worldwide (1).
Over the last 20 years, recognition of the
endothelium as a full- ﬂedged organ in
sepsis has resulted in a large number of
experimental and clinical studies. During
a bacterial, fungal, or viral infection,
exogenous pathogen-associated molecular
patterns (PAMPs) and endogenous
damage-associated molecular patterns
(DAMPs) trigger activation of the
endothelium and may impair its structure
and/or function (2). Engagement of
pathogen recognition receptors, such as
TLRs (Toll-like receptors), drive endothelial
cells (ECs) to reprogram toward a
proinﬂammatory phenotype. ECs release
cytokines, chemokines, and procoagulant
factors and express proadhesive molecules.
Damage to the glycocalyx and EC apoptosis
lead to an increase in permeability
to proteins and ﬂuids, causing interstitial
leakage (3). Sepsis-related endothelial
changes should be considered appropriate
to limit bacterial spread, as well as
to orchestrate leukocyte recruitment
and bacterial elimination. However, severe
and/or persistent endothelial phenotypic
changes may contribute to impaired
microcirculatory blood ﬂow, tissue
hypoperfusion, and life-threatening organ
failure. Thus, it is very challenging to
discriminate between appropriate
activation and endothelial dysfunction,
especially considering that EC responses
may differ among organs (4, 5). Although
some experimental studies have been
encouraging, recent negative phase 3
clinical trials underscore the need to better
understand the pathophysiological
mechanisms of septic shock – and sepsis-
induced organ failure (6, 7). Despite their
inherent limitations, experimental animal
sepsis models and in vitro studies allow for
a better understanding of the multiple
pathways that are induced in ECs in
response to infection, and how they are
regulated. Investigating the activation
pathways that lead to sepsis-induced
endothelial dysfunction (8) is a promising
avenue toward limiting sepsis-induced
organ failure. In this review, we aim to
summarize the functional phenotypic
modiﬁcations of ECs observed during
inﬂammation and sepsis (9), and review
the state of translational endothelial-driven
approaches in sepsis.

Recent improvements of in vivo imaging

characterization of the changes that occur

atomic force microscopy to perform a
nanomechanical analysis of microvessels,
Wiesinger and colleagues directly observed
reduced glycocalyx thickness after sepsis-
like challenges (enzymatic digestion, LPS,
and TNF-a [tumor necrosis factor-a]) (11).
Most recently, electron microscopy has
enabled three-dimensional ultrastructure
visualization of the capillary glycocalyx at
baseline and after endotoxemia in the heart,
kidney, and liver (12), and lung
microvasculature (13). Kataoka and
colleagues showed that destruction of the
glycocalyx increased macromolecule
permeability and leukocyte adhesion in the
subcutaneous microcirculation in the
kidney (14). These cumulative damages
may contribute to alterations in
microvascular rheology, affecting capillary
blood ﬂow, and ultimately tissue perfusion
(15).
After an acute injury, it takes
approximately 5 –7 days for the glycocalyx
layer to be restored, but it takes
substantially longer to fully recover
hydrodynamic activity (16). This delayed
healing may explain, at least in part, why
microcirculatory blood ﬂow remains
impaired in patients with septic shock
despite resuscitation and optimization of
macrocirculatory hemodynamics (17, 18).
In humans, damage to the glycocalyx has
also been described in other critical
conditions, including trauma (19),
post–cardiac arrest syndrome (20), and
ischemia-reperfusion syndrome (21), and
severe alterations are associated with worse
outcomes. Shed glycocalyx components,
such as Syndecan-1 and hyaluronan, have
been detected in the plasma of patients with
sepsis (22), with higher levels in more
severely ill patients (23) (Table E1 in the
online supplement).

Our understanding of the molecular
mechanisms involved in the hyperselective
and regulated permeability of the
endothelium has improved over the last two
decades and is well described in speci ﬁc
reviews (24, 25). In response to DAMPs
or PAMPs, the vascular barrier is
impaired (Figure 1) owing to glycocalyx
breakdown, EC apoptosis, and junction
protein dysregulation. Endothelial
hyperpermeability leads to interstitial
edema, which consecutively increases
interstitial pressure and worsens tissue
hypoperfusion. Endothelial intercellular
permeability is regulated by adherens
junctions (cadherin –cadherin) and tight
junctions (claudin/occludin). During acute
inﬂammation, phosphorylation of the
vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin
intracellular domain leads to its
internalization in clathrin-coated vesicles
(26–28), which promotes intercellular
permeability. In addition, cytokine-induced
phosphorylation of the VE-cadherin

recruitment of innate immune cells, which

sepsis. However, recent experimental

approaches that selectively limit vascular
leakage in experimental models of sepsis.

Proadhesive EC Phenotype
ECs regulate leukocyte traf ﬁcking through
the production of chemokines and the
expression of adhesion molecules (Figure 2),
primarily in the venous part of the
microcirculatory network. Integrin family
glycoproteins, including CD 62E/P (58),
VCAM-1, PECAM (platelet endothelial cell
adhesion molecule-1), and ICAM-1/2
(intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1/2)
(59) orchestrate leukocyte adhesion, rolling,
and crawling, which are necessary steps
before trans- or paracellular diapedesis
(60). English and colleagues reported that a
metalloproteinase termed ADAMS9 (a
disintegrin and metalloproteinase with
thrombospondin motifs 9) that is expressed
within cell–cell junctions is also involved in
transendothelial migration of monocytes
(61). Using confocal microscopy, Yan and
colleagues observed that SHP-2 (Src
homology domain 2 –containing
phosphatase-2) negatively regulates
neutrophil adhesion and promotes
transendothelial migration by enhancing
ICAM-1–VE-cadherin interactions (62).
SHP-2 is a protein tyrosine phosphatase
that is known to interact with ICAM-1 (63).
ICAM-1–regulating proteins seem to be
critical for adhesion and transendothelial
leukocyte migration. Endothelial CD2AP
(CD2-associated protein) limits ICAM-1
clustering and ICAM-1 –dependent
transendothelial migration (64). Annexin-2
also limits neutrophil transendothelial
migration by controlling the spatial
distribution of ICAM-1 (65). The B2
integrins LFA-1 (66) and Mac-1 (67) are
pivotal for neutrophil arrest and diapedesis,
respectively. Shed adhesion molecules such
as E-selectin, VCAM-1, and ICAM-1 can
be detected in the plasma of patients with
sepsis (Table E1), in whom high levels
correlate with organ failure severity (68).
Based on experimental and clinical studies,
blocking of adhesion molecules has been
proposed as a potential therapeutic
approach in sepsis (69). However, full
inhibition is probably deleterious because
leukocyte recruitment into the tissue is
mandatory to kill microbes and limit
dissemination (70).
Prothrombotic EC Phenotype
Under homeostatic conditions, the
glycocalyx layer and anticoagulant
molecules produced by ECs prevent
microvascular thrombosis and maintain
optimal blood ﬂuidity (71). During sepsis,
EC-related anticoagulant and ﬁbrinolytic
functions (72, 73) are impaired, leading to
platelet adhesion and coagulation cascade
activation (Figure 3). Activated ECs
express tissue factor (74) and release
phosphatidylserine positive microparticles
(75) that amplify local and disseminated
coagulation (76, 77). In addition, APC
(activated protein C) (78) and TFPI (tissue
factor pathway inhibitor) stocks are
reduced, which is responsible for
paradoxical hemorrhagic syndromes (76,
79). Recombinant APC, an inhibitor of
factor Va and factor VIIIa, reduced
mortality in a large randomized trial in
patients with sepsis (80), but subsequent
trials failed to con ﬁrm its protective
role (81, 82). APC was shown to have
coagulation-independent effects on ECs,
modulating cell survival through a PAR-1
(protease-activated receptor-1)-dependent
mechanism (83), endothelial permeability
(84), and cytokine secretion (85).
Therefore, APC will remain a therapeutic
option to be retested in patients with sepsis
in the future (86, 87). Thrombomodulin
(TM) also has coagulation-independent
functions and can scavenge toxic mediators,
such as complement factors, DAMPs, and
PAMPs (88).
During sepsis, the ﬁbrinolytic pathway
is also engaged, leading to PAI-1
(plasminogen activator inhibitor-1) release
by ECs and non-ECs (e.g., smooth muscle
cells and monocytes). SERPINE-1 (the gene
encoding PAI-1) expression and plasma
PAI-1 levels are markedly elevated during
systemic in ﬂammation. In rodents, during
LPS challenge or infection with gram-
negative bacteria ( Klebsiella pneumoniae ),
PAI-1 inhibition results in attenuated
hypercoagulation and thus reduced
mortality (89). Conversely, during
K. pneumoniae infection, Serpine-1
deﬁciency reduces neutrophil in ﬁltration
into the lungs, impairing the host defense,
with more bacterial dissemination and
increased mortality (90). Interestingly,
Serpine-1 de ﬁciency does not modify the
outcome of Streptococcus pneumoniae
infection (91). These results indicate that
the types of bacteremia and coagulopathy
induced by bacterial blood dissemination
should be considered separately.
In humans, high plasma PAI-1 levels
correlate with high mortality rates and the
onset of multiorgan failure (Table E1) (92).
Overall, thromboelastography with
urokinase has con ﬁrmed ﬁbrinolysis
impairment in patients with sepsis
compared with healthy controls. Alterations
in the ﬁbrinolysis/antiﬁbrinolysis balance
are associated with more severe organ
failure, higher lactate levels, and increased
mortality (93). In a neonatal piglet model
of endotoxin-induced disseminated
intravascular coagulation, Davis-
Jackson and colleagues reported that
supplementation with recombinant
tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and
antithrombin III reduced organ failure
severity and improved survival (94). A
strategy based on ﬁbrinolysis stimulation
in the context of sepsis is currently under
investigation (95).

Platelet–endothelium interactions also
contribute to sepsis-related coagulopathy
and associated impaired microvascular
blood ﬂow (96). In the context of
sepsis, platelets are consumed in
platelet–leukocyte aggregates and promote
the release of neutrophil extracellular traps
to limit the spread of bacteria (97).
Ultimately, aggregates adhere to the
endothelium, amplify local in ﬂammation,
and impair tissue perfusion (98).
Upregulation of CD40-L and Gp2b3a on
thrombin-activated platelets promotes
chemokine secretion, adhesion molecule
expression, and tissue factor release by
ECs (99). During sepsis, platelets are also
a signi ﬁcant source of microparticles,
which amplify systemic procoagulant,
proadherent, and proin ﬂammatory
activities (100). Red blood cells (RBCs)
are also key players in microvascular
immunothrombosis. RBCs from patients
with sepsis are more spherical and lower in
deformability. A decrease in the sialic acid
content of membranous glycophorin-A
reduces the negative electric charge on the
RBC surface, promoting aggregation and
microvascular thrombosis (101). In a
prospective study, low RBC deformability
signiﬁcantly correlated with mortality in
patients with sepsis (102).
Vascular Tone and Oxidative
Stress
During sepsis, the vascular tone is impaired
at least in part by disrupted production of
nitric oxide (NO), prostacyclin, and
endothelin. In rodents, LPS or TNF- a
administration was shown to downregulate
endothelial NO synthase (NOS) expression
(103, 104). Under physiological conditions,
heparan sulfate docks superoxide
dismutase, which quenches oxygen free
radicals and maintains NO bioavailability,
but this antioxidant, vasculoprotective
effect is compromised when the glycocalyx
is damaged (105, 106). In healthy
volunteers, exposure to endotoxin was
shown to impair endothelium-dependent
relaxation (107). After endotoxin exposure
in rodents, inducible NOS (iNOS) is
expressed and produces large amounts of
vasodilating NO that can complex with
superoxide anion to generate cytotoxic
peroxynitrite. In response to oxidative
stress, ECs synthesize and accumulate
reactive oxygen species that damage DNA,
proteins, and lipids. Several human NOS3
and NOS2 gene polymorphisms are
associated with plasma nitrite/nitrate levels
and poor outcomes in patients with septic
shock (108). Blocking iNOS activity has
been proposed as a therapeutic approach to
improve microvascular blood ﬂow in sepsis.
El-Awady and colleagues reported that in
LPS-treated rats, agmatine inhibited iNOS
expression and attenuated vascular
hyporeactivity, vascular oxidative stress,
and endothelial dysfunction (109).
However, treatment with NO inhibitors did
not improve outcomes in critically ill
patients with sepsis and may even worsen
hemodynamics (110, 111). The failure to
translate the bene ﬁt of NOS blockade from
rodents to humans with sepsis may be due,

concentrations (114). Along these lines,
patients with septic shock were found to
have lower plasma nitrite/nitrate
concentrations than experimental animal
models of sepsis (115).
Gap between Experimental
Studies and Human Sepsis
ECs share common properties and plasticity
but also display organ-speci ﬁc features
(5, 116). For example, the distribution of
tight and adherens junctions varies
substantially along the vascular tree.
Tight junctions are highly expressed in the
blood–brain barrier and brain
microcirculation but are less expressed
and organized in postcapillary venules
to allow optimal exchange between blood
and tissue. ECs do not express TM in
the brain, but it is a critical coagulation
factor in other organs (4). In sepsis
models, the lung endothelium displays
higher adhesion molecule expression
and higher permeability than the kidney
and liver (117). Moreover, the lung
capillaries have a smaller diameter than
capillaries in other organs, and in the
case of increased alveolar pressure can
be downsized even more (118). Such
structural speci ﬁcity may explain, at least
in part, why neutrophil recruitment
occurs in the small capillaries spanning
the alveolar network in the lung (119, 120),
whereas in other organs it usually
takes place in postcapillary venules (121).
Endothelial structural and functional
heterogeneity may explain, at least
in part, the differences in phenotype
and outcome between pneumonia-,
meningitis- and pyelonephritis-related
sepsis.
In addition, most pathophysiological
sepsis studies in rodents have been
performed with the use of an acute LPS
challenge (i.e., endotoxemia). Although
these acute “switch-on” studies are
informative and of interest, they have to be
analyzed with caution because they are far
from the clinical setting and do not
recapitulate the full mechanisms of sepsis in
humans who have comorbidities and are
receiving different treatments. Additional
danger signals, such as HMGB1 (high-
mobility group box 1), hyaluronan, and
heat shock protein, are implicated in the
modulation of both immune and
endothelial responses (122, 123). Finally,
in most experimental studies in sepsis,
ﬂuid resuscitation is not performed, and
thus the contribution of vascular leakage
to organ dysfunction in these models
is underestimated.
Identiﬁcation of EC
Dysfunction in Patients
with Sepsis
There are several tools that can be used
to identify the presence of EC dysfunction
in humans. One of the most widely
recognized tools is the use of biomarkers
of endothelial dysfunction that are
known to be upregulated in sepsis (124),
including shed glycocalyx products, shed
adhesion molecules, Angiopoietin-2 (125),
proteins from the coagulation cascade,
and the ﬁbrinolytic/antiﬁbrinolytic
pathway (126) (Table E1). Functional
hemodynamic monitors that are able
to target endothelial dysfunction show
promise for point-of-care identi ﬁcation
of endothelial dysfunction. Skibsted and
colleagues used near-infrared spectroscopy
in conjunction with a vascular occlusion
test to identify endothelial dysfunction
(127). The authors found a signi ﬁcant
relationship between near-infrared
spectroscopy–derived measurements
and biomarkers of endothelial dysfunction
in patients with sepsis. Hand-held vital
microscopy has been key in identifying
microcirculatory alterations associated
in sepsis, and has been used to identify
glycocalyx degradation (128). Acetylcholine
is well known to elicit endothelial-
associated vasodilation. De Backer
and colleagues used a challenge with
acetylcholine to identify the functionality
of EC-mediated vasodilation in patients
with sepsis in the sublingual area (129),
and Bourcier and colleagues used it to
analyze blood ﬂow in the skin forearm
area (130). Other clinical modalities
that can identify EC dysfunction in
clinical settings have been covered
elsewhere (117).
Therapeutic Ambitions and
Bedside Issues
Preclinical treatments targeting sepsis-
induced endothelial dysfunction provide
encouraging results, but so far none have
improved survival in large randomized
clinical trials (131) (Table E2). Nonselective
NOS blockers were associated with
increased mortality in patients with septic
shock (110), whereas the use of selective
iNOS inhibitors was protective in
preclinical studies. Methylene blue, which
has the ability to block cyclic guanosine
monophosphate production, scavenge
NO, and inhibit NOS, has been tested in
small-sample-size studies and was found
to have a bene ﬁcial effect on vascular and
kidney functions (132). Nitroglycerin
(133, 134) and vasodilating agents such
as Ilomedine (135) are now under
investigation.
Strategies targeting endothelium-
related coagulation disorders in sepsis
have been extensively evaluated, but the
results of interventional studies are
inconsistent (136, 137). APC (138) and
TFPI have successfully passed phase 2 but
did not improve survival in patients with
sepsis or septic shock; rather, they increased
the risk of major bleeding (82). Recently,
Vincent and colleagues reported that
in the international phase 3 SCARLET
trial, recombinant TM did not improve
Day 28 survival in patients with organ
failure and sepsis-associated coagulopathy
(139).
Statins have also been tested because
of their vascular antiin ﬂammatory
properties. Ulinastatin attenuates
pulmonary endothelial glycocalyx damage
and inhibits endothelial heparanase activity
in LPS-induced acute lung injury (140).
A single-center randomized trial showed
that atorvastatin decreased the plasma
levels of IL-1b, IL-6, TNF-a, and C-reactive
protein but had no effect on mortality
in patients with septic shock (141).
In a meta-analysis of controlled trials,
investigators also reached the same
conclusion— that statins do not improve
mortality in sepsis (142, 143). Ongoing
clinical trials are exploring the use of
microparticles, carnitine, vitamin C (144),
and vitamin B1 as endothelium-directed
adjuvant therapies.

Conclusions
ECs are undoubtedly a major player in the
systemic response to infection and onset of
organ failure. Endothelial dysfunction
induced by sepsis is a multifaceted
syndrome that includes impaired
coagulation, ﬁbrinolysis, permeability,
leukocyte recruitment, and vascular tone.
The molecular mechanisms underpinning
these functions are intricate, but some of
them are regulated separately, paving the
way for future therapeutics that can target
speciﬁc pathogenic pathways without
impairing protective innate immune
responses. A better characterization of
endothelial dysfunction may aid in the
selection of patients with sepsis who would
be most likely to respond to EC-targeted
therapies. n
