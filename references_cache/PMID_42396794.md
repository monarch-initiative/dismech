# DNASE1L3 Deficiency With Novel Missense Variant: Enzymatic and Plasma Fragmentomic Evidence of Pathogenicity and Partial Response to JAK Blockade.
**Authors:** Leon Tenorio AA, Sugio T, Cheng J, Bonner DE, Esfahani MS, Kasinathan S, Hsu JJ, Moyer A, Pimentel Vera L, Carter J, Reuter CM, Marwaha S, Balboni I, Undiagnosed Diseases Network, Wheeler MT, Bernstein JA, Alizadeh A, Gomez-Ospina N
**Journal:** ACR Open Rheumatol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/acr2.70184](https://doi.org/10.1002/acr2.70184)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Biallelic loss-of-function variants in DNASE1L3 cause inherited systemic lupus erythematosus and hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis. These disorders arise from defective clearance of extracellular chromatin, leading to autoantibody formation, immune complex deposition, and complement consumption. The full clinical and therapeutic spectrum of DNASE1L3 deficiency remains incompletely defined.

METHODS: We evaluated a 24-year-old woman with lifelong systemic inflammation characterized by polyarthritis, urticarial vasculitis, episcleritis, recurrent abdominal pain with intestinal angioedema, and persistent hypocomplementemia. Testing included autoantibody profiling, whole-exome sequencing, DNASE1L3 enzymatic assays in cell lysates and supernatants, and plasma analysis of cell-free DNA fragmentation.

RESULTS: The patient lacked antinuclear, anti-double-stranded DNA, and anti-Smith antibodies, though antiphospholipid antibodies were intermittently positive. The disease was refractory to multiple immunosuppressive agents but showed partial improvement with JAK inhibition. Genetic analysis revealed compound heterozygous DNASE1L3 variants: a pathogenic frameshift (c.290_291delCA) and a novel missense change (c.179T>G, p.Ile60Ser). Functional testing showed markedly impaired DNA degradation for both variants, with residual activity in the missense mutant. Plasma DNA analysis demonstrated reduced mononucleosomal peaks, altered fragment length distribution, and loss of cytosine-cytosine-rich end motifs, confirming reduced nuclease activity in vivo.

CONCLUSION: This case supports the pathogenicity of the DNASE1L3 p.Ile60Ser variant broadening the genetic spectrum. Plasma DNA fragment analysis provides a sensitive biomarker of impaired nuclease function, and JAK inhibition may offer partial therapeutic benefit in DNASE1L3-related systemic inflammation.

Deoxyribonuclease 1‐like 3 (DNASE1L3), also known as DNase γ, DNase Y, or LS‐DNase, is a secreted endonuclease of the DNASE1 family. It is mainly expressed by macrophages and dendritic cells and circulates in plasma as a dominant extracellular nuclease. DNASE1L3 uniquely binds and degrades chromatin as well as protein‐ or lipid‐complexed DNA, efficiently digesting nucleosomal DNA, DNA within immune complexes, and membrane‐bound DNA released during apoptosis. This function is essential for extracellular DNA clearance. In the absence of DNASE1L3, chromatin‐rich microparticles released during apoptosis accumulate and act as potent immunogenic stimuli, ultimately promoting activation of autoreactive B cells, production of autoantibodies, immune complex deposition, complement consumption, and type I interferon responses. Beyond DNA clearance, DNASE1L3 regulates inflammasome‐dependent cytokine release, including interleukin‐1 (IL‐1), and degrades neutrophil extracellular traps, linking it to inflammation, thrombosis, and cancer metastasis.
1



Biallelic loss‐of‐function variants in DNASE1L3 cause systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE‐16; OMIM: 614420),
2
 hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis syndrome (HUVS),
3
 and overlap syndromes with antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)–associated vasculitis (AAV), a group of autoimmune diseases marked by broad autoantibody production against nuclear, cytoplasmic, and surface antigens. SLE‐16 usually presents in childhood, often before age five years, and is characterized by lupus nephritis (~60%), pulmonary hemorrhage (~15%), ANCA positivity, hypocomplementemia, and urticarial rash. HUVS manifests with chronic urticarial vasculitis, severe hypocomplementemia, arthritis, glomerulonephritis, and uveitis, and often progresses to SLE. An SLE–AAV overlap syndrome combines features of both—hypocomplementemia, immune complex glomerulonephritis, and ANCA positivity—and is frequently refractory to standard immunosuppressive therapy with poor renal outcomes.

In plasma, nearly half of circulating cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) arises from DNASE1L3‐mediated fragmentation.
1
 This activity leaves distinct molecular signatures, notably fragments ending in cytosine—especially CCNN end motifs—forming the “founder profile I” (F‐profile I). DNASE1L3 also determines cfDNA jaggedness, the single‐stranded protruding ends of multinucleosomal fragments, which are markedly reduced in DNASE1L3‐deficient mice and patients with sporadic or DNASE1L3‐related SLE.
4
 Deficiency causes accumulation of long cfDNA species (di‐, tri‐, and tetranucleosomes) and shifts end motifs toward adenine‐rich and guanine‐rich termini, reflecting undigested substrates of upstream nucleases, such as DNA fragmentation factor subunit beta. These fragmentomic alterations serve as in vivo readouts of DNASE1L3 activity and provide mechanistic insight into how defective DNA clearance drives systemic autoimmunity.

Patients were enrolled in protocols approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Stanford University (47026) and the National Institutes of Health (15‐HG‐0130). Written informed consent for genetic testing, publication of clinical data, and use of images was obtained from the patient. Beyond the Undiagnosed Diseases Network study (members listed in Appendix A), clinical and laboratory data were obtained during routine care and later reviewed for research purposes.

Mutant plasmids carrying the p.Thr97Ilefs*2 and p.Ile60Ser variants were generated by site‐directed mutagenesis of wild‐type DNASE1L3 cDNA (New England Biolabs) and sequence verified to confirm the desired mutations. K562 cells were transfected with equal amounts of wild‐type, mutant, or empty vector constructs using a 4D‐Nucleofector (Lonza, program FF‐120) and cultured in RPMI with 10% serum. After 24 hours, cells were counted and transferred to serum‐free medium; supernatants and lysates were collected 72 hours post transfection. Lysates were prepared in DNase assay buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.4, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, 1% Triton X‐100, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride), and protein levels were normalized using the Pierce bicinchoninic acid assay. For activity testing, pMax green fluorescent protein plasmid (500 ng) was incubated with normalized lysates (5 μg protein) for 30 minutes at 37°C or with normalized supernatants (5 μg protein) for 0.5 to 4 hours at 37°C. Digestion products were resolved on 0.9% agarose gels in Tris‐borate‐EDTA buffer and visualized on a Bio‐Rad system. Conditions included wild‐type DNASE1L3, empty vector, and both patient‐derived variants.

Peripheral blood was collected into Streck Cell‐Free DNA or EDTA tubes and processed within two hours of phlebotomy. Plasma was separated by two‐step centrifugation (1,600g for 10 minutes and 16,000g × 10 minutes for Streck; 2,500g for 10 minutes for EDTA) to obtain platelet‐free plasma, and aliquots were stored at −80°C. cfDNA was extracted from 1 to 2 mL plasma using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen), quantified by the Qubit double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA) assay (ThermoFisher), and assessed for integrity Bioanalyzer high‐sensitivity DNA chips (5200 Fragment Analyzer System, Agilent). Whole‐genome sequencing libraries were prepared using a ligation‐based protocol preserving fragment ends and sequenced (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeqX platform to a depth of more than 100 million reads or sample. Adapter sequences and low‐quality bases were trimmed, reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19), and polymerase chain reaction duplicates were removed before downstream analysis (samtools markdup, samtools version 1.17). cfDNA fragment size distributions were computed using a custom script that assesses the template length column of each paired read. End‐motif frequencies were determined by extracting the 4‐bp sequences at the 5′ ends of cfDNA fragments and classifying them by base preference with a custom script. Particular attention was given to CC‐rich motifs and frequencies were normalized to the total number of fragments per sample. Results were compared with our own established reference datasets from healthy controls (n = 3).

We report a 24‐year‐old woman with lifelong systemic inflammation. Born at term after an uncomplicated pregnancy, she was first evaluated in infancy for gastroesophageal reflux. By age 3, she developed persistent polyarthritis involving hands, wrists, elbows, knees, and ankles, followed by recurrent rashes, urticarial vasculitis, bilateral episcleritis, intermittent lymphadenopathy, and episodic abdominal pain with intestinal angioedema documented on imaging. Initial diagnoses included antinuclear antibody (ANA)‐ and rheumatoid factor‐negative polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis, but her systemic features suggested an undifferentiated autoinflammatory process. By adolescence, her presentation resembled HUVS with persistent mucocutaneous and joint involvement and hypocomplementemia (Figure 1A and B). Skin biopsy at age 15 showed perivascular lymphocyte‐predominant infiltrate with eosinophils and neutrophils, consistent with urticarial dermatitis. Her history was complicated by appendectomy at age 8, pancreatitis following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography for choledocholithiasis at 13 with recurrent episodes thereafter, spinal fusion for scoliosis at 14, and group A Streptococcus toxic shock syndrome at 18 requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support. At age 24, she continued to experience intermittent flares of arthritis, vasculitic skin lesions, and episcleritis (Figure 1A–C) but showed no evidence of glomerulonephritis.

The patient had persistent hypocomplementemia with a markedly reduced C3 level (26–78 mg/dL; reference: 86–184 mg/dL, ages 14–24 years) and a C4 level consistently <8 mg/dL (reference: 20–59 mg/dL). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C‐reactive protein, and ferritin levels remained normal, and cytopenias occurred only during infections or ECMO‐related critical illness. C1q binding was elevated at 15.2 μgE/mL (reference: 0.0–3.9 μgE/mL), indicating increased circulating immune complexes. Antiphospholipid antibodies were intermittently positive, including phosphatidylserine/prothrombin IgM (8.7–53.1 U; reference ≤30), anticardiolipin IgM (8.6–18.3 MPL U/mL; reference: 0–12.5), and IgG (5–40 GPL U/mL; reference: 0–15). The D‐dimer level was variably elevated (0.55–16.93 μg/mL; reference <0.50 μg/mL), and the lactate dehydrogenase level ranged from 314 to 1,150 U/L (reference: 135–214 U/L). Autoantibodies, including ANA and anti‐dsDNA (ages 14, 15, 17, and 23 years); anti‐Ro, anti‐La, anti‐Sm, anti‐RNP, anti‐Scl70, and anti‐Jo1 antibodies (ages 14, 17, and 18 years); anticentromere (age 14 years); and rheumatoid factor (age 17 years), were negative. ANCAs and antimyeloperoxidase and anti‐proteinase 3 antibodies were also negative (ages 14 and 17 years). No kidney biopsy was performed. Overall, results indicated immune complex–mediated inflammation with complement consumption.

The patient's treatment evolved in response to refractory inflammation. Early management included methotrexate and intermittent glucocorticoids for flare control. She received anakinra, an IL‐1 receptor antagonist, followed by canakinumab, an anti‐IL‐1β monoclonal antibody. After missing a canakinumab dose at age 13 years, arthritis and rash flares required resuming anakinra and prednisone. Following spinal fusion at 14 years of age, worsening inflammatory symptoms prompted reinitiation of methotrexate, though her disease remained poorly controlled. At 17 years of age, she received tocilizumab, an anti‐IL‐6 receptor antibody, which was discontinued due to severe skin reactions. After surviving group A Streptococcus toxic shock syndrome at 18 years of age, she began ruxolitinib (JAK1 or 2 inhibitor), achieving marked improvement in arthritis, vasculitis, and abdominal symptoms, allowing prednisone withdrawal. At 23 years of age, loss of ruxolitinib access led to relapse; steroids were reintroduced, and hydroxychloroquine was added. She later transitioned to baricitinib, another JAK1/2 inhibitor, with partial improvement, though less robust than on ruxolitinib, possibly due to baricitinib's longer half‐life (12–13 hours vs 3–6 hours). She remains off systemic glucocorticoids, maintained on baricitinib and hydroxychloroquine, with persistent low‐grade inflammatory activity.

Given the patient's early‐onset, severe phenotype, quint whole‐exome sequencing was performed on the patient, her parents, and two asymptomatic brothers. Analysis identified compound heterozygous DNASE1L3 variants (Figure 1D): a paternally inherited frameshift (NM_004944.4: c.290_291del, p.Thr97Ilefs*2) and a maternally inherited missense variant (NM_004944.4: c.179T>G, p.Ile60Ser). The unaffected brother II‐1 did not harbor either variant, whereas II‐2 was heterozygous for p.Ile60Ser, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. All variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Variant classification followed American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) criteria. The p.Thr97Ilefs*2 allele is rare in gnomAD version 4.1.0 (minor allele frequency = 0.0001051) with no homozygotes reported and is predicted to introduce a frameshift and premature stop codon. It was classified as pathogenic based on PVS1 (null variant), PS3 (loss of DNASE1L3 activity in functional assays), PM2_supporting (absence of homozygotes in population databases), and PM3 (previously observed in trans with pathogenic alleles in autosomal recessive HUVS and SLE). The p.Ile60Ser variant is extremely rare (MAF = 0.000003390 in gnomAD version 4.1.0) and predicted to be deleterious (Combined Annotation‐Dependent Depletion score = 28.1). The p.Ile60Ser has not been previously associated with disease. It is listed in ClinVar (variation ID: 1172534, accessed October 2, 2025) and was initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

Although pathogenicity of the c.290_291delCA frameshift variant was established, the functional impact of the p.Ileu60Ser missense variant was uncertain. Isoleucine 60 lies in the N‐terminal region of DNASE1L3, outside of the catalytic domain and DNA‐binding interface. Although distant from functional hotspots, this residue is highly conserved, and AlphaMissense predicted it to be likely pathogenic (high confidence score of 0.950).

To assess the functional consequences of both variants, we employed a plasmid nicking assay, which measures DNase activity based on the ability of the expressed protein to linearize supercoiled plasmid DNA. Loss of DNase activity is expected to result in retention of the supercoiled form, whereas a functional enzyme cleaves and linearizes the DNA. Supernatants from K562 cells transfected with wild‐type DNASE1L3 plasmid efficiently linearized supercoiled plasmid DNA, confirming robust extracellular nuclease activity (Figure 1E [top panel] and F). In contrast, supernatants from K562 cells overexpressing either the c.290_291delCA or p.Ileu60Ser plasmid variants failed to linearize plasmid DNA, indicating a loss of extracellular activity. Similar results were observed in cell lysates: wild‐type transfections produced detectable DNA cleavage, whereas lysates from cells expressing either variant showed minimal to no activity (Figure 1E [bottom panel] and F), suggesting that intracellular nuclease function is also impaired. Quantitative analysis of DNA degradation kinetics over time (Figure 1G) revealed significantly faster plasmid linearization in wild‐type supernatant compared to either variant. Notably, the p.Ileu60Ser variant exhibited a some residual activity compared to the frameshift allele, suggesting that although functionally impaired, it may retain partial catalytic function. Applying ACMG or AMP criteria, the p.Ile60Ser variant can be classified as “likely pathogenic,” supported by the following evidence: PS3 (functional studies demonstrate loss of enzymatic activity), PM2_supporting (absent or extremely rare in gnomAD version 4.1 with no homozygotes), and PP3 (multiple in silico predictions indicating a deleterious effect).

In DNASE1L3 deficiency, plasma cfDNA is elevated and accumulates in apoptotic microparticles. Fragmentation patterns are altered, showing more long multinucleosomal (>250 bp) and short (<120 bp) fragments. The DNASE1L3 end‐motif signature—CC‐rich C‐end fragments (F‐profile I)—is reduced, with a shift toward A‐ and G‐ends, and jagged ends on multinucleosomal DNA are diminished. Plasma cfDNA from the proband showed clear deviations from her unaffected brother (II‐1, wild‐type/wild‐type (WT/WT), Figure 1H), mother (I‐2, WT/p.Ile60Ser, Figure 1D), and healthy controls. cfDNA concentration was highest in the proband (23.2 ng/mL vs 5.8, 11.2, and 6.9–8.1 ng/mL, respectively). Fragment size distribution showed that the proband had a relative reduction in the canonical ∼166 bp mononucleosomal peak, an increased density of subnucleosomal fragments, accompanied by an increased proportion of longer fragments (>250 bp) (Figure 1H). End‐motif analysis revealed striking differences in the proband, with a marked reduction in cytosine‐rich 4‐mer motifs, particularly CC‐containing sequences (eg, CCCA, CCTG, CCAG, CCAA), compared to healthy and family controls (Figure 1I and J). These results confirm loss of DNASE1L3‐associated fragmentomic signatures and impaired nuclease activity in the proband.

This case highlights the importance of considering monogenic autoimmune or autoinflammatory disorders in early‐onset lupus‐like disease and underscores the diagnostic challenges in patients who fail to meet conventional criteria for lupus or related rheumatologic conditions. The proband's lifelong systemic inflammation with recurrent urticarial vasculitis, polyarthritis, episcleritis, and hypocomplementemia most closely resembled HUVS. Despite decades of disease, the patient lacked ANA and anti‐dsDNA antibodies characteristic of lupus but showed persistently low complement, elevated circulating immune complexes, and intermittent antiphospholipid antibody positivity, findings consistent with immune complex–mediated pathology secondary to defective extracellular DNA clearance.

Molecular testing revealed compound heterozygous DNASE1L3 variants comprising a known pathogenic frameshift mutation and a previously unreported missense substitution. Functional in vitro assays, corroborated by in vivo evidence of defective cfDNA degradation, demonstrated impaired DNASE1L3 activity from both alleles. According to ACMG or AMP criteria, the p.Ile60Ser variant can thus be classified as likely pathogenic. These findings provide experimental confirmation for the reclassification of the novel variant and emphasize the importance of functional genomics in clarifying variants of uncertain significance in rare monogenic autoimmune diseases.

Complementary plasma cfDNA fragmentomics provided an in vivo readout of nuclease deficiency, showing hallmark features of DNASE1L3 loss: reduced mononucleosomal peak, accumulation of long multinucleosomal fragments, and depletion of CC‐rich cytosine end motifs. These alterations corroborated the enzymatic assays and confirmed the use of cfDNA profiling as a reliable biomarker for DNASE1L3‐related disease.
4



Reviews of monogenic lupus and interferonopathies support JAK inhibition as a rational therapy in DNASE1L3‐related disease, given its overlap with type I interferon–driven autoimmunity. However, clinical experience with JAK inhibition in monogenic DNASE1L3 deficiency is limited. In a child with homozygous DNASE1L3 variants and refractory glomerulonephritis, ruxolitinib improved cutaneous, articular, and constitutional manifestations but did not prevent progression to end‐stage kidney disease.
5
 Our patient's response, the published case, along with evidence that interferon signatures in DNASE1L3 deficiency can be transient rather than sustained, suggests that the timing and disease context in which JAK inhibitors are introduced may critically influence their efficacy. JAK inhibition thus emerges as a promising adjunct for systemic control, whereas therapies restoring extracellular DNA clearance—such as recombinant DNASE1L3—may offer more effective prevention of progressive tissue injury.

This study expands the molecular spectrum of DNASE1L3 deficiency, establishes the pathogenicity of a novel missense variant, and highlights cfDNA fragmentomics as a useful biomarker. The partial response to JAK inhibition supports its use as an adjunct therapy and underscores the contribution of interferon signaling but also its limitations.
