# Anesthetic management of primary renal sarcoma with Mayo IV tumor thrombus using moderate hypothermic circulatory arrest and antegrade cerebral perfusion: a case report.
**Authors:** Huang Z, Chen S, Zhang G, Lu L, Lin Y
**Journal:** BMC Urol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12894-026-02161-x](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02161-x)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Primary renal sarcomas in adults are rare, accounting for 1% of all renal tumors. They progress rapidly and are resistant to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Currently, surgery is the only effective treatment.

CASE PRESENTATION: A 49-year-old man was admitted with a left kidney mass and inferior vena cava (IVC) tumor thrombus extending into the right atrium (Mayo grade IV). He underwent laparoscopic radical nephrectomy and tumor thrombus removal under extracorporeal circulation with moderate hypothermic circulatory arrest (25 °C) and bilateral selective antegrade cerebral perfusion. This technique ensured minimal bleeding while providing effective protection for the brain and coagulation system. The total circulatory arrest time was 91 min, with stable cerebral oxygen saturation throughout.

CONCLUSIONS: Moderate hypothermic circulatory arrest combined with bilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion, along with a multidisciplinary perioperative management strategy, ensured a favorable prognosis for this patient with a Mayo level IV tumor thrombus. This report aims to provide a reference for the perioperative management of patients with Mayo grade IV tumor thrombi.

Renal cell carcinoma with Mayo grade IV tumor thrombi invading the right atrium requires multidisciplinary collaboration and carries high perioperative risks [1]. Moderate hypothermic circulatory arrest (MHCA) combined with antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP) is an established technique for reducing hypothermia-related complications and protecting against cerebral ischemia in complex aortic surgery [2]. However, its application in renal sarcoma with Mayo grade IV tumor thrombi remains exploratory [3]. This case report focuses on the anesthetic management of a patient with Mayo grade IV renal sarcoma, discussing the implementation of MHCA with ACP and strategies for circulatory and cerebral protection.

A 49-year-old man (60 kg, 175 cm) was admitted with a “left kidney mass for four months and inferior vena cava tumor thrombus for seven days.” This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the Aerospace Center Hospital (2023, No.126). Informed consent was obtained from the patient for this case report. He had undergone left renal artery embolization for hematuria four months before admission. Three months before admission, he had experienced a cerebral infarction, which improved after medication without sequelae. He had a 20-year history of high blood pressure, with appreciable sustenance.

Abdominal enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed an abnormal soft tissue density mass with progressive enlargement in the lower part of the left kidney, measuring approximately 64 × 63 × 67 mm. The left renal vein was significantly widened, with multiple soft tissue density shadows accompanied by uneven enhancement. The lesion involved the inferior vena cava and extended into the right atrium, with a significant widening of the inferior vena cava (Fig. 1 (A), (B)). Abdominal enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a left kidney mass with internal bleeding. The left renal vein and inferior vena cava showed widening, with a maximum diameter of approximately 47 mm, accompanied by abnormal signals and enhancement extending into the right atrium, suggesting a high probability of a tumor thrombus (Fig. 1 (C), (D)). Coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed normal coronary arteries. The density within the right atrium and inferior vena cava was uneven, and significant enhancement of T2W1 bands was observed, suggesting the presence of cancer thrombus in the right atrium and inferior vena cava. Chest enhanced CT did not show pulmonary embolism. Preoperative laboratory test results showed no abnormalities, except for a hemoglobin level of 10.4 g/dL. Prior to surgery, eight units of red blood cells, 1000 mL of fresh frozen plasma, and one unit of platelets were prepared.

In the operating theatre, the patient’s blood pressure was monitored by puncture and catheterization of the left radial artery under local anesthesia. During surgery, electrocardiogram, blood oxygen saturation, end-tidal carbon dioxide partial pressure, nasopharyngeal temperature, bladder temperature, near-infrared spectroscopy cerebral oxygen saturation, and urine output were continuously monitored. Sufentanil, midazolam, etomidate, and cisatracurium besylate were administered to induce anesthesia. After endotracheal intubation, a right internal jugular vein puncture was performed under ultrasound guidance to place the catheter and monitor central venous pressure. Propofol, sevoflurane, sufentanil, and atracurium were used to sustain anesthesia.

After successful anesthesia, the patient underwent laparoscopic left nephrectomy in the right-fold position. The patient was then placed in the supine position, and the urology department extensively freed the inferior vena cava to the level of the diaphragm under laparoscopy. Simultaneously, the cardiology department performed a midline thoracotomy, followed by catheterization of the right axillary artery, superior vena cava, and right femoral vein to establish extracorporeal circulation, which was then cooled. Circulation was stopped at 25 ℃, with ice cap brain protection. The aorta was blocked between the left common carotid and left subclavian arteries at the descending part of the aortic arch. Bilateral common carotid artery anterograde cerebral perfusion was performed via the right axillary artery, with a perfusion pressure of 50–60 mmHg and flow rate of 0.7–0.8 L/min. The right renal vein, distal inferior vena cava of the right renal vein, and inferior vena cava were clamped at the point where the inferior vena cava entered the right atrium. The inferior vena cava was opened and a brittle tumor thrombus that adhered to the inner wall of the vessel was indicated. Simultaneously, the cardiac surgeon performed a free incision of the inferior vena cava toward the distal end, revealing clear and complete borders of the tumor thrombus head. The urology and cardiac surgery teams jointly performed an up-and-down resection of the tumor thrombus, using distilled water repeatedly and then suction. The incision in the inferior vena cava was then sutured using a sliding thread (Fig. 2). The blocking band was released, and intracorporeal circulation was restored. The patient was gradually rewarmed, spontaneous heartbeat resumed, and extracorporeal assistive circulation was continued. The extracorporeal circulation was subsequently terminated efficiently. Protamine, platelets, and plasma were used to restore coagulation. The incision was closed after achieving sufficient hemostasis. The operative, extracorporeal circulation, aortic clamp, circulatory arrest, anterograde cerebral perfusion, and parallel times were 852; 301; 225; 91; and 60 min, respectively. The intraoperative outputs included 2,000 and 3,800 mL of blood and urine, respectively. The intake included 12 units, 1,600 mL, one unit, 200 mL, 366 mL, and 8,000 mL of suspended red blood cells, plasma, platelets, machine blood, washed red blood cells, and liquid, respectively. After surgery, the patient was returned to the intensive care unit (ICU) with a tracheostomy tube in place. Anti-infection therapy and 400-mL plasma infusion were provided to treat coagulation disorders. The patient was transferred to the general ward on postoperative day one. The patient was discharged from the ward on the 25th day after surgery. After a 15-month follow-up, no tumor recurrence or metastasis was observed. The patient is currently receiving immunotherapy at our hospital.

The inferior vena cava tumor thrombus and left renal malignancy showed necrosis. Immunohistochemistry results were consistent with the diagnosis of sarcoma (Fig. 3).

Renal cell carcinoma has the characteristic of invading blood vessels, with approximately 4–10% invading the inferior vena cava, of which 1% extend upward to the right atrium [3]. Widespread tumor thrombi caused by renal sarcomas are uncommon [4, 5]. Reports indicate that, for patients without distant metastasis and lymph node invasion, radical nephrectomy combined with surgical removal of tumor thrombus can significantly prolong survival and improve quality of life [6]. The 5-year survival rate after surgery is approximately 20–53%.

Research has shown that, for Mayo grade IV tumor thrombi, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) is required [7, 8], which can enable the complete removal and repair of inferior vena cava thrombi with minimal blood loss. During circulatory arrest, tissue and organ ischemia, hypoxia, and anaerobic metabolism increase, and ischemia-reperfusion injury cannot be ignored. The safe time limit for DHCA is usually 30–45 min. However, during DHCA, insufficient cerebral perfusion and prolonged cerebral ischemia and hypoxia, as well as microcirculatory disorders and inflammatory reactions, are important causes of brain damage. We adopted the MHCA technique, which can reduce hypothermia-related complications and effectively avoid cerebral ischemic injury. Currently, the optimal temperature and safe time limits for MHCA remain unclear. We believe that the target temperature is related to the surgical method, duration, cerebral perfusion, and brain function monitoring during surgery. Selective cerebral perfusion technology has been reported to prolong the safe time limit for low-temperature circulatory arrest and increase the minimum permissible temperature [2, 3].

We chose the mild hypothermia circulatory arrest (25 °C) and anterograde cerebral perfusion (perfusion pressure, 50–60 mmHg; flow rate, 0.7–0.8 L/min) based on advances in cerebral protection research for major vascular surgery [9, 10]. During this period, we continuously monitored regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) and developed warning measures. When rSO₂ falls below 50% or decreases by more than 20% from baseline, initial management includes ruling out cannula malposition or kinking, followed by interventions such as increasing flow and perfusion pressure, transfusing red blood cells to enhance cerebral oxygen delivery, or lowering temperature to reduce cerebral oxygen consumption when necessary. During the 91-min circulatory arrest period, the cerebral oxygen saturation was approximately 65% on the left and 50% on the right. Our handling measures were safe and effective.

Although a low temperature of 25 °C can prevent platelet and thrombin dysfunction caused by prolonged deep hypothermia, coagulation dysfunction caused by extracorporeal circulation and low-temperature circulatory arrest is also a key concern. After administering 200 mg of protamine and stopping extracorporeal circulation, we empirically administered 1 unit of platelets, 1,000 mL of fresh frozen plasma, and 1 g of fibrinogen. The laboratory test results indicated PLT, 132 × 10⁹/L; PT, 13.0 s; FIB, 3.0 g/L; and INR, 1.17. Continuously monitoring platelet count and coagulation function indicators can guide subsequent treatment plans.

This case demonstrates the feasibility of using MHCA with ACP for the successful management of a rare primary renal sarcoma with Mayo grade IV tumor thrombus. The multidisciplinary approach, combined with meticulous anesthetic management including temperature control, cerebral oximetry, and goal-directed coagulation therapy, contributed to the excellent patient outcome. Long-term follow-up is ongoing. As a single case report, this study has limited generalizability, and large-scale studies are necessary to develop optimal protocols.
