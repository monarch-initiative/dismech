# CGGV:assertion_9630f9a3-3f90-49c6-ae8b-6313c950b1b2-2020-09-01T100000.000Z  BCL11A / Dias-Logan syndrome

**CGGV:assertion_9630f9a3-3f90-49c6-ae8b-6313c950b1b2-2020-09-01T100000.000Z** - BCL11A / Dias-Logan syndrome (Definitive)

## Evidence summary

BCL11A was first reported in relation to autosomal dominant Dias-Logan syndrome in 2016, at which time Dias et al. (PMID: 27453576) described nine individuals with similar phenotypes, all of which had predicted loss-of-function variants in BCL11A . All individuals had neurodevelopmental concerns, such as intellectual disability and developmental delay, and persistent fetal hemoglobin. At the time of curation, 27 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants have been reported, predominantly frameshift variants, though nonsense and pathogenic missense variants have also been observed. Dias-Logan syndrome (also known as Intellectual disability with persistence of fetal hemoglobin) is characterized by developmental delay/intellectual disability, and fetal hemoglobin that remains high in childhood (wheras is normally low after birth), infantile hypotonia, and distinctive but variable facial features. Other features such as language delay, microcephaly, and autism spectrum disorder are also associated with this syndrome.

In 2007, Rajcan-Separovic et al. (PMID: 16963482) described a neurodevelopmental syndrome associated with deletions in 2p15-16.1. This group of features was labeled Dias-Logan syndrome after the publication by Dias et al. in 2016 demonstrating the pathogenic nature of BCL11A haploinsufficiency. Note that two probands, subjects 5 and 7, could not be entered in the gene curation interface, but were counted towards the scoring total. These subjects both had de novo frameshift variants, and were given scores of 2 and 1.5, respectively (second score downgraded due to lack of information about fetal hemoglobin levels). The phenotypic spectrum associated with BCL11A variants continues to expand, with features such as seizures being reported in more recent cases, along with the typical presentation of Dias-Logan syndrome (e.g., Soblet et al., 2018, PMID: 28960836; Yoshida et al., 2018, PMID: 28589569). Most recently, a case was reported by Korenke et al. (2020, PMID: 32903878) of a 13 year old male patient with classic features of Dias-Logan syndrome.

This gene-disease relationship is also supported by experimental evidence, including mouse and cell models. Dias et al. (2016) showedy that Bcl11a haploinsufficient mice had brain and behavioral abnormalities in line with features observed in Dias-Logan syndrome patients, including microcephaly, impaired cognition, and abnormal social behavior. Using cell models, this study also demonstrated that three missense variants observed in patients disrupted protein localization, dimerization, and transcriptional regulation, consistent with a loss-of-function effect. This evidence was used to award default points to the de novo missense variants in the genetic evidence section.

In summary, there is definitive evidence supporting the relationship between BCL11A and autosomal dominant Dias-Logan syndrome. This has been repeatedly demonstrated in both the research and clinical diagnostic settings, and has been upheld over time. This classification was approved by the ClinGen Intellectual Disability and Autism Gene Curation Expert Panel on September 1, 2020 (SOP version 7).
