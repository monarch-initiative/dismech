OBJECTIVE: Brain somatic mutations in mTOR pathway genes are a major genetic 
etiology of focal cortical dysplasia type II (FCDII). Despite a greater ability 
to detect low-level somatic mutations in the brain by deep sequencing and 
analytics, about 40% of cases remain genetically unexplained.
METHODS: We included 2 independent cohorts consisting of 21 patients with 
mutation-negative FCDII without apparent mutations on conventional deep 
sequencing of bulk brain. To find ultra-low level somatic variants or structural 
variants, we isolated cells exhibiting phosphorylation of the S6 ribosomal 
protein (p-S6) in frozen brain tissues using fluorescence-activated cell sorting 
(FACS). We then performed deep whole-genome sequencing (WGS; >90×) in p-S6+ 
cells in a cohort of 11 patients with mutation-negative. Then, we simplified the 
method to whole-genome amplification and target gene sequencing of p-S6+ cells 
in independent cohort of 10 patients with mutation-negative followed by low-read 
depth WGS (10×).
RESULTS: We found that 28.6% (6 of 21) of mutation-negative FCDII carries 
ultra-low level somatic mutations (less than 0.2% of variant allele frequency 
[VAF]) in mTOR pathway genes. Our method showed ~34 times increase of the 
average mutational burden in FACS mediated enrichment of p-S6+ cells (average 
VAF = 5.84%) than in bulky brain tissues (average VAF = 0.17%). We found that 
19% (4 of 21) carried germline structural variations in GATOR1 complex 
undetectable in whole exome or targeted gene sequencing.
CONCLUSIONS: Our method facilitates the detection of ultra-low level somatic 
mutations, in specifically p-S6+ cells, and germline structural variations and 
increases the genetic diagnostic rate up to ~80% for the entire FCDII cohort.

activation of mTOR kinase and cause FCDII
pathophysiology.
7– 9 In addition, germline mutations in
Gap Activity Toward Rags 1 (GATOR1) complex genes,

a 2-hit mechanism. 10– 12 These genetic variations in
mTOR pathway genes account for /C2460% of patients with
FCDII.11,12 However, the remaining 40% of patients with
FCDII are mutation-negative upon deep sequencing of
bulk brain tissues and remain genetically unexplained.
Two possible scenarios may explain these mutation-
negative FCDII cases (herein, mut-negative FCDII). First,
there may be extremely low-level somatic mutations
undetectable by deep sequencing in bulk brain tissues.
Despite the outstanding performance of bioinformatics
algorithms for somatic mutations, reliable detection of
low-level mutations in deep sequencing is limited to those
present at a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 0.5% and
higher.
13 A recent observation in a mouse model of FCD
with Rheb somatic mutations via in utero electroporation
showed that 50 to 100 mutation-harboring cells per corti-
cal section elicited behavioral and electrical seizures at a
VAF of less than 0.1%.
14 Thus, patients with mut-
negative FCDII may carry extremely low-level somatic
mutations in mTOR pathway genes undetectable by deep
sequencing of bulk brain tissues. Second, there may be
novel genetic variations undetectable by whole exome or
targeted gene sequencing.
15 For example, in a recent study
of deep whole exome sequencing (WES) in 44 FCDII and
16 hemimegaloencephaly (HME) individuals, genetic
defects in the mTOR pathway were found in 30% and
75%, respectively.
15 No other genes had multiple disease-
causing somatic variants in the FCDII and HME
cohorts.15 Therefore, novel genetic variations eliciting
large structural variations or genetic variations in a non-
coding region or novel genes could lead to aberrant hyper-
activation of the mTOR pathway.
To address this, we developed a method of
ﬂuorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-mediated
enrichment of mTOR hyperactivated neurons from
mut-negative FCDII brains and then performed deep
whole genome sequencing (>X90 read depth). Here, we
showed that in 21 patients with mut-negative FCDII who
lacked apparent mutations in deep sequencing of bulk
brain tissues, 28.6% (6 of 21) carried ultra-low level
somatic mutations in MTOR or AKT3, and 19% (4 of
21) carried germline structural variations in GATOR1
complex genes, including NPRL3 or DEPDC5. These
results suggest that our analytic method of detecting ultra-
low level somatic mutations and structural variations
increases the genetic diagnosis rate of FCDII to /C2480% for
the entire FCDII cohort.
Patients and Methods
Patient Ascertainment
This study included 21 patients with mut-negative FCDII
(16 men and 5 women); 13 patients were pathologically
diagnosed with FCDIIa and 8 with FCDIIb
(Supplementary Table S1). In total, 18 subjects with
FCDII who had undergone epilepsy surgery at Severance
Children’s Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medi-
cine, between 2004 and 2020 were enrolled. Patients
underwent comprehensive evaluation with video electroen-
cephalography (EEG) monitoring, high-resolution mag-
netic resonance imaging (MRI), ﬂuorodeoxyglucose-
positron emission tomography (PET), and subtraction
ictal single-photon emission computed tomography co-
registered to MRI to localize anatomic lesions. All
18 patients met study entry criteria for FCDII.
16 Pres-
urgical evaluation and surgical intervention procedures
have been documented in previous studies.
17 Presurgical
evaluation and clinical phenotyping was conducted by the
neuropediatricians. The pathologic diagnosis with FCDII
was assessed by an experienced neuropathologist and
reconﬁrmed for this study according to the consensus clas-
siﬁcation by the International League Against Epilepsy
Diagnostic Methods Commission.
16 To validate our
method in other independent FCDII cohorts, we received
3 brain tissues of FCDII cases from the University Medi-
cal Center Utrecht (UMCU, the Netherlands). None of
the 21 patients had achieved genetic diagnosis upon previ-
ous targeted gene sequencing of the resected brain speci-
mens, and previous genetic results were thoroughly
reviewed prior to study enrollment. This study was per-
formed in accordance with protocols approved by Sever-
ance Hospital and the KAIST Institutional Review Board
and Committee on Human Research, and all human tis-
sues were obtained with informed consent.

Discussion
Recent advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS)
technique and bioinformatic algorithms for detecting
somatic mutations have helped greatly in identifying
genetic causes of FCDII. Conventional sequencing of bulk
tissues, however, has shown a limited ability to detect
low-level somatic mutations underlying FCDII.
32,33 Here,
we show that target cell isolation by FACS plus WGA
facilitates the identi ﬁcation of ultra-low level somatic
mutations and that low-depth WGS helps in the detection
of germline SVs in FCDII that are likely to go unnoticed
with deep whole exome or gene panel sequencing. Among
21 patients with mut-negative FCDII who lacked appar-
ent genetic variations on deep whole exome or gene panel
sequencing of bulk brain tissues, we found that ultra-low
level somatic mutations in the mTOR pathway accounted
for 28.6% (6 of 21) of them and that germline SVs
undetectable with conventional tools accounted for 19%.
Thus, this study estimates that our proposed approach
increases the diagnostic rate by up to an overall 80% of
the entire FCDII cohorts by overcoming the current limits
of detecting ultra-low level somatic mutations (previously
60%).
11,12
Previous studies have shown that either LCM for
dysmorphic or ﬂuorescence-activating nuclei sorting
(FANS) for neuronal or non-neuronal lineage separation
enriches targeted cells from patient brain tissues. 12,34
Although LCM allows for accurate cell separation, it is a
manually performed technique that results in low
throughput. Although FANS allows for high-throughput
isolation, there are a few exceptional cases with high muta-
tion burden in the non-neuronal lineage; some cases have
much higher VAFs in non-neuronal nuclei than neuronal
nuclei.
34 Given that mutation-carrying neural stem cells
can differentiate into neuronal and glial lineage cells dur-
ing brain development, non-neuronal cell types, such as
glial lineage cells, could harbor mutations. A previous
study using FANS analysis reported that non-neuronal
nuclei could have higher VAFs that in some FCD cases.
34
Thus, the highest VAF obtained with pS6 FACS-sorting
compared to LCM suggests that somatic mutations could
be harbored not only in neurons but also in other cell
types. In this study, we showed that FACS-mediated
enrichment of p-S6
+ cells robustly increase the detectable
mutational burden (mean VAF of 5.84%) over that in
bulk brain tissue (mean VAF of 0.17%). In addition, we
ampliﬁed a small amount of genomic DNA extracted from
FACS-enriched p-S6
+ cells to generate a high-quality
NGS library from frozen brain tissues. Technical replica-
tion and bioinformatic ﬁltering allowed us to ﬁlter out
potential errors arising from WGA and to ﬁnd accurate
somatic mutations. By doing so, we were able to identify
ultra-low level somatic mutations in mTOR pathway
genes that were undetectable upon deep sequencing of
bulk brain tissues.

Studies on brain somatic mutations of the mTOR
pathway have reported that mutation-harboring cells are
correlated with the extent of cortical malformation.
34 Dys-
regulation of the mTOR pathway contributes to disrup-
tion of neural circuit formation and alteration of already
established neural circuits. 38 Interestingly, recent evidence
has shown that an extremely small subset of neurons with
aberrant hyperactivation of mTOR kinase (eg, 0.1%) is
sufﬁcient to induce epilepsy via a non-cell autonomous
mechanism.
14,39 Consistent with this ﬁnding, our present
results demonstrated that ultra-low level somatic muta-
tions activating the mTOR pathway are present in
patients with mut-negative FCDII. Although the exact
mutational burden threshold that causes intractable epi-
lepsy in FCDII remains unclear, our study suggests that it
is much lower than the level detectable with deep
sequencing of bulk brain tissues.
The hallmark of FCDII were cortical dyslamination
and dysmorphic neurons without or with balloon cells in
FCDIIa and FCDIIb, respectively. Based on
immunophenotype studies, both dysmorphic neurons and
balloon cells express undifferentiated and differentiated
neural cells, and balloon cells express both markers of glia
and neurons,
40 because the histopathological distinction
between FCDIIa and FCDIIb is problematic in the patho-
logical diagnosis of non-representative or small surgical
specimens.
6 The current question is whether FCDIIa and
FCDIIb subtypes represent distinct entities based on his-
tologic ﬁndings. Genetic analysis of several studies, includ-
ing ours, revealed that the identical mutations in the
mTOR pathway gene cause disease, suggesting that the
2 subtypes represent a spectrum of the same disorder.
10– 12
Although the exact biological basis of FCD II subtypes
remains elusive, our study found somatic variants in more
cases with FCDIIb. Alteration of AKT3 and PIK3CA were
frequently found in HME and mainly identi ﬁed in
patients with FCDIIa (Supplementary Table S4). How-
ever, in the case of the MTOR mutations, they were simi-
larly found in patients with FCDIIa and FCDIIb (see
Supplementary Table S4). Interestingly, VAFs of somatic
mutations in MTOR were slightly higher in patients with
FCDIIb patients than patients with FCDIIa (2.7% vs
5.2%, p value = 0.025). Based on the high VAF and
mixed differentiation state of balloon cells, it can be
inferred that FCDIIb develops earlier than that of
FCDIIa. In order to address this issue, it may be helpful
to identify the cell of origin in FCDII, such as single cell
analysis.
We have several limitations. In our custom target
gene panel, RHEB gene was not included.
12,14 Targeted
gene sequencing is a useful tool for analyzing speci ﬁc
mutations with deep sequencing depth, but due to their
predesigned, made-to-order approaches, we missed
recently updated genes. Dysmorphic neurons are signi ﬁ-
cantly enlarged, ranging from 16 to 43 μm compared than
12 to 25 μm in normal neurons in layer 3.
6 In terms of
technical issues using FACS, 2 diameter nozzles are
available, 80 μm and 100 μm, and in practice, the noz-
zle is clogged if cells larger than 40 μmp a s st h r o u g h .
Subsequently, it is recommended to ﬁlter with a strainer
with a diameter of 40 μm in the last step of preparation.
In that case, it was not possible to collect dysmorphic
neurons of larger size, which was a potential advantage
for collecting via LCM. Deep whole genome sequencing
(>90/C2read depth) of p-S6
+ cells enriched from FCDII
brain tissue is not practical in the clinical setting due to
several reasons. The acquisition of WGS compatible
amount of genomic DNA from FACS sorted cells is
time-consuming and laborious. In addition, the cost
and data burden of bioinformatic analysis of deep WGS
data is still challenging.
Despite the advance that our current approach pro-
vides, a genetic cause is still not discernable in /C2420% of
patients with FCDII. Several mechanisms could account
for these patients. First is that the level of somatic muta-
tions in these patients with FCDII may be too low to be
detected by our current methods. Second may be novel
genes with somatic SNVs or SVs not yet implicated in
FCDII. Considering that a recent study of 44 FCDII and
16 HME cases using deep WES showed that most
detected SNVs were involved in mTOR signaling pathway
genes,
15 we suspect that a larger FCDII cohort might be
necessary to detect novel genes affected in FCDII. There
may be nonspeci ﬁcally upregulated mTOR pathway activ-
ity, in which case non-targeted sequencing of the p-S6 +
cells could reveal novel genes other than mTOR pathway
genes. For somatic SVs, deep WGS should be performed
on samples with high cellular purity because the detection
