# A novel BCAP31 variant associated with nonsyndromic auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder: mitochondrial dysfunction, cisplatin sensitivity, and amenability to mitochondrial transplantation.
**Authors:** Kim Y, Kim Y, Kim BJ, Yu SH, Han JH, Kim M, Yi N, Lee SE, Kim JA, Han K, Kim CH, Kang YC, Choi BY
**Journal:** J Transl Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12967-025-06610-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06610-3)

## Content

BACKGROUND: A novel in-frame insertion variant in the B-Cell Receptor-Associated Protein 31 (BCAP31) gene, which encodes a crucial ER membrane protein involved in the quality control and transport of transmembrane proteins, as well as in ER-mitochondria apoptotic signaling, was determined in a family demonstrating X-linked, recessive, nonsyndromic auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder (ANSD).

METHODS: Exome sequencing analysiswas followed by bioinformatics analysis to identify the cause of hearing loss in a family whose pedigree indicated an X-linked recessive mode of inheritance. Immunohistochemistry was performed to locate Bcap31 in the mouse cochlea. Mitochondrial function was evaluated by measuring intracellular ATP, ROS and mitochondrial membrane potential in control and patient-derived lymphoblastoid cells (LCLs) before and after the administration of mitochondria isolated from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs).

RESULTS: ANSD observed in our study is characterized by initial inner hair cell damage, followed by accelerated degeneration of cochlear outer hair cells. Functional studies of patient-derived LCLs revealed mitochondrial dysfunction, evidenced by increased ROS, reduced ATP levels, and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential compared with normal LCLs. Further, these cells demonstrated heightened sensitivity to cisplatin-induced apoptosis, as indicated by the increased proapoptotic gene expression. Notably, the administration of mitochondria isolated from umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells significantly restored mitochondrial dysfunction and alleviated cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity in the patient-derived cells.

CONCLUSIONS: These results indicate BCAP31 dysfunction as a potential cause of transient ANSD, progressing to sensorineural hearing loss through mitochondrial impairment. Furthermore, they highlighted the therapeutic potential of allogenic mitochondrial transplantation as a novel strategy for treating hearing loss with an underlying component of mitochondrial dysfunction. This study contributes to the understanding of BCAP31's role in auditory neuropathy and mitochondrial health.

The B-Cell Receptor-Associated Protein 31 (BCAP31) gene is located in Xq28 and encodes BCAP31, which is an integral endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane protein. BCAP31 is physiologically involved in ER-associated degradation, ER protein trafficking to the Golgi apparatus, and oxidative phosphorylation complex 1 subunit importation into mitochondria [1]. Further, BCAP31 plays a crucial role in cell death by mediating apoptotic signaling through the cross-talk between the ER and the mitochondria [2]. Hence, a deficiency in BCAP31 could be associated with the pathogenesis of severely impaired syndromic diseases such as dystonia, intellectual/developmental disability, encephalopathy, liver disease, and sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), named deafness, dystonia, and central hypomyelination syndrome (DDCH syndrome, MIM #300475) [3–5].

SNHL is a prevalent auditory disorder caused by histopathological damage in the cochlea. Its causes include mutations either in mitochondrial genes or in nuclear genes that are closely associated with mitochondrial function [6, 7]. Accordingly, mitochondrial abnormalities, including mitochondrial DNA damage, an excess of reactive oxygen species (ROS), an inflammatory activation, and an abnormal communication between mitochondria and ER, have been frequently observed in patients with SNHL [8]. Corticosteroids are the primary treatment for hearing loss, regardless of the etiology, because of their anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects. However, several patients with hearing loss do not adequately recover and even suffer from the severe adverse effects of corticosteroids. Thus, developing novel therapeutic strategies based on an improved understanding of the pathophysiology, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, is a crucial medical requirement to cure or slow disease progression.

A growing body of evidence indicates that foreign mitochondria can be efficiently transferred into any cell type via simple co-incubation in vitro and internalized into tissues through local or systemic injection in vivo [9, 10]. Reportedly, systemically injected mitochondria preferentially migrate to cells and tissues with damaged mitochondria [11]. Micropinocytosis may medicate the migration of mitochondria into the cell or tissue [12]. According to the feasibility of mitochondrial transfer, mitochondrial transplantation therapy has been effective in treating diseases, including ischemia/reperfusion injury, tissue injury, acute/chronic inflammatory disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases [13–15]. Healthy mitochondria transplantation has been proposed to improve mitochondrial defects by either increasing mitochondrial biogenesis or substituting abnormal mitochondria with healthy mitochondria, although the underlying mechanisms of such effects remain unclear. Therefore, mitochondrial transplantation is a potential therapeutic strategy for treating diseases caused by mitochondrial dysfunction.

For the first time in the literature, we report a novel, single amino acid insertion BCAP31 variant related to nonsyndromic deafness, a milder phenotype than previously reported in association with more severe BCAP31 alterations, which include nonsense, frameshift, splice region variants or exon deletion, compatible with severe loss-of-function [16]. We propose mitochondrial dysfunction, as a potential mechanism, in the patient-derived lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs), which was further exacerbated by cisplatin administration. Interestingly, the application of mitochondria isolated from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) mitigated mitochondrial dysfunction and alleviated cisplatin-induced ototoxicity in the patient-derived LCLs. Altogether, our results indicate that BCAP31-associated transient ANSD, which rapidly progressed to full-blown, general SNHL, may be caused by mitochondrial dysfunction and that mitochondrial transplantation therapy is a potential therapeutic option for BCAP31 variant-induced nonsyndromic hearing loss.

This study recruited nine participants from a hearing loss family whose pedigree indicated an X-linked recessive mode of inheritance. The participants underwent comprehensive phenotypic assessments, including medical history interviews, physical examinations, imaging, and audiological evaluations. ANSD can be diagnosed when the ABR is absent or abnormal while OAE or CMs remain normal, indicating intact OHC function [17, 18].

Genomic DNA samples were extracted from the peripheral blood of the study participants via standard procedures, and 11 recurrent variants of five deafness genes were initially screened as previously described [19]. Exome sequencing (3 Billion Inc, Seoul, Korea) was conducted, in which all exon regions of all human genes (~ 22,000) were captured using xGen Exome Research Panel v2 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, Iowa, USA) [20] and the reads were mapped onto the Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 37 (GRCh37) reference genome. Bioinformatics analyses were then conducted to narrow down the candidate variant as previously described [18, 21, 22]. Subsequently, a segregation study was performed. The pathogenic potential of the novel variants was assessed following the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) [23, 24].

The mice (Wild Type, n = 5) were euthanized and decapitated. Both cochleae were extracted and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. Cochlear turns were carefully excised, and whole-mount preparations were established and incubated in blocking/permeabilizing buffer (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] with 5% goat serum and 0.25% Triton X-100). The preparations were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-Bcap31 primary antibodies (NBP1-89357, NOVUS Biologicals, CO, USA) diluted 1:200 in the blocking/permeabilizing buffer. After three washes, the cochlear turns were reacted with fluorescence-labeled antirabbit secondary antibodies diluted in blocking/permeabilizing buffer for 2 h at room temperature. After three washes with PBS, the cochlear turns were reacted with 1:1000 rhodamine phalloidin (R415, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) diluted in blocking/permeabilizing buffer for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were then rinsed three times with PBS. High-resolution images were obtained with a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Human UC-MSCs were obtained by primary culture of the umbilical cord from a healthy pregnant woman with informed consent. UC-MSCs were cultured in minimum essential medium eagle alpha modification (α-MEM; Hyclone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 10 ng/ml basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF-2; CHA Meditech Co, Korea).

The mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) of the succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit C was fused with the GFP gene and dsRED, resulting in MTS-GFP and MTS-dsRED, respectively. Lentiviral expression vectors were constructed by cloning the MTS-dsRED and MTS-GFP genes into a lentiviral vector plasmid with EF1α promoter. The HEK293T cells for lentivirus production were seeded on 10-cm dishes at 70–80% confluency. The cells were then cotransfected with 4-µg lentiviral expression vector plasmid along with 4-µg pLP1, 4-µg pLP2, and 3-µg pLP/VCVG (Packaging Mix, Invitrogen). The transfection mixtures were prepared in 1-mL Opti-MEM containing DNA and 30-µL 293 Tran (Origene, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The lentiviral supernatant samples were collected after 24, 48, and 72 h posttransfection. The collected lentiviral supernatants were concentrated using polyethylene glycol 6000 and suspended in an α-MEM medium. The mitochondria were labeled with GFP and dsRED by seeding UC-MSCs into 150-mm plates at 70–80% density and then incubating them with lentiviral supernatants and 4-µg/mL polybrene, followed by further incubation for 72 h.

For mitochondria isolation, UC-MSCs at passage 7 or HEK293 cells were harvested, depressurized in sucrose-HEPES-EGTA (SHE) buffer (0.25 M of Sucrose, 20 mM of HEPES [pH: 7.4], 2 mM of EGTA, 0.1% defatted bovine serum albumin [BSA]) using nitrogen cavitation (Parr Instrument, USA) [25], and then centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris and nuclei. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to pellet the mitochondria. The pellet was washed twice by suspension in 500-µl SHE buffer followed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The final pellet was resuspended in 100-µl suspending buffer and kept on ice until use. Isolated mitochondria were quantified by identifying protein concentrations using a bicinchoninic acid assay.

LCLs (1 × 105cells/well) were seeded into 24-well plates and mixed with 1 µg of mitochondria that were isolated from UC-MSCs infected with lentivirus expressing MTS-GFP or MTS-dsRED. After 24 h of incubation, images were obtained using a confocal microscope (Fluoview FV3000, Olympus, Japan). Human dermal fibroblasts (5 × 104cells/well) were grown in 24-well plates, infected with lentivirus expressing MTS-GFP, and subsequently incubated with 1 µg of mitochondria isolated from UC-MSCs infected with lentivirus expressing MTS-dsRED. Confocal images were obtained at D0, D1, D3, D5, D7, and D9.

The intracellular ATP concentration was measured via a luciferin–luciferase reaction using CellTiterGlo Luminescent reagent (Promega, USA), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. 5 × 103 of LCLs were seeded into a 96-well white tissue plate and incubated with PN-101 for 24 h. After washing with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline, 100 µl of CellTiter-Glo® reagent (Promega, USA) was added to each well. LCLs were incubated for 10 min at room temperature on an orbital shaker to induce cell lysis. The luminescence signal was measured using a SYNERGY HTX multimode reader (BioTek, USA), and the data were analyzed using Gen5 version 3.09 software. The study was conducted in triplicate to ensure consistent results.

MtMP was measured using fluorescence of the potential-dependent tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE). In brief, cells were incubated with 500 nM of TMRE at 37℃ for 30 min. Cells were collected, suspended in PBS, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX LX).

The cytosol fraction was separated by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃ from cell lysates. Each fraction was boiled in an equal volume of SDS-sample buffer and loaded onto 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels. Samples were transferred to Amersham Protran polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (GE Healthcare, USA). The membranes were then blocked with Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 that contains 5% (w/v) skim milk. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in PBS that contained 0.1% BSA. This study involved a multigenerational Korean family with nonsyndromic hearing loss demonstrating an X-linked recessive pattern of inheritance. Hearing loss was diagnosed in five family members, whose onset was peri- or postlingual and was progressive (Fig. 1A). All affected members used hearing aids from the time of diagnosis.

The proband (SB740-1313) was a four-year-old male who exhibited bilateral symmetric nonsyndromic SNHL with an average hearing threshold of 53 dB HL based on play audiometry and with a 70% speech discrimination score (SDS). He passed the newborn hearing screening at birth. His hearing threshold decreased by 5 dB per year, and his SDS after 3 years of follow-up rapidly deteriorated from 70 to 20% (Fig. 1B). The disproportionately low SDS relative to pure tone thresholds and the absence of auditory brainstem response (ABR) waves despite the presence of cochlear microphonics (CMs) were observed in the SB740-1313 patient (Fig. 1B). SB740-1313 eventually experienced significant deterioration in speech discrimination, with only 30% discrimination for monosyllabic words and 83% for sentence comprehension, requiring cochlear implantation. Four months postoperatively, the patient demonstrated marked improvement, achieving 75% discrimination for monosyllabic words and 100% for sentences (data not shown). Further inquiry into the family’s medical diagnosis revealed that his uncles, including SB740-1336 and SB740-1790, suffered from severe nonsyndromic SNHL, demonstrating an average hearing threshold of 64 dB HL and 60 dB HL based on pure-tone audiometry and with 56% and 52% SDS, respectively. His grandmother, SB740-1338, had a mild-to-moderate asymmetric SNHL with an average hearing threshold of 25 dB HL and a 100% SDS on the right side and 55 dB HL and a 72% SDS on the left side (Fig. 1B). In contrast, his aunt, SB740-1337, exhibited normal hearing with a 100% SDS on both sides. In this pedigree, males demonstrate more severe hearing loss, whereas females tend to show milder symptoms if present, thereby providing a degree of evidence supporting that the causative gene may be located on the X chromosome.

We revealed no neurological or developmental deficits after a thorough examination by a pediatric neurologist and ophthalmologist. Exome sequencing and subsequent analysis of three members (1313, 1336, and 1338) of the family determined the presence of the c.397_398insGAG (p. Asp132_Ala133insGly) variant in the BCAP31 gene in the X chromosome either in a hemizygous (1313 and 1336) or heterozygous state, which segregated with hearing loss in the studied family (Fig. 1C). A member (SB740-1338) with mild hearing loss demonstrated a heterozygous variant in the BCAP31 gene, whereas no mutation was observed in SB740-1337 who had normal hearing. No other possible cause of hearing loss in the patient was detected according to the next-generation sequencing data. Aspartate and alanine at positions 132 and 133 are highly conserved among organisms. GAG insertion at position 398 causes glycine insertion between aspartate and alanine. This variant was predicted to be likely pathogenic, following the guideline for the interpretation of classifying pathogenic variants (PS3_Supp, PM2, PM4, PP1_Mod) (Table 1) [23, 24].

BCAP31 plays a crucial role in the cross-talk between mitochondria and ER; thus, cells with the BCAP31 variant would demonstrate mitochondrial dysfunction. Hence, LCLs were generated by infecting B lymphocytes with Epstein–Barr virus and named C1 and C2 from the two controls and PB-1 and PB-2 from two patients, SB740-1313 and SB740-1790, respectively. We then examined the intracellular ATP and mitochondrial membrane potential (MtMP) in the C1, C2, PB-1, and PB-2 LCLs. Impaired ATP generation could be associated with mitochondrial dysfunction because mitochondria produce ATP via oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [27]. Figure 3A illustrates an approximately 18–20% ATP reduction in PB-1 and PB-2 LCLs when compared to the controls. Further, MtMP is an essential component in electron transport and OXPHOS processes, and therefore, it is related to mitochondrial function. The MtMP levels of LCLs were measured using a fluorescent probe JC-10 assay via flow cytometry. Figure 3B shows that PB-1 and PB-2 LCLs demonstrated approximately a significant 19.4–27.8% MtMP level reduction compared to the controls.

A genetic predisposition has been implicated in the prevalence and severity of ototoxic hearing loss [28]. To investigate the association of the BCAP31 variant with the susceptibility to cisplatin-induced ototoxicity, we assessed the cytotoxicity induced by different cisplatin concentrations in LCLs. The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay indicated that the cytotoxicity caused by cisplatin was significantly higher in mutant LCLs than in the controls. At a cisplatin concentration of 50 mM, PB-1 and PB-2 showed approximately 63.5% cytotoxicity, whereas C1 demonstrated approximately 35.2% cytotoxicity (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3C). Together, these results indicate that BCAP31 alteration may contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction as well as genetic susceptibility to ototoxic side effects.

We then investigated whether mitochondrial transplantation restored mitochondrial dysfunction observed in BCAP31 mutant LCLs. We first tested whether the transfer of mitochondria occurs in LCLs. We isolated green fluorescence protein (GFP)-labeled mitochondria (MTGFP) and red fluorescence protein (dsRED)-labeled mitochondria (MTdsRED) from UC-MSCs infected with lentivirus that express mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS)-GFP and MTS-dsRED, respectively. MTGFP and MTdsRED were used to track the migration of the exogenous mitochondria in LCLs. Isolated MTGFP and MTdsRED were coincubated with LCLs, and confocal microscopy was used to observe their localization (Fig. 4A). The microscopy data indicated that both MTGFP and MTdsRED were successfully transferred into the LCLs by simple co-incubation (Fig. 4B).

We isolated functionally intact mitochondria (referred to as a PN-101) from human UC-MSC using a differential centrifugation protocol [29]. BCAP31 mutant LCLs were treated with PN-101, and intracellular changes in ATP content were evaluated. PN-101 significantly increased the intracellular ATP by 15% and 13% in PB-1 and PB-2, respectively (Fig. 5A). Similar increasing effects of the MtMP level were significantly observed in PB-2 with PN-101 treatment. PB-1 demonstrated increased MtMP with PN-101 treatment although not statistically significant (Fig. 5B). Together, these results indicate that PN-101 can be internalized into LCLs, and it increases the intracellular ATP content and MtMP. We then examined the association of PN-101 treatment with cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity. To investigate the protective effects of PN-101 in a therapeutic window context, LCLs were exposed to cisplatin, followed by PN-101 treatment after 6 h (Fig. 5C). Figure 5D illustrates that PN-101 treatment significantly attenuated the cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity in both PB-1 and PB-2. Afterward, we investigated the expression of the apoptotic marker protein cytochrome C (cyt C) in control and patient-derived LCLs using western blot assay. As expected, the cyt C expression was increased with the cisplatin treatment. PN-101 treatment remarkably alleviated the cisplatin-induced cyt C expression in normal LCLs as well as mutant LCLs (Fig. 5E and F). Together, these data strongly indicate that mitochondrial transplantation improves mitochondrial dysfunction in BCAP31 mutant LCLs and may have an anti-apoptotic effect by attenuating the sensitivity to the ototoxic chemical.

To assess the duration of transplanted mitochondria retention in recipient cells, we investigated the dynamics of PN-101 internalization in time. To make it, human dermal fibroblasts were first infected with lentivirus-mediated MTS-GFP to label the endogenous mitochondria with GFP proteins, and then co-incubated with MTdsRED as shown in Fig. 6A. The representative confocal images in Fig. 6B clearly exhibited uptake of MTdsRED by dermal fibroblasts. Transplanted MTdsRED were evident in the cytosol of the recipient dermal fibroblasts 1 day post-transplantation (white open arrow). The co-localization of dsRED-labeled exogenous mitochondria with GFP-stained endogenous mitochondria was clearly evident at 3, 5 and 7 days post-transplantation (white filled arrow), suggesting that the internalized MTdsRED appeared to undergo fusion dynamics (Fig. 6B). However, transplanted MTdsRED gradually decreased over time and rarely detected 9 days post-translation. Next, we assessed the duration and long-term efficacy of PN-101 by quantifying the total mtDNA amount (Fig. 6C). Following a single administration of PN-101 to fibroblasts, the total mtDNA amount exhibited a considerable rise of 2.6 to 2.8 times at 3 and 5 days post-transplantation, but gradually declined over time, returning to baseline control values (Fig. 6D). In contrast, additional treatment with PN-101 on Day 3 inhibited the reduction in total mtDNA levels seen in fibroblasts that received a single dose of PN-101 (Fig. 6E), indicating that repeated administration of mitochondria may confer sustained efficacy to the transplanted mitochondria.

For the first time in the literature, we report a novel BCAP31 variant associated with nonsyndromic deafness, a milder phenotype than that previously reported in association with BCAP31 alterations [16]. Rinaldi et al. indicated that BCAP31 may be a dosage-sensitive gene and that the current literature associates BCAP31 impairment to a severe neurological phenotype in males with genotype–phenotype correlation based on the deletion size [5, 30]. All the BCAP31 pathogenic variants already ascertained were transmitted by healthy mothers; however, only in one case, the mother had SNHL, possibly due to her carrier status for BCAP31 [4]. Similarly, we identified mild hearing loss in SB740-1338, a female carrier of c.397_398insGAG.

An interesting aspect of this study is the novel observation that the BCAP31 one-amino-acid insertion variant, in contrast to the previously reported phenotypes associated with this gene, is related to a milder form of nonsyndromic hearing loss. A more notable aspect to consider is that the patient carrying this variant (SB740-1313) presented with ANSD. The ANSD phenotype was not evident during the patient’s initial ascertainment; however, it became apparent after a 3-year follow-up. Notably, during ANSD diagnosis, DPOAEs were already absent, with only CMs remaining. This indicates that the ANSD related to this BCAP31 variant may not be present from the onset but rather represents a stage within hearing loss progression.

The observation that ANSD does not persist from the onset but rather manifests as a stage within hearing loss progression is likely more prevalent in postlingual ANSD than in prelingual cases. This indicates that the actual prevalence of postlingual ANSD may be significantly higher than that currently reported [31]. In this study, an examination of BCAP31 expression in the cochlea revealed that BCAP31 is primarily expressed in IHCs but is also significantly present in OHCs. This widespread expression indicates that, unlike ANSD caused by genes that are exclusively expressed in IHCs, BCAP31-related ANSD may cause an early and rapid loss of both otoacoustic emissions (OAEs) and CMs. Consequently, the auditory phenotype may present with characteristics of ANSD only for a limited period, eventually resembling that of conventional severe to profound sensorineural hearing loss with an accelerated OHC decline. This phenotype is further interpreted in the context of the role of BCAP31 in the cross-talk between the mitochondria and ER.

SB740-1313, an affected male of the BCAP31 variant demonstrating mitochondrial dysfunction in the LCL study, not surprisingly presented with an ANSD phenotype. Considering the predominant involvement of IHCs in energy-intensive processes, such as mechanoelectrical coupling and synaptic activity, it is reasonable to expect that mitochondrial dysfunction would have a far greater influence on IHCs compared to OHCs, which bear a significantly lower energy burden because the activity of prestin—the motor protein of cochlear OHC—is not directly dependent on ATP [32–34]. Recent studies have revealed that mitochondrial dysfunction increases susceptibility to oxidative stress and apoptotic pathways in auditory neurons, thereby further contributing to the neural deficits observed in ANSD [35]. However, the presence of BCAP31 in both OHC and IHC potentially indicates ANSD as a transient condition, eventually progressing to advanced SNHL also due to OHC decline. Moreover, the association between mitochondrial dysfunction and ANSD emphasizes the potential for therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring mitochondrial health, such as mitochondrial transfer, as suggested in the present study, to mitigate or partially restore auditory function in affected individuals. Additionally, maintaining the health of spiral ganglion neurons potentially via mitochondrial transfer could further improve and sustain the performance of the cochlear implant over time, even in cases where auditory function is restored through cochlear implantation.

These syndromic features derived from BCAP31 dysfunction were reportedly caused via a mechanism of loss-of-function (LOF) and alterations of BCAP31, associated with the disease ranging between nonsense, frameshift, or splice region variants, mostly causing large deletion, which is compatible with severe LOF mechanism [36, 37]. Further, nonsyndromic hearing loss cases have never been reported. However, theoretically, milder alterations may be associated with a milder phenotype, including nonsyndromic hearing loss, potentially adding a newly identified gene causing nonsyndromic deafness in the X chromosome in the literature.

Mitochondria have their DNA, but nuclear DNA still substantially influences mitochondrial function in several ways. Nuclear modifier genes are well acknowledged to modulate the phenotypic expression of mitochondrial DNA mutations. In detail, a nuclear modifier allele (A10S) in the TRMU (methylaminomethyl-2-thiouridylate-methyltransferase) gene worsens mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby further affecting a deafness phenotype associated with the m.1555 A > G variant [38]. Moreover, many proteins encoded by nuclear genes, such as LARS2, NARS2, and KARS, are transported into the mitochondria, where their variations can also be associated with the deafness mechanism [39–41]. Mitochondrial variants, such as the m.1555 A > G variant in MTRNR1 and the m.3243 A > G variant in MTTL1, are associated with hearing loss in the absence of any mutation in nuclear DNA highlighting that impaired mitochondrial function alone can lead to the development of hearing loss [42]. Further, BCAP31 variants are closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, since they play a crucial role in importing the OXPHOS complex 1 subunits into the mitochondria and mediating the apoptotic signaling through the cross-talk between the ER and the mitochondria [2, 16]. Individuals with hearing loss exhibited congenital BCAP31 deficiency and significantly low OXPHOS complex 1 activity [43]. In parallel with these, our current study demonstrated decreased intracellular ATP and MtMP levels in LCLs with the BCAP31 variant. These reports indicate that mitochondrial abnormalities caused by variations in either mitochondrial DNA- or nuclear DNA-encoding proteins could contribute to hearing impairment. Therefore, restoring mitochondrial dysfunction is considered one of the best options for treating this kind of hearing loss.

According to the evidence that mitochondria are efficiently taken up by various cells via micropinocytosis, the transplantation of mitochondria that were isolated from healthy cells or tissues has appeared as an attractive therapeutic option for treating mitochondrial abnormalities [13, 14, 44, 45]. The systemic and local injection of syngeneic, allogeneic, or xenogeneic mitochondria has been effective in treating various diseases caused by mitochondrial dysfunction [46–52]. The mechanism underlying the beneficial effects of mitochondrial transplantation remains unclear; however, the transplantation of healthy mitochondria has been indicated to mitigate mitochondrial deficits and restore cellular function by replacing damaged mitochondria with healthy mitochondria and increasing the bioenergetic function [13, 14, 44, 45]. In line with this, the current study revealed that the transplantation of mitochondria that were isolated from human stem cells rescued the defect of bioenergetic production and mitochondrial membrane potential, which were observed in BCAP31 mutant cells, thereby indicating the potential role of mitochondrial transplantation as a novel therapeutic strategy for hearing impairment.

Moreover, in our current study, we demonstrated that cells derived from patients with hearing impairment with a BCAP31 variant exhibited not only impaired mitochondrial function but also significantly heightened sensitivity to cisplatin-induced ototoxicity. These results reveal the potential vulnerability of BCAP31-mutant cells to oxidative stress and indicate a mechanistic association between mitochondrial dysfunction and increased susceptibility to ototoxic drugs.

Notably, when mitochondrial transplantation was applied to BCAP31-mutant, patient-derived cells, a marked reduction was observed in cisplatin cytotoxicity compared with untreated BCAP31-mutant cells, although cisplatin sensitivity in these cells remained comparable or somewhat increased relative to control cells that also received cisplatin and mitochondrial transplantation. These results reveal that mitochondrial transplantation may partially rescue the heightened sensitivity to cisplatin observed in BCAP31-mutant cells by stabilizing mitochondrial function and possibly improving cellular resilience to drug-induced stress. Such results provide valuable information into the therapeutic potential of mitochondrial transplantation for mitigating drug sensitivity in disorders associated with mitochondrial deficits, emphasizing an avenue for further exploration in treatment strategies for cisplatin sensitivity.

Given that the replaced mitochondria are functional, it is crucial to determine how long they remain active at the target cells, tissues, or organs. We tracked the long-term fate of the transplanted mitochondria using confocal microscopy and the total mtDNA quatity. We found a decline in transplanted mitochondria as the culture time increased. This observation corroborates a previous finding by Pour et al. [53], in which they observed a decrease in mitochondrial function with increasing of the passage. There are several reports on the fate of transplanted mitochondria in receipient cells. Macrophages and endothelial cells degrade the transplanted mitochondria within a few days [54, 55]. The signal from transplanted mitochondria can be detected in recipient neurons for up to 12 weeks after a peptide-mediated mitochondrial transplantation and repetitive mitochondrial injections showed noticeably better mitoprotection compared with the single injection therapy [52, 56]. GFP-labeled transplanted mitochondria remain detectable in Purkinije neurons for up to 4 weeks [57]. Transplanted mitochondria can be detected for up to 28 days post-transplantation in mice with ischemia-reperfusion injury [47]. Our current data demonstrate that the transplanted mitochondria isolated from UC-MSCs persist in the dermal fibroblasts for up to 7 days post-transplantation. These discrepancies in the fate of transplanted mitochondria may be contingent on the recipient cell type and cellular source of the transplanted mitochondria. Accordingly, understanding the fate of transplanted mitochondria would be important for determining the therapeutic benefits amd mode of action.

Hearing loss due to various genetic variants is currently being investigated through gene replacement methods targeting specific genes or variant-specific editing technologies [58–61]. Mitochondrial transplantation, however, has the potential to be broadly applicable to hearing loss caused by variants in numerous hearing loss genes that cause mitochondrial dysfunction. In this study, we demonstrated that mitochondrial dysfunction occurs in patient-derived LCLs harboring BCAP31 variants and that this dysfunction can be rescued through mitochondrial transplantation. However, we did not directly assess changes in auditory function. Indeed, further research is warranted to understand the in vivo function and longevity of these transplanted mitochondria, especially with repeated transplantation procedures, and for changes in auditory function over an extended follow-up period.

We discovered a novel nonsyndromic ANSD gene, BCAP31, which is notable for potentially causing temporary ANSD that manifests the distinctive audiological phenotype only during a specific period due to an accelerated OHC decline and loss of OAE, in contrast to previously established ANSD genes. Using patient-derived cells, we revealed that the BCAP31 variant markedly decreases mitochondrial function and increases sensitivity to cisplatin ototoxicity. Most importantly, our experiments on patient-derived cells revealed that the transplantation of mitochondria that were isolated from UC-MSCs not only rescued mitochondrial dysfunction but also alleviated cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity in BCAP31 mutant patient-derived LCLs, thereby providing further insights into mitochondrial transplantation as a potentially promising strategy for hearing loss.
