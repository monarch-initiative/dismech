Esophageal cancer (EC), encompassing primarily esophageal squamous-cell 
carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), continues to pose a 
significant global health burden, which is largely due to delayed diagnosis and 
limited therapeutic efficacy. In this review, we synthesize current insights 
into key aspects of EC, including established risk factors, the stepwise process 
of tumorigenesis, strategies for early detection and prevention, and emerging 
therapeutic approaches. Emphasis is particularly placed on the molecular 
underpinnings of (1) early tumor initiation and malignant progression, with a 
focus on genetic mutations, epigenetic modifications, and alterations within the 
tumor microenvironment; (2) the clonal expansion and metabolic reprogramming 
that play essential roles in shaping the evolutionary trajectory of esophageal 
cancer, thereby promoting intratumoral heterogeneity; (3) the transition from 
normal epithelium to invasive carcinoma in both ESCC and EAC driven by 
sequential disruptions of molecular networks governing epithelial homeostasis; 
and (4) advances in early screening techniques and minimally invasive 
interventions that may offer new opportunities for EC prevention and early 
detection, especially in high-risk populations. In addition, we summarize recent 
progress in precision treatment, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, 
immunotherapy, and multidisciplinary treatment. By delineating the molecular and 
cellular mechanisms underlying EC initiation and progression, this review aims 
to inform future directions in early diagnosis, risk stratification, and 
individualized therapy, ultimately contributing to improved clinical outcomes 
and precision prevention of EC.

INTRODUCTION
Esophageal cancer (EC) remains one of the most lethal digestive
tract malignancies and is the seventh leading cause of cancer-
related deaths worldwide.
1 The epidemiology, mortality, and
histopathological characteristics of EC demonstrate signi ﬁcant
geographical and ethnic variations. Accordingly, EC was respon-
sible for approximately 511,000 new cancer cases and 445,000
deaths globally in 2022.
1 The disease exhibits signi ﬁcant sex-based
and regional disparities, with male incidence and mortality rates
being approximately 2 to 3 times higher than those in females. 1
EC is mainly categorized into two histological subtypes,
squamous-cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma (AC), which
differ substantially in their geographical distribution, etiology, and
pathogenesis. SCC predominantly affects the middle and upper
parts of the esophagus, with the highest incidence in Iran, Japan,
Korea, and China. In contrast, EAC typically develops in the lower
part of the esophagus and is the predominant subtype of EC in
Europe and the United States.
2,3
China faces the world ’s largest EC burden. It was estimated that
there were 224,000 new cases and 187,500 deaths from EC in
China in 2022, with age-standardized incidence and mortality
rates of 8.32 and 6.68 per 100,000, respectively.
4 EC ranks as the
ﬁfth leading cause of cancer death in China. 4 ESCC remains the
predominant histological subtype, accounting for more than 80%
of EC cases in the Chinese population. The prognosis of both ESCC
and EAC patients is poor, with 5-year survival rates ranging from
only 10 to 30%, largely due to delayed diagnosis and therapeutic
ineffectiveness. Owing to the insidious onset of precancerous or
early-stage EC, patients do not exhibit obvious symptoms,
resulting in a delayed diagnosis, often at an advanced stage.
Together with limited treatment options and high recurrence and
metastasis rates, these factors contribute to the persistently low
5-year survival rate.
5

certain environmental exposures. ESCC often arises from chronic

Recent multi-omics studies have revealed key molecular
differences and overlap between ESCC and EAC. 13–15 While TP53
mutations are frequent in both types of tumors, EAC has a high
prevalence of early ampli ﬁcation of the oncogenes ERBB2, CCNE1,
and KRAS.16 Epigenetic mechanisms, including promoter hyper-
methylation of tumor suppressor genes such as CDKN2A and APC,
are also crucial, particularly in early EAC development. 17,18 In ESCC,
mutations in TP53, NOTCH1, NFE2L2 , and KMT2D, as well as
widespread epigenomic and transcriptomic reprogramming, are
prominent features.
19
Screening, early diagnosis, and timely treatment of EC are
essential for reducing morbidity and mortality while improving
prognosis and survival outcomes. Early detection and intervention
signiﬁcantly improve patient outcomes, particularly at high-grade
precancerous lesions. When detected early, most ECs and their
precancerous lesions can be removed endoscopically and mini-
mally invasively, enabling patients to receive radical treatment
and achieve a clinical cure, with a 5-year survival rate of up to
95%.
20
Contemporary management of EC encompasses various ther-
apeutic modalities, with surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy
forming the cornerstone of treatment.
21,22 Both ESCC and EAC are
currently treated with multimodal approaches, and the develop-

EPIDEMIOLOGY
Disease burden
EC is the seventh most common cause of cancer-related mortality
globally, as reported in the 2022 Global Cancer Statistics.
1,26,27
Five-year survival rates for EC remain poor in most countries,
typically ranging from 10 to 30% after diagnosis. 28 There are
obvious sex differences in the incidence and mortality of EC, with
males having nearly 3-fold higher incidence and mortality rates
than females (age-standardized incidence rates of 7.6 vs 2.6 per
100,000, and mortality rates of 6.5 vs 2.2 per 100,000,
respectively).
1
Signiﬁcant demographic disparities in the incidence of EC have
been observed. Asia has the heaviest cancer burden due to its
high population density, accounting for 59.2% of the total
incidents in the world. Speci ﬁcally, China alone accounts for
53.7%. Western Africa and Central America exhibit the lowest
burdens.
29–32 Based on Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End
Results data from the United States, the incidence of ESCC has
continued to decline, while EAC has plateaued after a period of
increase.33
Marked regional variation exists between EAC and ESCC. EAC
shows the highest incidence rates in Northern Europe and North
America, while ESCC is most prevalent in Eastern and South-
Central Asia and South Africa. 29,32 Among them, ESCC is the most
common histological type in China, accounting for 85.8% of all
cases, followed by EAC (11.0%) and others (3.2%).
5,34 The age-
standardized incidence and mortality rates were approximately
3-fold higher in males than in females, and nearly double in rural
regions compared with urban areas. Among the seven adminis-
trative divisions, Central China exhibited the highest rates, with
East and Southwest China following closely behind. In recent
years, due to improvements in living standards and increased
awareness of cancer prevention and control, the incidence and
mortality rates of EC in China have shown a declining trend from
2000 to 2016, with the average annual percent changes of −4.3%
(95% CI = −4.7 to −3.9%) and −3.8% (95% CI = −6.0 to −1.4%),
respectively.
5
Predisposition and susceptibility of EC
Advances in DNA sequencing technology and genome-wide
association studies (GWAS) have greatly facilitated the identi ﬁca-
tion of susceptibility genes or risk loci for EC development. These
ﬁndings provide a foundation for understanding the etiology of
EC and open possibilities for early prevention and intervention.
Early studies used candidate gene approaches to identify EC
susceptibility loci. Genetic variants in the metabolic enzyme
cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) may play a role in mediating suscept-
ibility to ESCC.
35 Moreover, the COX pathway has also been
implicated in the risk of developing BE, the precursor lesion to
EAC. Speci ﬁcally, 3 genetic variants of MGST1, a gene involved in
the COX pathway, have been associated with an increased risk of
both BE and EAC. Other genetic risk loci for EC have also been
documented. The GSTP1 polymorphic variant (GSTP1b) has been
reported to be signi ﬁcantly enriched in BE (70% versus 41%,
P < 0.001) and EAC (76% versus 41%, P = 0.005), which was
correlated with reduced GST enzyme activity. The 461G>A
mutation in the S100A14 locus is associated with susceptibility
to ESCC among smokers, with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.01 (95%
CI = 1.50–2.69).
36 TP53 polymorphisms have been shown to be
genetic determinants of the development of ESCC. An increased
risk of ESCC has been associated with the TP53 codon 72Arg>Pro
(OR = 1.83, 95% CI = 1.43–2.35, P < 0.001).37 The 49G>A poly-
morphism in the CTLA-4 leading sequence, which causes an Ala-
to-Thr substitution, has been associated with increased suscept-
ibility to multiple cancers, including lung, breast, esophageal, and
gastric cardia cancers. 38 A six-nucleotide deletion in the CASP8
promoter has been associated with decreased susceptibility to
EC.
39
GWAS approach plays an important role in identifying cancer
susceptibility loci, especially single-nucleotide polymorphisms
(SNPs). Through large-scale GWAS in multiple cohorts from ﬁve
regions in China, susceptibility loci of ESCC on chromosomes
3q27, 4q23, 5q11, 6p21, 10q23, 12q24, 16q12.1, 17p13, 17q21,
18p11, 21q22, and 22q12 have been identi ﬁed.
40 The associated
genes include RPL6, PTPN11, PDE4D, RUNX1, and UNC5CL, all of
which are cancer-related genes. Variants in SLC39A6 have been
associated with reduced survival time in patients with ESCC. 41
Speciﬁcally, rs7242481, located in the 5 ′ untranslated region (UTR)
of SLC39A6, disturbs a transcriptional repressor binding site and
results in the upregulation of SLC39A6 expression. Moreover,
SLC39A6 promotes the proliferation and invasion of ESCC cells. The
rs2274223 SNP in PLCE1 has also been reported to be a genetic
risk factor for ESCC development. 42 Its expression in ESCC is
signiﬁcantly greater than that in normal esophageal tissues. 42
Pooled analysis of three GWASs of ESCC was subsequently
conducted, and 3 new risk loci were found and achieved genome-
wide signi ﬁcance: rs7447927 in TMEM173, rs1642764 in ATP1B2,
and rs35597309 in the HLA class II region. 43 Six susceptibility loci
in CCHCR1, TCN2, TNXB, CYP26B1, and FASN have been found in
ESCC from an exome-wide interrogation of large Chinese
population cohorts. Further analysis demonstrated that the
CYP26B1 variant has an increased ability to catabolize serum all-
trans retinoic acid, an anticancer nutrient, which may contribute to
increased susceptibility to ESCC.
44 Reportedly, susceptible loci for
EAC are also implicated in ESCC development. The G allele of
rs11789015 at 9q22 has been shown to be associated with a
reduced risk of both EAC and ESCC, likely by downregulating
BARX1, a gene often overexpressed in ESCC that promotes tumor
invasion.
45 The missense mutation of the RHBDF2 gene leads to
the occurrence of tylosis ( hyperkeratosis palmaris et plantaris ), a
condition that confers a lifelong high risk for developing ESCC. It
has been reported that in families with tylosis, the lifetime risk of
developing ESCC by the age of 65 is as high as 95%. 46 Exome
sequencing of nonsense mutations in DNAH9 and missense
mutations in GKAP1, BAG1, NFX1, FUK , and DDOST has suggested
that these mutations are related to the occurrence of ESCC. 47

MSRA (rs17749155, P = 5.2 × 10⁻¹⁰), TPPP and CEP72 (rs9918259,
P = 3.2 × 10⁻⁹), TMOD1 (rs7852462, P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁸), FMNL2

Environmental carcinogens and lifestyle risk factors for EC
Currently, risk factors for EC have been widely identi ﬁed, including
lifestyle and dietary habits, certain environmental exposures,
nutritional de ﬁciencies, and socioeconomic status.
66–70 Based on
current evidence, certain factors, such as tobacco smoking, have
been identi ﬁed as common risk factors for different subtypes of
EC.70,71 However, other factors are subtype-speci ﬁc. Speci ﬁcally,
gastroesophageal re ﬂux, obesity, and tobacco use are indepen-
dent risk factors for EAC. 72,73 Obesity and GERD together
contribute to 70.3% of EAC cases (95% CI = 64.4–76.2%).74
Obesity, de ﬁned by increasing body mass index (BMI), is
signiﬁcantly associated with a heightened risk of GERD symptoms
and EAC. A pooled analysis revealed a graded association between
BMI and EAC risk. The adjusted ORs are 1.52 (95% CI = 1.15–2.01)
for individuals with a BMI of 25 –30 kg/m² and 2.78 (95%
CI = 1.85–4.16) for individuals with a BMI greater than 30 kg/m²
compared with those with a normal weight. 75 In addition, obesity
in early life is associated with the development of EAC and
gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma. 11,76 Studies using
genetically engineered mouse models have shown that a high-
fat diet accelerates EAC progression through gut microbiota-
dependent inﬂammation and stem cell expansion, independent of
body weight. 77 Research has shown that the Mediterranean diet
and a healthy dietary pattern are negatively correlated with EC risk
in humans. 78
In addition to these general and lifestyle-related risk factors, BE,
deﬁned as intestinal metaplasia in the distal esophagus, is a
complication of GERD and a precursor lesion in most cases of EAC.
Patients with BE have an approximately 3 –5% risk for developing
EAC in their lifetime. 79 However, the risk of developing EAC from
BE varies among individuals. Longer Barrett ’s segments, larger
hiatal hernias, and severe acid re ﬂux contribute to progression to
EAC, with white males being more susceptible. 80 Several
characteristics have been associated with higher rates of
progression from BE to EAC. A systematic review and meta-
analysis of 20 cohort studies consisting of 74,943 patients with BE
reported that older age (OR = 1.027, 95% CI = 1.007–1.046), male
sex (OR = 2.16, 95% CI = 1.84–2.53, versus female sex), history of
smoking (OR = 1.47, 95% CI = 1.09–1.98, versus never smoking),
and low-grade dysplasia (OR = 4.25, 95% CI = 2.58–7.00) were
associated with an increased risk of progression to EAC.
81
Hormones may in ﬂuence EAC risk. In men, higher circulating
testosterone levels are inversely associated with the risk of EAC,
with those in the highest quartile showing a 56% reduced risk
compared with those in the lowest quartile after adjusting for BMI
(OR = 0.44, 95% CI = 0.22–0.88, P = 0.02).
82 These ﬁndings high-

Additionally, further research is needed to optimize and validate
tools used for screening BE and EAC.

CLINICAL AND PATHOLOGICAL FEATURES
Clinical features
Typical clinical manifestations of EC include progressive dyspha-
gia, choking, and sensations of burning or a foreign body.
2,21
Patients may feel pain behind the sternum, regurgitation of food
or sour liquid, and heartburn. Patients may initially have dif ﬁculty
consuming solid foods, which gradually progresses to the need for
semiliquid or liquid diets. Other symptoms include fever, chest
tightness, shortness of breath, vomiting, melena, hoarseness, or
coughing while drinking water. In advanced stages, patients often
present with weight loss, fatigue, and generalized physical
weakness.
104 Early EC usually lacks speci ﬁc signs.105 As the disease
progresses into the intermediate and advanced stages, various
signs may appear due to the invasion of adjacent organs. For
instance, enlarged cervical or supraclavicular lymph nodes may
suggest lymphatic metastasis; jaundice, hepatomegaly, or liver
tenderness may indicate hepatic involvement; and reduced chest
wall movement, shallow, rapid breathing, and tracheal deviation
toward the unaffected side may be indicative of malignant pleural
effusion.
106
Pathological features
The latest WHO histological classi ﬁcation of esophageal malig-
nancies includes ﬁve categories: adenocarcinoma, adenoid cystic
carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma,
and neuroendocrine neoplasms.
106 SCC and AC are the two most
common types of EC. SCC is most commonly located in the middle
third of the esophagus, followed by the lower third. 107 In a large
Japanese patient cohort, more than half (55%) of SCCs occurred in
the middle esophagus, 32% in the lower esophagus, and 14% in
the upper esophagus.
108 The grading of ESCC is based on the
degree of cytological atypia, mitotic activity, and presence of
keratinization.
109 Grade 1 tumors are well differentiated and
feature enlarged cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and keratin
pearl formation, minimal atypia, and low mitotic activity. Grade 2
tumors are moderately differentiated, with increased atypia,
disorganized architecture, more frequent mitoses, and occasional
partial hardening of surface cells. Grade 3 tumors are poorly
differentiated, consisting mainly of basal-like cells forming
irregular nests, often with central necrosis and rare keratinization.
Moreover, SCC can also be classi ﬁed into three histologic subtypes
on the basis of growth patterns and cytologic features: verrucous,
spindle cell, and basaloid SCC.
106 Among these subtypes, basaloid
SCC accounts for approximately 5% of all EC cases, 106 showing a
solid or nested growth pattern of basaloid cells and a marked
male predominance.110 Currently, no molecular testing is routinely
required to support the diagnosis of ESCC.
The second most common pathological type is AC. AC can
exhibit gastric, intestinal, or mixed differentiation, re ﬂecting its
distinct pathogenesis. Under chronic exposure to acid and bile
reﬂux, stem or progenitor cells located at the basal layer of the
gastroesophageal junction epithelium undergo reprogramming
toward a columnar epithelial phenotype.
111 This metaplastic
epithelium may subsequently undergo either gastric or intestinal
differentiation.112 The presence of goblet cells characterizes
intestinal-type metaplasia, which de ﬁnes BE. 113 The grading of
AC is determined by the proportion of glandular structures within
the tumor: tumors with more than 95% well-formed glandular
structures are classi ﬁed as grade 1, those with less than 50% are
classiﬁed as grade 3, and cases falling between these thresholds
are classi ﬁed as grade 2. Immunohistochemical staining can
support the diagnosis, typically showing positivity for CK7 and
CK19, and negativity for CK20.
114
Other uncommon histological types include adenoid cystic
carcinoma, neuroendocrine tumors, and neuroendocrine carcino-
mas. Adenoid cystic carcinoma of the esophagus is a rare
malignant tumor composed of epithelial and myoepithelial cells
that are typically arranged in gland-like or tubular structures.
115 It
constitutes only approximately 0.1% of all EC cases. 116 Neuroen-
docrine neoplasms are rare epithelial tumors characterized by
neuroendocrine differentiation and are exceedingly rare, account-
ing for only 0.04 –0.1% of all gastroenteropancreatic neuroendo-
crine neoplasms.
117

mutations, including TP53, NOTCH1, MLL2, FAT1, NFE2L2, PIK3CA,

Epigenomic and epitranscriptomic alterations in EC
Epigenetic regulation refers to mechanisms that control gene
expression without altering the DNA sequence. In addition to
genomic alterations, the inclusion of epigenomic and epitran-
scriptomic mechanisms greatly enhances the complexity and
ﬂexibility of biological trait regulation. Numerous studies have
identiﬁed various epigenomic and epitranscriptomic alterations in
esophageal carcinogenesis,
163–165 including alterations in the
expression of epigenetic-related enzymes, changes in noncoding
RNA expression, DNA methylation changes leading to gene
silencing or activation, and RNA modi ﬁcations such as adenosine
N6-methylation, which affect RNA stability, all of which contribute
to EC progression. 166–168
From a macroscopic perspective, several studies of EC have
revealed extensive genome-wide hypomethylation changes in
cancer tissues compared with normal tissues.
169–171 Hypomethyla-
tion in gene promoters and gene bodies is often associated with
increased expression, and speci ﬁc methylation patterns have
demonstrated diagnostic and prognostic value. 172,173 Epigenetic
silencing through promoter hypermethylation represents a pivotal
mechanism for tumor suppressor gene inactivation during the
neoplastic progression of BE to EAC. 17,18 One of the most affected
genes is CDKN2A, whose promoter region is frequently hyper-
methylated at the early stages of BE progression. In a longitudinal
study of BE patients, CDKN2A promoter hypermethylation was
detected in nearly all individuals who developed dysplasia but
was absent in those who remained nondysplastic, suggesting that
this epigenetic alteration precedes and potentially facilitates
neoplastic transformation.
174 Similarly, another study reported
that 82% of EAC tumors and 30% of premalignant lesions,
including intestinal metaplasia, harbored CDKN2A promoter
hypermethylation, with a strong correlation between methylation
and loss of p16 protein expression.
175 Interestingly, LOH at the
CDKN2A locus was also observed but was not signi ﬁcantly
associated with loss of protein expression, indicating that
methylation is the predominant silencing mechanism. 175
The tumor suppressor gene APC on chromosome 5q21 is also
frequently epigenetically altered in EAC. Although truncating
mutations of APC are rare in EAC, hypermethylation of the APC
promoter is observed in up to 92% of ACs, compared with only
50% in ESCC and none in normal tissue.
176 Recent studies have
validated the clinical utility of epigenetic biomarkers for BE risk
stratiﬁcation. The methylation-based EsopPredict assay demon-
strates high sensitivity (81 –100%) in predicting EAC progression
via spatiotemporal biopsy analysis, effectively addressing surveil-
lance challenges such as tissue heterogeneity.
177 Multicenter
validation con ﬁrmed robust risk strati ﬁcation, with high-risk
patients having a 15.2-fold greater 5-year progression risk than
low-risk individuals did, supporting personalized BE manage-
ment.
178 Moreover, the level of hypermethylated APC DNA in
plasma has been associated with poor survival in EAC patients,
suggesting its utility as a noninvasive prognostic biomarker. 176
However, further studies are needed to establish the clinical
reliability of this biomarker.

genes encoding epigenetic modi ﬁcation proteins, and the other is
the stimulation from environmental signals.

epigenetic dysregulation in driving ESCC formation.

supporting the expression of numerous ESCC-related genes.

The origin of EC cells
The esophageal epithelium consists of nonkeratinized squamous
epithelia containing the basal layer, the suprabasal layer, and the
differentiated layer.
191 Through division, differentiation, and self-
renewal, basal layer cells maintain tissue homeostasis and have
been hypothesized to be the origin of ESCC cells. 192 Basal cells
endure long-term mutation accumulation and clonal selection
within histopathologically normal tissue, driven by sophisticated
interactions between genetic factors and the microenvironment,
which might ultimately generate invasive cancers.
192
Driven by the demand for cancer prevention and early
diagnosis, studies concerning markers of basal cells and the
molecular mechanisms involved in transformation are increasing.
Various markers have been proposed to identify and track basal
cells, most likely those basal stem cells considered to be the origin
of ESCC. Liu et al. reported that the interaction between SOX2 and
STAT3 activation in basal layer cells facilitates malignant
transformation, whereas SOX2-negative nonbasal layer cells are
not subject to this signaling-induced transformation.
193 Giroux
et al. reported KRT15 + long-lived basal layer cells in mice and
suggested KRT15 as a marker for basal layer stem cells. 194 Yu et al.
identiﬁed two distinct esophageal epithelial cell populations:
p63⁺SOX2⁺ cells, which are responsible for maintaining stemness
and proliferation, and p63 −SOX2⁺ cells, which are involved in
differentiation and damage repair; notably, the latter population is
absent in ESCC-derived organoids. 195 Three-dimensional organoid
culture of ESCC tissues may help identify their original cells. 196
SOX2/WNT/BMP signaling promotes self-renewal of esophageal
basal layer cells, and KRT15 ⁺ long-lived basal cells in mice are
considered putative esophageal epithelial stem cells and potential
cells of origin for ESCC.
197 With advances in methodology, more
cancer stem cell markers have been proposed, which might be
useful for further de ﬁning the origin of ESCC. 198–205
Through mutations or epigenetic alterations, basal layer cells
acquire abnormal expression of cancer-related genes to complete
malignant transformation. Many studies have highlighted chronic
inﬂammation as a signi ﬁcant promoter of cancer, creating an
inductive environment conducive to cancer cell transformation
and laying the foundation for cancer development through
interactions with precancerous epithelial cells.
206–208 For example,
Klf4 overexpression leads to an imbalanced cell fate with increased
renewal and proliferation due to basal layer in ﬂammation, driving
EC in mice.209 CTNND1 deletion-induced chronic inﬂammation and
immune in ﬁltration promote cancer transformation. 210 The inter-
action between SOX2 and STAT3 in an in ﬂammatory environment
promotes cancer transformation. 193 Using immunohistochemical
analysis of ESCC tissues, Ye et al. reported CK14 +CD98hc+CK6-Ki67-
in basal cells, CK14 -CD98hc−CK6+Ki67+ in proliferating cells, and
CK14+CD98hc+CK6+Ki67+ in both basal and proliferating cells,
suggesting that CD98hc serves as a basal-layer cell marker
associated with chronic in ﬂammation. Its ectopic expression
disrupts the connection between basal layer cells and the
basement membrane, damaging the integrity between the
stromal layer and the epithelium and thereby inducing carcino-
genesis.
211 Moreover, TP53 mutation, the most prominent
genomic mutation in ESCC, has been shown to induce aberrant
interactions among basal layers in early precancerous lesions.
Mechanistically, TP53 mutation caused the overexpression of TP63,
which transcriptionally upregulated EFNB1 and EPHB4 in epithelial
cells and strengthened their interaction, further triggering EMT
and the cell cycle via downstream SRC/ERK/AKT signaling.
212
Carcinogen exposure also triggers chronic in ﬂammation and
promotes carcinogenesis. 213,214
At the cellular level, BE contains a distinct stem cell population
that drives regenerative growth and may serve as a cellular origin
for malignant transformation.
215 Lineage-tracing studies have
revealed that EAC can alternatively arise from nongoblet columnar
epithelium, expanding our understanding of BE-related carcino-
genesis.
216 When isolated and transformed in vitro, BE-derived
stem cells exhibit a wide range of genomic alterations and long-
term self-renewal capabilities and are deemed the origin cells of
EAC.217 Importantly, Lgr5 + stem cells within esophageal glands
have been functionally implicated in EAC development, indicating
the existence of multiple cellular reservoirs for tumor initiation. 218
The cancer development process also involves immune escape
and immunosuppression of cancer cells, which serve as a
signiﬁcant force in shaping cancer characteristics.
131,219 On this
basis, we believe that precancerous cells are progenitor cells that
accumulate genome damage during replication, fail to achieve
normal differentiation, and enter a state characterized by
abnormal, mixed differentiation and stemness features. The
abnormal regulatory spectrum of esophageal-speci ﬁc transcrip-
tion factors characterizes a precancerous epithelial state that
precedes the emergence of overt cancer cells.
220 This model
provides a foundation for further revealing early changes
determined by the internal fate of the epithelium during
carcinogenesis. Pan et al. validated this phenomenon through
single-cell transcriptional analysis and described the phenomenon
of “confused epithelial cell identity ” as an early epithelial cancer
transformation signal.

brush cytology specimens revealed that genetic diversity predicts
progression but could remain in stable dynamic equilibrium over
time. Interestingly, clonal diversity indices adapted from ecology
and evolutionary biology could predict progression from BE to
EAC, even after controlling for established genetic risk factors such
as TP53 mutations and aneuploidy, which underscores the
possibility that the accumulation of diverse clones, rather than
genetic homogenization, may be a key principle of EAC neoplastic
evolution.
237
Evolutionary analysis at the crypt and biopsy level adds another
dimension to our understanding of BE evolution. In a study
involving extensive sampling from BE patients with and without
progression over 6.1 –7.6 years of surveillance, BE segments were
shown to be largely clonal.

mucosal areas before the acquisition of aneuploidy or cancer. 240

as LOH at multiple chromosomal loci, including 5q, but not in a
ﬁxed order, underscoring the complexity of EAC pathogenesis.
240

for risk strati ﬁcation and early intervention. However, the lack of
longitudinal sampling in most studies remains a key obstacle to
accurately modeling individual progression trajectories. 241
Metabolic reprogramming of EC
Metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a hallmark of cancer. 242
ESCC exhibits widespread metabolic aberrations that contribute to
its pathogenesis. 150,243 ESCC cells display glycolytic aberrations
characterized by increased TIGAR expression, which reprograms
glucose metabolism through AMPK. 244 Penﬂuridol, which targets
AMPK/FOXO3a/BIM, can inhibit glycolysis and suppress tumor
progression.
245 Abnormalities in lipid metabolism have also been
observed in ECs. 246,247 Exome-wide association analysis revealed
that genetic variants in the fatty acid biosynthesis enzyme FASN
are associated with an increased risk of ESCC. 44 Upregulation of
CPT1A, a key enzyme in fatty acid oxidation, protects ESCC cells
from apoptosis by maintaining redox homeostasis. 248 Amino acid
metabolic abnormalities, such as the overexpression of tryptophan
2,3-dioxygenase 2, upregulate IL-8 and guide M2 macrophage
polarization.
249 Aberrations in alanine, aspartate, and glutamate
metabolism throughout the course of ESCC evolution have been
shown to further contribute to tumor progression. 250 Targeting
these pathways may improve patient prognosis.
In EAC, aberrant arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism has been
suggested to play an important role in tumor development. For
example, COX2 is overexpressed in human EAC, and its high
expression level is associated with a poor clinical prognosis. 251
Cox2 is also overexpressed during rat esophageal adenocarcino-
genesis.252 Targeting COX2 with speci ﬁc inhibitors can induce
apoptosis in human EAC cell lines and primary esophageal
epithelial cells from biopsy samples, suggesting its potential
antitumor ef ﬁcacy.
253 The 5-lipoxygenase (5-Lox) pathway also
plays a key role in esophageal carcinogenesis by generating AA
metabolites and activating in ﬂammatory cells.
254 Inhibiting the
5-Lox and COX2 pathways with zileuton and celecoxib signi ﬁ-
cantly reduces EAC incidence, supporting their potential as
chemopreventive targets. 255 When the AA pathway is dysregu-
lated, the EAC also exhibits reprogramming of de novo lipogen-
esis, which leads to phospholipidome alterations.
256 This process
is driven by the overexpression of ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY), a key
enzyme that provides acetyl-CoA building blocks for new fatty
acid synthesis. The increased production of these fatty acids
results in a remodeled cancer cell membrane characterized by an
enrichment of phospholipids with longer and more polyunsatu-
rated acyl chains. Silencing ACLY in EAC cells was shown to reverse
these structural changes, directly linking this enzyme to the
altered phospholipid pro ﬁle of EAC.
256
Metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells often results from
mutations in oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, or genes
encoding metabolic enzymes themselves.
257 Lipidomic pro ﬁling
of transformed gastroesophageal junction organoids with dual
knockout of TP53 and CDKN2A revealed marked upregulation of
platelet-activating factors (PTAFs), a distinct class of lipids.
Mechanistically, the loss of TP53 and CDKN2A leads to increased
expression of the transcription factor FOXM1, which has been
shown to transcriptionally activate the platelet-activating factor
receptor (PTAFR). This activation ampli ﬁes the PTAF –PTAFR
signaling loop, thereby promoting tumor progression.

undergoes a complex transition from proin ﬂammatory to immu-
nosuppressive. Early re ﬂux esophagitis may be dominated by Th1-
type inﬂammation, whereas at the BE stage, the Th2-type immune
response predominates, characterized by increased IL-4-
producing cells and elevated IL-6 and IL-10 levels. The soluble
factors secreted by BE can even inhibit the production of IFN- γ
and TNF-α by peripheral T cells.
276 As lesions progress to HGD and
EAC, immunosuppressive features further intensify. Multiplexed
immunohistochemistry and gene expression deconvolution ana-
lysis revealed that the numbers of FOXP3
+ Tregs and CD163 + M2-
type TAMs tend to increase from BE to EAC. 277,278 These cells
signiﬁcantly accumulate at the HGD stage, but Treg numbers
decrease at the EAC stage. 278 Moreover, a signi ﬁcant decrease in
the proportion and in ﬁltration of CD8 + cytotoxic T cells, especially
within the tumor area of EAC, has been observed. 277,278 In
addition, a signiﬁcant reduction in eosinophils and increase in pro-
B cells, 278 as well as an increase in a new CSF1R +CD1C+ myeloid
cell subgroup ,277 were observed during EAC progression. Despite
different evolutionary details, both ESCC and EAC eventually form
an immunosuppressive microenvironment conducive to tumor
growth and escape. Therefore, precise characterization of the
immune microenvironment is critical for formulating clinical
treatment strategies, as patients exhibit heterogeneous immune
microenvironment features closely related to their sensitivity to
immunotherapy.
212 For example, the presence of a subpopulation
of CD8+ T cells expressing SPRY1 can enhance the activation of the
immuno-tumor microenvironment and indicate responsiveness to
immune checkpoint inhibitors.
279
Crosstalk between tumor cells and various cellular components
within the TME can occur through various molecular pathways,
including those involving cytokines and chemokines.
280,281 These
signaling molecules not only directly act on tumor cells to regulate
cancer gene expression and cellular behavior by activating
signaling pathways such as the JAK/STAT and NF- κB pathways
but also broadly in ﬂuence other components of the TME,
including regulating immune cell recruitment and differentiation,
affecting angiogenesis, and shaping a favorable microenviron-
ment for tumor metastasis, thus playing a role in various stages of
tumor initiation, progression, invasion, and metastasis.
274,282,283 In
the developmental sequence of EAC, speci ﬁc cytokine changes are
particularly prominent. Studies have shown that the expression of
IL-6 and CXCL8 signi ﬁcantly increases during the progression from
BE to HGD and EAC,
278 which may be closely related to the
observed dynamic changes in immune cells. In addition, TNF- α
can increase IL-8 expression in a dose-dependent manner. 284
Moreover, various proin ﬂammatory (such as IFN- γ, TNF- α, IL-1 β,
and IL-2) and anti-in ﬂammatory/immunosuppressive (such as IL-
10 and IL-6) cytokines can be detected in EAC tissues, forming a
complex mixed cytokine spectrum. 276 In ESCC, in addition to the
general upregulation of protumor and immunosuppressive
cytokine/chemokine pathways, 285–288 speciﬁc signaling axes are
more clearly de ﬁned. For example, invasive epithelial cells drive
the expression of downstream chemokines (such as CXCL1 and
CXCL8) through the JAG1-NOTCH1 signaling pathway, which not
only mediates the formation of the CAF-Epi niche but also further
promotes the recruitment and activation of CAFs, constituting a
key mechanism by which interactions between tumor cells and
stromal cells drive tumor progression.
267 Additionally, the intrinsic
genetic characteristics of tumor cells can directly regulate their
secreted cytokine spectrum, thereby shaping the immune
microenvironment. For example, CDKN2A inactivating mutations
common in ESCC have been shown to mediate immune evasion
by upregulating CCL2, recruiting exhausted T cells and M2-type
TAMs via the CCL2 ‒CCR2 axis.

Microbiota
The complex ecosystem of microorganisms that coexist with
human cells has emerged as a critical factor in cancer biology,
inﬂuencing initiation, progression, and treatment responses.
290–292
Tissue biopsy cultivation and high-throughput sequencing tech-
nologies have revealed the existence of complex microbial
communities within the esophagus.
293 Microbial dysbiosis, an
imbalance in the composition and function of the microbiota,
plays a key role in the development and progression of EC.
Different subtypes of EC are associated with speci ﬁc microbial
characteristics.
294 For example, the esophageal microbiota in ESCC
patients typically exhibits reduced diversity and enrichment of
oral anaerobes and pathogens.
295–297 In particular, the periodontal
pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is signi ﬁcantly
associated with increased ESCC risk and poor prognosis. 298
Similarly, Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) , which plays an
important role in colorectal cancer, 299 is also enriched in ESCC
tumor tissues and is associated with tumor invasiveness and
shorter patient survival time. 300,301 In EAC and its precancerous
lesions (such as BE), signi ﬁcant changes in microbial composition
are observed. The BE stage typically shows a shift from
predominantly Gram-positive bacteria to Gram-negative bacteria
(such as Veillonella and Prevotella).302 A special phenomenon is
that Helicobacter pylori infection in the stomach has a protective
effect against EAC, and its eradication may indirectly promote the
occurrence of EAC. 303–305
The mechanisms by which the microbiome drives EC develop-
ment involve multiple levels. First, an imbalanced microbiome,
especially enriched Gram-negative bacteria, can activate the TLR4/
NF-κB pathway by releasing lipopolysaccharide, inducing and
maintaining chronic in ﬂammation, thus promoting the progres-
sion from in ﬂammation to BE and then to EAC.
306 Second, speciﬁc
bacteria can modulate the immune microenvironment to favor
tumor cell survival. 307,308 For example, P. gingivalis can induce IL-6
production, recruit myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and inhibit
antitumor immunity
309; F. nucleatum can upregulate PD-L1
expression on tumor cells, helping tumors evade immune
surveillance.310 Additionally, microbial metabolites or effector
molecules can directly affect cancer cell behaviors. 311 F. nucleatum
can produce putrescine in ESCC cells, promoting cell prolifera-
tion300; P. gingivalis can confer survival advantages and prolif-
erative capacity to cells by activating the JAK/STAT3 pathway,
inhibiting apoptotic signals,
312–314 and even inducing atypical
hyperplasia in vitro in normal esophageal epithelium. 315 Further-
more, F. nucleatum can induce resistance to chemotherapeutic
drugs in ESCC cells by inducing autophagy. 316
The impact of the microbiota on EC is not limited to the local
esophageal environment. Changes in the oral microbiome,
especially in bacteria associated with periodontal disease, such
as P. gingivalis, have been shown to be related to an increased risk
of EC, suggesting the existence of an “oral‒esophageal axis ”
interaction.
317,318 Epidemiological studies have linked poor oral
hygiene habits with a signi ﬁcant increase in ESCC risk. 318
Comparative analysis revealed that the microbiomes of paired
saliva and tumor samples from some ESCC patients are very
similar, with speci ﬁc enrichment of Fusobacterium and Prevotella
observed particularly in tumors. 297 This ﬁnding supports the
model of oral bacteria directly colonizing esophageal tumors or
their metabolic products affecting distant organs. Therefore, the
detection of speci ﬁc microbes (such as P. gingivalis and F.
nucleatum) or microbial community patterns in oral tissues may
serve as biomarkers for early diagnosis, risk assessment, and
prognostic evaluation. The overall composition of the gut
microbiome is also closely related to EC risk. 319,320 Mendelian
randomization studies have suggested causal associations
between speciﬁc intestinal genera and EC risk, with Gordonibacter
and Oxalobacter increasing risk and Turicibacter and Romboutsia
decreasing risk, suggesting that the gut microbiota can serve as
potential risk biomarkers.
321 Gut microbiota dysbiosis may also
indirectly participate in the pathological process of EC by affecting
systemic in ﬂammation levels and immune status.
322 Additionally,
lifestyle and environmental factors, such as dietary habits, alcohol
consumption, smoking, and medication use, can in ﬂuence EC risk
by modulating the microbial composition. 323–325 The composition
of the microbiome also affects EC patients ’ response to treatments
such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy. 293,326,327 For exam-
ple, a high abundance of F. nucleatum in tumors is associated with
a poor response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in ESCC, 328 while
the diversity of the gut microbiota and changes in the abundance
of speci ﬁc species are also related to treatment ef ﬁcacy and
patient survival time. 329,330 These ﬁndings collectively point to the
potential of targeting the microbiome through dietary interven-
tions, probiotics or prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation
as a new strategy for EC prevention and adjuvant therapy.
PREVENTION OF THE EC
Reducing exposure to risk factors
Most cancers are preventable, and prevention strategies can be
categorized into primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention
strategies.
331 Prevention of EC begins with reducing exposure to
known risk factors. Risk factors differ between ESCC and EAC. The
key risk factors for ESCC include tobacco smoking and alcohol
consumption. Population-based studies provide evidence for the
effectiveness of these preventive measures, highlighting the
impact of lifestyle changes. The effect of tobacco smoking
cessation on the risk of EC has been extensively studied. A
comprehensive meta-analysis covering population-based studies
revealed that smoking cessation is associated with a decrease in
the risk of ESCC over time.
332 Compared with current smokers
(relative risk [RR] = 4.18, 95% CI = 3.42–5.12), former smokers had
a lower risk (RR = 2.05, 95% CI = 1.71–2.45), and this protective
effect became more pronounced with longer time since quitting.
A signi ﬁcant risk reduction is observed after 5 years of quitting,
with risk continuing to decline and approaching that of never
smokers (RR = 0.22, 95% CI = 0.18–0.28) after 20 years or more
(RR = 0.34, 95% CI = 0.25–0.47).
332 However, the impact of
smoking cessation on the risk of EAC is limited. With nonsmokers
as a reference, the risk of EAC for former smokers (RR = 1.66, 95%
CI = 1.48–1.85) was only slightly lower than that for current
smokers (RR = 2.34, 95% CI = 2.04–2.69). The risk of EAC did not
signiﬁcantly decrease over time after smoking cessation. Even
after a long period of smoking cessation, compared with that of
individuals with ESCC, where the risk ratio decreases to 0.34 after
20 years of smoking cessation, the risk ratio for EAC only decreases
to 0.72 after 20 years of smoking cessation.
332 Furthermore, a large
population-based case ‒control study in China revealed a positive
correlation between environmental tobacco exposure and the risk
of EC among never smokers.
333 The study reported overall ORs of
1.44 (95% CI = 1.31–1.58) and 1.56 (95% CI = 1.35–1.82) among
nonsmokers.333 This ﬁnding emphasizes the importance of
implementing comprehensive smoke-free policies in public and
private spaces to protect nonsmokers from the harmful effects of
passive smoking.
A comprehensive review of evidence recently conducted by the
International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) concluded
that reducing or completely stopping alcohol consumption does
indeed lower the risk of EC.
334 A case ‒control study from Latin
America revealed that, compared with current drinkers, individuals

Dietary improvement is another avenue for prevention. In areas
where drinking extremely hot beverages and consuming pickled
vegetables are common, promotional measures should be
considered to mitigate the potential increased risk of ESCC
associated with these habits. Additionally, a population-based
absolute risk model developed in China identi ﬁed smoking and
the consumption of onion and garlic as important modi ﬁable
lifestyle-related risk factors for EC.

shown modest bene ﬁts in malnourished populations with high
ESCC incidence. A trial in Linxian County, Henan Province, China,
tested vitamin and mineral supplements in approximately 30,000
adults. Supplementation with β-carotene, vitamin E, and selenium
led to signi ﬁcant reductions in EC mortality and incidence. After 5
years, those receiving this combination had a 42% lower
prevalence of EC according to endoscopic surveys, and survival
rates also improved.
346 While vitamin supplements are not a
panacea, this trial suggests that correcting micronutrient de ﬁ-
ciencies in high-risk areas may be helpful.
Furthermore, medication-based preventive measures are being
explored, with a meta-analysis suggesting a protective effect of
the use of aspirin and nonsteroidal anti-in ﬂammatory drugs
(NSAIDs) for ESCC (OR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.43–0.78).254 The chronic
inﬂammatory environment in BE provides a theoretical basis for
the use of drugs with anti-in ﬂammatory properties. A meta-

the use of COX inhibitors was associated with a signi ﬁcant 36%
reduction in the combined risk of HGD or EAC (RR = 0.64, 95%
CI = 0.53–0.77).

important prospective evidence for evaluating the chemopreven-
tive effects of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and aspirin in BE
patients.
348 This trial (median follow-up of 8.9 years) revealed that
high-dose PPI (80 mg daily) signi ﬁcantly delayed the time to
progression to the composite endpoint (all-cause mortality, EAC,
or HGD) compared with low-dose PPI (20 mg daily) ( P = 0.038). For
aspirin, the main effect did not reach statistical signi ﬁcance in the
overall analysis ( P = 0.068) but showed a protective effect after
excluding patients with early use of NSAIDs ( p = 0.043).
Importantly, the combination of high-dose PPI and aspirin had
the strongest protective effect, particularly compared with the
control group, which received low-dose PPI without aspirin
(P = 0.0068). Compared with high-dose PPI alone, the addition
of aspirin tended to further reduce the risk of the composite
endpoint (event rate approximately 9% versus 13%), although this
speciﬁc comparison did not reach statistical signi ﬁcance
(P = 0.068). These results suggest that high-dose PPI use
combined with aspirin may be bene ﬁcial for BE patients, but the
potential adverse effects of long-term aspirin or NSAID use (such
as bleeding risk, although serious events were rare in this trial)
require careful risk ‒beneﬁt assessment before making recom-
mendations at a broad population level. In addition to behavioral
modiﬁcations such as smoking cessation and dietary changes,
adopting WHO-recommended physical activity (at least 150 min of
moderate-intensity exercise per week) has been demonstrated to
play a pivotal role in cancer prevention and management.

In conclusion, the cornerstone of EC prevention remains
reducing exposure to established lifestyle risk factors. Tobacco
cessation, reducing or completely stopping alcohol consumption,
and dietary improvements have shown practical effects. These
measures, combined with general cancer prevention strategies,
constitute the ﬁrst line of defense against ESCC and EAC.
Early detection and diagnosis of EC
Early detection of EC is crucial for improving patient prognosis, as
typical symptoms such as dif ﬁculty swallowing, weight loss, and
chest pain usually appear only when the disease has progressed
to later stages. For both EAC and ESCC, the multistep nature of EC
development provides opportunities for secondary prevention,
including early detection and intervention (Fig. 4, Table 2).
350
Endoscopic screening is currently the gold standard for detecting
esophageal precancerous lesions or early tumors, and some high-
incidence countries have implemented endoscopic examinations
in population-based screening programs. Evidence from these
programs demonstrates the life-saving potential of screening. For
instance, a large multicenter population cohort study based on six
high-risk regions in China reported that individuals receiving one-
time endoscopic screening had not only a signi ﬁcantly reduced
risk of EC incidence (RR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.69–0.79) compared
with controls but also a substantially reduced mortality risk of 60%
(RR = 0.40, 95% CI = 0.36–0.45).
351 However, a 9-year follow-up
report from a large-scale, community-based randomized con-
trolled trial (ESECC study) conducted in high-risk areas of northern
China provided a different perspective. This trial evaluated the
effectiveness of Lugol’s iodine chromoendoscopy screening. In the
primary intention-to-treat analysis, compared with the control
group, the screening group presented a 19% reduction in the
incidence of EC (adjusted HR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.60–1.09) and an
18% reduction in mortality (adjusted HR = 0.82, 95%
CI = 0.53–1.26), but these reductions were limited and did not
reach statistical signi ﬁcance.
352 The researchers noted that
insufﬁcient screening compliance and delayed treatment for
those who screened positive might have weakened the overall
effectiveness of screening. Despite differences in design and
results across studies, this evidence from China collectively
emphasizes the tremendous potential of endoscopic screening
in reducing the burden of EC while also highlighting the
importance of optimizing screening strategies and ensuring
appropriate follow-up care.

only approximately 9% in AI-guided endoscopy. AI captures
lesions missed by less experienced endoscopists, helping to
improve diagnostic levels and reduce experience-based dispa-

convolutional neural network that accurately detects HER2 over-
expression in gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma, offering a
reliable and automated alternative to manual assessment and
paving the way for personalized therapy.
366 The integration of
these technologies signi ﬁcantly enhances the ability to detect ECs
at earlier, more treatable stages.

Early intervention in EC
When EC or its precancerous lesions are detected at an early stage,
a series of minimally invasive interventional treatments can be
employed to eradicate local lesions, prevent disease progression
to later, more dif ﬁcult-to-treat stages, and avoid complications
associated with late-stage treatment. Endoscopic resection (ER)
techniques, such as endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), have
become key methods for early intervention, effectively replacing
esophagectomy in many cases. ER techniques allow doctors to
remove focal lesions through endoscopy. For example, ESD
removes larger and potentially deeper lesions in one piece by
dissecting the submucosal layer beneath the lesion, thereby
avoiding open surgery.
20,373 For early ESCC, a large retrospective
cohort study from China compared patients with T1a-m2/m3 or
T1b stage disease who received either ESD ( n = 322) or
esophagectomy ( n = 274). The results showed no signi ﬁcant
differences in overall survival (OS) or cancer recurrence/metastasis
rates between the two treatment methods after a median follow-
up of 21 months. Notably, the disease-speci ﬁc mortality rate was
signiﬁcantly lower in the ESD group (3.4% versus 7.4%, P = 0.049),
and the rate of serious perioperative non-fatal adverse events was
also signi ﬁcantly lower than in the esophagectomy group (15.2%
versus 27.7%, P < 0.001).
374 These ﬁndings suggest that organ-
preserving ESD for speci ﬁc early ESCC patients is comparable to
surgery while offering superior safety. Similarly, endoscopy has
shown encouraging long-term ef ﬁcacy in treating early EAC. A
large study of ER in 1000 patients with intramucosal AC reported
after an average follow-up of nearly 5 years. In this study, patients
achieved a 93.8% long-term complete remission rate, with a
calculated 10-year survival rate of 75%. More importantly, this
treatment approach was safe, with an extremely low rate of major
complications (1.5%) and almost zero treatment-related mortality
(tumor-related mortality of only 0.2%).
375 In summary, these
studies collectively con ﬁrm that ER can provide tumor control
effects and long-term survival comparable to esophagectomy for
carefully selected early EC patients, while signi ﬁcantly reducing
perioperative risks and complications. This makes endoscopic
treatment an ef ﬁcient and safe curative method for early EC,
which has become the standard treatment option in many cases
as an alternative to surgery. A meta-analysis covering 15 studies
with a total of 4731 early EC patients further con ﬁrmed that
compared with esophagectomy, ER signi ﬁcantly reduced the risk
of major adverse events (RR = 0.46) and surgery-related mortality
(RR = 0.27). However, long-term monitoring after ER is crucial for
monitoring any signs of recurrence. The meta-analysis also
revealed that ER may be associated with higher tumor recurrence
rates (RR = 1.69, 95% CI = 0.99–2.89) and lower R0 resection rates
(RR = 0.92), and in terms of long-term OS, esophagectomy may
have a slight advantage (HR = 1.21, 95% CI = 1.02–1.43).
376
Additionally, endoscopic ablation techniques, especially radio-
frequency ablation (RFA), have become the main approach for
treating BE with dysplasia, particularly for ﬂat lesions. RFA uses
controlled heat produced by radiofrequency energy to destroy
abnormal Barrett ’s mucosa, aiming to eliminate dysplasia and
intestinal metaplasia, thereby preventing progression to EAC. Its
effectiveness has been con ﬁrmed in several key clinical trials. A
multicenter, sham-controlled randomized trial included 127 BE
patients with low-grade dysplasia (LGD) or HGD. The results
showed that at 12 months, patients in the RFA group had
signiﬁcantly higher rates of complete dysplasia clearance than did
those in the sham group (LGD: 90.5% versus 22.7%; HGD: 81.0%
versus 19.0%; P < 0.001) and signi ﬁcantly higher overall complete
intestinal metaplasia clearance rates (77.4% versus 2.3%,
P < 0.001). More importantly, RFA signi ﬁcantly reduced the risk
of disease progression (3.6% versus 16.3%, P = 0.03) and cancer
incidence (1.2% versus 9.3%; P = 0.045).
377 For the speci ﬁc and
common subgroup of LGD, a European multicenter randomized
trial (SURF trial) directly compared RFA with endoscopic surveil-
lance.
378,379 This study was terminated early owing to the clear
superiority of RFA, as over 3 years of follow-up, RFA signi ﬁcantly
reduced the risk of progression to HGD or adenocarcinoma from
26.5% in the surveillance group to 1.5% (absolute risk reduction
25.0%, P < 0.001) and reduced the risk of progression to
adenocarcinoma from 8.8 to 1.5% (absolute risk reduction 7.4%,
P = 0.03). The RFA group achieved a complete dysplasia clearance
rate of 92.6% and a complete intestinal metaplasia clearance rate
of 88.2%. A meta-analysis including 3802 patients further
consolidated these ﬁndings, reporting pooled complete clearance
rates of approximately 91% for dysplasia and 78% for intestinal
metaplasia after RFA.
380 However, the meta-analysis also revealed
that even after successful clearance, the recurrence rate of
intestinal metaplasia was approximately 13%, emphasizing the
necessity of continued monitoring after treatment. In terms of
safety, the main adverse event of RFA is esophageal stricture, with
an incidence of approximately 5% reported in the meta-analysis
and 11.8% reported in the SURF trial, but this can usually be
resolved through endoscopic dilation. Overall, these high-quality
studies collectively con ﬁrm that RFA is an effective means of
treating BE with dysplasia and is capable of signi ﬁcantly reducing
the risk of cancer progression with acceptable safety.

Targeted therapy
Targeted therapy interferes with speci ﬁc molecular pathways that
drive cancer cell growth and survival, and its application is guided
primarily by speci ﬁc biomarker detection results.

platinum-based chemotherapy signi ﬁcantly improved OS (median
OS: 13.8 months versus 11.1 months, HR = 0.74, P = 0.0046).

in advanced gastric or esophagogastric junction (EGJ) adenocarci-

plus paclitaxel signi ﬁcantly prolonged OS and progression-free

pretreated metastatic ESCC patients with con ﬁrmed EGFR over-
expression.352 This study met its primary endpoint, demonstrating
a con ﬁrmed objective response rate of 39% and a median OS of
7.8 months, with a manageable toxicity pro ﬁle. These ﬁndings
highlight the potential of TKIs in selected ESCC populations.
For tumors with speci ﬁc genetic alterations, targeted therapy
options exist regardless of the histological type. For patients with

mutations, dabrafenib combined with trametinib is an approved
regimen.394 These medications are available for advanced EC
patients with corresponding gene alterations who have failed
previous treatments on the basis of their tissue/site-agnostic
indications. In addition to established targets, several novel targets
are under active investigation for EC treatment. The c-MET
pathway is a key player in tumor progression and is typically
activated by its ligand, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF). While
early attempts to target HGF with antibodies such as rilotumumab
yielded disappointing results in broader MET-positive populations
in the RILOMET-1 trial (HR = 1.34, P = 0.003),
395 selective MET TKIs
have shown promise, particularly in tumors with MET gene
ampliﬁcation. For example, early clinical data with AMG 337
demonstrated an objective response rate of 29.6% in patients with
MET-ampliﬁed solid tumors, including EC.
396 The PI3K/AKT/mTOR
cascade is frequently dysregulated in ESCC. Inhibitors targeting
different nodes of this pathway are being explored. 397,398 The
NOTCH signaling pathway is also a complex but compelling target
in ESCC. The NOTCH pathway, known for its role in cancer
stemness and epithelial ‒mesenchymal transition, is another
promising target, with gamma-secretase inhibitors (e.g., nirogace-
stat) currently in early-phase trials.
399,400 The development of
these targeted therapies is increasingly guided by precise
biomarker identi ﬁcation to select patient populations most likely
to bene ﬁt.
Immunotherapy
The advent of ICIs, such as anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 agents, has
signiﬁcantly reshaped the therapeutic landscape for advanced EC
(Table 3).
401,402 The combination of an anti-PD-1 antibody with
platinum-based chemotherapy has now superseded chemother-
apy alone as the standard ﬁrst-line treatment for most patients
with unresectable locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic
disease, as supported by several pivotal phase 3 clinical trials.
Two key global trials established the bene ﬁt of this combination
approach across different histological subtypes and patient
populations. The KEYNOTE-590 study demonstrated that adding
pembrolizumab to cisplatin and 5- ﬂuorouracil chemotherapy
signiﬁcantly improved OS and PFS compared with chemotherapy
plus placebo.
403 Notably, in patients with ESCC and a PD-L1
combined positive score (CPS) of 10 or greater, the median OS was
signiﬁcantly extended (13.9 versus 8.8 months, HR = 0.57, 95%
CI = 0.43–0.75, P < 0.0001), with bene ﬁts also observed in the
overall ESCC population (OS HR 0.72, 95% CI = 0.60–0.88,
P = 0.0006), the total CPS ≥ 10 population (OS HR 0.62, 95%
CI = 0.49–0.78, P < 0.0001), and the overall study population
irrespective of histology or PD-L1 status (OS HR 0.73, 95%
CI = 0.62–0.86, P < 0.0001). Similarly, PFS improved across these
groups. Complementing this, the CheckMate 649 trial focused
primarily on HER2-negative gastric, gastroesophageal junction,
and esophageal adenocarcinomas. Nivolumab plus chemotherapy
signiﬁcantly improved OS and PFS compared with chemotherapy
alone in patients with a PD-L1 CPS ≥ 5 (OS HR 0.71, 98.4%
CI = 0.59–0.86, P < 0.0001 at primary analysis; 3-year follow-up OS
HR 0.70, 95% CI = 0.61–0.81).
404 While both trials con ﬁrmed the
ﬁrst-line ICI-chemotherapy bene ﬁt globally, they differed in
primary patient populations (KEYNOTE-590 included substantial

chemotherapy backbones.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES
Signiﬁcant advances in EC research and management have
emerged across multiple fronts, from elucidating the pathogenesis
to innovating therapeutic approaches for locally advanced and
metastatic diseases. In particular, improvements in the identi ﬁca-
tion of premalignant lesions, early diagnosis, and the advent of ICI-
based immunotherapy have improved the survival outcomes of
patients. However, challenges persist, and further research is
necessary to address unmet needs in early detection and
screening, develop more effective and personalized therapeutic
strategies, and further elucidate the molecular underpinnings
of EC.
A fundamental challenge in managing EC lies in the current
limitations of early detection and diagnosis. Owing to insidious
symptoms and the lack of highly sensitive and speci ﬁc biomarkers,
the diagnosis of precancerous or cancerous esophageal lesions is
primarily based on endoscope-based pathologic changes, which
are expensive, painful, and often cause late diagnosis. Even when
precancerous lesions are detected, when and how they develop
into invasive cancer are unknown, which presents challenges for
patient management. In fact, clinical observations have shown
that some precancerous lesions might not progress to cancer. This
highlights an important aspect of precancerous lesions: they are
heterogeneous, and the factors that determine whether they will
evolve into cancer remain elusive. To interrupt malignant
progression and identify people who may have progressed to
cancer, deeper insight into the mechanisms that drive the
transition from premalignant lesions to malignant progression is
needed.
The current understanding of carcinogenesis emphasizes the
interaction between cell autonomous and non-cell autonomous
factors, referring to genetic and epigenetic changes, environ-
mental carcinogen exposure, tissue microenvironment imbalance,
and immune dysfunction. These factors can have either promoting
or inhibitory effects on carcinogenesis, depending on the context.
For example, NOTCH1 mutation may help preserve tissue home-
ostasis by competing with other malignant clones
438 in ESCC.
These alterations may be detectable in precancerous lesions;
however, they are not the primary and essential fundamental
drivers of cancer initiation. Only precancerous lesions that possess
a special set of characteristics can progress to cancer. Future
research must prioritize the identi ﬁcation of these core genetic
and environmental signatures and elucidate how they contribute
to the transition from precancerous conditions to invasive cancer.
These factors can serve as early intervention endpoints in clinical
trials and targets for preventing cancer progression. By elucidating
how these factors contribute to the transition from precancerous
conditions to invasive cancer, we may develop more effective and
personalized therapeutic strategies.
Recent advancements in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics,
along with proteomics and bioinformatics, have highlighted the
intercellular communication between malignant epithelial cells
and stromal cells, which is of particular interest for future research
(Table 4). Identifying additional biomarkers that robustly correlate
with esophageal carcinogenesis, progression, and treatment
response, as well as characterizing neoantigens and other
mechanisms to induce or maintain antitumor immune responses,
is essential for improving EC management.
The path forward in EC research and treatment still requires a
coordinated effort across multiple disciplines, from basic science
to clinical practice. By addressing these critical areas, we can work
toward the ﬁnal goal of reducing the burden of EC through
improved prevention, early detection, and more effective ther-
apeutic strategies.
