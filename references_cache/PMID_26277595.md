# Genetic errors of the human caspase recruitment domain-B-cell lymphoma 10-mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma-translocation gene 1 (CBM) complex: Molecular, immunologic, and clinical heterogeneity.
**Authors:** Pérez de Diego R, Sánchez-Ramón S, López-Collazo E, Martínez-Barricarte R, Cubillos-Zapata C, Ferreira Cerdán A, Casanova JL, Puel A
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol (2015)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaci.2015.06.031](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2015.06.031)

## Content

Three members of the caspase recruitment domain (CARD) family of adaptors (CARD9, CARD10, and CARD11) are known to form heterotrimers with B-cell lymphoma 10 (BCL10) and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma-translocation gene 1 (MALT1). These 3 CARD-BCL10-MALT1 (CBM) complexes activate nuclear factor κB in both the innate and adaptive arms of immunity. Human inherited defects of the 3 components of the CBM complex, including the 2 adaptors CARD9 and CARD11 and the 2 core components BCL10 and MALT1, have recently been reported. Biallelic loss-of-function mutant alleles underlie several different immunologic and clinical phenotypes, which can be assigned to 2 distinct categories. Isolated invasive fungal infections of unclear cellular basis are associated with CARD9 deficiency, whereas a broad range of clinical manifestations, including those characteristic of T- and B-lymphocyte defects, are associated with CARD11, MALT1, and BCL10 deficiencies. Interestingly, human subjects with these mutations have some features in common with the corresponding knockout mice, but other features are different between human subjects and mice. Moreover, germline and somatic gain-of-function mutations of MALT1, BCL10, and CARD11 have also been found in patients with other lymphoproliferative disorders. This broad range of germline and somatic CBM lesions, including loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutations, highlights the contribution of each of the components of the CBM complex to human immunity.

The CARD-BCL10-MALT1 (CBM) complex is a cytosolic heterotrimer formed by a caspase recruitment domain (CARD) protein, B-cell lymphoma 10 (BCL10) and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma-translocation gene 1 (MALT1). Three CARD-family adaptor molecules have been shown to form CBM complexes: CARD9, CARD10 (also known as CARD-containing MAGUK protein 3, CARMA3), and CARD11 (also known as CARMA1). Human CARD9 is a 536-amino acid adaptor protein. It possesses an N-terminal CARD domain (mediating homophilic interactions between CARD-containing molecules) and a C-terminal coiled-coil region that serves as an oligomerization domain. CARD10 is a 1,032-amino acid protein that binds to BCL10-MALT11. CARD11 is 1,154 amino acids long and belongs to both the membrane-associated guanylate kinase (MAGUK)2 and CARD families. MALT1 is a 824-amino acid caspase-like cysteine protease that contains a CARD domain, two immunoglobulin (Ig)-like (Ig-like) domains and a C-terminal caspase-like domain. Finally, BCL10 is a 233-amino acid intracellular signaling protein characterized by an amino-terminal CARD motif and a Ser/Thr-rich carboxyl terminus of unknown function3, 4. The two immunoglobulin Ig-likedomains of MALT1 bind to BCL10 through a motif composed of a short stretch of amino acids located just after the CARD of BCL10. CARD motifs of BCL10 and MALT1 allow binding to the three CARD proteins forming the heterotrimer.

In humans and mice, the CBM complex mediates NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) activation, in a cell type-specific and non-redundant manner, after the stimulation of various immune receptors, as described below. The NF-κB transcription factor plays a critical role in innate and adaptive immune regulation, cell memory, cell survival/apotosis, and cell cycle progression. Different cell types contain different members of the CARD family: CARD9 in myeloid cells5, CARD10 in epithelial cells6-8, and CARD11 in lymphocytes5 (Figure 1). In lymphocytes, the CBM complex acts downstream from the T-cell receptor (TCR) or the B-cell receptor (BCR), controlling lymphocyte activation and adaptive immunity9-12. It has also been implicated in BAFFR and TLR4 signaling in B cells13, 14, and in the signaling of several natural killer receptors (NKRs), such as NK1.1, Ly49H, NKG2D, and Ly49D15, 16. In myeloid cells, the CBM complex is involved in innate immune responses, in which it acts downstream from C-type lectin receptors, such as dectin-1, dectin-2, or Mincle17-24, or other receptors, such as FcγR and Toll-like receptors (TLRs). Finally, the CBM complex is involved in signaling downstream from TLR417, 25, 26 and G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling in epithelial cells27, 28 (Figure 1). We describe here the broad spectrum of clinical and immunological features of the primary immunodeficiencies (PID) caused by inborn errors of four of the five individual members of the human CBM complex. Studies of these PIDs should improve our understanding of the specific role of each of these components in immunity.

The first patients with autosomal recessive (AR) MALT1 deficiency to be reported were two siblings from a consanguineous Lebanese family (P1 and P2)29. Both had experienced recurrent pulmonary infections since the age of four months, resulting in bronchiectasis. Both had also developed mastoiditis, chronic aphthous ulcers, cheilitis, gingivitis, esophagitis, gastritis and duodenitis. Despite a history of vaccination with tetanus and unconjugated pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccines, anti-tetanus antibody and antibodies to all pneumococcal serotypes titers were nonprotective in both patients. P1 also suffered from meningitis (caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae). Biopsy of the small intestine showed villous atrophy in P1 and abnormally large numbers of intraepithelial lymphocytes in P2. Candida albicans was cultured from the duodenal biopsy sample and stools of P2. Cytomegalovirus (CMV) was repeatedly recovered from the urine of both patients. Both patients displayed growth retardation, but normal neurological development. Despite intravenous immunoglobulin replacement therapy, both died from respiratory failure, P1 at 13.5 years of age (yo) and P2 at 7 yo. A third patient (P3), was subsequently described by McKinnon et al.30. This patient, a 15 yo girl with consanguineous parents of Kurdish descent, presented with growth retardation, an eczema-like rash since age two weeks, and persistent severe dermatitis, skin infections with Staphylococcus aureus, varicella-zoster virus, and herpes simplex virus 1. P3 also developed severe inflammatory gastrointestinal disease, and suffered from multiple episodes of pneumonia, chronic inflammatory lung disease, bronchiectasis, clubbing, and chronic granulation tissue. Very recently a fouth patient (P4) has been reported. This patient, a male infant with non-consanguineous parents developed generalized rash, intestinal inflammation and severe infections including CMV pneumonitis.31.

P1 and P2 were homozygous for a missense mutation (S89I) located in the CARD domain of MALT1. The mutation had no impact on MALT1 mRNA levels, but no protein was detectable in the lysates of PHA T-cell blasts. P1 and P2 had normal numbers of CD3+CD4+ cells, CD3+CD8+ cells, and CD19+ cells, and normal serum Ig levels. An analysis of naive and memory T cells was performed once, on CD4+ cells from P1, and the percentages were within the normal ranges (68% CD4+CD45RA+ cells, and 38% CD4+CD45RO+ cells). B-cell subsets were not examined29. P3 was homozygous for a missense mutation (W580S). This residue is part of an alpha-helical linker region, and the aromatic group of W580 interacts with hydrophobic residues of the caspase-like domain. The mutation resulted in normal MALT1 mRNA levels, but very low levels of protein. Lymphocyte numbers were within the normal range. The proportion of CD31+ T cells was high, with skewing toward the CD4+ T helper subset. CD4+CD25+CD127+loFoxP3+ regulatory T-cell numbers were normal. However, P3 had very low B-cell counts, with developmental arrest characterized by high percentages of naïve B cells, and an almost complete absence of marginal zone B cells and low levels of switched memory B cells. Serum Ig levels were normal30. P4 was a compound heterozygote with two non-synonymous variants affecting MALT1 exon 10, a splice acceptor defect, cDNA c.1019-2A > G; and a single nucleotide deletion, leading to a frameshift within the paracaspase domain and truncation after 18 missense codons, Y353fs*18. No protein was detectable in the lysates of PBMC. P4 showed expansion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations with high proportions of naïve CD45RA+ T cells. B and NK cells were present, but low Igs levels31. (Figure 1 and Table 1).

PBMCs from P1 and P2 displayed impaired proliferative responses to mitogens (phytohemagglutinin (PHA), concanavalin A (ConA) and lectin from Phytolacca americana (pokeweed) (PWM), and anti-CD3), or antigens (tetanus toxoid (TT), diphtheria toxin (DT) and Candida). Their T cells (PHA T-cell blasts) failed to degrade IκB-α or to produce IL-2 after stimulation with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) plus ionomycin (PMA/ionomycin)29. Similarly, no IκB-α degradation or p65 phosphorylation was observed in primary T cells from P3 after PMA/ionomycin stimulation. The roles of MALT1 in PBMCs from P3 were investigated, by testing the effect of the mutation on the paracaspase activity and the scaffold function central to CBM complex formation. MALT1 paracaspase activity was studied by assessing the cleavage of its substrate, BCL10, and was found to be absent in P330, 32. PBMCs from P4 displayed impaired proliferative responses to PHA and ConA. Moreover these cells were unable to phosphorylate NF-κB or degrade IκB. After activation with PMA/ionomycin or anti-CD3/CD28, P4 T cells had reduced levels of IL-2 and IFN-γ31. Like the patients, Malt1-deficient mice had normal numbers of peripheral T cells with a normal subset distribution, but a defective proliferative response to mitogens or anti-CD3 antibody. The number of follicular B cells in the spleens of Malt1-/- mice was normal, but there were very few marginal zone B cells and peritoneal B1 cells, the cells responsible for producing antibodies against type II T-independent antigens. BAFF receptor signaling was impaired in the mutant mice, and Ig levels were low, at odds with the findings for the human deficiency. MALT1 is essential for T-cell activation, proliferation and IL-2 production in response to TCR ligation in mice. It is required for the NF-κB activation induced by the TCR, but not for TNF-α or IL-1 signaling11, 33 (Table 2).

In 2014, an Amerindian boy (P5) from Ecuador, born to consanguineous parents, was reported to have AR complete BCL10 deficiency. P5 presented with gastroenteritis, otitis and respiratory infections from the age of six months. He was subsequently hospitalized for severe viral infections: influenza A and B with pulmonary infiltrate, adenovirus and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). He developed acute secondary respiratory failure to infection, oral candidiasis and diaper dermatitis. Prolonged diarrhea and chronic nonspecific colitis with moderate lymphocytic infiltration in the lamina propia was observed. At the age of 2 years and 10 months, P5 suffered seizures and status epilepticus. Secondary diffuse leukoencephalopathy was diagnosed. At the age of 3 years and 4 months, P5 suffered from diarrhea caused by Clostridium difficile, and, after 24 hours, he experienced dizziness, disorientation and generalized weakness, with focal abnormal movements. Encephalitis of unknown etiology was suspected, and P5 eventually died due to respiratory failure34.

P5 had a homozygous splice-site mutation (G/A), affecting the first G residue of intron 1 of the gene encoding BCL10 (g.85741978C > T; IVS1+1G>A). This nucleotide is the first nucleotide of intron 1 and is invariant, as it is part of the donor site for splicing. The BCL10 mutation resulted in an absence of wild-type (WT) mRNA and protein. This patient was found to have a profound deficit of memory T and B cells, and abnormally small numbers of CD4+ central memory T cells and CD4+ regulatory T cells. Most B cells (97.1%) were naïve, consistent with the profound hypogammaglobulinemia observed in this patient treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (Figure 1 and Table 1)34.

Myeloid cells form CARD9-BCL10-MALT1 heterotrimers. BCL10-deficient myeloid cells displayed a normal pattern of cytokine production in response to stimulation with TLR1/2, TLR4, TLR2/6 and Dectin-1. By contrast, BCL10-deficient fibroblasts (CARD10-BCL10-MALT1 is the heterotrimer formed in fibroblasts) were unable to translocate the NF-κB p65 subunit to the nucleus and cytokine production in response to stimulation with TLR4, TLR2/6 and Dectin-1 was abolished. Mouse studies, performed on epithelial cells, such as intestinal cells, showed that IL-8 production upon TLR4 stimulation was BCL10-dependent. However, other studies suggested that TLR4 signaling could occur by both BCL10-dependent and BCL10-independent pathways17, 25, 35-37. BCL10 silencing in mouse macrophages resulted in impaired TLR4 responses20, 38. Furthermore, mouse Bcl10-/- dendritic cells (DCs) responded poorly to zymosan in terms of TNF-α and IL-10 production39. Finally, by contrast to the findings for humans, some studies on mouse macrophages have shown that the Dectin-1 signaling pathway activates NF-κB via the Syk-CARD9-BCL10 complex23, 34. Human BCL10 deficiency affected the final maturation of T and B cells into memory cells, but not the development of naïve T and B cells. Human T-cell proliferation was blocked following TCR stimulation in vitro. As in humans, mouse Bcl10 has been implicated in NF-κB activation in response to stimulation of the BCR and TCR3, 4, 37, 40. Despite normal early development in the bone marrow, mouse mature B-cell levels were low in the peripheral blood41. It has been shown that double-positive thymocytes differentiate to generate normal numbers of total CD4+ and CD8+ cells, producing normal amounts of TCR- α/β/CD3 complexes6 (Table 2).

In two different studies, two patients (P6 and P7) were found to have AR CARD11 deficiency42, 43. P6 was a girl born to consanguineous parents of Central European ancestry. At six months of age, she presented with severe tachypnea and required oxygen supplementation and mechanical ventilation. She suffered from severe interstitial pneumonia caused by Pneumocystis jirovecii. P7, a girl born to consanguineous parents of Palestinian descent, presented with an upper respiratory tract infection at nine months of age. At the age of 13 months, she presented with fever and severe dyspnea caused by Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. P7 was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin.

A third patient with a compound heterozygous mutation has also been described, but the data for this patient have yet to be published.

P6 had a homozygous premature stop codon mutation, Q945X, resulting in truncation of the essential guanylate kinase-like (GUK) domain of CARD1142. This mutation led to the production of a truncated protein in transfected CARD11-deficient JPM50.6 cells. P6 had agammaglobulinemia, but her T-cell and B-cell counts were, unexpectedly, normal. Immunophenotyping revealed a predominantly naïve phenotype in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. B cells from peripheral blood were mostly naïve, and had a transitional B-cell phenotype. Regulatory T cells were absent. P7 had a homozygous 1377-bp genomic deletion, encompassing the entire sequence of exon 21, encoding amino acids 902-946. The corresponding protein was not detected in EBV-transformed B cells43. Like P6, P7 had hypogammaglobulinemia. She had normal CD4 and CD8 T-cell counts, with a predominantly naïve phenotype, and very low levels of regulatory T cells, effector memory cells, and terminally differentiated effector cells. Most of her B cells were naïve, transitional B cells32 (Figure 1, Table 1).

Lymphocyte proliferation was strongly impaired following the stimulation of PBMCs from P6 with T-cell mitogens, such as PHA, ConA, or anti-CD3. By contrast, the proliferation induced by the classical B-cell mitogen PWM was similar to that for healthy controls. In P6, the mutation was loss-of-function and disturbed TCR-mediated T-cell function. Studies in PBMCs showed that mRNA levels after simulation with LPS (IL-1β, IL-6 and CXCL8) or PMA/ionomycin (IL-2, IFN-γ, IL-4, 5, 10, 17 and 22) were very low. Studies on PBMCs from P7 showed that the degradation of IκB-α and the phosphorylation of p65 after stimulation with anti-IgM antibody or PMA were abolished, whereas the response to stimulation with CD40L was normal. Furthermore, upon BCR stimulation, no induction of intracellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and CD25 was observed in B cells. Following PMA/ionomycin stimulation, the percentages of IL-2-, IL-17- and IFN-γ-producing CD4+ T cells were low. The upregulation of OX40, CD25 and ICOS expression after TCR/CD28 stimulation was also strongly impaired. Finally, T-cell proliferation in response to CD3/CD28 stimulation was completely abolished in vitro42, 43. These immunologic findings for human patients with CARD11 deficiency are consistent with the phenotype of Card11-/- mice, which display a selective defect of NF-κB activation, impairing antigen receptor- or PKC-induced proliferation and cytokine production in T and B cells5. In Card11-/- mice or mice homozygous for a hypomorphic allele, the antigen-independent development of T and B cells in the thymus and bone marrow, respectively, was normal, but peripheral activation, antigen-dependent proliferation, and lymphocyte differentiation were impaired15, 44. The development of CD5+ peritoneal B cells was disrupted in these mice, as was B-cell proliferation in response to both the ligation of both BCR and CD40. Serum Ig levels were low, and, after vaccination, Card11 mutant mice responded poorly to T-dependent and T-independent antigens44. Mature B-cell numbers were found to be very low in mouse models9, 44. The crucial defect in late B-cell differentiation leading to low serum Ig levels and defective T-dependent and T-independent immune responses was common to all mouse models9, 15, 44. Card11-/- T cells were defective for the recruitment of BCL10 to clustered TCR complexes and lipid rafts, NF-κB activation, and the induction of IL-2 production (Table 2). Thus, in both humans and mice, CARD11 plays a key role in antigen receptor signaling, leading to the activation and proliferation of B and T cells45.

Since with the first description of AR CARD9 deficiency in 2009, 38 patients with this condition have been reported, making it by far the commonest genetic disorder of the CBM complex 46-53 (Table 3). All these patients displayed selective susceptibility to various fungal diseases, including mucocutaneous candidiasis, superficial, extensive or deep dermatophytosis, subcutaneous phaeohyphomycosis, Candida central nervous system or digestive tract infections, or invasive Exophiala infection (Table 3)46-53.

Thirteen different mutations have been identified and have been found in the homozygous (R18W50, R35Q51, R70W51, Y91H47, R101C52, D274fsX6053, Q289X49, 51, 52, Q295X48, 51, E323del50) or compound heterozygous (L64fsX59/Q158X53 and G72S/R373P46) state. The patients analyzed had complete blood counts within the normal ranges, with normal counts of CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, effector memory T cells, regulatory T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, and normal basal levels of serum Igs. Most, but not all patients had significantly lower proportions of IL-17-producing T cells than healthy donors46, 48, 52, 53 (Figure 1, Table 3).

Glocker et al. showed that the mutation identified (Q295X) impaired the production of TNF-α in response to curdlan (Dectin-1 agonist) stimulation in transfected bone marrow-derived macrophages from Card9-/- mice, whereas the WT CARD9 allele corrected the Dectin-1 signaling defect, and studies in monocytes showed that Candida-induced IL-6 and IL-1β release was abolished48. Drewniak et al. showed that the release of IL-6 and IL-1β by CARD9-/- leukocytes following stimulation with C. albicans was impaired, whereas that following stimulation with Staphylococcus aureus was not. They also reported a significant impairment of C. albicans killing by CARD9-deficient neutrophils (mostly for unopsonized C. albicans)46. In 2013, Lanternier et al. showed that IL-6 production by whole blood stimulated with heat-killed C. albicans or S. cerevisiae was impaired52. Gavino et al. reported an impairment of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) production by zymosan-stimulated PBMCs47. In 2014 and 2015, Lanternier et al. showed that TNF-α production by Monocyte-derived dendritic cells (MDDCs) in response to fungal agonists (curdlan, zymosan, heat-killed S. cerevisiae, C. albicans and E. dermatitidis stimulation) was impaired50, 51. Similarly, Wang et al. studied macrophages and DCs stimulated with Phialophora verrucosa, and found that the levels of IL-1β, IL-23p19, IL-6 and TNF-α were lower in the patient's cells than in control cells53. Table 3 summarizes the cellular phenotypes observed in the various CARD9-deficient patients studied. Card9-/- mice display normal development of T- and B-cell subsets, including regulatory T cells, recirculating B cells, mature follicular and marginal zone B cells, and B1 B cells. Like some CARD9-deficient patients, they have small numbers of Th17 cells, which have been implicated in mucocutaneous antifungal immunity46, 48, 52, 53. No significant differences in basal Ig levels were detected. TCR- and BCR-induced NF-κB activation was normal; CARD9 is specifically required to transduce zymosan-activated Dectin-1 signals to NF-κB39, 54. Card9-/- mice display impaired cytokine production by DCs following stimulation with zymosan or whole C. albicans27, 39, demonstrating the importance of CARD9 for innate antifungal immunity19 (Table 2).

MALT1 was first implicated in mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma, a B-cell lymphoma frequently involving the gastrointestinal tract, in 1983. This type of tumor constitutes the commonest subset of extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphomas55. In 1999, chromosomal translocations of BCL10 (t(1;14)(p22;q32) and t(11;18)(q21;q21)) were also shown to be associated with MALT lymphomas 56-58. These translocations generate MALT1 and BCL10 somatic gain-of-function (GOF) leading the constitutive activation of NF-κB signaling. BCL10 is also a molecular determinant of other types of lymphoma59-61. Somatic GOF mutations affecting the coiled-coil domain of CARD11 have also been observed in several forms of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and, as in MALT1 lymphomas, these mutations cause the constitutive activation of NF-κB signaling62-68. Germline GOF CARD11 mutations have also been described in other patients69. These mutations cause a congenital B-cell lymphoproliferative disease. Six affected patients have been identified, and the data for two of these patients remain unpublished. In the four patients described to date, there is a family history of polyclonal expansion of naive B cells, splenomegaly, and a potential predisposition to B-cell malignancy69. All carry a novel heterozygous germline mutation, E127G, affecting the coiled-coil domain of CARD11. Another heterozygous mutation, G116S, was also identified in a different patient with very similar signs of disease. All these mutations promote constitutive NF-κB activation, but they render the patients' T cells less responsive to antigen receptor-induced activation. Finally, a thrid GOF missense mutation, G123D, it has been described in another patient with persistent polyclonal B-cell lymphocytosis70. The oncogenic potential of constitutive NF-κB activation due to germline CARD11 GOF mutations may promote B-cell malignancy due to the larger number of naïve B cells69.

In this review, we have summarized and compared the recently described human CBM complex deficiencies. Some of these deficiencies were first reported in studies of single patients71. In-depth mechanistic studies can now establish the causal role of single-gene inborn errors in immunodeficient patients71-73. Such studies are essential to determine the role of these genes in human immunity71. They reveal both similarities to and differences from the situation in mice, such as the differences in innate response found for BCL10 deficiency and differences in Ig production for MALT1 deficiency. No germline or somatic mutations of human CARD10 have yet been identified; studies in Card10-/- mice have shown these mice to have impaired NF-κB activation following stimulation with epidermal growth factor (EGF), and to display several characteristics of EGFR-associated malignancy, including cell proliferation, survival, migration, and invasion6. The overexpression of CARD10 has been shown to be associated with cancer74. Given the specificity of CARD10 expression in the CBM complex of epithelial cells, human CARD10 deficiency would be unlikely to result in a combined immunodeficiency. As TLR4 signaling occurs upstream from the CARD10-BCL10-MALT1 complex in epithelial cells and some studies have shown TLR4-BCL10-/- status to be associated with inflammation and colitis6, 17, 25, 35, 36, 75, it could also be hypothesized that CARD10 defects would be associated with inflammatory colitis.

Overall, defects of CARD11, BCL10 and MALT1 generate severe forms of combined immunodeficiency. The BCL10-deficient patient and two of the four MALT1-deficient patients died, whereas one MALT1-deficient patient and one of the two CARD11-deficient patients had undergone bone marrow transplantation and the other patient is awaiting transplantation. Bone marrow transplantation appears to be the best treatment for these three related disorders, given the tragic outcomes of patients not treated in this way. By contrast, in CARD9 deficiency, innate immunity is affected in a very specific way, as shown by the patterns of susceptibility to invasive disease, generally caused by a single fungal pathogen in each patient (and thus by diverse fungi at the population level). None of the CARD9-deficient patients described to date had pulmonary fungal infection. In one CARD9-deficient patient with C. albicans meningoencephalitis47, adjuvant GM-CSF treatment appears to have been beneficial. In addition, for CARD9-deficient patients with extensive dermatophytosis, terbinafine has been shown to be successful as a first-line treatment and posaconazole as a second-line treatment49.

Finally, the overproduction of MALT1 and BCL10 has been implicated in MALT lymphoma56-58, and several therapeutic studies have thus focused on inhibiting expression of the CBM complex76. BCL10 deficiency appears to have an impact on lymphocytes and fibroblasts, but not on myeloid cells. This finding is highly pertinent and should be taken into account when considering treatment options for MALT lymphoma, because BCL10 inhibition may have consequences for lymphocytes and non-hematopoietic cells.
