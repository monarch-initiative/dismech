Familial adult myoclonus epilepsy (FAME) is a genetic neurological disorder 
characterized by cortical myoclonus and epileptic seizures with clinical 
features that overlap with other movement disorders and epileptic syndromes, 
particularly essential tremor (ET), progressive myoclonic epilepsy (PME), and 
juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME). The key clinical manifestations include an 
autosomal dominant family history, tremorlike cortical myoclonus, generalized 
tonic-clonic seizures, photosensitivity, mild cognitive impairment, and other 
associated symptoms. Electrophysiological examinations are essential in 
demonstrating cortical hyperexcitability and confirming the cortical origin of 
myoclonus. Neuroimaging studies typically show mild cerebellar atrophy or 
nonspecific structural changes on magnetic resonance imaging. Notably, 
photosensitivity is confirmed by clinical, electrophysiological, and 
neuroimaging evidence, highlighting its significant yet underestimated role in 
the underlying etiology of FAME. Genetic analysis, particularly long-read genome 
sequencing, is recognized as the gold standard for definitive diagnosis. We 
propose an integrated and clinically oriented diagnostic approach for FAME, 
including clinical assessment, electrophysiological tests, neuroimaging studies, 
and genetic analysis. The differential diagnosis of FAME, ET, PME, and JME is 
also thoroughly discussed. Antiseizure medications are the cornerstone of FAME 
treatment, with a combination of valproate or levetiracetam with benzodiazepines 
serving as the first-line therapy. A detailed review and a well-established 
diagnostic workflow for FAME can enhance the understanding of FAME; the 
identification of specific biomarkers for FAME requires further investigation.

Familial adult myoclonus epilepsy (FAME) is a neurogenetic disorder caused by intronic pentanucleotide repeat expansion with autosomal dominant (AD) inheritance. Clinically, it is characterized by tremorlike cortical myoclonus, infrequent epileptic seizures, and photosensitivity. Although FAME has not yet been recognized as an epilepsy syndrome by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE),
1
 it is sometimes considered a mild variant of progressive myoclonic epilepsy (PME) due to its relatively benign progressive course.
2



FAME was first identified in 1985 in a Japanese family spanning three generations, presenting with adult onset fine tremulous movement in the fingers, myoclonic jerks, and generalized seizures.
3
 In 1990, action and postural tremors originating from the cerebral cortex were reported in FAME patients, leading to the introduction of the term "cortical tremor" to describe this unique familial symptom.
4
 Over time, various terms have been used globally to refer to FAME, including familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy, benign adult familial myoclonus epilepsy, familial essential myoclonus and epilepsy, and autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy. In 2005, it was recognized that these terms all referred to the same clinical entity.
5
, 
6
 Subsequently, the 2022 International Workshop on FAME, endorsed by the ILAE, established "FAME" as the standardized term for this condition.
7



Due to the lack of specific biomarkers and limited public awareness, FAME is frequently misdiagnosed as other movement disorders or epilepsy syndromes, such as essential tremor (ET), PME, and juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME). A survey conducted across 11 international centers during the 2022 International Workshop revealed a misdiagnosis rate of approximately 20.4% (41/201) for FAME, with the most common misdiagnoses being epilepsy combined with ET (eight patients), focal epilepsy (six patients), and JME (six patients).
7
 As a result, the prevalence of FAME is likely underreported, with an estimated rate of fewer than 1 in 35 000.
8
 Moreover, the previous diagnostic criteria for FAME were primarily developed for research purposes and did not fully consider their feasibility in real‐world clinical settings, such as limited health care resources, time constraints, and financial burden on patients.

We therefore assessed the clinical symptoms, neurophysiological characteristics, and neuroimaging features of FAME and proposed an integrated diagnostic workflow for clinical practice, thereby enhancing our understanding and management of this condition.

Individuals with FAME typically have a family history of myoclonus and/or epilepsy, following an AD inheritance pattern. The repeat expansion itself is proposed as the primary pathogenetic factor, rather than the associated genes.
16
 However, TTTTA/TTTCA expansion in DAB1 causes spinocerebellar ataxia type 37, characterized by severe cerebellar ataxia without cortical myoclonus.
18
 This suggests that FAME‐associated genes may share intrinsic features, such as high expression in both the cerebral and cerebellar cortex.
19



Various expansion configurations have been detected in FAME: 5′‐(TTTTA)exp.(TTTCA)exp‐3′, 5′‐(TTTTA)exp.(TTTCA)exp.(TTTTA)exp‐3′, and 5′‐(TTTTA)exp.(TTTGA)exp‐3′.
16
 The most common form involves a TTTTA expansion followed by a TTTCA expansion, with the latter showing a stronger association with the disease than TTTTA.
20
 Additionally, somatic and germline instability of these repeat expansions has been reported.
16



Cortical myoclonus is the hallmark symptom of FAME, presenting as continuous, arrhythmic, fine myoclonic movements primarily affecting the bilateral distal upper limbs (e.g., fingers and hands) during posture and action. In some cases, patients may also experience arrhythmic jerks in the lower limbs, trunk, proximal upper limbs, or head, particularly in the eyelids.
21
 Moreover, cortical myoclonus is typically the initial symptom, with onset most commonly in the second or third decade of life (range = 3–70 years
17
). In addition to action and posture, other known triggers include sensory stimuli (i.e., tactile, auditory, and photic stimuli), fatigue, insomnia, emotional stress, sleep deprivation, alcohol, noise, and hunger.
17
, 
22
 In the early stages, myoclonus is generally mild but gradually progresses, eventually interfering with daily activities in later stages of the disease. Despite variability in symptom severity across individuals, the overall prognosis is generally favorable with appropriate treatment.

Although the clinical presentation of cortical myoclonus in FAME resembles tremulous movement, synchronous electromyographic (EMG) discharges between agonist and antagonist muscles have been documented,
5
, 
23
 indicating a classic pattern of myoclonus. Latorre et al. proposed that since both rhythmic tremulous and arrhythmic myoclonic jerks are recorded in FAME patients,
4
, 
5
, 
23
 cortical tremor should be considered a rhythmic variant of cortical myoclonus and thus included within its broader spectrum.
19
 The cortical origin and the hyperexcitability can be further confirmed through electrophysiological studies.

Epileptic seizures are another canonical clinical symptom of FAME, typically occurring at a later age (usually in the third or fourth decade
17
) compared to cortical myoclonus in most patients.
24
, 
25
 Seizures are relatively infrequent, with patients experiencing an average of five to 10 episodes over the entire course of the disease,
26
 and many have fewer than one episode per year. Notably, seizures exhibit clear sleep–wakefulness rhythmicity, with nearly all occurring during wakefulness.
22



The precipitating factors for epileptic seizures are similar to those for cortical myoclonus, including fatigue, photic stimulation, emotional stress, and alcohol. Additionally, prolonged prodromes (ranging from minutes to hours) and myoclonic jerks often precede seizures. Common prodromic symptoms include palpitations, chest discomfort, dizziness, headache, and blurred vision.
22
 Importantly, timely administration of antiseizure medications (ASMs) during the prodromic phase can effectively prevent the progression to subsequent seizures.

A clinical history of photosensitivity has been reported in 24.8% (31/125)
22
 and 39.6% (40/101)
27
 of FAME patients. However, due to limited recognition of this clinical feature, large cohort studies are urgently needed. Photosensitivity is characterized by an exaggerated sensation of discomfort or stress in response to external photic stimuli, which can trigger myoclonus and generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCSs). In severe cases, individuals may be compelled to adopt nocturnal lifestyles to avoid the sunlight, significantly disrupting their daily life and work. Specifically, some patients exhibit pattern sensitivity (i.e., susceptibility to striped patterns) and scotosensitivity (i.e., susceptibility to dark environments).
22



Although FAME is generally considered a mild progressive condition, photosensitivity can occasionally result in accidental death. Studies have reported cases where photosensitive patients experiencing GTCSs triggered by glare or reflection lose their consciousness, leading to drowning and other fatal accidents.
22
 Given the unavoidable and frequent exposure to various photic stimuli in daily life, there is a need for more comprehensive and advanced studies to mitigate catastrophic outcomes in FAME patients. To date, given the clinical heterogeneity and paucity of large‐scale research, photosensitivity remains as a supportive diagnostic clue.

Other forms of visual sensitivity have been identified in several patients through video‐electroencephalographic (EEG) recording. These include eye‐opening sensitivity, eye‐closure sensitivity, and eye‐closed sensitivity, which manifest as paroxysmal epileptic discharges (EDs) during eye opening, paroxysmal EDs within seconds after eye closure, and persistent EDs during eye closure, respectively.
27

 However, longitudinal studies with extended follow‐up periods are necessary to elucidate the detailed progression of cognitive impairment. Given the gradual progression of FAME, individuals are more susceptible to mild cognitive decline in the advanced stage of disease, often decades after onset.
17
 Severe cognitive impairment early in the disease course is more characteristic of other epilepsy syndromes, such as PME.

Additional symptoms in FAME primarily include cerebellar signs, psychiatric disorders, and other nonspecific neurological manifestations, which vary across different pedigrees and FAME subtypes (Table 1). Subtle cerebellar signs, such as ataxia, gait instability, impaired smooth pursuit, and downbeat nystagmus,

 However, prominent cerebellar dysfunction or other profound neurological symptoms should be regarded as red flags confounding differential diagnosis with more aggressive conditions such as PME or spinocerebellar ataxia.

Compared to healthy controls and patients with JME, individuals with FAME are more predisposed to psychiatric comorbidities.

 Specifically, depression, generalized anxiety disorder, and various personality disorders (e.g., paranoid, psychasthenia, schizophrenia, hypomania, hypochondriasis) have been reported in some pedigrees.

Photoparoxysmal responses (PPRs) triggered by intermittent photic simulation (IPS) are frequently observed in FAME,

 with prevalence rates ranging from 24.5% to 79.5% (Figure 1C,D).

 Patients may also exhibit time‐locked photomyogenic responses. In our previous study, 46.2% showed a positive photomyoclonic response (PMR) upon IPS.22 Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) protocols further reveal increased facilitation and decreased inhibition within the primary motor cortex (M1). Specifically, short‐interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) and long‐interval intracortical inhibition (LICI) demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy in discriminating FAME from JME and healthy controls.

Surface EMG recordings in FAME patients reveal irregular, arrhythmic, high‐frequency, and synchronous myoclonic jerks between agonist–antagonist muscles, occurring in 50–100‐ms bursts.
35
 Confirmation of cortical myoclonus is crucial for diagnosing FAME. Somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs) and C‐reflex are two principal EMG‐related investigations used to confirm cortical hyperexcitability. In FAME patients, g‐SEPs are characterized by magnified amplitudes of P25 (>8.6 μV) and N33 (>8.4 μV) component,
40
 serving as a key marker of somatosensory cortex hyperexcitability. These amplitudes also vary with the real‐time disease state.
41
, 
42
 Intriguingly, high‐frequency oscillations superimposed on the giant P25 component have emerged as promising biomarkers, with superior diagnostic performance to distinguish FAME from other cortical myoclonus disorders.
43
 Enlarged visual evoked potentials are also common in FAME, indicating hyperexcitability of the primary visual cortex.
35
, 
44
, 
45
 The C‐reflex, or long latency reflex I, refers to motor activities induced by somatosensory stimuli via transcortical pathways.
35
 Given its dependence on both primary somatosensory cortex and M1, an enhanced C‐reflex indicates sensorimotor hyperexcitability, distinguishing it slightly from g‐SEPs.

Confirming the cortical origin of myoclonus in FAME requires integrated analysis of EEG and EMG data. Jerk‐locked backward averaging (JLA) typically reveals a biphasic spike or wave localized to the contralateral sensorimotor cortex, preceding and time‐locked to myoclonic events (Figure 1E,F).
5
, 
38
, 
46
 For patients with negative JLA results but suspected cortical myoclonus, EEG–EMG corticomuscular coherence provides a more advanced method to measure the functional coupling and establish the causal link between EEG and EMG signals.
47
 In FAME patients, increased coherence, particularly in the beta band (8–30 Hz) with a peak at 17 Hz, has been reported, corroborating the cortical origin of myoclonus.
48



Neuroimaging investigations in FAME primarily employ magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to elucidate structural and functional alterations. Conventional MRI findings typically reveal subtle structural changes, including mild enlargement of subarachnoid spaces or lateral ventricles,
24
, 
49
 as well as minimal cerebellar atrophy.
29
 Additionally, T2‐weighted hyperintensities within the periventricular white matter (WM) are occasionally observed.
50
 However, these findings are generally nonspecific and do not provide definitive diagnostic value.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) has unveiled a pervasive decrease in WM integrity, with reduced mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity, and axial diffusivity across commissural, projection, and association fibers. Notably, the most pronounced alterations are localized to the right sagittal stratum.
51
 These observations imply widespread disruptions in signal transmission, which may underpin the photosensitive characteristic of FAME. Moreover, whereas cerebral fractional anisotropy (FA) remains preserved, cerebellar FA is significantly diminished, accompanied by reduced cerebellar fiber density.
52
 This dichotomy underscores the selective vulnerability of cerebellar WM in FAME. In contrast, other epilepsy syndromes, such as JME, typically exhibit decreased FA and increased MD in specific regions.
51
 Additionally, voxel‐based morphometry (VBM) studies have revealed increased WM volume in the right sagittal stratum and cerebellum,
51
, 
53
 along with decreased cerebellar gray matter volume.
54
, 
55



Functional MRI (fMRI) studies have revealed aberrant functional connectivity (FC) patterns within the cerebellum and between the cerebellum and cerebrum.
56
 Notably, an increased FC between the primary visual cortex and the right precentral gyrus supports the hypothesis of an altered visual–motor network.
51



Accumulating evidence from neuroimaging, genetics, and pathology supports RNA‐mediated toxicity as the most compelling pathogenetic hypothesis.
9
, 
18
, 
51
 RNA toxicity initiates primary cerebellar pathology and reduces inhibitory output, thereby triggering cortical hyperexcitability via the cerebellothalamocortical loop and giving rise to cortical myoclonus.
19
, 
51
, 
57
 The confluence of evidence from DTI, VBM, and fMRI studies further underscores the critical role of photosensitivity in FAME pathophysiology. Further neuroimaging research is anticipated to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and provide deeper insights into the disease process.

Given the genetic etiology of FAME, genetic analysis remains a powerful tool in its diagnosis. Despite the availability of diverse molecular methodologies for detecting repeat expansions, each technique exhibits distinct clinical benefits and technical limitations.

Repeat‐primed polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and long‐range PCR are the most accessible molecular techniques for detecting repeat expansion disorders in established clinical settings. Considering factors such as cost, time, DNA input requirements, and accuracy, repeat‐primed PCR is recommended as the initial step in genetic analysis. This method uses repeat‐specific primers to detect the size and configuration of TTTTA and TTTCA repeat expansions. However, false‐negative results may occur in cases with novel repeat configurations (e.g., 5′‐[TTTTA]exp.[TTTCA]exp.[TTTTA]exp‐3′, 5′‐[TTTTA]exp.[TTTGA]exp‐3′), or in instances of extremely short TTTCA expansions.
16
 Therefore, long‐range PCR is recommended as the subsequent step. Although long‐range PCR only measures repeat expansion size, it provides additional diagnostic insights for patients with negative TTTCA results from repeat‐primed PCR, especially when the overall expansion size cannot be explained by TTTTA expansion alone. Long‐range PCR studies have identified expanded alleles ranging from 5 to 8 kb,
10
, 
58
 although larger repeat alleles (>10 kb) may remain undetected.
16

Despite the relatively mild progressive course of FAME, accidental death induced by photosensitivity and long‐term neurocognitive impairment driven by persistent IEDs and suboptimal ASM regimens pose significant threat to patients' survival and well‐being,
63
 which underscores the critical need for early and accurate diagnosis of FAME.

Currently, there is no established diagnostic guideline for FAME in routine clinical practice. Genetic analysis, particularly long‐read genome sequencing, is regarded as the gold standard due to its high sensitivity in detecting repeat expansion disorders.
16
 However, the high cost and limited accessibility of molecular techniques have restricted their widespread application in diagnostics. In contrast, medical history assessment and electrophysiological studies are more accessible and practical options for most patients. As a result, the diagnostic process for FAME can be streamlined to emphasize family history, clinical symptoms, and auxiliary examinations.
6
, 
17
, 
43
, 
51
, 
56
 The diagnostic criteria for FAME are as follows (Table 2): (1) family history of myoclonus or epilepsy with AD inheritance; (2) presence of myoclonus or tremor; (3) infrequent epileptic seizures; (4) electrophysiological demonstration of cortical myoclonus, with cortical origin confirmed by JLA or EEG–EMG corticomuscular coherence, and cortical hyperexcitability validated by g‐SEP or C reflex; (5) absence of other severe neurological symptoms, such as evident cognitive decline or cerebellar ataxia; (6) absence of significant progression of symptoms in the early stage of the clinical course; and (7) genetic confirmation of pathogenic FAME‐related repeat expansions. Patients meeting the diagnostic criteria are further classified into three subgroups. “Definite” FAME is defined when all items of (1)–(6) are satisfied, or (2) and (7) are satisfied. “Probable” FAME is defined when (1), (4), (5), (6), and either (2) or (3) are satisfied. “Possible” FAME is defined when (1), (5), (6), and either (2) or (3) are satisfied. Patients classified as “probable” or “possible” FAME are recommended for subsequent genetic testing. Those with molecular confirmation of known pathogenetic repeat expansions are recategorized as “definite” FAME, whereas genetically negative cases retain their initial classification, necessitating longitudinal clinical follow‐up and periodic genetic reassessment.

A systematic diagnostic workflow for practical application in clinical settings can be established (Figure 2). The process is sequentially organized into four main components: clinical assessment, electrophysiological tests, neuroimaging studies, and, finally, genetic analysis. As the cornerstone of preventing diagnostic oversight, clinical assessment serves as the first step to comprehensively identify the core symptoms of FAME. This includes assessing the presence of myoclonus, epileptic seizures, an AD family history, photosensitivity, and the absence of other severe neurological symptoms (e.g., dementia, ataxia). Additional factors, such as tremorlike movements resistant to beta‐blockers,
64
 onset during the second or third decade of life, and myoclonus in distal upper limbs, provide supplementary diagnostic value.

Detailed electrophysiological tests remain critical for identifying the features of cortical myoclonus. g‐SEP and enhanced C‐reflex are used to assess cortical hyperexcitability, whereas JLA and EEG–EMG coherence are employed to confirm cortical origin. Characteristic EEG findings include IEDs with occipital dominance that occur preferentially during wakefulness, and positive PPR/PMR. A classic myoclonic pattern in 50–100‐ms bursts on polymyographic recordings, along with reduced SICI and LICI in TMS protocols, can provide further diagnostic support.

In clinical practice, neuroimaging primarily serves as a tool to exclude other neurological disorders or evident structural abnormalities, such as stroke, tumors, or hippocampal sclerosis. Structural MRI in FAME patients typically reveals mild cerebellar atrophy or no significant change in FAME patients.

Finally, genetic analysis should be conducted in patients with a suspected diagnosis of FAME. Long‐read genome sequencing represents the first‐tier method due to its capability of providing the complete sequence of repeat expansions.
65
 Short‐read genome sequencing combined with bioinformatic analysis serves as an alternative approach for definitive diagnosis. Given the founder effect in FAME subtypes, targeted long‐read sequencing of the seven FAME‐related genes is recommended as a fallback strategy in the absence of whole‐genome sequencing capabilities. When sequencing‐based methods are unavailable, repeat‐primed PCR remains a viable option for detecting canonical TTTTA/TTTCA repeat expansions. Patients with inconclusive repeat‐primed PCR results should undergo long‐range PCR subsequently. In clinical practice, the choice among these techniques depends on factors such as patient preference and the availability of medical resources.

In clinical practice, FAME is typically characterized by tremorlike cortical myoclonus and epileptic seizures, which can overlap with other tremulous movement disorders, such as ET, and epileptic syndromes like PME and JME. Due to the limited awareness and recognition of FAME, misdiagnoses are common, potentially delaying appropriate treatment and exacerbating the condition (Table 3).

Tremorlike myoclonic movements are often the first symptom in FAME patients, leading to a high likelihood of misdiagnosing as ET. Neither FAME nor ET has a definitive diagnostic marker or identifiable structural lesions in clinical settings. The key distinction between FAME and ET lies in the demonstration of cortical myoclonus. ET is defined by its clinical features rather than its etiology and is classified as a tremor syndrome according to the 2018 Consensus Statement.
66
, 
78
 In clinical practice, 50%–70% of ET cases have an AD family history.
78
 ET is characterized by a 4–12‐Hz
72
 bilateral action tremor without associated epileptic seizures. Head or voice tremors are commonly observed in ET patients. ET patients may also experience nonspecific features, such as cognitive decline, gait impairment, and neuropsychiatric disturbances. Although no specific electrophysiological test has been developed for ET, polymyographic recordings can reveal rhythmic, unidirectional discharges with alteration between agonist and antagonist muscles, a classic EMG pattern of tremor,
26
, 
79
 which differs from the pattern seen in FAME.

Neuroimaging and pathological findings offer valuable insights into the pathophysiological mechanism of ET, but these findings are not yet sufficient for clinical application. Furthermore, postmortem studies have revealed loss and morphological alterations of Purkinje cells in ET,
66
 a phenomenon also observed in FAME.
38
, 
56
 As a result, the involvement of the cerebellar–thalamic–cortical loop has been suggested in ET, although the precise pathogenesis remains poorly understood.

In terms of treatment, propranolol is the first‐line US Food and Drug Administration‐approved medication. Other effective treatment options for ET include primidone, topiramate, gabapentin, and sodium channel blockers (e.g., carbamazepine).

FAME and PME share similar clinical features, including action‐induced myoclonus and GTCSs.
80
 Distinguishing PME from FAME is crucial, as their long‐term prognoses and therapeutic strategies differ significantly. PME typically begins in later childhood or adolescence,
69
 and most PMEs have an autosomal recessive (AR) inheritance pattern. A defining distinction between PME and FAME is the progressive nature of the disease. PME is characterized by the progressive worsening of myoclonus, seizures, and other symptoms, such as ataxia and cognitive decline, leading to severe functional impairment over time. For example, Lafora disease, a common cause of PME, has a median survival of 11 years.
81
 In contrast, FAME typically remains stable, with only mild progression of symptoms over the years.

PME encompasses several forms, with Unverricht–Lundborg disease (ULD) being the most prevalent type. ULD is associated with a relatively benign disease course, making it a key consideration in the differential diagnosis between PME and FAME. Other common causes of PME include neuronal ceroid lipofuscinoses, myoclonic epilepsy and ataxia due to KCNC1 mutation, myoclonic epilepsy associated with ragged red fibers (MERRF), and sialidosis.

ULD is an AR disorder caused by pathogenetic mutations in the CSTB gene, with approximately 90% of disease‐causing alleles featuring a dodecamer‐repeat expansion in this gene.
67
 The typical onset of ULD occurs in late childhood to adolescence, peaking around ages 12–13 years.
69
 Action myoclonus, predominantly affecting the proximal muscles, is the hallmark feature at onset and the primary cause of significant functional disability in ULD patients. This often leads to the inability to walk by the fourth to fifth decade of life.
69
 Epileptic seizures, particularly GTCSs in approximately 50% of cases,
77
 are another common symptom. Photosensitivity is also frequently observed in patients with PMEs. Other neurological symptoms include ataxia (most common), cognitive impairment, dysarthria, and psychiatric disorders. IEDs typically present as generalized spike‐and‐wave patterns on EEG.
77
 TMS‐EMG studies have demonstrated decreased γ‐aminobutyric acidergic (GABAergic) inhibition in ULD patients, specifically impaired short‐interval and long‐interval intracortical inhibition, which contributes to cortical hyperexcitability.
82
 Correspondingly, g‐SEPs have also been reported in many patients with PMEs.
69



Currently, ASMs, including valproate, benzodiazepines, piracetam, and levetiracetam,
83
 remain the cornerstone of symptomatic treatment for PMEs. However, lamotrigine should be used cautiously, as it may exacerbate myoclonus, and valproic acid should be avoided in patients with MERRF due to its inhibition of carnitine uptake.
83



JME is an epilepsy syndrome that shares clinical features with FAME, such as epileptic seizures and myoclonic jerks. However, key clinical differences are crucial for distinguishing FAME from JME. JME typically presents with onset in adolescence.
70
, 
71
 Among the three common seizure types associated with JME, GTCSs are the most frequently observed, occurring in 80%–95% of JME patients, whereas absence seizures are seen in 15%–30%.
70
 Notably, myoclonic seizures, which typically occur shortly after awakening, are considered the hallmark feature of JME and are essential for its diagnosis. Interestingly, a reduction in myoclonic seizures during the fourth decade of life has been reported in the majority of patients with JME.
84
 Cognition is generally preserved in JME patients, although subtle frontal lobe dysfunction is frequently observed. Cortical myoclonus in JME predominantly occurs spontaneously in the proximal upper limbs and is associated with epileptiform discharges.
85
 A distinct electrophysiological feature of JME, as revealed by EEG recordings, is the presence of a normal background accompanied by 3–6‐Hz generalized spike–wave discharges with frontal predominance, commonly observed at sleep onset or upon awakening.
74
 Moreover, PPR can be induced in most patients with enhanced and prolonged photic stimulation,
86
 which supports the demonstration of photosensitivity and suggests the underlying upregulated occipitofrontal networks in its pathophysiological mechanism.

Valproate is widely regarded as the most effective and commonly used ASM for JME,
64
, 
70
, 
87
 followed by levetiracetam. However, valproate can cause drug‐induced tremor as a side effect, which further confounds the differential diagnosis between JME and FAME. In terms of treatment response, approximately 35% of JME patients are refractory to therapy, and 78% of individuals who discontinue medication after achieving seizure freedom experience a seizure recurrence.
76



The clinical management of FAME currently centers on symptomatic treatment using ASMs. Valproate and levetiracetam are commonly prescribed as first‐line treatments, often in combination with benzodiazepines, particularly clonazepam, which has a longer half‐life compared to others.
88
 Perampanel has also emerged as a promising option.
41
 It has been demonstrated that low‐dose perampanel treatment exhibits significant antimyoclonic efficacy, as evidenced by reduced Unified Myoclonus Rating Scale scores, and decreases sensorimotor hyperexcitability manifested through diminished N33 amplitudes, attenuated glutamatergic facilitation, and enhanced GABAergic inhibition in the motor cortex.
89
 Alternatively, FAME patients may opt for on‐demand treatment with benzodiazepines, taking them only when prodromal symptoms appear, rather than adhering to continuous regimens.

Conversely, gabapentin and sodium channel blockers are generally contraindicated, as they may worsen myoclonus and increase the risk of status epilepticus.
90
 However, our previous study found no evidence of exacerbated cortical myoclonus or seizures in FAME patients treated with sodium channel blockers.
22
 Further research with larger cohorts is needed to better understand the efficacy and potential side effects of these drugs in FAME.

FAME is a noncoding repeat expansion disorder characterized clinically by tremorlike cortical myoclonus and infrequent epileptic seizures. Molecular genetic testing is crucial for a definitive diagnosis of FAME. However, the variability in length, configuration, and location of repeat expansions, combined with the limited feasibility of widespread WGS, presents significant challenges to the effectiveness of genetic testing in clinical diagnostics. We propose an integrated and clinically oriented diagnostic workflow for FAME, comprising four key components: clinical assessment, electrophysiological tests, neuroimaging studies, and genetic analysis. Patients with supportive clinical symptoms and neurophysiological features, and no exclusionary findings on neuroimaging, should proceed to genetic analysis for definitive confirmation. Early identification and appropriate management of FAME can prevent inappropriate medication use and improve patients' quality of life.
