BACKGROUND: Kidney transplantation is the preferred treatment for end-stage
renal disease (ESRD), yet challenges persist in long-term graft survival and
post-transplant complications. Genomic profiling, especially whole-exome
sequencing (WES), offers new opportunities to personalize donor-recipient
matching and predict transplant outcomes. This study aimed to explore the
genetic landscape of both transplant recipients and donors using WES.
METHODS: This prospective cohort study included 64 kidney transplant recipients
(51 adults, 13 pediatric) and 61 donors in Kazakhstan. WES was performed to
identify monogenic causes of ESRD, evaluate rejection-associated immune
polymorphisms (31 SNPs), and screen for post-transplant risk using a 355-gene
Transplant Morbidity Panel. Variants were classified following ACMG/AMP
guidelines, and allele frequencies were compared to global reference datasets.
RESULTS: Among recipients, pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants were found in
15.4% of pediatric and 9.1% of adult cases, with additional variants of
uncertain significance detected in 23.1% and 23.6%, respectively. WES identified
monogenic nephropathies including Alport syndrome, cystinuria, and polycystic
kidney disease. In donors, 13.1% carried variants associated with latent renal
or systemic conditions despite no clinical manifestation at donation. The APOE
p.Cys130Arg variant, linked to lipid dysregulation, was observed more frequently
in recipients (allele frequency 0.17) than donors (0.09), though not
statistically significant. Among the 31 SNPs evaluated, significant associations
with acute rejection were observed for IL1B rs1143634 and TP53 rs1625895 under
dominant models. IL1B carriers showed increased rejection risk (OR = 4.62,
p = 0.039), whereas TP53 carriers appeared protected (OR = 0.058, p = 0.027).
Given the limited number of rejection cases, these findings should be considered
exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts. Additionally, 44.3% of
recipients carried at least one pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in the
Transplant Morbidity Panel, particularly in genes related to malignancy,
cardiomyopathy, and monogenic diabetes.
CONCLUSIONS: This study provides one of the first applications of WES in a
Central Asian kidney transplant population. Findings highlight the prevalence of
monogenic and comorbid risk variants in both recipients and donors and support
the use of genomic screening for improving pre- and post-transplant care. The
identification of immune-related SNPs and extra-renal findings further
underscores the utility of WES for personalized transplant management.
Integration of genomics into transplant workflows may help address the ongoing
gap between clinical need and transplant outcomes, particularly in low-resource
settings.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 9.5% of the world’s population and remains a major global health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where early detection and management are limited [1, 2]. In Kazakhstan, CKD prevalence and mortality have risen sharply since 2014, with projections exceeding 1.5 million prevalent cases and over 37,000 deaths by 2030 [3]. As CKD progresses to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), patients require dialysis or kidney transplantation. Although transplantation is the preferred therapy, access remains limited – over 3,600 adults and 79 children are currently on waiting lists in Kazakhstan despite expanded dialysis coverage [4–6]. These gaps underscore the need for strategies that can improve long-term outcomes, including genomic testing to better define disease etiology and guide personalized post-transplant care.

Despite progress in surgical and immunosuppressive protocols, long-term kidney graft survival has plateaued [7]. Chronic rejection, cardiovascular disease, infection, and malignancy continue to compromise graft function [8, 9]. Conventional immunological screening (HLA matching, antibody testing) helps reduce acute rejection but offers limited predictive value for long-term outcomes and neglects non-HLA genetic variation [10, 11]. For example, allogenomics mismatch scores derived from whole-exome sequencing (WES) correlate with long-term graft function independent of HLA loci [12].

Genomic technologies such as WES and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have expanded understanding of monogenic and polygenic contributors to transplant outcomes. Variants have been linked to new-onset diabetes, cardiovascular disease, skin cancers, and acute rejection [10]. Targeted exome panels identified actionable variants in 9.1% of recipients, enabling more tailored post-transplant management [9], while next-generation sequencing (NGS) is increasingly recommended for living donors with familial kidney disease risk [13–15].

In this study, we applied WES to both kidney transplant donors and recipients to explore the genetic architecture of ESRD and to assess potential risk variants influencing post-transplant complications. In recipients, WES was used to identify monogenic causes of ESRD and variants related to comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, infection, and malignancy. In donors, WES was applied to detect pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants associated with hereditary kidney disease, including subclinical risks relevant to living-donor selection and ethical evaluation.

This work expands upon our prior publication describing the protocol and preliminary results from a smaller donor–recipient cohort [16]. Here, we substantially increase the sample size and integrate a targeted Transplant Morbidity Gene Panel and an allograft rejection-associated SNP panel alongside exome-wide analyses. Together, these enhancements enable a more comprehensive evaluation of monogenic ESRD etiologies, donor-derived risk variants, and recipient-specific genetic factors potentially predisposing to rejection, thereby strengthening the translational relevance of WES in kidney transplantation.

This study is a prospective cohort study involving pediatric and adult kidney transplant recipients and their respective living or deceased donors. The research was conducted collaboratively between the “University Medical Center” Corporate Fund and the National Research Oncology Center, both located in Astana, Kazakhstan. The study period spanned from October 2023 to April 2025, during which participants were enrolled, blood samples were collected for genomic analysis, and each kidney transplant recipient underwent at least six months of clinical follow-up post-transplantation as part of a broader investigation into the integration of genomic profiling and monitoring in kidney transplantation.

This study was conducted in full compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, as well as the national ethical regulations governing biomedical research in Kazakhstan. Ethical approval was obtained from the Local Bioethics Committee of the “University Medical Center” Corporate Fund (Protocol No. 3, dated July 14, 2023). All procedures were designed to ensure the protection of participants’ rights, including the maintenance of confidentiality and anonymity throughout the study.

Written informed consent was obtained from all living participants and from the participants’ legal guardians for individuals younger than the age of 18. The consent process included all living donors and recipients (or their legal guardians when applicable). The consent procedures were carried out at the “University Medical Center” Corporate Fund and the National Research Oncology Center (Astana, Kazakhstan), where transplantation and post-transplant monitoring were conducted.

For deceased donors, organ procurement and the use of associated biological materials were conducted in accordance with Kazakhstan’s legal and ethical frameworks. Informed consent was obtained from the deceased donors’ legal representatives (parents or legal guardians), including consent for data use and publication. As part of standard national procedures, biological samples from deceased donors were transferred to the Research and Production Center of Transfusiology (Astana, Kazakhstan) for HLA typing and compatibility assessment. Written informed consent from the donors’ legal representatives was obtained prior to organ retrieval, explicitly authorizing the use of biological samples and test results for scientific and statistical research, with strict protections in place for personal data.

Genomic counseling and return of results. In accordance with our ethical approval and consent procedures, only pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants relevant to kidney disease were disclosed to recipients and referred for post-test genetic counseling. Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) were not reported and were not used in clinical decision-making. No genomic results were returned to living donors, as disclosure of donor findings was outside the scope of the study protocol. This framework was designed to provide clinically meaningful information for recipients while protecting donor privacy and minimizing the risk of undue anxiety or inappropriate exclusion based on uncertain or incidental findings. This approach was chosen to avoid unintended clinical consequences in the absence of a standardized framework for disclosure of genomic findings in living donors.

All study procedures upheld internationally recognized ethical principles, emphasizing voluntary participation, respect for donor autonomy, and rigorous protection of participant privacy and data integrity.

At the beginning of the study, genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from both donors and recipients. Peripheral blood samples were collected during the hospitalization for transplantation (prior to discharge) and subsequently used for DNA extraction and WES. Peripheral blood samples of 2 mL were collected and processed using the PureLink™ Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantification and purity of gDNA were assessed by measuring A260/A280 absorbance ratios using a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All extracted DNA samples were stored at -80 °C, with sample quality and integrity monitored regularly in accordance with standard biobanking protocols to ensure suitability for downstream genomic and molecular analyses.

WES was performed using genomic DNA extracted from donors and recipients. The quantity of gDNA was measured using the QuantiFluor® dsDNA System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) on a Victor Nivo Multimode Microplate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). DNA integrity was assessed using either the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer or the Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), with the DNA Integrity Number providing a standardized measure of DNA fragmentation based on fragment size distribution. Library preparation and exome capture were conducted using the Twist Human Core Exome (+ RefSeq) Kit, targeting > 70× coverage and generating approximately 6 Gb of sequencing data per sample. These procedures were carried out at Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Final library validation was performed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the DNA 1000 chip, as well as quantitative PCR according to Illumina’s standard quality control procedures.

Sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Base calling was processed in real-time using Illumina’s RTA software, and binary base call (BCL) files were subsequently converted to FASTQ format using bcl2fastq v2.20.0. Paired-end reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38, UCSC) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM) algorithm. Post-alignment processing included base quality score recalibration (BQSR) to enhance the accuracy of variant calling. Variants, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (InDels), were identified using the HaplotypeCaller module of the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK). For zygosity classification, heterozygous variants were defined as those with an alternate allele fraction between 0.3 and 0.7, while variants with allele fractions ≥ 0.8 were considered homozygous.

Genetic variants identified through WES were annotated according to the nomen-clature guidelines set by the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS; http://www.hgvs.org/mutnomen). Interpretation of variant pathogenicity followed the five-tier classification system recommended by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP), which in-cludes classification into benign, likely benign, variants of uncertain significance (VUS), likely pathogenic, and pathogenic categories.

Variant analysis and classification were performed using the Franklin platform (Franklin by Genoox, Genoox, Palo Alto, California, USA), applying a comprehensive three-step evaluation approach, as previously described [17, 18]. First, variants were filtered based on their minor allele frequency using multiple population databases, including gnomAD, GenomeAsia, 1000 Genomes, ExAC, and ESP 6500. Second, a detailed literature and database review was conducted to assess each variant’s potential involvement in disease etiology, referencing curated databases such as HGMD, ClinVar, UniProt, LOVD, and OMIM. Third, computational pathogenicity prediction tools were used to evaluate functional consequences in the context of the patient’s clinical phenotype. Tools included Revel, AlphaMissense, Varity, MutationAssessor, SIFT, MutationTaster, FATHMM, and DANN for missense variants; SpliceAI for splice-altering variants; and integrative scores such as GenoCanyon and fitCons for assessing regulatory or non-coding impact.

As part of the extended genomic evaluation, we performed a focused analysis of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) previously reported in the literature to be associated with allograft rejection in kidney transplant recipients. A total of 31 SNPs across 27 genes involved in immune regulation, cytokine signaling, drug metabolism, inflammation, and vascular response were selected based on prior association studies and meta-analyses. The following SNPs were specifically evaluated: ABCB1 (rs1045642, rs2032582), ACE (rs4341, rs4343), AGTR1 (rs5186), AIF1 (rs2269475), CD28 (rs3116496), CCR2 (rs1799864), CTLA4 (rs231775), CYP2E1 (rs2515641), EPHX2 (rs1042032), FCGR2A (rs1801274), ICAM1 (rs5498), IL1B (rs1143634), IL2RB (rs228942, rs228953), IL3 (rs40401), IL4 (rs2070874), IL4R (rs1801275), IMPDH2 (rs11706052), ITGB3 (rs5918), MTHFR (rs1801133), PTPN22 (rs2476601), PDCD1 (rs2227982), TGFBR2 (rs2228048), TGFB1 (rs1800470, rs1800471), TLR3 (rs3775291), TLR4 (rs4986790), TP53 (rs1625895), UGT2B7 (rs7439366) [19–21]. These SNPs were extracted from the WES data using variant filtering based on rsID and confirmed using annotation tools. Moreover, variants were analyzed for allelic distribution across the study population and further considered for correlation with clinical data related to graft function and rejection status, where available. Allele frequencies within the study population were calculated and compared with global reference frequencies from the dbSNP database to assess population-level variation and potential enrichment or depletion of relevant alleles.

To evaluate broader genomic risks associated with transplant outcomes, variants were also screened using a curated panel encompassing risk genes associated with cardiovascular complications, infection susceptibility, post-transplant malignancies, and immune-mediated graft injury, based on the panel described by Ma et al. (2024) [9]. This approach allowed for personalized genomic profiling and risk stratification of kidney transplant recipients, contributing to individualized post-transplant monitoring and therapeutic decision-making.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize clinical and genetic characteristics of the study cohort. Continuous variables, such as age, were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) and ranges, while categorical variables, including sex, transplant type, and variant classification, were reported as frequencies and percentages. For SNP-rejection association testing, we applied standard genetic models. The dominant genetic model compared carriers of at least one minor allele to non-carriers; the recessive genetic model compared homozygotes for the minor allele to heterozygotes and homozygotes for the major allele; and the additive genetic model treated the number of minor alleles as a continuous predictor. Fisher’s exact test was used for primary inference under the primary significant models. To address concerns regarding statistical power, we performed an exact Fisher’s test power analysis under a dominant genetic model (two-sided α = 0.05, target power = 0.80) using the observed event probabilities and group allocations. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 64 kidney transplant recipients (51 adults and 13 pediatric) and 61 donors were included in the main cohort of this study. In addition, preliminary data from six transplant recipients (4 adults and 2 children) were available from earlier analysis [16]. While these additional cases were not included in the analysis of the genetic landscape of end-stage kidney disease, they were included in the evaluation of allograft rejection-related genetic variants and the Transplant Morbidity Gene Panel.

The mean age at transplantation was 11.8 years (SD = 3.92, range: 6–17) for pediatric recipients and 37.3 years (SD = 9.78, range: 21–60) for adult recipients (Suppl. Tables 2 and 3). Among pediatric recipients, 8 (61.5%) were male, while among adults, 43 (84.3%) were male. The majority of transplantations were living-donor kidney transplants (LDKT), performed in 56 (87.5%) of all cases. Among children, 7 of 13 (53.8%) received LDKT, while this figure was significantly higher in adults, 49 of 51 (96.1%).

Among the study participants, 9 kidney transplant recipients experienced graft rejection, including 1 pediatric and 8 adult patients. These cases were identified based on clinical evaluation.

Among pediatric kidney recipients, the most common causes of ESRD were congenital and developmental anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) (n = 9) including kidney hypoplasia, horseshoe kidney, infravesical onstruction and vesicoureteral reflux. Two patients had glomerular diseases, 1 patient had cardiorenal syndrome and 1 patient had spinal bladder.

Among adult kidney transplant recipients (n = 51), the predominant etiology was chronic glomerulonephritis (n = 39; 76.5%), followed by hypertensive glomerulopathy (n = 4; 7.8%), IgA nephropathy (n = 2; 3.9%), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (n = 1; 2.0%), polycystic kidney disease (n = 2; 3.9%), chronic kidney disease of unspecified origin (n = 2; 3.9%), and structural uropathy (neuromuscular ureteral dysplasia with hydronephrosis; n = 1; 2.0%).

WES was conducted in kidney transplant recipients to assess the genetic etiology of ESRD. The analysis identified variants in 23 recipients (35.9%), comprising 2 pathogenic (P), 6 likely pathogenic (LP), and 15 VUS variants, as classified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines (Table 1). Segregation analysis in family members was not performed at this stage; therefore, these variants remain classified as VUS.

Among pediatric recipients (n = 13), pathogenic variants were identified in 1 patient (7.7%), likely pathogenic in 1 patient (7.7%), and VUS in 2 patients (23.1%; Fig. 2A). Notable genes included SLC3A1, COL4A3, GREB1L, DHRS9, and SOX17. One child had a pathogenic stop-gain variant in SLC3A1 (p.Arg671*), consistent with a diagnosis of cystinuria. Another one recipients carried a likely pathogenic variant in COL4A3 (p.Ser1628Ala), associated with Alport syndrome. Additionally, VUS were detected in genes such as GREB1L (p.Ala1296Thr), DHRS9 (p.Arg192Trp) and SOX17 (p.His157Gln), which may have developmental or regulatory roles but require further evidence for pathogenic classification. Overall, the pathogenic, likely pathogenic, and VUS identified in our pediatric cohort were consistent with the patients’ clinical features, although the clinical impact of VUS remains uncertain.

In adult recipients (n = 51), 1 patient (2.0%) carried a pathogenic variant, 4 patients (7.8%) carried likely pathogenic variants, and 13 patients (25.5%) harbored VUS (Fig. 2B). Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants were identified in genes including COL4A5 (p.Gly1366Val), COL4A3 (p.Gly943Arg), PKD1 (p.His2887Thrfs*107), MUC1 (p.Ala683Argfs*398), and SLC3A1 (p.Met467Thr). These are commonly associated with hereditary nephropathies such as Alport syndrome, polycystic kidney disease, cystinuria, and tubulointerstitial kidney disease. VUS variants were found in a broad range of kidney-related genes, including UMOD (p.Pro4Ser), PAX2 (p.Pro346Ser), FN1 (p.Thr1423Ala), ANLN (p.Val226Ala, c.2749-4_2749-3dup), TBC1D8B (p.Ser934Arg), SPRY2 (p.Met259Val), LAMA5 (p.Asp2034Gly and p.Ser3613Arg, compound), ACTN4 (484 + 6 A > G), MUC1 (p.Pro623_Ser639del, p.Gly878Ala, p.Asp172Glu, p.Thr173Ser), and SLC3A1 (p.Arg658His) many of which are involved in glomerular structure, tubular function, or immune signaling pathways. It is also worth noting that among adult recipients, only the patient with the PKD1 c.8659del (pathogenic) variant and the patient with the PAX2 c.1036 C > T (likely pathogenic) variant had findings consistent with their clinical diagnoses of polycystic kidney disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, respectively, while the remaining cases were clinically classified as chronic glomerulonephritis or CKD of unspecified etiology.

A duplication in CFHR5 (p.Glu163Argfs*35), associated with C3 glomerulonephritis, was also in another donor identified. Two donors carried different variants in the COL4A3 gene, specifically, p.Leu14_Leu21del and p.Gln817*, both consistent with Alport syndrome. A duplication in SEC63 (p.Pro536Thrfs*24), implicated in polycystic liver disease with or without kidney cysts, and a missense variant in UMOD (p.Val109Glu), linked to medullary cystic kidney disease with or without hyperuricemia, were each detected in separate individuals. A missense variant in the APOE gene (c.388T > C; p.Cys130Arg), associated with syndromic dyslipidemia and lipoprotein glomerulopathy, was identified in both kidney transplant recipients and donors. Among the 70 recipients, 20 were heterozygous and 2 were homozygous for the alternative C allele, corresponding to an allele frequency of 0.17. In the donor group (n = 67), 8 individuals were heterozygous and 2 were homozygous, yielding a C allele frequency of 0.09. Although the C allele appeared more frequently among recipients, the difference in allele distribution between recipients and donors was not statistically significant (p = 0.095).

Overall comparison of genotype distributions across different inheritance models (dominant, recessive, additive) identified two variants with significant associations to rejection episodes (Table 2). The strongest signals were observed for IL1B (rs1143634) under the dominant model (χ² = 4.81, p = 0.028) and for TP53 (rs1615895) under the dominant (χ² = 8.10, p = 0.004) and additive models (χ² = 8.10, p = 0.017). In Fisher’s exact test, both loci showed significant dominant-model associations (IL1B rs1143634: p = 0.043; TP53 rs1615895: p = 0.042).

Binomial logistic regression provided further insight into the direction and magnitude of these effects. For IL1B rs1143634, carriers had a significantly higher risk of acute rejection compared to non-carriers (OR = 4.62, 95% CI = 1.08–19.69, p = 0.039). In contrast, for TP53 rs1615895, non-carriers exhibited increased risk, with carriers showing a protective effect (OR = 0.058, 95% CI = 0.005–0.729, p = 0.027). Model fit statistics supported these associations (IL1B: χ² = 4.24, p = 0.040; TP53: χ² = 5.03, p = 0.025).

Exact Fisher’s power analysis demonstrated that the current cohort (N = 70; 9 rejection events) was underpowered to detect the observed SNP effects with high confidence. For TP53 rs1615895, an adequately powered study would require ~ 148 participants under the observed allocation, while for IL1B rs1143634, ~ 157 participants would be necessary. These results indicate that the present study has sufficient sensitivity only for very large effects, and single-SNP associations should be interpreted as exploratory.

This extended genetic screening focused on genes implicated in cardiometabolic disorders, immune dysfunction, malignancies, and thrombophilic conditions. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in multiple clinically relevant categories.

Among the 70 kidney transplant recipients analyzed using the Transplant Morbidity Gene Panel, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified across multiple clinical categories. Variants linked to monogenic forms of diabetes were detected in six recipients. These included two individuals each with variants in GATA4 (p.Pro163Ser) and HNF4A (p.Leu319del), and one individual each with variants in SLC40A1 (p.Ser209Leu) and KCNJ11 (p.Val44Ala). Hypertension-related gene variants were identified in four cases, including three carriers of WNK1 (p.Gln584Alafs*27) and one carrier of WNK4 (p.Ala613Leufs*48). A single case with a variant associated with dyslipidemia was also identified (ABCA1:p.Trp840Arg). Variants associated with cardiomyopathy were observed in five individuals, affecting the FBN1, LZTR1, TGFBR2, and TNNT2 genes. In addition, arrhythmia-associated variants were detected in four recipients, involving the CACNA1C, KCNH2, RYR2, and SCN5A genes. Other notable variants were identified in ATR, RNF43, TYR, CHEK2, ITGB4, PALB2, and SPINK1, many of which are implicated in hereditary cancer syndromes or cancer predisposition.

In the immunodeficiency category, two rare variants were detected: MST1 (p.Cys699Arg) and BLM (p.Glu265*), each in one individual. For thrombophilia, one case each was found to carry pathogenic variants in F5 (p.Gly449*) and MTHFR (p.Trp95Glyfs*16). Additionally, two ACMG-recommended secondary findings were reported: one in COL3A1 (p.Arg1034His), a gene associated with vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and one in RPE65 (p.Arg124*), linked to inherited retinal disorders. These results demonstrate a considerable prevalence of potentially actionable or informative genetic variants among kidney transplant recipients, with implications for personalized post-transplant management and long-term risk assessment.

WES also uncovered additional extra-renal genetic findings of potential clinical relevance. A likely-pathogenic variant in the PRSS1 gene (p.Ala16Val), which is associated with hereditary chronic pancreatitis, was identified in 64.3% of the study population, including both pediatric and adult recipients, indicating a high carrier frequency in this cohort. Notably, one adult kidney transplant recipient with a confirmed clinical diagnosis of chronic pancreatitis was among those carrying this variant, suggesting a likely genetic etiology.

During the 6-month follow-up period after transplantation, no recipients exhibited clinical manifestations attributable to the identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, including those related to early-onset conditions such as immunodeficiencies or thrombophilia. However, longer follow-up is required to determine whether such variants contribute to late post-transplant complications or comorbidities.

WES identified variants classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic/VUS in genes associated with cardiomyopathy among kidney transplant recipients. One pediatric recipient, with a known clinical history of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), was found to carry compound heterozygous missense variants in the TTN gene (p.Gln3469* and p.Glu3405Lys). TTN is a well-established gene associated with familial cardiomyopathies, and the presence of these variants provides genomic support for the clinical diagnosis and may aid in guiding long-term cardiac surveillance. Among adult recipients, three additional cases with cardiomyopathy were identified. One individual harbored a likely pathogenic variant in the FBN1 gene (p.Glu2667Lys), a gene associated with connective tissue disorders and aortic root dilation, which may contribute to cardiomyopathic changes. Another case carried a VUS variant in MYH7 (p.Asn1408Lys), a gene frequently implicated in hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathies.

This study represents one of the most comprehensive applications of WES in kidney transplantation performed to date in Central Asia, integrating genomic data from both recipients and donors to investigate the genetic etiology of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), assess potential rejection-associated polymorphisms, and evaluate risk for post-transplant complications.

When stratified by age group, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants associated with ESRD were identified in 15.4% of pediatric kidney transplant recipients (2 of 13) and 9.8% of adult recipients (5 of 51) in the primary cohort. However, when accounting for additional cases from preliminary data reported by Bayanova et al. (2025) [16], which included two pediatric and four adult recipients without identified pathogenic variants (one adult excluded due to insufficient clinical data), the overall prevalence of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants was slightly reduced. In the combined dataset, the final frequency of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants was 13.3% among pediatric cases (2 of 15) and 9.1% among adult cases (5 of 55). These findings are consistent with previously reported diagnostic yields from WES studies in both pediatric and adult populations. For instance, Feng et al. (2024) reported a 46.7% diagnostic yield in a Chinese cohort of 244 individuals with suspected monogenic kidney disease, with WES confirming the initial clinical diagnosis in 30.3% and revising it in an additional 16.4% [23]. Similarly, in a Luxembourg pediatric cohort, Caliment et al. (2024) observed an overall genetic yield of 63% (28 of 44), where WES confirmed the suspected diagnosis in 41% and clarified it in 25%, avoiding invasive biopsy in some cases [24]. Another study from Boston Children’s Hospital found 32.7% of pediatric renal transplant recipients had a genetic cause of their kidney disease identified by WES [25]. In adult populations, diagnostic yields have varied depending on clinical selection criteria. At Columbia University Medical Center, Lata et al. (2018) found a 24% diagnostic rate among 92 adults with CKD of unknown origin [26], while a multicenter study in Ireland by Connaughton et al. (2019) reported a 37% yield in a carefully selected cohort of families, with a monogenic cause found in 36% of those with a positive family history [27].

Our comparatively lower yield likely reflects the fact that we included all kidney transplant recipients during the study period without applying restrictive clinical inclusion criteria. In contrast, prior studies with stricter selection have achieved much higher diagnostic rates. For example, Lata et al. (2018) reported a 24% diagnostic yield among adults, where inclusion was limited to patients with undiagnosed CKD or clinical suspicion of genetic kidney disease, excluding those with clinically established polycystic kidney disease [26]. Connaughton et al. (2019) demonstrated that yield was strongly influenced by selection: a monogenic cause was identified in 36% of families with a positive family history, 69% of patients with extra-renal features, but only 15% of those without such features [27]. Similarly, pediatric studies have reported yields ranging from 33% to 63% when patients were selected based on age of onset, syndromic features, or clinical suspicion [23, 24]. Our data therefore highlight the need to identify clinical indicators and phenotypic features suggestive of an underlying genetic etiology to guide testing, while also recognizing that an unselected approach may uncover unexpected findings relevant to donor counseling and post-transplant management.

In our cohort, the molecular spectrum of disease-associated variants differed between pediatric and adult kidney transplant recipients, reflecting the known variability in the genetic architecture of ESRD across age groups. Among pediatric recipients, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in SLC3A1 and COL4A genes commonly associated with cystinuria and Alport syndrome, respectively. In adults, the most frequently implicated genes included COL4A5, COL4A3, MUC1, PKD1, and SLC3A1, corresponding to well-established causes of hereditary glomerular disorders, autosomal dominant tubulointerstitial kidney disease, and polycystic kidney disease. It is also important to note that 3 of 15 pediatric patients (20.0%) and 13 of 55 adult patients (23.6%) carried VUS variants in genes with plausible relevance to kidney disease. These included GREB1L, DHRS9 and SOX17 in children, genes involved in renal development and metabolic regulation, and SPRY2, UMOD, TBC1D8B, PAX2, MUC1, FN1, ANLN, SLC3A1, LAMA5, and ACTN4 in adults. While the clinical significance of these variants remains unresolved, many are located in genes previously linked to glomerulopathies, hereditary tubulopathies, or developmental renal anomalies. Their presence highlights the diagnostic and interpretative challenges of genomic testing in nephrology, particularly in transplant recipients with complex or overlapping phenotypes.

Overall, the pathogenic, likely pathogenic, and VUS identified in our pediatric cohort were broadly consistent with the patients’ clinical features, although the clinical impact of VUS remains uncertain. In adults, pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants in PKD1 and PAX2 corresponded to clinical diagnoses of polycystic kidney disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, respectively, while most other cases were clinically classified as chronic glomerulonephritis or CKD of unspecified etiology. Importantly, several VUS occurred in genes with established roles in renal pathology (e.g., GREB1L in renal hypodysplasia/aplasia, DHRS9 in cardiorenal syndrome, SOX17 in vesicoureteral reflux, and UMOD, PAX2, ACTN4, ANLN, and MUC1 in glomerular or tubulointerstitial disease), suggesting potential genotype-phenotype correlations even where causality cannot yet be established. These observations strengthen the interpretative value of WES but also highlight the challenge of managing uncertain findings.

The absence of functional validation further limits the immediate clinical actionability of VUS, as their biological relevance and contribution to disease risk cannot yet be confirmed. We did not perform segregation studies in relatives, which could have provided additional evidence toward reclassification. Future studies incorporating family-based analyses will therefore be essential to refine the pathogenicity of these variants. Moreover, addressing this gap will require functional studies, such as in vitro assays, patient-derived models, and integration with transcriptomic or proteomic data, to distinguish benign from pathogenic effects. Collectively, these results underscore the potential of WES not only to identify actionable monogenic causes of ESRD but also to uncover candidate variants that warrant deeper investigation.

In our study, WES identified pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants or VUS in 13.1% of kidney donors. These included variants in genes associated with subclinical or latent kidney disease risk, such as COL4A3, COL4A5, UMOD, and SLC3A1, as well as systemic disorders like monogenic diabetes (HNF4A) and C3 glomerulonephritis (CFHR5). Notably, none of the donors exhibited overt renal disease at the time of donation. However, the presence of such variants in asymptomatic individuals underscores the potential utility of pre-donation genetic screening to enhance risk stratification, support donor counseling, and minimize the transmission of recessive or dominantly inherited alleles to recipients. For instance, COL4A3 variants can follow either autosomal dominant inheritance (OMIM: 104200) or autosomal recessive inheritance (OMIM: 620536). Thus, identifying heterozygous carriers among donors is particularly important, as disease expression and transmission risk may differ depending on the inheritance model.

In our cohort, none of the donors carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants developed renal impairment or proteinuria during the available 6-month follow-up. Nevertheless, long-term risk cannot be excluded, and prior studies have shown that related living kidney donors may have an elevated risk of developing ESRD, especially in families with known or suspected monogenic renal disease. For example, Thomas et al. (2017) demonstrated that targeted panel testing of 115 renal disease genes helped identify causal mutations in transplant candidates and guided safe donor selection, excluding pathogenic variants in relatives who were otherwise acceptable as donors [28]. Similarly, cases reported by Winn et al. (1999) described related donors who developed biopsy-proven FSGS and subsequent ESRD despite appearing healthy at the time of donation [29]. These examples underscore the importance of genetic caution when evaluating first-degree relatives, particularly siblings, in the setting of familial nephropathy. The clinical relevance of genetic risk is further exemplified by the case reported by Kofman et al. (2014), in which monozygotic twin brothers, both heterozygous for APOL1 G1 and G2 risk alleles, experienced progressive renal decline, one as a transplant recipient and the other as the donor, despite the absence of renal disease at baseline [30].

Although no clinical manifestations were observed in variant-positive donors during the 6-month follow-up, this short timeframe does not exclude the possibility of later renal decline or disease onset. Prospective studies with structured long-term monitoring will be critical to determine whether donor-carried variants ultimately translate into clinically relevant outcomes. In this context, the integration of genomic analysis into pre-donation evaluation protocols, especially for related donors, appears warranted. WES or targeted gene panels can serve as valuable tools for identifying hidden genetic risks in otherwise healthy individuals, thereby facilitating safer transplant decisions, optimizing long-term outcomes, and aligning with the ethical imperative to protect donor health.

The APOE c.388T >C (p.Cys130Arg) variant, linked to syndromic dyslipidemia and lipoprotein glomerulopathy, was more frequent in kidney transplant recipients (allele frequency: 0.17) than donors (0.09), though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.095). Both frequencies exceeded the global reference (0.07), with the variant known to be more prevalent in African populations (0.13). Previous studies have associated APOE polymorphisms with diabetic nephropathy [31] and lipoprotein distribution in CKD [32], supporting its relevance to renal and metabolic risk. Our findings indicate a possible trend toward enrichment of APOE c.388T >C in recipients compared to donors, but the modest difference and lack of statistical significance highlight that this observation should be interpreted with caution. Validation in larger and more diverse cohorts will be necessary before drawing conclusions about its relevance to transplant outcomes.

Regarding immune-related polymorphisms, we analyzed 31 SNPs previously reported to be associated with kidney allograft rejection [19–21]. Among them TP53 (rs1625895) and IL1B (rs1143634) demonstrated a statistically significant association with rejection episodes. Interestingly, in our cohort, the TP53 (rs1625895) T allele, commonly considered the reference variant, was more frequently observed in recipients who experienced graft rejection, suggesting a possible population-specific immunogenetic influence. Although this finding requires validation in larger and more diverse cohorts, it raises the possibility that TP53-mediated immune regulation, particularly through apoptotic pathways, may contribute to transplant outcomes. This observation is supported by prior evidence that links TP53 function to graft injury through apoptosis, especially in the context of ischemia-reperfusion injury. For example, Azarpira et al. (2014) examined the association between TP53 rs1625895 and rejection in a cohort of 100 renal allograft recipients and found that while genotype frequencies were similar between patients and healthy controls, the WW genotype (corresponding to the wild-type TP53 allele) was more frequent in recipients with acute rejection [33]. Their findings suggest that wild-type p53 may exacerbate apoptosis in response to inflammatory stimuli, potentially worsening allograft outcomes. Mechanistic studies of ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) support this interpretation [33, 34]. IRI is marked by oxidative stress and inflammation, with apoptosis as a central pathway in which TP53 plays a key role [35, 36]. Wever et al. (1998) further demonstrated tubular expression of p53 during acute rejection, consistent with p53-driven apoptosis [37]. Together, these findings indicate that TP53 activation under ischemic and inflammatory stress may accelerate apoptosis and worsen graft outcomes.

Moreover, we observed a significant association between IL1B rs1143634 and acute rejection, with carriers showing an increased risk under a dominant model (OR = 4.62, p < 0.05). This aligns with earlier reports that the rs1143634 T allele enhances IL-1β secretion [38] and may predispose to rejection. Manchanda and Mittal (2008) [39] similarly found higher rejection rates in carriers, and Bhat et al. (2017) [40] reported a comparable trend, though not statistically significant due to limited sample size. Not all studies have confirmed this effect; for example, Hu et al. (2019) reported no association in a larger tacrolimus-based cohort [41]. The discrepancy may reflect differences in sample size, population, and immunosuppressive protocols. Given that our findings are based on only nine rejection cases, they should be considered exploratory. Overall, our data add to evidence suggesting a possible role for TP53 rs1625895 and IL1B rs1143634 in transplant outcomes. Future validation in larger, multi-center cohorts, complemented by transcriptomic and immunohistochemical analyses of p53 activity during rejection, will be essential to clarify the role of TP53 rs1625895 in transplantation. Similarly, the observed association of IL1B rs1143634 with acute rejection warrants replication in broader populations, ideally integrating cytokine profiling and functional assays of IL-1β secretion to better establish its contribution to allograft outcomes.

In this study, SNP analysis was restricted to 31 pre-selected variants previously associated with transplant rejection, due to the limited number of rejection cases. While this candidate-gene approach identified associations with TP53 rs1625895 and IL1B rs1143634, it does not capture the broader spectrum of potential genetic risk factors. Larger multicenter cohorts will be required to leverage unbiased, genome-wide analyses of rejection events with appropriate multiple-testing correction, which may reveal novel susceptibility loci and enable more robust genetic risk modeling.

A novel aspect of our study was the use of a Transplant Morbidity Gene Panel (355 genes), previously developed by Ma et al. (2024), to screen for monogenic conditions associated with post-transplant complications [9]. In our cohort, 44.3% of recipients carried at least one pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant, most commonly in genes linked to malignancy (n = 10), monogenic diabetes (n = 6), cardiomyopathy (n = 5), and hypertension or arrhythmia (n = 4). These results are consistent with Ma et al.’s larger study, where diagnostic variants were found in 9.1% of 1590 kidney transplant recipients, enabling actionable interventions such as surveillance, specialist referral, and immunosuppression adjustment [9]. Our findings support the broader utility of this panel to uncover hidden comorbidities and improve personalized management, especially in settings where genetic risk screening is not routinely performed. In our analysis, the Transplant Morbidity Gene Panel was applied only to recipient samples, whereas donor sequencing was focused on variants associated with ESRD or latent kidney disease risk. Consequently, genes with known implications for graft injury or peri-transplant management, such as APOL1 and thrombophilia-associated genes, were not systematically evaluated in donors. Future studies should incorporate these variants into donor analyses, as their presence may influence graft survival and inform perioperative strategies, including anticoagulation protocols.

Importantly, WES also revealed extra-renal findings, most notably the PRSS1 p.Ala16Val variant, which was present at a high frequency (64.3%) in our cohort. PRSS1 mutations are well recognized in hereditary pancreatitis (HP), with high-penetrance variants such as p.Asn29Ile and p.Arg122His, and lower-penetrance variants including p.Ala16Val, p.Asp22Gly, p.Lys23Arg, p.Asn29Thr, and p.Arg122Cys [42]. The reported penetrance of PRSS1-related HP varies, and for p.Ala16Val it has been estimated at 43% in European cohorts [43]. Mechanistic studies show that the A16V mutant produces only a modest increase in autoactivation, consistent with its variable penetrance. The basis of increased activation appears mutation-specific, involving either resistance to degradation (N29I, N29T, V39A, R122C, R122H) or increased N-terminal processing by CTRC (A16V and N29I) [44]. In line with this, only one recipient in our study carrying the p.Ala16Val allele had clinically confirmed chronic pancreatitis, suggesting that this variant represents a low-penetrance risk factor whose expression likely depends on additional genetic or environmental triggers. The PRSS1 p.Ala16Val variant was identified at a higher apparent frequency than expected, with the reference allele (C) at 0.68 in our cohort compared to 0.89 in dbSNP (Global). This frequency is closer to that reported in Latin American populations. However, no Central Asian reference databases are currently available for direct comparison. This gap highlights the need for the development of regional genomic reference resources to better contextualize genetic findings in Central Asian populations and to improve the accuracy of variant interpretation in clinical and research settings.

Beyond PRSS1, WES also identified variants in TTN and FBN1 related to familial cardiomyopathies. In one pediatric recipient with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), compound heterozygous TTN variants supported the genetic basis of disease, while in adult recipients, additional cardiomyopathy-related variants were observed in FBN1, MYH7, and TTN. Together, these findings highlight the potential diagnostic value of WES for transplant-related risk assessment while also highlighting its role in uncovering extra-renal genetic predispositions that may inform long-term surveillance and management.

Together, our results demonstrate the feasibility and potential value of integrating WES into kidney-transplantation workflows, not only to clarify ESRD etiology, assess rejection risk, and evaluate donor-recipient compatibility and post-transplant comorbidities. Given the diversity of genetic findings across recipients and donors, WES may serve as a foundation for future research toward personalized transplant medicine. However, these implications should be viewed cautiously. Although promising, our findings are best interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating; the short-term follow-up and descriptive design are not sufficient to support conclusions about clinical management or patient outcomes. This study therefore provides an initial framework for identifying genetic variants of interest that warrant validation in larger, longitudinal, multi-center cohorts, ideally integrating long-term outcomes, functional assays, and family-based analyses.

In the broader context of transplant genomics, emerging evidence illustrates how molecular profiling may inform post-transplant care while underscoring the need for validation before routine implementation. For example, recurrence of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) after transplantation has been linked to variants in A2M, EXPH5, and PLEC, together with elevated IL-17 levels, suggesting candidate biomarkers for early identification and targeted intervention [45]. In the malignancy setting, WES has identified distinct genetic profiles of urothelial carcinoma in kidney-transplant recipients versus patients on dialysis, pointing to potentially different oncogenic mechanisms and surveillance considerations [46]. With respect to donor selection, APOL1 genotyping is now technically accessible; however, its role in eligibility remains uncertain because of ethical concerns and limited predictive clarity, and penetrance appears low and likely modified by secondary (environmental or genetic) hits [47–49]. Outside of transplantation, several studies show that WES can achieve moderate-to-high diagnostic yields and occasionally impact management decisions in carefully selected cohorts – for instance, Jung et al. (2023) reported a 36.6% yield across diverse kidney diseases with reported effects on diagnosis/management [50], and Aghamir et al. (2023) identified pathogenic variants associated with poorer graft survival in patients with prior rejection, highlighting a potential role for risk stratification that requires replication [51]. Reviews implicating immune-related polymorphisms in viral susceptibility further offer hypothesis-generating avenues to optimize post-transplant outcomes [52, 53]. Taken together, these data provide context for our findings while reinforcing that translation to routine clinical practice should await larger, longitudinal validations.

While resource constraints remain a barrier to genomic medicine, several factors support the feasibility of WES integration even in low- and middle-income countries. First, the cost of WES has declined significantly, often approaching the cumulative cost of sequential single-gene tests, making it a cost-efficient option when evaluating complex conditions such as ESRD. Second, scalable infrastructure models are emerging: DNA extraction and library preparation can be performed locally, while sequencing and bioinformatic pipelines may be outsourced to regional or international facilities, as demonstrated in our study. Third, cloud-based analytic platforms and standardized variant-interpretation frameworks reduce the need for high-maintenance local computational infrastructure. Finally, WES provides a single, comprehensive test that can address multiple diagnostic and risk-assessment needs in both donors and recipients, thereby maximizing yield from limited resources. Collectively, these factors suggest that implementation of WES in low-resource settings is increasingly practical, provided that efforts are paired with training, counseling, and international collaboration.

The implementation of WES in kidney transplantation raises important ethical questions, particularly regarding the management of incidental findings in living donors. In this study, we adopted a conservative framework: only P/LP variants relevant to kidney disease were returned to recipients, who were subsequently referred for genetic counseling. In contrast, donor WES findings were not reported back. This approach avoids potential psychosocial and clinical consequences in living donors and acknowledges the absence of widely accepted policies for the return of genomic findings in this setting. While limiting disclosure in this way ensured clarity and ethical consistency, it also highlights the need for future work to establish structured frameworks for donor result disclosure, ideally including genetic counseling and long-term follow-up.

Beyond clarifying the etiology of ESRD, WES uncovered secondary findings with implications for post-transplant management. Variants in hereditary cancer predisposition genes such as CHEK2, PALB2, ATR, and RNF43 were reported to the treating clinicians, enabling them to consider enhanced malignancy surveillance given the heightened cancer risk under immunosuppression [54]. Likewise, cardiomyopathy-associated variants (including compound heterozygous TTN variants in one pediatric case and an FBN1 variant in an adult) were communicated to clinicians to guide cardiac monitoring and long-term follow-up. Similarly, monogenic diabetes–associated variants in genes such as HNF4A and KCNJ11 were referred to clinicians for individualized metabolic follow-up. In each case, secondary findings were shared only with the clinical team rather than directly with recipients, ensuring that actionable information was available to support surveillance and comorbidity management in the post-transplant period while respecting the ethical boundaries of the study protocol.

This study has several limitations. First, although WES provided valuable insights into the genetic landscape of kidney transplant recipients and donors, functional validation of the identified variants, particularly VUS variants, was not performed. As a result, the clinical impact of some findings remains speculative. Future work will include reanalysis of VUS and family-based studies to refine pathogenicity assessments. Second, the sample size, especially for pediatric recipients and rejection cases, was relatively limited, constraining statistical power. Exact Fisher’s power analysis under dominant models confirmed that substantially larger sample sizes would be required to robustly evaluate SNP associations (e.g., ~ 148 participants for TP53 rs1615895 and ~ 157 for IL1B rs1143634). Thus, the present single-SNP findings should be regarded as preliminary and validated in larger or multi-center cohorts. Moreover, given that the majority of transplants were from living donors, stratified analysis by donor type was not feasible. Although all patients received standard tacrolimus-based immunosuppression, therapeutic drug levels were not systematically analyzed, which may have limited our ability to fully assess pharmacogenetic or pharmacokinetic influences on rejection. Third, donor follow-up data regarding long-term health outcomes were not available, preventing assessment of whether identified donor variants contribute to post-donation morbidity. Fourth, transcriptomic, epigenetic, or environmental data were not integrated, limiting our ability to capture gene-environment interactions or regulatory mechanisms that may influence graft function. Fifth, although our return-of-results framework disclosed pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants relevant to kidney disease in recipients with referral to genetic counseling, donor WES results were not reported back. This conservative approach minimized potential psychosocial and clinical risks for donors but restricted our ability to evaluate the impact of disclosure on donor decision-making and long-term outcomes. Finally, the study was conducted in a single national cohort, which may limit the generalizability of results to more diverse populations.

Future research should include larger, multi-center cohorts with extended follow-up to better evaluate the predictive value of WES findings for graft survival and complications. In particular, longitudinal studies linking genetic risk with clinical outcomes, such as rejection episodes, cardiovascular events, or post-transplant malignancies, will be essential. Functional studies are also needed to clarify the pathogenicity of novel or population-specific variants. Moreover, integrating WES with other omics platforms (e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics) and machine learning approaches may enhance personalized risk stratification and decision-making in kidney transplantation. Finally, research on the ethical, psychosocial, and health policy implications of genomic screening in living donors is critical to ensure responsible implementation in clinical practice.

This study demonstrates the feasibility and clinical relevance of implementing whole-exome sequencing in the context of kidney transplantation. By analyzing transplant recipients and donors, we identified a considerable number of variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in genes linked to monogenic kidney diseases, post-transplant complications, and comorbid conditions such as cardiomyopathy. Over one-third of recipients carried variants related to ESRD etiology, and a substantial proportion (44.3%) harbored actionable or informative findings in genes associated with transplant morbidity. Notably, genetic variants were also detected in donors, underscoring the importance of genomic screening in pre-transplant evaluations.

Our findings support the integration of WES into routine transplant workflows to enhance diagnostic precision, personalize long-term care, and improve risk stratification for both recipients and donors. As genomic medicine continues to evolve, future efforts should focus on scaling up such approaches through multicenter collaborations, functional validation, and incorporation into national transplant policy frameworks.
