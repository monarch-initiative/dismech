# Peripheral Immune Challenge Drives Enteric α-Synuclein and Tau Pathology in LRRK2 G2019S Mice.
**Authors:** Giachino C, Bertoli F, Tirolo C, Giuliano C, Mearelli M, Jakobi M, Schneiderhan-Marra N, L'Episcopo F, Deleidi M
**Journal:** Aging Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.14336/AD.2026.0342](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2026.0342)

## Content

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by α-synuclein aggregation and dopaminergic neuron loss. Non-motor symptoms, including gastrointestinal dysfunction, often precede motor onset, suggesting a gut origin of pathology. LRRK2 variants increase susceptibility to both PD and Crohn's disease, pointing to a potential link between LRRK2 hyperactivity and early intestinal abnormalities; however, it remains unclear how LRRK2 contributes to enteric α-synuclein pathology and co-occurring proteinopathies. In this study, we investigated the interaction between chronic peripheral inflammation and the LRRK2 G2019S variant using a longitudinal mouse model combining ageing and repeated low-dose LPS exposure. We quantified peripheral cytokine levels, colonic immune cell infiltration (CD4⁺ T cells and macrophages), and the temporal progression of enteric α-synuclein and tau pathology across different ages. Our results showed that chronic LPS exposure induced an age-dependent increase in circulating cytokines and colonic immune infiltration in LRRK2 G2019S mice, accompanied by enhanced enteric α-synuclein accumulation and phosphorylation, alongside elevated tau phosphorylation, compared with wild-type controls. Notably, total neuronal counts remained stable, indicating a distinct pre-degenerative stage of pathology. Overall, our findings demonstrate that chronic peripheral inflammation synergizes with LRRK2 G2019S to trigger early intestinal inflammation and α-synuclein/tau co-pathology in the absence of overt neurodegeneration. These results suggest that LRRK2 acts as a critical link between systemic immunity and neuronal protein homeostasis, increasing the vulnerability of the gut to inflammatory stress. Characterizing this early pathological window provides key insights into PD initiation and highlights the gut as a promising target for early therapeutic intervention.

INTRODUCTION 
 
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is an age -related 
neurodegenerative disorder with a steadily increasing 
prevalence. The average age at diagnosis is around 60 
years, by which time 50 –60% of dopaminergic (DA) 
neurons have already been lost. This indicates that the 
pathological process begins long before the onset of 
clinical symptoms, underscoring the urgent need to 
identify early disease mechanisms to enable timely 
detection and intervention.  
Emerging evidence suggests that PD may originate in 
the gut before manifesting in the brain, implicating an 
age-dependent dysfunction of the gut-brain axis in disease

onset [1-3]. In addition to its characteristic motor 
symptoms, PD is associated with a wide spectrum of non-
motor manifestations that substantially contribute to 
disease burden, including sleep disturbances, hyposmia, 
and gastrointestinal dysfunction such as constipa tion and 
dysphagia [4]. Gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms often 
precede the clinical diagnosis of PD by years, and Lewy 
body (LB) pathology within the enteric nervous system 
(ENS) can be detected up to two decades before motor 
symptoms emerge [2, 5].  The gut harbors a complex 
network of neurons, immune cells, and microbial 
communities that communicate continuously with the 
central nervous system (CNS) via immune, metabolic, 
and neuronal pathways [6]. Disruptions in this 
bidirectional communication, driven by genetic 
susceptibility and environmental exposures, may 
contribute to PD pathogenesis. 
Among the key genetic risk factors for PD is leucine-
rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), a multi -domain protein 
with kinase and GTPase functions.  LRRK2 is highly 
expressed in both innate and adaptive immune cells and is 
tightly regulated by inflammatory stimuli [7]. Variants in 
LRRK2, particularly the G2019S variant, represent the 
most prevalent known genetic cause of autosomal 
dominant, late -onset PD and are found in 1 -2% of 
sporadic PD cases worldwide [8]. While both idiopathic 
and LRRK2 -associated PD share age -dependent 
progression of motor and non -motor symptoms [9], the 
incomplete penetrance of LRRK2 variants [9]  suggests 
that additional risk factors, such as aging, chronic 
inflammation, and environmental insults, are required to 
initiate disease. Notably, several single nucleotide 
polymorphisms (SNPs) in LRRK2 that enhance kinase 
activity have been identified as susceptibility loci for both 
PD and Crohn’s disease (CD) [10].  
Consistent with its role in intestinal inflammation, 
transgenic mice overexpressing LRRK2 show increased 
vulnerability to dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) -induced 
colitis [10-12], indicating that LRRK2 modulates mucosal 
immune responses in the gut. There is also growing 
evidence that LRRK2 plays a critical role in host defense 
mechanisms, particularly in response to enteric bacterial 
infections. LRRK2 expression is strongly induced by pro-
inflammatory cytokines, including interferon -gamma 
(IFN-γ) and type I interferons, both of which are elevated 
during infections [13-15].  
Furthermore, LRRK2 modulates inflammation in 
response to pathogens such as Salmonella enterica , 
Listeria monocytogenes, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis 
[16-18]. These findings underscore LRRK2’s function 
beyond neurons, as a key player in innate immunity, 
especially in the gut where microbial exposure is constant. 
Mechanistically, LRRK2 regulates key signaling 
pathways downstream of pattern recognition receptors 
(PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD2, 
which recognize microbial components such as 
lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and muramyl dipeptide [17]. 
Upon activation, LRRK2 modulates downstream 
effectors including NF -κB and interferon regulatory 
factors (IRFs) [19], thereby coordinating the transcription 
of cytokines, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides that 
shape the inflammatory response. Furthermore, LRRK2 
has been identified as a critical regulator of the 
transcription factor  NFAT1; this interaction modulates  
the severity of intestinal inflammation and has been 
shown to influence susceptibility to inflammatory bowel 
disease by controlling the production of pro-inflammatory 
cytokines [14, 20]. One of the most direct roles of LRRK2 
in immunity involves the regulation of intracellular 
bacterial processing. Following phagocytosis, pathogens 
are directed to phagosomes that mature and fuse with 
lysosomes to form bactericidal compartments [21]. In the 
absence of LRRK2, or in the presence of PD -associated 
variants such as G2019S, phagosome maturation is 
impaired, resulting in defective bacterial clearance and 
dysregulated cytokine production, indicative of broader 
immune dysfunction [21].  
Consistent with this, recent work has shown that 
hyperactive LRRK2 variants linked to both PD and CD 
disrupt autophagy-dependent Paneth cell homeostasis and 
promote intestinal inflammation, further highlighting the 
role of LRRK2 in regulating immune and ep ithelial 
integrity within the gut [22]. 
Although LRRK2 -PD clinically resembles sporadic 
PD, it displays heterogeneous CNS pathologies: roughly 
half of cases show cortical tau and/or LBs pathology, and 
α-synuclein oligomers can be present even in the absence 
of classic LB s [23-25]. Reflecting this variability in α -
synuclein and tau co -pathology, pathogenic LRRK2 
variants have been reported to exacerbate both α -
synuclein and tau aggregation in some animal models, 
although results differ between models [26-29]. 
Mechanistically, LRRK2 is recruited to damaged or 
stressed lysosomes, where it phosphorylates specific Rab 
GTPases to regulate vesicular trafficking and lysosomal 
homeostasis. Both α-synuclein and tau aggregates disrupt 
endolysosomal function, and their clearance relies heavily 
on lysosome -mediated degradation pathways [30]. By 
modulating Rab-dependent lysosomal dynamics, LRRK2 
may influence the accumulation and propagation of these 
pathogenic proteins, positioning it as a critical mediator of 
early α-synuclein and tau pathology in neurodegenerative 
diseases. 
Importantly, both α -synuclein and tau are highly 
expressed in the ENS and in enteroendocrine cells, where 
they contribute to neuronal integrity and gut function [31, 
32]. A growing body of evidence supports a bidirectional 
relationship between inflammation and α -synuclein

aggregation, in which immune activation not only 
responds to misfolded α -synuclein but actively promotes 
its accumulation and pathogenic conversion. Recent 
studies have demonstrated elevated colonic α -synuclein 
levels in response to acute experimental coli tis [12]. 
Notably, increased levels and aggregation of α -synuclein 
have been reported in patients with inflammatory bowel 
diseases and during a variety of enteric infections, 
suggesting that sustained mucosal inflammation creates a 
permissive environment for synuc leinopathy within the 
gut [33-35]. Although less extensively studied, a similar 
link has begun to emerge for tau, with inflammatory 
stimuli shown to enhance tau phosphorylation, impair its 
clearance, and promote the formation of pathogenic tau 
species in peripheral and central tissues [36, 37].   
Together, these observations underscore 
inflammation as a shared upstream driver of both α -
synuclein and tau pathology, providing a mechanistic 
framework through which chronic immune dysregulation 
may initiate or amplify protein aggregation in the ENS. 
Despite these insights, it remains unclear whether 
systemic low -grade inflammation directly cooperates 
with LRRK2 to drive gut inflammation and protein co -
pathologies. To address this gap, we hypothesized that 
chronic low -grade inflammation synergizes with th e 
LRRK2 G2019S variant to promote intestinal immune 
activation and protein aggregation. To test this, we 
developed a mouse model integrating three major PD risk 
factors: LRRK2 genetic susceptibility, chronic 
inflammation, and aging. 
 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
 
Animals 
 
Male C57BL/6J wild -type (WT) and C57BL/6J -
Tg(Lrrk2-G2019S)2AMjff/J (LRRK2 G2019S) mice 
(Stock No. 018785) were purchased from The Jackson 
Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Animals were 
housed at the C.A.Pi.R. animal facility (University of 
Catania, Italy) and maintained on a 12 -h light/dark cycle 
with ad libitum access to food and water. To minimize 
potential cage effects, mice were housed in groups of 3-4 
per cage and randomly assigned to experimental groups, 
with a three-week acclimation period prior to t he start of 
experiments. The research project (Authorization n. 
345/2019-PR, issued by the Italian Ministry of Health on 
May 5, 2019) and all experimental procedures were 
approved by the University Institutional Animal 
Experimentation and Ethical Committee  (OPBA, 
University of Catania). All procedures were performed in 
accordance with the European Union Directive 
(2010/63/EU) and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of 
Laboratory Animals. 
Lipopolysaccharide treatment 
 
Three-month-old WT and LRRK2 G2019S mice were 
exposed to intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of a low dose 
of lipopolysaccharide LPS (0.1 mg/kg Escherichia coli  
serotype O111:B4, Sigma-Aldrich), administered twice a 
week for 12 weeks.  
To ensure a consistent inflammatory stimulus, 
injections were performed every Tuesday and Friday 
morning (between 09:00 and 12:00 h), totaling 24 
injections per animal.  WT and LRRK2 G2019S mice 
exposed to i.p. injections of 0.9% sterile NaCl (saline) 
were used as controls. Clinical evaluations (body weight, 
coat condition, lethargy, locomotor activity, and 
grooming behavior) were performed weekly throughout 
the study until experimental endpoint. 
 
Tissue handling  
 
Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially 
perfused with 0.9% NaCl, followed by 4% 
paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate buffer (pH 7.2, 
4°C). Tissues were carefully harvested, post -fixed for 2–
4 hours in 4% PFA, and then cryoprotected in 15% 
sucrose in phosphate buffer overnight at 4°C. Blood was 
collected into EDTA -coated tubes, and plasma was 
separated by centrifugation and stored at –20°C. Tail 
biopsies from each mouse were genotyped via PCR 
followed by electrophoretic analysis according to the 
manufacturer’s protocols (The Jackson Laboratory).

using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed 
by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test. A p-value of < 
0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS 
 
LRRK2 G2019S enhances systemic inflammatory 
responses to chronic low-dose LPS exposure 
 
As aging is recognized as the main risk factor influencing 
both the clinical presentation and progression of sporadic 
and LRRK2 -associated PD, we examined whether the 
LRRK2 G2019S variant, in combination with chronic 
moderate systemic inflammation, interacts with the aging 
process to modulate intestinal and systemic immune 
responses. To assess the impact of LRRK2 G2019S on gut 
inflammation and pathology, wild -type (WT) and 
hemizygous transgenic C57BL/6J -LRRK2 G2019S 
(LRRK2 G2019S) mice received intraperitoneal 
injections of low -dose lipopolysaccharide (LPS: 0.1 
mg/kg) or saline (NaCl) twice weekly for 12 consecutive 
weeks. Age- and background-matched C57BL/6 WT non-
transgenic mice served as controls. Weekly clinical 
assessments, including body weight, posture, letha rgy, 
movement reluctance, and grooming behavior revealed 
that chronic LPS administration did not significantly 
affect general health parameters or lifespan in either 
young or aged mice of both genotypes compared to NaCl-
injected controls. Mice were sacrifi ced at 3, 6, and 16 
months of age for subsequent analyses. We first measured 
plasma cytokine levels in WT and LRRK2 G2019S mice 
in response to LPS at different ages. Unlike previous 
reports [38, 39], LRRK2 G2019S mice exhibited elevated 
baseline levels of IL -6 and IL -4 at 3 months of age 
compared with WT mice (Fig . 1A).  Furthermore, 6 -
month-old LRRK2 G2019S mice treated with LPS 
showed significantly higher plasma concentrations of IL-
6, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-α, IL-17A, and IL-4 than WT 
counterparts. At 16 months, levels of IL -4, IL-2, and IL-
17A returned to baseline values, whereas plasma levels of 
the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL -6, IFN-γ, and TNF -α 
remained significantly elevated in LPS -treated LRRK2 
G2019S mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 1A). 
 
LRRK2 G2019S promotes colonic infiltration of CD4⁺ 
T cells and macrophages 
 
To investigate whether the LRRK2 G2019S variant 
affects local immune cell dynamics in the gut, we 
analyzed CD4⁺ T -cell and macrophage populations in 
colonic tissue from WT and LRRK2 G2019S mice at 3, 6, 
and 16 months of age. These analyses were designed to  
capture both the baseline immune landscape and 
alterations induced by chronic peripheral inflammation. A 
significant increase in CD4⁺ T -cell infiltration was 
detected in the colon of untreated LRRK2 G2019S mice 
starting at 6 months, compared with age -matched WT 
controls (Fig . 1B, C), indicating an early onset of local 
immune activation. Chronic LPS administration further 
enhanced CD4⁺ T-cell infiltration in 6-month-old LRRK2 
G2019S mice, whereas no significant changes were 
observed in WT animals, suggesting a genotype -specific 
response to peripheral inflammatory stimuli. In parallel, 
quantification of CD68⁺ macrophages revealed a n 
increase in the colonic tissue of 16 -month-old LRRK2 
G2019S mice relative to WT controls, which was further 
exacerbated by chron ic LPS exposure (Fig . 1B, C). 
Together, these findings indicate that LRRK2 G2019S 
promotes age -dependent immune cell infiltration in the 
colon and enhances sensitivity to peripheral inflammatory 
challenges. 
 
Chronic low-grade inflammation triggers α -synuclein 
aggregation and phosphorylation in the  colon of 
LRRK2 G2019S mice 
 
To investigate whether chronic inflammation and LRRK2 
G2019S expression influence α -synuclein pathology in 
the gut, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of 
colonic tissues using the aggregate -specific α -syn 
antibody MJF-14 [40, 41]. Quantitative analysis revealed 
that LPS treatment significantly increased α -synuclein 
aggregation in both WT and G2019S mice at 6 and 16 
months compared with their respective saline -treated 
controls (Fig. 2A, B). Importantly, the G2019S mutation 
further ex acerbated this effect, as LPS -treated G2019S 
mice showed greater α-synuclein accumulation than LPS-
treated WT mice at both time points, with the most 
pronounced difference observed at 16 months (Fig. 2B).  
Immunofluorescence colocalization analysis at 16 
months, combining MJF -14 and tyrosine hydroxylase 
(TH) staining, revealed that α -synuclein aggregates 
localize within enteric dopaminergic neurons 
(Supplementary Fig . 1A, B). Notably, despite the 
presence of aggregated α -synuclein, we did not detect 
proteinase K -resistant α -synuclein, suggesting the 
aggregates may represent non-fibrillar species.

(pS129 α-syn). At 6 months, LPS -treated G2019S mice 
exhibited significantly elevated pS129 α-synuclein levels 
compared with both saline -treated G2019S and LPS -

DISCUSSION  
 
In this study, we examined how chronic low -grade 
systemic inflammation interacts with the LRRK2 G2019S 
variant and aging to influence colonic inflammation and 
the development of α -synuclein and tau pathology in the 
gut.  We found that low -grade peripheral immune 
activation induced an age -dependent rise in circulating 
cytokines, along with increased infiltration of CD4⁺ T 
cells and macrophages into the colonic tissue of LRRK2 
G2019S mice. This heightened inflammatory response 
was accompanied by greater α-synuclein aggregation and 
phosphorylation, as well as increased tau 
phosphorylation, relative to WT controls. These findings 
suggest that LRRK2 serves as a critical link between 
peripheral immune responses and early proteinopathy 
changes in the gut. Importantly, these alterations occurred 
in the absence of overt neuronal loss, indicating a pre -
degenerative stage of gut pathology. Together, our data 
support the concept that the gut may represent an early site 
of protein aggregation, where LRRK2 -mediated 
dysregulation of immune signaling contributes to the 
initial stages of PD -like pathology. Moreover, we 
observed that systemic immune alterations, including 
elevated cytokine levels and increased CD4⁺ T -cell 
infiltration, emerge as early as 6 months of age in LRRK2 
G2019S mice, suggesting that peripheral immune 
activation potentially drives local protein aggregation. 
These findings align with the growing evidence that 
peripheral immune mechanisms modulate the onset and 
progression of PD, providing a mechanistic lin k between 
genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and early 
proteinopathy in the gut. 
PD is a complex disorder affecting both the central 
and peripheral nervous systems, producing a broad 
spectrum of motor and non-motor symptoms [47, 48]. Its 
etiology likely reflects a convergence of genetic 
susceptibility and environmental factors, including aging, 
inflammation, and microbial exposures. Among genetic 
contributors, LRRK2 stands out for its dual role in 
neuronal and immune regulation, including modulation of 
α-synuclein and tau pathology, mitochondrial function, 
and glial reactivity. LRRK2 kinase activity is increased in 
both midbrain dopaminergic neurons and microglia in 
postmortem brains of patients with idiopathic PD, 
implicating it as a contributor to sporadic, age -dependent 
PD [49]. Rodent models of LRRK2 have provided 
important insights, although many transgenic and knock -
in models lack robust neurodegeneration [50-53]. 
Overexpression of pathogenic LRRK2 variants, including 
G2019S, can induce pathological α -synuclein and tau 
accumulation, altered dopamine homeostasis, and 
autophagic or mitochondrial impairments [54-56]. 
However, overexpression systems can introduce artifacts, 
and viral vector approaches, while informative, may not 
replicate the peripheral expression patterns of LRRK2 
observed in human PD [55, 57].  Knock-in models, in 
which the variant is introduced into the endogenous locus, 
offer a more physiological strategy but also have inherent 
limitations.  Differences in regulatory regions between 
mouse and human LRRK2 may lead to altered tissue -
specific exp ression or dysregulated responses to 
environmental or exogenous stimuli [58, 59]. Although 
LRRK2 G2019S knock-in mice reproduce aspects of PD 
susceptibility, they generally develop only mild 
phenotypes, suggesting that additional environmental or 
inflammatory challenges may be required to unmask early 
pathological features. Consistent with this, studies using 
LRRK2 G2019S knock -in mice have shown that an 
exaggerated inflammatory response in the gut emerges 
only after exposure to an environmental trigger, such as 
DSS-induced colitis [10]. This response was markedly 
more severe than in wild -type mice and was sufficient to 
induce neuroinflammation. However, pronounced motor 
deficits and exacerbated neurodegeneration occurred only 
when gut inflammation coincided with a second 
pathogenic insult represented by α -synuclein injection in 
the brain [10]. In line with these findings, a recent study 
on SARS-CoV-2 infection demonstrated that neither viral

exposure nor genetic predisposition alone was sufficient 
to trigger PD -like pathology [60]. Infection with SARS -
CoV-2 did not trigger parkinsonian features in wild -type 
mice.  In contrast, genetically susceptible LRRK2 
G2019S mice developed marked neuroinflammation and 
substantia nigra degeneration only when SARS -CoV-2 
infection was followed by exposure to a subtoxic dose of 
MPTP [60].  Together, these findings reinforce the 
concept that LRRK2 -linked vulnerability requires an 
additional environmental ‘hit’ to precipitate the 
pathological cascade, better reflecting the multifactorial 
and age -dependent disease progression observed in 
patients with PD. 
This convergence of LRRK2’s roles in intestinal 
immunity and neuroinflammation highlights its central 
position within the gut -brain axis. Early -life infections, 
microbial dysbiosis, and chronic gut inflammation may 
engage and potentiate LRRK2 -dependent imm une 
pathways, thereby initiating or accelerating 
neurodegenerative processes. Beyond these triggers, 
aging emerges as the most dominant and pervasive driver 
of PD, fundamentally reshaping homeostasis across the 
gut-brain axis. In the enteric compartment, aging is 
associated with impaired epithelial barrier integrity, 
compositional and functional alterations of the 
microbiota, and a chronic state of systemic low -grade 
inflammation, all of which may synergize with and 
amplify the consequences of LRRK2 hyperactivation [61, 
62]. At the cellular level, this increased vulnerability 
reflects a progressive erosion of proteostatic and 
metabolic homeostasis, prominently involving 
dysfunction of the autophagy -lysosomal system and 
depletion of essential metabolic cofactors [63, 64]. This 
age-dependent decline is driven by dysregulation of 
nutrient- and stress-sensing pathways, including sustained 
mTOR signaling and impaired lysosomal biogenesis, 
which together reduce autophagic flux by compromising 
autophagosome formation and degradative capacity. As a 
result, cellular quality-control systems progressively fail, 
leading to inefficient clearance of damaged proteins and 
dysfunctional organelles. In parallel, age -associated 
NAD⁺ depletion further weakens sirtuin -dependent 
mitochondrial quality-control pathways, including 
mitophagy, thereby promoting mitochondrial 
dysfunction, chronic cellular stress, and the establishment 
of a senescence -associated pro -inflammatory state. 
Collectively, these converging failures in metabolic, 
lysosomal, a nd mitochondrial maintenance lower the 
threshold for proteotoxic stress and facilitate the 
accumulation and potential propagation of misfolded 
protein species.  
In this context, aging acts as a critical permissive and 
integrative factor that links systemic inflammation and 
impaired cellular clearance mechanisms, thereby creating 
a biological environment that is highly permissive for the 
emergence and progression of neurodegenerative 
pathology in PD. 
Our study integrates three PD risk factors, LRRK2 
genetic susceptibility, chronic low -grade systemic 
inflammation, and aging, in a mouse model. We 
demonstrate that even sub-threshold doses of LPS trigger 
significant recruitment of CD4⁺ T cells to the colon and 
potentiate α -synuclein and tau co -pathology in LRRK2 
G2019S mice, without causing overt neuronal los s in the 
gut. These findings mirror observations in human PD, 
where enteric α-synuclein and tau pathology can precede 
motor symptoms by decades [37, 65, 66].  Furthermore, 
our data indicate that peripheral immune dysregulation, 
through elevated cytokines such as IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-
α, may amplify local protein aggregation and potentially 
modulate CNS pathology via the gut -brain axis. The 
persistent elevation  of IL -6, TNF -α, and IFN -γ in aged 
LRRK2 G2019S mice suggest a shift toward chronic 
innate inflammation (“inflammaging”), while the decline 
in IL-4, IL-2, and IL-17A reflects loss of adaptive immune 
competence (“immunosenescence”). Together, these 
findings suggest that LRRK2 -driven immune 
dysregulation interacts with chronic low -level 
inflammation and aging to promote a sustained pro -
inflammatory milieu, which may contribute to increased 
PD susceptibility or progression [38, 67, 68].  
The co-occurrence of α-synuclein and tau pathologies 
in the gut mirrors observations in LRRK2 -PD brains, 
where cortical tau and LB pathology frequently overlap 
[69, 70]. For instance, a recent work has shown that α -
synuclein positivity is associated with higher levels of 
phosphorylated tau -181 in the cerebrospinal fluid and 
increased tau deposition, correlating with a more 
aggressive disease course [71]. Consistent with these 
findings, studies using co -pathology mouse models have 
shown that the presence of both proteins triggers 
pronounced infiltration of peripheral CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T 
cells into the brain [72, 73].  
This interaction appears to establish a detrimental 
feedback loop in which protein co -aggregation amplifies 
inflammation, thereby accelerating neurodegeneration.  
However, the temporal sequence of these pathogenic 
events in the brain remains debated [74], with growing 
evidence suggesting that tau pathology may precede and 
even trigger α -synuclein aggregation. Several studies 
report substantial neuronal loss and dysfunction in the 
substantia nigra before the appearance of LB pathology 
[75-78], while others show that tau accumulation alone 
can drive severe neurodegeneration and parkinsonism in 
the absence of synucleinopathy [69, 79, 80]. These 
observations support the hypothesis that tau pathology 
may act as an upstream event contributing to α -synuclein 
aggregation in PD. In this context, our observation of a

similar α-synuclein-tau co-pathology in the gut suggests 
that this pathogenic synergy is not restricted to the brain 
but may originate in the ENS, potentially contributing to 
the initiation and propagation of pathology along the gut-
brain axis [42, 72, 74].   
Our findings suggest that early -life exposure to 
chronic, low -dose systemic inflammation interacts with 
the LRRK2 G2019S variant to shape the trajectory of α -
synuclein pathology in an age -dependent manner. LPS 
treatment was administered at 3 months of age for 12 
weeks, a developmental window in which the immune 
system remains highly responsive and environmental 
modifiers may exert long-lasting effects.  
Consistent with this, LRRK2 G2019S mice exhibited 
a robust increase in pS129 α -synuclein at 6 months, 
indicating that inflammatory stimulation enhances α -
synuclein phosphorylation.  
LPS administration triggered a marked increase in 
pathogenic tau phosphorylation at 6 months, which 
persisted in aged mice. A direct mechanistic link between 
LRRK2 and the observed co -pathologies is supported by 
evidence that both α-synuclein and tau aggregation impair 
lysosomal and endolysosomal function via pathways 
directly modulated by LRRK2 through Rab GTPases [30, 
81-83].  Specifically, the LRRK2 G2019S variant induces 
lysosomal abnormalities, including altered morphology, 
reduced acidification, and impaired autophagic flux [84].  
These defects are associated with disrupted α -
synuclein degradation, leading to the accumulation of 
detergent-insoluble α -synuclein and its enhanced 
neuronal release.  
Moreover, lysosomal overload induced by 
lysosomotropic drugs recruits LRRK2 and its substrate 
Rab10 to the lysosomal surface, promoting Rab10 
phosphorylation and exosome -mediated release of 
insoluble α -synuclein from microglia and macrophages 
[85]. In line with these findings, recent studies reported 
that the LRRK2 -mediated phosphorylation of Rab 
proteins (specifically pRab10 and pRab12) is elevated not 
only in PD but also in other neurodegenerative disorders 
characterized by altered α -synuclein or t au pathology, 
such as Alzheimer’s disease and progressive supranuclear 
palsy [81, 86]. Importantly, the phosphorylated Rabs were 
not randomly distributed but were highly concentrated on 
the membranes of granulovacuolar degeneration (GVD) 
bodies, which are small vesicles considered hallmarks of 
cellular stress and closely associated with endolysosomal 
and autophagic dysfunction. The number of pRab -
positive GVD bodies increased with advancing Braak 
stages of tau pathology, and at later stages, pRab markers 
were also observed within mature protein aggregates, 
including neurofibrillary tangles (tau) and Lewy bodies 
(α-synuclein) [81].  
Our findings support a model in which LRRK2 
hyperactivity sensitizes the gut to inflammatory triggers, 
promoting early co-pathologies in the gut that may act as 
seeds for CNS propagation.  
Notably, the preservation of enteric neuron numbers 
suggests that protein aggregation precedes neuronal loss, 
marking a prodromal phase that may be amenable to early 
therapeutic intervention. Although damage to the enteric 
nervous system is a common featur e across PD models, 
the extent of neuronal loss varies considerably among 
them [87]. For instance, pronounced loss of enteric 
neurons has been reported in A53T transgenic and 
rotenone-treated mice, whereas MPTP -treated models 
exhibit neuronal stress without overt degeneration  [87]. 
Similarly, studies on human post -mortem tissues report 
inconsistent results: some describe a reduction in enteric 
neuron numbers in PD patients, while others find no 
significant difference compared to healthy control s [45, 
88, 89].  
However, neuron counts alone may not fully capture 
gut pathology, as enteric neurons can exhibit functional 
impairments, including altered signaling or synaptic 
activity, even without cell death [45].  
Moreover, quantifying total enteric neurons is 
notoriously challenging, contributing to conflicting 
results.  
Methodological variability begins with sample 
preparation (whole mounts versus tissue sections), marker 
selection (e.g., Giemsa, Cuprolinic blue, NADH 
diaphorase, PGP 9.5, HuC/D), and normalization 
strategies (serosal area, gut length, ganglion, or ganglionic 
area) [90-92]. Marker expression may be downregulated 
in metabolically stressed or diseased neurons, potentially 
leading to apparent, but not actual, cell loss. Myenteric 
neuron density also varies along the intestinal tract, being 
generally lower in the small intestin e than the large 
intestine, and within segments, highlighting the 
importance of regional specificity.  
 
Limitations and Future Perspectives 
 
While our findings provide novel insights into the 
initiation of enteric proteinopathy , several limitations 
should be acknowledged. First, our systemic LPS model 
differs from primary lumen -initiated mucosal injury 
models. However, it captures key features of the chronic 
endotoxemia observed in PD patients with increased 
intestinal permeabil ity [93]. This “systemic -to-enteric” 
axis is further supported by the clinical association 
between the Systemic Immune -Inflammation Index  and 
PD risk, highlighting peripheral inflammation as a 
potential driver of disease initiation in genetically 
susceptible individuals [94].  Second, the impact of 
biological sex on LRRK2 -associated PD pathology

remains complex. Clinical studies in LRRK2 mutation 
carriers have yielded inconsistent findings, with some 
reporting higher disease penetrance in females and others 
observing no significant sex -related differences [95-98]. 
Our decision to focus on males was guided by recent 
evidence showing that prodromal intestinal inflammation 
exacerbates LRRK2 G2019S endophenotypes in a sex -
dependent manner, with females displaying relative 
protection against α -synuclein aggregation [12]. By 
prioritizing the male sex, we increased the sensitivity of 
our model to detect subtle, pre -degenerative pathological 
changes. Future studies incorporating female cohorts will 
be essential to complete our knowledge of the LRRK2-gut 
axis and to unravel the specific biological mechanisms 
underlying the sex -specific resilience observed in this 
model. Addressing these factors will be crucial for the 
development of early, sex -stratified therapeutic 
interventions targeting the gut-immune interface. 
 
Conclusions 
 
Overall, this work supports the concept that LRRK2 
mediates a convergence of genetic and environmental risk 
factors, linking peripheral immune activation, intestinal 
inflammation, and early α -synuclein/tau co -pathology. 
Our findings underscore the importance of examining gut-
brain interactions and peripheral immunity in PD and 
suggest that targeting early inflammatory and protein 
aggregation events in the gut may provide opportunities 
for disease-modifying interventions.
