Heterozygous missense mutations in EZH2 cause Weaver syndrome (WS), a 
developmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability and overgrowth. 
EZH2 encodes the enzymatic subunit of Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), 
which mediates monomethylation, dimethylation, and trimethylation of histone H3 
lysine 27 (H3K27me1/2/3). Most WS-associated EZH2 variants lack functional 
characterization but are presumed loss-of-function. However, the lack of early 
truncating mutations in EZH2 led us to hypothesize a dominant-negative mechanism 
for WS, which was supported by our structural analysis of all known 
WS-associated EZH2 variants. We isogenically modeled 10 representative variants 
in embryonic stem cells and showed that they reduce global H3K27me2/3 with 
concomitant increases in H3K27ac and chromatin decompaction. Notably, the 
pattern of H3K27me2/3 reductions indicated dominant-negative interference on 
PRC2 activity even when WS variants were expressed at low levels. RNA-seq 
identified weakly Polycomb-bound genes that lose canonical PRC1 (cPRC1) 
occupancy and become derepressed, including several phenotypically relevant 
growth control genes. Comparative analysis of a gain-of-function EZH2 variant 
causing growth restriction revealed reciprocal chromatin and transcriptional 
changes compared with WS-associated variants. Taken together, our findings 
support a model in which EZH2 variants associated with opposing developmental 
growth syndromes affect not only H3K27me3 but also intergenic H3K27me2, 
chromatin architecture, and cPRC1 recruitment.

Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) is a highly conserved chromatin regulatory complex essential for lineage specification and cellular memory in multicellular organisms (Margueron and Reinberg 2011; Schuettengruber et al. 2017; Laugesen et al. 2019). Mammalian PRC2 consists of three core subunits (SUZ12, EED, and either the EZH1 or EZH2 histone methyltransferase [HMT]) that together catalyze the deposition of up to three methyl groups on histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27). This trimeric core of PRC2 interacts with accessory proteins that facilitate its recruitment to chromatin and modulate its methyltransferase activity (Healy et al. 2019; Laugesen et al. 2019; Glancy et al. 2021). Both core and accessory PRC2 components are essential for normal mammalian development (Deevy and Bracken 2019). PRC2 broadly deposits H3K27me3 across transcriptionally silent developmental gene promoters (Boyer et al. 2006; Bracken 2006; Lee et al. 2006). The H3K27me3 modification allosterically activates PRC2 via specific interactions with EED that promote further PRC2 binding to chromatin (Lee et al. 2018). H3K27me3 contributes to the recruitment of the canonical Polycomb repressive complex 1 (cPRC1) via specific interaction with its constituent CBX proteins (Bracken et al. 2019; Glancy et al. 2023). cPRC1 then promotes chromatin compaction and transcriptional repression (Bracken et al. 2019). The functions of PRC2-mediated H3K27me1 and H3K27me2 are less well understood. Although H3K27me1 is localized along actively transcribed gene bodies (Ferrari et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2015), H3K27me2 is extensively deposited throughout intergenic regions of the genome (Conway et al. 2015; Streubel et al. 2018). This broad deposition profile of H3K27me2 prompted us to propose that it could function as a repressive “blanket” to counter accumulation of H3K27 acetylation (H3K27ac), a histone modification that is enriched at transcriptionally active regions and mutually exclusive from H3K27me2/3 on a given histone tail (Ferrari et al. 2014; Conway et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2015).

Heterozygous mutations in the EZH2, EED, and SUZ12 genes cause a triad of congenital overgrowth and intellectual disability syndromes with highly overlapping clinical presentations: Weaver syndrome (WS; MIM #277590), Cohen–Gibson syndrome (COGIS; MIM #617561), and Imagawa–Matsumoto syndrome (IMMAS; MIM #618786), respectively. These PRC2-related overgrowth syndromes are rare, with collectively <100 cases reported to date (Cyrus et al. 2019a,b; Hetzelt et al. 2022). However, it is likely that their true incidence is underestimated as a result of their very wide clinical presentation, which most often includes prenatal and postnatal overgrowth, tall stature, advanced bone age, variable intellectual disability, and a subtle but characteristic facial appearance (Tatton-Brown and Rahman 2013; Cyrus et al. 2019a). WS is the most extensively studied PRC2-related overgrowth syndrome, with EZH2 first reported as the causative gene in 2011 and ∼70 affected individuals reported to date (Tatton-Brown et al. 2011; Gibson et al. 2012). Most cases of WS are de novo, but the few familial cases exhibit an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern (Fryer et al. 1997; Proud et al. 1998; Tatton-Brown et al. 2013). Notably, almost all reported WS-associated EZH2 mutations are missense variants, with the majority affecting its catalytic SET domain (Tatton-Brown et al. 2013). Rare truncating nonsense and frameshift mutations have also been reported, though, intriguingly, they all occur within the last exon of EZH2 and therefore are likely to escape nonsense-mediated decay at the mRNA level; in other words, an almost full-length truncated EZH2 variant will be expressed (Hentze and Kulozik 1999; Imagawa et al. 2017). The lack of early truncating mutations in more N-terminal exons of EZH2 suggests that haploinsufficiency is unlikely to be the major mutational mechanism of WS. Regardless, WS-associated EZH2 variants are presumed loss-of-function hypomorphs due to the reduced HMT activity demonstrated for the WS-associated EZH2 variants studied to date (Cohen et al. 2016; Imagawa et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2018; Lui et al. 2018; Jani et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2024). Taking these pieces of genetic and functional evidence together, we speculate that WS-associated EZH2 variants are indeed partial loss-of-function but with potential dominant-negative effects.

Thus far, functional molecular studies of these WS-associated EZH2 variants have used in vitro HMT assays, DNA methylation profiling, or assays of global H3K27 methylation (Cohen et al. 2016; Lui et al. 2018; Jani et al. 2019; Choufani et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2024). However, these in vitro assays and global assessments of H3K27 methylation levels do not capture the full complexity of the Polycomb system. Namely, they fail to delineate the three different H3K27 methylation states in cells (both genome-wide and on Polycomb target genes) and do not consider the possible downstream consequences on H3K27ac, cPRC1 recruitment, chromatin compaction, or target gene expression. Given that PRC2 function is frequently deregulated in cancer, with both activating and inactivating mutations in EZH2, a key motivation for a thorough molecular study of WS-associated EZH2 variants is the fact that this information may help clinicians more accurately predict what cancers their affected patients may be predisposed to (Conway et al. 2015; Laugesen et al. 2016). As such, we wished to test our hypothesis that WS-associated EZH2 variants may have dominant-negative effects.

Here we detail the consequences of WS-associated EZH2 variants on the genome-wide functions of PRC2 in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs). We show that WS-associated EZH2 variants are hypomorphs that can dominant-negatively impair PRC2-mediated H3K27 methylation, which could explain the lack of early truncating mutations in individuals with WS. We confirm that heterozygous WS-associated EZH2 variants reduce intergenic H3K27me2/3 with corresponding increases in H3K27ac and signs of impaired chromatin compaction. RNA-seq revealed several hundred genes with weak Polycomb occupancy that lose cPRC1 binding in WS models, with several implicated in growth regulation becoming derepressed. Finally, a comparative analysis of WS-associated versus growth restriction-associated EZH2 variants highlighted the reciprocal chromatin and transcriptional changes potentially underpinning their opposing aberrant growth phenotypes. Taken together, our results shed new light on the molecular etiology of WS and highlight how the precise equilibrium of H3K27 methylations is crucial for the regulation of growth during development.

We sought to perform a functional investigation of EZH2 variants associated with Weaver syndrome (WS). We first performed an extensive literature review, identifying 67 individuals diagnosed with Weaver syndrome who were reported to have a coding mutation in EZH2 (Fig. 1A; Supplemental Table S1). Among this group, 61 individuals presented with missense variants affecting 34 different residues within EZH2, three individuals had in-frame indel mutations, and three individuals had C-terminal truncating mutations that are expected to produce a nearly full-length protein product. Supporting impaired HMT activity as a central mechanism in WS, the single amino acid variants tend to cluster around the SET and SRM domains of EZH2, which together interface with the histone H3 tail substrate to mediate H3K27 methylation (Fig. 1B).

Interestingly, Gerasimavicius et al. (2022) showed that dominant-negative mutations are highly enriched at protein interfaces. They also demonstrated that the degree to which missense variants cluster in three-dimensional (3D) protein structures and their effects on complex stability correspond well with the underlying molecular disease mechanism. Although loss-of-function variants tend to show weak structural clustering and tend to disrupt protein structure, non-loss-of-function (i.e., gain-of-function and dominant-negative) variants tend to be highly clustered, with mild effects on protein structure (Gerasimavicius et al. 2022). We compared these features among WS-associated missense EZH2 variants versus known loss-of-function and gain-of-function oncogenic EZH2 variants from leukemia and follicular lymphoma, respectively (Supplemental Table S2). Using the crystal structure of EZH2 in the PRC2 complex (Justin et al. 2016), we found that WS and follicular lymphoma variants clustered strongly, whereas leukemia variants showed weaker clustering (Fig. 1C). Next, we estimated the change in the free energy of folding (ΔΔG) associated with each pathogenic missense variant, where higher ΔΔG values indicate variants that are more likely to disrupt the structural stability of PRC2. Interestingly, although we found that leukemia and lymphoma variants tended to be highly disruptive to protein structure, WS variants were predicted to be much less structurally damaging (Fig. 1D). To summarize, (1) leukemia variants showed low clustering and were highly structurally damaging, consistent with a loss-of-function mechanism. (2) Lymphoma variants were highly clustered and also highly disruptive to protein structure, consistent with gain-of-function mutations that act via localized structure damage (Backwell and Marsh 2022). (3) The WS variants were highly clustered and structurally mild, strongly supportive of an underlying non-loss-of-function mechanism. Although this protein structural analysis alone is not able to distinguish between dominant-negative and gain-of-function effects, it is striking that at the protein structural level, WS variants are clearly distinct from both the loss-of-function leukemia variants and gain-of-function lymphoma variants.

Taken together, this protein-level analysis of the mutational spectrum of WS strongly indicates that the associated pathogenic EZH2 variants are non-loss-of-function and potentially dominant-negative in nature.

To functionally evaluate whether WS-associated EZH2 variants are indeed dominant-negative, we sought to compare the effects of heterozygous WS variant expression versus the Ezh2 heterozygous-null condition. To this end, we generated Ezh2 heterozygous knockout mESCs (Supplemental Fig. S1) and then exogenously expressed FLAG/HA-tagged human wild-type EZH2 (EZH2-WT) or variant EZH2 (Fig. 2A). We chose to investigate 10 EZH2 variants that are broadly reflective of the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of WS (Fig. 1A; Supplemental Table S1). For comparison, we also expressed the well-characterized lymphoma-associated EZH2-Y641F gain-of-function variant (Supplemental Fig. S2), which is known to increase H3K27me3 and decrease H3K27me2 when expressed in cells (Sneeringer et al. 2010; McCabe et al. 2012; Conway et al. 2015). The exogenous WT and variant EZH2 proteins were expressed at equal levels.

When expressed in Ezh1/2 double-knockout mESCs, which lack H3K27 methylations (Højfeldt et al. 2018), all 10 WS-associated variants displayed some degree of H3K27 methyltransferase activity (Supplemental Fig. S2A–C), supporting their partial but not total loss of function. However, we did not find evidence supporting additive effects on PRC2 activity for these variants when expressed in the Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygote (Fig. 2B,C). Rather, we observed apparent negative interference on the activity of EZH2 expressed from the endogenous WT allele. Immunoblot analyses for H3K27 methylation showed that, compared with cells exogenously expressing EZH2-WT, nine out of the 10 WS-associated EZH2 variants induced global reductions in H3K27me3 and/or H3K27me2 (Fig. 2B,C). Importantly, these reductions occurred in a dominant-negative manner because the average decrease was more severe versus the parental Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygote knockout cell line (Fig. 2B,C). Interestingly, EZH2-A677T (variant 7) only exhibited dominant-negative interference for H3K27me2. Moreover, on closer inspection of the original reporting study, the outlier EZH2-D233V variant (variant 5) that had WT-comparable H3K27me3 and H3K27me2 levels was associated with a relatively mild clinical presentation of WS (Tatton-Brown et al. 2013), supporting the utility of this cellular assay as a functional readout of variant severity. For the other nine WS-associated EZH2 variants studied, the strong global reductions in H3K27 methylation were associated with twofold to threefold increases in global H3K27ac (Supplemental Fig. S2D,E). By comparison, no major changes were observed in H3K36me2 or H3K36me3 levels (Supplemental Fig. S2B,D,F,G). Collectively, these data comprise the first experimental evidence supporting dominant-negative effects on global H3K27me3 and/or H3K27me2 levels by WS-associated EZH2 variants.

To investigate whether the observed dominant-negative effects were driven by the overexpression of exogenous versus endogenous EZH2, we repeated the experiment in Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygote cells with a subset of five WS-associated EZH2 variants using a much weaker expression plasmid (Fig. 2D). Under the control of the weak hPGK promoter, exogenous EZH2 was expressed at an average 1:16 ratio versus endogenous EZH2 (Fig. 2E,F). Immunoblot analyses for H3K27me3 showed that, even at just 1/16th relative abundance compared with endogenous EZH2, all five WS-associated EZH2 variants depleted mean H3K27me3 levels below that of the Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygote cells, indicating dominant-negative interference (Fig. 2E,G). Remarkably, we also observed dominant-negative depletion of H3K27me3 (greater than fourfold mean reduction) in Ezh2fl/fl wild-type cells when WS-associated EZH2 variants were expressed at an ∼50:50 ratio with endogenous EZH2 (Supplemental Fig. S3A–D). Analysis of complex composition by anti-FLAG immunoprecipitation (FLAG-IP) Western blot confirmed that WS-associated EZH2 variants assemble into PRC2 in a manner comparable with EZH2-WT, and thus the observed dominant-negative impairment of global PRC2 activity is not due to aberrant or deficient complex assembly (Supplemental Fig. S3E). Taken together, these data suggest that the consistently observed dominant-negative effects of WS-associated EZH2 variants on PRC2 activity are not an artifact of the overexpression model system.

Finally, we sought to extend our analyses to a more physiologically relevant human cell model. To this end, we examined five human lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs), including three from clinically unaffected individuals (non-WS, n = 3) and two from individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of Weaver syndrome (WS; n = 2), as depicted in Figure 2H. Immunoblot analyses showed that, compared with LCLs from the three non-WS individuals, LCLs from the two individuals with Weaver syndrome exhibited a significant mean fivefold reduction in H3K27me3, with a corresponding 40% mean increase in H3K27ac (Fig. 2I,J). Because haploinsufficiency would predict only a twofold reduction in H3K27me3, these data support a dominant-negative model for Weaver syndrome. We also quantified the levels of H3K27me2 and H3K27me1 and found that, compared with the mean of the three clinically unaffected non-WS cell lines, the two cell lines from individuals with WS exhibited nearly a twofold reduction in H3K27me2, with no significant changes in H3K27me1 levels (Fig. 2I,J). Given the concordance between these human LCL data and findings from mouse ESCs, our analyses provide supporting evidence that WS-associated EZH2 variants exert dominant-negative effects on global PRC2 activity.

We previously speculated that disruption of intergenic H3K27me2/3 could contribute to the etiology of Weaver syndrome (Deevy and Bracken 2019). To test this, we performed exogenous reference genome-normalized ChIP-seq (ChIP-Rx) for H3K27me2, H3K27me3, and H3K27ac. These and all subsequent analyses focused on a subset of five WS-associated EZH2 variants (Fig. 3A; Supplemental Fig. S4A,B) that were selected based on their frequency of occurrence, striking clinical phenotype, or both: R679C (variant 1), K634E (variant 2), K563E (variant 4), A677T (variant 7), and Y728* (variant 9).

Consistent with our immunoblot analyses (Fig. 2B,C), we found that all five chosen WS-associated EZH2 variants exhibited reduced H3K27me2 and H3K27me3 genome-wide compared with EZH2-WT (Supplemental Fig. S4C), as shown along the length of chromosome 9 as an example (Fig. 3B). Specific examination of intergenic regions confirmed that all five WS-associated EZH2 variants depleted intergenic H3K27me2 and H3K27me3 compared with EZH2-WT (Fig. 3C). The same relative trend was observed for H3K27me2 and H3K27me3 at gene bodies (Supplemental Fig. S4D). The five WS-associated variants similarly exhibited lower intergenic (Fig. 3C) and gene body (Supplemental Fig. S4D) H3K27me2 compared with the parental Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygote. Except for EZH2-A677T, the WS-associated EZH2 variants also displayed reduced intergenic (Fig. 3C) and gene body (Supplemental Fig. S4D) H3K27me3 compared with the parental Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygote. These observations further highlight the potential of WS-associated EZH2 variants to exert dominant-negative effects on PRC2 activities.

Consistent with our immunoblot analyses (Supplemental Fig. S2D,G), all five WS-associated EZH2 mutations caused no major change in genome-wide H3K36me2 (Supplemental Fig. S4C) but increased genome-wide, intergenic, and gene body H3K27ac (Fig. 3B,C; Supplemental Fig. S4C,D). In cells expressing these WS-associated EZH2 variants, H3K27ac tended to be gained on chromatin wherever H3K27me3 or H3K27me2 was reduced, suggesting that the increase in H3K27ac may be the direct result of an increased availability of unmethylated H3K27 substrate (Fig. 3D; Supplemental Fig. S4E). Given the scale of the changes that we observed on the landscape of H3K27 modification in response to WS-associated EZH2 variants, we next sought to investigate whether these changes correspond to an altered chromatin architecture. To do this, we performed H3 staining followed by stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) imaging to assess chromatin architecture. As established previously (Ricci et al. 2015), STORM imaging of H3-stained cells reveals nucleosomes either densely packed into clusters, representing heterochromatin, or as a diffuse fluorescent signal, indicative of euchromatin. Compared with EZH2-WT, cells expressing WS-associated EZH2 variants exhibited less compact chromatin, with histone H3 distributed in fewer and less densely packed nucleosome clusters. This was visually apparent as a more diffuse histone H3 signal outside clusters (Fig. 3E) and is quantified in Figure 3F. These data suggest that chromatin may be more densely packed in cells expressing EZH2-WT compared with cells expressing WS-associated EZH2 variants. Taken together, the ChIP-Rx and STORM data point to a role for PRC2 in regulating global chromatin compaction and implicate its impairment in the etiology of Weaver syndrome.

We next examined the consequences of WS-associated EZH2 variants on the ability of PRC2 to deposit H3K27me3 at Polycomb target genes (Fig. 4A). Four of the five WS-associated EZH2 variants had reduced mean levels of H3K27me3 at Polycomb target gene promoters (Fig. 4B). Surprisingly, the EZH2-A677T variant instead showed moderate increases in mean H3K27me3 deposition at these loci (Fig. 4B,C). We observed converse changes in mean H3K27me2 levels at Polycomb target promoters: Four out of the five EZH2 variants showed increased levels of promoter H3K27me2, whereas the EZH2-A677T variant depleted H3K27me2 from these sites (Fig. 4B,C). We noted that the pattern of increased H3K27me3 and reduced H3K27me2 at Polycomb target genes caused by the EZH2-A677T variant was similar to, albeit much less profound than, the oncogenic gain-of-function EZH2-Y641F and EZH2-A677G variants (Supplemental Fig. S5A,B), which have been reported in lymphoma to have an enhanced ability to convert H3K27me2 to H3K27me3 as a result of their enlarged lysine tunnels (McCabe et al. 2012). Supporting this, structural modeling of in silico mutated EZH2 points to an analogous mutational effect potentially underpinning the similarities between EZH2-A677T and EZH2-Y641F (Supplemental Fig. S5C). Specifically, the variant A677T residue is expected to cause steric clashes with Y641 (red discs in Supplemental Fig. S5C, middle panel). This could in turn shift Y641 and therefore enlarge the lysine tunnel, as predicted to occur in lymphoma when (1) Y641 loses its hydroxyl upon mutation to phenylalanine in the EZH2-Y641F variant (Supplemental Fig. S5C, right panel) and (2) A677 is mutated to a smaller glycine residue in the EZH2-A677G variant (McCabe et al. 2012).

A heterozygous gain-of-function mutation in EZH2 is implicated in an opposing developmental phenotype characterized by growth restriction, and the resulting EZH2 variant (p.A733T) (Fig. 5A) demonstrates increased HMT activity in vitro (Choufani et al. 2020). We wished to determine the consequences of this gain-of-function EZH2 variant on the genome-wide deposition of H3K27me2 and H3K27me3 compared with WS-associated EZH2 variants. To this end, we expressed either EZH2-WT, the growth restriction-associated EZH2-A733T variant, or the WS-associated EZH2-Y728* variant in Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygous mESCs and performed immunoblot analyses (Fig. 5B; Supplemental Fig. S6A,B). As expected, the EZH2-A733T variant induced marked global increases in H3K27me3, accompanied by global reductions in H3K27me1, H3K27me2, and H3K27ac (Fig. 5B). FLAG-IP Western blot confirmed that EZH2-A733T assembles into PRC2 in a manner comparable with EZH2-WT (Supplemental Fig. S6C). Next, we performed ChIP-Rx for H3K27me2, H3K27me3, and H3K27ac on Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygous mESCs expressing either WT EZH2, the growth restriction-associated EZH2-A733T variant, or one of two representative WS-associated variants: EZH2-Y728* and EZH2-A677T. In direct contrast to the WS-associated EZH2 variants, the growth restriction-associated EZH2-A733T variant caused genome-wide and intergenic increases of H3K27me3 coupled with decreased H3K27ac (Fig. 5C; Supplemental Fig. S6D,E). We observed that EZH2-A733T caused high levels of H3K27me3 to spread pervasively from PRC2 bound sites into surrounding intergenic regions, with reciprocal H3K27ac reductions (Fig. 5D). Accordingly, a genome-wide correlation analysis showed that H3K27ac was reduced where H3K27me3 was gained (Fig. 5E, right panel). H3K27me2 was also reduced in these same regions (Fig. 5E, left panel) as it was converted to H3K27me3 (Supplemental Fig. S6E). The EZH2-A733T variant also increased mean levels of H3K27me3 at PRC2 target gene promoters with a corresponding decrease in mean H3K27me2, consistent with its gain-of-function mechanism (Fig. 5D; Supplemental Fig. S6F).

Given that EZH2 is a key developmental repressor, we hypothesized that this gene upregulation might result from impaired Polycomb function at their promoters. To investigate this, we performed ChIP-Rx for the canonical PRC1 (cPRC1)-associated subunit CBX7 in Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygote cells expressing WT or WS-associated EZH2 variants. As shown in Figure 6B, we identified a cohort of 276 Polycomb target genes that lose CBX7 enrichment >1.5-fold in the presence of each of the five WS-associated EZH2 variants. Among these genes, a statistically significant portion was also transcriptionally upregulated across all five variants (n = 16 observed vs. n < 1 expected; P < 0.0001). CBX7 promoter occupancy was also dominant-negatively impaired across this gene cohort compared with all other Polycomb target genes (Fig. 6C). Further ChIP-Rx for SUZ12 demonstrated a general reduction in PRC2 occupancy at those same promoters with >1.5-fold reduced CBX7 binding (Fig. 6C). Notably, genes with reduced CBX7 binding tend to be “weaker” Polycomb targets, characterized by lower baseline levels of PRC2, cPRC1, and H3K27me3 compared with the other Polycomb target genes (Fig. 6C,D). This trend of reduced cPRC1 and PRC2 occupancy is illustrated at a representative four upregulated genes: Vim, Gja1, Myb, and Bmpr2 (Fig. 6D). In contrast, the majority of Polycomb-bound genes (e.g., Bmp2) remained transcriptionally stable, showing minimal changes in SUZ12 and CBX7 occupancy across the five WS cell lines. Taken together, these findings suggest that WS-associated EZH2 variants may commonly reduce cPRC1 binding at weak Polycomb target genes, coinciding with their increased expression.

To compare the transcriptional effects of the WS-associated EZH2 variants with the growth restriction-associated EZH2-A733T, we analyzed the expression of 25 genes that were upregulated in at least four out of five WS lines and showed >1.5-fold reduced CBX7 binding. This revealed that many of these genes show opposing expression changes in the presence of the growth restriction-associated A733T variant (Fig. 7A,B). Opposing effects between the WS versus the growth restriction variants were also observed in terms of Polycomb binding at the promoters of these genes (Fig. 7C; Supplemental Fig. S7A). For example, although Vim, Gja1, Myb, Bmpr2, Akt3, and Cntln had strongly decreased SUZ12 and CBX7 binding in cells expressing WS variants, they had increased binding in cells expressing the growth restriction variant. We observed similar effects on CBX7 binding for the growth restriction-associated EZH2-A733T variant across the broader set of genes losing CBX7 binding in WS lines, as exemplified by members of the IGF1–PI3K–Cyclin D pathway, which is linked to human height, including Igf1r, Pik3c2a, and Ccnd1 (Supplemental Fig. S7B; Tatton-Brown et al. 2017; Bicknell et al. 2025). In contrast, the majority of other Polycomb-bound genes (e.g., Bmp2) were not transcriptionally dysregulated and did not have strong SUZ12 or CBX7 changes at their gene promoters in the cells expressing EZH2-A733T.

Weaver syndrome affects multiple tissues, including osteoblasts and neurons. To investigate how WS-associated EZH2 variants impact differentiation, we assessed whether WS model ESCs show impaired lineage specification, focusing on neuronal differentiation given the syndrome's association with impaired neurodevelopment. ESCs were briefly differentiated by removing 2i/LIF and treating them with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) for 24 h to induce neuronal gene expression programs (Agger et al. 2007; Conway et al. 2021), followed by RNA-seq (Fig. 7D). Analysis of genes normally upregulated or downregulated by ATRA in wild-type ESCs revealed a striking dominant-negative effect in all five WS-associated EZH2 variant lines compared with the Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygote, with broadly opposing changes seen for the growth restriction-associated EZH2-A733T (Fig. 7E). The top 400 upregulated and 600 downregulated genes showed greater changes compared with Ezh2fl/Δ heterozygous cells. Notably, 59% of the top 400 upregulated genes are PRC2 targets, indicating impaired Polycomb target repression during differentiation. These 400 genes were enriched for roles in gliogenesis, forebrain development, glial differentiation, and other neuronal and sensory system processes, in keeping with the clinical features of Weaver syndrome (Fig. 7F). Together, these findings suggest that WS-associated EZH2 variants can specifically disrupt neuronal differentiation pathways early in development.

Here we present an investigation of the chromatin and transcriptional effects of a broad range of WS-associated EZH2 variants that cause developmental overgrowth, as well as an opposing EZH2 variant associated with developmental growth restriction. We provide the first evidence that WS-associated EZH2 variants can confer dominant-negative effects on the global H3K27 methylation activities of PRC2, with consequently reduced intergenic H3K27me2 deposition, cPRC1 binding, and derepression of a select cohort of sensitive Polycomb target gene repression. Strikingly, we observed opposing effects for an EZH2 variant associated with developmental growth restriction, supporting the idea that opposing chromatin defects are central to the underlying pathophysiology of EZH2-related growth disorders.

Here, we used 3D protein structural data to predict that WS-associated EZH2 variants are not simple loss-of-function alleles but instead likely act in a dominant-negative manner. This prediction aligns with the known mutational spectrum and inheritance pattern of WS. Previously published models of WS suggest that the associated EZH2 variants are partial loss-of-function, but these models lacked a matched comparison with the EZH2 heterozygous-null state, limiting their ability to test for dominant interference (Cohen et al. 2016; Lui et al. 2018; Jani et al. 2019; Choufani et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2024). We experimentally addressed this question by creating an Ezh2 heterozygous knockout mESC line in which we exogenously expressed human WS-associated EZH2 variants (Fig. 2), noting that the mouse (Q61188) and human (Q15910) EZH2 proteins share 98.4% amino acid sequence identity. In doing this, we were able to show for the first time that PRC2 activity tends to be more severely impaired by the heterozygous expression of WS-associated EZH2 variants compared with the isogenic heterozygous loss of EZH2.

We hypothesize that these dominant-negative effects may be mediated through the heterozygous expression of hypomorphic WS-associated EZH2 variants that occupy normal PRC2 binding sites but deposit less H3K27me2/3. We show that the expression of WS-associated EZH2 variants preserves the locations but reduces the levels of both H3K27me3 and H3K27me2. H3K27me3 is known to allosterically activate PRC2 and stabilize its binding on chromatin, whereas PRC2 was recently shown to have binding affinity for both H3K27me2- and H3K27me3-modified nucleosomes (Lee et al. 2018; Lukauskas et al. 2024). As such, allosteric activation and binding of PRC2 is expected to be decreased as a result of reduced H3K27me2/3 irrespective of whether it contains WT or variant EZH2. Therefore, we propose a dominant-negative mechanism in which WS-associated EZH2 variants impair both the allosteric activation and binding of WT EZH2 containing PRC2.

Our discovery of dominant-negative effects by WS-associated EZH2 variants may help answer the question posed by Lui et al. (2018): If the causative mutations are indeed loss-of-function, why have no early truncating mutations of EZH2 been reported in WS? One possibility proposed by Gao et al. (2024) is that manifestation of the Weaver phenotype may require biallelic EZH2 protein expression irrespective of catalytic activity. Our data agree that normal EZH2 protein levels may be necessary but suggest that perturbed catalytic activity is central to the phenotype. We rather propose that the Weaver phenotype is in most cases a consequence of dominant-negative interference of PRC2 activity during development, which necessitates the expression of both a WT and a pathogenic protein product.

Another potential explanation is that heterozygous-null mutations in EZH2 are lethal. However, as of August 2025, the Database of Chromosomal Imbalance and Phenotype in Humans Using Ensembl Resources (DECIPHER) reports 96 patients with heterozygous deletions encompassing EZH2 (Firth et al. 2009), so this is not likely. A third possibility is that heterozygous-null mutations in EZH2 cause a phenotype that is distinct from WS. Indeed, among the subset of 82 heterozygous whole-gene deletion EZH2 variants with phenotype documentation available in DECIPHER, none are associated with overgrowth or tall stature. On the contrary, and perhaps surprisingly, short stature and small for gestational age are the second and third most common phenotypes reported, respectively.

Finally, it is possible that heterozygous-null mutations in EZH2 are clinically silent. Supporting this, several early truncating variants in EZH2 have been uploaded to ClinVar as of August 2025 (Landrum et al. 2018). In this scenario, EZH2 haploinsufficiency may not sufficiently impair PRC2 activity to produce the Weaver phenotype, which we speculate may be due to partial compensation by EZH1. Although EZH1 catalytic activity and abundance in ESCs is substantially lower than EZH2 (Margueron et al. 2008; Shen et al. 2008; Streubel et al. 2018), it might sufficiently compensate for EZH2 at later stages of development when EZH2 expression levels decrease (McCole et al. 2025). Interestingly, the mutational spectrum for the closely related SUZ12 mutant Imagawa–Matsumoto syndrome includes heterozygous early truncating variants, whole-gene deletion variants, and even homozygous missense variants (Cyrus et al. 2019b; Park and Jang 2023; Yüksel Ülker et al. 2023). Because loss of SUZ12 function would affect both EZH1- and EZH2-containing PRC2 complexes, the contrasting mutational spectrum of Imagawa–Matsumoto syndrome versus Weaver syndrome supports functional compensation for heterozygous-null mutations in EZH2 by EZH1.

Overall, our findings agree with prior reports that WS-associated EZH2 variants are partial loss-of-function and extend them by providing the first evidence supporting their dominant-negative effects.

Individuals with WS are highly predisposed to developing cancers (Kamien et al. 2018). Indeed, some WS variants are directly found in cancer malignancies, including EZH2-P132L, EZH2-R679C (variant 1), EZH2-E740K (variant 8), and EZH2-Y728* (variant 9). Usemann et al. (2016) found the heterozygous EZH2-P132L variant in a young patient with WS who developed acute myeloid leukemia. EZH2-R679C was detected in the homozygous condition in a secondary myelofibrosis case, whereas EZH2-Y728* was found to remain in the heterozygous condition in a case of primary myelofibrosis that transformed into acute myeloid leukemia (Ernst et al. 2010). Nevejan et al. (2020) identified EZH2-E740K as the driver mutation in a case of donor cell-derived acute myeloid leukemia after stem cell transplantation. These observations provide potentially relevant insight for predicting cancer predispositions in affected WS cases. There are also documented examples of alternative variants occurring in cancer at the same residues mutated in WS, most notably including the oncogenic EZH2-A677G variant (McCabe et al. 2012; https://www.cbioportal.org), which shares the same residue as the WS-associated EZH2-A677T characterized in this study.

Here, we show that the WS-associated EZH2-A677T variant behaves somewhat like gain-of-function EZH2-Y641F and EZH2-A677G variants previously reported in B-cell lymphoma (Sneeringer et al. 2010; McCabe et al. 2012). Like the B-cell lymphoma variants, we show that EZH2-A677T has an enhanced ability to convert H3K27me2 to H3K27me3. In accordance with this “oncogene-like” mechanism of action, the individual with WS caused by this EZH2-A677T variant developed both neuroblastoma and acute lymphoblastic leukemia at just 13 months old (Tatton-Brown et al. 2013). Considering these potentially cancer-relevant insights, we propose that genomic profiling of H3K27 modifications upon ectopic expression of WS-associated EZH2 variants may represent a useful means to predict cancer predisposition in individuals.

We identified a cohort of 25 Polycomb target genes that were consistently derepressed by at least four out of five Weaver syndrome-associated EZH2 variants. Interestingly, many of these dysregulated genes are themselves implicated in growth phenotypes or disorders. For example, activating mutations in AKT3 are associated with a human disorder featuring macrocephaly and overgrowth (Alcantara et al. 2017; Tatton-Brown et al. 2017), and this gene is differentially methylated in individuals with WS (Choufani et al. 2020). AKT3 is a protein kinase expressed during human neurogenesis and is part of the mTOR pathway that is central to cell growth (Engelman et al. 2006; Poduri et al. 2012). Notably, two additional upregulated Polycomb target genes—GJA1 and RAP1A—have been linked to short stature in humans, with RAP1A further associated with a rare form of Kabuki syndrome (Pizzuti et al. 2004; Bögershausen et al. 2015; Choi et al. 2018; Shimomura et al. 2024).

Notably, although it is likely that the opposing chromatin changes observed between overgrowth and growth restriction-associated EZH2 variants will be conserved across many lineages, the specific genes that are transcriptionally dysregulated may vary across cell types due to specific localization of the chromatin defects and the availability of cell type-specific transcription factors. Therefore, much can be learned from studying these EZH2 variants in additional lineage-specific contexts such as chondrocyte, neural stem cell, or lymphoblastoid cell lines or during differentiation, where the effects of the variants may be more pronounced.

We show that the degree of dominant-negative impairment of PRC2 functions by WS-associated EZH2 variants correlates with the clinical phenotype. For example, we identified one EZH2 variant reported to cause familial Weaver syndrome (EZH2-D233V) that did not impair the global histone methylation activities of PRC2. In line with this, we noted that the individuals diagnosed with WS who had this mutation had relatively mild clinical presentations, with heights no more than +2 standard deviations (SDs) from the norm and occipito–frontal circumferences no more than +0.6 SD (Tatton-Brown and Rahman 2013). In contrast, the WS-associated EZH2 variant Y728* was the most severely impaired in our cellular assays of HMT activity, and accordingly, the individual with this variant had a strong presentation of clinical overgrowth, with a recorded height +7.6 SDs and occipito–frontal circumference +2.7 SDs (Tatton-Brown et al. 2011).

Therefore, we propose that the cellular assay and investigative strategy designed here could be extended to study other growth disorders caused by mutations in chromatin regulators, such as SUZ12, EED, ASXL1–3, NSD1, DNMT3a, CHD8, ARID1B, BRM, and CBP/EP300 (Tatton-Brown et al. 2017; Alfert et al. 2019; Gamu and Gibson 2020; Cuddapah et al. 2021). Notably, loss-of-function mutations in CBP/P300 H3K27 acetyltransferases cause Rubinstein–Taybi syndrome, which is characterized by short stature, microcephaly, and other features directly opposing WS clinical presentation (Gamu and Gibson 2020). It is possible that the defects in H3K27 acetyltransferase activity could lead to lower levels of H3K27ac across the genome, similar to the effects of the growth restriction-associated EZH2 variant studied here and in contrast to the elevated levels of H3K27ac observed in our cells expressing Weaver syndrome-associated variants.

In summary, we propose a model in which WS-associated EZH2 variants dominant-negatively impair PRC2-mediated H3K27 methylation, altering chromatin architecture and disproportionately reducing PRC2 activity at a cohort of weakly bound Polycomb target genes. This correlates with reduced cPRC1 binding and derepression of these genes, many of which play key roles in growth and neurodevelopment, thereby offering new mechanistic insights into Weaver syndrome.

Cells were resuspended in 1 vol of high-salt buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.2, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5% [v/v] NP-40, 1 mM EDTA at pH 7.4, 2 µg/mL aprotinin, 1 µg/mL leupeptin, 1 mM PMSF) and sonicated. Lysates were rotated for 20 min at 4°C before dilution with 1 vol of no-salt buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.2, 0.5% [v/v] NP-40, 1 mM EDTA at pH 7.4, 2 µg/mL aprotinin, 1 µg/mL leupeptin, 1 mM PMSF). Lysates were then clarified at ≥20,000 RCF for 20 min at 4°C before preclearance by rotating with equilibrated mouse IgG-coupled beads (Sigma) for 1 h at 4°C. Input samples were taken from precleared lysates, and the remaining lysate was incubated with equilibrated anti-FLAG beads (a gift from Dr. Claudio Ciferri) and ≥250 U of benzonase nuclease (Sigma), rotating overnight at 4°C. Beads were then washed five times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% [v/v] NP-40, 1 mM EDTA at pH 7.4, 2 µg/mL aprotinin, 1 µg/mL leupeptin, 1 mM PMSF). FLAG-tagged immunoprecipitated peptides were eluted from the beads by incubation with shaking at 25°C with elution buffer (wash buffer with 250 µg/mL 3xFLAG peptide [Sigma]). Samples were centrifuged at ≥20,000 RCF, and the eluate supernatant was transferred to a new tube and denatured in an appropriate sample buffer for Western blot.

For STORM imaging, 35 mm MatTek (P35G-1.5-14-C) plates were gelatinized with 0.1% gelatin. Five-hundred-thousand mESCs were seeded per plate for 20–22 h before 6 min of fixation in 1:1 methanol:ethanol at −20°C. Samples were blocked in blocking buffer (PBS with 10% donkey serum) for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with H3 antibody (Abcam ab1791) 1:70 dilution in blocking buffer + 0.1% Triton X-100. Samples were incubated in 1:200 dilution of Alexa fluor 647 secondary antibody in PBS in the dark for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then postfixed in 2% PFA for 5 min and stored in PBS at 4°C until imaging. Direct STORM (dSTORM) imaging was performed using a Nikon N-STORM microscope equipped with a 1.49 NA CFI apochromat TIRF objective, exciting the Alexa fluor 647 dye with the 647 nm laser light in highly inclined and laminated optical (HILO) sheet mode (Tokunaga et al. 2008). The 405 nm laser light (activation laser) was used for reactivating the Alexa fluor 647 into a fluorescent state. The activation laser power was automatically increased by the NIS software to keep the number of localizations per frame constant up to a maximum of 50% of the laser power. Each dSTORM acquisition consisted of 40,000 images recorded with an ORCA-Flash4.0 sCMOS camera (Hamamatsu) with an exposure time of 20 msec, a pixel size of 161.5 nm, and a field of view of 128 pixels × 128 pixels. During dSTORM acquisitions, cells were kept in imaging buffer (100 mM MEA, 1% glucose, 560 μg/mL glucose oxidase, 34 μg/mL catalase in PBS). Two regions of interest (ROIs) of 32 pixels × 32 pixels for each dSTORM image were processed using ThunderSTORM (Ovesný et al. 2014) with a predetection wavelet filter (B-spline, scale 2, order 3), initial detection by nonmaximum suppression (radius 1, threshold at 1 standard deviation of the F1 wavelet), and subpixel localization by integrated Gaussian point spread function and maximum likelihood estimator with a fitting radius of three pixels. Detected localizations were filtered (intensity >500 photons, σ range of 50–500, and localization uncertainty of <20 nm). The filtered data set was then corrected for sample drift (cross-correlation of images from five bins at a magnification of 5), and repeated localizations were removed by merging points that reappeared within 20 nm. STORM images were visualized using the normalized Gaussian method with a lateral uncertainty of 20 nm. Cluster analysis was performed thanks to a supervised machine learning approach using a trained neural network (Williamson et al. 2020). The cluster analysis by machine learning (CAML) workflow, consisting of three stages and corresponding Python scripts, was used to (1) prepare the data by converting the X,Y localization tables in a list of near-neighbor distances, (2) evaluate the input data with a trained model, and (3) extract clustering information. The 87B144 model was used (Williamson et al. 2020), considering the possibility that >100 localizations per cluster could occur in our data set. More than 30 nuclei were analyzed per condition.

Cells were counted, and normalized cell pellets (each of equal cell number, up to 50 × 106) were washed twice with DPBS before cross-linking for 10 min with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma) in DPBS. Cross-linking was quenched with 125 mM glycine for 5 min followed by two PBS washes. Cross-linked cells were lysed in an appropriate volume of SDS–lysis buffer (100 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris at pH 8.1, 5 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 0.5% SDS, 2 µg/mL aprotinin, 1 µg/mL leupeptin, 1 mM PMSF). Chromatin was pelleted by centrifugation at 350 RCF for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatants were discarded, and chromatin was resuspended in ChIP buffer (2:1 dilution of SDS–lysis buffer/triton dilution buffer: 100 mM Tris at pH 8.6, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 5% Triton X-100, 2 µg/mL aprotinin, 1 µg/mL leupeptin, 1 mM PMSF). For quantitative ChIP analyses (ChIP-Rx), spike-in chromatin was prepared from NTERA-2 cells and added to mESC samples (1:10 cell number ratio) at this point in the preparation procedure.

Chromatin samples were sheared using a Branson SFX150 Sonifier for a total sonication time of 4–5 min at 50% power and a duty cycle of 1 sec on and 4 sec off. ChIPs were performed overnight at 4°C. For details on the antibodies used, see Supplemental Table S3. Following overnight incubation, each sample received 40 µL of Protein G Dynabeads and was incubated for a further 3 h at 4°C. Beads were washed three times in mixed micelle buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris at pH 8.1, 5 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 5.2% sucrose, 1% Triton X-100, 0.2% SDS), twice with buffer 500 (0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 50 mM HEPES at pH 7.5, 1% Triton X-100), twice with LiCl detergent wash (0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 0.5% NP-40, 10 mM Tris at pH 8.0), and once with TE (10 mM Tris at pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA at pH 8.0). Samples were eluted from beads using elution buffer (0.1 M NaHCO3, 1% SDS) while shaking for 1 h at 65°C, and then supernatants were incubated overnight at 65°C to reverse cross-linking. Enriched DNA fragments were treated with RNase (Thermo Fisher) and Proteinase K (Sigma) prior to purification using a Qiagen MinElute PCR purification kit (Qiagen 28006).

ChIP-purified DNA was quantified by fluorimetry using the Qubit dsDNA high-sensitivity assay kit (Thermo Fisher), and 0.5–50 ng of DNA/ChIP was used for library preparation using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA library kit for Illumina (NEB E7645) and NEBNext multiplex oligos for Illumina (index primers sets 1 and 2; NEB E7335 and E7500). Library DNA was quantified using Qubit, and size distributions were ascertained on a TapeStation (Agilent) using the D1000 ScreenTape assay reagents (Agilent 5067–5583). This information was used to calculate pooling ratios for multiplex library sequencing. Pooled libraries were diluted and processed for either 75 bp single-end or 35 bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 500 instrument (ID: NB501524) using the NextSeq 500 high-output v2 kit (Illumina FC-404-2005) for 75 cycles in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

RNA was isolated from cells using the NucleoSpin RNA kit (Macherey-Nagel) per the manufacturer's protocol. RNA-seq libraries were prepared by Novogene as follows: mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Following fragmentation, the first strand of cDNA was synthesized using random hexamers, followed by second strand synthesis using dTTP. cDNA was subjected to end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation before being size selected and amplified for final library generation. The library was assessed by Qubit, Bioanalyzer, and RT-qPCR for quality control and quantification. Libraries were sequenced paired end to an approximate read depth of 55 million paired-end reads on the Illumina NovaSeq X plus platform.

FoldX 5.0 (Delgado et al. 2019) was run to estimate the change in Gibbs free energy of folding (ΔΔG) of missense mutations as the average of three replicates using the EZH2 chain in the X-ray structure 5hyn (Justin et al. 2016). The FoldX “RepairPDB” command was run before modeling the mutations. The extent of disease clustering (EDC) metric was calculated as described previously (Gerasimavicius et al. 2022). Leukemia-associated missense variants were obtained from COSMIC v99 (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk).

Chromosome names for the human genome were modified with the prefix hg38_, and a metagenome was created by concatenating the mouse and human reference genomes (mm10 and hg38, respectively) before indexing with Bowtie 2 version 2.3.5 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012) as described previously (Orlando et al. 2014). Reads were aligned to the metagenome using Bowtie 2 with default parameters, with the exception of –no-unal. Nonunique read alignments were filtered out using SAMtools version 1.10 (Li et al. 2009) to exclude those with an alignment quality of <2, and the hg38_prefix appended to chromosome names was used to separate reads as aligned to the reference mouse or spike-in human genomes. SAMtools was used to convert SAM files to BAM files and to remove duplicate aligned reads. Spike-in normalization factors were calculated for each ChIP using the formula for normalized reference-adjusted reads per million (RRPM) as described previously (Orlando et al. 2014): one per million spike-in reads. bigWig files were generated using the bamCoverage tool from deepTools suite version 3.5.0 (Ramírez et al. 2016) with a bin size of 10 and scaled using the RRPM normalization factor derived for each sample. BAM files were converted to BED format using the bamToBed function in BEDTools version 2.27.1 (Quinlan and Hall 2010) and used for peak calling with HOMER version 4.10 (Heinz et al. 2010) using BED files from input ChIP-Rx as a control. The computeMatrix function in deepTools was used to score the coverage of bigWigs in genomic regions of interest prior to generating average and tornado plots using the plotProfile and plotHeatmap functions, respectively. featureCounts version 1.6.4 was used to calculate the average ChIP signal at the TSSs of genes of interest from the BAM files. The average signals were multiplied by the spike-in normalization factors, and heat maps were generated using GraphPad version 9.5.1.

The mm10 reference genome was split into 10 kb windows using the makewindows function in BEDTools, and regions in the ENCODE mm10 blacklist were removed using BEDtools intersect with the -v and -wa flags (Amemiya et al. 2019). BEDTools coverage was used to summarize overlapping read alignments in BAM files within these bins and scale them using RRPM normalization. The resulting counts were used for the generation of chromosome-wide coverage line graphs and XY density scatter plots.

Promoter regions were identified by taking −1500 bases upstream of and +600 bases downstream from annotated transcription start sites in the Ensembl 98 mm10 genome annotation. Gene body regions were defined by taking the gene annotations as defined in the Ensembl 98 mm10 genome annotation and converting them to the bed format using a custom script. The BEDTools complement tool was used to generate a BED file of intergenic regions by supplying the gene body BED file a FASTA index of the mm10 genome as input (generated using SAMtools faidx). CBX7 read density over Polycomb target promoters associated with each Weaver syndrome-associated EZH2 variant-expressing mESC was calculated using the deepTools multiBigwigSummary function and compared with that of wild-type EZH2 to determine fold changes. Promoters showing a loss of >1.5-fold across all five variants were defined as CBX7 loss regions.

RNA-seq reads were preprocessed to remove low-quality reads and Illumina sequencing adapters using cutadapt (version 3.2) (Martin 2011). The STAR (version 2.7.3a) algorithm (Dobin et al. 2013) was used to align reads to the mouse reference genome (mm10) in two-pass mode, enabling the identification of novel splice junctions during alignment. SAM files were converted to sorted BAM files using SAMtools (version 1.10) (Li et al. 2009). Read alignments were assigned to gene identifier features annotated in the Ensembl 100 mm10 genome annotation using featureCounts (version 1.6.4) (Liao et al. 2014). Differentially abundant transcripts were identified using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.38.3) (Love et al. 2014), applying a fold change threshold of >1.5 or >2.83 depending on the experimental setup, with a baseMean of >20 and a Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P-value of <0.05. Batch effects were reduced by the inclusion of the formula “∼ batch + mutation status” to the design of the linear model. Gene-level TPM values were calculated using StringTie (version 1.3.6) (Pertea et al. 2015) Heat maps were generated using the pheatmap R package (version 1.0.12) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.14.6) (Yu et al. 2012), with a P-value adjustment via the Benjamini–Hochberg method and significance thresholds of P-value < 0.05 and q-value < 0.05.

Assuming a total of N = 21,859 expressed genes, the association between the WS upregulated genes (n = 77) and those with a >1.5-fold reduction in CBX7 binding (n = 276) was determined using a 2 × 2 contingency table and a χ2 test with Yates’ correction.

In silico mutagenesis was carried out in the context of the structure of PRC2.2 with a substrate nucleosome (PDB 6WKR). In silico mutagenesis was carried out using PyMol, and van der Waals clashes between the mutated amino acids and residues in their vicinity were visualized using the show_bumps script, with clashes represented by discs. Red discs in the figures represent severe clashes, and their diameter increases with clashes severity. Green discs in the figures represent almost clashes. In cases where more than one rotamer was possible, the rotamer that provides the least severe clashes is presented.
