# Diagnostic Yield of Laboratory Methods and Value of Viral Genotyping during an Outbreak of Mumps in a Partially Vaccinated Population in British Columbia, Canada.
**Authors:** Nunn A, Masud S, Krajden M, Naus M, Jassem AN
**Journal:** J Clin Microbiol (2018)
**DOI:** [10.1128/JCM.01954-17](https://doi.org/10.1128/JCM.01954-17)

## Content

Mumps remains endemic in North America despite routine use of the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine. In 2016, an outbreak of mumps in British Columbia, Canada, provided an opportunity to determine the diagnostic utility of laboratory testing methods. Specimens from patients with clinical mumps were tested for infection using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for antibody detection and an in-house reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR) targeting viral fusion and small hydrophobic (SH) genes. Viral genotyping was performed by SH gene sequencing. Laboratory data was linked with epidemiologic case data. Of the 139 confirmed cases, 94 (68%) had reported or documented history of MMR vaccination. Specimens were typically collected 1 day (for buccal and IgM tests) or 2 days (for urine tests) after symptom onset. Most confirmed cases (69%) were confirmed by buccal swab RT-PCR. Among cases tested by multiple methods, the percent positivity for buccal swab RT-PCR was 90% (96/107) compared to 43% (30/69) for both IgM ELISA and urine RT-PCR. Mumps IgM detection was higher in confirmed cases with no history of vaccination than in those with history (64% versus 34%, P = 0.02). The outbreak strain was identified as genotype G related to MuVi/Sheffield.GBR/1.05 but with conserved variations in five nucleotides within the SH gene that allowed linkage of geographically distinct cases. In conclusion, RT-PCR of buccal specimens had the highest diagnostic yield during a mumps outbreak in a partially vaccinated population. To optimize mumps diagnostic potential, clinicians should collect specimens depending on when the patient presents for care and their immunization history.

KEYWORDS: PCR, diagnosis, genotyping, mumps, serology

Mumps is a highly infectious febrile illness characterized by inflammation of the parotid or other salivary glands (1, 2). Since the introduction of measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine in the early 1980s, there has been a dramatic decline in the number of reported mumps cases in the United States and Canada (3–5). However, mumps continues to be endemic in North America, with many outbreaks occurring in the last several years (6–9).

Under current mumps control guidelines, adults born before 1970 are presumed to have natural immunity to mumps (5, 10). In 1981, the combined MMR vaccine was introduced in British Columbia (BC), Canada, for children aged 12 months and over. In 1996, a two-dose MMR vaccination schedule was implemented as a part of a measles control strategy, with the second dose given at 18 months of age. In 2012, the second dose was moved to school entry (4 to 6 years of age) and, beginning in 2014, the second dose has been given with a combined MMRV (MMR plus varicella) vaccine. From 2008 to 2016 the number of confirmed mumps cases reported annually in BC ranged from 8 to 148.

Previously in BC, three outbreaks of mumps occurred in 1997, 2008, and 2011, largely affecting two populations: unimmunized faith-based communities and young adults in a susceptible cohort that received only one dose of mumps-containing vaccine as part of routine childhood immunization (11, 12). Based on small hydrophobic (SH) gene sequencing, the 2008 and 2011 outbreaks were found to be caused by a genotype G strain; this is the most commonly circulating genotype identified in Europe and North America (13).

There are several potential explanations for ongoing mumps activity among young adults despite high vaccination uptake. The effectiveness of mumps vaccine has been estimated at 78% (range, 49 to 91%) for one dose and 88% (range, 66 to 95%) for two doses (14–16). The protective antibody levels induced by the MMR vaccine are thought to decline faster over time than those induced by naturally acquired infection (2, 17). Antigenic differences between the North American vaccine type (genotype A, Jeryl Lynn strain) and circulating strains, predominantly genotype G, may contribute to a poorer protective vaccine response (5, 18). It is also possible that with widespread MMR vaccination the population is less likely to be protected by subclinical boosting from circulating wild-type mumps virus (19).

Interpretation of diagnostic tests for mumps can be challenging because results are affected by the time of specimen collection relative to symptom onset and vaccination history. In unvaccinated individuals, the detection of IgM antibodies becomes reliable only 3 days after symptom onset (20). In vaccinated individuals, the IgM response may be absent or short-lived, acute IgG titers can be high, and a significant increase may not be detected between paired sera (2). For populations that have been immunized, reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR) of buccal swab specimens is therefore often considered to be more sensitive and specific. In Canada, including BC, it is recommended that specimens be collected within 5 days of symptom onset when the viral load is highest (5, 12). However, mumps virus RNA can be detected in buccal swabs up to 9 days after symptom onset (11), although oral virus shedding may be limited to the first 2 to 3 days in vaccinated individuals (20–22). Simultaneous collection of a urine sample can improve the detection yield when the patient presents more than 5 days after symptom onset since mumps RNA is shed in urine longer (11).

In April 2016, a large outbreak of mumps occurred in BC following a mass gathering for a sporting event in Vancouver. Serologic and RT-PCR testing on individual cases from the outbreak provided an opportunity to analyze the performance and diagnostic utility of various testing methods during an outbreak among young adults, stratified by vaccination status. We also discuss the added benefits of genotyping data for outbreak characterization.

Mumps is a reportable disease in BC, with cases reported to the British Columbia Centre for Disease Control (BCCDC). Routinely collected demographic, clinical, and epidemiologic case data are maintained in a surveillance database. The provincial mumps case definition was used to categorize patients as laboratory confirmed, epidemiologically linked confirmed, probable, and suspect (Table 1) (12). Cases were considered to have a history of vaccination if they reported documented or undocumented immunizations, while cases with no history of vaccination reported being unimmunized or having unknown immunization status.

Case definitions for reportable mumps disease in British Columbia, Canada

Specimens from patients presenting with clinically compatible mumps included buccal/oral swabs, urine and blood samples and were tested at the BCCDC Public Health Laboratory (PHL) (the provincial laboratory). All specimens submitted for testing from 1 April 2016 to 31 October 2016 (MMWR epidemiologic weeks 13 to 41 [23]) were included in analyses.

Buccal/oral swabs and urine specimens were tested for mumps virus by RT-PCR using previously published primers targeting fusion (F) and SH genes (24, 25). Briefly, 200-μl portions of primary specimens were extracted and eluted into 60 μl using the NucliSENS easyMAG platform (bioMérieux, St Laurent, Quebec, Canada) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primers and probes were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). Nucleic acids (5 μl) were amplified using the 7500 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). The thermocycling conditions were as follows: 50°C for 5 min, 95°C for 20 s, and then 45 cycles at 95°C for 3 s and 60°C for 30 s. Samples that tested positive for mumps RNA were referred to the National Microbiology Laboratory, Winnipeg, Manitoba, Canada, for genotype determination by SH gene sequencing (26). The sequence of one genotyped sample was submitted to GenBank (accession number KX101020).

Mumps virus-specific IgM and IgG antibodies were detected in sera with the Enzygnost commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) on a BEP 2000 Advance System (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis was done in Stata SE 14 (27). Ethics review board approval was not required since all data were collected as a part of routine surveillance and diagnostic testing under public health legislated authority.

From 1 April 2016 through 31 October 2016, clinical specimens were received at the BCCDC PHL for mumps diagnostic testing from 1,242 individuals. During this time, 139 confirmed, 20 probable, and 9 suspect mumps cases were reported to BC public health. An epidemic curve for the confirmed mumps cases is shown in Fig. 1. Of the confirmed cases, the median age was 27 years (range, 1 to 72 years), 71 (51%) were female, and 68 (49%) were male. All confirmed cases reported parotitis except one who could not be interviewed and 13 males (19%) reported orchitis. Of the 139 confirmed cases, 60 (43%) reported having one or more MMR doses without documentation, 23 (17%) had two documented doses, 11 (8%) had one documented dose, 31 (22%) had an unknown vaccination status, and 14 (10%) reported receiving no doses. Confirmed cases were identified in all five BC health regions: 78 (56%) in the Vancouver Coastal region, 29 (21%) in the Fraser region, 27 (19%) in the Island region, 3 in the Northern region, and 2 in the Interior region.

Of the 139 confirmed cases, 135 (97%) had laboratory testing performed at the BCCDC PHL (the remaining were reported to BC public health but had testing done out of province). Of these, 117 (84%) were tested for mumps virus RNA by RT-PCR: 107 (77%) by buccal swab, 69 (50%) by urine, and 4 (3%) by throat or nasopharyngeal swab. Half of the confirmed cases (n = 69) were tested for mumps virus IgM and IgG by ELISA and 60/68 (88%) had reactive IgG results within 5 days after symptom onset, which was considered the acute phase (no date of onset was available for one patient; the average titer excluding 11 results above the limit of quantification was 4,493) (20). The majority of the 139 confirmed cases (n = 96, 69%) were confirmed by buccal RT-PCR. Of the remaining cases, 10 (7%) were confirmed by urine RT-PCR, 14 (10%) were confirmed by IgM serology, 5 (4%) were confirmed by IgG seroconversion, 2 (1%) were confirmed by nasopharyngeal or throat swab RT-PCR, and 11 (8%) were confirmed by clinical presentation and an epidemiologic link to a case.

Most (n = 89; 64%) of the 139 confirmed cases were tested by two or more laboratory methods indicated for mumps diagnosis, permitting comparison of the diagnostic yield between the tests completed (Table 2). Since nasopharyngeal and throat swabs are not recommended for mumps diagnosis, these four specimens were excluded from analysis. Overall, for the remaining 131 confirmed cases tested in BC, the percent positivity for buccal swab RT-PCR was 90% (96/107) compared to 43% (30/69) for IgM enzyme immunoassay and 43% (30/69) for urine RT-PCR (Table 2). For patients tested by both buccal and urine RT-PCR, the percent positivity of urine specimens was still lower (23/60, 38%) compared to buccal RT-PCR (53/60, 88%). PCR cycle threshold (CT) values of positive buccal specimens ranged from 22.2 to 38.9 (medians of 28.9 for F and 29.0 for SH), while values for positive urine samples ranged from 25.9 to 40.9 (medians of 32.8 for F and 32.8 for SH). Of the nine confirmed cases that tested negative or indeterminate by any RT-PCR test, the confirmed case definition was met in five cases by epidemiologic linkage, in three cases by IgM enzyme immunoassay, and in one case by seroconversion.

The majority of samples (224/256; 88%) from the 131 laboratory confirmed cases were collected within 5 days of onset of illness (Table 3). The median delay between symptom onset and specimen collection was 1 day for buccal and IgM tests and 2 days for urine tests. The positivity rates for both IgM and viral RNA in urine peaked to ≥80% after day 5, while viral RNA was detected in buccal swabs at high rates of ≥83% starting from the day of symptom onset. Mumps virus was detected by RT-PCR up to days 9 and 10 after symptom onset for buccal and urine specimens, respectively, among three patients: one with a history of one MMR dose and two with unknown vaccination histories. Three cases were identified by positive urine RT-PCR when buccal swabs (collected on days 2 and 6 after symptom onset) were negative. There was no association between virus detection in urine and orchitis in males.

There was no significant difference in the ability to detect mumps virus by RT-PCR between confirmed cases with and without a history of immunization; however, the RT-PCR percent positivity was higher for unvaccinated confirmed cases by urine RT-PCR (57% versus 38%). The outbreak strain was identified as genotype G related to MuVi/Sheffield.GBR/1.05 but with conserved variations in five nucleotides within the SH gene, a sequence which had never been identified before in BC. Of the 139 confirmed cases during the outbreak time period, 95 had a genotype G virus with an identical match to the outbreak strain, 4 had a genotype G virus similar to the outbreak strain but with variation in 1 to 2 nucleotides, 5 had a genotype G virus that did not contain the outbreak strain signature, and 2 were travel-associated with genotypes K and F. The outbreak strain was identified in all geographic regions affected by the outbreak.

A resurgence of mumps has occurred among partially or highly vaccinated young adult populations in recent years (1, 2, 8, 9, 28). The prevailing explanation is secondary vaccine failure due to waning immunity over time; however, a mismatch between the vaccine (genotype A) and the commonly circulating strain (genotype G) in North America may be a factor (17, 19). For public health jurisdictions managing mumps outbreaks within vaccinated populations, interpreting diagnostic test results can be challenging because virus shedding may be reduced or shortened, and an IgM response may not occur or may be delayed (14).

During a 2016 BC outbreak of mumps, most confirmed cases had a history of one or two mumps-containing vaccinations, like other recent outbreaks in North America (6–9). Many of these outbreak cases demonstrated reactive anti-mumps IgG titers during the acute phase of illness, likely attributed to a previous vaccine or natural infection. It has been shown that detection of IgG, although traditionally associated with immunity, does not imply protection against mumps (29).

In confirmed cases tested by multiple methods, buccal RT-PCR had the highest diagnostic yield, similar to previous outbreak findings (11, 21, 22, 30). While Rota et al. found that buccal swab RT-PCR positivity in a highly vaccinated population decreased significantly after 2 days after symptom onset (20), our study, like the one by Hatchette et al. revealed that mumps virus RNA can be detected in buccal swabs up to 9 days following parotitis onset (30). Accordingly, laboratory diagnostic guidelines for mumps should be updated to preferentially recommend buccal swab collection for virologic diagnosis for patients presenting within a few days of symptom onset. Oral specimen collections are less invasive than blood collection and allow for genotyping to support epidemiologic investigations. Consistent with previous studies (11, 21, 22, 30), the incremental value of urine RT-PCR testing in this mumps outbreak setting was small. However, our results support previous findings that urine RT-PCR may be useful for identifying mumps virus in the 6 to 10 days after symptom onset (11). Urine testing may be more valuable in diagnosing sporadic cases of mumps, which was not evaluated in the present study.

While the percent positivity of IgM tests was low compared to buccal swab RT-PCR, positivity increased with days since symptom onset, confirming findings of other studies (20–22) highlighting that specimen collection needs to be delayed when using IgM detection as a diagnostic test for mumps. Stratifying by vaccination status, our results showed that IgM provides a significantly inferior diagnostic yield among patients with a history of vaccination, which is consistent with previous findings (2, 11, 20, 21) and confirms the unreliability of anti-mumps IgM testing for the acute diagnosis of mumps in vaccinated populations. In this study, the IgM capture method used is known to be more sensitive than indirect ELISA methods (20, 21). Furthermore, the presence of mumps IgG was not associated with seroprotection in vaccinated populations, as has been reported elsewhere (11, 20–22, 30).

Genotype results were valuable in the present outbreak investigation because the conserved variance of five nucleotides in the SH gene provided a strong indication that the outbreak strain virus had been imported from elsewhere at an international sporting event. In a 2016 systematic review of mass gatherings, sporting events were recognized as settings with potential for introduction of viruses spread via the respiratory route (31). As the 2016 BC mumps outbreak progressed, detailed genotype data helped outbreak investigators determine that the mumps activity in several regions of BC was part of the same outbreak rather than different clusters of endemic mumps activity. Other investigators have been able to use mumps genotyping data to identify specific chains of transmission (32) and distinct viral clusters (21, 33). Further genomic analysis, including sequencing of the F gene and HN genes (34), could provide increased resolution to investigate transmission patterns.

The present observational study is unique in that it provides a comprehensive analysis on the yield of laboratory methods for the diagnosis of mumps during an outbreak, considering both vaccination status and the time of specimen collection relative to symptom onset, as well as the value of mumps virus genotyping to understand the epidemiology of the outbreak. While our study is limited by its retrospective nature, the one-time submission of specimens, and its relatively small sample size, it confirms that RT-PCR of buccal specimens had the highest diagnostic yield during a mumps outbreak in a partially vaccinated population and that mumps virus genotyping can be critical for spatial investigation of an outbreak. Clinicians have an important role to play in collecting the appropriate diagnostic specimens. Our study revealed that ordering physicians in BC did not follow provincial guidelines to determine which specimens should be collected to optimally diagnose mumps based on clinical history, since there was no association between the specimen type collected and the day of specimen collection relative to symptom onset. Given that 333 patients under investigation for mumps during the 2016 outbreak period in BC were tested by two or more methods, a mumps outbreak can be a substantial burden on provincial laboratory resources. Therefore, when mumps is considered in a patient, vaccination history should be collected and diagnostic tests should be ordered based on vaccination history, as well as the number of days from symptom onset, for appropriate patient management and outbreak assessment.

It is important that data on diagnostic methods of mumps like those collected in our study continue to be analyzed and presented to provide a better understanding of diagnostic limitations and subsequently result interpretations. Mumps continues to be endemic in North America (6–9), and it is clear that physicians and public health professionals need to change their practices by discontinuing early IgM testing after presentation (especially for vaccinated individuals) and IgG testing to assess immune status. Studies like ours will ultimately help to drive changes in clinical and laboratory practices to support modern case and outbreak management (i.e., deciding which tests to offer and when). The results from this study, along with those from previous studies, highlight the value of RT-PCR and genotyping during mumps outbreaks and inform clinical and laboratory best practices.
