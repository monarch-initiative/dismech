# Phenotypic Delineation of Combined Oxidative Phosphorylation Deficiency-12: Clinical Features of 2 Patients.
**Authors:** Bahar Ister M, Cinar M, Ceylaner S, Uzun OU
**Journal:** Mol Syndromol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000541501](https://doi.org/10.1159/000541501)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Combined oxidative phosphorylation deficiency-12 (COXPD12) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Neurological findings and lactic acidosis can be presenting signs of COXPD12.

CASE PRESENTATION: Here, we present identical dysmorphic facial features that have not been described before in the literature, in 2 patients with two different EARS2 gene variants. Case 1 was a 2.5-month-old male who presented with hypotonia and lactic acidosis. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed diffusion restriction in the supratentorial deep white matter, around the ventricle, in the bilateral periaqueductal gray matter at the level of the basal ganglia, and in the dentate nuclei in the tegmentum. Case 2 was a 2-month-old boy who also presented with lactic acidosis and hypotonia. Diffusion MRI reported hypomyelination. Dysmorphic facial features including slight metopic ridge, ptosis, wide palpebral fissure length, sparse eyebrows, bulbous nose, thin upper lip, full cheeks, small chin, large ears, thin ear helix, and prominent antihelix were common findings in both patients. Molecular genetic analysis indicated c.319C>T(p. Arg107Cys) common genetic variant in our 2 patients. In case 2, the second allele was a novel genetic variant.

CONCLUSION: For COXPD12 disease, facial features are considered the main diagnostic clue, such as hypotonia or lactic acidosis; thus, the characteristic facial phenotype will help clinicians diagnose the disease.

Combined oxidative phosphorylation deficiency-12 (COXPD12) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder with neurological findings and lactic acidosis.

In the literature, ptosis, cleft palate, hypertelorism, wide nose, and short columella have been reported in patients diagnosed with COXPD12.

We present completely similar dysmorphic facial features including slight metopic ridge, ptosis, wide palpebral fissure length, sparse eyebrows, bulbous nose, thin upper lip, full cheeks, small chin, large ears, thin ear helix, and prominent antihelix in 2 patients with two different EARS2 gene variants.

The images in this report highlight the characteristic facial phenotype and will help clinicians diagnose the disease.

Mitochondrial disorders are a group of clinically heterogeneous diseases resulting from dysfunction of the oxidative phosphorylation system and are commonly defined by lack of cellular energy. The most affected organs in mitochondrial disorders are those that depend on high-rate aerobic metabolism, such as heart, muscles, brain, nerves, kidneys, eyes, ears, and endocrine system; however, any organ can be affected. Depending on the specific type of mitochondrial disease, common symptoms include poor growth, encephalopathy, ataxia, muscle weakness, low muscle tone, seizures, lactic acidosis, cardiomyopathy, visual and hearing loss, developmental delay, and gastrointestinal problems [1]. In mitochondrial diseases, children may have dysmorphic features such as microcephaly, round face, low-set ears, a short neck, a high forehead, curved eyebrows, outside corners of the eyes that point downward, a prominent bridge of the nose, thin lips, and a small chin [2].

Combined oxidative phosphorylation deficiency-12 (COXPD12) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by lactic acidosis, hypotonia onset in infancy and delayed psychomotor development, or early developmental regression, associated with T2-weighted hyperintensities in the deep cerebral white matter, brainstem, and cerebellar white matter. It is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in the EARS2 gene on chromosome 16p [3]. To date, no dysmorphic features have been described in the literature except ptosis, cleft palate, hypertelorism, wide nose, and short columella [3–5]. Here, we present completely similar dysmorphic facial features in 2 patients with two different EARS2 gene variants.

A 2.5-month-old male presented to another hospital with respiratory failure, which began with grunting and moaning while waking up and required intubation for 7 days. Because of lactic acidosis and metabolic acidosis, they referred the patient to our clinic. His prenatal follow-up was normal. He was born full-term, 4,000 g birth weight to a 21-year-old hypothyroid G2P2 mother by cesarean section. His parents were non-consanguineous Turkish individuals; he had a healthy 2-year-old brother.

On physical examination, he had a weight of 4 kg (0.4th percentile, −2.65 SDS) and a height of 58 cm (16th percentile, −0.99 SDS). His head circumference was 39 cm (8th percentile, −1.36 SDS). His vital signs were normal, but he was hypotonic; he could not hold his head up despite being 3 months old. Dysmorphic facial features including a slight metopic ridge, ptosis, wide palpebral fissure length, sparse eyebrows, bulbous nose, thin upper lip, full cheeks, small chin, large ears, thin ear helix, and prominent antihelix were noticed (Fig. 1). Bilateral ophthalmologic examinations were normal.

Case 1 with slight metopic ridge, ptosis, wide palpebral fissure length, sparse eyebrows, bulbous nose, thin upper lip, full cheeks, small chin, large ears, thin ear helix, and prominent antihelix.

In his laboratory results, leukocyte count was 13,000/µL, hemoglobin count was 11 mg/dL, platelet count was 413,000/µL. AST was 170.9 U/L (reference range: <40), ALT: 153 (ref range: <41), creatine kinase: 156 U/L (reference range: <190). Levels of electrolytes including sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium, calcium, and phosphate were normal. Plasma lactate level was elevated at 95 mg/dL (reference range: 4–20); bicarbonate level was low at 19 mmol/L (reference range: 23–29). Serum ammonia levels were normal.

Plasma and urinary amino acids, urinary organic acids, and tandem mass spectrometry results were nonspecific. The abdominal ultrasonography showed a slightly enlarged liver (hepatomegaly). Transfontanelle ultrasonography was normal. Echocardiography demonstrated a patent foramen ovale.

A brain magnetic resonance imaging study showed diffusion restriction in the supratentorial deep white matter, around the ventricle, in the bilateral periaqueductal gray matter at the level of the basal ganglia, and in the dentate nuclei in the tegmentum. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) identified compound heterozygous likely pathogenic variants in EARS2 (NM_001083614.2): c.319C>T (p.Arg107Cys)/c.1294C>T (p.Arg432Ter); compound heterozygous variants were confirming the diagnosis of COXPD-12.

He is 1 year old now. He is hypotonic, can not even sit without support, and has prominent facial dysmorphic features.

Patient 2 was a 2-month-old boy, born at 39 weeks of gestation after a normal uncomplicated pregnancy and a normal spontaneous vaginal delivery; he was the third child of non-consanguineous Turkish parents. His birth weight was 2.75 kg. He had a healthy 3-year-old sister; the second child of the family had died of unknown cause when she was 2 months old. One month after birth, he started to reject breast milk and formula. He was admitted to emergency department with vomiting and inadequate oral intake. During observation, he exhibited some new symptoms, such as staring into space and loss of consciousness for short period 2 times; he was admitted to pediatric intensive care unit and levetiracetam was started. The electroencephalogram (EEG) was normal, but diffusion magnetic resonance imaging reported hypomyelination. The patient was referred to us for further examination.

On physical examination, his weight was 4 kg (2.8th percentile, −1.91 SDS), height was 56 cm (24.8th percentile, −0.68 SDS), head circumference was 38 cm (11th percentile, −1.22 SDS). Vital signs were normal, but he was hypotonic. Dysmorphic facial features were identical to those defined in Case 1 including a slight metopic ridge, ptosis, wide palpebral fissure length, sparse eyebrows, bulbous nose, thin upper lip, full cheeks, small chin, large ears, thin ear helix, and prominent antihelix (Fig. 2).

Case 2 with similar dysmorphic findings.

In his laboratory results, leukocyte count was 30,810/µL, neutrophil count was 5,650/µL, lymphocyte count was 21,280/µL, hemoglobin count was 9.4 mg/dL, platelet count was 200,000/µL. AST was 165.9 U/L (ref range: <40), ALT: 89 (ref range: <41), GGT: 241 U/L (ref range: 10–71), ALP: 704 U/L (ref range: 40–129), LDH: 674 U/L (ref range: 135–225); albumin level was 29 g/L (ref range: 39.7–49.4); levels of electrolytes including sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium, calcium, phosphate were normal. Plasma lactate level was elevated at 99 mg/dL (ref range: 4–20). Bicarbonate level was normal at 23.8 mmol/L (ref range: 23–29). Serum ammonia levels were normal. Intravenous bicarbonate treatment was arranged according to the daily blood gas monitorization of the patient. Sodium dichloroacetate was added at a dose of 25 mg/kg to the patient whose lactic acidosis did not subside on the 3rd day of hospitalization. Since he had fever, he was examined for sepsis, and the patient, who also had seizures, underwent lumbar puncture. Antibiotic therapy was started.

The patient, whose lactate elevation continued despite treatment, whose acidosis did not subside despite intravenous high-dose bicarbonate treatment, and who developed respiratory distress, was transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit for continued follow-up and treatment due to the need for continuous renal replacement therapy. Echocardiography was normal. He had severe hypoalbuminemia and received 1 g/kg albumin solution. Plasma alanine level was high. Urinary amino acids, urinary organic acids, and tandem mass spectrometry results were nonspecific. Dialysis with lactate-free dialysis fluid was applied to the patient, whose lactic acidosis continued despite IV and oral bicarbonate treatments. The patient, whose lactic acidosis did not subside despite dialysis, died due to respiratory arrest and hypotension. Although typical facial appearance suggested COXPD12, WES was performed on the patient.

WES identified compound heterozygous likely pathogenic variants in EARS2 (NM_001083614.2): c.319C>T (p.Arg107Cys)/c.1020del (p.Cys341ValfsTer23) confirming the diagnosis of COXPD12.c.1020del (p. Cys341ValfsTer23) was a novel variant.

Inborn metabolic diseases are present with a wide variety of clinical phenotypes. An increasing number of metabolic diseases associated with dysmorphic features are being recognized such as pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency, cholesterol biosynthetic disorders, peroxisomal disorders, multiple acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency, congenital disorders of glycosylation, and mitochondrial disorders [6]. Here we present two COXPD12 cases with completely identical phenotypic features including slight metopic ridge, ptosis, wide palpebral fissure length, sparse eyebrows, bulbous nose, thin upper lip, full cheeks, small chin, large ears, thin ear helix, and prominent antihelix.

Mitochondrial diseases are associated with short stature, neurosensory hearing loss, progressive external ophthalmoplegia, axonal neuropathy, diabetes mellitus, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and renal tubular acidosis and dysmorphic facial features such as microcephaly, round face, high forehead, low-set ears, and a short neck. Skin findings may include multiple lipomatosis, scaly pruritic erythema, reticular pigmentation, hypertrichosis, eczema, or vitiligo. Patients may also exhibit ophthalmologic abnormalities such as retinitis pigmentosa [2].

Mutations of EARS2 gene, which encodes the mitochondrial glutamyl-tRNA synthetase, cause leukoencephalopathy involving the thalamus and brainstem with high lactate, also known as COXPD12. COXPD12 is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder characterized by infant-onset hypotonia, lactic acidosis, agenesis of the corpus callosum, and early developmental regression [7]. Dysmorphic facial features had not been previously described in this disorder except for ptosis, hypertelorism, a wide nose, and a short columella.

Steenweg et al. [3] reported a patient with COXPD12 who had bilateral ptosis, and ophthalmoplegia, a severe visual defect, and absence of speech were present in a 6-year-old boy. Talim et al. [4] presented a boy with COXPD12 who died at 3 months; he had hypospadias and incomplete cleft palate and presented with hypotonia, failure to gain weight, and lactic acidosis in the neonatal period. Boutaud et al. [5] reported 2 sibling fetuses with corpus callosum agenesis and EARS2 gene mutation. Splenium agenesis, hypertelorism, wide nose, and short columella were found in one of the siblings, and microcephaly was found in the other. Our patients did not have hypospadias, cleft palate, splenium agenesis, hypertelorism, short columella, or microcephaly. A bulbous nose was found at our patients’ examination instead of a wide nose. In conclusion, after observing facial phenotype, the authors suspected COXPD-12 in the second patient based on the characteristic phenotype, neurologic abnormalities, and lactic acidosis. The images in this report highlight the characteristic facial phenotype and will help clinicians diagnose the disease.
