# Clonal haematopoiesis subtypes confer dose-dependent risk of serious bacterial infection.
**Authors:** Fewings E, Yu Z, Gutierrez JC, Truong B, Fiziev PP, McRae J, Schwartzentruber J, Bick AG, Natarajan P, Lareau CA, Ulirsch JC, Farh KK
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-77934-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-77934-x)

## Content

Clonal haematopoiesis (CH) arises from somatic mutations in haematopoietic stem cells that drive clonal expansion and increase haematological malignancy risk. Here we show that CH confers mutation- and dose-dependent risk of serious bacterial infection (SBI). Using a multi-parameter somatic variant calling and filtering approach, we identified 19,822 mutations across 43 CH genes in 40,560 of 590,537 individuals from three population cohorts (6.87%), validated in a combined analysis of 889,215 individuals. CH was associated with SBI development (HR = 1.22, P = 4.66 × 10⁻²⁹) and 28-day post-infection mortality (RR = 1.84, P = 0.0012), independent of malignancy and robust to adjustment for inflammatory comorbidities and ancestry. Recurrent mutations in SRSF2, DNMT3A, SF3B1, IDH2, and NRAS (n = 2143) conferred substantial absolute risks: 22-25% of individuals with expanded SRSF2 P95 clones developed SBI within 10 years. These associations were consistent across diverse populations and largely independent of bacterial strain.

Clonal haematopoiesis (CH) occurs when somatic mutations in haematopoietic stem cells (HSPCs) provide a relative growth or survival advantage to mutated HSPCs 1– 3 . The chance of acquiring one or more of these mutations increases with age, leading to detectable CH in peripheral blood populations for over 10% of individuals by age 65 1 , 3 , 4 . As clones expand and grow, so does the risk of developing a haematopoietic malignancy 5 , 6 . In addition to greatly increasing the risk of haematological malignancy, several lines of evidence from epidemiological studies and animal models have linked CH with non-malignant, age-related inflammatory conditions including coronary heart disease (CHD 7 ), stroke 8 , chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD 9 ), chronic kidney disease (CKD 10 ), and chronic liver disease (CLD 11 ). In some instances, CH may be a consequence of the immune system’s response to pathogenic infections across decades 12 , 13 ; however, the mechanisms by which CH leads to immune system dysfunction remain poorly understood 12 .

CH is increasingly recognized as a set of similar, but distinct, subtypes whose phenotypic presentation corresponds to the underlying mutation(s) and affected gene(s) 4 , 12 , 14 . For example, DNMT3A clones are the most common but expand slowly and have mild clinical impacts, SRSF2 clones expand quickly, and are therefore often identified with larger clone sizes, and are preferentially linked to myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). More broadly, increasing clone size, as measured by variant allele fraction (VAF), has been associated with higher risks of haematological malignancy and other adverse clinical outcomes 12 . JAK2 / TET2 clones are most strongly linked to CLD 15 . Although CH has been tentatively linked to mildly increased rates of bacterial infection 4 , 16 , 17 , it is unclear if there are high risk molecular subtypes driving this association.

In this study, we investigated the risk of developing a serious bacterial infection (SBI) among 889,215 individuals (including 590,537 individuals in a three-cohort discovery phase) with specific CH-driver mutations in five distinct population cohorts 18– 21 . We identified molecular subtypes based upon the mutated gene or hotspot residue that showed a significant and dose-dependent (extent of clonal expansion) association with infection risk and severity prior to, or independent from, a clinical diagnosis of myeloid malignancy. Interestingly, individuals with specific combinations of subtypes had higher rates of infection than those with either mutation alone. Notably, the observed increase in infection rate was largely independent of the causal pathogen. The scale of these analyses enables quantification of infection risk heterogeneity across molecular subtypes of clonal haematopoiesis. Overall, this study defines mutation- and clone-size–specific risk of serious bacterial infection and refines the non-malignant clinical spectrum of clonal haematopoiesis.

Using a comprehensive variant calling and filtering approach, we identified somatic mutations in previously defined CH genes in the UKBiobank (UKBB) 18 , Genomics England (GEL) 22 , Alliance for Genomic Discovery (AGD), All of Us (AoU) 21 , and Mass General Brigham Biobank (MGBB) 20 cohorts (Table 1 and Fig. S1). Briefly, after calling likely somatic variants with Mutect2 23 , we further enriched for real somatic mutations by requiring a low median variant allele fraction (VAF) across carriers of novel mutations, performing read-backed phasing of known germline variants and putative somatic variants, accounting for local germline copy number, and filtering to mutations with predicted functional or pathogenic effects 24 (Fig. S1 and Supplementary Data 1). Notably, individuals with somatic variants identified by Mutect2 but removed by our filters had a similar age range to non-carriers (Cohen’s d score = 0.19) and were younger than individuals with CH (Cohen’s d score = 0.55, Fig. 1), consistent with a germline origin for mutations that were filtered out. In the discovery cohorts (UKBB, GEL, AGD), 19,822 unique mutations were detected across 43 CH genes in 40,560 individuals (6.9% of 590,537, Table 1), including 5,187 individuals with large, expanded clones that substantially contribute to mature blood cell populations (VAF > 0.4). Consistent with previous studies 4 , 25 , DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 were the most frequently mutated (Fig. 1b). Comparing between the discovery and validation cohorts (discovery plus AoU, MGBB), the relative prevalences of molecular subtypes based upon specific genes (SRSF2, SF3B1, TET2, ASXL1, DNMT3A) or recurrently mutated residues (SRSF2 P95, SF3B1 K700, DNMT3A R882, NRAS G12, IDH2 R140) were similar (Table 1).

This study leveraged extensive clinical records to establish the timing of clonal haematopoiesis detection (using the sample extraction date as the genotyping date) and to ascertain incident diagnoses during subsequent follow-up. This design enabled assessment of associations between CH and later disease in a retrospective cohort framework. Gene-level associations between CH and disease phenotypes were identified using Cox proportional hazards models followed by fixed-effect meta-analysis across the discovery cohorts. (Fig. 2a, Fig. S1 and Supplementary Data 3). Subsequent hotspot- and clone size-specific analyses were performed using pooled individual-level data from these cohorts in a mega-analysis framework (Fig. S1). As expected, CH was strongly associated with the risk of developing haematological malignancy (HRmeta = 2.23, Pmeta = 3.82 × 10−78), and higher rates of these malignancies were observed for expanded clones (VAF > 0.4) (HRmeta = 3.41, Pmeta = 1.04 × 10−82). In subtype analyses (defined by the presence of somatic mutations in known driver genes), we observed significant (FDR < 0.05) associations for 17 out of 43 CH genes. Notably, subtypes defined by molecular lesions in DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, SRSF2, and SF3B1 exhibited strong, dose-dependent (size of clonal expansion) relationships with haematological malignancy (all Pmeta < 3.67 × 10−12, Fig. 2d and Supplementary Data 4). Finally, we tested for associations between CH and other types of cancer, finding significantly higher rates of lung cancer (HRmeta = 1.47, Pmeta = 9.81 × 10−16) and breast cancer (HRmeta = 1.09, Pmeta = 0.01). In addition to malignant outcomes, we investigated whether CH was associated with benign sequelae of immune system dysfunction in individuals with no previously diagnosed haematological malignancy. First, we tested whether CH was associated with age-related inflammatory diseases, replicating previous associations with coronary heart disease (CHD) (HRmeta = 1.09, Pmeta = 7.29 × 10−12) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) (HRmeta = 1.10, Pmeta = 1.70 × 10−6) and, to a lesser extent, chronic liver disease (CLD) (HRmeta = 1.05, Pmeta = 0.03). Next, we tested whether CH was associated with viral or bacterial infection, finding a significant (FDR < 0.05) association between CH and SBI, which includes sepsis and bacterial pneumonia (HRmeta = 1.22, Pmeta = 1.51 × 10−29, Fig. 2a and Supplementary Data 3), but not CH and viral infections (HRmeta = 1.05, Pmeta = 0.14, Fig. 2a and Supplementary Data 3). To assess whether this association was confounded by baseline inflammatory comorbidities, we repeated analyses adjusting for prevalent CHD, CKD, and CLD. Associations between CH subtypes and SBI remained significant after adjustment, with hazard ratios attenuated by ~10–15% but preserving the observed dose-dependent relationships (Supplementary Data 5).

Sensitivity analyses incorporating age² and precomputed genetic principal components (where available) yielded nearly identical effect estimates (<2% change in hazard ratios), with negligible changes in model fit statistics (Supplementary Data 6), indicating minimal confounding by non-linear age effects or ancestry. Ethnicity information was available in the UKBB and AGD cohorts and was incorporated into a combined analysis of 564,373 individuals from these datasets. CH–SBI associations were consistent across major ancestry groups, with no evidence of heterogeneity (Cochran Q P = 0.41; I² = 0%). Adjustment for ethnicity did not materially alter effect estimates (Supplementary Data 2), supporting robustness across populations.

Although we only considered infections in individuals with no diagnosed haematological malignancy, the subtype and dose-dependent associations between CH and SBI closely resembled those between CH and haematological malignancy. Specifically, seven out of the eight CH subtypes associated with haematological malignancy were also significantly associated with SBI (Fig. 2a). Moreover, clonal expansion overall (haematological malignancy: HRmeta = 3.41, Pmeta = 1.04 × 10−82; SBI: HRmeta = 1.49, Pmeta = 1.10 × 10−38) and within subtypes defined by DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, SRSF2, and SF3B1 had correspondingly higher rates of both SBI and haematological malignancy (Fig. 2a). Notably, individuals with large, expanded splicing factor gene clones (VAF > 0.4) had some of the highest risks for developing SBI (HRmeta = 5.26, Pmeta = 1.51 × 10−11 for SRSF2; HRmeta = 5.45, Pmeta = 3.11 × 10−5 for SF3B1), but even clones with DNMT3A mutations, typically considered the most mild subtype 15 , displayed greatly elevated rates of SBI once expanded (HRmeta = 2.63, Pmeta = 1.39 × 10−8) (Fig. 2d). To further exclude the possibility that occult or diagnosed malignancy contributed to these findings, we repeated analyses after excluding individuals with any prior cancer diagnosis (haematologic or solid). Dose-dependent associations between CH subtypes and SBI were preserved in the cancer-free population (Supplementary Data 7), indicating that results are not driven by malignancy.

Formal pairwise z-tests comparing log-transformed hazard ratios confirmed that SBI risk differed significantly across selected CH subtypes after accounting for standard errors (Supplementary Data 8). In particular, SRSF2-associated risk exceeded that of DNMT3A and TET2 across clone-size strata, while SF3B1-associated risk exceeded DNMT3A and TET2 in medium and large clones; SRSF2 and SF3B1 did not differ significantly among large clones. We next further explored the role of 5 commonly mutated residues (somatic hotspots) associated with haematological malignancy as exemplar clonal markers (SRSF2 P95 26– 28 , SF3B1 K700 29 , DNMT3A R882, NRAS G12, IDH2 R140 30 , 31 , Table 1). These markers allow for more straightforward comparisons across CH subtypes by reducing bias due to (1) residual germline variant contamination in large clonal expansions and (2) heterogeneity in variant effects within genes. Carriers of these clonal mutations showed an increased incidence of haematological cancers or MDS, and SBI (Fig. 2b). In contrast, KRAS-mutant clones did not show a significant association with SBI (HR = 1.19, P = 0.23), despite similar carrier numbers to NRAS G12, supporting mutation-specific rather than pathway-wide effects. While individuals with no identifiable clone had a haematological cancer or MDS incidence rate of 0.49% after 15 years, clone carriers had an incidence rate of 1.10% over the same period. Similarly, individuals with no identifiable clone had a SBI incidence rate of 5.46% and clone carriers had an incidence rate of 8.49%. To determine whether CH-associated mutations remained informative once haematological malignancy was already established, we looked at the association of CH with SBI in individuals previously diagnosed with haematological cancer or MDS (Fig. 2c). Carriers of recurrent clonal mutations retained their association with SBI, suggesting that these mutations are robust markers of infectious disease outside of their role in haematological malignancies (Supplementary Data 9). Moreover, to understand the extent to which cytopenia disorders may be impacting our results, we repeated analysis in the UKBB cohort excluding individuals with leukopenia, neutropenia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytopenia by clinical standards using available blood count data. Mutations at residues SRSF2 P95, SF3B1 K700, DNMT3A R882, NRAS G12, and IDH2 R140 remained significantly associated with the development of a serious bacterial infection in individuals with no detectable cytopenia (HR = 3.52, P = 2.62 × 10−22 for SRSF2 P95; HR = 2.36, P = 4.50 × 10−3 for SF3B1 K700; HR = 1.53, P = 1.77 × 10−5 for DNMT3A R882; HR = 3.60, P = 1.05 × 10−2 for NRAS G12, HR = 5.00, P < 8.75 × 10−16 for IDH2 R140) (Supplementary Data 10). Consistent with the cytopenia sensitivity analysis, exclusion of 26,821 individuals with anaemia-related disorders (ICD10 D60–D64) did not materially alter the observed associations between CH and SBI in the UKBB cohort. Associations remained significant for SRSF2 P95 (HR = 3.23, P = 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁴), DNMT3A R882 (HR = 1.51, P = 2.1 × 10⁻⁴), NRAS G12 (HR = 5.58, P = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴), and IDH2 R140 (HR = 3.78, P = 2.7 × 10⁻⁷)(Supplementary Data 10). SF3B1 K700 remained directionally associated with SBI (HR = 1.62) but was no longer statistically significant following exclusion (P = 0.24), potentially reflecting the relatively small number of remaining carriers (n = 58).

In the discovery cohort, we observed that 0.23% (n = 1329) of individuals had medium or large clonal P95, K700, R882, G12, or R140 expansions. In a mega-analysis, 25% of individuals with no diagnosed haematological malignancy and a large clonal P95 expansion developed a SBI within 10 years of follow-up time (Pmega = 5.78 × 10−19, Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 11). Large clonal R882 expansions were more common but only 9% of individuals developed a SBI in the same time-frame (Pmega = 6.88 × 10−5, Fig. 3c and Supplementary Data 11). To explore whether CH mutation carriers who develop SBI are at increased risk of haematological progression, we examined subsequent blood cancer diagnoses among CH carriers using Cox proportional hazards models with SBI status as the exposure. Among CH carriers with complete covariate data, SBI was associated with a substantially higher risk of subsequent haematological malignancy after adjustment for age and sex (HR = 3.34, 95% CI 2.60–4.30, P = 7.89 × 10⁻²¹). This association remained similar after additional adjustment for clone size (maximum VAF; HR = 3.24, 95% CI 2.48–4.22, P = 5.84 × 10⁻¹⁸), suggesting that the relationship was not explained by clonal burden alone (Supplementary Data 13). Gene-stratified analyses showed consistent associations for ASXL1, DNMT3A, TET2 and SRSF2, while estimates for SF3B1 were imprecise due to the small number of carriers with both SBI and haematological malignancy.

Finally, we investigated whether CH impacted SBI severity assessed by 28-day mortality rates following SBI in comparison to all individuals diagnosed with SBI. Restricting to individuals with a hotspot subtype, we observed that expanded clone sizes were significantly associated with 28-day mortality (RRmeta = 1.29, Pmeta = 0.0012, Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 14). Large clonal P95 expansions had the highest 28-day mortality rates (RRmeta = 1.77, Pmeta = 0.0028, Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 14).

To investigate if carrying multiple CH mutations impacted SBI, we performed a mega-analysis in the discovery cohorts comparing carriers of multiple clonal mutations to carriers of single mutations in the genes previously explored. Overall, we found that 820 individuals carried more than one clonal mutation. Focusing first on the most common hotspot overlap (SRSF2 P95 with TET2 or IDH2 mutations), we observed that 18% SRSF2 P95-only carriers had a SBI within 10 years of follow-up, whereas 36% of individuals with an additional IDH2 mutation had a SBI over the same period (Pmega = 3.51 × 10−5, Fig. 4c, Supplementary Data 15). Consistent with a stepwise accumulation of mutations during expansion 32 , P95 clones were more expanded when individuals carried an additional TET2 or IDH2 mutation (Pmega = 8.10 × 10−5 and 8.90 × 10−7, respectively, Fig. 4d). Controlling for P95 clonal fraction moderated but did not fully account for the increased risk of SBI (Pmega <1 × 10−300). Interaction analyses demonstrated that co-occurring mutations were consistent with multiplicative effects on the log-hazard scale, with no statistically significant deviation from additivity (all interaction P > 0.4; Fig. 4e and Supplementary Data 15).

In addition to single nucleotide or short indel variants, somatic copy number variants (CNVs) have been shown to drive CH 16 . Previous work has shown that CH subtypes defined by clonal CNV expansions are associated with higher rates of infection 16 . Here, we compared rates of SBI between CH subtypes from this study, CH due to somatic CNVs (deletions only), and carriers of both subtypes. In the UKBB cohort, we observed that CH due to either short variants (SNPs or short indels) or CNVs were significantly associated with SBI (P = 8.55 × 10−66 and 5.93 × 10−82, respectively) (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Data 16). However, individuals with DNMT3A R882 or SRSF2 P95 mutations had a higher risk of SBI when they were also carriers of a somatic CNV (P = 0.014 and 0.011, respectively; Fig. 5b, Supplementary Data 17). Overall, CH driven by SNPs and short indels conferred a higher risk of SBI than CNVs reported previously. Specifically, carriers of SNP or short-indel–associated expanded clones (VAF > 40%) had more than a two-fold greater risk of SBI (HR = 7.90) compared with carriers of CNV-driven clones reported in earlier studies (HR = 2.41; Fig. 5a and Supplementary Data 16)¹⁵. Primary bacterial strains detected in culture as part of the SBI diagnostic process were available in the AGD cohort. Of the 7262 individuals diagnosed with SBI, 3520 (48%) had a specific strain detected. In both CH carriers and non-carriers, the most common strains were Staphylococcus, E. coli, Streptococcus, and Klebsiella. The proportion of strains did not differ by CH status (P = 0.88), nor were there any apparent strain-specific differences in SBI rates across CH subtypes (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Data 18, Fig. S4), suggesting the effect of CH on SBI is largely strain independent.

Hospitalisation records in the AGD cohort were used to assess the association between CH and SBI in individuals with a recent hospital visit in comparison to those with no recorded visit. CH (as defined by any of the previously described molecular subtypes) was significantly associated with SBI development in both individuals with a hospitalisation less than 100 days prior to infection (HR = 2.20, P = 1.28 × 10−5), and those without any record of hospitalisation (HR = 2.00, P = 4.25 × 10−7) (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Data 19).

To explore potential biological correlates of CH, we examined plasma proteomic profiles in 53,030 UKBB participants with Olink Explore measurements, including 2018 CH carriers. After adjustment for age and smoking status, CH was associated with significantly lower circulating FLT3 ligand (FLT3LG; β = −0.166, FDR = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰) and higher FLT3 receptor abundance (β = 0.093, FDR = 0.043), suggesting altered haematopoietic progenitor signalling in CH carriers (Supplementary Data 20). In contrast, classical inflammatory cytokines including IL6 and TNF were not significantly altered after correction for multiple testing.

Understanding the association between specific somatic mutations in HSPCs and immune system function is key to identifying high-risk individuals and improving biological risk stratification. By highlighting individual driver mutations that greatly disturb immune system function, this study provides a more specific understanding of the relationship between CH molecular subtypes and infectious diseases in geographically and ethnically diverse populations.

Of the well-studied and often commonly mutated genes (including DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1) found in our discovery cohort, rare mutations in splicing factor genes SRSF2 and SF3B1, which have been shown to affect the differentiation of stem and progenitor cells in the blood 29 , 33 , 34 , coincided with higher rates of both haematological malignancies and SBIs. Five specific amino acid changes were noted for their more extreme association with SBI. Among those mutations, SRSF2 P95 and SF3B1 K700 had the most robust association, with 25% and 18% of large clone carriers respectively developing a SBI within 10 years. Notably, these results persisted in individuals who were never diagnosed with haematological malignancy, MDS, a cytopenia disorder, or aplastic anaemia.

Proteomic profiling provided additional support that CH is accompanied by altered haematopoietic signalling rather than a simple systemic inflammatory cytokine signature. Consistent with recent plasma proteomic analyses linking CHIP, particularly TET2-mutant CHIP, to altered FLT3LG abundance 35 , we observed perturbation of the FLT3/FLT3LG axis in CH carriers. These findings point toward altered haematopoietic progenitor biology as a potential correlate of clonal expansion, although further mechanistic studies will be required to define causal pathways linking CH subtypes to SBI risk. Clonal expansions with DNMT3A R882 mutations impacted infectious disease risk to a lesser extent than splicing factor counterparts, with only 9% of carriers developing a SBI within 10 years. However, these hotspot mutations occur frequently in a general population (DNMT3A R882 occurs at ~3.5x the frequency of SRSF2 P95 mutations, Table 1). Therefore, mutations affecting DNMT3A R882 may be linked to a greater number of absolute SBI cases in a general population than its splicing factor counterparts SRSF2 P95 and SF3B1 K700 and may contribute substantially to overall SBI burden at the population level.

Interestingly, no association was identified between CH and viral infections, including severe COVID-19 infection, in this study, despite previous reports linking CH to increased susceptibility to adverse COVID-19 outcomes 4 . This may reflect differences in the underlying immune mechanisms governing bacterial and viral host defence, variation in viral endpoint definitions, or reduced statistical power for individual viral pathogens.

Our results showcase stark differences in patient risk for SBI based not only on the existence of a haematological clone, but on that clone’s unique molecular subtype and its subsequent expansion rate. For example, co-occurring mutations in SRSF2 P95/TET2, SRSF2 P95/IDH2, and TET2/SF3B1 increased the risk of SBI when compared to either TET2 or SRSF2 P95 mutations alone. It should be noted that our results reflect a single sampling event of clones with varying growth rates. For example, clonal expansions in SRSF2 P95H carriers become dominant in the blood within 11 years 15 . As such, longitudinal sampling may be necessary to capture the more immediate risk of CH across subtypes 15 .

Mortality rates following SBI in hospitalised adults (60 years or older) is estimated to be between 37% and 49% 36 . In this study, we identified molecular subtypes of CH that may prove to be effective biomarkers of SBI. These subtypes are readily measurable and may inform future risk stratification efforts.

Phenotypes were provided at initial assessment and were updated through routinely available national health datasets 18 . From the 500,000 registered UKBB participants, 448,083 participants were selected for analysis based on the availability of whole exome sequencing data and matching phenotypic information. Participants were aged between 37 and 73 years old at time of registration (median age: 58 years old) and 54% were females.

Whole genome sequencing and matching phenotypic data was acquired for 50,243 participants (of 100,000 enrolled) from Genomics England (GEL) (Research Registry ID: RR726). The average age of participants at time of blood extraction was 49 years with 55% females.

Whole genome sequencing data from the Alliance for Genomic Discovery (AGD) cohort was accessed via the Illumina Connected Analytics (ICA) platform. Of the 124,000 enrolled participants, 92,211 were selected for inclusion in further analysis on the basis of phenotypic data availability. The average age of participants at time of blood extraction was 60 years with 59% females.

Summary statistics from logistic model association tests between genotype categories and disease were provided for 53,345 individuals from Mass General Brigham Biobank (MGBB) using whole exome sequencing data (average age at time of blood extraction: 54 years, 56% females), and 245,333 individuals from the All of Us (AoU) dataset using whole genome sequencing data (average age at time of blood extraction: 56 years, 61% females).

UKBB cohort whole exome sequencing CRAM files aligned to reference genome hg38 were made available through the DNAnexus portal. A DNAnexus runnable applet was built to generate variant call files (VCFs) containing potential clonal mutations. Hg38 aligned BAM files from Genomics England whole genome sequencing data were made available on the Genomics England research environment and AGD whole genome sequencing data was provided through the ICA platform. For all datasets, the Genome Analysis Toolkit’s (v4.3.0.0) Mutect2 variant caller was run on each CRAM/BAM file, using both a germline resource VCF and panel of normals to compensate for the lack of comparative normal tissue 23 . Intervals covering the exons of 43 previously defined CH genes were supplied with an additional padding of 10 base pairs 25 . To allow for the detection of small clones, minimum variant allele fraction (VAF) filters were lowered to 0. FilterMutectCalls tool was run on the resulting VCF, filtering for a minimum of 2 reads covering the alternative allele.

Indels were left-aligned and normalised and multiallelic sites were split into multiple rows using bcftools norm (v1.9) 37 . Processed VCF files were merged into batches for speed of processing. Variants were annotated with COSMIC IDs and COSMIC allele counts 38 , then annotated with Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) (v91.3-0) 39 . Batches were merged per dataset to allow for further variant quality filtering.

Quality filters were applied to ensure variants had a median GERMQ score (a quality score generated by Mutect2) of greater than 10 and a median POPAF score (a score based on the variants presence in the supplied germline resource VCF) of greater than 3. Variants had to be labelled as “PASS” for the Mutect2 internal filters in at least one occurrence. Genotypes were set to missing if the site was covered by fewer than 7 reads, or if allelic depth was fewer than 4 reads (or fewer than 2 reads for variants previously described in COSMIC) 38 , 40 . Using population allele frequency (AF) annotations from GnomAD 40 , high or moderate impact variants (as labelled by VEP) were filtered to select for those with an AF less than 0.0005. Variants in a list of known CH associated regions or variants seen in 10 or more COSMIC cases were filtered to select for those with an AF less than 0.001. Additional filtering tags were applied to the resulting variants as described below.

Copy number data for UKBB individuals was downloaded from UKBB return 3094 and EIDs were converted to align with the application specific EIDs used in this study 16 .

Variants were grouped by their unique variant ID and the range of VAFs that had been observed were assessed. A true clonal variant is likely to exist on some but not all haplotype reads and is likely to be heterozygous. Therefore, true clonal mutations are likely to have a VAF of below 0.35 for heterozygous variants, whereas heterozygous germline variants exist with a VAF of around 0.5. Variants were labelled with the ‘PASS’ tag if the median VAF across all observations is less than 0.35.

Following the above principles, that a true clonal mutation is likely to exist on some but not all haplotype reads, we used phasing to tag likely clonal mutations. On the UK Biobank DNAnexus portal, Genomics England research environment and ICA platform, we pulled all reads covering potential mutations from the original CRAM/BAM files. We then assessed whether those reads also covered a common, heterozygous, germline SNP (SNPs occurring more than 5 times in GnomAD germline database) 40 . This threshold was selected to conservatively exclude common germline polymorphisms while retaining rare somatic variants unlikely to represent population variation. Using that germline SNP, we divided the reads into haplotypes. Variants were labelled with the ‘PASS’ tag if they existed in only one of the haplotypes, and if, within that haplotype, they did not exist on all reads. This flag was only possible for a small number of variants which coexisted on reads with a common germline SNP. If this was not the case, a variant was labelled as ‘UNCALLABLE’ for this tag.

Applying a hard variant allele fraction cut-off for individual variants where there may be allelic imbalance during the PCR amplification process, leading to one allele being sequenced more than another, can be problematic 41 . To mitigate this, we assessed the VAFs of all common, heterozygous, germline SNPs (SNPs occurring more than 5 times in GnomAD germline database) within a 1 megabase region of our variants of interest 40 . We applied a cumulative density function to our germline variants to determine the likelihood that a potential clonal variant’s VAF deviates significantly from the germline VAFs in the region. Variants with a significant P value for this test (Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate corrected p value of less than 0.01) were labelled with the ‘PASS’ tag.

PrimateAI-3D variant annotation tool was used to assess variant pathogenicity 24 . Variants were labelled with the ‘PASS’ tag if their PrimateAI-3D score was above the median score per gene.

A final list of clonal variants was created from variants that were labelled as ‘PASS’ by the AF based tag and Median VAF tag, in addition to being labelled ‘PASS’ or ‘UNCALLABLE’ by the phasing tag, neighbourhood germline VAF tag, and PrimateAI-3D tag. Variants that passed the above filter in any tested individual were included in the final analysis.

Differences in median age between clone carriers and non-carriers was assessed by Cohen’s d score, where scores of above 0.5 reflect a moderate effect size in a large population. Association between clonal mutations in previously described CH genes and phenotypes was tested using a Cox proportional hazard model, correcting for age and sex. Sensitivity analyses additionally incorporated a quadratic age term (age²) and precomputed genetic principal components (where available) to account for potential non-linear age effects and population stratification. Tested ICD10 codes were grouped into related phenotypes (Supplementary Data 21) where time of disease was taken as the earliest date of diagnosis for ICD10 codes in the phenotype group. Analysis start time was taken as the date of blood sample collection. End time was either the diagnosis date of phenotype, diagnosis date of haematological malignancy, recorded date of death, or last date of follow-up if none of the previous events occurred. Results of this analysis in UKBB, GEL, and AGD datasets were compiled into a meta-analysis using a fixed effects model from the metafor R library (v4.6-0) 42 . Significant genotype-phenotype relationships were identified as those with a false discovery rate below 0.05 and a hazard ratio above 1.2. The hazard ratio threshold (HR > 1.2) was prespecified to focus on associations of potential clinical relevance rather than statistical significance alone.

Genes with an increasing relationship with both haematological cancer or MDS and SBI as clone size increases were further explored. Within these genes, residues driving strong associations were identified by subsetting carriers of mutations at each commonly mutated amino acid and comparing their association to disease in comparison to non-carriers. Residue-based hazard ratios and p values were then compared to those derived from the whole gene to identify any residues which have a stronger effect on disease than the whole gene if applicable.

Variant calls affecting genes of interest (DNMT3A, ASXL1, TET2, SRSF2, SF3B1, and IDH2), or specific residues of interest DNMT3A R882, SRSF2 P95, SF3B1 K700, IDH2 R140, or NRAS G12 from UKBB, GEL, and AGD data were anonymised, including jittering ages at disease diagnosis and blood extraction (by a standard deviation of 0.5) and binning variant allele fractions into ‘small’, ‘medium’, and ‘large’ clone sizes (small: 0 < VAF < = 0.1, medium: 0.1 < VAF < = 0.4, large: 0.4 < VAF < 1). Unless otherwise specified, expanded clones refer to VAF > 0.4. Data from the three studies was compiled into a large mega-analysis of 590,537 samples where survival analysis was performed using a Cox proportional hazard model as described above.

To test for a relationship between clone size and disease risk, individuals were defined as having a small, medium, or large sized clone based on VAF (small: 0 < VAF < = 0.1, medium: 0.1 < VAF < = 0.4, large: 0.4 < VAF < 1). Cox proportional hazard models were run per HUGO gene symbol, for small, medium, and large clones separately.

Cumulative incidence of haematological cancer or MDS and SBI was assessed using R library casebase (v0.10.6), fitting smooth hazard models on carriers of mutations at DNMT3A R882, SRSF2 P95, SF3B1 K700, IDH2 R140, or NRAS G12 and non-carriers separately.

The association of mutated residues with 28-day mortality with SBI was assessed by performing a Cox proportional hazards model for medium and large sized clones. Individuals with a recorded date of death within 28 days of recorded SBI diagnosis were compared to individuals with SBI and either no recorded date of death or date of death more than 28 days after SBI diagnosis. The impact of cytopenias on results was assessed using raw blood count data provided by UKBB. Leukopenia was defined as any individual with a white blood cell/leukocyte count of below 4. Neutropenia was defined as any individual with a neutrophil count of below 1.5. Lymphopenia was defined as any individual with a lymphocyte count of below 1. Thrombocytopenia was defined as any individual with a platelet count of below 150.

Carriers of more than one clonal mutation were assessed for their association to disease in comparison to individuals with no identifiable mutation and those with a single mutation via a Cox proportional hazard model. Clone size of different mutation carriers was compared in a two-sided t test with equal variances. For UKBB dataset only (due to data availability), carriers of CNVs in addition to mutations affecting residues of interest were compared to individuals with no identifiable mutation and those with a single mutation via a Cox proportional hazard model.

Bacterial strains driving the CH-SBI relationship were assessed in the AGD dataset only (due to data availability). Where available, individuals were labelled with an identified bacterial strain where the date of diagnosis for that strain was within 30 days of SBI diagnosis. Carriers of clonal mutations at DNMT3A R882, SRSF2 P95, SF3B1 K700, IDH2 R140, or NRAS G12 were compared to non-carriers for an association to SBI driven by a specific bacterial strain (including Pseudomonas, Enterococcus, Escherichia coli, Mycobacteria, Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, or Klebsiella) via a Cox proportional hazard model.

Hospitalisation records in the AGD dataset were accessed where available. A prior related hospitalisation event was defined as any inpatient hospital visit within 100 days of an identified SBI diagnosis.

To validate the association between somatic mutations in ASXL1, DNMT3A, TET2, SF3B1, SRSF2, IDH2, and NRAS and bacterial sepsis or pneumonia infections, results were replicated in two additional datasets. For data from AoU and MGBB cohorts, Samtools Mpileup 43 was used to call variants at 19,822 positions in the above genes that passed filters in UKBB, GEL and AGD data as per the previously described specifications. Identical genotype-level filters (read depth, allelic depth, VAF thresholds and functional impact criteria) were applied to the validation cohorts to ensure consistency with the discovery pipeline. This validation was performed in 53,345 samples from MGBB, and 245,333 samples from the AoU dataset (Table 1).

Due to limitations in data transfer and data availability, summary statistics from a logistic model testing an association between the presence of any validated variant in ASXL1, DNMT3A, TET2, SF3B1, and SRSF2 and SBI in UKBB, GEL, AGD, MGBB, and AoU, were aggregated and combined into a meta-analysis using a fixed effects model from the metafor R library (v4.6-0) 42 . In each dataset, variants were grouped into genes and tested for their association with haematological cancers or MDS and SBI in a logistic model compared to individuals with no detected clone, correcting for sex and age at DNA extraction. Variants were further grouped by clone size using VAF as a marker (small: 0 < VAF < = 0.1, medium: 0.1 < VAF < = 0.4, large: 0.4 < VAF < 1) and tested each association with disease. To test for an increasing association with disease correlating with an increase in clone size, the categorical clone size variable was converted to a continuous variable (no clone: 0, small: 1, medium: 2, large: 3) and tested in a logistic model correcting for sex and age at DNA extraction.

Plasma proteomic associations with CH were assessed in 53,030 UKBB participants with available Olink Explore proteomics data, including 2018 CH carriers and 51,012 non-carriers. Protein abundance values were inverse rank-normalised before analysis. CH carrier status was defined using the same filtered CH variant calls described above, restricted to mutations in the main CH genes analysed in this study (DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, SF3B1 and SRSF2) or recurrent hotspot mutations where relevant. For each protein, linear regression was used to test the association between protein abundance and CH carrier status, adjusting for age at sample collection and smoking status. Analyses were performed for all CH carriers compared with non-carriers, and separately for CH gene subtypes and hotspot mutations where carrier counts were sufficient. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate within each comparison set.
