# Alveolus-on-a-chip: A Novel Tool for Modeling Lung Transplant Cold Storage Ischemia/Reperfusion Injury.
**Authors:** Langerude L, Gill B, Taylor G, Patel K, Nadig S, McCarthy J, Mulligan J, Tu Z, Atkinson C
**Journal:** Transplantation (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1097/TP.0000000000005682](https://doi.org/10.1097/TP.0000000000005682)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Current in vitro models used to investigate lung transplant cold storage (CS) ischemia/reperfusion injury (IRI) use single-cell lines in static culture. Although these models have informed our understanding of CS-IRI, they lack physiological complexity. We hypothesized that using a lung alveolus-on-a-chip model would facilitate the development of more physiologically relevant CS-IRI in vitro models.

METHODS: We used the Emulate Organ-on-a-Chip 3-dimensional microfluidic air-liquid interface alveolus-on-a-chip model seeded with human primary alveolar epithelial cells and human lung microvascular endothelial cells. To simulate CS and IRI, alveolus chips were removed from the perfusion, and endothelial channels were perfused with Perfadex and stored at 4 °C for 6 h to recapitulate CS. Following CS, alveolar chips were reperfused under laminar media flow and stretch conditions for 24 h at 37 °C to simulate IRI. The impact of CS-IRI was compared with that of control alveolus chips using immunofluorescent staining for cellular markers, chemokine/adhesion molecule arrays to assess epithelial and endothelial injury/activation, and mRNA collected for NanoString analysis.

RESULTS: Brightfield imaging and immunostaining indicated a loss of barrier function after CS-IRI. Assessment of chemokines and adhesion molecules demonstrated significant increases in markers of inflammation. NanoString analysis identified 42 human primary alveolar epithelial cell and 49 human lung microvascular endothelial cell genes that were significantly differentially expressed between the normal and CS-IRI groups ( P  < 0.01).

CONCLUSIONS: Here, we report a novel CS-IRI model that incorporates physiological flow, stretch, and cell-cell interactions. We demonstrated key changes associated with IRI and hypothesized that future studies will provide novel insights into the pathophysiology of CS-IRI post-lung transplantation.

Primary graft dysfunction (PGD) is the leading cause of early morbidity and mortality after lung transplantation (LTx) and a major risk factor for chronic lung allograft dysfunction.1-3 At the core of PGD pathogenesis, ischemia/reperfusion injury (IRI) is an inevitable consequence of the physical challenges applied during donor organ procurement, static cold storage (CS), and revascularization during implantation. IRI initiates a cascade of inflammatory and oxidative stress responses that disrupt the alveolar-capillary barrier, resulting in pulmonary edema, impaired gas exchange, and early graft failure.4,5

Although rodent LTx models have provided important insight, their technical complexity, low throughput, and interspecies differences limit translational discovery. Conventional in vitro systems similarly fail to capture the multicellular architecture and biomechanical forces that shape IRI responses in the human lung. Human alveolus-on-a-chip platforms reconstitute the alveolar-capillary interface under cyclic stretch and vascular shear, preserving barrier function and enabling real-time assessment of injury responses.6-9 Beyond biological fidelity, alveolus-on-a-chip systems align with the US Food and Drug Administration’s 2025 Roadmap to Reduce Animal Testing, which prioritizes the development and regulatory acceptance of new approach methodologies, including organ-chip technologies. Enabled by the Food and Drug Administration Modernization Act 2.0, this initiative highlights the need for human-relevant, scalable, and ethically responsible platforms for mechanistic research and therapeutic development.10,11 Lung-chip approaches offer a compelling alternative to animal models by reducing reliance on technically burdensome in vivo experiments while supporting higher-throughput screening of candidate therapeutics.

However, these systems have not been applied to model CS-associated IRI relevant to LTx. Here, we describe a novel alveolus-on-a-chip model of CS and IRI that recapitulates key features of PGD, providing a scalable, human-relevant platform for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic discovery.

Human lung microvascular endothelial cells (HuLECs; American Type Culture Collection) and human primary alveolar epithelial cells (HPAECs; Cell Biologics) were expanded per supplier protocols and seeded into Emulate organ-chips (Boston, MA). HuLECs were cultured in Microvascular Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 BulletKit (Lonza) and HPAECs in Small Airway Epithelial Cell Growth Medium (SAGM) BulletKit (Lonza). Chip activation, extracellular matrix (ECM) coating, seeding, and culture were adapted from the Emulate protocol EP180.12

Polydimethylsiloxane chips were activated by filling both channels with 0.5 mg/mL Emulate Reagent (ER)-1 in ER-2 (Emulate), UV exposure for 10 min, refreshing the ER-1 solution, and repeating the UV exposure for 5 min. Channels were washed with 200 µL cold sterile PBS and aspirated before ECM coating. The epithelial channel was coated with collagen IV (200 µg/mL), fibronectin (30 µg/mL), and laminin (5 µg/mL) in PBS; the endothelial channel was coated with collagen IV and fibronectin at identical concentrations. Chips were incubated overnight at 37 °C.

For epithelial seeding, channels were washed twice with 200 µL SAGM and HPAECs were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/mL (35 µL/channel). Chips were maintained for 72 h with daily SAGM changes. For endothelial seeding, endothelial channels were washed with 200 µL endothelial cell growth medium and HuLECs were seeded at 5 × 106 cells/mL (20 µL/channel). Chips were inverted immediately after seeding to promote attachment to the shared porous membrane, incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, reverted, gently washed (200 µL/channel), and cultured for an additional 24 h. Chips were then connected to pods and perfused in Zoë at 30 µL/h/channel for 24 h at 37 °C.

Air-liquid interface (ALI) was initiated by removing epithelial media and switching endothelial perfusate to ALI medium (medium 199 supplemented with endothelial growth factor 10 ng/mL, basic fibroblast growth factor 3 ng/mL, vascular endothelial growth factor 0.125 ng/mL, and L-glutamax 1 mM [Thermo Scientific], plus hydrocortisone 1 µg/mL, heparin 50 mg/mL, dibutyryl-cAMP 80 µM, dexamethasone 20 nM, fetal bovine serum 2%, and penicillin/streptomycin 1% [Sigma]). ALI perfusion was maintained at 30 µL/h for 48 h. Chips were maintained at 5% CO2 and subjected to 5% cyclic stretch at 0.20 Hz to model breathing.

This protocol generates an alveolus-on-a-chip with type I and type II alveolar epithelial cells cultured at ALI atop a porous membrane interfacing with a perfused endothelial channel; cyclic vacuum actuation applies physiologic stretch across the membrane (Figure 1A).

Once established, alveolus chips were maintained under baseline conditions (30 µL/h perfusion and 5% cyclic stretch at 0.20 Hz) at 37 °C for 24 h. To model donor cold static storage–associated IRI (CS-IRI), chips were removed from the Emulate device and endothelial channels were gravity perfused with 200 µL Perfadex (XVIVO) while aspirating the outflow. Chips were then placed in a hyperoxygen chamber at 4 °C for 6 h, as previously described.13 Hyperoxygen exposure mimics clinical donor lung storage, in which explanted lungs are oxygen-inflated during CS; the 6 h duration reflects prior in vitro studies and typical clinical cold ischemic times.13-16 Following CS, endothelial channels were flushed with 200 µL warm, degassed perfusion media, chips were reconnected to the Emulate system, and perfusion/stretch were resumed for 24 h at 37 °C. Control chips were handled identically, but without CS exposure, and maintained under standard perfusion/stretch conditions.

In total, 10 chips/group were analyzed across 4 independent runs, using the same HPAEC lot. Three chips/group were allocated to NanoString RNA analysis and 3 chips/group to immunofluorescence. Simulated alveolar lavage was collected from 3 runs, and perfusion media were collected from all chips across all runs (Figure 1).

Alveolus chips were fixed by perfusing 100 µL 4% paraformaldehyde into both channels and incubating for 15 min. Channels were washed 3 times with 200 µL PBS and stored in PBS at 4 °C for up to 2 wk before staining. Zonula-occludens 1 (ZO-1) (epithelium) and VE (vascular endothelial)-cadherin (endothelium) immunofluorescence was performed as described.17,18. Chips were permeabilized with 100 µL 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, washed 3 times with PBS, and blocked with 100 µL 10% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Fluorophore-conjugated primary antibodies were perfused into the appropriate channels and incubated overnight at 4 °C (Table S1, SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364). After PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with 100 µL 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI; Abcam), washed, and chips were imaged.

To assess the impact of CS-IRI on cell-cell junctions, we performed standardized microscopic image analysis. For each chip and channel, 5 immunofluorescence images stained for ZO-1 and VE-cadherin were acquired at fixed longitudinal positions (Figure S1, SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364) using an EVOS M7000 microscope (Thermo Scientific). Quantification was performed per image, and the mean of 5 fields was used as the chip-level value for plotting and statistical analysis; individual field values were also visualized to confirm that apparent outliers were not driven by a single image. Nuclei counts and junctional contacts were quantified in ImageJ as previously described19,20 (nuclei-counting macro in File S1, SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364). Junctional continuity/confluence was quantified using a stereology-derived line-intersection method by overlaying images with grid lines and counting membrane intersections (VE-cadherin for endothelium; ZO-1 for epithelium), averaged across 5 fields per chip. As an orthogonal measure, junctional staining was also analyzed by calculating mean gray value and by binarizing the signal to determine the junction-positive area fraction (junction area/total area) for each chip.

Images were acquired on an ECLIPSE Ti2 inverted microscope with a Yokogawa CSU-W1 SoRa spinning-disk system using NIS-Elements (Nikon). Z-stacks spanning from the basal endothelial layer to the apical epithelial layer were collected at the chip center (0.8 µm step size). Images were 3-dimensional (3D) deconvolved in NIS-Elements using the Richardson–Lucy algorithm, and 3D reconstructions, cross-sections, and videos were generated in Imaris 11 (Oxford Instruments) (Figures S2 and S3, SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364; Videos S1 and S2, SDC,).

Perfusion media from endothelial channels were collected from the pod reservoir at 24 h. Alveolar compartment cytokines were assessed by a PBS apical rinse (mock bronchioalveolar lavage [BAL]), in which 200 µL PBS was introduced into the epithelial channel using gravity wash, collected, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Perfusion and alveolar rinse samples were analyzed undiluted using the Human Adhesion Molecule and Human Proinflammatory Chemokine LEGENDplex panels (BioLegend) per manufacturer instructions. Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using LEGENDplex software. Values below the standard curve were retained by assigning the minimum detectable value for each analyte, as described previously.21 Analyte ranges are provided in Tables S2 and S3 (SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364). Perfadex incubated in endothelial channels during CS was similarly analyzed, with all analytes below detection.

RNA was isolated separately from epithelial and endothelial channels. RLT lysis buffer (150 µL; Qiagen) was introduced into each channel using a gravity wash; endothelial lysis was immediate, whereas epithelial channels required a 10 to 15 min incubation. Complete cell dissociation was confirmed by brightfield microscopy, and lysates were collected within ~1 min per chip. RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and analyzed with the nCounter Human Organ Transplant Panel (NanoString). Genes with normalized counts >100 were considered expressed, and differentially expressed genes (P < 0.05) were analyzed by Gene Ontology enrichment (Panther version 19.0). Normalized data were further analyzed by principle component analysis-X and hierarchical clustering (Ward’s method) in SIMCA (version 18.0; Sartorius). Ingenuity Pathway Analysis was used to identify upstream regulators and causal networks.

GraphPad Prism 10 version 10.2.3 for Windows (GraphPad, San Diego, CA) was used for statistical analysis and to generate graphs for all data, except those generated by NanoString analysis. For analyses involving >2 groups, an ordinary 1-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons was used. For all other analyses, Welch’s unpaired 2-sample t test was used to determine significance. An alpha value of 0.05 was used to determine significance.

These data suggest that barrier function is impaired as a consequence of CS, in agreement with reports using static models of CS-IRI.22

To define junctional injury, we immunostained for ZO-1, a tight junction protein regulating airspace fluid accumulation.23 ZO-1 was present with largely preserved membranous localization across control, EoCS, and CS-IRI chips at 24 h reperfusion (n = 3/group), although focal reductions were observed in CS-IRI (Figure 2B). Nuclei counts did not differ between groups (control 806.1 ± 43.3; EoCS 757.6 ± 89.4; CS-IRI 740.7 ± 27.9; Figure 2C), indicating no epithelial cell loss. Using Weibel graticule line-intersection to quantify junctional continuity, membrane ZO-1 intersections decreased at EoCS versus control (145.3 ± 8.5 versus 122.9 ± 5.2; P < 0.05) but returned toward baseline after reperfusion in CS-IRI (139.4 ± 7.3; Figure 2D). Mean gray value analysis showed no differences in total ZO-1 signal (control 38.5 ± 3.7; EoCS 43.8 ± 6.3; CS-IRI 33.56 ± 1.6; Figure 2E), consistent with junctional redistribution rather than loss.

Endothelial integrity was assessed by VE-cadherin staining,24,25 which showed less distinct membrane patterning and reduced intensity in EoCS and CS-IRI chips (n = 3/group; Figure 3A). Endothelial nuclei significantly declined after reperfusion in CS-IRI (control 122.9 ± 39.9 versus CS-IRI 41.5 ± 14.8 nuclei/field; P < 0.05), with an intermediate, nonsignificant decrease at EoCS (84.3 ± 23.6; Figure 3B). Weibel graticule analysis confirmed reduced VE-cadherin membrane continuity in both injury conditions (control 59.9 ± 2.2 versus EoCS 37.6 ± 1.6 and CS-IRI 32.4 ± 4.8 intersections; P < 0.05; Figure 3C). Total VE-cadherin signal (mean gray value) was unchanged at EoCS but decreased significantly after reperfusion in CS-IRI (23.2 ± 1.2 versus control 46.1 ± 0.8 and EoCS 44.3 ± 4.9; P < 0.05; Figure 3D), consistent with endothelial loss.

A paradigmatic feature of LTx IRI and PGD is the release of proinflammatory chemokines that promote lung injury, immune cell infiltration, and activation.4,26 Numerous studies have shown that AL levels of proinflammatory cytokines are present in early post-LTx.27,28 Therefore, to assess epithelial inflammatory activation, we performed an apical rinse (BAL surrogate) of the alveolar channel and quantified chemokines using a proinflammatory bead array (Table S2, SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364). Of 13 analytes, 7 were detectable in apical samples, and all were increased in CS-IRI versus controls, although not all reached significance (Figure 4A; Table S2, SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364). Notably, IL-8 and GROα, key neutrophil chemotactic mediators previously associated with PGD,28 were significantly upregulated (Figure 4A).

Perfusion media were analyzed using the same panel; 8 analytes were detectable (Figure 4B; Table S2, SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364). Five showed no significant change, whereas MCP-1 and the T cell–associated chemokines RANTES, I-TAC, and IP-10 were significantly reduced in CS-IRI compared with controls (Figure 4B). Together, these findings indicate compartment-specific inflammatory signaling in CS-IRI chips, with enhanced epithelial neutrophil chemokine release and reduced vascular chemokines release associated with immune recruitment, potentially reflecting endothelial loss or impaired endothelial cytokine production after IRI.

Cell adhesion molecules regulate leukocyte transmigration and intercellular signaling and play a central role in LTx IRI.29 To assess adhesion molecule regulation in the CS-IRI model, we quantified soluble epithelial and endothelial adhesion molecules in alveolar lavage and perfusion media using a cytometric bead array (Table S3, SDC,
https://links.lww.com/TP/D364). In alveolar lavage, soluble intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-1, activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule (ALCAM), and epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) were significantly increased after CS-IRI (p < 0.05; Figure 5A), whereas vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)-1 and platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule (PECAM)-1 were unchanged; no other analytes were detected at sufficient levels. In perfusion media, CS-IRI was associated with increased ALCAM and PECAM-1 and decreased EpCAM (P < 0.05; Figure 5B), with no significant changes in ICAM-1 or VCAM-1. Collectively, these findings demonstrate selective, compartment-specific regulation of adhesion molecules after CS-IRI.

We used a human alveolus-on-a-chip platform to develop a CS-IRI model relevant to PGD after LTx.3 By reconstructing the multicellular, biomechanical alveolar-capillary interface, this system enables compartment-specific evaluation of epithelial and endothelial injury responses30-32 and it reproduces canonical IRI features, including transient edema, barrier dysfunction, inflammatory chemokine induction, adhesion molecule upregulation, and transcriptional reprogramming,3,33 consistent with clinical and experimental observations,22,34-36 underscoring the translational significance and utility of this platform.

Prior studies of static culture show that CS-IRI disrupts epithelial tight junctions and endothelial adherens junctions, leading to barrier failure and pulmonary edema.37-39 In contrast, the alveolus-on-a-chip incorporates perfusion and cyclic stretch, which preserve cell differentiation and endothelial quiescence through shear-dependent regulation of barrier and inflammatory programs.40,41 Consistent with this physiology, 70% of chips exhibited alveolar edema at EoCS, which fully resolved after 24 h of reperfusion. Epithelial ZO-1 total expression remained stable, but membrane localization was transiently disrupted at EoCS, as reflected by reduced Weibel graticule intersections, with recovery after reperfusion, providing a mechanistic explanation for reversible edema. In contrast, endothelial VE-cadherin showed sustained membrane disruption during CS and reperfusion, with overall expression declining only after reperfusion. This decrease likely reflects endothelial cell loss rather than reversible junctional remodeling, supported by reduced endothelial nuclei counts and confocal evidence of cell loss.

The divergent epithelial and endothelial injury responses observed here align with growing evidence that IRI triggers integrated inflammatory cell-death programs, not just isolated apoptosis. Early reperfusion injury in human LTx has been linked to coordinated PANoptosis signaling, involving shared regulators such as Receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1, caspase-8, and inflammasome components (eg, NOD‐, LRR‐ and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3) that converge across apoptotic, pyroptotic, and necroptotic pathways.42 In our model, endothelial injury appeared more severe, suggesting heightened susceptibility to PANoptosis-related signaling, whereas epithelial cells showed a more injury-tolerant phenotype. Supporting this interpretation, transcriptomic profiling demonstrated dysregulation of upstream PANoptosis-associated pathways, including interferon/signal transducer and activator of transcription signaling (signal transducer and activator of transcription 1, interferon regulatory factor 1, interferon alpha and beta receptor subunit 1, interferon gamma receptor 2), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-nuclear factor kappa B death signaling (TNF, TNF receptor superfamily member 1A, RELB, nuclear factor kappa B inhibitor alpha), cellular stress regulators (hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha, tumor protein p53, superoxide dismutase 2), and induction of guanylate-binding proteins (guanylate binding protein 1/2/4). Although these data do not prove PANoptosis execution, they indicate activation of convergent upstream regulators and motivate future pathway-specific perturbation and functional death assays. Collectively, the alveolus-on-a-chip CS-IRI platform offers a scalable, human-relevant system to dissect regulated cell-death programs and test strategies to preserve barrier integrity during early graft injury.

Proinflammatory chemokine release is a hallmark of IRI and PGD,3 with elevated IL-8 and GROα reported in bronchoalveolar lavage from LTx recipients with severe PGD.43,44 Our model recapitulated this response, demonstrating significant epithelial IL-8 and GROα induction after CS-IRI, consistent with neutrophil-directed inflammatory signaling. In contrast, perfusion media showed reduced levels of monocyte- and T cell–associated chemokines (MCP-1, RANTES, I-TAC, IP-10), potentially reflecting flow-dependent endothelial suppression or endothelial cell loss.45 Together, these findings highlight compartment-specific cytokine regulation and underscore the ability of the alveolus-on-a-chip platform to resolve epithelial-vascular inflammatory dynamics that are not captured in static models.

The expression of adhesion molecules is another key mediator of early allograft injury. Increased ICAM-1 levels have been shown to correlate with PGD severity in both human and animal LTx studies.46 Here, we observed significant elevations in soluble ICAM-1, ALCAM, and EpCAM in the epithelial compartment of our model, suggesting activation of both epithelial and endothelial barriers. Elevated ALCAM and PECAM-1 levels in the vascular compartment further confirmed the endothelial perturbation. Static models often lack the ability to distinguish these compartmentalized responses or to induce coordinated upregulation of adhesion molecules owing to their lack of structural polarity and perfusion. These results reinforce the potential benefit of the alveolar lung-chip model in capturing coordinated epithelial-vascular responses central to PGD pathogenesis.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed gene expression changes in both alveolar epithelial and endothelial cells as a consequence of CS-IRI modeling. We opted to use the human organ transplant NanoString panel as a proof-of-concept study to demonstrate that transcriptomic data can be obtained from the alveolar lung-chip model system. We acknowledge the limitations of this approach, as well as the human organ transplant panel, which was constructed to mark transplant-specific changes in immune infiltrate and graft injury, and many of the targets under investigation in this panel were not present in our model system. Nevertheless, our data show the feasibility of RNA analysis in this model system and that CS-IRI induces transcriptional changes distinct from those in control cultures, showing that the model induces changes consistent with reports of IRI in RNA analysis of human tissue samples and in static culture models.22,36 These results validate the relevance of our model and support its use in mechanistic investigations and early phase therapeutic screening in future studies.

The alveolus-on-a-chip offers distinct advantages over static cultures and small-animal models for studying lung IRI. Static systems lack the multicellular architecture and biomechanical cues that shape in vivo injury responses and often rely on large airway epithelial cells rather than alveolar type I and II cells, the primary targets of IRI.13,16,36,47,48 In addition, the absence of physiologic stretch limits alveolar type I differentiation in static culture. In contrast, the alveolus-on-a-chip supports coculture and functional preservation of alveolar type I and II epithelium under cyclic stretch and perfusion, enabling physiologically relevant modeling of epithelial-endothelial interactions, vascular shear-dependent signaling, and barrier regulation. Compared with murine transplant models, which are surgically complex and low throughput, this platform provides a scalable, reproducible system for rapid mechanistic interrogation and therapeutic screening using human-derived cells. This approach enhances translational relevance and aligns with FDA initiatives promoting new approach methodologies to reduce reliance on animal testing.10,11

To further enhance transplant relevance, future iterations of the alveolus-on-a-chip CS-IRI model could incorporate circulating immune components and alloimmune stimuli central to graft injury. Perfusion of peripheral blood mononuclear cells through the vascular channel would enable modeling of leukocyte adhesion, transmigration, and endothelial–immune crosstalk during reperfusion, an approach that has been successfully implemented in other immune-competent organ-on-chip platforms. In parallel, controlled introduction of donor-specific antibodies or lung-restricted autoantibodies would allow interrogation of antibody-mediated endothelial injury, complement activation, and Fc receptor–dependent signaling. Additional refinements, such as use of patient-derived cells or HLA-mismatched immune populations, could further improve donor-recipient specificity. Together, these advances would extend the alveolus-on-a-chip into a scalable, human-relevant platform for dissecting the integrated effects of ischemia, alloimmunity, and antibody-mediated injury and for evaluating targeted therapeutic strategies before in vivo validation.

Despite these advantages, this model has important limitations. First, it does not currently incorporate circulating immune cells, which are central to IRI and PGD pathogenesis through endothelial activation, cytokine amplification, and leukocyte recruitment. Although immune migration has been modeled in alveolus-on-a-chip systems for respiratory infection,7 the future incorporation of immune cells would enable studies of leukocyte trafficking, antigen presentation, and innate–adaptive crosstalk. Emerging immune-competent lung-on-a-chip platforms may address this limitation, although they are not yet commercially available.49

Second, although human-derived cells are used, the model relies on commercially available or cryopreserved epithelial cells and an immortalized endothelial line, which may not capture donor-specific heterogeneity. Incorporating donor-derived cells paired with recipient immune populations, or isogenic induced pluripotent stem cell–derived chips,50 could improve modeling of alloimmune responses and HLA mismatch. Third, the model captures acute injury during 24 h but does not address chronic processes such as fibrosis or long-term remodeling, which may require extended culture or inclusion of fibroblasts. Finally, the system is costly and does not capture systemic or neurohumoral influences present in vivo. Accordingly, this platform should be viewed as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, animal transplantation models.

To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of a human alveolus-on-a-chip model applied to study CS-IRI in LTx. Our findings show that this model reliably recapitulates key elements of IRI pathophysiology observed in the clinical scenario, including edema formation, barrier dysfunction, cytokine release, soluble adhesion molecule expression, and transcriptional reprogramming. Future iterations of this platform could incorporate immune cells, donor-recipient mismatch modeling, and the use of patient-derived cells to explore personalized responses to injury.

In conclusion, the alveolus-on-a-chip model provides a powerful new tool for investigating the mechanisms underlying LTx-related IRI and PGD. It bridges the gap between simplistic static cultures and low-throughput in vivo models and holds promise for accelerating therapeutic discovery and precision medicine approaches in LTx.
