# Rare Biallelic CTU2 Variants in an Individual With CAKUT: Clinical Characterization and Minigene Splicing Analysis.
**Authors:** Liu Q, Cheng X, Zheng B, Wang C, Zhou W, Zhang A
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.70277](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.70277)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) are clinically heterogeneous and remain genetically unexplained in many patients. Biallelic variants in CTU2 have been reported in DREAM-PL syndrome, a severe multisystem disorder characterized by dysmorphic facies, renal agenesis, ambiguous genitalia, microcephaly, polydactyly, and lissencephaly. However, to date, there have been no reports on the involvement of CTU2 in CAKUT.

METHODS: Exome sequencing (ES) was performed in 200 patients with CAKUT. Candidate CTU2 variants were validated by Sanger sequencing, and segregation analysis was conducted in available family members. Variant rarity was assessed using public population databases and an in-house cohort of 200 ethnically matched kidney disease-free controls. Potential functional effects were evaluated using in silico prediction tools and minigene splicing assays.

RESULTS: The affected individual, who had a diagnosis of CAKUT, presented with bilateral hydronephrosis, chronic kidney disease, and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. Genetic analysis revealed compound heterozygous CTU2 variants, NM_001012759.3: c.913C>T, p.(Arg305Trp), and c.1492C>G, p.(Gln498Glu), which were inherited from his father and mother, respectively. Both variants were rare and had not been previously reported in CTU2-related disease. In silico splicing analyses suggested that both variants may alter putative exonic splicing enhancers (ESE) motifs. Minigene splicing assays showed that c.913C>T altered pre-mRNA splicing by increasing exon 9 skipping, whereas c.1492C>G had no statistically significant effect on exon inclusion.

CONCLUSION: This study identifies two rare CTU2 variants in an individual with renal-predominant CAKUT and provides in vitro evidence that c.913C>T partially alters splicing. While these findings do not establish CTU2 as a definitive cause of isolated CAKUT, they support further case collection and kidney-relevant functional studies of CTU2 in renal developmental phenotypes.

Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) constitute the most common spectrum of birth defects, occurring in approximately 1 in 500 live births (Wühl et al. 2013). This clinically heterogeneous spectrum encompasses a wide array of structural defects, ranging from renal parenchymal malformations (e.g., renal agenesis, hypodysplasia, hydronephrosis, and ectopic kidneys) to anomalies of the urinary collecting system (e.g., ureteropelvic junction obstruction [UPJO], vesicoureteral reflux [VUR], and posterior urethral valves [PUV]) (Ichikawa et al. 2002). As a leading cause of renal failure in children, CAKUT accounts for approximately 40%–50% of pediatric end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) cases worldwide (Jain and Chen 2019). Phenotypically, CAKUT may present as an isolated feature or as a component of a broader syndromic disorder. Genetic investigations have increasingly elucidated the monogenic basis of CAKUT. To date, pathogenic variants in over 170 genes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of human CAKUT. Of these, approximately 23 genes are linked to isolated forms, with the majority (151 genes) associated with syndromic presentations (Seltzsam et al. 2022). Despite these advances, pathogenic variants in known CAKUT‐causing genes explain only 6%–20% of cases, suggesting that many causative genes remain to be discovered (Rao et al. 2019; Rasmussen et al. 2018; Van Der Ven et al. 2018).

The CTU2 gene (MIM*617057), located on chromosome 16q24.3, encodes a cytoplasmic thiouridylase involved in the post‐transcriptional modification of transfer RNAs (tRNAs). Specifically, the CTU1‐CTU2 complex catalyzes the 2‐thiolation of the wobble uridine (U34) of specific tRNAs (Björk et al. 2007), a modification that enhances codon‐anticodon recognition accuracy and translational efficiency (Dewez et al. 2008). Biallelic pathogenic variants in CTU2 have been reported in DREAM‐PL syndrome, a severe multisystem disorder characterized by dysmorphic facies, renal agenesis, ambiguous genitalia, microcephaly, polydactyly, and lissencephaly. In the original study of Saudi Arabian kindred, affected individuals homozygous for a frameshift variant (NM_001012762.1: p.(Thr247Alafs21)) exhibited a consistent, severe phenotype, prominently including renal agenesis (Shaheen, Patel, et al. 2016). Notably, bilateral or unilateral renal agenesis is a cardinal feature of this syndrome, observed in most reported cases with protein‐truncating variants (PTVs).

Although CTU2‐related disease has been primarily described as a severe syndromic disorder with prominent renal agenesis, no studies have yet reported the involvement of CTU2 in CAKUT. In this study, we report an individual with CAKUT carrying compound heterozygous CTU2 variants and evaluate their potential effects on pre‐mRNA splicing using minigene assays. Our findings expand clinical observations associated with CTU2 variation and support further investigation of CTU2 in renal developmental disorders.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children's Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (202306015–1). Written informed consent for publication of the patient's case details and any accompanying images was obtained from his parents/legal guardians.

We enrolled 200 patients with renal structural anomalies consistent with CAKUT, as diagnosed by pediatric specialists based on clinical and radiological findings. For comparison, a control cohort comprising 200 unrelated individuals of matched ancestry with no personal or family history of kidney disease was enrolled. Available clinical evaluation, renal ultrasonography, and urinalysis were used to exclude CAKUT or other kidney abnormalities in these controls. This in‐house control cohort enabled consistent sequencing and variant filtering pipelines across cases and controls, minimizing technical variability while providing a well‐characterized reference group that reflects the genetic background of our local population, thereby enhancing the reliability of rare variant interpretation.

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using a DNA isolation kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. ES was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. All identified variants were annotated using public databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project, dbSNP, the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), and ExAC. Candidate variants were validated by Sanger sequencing, and segregation analysis was performed in available family members.

Variant interpretation was initially performed according to the 2015 American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) standards and guidelines. To align with current practices, the identified CTU2 variants were subsequently re‐evaluated using the ACMG/AMP framework with reference to the ACGS Best Practice Guidelines for Variant Classification in Rare Disease (2024), interpreted in the context of CTU2‐associated disease and autosomal recessive inheritance. For variants with predicted or experimentally observed splice effects, the interpretation of splicing data was considered in accordance with current recommendations for incorporating RNA/splicing evidence into variant classification (Durkie et al. 2024). To evaluate the rarity of biallelic CTU2 variation in the local population, we also examined coding exons and canonical splice‐site regions of CTU2 in 200 unrelated kidney disease‐free controls.

Cross‐species conservation of the affected amino acid residues was assessed by multiple sequence alignment of CTU2 orthologous or homologous protein sequences from selected vertebrate species and more distant model organisms. The potential effects of missense changes were evaluated using SIFT, PolyPhen‐2, MutationTaster, and REVEL. Potential splice effects were assessed using three complementary in silico tools: SpliceAI for prediction of splice donor/acceptor gain or loss; Human Splicing Finder (HSF) for potential effects on splice sites and exonic splicing regulatory motifs; and ESE Finder 3.0 for prediction of changes in putative exonic splicing enhancer (ESE) motifs recognized by SR proteins, using the default thresholds (SRSF1, 1.956; SRSF2, 2.383; SRSF5, 2.670; SRSF6, 2.676). Computational predictions were interpreted as supportive evidence only and were not considered sufficient to establish pathogenicity.

Both CTU2 variants identified in the reported individual were analyzed using an in vitro minigene splice assay: c.913C>T, p.(Arg305Trp), in exon 9 and c.1492C>G, p.(Gln498Glu), in exon 15. We generated fragments containing exon 9/15, where the variants were located, together with 150–200 bp of flanking intronic regions and XhoI/BamHI restriction sites. These fragments were amplified from the patient's genomic DNA by PCR using 2× Phanta Flash Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The primer sequences were as follows: forward 5′‐accagaattctggagctcgagTTACTGGAGAGTAGCCTCTC‐3′ and reverse 3′‐atcaccagatatctgggatccTCAGAGGATGCAGCACTGCT‐5′ for c.913C>T; forward 5′‐accagaattctggagctcgagCATGAAGGACTTGGTGAGTAC‐3′ and reverse 3′‐atcaccagatatctgggatccCATGATCAAGTGGACTCGTG‐5′ for c.1492C>G. The forward primer included an XhoI site and the reverse primer contained a BamHI site. The pSPL3 vector was digested with XhoI and BamHI and subsequently ligated with the purified PCR products to construct the wild‐type minigenes (EX9‐WT and EX15‐WT). The mutant minigenes (c.913C>T and c.1492C>G) were generated by site‐directed mutagenesis using the Fast Mutagenesis Kit (Vazyme, China) to introduce each variant into the corresponding wild‐type construct. All constructs were confirmed by direct sequencing.

HEK293T cells were cultured in 12‐well plates, with 0.5 mL of medium (DMEM, 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) in each well at 37°C in 5% CO2. When confluency reached approximately 90%, cells were transiently transfected with 1 μg of purified plasmids (pSPL3, EX9‐WT, EX15‐WT, c.913C>T, or c.1492C>G) using 3 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Life Technologies). 48 h after transfection, total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol and chloroform, followed by on‐column DNase I digestion to eliminate genomic DNA contamination.

First‐strand cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara). The resulting cDNA served as a template for amplification with specific primers located in the two cassette exons of the pSPL3 vector: forward SD 5′‐TCTGAGTCACCTGGACAACC‐3′ and reverse SA 5′‐ATCTCAGTGGTATTTGTGAGC‐3′. PCR products were separated by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the bands of interest were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The presence of an aberrant transcript, together with a relative reduction in the normal transcript in the mutant construct compared with the wild‐type construct, was interpreted as evidence of altered splicing in this in vitro assay.

The proband was a 15‐year‐old male born to healthy, non‐consanguineous Chinese parents. He initially presented with proteinuria at the age of 8, and urinary tract imaging revealed bilateral hydronephrosis, leading to a clinical diagnosis of CAKUT. At the time of evaluation, he also showed evidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 2. General physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory evaluation showed heavy proteinuria (24‐h urine protein: 1.2 g; urinary microalbuminuria: 533.00 mg/L) and impaired renal function (serum creatinine: 103 μmol/L; endogenous creatinine clearance rate [Ccr]: 67.5 mL/min). Renal ultrasonography demonstrated bilateral hydronephrosis with marked renal pelvic separation (Figure 1A). Renal biopsy revealed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS). He was treated with several traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) preparations aimed at improving renal function and reducing proteinuria (e.g., Huangkui and Bailing capsules), but no clinical improvement was observed. During 5 years of follow‐up, he required recurrent hospitalizations for urinary tract infections (UTIs) and CKD‐related complications. The patient was also found to have congenital pulmonary stenosis, which was corrected by valvuloplasty at 2 years of age. Apart from congenital pulmonary stenosis, no other extra‐renal congenital anomalies were documented. ES identified two heterozygous CTU2 variants (NM_001012759.3: c.913C>T, p.(Arg305Trp) and c.1492C>G, p.(Gln498Glu)). Sanger sequencing confirmed that c.913C>T was inherited from the father and c.1492C>G from the mother, consistent with a compound heterozygous genotype (Figure 1B). No additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in known CAKUT‐associated genes were detected.

To evaluate the rarity of biallelic CTU2 variants in the local population, we interrogated exome sequencing data from 200 unrelated controls without a history of kidney disease. No homozygous or compound heterozygous coding or canonical splice‐site CTU2 variants were identified in this control cohort. Consistent with this observation, both CTU2 variants identified in the proband were rare in public population databases, and neither has been previously reported in association with CTU2‐related disease.

Evolutionary conservation analysis showed that Arg305 is highly conserved across the selected vertebrate orthologs analyzed, whereas Gln498 showed weaker conservation (Figure 1C). Computational predictions are summarized in Table 1. Among the two variants, p.(Arg305Trp) showed stronger computational support for a potential deleterious effect, whereas p.(Gln498Glu) showed predominantly benign or tolerated in silico predictions.

To evaluate potential splice effects, we combined splice donor/acceptor prediction with analyses of exonic splicing regulatory motifs. SpliceAI predicted minimal splice donor/acceptor gain or loss for c.913C>T (Δ scores < 0.2), whereas c.1492C>G showed a predicted acceptor‐loss effect (Δ = 0.45) (Table 1). In complementary motif‐based analyses, ESE Finder predicted that both variants might alter putative SR protein‐binding exonic splicing enhancer motifs (Figure 2A). Specifically, c.913C>T was predicted to affect SRSF1/SRSF6‐related motifs in exon 9, whereas c.1492C>G was predicted to alter SRSF2/SRSF5/SRSF6‐related motifs in exon 15.

Based on these predictions, both c.913C>T and c.1492C>G variants were selected for in vitro minigene splicing assays (Figure 2B). In the HEK293T‐cell minigene system, c.913C>T was associated with an altered splicing pattern compared with the wild‐type (WT) construct. The wild‐type construct produced a predominant full‐length band (395 bp), corresponding to the inclusion of CTU2 exon 9 between the vector exons SD and SA. In contrast, the c.913C>T construct generated both the full‐length transcript and a smaller 263 bp product (Figure 2C). Sanger sequencing confirmed that the smaller product resulted from complete skipping of exon 9. Semi‐quantitative analysis showed a reduction in exon 9 inclusion in the c.913C>T construct compared with the wild‐type construct (79% vs. 99%, **p < 0.01, Figure 2E), supporting a splice‐altering effect in vitro. For c.1492C>G, an exon 15‐skipped product was observed in the minigene assay; however, semi‐quantitative analysis did not show a statistically significant difference in exon 15 inclusion between the mutant and wild‐type constructs (Figure 2D,F).

Taken together, these findings support that c.913C>T affects splicing in the minigene system and is associated with increased exon 9 skipping. We therefore undertook a refined re‐evaluation using the ACMG/AMP framework with reference to the current ACGS recommendations.

Variant classification was performed using the ACMG/AMP framework with reference to the 2024 ACGS recommendations. For variants with predicted or experimentally supported splice effects, interpretation of the minigene results followed current guidance for incorporating RNA/Splicing evidence into variant classification. The applied evidence criteria for each variant are summarized in Table 1.

For c.913C>T, the variant is rare in population databases, with no homozygotes reported in gnomAD, supporting PM2_Moderate. The minigene assay showed increased exon 9 skipping and reduced full‐length transcript levels, providing in vitro evidence for a splice‐altering effect. Although exon 9 skipping is predicted to be in‐frame, NMD prediction suggested that the aberrant transcript may fall within a nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay‐sensitive region (Figure 2G). Therefore, this splicing alteration may reduce the abundance of the normal CTU2 transcript and potentially affect CTU2 protein levels through transcript instability or NMD‐mediated degradation. However, in the absence of patient‐derived RNA analysis or direct protein‐level evidence, this result should be interpreted as supportive functional evidence of altered splicing rather than definitive proof of a null allele. Accordingly, c.913C>T remains classified as a variant of uncertain significance, with in vitro evidence supporting a splice‐altering effect and a predicted potential impact on transcript and protein abundance.

For c.1492C>G, the variant is also rare in population databases, with no homozygotes reported in gnomAD, supporting PM2_Moderate. Computational evidence, including a low REVEL score and predominantly benign or tolerated missense predictions, supported BP4_Supporting. No statistically significant splicing alteration was observed in the minigene assay; therefore, no RNA‐based pathogenic evidence was applied. Based on PM2_Moderate and BP4_Supporting, c.1492C>G remains classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

Taken together, both CTU2 variants remain classified as variants of uncertain significance under the ACMG/AMP framework with reference to the 2024 ACGS recommendations. The observed splice‐altering effect of c.913C>T provides preliminary functional evidence, but its clinical significance remains uncertain. Further mechanistic and functional validation is required to determine the biological and clinical relevance of these variants.

In this study, we report an individual with CAKUT carrying compound heterozygous CTU2 variants inherited in trans and provide in vitro evidence that one variant, c.913C>T, partially alters pre‐mRNA splicing by increasing exon 9 skipping. The patient presented with bilateral hydronephrosis, chronic kidney disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and congenital pulmonary stenosis, but did not show the severe syndromic manifestations previously associated with CTU2‐related disease. This milder, renal‐predominant presentation differs from the severe multisystem phenotypes reported in most individuals with CTU2‐related disease.

Previous studies have shown that most reported individuals with CTU2‐related disease carried biallelic protein‐truncating or loss‐of‐function variants, although missense‐containing or splice‐altering genotypes have also been described (Table 2). These individuals generally presented with severe multisystem phenotypes, frequently including renal agenesis or other major renal anomalies, together with extrarenal developmental abnormalities (Shaaban et al. 2024; Shaheen, Al‐Salam, et al. 2016; Shaheen et al. 2019). Therefore, the contrast between previously reported severe syndromic cases and the renal‐predominant phenotype observed in the present individual raises the question of whether the degree of residual CTU2 function may influence phenotypic severity.

In the study by Shaheen et al. (2019), the noncanonical splice variant c.282 + 5G>A, located downstream of exon 4 and predicted to affect exon 4 donor‐site splicing, was associated with abnormal CTU2 splicing. Notably, patient‐derived cells carrying this noncanonical splice variant showed a relatively milder reduction in tRNA thiolation compared with cells carrying more disruptive CTU2 variants. This suggested that residual normal splicing may allow production of a small amount of functional Ctu2 protein. This observation supports the possibility that some splice‐altering CTU2 alleles may retain partial function, resulting in less severe impairment of CTU2‐dependent tRNA thiolation.

Molecularly, the two CTU2 variants in the present individual showed different levels of supporting evidence. The c.913C>T variant, although annotated as a missense change, p.(Arg305Trp), demonstrated a splice‐altering effect in the minigene assay, with reduced full‐length transcript levels and increased exon 9 skipping. Because exon 9 skipping is predicted to preserve the reading frame, this splicing consequence differs from the classical protein‐truncating loss‐of‐function alleles previously reported in DREAM‐PL syndrome. NMD prediction suggested that the aberrant transcript may fall within a nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay‐sensitive region, raising the possibility that this splicing alteration could reduce transcript stability and potentially affect CTU2 protein abundance. However, this prediction does not provide direct evidence of NMD in patient‐derived cells. Moreover, the skipped region has not been established as an indispensable functional domain or active site of CTU2. Thus, c.913C>T is best regarded as a splice‐altering variant with uncertain protein‐level consequences.

The second variant, c.1492C>G, was inherited in trans with c.913C>T and is rare in population databases. However, the minigene assay did not show a statistically significant effect on exon 15 inclusion, and current computational evidence is insufficient to support pathogenicity. Therefore, although the allelic configuration is compatible with a potential partial‐function or hypomorphic CTU2 genotype, both variants should be interpreted cautiously at present, and further validation is required.

The milder, renal‐predominant phenotype observed in this individual, together with previous observations of residual CTU2 function in a noncanonical splice‐altering allele, raises the possibility that partial or hypomorphic CTU2 variation could contribute to less severe or more organ‐restricted phenotypes in some contexts. Such allelic combinations might be overlooked in clinical diagnosis when the presentation lacks the severe multisystem manifestations previously associated with CTU2‐related disease. However, this remains a hypothesis generated from limited evidence, and the present case should not be considered sufficient to establish an attenuated CTU2‐related phenotype or to expand the CTU2 disease spectrum.

Public expression data and the known biological function of CTU2 provide biological context, but not direct evidence of renal pathogenicity. Human Protein Atlas (Uhlén et al. 2015) data indicate that CTU2 is broadly expressed across human tissues with low tissue specificity, and both CTU2 mRNA and protein are detectable in kidney tissue. Functionally, CTU2 participates in cytosolic tRNA 2‐thiolation at the wobble uridine position, a modification involved in translational efficiency, codon decoding fidelity, and genome integrity (Philipp et al. 2014). Studies in multiple non‐renal experimental systems have also implicated CTU2 or related tRNA thiolation in cell‐growth regulation, with CTU2 depletion impairing proliferation (Delaunay et al. 2016; Rapino et al. 2018; Xue et al. 2024). Given that nephrogenesis depends on coordinated cell proliferation, differentiation, and morphogenesis, altered CTU2 splicing or impaired tRNA modification represents a biologically plausible mechanism for future investigation. Nevertheless, direct evidence from kidney‐relevant cells or developmental models is still lacking.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is based on a single affected individual, limiting genotype–phenotype inference. Second, minigene assays are simplified artificial models and may not fully recapitulate splicing regulation in developing kidney tissue, particularly because the constructs include only limited flanking intronic sequences and do not capture the full genomic context. Third, although c.913C>T altered splicing in vitro, endogenous RNA from patient‐derived kidney‐relevant cells was unavailable, and the protein‐level consequences of exon 9 skipping were not assessed. Finally, we did not evaluate CTU2 protein abundance, CTU1‐CTU2 complex formation, tRNA thiolation status, or downstream renal developmental pathways, and independent replication in additional unrelated individuals with compatible phenotypes and biallelic CTU2 variants is still lacking.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide preliminary case‐based evidence that a rare exonic CTU2 variant can alter splicing in vitro. This study highlights the value of combining in silico prediction, minigene analysis, and updated variant‐interpretation frameworks when evaluating rare exonic variants with potential splicing effects. Additional cases, endogenous RNA studies, and kidney‐relevant functional models will be required to clarify whether partial CTU2 dysfunction contributes to renal developmental anomalies.

In conclusion, we report an individual with renal‐predominant CAKUT carrying two rare CTU2 variants and provide in vitro evidence that c.913C>T can alter pre‐mRNA splicing by increasing in‐frame exon 9 skipping in vitro. Although the current evidence is insufficient to establish a definitive causal role for CTU2 in this patient's renal phenotype, these findings add to the catalog of rare CTU2 variants, highlight the importance of functional evaluation for exonic variants with potential splicing effects, and support further investigation of partial CTU2 dysfunction in renal developmental phenotypes.
