Abstract
Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) represents nearly half of all heart failure cases globally. The 
increased prevalence of cardiometabolic disease, driven by unhealthy lifestyles, has led to a growing population of 
people developing the so called “cardiometabolic HFpEF (cmHFpEF)” phenotype. This condition represents an end 
stage cardiometabolic phenotype which results from the clustering of metabolic stress (obesity), hemodynamic 
stress (hypertension), immune activation, and systemic inflammation. This form of HFpEF is preceded by a 
“metabolic cardiomyopathy” phenotype, characterized by myocardial metabolic remodeling, rewiring of lipid 
metabolism, and inflammation eventually fostering left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction and atrial 
dilatation. Recent work over the last years has unveiled the molecular cues underpinning cmHFpEF pathogenesis 
thus contributing to the identification of novel therapeutic approaches to treat this complex syndrome. The 
present review provides an overview of recent advances in cmHFpEF biology and pathophysiology with particular 
emphasis on the following aspects: (i) metabolic alterations associated with cmHFpEF; (ii) changes of the immune 
landscape; (iii) microvascular dysfunction; (iv) inflammation; (v) chromatin remodeling. Additionally, we will discuss 
potential mechanisms-based therapeutic strategies to tackle this growing health concern.

Introduction
HFpEF is a clinical syndrome characterized by dyspnea 
at low-normal activity levels, impaired left ventricular 
relaxation, elevated left ventricular filling pressures, fluid 
and sodium retention, as well as abnormalities in sys -
tolic function, including impaired longitudinal strain and 
delayed myocardial untwisting [ 1–3]. Described as the 
“single largest unmet need in cardiovascular medicine” , 
HFpEF has come to represent more than half of all new 
HF diagnoses and associates with a poor 5-year progno -
sis (40% mortality rate) despite optimal medical treat -
ment [ 4]. While heart failure prevalence varies globally, 
HFpEF has become increasingly prominent, particularly 
in contrast to the stable or declining rates of heart failure 
with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) [5, 6].
But what has driven HFpEF to the forefront of pub -
lic health concerns? The global rise in cardiometabolic 
diseases, driven by modern lifestyles and the increas -
ing prevalence of metabolic comorbidities, represents 
a major factor implicated in the growing incidence of 
HFpEF (Fig. 1) [4]. Although HFpEF may include a variety

Diabetes and obesity have reached epidemic proportions, 
with 46% increase in the number of people affected by 
diabetes estimated for 2045. Approximately 650 million 
people, which is about 13% of the global population, are 
living with obesity. By the year 2045, the number of peo -
ple with diabetes is expected to increase by 46%, which 
has raised serious concerns among scientists [ 8, 9]. Mul-
tiple studies have demonstrated an association between 
obesity and heart failure (HF), with a “linear” relation -
ship observed between body mass index (BMI) and HF 
incidence in people with a BMI in the overweight range 
or higher [10, 11]. However, the linearity of this relation -
ship may oversimplify the complex interplay of fat distri -
bution, metabolic health, and cardiorenal comorbidities. 
Emerging evidence indicates that obese patients develop 
a specific heart failure phenotype, known as cardiometa -
bolic HFpEF, wherein body weight reduction by dietary 
restriction or GLP1-RA (i.e. semaglutide) have shown 
promise. The STEP HFpEF trial showed that patients 
experienced weight loss, symptom reduction, and 
improved exercise function due to once-weekly subcuta -
neous semaglutide administration compared to placebo 
[12]. The SUMMIT trial demonstrated a lower risk of a 
composite of death from cardiovascular causes or wors -
ening heart failure, along with improved health status 
[13]. While the findings are encouraging, most trials are 
short of duration and focus on surrogate endpoints.
CmHFpEF refers to a distinct phenotype of HFpEF 
that is closely linked to obesity, insulin resistance, type 
2 diabetes, and systemic inflammation. This phenotype 
is characterized by a high burden of cardiometabolic 
comorbidities, often characterized by visceral adipos -
ity, impaired metabolic flexibility, and low-grade chronic 
inflammation, all of which contribute to myocardial 
remodeling and dysfunction [ 14–16]. Epidemiological 
data show that cmHFpEF is the most prevalent HFpEF 
phenotype and its incidence is expected to increase expo-
nentially in the decades to come [ 17, 18]. Over 80% of 
patients with HFpEF are either overweight or obese, and 
in recent HFpEF trials the average BMI of the enrolled 
patients exceeded 36  kg/m 2 [ 19]. Most importantly, 
cmHFpEF represents one of the leading causes of mortal-
ity in obese patients [12, 20, 21].
Despite an intense research interest in HFpEF, evi -
dence-based treatment options remain limited. Random -
ized trials targeting the neurohormonal and sympathetic 
nervous system using angiotensin-converting enzyme 
inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin II receptor blockers 
(ARBs) and beta blockers, which provide clear bene -
fits in HFrEF, have failed to meet their primary efficacy 
outcome in HFpEF [ 22–24]. A recent breakthrough 
has been the positive outcome trials using sodium-
glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and more 
recently GLP1- receptor agonists (GLP1-RA) in HFpEF. 
Based on the results of recent landmark trials includ -
ing EMPEROR-Preserved, DELIVER, STEP-HFpEF and 
SUMMIT, SGLT2i and GLP1-RA are now guideline-
recommended therapies for the treatment of HFpEF 
[13, 25–27]. The PARAMOUNT and PARALLAX-HF 
trials show that sacubitril/valsartan reduces the levels 
of natriuretic peptides but has limited impact on other 
outcomes in HFpEF patients [ 28]. In the TOPCAT trial 
treatment with spironolactone failed to improve CV 
outcomes in HFpEF, while increasing the risk of hyper -
kalemia and renal [ 29]. However, subsequent analy -
ses of this trial showed promises of this drug in specific 
HFpEF subgroups thus providing hints on the potential 
benefit of aldosterone antagonism in HFpEF [ 30, 31]. 
More recently, the FINEARTS-HF trial demonstrated 
the effectiveness of finerenone in lowering the compos -
ite endpoint of worsening heart failure and death ver -
sus placebo [ 32]. Despite the benefits of these drugs on 
HFpEF-related outcomes, the overall impact on mortality 
remains modest thus leaving HFpEF patients exposed to 
a substantial residual risk.
With HFpEF projected to become the predominant 
form of HF over the next decades [ 33], an SGLT2i or 
GLP1-RA evidence-based treatment remains insufficient 
[34, 35]. A better understanding of the molecular mecha -
nisms underpinning the cmHFpEF phenotype could set 
the stage for novel therapeutic approaches in this setting.
In the present review we provide an overview of the 
most important molecular mechanisms implicated in 
the pathogenesis of HFpEF. We also discuss new inter -
vention studies modulating relevant pathways underpin -
ning lipotoxic damage and inflammation in the HFpEF 
myocardium.
Metabolic alterations associated with HFpEF
Obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) not only 
impair systolic function and left ventricular suction, 
but they also trigger metabolic disturbances that con -
tribute to increased inflammation, tissue fibrosis, and 
heightened stiffness of cardiac muscle cells, key features 
seen in HF [ 36, 37]. Notably, cardiometabolic altera -
tions are more strongly associated with the develop -
ment of HFpEF than with HFrEF [ 38]. A low-grade 
systemic inflammatory state combined with metabolic 
derangement and described as “meta-inflammation” , is 
commonly observed in conditions of chronic overnutri -
tion and obesity and establishes bidirectional crosstalk 
between inflammatory pathways and altered metabolic 
processes [ 39]. In this regard, a major role is played by 
adipose tissue which has been increasingly recognized as 
a dynamic organ involved in the regulation of metabolic,

endocrine, and immune responses, ultimately impair -
ing the heart by fostering lipotoxic injury, hypertrophy 
and cardiac remodeling (Fig. 2) [ 40, 41]. Several organs 
contribute to metabolic homeostasis and inflammation 
including the liver, skeletal muscle, and the kidney [ 42]. 
These tissues coordinate nutrient sensing, glucose and 
lipid metabolism, as well as immune responses, all of 
which are frequently impaired in cardiometabolic disease 
[43]. Among them, adipose tissue has gained prominence 
as a key driver of meta-inflammation, an aspect particu -
larly relevant in the context of HFpEF pathophysiology 
[44]. In cardiometabolic disease, and more specifically 
in end stage cardiometabolic phenotypes such as HFpEF, 
defects in metabolic flexibility are associated with adipo -
cyte expansion, defective insulin signaling and metabolic 
inflammation [45]. Given the central role of adipose tis -
sue in the regulation of meta-inflammation, it is critical 
to understand the function of different adipose tissue 
compartments (including epicardial and pericardial fat) 
and their link with cardiac remodeling and HFpEF devel -
opment. During fasting, adipose tissue acts as an energy 
reservoir for fatty acids [ 46]. However, in conditions of 
metabolic stress and obesity the adipose tissue releases 
pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines with subse -
quent recruitment of immune cells and tissue inflamma -
tion [47]. In cardiometabolic patients, immune cells like 
monocytes, macrophages, B cells, and T cells, are the sec-
ond largest population in the heart after adipocytes, sug -
gesting a significant activation of the immune response 
[48].
Fat depots and metabolic impairment
The expansion of fat depots surrounding the heart, 
namely visceral (VAT) and epicardial adipose tissue 
(EAT) is commonly observed in individuals with cardio -
metabolic disturbances, such as obesity and type 2 diabe -
tes [49]. Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) plays a protective 
role in the heart under physiological conditions. In this 
state, EAT acts as a source of energy for the myocardium, 
particularly during periods of increased cardiac demand, 
such as exercise or fasting. Specifically, EAT provides a 
substantial supply of free fatty acids, which are crucial 
for the heart's energy metabolism [50]. Additionally, EAT 
secretes various anti-inflammatory adipokines, that are 
signaling proteins specifically released by adipose tissue, 
such as adiponectin and omentin, which maintain tis -
sue homeostasis and metabolic flexibility [ 51]. However, 
as cardiometabolic stress accumulates, EAT undergoes 
both volumetric expansion [ 52] and phenotypic changes, 
characterized by increased secretion of pro-inflamma -
tory cytokines and infiltration by immune cells [ 53]. This 
transformation contributes to myocardial inflammation, 
fibrosis, and diastolic dysfunction. EAT from patients 
with cardiometabolic HFpEF was reported to secrete sev-
eral pro-inflammatory adipokines such as tumor necrosis 
factor-alpha (TNF-α), monocyte chemoattractant pro -
tein-1 (MCP-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1 beta 
(IL-1β), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), resis -
tin, S100A9, and many others [ 54–57]. Beyond its meta -
bolic effects, EAT and paracardial fat may mechanically 
constrain the heart, reducing myocardial distensibility 
and impairing systolic and diastolic function especially 
in obese patients, where increased pericardial restraint 
can elevate filling pressures and reduce compliance [ 58]. 
Of clinical relevance, accumulation of EAT has consis -
tently been associated with left ventricular hypertrophy, 
diastolic dysfunction and dilatation of the atria, which 
are considered typical hallmarks of HFpEF [ 59]. In addi -
tion, EAT has been related to increased cardiac filling 
pressures and exercise intolerance in HFpEF. Consistent 
with these observations, longitudinal studies have shown 
that higher EAT at baseline is associated with new-
onset HFpEF, as well as a worse prognosis in those with 
established HFpEF, independent of overall obesity and 
important comorbidities [ 56, 59]. In 188 patients with 
HFpEF, Pugliese et al. showed that EAT accumulation 
is associated with worse hemodynamic and metabolic 
profile and is an independent predictor of heart failure 
hospitalizations and mortality [ 60]. Although these data 
clearly support an association between EAT expansion, 
HFpEF-related features and adverse outcome, the causal 
role of EAT in the pathophysiology of HFpEF remains to 
be proven. Reverse causality and confounding factors, 
such are systemic inflammation or coexisting conditions, 
cannot be ruled out. Studies in preclinical models will

Defective metabolic flexibility
Growing evidence indicates that HFpEF is a multisys -
temic syndrome where inflammatory signals and release 
of free fatty acids from other organs contribute to derail 
cardiac metabolism and function by affecting metabolic 
flexibility in cardiomyocytes [ 61]. The accumulation of 
free fatty acids represents a condition of fuel overload for 
cardiomyocytes, with subsequent mitochondrial dysfunc-
tion and alteration of myocardial energetics. Indeed, in 
HFpEF, the heart loses its ability to effectively adjust sub -
strate utilization in response to variations in nutritional 
status, energy substrate availability, and hemodynamic 
load, resulting in inefficient ATP production and subse -
quent adverse effects on cardiac function and structure 
[62]. For instance, a study demonstrated that the produc-
tion of adipocytes-related factors leads to inhibition of 
cardiomyocytes contraction by lowering the intracellu -
lar calcium transients [ 63]. Such maladaptive metabolic 
remodeling of the HFpEF myocardium promotes the 
accumulation of toxic metabolic intermediates ultimately 
leading to cardiomyocyte apoptosis and cardiac fibrosis 
[64]. These metabolic disturbances disrupt myocardial 
energetics and impair contractile reserve, contributing to 
ventricular stiffening and diastolic dysfunction. Defective 
mitophagy and impaired mitochondrial turnover exacer -
bate oxidative stress, which activates pro-inflammatory 
pathways such as NF-kB and NLRP3 inflammasome sig -
naling, further driving fibrosis and myocardial stiffness 
[65]. The activation of this inflammatory cascade leads to 
the secretion of pro-inflammatory markers, such as TNF- 
α, IL6, IL-1β, which perpetuate fibrosis and myocardial 
stiffness [66]. The pathophysiological features have been 
further investigated through advanced metabolic imaging 
[67]. A pivotal study, utilizing positron emission tomog -
raphy (PET) study in HFpEF patients revealed impaired 
myocardial energetic and mechanical reserve, along with 
reduced coupling between blood flow and oxygen con -
sumption during stress [ 68]. These findings highlight 
energetic inefficiency as a central contributor to HFpEF 
and underscore the value of non-invasive imaging in 
capturing human-specific metabolic alterations beyond 
what animal models can offer [ 67]. Whether alterations 
of metabolic flexibility are casually involved or merely 
reflect downstream consequences of other pathophysi -
ological changes remains to be determined. Monitor -
ing changes in substrate utilization and fuel overload 
could represent an attractive strategy to predict meta -
bolic impairment and cardiomyocyte exhaustion. In this 
regard, a recent study found that higher concentrations 
of lipid and amino acid-derived metabolites, particularly 
those associated with mitochondrial damage (e.g., butyr -
ylcarnitine and N-formylmethionine), could serve as 
early biomarkers of preclinical left ventricular diastolic 
dysfunction [69].
Immunometabolic alterations
In HFpEF, a confluence of metabolic and immune path -
ways culminates in a state of persistent cardiac inflam -
mation and dysfunction. Insulin resistance is a core 
mechanism in HFpEF, impeding glucose uptake by car -
diac cells and disrupting anti-inflammatory signals. This 
leads to uncontrolled NF-κB activation via TLR4 in 
immune cells, thus promoting transcription of NF-kB-
dependent inflammatory genes including TNF-α, IL-6, 
and IL-1β, which are instrumental in propagating sys -
temic inflammation and drive myocardial injury [ 66, 70]. 
Adipose tissue, especially when dysfunctional in the con -
text of obesity, becomes an active player in this inflam -
matory response [ 55]. This dysfunctional tissue changes 
its secretory profile: it decreases the release of adiponec -
tin, a protective adipokine, while increasing the levels of 
leptin and resistin [56, 59]. These changes influence renal 
sodium reabsorption, contribute to volume overload and 
foster the development of cardiac fibrosis, ultimately 
resulting in adverse remodeling and impaired LV relax -
ation [71].
In conditions of metabolic stress and fuel overload, the 
mitochondria generate high amounts of reactive oxygen 
species (ROS), which contribute to amplify the inflam -
matory response [ 72]. ROS stimulate the NLRP3 inflam -
masome, a multiprotein complex that plays a key role in 
the immune system's response to stress. Activation of 
the NLRP3 inflammasome is a crucial step in the matu -
ration and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such 
as IL-1β [73]. Specifically, it aids in the conversion of the 
inactive precursor pro-IL-1β to its active, inflammation-
promoting form. In HFpEF, this sustained inflammatory 
state is closely linked to fibrotic remodeling, as cytokines 
like IL-1β and TNF-α stimulate TGF-β1–SMAD sig -
naling and activate NF-κB, driving extracellular matrix 
deposition and structural cardiac changes. This vicious 
cycle of inflammation and fibrosis ultimately contributes 
to cardiomyocyte injury, microvascular dysfunction, and 
the progression of HFpEF, as demonstrated in preclinical 
studies [73, 74].
In cardiometabolic HFpEF, immune cell dynamics are 
also fundamentally altered, with increased infiltration 
of monocytes that differentiate to pro-inflammatory 
M1 macrophages and effector T cells within cardiac 
and adipose tissues, secreting additional inflammatory 
mediators like IFN-γ and perpetuating a vicious cycle 
of inflammation and fibrosis [ 74–77]. These immune 
cells, once activated, not only affect local tissue environ -
ments but also exert systemic effects, contributing to

the chronic, low-grade inflammation that characterizes 
HFpEF. Furthermore, adipose tissue expansion leads to 
local hypoxia, which in turn activates hypoxia-inducible 
factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), driving the expression of angio -
genic factors such as VEGF [75]. However, the angiogenic 
response is typically insufficient, resulting in chronic 
hypoxia and perpetuating a state of inflammation and 
subsequent fibrosis [76, 77].
The intricate link among insulin resistance, adipokine 
dysregulation, oxidative stress, and immune cell infiltra -
tion contributes to the hallmark pathophysiological fea -
tures of HFpEF, namely increased myocardial stiffness 
and impaired diastolic relaxation, eventually leading 
to increased filling pressures and venous congestion in 
HFpEF. Evidence suggests that adipokine dysregulation, 
such as elevated levels of leptin, directly contributes to 
the development of diastolic dysfunction [ 59]. Addi -
tionally, neutrophils contribute to oxidative stress and 
cytokine release, particularly IL-6 and myeloperoxidase 
(MPO), further perpetuating myocardial inflammation 
and dysfunction [ 78]. Even though several studies have 
implicated chronic low-grade inflammation as a central 
mechanism in HFpEF, the causal role of inflammation 
in HFpEF remains to be fully elucidated. A thorough 
understanding of the immune-metabolic landscape in 
HFpEF underscores the potential for targeted interven -
tions aimed at modulating these key pathways to amelio -
rate disease progression and improve patient outcomes. 
Functional studies in preclinical HFpEF models are 
important to defining causality. Without such validation, 
caution should be exercised in interpreting these findings 
causative.
Microvascular endothelial dysfunction
Endothelial dysfunction (ED) stands at the crossroads 
of cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, 
acute coronary syndromes and stroke and it is mainly 
driven by inflammation and oxidative stress (Fig. 3). 
These factors not only independently damage the vascu -
lar system but also amplify the vascular injury induced 
by risk factors (i.e. obesity, diabetes), thereby precipitat -
ing microvascular function within the myocardium with 
subsequent impairment of LV relaxation, development 
of cardiac hypertrophy and HFpEF [ 79]. HFpEF arises 
from a constellation of endothelial-related alterations 
including inflammation, ROS accumulation and nitric 
oxide degradation, defective angiogenesis, and reduced 
microvascular vasodilation and capillary recruitment 
affecting oxygen delivery as well as distribution of piv -
otal hormones such as insulin, leptin and ghrelin to the 
myocardium [ 80, 81]. In this regard, growing evidence 
suggests that impaired delivery of insulin has detri -
mental actions on myocardial and skeletal muscle func -
tion by promoting mitochondrial dysfunction, reducing 
contractile efficiency, and impairing relaxation kinetics 
[82]. However, drugs that potentiated insulin signaling 
such as sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, and incretins 
were generally associated with an augmented risk of HF 
development or worsening of the preexisting HF, while 
antidiabetic drugs that counter hyperglycemia through 
non-insulin pathways such as metformin and sodium-
glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT-2 inhibitors) 
decreased the risk of HF development [ 26, 83, 84]. Hence 
the clinical significance of capillary recruitment in the 
HFpEF myocardium remains to be determined. Besides 
transport of relevant hormones, reduced capillary den -
sity compromises blood flow and myocardial oxygenation 
thus creating a condition of chronic hypoxia which fos -
ters inflammation and hypertrophy. This proinflamma -
tory milieu is characterized by elevated levels of IL-6, 
TNF-α, sST-2, pentraxin-3 and changes in endothe -
lial adhesion molecular phenotype with the increased 
expression of vascular cell adhesion protein 1 (VCAM-1) 
and E-selectin which together promote monocyte migra -
tion. Eventually, the consequent local ROS accumula -
tion transforms NO into peroxynitrite cutting down its 
bioavailability [ 85–88]. As a consequence, the down -
stream soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) function, and 
thus cGMP-induced protein kinase (PKG) activation, is 
reduced, causing an increase in the cardiomyocyte rest -
ing tension secondary to hypophosphorylation of titin 
and abolition of the antihypertrophic and antifibrotic 
effects of PKG [89, 90].
Microvascular inflammation also stimulates the differ -
entiation of endothelial cells (EC) towards a pro-fibrotic 
phenotype through a process known as “endothelial-
mesenchymal transition (EndoMT)” [ 91]. EndoMT is 
becoming increasingly recognized as a major driver of 
myocardial fibrosis and is characterized by the loss of EC 
markers and a shift toward a mesenchymal or myofibro -
blastic phenotype with increased expression of α smooth 
muscle actin (α-SMA). Furthermore, EndoMT was seen 
to act in paracrine manner and to induce cardiomyocyte 
apoptosis through TGF-β1 and SMAD signaling [ 92, 93]. 
In response to hemodynamic stress, as observed in car -
diometabolic HFpEF, EndoMT generates a substantial 
number of cardiac fibroblasts which lead to a quantitative 
increase in total collagen content as well as to a dispro -
portion between its various isoforms, with an overex -
pression of collagen type I relative to type III, and an 
increased degree of crosslinking between collagen mol -
ecules [94]. However, further studies are needed to fully 
establish its causal role in human HFpEF.
ECs contribute to cardiac fibrosis also by produc -
ing molecules like CTGF and EDN1. Targeting EDN1 
has been shown to prevent features of hyperglycemia-
induced cardiomyopathy in experimental models of 
diabetes, including capillary loss, decreased CDH5

expression, increased TGFβ1 levels, and cardiac fibrosis 
[69]. Another mechanism linking endothelial dysfunction 
with myocardial fibrosis consists in the release of TGF-
β1-rich exosomes from cardiac microvascular ECs, with 
subsequent activation of cardiac fibroblasts[ 95]. Acti -
vated ECs also play a role in inflammation by express -
ing adhesion molecules (SELE, ICAM-1, VCAM-1) that 
facilitate leucocyte movement into tissues [96]. Addition-
ally, they produce cytokines like IL-6, which are involved 
in the initial response to injury, and chemokines such as 
CXCL8 and CCL2, which attract cells to the inflamma -
tion site [ 97]. Upregulation of the immune system trig -
gers the release of inflammatory cytokines, which not 
only results in plaque development and smooth muscle 
cell morphological changes but also contribute to oxida -
tive stress [98].
Furthermore, ECs present antigens via class II HLA 
molecules, implicating them in the immune response 
and inflammation in the setting of HFpEF [ 99]. The pro-
inflammatory environment induced by immune cells can 
lead to smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) transitioning from 
a contractile to a synthetic migratory phenotype, a phe -
nomenon known as “phenotypic modulation” . This switch 
is accompanied by the downregulation of contractile 
markers, such are SM22α, SM‐MHC, α‐SMA, and cal -
ponin and upregulation of stem-cell-like markers such 
is Sca-1/Ly6a that is mediated by the transcription fac -
tor KLF4 [ 100]. While this switch is a part of the natural 
vascular repair, its chronic activation is detrimental. Syn -
thetic VSMCs lose their ability to contract and instead 
they proliferate, migrate, and contribute to vascular 
remodelling [101] (Table 1).

Epigenetic remodeling
Epigenetics refers to changes in gene expression or cel -
lular phenotype that do not involve alterations to the 
DNA sequence itself but are influenced by environmen -
tal, genetic, and lifestyle factors [ 78]. In the context of 
HFpEF, epigenetic modifications, namely DNA methyla -
tion and histone modifications, play an important role 
in disease pathogenesis and progression (Fig. 4) [105]. In 
HFpEF, changes in DNA methylation patterns have been 
observed in genes involved in inflammation, fibrosis, and 
cellular signaling [ 106].  A network-oriented genome-
wide DNA methylation study of circulating CD4 +  T 
lymphocytes from HFpEF patients showed hypometh -
ylated HFpEF-specific differentially methylated posi -
tions (DMPs) associated with FOXB1, ELMOD1, and 
DGKH genes wherein ROC curve analysis revealed that 
increased expression levels had a reasonable diagnostic 
performance in predicting HFpEF (AUC ≥ 0.8, p < 0.05 
[105, 107].

Mechanisms-based therapies in HFpEF
The window of therapeutic intervention in HFpEF, espe -
cially its cardiometabolic phenotype, remains poorly 
defined [ 126]. As the prevalence of this condition rises, 
drawing a comprehensive blueprint of its management, 
from existing challenges to future prospects, is urgently 
needed [ 126, 127]. At the forefront, challenges in effec -
tive treatments loom large. A major hurdle is the intri -
cate heterogeneity of HFpEF [ 107]. This conglomerate 
of diverse etiologies and pathophysiology necessitates 
a depth of understanding that transcends conventional 
cardiological paradigms [ 112]. While our grasp on the 
disease has evolved, the labyrinthine molecular under -
pinnings of HFpEF often result in therapeutic strate -
gies that may be suboptimal. Precision medicine has the 
potential to transform HFpEF management, replacing a 
generic approach with tailored therapies based on indi -
vidual profiles [ 128]. Epigenomics, transcriptomics, and 
metabolomics enable understanding disease trajectories 
and predicting responses, paving the way for a more per -
sonalized and effective care approach [ 129]. Established 
therapies in HFpEF such as SGLT2i and GLP-1RA are 
discussed in detail elsewhere. The growing understand -
ing of the molecular cues underlying HFpEF has paved 
the way for mechanism-based approaches in this set -
ting. In this context, the role of epigenetic modulation 
and targeting of these modulations is gaining significant 
attention.
Epi-drugs
Histone deacetylase inhibitors(HDACi) like sodium 
butyrate and the FDA-approved Vorinostat (SAHA) 
have emerged as potential therapeutic agents in HFpEF 
[130]. These inhibitors exert cardioprotective effects 
by reducing myocardial inflammation and oxidative 
stress, which are critical players in HFpEF pathophysiol -
ogy [ 131]. HDACi have been reported to blunt myocar -
dial inflammation, exemplified by the inhibition of the 
NF-kB signaling pathway, and mitigate oxidative stress 
in experimental models of HF [ 132]. Vorinostat, a potent 
pan-inhibitor of class I, II, and IV HDACs, modulates 
the autophagic response in cardiac cells, providing pro -
tection against ischemia-reperfusion injury, a condition 
that can exacerbate CmHFpEF [ 133]. It has been found 
to reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, 
further underscoring its potential utility in managing 
inflammation associated with the disease [ 131]. Addi -
tionally, it attenuates Takotsubo-like myocardial injury, 
which shares common features with HFpEF, by targeting 
an epigenetic axis [134].
The integration of bromodomain and extra-terminal 
motif (BET) inhibitors into the treatment paradigm for 
HFpEF represents a cutting-edge approach in epigenetic 
therapy. These inhibitors, particularly JQ1, have been 
effective in experimental models of heart failure with 
preserved and reduced ejection fraction, by attenuating 
cardiac hypertrophy and inflammation [ 135–137]. The 
BET inhibitor RVX-208 is an oral small-molecule inhibi -
tor of bromodomain and extraterminal (BET) proteins 
with BD2 selectivity, which has recently been shown to 
modulate transcriptional programs implicated in cancer, 
renal and cardiovascular disease. Of clinical relevance, 
RVX-208 (also known as Apabetalone) was associated 
with a significant reduction of cardiovascular events 
(50%) and heart failure hospitalizations (52%) in a recent 
phase III clinical trial including patients with diabe -
tes and established cardiovascular disease [ 138]. Taken 
together, these data suggest that BET reader proteins act 
as pivotal orchestrator of gene transcription and their 
pharmacological modulation by BETi could be a prom -
ising strategy to reset pathological gene expression thus 
rescuing HFpEF features.

RNA therapies
Therapeutic modulation of microRNAs in the context of 
HFpEF seems also promising. Recent clinical trials have 
underscored the role of specific miRNAs in improving 
cardiac damage, suggesting their potential in treating 
HFpEF [ 139]. The inhibition of miR-132, for example, 
has been demonstrated to be safe and effective, leading 
to favorable changes in myocardial fibrosis markers [140]. 
The miRNA drug CDR132L, targeting miR-132, has 
shown promising benefits in patients with chronic HF. 
In preclinical studies, CDR132L, a synthetic inhibitor of 
miR-132, has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing dia-
stolic dysfunction and left atrial size and remodeling—
key aspects of HFpEF [ 141]. Remlarsen, a miR-29 mimic 
that exhibits potent antifibrotic activity, has shown prom-
ise in Phase II clinical trials for reducing keloid scar for -
mation. Given the critical role of fibrosis in HFpEF, such 
results suggest potential applicability of miR-29 mim -
ics like Remlarsen in its management [ 106, 142]. Recent 
trials have demonstrated that specific microRNAs can 
improve cardiac damage and have potential in disease 
treatment. For instance, the inhibition of miR-132 was 
safe and showed positive trends in myocardial fibrosis 
markers, with the miRNA drug CDR132L showing ben -
eficial effects in chronic HF patients [143].
Soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators
Soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators, particularly 
vericiguat, represent a promising new class of medica -
tions for treating HFpEF. They work by enhancing the 
nitric oxide-cGMP pathway, leading to improved blood 
vessel function, reduced cardiac workload, and better 
overall heart function. Early clinical trials have shown 
positive effects in reducing heart failure hospitaliza -
tions and improving symptoms, making these drugs an 
exciting addition to the therapeutic arsenal for HFpEF 
[144]. Vericiguat, a sGC stimulator, has been shown to 
be effective in HFpEF. This drug works by improving 
vascular compliance, reducing pulmonary and systemic 
vascular resistance, and improving overall heart func -
tion. A recent meta-analysis of VITALITY-HFpEF and 
VICTORIA trials confirmed the benefits of Vericiguat in 
HF patients with LVEF < 45%, especially in patients with 
LVEF < 24%; while a less pronounced effect was observed 
in HF patients with LVEF ≥ 45% [ 145, 146]. Clinical tri -
als focusing on specific HFpEF phenotypes are needed to 
appraise the potential soluble guanylate cyclase stimula -
tors in cmHFpEF.
Anti-inflammatory therapies
Given the pronounced role of systemic and cardiac 
inflammation in HFpEF, several approaches target -
ing inflammatory targets have been tested over the last 
decade. In the DHART trial, 2 weeks treatment with 
anakinra, an IL-1 receptor antagonist, reduced systemic 
inflammation, increased aerobic exercise tolerance and 
peak VO2 in patients with HFpEF [ 147]. However, these 
beneficial effects were not confirmed in the larger follow-
on study DHART2 where, despite a significant reduction 
in CRP , NTproBNP and an improvement in exercise tol -
erance with anakinra, there was no improvement in the 
primary endpoint of peak VO2 [148].
Interleukin (IL)-6 is a central inflammatory mediator 
and potential therapeutic target in HF. Prior studies have 
shown that IL-6 concentrations are elevated in patients 
with HF, while data in HFpEF are scarce [ 149]. A recent 
study showed that HFpEF patients with the highest IL-6 
levels had greater  body mass index; higher N-terminal 
pro–B-type natriuretic peptide, C-reactive protein, and 
tumor necrosis factor-α levels; worse renal function and 
were more likely to have diabetes [ 149, 150]. Moreover, 
patients with HFpEF and highest IL-6 concentrations 
had more severely impaired peak oxygen consumption 
as well as 6-min walk distance. IL-6 concentrations were 
also strongly associated with increases in total body fat 
and trunk fat, more severe symptoms during submaxi -
mal exercise, and poorer patient-reported health sta -
tus. These findings support testing the hypothesis that 
therapies that inhibit IL-6 in patients with HFpEF may 
improve clinical status. The HERMES trial, an interna -
tional, randomized, double-blind study evaluating the 
effect of ziltivekimab 15  mg compared with placebo in 
addition to standard care, will determine the efficacy and 
safety of the anti-IL-6 therapy in patients with HFpEF 
and HFmrEF [151].
Inhibition of myeloperoxidase (MPO), a heme contain -
ing peroxidase that is expressed mainly in neutrophils, 
may represent a promising approach in HFpEF. In the 
SATELLITE trial, the MPO inhibitor AZD4831 showed 
a 69% decrease in MPO activity within the 30 days trial 
period and an increase in exercise capacity and well -
ness score, highlighting its role as a potential treatment 
for HFpEF. Further studies and trials are needed to fully 
understand the efficacy of MPO inhibitors in HFpEF 
[152].
Lifestyle interventions
Beside the pharmacological therapies, lifestyle interven -
tions are gaining importance as they address metabolic 
and cardiovascular dysfunctions that characterize cmH -
FpEF. Weight loss, calorie restriction, or intermittent 
fasting do not only improve metabolic health and boost 
cardiovascular fitness but also help reduce inflamma -
tion. Physical exercise improves cardiorespiratory fit -
ness and diastolic function, enhancing life quality and 
reducing hospitalization risk in HFpEF patients [ 153, 
154]. Kitzman et al. demonstrated that both aerobic exer-
cise and caloric restriction significantly improved peak

with additive effects when combined, though neither

Diet, including sodium restriction, Mediterranean, 
and DASH diets, also supports better cardiovascu -
lar outcomes [ 156]. Additionally, managing stress and 
optimizing sleep reduces sympathetic overactivity and 
inflammation [ 157]. Different molecular studies high -
light how these lifestyle changes affect cellular mecha -
nisms like autophagy, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial 
function, emphasizing the importance of lifestyle inter -
ventions not only for clinical outcomes but also for 
advancing HFpEF research and treatment development 
[158,159]. However, a recent study highlighted the lim -
ited number of dietary intervention trials conducted spe -
cifically in patients with HFpEF, despite the high burden 
of diet-sensitive comorbidities in this population [ 160]. 
The potential reversibility of the cmHFpEF phenotype 
through weight loss is currently being investigated in 
ongoing studies, including those evaluating GLP-1 recep-
tor agonists and other anti-obesity medications, making 
this a particularly timely and clinically relevant area [34].
While challenges in managing cardiometabolic HFpEF 
are undeniable, the confluence of advanced pharmacolog-
ical strategies and the promise of personalized medicine 
paints a hopeful trajectory. The path forward beckons 
with the promise of treatments that are not only more 
precise but also profoundly effective, heralding a new era 
in the management of HFpEF. Broad strategies targeting 
inflammation have failed to improve outcomes in HFpEF 
suggesting that a better understanding of the immune 
landscape and inter-organ crosstalk are warranted to 
direct more precise interventions in this setting. Basic 
and translational research in this area is fundamental to 
refine existing knowledge and unveil specific molecular 
targets driving inflammation and metabolic alteration in 
this complex syndrome.
Conclusions
The increasing prevalence of HFpEF is closely linked with 
the rising incidence of cardiometabolic disorders, including 
obesity, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension. These 
conditions promote metabolic inflammation, resulting in 
epigenetic modifications that impair myocardial relaxation 
and exacerbate diastolic dysfunction. The cmHFpEF phe-
notype, commonly observed alongside metabolic disorders, 
highlights the critical intersection of metabolic excess and 
cardiovascular pathology. The obesity epidemic underscores 
the central role of cardiometabolic stress in cardiovascular 
health, underscoring the need to address CmHFpEF as a 
multifaceted medical and public health challenge. The intri-
cate pathophysiological and molecular aspects of cmHFpEF 
demand rigorous scientific investigation, with a focus on its 
metabolic associations and subsequent immunometabolic 
alterations. By unraveling these underlying immunometa-
bolic changes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, 
endothelial dysfunction, and pivotal molecular pathways, 
we can advance the therapeutic landscape. This necessitates 
a paradigm shift towards precision medicine, targeting the 
molecular origins of the disease, to enhance patient-specific 
outcomes in the management of HFpEF.
