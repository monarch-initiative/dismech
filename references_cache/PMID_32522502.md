# First presentation of LPIN1 acute rhabdomyolysis in adolescence and adulthood.
**Authors:** Pizzamiglio C, Lahiri N, Nirmalananthan N, Sood B, Somalanka S, Ostrowski P, Phadke R, O'Donovan DG, Muntoni F, Quinlivan R
**Journal:** Neuromuscul Disord (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.nmd.2020.05.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nmd.2020.05.004)

## Content

LPIN1 mutations are a known common cause of autosomal recessive, recurrent and life-threatening acute rhabdomyolysis of childhood-onset. The first episode of rhabdomyolysis usually happens in nearly all cases before the age of 5 and death is observed in 1/3 of patients. Here we present two cases of acute rhabdomyolysis with a milder phenotype caused by LPIN1 mutation presenting in adolescence (11 years old) and adulthood (40 years old) after Parvovirus infection and metabolic stress, respectively. In our opinion, the mutation types, epigenetic factors, the environment exposition to triggers or the existence of proteins with a similar structure of LPIN1, may have a role in modulating the onset of rhabdomyolysis. LPIN1 should be included on a panel of genes analysed in the investigation of adult individuals with rhabdomyolysis. Metabolic and viral stressors should be included in the list of possible rhabdomyolysis precipitant.

Introduction:  47 
 48 
Lipin-1 (LPIN1) is an 890 aminoacid intracellular protein involved in different pathways of fatty 49 
acid metabolism (1). It belongs to the family of phosphatidate phosphatase (PAP) enzymes and its 50 

 3 
main role is to catalyse the conversion of phosphatidate to diacylglycerol, the penultimate step of 51 
triglyceride synthesis which constitute the major energy storage in our body (2).  52 
In humans, LPIN1 is mainly expressed in skeletal muscles and adipose tissue but lower levels have 53 
been found in the gastrointestinal tract as well (3).  54 
 55 
LPIN1 mutations are a known cause of autosomal recessive, recurrent and life-threatening acute 56 
rhabdomyolysis of childhood-onset. The first description dates back to 2008 (4). Since then, other 57 
cases have been described in the literature (5,6) and LPIN1 mutations are now considered the 58 
second most common cause of early-onset acute rhabdomyolysis, after primary fatty acid oxidation 59 
defects (5). The episodes are usually triggered by fever, fasting or general anaesthesia (7) and the 60 
outcome is severe with death in 1/3 of patients ( 5). Clinically patients present with episodes 61 
characterised by myalgia and myoglobinuria; creatine kinase (CK) increases over 100,000 UI/L but 62 
can reach 1x106 UI/L. Between episodes, clinical examination, CK and acyl-carnitine profile are 63 
usually normal (7). 64 
 65 
To our knowledge, there has only been two reports of two individuals presenting with adult-onset 66 
rhabdomyolysis (6,8). We describe two cases of LPIN1 mutation with a milder phenotype 67 
presenting in adolescence and adulthood respectively. Our aim is to highlight the importance of 68 
looking for LPIN1 mutations in adults presenting with acute rhabdomyolysis. 69 
 70 
Case report: 71 
Case 1: An 11-year-old Caucasian girl of British origin presented to A&E with diffuse muscle pain, 72 
swelling and brown coloured urine. Her CK was 560,000 IU/L. At first, an inflammatory cause was 73 
suspected as there were no apparent precipitating factors such as exercise, infection, fasting or 74 
general anaesthesia. The autoimmune screening was negative but she was later found to have 75 
positive serology indicating Parvovirus infection despite a lack of fever. Six months later she 76 

 4 
presented with the second episode of acute rhabdomyolysis; CK was 250,000 IU/L, once more, 77 
there was no clear precipitant. Both episodes were treated with intravenous fluids and she did not 78 
require dialysis. Her CK went back to normal a fter a couple of weeks on both occasions. 79 
Neurological examination was normal between the episodes.  80 
The past medical history was unremarkable apart from a six-year history of painful calves: she 81 
described suffering from calf cramps 30 minutes after physical education at school. Motor 82 
development was normal (she walked before 1 year of age) and learning difficulties were not 83 
reported. There was no history of hypoglycaemia in infancy or worsening of symptoms with 84 
fasting. She was able to run. Family history was negative for rhabdomyolysis and muscle cramps. 85 
Parents were unrelated. Other investigations included carnitine and acylcarnitines which were 86 
normal. The electrodiagnostic study was not done. Following the first episode, a muscle biopsy was 87 
performed (Figure 1). It showed very mild myopathic changes with one macrophage cluster and one 88 
basophilic regenerating fibre per haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) level and increased lipid droplets. 89 
Electron microscopy revealed 30nm viral particles in the pinocytotic vesicles of intramuscular 90 
capillaries but not muscle fibres. Parvovirus was detected by real-time PCR of the frozen muscle 91 
homogenate. After the second episode, next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel of 30 genes 92 
commonly associated with acute rhabdomyolysis was performed which identified  homozygous in-93 
frame deletion of exon 18 (c.2295-865_2410-30del) in LPIN1. See Table 1 for clinical details and 94 
for the list of genes included in the NGS panel. 95

Case 2: A 40-year-old Caucasian female of Italian origin presented to the A&E department 97 
complaining of muscle pain and diffuse muscle weakness. Neurological examination was 98 
unremarkable. She was oliguric and passed low volumes of rust-coloured urine. Four days before, 99 
she had returned from a holiday in South Africa where she was Kayaking in the hot weather. The 100 
precipitant may have been stress-related since she reported a highly stressful driving lesson 12 101 
hours before the onset of symptoms. At presentation, her CK was 102,185 IU/L with transaminitis 102

(ALT 1407 IU/L [NR 0-50 IU/L]) and a serum creatinine (sCr) of 158 umol/l [NR: 49-90 umol/l] 103

She was discharged on day 16 at which point her sCr was 488 umol/L and CK was 160 IU/L.   108 
One year later, she had a second less severe episode. Once again, she was very stressed after driving 109 
on a motorway; a few hours later, she developed marked shoulder and proximal upper limb pain 110 
and swelling. The CK level was 45,987 IU/L. Renal function was normal and she was treated with 111 
intravenous fluids. 112 
A few months later, she possibly had another milder episode associated with infection when she 113 
developed a paronychia on her right index finger and noticed myalgia in her right arm for a few 114 
days, although she had no myoglobinuria. 115 
She had no past medical history and was not on any regular medication. Her parents are of Sicilian 116 
origin and are first cousins. She was conceived naturally and born after an uneventful pregnancy. 117 
She did not have any health problems or muscle pains during childhood. She described herself as 118 
not being a particularly athletic child, but was able to participate in sport at the same level as her 119 
peers without difficulty. Her exercise levels increased as an adult, and she regularly swims and rides 120 
a mountain bike, including long distances, but had not had any abnormal muscle pains prior to the 121 
presenting episode. She has a brother who has not had any similar episodes .  She has a young 122 
daughter, who is well. 123 
Investigations included infection and autoimmune screening, which were negative. Plasma 124 
acylcarnitine was within normal limits (27 umol/l, normal values 15-53). A NGS panel of 30 genes 125 
associated with acute rhabdomyolysis showed the same mutation detected in Case1: LPIN1 126 
homozygous deletion of exon 18 (c.2295-865_2410-30del). The muscle biopsy and the 127 
electrodiagnostic study were not performed. See Table 1 for details.  128

Discussion: 130 
LPIN1 is a known cause of rhabdomyolysis in early childhood. According to literature, the first 131 
episode of rhabdomyolysis usually happens at a mean age of 21 months and in nearly all cases 132 
before the age of 5 (5). Here we present two cases, the first one with onset in adolescence (11 years 133 
old) and the second one in adulthood (40 years old).  134 
LPIN1 protein is encoded by the LPIN1 gene located in chromosome 2p25.1 and contains 20 exons 135 
(9). Over 25 mutations in LPIN1 gene has been described so far (10) but a genotype/phenotype 136 
correlation has not been described yet. Most of them are nonsense or deletion mutations, resulting 137 
in a loss of function of the protein (10). Both our patients present with the same in-frame deletion of 138 
exon 18, which is the most common in the Caucasian population and is found in approximately 139 
68% of patients (6). Deletion of exon 18 is not usually associated with a later onset of disease, 140 
although the presence of an in-frame deletion instead of a loss of function mutation could be the 141 
biggest contributor to the milder phenotype observed in our patients. In our opinion, other factors 142 
may have a role in modulating the onset of rhabdomyolysis, such as epigenetic factors or the 143 
environment exposition to triggers. Moreover, in mammalian, the LPIN family includes other two 144 
proteins named LPIN2 and LPIN3 (3) encoded by different genes. LPIN2 and LPIN3 share with 145 
LPIN1 a similar structure and the role as a PAP enzyme (11). For these reasons, they could help 146 
compensate for the lack of LPIN1 if this protein is mutated or non-functional. However, few data 147 
on the localisation of LPIN protein family in skeletal muscle tissue sections are available (3,11) and 148 
further studies are needed to clarify the expression patterns and any compensatory role of the other 149 
LPIN family members, including LPIN2 and LPIN3, in skeletal muscle with LPIN1 deficiency. 150 
In heterozygous carriers, LPIN1 mutation can cause cramps, myalgia and can trigger statin-induced 151 
myotoxicity (12). However, in our cases parents of both patients were asymptomatic. 152 
 153 

 7 
The pathophysiology of LPIN1-induced rhabdomyolysis still needs to be clarified. In Figure 2, 154 
possible mechanisms are illustrated. According to a recent article by Vissing et al., LPIN1 155 
deficiency affects lipolysis and subsequently limits the fatty acid oxidation during exercise  (13). 156 
Moreover, sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) is the site of phospholipid production and SR stress has 157 
been recently hypothesized to have a primary role in causing LPIN1 myopathy (14) in mouse 158 
skeletal muscles. In fact, SR stress leads to the activation of lipogenesis and indirectly damage 159 
mitochondrial function, an important energy pathway inside cells ( 15, 16). An indirect sign of 160 
lipogenesis activation is the frequent finding of lipid droplets accumulation in muscle biopsies of 161 
patients with LPIN1 mutations (5). However, lipid droplets should not be considered a specific 162 
pathological finding in muscles of LPIN1 mutations as they can be present in a variety of other lipid 163 
myopathies (17). Other possible findings are signs of mitochondrial dysfunction such as ragged red 164 
fibres or cytochrome oxidase negative fibres (18). Lipid droplets have been found in the muscle 165 
biopsy of our first patient. In the second patient, a muscle biopsy was not performed due to the 166 
availability of an extensive and less invasive genetic panel that led to the final diagnosis.  167 
 168 
Several triggers of rhabdomyolysis in LPIN1 have been described, including fever, exercise, 169 
fasting, infections and general anaesthesia (19). All those situations require a high energy demand, a 170 
need that cannot be satisfied in LPIN1 disorder due to reduced fatty acid oxidation (13). 171 
Our second patient developed acute rhabdomyolysis following stressful events. During stress, there 172 
is an increased sympathetic tone with catecholamine release ( 20). Catecholamine role includes 173 
stimulation of lipolysis and release of fatty acids (21), processes that are impaired with LPIN1 174 
mutation (13). Stress should also be included in the list of possible precipitant of myoglobinuria in 175 
these patients. Infection-related myoglobinuria was observed in both patients, as already described 176 
(5, 10).  177 
Parvovirus infection may have triggered rhabdomyolysis in our first patient. Parvovirus B19 is a 178 
rare but recognised cause of rhabdomyolysis in patients with underlying muscle diseases (22) and 179 

 8 
including patients with no other medical history (23). Parvovirus B19 belongs to the family of 180 
Picornaviridae, which has an affinity for endothelial cells.  181 
 182 
LPIN1 mutations cause severe episodes of rhabdomyolysis that can lead to death. In a case series, 183 
death was observed in 1/3 of young patients (5). In our second case, the first episode of 184 
rhabdomyolysis was severe resulting in ITU admission and dialysis while all the other episodes 185 
were less severe, requiring intravenous fluids for both patients. 186 
Diagnosis of LPIN1 related rhabdomyolysis is important for management. According to Pichler et 187 
al. (24), management should include hyperhydration with glucose infusion to establish anabolism 188 
during rhabdomyolysis. Moreover, between episodes, it would be useful to avoid fasting  and 189 
increase the caloric intake with carbohydrate during a situation that could lead to catabolism. Our 190 
patients are under follow up in a specialised clinic for rhabdomyolysis and related disorders and 191 
since receiving instructions and advice on how to avoid rhabdomyolysis, no further episodes have 192 
been reported.  193 
 194 
In conclusion, we would like to highlight the use of next generation sequencing panels in the 195 
investigation of rhabdomyolysis. LPIN1 mutations should not be considered a cause of 196 
rhabdomyolysis in young children alone. Metabolic and viral stressors should be included in the list 197 
of possible rhabdomyolysis precipitants and episodes managed appropriately.   198
