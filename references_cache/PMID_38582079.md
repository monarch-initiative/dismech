Tauopathies are age-associated neurodegenerative diseases whose mechanistic 
underpinnings remain elusive, partially due to a lack of appropriate human 
models. Here, we engineered human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived 
neuronal lines to express 4R Tau and 4R Tau carrying the P301S MAPT mutation 
when differentiated into neurons. 4R-P301S neurons display progressive Tau 
inclusions upon seeding with Tau fibrils and recapitulate features of tauopathy 
phenotypes including shared transcriptomic signatures, autophagic body 
accumulation, and reduced neuronal activity. A CRISPRi screen of genes 
associated with Tau pathobiology identified over 500 genetic modifiers of 
seeding-induced Tau propagation, including retromer VPS29 and genes in the 
UFMylation cascade. In progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and Alzheimer's 
Disease (AD) brains, the UFMylation cascade is altered in 
neurofibrillary-tangle-bearing neurons. Inhibiting the UFMylation cascade 
in vitro and in vivo suppressed seeding-induced Tau propagation. This model 
provides a robust platform to identify novel therapeutic strategies for 4R 
tauopathy.

Tauopathies, characterized by accumulation of Tau aggregates, are a heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative diseases. These include Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common tauopathy, as well as subtypes of frontotemporal lobar degeneration with Tau pathology (FTLD-Tau) such as Pick’s disease (PiD), corticobasal degeneration (CBD), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), and several others1. Tau is encoded by a single gene (MAPT) and gives rise to six isoforms, including isoforms containing either three (3R) or four (4R) microtubule-binding repeats, due to alternative splicing of exon 102. There are three subtypes of tauopathies, 3R, 4R, and 3R/4R mixed tauopathies that exhibit distinct Tau filament structures revealed by cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Tau filaments in AD (3R/4R)3, PiD (3R), CBD (4R), and PSP (4R)4 are structurally distinct. Among the MAPT mutations that cause familial cases of FTLD-tau, many alter the ratio of 3R to 4R5,6; several of the mutations, including P301S/L7, are located in exon 10 and are therefore 4R-specific.

Human iPSC-derived neurons are invaluable in modeling neurological diseases, including but not limited to tauopathies8,9. Combined with CRISPR-Cas9 technology, iPSC-derived neuronal platforms enable isogenic controls for precise disease modeling10 and functional genomics to identify disease modifiers11. However, iPSC-derived neurons express very low levels of 4R Tau even after extended periods of culture and are thus unsuitable to model 4R tauopathy, such as PSP12,13. The low levels of exon-10-containing Tau also limit their relevance in modeling the dominant familial FTLD-tau mutations located in exon 109. Moreover, it has been difficult to recapitulate robust Tau aggregation in human iPSC-derived neurons. While no insoluble Tau aggregates were observed in MAPT-P301L or MAPT-IVS10+16 iPSC neurons9, limited Tau inclusions were observed in the processes after 120 days. An important factor contributing to this could be the lack of 4R Tau in iPSC-derived neurons 14.

In the current study, we report the establishment of a robust and scalable human iPSC 4R tauopathy model. We engineered hiPSC lines to express 4R Tau and 4R Tau carrying the P301S MAPT mutation when differentiated into neurons. Upon seeding with Tau fibrils, we showed that 4R-P301S neurons develop a progressive spread of Tau aggregation, aberrant neuronal activity, and endolysosomal pathway dysfunction. Using CRISPRi-based functional genomic screening, we identified novel genetic modifiers and pathways and established a robust platform to discover potential therapeutic strategies.

4R Tau contains four microtubule-binding domains (MTBDs) and plays an important role in the pathogenesis of tauopathies. However, modeling 4R tauopathy in iPSC neurons has been difficult since hiPSC-derived neurons, including i3Neurons (i3N) that express inducible Neurogenin-2 transcription factor, express very low levels of 4R Tau12,13,15. To enhance expression of 4R Tau, we edited the MAPT locus of i3Neurons via CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing with a donor plasmid containing mutations at the intron-exon 10 junctions and silent mutations within exon 10 to promote exon 10 inclusion16 (Figure 1A). Clones with one or both alleles of MAPT edited to express 4R Tau were selected. Two 4R-homozygotic clones and one heterozygotic clone were confirmed to have normal karyotypes and express markers of pluripotency (Figure S1A-D).

We differentiated homozygotic clone #1 into excitatory neurons with the addition of doxycycline15. The parental i3N line and a heterozygotic line with one allele of MAPT locus edited were included as controls. Western blot analyses were performed using RD3 recognizing 3R MTBDs and ET3 antibody specific for exon 1017 in combination with HT7, a pan-Tau antibody. Treatment with phosphatase allowed more accurate alignment with the six isoforms of recombinant Tau and served as a molecular weight (MW) control (Figure 1B). Compared to i3N neurons, which express only 3R Tau labeled with RD3 antibody, neurons derived from homozygotic 4R Tau (clone #1) express exclusively 4R Tau labeled with ET3 (Figure 1B). In contrast, neurons derived from a 4R-hetero clone #1 (Figure S1C) express both 3R Tau and 4R Tau (Figure 1B). Immunocytochemistry confirmed the immunoreactivity of ET3 in 4R homozygous clone #1 neurons (Figure 1C) and clone #2 (Figure S1E), but not in i3N neurons (Figure 1C). Interestingly, compared with i3N neurons, both 4R homozygous and heterozygous neurons express higher levels of phospho-Tau species and paired helical filaments (PHFs), which have been associated with AD and primary tauopathies (Figure 1D, 1E, S1F, S1G).

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD)-linked MAPT mutation P301S located in exon 10 is highly aggregation-prone18,19,20. P301S mutation was introduced to Tau (4R) line with CRISPR/Cas9 (Figure 1A, Figure S1H). Two clones of homozygous 4R-P301S tau-expressing (4R-P301S) iPSCs were confirmed with normal karyotypes and markers of pluripotency (Figure S1I-S1K). To dissect the molecular alterations induced by the P301S mutation, we performed bulk RNA-sequencing analyses of 4R and 4R-P301S neurons. Unsupervised cluster and principal component analyses (PCA) revealed that 4R and 4R-P301S neurons clustered separately (Figure S1L, 1M), consistent with the pathogenic nature of P301S mutation. Over 2200 genes were altered by the P301S mutation (Table S2). Upregulated genes showed enrichment in Neuron differentiation and Cell-Cell signaling pathways (Figure 1F), while downregulated genes were enriched in Organelle localization, Intracellular transport, and Transmembrane transport (Figure 1G, 1H). The P301S mutation downregulated expression of Transmembrane p24 trafficking proteins (TMED) (Figure 1H), which play essential roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis, proper folding, and transport of proteins in the cell. Other downregulated genes include STX-17, involved in the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, and VPS13A, an essential protein facilitating non-vesicular lipid transfer between the ER and other organelles (i.e, the mitochondria, endosomes, and lysosomes). (Figure 1H). These findings suggest that 4R-P301S neurons are likely to be more vulnerable to proteostasis imbalance.

Using MC1 antibody, a conformation-specific antibody that recognizes pathogenic Tau from human patients21, we detected no obvious insoluble Tau inclusions in 4R and 4R-P301S neurons even after weeks in culture (Figure S2A). To model seeding-induced Tau propagation, we treated 4R and 4R-P301S neurons with K18-P301L-Tau (K18) fibrils, a truncated form of human Tau containing only the aggregation-prone repeat domain of the microtubule-binding domain22 (Figure 2A). Fibrils added to media led to MC1-positive inclusions only in 4R-P301S, not in 4R nor i3N (3R) neurons, after 3–5 weeks of incubation with weekly half-media dilutions (Figure 2B, Figure S2B, S2C). Since MC1 immunoreactivity requires domains outside of K18, the MC1+ inclusions are made of endogenous 4R Tau upon seeding with Tau fibrils, a process reflecting templating-induced propagation of misfolded protein. Weekly analyses at 1–5 weeks post-seeding showed a progressive increase in MC1+ inclusions (Figure 2C, 2D), supporting a prion-like model of Tau propagation. Further analyses revealed that MC1+ inclusions were immunoreactive to antibodies against oligomers and phosphorylation (Figure 2E). We also performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM), where we detected and quantified prominent fibrillar Tau structures in the soma of 4R-P301S neurons seeded with Tau (Figure 2F, 2G).

To further analyze the biochemical nature of the inclusions, lysates were sequentially solubilized by Triton-X, followed by SDS. In 4R neurons, the majority of Tau and phospho-Tau was observed in the Triton-soluble fraction with no high-molecular-weight (MW) Tau and phospho-Tau observed, consistent with the lack of MC1+ aggregates with or without K18 Tau seeding (Figure 2H). Quantification confirmed that Tau-seeding of 4R-P301S neurons significantly reduced levels of AT8-positive phospho-Tau in the Triton-soluble fraction, while markedly elevating those in the Triton-insoluble fraction (Figure 2I). Another species of phospho-Tau (AT270) was also observed in the SDS-soluble fraction of 4R-P301S neurons seeded with K18-Tau fibrils (Figure S2D).

Next, we used flow cytometry with MC1 antibody to measure somatic Tau inclusions quantitatively. We examined the extent of Tau inclusions at two timepoints, using two independent 4R-P301S clones to confirm the reproducibility of the phenotype. At Day 21 post-seeding of K18 fibrils (1.5 μg/ml), 4R-P301S neurons exhibited highly consistent levels of MC1+ population of neurons (Figure S2E, S2F). The percentage of MC1+ neurons increased 5–6-fold between Day 21 and Day 42 post-seeding, reflecting seeding-induced propagation/amplification (Figure 2J, 2K). Increasing Tau fibrils from 1.5 μg/ml to 3.0 μg/ml resulted in a modest increase in Tau inclusions, either at Day 21 (Figure S2G, S2H) or 42 (Figure S2I, S2J) post-seeding. To address the relative contributions of the initial “seeding” vs. subsequent “propagation”, we used AlexaFluor488-tagged K18 seeds to track the seeded neurons. We then immunostained with MC1 and quantified neurons positive for 488+ tau seeds only, positive for 647+ MC1 only, or positive for both 488+ seeds and 647+ MC1 (Figure S2K). Our analysis revealed that the majority (> 70%) of neurons bearing somatic Tau aggregates contained only MC1+ inclusions without detectable seeds, regardless of seed concentration (Figure S2L). These results suggest that most Tau inclusions can be attributed to propagation mechanisms rather than to the initial fibril seeding process. We also addressed whether the mutant Tau fibrils induced species-specific templating of mutant Tau in 4R-P301S neurons by seeding neurons with WT 0N4R Tau fibrils for 3 weeks and detected MC1-positive inclusions only in 4R-P301S, not in 4R neurons (Figure S2M). Thus, Tau aggregation in 4R-P301S neurons is not limited to seeding with fibrils with the same mutated residue.

To interrogate the transcriptomic alterations in MC1+ Tau inclusion-bearing neurons, we performed bulk RNA-seq and identified the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in 4R-P301S+K18 that developed seeding-induced inclusions and 4R+K18 neurons that did not have inclusions (Figure S3A, S3B, Table S2). To assess gene expression similarities between our in vitro platform and post-mortem human AD brains, we performed a gene overlap analysis of the DEGs with those comparing AT8+ (tangle-bearing) vs AT8- excitatory neurons in human AD brains23 (Figure 2L). A statistically significant gene overlap was detected in the downregulated DEGs (odds ratio = 5.7, p-value = 2.4e-12). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses revealed that the molecular functions of these overlapping proteins largely comprised action potential and calcium transport networks (Figure 2M, Table S2). To exclude possible effects of the P301S mutation, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing to compare the transcriptomes of tau-seeded vs. non-seeded 4R-P301S neurons (Figure S3C-G). We reasoned that pseudo-bulk analyses of the DEGs would be enriched with molecular signatures of inclusion-containing human neurons (Table S2). Indeed, comparison with AT8+ vs AT8- excitatory neurons in human AD brains revealed a highly significant overlap with the upregulated DEGs (odds ratio = 11.4, p-value = 3.1e-45, Figure 2N). Synaptic localization and vesicular trafficking networks are some of the prominent overlapping pathways (Figure 2O, Table S2).

4R-P301S neurons exhibited downregulated pathways in Organelle localization, Intracellular transport, and Transmembrane transport (Figure 1G, 1H). To further dissect alterations of cellular machinery caused by Tau inclusions, we performed TEM analyses to compare subcellular structures in 4R and 4R-P301S neurons with or without K18 seeding. Marked accumulation of abnormal vesicular structures with multilayer membranes was observed exclusively in 4R-P301S neurons treated with K18 fibrils (Figure 3A, 3B). These abnormal structures were reminiscent of multilamellar bodies (MLBs), organelles containing multiple concentric membrane layers of lysosomal origin24. MLBs were observed in the soma (Figure 3C), as well as in the processes of 4R-P301S neurons (Figure 3D).

The accumulation of MLBs in 4R-P301S neurons with Tau inclusions strongly suggests dysfunctional endolysosomal membrane trafficking. Since lysosomal exocytosis has been linked with the spread of misfolded protein aggregates, including alpha-synuclein25, we reasoned that impaired lysosomal fusion could be involved in our seeding-induced tauopathy model. To block lysosomal fusion, we inhibited the activity of VAMP7, a calcium-dependent v-SNARE protein that mediates lysosomal membrane fusion26, by overexpressing dominant-negative VAMP7 (VAMP7DN)25, a truncated form of VAMP7 (Figure 3E). 4R-P301S neurons were infected with Lenti-GFP-VAMP7DN or Lenti-GFP-control, followed by seeding with K18 fibrils, and analyses were performed 2 weeks later (Figure 3F). Perturbing VAMP7-mediated membrane fusion significantly reduced extracellular Tau released after KCl-induced depolarization, relative to total Tau, measured with ELISA as described27 (Figure 3G). Strikingly, blockage of VAMP7 activity resulted in a significant increase in MC1+ Tau inclusions normalized to cell number (Figure 3H, 3I). Taken together, our findings support a close association of endolysosomal dysfunction with Tau propagation in seeded 4R-P301S neurons.

Tau pathology correlates strongly with cognitive decline28,29, though how neuronal functions are affected by Tau inclusions remains poorly defined due to the challenge of labeling Tau inclusions in live neurons. To track live human neurons with or without Tau aggregates for calcium imaging, we modified the 4R-P301S line to insert a HaloTag at the 5′ end of MAPT locus using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (Figure 4A). Upon binding of the HaloTag to a synthetic ligand, HaloTag undergoes a conformational change and fluoresces at a given wavelength, enabling live-cell imaging of endogenous Tau molecules30. Two independent homozygous 4R-P301S-HaloTag iPSC clones were confirmed with normal karyotypes and markers of pluripotency (Figure S4A-C), and clone #1 was used for functional experiments. Upon addition of JFX-549 synthetic ligand30, 4R-P301S neurons with and without obvious aggregated Tau can be visually distinguished at 568 nm (Figure 4B).

To monitor neuronal activity simultaneously, we transduced seeded 4R-P301S neurons with a new generation of genetically encoded calcium sensor GCaMP8-fast (Lenti-hSynapsin-hGCaMP8f)31 (Figure 4C, S4D). Calcium transients, measured by the change in fluorescence over basal fluorescence (dF/F), were recorded over 2 minutes per somatic region of interest (ROI) within an image frame (Figure 4C). ROIs in the soma were then classified into visually distinguishable -inclusion or +inclusion bins, and spontaneous neuronal activities from single neurons were extracted from the calcium trace (Figure 4C). Compared to neurons lacking inclusions, those with inclusions displayed a significant decrease in spike amplitudes, whereas firing and synchronicity rates remained unchanged (Figure 4D). To measure evoked responses, 50 mM KCl was perfused to induce chronic depolarization (Figure 4E). Neurons with visible somatic inclusions exhibited significant lower trace peak heights than those without visible inclusions (Figure 4F, 4G). Thus, both spontaneous and stimulation-evoked neuronal activity were reduced in human neurons with Tau inclusions.

The release of Tau is enhanced by neuronal activity in mouse brains and human neurons27,32–34. FTD mutations, such as V337M, induce hyperexcitability in human neurons10. To directly examine the effects of neuronal activity on Tau seeding and spread, we induced chronic silencing of neuronal activity by an inhibitory DREADD receptor, hM4Di, genetically modified to respond specifically to the synthetic ligand clozapine-N-oxide (CNO), resulting in suppression of neuronal firing35. 4R-P301S-HaloTag neurons were infected with mCherry-hM4Di lentivirus, followed by K18 seeding (Figure 4H). One week after seeding, CNO was applied to the culture weekly for 2 additional weeks to determine the outcome of chronic suppression of neuronal activity (Figure 4H). Robust expression of mCherry-hM4Di receptor was confirmed before and after CNO administration (Figure S4E). Using calcium imaging, we confirmed that neuronal activities were silenced upon addition of CNO (Figure 4I). Levels of Tau inclusions, measured with MC1+ aggregates, were significantly reduced by CNO treatment compared to those with vehicle treatment (Figure 4J). Thus, neuronal activity promotes seeding-induced Tau propagation in human neurons.

This new model of 4R Tau propagation is engineered from our i3N neuron platform15, which facilitates the large-scale production of iPSC-derived glutamatergic neurons and enables scalable CRISPRi-based functional genomics analysis11,36. To identify genetic modifiers of Tau propagation, we engineered 4R-P301S iPSCs to stably express dCas9 by inserting CAG promoter-driven dCas9-BFP-KRAB into the CLYBL safe harbor locus by CLYBL-TALENs11. Two clones of heterozygous 4R-P301S-dCas9 lines were confirmed to have normal karyotype (Figure S5A, S5B). Clone #1 was stained for pluripotency markers (Figure S5C) and was used for subsequent CRISPRi experiments. The knockdown efficiency of the 4R-P301S-dCas9 iPSCs was confirmed by TFRC staining and flow cytometry of cells transduced with Lenti-sgRNA targeting TFRC (Figure S5D, S5E).

The 4R-P301S-dCas9 iPSCs were transduced with a custom lentiviral CRISPRi sgRNA library targeting genes involved in Tau pathobiology based on a genome-wide CRISPRi screen for modifiers of Tau oligomer levels in human iPSC-derived neurons37. This library consists of sgRNAs targeting 1,073 genes with five sgRNAs per gene and 250 non-targeting control (NTC) sgRNAs (Table S3). After library transduction, iPSCs were differentiated into neurons, and K18-Tau was seeded at D7 (Figure 5A). Neurons were collected at D19, fixed, stained for MC1, and FACS sorted based on MC1 signal in the 488 nm channel into either MC1+ or MC1- bins (Figure 5A). Frequencies of cells expressing each sgRNA were determined by next-generation sequencing to uncover genes for which sgRNAs showed significant changes in frequency, either an increase or a decrease in relation to Tau inclusion phenotype (Figure 5A and Table S4).

As expected, MAPT sgRNAs were enriched in cells with reduced Tau inclusions (Figure 5B). Many of the other hits that reduced Tau inclusions were mitochondrial genes, including many components in complex I, complex II, and cytochrome C (Figure 5B, 5C). We also observed enrichment of genes involved in UFMylation, a post-translational modification analogous to ubiquitylation (UBA5, UFM1, UFBP1, UFL1, UFC1) (Figure 5B, 5C) related to processes such as ER-associated protein degradation, ribosome-associated protein quality control at the ER, and ER-phagy38,39. GO enrichment analyses revealed striking enrichment of distinct mitochondria and biosynthesis-related pathways (Figure S5F).

The top genes whose knockdown increased Tau inclusions (Figure 5B) were highly enriched in multiple pathways of vesicular trafficking, as revealed with GO enrichment analyses (Figure S5G). Top hits include genes important for endolysosomal biogenesis and trafficking, such as the Late Endosomal/Lysosomal Adapter (LAMTOR) complex responsible for MAPK and mTOR activation, as well as core subunits of the Homotypic fusion and Protein Sorting (HOPS) complex, VPS16, and VPS41, which are critical for endosome-to-lysosome trafficking and fusion (Figure 5C). Genes involved in Golgi trafficking, such as Conserved Oligomeric Golgi (COG) complex40 are also strong modifiers of Tau aggregation. Inhibition of VPS54, a subunit of the GARP (Golgi-Associated Retrograde Protein) complex, also exacerbated Tau inclusions (Figure 5B, 5C). VPS54 is involved in the retrograde transport of vesicles from endosomes to the trans-Golgi network (TGN). Notably, VPS29, a subunit of the retromer complex, emerged as one of the strongest modifiers. The retromer complex is responsible for the retrograde transport of cargo from endosomes to the Golgi apparatus and plasma membrane, and VPS29 contributes to cargo recognition and stability of the retromer complex.

To validate these results, we deleted VPS29 in 4R-P301S lines. Three independent clones of 4R-P301S;VPS29−/− lines were generated and confirmed with normal karyotypes and markers of pluripotency (Figure S6A-C). Using qRT-PCR, we confirmed the deletion of VPS29 in 2 clones (Figure S6D). All three clones and the 4R-P301S parental line were differentiated into neurons and seeded with K18-Tau fibrils. Three weeks after K18-Tau seeding, immunocytochemistry revealed a striking increase of MC1+ inclusions in all three clones of 4R-P301S;VPS29−/−, from 20% in the parental line to >80% in 4R-P301S;VPS29−/− neurons (Figure 5D, 5E). To further confirm the effects of VPS29, we performed flow cytometry analyses in two of the clones. More than 90% of 4R-P301S;VPS29−/− neurons exhibit MC1+ Tau inclusions, compared with 20–30% in 4R-P301S neurons (Figure 5F, 5G), establishing VPS29 as a predominant modifier of Tau propagation in our 4R human tauopathy model.

We next selected top candidates from the UFMylation cascade (Figure 6A) to validate the sgRNA hits that reduce Tau inclusions. Lentiviral constructs expressing UBA5, UFM1, or UFBP1 shRNAs under the U6 promoter and GFP under the SV40 promoter were generated for knockdown in hiPSC neurons. Using qRT-PCR, we confirmed an efficient knockdown of UBA5 and UFM1 (Figure S7C, S7D), but knockdown of UFBP1 resulted in significant toxicity (data not shown). In flow cytometry analyses performed 21 days post-seeding, the MC1+ signal was measured in GFP+ neurons, so only those expressing the given shRNA were included (Figure S7A). Seeding induced robust MC1+ inclusions in neurons infected with control GFP-expressing lentivirus. However, MC1+/GFP+ neurons were markedly fewer with Lenti-shUBA5 or Lenti-shUFM1 than with Lenti-GFP controls (Figure 6B, 6C). In agreement with flow cytometry analyses, immunocytochemistry quantification revealed that treatment with Lenti-shUBA5 or Lenti-shUFM1 resulted in significantly fewer in MC1+/GFP+ neurons than the Lenti-GFP controls (Figure 6D, 6E). The inclusion-promoting effects of UFMylation are not due to its effect on MAPT mRNA since levels of MAPT mRNA were not affected by Lenti-shUBA5 nor Lenti-shUFM1 (Figure S7B).

We next compared the levels of UFMylation in K18-seeded and unseeded 4R-P301S neurons in Triton-soluble and Triton-insoluble (SDS-soluble) lysates, a fraction enriched with high MW Tau species from 4R-P301S neurons with Tau inclusions. Immunoblotting for an anti-UFM1 antibody preferentially recognizing free UFM1, revealed a strong reduction of free UFM1 (9.1 kDa) only in the triton-insoluble fraction of seeded 4R-P301S neurons (Figure 6F, 6G). To begin to dissect the underlying mechanisms, we examined whether the steady-state levels of Tau protein were affected by UFM1 downregulation in 4R-P301S neurons without seeding. Downregulation of UBA5 and UFM1 with Lenti-shUBA5 or Lenti-shUFM1 infection resulted in a significant reduction of Tau protein, detected with a pan-Tau antibody (HT7) using western blot (Figure 6H, 6I). We further confirmed the reduction of Tau protein using an ELISA assay (Figure 6J). Thus, inhibition of UFM1 lowers soluble steady-state Tau protein, which could underlie the reduced Tau propagation in seeded human 4R-P301S neurons.

To determine whether UFMylation reduces Tau propagation in vivo, we inhibited Uba5 in a fibril-induced Tau spreading mouse model as described previously41,42. Injection of Lenti-shUba5 in the hippocampus of PS19 mice, which express human MAPT-P301S mutation, lowered mouse Uba5 by over 50% compared with Lenti-GFP control (Figure 7A, 7B). The spread of Tau inclusions was triggered with unilateral injection of Tau fibrils (K18) to the contralateral side, which was injected with either control lentivirus or lentivirus expressing shRNA targeting Uba5 (Figure 7C, 7D). The spread of Tau aggregates to the contralateral hippocampus was quantified by normalizing to aggregates in the ipsilateral inoculation side. Compared with Lenti-GFP control, Lenti-shUba5 significantly reduced the amount of Tau aggregates in the contralateral side, indicating that Uba5 knockdown reduces Tau spreading in vivo (Figure 7E, 7F).

We next investigated UFMylation in human patients with 4R tauopathies. PSP brains were co-immunostained with anti-UFM1, which preferentially labels free UFM1, and MC1 to label Tau inclusions. MC1+ tangle-bearing neurons exhibited lower levels of free UFM1 immunoreactivity (Figure 7G, 7H), which aligns with the lower levels of free UFM1 in lysates from 4R-P301S neurons with Tau inclusions. In AD brains, we performed similar co-immunostaining with MC1 and anti-UFM1. Like in PSP brains, MC1+ neurons exhibited lower levels of UFM1 immunoreactivity (Figure 7I, 7J). Our findings support that UFMylation could promote Tau pathology in both PSP- and AD-related tauopathy.

Here we report a robust 4R tauopathy model in iPSC-derived human neurons, recapitulating seeding-induced propagation of Tau inclusions in human tauopathies with endogenous levels of Tau. Our model extends the recent progress in modeling 4R tauopathy in human neurons14,43 and provides a unique platform to investigate the cellular machinery underlying Tau propagation. Compared with other hiPSC models with limited somatic inclusions43, our model exhibits somatic 4R Tau inclusions upon seeding with Tau fibrils in a time-dependent manner. Tracking seeded neurons with fluorophore-tagged fibrils revealed that propagation, not seeding per se, is responsible for most of the Tau pathology in our 4R-P301S model. 4R Tau has a higher propensity to aggregate and become hyperphosphorylated44,45, which could be attributed to the fact that MTBDs on Tau contain two hexapeptides (VQIVYK and VQIINK) that are important for Tau filament formation. VQIVYK encompasses residues 306–311 and is present in all isoforms, whereas VQIINK encompasses residues 275–280 and is in the second MTBD (R2) encoded by exon 10, and thus is only present in the 4R isoforms. Furthermore, the P301S mutation increases Tau’s susceptibility to conformational changes that promote the exposure of these hexapeptides, leading to a higher propensity for beta-strand formation. Along with fibrillar Tau structures, we observed striking endolysosomal alterations in the soma of inclusion-bearing neurons, reminiscent of those present in AD brains. Different from the direct reprogramming of fibroblasts by viral-mediated transduction14, our engineered iPSC lines allow fast, differentiation of large quantities of neurons homogeneously, facilitating the application of CRISPRi/a screenings. Our platform enables systematic investigation of how strain selectivity, and/or selective vulnerability in cellular machinery, contributes to the heterogeneity of tauopathies.

Given the close association of the progression of Tau aggregation with cognitive decline, there is an urgent need to model the propagation of tauopathy in human neurons. Despite obvious differences between our in vitro Tau inclusion model and tangle-bearing neurons in AD brains, significant similarities were observed, highlighting the pathophysiological relevance of our iPSC-derived tauopathy model. TEM analyses of inclusion-bearing neurons revealed a striking accumulation of MLBs, a phenomenon often observed in tauopathy and other neurodegenerative conditions. However, unlike Tau inclusions in AD, PSP, and CBD, which consist of wild-type tau, 4R neurons lacking P301S mutation exhibited no Tau inclusions in our current experimental condition. There are many possible explanations, including the challenges of modeling a chronic process that takes decades in a culture lasting only weeks and the fact that the P301S mutation likely accelerates the process greatly. Transcriptomic analyses also revealed that that P301S mutation by itself markedly downregulates genes involved in intracellular and membrane trafficking, which could render 4R-P301S neurons more vulnerable to Tau proteostasis imbalance triggered by seeding. Consistent with this notion, functional knockdown of the v-SNARE lysosome-associated protein VAMP7 promoted Tau propagation.

The spread of Tau pathology often follows a predictable, trans-neuronal pattern, consistent with the role of neuronal activity in Tau spread46,47. Our tauopathy platform enabled the tracking of Tau pathology in live human neurons and uncovered intriguing crosstalk between neuronal activity and Tau propagation. FTDP-17 patients carrying P301S mutation exhibit neuronal hyperexcitability48,49. Other FTD mutations, such as V337M, also induce hyperexcitability in human neurons, suggesting a common pathogenic phenotype10. By chronically silencing neuronal activity using inhibitory DREADDs, we found that Tau propagation is significantly ameliorated, providing direct evidence that hyperexcitability associated with P301S mutation could promote Tau propagation.

Despite the correlation between the spread of Tau aggregates with cognitive decline, Tau’s cytotoxic mechanisms remain poorly understood50–52. It is challenging to measure neuronal function while simultaneously monitoring their Tau pathology, as it often requires antibody staining of fixed neurons. By Halo-Tagging 4R-P301S tau, we directly compared calcium influx of live neurons with or without somatic inclusions, providing the first direct evidence that Tau inclusions impair neuronal activity in human neurons. Our observation challenges the view that Tau aggregates are not toxic per se and is consistent with both the negative correlation of tauopathy measured by PET Tau tracer with neuronal activity measured with FDG PET53, as well as the suppression of baseline excitability and neuronal activity seen in aggregate-bearing neurons in human Tau (hTau) P301L mice54. In contrast, we and others have also shown that prior to forming aggregates, pathogenic Tau in soluble form induces hyperexcitability10,55,56. Thus, it is conceivable that the hyperactivity at the early stage of disease promotes Tau propagation, while at latter stages once aggregates are formed, further neuronal toxicity could lead to hypoexcitability. The ability to track Tau species will allow more detailed characterization of the functional consequences of different Tau species in living human neurons.

The cellular machinery underlying the spread of Tau aggregates remains poorly understood, partially due to a lack of an authentic robust Tau propagation model in human neurons. Our current model enables systematic analyses of cellular machinery using functional genomics. In a CRISPRi screen of a curated set of >1000 genes implicated in Tau pathobiology, we uncovered several strong modifiers of Tau propagation. For example, some of the key steps of Tau propagation that remain poorly understood are endocytic compartmental seeding, escape, and hastening the aggregation of naive Tau in the cytoplasm. Our CRISPRi screen revealed that the top genes whose knockdown increases inclusions are those involved in almost all aspects of vesicular trafficking and autophagy, including those involved in the endolysosomal biogenesis and trafficking, endosome-to-lysosome fusion, Golgi trafficking, retrograde transport of vesicles from endosomes to TGN, as well as a component of retromer complex required to transport cargos from endosome to TGN or plasma membrane. Based on our screen, we propose that the rate-limiting factor in propagation is the breaching of the organelle membranes and the escape of seeds from endocytic compartments, enabling the templating of naive Tau in the cytosol57.

As one of the core components of the retromer complex, VPS29 serves as a hub for vesicular trafficking58,59 and emerged as the top hit in this targeted screen. The two other components of the retromer complex, VPS35, whose mutation is linked with Parkinson’s disease60, and VPS26 are not included in the library. VPS29 deficiency in 4R-P301S cells markedly enhanced seeding-induced propagation by 2–3-fold. Retromer activity is diminished in the entorhinal cortex of late-onset AD patients61, consistent with data supporting the entorhinal cortex as one of the most vulnerable regions for Tau accumulation62. In a Drosophila model that overexpresses hTau, reduction of retromer activity induces a potent enhancement of Tau toxicity, including synapse loss, axon retraction, and survival via the production of a C-terminal-truncated isoform of hTau63. Alterations in retromer activity induced by VPS35 with D620N mutation promote Tau aggregation in the forebrain of a mouse model of Parkinson’s disease64. Concordantly, a deficiency in retromer complex activity exhibit increased levels of pathological Tau species in the cerebrospinal fluid65. Given our findings in the TEM analysis of MLBs, the VAMP7DN study, the enrichment of endolysosomal machinery among the CRISPRi screen hits and the validation of VPS29, we propose that blockage of endolysosomal trafficking is a major driver in the spread of Tau inclusions.

The proof-of-principle CRISPRi screen also uncovered unique modifiers of Tau propagation. Many of our top hits were mitochondrial genes, including many components of complex I, complex II, and cytochrome C. Interestingly, some of these genes overlap with the mitochondria-interacting proteins modified by FTD mutations in previous studies27. The mechanisms underlying reduced propagation here remain unknown. Since reduced neuronal activity ameliorates Tau propagation, lowering mitochondrial proteins might suppress neuronal activity, thus blocking the propagation.

Out of the top hits that promote Tau propagation are five out of the six genes in the UFMylation cascade, a process mediated by an enzyme cascade involving UBA5 (E1), UFC1 (E2), and an ER-localized trimeric ligase complex (E3) composed of UFL1, UFBP1, and CDK5RAP366. Validating the CRISPRi screen, we showed that inhibiting UFMylation via UBA5 and UFM1-targeted shRNA markedly blocked Tau propagation. Using an antibody that preferentially binds to free UFM1, we showed that free UFM1 is reduced in inclusion-bearing neurons in our model. Uba5 knockdown significantly ameliorated Tau spread in the hippocampus of tauopathy mice. We also observe free UFM1 reduction and tangle-bearing cells in both human PSP brains and AD brains, suggesting a possible convergence in mechanisms underlying pathogenic Tau propagation between familiar and sporadic tauopathies. Mechanistically, UFM1 knockdown in 4R-P301S neurons significantly reduces expression levels of Tau, suggesting that UFMylation enhances steady-state Tau protein abundance and may explain amelioration of Tau propagation. Together, these findings provide the first direct evidence of involvement of UFMylation in tauopathy and that targeting UFMylation could be beneficial in reducing Tau propagation. UFMylation modulates several biological processes, and its deficiency leads to neurodevelopmental disorders67. One of the principal targets of UFMylation is RPL2668, whose UFMylation activates translocation-associated ribosomal quality control69. Genetic inactivation of UFM1 or UFMylating enzymes causes the accumulation of translocation-stalled proteins at the ER and triggers ER stress. UFMylation is also strongly implicated in ER-phagy38. Further studies are needed to determine if UFMylation-enhanced Tau propagation is mediated by ER stress/ER-phagy, ribosomal quality control, or other unknown mechanisms.

In summary, we developed and characterized a human 4R iPSC platform exhibiting seeding-induced tau propagation and identified known and novel genetic modifiers. We anticipate this cellular model will serve as a powerful platform to reveal the mechanisms underlying 4R tauopathies and identify new targets for drug development.

One main limitation of the platform in the current stage is its inability to recapitulate features of aging neurons in the human brain. Additionally, unlike NFTs in AD brains which consist of 3R/4R isoforms of tau, our 4R model expresses only 4R Tau and requires the FTD-linked P301S mutation to develop aggregates. Thus, it models FTD-linked 4R tauopathy, not AD tauopathy per se. Further modification of this platform is needed to investigate whether AD-tauopathy can be recapitulated in the presence of amyloid β and/or via modifying AD-linked risk alleles. Moreover, studies have shown that pathogenic Tau spread is strongly modified by other cell types, including microglia. The lack of other cell types in our platform limits its ability to address only neuron-autonomous mechanisms. Lastly, we do not fully understand the potential long-term consequences of sustained UFMylation inhibition on essential cellular pathways and therefore must potentially consider conditional inducibility or precise titration of knockdown for future in vivo studies to mitigate potential off-target effects.

For comparison of resistant and non-resistant cell lines, a one-way ANOVA and a Tukey test with multiple testing correction. For two phenotypes and two colonies’ comparison, we used two-way ANOVA analysis, followed by Šidák’s multiple comparison’s test. The statistics used in ClueGO analyses revealed enriched networks with p-values < 0.05 from right-sided hypergeometric testing with Bonferroni correction. n=2–3 independent biological replicates were used for all experiments. n.s. denotes a non-significant difference. *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using RNA-sequencing of AT8- and AT8+ (NFT-bearing) excitatory neurons from a published human AD brain dataset23 and the FindMarkers function and MAST in Seurat. Differential gene expression overlap analysis was performed using the 4R-P301S+K18 vs 4R+K18 DEGs from the bulk RNA-seq analysis, 4R-P301S+K18 vs 4R-P301S DEGs from the pseudo bulk scRNA-seq analysis, and AT8+ vs AT8- DEGs for the AD brain analysis. Gene overlap statistical significance was assessed using the GeneOverlap R package85. Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analyses were performed using ClueGO version 2.5.9 in Cytoscape version 3.8.286,87. Enrichment and network analyses were applied from three ontologies including GO Molecular Function, GO Biological Process, and GO Cellular Components.

For every recorded time-lapse image, we selected ROIs covering all identifiable cell bodies using a semi-automated algorithm in NIS-Elements AR (Nikon). Neurons were categorized based on HaloTag signal punctate structures corresponding to large Tau inclusions (-puncta=without inclusion, +puncta=with inclusion). Further quantifications were performed using custom-written MATLAB (Mathworks) scripts. For single-cell activity, the fluorescence time course was measured by averaging all pixels within individual ROIs. Then we use the CaPTure toolbox to extract spike activity by a rolling average method called ‘DFF’88,89. A percentile-based threshold (mean + 2 standard deviations of fluorescence) was employed to detect peak events. The amplitude and frequency of each ROI was analyzed as the single-neuron activity. To determine the network activity, the synchronous firing rate of the entire cell population in the FOV was measured90. For KCl stimulation experiments, each image frame was divided by an average of all frames acquired during the first 0.5-sec window, which functioned as a baseline. Photobleaching was corrected by fitting exponentially weighted moving averages89

The primary screen was analyzed using the published MAGeCK-iNC bioinformatics pipeline11. Briefly, raw sequencing reads from next-generation sequencing were cropped and aligned to the reference using Bowtie v.0.12.991 to determine sgRNA counts in each sample. The quality of each screen was assessed by plotting the log10(counts) per sgRNA on a rank-order plot. Next, counts files of the two samples subject to comparison (MC1- and MC1+) were input into MAGeCK and log2 fold changes and p-values were calculated for each gene-targeting sgRNA. Following that, gene level knockdown phenotype scores were determined by averaging log2 fold changes of the top 3 sgRNAs targeting this gene with the most significant p-values. The statistical significance for each gene was determined by comparing the set of p-values for sgRNAs targeting it with the set of p values for non-targeting control sgRNAs using the Mann-Whitney U test, as described previously84. To correct for multiple hypothesis testing, we also calculated raw phenotype scores and significance p-values as well as for ‘negative-control-quasi-genes’ that were generated by random sampling with replacement of five non-targeting control (NTC) sgRNAs from all NTC sgRNAs. The final phenotype score for each gene was calculated by subtracting the raw phenotype score by the median raw phenotype score of ‘negative-control-quasi-genes’ and then dividing by the standard deviation of raw phenotype scores of ‘negative-control-quasi-genes’. The hit strength, defined as the product of knockdown phenotype score and –log10(p-value), was then calculated for all genes in the library and for ‘negative-control-quasi-genes’ generated above. Hit genes were determined based on the hit strength cutoff corresponding to a false-discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05. To find enriched annotations within hit genes, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed for MC1- and MC1+ neurons using the Molecular Signatures Database C5 Ontology gene sets77,78.
