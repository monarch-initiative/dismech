# Epidemiology and prognostic factors of necrotizing fasciitis in resource-limited regions based on 119 cases.
**Authors:** Chen Y, Huang Y, Shou J, Song R, Tan J, Deng J
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-025-13278-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-13278-8)

## Content

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare, life-threatening soft tissue infection with high amputation and mortality rates, often due to delayed diagnosis and treatment. This study analyzes the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors of NF to improve treatment strategies. A retrospective study was conducted on 119 NF patients treated at a tertiary hospital in Guangxi, China, from 2017 to 2023. Data on demographics, laboratory parameters, microbiology, treatments, and outcomes were collected and analyzed. Comparisons were made between survivors and non-survivors, and between patients with and without amputation. The predictive performance of the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Among 119 patients, the mean age was 56.7 years with a male-to-female ratio of 5.3:1. The lower extremities were most commonly affected (58.8%), and diabetes was prevalent (62.3%). Gram-negative bacteria were predominant (62.7%), particularly Escherichia coli (21.6%) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (19.4%), while Gram-positive bacteria were led by Staphylococcus aureus (14.9%). The overall antibiotic resistance rate was 18.7%, with Gram-negatives bacteria highly susceptible to ertapenem, imipenem, and piperacillin-tazobactam, and Gram-positives bacteria to vancomycin and levofloxacin. Amputation and in-hospital mortality rates were 16.8% and 11.8%, respectively. Risk factors for amputation encompassed lower extremity involvement, cutaneous and muscle necrosis, diabetes, elevated LRINEC scores, low RBC counts, and decreased albumin (all p < 0.05). Mortality was associated with advanced age, hypotension, coronary heart disease, hypoalbuminemia, elevated serum creatinine, uric acid, and lactate (all p < 0.05). The LRINEC score showed moderate predictive power for amputation (AUC = 0.726, cutoff = 8) but limited utility for mortality prediction (AUC = 0.592). NF predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly males, with diabetes as a common underlying condition. Early surgical intervention and empirical use of carbapenems combined with vancomycin are recommended for infection control. Enhancing early treatment effectiveness and optimizing disease management are key to improving patient outcomes in resource-limited settings.

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare but life-threatening soft tissue infection characterized by extensive necrosis of the fascia and surrounding tissues, frequently accompanied by systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and septic shock1. Although rare, with an incidence of 0.3 to 15 cases per 100,000 individuals, NF has devastating outcomes, including amputation rates of 20%-50% and mortality rates up to 30%-40%2,3. These outcomes make NF a critical challenge in emergency and critical care medicine.

High amputation and mortality rates stem from the nonspecificity of early symptoms, which often lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment4. Early-stage NF typically presents with localized pain and swelling, resembling cellulitis or other minor infections5. By the time hallmark signs such as skin necrosis or tense bullae appear, the infection has usually progressed significantly, increasing the risk of severe outcomes. The Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC), initially praised for its diagnostic utility positive predictive value (PPV) 92%, negative predictive value 96% for scores ≥ 6)6, has shown inconsistent results in subsequent studies, particularly for mild or early NF, limiting its reliability as a sole diagnostic tool7–9. Comprehensive assessments combining clinical findings with other diagnostic tools are essential to improve accuracy and avoid delays. NF is highly aggressive, with infection spreading to deep fascia within hours and often triggering SIRS. Timely surgical debridement is critical, as delays beyond 24 h can increase mortality risk ninefold10. Barriers to prompt intervention include the nonspecificity of symptoms, resource limitations, and patients’ financial or psychological concerns about surgery. Consequently, debridement is often performed only after substantial disease progression, further exacerbating outcomes. Effective management of NF demands a multidisciplinary approach integrating surgical debridement, intensive care, antimicrobial therapy, and nutritional support11. However, such comprehensive care relies on advanced healthcare resources, posing significant challenges for primary care institutions.

While NF presents universal diagnostic and treatment challenges, certain socio-economic and healthcare disparities in Guangxi, China, pose additional barriers to effective management. Limited medical resources, inadequate early diagnosis and treatment capabilities, and untimely referrals often delay patients’ access to specialized care. In economically disadvantaged areas, traditional beliefs, low health literacy, and financial constraints frequently result in delayed medical attention. Many patients initially rely on traditional therapies, such as herbal remedies or topical poultices, which often delay effective medical intervention and exacerbate disease progression. Reducing amputation and mortality rates remains the primary goal of NF management. This study systematically analyzes demographic, laboratory, surgical, and prognostic data of NF patients treated at our hospital over the past seven years, aiming to identify actionable strategies and provide targeted guidance for optimizing NF management in resource-limited settings.

This retrospective cross-sectional study investigated the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors of NF patients treated at a large tertiary hospital in southern China between January 2017 and December 2023. By focusing on cases in a resource-limited setting, the study aims to address gaps in existing literature on NF management and outcomes.

Inclusion criteria required patients to meet the diagnostic standards for necrotizing fasciitis as defined by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines12. Core criteria included the rapid onset of severe pain, the presence of tense bullae, skin discoloration or necrosis, and intraoperative findings of fascial necrosis. Exclusion criteria involved cases with incomplete medical records or missing key data.

In this study, detailed reviews of medical records were conducted for all patients. Information collected included demographics, health economic indicators, laboratory test results, microbiology and antibiotic susceptibility results, surgical and treatment details, as well as disease management and outcomes. Importantly, all laboratory tests and microbiological culture data were obtained from the first examination upon admission, ensuring consistency in baseline measurements. The LRINEC was calculated based on six blood parameters, including C-reactive protein, white blood cell count, hemoglobin, sodium, creatinine, and glucose levels, as detailed in Table 1. The primary outcomes of this study included major limb amputations, defined as those involving large segments, such as the lower leg, thigh, forearm, or upper arm, while excluding minor amputations of distal digits, such as toes or fingers. Additionally, in-hospital mortality directly attributed to NF or its related complications, such as septic shock or multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, was assessed. To ensure data integrity and patient confidentiality, all extracted records were fully anonymized, thereby minimizing potential selection bias and aligning with ethical research guidelines.

Blood culture samples were collected from all patients upon admission, prior to the initiation of antibiotic therapy. Blood samples were obtained using standard aseptic techniques by drawing blood from both sides of the veins. The samples were inoculated into both aerobic and anaerobic blood culture bottles and incubated at 37 °C for 6 days. Positive cultures were then transferred to appropriate solid media for further identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Strain identification was performed using Gram staining, and the VITEK 2 Compact automated microbiology system was used to identify isolated single colonies and conduct in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing. All procedures were strictly followed in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the 4th edition of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) Manual, and results were interpreted based on the M100-S31 document issued by CLSI13.

All patients received empirical antibiotic therapy upon admission, typically consisting of piperacillin-tazobactam combined with vancomycin. The antibiotic regimen was adjusted based on the results of drug susceptibility testing. Critically ill patients were transferred to the ICU for intensive care and underwent emergency or planned surgical debridement as needed. The core measures of supportive treatment included maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance, protecting vital organ functions, and providing adequate nutritional support.

Continuous variables were analyzed using independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, depending on normality, which was assessed via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted using MedCalc software (version 20.218; MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium) to evaluate the predictive performance of the LRINEC score, including calculations of the area under the curve (AUC), optimal cutoff values, sensitivity, and specificity. All other statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study included 119 patients diagnosed with necrotizing fasciitis, with a male predominance (male-to-female ratio: 5.3:1) and a mean age of 56.7 ± 11.7 years. The median hospital stay was 30 days (IQR: 15–44), and the median hospitalization cost was 70,230 RMB (IQR: 33,167–118,393).The lower extremities were the most commonly affected sites (58.8%), followed by the perineum (31.0%). The most prevalent comorbidity was diabetes mellitus, observed in 62.3% of patients, followed by hypertension (17.7%) and gout (15.1%).Cutaneous manifestations were predominantly necrosis (47.1%), followed by redness and swelling (36.1%) and tense blisters (16.8%). Shock, defined as systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg, was reported in 12.8% of patients, and 18.3% had positive blood cultures. The median LRINEC score was 8 (IQR: 5–10), with 58.0% of patients scoring ≥ 8.The rates of major limb amputation and in-hospital mortality were 16.8% and 11.8%, respectively. Detailed data are provided in Tables 4, 5 and 6.

The pathogens associated with necrotizing fasciitis were diverse. Single bacterial infections accounted for 47.1% of cases, polymicrobial infections for 30.3%, and no pathogen was isolated in 22.7% of cases. Gram-negative bacteria were the most prevalent (62.7%), with Escherichia coli (21.6%) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (19.4%) being the most common species. Gram-positive bacteria comprised 35.1% of the isolates, predominantly Staphylococcus aureus (14.9%) and Streptococcus pyogenes (6.7%). Additionally, a small proportion of fungal pathogens (2.2%) were identified (Table 2). A total of 25 resistant bacterial strains were isolated, yielding an overall resistance rate of 18.7% (25/134). Among these, extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing strains (ESBLs+) were the most common resistance mechanism, including Escherichia coli (14 strains) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (3 strains). Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) was detected in 5 strains, while the remaining resistant strains included methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative staphylococci (MRSCoN) and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRABA), totaling 3 strains.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing demonstrated high sensitivity of gram-negative bacteria to ertapenem, imipenem, piperacillin-tazobactam, and amikacin (> 90%). Notably, E. coli exhibited 100% sensitivity to ertapenem, while K. pneumoniae showed 100% sensitivity to imipenem. Gram-positive bacteria exhibited high sensitivity to vancomycin and levofloxacin (> 90%), with streptococci demonstrating 100% sensitivity to both agents. These findings provide critical insights for the empirical antibiotic therapy of NF patients, highlighting the importance of early and targeted treatment strategies (Table 3).

Non-survivors were significantly older than survivors (63.5 ± 12.4 vs. 55.8 ± 11.1 years, P = 0.018). In addition, hospital stay and costs were notably higher in the amputation group compared to the non-amputation group (P = 0.017 and P = 0.003, respectively). ICU stay was significantly prolonged in both the amputation group and the non-survivor group (P = 0.039 and P < 0.001, respectively). Of particular note, coronary heart disease was more prevalent in non-survivors than in survivors (28.6% vs. 6.7%, P = 0.030).

Lower limb infections were significantly more common in both the amputation group (90.0% vs. 52.5%, P = 0.002) and the non-survivor group (92.9% vs. 54.3%, P = 0.006). Skin necrosis was also more frequent in the amputation group compared to the non-amputation group (80.0% vs. 40.4%, P = 0.004), while diabetes mellitus as the primary causative factor was observed in a higher proportion of amputation cases (80.0% vs. 53.5%, P = 0.029).

Surgical data revealed that the amputation group underwent significantly more procedures (median 4 vs. 1, P = 0.008) with longer cumulative surgery durations (239 min vs. 100 min, P = 0.004). Furthermore, intraoperative muscle necrosis was markedly more frequent in the amputation group (70.0% vs. 12.1%, P < 0.001). Among non-survivors, systolic blood pressure was significantly lower compared to survivors (92.9 ± 19.3 vs. 120.4 ± 19.6 mmHg, P < 0.001).(Tables 4 and 5).

This study analyzed the clinical characteristics, microbiological features, and prognostic factors of 119 patients with NF. We found that NF predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly males, with the lower extremities being the most common site of infection. Diabetes-related wound infections were identified as the primary etiology, with nearly half of the cases attributed to diabetic foot infections, highlighting the critical role of diabetic foot in the pathogenesis of NF. Diabetic patients, due to neuropathy, impaired tissue perfusion, and immune dysfunction, are prone to rapid inflammation and tissue necrosis. Additionally, the prevalence of immunosuppression and chronic diseases in elderly individuals further elevates the risk of amputation14,15. These findings align with previous studies16–18and provide a foundation for targeted prevention and intervention strategies in high-risk populations.

NF is categorized into four types based on the causative pathogens. Type I (polymicrobial) accounts for 55%-90% of cases, while Type II (monomicrobial) represents 10%-15%. Types III and IV, involving rare specific pathogens, are less common19. In our study, Type II infections predominated at 47.1%, emphasizing the prevalence of monomicrobial infections as the primary form. This aligns with reports by RAHIM et al.20, which noted a rising incidence of Type II NF, with some studies, such as John Magala et al.21, documenting Type II prevalence as high as 91%.

Our findings revealed that Gram-negative bacteria accounted for 62.7% of NF cases, differing from other published reports18,22,23. Among Gram-positive isolates, Staphylococcus aureus was the most common pathogen, while Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae dominated among Gram-negative isolates. These results align with the findings of Tark K et al.24. Despite regional variations in predominant strains, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa being more frequently isolated in Sanjiv R et al.’s study25, E. coli and K. pneumoniae remain the most common Gram-negative pathogens, whereas S. aureus and Streptococcus spp. are predominant among Gram-positive bacteria18,23,24.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that Gram-negative bacteria demonstrated over 90% sensitivity to ertapenem, imipenem, piperacillin-tazobactam, and amikacin, while Gram-positive bacteria exhibited similarly high sensitivity to vancomycin and levofloxacin. Based on these results, we recommend vancomycin or levofloxacin combined with carbapenems or piperacillin-tazobactam as the initial empirical treatment for NF. In tertiary hospitals with adequate pharmaceutical supply, we recommend carbapenems combined with vancomycin as the preferred empirical regimen. In contrast, in primary care or resource-limited settings, piperacillin-tazobactam combined with levofloxacin may serve as a cost-effective and accessible alternative while still ensuring broad antimicrobial coverage. This recommendation is consistent with guidelines issued by the Korean Society of Infectious Diseases, which advocate glycopeptides combined with piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenems as the empirical therapy for NF26.

Although antibiotic resistance is an increasing concern and some hospitals implement antimicrobial stewardship systems to manage it27, early consideration of resistance in NF is unnecessary. In the initial phase, prompt and potent broad-spectrum antibiotics are paramount. Resistance should be addressed during the wound healing phase, after infection control is achieved. Prompt and effective antimicrobial treatment is crucial to reducing adverse outcomes in NF patients. Adhering to this empirical treatment strategy before pathogen identification is expected to improve treatment success rates, reduce complications, and enhance clinical outcomes.

We identified some clinically significant indicators while analyzing the risk factors associated with amputation in patients with NF. We observed that the skin appearance and muscle necrosis in NF were significantly associated with amputation. Wang et al. developed a clinical staging system for NF based on skin appearance, categorizing it into three stages: Stage I (erythema and pain), Stage II (blistering), and Stage III (skin necrosis)5. Our study revealed that the amputation rate was significantly elevated during the skin necrosis stage. Additionally, Natthaya C. developed an NF amputation prediction model utilizing four clinical predictive factors, including wound appearance28. Pathological examinations further identified thrombosis in small and medium-sized vessels as a cause of tissue gangrene29. Moreover, we found that intraoperative detection of muscle necrosis was strongly linked to elevated amputation rates, suggesting that the depth of infection is a critical determinant of outcomes. Given that creatine kinase and lactate dehydrogenase are common biomarkers for muscle injury30, monitoring these indicators could enable earlier detection of muscle lesions and support timely clinical intervention.

Prolonged PT and APTT, along with decreased ALB levels, are closely associated with amputation and mortality in NF patients. Prolonged PT and APTT reflect coagulation dysfunction, increase bleeding risk, and poor prognosis, and are recognized as independent prognostic factors for severe diseases, including NF31,32. Hypoalbuminemia, caused by the inflammatory state through increased extravasation of serum albumin and plasma solutes, reflects the severity of inflammation and the progression of critical illnesses33. Therefore, treatment should prioritize addressing the underlying inflammatory causes rather than focusing solely on nutritional support.

Additionally, we identified advanced age, comorbid coronary artery disease, elevated lactate levels, hypotension, and positive blood cultures as factors closely associated with mortality in NF patients. Karnuta J. et al. reported nearly identical conclusions in their study31. Lactate, a byproduct of anaerobic metabolism, has been consistently linked to mortality in NF, with persistent elevation indicating disease progression31,34,35. Hypotension reflects circulatory failure and inadequate organ perfusion, while positive blood cultures suggest systemic infection and sepsis, both of which increase mortality risk. A study on patients infected with Streptococcus suis found that infectious shock is an independent risk factor for mortality36. These factors highlight the progression of NF to advanced stages and serve as critical prognostic indicators. Clinically, proactive interventions are essential to prevent NF from reaching these critical stages.

The LRINEC was developed in 2004 to address the challenge of early diagnosis of NF, initially demonstrating good diagnostic performance with a positive predictive value6. Subsequent studies further validated its diagnostic capability37–39. However, some research has highlighted limitations, particularly in diagnosing NF cases with lower scores8. Despite these limitations and the advent of newer diagnostic models, the LRINEC remains the most extensively studied and widely applied tool in clinical practice37–40. In our study, a LRINEC score of ≥ 6 yielded a PPV of 75.6%, indicating its continued utility in aiding early diagnosis. Furthermore, among the 29 cases with LRINEC scores < 6, only 3 resulted in mortality and none required amputation, reflecting an 89.7% survival rate and a 100% limb salvage rate. While the LRINEC ‘s PPV for NF diagnosis may not be exceptional, these findings suggest it holds potential prognostic value. Univariate analysis revealed that several indicators within the LRINEC were significantly associated with amputation in NF patients. ROC curve analysis further confirmed that scores > 8 were predictive of higher amputation risk. Clinicians should consider these findings and discuss potential limb loss risks with patients, a conclusion corroborated by prior studies.16,41

However, studies linking the LRINEC to mortality in NF are limited, and some have reported no association. For instance, Karnuta J. et al. found that the LRINEC poorly predicted mortality, with an ROC AUC of 0.56 (95% CI 0.45–0.67)31, consistent with our study findings. This discrepancy may be explained by immunosuppression in advanced infection stages, where excessive white blood cell consumption, increased apoptosis, and bone marrow suppression lead to reductions in white blood cell count and CRP levels42,43. Fluctuations in inflammatory markers at different stages of infection may be one of the reasons for the poor predictive performance of LRINEC for mortality.

To effectively reduce the rates of amputation and mortality associated with NF, efficient disease management and prompt surgical intervention are essential. Delayed treatment, commonly observed in economically underdeveloped regions, significantly worsens outcomes. Our study found a median of 44 hours from diagnosis to the first surgery, with longer delays linked to higher risks of amputation and mortality. While not statistically significant, previous studies have underscored the principle that ‘time is life’ in NF treatment10,44. The surgical treatment of NF primarily focuses on initial debridement. While the process can be challenging, debridement and skin grafting are the most common approaches and typically do not require overly complex procedures. In this study, these methods resolved 90% of cases, except for those requiring amputation. Adhering to specific principles is crucial for optimal outcomes. Below is a summary of our hospital’s experience:


Timing of Surgery


Timing of Surgery

For stable patients, emergency surgery should be performed promptly in a sterile operating room. For critically ill patients, initial bedside fasciotomy and drainage in the ICU may be performed, followed by thorough debridement in the operating room once the condition stabilizes.


2.Management of Suspected Cases


Management of Suspected Cases

The “finger test” is useful for preliminary evaluation. If a small incision reveals “dishwater-like” fluid in the fascia and subcutaneous fat layers45, immediate surgical intervention is warranted. Early and proactive surgical management is recommended for all suspected NF cases to prevent severe complications from missed diagnoses.


3.Principles of Debridement


Principles of Debridement

During debridement, thorough drainage of wound cavities and spaces must be ensured. Thorough drainage of wound cavities and spaces is essential. Deep structures, such as the fascial layer and intermuscular spaces, should be fully exposed. Removal of most necrotic tissue is sufficient; complete clearance in a single procedure is not necessary.


4.Application of Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT)


Application of Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT)

Following initial debridement, NPWT can be routinely applied. However, due to severe infection and rapid necrosis, clogged drainage tubes and exacerbated infections are potential risks. Close monitoring of inflammation and clinical symptoms is crucial, with timely removal of NPWT or repeat debridement as needed.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design may limit the generalizability of the results and carries risks of incomplete data and bias. Second, with only 119 cases included, the low incidence of NF and limited sample size resulted in too few outcome events to perform multivariate regression analysis for identifying independent risk factors. Additionally, the absence of anaerobic culture media prevented the collection of data on anaerobic bacteria. Furthermore, limitations in imaging and patient conditions meant many cases lacked routine MRI or CT, preventing analysis of imaging’s role in NF. Future studies should adopt multicenter prospective cohort designs, expand sample sizes, and standardize treatment protocols to validate and further refine the conclusions of this study.

In conclusion, NF is a rare but rapidly progressing and life-threatening disease, with persistently high rates of amputation and mortality. This study underscores the critical importance of early diagnosis, timely debridement, and scientific management in improving NF prognosis. Clinicians should pay close attention to various clinical factors closely associated with adverse outcomes. Strengthening health education for high-risk populations, enhancing diagnostic and treatment capabilities of primary healthcare providers, and optimizing disease management can help reduce the incidence of NF and improve patient outcomes.
