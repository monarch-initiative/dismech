# A Twist1-regulated distal enhancer crucial for Alx1 gene expression and function during craniofacial development.
**Authors:** Huang Y, Iyyanar PPR, Xu J, Adhikari N, Wu Z, Lan Y, Jiang R
**Journal:** Dev Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ydbio.2026.03.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ydbio.2026.03.008)

## Content

Disruption of ALX1 causes frontonasal dysplasia syndrome-3, characterized by extreme microphthalmia, severe midfacial hypoplasia, and orofacial clefting. Recent studies have revealed critical tissue-specific roles of ALX1 in patterning both cranial mesoderm and cranial neural crest-derived facial mesenchyme. However, the molecular mechanisms regulating Alx1 gene expression during craniofacial development are largely unknown. In this study, we have identified a distal enhancer (Alx1-DE1) residing in a large intron of the neighboring Lrriq1 gene and demonstrate that deletion of this enhancer specifically affects Alx1 gene expression in the cranial neural crest-derived frontonasal mesenchyme and causes frontonasal and ocular defects partly phenocopying Alx1-deficient mice. We further functionally analyzed four evolutionary conserved regions, ECR1 - ECR4, in the Alx1-DE1 enhancer. Remarkably, ECR1, whose homologous region in the human genome harbors a lead single nucleotide variation significantly associated with facial and cranial vault shape differences, exhibits high enrichment of Twist1 transcription factor occupancy in mouse embryonic frontonasal tissues and drove Twist1-dependent reporter transgene expression specifically in the developing periocular and frontonasal mesenchyme in transgenic mice. These results reveal Alx1-DE1 as a crucial tissue-specific enhancer through which Twist1 and other major craniofacial developmental regulators control craniofacial patterning and morphogenesis.

Craniofacial malformations are associated with about one-third of all human congenital anomalies, with the most common facial phenotypes involving disruption of the midface and upper lip (Mai et al., 2019; Terrazas et al., 2017; Twigg and Wilkie, 2015). Among these, frontonasal dysplasia (FND) is a large class of craniofacial malformations characterized by ocular hypertelorism, midline clefting of the nose and/or upper lip and palate, and hypoplastic or cleft alae nasi (Farlie et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2019; Vargel et al., 2022). The frequent occurrence of these craniofacial anomalies reflects the central role of the embryonic frontonasal prominence (FNP) in craniofacial morphogenesis and the high sensitivity of the complex morphogenetic processes of craniofacial development to genetic and environmental perturbations (Farlie et al., 2016; Twigg and Wilkie, 2015). During mammalian embryogenesis, the face is constructed from five partly separate primordial tissue blocks formed around the stomodeum, with the FNP located rostrally and the paired maxillary and mandibular processes of the first pharyngeal arches forming the lateral and caudal sides of the oral cavity, respectively (Jiang et al., 2006). Each of the facial primordia is formed from migrating cranial neural crest cells (CNCCs) combined with cells of cranial mesoderm and the facial ectoderm, with the CNCC-derived craniofacial mesenchyme giving rise to all the facial skeletal structures including the frontal bones of the skull, nasal bridge and capsule, and cheek and jaw bones (Kontges and Lumsden, 1996; Minoux and Rijli, 2010; Noden and Francis-West, 2006). Previous studies have shown that regionally restricted expression of a combination of transcription factors, particularly members of the HOX and DLX families, plays crucial roles in specifying the regional identities of the CNCCs and determining the form and shape of the CNCC-derived facial skeletal structures (Creuzet et al., 2005; Depew et al., 2002; Hunt et al., 1991; Lappin et al., 2006). For example, Hoxa2 is expressed in the CNCCs populating the second and more posterior pharyngeal arches but not in the CNCCs populating the first pharyngeal arches and FNP (Creuzet et al., 2005). Genetic inactivation of Hoxa2 in mice caused duplication of mandibular arch CNCC-derived structures, including Meckel’s cartilage, in place of second arch derived structures whereas transgenic ectopic expression of Hoxa2 throughout of the anterior CNCCs resulted in complete absence of formation of frontonasal and jaw skeletal structures (Creuzet et al., 2005; Creuzet et al., 2002; Gendron-Maguire et al., 1993; Rijli et al., 1993). In the CNCCs populating the first pharyngeal arches, Dlx1 and Dlx2 are co-expressed in CNCCs in both maxillary and mandibular processes whereas Dlx5 and Dlx6 are only expressed in the mandibular process (Beverdam et al., 2002; Depew et al., 2002; Qiu et al., 1997). Mice with targeted disruption of both Dlx5 and Dlx6 exhibited homeotic transformation of the mandible into maxilla-like structures, whereas transgenic ectopic expression of Hand2, a direct target gene downstream of Dlx5/Dlx6, throughout the CNCCs caused transformation of maxilla into mandibular structures (Beverdam et al., 2002; Depew et al., 2002; Funato et al., 2016; Sato et al., 2008). These data indicate that proper regulation of the patterns of expression of region-specific transcription factors in the developing CNCCs are of paramount importance in craniofacial development. However, the molecular mechanisms regulating region-specific gene expression during frontonasal development are not well understood.

The CNCCs populating the embryonic FNP are HOX-negative and do not express the Dlx family genes, but members of the vertebrate aristaless-related subfamily of homeobox-containing genes, including Alx1, Alx3, and Alx4, exhibit highly specific enrichment in expression in the frontonasal CNCCs (Beverdam and Meijlink, 2001; Beverdam et al., 2002; Depew et al., 2002; Iyyanar et al., 2022; Qiu et al., 1997). Biallelic disruptions of each of the human ALX1, ALX3, and ALX4 genes have been associated with autosomal recessive FND syndromes, with ALX1-related FND3 (OMIM 613456) patients exhibiting the most severe craniofacial defects including severe frontonasal hypoplasia, bilateral facial clefting, and extreme microphthalmia, whereas the ALX3-related FND1 (OMIM 136760) and ALX4-related FND2 (OMIM 613451) patients showed distinctive mild-to-moderate frontonasal malformations (Farlie et al., 2016; Kayserili et al., 2009; Pini et al., 2020; Twigg et al., 2009; Uz et al., 2010). Genetic studies in mice have confirmed a predominant role of Alx1, with partial functional complementation by Alx3 and Alx4, in frontonasal development (Beverdam et al., 2001; Iyyanar et al., 2022; Qu et al., 1997; Qu et al., 1999; Zhao et al., 1996). We recently showed that Alx1−/− mutant mice recapitulate FND3 developmental defects including cleft lip and palate, disrupted nasal cartilages, and microphthalmia with optic nerve defect (Iyyanar et al., 2022). We found that Alx1 is transiently expressed in the early cranial mesoderm prior to its strong and highly specific activation in the frontonasal CNCCs (Iyyanar, 2026). Through generation and analysis of tissue-specific conditional Alx1 mutant mice, we demonstrated that Alx1 is required in the early cranial mesoderm for extraocular muscle (EOM) formation while Alx1 function in the CNCCs is critical for frontonasal morphogenesis (Iyyanar, 2026). Furthermore, we showed that Alx1−/− mouse embryos exhibited ectopic expression of jaw mesenchyme markers concomitant to loss of expression of frontonasal mesenchyme genes (Iyyanar et al., 2022), indicating that Alx1 plays a central role in specifying the frontonasal CNCC identity and patterning the mammalian midface.

Genetic studies in human and mice have shown that the Twist1 transcription factor also plays critical roles in frontonasal development. Mutations in TWIST1 have been associated with Sweeney-Cox (OMIM 617746) and Saethre-Chotzen (OMIM 101400) syndromes, with Sweeney-Cox syndrome characterized by hypertelorism with severe deficiency in frontal bones and hypoplasia of the nose and facial bones while Saethre-Chotzen syndrome exhibits craniosynostosis with hypertelorism and maxillary hypoplasia (el Ghouzzi et al., 1997; Howard et al., 1997; Kim et al., 2017). Whereas Twist1−/− mouse embryos die at mid-gestation, mice with neural crest lineage-specific inactivation of Twist1 resulted in dramatic disruption of frontonasal development accompanied by failure of activation of many ectomesenchyme genes, including Alx1, Alx3, and Alx4, in the FNP (Adhikari et al., 2025; Bildsoe et al., 2009; Chen and Behringer, 1995). Furthermore, Twist1 interacts cooperatively with specific homeodomain transcription factors, including Alx1 and Alx4, in CNCC development (Kim et al., 2024). In this study, we demonstrate that a Twist1-binding distal enhancer element upstream of Alx1 drove specific and Twist1-dependent expression in the embryonic frontonasal CNCCs. Moreover, we show that mice with targeted deletion of this Alx1 distal enhancer exhibit frontonasal dysplasia associated with significant loss of Alx1 mRNA expression in the developing FNP. These data indicate that Twist1-mediated direct regulation of Alx1 gene expression is a central part of the gene regulatory network controlling craniofacial morphogenesis.

Two chemically modified sgRNAs, Sg330 (target sequence 5’-CGTGATCTGATTAAGGGACT-3’) and Sg332 (target sequence 5’-GAGGTCAATACAGCCTTTTG-3’), target specific sequences flanking the Alx1-DE1 enhancer element were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA). A mixture of the sgRNAs and CAS9 protein (IDT, Catalog #1081061) at the concentration of 50:50:100 ng/μl was electroporated into C57BL/6J mouse zygotes. The treated zygotes were transferred into the oviductal ampulla of pseudopregnant CD1 female mice at ~25 eggs per recipient. Mice born were genotyped by PCR using specific primers DE1-F1 (5’-GAGTCTAAAGATGGAGTGACTTGC-3’) and DE1-R (5’-CACATTTTCCTATGATACAGTTCTTC-3’) to identify founder mice carrying deletion of the DE1 region. Mice heterozygous for two independent deletions of 4570 bp and 4673 bp, respectively, were established and sequence verified. Heterozygous Alx1ΔDE1/+ mice were maintained in the C57BL/6J background through crossing Alx1ΔDE1/+ male mice to C57BL/6J female mice. For timed mating, E0.5 was designated as the noon of the day on which a vaginal plug was identified.

The transgene construct was verified by Sanger sequencing. The Alx1-ECR1lacZ transgene construct was injected into pronuclei of single-cell stage FVB/NJ embryos and the injected embryos were transferred into the oviductal ampulla of pseudopregnant CD1 female mice at ~25 eggs per recipient. For analysis of LacZ reporter activity in F0 embryos, embryos were harvested at E10.5 and processed for whole mount X-gal staining as described previously (Iyyanar et al., 2023). To establish the Alx1-ECR1lacZtg/+ mice, the founder mice were genotyped by PCR using the primers Hsp68Z1 (5’-GTTCCATCCAGAGACAAGCGAAG-3’) and Hsp68Z2 (5’-CAAAGCGCCATTCGCCATTC-3’). Founder mice were crossed to CD-1 female mice, and embryos were collected from E8.5 to E11.5 in ice-cold PBS for X-gal staining assay.

Mouse embryonic palatal mesenchyme cells were used for cell culture and reporter assays. Culture and transfection conditions have been described previously (Adhikari et al., 2025). For all transfections, each well received a total of 1000 ng firefly luciferase reporter plasmid DNA together with 8 ng of pRL-TK Renilla luciferase control plasmid. The total amount of firefly reporter DNA was kept constant at 1000 ng per well by supplementing with the pGL3 vector as needed. For the negative control group, 1000 ng of the pGL3 vector was used. For experimental groups, reporter constructs of different sizes were transfected at amounts normalized to their plasmid length relative to pGL3, such that the molar amount of reporter plasmid was equivalent across conditions. Any remaining DNA required to reach a total of 1000 ng per well was supplemented with the pGL3 vector. Cells were harvested 48 hours after transfection and enhancer activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase reporter assay (Promega, #E1910) on a luminometer reader (Promega GLOMAX 96). Data were collected from 3 independent replicate assays and statistically analyzed by using One-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3.), with p value less than 0.05 considered significantly different.

Whole-mount in situ hybridization was carried out as previously described (Xu et al., 2019). For each probe analyzed, a minimum of three embryos of each genotype were analyzed, and only probes that detected consistent patterns of expression in all samples were considered as valid results.

Mouse embryos were harvested in ice-cold PBS on predetermined gestation days, and the heads were dissected, placed into RNALater (ThermoFisher AM7020), and stored at −80 °C until processing. Following genotyping, samples from littermates of the same genotype were pooled to collect RNAs using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). First-strand cDNAs were generated by reverse transcription of 500 ng of total RNAs for each pooled sample using the SuperScript III VILO kit (ThermoFisher 11754-050). RT-qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR system. The following primers were used to amplify Alx1 (5’-GAGCCCGCCAAGTAAAAACAG-3’ and 5’-GTCGCTTTGCCGTAAAAGGAC-3’), Lrriq1 (5’-GAGTTCAACTTGTGGCAAGGC-3’ and 5’-GAAGTCTTGCTGCCATT-3’), Hsp68-LacZ (5’-GCTGGCGTAATAGCGAAGAG-3’ and 5’-TCAGGAAGATCGCACTCCA-3’). Relative transcript levels were normalized to β-actin (PCR primers 5’-GAGAAGATCTGGCACCACACC-3’ and 5’-GCATACAGGGACAGCACAGC-3’). Three biological replicates were analyzed for each genotype, with three technical replicates per sample. The expression levels of mutant samples were calculated relative to wild-type controls (average set to 1). For statistical analysis, all results were presented as mean ± SEM. Student’s t test was used for pairwise comparison. One-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3) was used to compare all groups when more than two genotypes were included.

To identify cis-regulatory elements controlling Alx1 gene expression during craniofacial development, we re-analyzed previously published datasets on chromatin accessibility and histone modifications surrounding the mouse Alx1 locus. By scanning for open chromatin regions flanked by H3K27ac peaks, an active enhancer marker, in embryonic day 10.5 (E10.5) mouse FNP (Creyghton et al., 2010; Minoux et al., 2017), we identified several putative enhancer regions around the Alx1 locus. Among these, a prominent H3K27ac peak, designated as distal enhancer 1 (DE1), is located at approximately 83kb upstream of the Alx1 transcription start site (TSS) (Fig. 1A). A weaker H3K27ac peak was also detected approximately 105kb upstream of Alx1 TSS (Fig. 1A). Comparison with H3K27ac ChIP-seq datasets from E10.5 mouse heart (Gu et al., 2022) and forelimb tissues (Malkmus et al., 2021) suggests that DE1 is a tissue-specific enhancer active in the developing facial primordia (Fig. 1A).

To analyze if DE1 regulates Alx1 gene expression during craniofacial development, we generated two independent mouse lines carrying a deletion of 4570 bp and 4673 bp, respectively, spanning the DE1 region using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. Both lines exhibited identical phenotypes. The Alx1ΔDE1/+ heterozygous mice were viable, fertile, and exhibited no overt craniofacial abnormalities. Although the Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 pups were present at the Mendelian ratio (10 homozygotes among 38 pups born in five litters from heterozygous intercrosses) at two weeks of age, they exhibited shortened premaxilla (10 out of 10) and 70% of them (7 out of 10) exhibited malocclusion by one month of age (Fig. 1B–E). No malocclusion was observed in any of the wildtype or heterozygous littermates. We next examined whether the premaxillary defects resulted from disruption of frontonasal development. At E16.5, Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos exhibited hypoplastic and disrupted nasal cartilages (Fig. 2A, B), a phenotype similar to that previously reported in Alx1−/− mouse embryos (Iyyanar et al., 2022). Since Alx1−/− mice exhibit agenesis of extraocular muscles (EOMs) in addition to frontonasal defects (Iyyanar, 2026), we performed immunofluorescent staining to examine EOM organization in E16.5 Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos and control littermates. Frontal sections of E16.5 embryos showed that the EOM fibers were abnormally oriented and extended into the disrupted nasal cartilage regions (Fig. 2C, D) (n=3 for each genotype). Analysis of sagittal sections further confirmed that the Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos exhibited disruption of the orbital cartilage and disorganization of the EOMs (Fig. 2E, F) (n=3 for each genotype). These developmental defects in the Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos closely resemble the disrupted nasal cartilages and EOM disorganization recently reported in the neural crest lineage-specific Alx1 mutant (Alx1fl/fl; Wnt1-Cre) mice (Iyyanar, 2026).

Alx1 is expressed in the CNCCs populating the periocular region and in the medial and lateral nasal processes during early craniofacial development (Iyyanar et al., 2022). By whole mount in situ hybridization analysis, we found that Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos exhibited loss of Alx1 mRNA expression in the periocular region at E9.5, in comparison with their wildtype littermates (Fig. 3A, C) (n=3 embryos each genotype). Alx1ΔDE1/+ embryos also exhibited moderate reduction in Alx1 mRNA expression in the periocular region at E9.5, in comparison with the wildtype littermates (Fig. 3A, B). At E10.5, Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos exhibited dramatically reduced Alx1 mRNA expression in the lateral and medial nasal processes, in comparison with their wildtype littermates (Fig. 3D, F, G, I). On the other hand, deletion of DE1 had little effect on Alx1 expression in the cranial mesoderm at E7.75 (Supplementary Fig. S1). We further verified the effect of the DE1 deletion on the levels of Alx1 mRNA expression in the developing facial primordia by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3J). The Alx1 mRNA levels were reduced by more than 60% in the E9.5 Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos and reduced by more than 80% in the E10.5 Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos, compared with their wildtype littermates (n=3 embryos each genotype, p<0.01). Whereas the whole mount in situ hybridization assays detected moderately reduced Alx1 mRNA expression in the periocular and frontonasal tissues in the Alx1ΔDE1/+ embryos in comparison with the wildtype littermates (Fig. 3D, E, G, H), RT-qPCR assays did not detect statistically different Alx1 mRNA levels in the wildtype and Alx1ΔDE1/+ embryos (Fig. 3B). This discrepancy is likely due to dilution of the spatially restricted Alx1 mRNAs in the embryonic whole head tissue lysates. On the other hand, although the DE1 element is located in an intron of the Lrriq1 gene, Lrriq1 mRNA expression in the E9.5 and E10.5 facial tissues is weak and not significantly different in the wildtype, Alx1ΔDE1/+, and Alx1ΔDE1/ΔDE1 embryos (Fig. 3J). Altogether, these data indicate that DE1 is critical for driving endogenous Alx1 mRNA expression in the CNCC-derived periocular and frontonasal mesenchyme cells during early craniofacial development.

Comparative sequence alignment revealed that the DE1 region contains four highly conserved regions (ECR1 – ECR4) in mammalian genomes, with the ECR1 region having the strongest ATAC-seq and H3K27ac ChIP-seq peaks in E10.5 FNP tissues, whereas ECR4 also exhibited elevated chromatin accessibility (Fig. 4A–D). To characterize the functional elements within the DE1 enhancer, we tested enhancer activity of the entire DE1 region and the ECR subregions using transient transfection assays in a mouse embryonic palatal mesenchyme cell line derived from the anterior region of the E13.5 mouse palate. Whereas the control pGL4.23 vector containing a minimal promoter exhibited basal level of luciferase reporter activity about 5-fold over the promoter-less pGL3 vector, adding the whole DE1 region to upstream of the minimal promoter in the pGL4.23 vector increased luciferase reporter activity by more than 10-fold compared to the control pGL4.23 vector (Fig. 4E). Interestingly, subcloning just the ECR1 region upstream of the minimal promoter in the pGL4.23 vector resulted in significantly higher luciferase reporter activity than the DE1 construct. On the other hand, both the ECR4 region alone and the region containing ECR2/ECR3/ECR4 exhibited relatively low enhancer activity (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that the enhancer activity of DE1 is mostly driven by the ECR1 region.

We next analyzed if the ECR1 region could drive tissue-specific reporter gene expression mimicking patterns of endogenous Alx1 in vivo. We subcloned the ECR1 region upstream of the Hsp68 minimal promoter in the p2xINS-Hsp68-lacZ reporter vector (Vokes et al., 2007) (Fig. 5A). We found that the ECR1 region was able to drive specific LacZ reporter expression in the medial and lateral nasal regions in E10.5 F0 transgenic mouse embryos (Supplementary Fig. S2). To further analyze the spatiotemporal activity of ECR1-driven reporter gene expression, we generated Alx1-ECR1LacZtg transgenic mice. From three transgene-positive founder mice, one Alx1-ECR1LacZtg mouse line maintained consistent patterns of lacZ reporter expression in three generations of embryos examined. Whole mount X-gal staining of embryos from E8.5 to E11.5 revealed that lacZ expression in the Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+ embryos was first detectable specifically in the supraocular region at E9.5 (Fig. 5B). At E10.5 and E11.5, strong lacZ activity was specifically detected in supraocular region and in the medial nasal processes (Fig. 5C–F). Sectioning of the E10.5 Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+ embryos revealed that the lacZ activity was restricted to the CNCC-derived craniofacial mesenchyme in the supraocular region and in the lateral and medial nasal processes (Fig. 5G–I). Thus, the pattern of lacZ expression in the Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+ embryos closely matched with the previously reported pattern of endogenous Alx1 mRNA expression in the periocular and frontonasal CNCC-derived mesenchyme (Iyyanar et al., 2022).

We recently showed that the Twist1fl/fl; Sox10-Cre embryos, with lineage-specific inactivation of Twist1 in migrating neural crest cells, exhibited dramatic loss of Alx1 mRNA expression in the developing frontonasal CNCCs (Adhikari et al., 2025). Analysis of the ChIP-seq data on genome-wide Twist1 occupancy in E10.5 mouse FNP revealed a strong Twist1-binding peak colocalizing with the ECR1 region (Fig. 4B), suggesting that Twist1 may directly regulate Alx1 gene expression in the frontonasal CNCCs through the ECR1 enhancer. To test this hypothesis, we crossed the Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+ mice with Twist1fl/fl mice and subsequently generated Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+;Twist1fl/fl;Sox10-Cre mouse embryos to examine if ECR1-driven lacZ reporter gene expression in the frontonasal CNCCs required Twist1 function. As shown in Fig. 6, we found that the lacZ reporter expression in the developing periocular and frontonasal tissues from the Alx1-ECR1lacZtg allele requires Twist1 activity in a dose-dependent manner, with lacZ activity in the frontonasal tissues apparently reduced in the Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+;Twist1fl/+;Sox10-Cre embryos and dramatically lost in the Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+;Twist1fl/fl;Sox10-Cre embryos in comparison with the Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+ littermates (Fig. 6A–I). Quantitative analysis by RT-qPCR showed that lacZ mRNA levels were reduced by 50% in the Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+;Twist1fl/+;Sox10-Cre and by 80% in Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+;Twist1fl/fl;Sox10-Cre embryonic frontonasal tissues compared with the control littermates (Fig. 6J). RT-qPCR analysis also confirmed significant reduction in expression of the endogenous Alx1 mRNAs in the frontonasal tissues in the E10.5 Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+;Twist1fl/fl;Sox10-Cre embryos compared with the Alx1-ECR1LacZtg/+ littermates (Fig. 6J). These results indicate that the ECR1 enhancer is crucial for Twist1-mediated activation of Alx1 gene expression during frontonasal development.

Loss of ALX1 function causes FND3, characterized by bilateral cleft lip and palate, severe frontonasal hypoplasia, and extreme microphthalmia (Pini et al., 2020; Uz et al., 2010). A similarly severe craniofacial phenotype in the Burmese cats has also been associated with homozygous disruption in the coding region of the Alx1 gene (Lyons et al., 2016). We recently showed that mice lacking Alx1 function also exhibited similar craniofacial defects (Iyyanar et al., 2022), indicating that Alx1 plays an evolutionarily conserved critical role in frontonasal development. Previous studies have shown that Alx1 exhibits spatiotemporally restricted patterns of expression in the CNCCs populating the developing facial primordia in mouse and avian embryos (Beverdam and Meijlink, 2001; Iyyanar et al., 2022; McGonnell et al., 2011). In this study, we identified and functionally characterized a conserved distal enhancer that regulates Alx1 expression during craniofacial development. By integrating chromatin accessibility profiling, enhancer–reporter assays, and in vivo functional analyses through generation of enhancer-deletion mice, we demonstrate that Alx1-DE1 is critically required for activation of Alx1 expression in the CNCC-derived periocular and frontonasal mesenchyme. Transgenic mouse enhancer assays confirmed that the ECR1 region of Alx1-DE1 is sufficient to drive reporter gene expression in a spatiotemporal pattern partly recapitulating the pattern of endogenous Alx1 expression during frontonasal development. Furthermore, in line with the requirement of Twist1 function for activation of Alx1 gene expression in the frontonasal CNCCs (Adhikari et al., 2025; Soo et al., 2002), we found that Twist1 binds to the Alx1-ECR1 region in the E10.5 mouse embryonic FNP and that Twist1 is required for Alx1-ECR1lacZ reporter expression in a dose-dependent manner. Together, these data indicate that the Alx1-DE1 enhancer captures the core regulatory logic underlying transcriptional control of Alx1 expression during frontonasal development.

We recently showed that Alx1 is transiently expressed in the early cranial mesoderm and its function in the cranial mesoderm is necessary to specify EOM formation whereas tissue-specific inactivation of Alx1 in the neural crest lineage caused frontonasal hypoplasia, cleft palate, and disorganization of EOMs (Iyyanar, 2026). The Alx1DE1/DE1 mutant mice recapitulated the frontonasal hypoplasia (including disruptions in nasal and orbital cartilages) and disorganized EOM phenotypes, but not cleft palate, observed in the Alx1fl/fl;Wnt1-Cre mice (Iyyanar, 2026). Together with our data that Alx1DE1/DE1 embryos exhibited significant, but not complete, loss of Alx1 mRNA expression in the developing medial and lateral nasal processes (Fig. 3), these data indicate that the Alx1-DE1 region mediates much of the CNCC-specific input in the transcriptional regulation of Alx1 gene expression during frontonasal development whereas other cis-regulatory elements mediate the regulation of Alx1 expression in the cranial mesoderm and other tissues. Interestingly, whereas a 240kb haplotype encompassing the Alx1 gene is strongly associated with beak shape diversity across Darwin’s finch species and within the Geospiza fortis finch species (Lamichhaney et al., 2015), one of the lead single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified from meta-analysis of genome-wide association (GWAS) data on human facial shape variations is SNP rs11609649 (Feng et al., 2021; White et al., 2021), located in the middle of the conserved ECR1 region within Alx1-DE1 (Supplementary Fig. S3). This same SNP is also one of the most significant signals associated with forehead shape from meta-analysis of human cranial vault shape GWAS studies (Goovaerts et al., 2023). It is likely that the ALX1-ECR1 enhancer also plays a major role in regulating ALX1 gene expression during human frontonasal development and that the rs11609649 variant and/or its closely linked variants contribute to population-level facial diversity by modulating ALX1-ECR1 enhancer activity. Future studies could elucidate how specific variants modulate ALX1 expression dynamics during facial development.

Our data reveal that Twist1 directly regulates Alx1 gene expression through the Alx1-ECR1 enhancer. Whereas ECR1 exhibited spatiotemporal enhancer specificity in driving reporter transgene expression in the CNCC-derived frontonasal mesenchyme, Twist1 is expressed throughout the early CNCCs populating the FNP and pharyngeal arches (Adhikari et al., 2025; Soldatov et al., 2019). Thus, Twist1 alone could not account for the spatiotemporal specificity of the ECR1 enhancer. Twist1 is a member of the basic helix-loop-helix family of transcription factors and specifically binds to DNA sequences containing a single or double E-box (CANNTG) motif (Chang et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2024). Kim et al. recently showed that TWIST1 interacts with a subset of homeodomain-containing transcription factors, including ALX1, ALX4, MSX1, and PRRX1, to bind cooperatively to the 17-bp coordinator motif containing a E-box and a typical homeodomain recognition site (TAAT) separated by a 6-bp spacer to regulate chromatin accessibility and histone H3K27 acetylation at a large number of enhancer sites during in vitro CNCC differentiation from human pluripotent stem cells (Kim et al., 2024). Directly under the Twist1 ChIP-seq peak in the ECR1 region there is a coordinator-like sequence (Supplementary Fig. S3). The ECR1 sequence within 70bp of this coordinator motif also contains canonical binding motifs for TFAP2 and Six1/Six2 transcription factors (O’Brien et al., 2016; Seberg et al., 2017) (Supplementary Fig. S3). Previous studies have shown that Alx1 mRNA expression in the frontonasal CNCCs was significantly reduced in Tfap2afl/-;Tfap2bfll-;Wnt1-Cre and in Six1−/−Six2−/− embryos, respectively, in comparison with their control littermates (Liu et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2024). It is possible that Tfap2a/Tfap2b and Six1/Six2 transcription factors also regulate Alx1 gene expression through the ECR1 enhancer and contribute to the tissue-specificity and robustness of the enhancer activity. Our finding that the Alx1-ECR1 enhancer activity was significantly downregulated in the Twist1fl/+;Sox10-Cre embryos and almost completely lost in the Twist1fl/fl;Sox10-Cre embryos suggest that Twist1 functions to open the access of the Alx1-ECR1 enhancer to the other tissue-specific transcription factors, and that multiple tissue-specific transcription factors act cooperatively to regulate the level of Alx1 expression.
