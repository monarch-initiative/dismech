# Assessment and treatment of osteoporosis in a patient with a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by a RNU4-2 pathogenic variant (ReNU syndrome).
**Authors:** Holling T, von Kroge S, Hecher L, Amling M, Schinke T, Kutsche K, Oheim R
**Journal:** JBMR Plus (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1093/jbmrpl/ziaf084](https://doi.org/10.1093/jbmrpl/ziaf084)

## Content

A 16-yr-old male with a genetically undiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) was admitted to our outpatient clinic for skeletal assessment. DXA and HR-pQCT showed a severely reduced BMD and a pronounced reduction of trabecular and cortical bone mass. Lateral vertebral assessment identified multiple previously unrecognized vertebral fractures of the thoracic and lumbar spine. Laboratory tests indicated an activated bone turnover, which was confirmed by an increased number of osteoclasts and osteoblasts in an undecalcified tibia biopsy of the patient. Treatment of the severe osteoporosis was initiated with neridronate. Trio exome sequencing in the patient and healthy parents did not uncover a genetic cause of the disease. Importantly, however, targeted sequencing of the RNU4-2 gene, which encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA (a major component of the splicing machinery), identified a heterozygous causative variant in the patient. This led to the molecular diagnosis of ReNU syndrome. RNU4-2 pathogenic variants underlie a NDD with multisystemic involvement, including skeletal abnormalities. Therefore, this case not only underlines the relevance of osteologic assessment and therapy in individuals with NDDs, but also highlights the necessity of future research efforts to elucidate the bone pathologies in ReNU syndrome.

Caring for individuals with a neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) is generally challenging, as there are many risk factors that can potentially explain an osteoporotic phenotype, if present. ReNU syndrome, a newly established NDD associated with microcephaly, seizures, and short stature, is caused by heterozygous RNU4-2 pathogenic variants that are relatively common in NDD-affected individuals. RNU4-2 encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA, a critical component of the spliceosome.1–3 Although skeletal abnormalities have been reported in individuals with ReNU syndrome,1,2,4–10 the underlying bone cell pathologies still need to be defined in order to prevent fractures by specific treatment strategies. Here we describe our findings in a 16-yr-old male individual with ReNU syndrome and severe osteoporosis. Our case report demonstrates the high relevance not only of in-depth osteological assessment, but also of genetic testing to provide a molecular diagnosis of the NDD in affected individuals.

A 16-yr-old male with a NDD consisting of intellectual disability, microcephaly, and epilepsy (clinical characteristics of the NDD are provided in the supplemental information) was referred to our specialized bone clinic (National Bone Board) from the treating orthopedic surgeon due to progression of the knock knees (genu valgum) (Figure 1A), leading to an increasing gait disturbance, despite guided growth procedures in the past. Whereas minor hand and finger fractures and 1 proximal fractured humerus had been reported, lateral vertebral assessment identified multiple vertebral fractures of the thoracic and lumbar spine, which were not diagnosed previously (Figure 1B). BMD measurement using DXA revealed Z-scores in the osteoporotic range for all analyzed skeletal elements, that is, −3.9 (left hip), −4.9 (right hip), −4.7 (lumbar spine), and −3.0 (height-adjusted Z-score spine). The severe skeletal phenotype of the patient was also evident by HR-pQCT of the tibia, which demonstrated a remarkably low trabecular bone mass and cortical thickness, when compared to sex- and age-matched controls (Figure 1C). The quantification of serum and urine parameters indicated an activated bone turnover with bone formation and bone resorption markers in the upper reference range of age-related controls. Whereas parameters for calcium and phosphate were in the normal range, the patient had hyponatremia and low serum creatinine levels. In an attempt to elucidate the underlying cause of the severe bone pathology in the patient, we obtained an opportunistic proximal tibial bone biopsy during a planned eight-plate removal procedure. After undecalcified processing, we observed that the patient’s biopsy, which was compared to a proximal tibia biopsy from a skeletal-healthy donor, displayed pathologically enlarged areas of eroded surfaces and increased numbers of both osteoclasts and osteoblasts, when related to the bone surface (Figure 1D). Whereas these findings, together with the laboratory parameters in serum and urine, led us to begin anti-resorptive treatment (Figure 2A), the molecular diagnosis remained unknown at that time.

Since osteoporosis and recurrent fractures in individuals with NDD can be caused either by indirect effects on the skeleton or by defects intrinsic to bone cells, we sought to identify the underlying genetic cause in the patient. We performed trio exome sequencing using DNA samples from the patient and his healthy parents. Genetic studies were performed as approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hamburg Medical Chamber (PV7038-4438-BO-ff; Hamburg, Germany). We evaluated and interpreted rare variants with a minor allele frequency of <0.5% in all 20 000 protein-coding genes according to autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant (de novo), and X-linked inheritance patterns. We did not detect any (likely) pathogenic variant, which could explain the NDD and/or the osteoporosis in the patient (Table S1). As RNA encoding genes are not targeted by exome sequencing, we analyzed RNU4-2 in the patient’s DNA by Sanger sequencing (Supplemental Material and Methods) and detected the heterozygous single base insertion NR_003137.3:n.64_65insT, which is a highly recurrent RNU4-2 pathogenic variant.1,2,5,8,11 Segregation analysis in the patient’s parents revealed the absence of the RNU4-2 variant in mother and father (Figure 2B).

Given the severe osteoporotic phenotype of the patient, we initiated a bone-specific treatment before the molecular diagnosis was established. Anti-resorptive treatment with intravenous neridronate injections (2 mg per kg body weight, every 3 mo) was initiated, which was well tolerated by the patient without obvious side effects. The 3-yr treatment period started at 16 yr of age and was stopped after growth plate closure at the age of 19 yr. As assessed by DXA, we found that the BMD did increase initially and stabilized afterwards during treatment (Figure 2A). Importantly, there were no additional vertebral fractures. However, the patient experienced a patella fracture at the age of 19 yr following a fall on the left knee and a proximal humerus fracture 8 wk later. With respect to serum and urine parameters, we observed the expected reduction of bone turnover parameters, but sodium and creatine levels were still pathologically low (Figure 2C). A structured training program to improve muscle strength and coordination was not feasible for the patient, but mobility in general was promoted in a protected environment under supervision. Seizures were not fully controlled in the patient, despite intensive neurological care and various treatment approaches.

With the identification of the de novo RNU4-2 pathogenic variant n.64_65insT, we established the diagnosis of ReNU syndrome in the 16-yr-old patient and ended the long diagnostic odyssey. The n.64_65insT variant is the most common pathogenic variant in the analyzed cohorts of NDD-affected individuals and accounts for 75% of cases with ReNU syndrome.1,2,4–11 The patient reported here shows most of the clinical features that have been described for individuals with RNU4-2 pathogenic variants, such as intellectual disability, microcephaly, delayed speech and language development, seizures, hypotonia, and short stature, but also facial features and brain abnormalities (Table S2). Importantly, bone abnormalities have been described previously and were present in 72 out of 173 individuals (42%) with ReNU syndrome who underwent bone assessment, including the patient reported here. Specifically, 26% had fractures, 17% osteoporosis, 16% osteopenia, and 9% low BMD (Table S2). These data together with the recent report on an adult with RNU4-2 pathogenic variant and severe osteoporosis9 show the high prevalence of fractures and osteoporosis in individuals with this NDD. In the patient reported here, the severe bone phenotype was most evident with the use of HR-pQCT. We found a remarkable reduction of trabecular bone mass and cortical thickness, for which the respective values (bone volume per tissue volume and cortical thickness) reached only 25% and 22% of sex- and age-matched controls, respectively. Further insight into the bone pathology was gained by analysis of an iliac crest biopsy displaying pathologically enlarged areas of eroded surfaces and increased numbers of both osteoclasts and osteoblasts.

The severe impairment of skeletal integrity in patients with ReNU syndrome suggests a specific impact of the RNU4-2 variant on bone cell differentiation and/or function. RNA sequencing data generated from leukocytes of patients with a RNU4-2 pathogenic variant identified abnormal splicing events that are mainly caused by systematic changes in the usage of canonical 5′ splice sites.2,5 These data show the relevance of intact splice site recognition in leukocytes and suggest that alternative 5′ splice site usage in bone cells of individuals with ReNu syndrome negatively affects cell differentiation and/or function, leading to osteoporosis and fractures.

As exemplified in our case report, there is a special need to analyze patients with NDD for spinal fractures, for example, by lateral vertebral assessment, especially since the fractures may be asymptomatic and remain undiagnosed for a long time. To determine whether bone-specific treatment should be recommended, it is important to assess the skeletal status of these individuals, at least by DXA and in-depth serum and urine analysis. Osteological diagnostics and treatment are of crucial importance in order to enable early diagnosis of osteoporosis as a prerequisite for preventing further bone loss and the development of recurrent fractures. Since the vast majority of patients with NDD are children or adolescents, anti-resorptive treatment with intravenous bisphosphonates is currently the method of choice, as it is established for individuals in the same age groups diagnosed with other bone fragility disorders, such as osteogenesis imperfecta.12 However, with ongoing efforts to optimize bone-anabolic treatment options, alternative strategies may be available in the future that could potentially be applicable to NDD-affected individuals with osteoporosis. Importantly, such treatment decisions depend on a genetic diagnosis, as there may be distinct molecular causes for which specific treatment options may be preferable.
