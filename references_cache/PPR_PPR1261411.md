# Exploring the pathogenesis of traumatic brain injury and traumatic epilepsy based on bioinformatics analysis

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>  <bold>Background</bold>  :Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a well-established risk factor for the development of post-traumatic epilepsy (PTE). However, the molecular mechanisms linking TBI to PTE remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to explore the shared molecular signatures and underlying pathogenic mechanisms between TBI and PTE, thereby providing novel insights into the prevention and treatment of PTE.  <bold>Materials and Methods</bold>  :Publicly available transcriptomic datasets from patients with TBI and PTE were analyzed to identify common differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses, were performed to elucidate the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the shared DEGs. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were subsequently constructed to identify key hub genes. Candidate hub genes were further validated using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD). In addition, the expression levels of the identified core genes were experimentally validated in a controlled cortical impact (CCI)-induced mouse model of TBI.  <bold>Results</bold>  :Integrated bioinformatics analyses revealed significant transcriptomic similarities between TBI and PTE, highlighting shared molecular pathways and regulatory networks involved in disease progression. Among the identified hub genes, DUSP1 and EGR1 emerged as pivotal regulators, exhibiting significant differential expression across both TBI and PTE datasets. Experimental validation in the CCI-induced TBI mouse model further confirmed the dysregulated expression of these genes.  <bold>Conclusion</bold>  :Our findings demonstrate that DUSP1 and EGR1 represent key molecular links between TBI and PTE and may serve as promising therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of TBI-associated PTE. These results enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying epileptogenesis following TBI and provide a foundation for the development of novel targeted therapeutic strategies.  </p>

Introduction： 
Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major global cause of mortality and long-term 
disability, affecting more than 55 million individuals worldwide each year [1]. TBI 
induces immediate structural damage, including cerebral contusion, intracranial 
hemorrhage, and neuronal death, which subsequently initiates a cascade of secondary 
injury processes characterized by neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial 
dysfunction, and blood–brain barrier disruption [2–5]. These pathological events 
contribute to persistent neurological deficits and substantially increase the risk of 
chronic neurological complications.Post-traumatic epilepsy (PTE) is one of the most 
severe and debilitating long-term consequences of TBI, occurring in approximately 
20–50% of patients following moderate-to-severe brain injury [6]. PTE is 
characterized by recurrent unprovoked seizures that arise secondary to 
trauma-induced epileptogenic changes within the brain. Despite advances in 
antiepileptic therapies, nearly 30% of patients with PTE develop drug-resistant 
epilepsy, resulting in progressive cognitive decline, functional impairment, and 
psychological distress [7,8]. Consequently, PTE imposes a considerable socioeconomic 
burden on patients, caregivers, and healthcare systems.Accumulating evidence has 
established a close pathological association between TBI and PTE. Mechanisms 

 
 3 
implicated in post-traumatic epileptogenesis include neuronal hyperexcitability, 
synaptic reorganization, glial activation, chronic neuroinflammation, and disruption of 
the excitatory–inhibitory balance within neural circuits [9,10]. However, most previous 
investigations have focused on either TBI or PTE independently, limiting our 
understanding of the molecular mechanisms that drive the transition from traumatic 
injury to chronic epilepsy. In particular, the shared gene regulatory networks, 
signaling pathways, and molecular mediators linking these two conditions remain 
incompletely characterized. Furthermore, comprehensive studies exploring the 
common pathogenic mechanisms of TBI and PTE from a systems biology perspective 
are still scarce.Recent advances in bioinformatics and high-throughput sequencing 
technologies have enabled the integration of multi-omics data, including genomic, 
transcriptomic, and proteomic information, thereby providing powerful tools for 
identifying key molecular regulators and disease-associated pathways [11]. Through 
the analysis of publicly available datasets, common differentially expressed genes 
(DEGs) associated with both TBI and PTE can be identified and further characterized 
using functional enrichment analyses, pathway mapping, and protein–protein 
interaction (PPI) network construction. Such integrative approaches facilitate the 
systematic exploration of shared molecular mechanisms and may reveal critical 
biological processes involved in the pathogenesis of both disorders.Among the 
candidate molecular regulators, Dual Specificity Phosphatase 1 (DUSP1) and Early 
Growth Response 1 (EGR1) have attracted increasing attention because of their 
important roles in neuroinflammation, neuronal plasticity, neural repair, and 
epileptogenesis [12]. DUSP1 functions as a negative regulator of mitogen-activated 
protein kinase (MAPK) signaling and has been shown to attenuate neuroinflammatory 
responses and promote neuroprotection following TBI [13,14]. By suppressing excessive 
inflammatory activation, DUSP1 may reduce secondary neuronal injury and 
potentially influence the development of PTE. EGR1, an immediate-early 
transcription factor involved in neuronal activity-dependent gene regulation, plays a 
crucial role in neuronal plasticity, injury responses, and neural circuit remodeling [15]. 
Previous studies have demonstrated that EGR1 expression is rapidly induced after 

 
 4 
TBI and contributes to neurorepair through the regulation of neurotrophic signaling 
pathways [16,17]. Nevertheless, aberrant or sustained EGR1 activation may alter 
neuronal excitability and facilitate epileptogenic network remodeling, thereby 
promoting seizure susceptibility [18]. Collectively, these findings suggest that DUSP1 
and EGR1 may represent key molecular nodes at the intersection of post-traumatic 
recovery and epileptogenesis. 
In the present study, we employed an integrated bioinformatics strategy to 
systematically investigate the shared molecular mechanisms underlying TBI and PTE. 
By identifying common DEGs, characterizing enriched biological functions and 
signaling pathways, and constructing regulatory interaction networks, we sought to 
elucidate the molecular events that contribute to the progression from TBI to PTE. 
Particular emphasis was placed on the identification and functional characterization of 
hub genes, including DUSP1 and EGR1, to clarify their potential roles in the TBI–
PTE continuum. The findings of this study may provide novel insights into the 
molecular basis of post-traumatic epileptogenesis and offer potential therapeutic 
targets and biomarkers for the prevention and treatment of PTE. 
Materials and Methods 
Data Acquisition and Processing 
Gene expression datasets pertaining to traumatic brain injury (TBI) and 
post-traumatic epilepsy (PTE) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus 
(GEO) database by searching the keywords "Traumatic brain injury" and 
"Post-Traumatic Epilepsy." Two relevant datasets, GSE242025 (Siddiqui N et al., 
2022) [19] and GSE271985 (Lee Y et al., 2024; Lefton M et al., 2025) [20], were 
selected for subsequent analysis. Quality control and batch effect correction were 
performed using principal component analysis (PCA) and the ComBat function within 
the sva R package (version 3.52.0). Subsequently, differentially expressed genes 
(DEGs) were identified via the limma R package (version 3.60.4), applying thresholds 
of p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1. The overall analytical workflow is 
schematically summarized in Figure 1.

Identification of Crosstalk Genes 
To identify molecular intersections between TBI and PTE, differentially 
expressed genes (DEGs) were first extracted from the GSE242025 and GSE271985 
datasets using the limma R package. Subsequently, an online Venn analysis tool was 
employed to delineate the overlapping DEGs between both conditions. Genes 
exhibiting consistent differential expression across the two datasets were defined as 
crosstalk genes for further investigation. 
Functional and Pathway Enrichment Analysis 
The biological relevance of the identified crosstalk genes was examined through 
Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) 
pathway enrichment analyses. These analyses aimed to elucidate the involvement of 
these genes in disease-associated biological processes and signaling cascades. 
Statistical significance was assessed using false discovery rate (FDR) correction, with 
an adjusted p-value < 0.05 considered significant. 
Protein–Protein Interaction Network and Hub Gene Identification 
A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING 
database (https://string-db.org/) based on the common DEGs. Topological parameters, 
including degree, closeness centrality, and betweenness centrality, were calculated to 
identify hub genes and key functional modules within the network. 
Validation Using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database 
To corroborate the relevance of the identified hub genes in traumatic epilepsy, 
their expression and disease associations were further interrogated using the 
Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD). This resource facilitated the 
validation of gene–disease relationships and provided mechanistic insights into the 
potential roles of these genes in TBI and PTE progression. 
Single-Cell Transcriptomic Analysis 

 
 6 
Single-cell RNA sequencing data from TBI (GSE290150) and PTE (GSE274303) 
datasets were processed and integrated using the Seurat package (v5.1.0). Following 
quality control and normalization, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) 
was applied to visualize cellular heterogeneity. Clustering was performed at a 
resolution of 0.4 after evaluating stability and marker gene expression. Cell subtypes 
were annotated with the GPTCelltype R package (v1.0.1), and subtype-specific 
marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function. 
Animal Model of Traumatic Brain Injury 
Adult male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were maintained under specific 
pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature, humidity, and a 12-hour 
light/dark cycle. After anesthesia with isoflurane (2% induction, 1.5% maintenance), a 
moderate TBI was induced using the Feeney weight-drop method. Briefly, a 4 mm 
craniotomy was performed 2 mm lateral to the midline and 3 mm posterior to the 
bregma. A 40 g weight was dropped from a height of 7.5 cm onto the exposed dura. 
Mice were randomly assigned to Sham (n=6) or TBI (n=6) groups. 
Animal Behavioural Assessment 
Rotarod Test: Prior to the initial test, mice underwent a three-day training period 
on the rotarod apparatus . During the test, the rotarod speed was uniformly increased 
from 4 rpm to 40 rpm over a period of 3 minutes, and the time taken for the mouse to 
fall from the rotating rod was recorded. 
Balance Beam Test: Testing was conducted on a narrow balance beam 
(approximately 80 cm long, 1.5 cm wide, and 100 cm above the ground), with the 
mice’s motor coordination quantified by counting the time taken to traverse the beam. 
Both the balance beam and rotarod tests involved a three-day training period for the 
mice prior to modelling. Following traumatic brain injury (TBI) modelling, 
behavioural assessments were conducted on day 7. 
HE staining   

 
 7 
On day 7 following TBI, brain tissue from mice in each group was subjected to 
H&E staining, with healthy mice serving as the control group 
Immunofluorescence Staining 
Following transcardial perfusion with PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde, brains 
were post-fixed, dehydrated in sucrose gradients (20% and 30%), and sectioned at 25 
μm thickness. Sections were blocked with 10% donkey serum, then incubated 
overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Iba1 (abcam ab283346), DUSP1 
(Bioss bsm-52770R，), and EGR1 (Bioss bs-23707R). After washing, fluorescent 
secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at room temperature. The concentrations 
of primary and secondary antibodies were 1:800 and 1:1000, respectively.Nuclei were 
counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using fluorescence microscopy, and 
fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software, normalized to the Sham 
group. 
Statistical Analysis 
All statistical analyses were conducted using R and GraphPad Prism (v3.10.1). 
Data distribution and variance homogeneity were assessed to select appropriate tests: 
unpaired t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons, and one-way 
ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test for multi-group analyses. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 
was considered statistically significant.

Discussion 
The dysregulated expression of DUSP1 and EGR1 in both traumatic brain injury 
(TBI) and post-traumatic epilepsy (PTE) suggests their potential involvement in the 
shared pathological continuum linking these two conditions [21]. Increasing evidence 
indicates that TBI is not merely an acute neurological insult but often progresses into 
a chronic disease state that contributes to epileptogenesis and long-term neurological 
dysfunction [22,23]. The concurrent dysregulation of DUSP1 and EGR1 in both TBI and 
PTE may therefore provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms 
underlying this pathological transition. Notably, single-cell analysis of the CTD 
dataset revealed that both genes were predominantly expressed in microglia. This 
observation was further validated by immunofluorescence staining in a murine TBI 
model, which demonstrated significant cortical upregulation of DUSP1 and EGR1 
accompanied by marked co-localization with IBA1-positive microglia. 
DUSP1 as a Regulator of Neuroinflammation and Neural Repair 
DUSP1 is a dual-specificity phosphatase that negatively regulates the 
mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway through 
dephosphorylation, thereby preventing excessive pathway activation. This regulatory 
function contributes to the attenuation of neuroinflammation, reduction of cellular 
injury, and promotion of tissue repair following brain damage. Previous studies have 
shown that trauma-induced upregulation of DUSP1 suppresses the production of 
pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibits the activation of microglia and astrocytes, 
thereby mitigating neuronal damage and facilitating neurorepair [24]. Given the critical 
role of the MAPK signaling cascade in cellular proliferation, differentiation, and 
stress responses [25], DUSP1 serves as an essential negative regulator that helps 
maintain neural homeostasis and limits secondary injury.Following TBI, DUSP1 
expression is significantly elevated, further highlighting its neuroprotective role. 
Hyperactivation of the MAPK pathway after injury is known to exacerbate 
neuroinflammatory responses and neuronal loss. However, DUSP1-mediated 
dephosphorylation effectively restrains aberrant MAPK signaling, thereby 
suppressing downstream inflammatory cascades [26,27]. Specifically, increased DUSP1 

 
 18 
expression modulates trauma-induced neuroinflammation, reduces the release of 
inflammatory mediators, and attenuates glial activation. Collectively, these effects 
slow neuronal degeneration and promote neurological recovery. Consequently, 
DUSP1 is widely recognized as a key neuroprotective factor that preserves neuronal 
integrity and supports functional restoration following TBI. 
EGR1 in Neuronal Plasticity and Epileptogenesis 
EGR1 (early growth response 1) is an immediate-early gene and transcription 
factor that plays a pivotal role in neuronal development, injury responses, and 
synaptic plasticity. As a rapidly inducible transcriptional regulator, EGR1 responds to 
both physiological and pathological stimuli by controlling the expression of genes 
involved in cell survival, differentiation, and tissue repair [28]. In addition to its role in 
neuronal regeneration, EGR1 contributes to the maintenance of neural homeostasis 
through the modulation of neuroinflammatory processes.Following TBI, EGR1 
expression is markedly increased, suggesting a close association with repair and 
regenerative mechanisms. In response to neuronal injury and cell death, EGR1 
promotes the expression of neurotrophic factors, including brain-derived neurotrophic 
factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF), both of which are essential for 
neuronal repair and neurogenesis [29]. These neurotrophic factors support neuronal 
survival, synapse formation, and circuit reconstruction, thereby facilitating adaptive 
responses to traumatic injury.Moreover, EGR1 has been implicated in neural network 
remodeling during post-injury recovery [30]. By regulating neuronal excitability and 
promoting adaptive reorganization of neural circuits, EGR1 contributes to the 
restoration of functional connectivity within the injured brain [31]. This remodeling 
capacity is particularly important during the recovery phase, as it helps re-establish 
the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signaling required for normal neural 
network function. However, persistent or excessive EGR1 activation may also 
contribute to maladaptive synaptic reorganization and neuronal hyperexcitability, 
processes that have been closely associated with epileptogenesis. Therefore, EGR1 
may play a dual role in the post-traumatic brain, supporting neural repair while 
simultaneously participating in mechanisms that increase susceptibility to PTE. 

 
 19 
Dual-Edged Role of EGR1 in Epileptogenesis 
Despite its established role in neuronal repair and plasticity, EGR1 may also 
contribute to epileptogenesis when its expression is sustained or excessively elevated. 
By modulating neuronal excitability and neural network reorganization, aberrant 
EGR1 activation can reduce seizure thresholds and promote the development of 
hyperexcitable neuronal circuits. Furthermore, excessive EGR1 expression has been 
associated with neuronal apoptosis and functional deterioration, potentially 
aggravating epileptic pathology and contributing to adverse neurological outcomes. 
These findings suggest that EGR1 exerts a dual role following brain injury, 
functioning as both a mediator of neural repair and a potential driver of epileptogenic 
processes under pathological conditions. 
Integrated Co-Expression in Microglia and Pathophysiological Implications 
Both single‑cell transcriptomics and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed  
that DUSP1 and EGR1 are co‑expressed predominantly in microglia following TBI. 
This coordinated expression pattern implies that the two genes may function 
synergistically within microglia to regulate neuroinflammatory and repair processes 
relevant to both TBI and PTE. Although existing studies have partially elucidated 
their roles, the precise molecular interplay between DUSP1 and EGR1—and their net 
effect on disease progression—remains to be fully defined. Future investigations 
should focus on their interaction mechanisms, their combined influence on neuronal 
repair and network remodeling, and their potential as dual‑target therapeutic strategies. 
Collectively, the dynamic expression profiles of DUSP1 and EGR1 offer valuable 
biomarker insights and mechanistic clues linking acute brain injury to chronic 
epileptogenesis. A limitation of this study is the lack of functional validation due to 
constraints in both sample size and experimental resources. Specifically, budgetary 
and technical limitations precluded in vivo loss-of-function experiments (e.g., gene 
knockout or knockdown of DUSP1 and EGR1) to assess their roles in the progression 
from traumatic brain injury (TBI) to post-traumatic epilepsy (PTE). Consequently, the 

 
 20 
conclusiveness and generalizability of our findings are somewhat constrained. Future 
studies addressing these functional aspects are warranted to further elucidate the 
mechanistic roles of these two genes.   
 
Conclusion 
We analyzed transcriptomic data from TBI and PTE patients, identifying 
common DEGs and hub genes shared by both conditions, and performed a series of 
downstream analyses. Our findings suggest that DUSP1 and EGR1 may play critical 
regulatory roles in this process, potentially acting in synergy to modulate 
neuroinflammation, tissue repair, and neural plasticity, thereby conferring 
neuroprotection against TBI-induced injury and reducing the risk of PTE development. 
These insights offer a novel perspective that may inform the prevention and treatment 
of TBI-related PTE in the future.
