# Overexpression of FoxO3a in the dentate gyrus alleviates CUS-induced anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and cognitive impairment.
**Authors:** Yi S, Yang B, Zhang X, Zhao B, Wei L, Yao Z, Zhang R
**Journal:** Behav Brain Funct (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12993-026-00319-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12993-026-00319-z)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Excessive stress leads to injury and dysfunction, but the underlying mechanism remains unclear. As a human longevity gene, forkhead box O3a (FoxO3a) is a transcription factor that regulates various cellular processes, including the response to oxidative stress, apoptosis, and autophagy. This study aims to explore whether FoxO3a in the dentate gyrus (DG) of the hippocampus is involved in the formation of anxiety- and depressive-like behavior and cognitive impairment in stressed rats and to investigate the detailed mechanism.

METHODS: This study was conducted using the 6-week chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) model. Before the stress treatment, we injected an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector to overexpress FoxO3a specifically in the DG. Following the 6-week CUS treatment, a series of behavioral tests was conducted. Depression-like behavior was assessed using the sucrose preference test (SPT) and the open field test (OFT). The state of desperation was assessed with the forced swim test (FST) and tail suspension test (TST). Anxiety-like behavior was measured in the elevated plus maze (EPM) and OFT. Cognitive function was examined using the Y-maze test (Y-maze), novel object recognition test (NORT), and Morris water maze test (MWM). The level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) were measured. The levels of inflammatory factors were detected by ELISA. Pathological injury in DG was observed using thionine staining. The expression levels of FoxO3a, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95), synaptophysin (SYN), and proliferation marker Ki67 (Ki67) were determined using western blot.

RESULTS: CUS leads to various abnormal changes, including anxiety- and depressive-like behavior, cognitive impairment, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, neuropathological alterations in the DG, and decreased expression of FoxO3a, BDNF, PSD95, SYN, and Ki67. All these abnormal changes were significantly alleviated by targeted AAV-FoxO3a injection in the DG.

CONCLUSIONS: In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the downregulation of FoxO3a induced by CUS in the DG triggers oxidative stress and inflammatory response, inhibits cell proliferation, and induces abnormal synaptic plasticity, ultimately leading to anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors and cognitive impairment.

As a universal biological phenomenon, stress is an important self-regulation mechanism [1]. Moderate stress can lead to an adaptive response in the organism and form a superior environmental adaptability [2]. However, when overly strong or persistent stress stimulation exceeds the regulatory capacity of the body, it can induce body injury and functional disorders [3]. Recent researches indicate that stress, especially chronic stress, is a significant environmental factor contributing to the development of neuropsychiatric diseases in both humans and animals and is associated with anxiety, depression-like behavior, and cognitive dysfunction [4, 5]. Studies have shown that stress affects brain function via neuroendocrine, inflammation, and neuroplasticity, thereby leading to emotional and cognitive dysfunction [6–9]. However, the underlying neurobiological mechanism remains unclear, the precise molecular mechanism within critical brain regions that integrate the diverse stress signals remains poorly understood, and the efficacy of current medical treatment is limited. Therefore, deeply analyzing the occurrence mechanism of stress-related anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and cognitive dysfunction, and developing prevention and treatment methods are important issues faced by modern medicine.

As a core component of the limbic system, the hippocampus is not only responsible for memory formation and spatial navigation but also plays a key role in emotion regulation [10]. Structural and functional abnormalities of the hippocampus are closely related to anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and cognitive impairment [11]. Current researches have shown that chronic stress induces neuropathological alterations and reduces neurogenesis, especially in the hippocampus, which is also regarded as an important pathological basis for anxiety- and depressive-like behavior and cognitive dysfunction [12, 13]. Nonetheless, the mechanism within the hippocampus that integrates stress signals, leading to emotional and cognitive impairment, remains inadequately understood.

FoxO3a is closely related to human longevity [14]. As a key member of the FOXO transcription factor family, FoxO3a is a core regulator of cellular dynamic balance, stress response, and lifespan [15]. FoxO3a is involved in regulating a range of physiological and pathological processes, including apoptosis [16], proliferation [17], cell cycle progression [18], cell survival [19], and DNA damage repair [20]. The subcellular localization of FoxO3a has a significant impact on its activity and function [14]. Abnormal expression or function of FoxO3a can lead to various diseases, including cancer, and overexpression of FoxO3a in cells can inhibit tumor growth [21]. Given the multiple significant roles of FoxO3a in various life activities, we have reason to believe that FoxO3a plays an integrative role in stress signals. However, the role and detailed mechanism of FoxO3a in the stress-related physiological and pathological processes still need to be further explored.

Due to the unclear understanding of FoxO3a’s function in the stressed hippocampus, this study aims to elucidate its role. The present study established the rat model of chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) to simulate the diverse and unpredictable stressors that pose a risk to human health, and the adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector was used to overexpress FOXO3a in DG with a focus on investigating the mechanism of FoxO3a in DG in the formation of stress-related anxiety and depression-like behavior and cognitive dysfunction.

6-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (200 ± 20 g) were procured from Beijing Vital River laboratory animal company. The rats were housed under controlled conditions, which included a 12-hour light/dark cycle, an ambient temperature maintained at 21 ± 2 °C, and a relative humidity of 50 ± 5%, with ad libitum access to food and water. 80 rats were distributed according to chance into four groups: control (n = 20), 6 weeks of chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) (n = 20), CUS + adeno-associated viral-Empty (CUS + AAV-Empty) (n = 20), and CUS + AAV-FoxO3a (n = 20). The sample size was set to 20 rats per group, as determined by an a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1). To ensure independent and uncontaminated measurements for different endpoints, an independent groups design was employed. The 20 rats within each group were further randomly divided into three cohorts, as shown in Fig. 1A. Cohort 1 (n = 10): These rats underwent the full sequence of behavioral tests. After the tests, they were sacrificed, and their tissues were used to assess inflammatory response (n = 5) and oxidative stress (n = 5). Cohort 2 (n = 5): These rats were sacrificed specifically for Western Blot analysis without a behavioral test. Cohort 3 (n = 5): These rats were sacrificed specifically for Thionine staining without a behavioral test. This design prevents the potential confounding effects of behavioral stress on biochemical and morphological measures. All rats were housed in groups during the adaptation period and the AAV injection period, with 3 to 4 rats in each cage. All animals within a single cage belonged to the same group. To minimize the impact of social support on reducing chronic stress, all rats were housed singly at the beginning of CUS treatment. Furthermore, the cages were arranged on separate racks within the animal room to prevent any potential olfactory or auditory communication between rats from different groups. All experimental procedures were approved by the animal care and use committee of Henan Medical University (Permission No: XYLL-20230052).

AAV-Empty and AAV-FoxO3a were produced by HanBio (Shanghai, China) (Fig. S1). The CUS + AAV-FoxO3a group received the AAV vector expressing FoxO3a (GenBank: NM_ 001106395) (pHBAAV-CMV-FoxO3a-3flag-T2A-ZsGreen). The CUS + AAV-Empty group received the empty AAV vector (pHBAAV-CMV-3flag-T2A-ZsGreen) that contained all regulatory elements and the reporter gene but lacked the FoxO3a coding sequence. Rats were continuously anesthetized with 4% (v/v) isoflurane (792632, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany, in vehicle: O2) through breathing masks and fixed on a stereotactic brain device. After disinfection, the skin on the head was cut longitudinally in the middle, the periosteum was stripped off, and the position of the anterior fontanelle was determined. According to the stereotaxic atlas [22], AAV vectors were microinjected bilaterally into the dorsal DG (Anteroposterior (AP) = -3.8 mm, Mediolateral (ML) = ± 2.1 mm, Dorsoventral (DV) = -3.4 mm). After the virus injection, the coronal section showed that the injection site was on the dorsal side of the DG (Figure S2). After the skull was drilled open and the dura mater was punctured, the microsyringe was slowly sent to the selected position. The virus titer was 1 × 1012 particles/ml, the injection volume was 2.0 µL/side, and the injection process was completed within 10 min. After the injection was completed, the needle was retained for 10 min. Then the needle was removed, and the incision was sutured. Penicillin (200,000 U/kg, ST2555, Beyotime, Wuhan, China) and Streptomycin (260 mg/kg, Y271565, Beyotime, Wuhan, China) were used to prevent infection for 3 consecutive days. After regaining consciousness, rats were put back into the cages and allowed to recover for 7 days. Those without movement disorders underwent subsequent experiments. All the experimental procedures are shown in Fig. 1B.

The CUS plan was conducted with reference to a previous study [11]. CUS rats randomly received one of the following stimuli every day: (a) food deprivation (24 h), (b) water deprivation (24 h), (c) foot electrical stimulation (0.6 mA, lasting 1–2 s, with an interval of 10 s, 5 min each time), (d) overcrowding (12 h), (e) disruption of the light/dark cycle (24 h), (f) tilted cages 45° (24 h), (g) forced ice water swimming (4 °C, 5 min), (h) white flash stimulation (100 W, 1 flash/second, 12 h), (i) tail pinching (5 min), (j) restricted activity space (12 h). The types of stress stimuli received by CUS rats are shown in Fig. 1C. The stress period lasted for six weeks. Additionally, the same kind of stimulus did not occur continuously. High-intensity stress (foot electrical stimulation and forced ice water swimming) did not occur too frequently. During the stress treatment period, all rats were raised separately, and the feeding conditions of the control rats remained unchanged, while the feeding conditions of the CUS rats were changed according to random stimulation.

SPT was conducted with reference to a previous study [23]. During SPT, the rats were housed alone. On the first day, two bottles of 2% sucrose solution were prepared for each cage. On the second day, one bottle of 2% sucrose solution and one bottle of pure water were prepared for each cage. The bottles were replaced every 12 h to prevent habitual drinking. On the third day, the rats were given no water and food for 24 h. The formal experiment began on the fourth day. Each cage was equipped with one bottle of 2% sucrose solution and one bottle of pure water. After 12 h, the positions of the two bottles were swapped, and the liquid consumption was recorded after 24 h. The sucrose preference index = sucrose solution consumption/total liquid consumption.

FST was conducted with reference to a previous study [24]. The container is 50 cm in height and 20 cm in diameter. It is filled with clear water to a depth of 30 cm, and the temperature is maintained at 22–24 ℃. Pre-test: rats were placed in the container and allowed to swim freely for 5 min to adapt to the experimental environment. Formal test after 24 h: rats were placed in the container and allowed to swim freely for 6 min. The time when the rats stopped struggling during the last 5 min was recorded, that is, the time when only their heads remained above the water. The immobility duration was analyzed using the FST acquisition system (SMART video-tracking system, Panlab, Spain).

TST was conducted with reference to a previous study [25]. The height of the crossbar was set at 50 cm. The rat’s tail was fixed to the crossbar to ensure that the rat’s head was downward, and it couldn’t climb. The experiment lasted for 6 min. The immobility duration within the last 4 min was recorded, that is, the time when the rats stopped struggling. The immobility duration was analyzed using the TST acquisition system (SMART video-tracking system, Panlab, Spain).

EPM was conducted with reference to a previous study [11]. The elevated plus maze consists of two open arms (50 cm × 10 cm) and two closed arms (50 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm), and the device is 50 cm above the ground. During the test, rats were placed on the central square platform facing the open arm distal to the experimenter, and the observation time lasted for 5 min. The total distance in the EPM, open arm entries, and time in open arms were recorded by the EPM acquisition system (SMART video-tracking system, Panlab, Spain).

OFT was conducted with reference to a previous study [11]. The open field (1 m × 1 m × 0.4 m) was divided into sixteen equal-sized squares. Each rat was placed in the center of the open field equipment and allowed to explore freely for 5 min freely. The remaining squares were designated as the perimeter zone, and the four center squares were designated as the central zone. The total distance in the OFT, distance in the center, and time in the center were captured by the OFT acquisition system (SMART video-tracking system, Panlab, Spain).

The Y-maze was conducted with reference to a previous study [11]. The device consists of three arms (45 cm in length × 15 cm in width × 20 cm in height), and the angle between the three is 120°. The three arms were randomly divided into the new arm, the start arm, and the other arm. During the training period, the new arm was blocked by a baffle. Rats were placed in the starting arm and freely explored for 10 min. One hour later, the formal test was conducted. The baffle of the new arm was removed, and rats were placed in the starting arm, allowing the rats to move freely for 5 min. Entries in the novel arm and time in the novel arm were recorded by the Y-maze acquisition system (SMART video-tracking system, Panlab, Spain).

NORT was conducted with reference to a previous study [26]. NORT was conducted in the open field and consisted of adaptation, training, and testing sections, with an interval of 60 min. Adaptation stage: rats adapted to the open field for 5 min. Training stage: Two objects (4 cm × 4 cm × 4 cm) were placed in the open field and close to the same side, with a distance of 10 cm from the wall. The rats were placed on the opposite side with their backs to the objects and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Testing stage: one of the objects was replaced with an unfamiliar object of a different shape and color. The rats were placed at the same position as in the second phase and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The new object recognition acquisition system (Smart 2008, Germany) was adopted to record the time when rats were in contact with two objects respectively (the distance between the rats’ noses or mouths and the objects was less than 2 cm). The cognitive index (CI) was calculated based on the rats’ duration of exposure to the old and new objects: CI = N (time spent touching new objects)/N + F (time spent touching old objects) × 100%.

MWM was conducted with reference to a previous study [27]. The device consists of a water pool (150 cm in diameter and 60 cm in height), a blackout curtain, signs, and a constant temperature system. The pool was evenly divided into four quadrants. The platform was placed in one of the quadrants. The platform was 2 cm below the water surface, and the water temperature was 22–24 ℃. Adaptation stage: rats were put into the pool without the platform and allowed to swim freely for 1 min. Training stage: the platform was fixed in a certain quadrant of the pool and hidden under the water surface. Rats were placed into the pool successively from different starting points. Each training session lasted for 60 s. If the rats failed to find the platform within 60 s, the experimenter guided them to the platform and let them stay for 10 s. The training was conducted four times a day for five consecutive days until rats could quickly find the platform. Testing stage: the platform was removed, and the rats were placed in the pool from the fixed starting point and allowed to move freely for 60 s. The water maze acquisition system (SMART video-tracking system, Panlab, Spain) was used to record and analyze the movement information of rats.

Brain tissues were collected on the second day after the behavioral tests. Five rats were randomly selected from each group for perfusion treatment. 2% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, P3761, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA, in vehicle: 0.9% saline) was administered intraperitoneally to anesthetize the rats. After being fully perfused with pre-cooled 0.9% normal saline and 4% paraformaldehyde, the rats were decapitated, and their brains were removed, and then fixed at room temperature in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. After dehydration, transparency, waxing, and embedding, the brain was cut, and DG was obtained according to the stereotaxic atlas [22], with a slice thickness of 5 μm. Thionine staining was conducted with reference to a previous study [28]. Deparaffinized slices were immersed in a 4% thionine solution (Beyotime, Y267336, Shanghai, China, in the vehicle: ddH2O) at 60 °C for 90 s. The slices were then mounted with neutral gum (Beyotime, C0173) after being dehydrated by an ethanol series. The sections analyzed were chosen from the site of the bilateral virus injections (AP = −3.8 mm, ML = ± 2.1 mm, DV = −3.4 mm), AP ± 1 mm apart. Using systematic random sampling, one section was selected from every five consecutive sections, resulting in a total of five sections chosen for each rat. Finally, cell counting was performed by two investigators blinded to the experimental groups with Image J. Thionine staining marks neuronal cell bodies by labeling Nissl bodies (coarse endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes). In normal cells, Nissl bodies are uniform and clear, evenly distributed in the cytoplasm. However, in damaged cells, Nissl bodies are unevenly distributed and deeply stained, some neurons are pyknotic and dying, and Nissl bodies have even disappeared. Cells that were shrunken, hyperchromatic (dark-stained cells), or had a lack of nuclear detail were counted.

On the second day after behavioral tests, fresh brain tissues were harvested. 2% pentobarbital sodium was administered intraperitoneally to anesthetize the rats, and then the rats were quickly euthanized by decapitation, and their brains were removed. After washing in pre-cooled PBS (avoiding the nonspecific interference on subsequent analyses originating from the blood), the brains were sliced along coronal sections at the site of the bilateral virus injections (AP = −3.8 mm, ML = ± 2.1 mm, DV = −3.4 mm), AP ± 1 mm apart, according to the stereotaxic atlas [22]. To precisely extract the majority of MoDG, PoDG, and GrDG, we selectively dissected one-half of the medial tissue in the ventral hippocampus, because it is difficult to observe the characteristic “V”-shaped or “U”-shaped structure of DG on fresh tissue sections. The DG region was quickly dissected out from the hippocampus and immediately transferred the isolated DG to a pre-cooled centrifuge tube. Then the tissues were immediately stored at −80 °C for subsequent experiments.

ELISA was conducted with reference to a previous study [28]. ELISA complete kits (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) were used to measure the levels of transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1, D751002), interleukin-10 (IL-10, D731011), IL-1β (D731007), IL-2 (D731008), IL-6 (D731010), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, D731168). The DG was isolated and homogenized in pre-cooling PBS with protease inhibitor. Then, the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Each sample was tested three times, and the average value was taken. The standard curve was established using the standard substance. Biotinylated antibodies, HRP conjugates, substrate reagents, and stop solution were added in sequence according to the kit protocol. An ELISA reader was used to read the plates at 450 nm (Multiskan MS photometer type 352, Labsystems).

The level of ROS and activities of SOD, CAT, and GPx were measured to evaluate oxidative stress, as referred to a previous study [29]. The DG was isolated and homogenized in pre-cooling PBS. Then, the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was detected using the BCA protein assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime, Wuhan, China) and used to normalize the ROS levels. The supernatant was used for the following assays.

The ROS level in DG of rats was measured by 2,7-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA, 287810, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA, vehicle: methanol). 2 µl supernatant was mixed with 40 µl of 1.25 mM DCFDA in methanol. All samples were incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity was detected at excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 and 525 nm (ND3300LAPTOP, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA).

SOD activity was detected, as referred to the previous study [29]. The supernatant was mixed with EDTA (100 mM), riboflavin (2 mM), nitro blue tetrazolium (75 mM), methionine (13 mM), and sodium phosphate (50 mM, pH 7.8). The optic density at the wavelength of 560 nm (Multiskan MS photometer type 352, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) was recorded to analyze the SOD activity.

CAT activity (A007-1-1) and GPx (A005) activity detection kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). According to the instruction manual, the supernatant and reagents were added in sequence. The optic density at wavelengths of 405 nm and 412 nm (Multiskan MS photometer type 352, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) was recorded to analyze the CAT and GPx activity.

Western blot was conducted with reference to a previous study [28]. RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (P0013B, Beyotime, Wuhan, China) was used to treat the dentate gyrus on ice for 30 min, which was followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. The protein concentration was determined by the BCA protein assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime, Wuhan, China). Protein extract (25 µg /lane) was loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel (P0012A, Beyotime, Wuhan, China). After electrophoresis separation, the protein was transferred to the polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (FFP22, Beyotime, Wuhan, China). The membranes were incubated with rabbit FoxO3a monoclonal antibody (1: 2000, CST, #12829, MA, USA), mouse PSD95 monoclonal antibody (1: 1000, CST, #36233, MA, USA), rabbit SYN monoclonal antibody (1: 1000, CST, #36406, MA, USA), rabbit Ki67 monoclonal antibody (1: 1500, Abcam, ab16667, Cambridge, UK), mouse BDNF (mature type) monoclonal antibody (1: 1500, ABclonal, A18129, Wuhan, China), mouse α-tubulin monoclonal antibody (1: 2500, #3873, CST, MA, USA), and mouse β-actin monoclonal antibody (1: 2000, #3700, CST, MA, USA) overnight at 4◦C. The next day, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG (1:1000, A0208/A0216, Beyotime, Wuhan, China). Finally, the membranes were visualized using an Odyssey gel imaging system (LI-COR, Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA).

SPSS 24 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analysis. All data were found to be normally distributed by the Shapiro-Wilk test (P > 0.1). The data are given as mean ± SEM. All data were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey multiple comparisons as a post hoc test. P < 0.05 was the significant cutoff threshold.

The present study implemented the sucrose preference test (SPT), forced swim test (FST), tail suspension test (TST), elevated plus maze (EPM), and open field test (OFT) to evaluate the behavioral changes. SPT was used to evaluate depression-like behavior. FST and TST were used to assess the behavioral desperation state of rats. EPM was used to evaluate anxiety-like behavior. OFT was used to evaluate anxiety and depression behavior. Behavioral tests showed statistically significant intergroup variations in SPT [F (3, 36) = 65.31, p < 0.01], FST [F (3, 36) = 55.07, p < 0.01], TST [F (3, 36) = 45.82, p < 0.01], the open arm entries [F (3, 36) = 20.75, p < 0.01], the time in open arms [F (3, 36) = 19.16, p < 0.01], the time in the central area [F (3, 36) = 91.46, p < 0.01], and the distance in the central area [F (3, 36) = 73.80, p < 0.01]. There were no intergroup variations in the total distance in the EPM [F (3, 36) = 0.104, p = 0.96] and the total distance in the OFT [F (3, 36) = 0.115, p = 0.95]. Compared with the control group, the sucrose preference of was significantly reduced in CUS group (p < 0.01) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2A), the immobility duration in the FST was significantly prolonged in CUS group (p < 0.01) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2B), the immobility duration in the TST was significantly prolonged in CUS group (p < 0.01) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2C), the open arm entries in the EPM were significantly reduced in CUS group (p < 0.01) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2D, E), the time in open arms of the EPM was significantly reduced in CUS group (p < 0.01) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2D, F), while the total distance in the EPM remained unchanged in CUS group (p > 0.05) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p > 0.05, Fig. 2D, G), the time in the central area of OFT was significantly reduced in CUS group (p < 0.01) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2H, I), the distance in the central area of OFT was significantly reduced in CUS group (p < 0.01) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2H, J), while the total distance in the OFT remained unchanged in CUS group (p > 0.05) and CUS + AAV-Empty group (p > 0.05, Fig. 2H, K). Compared with the CUS group, the results of the CUS + AAV-Empty group showed no difference (p > 0.05 in Fig. 2A, p > 0.05 in Fig. 2B, p > 0.05 in Fig. 2C, p > 0.05 in Fig. 2D, E, p > 0.05 in Fig. 2D, F, p > 0.05 in Fig. 2D, G, p > 0.05 in Fig. 2H, I, p > 0.05 in Fig. 2H, J, and p > 0.05 in Fig. 2H, K). Compared with the CUS + AAV-Empty group, most of the results of the CUS + AAV- FoxO3a group showed noticeable changes (p < 0.01 in Fig. 2A, p < 0.01 in Fig. 2B, p < 0.01 in Fig. 2C, p < 0.01 in Fig. 2D, E, p < 0.01 in Fig. 2D, F, p < 0.01 in Fig. 2H, I, and p < 0.01 in Fig. 2H, J), while the total distance in the EPM (Fig. 2D, G, p > 0.05) and OFT (Fig. 2H, K, p > 0.05) showed no difference. These results suggested that CUS rats exhibited anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. The extended immobility duration in the FST and TST suggests behavioral hopelessness in rats, which aligns with the findings from the SPT and OFT that indicate depressive symptoms in CUS rats. Interestingly, AAV-FoxO3a treatment significantly improved the above-mentioned abnormal behavior changes of CUS rats. It is worth noting that both AAV-Empty and AAV-FoxO3a did not influence the behavior in the OFT and SPT of normal rats (Fig. S3).

As a widely accepted animal model for depression research, CUS can be used to simulate the diversity and complexity of stress factors in social life [24, 26]. Therefore, the CUS rat model was established for relevant experiments. In this study, the behavioral experiments showed that CUS induced obvious anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, such as reduced sugar consumption, increased desperate behavior, lower survival motivation, and reduced desire to explore unfamiliar environments. The above results indicated that CUS rats suffered significant psychological stress stimulation and the successful replication of the stress model. In addition, CUS induced cognitive impairment, manifested as a significant reduction in spatial learning and memory ability, and recognition and memory ability in CUS rats.

As a highly specialized subregion of the hippocampus, the unique anatomical structure and adult neurogenic characteristics of DG enable it to play a core role in emotional disorders and cognitive impairment by precisely regulating neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and neural circuit integration [30–32]. In this study, the results of the pathological experiment showed that obvious neuropathological alterations occurred in the hippocampus of CUS rats, and mainly occurred in DG. Accompanying this, the injury state to CA1, CA2, and CA3 in the CUS group was nearly indistinguishable from that in the control group. Therefore, neuropathological alterations induced by CUS in DG may be accompanied by related functional disorders. Of course, the detailed mechanism still needs to be explored in depth. To further analyze the mechanism of CUS-induced neuropathological alterations, anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, and cognitive impairment, we conducted this study from the following four perspectives: cell proliferation, synaptic plasticity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory response.

Neural stem cells in the adult hippocampus undergo self-renewal through mitosis and eventually generate new neurons, which is called adult hippocampal neurogenesis [12]. Hippocampal nerve proliferation in adult mammals (including humans) mainly occurs in the granule cell layer of DG [33]. DG is also one of the few areas in the adult brain where neural stem cells persist, and new neurons are generated [34]. Clinical studies have reported abnormal hippocampal neurogenesis in adult patients with AD and depressive disorder [35, 36]. After selective inhibition of DG neurogenesis by X-ray irradiation or antiproliferative drugs (such as cytarabine), significant depressive-like behavior occurred in mice [37]. Inhibiting neurogenesis in DG can also weaken the ability to distinguish similar environments [38]. Therefore, the neurogenesis level in DG can reflect the emotional state and cognitive function. This study detected the effect of CUS on cell proliferation in DG. Ki67 is a proliferation-related antigen, and its expression level can reflect the cell proliferation level [39]. The results of the present study showed that the expression level of Ki67 in DG was significantly decreased, showing that CUS significantly inhibited cell proliferation in DG, which may potentially affect neurogenesis in the DG, thereby leading to anxiety- and depressive-like behavior and cognitive impairment. However, the exact mechanism remains unclear and requires further research.

A series of studies have shown that abnormal synaptic plasticity is closely related to anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment, and the common characteristics of these diseases include destruction of synaptic structure, imbalance of neurotransmitter system, and reduction of neurotrophic factors, ultimately leading to the imbalance of neurological function [40–42]. BDNF is one of the most widely studied synaptic molecules and mediates synaptic plasticity [43]. Postsynaptic compact proteins constitute a uniform and dense protein layer aggregated on the cytoplasmic surface of the postsynaptic membrane, among which PSD-95 is one of the main components [44]. SYN is located on the presynaptic membrane, promoting the movement of synaptic vesicles towards the presynaptic membrane and facilitating the release of neurotransmitters, while also participating in the formation and development of synapses. It is one of the indicators showing changes in synaptic plasticity [45]. Therefore, BDNF, PSD-95, and SYN are closely related to the structural and functional integrity of synapses. This study detected the expression levels of BDNF, PSD-95, and SYN in the DG of CUS rats. The results showed that CUS significantly restrained the expression of BDNF, PSD-95, and SYN. Therefore, CUS caused abnormal synaptic plasticity in DG, which might be the pathological basis for anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and cognitive dysfunction in CUS rats.

Clinical studies have shown that the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα) increase significantly in the peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid of patients with depression, while the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-10) decrease significantly, which are related to the severity and disease progression of depression [46–48]. Meanwhile, research also shows that the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α increase significantly in the cerebrospinal fluid and blood of AD patients [49]. The above-mentioned research suggests that the levels of inflammatory factors are associated with anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and cognitive impairment. In the present study, the levels of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, and TNFα were significantly increased in CUS rats, while the levels of anti-inflammatory factors TGF-β1 and IL-10 were significantly decreased in CUS rats. The above results suggest that CUS may cause anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment in rats by triggering inflammatory responses in DG.

Oxidative stress is involved in emotional regulation disorders [50]. Animal experiments have confirmed that oxidative stress can induce obvious anxiety and depressive-like behaviors [51]. Oxidative stress also plays a key role in the occurrence and development of cognitive dysfunction [52]. Animal experiments have confirmed that antioxidant treatment can significantly improve cognitive function [53]. The occurrence of oxidative stress, including the excessive accumulation of ROS and the reduction of antioxidant enzyme activities such as SOD, CAT, and GPx, is considered to be the cause of chronic stress psychological abnormalities [54]. These studies indicate that oxidative stress is associated with anxiety, depressive-like behaviors, and cognitive impairment. Therefore, in this study, the level of ROS and the activities of SOD, CAT, and GPx were detected to evaluate the oxidative stress in CUS rats. In the present study, the level of ROS was significantly increased, while the activities of SOD, CAT, and GPx were significantly decreased. The above results suggest that CUS may cause anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment in rats by triggering oxidative stress in DG. Summarizing the above results, inhibition of cell proliferation, abnormal synaptic plasticity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory response are involved in the formation of anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and cognitive impairment in CUS rats. However, the exact mechanism remains unclear and requires further research.

Current research shows that FoxO3a plays a key role in maintaining neuronal homeostasis [55]. FoxO3a can protect neurons by alleviating oxidative stress [56]. In the early stage of neuronal injury, the expression level of FoxO3a is significantly reduced, and its nuclear translocation occurs [57]. In contrast, overexpression of FoxO3a can widely protect motor neurons from various toxic damages [58]. These studies suggest that FoxO3a is involved in maintaining the structure and function of neural tissues and cells. In the present study, CUS inhibited the expression of FoxO3a in DG, while FoxO3a overexpression significantly alleviated the degree of anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment. Meanwhile, the inhibition of cell proliferation, abnormal synaptic plasticity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory response in the DG were also significantly alleviated by FoxO3a overexpression. These results suggest that stress induces structural and functional abnormalities in DG by inhibiting the expression of FoxO3a, which may be the potential mechanism by which CUS induces anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment in rats. The biological effects of FOXO3a are mediated by post-translational modifications (mainly including phosphorylation and acetylation) or by its interacting partners, regulating nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity [59]. Of course, the specific and detailed mechanism still requires more in-depth research.

FoxO3a is recognized as a key transcription factor in cellular antioxidant defense. It can upregulate the expression of SOD, protecting cells from oxidative stress [56]. Our research has found that CUS leads to a downregulation of FoxO3a expression in the DG, which is closely associated with increased oxidative stress in this area of the brain. This strongly suggests that CUS may induce oxidative damage by impairing the antioxidant function of FoxO3a. Current study indicates that FoxO3a can inhibit the pro-inflammatory signaling pathway of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), potentially reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α [60]. Our findings demonstrate that the downregulation of FoxO3a in the DG of CUS-exposed rats may relieve this inhibitory effect, leading to increased neuroinflammation in the DG. Recent research suggests that reducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation may alleviate depressive-like behaviors and cognitive impairments induced by stress [7]. Previous researches also suggest that the disease progression of depression is related to the significant inhibition of FOXO3a signaling [61, 62]. All of this evidence outlines a clear pathological cascade: CUS induces anxiety- and depressive-like behavior as well as cognitive impairment. This effect is mediated by the downregulation of FoxO3a expression in the DG. The suppression of FoxO3a acts as a crucial molecular trigger, leading to oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, inhibiting cell proliferation, and reducing key synaptic proteins. The evidence shows that restoring FoxO3a expression in the DG reduces the pathological and behavioral deficits. This positions FoxO3a not just as a passive biomarker but as a key mediator in stress-induced dysfunction of the hippocampus. We propose the following mechanistic hypothesis: CUS serves as an upstream trigger that directly or indirectly suppresses FoxO3a level. Under normal physiological conditions, FoxO3a is believed to maintain cellular homeostasis by regulating transcriptional programs associated with antioxidant defense and anti-inflammatory responses. Its downregulation undermines these protective mechanisms, creating a conducive environment for the accumulation of reactive oxygen species and the propagation of pro-inflammatory signaling. This harmful microenvironment is inherently detrimental to neural plasticity processes, directly impairing the proliferation of neural progenitor cells and destabilizing synaptic structures. The functional consequence of these structural deficits is emotional and cognitive impairment. Our intervention using AAV-FoxO3a demonstrates that targeted restoration of this transcription factor sufficiently interrupts the pathological cascade at its core, thereby normalizing the cellular environment and restoring DG-dependent function. This model establishes the downregulation of FoxO3a as a critical pathogenic event linking chronic stress to hippocampal damage. Our study not only reveals a novel mechanism underlying stress-related neuropsychiatric disorders but also suggests that strategies aimed at enhancing FoxO3a function could potentially promote neuronal resilience and represent a promising therapeutic avenue.

Interestingly, our findings seem to contrast with previous studies suggesting that mice with a complete knockout of FoxO3a exhibit antidepressant-like behavior [63, 64]. This divergence may be attributed to the methodological and biological distinctions between the models. Systemic knockout eliminates FoxO3a throughout development and across all tissues, which may trigger compensatory adaptations not present in our adult, dentate gyrus–targeted intervention. Moreover, the role of FoxO3a may shift from a regulator of baseline emotional behavior under healthy conditions to a protective factor in the context of chronic stress–induced hippocampal dysfunction. Thus, while FoxO3a loss might convey resilience in certain paradigms, our data highlight that enhancing FoxO3a signaling within the hippocampus can counteract deficits in a chronic stress model. These results underscore the context-dependent nature of FoxO3a in stress responses and support its potential as a region-specific therapeutic target for stress-related disorders such as depression.

The observed downregulation of FoxO3a in the DG following chronic stress likely results from multiple molecular pathways. Chronic stress induces a neuroinflammatory state characterized by increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines activate signaling cascades, which are known to antagonize FoxO3a activity and suppress its expression, thereby disrupting its pro-survival and antioxidant functions [65]. Furthermore, epigenetic mechanisms play a crucial role. Stress-induced alterations in the activity of DNA methyltransferases can lead to increased DNA hypermethylation at the FoxO3a promoter region, resulting in its long-term transcriptional silencing [15]. Collectively, these glucocorticoid-driven, inflammatory, and epigenetic disturbances converge to suppress FoxO3a expression, positioning its downregulation as a key integrative event in stress-induced hippocampal dysfunction.

Our findings demonstrate that targeting an area of the brain traditionally linked to memory and learning—the dorsal DG—effectively alleviates anxiety and depression behaviors. This suggests that this region also plays a critical role in regulating emotion. Conventionally, the dorsal DG is viewed as a “cognitive” region for processing spatial and contextual information [66], while emotion is more associated with other brain areas, such as the ventral hippocampus [67]. However, under chronic stress, the function of the entire hippocampal system can be impaired [68]. The dorsal DG acts as a key “checkpoint” and “processing center” for information entering the hippocampus [69]. When its function is disrupted, it disturbs the signals subsequently sent to downstream regions responsible for emotion, such as the prefrontal cortex [70]. Therefore, restoring the FoxO3a protein in the dorsal DG was not merely about improving cognition, but more importantly, it repaired a critical node in the broader emotional processing pathway. This allowed downstream emotion-regulating brain regions to receive clearer, more normalized signals, which was reflected behaviorally in the animals’ restored ability to experience pleasure (Sucrose preference test) and reduced despair-like behavior (Forced swimming test). In simpler terms, our study shows that intervening in this “memory” region can improve emotional states by stabilizing a larger internal network responsible for mood regulation. This offers a fresh perspective for understanding stress and emotional disorders.

In conclusion, our research findings suggest that the low level of FoxO3a in the DG is a crucial factor linked to several pathological changes. These include oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, reduced cell proliferation, and synaptic dysfunction. Together, these alterations contribute to the emergence of anxiety behavior, depressive behavior, and cognitive impairment in rats subjected to CUS.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, although the CUS model is widely used, it is only a simulation model of human depression. Therefore, these research results still need to be verified in other models and clinical studies. Secondly, while TST in CUS rats yields interpretable data, it is suboptimal compared to its application in mice. TST can be more stressful for heavier rats, and its predictive validity in rats is less established than that of FST. Furthermore, we recognize that the sequence of behavioral tests, while optimized to minimize stress, may still pose a limitation. Thirdly, using Ki-67 as the sole marker for neurogenesis is insufficient, as it only indicates cell proliferation and does not provide insights into neuronal fate. Future studies should employ specific markers for newborn neurons to confirm the effects on neurogenesis. Fourthly, although our findings show a strong association between the downregulation of FoxO3a and abnormal changes, the experimental design does not establish direct causal relationships between all the measured factors, such as between oxidative stress and synaptic plasticity. We suggest that more targeted and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify these relationships. Fifthly, given that this study was conducted exclusively in male rats, its findings cannot be directly extrapolated to females, and future investigations are warranted to explore potential sex-specific differences in the observed effects. Finally, our interventions and measurements focused primarily on the DG. We acknowledge that the roles of other parts of the hippocampus or other brain regions in the observed behavior were not explored, representing an important avenue for future research.

Our findings suggest several promising directions for future research. First, it is important to determine whether FoxO3a primarily functions in neurons or glial cells within the DG. Second, future studies should identify the gene pool regulated by FoxO3a, which is associated with the observed ability to recover from stress. Third, future studies should employ larger cohorts to stratify subjects into stress-susceptible and resilient phenotypes, which will be crucial for identifying distinct molecular signatures and advancing toward personalized therapeutic strategies. Finally, it is necessary to clarify the network-level mechanism by which DG-specific FoxO3a affects emotional and cognitive functions.
