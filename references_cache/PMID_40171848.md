ROS1 rearrangements define a molecular subset of non-small cell lung cancer
(NSCLC) by accounting for 1%-2% of cases. Targeted therapy with ROS1 tyrosine
kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has significantly improved the outcomes for these
patients. First-generation inhibitors, such as crizotinib and entrectinib, have
demonstrated impressive efficacy, with objective response rates exceeding
60%-70%. However, the emergence of resistance mechanisms, including
solvent-front mutations such as ROS1 G2032R, and limited blood-brain barrier
penetration have limited the long-term efficacy of early-generation agents.
Next-generation TKIs, including lorlatinib, taletrectinib, and repotrectinib,
have been developed to overcome these challenges. These agents show enhanced
central nervous system (CNS) penetration and activity against on-target ROS1
resistance mutations. Repotrectinib, a potent, CNS-penetrant ROS1 inhibitor, has
demonstrated superior activity in both TKI-naive and -resistant tumors,
including those harboring the G2032R mutation. Zidesamtinib, a highly selective
next-generation ROS1 inhibitor, further addresses TRK-mediated off-target
neurological toxicities seen with prior agents, and is poised to offer improved
tolerability. Ongoing research is focused on optimizing sequencing strategies
for ROS1 inhibitors and exploring combination approaches to prevent or overcome
resistance. In addition, the development of novel diagnostic tools, including
RNA-based next-generation sequencing, has enhanced the detection of functional
ROS1 fusions by ensuring that patients with actionable mutations receive
appropriate targeted therapies. These advances highlight the evolving landscape
of treatment for ROS1-positive NSCLC, with the aim of maximizing long-term
survival and quality of life.

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSLC) is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in both the United States and globally1. In addition to mutations involving KRAS, EGFR, ERBB2, and BRAF, driver rearrangements involving multiple genes (ALK, RET, ROS1, NTRK1/2/3, FGFR1/2/3, ERBB2, ERBB4, NRG1, LTK fusions) have been identified in patients with NSCLC, refining disease classification into molecularly selected subgroups with therapeutic implications2. Among these, ROS1 rearrangements account for approximately 1–2% of NSCLC diagnoses and present unique opportunities – and challenges – in clinical practice3–6.

ROS1 (ROS proto-oncogene 1) is an evolutionarily conserved7 tyrosine kinase insulin receptor encoded by the ROS1 gene on chromosome 6 (region 6q22.1). ROS1 was first discovered in 1981 in cells transfected with the chicken sarcoma RNA UR2 tumor virus8. The ROS1 receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) consists of 2347 amino acids and is arranged as a single-pass transmembrane protein comprising a signal sequence, a large extracellular N-terminal domain (exons 1 to 34) consisting of 9 fibronectin type 3 repeats and 3 β-propeller domains9, and an intracellular domain. The latter can be subdivided into a juxtamembrane segment, a protein kinase domain, and a C-terminal tail10. Phylogenetic analyses have revealed ROS1 shares an evolutionary origin and amino acid sequence homology with ALK (anaplastic lymphoma kinase)11, a finding that has impacted the design of ROS1 tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).

ROS1 was long considered an orphan receptor with an unknown activating ligand and unclear physiological function in its wild-type (WT) form. However, a 2020 study by Kiyozumi et al. identified neural epidermal growth factor–like 2 (NELL2) as a binding partner for the murine ROS1 receptor12. Because the extracellular domain of ROS1 contains sequences highly homologous to fibronectin—a cell adhesion molecule of the extracellular matrix – it has been speculated that cellular attachment may contribute to ROS1 activation.13.

The absence of a known ligand has historically hindered the investigation of downstream signaling pathways activated by WT ROS1. Nonetheless, several studies have used chimeric receptors incorporating the ROS1 kinase domain fused to the ligand-binding domains of either EGFR or TRK. Upon stimulation of cells expressing these chimeras with the cognate ligand, activation of various signaling pathways was observed13, including the Ras/Raf/Erk mitogen activated pathway kinase (MAPK)14–16, the phosphoinositide-3 kinase (PI3K)/v-akt murine thymoma viral oncogene homolog 1 (AKT)/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)17,18 and the STAT316,18,19 pathways13.

ROS1 protein expression is observed during embryological development and in many adult tissues including kidney, gastrointestinal tract, and cerebellum, with low level expression in lung, testis, cervix, eye, parathyroid, adrenals and peripheral neural tissue20,21. In animal models, Ros1 knock-out mice appear healthy except for male infertility due to improper epididymal epithelial cell development22.

Oncogenic activation of ROS1 can occur via chromosomal rearrangement, yielding a functional fusion protein with the 3’ kinase domain of ROS1 and a variable 5’ gene partner. The transforming capability of ROS1 fusions was first described in 1986 using DNA from the MCF-7 human breast cancer cell line transferred to the NIH-3T3 fibroblast cell line23. Later in 1987, a naturally occurring ROS1 gene fusion involving the gene partner FIG (also known as GOPC) was first described using the U118MG human glioblastoma cell line, confirming the oncogenic potential of the chimeric protein24. In 2007, a large-scale survey of tyrosine kinase signaling through a phosphoproteomic approach first identified ROS1 fusions in a subset of NSCLC tumors25. Since then, ROS1 rearrangements involving more than 50 gene partners have been documented26,27, with significant variability depending on cancer type28. ROS1 fusions alone are sufficient to induce oncogenesis in tumor models17,29–31 and are typically mutually exclusive with additional oncogenic drivers5, although rare co-occurrence with EGFR21,32,33 or KRAS32 alterations have been observed.

Due to the high incidence of NSCLC in the general population, NSCLCs are the most common cancers with ROS1 fusions and can have distinct characteristics when compared to other histologies with ROS1 fusions. While most cases of ROS1-rearranged glioblastoma involve intrachromosomal events such as the 6q22 microdeletion34, ROS1 fusion-positive NSCLCs typically arise from interchromosomal rearrangements5,35,36. In patients with NSCLC, the following 5’ gene partners have been identified at decreasing frequencies: CD74 (44–64%), EZR (16–29%), SDC4 (9–16%) and SLC34A2 (5–12%)5,35,36 (Figure 1). In contrast, more than half of non-NSCLC cases are associated with the GOPC fusion partner35. The kinase domain of ROS1 is located between exons 36 and 42 for the canonical isoform (NM_002944). In NSCLC, breakpoints are clustered around exons 32 to 34 whereas they typically involve exons 34 to 36 in the non-NSCLC cohort35.

Distinct N-terminal fusion partners have been shown to establish different subcellular localizations of the ROS1 oncoprotein and downstream activation patterns. For instance, SDC4-ROS1 and SLC34A2-ROS1 fusions localize to the endosomal compartment and have been shown to activate the MAPK pathway signaling more effectively compared to fusions localizing to the endoplasmic reticulum, such as CD74-ROS137. Different clinical characteristics have also been observed in ROS1-rearranged NSCLC depending on the fusion partner, with CD74 demonstrating an increased predilection for central nervous system (CNS) metastases38 and improved responses to crizotinib38,39. Unlike most fusions associated with aberrant oncogenic kinases such as ALK40 or RET41, gene partners associated with dimerization domains have not been described in the context of the ROS1 RTK6,13. ROS1 activation is thought to result from autophosphorylation of specific tyrosine residues in its intracellular domain which serve as docking sites for adaptor proteins, such as SH2-domain containing adaptor protein17,29.

In contrast to ROS1 fusions, most missense ROS1 mutations are classified as variants of unknown significance (VUS) as they do not cluster in any specific ROS1 domain and their impact on protein function and drug response remains unclear.28. Hence, such molecular alterations are not thought to represent predictive biomarkers for the efficacy of ROS1 TKIs and likely arise as secondary somatic events. Select point mutations involving the ROS1 kinase domain, however, are recurrent events mediating therapeutic resistance to ROS1 TKIs, a subject which will be covered later in this manuscript. ROS1 amplifications appear oncogenic and could be implicated in therapeutic resistance to EGFR inhibitors42. The impact of ROS1 amplifications and splice site variants is less characterized, and additional functional studies are warranted to explore their potential role in oncogenesis and therapeutic response.

ROS1-rearranged NSCLC is typically diagnosed in patients with a light smoking history or never-smokers. Such cancers are thought to arise in younger patients when compared to most NSCLC cases, with a median age of 50–57 years at diagnosis4,35. Incidence is also increased in female patients5,35 and those of South Asian or East Asian ancestry4,35.

Lung adenocarcinoma is the predominant histology5, including the mucinous and signet ring cell subtypes35,43; up to 53% of tumor samples showing abundant mucin on histological examination5. ROS1 rearrangements are also identified in patients with the squamous or large cell carcinomas, although rarely21,44. Patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC face a higher risk of venous thromboembolism45 when compared to all patients with NSCLC46. Mechanistically, such predisposition is thought to be mediated by high levels of extracellular mucin which can active platelets through interactions via P-selectin and L-selectin, promoting thrombosis47. Interestingly, PD-L1 tumor cell expression (tumor proportion score [TPS]) could be increased in ROS1-positive vs. ROS1-negative NSCLC35, although reports remain conflicting48. Using the PD-L1 22C3 CDx assay, up to 35–45% of patients had cancers with a high PD-L1 score (PD-L1 TPS ≥50%)35,48.

Distinct patterns of metastatic spread are observed. CNS involvement is common, with a study estimating an incidence of brain metastases of 36% in treatment-naïve patients. The CNS was the sole site of progression on crizotinib in 47% of patients49,50. A retrospective study also highlighted an increased prevalence of CNS metastases in patients with CD74-ROS1 fusion-positive NSCLC when compared to other ROS1 fusion partners51. From a mechanistic perspective, the CD74-ROS1 fusion protein was shown to activate the extended synaptotagmin-like protein (E-Syt1) which is involved in cancer cell invasiveness and metastasis16. Clinicopathologic features are, however, insufficiently robust to accurately predict cases of NSCLC with ROS1 rearrangements. As a result, upfront ancillary testing is indicated in all patients with a new diagnosis of NSCLC in order to evaluate for the presence of oncogenic drivers, including ROS1.

Given the significant therapeutic implications and multiple FDA approvals for treating ROS1-rearranged NSCLC, universal screening for ROS1 fusions should be implemented whenever feasible in newly diagnosed cases. Multiple diagnostic assays are available for detecting ROS1 rearrangements, though each comes with intrinsic limitations. Importantly, the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) over the past decade has enabled comprehensive sequencing platforms that allow for multiplex testing, detailed characterization of ROS1 fusion partners, and the identification of concurrent genomic alterations.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) can be used to detect ROS1 overexpression, a byproduct and surrogate of ROS1 fusions, in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues. IHC is associated with low costs and minimal operator requirements, with shorter turnaround time when compared to techniques such as fluorescence in-situ hybridization (FISH) and NGS52. Protein expression via IHC is associated, however, with a variable sensitivity (ranging from 94%53 to 100%54,55) and specificity (88%55 to 100%53,54) depending on the antibody clone in use (e.g. D4D6 versus SP384). In general, the sensitivity of the D4D6 clone is considered to be superior to its specificity. Cytoplasmic staining cut-offs which are however poorly standardized56 and immunoreactivity can be influenced by tissue fixation57. In addition, variable ROS1 protein expression has been observed in normal lung tissue21,28, including resident macrophages and type 2 pneumocytes56, potentially leading to false positive results. Despite these limitations, IHC may serve as an acceptable initial screening tool for detecting ROS1 rearrangements in resource-limited settings21,58. While a negative IHC result can be helpful in ruling out a ROS1 fusion, a positive IHC result should be confirmed with either additional assays such as FISH or NGS given the limited specificity of IHC (PMID: 29396253).

Before the development of high-throughput NGS, FISH has long represented the gold standard method for detection of oncogenic fusions such as those involving ROS14,6,32. Break-apart FISH is a single-gene assay which involves differentially labelled fluorescent probes targeting the 5’ and 3’ regions of the ROS1 gene. This technique allows for the visualization of gene rearrangements by detecting whether the probes remain adjacent (indicating no rearrangement) or separate (indicating a potential fusion event). Like IHC, FISH testing is performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples and is associated with single-cell resolution and a rapid turnaround time.

FISH is considered positive when more than 15% of nuclei, or more than 50 cancer cells, show either a split signal pattern or isolated ROS1 3’ signals4,5,55. Since most ROS1-rearranged NSCLCs result from interchromosomal rearrangements, the resulting split signals are generally straightforward to interpret. While FISH is highly sensitive and specific5, the technique remains technically challenging52 and prone to both false-positive results (e.g. in cases of non-functional gene fusions59,60) and false-negative results (e.g. in cases of small intrachromosomal rearrangements such as GOPC-ROS161). In addition, FISH is not amenable to multiplex testing and does not allow for the identification of the ROS1 fusion partner or exonic breakpoints predicting ROS1 oncogenicity and actionability.

Reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) involves the amplification of RNA transcripts by first converting RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) using reverse transcriptase, followed by amplification through polymerase chain reaction (PCR). This technique has the potential to identify gene fusions and their respective 5’ partners5,21, including in cases that are positive by FISH, also allowing for detection of alterations present at low variant allele frequencies (VAF)62. However, PCR as a fusion partner-specific assay is limited by the availability of primers, which are designed only for a finite set of known gene partners63. For instance, the AmoyDx RT-PCR assay has a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 85% and was previously approved as a companion diagnostic in Europe and Asia64. However, the assay only has primers for ROS1 fusions involving CD74, SDC4, SLC34A2, GOPC, EZR, TPM3 and LRIG3, limited its use in the context of novel, actionable gene partners65. RT-PCR also requires specialized techniques and relies heavily on pre-analytical considerations such as the quality and quantity of RNA extracted from FFPE samples66. More recently, anchored multiplex PCR has been employed to improve the detection of a wide range of fusion partners, enhancing the utility of RT-PCR in clinical diagnostics67 (see RNA-based NGS section).

Over the last decade, DNA-based NGS has emerged as a powerful tool for the detection of a wide array of oncogenic alterations, including ROS1 rearrangements. Targeted NGS focuses on a predefined set of genes or genomic regions of interest and allows for the identification of both known and novel ROS1 fusion partners, thus expanding the range of detectable alterations61. DNA-based NGS is associated with a higher sensitivity than IHC and FISH, including in cases of intrachromosomal rearrangements which would otherwise be missed by FISH68. With the ability to perform massive parallel sequencing, NGS is particularly suitable for the simultaneous evaluation of multiple oncogenic drivers, alongside other genomic markers such as tumor mutational burden (TMB). Reflective of oncogene addiction, TMB is typically low in the setting of ROS1-rearranged NSCLC35,48 with recurring co-alterations involving TP53 (32%), CDKN2A (31%), and CDKN2B (21%)35. Multiple tumor DNA-based NGS assays are clinically validated for the detection of ROS1 fusions, including the FDA-authorized Memorial Sloan Kettering Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets (MSK-IMPACT®)69.

While tumor DNA-based NGS has emerged as a standard-of-care diagnostic tool for identifying oncogenic drivers in NSCLC, it is associated with intrinsic limitations, such as prolonged turnaround times when compared to IHC, FISH or RT-PCR. Two major issues also complicate the interpretation of DNA-based NGS for ROS1 rearrangements. 1) Imperfect sensitivity: among fusions missed by DNA-based NGS and later confirmed by RNA-based testing, those involving ROS1 appear the most common70. Due to the dispersed distribution of fusion breakpoints and the complex intronic structure of ROS1, accurate detection of ROS1 fusions can pose significant challenges. In addition, intron 31 of ROS1 at the rearrangement breakpoint site contains highly repetitive sequences which can be difficult to map, sometimes resulting in insufficient genomic coverage and false-negative results70. 2) Imperfect specificity: it should also be recognized that not every ROS1 rearrangement picked up by DNA-based NGS produces a functional fusion protein that can be targeted by ROS1 TKIs. Specifically, non-canonical ROS1 rearrangements identified by DNA-based NGS may not be clinically actionable. For a fusion to be functional, the kinase domain of ROS1 must remain intact and in-frame within the rearranged gene, which requires post-transcriptional validation.70. In cases of negative or atypical DNA-NGS findings, orthogonal testing is often necessary62. Among available options, RNA-based assays have emerged as an essential tool in confirming functional fusions in the context of non-canonical ROS1 rearrangements, as well as detection of occult fusions in the context of driver-negative NSCLC70,71.

DNA-based NGS allows for the detection of somatic alterations occurring at low VAFs, including those identifiable on circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) extracted from plasma samples72. Liquid biopsies enable the non-invasive detection of ROS1 fusions as an alternative to tissue-based DNA NGS. Subclonal ROS1 rearrangements detected on ctDNA NGS can be reflective of tumor heterogeneity on a dynamic basis, including emergent ROS1 kinase domain mutations arising in the context of ROS1 TKI therapy73. The diagnostic yield of plasma ctDNA testing is however contingent on the DNA shedding rate, tumor location (e.g., CNS disease) and variations in tumor burden over time. Therefore, confirmatory sequencing on tumor tissue should be pursued whenever possible if ROS1 fusions are not detected in plasma ctDNA analysis.

RNA-based NGS can enhance the detection of ROS1 fusions as it sequences the coding regions of targeted genes rather than including introns. This approach bypasses the limitations of DNA-based NGS which can fail to accurately predict genomic breakpoints due to alternative splicing events61. Such assays allow for the definitive functional characterization of ROS1 fusions and are particularly useful when identifying rearrangements involving long intronic sequences with repetitive elements70. As an example, anchored multiplex PCR-based technique can be employed for this purpose and identifies actionable drivers missed by DNA-based NGS in up to 14% of lung tumors, including ROS1 fusions in 4% of cases70. Other groups have used more comprehensive techniques such as whole transcriptome sequencing to identify ROS1 and other fusions. However, RNA-based NGS has limitations related to pre-analytical factors, such as RNA extraction quality, which is crucial for ensuring accurate results. Given its ability to provide a more definitive assessment, RNA-based targeted NGS should be considered in all patients with driver-negative DNA-based NGS to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of actionable gene rearrangements.

For ROS1-rearranged NSCLC, treatment options beyond ROS1 TKIs include localized treatment options and chemotherapy-based regimens. These approaches can play a pivotal role in the context of symptomatic treatment or when ROS1 TKIs are no longer effective due to acquired resistance mechanisms, poor tolerance, or toxicity issues.

Considering the tropism of ROS1-rearranged NSCLC and its predilection for brain metastases, treatment necessitates a multidisciplinary evaluation in collaboration with radiation oncology and surgical teams. Early reports have suggested ROS1-rearranged NSCLC may be particularly sensitive to RT74. However, with the advent of next-generation, brain-penetrant ROS1 TKIs, the treatment paradigm of NSCLC associated with ROS1 fusions has evolved, and local therapy options can sometimes be deferred in patients with brain metastases, particularly in patients who are asymptomatic and do not have extensive intracranial disease.

In clinical practice, patients with ROS1-rearranged lung adenocarcinoma tend to exhibit a favorable sensitivity profile to standard chemotherapy75–77. When evaluating pemetrexed-based regimens, patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC demonstrate an objective response rate (ORR) of 58%75,78 and progression-free survival (PFS) of 7.2–7.5 months75,78. These efficacy outcomes are notably improved when compared to patients with ROS1 WT NSCLC, who demonstrate an ORR of 25% and a median PFS of 4.1 months on pemetrexed chemotherapy75. Pemetrexed susceptibility could be mediated by low mRNA expression levels of thymidylate synthetase (TS) in patients with NSCLC harboring ROS1 fusions when compared to ROS1 WT tumors79. Even within the context of ROS1-rearranged NSCLC, lower levels of TS were associated with improved PFS on pemetrexed (184 days in the TS-low vs. 110 days in the TS-high subgroup)80. However, such an association was not present when evaluating TS protein expression in IHC75.

Despite an immunophenotype characterized by higher PD-L1 expression35,50, patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC appear to have limited responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). The multicenter IMMUNOTARGET registry published in 2019 by Mazières et al. reported an ORR of 17% in patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC treated with ICI monotherapy50. Responses to ICIs in patients with ROS1 fusion-positive NSCLC were comparable to patients with NSCLC harboring any oncogenic driver other than KRAS (ORR 12%) or ALK-rearranged NSCLC (ORR 0%)50. However, primary progressive disease (PD) was observed in a majority of patients (83%), thus questioning the efficacy of ICIs in most patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC50. In contrast, patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC may derive benefit from ICI when used in combination with chemotherapy81. In a small retrospective study by Choudhury et al. at MSK, ICI monotherapy was associated with an ORR of 13% and a time-to-treatment discontinuation (TTD) of 2.1 months, whereas chemo-immunotherapy regimens led to an ORR of 83% and a TTD of 10 months48. In this study, no significant differences in terms of PD-L1 expression or TMB was observed between responders and non-responders48.

ROS1 inhibitors are the standard of care for treatment-naïve ROS1-rearranged NSCLC. Given that ROS1 shares 49% of its amino acid sequence with ALK82, with up to 77% homology at the kinase domain36, pre-existing multikinase ALK inhibitors were the first agents explored for the treatment of ROS1-rearranged NSCLC83. ROS1 inhibitors can be classified either based on their mechanistic ROS1 binding mode using the Roskoski classification, based on their generation, or based on their spectrum of activity against kinases other than ROS1.

Type I TKIs (crizotinib, entrectinib, ceritinib, brigatinib, lorlatinib, repotrectinib, taletrectinib, zidesamtinib) are ATP-competitive inhibitors that interact with the active conformation of the kinase, where the DFG (aspartic acid–phenylalanine–glycine) motif is in the “DFG-D in” state, thus inhibiting ROS1 catalytic activity. In contrast, type II TKIs such as cabozantinib bind to the inactive conformation of the kinase, where the DFG motif is in the “DFG-D out” state, thus stabilizing the inactive conformation84. Due to the conformational differences between ROS1 “DFG-D in” and “DFG-D out” states, the binding mode of type I and type II inhibitors relies on different binding pockets and has implications on collateral sensitivity when targeting acquired resistance mutations85.

Classification of ROS1 inhibitors based on “generations” relies on grouping these molecules based on their design features and the waves that they were first investigated in (Table 1 and Figure 2). First-generation inhibitors, such as crizotinib and entrectinib, were the first ROS1 TKIs to be investigated and have very limited activity in the context of acquired resistance mutations. Second-generation inhibitors, such as lorlatinib, taletrectinib or repotrectinib, are characterized by an improved and rationally designed structure that allows coverage of some mutations granting resistance to first-generation inhibitors. Next-generation inhibitors like zidesamtinib (NVL-520) are characterized by a highly optimized structure that allows maximal coverage of acquired resistance mutations to prior generation inhibitors, while also minimizing toxicity due to an improved selectivity profile. These inhibitors also vary depending on their intracranial activity, with some demonstrating better CNS penetration and efficacy (Table 2).

The toxicity profile of ROS1 TKIs is largely influenced by their kinase inhibitory profile. Multi-kinase (MKI) inhibitors with considerable activity against mesenchymal epithelial transition (MET), like crizotinib and cabozantinib86 can be associated with peripheral edema. ROS1 kinase inhibitors that also target ALK include crizotinib, ceritinib, brigatinib, entrectinib and lorlatinib. ALK TKIs have been associated with varying degrees of hyperlipidemia, hypertension and creatine phosphokinase (CPK) elevation. Similarly, TKIs with narrower inhibition profile but with meaningful TRKA/B/C activity, such as entrectinib, repotrectinib and taletrectinib, can cause off-target toxicities, including Trk-mediated neurotoxicity which can present as dizziness, cognitive disturbances and neuropathic pain, as more prominently seen with entrectinib and repotrectinib. Across classes, use of ROS1 TKIs can also be complicated by gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation), hepatotoxicity, QT interval prolongation and pneumonitis28.

Mechanisms of acquired resistance to ROS1 TKIs were initially characterized in the setting of first-generation ROS1 TKIs, with subsequent studies demonstrating cross-resistance of on-target mutations in patients treated with most early-generation ROS1 inhibitors (Figure 3). Many of the mutations arising upon resistance to first generation inhibitors are substitutions occurring on the solvent-front (SF) region of the ROS1 kinase domain. Among these, ROS1 SF G2032R represents the most common driver of resistance to early-generation TKIs, followed by D2033N87–89. ROS1 G2032R and D2033N are paralogs of ALK G1202R and D1203N resistance mutations, respectively. Whereas G2032R results in direct steric hindrance to crizotinib binding87, molecular-dynamics simulations of D2033N identified changes in electrostatic interactions with crizotinib as the main mediator of reduced crizotinib affinity90. The L1951R substitution, which occurs in the Beta-1 strand, has also been reported following crizotinib or ceritinib treatment89,91. Additional acquired resistance mutations identified in the kinase domain of ROS1 include S1986F/Y (either post-crizotinib88,92 or post-lorlatinib88), F1994L (post-ceritinib)93, F2004V/C/I (post-entrectinib94,95 and post-crizotinib96), gate-keeper (GK) L2026M (post-crizotinib)93,97, and L2155S (post-crizotinib98). Depending on genomic profiling at the time of acquired resistance, options include switching to a type II TKI, such as cabozantinib, or transitioning to next-generation ROS1 TKIs with potent activity against the on-target resistance mutation in place99.

The Catalytic-spine 6 (CS6) mutation ROS1 L2086F represents an emerging multi-drug resistant mutation, as it has been observed post-crizotinib89, post-lorlatinib88, post-repotrectinib100 and also in preclinical models with zidesamtinib resistance101. Due to shared structural vulnerabilities across the majority of available ROS1 TKIs, this mutation grants cross resistance to most first, second and next-generation ROS1 TKIs. Previously published data however suggest that inhibitor type switching represents a viable strategy in this setting85.

Importantly, resistance to ROS1 TKIs can also arise through ROS1-independent mechanisms. Bypass pathway signaling can occur through emergent KRAS88,102, NRAS88,103, BRAF73,104, KIT97, PI3KCA73 β -catenin89 or HER297 activating mutations. MET amplifications have also been reported in the setting of MET-sparing TKIs, such as lorlatinib105. In addition, small cell transformation, characterized by TP53 and RB1 inactivation, has been observed in crizotinib-treated ROS1-rearranged NSCLC106. Tailoring subsequent-line therapies to these specific resistance mechanisms, including the use of chemotherapy and targeted therapy combinations, is essential. Future trials are however needed to refine these strategies and to better guide clinical decision-making.

Crizotinib is a multitargeted, Type I, ATP-competitive first-generation TKI initially evaluated in the context of ALK fusions107. Early evidence however suggested ROS1 may represent a second therapeutic target of crizotinib, with the drug exhibiting higher potency against ROS1 than ALK36,108,109. Preclinical data supporting the use of crizotinib in ROS1-rearranged NSCLC cell lines were first available in 2012, with dose-dependent ROS1 inhibition observed on antiproliferative assays44,110. Later that year, the first clinical report of crizotinib was published, involving a young, non-smoking patient with NSCLC associated with a ROS1 fusion. That patient received crizotinib 250 mg twice daily with significant improvement in cancer-related symptoms and a near-complete response after 12 weeks of treatment4.

The PROFILE 1001 trial initially assessed the efficacy of crizotinib in patients with NSCLC harboring ALK fusions, as detected by RT-PCR or NGS, and the trial was expanded to include a cohort of patients with ROS1 fusions. Crizotinib demonstrated substantial activity in patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC, showing an ORR of 72%, a median PFS of 19 months36, and a median OS of 51 months108. As a result, in 2016, crizotinib became the first FDA-approved TKI for the treatment of ROS1-rearranged NSCLC. In 2018, a second large prospective study confirmed efficacy of crizotinib in ROS1 fusion-positive NSCLC, reporting a similar ORR of 72%, with a median PFS of 16 months and a median OS of 44 months111 (Figure 3).

Several limitations are however observed in patients treated with crizotinib, including the drug’s limited ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and emergent resistance mechanisms. One study suggested a shorter PFS on crizotinib in patients with CD74-ROS1 fusion-positive NSCLC when compared to non-CD74 fusion partners, possibly due to a higher prevalence of brain metastases in CD74-positive tumors51. Ultimately, 47–83% of patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC will experience CNS progression49,112. In addition to intracranial disease, the emergence of on-target, point mutations in the kinase domain of ROS1 has been reported in 38–53% of patients progressing on crizotinib88,89. Recurrent events associated with resistance to crizotinib include solvent-front (SF) substitutions, such as ROS1 G2032R (33–41%)87–89,95 and D2033N (2–6%)87–89, as well as the L2026M gatekeeper (GK) mutation93,97.

Entrectinib is another early-generation, type I, multitargeted TKI with activity against ROS1, TRK and ALK which was designed to penetrate the blood brain barrier113,114. Entrectinib received FDA approval in 2019 for the treatment of ROS1-rearranged NSCLC based on the combined analysis of 3 large multicenter trials (STARTRK-1, STARTRK-2 and ALKA-372–001)115. In this integrated analysis, 43% of patients presented intracranial disease at baseline115 when compared to 16% in the crizotinib trial, which specifically excluded untreated brain metastases36. Despite this, entrectinib demonstrated a comparably favorable ORR of 68%, a median PFS of 15.7 months (Figure 3), and a median OS of 47.8 months116 in the overall population, with an intracranial ORR of 55% and a median PFS of 13.6 months in patients with CNS disease at baseline115. Entrectinib was also tested in patients who progressed on crizotinib only in the CNS, although benefits were modest, with an ORR of 11% and a median PFS of 4.7 months; intracranial responses were observed in 19% of patients. Entrectinib is being compared head-to-head with crizotinib in an ongoing phase III trial enrolling patients with treatment-naïve, ROS1-rearranged NSCLC (NCT04603807). Similar to other early-generation ROS1 TKIs, entrectinib has limited activity against ROS1 G2032R, D2033N and L2026M resistance mutations117,118.

Ceritinib and brigatinib are second-generation ALK type I inhibitors which have also demonstrated activity against ROS1 in preclinical models119,120 as well as improved CNS permeability when compared to crizotinib121,122. Ceritinib was studied in a phase 2 trial including 32 patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC, the majority of which were crizotinib-naïve120. In this study, ORR was 62% with a median DOR of 21 months. The median PFS was 9 months in all patients versus 19 months for crizotinib-naïve patients. The intracranial ORR with ceritinib however remained low (25%) and most patients with baseline brain metastases were treated with SBRT prior to trial entry123.

Brigatinib was also evaluated as part of a phase 2 study124, demonstrating an ORR of 71% in patients with treatment-naïve ROS1-rearranged NSCLC (n=28), versus 32% in patients with prior crizotinib (n=19). In contrast, median PFS was 12 months and 7 months in those subgroups, respectively125. Limited evidence is available supporting the efficacy of brigatinib in the post-crizotinib126, post-ceritinib setting in the absence of an identifiable resistance mechanism127, but not in the context of ROS1 G2032R118.

Cabozantinib90 is a multikinase type II TKI with activity against ROS1, ALK, MET, AXL, KIT, RET, and VEGFR128, and which may be effective for specific on-target mutations arising on early-generation ROS1 TKIs90,118,129. In preclinical models, cabozantinib was first evaluated in the context of ROS1 G2032R kinase mutations where it overcame TKI resistance with an half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 14 nmol/L versus 254 nmol/L for crizotinib and 277 nmol/L for ceritinib128. Whereas clinical benefit has yet to be reported in the setting of G2032R130, cabozantinib has led to responses or prolonged disease control in patients with either D2033N109 or L2086F85,96 mutations. In the setting of multi-drug resistant ROS1 CS6 L2086F mutation, the quinazoline scaffold present in cabozantinib allows development of pi-pi stacking with the phenylalanine introduced by the mutation, with subsequent higher binding affinity and collateral sensitizing activity. Cell viability assays have also identified cabozantinib as a promising treatment in the context of ROS1 L2026M118 and F2004V131. Nevertheless, cabozantinib is often associated with an unfavorable toxicity profile and may face overlapping resistance with type I TKIs (e.g., ROS1 L2010M), whereas specific on-target resistance mechanisms may retain sensitivity to certain type I TKIs (e.g. G1957A)39.

Lorlatinib is a potent, CNS-penetrant, second generation, type I TKI with kinase selectivity against ALK and ROS1. Lorlatinib demonstrates improved inhibitory activity against ROS1 when compared to crizotinib and ceritinib92,93. A phase II study including 47 patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC reported an ORR of 61% and a median PFS of 21.0 months in TKI-naïve patients. Among crizotinib-pretreated patients, the ORR was 26% and median PFS was 8.5 months. Notably, an ORR of 67% was observed in the 6 patients with untreated brain metastasis132. The drug is currently guidelines indicated as later line TKI therapy for ROS1 fusion-positive lung cancers. Preclinical studies showed lorlatinib retains sensitivity to specific on-target mutations arising in the context of earlier-generation ROS1 TKIs, such as ROS1 S1986F/Y (1.0–1.6 nmol/L)92. Lorlatinib appears inactive, however, against ROS1 G2032R111 and L2026M92,133. In post-progression samples from patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC treated with lorlatinib, 46% acquired ROS1 mutations, with the most common being G2032R (32%), followed by L2086F (11%)88.

Repotrectinib (TPX-0005) is a next-generation, macrocyclic, type I TKI with activity against ROS1, TRKA/B/C, and ALK, designed to overcome resistance mutations such as ROS1 G2032R by avoiding steric interference134,135. Repotrectinib’s lower molecular weight is expected to enhance brain penetration134, and preclinical studies have shown greater potency against wild-type ROS1 when compared to entrectinib or crizotinib as well as activity in both CD74-ROS1 WT and G2032R-mutant tumors94,101,134–136. The TRIDENT-1 trial evaluated repotrectinib in patients with ROS1-positive NSCLC, including both TKI-naïve and pretreated groups. Among TKI-naïve patients, the ORR was 79%, with a median PFS of 35.7 months (Figure 3). This prolongation of median PFS highlights the utility of next-generation TKIs to substantially improve durability of clinical benefit, as has been seen in other oncogene-driven lung cancers such as EGFR-mutant and ALK fusion-positive NSCLCs. In patients previously treated with one ROS1 TKI, the ORR was 38%, with a median PFS of 9.0 months. Notably, in patients with the G2032R mutation, the ORR was 59%, confirming the efficacy of repotrectinib in this molecular subgroup. Repotrectinib also showed strong CNS activity, with an intracranial ORR of 89% in TKI-naïve patients and of 38% in previously treated patients117. The drug is currently approved for the treatment of both TKI-naïve and TKI-pretreated patients.

Taletrectinib is a selective type I inhibitor of ROS1 and TRKA/B/C (NTRK) which demonstrated efficacy against crizotinib-resistant ROS1-rearranged cancers in preclinical models, including cell lines harboring the G2032R, L2026M, L1951R and S1986F mutations137. However, the activity of taletrectinib appeared limited in the presence of ROS1 D2033N mutations137. In a phase I study involving 46 patients, the ORR was 33% in those with crizotinib-resistant NSCLCs138. Meanwhile, a Japanese phase I trial with 15 patients reported an ORR of 58% across all participants and 67% in treatment-naïve patients139. The pooled analysis of from the regional (TRUST-1140) and global (TRUST-2141) phase 2 registrational studies of taletrectinib included 273 patients with advanced or metastatic ROS1-rearranged NSCLC142. In the TKI-naïve cohort (n=160), ORR was 88.8% with a median PFS of 45.6 months. In the TKI-pretreated cohort (n=113), ORR was 55.8% and a median PFS of 9.7 months. Of note, objective responses were observed in 61.5% of patients (n=8/13) with G3032R resistance mutations. At the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) of 600 mg daily, taletrectinib demonstrated a favorable tolerability and safety profile and most AEs were either grade 1 or 2. The most common all-grade AEs were increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST; 72.1%) or alanine aminotransferase (AST; 68.8%), followed by diarrhea (63.2%) and nausea (47.2%). The rate of neurologic AEs was low, including for dizziness (21.1%) and dysgeusia (14.5%), and most were grade 1.

Zidesamtinib (NVL-520), is a next-generation type I ROS1 inhibitor that distinguishes itself from other ROS1 TKIs in that it is highly selective for ROS1 and avoids the inhibition of TRK that can be associated with neurotoxicities. Preclinical studies demonstrated the nanomolar activity of zidesamtinib in the setting of ROS1 WT and G2032R-mutant cancer cell lines. Specifically, zidesamtinib showed improved potency against ROS1 G2032R (IC50 of 7.9 nmol/L) when compared to both repotrectinib (IC50 of 30 nmol/L) and taletrectinib (IC50 of 100 nmol/L). Zidesamtinib was also highly selective (185-fold) for WT ROS1 when compared to TRK101. ARROS-1 is an ongoing phase I/II study of zidesamtinib in patients with advanced ROS1-rearranged solid tumors, including ROS1 TKI-pretreated NSCLC. Zidesamtinib was well tolerated with peripheral edema (19%), ALT or AST increase (11% each) and weight gain (11%) representing the most commonly reported AEs. Importantly, no AEs led to treatment discontinuation and TRK-mediated neurotoxicity was avoided using zidesamtinib. The phase 1 dose escalation portion of the study (n=104) selected the 100 mg daily dose as the RP2D. When looking at preliminary activity, ORR was 44% in response-evaluable patients with ROS1 TKI-pretreated NSCLC (n=71). In the setting of G2032R resistance mutations, ORR was 72% in repotrectinib-naïve patients versus 38% in those who previously received repotrectinib. The intracranial ORR was 50% (n=4/8), including responses in those who previously received brain-penetrant ROS1 TKIs such as repotrectinib or lorlatinib143. In 2024, zidesamtinib received FDA breakthrough designation144 and is currently being investigated as part of the phase 2 of ARROS-1 which will include patient cohorts with both TKI-pretreated and -naïve NSCLC harboring a ROS1 rearrangement (NCT05118789).

Additional TKIs have been investigated for the treatment of ROS1-rearranged NSCLC. Ensartinib is a multitargeted ALK- and ROS1-directed TKI which was evaluated in a phase II trial, demonstrating a modest ORR of 27%, with some evidence of intracranial activity145. Foretinib is another multikinase kinase inhibitor targeting ROS1 and with activity against ROS1 G2032R98, but for which clinical development was halted due to an unfavorable safety profile98.

The treatment landscape for ROS1-rearranged NSCLC is rapidly advancing, with next-generation TKIs demonstrating significant potential to improve patient outcomes. Drawing from the experience with other oncogenic drivers in NSCLC, the early introduction of these next-generation TKIs, such as repotrectinib, into the treatment paradigm for ROS1-rearranged NSCLC may enhance survival outcomes. Agents like repotrectinib are emerging as preferred first-line options due to their efficacy in overcoming emergent resistance mutations and their robust intracranial activity. However, challenges persist, particularly TRK-mediated neurotoxicity which can limit treatment tolerance and dose intensity. Continued investigation into newer-generation ROS1 TKIs and combination therapies is essential to further improve efficacy and minimize adverse effects. Future agents with refined selectivity profiles for ROS1 and reduced off-target TRK toxicity, such as zidesamtinib, are under development and may provide more effective and tolerable treatment options for patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC.
