# ALPK3 Cardiomyopathy: Integrative Review With Systematic Variant Curation, Mechanisms, and Translation.
**Authors:** Chang CW, Wang L, Chen Z, Bogomolovas J, Chen J
**Journal:** Circ Genom Precis Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1161/CIRCGEN.125.005368](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCGEN.125.005368)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in ALPK3 (α-protein kinase 3), an atypical α‑kinase acting as a sarcomeric M-band scaffold, cause cardiomyopathy with severity linked to zygosity. We present a comprehensive review with systematic curation of peer-reviewed clinical and experimental reports through June 9, 2025, encompassing 156 patient-level variants and all published preclinical models. Biallelic loss-of-function variants lead to severe, often lethal cardiomyopathy with prenatal or early onset presentation and extracardiac involvement. Heterozygous protein-truncating variants, defined as nonsense or frameshift (resulting from insertion/deletion events or splicing mutations), explain ≈1% to 4% of adult hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, often with apical/septal hypertrophy, right ventricular involvement, fibrosis, and risk of progression. ALPK3 lacks catalytic activity and maintains sarcomeric proteostasis by scaffolding MYOMs (myomesins), MuRF (muscle ring-finger protein) E3 ligases, and SQSTM1 (sequestosome-1)/p62. Loss of this scaffolding function displaces MYOMs, drives thick‑filament protein aggregation, and precipitates severe contractile dysfunction in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes and multiple mouse models. Therapeutic proof‑of‑concept has now been achieved on 2 fronts: (1) pharmacological correction of sarcomeric hypercontractility with the myosin inhibitor mavacamten and (2) durable phenotypic rescue in global knockout mice using an adeno-associated virus-delivered miniALPK3 gene‑replacement construct. Together, these data position ALPK3 cardiomyopathy as a compelling target for precision medicine. Early genetic diagnosis, genotype-tailored surveillance, and focused development of gene-replacement or editing strategies, potentially combined with modulators of the ALPK3-MuRF proteostatic axis, offer a realistic path to disease-modifying therapy for this once enigmatic condition.

Cardiomyopathies represent a diverse group of myocardial disorders characterized by structural and functional abnormalities of the cardiac muscle, frequently leading to heart failure and sudden cardiac death.1,2 Although inherited mutations in sarcomeric and related genes account for many cases, a considerable number of patients with early-onset or familial disease still lack a definitive molecular diagnosis, which highlights the need to uncover additional genes and mechanisms underlying cardiomyopathy.3 Recently, α-Protein Kinase 3 (ALPK3) has emerged as a critical gene associated with cardiomyopathies, highlighted by its unique localization at the sarcomeric M-band and its role as a potential scaffolding pseudokinase.4–6 Pathogenic ALPK3 variants cause a spectrum of zygosity-dependent clinical presentations: biallelic loss-of-function mutations result in severe, early-onset dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM),7 while heterozygous mutations typically lead to adult-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).8–12 Despite its clinical importance, significant gaps remain in understanding ALPK3’s precise molecular functions, genotype-phenotype correlations, and potential therapeutic strategies. To provide a comprehensive foundation for this review, we systematically curated all peer-reviewed clinical and experimental reports describing ALPK3 variants, disease models, or mechanistic insights. The search was limited to PubMed (inception–June 9, 2025); gray literature was not searched and no automation tools were used. Data were abstracted at the patient/variant level (genotype/zygosity, clinical phenotype, age at diagnosis, and key electrocardiography (ECG) / magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings including fibrosis; extracardiac features; outcomes) and, for experimental studies, at the model level (model type and principal cardiac phenotypes). Diagnostic labels are reported verbatim from the source literature to preserve fidelity. Where primary reports used “left ventricular non-compaction cardiomyopathy (LVNC),” we retain that term. In line with contemporary imaging nosology, this trabecular morphology is better described as excessive/hypertrabeculation and does not by itself define a distinct cardiomyopathy entity.13 This review synthesizes the current knowledge on ALPK3 cardiomyopathy, integrates findings from human genetic studies as well as insights from animal and cellular models, and explores promising therapeutic avenues, thereby providing a roadmap toward precision medicine for the affected individuals.

ALPK3 variants have been linked to a broad range of cardiomyopathy phenotypes, predominantly HCM,8,11 DCM,14 and occasionally LVNC14 and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM). 8 These conditions have been described across diverse populations and ethnic backgrounds, underscoring ALPK3’s global relevance in both pediatric and adult cardiomyopathy cohorts.7–10,14–35 Clinical severity varies extensively, from asymptomatic carriers to severe cases requiring heart transplantation, and in some instances, intrauterine or perinatal death.7,14,25 To contextualize this phenotypic and genetic spectrum, we systematically reviewed all published clinical and experimental studies reporting ALPK3 variants and cardiomyopathy. The resulting dataset includes 156 cardiomyopathy-associated variants from primary studies, alongside 2,757 ClinVar submissions, summarized in Figure 1. Although single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) are the most common overall, only ~4 % are classified as pathogenic or linked to actual patient cases, whereas nearly one-quarter of insertion/deletion events (indels) are disease-associated, with more than 60 % resulting in frameshift-mediated truncations. This enrichment underscores the predominance of truncating variation—defined as nonsense or frameshift (resulting from indels or splicing mutations)—in ALPK3-linked diseases. The zygosity of the variants strongly influences the clinical presentation and prognosis, distinguishing severe biallelic cardiomyopathy from heterozygous forms with variable clinical severity (Table 1) and is discussed in detail below.

Our analysis identified 42 patients with biallelic ALPK3 variants from previously published reports, summarized in Table 1 and Table S1. The cohort comprised 19 males, 18 females, and 5 patients whose sex was not specified; genotypically, there were 21 homozygotes (50%) and 21 compound heterozygotes (50%). Most patients carried two protein-truncating variants (PTVs), defined as nonsense or frameshift changes caused by indels or splicing mutations. Specifically, 29 patients (69%) carried two PTVs, 11 (26%) carried one PTV and one missense variant, and 2 (5%) carried two missense variants. Cardiomyopathy in patients with biallelic ALPK3 variants typically manifested early in life: 4 patients (10%) were diagnosed prenatally, 8 patients (19%) neonatally, and 8 patients (19%) within the first year of birth, highlighting early disease onset. At initial presentation, cardiomyopathy phenotypes were diverse: 22 patients (52.4%) had HCM, 13 patients (30.9%) presented with DCM, 6 patients (14.3%) had a mixed phenotype (DCM/HCM/LVNC), and 1 patient (2.4%) showed isolated LVNC. Disease morphology notably evolved over time; among the 13 patients initially diagnosed with only DCM, 10 (77%) transitioned to HCM, typically within the first year of follow-up. This transition predominantly involved patients carrying two truncating alleles. Some exceptions were observed, including 2 patients who died at birth or shortly thereafter and one older patient diagnosed at age 31 who carried a truncating and a non-truncating allele. These cases suggest that the transition from DCM to HCM may specifically characterize severe, fully truncating biallelic ALPK3 cardiomyopathy. Conversely, 3 of the 20 initially diagnosed HCM cases (15%) subsequently progressed (“burned out”) to a dilated, hypokinetic cardiomyopathy phenotype in adolescence or early adulthood. This pattern of progression from HCM to a dilated phenotype aligns with a classic remodeling trajectory commonly observed in various forms of severe genetic HCM.36

ECG abnormalities were frequent but atypical for classical HCM in patients with biallelic ALPK3 mutations. A prolonged QTc interval was observed in 18 of 28 patients with available ECGs (64%), far exceeding the 10–15% reported in large HCM series,37 and was present in both homozygous and compound-heterozygous genotypes, indicating a pronounced repolarization defect in ALPK3 cardiomyopathy. Ten patients (24%) were referred for implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implantation, including five females, four males, and one of unspecified sex. Notably, no left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction was reported in any patient, whereas such obstruction is observed in over half of classical HCM cohorts.38 Cardiac MRI revealed late gadolinium enhancement in 6 of 14 imaged patients (43%), a prevalence comparable to the 40–62% fibrosis rates reported in large pediatric HCM meta-analysis.39 These findings highlight a distinct electrocardiographic and structural profile in biallelic ALPK3 cardiomyopathy.

In addition to cardiomyopathy, extracardiac abnormalities were common (52%; 22 patients) and characteristic in patients with biallelic ALPK3 variants, including craniofacial dysmorphisms, cleft palate, skeletal muscle hypotonia, joint contractures, pterygia, scoliosis, short stature, and other musculoskeletal anomalies, supporting the concept of a distinctive “cardio-cranio-skeletal” syndrome associated with biallelic ALPK3 variants. This multisystem pattern is biologically plausible, because ALPK3 expression is not confined to the heart: robust expression has been documented in developing and adult skeletal muscle as well as in myocardium, providing a mechanistic rationale for the skeletal-muscle and craniofacial manifestations observed in these patients.

The overall clinical burden was substantial for patients with biallelic ALPK3 variants: 5 patients (11.9%) died (all carried PTVs), 5 (11.9%) were referred for heart transplantation, and 4 (9.5%) underwent heart transplantation at ages 4, 16, 28, and 33 years, emphasizing the high mortality and morbidity risk. Disease severity clearly correlated with truncation burden: patients carrying two truncating alleles (29 of 42 patients, 69%) presented symptoms the earliest, with a median age at diagnosis of 0.5 year, and accounted for 4 of the 5 observed deaths (14%), as well as heart transplantations at ages of 16 and 28 years. In contrast, patients harboring at least one missense or splice-altering allele (13 patients, 31%) developed symptoms later, with a median age of approximately 12 months (ranging from in-utero to 53 years). Despite this delayed onset, the presence of a single hypomorphic allele did not preclude severe outcomes: patients in this group still experienced progressive heart failure, which requires heart transplantation (ages 4 and 33 years), and one patient with a truncating plus a missense mutation genotype died at the age of 6.

Overall, biallelic ALPK3 variants lead to cardiomyopathy with a clear truncation-dose effect: individuals with two truncating alleles present with the earliest and most severe disease, while those with at least one non-truncating allele display more variable phenotypes, as some of the non-truncating alleles may still be partially functional. The clinical presentation is severe but atypical for classical HCM, characterized in some cases by a distinct DCM-to-HCM remodeling pattern, frequent QT prolongation, and pediatric ICD implantation, while notably lacking LVOT obstruction. Approximately half of the patients also exhibit skeletal muscle and craniofacial anomalies, consistent with ALPK3’s expression beyond the heart.

In contrast to the familial case reports that predominantly characterize biallelic ALPK3 cardiomyopathy, data supporting the pathogenicity of heterozygous ALPK3 mutations largely originates from cohort studies. Seven large, ethnically diverse sequencing cohorts of cardiomyopathy,8,9,14,29,30,40,41 including a total of 27,147 HCM probands from North America, Europe, and East Asia (Table 2), were re-analyzed using broad cardiomyopathy gene panels or whole-exome/whole-genome sequencing (WES/WGS). Each cohort study also reported background frequencies of ALPK3 variants based on population controls (> 75,000 individuals from gnomAD or matched local datasets). Across these cohorts, a total of 268 ALPK3 variants were detected in HCM probands, comprising 169 PTVs (63%) and 99 non-truncating variants (37%). This translates to a pooled prevalence of 0.62% for truncations, 0.36% for non-truncations, and an overall variance prevalence of 0.99%. These figures represent roughly a 6- to 10-fold enrichment of PTVs compared to population controls, in which PTVs consistently had a frequency of approximately 0.10 % (range 0.09–0.14%), whereas missense and small in-frame variants appeared at a frequency of ~1–3 %. Because PTVs were significantly enriched in HCM cases compared to controls, whereas non-truncating variants were not, this strongly suggests that PTVs have higher pathogenic potential. Notably, in five of the seven cohorts, ALPK3 PTVs were the second or third most frequently identified novel variants after re-analysis of cases previously missed by gene panels, typically ranking after cryptic-splice MYBPC3 variants, 14 and in the French cohort, after FLNC variants. 9 In parallel, the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) recently classified autosomal dominant ALPK3tv as having “strong” clinical validity for HCM.42 Therefore, ALPK3 represents the most frequent sarcomere-linked genetic cause identified when unresolved HCM cases undergo deeper genetic analysis.

To conduct an in-depth analysis, we combined 128 heterozygous patients identified through three large HCM cohort sequencing studies8,9,14 with an additional 39 cases derived from smaller pedigrees or single-center case series, which provided more detailed clinical annotations and occasional segregation data,7,10,21,23,26,31,32,34 resulting in a final cohort of 167 patients (Table 2). This cohort comprised 40 males (24 %), 21 females (13 %), and 106 individuals (63 %) whose sex was unspecified. The variant spectrum was heavily skewed toward truncating mutations, with 131 patients (78.4 %) harboring unequivocally truncating alleles, including nonsense, frameshift, or canonical splice-site (±2 bp) variants. Non-truncating missense or small in-frame indels were less common, observed in 35 patients (21 %). One variant affecting the translation initiation site remained unclassified following re-annotation.

In contrast to the biallelic ALPK3 cohort, disease onset among heterozygous carriers was distinctly later. No prenatal, neonatal, or infant-onset cases were reported. Instead, the vast majority (157 individuals, 94%) were diagnosed after the first year of life (no data on disease onset was reported for the remaining 10 patients). Most diagnoses occurred in mid-adulthood, with a median age at diagnosis of 52 years (IQR 39–64).

The cardiomyopathy phenotype at initial diagnosis was notably homogeneous. HCM was overwhelmingly predominant, observed in 142 patients (85%). Among them, detailed imaging data based on echocardiography and/or cardiac MRI were available for 88 cases, revealing specific patterns of hypertrophy: classic asymmetric septal hypertrophy in 20 patients (23%), apical hypertrophy in 17 patients (19%), concentric or neutral geometry in 14 patients (16%), and other, mixed, or unspecified hypertrophy patterns in 37 patients (42%). Other cardiomyopathy forms were considerably rarer: DCM in 12 patients (7%), mixed phenotypes (DCM/HCM/LVNC) in 5 patients (3%), isolated LVNC in 3 patients (2%), and ACM in 5 patients (3%). Among the 49 patients with available follow-up data (median duration of 5.7 years; range 1–24 years), no cases of phenotypic transition (e.g., DCM to HCM or HCM “burn-out” to DCM) were documented, indicating stable morphology over the reported observation periods. Among the 94 patients (including both HCM and non-HCM subtypes) who underwent cardiac imaging, only three (3%) had mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF; 40% ≤ LVEF < 50%) and another three (3%) had reduced LVEF (< 40%), consistent with predominantly preserved systolic function in this HCM-dominant cohort. ECG data were available for 80 patients. Prolonged QTc (>460 ms) was observed in 4 patients (5%). Atrial fibrillation was documented in 10 patients (12.5%), and ventricular tachycardia was also documented in 10 patients (12.5%). Correspondingly, 16 patients (10%) were referred for ICD implantation, including one patient for cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D). Extracardiac manifestations were exceptionally rare among mono-allelic carriers, noted in just 2 patients (1.2%), underscoring the limited extracardiac involvement compared to biallelic ALPK3 deficiency. Clinical outcomes were notably more favorable than those observed in biallelic disease. Mortality was relatively low, with 9 deaths (5.4%) occurring at a median age of 49 years. Five patients (3%) were referred for heart transplantation, three of whom (1.8%) had already undergone the procedure, resulting in transplant-free survival rates exceeding 92%.

In summary, mono-allelic ALPK3 PTVs produce a late-onset cardiomyopathy phenotype dominated by hypertrophic morphology, with preserved systolic function in most cases, infrequent LVOT obstruction, and minimal extracardiac manifestations. These characteristics markedly differ from the early-onset, multisystem phenotype of biallelic ALPK3 deficiency, highlighting a clear gene-dosage effect. Despite overall favorable outcomes, the substantial myocardial fibrosis identified in many patients indicates a need for vigilant long-term follow-up to monitor arrhythmic risk and potential disease progression.

The gene encoding ALPK3 was first identified in a differential-display screen aiming at uncovering the regulators of cardiomyocyte differentiation and was first named Myocytic Induction/Differentiation Originator (MIDORI).43 Northern blot and whole-mount in situ hybridization studies from this initial work revealed a robust expression pattern restricted predominantly to cardiac and skeletal muscle, implicating a key role of ALPK3 in striated muscle biology. Building on these findings, our recent study used CRISPR-mediated insertion of a 3×FLAG-HA epitope at the endogenous mouse Alpk3 locus, confirming at the protein level that ALPK3 is expressed exclusively in striated muscles with negligible expression elsewhere.44 This highly restricted expression provides a clear rationale for why pathogenic mutations predominantly manifest clinically as cardiomyopathy with concomitant skeletal muscle pathologies.

In humans, ALPK3 is located on chromosome 15q25.2,7,8 comprising 14 exons that encode a 201-kDa protein with 1705 amino acids. In mice, Alpk3 is located on Chromosome 7, also composed of 14 exons that encode a 180-kDa protein with 1680 amino acids. Both human and mouse ALPK3 proteins contain three folded domains, two immunoglobulin (Ig)-like domains and a C-terminal α-kinase domain, 4 while the remaining central region is predicted to be intrinsically disordered by in silico disorder prediction tools such as IUPred345 and PrDOS.46 With the overall amino acid sequence identity of 71% and 77% similarity between human and mouse ALPK3, the sequence conservation is notably higher within the structured domains, and is high in central intrinsically disordered region of the protein (Figure 1A). The presence of a C-terminal α-kinase domain classifies ALPK3 within the α-kinase family, a group of atypical protein kinases that is structurally and evolutionarily distinct from conventional serine/threonine kinases.47,48

Notably, despite containing a kinase domain, ALPK3 is primarily considered a pseudokinase due to its limited catalytic activity. Structural modeling, sequence comparisons, and global phosphoproteomic analyses strongly argue against ALPK3 possessing intrinsic catalytic activity.4,6 Central to kinase catalytic activity is a highly conserved catalytic lysine residue that coordinates ATP binding and transfer of the gamma phosphate group.49 Interestingly, while ALPK3 retains this conserved catalytic lysine (K1420), the presence of this residue alone is essential but insufficient to confer enzymatic activity. A similar scenario is observed for the titin kinase domain, another M-band localized pseudokinase. Titin’s kinase domain maintains an intact catalytic lysine but does not display physiologically meaningful kinase activity in vivo; instead, it primarily functions as a mechanically responsive scaffold recruiting proteostatic proteins, such as Sequestosome-1 (SQSTM1/p62) and the Muscle Ring-Finger Protein (MuRF) E3 ligase family members.50,51 To conclusively test whether ALPK3’s catalytic activity might have subtle physiological roles, we generated knock-in mice bearing a kinase-dead mutation (K1420R), abolishing the potential ATP processing at the conserved lysine,6 a strategy successfully used to inactivate the closely related atypical α-kinase transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M member 7 (TRPM7).52 In contrast to ALPK3 knockout animals, which have severely impaired cardiac function, kinase-dead ALPK3 mice exhibit completely normal cardiac morphology and function. This finding further supports the interpretation that ALPK3, although contains structural hallmarks of a kinase domain, is catalytically inactive in vivo and functions primarily as a scaffold at the M-band. While an in vitro study by McNamara et al.5 suggested weak kinase activity for recombinant ALPK3, potential technical limitations such as the possibility of contaminating kinases cannot be fully excluded, echoing earlier debates surrounding the kinase activity of titin’s kinase domain.51

Within cardiomyocytes, several independent studies including ours determined that ALPK3 localizes mainly to the sarcomeric M-band and to a lesser extent sarcomere-linked perinuclear space. 4,5,44 Mechanistic studies reveal that in cardiomyocytes, ALPK3 directly binds to key M-band proteins, including the myomesin isoforms myomesin 1 (MYOM1) and myomesin 2 (MYOM2), which are critical for maintaining sarcomere integrity. Moreover, ALPK3 interacts with the muscle-specific E3 ubiquitin ligases MuRF1 and MuRF2, as well as with the autophagy adaptor protein SQSTM1/p62, positioning it centrally within the proteostasis networks. Loss of ALPK3 function, either through genetic deletion in mice or via pathogenic mutations in patient-derived cardiomyocytes, potentially disrupts this scaffolding function of ALPK3. Consequently, MYOM1 and MYOM2 become displaced from their native M-band positions, accumulating in ectopic aggregates, accompanied by abnormal accumulation of thick-filament proteins such as myosin heavy chain 6 (MYH6) and MYH7, and disruption of normal sarcomere architecture.4,44 Indeed, patient-derived or engineered induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) harboring ALPK3 mutations display severely disorganized sarcomeric structures, mislocalized myomesins, disrupted calcium transients, prolonged action potentials, and compromised contractile performance.4,5,15 Global Alpk3-knockout mice develop severe, progressive dilated cardiomyopathy, with a subset of those animals undergoing transition into cardiac hypertrophy, which recapitulates the DCM-to-HCM transition observed in patients carrying biallelic ALPK3 truncations. These changes are accompanied by pronounced M-band disorganization with accumulation of thick-filament proteins.4,44,48

Additionally, studies using mouse models have revealed the timing and dosage requirement for ALPK3 in cardiac function. While global Alpk3 knockout mice develop severe, progressive dilated cardiomyopathy shortly after birth, our study using inducible cardiac-specific knockout in adult animals shows rapidly severe systolic dysfunction and chamber dilation after ALPK3 loss, underscoring ALPK3’s continuing requirement beyond the neonatal stage.44 Furthermore, while mice carrying homozygous truncating ALPK3 mutations (such as W1538X and K201X knock-ins) closely phenocopy human recessive ALPK3 cardiomyopathy, heterozygous mice only manifest cardiomyopathy under stress, providing strong evidence of haploinsufficiency as the pathogenic mechanism underlying the adult-onset form (murine models and their key phenotypes summarized in Table 3).

Collectively, the cellular, in vivo, and biochemical studies converge to establish ALPK3 as a pseudokinase whose primary function is scaffolding proteins critical for the structural maintenance and proteostasis at M-band. Loss of this scaffolding function destabilizes sarcomere integrity and perturbs intracellular signaling, ultimately triggering the spectrum of ALPK3-associated cardiomyopathies observed clinically. The colocalization of ALPK3 and MuRF proteins at the sarcomeric M-band, as evidenced by multiple independent studies, and the similar phenotypes resulted from their genetic deletion, including abnormal thick filament protein accumulation, sarcomere disorganization, and severe cardiomyopathy, strongly indicate a functional connection between ALPK3 and MuRFs.53,54 This functional partnership appears essential for maintaining cardiac proteostasis, as disruption of either partner leads to comparable pathological remodeling, with MuRFs increasingly recognized as HCM-associated genes themselves,55,56 suggesting they operate within a unified regulatory pathway critical for cardiomyocyte structural integrity and function.

ALPK3 cardiomyopathy is a genetically defined disorder for which no targeted therapies have been approved to date. Current clinical management follows standard guidelines for heart failure57 or HCM,58 focusing on symptom management and risk mitigation rather than addressing the underlying molecular defect. Pharmacological treatments commonly employed include beta-blockers and non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, aiming at reducing myocardial oxygen demand and alleviating symptoms.

Given the progressive nature of ALPK3 cardiomyopathy and the limitations of current therapies, pharmacological modulation of disease mechanisms has been explored as a potential strategy. Leinhos et al. investigated the use of mavacamten, a myosin ATPase inhibitor approved for obstructive HCM, in a knock-in mouse model carrying the patient-derived K201X truncation.41 Although homozygous animals exhibited contractile dysfunction, they did not develop overt ventricular wall thickening, and treatment was restricted to ex vivo isolated cardiomyocytes. In this setting, mavacamten partially corrected key pathological features, including abnormal sarcomere length, elevated diastolic calcium levels, and delayed relaxation, in both heterozygous and homozygous cells. As expected, based on its mechanism of action, mavacamten also reduced fractional shortening. While these results suggest that ALPK3-deficient cardiomyocytes retain pharmacological responsiveness toward mavacamten, the absence of a clear in vivo HCM phenotype in the experimental model and the lack of systemic treatment data limit conclusions regarding its therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, ALPK3 phenotypes only partially overlap with the current mavacamten indications. In heterozygous ALPK3 PTVs, hypertrophy is typically apical or concentric, LVOT obstruction is uncommon (~4% overall), and systolic function is preserved in most. In biallelic Alpk3 disease, early-onset DCM is common at presentation, 77% of those cases transition to HCM yet often remain systolically impaired,7,14 and 15% of initial HCM cases subsequently “burn out” to DCM. Thus, on the one hand, additional negative inotropy may be harmful in hypokinetic states, aligning with reversible EF reductions seen in a subset of patients receiving mavacamten for non-obstructive HCM (MAVERICK-HCM).59 Conversely, ALPK3K201X cardiomyocytes show reduced SRX (super-relaxed state) myosin occupancy, a “microscopic” hypercontractility signature in vitro.41 Because diminished SRX is a recognized HCM mechanism and mavacamten increases SRX,60 SRX restoration is a plausible therapeutic lever for ALPK3 as well (and has benefited non-HCM myosinopathies such as Laing distal myopathy with MYK-581).61,62 In ALPK3K201X cardiomyocytes, while ex vivo mavacamten partly corrected diastolic Ca2+ and relaxation, it also reduced fractional shortening. Thus, without in-vivo data, the net clinical effect in ALPK3 remains uncertain.41 Altogether, while ALPK3-deficient cardiomyocytes are pharmacologically responsive to myosin inhibition such as mavacamten at the cellular level in vitro, clinical translation should be strictly phenotype-driven. This is potentially valuable for the minority of patients with clear hypercontractility/obstruction, but it risks worsening pump function in the more prevalent hypokinetic forms, underscoring the need for stage- and physiology-targeted studies with careful systolic-function monitoring.

A more direct and disease-modifying approach is gene replacement therapy. Analysis of ALPK3 pathogenic variants reveals that over 70% are truncating, strongly supporting haploinsufficiency as the principal disease driver. Thus, replacing the missing ALPK3 scaffold represents an especially attractive therapeutic strategy. However, the full-length ALPK3 coding region (5040bp for mouse ALPK3 and 5115bp for human ALPK3) exceeds the packaging capacity of adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors. In silico mapping (Fig. 1A) shows that the protein’s central portion is an intrinsically disordered, less conserved stretch whose length varies widely across vertebrates and contains no recognized functional motifs. Deleting part of this flexible segment produces a 3.9 kb “miniALPK3” that preserves all structured domains, including the two immunoglobulin-like repeats and the C-terminal α-kinase fold, while fitting comfortably within the AAV packaging limit and enabling efficient, single-vector delivery. We validated this construct in vivo by generating knock-in mice expressing only miniALPK3. These mice demonstrated normal cardiac structure and function up to 15 months, confirming the deleted region is functionally dispensable. Subsequent therapeutic evaluation involved administering a MyoAAV-miniALPK3 vector under a cardiac-specific promoter to neonatal Alpk3 global knockout mice. Treated mice showed precise restoration of ALPK3 localization to the M-band, normalization of cardiac dimensions and contractility, suppression of pathological fetal gene and fibrosis markers, and significantly improved survival beyond 12 months compared to untreated controls, which experienced early mortality.63

Together, these preclinical studies highlight that ALPK3 cardiomyopathy is amenable to therapeutic intervention. Mavacamten demonstrates the potential to correct sarcomere and calcium-handling defects at the cellular level, whereas miniALPK3 gene therapy addresses the underlying protein deficiency, offering functional and survival benefits in vivo. These findings lay the foundation for future translation of targeted therapies in ALPK3-related cardiomyopathy.

Recent advances have firmly established ALPK3 variants as a genetic cause of cardiomyopathies, with both biallelic and monoallelic mutations giving rise to distinct clinical phenotypes. Biallelic loss-of-function mutations result in an aggressive, early-onset, syndromic form of cardiomyopathy, often marked by rapid deterioration and poor prognosis. Notably, 77% of surviving patients with biallelic ALPK3 mutations who initially present with a DCM phenotype later develop HCM, making this a distinctive pathological hallmark of ALPK3 cardiomyopathy. Reports of DCM-to-HCM transition outside the ALPK3 context are scarce, and the opposite trajectory—progressing from HCM to a “burned-out,” hypokinetic DCM phenotype is more common among cardiomyopathies.64,65 Documented exceptions are exceedingly rare; for instance, a homozygous MYBPC3 in-frame deletion was reported to cause childhood-onset DCM that later evolved into combined hypertrophic and dilated features.66 Another report described a woman whose DCM, upon guideline-directed medical therapy, resulted in normalized EF and an apical-variant HCM phenotype;67 a genetic cause was suspected but no testing was performed. The scarcity of such reports underscores the singularity of the ALPK3 DCM-to-HCM sequence. Mechanistically, this biphasic remodeling is unlikely to be a simple compensatory response and instead reflects an intrinsic consequence of ALPK3 loss. Notably, in vivo models show that ALPK3 ablation leads to primary cardiomyopathy in both neonatal and adult hearts,44 with the DCM-to-HCM progression reproduced only when ALPK3 is absent from early development.44 If this transition were merely a developmental effect, and ALPK3 function were dispensable in the mature heart, then inducible adult ALPK3 knockout would not produce a phenotype—yet it does, indicating an ongoing requirement for ALPK3 beyond development.

A plausible explanation for this timing comes from the developmental switch in cardiomyocyte growth patterns. In humans, cardiomyocyte proliferation predominates during prenatal and early postnatal growth, with cell-cycle withdrawal largely complete by birth and hypertrophic growth dominating thereafter.68 In mice, this proliferative-to-hypertrophic switch unfolds rapidly within the first postnatal week, coinciding with binucleation and permanent cell-cycle exit.69 One of the main triggers of this shift is the abrupt postnatal rise in oxygen tension, which induces cell-cycle arrest via a DNA damage response. 70 Concurrently, cardiomyocytes undergo a major metabolic transition, from glycolytic to mitochondrial oxidative metabolism,71 and experience a sharp increase in biomechanical load as systemic circulation takes over.

In the context of ALPK3 deficiency, our global knockout (gKO) mouse studies show marked accumulation of thick-filament proteins in hearts, accompanied by M-band disorganization.4,5,44 During the rapid proliferative stage, the accelerated rate of sarcomere biogenesis may not allow sufficient time for these thick-filament components to be properly integrated into stable M-band architecture, resulting in structurally weak myofibrils and early DCM. After birth, when proliferation ceases and hypertrophy becomes the primary growth mode, sarcomere assembly is slower and more load-driven, potentially allowing more efficient incorporation of existing thick-filament proteins. In some individuals, this may partially restore myofibrillar order and permit the development of hypertrophy, producing the observed DCM-to-HCM transition. In others, persistent proteostatic failure may prevent effective assembly despite the slower postnatal growth tempo, locking the heart into a dilated, hypocontractile state.

A better understanding of the molecular basis underlying this DCM-to-HCM transition in ALPK3 associated cardiomyopathies could yield important insights into cardiomyocyte plasticity, sarcomeric remodeling, and metabolic adaptation, and may reveal new targets for stage-specific therapeutic intervention.

In contrast, heterozygous PTVs typically lead to adult-onset HCM with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, underscoring ALPK3’s essential role in sustaining cardiac function beyond development. The marked phenotypic heterogeneity among individuals with ALPK3 variants strongly suggests the involvement of genetic modifiers, epigenetic factors, or environmental influences that shape disease severity and progression. Identifying these modifiers could open up new avenues for risk stratification and personalized therapy.

Mechanistic studies have revealed that ALPK3 functions as a scaffolding protein localized to the M-band of cardiomyocytes, orchestrating sarcomeric proteostasis through interactions with MuRF ligases and SQSTM1. Investigating the reciprocal regulation between ALPK3 and MuRF ligases may uncover a feedback mechanism essential for maintaining sarcomere homeostasis under mechanical or metabolic stress. These insights position ALPK3 at a central node linking structural integrity with dynamic sarcomere turnover, making it an appealing candidate for targeted therapeutic intervention.

Beyond its role in the heart, ALPK3 is also highly expressed in skeletal muscle, and musculoskeletal involvement is commonly observed in severe cases, suggesting broader yet underexplored functions in striated muscle. Investigating ALPK3’s roles in non-cardiomyocytes may clarify the molecular basis of its extracardiac manifestations and inform comprehensive clinical management.

Looking ahead, therapies such as mavacamten, which modulates myosin-actin cross-bridge cycling, and miniALPK3 gene therapy show early promise in preclinical settings. However, their efficacy and safety in ALPK3-related cardiomyopathy in humans remain to be validated. In parallel, emerging approaches targeting proteostasis and other yet unidentified pathways underlying ALPK3 cardiomyopathy and related diseases in other tissues could provide additional therapeutic options. Advanced gene-editing technologies will also be critical for developing personalized therapeutic strategies for ALPK3 disease.
