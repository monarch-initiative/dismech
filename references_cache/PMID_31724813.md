PURPOSE: We conducted a phase 1/2 trial of the poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1/2
inhibitor talazoparib in combination with low-dose temozolomide (TMZ) to
determine the dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D),
and pharmacokinetics of this combination in children with recurrent/refractory
solid tumors; and to explore clinical activity in Ewing sarcoma (EWS)
(NCT02116777).
METHODS: Talazoparib (400-600 µg/m2 /dose, maximum daily dose 800-1000 µg) was
administered q.d. or b.i.d. orally on day 1 followed by q.d. dosing concomitant
with q.d. dosing of oral TMZ (20-55 mg/m2 /day) on days 2 to 6, every 28 days.
RESULTS: Forty patients, aged 4 to 25 years, were enrolled. Talazoparib was
increased to 600 µg/m2 /dose b.i.d. on day 1, and q.d. thereafter, with 20 mg/m2
/day of TMZ, without DLTs. TMZ was subsequently increased, during which
dose-limiting neutropenia and thrombocytopenia occurred in two of three subjects
at 55 mg/m2 /day, two of six subjects at 40 mg/m2 /day, and one of six subjects
at 30 mg/m2 /day. During dose-finding, two of five EWS and four of 25 non-EWS
subjects experienced prolonged stable disease (SD), and one subject with
malignant glioma experienced a partial response. In phase 2, 0 of 10 EWS
subjects experienced an objective response; two experienced prolonged SD.
CONCLUSIONS: Talazoparib and low-dose TMZ are tolerated in children with
recurrent/refractory solid tumors. Reversible neutropenia and thrombocytopenia
were dose limiting. The RP2D is talazoparib 600 µg/m2 b.i.d. on day 1 followed
by 600 µg/m2 q.d. on days 2 to 6 (daily maximum 1000 µg) in combination with
temozolomide 30 mg/m2 /day on days 2 to 6. Antitumor activity was not observed
in EWS, and limited antitumor activity was observed in central nervous system
tumors.

We conducted a phase 1/2 trial of the poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1/2 inhibitor talazoparib in combination with low-dose temozolomide (TMZ) to determine the dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D), and pharmacokinetics of this combination in children with recurrent/refractory solid tumors; and to explore clinical activity in Ewing sarcoma (EWS) (NCT02116777).

Talazoparib (400–600 mcg/m2/dose, maximum daily dose 800–1000 mcg) was administered QD or BID orally on day 1 followed by QD dosing concomitant with QD dosing of oral TMZ (20–55 mg/m2/day) on days 2–6, every 28 days.

Forty patients, aged 4–25 years were enrolled. Talazoparib was increased to 600 mcg/m2/dose BID on day 1, and QD thereafter, with 20 mg/m2/day of TMZ, without DLTs. TMZ was subsequently increased during which dose-limiting neutropenia and thrombocytopenia occurred in 2/3 subjects at 55 mg/m2/day, 2/6 subjects at 40 mg/m2/day and 1/6 subjects at 30 mg/m2/day. During dose-finding 2/5 EWS and 4/25 non-EWS subjects experienced prolonged stable disease (SD) and one subject with malignant glioma experienced a partial response. In phase 2, 0/10 EWS subjects experienced an objective response; two experienced prolonged SD.

Talazoparib and low-dose TMZ are tolerated in children with recurrent/refractory solid tumors. Reversible neutropenia and thrombocytopenia were dose-limiting. The RP2D is talazoparib 600 mcg/m2BID on day 1 followed by 600 mcg/m2QD on days 2–6 (daily maximum 1000 mcg) in combination with temozolomide 30 mg/m2/day on days 2–6. Antitumor activity was not observed in EWS and limited antitumor activity was observed in central nervous system tumors.

Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs), a family of DNA repair enzymes, function through recruitment and activation of proteins that utilize the base excision repair (BER) pathway.1PARP inhibitors (PARPis) bind to the catalytic domain of PARP-1 and/or PARP-2 leading to replication fork damage and cellular apoptosis.2Talazoparib (PF-06944076, BMN-673) is a potent, specific inhibitor of PARPs 1/2 with an improved therapeutic index relative to other PARPis.2,3

PARPi activity in tumors with defects in the homologous recombination (HR) pathway, including tumors withBRCA1/24,5andPTENaberrations, is attributed to blockade of salvage repair pathways, including BER, resulting in cellular apoptosis through synthetic lethality.6In cancers with translocations involving the ETS family of transcription factors includingEWS-FLI1andEWS-ERGEwing sarcoma (EWS), PARP-1 is a co-factor of transcriptional regulation and as such, EWS cell lines have been shown to be exquisitely sensitive to olaparib, a first-generation PARPi.7–9

Combining PARPis with drugs or radiation that create single-strand DNA breaks10may increase the therapeutic utility of PARP inhibition. Temozolomide (TMZ), an alkylating agent, creates single-strand DNA breaks that are normally repaired through the BER pathway.11,12Pediatric Preclinical Testing Program (PPTP) studies demonstrated synergy between talazoparib and TMZ and resultant antitumor activity in EWS, glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) and Wilms tumor murine xenograft models when the combination was given for five days in a 28-day cycle.13

We conducted a dose-finding trial of talazoparib in combination with TMZ in children and adolescents with refractory or recurrent malignancies followed by a phase 2 trial in patients with EWS and peripheral neuroectodermal tumor (PNET). The primary objectives were to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and/or recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) of talazoparib in combination with TMZ; describe the toxicities and pharmacokinetics of this combination; and assess the antitumor activity in patients with recurrent/refractory EWS/PNET.

The study was conducted by the Children’s Oncology Group Phase 1/Pilot Consortium. Patients aged 1 to 21 years with recurrent or refractory solid tumors were eligible for the dose-finding (phase 1) component. Patients < 30 years old with measurable recurrent or refractory EWS/PNET were eligible for the phase 2 component. It should be noted that patients who carried a diagnosis of “Ewing-like” tumors – a small round blue cell tumor which shares many clinical, morphological and molecular characteristics with EWS but lacks the pathognomonicEWS-ETSfusions14– were excluded from this cohort as the lack of these fusions eliminated a major theoretical mechanism of PARPi activity. Subjects were required to be recovered from the acute toxic effects of prior therapy, have a performance score (Lansky/Karnofsky) of ≥ 50, have an absolute neutrophil count ≥ 1,000/mm3and platelets ≥ 100,000/mm3, adequate renal and hepatic function based on age, and cardiac function (shortening fraction ≥ 27% or ejection fraction ≥ 50%). Patients were excluded if they had a history of total body or craniospinal irradiation or radiation to ≥ 50% of the pelvis, uncontrolled infections, or, for the phase 1 component, a history of disease progression after exposure to a PARPi plus TMZ; they were excluded from the phase 2 if they had prior exposure to any PARPi. Institutional review board approval and written informed consent/assent were obtained in accordance with federal and institutional guidelines.

Talazoparib was supplied by BioMarin Pharmaceutical, Inc. (San Rafael, CA), Medivation, Inc. (San Francisco, CA), and Pfizer (New York, NY) and distributed by the Cancer Therapeutics Evaluation Program (National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD). TMZ capsules were obtained commercially. The PPTP performed extensive toxicity testing in non-tumored immunodeficient mice, varying the dose and schedule of talazoparib and temozolomide.13Dosing for longer than five days in a 28-day cycle caused excessive toxicity (weight loss).13Tolerable dosing schedules were then tested and optimized in xenograft models. Based on this PPTP data13, five days of combined talazoparib and TMZ were initially planned for each 28-day cycle. However, because of the long talazoparib half-life in adults (~50 hours),15predictive pharmacokinetic modeling was performed to ensure maximum talazoparib during TMZ exposure. A twice-daily loading dose on day 1 followed by once-daily dosing of talazoparib was predicted to achieve a day 6 area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) and a maximum concentration (Cmax) of ≥ 95% of the respective steady-state adult phase 1 study values during which talazoparib was given once daily continuously (data not shown). Therefore, a talazoparib loading dose was administered on day 1 either once daily (dose level (DL) 1) or twice daily (DL2–6) followed by once daily administration of talazoparib and TMZ on days 2–6 of each 28-day cycle. The starting dose of talazoparib was 400 mcg/m2/day, approximately 80% of the single agent MTD in adults, which was 1000 mcg/day (~600 mcg/m2/day) administered continuously.15The starting dose of TMZ was 20 mg/m2/dose based on preclinical models.13Talazoparib [400 (800 mcg/day maximum) or 600 (1000 mcg/day maximum) mcg/m2/dose)] was initially increased followed by escalation of TMZ (DL1–3: 20 mg/m2/dose; DL4–6: 30, 40 or 55 mg/m2/dose) (Table S1). Therapy could continue for up to 24 cycles in the absence of progressive disease (PD) or significant adverse events (AEs). During the phase 2 component of the trial, ≤ two intra-patient dose-level escalations were permitted if the subject did not experience serious toxicities in the preceding cycle.

The phase 1 dose-escalation component utilized a 3+3 design followed by a PK dose-expansion cohort at the RP2D. The MTD was defined as the maximum dose at which fewer than 33% of patients experience a DLT during cycle 1. The RP2D was the MTD. Toxicity was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v4.0 (http://ctep.cancer.gov). Non-hematologic DLT was defined as any grade ≥ 3 toxicity attributable to protocol therapy with the exclusion of the following grade 3 events: nausea/vomiting < 3 days duration; hepatic transaminase elevation that returned to ≤ grade 1 or baseline prior to the next treatment cycle; fever; infection; and hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia or hypomagnesemia responsive to oral supplementation. Hematologic DLTs included grade 4 neutropenia for > 7 days; a platelet count < 20,000/mm3on 2 separate days or requiring a platelet transfusion on 2 separate days within a 7-day period. Any AE resulting in a delay of > 14 days between cycles was also dose-limiting.

Any patient who experienced a DLT during protocol therapy was evaluable for toxicity. Of patients without DLTs, only those who received at least 85% of correctly administered prescribed protocol therapy and had appropriate toxicity monitoring were evaluable for the primary objectives.

The phase 2 component, utilized a 10 + 10 Simon optimal two-stage design.16Patients with a centrally confirmed complete (CR) or partial response (PR) were considered responders; all others were considered non-responders. Initially, ten patients with EWS/PNET treated at the RP2D would be evaluated, if ≥ one had a response, ten additional patients would be enrolled. If ≥ three subjects were responders, the therapy would be considered active with probability of 0.07 when the true response rate was 5% (type I error) and a probability of 0.88 (power) when the true response rate was 25%.

History, physical examination and laboratory studies were obtained routinely. Disease evaluations were obtained at baseline, after cycles 1, 3 and 5, then after every third cycle.

Tumor response in patients with central nervous system (CNS) tumors was based on sum of the products of longest diameter and its longest perpendicular diameter. Disappearance of all target lesions was CR; PR was ≥ 50% decrease; PD was any new lesion or > 25% increase; stable disease (SD) was defined as insufficient increase or decrease to be PD, PR, or CR. In patients with non-CNS solid tumors, response was assessed using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST v1.1).17

Blood samples for talazoparib and TMZ pharmacokinetic studies were collected prior to the day 1 dose and then at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 24 (optional) hours after the first talazoparib dose and pre-dose and at 1, 2, 4 or 8 hours post-dose on day 5 or 6.

Talazoparib plasma concentrations were measured using a validated liquid chromatography, tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) assay.15TMZ plasma concentrations were determined using a validated LC-MS/MS assay (seesupplement). The within-day and between-day precision (CV%) values and accuracy values met standard assay validation criteria. Pharmacokinetic parameters were estimated by standard methods18using Phoenix®WinNonlin®Version 6.4 (Certara Corporation, Princeton, NJ). The AUC over the 24-hour dosing interval (τ) on Day 5 and 6 was calculated by trapezoidal approximation. If a 24-hour blood sample was not drawn, the 24-hour plasma concentration was estimated to be equivalent to the pre-dose concentration, based on the assumption that steady-state was reached on day 5. Talazoparib accumulation was calculated as the ratio of day 5 or 6 AUC0–8h: day 1 AUC0–8hand was used to estimate the half-life. Oral steady-state clearance (Clss/F) was calculated using the equation, Clss= dose/AUCτ. The relationship of Clss/F and clinical parameters was performed using the Kruskal-Wallis Rank Test.

In phase 2 subjects, unstained, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sections from diagnosis or relapse were obtained. Slides were incubated with 53BP1 (Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX); phospho-RNApol II S2 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA) and phospho-γH2AX (Millipore, Burlington, MA); STAG2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX) and p53 (DO-7, Cell signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) antibodies (seesupplement). The proportion of positive cells and the intensity of the staining were determined by a pathologist (J.H.) using magnified slide digital images (Figure S1) as previously described.19–21

We report the first clinical trial of talazoparib in combination with TMZ in children and adolescents with recurrent or refractory tumors. The combination was well tolerated; reversible neutropenia and thrombocytopenia were the primary DLTs. The MTD/RP2D is talazoparib 600 mcg/m2/dose twice daily orally on day 1 followed by talazoparib 600 mcg/m2/dose (1000 mcg/day maximum) in combination with TMZ 30 mg/m2/dose once daily orally on days 2–6 of a 28-day cycle. At the RP2D, 16% (3/19) of evaluable patients experienced DLTs. There was a range of tolerability of the increasing TMZ dose; however, there were no apparent clinical, pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamics relationships between talazoparib dose and toxicity.

Based on robust pre-clinical data, PARP inhibition has been of significant interest in brain tumors and EWS8,9,31,32. However, clinical trials of the PARPi veliparib (ABT-888), alone and in combination with radiation and TMZ for children with CNS tumors33,34demonstrated no responses and few patients achieving prolonged stable disease in a phase 1 study34and a phase 2 study closed early for futility.33Furthermore, a phase 2 study of olaparib in 12 adult subjects with relapsed/refractory EWS demonstrated neither responses nor durable disease control.35

In vitro, the next-generation PARPi, talazoparib is a more potent PARPi than olaparib (IC500.57 vs. 1.98 nmol/L) or veliparib (IC500.57 vs 4.73 nmol/L) and is also more potent than veliparib in potentiating TMZ cytotoxicity (GI503 vs. 6203 nmol/L).36In our study, however, only modest clinical activity was observed despite talazoparib equivalent exposures on days 5 or 6 to adult exposure at steady-state. In adults, PARP inhibition was achieved with talazoparib at doses ≥ 600 mcg/day (350 mcg/m2/day) with an AUC0–24of 119 hr•μg/L. Our median talazoparib AUC0-τon day 5/6 was 140 (84–295) h•μg/L for 400 mcg/m2talazoparib (n=6) and 189 (94–456) h•μg/L for 600 mcg/m2talazoparib (n=22). These values are 1.2–1.6-fold higher that the AUC0–24hvalues found on Day 35 in the adult trial.15Thus, despite achieving talazoparib concentrations that adequately inhibited PARP activity in adults our duration of exposure may have been insufficient to induce clinically significant antitumor effects in children with EWS.

To better identify patients who might respond or be resistant to the talazoparib/TMZ combination, we attempted to identify Immunohistochemical biomarkers in the EWS cohort. EWS is known to have dysregulated transcription, with hyperphosphorylated RNA polymerase II leading to an accumulation of R-loops, which are messenger RNA that binds to template DNA while displacing the non-template DNA strand, and thus increased replication stress.37This defective RNA polymerase control also prohibits the release of the BRCA1 protein from an active transcription complex, inducing a “BRCA1-deficient” state, and preventing it from going to sites of DNA damage to promote homologous recombination.37These biological phenomena suggest a mechanistic basis for EWS PARPi sensitivityin vitro. Mutations in p53 allow circumvention of the need for BRCA1 in the process of homologous recombination.38Therefore, decreased 53BP1 expression, which has been shown to be associated with poor prognosis in EWS30, could also partially explain PARPi resistance. STAG2 has been shown to be an independent marker of poor prognosis in EWS.28–30and STAG2-deficient glioblastoma appears to carry increased sensitivity to PARP inhibition.39Despite biological rationale, no relationship was observed between these proteins and disease status or response in this small study.

It is possible that PARP inhibition has potent antitumor effects inde novoEWS but not in the heavily-pretreated, chemo-resistant patient population enrolled on this and other trials.35Recent investigations demonstrated that clinically-induced chemotherapy-resistant EWS cell lines have comparatively diminished olaparib sensitivity and resistance to PARPi/TMZ-mediated apoptosis that is associated with increased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2.40,41Further, these phenomena can be overcomein vitrowith the addition of the BCL-2/XLinhibitor, navitoclax.41This approach could be an avenue for future exploration. Furthermore, PARPis may be optimally synergized with xenobiotics, other than TMZ, that create single-strand DNA breaks, such as the topoisomerase I inhibitor, irinotecan. Such a study, with talazoparib, in children is currently ongoing.42

Despite disappointing results in EWS, modest antitumor activity was observed in children with CNS tumors. Of the thirteen subjects with CNS malignancies, one had a PR and five experienced SD (three with prolonged SD). PARP inhibition with or without TMZ has biological rationale and promising results in pre-clinical studies in pediatric CNS tumors. However, completed clinical trials, which all utilized earlier-generation PARPis, less potent than talazoparib, have been disappointing.33,34Our positive results in CNS malignancies occurred despite the facts that talazoparib has significant MDR1 efflux lability restricting delivery across the blood-brain barrier43and that TMZ doses and exposures in our study were 10–30% of those usually active in pediatric brain tumors.44,45These seemingly incongruent results should be further explored in additional studies to assess the role of talazoparib in CNS malignancies.
