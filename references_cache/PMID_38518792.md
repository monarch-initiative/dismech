Staphylococcus aureus is a leading cause of death by infectious diseases 
worldwide. Treatment of S aureus infections is difficult due to widespread 
antibiotic resistance, necessitating alternative approaches and measures for 
prevention of infection. Because S aureus infections commonly arise from 
asymptomatic colonisation, decolonisation is considered a key approach for their 
prevention. Current decolonisation procedures include antibiotic-based and 
antiseptic-based eradication of S aureus from the nose and skin. However, 
despite the widespread implementation and partial success of such measures, S 
aureus infection rates remain worrisome, and resistance to decolonisation agents 
is on the rise. In this Review we outline the epidemiology and mechanisms of S 
aureus colonisation, describe how colonisation underlies infection, and discuss 
current and novel approaches for S aureus decolonisation, with a focus on the 
latest findings on probiotic strategies and the intestinal S aureus colonisation 
site.

Staphylococcus aureus is a leading cause of infection-related mortality in the world, representing the only bacterial pathogen other than Mycobacterium tuberculosis with an attributed number of deaths that consistently exceed one million annually1. Virtually all severe S. aureus infections occur in the hospital setting and often in pre-disposed patients such as those with immune deficiencies or those undergoing surgery2,3. Most of them are lower respiratory tract, blood, and peritoneal/intra-abdominal infections1. Furthermore, S. aureus is a common source of mild to moderate skin infections in the community3,4. In some patients, S. aureus skin infections can be chronic or recurring. Moreover, S. aureus has been implicated in atopic dermatitis (AD, eczema)5.

The astounding diversity of infection types that S. aureus can cause is largely due to this pathogen’s high number of various virulence factors and mechanisms6. These comprise adhesion molecules to establish infection of tissues, an extremely large number of immune evasion mechanisms to tackle virtually all aspects of innate host defense, and – most infamously – a series of aggressive toxins that lyse many immune and other cell types6,7.

The only generally accepted mode of treatment for severe S. aureus infections is antibiotic therapy8. However, S. aureus has become resistant to a variety of antibiotics, as being most infamously present in methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). This situation is further exacerbated by the ongoing failure to develop a working vaccine or alternative modes of treatment, such as virulence-targeted drugs, despite considerable research efforts9,10.

S. aureus is an asymptomatic colonizer on human epithelial and mucosal surfaces and many studies have shown that infection is correlated with colonization, indicating that S. aureus infections originate from colonizing strains11–14. Decolonization of S. aureus is therefore a frequently proposed and often implemented approach to S. aureus infection prevention15–17. However, whether current procedures are effective has been controversial18. This review will give an overview on S. aureus colonization and discuss decolonization measures with a focus on how recent findings may stimulate new approaches.

S. aureus colonizes the human body with a distinct preference for specific areas. These include primarily the intestine, the nose, certain areas of the skin (the groin and axillae), as well as the pharynx and the vagina19,20 (Figure 1a). Only some people are colonized by S. aureus in this fashion, with frequencies ranging from about 1/5 to 1/3 of the population for nasal and intestinal colonization, and somewhat lower and more variable frequencies for the other areas11,21. Between 1 to 3% of the population can be colonized by MRSA22,23. Geographical and socio-economic differences in colonization frequencies may also exist21. Frequency of colonization (culture positivity) is higher in newborns and children than in adults24–27 (Figure 1b). High colonization rates in the population after birth of ~ 70% decrease to 20–30% in the first months of life and further drop to ~10–20% within 1–2 years. For intestinal colonization, this decreasing tendency over the first months of life is reflected by declining numbers of colonizing S. aureus in carriers26 (Figure 1c). Nasal colonization rates rise again until ~ 10 years (~50%), after which they drop to adult levels of ~20–30%, which are similar for nasal and intestinal colonization28,29. Many studies have distinguished permanent from temporary colonization, with “permanent” usually being defined by two positive samples taken with an at least one-week interval (“culture rule”)30. While colonization at different body sites is generally correlated, only temporary colonization at a certain site may be due to underlying “hiding” of S. aureus at another site11 and explain that people are often found to be recolonized with the same strain after topical decolonization31. Of note, both carriers and non-carriers of S. aureus have high anti-staphylococcal serum antibody levels, indicating that most people have been in contact with S. aureus at least at some point in their life32,33.

S. aureus colonization is usually only assessed in a qualitative fashion. In quantitative studies, CFU in the nose ranged from 102 to 106 CFU/swab, with the most frequently reported average in the 104-105 range34–36. Only a few studies have determined CFU in the feces as a proxy for intestinal colonization, reporting ~106-107 CFU per 100 g of feces, the average weight of human stool25,26,37. In a recent study, it was estimated that numbers of S. aureus in the intestine are several orders of magnitude higher than those in the nose37. As for the skin, S. aureus colony counts even on the more commonly S. aureus-colonized sites of the skin (axillae, groin) are negligible in comparison25,26,30,38,39 (Figure 1a).

Notably, none of the presently available data, including those on host SNPs and bacterial interference discussed below, satisfactorily explain why some people are colonized with S. aureus and some are not. It appears possible that the frequency of S. aureus colonization in the population may be much higher than previously assumed, but often remains unnoticed because (i) S. aureus colonization of the intestinal and nasal epithelia can only be assessed indirectly by analyzing fecal matter or by swabs, respectively, potentially producing many false negatives, and (ii) the nose still often remains the only analyzed site.

Traditionally, S. aureus colonization has predominantly been associated with the nose14. Permanent nasal colonization occurs in ~20% (range 12–30%) of individuals and ~30% are intermittent carriers (range 16–70%)14. Colonization levels are similar in the nasal vestibule and nasal cavity; and ~5% of S. aureus may be intracellular35. S. aureus nasal colonization is believed to be determined by the interaction with host defenses and specific bacterial genes40. Some single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in host genes, including in IL-441, C-reactive protein41, TLRs42, and defensins43 have been linked to the frequency of S. aureus colonization, but these differences are minor and there is no significant heritability of S. aureus colonization44. S. aureus factors that have been associated with colonization include teichoic acids45 and surface proteins such as ClfB46, which bind to the nasal epithelium via binding to the SREC-1 receptor and cytokeratin 10, respectively46,47 (Figure 1d). Whether biofilm formation contributes to nasal colonization is controversial48. Increased nasal colonization with MRSA has been observed in the U.S. - despite decreased overall S. aureus colonization rates49 - along with the rise of community-associated MRSA infections50. Colonization of the pharynx is closely connected to that of the nose, but colonization rates can slightly differ and even exceed those reported for the nose51.

The intestine by far has not received as much attention as S. aureus colonization site as the nose. However, it has recently been suggested that the intestine rather than the nose represents the primary S. aureus colonization site, based on the abovementioned comparison of nasal swab and fecal CFU25,26,30,37–39, and may serve as a reservoir from which other colonization sites are replenished11,37 (Figure 1a). In support of that notion, it has been shown that in comparison to only nasal colonization, presence of both nasal and intestinal colonization is associated with increased frequency of positive skin cultures52. Furthermore, in a clinical trial S. aureus was strongly diminished in the gut by exclusively gut-targeted intervention, and a concomitant decrease in S. aureus numbers in the nose was observed37.

Many questions about intestinal colonization by S. aureus remain unanswered. For example, we do not know where exactly in the intestine S. aureus colonizes. In mouse models, S. aureus appears to be associated with the mucus layer53. Whether strains colonizing the nose and intestine are the same has not yet been addressed. Only one study reported that the nose and rectal skin area of carriers was colonized by different strains in at least 40% of cases54.

Mechanisms of S. aureus intestinal colonization are yet poorly defined. S. aureus surface structures including capsule, teichoic acids, and the surface protein ClfA have been shown to affect intestinal colonization in mice55 (Figure 1d). In children, presence of several surface protein genes such as those coding for collagen-binding protein (Cna) and fibronectin-binding protein (FnbA) have been associated with gut colonization56,57. Dependence of attachment to the intestinal epithelium on surface proteins suggests direct interaction with the epithelium despite the presence of mucus and the lack of reports on mucus-destroying capacity of S. aureus. It is not clear how this works; possibly, S. aureus colonization of the intestine (and the nose) occurs in areas with decreased mucus presence (Figure 1d). Furthermore, intestinal colonization was reported to be strongly dependent on the Agr quorum-sensing system, which controls the expression of most virulence factors of S. aureus and affects virulence-associated phenotypes such as inflammation, immune evasion, and biofilm formation58; however, the specific Agr-regulated factors underlying intestinal colonization remain unidentified.

S. aureus is usually absent from healthy human skin or its density is extremely low; somewhat higher numbers are only found in moist areas such as the axillae and groin59 (Figure 1a). S. aureus on the skin has mostly been investigated from the angle of skin diseases, while factors contributing to asymptomatic colonization remain virtually unexplored. In addition to its role in AD as a controller of delta-toxin expression60, Agr may also matter for skin colonization, although this has only been shown in models that produced inflammation61,62.

In the context of the microbiome on human body surfaces, S. aureus is exposed to multi-faceted bacterial interactions (Figure 2). More recently, several studies have mechanistically addressed the competition of S. aureus with other members of the microbiome and unraveled modes of interaction that exceed the mere competition for nutrients. Most studies that have included the critical assessment of in vivo relevance in animal models of colonization have described bacteriocin- or quorum sensing-mediated interaction.

Some strains use directly lethal weapons, such as bacteriocins, to limit competitor proliferation63,64. There are many examples of bacteriocins that target S. aureus, and many of those are made by other staphylococci or phylogenetically closely related bacteria65. For example, lantibiotics (lanthionine-containing bacteriocins) and other bacteriocins produced by skin commensal coagulase-negative staphylococci have been implicated in limiting S. aureus growth on the skin and in the nose66,67. Cutimycin produced by Cutibacterium acnes was shown to be active against S. aureus and S. epidermidis in vitro and to be associated with reduced colonization of S. epidermidis in human hair follicles68. The latter may also impact S. aureus skin colonization, but probably due to the general lack of S. aureus on healthy skin this could not be assessed in that study.

Quorum-sensing (Agr)-mediated cross-inhibition via Agr signal peptides that inhibit Agr activity in strains of non-self can contribute to competition between S. aureus and other staphylococci69, because Agr controls factors that are likely important for nutrient acquisition and evasion of host defenses in the context of colonization70. The role of Agr in nasal colonization is not well understood. Data from the analysis of Agr subgroups of S. aureus and S. epidermidis in nasal carriers do not support a role of Agr-based cross-inhibition in the nose in healthy individuals71. As for skin colonization, it is unclear whether the role of Agr and Agr-based cross-inhibition in experimental skin disease61,62,72 extends to a significant role in situations unrelated to disease. However, regarding the intestine, Bacillus species, which temporarily colonize the human gut and produce Agr-inhibiting substances, were associated in two studies with complete absence of S. aureus not only in the feces but also noses of humans37,58.

There are many further studies implying interference between S. aureus and other members of the skin and nasal microbiome. For example, the secreted phenol-soluble modulin (PSM) peptides of S. aureus have been implied in conferring resistance to yet unidentified inhibitors from Corynebacterium pseudodiphtericum73. Coproporphyrin III made by C. acnes was shown to promote S. aureus biofilm formation, which is potentially important for colonization74. Streptococcus pneumoniae and S. aureus nasopharyngeal colonization is known to be negatively correlated and there is evidence linking this to hydrogen peroxide production by S. pneumoniae28,75.

In addition to direct bacterial interference, there has been much recent interest in mechanisms by which commensals can engage the hosts’ immune response to limit pathogen overgrowth64,76. S. epidermidis has been used in much of this research as the example “beneficial” commensal. Certain strains of S. epidermidis have been shown to drive a CD 8+ T-cell response that is stimulated by S. epidermidis antigen-stimulated dendritic cells in the skin and limits subsequent fungal colonization77. This mechanism may theoretically also affect S. aureus. Importantly, persistent pathogen exclusion in a real-life scenario requires that the “beneficial” commensal has increased protection from the host defense mechanism it stimulates. Liu et al. have shown that in the nose, certain strains of S. epidermidis stimulate production of host AMPs in nasal keratinocytes, which eliminate co-colonizing S. aureus but to which they have biofilm-mediated resistance78. Finally, as for the gut, there are many reports on immune-stimulating and pathogen exclusion effects of certain gut bacteria79. However, these do not contain data on S. aureus, as it has not traditionally been considered a gut colonizer.

Even though S. aureus is considered part of the normal human microbiota and colonization is normally asymptomatic, S. aureus can cause a wide range of infections. Several studies have consistently found that ~80% of infectious isolates clonally correspond to colonization isolates in the same patient12,13,80,81, indicating colonization as a source of infection, and that individuals who carry S. aureus are at an increased risk of developing S. aureus infections. For example, S. aureus nasal colonization is correlated with subsequent bacteremia13,81 and has also been associated with the exacerbation of rare conditions such as Wegener granulomatosis82. Furthermore, S. aureus is the most common cause of surgical site infections (SSIs), which develop due to the introduction of colonizing bacteria during surgery83. Moreover, colonization status is correlated with higher infection rates in patient groups with generally increased S. aureus colonization, such as dialysis patients84,85. Finally, MRSA infections are linked to colonization status both in the community and the hospital86, and patient groups with known increased MRSA colonization, such as those in long-term care facilities, more frequently develop MRSA infection87,88. While most studies only analyzed correlations between nasal colonization and infection, some recent studies included the analysis of intestinal or rectal colonization. Namely, S. aureus was significantly more frequently isolated from the rectal area, but not nares, of children with abscesses than from controls80, S. aureus intestinal colonization was associated with increased frequency of positive skin cultures52, and individuals with both rectal and nasal colonization were found at increased risk of S. aureus infection compared to those with only nasal colonization12.

S. aureus colonization and infection have a complex and multifactorial relationship, which is still not fully understood89. On the bacterial side, the notable correlation of colonization with risk of infection raises the question of whether there are specific molecular determinants that are required for S. aureus to become invasive. Despite distinct association of genetic variation with infection and infection types90, some studies have shown that invasive lineages were not different from those colonizing the nose or gut91,92. This may indicate that generally, the S. aureus genetic repertoire necessary for colonization and infection is similar and/or that host susceptibility plays a comparatively more decisive role. However, it is known that during the development of S. aureus infection, genetic adaptation may occur93–95, whose impact on the switch from commensal to pathogenic lifestyle remains to be explored in more detail. For example, any mutations that occur in Agr and make it dysfunctional96,97 increase biofilm formation98 and success in device-associated and chronic bone infection96,97, and are associated with persistent bacteremia99. This contrasts Agr’s demonstrated role during colonization37,61 and other infection types70.

As for host susceptibility, several risk factors have been discovered that increase the likelihood of S. aureus infection, including immunosuppression100, chronic illnesses such as diabetes101 or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)102, and rare genetic disorders such as chronic granulomatous disease (CGD). Genome-wide association studies have generally failed to detect host factors that significantly determine susceptibility to S. aureus infection in healthy people, until recently when SNPs in the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II region could be linked to infection90,103. Whether specific hereditary genetic traits determine colonization is poorly understood. One Danish study in twins found no significant role for host genetics in S. aureus nasal colonization44. Finally, it is possible that S. aureus colonization may predispose an individual to subsequent S. aureus infections via conditioning of the immune system to tolerate S. aureus antigens, but this has not been directly investigated104.

Prompted by the spread of antimicrobial resistance there has been increased recent interest in S. aureus decolonization measures. Oral antibiotics have been evaluated for S. aureus decolonization, sometimes in combination with topical antibiotic/antiseptic approaches105–109. While they may be of benefit in certain patient groups, as per IDSA recommendations oral antibiotics should not be given for the purpose of decolonization in healthy patients owing to the danger of antibiotic resistance development and the unavoidable associated destruction of the healthy gut microbiome8. Thus, targeting the skin and the nose has been in the focus of these efforts. Nasal decolonization is commonly accomplished using topical antibiotics, such as mupirocin, while topical antibiotics or antiseptics such as chlorhexidine are used for skin decolonization110 (Figure 3). Often, decolonization treatments combine antibiotic and antiseptic-based approaches to decolonize the nose, skin, and sometimes further areas such as the vagina111,112. Of note, antibiotic/antiseptic-based decolonization is only considered appropriate for patient populations who are at risk for infection for only a short period of time, such as preceding surgical interventions. This is because patients tend to become recolonized within weeks of being decolonized, preceding general microbiome recovery113, and prolonged exposure to antibiotics and antiseptics is advised against due to considerations of resistance development and harmful effects on the skin8, which include sustained disruption of the microbiota8,114

Mupirocin has long been the antibiotic of choice for topical decolonization of S. aureus115,116. Several studies have shown that mupirocin, an inhibitor of bacterial isoleucine-tRNA ligase, works fast and is effective at reducing nasal or skin colonization of S. aureus, although it requires repeated application over five days31,117,118 (Table 1). Long-term efficacy studies indicated that initial clearance is effective over several weeks, but recolonization beyond three months is common31,117,119. Notably, only-nasal application of mupirocin ointment fails to decolonize extranasal sites17,120. However, using antibiotics for decolonization also has several disadvantages, with the most crucial being the development of resistance and the destruction of the microbiota where the agent is applied (Figure 3). The latter is increasingly considered of major importance due to the roles of beneficial microbes that limit pathogen proliferation. As for the former, a significant increase in resistance of S. aureus or other pathogens can occur after repeated or extended courses of mupirocin application, and such usage should be avoided121,122.

Commonly used antiseptics for S. aureus decolonization are chlorhexidine (CH), which disrupts membranes, and povidone iodine (PI), an iodophor whose activity stems from the release of iodine. CH topical decolonization of the skin has been proven beneficial in decreasing infection in both surgical and nonsurgical patients and is usually combined with nasal decolonization112,123,124. PI has been shown to reduce nasal MSSA and MRSA in surgical patients and healthy volunteers125–127. In a recent trial, however, PI was inferior to mupirocin for nasal decolonization128. The use of antiseptics for decolonization also has disadvantages. One concern is the potential for skin irritation or allergic reactions, which can limit the use especially for repeated application. Furthermore, like antibiotics, antiseptics can disrupt the natural microbiota, potentially leading to the overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens (Figure 3).

Many clinical trials and retrospective analyses have been conducted to measure the impact of S. aureus decolonization procedures on the prevention of infection. The most important and largest studies using topical decolonization are summarized in Table 1. Systemic oral antibiotic-including trials were excluded due to their abovementioned associated dangers. Most trials used or evaluated decolonization procedures with nasal mupirocin, sometimes combined with CH or PI baths, bathing cloths, or showers.

In the large “REDUCE-MRSA” trial129, a universal approach was more effective than a targeted approach (targeting MRSA carriers after screening) or an isolation/screening-only approach, both in reducing MRSA positive cultures and bloodstream infections. However, there was no significant impact of the decolonization procedures on preventing MRSA blood infections specifically. While this trial was performed in ICU patients, in the recent ABATE trial, there was no significant reduction in MRSA colonization or bloodstream infection in non-critical care patients130. In the CLEAR trial131, post-discharge decolonization and hygiene education was 30% more effective than only hygiene education in preventing MRSA infection over the period of one year. Most trials reported similar outcomes, as there generally were significant effects of decolonization in ICU patients (see Table 1). In a recent trial in nursing homes, MRSA colonization and risk of all-cause and infection-related transfer to the hospital could be significantly reduced by universal decolonization132.133

Overall, there has thus been success in the implementation of S. aureus decolonization measures that likely have contributed to the decrease of healthcare-related infections observed for example in the US. Nevertheless, there is certainly room for improvement, with major remaining problems related to limited efficacy, recolonization, and harsh nature of decolonization agents. Furthermore, whole genome-sequencing studies point to the importance of considering strain diversity for decolonization134.

Several novel and investigational strategies to decolonize humans from S. aureus are currently being pursued that avoid the strong detrimental effects on the microbiome that are associated with the use of antibiotics and antiseptics. Some approaches attempted to use topically applied bacteriocins with activity against S. aureus. In a cotton rat nasal colonization model, the bacteriocin epidermicin NI01 from S. epidermidis was as efficient in reducing S. aureus colonization as mupirocin135. In one human trial, Bacillus subtilis bacteriocins were applied to the skin of acne patients and reduced S. aureus skin abundance and acne skin lesions136. However, other bacteriocins including bacitracin were inferior to mupirocin in human decolonization studies137,138.

While the use of bacteriocins represents an increase in specificity as compared to broad-spectrum antibiotics, they still commonly have a relatively broad target spectrum and thus potentially a considerable impact on microbiome composition. Phage or bacteria-derived lysins have some more specificity. In a mouse model of skin infection, an S. aureus-specific lysin reduced skin colonization more strongly than mupirocin139. Furthermore, a one-time application of lysostaphin cream eradicated MRSA from the nares of cotton rats140. Application of entire phages has also been investigated. In a mouse study, application of two staphylococcal phages eradicated S. aureus from the noses more efficiently than mupirocin. Yet, in another study, application of a two-phage combination failed to significantly reduce MRSA in a piglet nasal colonization model141. There are no results yet from human trials on the use of phages or lysins for S. aureus decolonization, which generally is subject to the same controversiality as the use of phages for the treatment of infection.

Other recently pursued approaches are based on the use of probiotic bacteria that have the potential to limit S. aureus colonization by (i) competition for nutrients, (ii) modulation of the host immune response to promote a more favorable environment for non-pathogenic bacteria, (iii) production of antimicrobial substances such as bacteriocins, and (iv) interference with mechanisms of S. aureus colonization (Figure 4). The at least temporary establishment of probiotic bacteria in the microbiome has the advantage of prolonged or even persistent production of the inhibitory substance or effect.

As for (i) and (ii), these represent rather non-specific approaches often pursued with orally administered probiotic bacteria and are based on the idea that virtually any bacterium that has a benign relationship with the host and/or to some extent stimulates defensive immune responses will decrease the relative abundance of pathogens. These approaches commonly have low efficacy. Also, the underlying probiotic mechanisms remain poorly understood. For example, in one trial, oral application of a Lactobacillus rhamnosus strain reduced intestinal S. aureus colonization by 15%, but it had no impact on other body sites142.

As for (iii), several bacteriocin-producing strains, mostly coagulase-negative staphylococci, have been used to reduce S. aureus colonization in pre-clinical research. Interestingly, most of them stem from the analysis and screening of the human microbiome. For example, by analysis of nasal isolates a strain of S. lugdunensis was discovered that produced a previously unidentified substance, termed lugdunin, that inhibits S. aureus growth67. Nasal colonization of lugdunin-producing S. lugdunensis was negatively correlated with nasal colonization of S. aureus and co-application of the lugdunin-producing strain and S. aureus led to a slight reduction of S. aureus compared to control67. In another study, a screen of skin isolates identified a strain of S. hominis that produced the bacteriocin micrococcin P1143 and significantly reduced S. aureus skin colonization. Furthermore, another strain of S. hominis that produces two lantibiotic-type bacteriocins reduced S. aureus on the skin of mice and humans in a human trial144. While in the latter trial that focused on AD145,146, application of the S. hominis strain failed to reduce eczema, the study showed that in principle, bacteriocin producers may be used safely in humans to reduce S. aureus skin colonization144.

Another approach to reduce S. aureus colonization in a probiotic fashion using live bacteria is based on quorum-sensing inhibition. This approach does not kill S. aureus but limits its quorum-sensing (Agr)-dependent ability to colonize. Application of an S. caprae strain, which produces cross-inhibitory AIP, significantly albeit only slightly restricted MRSA growth in a mouse skin colonization model72. In a recent human trial, the production of quorum-sensing inhibitory fengycin lipopeptides by B. subtilis was used to reduce S. aureus colonization. In that placebo-controlled trial, oral consumption of probiotic B. subtilis spores eliminated more than 95% of the total S. aureus colonizing the human body without affecting the intestinal microbiota37.

Recent research on S. aureus colonization has provided important insight into the mechanisms underlying S. aureus colonization, how S. aureus may interfere with other members of the microbiota, and how colonization relates to infection. However, there is still much we do not understand. For example, mechanisms of interaction within the microbiota are only beginning to be unraveled and we need further research to identify the underlying mechanisms and include in vivo assessments of relevance. Furthermore, we know that infection originates from colonization in likely a vast majority of cases, but we still do not completely understand how the transition from colonization to infection is accomplished and which host factors and potential host genetic polymorphisms impact that transition. Moreover, intestinal colonization and the underlying mechanisms and colonization dynamics regarding other colonization sites will require increased attention.

Currently employed strategies to decolonize S. aureus to prevent infection had success but can certainly be improved. Furthermore, these commonly use antibiotics, which is problematic in the light of antibiotic stewardship. Novel approaches that are in pre-clinical and for some cases already clinical development attempt to overcome the deleterious consequences of broad-spectrum antibiotics/antiseptics by using more specifically S. aureus-targeted substances and probiotics. Furthermore, the recent recognition of the potential key role of intestinal S. aureus colonization will require a change of focus regarding the setup of decolonization strategies. However, much further research is needed to determine the long-term safety and efficacy of novel decolonization strategies so that they can be successfully incorporated into the clinical setting.

We searched PubMed with no publication time limits on April 1, 2023, using the search terms “Staphylococcus aureus” AND “colonization”. Using additional search terms on this preselection (such as “intestinal” or “trial”), we focused in our review on publications that included mechanistic and epidemiological analysis of colonization, particularly intestinal colonization, described novel avenues for decolonization, or comprised human decolonization trials.
