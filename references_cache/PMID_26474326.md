# Temporal Bone Histopathology in NOG-Symphalangism Spectrum Disorder.
**Authors:** Quesnel AM, Nadol JB Jr, Nielsen GP, Curtin HD, Lesperance MM
**Journal:** Otol Neurotol (2015)
**DOI:** [10.1097/MAO.0000000000000861](https://doi.org/10.1097/MAO.0000000000000861)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To describe the human temporal bone histopathology in NOG-related symphalangism spectrum disorder, a spectrum of congenital stape fixation syndromes caused by mutations in the NOG gene. To discuss implications for clinical management.

PATIENT: A patient with a mutation in the NOG gene.

INTERVENTION(S): Removal of temporal bones, postmortem temporal bone computed tomography, histologic processing, and review of temporal bones.

MAIN OUTCOME MEASURE(S): Temporal bone histopathology and correlation with clinical, genetic, audiologic, and radiologic evaluations.

RESULTS: Both temporal bones demonstrated fixation of the stapes footplate to the otic capsule because of a circumferential bridge of calcified cartilage. In the right ear (unoperated), there was no additional abnormality of the ossicles or ossicular joints. In the left ear, fenestrations of the stapes footplate and the lateral semicircular canal were seen, consistent with a history of stapedectomy and fenestration procedure. Severe loss of spiral ganglion neurons throughout the left cochlea accounted for the profound sensorineural hearing loss; there was a normal number of spiral ganglion neurons in the right ear. In both ears, the cochleae demonstrated grossly preserved organs of Corti.

CONCLUSION: The temporal bone pathologic correlate for conductive hearing loss in this patient with a NOG mutation was circumferentially calcified cartilage bridging the stapedovestibular joint space. The temporal bone histopathology findings suggest that conductive hearing loss related to NOG mutation should be improved after stapedectomy; however, care must be taken in extrapolating to all patients with NOG mutations because there may be variability in the pathology, especially given the variability of NOG spectrum disorders.

Mutations in the NOG gene are responsible for multiple rare, autosomal dominant syndromes that include congenital conductive hearing loss and skeletal abnormalities. Noggin, the protein encoded by the NOG gene, acts as an antagonist of bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and is critical in normal skeletal and joint development.1,2 Three of the NOG-Related Symphalangism Spectrum Disorders (NOG-SSD) demonstrate hearing loss: (1) proximal symphalangism (SYM1), (2) multiple synostoses syndrome (SYNS1), and (3) stapes ankylosis with broad thumbs and toes (SABTT) (which includes Teunissen-Cremers Syndrome and its variants).3, 4 Hearing loss is predominantly conductive in these syndromes, and related to stapes fixation. Evidence for stapes fixation is based on clinical findings of a normal tympanic membrane, conductive hearing loss, absent stapedial reflexes, and operative findings of stapes fixation. Not surprisingly, many of these patients are initially diagnosed with otosclerosis, especially when the onset of hearing loss is unclear and skeletal abnormalities are subtle. Multiple authors have reported improvement of conductive hearing loss after stapedectomy in patients with NOG related syndromes.3-7 Although temporal bone histopathology in patients with congenital stapes ankylosis has been described, the histopathology specific to a confirmed case of one of these rare NOG-related syndromes has not yet been reported. We describe here the temporal bone histopathology, clinical course, audiometry, and radiology findings in a patient with a Stapes Ankylosis with Broad Thumbs and Toes Syndrome resulting from a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the NOG gene. These histopathology findings have clinical implications for the surgical management of these patients.

Clinical history, audiologic evaluations, and genetic testing results were collected during life through enrollment in the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) National Temporal Bone Registry.

After death, the temporal bones were fixed in formalin. High resolution computed tomography (CT) imaging was then performed on the temporal bone specimens with the same protocol used clinically. A 40-slice multidetector CT scanner (SOMATOM Sensation; Siemens), with 0.6-mm collimation was used to generate images with resultant 0.5-mm slice thickness.

The temporal bones then underwent standard processing for histologic examination, including decalcification with ethylenediamine tetraaceticacid (EDTA) and celloidin embedding. The specimens were sectioned serially in the horizontal plane at a section thickness of 20 um. Every tenth section was stained with Hematoxylin and eosin and mounted on a glass slide.8

The slides were examined by light microscopy. Axial CT images were reviewed with a neuro-radiologist blinded to the histological findings.

This patient reported that he was born with bilateral hearing loss, although this was not diagnosed until approximately 5 years old. His bilateral congenital hearing loss was progressive, and he began wearing hearing aids at age 22. He was diagnosed with otosclerosis and underwent a left fenestration procedure at age 34, and then a left stapedectomy at age 37. He had an initial slight improvement in hearing after the left ear stapedectomy, but subsequently developed a severe to profound mixed hearing loss in that ear. The time course of his left post-operative hearing loss is unknown. He wore a right hearing aid only and was dependent on his right ear for hearing for many years. Clinic notes indicate he had a stable profound hearing loss in the left ear for decades prior to his death. An audiogram done at age 66, 10 years prior to his death, demonstrates right mixed hearing loss, and profound hearing loss on the left (Figure 1). At age 67, he was examined by a geneticist and diagnosed with Stapes Ankylosis with Broad Thumbs and Toes. He underwent skeletal x-rays, which identified short, broad middle and distal phalanges of the thumbs bilaterally, and symphalangism (fusion of the distal and middle phalanges) of the right foot only. He was also noted to have hyperopia, a broad sloping forehead, bulbous nasal tip, and limited cervical range of motion. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the NOG gene (p.Q110X).9 His family history was significant for conductive hearing loss, which was inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with complete penetrance. The left ear demonstrated a canal wall down mastoidectomy cavity with fenestration of the lateral semicircular canal. The incus had been removed, and only a remnant of the malleus remained. A fenestration of the stapes footplate was identified, and the superstructure was missing.

Both ears were notable for stapes fixation due to obliteration of the stapedovestibular joint space with ossified cartilage (Figures 3, 4). These findings account for his conductive hearing loss in life. There were no histologic foci of otosclerosis in either ear. The ossified cartilage at the stapedovestibular joint space had the typical appearance of cartilage with chondrocytes, but the surrounding matrix was deeply basophilic consistent with mineralization (Figure 3B, 4B). The footplate was fixed circumferentially in both ears.

In the right ear (unoperated ear), the malleus and incus were normal, and were not fixed. Specifically, there was no calcification of the anterior malleal ligament, and no abnormality or calcification of the incudo-mallear joint.

In the left ear (operated ear), only a remnant of the manubrium of the malleus remained, and the incus had been removed. A central fenestration of the stapes footplate was visible, although there was no stapes prosthesis present (Figure 4A). The middle ear space was filled with fibrocystic adipose tissue, likely the result of a graft placed during fenestration surgery. There was a canal wall down mastoid cavity. The lateral semicircular canal had been fenestrated, but there was a thick fibrous layer of tissue and neo-ossification covering the fenestration (Figure 4C).

In both ears, the organ of Corti was largely preserved throughout the turns (Figure 4D). There was minor patchy loss of outer hair cells in both temporal bones, and slightly more patchy inner hair cell loss in the left (operated) temporal bone. Hair cell loss was quantified and is presented in the cytocochleogram in Figure 1. The stria vascularis was generally preserved in both temporal bones. In the right (unoperated) ear, the spiral ganglion neurons were well preserved, with a total spiral ganglion neuron cell count of 19, 017, corresponding to a 17% loss compared to the median for his age.10,11 In the left (operated) ear, there was a significant loss of spiral ganglion neurons throughout all turns of cochlea, with more loss in the apex than in the base. The total spiral ganglion neuron cell count for the left ear was 7,137, corresponding to 69% loss compared to the median for his age. 10,11

Prior histopathologic evaluation of temporal bones from patients with congenital stapes ankylosis has demonstrated a range of anomalies, from an atresia plate (i.e. an undeveloped stapes footplate with no stapes superstructure) to cartilaginous fixation of the stapedovestibular joint.12,13 Although NOG-related syndromes are rare, these cases may have included patients with NOG mutations; however, genetic analysis has not yet been previously reported or correlated with the histopathologic temporal bone findings. Over 30 distinct mutations in the NOG gene have been reported and result in a range of syndromic phenotypes.4 In this patient with SABTT due to a heterozygous Q110X NOG mutation, there was ossified cartilage extending across the stapedovestibular joints, with an otherwise normal middle ear, essentially normal cochlea and normal spiral ganglion cell counts in the unoperated ear. There may be variability in the temporal bone pathology among patients with NOG related disorders, and further temporal bone histology studies with correlative genetic analysis are needed to fully define the range of pathology across the phenotypic spectrum. Notably, there are also key differences in the human pathology demonstrated here compared to a heterozygous Nog knockout mouse model, in which an abnormal bony strut connects to the posterior crus of the stapes causing fixation.14

Notably, high resolution temporal bone CT did not identify the pathologic mineralized cartilage seen by histopathology in the stapedo-vestibular joint space. Since the normal stapedo-vestibular joint space is not always seen as a distinct space on high resolution CT, it may not be possible to confirm these pathologic changes on CT. CT is still useful in the evaluation of patients with suspected NOG related hearing loss, however, as CT can identify other etiologies of conductive hearing loss, including malleus fixation, otosclerosis, and third window syndromes.15,16

Multiple authors have reported operative findings of stapes fixation with improvement of conductive hearing loss after stapedectomy in patients with NOG related syndromes.3-7 However, some have reported that stapedectomy is less successful in closing the air-bone gap in these patients compared to patients with otosclerosis. In this case, in the unoperated right ear, there are no temporal bone pathology findings that would suggest an increased likelihood of sensorineural hearing loss or reason for persistent conductive hearing loss. Specifically, there was no malleus fixation or additional ossicular chain abnormalities identified. Thus, based on the temporal bone pathology, a successful result from right stapedectomy would be expected. In the left ear, the stapedectomy was performed after a prior fenestration procedure, and chances for successful hearing result were reduced by the absence of an incus, fibroadipose tissue in the middle ear, and prior fenestration of the labyrinth.

The cause of the post-operative sensorineural hearing loss in the left ear is unknown. There was no significant evidence of a traumatic stapedectomy, such as displaced stapes footplate fragments within the vestibule or injury to the saccular membrane. There was no saccular hydrops that might have pre-disposed the patient to saccular injury. There was no significant evidence of labyrinthitis, such as significant soft tissue fibrosis, new bone formation or lymphocytic infiltrate. The notable pathologic correlate to the profound sensorineural hearing loss in his left ear was the significant loss of spiral ganglion neurons, as compared to an age-matched reference population10,11 and compared to his contralateral ear with relatively preserved bone thresholds. Classic experiments in cats have demonstrated that pure tone thresholds remain normal with up to 80% loss of auditory nerve fibers.17 Thus, while the 69% loss of spiral ganglion neurons in this case was significant, there may be additional mechanisms contributing to his post-operative sensorineural hearing loss. Notably, there was a disproportionate loss of the peripheral dendrites of the spiral ganglion neurons, with relative preservation of the axons, suggesting a retrograde degeneration. There was also minor patchy inner hair cell loss, but not enough to account for the profound hearing loss. In summary, there were no findings at the light microscopic level that appear to have conferred an increased risk of sensorineural hearing loss with stapedectomy in this patient with NOG mutation. Generalization of these findings to NOG-Related Symphalangism Spectrum Disorder (NOG-SSD) patients, however, must be cautioned given this is a single pathologic report and there is known heterogeneity in the genotypes and phenotypes of these patients.

In the right ear, there was no specific pathologic correlate to explain the moderately elevated high frequency bone thresholds. Although there was some loss of hair cells and stria vascularis, the loss is distributed throughout the cochlea. There may be other mechanisms of sensorineural hearing loss not well seen at the light microscopic level, including degeneration of ribbon synapses and dendrites.

A heterozygous nonsense mutation in NOG resulted in Stapes Ankylosis with Broad Thumbs and Toes Syndrome in this patient. The temporal bone pathologic correlate for conductive hearing loss in this patient was circumferentially calcified cartilage crossing the stapedo-vestibular joint space in place of the normal annular ligament. The histopathological findings in this case suggest that conductive hearing loss related to NOG mutation should be improved after stapedectomy; however, care must be taken in extrapolating to all NOG mutation patients as there may be variability in the pathology.
