                      SLC12A3

Intronic pathogenic variants explain an important part of the missing heritability in Gitelman syndrome. Long-read sequencing should be considered in diagnostic workflows for Gitelman syndrome.

In some patients with apparent Gitelman syndrome, a
genetic diagnosis cannot be established. Previous studies dem-
onstrate that, in 8% –31% of all patients, only one pathogenic
variant in SLC12A3 can be found. 9–14 Recent studies showed
heterozygous carriers of one pathogenic variant in SLC12A3
may display a weak intermediate phenotype. Blanchardet al.15
compared 81 heterozygous carriers of a pathogenic/likely
pathogenic SLC12A3 variant with 82 healthy noncarriers and
79 patients with Gitelman syndrome of similar age, body mass
index, and sex ratio. They found heterozygous and noncarriers
were clinically and biochemically very similar. No differences
were observed in BP or in plasma electrolytes, although a subtle
intermediate phenotype was observed in heterozygous carriers,
characterized by slightly higher serum calcium concentrations

10,14,17,18 On the other hand, a
very recent, but small, study by Rossanti et al. 19 investigated
SLC12A3 introns in 13 patients with genetically unresolved
Gitelman syndrome and found that the additional yield might
be moderate: only one additional likely pathogenic variant was
identiﬁed. Thus, it is currently unclear whether the ﬁndings of
the ﬁrst four studies translate to other populations and whether
other intronic variants are still missed.
Recent advances in genetic techniques allow detection of
such intronic variants and structural and promoter variants,
thus increasing diagnostic yield for monogenic disorders.
For example, single-molecule molecular inversion probes
(smMIPs)
20 increased diagnostic yield in ABCA4-related dis-
ease,21,22 whole-genome sequencing in Alagille syndrome, 23
and long-read sequencing 24,25 in neurodevelopmental disor-
ders.26 Notably, a previous study by Vargas-Poussou et al. 9
in 448 unrelated patients with Gitelman syndrome detected
a large number of structural variants in SLC12A3. However,
the technique used in this study, multiplex ligation-dependent
probe ampliﬁcation (MLPA) screening of SLC12A3 exons, is
primarily suited to detect exonic deletions and not suited to
detect inversions, insertions, or intronic deletions. Of the afore-
mentioned three techniques, long-read sequencing has the
highest sensitivity for detecting such structural variations. Fur-
thermore, long-read sequencing has the potential to assess vari-
ant phasing.
24
Some genetic variants detected by long-read sequencing,
smMIPs, or genome sequencing require further investigation
to determine pathogenicity, especially for intronic variants.
Pathogenicity of newly identi ﬁed intronic variants can be
assessed using a midigene approach. 22,27–32 This technique
incorporates the genomic sequence containing the variant into
an expression vector to examine aberrant splicing defects.

of SLC12A3 in a large cohort of patients with Gitelman syn-
drome. We hypothesized that, in patients in whom only one

could explain the disease. We used long-read sequencing to
examine nonexon regions of SLC12A3 and performed variant
phasing to demonstrate whether two variants are on opposite
alleles.

Clinical Data
Clinical data were obtained as part of routine clinical care at
the two medical centers. Electrolytes were measured in serum
(sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, chloride, and bicar-
bonate) and urine (sodium, potassium, potassium-creatinine
ratio, calcium, magnesium, calcium-creatinine ratio). Reference
values in the two centers were similar. Provided potassium-
creatinine ratios are before supplementation. We also collected
the following information: birth year, sex, family history, con-
sanguinity, age at presentation, age at exploration, main symp-
toms, creatinine, renin levels, and aldosterone levels.
Generation of Long-Range PCR Amplicons
Producing long-range PCR amplicons of a gene of interest
allows long-read sequencing for many patients simultaneously.
To achieve this, primers were designed using Primer-BLAST
for amplicons of approximately 10–11 kb. Primers were chosen
to produce seven overlapping PCR amplicons that would span
all introns of SLC12A3 and approximately 3.5 kb directly
upstream of SLC12A3 (primer sequences in Supplemental
Table 1). Genomic DNA was isolated from whole blood. The
LongAmp Taq polymerase (catalog number M0287L; NEB) was
used for PCR reactions, and the protocol was optimized individ-
ually for every patient and every amplicon (Supplemental
Appendix). Of the 120 patient DNA samples, 95 patients were
selected for long-read sequencing on the basis of DNA quality.
In these patients, amplicon generation was successful for at least
six of the seven different amplicons.

Splice Assays
HEK293 cells were grown in poly-l-lysin –coated plates and
transfected with a wild-type or mutant midigene by using lip-

assessed using Sanger sequencing (Supplemental Appendix).

Long-Read Sequencing of SLC12A3 Increased
Diagnostic Yield
To examine intronic variants in patients with unresolved Gitel-
man syndrome, seven amplicons of 10 –11 kb in size were
designed to contain the exons and introns of SLC12A3, span-

56,947,674 (GRCh37/hg19). Long-range PCR successfully gen-
erated six or seven amplicons in 96 patients with unresolved
Gitelman syndrome. Of these 96 patients, 95 were selected for
long-read sequencing, together with one patient who had been
diagnosed (positive control; Figure 1A). Analysis of the long-
read sequencing data showed a high number of high- ﬁdelity
reads that were approximately 10–11 kb in size (Supplemental
Figure 2). Coverage of all amplicons ranged between 100 3
and 10,0003.
Long-read sequencing identi ﬁed a new likely pathogenic
or new pathogenic variant in 46 patients (47%; Figure 1, B
and C, Supplemental Table 3). In most of these patients
(45), one likely pathogenic or pathogenic variant had been
detected previously. In patients in whom no likely patho-
genic or pathogenic variant had previously been detected,
long-read sequencing detected a new likely pathogenic or
pathogenic variant in only one patient.

DISCUSSION
In approximately 8% –31% of patients with symptoms of
Gitelman syndrome, only one pathogenic variant is currently
found, depending on the techniques used. 9–14 Here, we show
that resequencing of exons and introns can help to identify a
second pathogenic variant in a signiﬁcant number of patients.
Long-read sequencing identi ﬁed a second variant in 67% of
patients for whom one likely pathogenic or pathogenic
SLC12A3 variant was previously found (45 patients). Of these
patients, 16 (36%) had a likely pathogenic or pathogenic
intronic variant; in most patients, the variant was located .20
bp into the intron. We identiﬁed novel intronic candidate var-
iants and conﬁrmed pathogenicity for six of these variants by
an SLC12A3 midigene splice assay. Moreover, patients in
whom two likely pathogenic or pathogenic variants are found
have a more severe phenotype than other patients.
This study provides evidence for the pathogenicity of six
novel intronic variants. Most of these variants are located deep
in introns, and were previously missed by commonly used
sequencing techniques that cover only exons and exon-intron
boundaries. These ﬁndings demonstrate the importance of

before start of the study, which would have picked up most
copy number variations and some structural variation disrupt-

Identifying additional exonic variants in resequencing stud-
ies is not unusual 22,57–59 and can be explained by several fac-
tors. Of the total 95 patients, 19 had previously been
investigated with SSCP only, which has a sensitivity of approx-
imately 80%–90%.60,61 Other variants had been missed because
of technical challenges associated with Sanger sequencing.
Lastly, updated annotations led to reclassiﬁcation of previously
dismissed variants.
An important challenge for all PCR ampli ﬁcation–based
sequencing technologies is the occurrence of ampli ﬁcation
bias.
62,63 Ampliﬁcation bias will inherently also occur in the
targeted tubulopathy gene panel that is used by many
clinicians and researchers.33 By looking at the apparent zygos-
ity of variants in regions where amplicons overlap, we
observed that ampli ﬁcation bias is also present in our data.
However, the fact that long-read sequencing reidentiﬁed 96%
of the previously known likely pathogenic or pathogenic var-
iants, and additionally identiﬁed 22 exonic variants that had
been missed before, suggests ampliﬁcation bias is only a minor
limitation in targeted long-read sequencing ofSLC12A3.
A last advantage of long-read sequencing is the possibility
to evaluate variant phasing.
24 In 22 patients, the two identiﬁed
variants located in trans (44%) and, in one other patient, the
two variants were actually positioned in cis.S e c o n d l y ,t h e
c.1670-191C.T occurred on a different allelic background in
approximately 70% of patients, excluding a founder effect for
those patients,64 in line with a study in a Taiwanese popula-
tion.14 Lastly, variant phasing allowed us to reclassify one
VUS as likely pathogenic.
A limitation of our midigene splice assay is that the degree
of mis-splicing might be dif ﬁcult to assess. For example, our
study shows residual normally spliced mRNA for the c.1670-
191C.Tv a r i a n tin vitro, whereas urinary mRNA analysis by
Lo et al.
14 suggests that 100% of mis-splicing occurs in vivo.
Conﬁrming our results, a very recent study by Rossanti et al.19
tested four known intronic variants, including the c.1670-
191C.T variant, using a minigene splice assay and found the
same aberrant splicing patterns. An explanation for the quanti-
tative splicing differences can be found in the source of
mRNA, i.e., a urinary versus a kidney cell model; similar chal-
lenges have been observed for some variants in the well-studied
ABCA4 gene.
65,66 Our results suggest the SLC12A3 midigene
splice assay should be used as a qualitative assay rather than as
a quantitative assay. Another limitation is the amount of miss-
ing data in the phenotypic analyses ( .50% for most parame-
ters) due to the retrospective nature of the study, which
presents a possible source of bias for the phenotypic analyses.
Our study illustrates two important indications for
SLC12A3 long-read sequencing. First, long-read sequencing
detects both intronic and additional exonic variants in
patients in whom only one likely pathogenic or pathogenic
variant was identiﬁed previously. Other techniques that detect
intronic variants, such as smMIPs and short-read whole-
genome sequencing, may provide an alternative to long-read
sequencing.
22,41,67 Secondly, we suggest considering long-read
sequencing in patients for which parental DNA is not avail-
able, because variant phasing can often provide insight on
whether two candidate variants are indeed located on oppo-
site alleles.
36,68 In contrast, the diagnostic yield in patients in
whom at most one VUS was previously detected in SLC12A3
was low in this study (second variant found in only one
patient). This low yield does not support routine sequencing
of SLC12A3 introns in all patients with suspected Gitelman
syndrome. Additionally, we suggest the inclusion of the
intronic c.1670-191C.T variant in Sanger and gene panel
sequencing approaches because this variant is recurrent in dif-
ferent populations.
