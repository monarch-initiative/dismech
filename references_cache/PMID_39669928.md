A previously healthy young male teenager had acute onset left sided hemiplegia associated with deviation of angle of mouth. The patient underwent thrombolysis with partial recovery of the weakness. Post thrombolysis, the patient was referred to us to rule out cardiological cause of stroke. The patient had a regular heart rate of 45 beats per minute, blood pressure was 110/74 mm of Hg. Physical examination revealed absence of a wave in the jugular venous pulse. Heart sounds were normal and there was absence of any cardiac murmur.

The ECG displayed a regular rhythm narrow QRS and no visible p waves/ fibrillatory wave retrograde conduction during the junctional beats (Figure 1).

Twelve lead electrocardiogram shows heart rate of 45 beats per minute, junctional rhythm without retrograde conduction during the junctional beats. There are no visible p waves/fibrillatory wave preceding the QRS complex. Lower panel shows MV Doppler inflow velocities (left) and septal annulus TDI (right). There was presence of E wave without A wave indicating loss of atrial contractility. Similar findings were obtained at TV inflow and lateral mitral annulus TDI (not shown).

On 2D echocardiography, mitral and tricuspid inflow demonstrated normal E wave but absent A wave which was confirmed by absence of a’ at lateral and septal annulus by tissue Doppler imaging (Figure 1 lower panel). There was no mitral valve prolapse/no patent foramen ovale on transoesophageal echocardiography. No thrombus/spontaneous echo contrast was found in bilateral atria. The left ventricle ejection fraction was normal.

FDG‐PET was normal and showed no evidence of myocarditis. The potassium levels/ thyroid function tests were within normal range and the patient was not on any drugs that could cause sinus node dysfunction.

The patient underwent an electrophysiology study. Intracardiac recordings showed a junctional rhythm varying between narrow complex and right bundle branch morphology. HV interval was 48 ms. There was absence of atrial activity in bilateral atria. Pacing at maximum output from right atrium and coronary sinus did not lead to capture of the atrium/ demonstrable p wave. (20 mV/2 ms) (Figure 2).

Intracardiac electrograms. There is absence of atrial electrograms on both coronary sinus and right atrium. HV interval is 48 ms. Pacing at maximum output from right atrium and coronary sinus did not lead to atrial capture/ presence of p waves on surface ECG.

Cardiac MRI showed presence of dilated right atrium and absence of contraction in bilateral atria. There was presence of biatrial late gadolinium enhancement. (Figure 3).

Presence of biatrial late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MRI shown in apical 4 chamber view.

Genetic analysis was sent to rule out atrial cardiomyopathy. The genetic analysis revealed heterozygous, autosomal dominant, missense variant (chr15:73616510 T > G c.2063A > C p. Asn688Thr) in the exon 7 of the HCN4 gene which has been described in association with familial sick sinus syndrome. The patient's paternal aunt also had a history of pacemaker implant at 48 years of age although the detailed history and genetic analysis for her are not available with us (Figure 4).

Family pedigree of the patient. The paternal aunt underwent pacemaker implantation at the age of 48 years (further details not available).

The patient was started on anticoagulation therapy to prevent further thromboembolic events, in view of history of cerebral infarction. The patient has been on follow up for the last 3 months with regular physiotherapy and has not had any episodes of syncope/neurologic symptoms/recurrence of stroke.

The etiology of stroke in the young can be divided into cardiac and non‐cardiac causes. The cardiac causes predominantly include rheumatic heart disease, mitral valve prolapse and congenital heart disease. Since the patient presented in junctional rhythm, our main differential included either transient causes (new onset atrial fibrillation, transient electrolyte abnormalities (hyperkalaemia), digoxin toxicity, acute myocarditis with atrial involvement, dilated cardiomyopathy) or permanent causes (associated with muscular dystrophies and genetic cardiomyopathies).

Atrial standstill is a rare condition with absence of atrial mechanical/electrical activity characterized by “(1) the absence of P waves in the ECG and of A waves in the intracardiac recordings, (2) narrow QRS complexes, (3) evidence of atrial paralysis (absence of A waves in the jugular venous pulse, in the atrial pressure recording and in the mitral Doppler recording), and (4) inability to stimulate the atria.” Our patient had evidence of all these findings.

The clinical presentation of atrial standstill depends on the age group of presentation. Patients in the paediatric age group present with high rates of thromboembolic events whereas in adults the presentation may be syncope or dyspnea secondary to congestive heart failure.
1



The electrophysiology study in these patients is important for risk stratification and detection of infrahisian disease. For patient planned for dual/ atrial based pacing, the possible atrial implant sites may be determined. The atrial muscles may be completely diseased and show a lack of capture, even at high output pacing. In patients of only RA involvement, CS pacing can be considered as a viable option. In elder age group, the natural history of atrial standstill has been described as early involvement of high and mid RA followed by progression to entire RA and then the left atrium.
2



The genetic analysis is important in these patients not only for the diagnostic purposes, but also because some of these genes may be associated with development of ventricular arrhythmias. Mutations in laminin (LMNA variants) have been associated with bradyarrhythmia, atrial fibrillation as well as ventricular arrhythmias.
3
 Similarly, mutation in SCN5A with connexin 40 polymorphisms have been reported in paediatric patients with atrial standstill/paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and thromboembolism/Brugada syndrome and ventricular arrhythmias.
4



The HCN4 gene encodes the potassium hyperpolarization‐activated cyclic nucleotide‐gated channel 4 protein. This shows slow kinetics of activation and inactivation, and is necessary for the spontaneous cardiac pacemaker impulse generation. Germline pathogenic variations in the HCN4 gene have been shown to be associated with familial sick sinus syndrome, specifically the autosomal dominant type 2 variety.
5



The mechanism by which sick sinus syndrome due to gene mutations result in atrial standstill have been best described for SCN5A mutation which is the most common genetic cause in paediatric patients with atrial standstill.
1
 In patients with SCN5A mutations, slowing down of both pacemaker function and conduction of action potential across the SA node‐atrium occurs.
6
 Histological examination of the atrial wall have been described to show presence of dense collagenous tissue, different stages of fibrosis with scattered myocardium.
7
 A combination of reducing pacemaking activity and atrial fibrosis contributes to the atrial standstill. This is supported by presence of right atrial enlargement and findings of late gadolinium enhancement on the cardiac MRI in our patient.

We present a case of HCN4 gene mutation presenting with atrial standstill and stroke in the young. This gene has been described with familial sick sinus syndrome and we describe its association with atrial standstill for the first time, to the best of our knowledge.
