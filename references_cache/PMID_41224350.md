OBJECTIVES: To assess the long-term effects and mortality rates associated with 
GH therapy in patients with Prader-Willi Syndrome (PWS). Prader-Willi Syndrome 
is a rare genetic disease characterized by growth hormone (GH) deficiency among 
other endocrine disorders.
METHODS: A systematic literature search was carried out on 4 databases including 
PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases. The search was based on 
two keywords: "Growth hormone" AND "Prader-Willi Syndrome". Outcome data 
included height-standard deviation score (SDS), weight SDS, body mass index 
(BMI)-SDS, insulin like growth factor 1 (IGF1), mortality, low density 
lipoprotein (LDL)-cholesterol, and blood glucose.
RESULTS: A total of 41 studies were included, of which 30 were involved in the 
meta-analysis. Following treatment with GH, height-SDS showed a significant 
increase compared to baseline in two timeline subgroups (≤2 years and >2 years), 
with a mean difference (MD) of 1.05 (95% CI: 0.92-1.18, p<0.00001) and 1.53 (95% 
CI: 1.23-1.82, p<0.00001), respectively. Patients on GH experienced a more 
pronounced increase in height-SDS compared to those who have not received GH. 
This was further associated with a lower BMI-SDS among GH-treated patients 
compared to their counterparts, with an MD of -1.02 (95% CI: -1.76 to -0.28, 
p=0.007; I²=84%, p=0.0003). Additionally, IGF1-SDS showed a marked increase 
after GH. Other metabolic effects include significant increase in LDL and blood 
glucose levels after GH treatment. The mortality rate in PWS patients undergoing 
GH treatment is estimated at 1.5% (95% CI: 0.8-2.2%), with causes including 
respiratory issues, cardiac arrest, infections, accidents, and gastrointestinal 
complications.
CONCLUSION: GH therapy in PWS significantly improves height and IGF-1 SDS, while 
relatively decreasing BMI compared to no-GH, indicative of lean mass growth and 
healthy development. Nonetheless, GH therapy requires careful metabolic 
monitoring due to its mixed effects on cholesterol and glucose levels.PROSPERO

Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare, complex genetic disorder caused by an abnormality in the paternal chromosome 15, specifically in the 15q11-q13 imprinting region. It affects approximately one in every 15,000 to 25,000 live births and manifests in both genders and across all ethnic groups. Its clinical presentation includes a variety of symptoms such as hypotonia, hyperphagia, obesity, developmental delays, and endocrine abnormalities such as growth hormone (GH) deficiency.1

The impact of PWS on patients and their relatives is profound. Hyperphagia, such as excessive hunger, leads to obesity. Without careful management, this obesity increases the risks of diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, among other conditions related to obesity.2 Endocrine disorders, including GH deficiency, can impact stature, body composition, and metabolism. In addressing the countless challenges posed by PWS, the use of GH has emerged as a cornerstone in the management of the condition. Besides promoting linear growth, GH therapy in PWS also enhances body composition, increasing lean body mass and decreasing fat mass. Additionally, GH treatment was associated with improved physical strength, agility, and, in some cases, cognitive function.3 The mechanism of action of GH in PWS is believed to involve not only the stimulation of the growth plate but also the enhancement of protein synthesis and the modulation of metabolism, which together contribute to the observed benefits.4

Despite the widespread adoption of GH therapy in PWS and the body of evidence supporting its short-term benefits, a significant gap exists in our understanding of its long-term effects. Most existing studies have focused on the outcomes of GH treatment over periods of less than one year, leaving questions about the sustainability of the benefits, eventual long-term adverse effects, and the overall impact on the lifespan and quality of life of afflicted individuals. Moreover, concerns have been raised, such as the risk of scoliosis progression, sleep apnea, and glucose metabolism alterations, among patients under long-term GH therapy. Yet, without comprehensive long-term data, these risks remain poorly quantified.5 Additionally, previous comprehensive reviews did not focus on the long-term effects of GH treatment and did not explore critical outcomes, particularly its impact on mortality.3,6

Given this challenge, our systematic review and meta-analysis aim at filling this critical gap in knowledge, focusing exclusively on studies with a follow-up period of more than one year. Our goal is to offer a comprehensive synthesis of the current evidence regarding the long-term outcomes and mortality associated with GH therapy in individuals with PWS.

This systematic review and meta-analysis was carried out in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. It included all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies, such as cohorts and cross-sectional studies that reported long-term effects (one year and more) of GH (the intervention) in patients with PWS.7 Considering controlled studies, the non-use of GH was the comparator.

A comprehensive search of literature was conducted on 4 databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane library databases, from inception to March 2025. The search was based mainly on 2 keywords: “growth hormone” AND “Prader-Willi syndrome”, aiming at a broader literature screening. Initial search results were screened for relevance with respect of the research question, based on titles and abstracts. Studies deemed relevant underwent full-text review. Exclusion applied for case reports or studies reporting only on short-term outcomes.

Two authors (AM and NG) carried out this process. Each author independently reviewed the full-text articles, and any disagreements were resolved either by mutual consensus or, when necessary, through arbitration by a third author (NAA).

Data of interest were extracted from the selected studies and organized into a pre-designed Excel spreadsheet. The datasheet captured basic study information such as design and sample characteristics, such as sample size, age of GH and no GH groups, gender, aim, and main findings. The outcome data were also extracted based on height-standard deviation score (SDS), weight SDS, body mass index (BMI)-SDS, insulin like growth factor 1 (IGF1), mortality, low density lipoprotein (LDL)-cholesterol, and blood glucose. Two independent authors (MA2 and NA2) carried out the data extraction. Any discrepancies were addressed through discussion or resolved by consultation with a third reviewer (AM, EM, FFM, or KAH).

We used the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) to assess the quality of observational studies (cohort and cross-sectional).8 For RCTs, the Cochrane risk of bias-2 tool (ROB2) tool was used. The ROB2 tool is a validated and widely recognized instrument designed to assess the risk of bias in RCTs, focusing on key areas including “the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results”.9 Furthermore, we used the “risk of bias in non-randomized studies - of interventions” (ROBINS-I) for non-randomized clinical trials.

The meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3.10 Changes in continuous variables were pooled as mean differences (MD). The random effect model was used to account for heterogeneity, which was analyzed through the visual assessment of the forest plots and the I2 test. We calculated the mortality rate using OpenMetaAnalyst software.11 A confidence interval (CI) of 95% was used and p-value was considered significant if it was <0.05.

The search strategy yielded a total of 1052 articles, including 361 duplicates. After exclusion of the duplicates, title and abstract of the remaining 691 articles were screened, of these, 51 underwent full-text screening. Finally, 41 studies were included, of them 30 were included in the meta-analyses, based on data availability, and 11 were only involved in the qualitative review.12-52 (Figure 1).

Of the total 41 studies, 12 were RCTs, 2 non-randomized clinical trials, 2 cross sectional studies, and 25 cohorts; all met our inclusion criteria. These studies encompassed data from 2,732 participants with PWS, aged from infancy to 35 years, followed for over a year, up to 10 years in some cases. The sample sizes of the included studies ranged from 10 to 522 participants; most of them initiating GH treatment in childhood, and the remaining in adolescence or early adulthood (Table 1).

Of the included RCTs, 8 were assessed as having a low risk of bias across all domains, reflecting strong methodological quality. Two studies raised some concerns, while another 2 were rated as high risk of bias, underscoring the need for improved reporting practices (Figure 2).

One non-randomized trial was assessed as having a low risk of bias and the other had a moderate risk, using ROBINS-1 tool (Figure 2). Regarding cohort studies, using NOS, 10 studies were of high quality (7-9 stars) and 15 of moderate quality (4-6 stars) (Table 2). Two cross sectional studies were rated as high-quality using NOS tool (Table 3).

After GH therapy, height-SDS significantly increased by reference to baseline in 2 timeline sub-analyses (≤2 years and >2 years), with an MD of 1.05 (95% CI: 0.92 to 1.18, p<0.00001) and 1.53 (95% CI: 1.23 to 1.82, p<0.00001), respectively. Heterogeneity was moderate to high, with I² calculated at 63% (p<0.00001) for the ≤2-year group and 95% (p<0.00001) for the >2-year group. (Figure 3). Considering controlled studies, patients who took GH had a significantly greater increase in height-SDS by comparison to those who did not take GH, with an MD between the 2 groups of 1.33 (95% CI: 0.74 to 1.91, p<0.00001) and I2 was 54% (p=0.03) (Figure 4).

Likewise, weight SDS showed a statistically significant rise compared to baseline values in the two timeline subgroups (≤2 years and >2 years), with an MD of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.5 to 0.94, p<0.00001) and 1.13 (95% CI: 0.87 to 1.39, p<0.00001). The I² values were 68% (p=0.0003) for the ≤2-year subgroup and 71% (p=0.002) for the >2-year subgroup (Appendix 1).

No significant differences were observed between baseline and post-treatment values regarding BMI-SDS in either follow up times (≤2 years, and >2 years), with an overall MD of 0.06 (95% CI: -0.12 to 0.25, p=0.5) (Figure 5). However, meta-analysis of controlled studies showed that GH treatment was associated with a lower BMI-SDS compared to no GH, with an MD of -1.02 (95% CI: -1.76 to -0.28, p=0.007) and I2=84%, p=0.0003 (Figure 5).

- Comparison of body mass index-standard deviation score values: a) pre- and post-growth hormone (GH) treatment, sub-grouped by follow-up time (≤2 years and >2 years); b) between patients treated with GH and those without GH treatment in controlled studies. CI: confidence interval

After GH therapy, IGF1-SDS significantly increased compared to baseline in both subgroups (≤2 years and >2 years). The ≤2-year subgroup demonstrated an MD of 2.73 (95% CI: 1.54 to 3.92, p<0.00001) with an I² of 99% (p<0.00001), while the >2-year subgroup showed an MD of 1.05 (95% CI: 0.75 to 1.36, p<0.00001) with an I² of 98% (p<0.00001) (Appendix 2).

LDL-cholesterol also decreased significantly after GH therapy, with an MD of -11.59 (95%CI: -17.21, -5.98, p<0.0001) and an I2 of 97%, p<0.00001 (Appendix 3). GH therapy was also associated with increased blood glucose levels, with an MD of 3.75 (95% CI: 2.6 to 4.91, p<0.00001) and an I² of 96% (p<0.00001) (Appendix 4).

The mortality rate in PWS patients under GH treatment, estimated from the meta-analysis, was estimated at 1.5% (95%CI: 0.8, 2.2%) with causes including disseminated intravascular coagulation, cardiac and respiratory arrest, immunodeficiency, pneumonia, sleep apnea, respiratory insufficiency, accidental death (falls), death in the bath, and stomach perforation (Figure 6).

Our systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the long-term outcomes of PWS treatment with GH. It showed that follow-up of PWS patients whether adults or children after being treated with GH resulted in an increase in height-, weight-, and IGF-1-SDS, along with blood glucose compared to the baseline values measured before treatment. Growth hormone was also associated with decreased levels of LDL-cholesterol but increased glucose levels after treatment, by comparison to baseline. Moreover, the height-SDS was significantly increased after GH therapy while the BMI-SDS decreased compared to patients who did not receive GH treatment. The subgroup analysis showed more change in height-SDS and weight-SDS after more than 2 years of follow-up. However, the change in IGF1 change was higher in less than or equal to 2 years subgroup of follow-up. Mortality rate in patients who took GH treatment was found to be minimal as it was estimated at 1.5% only. The reported causes were disseminated intravascular coagulation: cardiac and respiratory arrest, immunodeficiency, pneumonia, sleep apnea, respiratory insufficiency, accidental death (fall), death in the bath, and stomach perforation.

The distinction between our meta-analysis and previous ones lies in our analysis of the long-term effects, including its impact on mortality rates. We included studies that conducted longer follow-up after GH treatment to monitor its effects on various parameters.3,6

A prior meta-analysis, authored by Rosenberg et al,6 summarized recent research on GH treatment in PWS, revealing a 12-month improvement in body composition, with increased lean body mass by 1.95 kg associated with a decrease in fat mass of -2.2%. No changes were reported in BMI or other metabolic parameters including LDL cholesterol, fasting glucose, and bone mineral density. On the other hand, no significant safety concerns were reported. Our findings differ, particularly in LDL-cholesterol and glucose levels, due to our inclusion of both adults and children and our focus on long-term GH treatment outcomes, unlike the previous study, which was limited to adults treated for a year without assessing long-term effects. The short-term safety and efficacy of GH was thoroughly investigated. According to a previous comprehensive analysis, GH therapy in adults improves body composition without adversely affecting bone and cardiovascular health in the short run. The therapy was deemed safe and beneficial, with no significant adverse events observed.3

One of the major findings highlights the significant role of GH in enhancing the height-SDS of PWS patients, showing increases of 1.05 and 1.53 points after ≤2 years and >2 years of treatment, respectively. Additionally, BMI is decreased compared to control patients who didn’t receive GH therapy. This underscores the potential of GH treatment in normalizing growth patterns and aiding patients to reach normal height-for-age standards and weight, potentially enhancing their overall quality of life.

When otherwise healthy children are overweight during the growth phase, their eventual height usually ends up being normal, often above their genetic potential and midparental height.53 In contrast, children with PWS experience delayed growth and typically attain shorter stature, despite being overweight. GH secretion is consequently reduced. The average final height is less than the midparental height; for men, it is 161.6±8.1 cm (-2.4 SDS) and for females, it is 150.2±5.5 cm (-2.5 SDS). On GH therapy, the BMI is reduced by increasing height and allowing the growth process to occur naturally.54

Following 2 years of GH therapy, the “Psychological General Well-Being Index (PGWBI)” and the “36-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36)”, 2 validated questionnaires, showed improvements in quality of life of individuals with PWS.55 In another study, after receiving GH treatment for one year, subjects voluntarily reported improved psychological well-being.56 In a clinical trial, a 20-item behavioral scale completed by caregivers was used to document improvements after one year of GH therapy. The enhancement in overall quality of life, along with the absence of depression and anxiety, was associated with improvements in overall appearance, including height and weight. This led to increased self-confidence and protection for children from bullying.46

In this review, the meta-analysis of controlled studies showed that GH therapy is linked with a lower BMI-SDS compared with no GH, although no significant effect on BMI was demonstrated in pre-to-post GH analysis. This may indicate a role of GH in preserving body fat distribution when initiated at an early age. Consistently, Grootjen et al34 observed that children who received GH within their first year of life exhibited more optimal trunk/peripheral fat ratio and lower overall body fat percentages compared to those who began treatment between ages 2 and 5. This discrepancy likely stems from GH’s more pronounced effects on fat metabolism and its ability to prevent unfavorable fat distribution in earlier stages.

Additionally, early GH initiation is associated with a more significant and sustained increase in lean BMI in children, particularly beneficial for infants with PWS facing hypotonia and motor developmental delays. Grootjen et al34 noted that children receiving GH within their first year of life show a greater rise in lean body mass index than those who started between ages 2 and 5, potentially enhancing early childhood motor development.

Our findings, combined with the supportive evidence above highlight the relevance of initiating GH treatment early in infancy for patients with PWS. This early intervention is vital for mitigating PWS-associated risks like central obesity, insulin resistance, and cardiometabolic disease. Furthermore, initiating GH treatment early supports increased lean body mass and can significantly benefit motor development in infants with PWS, underscoring its importance for optimal health outcomes in this population.

The present study showed that GH therapy in PWS patients influences metabolic profiles, notably lowering LDL-cholesterol which might reduce dyslipidemia risks by improving fat distribution and metabolism. In addition to its direct impact on the hepatic LDL-cholesterol receptors, which control the amount of circulating LDL-cholesterol, GH also indirectly affects lean body mass stability which enhances physical activity.3,6,34 Given that physical exercise is known to raise HDL cholesterol levels, it may have a role in the prepubertal children with PWS experiencing an increase in HDL cholesterol.57 However, GH raises blood glucose levels, as demonstrated in this meta-analysis, suggesting a potential diabetes risk. A disturbance of glucose metabolism brought on by insulin resistance stimulation is one of the potential side effects of GH. Therefore, an oral glucose tolerance test is advised in obese children older than 12 having a family history of diabetes during GH medication, and fasting insulin and plasma glucose levels are monitored together with the levels of HbA1c.4 These dual outcomes highlight a complex impact of GH on metabolic health, underscoring the need for a more nuanced approach in its administration to optimize benefits and minimize risks.

The meta-analysis of patients on GH showed a pooled mortality rate estimated at 1.5% (95%CI 0.8 – 2.2%). However, due to a lack of data, a comparative analysis with patients who did not take GH was not possible. According to several reports, the annual mortality risk for people with PWS is estimated as high as 3% for all age groups. A common cause of death, including sudden deaths, is respiratory illness.45 The history of sudden death in children with PWS under GH therapy raises concerns about the potential development of sleep apnea during treatment. This association was brought to light when the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) was alerted in 2003 of at least 7 PWS children dying shortly after GH treatment initiation. While respiratory failure was suspected in these deaths, it remained uncertain whether such a complication was related to the known effect of GH or mortality increased via another mechanism. In either explanation, drug overdose was considered a potential cause.38 Other potential mechanisms involve sodium and water retention and fibroblast activation leading to soft-tissue edema.38 GH therapy was observed to increase extracellular water, in addition to increasing IGF-1, consistent with our findings. This complication is more concerning for conditions requiring combined testosterone replacement therapy, such as male hypopituitarism, as testosterone potentiates the fluid retention.58

The respiratory effects of GH treatment intersect with the presence or risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) or other sleep disorders. While GH therapy can improve both sleep quality and lung function and growth in some PWS patients, its use requires careful respiratory function assessment and monitoring for sleep disorders to ensure its safety and effectiveness, notably in children having respiratory disorders or OSA.59 However, there is controversy in the literature about such respiratory risks.60,61 Notably, a study found that during or after the first year of life, OSA in children with PWS developed irrespective of the start of GH treatment. 31 The authors concluded that GH therapy can be safely initiated early, but emphasized the importance of regular sleep assessments.

Consequently, consensus guidelines advise doing polysomnography (PSG) prior to starting GH treatment in children with PWS, particularly in obese and overweight ones. It is also advised to repeat PSG every 3-to-6 months after the treatment. When OSA is diagnosed, ENT evaluation is necessary because GH therapy may cause adenotonsillar tissue expansion.4

There was a significant heterogeneity in the participant’s characteristics because PWS is uncommon. Furthermore, the small number of patients in each trial alone increased the possibility of bias. This could be a result of the relatively small number of PWS patients that are probably going to be treated at each center as well as potential challenges in finding and getting adult PWS patients to agree to research. Furthermore, it can be a byproduct of the great genetic and phenotypic diversity of PWS. However, we included all the available studies that were done on the long-term outcomes of GH therapy, along with those that investigated mortality among PWS patients using GH. The subgroup analysis also showed the effect on the long run by increasing the follow-up period. We can recommend the use of GH in PWS children and adults with the appropriate dosing and close monitoring.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that GH treatment in PWS patients—both adults and children resulted in increased height-SDS, and IGF1-SDS compared to baseline values before treatment. Furthermore, compared to individuals not receiving GH therapy, there was a significant increase in height-SDS and a relative reduction in BMI-SDS. This has significant implications in achieving optimal statural growth and enhancing the patients’ quality of life and wellbeing.

Additionally, GH treatment was linked to lower LDL-cholesterol levels but elevated glucose levels during therapy. Notably, the subgroup followed for up to 2 years exhibited a larger increase in IGF1. This mixed effect on metabolism highlights the importance of monitoring PWS patients undergoing GH therapy to prevent and diagnose any adverse outcomes, particularly metabolic homeostasis disturbances. The mortality rate among patients on GH therapy was relatively low at 1.5%. Further comparative longitudinal studies are warranted to establish the benefits of GH treatment on survival among PWS patients.
