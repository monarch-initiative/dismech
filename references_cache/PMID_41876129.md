# Timing of high-dose folic acid supplementation in the periconceptional period among women taking antiseizure medications and risk of major congenital anomalies: a target trial emulation.
**Authors:** Sun Y, Alvestad S, Cohen JM, Igland J, Gilhus NE, Vegrim HM, Tomson T, Christensen J, Leinonen MK, Gissler M, Zoega H, Razaz N, Dreier JW, Bjørk MH
**Journal:** J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1136/jnnp-2025-337395](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2025-337395)

## Content

BACKGROUND: High-dose folic acid supplementation is recommended for women using antiseizure medication (ASMs) to reduce risk of major congenital anomalies (MCAs) in offspring, but evidence is lacking.

METHODS: We conducted a sequential target trial emulation using nationwide register data from Denmark, Iceland, Norway, and Sweden (1997-2020). Eligible women using ASMs were included and classified as initiators or non-initiators based on whether they filled prescription for high-dose folic acid within one week of inclusion. Risk difference (RD) and risk ratio (RR) for MCAs diagnosed before one year of age in offspring were estimated for initiators of high-dose folic acid (4 or 5 mg/day) supplementation in three periconceptional periods (13-52 weeks before, 1-12 weeks before, and 1-12 weeks after pregnancy onset) compared with non-initiators.

RESULTS: We included 18 255, 13 619, and 12 365 eligible pregnancies and identified 1047 (5.7%), 521 (3.8%) and 1778 (14.4%) initiators of high-dose folic acid in the three periods, respectively. Initiation of high-dose folic acid 1-12 weeks before pregnancy was associated with a lower prevalence of MCA in offspring (2.6% vs 4.8% among non-initiators), corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 2.2 percentage points (95% CI 0.5 to 3.5) and a 45% relative risk reduction (RR=0.55, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.91). No risk reduction was observed with initiation 1-12 weeks after (RR=1.27, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.64) or 13-52 weeks before (RR=0.99, 95% CI 0.65 to 1.36) pregnancy onset.

CONCLUSIONS: Initiation of high-dose folic acid in the 12 weeks before pregnancy was associated with a reduced risk of MCA in offspring of women using ASMs while initiation in the 12 weeks after pregnancy onset was not. High-dose folic acid supplementation (in doses ranging between ≥1 and 5 mg per day) has been recommended for women taking antiseizure medications (ASMs) to reduce risk of major congenital anomalies (MCAs) in offspring.

Most of the previous studies did not show a protective effect of folic acid supplementation during the periconceptional period on risk of MCAs in the offspring of women taking ASMs.

Initiation of high-dose folic acid supplementation 1–12 weeks before pregnancy was associated with an absolute risk reduction of 2.2 percentage points and a 45% relative risk reduction of MCAs in offspring.

In contrast, initiation of high-dose folic acid supplementation 1–12 weeks after pregnancy onset was not associated with a reduced risk of MCAs.

Initiation of high-dose folic acid supplementation 13–52 weeks before pregnancy was not associated with a reduced overall risk of MCAs except for subgroup analyses, which highlight the need for further research that considers sustained use in addition to timing of initiation.

This study provides evidence to support the effectiveness of high-dose folic acid supplementation among women taking ASMs for reducing MCAs and highlights the importance of preconception initiation of high-dose folic acid supplementation.

 Prenatal exposure to antiseizure medications (ASMs) is associated with increased risk of major congenital anomalies (MCAs),1
3 leading to restrictions in use for women of childbearing potential.
4
 However, ASM treatment is essential for prevention of seizures in women with epilepsy, making it crucial to assess whether any interventions can reduce this risk.

High-dose folic acid supplements are defined as tablets of folic acid ≥1 mg/day, but are often recommended in doses of up to 4 or 5 mg/day for women taking ASMs to reduce risk of MCAs.
5 6
 The evidence for such recommendations is limited, and findings are inconsistent,
7 8
 leading to varied guidelines on folic acid supplementation among women taking ASMs.
6 9
 The emerging safety concerns of high-dose folic acid supplementation in the periconceptional period on cancer risk in offspring and in women themselves
10 11
 have led to further diversity of recommendations.
12 13



The current situation calls for high-quality studies; ideally randomised controlled trials (RCTs), to provide evidence to guide recommendation of folic acid supplementation among women taking ASMs.
12
 However, RCTs are time-consuming, costly and often ethically challenging in pregnant populations. Target trial emulation using observational data provides researchers with an approach to mimic RCTs. The approach has advantages of quickly providing evidence, reducing ambiguity of causal questions and preventing design-induced biases, etc although it still has risk of confounding and bias inherent in observational studies.
14 15
 We applied target trial emulation to estimate the effect of initiating high-dose folic acid supplementation in the different periconceptional period on the risk of offspring MCAs among women taking ASMs.

We developed a target trial protocol and emulated it with observational register data from four Nordic countries,
16
 which have universal healthcare and nationwide register systems.
17
 The source population included all births with a valid gestational age between 22 and 46 weeks from Denmark (1997–2016), Iceland (2006–2016), Norway (2009–2020) and Sweden (2006–2016). It also included terminated pregnancies between 12 and 21 gestational weeks from Norway (2009–2020).

The key components
18
 of a hypothetical target trial and their alignment in the emulation are presented in online supplemental table S1 and summarised below. The codes we used to identify information on ASMs, high-dose folic acid prescription, MCAs and other related factors mentioned below are presented in online supplemental table S2. Information on prescription of ASMs, high-dose folic acid and other medications was obtained from the national prescription registers, which contain all prescriptions dispensed at pharmacies in each country. The last menstrual period (LMP) was used as a marker of pregnancy onset. The periconceptional period was defined from 52 weeks before to 12 weeks after pregnancy onset and divided into three key windows: 13–52 weeks before, 1–12 weeks before and 1–12 weeks after pregnancy onset. A negative control period (13–20 weeks after pregnancy onset) was included. We emulated 72 sequential weekly trials, each starting at the beginning of the week (online supplemental figure S1). Sequential trials allow individuals to enter at multiple points, enabling comparisons of time-varying treatment strategies and improving comparability between treatment groups. Furthermore, emulating a sequence of trials is expected to yield more precise results compared with emulating a single trial.
19 20



Eligibility criteria for inclusion in the emulated weekly trials were women aged 18–49 years with a recorded pregnancy, who had at least one ASM prescription fill within 90 days prior to inclusion and who had not filled prescriptions for high-dose folic acid in the 12 months prior to inclusion. Women were excluded if they had fills of teratogenic medications (other than ASMs) or had a diagnosis of a teratogenic infection in the 12 months prior to inclusion. Additionally, participants were required to have an available healthcare history during the 12 months prior to inclusion and 12 months after birth. Online supplemental figure S2 shows flowcharts from three example weekly trials illustrating the identification of eligible pregnancies.

A daily dose of 5 mg (Denmark, Iceland, Sweden) or 4 mg (Norway) folic acid was recommended for women using ASMs in the study periods,
5
 and 5 mg tablets account for the majority of prescriptions dispensed in Denmark (97%), Sweden (91%) and Iceland (100%), while 1 mg tablets account for the majority of prescriptions dispensed in Norway (97%). We identified women who filled a prescription of 1 mg or 5 mg tablet of folic acid (online supplemental table S2_b) within 1 week after inclusion as initiators of high-dose folic acid (treatment). Women without such prescriptions were non-initiators and served as a comparison group. We assume women started taking the prescribed folic acid in the week of prescription. Women who were prescribed 1 mg tablet folic acid were supposed to take the recommended daily dosage, which was 4 or 5 tablets per day to meet the recommendation. However, we do not have information on whether they took the recommended dosage or whether they used folic acid continuously. Women identified as non-initiators in a given trial could meet the eligibility criteria for subsequent trials, making them eligible for inclusion in multiple weekly trials (online supplemental figure S1). Baseline characteristics at time of inclusion (see the Statistical analysis section for details) were adjusted for, to best emulate the randomised treatment assignment in an RCT.

MCAs were the outcome defined according to the classification systems used for surveillance of congenital anomalies in Europe (EUROCAT 1.5), adapted for Nordic coding systems (online supplemental table S2_c).
21
 Information on MCAs was available from live births in this study. We identified pregnancies with one or more offspring with MCA diagnosed within 12 months after birth. Information on MCA was obtained from patient, medical birth and cause of death registers in Denmark and Norway and from patient and medical birth registers in Iceland and Sweden.
22



We conducted per protocol analyses to estimate the treatment effect under ideal conditions, that is, among participants adhering to the protocol. Participants who deviated from the protocol were excluded, including (1) participants (both initiators and non-initiators) who did not meet the eligibility criteria on ASMs treatment anymore, which was unfortunately unavailable and defined alternatively as those who had no ASM prescription fills from 4 weeks before pregnancy to birth; (2) non-initiators who deviated from ‘no treatment’ in a single trial by later initiating high-dose folic acid in subsequent sequential trials within the defined periconceptional period (between 52 weeks before and 12 weeks after pregnancy onset). Finally, participants (both initiators and non-initiators) who had missing information on MCAs due to termination of pregnancy or stillbirth were excluded as well. Censoring weights were applied to adjust for the women who were excluded from the analysis. This means we gave more ‘weight’ to women who stayed in the study and delivered a live baby (who had information on MCAs) and had similar baseline characteristics and treatment status (whether they took high-dose folic acid) to the women who were excluded. By doing this, the results better reflect what would have been seen if the excluded women had remained in the study.
23



We used binomial regression to estimate risk differences and log-binomial regression models to estimate adjusted risk ratios (RRs) of MCA for initiators versus non-initiators in the three periconceptional periods and the control period by first pooling data of sequential trials in the specified periods of interest. Baseline characteristics including demographic factors, health status and medical history (online supplemental table S1) were adjusted for using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW),
24
 and ‘trial-week’ was included as a covariate.
20
 Non-parametric bootstrapping with 500 samples calculated percentile-based 95% CIs.

We estimated the effect of initiation of high-dose folic acid in several subgroup analyses, including: (1) in more narrow time windows (5–8 weeks, 9–12 weeks and 5–12 weeks after pregnancy onset) to examine effect of high-dose folic acid initiation in more specific timings after potential awareness of pregnancy, (2) with different prescription strengths (1 mg or 5 mg tablet), (3) among women who used specific ASMs (valproate, lamotrigine or one of five ASMs with teratogenic potential, including valproate, topiramate, carbamazepine, phenobarbital and phenytoin) and (4) among women with a record of epilepsy in the 12 months before inclusion. In post hoc analyses, we additionally estimated the effect of initiation of high-dose folic acid (5) in more narrow time windows before pregnancy (13–24 weeks and 12–52 weeks before pregnancy), (6) only including pregnancies from Norway, where the folic acid recommendation was 4 mg per day and (7) among women who used levetiracetam. Each set of subgroup analyses was independently performed.

We performed multiple imputations (n=20) by chained equations for covariates with missing data for pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and smoking, which contained a larger proportion of missing values, together with cohabitation status and education (missing value <2%). We used multivariable regression rather than IPTW and clustered robust standard errors to estimate the RR with/without further adjustment for pre-pregnancy BMI and smoking.

All the analyses were performed using Stata (V.18).

This study used register data from four Nordic countries. Patients or the public were not involved in the development of the research questions or outcome measures, or in the design, implementation or dissemination plans of this study.

The number of unique eligible pregnancies was 18 255 for the sequential trials in the 13–52 weeks before pregnancy; 13 619 in the 1–12 weeks before pregnancy; 12 365 in the 1–12 weeks after pregnancy onset and 8178 in the control period, which corresponded to 451 142, 129 691, 113 370 and 51 942 eligible trial inclusions, respectively (online supplemental figure S3). Among these unique pregnancies, we identified 1047 (5.7%), 521 (3.8%), 1778 (14.4%) and 278 (3.4%) initiators of high-dose folic acid in the four periods (online supplemental figure S3). The proportion of women who initiated high-dose folic acid in the 72 weekly trials ranged from 0.1% to 3.2% peaking at gestational weeks 6 and 7 (figure 1A).

Baseline characteristics of initiators and non-initiators of high-dose folic acid supplementation are presented in tables1
 2 for the periods of 1–12 weeks before and 1–12 weeks after pregnancy onset and online supplemental tables S3 and S4 for the rest two periods. When censored participants were excluded, there were 938, 496, 1738 and 275 initiators of high-dose folic acid supplementation left in the four periods. Initiators of high-dose folic acid were more likely to have epilepsy as the indication for ASMs prescription or to be women taking ASMs with potential teratogenic effects compared with non-initiators. Initiators also had fewer hospitalisations in the 12 months before inclusion than non-initiators. Initiators in the period 13–52 weeks and 1–12 weeks before pregnancy had a higher level of education than non-initiators. Baseline characteristics were generally balanced after weighting.

Among 496 initiators of high-dose folic acid in the 1–12 weeks before pregnancy, 14 had offspring with MCA leading to a weighted prevalence of MCA of 2.6% while the prevalence was 4.8% in offspring of non-initiators (table 3, figure 1B). Among 1738 initiators of high-dose folic acid in the 1–12 weeks after pregnancy onset, 95 had offspring with MCA leading to a weighted prevalence of MCA of 5.7% while the prevalence was 4.5% in offspring of non-initiators (table 3, figure 1B). Initiation of high-dose folic acid 1–12 weeks before pregnancy was associated with a lower risk of MCA in offspring compared with non-initiation, corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 2.2% (95% CI 0.5 to 3.5) and a 45% relative risk reduction (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.91) (table 3, figure 2). Initiation of high-dose folic acid 1–12 weeks after pregnancy onset was not associated with a reduced risk of MCA in offspring compared with non-initiation, corresponding to an absolute increase of 1.2% (95% CI –0.3 to 2.9), and a relative increased risk of 27% (RR 1.27, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.64) compared with offspring of non-initiators (table 3, figure 2).

The RR of MCA was 0.99 (95% CI 0.65 to 1.36) for women who initiated high-dose folic acid 13–52 weeks before pregnancy, and the RR of MCA was 1.37 (95% CI 0.58 to 2.50) for women who initiated during the control period (table 3, figure 2).

Subgroup analyses were largely consistent with the main findings (online supplemental table S5, figure 2). Among women taking valproate, topiramate, carbamazepine, phenobarbital or phenytoin, high-dose folic acid initiation during the 1–12 weeks before pregnancy was associated with an 86% reduction of MCAs (RR 0.14, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.34). Among women taking lamotrigine, high-dose folic acid initiation during the 1–12 weeks before pregnancy was associated with a decreased risk of MCAs (RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.86). Furthermore, initiation of high-dose folic acid with a prescription strength of 1 mg/tablet 13–52 weeks before pregnancy was associated with a lower risk of MCA (RR 0.27, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.65). Initiation of high-dose folic acid in 5–8 weeks, 9–12 weeks and 5–12 weeks after pregnancy onset was not associated with a decreased risk of MCA (online supplemental table S5).

The findings from the sensitivity analyses—whether using an alternative method for adjusting baseline characteristics, applying multiple imputation for missing values or additionally adjusting for BMI or smoking—were consistent with those from the overall analyses (online supplemental table S6).

The findings from post hoc analyses were consistent with those from the pre-specified analyses (online supplemental table S7). Initiation in 13–24 weeks had a lower estimate of the MCAs risk (RR=0.88, 95% CI 0.49 to 1.33) than in 25–52 weeks (RR=1.08, 95% CI 0.55 to 1.81), and initiation of folic acid in 1–12 weeks before pregnancy from Norway was associated with a reduced risk of MCAs (RR=0.72, 95% CI 0.16 to 1.61), although the findings were not statistically significant. Initiation of high-dose folic acid in 1–12 weeks before pregnancy among women taking levetiracetam was not associated with a decreased risk of MCAs (RR=1.44, 95% CI 0.23 to 3.25), which might be a chance finding due to limited sample size.

This large multinational observational study, using a target trial emulation design, demonstrated that initiation of high-dose folic acid supplementation 1–12 weeks before pregnancy among women taking ASMs was associated with a 45% relative reduction in the risk of offspring MCA. In contrast, no significant reduction in risk of MCA was observed when high-dose folic acid supplementation was initiated after pregnancy onset. The association between initiation of high-dose folic acid supplementation and a lower risk of MCA was also shown in analyses restricting to subgroups of women who initiated high-dose folic acid in 13–52 weeks before pregnancy.

These findings are consistent with previous studies, which reported a lower risk of MCAs when supplementation began prior to pregnancy.25
27 One study reported no MCAs among 32 pregnancies exposed to maternal ASMs, where 81% of the mothers began high-dose folic acid supplementation (2.5–5 mg/day) before conception.
25
 In contrast, 15% of children born to mothers taking ASMs who did not take the supplementation had an MCA.
25
 In another study, none of the 97 children born to mothers with epilepsy who received 5 mg of folic acid before conception had MCAs, whereas 23% of children born to non-supplemented mothers had MCAs.
26
 A separate case control study (25 cases and 108 controls) found that periconceptional, defined as 28 days before to 28 days after pregnancy onset, folic acid supplementation (≥0.4 mg/day) among women with ASMs was associated with a 69% reduction in the risk of neural tube defects.
27
 Two additional studies using the same dataset showed that folic acid supplementation earlier in pregnancy,
28
 as opposed to later,
29
 was associated with a reduced risk of MCA. The first trimester is considered the sensitive window for congenital anomalies with 5–6 weeks after the LMP (3–4 weeks after fertilisation) for neural tube defects.
30
 Studies from the general population have shown that it takes at least 4 weeks of folic acid supplementation 0.8 mg per day to reach the preventive RBC folate concentration of more than 906 nmol/L.
31



Our finding that initiation of folic acid supplementation in the 3 months after pregnancy onset was not associated with a reduced risk of MCAs was, to some extent, consistent with previous studies. Most previous studies
3 29 32
 and reviews
7 8
 did not show a protective effect of folic acid supplementation during the periconceptional period on risk of MCAs in the offspring of women taking ASMs (online supplemental table S8). Most previous studies did not distinguish the timing of folic acid supplementation during the periconceptional period. As shown in our study and previous research,
33
 women are more likely to initiate supplementation after becoming aware of their pregnancy. Consequently, findings from studies that did not account for the timing of initiation may predominantly reflect the effects of post-conception supplementation, potentially obscuring the benefits associated with preconception use. Depending on individual factors and supplement dose, it can take several weeks from starting supplementation to reach optimal folate levels that are sufficient to prevent MCAs.
31 34 35
 Our findings provide evidence that initiating high-dose folic acid supplements only after pregnancy onset or awareness may be too late to offer protection against MCAs.

Our study showed that high-dose folic acid supplementation 1–12 weeks before pregnancy was associated with an 86% reduction in MCA risk in offspring of women taking ASMs with the highest potential for teratogenic effects (such as valproate, topiramate and carbamazepine), which is comparable to the 72% reduction in risk of MCA of folic acid among women at high risk of having a pregnancy with a neural tube defect in a previous RCT.
36
 In our study, high-dose folic acid supplementation was also associated with a reduced risk of MCAs in offspring to women taking lamotrigine, an ASM generally considered safer and linked to a lower risk of MCAs than other ASMs. Lamotrigine may have an impact on folate metabolism, possibly by inhibiting folate transporters, and the drug was originally synthesised as a folate antagonist
37
 and may be a weak teratogen.
38



This study did not find an overall association between initiation of high-dose folic acid supplementation in 13–52 weeks before pregnancy and a lower risk of MCA. The sub-analyses, however, showed that women who were prescribed 1 mg tablets in 13–52 weeks before pregnancy had a reduced risk of MCAs. The possible explanations could be that some women might discontinue folic acid supplementation before pregnancy, for example, if the prescribed tablets had all been taken or if the women simply stopped taking folic acid at a certain point. Women who were prescribed 1 mg tablets in 13–52 weeks before pregnancy had the possibility of a longer sustained use of folic acid, if they took a lower dose than the recommended dose, which increased their probability of having folic acid supplementation in the period closer to conception. The sub-analyses showed that high-dose folic acid initiation in a period closer to conception, for example, 13–24 weeks before pregnancy, had a lower risk of MCAs than initiation in a period further from conception, for example, 25–52 weeks before pregnancy. More research is needed on long sustained use of high-dose folic acid before pregnancy on risk of MCAs.

This study has several strengths. We used the target trial emulation approach, which clarifies the causal question and strengthens the relevance of causal inference for decision-makers by explicitly defining the intervention.
39
 Separating high-dose folic acid initiation across different periconceptional periods allowed us to demonstrate timing-specific effects. We conducted weekly sequential target trial emulation to align eligibility and treatment strategies as much as possible, enabling valid comparisons. The new-user design (initiators vs non-initiators of high-dose folic acid) minimised prevalent user bias and improved baseline balance.
40
 Including a negative control period helped identify both suspected and unsuspected sources of bias, although its function was limited due to small sample size.
41 42



This study has limitations. First, we estimated the effect of initiating high-dose folic acid, not sustained use. Second, both non-initiators and initiators of high-dose folic acid may have used low-dose folic acid, especially in the first trimester,
33 43
 but we did not have data on consumption of over-the-counter low-dose folic acid (eg, 0.4 mg or multivitamins). However, this likely did not confound our results, since low-dose folic acid in the first trimester may be too late to prevent MCA, while studies have found pre-pregnancy supplementation of low-dose folic acid has been found low.
33
 MCA prevalence among non-initiators remained consistent across periconceptional periods, supporting this. Lacking data on over-the-counter low-dose folic acid limited our ability to compare the effect of low-dose versus high-dose folic acid supplementation in the period of interest, for example, 1–12 weeks before pregnancy, on risk of MCAs as well. Third, although we adjusted for baseline characteristics, unmeasured factors like pregnancy planning, lifestyle or comorbidities may affect results. Residual confounding might explain the numerically higher estimate for risk of MCAs among women taking high-dose folic acid in 1–12 weeks after pregnancy onset and in the control period. High-dose folic acid initiators seem more likely to use ASMs for epilepsy and to use teratogenic ASMs. In subgroup analyses restricting to women with epilepsy, we achieved better adjustment for the factor since the MCA risk estimate in the period 1–12 weeks shifted towards one. Fourth, we excluded women who deviated from the research protocol (per protocol analysis), for example, ASMs discontinuers, which reduced the sample size. Furthermore, we had no exact information on ASMs use and did not exactly identify women with drug discontinuation after inclusion. We used an alternative definition to identify women who might discontinue ASMs, which might not reflect the real-world use. Fifth, we did not have data on spontaneous abortion or terminated pregnancies in most countries except for terminated pregnancies from Norway. We had no information on MCAs of included pregnancies ending in termination or stillbirth. The impact of unavailable or excluded pregnancies depends on how high-dose folic acid influences MCA severity or frequency. If high-dose folic acid could reduce the severity of MCAs, for example, neural tube defects, leading to more live births with less severe MCAs among initiators of high-dose folic acid than non-initiators, our findings could in fact be underestimated. An RCT of high-dose folic acid in the general population has shown that high-dose folic acid supplementation reduced the risk of an overall composite adverse pregnancy outcome including spontaneous abortion and MCAs.
44
 In our study, termination and stillbirth rates were similar between initiators and non-initiators. Sixth, due to a low proportion of women initiating high-dose folic acid before pregnancy, the sample size was still too small to do analyses for specific types of MCAs like neural tube defects even though we used the nation-wide data from four Nordic countries. Seventh, the Nordic countries do not have mandatory folic acid fortification of foods like flour, rice or cereals,
45
 which might affect the generalisability of our findings to countries with such policies.

Among women taking ASMs, initiating high-dose folic acid supplementation within the three months before pregnancy was associated with a 45% lower risk of MCAs. In contrast, starting supplements during the first three months of pregnancy was not associated with a lower risk of MCAs. This highlights the importance of preconception counselling for women using ASMs and the timely initiation of folic acid supplementation. Our study calls for research that considers the maintenance of high-dose folic acid supplementation after its initiation before pregnancy and daily dosage besides timing of supplementation.
