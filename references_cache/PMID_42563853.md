# Expanding the Phenotype of CYFIP2-Related Developmental Epileptic Encephalopathy: Case Report and Literature Review.
**Authors:** Squire M, Lea JK, Fan ZJ, Hunter SE
**Journal:** Ann Child Neurol Soc (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/cns3.70036](https://doi.org/10.1002/cns3.70036)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Pathogenic CYFIP2 variants cause developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE), characterized by early-onset intractable epilepsy and developmental delay. The disease course has not been delineated. Codon Arg87 is a de novo mutational hotspot associated with a severe DEE phenotype via gain-of-function mechanisms. Currently, there are no targeted therapies for CYFIP2-related DEE. Genetic therapies initiated at early symptomatic stages have demonstrated benefit in other disorders and may be an important consideration for this patient population.

METHODS: We describe a child with a de novo CYFIP2 Arg87Cys variant and a unique clinical course, along with a comprehensive literature review of reported cases. We identified 41 additional patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic CYFIP2 variants.

RESULTS: The Arg87Cys variant was present in 12/42 (29%) patients. An additional 27 variants are described across 30 patients. All individuals had global developmental delay, 32/42 (76%) developed epilepsy, and 25/42 (60%) experienced seizure onset in the first year of life. Of those with seizures, 16/32 (50%) had epileptic spasms, and 5/32 (16%), including our patient, developed other seizure types before epileptic spasm onset. Interestingly, our patient is the only report of developmental and electroencephalographic (EEG) normalization after initial seizure onset and before spasms.

CONCLUSIONS: Our patient expands the phenotype of CYFIP2-related DEE by demonstrating developmental and EEG normalization after seizure onset and before onset of epileptic spasms and global developmental delay. Given the growing field of genetic therapies and other precision medicines, this period of normalization may represent a window of opportunity for future early intervention.

The cytoplasmic fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein 1 interacting protein 2 (CYFIP2, MIM 606323) gene encodes a component of the WASP‐family verprolin‐homologous protein (WAVE) regulatory complex, which is involved in actin dynamics and is critical for synaptic plasticity. CYFIP2 plays a pivotal role in regulating actin dynamics through its role in the WAVE regulatory complex (WRC) [1]. Pathogenic CYFIP2 variants were first reported in 2018 and have since been discovered to cause developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) type 65 [2]. DEE‐65 is characterized by early‐onset intractable epilepsy, including epileptic spasms and psychomotor developmental delay.

Of the pathogenic variants reported in CYFIP2, the Arg87Cys variant is common and is consistently associated with a DEE phenotype [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. This Arg87 hotspot variant is situated in a critical binding site within the WRC. Disease‐associated variants at this position disrupt WRC regulation, leading to inhibition of CYFIP2 and constant, uncontrolled WRC activation [8, 9]. Patients harboring the CYFIP2 Arg87Cys variant typically present with seizures in the first year of life, progressing to intractable epilepsy, often with infantile epileptic spasms with associated developmental regression [3, 4, 5, 6]. It has been proposed that variants affecting the Arg87 residue result in a toxic gain‐of‐function mechanism, likely contributing to the profound phenotype [3, 7].

Although there are currently no targeted treatments or genetic therapies for CYFIP2‐related DEE, genetic therapies for other DEEs are being investigated, including the antisense oligonucleotide therapy Zorevunersen for SCN1A‐associated Dravet syndrome [10, 11]. Zorevunersen clinical trials in infants and young children show preliminary evidence of reduced seizure frequency and improved cognition and behavior [12]. Genetic therapies have also been beneficial in neurodegenerative disorders [13, 14, 15, 16]. Efforts are underway to identify CYFIP2 Arg87Cys‐specific ligands as potential therapeutics [17]. Positive results in other DEEs and neurodegenerative disorders highlight the potential benefit of investigating genetic therapies and other targeted treatments in CYFIP2‐related DEE, and place importance on the timing of these treatments.

Here, we describe an infant with CYFIP2‐related DEE and present a novel finding of electroencephalogram (EEG) normalization and developmental gains following initial seizure onset and before the onset of infantile epileptic spasms and subsequent developmental delay. Additionally, to expand our understanding of CYFIP2‐related DEE phenotypes, we provide a comprehensive literature review of patients with pathogenic and likely pathogenic CYFIP2 variants.

This study was approved by the institutional review board at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient's parents, including consent for publication of this individual case report.

Clinical data were collected through electronic medical record review and in‐person interviews with the patient's parents. Seizure types were classified according to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classification system [18]. Antiseizure medication (ASM) response was determined by the treating physicians and corroborated through parental interviews. Neurological and developmental assessments were performed via direct examination. EEG and neuroimaging studies were independently reviewed by authors MS, ZJF, and SEH. Genetic testing reports were reviewed by all authors.

A systematic literature review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines to identify peer‐reviewed articles describing clinical presentations of patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic CYFIP2 variants. The initial search was performed in May 2024 and repeated in full in March 2025 to ensure completeness. A health sciences librarian collaborated on the search strategy. PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus databases were searched using the keyword “CYFIP2.” Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, and full‐text review was performed for all potentially eligible studies. Retrieved titles were reviewed by two authors (MS and SEH) to identify relevant studies.

Articles were included if they reported clinical data from humans with pathogenic or likely pathogenic CYFIP2 variants, including case reports, case series, or cohort studies. Review articles were included only if they presented detailed individual‐level patient data. Exclusion criteria included (1) no mention of CYFIP2; (2) a focus exclusively on animal models or molecular dynamics; (3) relevance only to nonneurological conditions (e.g., cancer, diabetes, obesity); (4) letter, commentary, or abstract‐only format; or (5) clinical findings unrelated to CYFIP2. Only cases involving CYFIP2 variants were included; cases in which epilepsy and/or developmental delay were attributed to variants in other genes were excluded.

For each included publication, we extracted individual‐level patient data. Variant data included nucleotide and amino acid changes. Clinical data included age at seizure onset, seizure types, ASMs trialed, drug resistance status, seizure resolution, age at last reported seizure, and comorbidities. Where possible, ILAE classifications were used [18], otherwise, direct quotes from articles were abstracted. When multiple seizure types or clinical findings were described for a single individual, all were recorded. Developmental outcomes were categorized based on reported presence or absence of developmental delay, using narrative descriptions or formal assessments when available. Type of developmental delay was classified according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) definitions [19]. Data were independently reviewed and cross‐checked by two authors (MS and SEH) to ensure accuracy.

This 2‐month‐old boy presented with generalized myoclonic‐tonic and tonic seizures, poor feeding due to weak suck, and developmental delay (poor visual tracking). He was born at 39 weeks 5 days via an unremarkable vaginal delivery. There was no family history of seizures. Sequence analysis of 302 genes via an epilepsy gene panel identified a pathogenic CYFIP2 c.259C > T (p.Arg87Cys) variant [20]. Both parents tested negative for the variant.

EEG showed abundant multifocal spikes (Figure 1A) and electroclinical seizures (Figure 1B). Seizures were initially managed with phenobarbital, levetiracetam, and lacosamide and remained well‐controlled on lacosamide monotherapy, with only one seizure over a 3‐month span. A repeat EEG at age 3 months demonstrated a normal background with no epileptiform or ictal activity (Figure 1C).

Despite early signs of developmental delay, by age 3 months, he was meeting all CDC‐defined milestones in motor (appropriate head control, pushing up in prone position, reaching and grasping, and bringing hands to midline and mouth), language (cooing, sound imitation, and lateralizing to voice), and social domains (social smile, visual tracking). His neurological examination was notable only for mild hypotonia.

At age 6 months, he developed epileptic spasms. EEG was consistent with hypsarrhythmia (Figure 1D), with a Burden of AmplitudeS and Epileptiform Discharges score of 4, consistent with epileptic encephalopathy [21, 22, 23]. Following the onset of epileptic spasms, he experienced significant developmental regression. He lost the ability to coo and babble, ceased social smiling, and demonstrated a decline in motor abilities, including decreased head support and inability to roll over or sit with or without support.

His spasms resolved following adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) therapy. Vigabatrin was initiated during the ACTH taper, and EEG subsequently improved, indicating only rare multifocal spikes during sleep.

A hyperkinetic choreoathetoid movement disorder emerged at age 7 months, characterized by episodic upward eye deviation, arm extension, and head drop movements, without an electrographic correlate. He also had difficulty initiating and maintaining sleep. Both the movement disorder and sleep disturbance improved with clonazepam. Laboratory workup revealed persistent neutropenia (white blood cell count of 2.4, absolute neutrophil count of 0.3) of unclear etiology. Sequence analysis of 40 genes via a neutropenia gene panel was negative [24]. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed benign enlargement of the subarachnoid spaces.

At age 13 months, he remained without seizures for 5 months on lacosamide, levetiracetam, and vigabatrin with moderate global developmental delay (sat independently, not pulling to stand or bearing weight on his legs, appropriate grasp and reach but no pincer grasp, vocalizing with vowel sounds without consonants or words). EEG demonstrated frequent generalized and grouped multifocal spikes and sharp waves during sleep (Figure 1E).

We conducted a systematic literature review using PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus with the keyword “CYFIP2” (Figure 2). After removing duplicates, 221 articles were screened by title and abstract, and 16 containing individual‐level data were included for full review, yielding nine articles for final analysis [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 25, 26, 27].

We identified an additional 41 patients with pathogenic and likely pathogenic CYFIP2 variants reported since 2018 [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 25, 26, 27]. Sixteen of the reported patients (39%) had p.Arg87 variants. The most common variant reported is Arg87Cys (n = 11). Other common variants included Arg87Leu (n = 2), Asp724His (n = 3), and Ile664Met (n = 2), along with 23 other unique variants (Table 2).

Of these patients, including ours, all (100%) had developmental delay, 32/42 (76%) developed epilepsy, with most (25/42, 60%) experiencing seizure onset within the first year of life. Of those with epilepsy, half (16/32, 50%) developed epileptic spasms, including eight with the Arg87Cys variant. Five patients demonstrated persistent epileptic spasms despite adequate hormonal therapy and continued to experience other drug‐resistant seizure types on follow‐up. Between initial seizure and epileptic spasm onset, there were no other patients described to have a normal EEG or improvement in developmental milestones.

ASMs utilized varied considerably (Table 2). Of the 28 patients with ASMs reported, valproic acid was the most commonly used, with 13/28 patients (46%) treated with the medication and five (19%) with reported seizure freedom. Seizure freedom was also achieved in nine additional patients, all of whom were treated with varying combinations of ASMs (Table 2).

Similar to our patient, 7/42 (17%) had hyperkinetic movements, 15/42 (36%) had sleep disturbances, and 5/42 (12%) had neutropenia of unclear etiology and significance.

We describe a patient with a de novo pathogenic CYFIP2 Arg87Cys variant, contributing to the expanding phenotypic spectrum of CYFIP2‐related DEE. Our patient demonstrated a transient period of seizure resolution and EEG normalization after seizure onset and before the onset of epileptic spasms, a pattern that has not been previously described in patients with CYFIP2‐related DEE. During this interval, he met all developmental milestones for age. However, the onset of epileptic spasms was associated with marked regression and subsequent global developmental delay.

Specific to treatment, our patient responded best to the addition of lacosamide to levetiracetam and had only one seizure on lacosamide monotherapy before the onset of epileptic spasms. Five other patients were treated with lacosamide therapy, including one with reported seizure freedom at age 5 years on lacosamide monotherapy (also with the Arg87Cys variant) [3, 6, 26]. Lacosamide selectively enhances the slow inactivation of voltage‐gated sodium channels and may also indirectly modulate collapsin response mediator protein 2 (CRMP2), which regulates microtubule dynamics and neuronal plasticity [28, 29, 30]. As CYFIP2 contributes to neuronal plasticity through regulation of actin dynamics, we hypothesize that lacosamide may help preserve microtubule‐actin interactions disrupted in CYFIP2‐related DEE [8]. However, interactions between lacosamide and CRMP2 remain under investigation [28, 29]. Further studies are needed to guide treatment strategies and determine if lacosamide may be an important consideration for patients with CYFIP2‐related DEEs.

The treatment of comorbidities is not well described in CYFIP2‐related DEEs. Our patient responded well to the addition of nightly clonazepam, which improved his hyperkinetic movements and sleep disturbance. Neutropenia was noted in four patients; three had the p.Arg87Cys variant, including our patient. RNA sequencing data from the Genotype‐Tissue Expression Portal (Version X) confirms CYFIP2 expression in whole blood, and the EBI Expression Atlas confirms expression in mature neutrophils [31, 32]. Additionally, the isoform containing the p.Arg87Cys variant is expressed in these cell types, suggesting a possible biological link between CYFIP2 dysfunction and neutropenia. However, no causal relationship has been established. Hematology has closely followed our patient's neutropenia, but no interventions have been necessary to date. Further investigation is needed to determine whether neutropenia may represent an underrecognized feature in a subset of individuals with CYFIP2‐related disorders and may serve as a useful biomarker.

The Arg87Cys variant is the most frequently reported and is consistently associated with a DEE phenotype [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Variants affecting the Arg87 residue are thought to produce a toxic gain‐of‐function effect, likely contributing to the severe clinical presentation [3, 7]. In contrast, truncating variants, presumed to cause loss of function, are more often associated with milder phenotypes, while missense variants at other residues demonstrate greater phenotypic variability [3]. Further investigation into the impact of CYFIP2 variants on WRC stability is needed to clarify these potential genotype‐phenotype correlations.

Currently, there are no known targeted treatments or genetic therapies for CYFIP2‐related DEE. In other genetic disorders, such as spinal muscular atrophy, initiating genetic therapies at either presymptomatic or early symptomatic stages results in better overall outcomes compared with treatment after symptom progression [13, 14, 15, 16]. Early targeted treatment may be an important consideration for future treatment in children with pathogenic CYFIP2 variants, as our patient demonstrated a period of normalization before the onset of epileptic spasms, during which targeted treatments such as genetic therapies may be beneficial. This approach could be limited by several factors, as epileptic spasms and developmental delay were presenting symptoms for multiple patients in the literature. The unique disease course reported for our patient has not been previously described. Future studies are needed to detail the seizure characteristics and natural history of CYFIP2‐related DEEs.

We describe a unique presentation of CYFIP2‐related DEE and present a comprehensive literature review of 41 additional patients. Our patient's presentation is unique in that there is a clear period of EEG normalization and developmental gains after initial seizure onset and before the onset of epileptic spasms. Our patient adds to the phenotypic spectrum of CYFIP2‐related DEE and may present an opportunity for investigation into and optimal timing of future targeted therapies.
