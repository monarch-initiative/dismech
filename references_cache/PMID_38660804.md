# Differential Impact In Vivo of Pf4-ΔCre-Mediated and Gp1ba-ΔCre-Mediated Depletion of Cyclooxygenase-1 in Platelets in Mice.
**Authors:** Tang SY, Lordan R, Meng H, Auerbach BJ, Hennessy EJ, Sengupta A, Das US, Joshi R, Marcos-Contreras OA, McConnell R, Grant GR, Ricciotti E, Muzykantov VR, Grosser T, Weiljie AM, FitzGerald GA
**Journal:** Arterioscler Thromb Vasc Biol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1161/ATVBAHA.123.320295](https://doi.org/10.1161/ATVBAHA.123.320295)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Low-dose aspirin is widely used for the secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease. The beneficial effects of low-dose aspirin are attributable to its inhibition of platelet Cox (cyclooxygenase)-1-derived thromboxane A2. Until recently, the use of the Pf4 (platelet factor 4) Cre has been the only genetic approach to generating megakaryocyte/platelet ablation of Cox-1 in mice. However, Pf4-ΔCre displays ectopic expression outside the megakaryocyte/platelet lineage, especially during inflammation. The use of the Gp1ba (glycoprotein 1bα) Cre promises a more specific, targeted approach.

METHODS: To evaluate the role of Cox-1 in platelets, we crossed Pf4-ΔCre or Gp1ba-ΔCre mice with Cox-1flox/flox mice to generate platelet Cox-1-/- mice on normolipidemic and hyperlipidemic (Ldlr-/-; low-density lipoprotein receptor) backgrounds.

RESULTS: Ex vivo platelet aggregation induced by arachidonic acid or adenosine diphosphate in platelet-rich plasma was inhibited to a similar extent in Pf4-ΔCre Cox-1-/-/Ldlr-/- and Gp1ba-ΔCre Cox-1-/-/Ldlr-/- mice. In a mouse model of tail injury, Pf4-ΔCre-mediated and Gp1ba-ΔCre-mediated deletions of Cox-1 were similarly efficient in suppressing platelet prostanoid biosynthesis. Experimental thrombogenesis and attendant blood loss were similar in both models. However, the impact on atherogenesis was divergent, being accelerated in the Pf4-ΔCre mice while restrained in the Gp1ba-ΔCres. In the former, accelerated atherogenesis was associated with greater suppression of PGI2 biosynthesis, a reduction in the lipopolysaccharide-evoked capacity to produce PGE2 (prostaglandin E) and PGD2 (prostanglandin D), activation of the inflammasome, elevated plasma levels of IL-1β (interleukin), reduced plasma levels of HDL-C (high-density lipoprotein receptor-cholesterol), and a reduction in the capacity for reverse cholesterol transport. By contrast, in the latter, plasma HDL-C and α-tocopherol were elevated, and MIP-1α (macrophage inflammatory protein-1α) and MCP-1 (monocyte chemoattractant protein 1) were reduced.

CONCLUSIONS: Both approaches to Cox-1 deletion similarly restrain thrombogenesis, but a differential impact on Cox-1-dependent prostanoid formation by the vasculature may contribute to an inflammatory phenotype and accelerated atherogenesis in Pf4-ΔCre mice.

Randomized control trials (RCTs) have provided evidence that low dose aspirin is effective in the secondary prevention of cardiovascular diseases (CVD).1 In patients who have previously suffered a heart attack or stroke, the cardiovascular benefit of low dose aspirin exceeds the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding. The place of low dose aspirin in primary prevention remains controversial.1,2 For example, a recent RCT in diabetic patients showed that low dose aspirin reduced serious vascular events but caused major bleeding events that offset this benefit.3 While inhibition of platelet thromboxane A2 (TxA2) is sufficient to explain the clinical effects of low dose aspirin, aspirin has other effects on thrombus formation unrelated to the inhibition of platelet cyclooxygenase-1 (Cox-1) activity.2,4 Aspirin has also mimics some of the effects of caloric restriction.5 The availability of genetic models of Cox-1 ablation in megakaryocytes (MKs)/-platelets facilitates elucidation of the relative importance of these observations.

The first available model utilized platelet factor 4 (Pf4) ΔCre -mediated recombination. However, this is not limited to the MK/-platelet lineage, especially during inflammation.6,7 Pf4-ΔCre -mediated deletion of Cox-1 in MK/-platelets ameliorates dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis in mice and prolongs the time to occlusion in the carotid artery in a mouse model of FeCl3-induced thrombosis.8,9 More recently, Nagy et al developed a transgenic mouse model in which cre expression is driven by the Glycoprotein 1bα (Gp1ba) locus,6 which is more specific to MK/-platelets than the Pf4-ΔCre transgenic mouse. However, recombination may be less efficient than in Pf4-ΔCre mice and the introduction of Cre through constitutive knock-in results in heterozygous deletion of GP1ba which itself may modify the platelet phenotype. The authors advocated Cre to be used as control for conditional deletion using Pf4-ΔCre - and Gp1ba-ΔCre - mediated recombination. Gollomp and Poncz summarized what is known about the two models and suggested that they be compared to assess their comparative impact on platelet biology in vivo.10

Here, we directly compared the effects of Pf4-ΔCre - or Gp1ba-ΔCre - mediated deletion of Cox-1 in platelets on ex vivo platelet aggregation, prostanoid biosynthesis, hemostasis, thrombosis and atherosclerosis.

All reagents used were purchased from MilliporeSigma (St. Louis, MO) unless otherwise stated. A detailed description of the experimental methods is provided in the Supplemental Methods.

All animals in this study were housed according to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Pennsylvania. Animals that developed skin lesions during the feeding of a high fat diet were excluded. All experimental protocols were approved by IACUC (protocol #804754).

Platelet specific Cox-1-deficient mice were generated by crossing male Pf4-ΔCre or Gp1ba-ΔCre mouse lines with female Cox-1flox/flox mice as described in the Supplemental Methods.

Blood was centrifuged at 200x g for 10 min at room temperature (RT) to obtain platelet-rich plasma (PRP). Platelet was obtained from PRP by centrifuging at 3000x g for 10 min at RT. Mature primary megakaryocytes (MKs) were isolated from bone marrows (femora and tibiae) by size exclusion via filtration as reported by Spindler et al.11

Platelet aggregation in response to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and arachidonic acid (AA) were measured using optical multichannel (optimul) platelet aggregometry in 96-well plates as described by Chan et al.12

Two mm of tail was amputated using a sterile scalpel under anesthesia with isoflurane. Urinary prostanoid metabolites and serum protanoids were measured using the liquid chromatography/ mass spectrometry/ mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS).

Urinary prostanoid metabolites were measured by LC/MS/MS as described.13 Such measurements provide a noninvasive, time integrated measurement of systemic prostanoid biosynthesis.14

Prostanoids in serum from mice and cell culture media of mouse aortas were measured as described by Hu and colleagues.15

Mice were used in this experiment after feeding a HFD for 12 weeks. Two mm of tail was amputated using a sterile scalpel under anesthesia with ketamine/ xylazine/ acepromazine. Blood was collected into a pre-weighed glass tube containing Drabkin’s reagent to estimate the amount of blood loss and hemoglobin content at 420 nm using a microplate reader.

Briefly16, ~14 weeks old female mice were anesthetized and secured on a thermal pad at 37°C. A midline cervical incision was made to locate the right common carotid artery and a Doppler flow probe was applied. Rose Bengal (10mg/ mL) was injected into the jugular vein of a mouse (50 mg/ Kg BW) and a 1.5mW green light laser (540nm) was used to induce thrombosis. Blood flow was monitored for up to 90 mins and occlusion time was recorded when blood flow was reduced to zero for up to one min.

Briefly17, anesthetized male mice (12-16 weeks old) kept at 37°C on a thermal pad was administrated intravenously with rat anti-mouse CD41 (20 μg/ml) and Alex-Flour 488 chicken anti-rat IgG (180 μg/ml) diluted in PBS. The cremaster muscle was exteriorizd, pinned over the microscopy stage, and bathed in a bicarbonate-buffered saline at 37°C. SlideBook 6.0 imaging software (Intelligent Imaging Innovations) was used to control all parts of the intravital microscope, data collection and image analysis. Digital images of thrombus formation were obtained with an Olympus BX61WI microscope.

Mice of both sexes on an Ldlr−/− background were fed a high fat diet (HFD, 21.2% fat, 0.2% cholesterol, TD.88137, Harlan Teklad, Madison, WI) from eight weeks of age for 12, 24 and 36 weeks. Body weights were recorded, and urine samples were collected from the mice before and after the HFD feeding.

Aortas (ascending aorta, aortic arch, descending aorta, innominate artery, carotid arteries, subclavian arteries, and descending aorta) of male Ldlr−/− mice fed a HFD for 24 weeks were dissected for single cells isolation with a cocktail of enzymes (DNase, liberase and hyaluronidase) in a petri dish at 37°C for 40 mins. Cell supernatant was filtered through a 40 μm strainer and washed with RPMI1640 containing 10% FBS to inactive the enzyme cocktail. Mouse aortas from two mice per group were pooled after enzymatic digestion for scRNA-sequencing. For scRNA-seq data cell quality control, cells were filtered for the presence of doublets using Scrublet.18 After identifying cell types, a low-dimensional cell embedding was estimated using scVI.19 Using the low-dimensional embedding, cells were then clustered using the Leiden algorithm20 with resolution 0.2. type marker gene identification.

50 μL of mouse plasma samples was extracted with 300 μL ice-cold methanol: chloroform solution (2:1). The top fraction was analyzed using Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC)-MS/MS as reported previously.21 Data analysis was normalized with the probabilistic quotient normalization in R.22

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were prepared from male C57Bl/6J mice and RCT assay was performed as previously described.23,24

All animals compared were the stated age and some were on the same LdIr−/− background (C57BL/6J). The residuals were tested for normality by the D’Agostino/ Pearson test. Post-hoc analysis was performed by pairwise t-tests, with the Bonferroni correction unless otherwise stated. All significant tests were further validated by non-parametric Mann-Whitney and Wilcoxon tests to make sure they were not biased by parametric assumption. A significance threshold of 0.05 was used for all tests after correcting for multiple testing. Sample sizes (10- 20) were based on power analysis of the test measurement and the desire to detect a minimal 10% difference in the variables assessed with α = 0.05 and the power (1-β) = 0.8.

For scRNA-sequencing, to assess differential expression of genes among the combinations of conditions, gene transcript counts were transformed as they were for all scRNA-seq analyses. Thereafter, difference of means of each pair of transformed count distributions was assessed using a Mann-Whitney test. Additional filtering was done by two further criteria. First, the effect size of the difference of means was estimated. Those with effect sizes ≥ 0.3 were kept. Second, the percentage of cells with detected expression was detected. Genes with at least 10% detected expression across cells in one of the two conditions were kept.

DNA extracted from mouse tail was used to confirm the genotypes of our mouse models by PCR analyses. PCR products of Pf4-ΔCre, Gp1ba-ΔCre, flox, and Ldlr are 450bp, 372bp, 210bp, and 550bp, respectively (Figure S1A). The excision product of floxed Cox-1 gene resulted in a PCR product of 327bp (Figure S1A, bottom left panel).

Pf4-ΔCre - or Gp1ba-ΔCre - mediated deletion of Cox-1 reduces Cox-1 mRNA and is shown in platelets and MKs isolated from platelet rich plasma (PRP) and bone marrow, respectively (Figure S1B and S1C). mRNA of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa integrin (CD41), was enriched in platelets and MKs. Platelet CD41 mRNA levels of Gp1ba-ΔCre control mice were significantly higher than in Pf4-ΔCre control mice. These results agree with the increase in platelet volume of the Gp1ba-ΔCre mouse model as reported by Nagy et al6. Despite having different Cox-1 mRNA levels in the two Cre controls, Cox-1 mRNA and protein in platelets and MKs from Cox-1−/− mice were depleted to a similar extent after Cre-mediated recombination (Figure S1B and S1C). Consistent with this, serum TxB2, PGE2, PGD2, and PGF2α levels were significantly suppressed in both knock out lines (Figure S1D).

PRP isolated from whole blood of controls or platelet Cox-1−/− hyperlipidemic (Ldlr−/−) mice fed a SLD was used to perform a platelet aggregation assay using a microplate reader.

Deletion of Cox-1 in platelets from both lines significantly inhibited platelet aggregation induced with AA or ADP (Figure S2).

Tail amputation activated platelet dependent hemostasis. In Pf4-ΔCre mice, tail injury activated Tx and PGI2 biosynthesis in both sexes, as reflected by excretion of their urinary metabolites, TxM and PGIM (Table 1). The increase of PGIM in male mice was not statistically significant compared to baseline. Deletion of Cox-1 in platelets significantly suppresses TxM but not PGIM. PGDM, PGEM and the F2-isoprostane, 8,12-iso-iPF2α-VI (F2iP) were not significantly altered after tail injury in Pf4-ΔCre mice (Table S1). Similar trends were observed in Gp1ba-ΔCre mice of both sexes (Table 1 and Table S1).

In summary, deletion of Cox-1 resulted in the expected and similar suppression of Tx in the setting of platelet activation in both lines. Due to differences in baseline urinary prostanoid metabolites, the percentage change with respect to baseline is also presented in Table 1.

Platelets were activated ex vivo by exposing whole blood to a borosilicate glass tube for one hour at 37°C. Deletion of Cox-1 in platelets in either line significantly suppresses serum levels of TxB2, PGD2, PGE2 and PGF2α in both sexes (Figure S3). As expected, given the absence of a vascular source, PGI2 was below the limit of detection in serum.

Mice fed a HFD for 12 weeks were used to measure the amount of blood loss after tail amputation to evaluate the hemostatic role of Cox-1 in activated platelets. Deletion of Cox-1 in platelets significantly increased blood loss in mice in both sexes (Figure 1A- Pf4-ΔCre - female, B- male; E- Gp1ba-ΔCre - female, F- male) in both lines. Reflecting blood loss, hemoglobin levels were significantly higher in mice deficient in platelet Cox-1 (Figure 1C-D, G-H).

The time to vascular occlusion after a photochemical-induced carotid vascular injury in female mice was significantly increased in both lines of Cox-1−/− mice as compared to controls (Figure 2A).

For male mice, thrombosis was monitored using laser-induced injury of small arteries in the cremaster muscle in conjunction with intravital microscopy. Again, deletion of Cox-1 in platelets restrains thrombogenesis comparably in both lines (Figure 2B). Maximal thrombus size was significantly reduced in Cox-1−/− mice (Figure 2C).

Feeding Pf4-ΔCre mice a HFD for 12, 24 and 36 weeks significantly increased atherosclerotic plaque area relative to total area, as assessed by en face lesion analysis (Figure 3A). Deletion of Cox-1 significantly increased lesional plaque accumulation at 36 weeks in female and at 24 and 36 weeks in male mice. By contrast, in Gp1ba-ΔCre mice, there were significant increases in atherosclerotic lesions with feeding time in mice regardless of genotype (Figure 3B- female and male) but deletion of Cox-1 significantly restrained lesional plaques at 36 weeks in female and at 24 and 36 weeks in male mice. Representative en face whole aortic lesion images stained with Sudan IV dye of male mice on 24 and 36 weeks of HFD are shown (Figure 3A and B, right panels).

Consistent with the en face analyses, aortic root lesion burden, as reflected by H&E staining, was significantly increased in Cox-1-deficient Pf4Δ-Cre mice, while a reduction in aortic root lesion burden was observed in Cox-1−/− deficient Gp1ba-ΔCre mice (Figure 3C,D).

Mice from both lines fed a HFD gained body weight (Figure S4A-B). However, deletion of Cox-1 did not significantly alter weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), heart rate (HR), blood glucose or triglycerides (Table S2) in either line or sex (Figure S4-5) fed a HFD.

Urinary PG metabolites were measured in male mice after feeding a HFD for 24 weeks. HFD significantly increased urinary TxM, PGIM and PGDM in both sexes in Pf4-ΔCre mice (Table 2) and deletion of Cox-1 in platelets significantly reduced these metabolites. Urinary PGEM and F2iP were not statistically altered after feeding a HFD regardless of sex or genotype of the mice (Table S3).

By contrast, in Gp1baΔ-Cre mice the increase in PGDM did not attain significance after feeding a HFD and deletion of Cox-1 in platelets did not significantly suppress urinary PGIM. PGEM was significantly reduced in males after feeding a HFD regardless of genotype (Table 2 and Table S3).

We isolated whole aortas from mice fed a HFD and treated the aortas in cell culture media with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 8 hours at 37°C. Tx was suppressed in both Cox-1 deficient lines, perhaps reflecting residual platelet contamination. However, PGE2 and PGD2 were only suppressed in the Cox-1 depleted Pf4-ΔCre line (Table S4).

In summary, while Tx biosynthesis (urinary TxM) and the aortic capacity to generate Tx after LPS treatment is suppressed in both lines, formation of prostanoids that can mediate an anti-inflammatory effect on atherogenesis was depressed in vivo (PGIM) and ex vivo (LPS evoked PGE2 and PGD2) only in the Cox-1 depleted Pf4-ΔCre line.

Aortic Cox-1 mRNA was reduced to a greater extent in Pf4-ΔCre Cox-1−/− mice (~76%) than in Gp1ba-ΔCre male mice (~49%) after feeding a HFD for 24 weeks (Figure S6A). Cox-1 expression was also reduced in peritoneal cells of this line but not in the Gp1ba-ΔCre mice (Figure S6B). Pf4-ΔCre Cox-2 mRNA was not altered by Cox-1 depletion (Figure S6A-B).

In an exploratory study to investigate the divergent atherosclerotic phenotypes between the two approaches to deletion of Cox-1 in Ldlr−/− mice, we performed sc-RNA sequencing using aortas from male mice fed a HFD for 24 weeks.

scRNA-seq analyses revealed nine distinct cell clusters with unique marker genes as displayed in Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projections (UMAPs). As shown in Figure 4A, one of the main differences between the two mouse lines was the identification of a cell cluster (black rectangle) that displayed signature genes of stem cells, endothelial cells, and monocytes (SEM cells).25-27 Mouse aortas from the Pf4-ΔCre mice (both control and platelet Cox-1 KOs) had many more SEM cells than those from the Gp1ba-ΔCre mice irrespective of Cox-1 expression. We studied vascular smooth muscle cells (SMCs) in more detail because they constituted about 50% of the isolated cells from the atherosclerotic aortas. Additionally, phenotypic switching of SMCs during atherogenesis has been related to pathophysiology of this disease.25,28 KEGG pathway analyses associated with changes in gene expression showed that TCA cycle, carbon metabolism, spliceosome, and protein processing in the ER genes were differentially altered between the two lines (Figure 4E). For macrophages, pathway enrichment analyses revealed that glycosphingolipid signaling was differentially expressed and modulated by Cox-1 deletion in the two lines (Figure S7A). Differential gene expression analyses of SMCs, macrophages, and SEM cells revealed upregulation of several genes associated with anti-inflammatory signaling in Gp1ba-ΔCre -mediated deletion of Cox-1 in platelets (Figure 4F and Figure S7B-D). Despite Bpifa1 being differentially expressed in T cells, relevant KEGG pathways (focal adhesion, oxidative phosphorylation, and protein processing in ER) were equally impacted between controls and platelet Cox-1−/− mice of both Cres (Figure S7E). Pf4 expression was restricted to macrophages but was unaltered by Cre or genotype (Figure S7F). Cell type proportions are presented in Figure S7G.

We isolated immune cells from mouse aortas by FACS to perform rt-qPCR. In CD3+ T cells, Cox-1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced in platelet Cox-1−/− mice mediated by Pf4-ΔCre but not in Gp1ba-ΔCre mice (Figure S8A). Interestingly, Pf4 was detected only in CD3+ T cells from Pf4-ΔCre mice and was significantly decreased in platelet Cox-1−/− CD3+ T cells. However, no significant differences in Cox-1 mRNA levels were detected in CD11b+ cells between control and platelet Cox-1−/− mice of both Cres. Cox-2 mRNA levels in CD3+ T cells were significantly reduced in Pf4-ΔCre Cox-1−/− mice, but in CD11b+ cells, Cox-2 levels increased in Pf4-ΔCre Cox-1−/− mice and decreased in Gp1ba-ΔCre mice. We tried to characterize CD11b+ cells based on the M1 or M2 macrophage markers,29 M1 (Tnf-α and IL17a) or M2 (IL-4 and IL-13) by rt-qPCR. Unexpectedly, only Tnf-α was significantly elevated in CD11b+ cells from Pf4-ΔCre Cox-1−/− mice (Figure S8B). IL-17A, IL-4 and −13 levels were below limit of detection. The gating strategy for sorting immune cells from mouse aortas is presented in Figure S8C.

Consistent with the scRNA-seq results, Pf4-ΔCre -mediated deletion of Cox-1 in platelets significantly increased plasma levels of IL-β as compared to controls after 12 weeks on a HFD (Figure S9A). No significant differences were observed in Gp1ba-ΔCre mice (Figure S9B). Splenic cleaved-caspase-1 protein expression was significantly upregulated only in platelet-Cox-1−/− Pf4-ΔCre mice (Figure S9C). Administration of an inflammasome inhibitor (MCC950) abrogated the differences in atherosclerotic plaque lesions, cleaved-caspase-1 protein and plasma IL-1β levels between controls and these platelet Cox-1−/− mice (Figure S9E).

Plasma levels of HDL-C were significantly decreased in platelet-Cox-1−/− mice mediated by Pf4-ΔCre after feeding a HFD for 24 weeks (Table S2). These findings prompted us further to investigate reverse cholesterol transport. ATP binding cassette (Abc)a1 and Abcg1 mRNA expression levels were downregulated in platelet Cox-1−/− mice as compared to controls (Figure S10A). Consistent with these results, liver H3-cholesterol levels were significantly decreased in platelet-Cox-1−/− mice compared with controls (Figure S10B). No significant differences in H3-cholesterol levels were detected in the plasma and feces between controls and platelet-Cox-1−/− mice (Figure S10B).

Plasma levels of HDL-C were significantly increased in male platelet-Cox-1−/− mice mediated by Gp1ba-ΔCre after feeding a HFD for 12 weeks (Table S2) and plasma levels of macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α), and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) were significantly decreased (Figure S11A-B).

Deletion of platelet Cox-1 in either line did not significantly alter plasma levels of IL-10 and IL-13 in mice fed a HFD for 24 weeks (Figure S11C and D).

Orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) revealed a distinct separation of plasma metabolites between Gp1ba-ΔCre controls (red) and platelet Cox-1−/− (green) mice (p= 0.02, Q2= 0.52) (Figure S12A). No such separation was observed between Pf4-ΔCre+/KI controls (blue) and platelet Cox-1−/− (purple) mice (p>> 0.1, Q2= ~0). MetaboAnalyst pathway analysis revealed amino acid metabolic pathways were impacted in Gp1ba-ΔCre -mediated deletion of Cox-1 in platelets, especially glycine, serine, threonine, tyrosine, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (Figure S12B). Indeed, plasma levels of α-tocopherol were significantly increased in Gp1ba-ΔCre platelet Cox-1−/− mice as compared to controls (Figure S12C).

Products of the lipoxygenase and epoxygenase pathways of arachidonic acid metabolism were unaltered by platelet Cox-1 deletion in either line (data not shown).

Genetic ablation of platelet Cox-1 in mice would be expected to recapitulate the effects of low dose aspirin – suppression of Tx and PGD formation, impairment of hemostasis, restraint of thrombogenesis and atherogenesis.30-32

There are two major findings in this paper. Firstly, under the conditions of no insult or acute injury with short term stress, use of Pf4-ΔCre - or Gp1ba-ΔCre to effect deletion of Cox-1 in platelets resulted in comparable and expected suppression of ex vivo platelet aggregation, prostanoid formation, impairment of hemostasis and restraint of thrombogenesis.9,17,31,33 This was an important comparison given concerns about the relative efficiency of recombination, the potential impact on platelet dependent thrombogenesis of partial loss of Gp1ba and the use of flox/flox but not Cre positive controls. As expected, urinary lipoxygenase and epoxygenase products of arachidonic acid were unaltered by Cox-1 deletion in in either line. The increase in PGI2 biosynthesis coincident with Tx after tail injury is expected, reflecting the interaction of platelets with vascular endothelial cells, which produce PGI2 to counteract the vasoconstrictive and platelets aggregation effects of Cox-1-derived TxA2.32,34 Interestingly, Cox-1 depletion reduced this protective PGI2 response only in the Pf4-ΔCre line. While Cox-1 deletion suppressed urinary TxM when an increase was evoked by either tail injury or a HFD, the failure to detect an impact on basal TxM likely reflected a deficiency of power relative to the very low levels of the metabolite in mice. Low dose aspirin markedly suppressed urinary TxM in humans.35 When whole blood was activated ex vivo, all serum prostanoids were fully suppressed by the deletion of Cox-1 in platelets driven by either Pf4-ΔCre or Gp1ba-ΔCre recombination. In this assay, there is no vascular source of PGI2 and suppression of platelet Tx (and PGD2) formation would be expected to reduce platelet dependent leukocyte activation and consequently PGE2. It is worth noting that differences in baseline prostanoid metabolites between the two models may reflects any number of things including “leakiness” of Cre-mediated recombination.

The second major observation was a surprise. While Gp1ba-ΔCre -targeted deletion of platelet Cox-1 restrained atherogenesis in hyperlipidemic mice as expected, Pf4-ΔCre -mediated deletion of Cox-1 had the opposite effect. While an off-target extra-platelet effect was unexpected, the differential impact on aortic Cox-1 mRNA levels and urinary PGIM could have contributed to the atherogenesis in the Pf4-ΔCre mice. Deletion of its receptor - the IPr - accelerates atherogenesis in hyperlipidemic mice.36 Furthermore Pf4 was extant only in macrophages in our scRNA analysis. The capacity for LPS evoked Tx formation by the atherosclerotic vasculature was reduced by Cox-1 depletion in both lines, however there was a concomitant depletion of PGE2 and PGD2, both of which have anti-inflammatory properties37 only in the Pf4-ΔCre line. Thus, a contrasting impact on atheroprotective PG formation – reactive PGI2 by the vasculature and PGE2 and PGD2, likely by infiltrating macrophages, may have contributed to the divergent impact of Cox-1 depletion on atherogenesis in the two lines.

To address this further we sought differences in gene expression, inflammatory and lipid biomarkers and in the plasma metabolome between the two lines.

Consistent with a restraint of atherogenesis and suppression of inflammatory soluble intracellular adhesion molecule-1 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) in hyperlipidemic mice treated with low dose aspirin,38 aortic scRNA-seq analyses revealed that SMCs, macrophages, SEM and T cells in the Gp1ba-ΔCre platelet Cox-1−/− mice expressed higher levels of genes associated with anti-inflammatory properties, including immunoglobulin (Igha and Igkc), secretoglobin (Scgb1a1, Scgb3a1, Scgb3a2) and bacterial permeability family member (Bpifa1). Administration of immunoglobulin to mice and genetic deletion of the latter genes modulate immune responses and activate inflammatory pathways in animal models.39-41 In addition, the systemic inflammatory markers MIP-1α and MCP-1 were reduced in the Gp1ba-ΔCre Cox-1−/− mice coincident with an increase in plasma levels of α-tocopherol and HDL-C.42,43 In contrast, Pf4-ΔCre Cox-1−/− mice had lower anti-inflammatory gene expression and elevated plasma levels of IL-1β due to the activation of the inflammasome. Consistent with an inflammatory environment, CD11b+ cells isolated from the atherosclerotic plaques of Pf4-ΔCre Cox-1−/− mice displayed a M1-like macrophage markers. Plasma HDL-C and the capacity for RCT were both reduced in Pf4-ΔCre platelet Cox-1−/− mice.

The expression of prostaglandin related genes was mostly below the limit of detection in all the cell clusters identified in scRNA analysis of aorta. Despite this, Cox-1 mRNA in mouse aortas and peritoneal cells (50-60% B cells, ~30% macrophages and 5-10% T- cells)44 from mice fed a HFD, as well as CD3+ T cells isolated from aortic plaques, as determined by rt-qPCR were markedly reduced in Pf4-ΔCre -mediated recombination as compared to the deletion by Gp1ba-ΔCre.

Overall, we have evaluated the impact of Cox-1 deletion in platelets mediated by Pf4-ΔCre or Gp1ba-ΔCre on prostanoid biosynthesis and models of cardiovascular disease (Figure S13). Our data support the notion that despite potentially different impacts on platelet function, both models simulate low dose aspirin’s effects on thrombosis, but they display a differential impact on atheroprotective prostanoids and inflammation, resulting in a divergent impact on atherogenesis.
