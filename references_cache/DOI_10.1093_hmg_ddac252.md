# Assigning pathogenicity for TAB2 variants using a novel scalable functional assay and expanding TAB2 disease spectrum
**Authors:** Weiyi Xu, Andrea Graves, Monika Weisz-Hubshman, Lamees Hegazy, Christina Magyar, Zian Liu, Eleni Nasiotis, Md Abul Hassan Samee, Thomas Burris, Seema Lalani, Lilei Zhang
**Journal:** Human Molecular Genetics (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1093/hmg/ddac252](https://doi.org/10.1093/hmg/ddac252)

## Content

AbstractHaploinsufficiency of TGF-beta-activated kinase 1 (MAP3K7) binding protein 2 (TAB2) has been associated with congenital heart disease and more recently multiorgan structural abnormalities. Missense variant represents a major proportion of non-synonymous TAB2 variants reported in gnomAD (295/576) and Clinvar (16/73), most of which are variants of uncertain significance (VUSs). However, interpretation of TAB2 missense variants remains challenging because of lack of functional assays. To address this issue, we established a cell-based luciferase assay that enables high-throughput screening of TAB2 variants to assess the functional consequence for predicting variant pathogenicity. Using this platform, we screened 47 TAB2 variants including five pathogenic controls and one benign control, and the results showed that the transcriptional activity of activator protein 1 (AP-1) but not nuclear factor kappa B predicts the TAB2 variant pathogenicity. This assay provides accurate functional readout for both loss-of-function (LOF) and gain-of-function variants, which are associated with distinct phenotypes. In all, 22 out of 32 tested VUSs were reclassified. Genotype–Phenotype association showed that most patients with partial LOF variants do not exhibit congenital heart disease but high frequency of developmental delay, hypotonia and dysmorphic features, which suggests that genetic testing for TAB2 is needed for a broader spectrum of patients with more diverse phenotypes. Molecular modeling with Npl4 zinc finger (NZF) domain variants revealed that the stability of the NZF domain in TAB2 protein is crucial for AP-1 activation. In conclusion, we developed a highly effective functional assay for TAB2 variant prediction and interpretation.

Disruption of TGF-beta-activated kinase 1 (MAP3K7) binding protein 2 (TAB2) function was initially associated with congenital heart defects (CHDs) through mapping of microdeletion regions in 12 patients with CHDs (1). Recent studies have expanded the clinical spectrum of TAB2-associated disorders to cardiomyopathy and developmental abnormalities in multiple organ systems, including dysmorphic features, global/intellectual developmental delay, skeletal abnormalities and hypermobility suggestive of connective tissue disorder (1–10), with occasional reports of hearing loss (4,11), skin abnormalities (4) and hypotonia (3). All these clinical features were described from patients carrying nonsense variants or a microdeletion overlapping the TAB2 gene body, which results in either truncation or complete deletion of the protein. Little is known about the clinical consequence of missense variants in TAB2-induced activities of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and MAPK signaling pathways.

To date, there are 295 missense variants documented in gnomAD, which accounts for 51.2% of the non-synonymous variants. Clinvar reports 16 TAB2 missense variants in which 15 are variants of uncertain significance (VUSs). In addition, 65 TAB2 missense variants were found in Baylor Genetics (BG) database in which 22 have not been previously reported in either gnomAD or Clinvar. After combining all the missense variants from these three databases, we found that, except for P208S that were classified as pathogenic in Clinvar, all the other 324 TAB2 missense variants remain VUSs or uncharacterized. This observation underscores a clinical need to develop a functional assay for TAB2 variant interpretation as well as to characterize the full spectrum of TAB2-associated disorders including intermediate phenotypes.

TAB2 or TGF-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1 or MAP3K7) binding protein 2 plays an important role in the recruitment and activation of TAK1 upon external stimuli from growth factors or cytokines, which subsequently leads to the activation of a wide range of biological processes through NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways (12–14). The TAB2 protein is highly modular, consisting of a coupling of ubiquitin conjugation to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) degradation (CUE) domain near the N-terminus, a coiled coils (CC) domain, a TAK1-binding (TBD) domain and a C-terminal Npl4 zinc finger (NZF) domain. Crystal structure of the TAB2 NZF domain and co-immunoprecipitation revealed its specific recognition of Lys 63-linked polyubiquitin chains, which is required for the activation of TAK1, recruitment of substrate proteins and formation of the TAK1 signaling complex (15–17).

Taking advantage of the signaling function of TAB2, we established a luciferase-basedin vitrofunctional assay that enables high-throughput functional screening for TAB2 missense variants. In this study, we screened 47 TAB2 variants including five pathogenic controls and one benign control and showed that the activity of activator protein 1 (AP-1) activation is highly associated with pathogenicity. Thus, AP-1 luciferase reporter assay can be utilized as a powerful prediction tool for TAB2 variant interpretation. Input of functional data led to the reclassification of 68.8% (22 of 32) of VUSs. Moreover, analysis of phenotype-genotype association suggests that patients with partial loss-of-function (p-LOF) variants appear not only to have a much milder cardiac phenotype, but also a higher frequency of developmental delay and hypotonia. We further utilized structural modeling for two NZF domain mutants and revealed that TAB2-induced AP-1 activation is highly sensitive to destabilization of the NZF domain. In summary, luciferase reporter assays for AP-1 activity enable high-throughput screening and prediction of TAB2 variant pathogenicity and phenotype heterogeneity.

The use of next-generation sequencing in clinical settings has greatly enhanced our ability to identify variants in disease-causing genes and make clinical diagnoses. However, the pathogenicity of missense variants is often difficult to determine, which leads to ambiguity in diagnosis and is currently a critical unmet need in clinical genetic diagnosis. With the intent to identify potential pathogenic TAB2 missense variants in the human population, we selected 40 TAB2 missense variants from the gnomAD database based on the following criteria: (1) the variant has a scaled CADD score (C-score) >15 (Fig. 1A). A cutoff at 15 for identifying potential pathogenic variants has been suggested by the developer of CADD, as the value is the median value for all possible canonical splice site changes and non-synonymous variants (18); (2) the variant has a minor allele frequency (AF) <0.0002 (Fig. 1B). In addition to the 40 variants from gnomAD database, we included R96K- and C684Y-TAB2, as they are documented in both BG database and Clinvar in patients with very significant cardiac features, which are part of the TAB2 clinical spectrum. In BG database, the two cases with R96K mutation and one case with C684Y mutation remain unsolved and probably solved by TAB2 mutation, respectively. Both variants have C-scores of over 20. Therefore, we suspected that they may be pathogenic.

As for the pathogenic control, we included four previously reported pathogenic variants, which are P208S-, Q230K-, Q540R- and E569K-TAB2. The former two variants caused structural heart defects (1), whereas the latter two led to a distinct frontometaphyseal dysplasia (FMD) phenotype in patients (6,7). They have C-scores ranging from 19.38 to 31 (Fig. 1A). A nonsense variant, Q127X-TAB2, was also included as an additional pathogenic control. Q127X was found in a patient in BG with CHDs and other developmental abnormalities. In fact, Q127X has the highest C-score of 36 among all the variants we selected (Fig. 1A).

H158Y-TAB2 was chosen as a benign control, because (1) there are four cases in BG with this variant but solved by mutations in other genes; (2) it has a C-score of 14.81, which is below the cutoff at 15 (Fig. 1A). In addition, the allele frequency of H158Y-TAB2 is 0.00013, which is the second highest among all the other variants from gnomAD (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, we also included another eight suspected benign variants (Q83K, T104M, R189C, P130A, T292A, R452L, A512G and R521W), as they each have a high allele frequency (Fig. 1B) and are predicted to be benign in VarSome. The variant positions of all the TAB2 variants in this study are shown inFigure 1C. The gnomAD allele frequency, CADD raw score and CADD C-scores of all the variants selected in this study are summarized inSupplementary Material, Table S1. Patients with matching clinical information in BG database were summarized inSupplementary Material, Table S2.

Currently, there is no available functional assay to determine the pathogenicity of TAB2 variants. Since NF-κB and AP-1 are two downstream targets of the TAB2-TAK1 complex, we set out to test whether the activity of these two transcription factors, measured using a luciferase assay, can report the activities of different TAB2 variants (Fig. 1D). To set up the luciferase assay, we co-transfected the HEK293T cells with (1) a TAB2 expression vector, in which expression of a TAB2 variant was driven by a constitutive CMV promoter; (2) a luciferase reporter plasmid, in which expression of firefly luciferase is under the control of an NF-κB or AP-1 responsive element in the promoter region and (3) aRenillaluciferase expression plasmid for normalization of transfection efficiency. Endogenous TAB2 was not knocked out as the vast majority (213/216) of patients carrying variants in the TAB2 gene are heterozygous according to our record in BG database. To maximize the dynamic range of our assay while controlling the expression level of TAB2 within a physiological range, we titrated the amount of TAB2 expression vector for transfection to determine the minimal amount of vector for achieving a maximal response from the reporter plasmid (Fig. 1E). TAB2-induced activation of NF-κB and AP-1 activities was observed in a dose-dependent manner, which confirms the utility of these two reporters in examining TAB2 functionin vitro. The maximal responses of NF-κB and AP-1 reporter were reached at 20 and 100 ng, respectively. However, AP-1 reporter activity only increased by 28.77% from 20 to 100 ng (4.99–6.43-folds versus 0.8 ng group) (Fig. 1E). Based on the dose response curve, we chose 20 ng as the optimal amount of TAB2 expression vector for transfection in our assay system. This will allow us to fully evaluate both loss- and gain-of-function (LOF and GOF) variants.

We assessed the performance of our luciferase-based functional assay in a medium-scale screening for the selected 47 TAB2 variants.

We found that the Q127X null mutant exhibited a significantly lower activity than WT in both AP-1 (51.3%) and NF-κB (5.2%) reporter assays (eight and first bars on the left inFig. 2AandB). This suggests that both assays can predict complete LOF variants.

A GFP control was also included to assess the endogenous level of AP-1 and NF-κB activities in HEK293T cells (first bar on the right inFig. 2AandB). As expected, the GFP control level was similar to the level of LOF variants in the NF-κB assay, which is about 5% of the WT-TAB2 level (Fig. 2B). These data indicate that the NF-κB reporter reading is a simple summation of the NF-κB reporter activity induced by both endogenous TAB2 and the TAB2 variants/GFP overexpression from the vector (Supplementary Material, Fig. S1A).

Surprisingly, in the AP-1 reporter assay, the GFP control level is similar to the WT-TAB2 level. The level of LOF variants is about 50% less than the level of GFP control (Fig. 2A). Based on these data, we proposed that the TAB2-induced AP-1 activation requires a putative limiting factor that interacts with the endogenous TAB2, ectopic TAB2 expressed from the vector and potentially other AP-1 activators in the cell but does not interact with GFP (Supplementary Material, Fig. S1B). Therefore, in cells with ectopic GFP expression, the limiting factor would interact only with the endogenous TAB2 and other AP-1 activators, which gives rise to the baseline AP-1 activity. In contrast, overexpression of LOF TAB2 variants would compete for the limiting factor but cannot induce AP-1 activity (Supplementary Material, Fig. S1B). Because Q127X variant induced similar level of AP-1 activity as other LOF variants did, we speculate that the limiting factor interaction site may be within the first 126 amino acids from the N-terminus.

Taken together, our data suggest that the amount of functional TAB2 protein is the limiting factor for TAB2-induced NF-κB activation, whereas for TAB2-induced AP-1 activity, an additional limiting factor is likely to be involved. These data may also explain why the same TAB2 mutations may lead to different consequences in the activation of AP-1 and NF-κB activity (Fig. 2). Future study is required to identify the putative limiting factor in TAB2-induced AP-1 activity.

We then looked at two previously reported LOF pathogenic variants, Q230K and P208S, which have been reported in two unrelated patients with outflow tract defects (one having left ventricular outflow tract obstruction and the other having bicuspid aortic valve) (1). As expected, they showed lower activities in the AP-1 reporter assay, indicating a LOF in MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the NF-κB reporter assay failed to detect reduction in luciferase activity for these two variants; instead, they both showed higher activities than WT (Fig. 2B). These results suggest that the AP-1 reporter assay correlates better with the clinical phenotype and is a more effective tool for TAB2 variant function prediction and interpretation of cardiovascular defects.

For the AP-1 reporter assay, we took the 95% confidence interval of the WT group as a cutoff to define variants of normal activity. Variants with lower or higher activities than WT-TAB2 were further divided into groups of LOF, p-LOF, partial GOF (p-GOF) or GOF based on the level of reporter activates (Fig. 2A). LOF is defined by a known null variant (Q127X). GOF is defined by variants with > 2-fold activity of WT. p-LOF and p-GOF are those with intermediate activities. The benign variant H158Y-TAB2 is categorized as p-GOF in the AP-1 reporter assay, suggesting that a TAB2 mutation that leads to a slight increase in AP-1 activity (<2-fold) may be tolerated and does not present with a clinical phenotype. For eight suspected benign variants, three showed normal activities, four fell into the p-LOF category, two of which were located right next to the WT and only one fell into the LOF category.

Results from the AP-1 reporter assay predicted two GOF missense variants, E569K and Q540R. In fact, using similar luciferase reporter assays, a previous study showed E569K variants caused an increase in MAPK activity but no apparent alteration in NF-κB signaling activity (6), which is consistent with our data. Patients carrying these two variants developed FMD and did not have any cardiac defects, which is distinct from the significant cardiac developmental anomalies observed in patients carrying LOF TAB2 variants.

Our data support the use of an AP-1 reporter assay to identify both GOF variants which cause FMD, and LOF variants, which lead to congenital cardiac anomalies and other developmental defects.

To further confirm that the functional readout faithfully reflects the activities of different TAB2 variants, rather than changes in the endogenous TAB2 level, we generated a TAB2-KO HEK293T line by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. Two sgRNAs were designed to delete the coding region in exon 2 (Supplementary Material, Fig. S2A), resulting in the complete loss of TAB2 protein (Supplementary Material, Fig. S2B).

This endogenous TAB2-KO HEK293T line also allowed us to accurately determine the transfection efficiency of TAB2 expression vectors using immunofluorescent (IF) staining followed by microscopy and flow cytometry. Under our experimental conditions, which is shared by all transfection experiments in this manuscript, the transfection efficiencies of R683H, WT, R96K and E569K expression vectors are all around 31% with very little variation among the four groups (Supplementary Material, Fig. S3). As all the TAB2 variant vectors are of exactly the same length, the same transfection efficiency is expected for all of them.

We then examined the activities of two LOF variants (Q127X and C684Y), WT, a GOF variant E569K and GFP control under the endogenous TAB2 knockout background. Consistent with the previous data obtained in the presence of endogenous TAB2 (Fig. 2AandB), Q127X and C684Y showed LOF, and E569K showed GOF in both AP-1 and NF-κB activity (Fig. 2CandD). These data demonstrated that the readout from the reporters faithfully reflects the functional impact of TAB2 variant expressed ectopically.

In addition, NF-κB activity is similar among Q127X, C684Y and the GFP control group, suggesting the quantity of functional TAB2 protein determines the NF-κB activity in the system (Supplementary Material, Fig. S4A). In contrast, basal AP-1 activity indicated by the GFP control group exhibited an intermediate level between the two LOF variants and WT. This result supports the presence of endogenous limiting factor and other AP-1 activator in the system (Supplementary Material, Fig. S4B). Under endogenous TAB2 knockout background, basal AP-1 activity in GFP group is generated by non-TAB2 activators, which also require the limiting factor; WT-TAB2 from ectopic expression induces stronger activation of AP-1 than other AP-1 activator alone, resulting in a higher activity than the GFP group; ectopic expression of LOF variant competes for the limiting factor with other AP-1 activator but does not induce any AP-1 activity, resulting in a lower activity in LOF group than GFP group. Future study is required to identify the limiting factor for AP-1 activation.

We classified the 47 TAB2 variants evaluated by the luciferase assay using ACMG classification criteria (19) (Fig. 3AandSupplementary Material, Table S3). To assess the significance of functional data input in variant classification, we first generated the variant classification without the functional data, which showed that 68.09% (32/47) variants are VUSs. We then reclassified the variants with the AP-1 reporter assay data. Overall, 21 VUSs were reclassified as likely pathogenic variants; 1 VUS was reclassified as a benign variant; 4 likely pathogenic variants were reclassified as pathogenic variants. Therefore, in this set of TAB2 variants, functional data from the AP-1 reporter assay led to the reclassification of 68.8% (22/32) of VUSs; 70.3% (26/37) of the variants that were VUS or likely pathogenic have increased confidence in variant calling with the functional data. We did not use the NF-κB reporter assay data for reclassification as it does not predict the variant pathogenicity in known cases (Fig. 2B). Detailed information of variant reclassification is summarized inSupplementary Material, Table S3.

Among all the 24 missense variants that were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, only 6 missense variants were shown to be LOF in the AP-1 assay (P296L, G224R, Q246L, Q230K, R683H and C684Y). C684Y is the only variant that showed LOF in both the AP-1 and NF-κB assay. Most of these pathogenic/likely pathogenic missense variants are partially LOF in the AP-1 assay, whereas the NF-κB activation function varies from p-LOF to p-GOF (Fig. 2A and BandSupplementary Material, Table S3). As the functional consequences of different TAB2 variants vary on AP-1 and NF-κB activity, these variants may influence phenotype heterogeneity and disease severity. We thus compared nine typical TAB2-associated clinical phenotypes between the following two cohorts (Fig. 3B): (1) LOF variants cohort, which includes 20 patients with nonsense variants, 11 with frameshift mutations, 2 with splicing mutations and 1 patient with the C684Y variant. All these cases were ascertained from a recent publication (8). The C684Y variant was also characterized as LOF in both the AP-1 and NF-κB assays in our study (Fig. 2A and B); (2) p-LOF cohort, which includes one patient with D9N, two patients with R96K and one patient with L676F. These cases were chosen based on the following: (1) the variants were reclassified as likely pathogenic (Supplementary Material, Table S3); (2) the variants have matching clinical information in BG database for analysis.

The results showed that the LOF cohort predominantly presented with cardiac, developmental and connective tissue phenotypes (Fig. 3B, left column). Structural heart defects, cardiomyopathy, skeletal abnormalities and dysmorphic features are the most common phenotypes in this cohort (55.9–88.2%). In contrast, the p-LOF cohort seems to have a much lower chance of developing a cardiac phenotype. In particular, 56% of the patients developed cardiomyopathy in the LOF cohort, but no patients were affected in the p-LOF cohort. And only one out of the four patients in the p-LOF cohort developed CHD. Developmental delay (75%), hypotonia (75%) and dysmorphic features (50%) appear to be the main phenotypes in the p-LOF cohort (Fig. 3B, right column). Taken together, our data may suggest that the heart may be less affected in patients with p-LOF variants. Importantly, genetic testing for TAB2 should be considered in a broader clinical spectrum, even when CHD is not present. However, this observation is based on a very small number of patients and variants; more TAB2 variants with matching clinical information are required to further validate this observation in future studies.

The C-terminal NZF domain of TAB2 recognizes the Lys 63-linked polyubiquitin chains, which is essential for TAK1-activated MAPK and NF-κB signaling (16,17). Three NZF domain missense variants, R683H-, C684Y- and L676F-TAB2, were interrogated in this study. C684Y- and L676F-TAB2 showed complete and p-LOF in both the AP-1 and NF-κB reporter assays (Fig. 2). However, it is interesting that R683H-TAB2 showed complete LOF in the AP-1 assay, but normal activity in the NF-κB assay (Fig. 2). To decipher the mechanism that underlies the differential behaviors among these NZF domain variants, we computationally modelled the structure of the NZF domain in R683H- and C684Y-TAB2 based on the crystal structure of human TAB2 NZF domain (15,16) (Fig. 4).

In WT-TAB2, a zinc ion is stabilized in the NZF domain pocket by four coordination bonds with the residues of C670, C673, C684 and C687 (Fig. 4A). Our molecular modelling predicts that in C684Y-TAB2, mutagenesis of Cys684 residue into tyrosine leads to the loss of its coordination bond with the zinc ion and collapse of the Zn-finger, which explains the LOF in both AP-1 and NF-κB activities for this mutant (Fig. 4B).

Mutagenesis of Arg683 into histidine residue followed by energy minimization revealed changes in the interaction pattern in the Zn-finger domain of TAB2 (Fig. 4C and D). Histidine is predicted to make Van der Waal contacts with the loop constituting amino acid residues Cys684-Glu688 (Loop C684-E688), whereas the carboxylic group of Glu688 rotates to make hydrogen bonding interactions with the backbone of Cys687, thus causing inward displacement of loop C684-E688. As a result, Zn slightly changes its position and makes another coordination bond with the backbone carbonyl oxygen of Gln686. The coordination bond with the residue Cys670 is lost because of an increase in the distance between Zn and Cys670 by about 1 Å. Therefore, although R683H distorts the Zn coordination geometry, it still maintains four coordination bonds that stabilize the structure of the NZF domain. Our structural modelling data suggest that the stability of the NZF domain is different in R683H- and C684Y-TAB2, which may explain the functional difference. These data may further suggest that slight geometry distortion in TAB2 NZF domain is tolerable for the activation of NF-κB signaling, but not for MAPK/AP-1 activation.

It is possible that the LOF and GOF phenotypes observed in different TAB2 variants are resulted from altered protein expression levels. To this end, we examined the protein expression levels of a complete LOF variant C684Y-TAB2, a p-LOF variant R96K-TAB2 and a GOF variant E569K-TAB2 (Fig. 5A). WT-TAB2 overexpression group was used as the positive control. GFP and Q127X-TAB2 groups were used as negative controls, as well as an indication of the endogenous TAB2 level in HEK293T cells. The western blot results showed that in our experimental condition, WT-TAB2 was overexpressed by about 3-fold compared with the endogenous TAB2 level. There was no significant difference in the expression levels of R96K- and E596K-TAB2 compared with WT-TAB2. Therefore, the functional alterations in R96K (p-LOF) and E569K (GOF) mutants are not caused by changes in the protein expression level, but because of intrinsic changes in the protein function.

In contrast, the expression level of C684Y-TAB2 is about 1.6-fold of the endogenous level, which is ~50% of the WT-TAB2 level (Fig. 5A). As is indicated by the molecular modeling data, C684Y-TAB2 has a disrupted NZF domain, which may result in improper folding and increased protein degradation.

Proteasomes degrade over 80% of the intracellular proteins, and the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway is the predominant route for degrading misfolded protein (20–23). We reasoned that if C684Y mutation leads to increased proteasome-mediated degradation, treatment of proteasome inhibitor MG132 could rescue the protein expression level of this variant. Our data showed that MG132 had no effects on the expression level of WT-TAB2 and increased the endogenous TAB2 level by about 60% (Fig. 5B). An increase in p27 protein level further confirmed the inhibition of proteasome function by MG132 (Supplementary Material, Fig. S5). Surprisingly, MG132 failed to increase the expression of C684Y-TAB2, suggesting that C684Y mutation does not lead to increased protein degradation by proteasomes. We speculate that this variant might have a lowered protein translation level, or it is more susceptible to alternative protein degradation mechanism independent of proteasomes (23). Taken together, C684Y-TAB2 is defective both in maintaining NZF domain stability and protein expression level.

Classification of missense variants remains a major challenge for clinical genetics diagnosis. In this study, we established a luciferase assay-based platform for predicting TAB2 variants’ pathogenicity, which is readily adaptable in a high-throughput manner. Two major considerations were taken into the design of our system for a better representation of the pathophysiological condition in humans. First, according to our record in BG database, 213 out of 216 patients carrying variants in the TAB2 gene are heterozygous. Therefore, we choose to perform functional interpretation of the TAB2 variants in the presence of endogenous TAB2 (Fig. 2A and B). Nonetheless, we showed that the result holds true on a TAB2 knockout background (Fig. 2C and D). Second, unlike most of the overexpression experiments, which demands a high level of expression, we transfected the minimal amount of TAB2 expression vector to achieve maximal dynamic range while restraining the expression of TAB2 variants at a physiological level (Figs 1E and5A). Our western blot data showed that the protein expression levels of WT-, R96K- and E569K-TAB2 were only about 3–4-fold of the endogenous TAB2 protein level (Fig. 5A). Some variants such as C684Y (~1.6-fold) may have an even lower expression level (Fig. 5A). Thus, the protein expression level of TAB2 variants is controlled within the physiological range in our assay system.

By using the five pathogenic control variants (Q127X, Q208S, Q230K, Q540R and E569K) and a benign variant H158Y-TAB2 as reference, we determined that AP-1 reporter activity is a better predictor for TAB2 variant pathogenicity than that of NF-κB (Fig. 2A and B). Among the five pathogenic control variants, patients with Q540R and E569K showed FMD without any cardiac defects, which is distinct from patients carrying the other three pathogenic variants with previously reported phenotypes caused by TAB2 haploinsufficiency (1–8,11). We believe that for the first time, our AP-1 reporter assay provided direct functional evidence that TAB2 AP-1 GOF variants are associated with FMD, whereas AP-1 LOF variants are associated with cardiac defects and other congenital anomalies. While our functional assay has high confidence in determining complete LOF variants and GOF variants with 2-fold difference compared with WT, cutoff for p-LOF or p-GOF variant is statistically determined as 95% confidence interval of the WT-TAB2 group (Fig. 2A and B) because of the lack of patient phenotype information. More input of clinical information in the future will help accurize the cutoff for p-LOF or p-GOF variant in the assay system.

Although both AP-1 and NF-κB are downstream targets of the TAK1 signaling complex, our data suggested that they are activated by distinct mechanisms. First, expression of the same TAB2 variant can lead to different or even opposite effects on AP-1 and NF-κB reporter activities. Second, when compared with endogenous levels, expression of WT-TAB2 increased NF-κB activity but had no effect on AP-1 activity, whereas Q127X-TAB2 expression decreased AP-1 activity but did not alter NF-κB reporter activity (Fig. 2A and B). Under the TAB2-KO background, endogenous AP-1 level is between those of Q127X and WT-TAB2 (Fig. 2C). These interesting observations provide novel insight into the biology of TAK1 signaling. Based on our data, we hypothesized that other than the activity of the TAK1 complex, additional AP-1 activators and a putative limiting factor exist and contribute to the activation of AP-1 (Supplementary Material, Figs S1andS4). A potential interaction site on the TAB2 protein may be located within the first 126 amino acids from the N-terminus, as the Q127X variant showed a similar level of reduction in AP-1 activity compared with that of other LOF variants (Fig. 2A and C). Future work to identify this putative factor will further expand our molecular understanding of the TAB2 clinical spectrum.

CHDs are considered to be the hallmark of TAB2 deficiency-associated disease. However, this observation is largely based on clinical phenotypes of patients with nonsense, frameshift, splicing variants and microdeletion in the TAB2 gene region (1–5,8), which only represent the phenotypes caused by complete LOF in both AP-1 and NF-κB activities. In our study, 20 out of 46 missense variants showed different functional consequences in AP-1 and NF-κB activities (Fig. 2A and B). Moreover, 18 of the 24 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants are p-LOF variants (Fig. 2A and BandSupplementary Material, Table S3). Molecularly, both AP-1 and NF-κB regulate a broad range of biological processes (24,25), and crosstalk mechanism between AP-1 and NF-κB also exists (26). Thus, intermediate phenotypes in patients with p-LOF TAB2 variants are expected to be present, but still lack investigation. By comparing clinical phenotypes with the LOF cohort from a previous publication (8), we found that patients with p-LOF variants appear to have a much milder or even lack of cardiac phenotype, but exhibit developmental delay, hypotonia and dysmorphic features with much higher frequency (Fig. 3B). Although this observation is based on a small number of patients, our data suggest that CHDs are only one of the major features of the TAB2 clinical spectrum, and genetic testing for TAB2 needs to be performed in a broader spectrum of patients including those with developmental delay, hypotonia and dysmorphic features. The full clinical spectrum of intermediate phenotypes in patients with different p-LOF variants is in urgent need to be characterized in future study.

Our data suggest that NF-κB activity does not directly associate with pathogenicity (Fig. 2B); however, it remains possible that NF-κB activity also influences phenotype heterogeneity and disease severity. We could not test this hypothesis because of a limited number of patients with matching phenotype information. Notably, a recent case report described an individual with both FMD and cardiac anomaly with a novel TAB2 missense variant P483L (9). Patients with Q540R or E569K only showed FMD but no cardiac defects. As Q540R and E569K mutations showed normal NF-κB activity in the luciferase reporter assay (Fig. 2B), we speculate that P483L mutation not only causes GOF in AP-1 activity but may also alter NF-κB activity. Alternatively, there could be additional undiscovered genetic etiology accounting for the cardiac phenotype. Future study is required to distinguish these hypotheses.

Previous studies on the molecular function of TAB2 have been mainly focused on the NZF domain, which is the only domain in the TAB2 protein with available crystal structure information (15,16). This has allowed us to predict how an NZF domain variant alters the NZF structure and how that could potentially impact protein function. We were particularly interested in two NZF domain variants in our study, R683H and C684Y, because of their immediate neighboring positions yet distinct functional profiles: both cause LOF in AP-1 activity, but R683H-TAB2 showed normal NF-κB activity, whereas C684Y-TAB2 showed LOF in NF-κB activity (Fig. 2A and B). Molecular modeling revealed that C684Y completely disrupts the NZF domain, whereas R683H distorts the NZF domain geometry but still manages to maintain its stability to some extent (Fig. 4). Insight from structural remodeling further supports our prediction that TAB2-induced AP-1 activation and NF-κB activation are mediated by different mechanisms, as they have different sensitivity to alteration in NZF domain geometry with the latter one being more tolerative. These data may further suggest that the NZF domain is directly involved in recruiting a substrate protein that leads to the activation of AP-1, whereas an NF-κB activator protein may be recruited by other domains in TAB2. This information is critical in interpreting novel variants in the TAB2 NZF domain.

In addition, we examined whether TAB2 variants may have an altered protein expression level, which contributes to the alteration in its function. Importantly, we showed that two GOF variants, Q540R- and E569K-TAB2, were expressed at a similar level to WT-TAB2, suggesting the GOF phenotype comes from the intrinsic properties of these two variants. In addition, we found that the C684Y-TAB2 was expressed at a lower level, about 50% of WT-TAB2 (Fig. 5A). The proteasome inhibitor MG132, which blocks the major protein degradation pathway—ubiquitin proteasome pathway (27), was able to increase the endogenous TAB2 shown in the GFP group, but failed to rescue the expression level of C684Y-TAB2 (Fig. 5B). We proposed three possible explanations for these results; future investigation is required to further delineate: (1) C684Y-TAB2 may undergo degradation through other pathways such as autophagy-mediated lysosomal degradation (28), as misfolded proteins with different solubility in the ER may be eliminated by different degradation pathways (29); (2) C684Y mutation may lead to reduced protein translation rate, or even gene transcription rate; (3) C684Y-TAB2 may be poorly recognized by the antibody at a much lower efficiency than WT-, R96K- and E569K-TAB2. We think the third possibility is unlikely. Because the antibody we used is developed against a synthetic peptide corresponding to residues surrounding Leu330 of human TAB2, which is proximal to the N-terminus NZF domain. We also do not expect C684Y to cause a significant topological distortion of the epitope so that this antibody cannot recognize it, but we are not able to completely exclude this possibility based on the current data.

The assay system that we developed in this study is readily transferable to variant interpretation for other proteins involved in the activation of AP-1 and NF-κB signaling pathways, which regulate a broad range of biological processes. In fact, NF-κB itself has 447 missense variants documented in ClinVar where 357 are VUSs, with 18 pathogenic variants and 20 benign variants that can serve as controls for a potential functional assay. For a broader research interest, the pipeline presented in this study can be generalized to design functional assays for any variants that alter downstream transcriptional regulators with customized luciferase reporters.

In summary, our study has established luciferase-based AP-1 and NF-κB reporter assays for TAB2 variant prediction and interpretation, which can be adapted in a high-throughput fashion and can be easily implemented in many diagnostic laboratory settings. In particular, both LOF and GOF in AP-1 activity may be associated with pathogenicity but with distinct phenotypes. Functional data from the AP-1 reporter assay led to the reclassification of 22 VUSs. The cardiac phenotype in patients with partial AP-1 LOF variants appears to be much less than those with complete LOF variants, yet these patients seem to have more prominent developmental delay, hypotonia and dysmorphism. Molecular modeling also revealed that TAB2-induced AP-1 activation is more sensitive to NZF domain distortion, whereas NF-κB activation is more tolerative. Future study with more variants, matching clinical information, as well as crystal structures of other TAB2 domains would be helpful to further validate and refine this assay system.

The patients we present here were mainly ascertained from exome databases of the Baylor Genetics Laboratory, Houston, TX, and two previous publications for the null allele cohort and patients carrying P208S and Q230K-TAB2 (1,8). To determine the C-score of TAB2 variants from selected patients, pre-computed results for all possible genomic SNVs from CADD v1.6 were used for analyses (30). Two scores were returned by the model, which are ‘raw’ and ‘scaled’. The ‘scaled’ version of the score is calculated to be the rank of the magnitude of each raw score compared across all ~8.6 billion SNVs of the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome and thus allows cross-model and cross-data comparison (18). Hence ‘scaled’ C-scores were reported as our main results.
