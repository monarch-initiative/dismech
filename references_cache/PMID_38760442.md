Gliomas are the most common malignant primary brain tumours in adults and cannot 
usually be cured with standard cancer treatments. Gliomas show intratumoural and 
intertumoural heterogeneity at the histological and molecular levels, and they 
frequently contain mutations in the isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) or IDH2 
gene. IDH-mutant adult-type diffuse gliomas are subdivided into grade 2, 3 or 4 
IDH-mutant astrocytomas and grade 2 or 3 IDH-mutant, 1p19q-codeleted 
oligodendrogliomas. The product of the mutated IDH genes, D-2-hydroxyglutarate 
(D-2-HG), induces global DNA hypermethylation and interferes with immunity, 
leading to stimulation of tumour growth. Selective inhibitors of mutant IDH, 
such as ivosidenib and vorasidenib, have been shown to reduce D-2-HG levels and 
induce cellular differentiation in preclinical models and to induce 
MRI-detectable responses in early clinical trials. The phase III INDIGO trial 
has demonstrated superiority of vorasidenib, a brain-penetrant pan-mutant IDH 
inhibitor, over placebo in people with non-enhancing grade 2 IDH-mutant gliomas 
following surgery. In this Review, we describe the pathway of development of IDH 
inhibitors in IDH-mutant low-grade gliomas from preclinical models to clinical 
trials. We discuss the practice-changing implications of the INDIGO trial and 
consider new avenues of investigation in the field of IDH-mutant gliomas.

Standard care for IDH-mutant lower-grade gliomas12,13 consists of 
maximal safe resection, followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy 
in grade 3 tumours. Postoperative management of grade 2 tumours 
is often based on the definition of low-risk versus high-risk patients. 
Several definitions have been used by cooperative groups (European 
Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer and Radiation 
Therapy Oncology Group) in clinical trials14,15. In clinical practice, low 
risk is commonly defined as patients aged ≤40 years with absent or 
modest asymptomatic residual tumour not needing immediate adju-
vant treatments and to be observed with MRI. Conversely, high risk is 
defined as patients aged >40 years and/or with incomplete resection 
needing radiotherapy and chemotherapy.
Long-term survivors of chemoradiotherapy are at risk of radiation- 
induced cognitive defects and/or chemotherapy-associated DNA 
hypermutation leading to malignant transformation 16,17. In recent 
years, small-molecule inhibitors of mutant IDH have emerged as a new 
strategy for the treatment of IDH-mutant cancers, such as subsets of 
acute myeloid leukaemia (AML)18, advanced metastatic cholangiocarci-
noma19 and chondrosarcoma20. The aim of this Review is to describe the 
pathway of development and clinical investigations of IDH inhibitors in 
IDH-mutant gliomas. We discuss the practice-changing implications of  
the INDIGO trial of vorasidenib and consider potential new avenues  
of investigation in the field of IDH-mutant gliomas.
Oncogenesis in IDH-mutant gliomas
Wild-type IDH1 and IDH2 oxidize isocitrate into α-ketoglutarate 
and produce NADPH. Point mutations in codon 132 of IDH1 or 
codon 172 of IDH2 lead to the generation of mutant IDH, which reduces 
α-ketoglutarate to d-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2-HG), consuming NADPH 
in the process21 (Fig. 1). Consequently, D-2-HG levels are 10-fold to 100-
fold higher in IDH-mutant glioma than in IDH-wild-type glioblastoma 
or non-neoplastic tissues. D-2-HG inhibits α-ketoglutarate-dependent 
TET1/2 and JmjC domain-containing dioxygenases, which demethyl-
ate DNA and histones, respectively22–26, so IDH-mutant gliomas show 
increased DNA and histone methylation27. IDH-mutant gliomas pref-
erentially arise in the frontal lobes 28–30, perhaps because this region 
shows elevated expression of glutamate dehydrogenase 2 (GDH2), 
which converts glutamate to α-ketoglutarate. As mutant IDH requires 
α-ketoglutarate as a substrate, increased levels of GDH2 in the micro-
environment might promote activity of this enzyme and facilitate 
gliomagenesis31,32.
Although IDH mutation is the earliest mutational event in IDH-
mutant astrocytomas and IDH-mutant, 1p19q-codeleted oligoden -
drogliomas33 and is known to increase DNA and histone methylation, 
the exact mechanisms through which this hypermethylation drives 
gliomagenesis are still being explored. Some tumour suppressor 
genes, such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A/B ( CDKN2A/B), 
retinoblastoma-associated protein (RB1) and RASSF1A, are preferen-
tially methylated and silenced in IDH-mutant gliomas34–37. In addition, 
pro-malignant genes such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), 
tissue factor (F3) and prominin 1 (PROM1) are methylated and silenced 
in these tumours37,38. In a different part of the genome, IDH mutation 
causes the methylation of a DNA region on chromosome 4 that serves 
as an insulator and normally inhibits the transcription of platelet-
derived growth factor receptor α (PDGFRA), a receptor tyrosine kinase 
gene located at 4q12. Because methylation of this insulator prevents it 
from blocking a key transcription factor binding site associated with 
PDGFRA, IDH mutation indirectly enhances rather than suppresses 
transcription of this gene39,40.
Key points
 • Gliomas are the most common malignant primary brain tumours 
in adults, and they frequently contain mutations in the isocitrate 
dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) or IDH2 gene. Mutant IDH produces the 
oncometabolite d-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2-HG), which induces DNA 
and histone hypermethylation and interferes with immunity, thereby 
stimulating tumour growth.
 • In preclinical models, selective inhibitors of mutant IDH were shown 
to decrease the production of d-2-HG, slow tumour growth and 
promote cellular differentiation.
 • In early clinical trials, ivosidenib, a mutant IDH1 inhibitor, and 
vorasidenib, a brain-penetrant pan-mutant IDH inhibitor, were found to 
reduce D-2-HG concentrations and induce high rates of MRI-detectable 
glioma stabilization or response.
 • The phase III INDIGO trial has shown superiority of vorasidenib 
over placebo in residual or recurrent non-enhancing grade 2 
gliomas after surgery, thereby raising the prospect of delaying 
of chemoradiotherapy.
 • Future studies should evaluate vorasidenib in enhancing tumours 
of different grades of malignancy, investigate novel IDH inhibitors 
and combination of drugs, and develop advanced tools to monitor 
response and relapse.
Introduction
Gliomas are the most common malignant primary brain tumours in 
adults; they show intratumoural and intertumoural heterogeneity at the 
histological and molecular levels and tend to infiltrate throughout 
the brain1. Mutations in isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) or IDH2 are 
frequently observed in gliomas, as discovered through whole-exome 
sequencing over 15 years ago2,3. Gliomas with IDH1 or IDH2 mutations 
were consistently found to be less aggressive than IDH1-wild-type and 
IDH2-wild-type infiltrative gliomas, so screening for these mutations, 
through sequencing or use of an antibody specific for the most common 
variant, IDH1 R132H, soon became the standard of care for prognos-
tication in people with glioma4,5. The common practice is to perform 
upfront immunohistochemical screening with the IDH1 R132H anti-
body, followed by sequencing for non-canonical variants in codon 132 of 
IDH1 and 172 of IDH2 in immunonegative cases if the patient is younger 
than 60 years and/or the tumour does not contain necrosis or micro-
vascular proliferation. In extremely rare cases, IDH1 mutations can be 
found in other codons, such as 49, 97, 100 and 139 (refs. 6–9).
The WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, 
published in 2021 (ref. 10), subdivides adult-type diffuse gliomas into 
three subtypes: IDH-mutant astrocytomas (grade 2, 3 or 4); IDH-mutant, 
1p19q-codeleted oligodendrogliomas (grade 2 or 3); and IDH-wild type 
glioblastomas (grade 4). Grade 2, 3 and 4 IDH-mutant gliomas tend 
to occur at a younger age than grade 4 glioblastomas, and seizures 
represent the presenting — and often only — symptom in up to 80% of 
cases11. Lower-grade gliomas are slowly growing grade 2 or 3 tumours 
with a high risk of malignant transformation, which is characterized 
by the appearance of or an increase in contrast enhancement on MRI. 
Most of these tumours are incurable.

The pro-transformation effects of IDH mutation might also be 
attributable to D-2-HG-mediated inhibition of the histone lysine 
demethylase KDM5 and subsequent elevation of H3K4 trimethyla -
tion41 (H3K4me3). This idea aligns with a separate study showing that 
in human astrocytes lacking the tumour suppressors pRb and p53, 
IDH mutation increased H3K4me3, which in turn was associated with 
increased binding of the Myc–Max complex to the telomerase reverse 
transcriptase (TERT) promoter and, consequently, TERT reactivation42. 
Furthermore, D-2-HG is exported from IDH-mutant glioma cells into 
the microenvironment43, wherein it can suppress antitumour T cell 
activity and promote intratumoural immunosuppression44–46. Indeed, 
IDH-mutant tumours show fewer tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes 
than their IDH-wild type counterparts, and they escape surveillance 
by natural killer cells through epigenetic silencing of NKG2D ligands47. 
Moreover, D-2-HG delays dendritic cell maturation and specifically 
suppresses MHC class I-mediated and MHC class II-mediated anti -
gen presentation and co-stimulation by innate lymphoid cells, which 
translates into a reduction in the capacity to activate T cells48.
Although numerous studies have linked IDH mutation to suppres-
sion of cellular differentiation22,26,49–54, various strains of IDH-mutant 
transgenic mice develop enchondromas, sarcomas and haematopoi-
etic malignancies but not gliomas 49,50,55–58. Expression of mutant IDH 
in the embryonic mouse brain causes disorganization of perivascular 
collagen and fatal perinatal cerebral haemorrhage 59. One group has 
shown that IDH mutation in the mouse subventricular zone caused 
progenitor cell populations to expand60, whereas a different group has 
reported that expression of mutant IDH in neural stem cells triggered 
p53-mediated cell cycle arrest and suppressed differentiation61. Overt 
gliomas did not develop in either case. Therefore, in natural settings, 
gliomagenesis might result from the actions of mutant IDH over a very 
long time frame. In this context, IDH  mutation might only indirectly 
facilitate gliomagenesis by delaying differentiation and acting as a 
metabolic stressor that increases selection pressure for other more 
potent cancer-causing mutations, such as those involving the cellular 
tumour antigen p53 (TP53), transcriptional regulator ATRX (ATRX ) 
or TERT genes62.
The cellular origin of IDH-mutant gliomas also remains to be 
fully resolved. Experiments in genetically engineered mouse models 
have suggested that these tumours arise from neural precursor cells, 
astrocytes or oligodendroglial precursor cells63. One study has indi-
cated that mutant IDH1 caused immortalized human astrocytes to 
revert to a neural precursor-like state64. Single-cell RNA sequencing 
in human gliomas has shown that both IDH-mutant astrocytomas and 
IDH-mutant, 1p19q-codeleted oligodendrogliomas contained mixtures 
of proliferating stem-like cells, quiescent oligodendroglial-like cells 
and quiescent astrocyte-like cells65. The main difference between these 
two types of gliomas is that oligodendrogliomas tend to have more 
oligodendroglial-like and astrocyte-like cells, with more neurons in 
the non-neoplastic background, whereas astrocytomas have more 
stem-like cells, along with more activated microglia and macrophages65. 
The available evidence indicates that IDH-mutant astrocytomas and 
oligodendrogliomas share the same basic lineage, and most of their 
differences in mRNA expression patterns are driven by a few secondary 
molecular alterations, such as TP53 inactivation and 1p19q codeletion.
Specific combinations of secondary molecular alterations that 
could contribute to gliomagenesis tend to recur in IDH-mutant gliomas. 
TP53 alterations are present in around 90% of IDH-mutant astrocytomas 
but only 3% of IDH-mutant, 1p19q-codeleted oligodendrogliomas 10. 
Around 75% of IDH-mutant, TP53-mutant astrocytomas also have inac-
tivating mutations in ATRX, in contrast to just 1–3% of IDH-mutant, 
1p19q-codeleted oligodendrogliomas. ATRX inactivation is associated 
with alternative lengthening of telomeres (AL T) — a telomere main-
tenance mechanism involving homologous recombination that is 
characteristic of IDH-mutant astrocytomas with ATRX loss66–69. Several 
groups reported that ATRX loss by itself is insufficient to induce AL T70–72, 
with one study showing that both IDH mutation and ATRX deletion are 
required to trigger AL T73. D-2-HG inhibits the histone demethylase 
KDM4B, and inhibition of this enzyme might be required for ATRX  
inactivation to trigger AL T74,75. Patient age also seems to influence AL T, 
as IDH-mutant astrocytomas from younger patients generally show 
more AL T than those from older patients76. Deletion of ATRX in neural 
precursor cells caused the cells to become more astrocyte-like72, and 
combining IDH mutation with suppression of p53 and ATRX silenced 
SOX2 transcription, causing neural stem cells to behave like infiltrative 
glioma cells77. Because ATRX inactivation causes genomic instability 
and p53-induced cell death78–80, inactivation of this gene in IDH-mutant 
cells might enhance TP53 mutations. The IDH mutation by itself can 
cause p53-mediated cell cycle arrest in neural stem cells and, therefore, 
might also encourage subclones to inactivate p53 (ref. 61), especially 
in conjunction with ATRX loss.

IDH inhibitors in preclinical models
As IDH mutations seem to facilitate gliomagenesis, inhibitors of mutant 
IDH might be an effective therapy against these tumours. T esting of 
such inhibitors requires accurate in vivo models, which have proved 
difficult to develop.
Most patient-derived IDH-mutant gliomas grow poorly in cul -
ture91, owing in part to methylation-induced suppression of pro-
oncogenic genes, such as the aforementioned EGFR, F3 and PROM1, 
the activities of which normally promote the stem cell-like behaviour 
that is needed for a patient-derived xenograph (PDX) to develop 37,38. 
Several years after IDH mutations were first discovered in gliomas, 
the first IDH-mutant PDX, BT142, was established from a CNS WHO 
grade 4 astrocytoma with the canonical IDH1 R132H mutation92. During 
subsequent passages, BT142 lost the wild-type IDH1 allele, resulting 
in decreased D-2-HG production 93. This finding is consistent with 
biochemical studies showing that mutant IDH1 produces more D-2-HG 
when it heterodimerizes with wild-type IDH1 through a process known 
as substrate channelling 94,95, and with clinical studies showing that 
disruption of the 1:1 wild-type:mutant IDH stoichiometry is often asso-
ciated with disease progression6,96–100. During progressive disease, the 
tumours might no longer be D-2-HG-dependent and could be driven 
by tertiary genomic alterations affecting, for example, the activity of 
the CDK–RB pathway or phosphoinositide 3-kinase. Moreover, nearly 
all other IDH-mutant PDX models were developed from high-grade 
gliomas that had additional pro-malignant alterations, such as PIK3CA 
mutation or MET amplification 101. Likewise, Sleeping Beauty trans -
posase or RCAS–TVA genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) 
look and behave like high-grade IDH-mutant gliomas, and the IDH muta-
tion serves as a passenger mutation rather than a driver mutation102–105. 
In these models, the oncogenic drivers include PDGF or NRAS coupled 
with p53 knockdown102,104. Therefore, although these in vivo models 
of IDH-mutant gliomas are useful, they might not respond to mutant 
IDH inhibitors in the same way as lower-grade IDH-mutant gliomas in  
clinical trials.
Given the paucity of IDH-mutant glioma models and the urgent 
need to fast-track IDH inhibitors to clinical trials, relatively few studies 
on mutant IDH inhibitors in preclinical models have been published to 
date. The first in vivo preclinical study of an IDH inhibitor in glioma has 
shown that AGI-5198, a first-generation IDH1 R132H-specific inhibitor, 
reversed histone (but not DNA) methylation and slowed the growth 
of subcutaneously engrafted TS603 IDH-mutant grade 3 oligoden -
droglioma in mice51. A next-generation dual inhibitor of mutant IDH1 
and IDH2, vorasidenib, was shown to penetrate the mouse brain and 
reduce D-2-HG production by over 97% in an orthotopically engrafted 
TS603 PDX106. Experiments using 1H magnetic resonance spectroscopy 
(MRS) have shown that both vorasidenib and another mutant IDH 
inhibitor, BAY-1436032, reduced D-2-HG production in IDH-mutant 
BT257 and SF10417 PDXs and extended survival of the engrafted 
mice107. In support of the idea that D-2-HG suppresses antitumour 
immunity, vorasidenib combined with an immune checkpoint inhibi-
tor extended IDH-mutant glioma GEMM survival more than either 
drug alone 108,109. Mutant IDH inhibition was also shown to enhance 
radiotherapy in an IDH-mutant glioma GEMM by suppressing the DNA 
damage response110.
Owing to its similarity with the excitatory neurotransmitter gluta-
mate, D-2-HG contributes to the genesis of seizures105,111. In both in vitro 
and mouse models, vorasidenib was found to inhibit seizures induced 
by IDH-mutant glioma, in addition to its effects on tumour growth105.
Early proof-of-concept clinical trials
Early clinical trials of selective mutant IDH inhibitors in patients with 
glioma have evaluated the tolerability of these drugs and attempted to 
replicate the findings from preclinical models (Fig. 2, Box 1).
Ivosidenib, an orally available mutant IDH1 inhibitor, was investi-
gated in a single-arm, multicentre, open-label, phase I dose-escalation 
and dose-expansion study in patients with advanced IDH1-mutant 
solid tumours, including grade 2 and 3 gliomas and glioblastomas 112 
diagnosed according to the 2007 WHO classification 113. All patients 
had recurrent tumours or were unresponsive to surgery, radiotherapy 
and/or chemotherapy. In the dose-escalation phase, patients were 
enrolled into sequential cohorts of increasing doses, ranging from 
100 mg twice daily to 1,200 mg once daily in continuous 28-day cycles. 
For the dose-expansion phase, patients were separated into two cohorts

Vorasidenib, an orally available brain-penetrant dual inhibitor 
of mutant IDH1 and IDH2, was evaluated in a single-arm, multicentre, 
open-label, phase I, dose-escalation study in patients with advanced 
solid tumours, including grade 2, 3 or 4 gliomas 116. All patients had 
recurrent tumours or were unresponsive to surgery, radiotherapy 
and/or chemotherapy. Dose escalation was performed in cohorts of 
3–6 patients, with a starting dose of 25 mg up to 300 mg daily in 28-day 
cycles. Overall, 52 patients (48 with lower-grade gliomas and 4 with 
glioblastoma) were enrolled, 30 of whom had received radio therapy 
and 39 of whom had received chemotherapy. Five adverse events 
comprising grade 2 or higher elevation of blood transaminase levels, 
which occurred at doses of ≥100 mg, were designated as dose-limiting 
toxicities. Two patients discontinued treatment and seven required 
a dose reduction owing to adverse events. Ultimately, doses of <100 mg 
were recommended for further exploration in gliomas. In patients 
with non-enhancing gliomas, the objective response rate (ORR) 
according to RANO criteria was 18% (one partial response and three 
minor responses), with a duration ranging from 7.4 to 27.7 months. 
Sixteen (72.7%) of 22 patients with non-enhancing gliomas achieved sta-
ble disease; many of these individuals displayed a reduction of tumour 
area of <25%, which did not meet the criteria for a minor response. 
Only two (9.1%) of the patients in this subgroup showed disease pro -
gression. The median PFS of individuals with non-enhancing gliomas 
was 36.8 months (95% CI 11.2–40.8). No patients with enhancing glio-
mas had a radiographic response, with 17 of 30 (56.7%) displaying 
stable disease and 13 of 30 (43.3%) displaying progressive disease. 
The PFS of individuals with enhancing gliomas was 3.6 months  
(95% CI 1.8–6.5).
Two findings emerged from these early clinical trials. First, 
although the studies used historical data for comparison, which has 
limited value, the response rate and median PFS in patients with grade 2 
non-enhancing tumours compared favourably with outcomes reported 
after conventional chemotherapy117–119. Second, the antitumour activity 
of IDH inhibitors seems to be limited in enhancing tumours regard -
less of grade. This finding, which needs to be confirmed in further 
studies, might be related to the fact that in advanced glioma phases, 
additional molecular alterations that overcome the importance of IDH 
mutations emerge. Therefore, IDH inhibitors might be most active 
at early stages of tumour evolution, although controlled clinical trials 
in advanced phases of disease are not yet available.
Before ivosidenib or vorasidenib could be advanced to a phase III 
trial, a randomized perioperative phase I study was conducted to docu-
ment inhibition of the mutant IDH enzyme and mutant IDH-related 
pharmacodynamic effects in on-treatment tumour biopsies 120. 
Forty-nine patients with recurrent IDH1-mutant (R132H) non-enhancing 
grade 2 (n = 43) or grade 3 (n = 6) oligodendrogliomas or astrocytomas 
requiring surgical resection were randomly assigned to vorasidenib 
(10 mg or 50 mg once daily) or ivosidenib (250 mg twice daily or 500 mg 
once daily) or to no treatment. The patients received the drugs for 
28 days up to and including the day of surgery. All patients had under-
gone at least one previous surgery, 14 had received radiotherapy and 
24 had received systemic therapies.

ent with those from previous studies. The D-2-HG concentration in 
resected tumour tissue was maximally reduced with the higher doses 
of both vorasidenib (by 92.6%) and ivosidenib (by 91.1%). Exploratory

increase in 5-hydroxymethylcytosine in DNA, reversal of the proneural

linked to stem cell properties (consistent with an effect on cellular dif-

progression (G2/M checkpoint). Moreover, histological comparison of 
tumour tissue resected after IDH inhibitor treatment with that retrieved 
at the original surgery revealed an inverse correlation between tumour 
D-2-HG concentration and both tumour cell proliferation (as measured 
using the Ki67 index) and immune cell activation (as measured by the 
presence of tumour-infiltrating CD3+ and CD8+ T cells).

The INDIGO trial
Study design
Following the promising results of early clinical trials, a double-blind 
phase III trial of vorasidenib, known as INDIGO, was launched (Box 2). 
The trial included patients with residual or recurrent non-enhancing 
grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma (diagnosed according to the 2016 WHO cri-
teria122) who had not received prior radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and 
prospectively compared vorasidenib (40 mg once daily, administered 
continuously in 28-day cycles) with a matched placebo123. The most 
recent surgery could range from 1 to 5 years before inclusion in the trial, 
and the participants were candidates for a watch-and-wait approach, 
that is, not needing immediate radiotherapy and chemotherapy 
according to local investigator judgement.
IDH1 and IDH2 mutations (including the different variants) were 
confirmed centrally before inclusion into the study, and the cohort 
was stratified according to chromosome 1p19q codeletion status and 
baseline tumour size (diameter along the longest axis <2 cm or ≥2 cm). 
MRI scans were reviewed both at baseline and during treatment to 
evaluate the response according to the RANO criteria for lower-grade 
gliomas115. Patients who were initially assigned to placebo were allowed 
to cross over to vorasidenib if imaging-based progression was con -
firmed. The primary end point was imaging-based PFS according to 
blind assessment by an independent review committee, and the key 
secondary end point was time to next intervention. From January 
2020 to February 2022, 331 patients were randomly assigned to receive 
vorasidenib (n = 168) or placebo ( n = 163). The two groups were bal -
anced with respect to baseline characteristics, including the proportion 
of astrocytomas versus oligodendrogliomas.
Results
At a median follow-up of 14.2 months, 226 patients (68.3%) were con-
tinuing to receive vorasidenib or placebo123. Imaging-based progression 
occurred in 28% of patients receiving vorasidenib and in 54% of the 
placebo group. Imaging-based progression-free survival was signifi-
cantly longer for patients in the vorasidenib group than for those in 
the placebo group: 27.7 months (95% CI 17.0 to non-estimable) versus 
11.1 months (95% CI 11.0–13.7), with a hazard ratio (HR) for disease pro-
gression or death of 0.39 (95% CI 0.27–0.56, P < 0.001). The time to next 
intervention was significantly delayed in the vorasidenib group com-
pared with the placebo group (HR 0.26, 95% CI 0.15–0.43, P < 0.001). 
The likelihood of not receiving the next intervention by 18 months was 
85.6% (95% CI 77.8–90.8) in the vorasidenib group compared with 47.4% 
(95% CI 35.8–58.2) in the placebo group; by 24 months, the likelihood 
was reduced to 83.4% (95% CI 74.0–89.6) in the vorasidenib group and 
to 27% (95% CI 7.9–50.8) in the placebo group.
The safety profile of vorasidenib was consistent with previous 
studies. Adverse events that led to interruption of treatment occurred 
in 3.6% of the vorasidenib group and 1.2% of the placebo group. An 
increased blood alanine aminotransferase level of grade 3 or higher 
occurred in 9.6% of patients who received vorasidenib and in none 
of the patients who received placebo. Adverse events that led to the 
discontinuation of vorasidenib occurred in 3.6% of the vorasidenib 
group compared with 1.2% of the placebo group.
Another secondary end point of the INDIGO trial was the TGR in 
terms of percentage change in tumour volume by 6 months. In the 
vorasidenib-treated patients, the TGRs before and after treatment 
with vorasidenib were 13.2% and −3.3%, respectively, compared with 
18.3% and 12.2% in the placebo group124. In patients who crossed over 
from placebo to vorasidenib (n  = 38), the TGRs before and after the 
crossover were 22.4% and 5.2%, respectively.
Implications for glioma management
The results of the INDIGO trial tend to be practice-changing: given the 
magnitude of improvement in primary and secondary end points over 
placebo, vorasidenib is expected to be approved by authorities such as 
the FDA and the European Medicines Agency and to become a standard 
first-line treatment, at least for non-enhancing grade 2 IDH1-mutant and 
IDH2-mutant gliomas with residual or progressive tumour following 
surgery. Treatment with this drug might replace the watch-and-wait 
policy in low-risk patients with residual tumour after surgery and allow 
radiotherapy and chemotherapy to be delayed, thereby minimizing the 
long-term adverse effects of these therapies on the brain. However, 
the traditional distinction of low-risk versus high-risk patients based 
on age and extent of resection is subjective and varies according to 
individual physician judgement and patient preference. In particular, 
the definition of total resection varied in the different studies, includ-
ing the INDIGO trial, in which the residual tumour was not codified.

A new question that arises from the INDIGO results is whether 
the use of vorasidenib should be extended to enhancing grade 2 or 
grade 3 tumours 125. Two findings suggest that this extension might 
be warranted. First, at least one-third of grade 2 oligodendrogliomas 
are enhancing and could have been excluded from the INDIGO trial. 
Second, the distinction of grade 3 versus grade 2 gliomas (especially 
oligodendrogliomas) according to the 2021 WHO classification is not 
always clearly defined in terms of diagnostic criteria and outcome126. 
At present, the details of the registration label for vorasidenib are 
still unknown. If the approval is restricted to patients with grade 2 
non-enhancing gliomas (as per the INDIGO trial), a randomized trial 
against placebo in patients with enhancing grade 2 and 3 tumours will 
be needed.
Extension of vorasidenib treatment to grade 4 IDH-mutant astro-

gested a modest effect in aggressive enhancing tumours. T o investigate 
this possibility further, clinical trials should focus on the addition of 
vorasidenib to the standard of care of radiotherapy and temozolomide 
in newly diagnosed tumours.
The INDIGO trial was not designed to explore the efficacy of 
vorasidenib for newly diagnosed, completely resected grade 2 
tumours. The prospect of applying the drug in this ‘preventive setting’ 
is appealing, but it needs to be investigated in a specific clinical trial 
with a precise volumetric definition of complete resection, and a long 
follow-up period will be required.
Another important clinical problem posed to neuro-oncologists 
will be how to manage patients with lower-grade gliomas who have 
already received first-line chemoradiotherapy: should they implement 
early maintenance with an IDH inhibitor or wait for tumour progres -
sion? Owing to the rarity of this type of glioma, it will be difficult to 
investigate IDH inhibitors in all possible scenarios. Thus, in addition 
to clinical trials, studies based on prospective registries at international 
and national levels could help to resolve these questions.
Seizures occur in up to 80% of patients with grade 2 tumours, are 
often pharmacoresistant, might respond favourably to radiotherapy 
and chemotherapy (in addition to surgical resection), and can predict 
tumour progression11. One of the pathogenetic mechanisms under -
lying these seizures is an excess of the excitatory neurotransmitter 
glutamate in peritumoural areas 127,128. D-2-HG structurally and func -
tionally mimics glutamate, thereby contributing to the genesis of 
seizures. Consequently, patients with IDH-mutant gliomas have a 
higher seizure propensity than those with IDH-wild type tumours129, and 
preliminary preclinical and clinical studies suggest that IDH inhibitors 
have antiepileptic activity105,130. In the INDIGO trial, seizure activity was 
a secondary end point, along with cognition and quality of life. How-
ever, as uncontrolled seizures were a major exclusion criterion, the true 
effect of vorasidenib on uncontrolled or pharmacoresistant seizures 
remains unknown. Future studies should investigate seizure control 
following treatment with vorasidenib or other new IDH inhibitors 
by prospectively documenting seizure characteristics using patient 
and/or caregiver seizure diaries131.
Novel approaches and ongoing trials
In addition to further testing of vorasidenib and ivosidenib, several clini-
cal trials of novel small-molecule IDH inhibitors and other approaches 
have either been completed or are ongoing (Tables 1 and 2).
Small-molecule IDH inhibitors
BAY1436032 is an orally available, brain-penetrant inhibitor of all known 
forms of R132-mutant IDH1 and has shown efficacy in preclinical models 
of glioma132. This compound has been investigated in an open-label, 
multicentre, phase I dose-escalation and dose-expansion basket trial, 
including patients with grade 2 and 3 gliomas and glioblastomas that 
recurred after multiple lines of therapy133. The drug was well tolerated 
and a maximum tolerated dose was not reached. The best clinical out-
comes were observed in patients with lower-grade gliomas (n = 35), with 
an ORR of 11% (one complete response and three partial responses) 
and stable disease in 43% of cases according to the RANO criteria. Four 
of these patients (one complete response, one partial response and 
two stable disease), all of whom had tumours that showed contrast 
enhancement, had been on treatment for over 2 years at the time of 
publication of the trial. Conversely, the best response in patients with 
IDH-mutant glioblastomas was stable disease, which was achieved 
in 29% of 14 patients and was of brief duration.
Olutasidenib is an orally available, brain-penetrant selective 
inhibitor of mutant IDH1, which has shown efficacy in patients with 
high-risk relapsed or refractory AML 134. This compound was investi-
gated in an open-label, multicentre, phase Ib/II basket trial, including 
patients with grade 2–4 gliomas that had recurred after multiple 
lines of therapy 135. In 23 (88%) of the 26 participants, the tumours 
showed contrast enhancement on MRI. The drug was well tolerated, 
with rare grade 3–4 adverse events including moderate increases in

Vaccination strategies
As tumour-specific neoantigens, mutant forms of IDH are potential 
targets for vaccination138,139.
The NOA16 trial (NCT02454634) was a multicentre, phase I, open-
label clinical trial that evaluated an IDH1 R132H peptide vaccine in 
32 patients with IDH-mutant grade 3 and 4 astrocytomas140. The results 
showed that vaccination was safe, immunity was induced in 93.3% of the 
participants, 3-year PFS was 64% and overall survival was 84%.

Other approaches
Another promising therapeutic strategy for IDH-mutant gliomas is 
to correct the epigenetic dysregulation induced by D-2-HG. The DNA 
methyltransferase inhibitors 5-azacytidine and decitabine might induce 
DNA hypomethylation, leading to reactivation of tumour suppressor 
genes and genes that promote glial differentiation, and to restoration 
of insulator function to downregulate aberrant oncogene expression. 
Both compounds have been shown to have activity in preclinical models 
of gliomas142,143 and have been used in association with ivosidenib in  
AML144. These agents are also being investigated as monotherapy  
in early clinical trials in patients with IDH -mutant gliomas, which 
are expected to be followed by trials to investigate their efficacy in 
combination with IDH inhibitors.
Poly(adenosine diphosphate ribose) polymerase (PARP)-mediated 
DNA repair remains intact in IDH-mutant neoplastic cells and allows the 
maintenance of genomic integrity and cell survival following exposure 
to radiation and chemotherapy 145. These findings provide a ration -
ale to investigate PARP inhibitors in association with IDH inhibitors 
in clinical trials.
Novel tools for response assessment
Advanced neuroimaging
Imaging measures for response assessment and surveillance in IDH-
mutant lower-grade gliomas are needed to provide early evidence 
of benefit from new agents in clinical trials. These measures are of 
particular importance in slow-growing tumours, for which survival 
times — a standard end point in glioma clinical trials — can take many 
years to determine. Optimal imaging biomarkers of treatment efficacy 
in lower-grade gliomas have not been clearly defined to date, but some 
concepts are well established.
In the RANO criteria for low-grade gliomas115, bidirectional meas-
urements of T2 hyperintense lesion size (‘tumour area’) on a single slide 
was recommended for response assessment. However, these 2D meas-
urements of tumour size have several limitations, such as difficulty of 
capturing subtle changes in T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion 
recovery (T2-FLAIR) images, irregular shapes owing to tumour growth 
in different directions, residual glial scarring and confounding post-
radiation effects. T o address these issues, studies were conducted in 
which the volume of diametric expansion was measured to calculate 
the speed of growth of untreated low-grade gliomas 146,147. In subse-
quent studies, 3D volumetric analysis was performed on MRI scans 
of IDH-mutant lower-grade gliomas148–150. In a cohort of patients with 
non-enhancing lower-grade gliomas who received ivosidenib, Ellingson 
et al.150 demonstrated reduced intrareader discordance and growth rate 
measurement variability when 3D measurements were used instead 
of 2D measurements. In addition, 2D measurements provided signifi-
cantly shorter PFS estimates than 3D measurements, possibly owing 
premature reporting of progressive disease.
A recent study 151 has investigated the tumour volume growth 
rate (TVGR) in 128 IDH-mutant gliomas. Across the entire cohort, 
a 10.46% growth rate was reported during the observation period 
after surgery, regardless of initial tumour size or extent of resection, 
although grade 4 tumours with CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion grew 
faster than lower-grade tumours. Moreover, changes in growth rates 
over time were associated with a worse prognosis. Interestingly, the 
authors did not find any difference in TVGR during the observational 
phase between oligodendrogliomas and astrocytomas, contrary to a 
previous report by Huang et al.148. One explanation for this discrepancy 
could be that the survival benefit in oligodendrogliomas is secondary 
to their chemosensitivity and not to their inherent natural growth 
before treatment.
Perfusion and diffusion MRI metrics have also been suggested as 
early biomarkers of the response to ivosidenib or vorasidenib treat -
ment in IDH-mutant gliomas 152. An early increase in cerebral blood 
volume (CBV) within 3–6 weeks following treatment initiation was 
observed in around 65% of patients but was no longer present after 
2–4 months of treatment, and improved PFS was seen in patients who 
exhibited less elevated relative CBV values at the later timepoint. This 
early CBV rise was attributed to downstream alteration caused by a 
decrease in D-2-HG levels, leading to elevation of hypoxia-inducible 
factor 1α with induction of hypoxia and subsequent proangiogenic 
signalling. No significant changes in apparent diffusion coefficient 
(ADC) — a measure of cell density— were observed after 3–6 weeks of 
treatment, suggesting that one of the main effects of IDH inhibitors 
could be cell differentiation rather than cytotoxicity. Decreases in ADC 
or low ADC values after 2–4 months of treatment were suggestive of 
tumour progression and were associated with shorter PFS.
MRS can detect D-2-HG production and allows non-invasive iden-
tification of IDH-mutant gliomas. Early MRS studies detected elevated 
D-2-HG levels in IDH-mutant gliomas compared with IDH-wild-type 
tumours, and D-2-HG levels measured in vivo with MRS correlated 
significantly with those measured ex vivo in corresponding tumour 
samples153,154. D-2-HG detection by MRS is technically challenging 
owing to an important resonance overlap with glutamate and glu -
tamine, and several advanced MRS sequences have been proposed 
to resolve this spectral overlay. Overall, a sensitivity up to 95% and 
specificity of 91–100% for the detection of IDH-mutant glioma have 
been reported, with higher values being obtained using a MEGA-PRESS 
sequence as compared with standard sequences155,156. D-2-HG detection 
is affected by tumour cell density 157, and its levels correlate directly 
with choline levels on MRS158,159 and IDH mutation type159,160: the levels 
are higher in IDH2-mutant gliomas than in IDH1-mutant or non-R132H 
IDH-mutant gliomas.
Importantly, D-2-HG MRS allows non-invasive longitudinal moni-
toring of D-2-HG levels across the different phases of the disease, from 
the post-surgical indolent state through post-treatment follow-up. 
Interestingly, the patterns of MRS changes that are observed following 
conventional cytotoxic treatment158,159 or IDH inhibitor treatment159,161 
are different. Patients who received cytotoxic treatments (temozo -
lomide or vincristine) displayed a gradual decrease in D-2-HG levels 
on MRS over 12 months, which preceded tumour volume changes, 
and Choi et al.158 observed a more rapid decrease in oligodendroglio-
mas than in astrocytomas, although this finding was not confirmed 
by Di Stefano et al. 159. Conversely, a rapid decrease in D-2-HG levels 
was reported within a few days after beginning IDH inhibitor treat -
ment, and this change did not necessarily translate into a tumour 
response on conventional MRI. However, D-2-HG monitoring might

In patients with non-enhancing grade 2 gliomas receiving alkylat-

metabolic imaging method might be useful for patient selection and 
disease monitoring165.
A future prospect is the use of specific PET probes for IDH muta-
tions to assess the response to IDH inhibitors166. In addition, artificial 
intelligence-based algorithms applied to preoperative MRI scans have 
been shown to non-invasively identify IDH1 and IDH2 mutations and 
1p19q codeletion in gliomas with high accuracy167,168.
Liquid biopsy
Liquid biopsy is another tool with the potential to detect and monitor 
IDH mutations during IDH inhibitor treatment. Plasma and urine show 
low sensitivity as sources of D-2-HG159,169; however, several studies have 
reported that cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of D-2-HG are significantly 
higher in patients with IDH-mutant gliomas than in those with IDH-
wild-type gliomas170–172. The levels of D-2-HG in CSF seem to be stable, 
even following multiple freeze–thaw cycles, which is important for 
shipping frozen samples to a central laboratory. Whether the amount 
of D-2-HG in the CSF varies depending on the site of collection remains 
controversial. One study170 has reported that D-2-HG levels were sig-
nificantly higher in CSF of ventricular or cisternal origin than in CSF 
of lumbar origin, whereas another study found no difference between 
lumbar and intraventricular CSF samples172. The CSF level of D-2-HG 
has been reported to fall following near gross total resection173, but 
information on the usefulness of liquid biopsy for D-2-HG for disease 
monitoring is lacking. Detection of the IDH1 R132H mutation in circu-
lating tumour DNA by targeted exome sequencing and digital droplet 
PCR could be of crucial relevance in clinical practice; however, the use 
of this approach in CSF samples was not always informative172,174,175. In 
the future, technological advances that improve sequencing sensitivity 
could reduce the number of non-informative cases.
Larger cohorts of patients with IDH-mutant gliomas are needed 
to establish cut-off and reliable change values for D-2-HG in the CSF , 
with sufficient discriminating power to be used in the clinical setting. 
Factors that might influence CSF D-2-HG levels, such as tumour burden 
and proximity to the subarachnoid space, should be investigated. Also, 
standardization of CSF collection and handling procedures will be 
of the utmost importance.
Conclusions
With the positive results of the INDIGO trial and the favourable safety 
profile of vorasidenib, a targeted treatment option has become availa-
ble for patients with newly diagnosed IDH-mutant tumours, particularly 
those with grade 2 non-enhancing disease, marking an important devel-
opment for the field of neuro-oncology. For a large subset of patients 
with lower-grade IDH-mutant gliomas, vorasidenib will be an option 
after surgery and before radiotherapy and chemotherapy, providing 
a new approach to first-line treatment of these tumours. Importantly, 
according to the definition of high-risk grade 2 gliomas used by inter-
national guidelines12,13, 90% of the patients treated in the INDIGO trial 
would qualify for radiotherapy and chemotherapy treatment. Follow-
ing the INDIGO trial, the concept of high-risk and low-risk IDH-mutant 
glioma will need to be revised, which is long overdue as those concepts 
were developed using clinical data obtained in the pre-IDH era.
At present, it is unclear how the regulatory label for vorasidenib 
will be worded, especially with respect to enhancing grade 2 tumours 
and grade 3 tumours. This wording will influence the next genera -
tion of trials to further explore the role of vorasidenib and other IDH 
inhibitors in the treatment of patient with IDH-mutant lower-grade 
glioma. Additional follow-up of the INDIGO trial participants is needed 
to understand the long-term outcomes of first-line treatment with 
IDH inhibitors, and of other salvage therapies that might be adminis-
tered to patients who experience disease progression while receiving 
IDH inhibitors. It will also be important to investigate the efficacy 
of IDH inhibitors given as adjuvant treatment after gross total resection 
or as maintenance therapy after completion of chemoradiotherapy in 
the first-line setting.
Another important priority is to understand which biomarkers 
are associated with the response to IDH inhibitors and its duration. T o 
address these questions, prospective ‘real-world’ series will be needed, 
including collection of tumour, blood and CSF samples, together with 
advanced imaging findings from patients who experience disease 
progression while being on IDH inhibitors to determine how gliomas 
become refractory to these drugs.
In conclusion, the INDIGO trial has provided the first demonstra-
tion that a large subset of diffuse gliomas can respond to a targeted 
treatment, and it has also raised many new questions that will inform 
future research (Box 3). For example, can we further amplify the effect 
of IDH inhibition by combining IDH inhibitors with other treatment 
strategies, such as immune-checkpoint inhibitors, demethylating 
agents and chemoradiotherapy? Also, are patients who relapse after 
chemoradiotherapy unresponsive to vorasidenib or could some of 
these individuals benefit from the drug at that stage of their disease? 
Finally, for other IDH inhibitors that are currently under investiga -
tion, we need to devise appropriate research strategies, which could 
include head-to-head comparisons with vorasidenib, exploration 
of efficacy in patients whose tumours are refractory to vorasidenib, 
and/or combination strategies.
