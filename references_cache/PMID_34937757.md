Posterior embryotoxon, an anteriorly displaced Schwalbe’s line, is the most common feature of Axenfeld Rieger syndrome. We report a case of Axenfeld anomaly with unusual corneal manifestation, that is, a fairly symmetric, hyperproliferated posterior embryotoxon mimicking double cornea as well as a double pupil.

Keywords:eye, glaucoma, anterior chamber

Axenfeld Rieger syndrome (ARS) represents a spectrum of disorders caused by abnormal migration and differentiation of neural crest cells.1It includes Axenfeld anomaly (with posterior embryotoxon, iridocorneal adhesions), Rieger anomaly (with additional iris hypoplasia, correctopia, polycoria) and Rieger syndrome (with additional systemic features like maxillary hypoplasia, dental anomalies, congenital heart defects, redundant periumbilical skin, etc).2 3Hence, ARS primarily manifests as anomalies within the angle and the iris. The corneal manifestations reported include peripheral corneal opacification due to abrupt termination of Schwalbe’s line, buphthalmos, increased central corneal thickness and eventual corneal decompensation.3Here, we report an unusual manifestation of this disease, a retrocorneal membrane, not known hitherto.

A 9-year-old girl presented with complaints of whitish discolouration of both eyes since birth. The perinatal period was uneventful. There was no history of pain, redness, trauma or intraocular surgery. No similar illness was seen in other family members. The best-corrected visual acuity was 6/18 p (+4DS/−1DC) in the right eye (RE) and 6/24 (+6DS/−1.5DC) in the left eye (LE). Telecanthus, broad and flat nasal bridge were present. An alternative divergent squint (60PD Base in) was seen. Direct and consensual pupillary responses were equal in both eyes. Both eyes (BE) had microcornea, RE 9.5*10 cm, LE 10*10.5 cm. Peripheral corneal opacification was seen in BE with an inferonasal clear island in the RE leaving only a slit-like pupillary area clear in the central visual axis (figure 1A) and a central clear island in the LE (figure 1B). BE had spheroidal degeneration temporally with multiple iridocorneal adhesions. On slit-lamp examination, a retrocorneal avascular glassy opaque membrane was seen BE obscuring the iris. RE fundus examination was not possible due to visual axis obstruction caused by retrocorneal membrane (USG was anechoic with no optic nerve head cupping), LE fundus examination revealed CDR (cup-disk ratio) of 0.4:1 with a healthy neuroretinal rim. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was 30 mm Hg BE.

A, B: Slit-lamp photography of both eyes showing retrocorneal glassy membrane, C: anterior segment OCT of in the right eye (RE) simulating the appearance of double corneae and double anterior chambers with a small slit pupil, D: Left eye (LE) showed larger central opening mimicking a double pupil I-iris, P-pupil, CM-corneal membrane.

Anterior segment Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) (ASOCT) of RE simulated the appearance of double corneae and double anterior chambers with a small slit pupil (figure 1C) and LE showed a larger central opening mimicking a double pupil (figure 1D). Central corneal thickness RE was 814 µm and LE was 756 µm. Axial length RE was 22.3 mm and LE was 21.9 mm. The average keratometry reading was 32.5D in the RE and 31.5D in the LE. Specular microscopy showed reduced endothelial count (RE: 1304 cells/mm3, LE: 1464 cells/mm3) and pleomorphism (hexagonality (HEX) RE: 29, LE: 37). There were no other systemic abnormalities like maxillary hypoplasia, microdontia or congenital heart defects.

For the retrocorneal membrane, differentials were kept as epithelial down growth, persistent pupillary membrane and embryotoxon. A final diagnosis of Axenfeld anomaly with exaggerated embryotoxon along with ametropic amblyopia was arrived at. The patient could not afford genetic testing; hence the same was not carried out to look for genes PITX2 or FOXC1.

Three topical glaucoma medications were administered. Refractive glasses were prescribed. In consultation with the cornea specialist, no intervention was done for retrocorneal membrane because of low endothelial count and reasonably good vision.

At 6 months follow-up, the IOP was controlled (RE-16 mm Hg and LE-14 mm Hg) on three topical glaucoma medications. LE fundus revealed CDR of 0.4:1 with a healthy neuroretinal rim.

Posterior embryotoxon is an anteriorly displaced Schwalbe’s line and is pathognomonic feature of ARS.4It occurs due to developmental arrest of the tissues derived from neural crest cells during late gestation.5Posterior embryotoxon may present heterogenously such as dislocated embryotoxon in the anterior chamber, or as prominent anterior chamber cords.6But there are no reports of the exaggerated hyperproliferative appearance of PE as seen in our patient. In the absence of the iris abnormalities such as corectopia and polycoria, iris strands inserting on the posterior surface of the membrane helped us to confirm the diagnosis. The gonioscopic appearance of an abnormally prominent ridge of anteriorly displaced Schwalbe’s line (PE), with strands of tissue extending from peripheral iris to this ridge, is considered sine-qua-non of ARS.5High-resolution ASOCT aids in identifying distinctive features of ARS.7Furthermore, there are very infrequent reports of ASOCT in patients with ARS depicting posterior embryotoxon, with none reporting the features akin to our patient.7 8The hyperproliferation could be due to uninterrupted hyperplasia from mesenchymal proliferation during gestation at the time of arrest of neural crest cell migration.

The peripheral corneal opacification in our patient could be explained by the absence of the Descemet membrane with the subsequent loss of corneal transparency peripheral to Schwalbe’s line (which is very anteriorly displaced in our patient). A close differential diagnosis could be partial sclerocornea which also presents with congenital corneal opacity. Partial sclerocornea usually has a peripheral white vascularised corneal rim blending with sclera obliterating limbus,9and can also be associated with cornea plana and glaucoma.10In our case, the absence of completely opaque peripheral cornea, with angle structures beginning much posterior than immediately at the termination of Descemet membrane rule partial sclerocornea out. Peters anomaly was ruled out as other than the iridocorneal adhesions and glaucoma, central congenital corneal opacity was absent in our patient. The persistent pupillary membrane can also present as a white translucent structure, but it starts from the iris, crosses the pupil, and gets attached to the iris, unlike the retrocorneal membrane found in our patient.

Uncorrected refractive error contributing to ametropic amblyopia appears to be the most likely cause for reduction in the best-corrected visual acuity of our patient. Raised IOP in ARS is often refractory to medical management. Only one-third of the patients’ IOP is controlled by medical management and the rest two-thirds require surgical intervention.11Because of controlled IOP, fairly good acuity and clear central cornea, no intervention was sought for this case. However, whenever required, it will require a combined effort from the corneal and glaucoma surgeons to deal with this rare presentation. To our knowledge, this is the first case report describing a fairly symmetric, hyperproliferated posterior embryotoxon mimicking double cornea as well as a double pupil in Axenfeld anomaly.

I am happy that the doctors have diagnosed my condition and given the best possible treatment.

Axenfeld Rieger syndrome presents with varied clinical spectrum and sometimes may mimic many other anterior segment dysgenesis disorders.

Hyperproliferated posterior embryotoxon can mimic double cornea as well as a double pupil.

In such rare presentations like hyperproliferated posterior embryotoxon, anterior segment OCT aids in diagnosis. Genetic testing confirms the diagnosis.
