# Autism-associated ARHGEF9 variants impair GABAergic synapses and ultrasonic communication by reducing gephyrin phosphorylation.
**Authors:** Jung H, Kim B, Jang G, Lee H, Kim Y, Kim H, Lee HJ, Kim D, Yang Y, Jeong WC, Kim S, Han AR, Kim HM, Papadopoulos T, Durand B, Francannet C, Piton A, Kim JY, Ko J, Um JW
**Journal:** Mol Psychiatry (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41380-025-03313-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-025-03313-5)

## Content

Missense and truncating variants of the X-chromosome-linked ARHGEF9 gene encoding collybistin (Cb) cause intellectual disability and epilepsy. Here we report novel rare variants in ARHGEF9 identified via exome sequencing in male individuals with autism spectrum disorders (ASD). Functional analyses revealed that p.R290C promotes abnormal gephyrin clustering in COS-7 cells and reduces inhibitory synapse density in cultured hippocampal neurons. Electrophysiological recordings revealed that p.V374F and p.G485S, in conjunction with p.R290C, induce defective inhibitory synaptic transmission. Furthermore, only the p.G485S markedly decreased phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate-binding activity. Mice with conditional knockout (cKO) of Cb displayed altered inhibitory synaptic density and transmission in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). Moreover, ultrasonic vocalization was impaired in mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice. Strikingly, proteomic analyses showed that mPFC-specific Cb-cKO reduced gephyrin phosphorylation levels. Expression of a subset of ASD-associated ARHGEF9 variants failed to rescue impaired GABAergic synaptic transmission and reduced gephyrin phosphorylation levels in mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice. Our data suggest that perturbation of diverse facets of Cb activity potentially triggers the onset and/or progression of ASDs via inducing altered balance in gephyrin phosphorylation.

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) refers to a collection of diverse and prevalent neurodevelopmental conditions characterized by specific core symptoms, such as social communication deficits and stereotypical repetitive behaviors [1–4]. Studies over the past few decades have shown that ASD is highly heterogeneous in terms of clinical symptoms and causative factors and has comorbid conditions [1, 5]. For example, approximately 70% of patients diagnosed with ASD also have intellectual developmental disorder (IDD), while at least 10% of individuals with IDD present ASD [6, 7]. Using next-generation sequencing technologies, hundreds of genes have been identified as commonly related to ASD and IDD and despite their intergenetic diversity, many affect synaptic structures and/or functions [7–9]. In particular, studies focusing on the molecular basis of X-linked IDD (XLID) have significantly contributed to our understanding of the mechanisms by which dysfunction of specific genes triggers pathogenesis of ASD and/or IDD [8, 10]. Notably, prominent sexual dimorphism has been reported in the prevalence of ASD and IDD (with higher prevalence in males), although the underlying mechanisms remain to be established [11–14].

Several genes associated with XLID and synaptic regulations have been linked to ASD. Most XLID genes associated with ASD, including interleukin 1 receptor accessory protein-like 1 (IL1RAPL1), OLIGOPHRENIN-1 (OPHN1), Neuroligin-3 (NLGN3), Tetraspanin 7 (TSPAN7), and ARHGEF6, play primary roles in regulating excitatory synaptic structure, function, and plasticity [15, 16]. Highly regulated expression of these genes collectively contributes to the excitation-inhibition balance at synaptic, circuit, and network levels [17, 18]. A subset of XLID genes is exclusively expressed at GABAergic synapses [19], suggesting that deficits in the GABA system could be also directly related to ASDs [20, 21]. ARHGEF9, one such XLID gene, encodes collybistin (Cb), a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for Cdc42 that is required for the regulation of GABAergic and glycinergic synapses via interactions with gephyrin, neuroligin-2 (Nlgn2), and α2 subunit of GABAA receptors [22–26]. Three isoforms (CbI, CbII and CbIII) have been identified in rodents while only CbIII (or hPEM2) is expressed in humans [22]. CbI is not detected in adult brain, whereas CbII and CbIII are significantly expressed during all the developmental stages [22, 27]. Cbs are composed of several functional domains, specifically, a regulatory src homology 3 (SH3), pleckstrin homology (PH), and Dbl-homology (DH) domain [28]. Splice variants of CbII (CbIISH3+ and CbIISH3−) and CbIII (CbIIISH3+ and CbIIISH3−) exist, which differ by the presence or absence of the SH3 domain [22, 27]. Intriguingly, several missense and nonsense mutations in ARHGEF9 have been identified as the cause of IDD syndromes and epileptic encephalopathy, probably through inducing loss-of-function phenotypes [29–31]. However, the mechanisms by which dysfunction of Cb causes IDD and its potential involvement in pathogenesis of ASD have not yet been explored. In light of its direct interactions with gephyrin and Nlgn2 (also linked to ASD) [32–34], it is plausible that Cb plays a role in ASD pathogenesis.

Here, we report four rare potential disease-causing variants of ARHGEF9 identified during the genetic screening of individuals with IDD or ASD. We investigated the effects of these ARHGEF9 missense variants on gephyrin clustering, phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P)-binding activity and inhibitory synapse activities in cultured neurons. In addition, the synaptic and behavioral effects of Cb deficiency were explored using Cb conditional knockout (cKO) mice. Extensive proteomics analyses, in conjunction with biochemical and molecular replacement experiments, uncovered a novel mechanism whereby possible alterations in gephyrin phosphorylation mediated by Cb deficiency and/or missense mutations cause impairment of GABAergic synapses and ASD-like behavioral deficits.

Genetic analyses performed on more than 2000 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders revealed rare missense variants in the ARHGEF9 gene in four males – two initially evaluated for IDD (P1, P2) and two for ASD (P3, P4) (Table S1; Fig. 1A). Two variants, c.860 G > A p.R287H and c.868 C > T p. R290C, alter very conserved amino acids in the linker between DH and PH domains and are predicted to be damaging, according to multiple prediction programs (Table S1; Fig. 1B). The first variant (c.860 G > A p.R287H), which has never been reported, occurred de novo in a male (P2) with a moderate form of IDD. The second (c.868 C > T p. R290C), previously reported in a family with four males affected by developmental epileptic encephalopathy [35], was identified in a boy (P3) with ASD associated with moderate IDD (Table S1; Fig. 1A). Clinical evaluation a posteriori revealed that he had some generalized tonic clonic seizures. Segregation in the family revealed the presence of the variant in the mother, who has mild learning difficulties, and in one sister, who has more severe learning difficulties, speech impairment and hypothyroidism, as well as a history of hyperthermic convulsive seizures (five episodes treated with Depakine by the age of 6 year old). The second sister is asymptomatic and does not carry the variant. An analysis of HUMARA and FMR1 loci revealed random X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) in the affected sister, whereas a mild bias was observed in the mother (83/17 for HUMARA, 91/9 for FMR1). An analysis of the maternal grandparents, both asymptomatic, revealed that the variant occurred de novo in the mother. The two additional rare variants identified, c.1120 G > T p.V374F and c.1453 G > A p.G485S, are located in and after the PH domain, have a lesser effect on conserved amino acids, and are predicted to be less damaging. The p.V374F variant was identified in a boy (P1) with severe IDD and no epilepsy (except an isolated neonatal convulsion) and the p.G485S variant was identified in a boy (P4) with ASD. We next investigated the effect of these missense variants on human CB using structural analysis of rat Cb-derived crystal structures [25]. Cb consists of three domains: an N-terminal Src homology 3 (SH3) domain, a Dbl homology (DH) domain, and a C-terminal pleckstrin homology (PH) domain connected by a flexible helix (DH/PH tandem). In the “closed” form (Fig. S1A, left), the SH3 domain interacts with the DH and PH domains, restricting their movement. Conversely, the “open” form (Fig. S1A, right) lacks the SH3 domain, allowing the PH domain to adopt a different position relative to the DH domain. Cb activity in mediating gephyrin clustering is linked to interactions between the PH domain and phosphoinositides. However, interactions between the SH3 domain and the DH/PH tandem are known to disrupt these interactions, thereby inhibiting Cb activity [22, 25, 27]. Additionally, the open conformation is thought to be more favorable for Cb activity, potentially due to the relative shift of the PH domain observed in this form (Fig. S1B).

Two variants, R287H and R290C (corresponding to R294H and R297C in rat CbIISH3+, and R234H and R237C in rat CbIISH3-), are located within the long helix connecting the DH and PH domains (Fig. S1B–D). These positively charged residues (R290 in humans and R297 in rats) interact with several residues in both the DH and PH domains, contributing to the maintenance of the open conformation (Fig. S1D). The relative movement of the PH domain associated with conformational changes is known to correlate with Cb activity [25]. Moreover, the flexibility of the DH/PH tandem, facilitated by the helical connection, is also linked to the enzymatic activity of Dbl family proteins [36]. Therefore, these variants (R287H and R290C) are likely to disrupt Cb activity by interfering with the formation and stabilization of the open conformation. Another mutation, V374F (I381F for rat CbIISH3+), resides on the β5 strand within the C-terminal β-sheet (β5-β7) of the PH domain (Fig. S1E). The PH domain in Cb, like other Dbl family proteins, comprises a β-sandwich fold formed by two antiparallel β-sheets and a C-terminal α-helix [25, 37]. The Cb activity relies on a functional PH domain capable of binding lipids. Notably, interaction with phosphoinositides by positively charged residues in the β3-β4 loop of the PH domain is crucial for its activation [28, 38]. Additionally, the β1-β2, β5-β6, and β6-β7 loops are known to participate in interactions with the inositol phosphate headgroup, contributing to the PH domain’s function in protein-membrane binding (Fig. S1E). While the C-terminal β-sheet of the PH domain in Cb does not directly interact with lipids, it could still influence activity through interactions with other proteins. This is supported by numerous structural studies demonstrating that both β-sheets within PH domains often serve as platforms for protein-protein interactions [39, 40]. Therefore, it is plausible that V374F, with its bulkier side chain, could disrupt interactions involving the C-terminal β-sheet, potentially affecting Cb activity (Fig. S1E). The G485S mutation was excluded from the structural analysis due to its absence in the available rat Cb crystal structures [25, 37].

To further investigate the impact of specific amino acid substitutions on the synaptic function of Cb, we generated mammalian expression vectors encoding ARHGEF9 variants identified in individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) using plasmids encoding full-length rat CbIISH3- and CbIISH3+ (rCbIISH3- and rCbIISH3+, respectively) as well as human CbIIISH3+ (hCbIII) (Fig. S2A). R290C and R287H mutations of hCbIII (equivalent to R294H and R297C in rCbIISH3+ and R234H and R237C in rCBIISH3-) are located in the linker between DH and PH domains, V374F of hCbIII (equivalent to I381F in rCbIISH3+ and I321F in rCbIISH3-) in the PH domain, and G485S only found in hCbIII (Fig. S2A). Moreover, two additional expression vectors for the NE232-233AA variant (disrupting Cdc42 activation) [28] and RR303-304NN variant (with defective binding to PI3P) were constructed [41]. The various Cb expression vectors (FLAG-tagged wild-type [WT] or indicated variants) were transfected into human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells and immunoblot analyses performed. Our results showed that total expression levels of all tested Cb variants were comparable to those of Cb WT (Fig. S2B and S2C).

To assess the functional effects of these mutations, we initially co-expressed FLAG-tagged hCbIII, FLAG-tagged rCbIISH3- or their corresponding point mutants, with EGFP-tagged gephyrin in COS-7 cells. As reported previously [27], EGFP-gephyrin consistently formed large intracellular aggregates upon expression in COS-7 cells, but was distributed into small, submembranous microclusters when co-expressed with rCbIISH3-. However, in the presence of the SH3 domain, intramolecular interactions between SH3 and DH/PH domains favored a closed conformation of Cb, preventing its binding to PI3P, which is a prerequisite step for Cb-mediated gephyrin microclustering [25]. In keeping with data from prior studies, EGFP-gephyrin accumulated as large intracellular aggregates in COS-7 cells co-transfected with hCbIIISH3+ (Fig. S3). Interestingly, under conditions of single expression, hCbIIISH3+ was evenly distributed in the cytoplasm of transfected COS-7 cells whereas co-expression of gephyrin and hCbIIISH3+ in COS-7 cells induced the formation of colocalized aggregates (Fig. S3). All CbIII point mutants colocalized with gephyrin as large intracellular aggregates, indicating that interactions between gephyrin and CbIII were not altered by NDD-associated Cb mutations (Fig. S3). CbIISH3- was able to recruit gephyrin under conditions of co-expression to the plasma membrane in the form of submembranous microclusters, as anticipated (Fig. S4). Notably, RR/NN failed to redistribute gephyrin to the plasma membrane, whereas the activity of NE/AA was similar to that of WT, suggesting that the PI3P binding activity of Cb, but not its GEF activity towards Cdc42, is crucial for gephyrin microclustering, in line with previous findings [28]. Among the NDD-associated Cb variants, only rCbIISH3- R237C (equivalent to R290C in hCbIII) failed to redirect gephyrin to the plasma membrane, indicating that gephyrin is distributed in a mixed form (both as large intracellular aggregates and submembranous microclusters) (Fig. S4). Nlgn2 is also reported to bind the Cb SH3 domain and relieve intramolecular interactions and consequently autoinhibition [42]. In our experiments, triple expression of hCbIIISH3+, Nlgn2 and EGFP-gephyrin in COS-7 cells robustly induced the formation of gephyrin microclusters (Fig. 1C–E). However, Nlgn2 failed to promote the redistribution of gephyrin into microclusters upon co-expression with RR/NN or hCbIII R290C (Fig. 1C–E).

To examine whether the NDD-associated Cb variants affect GEF activity, the Cdc42 activation assay was conducted. Cdc42 was expressed either alone or together with CbIISH3- or its indicated mutants in HEK293T cells. The amount of activated Cdc42 was determined by the extent of co-sedimentation using immobilized GTP-PAK1-PBD (an established effector that specifically interacts with GTP-bound GTPases) [41]. GTP-Cdc42 levels were not significantly different in cells co-expressing either CbIISH3- WT or NDD-associated CbII variants (Fig. S5A, B). Our results confirmed that the NE/AA mutant reduced GTP-Cdc42 levels (Fig. S5A, B).

Next, we investigated whether NDD-associated Cb variants influence PI3P binding activity determined based on the extent of co-sedimentation with immobilized PI3P beads. First, differences in PI3P binding activity among rCbIISH3-, rCbIISH3+ and hCbIII were examined. Expression of hCbIII was markedly lower than that of rCbIISH3- or rCbIISH3+ upon transfection into HEK293T cells (Fig. S5C). Moreover, the PI3P binding activity of rCbIISH3- was comparable to that of rCbIISH3+ (Fig. S5C). We further assessed the effect of hCbIII mutations on PI3P binding. All mutants, except G485S, exhibited comparable PI3P binding affinity to Cb WT (Fig. S5D, E). In contrast, the G485S variant did not bind to PI3P, similar to the RR/NN variant (Fig. S5D, E).

To corroborate these immunocytochemical results, we measured miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs) in cultured hippocampal neurons via whole-cell electrophysiological recordings (Fig. 2G, H). Our results showed a marked decrease in the frequency, but not amplitude, decay time or rise slope of mIPSCs in Cb-cKO neurons (Fig. 2G, H) with no alterations in mEPSCs (Fig. S6). Co-expression of hCbIII WT or hCbIII R287H reversed the decreased in mIPSC frequency whereas co-expression of R290C failed to rescue frequency (Fig. 2G, H). Notably, expression of V374F or G485S also failed to rescue the deficits in inhibitory synaptic transmission (Fig. 2G, H), despite robust gephyrin clustering activities similar to Cb WT (see Fig. 1). Our collective results suggest that the inhibitory synaptic phenotypes of NDD-associated Cb variants potentially reflect perturbation of diverse facets of Cb functions.

We further analyzed whether Cb-cKO mice exhibit the behavioral features manifested in patients with ASDs and/or ID. To this end, we performed a battery of behavioral tests, including measurement of locomotion, anxiety, social behavior, communications, and sensory motor gating (Figs. S9 and 3M–Q). mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice displayed comparable exploratory behavior as their control counterparts (assessed via the open-field test) (Fig. S9A, B), normal sociability and normal social novelty recognition memory (evaluated based on the three-chamber test), showing preference for the first novel mouse over an inanimate object (Fig. S9E, F) and a novel stranger over a familiar mouse (Fig. S9E, F). Moreover, mPFC Cb-cKO mice exhibited normal anxiety-like behavior in the elevated plus-maze test (Fig. S9C, D) and similar repetitive behavior (assessed using the marble burying test) as control mice (Fig. S9H).

As sensory gating deficits have been reported in patients with various neurodevelopmental disorders beyond schizophrenia [49, 50], attentive processing and prepulse inhibition (PPI) were further examined. This paradigm assesses sensorimotor gating by measuring whether a startle response induced by a loud acoustic stimulus is lowered by a preceding, less loud stimulus [49, 51, 52]. As expected, a low-intensity acoustic prepulse stimulus inhibited the startle response caused by a subsequent loud stimulus in control mice (Fig. 3M–O). Following exposure to acoustic prepulse of increasing intensities, control mice exhibited progressive inhibition of the startle response (Fig. 3M–O). In contrast, mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice displayed comparable startle responses across all intensities (Fig. 3M–O).

Adult male mice emit ultrasonic vocalizations (USV; 50–80 Hz) as a means for communication during social interactions and courtship [53, 54]. Therefore, we examined whether mPFC-specific deletion of Cb affects USVs. In our experiments, mPFC-specific male Cb-cKO (~10-week-old) mice showed severe impairment in the number of emitted USVs to similarly aged female mice (Fig. 3P, Q), indicating that Cb might modulate social communication functions by specifically regulating USVs.

Gephyrin exhibited a much greater downregulation in phosphorylation compared to changes at the total protein level (Fig. 4G). A detailed examination of phosphopeptides revealed changes in phosphorylation at nine residues in gephyrin: S188, S194, T198 (or T199 or S200), T265, S270, S280 (or T281), S283, T286, and S338 (Fig. S11). Changes varied from 0.15 to 1.4 according to site between control and Cb-cKO mice (Fig. 4H, I). Specifically, S283 phosphorylation levels decreased most, followed by S286 and S270 phosphorylation levels. In contrast, neither S188 nor S194 phosphorylation levels were markedly altered in Cb-cKO mice. Collectively, our results suggest that collybistin is required for maintenance of proper gephyrin phosphorylation at various residues in adult mPFC neurons.

To further determine whether reduced gephyrin phosphorylation affects gephyrin clustering in COS-7 cells, we generated two additional gephyrin point mutants in which the six gephyrin serine/threonine residues identified from proteomic analyses were substituted with either glutamate or aspartic acid to generate phosphor-mimetic mutant (PMM; T265D/S270E/T281E/S283D/T286E/S305D; Ser305 in rat corresponds to Ser338 in mouse), or with alanine to generate phosphorylation-defective mutant (PDM; T265A/S270A/T281A/S283A/T286A/S305A) (Fig. S13A, B). EGFP-tagged gephyrin PMM or gephyrin PDM formed large intracellular clusters in COS-7 cells, as seen for WT gephyrin (Fig. S13C, D). In addition, both EGFP-gephyrin PMM and EGFP-gephyrin PDM were distributed into submembranous microclusters when co-expressed with rCbIISH3- (Fig. S13C, D). These results suggest that gephyrin phosphorylation per se did not affect Cb-mediated gephyrin microclustering.

We subsequently investigated whether ASD-associated Cb variants impair physiological gephyrin phosphorylation cascades. AAVs expressing Cre, together with AAVs expressing rat CbII WT or ASD-linked Cb variants, were stereotactically injected into the mPFC of adult Cb-cKO mice, and an immunoblot analysis of mPFC lysates from mice infected with the indicated Cb variants was performed using mAb7a antibodies. Notably, expression of CbII WT restored gephyrin phosphorylation levels, whereas expression of R297C (equivalent to hCbIII R290C) failed to do so (Fig. 5C–E). In contrast, expression of R294H or I381F (equivalent to R287H or V374F in hCbIII) partially rescued gephyrin phosphorylation levels (Fig. 5C–E). Thus, our results suggest that these ASD-linked Cb mutations might impair the function of Cb in maintaining proper levels of gephyrin phosphorylation in mPFC neurons. To examine whether reduced gephyrin phosphorylation has a negative effect on inhibitory synaptic transmission, we performed electrophysiological analyses in adult mPFC neurons from mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice expressing gephyrin WT or gephyrin PDM. Interestingly, Cb deficiency-mediated impairment of GABAergic synaptic transmission was completely rescued by overexpression of gephyrin WT, but not by gephyrin PDM (Fig. 5F–H). These results indicate that phosphorylated gephyrin is required for proper maintenance of GABAergic synaptic transmission in mPFC. Cb is known to play a critical role in organizing GABAergic synapses, together with gephyrin and GABAA receptors, in the mammalian brain [23, 33, 59]. Clinically, ARHGEF9 encoding Cb located on the X-chromosome is strongly linked to XLID associated with epilepsy [41, 60–65]. However, the issue of whether Cb is also linked to ASD or IDD remained to be established. Data from the present study clearly indicate that Cb is involved in the mechanisms underlying ASD pathogenesis, based on extensive characterization of ARHGEF9 variants identified in individuals evaluated for ASD and/or IDD by genetic testing. Notably, three out of four ARHGEF9 variants inhibited distinct facets of Cb functions with presenting epilepsy. Given that ASD/IDD-related genes frequently affect the functions of the mPFC responsible for performing higher-order activities related to cognition and sociability [44, 66], we generated mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice to examine whether Cb deficiency in mPFC neurons could induce ASD-like behavioral abnormalities and electrophysiological impairments. Our results showed that mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice exhibit marked reduction of USV calls in line with clinical diagnostics indicating impaired social communicative ability of ASD patients with ARHGEF9 variants. In contrast, repetitive behavior and sociability of mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice were comparable to control mice, suggesting that Cb in mPFC neurons is engaged with selective aspects of social behaviors. Importantly, expression of pathogenic Cb variants failed to rescue the impairment of inhibitory synaptic functions observed in the mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice, suggesting that ASD-linked Cb mutations induce loss-of-function phenotypes.

Proteomics analyses clearly showed that mPFC-specific deletion of Cb induces a marked reduction in phosphorylation levels of gephyrin. Moreover, multiple gephyrin residues located within the unstructured linker region that were previously reported to be phosphorylated [33] were less phosphorylated upon Cb deletion. Notably, prior studies showed that glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β)-mediated gephyrin phosphorylation at Ser270 negatively regulates gephyrin clustering in hippocampal cultured neurons [55], and phosphorylation of gephyrin at Ser270 tends to decrease during developmental stages in the mouse hippocampus [58]. These results imply that gephyrin phosphorylation is detrimental to normal GABAergic synapse development. However, they are at odds with a subsequent report that Cb knockdown in hippocampal cultured neurons reduces cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK)-mediated gephyrin phosphorylation at Ser270, as shown using gephyrin mAb7a antibodies that also detect gephyrin Thr276 phosphorylation [56]. Notably, gephyrin phosphorylation at Thr276 and Ser277 is critical for gephyrin cluster formation and oligomerization [33], and gephyrin phosphorylation at Ser305 is important for activity-dependent GABAergic synapse formation [67]. Thus, our results are in alignment with the idea that Cb is required for maintaining proper levels of gephyrin phosphorylation at various serine and/or threonine residues, including Ser270 and Thr276. It is also possible that Cb-mediated gephyrin phosphorylation at Thr276 and other residues (e.g., Ser188), but not at Ser270, acts as a signaling element that facilitates GABAergic synapse development in adult mPFC neurons. In support of this speculation, AAV-mediated introduction of pathogenic Cb variants failed to rescue the reduced gephyrin phosphorylation levels in adult mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice. Among the six identified phosphorylated residues (Thr265, Ser270, Thr281, Ser283, Thr286, and Ser338), Ser270 and Ser338 were previously identified and investigated with respect to functional significance. Our data provide compelling clues, hinting that phosphorylation of the newly identified sites in Cb is likely to be critical for promoting GABAergic synapse development, as is the case for several other previously identified sites. Cb-induced gephyrin phosphorylation in the C-domain likely manifests during mouse brain development in both the hippocampus and mPFC, given that increases in gephyrin phosphorylation matched the increase in total Cb level. Our results are apparently at odds with a recent study that reported that gephyrin phosphorylation at Ser270 in the mouse hippocampus decreases during development. Notably, we used the antibody, mAb7a, which recognizes gephyrin protein species that are phosphorylated at several residues, whereas the prior study used anti-phospho-Ser270 antibodies. Although further clarification is required, it is tempting to speculate that Cb-mediated gephyrin phosphorylation at its C-domain (except Ser270) is required for proper GABAergic synapse development. Because gephyrin is phosphorylated at three residues (Ser188, Ser194 and Ser200) by Pin1, a peptidyl-prolyl isomerase [68], it is plausible that Cb indirectly regulates Pin1-mediated synaptic functions, including negative modulation of Nlgn2–gephyrin interactions [69] and regulation of synaptic NMDA receptors [70]. In support of this interpretation, we found that GluN2A levels were reduced in mPFC-specific Cb-cKO mice (see Table S2).

The current study raises several questions. The first is whether and how Cb-mediated regulation of gephyrin phosphorylation and clustering dictates proper ultrasonic vocalization in mice. Altered phosphorylation of gephyrin induced by Cb deletion may trigger the conformational changes of gephyrin, possibly leading to altered subsynaptic localization of gephyrin [71, 72]. This helps our understanding how Cb-mediated synaptic inhibition causes ASD pathogenesis. Given the recent studies showing that PL neurons modulate ultrasonic vocalization in mice [44, 73, 74], future experiments are warranted to confirm the involvement of Cb in regulating synaptic mechanisms of specific neural circuits connected with PL neurons. Another important avenue for future investigation would be to elucidate how Cb broadly modulates the network of gephyrin phosphorylation cascades across diverse brain areas during different developmental stages. While more work is required, the current study provides a framework for understanding how Cb, gephyrin, and related components shape GABAergic synapse signaling properties, and how dysregulation of the Cb–gephyrin functional axis contributes to a subset of brain disorders, including ASDs and NDs.

Injected mice were allowed to recover for at least 4 weeks following surgery. Male mice (9 week-old) were used for behavioral tests, which were conducted in a soundproofed room with dim lighting (< 5 lux). Tests were performed in the following order: open-field, elevated plus-maze, three-chamber sociability, marble burying, and prepulse inhibition (PPI). After placing individual male mice in single housing for 3 days, the ultrasonic vocalization (USV) test was performed.

Mice were placed into a white acrylic open-field box (40 × 40 × 40 cm) and allowed to explore freely for 30 min. The total distance moved, time spent in the center zone of the field, and number of the center zone entries were recorded using a top-view camera. Results were analyzed using EthoVision XT 15 (Noldus) behavioral tracking software.

Standard housing cages were filled with wood chip bedding to a depth of 5 cm. Mice were introduced to the cages without marbles for a 30 min habituation period. After habituation, mice were removed from the cages and 15 marbles placed on the surface of the bedding in an arrangement of five rows of three (3 × 5). Mice were replaced in the cages and allowed to explore for 30 min. After removal of mice, the number of marbles that were buried approximately 3/4 deep within the bedding were counted.

A “+”–shaped acrylic maze with a white wall and floor, two open arms (30 × 5 × 0.5 cm), and two closed arms (30 × 5 × 30 cm) positioned at a height of 75 cm from the floor was used. Mice were placed in the center of the maze and allowed to move for 5 min. Tests were recorded using a top-view infrared camera and the total distance moved, number of open arms, and cumulative duration of time at the open arms analyzed using EthoVision XT 15 (Noldus).

A white acrylic box divided into three chambers (20 × 40 × 22 cm each) with small openings on the dividing walls was used. Both side chambers contained wire cups in the corners that were used to enclose the social conspecifics. Experimental mice were placed in the middle chamber and allowed to freely explore for 10 min. After the habituation session, an age-matched social conspecific was introduced into the wire cup on the left side-chamber and the sociability of subjects assessed by measuring their exploration times of the enclosed conspecific and the empty cup during the second 10-min period. An exploration was counted in cases where the subject mice directed their nose towards the wire cups. In the final 10 min, a new social conspecific was introduced into the empty wire cup on the right-side chamber and social recognition assessed by measuring exploration times by subjects of the familiar and novel conspecifics in the wire cup.

SR-Lab startle response systems (San Diego Instruments) were used to measure the startle responses of mice upon exposure to serial acoustic pulses. The sound level and the vibration sensing of each system were calibrated before every session. Mice were gently introduced into acrylic enclosures and allowed to acclimate to the background white noise set at 65 dB for 5 min. Subsequently, mice underwent 4 blocks of trials (consisting of 10 trials each) with pulse presentations ranging from 75 to 120 dB in a semi-random order (ASR). The next day, mice were further subjected to 12 blocks of trials consisting of 5 types: 122 dB pulse alone and paired with weaker prepulse stimuli (73, 76, 79 or 82 dB; PPI). The maximum startle response amplitude under each trial type was averaged for data analysis. The extent of prepulse inhibition was calculated as follows. PPI% = [(average startle amplitude on pulse alone trials) - (average startle amplitude on prepulse-paired pulse trials)]/(average startle amplitude on pulse alone trials) × 100.

To promote ultrasonic vocalizations (USV), male mice were subjected to pre-conditioning steps, which involved exposure to fertile female mice overnight, followed by single housing in individual cages for 3 days. On the test day, mice were allowed to acclimate to the soundproof recording chamber with red lighting for 5 min. Subsequently, anesthetized 6-week-old female mice were introduced into the chamber to elicit USV calls. The vaginal estrous condition of each stimulus female was assessed through visual inspection. Mouse vocalizations were recorded using Avisoft Recorder software and analyzed with Avisoft SASLab Pro (Avisoft Bioacoustics).

All data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The normality of data distributions was evaluated using the D’Agostino & Pearson test. Data were statistically assessed using a Mann-Whitney U test, student’s t-test, and ordinary one/two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Tukey’s test, or Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software) was used to analyze data and prepare bar graphs. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. No formal statistical power calculations were performed. For animal experiments, sample sizes were determined based on previous studies using similar protocols and ethical considerations to minimize animal use. For cell culture and acute slice electrophysiology experiments, the number of recorded neurons/cells was based on established practice in the field and prior experience with assay variability. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were pre-established: animals were excluded if viral expression was not detected, and outliers were excluded when identified by statistical outlier tests performed in Prism 8.0. Animals were allocated to experimental groups in a pseudo-random manner, ensuring that littermates or cage-mates were distributed across groups. For slice and cell-based experiments, samples were processed in random order to avoid batch effects. Investigators were blinded to group allocation during both the experiments and the subsequent outcome assessments, including electrophysiological recordings and imaging quantification.
