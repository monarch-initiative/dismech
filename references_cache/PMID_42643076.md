# Vestibular Migraine: A Systematic Literature Search and Narrative Review of Diagnostic Criteria, Pathophysiology, Differential Diagnosis, and Treatment Approaches.
**Authors:** Demir ÜF, Karakus Dilbaz F
**Journal:** Medeni Med J (2026)
**DOI:** [10.4274/MMJ.galenos.2026.94020](https://doi.org/10.4274/MMJ.galenos.2026.94020)

## Content

This review aimed to summarize current evidence on the diagnostic criteria, pathophysiological mechanisms, differential diagnosis, and treatment approaches of vestibular migraine. A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for studies published between May 2021 and May 2026. The search focused on vestibular migraine, migrainous vertigo, migraine-associated vertigo, diagnostic criteria, pathophysiology, treatment, prophylaxis, rehabilitation, and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-related therapies. Study selection followed a PRISMA-informed process, and findings were synthesized narratively because of heterogeneity in study design, populations, interventions, and outcome measures. A total of 663 records were identified, and 72 studies were included in the final synthesis. Current evidence supports vestibular migraine as a complex disorder involving trigeminovascular activation, CGRP signaling, central sensitization, vestibulo-thalamo-cortical networks, and cerebellar compensation. Diagnosis remains primarily clinical and requires careful assessment of attack duration, vestibular symptom type, migraine-associated features, and exclusion of alternative vestibular or vascular disorders. Treatment evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. Lifestyle modification, conventional migraine prophylaxis, vestibular rehabilitation, and CGRP-targeted therapies may be useful in selected patients, but robust vestibular migraine-specific randomized trials are still insufficient. Vestibular migraine should be approached as a multisystem neurovestibular disorder rather than a simple coexistence of migraine and vertigo. Individualized, phenotype-based management and stronger controlled studies are needed to improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic decision-making.

INTRODUCTION
The observation that vestibular symptoms, 
particularly vertigo and head-motion-induced dizziness 
with nausea, may accompany migraine attacks dates 
back several decades. The relationship between migraine 
and vestibular symptoms has historically been described 
using various terms, such as “migraine-associated 
vertigo,” “migraine-related vertigo,” “migraine-related 
dizziness,” “migraine-related vestibulopathy,” and 
“benign recurrent vertigo.” This terminological confusion 
became more standardized with the joint diagnostic 
approach developed in the 2010s by the International 
Headache Society (IHS) and the B árány Society1,2.
Vestibular migraine (VM) is recognized as one of the 
most important causes of recurrent episodic vestibular 
symptoms. Its clinical significance stems not only 
from its prevalence; diagnostic delay, loss of quality of 
life, functional limitations, anxiety, and its association 
with chronic dizziness syndromes transform VM into a 
critical intersection area for neurology, otolaryngology, 
emergency medicine, and primary care practice 3-6. 
Nevertheless, VM remains underdiagnosed relative to 
its actual prevalence, and patients are often managed 
under alternative diagnoses such as benign paroxysmal 
positional vertigo (BPPV), M énière’s disease, non-specific 
dizziness, or anxiety disorders.
This review combines a systematic literature 
search with narrative synthesis to provide a clinically 
oriented overview of the current evidence on VM. The 
primary objective is to comprehensively present the 
epidemiology, diagnostic criteria, pathophysiology, 
differential diagnosis, and treatment approaches of 
VM while transparently distinguishing higher-certainty 
clinical evidence from observational, case-level, and 
mechanistic evidence.
Objectives and Research Questions
This narrative review seeks to answer three main 
research questions: 
1) What are the current criteria used for diagnosing 
VM, and how should their clinical value be interpreted in 
differential diagnosis? 
2) What are the proposed pathophysiological 
mechanisms for VM? 
3) What are the efficacy and safety profiles of 
acute treatment, prophylactic treatment, vestibular 
rehabilitation, and new treatment approaches for VM?
MATERIALS and METHODS
This review was conducted using a systematic literature 
search and PRISMA-informed study selection process 7, 
followed by narrative synthesis. A meta-analysis was not 
performed because of substantial heterogeneity in study 
design, diagnostic criteria, populations, interventions, 
follow-up periods, and outcome measures. Therefore, 
the results are presented as a narrative synthesis, with 
explicit attention to the level and certainty of evidence.
Data Sources and Search Strategy
The literature search was conducted in PubMed/
MEDLINE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The 
primary disease block consisted of the terms “vestibular 
migraine,” “migrainous vertigo,” and “migraine-associated 
vertigo.” The secondary topic block included diagnosis, 
diagnostic criteria, pathophysiology, treatment, therapy, 
management, prophylaxis, rehabilitation, calcitonin 
gene-related peptide (CGRP), and “calcitonin gene-
related peptide.” Searches were adapted to the syntax and 
field-tag requirements of each database. The literature 
was searched from May 2021 to May 2026. The database 
selection was designed to capture the major peer-
reviewed biomedical, multidisciplinary, and evidence-
synthesis literature relevant to VM. A comprehensive 
search strategy was employed, incorporating PubMed/
MEDLINE to identify biomedical and clinical studies, Web 
of Science to expand multidisciplinary citation coverage, 
and the Cochrane Library to capture randomized trials 
and systematic reviews. In addition, we performed a 
supplementary targeted search of ClinicalTrials.gov and 
World Health Organization International Clinical Trials 
Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP) on 27/06/2026 using 
the terms “vestibular migraine”, “migrainous vertigo”, 
“migraine-associated vertigo”. The findings were used 
to contextualize ongoing or unpublished therapeutic 
research, but they were not included in the main evidence 
synthesis because this review was designed as a narrative 
synthesis. The findings are presented in Supplementary 
File 1. We do not have institutional access to Embase. 
Consequently, this review may therefore overlook 
unpublished studies, ongoing trials, conference-only 
reports, negative studies, and regulatory/registry-based 
evidence. This limitation is of particular pertinence for 
emerging therapeutic areas, including CGRP-targeted 
therapies, gepants/ditans, neuromodulation, and other 
investigational interventions.
An example PubMed query was structured as follows: 
[“vestibular migraine” (Title/Abstract) OR “migrainous 
vertigo” (Title/Abstract) OR “migraine-associated vertigo” 
(Title/Abstract)] AND (diagnosis OR pathophysiology OR 

 
treatment OR therapy OR management OR prophylaxis 
OR rehabilitation OR CGRP OR “calcitonin gene-related 
peptide”). Field tags and filters were adapted to the 
syntactic features of the databases.
The relatively modest number of PubMed/MEDLINE 
records (n=32) reflects the intentionally restrictive 
title/abstract-based disease block, the recent search 
window (May 2021 to May 2026), and the prioritization 
of VM-specific, clinically interpretable records rather 
than broadly indexed, non-specific dizziness or vertigo 
records. Broader multidisciplinary coverage was 
provided by Web of Science, while Cochrane sources 
were searched to identify randomized trials and 
evidence syntheses.
Study selection was conducted based on the PRISMA 
2020 flowchart. A total of 663 records were identified 
based on recent database exports. Of these records, 
531 were from Web of Science, 88 were from Cochrane 
CENTRAL/Cochrane Library study records, 32 were from 
PubMed/MEDLINE, and 12 were from Cochrane Reviews 
exports.
Duplicate checks were performed based on matching 
DOI, PMID, study registration numbers, titles, and author 
information. As a result of this process, 47 duplicate 
records were removed. After removing duplicates, 616 
unique records underwent title and abstract screening. 
Following title and abstract screening, 516 records were 
excluded, and 100 reports were selected for full-text 
evaluation. After full-text evaluation, 72 studies were 
included in the final synthesis. The PRISMA 2020 flow 
diagram is summarized in Figure 1.
No prospective protocol registration was performed 
for this review. This limits transparency regarding 
prespecified methods and is acknowledged as a 
methodological limitation.
Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion criteria: human studies; clinical data related 
to VM or suspected VM; studies using the International 
Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD)-3, B árány 
Society, or similar diagnostic frameworks; studies 
contributing to the pathophysiology, differential 
diagnosis, vestibular testing, or treatment outcomes of 
VM; randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, 
meta-analyses, observational studies, diagnostic criteria/
consensus documents, and clinically informative case 
series.
Exclusion criteria: animal or in vitro  studies; general 
studies of non-specific dizziness, lightheadedness, or 
vertigo lacking VM-specific analysis; studies published 
solely as conference abstracts; records with unverifiable 
full bibliographic information; publications that do not 
provide clinically interpretable data regarding treatment 
or diagnosis.

Evidence Synthesis
To improve transparency in interpretation, treatment 
evidence was summarized using a GRADE-informed 
certainty framework. Because this was a narrative 
synthesis rather than a formal guideline or meta-analysis, 
formal outcome-level GRADE ratings were not assigned. 
Instead, the certainty of evidence was categorized 
narratively as follows: randomized controlled trials 
and high-quality systematic reviews were considered 
higher-certainty evidence; consistent prospective or 
comparative observational studies were considered 
moderate- to low-certainty evidence, depending on 
design limitations; retrospective series and uncontrolled 
cohorts were considered low-certainty evidence; and 
case reports, small case series, and preclinical studies 
were considered hypothesis-generating evidence. 
Treatment conclusions were then phrased according to 
this hierarchy. The selected characteristics of key studies 
are presented in Table 2.
Accordingly, the term “GRADE-informed” is used only 
to indicate that interpretation was guided by GRADE 
principles such as study design, consistency, directness, 
precision, and certainty; it should not be read as a formal 
GRADE assessment, a rating table, or a guideline-level 
recommendation.
Epidemiology and Clinical Burden
VM is one of the most prominent clinical phenotypes 
reflecting the relationship between migraine and the 
vestibular system. Although different studies have 
reported varying prevalence rates in the general 
population, VM is recognized as one of the primary 
causes of recurrent episodic vertigo3-6,8 . 
Its higher prevalence in women suggests a possible 
contribution of hormonal factors. VM can occur 
from childhood through old age; however, it is more 
pronounced in adult women and individuals with a 
history of migraine.
Although broad complaints, such as dizziness or 
lightheadedness, may be reported more frequently by 
patients with migraine than in the general population, 
these non-specific symptoms do not, by themselves, 
establish VM. Diagnosis should instead focus on 
qualifying vestibular symptoms, including spontaneous 
vertigo, positional vertigo, visually induced vertigo, 
head-motion-induced vertigo, or head-motion-induced 
dizziness with nausea1-5. 
However, this association does not always imply VM; 
diagnosis should therefore be based on the duration of 
episodes, the type of vestibular symptoms, the migraine-
associated symptoms, and the exclusion of alternative 
vestibular disorders.
The clinical burden is not limited to attack frequency. 
Recurrent vestibular symptoms, fear of falling, avoidance 
of movement, visual motion sensitivity, anxiety, depressive 
symptoms, and sleep disturbances can significantly 
reduce functional capacity. There may be a bidirectional 
relationship between VM and persistent postural-
perceptual dizziness (PPPD); recurrent vestibular attacks 
may trigger a cycle of chronic dizziness, avoidance, and 
hypervigilance.
Both the prevalence of VM and the associated 
demographic patterns vary by study design; however, 
population surveys and systematic reviews agree that the 
prevalence is a low single-digit percentage and that there 
is a marked predominance among women. Clinically-
based series report much higher rates due to referral bias.

Basic demographic characteristics:
- Gender: VM predominantly affects women; tertiary 
care clinic data report a female-to-male ratio of ~3.3:1, 
and women constitute the majority in most trial and 
cohort samples12,14,15 .
- Age groups: VM is most commonly diagnosed in 
young and middle-aged adults; some clinical series 
show higher rates in younger patients with prevalence 
decreasing with age, though reports vary and secondary 
peaks have been identified in older adults; this reflects 
heterogeneity between the general population, clinical 
samples, and diagnostic criteria9,12,14 .
- Ethnic origin and familial patterns: systematic 
review data indicate prevalence differences by ethnic 

 
origin (reported pooled estimates: Black ~3.13%, White 
~2.64%, Asian ~1.07%) and evidence of familial clustering 
(recurrence rates in siblings indicating increased familial 
risk), suggesting the presence of both genetic and 
environmental influences10.
- Limitations and heterogeneity: reported prevalence 
varies significantly depending on the study setting 
(differences between population-based studies and 
specialty clinics) and the diagnostic criteria used, which 
contributes to inconsistent associations with age and sex 
in some series; these methodological differences account 
for a large portion of the inconsistencies observed across 
studies9,10,14 .

In this review, terminology follows the Bárány Society/IHS 
framework. The umbrella term “vestibular symptoms” is used 
to refer collectively to the qualifying symptoms of VM. “Vertigo” 
refers to a false sensation of self-motion or environmental 
motion, and includes spontaneous, positional, visually- 
induced, or head-motion-induced vertigo. “Dizziness” is used, 
as specified in the diagnostic criteria, only when referring to 
non-vertiginous spatial disorientation—particularly head-
motion-induced dizziness with nausea—or when reporting 
study-specific outcome measures. “Lightheadedness” is not 
used as a defining VM symptom and is reserved for non-
specific or non-vestibular complaints.
• Spontaneous or positional vertigo and head-motion-
induced dizziness with nausea are typical qualifying 
vestibular symptoms and may occur alongside migraine-
type headache, photophobia, phonophobia, and 
nausea16,17 . 
• Patients with sensory hypersensitivity typically 
report motion intolerance and auditory hypersensitivity, 
such as phonophobia or tinnitus17,18 .

Phenotypic variability in symptoms may be categorized 
into motion-sensitive, positional vertigo-dominant, 
auditory-dominant, or mixed types, corresponding 
to variable involvement of the vestibular, visual, and 
auditory pathways 17. In VM, vestibular symptoms may 
present as spontaneous vertigo, positional vertigo, 
visually induced vertigo, head-motion-induced vertigo, 
head-motion-induced dizziness with nausea, or internal/
external vertigo. Various types of vertigo may be observed 
during separate attacks in the same patient. The duration 
of symptoms typically ranges from minutes to hours; 
in some patients, symptoms may last for several days. 
In terms of diagnostic criteria, vestibular symptoms are 
expected to be moderate or severe in intensity and to 
last between 5 minutes and 72 hours1,2.
In some patients, the classic migraine phenotype 
predominates during the early years of the disease; 
over time, vestibular symptoms may be added to the 
clinical picture. In some patients, however, as headache 
symptoms diminish, vestibular symptoms come to

Diagnostic Approach
No specific biomarker or standalone diagnostic 
vestibular test exists for VM. Diagnosis is clinical and 
relies on a well-structured history, neurological/
otological examination, detailed assessment of attack 
phenomenology, and exclusion of differential diagnoses. 
Neurological and otological examinations may be 
normal during the interictal period. The following 
points should be systematically addressed during the 
diagnostic interview: duration and frequency of attacks; 
the type of qualifying vestibular symptom, including 
spontaneous vertigo, positional vertigo, visually induced 
vertigo, head-motion-induced vertigo, or head-motion-
induced dizziness with nausea; timing and characteristics 
of headaches; photophobia/phonophobia/aura; nausea/
vomiting; the laterality and persistence of hearing 
symptoms; history of migraine; family history; triggers; 
anxiety/avoidance behavior; medication use; and vascular 
risk factors. Vestibular tests should be used primarily 
for differential diagnosis and phenotyping rather than 
for establishing a definitive diagnosis. Abnormalities 
may be detected in tests such as caloric testing, video 
head impulse test (vHIT), vestibular evoked myogenic 
potentials (VEMP), and posturography; however, 
these findings are not specific to VM. Vestibular and 
audiological assessments should therefore be interpreted 
as supportive and differential diagnostic tools rather 
than VM-specific biomarkers. VEMP testing may provide 
additional supportive information in selected patients, 
particularly when an overlapping vestibular disorder is 
suspected; however, abnormal findings are not specific 
to VM and should be interpreted within the broader 
clinical context. Pure-tone audiometry is particularly 
important when Ménière’s disease is considered, because 
fluctuating or progressive unilateral sensorineural 
hearing loss should prompt reconsideration of the 
diagnosis. Vestibular testing and audiological evaluation 
are most useful when the clinical phenotype is atypical,

Differential Diagnosis
BPPV is one of the conditions most commonly 
confused with VM. In BPPV, short-lived typical positional 
vertigo triggered by head movements and positive Dix-
Hallpike or roll tests are expected. While positional 
symptoms may be present in VM, attack duration may be 
more variable, migraine-like symptoms may occur, and 
positional tests may not show the typical BPPV pattern. 
Ménière’s disease can be particularly difficult to distinguish 
from VM in the early stages. Tinnitus and a sensation of 
fullness in the ear may be present in both conditions. In 
Ménière’s disease, fluctuating, mostly unilateral hearing 
loss and audiological progression are characteristic 
features. In VM, hearing complaints are generally bilateral 
or non-specific; persistent, progressive unilateral hearing 
loss should prompt reconsideration of the VM diagnosis. 
PPPD may occur after VM or concurrently with VM. In 
PPPD, chronic dizziness and persistent symptoms that 
worsen with standing, movement, and complex visual 
stimuli predominate. Identifying the episodic attack 
pattern in VM is important; however, the two conditions 
are not mutually exclusive. Transient ischemic attacks and 
strokes involving the posterior circulation should be ruled 
out, particularly in patients with advanced age, vascular 
risk factors, new-onset neurological findings, diplopia, 
dysarthria, ataxia, hemiparesis, new, severe headache, 
or persistent neurological deficits. In these cases, rather 
than proceeding with symptomatic treatment based on 
a provisional diagnosis of VM, an urgent neurological 
evaluation is required 3,4,23,25 . The differential diagnosis of

Pathophysiology
The pathophysiology of VM has not been fully 
elucidated; current evidence suggests that a multilevel 
model—where migraine biology intersects with vestibular 
networks—is more likely than a single mechanism. 
This model includes the trigeminovascular system, 
brainstem nuclei, vestibular nuclei, thalamus, temporo-
parieto-insular vestibular cortex, cerebellum, and visual-
vestibular sensory integration networks24,26-29 .
CGRP, expressed in trigeminal ganglion neurons, has 
been linked to the pathophysiology of migraine, and 
agents targeting this peptide or its receptors have been 
developed as treatments for migraine. CGRP receptors 
expressed in the peripheral and central vestibular 
systems may also be involved in the pathophysiology 
of VM 30,31 . During a migraine attack, trigeminovascular 
activation and CGRP release may affect not only 
pain pathways but also structures anatomically and 
functionally linked to the vestibular system. Trigeminal 
innervation and CGRP expression in inner ear structures 
are interpreted as a biological bridge between migraine 
biology and otologic/vestibular symptoms 30-32 . The 
ability of CGRP-targeted therapies to improve VM 
symptoms indirectly supports the clinical significance 
of these pathways31,33,34 .

Central Multisensory Dysfunction
Evidence suggests that attacks are primarily caused 
by abnormal central processing and integration of 
vestibular, visual, and nociceptive inputs, rather than 
by entirely peripheral labyrinthine insufficiency. Studies 
have identified impairments in canal-otolith integration, 
alterations in vestibulo-ocular responses, and dysfunction 
in the vestibulo-thalamocortical and cerebellar networks 
that process multisensory vestibular signals.
• Patients with abnormal integration exhibit reduced 
motion detection thresholds and delayed formation of tilt 
perceptions during combined canal-otolith stimulation; 
this likely implies central synthesis defects of cerebellar 
origin37. 
• Vestibulo-thalamocortical dysfunction: functional 
imaging and physiological data support a modulation 
disorder in multimodal cortical networks that normally 
balance vestibular with visual/auditory cues, a condition 
that causes motion intolerance and vertigo17,18 . 
• The brainstem and cerebellar nuclei, vestibular 
nuclei, and vestibulocerebellum have repeatedly been 
identified as sites where vestibular and nociceptive 
(trigeminal) signals interact; this interaction may trigger 
vestibular symptoms in migraine episodes18,37 .
Trigemino-Vestibular Pathway and Sensitivity
Trigeminal nociceptive pathways interact with 
vestibular nuclei, which generate central sensitivity 
during attacks. Experimental models demonstrate both 
glutamatergic projections from the caudal trigeminal 
nucleus to the vestibular nuclei and increased neuronal 
activation in vestibular centers following migraine 
provocation.
• The trigemino-vestibular projection is glutamatergic, 
originates in the caudal trigeminal nucleus, and projects 
to the vestibular nuclei, where it mediates vestibular 
dysfunction in chronic nitroglycerin migraine models; 
silencing this projection reduces vestibular dysfunction38. 
• Vestibular nucleus sensitivity: in chronic migraine 
models, increased expression of the immediate-early 
gene (c-fos) and neuronal activation are observed in the 
vestibular nuclei, accompanying vestibular behavioral 
abnormalities39. 
• Synaptic plasticity: following chronic migraine 
paradigms, abnormal synaptic protein expression and 
altered synaptic morphology have been reported in the 
vestibular nucleus, consistent with central sensitization 
processes40.

Treatment
The evidence base for VM treatment remains 
heterogeneous and generally limited. Therefore, 
treatment recommendations should be interpreted 
according to the certainty of the evidence. Higher-
certainty evidence is available only for a limited number 
of VM-specific interventions evaluated in randomized 
trials or systematic reviews. Observational studies may 
support real-world feasibility and signals of benefit, 
but they cannot establish definitive treatment effects. 
Case reports, small case series, and preclinical studies 
are useful for biological plausibility and hypothesis 
generation, but should not be used as the basis for strong 
clinical recommendations. 
Lifestyle and Trigger Management
Sleep patterns, regular meals, hydration, caffeine/
alcohol management, stress management, and 
recognition of personal triggers are fundamental 
components of migraine management. Patients should 
be informed that vestibular symptoms in VM may 
decrease in parallel with the headache burden. Lifestyle 
modifications are particularly important for patients

Acute Attack Treatment
In acute attacks, short-acting antiemetics and 
vestibular suppressants may be used for short-term 
symptom control; however, prolonged use of vestibular 
suppressants is not recommended because it may 
interfere with central compensation. Evidence specific 
to acute VM treatment remains limited. Triptans have 
been used in clinical practice, but randomized VM-
specific evidence is of low or very low certainty and 
does not establish a consistent, clinically meaningful 
benefit. Other acute options, including 5-HT1F agonists, 
gepants, and neuromodulation, should be considered 
investigational or individualized until supported by 
adequately powered VM-specific trials42,43.
Overall, evidence for acute VM-specific 
pharmacological treatment remains of low to very 
low certainty. Short-term antiemetics and vestibular 
suppressants may be used symptomatically in selected 
patients, but this practice is supported mainly by 
clinical experience and by extrapolation from vertigo 
and migraine management, rather than by robust VM-
specific randomized evidence. Triptans have limited 
and inconsistent support from VM-specific trials, and 
newer acute agents, such as gepants and ditans, as well 
as neuromodulation approaches, should be considered 
investigational until adequately powered VM-specific 
randomized trials are available. 
Acute treatment aims to halt the migraine process 
and reduce vestibular symptoms (e.g., nausea and motion 
intolerance); evidence regarding specific emergency 
medications for VM is insufficient and largely derived 
from studies of migraine headaches. Randomized data 
are limited to small-scale trials involving triptans; other 
commonly used agents are supported by observational 
data and expert consensus rather than by high-quality 
randomized controlled trials.
• Triptans—two randomized studies tested 10 mg 
rizatriptan and 2.5 mg zolmitriptan for acute VM attacks; 
the combined evidence was assessed as being of very 
low certainty and showed little or no significant benefit 
compared to placebo at 2 hours15. 
• Antiemetics and vestibular suppressants—
agents such as dimenhydrinate, promethazine, and 
benzodiazepines are frequently used symptomatically to 
control nausea and reduce severe vestibular symptoms 
during attacks, but evidence from controlled trials is 
insufficient30,44 . 
• Antihistamines—vestibular sedatives/antihistamines 
are recommended as symptomatic treatment options in 
reviews and clinical practice guidelines30,45 . 
• Corticosteroids—they have been reported as a 
potential acute option in some practice guidelines, but 
evidence specific to VM is limited46. 
• Practice guidelines—current practice guidelines 
recommend the use of symptomatic vestibular 
medications alongside migraine-specific abortive 
treatment (when appropriate) and the personalization 
of treatment selection based on comorbidities and 
contraindications; however, recommendations at the 
official guideline level are limited due to low-quality 
evidence44,46 .

Prophylactic Pharmacological Treatment
Prophylaxis should be considered in cases of 
frequent, prolonged, or severe attacks that significantly 
impair quality of life. Agents used in traditional 
migraine prophylaxis—such as beta-blockers, tricyclic 
antidepressants, topiramate, valproate, and flunarizine—
are also used in VM. Cochrane reviews and network 
meta-analyses indicate that, due to limitations in the 
number and quality of studies, it is difficult to definitively 
identify a first-line agent; however, selected agents may 
be beneficial in reducing the frequency of vestibular 
symptoms and disability. Treatment selection should 
be based on comorbidities: tricyclic antidepressants for 
insomnia or anxiety; beta-blockers for hypertension or 
tachycardia; and topiramate may be considered for obesity 
and high migraine frequency. Pregnancy, childhood, old 
age, cardiac conduction disorders, depression, a history 
of kidney stones, and cognitive side effects should be 
considered when selecting medications52-56 .
CGRP-Targeted Therapies 
CGRP-targeted therapies represent a biologically 
plausible and clinically promising approach for selected 
patients with frequent or refractory VM; however, the 
certainty of the evidence differs substantially by study 
type. The strongest direct evidence currently comes 
from limited VM-specific randomized clinical data. 
Prospective and retrospective observational studies 
provide supportive but lower-certainty evidence because 
of selection bias, lack of randomization, placebo effects, 
and heterogeneous outcome definitions. Case reports 
should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only. 
Preclinical studies support the biological plausibility of 
CGRP involvement in vestibular pathways, but do not 
establish clinical efficacy in human VM. 
CGRP-targeted monoclonal antibodies represent 
a promising mechanism-based approach in VM, 
particularly for frequent or refractory phenotypes. 
A placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial with 
galcanezumab reported improvement in VM-specific 
disability and dizziness-day measures33, and systematic 
reviews and real-world data suggest potential 
benefit31,34 . However, the overall evidence base remains 
limited by the number of VM-specific randomized 
trials, follow-up duration, cost considerations, and 
uncertainty regarding patient-selection criteria and 
response markers.
CGRP is expressed in the trigeminal and vestibular 
pathways and contributes to VM by increasing excitability 
and sensitivity, both centrally (vestibular nuclei and 
trigeminal pathways) and peripherally (inner ear efferent 
synapses). Blocking CGRP signaling alleviates these 
changes in preclinical models and reduces vestibular 
symptoms in clinical cohorts.
• Anatomical expression: CGRP is found in the 
vestibular cerebellum, vestibular nuclei, and cochlear/
vestibular efferent fibers, providing the anatomical 
substrates for direct CGRP effects on vestibular function57. 
• Central sensitization: following repeated migraine 
triggers, CGRP levels and CGRP receptor components 
(calcitonin receptor-like receptor and receptor activity-
modifying protein 1) are upregulated in the vestibular 
nuclei, and in animal models, local CGRP inhibition or 
CGRP receptor antagonism reduces c-fos activation and 
vestibular dysfunction39,40 . 
• Intracellular signaling: CGRP1 receptor activity in 
the vestibular nucleus activates the PKC/ERK/CREB

DISCUSSION
This PRISMA-informed systematic literature search 
with narrative synthesis demonstrates that VM is a 
clinically common yet diagnostically challenging 
neurovestibular syndrome3-6. Although current diagnostic 
criteria provide a standardized framework, diagnosis still 
depends on careful clinical history, neurological and 
otological examination, and exclusion of alternative or 
coexisting vestibular disorders 1,2,19 . This dependence on 
clinical interpretation creates a risk of both overdiagnosis 
and underdiagnosis, particularly in patients with atypical 
vestibular symptoms, prominent auditory complaints, or 
chronic dizziness patterns.
Historically, vertigo in migraine was often 
conceptualized either as a non-specific accompanying 
symptom of headache or as a functional/psychogenic 
dizziness label when otological findings were 
unrevealing3-6. The evidence synthesized in this review 
supports a more specific neurovestibular model. In 
this framework, vestibular symptoms are not merely an 
incidental extension of migraine pain; rather, they may 
arise from interactions between migraine biology and 
vestibular network dysfunction.
From a pathophysiological perspective, VM should be 
considered a complex disorder situated at the intersection 
of trigeminovascular, vestibular, thalamocortical, 
cerebellar, and visual-vestibular integration systems24,28,29 . 
Trigeminovascular activation and CGRP signaling provide 
a biological bridge between migraine mechanisms and 
vestibular symptoms 30-32 . Central sensitization, altered 
thalamocortical sensory processing, and cerebellar 
compensation may further explain motion sensitivity, 
visual dependence, and the tendency toward chronic 
dizziness in some patients 23,35,37 . This network-based 
interpretation has practical consequences: patients 
with recurrent vertigo and migraine features should not 
be categorized solely by headache frequency but also 
by attack phenomenology, vestibular symptom type, 
auditory profile, motion sensitivity, and chronification 
risk.
A key finding of this synthesis is that VM frequently 
phenotypically overlaps with common neurotological 
disorders. Positional vertigo may resemble BPPV, 
whereas tinnitus and aural fullness may resemble early 
Ménière’s disease19,22,23 . Chronic visually induced dizziness 
may suggest PPPD, and late-onset acute vestibular 
symptoms may mimic posterior circulation ischemia 3,4,25 . 
These overlaps do not invalidate the diagnosis of VM, but 
they require a diagnostic strategy that actively searches 
for distinguishing features, such as canal-specific 
nystagmus, progressive unilateral sensorineural hearing 
loss, persistent neurological deficits, vascular risk factors, 
or chronic non-episodic dizziness patterns. Thus, VM 
should be approached as a diagnosis requiring positive 
clinical criteria and careful exclusion of alternative or 
coexisting vestibular disorders rather than as a residual 
diagnosis after normal testing1,2,19 .
In daily neurotological and audiological practice, the 
findings support a structured assessment pathway. A 
detailed attack history should be accompanied, when 
clinically indicated, by pure-tone audiometry and 
targeted vestibular testing such as vHIT, caloric testing, 
VEMP, and positional maneuvers 19,22,23 . These tests 
should not be interpreted as stand-alone diagnostic 
biomarkers for VM. Their main value lies in identifying 
Ménière’s disease, BPPV, vestibular neuritis, bilateral 
vestibulopathy, or central red flags and phenotyping 
patients with persistent imbalance or visual dependence. 
Audiological follow-up is particularly important when 
auditory symptoms are unilateral, progressive, or 
disproportionate to the vestibular attack pattern19,22 .
Therapeutically, the reviewed evidence argues against 
applying a uniform treatment ladder to all patients. Acute 
symptomatic medications may be appropriate for short-
term control of severe nausea and vertigo, but prolonged 
vestibular suppression should be avoided 30,44-46 . 
Conventional migraine prophylaxis remains clinically 
relevant, yet the choice of beta-blockers, tricyclic 
antidepressants, topiramate, valproate, flunarizine, or 
other agents should be individualized according to 
comorbidities, contraindications, pregnancy potential, 
age, cognitive side effects, and patient preference 52-55 . 
Vestibular rehabilitation should be considered early 
when motion sensitivity, imbalance, visual dependence, 
or PPPD overlap is present, because these domains may 
persist even after headache frequency improves63-66 .
Emerging data on CGRP-targeted therapies represent 
one of the most important changes in the therapeutic 
landscape of VM 31,33,34 . The available randomized and 
observational findings suggest benefit in selected 
frequent or refractory VM phenotypes 33,59-61 . However, 
replication studies, longer follow-up, cost-effectiveness 
analyses, and predictors of response are still needed 
before these agents can be positioned as definitive 
first-line therapy for VM. For clinicians, the most 
appropriate current implication is cautious escalation: 
CGRP-targeted treatment may be considered when 

 
conventional prophylaxis is ineffective, poorly tolerated, 
or contraindicated, particularly in patients with strong 
migraine biology and substantial vestibular disability.
The synthesis also highlights the need for closer 
collaboration between neurology, otolaryngology, 
audiology, vestibular physiotherapy, and primary care. 
VM management should include patient education, 
recognition of red flags, documentation of attack 
frequency and dizziness-related disability, and 
assessment of anxiety, sleep disturbance, and PPPD 
comorbidity19,22,24 . Follow-up should use comparable 
outcome measures, preferably dizziness-day counts and 
disability scales. Such a multidisciplinary approach may 
reduce diagnostic delay, avoid unnecessary repeated 
imaging or vestibular procedures, and improve alignment 
of mechanism-based treatment with the patient’s 
dominant clinical phenotype.
Study Limitations
This review has several limitations. Although the 
literature search and study selection were structured 
according to PRISMA-informed principles, the synthesis 
was narrative rather than quantitative because of 
heterogeneity in study design, diagnostic criteria, 
interventions, and outcomes. Although a supplementary 
targeted search of ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP was 
performed, Embase was not searched because of a lack of 
institutional access, and a comprehensive grey literature 
search was not undertaken. Registry findings were used 
to contextualize ongoing or unpublished therapeutic 
research but were not incorporated into the main evidence 
synthesis; therefore, the possibility of publication and 
selective reporting bias remains. This limitation may 
result in an overrepresentation of positive findings in 
the published literature, particularly in rapidly evolving 
treatment areas such as CGRP-targeted therapies and 
other pharmacological or device-based interventions. 
Consequently, conclusions regarding these therapies 
should be interpreted as provisional and based primarily 
on the available published evidence rather than on the 
totality of completed or ongoing research. No prospective 
protocol registration was performed. The included 
evidence base spans randomized trials, observational 
studies, systematic reviews, case series, and preclinical 
studies; therefore, conclusions should be interpreted 
in accordance with the certainty of the evidence rather 
than as uniform clinical recommendations. Finally, VM-
specific outcome measures remain heterogeneous, 
limiting direct comparisons across treatment studies.
These limitations are particularly relevant to the 
treatment sections. Acute therapies, CGRP-targeted 
therapies, vestibular rehabilitation, and experimental 
interventions are supported by evidence of varying levels 
of certainty; therefore, the manuscript avoids ranking all 
interventions equally and emphasizes individualized, 
phenotype-based management.
Future Research Areas
Future research should prioritize four clinically 
relevant areas: development of standardized VM-
specific outcome measures; adequately powered 
randomized trials for acute, preventive, CGRP-targeted, 
and rehabilitation-based interventions; biomarker 
and vestibular testing studies that help distinguish 
VM from M énière’s disease, BPPV, PPPD, and posterior 
circulation disorders; and longitudinal studies evaluating 
chronification, PPPD overlap, disability, and treatment 
response. Machine learning-based diagnostic models, 
combinations of VEMP/vHIT/caloric testing, and attack-
monitoring approaches that use wearable technologies 
may contribute to future clinical decision support, but 
these tools require external validation, interpretability, 
and clinical applicability beyond algorithmic accuracy.
In the future, evidence syntheses should incorporate 
Embase, prospective trial registries, and grey literature 
sources. This will reduce publication bias and more 
effectively capture ongoing or unpublished therapeutic 
studies.
CONCLUSION
VM is not merely the concurrent occurrence of 
migraine and vertigo; it is a complex neurovestibular 
disorder involving the trigeminovascular system, central 
sensitization, vestibulo-thalamic-cortical networks, CGRP 
signaling, and cerebellar compensation. Diagnosis should be 
structured according to ICHD-3 and Bárány Society criteria, 
but differential diagnosis must be performed carefully 
for each patient. Treatment should be individualized and 
guided by the certainty of available evidence. Lifestyle 
modification and conventional migraine-preventive 
strategies may be considered according to phenotype, 
comorbidities, contraindications, and patient preference, 
although VM-specific evidence remains limited for many 
agents. Vestibular rehabilitation may be useful as an adjunct 
in selected patients with motion sensitivity, imbalance, or 
overlap with PPPD. CGRP-targeted therapies are promising 
for selected, frequent, or refractory phenotypes, but 
current evidence remains insufficient to establish them as 
the definitive first-line VM therapy. Stronger VM-specific 
randomized trials, standardized outcome measures, and 
biomarker-based phenotyping are needed to transform 
clinical practice in this field.
