# A case of lipoprotein glomerulopathy in which complete remission was achieved by pemafibrate therapy.
**Authors:** Tomita C, Sato T, Suzuki Y, Arakawa M, Shimizu Y, Suzuki Y, Yasuda H, Misaki T
**Journal:** CEN Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s13730-026-01148-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13730-026-01148-9)

## Content

Lipoprotein glomerulopathy (LPG) is a rare hereditary glomerular disease characterized by lipoprotein thrombi within dilated glomerular capillaries for which effective treatments remain limited. This report concerns a 25-year-old Japanese woman with an 8-year history of persistent proteinuria and severe dyslipidemia who presented with heavy proteinuria (3.31 g/gCr) despite preserved renal function. Kidney biopsy revealed features typical of LPG on light and electron microscopy, and serum apoE levels were markedly elevated. The patient was started on pemafibrate, a selective peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-alpha modulator known to exert stronger triglyceride-lowering effects and to have a more favorable hepatic and renal safety profile compared with conventional fibrates. Following treatment, the patient's triglyceride levels decreased substantially and her proteinuria progressively improved. Complete remission (0.05 g/gCr) was achieved within 7 months, and she remained stable thereafter. Although partial remission with fibrates has been previously reported in patients with LPG, there have been no reports of complete remission in those treated with pemafibrate. To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of complete remission of proteinuria induced by pemafibrate in a patient with LPG. Pemafibrate may be a promising targeted therapy for this rare glomerular disorder.

Lipoprotein glomerulopathy (LPG) is a rare hereditary glomerular disease characterized by dilated glomerular capillaries containing lipoprotein thrombi [1]. First described in Japan in 1989 [2], the number of reported cases has been gradually increasing, with the majority from Japan and China [3]. Clinically, LPG usually presents as asymptomatic proteinuria, with approximately 70% of cases eventually developing nephrotic syndrome, often in the absence of hematuria [1, 3].

LPG is caused by heterozygous variants in the APOE gene and is often associated with hypertriglyceridemia and an elevated serum apolipoprotein E (apoE) level [3–5]. Without appropriate treatment, the chronic kidney disease caused by LPG progresses, with approximately half of the reported cases having reached end-stage kidney disease within 1–27 years [1, 3].

Conventional therapies for nephrotic syndrome, including corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents, and anticoagulants, are generally ineffective in LPG [1]. Lipid-lowering agents, including statins, can improve hyperlipidemia but generally do not reduce proteinuria in LPG [6]. However, fibrates have been reported to improve both proteinuria and dyslipidemia in this condition [1]. In this report, we describe a rare case of LPG in which complete remission of proteinuria was achieved with pemafibrate therapy.

A 25-year-old Japanese woman was admitted to our hospital for evaluation of heavy proteinuria and underwent percutaneous kidney biopsy. She had a history of obesity since junior high school and had first presented to our outpatient clinic 8 years earlier following detection of proteinuria during routine school health screening. At that time, she had a serum creatinine of 0.50 mg/dL, triglycerides of 705 mg/dL, and a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.96 g/gCr, with 1–4 red blood cells per high-power field in urine sediment. Her family history was notable for dyslipidemia in her mother and grandfather but not for kidney disease. Abdominal ultrasonography did not reveal any structural abnormalities of the kidneys or urinary tract. The patient had declined a renal biopsy at that time. Weight reduction counseling was provided in view of her body mass index (BMI) of 28 on admission. During outpatient follow-up, her BMI decreased to 23, and initiation of olmesartan resulted in a transient reduction of proteinuria to 0.3 g/gCr. However, her BMI gradually increased thereafter, accompanied by a steady increase in her urinary protein level, which reached approximately 1.0 g/gCr two years before admission. This eventually progressed to heavy proteinuria, reaching 3.31 g/gCr at the time of admission at the age of 25 years.

On the present admission, her height was 148.5 cm, her weight was 62.8 kg, with her BMI was 28. Blood pressure was 111/82 mmHg, and physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory findings on admission are summarized in Table 1. Total protein was 6.3 g/dL, albumin was 3.5 g/dL, and serum creatinine was 0.58 mg/dL, indicating preserved renal function, and lipid parameters were consistent with dyslipidemia (total cholesterol 250 mg/dL, triglycerides 259 mg/dL). Urinalysis revealed heavy proteinuria of 3.31 g/gCr without hematuria. A percutaneous renal biopsy was performed on the second hospital day.

Plasma apolipoprotein testing revealed an apoE level of 16.6 mg/dL (more than twice the upper limit of normal). Lipoprotein electrophoresis demonstrated elevated very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and intermediate-density lipoprotein, showing a broad-beta pattern characteristic of type III hyperlipoproteinemia (Fig. 2). The patient had persistent proteinuria of approximately 1–3 g/gCr. Pemafibrate was initiated at a dose of 0.2 mg with no observable adverse effects. Although her triglyceride levels decreased, complete remission was not achieved, and the dose was increased to 0.4 mg based on urinary protein levels. Urinary protein decreased to 0.05 g/gCr after 7 months, and complete remission was maintained thereafter (Fig. 3). BMI remained stable throughout this period.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to demonstrate the therapeutic effectiveness of pemafibrate in LPG, which is an extremely rare glomerular disease. This case involved a young Japanese woman with long-standing proteinuria and severe dyslipidemia who was ultimately diagnosed with LPG based on characteristic findings on light and electron microscopy together with a markedly elevated serum apoE level. Despite preserved renal function, her proteinuria progressed to heavy proteinuria. Notably, treatment with pemafibrate resulted in a marked reduction in triglycerides and complete remission of proteinuria. Complete remission was defined as a urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio of  < 0.3 g/gCr and partial remission as 0.3–3.5 g/gCr together with a reduction of at least 50% from baseline in accordance with KDIGO criteria and previous studies [7, 8]. Although improvement with conventional fibrates has been reported, this is the first known case in which pemafibrate has achieved complete remission of LPG, highlighting its potential as an effective therapeutic option for this disease.

LPG is diagnosed based on a characteristic combination of pathological and biochemical findings. Pathologically, light microscopy typically shows markedly dilated glomerular capillary lumina containing pale, thrombus-like material, while electron microscopy reveals lamellated, fingerprint-like structures composed of lipid-rich granules and vacuoles [1, 6]. Although no universally accepted diagnostic criteria have been established, Japanese nephrologists have proposed practical diagnostic items based on the cases accumulated to date, including proteinuria, characteristic features seen on light and electron microscopy, and an elevated apoE level accompanied by type III–like hyperlipoproteinemia [1]. The diagnosis in clinical practice generally relies on this constellation of findings, and although genetic testing is not strictly necessary for definitive diagnosis, APOE gene sequencing enables definitive confirmation when available [1, 3]. In our patient, the typical pathological findings together with markedly elevated apoE strongly supported the diagnosis despite the absence of genetic testing.

Accumulating evidence indicates that LPG is not a disorder intrinsic to the kidney but rather a systemic disease driven predominantly by circulating abnormal apoE-containing lipoproteins. In line with this concept, there have been several reports of recurrence of LPG after kidney transplantation [9–12], demonstrating that replacement of the native kidney does not eliminate the underlying pathogenic factor. These observations support the view that intraglomerular lipoprotein thrombus formation is primarily determined by systemic lipid abnormalities rather than donor kidney-specific susceptibility.

There is still no established therapy for LPG. Conventional treatments for nephrotic syndrome, including corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents, and anticoagulants, have generally been ineffective [1]. Apheresis therapies have also been attempted. One report described complete remission after 10 months of low-density lipoprotein apheresis [13], although its overall efficacy remains uncertain. Immunoadsorption can also transiently reduce serum apoE levels and proteinuria; however, relapse typically occurs after discontinuation, requiring repeated treatments [14]. Statins have been used in several cases; however, their efficacy remains uncertain; only three case reports have described partial remission, and there have been no reports of complete remission [15–17]. In contrast, fibrates appear to offer more consistent clinical benefit. In a cohort study by Hu et al., fenofibrate significantly improved proteinuria and lipid parameters over more than 3 years of follow-up in 35 patients with LPG, 16 of whom received fibrate therapy [18]. A literature search identified a further 11 cases of LPG treated with fibrates [19–29]. These cases are summarized in Table 2, with addition of the present case. Among the previously reported cases, complete remission was achieved in two patients [19, 27], partial remission in five [20, 22, 23, 26, 29], and no response in one [25], with three cases not being classifiable because of differences in reporting methods and lack of quantitative data [21, 24, 28]. Although the overall number of cases reported is small, the findings indicate a clear trend towards clinical benefit with fibrate therapy, particularly in view of the limited efficacy of other conventional treatments. Fibrates and other peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-alpha (PPAR-α) agonists do not correct the structural abnormality of apoE itself; instead, they modulate the metabolism of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins. Activation of PPAR-α reduces hepatic production of VLDL, enhances lipoprotein lipase-mediated catabolism of VLDL and intermediate-density lipoprotein, and suppresses expression of apolipoprotein C-III, a key inhibitor of remnant clearance [30–32]. Collectively, these effects decrease both the circulating burden and residence time of apoE-rich remnant particles, thereby reducing the likelihood that abnormal lipoproteins will be retained within glomerular capillaries and form lipoprotein thrombi.

Pemafibrate is a novel selective PPAR-α modulator that has been in clinical use since 2018 [33]. The receptor selectivity of pemafibrate is markedly higher than that of conventional fibrates; moreover, pemafibrate has been shown to have superior lipid-modifying effects, including more potent triglyceride-lowering ability and a greater ability to increase high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [34, 35]. In the present case, pemafibrate was selected because it is eliminated mainly via the biliary route, unlike most conventional fibrates that are excreted primarily via the renal route. This contributes to the favorable safety profile of pemafibrate, particularly with respect to renal function [34]. However, at present, the superiority of pemafibrate over conventional fibrates in LPG has not been established. Nevertheless, pemafibrate is characterized by high selectivity and potent agonist activity toward PPAR-α, which may theoretically result in more efficient suppression of production of remnant lipoproteins and enhanced clearance of triglyceride-rich particles. PPAR-α activation also has anti-inflammatory and endothelial-protective effects, which have been demonstrated in experimental models [36, 37], and may help to stabilize the glomerular microvasculature. Such pharmacological properties raise the possibility that pemafibrate can exert stronger metabolic effects relevant to the pathophysiology of LPG, although this hypothesis remains to be validated in larger clinical studies. In this case, proteinuria progressed to the heavy range within a relatively short period, and pemafibrate was initiated within a year of progression. Such relatively early intervention may have contributed to rapid clearance of intraglomerular lipoprotein thrombi and achievement of complete remission. Although causal inference is limited in an isolated case, the clinical course observed in this patient is consistent with the proposed disease model in which both systemic lipid abnormalities and temporal reversibility of glomerular lesions have a strong influence on the response to treatment.

Although a marked reduction in urinary protein was observed after initiation of pemafibrate, the potential contribution of ongoing angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) therapy cannot be excluded. In this case, proteinuria had previously shown a transient response to ARB therapy but subsequently worsened, suggesting that treatment with an ARB alone was insufficient to achieve remission.

After initiation of pemafibrate, changes in triglyceride levels and urinary protein did not occur in parallel. This lack of synchrony between lipid parameters and proteinuria may reflect the structural characteristics of glomerular injury. Furthermore, because the patient was evaluated during afternoon outpatient visits, all blood samples were obtained under non-fasting conditions. Therefore, triglyceride levels may have been influenced by postprandial status, which could have contributed to variability in the measurements.

This research has some limitations. First, genetic testing for APOE variants was not performed because consent could not be obtained. However, a reliable diagnosis of LPG could be made based on characteristic clinical and pathological findings. Second, the long-term efficacy and safety of pemafibrate in LPG remain unclear and require further investigation.

In conclusion, we have encountered a patient with LPG in whom complete remission of proteinuria was achieved after initiation of pemafibrate. Our experience with this case supports a therapeutic strategy targeting systemic lipid metabolism in LPG and highlights the potential importance of early PPAR-α-mediated intervention in this rare disorder.
