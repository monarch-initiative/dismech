# Altered BAFF-receptor signaling and additional modifier loci contribute to systemic autoimmunity in A/WySnJ mice.
**Authors:** Mayne CG, Nashold FE, Sasaki Y, Hayes CE
**Journal:** Eur J Immunol (2009)
**DOI:** [10.1002/eji.200838569](https://doi.org/10.1002/eji.200838569)

## Content

Systemic lupus erythematosus pathology reflects autoantibody-mediated damage due to a failure of B-lymphocyte tolerance. We previously reported that B-lymphopenic A/WySnJ mice develop a lupus-like syndrome and linked this syndrome to the B-cell maturation defect-1 (Bcmd-1) mutant allele of the B-cell-activating factor belonging to the TNF family-receptor (Baffr) gene. Here, we further evaluate the genetic basis for autoimmunity in A/WySnJ mice. We produced B6.Bcmd-1 and AW.Baffr(-/-) congenic mice (N5), and compared them with B6.Baffr(-/-) and A/WySnJ mice with respect to B-lymphocyte development. Bcmd-1-expressing mice had more B cells with greater maturity than Baffr(-/-) mice regardless of genetic background, indicating that Bcmd-1 encodes a partially functional BAFF-R. We also compared these mice for lupus phenotypes to determine whether Bcmd-1 is necessary and sufficient for disease, or whether the Baffr(-/-) (-) allele can also cause autoimmunity. The Baffr(-/-) allele did not lead to autoimmunity on either genetic background. In contrast, the Bcmd-1 allele was necessary and sufficient for development of low levels of IgM autoantibodies in B6.Bcmd-1 mice. However, Bcmd-1 plus unidentified A/WySnJ modifier genes were necessary for development of IgG autoantibodies and renal pathology. We propose that in A/WySnJ mice an excess of BAFF per B cell rescues self-reactive B cells through a partially functional BAFF-R in a B-lymphopenic environment.

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a debilitating autoimmune disease of unknown etiology. Lupus pathology is thought to reflect autoantibody-mediated damage due to a failure of B and T lymphocyte tolerance. The genetic causes and immunological mechanisms that result in disease are numerous, complex, and incompletely elucidated. Although genetic association studies in human SLE patients have identified some genetic risk factors for this disease, most susceptibility loci have been identified by studying systemic autoimmunity in inbred mouse strains [1, 2]. The NZB/W, BXSB, and MRL/lpr mouse strains spontaneously develop a lupus-like syndrome. Dissection of the loci that are responsible for the loss of self-tolerance in these mice by congenic strain construction (e.g. NZM2410 and B6.Sle1–3) has led to an appreciation for the component phenotypes and polygenic nature of this disease [1–4].

We previously described a lupus-like syndrome in A/WySnJ strain mice, and linked this autoimmunity to a mutant allele of the Baffr gene, B cell maturation defect-1 (Bcmd-1). A/WySnJ mice developed a late onset lupus-like syndrome with a high frequency of splenocytes secreting IgM antibodies to dsDNA, high titers of circulating IgM and IgG to dsDNA, and renal pathology due to immune complex (IC) deposition in the glomerulus. This autoimmunity appeared to be due to B-2 cells since autoantibody-forming B cells were not present in the peritoneum [5].

The B cell activating factor belonging to the TNF family (BAFF) is a B cell-specific survival factor. BAFF binds three receptors, BCMA (B cell maturation antigen), TACI (transmembrane activator and CAML interactor), and BAFF-receptor (BAFF-R), but promotes peripheral B cell survival primarily through engagement of BAFF-R [6–11]. The A/WySnJ mouse strain harbors a spontaneous BAFF-R mutation. A retrotransposon insertion into the A/WySnJ Baffr locus created the Bcmd-1 mutant allele [9, 11–14]. The Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R has an altered C-terminus, but it is expressed on the B lymphocyte surface and binds BAFF [8, 9, 15]. However, the severe lymphopenia in A/WySnJ mice suggests that this mutant BAFF-R lacks some or all survival signaling function [9, 16]. The B lymphopenic phenotype of lupus-prone A/WySnJ mice is especially noteworthy, since many lupus patients exhibit lymphopenia. In contrast, most other lupus-prone mouse strains have excessive B lymphocyte numbers [1, 17]. Therefore, the A/WySnJ strain may model development of lupus in some humans with genetically-determined B lymphopenia.

Although we linked the A/WySnJ systemic autoimmunity to Bcmd-1, much remains unknown about this syndrome. The A/WySnJ B cells appeared not to respond to BAFF in vivo [16] or in vitro [18], so Bcmd-1 is widely considered to be a complete loss-of-function mutation. That being so, it is unclear whether the Bcmd-1 mutation in particular, or a simple loss of BAFF-R function would drive the loss of B lymphocyte self-tolerance. Further, it is not known whether A/WySnJ modifier loci combine with a Baffr mutation to drive the lupus-like disease. Finally, we do not know which if any of three suggested hypotheses can explain how auto-reactive A/WySnJ B cells are spared from deletion in this B-lymphopenic environment. An excess of BAFF per B cell might spare these cells through residual BAFF-R survival signaling or through TACI or BCMA signaling. Alternatively, insufficient CD21 expression due to a dysfunctional BAFF-R might alter the threshold for auto-reactive B cell deletion [19].

The experiments reported here aimed to better define the ability of the Bcmd-1 mutation (as compared to a true Baffr-null mutation) to support peripheral B cell development, to evaluate possible involvement of A/WySnJ modifier loci, and to test the models that might explain this particular autoimmune syndrome. To address the first question, we introgressed Bcmd-1 onto the C57BL/6 background (B6.Bcmd-1), and the Baffr-null allele onto the A/WySnJ background (AW.Baffr−/−). We intercrossed siblings, selected mice homozygous for the Baffr allele of interest, and compared peripheral B cell development in the resulting congenic and parental strains. To identify possible contributions from a true Baffr-null mutation and A/WySnJ modifier loci, we evaluated the AW.Baffr−/−, B6.Baffr−/− and B6.Bcmd-1 mice for each autoimmune phenotype we had previously reported in A/WySnJ mice. We found evidence consistent with residual survival signaling from the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R, an obligatory role for the particular Bcmd-1 mutation, and an accessory role for A/WySnJ modifier loci in the genesis of the full autoimmune phenotype. We discuss these data in the context of a model linking loss of self-tolerance in peripheral B lymphocytes to partial loss of BAFF-R function.

Although A/WySnJ mice are B lymphopenic, they have more B lymphocytes than B6.Baffr-null (B6.Baffr−/−) mice [6, 8]. This observation suggests either that Bcmd-1 encodes a partially functional BAFF-R, or that it encodes a completely nonfunctional BAFF-R and other C57BL/6 genes diminish B lymphocyte development. In fact, the retrotransposon insertion in A/WySnJ mice resulted in a mutant BAFF-R that is 95% identical to wild type, suggesting that many functional domains of BAFF-R may be retained in the mutant protein (Fig. 1B, Fig. 1C). To gain new insight into the functional capabilities of the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R, and determine if the excess BAFF per B cell model could apply to systemic autoimmunity in A/WySnJ mice, we constructed two new reciprocal congenic strains, B6.Bcmd-1 and AW.Baffr−/−. The AW.Baffr−/− mice have 13.9 Mb of homozygous B6.Baffr−/−-derived DNA bounded by D15Mit68 and D15Mit171. The B6.Bcmd-1 mice have 23.1 Mb of homozygous A/WySnJ-derived DNA bounded by D15Mit156 and D15Mit272 (Fig. 1A). Tightly linked loci derive from the congenic interval donor. Additionally, any particular unlinked locus has a 3% chance of deriving from the congenic interval donor at backcross generation N5. The new congenic strains were compared to the parental strains, B6.Baffr−/− and A/WySnJ, with respect to splenic B lymphocyte development.

We first characterized total peripheral B cell numbers in the parental and reciprocal congenic strains through flow cytometric analyses (Fig. 2A, Table I). The A/WySnJ spleens had ~15 million IgM+ B lymphocytes, about 25% of the splenocyte pool. However, the AW.Baffr−/− spleens had only ~5 million IgM+ B cells, just 12% of the splenocyte pool. A similar decrease was apparent when the two alleles were compared on the C57BL/6 genetic background. The B6.Bcmd-1 spleens had ~11 million IgM+ B lymphocytes, about 30% of the splenocyte pool, but the B6.Baffr−/− spleens had only ~4 million IgM+ B cells, just 19% of the splenocyte pool. All four strains were significantly B lymphopenic compared to B6 controls (Table I) [5, 6, 8]. However, the segregation of more severe B lymphopenia with the null allele supports the proposition that the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R retains some survival signaling function.

Next, we evaluated peripheral B cell maturation in the parental and reciprocal congenic strains through analysis of transitional and mature B (MB) cell subsets. These subsets were first defined by their relative expression of IgM and IgD (Fig. 2A). The B6 control spleens had mainly MB cells, with small populations of transitional B cells (Table II). In contrast, the A/WySnJ spleens had about equal proportions of MB and transitional B cells, whereas the AW.Baffr−/− spleens had a significantly smaller proportion of MB cells than transitional B cells. Similarly, the B6.Bcmd-1 spleens had a higher ratio of MB to transitional B cells than the B6.Baffr−/− spleens. The greater maturity of the Bcmd-1 B cells compared to the Baffr−/− B cells was also evident when the transitional and MB cell subsets were evaluated by their relative expression of IgM, C1qRp, and CD23 (Fig. 2B). Since CD23 expression was slightly reduced on Baffr−/− B cells [8, 9, 15], the IgM/C1qRp/CD23 stain would be expected to give an underestimate of the MB pool compared to the IgM/IgD stain, as we observed for these B cells (Table II). This concern does not apply to Bcmd-1 B cells, which had normal CD23 expression [5, 8, 9, 15]. Importantly, the relative proportion of MB cells compared to transitional B cells was higher for Bcmd-1 B cells than for Baffr−/− B cells, regardless of the staining method and the genetic background (Table II). We conclude that the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R retains enough survival signaling function to allow some transitional B cells to progress to the MB cell stage.

Our analysis of the numbers of splenic B cells at each maturation stage reinforced this conclusion (Fig. 3). The A/WySnJ spleens had ~7 million MB cells, whereas the AW.Baffr−/− spleens had only 1–2 million MB cells, although the two strains had similar numbers of transitional B cells (3–5 million). Similarly, the B6.Bcmd-1 spleens had ~3–4 million MB cells, whereas the B6.Baffr−/− spleens had only one tenth that number (Fig. 3). This pronounced maturational difference was clear whether the peripheral B cell subsets were defined by their relative expression of IgM and IgD (Fig. 2A), or of IgM, C1qRp, and CD23 (Fig. 2B). We conclude that on either the C57BL/6 or A strain background, the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R retains enough survival signaling function to allow some transitional B cells to reach the MB developmental stage, whereas maturation to this stage is very rare without BAFF-R signaling.

We next sought to test three hypotheses that might explain systemic autoimmunity in B lymphopenic A/WySnJ mice. Self-reactive B cells might be spared from deletion by; altered BAFF signaling through the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R, excess BAFF signaling through TACI or BCMA, or insufficient CD21 expression and signaling due to dysfunctional BAFF-R signaling [5]. The excess BAFF-TACI model and the suboptimal CD21 model predict that mice harboring a null Baffr allele will develop autoimmunity because they have B lymphopenia, an excess of BAFF per B cell, intact TACI-mediated signaling, and suboptimal CD21 expression and signaling [6, 8]. Therefore, to test the excess BAFF-TACI and suboptimal CD21 models, we evaluated B6.Baffr−/− mice for signs of autoimmunity. Like the B6 control mice, the B6.Baffr−/− mice did not produce circulating IgM or IgG antibodies specific for dsDNA (Fig. 4A and 4B). They also had very few (4282±1126) dsDNA-specific antibody-forming B cells in their spleens (Fig. 4C) like control A/J mice (3907±1326). Moreover, like the B6 control mice, the B6.Baffr−/− mice did not develop proteinuria (Fig. 5A and 5B), and did not show evidence of immunoglobulin deposition their glomeruli (Fig. 5C). We conclude that the Baffr-null mutation on the C57BL/6 background is not sufficient to drive the autoimmune phenotype, providing some evidence against the excess BAFF signaling through TACI or BCMA and insufficient CD21 signaling models.

The lack of autoimmunity in B6.Baffr−/− mice might be due to the absence A/WySnJ modifier alleles that are needed to spare self-reactive B cells from deletion and drive autoimmunity. To investigate possible contributions of B6 suppressor or A/WySnJ enhancer loci to the lupus phenotype, we constructed the AW.Baffr−/− congenic strain and evaluated the mice for systemic autoimmunity. Like B6.Baffr−/− mice, the AW.Baffr−/− mice did not produce circulating dsDNA-specific antibodies or develop dsDNA-specific antibody-forming B cells (Fig. 4). Thus, the Baffr-null mutation does not spare the dsDNA-reactive B cells from deletion whether the genetic background is C57BL/6 or A/WySnJ, strengthening the evidence that Bcmd-1 is not equivalent to a Baffr-null mutation.

Having ruled out a Baffr-null mutation as a driver for systemic autoimmunity, we next asked whether Bcmd-1 is necessary and sufficient, or whether A/WySnJ modifier alleles are also needed to drive systemic autoimmunity. To determine if Bcmd-1 is necessary and sufficient to subvert peripheral B cell self tolerance regardless of the genetic background, we constructed the B6.Bcmd-1 congenic strain and compared mice of this strain to A/WySnJ mice with respect to the systemic autoimmune phenotypes we reported previously [5]. The B6.Bcmd-1 mice had significantly higher levels of serum IgM to dsDNA than B6.Baffr−/− mice (titer ~200), but lower levels of IgM to dsDNA than A/WySnJ mice (titer~1600) (Fig. 4A), Moreover, the B6.Bcmd-1 mice did not produce pathogenic IgG to dsDNA (Fig. 4B). The B6.Bcmd-1 mice had significantly higher numbers of dsDNA-specific antibody-forming splenic B cells than either of the Baffr−/− congenic strains, but lower numbers of these cells than A/WySnJ mice (Fig. 4C).

To determine if B-1 B cells were major contributors of dsDNA-specific IgM antibodies, we first investigated peritoneal B cells, because B-1 cells are enriched in the peritoneum [20]. Like the A/WySnJ mice [5], the B6.Bcmd-1 mice had very few dsDNA-specific antibody-forming B cells in the peritoneum (data not shown). However, B-1 cells can re-circulate and constitute a minority population in the spleen and other peripheral lymphoid organs. So we next analyzed splenocytes from the four strains of interest, A/WySnJ, B6.Baffr−/−, B6.Bcmd-1, and AW.Baffr−/−, and control B6 mice. These five strains all had 0.4–1.3 million B220+CD11b+ splenic B-1 cells, with no significant differences in the mean values (Supplemental Fig. 1). These results confirmed previous results from our lab [21] and others [6]. Since Baffr-null and Bcmd-1-expressing mice had large differences in the numbers of splenic B cells secreting IgM antibodies to dsDNA, but no differences in splenic B-1 cells, it appears unlikely that B-1 B cells are the source of these IgM antibodies. Together, these data suggest that Bcmd-1 is necessary and sufficient to spare dsDNA-specific, IgM-producing B-2 B cells from tolerance induction in A/WySnJ mice. However, A/WySnJ modifier alleles evidently expand the number of dsDNA-specific, IgM-producing B-2 B cells, and trigger production of IgG to dsDNA.

We previously showed that autoimmune A/WySnJ mice with circulating IgG autoantibodies developed proteinuria, and that proteinuria correlated perfectly with renal histopathology [5]. Since A/WySnJ modifier alleles were needed for dsDNA-specific IgG production, and autoreactive IgG immune complex (IC) deposition in the glomeruli triggers renal pathology in lupus patients [1], we suspected that B6.Bcmd-1 mice would not develop significant renal pathology. To test this prediction, we analyzed fresh urine samples for protein (a sign of renal dysfunction). We defined urinary protein >100 mg/dL as evidence of moderate renal damage and protein >300 mg/dL as evidence of severe damage. By age 11 mo, 47% of the A/WySnJ mice had moderate proteinuria, and the mean urinary protein value for the group was 95 mg/dL, significantly higher than all other strains tested (p<.05; Fig 5A and 5B). By age 13 mo, 65% of A/WySnJ mice had moderate to severe proteinuria. In contrast, 20% of B6.Bcmd-1 mice developed mild proteinuria later (age 13 mo), whereas AW.Baffr−/− and B6.Baffr−/− mice showed no evidence of proteinuria.

We previously performed immunofluorescence staining to detect IgG-containing IC deposited in the glomeruli of A/WySnJ mice [5]. Further experiments confirmed this result, and revealed no significant IC deposition in AW.Baffr−/− or B6.Baffr−/− glomeruli (Fig. 5C). However, consistent with the proteinuria data, there appeared to be dim immunofluorescence staining of some glomeruli from a few B6.Bcmd-1 mice. Together, the proteinuria and immunofluorescence data support the conclusion that Bcmd-1 plus A/WySnJ modifier alleles are needed to trigger production of auto-reactive IgG antibodies, IC formation and deposition in the glomeruli, and renal dysfunction.

The new evidence we have reported here establishes unequivocally that the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R has some residual function that is both necessary and sufficient for the development of an IgM response to dsDNA, even though it has lost enough survival signaling function to cause B lymphopenia. The new evidence supporting this conclusion derives from studies of Bcmd-1 and Baffr-null reciprocal congenic strains with A/WySnJ or C57BL/6 background genes. We found that Bcmd-1 mice had more plentiful and more mature B lymphocytes than Baffr-null mice with comparable background genes, indicating that the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R supported some B-2 B cell survival. We also found that some dsDNA-specific B cells survived and became IgM-producers only in the Bcmd-1 mice, whether the background genes derived from strain A/WySnJ or C57BL/6. Thus, Bcmd-1 performed an obligatory role in the loss of B lymphocyte tolerance to dsDNA. However, A/WySnJ mice produced strong IgM and IgG responses to dsDNA and developed renal pathology as they aged, whereas the B6.Bcmd-1 mice developed only weak IgM responses to dsDNA and did not develop renal pathology. Consequently, we conclude that A/WySnJ modifier loci performed essential accessory functions in the genesis of the full lupus-like phenotype that we described previously [5].

Several models can be considered to explain how Bcmd-1 enables the development of an IgM response to dsDNA. The transitional B cells that receive chronic BCR stimulation through self antigen binding are believed to die unless a high level of survival signaling rescues them [22–24]. The CD21 complex, functioning as a BCR co-receptor, may amplify the BCR signal in self-reactive B cells to the threshold for deletion [19]. Since BAFF-R-mediated signals enhance CD21 expression, and CD21 expression is suboptimal in Baffr-null and Bcmd-1 B cells [6, 8](Amanna, Mayne, and Hayes, unpublished), it is possible that BCR plus CD21-mediated signaling in the self-reactive Baffr-null and Bcmd-1 B cells might be below the threshold for negative selection. The suboptimal CD21 model predicts that B6.Baffr−/− and AW.Baffr−/− mice would develop IgM antibodies to dsDNA. We did not find IgM antibodies to dsDNA in these congenic strains, indicating that insufficient CD21 co-receptor signaling is probably not the explanation for the failure to delete dsDNA-reactive B cells.

An alternative possibility is that an excess of survival signaling rescued the dsDNA-reactive B cells. In normal individuals, there is competition between B lymphocytes of other specificities and self-reactive B cells for available BAFF [22–24]. However, transitional B cells that have received chronic BCR stimulation through self antigen binding are thought to require more BAFF/B cell to sustain them than the unstimulated B cells. Accordingly, when BAFF is limiting, the self-reactive B cells are deleted, whereas the others are retained in the repertoire. However, in B-lymphopenic individuals, an excess of BAFF per B cell could rescue chronically BCR-stimulated B cells from deletion [23, 24]. In A/WySnJ mice with wild-type levels of BAFF, this ratio could be increased 5–6 fold yielding a stronger survival signal in these cells [5]. In this model, it is not clear which of the three BAFF receptors, BAFF-R, TACI, or BCMA, transmits the BAFF-mediated survival signals. We theorized that TACI signaling might rescue dsDNA-specific B cells in A/WySnJ mice, because TACI was essential for IgM responses to TI-II antigens that can cross-link BCR molecules [10], and dsDNA has a repeating polymer structure like a TI-II antigen. The excess TACI-signaling model predicts that the B-lymphopenic B6.Baffr−/− and AW.Baffr−/− mice would develop IgM antibodies to dsDNA through their functional TACI receptors. Since these strains did not develop IgM antibodies to dsDNA, an excess of TACI signaling probably does not explain the failure to delete dsDNA-reactive B cells.

An excess of BAFF-mediated survival signaling through BAFF-R could rescue the transitional B cells receiving chronic BCR stimulation if the BAFF-R is functional [5]. The problem is that the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R is considered to be nonfunctional [16, 18]. To test the assumption that Bcmd-1 encodes a nonfunctional receptor, we compared peripheral B cell development in Bcmd-1 and Baffr-null mice. We found that Bcmd-1 mice had more plentiful and more mature B lymphocytes than Baffr-null mice with comparable background genes, indicating that the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R is at least partially functional. A limitation of our analysis is that we evaluated the mice at generation N5, when ~3% of the non-selected genome derives from the original parental strain. Moreover, the congenic segments were large, 13.9 Mb in AW.Baffr−/− mice and 23.1 Mb in B6.Bcmd-1 mice, so many genes in linkage disequilibrium with Baffr also derived from the original parental strain. Therefore, it is theoretically possible that the failure of the Bcmd-1 congenics to phenocopy the Baffr-null congenics was due to passenger background genes or genes in linkage disequilibrium with Bcmd-1. We compared Bcmd-1 to Baffr on both the C57BL/6 and A/WySnJ backgrounds, so it is improbable that a modifier locus was carried along by chance in both congenic strains unless it was in linkage disequilibrium with Baffr. It is also unlikely that a gene linked to Bcmd-1 accounted for the failure to phenocopy, because we previously demonstrated through retroviral transduction that a wild-type Baffr gene complemented all B cell developmental abnormalities in A/WySnJ mice [5].

There is good evidence from previous studies that the Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R is at least partially functional. The mutant BAFF-R is 95% identical to the wild-type BAFF-R at the amino acid level, is correctly expressed in B cell development, and binds BAFF [8, 9, 15]. Moreover, the C-terminal eight amino acids that were truncated in the mutant BAFF-R are thought to have no signaling role. The mutant BAFF-R retains the putative TRAF3-binding motif implicated in BAFF-R signaling [25, 26], activates NF-κB2 [12, 27–29], and enhances Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL gene expression [12, 27–29]. The failure of A/WySnJ B cells to respond to BAFF in vivo may have been due to the fact they already had an excess of BAFF in their B-lymphopenic environment. Therefore, we suggest that Bcmd-1 generates a B-lymphopenic environment due to its partial loss of survival signaling function. Subsequently, an excess of BAFF signaling through the partially functional BAFF-R, together with BCR cross-linking by dsDNA derived from dying lymphocytes, enables some dsDNA-specific B cells to evade death and become activated.

The ratio of BAFF per B cell needed to rescue an autoimmune B cell competing for BAFF in a diverse B cell pool is not known. Previous analyses of MD4/ML5 double transgenic mice carrying an Ig locus with monoclonal specificity for hen egg lysozyme (HEL), a transgene expressing the soluble HEL antigen for this BCR, and a wild-type Baffr locus showed that a 2-fold increase in the BAFF/B cell ratio rescued about half of the self-specific cells [24, 30, 31]. However, none of the HEL-specific B cells were rescued when they competed for BAFF in a polyclonal B cell repertoire. In BAFF-Tg mice over-expressing BAFF, what is probably a very high BAFF/B cell ratio rescued auto-reactive B cells competing in a polyclonal B cell repertoire [7, 32, 33]. In Bcmd-1-expressing mice with a partially functional BAFF-R, the BAFF/B cell ratio was elevated ~5-fold and about 30,000 autoimmune B cells were rescued from a mixed repertoire of ~15 million B cells. Therefore, a 5-fold increase in BAFF/B cell might be more than enough to rescue autoimmune B cells in mice with a fully functional BAFF-R and a polyclonal B cell repertoire.

Interestingly, although B6.Bcmd-1 mice had some IgM-producing dsDNA-specific B cells, they had a comparatively low frequency of these cells and they did not develop IgG-producing dsDNA-specific B cells and renal pathology like A/WySnJ mice. Therefore, Bcmd-1 is necessary but not sufficient to drive the full lupus-like syndrome. Some A/WySnJ modifier loci are also needed. We propose that the A/WySnJ modifier loci either promote a break in T cell tolerance to dsDNA or otherwise drive class-switch recombination and somatic hypermutation to explain why A/WySnJ but not B6.Bcmd-1 mice develop pathogenic IgG antibodies and renal pathology. The A/WySnJ mice have mastocytosis in addition to their Bcmd-1-determined B-lymphopenia [13], whereas B6.Bcmd-1 mice do not have mastocytosis. Since mast cells can express the T cell co-stimulatory molecules CD18, CD54, CD80, and CD86 and produce IL-4 under some conditions [34], perhaps the A/WySnJ mast cells play a role in activating dsDNA-specific T cells to support an IgG response to dsDNA. The polygenic nature of the lupus-like syndrome in A/WySnJ mice is similar to other lupus-prone strains. The Sle loci of NZB/W, the yaa locus of BXSB, and the lpr mutation in MRL/lpr all show more pronounced phenotypes on their autoimmune-prone backgrounds than on resistant backgrounds like C57BL/6 [1, 2]. Others introgressed Bcmd-1 onto strain MRL/lpr [35]. The resulting congenic mice developed systemic autoimmunity despite their reduced peripheral B cell numbers, consistent with our finding that Bcmd-1 allows autoimmunity to develop in a B lymphopenic environment. It will be interesting to define the mechanistic contributions of the A/WySnJ modifier loci through congenic strain construction, as has been done so elegantly with the B6.Sle congenic series [1–3, 36].

Genetic and biological dissection of SLE pathogenesis in lupus-prone mouse strains has greatly increased our understanding of human SLE [1]. The A/WySnJ mice provide the first evidence linking systemic autoimmunity to a Baffr mutation. This lupus-like syndrome in A/WySnJ mice provides a unique opportunity to study SLE in a B lymphopenic environment, since most other lupus-prone mouse strains have B cell hyperproliferation. In addition, there is also a possibility of defining new SLE modifier genes, since Bcmd-1 was necessary but not sufficient to drive the full lupus-like disease. Our results showing the development of a lupus-like disease in animals with B-lymphopenia and a partially functional BAFF-R suggest a cautionary note for current clinical research utilizing B cell depletion in patients with systemic autoimmunity or B cell malignancy [37]. The use of monoclonal antibodies or cytokine antagonists to impede B cell survival may have unintended adverse outcomes if the therapies create B lymphopenia without considerably decreasing the amount of BAFF available to the remaining B cells. Our current research is directed to understanding the biochemistry of Bcmd-1-encoded BAFF-R signaling in B lymphocytes, and the contributions of the A/WySnJ modifier loci to the development of IgG antibodies to dsDNA and renal pathology. The results of these studies will no doubt yield new insight into SLE pathogenesis in human patients, and hopefully new prospects for treatment of this disease.

Mice studied for antibodies to dsDNA, antibody-forming cells, proteinuria, and renal pathology were 7–4 mo of age. All mice used in the present research were produced and maintained in our specific pathogen-free mouse colony at the Department of Biochemistry, University of Wisconsin-Madison, under Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocol #A073000-A01149-5-01-04.

Genotyping of selected chromosome 15 simple-sequence length polymorphism markers to determine the size of each congenic interval was performed by PCR amplification of tail DNA exactly as we described [13]. The Baffr genotyping was also performed as we described, except that the reverse primer (5′-TTCTTTGAGCGGAGGCCAGG-3′) was specific for all Baffr alleles, and the two forward primers were specific for the wild-type and Bcmd-1 alleles (5′-CTGAGGGAGACCTGGAGTTG-3′; 452 bp product), or the Baffr− allele (5′-ATGGGCCACTCAAGATGATCTG-3′; 331 bp product) [5].

Three-color flow cytometric analysis of splenic B lymphocytes was performed exactly as we described [5]. In brief, splenocytes were dissociated, erythrocytes were depleted, and duplicate samples were stained with optimal amounts of three fluorescent mAb conjugates. Reference samples were stained with one fluorescent mAb conjugate. Stained samples were analyzed on a FACScalibur™ using CELLQuest™ software (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). The fluorescent mAb conjugates used for the analysis were reported previously [5]. PE-coupled rat mAb to mouse CD21/35 was a generous gift from Dr. John Kearney (University of Alabama, Birmingham, AL).

An ELISA quantified the serum antibodies to nuclear antigens as we described [5]. In brief, diluted serum samples were aliquoted into triplicate wells of ELISA plates that had been coated with fragmented calf thymus DNA and blocked. The plates were washed and bound antibodies were detected with biotin-coupled goat Ab to mouse immunoglobulin, streptavidin-coupled alkaline phosphatase, and p-nitrophenyl phosphate substrate.

Our published ELISPOT method quantified the AFC specific for dsDNA [5]. In brief, splenocytes were added into triplicate wells of ELISPOT plates that had been coated with fragmented calf thymus DNA. The plates were incubated at 37°C overnight and washed, before DNA-specific ELISPOTs were detected with biotin-conjugated rat anti-mouse IgM, streptavidin-conjugated horseradish peroxidase, and 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole. The spots were counted twice on a dissecting microscope, and reported as mean number of AFC per spleen.

We employed a published immunofluorescence method to assess IgG deposition in the kidney [38]. In brief, snap-frozen kidneys were sectioned at 10μm, and the sections were fixed with acetone and blocked with 2% normal rabbit serum (Jackson Immunoresearch) in 0.5% Triton X-100/PBS. An overnight incubation with FITC-conjugated rabbit antibodies to mouse IgG (1:100 dilution in blocking solution; Jackson Immunoresearch) detected the IgG deposits. Sections were examined using an Eclipse E600 microscope (Nikon) fitted with a 40X objective. Fluorescent images were acquired using a SPOT RTKE camera and software (Diagnostic Instruments). Proteinuria was quantified as we reported previously [5].
