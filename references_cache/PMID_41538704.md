# Single-cell profiling of ANKRD26 thrombocytopenia reveals progenitor expansion and polyploid apoptosis via JUNB-p21.
**Authors:** Chen L, Hu L, Liu X, Wang X, Tan C, Chen M, Yin X, Yang W, Yang Z, Xiang Y, Xiao Y, Xiang L, Li X, Li J, Zhang W, Wang X, Lin C, Zhang Y, Gou W, Jiang Y, Yu T, Yang R, Li SC, Ran Q, Li Z
**Journal:** Blood (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1182/blood.2025030017](https://doi.org/10.1182/blood.2025030017)

## Content

ANKRD26-related thrombocytopenia 2 (THC2) is a rare inherited platelet disorder caused by germ line variants in the 5' untranslated region (UTR) of ANKRD26. Although prior studies using in vitro models or isolated case reports have suggested impaired megakaryopoiesis as a central mechanism, detailed insights have remained elusive, primarily due to the rarity, fragility, and heterogeneity of megakaryocytes (MKs). Here, we present a comprehensive, crossvalidated analysis of bone marrow samples from 4 independent patients with THC2, integrating single-cell transcriptomics and ex vivo functional profiling. Across all patients, we analyzed CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs; 47 281 THC2 HSPCs vs 51 907 control cells) and primary MKs (pMKs; 7309 THC2 pMKs vs 5077 control cells), uncovering a consistent pattern of MK progenitors (MkP) expansion and a marked reduction in polyploid MKs, indicating a conserved pathophysiologic phenotype. In our index patient, we identified the 5' UTR single-nucleotide variant in ANKRD26 that led to significantly elevated expression across 4 MK lineage subsets, spanning multipotent progenitors, common myeloid progenitors, MK-erythroid progenitors, and MkP as well as in terminally enriched pMKs. Confocal imaging localized ANKRD26 to the centrosome, implicating it in mitotic regulation during MK maturation. Mechanistically, we discovered that elevated ANKRD26 induces apoptosis in polyploid MKs via JUNB-mediated transcriptional activation of CDKN1A (p21), operating independently of the canonical p53-PIDDosome axis. This multipatient study provided the most comprehensive cellular and molecular portrait of ANKRD26-driven thrombocytopenia to date, offering novel insights into defective megakaryopoiesis and identifying candidate therapeutic targets to restore platelet production.

Thrombocytopenia 2 (THC2) is a very rare, autosomal dominant, inherited blood disorder. About 300 people have had documented THC2 worldwide.1 In China, THC2 has only been sporadically reported in several families.2, 3, 4, 5 Patients with THC2 present lifelong mild-to-moderate thrombocytopenia without associated syndromes or abnormal platelet size.1 Mild-to-moderate bleeding clinical manifestations, including cutaneous ecchymoses, epistaxis, and gingival bleeding, are common. Female patients may present with menorrhagia, and severe cases may demonstrate increased postpartum hemorrhage.1 Notably, ANKRD26 pathogenic variants elevate the risk of myeloid malignancies, including myelodysplastic syndrome, acute myelogenous leukemia, and chronic myelogenous leukemia.6

The dysfunctional gene ANKRD26 causes THC2 disease.7,8 THC2-associated ANKRD26 mutations localize to the 5' untranslated region (UTR), disrupting RUNX1/FLI1 binding and transcriptional repression.9 This leads to sustained ANKRD26 expression in megakaryocytes (MKs), which during late differentiation hyperactivates ERK signaling through thrombopoietin receptor retention, impairing proplatelet formation and causing thrombocytopenia.9,10 However, several key questions remain unresolved. (1) It is unknown whether RUNX1/FLI1 dysregulation extends beyond MKs to other lineages (eg, neutrophils), given the occurrence of neutrophilia in patients with THC2 and their predisposition to hematopoietic malignancy.4,6,8 (2) In addition to the proplatelet formation disorder, the MKs in patients with THC2 have an increased number but smaller cell size with hypolobulated nuclei,8 and the mechanism is unclear. Tumor cells initiate the PIDDosome-p53-p21 pathway in the presence of supernumerary centrosomes (polyploid cells) and kill the polyploid cells, which is a normal regulatory mechanism the body uses to combat tumor cells.11,12 Researchers speculate that this mechanism might be the reason for the hypolobulated nuclei in the MKs of patients with THC2, but no one has yet verified this cause. (3) Discrepancies in ANKRD26 subcellular localization, with membrane localization reported in Ba/F3/UT7 systems10 vs centrosomal localization in A549/HEK293T/RPE1 cells,11,12 lacking direct evidence in THC2 MKs.

To explore defective megakaryopoiesis in THC2, in vitro cultured MKs from CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) were used.9 However, in vitro cultured MKs behaved differently from in vivo MKs. Besides, MKs derived from the yolk sac, fetal liver, and the bone marrow (BM) exhibit marked heterogeneity, with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) revealing distinct subsets including platelet-generating, hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) niche-supporting, and immune-modulatory populations.13, 14, 15 Therefore, using in vitro cultured MKs at a heterogeneous cell population level, the unresolved questions as above were hard to reveal, especially for THC2 without mouse models for in vivo research.16

We previously identified China's largest THC2 pedigree (n = 31), with 10 confirmed ANKRD26 c.-128G>T carriers. Notably, 5 patients with THC2 also carried HBB c.52A>T mutations associated with β-thalassemia.4 This study included 2 patients with THC2 without β-thalassemia from the pedigree and 2 additional patients with ANKRD26 c.-126T>G mutations from another Chinese family.2 By using BM-derived primary CD34+ HSPCs and MKs, and a method of single-cell transcriptome, we try to answer these unsolved questions regarding the defects in megakaryopoiesis of THC2. Our data will provide novel insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms of THC2 pathogenesis.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of The Army Medical University (approval no. 2020-074-01). Peripheral blood and BM blood samples from 4 patients with THC2 (without β-thalassemia) and 8 healthy participants were obtained with written informed consent. All participants were of Han Chinese ethnicity, and none were taking any medications at the time of sample collection.

BM blood was aspirated and immediately resuspended in an equal volume of CATCH medium (calcium-magnesium-free Hank’s salt solution with adenosine, theophylline, and sodium citrate).17 The blood cells were filtered (100 μm mesh) and layered over a 2-density gradient of Percoll (1.058 and 1.076 g/mL; GE Healthcare). After centrifugation (400g, 30 minutes), cells in the upper interphase were collected, washed, and further layered over a 3-density gradient of bovine serum albumin (8.33%, 16.67%, and 25%; Sigma).17 After sedimentation (1g, 30 minutes), the top layer was collected, washed, stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate–conjugated anti-CD41 antibody (BioLegend), and sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS; Beckman Coulter) for small primary MKs (pMKs) enrichment, and the bottom layers were collected for enrichment of large pMKs using magnetic cell sorting using CD61 MicroBeads and Large Cell Separation Columns (Miltenyi Biotec).18 All the pMKs were mixed and resuspended in serum-free StemSpan SFEM II media (Stemcell Technologies) with StemSpan Megakaryocyte Expansion Supplement (Stemcell Technologies) before detection. Cells in the lower interphase of Percoll were collected for CD34+ HSPC isolation using EasySep Human CD34 Positive Selection Kit II (Stemcell Technologies). Enriched HSPCs were frozen in liquid nitrogen before use.

The CD34+ HSPCs and pMKs were collected and used to perform the scRNA-seq using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform (Novogene). More detailed information about library construction, data processing, and quality control is described in the supplemental Methods (available on the Blood website).

For pMKs, Hoechst 33342 with a final concentration of 10 μg/mL was added to the culture medium for 2 hours, then cells were collected and stained with anti-CD41, anti-CD42b, anti-CD45, and annexin V for 30 minutes. Phorbol myristate acetate (PMA)–stimulated HEL cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 for 30 minutes at 37°C, then the cells were collected and stained with annexin V for another 30 minutes. Cells were acquired on a CytoFLEX SRT flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and FACS data were analyzed with FlowJo software (TreeStar). The ploidy level was measured in the CD45−CD41+ megakaryocyte population. The calculation formula of polyploidy level (N ≥ 8; where N indicates the times of haploid DNA content) is as follows: polyploidy level (≥8N) = (8 × number of 8N cells + 16 × number of 16N cells + 32 × number of 32N cells)/(total number of 8N, 16N, and 32N cells).9 The percentage of apoptotic cells was measured by annexin V staining.

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 or R (version 4.4.2). Differences in the average gene expression level of ANKRD26 from scRNA-seq data and differences in FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments) from RNA-seq data were analyzed by an unpaired Student t test or an unpaired Student t test with Welch correction when the variances differed. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between numerous THC2 and control cells of scRNA-seq were analyzed by Wilcoxon, and the P values were adjusted by Bonferroni correction or Benjamini-Hochberg correction to determine the false discovery rate. Differences were considered significant when the P value was <.05, and the results were denoted as not significant; ∗P < .05; ∗∗P < .01; ∗∗∗P < .001.

The mutation types of the ANKRD26 gene were confirmed in all patients by Sanger sequencing of the polymerase chain reaction product (supplemental Figure 1A; Table 1). The patients with THC2 showed lower platelet counts and higher neutrophil counts (Table 1). After quality control, 99 188 CD34+ HSPCs (47 281 THC2 HSPCs vs 51 907 control HSPCs) single-cell profiles were included in the downstream analyses (supplemental Figure 2A-B; Table 1). Cell cycle regression was performed before unsupervised clustering (supplemental Figure 2C). Dimensional reduction was performed by uniform manifold approximation and projection. At resolution 1, we identified a total of 34 clusters (Figure 1D). Based on the expression of 4 well-known lineage-specific transcriptional factor (TF) genes (HLF, GATA1, CEBPA, and EBF1),19 and other specific marker genes (Figure 1E; supplemental Figure 2D; supplemental Table 1), we annotated the cell clusters into 18 different cell types (Figure 1F). These populations included HSCs, multipotent progenitors (MPP), common myeloid progenitors (CMP), MK-erythroid progenitors (MEP), MK progenitors (MkP), erythrocyte progenitors, granulocyte-macrophage progenitors, eosinophil–basophil–mast cell progenitors, neutrophil progenitors (NeuP), monocyte–dendritic cell progenitors, dendritic cells, common lymphoid progenitors, B-cell progenitors, natural killer and T cells, and 4 unknown clusters with dispersed distribution and nonspecific marker genes. By comparing the proportion of each cell type cluster between patients with THC2 and healthy controls, we found that CD34+ HSPCs from patients with THC2 had a higher proportion of HSCs, MPP, and subsets in myeloid lineages (CMP, MEP, MkP, granulocyte-macrophage progenitor, eosinophil–basophil–mast cell progenitors, NeuP, and monocyte–dendritic cell progenitors), whereas a lower proportion of lymphoid lineage subsets (common lymphoid progenitors and B-cell progenitors; Figure 1G; supplemental Figure 3).

To investigate why the fractions of CD34+ HSPC subsets changed, ANKRD26 expression was analyzed and compared across all 14 subsets. Interestingly, only 4 subsets in the MK lineage showed a significantly higher average expression level of ANKRD26 in patients with THC2, including MPP, CMP, MEP, and MkP (Figure 2A). RUNX1 and FLI1 normally repress ANKRD26 transcription by binding its 5′ UTR. In THC2, mutations impair this binding, leading to increased expression of the ANKRD26. Among 5 MK-associated subsets (HSCs, MPP, CMP, MEP, and MkP), the MkP subset showed the highest RUNX1/FLI1 levels and was thus most affected (supplemental Figure 4A-B).9 Hence, DEGs of MkP between patients with THC2 and healthy controls were delineated (Figure 2B; supplemental Table 2). In addition to the ANKRD26 gene, most upregulated genes were DNA-binding transcription activators, especially those transcription activators related to myeloid cell differentiation, whereas downregulated genes were enriched in the nucleosome-associated functions, indicating MkP in THC2 HSPCs are more active (Figure 2C-D; supplemental Figure 4C-D). In particular, genes in the negative regulation of cell cycle phase transition, including G1/S, G2/M, and metaphase/anaphase transition, were all downregulated in THC2-MkP, indicating that the persistently expressed ANKRD26 might be able to promote MkP proliferation (Figure 2D). To verify such a deduction, we overexpressed the ANKRD26 in HEL, a human megakaryocytic cell line with a low expression level of intrinsic ANKRD26 (supplemental Figure 4E). This action led to a considerable increase in cell proliferation (Figure 2E) and in the percentage of EdU-positive cells (Figure 2F; supplemental Figure 4F). In contrast, the knockdown of ANKRD26 with shANKRD26 blocked the promotion of cell proliferation and the percentage of EdU-positive cells (Figure 2E-F; supplemental Figure 4G-H). Again, when the ANKRD26 was interfered in K562, a human megakaryocytic cell line with a high expression level of intrinsic ANKRD26 (supplemental Figure 4I), its cell proliferation and the percentage of EdU-positive cells were significantly decreased (Figure 2G-H; supplemental Figure 4J). The increased proportion of MK lineage subsets in CD34+ HSPCs indicates that the defects of megakaryopoiesis in patients with THC2 were not at the HSPC stage. Hence, BM pMKs were enriched for further investigation (Figure 3A). After quality control and removal of the contamination of other cells (supplemental Figure 5; supplemental Table 3), 12 386 pMKs were obtained and divided them into 4 subpopulations based on Gene Ontology analysis and reported marker genes,13, 14, 15 termed MK1 to MK4, respectively (Figure 3B-D; Table 1; supplemental Table 4). Cytoplasmic translation genes, such as RPS27L and RPL31, were enriched in MK4, indicating that cells in MK4 are undergoing active protein synthesis (Figure 3C-D). To explore why a comparable constitution of pMK subsets did not produce similar amounts of platelets in patients with THC2, we checked the expression level of the gene ANKRD26 in each pMK subset. All subsets showed a higher average expression level of ANKRD26 in patients with THC2, and the difference in MK3, the platelet-producing MKs, was significant (Figure 4A). Then, DEGs with more than twofold change in MK3 cells were further examined (Figure 4B; supplemental Table 5). We found that upregulated genes in THC2-MK3 were mainly enriched in “response to virus” and “regulation of T-cell activation” (Figure 4C-D), although no signs of infection were present in any of the patients at the time of sample collection. These genes, including CXCL10, CXCL11, and HES1 (Figure 4B), were different from those marker genes in MK2, the immune MK subset, which included S100A8 and S100A9 (Figure 3D). Notably, S100A8 and S100A9 were significantly downregulated in THC2-MK3 (Figure 4B). Besides, the apoptosis-related pathways were enriched in THC2-MK3 (Figure 4C-D). Lots of apoptosis genes, such as BAX, GADD45A, GADD45B, GADD45G, CASP8, and CASP10, were upregulated in THC2-MK3 (Figure 4E). Genes enriched in DNA replication and mismatch repair were downregulated, and the cell cycle was blocked at the G1/S phase transition (Figure 4C-D). It was reported that a high level of p21 in cultured MKs arrest the endomitotic cell cycle and decrease polyploidy level.20 To investigate this possibility, we meticulously evaluated the polyploidy level of THC2-pMKs. Small MKs with hypolobulated nuclei were observed in the THC2 BM aspirate smear and biopsy (Figure 5A-B). The diameter of THC2-pMKs was significantly smaller than control-pMKs (Figure 5C-E). In the control group, the predominant polyploidy class of MKs was 16N. However, THC2 samples exhibited an exceedingly small number of 16N cells (Figure 5F-G). Moreover, the percentage of polyploid MKs (N ≥ 8) and polyploidy level (N ≥ 8) in THC2-pMKs was approximately half that of the normal counterparts (Figure 5H). The mean fluorescence intensity of forward scatter and CD41 was significantly decreased in THC2-pMKs (Figure 5I). Centrosome-located ANKRD26 was also observed in ANKRD26-overexpressing HEL cells (Figure 6H). Upon PMA stimulation, we also observed an increased expression of CDKN1A mRNA and p21 protein in ANKRD26-overexpressed HEL cells (Figure 6I-J). The top 20 positively correlated TFs were further checked with TFs targeting CDKN1A identified in BM cells from 2 TF databases (hTFtarget and Cistrome DB; supplemental Table 7). JUNB was identified as the most potent TF for positive regulation of CDKN1A transcription (Figure 7A). The expression of JUNB was positively correlated with CDKN1A in CDKN1A-expressing THC2-MK3 cells (Figure 7B). Our single-cell transcriptomic analysis of patients with THC2, including both CD34+ HSPCs and pMKs, reveals that the ANKRD26 5' UTR mutation causes its overexpression throughout megakaryopoiesis, from HSPCs (MPP, CMP, MEP, and MkP) to mature MKs (pMKs). However, ANKRD26 exerted different effects on pMKs and MkP. ANKRD26 enhances MkP proliferation, whereas it triggers apoptosis in polyploid pMKs via JUNB-p21, causing thrombocytopenia through selective loss of high-ploidy MKs. Although ANKRD26 localizes to centrosomes and increases p21 in THC2-pMKs, apoptosis occurs independently of the PIDDosome-p53 pathway due to p53 deficiency. These findings elucidate the molecular basis of defective megakaryopoiesis in THC2 and establish a valuable resource for future research.

These data sets fill a gap in prior knowledge in the literature. Because the lack of a specific mouse model and the difficulty of getting the rare and fragile MKs, investigations of THC2 at an in vivo and ex vivo level have been limited.16 However, in vitro cultured MKs behaved differently from in vivo MKs. Mature MKs can attain 128N in vivo and release thousands of platelets per cell, whereas in vitro cultured mature MKs rarely reach 64N and produce <100 platelets per cell.22 MKs derived from an in vitro culture produce platelets by forming proplatelets, whereas in vivo MKs mainly undergo membrane budding.23 Our ex vivo analysis reveals significantly reduced polyploidy in the pMKs of patients with THC2, which was overlooked in earlier in vitro studies using cultured MKs.9 Considering the importance of polyploid MKs in platelet production,24 the dramatically decreased polyploidy level might be another potential mechanism for developing THC2. Previous flow cytometry analyses of in vitro–differentiated MKs failed to detect ploidy differences between patients with THC2 and healthy controls, potentially due to the inappropriate cell type used.9 These studies also overlooked the increased apoptosis in THC2-pMKs.9 Failure of high polyploid level formation is also an efficiency-limiting factor for in vitro manufacturing of induced pluripotent stem cells–derived platelets.25, 26, 27 Our study reminded us that ANKRD26 and p21 might be targets for improving the polyploidy level of induced pluripotent stem cell-derived MKs, which is worth further investigation.

The scRNA-seq has been widely used in decoding cell heterogeneity during hematopoiesis,28,29 including MKs13, 14, 15; however, none of these studies related to patients with THC2.19,30 Some researchers considered that polyploid MKs, which have a diameter of up to 100 μm, cannot be captured by the 10x Genomics platform.13,15 However, the predominant polyploidy class of normal MKs was 16N (Figure 5G).13 MKs with higher ploidy levels than 16N are rare and fragile in BM. Before 10x Genomics processing, we measured pMK diameters in all samples. Both THC2 and control pMKs measured <50 μm, with only rare control cells exceeding 40 μm (Figure 5C-D). Mean diameters were <30 μm (control) and <20 μm (THC2; Figure 5E). Moreover, live MKs exhibited sufficient deformability to traverse 10x microfluidic channels without causing device obstruction. The 10x platform eliminated the need for high-purity MK enrichment required by Smart-Seq2,13,15 as contaminants could be bioinformatically removed (supplemental Figure 5). This approach prevented FACS-induced damage to fragile polyploid cells (Figure 3A). To better recapitulate the constitution of BM pMKs, we also enriched the small pMKs by FACS and mixed all the pMKs for analysis (Figure 3A). Most pMKs exhibited <10% mitochondrial gene expression, confirming cell viability (supplemental Figure 5H). Our study provided valuable experience for future research on other platelet-related diseases by using pMKs for scRNA-seq.

In this study, ANKRD26 overexpression enhanced MkP and HEL cell proliferation, consistent with reports that dysregulated ANKRD26 disrupts the proliferation/differentiation balance of myeloid progenitors.10 An increased number of BM MKs and blood neutrophils is common in patients with THC2,8 whereas erythrocyte/hemoglobin levels remain stable unless concurrent hemoglobinopathy exists.4,8 We detected significantly higher expression of ANKRD26 in MPP, CMP, MEP, MkP, and pMK subsets (Figures 2A and 4A). We also found that the proportion of myeloid lineages, except erythrocyte progenitors, was increased in CD34+ HSPCs (Figure 1G). However, we did not find the expression of ANKRD26 significantly changed in the NeuP (Figure 2A). Why the neutrophil development was also affected remains unknown. We speculated that ANKRD26 might alter the differentiation bias of CD34+ HSPCs. The mechanism underlying ANKRD26's role in granulocyte differentiation, whether direct or indirect, requires further elucidation.

ANKRD26 subcellular localization remains debated. Although exogenous expression in Ba/F3 and UT7 cells showed membrane localization,10 studies in A549, HEK293T, and RPE1 cells demonstrated centrosomal localization.11,12 A recent study demonstrated that exogenous ANKRD26 localizes to HEK293T cell membranes as a retinoic acid–responsive protein, with its N-terminal domain (1-217 amino acids [aa]) being indispensable for membrane binding and shaping.31 Membrane-localized ANKRD26 in patients with THC2 disrupts thrombopoietin receptor internalization, maintaining surface receptor levels.10 Under high thrombopoietin conditions, this leads to ERK pathway hyperactivation and impaired proplatelet formation.9,10 However, a WAC::ANKRD26 fusion mutation causing persistent expression of N-terminally truncated ANKRD26 (Δ1-402 aa) in MKs was associated with thrombocytopenia in one family.32 Similarly, an ACBD5::ANKRD26 fusion transcript expressing Δ1-521 aa ANKRD26 led to thrombocytopenia in another family.33 These findings reinforce that N-terminal truncation disrupts subcellular localization. We propose that the membrane-localized ANKRD26 may not drive thrombocytopenia in THC2, as our analysis of patient pMKs and HEL overexpression models revealed exclusive centrosomal localization (Figure 6A,H). This contrasts with prior studies limited to engineered cell lines, establishing the first direct evidence in unmodified patient cells. The distinct subcellular localizations of ANKRD26 may mediate diverse biological functions. In particular, the regulatory mechanism by which centrosomal ANKRD26 modulates AP-1 transcriptional activity warrants further investigation.

Tumor cell studies suggest supernumerary centrosomes activate the PIDDosome-p53-p21 pathway to induce polyploid cell apoptosis, a mechanism proposed to underlie the low ploidy levels in patients with THC2.11,12 Our findings challenge the proposed PIDDosome-p53-p21 pathway in THC2 pathogenesis, as the key regulatory factor p53 is low in pMKs (Figure 6). Instead, we identified JUNB as the upstream regulator driving p21 overexpression in THC2-MKs, confirmed by ChIP-seq and luciferase assays. Functional studies demonstrated that JUNB or p21 knockdown rescued ANKRD26-mediated apoptosis in polyploid HEL cells (Figure 6K-M and Figure 7I-K), establishing this alternative pathway as the molecular mechanism underlying MKs apoptosis in THC2. However, the mechanism linking centrosomal ANKRD26 to JUNB upregulation remains to be elucidated.

In summary, to our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically investigate the changes in BM CD34+ HSPCs and pMKs from patients with THC2 at single-cell resolution, providing new insights into the impaired megakaryopoiesis of THC2. Although the study is limited by the relatively small cohort size and the exclusive inclusion of individuals of Chinese ancestry. These factors may affect the generalizability of our results. Future studies should aim to validate these observations in larger, ethnically diverse populations to establish broader clinical relevance. The distinct subcellular localizations of ANKRD26 may mediate diverse biological functions. In particular, elucidating how centrosomal ANKRD26 regulates AP-1 transcriptional activity will refine understanding of megakaryopoietic control. Furthermore, the observation that ANKRD26 dysregulation influences myeloid lineage bias prompts future work on granulopoietic differentiation. These directions will inform both disease mechanism and potential therapeutic innovation.
