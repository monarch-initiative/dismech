# Autopsy Findings in AGTPBP1 Gene-Related Infantile Neurodegeneration: A Case Report and Review of Literature.
**Authors:** Ambalavanan N, Singh S, Sankhyan N, Gupta A, Chatterjee D
**Journal:** Case Rep Neurol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000551757](https://doi.org/10.1159/000551757)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Homozygous mutations in the AGTPBP1 gene are associated with childhood-onset neurodegeneration and cerebellar atrophy (CONDCA). These mutations disrupt neuronal maintenance, leading to progressive motor and cognitive deficits. This case highlights the pathological findings and systemic complications in a 4.5-month-old boy with this rare genetic disorder.

CASE REPORT: A 4.5-month-old boy presented with global developmental delay and progressively worsening floppiness of the body for the past 2 months. There was a history of similar illness in his previous sibling, who died at 2.5 months of age. The serum creatine kinase of the index child was mildly elevated. Antemortem muscle biopsy had revealed the presence of neurogenic atrophy. Peripheral blood count revealed persistent lymphopenia (ALC range: 1,270/μL to 2,482/μL). The child developed severe respiratory distress and succumbed to his illness at 5 months of age. The autopsy revealed the atrophy of cerebellar folia with a striking reduction of Purkinje and granular cells and preserved molecular layer. There was thinning of the corpus callosum and anterior horn cell degeneration in the spinal cord. Lung examination demonstrated fibrinous bronchitis, bronchiolitis, and pneumonia; a result of adenovirus infection confirmed by electron microscopy and PCR. The thymus was absent. Genetic testing identified a homozygous mutation in the AGTPBP1 gene (c2833C>T), confirming the diagnosis of CONDCA.

CONCLUSION: This is the first autopsy description of CONDCA with detailed neuropathological evaluation. Although cerebellar atrophy is well known, this case reveals a wider neuropathological change and thymic aplasia in such patients. This case highlights the structural consequences of the AGTPBP1 gene-associated enzyme deficiency crucial for post-translational modifications of tubulin, resulting in the degeneration of specific sets of neurons and immune deficiency secondary thymic involvement.

Childhood-onset neurodegeneration with cerebellar atrophy (CONDCA; OMIM 618276) is a recently described, rare, and severe autosomal recessive disease that affects the central and peripheral nervous systems. Affected children present with early-onset global developmental delay with cognitive and motor decline [1–3]. Several genes have been associated with the development of CONDCA, including AGTPBP1, EXOSC3, TSEN2, TSEN34, TSEN54, SNX14, SYNE1, CASK, TUBB2B, MSI1, VLDLR, GRID2, LAMA1, REEP1, and ATM [4], etc. These genes are involved in the neuronal migration, RNA metabolism, protein homeostasis which are crucial for cerebellar function and development. The ATP/GTP-binding protein 1 (AGTPBP1) gene (OMIM 606830) catalyzes the deglutamylation of polyglutamylated proteins. Its deficiency leads to cerebellar ataxia, peripheral neuropathy in mice, and a lower motor neuron-like disease in sheep. In mutant mice, cerebellar atrophy resulting from Purkinje cell degeneration is observed, likely due to increased tubulin polyglutamylation in the affected brain regions [3]. The disease phenotype in humans has not been completely understood due to its rarity. Most of the pathological changes in CONDCA have been described in mouse models. To our knowledge, this report is the first description of autopsy findings in an infant with this rare neurodegeneration. This report adheres to the CARE Case Report guidelines; the completed checklist is provided as online supplementary File 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551757).

A 4.5-month-old male infant, born at term with a birth weight of 2,750 grams, had an uneventful perinatal period. The child was breastfed from birth and exhibited normal developmental milestones, including adequate suck and swallow, social smiling, and head control, till 2.5 months of age. Thereafter, the parents observed a gradual decline in abilities characterized by thinning of the muscles, reduced limb movements, diminished interaction, poor object tracking, a weak cry, and the appearance of a squint. The child developed respiratory difficulty with excessive oral secretions prompting consultation and admission to hospital. The family history was notable for consanguinity (uncle-niece marriage), along with the history of similar illness in his elder brother, who died at 2.5 months of age. However, there was no known history of neuromuscular disorders in other family members.

On anthropometric evaluation, the infant weighed 4 kg (expected weight: 5.5 kg), with an occipitofrontal circumference of 38 cm (expected: 42 cm) and a length of 60 cm. Examination revealed a thin-built, lethargic baby, with weak suck, pooling of oral secretions, a frog-like posture, and right convergent squint. He had decreased muscle bulk and tone with reduced muscle power involving both the proximal and distal muscle groups (MRC grade 0–1/5). Deep tendon reflexes were absent with extensor plantar response. There was no evidence of facial weakness or oral or tongue fasciculations.

On investigations, his total leukocyte counts varied between 12,700 and 14,600/μL with low absolute lymphocyte counts ranging from 1,270/μL to 2,482/μL (Normal - >4,500/μL). His blood ammonia, lactate, prolactin, and biotinidase levels were in normal range. The serum creatine kinase being mildly elevated (333 U/L). Magnetic resonance imaging of brain did not reveal any significant abnormalities except thinning of the corpus callosum. He developed a pneumonia and subsequently required a tracheostomy. On day 20 of hospitalization, he deteriorated and succumbed to his illness. An autopsy was performed to understand the cause of death.

A complete autopsy was performed on this child. External examination revealed an infant who appeared malnourished, with wasting. There were no external abnormalities, injuries, or dysmorphic facies to suggest any evidence of congenital anomalies.

Postmortem muscle biopsy showed numerous angulated atrophic fibres. There were some foci showing group atrophy. Isolated muscle fibres showed hypertrophic changes. There were no internalized nuclei, no subsarcolemmal collection, and no rimmed vacuoles. Modified Gomori trichrome stain did not highlight any nemaline rod or inclusions. There was prominence of type 1 fibres on NADH stain (Fig. 1). Overall features were of neurogenic atrophy.

Brain (582 g) showed atrophic changes along with dilatation of the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles. The corpus callosum was thinned out (Fig. 2). The cerebellum appeared grossly unremarkable.

Sections from the spinal cord showed loss of neurons in the anterior horn, associated with reactive gliosis. The cerebellum showed normal-sized folia with a marked loss of Purkinje cells. There was a prominence of Bergmann glial cells. NeuN immunohistochemistry did not reveal any Purkinje cells. There was also variable loss of granular layer. The molecular layer was however preserved (Fig. 3).

Postmortem coronal slices of the brain showing dilatation of the lateral ventricles, thinning of the corpus callosum, and cerebellar atrophy.

The sections from the corpus callosum showed thinned out corpus callosum with marked loss of myelin. The sections from the cortex or thalamus did not reveal any significant neuronal loss.

Lungs (176 g) were heavy and solid in appearance. The cut surface was discolored and showed multiple foci of hemorrhage. Microscopic examination revealed extensive ulceration of the tracheobronchial tree. There was fibrinous bronchitis and bronchiolitis with presence of extensive nuclear debris. The alveolar spaces were also filled with fibrinous debris. Many intranuclear viral inclusions were identified in the pneumocytes, which were both eosinophilic as well as basophilic. On electron microscopy, it showed lattice like arrangement of virions approximately 70 nm size, conforming to the morphology of adenovirus virions (Fig. 4). The lung tissue was also subjected to real time PCR which showed positivity for adenovirus. In addition, there were foci of bronchopneumonia.

The liver was grossly unremarkable. Microscopy showed micro and macrovesicular steatosis. There was a prominence of Kupffer cells with hemophagocytosis. The spleen and pancreas were microscopically unremarkable. Kidneys showed normal glomeruli. There was evidence of pigment cast nephropathy. Bone marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis with evidence of hemophagocytosis.

The thymus could not be identified grossly. Anterior mediastinal soft tissue was extensively sampled for histological examination and did not reveal any remnant of thymus.

The summary of findings in this child includes neurogenic atrophy in skeletal muscles, dysgenesis of the corpus callosum, cerebellar folia atrophy with marked reduction of Purkinje cells, anterior horn cell degeneration in the spinal cord, and absent thymus. The child also developed adenovirus pneumonitis and secondary hemophagocytosis.

An antemortem blood sample was sent for next generation exome sequencing, the results of which were available after the death of the child. The analysis revealed a homozygous nonsense variation in the AGTPBP1 gene, which is associated with CONDCA. The identified variant, c.2833C>T, is not present in publicly available population databases such as 1000 Genomes, ExAC, EVS, or the Indian Exome Database. This variant has not been previously described in the literature or clinical databases. Genetic analysis of both parents revealed heterozygous carrier state for chr9:c.2833C>T, (p.Gln945Ter) variant in the AGTPBP1 gene. A diagnosis of CONDCA associated with AGTPBP1 gene was concluded.

This case highlights the fatal clinical course in a child with a novel AGTPBP1 gene mutation, corroborated by autopsy findings. The AGTPBP1 gene encodes cytosolic carboxypeptidase 1, an enzyme crucial for post-translational modifications of tubulin through detyrosination, a process vital for maintaining neuronal integrity and axonal stability, particularly in the central nervous system. Mutations in AGTPBP1 are associated with autosomal recessive cerebellar-onset neurodegeneration with or without cerebellar atrophy (CONDCA), characterized by progressive neurological deterioration, hypotonia, intellectual disability, and motor dysfunction. In addition to its role in the nervous system, AGTPBP1 contributes to the maintenance of microtubule networks in non-neuronal tissues, which may explain the systemic manifestations in affected patients. The clinical information about this rare entity is based on isolated case reports and animal models. To our knowledge, no detailed pathological evaluation or autopsy report exists for this entity.

The child in this case exhibited hallmark phenotypic features of AGTPBP1 mutations (Table 1), including motor unit hypotonia, global developmental delay, and cerebellar atrophy with significant Purkinje cell loss. This aligns with previous reports by Shashi et al. [1] and Ashaat et al. [5], who described similar cerebellar atrophy and neurodegeneration. However, cerebellar dysfunction was the most prominent clinical feature in those cases. Turay et al. [6] reported a case of AGTPBP1 mutation, which did not display cerebellar atrophy and not fitting under CONDCA category of neurodegenerative disorders. Our case demonstrates more extensive systemic involvement, including anterior horn cell degeneration and neurogenic atrophy in the skeletal muscles, which has not been as frequently highlighted in the literature. A study by Sheffer et al. [3] also observed progressive motor function loss in individuals with AGTPBP1 mutations, corresponding with Purkinje cell degeneration, further supporting our findings.

Summary of AGTPBP1 gene functions and associated disorders

The index case presented with floppy infant syndrome in early age. Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is the prototype disease associated with such presentation. SMA results in progressive degeneration of anterior horn cells in spinal cord and cause neurogenic atrophy of the skeletal muscles. More than 95% of SMA cases are due to mutation in SMN1 gene in chromosome 5q. Minor proportion of SMA are due to mutation of other genes, clubbed as non-5q SMA. Non-5q SMA has been associated with other genetic mutations, and AGTPBP1 has been implicated as one of them [10]. Thus, cases of AGTPBP1 gene mutation-associated neurodegeneration have been described as SMA in the literature, as CONDCA phenotype has been described more recently, and all cases may not have features of upper motor neuron dysfunction. Only targeted sequencing of SMN1 gene may miss these rare genetic alterations. Detailed genetic alterations like whole exome sequencing can provide information regarding the underlying genetic variants. Additionally, this case uniquely presented with thinning of the corpus callosum, a feature less commonly reported, suggesting a broader impact on axonal development beyond the cerebellum. In addition to neurodegeneration involving the cerebellum and the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord, the index case also demonstrated the absence of thymus. The lymphopenia suggests that the disorder may be causing a primary immunodeficiency (PID) in the affected children. AGTPBP1 gene has been shown to be essential for T-cell development in zebrafish [11]. A lymphocyte subset analysis could not be performed as PID was not suspected during life. The whole exome sequencing also did not reveal any other genetic variants linked to known PIDs. This child also showed extensive adenovirus pneumonitis and bronchopneumonia, which could be a manifestation of the underlying immunodeficiency.

The AGTPBP1 mutation identified in our patient (c.2833C>T) is novel, with no prior reports in population databases or studies. Shashi et al. [1] found that nonsense mutations often lead to earlier and more severe presentations than missense mutations, consistent with the severe phenotype observed here.

Currently, AGTPBP1-related disorders are managed symptomatically, with no targeted therapies available. However, research into restoring tubulin detyrosination may offer future therapeutic options. However, accurate diagnosis and differentiation from SMA is essential, as gene therapy is available for the later. This case emphasizes the importance of early genetic diagnosis and comprehensive phenotyping, critical for advancing treatment strategies for these rare disorders. This case report for the first time describes the autopsy findings of a child with a novel AGTPBP1 nonsense mutation, broadening the phenotypic spectrum of CONDCA. Notable findings include cerebellar atrophy, corpus callosum dysgenesis, anterior horn cell degeneration, and neurogenic muscle atrophy, highlighting the systemic and neurological effects of AGTPBP1 dysfunction. A novel finding was the absence of thymus, which may establish the possible link of this gene with thymic development. This case widens the spectrum of AGTPBP1-related disorders. Further studies are needed to refine genotype-phenotype correlations and explore potential therapeutic options for this rare condition.
