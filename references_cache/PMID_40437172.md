# Cluster analysis reveals the clinical spectrum of Erdheim-Chester disease.
**Authors:** Tesi M, Pegoraro F, Peyronel F, Emile JF, Catamerò F, Koster MJ, Goyal G, Collin M, Milne P, Boussouar S, Cohen-Aubart F, Papo M, Amoura Z, Estrada-Veras JI, O'Brien K, Razanamahery J, Goulabchand R, Idbaih A, de Menthon M, Gensous N, Aouba A, Ledoult E, Le Scornet T, Néel A, Go RS, Mazor RD, Campochiaro C, Dagna L, Diamond EL, Vaglio A, Haroche J
**Journal:** Leukemia (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41375-025-02656-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41375-025-02656-w)

## Content

Erdheim-Chester disease (ECD) is a clonal-inflammatory neoplasm driven by mutations in MAPK pathway proto-oncogenes, such as BRAF. Clinical manifestations are protean, affecting virtually every system. This cohort study analyzed 661 patients with ECD to classify them based on clinical features and mutational profiles using unsupervised clustering. Nineteen clinical and mutational variables were subjected to hierarchical clustering combined with k-means. A three-cluster model emerged as the most stable. Most patients were classified according to key features, namely BRAFV600E mutation, and large-vessel, heart, and perirenal involvement. The "Widespread Disease" (WID) cluster (320 patients, 49%) was associated with the presence of the key features and the "Limited Disease" (LIM) cluster (282 patients, 42%) was associated with their absence. The "MAP2K1-RDD" cluster (MAP) was assigned 59 patients (9%), based on MAP2K1 mutation and/or overlapping Rosai-Dorfman-Destombes disease (RDD). Survival analysis revealed worse outcomes for WID compared to LIM (hazard ratio 1.54, 95% CI 1.09-2.17), while no significant survival difference was found for MAP. The identification of these clusters, based on mutational profiles, organ involvement and overlapping conditions, offers a data-driven validation of established clinical observations. These findings substantiate the role of the somatic mutation type in shaping the ECD phenotype.

Erdheim–Chester disease (ECD) is a rare non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis characterized by tissue infiltration by foamy histiocytes [ 1 , 2 ]. This disease has a putative neoplastic and inflammatory nature: it is thought to be driven by mutations in proto-oncogenes such as BRAF, while immune-mediated mechanisms contribute to disease development and progression. Mutations activating the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway are found in more than 80% of patients with ECD, mainly the BRAFV600E activating mutation — in 57% to 70% of cases — followed by MAP2K1 mutations in about 20% [ 1 , 2 ]. The discovery of such mutations as well as the co-occurrence of Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis (LCH) in around 15% of patients with ECD, led to the 2016 revision [ 3 ] of the classification of histiocytoses in which LCH and ECD belong to the same “L”(Langerhans) group, as both conditions are considered inflammatory myeloid neoplasms. An overlap between ECD and Rosai-Dorfman-Destombes disease (RDD) has also been described [ 4 ]. Around 10% of ECD cases are associated with myeloproliferative neoplasms and/or myelodysplastic syndromes.

The clinical presentation of ECD is therefore protean, ranging from smoldering unifocal presentations to a multiorgan life-threatening disease. Almost any organ can be involved, but the most common lesions include long-bone involvement (80–95%) [ 5 ], retroperitoneal fibrosis, interstitial lung disease, pericardial and myocardial infiltration, central nervous system (CNS), retro-orbital, and large-vessel involvement. These manifestations may mimic those of other histiocytoses and of neoplastic and systemic immune-mediated diseases.

Recent research [ 4 , 6– 11 ] has established a correlation between specific somatic mutations and the resulting phenotypes in ECD. Although a particular mutation may be associated with characteristic clinical presentations, the pathogenic mechanisms underlying this link remain poorly understood and insufficient to explain the wide variability of disease manifestations. Given this complexity, we hypothesized that what is currently classified as a single disease may actually consist of distinct clinical entities with different biological origins. Therefore, we employed an unbiased approach, namely cluster analysis, to explore this heterogeneity.

Cluster analysis is a data-driven unsupervised statistical method whereby objects are divided into groups based on their similarity. In medical research, cluster analysis has been used to identify phenotypic subgroups of patients in various diseases [ 12– 15 ]. In the present study we used a cluster analysis approach and successfully identified distinct and clinically meaningful subgroups within a large cohort of patients with ECD, each defined by unique combinations of organ involvement and mutational patterns, which effectively recapitulate the disease’s variability.

Patients with ECD from nine centers were included. Smoking, diabetes mellitus, obesity, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, and coronary artery disease were recorded as cardiovascular risk features. Organ involvement at any time included: long bones, central nervous system (CNS) (except hypothalamus/pituitary), hypothalamus or pituitary gland, face or orbits, heart, large vessels, lungs, perirenal region, testes, lymph nodes, digestive tract and other systems. The overlapping diseases included LCH, RDD, and hematologic malignancy. BRAFV600E was reported as well as mutations in MAP2K1 and other relevant genes. The treatment data was structured into three chronological lines of therapy, each with the respective response assessed as previously described [ 10 ]. Overall follow-up duration and the status at the last follow-up (alive/dead) were collected. Organ pattern, mutational status, and overlapping disorders were selected as nineteen binary input variables for the cluster analysis. The rate of missing (or inconsistent) values was computed record-wise and variable-wise. Records having more than 40% missing values across the overall variables or more than 30% across input variables were excluded from any further analysis (extended version on Supplementary Material).

Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA), a variant of principal component analysis tailored on categorical variables, was performed on the input dataset of nineteen binary variables [ 16 ]. MCA maps the original observations on a lower-dimensional space while minimizing the loss of variance, which in general results indirectly in maintaining the pairwise similarities between observations as much as possible. MCA was used before cluster analysis because it reduces the dimensionality, provides a distance measure between categorical observations, and handles potential collinearity. The number of retained principal components was determined through cross-validation [ 17 ] to capture enough structure in the data without overfitting, and then also used in the imputation of missing data. The imputation algorithm is an Expectation Maximization (EM)-type algorithm [ 18 ], whereby missing values are filled in with the expected values during the Expectation step and the axes and components are then obtained during the Maximization step. The imputation algorithm iterates between the Expectation and Maximization steps until convergence. A regularized version of the algorithm was used to avoid overfitting.

As the clustering algorithm, a combination of agglomerative hierarchical clustering (with Ward’s method) [ 19 ] and k-means clustering [ 20 ] was used. In agglomerative hierarchical clustering, each MCA-transformed observation is initialized as a one-element cluster. The pairwise Euclidean distances between clusters are computed. Ward’s criterion was used for merging the clusters: the two clusters which result in the smallest increase in within-cluster variance when combined are merged. The distances are recalculated between the newly formed cluster and the remaining clusters. The process of cluster merging and distance update is repeated iteratively. This iteration can theoretically repeat until all observations belong to the same all-encompassing cluster, and the number of steps the process is stopped before the end determines the number of clusters in the model. This process can be plotted as a dendrogram, with a horizontal line corresponding to the particular step at which the iterative merging was stopped before the end. To inform this decision, i.e. where to cut the dendrogram, the heuristic criterion of inter-cluster variance gain was used. This criterion suggests a division into Q clusters when the increase of inter-cluster variance between Q − 1 and Q clusters is much greater than the one between Q and Q + 1 clusters. Several models with different cluster numbers were tested. In each case, the centroids of the resulting clusters were set as the cluster centroids for k-means clustering. In the k-means algorithm, once cluster centroids are initialized, each observation is assigned to the nearest cluster centroid. The cluster centroids are updated based on the mean of all observations assigned to each cluster. The process of assignment and centroid update is repeated iteratively until convergence.

To compare the tested models and identify the one with the optimal cluster number, and to assess whether the obtained clusters were stable and therefore meaningful, a cluster-wise stability measure was used. Briefly, a bootstrap sample is drawn from the original data set, and the clustering algorithm is run on the bootstrap sample. For each resulting cluster, the ratio is computed between the number of patients that are correctly assigned to their cluster (“intersection”) and the number of patients that are assigned to the same cluster by either the original clustering or the bootstrap clustering (“union”). This ratio (intersection over union) is called cluster-wise Jaccard similarity index. This process was repeated 2000 times and the Jaccard index was averaged over the iterations. A stable cluster should yield a Jaccard index greater than 0·75, while a cluster yielding a Jaccard index greater than 0·6 is not stable but still indicates a pattern in the data and holds some significance [ 21 ]. The optimal number of clusters was chosen which yielded the highest cluster-wise Jaccard indexes.

To identify which input variables drove the clustering, a Chi-squared test (Fisher’s exact test in case any expected cell count <5) was performed for each input variable to test the association with cluster membership. In order to characterize the obtained clusters, associations between cluster membership and positive/negative categories of input variables were tested. For each variable, this was achieved by employing the hypergeometric distribution to model the probability of observing the distribution of positive (and negative) cases within a given cluster. The significance of the association was further quantified using the v-test, which converts the p-value from the hypergeometric test into a test statistic following approximately a standard normal distribution. Positive and negative values of the v-test indicate an over-represented and under-represented category in a cluster, respectively.

A classification tree based on the input variables with the strongest cluster association was manually built to approximately reproduce the obtained clusters with the best possible accuracy of classification and with as few sequential heuristic criteria as possible (principle of model parsimony).

In addition to the nineteen input variables, other phenotypic characteristics are presented in cluster-wise summary form. The cardiovascular risk was calculated as a score for every patient by adding one point for the presence of each of the cardiovascular risk variables. Summary statistics are presented as a count of positive cases and percentages (excluding missing data) for binary variables, and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for numeric variables.

Survival times were calculated as the time from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or to the date of last contact. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate the death hazard ratio between clusters (extended version on Supplementary Material).

An experimental approach was followed to further characterize the obtained clusters, limited to the clusters that showed high stability as measured through the Jaccard index. This approach consisted of performing a new cluster analysis only on the observations assigned to the cluster to be investigated. The rationale behind this approach is that a cluster that yields a high stability score can be considered as a well-defined entity in its own right, and therefore it’s worth being further stratified through a cluster analysis. In this sub-cluster analysis (or sub-clustering), the same methodology was used as for the parent cluster analysis.

All statistical analyses were made with R, version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The FactoMineR package [ 22 ] was used for multiple correspondence analysis, imputation of missing data, and clustering. A p-value ≤ 0·05 was considered statistically significant. The code is available at: https://github.com/miki-tesi/ECD-clusters/.

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. Subjects participating to this study signed a written informed consent, as well as subjects whose photos were published within this work.

A total of 694 patients with ECD from nine participating centers were considered for study inclusion. After data quality screening, 661 patients were included, namely 402 from Pitié-Salpêtrière hospital, Paris, France, 64 from Meyer Children’s Hospital IRCCS, Florence, Italy, 63 from San Raffaele Hospital, Milano, Italy, 53 from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, New York, NY, USA, 48 from National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA, 12 from Newcastle Upon Tyne Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK, eight from Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, USA, six from Assuta Medical Centre, Tel Aviv, Israel, 5 from University of Alabama at Birmingham, Birmingham, AL, USA (Fig. S1). The average and maximum rate of missing entries by patient record were 8% and 34% across the overall variables, and 0·8% and 26% across the input variables, respectively. The rate of missing entries by variable was lower than 0·3% for all input variables except those related to mutational status (BRAF, 8%; MAP2K1, 3%; other genes, 4%).

Table 1 summarizes population characteristics. The median follow-up was 4·3 (IQR 1·8 – 7·3) years, and the median age at diagnosis was 58 years (IQR 46 – 66), with 157 (24%) deaths. There were 460 men (70%) and 201 women (30%). Long-bone involvement was most frequent (n=558, 85%), followed by perirenal (n=442, 67%), and large-vessel (n=340, 52%) infiltration. Representative radiological and histological findings are shown in Fig. S2. Overlapping conditions included LCH (n=82, 12%), RDD (n=18, 3%), and hematologic malignancies (n=57, 9%). The BRAFV600E mutation was found in 376 (62%) patients, 45 (7%) patients had MAP2K1 mutations, and 58 (9%) other mutations such as KRAS, NRAS, TET2, JAK2, DNMT3A and BRAF non-V600E.

The dataset was reduced to four dimensions through MCA, with imputation of missing data. Several models with different cluster numbers were tested. As shown in the dendrograms in Fig. 1A, a three-cluster model was obtained, in keeping with the heuristic criterion of inter-cluster variance gain (Fig. 1B) as well as the cluster stability measure. The obtained average Jaccard indexes, namely 0·87, 0·81, and 0·65 for the clusters that were later labeled WID, LIM, and MAP respectively, indicate stable clusters (Fig. 1C). Table 1 shows the phenotypic characteristics and follow-up data within each cluster. Fig. 2A shows a dot plot depicting the frequency of disease features by cluster and in the overall patient population, while Fig. 2B displays an association heatmap showing the association between the disease features and each cluster. Fig. S3 shows a radar chart for each cluster, which depicts the frequency of each disease feature within the cluster. Fig. 3 is a visual summary of the clusters. Fig. 4 is a classification tree which approximates the clustering outcome with only two heuristic rules.

The majority of patients (602, 91% of the overall patient population) are classified in the two main clusters according to some key features, the most important being the BRAFV600E mutation and the large-vessel, heart, and perirenal involvement. However, long-bone, facial/orbits, CNS, lung, skin and hypothalamic/pituitary involvement are also discriminant features, although with less significance (Table 1, Fig. 2, Fig. S3, Fig. 3). The more key features the patient exhibits, the higher the probability that the patient is assigned to the “Widespread disease” (WID) cluster (n=320, 48%) than to the “Limited disease” (LIM) cluster (n=282, 43%). The median number of affected organs is 5 (IQR 5 – 7) in WID as opposed to 3 (IQR 2 – 4) in LIM. Patients in WID are also generally older at diagnosis (59 years, IQR 49 – 67) while patients in LIM are younger (54 years, IQR 43 – 64).

The third cluster (n=59, 9%) was strongly associated with the MAP2K1 mutation, after which the cluster was named “MAP”, and RDD (all 18 RDD cases were in this cluster). Furthermore, by decreasing order of significance, this cluster is also characterized by testicular involvement, relatively low rate of long-bone, and CNS involvement, and relatively high rate of other mutations, digestive tract, and lymph node involvement (Table 1, Fig. 2, Fig. S3, Fig. 3). Patients in this cluster are significantly older at diagnosis (65 years, IQR 49 – 71), and are more often male (80%).

Treatment frequencies and responses by cluster and in the overall cohort are summarised in Table S1. No significant difference was observed in treatment frequencies between clusters in the case of interferon α (IFNα) and interleukin 1 receptor antagonists (IL-1RA), while it was expectedly observed for BRAF Inhibitor (BRAF-i) and MEK Inhibitor (MEK-i), as these therapies are prescribed based on the mutational profile, which is strongly associated with the clusters. BRAF-i response was significantly different between the clusters. Namely, WID patients had a response rate of 77% while LIM patients only 57%.

The mortality and the median follow-up in the overall cohort and for each cluster are shown in Table 1. In Cox regression, the hazard ratio for death between the WID and the LIM cluster was estimated at 1·54 (95% Confidence Interval is 1·09 – 2·17), with LIM set as the reference group due to lower mortality. On the other hand, no significant survival difference was observed between MAP and the other clusters, possibly due to its small number. Fig. 5 shows the Kaplan-Meier curves for each cluster (only for visualization purposes) and reports the WID-to-LIM death hazard ratio. As the WID and LIM clusters substantially differed in terms of number of organs involved, an additional Cox model was fitted by including the number of organs as a covariate besides cluster membership. The effect of the organ count on survival was not significant in this analysis, while cluster membership retained a significant effect (WID-to-LIM adjusted hazard ratio 1.65, 95% CI 1.03–2.62).

The WID cluster (320 patients) underwent sub-clustering using the same methodology, except for MAP2K1 mutation and the RDD-overlap variables, as there were no patients with these characteristics. A three-cluster model emerged as the most stable, as shown in Table S2 and in Fig. 6, where the subpanel 6A depicts the dendrograms and the Jaccard indexes for each cluster, 6B the dot-plot of the frequency of disease features by cluster, and 6C the heatmap of the association between the variables and cluster membership. Sub-cluster 1 (n=110) is characterized by a higher rate of overlapping LCH, BRAFV600E, and of CNS and skin involvement and a lower rate of perirenal and cardiac involvement as compared to the overall WID cluster, whereas sub-cluster 2 (n=166) is hallmarked by the opposite. Sub-cluster 3 (44 patients) strongly associates with overlapping hematologic malignancy and with the involvement of digestive tract and lymph nodes (Table S1 and Figs. 6B and 6C), even though with a low Jaccard index, indicating instability.

Our cluster analysis of 661 patients with ECD identifies three distinct clusters based on clinical presentation and mutational status: the Widespread disease cluster (WID), the Limited disease cluster (LIM) and the MAP2K1/RDD-associated cluster (MAP). Most patients with ECD (91%) are distributed along a spectrum ranging from widespread (corresponding to the WID cluster) to limited disease (represented by the LIM cluster), with the frequency of BRAFV600E mutation and large-vessel, heart, and perirenal involvement being the key distinguishing features. The remaining 9% of patients, grouped in the MAP cluster, have a different phenotype, often associated with MAP2K1 mutation and RDD overlap (Fig. 3). An exploratory sub-clustering of the WID cluster alone brought up a phenotypic dichotomy encompassing a disease type with frequent LCH overlap, BRAFV600E, and CNS and skin involvement as opposed to another form of disease with higher rate of cardiac and perirenal involvement. The survival analysis showed a worse outcome for WID compared to LIM, whereas survival in MAP did not prove significantly different, possibly due to the limited size of this cluster. The death hazard ratio between WID and LIM was estimated at 1·54. This is arguably a small effect size if compared to the remarkable difference in disease extent between the two clusters, and considering the older age at diagnosis of the WID patients. However, it must be considered that therapy reasonably levels out survival, blurring the putative higher mortality of WID patients. In particular, WID patients had a higher frequency of BRAFV600E, and they were therefore more frequently treated with BRAF-i, which is known to improve the prognosis of ECD. Interestingly, WID patients also seemed to have a higher sensitivity (i.e., higher response rates) to BRAF-i than LIM patients.

Previous studies have explored the heterogeneity of ECD and other histiocytoses. Cohen-Aubart et al. [ 6 ] compared ECD patients harboring BRAFV600E with wild-type patients, showing that the former more frequently had cardiac involvement. Chazal et al. [ 7 ] stratified patients based on the presence of perirenal involvement, finding that this was significantly associated with large-vessel involvement, heart involvement and BRAFV600E. Razanamahery et al. [ 4 ] showed that 13 patients with ECD-RDD overlap exhibited an unexpectedly high frequency of MAP2K1 mutation (69%) while their BRAF status was wild-type. Twelve of them were male and seven had testicular infiltration by RDD. Kemps et al. [ 9 ] found that patients with an ALK rearrangement had a high prevalence of neurologic involvement, while Papo et al. [ 8 ] reported that patients with a BRAF in-frame deletion had a high prevalence of vulvar and hepatic manifestations. Finally, Pegoraro et al. [ 10 ] studied a cohort of patients with ECD-LCH overlap, which showed a higher frequency of BRAFV600E than ECD alone.

Altogether, these results along with the findings of our cluster analysis suggest that the somatic mutations play a role in shaping the histiocytosis phenotype. In particular, our study highlights the association of BRAFV600E with an ECD phenotype showing multi-organ involvement and preference for heart, large vessels and peri-kidney, with this mutation’s frequency rising even further in the case of LCH-overlap. In addition, we also detected a distinct ECD phenotype, mainly driven by MAP2K1, with frequent RDD overlap and testicular involvement. However, although this cluster analysis supports the mutation-phenotype link hypothesis, there is no exact one-to-one correspondence between a specific mutation and a particular phenotype. Furthermore, there are minor mutations, other than BRAFV600E and MAP2K1, that add up to the mutational landscape of histiocytosis [ 11 , 23 , 24 ]. So there must be other variables at play in shaping the disease phenotype apart from the type of mutation.

The current understanding of histiocytosis pathogenesis hints at the initiating cell, that is the first cell to undergo neoplastic mutation, as a key determinant of the resulting phenotype. Berres et al. in 2014 [ 25 ] proposed a hypothetical model of LCH in which the initiating cell, whether a hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell (HSPC) or a tissue-resident precursor, defines the extent of the disease. In 2017, two different studies [ 26 , 27 ] gathered evidence to demonstrate that ECD and LCH can originate from HSPCs. The same year, Mass et al. [ 28 ] explored a different hypothesis, grounded in the observation that several tissue-resident macrophage populations originate from yolk-sac-derived erythro-myeloid progenitors (EMPs) and are self-maintained independently of HSPCs [ 29 , 30 ], and found that BRAFV600E expression in HSPCs can cause a tumoral disease, but results in a neurodegenerative inflammatory disorder when expressed in EMPs. Overall, this evidence suggests that the clinical plasticity of histiocytoses may result not only from the type of mutation harbored by the neoplastic clone, but also from the initiating cell, whether a HSPC, a EMP or a differentiated histiocyte. This may provide a framework to conceptualize the clusters identified in the present study, and help understand the part of variability in histiocytosis that is not being explained by looking at the type of mutation only. This might be the case of patients who fell in the LIM cluster despite harboring BRAFV600E.

Our results may have clinical implications, which mainly pertain to disease staging and treatment. Patients displaying BRAFV600E may be at higher risk of a disseminated disease and thus may require extensive staging and prompt treatment. Interestingly, WID patients appear to have a higher sensitivity to BRAF-i, which encourages their use. Diagnostic investigations might also be tailored on the basis of the main cluster features: for example, patients with MAP2K1 mutations should be assessed for involvement of unusual sites like the testes, and treated appropriately (e.g., MEK-i). Our findings may also inform prognosis, since LIM patients have a prolonged survival as compared to WID patients.

Our study has limitations. Rare phenotypes may not emerge as distinct clusters due to small sizes. However, this study is distinguished by its unprecedented sample size, encompassing data from 661 patients with ECD, which supports the generalizability of its findings. Another constraint is that our data on somatic mutations other than BRAFV600E and MAP2K1 often doesn’t include the specific type of mutation. Nevertheless, cluster analysis as a data mining method relies on large trends, which means that any mutation reported only in a few cases would be useless. A further hindrance is that virtually all patients included in this analysis were treated with a variety of therapies, making it impossible to assess the natural course of untreated ECD. Additionally, patients with more severe presentations are often treated more aggressively, which makes it harder to untangle the impact of therapy from the intrinsic effects of the different disease phenotypes on survival. As a result, the observed poor difference in mortality between clusters may, at least in part, be a consequence of the variety of therapies, which tend to level out the survival across patient groups.

In conclusion, the unsupervised clustering approach used in our study effectively recapitulates the main ECD phenotypes, offering a data-driven validation of clinically observed patterns. The identified clusters suggest that the disease phenotype is driven by the types of somatic mutations, although these do not fully explain the observed variability of ECD. Further studies are therefore needed to capture the biological and environmental factors shaping disease phenotypes in the spectrum of ECD and associated forms of histiocytosis.
