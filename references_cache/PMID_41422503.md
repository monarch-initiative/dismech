# Repeat-associated non-AUG translation as a common mechanism for the polyGln ataxias.
**Authors:** Banez-Coronel M, Zu T, Aldridge M, Guo S, Ajredini R, Morrison D, Tays AB, Duvick LA, Pletnikova O, Yachnis AT, Troncoso JC, Paulson HL, McLoughlin HS, Ashizawa T, Subramony SH, Orr HT, Ranum LPW
**Journal:** Cell Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.celrep.2025.116741](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2025.116741)

## Content

Determining if repeat-associated non-AUG (RAN) proteins contribute to the CAG-polyglutamine (polyGln)-encoding spinocerebellar ataxias (CAG-SCAs) is critical for understanding disease mechanisms and for therapy development. Immunohistochemistry shows that sense polyserine (polySer) (AGC frame) and antisense polyleucine (polyLeu) (CUG frame) RAN protein aggregates accumulate throughout the cerebellum and pons, in SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 autopsy brains, and in damaged neurons. Cerebellar white matter regions, with prominent polySer and polyLeu but minimal polyGln, show neuroinflammation and demyelination. In SCA3 mice, RAN protein aggregates increase with age. SCA1 Pcp2-ATXN1[82Q] (Pcp2-82Q) mice designed to express ataxin-1 (ATXN1)-polyGln in Purkinje cells show sense and antisense RAN protein aggregates throughout the cerebellum. Disrupting the ATXN182Q:capicua binding, which improves behavior and neuropathology, also reduces RAN protein aggregates. In neural cells, toxic polySer and polyLeu proteins impair autophagy, and reducing RAN protein levels with metformin reduces cytotoxicity. These data identify sense and antisense RAN proteins as a common molecular mechanism shared by the CAG-SCAs.

The polyglutamine (polyGln)-encoding spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) are a group of dominantly inherited repeat expansion diseases that cause neurodegeneration in the cerebellum and brainstem. Among these, SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 are caused by CAG⋅CTG repeat expansion mutations in which the CAG expansion mutations encode long polyGln tracts within their respective and diverse proteins.1,2 Although the proteins encoded by each of these genes have different functions, these progressive neurodegenerative disorders primarily affect the cerebellum and brainstem, which in turn leads to balance, speech, and eye movement abnormalities. While substantial insights have been gained in understanding the clinical, pathological, and molecular features of the polyGln SCAs, our understanding of these diseases is incomplete, and no effective treatment strategies are available for any of these disorders.

Regardless of the position of the mutation within its respective gene, a growing number of microsatellite expansion mutations produce both sense and antisense expansion transcripts.3–10 Additionally, microsatellite expansion mutations can be translated in all three frames in the absence of an AUG- or AUG-like initiation codon.11 This repeat-associated non-AUG (RAN) translation was initially reported in SCA8 and myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1)11 and subsequently in other non-coding expansion disorders (e.g., C9orf72 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis/frontotemporal dementia [ALS/FTD] and fragile-X associated tremor and ataxia syndrome [FXTAS]11–14). In 2015, sense and antisense RAN proteins were also reported in the polyGln disorder Huntington’s disease (HD).15 RAN protein aggregates have been previously reported in patient tissues in 14 different diseases.16–20

Evidence that RAN proteins are toxic has been shown in multiple studies.7,8,11,15,21–28 For example, in C9orf72 ALS/FTD mice, treatment with anti-polyGlycine-Alanine RAN protein antibodies (α-GA)21 or decreasing RAN protein levels by inhibition of the protein kinase R (PKR) pathway24 showed improved behavior, decreased neuroinflammation, and increased motor neuron survival. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) studies performed on autopsy brains from patients with myotonic dystrophy type 2 (DM2) show that RAN protein aggregates preferentially accumulate in necrotic brain regions.7 Similarly, in SCA8 autopsy tissue, polySer RAN protein aggregates are found in white matter (WM) regions showing demyelination and axonal breakage.29 In HD, sense and antisense RAN proteins are abundantly found in regions with activated caspase-3 staining, activated microglia, and astrogliosis.15

A major obstacle in determining if RAN proteins contribute to the CAG⋅SCAs has been the lack of tools to detect shared homopolymeric repeats across diseases. The original report showing that RAN proteins accumulate in HD used locus-specific C-terminal (Ct) antibodies.15 While useful and important for locus-specific confirmation, disease-specific C-terminal antibodies, such as those used to study RAN proteins in HD cannot be used to detect RAN protein accumulation in other CAG⋅CTG expansion disorders.

Using a combination of newly developed polySer and polyLeu repeat motifs and locus-specific C-terminal antibodies, we show that polySer (sense) and polyLeu (antisense) RAN protein aggregates are a shared pathologic feature found in affected SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 autopsy brains. RAN protein aggregates prominently accumulate in vulnerable brain regions, including damaged WM regions of the cerebellum. RAN proteins are found in SCA1 and SCA3 mice. Time course studies using SCA3 mice show that RAN protein aggregates increase with age. Alternative codon experiments show locus-specific SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins are toxic to neural cells. Additionally, decreasing RAN protein levels with metformin reduces cell toxicity.

To explore the contribution of RAN translation across the CAG-polyGln-encoding spinocerebellar ataxias (CAG-SCAs), we developed custom polyclonal antibodies (α-polySer or α-polyLeu) directed against polySer (sense AGC frame) and polyLeu (antisense CUG frame) repeat motifs (Figure 1A). Antibody specificity was tested in HEK293T cells expressing minigenes containing an N-terminal ATG-initiated FLAG epitope tag followed by expanded polySer or polyLeu repeat tracts (Figures 1B and 1C). Immunofluorescence (IF) experiments in transfected HEK293T cells show colocalization between α-polySer or α-polyLeu and α-FLAG-epitope signals (Figures 1D and 1E). In transfected cells overexpressing polySer or polyLeu proteins, the α-polySer and α-polyLeu antibodies detect soluble and aggregated protein forms of polySer and polyLeu proteins with different repeat lengths (Figures S1A and S1B). The ability of these novel antibodies to detect polySer and polyLeu expansion tracts in human postmortem tissue was tested using RAN-positive SCA8 and HD autopsy brains. IHC experiments using our novel α-polySer antibody showed nuclear polySer staining similar to that found with our previously validated SCA8-specific C-terminal antibodies (α-SCA8-Ser-Ct and α-HD-Ser-Ct)15,29 in SCA8 and HD autopsy brains, respectively (Figures 1F, S1C, and S1D). Similarly, IHC staining using our novel α-polyLeu antibody showed distinct perinuclear polyLeu aggregates in HD autopsy brains consistent with staining using our previously validated HD-Leu-Ct antibody (Figures 1G and S1D). No similar staining was detected with either antibody in controls or in HD tissue stained with α-polySer or α-polyLeu pre-immune sera (Figures 1 and S1). These results demonstrate that novel α-polySer and α-polyLeu repeat antibodies are specific and sensitive tools to detect RAN proteins containing polySer or polyLeu repeat tracts across a large category of CAG⋅CTG expansion disorders.

Human autopsy brains from patients diagnosed with SCA were collected in collaboration with the National Ataxia Foundation as part of the University of Florida Brain Bank and genetically tested to confirm diagnosis (Table S1). IHC using our newly validated α-polySer antibody detected polySer and polyLeu aggregates in genetically confirmed SCA1 (n = 5), SCA2 (n = 5), SCA3 (n = 6), SCA6 (n = 4), and SCA7 (n = 5) human autopsy brains (Figures 2A and 2B; Table S2). Robust staining of these sense-polySer and antisense-polyLeu proteins was seen throughout the cerebellum, with the most prominent signal detected in WM regions of the cerebellum, the deep cerebellar dentate nuclei (DCN), and Bergmann glia surrounding the Purkinje cells (Figures 2A, 2B, S2A, and S2B; Table S2). No similar staining was detected in SCA5 (n = 2), which is not caused by a repeat expansion or in unaffected control (n = 5) brains.

In the granule cell layer (GCL), α-polySer staining was found in each of these polyGln SCAs, but was variable between cases. Some cases showed intense soluble and aggregate staining (e.g., SCA2 and SCA6, Figure S2A) while other cases showed mainly aggregates (e.g., SCA1; Figures 2A and S2A). In the DCN, polySer aggregates are detected in dentate neurons, glia, and throughout the neuropil (Figures 2A and S2A). Although the frequency of polySer staining varies among individuals, distinct disease-specific distribution patterns are observed across the polyGln SCAs (Table S2). For example, prominent polySer accumulation in Bergmann glia is frequently observed in SCA2, SCA6, and SCA7 cases, but was less frequently seen in the SCA3 cases examined (Table S2). In the deep WM (DWM) regions containing the dentate nuclei, neuronal polySer aggregates were more frequently found in SCA2, SCA6, and SCA7 compared to SCA1 and SCA3.

IHC using our novel α-polyLeu antibody also showed frequent aggregates in the Bergmann glia, cerebellar cortical WM, and DWM regions of the DCN (Figures 2B and S2B; Table S2). In contrast, in Purkinje cells and dentate neurons, polyLeu aggregates were more frequent and intense than polySer aggregates (Figures 2A, 2B, S2A, and S2B; Table S2).

Double-labeling experiments were performed to better understand the cellular distribution of their disease-specific polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins compared with their corresponding polyGln proteins. Granule cells showed frequent polySer and polyLeu staining across the SCAs but only a rare polyGln signal. Similarly, frequent polySer and polyLeu aggregates were found throughout the cerebellar WM regions. In contrast, polyGln aggregates were only rarely detected in these WM regions and when present, often colocalized with polySer or polyLeu aggregates (Figures 2C, 2D, S2C, and S2D). In Purkinje cells and dentate neurons, polySer and polyLeu aggregates were frequently detected in the same cells, and notably, both Purkinje and dentate neurons positive for polyGln and polySer or polyGln and polyLeu showed morphological signs of damage compared to cells with only polyGln aggregates (Figures 2C, 2D, S2C, and S2D).

Double labeling for sense polySer and antisense polyLeu RAN proteins showed that both proteins are frequently found within the same cell and often co-aggregate or are found adjacent to each other (Figures 2E and S2E). Although infrequent, discrete regions of the GCL positive for just sense polySer or antisense polyLeu aggregates were also found.

Additional double-labeling experiments show that polySer and polyLeu aggregates accumulate in microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2)-positive neurons with signs of degeneration (Figure S3A). PolySer and polyLeu aggregates were also found in OLIG2-positive oligodendrocytes and astrocytes (identified by nuclear morphology) throughout the cerebellum across the CAG-SCAs (Figure S3B). Additionally, polySer and polyLeu aggregates are frequently found in Iba1-positive microglia, predominantly those with amoeboid morphology (Figure S3C), suggesting that RAN proteins are expressed in microglia and/or that microglia are recruited to RAN-positive regions and engulf RAN proteins or RAN-positive cells. Additionally, polySer- and polyLeu-positive signals are commonly detected in SMI-32-labeled axons, which often show enlargement consistent with axon swelling (Figures 2F and 2G). In summary, these data identify sense polySer and antisense polyLeu RAN proteins as novel and frequent histopathological signatures found in the cerebellar regions across the polyGln CAG-SCAs.

Similarly, the pons, which can be significantly affected in the polyGln SCAs, showed frequent and robust RAN polySer and polyLeu staining in neurons and glia in SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7, but not controls (Figures 3A and S4A). PolySer and polyLeu staining showed frequent, robust aggregates in pontine neuropil and glia and more diffuse staining in pontine neurons (Figures 3A and S4A). In the less affected frontal cortex, polySer and polyLeu staining was very rare, and when detected, the signal was usually diffuse with occasional microaggregates (Figures 3B and S4B). No similar signal was detected in unaffected or SCA5 controls (Figures 3 and S4).

To test if staining using the α-polySer and α-polyLeu antibodies detects RAN proteins expressed from the expanded repeats at the SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, or SCA7 loci, we generated locus-specific antibodies against the unique Ct regions of each of these proteins. IHC of serial sections confirms that α-polySer- and α-polyLeu-positive samples also showed positive staining for their corresponding locus-specific Ct-antibody (Figure 4). For example, cerebellar tissue from SCA1 cases positive for α-polySer also showed positive staining with the SCA1 locus-specific C-terminal antibody but not the other locus-specific C-terminal antibodies (i.e., α-SCA2-Ser-Ct, α-SCA3-Ser-Ct, α-SCA6-Ser-Ct, or α-SCA7-Ser-Ct). Similarly, cerebellar tissue from patients with SCA7 positive for α-polyLeu was also positive for the α-SCA7-Leu-Ct but negative for α-SCA1-Leu-Ct, α-SCA2-Leu-Ct, α-SCA3-Leu-Ct, and α-SCA6-Leu-Ct (Table S3).

The only notable exceptions were found in individual SCA1, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 cases that showed rare but positive α-SCA2-Leu-Ct aggregate staining, not typical of SCA2 cases (Table S3). All SCA cases were genetically confirmed as SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, or SCA7, with no cases showing more than one mutation or premutation allele. While premutation alleles at the SCA2 locus do not explain the rare staining seen in a subset of patients without SCA2 (Table S3), it is possible that the presence of a close cognate AAG codon in the polyLeu reading frame may allow occasional readthrough and expression of SCA2 polyLeu proteins from normal-length alleles.

In summary, while SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 all express polySer and polyLeu containing expansion proteins that form aggregates in vulnerable brain regions, each of these proteins contains unique C-terminal regions that substantially vary in both sequence and length (Figure 4).

WM abnormalities have been reported in SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA730–32 and linked to disease severity in SCA2, SCA3, and SCA7.33–37 Interestingly, WM regions show only very rare and infrequent polyGln aggregates. To test if RAN protein aggregates could explain the histopathological changes found in brain regions with WM abnormalities, we performed a series of comparative histological studies on the cerebellum and frontal cortex using a-polySer and a-polyLeu, 1C2 (polyGln), a-Iba1 (microglia), and luxol fast blue (LFB, demyelination) staining. Quantification of IHC staining of serial sections shows prominent a-polySer and a-polyLeu staining in cerebellar WM regions with demyelination and WM loss in SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 (Figures 5A–5C and S5A; Table S4). RAN-positive regions also have increased numbers of astrocytes (nuclear morphology) and microglia, which often show a round amoeboid shape indicative of neuroinflammation and active phagocytosis (Figures 5A–5C and S5A; Table S4).38–41 Consistent with previous studies, polyGln aggregates in these WM regions are extremely rare (Figures 5A–5C and S5A; Table S4). While prominent RAN protein pathology is found in damaged cerebellar WM, RAN protein aggregates are rare in the less affected WM regions of the frontal cortex, which shows minimal demyelination and microglial proliferation/activation (Figures 5A, 5B, 5D, and S5B; Table S4). Taken together, these data suggest that RAN proteins are important drivers of cerebellar WM pathology across the CAG⋅CTG polyGln ataxias.

Mouse models have been used for many years to understand the effects of mutant polyGln proteins in the polyGln-encoding SCAs. Using SCA1 and SCA3 as examples, we tested the hypothesis that these models also express RAN proteins. We first chose SCA1 knockin (KI) (SCA1154Q/WT)42,43 and SCA3 YAC (SCA3Q84/WT)44 models because the repeat expansion mutations are located in the context of their corresponding full-length mouse or human genes and endogenous promoter regions. In SCA1 KI mice, sense polySer and antisense polyLeu RAN proteins accumulate as nuclear and perinuclear aggregates in the frontal cortex of 22-week-old SCA1154Q/WT KI mice (Figure S5A). Rare aggregates were also detected in the cerebellar DWM regions and around the dentate nuclei (not shown). In SCA3 YAC mice, polySer and polyLeu RAN protein aggregates were found primarily in the GCL and in cerebellar WM and pons (Figures S5B and S5C). Previous longitudinal studies in SCA3 YAC mice have shown worsening behavioral and neurodegenerative phenotypes with age, which have been proposed to result from increased polyGln aggregates. Here, we show that sense and antisense RAN proteins also increase with age (Figures 6A and 6B) in the cerebellum and pons, which were both previously reported as vulnerable regions in these mice.44

We also examined RAN protein accumulation using the Pcp2-ATXN1[82Q] SCA1 mouse model that was originally designed to limit transgene expression to cerebellar Purkinje cells and drive expression of a human ataxin-1 (ATXN1) cDNA with 82 CAG repeats. Surprisingly, IHC staining showed sense polySer and antisense polyLeu proteins accumulate in the molecular cell layer, in the Bergman glia surrounding the Purkinje cells, as well as the GCL and cerebellar WM regions (Figures 6C, S6D, and S6E).

To better understand the potential contribution of RAN proteins in SCA1, we examined RAN protein accumulation in the previously published Pcp2-ATXN1[82Q] mice with valine to alanine 591 (V591A) and serine to aspartic acid 602 (S602D) mutations in the AXH domain of ATXN1.45,46 These mutations block binding of mutant ATXN1 to capicua (CIC).45 Disruption of the ATXN1-CIC interaction in V591A/S602D ATXN1[82Q] mice prevents cerebellar toxicity, which initially suggested that the ATXN1-CIC complex is a primary driver of cerebellar pathology.45 Surprisingly, we now show that blocking ATXN1-CIC interaction in the V591A/S602D mice also dramatically reduces polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins but not polyGln protein levels (Figures 6D, 6E, S6D, and S6E).

Additionally, RAN-positive regions with no detectable polyGln protein show increased neuroinflammation (Iba1) in the granule and molecular cell layers, surrounding Bergmann glia, as well as the cerebellar WM regions. Iba1 staining is dramatically reduced along with RAN protein levels in the V591A/S602D double-mutant mice (Figures 6C–6E). While it is currently unclear why the CIC binding site mutations in ATXN1 alter SCA1 RAN protein levels, possible mechanisms include decreased activation of stress pathways or subtle sequence variations in the transgene that prevent binding of important translational factors required for RAN translation. Taken together, these data support a toxic role for RAN polyLeu and polySer proteins in a widely used model of SCA1, previously thought to express only polyGln proteins in Purkinje cells. Although these established ataxia mouse models have been used for decades to study the effects of polyGln, it is now important to recognize that these mice also express both sense and antisense RAN proteins, and the impact of these proteins, especially for the development of therapeutic strategies, needs to be considered.

The accumulation of polySer and polyLeu proteins in both gray and WM brain regions in human and mouse suggests that RAN proteins are toxic to both neurons and glia. To test the toxicity of individual polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins in the absence of RAN translation or RNA gain-of-function effects caused by CAGEXP or CUGEXP RNAs, we generated alternative codon minigenes with non-hairpin-forming repeats that do not undergo RAN translation.11 Each of these constructs contains an N-terminal FLAG epitope tag, a polySeror polyLeu-encoding repeat tract, and locus-specific (SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, or SCA7) C-terminal regions unique to each of the polySer or polyLeu proteins (Figure 7A). These constructs were transfected into T98 (human glial), N2A (mouse neuroblast), and HEK293T (human embryonic kidney) cells and tested for cell toxicity by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assays 36 h after transfection. Both polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins significantly increased cell death in T98 and N2A neural cells (Figure 7B). Additionally, polyLeu proteins were consistently more toxic to both N2A and T98 cells than the corresponding polySer protein for each disease (Figure S7A). A similar but not significant trend toward toxicity was seen with polySer in HEK293T cells. In contrast, toxicity was not seen for any of the SCA polyLeu proteins in non-neural HEK293T cells (Figure 7B).

In summary, these data demonstrate that SCA-derived polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins show cell-specific toxicity with increased cell death in T98 and N2A neural cells, which was not observed in HEK293T cells.

IF staining of T98 transfected cells shows that both polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins form defined cytoplasmic aggregates. SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 polySer aggregates form round vesicle-like structures (Figure 7C). In addition, SCA6 polySer proteins can also accumulate as filamentous perinuclear aggregates (Figure 7C). For SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, and SCA7, the antisense polyLeu proteins form denser, more angular, and focal cytoplasmic aggregates. In contrast, SCA6 polyLeu proteins showed more abundant aggregates with both dense and diffuse staining (Figure 7C).

Ubiquitin-positive aggregates have been previously reported in WM regions negative for polyGln in SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, and SCA7 but not in SCA6 autopsy brains. To test if RAN protein aggregates are ubiquitin-positive, we overexpressed SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 polySer and polyLeu alternative-codon constructs in T98 cells. IF staining of these cells shows that SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 polySer and polyLeu RAN protein aggregates colocalize with ubiquitin signal (Figure 7C). In human autopsy brains, IHC of RAN-abundant and polyGln-poor cerebellar WM regions show polySer aggregates are ubiquitin-positive in SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, and SCA7 but not in SCA6 (Figure 7D). These results suggest that RAN proteins impair protein homeostasis.

Autophagic impairment has also been reported across different neurodegenerative disorders, including the SCAs.47–52 To test the effects of SCA polySer and polyLeu RAN protein on autophagy, we compared LC3B cleavage in RAN vulnerable (T98, N2A) and more resistant (HEK293T) transfected cells. Immunoblotting shows that polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins increase LC3BII levels relative to total LC3B in vulnerable T98 and N2A cells (Figures 7E and S7B), a change consistent with increased autophagosome formation. In contrast, HEK293T cells, which are more resistant to polySer and polyLeu RAN protein accumulation, showed no pronounced differences in LC3BII cleavage. Taken together, these results show that CAG-SCA RAN proteins alter key protein degradation pathways that are likely to contribute to the increased cell death seen in vulnerable neural cells.

To test the toxicity of CAG-SCA RAN proteins in the context of the disease-causing gene, we generated a series of constructs expressing cDNAs for human ATXN1, ATXN2, ATXN3, CACNA1A, and ATXN7 containing either a non-expanded CAG repeat, a polyGln-encoding RAN-permissive CAG repeat expansion, or a polyGln-encoding, RAN-negative, CAA repeat expansion. This strategy enables the comparison of the effects of RAN + polyGln vs. polyGln only across glial, neuronal, and non-neural cells (Figures S7C–S7E).

These constructs were transfected into T98, N2A, and HEK293T cells, and cell toxicity was assessed by LDH assays 36 h after transfection. In both T98 and N2A cells, the CAG-expanded (RAN + polyGln) SCA constructs caused significantly greater toxicity than their CAA-expanded (polyGln-only) counterparts, whereas no significant differences were observed in non-neural HEK293T cells (Figures S7F–S7H). These results are consistent with experiments showing that the expression of individual CAG-SCA polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins preferentially affected T98 and N2A cell viability (Figure 7B). Interestingly, the expression of individual CAG-SCA-derived polySer or polyLeu proteins leads to increased cell toxicity compared to the expression of the corresponding full-length polyGln protein. (Figures 7B, S7G, and S7H).

Additionally, CAG-expanded SCA minigenes induced increased LC3B cleavage compared to CAA-expanded counterparts in T98 and N2A cells, but not in HEK293T cells (Figure S7I). These results suggest that RAN proteins contribute to the autophagic defects observed in the CAG-SCA brain.

Taken together, these results further support a cell-type-specific vulnerability to RAN protein accumulation that preferentially affects neural cells.

Metformin, an FDA-approved type 2 diabetes drug, has been shown to have beneficial therapeutic effects in both mice and humans in several repeat expansion disorders, including fragile X syndrome (FXS),53,54 HD,55–58 and DM1.59 Recently, Zu et al.24 showed that metformin reduces RAN protein levels and improves disease in C9orf72 ALS/FTD BAC mice. To test if metformin reduces RAN proteins and proteotoxicity in an SCA3 cell culture system, we transfected T98 cells with an SCA3 minigene. This construct contains 20 bp of endogenous human sequence upstream of the SCA3 repeat, an expanded CAG with 103 repeats, and three different Ct epitope tags to monitor RAN protein expression in all reading frames (Figure 7F). Expression of a non-ATG SCA3 (CAG)103 minigene led to the accumulation of polyAla and polyGln RAN proteins and cell toxicity. Metformin-treated cells showed dose-dependent decreases in both RAN protein levels and cell toxicity (Figures 7F and 7G). These results suggest the potential utility of metformin as a repurposed drug to target RAN proteins expressed in CAG⋅CTG expansion disorders.

Using both repeat and locus-specific C-terminal antibodies, we show that novel polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins, expressed from sense and antisense expansion transcripts, respectively, form aggregates in SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 autopsy brains. These proteins accumulate in regions of the brain most affected by these diseases, including neurons with pathogenic changes and glia throughout the cerebellum and pons. While most studies of the polyGln SCAs have focused on degenerative changes in neurons, especially Purkinje cells, recent reports have highlighted additional and extensive WM abnormalities in these disorders.30–36 These WM regions show frequent sense polySer and antisense polyLeu RAN protein aggregates in the near absence of polyGln and striking pathological changes, including demyelination, gliosis, and microglial activation. In contrast, polyGln aggregates are extremely rare in cerebellar WM. Cell culture studies show that SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins are toxic to both neuronal and glial cells. In SCA3-YAC-84Q mice, cerebellar and pontine RAN protein aggregates increase with age. Surprisingly, Pcp2-ATXN1[82Q] SCA1 mice that were designed to express mutant polyGln-expanded ATXN1 in Purkinje cells also show prominent RAN protein aggregates throughout the cerebellum. Mutant mice with a disrupted ATXN1[82Q]:CIC binding site and improved phenotypes45 also show markedly reduced RAN protein aggregates. In neural cells, locus-specific polySer and polyLeu proteins form ubiquitin-positive inclusions and cause autophagy abnormalities. Finally, inhibition of RAN translation using the FDA-approved drug metformin decreases RAN protein levels and toxicity in an SCA3 cell culture model. Taken together, these data identify RAN translation as a common mechanism across the polyGln SCAs and metformin as a promising therapeutic strategy for the CAG-SCAs that warrants further research.

The polyGln SCAs are a prevalent group of ataxias that express elongated polyGln tracts as part of larger ATG-initiated open reading frames. Although there are currently no effective treatments to prevent or slow disease progression, multiple groups are focused on targeting the sense CAGexp transcripts in these disorders.60–65 While antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) strategies for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)66–68 and SOD1 ALS69–71 have been tremendously successful, unfortunately, ASO clinical trials for several repeat expansion diseases, including HD72 and C9orf72 ALS/FTD (https://investors.biogen.com/news-releases/news-release-details/biogen-and-ionis-announce-topline-phase-1-study-results), have failed. Although the ASO drugs used in each of these studies reduced proteins produced from targeted sense transcripts, both studies were halted because, at the highest doses, these drugs led to an increase in treatment-related adverse events.72,73 The biology of naturally occurring transcripts is not fully understood, but changes in levels of one RNA strand can affect the expression of the opposite strand through multiple mechanisms, including RNA editing, RNA interference, nuclear localization, transcriptional interference, splicing alterations, or translational regulation.74 Previous studies in SCA73 and HD4 have shown that sense and antisense transcripts regulate each other and that knockdown of one RNA results in the upregulation of the opposite strand transcript.

The overall strategy for the GAPmer ASOs used in the C9orf7275 and HD72 studies was to decrease the levels of sense expansion transcripts by harnessing the power of the nuclear RNAse H pathway. ASO treatments that lower sense expansion transcripts may, however, disrupt the normal balance between sense and antisense expansion transcripts through one or more of the mechanisms described above. Moreover, changes to endogenous antisense transcripts may be difficult to detect because they are expressed at lower levels. Upregulation of naturally occurring antisense transcripts after ASO treatment in C9orf72 ALS/FTD and HD could provide a molecular explanation for the worsening of the disease that was seen in patients treated with the highest doses of ASO.72,75 Our observations that sense polySer and antisense polyLeu RAN proteins accumulate in CAG-SCA autopsy brains highlight the contributions of both sense and antisense mechanisms. Moreover, in each of the CAG-SCAs, antisense polyLeu RAN proteins are abundant in human brains and highly toxic to cells. Taken together, these data highlight the need to better understand the normal biology and the contributions that naturally occurring antisense expansion transcripts play in repeat expansion diseases, and to test strategies that reduce both sense and antisense expansion transcripts and/or RAN proteins.

WM abnormalities have been reported across the polyGln SCAs.30–32 MRI studies show that WM abnormalities are evident in early and premanifest stages of disease and are associated with motor and cognitive impairment in SCA2, SCA3, and SCA7.30–36 The prominent accumulation of sense and antisense RAN proteins and near absence of polyGln in SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 cerebellar WM regions showing demyelination and neuroinflammatory changes, combined with the toxicity of polySer and polyLeu in T98 glial cells, suggests that RAN proteins contribute to the WM degeneration observed in these disorders. Similar WM abnormalities are found across other CAG⋅CTG expansion disorders, including HD, DM1, and SCA8. In HD and SCA8, RAN protein aggregates accumulate in similar WM regions and are toxic to glial cells. These data link the molecular and cellular pathology of CAG⋅CTG expansion mutations across the polyGln SCAs with HD and the non-coding CAG⋅CTG disorders DM1 and SCA8.

An unresolved question in the polyGln field is what, at the molecular level, underlies selective patterns of neurodegeneration across diseases in which the CAG expansion RNAs and mutant polyGln proteins are broadly expressed throughout the body.2,76,77 Cell culture experiments demonstrate that polySer and polyLeu proteins made from SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and SCA7 expansion mutations are toxic to neuronal and glial cells. In contrast, non-neural HEK293T cells are less affected by polySer and polyLeu overexpression. In the CAG-SCAs, RAN aggregates are primarily found in vulnerable regions of the cerebellum and brainstem with minimal accumulation in the relatively spared frontal cortex. In contrast, adult-onset HD cases show abundant RAN pathology in the more affected frontal cortex and less accumulation in the less involved cerebellum. The differences in RAN protein load, combined with the selective vulnerability of specific cells to RAN protein accumulation, may contribute to the selective patterns of degeneration across these disorders.

While several mechanistic studies have shown that RAN translation is relatively inefficient compared to canonical AUG-initiated translation under normal cellular conditions,78–80 activation of the integrated stress response (ISR) substantially enhances RAN translation.24,81 Activation of the ISR can be triggered by expansion RNAs and the activation of the PKR pathway24 or by the activation of PERK through the accumulation of toxic proteins.82–85 Additionally, declining efficiency of the ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagy pathways with age86–90 is likely to further exacerbate the accumulation of RAN protein aggregates. Of note, autophagic impairment and ubiquitin inclusions in polyGln-negative WM regions have been reported in multiple CAG-SCAs.47,49–51,91–93 Our data showing that RAN proteins but not polyGln are highly abundant in affected WM regions, and that SCA polySer and polyLeu proteins colocalize with ubiquitin and alter autophagy, suggest that RAN proteins contribute to proteosome and autophagy abnormalities. Taken together, these data suggest that strategies that enhance autophagy and/or proteasome function may reduce RAN protein load and provide therapeutic benefit.

Stress and age-associated changes in PKR activity and compromised protein folding and degradation are also likely to contribute to the progressive accumulation of RAN protein aggregates in disease-vulnerable brain regions and disease progression in the CAG-SCAs. Supporting this possibility, we show that RAN protein aggregates increase with age and phenotypic severity in SCA3 YAC mice. These data are similar to other studies, which show that RAN protein levels increase with age and severity in C9orf72,23,94 SCA8,29 and HD95 mice.

Although SCA1 and SCA3 ataxia mouse models have been used for decades to study the effects of polyGln, it is now important to recognize that both of these mouse models and human patients also express sense and antisense RAN proteins. Moreover, the impact of these proteins, especially for the development of therapeutic strategies, should be considered. Our discovery that disruption of the ATXN1-CIC interaction in SCA1 mice decreases sense and antisense RAN proteins, but not polyGln levels, supports the original hypothesis that the interaction between mutant polyGln:CIC plays a key role in SCA1, with our new discovery that blocking the mutant ATXN1-CIC interaction decreases RAN protein levels.

While it is currently unclear if and how ATXN1-CIC and RAN translation pathways are connected, possible mechanisms include a decrease in phospho-PKR or the integrated stress pathway caused by the lack of ATXN1-CIC interaction, or that subtle sequence variations in the transgene prevent the binding of important translational factors required for RAN translation. Although surprising, the expression of unexpected sense and antisense RAN proteins from a cDNA construct is consistent with our previous findings that cells transfected with minigenes designed to drive the expression of sense expansion RNAs also produce antisense RNAs,96 and both sense and antisense transcripts can be translated into RAN proteins.11,96 An additional layer of complexity is that these data also demonstrate that RAN proteins can be expressed across repeat expansion mutations independent of the intended promoter. This may involve a combination of leaky sense and antisense transcription outside of Purkinje cells and/or uptake of RAN proteins by microglia and surrounding tissue.

Metformin, a widely used FDA-approved drug for the treatment of type 2 diabetes can affect autophagy by interfering with the mitogen-activated protein kinase/mTOR signaling,97 and its therapeutic potential in the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders has recently been explored.98 A recent study shows that metformin decreases RAN protein accumulation across multiple types of repeat expansion mutations, likely by inhibiting the PKR pathway.24 In C9 ALS/FTD BAC mice, metformin treatment reduced RAN protein levels, improved behavioral deficits, and increased motor neuron survival.24 Metformin has also shown promise in HD and DM1.55–59 These data, combined with our results showing decreased cell toxicity and SCA3 RAN protein levels, suggest metformin as a potential therapeutic strategy for the CAG-SCAs that warrants further research.

The accumulation of toxic sense and antisense RAN proteins across the polyGln CAG-SCAs fundamentally shifts our understanding of these diseases. We show that each of these mutations, which were previously thought to encode a single mutant polyGln protein, expresses a cocktail of sense and antisense expansion proteins that accumulate in affected brain regions, including damaged cerebellar gray and WM regions. In addition to expanding the list of RAN protein diseases, the shared mechanism of RAN translation across numerous types of CAG⋅CTG diseases provides novel opportunities for drug and biomarker development across these disorders.

We show that polySer and polyLeu RAN proteins accumulate across the polyGln SCAs, but it is likely that additional sense and antisense RAN proteins are expressed from other reading frames and may also contribute to diseases. Additional antibodies are needed to explore if additional RAN proteins are expressed and their potential contributions to these diseases. Additional studies to evaluate the potential of metformin and other RAN protein-lowering strategies are needed for CAG⋅CTG expansion disorders.

Because several mouse cohorts had small sample sizes, we were unable to evaluate potential sex-specific effects. As a result, any influence of biological sex on RAN protein expression or related phenotypes remains unresolved.
