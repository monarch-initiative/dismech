# An Exploratory Study of Neural Response to Food Cues Following Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder.
**Authors:** Becker KR, Graver H, Van De Water AL, Dalton AG, Karlson R, Palmer LP, Stern C, Breithaupt L, Burton-Murray H, Kuhnle M, Kristine Hauser NP, Misra M, Lawson EA, Eddy KT, Holsen L, Thomas JJ
**Journal:** Int J Eat Disord (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/eat.70062](https://doi.org/10.1002/eat.70062)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is characterized by limited dietary volume and/or variety due to fear of aversive consequences, lack of interest in eating, and/or sensory sensitivity. Uncontrolled studies suggest that cognitive-behavioral therapy for ARFID (CBT-AR) is beneficial, but neural mechanisms remain unknown. Prior work has shown hyperactivation in regions of the salience network (SN) during the presentation of food stimuli in ARFID compared to healthy controls, and the SN shows reduction from pre- to post-CBT in anxiety disorders. We tested hypotheses that (1) higher pre-treatment food-related activation in the SN (anterior cingulate cortex [ACC], anterior insula, amygdala) would be prospectively associated with greater reductions in ARFID severity at post-treatment, and (2) food-related activation in the SN would decrease from pre- to post-treatment.

METHOD: In a validated functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) food cue paradigm, youth with ARFID viewed images of food and non-food in a fasted state at pre-treatment (N = 24) and a subset (N = 14) also completed post-treatment scans.

RESULTS: Pre-treatment activation in response to food versus objects was not associated with changes in ARFID severity or remission post-treatment. However, hyperactivation in the right ACC (MNI coordinates: 3, 53, 17; k(E) = 579, p(FWEcorr) = 0.013) decreased from pre- to post-treatment with a large effect size (d = 1.1).

CONCLUSION: These pilot findings raise the possibility that changes in SN neural activity may be candidate mechanisms through which CBT-AR exerts its effects. Randomized controlled trials are needed to replicate and extend these results.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02963220.

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is a feeding and eating disorder defined by limited food intake motivated by one or more of the three theorized primary presentations: 1) fear of aversive consequences (e.g., choking, vomiting), 2) sensory sensitivity (e.g., to food taste or texture), or 3) lack of interest in food/eating (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders: DSM-5™, 5th Ed., 2013; Thomas, J.J., & Eddy, K.T., 2018). Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) (Linardon, J., et al., 2017) has been adapted for ARFID and shows promising early results for children (Dumont, E., et al., 2019), adolescents (Lock, J., et al., 2019), and adults (Hellner, M., et al., 2025; MacDonald, D., et al., 2024; Thomas, J.J., et al., 2021). Neurobiological predictors of treatment outcome and neurobiological mechanisms of action of CBT-AR remain unknown but are important in providing objective evidence of treatment effects. Additionally, discussing underlying neurological maintenance mechanisms as well as mechanisms of action for treatment can help engage patients and their families in behavioral therapies (Hill, L., et al., 2016) and may suggest new targets to improve therapy outcomes.

Current research suggests distinct patterns of neural activity in ARFID from healthy controls. Recent findings comparing youth with ARFID to typically developing youth report that those with ARFID may be associated with structural differences including thinning of the right isthmus of the cingulate gyrus, which may play a role in interoception and sensory integration (Moreau, C.A., et al., 2024). Children with ARFID symptoms have also shown greater frontal and superior frontal cortical thickness, which may suggest impaired synaptic pruning during development (Sader, M., et al., 2025). Structural differences in cortical thickness may be related to disorder specific symptoms via altered communication across cortical layers or communication with relevant brain regions. Structural differences in these frontal regions may suggest underlying difficulties with attentional resources and inhibition in ARFID (Sader, M., et al., 2025) and lay the foundation for exploring brain activity in response to relevant stimuli.

When viewing images of disorder-relevant fears (e.g., vomiting, choking), youth with ARFID and healthy controls (HC) show robust activation in the amygdala, which is a region involved in the perception of fear as well as developing/learning fears and fear expression (La Buissonniere-Ariza, V., et al., 2021). It is also one of the primary regions of the salience network (SN) along with the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; involved in avoidance learning and affect regulation) (Braem, S., et al., 2017), and the anterior insula (aINS; involved in awareness of internal body states and emotions) (Mason, L., et al., 2016). The SN detects attention-commanding external or internal environmental cues, to allocate cognitive and attentional resources toward potential threats to enhance survival (Schimmelpfennig, J., et al., 2023). Hyperactivity in the SN may be particularly related to maladaptive behavior patterns in mood and anxiety disorders because of disruptions in the integration of sensory, emotional, and cognitive information (B. Menon, 2019; V. Menon, 2018; Schimmelpfennig, J., et al., 2023). Our team’s published work in a sample of children and adolescents with mixed presentations of ARFID demonstrates hyperactivation of the ACC compared to HC when viewing food cues versus objects. Additionally, in the subsample of those with the fear-of-aversive-consequences presentation of ARFID, results showed amygdala hyperactivation compared to HC (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2025). Thus, findings across structural and fMRI studies highlight SN regions as potential candidates underlying ARFID psychopathology, similar to anxiety disorders.

Although no studies have evaluated the effects of CBT on neural activation in ARFID, numerous studies have evaluated the effects of CBT on neural activation in anxiety disorders. ARFID co-occurs with anxiety disorders in at least 40% of cases (Kambanis, P.E., et al., 2020), and primary reasons for dietary restriction in the context of ARFID include food neophobia and fears related to consequences of eating (Thomas, J.J., & Eddy, K.T., 2018). Further, the primary intervention of CBT-AR across presentations is behavioral exposure (Thomas, J.J., & Eddy, K.T., 2018) which also forms the basis of CBT treatment for anxiety disorders (Barlow, D.H., & Allen, L.B., 2004; Picó-Pérez, M., et al., 2023).

Predictors of CBT outcomes for anxiety disorders using emotional processing paradigms suggest correlations between reductions in BOLD activation in brain regions involved in salience processing and improvements in symptom severity following CBT. SN regions have shown hyperactivation compared to controls in individuals with anxiety disorders which decrease post-CBT treatment (Chakrabarty. T., et al., 2016; La Buissonniere-Ariza, V., et al., 2021; Mason, L., et al., 2016; Schrammen, E., et al., 2022; Williams, L.M., 2016). Further, pre-treatment hyperactivation in the ACC has been shown to predict positive CBT outcomes in anxiety disorders (Picó-Pérez, M., et al., 2023), and decreased activation in salience regions following CBT has been interpreted as a normalization of neural function (Schrammen, E., et al., 2022). It may be that exposure and associated cognitive restructuring uniquely target the role of SN in balancing cognitive and affective processing (Schrammen, E., et al., 2022). Because CBT-AR, like CBT for anxiety disorders, is an exposure-based therapy, pre-treatment hyperactivity in salience regions may predict treatment improved outcomes and may also show reduced activation pre- to post-CBT-AR.

We aimed to explore whether neural activation in regions of the SN identify those most likely to respond to CBT-AR and if neural activity in the SN changes pre-post CBT-AR. We hypothesized that 1) greater fasting pre-treatment activation in response to food cues (compared to objects) in ACC, aINS, and amygdala would be prospectively associated with greater reductions in ARFID symptom severity and ARFID remission following CBT-AR as well as 2) pre-treatment activation in the ACC, aINS, and the amygdala in response to food cues (versus objects) would decrease pre-post CBT-AR.

Twenty-four male and female adolescents and young adults between the ages of 10 and 23 with ARFID as determined by the Pica, ARFID, and Rumination Disorder Interview (PARDI) were recruited from a large study on the neurobiology of ARFID (R01MH108595; see Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1 for participant descriptives). Prior to enrollment, participants on psychotropic medications also had to confirm that their dose had been stable for 12 weeks or longer and that they were willing to stay on a stable dose during the trial (see Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2 for medications used by participants).

Participants were offered, and agreed to receive, 20-30 sessions of cognitive behavioral therapy for ARFID (see Figure 1 for participant flow diagram). Behavioral outcomes for this treatment have already been published (Burton-Murray, H., et al., 2024; Palmer, L.P., et al., 2025; Thomas, J.J., et al., 2020, 2021) as have baseline imaging data compared to healthy youth (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2025). This analysis is distinct in that we report on neural associations with treatment outcomes and changes in brain responsivity to food cues pre- to post-treatment.

Exclusion criteria were determined as part of the larger study (described more fully in Thomas, J.J., Holsen, L.M., et al., 2025) and included: clinically significant levels of weight/shape-based eating disorder psychopathology as evidenced by an Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (Fairburn, C., & Beglin, S., 2008) global score >4.0 in the past 4 weeks, and/or any compensatory behaviors, or diagnosis of another eating disorder as determined by the Eating Disorder Assessment for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual Version 5 (EDA-5) (Sysko, R., et al., 2015). We excluded participants who were taking hormones, were pregnant, or were breastfeeding; had current psychosis, alcohol use disorder, substance use disorder, or active suicidal or homicidal ideation on the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for school age children—present and lifetime version (KSADS-PL) (Kaufman, J., Birmaher, B., Axelson, D., Perepletchikova, F., Brent, D., & Ryan, N., 2013); had a lifetime history of gastrointestinal tract surgery; had an intellectual disability evaluated by medical history; had contraindications for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (e.g., braces, history of brain trauma); and/or had a hematocrit <30%.

We pre-registered the study at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02963220) and the Massachusetts General Hospital’s Human Research Committee approved the procedures prior to recruitment. Patients under 18 years old provided assent and one or both caregivers provided consent. Patients over 18 years old provided informed consent. Data were collected November 2016 through September 2020.

We offered the study treatment at Massachusetts General Hospital Eating Disorders Clinical and Research Program at no cost. We compensated participants $250 for completing pre-treatment assessments, which consisted of a 3-hour screening visit and an 8-hour baseline neurobiology visit that included fMRI scans, interviews, and self-report questionnaires. Participants provided demographic information such as sex, race, and ethnicity via a self-report questionnaire. Patients could earn an additional $100 for completing weekly questionnaires throughout the course of treatment and $100-$150 for completing a second neurobiology visit within six weeks post-treatment. Participants were originally paid $100 for the post-treatment scanning visits but the payment increased to $150 part-way through the study to encourage completion of the post-treatment neurobiology visit. Trained research assistants who were not involved in delivering treatment conducted all pre- and post-treatment assessments.

Patients underwent functional MRI scan. During the fMRI session, participants viewed a well-established food motivation paradigm (Eddy, K.T., et al., 2023; Holsen, L.M., et al., 2012; Thomas, J.J., et al., 2025). The task included four categories of stimuli: 100 images of high-calorie foods, 100 of low-calorie foods, 100 non-food objects, and 100 fixation images. Stimuli were presented in a block design, with food and non-food pictures carefully matched for visual characteristics such as brightness, color, and saliency. Each image was presented for 3 seconds using Presentation software (Neurobehavioral Systems, Albany, CA). Participants completed five runs, each lasting 4 minutes. Every run contained 16 blocks, with 5 images per block. Blocks were presented in a randomized order, while the sequences of images within each block was fixed. To ensure participants attended to the stimuli, they were instructed to press a button whenever the image on the screen changed. For the current analyses, we focused on a contrast comparing all food-related images (collapsed across high- and low-calorie categories) versus non-food stimuli. This approach was chosen because individuals with ARFID restrict food for diverse reasons unrelated to caloric content (Thomas, J.J., & Eddy, K.T., 2018).

We collected all MRI data at the Athinoula A. Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging on a Siemens 3T Skyra scanner using a 12-channel head coil (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). We collected whole-brain functional imaging using a gradient-echo planar imaging pulse sequence (33 contiguous oblique-axial slices, 4.0mm thick, repetition time/echo time [TR/TE] = 2000/30 ms, flip angle = 85°, field of view [FOV] = 216x216 mm, 120 total images per run). We also collected high-resolution anatomical images with a sagittal T1-weighted 3D Magnetization-Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo [MPRAGE] sequence (128 sagittal slices, 1.0 mm thick, TR/TE=2530/3.43 ms, flip angle=7°, FOV=256×256 mm, effective slice thickness=1.33 mm). We used these anatomical images to co-register the functional datasets.

The PARDI (Bryant-Waugh, R., et al., 2019) is a structured interview that assesses the presence (yes/no) and severity of ARFID with acceptable internal consistency (Bryant-Waugh, R., et al., 2019) and 100% inter-rater agreement on presence/absence of ARFID (kappa = 1.0) (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2020, 2021). We classified patients as in remission if DSM-5 diagnostic criteria were no longer met on the PARDI following CBT-AR (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2020, 2021).

The KSADS-PL is a semi-structured interview assessing current and lifetime psychiatric diagnoses in children and adolescents (Kaufman, J., Birmaher, B., Axelson, D., Perepletchikova, F., Brent, D., & Ryan, N., 2013). We conducted the KSADS-PL before treatment to assess inclusion/exclusion criteria and diagnose comorbid psychiatric disorders. Inter-rater reliability for 10% of randomly selected cases at pre-treatment was 100% (kappa = 1.0) for comorbid diagnoses (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2020).

The State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; Spielberger, Gorsuch, Lushene, Vagg, & Jacobs, 1983) is a 20-item measure of anxiety symptoms. The trait subscale was used as a covariate in neural change models.

Height/weight were taken at both neurobiological visits. At pre- and post-treatment, a nurse practitioner measured height in triplicate on a stadiometer and averaged the three height measurements, and measured weight on a calibrated scale in a hospital gown without shoes. We calculated body mass index (BMI) as the ratio of weight (in kg) divided by height (in meters)2, and BMI percentile from standard charts (Kuczmarski, R.J., et al., 2002). We calculated BMI percentile using the Center for Disease Control and Prevention BMI growth charts; for participants over 20 years old, we used 20 as the reference age (Becker, K.R., et al., 2021).

Participants received CBT-AR, which is a four-stage exposure-based treatment for ARFID that includes psychoeducation, treatment planning, addressing maintenance mechanisms via food exposures, and relapse prevention (Thomas, J.J., & Eddy, K.T., 2018). For a detailed description of this approach, see Thomas, J.J., & Eddy, K.T. (2019). We offered up to 20 sessions for patients who were not underweight and up to 30 sessions for patients who were underweight; all sessions lasted 50 minutes. 21 participants received in-person treatment while 3 participants received a varying number of in-person and virtual CBT-AR sessions depending on when they started (e.g., those that started earlier in 2019 had fewer sessions of virtual treatment compared to those that started treatment in the beginning of 2020 after the onset of the coronavirus pandemic ).

We first preprocessed the MRI data by realigning images and performing geometric unwarping use magnitude and phase images from the fieldmap scan. We then applied slice-time correction and co-registered the echo planar imaging (EPI) data to each participant’s segmented, skull-stripped, bias-corrected T1 image. Next, we normalized the images to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, resampling to 3 mm isotropic using 4th degree B-spline interpolation, and smoothed the data with a 6-mm isotropic full width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel.

Next, we evaluated motion in each EPI run using two approaches. First, we assessed the degree of linear movement and classified in the orthogonal planes according to a threshold of 3 mm linear movement. Second, we used the ART toolbox (Artifact Detection Tools; www.nitrc.org/projects/artifact_detect) to identify outlier volumes based on image intensity relative to the global mean image (threshold: 3.5 SD) and motion (threshold: 0.8 mm, measured as scan-to-scan movement). We excluded individual runs if they met either of the following criteria: 1) linear movement >3mm and >10% outliers; 2) >20% outliers. If a subject had ≥3 runs within a session that were excluded according to these criteria, the subject’s dataset for that session was entirely excluded (see Figure 1 for participant flow diagram). We conducted first-level analyses on the food vs. non-food images contrast using linear contrasts and statistical parametric mapping (SPM) t-maps.

Results from first level models were submitted to second-level random effects group analysis. We regressed change in PARDI severity scores from pre-to post-treatment on pre-treatment activation of predefined regions of interest (ROIs) in the salience network (ACC, aINS, amygdala) using a regression model in SPM12. A priori ROIs were defined anatomically for the ACC, aINS, and amygdala, using the AAL3 atlas (Rolls, E.T., et al., 2020) and we applied small-volume correction threshold of p<0.05, family-wise error corrected (FWEcorr). For clusters reaching statistical significance (or trend-level significance), we extracted parameter estimates using the REX toolbox for plotting and data visualization. On an exploratory level, we also examined whether brain activation at the whole brain level [p(FWEcorr)<0.05] was prospectively associated with pre-to-post-treatment change in ARFID severity.

To examine if pre-treatment brain activation was associated with post-treatment remission status, we used a binomial logistic regression model. Beta values representing baseline brain activation to food vs. non-food images in functional clusters within each ROI (ACC and amygdala) meeting trend-level significance in the SPM12 regression model were extracted using the response exploration (REX) toolbox in MATLAB version 7.11.0. These beta values served as independent variables, while post-treatment remission status — coded as 0 for remission and 1 for non-remission — was the dependent variable. We conducted this analysis in R Studio version 4.3.2, where we regressed post-treatment remission status onto pre-treatment brain activation in the ACC, aINS, and the amygdala, to assess the predictive value of activation in these brain regions for post-treatment outcomes. We used R2 as an effect size measure for regression models with the benchmarks of: .02≥small, .13≥medium, .26≥large (Cohen, 1988).

In SPM12, we conducted a paired t-test of the food vs. non-food objects contrast comparing pre- to post-treatment scans (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2025) using the small-volume correction approach, restricting voxel-wise analyses to voxels within a priori ROIs. Predefined ROIs were the ACC, the aINS, and the amygdala as key nodes of the salience network. Again, we applied small-volume correction threshold of p(FWE-corrected)<0.05 within each ROI. On an exploratory level, we also examined changes in brain activation from pre- to post-treatment at the whole brain level [p(FWEcorr)<0.05]. For clusters reaching statistical significance, parameter estimates were extracted using the REX toolbox for plotting and data visualization. For effect size calculations we used Cohen’s d with the following interpretation: 0.2≥small effect, 0.5≥medium effect, 0.8≥large effect (Cohen, 1988).

In this subset of our previously published treatment sample with usable pre-treatment neuroimaging data (N=24), the average change in ARFID severity from pre- to post-treatment was −1.26 (SD = 0.86, 95% Cl [−1.71, −0.81], d=−1.46). Further, 14 out of 24 participants (58.3%) no longer met diagnostic criteria for ARFID at post-treatment.

Analysis of pre-treatment activation to food (vs. non-food) images in relation to ARFID severity and remission status post-treatment yielded no significant results in the aINS. However, there were trend-level associations between pre-treatment activation in response to food (vs. non-food) images and reduction in PARDI severity scores at the end of treatment in the ACC [maximum voxel MNI coordinates (x, y, z): −6, 5, 29, T=4.25, k(E)=215, p(FWEcorr)=0.081] and amygdala [maximum voxel MNI coordinates (x, y, z): −15, −1, −16; T=3.55, k(E)=26, p(FWEcorr)=0.062], with moderate to large effect sizes (R2=0.24 and R2=0.29, respectively). Whole-brain analysis did not yield any additional regions showing significant prediction of treatment-associated change in ARFID severity.

Similarly, pre-treatment activation during the food-cue task also was not prospectively associated with remission status at the end of treatment (ACC OR = 0.53; 95% CI [0.13, 1.68]; amygdala OR = 0.70; 95% CI [0.26, 1.7]).

We also ran exploratory models controlling for baseline PARDI severity as well as relationships between pre-treatment neural activity and subscale specific change post-treatment. None of the exploratory models were significant (all pFWE>.10)

Among participants with pre- and post-treatment neuroimaging data (n=14) the average change in ARFID severity from pre- to post-treatment was –1.30 (SD=0.94, 95% Cl [−1.84, −0.75]), representing a large effect size (d=1.1). Additionally, 9 out of 14 participants (64.3%) no longer met diagnostic criteria for ARFID.

ACC activation changed from pre- to post-treatment for the contrast of food versus non-food [maximum voxel MNI coordinates (x, y, z): 3, 53, 17; k(E)=579, p(FWEcorr)=0.013], with a large effect size (d=1.1) (see Figure 2). There were no significant pre- to post-treatment changes in the aINS or the amygdala. We did not identify additional clusters of activation showing changes from pre- to post-treatment at the whole-brain level.

To explore whether trait anxiety levels contributed variance to changes in neural activity in the salience network regions, we also conducted exploratory paired t-tests that included pre- and post-treatment STAI-trait subscales scores as covariates. Findings of this exploratory analysis were consistent with the original unadjusted model, with lower activation in the neural response to food cues from pre- to post-treatment in the ACC, with a slight attenuation in the significance level p(FWE)=.082 (cluster level) and effect size (d=.66).

This exploratory study represents a pilot investigation of neural mechanisms of CBT-AR, which is emerging as a promising intervention for those with ARFID across ages and symptom presentations (Hellner, M., et al., 2025; MacDonald, D.E., et al., 2024; Thomas, J.J., et al., 2020, 2021). These findings represent preliminary evidence of a possible neurobiological treatment response. While a lack of a control group prevents definitive conclusions regarding treatment mechanisms or neurobiological effects, results suggest changes in SN response as a potential neurobiological mechanism of action for symptom improvement in ARFID.

Changes in ACC activation from pre- to post-CBT treatments is not unique to ARFID. In fact, the ACC is one of the most commonly altered regions following CBT across anxiety disorders, depression, and OCD (Schrammen, E., et al., 2022; Yuan, S., et al., 2022). It may be that reduction of ACC hyperactivation following CBT represents a transdiagnostic effect of CBT interventions (Schrammen, E., et al., 2022). In our exploratory post-hoc analyses, which included STAI scores as a covariate, we found support for this hypothesis as general anxiety processing partially attenuated the effect size, but did not explain the observed lower neural response to food cues post-treatment. Thus, our results suggest that changes in response to food cues may reflect both shared variance with trait anxiety as well as neural processing that is more ARFID-specific.

The ACC is involved in allocating cognitive resources to manage strong emotional reactions. Individuals with ARFID often report feeling nervous and/or disgusted to try new foods beyond their preferred set (Thomas, J.J., & Eddy, K.T., 2018). Given the role of the ACC in conflict monitoring, directing attention, and decision making, hyperactivation in the ACC compared to healthy controls in a fasted state for those with ARFID could represent cognitive interference elicited from being hungry while also experiencing anxiety, disinterest, and/or disgust around non-preferred food cues (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2025). Exposure-based learning allows for the testing of fear/negative emotion-based predictions, fostering more realistic appraisals of threatening stimuli (Jacoby, R.J., & Abramowitz, J.S., 2016). Though a causal relationship cannot be drawn from our data given the absence of a control group, lower ACC activation following CBT-AR may represent overall lowered saliency to food cues with repeated food and eating-related exposures (the primary intervention of CBT-AR across presentation types). Thus, perhaps the ACC is needed less in its mediating role as thinking and emotions around non-preferred food/eating cues becomes more balanced with exposure-based interventions.

It is somewhat surprising that neither the aINS or the amygdala showed changes in activation with treatment, given that neural activity in these regions is commonly altered in neural investigations of CBT for anxiety disorders (Schrammen, E., et al., 2022; Yuan, S., et al., 2022). Given the small sample size in the present study, it is important to interpret non-significant findings with caution, as the study was likely underpowered to detect subtle treatment-related changes in neural activity. It is also possible that our sample’s distribution of ARFID presentations impacted our ability to see changes in these brain regions. All participants had a sensory sensitivity presentation either as a single or combined presentation. Fear of new foods is not reported by every individual with a sensory sensitivity presentation of ARFID (Harris, A.A., et al., 2019; Menzel, J.E., et al., 2019; Thomas, J.J., & Eddy, K.T., 2018). In addition, the fear of aversive consequences presentation, was only present in 33% (n=8) of the sample and a sole presentation for one participant. Thus, the amygdala — the primary neural hub for fear perception, fear learning, and fear expression — may not have been hyperactive for a substantial proportion of this sample and, therefore, not a primary mechanistic neurological target for these individuals. Consistent with this interpretation, results from our team’s larger study of neural responses to food cues in youth with ARFID specifically showed hyperactivation in the amygdala compared to healthy controls only for those with the fear of aversive consequences presentation (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2025). However, the insula was also not hyperactive compared to healthy youth for any presentation in the larger study (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2025). In contrast, the ACC showed hyperactivity across all presentations in the larger study (Thomas, J.J., et al., 2025) presumably because its role in integrating sensory and general (rather than specific) emotional information is likely common across presentations. Thus, exposures specific to any ARFID presentation may impact ACC function. Larger samples that allow for analyses by presentation type are needed to more fully understand the role of each SN region in CBT-AR treatment response. Further, future studies should explore changes (pre- vs. post CBT-AR) in within-network connectivity for the SN, as BOLD activation in individual brain regions as a metric of brain function may not be sufficient to fully capture the effect of treatment (Mason, L., et al., 2016).

Despite prospective relationships both for ARFID symptom severity and ARFID remission status failing to reach significance, the trending relationships between pre-treatment hyperactivation in the ACC and the amygdala and post-treatment ARFID symptom severity may suggest consistency with research in anxiety disorders. Further, these results are important to share given that this is the first study to explore neural associations with treatment response in ARFID. Greater activation in the SN regions predicts symptom improvement following CBT in anxiety-related disorders (Picó-Pérez, M., et al., 2023). Thus, it may be also that those with greater activation in these SN regions in response to food cues receive enhanced benefit from the food exposure interventions in CBT-AR, though controlled investigations in larger studies are needed.

Of course, limitations warrant discussion. First, the sample size was small, reducing the generalizability of findings, restricting our ability to account for possible moderators, and limiting likelihood of finding significant predictive relationships despite observed large effect sizes. As very little is known about the neurobiology of ARFID, we included trend-level results yielding moderate effect sizes in our report. While not reaching strict statistical significance, these findings seem important to report in an exploratory pilot study to avoid Type 2 errors, while allowing for generating new hypotheses and developing early-stage theoretical models of the mechanisms through which treatments may impact clinical outcomes.

Second, while within published clinical ranges (Cooper-Vince, C.E., et al., 2024), pre-treatment scores on the PARDI were low, which may limit generalizability of our findings to those with severe presentations. Several reasons may explain lower scores on the PARDI in this sample such as: eligibility requirements that participants’ symptoms be at appropriate ARFID severity level for outpatient care, lower insight into symptoms and their impact on quality of life, and ARFID presentation heterogeneity such that high scores on certain items measuring one presentation may not be matched by high scores on another. Our sample is limited by low racial and ethnic diversity. Replication is needed, and future research should focus on diverse and larger samples to explore effects of age, baseline severity, ARFID presentation type, gender, race, ethnicity, comorbid anxiety and mood disorders, and low-weight status on observed relationships. Further, this sample is a single arm treatment study without a control group. A larger RCT is needed and is currently underway — https://reporter.nih.gov/project-details/10924032 — to determine if CBT-AR has a causal effect on changes in ACC activation pre to post treatment. Finally, the current paradigm was used as a pilot test of brain activation changes in response to food cues around CBT treatment for youth with ARFID but did not assess each participant’s emotional response to specific food stimuli. This may be particularly relevant for those with ARFID as different foods could elicit distinct emotional reactions across participants with different presentations. However, the preliminary findings from this study have encouraged further research in our group to address this limitation, via the development and employment of a personalized food paradigm that tailors the paradigm stimuli to individual participants’ food preferences.

In closing, the present study explores changes in the SN as a possible neurobiological mechanism underlying CBT-AR. While preliminary, findings underscore the potential for integrating neuroscience-informed approaches to enhance treatment efficacy in eating disorders. Results are important in demonstrating the feasibility of pairing neurobiological assessments with CBT interventions in eating disorders and in providing pilot data for larger controlled studies to test if SN normalization is a mechanism of action for CBT-AR.
