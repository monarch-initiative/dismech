BACKGROUND: Previous studies showed a higher frequency of concurrent autoimmune 
disease (AD) in patients with Myasthenia gravis (MG), but very few cases on MG 
patients complicated with AIR have been reported in preview literature. The goal 
of this study was to present three cases of autoimmune retinopathy (AIR) with 
myasthenia gravis (MG), conduct a literature search, and summarize the clinical 
characteristics of this entity.
METHODS: Case series and literature review.
RESULTS: The seven AIR patients with MG were six females and a male with a mean 
age of 41.29(± 15.22)years old. Five had thymoma, and two had no tumor. Their 
symptoms of MG improved after treatment. The main ocular manifestations of AIR 
were bilateral, subacute, painless vision loss. The fundus examinations showed 
diffuse pigmentary retinopathy and atrophy of retina. Electroretinography (ERG) 
showed dysfunction of rod and cone cell. Hereditary retinopathy was ruled out. 
ARA (anti-retinal antibody) testing showed positive result for anti-retinal 
antibodies (ARAs), confirming the diagnosis of AIR.
CONCLUSIONS: There is a certain frequency of concurrent MG and AIR. Patients 
with AIR have distinct features in the course of the disease, multimodal 
auxiliary examination and ARA testing. Suspected cases of MG should be 
comprehensively screened for early diagnosis and treatment of AIR.

Autoimmune retinopathies (AIRs) are a heterogeneous group of immune-mediated degenerative retinal disorders, caused by circulating antiretinal antibodies (ARAs), characterized by bilateral, often asymmetric, progressive, painless visual acuity or visual field loss over weeks to months with photopsias and scotomas [1]. The presence of ARAs in the peripheral blood is considered essential for the diagnosis of AIR [1]. Commonly identified ARAs associated with AIR include anti-recoverin (23 kDa), anti-α-enolase (46 kDa), and anti-carbonic anhydrase II (30 kDa) [2, 3]. AIR can be classified into 2 categories: paraneoplastic AIR (pAIR), which was caused by a distant effect of tumor; and non-paraneoplastic AIR (npAIR), which may or may not be associated with an underlying systemic disorder [4].

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a group of autoimmune disease with antibodies targeting neuromuscular junctions. Based on the clinical features and serum antibodies, MG can be classified as early/late-onset, thymoma/non-thymoma, acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibody/muscle-specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK)/low density lipoprotein receptor related protein 4 (LRP4) antibody positive/antibody-negative, and generalized/ocular subgroups [5]. In 75–90% cases, MG is combined with abnormalities of thymus, including thymus hyperplasia (85%) and thymoma (15%) [6].

Previous studies showed a higher frequency of concurrent autoimmune disease (AD) in patients with MG, including autoimmune thyroid disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis and etc [7]. However, very few cases on MG patients complicated with AIR have been reported in preview literature, and only four studies [8–11] were found in PubMed. Here we reported the clinical presentations, the examination results and the management, as well as the prognosis of three patients with MG and AIR. A comprehensive literature review with another four cases with MG and AIR were conducted.

This is a retrospective, observational case series. The clinical details of three patients with AIR and MG referred to Department of ophthalmology, Xinhua hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine from 2022 to 2024 were reviewed. The clinical data and the results of ophthalmology examinations including visual acuity, intraocular pressure, anterior segment examinations by slit-lamp microscope(Meyo), fundus examination by ultra-widefield fundus photograph(Optos), optical coherence tomograph (OCT)(Optovue), static perimetry(Zeiss), and electroretinography (ERG) (Diagnosys LLC) were collected. ARAs were detected by immunoblot (Kindstar Global, Shanghai). Western blot was carried out following a similar procedure as that in a published study [12]. Briefly, the antigen (2 µg) was separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes, with a pre-stained marker utilized to monitor the transfer effectiveness. Then locked membranes were incubated with patients’ serum samples (1/200 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. For the secondary antibody incubation, membranes were incubated with Goat anti-Human-HRP secondary antibody (1/10,000 dilution) for 1.5 h at 37 °C. And the immunogenic reaction was detected by ECL Prime. And targeted next generation sequence (NGS) (MyGenostics, Beijing) was also conducted to exclude genetic disorders.

With the search terms “autoimmune retinopathy” and “myasthenia gravis” on PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for articles published from 1900 to 2024, four cases [8–11] were identified. Studies that did not provide clinical data on patients were excluded. A 51-year-old female was diagnosed as ocular myasthenia gravis (OMG) presenting with ptosis, which improved with cholinesterase inhibitor in 2015. In March 2017, a type B2 thymoma was confirmed after the thymectomy. One month after the surgery, she experienced difficulty breathing and developed a myasthenic crisis, treated with high-dose methylprednisolone along with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Oral prednisone was tapered and tacrolimus were maintained 3 years. In March 2023, she was referred to our department complaining significant but painless bilateral vision loss for three months.

On ophthalmology examinations, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/63 in the right eye and hand motion in the left. Intraocular pressure was within normal limits and anterior segment examination was unremarkable in both eyes. Fundus examination showed retinal atrophy and hyperpigmentation in both eyes, with leopard pattern changes observed in the posterior pole (Fig. 1. A). Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) showed hypo-autofluorescence in the posterior pole, extending peripherally along the vascular distribution. The preserved small area with normal autofluorescence in the posterior pole of the right eye was consistent with the visual field plot showing (Fig. 1. B). OCT revealed the outer retinal atrophy in both eyes, with observed retinal thinning in the macular periphery (Fig. 1. C). Static perimetry showed a ring scotoma in the right eye and a central visual field defect in the left eye (Fig. 1. D). ERG demonstrated significant reduction in cone and rod responses for both eyes, indicating substantial damage of binocular visual functions (Fig. 1. E). ARA testing revealed positive results for anti-recoverin and targeted gene variant analysis did not reveal any associated pathogenic mutations. The diagnosis of AIR was established. And based on laboratory findings ruled out inflammatory disorders and the history of thymoma, the diagnosis of CAR was established.

After the diagnosis of AIR, the patient was also diagnosed with pure red cell aplasia by bone marrow puncture and biopsy. Cyclosporine instead of Tacrolimus was used for both anemia and MG. Her symptoms of MG and anemia were well controlled, while her visual acuity continued to decline, reaching hand motion in both eyes by December 2023. A 60-year-old female was diagnosed with MG, presenting with ptosis and proximal muscle weakness in 2019. With immunosuppressive therapy of prednisone and azathioprine, her symptoms of MG improved and she started to take medicine irregularly. In June 2022, she found blurred vision and dark shadows in both eyes. Ophthalmic examination of local hospital revealed macular edema, and no treatment was undertaken. In September 2022, she experienced a myasthenic crisis and received methylprednisolone and IVIG. As maintenance therapy, oral prednisone and tacrolimus were used, which finally led to an improvement in the following 2 months. However, her ophthalmic manifestations persisted and she was referred to our department in March 2023.

On ophthalmology examinations, her BCVA was 20/150 in the right eye and 20/63 in the left eye. Intraocular pressure was within normal limits and anterior segment examination was unremarkable in both eyes, except for mild lens opacity. Fundus examinations showed vascular attenuation in both eyes and slight pigment mottling in the posterior pole of the right eye (Fig. 2. A). FAF demonstrated normal findings in both eyes (Fig. 2. B). OCT revealed macular atrophy with edema in both eyes (Fig. 2. C). Static perimetry showed ring scotoma with visual field index of 4% in both eyes, indicating significant central visual field defect (Fig. 2. D). ERG demonstrated significant reduction of cone and rod responses for both eyes, indicating substantial damage to binocular visual functions (Fig. 2. E). ARA testing showed positive for anti-ENO1 and targeted gene variant analysis did not reveal any associated pathogenic mutations, thus supporting the diagnosis of AIR.

The infusion of 600 mg of Rituximab every six months controlled her MG symptoms but did not stop the decline in visual acuity, which progressed to hand motion vision in both eyes by her last follow-up in June 2024. A 65-year-old female was diagnosed with MG in September 2006 and underwent thymectomy in January 2007, confirming type B2 thymoma. She was treated with oral steroids, azathioprine and pyridostigmine. In the last decade, her vision gradually deteriorated. Initially, she complained of vision blurring in several months. Her vision was still sufficient for driving, but until the end of 2016, she developed progressive deterioration of central visual acuity accompanied by central visual field defect. Cataract surgery did not improve the symptoms. She was suspected of retinal pigmentosa (RP), but no related pathogenic gene mutations were found in targeted gene variant analysis in August 2017. Her visual acuity continued declined rapidly.

On ophthalmology examinations, BCVA was finger counting in the right eye and 20/200 in the left. Fundus examination demonstrated retinal pigmentary changes in both eyes (Fig. 3. A). FAF showed extensive hypo-fluorescence encroaching onto the macula (Fig. B). OCT revealed obliteration of the ellipsoid zone of both eyes, with macula thinning (Fig. 3. C). Static perimetry showed completed vision loss in the right eye and remaining tubular vision in the left eye, indicating a significant visual field defect (Fig. 3. D). ERG disclosed nearly non-recordable responses of cone and rod for both eyes, indicating a severe impairment of binocular visual function. (Fig. 3. E). Serum ARA testing in December 2017 yielded positive results for anti-aldolase, anti-enolase, anti-pyruvate kinase M2(PKM2) and another 2 proteins (molecular weight 55 and 98 kDa). Consequently, the patient received a diagnosis of AIR. And based on laboratory findings ruled out inflammatory disorders and the history of thymoma, the diagnosis of CAR was established.

Altogether, seven cases of AIR with MG were identified, including four cases previously reported in the literature. All the features of patients and their prognosis are summarized in Table 1.

According to our results, 6/7(85.71%) were female, 1/7(14.29%) was male. The average age at diagnosis of AIR was 41 years old, ranging from 23 to 60 years old. 7/7 (100%) cases had been diagnosed with MG, and of them, 5/7 (71.43%) were diagnosed with thymoma. Our results showed that 6/7 (85.71%) experienced MG onset prior to AIR, with 1/7(14.29%) received simultaneous diagnoses for both conditions. Painless and progressive vision loss, along with visual field defects in both eyes, were common presenting symptoms among all seven cases. Their fundus examinations revealed diffuse pigmentary retinopathy and retinal atrophy, consistent with hypo-autofluorescence in FAF images. OCT showed atrophy of the outer retinal layer atrophy in the macula. ERG showed indicated dysfunction of rod and cone cells. ARAs were present in 6/7 (85.71%) cases, excepting the case reported by Sanghai et al. [8]. Thymectomy, steroid therapy, and immunosuppressive therapy were administered to all patients. While treatment resulted in significant improvement of MG symptoms and thymoma, it did not affect the symptoms of AIR. After treatment, the vision of 3/7 declined to hand motion, 2/7 declined to light perception, and 1/7 declined to 20/125, all of which tested positive for ARAs. Only the 1/7, who was ARA-negative, had a positive response to immunosuppressive therapy and eventually remained stable retinopathy.

Although MG patients show a high frequency of concurrence of autoimmune diseases, few cases of AIR have been reported yet. Here were reported and reviewed seven cases to describe the clinical features and treatment outcome. AIR is an immune-inflammatory-mediated retinopathy characterized by the destruction of retinal self-antigens. Due to the rarity of AIR cases, currently no standardized criterion of diagnosis, treatment, and serologic detection has been established. In 2016, the American Uveitis Society published a consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of np-AIR [13]. According to the consensus, the presence of serum ARAs is one of the basic diagnostic criteria. And detection of characteristic fundus abnomalities and exclusion of genetic retinopathy are needed. Recently, the American Academy of Ophthalmology(AAO) established a Task Force on the diagnosis and study of AIR [14]which proposed the following diagnostic framework: (1) signs of disease progression based on subjective symptoms and objective testing within 6 months; (2) examination with < 1 + anterior chamber cells, vitreous cells, or vitreous haze; (3) OCT with outer retinal disruption and loss of the external limiting membrane/outer retinal bands/ellipsoid zone often relatively sparing the fovea; (4) characteristic fundus autofluorescence abnormalities; (5) full-field electroretinogram (ERG) with reduction of both rod and cone responses; and (6) positive antiretinal antibodies. Based on the consensus and Task Force recommendations, the three cases reported in this article -- all presented with a disease course exceeding six months, normal anterior segments, fundus abnormalities and positive ARAs -- met the diagnostic criteria of AIR.

Typically, the onset age of AIR is 50 to 60 years, with 63–66% female [15]. The three cases reported in this article are females with an average age of onset of 56.67 (± 4.93) years old, which was consistent with previous studies. The four cases reported in the literature were one male and three females with a mean age of onset of 29.75(± 4.97) years old, which was earlier than the data reported before. All seven AIR cases with MG in our study and literature presented with bilateral, subacute, painless, progressive vision loss, visual field defects in static perimetry, retinal pigmentary changes in fundus examination, the thinning outer retinal of macula in OCT, reduced vision function in ERG and positive ARAs, which met the diagnostic criteria for routine AIR. Although there were extensive chorioretinal lesions in fundus of case 1 and 3, there was no apparent cause responsible for visual function abnormality. As more and more cases were being reported, the consensus seems applicable to the current situation. The incidence of AIR is low and the clinical symptoms are insidious, but it is highly alarming because it can involve both eyes, cause vision loss in a short time and almost have worse prognosis than other retinopathy. Therefore, it is important to diagnose AIR early so that relevant treatment can be initiated as soon as possible.

AIR is divided into two groups: pAIR and npAIR, with pAIR being further divided into cancer-associated retinopathy (CAR), melanoma-associated retinopathy (MAR), and bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytes (BDUMP). CAR refers to a tumor-related retinal degenerative disease caused by mass compression or tumor metastasis of non-ocular primary tumors, and is considered as a distant effect of cancers, including breast cancer (31%), lung cancer (16%), melanoma (16%) and others [16].

In fact, numerous proteins found in the retina are also expressed in the thymus and other secondary lymphoid tissues [17]. Due to the stimulation of tumors, infections, trauma and other factors, the body produces antibodies (i.e. ARAs) similar to a certain tissue structure of the retina and releases them into the serum. And then ARAs destroy the blood-retinal barrier and target retinal self-antigens, such as recoverin protein, leading to the death of photoreceptor cells, ganglion cells and bipolar cells, causing retinopathy [18].

MG is also an antibody-mediated acquired autoimmune disease and 10%−15% of patients are comorbid with thymoma. Patients with thymoma can be comorbid with a variety of autoimmune diseases, such as myocarditis, myositis and hemolytic anemia. In some cases, thymectomy may also increase the risk of co-morbidities with additional autoimmune disorders [19, 20]. To be noticed, thymoma is rarely reported in patients with pAIR. However, in seven cases reported and reviewed here, 5/7 (71.4%) of the patients had a history of thymoma confirmed by thymectomy, which is higher than common MG patient population. And case1 is also accompanied with aplastic anemia. In the other patients, the concurrence of MG and AIR might be a phenomenon of autoimmune diseases comorbidity, which was common in various autoimmune diseases.

Currently, with a small number of retrospective studies, there is no standard treatment for AIR. Due to the autoimmune properties, common treatment includes systemic or topical glucocorticoids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), plasma exchange, and oral antimetabolites or CD20 monoclonal antibodies (such as rituximab) [21, 22]. Some studies have reported varying degrees of improvement in visual acuity and visual field following immunosuppressive therapy, along with reduced titers of ARAs [22].

The six cases reported and reviewed here, who were ARA-positive, had poor prognosis of AIR after immunosuppressive therapy and their visual acuity were continuously declined to hand motion or worse. However, we found the patient reported by Sanghai et al. [8]who was ARA-negative, and eventually had stable retinopathy and retained central vision after immunosuppressive therapy and thymoma resection. Her last recorded visual acuity was 20/20, although she still experienced photophobia, flashing lights, and reduced light-to-dark adaptation. It is suggested that the presence of ARA may be an important factor affecting the prognosis of AIR patients.

The prognosis of thymoma associated MG patients is poor compared to non-TAMG patients [23]. Early resection of thymoma in patients with early-onset generalized AChR MG may result in higher remission rates [24]. In our study, for five patients with thymoma, after early thymectomy and immunosuppressive therapy, their symptoms of MG had improved to a great extent. And case 1 also achieved remission of pure red cell aplasia after immunosuppressive therapy. However, thymectomy and immunosuppressive therapy seem to be no effect to the symptoms of AIR.

Some study showed once extensive retinal degeneration occurs, it cannot be reversed [22]. All three cases reported here already suffered destroyed vision loss in both eyes at initial visit. During follow-up, our analysis of these clinical data demonstrated that MG preceded ocular symptoms in patients, and the response to treatment for improving ocular symptoms was suboptimal, resulting in the continuous deterioration of visual acuity and visual field, consistent with previous literature findings [9, 11]. This could be attributed to the fact that ocular manifestations of MG may divert attention from ocular symptoms and hinder early recognition of AIR. Consequently, while both MG and AIR are managed through immunosuppressive therapy, the treatment for MG may not be aggressive enough or the duration and dosage at the site of action may be insufficient to stabilize vision loss, leading to extensive retinal degeneration at the time of diagnosis and poor prognosis.

This article has some limitations. RP is genetically heterogeneous, with pathogenic variants identified in over 200 genes and novel associations still being discovered [25]. Even if NGS did not identify the corresponding pathogenic gene, RP cannot be fully ruled out. AIR is known to be associated with some ARAs that target retinal antigens, but their precise role and diagnostic accuracy have not been established [26]. ARAs are present not only in patients with AIR, but also in RP and even in healthy individuals. The increased prevalence of positive ARAs may indicate a greater likelihood of AIR diagnosis [27]. Further research is warranted to investigate the clinical validity of ARAs or other biomarkers for the diagnosis of AIR.

In conclusion, AIR concurrent with MG is rare, and the diagnosis can be challenging due to combined ocular symptoms from MG and AIR. Therefore, diagnosis may be easily missed in clinical practice. The diagnosis relies on careful evaluation, multimodal auxiliary examination and ARA detection. MG patients with vision loss should undergo a comprehensive ocular examination to make a definitive diagnosis of AIR as soon as possible for early treatment.
