# Hyphaema, heterochromia and hyperpigmented cutaneous nodules.
**Authors:** Mamtani N, Snehi S, Bang PH, Singh K, Pattebahadur R, Kandi M, Chaudhari S, Dwivedi P
**Journal:** Oman J Ophthalmol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.4103/ojo.ojo_296_24](https://doi.org/10.4103/ojo.ojo_296_24)

## Content

Juvenile xanthogranuloma (JXG) is a rare, benign granulomatous disorder predominantly affecting young children, characterized by the presence of Touton giant cells. While typically presenting as cutaneous lesions, JXG can also involve the eyes, potentially leading to severe complications such as hyphema and glaucoma. Here, we present the case of an 11-month-old girl with multifaceted systemic and ocular manifestations of JXG. She presented with unilateral redness, watering, photophobia, and hyperpigmented nodules distributed across her body. Ocular examination revealed hyphema, heterochromia iridis, fibrinous reaction, and elevated IOP with signs of glaucomatous optic neuropathy. Despite initial treatment with topical steroids and antiglaucoma medications, the patient experienced recurrent hyphema. Further evaluation identified peripheral anterior synechiae and delicate vessels spanning the trabecular meshwork. Histopathological analysis of the skin nodules confirmed the diagnosis of JXG, with systemic involvement noted in the spleen and other organs. Multidisciplinary management, including chemotherapy, resulted in the regression of hyphema and the fibrinous reaction. This case underscores the importance of comprehensive evaluation and a multidisciplinary approach in managing JXG, highlighting the challenges faced by ophthalmologists in preventing complications such as rebleeding and refractory glaucoma.

Juvenile xanthogranuloma (JXG) is a non-Langerhans histiocytic skin disorder mainly affecting infants and children, 75% of the cases occurring in the first 9 months of life.[ 1 ]

JXG is a non-Langerhans cell histiocytic disorder primarily affecting children, commonly presenting with cutaneous lesions.[ 2 ] It can also involve other systems, including the eyes, where manifestations such as iris granulomas may lead to iris heterochromia and serious complications such as hyphema and glaucoma.[ 3 ]

We report the case of an 11-month-old girl who developed spontaneous recurrent hyphema due to ocular JXG. Timely recognition of this condition is crucial to evaluate and manage potential systemic involvement and prevent serious ocular complications. The aim of this report is to illustrate the diagnostic clinical features and the complexities in managing JXG.

An 11-month-old girl brought with redness, watering, and photophobia in her left eye with a relatively whitish appearance compared to the right eye [Figure 1a] persisting for 1 month, along with cutaneous nodules on her face, trunk, and limbs noticed over the past 2 months. There was no history of antecedent trauma or recent febrile illness. The patient was born from a nonconsanguineous marriage via full-term normal vaginal delivery, part of twins, with no similar features noted in her twin brother.

(a) Acquired heterochromia iridis. (b) Hyperpigmented cutaneous nodules on face, neck, limbs, and trunk. (c) Left eye at presentation shows hyphema, posterior and anterior synechiae and multiple iris granulomas causing hypochromic appearance of iris. (d) Yellow-red, firm and well-defined cutaneous lesions

The cutaneous nodular lesions initially appeared on the scalp, followed by the face, neck, upper chest, and back, progressively increasing in number and size over 2 months [Figure 1b and d].

On presentation, the patient was agitated and had left-eye eyelid edema and photophobia. On examination, the right eye anterior segment was normal. In the left eye, examination revealed hyphema with organized blood in the peripheral superotemporal and inferonasal quadrants, along with broad peripheral anterior synechiae temporally and posterior synechiae [Figure 1c]. Multiple iris granulomas with hypopigmented membrane over the iris and pupillary margin contributed to its hypochromic appearance, resulting in heterochromia iridis [Figure 1a]. There was also a diffuse, elevated pinkish conjunctival lesion in the inferior quadrant, suggestive of a conjunctival granuloma. The corneal diameters were equal in both eyes (11 mm), and no limbal stretching was observed in the left eye. However, the intraocular pressure (IOP) and axial length were higher in the left eye (axial length: 18.88 mm in the right eye and 20.08 mm in the left eye; IOP: 14 mmHg in the right eye and 20 mmHg in the left eye).

Ultrasound B-scan was normal, with no vitreous hemorrhage or mass lesion. The patient also had discrete, round, hyperpigmented cutaneous nodular lesions [Figure 1d] distributed across the body, particularly on the face, limbs, and trunk. Other gross systemic examination yielded normal findings.

The patient was started on conservative management with prednisolone 1% eye drops six times daily, homatropine 2% eye drops twice daily, and dorzolamide eye drops for antiglaucoma treatment, with instructions for punctal occlusion. Concurrently, the patient underwent evaluation by a pediatric oncologist and dermatologist. A peripheral blood smear examination revealed microcytic hypochromic anemia, along with elliptocytosis, teardrop cells, pencil cells, and activated lymphocytosis. Liver and kidney function tests yielded normal results.

An incisional skin biopsy was performed above the right shoulder. Histopathologic examination of the dermis showed infiltration of histiocytic cells arranged in sheets, characterized by eosinophilic to granular cytoplasm, ovoid nuclei, scattered eosinophils [Figure 2a], and multinucleated Touton giant cells [Figure 2b, arrow]. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated positivity for CD68 and CD163, while testing negative for CD1a, Langerin, and S100 proteins, findings suggestive of JXG.

(a) Histopathology of hyperpigmented cutaneous nodule: Skin with dense histiocytic infiltration in the dermis (×20 magnification). (b) Scattered Touton giant cells (arrows) (×40 magnification). (c) PET scan: Diffuse low-grade metabolism in the spleen

To assess the involvement of other organs, further imaging with magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) was conducted. While no involvement of the central nervous system was seen, the PET-CT scan indicated increased focal metabolism in the left shoulder and diffuse low-grade metabolism in the spleen [Figure 2c, arrow], without skeletal or marrow lesions, and no evidence of significant supra- and infradiaphragmatic adenopathy. Collectively, these clinical and laboratory findings confirmed the diagnosis of JXG.

At the 4-week follow-up, the IOP decreased to ten mmHg, and the fibrin was resolving, but fresh hyphaema was noted inferiorly [Figure 3a]. The inferior conjunctival granuloma also showed reduction [Figure 3b]. Given the multifocal systemic involvement and the high-risk iris lesion with recurrent bleeding, the child was started on oral prednisolone and chemotherapy with injectable vinblastine (6 mg/m² based on body weight during the induction phase), along with adequate hydration.

(a) Left eye follow-up shows hypochromic iris, regressed nodules, broken synechiae, pigment clumps and recurrent inferior bleeding despite treatment. (b) Inferior conjunctiva reveals a localized granuloma. (c) Two-month review shows further resolution of fibrinous membrane and iris granulomas

At the 2-month follow-up post-systemic therapy, the patient showed significant improvement. The left eye showed resolving fibrinous membrane, broken posterior synechiae, and decreased iris granulomas [Figure 3c]. Gonioscopy revealed broad-based anterior synechiae and a prominent vascular trunk bridging the trabecular meshwork. Given that the IOP was controlled at ten mmHg with topical dorzolamide, and considering the active disease stage with the presence of prominent vessels increasing the risk of intraoperative bleeding and potential worsening, no glaucoma surgery (goniotomy) was performed. Instead, the patient continued on topical antiglaucoma medication. The patient is being monitored with serial IOP and axial length measurements while undergoing the induction phase of chemotherapy.

JXG is a rare non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) with an incidence of approximately one per million in children. The term “Xantho-Granuloma” is derived from its characteristic appearance and cellular composition, as the lesions exhibit a yellowish hue (“xantho”) due to the presence of lipid-laden histiocytes and multinucleated Touton giant cells (“granuloma”). These cells accumulate excessively and can form lesions or nodules in various tissues and organs.[ 4 ]

The pathogenesis is presumed to involve granulomatous histiocytic reactions to unknown stimuli, such as infectious or physical factors, although the exact etiology remains unclear.[ 5 ]

It typically manifests as cutaneous lesions, which usually appear as reddish-yellow nodules during the 1st year of life, most frequently involving the head and neck. These lesions are typically asymptomatic and tend to resolve spontaneously by the age of 3–6 years.[ 2 ] Although less common, giant facial plaque-type cutaneous lesions have also been reported.[ 4 ]

Systemic involvement in JXG, although rare, can affect various organs, including the lungs, eyes, liver, spleen, lymph nodes, bones, and gastrointestinal tract. JXG has also been associated with juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia and neurofibromatosis.[ 6 ]

The likelihood of ocular involvement in JXG increases proportionally with the number of cutaneous lesions.[ 7 ] Notably, 0.3%–10% of cases manifest ocular involvement, presenting as solitary iris granuloma or, less commonly, affecting the posterior segment, conjunctiva, and cornea.[ 8 ] These lesions may cause spontaneous hyphema. Hyphema in JXG is caused by the invasion and disruption of blood vessels by iris granulomas, localized inflammatory responses, increased vascularity, and mechanical effects. Glaucoma in JXG results from both angle-closure mechanisms (obstruction of the iridocorneal angle by the iris tumor and peripheral anterior synechiae) and open-angle mechanisms (trabecular meshwork blockage by blood cells).

Differential diagnoses for spontaneous hyphema in children include trauma, retinoblastoma, persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous, and blood dyscrasias. For iris granuloma, differential diagnoses include granulomatous disorders such as tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, and syphilis.[ 9 ] These differential diagnoses should be considered, and ultrasound B scans and systemic investigations are crucial for accurate diagnosis.

JXG typically follows a benign clinical course, with solitary skin lesions managed through surgical excision or simple observation, allowing for spontaneous regression. However, in cases involving significant multisystem involvement, chemotherapy regimens used for LCH have shown effectiveness. While JXG is related to LCH from a cytogenesis perspective, it has always been considered a distinct entity.[ 10 ] Due to the rarity of systemic JXG, treatment protocols have largely been adapted from those used for LCH. A comprehensive review of multisystem JXG treated with chemotherapy suggests that symptomatic cases can be successfully managed using LCH-based regimens, which typically include corticosteroids and vinca alkaloids. In addition, cladribine appears to be a reasonable second-line treatment option.[ 10 11 ]

In the present case, after extensive deliberation and consultation with the treating pediatric oncologist, the decision to begin systemic chemotherapy was made. Given the child’s high-risk iris lesions, which could result in recurrent hyphema and multisystem involvement, a tailored decision was made to begin chemotherapy as per Histiocyte Society Treatment Guidelines LCH protocol (intravenous injection vinblastine 6 milligram(mg) per meter square body area for six cycles) along with tapering systemic steroids as per body weight. Although there is not a widely recognized chemotherapeutic regimen for treating systemic JXG, current treatment plans may include immunosuppressive medication, radiation, chemotherapy, and surgery. Previous studies have shown that patients with multisystem JXG may achieve prompt remission with a combination of corticosteroids and vincristine, a regimen used for treating LCH.[ 12 ]
