BACKGROUND: Nerve transfer surgery is sometimes offered to patients with acute
flaccid myelitis (AFM). The objectives of this study were to evaluate surgical
efficacy, assess which clinical and neurophysiological data are valuable for
preoperative planning, and report long-term outcomes.
METHODS: This is a single-center, retrospective case series of patients with AFM
who received nerve transfer surgery. All patients had preoperative
electromyography and nerve conduction studies (EMG/NCS). Matched control muscles
that did not receive nerve transfer surgery were defined in the same cohort.
RESULTS: Ten patients meeting inclusion criteria received a total of 23 nerve
transfers (19 upper extremity, four lower extremity). The mean age at symptom
onset was 3.8 years, surgery was 0.5 to 1.25 years after diagnosis, and mean
follow-up was 2.3 years (range 1.3 to 4.5 years). Among muscles with
preoperative strength Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 0, muscles receiving
nerve transfers performed significantly better than those that did not (MRC
grade 2.17 ± 0.42 vs 0 ± 0, respectively, P = 0.0001). Preoperative EMG/NCS
predicted worse outcomes in recipient muscles with more abundant acute
denervation potentials (P = 0.0098). Donor nerves found to be partially
denervated performed equally well as unaffected nerves. Limited data suggested
functional improvement accompanying strength recovery.
CONCLUSIONS: Nerve transfer surgery is an effective strategy to restore strength
for patients with AFM with persistent, severe motor deficits. Postoperative
outcomes in patients with complete paralysis are better than the natural history
of disease. This study demonstrates the utility of preoperative clinical and
electrophysiological data in guiding patient selection for nerve transfer
surgery.

Acute Flaccid Myelitis (AFM) is a poliomyelitis-like illness that causes paralysis in children. Patients affected by AFM present with profound, typically asymmetric, weakness after a viral prodrome. While many patients with AFM exhibit spontaneous recovery over time, recovery is often incomplete1–5. More than 75% of patients with AFM demonstrate varying degrees of motor deficits at twelve-month follow-up3–5. Longitudinal studies have illustrated that the potential for spontaneous recovery in AFM declines over time3–5. The probability of a patient with AFM recovering antigravity function throughout an affected upper extremity past three months from the onset of neurologic symptoms has been reported to approach zero6. In this context, nerve transfers have provided a substantial amount of hope for families of patients with AFM with persistent, debilitating motor deficits.

Nerve transfer surgeries have been utilized since the late 19th century, most commonly in the setting of traumatic brachial plexus pathologies7. Fascicular nerve transfers in the upper extremity were first described in 19948. These surgical techniques are now being used to restore strength and function for patients in the convalescent phase of AFM. Multiple case series have shown promising results in this population, supporting the application of nerve transfers for select patients with AFM who are suspected to be outside the window for meaningful spontaneous recovery9–16. However, there are significant gaps in our knowledge about the utilization of this procedure in the AFM population. The purpose of this study is to present data to strengthen clinical decision making regarding the appropriate selection of patients with AFM being considered for nerve transfer surgery and to report long-term motor recovery outcomes in our cohort.

While the current literature suggests the possibility of clinical benefit, the efficacy of nerve transfer surgery in this population remains unproven. To our knowledge, no prior studies have included a control group to compare surgical outcomes to the natural history of recovery in AFM. We hypothesized that nerve transfer would result in superior outcomes compared with natural history for a subset of patients with severe injury, based upon our own clinical experience and the outcomes reported in prior published case series9–16.

We also examined the utility of preoperative clinical and electrophysiologic evaluations in patient selection for nerve transfer surgery in children with AFM. A prior investigation of adults with brachial plexus pathology who underwent nerve transfer surgeries suggested that preoperative electromyography (EMG) is helpful in determining which patients are more likely to benefit from nerve transfer surgery17. The selection of donor nerves in children with AFM is uniquely challenging, as the potential donors have also often been injured to variable degrees. Prior studies have suggested which nerve-muscle combinations are more likely to lead to a favorable surgical outcome9,10,13. However, there is no existing data to determine how “intact” a nerve must be to be used successfully as a donor, or if certain electrophysiologic findings in a recipient nerve or muscle may be predictive of a successful surgical outcome.

The optimal timing of nerve transfer surgery in the AFM population has not been established, and another objective of our study was to determine the impact of surgical timing on strength outcomes within our cohort. The literature on nerve transfer in neonatal brachial plexus injury and adult brachial plexus injury clearly shows improved functional outcomes with earlier intervention18,19. A systematic review of forty-four patients with AFM who underwent upper extremity nerve transfers reported a similar temporal relationship, however this finding did not reach significance20. The current recommendations are to refer patients with AFM and persistent deficits beyond three to nine months for a surgical evaluation6,13,20,21. In this timeframe, motor recovery has often begun to plateau, and time remains to achieve the goal of reinnervation of the target muscle within two years from the time of injury; this is the window of opportunity during which denervated muscles are thought to remain receptive to reinnervation22.

To address these questions, we present a single center retrospective case series of twenty-three nerve transfer surgeries performed in ten patients, with analysis of long-term outcomes with respect to preoperative data, timing of surgery, and comparison to a control group of muscles that did not receive intervention.

This is a single-institution retrospective case series of patients with AFM who underwent nerve transfer surgeries at the Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, Maryland. Cases were identified by querying the electronic medical record for patients with AFM with a history of nerve transfer surgery between 2013 and 2020, followed by manual review of records. Additional inclusion criteria were age between 0–21 years, definite or probable AFM as defined by the AFM Working Group criteria23, a neurologic exam and EMG/nerve conduction studies (NCS) performed as part of the nerve transfer evaluation, and at least six months of postoperative follow-up. The study was approved by the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine Institutional Review Board.

Twelve total patients with AFM underwent nerve transfer procedures at our institution within the study timeframe. Ten of these patients had at least six months of postoperative follow-up and were included in the study. EMG/NCSs were performed by electrodiagnosticians at the Johns Hopkins Hospital or Kennedy Krieger Institute; baseline neurologic examinations were performed concurrently utilizing the Medical Research Council (MRC) grading scale for muscle strength. Amongst the ten patients meeting inclusion criteria, we identified a total of 19 muscle-nerve pairs that received nerve transfer surgery (Table 1). Double Oberlin nerve transfers (median and ulnar nerve fascicles transferred to the biceps and brachialis, as further described below) were included as a single nerve transfer, because the functional outcome of each donor-recipient pair cannot be clinically separated from one another. Recipient muscles were selected based on strength less than MRC 3, EMG/NCS findings consistent with severe or complete denervation, and the availability of a donor nerve with EMG/NCS findings demonstrating either no injury or partial denervation. A subset of 12 muscles was identified within the intervention group that were completely denervated at the time of pre-operative evaluation. This determination was made of the basis of strength MRC 0 and absent recruitment of voluntary motor units on needle EMG. Within the same cohort of ten patients, we also identified a control group of 9 completely denervated muscles that did not receive nerve transfer surgery (Table 2). These control muscles were evaluated in identical fashion to the muscles in the intervention group and met the same strength and EMG/NCS criteria for complete denervation. However, these muscles ultimately were not the recipients of nerve transfer surgery due to the lack of an available donor nerve. The reason these control muscles were not selected to receive surgical intervention was not due to objectively worse recipient muscle health or expected receptivity, but rather due to a limited number of available donor nerves relative to the number of potential recipient muscles. Statistical comparisons between the intervention and control groups were made only between these matched completely denervated subsets, and not for the entire intervention group.

Validated functional measures were compared before and after surgery, when available, including the Upper Extremity Measurement Scale (UEMS)24 and the self-care and mobility domains of the Functional Independence Measure for Children (WeeFIM)25. In addition, broad functional outcomes were determined for all patients by extrapolating from history and neurologic exam data onto the following scales. Upper extremity: (3) normal, (2) weakness present, independent in activities of daily living (ADLs), (1) weakness sufficiently severe to impair ADLs, and (0) no movement. Lower extremity: (4) normal, (3) weakness present but ambulates without assistance, (2) ambulates with assistance, (1) non-ambulatory, some movement intact, and (0) non-ambulatory, no movement.

Nerve transfer strategy was tailored to the individual patient and guided by the pattern of weakness and EMG/NCS findings (Table 1). Oberlin (median and/or ulnar nerve to biceps and/or brachialis muscle), triceps branch of the radial nerve to the axillary nerve, spinal accessory nerve to suprascapular nerve, and intercostal nerve transfers were performed using standard technique26. Sciatic fascicle-to-femoral transfer was performed as previously described27. All nerve transfers were performed in end-to-end fashion. Fibrin glue with or without 8–0 nylon suture was used to coapt the transferred neural elements.

Additional neurologic examinations, also utilizing the MRC scale, were extracted from routine follow-up appointments as documented by members of the multidisciplinary team including neurosurgeons, neurologists, occupational therapists, and physical therapists.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software.

The mean age at onset of AFM for patients included in our study was 3.8 years (range 2–7). The mean duration of follow-up was 2.3 years (range 1.3–4.5). Refer to Table 1 for a summary of individual patient characteristics, nerve transfer strategies, and duration of follow-up. EMG/NCSs were performed a mean 27 days (range 1–71) prior to surgery.

Amongst muscles with MRC 0 strength at the time of preoperative evaluation, those that received a nerve transfer had significantly better outcomes than those that did not (p=0.0001). The intervention group achieved mean MRC strength scores of 2.17 ± 0.42 (range 0–4) on long-term follow-up, whereas none of the muscles in the control group showed any improvement (Fig 1.A). The average follow-up time for the controls and intervention groups were 2.3 and 2.1 years, respectively.

Overall, the preoperative strength scores of recipient muscles ranged from MRC 0–3 (Fig 1.B). There was no significant difference in long term outcomes when recipient muscles were categorized by preoperative strength (p=0.41). Outcomes did differ by duration of follow-up amongst our cohort, demonstrating slow and gradual improvement over time. A linear regression was calculated to predict the rate of recovery over time. The slope of the regression line was 0.63 ± 0.20 points/year on the MRC scale (R2=0.27, p=0.0039) (Fig 1.C). (Fig 1.C). Importantly, none of the donor muscles demonstrated significant degradation in strength after nerve transfer (Fig 1.D).

A major open question when performing nerve transfer surgeries in patients with AFM is what extent of injury to the donor nerve can be tolerated before patient outcomes are adversely affected. It is also unknown whether the viability or receptivity of a recipient muscle can be predicted preoperatively. We therefore sought to evaluate the extent to which preoperative electrophysiologic findings predicted postoperative outcomes. There was no significant difference in outcomes when categorized by the severity of abnormal spontaneous activity on needle EMG (fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves, indicative of denervation) in the territory of the donor nerves (p=0.99), demonstrating that partially denervated nerves can be viable donors (Fig 2.A). Similarly, there was no difference in outcomes based on the recruitment pattern of muscles innervated by the donor nerve (p=0.55) (Fig 2.B), nor the compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude of the donor nerve (R2=0.31, p=0.44) (Fig 2.C). There was no significant difference in surgical outcomes when categorized based on normal versus subnormal (range MRC 4- to 4+) preoperative strength of the donor muscles (p=0.20) (Fig 2.D). Outcomes did differ, however, by the severity of abnormal spontaneous activity in the recipient muscles. Those muscles with the most abundant spontaneous activity had the worst outcomes (p=0.0098) (Fig 2.E). There was no significant difference in strength outcomes of recipient muscles when categorized by the recruitment pattern of the recipient muscle (p=0.14) (Fig 2.F).

Functional restoration is another important measure of nerve transfer efficacy. Out of the ten patients included in this study, quantitative functional data in the form of UEMS and WeeFIM scores were retrospectively available for three patients. Among these patients, all three demonstrated improved UEMS subscores on the surgically intervened arm. On the non-intervention side, two of the patients were severely impaired and showed no improvement while the third patient had mild impairments and showed spontaneous improvement in UEMS subscore (Fig 3.A and 3.B). One of the three patients showed improvement in the WeeFIM self-care domain (Fig 3.C). All three of these patients showed improvement in the WeeFIM mobility domain (Fig 3.D). Broad functional outcomes for the surgically intervened extremity(s) for all patients were extracted from clinical history and neurologic exam data. This analysis showed a statistically significant improvement in upper extremity function after surgery (p=0.0007) (Fig 3.E). Additionally, minor functional improvement was also noted in both cases of lower extremity nerve transfers (Fig 3.F).

Recommendations about nerve transfer surgery often emphasize the importance of early intervention21. Within the relatively narrow time window used in our cohort, who underwent surgery a range of 169 to 448 days following the onset of paralysis, there was no significant correlation between time to surgery and strength outcomes (R2=0.091, p=0.21) (Fig 4).

Nerve transfer surgery has well-established efficacy in a range of brachial plexus pathology. While prior case series and clinical experience have suggested efficacy in AFM, no studies to date have compared the outcomes of nerve transfer surgery to the natural history of the disease. Among muscles with strength MRC 0 at the time of presurgical evaluation, our cohort demonstrated significantly better outcomes in muscles which received nerve transfer compared with similarly affected muscles which did not receive nerve transfer. These findings support the hypothesis that nerve transfer surgery enhances recovery in comparison with the natural history of AFM in appropriately selected muscles.

Another aim of this study was to evaluate the utility of preoperative clinical and electrophysiologic data in selecting the donor or recipient nerves and muscles that are most likely to lead to favorable outcomes. Whereas the traditional candidates for nerve transfer surgery have most often experienced a focal traumatic injury, patients with AFM have experienced widespread injury to many motor nerves with varying degrees of severity. As a result, the potential donor nerves often show evidence of injury, and a common clinical question that arises is: how injured must a nerve be before it is no longer considered a viable donor? We found that even donor nerves innervating muscles with sub-normal strength could be used successfully as donors. There was no significant difference in outcomes amongst donor nerves innervating muscles with strengths ranging from 4- to 5. Similarly, a subset of donor nerves showed electrophysiologic evidence of partial denervation, and again these findings did not predict worse outcomes. In all cases, there was no significant degradation of strength postoperatively in the territory of the donor nerve. We therefore conclude that partially denervated nerves are viable donors. We suggest that the primary role for EMG/NCS in donor nerve selection is to confirm intact voluntary motor unit potentials, especially in cases where such a determination cannot be confidently made on clinical grounds alone (for example, when multiple target muscles contribute to a single movement).

In addition, we evaluated whether the characteristics of a recipient muscle could predict its receptivity to a successful nerve transfer. Outcomes did not differ by preoperative strength in the range of MRC 0–2, though there was a trend toward better outcomes in those muscles that retained some function, as measured by a non-zero strength score and/or EMG recruitment pattern. Furthermore, we had hypothesized that the presence of spontaneous activity on needle EMG of a chronically denervated muscle is evidence of persistent viable tissue, and therefore a promising candidate to receive a nerve transfer. To the contrary, our analysis suggests that denervated muscles without spontaneous activity are at least as good candidates for nerve transfer surgery.

We additionally sought to determine the rate of recovery following nerve transfer surgery. Improvement continued throughout the four-year span of follow-up in this study. The implications are two-fold: (1) patients should be advised of the likelihood of a slow but prolonged postoperative improvement in strength spanning several years, and (2) the outcomes reported in our study are likely an underestimate of the true long-term benefit, as many of the patients in the cohort have only one to two years of follow-up to date and are continuing to improve.

It is important to consider whether the measured improvements in strength translate to functional restoration. We were able to obtain preoperative and postoperative functional data on validated quantitative scales for three patients in our study who underwent unilateral upper extremity nerve transfers. This allowed us to report functional outcomes between the surgically intervened and non-intervened arms, though the number of patients with this data available was too small to allow for reliable statistical comparisons. Overall, there were improvements seen on the operated side in the UEMS subscore for all patients, whereas recovery was seen in non-operated limbs only when the baseline impairment was mild. All three patients in this subset demonstrated improvement in the mobility and self-care domains of the WeeFIM. In addition, basic functional outcome scores were extracted from the history and neurologic exam data demonstrating presence or absence of weakness, independence in activities of daily living, and ambulatory status. This analysis demonstrated at least modest improvements in most patients receiving nerve transfer surgeries, including two patients who newly achieved independence in their ADLs after surgery. These findings suggest that nerve transfer surgeries can lead to meaningful improvements in function. More data is needed to draw strong conclusions, and we encourage the collection of functional data and its incorporation into future studies.

Another goal of this study was to identify an optimal surgical window for nerve transfer surgery in this population. Data from other disease states have suggested that reinnervation of the recipient muscle should be achieved within two years of the injury, after which denervated muscles tend to fibrose22. Accounting for the time required for a donor nerve to regenerate and reach its target, the recommendation has typically been to take an “earlier is better” approach. On the other hand, rare cases of AFM have been reported in which patients achieve a marked and spontaneous recovery one to two months after the onset of symptoms3, suggesting that very early intervention might erroneously include patients who would have improved without intervention. There was no correlation between time to surgery and outcome in our study, with the limitations that we utilized a limited time window (six to fifteen months) and n values are small. We therefore recommend no changes to current practice regarding the timing of surgery, but note that good outcomes can be achieved in patients who present late to evaluation.

There were several limitations in our study, the most significant of which was the small size of our cohort and low power. We were particularly interested in the prognostic value of preoperative CMAP in the donor muscles, as this is a quantitative and reproducible measure. Unfortunately, CMAP data was extremely limited in our cohort, with available CMAP data from only four donor muscles from four patients. In addition, inter-rater variability in strength scoring on the MRC scale likely contributed to variability within our dataset. The retrospective nature of this study and the inability to blind the study team to intervention status are further limitations.

Several additional questions remain unanswered. Although we were able to demonstrate that outcomes from surgery are better than the natural history of disease for muscles rated MRC 0, there were insufficient data available to perform a similar analysis for partially denervated muscles. It is known that muscles with non-zero strength in AFM patients can slowly improve over time1–6. Therefore, there is some level of injury severity where natural recovery may perform equally or better than nerve transfer surgery; further data is needed to clarify this point. Similarly, we were not able to determine a cutoff for how severely injured a prospective donor nerve must be before it is no longer beneficial to use it. Nerves with mild to moderate denervation performed as well as normal donor nerves in our cohort. We recommend avoiding the use of nerves as donors that are judged to be completely or severely denervated on needle EMG.

The findings presented here show that nerve transfer surgery is an effective option for patients with AFM with persistent, severe weakness. Data from the clinical neurologic exam and electrophysiologic evaluation can be helpful in selecting patients who are most likely to have a successful outcome.
