<h2>Abstract</h2>
<section><div><div>Context</div><p>Hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets (HR) consists of a group of inherited hypophosphatemia due to mutations of different genes, which need genetic analysis to make a differential diagnosis. Among them, autosomal recessive hypophosphatemic rickets type 1 (ARHR1), caused by a homozygous mutation of dentin matrix protein 1 (<em>DMP1</em>), is extremely rare, with only 30 reported patients. To date, there has been no case with compound heterozygous <em>DMP1</em> mutations.</p></div><div><div>Objective</div><p>To report the first compound heterozygous mutations of DMP1 causing ARHR1 and confirm the effect of the mutation on DMP1 protein.</p></div><div><div>Methods</div><p>We report the clinical features of a Chinese patient with HR. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on the proband. Then, Cytoscan HD array, Sanger sequencing, and genomic quantitative PCR (qPCR) were used to confirm the mutations. A cell experiment was conducted to explore the effect of the mutation.</p></div><div><div>Results</div><p>The proband is a 4-year-old boy, who developed genu varum when he was able to walk at age 1 year and tooth loss after a mild hit at age 3.5 years. Physical examination, biochemical measurement, and imaging finding indicated HR. Family history was negative. WES performed on the proband revealed a novel start codon mutation (c.1A &gt; T, p.Met1Leu) in <em>DMP1</em> and a large deletion involving most of the small integrin-binding ligand <em>N</em>-linked glycoprotein (SIBLING) family gene, including <em>DSPP</em>, <em>DMP1</em>, <em>IBSP</em>, and <em>MEPE</em>. The novel paternally inherited start codon mutation, which resulted in decreased expression of DMP1 protein with smaller molecular weight and cleavage defect, was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The maternally inherited deletion was validated by Cytoscan and qPCR, and the breakpoint was finally identified by long-range PCR and Sanger sequencing. Manifestation of dentin dysplasia (DD) or dentinogenesis imperfecta (DGI) caused by <em>DSPP</em> mutations was absent in the patient and his mother, confirming that haploinsufficiency could not lead to DD or DGI.</p></div><div><div>Conclusion</div><p>We report for the first time compound heterozygous <em>DMP1</em> mutations consisting of a large deletion and a novel start codon mutation (c.1A &gt; T, p.Met1Leu) in a Chinese patient with ARHR1.</p></div></section> <div></div>
<div><a>ARHR1</a>, <a><em>DMP1</em></a>, <a>compound heterozygous mutations</a>, <a>deletion</a>, <a>start codon mutation</a>, <a>WES</a></div><p>Homozygous mutation of <em>DMP1</em> (dentin matrix protein 1) has been identified to cause an autosomal recessive form of hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets (HR) named autosomal recessive hypophosphatemic rickets type 1 (ARHR1) (<span></span><a>1</a>, <span></span><a>2</a>). DMP1 has been proved to be critical for the osteoblast-osteocyte differentiation that is important for mineralization (<span></span><a>1</a>). Although increased synthesis and decreased proteolysis of intact fibroblast growth factor 23 (iFGF23) protein were found in <em>DMP1</em> knockout (<em>DMP1</em>-KO) mice (<span></span><a>3</a>), the exact mechanism remains unclear. Notably, hereditary HR consists of a group of inherited renal phosphate-wasting disorders leading to subsequent hypophosphatemia and defects of bone mineralization (<span></span><a>4</a>). Mutations of different causative genes lead to corresponding HR inherited in X-linked form (<span></span><a>5</a>), autosomal dominant form, or recessive form (<span></span><a>1</a>, <span></span><a>6</a>, <span></span><a>7</a>). Patients with different subtypes of HR share similar clinical manifestations characterized by deformed lower limbs and abnormal teeth. However, the severity of HR symptoms is highly heterogeneous from case to case, even among patients carrying the same mutation. Therefore, among the clinically indistinguishable cases, genetic testing of HR is a critical and necessary step for definite diagnosis.</p><p>Whole-exome sequencing (WES) is a powerful tool for discovering pathogenic variants in genetic diseases, detecting both single-nucleotide variations and small insertions/deletions in causative genes. In addition, recent computational advances have also made it possible to identify copy number variations (CNVs) by WES (<span></span><a>8</a>). This is a great improvement over the limitations of array-based CNV detection methods, such as noisy signal and low resolution. Furthermore, routine Sanger sequencing, although less costly and time-consuming, is not suitable for the detection of such gene alterations.</p><p>To date, only 13 distinct mutations of <em>DMP1</em> have been reported in a total of 30 patients around the world, as we previously reviewed (<span></span><a>9</a>). All cases described are due to homozygous mutations; so far there has been no case with compound heterozygous mutations of <em>DMP1</em>.</p><p>Here we describe the first ARHR1 case with compound heterozygous <em>DMP1</em> mutations, including a novel start codon mutation inherited from the proband’s father (c.1A &gt; T, p.Met1Leu) and a large deletion inherited from his mother covering the entire <em>DMP1</em> gene and the adjacent genes.</p> <h2>Materials and Methods</h2>
<h3>Participants and Sample Preparation</h3>
<p>This study enrolled one family with an individual affected by HR. The family underwent clinical examinations at the Department of Endocrinology in Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH). This study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Department of Scientific Research at PUMCH, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before entering the study.</p><p>Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood of the patient and his family members using QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. A NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) was used for evaluating the purity and concentration of DNA.</p> <h3>Whole-Exome Sequencing</h3>
<h4>Exome sequencing, variant calling, and annotation</h4>
<p>A total of 50 ng DNA was sheared into fragments of approximately 200 bp and blunt-ended followed by addition with deoxyadenosine at the 3′ ends. The DNA fragments were then ligated with barcoded sequencing adaptors. Subsequently, the DNA fragments were hybridized and captured by Berry's NanoWES Human Exome V2.0 (Berry Genomics) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The hybrid products were eluted and collected, and then subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and purification to construct libraries. The libraries were quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR), and size distribution were determined using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Finally, a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) with 150-bp pair-end sequencing mode was used for sequencing the genomic DNA. Raw image files were processed using CASAVA v1.82 for base calling and generating raw data.</p><p>Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19/GRCh37) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner tool, and PCR duplicates were removed by using Picard v1.57 (<a>http://picard.sourceforge.net/</a>). Verita Trekker Variants Detection System by Berry Genomics was employed for variant calling. Variant annotation and interpretation were conducted by ANNOVAR (<a>https://annovar.openbioinformatics.org/en/latest/</a>) and the Enliven Variants Annotation Interpretation System authorized by Berry Genomics. We mainly focused on protein-altering variants such as missense, nonsense, splice-site variants, and coding indels, with alternative allele frequencies less than 0.005 in the gnomAD (<a>http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/</a>), the 1000 Genome Project (<a>https://www.internationalgenome.org</a>), Berrybig data population database, and dbSNP (<a>http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp</a>). Functional effect was predicted by different bioinformatics software, including Mutation Taster (<a>http://www.mutationtaster.org/</a>), Polyphen-2 (<a>http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2</a>), Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT, <a>http://sift-dna.org</a>), Mendelian Clinically Applicable Pathogenicity (M-CAP, <a>http://bejerano.stanford.edu/mcap</a>), Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner (REVEL, <a>https://sites.google.com/site/revelgenomics</a>), and Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD, <a>http://cadd.gs.washington.edu</a>). Variant novelty was identified in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD, <a>http://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php</a>) and ClinVar (<a>https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar</a>). The pathogenicity of identified variants was assessed according to the standards and guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (<span></span><a>10</a>).</p> <h4>Copy number variation calls and annotation based on whole-exome sequencing data</h4>
<p>CNVs were detected by CNVkit (<a>https://github.com/etal/cnvkit</a>) and XHMM (<a>https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/xhmm</a>) from WES data and annotated by AnnotSV (<a>https://www.lbgi.fr/AnnotSV</a>). Interpretation of detected CNVs was based on size, frequency, number of key genes, and overlap with known disease-associated regions. The population frequency of CNVs were obtained from internal databases at the Berry-Genomics, gnomAD (<a>http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/</a>), and Database of Genomic Variants (DGV, <a>http://dgv.tcag.ca</a>).</p> <h3>Identification of the Start Codon Mutation</h3>
<p>The mutation found by WES was confirmed by Sanger sequencing in the proband, his parents, and sister. The primer sequences of <em>DMP1</em> designed by Oligo 7.60 software (Molecular Biology Insight Inc) used to confirm the mutation found by WES were as follows: 5′-CAAGTTTCCTCTACTCTGC-3′ (forward) R: 5′-CTACTAAGTTGGAATCACAG-3′ (reverse). The PCR products were sequenced by ABI 3730XL sequencer (Applied Biosystems). Sequences were compared with the published <em>DMP1</em> sequence (Accession No. DNA: NG_008988.1, complementary DNA: NM_004407.4) by Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) on the National Center for Biotechnology Information database.</p> <h3>Microarray Analysis</h3>
<p>CNVs in genomic DNA in the proband and his parents were further analyzed by the Affymetrix CytoScan HD Array (Affymetrix) according to the manufacturer's procedure (Shanghai Biotechnology Corp). Raw data were further analyzed and visualized by Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS) software (Affymetrix). Only regions larger than 50 kb comprising at least 25 contiguous markers were considered. Finally, common CNVs were distinguished from rare CNVs by DGV (<a>http://dgv.tcag.ca/</a>) and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, <a>http://omim.org</a>).</p> <h3>Genomic Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction</h3>
<p>Semiquantitative qPCR was performed to confirm the presence of the deletion by detecting the copies of DNA fragments within the deleted region in the proband, his sister, and his parents. Primers used to quantify the relative copies of <em>DMP1</em> (exon 1-6), dentin sialophosphoprotein (<em>DSPP)</em> (exon 1-5), and genes around the breakpoint are listed (Supplementary Table S1) (<span></span><a>11</a>). qPCR was performed on an ABI7500 PCR system (Applied Biosystems) using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The relative expression level of targeted genes was normalized to <em>GAPDH</em> and quantified by the comparative 2<sup>−ΔΔCt</sup> method.</p> <h3>Identification of the Breakpoint</h3>
<p>Based on the deletion junction of the CNV reported by CytoScan HD Array and WES, forward primer (5′-ATGCTCTTAAAAATAAACGAT-3′) and reverse primer (5′-CAGGGTGAAGAATTTGGTTT-3′) flanking the deletion junction were designed and used for long-range PCR. Appearance of the band of PCR products in the gel electrophoresis was considered as a visual confirmation of the mutant allele. The PCR product was sequenced by primer walking to identify the deletion breakpoints. qPCR was performed on the exons of the genes around the breakpoints.</p> <h3>Evaluation of DMP1 Protein Expression by Western Blotting</h3>
<h4>Construction of plasmids</h4>
<p>Complementary DNA sequence of wild-type (WT) <em>DMP1</em> was synthesized and subcloned into pcDNA3.1(+) vector. Flag tag was then cloned into the C-terminal of <em>DMP1</em> to generate the final wild construct (WT-DMP1-Flag). The mutant (c.1A &gt; T) construct (MT-DMP1-Flag) was generated using Site-directed Mutagenesis based on the wild construct.</p> <h4>Cell culture and transfection</h4>
<p>HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) (HyClone), supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin) (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO<sub>2</sub> atmosphere. For transfection, cells were seeded on 6-well plates in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum. After overnight culture, seeded cells were transfected with 2.5 μg of plasmid DNA, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Transfection medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM after 6 hours.</p> <h4>Western blotting</h4>
<p>Conditioned serum-free media were collected from transfected HEK293T cells and concentrated 20 times. Cells were washed 3 times with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (Beijing Solarbio Science &amp; Technology), and then lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Scientific). Samples were electrophoresed on 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and electrophoretically transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore). After blocking with Quick Blocking Buffer (Beyotime), membranes were incubated with anti-Flag antibody (catalog No. 14793, RRID: AB_2572291, Cell Signal Technology) or GAPDH antibody (catalog No. 5174, RRID: AB_10622025, Cell Signal Technology) overnight. After a wash in 1×TBST (Beijing Solarbio Science &amp; Technology), blots were incubated with the secondary anti-rabbit antibody (catalog No. 111-035-003, RRID: AB_2313567, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc) for 1 hour. Proteins were visualized using the Immobilon Western HRP Substrate (Millipore).</p> <h3>Identification of DMP1 Protein Expression by Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry</h3>
<h4>Sample preparation</h4>
<p>Transfected HEK293T cells were lysed with phosphate-buffered saline containing 1 M urea and in-solution digestion was underwent sample preparation. In brief, protein was reduced with dithiothreitol and directly alkylated using iodoacetamide for 1 hour at 37 °C in the dark. Mass Spec Grade Trypsin (Promega) was used for protein digestion, with an enzyme to protein ratio of 1:100. The digested samples were desalted by Sep-Pak columns C18 columns and dried by SpeedVac, redissolved in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid for further liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis.</p> <h4>Mass spectrometry analysis and data processing</h4>
<p>The samples for MS analysis were acquired by the direct-DIA (data independent analysis) method. LC-MS/MS data acquisition was performed on a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) interfaced with an EASY-nLC 1200 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A 60-minute gradient at a flow rate of 0.3 μL/min was used for LC separation (mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in H<sub>2</sub>O; mobile phase B: 80% acetonitrile in H<sub>2</sub>O): 0-5 minutes, 2%-12% solvent B; 5-40 minutes, 12%-25% solvent B; 40-50 minutes, 25%-35% solvent B; 50-55 minutes, 35%-100% solvent B; 55-60 minutes, 100% solvent B). For DIA analysis, a variable isolation window DIA method with 60 windows was used. The maximal injection time was 50 ms for the full scan and the DIA scans. The full scan was set at a resolution of 120 000 over an m/z range of 350 to 1250 and was followed by DIA scans with resolution of 30 000, HCD collision energy of 32%, and AGC target of 1E6.</p><p>Raw MS files were searched against the Uniprot Human database (<a>https://www.uniprot.org/</a>) and the mutant DMP1 protein sequence by using Spectronaut software (version 16.0, Biognosys) with the default settings. All results were filtered according to a Q value cutoff of 0.01 (corresponding to a false discovery rate of 1%), and two unique peptides for protein identification.</p> <h2>Results</h2>
<h3>Patient Report</h3>
<p>The proband, a 4-year-old boy, was born from nonconsanguineous parents. Teeth eruption was normal, starting at age 5 months. However, he developed genu varum and gait instability when he was able to walk at age 1 year. He was diagnosed with HR at age 1.5 years with unclear etiology and received irregular supplementation of oral phosphate and calcium. Pathogenic variants or variants of uncertain significance were not identified in other hereditary HR-associated genes, such as <em>PHEX</em>, <em>FGF23</em>, <em>ENPP1</em>, <em>FAM20C</em>, <em>FGFR1</em>, <em>INPPL1</em>, <em>SLC34A1</em>, <em>SLC34A3</em>, <em>SLC9A3R1</em>, <em>CLCN5</em>, and <em>OCRL1</em>.</p> <h3>Validation of Start Codon Mutation</h3>
<p>To validate the start codon mutation, we performed Sanger sequencing on each of the family members (<span></span><a>Fig. 2A</a>). The mutation of <em>DMP1</em> (c.1A &gt; T) was confirmed in the proband (II-1), who had a homozygous genotype, and in his father (I-1) and sister (II-2), both of whom had a heterozygous genotype (<span></span><a>Fig. 2B</a>). The start codon mutation affected a highly conserved methionine located in the signal peptide of DMP1 (<span></span><a>Fig. 2C</a>). Notably, this mutation was absent in the proband's mother (I-2), suggesting that the existence of the CNV in the proband that he might have inherited from his mother made the mutation appear homozygous in the proband.</p> <a></a>

Although the proband's father and sister had a comparable copy for the whole <em>DMP1</em> and <em>DSPP</em> with the healthy control, the proband and his mother had only a half-fold copy (<span></span><a>Fig. 4A</a> and <span></span><a>4B</a>). C-terminal fragment was not detected in the MT in the cellular lysate or culture medium.</p> <a></a>

As shown in <span></span><a>Fig. 7B</a>, the MS2 spectrum confirmed that MT-DMP1 was translated by using a downstream alternative start site.</p> <h2>Discussion</h2>
<p>In the present study, we identify for the first time novel compound heterozygous mutations of <em>DMP1</em> in patients affected by ARHR1: a large deletion including <em>DMP1</em> and a novel start codon mutation (<em>DMP1</em>: c.1 A &gt; T).</p><p>HR is a collective term for a group of rare bone diseases characterized by hypophosphatemia and deformed lower limbs. It has been reported that mutations of <em>PHEX</em> account for most cases of HR in an X-linked dominant manner (<span></span><a>12</a>), while the mutations of <em>FGF23</em> (ADHR, OMIM No. 193100), <em>DMP1</em> (ARHR1, OMIM No. 241520), and <em>ENPP1</em> (ARHR2, OMIM No. 613312) are responsible for rare HR in an autosomal dominant or recessive manners (<span></span><a>1</a>, <span></span><a>6</a>, <span></span><a>7</a>). A reported 13 mutations of <em>DMP1</em> include 4 nonsense mutations and 4 small deletions resulting in premature termination codons, 3 splicing mutations resulting in skipping of exons, 1 large deletion of the entire exon 3-6 + 3′ region, and 1 start codon mutation (<span></span><a>9</a>). All patients with ARHR1 were identified to harbor the aforementioned homozygous mutations of <em>DMP1</em>. Here we report the first compound heterozygous mutations of <em>DMP1</em> (c.1A &gt; T/deletion) indicated by WES in the proband. The start codon mutation (c.1A &gt; T) was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and was found to have been inherited from his father. The heterozygous deletion involving <em>DMP1</em> was further supported by Cytoscan HD array and confirmed by qPCR and the identification of the breakpoints. We also found that the heterozygous deletion of <em>DMP1</em> in the proband was inherited from his mother.</p><p>Notably, although the start codon mutation identified in this study has not hitherto been reported, another start codon mutation (c.1A &gt; G, p.Met1Val) has been reported previously (<span></span><a>1</a>). Different from the changed length or expression level of protein due to a start codon mutation (<span></span><a>13</a>), the MT protein was retained within the cells without secretion to the cell medium, which might have resulted from the change of methionine at the translational initiation and subsequent impaired function of the signal sequence (16 amino acids) of the DMP1 protein (<span></span><a>1</a>, <span></span><a>14</a>). In this case, the novel start codon mutation found in this study (c.1A &gt; T, p.Met1Leu) is supposed to have a similar pathogenicity to the reported one (c.1A &gt; G, p.Met1Val), leading to the failure of secretion of the DMP1 protein. In contrast, we found the MT DMP1 protein (c.1A &gt; T, p.Met1Leu) could be secreted into the cell medium, suggesting the reserved function of signal peptide. However, intact DMP1 tended to have a smaller molecular weight and became faint, suggesting a lower expression level of the DMP1 protein. As previously reported, a start codon mutation may lead to use of an alternative start site upstream or downstream, determined by the presence of an ATG codon and nearby Kozak sequence recognized by translational machinery (<span></span><a>15</a>). We speculate that the reinitiation of translation might occur at the downstream ATG site (21 nucleotides downstream) due to the loss of original start site, resulting in the smaller molecular-weight protein in the MT. Consistent with this speculation, LC-MS analysis confirmed the reinitiated translation of MT DMP1 by the downstream alternative start site. Regarding the band with larger molecular weight, it is unlikely to be the result of reinitiation of translation at the upstream ATG because of the absence of another ATG in the 5′UTR region. In this case, further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanism behind the appearance of bands with larger molecular weight. Actually, the absence of the C-terminal fragment of DMP1 in the MT is more noteworthy. Both C-terminal and N-terminal fragment of DMP1 have been proved to promote hydroxyapatite (HA) formation and growth (<span></span><a>16</a>), whereas full-length DMP1 inhibits formation of HA (<span></span><a>17</a>). So far, the mechanism by which DMP1 participates in the regulation of FGF23 is unclear. However, a previous animal study found that the level of serum FGF23 in <em>DMP1-</em>KO<em>/D213A-Tg</em> (cleavage site) mice was significantly higher than that in WT mice, although slightly lower than that in <em>DMP1</em>-KO mice (<span></span><a>18</a>). These results suggested that not only the full-length DMP1, but also the processed fragments, were involved in the regulation of FGF23. Based on these findings, the affected cleavage of DMP1 reflected by absence of C-terminal fragment in the mutant might explain the dysregulation of FGF23 and inhibition of HA in the patient, while the mechanism of how the start codon mutation affects DMP1 cleavage remains to be further elucidated.</p><p>The proband harbored a heterozygous deletion in chr4q22.1 inherited from his mother that covers 4 gene loci, including <em>DSPP</em>, <em>DMP1</em>, <em>IBSP</em>, and <em>MEPE</em>. Among them, only <em>DSPP</em> and <em>DMP1</em> have been reported to be associated with genetic diseases. Heterozygous mutations of <em>DSPP</em> could cause dentin dysplasia (DD) or dentinogenesis imperfecta (DGI) inherited in autosomal dominant form (<span></span><a>19</a>, <span></span><a>20</a>). Patients carrying heterozygous mutation of <em>DSPP</em> present with teeth discoloration, cervical region constriction, pulp stones, teeth attrition, and periapical inflammation (<span></span><a>21</a>). However, only mice lacking both <em>DSPP</em> alleles (−/−) present with DGI (<span></span><a>22</a>), whereas the teeth of heterozygotic mice (+/−) appear normal. This finding suggested that loss of a single allele is recessive and that haploinsufficiency does not explain DD or DGI (<span></span><a>23</a>). In a cell experiment, dominant negative effects of heterozygous <em>DSPP</em> mutation were observed as the secretion of WT DSPP is reduced when coexpressed with MT DSPP proteins (<span></span><a>23</a>). This finding explains the abnormal teeth observed in patients with heterozygous <em>DSPP</em> mutation but normal teeth in heterozygotic mice (+/−). However, heterozygous deletion of <em>DSPP</em> and whether it is pathogenic have not been reported in humans. In this study, the proband's mother without manifestation of DGI or DD is a carrier of a heterozygous deletion of <em>DSPP</em>, further confirming that haploinsufficiency could not lead to DD or DGI in humans. In this condition, although both pathogenic mutations of <em>DMP1</em> and <em>DSPP</em> could lead to a dental phenotype, the dental abnormality in the proband, who simultaneously harbored compound heterozygous <em>DMP1</em> mutations and a heterozygous <em>DSPP</em> deletion, could be considered the result of compound heterozygous <em>DMP1</em> mutations. Actually, the dental abnormality found in the proband was not consistent with the dental characteristics of DGI or DD. In contrast, the enamel chipping, pulpal enlargement, and premature absence of teeth due to mild to moderate trauma in the proband were consistent with the dental phenotype of ARHR1 due to <em>DMP1</em> mutation.</p><p>It should be noted that all the genes involved in the heterozygous deletion belong to the small integrin-binding ligand <em>N</em>-linked glycoprotein (SIBLING) family, which plays important roles in the dentinogenesis and osteogenesis (<span></span><a>24</a>, <span></span><a>25</a>). Besides the 4 genes confirmed to be involved, <em>SPP1</em> is also a member of the SIBLING family (<span></span><a>26</a>). Recently a study has proposed that accumulation of osteopontin due to a mutation of <em>SPP1</em> accounts for the excessive bone mass with regions of osteomalacia and periosteocytic lesions in ARHR1 patients (<span></span><a>27</a>). However, in our proband and his mother, <em>SPP1</em> was normal. As a unique individual who is heterozygous for most of the SIBLING family gene, the proband's mother was clinically normal, suggesting that the heterozygous deletion of the SIBLING family gene would not affect the proband's clinical phenotype.</p><p>There are some limitations to the present study. First, the exact mechanism behind the pathogenicity of the novel start codon mutation found in this study needs further study to clarify. Second, whether patients with compound heterozygous <em>DMP1</em> mutations present with more severe manifestation than patients with homozygous <em>DMP1</em> mutations requires more cases to explore.</p><p>In conclusion, we report for the first time compound heterozygous <em>DMP1</em> mutations consisting of a novel start codon mutation inherited from his father (c.1A &gt; T, p.Met1Leu) and a novel deletion inherited from his mother covering the entire <em>DMP1</em> gene and adjacent genes in a patient with ARHR1. In addition, heterozygous deletion of the <em>DSPP</em> gene found in this family further confirmed that haploinsufficiency could not lead to DD or DGI.</p> <h2>Acknowledgments</h2>
