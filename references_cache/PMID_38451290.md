# Heterozygous MAP3K20 variants cause ectodermal dysplasia, craniosynostosis, sensorineural hearing loss, and limb anomalies.
**Authors:** Brooks D, Burke E, Lee S, Eble TN, O'Leary M, Osei-Owusu I, Rehm HL, Dhar SU, Emrick L, Bick D, Nehrebecky M, Macnamara E, Casas-Alba D, Armstrong J, Prat C, Martínez-Monseny AF, Palau F, Liu P, Adams D, Undiagnosed Diseases Network, Lalani S, Rosenfeld JA, Burrage LC
**Journal:** Hum Genet (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00439-024-02657-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00439-024-02657-2)

## Content

Biallelic pathogenic variants in MAP3K20, which encodes a mitogen-activated protein kinase, are a rare cause of split-hand foot malformation (SHFM), hearing loss, and nail abnormalities or congenital myopathy. However, heterozygous variants in this gene have not been definitively associated with a phenotype. Here, we describe the phenotypic spectrum associated with heterozygous de novo variants in the linker region between the kinase domain and leucine zipper domain of MAP3K20. We report five individuals with diverse clinical features, including craniosynostosis, limb anomalies, sensorineural hearing loss, and ectodermal dysplasia-like phenotypes who have heterozygous de novo variants in this specific region of the gene. These individuals exhibit both shared and unique clinical manifestations, highlighting the complexity and variability of the disorder. We propose that the involvement of MAP3K20 in endothelial-mesenchymal transition provides a plausible etiology of these features. Together, these findings characterize a disorder that both expands the phenotypic spectrum associated with MAP3K20 and highlights the need for further studies on its role in early human development.

Ectodermal dysplasias (EDs) represent a group of more than 200 clinically heterogeneous disorders, characterized by primary defects in the development of two or more ectodermal derivatives, including hair, teeth, nails, sweat glands, and various other glandular structures (Wright 2019). While the collective incidence of all EDs is estimated to be approximately 7 per 10,000 live births (Priolo 2001), the etiology of isolated EDs and ED syndromes often remains uncertain due to their rarity, clinical overlap, and frequent de novo occurrence within families. A subset of these disorders can also be associated with limb anomalies, such as ectrodactyly, in some cases (Wright 2019). Ectrodactyly, also known as split hand/foot malformation (SHFM), is a highly variable phenotype that is characterized by a range of phenotypes, including syndactyly, deep median cleft of the hand and/or foot due to absence of central rays, and aplasia and/or hypoplasia of the phalange (Guero 2019; Priolo 2009; Umair 2020).

Recently, biallelic variants in MAP3K20 (also known as ZAK) have been associated with SHFM type 7 (MIM # 616890), also known as split-foot malformation with mesoaxial polydactyly, in two separate consanguineous families (Spielmann 2016). This phenotype was also characterized by nail abnormalities of the hands in both families and hearing loss in one of the families. However, no other ED phenotypes were noted. Affected individuals had a homozygous missense variant or a homozygous deletion that affected the sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain. Likewise, a homozygous CRISPR/Cas-mediated deletion of the SAM domain of the longer isoform of MAP3K20 (Map3k20-alpha) in mice led to limb defects, including limb duplications and polydactyly with incomplete penetrance (Spielmann 2016). A single individual with SHFM carrying a rare heterozygous missense variant in MAP3K20 (p.(Ala505Ser)) (Funk 2020) was reported, however this individual also had a deletion involving the SHFM1 locus of chromosomal region 7q21.3-q22.1, which includes SHFM1, DLX5, and DLX6 (Funk 2020). Thus, to date, heterozygous variants in MAP3K20 have not been definitively associated with SHFM or other phenotypes in humans.

In this report, we describe five individuals with heterozygous de novo variants in MAP3K20 and associated skeletal findings such as craniosynostosis and limb anomalies, sensorineural hearing loss, and features suggestive of an ED (sparse hair with skin and dental abnormalities). These five variants impact conserved residues in the linker region between the kinase domain and leucine zipper domain of MAP3K20. Thus, we propose that heterozygous variants in this specific region of MAP3K20 cause a novel syndromic form of ED with craniosynostosis, sensorineural hearing loss, limb anomalies, and other skeletal abnormalities.

Quartet exome sequencing (ES) was performed for P1 at the NIH Intramural Sequencing Center using the Sureselect Human All Exon System (Agilent Technologies Inc, Santa Clara, CA) and a GAIIx sequencer (Illumina Inc, San Diego CA). Library construction, sequence generation, sequence alignment to the reference genome, variant calling and potential pathogenic variant identification were performed as described (Adams 2012; Teer 2010). Trio ES was performed for P2 at Hospital Sant Joan de Déu using the Sureselect Human All Exon System (Agilent Technologies Inc, Santa Clara, CA) and a NextSeq500 sequencer (Illumina Inc, San Diego CA). Trio genome sequencing (GS) was performed for P4 at the Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN) sequencing core (Hudson Alpha Clinical Services Laboratory) using the Illumina HiSeq X sequencing platform as previously described (Splinter 2018). Codified Genomics software (https://www.codifiedgenomics.com) was used for the research reanalysis of the data. De novo, biallelic, and X-linked variants with a minor allele frequency of <0.01% within gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) (Karczewski 2020) and the Baylor College of Medicine UDN site’s internal database were prioritized for review. P3 was identified through a search of clinical exome sequencing data at Baylor Genetics; ES was performed according to previously described methods (Yang 2014). P5 was identified both through Baylor Genetics clinical lab as well as through an international collaboration facilitated by Matchmaker Exchange (Pais 2022; Philippakis 2015; Sobreira 2015). Trio genome sequencing was performed for P5 by the Genomics Platform at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard. PCR-free preparation of sample DNA (350 ng input at >2 ng/ul) was performed using Illumina HiSeq X Ten v2 chemistry. Libraries were sequenced to a mean target coverage of >30x. Genome sequencing data was processed through a pipeline based on Picard and mapping done using the BWA aligner to the human genome build 38. Variants were called using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) HaplotypeCaller package version 4.0. GS data was additionally processed to detect structural variants (SVs) using GATK-SV(Collins 2020). SVs were annotated with the GATK SVAnnotate tool. Connections with providers for P2 and P1 were made following deposition of P4’s variant data in ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/1327113/). Sanger sequencing was used to confirm variants in all but P2. All MAP3K20 variants in humans refer to NM_016653.2.

Total RNA was extracted from participant and control dermal fibroblasts using the Qiagen RNeasy kit (Cat. # 74104). One-step reverse-transcriptase PCR was performed using the SuperScript III One-Step RT-PCR System (Cat. # 12574-018, Invitrogen) and primers that amplified MAP3K20 exons 9-11 (Fwd: 5’-AAGCAGTTGCCCCAGAAGTT-3’ and Rev: 5’-TGTCAGCTTTTGCTCCCACA-3’). The resulting cDNA was separated by gel electrophoresis and the individual bands were excised and extracted with the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Cat. # 28704, Qiagen) for Sanger sequencing using the same primers.

The mutated arginine side chain of the Cys273 was built using the crystal structure of the MAP3K20 kinase domain (PDB ID: 5X5O) as a template. The best fitting rotamer with the least structural clash was chosen to build the side chain model, followed by minor adjustments of the chi angles. The structural modelling was done using Coot (Emsley 2010).

P1 is a 17-year-old Mexican, Native American, and Filipino female. Pregnancy was notable for a maternal history of well-controlled type 1 diabetes and bupropion exposure. She was born at 38 weeks of age with Apgar scores of 9 and 9 at 1 and 5 minutes. Neonatal history was unremarkable except for jaundice that did not require phototherapy and natal teeth. Developmentally, there were no concerns for developmental delay with normal achievement of gross motor, fine motor, and language milestones. At age 2 years, a three-dimensional computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrated closure of all cranial sutures, and she was diagnosed with mild chronic papilledema and slight deterioration of the optic nerve heads. Surgical release of the lambdoidal and sagittal sutures was performed. Other relevant clinical findings include a history of hypercholesterolemia and obstructive sleep apnea secondary to adenoid hypertrophy. During her NIH evaluation at 4 years of age, she had microphthalmia, hypertelorism, telecanthus, and bilateral fleshy fibromas in the inner canthi of the eye, micrognathia, sparse hair, sparse, arched eyebrows, and a small nose with convex nasal ridge (Fig. 1). Skin examination was notable for small, brittle dysplastic nails on hands/feet, dry skin on hands and feet, and hyperpigmented atrophic linear plaques in a Blaschkoid distribution (Fig. 1). Skin biopsy of these linear plaques demonstrated atrophic lichenoid dermatitis. Dental examination was notable for alveolar ridges on the upper gingiva and small, pointed teeth. P1 had a quartet ES (parents and unaffected sibling) performed by the Undiagnosed Diseases Program which revealed a de novo heterozygous MAP3K20 variant (c.850_851+1delAGG) resulting in the deletion of the last two nucleotides of exon 10 and first nucleotide of intron 10. Sequencing of cDNA generated from the participant’s fibroblasts demonstrated that this variant leads to an in-frame deletion of arginine at position 284 (p.(Arg284del), Fig. 2).

P2 is a 4-year-old female from Spain. Prenatal ultrasound was notable for hand syndactyly observed at 20 weeks gestation. Pregnancy and birth history were otherwise unremarkable with Apgar scores of 9 and 10 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. Although early developmental milestones were achieved within normal age ranges (walking at 17 months, first words at 12 months), mild motor clumsiness, fine motor skill difficulties and language delays were noted at 3 years of age. Bilateral moderate mixed hearing loss was noted, requiring hearing aids. She had brachyturricephaly noted after birth and bilateral coronal synostosis surgically corrected at 12 months of age. Furthermore, cranial CT revealed a progressive growth of the mastoid process and over the nasal root, along with fibrous dysplasia involving skull base and facial bones (fibrous dysplasia was not found in other bones, Supplementary Figure 1). Brain magnetic resonance imaging also indicated ventriculomegaly with an enlargement of extra-axial spaces. On physical examination, facial findings included sparse and arched eyebrows, hypertelorism, exotropia, epiphora, palpebral ptosis, bilateral nasolacrimal duct obstruction, short nose, anteverted nares, maxillary hypoplasia, and anteverted ears. She also had diffuse sparse hair with histopathologic examination demonstrating trichorrexis nodosa and trichoptilosis. She had terminal transverse defects of her limbs (with brachydactyly, polysyndactyly, and camptodactyly of hands and feet) with absence of nails of some digits. There were keratosis pilaris-like lesions on perioral area, as well as trunk and extremities, which gradually resolved over the months, along with thickened skin in the periocular and perioral areas. She had multiple bilateral and symmetric erythematous and infiltrative patches, some of them with an annular pattern, on her extremities and face, that did not respond to high-potency steroids. The histopathology of these lesions showed psoriasiform epidermal hyperkeratosis with numerous necrotic keratinocytes and reactive keratinocytes. She also showed bilateral linear plantar keratoderma, which started at 3 years of age. Photographs of P2 are shown in Fig. 1. Trio ES identified a de novo heterozygous MAP3K20 variant (c.837_839delCAA p.(Asn279del)).

P3 is a 26-year-old Hispanic female who was born prematurely (unknown gestational age), and examination after birth was notable for ectrodactyly in both feet. Her right foot had 4 toes with syndactyly of the two middle toes, and her left foot had one toe in place of digits 2, 3 and 4. In addition, she had brachydactyly in both hands with clinodactyly of the 4th and 5th fingers bilaterally and diffusely hypoplastic nails. She also had a history of bilateral sensorineural hearing loss requiring bilateral cochlear implants, tooth dysgenesis, and precocious puberty. Although she has a history of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, she had no known history of developmental delays. Photographs of P3 are shown in Fig. 1. Proband ES revealed a heterozygous variant in MAP3K20 (c.817T>C p.(Cys273Arg)); subsequent Sanger sequencing in the parents confirmed that this variant was de novo.

His most recent physical examination from genetics at 4 years of age was notable for dysmorphic facial features, including hypertelorism with upslanting palpebral fissures, scant eyebrows and eyelashes, small nose, downturned corners of the mouth, flat occiput, shallow eye orbits, and papilledema. P5 is a 13-year-old non-Hispanic white female. For prenatal complications, she had intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) and oligohydramnios. She was born at 31 weeks via emergency caesarean section and intubated for respiratory distress related to prematurity. She required extended hospitalization in the neonatal intensive care unit for weaning of respiratory support, feeding difficulties secondary to prematurity, hyperbilirubinemia, and anemia/thrombocytopenia requiring intermittent transfusions. As a newborn, she had a normal echocardiogram and renal ultrasound. However, she had multiple skeletal anomalies of her extremities. The right hand had 4 digits present with missing 3rd digit and with the fourth digit as a single phalanx. In addition, there was an absent distal interphalangeal joint on the index finger. Although the left hand had all 5 digits, syndactyly and camptodactyly were present. She had dysplastic nails in the upper extremities with 4 fingers missing nails while the remaining 4 digits had thickened/unusually shaped nails. For the lower extremities, there was malformation of tibia and fibula on the right with a single ray bilaterally. Radiographs for P5 are shown in Fig. 1. She failed her newborn hearing screen and was diagnosed with bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. She is on an individualized education program (IEP) at school. As a child, she had craniosynostosis involving multiple sutures and multiple ophthalmologic issues (abnormally thick cornea by ultrasound pachymetry, bilateral hyperopia with severe astigmatism, papilledema, and lightly dysplastic right optic nerve). Her most recent physical examination at 13 years of age was notable for ophthalmologic/hearing issues noted above in addition to oligodontia (missing ~7 permanent teeth), scoliosis, microcephaly, patchy areas of cutis aplasia, retention hyperkeratosis, tinea versicolor, and dysmorphic facies/dentition. Notable facial features included pointed ears, hypertelorism, fine nose, delicate nasal bridge, and high and narrow palate. The genetic variant in MAP3K20 identified in P5 causes the same protein change as the variant in P2 and P4 (de novo c.837_839delCAA p.(Asn279del)). All five individuals have de novo heterozygous variants in MAP3K20 identified by ES or GS and that impact exon 10, which encodes a short linker region between the kinase and the leucine zipper domains (Table 2, Fig. 2). Three of the individuals in this report have variants that cause the same in-frame deletion of the asparagine at position 279 which sits between the end of the kinase and leucine zipper domains. This amino acid is highly conserved among vertebrates (Fig. 2), and the variant is not identified in gnomAD (Chen 2024). P3 has a heterozygous variant (c.817T>C p.(Cys273Arg)) that impacts a conserved amino acid in the same region near the end of the kinase domain of MAP3K20 (Fig. 2). This variant is also not identified in gnomAD (Chen 2024). The CADD score is 28.2 and Revel score is 0.544 (Ioannidis 2016; Rentzsch 2021). Sift predicts that this variant is deleterious, and Polyphen-2 predicts that this variant is probably damaging (Adzhubei 2010; Kumar 2009). P1 has a heterozygous novel variant (c.850_851+1delAGG) resulting in the deletion of the last two nucleotides of exon 10 and first nucleotide of intron 10. This variant is also not present in gnomAD (Chen 2024), and cDNA sequencing demonstrated that this variant also leads to an in-frame deletion of a conserved amino acid in this same region (arginine at position 284) between the kinase and the leucine zipper domains (Fig. 2).

Because the sites of the variants are immediately followed by the leucine zipper region, which is potentially involved in dimer formation, we attempted structural prediction of the dimer of MAP3K20 using AlphaFold2-multimer (Evans 2022) in order to analyze the effect of these variants on dimer formation. Interestingly, in the dimer structure, the leucine zipper region was predicted to form a 90 degree bent helix contrasting the continuous long helix of the AlphaFold-predicted monomer structure (Jumper 2021). Because of the different predicted structures for the leucine zipper region, the drastically different domain arrangement, and the fact that the part of the leucine zipper region involved in the dimer formation in the predicted structure is remote from the variant sites, we limited our structural analysis to in silico modeling of these variants using the published crystal structure of the kinase domain (PDB code: 5X5O, Fig. 3A) (Chang 2017) instead of using the AlphaFold predictions.

Our analysis showed that the Cys273Arg variant would cause a steric clash between the arginine side chain and neighbouring residues because of space constraints that cannot accommodate the larger side chain of arginine in place of cysteine. Additionally, the positive charge of the arginine side chain likely destabilizes the surrounding hydrophobic cluster consisting of Met104, Ile107, Leu196, Leu200, and Phe235 (Fig. 3B, Suppl Fig. 2). Asn279 is located in a kink region between two α-helices (Fig. 3A). We note that the ionic interaction between the side chains of Asn279 and Glu282 likely stabilizes the kink conformation (Fig. 3C). Maintaining the side chain conformation of Trp283 is critical for structural stability because Trp283 is forming a π-stacking interaction with the side chain of Phe276 and hydrophobic interaction with the side chain of Lys280, which extend the hydrophobic core. Also, the indole group of the side chain of Trp283 side chain forms a hydrogen bond with the main chain carbonyl of Phe276 adding to the structural stability. We posit that the deletion of either Asn279 or Arg284 could affect the Trp283 conformation resulting in deviation of the helical axis of the following helix in addition to destabilizing the hydrophobic core. Furthermore, deletion of Arg284 would affect the Leu/Ile register of the leucine rich sequence in the helix and could interfere with the hydrophobic interaction with this helix and other residues in the kinase domain.

The clinical phenotype of each individual is provided in Fig. 1, Table 2, and Supplementary Table 1. The most common phenotypic features in this cohort are craniosynostosis involving one or more sutures (4/5, 80%), bilateral sensorineural hearing loss (4/5, 80%), and limb anomalies (4/5, 80%), such as ectrodactyly, syndactyly, missing digits, camptodactyly and brachydactyly. Features suggestive of an ED are present in all individuals. These features include oligodontia, tooth agenesis, or conical shaped teeth (4/5, 80%), sparse hair and/or eyebrows and lashes (4/5, 80%), hypoplastic or dysplastic nails (5/5, 100%), and skin abnormalities, such as hyperkeratosis (3/5, 60%). A wide variety of ophthalmologic complications (4/5, 80%) were also common. Other shared craniofacial features include hypertelorism (4/5, 80%), short/small nose (3/5, 60%), high palate (2/5, 40%), fibrous dysplasia involving skull base and/or facial bones (2/5, 40%), and maxillary hypoplasia (2/5, 40%). P2 had a very interesting finding of progressive bony growth over the mastoid process and nasal root (Supplementary Figure 1). Likewise, 3/5 or 60% of the individuals had a history of developmental delays although, in some individuals, these delays were primarily secondary to hearing loss and/or limb anomalies. Thus, it is unclear if there is a true neurocognitive phenotype associated with this diagnosis. Pregnancy complications such as oligohydramnios, polyhydramnios, and intrauterine growth restriction were noted in 2/5 individuals, and 3/5 were born prematurely (29-31 weeks gestation).

Although previously associated with two different recessive disorders, no autosomal dominant disorders have been ascribed to MAP3K20 in the literature. In this study, we report five individuals with heterozygous de novo variants involving a narrow region of exon 10 of MAP3K20 that forms the linker region between the protein kinase and leucine zipper domains. Three of the individuals (P2, P4, P5) have a heterozygous variant that leads to an in-frame deletion of the same conserved amino acid (p.(Asn279del)). P2, P4 and P5 have an overlapping phenotype that includes limb anomalies, various features consistent with an ectodermal dysplasia, craniosynostosis, and hearing loss. P2 and P4 also have a very distinctive skull phenotype with fibrous dysplasia at the skull base noted in both individuals. P1 has an in-frame deletion impacting the arginine at position 284 (p.(Arg284del)) (Fig. 2A) and an overlapping phenotype that includes craniosynostosis and ED features but lacks the limb anomalies and hearing loss. In contrast, P3 is the only individual within the cohort with a heterozygous missense (p.(Cys273Arg)) variant and appears to have the mildest phenotype. With limb anomalies, hearing loss, and only mild ED features, P3 is also the most similar to that of the published cases with biallelic variants in MAP3K20 (Spielmann 2016). Thus, the affected individuals have an overlapping phenotype that varies depending on the specific variant in the region. Moreover, the similarity of the phenotype in P1, P2, P4 and P5 is supported by in silico modeling that suggests that these two in-frame deletion variants are predicted to have a similar impact on the protein structure.

The two previously reported disorders associated with biallelic variants in MAP3K20 cause two distinct phenotypes that appear to be determined by the domain in which the variants occur. As a mitogen-activated protein kinase, MAP3K20 contains a protein kinase domain, leucine zipper domain and a SAM domain. MAP3K20 dimerizes, and this dimerization is mediated by the leucine zipper domain (Liu 2000; Spielmann 2016). It has two major isoforms, a long isoform and a short isoform, which does not contain the SAM domain (Fig. 2B) (Liu 2000; Spielmann 2016). Functional studies of a reported variant in the SAM domain demonstrated constitutive activation of the kinase in vitro, suggesting that the SAM domain may be necessary for maintaining MAP3K20 (long isoform) in an inactive state until autophosphorylation occurs (Vind 2020).

Split-foot malformation with mesoaxial polydactyly (SHFM type 7), a phenotype similar to our probands, has been associated with biallelic variants impacting the SAM domain of MAP3K20 in two consanguineous families (Spielmann 2016). A homozygous c.1103T>G p.(Phe368Cys) missense variant was identified in MAP3K20 in a Pakistani family (Spielmann 2016). The four affected individuals shared a variable split-foot phenotype, fingernail abnormalities, and sensorineural hearing loss (Spielmann 2016). In the second family, a single affected individual with split foot malformation and duplication of the fingernail bed of the fourth digit but no hearing impairment was identified to have a deletion of exons 12 through 16 of MAP3K20, a region which includes the entire SAM domain (Spielmann 2016). Interestingly, besides the nail abnormalities, no other features of an ED were reported in these individuals (Spielmann 2016).

In contrast, biallelic frameshift and nonsense variants in the protein kinase domain, which is present in both the long and short isoforms, have been associated with a very different phenotype, centronuclear myopathy-6 with fiber-type disproportion (CNM6, MIM: 617760) (Ahmad 2023; Vasli 2017). CNM6 is associated with muscle weakness, hypotonia, and muscle biopsy findings of fiber disproportion (Vasli 2017). The affected individuals exhibited a range of severity, from mild to severe, with some requiring mechanical ventilation but no limb malformations (Vasli 2017). The four variants associated with CNM6 are associated with nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. Thus, biallelic loss-of-function variants in the kinase domain lead to CNM6, whereas biallelic variants in the SAM domain, which have been associated with increased kinase activity (Vind 2020), have been associated with limb anomalies and hearing loss.

While the disorder described here is phenotypically similar to SHFM type 7, it differs genotypically as it is caused by a heterozygous variant whereas the other two MAP3K20-associated disorders are due to biallelic variants. The pLI of MAP3K20 is 0, which indicates that this gene is highly tolerant of heterozygous loss-of-function variants and that haploinsufficiency is unlikely to cause a severe phenotype (Chen 2024). However, this prediction does not preclude the possibility that the heterozygous variants within the kinase-leucine zipper linker region are affecting MAP3K20 activity through a gain-of-function or dominant negative mechanism. Previous studies showed that variants in linker regions of other kinases can alter intra- or inter-molecular interactions and lead to dysregulation of kinase activity (Gonfloni 1997; Heurtier 2017). A gain of kinase activity has been associated with biallelic variants in the SAM domain causing a similar phenotype as our cohort (Spielmann 2016; Vind 2020). Thus, we could speculate that a similar mechanism may explain the pathogenicity of the MAP3K20 heterozygous in-frame deletion and missense variants presented here. If the helix following the linker region continues as a single long helix through the entire leucine zipper region as shown in the AlphaFold prediction of the MAP3K20 monomer, a change in the helical axis caused by the variants would either interfere with leucine zipper dimerization or affect the kinase domain arrangement when the dimer is formed, resulting in dysregulation of the kinase activity. However, although our structural modeling studies support a functional impact of the variants on the protein structure, further studies are needed to evaluate and confirm the disease mechanism.

The association of MAP3K20 with disorders causing abnormalities in the formation of various tissues and morphologic features suggests an early or common developmental function for this protein. MAP3K20 was identified as a strong promoter of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which is an integral, reversible process in human development that enables a polarized epithelial cell to assume a mesenchymal cell phenotype (Li 2018). EMT is required to transition adherent neuroepithelial cells into migratory neural crest cells, which are essential for craniofacial skeleton and cranial suture development (Siismets 2020). EMT signaling is also required for the development of ectodermal features like hair, nails and teeth (Kahata 2018), development of the inner ear (Hu 2012) and during embryonic myogenesis for proper muscle formation (Dill 2021). Providing further evidence for MAP3K20 involvement in EMT, expression of the key EMT transcription factor ZEB1 was shown to require MAP3K20 activity (Li 2018), and expression of TP63, another important EMT transcription factor already implicated in several EDs, was reduced in the affected tissue of MAP3K20 mutant mice (6, 20-21). Thus, the role of MAP3K20 in regulating EMT may provide a unifying explanation for the unique combination of features observed in the described individuals.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence for an autosomal dominant phenotype associated with heterozygous de novo variants in the linker region between the kinase domain and leucine zipper domain of MAP3K20 that encompasses craniosynostosis, limb anomalies, sensorineural hearing loss, and ED. The observed clinical variability highlights the complexity of the disorder and the potential involvement of MAP3K20 in EMT as a plausible etiology of these manifestations. Further research is needed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and genotype-phenotype correlations of MAP3K20-related disease, which will have important implications for genetic counseling and clinical management for individuals affected by MAP3K20-related conditions.
