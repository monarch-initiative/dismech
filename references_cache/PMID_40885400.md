# Dietary Polyunsaturated to Saturated Fatty Acid Ratio as an Indicator for LDL Cholesterol Response: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Clinical Trials.
**Authors:** Hart TL, Damani JJ, DiMattia ZS, Tate KE, Jafari F, Petersen KS
**Journal:** Adv Nutr (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.advnut.2025.100502](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2025.100502)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Dietary guidelines recommend limiting saturated fatty acid (SFA) intake by replacing SFA with polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA). PUFA and SFA have opposing effects on low density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and therefore, the dietary ratio of PUFA to SFA (P:S) may be a better indicator of LDL cholesterol response than SFA alone.

OBJECTIVES: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials was conducted to examine LDL cholesterol responses to higher P:S ratio diets compared with isoenergetic, total fat-matched diets with lower P:S ratios in healthy adults.

METHODS: A systematic search of PubMed, Cochrane Central, and Web of Science was conducted. Randomized complete feeding trials lasting ≥3 wk, including 2 test diets with P:S ratios differing by >0.3 that were matched for energy, fiber, and total fat, were included. Random effects meta-analysis was used to evaluate the mean difference (MD) in LDL cholesterol with higher P:S ratio diets compared to lower P:S ratio diets. Heterogeneity in the effect of the P:S ratio by SFA content of the test diets was also evaluated.

RESULTS: In total, 1001 publications were identified, and 24 publications reporting 24 trials (n = 1011) were eligible. Higher P:S ratio diets (median P:S ratio 1.2; PUFA 10.6% kcal; SFA 8.0% kcal; and MUFA 12.6% kcal) lowered LDL cholesterol [MD -9.83 mg/dL; 95% confidence interval (CI): -13.63, -6.04; I2 = 79%] compared with lower P:S ratio diets (median P:S ratio 0.4; PUFA 4.4% kcal; SFA 12.5% kcal; and MUFA 14.6% kcal). Heterogeneity in the P:S ratio effect was observed by the test diet SFA content (P < 0.001). Higher compared to lower P:S ratio diets lowered LDL cholesterol (MD -15.72 mg/dL; 95% CI: -20.51, -10.92; I2 = 68%) when the test diets differed in SFA (≥2% kcal), but not when diets were SFA-matched (MD -3.45 mg/dL; 95% CI: -7.88, 0.98; I2 = 70%).

CONCLUSIONS: Compared with lower P:S ratio diets, higher P:S ratio diets were associated with greater LDL cholesterol reductions in generally healthy adults, and this effect was stronger when PUFA replaced SFA. This trial was registered at Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) as CRD42023452550.

LDL particles cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [1]. ASCVD remains the leading cause of death in the United States, accounting for ∼60% of cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related deaths [2]. Lowering LDL cholesterol, a surrogate for circulating LDL particle concentration, is a primary target for ASCVD prevention [3,[4]]. Current dietary guidelines recommend limiting intake of SFAs and replacing SFA with unsaturated fat, especially PUFAs [5]. PUFA and SFA have opposing actions on hepatic LDL receptors and, therefore, LDL cholesterol [6,7]. SFA raises LDL cholesterol by reducing hepatic LDL receptor activity, whereas PUFA lowers LDL cholesterol by increasing hepatic LDL receptor activity via decreased receptor turnover and increased receptor synthesis [6,7]. SFA decreases the formation of cholesterol esters, increasing free cholesterol, thus inhibiting the activation of sterol receptor binding protein, which is necessary to increase LDL receptor expression [8]. Conversely, PUFA increases cholesterol ester formation, reducing free cholesterol and upregulating LDL receptor expression [8]. Additionally, PUFA improves cell membrane fluidity to enhance the binding capacity of LDL to receptors [8]. Although the ratio itself has not been explored mechanistically, the opposing actions of PUFA and SFA should be further investigated. Therefore, the dietary ratio of PUFA to SFA (P:S) may be a better indicator of LDL cholesterol concentration than SFA intake alone.

Dietary intake of SFA and PUFA are key modifiable determinants of LDL cholesterol concentration, and therefore CVD risk [9]. Replacement of 5% of energy from SFA with PUFA is associated with a 25% lower risk of coronary artery disease [10]. In alignment, replacing 1% of energy from SFA with PUFA lowers LDL cholesterol by 2.1 mg/dL, and conversely, replacing 1% of energy from PUFA with SFA increases LDL cholesterol by 2.2 mg/dL [11]. Several predictive regression equations have been developed to estimate the effect of diets on LDL cholesterol based on the dietary fatty acid composition. These equations generally include regression coefficients that show SFA increases LDL cholesterol twice as much as PUFA reduces LDL cholesterol [[12], [13], [14]]. This suggests that the effect of SFA on LDL cholesterol is twice as potent as PUFA. Thus, the P:S ratio may assist in estimating the effect of dietary intake on LDL cholesterol.

The dietary P:S ratio was commonly used from the 1960s to the early 2000s [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. However, no recent research has evaluated the dietary P:S ratio as an indicator for LDL cholesterol response. Pooling data from randomized controlled feeding trials to evaluate the dietary P:S ratio as an indicator for LDL cholesterol response is needed to assess the relevance of this ratio. Therefore, the aim of this research was to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials to examine LDL cholesterol responses to diets with a higher P:S ratio compared with isoenergetic, total fat-matched diets with a lower P:S ratio in generally healthy adults. Additionally, we explored heterogeneity in the effect of the P:S ratio in trials with approximately SFA-matched diets (≤2% kcal difference) compared with non-SFA-matched diets (>2% kcal difference) and in the context of SFA recommendations (<10% kcal). It was hypothesized that diets with a higher P:S ratio would lower LDL cholesterol compared to diets with a lower P:S ratio, and there would be no effect heterogeneity based on the SFA content of the test diets.

A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [19] to examine LDL cholesterol responses to diets with a higher P:S ratio compared to isoenergetic, total fat-matched diets with a lower P:S ratio in generally healthy adults, who were free of chronic disease. This systematic review was prospectively registered at the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID: CRD42023452550).

The full inclusion and exclusion criteria for publication selection are presented in Table 1. Randomized clinical trials that included adults (≥18 y) who were relatively healthy regardless of body weight status and blood cholesterol concentration were eligible. Trials including adults with chronic diseases that may affect lipoprotein metabolism were excluded (e.g., diabetes, CVD, and kidney disease). Only controlled feeding trials, defined as providing ≥85% of food to participants, with reported calculated or chemically analyzed macronutrient and fatty acid dietary profiles were eligible for inclusion. For inclusion, trials had to have an intervention period of ≥3 wk because lipids/lipoproteins stabilize within this time period [20]. Only trials examining 2 test diets with P:S ratios differing by >0.3 were eligible to ensure adequate separation between the diets. In addition, test diets had to be approximately matched for total energy, fiber, and total fat. For trials including >2 test diets, data were only extracted for the 2 diets with the most extreme P:S ratios (i.e., lowest and highest). Because phytosterols, trans-fats, and polyphenols are known to affect LDL cholesterol, trials reporting differences in these components between study diets were excluded. Trials including concomitant interventions (e.g., weight loss and exercise) that did not allow isolation of the P:S ratio effect were not eligible. Nonrandomized studies, observational studies, narrative or systematic reviews, meta-analyses, animal studies, and abstract/conference proceedings were not eligible. Trials that did not include postintervention measurement of LDL cholesterol concentration were excluded. A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane Central, and Web of Science and was limited to English with no date restrictions imposed. An initial search was conducted on 15 August, 2023, and updated on 2 January, 2025. Search terms are detailed in Supplemental Table 1. All search results were uploaded into Rayyan Systematic Review Application (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Qatar) [21], and duplicates were removed prior to title/abstract screening. Title and abstract screening and full-text review were conducted in duplicate (TLH, ZSD, KET, FJ, and JJD). Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer.

After full-text screening was completed, data were extracted from all trials into a spreadsheet by one author (TLH). Extracted data were reviewed independently by 2 other authors (ZSD, KSP). The data extracted included: study design (parallel/crossover), length of intervention, participant characteristics (health status, sample size, percentage of males, mean age, and BMI), and intervention and comparator diet characteristics, including macronutrient distribution, fatty acid profile, P:S ratio, and fiber content. Mean LDL cholesterol concentration and variance (SE, SD, 95% CI) at baseline and endpoint (postdietary intervention) were extracted. When reported, the endpoint between-diet difference in LDL cholesterol and variance were extracted. Mean change in LDL cholesterol was calculated as the MD between baseline and endpoint LDL cholesterol concentrations.

Risk of bias was independently assessed by ≥2 authors (TLH, ZSD, KET, FJ, and JJD) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for each publication [19]. Disagreements were resolved by a third author. Risk of bias was identified as low, some concerns, or high for all included studies.

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation tool [22] was used to investigate the certainty of evidence of the effect of the P:S ratio on LDL cholesterol concentration. Two authors (TLH and JJD) independently performed the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation assessment and assigned the certainty of the evidence as very low, low, moderate, or high. The certainty of the evidence was initially rated as high because the meta-analysis was restricted to randomized clinical trials. The certainty was then downgraded based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias or upgraded based on the magnitude of the effect size, presence of confounding, or a dose-response.

The meta-analysis was conducted using the RevMan Web tool (Cochrane Collaboration, 2024 v.7.2.0). The DerSimonian–Laird random effects model was used to account for the heterogeneity in participant characteristics and intervention aims [23]. Heterogeneity was tested using Cochran’s Q test and quantified by the I2 statistic. The primary analysis evaluated the MD in LDL cholesterol concentration following diets with a higher P:S ratio compared to diets with a lower P:S ratio. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out approach to detect influential trials that contributed to heterogeneity. A subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate heterogeneity in the effect of the P:S ratio in trials with approximately SFA-matched diets (≤2% kcal difference) compared with non-SFA-matched diets (>2% kcal difference). Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate heterogeneity in the effect of the P:S ratio on LDL cholesterol by mean baseline BMI and mean baseline LDL cholesterol. Trials that did not report a baseline mean BMI and/or baseline LDL cholesterol were excluded from the subgroup analyses, respectively. For the baseline BMI analysis, trials were divided by those with a mean BMI in the normal range (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) compared with a mean BMI in the overweight/obesity range (≥25.0 kg/m2). For the baseline LDL cholesterol analysis, trials were divided using a median split (i.e., <134 mg/dL or ≥134 mg/dL). An additional exploratory subgroup analysis was conducted to investigate heterogeneity in the effect of the P:S ratio on LDL cholesterol according to whether the intervention and comparator diets met the dietary guidelines for limiting SFA intake to <10% of kcal [5]. For this analysis, trials were divided into 3 subgroups: 1) both intervention and comparator diets had <10% of kcal from SFA; 2) both intervention and comparator diets had ≥10% of kcal from SFA; and 3) the intervention diet had <10% of kcal from SFA and the comparator diet had ≥10% of kcal from SFA. For all analyses, when necessary, LDL cholesterol concentration was converted from mmol/L to mg/dL with the equation: mg/dL = 38.67 × mmol/L. Crossover studies were incorporated by approximating a paired analysis with imputation of the SD of the MD [19]. Briefly, none of the included crossover studies reported the correlation between LDL cholesterol following the higher and lower P:S ratio diets or reported information to derive correlation estimates. Therefore, the SD of the MD was calculated using a correlation coefficient of 0.5. The SD of the between-diet mean difference was estimated using the following formula: SDdiff=√SDhigher P:S ratio2 + SDlower P:Sratio2-(2×0.5×SD higher P:Sratio×SDlower P:Sratio) [19]. A sensitivity analysis was performed using a correlation coefficient of 0.6 to assess the impact of this assumption on the results. Publication bias was investigated by visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry [24]. Data are presented as weighted mean difference (WMD) and 95% CI unless otherwise stated.

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 controlled feeding trials (n =1011) showed that a higher dietary P:S ratio lowered LDL cholesterol concentrations to a greater extent than a lower dietary P:S ratio in generally healthy adults. A higher dietary P:S ratio was associated with a greater reduction in LDL cholesterol when the test diets differed in SFA compared with when the test diets were matched for SFA. This suggests that the dietary P:S ratio is only a predictor of LDL cholesterol response under conditions where SFA is replaced by PUFA. These findings support use of the P:S ratio in conjunction with the dietary fatty acid composition as an indicator for diet-induced LDL cholesterol responses.

It was hypothesized that diets with a higher P:S ratio would lower LDL cholesterol compared with diets with a lower P:S ratio, given that PUFA and SFA exert opposing effects on LDL cholesterol [11]. The results of this meta-analysis support our hypothesis but also highlight that the magnitude of LDL cholesterol lowering observed in response to a higher dietary P:S ratio is driven by the extent to which SFA is replaced with PUFA. Specifically, findings from our subgroup analysis showed that the P:S ratio had the greatest utility when the higher P:S ratio diets were lower in SFA and higher in PUFA than the lower P:S ratio diets. In contrast, when the diets were matched for SFA and differed in PUFA and MUFA, no difference in LDL cholesterol was observed between diets with a higher compared to lower P:S ratio. This aligns with prior research showing that replacement of SFA with PUFA lowers LDL cholesterol [11]. Although limited research has investigated substitution of MUFA for PUFA, available data suggest minimal effect on LDL cholesterol [48,49,]. Our findings are consistent with current dietary recommendations to reduce SFA and replace SFA with PUFA [5] and support the use of the P:S ratio as an indicator for LDL cholesterol responses when SFA is replaced with PUFA.

An exploratory subgroup analysis was conducted to assess heterogeneity in the effect of the P:S ratio on LDL cholesterol based on whether the test diets met the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommendation to limit intake of SFA to <10% kcal [5]. Findings showed that greater LDL cholesterol-lowering occurred when diets had a higher P:S ratio and an SFA content <10% kcal compared with a lower P:S ratio and an SFA content ≥10%. Furthermore, when both diets had ≥10% kcal from SFA, no difference in LDL cholesterol was observed between diets with a higher compared to lower dietary P:S ratio. Interestingly, when both diets had <10% kcal from SFA and an approximately matched SFA content, greater LDL cholesterol lowering was observed with a higher compared with lower dietary P:S ratio. This finding does not align with results from the subgroup analysis showing the P:S ratio does not predict LDL cholesterol response when SFA is matched. However, cautious interpretation is warranted given that study-level subgroup analyses are prone to ecologic bias.

Across all analyses conducted, substantial heterogeneity (I2 = ≥50%) was observed and therefore the results should be interpreted with caution [50]. To identify a potential source of the observed heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis by mean baseline BMI of participants was conducted, and showed no effect modification based on BMI category. Other likely sources that contributed to high heterogeneity include baseline LDL cholesterol and the type of dietary SFA. Evidence supports that adults with elevated cholesterol exhibit larger cholesterol reductions in response to targeted dietary intervention [51,52]. We conducted a subgroup analysis for baseline LDL cholesterol and did not observe any effect modification by baseline LDL cholesterol. However, LDL cholesterol concentration varied substantially within included trials (means ranged from 94–184 mg/dL), which may have reduced our ability to detect baseline LDL cholesterol related effect modification. Among the 24 included trials, only 6 trials required participants to have an elevated LDL cholesterol [25,30,33,37,39,47]; the remaining 18 trials included participants with normal and elevated LDL cholesterol. We were unable to assess whether the type of SFAs included in the test diets contributed to the observed heterogeneity because this information was not consistently reported in the included publications. SFAs exert different effects on LDL cholesterol based on their fatty acid chain length [9]. Although lauric, myristic, and palmitic acids raise LDL cholesterol, stearic acid has a neutral effect [9]. In the 24 included trials, the types of SFAs included in the test diets varied, likely contributing to between-trial variation in LDL cholesterol responses.

A strength of this systematic review and meta-analysis was the inclusion of only controlled feeding randomized clinical trials, which ensures that participants received diets with the target macronutrient profile. A limitation of this meta-analysis is that the fatty acid profile of the test diets was chemically analyzed in only 7 studies and was estimated from various nutrition databases in 17 studies, which may introduce variability and estimation error. Additional limitations include that the correlation coefficient used to calculate the standard deviation of the WMD for crossover trials was assumed to be 0.5, and that half of the included trials took place in North America, limiting diversity. Lastly, the phytosterol composition of the test diets was only reported for a few trials. Although we do not expect that the phytosterol composition differed between diets, any differences may confound the LDL cholesterol response.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 randomized controlled trials provides moderate quality evidence that diets with a higher P:S ratio lower LDL cholesterol to a greater extent than diets with a lower P:S ratio in generally healthy adults. However, the capacity for the dietary P:S ratio to predict LDL cholesterol responses appears to be driven by the extent to which the P:S ratio difference between 2 test diets reflects SFA replacement with PUFA. These findings suggest that consideration of the P:S ratio in addition to dietary fatty acid composition may assist in predicting diet-induced LDL cholesterol responses.
