The clinical significance of comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) has been
established in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (PC). However,
the role of genomic profiling in localized PC remains unclear. In this
exploratory study, we evaluated somatic genomic alterations in localized PC
using an in-house CGP platform to examine their associations with biochemical
recurrence (BCR) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) after radical prostatectomy.
DNA extracted from surgical specimens of 314 patients with localized PC was
analyzed for alterations in 164 cancer-related genes. Six genes (PTEN, BRCA2,
POLD1, ERBB3, MYC, and SETD2) were more frequently altered in patients who
developed BCR in exploratory analyses. Patients harboring alterations in any of
these genes (n = 96) showed higher pathological T stage, increased BCR rates
(27.1% vs. 6.4%), and inferior RFS compared with alteration-negative patients
(n = 218; p < 0.001). In multivariate analysis, the presence of these
alterations was independently associated with worse RFS. Among individual genes,
BRCA2 alteration, and particularly BRCA2-SETD2 co-alteration, were associated
with unfavorable outcomes, although the latter finding was based on a limited
number of cases. In patients who developed BCR, alterations were associated with
shorter PSA doubling time and poorer outcomes after salvage radiotherapy,
particularly in margin-negative cases; however, these subgroup analyses were
based on small numbers and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. These
findings suggest that somatic genomic alterations identified at prostatectomy
are associated with early recurrence in localized PC. Further validation in
independent cohorts is required to determine whether genomic profiling may
contribute to future risk stratification and management strategies.

Radical prostatectomy (RP) remains the gold‐standard treatment for localized prostate cancer (PC), offering favorable oncological outcomes [1, 2]. However, approximately 20%–50% of patients experience biochemical recurrence (BCR) after RP [3]. Conventionally, clinicopathological features, including baseline PSA level, clinical stage, Gleason score, and biopsy characteristics, have been used for recurrence risk stratification. Nevertheless, these risk groups encompass heterogeneous tumor behaviors, making accurate BCR prediction challenging.

Early and accurate detection of recurrence sites is essential for determining salvage therapies in patients with BCR; however, it remains challenging because of the limited sensitivity of conventional imaging, such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and bone scan at low PSA levels (0.2–0.4 ng/mL). Prostate‐specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET) has emerged as a highly effective imaging modality. Studies show that up to 45% of post‐RP BCR patients with PSMA‐PET detection exhibit positive findings, even at low PSA levels (0.2–0.49 ng/mL) [4, 5]. Despite its efficacy, PSMA‐PET remains accessible only in specialized centers, and over half of BCR cases still show no detectable lesions on PSMA‐PET. Therefore, tissue‐based molecular profiling is required to improve BCR risk prediction and guide optimal salvage therapy selection.

Recent studies have established the clinical significance of comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP), including BRCA alterations, and the therapeutic application of poly ADP‐ribose polymerase inhibitor (PARPi) based on CGP results in metastatic castration‐resistant PC (mCRPC). However, the role of CGP in localized PC remains poorly understood [6].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the molecular biological characteristics of localized PC using an in‐house CGP to predict BCR risk and to establish an optimal salvage therapy strategy.

This study included 314 patients with clinically localized PC who underwent robot‐assisted RP (RARP) at Fujita Health University between 2021 and 2023. The primary endpoint was to evaluate the association between the molecular biological characteristics of localized PC and both BCR risk and recurrence‐free survival (RFS). Secondary endpoints focused on identifying which patients benefited from salvage radiotherapy (SRT). BCR was defined as a PSA level > 0.2 ng/mL confirmed on at least two consecutive measurements. At BCR time, SRT (to RP bed) or androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) was administered based on the surgeon's discretion.

Genomic testing was performed in‐house using the PleSSision‐Rapid‐Neo testing platform as described in previous studies [6, 7]. Briefly, the hematoxylin and eosin‐stained slides were marked by pathologists, and genomic DNA was extracted from 10‐μm‐thick formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue sections of microdissected tumor‐rich areas with the use of a Maxwell RSC FFPE Plus DNA Kit (AS1720) for capture probe sequencing. The quantity and quality of DNA were assessed with a 4200 TapeStation System (Agilent Technologies) and high quality DNA, defined as having a DNA integrity number (DIN) ≥ 2.0, was used for the sequencing library preparation. The DNA (10–100 ng) was processed for preparation of a targeted sequencing library with SureSelectXT Low Input Target Enrihment with Pre‐Capture Pooling (Agilent Technologies). Briefly, DNA extracted from FFPE tissues was enzymatically fragmented using the SureSelect Enzymatic Fragmentation Kit and SureSelect custom design panel (Agilent Technologies). Target regions of all 145 genes were specifically enriched using oligonucleotide probes. The overhung DNA fragments were then end‐repaired, adenylated, ligated to index/sequencing adapters, enriched by PCR, and purified according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quality and quantity of the purified pre‐capture library were evaluated using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation with D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies). Hybridization capture and library purification were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. The captured library pools were enriched by PCR and purified and quantified using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation with High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies). The enriched libraries were sequenced with a NextSeq 2000 instrument (Illumina), generating 150‐bp paired‐end reads.

The obtained sequence data were analyzed with the GenomeJack pipeline of Mitsubishi Electric Software Corporation (http://genomejack.net) for mapping to the reference genome and variant calling. Mutation annotations were performed on the basis of information from the COSMIC (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic), ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar), CIViC (https://civicdb.org/home), SnpEff (https://pcingola.github.io/SnpEff), and Clinical Knowledgebase (CKB: https://ckb.jax.org) databases. Copy number alteration (CNA) was calculated from the resulting data. In the case of CNA, for determination of the baseline data used for count correction per amplicon, the number of reads sequenced in each of the 145‐panel amplicon probe design domains was counted so as to calculate the reads per million (RPM) value (the number of reads per one million sequence reads). The coefficient of variation (CV), mean, and median values for RPM of each amplicon in at least 100 FFPE samples were then determined, and the RPM median of the amplicons with a CV of < 0.32 and mean of > 10 was set as the baseline. The number of reads sequenced for each of the 145‐panel amplicon probe design domains was counted to calculate the copy number (CN) and to determine the RPM value for each sample. The log2 (sample RPM median/baseline RPM median) value was determined for amplicons that satisfied the conditions of CV < 0.32 and mean > 10, and the overall SD and median value of this log2 ratio for each gene were calculated. Genes with a log2 ratio median value exceeding the SD or exceeding 2SD were categorized as amplification‐like and amplification, respectively. In addition, genes with a log2 ratio median value below –SD or below −2SD were categorized as loss‐like and loss, respectively. As a control, 50 genomic DNA samples were applied to normalize the read depth per amplicon. Only amplicons with a CV of the read depth of ≤ 1.5 were used. CNA was calculated for genes that had more than six amplicons, and it was measured as the median value of each amplicon per gene. MSI was scored with the use of the MSIsensor program [8, 9], included with the testing panel, with scores of ≥ 0.2 defined as MSI‐high.

Variant call files were converted to mutation annotation files using vcf2maf [10] and processed with maftools [11] in R version 4.3.1 [12]. Briefly, common variants, as defined by GenomeJack, were excluded from the analysis. A variant was considered common if its minor allele frequency exceeded 1% in any of the HGVD, ToMMo, or ExAC population databases, excluding those classified as pathogenic in ClinVar. Pathogenic variants in ClinVar were retained regardless of frequency. For visualization and clinically relevant analysis, only non‐synonymous mutations (e.g., missense, nonsense) and splice site mutations (including both donor and acceptor sites) were considered. We defined an ‘alteration’ in PTEN, BRCA2, POLD1, ERBB3, MYC, or SETD2 as the presence of one or more of the following events: missense mutation, in‐frame deletion, nonsense mutation, frameshift insertion/deletion, splice site mutation, copy number loss (for tumor suppressors PTEN/BRCA2), or copy number amplification (for oncogenes MYC/ERBB3). For analyses focusing on ‘driver alterations’, we further restricted the definition to truncating or loss‐of‐function (LOF) mutations or CN loss/LOSS‐like for PTEN and SETD2; ClinVar pathogenic/likely pathogenic or truncating mutations plus CN loss/LOSS‐like or CN loss for BRCA2; missense mutations in the proofreading domain for POLD1; and CN amplification/AMP‐like for ERBB3 and MYC. VUS were not considered driver alterations unless they met these criteria. This granular ‘driver alteration’ definition was used for additional analyses assessing recurrence‐free survival and other clinically relevant outcomes.

Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or the chi‐squared test. Quantitative variables were analyzed using Student's t‐test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for non‐normally distributed data. Biochemical recurrence‐free survival (RFS) was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method and the log‐rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify RFS risk factors. Variables demonstrating clear statistical significance in the univariate analysis (p < 0.001) were included in the multivariate analysis to minimize model overfitting. All statistical significant tests were two‐sided, with a significance defined as p‐value < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Disease progression following SRT occurred in 13 patients (4.1%). Baseline clinicopathological characteristics did not significantly differ between patients who received SRT and those who received ADT (Table S1).

To evaluate genetic alteration patterns and their clinical correlations, we generated an onco‐plot (Figure 1). Overall, 288 samples (91.72%) showed no detectable alterations. The most frequently mutated genes were AR (17%), KMT2D (16%), and BRCA2 (16%). No significant associations were observed between genetic alterations and clinical factors.

We then analyzed gene alteration frequencies in the patients with BCR. Forest‐plot analysis identified six significantly altered genes (Figure 2A, Table S6): PTEN (OR = 12.624, p < 0.001), BRCA2 (OR = 3.487, p = 0.003), POLD1 (OR = 5.053, p = 0.01), ERBB3 (OR = 4.479, p = 0.02), MYC (OR = 4.019, p = 0.02), and SETD2 (OR = 3.259, p = 0.03). These alterations were present in 96 samples (30.57% of the cohort). BRCA2 alterations were the most prevalent (16% of all samples; Figure 2B).

(A) Forest‐plot of frequently altered genes in patients with biochemical recurrence (BCR). (B) Onco‐plot of six selected genes in 314 samples. Stacked bar plots above and to the right display the frequency of alteration types per sample and per gene, respectively.

Based on these findings, we stratified patients into two groups: those harboring any of the six gene alterations (alteration group) and those without (alteration‐negative group). The alteration group demonstrated significantly higher pathological T stage (p < 0.001), BCR rate (27.1% vs. 6.0%, p < 0.001), disease progression following SRT (11.5% vs. 0.9%, p < 0.001; Table 1), and worse RFS (p < 0.001; Figure 3A). These associations remained significant regardless of RM status (RM1: p = 0.005; Figure 3B, RM0: p < 0.001; Figure 3C). To further account for potentially delayed recurrence patterns, we also performed a cumulative incidence analysis (Figure S1A–C). The overall distribution of recurrence events did not substantially change the interpretation of our original findings, and no clear pattern of late recurrence was observed in either group. Multivariate analysis identified the presence of any six gene alterations as an independent predictor of inferior RFS (HR = 2.812; 95% CI = 1.389–5.309; p = 0.003), with stronger predictive value than clinical factors including RM1 (Table 2).

To focus on alterations with stronger biological impact, we performed additional analyses using a more granular, driver‐focused classification (Table S2). These analyses yielded results that were consistent with the primary findings (Table S3, Figure S2A–C).

For BCR with RM1, SRT was effective in both the alteration‐negative and alteration group (60% vs. 80%, respectively; p = 0.56). In RM0 BCR, outcomes after SRT appeared to differ between alteration‐positive and alteration‐negative patients (10% vs. 100%, p = 0.03) (Table 3); however, this analysis was based on very small numbers (n = 2 and n = 10) and should be interpreted with caution. The alteration group had a higher rate of short PSADT (< 6 months) than the alteration‐negative group in RM1 BCR (92.9% vs. 75.0%, p = 0.56), a difference that was even more pronounced in RM0 BCR (91.7% vs. 40.0%, p = 0.03).

Since BRCA2 was the most frequently altered (35%) of the six genes (Figure 4), we compared the BRCA2 alteration group with the non‐BRCA2‐altered group. The BRCA2 alteration group showed a distribution toward lower pathological stage (pT2: 38.5% vs. 30.8%, pT3a: 46.1% vs. 15.4%, pT3b‐4: 15.4% vs. 53.8%; p = 0.02), and a lower Gleason score (GS) (GS7: 76.9% vs. 38.5%, GS8‐10: 23.1% vs. 61.5%; p = 0.03) despite worse oncological outcomes (Table 4). The BRCA2 alteration group was associated with a significantly higher rate of progression after SRT (53.8% vs. 19.2%; p = 0.04).

Alterations in PTEN (p < 0.001), BRCA2 (p < 0.001), POLD1 (p = 0.002), ERBB3 (p = 0.01), MYC (p = 0.004), and SETD2 (p = 0.004) were associated with significantly worse RFS (Figure S3A–F). In multivariate analysis, pT stage, RM positivity, and BRCA2 alterations were independent predictors of RFS (Table S4). Particularly, BRCA2 alteration was an independent adverse predictor (HR = 2.735; 95% CI 1.789–4.180; p < 0.001) among those factors.

Since BRCA2 alteration was the most frequent and significant among the six selected genes, we assessed the effect of co‐alterations between BRCA2 and the other five genes compared with isolated BRCA2 alteration. The presence of co‐alteration involving BRCA2 with PTEN (p = 0.59, Figure S4A), POLD1 (p = 0.69, Figure S5B), ERBB3 (p = 0.82, Figure S4C), and MYC (p = 0.43, Figure S4D) did not significantly affect RFS, while SETD2 was associated with significantly worse RFS than BRCA2 alone (p = 0.01, Figure S4E). In multivariate analysis, PSA level, pT stage, RM positivity, and BRCA2‐SETD2 co‐alteration were independent predictors of RFS (Table S5). Among these factors, the BRCA2‐SETD2 co‐alteration was associated with inferior RFS (HR = 13.99; 95% CI = 3.972–49.25; p < 0.001), although this finding was based on only five cases and showed wide confidence intervals.

In this single‐center cohort of patients undergoing RP for clinically localized PC, we identified an exploratory six‐gene alteration set (PTEN, BRCA2, POLD1, ERBB3, MYC, SETD2) that was associated with BCR and inferior RFS. In addition, the presence of any alteration within this set predicted worse outcomes independently of conventional clinicopathological factors, including resection margin status. Moreover, the alteration was associated with short PSA doubling time and differences in outcomes after SRT, particularly in margin‐negative patients. Among individual genes, BRCA2 alteration, particularly when co‐altered with SETD2, was associated with unfavorable outcomes, although this finding was based on a very small number of cases and should be interpreted cautiously. These findings suggest that somatic profiling at prostatectomy may contribute to risk stratification in localized PC; however, prospective validation is required before clinical implementation. Importantly, our analyses were restricted to somatic alterations identified from tumor tissue without matched normal sequencing. Therefore, we cannot distinguish somatic from potential germline variants with absolute certainty, nor can we systematically determine biallelic inactivation status. While prior studies have demonstrated biological overlap between germline and somatic BRCA2 alterations, our findings should not be interpreted as directly equivalent to germline BRCA2‐associated disease. The absence of matched normal DNA limits definitive biological interpretation and may affect clinical generalizability. Future studies incorporating paired tumor–normal sequencing will be required to clarify the relative contributions of somatic and germline events.

The six selected gene alterations were individually associated with poorer RFS (Figure S3). Our data align with prior reports showing that PTEN loss can serve as an early predictor of recurrence after surgery, reinforcing its value as a prognostic biomarker in localized settings [13]. While the direct association of POLD1 with PC progression and metastasis has rarely been studied, Wang et al. reported that POLD1 is a key prognostic gene associated with DNA methylation that promotes metastasis [14]. Although ERBB signaling plays an important role in PC, particularly in mediating ERBB3 (HER3) and enzalutamide resistance [15], the clinical significance of ERBB3 genomic alteration in localized PC remains unclear. The C‐MYC oncogene represents one of the most frequent chromosomal abnormalities associated with PC progression. Previous studies demonstrated that elevated C‐MYC mRNA expression predicts BCR after RP [16]. Among the six identified genes, we focused on BRCA2 and SETD2.


BRCA2 alterations were the most common (16% in the entire cohort; 35% in patients with BCR) and an independent predictor of RFS. Moreover, outcomes after SRT appeared less favorable in BCR patients with BRCA2 alterations compared to those without alterations, despite their lower pT stage and GS (Table 4). These results may be consistent with the possibility that BRCA2 alteration is associated with early micrometastatic dissemination before overt local progression.

The frequency of BRCA2 alterations varies across studies depending on patient populations. A meta‐analysis demonstrated that somatic BRCA2 mutations are significantly more frequent than germline mutations, with prevalences of 10.26% (somatic) versus 4.51% (germline) among metastatic patients, and 6.29% (somatic) versus 3.25% (germline) across all prostate cancer stages [17]. Castro et al. reported that germline BRCA1/2 mutations confer a more aggressive prostate cancer phenotype, with a higher probability of nodal involvement and distant metastasis [18]. They further demonstrated that patients harboring germline BRCA mutations had significantly worse survival than non‐carriers when treated with conventional surgery or radiation therapy [19]. These findings underscore the clinical significance of germline BRCA1/2 mutations, particularly BRCA2, in prostate cancer. However, differences in BRCA2 protein function between germline and somatic mutations remain incompletely understood. The rate of biallelic inactivation is reportedly comparable (approximately 70%) between germline and somatic loss‐of‐function BRCA2 carriers in prostate cancer [20]. In addition, Chakraborty et al. demonstrated that heterozygous BRCA2 deletion significantly reduces BRCA2 protein levels in human prostate cancer cell lines [21]. This observation is consistent with our previous finding that somatic BRCA2 copy number loss correlates with poor prognosis in prostate cancer [6]. Moreover, tumor profiling of matched samples has revealed that somatic BRCA2 alterations often occur early in patients who later develop metastatic disease [22], suggesting the clinical importance of early assessment of somatic BRCA2 alterations for risk stratification and treatment planning. In summary, somatic BRCA2 alterations may impair BRCA2 protein function, and their early detection may help predict prostate cancer progression and metastatic risk, independent of germline BRCA2 mutation status.


SETD2, a histone methyltransferase and epigenetic modifier, functions as a tumor suppressor via H3K36 trimethylation but remains understudied in PC biology. Yuan et al. demonstrated that SETD2 inhibits PC metastasis by modulating the EZH2 and AMPK signaling pathways [23], suggesting that SETD2 alteration promote PC metastasis. In this study, the BRCA2‐SETD2 co‐alteration was associated with inferior RFS in multivariate analysis (Figure S4E, Table S5), potentially reflecting early micrometastatic disease. The poor prognosis observed in tumors with BRCA2–SETD2 co‐alteration suggests a potential biological synergy between homologous recombination deficiency and epigenetic dysregulation (Figure 5). Loss of BRCA2 impairs double‐strand break repair and replication fork stability, while SETD2 inactivation disrupts H3K36me3‐dependent chromatin regulation and mismatch repair, thereby promoting replication stress and genomic instability. Prior studies support a critical role of SETD2‐mediated H3K36 trimethylation in coordinating DNA repair, mismatch repair, and replication fork stability, thereby maintaining genome integrity [23, 24, 25]. The combined defects in DNA repair and epigenetic regulation may cooperatively amplify chromosomal instability and mutational burden, facilitating the emergence of aggressive clones with enhanced micrometastatic potential. This mechanism may provide a biological rationale for early recurrence and for the exploratory differences in outcomes after local salvage radiotherapy observed in BRCA2–SETD2 co‐altered cases, particularly in the RM0 setting.

Loss of BRCA2 results in homologous recombination deficiency and impaired double‐strand break repair, while SETD2 inactivation disrupts H3K36me3‐dependent chromatin regulation, transcriptional fidelity, and mismatch repair. Co‐occurrence of these alterations may cooperatively amplify genomic instability, facilitate the emergence of aggressive clones, and accelerate micrometastatic progression, potentially explaining the poor prognosis observed in BRCA2–SETD2 co‐altered tumors.

However, this model remains hypothetical and requires validation in future translational studies. In addition, we previously demonstrated that BRCA2‐RB1 co‐deletion, primarily through somatic heterozygous deletions, confers poor prognoses in localized PC [6], although isolated RB1 alteration showed no significant association with poor RFS in this study.

Our findings informed a hypothesis‐generating conceptual model summarizing exploratory associations between genomic alterations and post‐recurrence clinical patterns (Figure S5). This model is intended solely to illustrate potential biological and clinical relationships observed in our retrospective cohort and should not be interpreted as guidance for clinical decision‐making. First, radical prostatectomy (RP) appears effective for tumors harboring these alterations when disease remains localized, as evidenced by 62.5% (60/96) of alteration‐positive patients remaining BCR‐free. Following BCR, precise localization of recurrence sites is critical for guiding salvage treatments such as SRT, systemic therapy, or metastasis‐directed interventions [26, 27].

In RM0 BCR (Figure S5), outcomes after SRT appeared to differ between groups; however, these analyses were based on very small numbers and should be interpreted cautiously. In high‐risk BCR patients with alterations, early systemic therapy has been discussed in prior literature as a potential therapeutic consideration [28] (Figure S5d,e).

Optimal management of RM0 BCR remains clinically challenging. Given radiation toxicity, unnecessary SRT may be best avoided, particularly in patients without histologically confirmed local recurrence (Figure S5c). In our cohort, RM0 BCR without alterations followed a relatively indolent course (Figure 3C), suggesting that immediate SRT or hormonal therapy may not always be required. However, SRT decisions are typically made without histological or radiographical confirmation, as target biopsy is difficult at PSA < 0.4 ng/mL, and radiological sensitivity is at most 40%, even using PSMA‐PET [4, 5]. Therefore, PSA doubling time (PSA‐DT) remains a key decision metric, being correlated with distant progression and poor salvage outcomes [29, 30]. In this study, outcomes after SRT appeared less favorable in altered RM0 BCR (10%) compared to that without alteration, while short PSA‐DT (< 6 months) was significantly higher in the alteration group (91.7% vs. 40.0%, Table 3). This observation may be consistent with the possibility that short PSA‐DT in RM0 cases reflects early systemic or micrometastatic disease rather than isolated local recurrence. Since PSA‐DT measurement requires longitudinal monitoring, detecting alterations at prostatectomy may help generate hypotheses regarding earlier systemic intervention for aggressive, low‐volume micrometastases (Figure S5f,g), which should be evaluated in prospective studies. Further studies with independent cohorts are required to validate our results and to determine whether genomic alterations could inform salvage treatment approaches, including the timing or intensity of systemic therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, the single‐center retrospective design and relatively short follow‐up may have limited the detection of late recurrence events. Given the restricted sample size and multiple comparisons performed, false‐positive associations cannot be fully excluded, and findings such as the BRCA2–SETD2 co‐alteration (n = 5) and the RM0 SRT subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Second, although the limitation of lacking matched normal sequencing has been discussed above, this constraint may have affected biological interpretation and the ability to distinguish somatic from germline events. Third, heterogeneous alteration types were grouped within each gene, which may obscure functional differences. Finally, recurrence sites after SRT were not radiographically or histologically confirmed, limiting definitive conclusions regarding local versus systemic progression. These findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, requiring validation in independent cohorts.

In summary, targeted somatic profiling at prostatectomy may help refine risk stratification for BCR and RFS. BRCA2, particularly when co‐altered with SETD2, was associated with a higher risk of early recurrence in this exploratory cohort. These observations generate hypotheses regarding the potential role of genomic information in salvage decision‐making, which should be evaluated in prospective studies.
