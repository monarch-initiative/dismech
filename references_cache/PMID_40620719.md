Opsismodysplasia (OPS) is a rare autosomal recessive skeletal dysplasia characterized by a profound delay in bone maturation. Initially described by Zonana et al. [1] in 1977 and later named OPS by Maroteaux et al. [2] in 1984, the condition manifests clinically with rhizomelic micromelia, distinctive facial features such as frontal bossing and a depressed nasal bridge, and radiological findings including delayed ossification centers and severe platyspondyly [1,2].

The genetic basis of OPS was elucidated in 2013 with the identification of variants in theINPPL1gene, which encodes inositol polyphosphate phosphatase-like 1 (SHIP2) [3,4]. While these variants contribute to the pathogenesis of the disorder, there is genetic diversity, and some clinical presentations do not involve INPPL1 variants [3]. Additionally, although OPS can present with renal phosphate wasting and, in some cases, hypophosphatemic rickets, not all patients exhibit these complications.

Bisphosphonates, such as pamidronate, have been utilized in the management of OPS to enhance bone mineralization and mitigate fracture risk. Previous reports have documented the efficacy of bisphosphonate therapy in cases where OPS was complicated by hypophosphatemic rickets [5]. According to current literature, Khwaja et al. [5] are the only researchers to have reported the use of bisphosphonate therapy in OPS. In that case study, two siblings, both severely affected and carrying a homozygous missense variant in exon 17 ofINPPL1(p.Pro659Leu), received bisphosphonate treatment to address significant bone demineralization and renal phosphate wasting. Both children responded positively to the treatment, with the more severely affected child showing significant improvement in lumbar and hip Z-scores. After 7 years of pamidronate therapy, this child, now 10 years old, is no longer confined to a wheelchair, indicating that bisphosphonates may be beneficial for OPS patients experiencing severe bone demineralization [5].

In this report, we present a case of OPS treated with intravenous pamidronate, despite the absence of hypophosphatemic rickets, and highlight a novel variant identified in the gene, which has not been previously reported in the literature. This novel genetic finding could provide new insights into the pathophysiology of OPS and its response to bisphosphonate therapy.

A 2-year-and-2-month-old female patient was referred to our pediatric endocrinology clinic with complaints of inability to walk and short stature. There were no significant features in her personal or family medical history. Anthropometric measurements were as follows: weight – 9.8 kg (SDS: −1.87), height – 69 cm (SDS: −5.4), BMI – 20.8 kg/m2(SDS: 2.73), and head circumference – 49.5 cm (SDS: 0.93).

On physical examination, she exhibited a depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nose, blunt fingers, rhizomelia, O-shaped leg deformity, stage 1 breast development, stage 1 pubic hair, and no axillary hair (shown inFig. 1). Laboratory tests revealed normal hemogram; biochemistry showed Ca: 10.1 mg/dL, P: 4.59 mg/dL, ALP: 200 IU/L, vitamin D: 38.70 ng/mL. Tubular phosphorus reabsorption was 95%. DEXA SDS was −3.16.

The bone survey reported the following: head – normal calvarial bone configuration, J-shaped sella was not observed; extremities – rhizomelic shortening of the femur and humerus, femoral head ossification could not be assessed due to the early age, irregularities and slight widening at the metaphyseal regions, shortening of the metacarpals in the hand; vertebra – decreased height and flattening of the vertebral bodies at the thoracolumbar level consistent with vertebra plana, intervertebral disk spaces were widened; pelvis – the sacrum, pelvic shape, and iliac wing configurations were normal as far as could be evaluated; ribs – thickening and coarsening at the rib ends. These findings suggested a high suspicion of spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. Genetic analysis revealed a novel homozygous variant in theINPPL1gene (c.2111G>A; p.Cys704Tyr) (shown inFig. 3). By Sanger sequencing, segregation analysis showed that both parents were heterozygous carriers for this variant. After obtaining consent for off-label drug use, we initiated intravenous pamidronate treatment (0.5 mg/kg/3 months).

After the initial pamidronate infusions (two courses of 3-day infusions), she began to hold her head up and started sitting up following the second dose. She began walking after the third dose of treatment and has continued to receive treatment for 1 year. Significant improvements were observed, including the achievement of independent walking abilities. By the end of the first year of treatment, whole-body bone mineral density had increased, and the DEXA SDS had improved to −2.5. Skeletal dysplasias encompass a wide spectrum of disorders affecting cartilage and bone development, resulting in diverse skeletal abnormalities. OPS, a rare subtype of skeletal chondrodysplasia, is characterized by growth plate defects, delayed bone maturation, and distinctive clinical features such as short limbs, small hands and feet, macrocephaly, and specific facial characteristics. Radiographically, OPS typically exhibits short long bones with delayed epiphyseal mineralization, metaphyseal cupping, and severe platyspondyly [6,7].

Genetically, OPS is linked to mutations in theINPPL1gene, which encodes the inositol polyphosphate phosphatase-like 1 (SHIP2) protein [8,9]. These mutations disrupt normal cellular function, contributing to OPS pathogenesis, although the genetic heterogeneity suggests involvement of additional factors. Bisphosphonate therapy, particularly pamidronate, has demonstrated effectiveness in improving bone mineral density and reducing fracture risk in OPS patients. The case study, including those of siblings with severe OPS due to homozygous missense variant inINPPL1(p.Pro659Leu), has shown significant clinical improvements following bisphosphonate treatment [5].

The findings indicate age-dependent manifestations of hypophosphatemia, emphasizing the importance of long-term phosphate monitoring in survivors. Additionally, bisphosphonate therapy has proven beneficial in managing severe undermineralization, leading to notable gains in mobility and bone health.

In our patient who is consistent with OPS based on clinical features, hypophosphatemic rickets was not notably observed. This observation highlights the variability in clinical manifestations of OPS and suggests that not all cases present with hypophosphatemia, underscoring the importance of comprehensive diagnostic evaluation in these patients. To date, 29 different variants in theINPPL1gene have been identified in 36 OPS patients. Although the reported variants are most commonly frameshift variants, nonsense, splice site, deletion, and missense variants have also been reported [10]. This is the second article showing a positive effect of bisphosphonate therapy in OPS patients. The case study demonstrated the potential efficacy of pamidronate therapy in patients withINPPL1variant associated with OPS. In the literature, two cases carrying the c.1976C>T; p.Pro659Leu variant in theINPPL1gene showed favorable responses to pamidronate treatment [5]. Similarly, in our patient, who carries a novel missense variant in the same gene (c.2111G>A; p.Cys704Tyr), a positive response to pamidronate was observed. This finding suggests the therapeutic benefit of pamidronate in OPS patients.

Although the underlying mechanism is unclear, these results support the idea that variants in the INPPL1 gene are central to the development of OPS and may also affect how patients respond to treatment. Considering the genetic heterogeneity of OPS, it remains crucial to investigate whether otherINPPL1variants yield similar therapeutic responses to bisphosphonate treatment. We think future studies with larger cohorts are needed to establish a definitive correlation between specificINPPL1variants and bisphosphonate responsiveness.

The clinical variability and genetic heterogeneity observed in OPS underscore the critical role of genetic testing in accurate diagnosis and tailored management strategies. Furthermore, our patient initially presented as nonambulatory, non-sitting, and non-crawling, with a DEXA score of −3.16 SDS. Following the initiation of bisphosphonate therapy, significant clinical improvement was observed, including the achievement of independent walking abilities. By the end of the first year of treatment, her DEXA values had significantly improved, highlighting the therapeutic efficacy of bisphosphonates in enhancing bone mineralization and functional outcomes in OPS patients.

Although the long-term effects of pamidronate therapy are still being explored, potential side effects or consequences of treatment should be considered. These may include concerns about bone health, renal function, and the overall need for ongoing monitoring during therapy. Pamidronate has demonstrated clinical efficacy in improving bone mineral density and functional mobility, but it remains uncertain whether lifelong treatment will be necessary [11]. Continued courses of pamidronate therapy may be necessary at regular intervals to sustain the clinical improvements achieved. The frequency and duration of therapy are highly individualized, and more research is needed to understand if discontinuation of treatment could lead to the recurrence of symptoms or a decline in clinical status over time.

In conclusion, pamidronate treatment has led to significant clinical improvements in our case and in two previously reported OPS cases. However, further research is needed to determine its long-term effects. Although we observed increases in bone mineral density and improvement in mobility with pamidronate treatment, it remains unclear whether lifelong treatment is necessary. Therefore, continuous monitoring and personalized treatment approaches are essential for managing OPS patients.
