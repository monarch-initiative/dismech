BACKGROUND & AIMS: Organs of the gastrointestinal tract contain tissue-resident
immune cells that function during tissue development, homeostasis, and disease.
However, most published human organoid model systems lack resident immune cells,
thus limiting their potential as disease avatars. For example, human intestinal
organoids (HIOs) derived from pluripotent stem cells contain epithelial and
various mesenchymal cell types but lack immune cells. In this study, we aimed to
develop an HIO model with functional tissue-resident macrophages.
METHODS: HIOs and macrophages were generated separately through the directed
differentiation of human pluripotent stem cells and combined in vitro. Following
2 weeks of coculture, the organoids were used for transcriptional profiling,
functional analysis of macrophages, or transplanted into immunocompromised mice
and matured in vivo for an additional 10-12 weeks.
RESULTS: Macrophages were incorporated into developing HIOs and persisted for 2
weeks in vitro HIOs and for at least 12 weeks in HIOs in vivo. These cocultured
macrophages had a transcriptional signature that resembled those in the human
fetal intestine, indicating that they were acquiring the features of
tissue-resident macrophages. HIO macrophages could phagocytose bacteria and
produced inflammatory cytokines in response to proinflammatory signals, such as
lipopolysaccharide, which could be reversed with interleukin-10.
CONCLUSIONS: We generated an HIO system containing functional tissue-resident
macrophages for an extended period. This new organoid system can be used to
investigate the molecular mechanisms involved in inflammatory bowel disease.

Human gastrointestinal organoid technologies have transformed the field of gastrointestinal disease research. By recapitulating the essential signals and stages of embryonic organogenesis in vitro, human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) could be differentiated into human intestinal organoids (HIOs), which contained epithelial and codeveloping mesenchyme.1,2 These HIOs largely recapitulated the morphology, cellular complexity, and transcriptional maturity of the 1st or early 2nd trimester human fetal intestine, especially following the in vivo maturation of HIOs following transplantation into immunocompromised mice.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 hPSC-derived HIOs codevelop an epithelial and mesenchymal component; however, the initial generation HIOs lacked essential cell-types of the complex human intestine, including vasculature, enteric neurons, and immune cells. Manipulation of signaling pathways can promote the codevelopment of these cell types (eg, endothelial cells could be expanded in HIO cultures with added exogenous vascular endothelial growth factor).10 An alternative approach is to separately derive cell types, such as mesenchyme and enteric neurons, from hPSCs and combine these with organoids, resulting in engineered gastrointestinal tissues with functionally innervated smooth muscle.11,12 However, to more faithfully model disease pathogenesis, including inflammatory bowel disease and necrotizing enterocolitis, HIO systems must contain resident immune cells, specifically macrophages, which they currently lack. Tissue-resident macrophages originate from hemogenic endothelium within the yolk sac and aorta-gonad-mesonephros region of embryo and are specified to tissue-specific subsets during organogenesis; resident macrophages contribute to organogenesis and promote tissue regeneration among other niche-specific functions.13, 14, 15, 16 Intestinal macrophages have been recently recognized as essential players in maintenance of intestinal homeostasis and immune response. Additionally, macrophages are capable of engulfing foreign agents including microbes and apoptotic cells and in response secrete cytokines to regulate inflammation.17 However, because of their heterogeneity and poorly understood mechanisms of origin, it has been difficult to have a clear characterization of this cell type.18

Recent efforts by our group and others are focused on engineering organoid models with immune components. Unlike HIOs, human colonic organoids (HCOs) derived from hPSCs not only codeveloped colonic epithelium and mesenchyme, but also contained hemogenic epithelium that gave rise to multipotent erythromyeloid progenitors (EMPs).19,20 These EMPs within HCO cultures spontaneously differentiate into functional monocytes and macrophages that have molecular and transcriptional markers reminiscent of fetal intestinal/colonic tissue resident self-maintaining macrophages. These macrophages were not essential for HCO patterning and growth, but these data suggested that the HCO microenvironment, including mesenchymal macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; CSF1) production, influenced hematopoietic differentiation and maturation of developing myeloid lineage. In contrast to HCOs, the culture conditions to generate HIOs did not favor codevelopment of resident immune cells. As an alternative approach, it was recently reported that HIOs could be transplanted into humanized mice containing human fetal cord blood to introduce immune populations into HIOs.21 Most of the immune cells within the HIO grafts formed subepithelial lymphoid-like aggregates that had a role to promote M cell differentiation of HIO epithelium. Last, Tsuruta et al22 reported a method for injecting hPSC-derived monocyte-like cells into the subepithelial space of apical-out HIOs. On maturation with M-CSF, these macrophage-like cells had some limited functionality, but lacked maturity and the appropriate machinery to respond robustly to inflammatory stimuli. Together, these recent studies demonstrated 3 innovative approaches to introduce immune components into gastrointestinal organoids, but additionally a robust, high-throughput approach to generate HIOs with functional, resident macrophages has not been developed.

In this study, we introduced hPSC-derived monocytes/macrophages into hPSC-derived HIOs to investigate the macrophages’ identity, functionality, longevity, and their utility to study inflammatory responses in the context of HIOs. hPSC-derived macrophages incorporated into HIOs in vitro mounted a robust inflammatory response, phagocytosed bacteria, and expanded along with the HIO tissue following transplantation into mice. Transcriptomic comparison of HIOs with macrophages to human fetal intestinal macrophages suggest that they are tissue-resident intestinal-like macrophages. Our model provides a tractable approach to study of the pathophysiological functions of macrophages in HIOs as an early model of inflammatory bowel disease and to study development of tissue resident macrophages in the developing gastrointestinal tract of humans.

We recently demonstrated that hPSC-derived HCOs codeveloped EMPs that nearly exclusively differentiated into a robust tissue resident macrophages population following 2 weeks in culture, neither of which are present in HIO cultures.20 Therefore, we tested whether it was possible to transfer the EMPs from HCOs into HIOs. The supernatant from Day 21 HCOs, which contained EMPs and early myeloid lineages, was mixed with Day 21 HIOs and replated them into fresh Matrigel and cocultured together for 2 weeks (Figure 1A and B). Although not evident in gross organoids, we observed abundant submucosal CD163+ macrophages in Day 35 HCOs and HIOs that received HCO supernatant (HIO + HCO’s EMPs), but little to no macrophages in control HIO cultures (Figure 1B and C). CD34+ endothelial cells were observed in both HIOs and HCOs. Relative expression of CD163 and CD14 macrophage markers was significantly increased in HIO + HCO’s EMPs compared with HIO control subjects, although it was markedly less that HCO control subjects (Figure 1D). The HIOs expressed higher levels of PDX1 than HCOs, confirming HIOs maintained their proximal/duodenal identity in presence of macrophages. These data show the feasibility of transferring EMP and early myeloid cells from HCOs to HIOs, and that these EMPs differentiated into CD163+ macrophages following 2 weeks coculture. To improve scalability, reproducibility, and to quantitatively control the number of macrophages incorporated into HIOs, we devised an alternative approach to directly differentiate monocytes/macrophages from hPSCs (Figure 2A).23 Differentiation of hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) from hPSCs was performed using the STEMdiff Hematopoietic Kit and these HPCs were differentiated into monocytes/macrophages using exogenous M-CSF and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) for an additional 7–14 days (Figure 2A and B).24 After 3 weeks in culture, this protocol generated >75% viable CD45+ hematopoietic cells with >60% expressing both CD45+ and CD14+, supporting efficient monocyte/macrophage differentiation (Figure 2C). Of these CD45+CD14+ cells, >60% of cells also expressed macrophage marker CD163 and CD11c, whereas CD163- cells had monocyte/premacrophage identity (Figure 2C). When these hPSC-derived monocytes/macrophages were plated in Matrigel and cultured for 2 weeks in HIO media, the CD45+ CD14+ monocyte/macrophage population required exogenous M-CSF and GM-CSF for survival (Figure 2D-F). Next, we incorporate these hPSC-derived monocyte/macrophages with separately derived HIOs generated from aggregation of mid/hindgut monolayer (Figure 3A). We mixed 100,000 hPSC-derived monocytes/macrophages with 3–10 Day 21 HIOs (HIO + Macro) and replated into fresh Matrigel and cultured for 2 weeks in HIO media without supplemented M-CSF and GM-CSF. We confirmed the presence of CD163+ macrophages in Day 35 HIO + Macro but not HIOs without added monocyte/macrophages (Figure 3B). Furthermore, the macrophages were present in HIO + Macro regardless of whether monocyte/macrophages were placed in Matrigel or in the medium overlaying the Matrigel, although mixed in Matrigel gave more consistent abundant macrophages and this method was used in future experiments. Expression of monocyte/macrophage markers CD163, CD14, and ITGAX/CD11c were significantly enriched in Day 35 HIO + Macro that were compared with HIO control subjects (Figure 3C). Additionally, the expression of cytokines interleukin (IL)-6, IL10, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α were all increased in HIO + Macro. Flow cytometry confirmed that approximately 3.2% of all viable cells in the HIO + Macro cultures were CD45+CD14+ monocytes/macrophages, whereas HIO controls had relatively none (Figure 3D and E). CD45+CD14+CD11c+CD163+ macrophages made up 1.58 ± 0.47% (n = 4) of total viable cells within cultures (Figure 3D). Of the CD45+CD14+ population, approximately 50% of cells coexpressed CD163+ and CD11b+ following 2 weeks cocultured with HIOs compared with initially around 61% (Figures 2C and 3D). We next reclustered the mesenchymal and endothelial cells from HIO and HIO + Macro and observed that all conditions largely overlapped (Figure 4G-I). Less variability was observed in mesenchymal populations between separate replicates/differentiations as was seen with the epithelium, although replicate 2 had more SFRP1 PRRX1 early fibroblasts at expense of more intestinal-like subepithelial fibroblasts (Figure 4H-J). HIO + Macro had slightly higher proportion of GDF10 extracellular matrix fibroblasts, which express high levels of collagens, including COL1A1 and COL3A1, than HIO control subjects. Although we found no significant changes in the gene expression between conditions, we did see expression of migratory VIM was trending up in HIO + Macro replicates (Figure 4L). These data suggest that coculture with unstimulated monocyte/macrophages for 2 weeks makes little to no significant changes to HIO epithelial and mesenchymal populations or expression above differentiation-to-differentiation variability.

We showed that the monocytes/macrophages cocultured with HIOs for 2 weeks expressed a lot of common-, gastrointestinal-, and tissue resident-enriched macrophage markers (Figure 3K and L), but it was unclear whether these were expressed by hPSC-derived monocyte/macrophages independent of HIO. We next wanted to determine if the HIO microenvironment was altering the monocyte/macrophage transcriptional signature compared with HIO-naive monocyte/macrophages. To test this, we bioinformatically compared Day 28 and Day 42 hPSC-derived monocyte/macrophages with those isolated HIO + Macro following 2 weeks of coculture (Figure 5A). The Day 28 hPSC-derived monocytes/macrophages represented the timepoint before combination with HIOs and the Day 42 monocytes/macrophages were cultured without HIOs for an additional 2 weeks but required exogenous M-CSF and GM-CSF. We examined the secretion and expression of inflammatory cytokines into the culture medium of HIOs with and without added macrophages using Luminex-multiplexed enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. Without stimulation, HIO + Macro had significantly increased secretion of IL1Ra, IL2, IL8, and CCL3 than HIOs alone (Figure 7C). This was complemented by the increased expression of cytokines and chemokines in HIO + Macro (Figures 3C and 5G). Expression of IL12, IL23, CCL5, and TGFβ were unaltered in HIO cultures with and without macrophages (data not shown). We then investigated if HIO could mount a cytokine storm in response to an inflammatory trigger by adding lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to HIO + Macro cultures (Figure 7A). Addition of LPS for 24 hours stimulated secretion of multiple cytokines including IL1a, IL1B, IL1ra, IL6, IL8, IL10, CCL3, CCL4, and TNF-α as compared with vehicle treated HIO + Macro control subjects (Figure 7D). The expression of IL8, CCL5, and TNF-α, but not IL6, IL10, IL12, IL23, and TGF-β, were all significantly increased with LPS treatment (Figure 7E, data not shown). In the intestine, inflammatory responses of macrophages are held in check with anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL10. We therefore investigated if the inflammatory response induced by LPS in HIO + Macro could be reversed by IL10 (Figure 7A). Treatment of HIO + Macro with IL10 for 24 hours completely reversed the LPS-induced secretion of IL6, IL8, CCL3, and CCL4 (Figure 7F). These results indicate that HIO macrophages have functions consistent with intestinal macrophages.

Several studies have shown hematopoietic cells forming in several waves during development; most of these arise from hemogenic endothelium found at several sites of the embryo, such as aorta-gonad-mesonephros and yolk sac during embryonic development.14,16,30 Tissue-resident immune cells can rise from the lymphoid or the myeloid lineages and have been shown to be present in several tissues. These play a key role in immune response and tissue homeostasis.31 A diverse population of immune cells populate the gastrointestinal tract. Intestinal macrophages are involved in maintaining tissue homeostasis, monitoring for pathogens, and repairing tissue after injury and infection.27,32, 33, 34 We recently identified a method to drive codevelopment of resident macrophages in HCOs derived from hPSCs. These HCO-associated macrophages were functional, mounted an inflammatory response to LPS by producing inflammatory cytokines, migratory, and phagocytic. Unfortunately, we were unable to drive codevelopment of macrophages in hPSC-derived HIOs because the required culture conditions for HIO development inhibited formation of macrophages. However, as described here, we were able to transfer EMPs from Day 21 HCO cultures to HIOs where they integrated into the organoid and differentiated into CD163 macrophages. However, the approach of transferring immune cells from HCO cultures to HIO cultures was laborious and difficult to control and scale-up. We therefore developed a new approach to incorporate immune cells robustly and reproducibly into HIOs using monocyte/macrophages that were separately derived from hPSCs. Within 2 weeks, macrophages had incorporated into HIOs in vitro and persisted following transplantations; we observed CD14+ CD163+ macrophages established tissue residency. Bujko et al35 previously reported that most HLA-DR+ mononuclear phagocytes in the human small intestine were mature macrophages expressing CD14, CD68, and CD163. Macrophages in HIOs seemed to have many of same functions as those observed in vivo and scRNA-seq supported that the macrophages were transcriptionally similar to those of the fetal intestine. From these findings we conclude that hPSC-derived monocyte/macrophages can be reproducibly incorporated into HIOs.

There have been other studies that have combined intestinal epithelium with an immune population.20,21,29 However, they do not focus on the development of both populations while demonstrating direct interaction and inflammatory response from the macrophage population. Coincident with submitting this manuscript, Song et al29 reported a similar approach where macrophages were added to cultures of HIOs, along with enteric neurons, and shown to suppress the growth of transplanted organoid tissue. Although this recent study supports the robustness of this approach, our study additionally demonstrated the functionality of macrophages in vitro and their ability to mount an inflammatory response to LPS and bacteria. Other groups have shown successful coculture of macrophages with primary intestinal tissue to study intestinal inflammation. Kakni et al36 used a microwell system to show the role of macrophages in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and chronic pathologies of the digestive system when combined with mouse-derived intestinal organoids. They showed an increased inflammatory response when adding macrophages. Additionally, studies have been done with human intestinal stem cell–derived epithelial organoids that were cocultured with macrophages to study barrier function and cytokine secretion. Noel et al37,38 showed that the addition of macrophages enhanced barrier function and produced crosstalk between macrophages and epithelium by cytokine production. Our study combines the use of hPSC-derived HIOs and macrophages to coculture both populations and show their functionality, and describing the changes that occur on addition of the exogenous macrophages.

For the HIO recombination experiments, we used a recently published method to force aggregation of the mid-hindgut monolayer to generate the HIOs.23 In addition to allowing the use of cryopreserved cells and hPSC lines with poor spheroid morphogenesis, this aggregation method is a more cost-effective way to scalable up HIO cultures. Surprisingly, HCOs generated by forced aggregation largely lacked the ability to codevelop hematopoietic lineages. Therefore, the use of this aggregation method to generate organoids likely requires independently differentiated immune cells for coculture. We did not observe any major differences in the growth and development of HIO or HCOs with or without macrophages, suggesting that the normal processes of tissue assembly, growth, differentiation, and morphogenesis did not require resident macrophages. Unlike a previously reported model, the macrophages in HIOs generated in this study expressed CD14 and weak levels of TLR4 but responded to LPS. CD14 and TLR4 are expressed in subpopulations of intestinal macrophages, with TLR4 being more markedly expressed within the colon.33,35,39 We observed that exogenous M-CSF or GM-CSF is not required to differentiate or maintain macrophages within HIOs and CSF1 is provided by the organoid mesenchyme. Maintained exogenous M-CSF and GM-CSF on hPSC-derived monocyte/macrophages promoted a more mature yet inflammatory expressional signature and potentially limited the expression of tissue resident and intestinal-enriched genes. More work is needed to assess the transcriptional and functional interactions between macrophages and the surrounding cells within the HIO microenvironment, including epithelial, mesenchymal, neural, vascular, and other immune populations, especially following inflammatory challenges. These interactions will likely be more pronounced using hPSCs from patients with early onset inflammatory bowel disease, or when organoids are challenged with microbiota and/or other inflammatory insults, such as Clostridium difficile.40,41 The addition of macrophages to HIOs can be the ideal model to combine cell lineages with different genotypes to explore intercellular communication and model interactions between cell types in normal development and disease.42,43 In this study, we provide a new scalable model to build on to better understand intestinal immune development and associated diseases.

Dissociation of HIOs and macrophages was performed using protocols modified from several publications.6,20 Briefly, HIO supernatants were collected and spun down at 500 g. HIOs were pretreated with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and cell recovery solution (Corning) to remove Matrigel. Organoids were then finely minced with a scalpel in ice-cold Hanks’ balanced salt solution with Mg2+ and Ca2+ and then pelleted with HIO supernatants. All tubes and tips were coated with 1% bovine serum albumin. Minced tissue was dissociated with enzymes from the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and incubated at 37°C for approximately 60 minutes with fluxing through P1000 and P200 pipette tips, like the process previously published for HIOs. All dissociated cells were filtered through bovine serum albumin–coated 70- and 40-μm cell strainers to remove undissociated tissues. Single-cell suspension with >80% viability was confirmed using a hemocytometer. The Day 28 and Day 42 hPSC-derived monocytes/macrophages-only conditions (ie, not cultured with HIOs/HIO naive) were treated the same as HIOs to control for temporal exposure to enzymes, mechanical disruptions, and temperature swings.

scRNA-seq library preparation for in vitro HIOs was performed by CCHMC Single Cell Genomics Facility using the Chromium 3’v3 Next GEM kit (10X Genomics). Approximately 12,000 cells were loaded to achieve 8000 captured cells per sample to be sequenced. Sequencing was performed by the CCHMC Genomics Sequencing Facility using the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) sequencing platform with an S4 flow cell to obtain approximately 300 million reads per sample. Raw scRNA-seq data were converted to FASTQ files and aligned to a human genome (hg38) using CSBB shiny app (https://github.com/praneet1988/CSBB-Shiny). Human fetal endodermal tissue data were previously published (E-MTAB-10187, E-MTAB-10268, E-MTAB-8221, E-MTAB-9228, E-MTAB-9489) and metadata kindly provided by the Camp laboratory6 and human fetal, pediatric, and adult intestinal datasets were downloaded from https://gutcellatlas.org.25 HCO and d28 Macro datasets (GSE240363) were previous published.20 Individual analysis of all datasets was first performed by running a quality control, and clustering was performed using Seurat v3.6 and v4.2.44 Basic R filtering parameters for gene detection included ≥3 cells and all cells with a minimum of 100 detected genes and a maximum of 8500 genes. Percent.mito parameter was set at all cells <20 and nCounts >1000. Data were normalized using SCTransform Seurat. The cell cycle effect was regressed out using previously established methods in Seurat. Normalized expression levels underwent principal component analysis followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection with subsequent Louvain clustering. Marker genes were determined using the “FindAllMarkers” function (Wilcoxon rank sum test) in Seurat.

After individual analysis, we integrated hPSC-derived datasets and/or human datasets using scRNA-seq Seurat integration and Harmony.45 Specifically, we integrated all cells from hPSC-derived datasets together using 2000 integration features and basic integration methods from Seurat. We annotated clusters using “FindAllMarkers” function. We identified Seurat clusters expressing multiple known epithelial, mesenchymal/endothelial, or myeloid genes and bioinformatically reclustered these cells for further analysis of each cell type. For integration with human datasets, myeloid cells from hPSCs-derived cultures were bioinformatically isolated then integrated with human myeloid-only datasets used as a reference. For all integrations and reclusters we normalized expression levels, ran new principal component analysis, followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection and Louvain clustering. Following normalization and scaling, RNA expression from different datasets was compared using dot plots or feature plots. Seurat’s “MapQuery” function was used to project hPSC-derived query datasets onto human reference datasets based on available metadata classifications. Similarity score predictions were determined by machine learning to deduce relationship function of EleFHAnt as used previously.20 More specifically, EleFHAnt uses expressional reference dataset to train both random forest and “support vector machines” classifiers to assign a similarity score to query dataset cells. Both MapQuery and EleFHAnt are performed on integrated hPSC-derived samples that are not integrated with reference human datasets.
