# Update of PAX2 mutations in renal coloboma syndrome and establishment of a locus-specific database.
**Authors:** Bower M, Salomon R, Allanson J, Antignac C, Benedicenti F, Benetti E, Binenbaum G, Jensen UB, Cochat P, DeCramer S, Dixon J, Drouin R, Falk MJ, Feret H, Gise R, Hunter A, Johnson K, Kumar R, Lavocat MP, Martin L, Morinière V, Mowat D, Murer L, Nguyen HT, Peretz-Amit G, Pierce E, Place E, Rodig N, Salerno A, Sastry S, Sato T, Sayer JA, Schaafsma GC, Shoemaker L, Stockton DW, Tan WH, Tenconi R, Vanhille P, Vats A, Wang X, Warman B, Weleber RG, White SM, Wilson-Brackett C, Zand DJ, Eccles M, Schimmenti LA, Heidet L
**Journal:** Hum Mutat (2012)
**DOI:** [10.1002/humu.22020](https://doi.org/10.1002/humu.22020)

## Content

Renal coloboma syndrome, also known as papillorenal syndrome is an autosomal-dominant disorder characterized by ocular and renal malformations. Mutations in the paired-box gene, PAX2, have been identified in approximately half of individuals with classic findings of renal hypoplasia/dysplasia and abnormalities of the optic nerve. Prior to 2011, there was no actively maintained locus-specific database (LSDB) cataloguing the extent of genetic variation in the PAX2 gene and phenotypic variation in individuals with renal coloboma syndrome. Review of published cases and the collective diagnostic experience of three laboratories in the United States, France, and New Zealand identified 55 unique mutations in 173 individuals from 86 families. The three clinical laboratories participating in this collaboration contributed 28 novel variations in 68 individuals in 33 families, which represent a 50% increase in the number of variations, patients, and families published in the medical literature. An LSDB was created using the Leiden Open Variation Database platform: www.lovd.nl/PAX2. The most common findings reported in this series were abnormal renal structure or function (92% of individuals), ophthalmological abnormalities (77% of individuals), and hearing loss (7% of individuals). Additional clinical findings and genetic counseling implications are discussed.

The association of renal failure and abnormalities of the optic nerve were first reported nearly 40 years ago [Karcher, 1979; Rieger, 1977]. The phenotype was further expanded in subsequent reports [Bron et al., 1989; Weaver et al., 1988]. The syndrome described by these authors has been called both “renal coloboma syndrome” and “papillorenal syndrome” in the literature [MIM# 120330]. The distinction in terminology arises from discussion of whether the optic nerve abnormalities represent a true colobomatous defect, or dysplasia of the optic nerve [Parsa et al., 2001]. Mutations in the PAX2 gene [MIM# 167409] were first identified in 1995 in two families with an autosomal-dominant syndrome characterized by optic nerve abnormalities and renal hypoplasia/dysplasia [Sanyanusin et al., 1995a,b]. Since that time, 52 distinct mutations and three genomic rearrangements have been reported in 173 patients from 86 families worldwide (including those presented in this manuscript).

Due to the rarity of this condition, there are no formal diagnostic criteria. Therefore, it is difficult to determine what constitutes a classic case of renal coloboma syndrome and what percent of affected individuals have mutations in the PAX2 gene. Prior to 2011, there was no actively maintained comprehensive locus-specific database (LSDB) that included up-to-date nomenclature of all published and unpublished PAX2 variation.

PAX2 is a member of the “paired box” (PAX) family of transcriptional regulator genes. Nine members of the PAX family of genes have been described in humans and have been divided into four groups depending upon the presence or absence of four domains: the paired domain, the octapeptide domain, the homeodomain, and the transactivation domain. The PAX2 gene shares similarities with the PAX5 and PAX8 genes in that they all contain a paired domain, octapeptide domain, a partial homeodomain, and the transactivation domain.

The PAX2-paired domain is encoded by exons 2–4 (amino acids 16–142). Most pathogenic mutations are located in the paired domain, which has known DNA-binding properties. The octapeptide domain is encoded by amino acids 185–192 in exon 5 of the PAX2 gene. The octapeptide domain appears to have a role in repression of transactivation of target genes that is mediated through an interaction with members of the groucho family of corepressors [Eberhard et al., 2000]. A single frameshift mutation has been reported in the octapeptide domain [Sanyanusin et al., 1995a]. The partial homeodomain is encoded by exon 7 (amino acids 256–278). This partial homeodomain still appears to have DNA-binding properties, though its exact function is not known. All reported pathogenic mutations in this region are nonsense mutations. A serine and threonine-rich transactivation domain is encoded by exons 8–10. This domain appears to function as an activator and repressor of gene transcription. A limited number of nonsense and frameshift mutations have been reported in this domain.

The PAX2 gene maps to chromosome 10q24.3–10q25 [Narahara et al., 1997; Pilz et al., 1993; Stapleton et al., 1993]. The gene contains 12 exons distributed over approximately 84 kb of genomic DNA [Sanyanusin et al., 1996] spanning nucleotides 102, 505, 468–102–589, 697 (GRCh 37/hg19 assembly, February 2009) (UCSC Genome Database, Accession date August 16, 2011, www.genome.ucsc.edu). Alternative splicing yields five distinct PAX2 transcripts [Ward et al., 1994]. These transcripts are defined by three differences. Exon 6 is an in-frame 69-bp exon that is included in isoforms A and E. Exon 10 is an out-of-frame 83-bp exon that is only present in isoform C. Finally, there are two distinct splice sites at the 3′end of intron 11. The inclusion or exclusion of exon 10, in addition to the competing intron 11 splice acceptor sites, leads to distinct 3′terminus structure and reading frames among the transcripts. None of the known transcripts contain all 12 coding exons of the PAX2 gene.

The normal role of PAX2 in development has been largely elucidated through vertebrate animal models. Five different mouse models of renal coloboma syndrome have been described. The Kidney Retinal Defects (Krd) mouse is a knockout model with a transgene-induced chromosome 19 deletion involving 400 genes, including the PAX2 gene [Keller et al., 1994]. A murine Pax2 knockout model was developed by means of a recombination event resulting in deletion of exons 1 and 2 of the Pax2 gene, including the ATG initiation codon in exon 1 [Torres et al., 1995]. The PAX21Neu mouse model, recovered from an ENU mutagenesis screen for mouse ocular phenotypes, harbors the guanine insertion analogous to the common recurring c.76dup mutation in humans [Favor et al., 1996]. The PAX2A220G model contains a missense mutation (p.Thr74Ala) in the highly conserved paired domain of the murine pax2 gene [Alur et al., 2008]. Most recently, the Opdc mutant mouse, recovered from an ENU screen, harboring a missense allele I40T in the first alpha helix of the paired domain appears to be a hypomorphic model with background strain-specific phenotypic effects on the homozygous phenotype, particularly in respect to the renal phenotype [Cross et al., 2011].

Nearly all homozygous mutant mice are anephric, with most not surviving past the immediate postnatal period. Among the significant findings noted in these mice are midbrain/hindbrain malformations, cranial neural tube closure defects (exencephaly), and absent cochlea. The ocular phenotype is characterized by a failure of closure of the optic fissure and failed basement membrane dissolution. Heterozygous mutant mice recreate many aspects of the human renal phenotype including renal hypodysplasia, cystic renal dysplasia, and oligomeganephronia. Ocular findings in heterozygous mice again are reminiscent of the human phenotype with optic nerve dysplasia, abnormal vessels that emerge from the periphery of the optic disc, and thinning of the retina [Alur et al., 2008; Favor et al., 1996; Porteus et al., 2000].

Zebrafish harbor two copies of the PAX2 gene, called pax2a and pax2b. The expression patterns of zebra fish pax2 expression closely mimic the patterns seen in other vertebrates. A series of pax2a mutants have been described in which the primary feature was the loss of the midbrain/hindbrain boundary, also known as the isthmus [Lun and Brand, 1998]. The no-isthmus or noi mutant zebrafish show abnormal midbrain-hindbrain boundaries, abnormal nephric and otic structures, delayed closure of the optic fissure, and colobomatous eye defects [Macdonald et al., 1997].

PAX2 is highly expressed in the nephric duct and the ureteric bud, which emerges from the nephric duct at E10.5–E11 in the mouse. It is expressed in the metanephric mesenchyme prior to induction, in the cap mesenchyme surrounding the ureteral bud tips, and in the comma and S-shaped bodies. Reduction in PAX2 expression in the visceral epithelium of the future glomeruli of the S-shaped body is accompanied by a marked increase in WT1 expression. In the mature kidney, PAX2 expression persists in the collecting ducts [Dressler, 2011].

There is evidence that one important role of PAX2 during renal development may be suppression of apoptosis in the developing ureteric bud. Increased apoptosis in renal coloboma syndrome may underlie the decreased nephron number, the hypertrophy of remaining nephrons, and the renal hypoplasia that are common in renal coloboma syndrome [Dziarmaga et al., 2006; Porteous et al., 2000].

During normal eye development, PAX2 expression is first observed in the optic sulcus, followed by expression throughout the optic vesicle [Nornes et al., 1990]. PAX2 expression becomes limited to the ventral optic cup and the optic stalk after the optic vesicle invaginates to form the optic cup. In the optic cup and stalk, PAX2 expression is most evident at the closing edges of the optic fissure. As closure of the optic fissure progresses, PAX2 expression is decreased [Otteson et al., 1998]. Following closure of the optic fissure, PAX2 expression remains in some cells in the optic stalk and in-cuff cells surrounding the optic stalk, as it exits the inner layer of the optic cup. PAX2 expression diminishes and appears to be absent in the postnatal eye.

In addition to its normal role in renal and eye development, PAX2 is also known to be expressed at the midbrain/hindbrain boundary, the cerebellum, the hypothalamus, the spinal cord the otic vesicle, and the pancreas.

All publications referencing PAX2 mutations from 1995–2011 were reviewed. This search identified 105 mutation-positive individuals from 53 distinct families. Additional family members with a clinical diagnosis of renal coloboma syndrome, but no mutation testing were excluded from the database. This literature search identified 33 unique mutations in the 53 families, with the most frequently reported recurring mutation being the c.76dup mutation in exon 2. An LSDB was created using the Leiden Open Variation Database software version 2 (Fokkema et al., 2011). www.lovd.nl/PAX2. Each unique family was assigned a family number by the curator. Each mutation positive member of the family was then assigned a unique patient number. Where available, information about ocular phenotype, renal findings, renal pathology, neurological findings, and other incidental findings were collected. Prenatal findings were also noted for cases in which prenatal ultrasound identified renal anomalies and/or oligohydramnios. When available, age at onset, age at diagnosis, age at end-stage renal failure, and age at renal transplant were recorded. When available, information about the familial origin of the mutation was noted, as was the testing status in the parents. For two mutations in the series, the original authors were recontacted in order to clarify the exact nature of the original mutation.

In order to provide a more complete picture of variation in the PAX2 gene, a consortium of three laboratories was formed including The University of Minnesota Medical Center, Fairview in the United States, the University of Otago in New Zealand, and the Genetic department in Necker-Enfants Malades Hospital in Paris, France. These collaborators were able to provide an additional 28 novel variants from 68 new individuals in 33 new families to supplement the records in the database. This represents a more than 50% increase in the total number of reported patients, families, and mutations from the previously published literature. In total, the literature review and consortium identified 53 unique mutations, two variations of uncertain clinical significance, six synonymous variants, and four novel benign missense variations in 173 individuals from 86 families (Tables 1 and 2, and Fig. 1).

The PAX2 literature has been complicated by multiple systems of nomenclature over the years. Initially, mutations were identified in reference to the published mRNA which contained 550 nucleotides of cDNA in the 5′UTR [Eccles et al., 1992]. Legacy names for these mutations have been provided in Table 1.

One factor complicating the nomenclature is that there are no known PAX2 transcripts that contain all of the coding exons. All known transcripts lack either exon 6 or exon 10. Isoform E (NM_003990.3) encodes the longest transcript, though it lacks exon 10. Only one sequence variant to date has been described in exon 10 and this variant has been numbered in reference to isoform C, which is the only transcript containing exon 10 (NM_003988.3).

The significance of exon 10 is unclear at the present time. Inclusion of exon 10 in isoform C results in the inclusion of 83 nucleotides of coding mRNA. This insertion shifts the reading frame and introduces a stop codon in exon 11. This leads to COOH terminus with a unique serine, proline, threonine rich tail, which is not present in other isoforms. In contrast to the other highly conserved exons of the PAX2 gene, exon 10 is not present in mice. Processed mRNA containing exon 10 has been isolated from human fetal and juvenile cDNA libraries, suggesting that this is an expressed transcript in humans [Ward et al., 1994]. Because the biological significance of this particular transcript has not been clearly delineated, it is not certain if one variation identified in this exon is clinically significant.

In this report, nucleotide numbering reflects the cDNA transcript with +1 corresponding to the A of the ATG translation initiation codon in the reference sequence, according to journal guidelines (www.hgvs.org/mutnomen). The initiation codon is codon 1.

Four benign variations have been catalogued in the coding region of the PAX2 gene, three of which are synonymous. The c.867 C/T and c.978 A/C variants in exon 8 of the PAX2 gene were first described in 1999 [Schimmenti et al., 1999]. Both variants are silent at the amino acid level. The “A” allele at the c.978 polymorphism is part of a haplotype that has been associated with reduced nephron number and kidney size in newborns [Quinlan et al., 2007].

The c.360C>T variant in exon 3 and c.1001C>T variant in exon 9 were described in 2000 [Gelb et al., 2000]. The c.360C>T is a synonymous variation. The c.1001C>T is the only published benign polymorphism that alters an amino acid within the coding region of the PAX2 gene. This variant encodes the p.(Ala311Val) amino acid substitution. This variant was identified in the unaffected parent of a child with anophthalmia, but was not found in the affected child, and is thus considered to be nonpathogenic [Lisa Schimmenti personal communication]. These latter two variants have only been reported on one occasion.

Three new synonymous variants were reported as part of this study. The c.453G>A variant was identified in a single individual with chorioretinal colobomas (Supp. Table S1, Family 87). This variant does not alter the amino acid at residue 151. A single individual was identified with a c.510C>A variant in exon 4 of the PAX2 gene. This variant is silent at the amino acid level: p.(Ala170Ala). Finally, the c.804T>C variant was identified in a single individual. This variant is silent at the amino acid level: p.(Phe268Phe). While these variants are not expected to alter an amino acid, the possibility that they are pathogenic through some other mechanism cannot be excluded. Possible mechanisms through which synonymous variations may lead to disease are discussed in detail elsewhere [Sauna and Kimchi-Sarfaty, 2011]

Three additional benign missense variations were identified in this study. The c.478G>A variant leads to a substitution of threonine for alanine at amino acid 160 p.(Ala160Thr). This variation was identified in a fetus with unilateral renal agenesis and multicystic renal dysplasia of the remaining kidney (Supp. Table S1, Family 89). The variant was also present in the unaffected mother. The c.491C>A variant leads to a substitution of asparagine for threonine at amino acid 164 p.(Thr164Asn) (Supp. Table S1, Family 56). This variation occurred in cis with a different pathogenic mutation that segregated with the phenotype in the family. At least one unaffected family member harbored the p.(Thr164Asn) variant without the second pathogenic mutation on the same chromosome. Finally, a c.527G>C variation was identified in a child with microphthalmia (Supp. Table S1, Family 88). This variation predicts an amino acid substitution p.(Ser175Thr). This variation was also present in the child’s unaffected mother. Analysis using the polyphen2 algorithm predicted that all three of these missense variations are likely benign [Adzhubei et al., 2010].

An additional four polymorphisms are located in nearby intronic regions and may be encountered during diagnostic testing: c.43+10 C/G, c.43+43 C/A, c.861+9 G/A, and c.1091–62 G/A. None of these four variants appear to alter conserved splicing sequences.

Two missense variations of uncertain significance were identified as part of this study. Both are in the 3′region of the gene, which is believed to play a role in transcriptional activation and repression. These represent the most 3′variations identified to date.

The c.1102A>T mutation in exon 10 was identified in a single subject with bilateral optic nerve colobomas, bilateral renal hypoplasia, and renal failure at age 14 requiring transplantation at age 16 (Supp. Table S1, Family 77). This individual also harbored a c.410+5G>A variant, which has been reported in one other affected individual [Weber et al., 2006]. At the time of this publication, parental studies had not been completed to determine if the two variants are present in the cis or trans configuration.

The c.1160G>A mutation was identified in a single subject with bilateral optic nerve colobomas (Supp. Table S1, Family 78). Because alternative splicing leads to alternate reading frames at the 3′ end of the PAX2 gene, this variant can either lead to a substitution of asparagine for serine or threonine for alanine. Polyphen2 analysis predicted this to be a benign variant in all isoforms of the PAX2 gene. Family studies could not be performed to determine if this variant segregated with the phenotype in the family.

Excluding the two missense variants of uncertain significance described above, a total of 53 distinct mutations were determined to be pathogenic or likely pathogenic. The majority of mutations in this gene are expected to result in significant truncation of the PAX2 protein through a shift in the reading frame (n = 16), disruption of a conserved splice site (n = 6), genomic deletion or disruption (n = 3), or through the introduction of a premature stop codon (n = 11). These mutations were located in all four known functional domains of the PAX2 gene (paired, octapeptide, partial homeodomain, and the transactivation domain).

There were four recurring mutations in this series. The well-described recurring c.76dup mutation was identified in a total of 26 independent families. This mutation has been described as “619insG” in older literature. Normal parental sequencing in many of these cases demonstrates that this is a recurring mutation, rather than a common mutation derived from a single founder. The other recurring mutations included p.(Arg252*), which was identified in three independent families, the p.(Arg115*) mutation identified in three independent families, and the c.410+5G>A mutation identified in two independent families. Parental testing in all but one of these families demonstrated that the mutations arose independently rather than arising in a single founder.

Seventeen different mutations would still be expected to result in a full-length PAX2 protein. A total of 11 missense mutations and six in-frame deletions or duplications were identified. All of these variants were located in the highly conserved paired domain encoded by exons 2–4. With the exception of the p.(Ala111Thr) mutation, which was identified in two families, all missense and in-frame duplication/deletion mutations were private mutations identified in a single family. All of these missense mutations were expected to be pathogenic based upon: segregation with the phenotype in the family, absence in a large number of controls, location in the highly conserved paired domain, and in silico predictions [Adzhubei et al., 2010]. As discussed above, there is not sufficient evidence to characterize the two missense in exons 10 and 11 as clearly pathogenic at the present time.

Renal disease was the most highly penetrant feature seen in this series, with renal disease identified in 159/173 (92%) of mutation-positive individuals. It should be noted that one of the labs in the consortium is a renal disease laboratory, which may have skewed these findings. Of the 14 individuals without a reported renal phenotype, four had normal laboratory values and renal ultrasound; two were reported to have normal laboratory values only; seven were reported to have “no clinically evident renal disease” without additional data reported, and one individual harbored a variant of uncertain significance. The 92% clinical penetrance should be viewed as a minimum figure for penetrance as several individuals did not have a renal ultrasound to verify the lack of sub-clinical findings.

The most common renal findings in this series were renal hypodysplasia (n = 114; 65%), vesicoureteral reflux (n = 25; 14%), renal cysts (n = 13; 8%), and multicystic dysplastic kidneys (n = 7; 6%). Nineteen individuals (13%) were reported to have “renal failure” without specific details provided about the specific nature of renal anomalies. In some of these 19 cases, the individual may have had a renal transplant prior to the identification of the familial PAX2 mutation and details about the original renal findings were unavailable. The following genitourinary findings were all reported in less than five cases each: UPJ obstruction, medullary sponge kidney, horseshoe kidney, nephrolithiasis, pyeloureteral duplication, renal malrotation, and anomalous renal pelvis. Renal findings that are not part of the typical congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) spectrum included intrarenal teratoma (Family 54, Online supplement 1) and nephrolithiasis (one report each). Many patients presented with more than one renal finding.

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) Stage 5 requiring kidney transplant was commonly reported. Of 53 individuals where the age at diagnosis of CKD Stage 5 was reported, the average age was 19.5 years. The range of onset for CKD Stage 5 was birth to 79 years.

Several authors have addressed the role of PAX2 mutations in renal hypoplasia as a presenting finding either in utero or in childhood. A review of four case series revealed that 13/148 (~9%) of individuals tested due to a primary diagnosis of renal hypoplasia had mutations in the PAX2 gene [Martinovic-Bouriel et al., 2010; Nishimoto et al., 2001; Salomon et al., 2001; Weber et al., 2006]. Of the 13 mutation-positive individuals, further ophthalmological evaluation revealed optic nerve abnormalities in 10 individuals, while three mutation-positive individuals reportedly had a normal ophthalmological exam.

Eighteen of the mutation-positive individuals reported in this series had abnormalities identified by prenatal ultrasound including oligohydramnios/anhydramnios, cystic renal dysplasia, multicystic dysplastic kidneys, and renal hypoplasia. Seven fetuses had severe prenatal renal failure with confirmation of the presence of a PAX2 mutation [Martinovic-Bouriel et al., 2010; Supp. Table S1, Families 31, 55, 71, and 82]. In addition to these seven cases with confirmation of a PAX2 mutation, an additional four cases of severe prenatal renal failure reported in renal coloboma syndrome families where the presence of the familial mutation could not be verified in the affected fetus [Ford et al., 2001; Supp. Table S1, Families 56 and 70].]. In several instances, parents with mild renal disease had pregnancies with severe renal disease presenting in utero.

Classic ophthalmologic findings include optic nerve coloboma, morning glory anomaly, and excavation of the optic disc. At least one large series has demonstrated that PAX2 mutations are not associated with other types of coloboma, such as iris coloboma [Cunliffe et al., 1998].

In this series, 134/173 (77%) patients were reported to have ophthalmological abnormalities. Of the remaining 49 patients, 12 (7%) were reported have a normal ophthalmological exam, while ophthalmological examination was not performed in 37 (21%) patients, most often due to a lack of clinically evident visual problems.

Abnormalities of the optic nerve were noted in 125 cases with the most common findings described as optic nerve coloboma (n = 84); optic disc dysplasia (n = 21); excavation of the optic disc or “pits” (n = 14); optic disc hyperplasia (n = 11); morning glory optic discs (n = 10); and hypoplastic optic discs (n = 7). Additional findings included gliosis of the optic nerve and absent optic nerve head. Several patients were reported to have more than one finding involving optic nerves.

Many patients were reported to have more extensive involvement outside of the optic nerve. Retinal findings were reported in 23 patients (retinal coloboma (n = 6), abnormal retinal pigment epithelium (n = 6), abnormal retinal vessels (n = 8), chorioretinal degeneration (n = 3), and one report each of retinal detachment, retinal staphyloma, and retinal edema). Macular abnormalities were reported in six patients (macular degeneration, papillomacular detachment, hyperpigmentation of the macula, cystic degeneration of macula). Lens abnormalities were reported in two cases (posterior lens luxation, lens opacity). Finally, microphthalmia was reported in three cases.

Iris colobomas do not appear to be part of the spectrum of eye malformations seen in renal coloboma syndrome. None of the mutation-positive subjects reported in this series had iris colobomas. In addition, the authors have not identified mutations in any patients with iris coloboma in their laboratories.

A wide range of nonrenal and nonophthalmological findings have been reported in individuals with renal coloboma syndrome. The PAX2 gene is expressed in many tissues outside the kidney and eye including: the otic vesicle, genitourinary tract, pancreas, cerebellum, hypothalamus, and the midbrain/hindbrain boundary. For some nonrenal and nonophthalmological findings, they fit with the known pattern of expression of the PAX2 gene. Hearing loss was the most common such manifestation in 12/173 individuals (7%). CNS anomalies included one report each of Chiari type 1 malformation, delayed myelination, enlarged extra-axial space, and enlarged cisterna magna. Clinical neurological findings included one report each of seizures, developmental delay, and intellectual disability. Elevated pancreatic amylase was reported in one individual, while gout and hyperuricemia were reported in a single family.

For other findings, a cause/effect relationship was not as clear and in many cases these may have been coincidental findings. Cardiac defects were reported in two cases (one report each of ventricular septal defect and atrial septal defect). Abnormalities of the hand were reported in four cases (clinodactyly (2), polydactyly, and locked metacarpophalangeal joint). Ligamentous laxity and soft skin were reported in some of the original families with PAX2 mutations. Nonrenal genitourinary anomalies included hypospadias, cryptorchidism, and unicornuate uterus were each reported in less than five cases.

Several authors have speculated about potential genotype/phenotype correlations. One author proposed that haploinsufficiency may underlie the renal phenotype, while a dominant negative effect from an abnormal (i.e., truncated) protein may underlie the ocular phenotype. This was based upon the finding of a young girl with renal disease and no ocular phenotype and a cytogenetic deletion involving the PAX2 locus [Benetti et al., 2007]. In contrast, a recent report of a second deletion of the PAX2 gene [Raca et al., 2011] demonstrated that haploinsufficiency may underlie both the ocular and renal phenotypes.

In terms of the type of mutation, no clear genotype/phenotype correlation emerged from this study. Individuals with normal ophthalmological evaluation were found to harbor gene deletions, missense mutations, nonsense mutations, and in-frame duplications. Likewise, individuals with normal renal function were found to harbor both missense and nonsense mutations.

Correlations between the phenotype and the location of the mutation are more difficult to perform. The majority of families reported to date have mutations in the paired domain (38 unique mutations in 66 distinct families), while smaller numbers of families have mutations in the partial homeodomain (five unique mutations in eight families), the transactivation domain (four mutations in four families), or the octapeptide domain (one mutation in one family). Individuals with normal ophthalmological examination were identified with mutations in three of these regions. Individuals with normal renal evaluations were seen both with mutations in the paired domain and the partial homeodomain.

At the present time, it would seem that regardless of the type of mutation or the location of the mutation, most lead to a highly penetrant and highly variable phenotype involving abnormalities of both the kidneys and optic nerve. The tremendous intrafamily variability described in renal coloboma syndrome argues that factors other than PAX2 genotype play a significant role in the clinical penetrance and variable expression of the features of renal coloboma syndrome.

It has been estimated previously that approximately 50% of PAX2 mutations occur de novo, while the remaining 50% are inherited from a clinically affected family member. Of the 86 families presented here, family history information was unavailable in five cases. Of the 81 remaining cases where family history was discussed, the family history was reported to be negative in 53 cases (65%). The de novo origin of the mutation was confirmed by parental testing in 23 of these 53 cases.

Of the remaining 29 families, 14 were two-generation families with a possible de novo origin in the older generation. There were 15 families in which at least three generations were affected and the origin of the mutation was unclear.

In two of the 53 apparently de novo families described above, there was more than one affected child despite the absence of clinical findings or the familial mutation in the parent. Both maternal and paternal germline mosaicism have been reported in families where clinically unaffected and mutation-negative parents gave birth to more than one affected child [Amiel et al., 2000; Cheong et al., 2007]. Taken together, germline mosaicism was identified in approximately 4% of families where the family history was reported to be negative.

In addition to the isolated germline mosaicism described above, a single instance of likely somatic mosaicism is described in a single family (Supp. Table S1, Family 10). In this family, a father with bilateral abnormalities of the optic disc had two children affected with renal coloboma syndrome. The c.76dup mutation was identified in both affected children. Despite the presence of characteristic ophthalmological findings in the father, the familial mutation was not identified in DNA extracted from leukocytes, fibroblasts, and oral mucosa. The mutation was also absent in all three tissues in the mother of the two affected children.

It has been difficult to characterize what percentage of individuals with renal coloboma syndrome actually have mutations in the PAX2 gene. Part of this difficulty arises from the variable phenotypes ranging from isolated disease of the optic disc to isolated renal disease. Another difficulty arises from the fact that many patients evaluated in clinical laboratories do not have phenotypes consistent with renal coloboma syndrome (e.g., iris coloboma). These authors have previously reported that in their collective experience, approximately 23% of probands tested have mutations in the PAX2 gene [Bower et al., 2011]. In one case series of individuals with classic findings of renal coloboma syndrome, mutations were identified in approximately half of the individuals [Dureau et al., 2001].

The explanation for gene-negative individuals with classic findings remains unclear. It is possible that such patients could harbor mutations in promoter, 3′UTR, or deep intronic regions. Analysis for exonic copy number variation by MLPA has indicated that large-scale duplications or deletions are not a common finding (M. Bower and L. Heidet, personal communication). Genetic heterogeneity is another possibility, though no mutations in other genes have been clearly implicated in causing the specific combination of optic nerve and renal abnormalities seen in renal coloboma syndrome.

Family 10 (Supp. Table S1) raises another intriguing possibility, which is that some individuals with classic findings may be mosaic for the PAX2 mutation. The father in family 10 may harbor the mutation in eye tissues and in his germline, but the mutation could not be detected in samples from blood, skin, and oral mucosa. Thus, some probands may test negative because the mutation may not be present in the tissues evaluated.

Renal coloboma syndrome is characterized by extremely variable clinical expression, even within the same family. As illustrated in Family 82 (Supp. Table S1), individuals in the same family can present with childhood-onset renal failure, mild renal insufficiency, normal renal function, and lethal fetal renal hypodysplasia. Even in families with relatively mild clinical presentation, the possibility of severe ophthalmological and/or renal disease should be discussed as part of genetic counseling.

Given that a large proportion of individuals with classic clinical findings have no mutations in the coding region of the PAX2 gene, current research is focusing on the role of noncoding variation and other genes in renal coloboma syndrome. One obvious group of candidate genes are the transcriptional targets of the PAX2 protein and current efforts are attempting to determine if these downstream genes may also be candidates for causing renal coloboma syndrome. It is possible that these downstream targets may also play a role in the tremendous intrafamily and interfamily clinical variability seen in renal coloboma syndrome.

Genomic rearrangements, which would not be detected by standard Sanger sequencing, have been identified in a limited subset of patients with renal coloboma syndrome. Screening of other patients by MLPA has not revealed intragenic deletions to be a common cause of renal coloboma syndrome. Efforts are underway to determine if such rearrangements are found in any subsets of patients with renal coloboma syndrome.

Another area of interest is more completely characterizing the clinical aspects of renal coloboma syndrome. The renal and ophthalmological aspects of the syndrome are highly penetrant and can be easily connected with known patterns of PAX2 expression in development. Other aspects of the syndrome, such as hearing loss, are not as highly penetrant, but can still be plausibly connected to known patterns of PAX2 expression. A large number of nonrenal and nonophthalmological findings have been reported in affected individuals. It is hoped that establishing a comprehensive LSDB with detailed clinical data may help to elucidate which of these clinical findings may constitute a part of the renal coloboma syndrome, and which findings may simply be coincidental.

Finally, it is of interest to determine the exact mechanism (or mechanisms) by which PAX2 mutations lead to renal coloboma syndrome. Haploinsufficiency is believed to underlie the phenotypes for other PAX genes, such as PAX6-related aniridia [Prosser and van Heyningen, 1998]. In addition, mouse models have demonstrated that haploinsufficiency could plausibly underlie several aspects of renal coloboma syndrome including reduced optic nerve axon number [Alur et al., 2008], and decreased branching of the ureteric bud [Clark et al., 2004; Dziarmaga et al., 2003; Dziarmaga et al., 2006].

While haploinsufficiency remains the most plausible mechanism by which PAX2 mutations cause renal coloboma syndrome, the possibility that other mechanisms may contribute cannot be excluded. Most PAX2 mutations are nonsense or frameshift mutations. It is not clear if these abnormal transcripts are degraded through a mechanism such as nonsense-mediated RNA decay, or if the transcripts produce a partially functional PAX2 protein with abnormal DNA binding or transactivation properties. There are similar questions for in-frame mutations such as missense mutations and small in-frame indels. Such mutations have been shown to alter the folding of critical DNA-binding domains or alter DNA-binding properties in the PAX3 gene associated with Waardenburg syndrome [Birrane et al., 2009]. It remains possible that missense mutations and in-frame indels could exert additional dominant effects through novel sequence binding.
