# Cep78 knockout causes sterility and oligoasthenoteratozoospermia in male mice.
**Authors:** Liu M, Wen Z, Zhao D, Tian W, Lv Q, Zhang C, Zhang X, Meng F, Liu H, Gao J, Yao Z
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-024-84006-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-84006-x)

## Content

Oligoasthenoteratozoospermia (OAT) is a common cause of infertility among males, and the majority of cases of idiopathic OAT are thought to be attributed to genetic defects. In this study, the role of the CEP78 protein in spermatogenesis was initially investigated using Cep78 knockout (Cep78-/-) mice. Notably, the male Cep78-/- mice exhibited the OAT phenotype and sterility. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the functions of the Cep78 gene in spermatogenesis, the histomorphology of germ cells was investigated during different stages of mitosis, meiosis, and spermiogenesis. Apoptotic assays and RNA-sequencing analyses were additionally performed using the testicular tissue samples of control and Cep78-/- mice. The findings strongly suggested that defects in the Cep78 gene can lead to male infertility with OAT and that the CEP78 protein is essential for acrosomal biogenesis, sperm head shaping, and formation of flagella during spermiogenesis. The findings are expected to expand the spectrum of genetic defects in OAT and enhance the accuracy of genetic screening and clinical diagnosis.

Infertility is a serious global health concern that affects approximately 15% of couples actively attempting to conceive1. Oligoasthenoteratozoospermia (OAT), which is characterized by a reduction in sperm count, impaired sperm motility, and abnormal sperm morphology, is a prevalent factor that contributes to infertility among males2. However, despite extensive clinical investigations, the precise causes underlying the incidence of OAT remain to be completely elucidated to date.

Spermiogenesis represents the concluding stage of spermatozoal maturation, and is characterized by the intricate and meticulously orchestrated differentiation of spermatids. This transformative process entails significant nuclear and cytoskeletal modifications in the round haploid germ cells, which ultimately leads to the development of highly polarized spermatozoa with fertilization potential3. The transformation of round haploid germ cells into mature spermatozoa is characterized by three significant modifications, namely, the formation of the acrosome that envelops the upper portion of the nucleus, species-specific nuclear compaction and restructuring, and the development of the flagellum that functions as a motility apparatus4. It has been reported that defects in the genes involved in these processes contribute to acrosomal deformities, defects in the sperm head, and flagellar immobility, which lead to the occurrence of OAT and male sterility5–7.

In order to understand the molecular origins of OAT, it is necessary to elucidate the underlying processes by which the intricate molecular complexes are assembled during spermiogenesis. Previous studies have identified that mutations in the Cep78gene induce OAT or male sterility in humans, and the underlying mechanisms were further investigated8–10. However, there are other potential mechanisms underlying the role of Cep78 in spermiogenesis, which remain to be completely understood. In this study, the Cep78 knockout (KO) mouse model was used to represent the typical OAT phenotype. Phenotypic comparison revealed that the observed phenotypes of this model aligned with those documented in individuals with loss-of-function mutations in the Cep78 gene. The present study confirmed that the depletion of the CEP78 protein may lead to the occurrence of OAT and contribute to male infertility. The findings further demonstrated that the CEP78 protein plays a crucial role in spermiogenesis by regulating the expression of genes associated with the formation of the accessory structures of the sperm head and flagella.

The Cep78−/− mice were generated by using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing system provided by VIEWSOLID BIOTECH (Beijing, China). The fertilized eggs of C57BL/6 mice were co-injected with gRNAs and Cas9 mRNA and induced a 1548-bp deletion including exon 2 to exon 4 of the Cep78 gene, the gRNA target sites are as follows: AGCCAACTCTAGTGGACTTGG and CGTACACCTTTAATCACTTGG. The founder and F1 animals were identified by DNA sequencing, and the genotypes of offsprings were determined by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification.

The PCR primers were designed around the gRNAs (Fig. 1A), with reverse primer 2 (R2, TAGTATGCCAAGGCACAGAAATGA) designed in the deleted region, and forward primer (F, ATGAGCTAGATTTGCTAGGGGC) and reverse primer 1 (R1, AGTCCAGTGTCTATCAGGGCT) designed in the non-deleted region. Therefore, in the WT allele, F and R2 can amplify a 793-bp band, while F and R1 cannot amplify a band due to the long distance. In KO allele, F and R2 do not amplify a band, while F and R1 can amplify a 1049-bp band. Amplification was performed for 3 min at 95 °C, followed by 33 cycles, with each consisting of 30 S at 95 °C, 30 S at 60 °C, and 30 S at 72 °C, with a final extension step of 10 min at 72 °C.

The mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation, following which the testes were dissected out and preserved by overnight fixation in Bouin’s solution for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining, or in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence staining and TUNEL assays. The samples were subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5-µm-thick sections for subsequent H&E and PAS staining. Sperm smears were prepared from the epididymis.

For preparing the testis smears, the testes were stripped of the testicular capsule, cut into pieces in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer. The filtered cell suspensions were then evenly distributed onto a glass slide. The tissue sections and sperm smears were stained using H&E and PAS staining techniques as previously described11.

The TUNEL assay was performed using a TUNEL Kit (#KGA7072, keyGEN BioTECH, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the immunofluorescence studies, the tissue sections and sperm smears were initially blocked in 5% normal goat serum for 30 min and subsequently incubated overnight at 4℃ with the primary antibodies or Peanut Agglitinin (PNA) (#L7381, 1:1000, Sigma Aldrich, Germany). The tissue sections were subsequently washed thrice with PBS-containing Tween 20, incubated with the secondary antibodies at 37℃ for 1 h, and finally incubated with 4’, 6-diamidino-2- phenylindole (DAPI). The fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, BX53, Japan). The antibodies used in this study are enlisted in Table 1.

Proteins were extracted from mouse testicular tissues using a universal protein extraction lysis buffer, which was enhanced with a protease inhibitor (1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride, PMSF) to prevent degradation. The extracted proteins were subjected to separation via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), employing either 10% or 12.5% gels. Following electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA) for immunoblot analysis. These membranes were first blocked with Protein Free Rapid Blocking Buffer (Shanghai Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) for 20 min to prevent non-specific binding. They were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight to allow for specific protein recognition. After a thorough wash with TBST to remove unbound antibodies, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h to facilitate detection. Finally, the protein bands were visualized using the Spark ECL super kit (Sparkjade, Shandong, China) and the Tanon 5200 Fully Automatic Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System.

Semen cells collected from the shredded caudal epididymal tissues were subjected to TEM analysis. The samples were post-fixed in 1% osmic acid for 1 h at 4 °C, stained in aqueous 3% uranyl acetate for 2 h, dehydrated with ascending concentrations of acetone, embedded in epoxy resin, sliced into ultra-thin sections, and stained in 0.3% lead citrate. The ultrastructure of the spermatozoa was finally visualized under a JEM-1400 Flash transmission electron microscope.

For the SEM analysis, the spermatozoa were incubated overnight in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C, washed twice with water (for 5 min per wash), and dehydrated through a graded alcohol series. The glass slides were gently glued to the conductive adhesive, and the specimens were subsequently sputter-coated using an ionic sprayer meter (Eiko E-1045, Hitachi Ltd.). The spermatozoa were finally visualized using a scanning electron microscope (Inspect, FEI, USA).

Sperm motility assessments were conducted in accordance with previously established methods12. Briefly, 10 µL of the sperm specimens was placed in a glass cell chamber, and the temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating platform. The spermatozoa were subsequently examined using an Olympus B× 51 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the observation area of each chamber was captured using a CCD camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The total sperm count, motile sperm count, and proportion of progressively motile spermatozoa were subsequently determined by computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA); Hamilton Thorne, Beverly, MA, USA) of the sperm samples.

The total RNA was extracted from the entire testes at 8 weeks post birth. RNA-Seq libraries were prepared from 1 µg of RNA using an NEBNext UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA). Sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the DESeq2 package in R. The DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses using the clusterProfiler package in R. Library construction, sequencing, and data analyses were performed by Annoroad Genomics Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China).

The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 8.0. The data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD)(n = 3). P values less than 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. The data obtained from the control and experimental groups were compared using two-sided Student’s t tests.

Previous studies have reported that CEP78 is a centriolar wall protein that localizes to mature centrioles and partakes in regulating centrosomal duplication13,14. In order to investigate the biological functions of CEP78, Cep78 null mice were generated in this study using a CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing technology. Sanger sequencing of the genomic DNA of the Cep78 KO mice revealed that a 1548-bp fragment was deleted between exons 1 and 5 of the Cep78 gene (Fig. 1A). The genotypes of the offspring, WT, heterozygous, and KO mice were identified by PCR amplification (Fig. 1B). The results of western blotting further confirmed that the full-length CEP78 protein was absent in the testes of Cep78−/− mice (Fig. 1C). The subcellular localization of the CEP78 protein was further determined by immunofluorescence co-staining with anti-CEP78 and anti-α/β-Tubulin antibodies (Fig. 1D). The findings revealed that the CEP78 protein was primarily expressed in the head-to-tail coupling apparatus of the elongated spermatids and near the centrosome of the metaphasic spermatocytes of WT mice, but was not detectable in the Cep78−/− mice. Altogether, these findings confirmed the successful depletion of the CEP78 protein in Cep78−/− mice.

The Cep78−/− mice were healthy and exhibited normal growth, but the male Cep78−/− mice were completely sterile (Fig. 2A). The size and weight of the testes of the male Cep78−/− mice were normal and comparable to those of the control WT mice (Fig. 2B, C). Histological examination revealed that both spermatocytes and round spermatids were present in the testicular seminiferous tubules and epididymis of male Cep78−/− mice, but elongated spermatids were absent in male KO mice (Fig. 2D).

The number of spermatozoa in the cauda epididymis of the Cep78−/− mice was significantly lower than that of the control WT mice, and H&E staining of the spermatozoa revealed the occurrence of teratospermia in the Cep78−/− mice (Fig. 3A). Semen analysis by CASA revealed that the motility, progressive motility, curvilinear velocity, and other sperm function parameters of the Cep78−/− mice were significantly lower than those of the control WT mice (Table 2). Additionally, the majority of spermatozoa exhibited negative or aberrant PNA staining (marker of the outer acrosomal membrane of spermatids) in Cep78−/− mice (Fig. 3B). Compared to that of the WT control, the spermatozoa of the Cep78−/− mice exhibited severe morphological defects, including hyper-constricted head shapes and multiple flagellar deformities, such as short, bent, curved, or absent flagella (Fig. 3C). Additionally, the results of SEM and TEM analyses revealed the absence of mitochondria and axonemes, and the principal segment of numerous sperm tails exhibited a defective 9 + 2 microtubular pattern and absence of outer dense fibers (Fig. 3D, E and F). Altogether, these findings indicated that the Cep78 gene plays a crucial role in spermatogenesis and male fertility.

To determine whether the knockout of the Cep78 gene affects the mitosis, meiosis, and spermiogenesis of germ cells, the testicular sections of the adult 8-week-old mice were studied by immunohistochemical staining using DDX4 (germ cell marker) and SOX9 (marker of sertoli cells). The findings revealed that there were no significant quantitative differences about germ cells and sertoli cells between the WT control and Cep78−/− mice (Fig. 4A). The proliferation of germ cells was normal in both groups as indicated by the Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen marker (Fig. 4B). The number of differentiated spermatogonia was similar between the control WT and Cep78−/− mice as indicated by Promyelocytic Leukemia Zinc Finger and KIT proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase markers (Fig. 4C). Additionally, the number of cells that stained positive for γH2AX (marker of unrepaired DNA lesions), STRA8 (marker of meiotic initiation), and SYCP3 (marker of the synaptonemal complex) was comparable between the WT control and Cep78−/− mice (Fig. 4D). These findings therefore indicated that the knockout of the Cep78 gene did not affect mitosis or meiosis, suggesting that spermiogenesis was disrupted in the Cep78−/− mice.

Acrosomal biogenesis, sperm head shaping, and flagellar elongation are committed processes of spermiogenesis. In this study, the acrosomal structures were first detected by PNA staining of the spermatozoa of the control WT and Cep78−/− mice across the 12 stages of spermiogenesis. Although acrosomal formation should have ideally progressed into stages VII–VIII of spermiogenesis, the PNA-positive structures were found to be poorly condensed and exhibited a more expanded staining pattern after stages IX–X of spermiogenesis in Cep78−/− mice (Fig. 5A). The results of PAS staining further revealed that the sperm head shapes were irregular after stages IX–X of spermiogenesis (Fig. 5B). Secondly, the manchette organization, which is a transient structure formed during spermiogenesis (steps 8–14 in mice) was also investigated in this study. The manchette was characterized by the presence of elongated or misshapen microtubules in the spermatids at steps 11–13 (Fig. 5C), suggesting that the distal migration of the manchette might have been defective. The manchette is essential for sperm head shaping and flagellar elongation.

To explore the mechanism underlying the regulatory role of CEP78 in spermatogenesis, RNA-Seq analysis was performed to compare the transcription profiles in the testes of adult WT and Cep78−/− mice. The findings revealed that there was a good consistency of the transcription levels of all genes between the WT and KO mice (Fig. 7A). The genes with a fold change in mRNA expression higher than 2 or less than 1/2 (KO/WT) were considered to be significantly upregulated or downregulated, respectively. A total of 291 genes were found to be differentially expressed, including 173 down-regulated and 118 up-regulated genes (Fig. 7B, C). The majority of downregulated genes were associated with spermatid, sperm head, and flagellar development (Fig. 7D). Altogether, these findings indicated that the knockout of the C78 gene can affect gene transcription profile during spermiogenesis.

OAT is characterized by infrequent sperm maturation, reduced sperm motility, and structural abnormalities in spermatozoa. It is a prevalent cause of infertility among males, and poses a threat to the reproductive well-being and cohesion of affected families15. Zhang et al. identified a pathogenic splicing mutation in the Cep78gene among male patients with infertility8. The present study investigated the potential role and functional significance of the CEP78 protein in spermatogenesis by employing a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout technology to generate Cep78 KO mice. The CEP78 null mice were infertile and displayed a phenotype similar to that of human individuals with OAT. This finding suggested that the ablation of Cep78 contributes to the development of OAT in both humans and mice.

It has been reported that the majority of cases of OAT are attributed to genetic defects16. Previous studies have demonstrated that disruptions in KATNB1, KATNAL2, and CFAP61cause morphological abnormalities in spermatozoa and hinder sperm production, which ultimately leads to the occurrence of OAT and infertility in both humans and mice17–19. Additionally, mutations in TDRD6 and NANOS1have been detected in patients with OAT, indicating their potential role in the development of idiopathic OAT20,21. Comparing the phenotype of Cep78 knockout mice with other known spermatogenesis-related gene knockout models, such as Tdrd6 and Nanos1, helps to clarify the specific role of CEP78. Tdrd6knockout mice exhibit severe sperm head abnormalities and reduced sperm motility22,23, while Nanos1knockouts result in a complete loss of germ cells due to failed meiotic initiation24. In contrast, our study found that Cep78 knockout mice showed normal mitotic and meiotic progression but had significant defects in spermiogenesis, particularly in acrosomal biogenesis and flagellar formation. This comparison underscores the unique role of CEP78 in the later stages of spermatogenesis.

Using murine models, previous studies have demonstrated that mutations in Galnt3, Prdm9, and Mkrn2are implicated in the development of OAT; however, these mutations have not been observed in human subjects with OAT25–27. The CEP78 protein is highly expressed in spermatocytes and spermatids, and is believed to play a crucial role in spermatogenesis (Please refer https://www.proteinatlas.org/about/licence for details on citing The Human Protein Atlas). The present study further demonstrated that defects in the Cep78 gene are associated with the development of OAT in both humans and mice. The application of mouse model results to humans offers several advantages, such as the ability to study gene function in a whole organism and the potential to model human diseases. However, there are also limitations, particularly in terms of species-specific differences in gene function and regulation. While our study found that Cep78 knockout in mice resulted in infertility and OAT phenotypes similar to those reported in humans with CEP78 mutations, there may be differences in the penetrance and expressivity of these phenotypes between mice and humans. For instance, while human patients with CEP78 mutations may present with a range of phenotypes, from mild to severe, our mouse model exhibited a more uniform and severe phenotype. Further studies are needed to understand these differences and to what extent the mouse model can accurately predict the human condition.

The human Cep78gene is located on chromosome 9q21.2 and consists of 18 exons that encode a protein of 689 residues. This gene encodes a centrosomal protein that plays an essential role in regulating centrosome-related processes throughout the cell cycle28. The CEP78 protein contains an N-terminal leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain that consists of six consecutive LRR repeats, as well as a C-terminal coiled-coil domain. The protein interacts with the N-terminal catalytic domain of polo-like kinase 4 (PLK4) and co-localizes with PLK4 at the distal end of the centriole. The CEP78 protein plays a crucial role in facilitating the centrosomal localization of PLK4 and the PLK4-induced overduplication of centrioles29. The CEP78 protein also plays an essential role in the formation of cilia30, and is implicated in various genetic disorders, including retinal degeneration, sensorineural hearing loss, cone-rod degeneration disease, and Usher syndrome10,31,32. Mutations in the Cep78gene can lead to defects in primary cilia. The CEP78 protein interacts with the EDD-DYRK2-DDB1VprBP complex to inhibit the transfer of ubiquitin from EDD to CP110, which is crucial for maintaining centriolar length and ciliary assembly33. Additionally, the CEP78 protein has been found to interact with CEP350 to promote ciliogenesis by downregulating the expression levels of CP110 via an EDD1-dependent mechanism34. In the context of spermatogenesis, CEP78’s role in centriole and flagella formation is particularly crucial. Centrioles are the basal bodies from which cilia and flagella extend, and they are essential for the formation of the manchette, a structure necessary for sperm head shaping and flagellar elongation. Our study revealed that the absence of CEP78 led to defects in the formation of these structures, resulting in spermatozoa with abnormal head shapes and flagellar deformities. The CEP78 protein’s role in centriole duplication and maintenance of centriolar length is therefore critical for proper spermiogenesis.

In the present study, the male Cep78-deficient mice were completely infertile and exhibited severely impaired spermatogenesis. The study also revealed that the CEP78 protein simultaneously regulated acrosomal biogenesis, sperm head shaping, and flagellar formation during spermatogenesis. Additionally, loss of function of the Cep78 gene inhibited spermatogenesis at stages VII and VIII, which led to defects in sperm head shaping and acrosomal biogenesis as revealed by PAS and PNA staining. Investigation of the manchette structure and tubulin immunofluorescence staining revealed that the CEP78 protein plays an important role in the formation of flagella. The concentration of spermatozoa in the epididymis of Cep78−/− mice was significantly lower than that of the WT mice, and the results of CASA revealed that there was a scarcity of motile spermatozoa in the epididymis of Cep78−/−mice. Oligospermia is primarily characterized by a marked reduction in the number of spermatids due to the apoptosis of germ cells or impaired release of spermatozoa from the seminiferous epithelium35. The results of TUNEL assays indicated that the germ cells underwent apoptosis in Cep78−/− mice. Furthermore, the results of RNA-Seq analysis revealed that the genes encoding components of the head and flagella were significantly downregulated in Cep78−/− mice compared to those of control WT mice. Our study found that the knockout of the Cep78 gene led to increased germ cell apoptosis, suggesting that the loss of CEP78 function may trigger apoptotic pathways, leading to a reduction in sperm count. This finding is significant as it implicates CEP78 in the survival and differentiation of germ cells during spermatogenesis. Additionally, we observed no effect of Cep78 knockout on meiosis, which contrasts with other gene knockouts that disrupt meiotic progression. This suggests that CEP78 specifically influences post-meiotic events, such as spermiogenesis. Furthermore, the defects in acrosome formation observed in Cep78 knockout mice highlight the importance of CEP78 in the biogenesis of this essential structure for sperm function. Hypothetically, the absence of CEP78 may disrupt the balance of proteins required for acrosome formation, leading to the observed abnormalities. Further research is needed to elucidate the exact mechanisms by which CEP78 influences these processes and their biological significance in the context of male fertility.

Our study focuses on CEP78’s key role in male infertility, complementing Zhang et al.‘s work. While Zhang et al.‘s research shows CEP78’s effects on sperm and its connection to USP16 and Tektin, our study uses CRISPR/Cas9 to create Cep78−/− mice, revealing infertility and sperm abnormalities. Zhang et al. uses genetic sequencing to identify CEP78 mutations, whereas we use genome editing and cellular analysis to understand CEP78’s role in sperm development. Both studies highlight CEP78’s importance in spermatogenesis, with Zhang et al. discovering a new mutation and our study detailing sperm morphology and motility issues in Cep78−/− mice. In summary, our findings indicated that the knockout of the Cep78 gene induces the development of the OAT phenotype in mice. The findings obtained herein enhance our comprehension of the processes involved in the development of sperm head shape and flagella during spermiogenesis (Fig. 8). These insights may contribute to the advancement of more effective therapeutic strategies for male infertility caused by genetic factors.
