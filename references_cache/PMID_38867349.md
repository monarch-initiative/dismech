Juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML) is a hematologic malignancy of young
children caused by mutations that increase Ras signaling output. Hematopoietic
stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is a potentially curative treatment, but
patients with relapsed or refractory (advanced) disease have dismal outcomes.
This phase II trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of trametinib, an oral
MEK1/2 inhibitor, in patients with advanced JMML. Ten infants and children were
enrolled, and the objective response rate was 50%. Four patients with refractory
disease proceeded to HSCT after receiving trametinib. Three additional patients
completed all 12 cycles permitted on study and continue to receive off-protocol
trametinib without HSCT. The remaining three patients had progressive disease
with two demonstrating molecular evolution by the end of cycle 2. Transcriptomic
and proteomic analyses provided novel insights into the mechanisms of response
and resistance to trametinib in JMML. ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier:
NCT03190915. Significance: Trametinib was safe and effective in young children
with relapsed or refractory JMML, a lethal disease with poor survival rates.
Seven of 10 patients completed the maximum 12 cycles of therapy or used
trametinib as a bridge to HSCT and are alive with a median follow-up of 24
months. See related commentary by Ben-Crentsil and Padron, p. 1574.

Juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML) is an aggressive myeloproliferative neoplasm of infants and toddlers(1). Upfront therapies typically include high-dose cytarabine or azacitidine, but the only definitive treatment is hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)(2–4). While HSCT cures ~50% of patients, the prognosis is dismal for those who relapse with most children dying from organ failure due to progressive JMML or transformation to acute myeloid leukemia (AML)(5–8). In the absence of a second HSCT, patients who relapse have a 2-year overall survival of ~10%(9).

JMML is initiated by germline and somatic driver mutations in NF1, KRAS, NRAS, PTPN11, CBL and, rarely, other genes(10). These mutations converge on Ras, resulting in constitutively elevated levels of active Ras-GTP in specific cell lineages(11). Growth factor receptor activation contributes to aberrant growth of JMML cells by stimulating nucleotide exchange on Ras, which further increases Ras-GTP levels, resulting in hyperactivation of the Raf/MEK/ERK (mitogen activated protein kinase; MAPK) pathway and other Ras effectors such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/Akt/mTOR)(12,13).

Genetically engineered mouse (GEM) models initiated by Nf1 inactivation or by oncogenic Kras or Ptpn11 expression were developed that accurately model key molecular, biologic, and biochemical features of JMML(14–17). Preclinical trials of the allosteric MEK inhibitors PD0325901 and trametinib in Kras and Nf1 mutant mice demonstrated dramatic phenotypic responses with reductions in white blood cell counts, resolution of splenomegaly, and reversion to a normal pattern of erythropoiesis(18,19). Mechanistically, MEK inhibitor treatment restored a normal pattern of proliferation and differentiation in Kras and Nf1 mutant hematopoietic cells but did not eliminate them(18,19).

Trametinib is an orally bioavailable allosteric inhibitor of MEK1/2(20) that is FDA-approved for the treatment of adults with advanced melanoma with BRAFV600E or BRAFV600K mutations(21) and for children with solid tumors in combination with dabrafenib(22). MEK inhibitors have recently shown efficacy in several pediatric brain tumors driven by MAPK pathway activation including gliomas(23) and in plexiform neurofibromas in children with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1)(24). Based on these observations and the promising efficacy signal in GEM models, we evaluated trametinib in a first ever prospective clinical trial in children with relapsed or refractory JMML.

Consistent with the known gender imbalance and young age at presentation in JMML(1), ADVL1521 enrolled 8 boys and 2 girls at a median age of 23.6 months (range of 10.7 – 78.7 months; Supplementary Table 1) who met WHO criteria for JMML at diagnosis and had molecular confirmation of a Ras pathway mutation at enrollment (Table 1). Each mutation (3 KRAS, 3 CBL, 2 PTPN11, 2 NRAS) was present at a high VAF at enrollment. One patient had a heterozygous germline CBL mutation with loss of heterozygosity in the JMML clone. The other nine Ras pathway mutations were somatic. Three patients had relapsed after HSCT and seven were refractory to chemotherapy and/or azacitidine.

Five patients had an objective response including three with a clinical partial response (cPR) and two with clinical complete response (cCR) (Table 1 and Figure 1). Two additional patients had stable disease (SD) and three others had progressive disease (PD). All seven patients with a cCR, cPR, or SD are alive with a median follow-up of 24 months from the first dose of trametinib (Figure 1). Patients 1, 4 and 9 completed the maximum 12 cycles of trametinib permitted on study and remain in clinical remission while continuing to receive trametinib off-protocol for an additional 6, 24, and 24 months, respectively. Patients 5, 7, 8, and 10 underwent HSCT after responding to trametinib (3 with a cPR and 1 with a cCR). These patients were previously deemed ineligible for HSCT by their treating physicians due to poor clinical status from visceral organ involvement, which improved dramatically after treatment with trametinib. All remain in remission with a median follow up of 24 months. Patients 2, 3, and 6, who had progressive disease on study, subsequently underwent HSCT but eventually relapsed. Patients 2 and 6 died with refractory JMML and patient 3 is alive following a second HSCT (Figure 1). Three patients enrolled on the trial who relapsed after HSCT and seven enrolled after being refractory to upfront therapy. One patient who relapsed after HSCT had SD and the other two had PD. Of the seven patients who were refractory at enrollment, 5 had an objective response, one had SD and one had PD. The overall survival at 4 years is 80% (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Serial targeted deep sequencing showed no significant reduction in the VAF of the initial Ras pathway mutation during treatment with trametinib. This included all patients with objective responses and those who completed 12 cycles on study and continue to receive trametinib off-protocol. By contrast, the Ras pathway mutation present at study enrollment was not detected after HSCT in the four patients who were transplanted after responding to trametinib. Two of three non-responders exhibited molecular evolution during treatment, which included outgrowth of cells harboring a NF1 mutation in patient 2, and monosomy 7 in patient 3, both meeting criteria for progressive disease. The PTPN11 mutation present at enrollment persisted at a high VAF in the patient with a secondary NF1 mutation.

Morphologic review is of limited value for assessing therapeutic responses in JMML, which is characterized by heterogeneous populations of myeloid and lymphoid cells at various stages of differentiation and less than 20% BM blasts at diagnosis(3). Two children with elevated BM blasts at enrollment (8.6% and 5.3%, respectively) progressed by the end of cycle 2 (Supplementary Table 2A-B). Bone marrow aspirates along with complete blood count and differentials were obtained at study entry and at each subsequent BM assessment. While there were no statistically significant changes in blood cell counts, there was a trend toward reduced total monocyte counts in patients who responded to trametinib. Interestingly, two of three patients who continued to receive trametinib after completing 12 cycles on study had thrombocytopenia at study entry that resolved after 6 months and has not recurred.

To determine how trametinib led to clinical responses, we analyzed samples from nine patients that were available for correlative biologic studies (Supplementary Table 3). Pre- and post-trametinib transcriptome (RNASeq) and multiplexed kinase inhibitor bead (MIB) kinome profiling data were obtained from 8 and 6 patients, respectively. Altogether, integrated RNASeq and kinome data from paired samples collected at the same timepoints was available in 5 patients and revealed on-target activity of trametinib. Specifically, gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) of bulk RNASeq data identified MAPK pathway downregulation among top-ranked differences between pre- and post-treatment samples. Functional kinome profiling showed that MAP2K1 (MEK1) and MAP2K2 (MEK2) exhibited loss of binding (indicative of decreased kinase activity) in post-treatment peripheral blood (PB) and BM samples (Figure 2A-B)(Supplementary Fig. S2)(Supplementary Table 4). Similarly, MAPK signaling was downregulated in post-treatment samples using bulk RNASeq (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Strikingly, gene sets associated with inflammatory signaling pathways including interferon, interleukin 6, tumor necrosis factor alpha and transforming growth factor beta were also downregulated after trametinib treatment (Supplementary Fig. S3B) and the MIB binding of multiple SRC family proteins were markedly reduced in response to trametinib in the absence of significant changes in mRNA expression (Figure 2C). An exception to this overall pattern was SRC, which showed decreases in both MIB binding and mRNA expression (Figure 2C). Additional integrated RNASeq and kinome analysis showed that MAP2K1 and MAP2K2 mRNA levels were unchanged despite a reduction in MIB binding after treatment (Figure 2C). In the 5 patients from whom data was available, MAP2K1 and MAP2K2 binding decreased after exposure to trametinib in patients 5, 7, and 8 who responded to treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4A-B), while MAP2K1 binding persisted and MAPK26 binding increased in the two non-responders (patients 2 and 4)(Supplementary Fig. S4C-D). In addition, we observed increased binding of several kinases associated with the cell cycle including CDK6, CDK9, WEE1, and AURKB in the non-responders (Supplementary Fig. S4C-D).

To gain deeper insight into how trametinib might affect distinct populations of hematopoietic cells, we generated single cell RNASeq data before and after treatment from 7 patients (n= 2 CR, 2 PR, 2 SD, 1 PD) from a median 6109 cells per sample (n= 12 BM, 2 PB). These data were used to map each single cell to a hematopoietic lineage and to ascertain any changes after trametinib treatment (Figure 3A). Pseudotime analysis revealed a more stable state of hematopoiesis (Supplementary Fig. S5). The most prominent difference in pre- and post-trametinib samples was a reduction in the proportions of classical and non-classical monocytes in BM and PB (Figure 3B-C). The percentage of B lymphocytes was markedly increased in post-trametinib samples, including naïve, pro-B and pre-B cells. By contrast, there was reduced representation of naïve and natural killer T cells after trametinib treatment. Additionally, there was broad downregulation of immune-related pathways in all cell populations. However, the downregulation of MAPK signaling genes was confined to specific cell types including macrophages (MΦ), classical monocytes, granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMP), and multipotent lymphoid progenitors (MLP) which uniquely displayed negatively enriched KRAS-related signatures following treatment. This observation suggests that trametinib therapy preferentially modulates MAPK signal output in specific cell lineages (Supplementary Fig. S6). Similarly, we also observed that the downregulation of SRC signaling was confined to select myeloid cell types including classical monocytes, macrophages (MΦ), and myeloid dendritic cells (Supplementary Fig. S7A-D).

DNA hypermethylation and the presence of >1 mutation at diagnosis independently correlates with aggressive disease and poor outcomes in JMML(10,25,26). We assessed DNA methylation and classified patient samples as having low, intermediate, or high methylation according to international consensus criteria(25). Six patients had low methylation, three had intermediate or high methylation and one patient was not evaluable (Supplementary Table 5). Methylation status was consistent from diagnosis to relapse and was not altered by trametinib therapy (Supplementary Table 5). Both patients with high methylation progressed after receiving 1 and 2 treatment cycles, respectively. These were also the only two patients with more than one pathogenic mutation at enrollment (Supplementary Table 5).

Trametinib was well tolerated with no dose limiting toxicities observed across the entire cohort, which started 55 cumulative cycles. There were no adverse events (AE) related to cardiac dysfunction of any grade. The only grade 4 AE was thrombocytopenia in a patient that also had progressive disease during the same cycle (Supplementary Table 6). There were seven grade 3 AEs deemed possibly related to trametinib – these events included neutropenia in two patients and one patient each with anemia, sepsis related to a viral infection, or grade 3 hypertension. As noted above, three patients continue to receive trametinib off label 6–24 months after completing 12 cycles on study.

Informed by the central role of Ras pathway mutations in JMML and promising preclinical data in GEM models, we conducted an open label, phase 2 trial of trametinib in children with relapsed or refractory disease. This is also the first completed study of a MEK inhibitor in any pediatric hematologic malignancy. The trial met its primary objective and demonstrated a 50% objective response rate with 70% of patients bridging to a successful HSCT or completing the maximum 12 cycles permitted on study. The three patients who completed 12 cycles on study continue to receive trametinib off study for as long as 2 years. Notably, there are only anecdotal reports of JMML patients relapsing post-HSCT who have experienced long-term survival in the absence of a repeat transplant.

Although limited by small numbers, our experience indicates that very young children can tolerate and obtain clinical benefit from long-term treatment with a MEK inhibitor. This observation may be relevant for implementing interventional strategies for non-cancer related complications of RASopathy developmental disorders, which are caused by germline mutations in Ras pathway genes(27). The patient with CBL syndrome-related JMML in this trial experienced a partial response without appreciable toxicity. Overall, our study also raises the possibility that carefully selected children with Noonan syndrome and other RASopathy disorders who either exhibit or are at risk of developing severe disease manifestations will benefit from treatment with an allosteric MEK inhibitor. Anecdotal reports of infants with life-threatening cardiomyopathy who improved dramatically after receiving trametinib support this idea(28).

We performed serial correlative analyses of PB and BM specimens from patients enrolled on this study. Paired RNAseq and kinome analysis of pre- and post-treatment samples confirmed on-target inhibition of MAPK signaling. Two of three patients who progressed on study harbored new alterations in JMML cells, which included a secondary NF1 mutation or monosomy 7. This observation suggests that trametinib treatment imposes selective pressure for JMML cells to acquire secondary mutations that enhance MAPK signaling. We also note that neither patient with more than one mutation at relapse who also had a high DNA methylation signature responded to trametinib. This is consistent with prior data demonstrating that increased mutational burden and high DNA methylation at original diagnosis are associated with a poor response to upfront therapy(25).

We unexpectedly observed a profound change in gene sets associated with inflammatory signaling pathways including JAK/STAT, interferon alpha, interferon gamma, tumor necrosis factor, and interleukins in response to trametinib therapy and found that this decrease is primarily driven by effects on monocytes. These transcriptional changes were associated with a dramatic down-regulation of SRC family kinase activity in post-treatment samples. While correlative in nature, there data raise the intriguing possibility that trametinib interrupts a paracrine loop whereby mutant monocytes drive growth of immature myeloid lineage cells. This idea is consistent with the landmark discovery that JMML progenitors are hypersensitive to granulocyte macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF)(29) and with genetic studies in mice showing that GM-CSF expression plays a central role in initiating and maintaining myeloid disease in a GEM model of JMML(30). Trametinib may therefore be modulating the crosstalk between oncogenic and immune signaling and we speculate that this might partially explain the durable clinical benefit experienced by some patients despite persistence of each Ras pathway mutation at a high VAF in the hematopoietic compartment(31). An alternative explanation for the clinical and hematologic responses to trametinib is that treatment directly rebalances the proliferation and differentiation of healthy hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells without eliminating mutant cells as previously observed in preclinical trials in murine JMML models(18,19).

Until recently, HSCT was the only viable treatment for JMML with most patients receiving myeloablative conditioning chemotherapy and transplant immediately after diagnosis. This paradigm is beginning to change with the identification of disease-modifying agents. A recent prospective trial of 18 newly diagnosed patients receiving the hypomethylating agent azacitidine before HSCT reported a leukemia-free survival rate of ~80% at a median follow-up of two years. These promising data are consistent with a previous retrospective study showing that administering moderate intensity chemotherapy before HSCT induced molecular remissions in approximately 40% of JMML patients and that these children had extremely favorable outcomes(3). However, the treatment of relapsed patients has remained essentially the same over the past several decades, with most patients proceeding to second or even third HSCT. Our study is the first completed prospective trial in this population. Although limited by the small number of patients, the long-term survival and correlative molecular analyses from this trial raise provocative questions about whether certain JMML patients might be spared the long-term adverse health risks of genotoxic HSCT conditioning regimens based on favorable molecular characteristics at diagnosis. This hypothesis will now be tested in a national, risk-stratified therapeutic trial (NCT05849662) of trametinib in combination with azacitidine in newly diagnosed patients.

This study was conducted by the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) under an Investigational New Drug (IND) application for trametinib (IND #119346) held by the National Cancer Institute. ADVL1521 was approved by the Cancer Therapy and Evaluation Program, the US Food and Drug Administration, the Pediatric Central Institutional Review Board, and institutional review boards at each participating center. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, written informed consent was obtained before study entry.

COG ADVL1521 enrolled 10 patients with JMML from June 2017 to December 2022 (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Table 7). Eligible patients were initially between 2–22 years of age. The study was amended in January 2019 to lower the minimum age to one month based on safety data from a study of trametinib in pediatric solid tumors (NCT02124772). Patients were required to have histologic verification of JMML at original diagnosis based on World Health Organization criteria(32). Patients who relapsed after HSCT or were refractory to one cycle of high dose cytarabine or two cycles of azacitidine were eligible for the study. All patients had persistent disease based on the presence of the initial Ras pathway mutation at high variant allele frequency (VAF) detected by an integral, next-generation sequencing (NGS) assay (Supplementary Table 8). Dosing of trametinib was age dependent (0.032 mg/kg per day for children one month to < six years of age and 0.025 mg/kg per day for children six years of age and older with a maximum of 2 mg) which was the recommended Phase 2 dose from NCT02124772(22). Each cycle consisted of daily trametinib for 28 days. Patients could remain on study for up to 12 cycles in the absence of progressive disease or untoward toxicity. Integral molecular testing was performed on peripheral blood at baseline and after the first cycle of therapy and every odd other cycle thereafter (3, 5, 7, etc.) for as long as a patient remained on study. Optional studies for pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic and correlative studies were collected for consenting patients (Supplementary Table 3). Clinical and laboratory assessments were obtained as outlined in the study protocol. Standard exclusion criteria applied with specific exclusion of patients who transformed to acute myeloid leukemia or were requiring treatment of left ventricular systolic dysfunction or retinal vein occlusion or central serous retinopathy based on known toxicities associated with trametinib. Study accrual duration was driven by two prior COG trials in newly diagnosed JMML patients, AAML0122 and ASCT1221.

Treatment-related adverse events were graded using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5. The primary outcome for the study was the objective response rate. Internationally agreed upon JMML-specific response criteria were used to assess response(33). This phase 2 study used a Simon two-stage design where the second stage would be entered after the first response and the drug would be deemed effective after three or more total responses. Overall survival was defined as the time from study enrollment to death or date of last contact. This report includes data current as of December 31, 2022.

We performed bulk RNASeq pre- and post-trametinib administration to identify differentially expressed genes and molecular pathways in response to treatment. Total RNA was first evaluated for its quantity, and quality, using Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. The RNA integrity number (RIN) was >9. One hundred nanograms of total RNA were used. Briefly, ribosomal RNA was removed from total RNA using QIAseq FastSelect rRNA HMR Kit (Qiagen, Catalog# 334387). After the depletion of rRNA, cDNA library preparation was carried out including RNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, ligation of index adaptors, and amplification, following the KAPA RNA Hyper Prep Kit Technical Data Sheet, KR0961 – v3.15 (Roche Corporate, Catalog #KK8541). Each resulting indexed library was quantified and its quality accessed by Agilent TapeStation, and multiple libraries were pooled in equal molarity. The library pool was then sequenced in 100b paired-end read format on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, Inc.). More than 30 million reads per sample were generated and 91% of the sequencing reads reached Q30 (99.9% base call accuracy).

Raw FASTQ data were directly imported from the sequencing facility and trimmed with Trimmomatic tool (v0.36) to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality regions from the reads. The resulting reads were then mapped to the human reference genome (hg38) using the STAR aligner (v2.5.1b). For quantifying gene-level expression, the STAR aligner’s “quantMode” feature was used with gene annotations obtained from GENCODE p5. Quality control metrics such as: uniquely aligned read counts, unique-to-multiple alignment ratios, and exon-to-intron ratios were evaluated using ngsutilsj (v0.3–2180ca6). Further QC assessments and subsequent analyses were performed entirely within R.

Normalization of aligned reads was achieved using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method from the EdgeR package (v3.24.3). Read counts were then log2-transformed (log2(cpm + 1)). To identify statistically significant differences in gene expression, the VOOM function from the limma package was used to estimate the mean-variance relationship and assign precision weights. These weights were then integrated into the empirical Bayes linear modeling framework provided by limma (v3.38.3) to calculate statistical outputs, including p-values, adjusted p-values, and log-fold changes (LogFC). Pathway analysis was conducted using both Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and gene set over representation, employing the R package fgsea (1.15.1) and goana from the limma package.

We conducted single cell RNASeq pre- and post-trametinib administration to determine cell type composition and differentially expressed genes and molecular pathways at single cell resolution. Cells were thawed slowly and washed with RPMI as previously described(34). Clean cells were counted with hemocytometer on a microscope for cell concentration and viability. The single cell gene expression analysis was conducted using a 10X Chromium single cell system (10X Genomics, Inc) and a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, Inc). Five to ten thousand targeted cell recovery per sample with viability of >75% was applied to a single cell master mix with lysis buffer and reverse transcription reagents, following the Chromium NextGEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits User Guide, CG000204 Rev D (10X Genomics, Inc). The quality of cDNA and library was examined by Bioanalyzer and Qubit. The resulting libraries were pooled in equal molarity and sequenced in a custom program for 28b plus 91b paired-end sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Depending on targeted cell recovery, approximately 50K reads per cell were generated and more than 90% of the sequencing reads reached Q30 (99.9% base call accuracy). A Phred quality score (Q score) was used to measure the quality of sequencing.

Fastq data files were aligned and filtered count matrices were generated with the 10x Cell Ranger software (version 7.0.1) using the GRCh38 human reference genome (version 3.0.0). Quality control (QC) and subsequent analyses were conducted in R using the Seurat package (version 5.0.0). Initial QC filtering was applied to each sample independently to remove cells exhibiting either > 15% mitochondrial gene expression or an aberrant total gene count (< 200 or > 4000 genes), criteria indicative of compromised cell integrity or doublet events respectively. Following QC, all samples were merged and normalized using the SCTransform function in Seurat, regressing for intrinsic gene variability such as mitochondrial gene content. The final integration was batch corrected using the Harmony integration tool (version 1.0). For the dimensional reduction and visualization of cellular heterogeneity, principal component analysis (PCA) was first applied, retaining the top 30 principal components. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was then applied to project the data into a two-dimensional space, based on the Harmony-adjusted principal components. Following this, the FindNeighbors function was utilized to construct a shared nearest neighbor graph from the top 30 dimensions, and delineation of the cell clusters was enabled with the FindClusters function with a resolution parameter set to 0.9. Cell types were inferred by leveraging known gene expression profiles through the application of the ‘sc-type’ algorithm in R, enabling the classification of cells based on their transcriptional signatures.

For pseudotime analysis, the resulting Seurat object was directly imported into Monocle3, and the default settings were used for trajectory analysis. Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) were designated as the root of the trajectory, with the root principal node being automatically identified using the function provided by the Monocle authors (get_earliest_principal_node). This established the initial state for pseudotime ordering. Subsequently, cell ordering was visualized using a density plot, facilitating the comparison of hematopoietic progression between pre- and post-trametinib treatment samples.

We analyzed kinome activity pre- and post-trametinib to investigate potential mechanisms of response and resistance For MIB affinity chromatography, Ficolled cell pellets were extracted in lysis buffer and flowed over kinase inhibitor coupled beads and bound fractions collected for MS analysis essentially as previously described(35). Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 480 raw files were searched using MaxQuant MaxLFQ (v. 1.6.11.0) and annotated kinase log2 LFQ intensities generated in Perseus (v. 1.6.10.50) were used. Raw MS files and complete methods available via the PRIDE repository (accession PXD042328). Integration of kinome and RNAseq data was performed in R version 4.2.2. Kinases with log2 fold changes ≥ +1 or ≤ −1 in MIB binding or RNAseq pre/post are labeled in red and blue respectively on corresponding scatter plots and were used as input to Enrichr to identify differentially regulated pathways against the Biocarta 2016 reference database(36). Coral was initially described in a preprint by Metz & Deoudes et al available at bioRxiv. Coral makes use of phylogenetic information derived from a prior publication(37). Tree plots made by Coral were modified from those created by Cell Signaling Technology.

We analyzed DNA methylation pre- and post-trametinib to evaluate methylation as a potential biomarker of response. Genomic DNA (300 ng) was bisulfite-converted using the TrueMethyl oxBS module according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Tecan). Converted ssDNA was quantified using the Qubit ssDNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 100 ng was used as input for the custom-made Targeted MethylSeq Assay (Tecan). Final libraries were quality checked using the Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Analysis Kit (Agilent) and quantified using the Qubit HS Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before pooling for next-generation sequencing. Single-index library pools were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 with paired-end 150 bp mode. More than 90% of the sequencing reads reached Q30 (99.9% base call accuracy). A low, intermediate, or high methylation designation was assigned based on international consensus definitions(25).
