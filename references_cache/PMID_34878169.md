# Further evidence of muscle involvement in neurodevelopmental disorder with epilepsy, spasticity, and brain atrophy.
**Authors:** Majethia P, Do Rosario MC, Kaur P, Karanvir, Shankar R, Sharma S, Siddiqui S, Shukla A
**Journal:** Ann Hum Genet (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1111/ahg.12452](https://doi.org/10.1111/ahg.12452)

## Content

TRAPPC4-related neurodevelopmental disorder with epilepsy, spasticity, and brain atrophy (MIM# 618741) is a recently described TRAPPopathy with clinical findings of developmental delay, seizures, postnatal microcephaly, spasticity, facial dysmorphism, and cerebral and cerebellar atrophy. Muscle involvement, a frequent finding in TRAPPopathies, was observed in one individual with TRAPPC4-related disorder previously. Only a single variant, an in-frame deletion in one family has been reported outside a recurrent disease-causing variant. We report three individuals from two Indian families harboring novel bi-allelic missense variants c.191T>C and c.278C>T (NM_016146.6) in TRAPPC4 with classic clinical presentation in one and milder and later onset in the other family. We provide further evidence for muscle involvement and review the detailed phenotypic findings in individuals reported with this disorder till date.

TRAPP complex plays a vital role in the secretory pathway and autophagy (Behrends et al., 2010; Loh et al., 2005; Scrivens et al., 2011). Defects in these TRAPP complex subunits cause a spectrum of neurodegenerative disorders collectively termed as ‘TRAPPopathies’ (Sacher et al., 2019). The common clinical findings include intellectual disability, regression or developmental delay, microcephaly, seizures, muscle involvement and brain atrophy. Neurodevelopmental disorder with epilepsy, spasticity, and brain atrophy (NEDESBA; MIM# 618741) is a recently described TRAPPopathy associated with pathogenic variants in TRAPPC4. Surprisingly, of the 33 individuals from 22 families reported to date, 31 individuals from 21 families have been reported with a recurrent homozygous pathogenic variant, NC_000011.9: c.454+3A>G (Ghosh et al., 2021; Kaur et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2020). Only two individuals from one family with milder manifestation of this disorder are reported with an in-frame variant (NM_016146.6: c.638_*4del) in TRAPPC4 (Saad et al., 2020).

We hereby report three additional individuals from two unrelated Indian families harboring novel missense variants in TRAPPC4. Our report expands the phenotypic and genotypic spectrum of this rare disorder.

Individuals from both the families in the study were clinically evaluated at the Department of Pediatrics, Lady Hardinge Medical College and Associated Kalawati Saran Children’s Hospital, New Delhi. They were recruited after obtaining informed consents for genetic testing, publication of data and clinical photographs. The study is approved by the institutional ethics committee, Kasturba Medical College and Kasturba Hospital.

Singleton exome sequencing (Illumina, Inc. San Diego, CA) was performed for proband 1 and proband 3 as described previously (Girisha et al., 2019). Variant filtering and prioritization strategy is outlined in supplementary table S1–S4. Validation and segregation analysis of the identified variant was performed by Sanger sequencing.

In silico protein modeling was carried out for both the missense variants: p.(Leu64Pro) and p.(Pro93Leu). The TRAPPC4 protein structure, obtained from Protein data bank (RCSB PDB), Protein PDB (PDB ID: 2J3T; accessed on 15th May 2021) (https://www.rcsb.org/) was loaded on PyMol (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 1.2r3pre, Schrödinger, LLC) and in silico mutagenesis was performed to deduce the structure of the mutant protein. This was followed by analyzing the alteration in the polar contacts of the wild-type residue of interest with its neighboring amino acids as compared to the mutant residue. The structural effect of the missense substitutions p.(Leu64Pro) and p.(Pro93Leu) was predicted using HOPE and I-Mutant.

Proband 1 (P1), second born male to a third-degree consanguineous couple, from family 1 was ascertained at the age of eight months (Fig. S1A). He was noted to have delayed crying and a history of regurgitation of feeds from the newborn period. At eight-months, he had attained partial neck holding, social smile, cooing and was able to recognize his mother. On examination, his weight was 3.27kg (−6.27SD), his length was 62cm (−3.7SD), and occipitofrontal circumference was 32.5cm (−7.6SD). There was no obvious facial dysmorphism. He had marked spasticity in all four limbs. Hearing evaluation was suggestive of non-recordable hearing response bilaterally. MRI brain performed at four-months of age showed delayed myelination, dilated ventricles, hyperintensities in the periventricular white matter on T2-weighted (T2W) axial sections (Fig. 1A-i–ii). T2W sagittal sections showed thin corpus callosum, dysmorphic brainstem, and cerebral atrophy (Fig. 1A-iii). T2W coronal sections showed white matter volume loss with prominence of the ventricular system (Fig. 1A-iv). Serum creatine phosphokinase levels [CPK (>2000U/L; ref: 46–171U/L)] and serum lactate level (14.4mmol/L; ref: 0.5–2.2mmol/L) were also found to be elevated at eight months. At one-year, the proband experienced a single episode of generalized tonic-clonic seizure with uprolling of eyeballs. On re-evaluation at one year-two months, he had failure to thrive, dystonia, spasticity and brisk deep tendon reflexes. Ophthalmological evaluation was unremarkable.

Proband 2 (fourth-born child, fifteen-years) and proband 3 (fifth-born child, ten-years) were born to third-degree consanguineous couple from family 2 (Fig. S1C). Affected siblings attained age-appropriate motor milestones, however speech delay was noted in early childhood (three years). They could speak only small sentences at the age of five years. They presented with generalized tonic-clonic seizures at seven years of age. Gradually progressive motor regression including abnormal movements and unsteadiness of gait were noted after the seizure episode in P2 and behavior abnormalities like aggression and hyperactivity were noted in P3. Anthropometric measurements for P2 (fifteen years) were: weight 37kg (−2.9SD), height was 154cm (−2.3SD), and occipitofrontal circumference was 50cm (−3.5SD) (CDC growth charts: United States; (Rollins et al., 2010). Anthropometric measurements in P3 (ten years) were: weight was 25kg (−2.07SD), height was 131.5cm (−1.36SD) and occipitofrontal circumference was 48cm (−4SD) (CDC growth charts: United States; Rollins et al., 2010). No obvious facial dysmorphism were noted in them (Fig. S1D). MRI at nine years of age showed mildly prominent ventricles, mild cerebral atrophy, white matter volume loss and nonspecific subtle T2 FLAIR hyperintensities in the fronto-parietal periventricular white matter (Fig. 1B). Serum CPK levels were found to be elevated (420.6U/L; ref: 46–171U/L) at ten years. An elder sibling (IV.2), eighteen years old, had severe developmental delay and intellectual disability secondary to birth related injury.

A novel homozygous missense variant NM_016146.6:c.191T>C p.(Leu64Pro) in TRAPPC4 was identified in P1 in concordance with the observed phenotype of the previously reported TRAPPC4 variants. Validation and segregation analysis of this variant was done by Sanger sequencing. P1 carried the variant in a homozygous state, while both the parents were heterozygotes (Fig. S1E).

A novel homozygous missense variant c.278C>T p.(Pro93Leu) in TRAPPC4 was identified in P3. Validation and segregation analysis of this variant was done by Sanger sequencing. P2 and P3 carried the variant in a homozygous state, both parents were heterozygotes (Fig. S1F) and the elder sibling (IV.2) had wild type allele. Detailed information on allele frequency and results of in silico predictions tools for both families have been described in supplementary material and table S5.

In silico mutagenesis of the p.(Leu64Pro) variant followed by comparative analysis of the wild type and mutant protein predicted the mutant protein to cause loss of interaction with the neighboring residue, Arg97 (Fig. 2A). HOPE predicted the effects caused by differences between the wild type and mutant amino acids with respect to domain and size. The wild-type residue (Leu64) belongs to the longin-like domain superfamily and is predicted to be buried in the core of this domain. The mutant residue (Pro64), which is smaller in size as compared to wild-type residue, is predicted to disturb the core structure of this domain thereby causing an empty space in the core of the protein. Further, I-Mutant also predicts the decreased stability of the mutant protein (DDG −2.92) in comparison to the wildtype protein.

In silico mutagenesis of the p.(Pro93Leu) variant followed by in silico analysis of the wild-type residue (Pro93) revealed the absence of any polar contacts with its neighboring amino acids, which remained unaltered in the mutant (Leu93) protein (Fig. 2B). However, it is still possible that contacts made by the protein with other molecules or domains might be affected by this variant. Similar predictions were made by HOPE, which predicted that the wild-type residue (Pro93), also belonging to the longin-like domain superfamily, lies on the surface of the protein. Owing to this, the mutant residue (Leu93) may disturb interactions with other molecules or other parts of the protein. Moreover, the wild-type residue proline is a secondary amino acid and when incorporated in the protein, acquires a static ring conformation. This renders the nearby backbone inflexible and further prevents the formation of certain secondary structures. Also, mutation at this residue (Leu93) is predicted to disturb this spatial conformation required at this position. Further, I-Mutant also predicts decreased stability of the mutant protein (DDG −0.80) in comparison to the wild-type protein. Based on the crystal structure of TRAPPC4 (also known as Synbindin) protein identified by Fan et al (2008), these variants are found to be located in the atypical PDZ-like domain (APD). However, HOPE predicts the variants to lie in the longin-like domain.

P1 presented in the early infantile period with developmental delay, feeding difficulties and seizures as observed in most of the individuals with recurrent splicing TRAPPC4 variant (Kaur et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2020; Ghosh et al., 2021). On the other hand, P2 and P3 presented with cognitive delay in early childhood. All individuals with NEDESBA, except for P2 and P3, either showed complete absence of motor development or had significant motor delay. Cognitive delay too has been consistently noted in all the reported individuals, including the individuals in our study, except for two individuals of a family reported by Saad et al. (2020). Regression of achieved milestones is seen in addition to delayed milestones in most individuals with this condition. However, regression was very late onset and atypical in P2 and P3 of family 2. Microcephaly is another consistent finding, ranging from −3SD to −8SD in individuals with recurrent splicing variant and the present study. Normal head circumference was noted in only individuals reported by Saad et al. (2020). A subtle yet clinically recognizable facial dysmorphic gestalt was observed in few individuals with c.454+3A>G (Ghosh et al., 2021; Kaur et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2020). Early-onset seizures (two months to six months) were noted in 23 out of 31 individuals with recurrent splicing variant along with P1 (one year). Seizure onset was late-onset (7 years) in P2 and P3. Seizure semiology include generalized tonic-clonic seizures, staring episodes, febrile seizures and complex partial motor seizures. Of note, seizures were not noted in individuals reported by Saad et al. (2020). Involuntary movements in the form of ataxia, tremors and dystonia which were previously reported in 12 out of 33 individuals, were also noted in P1 and P2. Hearing and ophthalmological abnormalities (cortical visual defects, no tracking, roving eye movements, nystagmus and optic atrophy) as seen in P1 are also reported in 30 out of 31 previously affected individuals (Ghosh et al., 2021; Kaur et al., 2020; Saad et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2020).

Neuroimaging findings, though not characteristic, have been observed in most individuals with NEDESBA. Marked cerebral atrophy is seen in those with recurrent splicing variant and was observed in P1 in the current study. Mild cerebral atrophy is seen in those with milder phenotype i.e., individuals reported by Saad et al. (2020) and in P3. Cerebellar atrophy, another significant finding reported in 21 out of 27 individuals with TRAPPC4 variant, was not observed in individuals from our study. Non-specific white matter abnormalities (delayed myelination, white matter volume loss, hyperintensities in periventricular region), thin corpus callosum and ventriculomegaly are also frequent imaging findings. Detailed clinical and radiological features of present and reported individuals are described in table 1.

TRAPP complex plays a vital role in the secretory pathway and autophagy (Behrends et al., 2010; Loh et al., 2005; Scrivens et al., 2011). Till date, defects in TRAPPC1, C3, C5, C10 and C13 have not been reported with any human disease. Of note, muscle involvement has been previously noted in individuals affected with TRAPPopathies, either as episodic rhabdomyolysis (Encephalopathy, progressive, early-onset, with episodic rhabdomyolysis, MIM# 618331 TRAPPC2L) or as muscular dystrophy (Muscular dystrophy, limb-girdle, autosomal recessive 18, MIM#615356, TRAPPC11) (Bögershausen et al., 2013; Milev et al., 2018). Kaur et al. (2020) previously reported muscle involvement in an individual with NEDESBA manifesting as probable episodic rhabdomyolysis with widely varying but persistently increased CPK (Kaur et al., 2020). Elevated CPK were also observed in P1 and P3, thus reiterating the significance of assessing CPK levels for this condition. Additionally, P1 also had elevated serum lactate levels as observed in two individuals reported by Kaur et al. (2020) and Ghosh et al. (2020).

Till date, only two disease causing variants have been reported from 22 families (Fig S2). The recurrent splicing variant c.454+3A>G has been shown to cause incompletely penetrant altered splicing further causing defects in the secretory pathway and autophagy through loss-of-function mechanism (Ghosh et al., 2021; Kaur et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2020). The other reported bi-allelic in-frame deletion leading to stop-loss in TRAPPC4 protein was predicted to cause extension of C-terminal of TRAPPC4 protein escaping the non-stop mediated decay, likely resulting in altered protein folding and integrity of TRAPP complex (Saad et al., 2020). The amino acid change p.(Leu64Pro) in family 1, located in the core of the TRAPPC4 protein, is predicted to cause severe decrease in stability as compared to the latter amino acid change p.(Pro93Leu) in family 2, which is present on the surface of the protein and is predicted to disturb the spatial conformation required at this position. The APD, where the variants identified in the current study are located, is required for the interaction of synbindin with “EFYA’ tail of Syndecan-2, a cell-surface heparan sulfate proteoglycan. This synbindin/syndecan complex plays a role in synaptic membrane trafficking, thereby regulating dendritic spine morphogenesis (Ethell et al., 2000). These amino acid changes may further impact the integrity of the TRAPPC4 protein or TRAPP complex, causing membrane trafficking dysfunction or defects in autophagy.

The report presents three individuals from two unrelated families affected with TRAPPC4-related neurological disorder with variable severity and clinical presentations, further expanding the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum. Early onset developmental delay, seizures, spasticity, microcephaly, muscle involvement, lactic acidosis and cerebral atrophy on neuroimaging appears to represent the severe end of spectrum of this disorder. We also highlight the role of elevated CPK in individuals with milder and atypical manifestations of this rare entity. Further reports would help delineate a better understanding of genotype-phenotype correlation of TRAPPC4-related NEDESBA.
