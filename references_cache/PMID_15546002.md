# Severe combined immunodeficiency caused by deficiency in either the delta or the epsilon subunit of CD3.
**Authors:** de Saint Basile G, Geissmann F, Flori E, Uring-Lambert B, Soudais C, Cavazzana-Calvo M, Durandy A, Jabado N, Fischer A, Le Deist F
**Journal:** J Clin Invest (2004)
**DOI:** [10.1172/JCI22588](https://doi.org/10.1172/JCI22588)

## Content

We investigated the molecular mechanism underlying a severe combined immunodeficiency characterized by the selective and complete absence of T cells. The condition was found in 5 patients and 2 fetuses from 3 consanguineous families. Linkage analysis performed on the 3 families revealed that the patients were carrying homozygous haplotypes within the 11q23 region, in which the genes encoding the gamma, delta, and epsilon subunits of CD3 are located. Patients and affected fetuses from 2 families were homozygous for a mutation in the CD3D gene, and patients from the third family were homozygous for a mutation in the CD3E gene. The thymus from a CD3delta-deficient fetus was analyzed and revealed that T cell differentiation was blocked at entry into the double positive (CD4+CD8+) stage with the accumulation of intermediate CD4-single positive cells. This indicates that CD3delta plays an essential role in promoting progression of early thymocytes toward double-positive stage. Altogether, these findings extend the known molecular mechanisms underlying severe combined immunodeficiency to a new deficiency, i.e., CD3epsilon deficiency, and emphasize the essential roles played by the CD3epsilon and CD3delta subunits in human thymocyte development, since these subunits associate with both the pre-TCR and the TCR.

Introduction
Severe combined immunodeficiencies (SCIDs) are a group of genetic 
disorders characterized by profoundly defective T cell differentiation 
(1). Several SCID conditions have been defined on the basis of enzy
-
matic, genetic, and immunological criteria (2). Adenosine deaminase 
deficiency results in a profound impairment of T, B, and NK lympho-
cyte development (3). One subset of SCIDs is characterized by the 
arrest of both T and B lymphocyte differentiation (T
–B– SCID), where-
as in a different subset B cell differentiation is unaffected but NK cell 
differentiation is impaired (T
–B+NK– SCID). The molecular bases of 
many of these conditions are now known and involve defects in either 
T and B cell receptor V(D)J recombination factors, i.e., Rag-1, Rag-2, 
or Artemis, in T
–B– SCID (4, 5), or IL-dependent T cell and NK cell 
differentiation due to mutation in the IL2RG gene (γc deficiency) or 
the γc-associated JAK3 kinase gene (Jak-3 deficiency) in T–B+NK– SCID  
(6, 7). In addition, another SCID subset is characterized by an absence 
of mature T lymphocytes in the presence of normally proceeding mat-
uration of NK and B cells (T–B+NK+ SCID). Deficiency of the α chain 
of the IL-7 receptor (IL-7Rα) (8) is the predominant form of T–B+NK+ 
SCID, but 2 cases associated with CD45 deficiency have been reported 
(9, 10). More recently, a mutation in the CD3D gene was identified in 3 
members of a kindred with the T
–B+NK+ form of SCID (11).
Intrathymic T lymphocyte differentiation requires appropriate 
signals from the pre-TCR (PreTα/TCRβ) and/or the TCR (TCRα/β 
or TCRγ/δ) (12–14). The pre-TCR, TCRα/β, and TCRγ/δ are associ-
ated with 3 signal-transducing complexes, namely the CD3δε and 
CD3γε heterodimers and a disulfide-linked CD3ζζ (15, 16). Howev-
er, in humans, the respective roles of the different CD3 chains in T 
cell differentiation are not well known. In CD3
γ–/– mice, thymocyte 
differentiation is blocked at the double negative stage (17), which 
suggests that the γε dimer is essential at an earlier intrathymic mat
-
uration step than the δε dimer. In these mice, T cell differentiation 
is probably blocked because the pre-TCR is unable to transduce the 
signal for progression. In contrast, thymocytes from CD3δ–/– mice 
do progress to the double positive stage (18).
Studies of human T cell immunodeficiencies associated with a 
defect in any one of these three subunits may provide insights into 
both the redundant and the nonredundant roles of the CD3 mol-
ecules. Until recently, only 2 immunodeficiencies associated with 
a CD3 chain defect had been described. CD3
γ deficiency did not 
impair intrathymic T cell differentiation. In these cases, peripheral 
CD4 T cells and the few CD8 T cells present expressed a CD3
γ-defi-
cient CD3 complex (19, 20). Conversely, the study of a T– SCID con-
dition associated with a CD3δ deficiency suggested that the CD3δ 
subunit is essential for T cell development in humans (11). In this 
study we describe a T
–B+NK+ SCID phenotype associated with either 
a CD3δ deficiency found in 2 families — with a hitherto undescribed 
mutation in 1 of the families — or a CD3ε deficiency in 1 family.

Discussion
We showed that the homozygous mutations found in the CD3D
 
and CD3E genes of the families studied (mutations in CD3D for 
families II and III and CD3E for family I) were associated with the  
T–B+NK+ SCID phenotype. The consanguinity, sex ratio, and 
absence of the phenotype in heterozygous carriers demonstrate 
autosomal recessive inheritance of the defects. Other potential 
genetic causes of SCIDs were excluded on the basis of the phe
-
notype and after analysis of the segregation of the polymorphic 
markers surrounding the SCID-associated loci. Deficiencies in γc 
and Jak-3 are usually associated with a T –B+NK– phenotype, and 
thus the possibility that the mutations lay within these genes was 
excluded. Similarly, an adenosine deaminase deficiency was exclud-
ed, as this generally results in T–B–NK– SCID. An IL-7Rα deficiency, 
which results in a T–B+NK+ phenotype (8) identical to that observed 
in the 3 families, could be ruled out by genetic analysis.

ciencies in peripheral T cells, including γ/δ T cells. Both the

not lead to impaired T cell development (22). This discrepancy 
between the phenotypes is probably related to differences in the 
extent of the CD3

CD3ε expression was found at the T cell surface as a consequence 
of a splice-site mutation on 1 allele that did not totally abrogate 
the normal intron 7 splicing (22). In contrast, the homozygous 
CD3E mutation in family I in our study created a premature 
stop codon near the start of the extracellular domain and thus 
resulted in the absence of CD3 ε. These observations show that 
an absence of CD3ε or CD3δ completely abrogates human T cell 
development. The assumption of the role of CD3 ε also requires 
confirmation by description of additional cases.
It was not possible to assess at what stage the CD3 ε deficiency 
blocked T cell differentiation in patients from family I, as no thymus 
material was available. In the mouse, Cd3
ε gene inactivation results 
in a complete block at the CD44–CD25+ double negative T cell dif-
ferentiation stage, showing that CD3ε is essential for transduction 
of the PreTα/TCRβ receptor survival, proliferation, and progression 
signals (23, 24). It is possible that the T cell differentiation is dis -
rupted at the same stage in the CD3ε-deficient human thymus.
In contrast, the thymi of patients or fetuses with the CD3δ deficien-
cy were analyzed both in this study and in that of Dadi et al. (11). Dadi 
et al. reported that T lymphocyte development was blocked at the 
pre-TCR expression stage (11), since the levels of CD4 and CD8
α/β  
mRNAs and proteins in thymocytes from the patients were lower 
than those in the thymocytes of the controls (11). By performing an 
immunohistochemical analysis, we found that T cell differentiation 
was blocked slightly later in our patients, at the ISP stage. Most cells 
did not express cKit or CD34, and the majority of T cells were CD4
+ 
and CD45RO–CD8–, both of which are characteristic of ISP cells (25). 
In mice and humans, ISP cells actively proliferate, a process that is 
driven by signals emanating from the pre-TCR and that results in the 
generation of double positive thymocytes. The finding that cell prolif
-
eration, as revealed by anti-Ki67 labeling, was strongly impaired in the 
patients in our study suggests that the defect in the pre-TCR–medi-
ated proliferation is at the ISP stage.
The differences in thymic phenotypes between the 2 studies are 
not highly significant, since the way the thymus samples were ana-
lyzed differed and the thymi used by Dadi et al. (11) originated from 
infants rather than a fetus. It is possible that in the absence of CD3
δ, 
some PreT α+/TCRβ+ thymocytes survive and progress up to the 
stage of ISP cells. Even if this is the case, the results from the 2 stud-
ies show that CD3δ is required during the early stage of human thy-
mopoiesis, most likely as a mediator of the signals transmitted from 
the PreT
α/TCRβ receptor. In addition, TCR γ/δ+ T cells were not 
detected in patients with CD3δ deficiency (11), which demonstrates 
that CD3δ is also required for the development of these T cells. This 
phenotype differs from that observed in the CD3δ–/– murine model 
(18). The size of the peripheral T cell pool in these mice is 10% of 
that in the controls, and the T cells express a reduced number of 
membrane TCR/γεζ CD3 complexes; these findings indicate that 
the CD3
δ deficiency blocks T cell development at the CD4 +CD8+ 
stage in mice. This interspecies difference could be the consequence 
of CD3δ playing a different role in CD3–pre-TCR or CD3-TCR com-
plex assembly or signaling in humans versus mice. CD3δ is involved 
in the ERK activation associated with positive selection in mice (26). 
Therefore, it is presumed that CD3
δ plays an additive role at an ear-
lier stage of T cell differentiation in mice than in humans.

We have identified a novel genetic cause of SCID, and, as a result, 
10 mechanisms underlying this syndrome have now been character-
ized: ADA (3), γc (6), Jak-3 (7), IL-7Rα (8), Rag-1, Rag-2 (4), Artemis

(patient I-1) died at 5 months of age of pneumonitis in 1983. No investiga-
