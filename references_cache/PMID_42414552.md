Both sustained attention (SA) and executive functioning (EF) are critical for 
adaptive behavior and compromised across a range of psychiatric disorders, yet 
their etiological relationships remain underexplored. Here, we report the first 
multivariate characterization of molecular genetic influences on SA (vigilance, 
lapse recovery cost, lapse propensity) and EF (processing speed, response 
selection, working memory). In collaboration with 23andMe, we report previously 
unpublished genome-wide association studies of these phenotypes in a single 
cohort of more than 20,000 individuals enriched for diagnoses of major 
depressive disorder and bipolar disorder. We used Genomic Structural Equation 
Modeling to formally model patterns of genetic covariance among these task-based 
measures of cognition, and their relationships with other cognitive, clinical, 
and imaging-derived phenotypes. We identified two distinct latent genetic 
factors: one influencing EF and one influencing SA. Both the EF and SA factors 
were genetically correlated with cognitive and clinical phenotypes, with each 
latent factor uniquely linked to liability for psychiatric disorders, including 
attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. However, genetic correlations with 
imaging-derived neuroanatomical phenotypes were modest and non-significant after 
correction for multiple comparisons. Collectively, these results provide an 
initial multivariate genetic characterization of SA and EF, though replication 
in larger and more diverse samples will be important. They suggest that genetic 
influences on sustained attention are generally distinct from those that 
influence executive function. The EF and SA factors show distinct patterns of 
genetic overlap with multiple cognitive and psychiatric outcomes, underscoring 
the need for more granular cognitive phenotyping to generate new insights into 
the genetic architecture of cognition and the etiology of psychopathology.

INTRODUCTION
Sustained attention, deﬁned as the ability to maintain a stable on-task
attentional state over extended periods and recover that state
following transient disengagement, is a fundamental aspect of
higher-order cognition [ 1, 2]. Sustained attention is prominently
implicated in numerous forms of psychiatric and neurological
dysfunction. Consequently, sustained attention has long been of
interest to scientists and clinicians, as dif ﬁc u l t i e si nt h i sd o m a i na r e
prevalent across diverse patient populations, including those with
psychiatric, neurodevelopmental, and neurodegenerative disorders,
as well as acquired brain injuries [1, 3– 5]. Underscoring its relevance
to psychopathology, the National Institute of Mental Health has
recognized multiple indices of susta ined attention (e.g. attentional
lapses, response time variability) as critical elements of the Cognitive
Systems domain within the Research Domain Criteria framework [6].
An important conceptual question is how sustained attention
relates to executive function. While sustained attention is closely
related to executive cognition, dominant cognitive neuroscience
models typically distinguish attentional stability (or vigilance) from
executive control operations such as working memory updating,
inhibitory control and set shifting [ 7– 10]. This is consistent with
work suggesting that sustained attention modulates the ability to
implement executive control, while remaining partly separable
from the executive operations typically emphasized in classic
executive function taxonomies [ 11– 13]. These ﬁndings raise the
possibility that sustained attention and executive function may
reﬂect partially distinct etiological in ﬂuences despite their
phenotypic interdependence.

genetically informative work has largely been limited to twin
heritability studies [ 14– 16] and relatively small genome-wide
association studies (GWAS) [ 17– 20]. This slow progress is even
more apparent when compared to GWAS of other complex traits
related to cognition, such as general intelligence [ 21] and
educational attainment [ 22], which have received considerably
more focus and resources. Prior multivariate genomic studies of
executive function have advanced our understanding of shared
etiology among cognitive processes [ 23]. Given the substantial
progress in understanding other aspects of cognition, joint
characterization of the etiological in ﬂuences that drive individual
differences in executive function and sustained attention could
provide critical insights into the heritable components of these
important phenotypes, and inform our understanding of the
latent genetic structure of cognition.
In the present study, we address this gap in the literature by
describing the largest GWAS of the gradual-onset continuous
performance task (gradCPT), which measures the ability to
maintain consistent responding to frequently presented stimuli
for extended epochs and inhibit responding to infrequent,
unpredictably presented stimuli. Speci ﬁcally, we report GWAS
results for multiple indices of sustained attention (vigilance, lapse
propensity, and lapse recovery cost) measured by this task, as well
as GWAS results for several measures of executive function in the
AFFECT study [ 24], a large cohort enriched for individuals with
diagnoses of major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder
(maximum N = 23,318 individuals with European-like genomes).
We then use Genomic Structural Equation Modeling (Genomic-
SEM) [25] to formally characterize patterns of genetic sharing and
differentiation among these cognitive phenotypes in an explora-
tory, data-driven approach. Finally, to determine the validity of our
observed factors and better understand the common and unique
impacts of sustained attention and executive function on
development and psychopathology, we evaluate genetic relation-
ships between the identi ﬁed latent factors and a variety of
cognitive, neuropsychiatric, and imaging-derived phenotypes.
Collectively, our results provide new insights into the genetic
architectures of higher-order cognitive processes and clarify their
broader etiological structure.
METHODS
Sample recruitment and phenotyping procedures
Participants were recruited as part of the Affective disorders, Environment, and
Cognitive Trait (AFFECT) study in collaboration with 23andMe, Inc [24]. Study
design, participant recruitment, and data collection are described in a prior
publication [24]. Brieﬂy, participants were invited to participate in an online
study involving genetic data collection and a series of surveys including task-
based cognitive assessments over the course of 9 months. Recruitment was
conducted using a combination of the 23andMe research participant pool and
social media advertisements. The overall sample was designed to be enriched
for participants with a self-reported diagnosis of bipolar disorder or major
depressive disorder with pharmacological treatment, while control partici-
pants were restricted to those reporti ng no history of major psychiatric
diagnoses or prior psychiatric prescription. A total of 23,835 non-psychiatric
controls, 14,768 major depressive disorder cases, and 9864 bipolar disorder
cases completed consent procedures, provided a DNA sample, and answered
the baseline set of questionnaires. Participants provided informed consent and
volunteered to participate in the research online, under a protocol approved
by the external AAHRPP-accredited IRB, Ethical & Independent (E&I) Review
Services. As of 2022, E&I Review Services is part of Salus IRB ( https://
www.versiticlinicaltrials.org/salusirb). All methods were performed in accor-
dance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.
An extensive cognitive battery was administered to each participant
across nine online data collection sessions over the course of a nine-
month study period [ 24]. In the current study, we focus a subset of tasks
from the full battery that yield measures of distinct executive processes:
the Digit-Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) [ 26], the gradCPT [ 27, 28], and
the THINC-Integrated Tool (THINC-it) [ 29]. The DSST was administered at
the ﬁrst session, gradCPT was assessed at sessions 3 and 9, and the THINC-
it was administered at sessions 2 and 7. The DSST takes approximately
2 min to administer, the gradCPT is a 12-min paradigm, and the THINC-it
takes 10 – 15 min to complete in total. From these tasks, a set of seven
variables indexing conceptually distinct executive processes were
selected for GWAS analysis. Processing speed was measured using the
number of correct trials from the DSST. Measures of vigilance (sustained
attention variability), lapse recovery cost, and lapse propensity, were
derived from the gradCPT, using the coef ﬁcient of variation of response
times for correct responses to target trials, post-error slowing (the
difference in response time for target trials following correct vs. incorrect
responses to targets), and false-alarm rate for no-go trials, respectively.
Measures of response selection, working memory, and cognitive ﬂexibility
derived from three of the THINC-it tasks: log-transformed mean reaction
time for correct symbols in the “Spotter” (choice reaction time) task,
number of correct responses in the “Symbol Check ” (1-back test) task, and
the time to complete the “Trails” (trails making test B) task. If multiple
observations were available for a participant, the median value was used.
All cognitive phenotypes were oriented such that positive values indicate
better performance.
Preprocessing, quality control, and GWAS procedures
Complete SNP genotyping and imputation procedures are described in a
prior publication [ 24]. Brie ﬂy, saliva samples underwent DNA extraction
and genotyping at the National Genetics Institute, a CLIA-licensed
clinical laboratory under the Laboratory Corporation of America.
Genotyping, phasing, and imputation followed the standardized
23andMe pipeline [ 24]. Following preprocessing and quality control,
approximately 9.2 million high-quality genotyped and imputed SNPs
were available for analysis.
All GWAS analyses were restricted to individuals with European-like
genomes, as identi ﬁed using a local ancestry-based algorithm [ 30] in a set
of maximally unrelated individuals [ 31]. GWAS were performed using linear
regression on imputed and genotyped SNPs. Each cognitive phenotype
was regressed on genotype dosage with sex, age, ﬁve genetic principal
components, and genotyping platform as covariates. For each GWAS, SNPs
with fewer than 20% of the available sample size for a given trait were
removed prior to subsequent analyses. As noted in Table 1, ﬁnal analytic
sample sizes for each GWAS ranged from 10,129 participants with data on
lapse recovery cost to 23,318 participants with data on processing speed.
GWAS were performed by members of the 23andMe research team, with
summary statistics provided to the MGH study team for subsequent
modeling.
GWAS summary statistics were prepared for downstream analysis using
GenomicSEM R package version 0.0.5 [ 25]. In line with best practices,
variants were only retained if they had imputation quality scores ≥0.9
(when available), minor allele frequency ≥0.01, and were present in the
HapMap3 reference panel. For external psychiatric phenotypes, the sum of
effective sample sizes were calculated for use in GenomicSEM [ 32].
Heritability, genetic correlation, and factor analyses
SNP-based heritability ( h2
SNP ) was estimated for each cognitive trait by
applying LD score regression (LDSC) [ 33], as implemented in the
GenomicSEM R package [ 25]. Similarly, GenomicSEM was used to estimate
the genome-wide covariance and sampling variance matrices via multi-
variable LDSC.
Having no a priori hypothesis as to the multivariate genetic factor
structure of our cognitive measures, we applied two non-graphical scree
tests to determine the optimal number of latent factors required to
describe the observed genetic covariance matrix: the acceleration factor
and optimal coordinates [ 34]. Parallel analysis [ 35] was also performed as a
complementary metric for determining the optimal number of factors.
These ﬁndings (see Results section) led us to perform exploratory factor
analysis (EFA) with promax rotation for one, two, and three latent factors
(i.e., k ± 1) using the GenomicSEM R package. Model selection was guided
by assessing incremental variance explained and parsimony of the implied
latent factor structure.
Based on EFA model results (see Results section), con ﬁrmatory factor
analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the ﬁt of the EFA-implied optimal
factor model using unit variance identi ﬁcation. Model ﬁt was evaluated
using the model χ
2 statistic, the comparative ﬁt index (CFI), and the
standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

Genetic correlations with other complex traits
Genetic correlations ( rg) were estimated between the identi ﬁed latent
factors and other complex traits measured in external samples (Supple-
mentary Table 1) using GenomicSEM. These phenotypes were organized
into three broad categories: cognition and education, psychopathology,
and cortical morphology (see Supplementary Table 1 for a complete list). A
χ
2 difference test was used to assess whether the latent factors differed in
their genetic correlations with other traits. Essentially, this involved testing
whether model ﬁt signiﬁcantly worsened when the r
g between each factor
and the external trait were constrained to be equivalent. Additionally, QTrait
heterogeneity tests were used to evaluate the extent to which genetic
relationships between the factors and an external trait were adequately
explained by the common pathway implied by the factor structure [ 36]. To
account for multiple testing, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used
to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at α = 0.05 within each family of
tests for each factor. Although raw p-values are reported in the text,
statistical signi ﬁcance was determined by an FDR-corrected p-value (q-
value) of less than 0.05.
Conditional genetic associations between cognitive factors
and externally measured traits
Being interested in the unique contributions of the model-identi ﬁed latent
genetic factors, we used GenomicSEM to ﬁt multiple genetic regression
models, quantifying the degree to which genetic associations between the
factors and other traits were unique (i.e., the conditional genetic
association). Speci ﬁcally, each external trait Y was regressed onto the
two identi ﬁed latent cognitive factors simultaneously, taking the form
Y~F1 + F2. This provides estimates of the independent (or unique)
association of each factor with Y, conditional on its association with the
other factor.

DISCUSSION
Sustained attention and executive control are fundamental
aspects of higher-order cognition that are integral to adaptive
human behavior. De ﬁcits in both are evident across the spectrum
of psychiatric illness. Using data from the AFFECT study, we report
the ﬁrst multivariate examination of molecular genetic in ﬂuences
on multiple sustained attention and executive processes, provid-
ing novel insights into the complex genetic architecture of
cognition. Our ﬁndings reveal that sustained attention is
signiﬁcantly heritable and in ﬂuenced by common genetic variants
with complex patterns of pleiotropy. These in ﬂuences exhibit
modest patterns of genetic overlap with other cognitive
phenotypes and unique patterns of genetic association with
psychopathology independent of executive functioning. Notably,
genetic in ﬂuences on sustained attention stability appear dissoci-
able from those driving executive ability.
The results of our study provide the ﬁrst large-scale empirical
evidence that individual differences in sustained attention
processes are in ﬂuenced by a shared polygenic common variant
architecture. Consistent with ﬁndings from prior GWAS of
cognitive traits, the SNP-based heritability estimates considered
here were generally modest for task-based measures of executive
function and sustained attention, ranging from 0.08 for cognitive
ﬂexibility to 0.18 for processing speed and lapse propensity.
Moreover, the common genetic variants in ﬂuencing these
phenotypes tended to be quite pleiotropic, but with distinct
patterns of genetic sharing observed across the six phenotypes.
We found that patterns of genetic sharing tended to segregate
across distinct cognitive domains, with processing speed, working
memory, and response selection being strongly genetically
correlated with each other, while vigilance, lapse propensity,
and lapse recovery cost were more genetically interrelated. Our
exploratory results are generally in line with prior phenotypic
factor analyses, which found that measures derived from the
gradCPT tend to be distinct from those derived from measures of
executive function; further, they are distinct even from a latent
factor indexing general attentional capacity [ 38]. Moreover, prior
work suggests that vigilance (i.e., response time variability), which
loaded most strongly onto the Sustained Attention factor, also
appears to exhibit life-course trajectories distinct from those
related to ﬂuid intelligence components of executive functioning
[39].
Our genomic factor analysis provided deeper insights into the
observed patterns of complex genetic sharing and differentiation,
underscoring the segregation of genetic covariance into two
domains. We found that the observed patterns were best
explained by two latent genetic factors: one that in ﬂuences
“classic” executive processes, and one that in ﬂuences sustained
attention. Interestingly, the Executive Function and Sustained
Attention factors exhibited only a modest, nonsigni ﬁcant genetic
correlation with one another. However, this estimate is imprecise
at current sample sizes, with a 95% con ﬁdence interval that
extends from −0.04 to 0.54. Notably, lapse recovery cost appeared
to have particularly complex relationships with other phenotypes.
In contrast to traditional performance monitoring tasks where
explicit feedback is provided, post-error slowing in the gradCPT
may reﬂect the cognitive demand associated with re-engaging an
on-task state (i.e., a lapse recovery cost) rather than an adaptive
adjustment of executive control. This is consistent with the
genetic overlap between greater recovery cost and poorer
executive performance across internal and external measures.
Our genetic correlation analyses substantiated the convergent
and discriminant validity of these factors, as both were
signiﬁcantly related to a wide variety of cognitive traits, with
Executive Function
exhibiting stronger and broader patterns of
associations. This is further evidenced by signi ﬁcant indicator-level
heterogeneity observed for genetic correlations between Sus-
tained Attention and externally measured cognitive traits, in
contrast to largely homogeneous associations between these
traits and indicators for Executive Function . Although associations
between Sustained Attention and other cognitive phenotypes
could largely be attributed to its modest genetic overlap with
Executive Function , both factors exhibited signi ﬁcant independent
associations with educational attainment.
These independent genetic contributions are consistent with
experimental work dissociating executive ability from attentional
stability [1, 2, 11, 12], and in line with neuroimaging studies linking
these cognitive domains to distinct, but interacting, neural
circuitry. Whereas executive function is most strongly associated
with the engagement of dorsal attention and frontoparietal
control systems [ 40– 44], sustained attention appears to uniquely
rely on arousal- and engagement-related circuits that may help
orchestrate the large-scale network dynamics associated with on-
task performance (e.g. dorsal attention network engagement and
default-mode network suppression) [ 1, 45– 52]. Notably, a con-
nectomic pro ﬁle that robustly predicts individual differences in
performance for multiple facets of attention (including sustained
attention) [53] failed to predict performance on executive function
tasks, further emphasizing differences in the neural mechanisms
that support executive function ability versus sustained attention
stability [ 44].
When we examined genomic relationships between the
Executive Function and Sustained Attention factors and psychiatric
outcomes, we found that both factors were generally negatively
correlated with clinical phenotypes, with stronger relationships
observed for the Executive Function factor. In contrast to external
cognitive traits, we observed higher concordance of indicator-
level genetic correlations between external psychiatric disorders
and the Sustained Attention factor. After correcting for multiple
tests, we found that Sustained Attention was only signi ﬁcantly
genetically correlated with ADHD and alcohol use disorder, while
Executive Function was signi ﬁcantly genetically correlated with
ADHD, anxiety disorder, alcohol use disorder, bipolar disorder,
cannabis use disorder, major depressive disorder, obsessive-
compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia. This difference is
potentially attributable, in part, to the narrower focus of sustained
attention as a cognitive construct and the notably lower statistical
power of the Sustained Attention factor. Interestingly, follow-up
genetic regression models revealed that Sustained Attention and
Executive Function are each uniquely genetically related to ADHD,
indicative of a multifaceted etiological relationship between
cognition and ADHD. These results highlight the importance of
considering a more comprehensive range of cognitive domains
beyond executive function when studying inter-individual varia-
tion in cognition across disorders [ 54].

Finally, we found that the Executive Function and Sustained
Attention factors were not robustly genetically correlated with
measures of cortical thickness or surface area. While nominally
signiﬁcant associations were observed, no genetic correlations
survived FDR correction. Given the modest effect sizes of brain-
behavior relationships at both phenotypic and genetic levels of
analysis [ 37, 55, 56], it is likely that the cognitive process GWAS
reported here are not suf ﬁciently well-powered to characterize
nuanced etiological relationships in relatively small imaging genetic
samples. Prior GWAS of general cognitive function [21] with a much
larger sample size (N = 269,867) exhibited only modest genetic
correlation with total cortical surface area ( r
g = 0.27) and non-
signiﬁcant genetic correlation with average cortical thickness [ 37].
Meanwhile, genetic correlations with region-speci ﬁc surface area
and thickness ranged from −0.15 to 0.27, with the vast majority
failing to reach statistical signi ﬁcance. Thus, dramatically larger
sample sizes will be necessary for GWAS of attention-related
cognitive phenotypes to uncover potential genetic sharing with
region-speciﬁc variation in cortical morphology.
At the level of performance, sustained attention is closely
intertwined with executive function, but cognitive neuroscience
models typically distinguish vigilance or attentional stability-
related processes from executive control operations such as goal
maintenance, working memory updating, and response selection
[7– 10, 47– 50]. Here, the relevant question is not whether
sustained attention is phenotypically important for executive
performance, but whether the common variant in ﬂuences on
these domains are suf ﬁciently overlapping to be explained by a
single latent genetic factor. Our results do not support such a
unidimensional architecture. In fact, our results suggest that
common genetic in ﬂuences on executive control and sustained
attentional stability are partially separable. The estimated genetic
correlation between Sustained Attention and Executive Function
was modestly positive, but statistically non-signi ﬁcant ( r
g = 0.25,
SE = 0.15, 95% CI = −0.04 to 0.54). Thus, while there may be some
degree of genetic overlap between these two factors, our current
level of precision cannot distinguish modest overlap from near-
independence with con ﬁdence. However, the SNP-based herit-
abilities and covariances estimated here re ﬂect only additive
common variant in ﬂuences, rather than the full set of factors
contributing to phenotypic variance and covariance (e.g., environ-
mental factors). Accordingly, our results are more informative
about the organization of common variant liability across these
domains than about the precise neurobiological mechanisms that
underlie their phenotypic expression.
Several additional limitations should be considered when
interpreting the present ﬁndings. Most notably, although our
GWAS of task-based attentional processes are among the largest
to date, the AFFECT study sample is still considerably smaller than
recently published molecular genetic studies of other executive
functions (for example, those in the UK Biobank) [ 57, 58]. While
the present study is generally well-powered for characterizing the
genome-wide correlational structure of executive processes and
investigating their links to other complex traits, it is not well-
powered for variant discovery. This is especially true given the
modest SNP heritability estimates of investigated traits. Further-
more, to reduce confounding related to population strati ﬁcation,
the current analyses were restricted to those of European ancestry.
As AFFECT study samples were collected in collaboration with a
consumer genetics company, it is also possible that there are
unique ascertainment biases present. Additionally, this cohort is
enriched for patients with diagnoses of major depressive disorder
or bipolar disorder, which may limit the generalizability of its
ﬁndings to non-psychiatric populations. Furthermore, although
task-based measures of cognitive function are often more
engaging for participants, all cognitive phenotyping was per-
formed through multiple online assessments over an extended
period, which may introduce participation and attrition biases or
reduce the precision and reliability of our cognitive measures
[59, 60]. Moreover, administering cognitive tasks at separate
sessions could potentially in ﬂate their within-task correlations and
bias factor solutions. However, this may be partially mitigated by
the fact that both the gradCPT and THINC-it were measured twice.
Additionally, patterns of correlations between each factor and
cognitive traits measured in independent samples provided
external validation for our identi ﬁed factor solution. This is the
ﬁrst attempt to characterize the molecular genetic architecture of
sustained attention and its links with other cognitive processes.
Future work will be necessary to replicate and build on these
ﬁndings, ideally by incorporating data from in-person assessments
while expanding the ancestral and contextual diversity of
participants. Further, incorporating multiple sustained-attention
task variants, richer executive batteries, and more mechanistically
proximal neurobiological phenotypes may further re ﬁne the
structure of these liabilities.
This study describes the ﬁrst multivariate genomic examination
of sustained attention and executive function, and their links to
other cognitive and psychiatric phenotypes. We found that
sustained attention processes are in ﬂuenced by genetic factors
that are relatively distinct from those that in ﬂuence executive
function. Critically, we identi ﬁed several instances of unique or
differential genetic overlap with psychiatric disorders like ADHD,
validating the notion that the dimensions of cognitive variation
that drive psychiatric symptoms are multifactorial in nature [ 54].
We also found evidence of unique genetic relationships with
educational attainment, where Sustained Attention and Executive
Function were jointly related to this complex life outcome. Our
results suggest that expanding the scope of cognitive phenotypes
studied may lead to additional insights into the latent genetic
architecture of cognition and clarify how distinct cognitive
liabilities may relate to psychiatric risk.
