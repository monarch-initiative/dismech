# CGGV:assertion_14eaa3eb-1b94-4ae3-a233-7e47e6817021-2025-04-03T160000.000Z  DGKE / atypical hemolytic-uremic syndrome with DGKE deficiency

**CGGV:assertion_14eaa3eb-1b94-4ae3-a233-7e47e6817021-2025-04-03T160000.000Z** - DGKE / atypical hemolytic-uremic syndrome with DGKE deficiency (Definitive)

## Evidence summary

The DGKE gene is located on chromosome 17 at q22 and encodes the protein diacylglycerol kinase epsilon. DGKE phosphorylates and inactivates arachidonic acid-containing diacylglycerol (AA-DAG) to the corresponding phosphatidic acid. AA-DAG is major signaling molecule that activates protein kinase C (PKC). PKC, in turn, increases the production of various prothrombotic factors in endothelial cells. Thus, loss of DGKE may result in sustained AA-DAG signaling, causing a prothrombotic state (PMIDs: 23542698). It is expressed in the endothelium of glomerular capillaries and podocytes (PMID: 23542698).

Multiple disease entities have been reported in association with this gene. Per criteria outlined by the ClinGen Lumping and Splitting Working Group, there was evidence of differences in their phenotypic spectrum. Therefore, the following disease entities have been split into multiple disease entityies: atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) and membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN). Autosomal recessive MPGN has been curated separately.

DGKE was first reported in relation to autosomal recessive aHUS in 2013 (Lemaire et al., PMID: 23542698). The disease is characterized by acute onset in the first year of life of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and renal failure. The mechanism of pathogenicity is known to be loss of function.

At least twelve variants (missense, nonsense, and frameshift) have been reported in 10 probands in three publications (PMIDs: 23542698, 25135762, 26018111 included in this curation). More evidence is available in the literature, but the maximum score for genetic evidence (12 pts.) has been reached, considering case-level data.

This gene-disease relationship is also supported by expression

studies and an animal model. Expression of DGKE was identified in the glomerular endothelium with altered expression demonstrated in patients diagnosed with aHUS (PMID: 23542698). Additionally, DGKE knockout mouse models demonstrated glomeruli impairment supportive of disease (PMID: 26887830). A total of 2/6 pts. for experimental evidence was reached.

In summary, there is definitive evidence supporting the relationship between DGKE and autosomal recessive atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome. This has been repeatedly demonstrated in both the research and clinical diagnostic settings and has been upheld over time.
