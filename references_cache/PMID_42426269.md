Abstract 
 
Timothy syndrome (TS) is a multisystem disorder with autistic-like features, seizures and life-threatening 
arrhythmias. Most TS cases are caused by a de novo  missense mutation in the CACNA1C gene that 
encodes the pore -forming subunit of the voltage -gated L-type calcium channel Ca V1.2. Patients show 
highly variable clinical presentations and survival, which may be impacted by acute episodes of 
hypoglycemia and respiratory dysfunction. In addition, fever, known to activate Ca V1.2 channels, may 
exacerbate TS, yet its impact is poorly understood. We generated a zebrafish model carrying the 
homologous cacna1c mutation. These fish exhibited arrhythmias, increased susceptibility to 
pentylenetetrazole-induced seizures, microcephaly, cerebellar hypotrophy and abnorma l GABAergic 
neuron development. Transcriptomic analysis revealed dysregulated expression of neuropeptides 
including bdnf and vgf. Notably, elevated water temperature elicited arrhythmia and seizure-like behavior 
even in overtly normal heterozygotes, implic ating fever as a modifiable risk. This model provides a 
platform for dissecting TS pathophysiology and identifying strategies to mitigate fever-related risk. 
 
Introduction 
 
Timothy syndrome (TS) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by a de novo gain of function 
mutation in CACNA1C, the gene encoding the pore -forming α-1 subunit of the L -type voltage -gated 
calcium channel (LTCC) CaV1.2. Major symptoms of TS include cardiac arrhythmia with QT interval 
prolongation, cutaneous syndactyly and autisti c behaviors (reviewed in 1). Most patients have a G406R 
missense mutation in either of two mutually exclusive, alternatively spliced isoforms of exon 8; mutations 
in exon 8A cause Timothy Syndrome 1 (TS1), whereas mutations in exon 8 cause Timothy Syndrome  2 
(TS2). Each exon 8 variant encodes 34 amino acids with partially similar amino acid sequences, and 
channels with mutations in either exon 8 or 8A show increased inward calcium current due to a reduction 
in voltage-dependent inactivation2,3. Clinical features show a high degree of variability, that may reflect 
differences in genetic background or mosaicism, especially in light of complex tissue-specific patterns of 
CACNA1C alternative splicing 4–6. However, acute challenges such as hypoglycemia, anesthesia and 
respiratory distress may also contribute to variable outcomes. Indeed, a recent TS2 mouse model 
demonstrated abnormal activity in CACNA1C expressing hypothalamic glucose-sensing neurons that may 
relate to patient hypoglycemia 7, emphasizing the value of models for dissecting pathophysiology 
associated with potentially preventable triggers.  
 
Notably, fever has an established role in aggravating other long QT syndromes 8 including in Brugada 
syndrome, where fever-induced ventricular fibrillation carries a high risk of sudden death 9. Intriguingly, 
the Ca V1.2 channel has an unusual property of spontaneously opening at temperatures above 37 °C, 
leading to cellular depolarization10. This may be intensified in TS CaV1.2 channels, which show delayed 
inactivation. Moreover, because  TS patients are prone to infections, elevated L -type calcium channel 
activity during fever may lead to tachyarrhythmia, which is a major cause of mortality;  in fact infection 
was reported as an associated factor for sudden death in a TS case report11. Together, this highlights fever 
as a potentially mitigatable condition for patients. However, the impact of fever in TS is uncertain and 
difficult to study without a suitable model.

In zebrafish, body temperature can be precisely controlled by adjusting the temperature of the surrounding 
water. Moreover, animals are raised at 28 °C, well below the spontaneous opening threshold for Ca V1.2 
channels. A zebrafish cacna1c loss of function allele caused developmental defects in ventricular 
cardiomyocytes and mandible; however, gain of function phenotypes relevant to Timothy syndrome have 
not been examined12,13. We therefore generated a zebrafish knock-in model with a mutation homologous 
to the TS2 exon 8 G406R mutation, compared phenotypes to clinical manifestations and tested the effects 
of elevated water temperature on behavior and physiology. 
 
Homozygous mutants showed a range of phenotypes reminiscent of symptoms in patients. At embryonic 
stages, both cardiac chambers showed reduced contraction frequency, reminiscent of prenatal and early 
postnatal bradycardia reported in patients 5. Several days later, contraction frequency normalized but 
mutants demonstrated an in termittent 2:1 atrioventricular block, recapitulating a characteristic patient 
arrhythmia. Epilepsy is present in a high proportion of patients, and accordingly, homozygous mutants 
showed heightened sensitivity to pentylenetetrazol, a seizure -inducing agen t. We used brain -wide 
morphometry to systematically assess changes in brain structure and composition. Mutants showed mild 
microcephaly and a disproportionately severe reduction in cerebellar tissue adjacent to the midbrain -
hindbrain boundary (MHB). Differentiated neuron markers were inappropriately present at the midline in 
mutants, and inhibitory neuron markers were altered in a region -specific manner. Importantly, 
heterozygous larvae did not manifest cardiac abnormalities or seizure -like behavior under n ormal 
conditions, but when tested at elevated temperature produced seizures and arrhythmias. These findings 
establish a new model for studying the pathophysiology of TS2 and support the possibility that fever is a 
preventable risk in TS patients.

to 7 dpf, before sex differentiation. Numbers for brain imaging and behavioral experiments were based 
on previous work 14,15. Larvae used in seizure experiments, GCaMP imaging and temperature -induced

so that the investigator was blind to the genotype. Due to technical constraints, brain morphometry and 
analysis of heart rate after splice switching were performed on pre -genotyped larvae and not performed 
blind. All data are included (no data points were excluded). We have complied with all relevant ethical 
regulations for animal use.

Behavioral analysis. To measure seizure-like activity, larvae derived from a heterozygous incross were 
transferred to individual wells of a 36-well testing arena filled with E3 embryo medium with 1% DMSO 
(vehicle control) or freshly prepared 1 mM PTZ in E3 with 1% DMSO. Wells had dimensions 7 × 7 mm.

experiment, and for each measure we assessed significance using ANOV A to calculate the main effect of 
genotype, controlling for the experiment and controlling the false discovery rate with Benjamini -
Hochberg correction. To assess activity for larvae at 37°C, we used the same temperature controller as for 
cardiac contraction experiments, with larvae in a 4 -well grid inside the chamber, with each well having 
the same dimensions as above.

Results 
 
Zebrafish with the Timothy syndrome 2 mutation are viable with increased neuronal activity 
Human and zebrafish Cacna1c protein sequences are highly conserved, with 78.9% identity. We examined 
genomic sequence in zebrafish to assess the feasibility of generating a zebrafish mutation that precisely 
recapitulated patient mutations in Timothy syndrome. The G406R mutation in Timothy syndrome is due 
to a missense mutation that can occur at the end of  either of the two mutually exclusive exon 8 variants, 
exon 8 or exon 8A. Patient mutations that affect either exon 8 or 8A have overlapping clinical 
manifestations, including autism, seizures and cardiac arrhythmia. However, mutations in exon 8 lead to 
worse outcomes than 8A mutations, and present other differences such as a lack of syndactyly 3. These 
phenotypic variations may be because exon 8 is around 4-fold more abundant in brain and heart tissue, or 
because exons 8 and 8A show distinct temporal patter ns of expression during neural development. 
Similarly, the zebrafish homolog of cacna1c comprises alternate exons 8 and 8A, which encode amino 
acid sequences that are 100% and 94% identical to human exons 8 and 8A respectively (Fig. 1A) .

Quantitative PCR at 2, 4 and 6 dpf did not detect an age-related change in exon 8 usage vs exon 8A (F2,10 
= 2.89, p = 0.11), and exon 8 was at least 13-fold more abundant than exon 8A at all ages (Fig. S1A).  
 
We generated a G419R missense mutation (corresponding to human G406R) at the end of the more highly 
expressed exon 8, thereby creating allele cacna1cy680 as a potential model for Timothy syndrome 2 (TS2 
mutant, Fig. 1B). Homozygous mutant (herein 'mutant') and heterozygous TS2 larvae were overtly similar 
to their wild-type siblings in body size and overall appearance (Fig. 1C). Early mortality is common in 
TS patients. Only 23% of mutants from a TS2 heterozygous incross survived to adulthood when raised in 
mixed genotype pools, significantly less than the survival rates of 69% and 90% for heterozygous and 
wild-type fish respectively (ANOV A main effect of genotype, F 2,6 = 25.037, p = 0.001; Fig. S1B). 
However, the mortality of mutants raised in same-genotype pools was only 18% greater than that of wild-
type siblings; one possibility is that diminished survival in mutants raised in mixed -genotype groups 
reflects a reduced ability to compete with siblings (Fig. 1D). Surviving mutants were sexually dimorphic 
and fertile, although males were smaller than wild -type siblings (ANOV A, interaction of genotype and 
sex, F2,50 = 3.5, p = 0.037 ;  Fig. S1C). Because the G419R mutation resulted from nucleotide changes at 
the 3' end of exon 8, we questioned whether the relatively normal growth and survival observed in mutants 
might be due to a splicing change that minimized the mutation ’s effects by increasing expression of 
isoforms incorporating the wild-type exon 8A. However, we found no significant changes in the frequency 
of splicing into or out of either exon 8 or 8A in mutants, making it unlikely that splice changes mask the 
mutant phenotype (Fig. S2). Thus, unlike in mammals where TS mutations are dominant and lead to high 
mortality, heterozygous TS2 zebrafish are overtly normal and even homozygotes are viable and fertile7.  
 
We anticipated that the TS2 mutation would cause elevated activity in cacna1c-expressing neurons due 
to sustained depolarization. To test this, we introduced the mutation into fish with pan -neuronal GCaMP 
expression and measured fluorescence intensity over a 5 minute period for each larva. Mutant larvae 
showed greater changes in fluorescence compared to wild-types, confirming that the TS2 mutation leads 
to increased neuronal activity (Fig. 1E-F, Supp. Video 1). Activity was elevated in all brain subdivisions,

Mutants exhibit embryonic bradycardia and intermittent cardiac arrhythmia 
Life-threatening episodes of arrhythmia (including long QT-interval, bradycardia and 2:1 atrioventricular 
block) are a characteristic feature of Timothy syndrome. The loss -of-function cacna1c mutation (island 
beat) had dissimilar effects on muscle cells from two cardiac chambers  in zebrafish, with atrial 
cardiomyocytes showing uncoordinated contractions and ventricular cardiomyocytes failing to contract, 
even in response to direct electrical stimulation 12. We therefore used high -speed video recordings to 
monitor each cardiac cham ber separately. Contraction frequency was normal in 2 dpf heterozygous 
embryos (Fig. S4A) but was significantly reduced in both chambers in homozygotes (Fig. 3A).  
 
The expression of alternative splice isoforms of cacna1c has been leveraged to correct deficits in a human 
TS1 organoid model36. An antisense oligonucleotide that prevented splicing of exon 8A, which included 
the gain of function mutation, led to increased expression of the unaffected exon 8 and rescued deficits in 
neural development. We designed an antisense morpholino to block splicing from exon 7 to exon 8 at the 
intron 7–exon 8 (i7e8) junction, leading to increased inclusion of exon 8A (Fig. S4B). E mbryos injected 
with the i7e8 morpholino showed increased ca rdiac contraction frequency (ANOV A main effect of 
treatment, F 1,44 = 16.7, p < 0.001 ; Fig. 3B, Fig. S4C -D), such that mutants treated with the i7e8 
morpholino showed a contraction frequency similar to wild-types and were therefore normalized. 
 
By 3 dpf, we noted that mean atrial contraction rates in homozygotes were no longer significantly reduced, 
but that in some larvae, the ventricle contraction frequency was half that of the atrium, a phenomenon 
seen in 2:1 atrioventricular (A V) block (Fig. 3C -E, Supp . Video 2 -3). Whereas all wild -type and 
heterozygous larvae showed matched (1:1) A V contraction frequency, 22% (6/27) of mutants had a 2:1 
rhythm, and 14% an intermediate ratio (Fig. 3C). To assess whether arrhythmia was intermittent or a stable 
phenotype in individual mutant larvae, we recorded from the same larvae twice, at an interval separated 
by 4 -6 hours. Around 10% (4/41) of mutants switched between matched A V rhythms and a 2:1 beat 
frequency (Fig. 3F). We observed an abrupt switch from a matched A V rhythm to a 2:1 rhythm during one

TS2 larvae display elevated seizure susceptibility 
As seizure disorders are seen in a majority of TS2 patients 5, we assessed whether TS2 fish showed 
heightened seizure susceptibility. We exposed larvae to pentylenetetrazol (PTZ), a GABA receptor 
antagonist that, at low doses, only sl ightly elevates the risk of seizure -like activity in larval zebrafish 37. 
We scored seizures by detecting large-amplitude swimming movements that were detected at a frequency 
of less than one event per 15 minute recording period in untreated wildtype larvae  (Fig. S5A-B). There 
were no genotype-related differences in locomotor activity in larvae not exposed to PTZ (Fig. 4A-B, PTZ 
0 mM). However, after PTZ treatment, homozygous mutants tended to show long periods of immobility 
interspersed with unusually vigorous swim bouts, similar to previous descriptions of seizure-like activity 
in zebrafish (Fig. 4B, PTZ 1 mM). We also imaged brain activity using pan-neuronal GCaMP expression 
in immobilized larvae during PTZ treatment (Fig. 4C). Brain activity showed a much  stronger elevation 
in homozygous mutants than in wildtype siblings, consistent with greater susceptibility to seizures (Fig. 
4D). "Frequent startle reflexes" and distress caused by loud noises were reported for TS2 patients3,5. While 
the exact cause of th ese auditory-associated symptoms in TS2 are unknown, we tested auditory sensory 
and sensorimotor gating but did not detect changes in escape reactivity or prepulse inhibition (Fig. S5C -

auditory reactivity.

To examine brain structure changes in TS2 mutants systematically, we measured brain structure and

Elevated temperature exacerbates phenotypes in TS2 mutants 
Case reports suggest that individuals with long QT syndrome (including in one TS2 case) may be 
especially at risk for severe arrhythmias during febrile illness8,11. Opening of CaV1.2 calcium channels at 
hyperpolarized membrane potentials when the temperature is above 37°C may contribute to this 
pathology10. We reasoned that individuals with even one TS2 gain of function allele might be highly 
sensitive to increased temperatures, potentially explaining why heterozygous mutations have such severe 
consequences in mammals, whereas heterozygous zebrafish (which are maintained at 28°C) showed 
relatively subtle changes. We speculated that we might reveal phenotypes in heterozygous larvae by 
shifting them to 37°C, a temperature that is normally well tolerated by zebrafish. Consistent with a 
previous report, cardiac contraction frequency in 5 dpf wild-type larvae increased to around 290 beats per 
minute (bpm) at 37°C (Fig. 7A 41. Atrioventricular contraction rates remained coupled and spectrograms 
revealed no additional frequencies. However, heterozygous and mutan t larvae showed significantly 
elevated contraction frequencies compared to wild-types, and ventricle spectrograms revealed a range of 
additional frequencies suggesting arrhythmia (Fig. 7A,B; Fig. S10A,B).  Heart rate in larvae of all 
genotypes exposed to 37°C for 30 s decreased after 90 minutes of recovery at 28°C, but remained slightly 
elevated (Fig. S10C), Thus, as hypothesized, at a tem perature expected to activate L -type calcium 
channels, heterozygous larvae exhibited arrhythmia, whereas at lower temperatures, they resembled wild‐
type larvae. 
 
We asked whether heat sensitivity extended to the manifestation of seizure -like activity. At 28°C, larvae 
that were not treated with pentylenetetrazol showed very few seizure-like locomotor episodes, irrespective 
of genotype (Fig. 4B). In contrast, at 37°C, both heterozygous and homozygous mutants showed a 
significantly greater frequency of seizure -like episodes than wild -types, even in the absence of 
sensitization with pentylenetetrazol (ANOV A main effect of genotype F2,145 = 17.2, p < 0.001; Fig. 7C). 
The increase in seizure-like episodes was not part of a generalized increase in movement:  the swimming 
speed during movement bouts was not significantly different between mutation carriers and wild -type 
embryos (F2,145 = 0.88, p = 0.42 ; Fig. S10D). Elevated seizure-like behavior was greater and more robust 
across experiments in homozygous than heter ozygous larvae (Fig. S10E -F). We next examined how 
elevated temperature effected neural activity in mutants. As previously reported, GCaMP fluorescence 
was reduced at increased temperature (ANOV A, main effect of temperature, F1,7 = 16.5, p = 0.005 ; Fig. 
S11)42. However, at elevated temperature, activity transients were significantly increased in homozygotes 
compared to 28°C, corroborating the idea that the mutant channel causes increased heat sensitivity (Fig. 
7D-E). Together with the appearance of cardiac and behavioral phenotypes in heterozygous larvae when 
tested at an elevated temperature, these results suggest that normal rearing temperatures protect larvae 
against adverse effects of the cacna1c gain of function mutation and support the idea that fever may be a 
preventable risk in TS.

Discussion 
 
Acute stressors, particularly hypoglycemia and respiratory distress, have been linked to serious adverse 
outcomes in TS5. However, risks associated with fever, which causes lethal arrhythmias in other long QT 
syndromes, are unknown. We generated a new zebrafish model with the homologous gain -of-function 
mutation to assess TS pathophysiology. Cacna1c is highly conserved; in particular, the 30 amino acid 
residues that flank the mutation site  are identical in human and zebrafish. Cacna1cG419R zebrafish 
recapitulate key features of Timothy syndrome: bradycardia, episodic arrhythmia and seizure 
susceptibility. Homozygous mutant TS2 zebrafish showed a reduction in the midbrain-hindbrain boundary 
and, correspondingly, a reduction in the size of adjacent midbrain and hindbrain areas. Several 
neuropeptide genes showed elevated expression in mutants, including bdnf and vgf, genes implicated in 
the control of neurogenesis. Although TS is a dominant disorder, heterozygous zebrafish showed only 
subtle abnormalities in brain morphology and transcription but normal survival and fertility. Under 
standard conditions, heterozygotes did not show arrhythmia or seizure susceptibility, but produced these 
phenotypes when body temperature was raised to 37°C, a condition where CaV1.2 channels open 
spontaneously [8].  
 
Signature features of TS include a prolonged QT interval, syn dactyly and neurodevelopmental 
abnormalities including autism and seizures3. TS2, which arises from a G406R mutation in exon 8 rather 
than exon 8A, is clinically similar but without syndactyly. To assess the extent to which deficits in 
Cacna1cG419R zebrafish resemble clinical manifestations in TS2, we focused on cardiac and seizure 
phenotypes, which have been extensively characterized in other zebrafish models. TS2 mutant fish 
exhibited bradycardia at 2 dpf and intermittent arrhythmia similar to 2:1 atriove ntricular block (A VB) as 
older larvae. Bradycardia and A VB are present in a majority of TS2 patients perinatally5. Homozygous 
(but not heterozygous) TS2 larvae exposed to a low dose of the GABA receptor antagonist 
pentylenetetrazol showed an increase in the number of seizure -like locomotor events compared to 
siblings. Together, the presence of specific cardiac arrhythmias – bradycardia and 2:1 A VB – and seizure 
susceptibility indicate that TS2 larvae recapitulate key TS clinical manifestations and may therefore 
provide a useful tool for investigating how gain -of-function mutations in this disorder disrupt normal 
development.  
 
During neural development, there is a switch from the inclusion of exon 8A in neural progenitors to the 
inclusion of exon 8 in mature neurons 4,6. It was recently shown that the use of an antisense inhibitory 
oligonucleotide to prevent the inclusion  of exon 8A increased exon 8 inclusion and rescued 
neurodevelopmental phenotypes in a TS1 brain organoid model 36. I njection of morpholino antisense 
oligonucleotides into zebrafish embryos is routinely used to block pre -mRNA splicing43, but it was not 
clear whether a similar strategy would be effective in zebrafish TS2 mutants where the mutation affects 
exon 8, which shows more than 10 -fold greater utilization than exon 8A throughout development. 
However, a morpholino against the intron 7 - exon 8 boundary was highly effective in switching isoform 
expression from exon 8 to exon 8A. Cardiac contraction frequency in exon 8A -containing larvae was 
increased irrespective of genotype, such that mutants injected with the splice -blocking morpholino 
showed similar con traction frequency to wild -types injected with a control morpholino. This illustrates

the utility of the zebrafish model for the development of antisense oligonucleotide -based therapeutics, 
where the external development and optical transparency of early l arvae can enable studies of 
oligonucleotide effects on various cell types and help in delineating the optimal developmental window 
for treatment. 
 
Consistent with a key role for intracellular calcium signaling in regulating gene expression in neurons44,45, 
gene expression in neurons derived from Timothy syndrome patient stem cells and stem cells from healthy 
controls differed considerably 46. Accordingly, RNA -seq data from homozygous mutants showed 
widespread changes, prominently in the expression of genes t hat encode neuropeptides. A known 
downstream target of calcium influx through cacna1c containing L-type calcium channels is bdnf47. RNA-
seq revealed elevated expression of several neuropeptides in mutants, including bdnf, its downstream 
factor vgf and si:dkey-175g6.2, a second homolog of vgf present in zebrafish, which was also elevated in 
TS2 heterozygous larvae. Increased expression of these neuropeptides may contribute to changes in 
neurogenesis: overexpression of Bdnf in mouse visual cortex led to accelerated maturation of GABAergic 
innervation; a similar process could account for the elevated GABAergic signal observed in some brain 
regions of homozygous mutants 48. Mice that overexpressed Vgf showed microcephaly and impaired 
cerebellum development 49. Associations with differences in the size of brain or its divisions were reported 
for several other genes differentially regulated in homozygous mutants. Elevated expression of tent5d 
(also known as fam46d) and reduced expression of mcm7 and bub1 orthologs have been reported in mice 
and human patients with microcephaly 50–52. We also observed decreased expression of gcgb (glucagon 
b) in mutants, consistent with reports of lower glucagon immunoreactivity and impaired glucagon 
secretion during hypoglycemia  in a TS mouse model and possibly contributing to life -threatening 
hypoglycemia episodes in patients 7. Thus, cacna1c may have a  conserved role in regulating energy 
metabolism in vertebrates. 
 
We found that cacna1c is broadly expressed throughout stages of neural development in zebrafish, with 
conspicuous expression in the cerebellum. The relative expression levels of cacna1c over time paralleled 
that of the mature neuron marker, sypa; however, other evidence suggests that cacna1c may also regulate 
early developmental processes. Brain size was diminished in mutants, and in particular the total volume 
of brain regions that are predominantly cellular was reduced compared to the total volume of neuropil -
rich regions. There was a disproportionately large reduction at the midbrain -hindbrain boundary (MHB) 
and a matching loss of  en1b expression, a MHB marker. The MHB controls the development of the 
adjacent cerebellum and optic tectum, and consistent with this, we saw a significant reduction in cell 
density in the major neuron-containing area of the optic tectum, the stratum periventriculare. In parallel, 
we observed an increase in the expression of two mature neuron markers – the transgenic line 
tuba:mCardinal, and in situ hybridization for elavl3 – adjacent to the brain midline in mutants, a region 
that normally includes stem cells and proliferating neuroblasts. L-type calcium channels have been 
implicated in regulating neuronal proliferation53. In humans, CACNA1C is strongly expressed in the outer 
subventricular zone, where it may contribute to the depolarizing effects of GABA on neural progenitor 
cells and regulate cell fate decisions4. Premature terminal differentiation of proliferating neuroblasts might 
explain the smaller overall brain volume in mutants, with increased neuron signal at the midline. Indeed, 
bdnf and vgf, genes that were upregulated in homozygous mutants, are known to regulate 
neurogenesis34,35. However, LTCCs are also known to regulate neuronal migration 54. TS2 mice showed

an increase in tangential migration of inhibitory neurons from the median ganglionic eminence (MGE) 55. 
Conversely, electroporation of Cacna1c mRNA with the TS mutation suppressed the radial migration of 
excitatory neurons into the upper cortical layers in mice 56, and in an organoid model, neurons from TS1 
patient-derived stem cells showed delayed migration of MGE interneurons 36,57,58. On the other hand, 
neither inhibition nor activation of L-type calcium channels affected the migration of newborn neurons in 
the olfactory bulb of postnatal mice59. The effects of LTCC activity on neuronal migration appear to vary 
with stage and cell type in the developing brain, and thus, the expression of mature neuron markers at the 
midline region in TS2  mutants might reflect premature terminal differentiation of neuroblasts into 
neurons, suppressed neuronal migration, or both.  
 
Multiple zebrafish models exhibiting seizure -like behavior have been previously characterized (for 
example 60–62). However, only a minority of zebrafish genetic models of epilepsy disorders show seizure-
like swimming behavior; rather neural hyperexcitability may be a more reproducible proxy for human 
seizure disorders 63–65. Accordingly, TS2 larvae exhibited seizure -like swimming when sensitized with 
PTZ and elevated baseline neural activity. Zebrafish have also been widely used to study cardiac 
arrhythmia, including analysis of mutants that like the homozygous mutant, manifest atrioventricular 
block66,67.  
 
Significant cardiac and behavioral changes were detected only in homozygous mutant fish, which could 
be raised with only minor loss of viability to fertile adults. In contrast, patient mutations are dominant and 
lethal. It is not uncommon for mutations that cause haploinsufficie nt psychiatric disorders to be 
homozygous viable in zebrafish68, however, given the severe effects in TS patients we considered whether 
phenotypic effects of the mutation were compensated or masked in zebrafish. It is unlikely that a 
redundant gene mitigat es cacna1c mutations because inactivating mutations in cacna1c cause a lethal 
impairment of heart ventricle development in zebrafish 12. Patient mutations affect the second to last 
nucleotide of exon 8 or 8A, and in an induced pluripotent stem cell model, t his disrupts splicing leading 
to a shift in isoform use 4. However, we did not detect differences in isoform expression or splice site 
selection in TS2 mutants. Of the 8 other L-type voltage-gated calcium channel genes in zebrafish, the only 
significant change at either 2 or 6 dpf was a 10% increase in expression of cacna1sb at 2 dpf, making it 
unlikely that genetic compensation explains the survival of homozygous mutants. We therefore think that 
cellular adaptation to the mutation is unlikely to explain why  heterozygous zebrafish show only mild 
phenotypes. Rather, we speculate that biological and/or environmental factors may account for 
heterozygous viability. First, in the human heart, L-type calcium channels constitute more than 90% of all 
calcium channel transcripts, compared to only 35% in zebrafish, where the majority are T -type calcium 
channels69. Second, zebrafish ventricular myocytes primarily rely on extracellular calcium influx for 
contraction, whereas calcium -induced calcium release from the sarcop lasmic reticulum in human 
cardiomyocytes can amplify the effects of changes in extracellular calcium influx 70. Third, it has been 
argued that due to intrinsic differences in the cardiac conduction system and repolarization properties in 
fish, delayed repolarization of cardiomyocytes is less likely to cause arrhythmia than in mammals71. These 
factors may reduce the sensitivity of zebrafish to activating mutations in cacna1c.  
 
Notably, the mild phenotype of heterozygous larvae enabled us to directly test our  central idea: whether 
elevated body temperature is a risk factor in TS. This hypothesis is based on previously described

temperature sensitivity of L -type calcium channels 10. Electrophysiological experiments on ventricular 
cardiomyocytes and Ca channels expressed in Xenopus oocytes pointed at accelerated transitions between 
inactivated states, rather than channel activation or Ca -dependent inactivation, as the mechanism 
underlying the increased probability of open state for L -type calcium channels at elev ated temperature 
72,73. Elevated temperature unmasked seizure -like behavior in heterozygous larvae, which were normal 
under standard rearing conditions. Moreover, in contrast to measurements at 28°C where a phenotype 
occurred only in homozygous mutants, at  37°C both mutant and heterozygous larvae showed atrial and 
ventricular tachycardia. As TS patients are prone to infections, elevated L -type calcium channel activity 
during fever may lead to tachyarrhythmia, which is a major cause of mortality, and in fact  infection has 
been reported as an associated factor for sudden death in TS 5. These findings emphasize the importance 
of studying whether patient outcomes may be improved by close monitoring and management of body 
temperature and provide a new model for st udying the pathophysiology of TS and testing strategies that 
may mitigate risk associated with fever.
