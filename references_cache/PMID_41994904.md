# Bone Marrow Pathology in Cold Agglutinin-Mediated Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia: A Study of 56 Cases.
**Authors:** Becking AL, Bernelot Moens SJ, Kersten MJ, Mulder FVM, Pals ST, Koens L, Vos JMI
**Journal:** Eur J Haematol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/ejh.70194](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejh.70194)

## Content

Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) is a rare form of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). CAD occurs in the context of a small clonal B-cell lymphoproliferation restricted to blood and/or bone marrow (BM), without overt or extramedullary lymphoma. The WHO-HAEM5 introduced a description of the CAD-associated lymphoproliferative disorder (CAD-LPD) in the BM as a desirable criterium for CAD. Still, data on BM pathology underlying CAD remain scarce. We assessed the clinical and pathologic characteristics of a large patient cohort with cold-type AIHA. A total of 85 BM biopsies of 56 patients were evaluated. In 10 patients (18%), pathology revision revealed other overt B-cell malignancies leading to a diagnosis of cold agglutinin syndrome. In the remaining 46, evidence for B-cell clonality was found in the vast majority (45; 98%), manifesting as a paraproteinemia and/or clonal B-cells or plasma cells in BM. Most of these 45 had an M-protein (89%), evidence of monoclonal B-cells and/or plasma cells in BM tissue (89%), and were MYD88 wild-type (100%). Surprisingly, while ancillary methods detected clonality in the majority (84%), only 5 (11%) of 45 CAD cases strictly fulfilled the WHO-HAEM5 morphological description for CAD-LPD. These data imply that sensitive techniques should be used in BM evaluation in CAD.

Cold agglutinin‐mediated autoimmune hemolytic anemia (cAIHA) is a rare disease, accounting for 15%–25% of all cases of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) [1, 2]. Its prevalence varies with climate, ranging from 5 to 20 per million individuals, and is associated with increased mortality and high disease burden [3, 4, 5]. Autoantibodies in cAIHA, referred to as cold agglutinins, are usually directed against the “I” (or “i”) surface antigen on red blood cells (RBCs) [6]. Most cold agglutinins are immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) gene V4‐34 encoded immunoglobulin M kappa (IgMκ), with optimal binding below body temperatures. Sensitization by IgM and deposition of complement component C3b on RBCs activates the classical complement cascade, resulting in extravascular hemolysis [7, 8, 9, 10]. In addition, the pentameric IgM bridges the distance between RBCs, causing hemagglutination and circulatory symptoms in the acral areas [11].

A cAIHA diagnosis is established with chronic hemolysis and a direct antiglobulin test (DAT) strongly positive for anti‐C3d [12]. A definite diagnosis requires a cold antibody titer of ≥ 64 at 4°C as well, although this cutoff is debated. cAIHA is further subdivided into cold agglutinin syndrome (CAS) and cold agglutinin disease (CAD) [12]. The distinction between the two lies in the presence of an underlying condition in CAS, such as an infection, autoimmune disease, or B‐cell lymphoma that is clinically or radiologically overt. In addition, although CAD is not associated with a lymphoma, the majority shows evidence of a small clonal B‐cell lymphoproliferation in blood and/or bone marrow, thereby further complicating the distinction from CAS [3, 13]. Consequently, CAD cases are often unjustly categorized as other B‐cell lymphoproliferative disorders in which IgM paraproteinemia may occur, including lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) or Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM), marginal zone lymphoma (MZL), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) or small lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL), IgM monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined/clinical significance (MGUS or MGCS), and unclassifiable B‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (B‐NHL) [3]. Accurate identification of CAD is essential, particularly given emerging disease‐tailored therapeutic agents, trials, and guidelines [14, 15, 16, 17].

To reappraise the lymphoproliferation underlying CAD, the bone marrow characteristics of 54 CAD patients were reviewed in a Nordic study [18]. The majority (74%) displayed a small homogeneous lymphoid infiltration, characterized by nodular B‐cell aggregates strongly resembling MZL, but without extramedullary disease. The presence of monotypic plasma cells was consistent with LPL, but key morphological findings diagnostic of LPL were lacking, including paratrabecular growth and lymphoplasmacytoid cells. In addition, the MYD88 L265P mutation, present in over 90% of patients with WM, was not found [19]. The identification of recurrent somatic mutations in the KMT2D and CARD11 genes and trisomies of chromosomes 3, 12, and 18 in a proportion of CAD patients further strengthens the notion of it being a distinct entity [20].

Based on these findings, the pathological classification “CAD‐associated lymphoproliferative disease” (CAD‐LPD) was proposed, and CAD was recognized as a new condition in the 5th edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumors (WHO‐HAEM5) [21]. In WHO‐HAEM5, the diagnosis of CAD is based on several essential and desirable features (Table 1). Desirable criteria for CAD include an observation of CAD‐LPD on bone marrow biopsy based on specific histological, cytological, and genetic properties (Table 1).

In the current study, we revisited a large, well‐characterized cohort of patients with cAIHA at our expertise center to re‐evaluate and accurately characterize CAD and its underlying histopathological bone marrow features in a real‐world setting. Secondary objective was to validate the WHO‐HAEM5 CAD and CAD‐LPD criteria within our cohort.

All cAIHA patients, seen at our tertiary referral hospital between 2000 and 2025, were screened using an extensive search strategy (Supporting Information). Inclusion criteria were presence of chronic hemolysis, a DAT positive for C3d (≥ 2+) and negative or weakly positive for IgG, and presence of cold‐reactive antibodies. Patients who did not undergo bone marrow examination were excluded, as well as patients with an associated infection or autoimmune disorder. To prevent inclusion bias, cases previously classified as CAS secondary to a lymphoma, regardless of its classification, were eligible. Diagnoses of underlying disease before revision were categorized into “no monoclonal gammopathy or bone marrow abnormalities,” “monoclonal gammopathy without bone marrow abnormalities,” “unclassifiable clonal B‐cell and/or plasmacytosis in bone marrow,” “LPL,” “MZL,” “CLL/SLL,” or “other.”

Retrospective collection of archival samples was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines of Amsterdam UMC Research Code, based on the Dutch “Code of conduct Health Research.” All patients provided written informed consent for the use of retrospective clinical data and tissue samples for research purposes, unless deceased at time of screening.

Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) or plastic‐embedded bone marrow trephine biopsies and stained sections were obtained. Previously performed histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) stainings were reviewed by two hematopathologists (LK, STP). If insufficient for accurate pathological classification, additional stainings were performed. IHC stainings contained at least CD20, CD79a, CD138, kappa, lambda, IgM, and IgG, where permitted by the fixation method. In addition, results of prior immunophenotyping by flow cytometry were reviewed, as well as B‐cell receptor (BCR) clonality by IGH gene rearrangement [22], and mutational testing by PCR‐based methods or Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) on FFPE trephine biopsies. Over time, three different targeted NGS panels were applied (Supporting Information). Genes of interest were MYD88, CXCR4, KMT2D (MLL2), and CARD11.

Clonality was defined by the presence of at least one of the following: monoclonal gammopathy, aberrant marker expression in IHC by B‐cells (CD5, CD10, BCL6, cyclin D1, or CD23) and/or plasma cells (CD56, cyclin D1, light chain or heavy chain monotypia), BCR clonality, monoclonal B‐cells and/or plasma cells in flow cytometry, or recurrent gene mutations. Based on reassessment of all results, a pathological diagnosis was rendered in accordance with WHO‐HAEM5. A CAD‐LPD diagnosis was made only when histopathological findings fully met the criteria outlined for CAD‐LPD in WHO‐HAEM5 (Table 1) [21].

A final diagnosis was established in consensus between the clinical investigators (ALB, JMIV) and pathologists (LK, STP). Cases were classified as CAD if they met all essential WHO‐HAEM5 criteria (Table 1) [21]. Cases with overt lymphoma were classified as CAS. For the purpose of this study, overt lymphoma was defined as malignant disease upon bone marrow revision, matching another WHO entity (except IgM MGUS, given its histopathological overlap with CAD), or an extramedullary lymphoma. Subsequently, the proportion of CAD patients meeting the desirable WHO‐HAEM5 criteria was assessed, including the description of CAD‐LPD. Final diagnoses were subdivided into: “CAD with CAD‐LPD,” “CAD with unclassifiable clonal B‐cell and/or plasmacytosis in the bone marrow,” “CAD with IgM paraproteinemia but normal/reactive bone marrow,” “CAS secondary to […],” or “no CAD or CAS.”

A total of 97 patients were identified who met the initial screening criteria (Figure 1). Of those, 14 (14%) were excluded as cold agglutinins occurred secondary to an infection or autoimmune disorder (n = 8), or no bone marrow biopsy was performed (n = 6). In the 83 patients included, cold agglutinins were detected with a 4°C titer of ≥ 64 in 36 (43%). In the remaining 48 (58%), the presence of cold agglutinins was proven with a positive direct agglutination test at 16°C. One or more bone marrow samples were available for revision in our tissue archive in 56 out of 83 patients (67%), all of which were included in the current analysis.

Of the 56 patients with bone marrow biopsies available for revision, 39 (70%) were female, and mean age (±SD) at time of cAIHA diagnosis was 63 ± 10 years. In 47 patients (84%), a paraproteinemia was detected in peripheral blood. In seven patients (13%), no paraprotein was found, while electrophoresis was not performed in 2 (4%). The vast majority of M‐proteins (46/47; 98%) were IgM isotype. Most were IgMκ only (39/47; 83%). The remaining had both IgMκ and IgGλ (n = 3), IgMκ and IgMλ (n = 1), IgMλ and IgGλ (n = 1), IgMλ and IgGκ (n = 1), IgMλ only (n = 1), and IgGκ only (n = 1).

Initial underlying disease diagnoses before study revision were: unclassifiable clonal B‐cell and/or plasmacytosis in the bone marrow (n = 22; 39%), monoclonal gammopathy without bone marrow aberrancies (n = 9; 16%), LPL (n = 7; 13%), CLL/SLL (n = 5; 9%), no monoclonal gammopathy or bone marrow abnormalities (n = 4; 7%), MZL (n = 4; 7%), and other (n = 3; 5%; myelodysplastic syndrome, Hodgkin lymphoma, and diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma). In two cases (4%), no prior diagnosis was made, as the initial bone marrow biopsy was conducted at our center after the implementation of WHO‐HAEM5.

Radiological imaging, performed in 54 cases (96%), showed evidence of organ enlargement in 14 patients (26%), comprising lymphadenopathy (n = 9), splenomegaly (n = 6), and/or hepatomegaly (n = 3). Malignant extramedullary disease was pathologically proven in only four patients (7%), comprising lymph node involvement of Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 1), LPL (n = 1), and large B‐cell lymphomas (n = 2; high‐grade transformation of prior MZL and WM).

In total, 85 bone marrow biopsies of 56 patients were reviewed, with a range of 1–6 samples per patient. For patients with multiple bone marrow samples available (n = 18), the most representative biopsy was selected for the descriptive analysis of histopathological characteristics. Marked hypercellularity of the marrow was observed in 43 patients (77%). A reduced myeloid‐to‐erythroid ratio (< 1.5) was identified in 47 patients (84%), with a marked left shift of erythroid precursors in 51 (91%).

A CD20‐positive B‐cell infiltrate was identified in 44 patients (79%), with a median infiltration rate of 4.5%, varying from 1% to 50%. Among these, 36 (82%) showed nodular aggregates, whereas 8 (18%) exhibited diffuse marrow infiltration only. Of the 36 cases with nodular infiltrates, 18 (50%) demonstrated paratrabecular orientation, while the remaining 18 (50%) showed infiltration of the intertrabecular surface only. B‐cells expressed CD79a and PAX5 in all evaluable cases (32/32 and 5/5, respectively). CD5 expression was observed in 13 of 28 (46%) and CD23 in 4 of 22 (18%) tested cases. CD10 was negative in all 11 tested cases, and BCL6 and cyclin D1 were negative in all but one (1/17 and 1/26, respectively). Based on histopathological examination, an aberrant plasma cell infiltration was found in the marrow of the majority (51/56; 91%). In these cases, the median plasma cell infiltration rate was 1%, ranging from 0.5% to 8%. Plasma cells were observed scattered throughout the marrow (n = 41), in a perivascular distribution (n = 16), surrounding the B‐cell aggregates (n = 10), and/or within the aggregates (n = 2). Immunohistochemical staining for heavy chains in 35 patients showed monotypia in 21 (60%), which was in favor of IgM in 18 (86%). Light chain immunohistochemical staining was done in 47 patients, showing light chain restriction in 15 (32%), of which 13 (87%) were kappa and 2 (13%) were lambda monotypic. Plasma cells were cyclin D1‐positive in 1 of 27 tested cases (4%), and MUM1 positive in 1/1 (100%). In 1 of 20 cases tested (5%), plasma cells were CD56‐positive. Evaluation of lymphoplasmacytic morphology was complicated by differences in biopsy handling and fixation but was observed in nine cases (16%). In 37/51 (73%) tested cases, an increase in CD3+ T‐lymphocytes was found. Mast cell infiltration (2/41; 5%) and grade 2 fibrosis (1/43; 2%) were rare.

DNA isolation for mutational analyses was successfully performed in 41 cases (73%), of which most (36/41; 88%) were analyzed by NGS. Two of 41 (5%) tested cases harbored the MYD88 L265P mutation. One of these represented only 0.6% of the sequencing reads, while IHC showed a B‐cell infiltrate of 6%, raising uncertainty as to whether this mutation was part of the neoplastic clone, possibly reflecting background noise. The CXCR4 gene, tested in 34 cases, was mutated in one patient (3%) without mutated MYD88. MLL2 (KMT2D) missense mutations were found in 2/18 (11%) tested patients and CARD11 missense mutations in 3/32 (9%). In two patients (4%), a pathogenic TP53 gene mutation was found. Of the cases with normal IHC, 3 of 16 tested (19%) had lymphoid mutations, as evidence of clonality. Additional variants of unknown significance (VUS), detected in 5 patients, are described in Table S1.

When integrating all results of IHC, BCR testing, flow cytometry, and mutational analyses—irrespective of monoclonal gammopathy status—bone marrow clonality was demonstrated in 47/56 cases (84%) as opposed to 33/56 (59%) by IHC alone. Taking serum electrophoresis results into account as well, clonality was detected in 53/56 (95%; Table 2). In the three patients without detected clonality, one lacked M‐protein testing, two lacked flow cytometry of B cells, and all three lacked flow cytometry of plasma cells, BCR testing, and mutational analyses.

Pathology revision according to WHO‐HAEM5 led to considerable changes in diagnoses. Revision classified the underlying bone marrow disease as well‐defined B‐cell malignancies in seven patients (13%). These included CLL/SLL (n = 5), LPL (n = 1), and a large B‐cell lymphoma (n = 1). The CLL/SLL cases all had mature B cells co‐expressing CD5 and CD23 with or without LEF1 expression. The LPL case showed kappa‐monotypic lymphoplasmacytic cells and harbored the MYD88 L265p mutation. In addition, there were three cases (5%) of extramedullary lymphomas without bone marrow involvement; one LPL, one Hodgkin lymphoma (without signs of B‐cell clonality in the bone marrow), and one MZL that transformed to a large B‐cell lymphoma. Consequently, all 10 of these cases (18%) were classified as CAS. When excluding these 10 CAS cases, 46 remained (Figure 2). Of those 46, 45 (98%) had a monoclonal gammopathy in peripheral blood (n = 39) and/or clonality in bone marrow by IHC, BCR clonality testing, flow cytometry, and mutational analyses (n = 40). No clonality could be detected in 1/46 patient, thereby excluding this case from fulfilling the essential criteria for CAD. The 45 remaining patients met all essential WHO‐HAEM5 CAD criteria.

A strict diagnosis of CAD‐LPD according to WHO‐HAEM5 (Table 1) was confirmed in only 5 out of 45 CAD cases (11%). All five had IgM kappa monoclonal gammopathy and 4/4 tested lacked the MYD88 exon 5 L265P mutation. The bone marrow of the remaining 40 cases did not meet CAD‐LPD criteria due to one or more of the following: no monotypic plasma cells in IHC (n = 23), lack of B‐cell infiltrate (n = 12), paratrabecular growth (n = 11), distribution of plasma cells (n = 9), lack of plasma cells (n = 6), diffuse instead of nodular B‐cell aggregates (n = 5), B‐cell immunophenotype (n = 4), lymphoplasmacytic maturation (n = 1), and/or increased mast cells (n = 1) (Figure 3). However, 34/40 (85%) had a monoclonal gammopathy in peripheral blood, 35/40 (88%) had evidence of B‐cell and/or plasma cell clonality in the bone marrow, and 29/29 (100%) tested were MYD88 wild‐type.

Of 18 patients (32%), more than one bone marrow sample was available for revision. Repeated bone marrow assessments often revealed variability in both the size and detectability of the marrow infiltrate. For example, one case met criteria for CAD‐LPD in only one out of six biopsies. Bone marrow examination of the remaining samples revealed normal/reactive marrow, limited nonaberrant B‐cell infiltrate, and unclassifiable clonal B‐cell disorder with uncertain monotypic plasma cell maturation. In another case with an unclassifiable clonal B‐cell disorder, initial BCR testing yielded no conclusively interpretable results. However, subsequent testing revealed a clonal IgH rearrangement with retrospective identification of the clone in the prior biopsy.

When excluding cases classified as CAS (n = 10) and/or without signs of clonality (n = 3), 45 patients (80%) remained that fulfilled all essential CAD criteria (Figure 2). While the proportion of those meeting the desirable CAD‐LPD criterium was low (5/45; 11%), the number of patients meeting the other desirable WHO‐HAEM5 criteria was high: 39/44 (89%) tested had a monoclonal gammopathy in plasma/serum (of which 38 were IgM kappa), 11/15 (73%) tested had an abnormal B‐cell kappa‐to‐lambda ratio by flow cytometry on bone marrow aspirate, and 33/33 (100%) were MYD88 wild‐type. The current study assessed the clinical and histopathological features of 56 cAIHA patients, in light of the new WHO criteria for CAD and CAD‐LPD [21]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study to systematically assess the lymphoproliferation underlying cAIHA and the first to do so within the framework of WHO‐HAEM5. Our results show that the majority of cAIHA cases (80%) meet the essential CAD criteria, including absence of an evident underlying malignancy but presence of a clonal B‐cell disorder, evidenced by a paraproteinemia or a B‐cell and/or plasma cell clone in the bone marrow. In addition, all cases classifying as CAD were MYD88 wild‐type, which further substantiates its status as an entity distinct from LPL. However, the histopathological lesions exhibited a heterogeneous pattern of mature B‐cell and/or plasma cell infiltration. Interestingly, CAD‐LPD, as defined as desirable criterium by WHO‐HAEM5, was identified in only a minority of CAD cases, whereas most exhibited an unclassifiable B‐cell clone. In addition, standard bone marrow histopathology alone demonstrated a lymphoproliferative disorder in a moderate proportion (59%), due to small clone size. Using more sensitive techniques, presence of clonal disease could be established in 84%. Consequently, most CAD cases default to a classification as CAD with an unclassifiable clonal B‐cell or plasma cell disorder, bringing it into the spectrum of monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance [23, 24].

The Norwegian study by Randen et al., which served as the basis for WHO‐HAEM5, reported a small underlying lymphoproliferative disorder with homogeneous histology in 54 CAD patients without signs of extramedullary lymphoma [18]. The notably lower number of cases meeting this homogeneous profile in our study, compared to Randen et al., was attributable to two factors. First, the B‐cell or plasma cell clone was often undetectably small in our cAIHA cohort, making classification impossible. We observed substantially lower median B‐cell and plasma cell bone marrow infiltration rates compared to those reported by Randen et al. (5% and 1% vs. 10% and 5%, respectively). Second, those with visible histopathological aberrancies showed—often subtle—differences from the WHO‐HAEM5 description, including paratrabecular instead of intertrabecular B cell orientation, absence of monotypic plasma cells in IHC, or lack of a B‐cell infiltrate. These discrepancies may be explained by the broad inclusion criteria used in our study, representing all symptomatic cAIHA cases with an available bone marrow biopsy. The more stringent selection criteria in the Norwegian study may have resulted in a selection bias, as only well‐documented primary CAD cases were included, potentially missing previously incorrectly classified CAD cases with small B‐cell clones. Our study additionally demonstrated lower prevalences of somatic mutations in KMT2D and CARD11 than Malecka et al. [20]. In addition to the differences in selection and a smaller median clone size, this could be related to a lower sensitivity of NGS on FFPE samples as opposed to NGS on cell‐sorted aspirate samples.

Importantly, our data convey some significant messages for the diagnostic workup of cAIHA. We confirmed that the occurrence of symptomatic cold agglutinins in chronic cAIHA in the absence of an underlying clonal lymphoproliferation is highly uncommon, in contrast to the often idiopathic nature of warm antibody‐mediated AIHA. This indicates that performing M‐protein testing and bone marrow biopsies in patients with chronic cAIHA provide a high diagnostic yield [6]. Our findings do indicate that the CAD‐associated bone marrow clone is usually (very) small. This is underscored by the fact that aberrant marker expression (i.e., monotypia of plasma cells or CD5‐expression in B‐cells) could not be detected by standard histopathological evaluation in nearly half (41%) of our cohort. In those cases, clonality had to be revealed by more sensitive techniques, such as BCR sequencing, flow cytometry, or NGS prompting their use when examining bone marrow in cAIHA patients. Additionally, our data demonstrate that repeating bone marrow examinations can be valuable as well, since a diagnostically elusive clone at first presentation—although rarely progressing to high‐grade lymphoma or showing transformation—may become apparent at a later stage [3]. These conclusions should be interpreted in light of potential referral bias and heterogeneity in ancillary testing within our study cohort. In addition, while the criterium of a cold agglutinin titer > 64 at 4°C remains subject to debate—and the presence of cold‐reactive antibodies was confirmed by other methods in all patients—the omission of this internationally recognized standard should be taken into consideration as well [25, 26].

In clinical practice, the subtle histopathological differences between the bone marrow disease underlying CAD and other B‐NHL, along with the challenging detection of the clone, may lead to underrecognition and misclassification of cAIHA cases. This may lead to omission of CAD‐specific therapeutic interventions. The latter has become more relevant with the recent introduction of complement inhibitor sutimlimab, the first therapy approved specifically for CAD [14, 15]. Pathologists should be aware that the lymphoproliferation underlying CAD may represent a very small clone and is less homogeneous than previously suggested, rather than relying too rigidly on the WHO‐HAEM5 CAD‐LPD criterium. For clinicians evaluating cAIHA cases, it is crucial to take an integral approach, incorporating clinical data as well as a platform of sensitive clonality detection techniques.

In summary, our findings confirm CAD to present as a cold antibody‐induced AIHA with evidence of a MYD88 negative, typically small clonal B‐ and/or plasma cell disease in the bone marrow. Our data underscore the importance of a comprehensive and sensitive approach when evaluating bone marrow samples in patients with suspected CAD. This entity scarcely occurs in the absence of an underlying lymphoproliferation; however, this clone is often too small to be detected by standard histopathology. In our CAD cohort, the WHO‐HAME5 criteria did not fit a substantial proportion of cases due to lack of concordance with the desirable CAD‐LPD definition.
