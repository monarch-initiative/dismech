# Mechanism of female CHH caused by compound heterozygous mutations in the LHB gene.
**Authors:** Ma H, Li C, Gao J, Wu W, Sun Z, Wang X, Nie M, Wu X, Mao J, Han Q
**Journal:** J Assist Reprod Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10815-024-03307-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10815-024-03307-2)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Luteinizing hormone (LH) plays a crucial role in the postnatal development and maturation of gonads. Inactivating mutations of the luteinizing hormone beta subunit (LHB)gene are extremely rare and can result in congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CHH).

METHODS: We conducted DNA sequencing on an 18-year-old female patient with undetectable LH and clinical symptoms of CHH. Pulsatile GnRH was administered to promote puberty development. In vitro construction of mutant genes, confocal microscopy, and protein functional assays were used to investigate the effects of genetic variants on hormone function and secretion. Experiments were conducted in HEK293T cells to examine the colocalization and dimerization of LH subunits, as well as to measure intracellular and extracellular LH concentrations.

RESULTS: Compound heterozygous mutations of c.252C>G (p.F84L) and c.364G>A (p.G122S) were found in the patient's genome. Pulsatile GnRH therapy was effective in promoting puberty development and ovulation. The LH alpha subunit was found to co-localize with both mutant beta subunits after immunofluorescence staining, and immunoprecipitation detected the dimerization of the LH alpha subunit with both mutant beta subunits. Higher intracellular LH concentrations and lower extracellular LH concentrations compared to the wild type indicate secretion dysfunction for LH.

CONCLUSION: Compound heterozygous mutations of c.252C>G (p.F84L) and c.364G>A (p.G122S) in the LHB gene may lead to CHH for female patient. These mutations do not impair the expression and dimerization of the alpha and beta subunits, but they do prevent the secretion of LH. The study expands our understanding of the clinical manifestation of LHB gene mutations in females and provides a treatment for these patients.

Keywords: Female infertility, Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, Luteinizing hormone, Luteinizing hormone beta subunit, Selective luteinizing hormone deficiency

Congenital gonadotrophic hypogonadism (CHH) is a condition characterized by impaired synthesis and secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in the hypothalamus, leading to reduced secretion of gonadotropins by the pituitary gland and subsequent sexual dysfunction. CHH primarily affects males, and can result in delayed or absent puberty and infertility [1]. Luteinizing hormone (LH) is a gonadotropin hormone secreted by the anterior pituitary gland upon stimulation by GnRH. Luteinizing hormone (LH) plays a crucial role in the reproductive system of females, and abnormalities in its levels can lead to a wide range of conditions. An insufficient LH level can cause luteal insufficiency, which results in a shorter and less effective luteal phase and can result in difficulties in maintaining a pregnancy. Additionally, low levels of LH can cause anovulation, where the ovaries do not release an egg, making it difficult for women to conceive. LH is also important for the maturation of oocytes, and premature maturation can result in reduced fertility. PCOS is a common condition associated with abnormalities in LH levels, where the ovaries produce too much androgen and follicles fail to develop and mature properly, leading to irregular menstrual cycles and difficulties in conceiving. Finally, LH abnormalities have been linked to recurrent miscarriage and infertility [2–5].

LH is a heterodimeric glycosylated protein that comprises two subunits: the α subunit and the β subunit. The α subunit is encoded by the CGA gene, which is shared with chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and human and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). On the other hand, the beta subunit is specific to LH and is encoded by the LHB gene, located on chromosome 19q13.3 [6]. Pathogenic mutations in the LHB gene can cause CHH by affecting LH synthesis, secretion, or dimerization, and can include homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations [7–11]. Genetic tests may be used to diagnose selective and isolated LH deficiency, a rare form of CHH [7].

This study aims to investigate the pathophysiological consequences of LHB gene mutations on LH function in patients with CHH, commencing with a female patient harboring compound heterozygous LHB gene mutations. The investigation intends to employ a comprehensive approach to elucidate the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying the observed impact of LHB mutations on LH secretion, signaling, and reproductive outcomes. Through an in-depth analysis of these mechanisms, this study endeavors to provide new insights into the pathogenesis of CHH and potentially facilitate the development of novel therapeutic strategies for CHH and related reproductive disorders.

In October 2021, an 18-year-old female patient was admitted to our medical center with a diagnosis of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. The patient presented with a height of 171 cm, weight of 48 kg, arm span of 171 cm, Tanner stage B1 breast development, and sparse pubic and axillary hair. Notably, the patient exhibited a small uterus volume (1.21*1.0 * 0.9 cm). Serum levels of FSH, estradiol, and LH were low, while those of IGF-I, cortisol, and TSH were normal. Whole-exome sequencing of the patient’s peripheral blood revealed the presence of compound heterozygous mutations in the LHB gene. Specifically, the proband’s exon 3 had two mutations: C252G, inherited from her mother, and G364A, possibly inherited from her deceased father (whose blood sample could not be available (Fig. 1)). In addition, the patient lacked spontaneous puberty and menarche, which are notable deviations from previous reports of female patients with LHB gene mutations [9, 12, 13]. Therefore, we conducted an investigation into the underlying mechanisms of this phenomenon.

Serum levels of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), testosterone (T), testosterone, prolactin, progesterone (P), and estradiol were measured using chemiluminescent immunoassay kit (ACS:180 Automatic Chemiluminescence Systems, Bayer, Germany). The absence of LH was verified on two separate occasions by analyzing serum samples.

Gonadorelin (Fengyuan Pharmaceutical Co., Anhui Province, China), a synthetic form of gonadotropin-releasing hormone, was administered via subcutaneous injection in a pulsatile manner. The treatment began with a dose of 10 µg per 90 min using a compact infusion device (Weichuang Medical Science Co., Shanghai, China). The levels of luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and estradiol were regularly monitored at intervals of 3, 7, and 15, days, and 1, 3, 4, and 6 months, respectively.

To create a fusion protein of the alpha subunit and His epitope, the complementary DNA (cDNA) of the CGA gene was cloned into the pcDNA3.1( +)-MCS-6xHis vector. An anti-His antibody was employed to verify the alpha subunit expression. Then, two mutations carried by the patient were introduced separately into LHB to generate two mutant LH β subunit expression vectors. To create fusion proteins of the beta subunit and Flag epitope, cDNA of the wild type, variants LHB1 and LHB2 were separately inserted into the pcDNA3.1( +)-MCS-3xFLAG vector (OBiO Technology (Shanghai) Corp., Ltd, Shanghai, China)). The LH β subunit expression in HEK293 cells was assessed by means of an anti-FLAG antibody.

All experiments in present investigation were performed using HEK293T cells. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% serum and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To perform transfection, the cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 6 × 105 cells per well and pre-incubated for 24 h before transfection. Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was employed for transfection. All transfection studies were conducted in three replicates and replicated more than three times to ensure accuracy and replicability. After 24 h of transfection, the cells were harvested and collected for further analysis.

To isolate total RNA, TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was used, followed by reverse transcription with the PrimeScript reagent kit (TaKaRa, China) to perform cDNA synthesis. Real-time PCR analysis was conducted employing a SYBR Green-based assay on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 instrument. Denaturation and polymerase activation steps involved an initial denaturation step at 95 ℃ for 40 s, followed by 40 cycles of amplification consisting of a denaturation step at 95 ℃ for 10 s and an annealing/extension step at 60 ℃ for 30 s. To determine gene expression levels, GAPDH was used as a normalization reference to account for variations in RNA input and reverse transcription efficiency, and the 2-ΔΔCT approach was used for calculation. After transfecting HEK293T cells for 24 h, the medium was removed, and the cells were lysed using a mixture of protease inhibitor and RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime). Proteins were then separated using a 15% gradient SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h and then subjected to overnight primary antibody treatment at 4 °C. Following a 25-min wash with TBST, the membrane was exposed to secondary antibodies for a duration of 1 h at room temperature. A list of all the antibodies used in this study is provided in Table 2. The resulting blots were visualized using ECL solution and quantified for protein expression using ImageJ software.

List of antibodies for immunostaining, immunoblotting and co-immunoprecipitation

The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS for 10 min, and subsequently blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min. Primary antibodies against the Flag and His tags (Sigma #F1804 and Cell Signaling #12,698, respectively) were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing, secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher #A-11078 for Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and #33,160 for Rhodamine-conjugated sheep anti-mouse IgG) were added and incubated at room temperature for 50 min. The cells were stained with DAPI (#C0060, Solarbio) for 10 min to visualize the nuclei. Finally, the cells were washed with PBS and observed under a confocal microscope (Olympus).

The immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay was performed using the Pierce Crosslink Magnetic IP/Co-IP kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). After transfecting cells for 24 h, cells were lysed using IP lysis/wash buffer (25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 5% glycerol), and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. The anti-His monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) was crosslinked with protein A/G magnetic beads and incubated with the cell lysate at 4 °C overnight. The beads were washed with ultrapure water, and the antibody-bound antigen was collected and denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. Finally, the obtained protein was analyzed by western blotting using anti-His (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA) and anti-Flag (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA) antibodies to detect the target protein.

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software for statistical analysis. The level of statistical significance and corresponding P-values were reported in the respective figures.

Over a period of 6 months, the patient’s breast development progressed from Tanner stage 2 to stage 4 during pulsatile GnRH therapy (10 µg/90 min). Furthermore, the patient experienced menarche during the third month of treatment, exhibiting a normal menstrual flow, and subsequently had a second menstrual period in the fifth month of intervention. Concurrently, serum levels of FSH, LH, and estradiol increased during pulse GnRH therapy and ultimately reached the normal adult range (Table 3, Fig. 2A, B). Ultrasound showed that the uterine volume remarkably increased from 1.2 × 1.1 × 0.9 to 4.1 × 3.2 × 2.4 cm. Analysis of Table 3 reveals that serum LH and FSH levels decreased to their pre-treatment levels after 1 month of treatment cessation, and subsequently increased again upon GnRH treatment resumption.

At 24 h post-transfection, proteins were harvested and the expression of LHβ subunit was evaluated by means of western blotting. The findings demonstrated that the sizes of the beta subunit proteins encoded by the two mutant genes are the same as those of the wild type (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the intracellular concentration of the two mutant proteins was observed to be higher than that of the wild-type gene.

Following a 24-h period post-plasmid transfection, the culture medium was collected and the concentration of LH was quantified. The results revealed a decreased extracellular concentration of LH in cells transfected with the two mutant genes relative to those transfected with the wild-type LHB gene (Fig. 3C).

Following transfection of HEK293T cells with CGA + LHB, CGA + LHB1, and CGA + LHB2 plasmids respectively, the cells were cultured for 24 h before being subjected to immunofluorescence staining and subsequent confocal microscopy analysis. The results demonstrated that alpha subunit and beta subunit were co-localized in all three experimental groups (Fig. 4A).

We performed co-immunoprecipitation using anti-His and anti-Flag antibodies to detect the His-tagged α subunit and Flag-tagged β subunit, respectively. Our results showed the formation of α-β heterodimers in both wild-type and mutant cells (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that functional α-β heterodimers were successfully formed in HEK293 cells co-transfected with plasmids containing His-tagged α subunit and Flag-tagged β subunit (wild-type or mutant).

MutationTaster predicts that the two mutations are unlikely to be pathogenic (Supplemental Table 1 and 2). The AAchanges scores in the table assess the biological impact of amino acid substitutions. These scores are derived from the Grantham matrix, which evaluates the physicochemical properties of amino acids and ranges from 0 to 215. A higher score indicates a greater difference between the original and new amino acids, suggesting a potentially more significant impact on protein function. The position of the mutation is shown in Supplemental Fig. 1 . In the 3D structure analysis of the protein, predictions made using SwissModel and PyMOL indicate that for LHB NM_000894 exon 3 c.252C > G (p.F84L), the substitution of phenylalanine at position 84 with leucine alters the protein’s 3D structure (Supplemental Fig. 2A, B, C, D). For LHB NM_000894 exon 3 c.364G > A (p.G122S), the substitution of glycine at position 122 with serine introduces a hydrogen bond, which affects the protein’s 3D structure (Supplemental Fig. 2E, F, G, H). The predictions for the secondary structure and glycosylation are shown in Supplemental Fig. 3. Both the wild-type and mutant forms have no glycosylation sites (Supplemental Fig. 3D, E and F). This indicates that glycosylation may not be the primary post-translational modification for this protein. The secondary structure of the LHB protein predominantly consists of strands, helices, and coils. While the c.252C > G mutation alters the overall secondary structure of the protein, the c.364G > A mutation does not.

We report a female patient with congenital gonadotrophic hypogonadism caused by compound heterozygous mutations in the LHB gene, c.252C > G (p.F84L) and c.364G > A (p.G122S), presenting clinically with delayed puberty and primary amenorrhea. The mutation of the LHB gene represents an autosomal recessive hereditary condition that has been definitively linked to the development of CHH in males [14]. However, the causal relationship between LHB gene mutations and female CHH has not been well established yet. These two mutations have been previously reported in association with infertility [14, 15]. However, the precise mechanisms by which these mutations impact LH function have yet to be fully elucidated.

In this study, western blotting analysis demonstrated that the mutated genes synthesized LH beta subunit of similar size to the wild type. Immunofluorescence experiments revealed co-localization of alpha and beta subunits in 293 T cells transfected with the mutated genes, while immunoprecipitation analysis confirmed mutated LH beta subunit dimerization with the alpha subunit. However, measurement of intracellular and extracellular LH concentrations showed that mutated LH secretion was less efficient than the wild type, suggesting impaired secretion due to defects in LH heterodimer trafficking or processing caused by the mutations or altered interactions with other cellular components required for secretion. Therefore, this study provided strong evidence that these LHB mutants are responsible for pathogenesis of CHH in this patient. Notably, the patient carrying both mutations responded well to pulse GnRH treatment, with increased LH synthesis and normal pubertal development, indicating the mutated LH retained the ability to bind to and activate the LH receptor. Nonetheless, additional research is required to elucidate the mechanisms by which these mutations impact LH secretion function. Silico experiments indicate that MutationTaster predicts the mutations are unlikely to be pathogenic. However, we find this prediction insufficient for our study because it lacks detailed information about the variants, such as mutation type, inheritance pattern, ethnicity, affected status, or allele source. Both mutations may lead to changes in the protein’s 3D structure. Neither the wild-type nor the mutant forms possess glycosylation sites. The first documented mutation in humans that resulted in the loss of LHB gene function, homozygous p.Q54R, was initially detected in a male patient with isolated LH deficiency [7]. This patient had abnormally high levels of LH. While the p.Q54R mutant did not impair LH dimerization or immunoreactivity, it did disrupt the binding activity of LH to its receptor. Another mutant, W28X, also led to an abnormal LH increment [16]. In contrast, other LHB mutations typically resulted in undetectable levels of LH due to impaired LH synthesis. These mutations include 28-39del [10], 10-12del [17], G36D [11], L72R [18], 118-120del [19], IVS2 + 1G → C [20], and IVS2 + 1G → T [10] (Table 4).

Patients carrying inactivating mutations of the LH beta-subunit gene typically exhibit similar clinical features, despite variations in the type of genotypic abnormalities (refer to Table 4). In male patients, normal sexual differentiation takes place, but the absence of LH can hinder puberty induction, inhibit Leydig cell maturation, and impair spermatogenesis, which relies on increased intratesticular testosterone levels. Affected men often experience infertility and marked reduction in spermatogenesis, which may persist despite long-term gonadotropin therapy.

To date, two women with an inactivating LH beta-subunit mutation have been described, both of whom experienced normal pubertal development and menarche at age 14, followed by oligomenorrhea and secondary amenorrhea [9]. This study provides further clinical evidence of the phenotype caused by LHB gene mutations in females, and lays a groundwork for further investigations into the mechanisms of LH action in both sexes.

In addition, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) was typically used as a substitute therapy for LH (Table 4), which was effective in promoting gonadal development. In our patients, pulsatile GnRH successfully increase serum LH, FSH, and estradiol to the normal range (Table 3). Regular menstruation and breast enlargement occurred. Notably, when GnRH treatment was temporarily stopped after 1 month of treatment, the patient’s serum LH and FSH levels decreased to low limit. After resuming GnRH treatment, LH and FSH levels rose again. These findings suggest that GnRH treatment effectively promotes the synthesis and secretion of LH and FSH in this patient, which is beyond our expectation. Therefore, based on the results of this study, pulsatile GnRH therapy may provide additional treatment option for these patients. It is supposed that external pulsatile GnRH promote larger amount of LH synthesis and excretion, which overcome the deficiency cause by LHB mutants.

In conclusion, we found a compound heterozygous nonsense LHB mutation in a female patient with CHH and provided evidence that these two mutants impair LH secretion, resulting in isolated LH deficiency. This study found that pulsatile GnRH therapy may succeed in restoring LH secretion and inducing ovulation, which provide therapeutic options for these patients.

An 18-year-old female patient with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism was admitted to our medical center, presenting with a lack of spontaneous puberty and menarche. These deviations from previous reports of female patients with LHB gene mutations prompted us to investigate the underlying mechanisms. The investigation revealed compound heterozygous mutations of c.252C > G (p.F84L) and c.364G > A (p.G122S) in the LHB gene, which may cause CHH in female patients. These mutations do not affect the expression and dimerization of the alpha and beta subunits but do inhibit the secretion of LH. This study enhances our understanding of the clinical manifestation of LHB gene mutations in females and offers potential treatment options for these patients.

Additional research is required to elucidate the mechanisms by which these mutations impact LH secretion function. Subcellular analysis was not performed to more precisely localize the intracellular accumulation of the LHB variant subunits. Further validation is also required to determine the activation potency of the mutant LH on the LH receptor.
