# Ganglioneuroblastoma intermixed: Clinicopathological implications of diagnosis at presentation and genomic correlations.
**Authors:** Nezami BG, Modak S, Cardenas FI, Sarungbam J, Sirintrapun SJ, Gopalan A, Chen Y, Al-Ahmadie H, Fine SW, Reuter VE, Tickoo SK
**Journal:** Pediatr Blood Cancer (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1002/pbc.30434](https://doi.org/10.1002/pbc.30434)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Ganglioneuroblastoma intermixed (GNBI) is classified as "favorable" histology by International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification system. However, the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) stratifies patients using wider clinicopathological and cytogenetic/molecular parameters. While the diagnosis of GNBI is typically made on resected tumor, it may sometimes be rendered on initial biopsy. We studied GNBI noted at diagnosis to evaluate its correlation with INRG staging and other clinicopathological and molecular features.

METHODS: In this retrospective study, clinical, radiological, pathological, cytogenetic, and molecular information from patients with GNBI at diagnosis seen between 1995 and 2021 was analyzed. INRG staging was performed.

RESULTS: Of the 15,827 neuroblastoma specimens, GNBI was noted in 237 patients. Of these, 53 had the initial pathological diagnosis of GNBI; median follow-up 3.5 (range: 0.2-14) years. Disease was locoregional in 41 (77%, 16 stage L1 and 25 L2); none relapsed. Twelve (23%) had metastatic disease at presentation; six (50%) relapsed, and two died of disease. MYCN was amplified in two metastatic tumors. Six of 31 (19%) tumors tested had recurrent cytogenetic abnormalities and nonrecurrent somatic gene mutations in 10/23 (43%). The presence of any adverse molecular/cytogenetic findings was associated with metastatic disease (p < .05). For patients with localized GNBI undergoing both biopsy and resection, GNBI was diagnosed in both in 17/19 (90%).

CONCLUSIONS: Localized GNBI at diagnosis has excellent long-term clinical outcome even without cytotoxic therapy. For localized GNBI, a biopsy sample is adequate to make the diagnosis. When associated with metastasis at diagnosis, prognosis is poorer, possibly due to associated adverse biological features.

Peripheral neuroblastic tumors (pNTs) are the most common extracranial solid tumors in childhood and are classified histopathologically into four main categories: neuroblastoma (NB), ganglioneuroblastoma-nodular (GNBN), ganglioneuroblastoma-intermixed (GNBI), and ganglioneuroma (GN). GNBI is rare in the primary setting, often occurring in older children as opposed to NB, which more often occurs in infants and neonates 1. It is considered a transitional state in which the tumor is on its way toward full differentiation from neuroblastoma to ganglioneuroma; this process being incomplete, as evidenced by scattered microscopic pockets of neuroblastoma. The proportion of the ganglioneuromatous component to “residual” neuroblastomatous foci exceeds 50% of the total volume in microscopic fields from representative tumor sections 2.

To risk-stratify pNTs and guide treatment, different classifications have been devised. International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC) incorporates age, histologic subtype and mitosis-karyorrhexis Index (MKI) and stratifies tumors as favorable or unfavorable histology with estimated 5-year event-free survivals of 90.4% and 26.9% respectively 3. The most recent International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) classification system introduced in 2009, includes stage, histologic classification, chromosomal status (ploidy and 11q aberration), genetics (MYCN amplification) and age of the patients 4.

According to INPC, GNBI is always a “favorable histology” tumor 2. Patients are often treated with tumor resection without administration of cytotoxic therapy 5,6. However, anecdotal reports of GNBI with aggressive behavior, inconsistent with the “favorable histology” have been published 1,2,7. There is limited information regarding the molecular features, clinical outcome and the behavior of primary GNBI particularly in the metastatic setting 1,5. In this study we studied the clinical course and molecular characteristics in patients with GNBI diagnosed at presentation.

After approval by the Institutional Review Board of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC), a retrospective search of the archives of the pathology department of MSKCC for peripheral neuroblastic tumors with a final report dated between 1995 and 2021 was undertaken. Tumors with the diagnosis of GNBI at presentation with pathological findings reviewed and confirmed at MSKCC were included. Chromosomal analysis for MYCN amplification, chromosome 1, 11 and 17 losses and gains were performed by Fluorescence In-situ Hybridization (FISH). DNA ploidy was tested using flow cytometry. Targeted somatic exome sequencing was performed using CLIA-certified next-generation sequencing platforms: either MSK-IMPACT or Foundation One NGS panel 8,9. Where available, diagnostic samples (obtained at biopsy or upfront resection) were compared histologically with samples obtained at definitive resection.

Demographic data, radiographic findings, treatment history and outcome were obtained from patient records. Patients were staged using INRG staging and stratified using INRG risk stratification.

Time to recurrence and time to death were defined as the time from initial diagnosis, until the time of first occurrence of relapse or death, respectively. Follow up time was determined by the last day of available clinical follow up or death or until the time of last contact if no event occurred. Chi-square test and Student’s t-test were used to analyze differences between groups, with p values < 0.05 being considered statistically significant.

Of a total of 15827 specimens from 2805 individual patients, GNBI was noted in 236 patients. Of these, GNBI was noted at initial diagnosis in 56 patients. Three patients were excluded due to lack of complete pathologic/clinical information, resulting in 53 patients being included in this study. Sixteen patients received initial diagnosis at MSKCC, the rest were diagnosed elsewhere before referral, though all histopathological slides were reviewed at MSKCC. Male to female ratio was 1:2 with median age at diagnosis of 53 months (range 14–280). The primary tumor was abdominal in 39 (74%) and thoracic in 14 patients (26%). The initial diagnosis was made on biopsy in 32 patients, and on upfront resection in 21. INRG stage was L1 in 16, L2 in 25 and M in 12 patients.

MYCN amplification was detected in 2/53 (4%) tumors, both metastatic at presentation. Other cytogenetic results were available for 31 patients with aberrations noted in 6 (19%): 1p loss and 11q loss was detected in 1 (with metastatic disease), 17q gain in 3 (2 locoregional and 1 metastatic), and complex (gain 1q, 11pq and 17q, loss 4p and 11q) in 1 patient with metastatic disease at diagnosis. Tumors were diploid in 65% (11/17 tested) and hyperdiploid in the remaining 35%. ALK mutations were seen in 2/30 patients tested. ATRX aberrations were absent in all 21 tumors tested. 43% (10/23 tested) tumors harbored at least one somatic mutation. In 3 patients with locoregional GNBI these included mutations in ALK, NUP93, RARA; and for 7 patients with metastatic disease (in addition to MYCN amplification): ALK, PHOX2B, ROS1, MDM2, and BRCA2 & NOTCH1. In total, 14 patients had at least one abnormal molecular finding (somatic mutations, cytogenetics abnormalities or MYCN amplification). (Table 1) Taken together, these were significantly more common in patients with metastatic disease (10/12, 77%) compared to those with locoregional disease (4/41, 10%, p < 0.0001).

Tumor resection was performed in 52/53 patients, including in all patients with stage M disease. Of the 41 patients with locoregional disease, 21 had definitive resection upfront, 1 had biopsy only, 8 had biopsy followed by surgery without any intervening anti-neuroblastoma therapy, and 11 had biopsy and neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgical resection. Samples from both initial biopsy and definitive resection were available for review in all 19 patients with locoregional disease. In 18/20 (90%) patients the definitive resection sample showed either GNBI or GN. In 1 patient (who did not receive any cytotoxic therapy) resection sample showed GNBN, and in another (who received low-dose neoadjuvant chemotherapy) resection sample showed differentiating NB with ganglioneuromatous elements. In the patient with GNBN noted at resection, there was a single 10mm discrete nodule noted in 1 of 4 submitted samples examined, whereas all other examined sections showed GNBI. Neither of the latter 2 patients relapsed. Of the 12 patients with metastatic disease, all of whom received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, surgery samples revealed GNBI in 4 (33%) and NB in 8 (67%). (Table 2)

All patients with metastatic disease were diagnosed at >18 months of age, were deemed to have high-risk NB, and were treated with multimodality therapy including high-dose chemotherapy, radiotherapy and anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody-mediated immunotherapy. Over a median follow up of 7.5 (range 4.1–14.3) years, 5 relapsed and 2 died of disease. Of the 41 patients with locoregional disease, 30 received no cytotoxic therapy and 11 received intermediate-risk neoadjuvant chemotherapy. All 41 remain alive progression-free at a median follow up of 4 years. 5-year PFS for locoregional and metastatic patients was 100% and 58% respectively, while 5-year OS was 100% for both groups, with the 2 stage M patients dying 6.3 and 6.8 years from diagnosis respectively. (Figure 1)

The diagnosis of GNBI is typically made on a diligent morphological examination of the entire resected tumor because distinguishing GNBI from GNBN is particularly challenging on limited samples. However, current treatment approaches for GNBI are not determined purely by INPC, and patients might be stratified based on radiological and biological findings as being intermediate risk resulting in the administration of neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Diagnosing GNBI on morphological examination of the definitively resected tumor does not take into account the impact of neoadjuvant chemotherapy that might be administered before definitive tumor resection. In this study, we examined the clinical impact of GNBI observed at diagnosis in patients who did or did not receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

In patients without metastatic disease at diagnosis, we observed that GNBI when diagnosed at initial presentation regardless of the size of the sample examined histopathologically (biopsy or resection), was associated with an excellent prognosis with a 100% PFS and OS. This excellent outcome was noted in the 21 patients who had a surgical resection upfront (with evaluation of the entire resected sample) and in the 20 patients in whom GNBI was observed in biopsy samples. In the latter, only 2 patients had revision of the diagnosis of GNBI when the entire resection sample was evaluated histologically. In one patient who did not receive any cytotoxic therapy between biopsy and resection, the diagnosis was changed to GNBN. In the other patient who received low dose chemotherapy between biopsy and resection, diagnosis was revised to NB with ganglioneuromatous differentiation. Interestingly, both patients are long-term progression free survivors. GNBI was noted both in L1 (n=15) and L2 (n=26) stages, suggesting that GNBI can occur in both invasive and less invasive tumors. Regardless of radiographic and biological factors, patients with locoregional disease had an excellent outcome. This is in keeping with previously reported observations for GNBI 1,2,5.

However, when GNBI at biopsy is associated with metastatic disease, the prognosis is inferior to those with localized disease despite administration of aggressive multimodality therapy. However, overall survival (85% at median 3.5 years) appeared to be better than that reported for metastatic NB patients in general 7, suggesting that a significant GNBI component in the main lesion may still be considered favorable histology even in a metastatic setting. Similar data have been reported by other investigators who have reported favorable outcomes in GNBI patients with metastatic disease 2,7,10.

We observed a higher rate of metastatic GNBI (26%) than previously reported by most investigators 2,7. This may in part be due to tertiary referral pattern of patients at our institution. Prior reports on GNBI have focused on resected tumors which were examined in their entirety. However, patients considered to have high or intermediate risk neuroblastoma per INRG criteria, that are used for stratification and treatment planning, often receive chemotherapy before resection and entire tumor samples are often unavailable for histopathological examination resulting a bias in the reported distribution of classically-diagnosed GNBI. In patients with stage M disease, GNBI noted at initial biopsy could not be corroborated in 67% samples examined at definitive resection. Thus, the definitive pathological biopsy diagnosis of GNBI in patients with concurrent metastatic disease requires high level of caution. At least a note in the pathology report indicating that the biopsy may not be truly representative of the entire tumor is highly recommended.

When taken together, molecular and segmental chromosomal aberrations were significantly less common in the locoregional group. In particular, MYCN amplification was not observed in any patient with locoregional GNBI. Again, this was expected since the reported rate of MYCN amplification in tumors with favorable histology is about 2%. 2,4,11. However, MYCN amplification was seen in 2/12 patients with metastatic GNBI indicating that the diagnoses of GNBI and MYCN amplification are not mutually exclusive. Segmental chromosomal aberrations such as gain of chromosome 17q, and deletion of chromosome 1p and 11q are often associated with high-risk NB. In our study, although data was available in only about 60% of our patients, in general, the incidence of such alterations was low (19%), though more frequent in patients with metastatic disease, again suggests the favorable biology. The pattern of genomic aberrations noted on exome sequencing also indicated a favorable biological phenotype. These included complete absence of ATRX mutations/deletions, which are associated with a poor prognosis, and the presence of ALK mutations in only 2 samples. Overall, the over-representation of molecular and segmental chromosomal aberrations in patients with metastatic GNBI suggests that these might have contributed to adverse biology and higher risk of relapse in patients with metastatic disease.

In summary, our study shows that GNBI at diagnosis can be encountered both in patients with locoregional and metastatic disease suggesting that clinical testing to detect metastatic disease is necessary even when the tumor is completely resected at diagnosis. The clinical behavior of GNBI may be influenced by the presence of adverse molecular and chromosomal aberrations. Overall, the prognosis for localized disease is excellent. Patients with metastatic GNBI should be considered high or intermediate risk and require multimodal therapy to effect favorable outcomes.
