BACKGROUND: Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) causes irreversible vision 
loss through ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury, characterized by oxidative 
stress and retinal ganglion cell (RGC) death. Current therapies are inadequate. 
This study investigates kynurenine (Kyn)—a tryptophan metabolite—as a novel 
therapeutic agent targeting dual pathways to combat retinal I/R injury.
METHODS: The unilateral pterygopalatine ophthalmic artery occlusion (UPOAO) 
model was established by inserting a silicone filament embolus into the 
pterygopalatine artery. The detection of tryptophan metabolites in serum and 
retinal tissues was carried out using metabolomics. In the UPOAO mouse model, 
kynurenine (Kyn) was administered via intravitreal injection. Simultaneously, 
R28 retinal cells were treated with Kyn during oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) 
induction to model ischemic injury in vitro. Electroretinography (ERG) was 
performed to examine the visual function of the mice. Western blot analysis and 
immunofluorescence were used to detect the apoptosis level of retinal ganglion 
cells (RGCs). The role of Kyn in activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) 
to reduce oxidative stress in RGCs was demonstrated by using an AhR inhibitor.
RESULTS: The neuroprotective effects of Kyn are mediated by a dual pathway: 
activation of the AhR-Nrf2 signaling axis, which transcriptionally upregulates 
antioxidant genes to mitigate oxidative stress, and modulation of downstream 
kynurenine pathway (KP) metabolites. While AhR-Nrf2 signaling provides 
transcriptional support for redox homeostasis, KP metabolites directly scavenge 
reactive oxygen species, synergistically counteracting oxidative damage and 
energy deficits in RIR injury.
CONCLUSIONS: Our findings highlight Kyn as a pathway-specific therapeutic 
candidate for CRAO, with potential for receptor-targeted and metabolic 
modulation strategies in treating retinal ischemia-reperfusion injuries.

Introduction
Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) is a devastating 
ophthalmic emergency that can rapidly progress to irre -
versible vision loss [ 1]. Although intra-arterial thrombol-
ysis may restore perfusion, it paradoxically exacerbates 
neuronal injury through ischemia-reperfusion (I/R)-
induced oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, 
and cell death in retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) [ 2–4]. 
However, no pharmacologic intervention has yet demon -
strated efficacy in preventing RGC demise.
Amino acid metabolic reprogramming emerges as 
a critical determinant of ischemia-reperfusion (I/R)-
induced tissue injury through dysregulated metabolic 
flux and redox-sensitive signaling cascades [ 5, 6]. Emerg-
ing evidence implicates dysregulated tryptophan (Trp) 
metabolism as a critical determinant of neuroretinal vul -
nerability in ischemic retinopathies [ 7, 8]. The kynuren -
ine pathway (KP), the dominant route of Trp catabolism 
(>95%), orchestrates neuroprotection and neurotoxic -
ity through a dual-edged metabolic network [ 9]. Key 
enzymes, including tryptophan-2,3-dioxygenase (TDO) 
and indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), convert Trp 
into kynurenine (Kyn) and downstream metabolites, such 
as kynurenic acid (KYNA) and 3-hydroxykynurenine 
(3HK) [10].
Kyn and its downstream metabolite, kynurenic acid 
(KYNA), serve as dual-action modulators of the aryl 
hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling axis, exhibiting 
context-dependent biological effects. KYNA, an anti-
inflammatory tryptophan metabolite, mitigates intestinal 
inflammation and experimental colitis by promoting reg -
ulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation [ 11]. This metabolic 
plasticity renders the KP a pivotal therapeutic target, 
yet its role in RGC survival during RIR injury remains 
underexplored.

In this study, we demonstrated that Kynurenine 
(Kyn) acts as a bifunctional neuroprotectant in reti -
nal ischemia-reperfusion (RIR) injury, mediating dual 
antioxidant and metabolic rescue pathways: (1) as an 
endogenous aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) agonist, 
Kyn activates AhR to transcriptionally upregulate anti -
oxidant genes (Nrf2, SOD1), restoring redox balance; and 
(2) its metabolic derivatives—including 3-hydroxykyn -
urenine (3HK), 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (HAA), and 
NAD⁺/NADH—enhance mitochondrial bioenergetics 
and suppress oxidative stress, thereby preserving retinal 
ganglion cell (RGC) viability.
Materials and methods
Human serum collection
Serum collection involved recruiting patients with 
central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) and cataract 
patients. The cataract patients served as controls for 
the non-CRAO group. This study adhered to the guide -
lines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved 
by the Institutional Review Board of Renmin Hospital 
of Wuhan University (Wuhan, China; approval number: 
WDRY2022-K278). As the study was retrospective and 
did not involve patient privacy information, the Ethics 
Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University 
waived the requirement for informed consent. Table  1 
lists the demographic and clinical characteristics of 
all patients. A 2 mL sample of whole blood was drawn, 
allowed to clot at room temperature, and then centri -
fuged for 10  min at 4  °C, 3000  g. The supernatant was 
collected and immediately transferred to liquid nitrogen. 
Targeted metabolomics analysis of the serum samples 
was performed by Shanghai ZKX Life Biotechnology Co. 
In brief, samples were prepared according to the protocol 
and subjected to HPLC-MS/MS analysis on an Agilent 
1290 Infinity UHPLC system. MultiQuant software was 
used for data quantification.
Animals
The experiment utilized 6-week-old male C57BL/6J mice 
weighing 20–22 g. The animals were housed in an envi -
ronment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and provided 
with adequate food and water. All animal experiments 
and procedures were conducted in accordance with the 
guidelines established by the Association for Research 
in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO). The study proto -
col and procedures were approved by the Experimental 
Animal Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital, Wuhan 
University (Approval No. WDRM-20231007 F). The mice 
were randomly assigned to experimental groups.
Unilateral pterygopalatine ophthalmic artery occlusion 
(UPOAO) model
As previously mentioned [ 12], the UPOAO model was 
introduced as the research model. Briefly, mice were 
anesthetized using a mixture of NO and O2 containing 
2% isoflurane. The mice were then placed in a supine 
position on a heating pad maintained at 37 °C. The neck 
area was shaved to fully expose the skin, followed by dis -
infection and surgical procedures. A midline incision was 
made on the neck, and the glands and surrounding soft 
tissues were bluntly dissected to locate the left common 
carotid artery (CCA), internal carotid artery (ICA), and 
external carotid artery (ECA). A 6 − 0 silk suture was 
used to ligate the proximal end of the CCA, the distal end 
of the ECA, and the distal end of the ICA. A loose knot 
was tied at the proximal end of the ECA. A “V”-shaped 
incision was made between the two ligations on the ECA, 
and a silicone filament embolus (length: 7 ± 0.1 mm, 
diameter: 0.21 ± 0.1 mm) was inserted through the inci -
sion. The embolus was advanced along the vessel into the 
pterygopalatine artery. To ensure the embolus success -
fully entered the pterygopalatine artery, the ECA was cut 
at the “V”-shaped incision after the embolus entered the 
CCA, and the ligation at the distal end of the ICA was 
released. The loose knot at the proximal end of the ECA 
was quickly tightened to secure the embolus. The skin 
was sutured, and the mice were monitored for recovery. 
Two hours later, the embolus was carefully removed, and 
the proximal end of the ECA was ligated. The ligation on 
the CCA was released to restore arterial perfusion, and 
the skin incision was closed. After recovery from anes -
thesia, the mice were routinely housed and cared for.
Preparation of Kynurenine solution and intravitreal 
injection
Kynurenine powder (10  mg, MCE, China) was weighed 
and dissolved in 1 mL of sterile PBS solution. After thor -
ough dissolution, the solution was filtered through a 
0.22 μm syringe filter and aliquoted for storage at -80 °C. 
For intravitreal injection, the solution was diluted to 
a concentration of 5  mg/mL. A volume of 1 µL of the 
Kynurenine solution or sterile PBS was injected into the 
vitreous body of both the UPOAO and control groups. 
Subsequent experiments were conducted 7 days after the 
injection.

Retinal histology and immunofluorescence
After euthanasia, the eyeballs were enucleated and 
immersed in eye fixation solution overnight. The eyes 
were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Hema -
toxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for 
retinal thickness analysis. The thickness of each retinal 
layer was measured at a fixed distance from the optic 
disc using ImageJ software. The number of cells in the 
ganglion cell layer was counted in at least six fields of 
view across three different sections. For immunofluores -
cence, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized 
and treated with PBS containing 5% bovine serum albu -
min (BSA) and 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room tem -
perature for blocking. The sections were then incubated 
with IDO1 antibody (1:50) at 4 °C overnight. After three 
washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with Alexa 
Fluor 488 (AntGene, Wuhan, China) in the dark for 1 h. 
Following three additional washes, the sections were 
mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium containing 
DAPI and coverslipped. The sections were observed and 
imaged using a confocal microscope. Fluorescence inten -
sity was quantified using ImageJ software, and data were 
analyzed accordingly.
Electroretinography (ERG)
Mice were dark-adapted for 12 h in a darkroom to ensure 
the retina reached maximum sensitivity. After anesthe -
sia, the mice were secured on a surgical platform, and the 
eyes were exposed. Mydriatic eye drops were applied to 
the corneal surface to dilate the pupils. Three minutes 
later, a topical anesthetic (proparacaine) was applied to 
the corneal surface. A ring electrode or gold foil elec -
trode was then placed on the cornea, with the reference 
electrode positioned on the scalp and the ground elec -
trode attached to the tail. Flash stimuli of varying intensi-
ties (e.g., 0.01–10 cd·s/m²) were delivered, and the a-wave 
and b-wave of the electroretinogram were recorded. The 
amplitude and latency of the a-wave and b-wave were 
measured for analysis.
Cell culture and OGD/R model
The R28 cell line was generously provided by Professor 
Xiaobo Xiao (Xiangya Hospital, Central South Univer -
sity, Changsha, China) [ 13, 14]. R28 cell is widely used 
in ophthalmic research, particularly in studies on reti -
nal degenerative diseases, neuroprotection, and regen -
eration. The cells were cultured in low glucose Dulbecco’s 
modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, CA, USA) sup-
plemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; BI, Israel) 
and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Servicebio, China) at 37 
°C in a humidified incubator with 95% O₂ and 5% CO₂ 
(v/v). For the oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygen -
ation (OGD/R) model, when cells reached approximately 
70% confluency, the medium was replaced with DMEM 
sugar-free medium (Gibco, CA, USA), and the cells were 
transferred to a tri-gas incubator (37 °C, 94% N₂, 5% CO₂, 
1% O₂) for 6 h. After OGD 6 h, the medium was replaced 
with complete DMEM, and the cells were returned to the 
normal incubator (37 °C, 95% O₂, 5% CO₂) for 24 h of 
reoxygenation. For drug treatment, selected compounds 
were added to the complete medium at the onset of reox-
ygenation (after 6 h of hypoxia) and incubated for 24 h. 
Drug concentrations were determined based on previous 
studies and CCK8 assay results.
CCK8 assay
R28 cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 10³ cells/well in 
a 96-well plate and cultured overnight. After OGD treat -
ment for 4  h, the cells were reoxygenated for 24  h in 
complete medium containing different concentrations of 
Kynurenine (Kyn). The medium was then replaced with 
basal medium containing 10% CCK8 reagent(Servicebio, 
China), and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark 
for 2  h. Absorbance at 450  nm was measured using a 
microplate reader. Control groups consisted of normally 
cultured cells without OGD/R treatment.
Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) staining
R28 cells were seeded at a density of 6 × 10⁵ cells/well in 
a 6-well plate. After reaching confluence and undergoing 
the specified treatments, the cells were washed once with 
PBS. A Calcein-AM/PI working solution was added, and 
the cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. 
The cells were immediately observed and imaged using 
an inverted fluorescence microscope. ImageJ software 
was used to process the images for subsequent statistical 
analysis.
RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and quantitative 
RT-qPCR
Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent 
(Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 5 min at room 
temperature, chloroform was added, and the mixture was 
vigorously shaken for 15 s, followed by 10 min of incuba -
tion. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min 
at 4 °C, and the aqueous phase was collected. Isopropanol 
was added to precipitate the RNA, and the samples were 
centrifuged again at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The RNA 
pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol and dissolved 
in RNase-free water. RNA quality and concentration 
were assessed using a spectrophotometer. Reverse tran -
scription was performed using a reverse transcription kit 
(Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruc-
tions to obtain cDNA. SYBR Premix (Vazyme, China) 
was used with the Biorad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System

Protein extraction and western blotting
After treatment, cells were placed on ice and washed 
twice with pre-chilled PBS. RAPI lysis buffer (Beyotime, 
China) was added, and the cells were lysed on ice for 5 
min. The lysate was collected into 1.5 mL microcentri -
fuge tubes and sonicated to ensure complete lysis. The 
samples were then centrifuged at 12, 000 g for 10 min 
at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. A 5× protein 
loading buffer (Yeasen, China) was added to the superna-
tant, mixed thoroughly, and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. 
The prepared protein samples were separated by sodium 
dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-
PAGE) at an appropriate concentration and transferred 
onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, Millipore, Bill -
erica, MA, USA) membrane. The membrane was blocked 
with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room tempera -
ture for 1 h. Primary antibodies (Table S2) were diluted 
according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incu -
bated with the membrane at 4 °C overnight. After five 
washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with an 
HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Abclone, 
China) at room temperature for 1 h. The membrane was 
washed five times with TBST, and protein bands were 
detected using a chemiluminescent substrate. Band 
intensities were quantified using ImageJ software for fur -
ther analysis [16].
TUNEL staining
TUNEL staining was performed according to the manu -
facturer’s instructions (Vazyme, China). For paraffin-
embedded sections, the sections were deparaffinized and 
permeabilized with proteinase K for 10  min, followed 
by three washes with PBS. The prepared TUNEL work -
ing solution was added to the sections and incubated at 
37 °C for 1 h. After three washes, the nuclei were coun -
terstained with DAPI. For cells, cells were seeded on 
confocal dishes and treated as required. After treatment, 
the cells were washed once with PBS, permeabilized 
with proteinase K for 5  min, and washed three times 
with PBS. The TUNEL working solution was added and 
incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After three washes, the nuclei 
were stained with DAPI. The samples were observed and 
imaged using a confocal laser microscope, and the rela -
tive number of TUNEL-positive cells was calculated.
ROS detection
Intracellular ROS levels in R28 cells were measured using 
an ROS assay kit. Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ 
cells/well in a 12-well plate and treated as required. 
Briefly, after treatment, the cells were collected and 
washed twice with PBS. A DCFH-DA probe-containing 
basal medium was added, and the cells were incubated at 
37 °C in the dark for 30 min. The cells were then washed 
three times with PBS to remove excess probe. ROS lev -
els were detected using a flow cytometer with an excita -
tion wavelength of 500 nm and an emission wavelength 
of 525 nm. For retinal ROS staining, frozen sections are 
fixed and then stained using a DHE (Dihydroethidium) 
fluorescent probe-based kit for rapid and sensitive detec -
tion of superoxide anion reactive oxygen species (ROS) in 
tissues. Under ROS-present conditions, the DHE probe is 
oxidized to form ethidium bromide, which binds to intra-
cellular nucleic acids and emits red fluorescence. The 
intensity of this red fluorescence is directly proportional 
to the ROS levels in the tissue, allowing indirect assess -
ment of intracellular ROS levels.
NADP/NADPH assay
NADP/NADPH levels in R28 cells were measured using 
an NADP/NADPH assay kit (Beyotime, China). Cells 
were seeded at a density of 6 × 10⁵ cells/well in a 6-well 
plate and treated as required. The absolute values of 
NADP and NADPH were measured according to the 
manufacturer’s instructions. The protein concentration 
of each sample was determined using a BCA assay kit to 
calculate the relative NADP/NADPH ratio.
Cell immunofluorescence and subcellular localization
Cells were seeded on glass coverslips and treated as 
required. After treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% 
paraformaldehyde and washed three times with PBS. 
The cells were blocked with PBS containing 5% bovine 
serum albumin (BSA) and 0.1% Triton X-100 at room 
temperature for 1 h. The cells were then incubated with 
AhR antibody (1:1000) at 4  °C overnight. After washing 
with PBS to remove unbound primary antibody, the cells 
were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 (AntGene, Wuhan, 
China) and Phalloidin Peptide in the dark for 1  h. The 
cells were washed three times with PBS, and the nuclei 
were stained with DAPI. The samples were observed and 
imaged using a confocal laser microscope.

Altered tryptophan metabolism was found in CRAO 
patients and mice model
Our initial objective was to explore whether tryptophan 
metabolism plays a role in CRAO pathology, prompting 
a targeted metabolomics analysis of serum samples from 
patients prior to thrombolytic therapy and from controls. 
Notably, CRAO patients exhibited a significant increase 
in serum tryptophan metabolites compared to controls 
(Fig. 1A and Figure S1A-1D). We utilized wild-type (WT) 
mice subjected to 90 min of silicone wire embolization-
induced acute retinal artery ischemia (referred to as 
UPOAO) or a sham operation, followed by seven days of 
reperfusion. Subsequently, targeted metabolomics of ret -
inal tissue was conducted. Notably, indoleacetic acid and 
L-kynurenine, representative metabolites of the indole 
and kynurenine pathways respectively, were significantly 
decreased in the UPOAO group compared to control 
mice (Fig. 1B). Additionally, the level of tryptophan was 
higher in CRAO patients compared to controls. Con -
sidering that IDO1 is the primary enzyme responsible 
for the enzymatic conversion of 95% of dietary trypto -
phan into kynurenine, we compared the expression of 
these enzymes in the retinas of mice [ 17] (Fig. 1C). RT-
PCR analysis of mouse retinal homogenates revealed a 
decreased IDO1 level specifically in the UPOAO group 
(Fig. 1D). The decline in IDO1 content in mouse retinal 
homogenates of UPOAO group was further validated 
by Western blot analysis (Fig. 1E). Immunofluorescence 
staining of eyeball sections showed spot-shaped IDO1 
distribution in the inner retina of normal controls (Fig. 
1F). As expected, a reduced IDO1 expression was dis -
cernibly observed in the inner retina of UPOAO mice 
compared to controls (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these find -
ings suggest that altered tryptophan metabolism is 
involved in regulating the outcome of visual impairment 
in CRAO.

Kynurenine reduces OGD-induced ROS in R28 partially 
through AhR pathway activation
As previously reported, Kyn served as a high-affinity 
ligand to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), thereby 
upregulating the antioxidant gene NRF2 [ 19]. To con -
firmed this result, we employed CH-223,191, a selective 
AhR antagonist with demonstrated efficacy in blocking 
nuclear translocation and DNA-binding activity [ 20, 21]. 
While CH-223,191 partially attenuated Kyn-mediated 
suppression of apoptotic markers cleaved caspase 3 and 
BAX in R28 OGD (Fig. 5A). Kyn was still able to induce a 
slight increase in NRF2 even when CH-223,191 was pres-
ent (Fig. 5A and B). CH-223,191 treatment failed to fully 
eliminate Kyn’s capability to counteract RGCs apopto -
sis by boosting the expression of antioxidant genes (Fig. 
5B and C). Notably, the partial preservation of Kyn’s 
cytoprotective effects despite AhR blockade suggests a 
non-canonical mechanism independent of direct AhR-
mediated Nrf2 activation. Taken together, these results 
suggest that Kyn protects RGCs from death by activat -
ing AhR-mediated expression of antioxidant genes and 
by promoting the generation of downstream metabolites 
that combat oxidative stress.
Discussion
Understanding the underlying mechanisms driving cen -
tral retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) is critical for devel -
oping effective preventive and therapeutic interventions. 
Currently, vascular embolism caused by atherosclerotic 
debris or iatrogenic fillers is a direct cause of CRAO. 
Additional risk factors include arteriosclerosis, vascu -
litis, and metabolic diseases. Retinal ischemia-reper -
fusion (RIR) injury is a common pathogenic pathway 
in CRAO that leads to retinal dysfunction [ 22, 23]. RIR

injury triggers profound reprogramming of amino acid 
metabolism, which exacerbates retinal neuronal dam -
age and visual impairment through mechanisms such as 
oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular apoptosis [24, 
25]. Here, we identify tryptophan-kynurenine (Trp-Kyn) 
metabolic axis dysregulation as a key contributor to RIR-
induced retinal ganglion cell (RGC) death, demonstrat -
ing its dual therapeutic potential through activation of 
the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and promotion of 
downstream antioxidant metabolite synthesis.
Trp-Kyn metabolism, a crux in host-microbiome com -
munication, orchestrates immune modulation and neu -
rological balance [ 26]. In inflammatory bowel disease, 
abnormal levels of tryptophan (Trp) and its metabolites 
disrupt intestinal homeostasis by activating the aryl 
hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), perturbing mitochondrial 
respiration and glycolysis, and igniting immune cascades 
that fuel colitis [11, 27]. In acute ischemic stroke patients 
with significant carotid stenosis, an elevated Kyn/Trp 
ratio correlates with increased stroke severity and poor 
prognosis [28, 29]. Meanwhile, high myopia progression 
is linked to gut microbiota dysbiosis, characterized by 
lower plasma levels of the microbial metabolite indole-
3-acetic acid (3-IAA) correlated with disease severity 
[30]. Notably, restoring 3-IAA levels via fecal microbiota 
transplantation (FMT) from healthy donors or direct 
supplementation mitigates HM progression in mice by 
preserving scleral collagen (COL1A1) expression through 
SP1-mediated transcriptional activation [ 30]. Our find -
ings expand this paradigm by revealing that Trp-Kyn 
metabolism, a pathway previously implicated in neuroin -
flammation and neurodegeneration, undergoes selective 
reprogramming in RIR injury.
Kyn emerged as a pivotal neuroprotective mediator in 
RIR injury, orchestrating a dual-pronged defense against 
oxidative and metabolic insults through AhR-dependent 
transcriptional activation and downstream metabolite-
mediated redox modulation. Kyn, an endogenous AhR 
ligand, activates the receptor to upregulate antioxidant 
genes such as Nrf2 and SOD1, thereby restoring cellu -
lar redox balance [ 31]. Concurrently, Kyn metabolism 
yielded neuroprotective downstream products, including 
3-hydroxykynurenine (3HK), 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid 
(HAA), and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD ⁺/
NADH) [ 31]. While 3HK and HAA directly scavenged 
ROS to curtail oxidative damage, NAD ⁺ replenishment 
enhanced mitochondrial bioenergetics and ATP synthe -
sis, mitigating the energy deficits characteristic of RIR 
injury.
We detected pronounced alterations in retinal Trp-
Kyn metabolite profiles, characterized by elevated Kyn 
accumulation and dysregulated downstream pathway 
activity. However, the link between these retinal meta -
bolic perturbations and clinical serum Trp fluctuations 
remains unresolved. Given the liver and intestine’s piv -
otal role in Trp metabolism, systemic metabolic shifts 
may underlie clinical Trp variations. The bidirectional 
crosstalk between the eye and liver, mediated by Trp 
metabolites, links pathologies like diabetic retinopathy 
[32] and NAFLD [33], necessitating integrative therapeu-
tic strategies [ 34]. Meanwhile, the gut microbiota’s regu -
lation of Trp metabolism and the protective effects of its 
metabolites traversing the blood-retina barrier via AhR-
dependent or -independent pathways remain deeper 
exploration.
In conclusion, Kyn represents a promising therapeutic 
candidate for RIR injury, acting through AhR-dependent 
and -independent mechanisms to restore redox balance. 
However, our study has several limitations. The absence 
of AhR-knockout animal to confirm whether Kyn’s neu -
roprotective effects are mediated solely by AhR signaling, 
or whether alternative receptors are activated through 
uncharacterized pathways. Nevertheless, our study estab-
lishes Kyn as a key regulator in RIR pathophysiology 
and a viable target for CRAO interventions, particularly 
through receptor-specific targeting and systemic meta -
bolic modulation.
