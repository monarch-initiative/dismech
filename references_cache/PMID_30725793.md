# Uncal Herniation.
**Authors:** Hall WA, Munakomi S

## Content

Uncal herniation represents a catastrophic manifestation of intracranial hypertension and reflects terminal failure of intracranial compensatory mechanisms (see Image. Brain Herniation). The uncus, which forms the anteromedial aspect of the parahippocampal gyrus within the temporal lobe, resides in the supratentorial compartment of the cranial vault, specifically within the temporal fossa. Inferior to the tentorium cerebelli lies the subtentorial compartment, which communicates with the supratentorial space through the tentorial notch (incisura). Critical neurovascular structures traverse this confined aperture, including the midbrain, cranial nerve III, the posterior cerebral arteries, and the superior cerebellar arteries. The cerebellum occupies the posterior aspect of the notch. When intracranial pressure rises beyond the limits of autoregulatory compliance, brain tissue may shift across rigid dural partitions; uncal herniation occurs when the medial anterior temporal lobe is displaced through the tentorial incisura into the suprasellar cistern. This process constitutes a life-threatening neurological emergency and signifies exhaustion of adaptive intracranial buffering capacity. Uncal herniation is the most common clinically recognizable herniation syndrome. Here, there is a passive horizontal distortion (the mesencephalon is twisted) rather than a vertical displacement of the brain. Clinically, an uncal herniation is characterized by a predictable pattern of neuroanatomic compression, including ipsilateral third nerve palsy, contralateral motor deficits from compression of the cerebral peduncle, and progressive brainstem dysfunction. This condition most commonly arises in the setting of space-occupying lesions, traumatic brain injury, intracranial hemorrhage, or malignant cerebral edema. Rapid recognition is essential, as delays in diagnosis and intervention are associated with irreversible brainstem ischemia and high mortality.  The conceptual evolution of uncal herniation reflects more than a century of clinicopathologic correlation. Jonathan Hutchinson first described unilateral pupillary dilation (Hutchinson pupil), initially attributed to midbrain involvement. Macewen linked pupillary changes to irritation, paralysis, or vascular compromise of the oculomotor nerve, while Bergmann incorrectly localized the phenomenon to the frontal lobe. Ropper later identified acute angulation of the third nerve over the clivus at autopsy. Hill demonstrated transtentorial pressure gradients and tamponade effects at the foramen magnum and tentorial notch in animal models. Adolf Meyer described posterior cerebral artery compression with hemianopia as a false localizing sign. Kernohan and Woltman subsequently reported ipsilateral hemiplegia due to notching of the crus cerebri against the contralateral tentorial margin, now known as the Kernohan–Woltman phenomenon. Jefferson characterized “tentorial and temporal pressure cones,” while Reid and Cone demonstrated hippocampal extrusion compressing the oculomotor nerve in primate studies. McNealy and Plum further delineated central and uncal herniation patterns. Finally, Miller Fisher and Allan Ropper challenged the notion of an active process, emphasizing a passive mechanical distortion with widening of the ischial space of Bichat instead. Together, these observations established the modern understanding of uncal herniation as a biomechanical and vascular consequence of intracranial compartment failure, demanding immediate recognition and intervention in contemporary neurocritical care practice.
