# Moniliformis moniliformis acanthocephaliasis in a paediatric patient.
**Authors:** Lin S, Otero J, Stern A, Kirkconnell S
**Journal:** BMJ Case Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1136/bcr-2024-263593](https://doi.org/10.1136/bcr-2024-263593)

## Content

Acanthocephala are known as thorny-headed worms and are common parasites in animals, but acanthocephaliasis in humans is rare, with limited medical literature available. Infections are acquired via direct ingestion of arthropod intermediate hosts. In this case report, a female toddler is evaluated for passage of a pseudosegmented worm in the stool, which was later identified as Moniliformis moniliformis She is treated with pyrantel pamoate with a resolution of worms in stool. This case report aims to expand knowledge surrounding infections by the Acanthocephala phylum and the nuances of identification and treatment.

Helminth or parasitic worm infections pose major health problems globally. Parasitic infections from nematodes (roundworms) and platyhelminth (flatworms) are common. However, infections caused by acanthocephalans (thorny-headed worms) in humans are considered rare; additionally, the medical literature on acanthocephaliasis is limited. 1 Unlike other helminth exposures, such as contaminated soil or consumption of undercooked meat, 2 humans become infected by acanthocephalans via direct ingestion of the infected arthropods, such as cockroaches or beetles. 1 This case report describes Moniliformis moniliformis acanthocephaliasis in a toddler. Accurate identification of the helminth-guided antiparasitic treatment and resolution of the infection. This case report aims to add knowledge surrounding these parasitic worms within the Acanthocephala phylum for the improvement of patient care.

A female toddler, with no significant medical history, presented for evaluation in primary care setting after an approximate 7 cm, tan, somewhat round and segmented appearing worm was discovered in her stool during a diaper change. She had been healthy, without fever or systemic symptoms. Her behaviour, including appetite, sleep and bowel movements, continued to be routine. There was no blood or mucus in her stools. She had not travelled out of the country or participated in camping activities. Her known animal exposure included contact with a relative’s dog. The family recalled seeing the child putting a cockroach in her mouth about 1–2 months prior. They also recalled removing a pair of nesting rats from a shed on their property about 4 months prior. There was no consumption of raw meat, including pork or fish. Other family members were asymptomatic. Her temperature and physical exam were normal.

Concern for worms in the stool is a common presenting complaint in paediatrics and knowledge of the different parasites is important to guide appropriate management. Gross inspection of the worm was notable for a segmented appearance, leading to initial suspicion of tapeworms (see figures1 2). However, pathology identified it as Moniliformis moniliformis, an acanthocephalan. Its unique characteristics include a ‘spindle-shaped, pseuodosegmented body’, in comparison to the true segmented cestodes or tapeworms. 3 Two of the most common helminth groups causing infections in paediatrics are nematodes (roundworms) and platyhelminths (flatworms). They can be differentiated from acanthocephalans (thorny-headed worms) in that nematodes are round with no segementation while platyhelminths may have segmentation but are flat. 4 The pseudosegmentation of Moniliformis moniliformis, an acanthocephalan, may lead the clinician to think of a tapeworm, a segmented platyhelminth, but Moniliformis moniliformis are rounder in appearance without true segmentation. The segments of tapeworms are proglottids, which can separate off and be seen in the stool. 5

The patient was initially prescribed praziquantel for suspicion of tapeworms and advised to hold treatment until formal identification was available. Once Moniliformis moniliformis was identified, an infectious disease specialist was consulted for guidance on treatment and over-the-counter pyrantel pamoate was recommended. The patient completed three doses of 11 mg/kg pyrantel pamoate, separated by 2-week intervals. A repeat stool study was performed 2 weeks following the last treatment and was negative for ova and parasites.

While Moniliformis moniliformis infections have been reported in several other countries, there have only been a few previously reported in the USA. 1 3 6 7 Each of these cases was reported in the state of Florida and involved a child less than 2 years, consistent with the demographics and geography of our patient. 3 6 In each case, the children were asymptomatic aside from presence of worms in stool, and history was notable for the children putting an insect or cockroach in the mouth. 3 6 Again, this history was replicated in our case, as after identification the mother did recall that the child had been found recently with a cockroach in her mouth. The toddler stage is likely at increased risk for infection due to the tendency to place varying objects, including insects, in the mouth. 1 3 However, it may be due to acanthocephaliasis better reported in diaper-aged children as the diaper itself aids collection and inspection. 1 Symptoms can vary from asymptomatic to including diarrhoea, weight loss, weakness and irritability. 1 3 7

Acanthocephalan species identified in medical case reports include Macracanthorhynchus hirudinaceus, Macracranthorhynchus ingens and Moniliformis moniliformis. 1 Acanthocephalans are notable for their spinous retractile proboscis. 3 This ‘thorny head’ allows the parasites to attach to the intestinal wall of the definitive host, which is primarily a rat or rodent for Moniliformis moniliformis. 1 3 In the life cycle, eggs of Moniliformis moniliformis are shed in the definitive host’s faeces. These eggs contain the first larval stage, called acanthor. 1 Arthropod intermediate hosts, like beetles and cockroaches, become infected after feeding on the rodent faeces. Within the arthropods, the acanthor moult into the second larval stage known as acanthella. 1 8 After 6–12 weeks, the larva then develops into the infective cystacanth form. 1 8 When the definitive host, a rodent or sometimes human, ingests the cystacanth-infected arthropod, the cystacanths release and attach to the host’s small intestine where they mature into adult worms and reproduce, completing the cycle. 1 8

Cases of human acanthocephaliasis are likely under-reported, as many medical providers may not recognise Acanthocephala infections in humans. Information on human acanthocephaliasis is not included in many medical microbiology textbooks, which may lead to inaccurate parasite identification, such as Ascaris lumbricoides. 1 Due to similar appearances of parasitic worms, it is important for medical providers to send specimens to laboratories for identification when possible. Accurate identification of the parasite can guide the appropriate selection of antiparasitic treatment and timely resolution of infection. Although there is currently no standard treatment for Moniliformis moniliformis, mild asymptomatic cases have been successfully treated with three doses of pyrantel pamoate at 11 mg/kg, separated by 2-week intervals, as was the case with our patient. 1

Acanthocephaliasis should be on the differential for a segmented appearing worm.

Moniliformis moniliformis can be differentiated from flatworms as they are not flat, and they can be differentiated from roundworms as they have a pseudosegmented appearance.

It is important for medical providers and laboratories to recognise infectious aetiologies caused by acanthocephalans, especially in toddler patients, as prompt and accurate identification of these parasitic worms can guide antiparasitic selection and timely treatment.
