# Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy.
**Authors:** Amrutkar CV, Benjamin R

## Content

Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME), historically designated Janz syndrome or impulsive petit mal, is one of the most common electroclinical syndromes among the idiopathic generalized epilepsies that present during adolescence. The other 3 idiopathic generalized epilepsies include childhood absence epilepsy, juvenile absence epilepsy, and epilepsy with generalized tonic-clonic seizures. JME is a well-characterized, polygenic, idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndrome characterized by a homogeneous phenotypic triad comprising myoclonic jerks that usually occur after awakening, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and typical but infrequent absence seizures that patients may not recognize. The initial clinical documentation of the syndrome dates to 1867, when Théodore Herpin detailed myoclonic jerks in a 14-year-old boy. A comprehensive cohort analysis was later published in 1957 by Janz and Christian, who evaluated 47 patients, termed the syndrome "impulsive petit mal," and proposed its classification as a distinct epileptic entity. Independently and concurrently in 1957, Castells and Mendilaharsu described a similar cohort with identical clinical manifestations in Uruguay. Lund introduced the formal nomenclature for juvenile myoclonic epilepsy in 1975. The syndrome achieved formal nosological recognition in 1989, when the International League Against Epilepsy officially incorporated JME into its international classification of epilepsies and epileptic syndromes.
