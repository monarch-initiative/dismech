Tay-Sachs disease is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive
neurologic impairment due to pathogenic variants in the HEXA gene that codes for
the alpha subunit of β-hexosaminidase. We report 2 cases of adult-onset
progressive weakness, ataxia, and neuropsychiatric symptoms in a 30-year-old man
and 37-year-old woman. Both patients had compound heterozygosity in the HEXA
gene with 4 distinct variants. The first patient had subsequent confirmatory
functional enzyme testing displaying reduced hexosaminidase concentration, and
the second patient had functional enzyme testing before genetic testing,
exemplifying alternative avenues for the diagnosis of late-onset Tay-Sachs
(LOTS) disease.

A 22-year-old man was referred to local neurology services with a pre-existing provisional diagnosis of spinal muscular atrophy after symptoms of slowly progressive generalized weakness, wasting, fasciculations with a lower limb predominance, and dysarthria commencing at age 17, with more recent painful cramps and thigh pains. There was a family history of tremor, otherwise no other family history of neuromuscular disease. Initial examination revealed a reduced body mass index of 16, lower limb fasciculations, and atrophy. There was Medical Research Council grade 4/5 strength to elbow and knee extension, with diffuse limb hyperreflexia and impaired tandem gait, with normal sensation and plantar responses. Cranial nerves displayed only mild dysarthria.

Electrodiagnostic testing revealed active and chronic neurogenic changes, and a subsequent motor neuron disease/spinal muscular atrophy gene panel, repeat expansion testing for spinocerebellar ataxia, and the Friedreich ataxia panel were nondiagnostic, as was a test for SMN1 exon deletions.

Magnetic resonance (MR) brain 1 year after presentation showed severe, symmetric cerebellar atrophy with mild diffuse cerebral atrophy (Figure 1). Subsequent presentations over 5 years displayed progressive severity and spread of weakness, cerebellar features, dysphagia, cognitive and memory impairment, impulsivity, and later psychosis. Examination findings over this time displayed progressive mixed motor neuron signs and preserved sensation.

After a 2-year absence, the patient presented with progressive dysphagia and a subsequent repeat MR brain displayed progression of severe diffuse atrophy, most marked in the cerebellum (Figure 2). A hereditary spastic paraparesis panel revealed compound heterozygosity for 2 pathogenic variants in the HEXA gene, (NM_000520.6):c.805G>A [p. (Gly269Ser)] and c.915_917 del[p.(Phe305del)]. Confirmatory enzyme testing revealed low plasma N-acetyl-beta-hexosaminidase A protein in leukocytes and plasma at 0.6 U/g and 5 U/L (normal ranges 2.9–14 U/g and 9–50 U/L), respectively.

The second patient was a 37-year-old woman presenting to the clinic with an approximately 10-year history of chronic progressive proximal leg weakness and incoordination. On examination, she displayed hip flexion, elbow and knee extension weakness, and diffuse hyperreflexia with flexor plantar responses, without cerebellar or sensory findings. MR muscles of the lower limb revealed muscular atrophy with fatty replacement, involving bilateral anterior and thigh compartments and gluteus minimi, without evidence of myositis, while spinal imaging was non-contributory. A myopathy and limited muscular dystrophy gene panel was negative. She had a muscle biopsy of the left vastus lateralis that displayed severe atrophy without evidence of myositis or necrosis, and normal mitochondrial enzymes were observed. An SMN1 gene analysis was negative.

The N-acetyl-beta-hexosaminidase A protein level was significantly low in leucocytes at 0.2 U/g (normal range 2.9–14 U/g). A neuromuscular gene panel identified a pathogenic variant in the HEXA gene (NM_000520.6) c.806-7G>A [p.(?)] and 1 likely pathogenic variant (c.1384G>A [p.Glu462Lys]). She progressed to have significant functional impairment around stairs and developed neuropsychiatric symptoms including depression.

Tay-Sachs disease (TSD) is an autosomal recessive inherited neurologic disorder that has variable characteristic clinical features varying in the age at onset. TSD often presents as infantile and juvenile onset, manifesting as visual changes, developmental delay, or seizures. There are currently insufficient data to estimate the prevalence of LOTS disease. Case reports and case series of LOTS disease have documented presentations with progressive weakness, often with a lower limb predominance affecting the iliopsoas and quadriceps, or upper limb involving the triceps, and mixed motor neuron signs including atrophy and fasciculations, with coexisting spasticity.1

Cerebellar features including dysarthria and incoordination may be present, in addition to tremor and neuropsychiatric features including cognitive decline, mood disorders, and psychosis.1,2 Isolated cerebellar atrophy may be found on neuroimaging.2,3 Breadth and severity of neurologic clinical features are highly variable,1-3 and currently, inadequate numbers exist to reliably predict phenotypic manifestations based on specific variants.

Pathogenic loss-of-function (LoF) variants in the HEXA gene that codes for the alpha subunit of β-hexosaminidase A result in reduced enzymatic degradation of neuronal lipids such as GM2 ganglioside, ultimately leading to cell damage and death. Two screening methods that can detect the underlying cause and effect of TSD are DNA sequencing analysis for the HEXA gene and an enzymatic assay for hexosaminidase A function, respectively.4,5

Currently, genetic testing is the mainstay of diagnosis; however, current limitations of this diagnostic avenue include laboratory costs, availability, possible incomplete variant discovery, and mosaicism. In addition, other genetic influences have been discovered including variants in GM2A, which code for the GM2-activator protein that facilitates the upstream interaction between GM2 with hexosaminidase A for degradation.6 Complete polygenic and environmental influences are not fully understood.

Accurate recognition of LOTS disease phenotypes is needed to identify the condition when suspicion remains high despite negative genotyping, such as in those with lower motor neuron signs in the presence of cerebellar and/or neuropsychiatric features with relative sensory preservation. This is particularly true in populations with raised carrier frequencies such as the French-Canadian or Ashkenazi Jewish populations, the latter possessing the common c.805G>A associated with LOTS disease.4,5 In these instances, N-acetyl-beta-hexosaminidase A enzyme testing remains a viable option and will display reduced levels compared with controls or carriers.7 Notably, patients with LoF GM2A may sometimes display normal levels if hexosaminidase testing is based on protein levels rather than functional assessment.7

LOTS disease should be considered in those with motor, incoordination, and neuropsychiatric symptoms, until both genetic testing and functional enzyme testing have occurred. The potential benefits of securing the diagnosis in LOTS disease include ending the diagnostic journey, providing closure, and minimizing further investigations. It also allows for funding for allied health and community support groups and, importantly, aids in adequate family planning (if diagnosed early enough). Furthermore, there remain several treatment options currently under research, including enzyme replacement and enhancing therapies, substrate reduction therapies, as well as antisense oligonucleotide, and gene therapies.8

Solidifying a diagnosis and consent for inclusion into databases allows for adequate epidemiologic estimates and refinement of genotype-phenotype associations and provides a potential recruitment into future research.
