# CFAP65 is essential for C2a projection integrity in axonemes: implications for organ-specific ciliary dysfunction and infertility.
**Authors:** Chen J, Ren C, Zhao S, Wu H, Wang J, Dong Y, Liu S, Pan Y, Xiao Z, Yang S, Zhang J, Liu M
**Journal:** Cell Mol Life Sci (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00018-025-05583-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-025-05583-2)

## Content

Defects in motile cilia and flagella lead to motile ciliopathies, including primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), which manifests as multi-organ dysfunction such as hydrocephalus, infertility, and respiratory issues. CFAP65 variants are a common cause of male infertility, but its localization and function have remained unclear. In this study, we systematically evaluated CFAP65's role using Cfap65 knockout mice and human patients with CFAP65 variants. The knockout mice displayed severe sperm flagellar defects (MMAF), high hydrocephalus incidence, but no significant impact on respiratory cilia. Similarly, the patients exhibited MMAF and infertility without respiratory symptoms. CFAP65 was found to anchor at the base of the C2a projection of the axoneme, interacting with proteins such as CFAP70 and MYCBPAP. Loss of CFAP65 caused disorganization of the sperm head-shaping microtubule structure and impaired protamine precursor removal, leading to nuclear condensation defects and poor assisted reproductive outcomes. Importantly, the assembly of CFAP65 was unaffected in mice with defects in the radial spokes (RSs) and nexin-dynein regulatory complex (N-DRC), indicating that CFAP65 assembly is independent of these components. However, CFAP65 deficiency led to the disintegration of the C2a projection, compromising ciliary and flagellar integrity. These findings establish CFAP65 as an essential component of the C2a projection, critical for the structure and function of sperm flagella and ependymal cilia, but not respiratory cilia, underscoring the organ-specific consequences of C2a projection defects in PCD.

Cilia and flagella are ubiquitous organelles in eukaryotes, with highly conserved structures from protists to humans [1]. Cilia are categorized into sensory non-motile cilia, motile embryonic node cilia, and typical motile cilia. The typical motile cilium features a 9 + 2 axonemal structure, comprising nine doublet microtubules (DMTs) and a central apparatus (CA), which consists of two singlet microtubules (C1 and C2) and associated projections (C1a-f and C2a-e in Chlamydomonas, C1a-e and C2a-e in mammals) [2, 3]. The CA plays a critical role in the development and regulation of motile cilia. Mutations in at least 11 CA-associated genes have been reported to cause motile ciliopathies, such as multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF) and primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) [4–14]. PCD is a genetically and clinically heterogeneous disease, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 1:10,000 [15], presenting with various combinations of symptoms including neonatal respiratory distress, persistent wet cough, recurrent and chronic upper and lower respiratory tract infections, bronchiectasis, otitis media with effusion, situs inversus or heterotaxy, hydrocephalus, and infertility [16].

CFAP65 (also known as CCDC108 or AKAP240) is highly conserved across species such as Chlamydomonas, Xenopus, Zebrafish, Mouse, Chicken, Chimpanzee, and Human. It is predominantly expressed in the testis but also in other motile cilia-containing organs such as the brain and lung. CFAP65 was first reported in chickens to be associated with reduced male fertility [17]. Several studies reported that CFAP65 deficiency leads to male infertility in humans and mice, presenting with the MMAF phenotype [18–23]. These studies have also observed severe phenotypes in Cfap65 knockout (Cfap65−/−) mice, including acrosome malformation, abnormal sperm head shapes, manchette organization defects, and disorganized axonemes and surrounding structures. However, the localization of CFAP65 across studies has been contradictory, ranging from the acrosome and midpiece to the entire flagellum. The lack of conclusive subcellular localization has led to considerable confusion. Some reviews have described CFAP65 as associated with the acrosome, mitochondrial sheath, or inner dynein arms [24–27], others as undefined [28]. Some have even classified CFAP65 as CA-associated, given that FAP65 (the Chlamydomonas homolog of CFAP65) has been reported to interact with FAP70 and potentially co-localize in one of the largest CA projections, the C2a projection [25, 29]. However, Shamoto et al. proposed that FAP70 localizes on DMTs, at the base of the outer dynein arms [30]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to clarify the current controversies and misconceptions about the location and role of CFAP65 in motile cilia and flagella of vertebrates.

In this study, we report the multi-organ ciliary/flagellar phenotypes of Cfap65−/− mice and evaluation of human patients with CFAP65 variants, along with morphological and mass spectrometric evidence of CFAP65 precise structural localization, confirming its function as a component of the C2a projection.

Therefore, we systematically examined whether organs containing motile cilia and flagella in Cfap65−/− mice exhibit abnormalities. We previously found that Cfap65−/− male mice recapitulated the MMAF phenotype observed in male infertility patients. Cfap65−/− female mice, whose oviducts contain motile cilia essential for fertility, remained fertile. Cfap65−/− mice on a pure C57BL/6 background exhibited a high incidence of hydrocephalus. Over two-thirds of Cfap65−/− neonatal mice developed hydrocephalus, characterized by delayed development, reduced body size and weight, and enlarged dome-shaped skulls (Fig. S2B), with most dying before three weeks of age. Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining of brain sections showed extensive ventricular expansion in Cfap65−/− mice with hydrocephalus (Fig. S2C). However, other Cfap65−/− mice without hydrocephalus showed no significant differences in individual size and testis weight compared to wild type (Cfap65+/+) mice, aside from male infertility (Fig. S2D). Given that mixed genetic background can improve the survival rate of PCD mice [37], we therefore crossed Cfap65−/− females with ICR males and found significantly reduced the incidence of hydrocephalus in Cfap65−/− mice on a C57BL/6 × ICR background (Table S1). Whole-mount staining for CEP164 and Ac-tubulin showed that ependymal ciliogenesis in Cfap65−/− mice is unaffected (Fig. S2E). Nevertheless, the surviving Cfap65−/− mice exhibited significantly reduced ciliary motility in the ependyma compared to Cfap65+/+ mice (Video S1, S2). To investigate how the loss of CFAP65 affects various cilia/flagella, we systematically analyzed the ultrastructure of motile cilia/flagella from different organs of Cfap65−/− mice. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) showed no significant morphological changes in the respiratory cilia of Cfap65−/− mice. Considering that the distribution of ependymal cilia in the lateral ventricular wall is region-dependent, we observed the equivalent regions of lateral ventricles [38] from Cfap65+/+ and Cfap65−/− mice and found no significant difference in ependymal cilia (Fig. 2A). However, sperm flagella exhibited more severe phenotypes, including missing, bent, coiled, shortened, and irregular flagella (Fig. S4A–H). Correspondingly, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed severe disorganization of the axonemal structure in Cfap65−/− sperm, with a complete loss of the CA structure (Fig. S4I–L).

Next, we analyzed the ultrastructure of tracheal cilia in Cfap65+/+ and Cfap65−/− mice and found that the arrangement of the nine DMTs surrounding the two central microtubules had no obvious abnormalities. However, closer examination revealed subtle changes in the CA ultrastructure in Cfap65−/− mice, with axonemes lacking the C2a projection (indicated by arrows, number of Cfap65+/+ axonemes observed = 29, Cfap65−/− axonemes observed = 34, number of mice per group = 3), while other C2 projections and RSs appeared unaffected (Fig. 2B–I). In ependymal cilia, the absence of CFAP65 similarly led to the loss of the C2a projection, and approximately half of the observed axonemal cross-sections exhibited a complete loss of the C2 microtubule (indicated by arrows, number of Cfap65+/+ axonemes observed = 33, Cfap65−/− axonemes observed = 31, number of mice per group = 3) (Fig. 2J–R). These results suggest that CFAP65 deficiency disrupts the C2a projection structure in motile cilia and flagella, severely impairing the 9 + 2 axonemal structure and function of sperm flagella, partially affecting ependymal cilia, and having minimal impact on respiratory cilia, reflecting the different requirements for C2a projection in the function of these motile cilia/flagella.

The loss of C2a structure in CFAP65-deficient axonemes suggests that CFAP65 is a component of the C2a projection in the CA. Additionally, two cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies have determined the structure of the Chlamydomonas CA and identified several additional CA-associated proteins [40, 41]. Further analysis of our mass spectrometry data revealed the presence of CA-associated proteins MYCBPAP, MYCBP, CFAP70, SPATA4, SPATA17, HSPA1L, PPP1CC (corresponding to Chlamydomonas C2 components FAP147, FAP174, FAP70, FAP178, C1 components MOT17, HSP70A, PP1c) (Fig. 3A). Through co-IP, we validated the interaction between CFAP65 and known mammalian homologs of Chlamydomonas C2a-associated components, finding that CFAP65 can directly interact with CFAP70, SPAG16, SPATA4, CFAP20, and indirectly with MYCBP through MYCBPAP (Fig. 3C–G). In Chlamydomonas, FAP65, FAP70, and FAP147 are components of the C2a projection. Our U-ExM results showed that the CFAP70 signal also localized in the CA (Fig. 3H). Combining our CFAP65 interactome data with the cryo-ET model of the mouse CA structure [42] and referencing the Chlamydomonas CA cryo-EM data [41], we manually assembled a structural model of the mouse axonemal C2a projection (Fig. 3I), revealing that CFAP65 anchors at the base of the C2a projection and extends to the C2e projection. In the absence of CFAP65, MYCBPAP may struggle to maintain the entire large C2a projection, ultimately leading to its disintegration.

The movement of typical motile cilia/flagella is powered by coordinated movements of the inner and outer dynein arms on DMTs, regulated by the CA and other DMT-associated structures such as the nexin-dynein regulatory complex (N-DRC) and radial spokes (RSs) [43]. Our results above suggest that CFAP65 acts as a crucial structural stabilizer of the C2a projection, although not essential for ciliogenesis, is critical for axonemal stability. The impact of CFAP65 deficiency varies across different types of cilia and flagella, which we believe may be related to different structural stability requirements of different ciliary axonemes, involving two factors: ciliary length and fluid dynamics. Mouse sperm flagella exceed 100 µm in length, requiring greater structural stability, whereas motile cilia in other tissues are only 5–10 µm long. What’s more, under identical fluid conditions in vitro, ependymal cilia generate stronger propulsion than respiratory cilia and may thus depend more on structural stability. If so, different ciliary axonemes should have varying affected components.

To verify this hypothesis, we further systematically examined the effects on the assembly of axonemal components in motile cilia/flagella of the patient and Cfap65−/− mice. IF results showed that CA components, including CFAP70, MYCBPAP, MYCBP, SPAG16, SPATA4, and HYDIN, were absent in sperm of the patient, while DMT-associated components such as DRC1 (N-DRC), DNAH1, DNAH5 (dyneins), IQUB, NME5, RSPH9 (RSs), were normally assembled (Fig. 4). In motile cilia/flagella of Cfap65−/− mice, the same CA components defects were also observed in the sperm flagella, without DMT proteins defects (Fig. 5A, S5). However, in respiratory cilia, IF results showed that only C2a projection-specific components such as CFAP65, CFAP70, and MYCBPAP were absent, and MYCBP signals were reduced rather than absent, as it is present in both C1b and C2a projections (Fig. 5B). Apart from these changes, no significant differences were observed in the IF of other CA and DMT axonemal components (Fig. 5B, S6). Similarly, IF results in ependymal cilia also showed that C2a-specific components CFAP65, CFAP70, and MYCBPAP were absent, and MYCBP signals were reduced. In addition, signals for SPAG16 and SPATA4, which are responsible for connecting C2 to C1, were also significantly reduced (Fig. 6A, B), while IF results for DMT-associated complexes showed no significant differences (Fig. S7). These results suggest that CFAP65 is crucial for the stability of the C2a projection structure in cilia/flagella, and different structural stability requirements of different ciliary axonemes result in varying dependence on CFAP65. Spermatozoa, as a specialized ciliated cell, possess another microtubule structure, the manchette, which is involved in nuclear deformation during spermiogenesis. This transient skirt-like microtubule structure surrounds the elongating spermatid head and plays a crucial role in shaping the sperm head [45]. Axonemal and manchette abnormalities are often observed simultaneously in MMAF sperm, possibly due to microtubule organization disruption caused by axonemal disintegration [46, 47].

To determine whether CFAP65 deficiency affected nuclear condensation, we next assessed the expression of TNP1 (Transition Protein 1) and PRM2 (Protamine 2) in the testes of Cfap65−/− mice. During nuclear condensation, histones are first replaced by transition proteins, which are then replaced by protamines. PRM2 is synthesized as a precursor protein that binds to chromatin and undergoes proteolytic processing, resulting in several intermediates that ultimately form mature PRM2 [48]. Incomplete processing of the protamine 2 precursor (PPRM2) is associated with low levels of mature PRM2, leading to impaired nuclear protein assembly [49]. IF analysis of testis sections revealed that TNP1 replacement was normal in Cfap65−/− spermatids. However, PPRM2 was abnormally retained at stages VII and VIII in the testes of Cfap65−/− mice (Fig. 8C), leading to impaired PRM2 maturation and affecting nuclear condensation. Tunnel apoptosis assay results also showed increased apoptosis rates in elongating spermatids within the seminiferous tubules of Cfap65−/− mice (Fig. 8D–F). These findings suggest that the loss of CFAP65-mediated axonemal disintegration leads to subsequent nuclear deformation and condensation abnormalities, severely reducing sperm quality and impacting assisted reproductive outcomes.

In this study, we reported for the first time that Cfap65 knockout in mammals results in the loss of the C2a projection in ciliary/flagellar axonemes, leading to a high incidence of hydrocephalus, severe MMAF, but no other respiratory symptoms of PCD. Importantly, we provided strong evidences that CFAP65 is an evolutionarily conserved component of the C2a projection, thereby resolving previous misconceptions due to conflicting reports about CFAP65 localization.

The CA plays a vital role in regulating ciliary movement and maintaining sperm flagellar organization. Several studies have shown that mice with CA projection defects exhibit varying degrees of PCD symptoms, such as SPAG17 (C1a projection) [13], SPEF2 (C1b projection) [14], CFAP54 (C1d projection) [10], and HYDIN (C1b projection) [9]. CFAP70 has been reported to cause severe MMAF and male infertility in humans and mice [50, 51]. Some reviews have confused CFAP65 with another protein, WDR65, categorizing it as part of the T/TH complex associated with inner dynein arms [26, 27]. Despite previous controversies regarding the localization of CFAP65 and CFAP70, our data indicate that CFAP65 and CFAP70 interact and both localize to the CA, consistent with recent cryo-EM studies suggesting that CFAP65 and CFAP70 are conserved components of the C2a projection and are crucial for its structural integrity. Cfap65−/− mice not only exhibited severe sperm malformations but also hydrocephalus and prepubertal mortality due to decreased ependymal cilia motility, while tracheal cilia showed only ultrastructural defects in the C2a projection with no significant respiratory dysfunction. This loss of the C2a projection is similar to that observed in the axonemes of Chlamydomonas fap70-1 mutants31. The phenotypes of the two newly identified patients carrying CFAP65 variants were similar to those of the mice. Ultrastructure expansion microscopy confirmed that CFAP65 and CFAP70 are both localized in the CA of flagellar axonemes. Co-immunoprecipitation also confirmed that CFAP65 interacts with proteins such as CFAP70 and MYCBPAP. These results demonstrate that CFAP65 is a conserved component of the C2a projection in ciliary axonemes and is indispensable for the structural integrity of the C2a projection in motile cilia and flagella. Consistent with our findings, recent results from Wang et al. also indicate that MYCBPAP is a CA component that interacts with CFAP65 and CFAP70, and its loss leads to male infertility in mice [52]. These data provide new evidences supporting the proposal that CA projections serve as important bridges for signal transmission between the CA and DMTs and are crucial for the motility of motile cilia.

Additionally, our understanding of C2 microtubules remains less developed than that of C1 microtubules, with some parts of the C2 microtubule density in cryo-EM models still unassigned to specific proteins [40, 41]. Given the unique position of CFAP65 at the base of the C2a projection, CFAP65 interaction proteins may serve as candidate components for the C2a projection, as well as the adjacent C2e projection and C2 bridge.

PCD patients often exhibit a range of diverse symptoms, reflecting the variability in ciliary function across different cell types. In our previous PCD mouse models, knockout mice for Drc1, Cfap61, and Iqub all exhibited male infertility [37, 44, 47]. Notably, Drc1 knockout mice, which lack the N-DRC, also exhibited severe hydrocephalus and prepubertal mortality, along with shortened and dysfunctional respiratory cilia, whereas no such symptoms were observed in Cfap61 and Iqub knockout mice, which lack RS components. Interestingly, Cfap65−/− mice, with a C2a projection deficiency, appear to fall between these two extremes, leading to a high incidence of hydrocephalus and male infertility, but no other respiratory symptoms.

While the complete 9 + 2 axonemal structure is crucial for normal respiratory ciliary function, Ostrowski et al. demonstrated that maintaining just 20% of normal mucociliary clearance can significantly reduce the severity of respiratory disease in a mouse model [53]. Therefore, it is understandable that the loss of the C2a projection does not lead to significant PCD respiratory symptoms. At the same time, hydrocephalus is a common feature of PCD mouse models. Its mechanism was first reported in Mdnah5 mutant mice, where ependymal ciliary flow is absent, leading to aqueduct closure and hydrocephalus during brain development [31]. On the C57BL/6 genetic background, C2a projection defects severely impaired ependymal ciliary function, but introducing the ICR background significantly improved the survival of Cfap65−/− mice, suggesting that genetic background is also an important determinant of PCD severity.

The length and fluid environment cause different cilia and flagella to have varying dependencies on axonemal structural stability, leading to axonemal defects of varying severity when the same axonemal components are absent. As we previously observed in Drc1, Cfap61, and Iqub knockout mice, Cfap65−/− mouse sperm flagella, due to their longer length, were the most severely affected, with complete disorganization of the axonemal structure, indicating that the C2a projection is critical for the stability of flagellar structure. Ependymal cilia, subjected to higher fluid flow pressure, are impacted to a lesser extent, while respiratory cilia are the least affected. Investigating these differences further may help uncover the underlying mechanisms of different ciliary functions, aiding in understanding the complexity and diversity of PCD phenotypes and providing a theoretical basis for therapeutic strategies tailored to different PCD phenotypes.

Typically, ICSI treatment can rescue male infertility caused by MMAF phenotypes, but previous reports have shown that assisted reproductive outcomes for MMAF patients with CFAP65 variants are poor, and the ICSI experiments in this study also did not yield satisfactory pregnancy outcomes, with significant obstacles observed during the fertilization stage.

Our results suggest that CFAP65 deficiency leads to a series of microtubule-related disorganizations in sperm flagella and the manchette, resulting in abnormal spermatid head deformation, impaired histone-to-protamine replacement, nuclear condensation defects, and severely reduced sperm quality, ultimately leading to poor ICSI outcomes. Further attempts to explore other rescue strategies, such as assisted oocyte activation (AOA) in ICSI, round spermatid injection (ROSI), and potential high-quality sperm selection methods, are necessary. Additionally, the relationship between abnormal sperm head morphology, disorganized flagellar structures, and fertilization failure requires further investigation to guide clinical treatment strategy selection.

Overall, our data suggest that CFAP65 deficiency leads to the loss of the C2a projection in respiratory cilia, decreased C2 microtubule stability and partial loss in ependymal cilia, and more severe destruction of sperm flagella, resulting in complete axonemal disintegration and subsequent nuclear condensation defects, ultimately leading to poor assisted reproductive outcomes. This emphasizes the differential requirements for the C2a projection in motile cilia and flagella across different organs, providing new insights into the cell type-specific mechanisms of axonemal stability in motile cilia and flagella, as well as the phenotypic diversity of PCD.
