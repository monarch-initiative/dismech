# Serial evaluation of gonads of complete androgen insensitivity syndrome from birth to puberty: Is gonadectomy necessary?
**Authors:** Ueda Y, Matsumoto F, Matsui F, Matsuyama S
**Journal:** Urol Case Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.eucr.2025.103068](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eucr.2025.103068)

## Content

The timing of gonadectomy in patients with complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS) remains controversial due to concerns about the potential risk of malignancy. We report a rare case of CAIS diagnosed shortly after birth, prompted by a discrepancy between the karyotype identified through amniocentesis and the phenotype at birth. Early diagnosis allowed for longitudinal evaluation of the gonads from birth through puberty. Although gonadal tumors developed during puberty, histopathological examination revealed benign lesions. These findings suggest that, with careful monitoring, gonadectomy might have been safely deferred until adulthood in this patient.

Androgen insensitivity syndrome is an X-linked recessive genetic disorder caused by mutations in the androgen receptor (AR) gene. The syndrome is classified into mild (MAIS), partial (PAIS), and complete (CAIS) forms. CAIS is characterized by female external genitalia and a 46, XY karyotype, whereas PAIS manifests with a varying degree of genital ambiguity, including bifid scrotum, clitoromegaly, micropenis, and hypospadias. MAIS is typically asymptomatic, with infertility being the primary clinical manifestation.1 CAIS is typically diagnosed based on primary amenorrhea during puberty or inguinal swelling during infancy. With the advancement of prenatal testing, early diagnosis based on the discrepancy between fetal karyotypes and phenotypes has been increasingly reported.2 According to molecular testing, the estimated prevalence of CAIS ranges from 1 in 20,400 to 1 in 99,000 genetic males.1

Gonadectomy is recommended for individuals with CAIS at various ages to reduce the risk of gonadal malignancies. However, the indications for and optimal timing of gonadectomy remain controversial. Although the risk of malignancy increases with age, reports of invasive testicular germ cell tumors (GCTs) in females with CAIS remain scarce.3 Herein, we present a case of CAIS that was diagnosed soon after birth owing to the discordance of karyotype in amniocentesis and the phenotype at birth. Early diagnosis of CAIS enables serial evaluation of the gonad, from birth to puberty, offering valuable insights into the progression of testicular tumors.

A 38-year-old healthy primigravida, with no family history of congenital anomalies underwent amniocentesis, which revealed a fetal 46, XY karyotype. At 37 weeks of gestation, she delivered a newborn with female genitalia and bilateral inguinal masses. Given the discrepancy between the karyotype and phenotype, CAIS was suspected soon after birth. At the age of 1 year and 3 months, sequencing of the AR gene revealed a known mutation (c.1715A > G, p. Tyr 572Cys). Based on these results, the infant was diagnosed with CAIS and referred to our hospital at the age of 2 years for gonadal management. Following discussions with her parents and the disorders/differences of sex development team, gonadectomy was deferred until postpuberty with ongoing monitoring. Ultrasonographic evaluation of both gonads localized in the high inguinal region and blood tests for luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone, testosterone, and estradiol were performed annually. Until the age of 10 years, no significant changes were observed, except for slight increase in LH levels. At the age of 11 years, ultrasonography detected a hypoechoic area within the right gonad along with elevated LH levels. By the age of 12 years, hypoechoic areas had developed in both gonads and gradually enlarged (Fig. 1). The vascularity of the area in the right gonad was confirmed using Doppler ultrasonography. Tumor marker levels, including α-fetoprotein (AFP) and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) were within normal range. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed low-intensity nodules in both gonads (right: 8 × 5 mm; left: 3 × 3 mm) with diffusion restriction, raising concerns of potential malignancy (Fig. 2). A laparoscopic bilateral gonadectomy was performed at the age of 12 years and 6 months. The histopathological diagnosis was bilateral testicular hamartoma (Fig. 3). Microscopically, seminiferous tubule structures proliferated in a background of stromal cells. The tumor was predominantly composed of Sertoli cells and no spermatogenesis was observed. A limited number of germ cells were identified; however, they were negative for Octamer-binding transcription factor 3/4 (OCT3/4), excluding malignancy. Sertoli cells were confirmed via Inhibin-α and D2-40 staining, while calretinin-positive Leydig cells were also detected. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient commenced estrogen replacement therapy with transdermal estradiol and was stable at the 2-year follow-up. Historically, gonadectomy has been recommended at the time of diagnosis to mitigate the risk of gonadal malignancy in patients with CAIS. As the prevalence of gonadal tumors is very low in prepubertal females with CAIS,1 current practice tends to favor deferring gonadectomy until after puberty to facilitate natural pubertal development, given that testosterone undergoes aromatization to estradiol. Although the risk of malignancy increases with age, reports of invasive testicular GCTs in adults with CAIS are rare.3 In a large series of 133 patients with CAIS who underwent gonadectomy, Chaudhry et al. reported that 18 of 27 patients (67 %) with gonadal abnormalities had benign tumors. Among these, testicular hamartoma was the most common, observed in eight patients, followed by Sertoli cell adenoma in six.4 Therefore, some females with CAIS choose to retain their gonads even after puberty. Deferring gonadectomy in adulthood offers several advantages to adolescents and young adults with CAIS who are hesitant to undergo surgery, including concerns about the need for hormone replacement therapy, potential impact on physical and psychological well-being, and overall quality of life.

In previous reports, the risk of gonadal tumor in patients with CAIS who retained their gonads into adulthood ranged from 0.8 % to 22 %.1 Several factors may contribute to the overestimation of the gonadal tumor risk in patients with CAIS. First, earlier studies often misclassified cases owing to the absence of AR gene sequencing, potentially including patients with gonadal dysgenesis, which carries a higher tumor risk.5 Second, most of the data were derived from case reports rather than from large-scale cohort studies. Finally, unlike the general population, CAIS-associated germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS) may exhibit reduced malignant potential.6 Conversely, studies involving low-risk patients who underwent early gonadectomy may have overestimated the actual risk. In contrast to CAIS, PAIS exhibits a higher incidence of GCTs, with a reported rate of 15 %,1 which is likely attributable to residual androgen activity. Although seminomas and yolk sac tumors have been documented in prepubertal CAIS cases, many lack confirmatory AR gene testing. In genetically confirmed cases of CAIS, the malignancy rate is only 1.5 %, with one reported prepubertal case of GCNIS.4

A major concern with deferring gonadectomy is the lack of reliable screening modalities for detecting early malignant transformation. Ultrasound and MRI are effective for identifying benign changes such as paratesticular cysts and Sertoli cell adenomas, but they are unable to visualize precancerous GCNIS lesions.3,7 Similarly, tumor markers, including AFP and hCG, are frequently within normal limits in seminomas and GCNIS, restricting their utility as screening tools. Biopsy with OCT3/4 staining has been proposed as a surveillance strategy6; however, standardized guidelines are currently lacking.

The present case highlights the potential for safe postpubertal conservative gonadal management of CAIS. Despite the development of gonadal tumors during puberty, histopathological analysis confirmed benign lesions, suggesting that gonadectomy may be deferred with vigilant monitoring. With an increasing number of pre- and neonatal CAIS diagnoses, longitudinal imaging studies such as this one are crucial for refining the optimal timing for gonadectomy in CAIS management.

This case report represents a rare instance of long-term gonadal follow-up from birth to puberty in a patient with CAIS. Serial ultrasound evaluations facilitate the early detection of gonadal abnormalities, ultimately guiding the timing of surgical intervention. Despite the presence of hypervascular testicular nodules, histopathology confirmed the benign nature of the lesions, emphasizing that not all imaging abnormalities are indicative of malignancy. Although postpubertal gonadectomy is currently the standard approach, careful observation may enable gonadal preservation in some females with CAIS.
