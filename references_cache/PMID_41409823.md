# Pediatric high-grade neuroepithelial BCOR tandem duplicated tumor: An illustrative case.
**Authors:** Bui NE, Lubisich J, Tran DK
**Journal:** Surg Neurol Int (2025)
**DOI:** [10.25259/SNI_439_2025](https://doi.org/10.25259/SNI_439_2025)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Central nervous system (CNS) high-grade neuroepithelial tumors with BCL-6 co-repressor (BCOR) exon 15 internal tandem-duplication (ITD) are rare, aggressive pediatric neoplasms characterized by poor prognosis and unclear treatment protocols. The diagnosis requires molecular confirmation, and early neurosurgical intervention is critical.

CASE DESCRIPTION: A 2-year-old male presented with progressive fatigue, ataxia, and signs of increased intracranial pressure. Imaging revealed a large left frontal mass with midline shift. Emergent bifrontal craniotomy was performed, achieving gross total resection (GTR) without preoperative MRI due to hemodynamic instability. Subsequent deoxyribonucleic acid methylation profiling confirmed the diagnosis of a CNS BCOR-ITD. The patient was treated with intensive chemotherapy following the ACNS0334 protocol, followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. He remains neurologically intact at the past follow-up 9 months after surgery.

CONCLUSION: This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of pediatric CNS BCOR-ITD, emphasizing the need to attempt early GTR and multimodal chemotherapy in providing a meaningful functional recovery for this patient.

Central nervous system (CNS) high-grade neuroepithelial tumor (HGNET) with BCL-6 co-repressor (BCOR) exon 15 internal tandem duplication (ITD) is a rare and aggressive pediatric brain tumor first defined through deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) methylation profiling in 2016.[ 9 ] These tumors primarily affect infants and young children, most often under 5 years of age,[ 3 ] and are now classified as embryonal tumors in the 5th edition of the World Health Organization Classification of CNS Tumors (WHO CNS5).[ 5 ] CNS BCORITD tumors are often misdiagnosed on initial histology due to their morphologic overlap with other embryonal or high-grade gliomas, and definitive diagnosis requires molecular confirmation, either by RNA-seq and DNA methylation profiling or reverse transcriptase quantitative polymerase chain reaction of the in-frame exon 15 tandem duplication of the last exon of the BCOR gene.[ 1 ]

Clinically, patients typically present with symptoms related to increased intracranial pressure (ICP) or focal neurological deficits. Radiographic findings often demonstrate a large, well-circumscribed mass with variable contrast enhancement and midline shift. Despite a distinct molecular signature, treatment remains non-standardized, with management strategies extrapolated from other pediatric high-grade neoplasms. Multimodal treatment is generally pursued, incorporating maximal safe resection, high-dose chemotherapy, and radiotherapy when appropriate.

In this report, we present the case of a 2-year-old male diagnosed with CNS BCOR-ITD, treated successfully with gross total resection (GTR) and intensive chemotherapy followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell rescue. This case illustrates the critical role of emergent surgical decision-making, highlights the diagnostic limitations of frozen pathology, and contributes to the growing body of knowledge on this rare tumor entity.

A 2-year-old male infant with no significant past medical history presented to an external emergency department with 2 days of progressive fatigue, ataxia, decreased oral intake, and headache. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the head revealed a large left frontal brain mass [Figure 1], prompting transfer to our institution for advanced care. On arrival, the patient was lethargic and bradycardic, rendering him unable to tolerate a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) due to hemodynamic instability. A repeat CT scan indicated a large left frontal and central mass extending into the ventricle, with a 5.5 cm rightward midline shift and extensive vasogenic edema. The patient was emergently taken to the operating room for the right posterior external ventricular drain (EVD) placement to manage ICP.

Postoperative contrast-enhanced (c) axial T1 magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and (d) coronal T1 MRI demonstrating gross total resection through a bifrontal craniotomy of the tumor with the resection cavity (white arrow) in the left central area.

Despite EVD placement, he remained unstable and was instead taken to surgery without a preceding MRI. The patient underwent a bifrontal craniotomy for tumor resection, utilizing neuronavigation and neuromonitoring. Postoperatively, he exhibited no neurological deficits and displayed purposeful movement in all extremities. A postoperative MRI confirmed the GTR. Initial frozen pathological samples raised concerns for a low-grade glioma. An EVD clamp trial was performed, revealing intermittent ICP elevations; however, it was eventually removed 4 days post-surgery without complications.

Nearly, 1 month after surgical resection, DNA methylation profiling and RNA sequencing confirmed the diagnosis of CNS BCOR-ITD. Following multidisciplinary discussion, including consultation with external pediatric neurooncology experts, the patient was enrolled in the Children’s Oncology Group ACNS0334 protocol. This treatment regimen consists of intensive induction chemotherapy with high-dose methotrexate followed by consolidation with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). This protocol was initially developed for children <36 months of age with primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PNET) or medulloblastoma and was used in this case by analogy, as there are no HGNET protocols or trials. Focal radiation therapy was planned for a later stage, contingent on patient age, and treatment response.

The patient completed the first induction cycle with high-dose methotrexate, followed by etoposide and cyclophosphamide with Mesna. Methotrexate clearance was achieved within the expected timeframe. During this phase, he developed a febrile rash, prompting discontinuation of cefepime due to concern for hypersensitivity.

Several weeks later, the patient underwent his first consolidation cycle with autologous HSCT following conditioning with carboplatin and thiotepa. Neutrophil engraftment occurred 13 days post-infusion without significant complications. At the most recent follow-up, he demonstrates improved oral intake, intact mobility, strength, and sensation, without cranial nerve deficit. Two additional cycles of HSCT are planned, each preceded by a 4-week conditioning regimen and spaced at least 28 days apart.

CNS HGNET s with BCOR exon 15 ITD are recently characterized neoplasms primarily affecting infants and young children. They were first delineated from the broad and now outdated category of CNS PNETs by Sturm et al.[ 9 ] using DNA methylation profiling on a large cohort of 323 tumors. This new classification recategorized previously undifferentiated PNETs into four new tumors based on molecular features: (1) CNS neuroblastoma with forkhead box R2 activation, (2) CNS Ewing sarcoma-family tumor with Capicua transcriptional repressor alteration, (3) CNS HGNET with meningioma 1 alteration and (4) CNS HGNET with BCOR alteration. We refer to these HGNET s as CNS BCOR-ITD to remain consistent with the WHO CNS5 classification.[ 1 , 5 ] In contrast to the apparent loss-of-function mutations described previously in medulloblastoma,[ 4 ] internal tandem duplication represents a unique oncogenic mechanism that results in increased expression of the B-cell lymphoma BCL6 corepressor (BCOR).[ 8 , 9 ] This molecular characteristic is shared by other mesenchymal tumors, such as primitive myxoid mesenchymal tumor, and is present in nearly 85% of clear cell sarcomas of the kidney,[ 11 ] suggesting a possible mesenchymal origin of these tumors.[ 3 ] CNS BCOR-ITD lacks established therapeutic standards due to rarity, and long-term outcomes remain guarded. Patients diagnosed with these neoplasms experience high rates of recurrence. Arsenic trioxide with radiotherapy has been proposed as a treatment which initially yielded positive results in two patients; however, both eventually developed recurrence or metastasis.[ 6 ] A Japanese cohort reported improved survival in patients who underwent radiotherapy, but early radiation in children under three remains controversial due to neurodevelopmental risks, and was not employed in our patient’s case for this reason.[ 12 ] Intensity-modulated radiotherapy and proton beam therapy offer reduced rates of neuro-cognitive toxicity and are being explored for the treatment of pediatric brain tumors.[ 11 ] On a molecular level, CNS BCOR-ITD is believed to co-activate the Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) and Wnt pathways, implicating potential targets for novel therapies.[ 7 ] At present, no targeted or immunotherapies are validated for this tumor type, but research is ongoing.

Radiologically, these tumors have been described as well-circumscribed, often supratentorial, heterogeneous masses without peripheral edema. There may be a central cystic component, hemorrhage, or focal central necrosis. Some tumors demonstrate variable contrast enhancement and diffusion restriction, but lack the ring-enhancing pattern of glioblastoma.[ 2 , 3 ] In our case, the large well-demarcated heterogeneous mass with midline shift is consistent with prior descriptions of rapid expansile growth and mass effect at presentation. The absence of a preoperative MRI in our patient limited advanced imaging characterization, emphasizing the need for clinical flexibility in these scenarios.

Pathologically, most of these tumors are highly cellular, vascular, and display perivascular pseudorosettes reminiscent of ependymomas. However, these rosettes lack glial fibrillary acidic protein, characteristic of true ependymomas. Immunohistochemically, they are negative for neuronal markers, such as synaptophysin, despite morphologic resemblance to embryonal tumors. Our patient’s tumor demonstrated microcalcifications, microcystic changes, and oval to spindle-shaped cells with pseudorosette formation on hematoxylin and eosin staining. DNA methylation profiling and RNA sequencing were required for full evaluation and diagnosis.

GTR is a critical therapeutic goal in CNS BCOR-ITD, as it is strongly correlated with improved progression-free and overall survival and is a good prognosis factor.[ 10 ] In this case, emergent surgical resection was performed due to signs of impending herniation and unstable intracranial dynamics. Neuronavigation and neuromonitoring facilitated safe resection, even in the absence of preoperative MRI. Unfortunately, due to the emergent nature of our patient’s presentation, he did not undergo staging before surgery. The authors of this paper advocate for spinal MRI in stable patients for staging of these tumors. Postoperatively, our patient recovered without neurologic deficits, and MRI investigation of the brain and spine performed on postoperative day 1 confirmed complete resection without metastasis. Due to the patient’s young age and high-grade pathology, adjuvant therapy was initiated under the ACNS0334 protocol, incorporating high-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy followed by autologous stem cell transplantation. The aim was to delay radiotherapy and mitigate long-term neurotoxicity while achieving cytoreduction.

CNS HGNET s with BCOR ITD remain one of the most diagnostically challenging and clinically aggressive entities in pediatric neuro-oncology. This case illustrates the critical importance of early surgical intervention, even under emergent conditions, and the value of molecular diagnostics in clarifying pathology when histology alone is inconclusive. GTR, integrated with risk-adapted chemotherapy and stem cell rescue, can offer meaningful disease control – even in very young children for whom radiotherapy may be contraindicated.

For the neurosurgeon, this case reinforces two essential principles: First, that operative timing and precision remain foundational to survival in pediatric intracranial tumors; and second, that effective treatment increasingly depends on recognizing the molecular identity of tumors that may appear deceptively similar on imaging or frozen pathology. As our diagnostic tools and systemic therapies evolve, the neurosurgeon’s role remains indispensable in the care of these complex patients.
