Interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) is a poorly understood and 
underdiagnosed syndrome of chronic bladder/pelvic pain with urinary frequency 
and urgency. Though IC/BPS can be hereditary, little is known of its genetic 
etiology. Using the eMERGE data, we confirmed known phenotypic associations such 
as gastroesophageal reflux disease and irritable bowel syndrome and detected new 
associations, including osteoarthrosis/osteoarthritis and Barrett's esophagus. 
An exome wide ultra-rare variants analysis in 348 IC/BPS and 11,981 controls 
extended the previously reported association with ATP2C1 and ATP2A2, implicated 
in Mendelian desquamating skin disorders, but did not provide evidence for other 
previously proposed pathogenic pathways such as bladder development, nociception 
or inflammation. Pathway analysis detected new associations with 
"anaphase-promoting complex-dependent catabolic process", the "regulation of 
MAPK cascade" and "integrin binding". These findings suggest perturbations in 
biological networks for epithelial integrity and cell cycle progression in 
IC/BPS pathogenesis, and provide a roadmap for its future investigation.

Abstract 28 
Interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) is a poorly understood and 29 
underdiagnosed syndrome of chronic bladder/pelvic pain with urinary frequency and 30 
urgency. Though IC/BPS can be hereditary, little is known of its genetic etiology.  Using 31 
the eMERGE data, we confirmed known phenotypic associations such as 32 
gastroesophageal reflux disease and irritable bowel syndrome and detected new 33 
associations, including osteoarthrosis/osteoarthritis and Barrett’s esophagus. An exome 34 
wide ultra-rare variants analysis in 348 IC/BPS and 11,981 controls extended the 35 
previously reported association with ATP2C1 and ATP2A2, implicated in Mendelian 36 
desquamating skin disorders, but did not provide evidence for other previously proposed 37 
pathogenic pathways such as bladder development, nociception or inflammation. 38 
Pathway analysis detected new associations with “anaphase-promoting complex-39 
dependent catabolic process”, the “regulation of MAPK cascade” and “integrin binding”. 40 
These findings suggest perturbations in biological networks for epithelial integrity and cell 41 
cycle progression in IC/BPS pathogenesis, and provide a roadmap for its future 42 
investigation. 43

twins are correlated (21). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) previously associated 60 
with conditions often co-morbid with IC/BPS were more prevalent in individuals with 61 
IC/BPS than in controls (22). Finally, our group has previously identified Mendelian 62 
disorders within an IC/BPS cohort (23).  63

Previous literature supports an association with nociplastic pain disorders (e.g., irritable 65 
bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia, and encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome 66 
[ME/CFS]), anxiety, and depression (4, 24-26). Other previously noted associations 67 
include reflux esophagitis (27), urinary calculus (28), allergic rhinitis (29), and vomiting 68 
(30). 69 
This study, to our knowledge, is the largest exome sequencing (ES) study of 70 
individuals with IC/BPS to date. Using a new phenotype database, we reaffirm the 71 
association of previously identified co-morbid phenotypes while identifying new 72 
associations. We have identified genes with multiple carriers of ultra-rare deleterious 73 
variants for which no control carriers were identified. We used genes with excess ultra-74 
rare damaging variants in individuals with IC/BPS to interrogate gene pathways. We 75 
identified significant associations in the Gene Ontology (GO) biological process pathway 76 
“anaphase-promoting complex-dependent catabolic process” (GO:0031145) and 77 
identified a significant association with the “transport of small molecules” (R-HSA-78 
382551) Reactome pathway (31-35). This study strengthens the prior association of 79 
IC/BPS with deleterious variants in genes with known Mendelian disease associations 80 
and takes another step toward unraveling the etiology of this debilitating disorder. 81

Phenome-wide co-morbidity analysis 101 
Prior epidemiological analyses identified many comorbidities with IC/BPS including 102 
fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, and anxiety (4, 24-26, 37). We performed a 103 
phenome-wide comorbidity analysis across 1,816 phecodes in the Electronic Medical 104 
Records and Genomics (eMERGE) network dataset (38, 39) (Fig. 1), comparing 360 105 
individuals (316 female) with IC/BPS to 99,249 individuals (53,224 female) without 106 
IC/BPS (Data S1). We validated prior comorbidities, including dysuria (odds ratio [OR] 107 
12.3, P = 5.1e-23) (1, 2), polyuria (OR 77.2, P = 2.1e-49) (1, 2), irritable bowel syndrome 108 
(OR 6.5, P = 6.3e-7) (40), esophagitis (OR 2.7, P = 8.3e-6) (27), gastroesophageal reflux 109 
disease (OR 2.8, P = 1.3e-5) (27), and allergic rhinitis (OR 3.8, P = 1.1e-6) (41). Novel 110 
associations were identified such as Barrett's esophagus (OR 11, P = 1.9e-5) and 111 
osteoarthrosis/osteoarthritis (OR 3.2, P = 1.1e-5). These provide new candidate biological 112 
pathways to investigate for IC/BPS. Prior phenotypic associations such as chronic pain 113 
(OR 2.8, P = 0.00030) (24) and anxiety (OR 2.2, P = 0.0010) (42), among others, were 114 
suggestive.  115 
Gene-based collapsing 116 
We first explored gene-based collapsing to identify associations of case/control status 117 
with individual genes (43-46). Each gene-based collapsing model was defined by a 118 
qualifying variant (Table S1). We created models with both ultra-rare (absent from control 119 
databases) and rare (minor allele frequency [MAF] < 1%) qualifying variants. We created 120 
models for damaging effects (damaging missense and predicted loss-of-function) and 121 
synonymous (presumed control). All models showed limited genomic inflation (/i1  < 1.1) 122 
suggesting good ancestry sub-structure matching (Fig. S1 – S2, Data S2 – S3). 123 
In our ultra-rare and rare damaging models, no association exceeded study-wide 124 
significance (Fig. 2, Fig. S3, Data S4 – S5). We highlighted seven genes (Table 2) in our 125 
ultra-rare functional model which had more than one case carrier of a qualifying variant 126 
(QV) and no control carriers (5,939 genes harbored multiple control carriers and no case 127 
carriers, data not shown). The top association was DNAAF4 (MIM: 608706) for which 3 128 
cases harbored ultra-rare functional variants and no controls harbored variants. DNAAF4 129 
is implicated as an autosomal dominant risk factor for dyslexia (47). Biallelic variants in 130 
DNAAF4 cause ciliary dyskinesia (48). Among the remaining genes with multiple case 131 
carriers and no control carriers, the gene most consistent with the known prior disease 132 
associations with IC/BPS is HTR7 (MIM: 182137) which encodes a serotonin receptor 133 
implicated in visceral hypersensitivity in irritable bowel syndrome (49). Other genes with 134 
at least 2 case carriers and no control carriers include CALB2 (MIM: 114051), SERPINF2 135 
(MIM: 613168), STX6 (MIM: 603944), TBC1D26 (MIM: NA), and MNT (MIM: 603039) (50-136 
54). None of the 13 individuals carrying variants in the above-mentioned genes had 137 
enrolled family members, and therefore we were unable to assess for segregation. Of the 138 
seven genes, two have a prior disease association but due to incomplete phenotyping 139 
information, we could not ascertain the presence or absence of related phenotypes in 140 
IC/BPS affected carriers. The more inclusive rare damaging model did not yield more 141 
compelling associations (Fig. S3, Data S4). 142

Discussion  232 
We conducted a phenome-wide comorbidity analysis, exome-wide analysis of ultra-233 
rare and rare damaging variants in IC/BPS encompassing collapsing-based gene burden 234 
tests, single variant analyses, gene-set and network-based pathway analyses, family-235 
based studies and CNV diagnostic evaluation to identify genetic risk factors for IC/BPS. 236 
The unbiased studies uncovered new comorbidities and new biological pathways likely to 237 
be important in the etiology of IC/BPS.  238 
Phenotype analyses using phecodes from eMERGE validated known associations 239 
while broadening the possibility of additional related phenotypes (Fig. 1). Confirmed 240 
associations included dysuria (1, 2), polyuria (1, 2), irritable bowel syndrome (30, 98), 241 
allergies (41), nausea (42), and myalgia (30). An association with GERD was also 242 
confirmed (27, 40). Many previously reported comorbid conditions for IC/BPS, including 243 
migraine and psychiatric disorders (anxiety disorders, depression, and mood disorders) 244 
were not significantly associated after correction. This is likely due to the small sample 245 
size and in no way negates the reality of these conditions in the IC/BPS population. 246

discontinuing treatment (103). 259 
Although to our knowledge, this is the largest genetic study of IC/BPS, none of the 260 
signals in the gene-based collapsing analyses and ExWAS reached exome-wide 261 
significance, reflecting the genetic heterogeneity of disease (Fig. 2, Fig. 4). Our study 262 
thus nominates multiple new candidate genes that will require confirmation in 263 
independent studies including some without current Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man 264 
(OMIM) disease associations such as HTR7, MNT, CALB2, STX6, and TBC1D26 (Table 265 
2) (104). HTR7 encodes a 5-HT receptor implicated in visceral hypersensitivity in IBS (49, 266 
105). Individuals with IC/BPS are at increased risk of having IBS (24). Another gene of 267 
interest is DNAAF4 (also known as DNAAF and DYX1C1), which is linked to dyslexia 268 
susceptibility and ciliary dyskinesia (48, 106). Interestingly, a significant increase in 269 
deleterious variants in this and other cilia biogenesis genes was identified in a study of 270 
familial urinary bladder cancer (107). In one family with multiple affected family members 271 
(Table S5, S6), we identified a rare segregating variant in PIEZO2, encoding a 272 
mechanosensitive ion channel implicated in urethra/bladder control of micturition (108). 273 
The analysis of the small number of available trios (Table S6) yielded a de novo variant in 274 
PLXNA4, which has been linked to a new form of bladder epithelial cells and discussed in 275 
the context of IC/BPS (94). Importantly, we confirmed the association with ATP2C1 and 276 
ATP2A2, which have been implicated in the desquamating skin disorders Hailey-Hailey 277 
and Darier disease (Table S2). ATP2C1 and ATP2A2 are expressed in the urothelium, 278 
suggesting the calcium transporter-related epithelial injury observed in these syndromes 279 
may also extend to the bladder urothelium and produce the symptoms of IC/BPS (23, 40).  280 
In the setting of genetic heterogeneity, pathway-based tests can improve analytic 281 
power by detecting signals that are distributed across biologically interconnected genes 282 
(Table 2, Fig. 3). These analyses provided no support for previously implicated pathways 283 
such as urogenital development, pain perception, depression, and irritable bowel 284 
syndrome. However, the NHC based analysis yielded multiple new candidate genes and 285 
pathways (Table 3, Data S7). SMAD3, which is included in the most enriched gene 286 
cluster, has been implicated as a therapeutic target for interstitial cystitis (109, 110) and in 287 
constipation-associated neuropathic pain (111). Notably, additional genes within the 288 
cluster (ANAPC7 [MIM: 606949], CDC20 [MIM: 603618], and SKIL [MIM: 165340]) are 289 
linked to bladder cancer (112-114), and FOXO1 is tied to urothelial fibrosis (115). Other 290 
top pathways included the positive regulation of MAPK cascade (Table 3) and the 291

Phenotype analysis 346 
Phenotype data was obtained through eMERGE-III (39). To test the association 347 
between interstitial cystitis and other phenotypes phenome-wide, we first harmonized the 348 
coded diagnoses data by converting all available ICD-10-CM codes to the ICD-9-CM 349 
system. This approach was motivated by the facts that the great majority of data for 350 
eMERGE-III participants is already coded using the ICD-9 system; ICD-10 codes are 351 
more granular and thus, a reverse conversion is more prone to mapping errors; and the 352 
current PheWAS R library supports only ICD-9 codes (38, 117). The data set initially 353 
contained 102,739 individuals with 19,411 ICD9 codes. 5 codes were excluded (X999, 354 
NoD.x, NoI9, IMO0001, IMO0002) and individuals with unavailable or ambiguous sex 355 
were excluded. Subjects with ICD9 code 595.1 were considered individuals affected with 356 
IC/BPS (360 individuals). Individuals without ICD9 code 595.1. were considered 357 
unaffected (99,249 individuals). ICD9 codes were then transformed into 1,817 unique 358 
phecodes with descriptions using the mapCodesToPhecodes and addPhecodeInfo 359 
functions in the R PheWAS library (118). All individuals with ICD9 code 595.1 were 360 
assigned phecode ”chronic interstitial cystitis” (592.13). For all phecodes other than 361 
595.1, case/control carrier status was determined using createPhenotypes function in the 362 
R PheWAS library using default settings including add.phecode.exclusions = T but 363 
modified min.code.count = 10 so that the case definition requires a minimum of 10 ICD-9 364 
codes from the “case” grouping of each phecode.  365 
The PheWAS function in the R PheWAS library was utilized to implement logistic 366 
regression. The package uses predefined “control” groups for each phecode. In total, all 367 
1816 phecodes were tested using logistic regression with each phecode patient-control 368 
status as an outcome and chronic interstitial cystitis adjusted for sex, age, recruitment 369 
site, total ICD9 codes, and 9 ancestry principal components as a covariate. To establish 370 
significant disease associations in PheWAS, we set the Bonferroni-corrected statistical 371 
significance threshold at 2.8×10−05 (0.05 divided by 1816), correcting for 1816 372 
independent phecodes tested. PheWAS plots were created using phewasManhattan 373 
function from the R PheWAS library. 374

Collapsing Analysis 408 
Collapsing analysis is used to identify an association between cases status and rare-409 
variant carrier status at the gene-level (43-46). We used the following steps: i) choose 410 
high-quality cases and selected controls enrolled broadly as “healthy family members”, 411 
“control mild neuropsychiatric disease” or “control”, and remove related individuals, ii) 412 
create clusters with cases and controls matched using geographic ancestry, iii) harmonize 413 
coverage separately in each cluster, iv) specify QV criteria to create collapsing models, v) 414 
assign indicator variable (0/1) to each case and control based on the absence/present of 415 
a QV in the gene, tested gene set, or variant, vi) test for association between case/control 416 
status and indicator variable using Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) test, and vii) 417 
visualize results. 418

Coverage Harmonization 456 
Because the included cases and controls have varied coverage throughout the coding 457 
region (exome vs. genome sequencing, multiple capture kits), we reduce bias introduced 458 
by unbalanced coverage between cases and controls using “coverage harmonization” 459 
(46). We have already screened samples to ensure that 90% of the consensus coding 460

where the absolute difference in percentages of cases compared to controls with at least 463

coverage differences caused by different capture kits, inclusion of both ES and GS or 465 
sequencing depth in general (44, 142, 143). Coverage harmonization is performed on 466 
each cluster independently (Fig. S1). 467

Quantile-Quantile Plots and Genomic Inflation Factor λ  501 
To visualize the distribution of statistical associations we created quantile-quantile 502 
(QQ) plots. The synonymous model was used as a negative control for gene-based 503 
collapsing (Figures S1 – S2). We defined a study-wide Bonferroni multiplicity-adjusted 504 
significance threshold of p < 5.4 x 10-7 (0.05 / [18,286 CCDS genes × 2 non-synonymous 505 
models + 55,607 variant sites in ExWAS]). No individual association met study-wide 506 
significance. 507 
QQ plots for collapsing analyses were created for each model by plotting expected vs. 508 
observed p-values for each gene in the collapsing model. As described previously 509 
empirical (permutation-based) expected probability distributions were generated for each 510 
model independently (44, 45). Within each cluster, the case and control labels were 511 
randomly permuted while the rest of the gene by sample matrix was kept fixed. The 512 
number of newly randomly labeled cases/controls with and without a QV per gene were 513 
used for the CMH test to test for an association between case/control status and QV 514 
status while controlling for cluster membership (see Collapsing by Gene and Statistical 515 
Enrichment). An empirical distribution of 1,000 p-values for each gene was created by 516 
repeating this process 1,000 times. For each permutation the p-values were ordered. The 517 
mean of each rank-ordered estimate across the 1,000 permutations (i.e., the average 1st 518 
order statistic, the average 2nd order statistic, etc.) represented the empirical estimates 519 
of the expected ordered p-values. The genomic inflation factor λ  based on the 520 
permutation-based expected p-values was estimated using a regression method as 521 
described previously (149, 150). 522
