# Homozygosity for a variant in SLC10A2 and infancy onset severe fat-soluble vitamin deficiency due to bile acid malabsorption.
**Authors:** Rungoe C, Stender S, Dalila N, Bartels ED, Jakobsen C
**Journal:** JPGN Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jpr3.70030](https://doi.org/10.1002/jpr3.70030)

## Content

We present a case of a young female patient with persistent and severe fat-soluble vitamin deficiency since infancy. Despite extensive investigations during childhood, the underlying cause remained elusive. The patient was generally asymptomatic while receiving continuous vitamin subsidy. Exome sequencing performed at age 18 revealed a homozygous missense variant (Pro65Leu) in the gene SLC10A2, which encodes a bile acid transporter in the ileum. A bile retention scan showed severe bile acid malabsorption, with only 1.6% of radioactively labeled bile acids retained 7 days after intake. Treatment with a bile acid sequestrant was attempted but discontinued due to side effects and no significant effect on intestinal vitamin uptake. This case highlights the importance of early genetic testing in patients with unexplained fat-soluble vitamin deficiency. It also emphasizes the need for further research to elucidate the clinical spectrum and management of primary bile acid malabsorption due to SLC10A2 deficiency.

The fat‐soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K (ADEK) are essential for human life.
1
 Deficiency of one or several of these vitamins is common and is most often secondary to insufficient dietary intake. Other causes include bile obstruction, chronic liver disease, or impaired intestinal uptake. In some cases, however, the cause of fat‐soluble vitamin deficiency remains elusive.

Here, we describe a young female patient with sustained and severe deficiency of fat‐soluble vitamins. The patient's parents were of Pakistani origin and were first cousins. Pregnancy and delivery were uncomplicated. Her birthweight was 2800 g at gestational age week 40+ (appropriate for age). The family history was without known hereditary diseases. The patient's mother was diagnosed with hypothyroidism and the patient's younger sister had childhood onset diabetes. The patient's first hospitalization was at 10 months of age when she was admitted with a nonfebrile generalized seizure related to hypocalcemia, which was treated with diazepam and calcium subsidy. At the same time, she was diagnosed with hypophosphatemia and increased levels of parathyroid hormone, as well as deficiency of fat‐soluble vitamins, including severe vitamin D deficiency. An X‐ray was performed and showed rachitis. The patient started treatment with fat‐soluble ADEK vitamins (liquid and supplement as tablets) and the levels normalized. Although she was followed in a specialized care setting during childhood with regular controls, measurements, and continuous subsidy of fat‐soluble vitamins, the cause of her vitamin deficiency remained unknown despite a range of investigations. Fecal elastase, plasma concentrations of lipids and lipoproteins, and fat excretion in feces were all normal. The enzyme activity of maltase and sucrase was normal but the patient was found to be without lactase activity. There were no signs of celiac disease (blood test and duodenal biopsy were normal), and gastroscopy was normal. Biopsy of her gastric mucosa revealed Helicobacter pylori, which was left untreated as she had no symptoms and the gastroscopy including histopathology was normal. Borgstrøm's test showed low levels of pancreatic enzymes, which led to suspicion of exocrine pancreas dysfunction. Pancreatic enzyme substitution was initiated, but the treatment was without effect and therefore stopped. General symptoms were sparse, though damage to the tooth enamel due to low vitamin D and calcium during infancy was evident. Growth and weight were consistently below target with final height at 155.5 cm (−2.22 SD). Menarche was not delayed. The patient was mostly asymptomatic and only rarely reported abdominal pain. She had normal stool frequency and consistency with no steatorrhea. The physical and neurological development was also normal. However, she needed continuous subsidy with ADEK, and the levels dropped markedly when she was noncompliant, or during doctor‐induced attempts to lower vitamin‐doses. The lowest measured levels of the respective vitamins were: vitamin A 0.66 µmol/l (reference range: 0.91–2.51 µmol/l), vitamin D2 + D3 < 12 nmol/L (reference range: 50–160 nmol/l), vitamin E 7.7 µmol/L (reference range: 14–23 µmol/l), and vitamin K < 0.11 nmol/L (reference range: 0.29–3.33 nmol/l).

At 18 years of age, the patient underwent whole‐exome sequencing. Her exome was screened for all homozygous variants with a global allele frequency <5 × 10−4 in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), and for homozygosity for missense, nonsense, or frameshift variants with an allele frequency of <0.005. An autosomal recessive mutation was considered the most likely cause of the patient's disease, based on her parent's consanguinity. We identified homozygosity for a rare missense variant in SLC10A2: c.194 C > T, p.(Pro65Leu). SLC10A2 encodes a protein that is expressed in the ileum where it mediates the intestinal absorption of bile acids (Figure 1).
2
, 
3
 Homozygosity or compound heterozygosity for mutations in SLC10A2 has previously been found to cause bile acid malabsorption in pediatric patients, including severe deficiency of fat‐soluble vitamins.
3
, 
4
 The homozygous missense variant found in the patient was therefore considered a likely cause of her sustained vitamin deficiency, secondary to bile acid malabsorption. A Se‐75‐HCAT capsule bile retention scan confirmed severe bile acid malabsorption, with only 1.6% retained after 7 days. Plasma levels of bile acids were 8 µmol/L (reference <19 µmol/L). Oral treatment with the bile acid sequestrant cholestyramine was subsequently initiated based on the reported efficacy of this therapy in another patient with SLC10A2 deficiency‐related bile acid malabsorption.
4
 After 3 months of treatment, the patient reported having stopped taking cholestyramine due to side effects (nausea and diarrhea). Her vitamin ADEK were low and the treatment with cholestyramine was stopped. The patient, now 19 years old, currently receives vitamin subsidy (“DEKAS plus,” a liquid multivitamin, as well as tablet supplement with zink 22 mg three times per day, iron supplement 50 mg/day, vitamin D 35 µg/day, and vitamin K 10 mg/day) and is asymptomatic.

The biological role of SLC10A2 in the intestinal uptake of bile acids. BAs are excreted from the liver to the intestine via the bile. Approximately 95% of the excreted BAs are reclaimed from the intestine via uptake by SLC10A2, a transmembrane transport protein which is expressed in the brush border of the enterocytes. Bile acids circle back to the liver via the blood, completing the enterohepatic circulation. BAs, bile acids.

Bile is synthesized from cholesterol in the liver and excreted to the small intestine where it facilitates absorption of fat‐soluble vitamins and cholesterol. More than 90% of bile acid is normally reabsorbed in the ileum via a transporter encoded by the gene SLC10A2. The patient described here was homozygous for SLC10A2 Pro65Leu, a missense variant located in the first intracellular loop of the encoded protein (Figure 2A).
5
 The variant has a frequency of 0.007 among individuals of South Asian ancestry and a frequency of 0.001 in individuals of European ancestry. The Pro65Leu variant is predicted to be pathogenic by AlphaMissense (Figure 2B).
6
 The amino acid residue 65 is completely conserved in vertebrates from human to zebrafish, indicating that proline at this position is critical for the normal function of SLC10A2 (Figure 2C). Homozygosity or compound heterozygosity for other missense variants in SLC10A2 has been described as the cause of primary bile acid malabsorption type 1 (PBA1; OMIM: 61291) in two patients in the literature.

 The patient was compound heterozygous for two missense‐variants in SLC10A2 (Thr262Met and Leu243Pro). Functional studies in cells confirmed that the variants caused a loss of normal SLC10A2 transport activity.
3
 In 2021, Qie et al. described a 3‐month‐old female infant with persistent diarrhea and stunted growth.
4
 Exome sequencing revealed homozygosity for a missense variant (Cys105Arg) in SLC10A2. After starting treatment with cholestyramine, the diarrhea subsided, and the patient's growth caught up. Compound heterozygosity for a missense and nonsense variant in SLC10A2 (Pro251Leu and Cys106Ter) was recently reported in a patient with allergic colitis.

This case highlights the importance of considering SLC10A2 deficiency in patients with unexplained fat‐soluble vitamin deficiency and emphasizes the need for early genetic testing in such cases, particularly when other common causes have been excluded. It also emphasizes the need for further research to elucidate the clinical spectrum and management of bile acid malabsorption due to SLC10A2 deficiency.
