# Camptodactyly-arthropathy-coxa vara-pericarditis (CACP) syndrome masquerading as juvenile idiopathic arthritis: a case series with a novel PRG4 variant and emphasis on bone health.
**Authors:** Fathi M, Chitzan-Zadeh K, Darbandi M, Tababtabaei SA, Mavalizadeh F
**Journal:** Pediatr Rheumatol Online J (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12969-026-01251-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12969-026-01251-2)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Camptodactyly-arthropathy-coxa vara-pericarditis syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by loss-of-function variants in the proteoglycan 4 gene, which encodes lubricin. It is frequently misdiagnosed as juvenile idiopathic arthritis, leading to prolonged immunosuppressive therapy.

CASE PRESENTATION: We report two unrelated boys born to consanguineous parents who presented with progressive large-joint swelling and were initially diagnosed with juvenile idiopathic arthritis. Both received multiple disease-modifying and biologic agents without clinical response. Inflammatory markers remained persistently normal, and radiographs showed preserved joint spaces without erosive changes. Camptodactyly developed later in the disease course. One patient developed pericardial effusion and showed a reduced bone mineral density on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry with limited hip motion and acetabular changes; the other patient showed only a qualitative radiographic image of a reduced bone mineral density but was not confirmed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. Whole-exome sequencing identified a previously reported homozygous frameshift variant in proteoglycan 4 gene (c.2208del; p.Thr737ProfsTer175) in the first patient and a homozygous nonsense variant (c.4104T > A; p.Tyr1368Ter) in the second which was absent from ClinVar and Varsome (accessed April 2026) and it was classified as pathogenic by ACMG/AMP.

CONCLUSIONS: Camptodactyly-arthropathy-coxa vara-pericarditis syndrome should be considered in children with non-inflammatory arthropathy unresponsive to immunosuppressive therapy. The recognition of the broader phenotypic burden including reduced bone mineral density supports the need for a standard method to assess bone health beyond the classical tetrad approach. Early whole-exome sequencing can shorten the diagnostic journey and redirect management toward appropriate supportive care.

Camptodactyly–arthropathy–coxa vara–pericarditis syndrome (CACP) is a rare autosomal recessive arthropathy caused by loss-of-function variants in the proteoglycan 4 gene (PRG4), which encodes lubricin, the principal boundary lubricant of synovial joints and a glycoprotein also expressed in the pericardium and pleural surfaces [1]. Lubricin deficiency leads to non-inflammatory synovial hyperplasia, progressive joint enlargement, flexion contractures, and a characteristic radiograph pattern of preserved joint spaces without erosive change [2]. The classical CACP tetrad compromising camptodactyly, non-inflammatory arthropathy, coxa vara, and pericarditis may evolve gradually over years. Reduced bone mineral density (BMD) had also been increasingly recognized as part of its broader phenotypic spectrum [3].

Because early-onset joint swelling and contractures closely resemble juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), CACP is frequently misclassified. This exposes affected children to prolonged and ineffective courses of immunosuppressive therapy [4].

In contrast to JIA, however, CACP is characterized by persistently normal inflammatory markers, limited pain relative to the degree of joint enlargement, absence of erosive changes on imaging, and a poor sustained response to disease-modifying or biologic agents [5].

Diagnostic delay may therefore carry the dual cost of unnecessary therapeutic escalation and continued progression of structural deformity. Early molecular confirmation can resolve this diagnostic ambiguity and redirect management toward supportive measures, particularly in populations with high rates of consanguinity, where autosomal recessive disorders are more prevalent [6].

Here, we report two boys initially managed as JIA who were ultimately diagnosed with CACP syndrome by whole-exome sequencing (WES). We highlight the clinical and radiographic clues that distinguish mechanical from inflammatory arthropathy, describe an additional; truncating PRG4 variant, and emphasize the importance of assessing bone health as a part of routine follow-up.

An 8-year-old boy, born to consanguineous parents, first presented to our rheumatology clinic at the age of three with joint swelling and difficulty performing daily activities. There was no family history of musculoskeletal or cardiac disorders. According to the patient’s mother, the clinical course began at one year of age with the development of flexion deformities of the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints of the third and fourth fingers of the left hand. At two years of age, bilateral knee joint swelling and effusions became apparent. The mother also reported morning stiffness, functional limitation in early hours of the day, inability to sit in a cross-legged position, and difficulty using the toilet in the early morning.

The wrists and ankles were also swollen. No limb or gait abnormality was observed. Subsequent ultrasonographic evaluation of the knees demonstrated joint effusions measuring 7.5 mm in the right knee and 10 mm in the left, accompanied by mild bilateral synovial thickening. Notably, there were no associated clinical signs of active inflammation, such as tenderness or erythema on physical examination (Fig. 1A).

Laboratory investigations revealed that immunoglobulin profiles such as IgA, IgG, IgM, and IgE, as well as Wright, Coombs Wright, and 2-mercaptoethanol tests, were within normal limits. Inflammatory markers, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein, were normal. Rheumatoid factor was also within the normal range (1.1 IU/mL). No pericardial effusion was observed on transthoracic echocardiography.

Based on the presence of joint effusions, flexion contractures, and finger deformities, juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) and skeletal dysmorphia (pseudo-.

rheumatism) were considered as the initial working diagnosis. Accordingly, treatment was initiated with disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), including oral methotrexate at a dose of 15 mg/m 2/week, in combination with daily nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) using oral naproxen.

After four months of follow-up, no clinical improvement was observed despite ongoing therapy. Consequently, an intra-articular corticosteroid injection was administrated to the left knee. Due to persistent joint swelling, treatment was escalated to adalimumab (CinnoRA), administered as one injection every two weeks, in combination with oral prednisolone (5 mg/day). Two months later, the flexion contractures of fingers had resolved (Fig. 2A); however, persistent swelling of the ankles and knees remained (Fig. 1A).

Given the incomplete clinical response despite treatment and refractory joint manifestations, the patient was referred for genetic evaluation. WES identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in the PRG4 gene (NM_005807.6: c.2208del; p.Thr737ProfsTer175), consistent with the patient’s clinical phenotype. Following confirmation of the genetic diagnosis, all disease-modifying and anti-inflammatory pharmacological treatments were discontinued, and the patient was transitioned to symptomatic management and physiotherapy.

An 11-year-old boy, born to consanguineous parents, was referred to our rheumatology department at the age of five. According to the medical history, he had been under follow-up since he was 3.5 years of age at another center due to progressive knee swelling and deformity, where he was initially diagnosed with JIA. However, over time, the patient developed progressive deformities and flexion contractures of both elbows, knees, and ankles. During the period, he received multiple intra-articular corticosteroid injections involving both wrists, ankles and knees.

On physical examination, flexion contractures were observed in both elbows. Both knees, ankles, and elbows were swollen and their range of motion was limited. No joint effusion was detected on examination. The parents did not report morning stiffness. No limp or gait abnormality was observed.

All laboratory investigations, including inflammatory markers and autoimmune studies, were unremarkable. However, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated the presence of mild pericarditis.

The patient was started on oral prednisolone (1.25 mg/day), methotrexate (15 mg/m2/week), and naproxen. During follow-up, given the patient’s chronic large-joint contractures with reduced wright-bearing mobility and glucocorticoid exposure, bone health was assessed with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA). DEXA showed a lumbar spine (L1–L4) bone mineral density (BMD) Z-score of − 2.4. Therefore, the intravenous pamidronate therapy was subsequently initiated. However, it was not continued due to parental concern regarding hospitalization during the COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, the patient was lost to follow-up for approximately three years.

Upon re-presentation, the parents reported that the patient developed camptodactyly of multiple fingers (Fig. 3) along with limited range of motion of the hip joints over the preceding six months and persistent swelling of both ankles; however, flexion contractures of the knees had resolved. Based on orthopedic consultation, finger splints were prescribed.

On subsequent follow-up, DEXA was reassessed, demonstrating a Z-score of − 2.2 at the femur, while the lumbar spine BMD had normalized. Repeated echocardiogram showed no evidence of pericarditis. Pelvic radiography revealed no signs of coxa vara; however, bilateral cystic changes were noted along the acetabular regions (Fig. 4A and B).

Based on the persistent musculoskeletal manifestations and evolving deformities, skeletal dysplasia was suspected, and WES identified a homozygous nonsense variant (NM_005807.6:c.4104T > A; p.Tyr1368Ter). Following genetic confirmation, all pharmacological treatments were discontinued, and the patient was enrolled in a structural physiotherapy program focused on improving joint mobility, with continued use of finger splints.

The CACP syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive arthropathy first described by Laxer et al. and Bulutlar et al. in 1986, characterized by the tetrad of camptodactyly, non-inflammatory arthropathy, coxa vara, and pericarditis [7, 8]. It is caused by mutations in the PRG4 gene located on chromosome 1q25-31 [1, 9], which encodes lubricin, a glycoprotein produced by synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes and expressed in articular cartilage, meniscus, tendons, and non-skeletal tissues including the pericardium and pleural cavity [10]. Lubricin deficiency impairs joint lubrication and leads to synovial hyperplasia without evidence of inflammation, thereby explaining the clinical tetrad of the disease [11].

Camptodactyly is typically the initial manifestation of CACP and affects the majority of patients in early childhood. In the genotype–phenotype analysis by Yilmaz et al., involving 35 patients from 11 unrelated families, camptodactyly was frequently the earliest presenting feature, often preceding overt arthropathy, with large-joint involvement predominantly affected the knees and hips [3]. A broader pooled review by Maniscalco et al. of 146 patients confirmed these findings, with arthropathy reported in 100%, camptodactyly in 97.3%, coxa vara in 95.2%, and pericardial effusion in 15.1% of cases [6]. In present series, however, progressive large-joint swelling was the presenting symptom, and camptodactyly appeared later in the disease course. This variability underscores that the absence of early camptodactyly does not exclude CACP and reinforces the need for heightened clinical suspicion in children presenting with non-inflammatory joint disease.

The clinical overlap with JIA remains a principal diagnostic challenge in pediatric rheumatology. Both patients in this report were initially misdiagnosed with JIA on the basis of arthritis, joint contractures, and subsequent camptodactyly, and were treated with multiple immunosuppressive and biologic agents without clinical improvement. A comparable pattern has been reported in the Southern Turkey cohort by Kisla Ekinci et al., in which most patients received immunosuppressive therapy prior to genetic confirmation [4]. Accordingly, progressive joint enlargement, persistently normal inflammatory markers, and absence of systemic inflammation should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis.

Radiologic findings in CACP are typically characterized by preserved joint spaces, with erosive destruction rarely described (5% of cases) and absence of inflammatory bone marrow changes. Coxa vara deformity is nearly universal (95.2%), frequently associated with femoral changes. Bone cysts secondary to joint capsule herniation represents a characteristic feature of the syndrome, reported in 14.4% of cases. Periarticular soft tissue swelling and osteopenia are additional recognized radiographic features [12]. Published cohorts from Saudi Arabia and Europe consistently describe non-erosive arthropathy with gradual hip involvement and acetabular cystic changes [2, 13]. In the present patients, plain radiographs similarly demonstrated preserved alignment without marginal erosions or destructive changes, supporting a mechanical rather than inflammatory articular process despite prolonged immunosuppressive exposure. Notably, reduced BMD involving the phalanges and metacarpal bones was observed in the first patient, while periarticular osteopenia on lumbar radiographs was identified in the second patient, both consistent with the osteoporotic tendency reported in the literature.

In the first case, WES identified a previously reported homozygous frameshift variant in PRG4 (NM_005807.6:c.2208del; p.Thr737ProfsTer175), consistent with the established autosomal-recessive mechanism of CACP syndrome [3]. In the second case, a homozygous nonsense variant (NM_005807.6:c.4104T > A; p.Tyr1368Ter) was identified. To the best of our knowledge, the c.4104T > A (p.Tyr1368Ter) variant in PRG4 has not been previously reported and was absent from ClinVar and Varsome databases (accessed April 2026). This nonsense variant introduces a premature termination codon predicted to result in loss of function and was classified as pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria (PVS1, PM2, PP1, PP4). Parental testing confirmed heterozygous carrier status in both parents, consistent with autosomal-recessive segregation. Collectively, these findings expand the mutational spectrum of truncating PRG4 variants associated with CACP syndrome.

Hip involvement is a hallmark feature that may evolve gradually. Coxa vara deformity, defined as a reduced femoral neck-shaft angle, has been reported in 50–90% of cases across cohorts [3, 6]. Although neither patient demonstrated established coxa vara at initial evaluation. The second patient exhibited limited hip motion and acetabular changes, suggesting evolving structural involvement. These findings align with prior observations that hip deformities often develop progressively and warrant longitudinal imaging surveillance. Pericardial involvement has been reported in a minority of CACP patients, with prevalence ranging from approximately 15% in the pooled literature to 33% in some earlier series, and may be associated with fibrous pericardium thickening [5, 6]. One of the two patients developed pericarditis, consistent with the recognized but variable cardiac spectrum of the disease. Importantly, pericardial involvement may reinforce an inflammatory interpretation and prompt intensified immunosuppressive treatment; however, it should be interpreted within the context of PRG4 deficiency rather than systemic autoimmunity.

Reduced BMD is an underrecognized feature of CACP syndrome. In the Southern Turkey cohort reported by Kisla Ekinci et al., one patient demonstrated both low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and low BMD, prompting initiation of calcium and vitamin D supplementation [4]. Osteoporosis was observed in 36.3% of cases in the review by Maniscalco et al. of 146 patients [6], and the Human Phenotype Ontology catalog for CACP syndrome lists both osteoporosis and bone cysts among the recognize phenotypic features. In the second patient of the present report, DEXA evaluation demonstrated a low Z-score, consistent with these prior reports. However, in the first patient, bone involvement was only suggested by qualitative radiographic evidence of reduced phalangeal and metacarpal density, since no DEXA had been performed. Therefore, this finding is descriptive. The pathogenesis of BMD reduction in children with CACP is likely to be multifactorial, reflecting reduced weight-bearing secondary to chronic arthropathy and contractures, compounded by prolonged glucocorticoid exposure prior to genetic confirmation. These findings support routine screening with DEXA rather than incidental radiographic imaging as a standard method of evaluating bone health in children with CACP syndrome, particularly those with prolonged immobility or prior glucocorticoid exposure, alongside initiation of calcium and vitamin D supplementation, weight-bearing physiotherapy as tolerated, and fracture surveillance.

No disease-modifying therapy currently exists for CACP syndrome. Immunosuppressive agents have not demonstrated durable efficacy, as the underlying mechanism is mechanical rather than immune-mediated. Intra-articular hyaluronic acid has been reported to provide transient symptomatic relief [6], and Yttrium-90 radiosynovectomy has been described as safe but without sustained benefit on arthropathy [12]. Management therefore remains supportive and includes physiotherapy, splinting, orthopedic monitoring, and surveillance for hip, bone, and pericardial complications [14]. In both patients, molecular confirmation enabled discontinuation of ineffective immunosuppressive therapy and transition to appropriate supportive care.

Both patients were born to consanguineous parents, consistent with epidemiologic observations that CACP is more frequently reported in populations with higher rates of consanguinity [15]. In the pooled review by Maniscalco et al., 65.7% of reported patients were born to consanguineous parents [6]. A positive consanguinity history should therefore heighten suspicion for monogenic arthropathies and prompt earlier genetic evaluation.

This report is limited by the small number of patients and by the relatively short duration of follow-up, which may not fully capture the progressive nature of hip and pericardial involvement in CACP. Moreover, the bone health assessment was not uniform between the two patients. Second patient had undergone DEXA, while the bone health of the first patient was determined only by qualitative radiographic imaging. This methodological discrepancy limits direct comparison of bone involvement between cases and highlights the need for systematic screening of DEXA in future CACP cohorts rather than incidental radiographic observations. Nevertheless, these cases underscore the importance of early genomic evaluation in children with non-inflammatory arthropathy unresponsive to standard therapy. Timely recognition of CACP may prevent prolonged exposure to ineffective immunosuppressive agents and facilitate appropriate supportive management.

CACP syndrome remains one of the most challenging mimics of JIA in pediatric rheumatology. The presentation of swollen, painful large joints in a young child readily suggests an inflammatory process, yet the absence of elevated inflammatory markers, the presentation of joint architecture on imaging, and the persistent lack of response to escalating immunosuppressive therapy should prompt reconsideration.

Our two patients illustrate the diagnosis paradox well. Years of empirical treatment, progressive deformity, and additional findings of reduced BMD and pericardial involvement in one child, all ultimately attribute to a single defect in lubricin production.

Five practical messages emerge from our series. First, non-inflammatory arthropathy unresponsive to conventional therapy warrants early genetic evaluation rather than further therapeutic escalation; WES can shorten the diagnostic journey considerably. Second, the phenotype spectrum of CACP is broader than the classical tetrad implies. Camptodactyly may appear later rather than first, pericardial and bone involvement may emerge gradually, and a history of a consanguinity should heighten suspicion from the initial visit. Third, once the diagnosis is established, management must shift from suppression of presumed inflammation to preservation of function with appropriate supportive care. Fourth, routine screening with DEXA rather than incidental radiographic imaging should be considered as a standard method of evaluating bone health in children with CACP syndrome, particularly those with prolonged immobility or prior glucocorticoid exposure, alongside initiation of calcium and vitamin D supplementation, weight-bearing physiotherapy as tolerated, and fracture surveillance. Fifth, earlier recognition of CACP spares children from prolonged exposure to ineffective and potentially harmful immunosuppressive therapy and redirects care toward supportive measures.
