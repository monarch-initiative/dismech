# Frontiers in congenital disorders of glycosylation consortium, a cross-sectional study report at year 5 of 280 individuals in the natural history cohort.
**Authors:** Lam C, Scaglia F, Berry GT, Larson A, Sarafoglou K, Andersson HC, Sklirou E, Tan QKG, Starosta RT, Sadek M, Wolfe L, Horikoshi S, Ali M, Barone R, Campbell T, Chang IJ, Coles K, Cook E, Eklund EA, Engelhardt NM, Freeman M, Friedman J, Fu DYT, Botzo G, Rawls B, Hernandez C, Johnsen C, Keller K, Kramer S, Kuschel B, Leshinski A, Martinez-Duncker I, Mazza GL, Mercimek-Andrews S, Miller BS, Muthusamy K, Neira J, Patterson MC, Pogorelc N, Powers LN, Ramey E, Reinhart M, Squire A, Thies J, Vockley J, Vreugdenhil H, Witters P, Youbi M, Zeighami A, Zemet R, Edmondson AC, Morava E
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgme.2024.108509](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgme.2024.108509)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Our report describes clinical, genetic, and biochemical features of participants with a molecularly confirmed congenital disorder of glycosylation (CDG) enrolled in the Frontiers in Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation (FCDGC) Natural History cohort at year 5 of the study.

METHODS: We enrolled individuals with a known or suspected CDG into the FCDGC Natural History Study, a multicenter prospective and retrospective natural history study of all genetic causes of CDG. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of baseline study visit data from participants with confirmed CDG who were consented into the FCDGC Natural History Study (5U54NS115198) from October 2019 to November 2023.

RESULTS: Three hundred thirty-three subjects consented to the FCDGC Natural History Study. Of these, 280 unique individuals had genetic data available that was consistent with a diagnosis of CDG. These 280 individuals were enrolled into the study between October 8, 2019 and November 29, 2023. One hundred forty-one (50.4%) were female, and 139 (49.6%) were male. Mean and median age at enrollment was 10.1 and 6.5 years, respectively, with a range of 0.22 to 71.4 years. The cohort encompassed individuals with disorders of N-linked protein glycosylation (57%), glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor disorder (GPI anchor) (15%), disorders of Golgi homeostasis, trafficking and transport (12%), dolichol metabolism disorders (5%), disorders of multiple pathways (6%), and other (5%). The most frequent presenting symptom(s) leading to diagnosis were developmental delay/disability (77%), followed by hypotonia (56%) and feeding difficulties (42%). Mean and median time between first related symptom and diagnosis was 2.7 and 0.8 years, respectively. One hundred percent of individuals in our cohort had developmental differences/disabilities at the time of their baseline visit, followed by 97% with neurologic involvement, 91% with gastrointestinal (GI)/liver involvement, and 88% with musculoskeletal involvement. Severity of disease in individuals was scored on the Nijmegen Progression CDG Rating Scale (NPCRS) with 27% of scores categorized as mild, 44% moderate, and 29% severe. Of the individuals with N-linked protein glycosylation defects, 83% of those with data showed a type 1 pattern on carbohydrate deficient transferrin (CDT) analysis including 82/84 individuals with PMM2-CDG, 6% a type 2 pattern, 1% both type 1 and type 2 pattern and 10% a normal or nonspecific pattern. One hundred percent of individuals with Golgi homeostasis and trafficking defects with data showed a type 2 pattern on CDT analysis, while Golgi transport defect showed a type II pattern 73% of the time, a type 1 pattern for 7%, and 20% had a normal or nonspecific pattern. Most of the variants documented were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic using ACMG criteria. For the majority of the variants, the predicted molecular consequence was missense followed by nonsense and splice site, and the majority of the diagnoses are inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern but with disorders of all major nuclear inheritance included.

DISCUSSION: The FCDGC Natural History Study serves as an important resource to build future research studies, improve clinical care, and prepare for clinical trial readiness. Herein is the first overview of CDG participants of the FCDGC Natural History Study.

Congenital disorders of glycosylation (CDG) are monogenic disorders in which the regulated, enzymatically driven process of synthesis or transfer of glycans onto an organic molecule is disrupted. The first CDG that was clinically described, PMM2-CDG, had its underlying genetic and enzymatic defect determined in the mid-late 1990s following its initial description in 1980 [1]. The field has been expanding rapidly with the advent of genomic sequencing, with many novel CDG identified in the last few years [2]. Since many biologic functions depend upon correct protein and lipid glycosylation, clinical CDG manifestations are widely variable within and among genetic causes and across the lifespan. The clinical findings in affected individuals reflect the role of glycosylation in the development and function of many organ systems. There is a significant knowledge gap regarding the progression and clinical variation in this group of disorders due to the rarity and novelty of CDG, the geographic spread of affected individuals, the broad clinical spectrum, and that the previous natural history studies focused on one disorder, were retrospective, or were limited to one site.

In the 2000s-2010s, an extensive network of physicians, scientists and patients/advocates focused on CDG arose naturally, with a goal of facilitating team-science and building partnerships benefiting each of these stakeholders. This network was formalized with the formation of the Frontiers in Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation Consortium (FCDGC - https://fcdgc.rarediseasesnetwork.org/) in 2019. With the advent of the consortium, the FCDGC Natural History (NH) Study, a clinical project with the aim to determine the natural history of CDG, delineate the spectrum of clinical features, develop and validate patient reported outcomes and disperse verified clinical information to families and non-expert clinicians, was initiated.

We initially aimed to collect natural history data on 100 individuals with CDG across nine clinical sites. Since that time, close to three times the number of individuals with CDG consented to participate and we have expanded our clinical sites to twelve and several additional sites in the United States and internationally are planning to join soon (Figure 1). Following initiation of the NH study, we have already begun documenting initial findings in publications describing different aspects of the NH of CDG including liver manifestations [3], patient reported outcomes [4], hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis abnormalities [5], and neurologic/neuroradiologic and coagulation features [6, 7]. We leveraged the FCDGC NH study to document case series of clinical and biochemical information on ultra-rare genetic causes of CDG with minimal published data including ALG13-CDG, ALG3-CDG, ALG8-CDG, and ATP6AP1-CDG [8–11]. Similarly, we published case reports where subjects experienced unique therapies, including heart transplantation in PGM1-CDG, and liver transplantation in PMM2-CDG [12, 13]. In collaboration with the biomarker core of the FCDGC, we found that severity of neuropathy and liver dysfunction were correlated with a novel biomarker, urine sorbitol, for PMM2-CDG [14]. This information coupled with findings from a drug screen[15, 16], led to a therapeutic trial of an aldose reductase inhibitor in PMM2-CDG (NCT04925960). As we enter five years of the study, we share an overview of the characteristics of the cohort that has been enrolled into the prospective arm of the FCDGC NH study.

The FCDGC Natural History (NH) study is an NIH-funded collaborative effort initially of nine clinical sites in the United States, now twelve clinical sites in North America and Europe, who work in close collaboration with the CDG CARE and other patient advocacy groups. Since 2019, the original nine clinical sites have recruited individuals with known and highly suspected CDG diagnoses into a NH study that collects demographic, clinical, biochemical, genetic, patient reported outcome and severity scoring data at initial and annual study visits that typically piggyback on clinical visits. Instruments employed included the Goal Attainment Scale (GAS) [17] and Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) questionnaires [18], and the Nijmegen Progression CDG Rating Scale (NPCRS) [4, 19]. Data is stored in a secure REDCap database. This NH protocol was approved by the NIH and the Mayo Institutional Review Board (IRB) that served as the central IRB with local context review at each FCDGC NH site (NCT04199000). All procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. To enroll, participants visited with one of the site study teams either in person or virtually for a review of medical history, physical examination, and laboratory tests. They and/or their legally authorized representative provided written or electronically signed consent and/or assent using an IRB-approved consent/assent tool as indicated.

The inclusion criteria for the prospective arm of the FCDGC NH study are: all individuals with a genetically, enzymatically, or molecularly confirmed diagnosis of CDG or NGLY1 deficiency, or individuals whose laboratory values are highly suggestive of CDG, but who do not have a verified genetic diagnosis. This analysis includes the subset of individuals who enrolled in the prospective arm of the study, have consent documented, and a CDG diagnosis clinically confirmed, with consistent genetic data. Included individuals have at least one variant documented in a gene associated with a dominantly or X-linked inherited CDG, or two variants documented in a gene associated with a recessively inherited CDG. The list of CDG related genes initially included 129 genes divided into twelve groups: N-linked protein glycosylation, O-linked protein glycosylation, O-fucosylation, glycosaminoglycan synthesis and O-xylosylation, other O-linked protein glycosylation, glycophosphatidylinositol biosynthesis, other lipid glycosylation, dolichol metabolism, Golgi transport, Golgi homeostasis, other multiple glycosylation pathway, and other glycan metabolism disorders (Supplementary Table 1), with the understanding that the list could be expanded as new genes and groups are discovered. Individuals with genetic causes of CDG not included on the original list were included after discussion between the site PI and Administrative Core.

Clinical data from clinical interview, physical examination, laboratory data – specifically interpreted CDT and ApoC-III analysis, dietary history, GAS scoring, caregiver proxy PROMIS questionnaires [18], and any clinically obtained radiologic, laboratory, or other functional tests were collected and recorded into the REDcap database hosted in the NIH Cloud managed by the Data Management and Coordinating Center at the Children’s Hospital Medical Center and the University of Cincinnati. The GAS is a method of scoring the extent to which patient’s individual goals are achieved in the course of intervention. In effect, each patient has their own outcome measure but this is scored in a standardized way to allow statistical analysis [17]; the results from the FCDGC NH GAS are being reported in a separate manuscript. When, or whether, an individual achieved the developmental milestones of head control, sitting without support, standing without support, walking without support, and saying their first words was recorded in the history, and documented in the database. The most common symptoms leading to suspicion of CDG were also systematically collected for each subject. Data cut for analysis was performed on February 23, 2024.

Severity was scored using the NPCRS [4]. Organ system involvement was determined as a binary measurement (involved or not involved) using data from the relevant portions of the physical examination, NPCRS questions, symptoms recorded as the basis of the clinical diagnosis, past medical history and adverse events that were also coded using the Clinical Data Acquisition Standards Harmonization (CDASH) terminology and domains.

Diagnostic genetic information was collected from mostly clinical genetic or genomic test reports. When possible, the data were captured in simplified HGVS convention [website HGVS Nomenclature (hgvs-nomenclature.org)]. The variant review team comprising a medical biochemical geneticist and several genetic counselors who completed a comprehensive reevaluation of all variants to determine pathogenicity according to ACMG guidelines [20].

We used descriptive statistics to investigate the characteristics of the entire cohort, each CDG group and for PMM2-CDG, the diagnosis with the greatest number of individuals.

Three hundred thirty-three subjects consented into the FCDGC NH Project. Of these, 280 unique individuals had genetic data available that was consistent with a diagnosis of CDG. These 280 individuals were enrolled into the study between October 8, 2019 and November 29, 2023. Data were collected from October 2019 to February 2024. There were 141 (50%) female, and 139 (50%) male subjects. Mean and median age at enrollment was 10.1 and 6.5 years respectively, with a range of 0.2 to 71.4 years. The age of enrollment distribution is significantly skewed with more younger individuals than older individuals (Figure 2). Eleven percent of individuals reported Hispanic or Latino ethnicity, 86% self-reported as not Hispanic or Latino, and 3% did not report ethnicity. Most individuals self-identified as White (79%), while 7% identified as Asian, 3% identified as Black or African American, and the remaining identified as mixed (White-Asian, White-Black, White-American Indian or Alaska Native at 2% total) or did not report race (9%).

The majority of individuals were diagnosed with N-linked protein glycosylation disorders (57%), with the second most common glycosylation group being glycophosphatidylinositol biosynthesis disorders (15%), and the third most common being disorders of Golgi homeostasis, trafficking and transport in aggregate (12%) (Figure 3). PMM2-CDG was the most common diagnosis comprising 94 individuals, followed by PIGN-CDG with 16 individuals, then ALG13-CDG with 13 individuals, followed by SLC35A2-CDG with 12 individuals, and PGM1-CDG with 11 individuals. A total of 57 types of CDG were enrolled, with the rest of the types having <10 individuals enrolled (Table 1).

The age of the first related symptom was noted as <= 12 months in 229/280 individuals, >12 months in 49 individuals, and undocumented in 2 individuals. The mean and median age of first related symptom in the 250 individuals with specific documented age was 1.9 and 0.2 years respectively, whereas the mean and median age of diagnosis in the cohort was 5.6 and 1.5 years respectively. Overall, the mean and median time between the first related symptom and diagnosis in the whole cohort (N=171) was 2.9 and 0.94 years respectively when calculating the time difference individually. When broken down by decade that diagnosis was made (N=133 with data available), the median time between first related symptom and diagnosis was 1.82 years for those diagnosed in the 1990s (N = 4), 1.17 years for those diagnosed in the 2000s (N=7, 5 with data), 0.98 years for those diagnosed in the 2010s (N=100, 79 with data), and 0.61 years for those diagnosed in the 2020s (N=83, 45 with data).

Developmental delay/disability, hypotonia, and feeding difficulties were the three symptoms that were noted at presentation for the largest percentage of the cohort (Figure 4). Symptoms were mapped into 15 systems with neurodevelopmental system the most commonly involved at presentation. When broken down by CDG groups, there were some striking differences between groups. Seizures was the most prevalent in GPI anchor disorders and oral maxillary defects was present in half of the individuals with other multiple glycosylation defects, of which PGM1-CDG is the most common diagnosis of that group (Figure 5). All individuals had developmental delay/disability by the time of enrollment; neurologic system was the second most frequently involved, GI system was the third, and musculoskeletal system, at the time of enrollment, rose to the fourth most frequent system involved (Figure 6). Cardiac involvement was noted in a minority of individuals and will be further detailed in a separate article (personal communication).

Individuals were scored at baseline using the NPCRS that ranges from 0 to 82 with mild scores defined as scores 0–14, moderate 15–25, and severe ≥ 26. Overall, median score was in the moderate range at 20 points with minimum score at 0, maximum at 48, first quartile (Q1) at 14, and third quartile (Q3) at 27. Disorders of multiple pathways had the lowest severity scores (Figure 7). When looking at the scores of ataxia, communication, feeding, and GI symptoms, the majority of individuals (57% overall, 87% PMM2-CDG) experienced challenges with ataxia, communication (83% overall, 90% PMM2-CDG), and GI symptoms (i.e. diarrhea/constipation/vomiting 53% overall, 57% PMM2-CDG). Approximately half of patients had feeding challenges requiring modified consistency or route of feeding (50% overall and in 49% PMM2-CDG) (Table 3).

In our cohort, 85% of the 256 individuals age >4 months with data achieved head control, 75% of the 255 individuals age >6 months achieved sitting without support, 52% of 243 individuals age >9 months achieved standing without support, 49% of the 224 individuals age >18 months achieved walking without support, and 69% of those age >12 months achieved first words. In those that did achieve each milestone, the median age of achieving head control was 7 months, sitting without support was 12 months, standing without support was 19 months, walking without support was 24 months, and saying first words was 20 months. In general, the percentage of those achieving developmental milestones was greater in the N-linked disorders than the GPI anchor disorders. Of interest, however, the proportion of individuals with PMM2-CDG achieving motor milestones of standing and walking was lower than those in the GPI anchor groups, but the proportion achieving first words was higher than in the GPI anchor group, in all N-linked disorders taken together, as well as the entire cohort taken as a whole (Table 4).

Data on pattern of CDT was collected on 212 individuals. Eighty-five percent of individuals with N-linked protein glycosylation defects showed a type 1 pattern. Of interest, 98% of individuals with PMM2-CDG had a type 1 pattern, but even in PMM2-CDG, the disorder classically screened for by CDT, 2% (N=2; CDT drawn at 5 years 9 months in one individuals and at age 72 years in the other individual) had a normal CDT pattern. Eighty-one percent of CDT analysis measured in individuals with Golgi defects exhibited a type 2 pattern. CDT pattern results in different CDG groups as well as for PMM2-CDG are detailed in Table 2. We also evaluated other laboratory parameters which are frequently measured and potential biomarkers in individuals with certain genetic causes of CDG. Of the 43 individuals with GPI anchor disorders, there was data on alkaline phosphatase for 34 individuals. Twenty-three of these individuals had a normal alkaline phosphatase (1 GPAA1-CDG, 2 PIGA-CDG, 3 PIGG-CDG, 12 PIGN-CDG, 1 PIGS-CDG, 4 PIGT-CDG), 7 had low alkaline phosphatase (1 PIGA-CDG, 1 PIGG-CDG, 2 PIGN-CDG, 3 PIGT-CDG), and 16 (4 PGAP3-CDG, 4 PIGA-CDG, 1 PIGG-CDG, 1 PIGL-CDG, 4 PIGN-CDG, 1 PIGO-CDG, 1 PIGW-CDG) had a history of high alkaline phosphatase at some point (Supplementary Table 4). Five individuals with GPI anchor disorders had both normal and low alkaline phosphatase (1 PIGG-CDG, 2 PIGN-CDG, 2 PIGT-CDG), 5 had both normal and high alkaline phosphatase (1 PIGA-CDG, 1 PIGG-CDG, 3 PIGN-CDG), and 1 individual with PIGA-CDG had documented low, normal, and high alkaline phosphatase levels at different time points. Of the 158 individuals with N-linked glycosylation disorders, including the 94 individuals with PMM2-CDG, 100 and 74 individuals with N-linked glycosylation disorders and PMM2-CDG had data on antithrombin III (AT III) documented, respectively. Of these, individuals with N-linked CDG and PMM2-CDG, 74% and 80% had low AT III, 40% and 36% had normal AT III documented, respectively, and 3% of both groups had high AT III. Fourteen percent and 16% of those with data had both normal and low AT III documented at different time points. One percent and 1.4% had high, low, and normal AT III levels, and 2% and 1.4% had high and normal AT III levels at some point in the N-linked and PMM2-CDG cohorts respectively.

As of February 2024, 8 out of the 280 individuals had documented deaths after enrollment. Age at death ranged from < 2 months to 21.4 years. The diagnoses included one individual each with NGLY1-CDDG, ALG9-CDG, ALG3-CDG, ALG14-CDG, two with ALG6-CDG, and two PMM2-CDG.

The diagnoses and the specific gene involvement, allele(s), ACMG pathogenicity classification and supporting evidence, molecular consequences, and variant type are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. Of the 512 variants, the majority (78%) were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, and a minority were classified as variants of uncertain significance (21%). The majority of variants were missense variants (72%), with nonsense accounting for 8.6%, splice site 7.8%, frameshift 7.6%, deletions 3%, in frame indel 0.8%, and duplications 0.6%. Eighty two percent (229/280) of diagnoses are inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, 16% (44/280) in an X-linked pattern, and 2% (5/280) in an autosomal dominant pattern. Although 27.5% of subjects had at least one variant of uncertain significance in their genotype, the CDG diagnosis was clinically confirmed by the site principal investigator by incorporating the genetic information with the clinical, biochemical and/or functional evidence.

Of the alleles in PMM2, 63/188 were the common p.Arg141His pathogenic variant, with the second most common allele being the p.Phe119 Leu pathogenic variant accounting for 13 alleles (Supplementary Table 3). The most common genotype was p.Arg141His | p.Phe119Leu accounting for 10 individuals in our cohort. The second most common was p.Arg141His | p.Val231Met (n = 6).

Here we summarize the baseline genetic, biochemical and clinical findings from the prospective cohort of individuals enrolled in the FCDGC NH study during its first 5 years. As anticipated, there was significant diversity in the genetic causes of CDG diagnoses with a large proportion comprising those with PMM2-CDG and other N-linked CDG. There was also substantial genetic heterogeneity. Of note, GPI-anchor disorders were well represented in our cohort. Engagement with the patient advocacy groups was key to recruitment, and our cohort and the diseases included may reflect engagement in these groups.

Our population is comprised mainly of younger individuals. This skew is likely attributable to the fact that diagnosis of CDG primarily relies on broad genomic testing in partnership with biochemical/research confirmation. Broad genomic testing became clinically available in the early 2010s [21], and is generally much more accessible in pediatric populations [22]. Of interest, the time between first related symptom and diagnosis decreased with decade of diagnosis from mean time of 1.8 years in the 1990s to 0.6 years in the 2020s. The number of individuals diagnosed with CDG starting in the 2000s also increased substantially compared to the two decades prior. These findings likely reflect increased accessibility to clinical genomic testing in the pediatric population and increased recognition of these conditions. Additionally, given our enrollment age skew, this trend may also suggest that adults with CDG are not being diagnosed and enrolled into the NH study. A large number of genetic causes of CDG were discovered in the 2010s [23, 24]; therefore individuals born before 2010 with a CDG discovered after that timepoint would experience a diagnostic delay of years. If adult CDG patients are increasingly diagnosed and enrolled into our NH study, the median time between first symptom, which typically would have occurred at less than 1 year of life and age of diagnosis would become greater. Increased mortality rates in affected adults could also contribute to the skewed ages within our cohort.

The most frequently encountered symptoms at presentation that led to diagnosis were developmental delay/disability, hypotonia, and feeding difficulties, each of which have a very broad differential, further challenging the diagnosis of CDG[25–27]. The symptoms more typically associated with CDG, including inverted nipples and abnormal fat pads, were only present in a minority of individuals, even in PMM2-CDG. Biochemical markers had improved, but not complete sensitivity. Of interest, 98% of individuals with PMM2-CDG had a type 1 pattern on CDT, but even in the disorder classically screened for by CDT, 2% did not have the classic pattern on CDT, which has been rarely noted in previous reports[28, 29]. When looking at alkaline phosphatase, a biochemical marker suggestive of GPI anchor disorders, the sensitivity was much lower, and levels fluctuated even in the same individual. Whether levels were high, low, and/or normal in our subjects was concordant with what was previously reported in the literature with two exceptions. We observed low alkaline phosphatase levels in PIGA-CDG and PIGG-CDG, which was not previously described (Supplementary Table 4)[30–41]. This may be because we collect longitudinal retrospective data across all clinical situations, and because the full phenotypic spectrum is not yet captured in the published literature. The non-specific presentation and lack of sensitivity of biochemical markers in most CDG emphasizes how critical broad genomic testing is to the diagnosis of CDG. It also informs the need to discover and develop improved biomarkers for this group of diseases for both diagnosis as well as disease monitoring. A simple newborn screening test, yet unavailable, would dramatically enhance ascertainment of new patients.

While the large majority (72.5%) of the individuals included in this report have genetic evidence that, on its own confers a diagnosis of a CDG, one limitation of this study is that there are a number of individuals in which the principal investigators rely on other information including the biochemical phentoype and clinical features to inform the clinical diagnosis of CDG. For all individuals with PMM2-CDG whos genotype included a VUS (N=7), for example, a type 1 pattern on CDTs was documented. That along with the documented features of developmental delay, hypotonia, faltering growth, and many times inverted nipples or abnormal fat pads allowed for the clinician to have enough confidence to assign a clinical diagnosis of PMM2-CDG. We ackowledge that there is a potential missassignment of diagnosis, especially in the individuals where a clinically available biochemical marker is not available. Many of these individuals, especially those with GPI anchor disorders, did undergo preliminary research functional analysis that was consistent with the diagnosis, and the majority of these individuals were diagnosed through broad genomic testing that did not identify other genetic diagnoses. We also follow these individuals longitudinally and revisit the diagnosis on an annual basis. If the diagnosis is refuted, that individual is removed from the natural history study and subsequent analysis.

As in other US-based rare disease consortia [42], we observed a smaller proportion of African American/Black participants than in the 2020 US census data. We also saw this effect, but to a lesser degree for the Hispanic/Latino participants. While this could reflect a true difference in disease incidence, it is more likely related to a disparity in access to medical care and research, and/or mistrust of clinical care and research due to historic abuses [43, 44].

The majority of the individuals reported here are still enrolled in the NH study and many have had additional longitudinal data collected over up to 3 additional years. Over half of the cohort has an additional year of data after baseline, approximately a quarter of the cohort has an additional 2 years of data after baseline, and a smaller number have 3 years of data after baseline. As these data are still being accumulated, longitudinal analysis has not yet been performed; only characteristics at enrollment are included in this report. We additionally have a memorial arm of the study with a cohort of deceased individuals for whom we are collecting retrospective data, which we do not present here. We plan to expand this cohort when exploring reasons for death and to contrast the effectiveness of some emerging interventions.

In rare diseases, NH studies are essential to increase the understanding of the disease course, which is necessary to provide informed counseling, improve supportive care for affected individuals, elucidate the pathophysiology of the disease, and establish clinical trial outcome measures, all in the quest to improve the lives of individuals living with these conditions. This study offers genetic and clinical characteristics of a large array of CDG diagnoses, which may be useful to clinicians seeing new CDG patients. In this study, we build the foundation, infrastructure, and network from which future research that aims to improve the lives of individuals with CDG can grow.
