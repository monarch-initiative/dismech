# Acute Pouchitis is Common in Pregnant and Postpartum Women.
**Authors:** Kayal M, Gottlieb Z, Glamour B, Ibing S, Spencer E, Dubinsky MC
**Journal:** Clin Transl Gastroenterol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.14309/ctg.0000000000000732](https://doi.org/10.14309/ctg.0000000000000732)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: The impact of pregnancy on the development of pouchitis in women who have undergone total proctocolectomy with ileal pouch anal anastomosis for ulcerative colitis is poorly understood.

METHODS: This was a retrospective study of women with ulcerative colitis who underwent total proctocolectomy with ileal pouch anal anastomosis and subsequently became pregnant at Mount Sinai Hospital. The primary outcome was acute pouchitis during pregnancy or the postpartum period defined as symptoms of increased stool frequency and urgency treated with antibiotics.

RESULTS: A total of 44 women with 63 pregnancies and complete data were identified. Acute pouchitis occurred in 14 pregnancies (22.2%) in 12 women and in the postpartum period of 10 pregnancies (15.9%) in 9 women. Acute pouchitis occurred more frequently in women with a history of acute pouchitis immediately before, during, or after pregnancy.

DISCUSSION: Acute pouchitis was common during pregnancy and the postpartum period, likely due to microbial shifts. Although not statistically significant, these results provide insight into the impact of pregnancy on the risk of pouchitis and establish the framework for preconception counseling that focuses on prevention and management of pouchitis during pregnancy.

Approximately 10%–15% of patients with ulcerative colitis, of whom 30%–40% are women, require total proctocolectomy (TPC) with ileal pouch anal anastomosis (IPAA) for medically refractory disease or dysplasia (1,2). Historically, TPC with IPAA has been associated with a significant risk of infertility in women, ranging from 44% to 82%, in part due to the pelvic dissection required and resultant scarring of the fallopian tubes (3). This risk has reportedly decreased to approximately 30% with the advent of modern, minimally invasive surgical techniques (4,5). Beyond infertility, data regarding women's health after IPAA have been limited.

Although acute pouchitis is common after TPC with IPAA, the impact of pregnancy on its development is poorly understood (6). Estrogen and progesterone shifts during pregnancy influence gut microbial diversity and density (7). Given the causal association between the microbiome and acute pouchitis, pregnancy may increase the risk of acute pouchitis. Unfortunately, few studies have rigorously evaluated this risk. A review of single-center studies from 2011 reported a 1.8% frequency of acute pouchitis during pregnancy (8). However, no studies have been completed since.

There is an urgent need to better delineate the risk of pouchitis in pregnancy and the postpartum period for patient counseling and physician preparedness. In this study, we aim to examine the frequency of acute pouchitis in pregnancy and the postpartum period and to identify associated risk factors.

This was a retrospective study of women with ulcerative colitis who underwent TPC with IPAA and subsequently became pregnant. The study was performed at Mount Sinai Hospital, a large quaternary care hospital with a dedicated inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) center inclusive of 2 unique programs—the IBD Preconception and Pregnancy Planning Clinic and the Pouch Program. Eligible women were identified by electronic medical record search using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) and International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes for IPAA and pregnancy, respectively, from 2011 to 2023 (9). Women with a baseline history of chronic pouchitis or Crohn's disease-like pouch inflammation were excluded.

Collected patient demographics and disease characteristics included date of birth, date of final surgical stage, history of pouchitis and treatment, prepregnancy disease activity defined as the number of episodes of acute pouchitis within the 6 months preceding pregnancy, and use of in vitro fertilization. Collected pregnancy characteristics included parity, date of delivery, gestational age, mode of delivery, postpartum complications such as small bowel obstruction and ileus, and previous pregnancy course.

The primary outcome was acute pouchitis during pregnancy or the postpartum period defined as symptoms of increased stool frequency and urgency treated with antibiotics with resolution of symptoms. The postpartum period was defined as the 6 weeks immediately following birth. The Fischer exact test was used to compare categorical variables, and Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables.

A total of 44 women with 63 pregnancies and complete data were identified. The majority of women were primiparous (31/44, 70.5%). The median time between IPAA and pregnancy was 6.6 [IQR 4.5–10.7] years, and the median age at delivery was 33.7 [30.8–35.8] years. In vitro fertilization was used for 14 pregnancies (22.2%). Acute pouchitis within the 6 months before conception occurred in 8 pregnancies (12.7%).

Small bowel obstruction in 6 pregnancies (9.5%) was managed conservatively. One spontaneous abortion at 19 weeks occurred. The mode of delivery was cesarean in 61 pregnancies (98.4%) and vaginal in 1 (1.6%). The median gestational age at delivery was 38 [IQR 37–39] weeks. Among the 61 patients who delivered through cesarean, 21 (31.4%) required lysis of adhesions at the time of cesarean and 8 (13.1%) had postcesarean ileus.

Acute pouchitis occurred in 14 pregnancies (22.2%) in 12 women, most commonly in the second trimester (10/14, 71.4%). The antibiotics prescribed included metronidazole (9/14, 64.3%), amoxicillin (2/14, 14.3%), ciprofloxacin (1/14, 7.1%), cephalexin (1/14, 7.1%), and tinidazole (1/14, 7.1%). No patient required more than a standard course.

Acute pouchitis occurred in the postpartum period of 10 pregnancies (15.9%) in 9 women. The antibiotics prescribed included metronidazole (6/10, 60.0%), ciprofloxacin (2/10, 20.0%), cephalexin (1/10, 10.0%), and rifaximin (1/10, 10.0%). No patient required more than a standard course.

A higher percentage of women who developed acute pouchitis in pregnancy had a previous episode of acute pouchitis either during pregnancy or the postpartum period, although this did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, a higher percentage of women who developed acute pouchitis in the postpartum period had a previous episode of acute pouchitis either within the 6 months before conception or in the postpartum period. In this single-center retrospective study, acute pouchitis was common during pregnancy and the postpartum period, potentially due to microbial shifts. The frequency of acute pouchitis during pregnancy and the postpartum period noted in this study is less than that in the general population after IPAA—approximately 50% within the first 2 years—but this is likely due to our small cohort (6). Acute pouchitis occurred more frequently in women with a history of acute pouchitis immediately before, during, or after pregnancy. Although not statistically significant, these results provide insight into the impact of pregnancy on the risk of pouchitis and establish the framework for preconception counseling that focuses on prevention and management of pouchitis during pregnancy.

Prevention studies to date have focused on the use of probiotics and diet to module pouch microbial composition and increase diversity. The De Simone Formulation of multistrain probiotics has been studied in 3 randomized controlled trials as secondary prevention in patients with a history of antibiotic-responsive pouchitis, and meta-analysis of these studies has reported an 87% lower risk of acute pouchitis over 12 months (10–12). Although these data are limited, we recommend considering the use of the De Simone Formulation as secondary prevention of pouchitis in line with the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) (13). This recommendation may be extended to pregnant women as probiotics have been shown to have no impact on adverse pregnancy outcomes; however, thoughtful consultation with maternal fetal medicine is advised (14). Fruit consumption has similarly been reported to be effective as secondary prevention. In a prospective observational study, patients at the highest tertile of fruit consumption had lower rates of pouchitis compared with patients at the lowest tertile, and reduction in fruit consumption over time was associated with pouchitis relapse (15). Based on these data, we recommend consumption of ≥1.5 fruit servings/day as secondary prevention of pouchitis.

Evidence to guide the management of pouchitis during pregnancy and the postpartum period is scarce. While guidelines provide a general framework for management, they do not specifically address pouchitis during pregnancy or the postpartum period (13). Antibiotics are first-line therapy for pouchitis, most commonly ciprofloxacin or metronidazole. During pregnancy, either can be used (16). Although historically there were concerns regarding the teratogenicity risk of ciprofloxacin, its use during the first trimester has not been associated with an increased risk of congenital abnormalities, stillbirths, or preterm births (17). During the postpartum period, ciprofloxacin may be used. However, metronidazole is not routinely recommended if breastfeeding is desired and use should be individualized (16). This recommendation is based on a comparative study of 35 infants exposed to metronidazole through breastmilk who had greater oral and rectal colonization with Candida compared with unexposed infants (18). In patients with allergies or adverse effects to either ciprofloxacin or metronidazole, amoxicillin-clavulanate may be considered.

The strengths of this study include standardized chart review, predefined outcomes, and a relatively large sample size given the niche topic. The major limitation of this study is the lack of endoscopic diagnostic criteria for the outcome of pouchitis; however, this reflects standard clinical care as pouchoscopy is not typically performed during pregnancy. In addition, functional outcomes were not assessed, and the reasons behind antibiotic choice were not elucidated due to the retrospective nature of the study and associated risk of bias. The exclusion of patients with chronic pouchitis or Crohn's disease-like pouch inflammation is both a strength and limitation—strength in that allows for a more cohesive cohort and limitation in that the data for chronic phenotypes, where pouchitis may occur more frequently, are not presented.

In light of the results of this study, we recommend preconception counseling for all women who have undergone TPC with IPAA. In addition to fertility, counseling should focus on the risk of pouchitis in pregnancy and the postpartum period, taking into account the woman's prior pregnancy experiences for individualized risk assessment. Strategies for pouchitis prophylaxis with diet and probiotics should be discussed, and plans for pouchitis management stratified by time of development, pregnancy vs postpartum, should be reviewed. A large, prospective registry of pregnant patients who have undergone TPC with IPAA is necessary to further support these results and inform clinical recommendations.
