# Efficacy of morphine on cough in patients with repeat expansions of RFC1 and refractory chronic cough.
**Authors:** Guilleminault L, Chazelas P, Magdelaine C, Villeneuve T, Lia AS, Magy L
**Journal:** ERJ Open Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1183/23120541.00840-2025](https://doi.org/10.1183/23120541.00840-2025)

## Content

Repeat expansion of RFC1 (RE-RFC1) is associated with refractory chronic cough but no treatment has emerged as a therapeutic option on cough in patients with RE-RFC1. Here, we show that morphine has a beneficial effect on cough in patients with RE-RFC1. https://bit.ly/4g3iviQ.


To the Editor:


Unexplained and refractory chronic cough (URCC) is a challenging condition in clinical practice. Indeed, the diagnostic work-up is particularly complex due to the high proportion of patients with non-obvious cause of cough [1]. Moreover, the treatment is also a problematic step in the management of URCC patients, considering the paucity of therapeutic options available. Recently, we have shown that 16.2% of patients with URCC have biallelic repeat expansion of RFC1 (RE-RFC1) and 8.8% monoallelic RE-RFC1 [2]. This genetic trait is observed in a rare disease called CANVAS (Cerebellar Ataxia, Neuropathy, and Vestibular Areflexia Syndrome) [3]. It is now established that chronic cough is a common and early symptom of CANVAS, frequently occurring years-to-decades before other core symptoms [4]. In CANVAS, cough is a very debilitating symptom, leading to an important alteration in quality of life. The mechanisms of cough due to RE-RFC1 remain unknown, and no drug has yet emerged as a therapeutic option for cough in patients with CANVAS.

Our study thus aims to evaluate the efficacy of morphine in the treatment of chronic cough in patients with RE-RFC1 and URCC.

A prospective study was conducted on patients with URCC according to American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society guidelines, and also RE-RFC1 [5, 6]. Inclusion criteria were: 1) chronic cough with cough duration of more than 12 months; 2) visual analogue scale (VAS) ≥4; 3) URCC defined as chronic cough with no identified causes (despite ear, nose and throat assessment, lung function tests and other tests determined by the investigators) or persistent cough despite an optimal treatment of the causes; 4) normal chest computed tomography scan; 5) no improvement of cough despite 12 weeks of inhaled steroids; and, 6) monoallelic or biallelic repeat expansion of RFC1. Exclusion criteria were current smoking or smoking cessation within a year and other common contraindications for morphine. This study was conducted in accordance with the French Ethical requirements (RnIPH 2021-145) and the National Commission for Data Protection and Liberties (CNIL number: 2206723 v 0).

Patients were asked to self-complete a 10 cm VAS for cough severity, Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ), Hull Airway Reflux Questionnaire and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale at baseline. The questionnaires and VAS were not repeated at follow-up. To identify RE-RFC1, long-range PCR and REPEAT-PRIMED PCR were performed using a previously described protocol [2]. Patients were asked to take slow-release oral morphine sulfate 10 mg twice daily or equivalent. A self-evaluation for morphine effect on chronic cough was collected during the follow-up visit at 6 months. This evaluation was made by the patient themself in the presence of the investigator. The impact on cough was rated as follows: no change, partial response with residual debilitating cough, good response with residual non-debilitating cough, or cough disappearance.

17 patients with chronic cough and biallelic RE-RFC1 were included. As previously described, cough duration was long, with a mean of 236±128 months. Total LCQ score was 4.6±1.7, VAS score was 5.4±3.2 and Hull Airway Reflux Questionnaire score was 25.4±14.6. The patients received other neuromodulators before the initiation of morphine. The effectiveness of pregabalin/gabapentin was low because four patients who were given one or the other had no change of cough. Among eight patients treated with amitriptyline, three (37.5%) showed no change on cough, four (50.0%) had a partial response with residual debilitating cough and one (12.5%) had good response with residual non-debilitating cough.

Among the 17 patients included in the study and treated with low dose morphine, one (5.9%) had a partial response with residual debilitating cough, 11 (64.7%) had good response with residual non-debilitating cough and four (23.5%) had cough cessation (figure 1). One (5.9%) patient had to discontinue morphine because of nausea. This means that all but one patient completed the 6-month period of treatment.

Effect of morphine on cough in patients with biallelic repeat expansion of RFC1 patients.

In patients with CANVAS, cough has a very negative impact on quality of life [7]. Effective cough treatment remains crucial for patients with CANVAS and URCC [8]. Unfortunately, the number of therapeutic options to treat cough is low. As suggested by Tatineni
et al. [7], in our cohort, it appears that neuromodulators such as amitriptyline or pregabalin/gabapentin has a modest effectiveness. These data are crucial since neuromodulators are recommended in refractory chronic cough according to international guidelines [5, 6]. Interestingly, we found that morphine seems to be highly effective on cough in patients with CANVAS. The effect of morphine on cough was first suggested by Morice
et al. [9] in URCC and is now recommended for URCC by the European Respiratory Society [6]. Our study suggests that this drug is also highly effective in patients with RE-RFC1.

Morphine is thought to depress the cough reflex, acting directly on the neural pathways in the brain. Morphine produces most of its analgesic effects by binding to the μ-opioid receptor within the central nervous system and the peripheral nervous system. Its mechanism of action and effect on cough remain fully unknown. The activation of central nervous system descending inhibitory pathways, as well as the inhibition of the peripheral nervous system afferent neurons may play a role in cough. Antitussive effects occur with doses lower than those usually required for analgesia. Cough pathophysiology in CANVAS has also not been elucidated yet. This neurological disorder is classically associated with a sensory neuronopathy involving the dorsal root and V cranial nerve ganglia root atrophy with a loss of neuronal cells. Recently, Hirons
et al. [10] demonstrated that capsaicin C2 and C5 thresholds did not differ significantly between patients with CANVAS and chronic cough and those with refractory chronic cough with no CANVAS. Capsaicin C2 and C5 thresholds were lower compared to those in healthy control subjects. This suggests that chronic cough in CANVAS has the characteristics of cough hypersensitivity, which may explain the effect of morphine on cough in CANVAS.

The present work has several limitations. First of all, there is no control arm. However, in this rare disease, it is particularly challenging to recruit enough patients in a randomised controlled trial. It is worth noting that other neuromodulators seem to have a modest effect on cough. This suggests that a placebo effect of morphine on chronic cough is unlikely in our study. Moreover, the effect of morphine on cough was sustained over time in our patients. Secondly, cough evaluation is subjective in our study. However, a standardised tool for cough response to drugs is still lacking. Although our score has not been validated yet, we estimate that it is a pragmatic scale and provides clear data on cough evaluation. VAS and LCQ scores were not repeated at 6 months. Although these data would have been interesting, their use to define cough response is not validated.

In conclusion, for the first time, we suggest that morphine has an interesting effect on URCC in patients with RE-RFC1. Further studies are needed to confirm this effect.
