## Content

AbstractAlthough among the six most common parasitic diseases, leishmaniasis is considered a neglected disease, being recognised as a serious public health burden worldwide. This fact impairs technological advancements and investments in innovative therapeutic approaches, so that commonly available drugs are still associated with low therapeutic rates and significant side effects. Pentavalent antimonials, such as Pentostan® and Glucantime®, were established as chemotherapeutic agents since the 1940s, being the first line of treatment for leishmaniasis; however, 60% of cases of leishmaniasis are resistant to pentavalent antimonials. Thus, the search for more effective therapies and new therapeutic agents with potential leishmanicidal activity is still in-demand. In this review, an overview about the disease and its pathophysiology is given, discussing the current state-of-the-art of the standard treatments and new alternatives based on drug delivery approaches, enhancing the need for more effective pharmaceutical treatments.

1 Introduction
Leishmaniasis is a parasitic disease transmitted by trypanosomes, genus Leishmania [ 1, 2]. Present in 98 countries, this 
disease affects about 12 million people and is considered an important public health problem in several regions of the 
world, including America, Asia, Europe and Africa [3–7].
Brazil is the country with the highest prevalence of cases of leishmaniasis [5], with records in all regions, especially in 
the North, Northeast and Midwest [8 ], and annual incidence of 26.000 cases [6, 7, 9, 10].
L. donovani and L. infantum are responsible for visceral leishmaniasis (VL), whereas cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) is 
attributed to L. brasilienses, L. peruviana, L. major, L. tropica, L. aethiopica, L. Mexicana and L. amazonensis, which are among 
the different species that cause this disease in humans [11– 13].
Despite its global relevance, leishmaniasis is still neglected as a public health problem. Since the 1940s, the pharma-
cological treatments available for the disease (antimonials and amphotericin B) remained practically the same [8 , 13, 
14], and are associated to low therapeutic rates and significant side effects, limiting their use and effectiveness [14, 15].

2  Leishmaniasis and its pathophysiology
Leishmaniasis is a parasitic disease that affects millions of people worldwide, being the second largest parasitic disease 
in terms of incidence, with about 1 million new cases each year. It causes significant morbidity and mortality and is con-
sidered a disease neglected by the World Health Organization (WHO). Leishmaniasis is an important global public health 
problem [5, 22]. Additionally, the anticipated increase in numbers is driven by escalating human mobility, encompassing 
travel and forced migration, alongside the growth of reservoir host populations as well as the expansion and spread of 
vector species are influenced by climate and habitat changes, urbanization, and globalization [23].
The main vector responsible for transmission of leishmaniasis is the sand fly, an insect belonging to the order Diptera, 
family Psychodidae, subfamily Phlebotominae, which infects the host through the bite of the female of the genus Phle -
botomus [3, 4]. A wide variety of mammals are identified as vertebrate hosts of the parasite, including wild and domestic 
animals, as well as humans, which can act as reservoirs of infection [18, 24]. Most likely, humans are merely accidental 
hosts. In regions where zoonotic leishmaniasis is prevalent, such those in Latin America and the Mediterranean countries, 
dogs are thought to be the primary domestic reservoirs of the infection in humans [25].
The life cycle of Leishmania (Fig. 2) is heteroxenic and presents two morphological phases: the promastigotes, elon-
gated, flagellated and extracellular forms, which inhabit the intestinal tract of the sand fly; and the amastigotes, rounded 
forms, without free flagellum and intracellular, found in the cells of the reticulum endothelium and in the bloodstream 
of the vertebrate host (e.g., humans, dogs) [26].
The female sand fly is infected by feeding on the contaminated blood of an infected host. Following transmission by 
sand flies, Leishmania parasites invade macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells in the vertebrate host [27]. The pro-
mastigotes are released during the next mosquito bite and subsequently infect the vertebrate host. The promastigotes 
are phagocytosed by neutrophils and macrophages that respond to the tissue damage during the bite. And within the 
phagolysosome, the transformation of promastigotes forms into amastigotes forms, and then begin the reproduction. 
Amastigotes are released when lysis of the macrophage occurs and after that will infect other macrophages. The cycle 
continues when a mosquito ingests free amastigotes or amastigote-infected macrophages during the bite [18]. The 
establishment of a protective immune response requires the synchronised activity of cells from both the innate and 
adaptive immune response [27].
The infecting species and the host’s immune response determine the clinical manifestations of the disease, such as 
VL and CL, which are subdivided into CL and Cutaneomucosal Leishmaniasis (CML) [12, 28]. It is important to highlight 
that the Leishmania genus comprises more than 30 species, twenty of which are pathogenic for humans [29– 31].
CL appears at the site of a mosquito bite, which introduces promastigotes into the skin, where the protozoa proliferate 
as amastigotes in macrophages. After activation of macrophages by sensitized lymphocytes, lysis of amastigotes occurs, 
triggering a red papule that turns into a lesion with purple edges. Despite healing, which occurs after several months 
and results in atrophic scars, treatment is necessary due to the patient’s weakness. Severe forms of CL may occur, such as 
lupoid, loss of body parts and, in half of the cases, disfigurement of the face throughout the disease [32]. This disease is 
endemic in 88 countries and Brazil registers the highest number of cases, especially in the North, Northeast and Midwest 
regions, with 41% of cases occurring in the State of Pará [8].
VL occurs in the endothelial system of the reticulum through an infection in phagocytic cells corresponding to metas-
tasis of parasites and contaminated cells from the initial site of infection [33]. The infection caused by VL reaches the 
visceral organs, when the protozoa migrate to the spleen and liver, causing symptoms such as fever, weight loss and 
hepatosplenomegaly associated with pancytopenia, low albumin and hypergammaglobulinemia. If not treated properly, 
VL is fatal in 95% of cases [34]. Due to its incidence and high lethality, visceral leishmaniasis has become an important 
public health problem in particular, in South America. It is estimated that VL is endemic in more than 70 countries and 
affects approximately 0.2 to 0.4 million new cases and 40,000 deaths each year, making it the second leading parasitic 
disease that causes death. Brazil has an occurrence of 90% of all cases registered in the Americas [35, 36].
Although leishmaniasis presents a high global prevalence, there are no vaccines nor the drugs currently used have 
limitations, such as limited effectiveness, important side effects, high toxicity, in addition to resistance [37]. Thus, there 
is a continuous search for a treatment that is more effective against leishmaniasis and presents fewer side effects, which

Cell death in Leishmania is a complex process that involves several mechanisms, such as the induction of oxidative 
stress, the disturbance of cellular ionic balance and the activation of proteins involved in the regulation of apoptosis [38]. 
Recent studies have used techniques such as analysis of cell morphology, flow cytometry and electronic microscopy to 
identify characteristics associated with cell death, such as DNA fragmentation, exposure of phosphatidylserine in the 
cell membrane and the formation of autophagic vacuoles [39].
One of the main mechanisms involved in Leishmania cell death is the induction of oxidative stress. In this process, 
there is an increase in the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that cause damage to lipids, proteins and cellular DNA. 
This damage can lead to changes in cell membrane permeability, loss of ionic homeostasis and, consequently, to cell 
death [39].
In addition to oxidative stress, other mechanisms are also important for cell death in Leishmania. For example, the acti-
vation of proteins involved in the regulation of apoptosis, such as the Bax protein, can lead to the release of cytochrome 
c proteins from the mitochondria, which in turn activate caspases, enzymes responsible for the degradation of cellular 
proteins and, therefore, death. mobile [40].
Leishmania cell death in Leishmanial parasite is different from the apoptosis found in mammalian cells. Leishmania  
cells undergo a programmed death, which involves the activation of processes such as DNA fragmentation, exposure 
of phosphatidylserine in the cell membrane, loss of plasmatic membrane integrity and release of cell contents [38].

therapies against this disease. The identification of new compounds capable of inhibiting the activity of glycoprotein

methods for diagnosing and monitoring the effectiveness of therapies.
3  Traditional treatments for leishmaniasis
The treatment of leishmaniasis, in general, is considered difficult, time-consuming and costly. The therapeutic options 
used for its control are associated with high toxicity, difficult administration and resistance, and the success rate vary 
significantly based on geographic region, treatment duration, and the species of parasite involved. Considering all the 
diversity in relation to the species and forms of disease manifestation, the pharmacotherapy of leishmaniasis is practi-
cally the same, with few drug options [14, 41–43].
Despite the importance of leishmaniasis in the world, few advances have been achieved in its treatment. In the 1910s, 
the first evidence showed that tartar emetic was effective in the treatment of leishmaniasis [44]. However, due to the 
severity of undesirable side effects and the toxic effects of tartar emetic, trivalent antimonials have been replaced by pen-
tavalent stimulant compounds. The first examples of this class were urea stibamine, a ureic derivative of p-aminophenyl 
stibinic acid, and sodium antimony gluconate, commercially known as  Pentostam® (Glaxo Wellcome) or  Solustibosan® 
(Bayer). Later, another example of this class was developed, N-methyl glucamine antimoniate, marketed as  Glucantime® 
(Rhône-Poulenc-Rohrer) or meglumine antimoniate. Pentavalent antimonials, such as  Pentostan® and  Glucantime®, were 
established as chemotherapeutic agents in the 1940s, being the first line of treatment for leishmaniasis [13, 14, 44]. 
However, 60% of cases of leishmaniasis are resistant to pentavalent antimonials [9].
The metabolism and mechanism of action of pentavalent antimonials are still not well elucidated [45, 46]. It is not 
clear whether the active metabolite of pentavalent antimonials is Sb (V) or Sb (III), since in the body Sb (V) seems to 
play a prodrug role, with a view reduced to Sb (III) [47– 49]. As a second treatment option, pentamidine, amphotericin 
B (AmB), miltefosine and paromomycin are used in case of resistance, due to high toxicity, but they do not have such a 
favourable therapeutic index and also have important adverse reactions (Table  1) [13, 14].
Additionally, there is evidence demonstrating that combination therapies are frequently utilized to reduce toxicity, 
improve drug tolerability, and overcome drug resistance. Current combinations include AmB with miltefosine or paro -
momycin, along with other drugs or pentavalent antimonials together with immunomodulators [16, 50].
Pentamidine has antifungal, antibacterial, antitrypanosomatid, antiviral and antitumor activity, and its mechanism 
of action interferes with the synthesis of polyamines, by inhibiting the use of S-adenosyl-L methionine, which inhibits 
enzymes such as ornithine decarboxylase and spermidine synthetase, consequently, prevents the synthesis of funda-
mental molecules for the life of the protozoan [51, 52]. Studies show that pentamidine has the ability to significantly 
increase the effectiveness of mitochondrial electron transport chain complex II inhibitors [ 53]. Furthermore, this drug 
binds to regions rich in A-T DNA sequences, causing its death [52]. Table 1 summarizes the currently used medicines for 
the treatment of Leishmaniasis.
Pentavalent antimonials and pentamidine are the most used drug in the treatment of leishmaniasis. When the treat-
ment with these two drugs is not effective, AmB is used. This drug has antifungal and leishmanicidal activity that acts 
on the sterols of the protozoan membrane, where there is an osmotic imbalance responsible for the death of the vector. 
Characteristics such as high molecular weight and amphoteric nature make it difficult to penetrate the skin, thus, its 
administration in liposomes is very useful in the treatment, as it increases its therapeutic index since it will be directed 
to the infected macrophages of the liver, spleen and bone marrow [52].

fosine in promastigotes of L. amazonensis induces a process of cell death programmed by apoptosis, determined by the 
externalization of phosphatidylserine, incorporation of propidium iodide, arrest of the cell cycle in the sub-G0/ phase G1

As Leishmania and fungi have similarities, such as ergosterol, which is the main steroid in plasma membranes, the use 
of antifungals in the treatment of leishmaniasis was suggested. Thus, it was observed that different species of Leishmania 
have different degrees of susceptibility to sterol synthesis inhibitors. Ketoconazole, itraconazole and clotrimazole are 
effective in the treatment of lesions caused by L. mexicana and L. major, and clotrimazole can be effective when used 
topically. On the other hand, ketoconazole when administered alone against L. brasiliensis is not effective, but when used 
in combination with terbinafine it becomes more effective [55, 56].
Currently, there is no universally accepted treatment guideline for leishmaniases. Since the 1920s, pentavalent antimo-
nies have been used as first-line option in treating this disease. However, these drugs have demonstrated severe adverse 
effects such as hepatotoxicity, pancreatic toxicity and cardiotoxicity [57]. Furthermore, resistance to these compounds has 
increased significantly in recent years [58, 59]. Such effects limit the use of these drugs and, mainly, their effectiveness. 
In order to find a solution to these problems, drugs such as miltefosine, amphotericin B, allopurinol, paromomycin and 
pentamidine have demonstrated positive efficacy results in the treatment of Leishmaniosis [60]. But, like antimonies, 
their use has certain limitations. These drugs have toxic side effects, drug resistance problems, high costs and challenging 
administration methods since most of the drugs that are available are for parenteral administration, with the exception 
of antifungals, which requires patient cooperation, as many abandon treatment for this reason [61–63]. Having said these 
problems and failed treatment attempts, it is extremely important to find a solution for anti- leishmania treatment that 
is safe, comfortable for patients and, at the same time, economically viable [64].
In this sense, with the aim of developing more effective drugs, with less harmful adverse reactions for the host, efforts 
need to be directed towards investing in new technologies, new substances and new treatment approaches/strategies 
against this disease. In this field, biotechnology can be applied to the development of drugs that present strategies 
against the mechanism that Leishmania presents to guarantee its survival.
4  Promising drugs for the treatment of leishmaniasis
In a study by Sangenito et al. (2016) [65], aspartyl peptidase inhibitors, such as nelfinavir and lopinavir, tested in trypomas-
tigotes forms of T. cruzi, induced strong mitochondrial swelling linked to reduced hydrolytic activity of dehydrogenases 
and plasmatic membrane depolarization; drastically affecting the plasmatic membrane in the flagellar region. These 
molecules also increased the generation of ROS, and resulted in the typical lipid metabolism disorder with accumulation 
of intracellular lipid droplets; and dilation of both the nuclear envelope (without DNA disruption) and the endoplasmic 
reticulum (with formation of autophagosomes) [66]. All these effects culminated in irreversible metabolic lesions that 
lead to the death of T. cruzi [ 67]. Morphological and ultrastructural changes, such as rounding, reduced cell size and 
flagellar shortening were also observed. Nelfinavir and lopinavir also inhibit aspartic peptidase and proteasome activi-
ties [65]. The evaluation of the effect of aspartyl peptidase inhibitors in other forms of the protozoan has already been 
observed, both in the amastigotes and epimastigotes forms of T. cruzi, and showed promising results contributing to 
the elucidation of the main mechanism of action of these drugs against these protozoa [68, 69]. The evaluation of the 
effect of lopinavir against the promastigote forms of L. amazonensis was also evaluated, and lopinavir generated lipid 
accumulation and affected the lipid composition in a concentration-dependent manner [1 ].
Another class that has shown potential therapeutic properties for leishmanial parasites are phytocompounds, espe -
cially phenolic compounds and their derivatives, with different biological responses against the protozoa [70]. These 
are widely distributed in nature and can come from the tropical forests of South America and Africa, such as Auronas, 
Lignans, Essential Oils, Saponins, Quinones, Alkaloids, Tannins, Chalcones, Iridoids, Flavonoids, Isoflavonoids, Terpenoids, 
Terpenes and Oxylipins [71, 72]. Marinho et al. [54] showed that the treatment of promastigotes of L. amazonensis with 
a calpain inhibitor, for 72 h at concentrations referring to IC50 and 2  × IC50, showing ultrastructural changes, such as 
intense vacuolization in the cytoplasm and cellular disorganization.
Vendrametto et al. [73] evaluated the effect of eupomatenoid-5 against L. amazonensis, and observed that the para-
site L. amazonensis underwent considerable ultrastructural changes, among them, the appearance of intense exocytic 
activity in the region of the flagellar pocket, figures similar to vacuoles in the cytoplasm of parasites treated with 9.0 µg/
mL. Cells treated with 25.0 µg/mL showed a very large structure, apparently an extension of the endoplasmic reticulum.

Previous studies by have investigated the potential effect of ICaf anti-leishmania on the promastigote and amastig-
ote forms of cutaneous (L. amazonensis) and visceral (L. chagasi) leishmaniasis [70, 76, 77]. Pharmacologically, ICaf is a 
well-tolerated drug, with good availability in the in vivo model in uncontaminated mice and which has a potent anti-
amastigote action [70, 76]. Thus, ICaf stands out as a promising compound and may emerge as a potential alternative for 
the treatment of leishmaniasis [70, 77]. Thus, the anti-Leishmania activity of this drug may be similar or superior when 
compared to traditional drugs, with regard to toxicity.
The search for compounds that can inhibit gp63 activity has been an important strategy for the development of new 
therapies against leishmaniasis. Several studies have been carried out with the aim of identifying compounds capable of 
inhibiting gp63 activity, including natural, synthetic and medicinal plant-derived compounds. Some studies have shown 
that gp63 inhibition can lead to Leishmania cell death [38, 54].
In a recent study by Kumari el al. [78], with the aim of evaluating the anti-leishmania effect of indocyanine green and 
iohexol, the studies aimed to demonstrate the inhibitory capacity of Leishmania donovani Sterol C-24 methyltransferase, 
an enzyme of the sterol metabolic pathway essential for the integrity and functions of the parasite [78]. Research has 
shown that indocyanine green and iohexol exert anti-leishmania effects, causing significant morphological changes, such 
as a fair amount of ROS generation and a decrease in intracellular ATP levels along significantly disrupted mitochondrial 
membrane potential, which leads to cell apoptosis [78].
In another recent study, conducted by Santos et al. [79], new compounds, such as lecithins, were explored for the 
treatment of Leishmaniasis. In this study, the objective involved evaluating the leishmanicidal effect of concanavalin A 
(ConA) on L. infantum promastigotes. As a result of the tests, it was observed that ConA interacts with the glycans of L. 
infantum, which results in an inhibition of promastigote growth [79]. Furthermore, it has also been demonstrated that 
this compound has the ability to interfere with the integrity of the parasite membrane and induce the production of 
ROS, leading to its death. Additionally, ConA combined with amphotericin B (AmB) was tested. These two compounds 
showed synergy, enabling the reduction of the necessary dose of AmB, mitigating its toxicity [79].
Nahanji et al. [80] carried out a study that addressed the treatment of cutaneous leishmaniasis with fluconazole (FLZ) 
and its limitations so that, with the aim of improving the treatment of this condition, nanotechnology was used and FLZ 
nanoemulsions (NEs) were produced [80]. The developed formulations demonstrated improved anti-leishmania activity 
compared to the free FLZ formulation. An increased ability to combat promastigotes and a considerable increase in the 
ability to combat amastigotes were obtained. Additionally, FLZ-Nes demonstrated a remarkable ability to target parasites 
within infected macrophages, attacking the site of infection. In in vivo studies, rats treated with FLZ-NE showed reduced 
disease severity, relief from tissue damage and improved ulcer healing [80].

5  Conclusions
Leishmaniasis is included in the list of neglected diseases, therefore, it is considered relevant in the Brazilian scenario 
and, although primarily distributed in rural areas and in small centers, the disease is expanding to more developed urban 
areas in the country. As a neglected disease, the treatment options are so fare limited to expensive drugs, with high 
toxicity and are associated with the appearance of undesirable characteristics, such as parasitic resistance and the need 
for hospitalization of the patient for the therapeutic treatment. Drugs used to treat leishmaniasis are associated with low 
therapeutic rates and significant side effects, such as cardiotoxicity, hepatitis, nephrotoxicity, pancreatitis, hypokalemia, 
nausea and vomiting, in addition to parasitic drug resistance. Such effects limit the use of these drugs and, mainly, their 
effectiveness. In addition, most of the drugs that are available are for parenteral administration, with the exception of 
antifungals, which requires patient cooperation, as many abandon treatment. Thus, the search for new drugs able to 
be administered orally or topically is a priority to increase patient’s compliance, improve the treatment outcomes and

to foster scientific research towards developing new, more sustainable drug alternatives in which biotechnology can be
