</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>In seven families, six different mutant alleles of <em>TRIOBP</em> on chromosome 22q13 cosegregate with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic deafness. These alleles include four nonsense (Q297X, R788X, R1068X, and R1117X) and two frameshift (D1069fsX1082 and R1078fsX1083) mutations, all located in exon 6 of <em>TRIOBP.</em> There are several alternative splice isoforms of this gene, the longest of which, <em>TRIOBP-6,</em> comprises 23 exons. The linkage interval for the deafness segregating in these families includes <em>DFNB28.</em> Genetic heterogeneity at this locus is suggested by three additional families that show significant evidence of linkage of deafness to markers on chromosome 22q13 but that apparently have no mutations in the <em>TRIOBP</em> gene.</p></section><hr/>
<p>Hereditary hearing loss unaccompanied by other associated clinical features is referred to as “nonsyndromic” and is a genetically heterogeneous neurosensory disorder (Friedman and Griffith <a>2003</a>; Morton <a>2004</a>; Smith <a>2004</a>). We ascertained ∼600 families segregating nonsyndromic deafness. Here, we report 12 of these families, 7 of which are segregating mutant alleles of <em>TRIOBP</em> on chromosome 22q13. The mouse ortholog <em>Triobp</em> (also referred to as “<em>Tara</em>”) encodes a protein that appears to be involved in cytoskeletal organization (Seipel et al. <a>2001</a>).</p>
<p>After written informed consent was obtained, consanguineous families segregating deafness as an autosomal recessive trait were recruited to our protocols. Approval for this study was obtained from institutional review boards in Pakistan, India, and the United States (at the National Institutes of Health under protocol OH93-N-016). Affected members of 11 of the 12 families reported herein (<a>fig. 1</a>) had prelingual, severe-to-profound hearing loss (audiograms shown in <a>fig. 2</a>) without any obvious associated symptoms or signs, such as dysmorphology, night blindness, vestibular abnormalities, goiter, and renal or cardiac defects, that might represent a syndromic form of deafness (Petit et al. <a>2001</a>). Physical examinations, including vestibular-function testing, funduscopy, and/or electroretinogram, were conducted on at least two affected individuals from each of these families. Hearing was evaluated using pure-tone audiometry. In family PKDF324, retinitis pigmentosa segregated independently of deafness (data not shown).</p>

<figcaption><p>Pedigrees of 12 consanguineous families from Pakistan and India. Blackened symbols represent individuals with clinically documented congenital deafness. <div><div><p><span>Note.—</span> All the primers were designed using <a>Primer3</a>.</p></div></div></section><p>No mutations of <em>TRIOBP</em> were identified in affected individuals from families PKDF324, PKSR19A, PKSR28A, IDM-17, and PKDF228. Three of these five families have significant LOD scores (<a>table 1</a>) indicating linkage of deafness to chromosome 22q13 markers, which suggests the presence of additional unknown exons of <em>TRIOBP,</em> undetected mutations, or locus heterogeneity.</p>
<p>From the mouse inner-ear cDNA library, we found previously reported short isoforms (<em>Triobp-1</em> and <em>Triobp-2</em>) and three additional isoforms (<em>Triobp-3, Triobp-4,</em> and <em>Triobp-5</em> [<a>GenBank</a> accession numbers <a>DQ228000</a>, <a>DQ228002</a>, and <a>DQ228001</a>, respectively]) (<a>fig. 5</a>). The longest isoform (<em>Triobp-3</em>) has 25 exons, of which 21 are coding and would produce a protein of 2,014 aa (224 kDa) that is 65% identical to human <em>TRIOBP-3</em> (<a>fig. 5</a><em>A</em> and <a>5</a><em>B</em>). Four consecutive exons of mouse <em>Triobp-3</em> (exons 5–8) correspond to the sequence encoded by exon 6 (3,319 bp) of human <em>TRIOBP.</em> In addition to characterizing the isoforms of <em>Triobp,</em> we also determined the expression pattern of <em>Triobp</em> isoforms in various tissues. <em>Triobp-1</em> is widely expressed (<a>fig. 5</a><em>C</em>), as reported elsewhere (Seipel et al. <a>2001</a>), whereas other isoforms have a more limited pattern of expression (<a>fig. 5</a><em>C</em>). <em>Triobp-3</em> is present in the brain, liver, kidney, retina, and inner ear, whereas <em>Triobp-5</em> is found in the whole embryo, brain, kidney, retina, pancreas, and inner ear (<a>fig. 5</a><em>C</em>). <em>Triobp-4</em> seems to be expressed in the kidney, retina, testis, and inner ear (<a>fig. 5</a><em>C</em>).</p>

<figcaption><p>Genomic structure of <em>Triobp</em> mouse isoforms and their expression in various tissues. <em>A, Triobp</em> splice variants. There are at least five different splice variants present in the mouse inner ear, including the previously reported short isoforms (<em>Triobp-1</em> and <em>Triobp-2</em>). Locations of the primers used for RT-PCR expression profile are shown as arrowheads. <em>B,</em> Predicted protein products of different <em>Triobp</em> splice variants. <em>Triobp-4</em> does not encode the PH domain and the coiled-coil regions. <em>Triobp-1</em> encodes only the PH domain and the four coiled-coil domains. <em>C,</em> Expression profiles of <em>Triobp-1, Triobp-3/5,</em> and <em>Triobp-4.</em> PCR primers (<a>table 2</a>), shown as arrowheads in panel A, were designed to amplify each isoform from cDNA prepared from different mouse tissues. <em>Left profile, Triobp-1</em> is widely expressed, whereas other isoforms have a more limited pattern of expression. <em>Triobp-3</em> (<em>arrow</em>) is present in the brain, liver (faint band), kidney (faint band), retina (P15), and inner ear (P1–P5). <em>Middle profile,Triobp-5</em> (<em>arrowhead</em>) is found in mouse embryo cDNA (E11.5), brain, kidney (faint band), retina, pancreas (faint band), and inner ear. <em>Right profile, Triobp-4</em> seems to be expressed in kidney (faint band), retina, testis (faint band), and inner ear. These transcripts do not provide an exhaustive list of the isoforms of <em>Triobp.</em> A smaller, uncharacterized cDNA is present in many tissues (<em>middle profile</em>). There are 33 inner-ear RIKEN ESTs for <em>Triobp</em> (168–488 bp; not shown). PCR amplification of <em>Actn1</em> cDNA encoding α-actinin was used as a control for the quality and quantity of RT-PCR templates.</p></figcaption></figure><p>Relatively little is know about the function of Triobp. In a yeast two-hybrid screen, a Triobp isoform equivalent to Triobp-1 (<a>fig. 5</a><em>B</em>) was identified as a protein that interacts with Trio, which is a member of a large family of Dbl homology guanine nucleotide exchange factors (DH-GEFs). DH-GEFs are involved in activating Rho guanosine triphosphatases (Rossman et al. <a>2005</a>), which mediate a variety of cellular functions, including actin remodeling (Rossman et al. <a>2005</a>). Triobp is also reported to have filamentous actin (F-actin) bundling activity on the basis of an in vitro binding assay in HeLa cells (Seipel et al. <a>2001</a>). Moreover, Triobp-1 stabilizes F-actin structures, as indicated by the relative resistance of Triobp-1–expressing cells to latrunculin B, an F-actin destabilizer (Seipel et al. <a>2001</a>). Perhaps in the inner ear, the function of Triobp-1 is important for formation or stabilization of the cytoskeletal structure of stereocilia and/or the cuticular plate, both actin-rich structures in sensory hair cells (Frolenkov et al. <a>2004</a>). However, all six mutations causing deafness in humans are located in exon 6 of <em>TRIOBP</em> (equivalent to exons 5–8 of mouse <em>Triobp</em>), which encodes an amino acid sequence that is not present in Triobp-1 (<a>fig. 5</a>). Given the complex alternative splicing of this gene, it appears that a variety of isoform-specific mutations of <em>Triobp</em> and antisera to Triobp will be essential to understanding the normal function of this gene in the auditory system.</p>
