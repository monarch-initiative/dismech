# Systemic sclerosis and primary biliary cholangitis: a systematic review of case-control studies comparing isolated and overlapping disease phenotypes.
**Authors:** Nursoy M, Mehmetoglu E, Syal A, Pasula R, Parperis K, Choudhary C, Derk CT
**Journal:** J Scleroderma Relat Disord (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1136/jsrd-2026-000002](https://doi.org/10.1136/jsrd-2026-000002)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: To explore variations in disease presentation between isolated systemic sclerosis (SSc), primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) and SSc-PBC overlap syndrome.

DESIGN: Systematic review conducted according to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines.

SETTING: Literature search of PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar for studies published through June 2025.

PARTICIPANTS: 13 studies (from 1331 initially identified records) including patients with isolated SSc, isolated PBC and SSc-PBC overlap were included. Eligible studies were case-control or cohort designs reporting demographic, serological or clinical outcomes.

INTERVENTIONS: Not applicable.

MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Differences in clinical phenotype, serological markers, systemic involvement and mortality between SSc-PBC overlap and isolated disease groups.

RESULTS: Patients with SSc-PBC overlap demonstrated distinct clinical characteristics compared with isolated disease phenotypes. Anticentromere antibody and antimitochondrial antibody positivity were significantly higher in overlap patients. The overlap group predominantly included patients with limited cutaneous SSc and had a higher prevalence of extrahepatic autoimmune diseases, particularly Sjögren's syndrome and Hashimoto's thyroiditis. Systemic involvement, including pulmonary, musculoskeletal and cardiac manifestations, was less pronounced in overlap patients compared with isolated SSc. Liver-related mortality was higher in the PBC-only group, whereas mortality in the overlap group was primarily attributable to SSc-related causes.

CONCLUSIONS: SSc-PBC overlap syndrome represents a distinct clinical and immunological entity. Recognition of this phenotype may facilitate earlier diagnosis, guide management strategies and improve patient outcomes.

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) and primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) are autoimmune diseases that can coexist, with prior studies suggesting shared immunologic features such as anticentromere antibody (ACA) and anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA). However, the clinical phenotype, systemic involvement and outcomes of patients with SSc–PBC overlap compared to isolated SSc or PBC remain incompletely characterised.

This study demonstrates that SSc–PBC overlap represents a distinct clinical phenotype with unique serological and clinical features. Overlap patients more frequently exhibit ACA and AMA positivity, are more likely to have limited cutaneous SSc, and have higher rates of extrahepatic autoimmune diseases such as Sjögren’s syndrome and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Compared with isolated SSc, systemic involvement is generally milder, while compared with PBC alone, overlap patients have increased rates of renal and pulmonary complications. Mortality patterns also differ, with liver-related mortality predominating in PBC-only patients and SSc-related causes in overlap patients.

Recognition of SSc–PBC overlap as a distinct entity may improve diagnostic accuracy and risk stratification, prompting earlier screening for associated autoimmune conditions and tailored management strategies. These findings support the need for future prospective studies to better define disease mechanisms and optimise multidisciplinary care approaches for this population.

 Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a chronic, multisystem connective-tissue disease characterised by immune dysregulation, microvascular damage and progressive fibrosis of changes in the skin and internal organs.1 2

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), formerly known as primary biliary cirrhosis, is a chronic autoimmune cholestatic liver disease marked by gradual destruction of the intrahepatic bile ducts, leading to fibrosis and, eventually, cirrhosis.3

The association between PBC and other autoimmune diseases as well as overlap syndromes in SSc has been extensively documented. A large 2005 cohort study reported that 32% of patients with PBC had at least one additional autoimmune condition,4 with more recent estimates reaching as high as 60%.5 Among those extrahepatic autoimmune conditions, rheumatological disorders, particularly Sjögren’s syndrome (SjS), are the most frequently reported.58 A meta-analysis by Deng et al9 reported a 35% prevalence of PBC-SjS’s overlap.9 Likewise, polyautoimmunity has been increasingly recognised in patients with SSc.10 In 2013, Elhai et al11 reported that nearly 25% of patients with SSc have coexisting autoimmune conditions, most commonly autoimmune thyroiditis (10.4%), SjS (7.7%) and inflammatory myopathies (5.6%).10 A 2016 study further underscored this finding, reporting a polyautoimmunity rate of 43.3% in patients with SSc.11

The earliest recognition of PBC–SSc association dates back to Milbradt in 1934, with additional cases later described by Murray-Lyon et al12 (1970) and Reynolds et al13 (1971), the latter leading to the term ‘Reynolds syndrome’.1214 Since then, prevalence studies have demonstrated that 2–3% of patients with SSc have overlapping PBC, whereas the prevalence of SSc in PBC has been estimated to be around 8%.15

The overlap between SSc and PBC is marked by distinctive immunohistopathological and clinical features, which together present a unique diagnostic puzzle.1416 At the core of their pathogenesis lies fibrosis, driven by common mechanisms involving both innate and adaptive immunity.5 17 Both diseases are marked by elevated TGF-β (transforming growth factor-beta) and IL-6 (Interleukin-6),16 along with clonally expanded CD8+ T cells expressing the TCRBV3 (T-cell receptor beta variable 3) region.15 These profibrotic cytokines play a crucial role in Th17 (T helper 17) and regulatory T cell differentiation and contribute to mesenchymal cell activation.16 The shared immunological environment ultimately leads to characteristic fibrotic histology: predominantly around the periportal region in PBC and perivascular or interstitial region in SSc.3 12

The association between SSc and PBC is further supported by overlapping autoantibody profiles. Antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA), a hallmark of PBC, are found in 80–95% of patients with PBC and in 25–30% of SSc cases.15 Anticentromere antibodies (ACA), which are highly specific (97%) and predictive for SSc,13 have also been detected in patients with PBC.18 19 In a study by Marí-Alfonso et al,20 ACA positivity was observed in 46% of 1572 patients with SSc, with a notable increase up to 73% among those with hepatobiliary involvement.21 Additionally, a Japanese study comparing the immunological features of patients with PBC–SSc, SSc alone and PBC alone found ACA positivity rates of 80%, 100% and 25%, respectively.19

HLA genes HLADQB1, HLADRB1, HLADQA1 are shared between PBC and SSc. However, these are not exclusive to either disease. In addition, non-HLA susceptibility genes IRF5 and STAT4 have been identified in both PBC and SSc.14 STAT4 has been associated with ACA positivity in PBC, an autoantibody already present in over 50% of patients with limited cutaneous SSc (lcSSc).22 23

Some clinical features are also shared by these two conditions. Fatigue is a prominent and debilitating symptom in both diseases. PBC typically presents with pruritus and slowly progressive jaundice, which may overlap with gastrointestinal involvement in SSc. While skin thickening, joint pain and Raynaud’s phenomenon could be the presenting signs of SSc, these manifestations may also occur in PBC due to chronic systemic inflammation.3 20 22

Although several studies have investigated the overlap between PBC and SSc, they often examine the topic from distinct rheumatological or hepatological perspectives. The primary objective of this study is to evaluate how the coexistence of SSc and PBC is described across these two specialties. By integrating the differing perspectives, this systematic review aims to identify shared features and explore key differences in demographics, autoantibody profiles and clinical characteristics of PBC–SSc overlap, compared with each disease in isolation.

We conducted a systematic review following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines to identify and evaluate studies examining the overlap between SSc and PBC.

A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar using the following search terms:

“Systemic Sclerosis” OR “Scleroderma” AND “Primary Biliary Cirrhosis” OR “Primary Biliary Cholangitis.” Our search included articles published through June 2025. A total of 1331 records were initially identified through database searches: 379 from PubMed, 832 from Scopus and 120 from Google Scholar. After removing duplicates and conducting a preliminary screening, 86 full-text articles were selected for detailed review. Of these, 74 were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 61 articles were excluded for the following reasons: non-comparative study design (n=19), non-relevant populations (n=19), absence of pertinent outcome data (n=12) and lack of full-text availability (n=11). 13 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final analysis (figure 1; online supplemental table 1).62333 Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist based on the individual study design (figure 2).

We included case–control or cohort studies that compared either:

SSc versus SSc–PBC, or

PBC versus PBC–SSc.

Studies had to be in English and provide extractable data on demographic, serological or clinical outcomes. Case reports, editorials and review articles were excluded.

Two reviewers independently extracted data from the included studies, focusing on population characteristics, autoantibody profiles, clinical manifestations and outcomes. Studies were categorised into two comparative groups:

SSc versus SSc–PBC (n=5).

PBC versus PBC–SSc (n=8).

Descriptive synthesis was used due to heterogeneity in study design and outcome reporting.

We identified 13 studies that compared patients with either SSc to those with SSc–PBC overlap, or patients with PBC to those with SSc–PBC overlap. Published between 1992 and 2024, these studies encompassed diverse geographical regions including Europe, North America and Asia. Most used retrospective cohort or cross-sectional designs, while three employed prospective cohort methodologies. Sample sizes ranged from 41 to 1554 participants (online supplemental table 1). Across these studies, patients with SSc–PBC overlap consistently exhibited a distinct clinical and serological profile compared with those with SSc or PBC alone (table 1).

In a 2023 multinational cohort study, Lepri et al reported significantly higher ACA positivity in patients with SSc–PBC compared with those with SSc alone. The overlap group also had fewer digital ulcers and a lower frequency of interstitial lung disease (ILD). However, baseline cardiac assessments revealed a higher mean systolic pulmonary arterial pressure (sPAP) in the SSc–PBC group. Among the 276 patients in the study, the mortality rate in the SSc–PBC group was 6.1%, higher than in the SSc-only group, though not all deaths were directly attributable to SSc. Additionally, the SSc–PBC group had a greater prevalence of extrahepatic autoimmune diseases, notably Hashimoto’s thyroiditis.29

In a 2021 study, David et al30 reported that AMA positivity was a distinguishing feature of patients with SSc–PBC overlap, while ACA positivity was comparable across both groups. Patients with overlap disease were more likely to have lcSSc and had significantly lower rates of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD), musculoskeletal involvement and ILD. Notably, individuals with SSc–PBC were more likely to have SjS compared with those with SSc alone.25

Cavazzana et al31 (2011) identified significantly higher levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), AMA (by indirect immunofluorescence) and ACA levels in patients with SSc–PBC compared with SSc alone. Consistent with the findings of David et al,30 the lcSSc variant was more prevalent among patients who are PBC screen-positive (80%) than among patients who are PBC screen-negative (63%).26 Akimoto et al32 (1999) reported that AMA was exclusively present in the overlap group, while ACA was significantly more frequent. Lung fibrosis was markedly less common in the SSc–PBC group, who demonstrated higher rates of calcinosis and telangiectasia. Additionally, the overlap group had higher frequency of SjS, reinforcing patterns found in other studies.27 Sargin et al (2019) found that AMA-M2 was present only in patients with SSc–PBC, who also had lower rates of pulmonary and cardiac involvement compared with those with SSc alone (table 1).28

Zheng et al34 (2017) demonstrated that ACA positivity was significantly higher in the SSc–PBC group (82.6%) compared with none in the PBC-only group. Patients with overlap disease also had a longer duration of PBC prior to SSc diagnosis (11.1 vs 7.3 years) and experienced a longer history of Raynaud’s phenomenon (18.5 vs 11.6 years). Additional features that distinguished the overlap group included higher rates of abnormal capillaroscopy (73.9% vs 3.9%), sclerodactyly, digital ulcers and cutaneous telangiectasia.34

Efe et al (2021) conducted a large multinational retrospective cohort study including 1554 patients with PBC, of whom 46 had coexisting SSc–PBC and were classified under the category of extrahepatic autoimmune diseases (EHAIDs). Compared with those with PBC alone, patients with EHAIDs were significantly more likely to be female (92.5% vs 86.1%) and exhibited higher rates of seropositivity for antinuclear antibody (ANA), AMA and anti-smooth muscle antibodies (SMA). At baseline, they also had lower levels of ALP, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and total bilirubin, suggesting less cholestasis and hepatocellular injury. Additionally, the EHAID group showed a lower incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (13 cases vs 29 in the PBC-only group), but a markedly higher burden of rheumatologic disorders, emphasising the complex clinical and immunologic phenotype of overlap patients.6

Rigamonti et al (2011) conducted a prospective cohort study in the UK including 580 patients with PBC, 43 of whom had SSc–PBC overlap. Patients in the overlap group had significantly lower rates of pruritus and fatigue at diagnosis compared with those with PBC alone. However, they had significantly higher numbers of episodes of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and septicaemia compared with those with PBC alone. Notably, liver-related mortality was lower in the SSc–PBC group (2 vs 6), while all six SSc-related deaths occurred in the overlap cohort. The 5-year survival based on the Mayo risk score was 84% in the overlap group versus 96% in PBC-only patients.24

Wang et al35 (2013) conducted a retrospective cohort study of 322 Chinese patients with PBC, including 9 with SSc–PBC overlap. ILD was significantly more prevalent in the overlap group compared with PBC-only patients (33.3% vs 7.6%). Overlap patients also had higher rates of arthralgia (22.2% vs 21.5%), Raynaud’s phenomenon (44.4% vs 18.6%) and renal involvement (11.1% vs 5.2%), suggesting a more systemic and multi-organ autoimmune phenotype.29

In a 2016 cross-sectional study, Mari-Alfonso et al compared patients with PBC to those with systemic autoimmune disease (SAD)–PBC and found a significantly higher prevalence of extrahepatic features such as Raynaud’s phenomenon, telangiectasia and inflammatory arthritis in the overlap group.30 Tojo et al36 (2002) reported that patients with SSc–PBC had a significantly higher prevalence of oesophageal varices compared with those with PBC alone (28.6% vs 9.3%). The 10-year survival rate was higher in the overlap group (87.5% vs 45.5%). Patients with SSc–PBC had lower AST (39.8 vs 63.6 IU/L) and IgM levels (460 vs 676 mg/dL), lower AMA titres (1:80 vs 1:160) and a higher frequency of a discrete speckled ANA pattern (93.5% vs 12.3%). In the overlap group HLA-DR9 was more prevalent (54.5% vs 24.3%) and anti-centromere protein (CENP B titres (1.22 vs 0.31) were higher. No significant correlation was found between the two groups in terms of splenomegaly intensity, and the prevalence of accompanying SjS was also similar (19.2% vs 20.6%).31

In Shoji et al37 (1992), a cross-sectional observational study of 41 patients, those with SSc–PBC (n=12) had lower mean anti-M2 titres and total bilirubin levels compared with PBC-only patients.32 Abe et al38 (2024) evaluated 302 patients with PBC, comparing those with and without SSc overlap. Patients with SSc-PBC exhibited significantly lower levels of ALT (alanine aminotransferance), γ-GTP (gamma glutamyl transferance) and total bilirubin compared with those with PBC alone. Notably, liver-related mortality was higher in the PBC-only group (34 deaths vs 3), while SSc-related deaths occurred exclusively in the overlap cohort. When liver transplant-free survival rates were compared, the PBC–SSc group showed a significantly better prognosis.33

Across all included studies, ACA positivity was the most consistent serological feature of SSc–PBC overlap, reported at significantly higher rates in the overlap group in 9 of 10 studies that evaluated it. AMA positivity was also frequently elevated or exclusively present in patients with SSc–PBC as compared with patients with SSc. Clinical manifestations such as ILD, digital ulcers, GERD and musculoskeletal involvement were less common in overlap patients compared with patients with SSc, supporting the notion of a milder systemic phenotype. However, the overlap group did not uniformly demonstrate a better prognosis; in some cohorts, non-hepatic complications such as SSc-related mortality and oesophageal varices were more prominent (table 1).

Autoimmune diseases frequently coexist and interact, with specific overlaps driven by shared genetic, environmental and pathogenetic factors. SSc, positioned at the crossroads of polyautoimmunity, is most often associated with autoimmune thyroid disease, followed by SjS. This overlap between PBC and SSc has been reported with a weighted prevalence of 3.0% among patients with SSc.10

While PBC is often described in association with SjS, affecting approximately one-fifth of patients, Marí-Alfonso et al proposed that this rate might be overestimated, noting that the prevalence of PBC–SjS dropped to 4% when positive serology was required, thereby placing SSc at the forefront.30 This observation was further supported by an Italian study of 170 PBC cases, in which SSc was likewise the most frequent connective tissue disease association.39

In this systematic review we have gathered 13 studies comparing the clinical, laboratory, genetic and phenotypic characteristics of patients with PBC in SSc, and patients with SSc in PBC.

Shared pathogenic backgrounds have been proposed for PBC and SSc, as both conditions are characterised by a fibrogenic response.23 Although the molecular targets of these two diseases are distinct, they converge on similar clinical outcomes, sclerosis in SSc and cirrhosis in PBC.28 This suggests that shared antigenic epitopes may contribute to common effects on the fibrogenic pathway.

CENP-C is a protein crucial for kinetochore assembly.40 Autoantibodies in PBC–SSc overlap recognise epitopes within CENP-C. Supporting this, Akimoto et al demonstrated that sera from patients with PBC–SSc more frequently reacted with (CENP-C) compared with sera from patients with SSc alone.27 35 Likewise, Shoji et al37 reported that patients with PBC–CREST had a higher frequency of reactivity to the 140 kD CENP-C polypeptide.32 Increased reactivity to the CENP-C 140-kD polypeptide in PBC complicated by SSc had already been described by McHugh et al41 in 1990.41

Genetic studies highlight that the susceptibility loci for PBC overlap with those implicated in other autoimmune diseases including rheumatoid arthritis, SSc, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory bowel disease and psoriasis.6 42 43 HLA-DQB1, HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 alleles have been reported as shared susceptibility loci between PBC and SSc in Caucasian and Hispanic patients,14 while Tojo et al36 identified a higher prevalence of HLA-DR9 in Japanese patients with PBC–CREST compared with those with PBC alone.31

PBC is most frequently observed in patients with lcSSc,23 25 26 particularly in middle-aged women,232528 and typically presents later in the disease course.27 This delayed presentation may reflect the gradual progression of immune dysregulation within a chronic inflammatory milieu, with endothelial injury eventually extending to hepatobiliary involvement. The predominance of PBC in limited rather than diffuse subsets also aligns with the slower tempo of organ involvement in ACA-positive SSc, where vascular and serological abnormalities often precede overt clinical disease.44

The most prominent serological feature of the overlap is the higher incidence of ACA. In all studies comparing SSc–PBC to SSc only, ACA positivity is higher in the overlap group.232528 Similar results are also obtained in four studies comparing the overlap patients to the PBC-only group.25 34 38 45

Another autoantibody of interest was AMA. Sargın et al33 reported a higher prevalence of AMA positivity in patients with Reynolds syndrome compared with those with SSc alone.28 Conversely, when compared with PBC-only patients, Tojo and Abe et al38 observed lower AMA and frequency in the overlap group, respectively.31 33

Autoimmune conditions like Sjögren and Hashimoto were common in the overlap group.23 25 27 while two other studies stated no significant correlation.36 45 Although this frequent complication of SjS in SSc–PBC overlap is not surprising, given that patients with PBC frequently develop SjS,25 27 Marí-Alfonso et al (SAD cohort) and Shoji et al37 (CREST cohort) likewise reported higher rates of sicca features and SjS, respectively, in overlap patients compared with those with PBC alone.30 32 Moreover, capillaroscopic anomalies were reported more frequently in patients with PBC when they had additional SAD or SSc.30

Interestingly, the PBC–SSc overlap has been reported to have a more favourable clinical prognosis than PBC alone.25 26 Several authors24 32 33 also suggested a lower liver-related mortality in the overlap group which supports the better prognosis highlighted by two studies.31 33 There were inconsistent results about cirrhosis progression. One study indicated a higher progression in the overlap group25 whereas a lower progression rate was found in another study.33

Patients with overlap require early initiation of ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) to prevent cirrhosis.25 Clinicians should therefore remain vigilant: in patients with PBC, systematic screening for Raynaud’s phenomenon, SSc-specific antibodies and the use of nailfold capillaroscopy where available is recommended, whereas in patients with SSc, regular assessment of antibody profiles together with liver function testing may facilitate the earlier detection of PBC.24 27

The SSc–PBC overlap syndrome represents a distinct clinical and immunological entity. Compared with patients with either SSc or PBC alone, individuals with overlap disease consistently exhibit higher rates of ACA positivity,232528 frequent detection of AMA26 28 and generally milder visceral organ involvement. Overlap patients also exhibited a distinct organ involvement profile compared with SSc-only cases such as decreased occurrences of ILD, GERD, musculoskeletal involvement and vascular involvement at follow-up.23 25 These findings were observed across both retrospective and prospective studies, underscoring the presence of a stable and reproducible clinical phenotype. This distinct profile has important implications for early diagnosis, individualised management strategies, and long-term prognostic assessment in patients with SSc-PBC overlap.

The following limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results of the study. Heterogeneity existed across study samples, as some studies included patients with CREST, others focused on patients with SAD and others compared patients with EHAID. Second, some of these studies were limited by small sample size, limiting statistical power. Another limitation is the variability of diagnostic criteria across studies and missing data which may have potentially affected the reported outcomes. Despite these limitations, we have transparently presented the data, highlighting the specific groups being compared. Our results are promising and should be validated by larger sample sizes. Ultimately, this may increase clinical awareness, promote earlier diagnosis and guide more effective management strategies.

The SSc–PBC overlap cohort demonstrates significant serological differences as compared with the PBC-only and SSc-only patient cohorts. This overlap is consistently marked by high ACA and AMA positivity. Clinically, patients with overlap disease demonstrate milder systemic involvement than SSc-only patients, with lower occurrence of interstitial lung disease, gastro-oesophageal reflux and musculoskeletal involvement. Compared with PBC-only, overlap patients have higher rates of renal complications, interstitial lung disease and extrahepatic autoimmune diseases such as SjS and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. They also have lower levels of liver enzymes and bilirubin levels. Evidently, the distinct clinical characteristics of the overlap cohort support the need to identify it as a separate subgroup as opposed to the combination of the isolated disease phenotypes.

These distinct features underscore the importance of recognising SSc–PBC overlap as a separate clinical entity, rather than a simple co-occurrence of two isolated diseases. Mortality in the overlap group is primarily related to SSc, whereas liver-related mortality is higher in the PBC-only cohort. Furthermore, overlap patients demonstrate improved liver transplant-free survival rates, suggesting a relatively more favourable hepatic prognosis.

Given these findings, early identification of the SSc–PBC overlap is critical. Prompt initiation of UDCA therapy may help prevent progression to cirrhosis. Enhanced clinical vigilance, including routine screening for overlapping features, can facilitate earlier diagnosis, optimise management strategies, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.
