# Recurrent pericardial effusion due to Hennekam lymphangiectasia-lymphedema syndrome: a case report and literature review.
**Authors:** Zhang R, Zhang J, Rao L, Chen Z, Wang M
**Journal:** BMC Cardiovasc Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12872-025-04842-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-025-04842-1)

## Content

Diagnosing and treating recurrent pericardial effusion (PE) have been challenging in clinical practice. This study presents a middle-aged male with long-term refractory PE without apparent cause and non-specific symptoms, and eventually diagnosed as Hennekam lymphangiectasia with lymphedema syndrome (HKLLS). He underwent anti-tuberculosis diagnostic treatment and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for nonspecific PE, and thoracic duct exploration, terminal adhesion lysis, and anastomosis of the tributary vertebral vein of the thoracic duct for suspected protein-losing enteropathy and intestinal lymphangiectasia in other hospitals 17 and 11 years ago, but with no satisfactory outcomes. During the disease course, laboratory tests showed decreased blood albumin and increased stool α1-antitrypsin, while endoscopy biopsies were normal. The patient's complex medical history underscores the diagnostic challenges. The whole-exome sequencing identified two mutations within the collagen and calcium-binding EGF-like domain-containing protein 1 (CCBE1) gene, diagnosing HKLLS type 1. This case enriches the understanding of the link between HKLLS and recurrent PE, highlighting the significance of whole-exome sequencing in diagnosing recurrent PE when traditional methods fall short. It also reminds clinicians to consider rare genetic disorders like HKLLS in the differential diagnosis of recurrent PE, even without typical symptoms.

Pericardial effusion (PE) is the abnormal accumulation in the pericardial space and is a common clinical condition. When it becomes recurrent, the diagnosis and treatment pose significant challenges in clinical practice. Recurrent PE often leads to complex clinical scenarios, as it may be caused by various factors, including infections, autoimmune diseases, neoplasms, and idiopathic origins. Identifying the underlying cause of recurrent PE is crucial for effective management. However, in many cases, despite comprehensive diagnostic evaluations using traditional methods such as physical examination, laboratory tests, imaging studies, and even invasive procedures, the cause remains elusive. This diagnostic uncertainty not only hampers the selection of appropriate treatment but also increases the risk of complications and adverse outcomes for patients. Among the rare diseases that can present with recurrent PE, Hennekam lymphangiectasia with lymphedema syndrome (HKLLS) is an extremely rare genetic disorder. HKLLS is characterized by lymphatic dysplasia, which can lead to various manifestations, including lymphangiectasia in different organs and lymphedema. The involvement of the lymphatic system in HKLLS can disrupt the normal fluid balance and drainage, potentially resulting in the accumulation of fluid in the pericardial cavity, presenting as recurrent PE.

A middle-aged male patient with long-term recurrent PE had undergone multiple diagnostic treatments and surgical procedures in other hospitals over the years without a precise diagnosis. This patient’s complex medical history, including the lack of response to common treatments for non-specific PE and the negative results from various examinations such as endoscopy and pathological biopsy, highlights the difficulties in diagnosing such challenging cases. The use of whole-exome sequencing in this case finally provided a breakthrough in diagnosis by identifying two mutations within the collagen and calcium-binding EGF-like domain-containing protein 1 (CCBE1) gene, and the patient was diagnosed with HKLLS type 1. This not only elucidated the underlying cause of the patient’s recurrent PE but also demonstrated the power of advanced genetic testing techniques in solving complex diagnostic problems in clinical practice. This case report not only enriches the understanding of the association between HKLLS and recurrent PE but also emphasizes the importance of whole-exome sequencing in the diagnostic workup of recurrent PE, especially when traditional diagnostic methods fail to provide clear evidence. It also serves as a reminder for clinicians to consider rare genetic disorders, such as HKLLS, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, or Williams syndrome, in the differential diagnosis of recurrent PE, even in the absence of typical clinical features of these disorders.

A 39-year-old Chinese Han male was admitted to our cardiology department due to repeated facial and lower limb swelling for over 17 years.

17 years ago, the patient had no discomfort such as fever, chest tightness, cough, sputum, or night sweats. Chest X-ray revealed an enlarged heart shadow, and the ultrasound cardiogram (UCG) indicated PE in a local hospital. The pericardial puncture was performed, 50 mL of yellow fluid was extracted, and no positive findings was tested. Isoniazid, rifampicin, and streptomycin were administered over one month for diagnostic treatment of ‘tuberculous (TB) pericarditis’. However, he developed scalp and facial edema, and there was still a large amount of PE during the follow-up examination. One month later, at a second hospital, 300 mL of yellow fluid was aspirated during pericardiocentesis, and the liver function test showed decreased albumin. He was treated with Meloxicam for nonspecific PE. After being discharged, he took traditional Chinese medicine on his own, and the edema was alleviated. Since then, repeated facial swelling persisted, and multiple UCGs all indicated moderate to large amounts of PE. 12 years ago, he visited a third hospital, and it was considered that the PE was related to hypoproteinemia. He got better after receiving albumin supplementation. 11 years ago, he went to a fourth hospital, the causes of the PE and hypoproteinemia were undetermined, but protein-losing enteropathy (PLE) and small intestinal lymphangiectasia were highly suspected. One month later, he underwent thoracic duct exploration surgery with terminal adhesion lysis and anastomosis in a fifth hospital. During the operation, multiple enlarged cervical lymph nodes (1 cm in diameter, purplish red in color, tough in texture, and with acceptable mobility) could be seen. Diuretics, potassium supplementation, and antiplatelet agents were given after surgery. He kept taking traditional Chinese medicine and medium-chain triglycerides for a long time after being discharged. However, the PE remained, along with occasional general fatigue, palpitation, and shortness of breath. Multiple UCGs still showed large-volume PE, leading to his admission to our hospital for further treatment (See Table 1).

After a comprehensive evaluation, combining clinical manifestations, laboratory data, and imaging results, eventually, the patient was diagnosed with Hennekam syndrome concomitant with PE. Advice on a high-protein and low-fat diet mainly composed of medium-chain and short-chain triglycerides was given, along with regular follow-up.

The pericardium consists of parietal and visceral layers, with a small amount of lubricating fluid in the normal pericardial cavity. The elastic properties of the pericardium and epicardium cooperate to inhibit ventricular dilation and determine the heart’s passive pressure-volume relationship. PE is the excessive fluid accumulation in the pericardial cavity, caused by various factors like idiopathic, infectious, etc. It can be classified by hemodynamic impact, presence of cardiac tamponade (CTP), and fluid characteristics [1]. As PE volume increases, the pericardium’s positive pressure on the epicardium rises, especially affecting the right ventricle. Slowly accumulating PE flattens and shifts the cardiac pressure-volume relationship, increasing volume and decreasing compliance at the same pressure. Excessive effusion volume and high pressure restrict cardiac diastole, limit filling, reduce output, and trigger CTP [1].

PE may be asymptomatic and detected incidentally. Symptom severity depends on fluid volume and accumulation rate. Small effusions may cause no or mild symptoms, like chest pain and palpitations. As effusion volume increases, dyspnea, orthopnea, fatigue, and edema may occur. In CTP cases, decreased blood pressure, pulsus paradoxus, and jugular vein distension may be present. UCG is the preferred diagnostic method for suspected PE. It assesses effusion volume, location, nature, cardiac tamponade, constriction, and guides pericardiocentesis accurately. Chest X-ray may show an enlarged ‘flask-like’ heart shadow. Cardiac MRI and CT can display PE and cardiac structures, with advantages in evaluating effusion nature. Laboratory tests on aspirated fluid help clarify the etiology, and relevant primary-disease exams like autoantibody and thyroid function tests are also carried out [2].

Treatment of PE targets different etiologies: anti-infection for infectious cases, autoimmune disease treatment, and anti-tumor therapy for neoplastic causes. When CTP or large-volume effusion with hemodynamic disorders and severe symptoms occurs, prompt pericardiocentesis/drainage is needed to evacuate fluid, relieve cardiac pressure, and improve symptoms. Without hemodynamic disorders, management depends on the presence of pericarditis. For inflammatory PE, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and glucocorticoids can relieve the inflammatory response. In non-pericarditis cases, management is based on patient tolerance and symptom severity, and diuretics may be used to reduce edema after careful consideration, since diuretics reduce the end-diastolic pressure, they can also worsen CTP [3].

PLE is the body’s inability to adequately compensate for the loss of intestinal plasma proteins, shown by elevated fecal α1-antitrypsin and hypoproteinemia without liver or kidney diseases. As a syndrome, identifying its underlying causes is crucial. Many acquired and congenital diseases can cause PLE, usually by damaging the intestinal mucosa or obstructing lymphatic vessels, leading to lymphangiectasia and impaired intestinal lymphatic drainage [4]. PLE features hypoproteinemia, edema, nutritional deficiencies, infections, and gastrointestinal symptoms like diarrhea, steatorrhea, abdominal pain, and vomiting. Hypoproteinemia is non-selective, with decreased albumin and immunoglobulin levels. Edema and serous cavity effusions result from reduced blood colloid osmotic pressure, while infections are induced by hypogammaglobulinemia and lymphopenia. In children, severe malabsorption and malnutrition can cause growth and development retardation [5].

The pathological mechanisms of PLE involve direct intestinal mucosa damage (erosive or non-erosive) and lymphatic drainage disorders, causing retrograde flow into the intestinal lumen. Circulating proteins and cells move from capillaries to the interstitium, are collected by lymphatic vessels, and are transported back to the blood. In erosive diseases, proteins diffuse from capillaries, while in non-erosive ones increase epithelial permeability. In intestinal lymphangiectasia, lacteals dilate, leak, and rupture. Increased capillary permeability worsens PLE. All diseases affect distal duct anterograde flow, reversing lymphatic fluid flow, and dilating and rupturing lacteals. Diseases like inflammatory bowel disease, peptic ulcer, infection, tumor, systemic lupus erythematosus, tuberous sclerosis, allergic diseases, and lymphatic reflux-related disorders can trigger PLE by damaging the intestinal mucosa, dilating lymphatic vessels, impairing lymphatic reflux, and causing protein loss into the intestinal lumen [4].

Diagnosing PLE first requires ruling out non-gastrointestinal hypoproteinemia causes and similar-symptom diseases. Further investigation can involve non-invasive thorax and abdomen imaging, cardiovascular function assessment, or invasive gastrointestinal imaging with tissue biopsy. α1-antitrypsin, a serum glycoprotein like albumin, is used to assess gastrointestinal protein loss by measuring fecal levels in random samples or, more precisely, the serum clearance rate with 24-hour fecal collection. Endoscopy and biopsy can detect anatomical issues, while abdominal MRI or CT can identify lesions. In focal intestinal lymphangiectasia, lymphangiography and related techniques can show lymph leakage sites for interventional therapy. Molecular studies like genomic DNA sequencing aid accurate diagnosis. Hereditary PLE is suggested by family history, early onset, and parental consanguinity. Genetic testing has identified PLE-causing diseases and enabled treatment development [6]. Each diagnostic step targets different disease aspects to find PLE’s root cause and secondary complications. In this case, whole-exome gene sequencing showed two CCBE1 gene mutations, linking PLE to HKLLS type 1.

Treatment of PLE aims to diagnose and correct underlying diseases and manage PLE manifestations by reducing gastrointestinal protein loss and sequelae. This includes correcting fluid-electrolyte imbalances, nutritional management, trace element restoration, and preventing organ complications. Albumin infusion offers short-term stabilization, while immunoglobulin replacement and antibiotic prophylaxis suit patients with recurrent infections and severe immunodeficiency. Dietary modification, especially a high-protein, low-fat diet with medium short-chain triglycerides, can improve PLE when lymphatic drainage disorders are involved. Overall, treatment is empirical as evidence-based symptomatic guidelines are lacking for most PLE-causing diseases. Treating the primary cause is key, and surgical resection may help non-responsive patients with localizable lesions [5]. New gene discoveries and drugs like Pozelimab, a monoclonal complement C5 inhibitor antibody, offer new treatment options, such as for CD55-deficiency patients with related severe PLE [7].

HKLLS is a rare autosomal recessive syndromic lymphedema with a prevalence below 1/1,000,000. Defective lymphatic vessel development links PLE, intestinal lymphangiectasia, and primary lymphedema. Affected children have lymphedema at birth, with extensive lymphatic dysplasia in multiple organs, especially the lower extremities and genitalia. Lymphangiectasia can occur in the intestine, lungs, thyroid, and kidneys [8]. Clinical manifestations include lymphangiectasia, lymphedema, abnormal facial features (flat face, broad nasal bridge, etc.), growth retardation, delayed puberty, variable intellectual disability, etc [9]. Previous studies reported a total of 35 Hennekam syndrome cases with these symptoms, showing that CCBE1 mutations are crucial in pathogenesis. Currently, there’s no curative treatment, but decongestive therapy for edema control may be beneficial [10, 11]. Research showed that Hennekam syndrome has genetic heterogeneity. Approximately 25% of cases are due to CCBE1 gene mutations. CCBE1 activates vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGFC) and promotes lymphatic vessel formation [12]. FAT atypical cadherin 4 (FAT4) gene mutations can also cause this syndrome. Hennekam syndrome has an allelic relationship with Van Maldergem syndrome, with high phenotypic overlap [13]. ADAM metallopeptidase with thrombospondin type 1 motif 3 (ADAMTS3) is related to HKLL, binding to CCBE1 to activate VEGFC and regulate lymphatic vessel formation [14]. Pathogenic mutations in the CCBE1, FAT4, and ADAMTS3 genes lead to HKLLS types 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Only one Hennekam syndrome case with PE was reported. A 38-month-old male infant had dyspnea, abnormal facial features, prepuce hyperplasia (consistent with Hennekam syndrome), left eye and right foot lymphedema, enlarged heart, and large-volume PE. Symptoms improved after pericardiocentesis and drainage [15]. In our case, the patient had hypoproteinemia, multi-serosal cavity effusions, and high fecal α1-antitrypsin, clinically consistent with PLE. Endoscopic biopsy showed moderate chronic mucosal inflammation, and whole-exome sequencing finally diagnosed HKLLS. The patient lacked facial malformations and cognitive impairment, potentially due to a CCBE1 heterozygous mutation.

Several factors led to the delayed diagnosis of this complex HKLLS case. Firstly, its extreme rarity and the lack of awareness among clinicians meant that HKLLS was often overlooked in differential diagnosis, even when faced with persistent symptoms like recurrent PE and hypoproteinemia. Secondly, the patient’s fragmented treatment history across multiple hospitals, with each institution adopting different diagnostic and treatment approaches, hindered a comprehensive understanding of the condition and led to information loss or miscommunication. Finally, the patient’s diverse symptoms, which mimicked more common conditions such as TB and autoimmune diseases, further delayed the identification of the underlying genetic defect, as common diagnoses were prioritized over rare genetic disorders like HKLLS.

In conclusion, diagnosing HKLLS poses significant challenges due to its highly heterogeneous clinical phenotypes and the absence of standardized international diagnostic guidelines. The combination of the rarity of the syndrome, the lack of clinician awareness, and the fragmented nature of the patient’s treatment history across multiple institutions all converged to cause a significant delay in the accurate diagnosis of this patient’s condition. This case report, supplemented by a comprehensive literature review, enriches the current understanding of the association between HKLLS and recurrent PE. It serves as a crucial reminder for clinicians to consider rare genetic disorders, such as HKLLS, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, or Williams syndrome, during the differential diagnosis of recurrent PE, even in the absence of typical clinical features of these disorders. Furthermore, this case underscores the importance of whole-exome sequencing in diagnosing recurrent PE, especially when conventional diagnostic methods prove inconclusive. To prevent similar diagnostic delays in the future, adopting a holistic approach to patient care, providing continuous medical education on rare diseases, and enhancing communication and data sharing among healthcare providers are imperative.
