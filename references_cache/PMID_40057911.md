# T-cell Dependency of Tumor Regressions and Complete Responses with RAS(ON) Multi-selective Inhibition in Preclinical Models of Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma.
**Authors:** Orlen M, Vostrejs WP, Sor R, McDevitt JC, Kemp SB, Kim IK, Kramer AB, Tovbis Shifrin N, Markosyan N, Clendenin C, Singh M, Quintana E, Menard M, Vonderheide RH, Stanger BZ
**Journal:** Cancer Discov (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1158/2159-8290.CD-24-1475](https://doi.org/10.1158/2159-8290.CD-24-1475)

## Content

UNLABELLED: Activating mutations in KRAS drive tumorigenesis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), promoting tumor cell proliferation and contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, rendering PDAC tumors insensitive to immunotherapy. RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitors, such as daraxonrasib (RMC-6236) and RMC-7977, target the active state of RAS, with potent antitumor activity in PDAC murine models. In this study, we report that RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition led to rapid and profound PDAC regressions in immunocompetent mice, decreasing myeloid cells and increasing T cells and macrophages in the tumor microenvironment. The depth and duration of tumor regression depended on T cells and conventional dendritic cells. Moreover, the combination of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitors with immunotherapy conferred deeper and more durable tumor regressions, including complete responses not seen with either treatment alone. In summary, concurrent inhibition of mutant and wild-type RAS is active in concert with T-cell immunotherapy, revealing RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitors as a potential therapeutic immuno-sensitizing strategy in PDAC.

SIGNIFICANCE: RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitors enhance antitumor immunity in preclinical models of PDAC, resulting in deeper and more durable responses when combined with immunotherapy. These findings support the clinical evaluation of immune-based strategies that may prolong the response to RAS inhibitor therapies as well as overcome issues of therapeutic resistance to inhibitors alone. See related commentary by Lasse Opsahl and Pasca di Magliano, p. 1537.

Most pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) tumors carry activating mutations affecting the small GTPase KRAS (1). Mutations prolong KRAS activity in its GTP-bound state, leading to enhanced cancer cell proliferation, survival, and invasion (1). In addition to these cell-autonomous effects, mutant KRAS drives the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) dominated by myeloid cells and dampens antigen presentation (2–5). This immunologically “cold” TME may explain why PDAC tumors rarely respond to T cell–dependent immunotherapies (6, 7).

The emergence of allele-specific KRAS inhibitors has changed the therapeutic approach to KRAS-mutant non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) for tumors bearing KRASG12C mutations (8). Nevertheless, almost all patients with NSCLC treated with KRASG12C inhibitors develop clinical resistance, often in association with an array of secondary KRAS mutations or bypass mechanisms involving hyperactivation of signaling through wild-type (WT) RAS (9–12). Although KRASG12C mutations are rare in PDAC, patients with pancreatic cancer treated with KRASG12C inhibitors exhibit similar patterns of resistance (13). These observations highlight the opportunity for RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitors that target multiple RAS variants in an allele-agnostic manner by forming a tri-complex with cyclophilin A and GTP-bound RAS, sterically blocking RAS–effector interactions (14). In an ongoing phase I/Ib clinical trial of patients with KRAS-mutated cancer (NCT05379985), the RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitor daraxonrasib (RMC-6236) has exhibited clinical activity in preliminary findings (14).

Recently, we and others reported that small-molecule inhibition of KRASG12D, the most common mutant allele of KRAS in PDAC (40%), is highly effective in multiple preclinical PDAC models (13, 15–17). Importantly, these studies showed that inhibiting KRAS signaling in cancer cells had a profound impact on the surrounding TME and that depth and duration of tumor regressions were T cell–dependent (15), corroborating genetic and pharmacologic studies in PDAC, NSCLC, and colorectal cancer models (18–21).Thus, targeting mutant KRAS could result in both cell-autonomous and non–cell-autonomous antitumor activities. However, because these studies used inhibitors selective for the G12D mutant allele, the direct effects of the compounds were limited to cancer cells harboring the mutation. The consequences of concurrently inhibiting mutant and WT RAS signaling in tumor and/or stromal cells have been less well characterized (14, 22, 23), in particular in models of PDAC. This question has particularly important implications for immune cells, as multiple studies have implicated WT RAS and the MAPK pathway in signaling through the T cell receptor and downstream effects on T-cell activation, differentiation, and function (24–28).

RMC-7977 is a preclinical tool compound representative of a class of oral RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitors, including the investigational drug candidate daraxonrasib, which targets the active GTP-bound forms of both mutant and WT KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS (22). In preclinical models, RMC-7977 exerted potent antitumor activity across multiple RAS-addicted cancers, including PDAC (22, 23, 29). Mice treated with this inhibitor had no significant body weight loss at doses tested despite effective inhibition of MAPK signaling in the tumor, a finding consistent with the favorable side effect profile observed so far in patients with RAS-mutant cancers treated with daraxonrasib (14, 23). Thus, normal tissues previously thought to depend on RAS signaling for homeostasis (30, 31) seem to maintain function when exposed to RMC-7977 at active concentrations. Whether this normal tissue−sparing effect of a RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitor extends to immune cells has not been explored comprehensively. In this study, we used an assortment of immunocompetent mouse models of PDAC to evaluate (i) the extent to which the adaptive immune system is required for the antitumor effects of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition, (ii) the potential synergy between chemotherapy or immunotherapy and RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition, and (iii) the direct effects of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition on T-cell activation, priming, and expansion.

The KPC/Y model of PDAC—in which mutant KrasG12D, mutant Trp53R172H, and a YFP lineage marker are expressed in pancreatic epithelial cells—recapitulates many of the histologic and molecular features of the human disease (32, 33). To determine whether immune cell infiltration in the TME affects the antitumor response to RMC-7977, we utilized congenic clonal cell lines derived from C57BL/6 KPC/Y mice that reproducibly give rise to tumors with varying amounts of T-cell infiltration when reimplanted into immunocompetent mice (Supplementary Fig. S1A), as previously reported (34). Subcutaneous tumors derived from these cell lines show patterns of immune infiltration and histologic and stromal features comparable with those arising in the orthotopic setting (34). We first investigated the antitumor activity of RMC-7977 in tumors that have a paucity of T cells (termed “T cell–low”), which are largely resistant to immunotherapy. We subcutaneously implanted 6419c5 tumor cells, a T cell–low line, into C57BL/6 hosts. Once tumors reached a size of 20 to 100 mm3, mice were treated with the RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitor RMC-7977 (25 mg/kg once daily) or vehicle for 7 days and sacrificed for analysis 4 hours after the last dose. All RMC-7977–treated tumors (8/8) regressed in this timeframe, whereas all vehicle-treated tumors (8/8) progressed (Fig. 1A); however, no complete responses (CR) were achieved with continued treatment over time (Supplementary Fig. S1B). To confirm these findings in the orthotopic setting, we injected 6419c5 cells directly into the pancreas under ultrasound guidance (35). After 7 days, tumor growth was significantly inhibited in RMC-7977–treated mice (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Next, to evaluate the compound’s effect on tumors of an intermediate immune phenotype, we treated mice bearing subcutaneous tumors derived from the KPC 4662 cell line (36) engineered to express either KrasG12D or KrasG12C (15). All 4,662 tumors regressed after 7 days of RAS(ON) inhibition, with 30% to 40% of tumors exhibiting a CR (Fig. 1B and C). Finally, we assessed the antitumor activity of RMC-7977 on mice bearing 2838c3, a T cell–high cell line, and found that 9/10 (90%) of tumors had a CR after 7 days of treatment (Fig. 1D). Thus, RMC-7977 has a potent antitumor effect across multiple immunocompetent PDAC models, such that the depth of response correlates with baseline levels of intratumoral T-cell infiltration.

RMC-7977 has been shown to suppress MAPK signaling in normal and cancer cells (22, 23). To assess MAPK activity in our models, we performed co-immunofluorescence (IF) for phosphorylated ERK1/2 (pERK), a downstream component of the MAPK pathway. After 7 days of RMC-7977 treatment, pERK expression was significantly reduced across subcutaneous and orthotopic tumors (Supplementary Fig. S2A–S2C). Loss of pERK staining was associated with a decrease in Ki-67–positive tumor cells and an increase in cleaved caspase-3–positive tumor cells compared with vehicle-treated tumors (Fig. 1E and F), indicating that RMC-7977 suppresses tumor cell proliferation while increasing tumor cell apoptosis.

We previously showed that allele-specific inhibition of KRASG12D altered the fibroblastic and matrix components of the tumor stroma (15). RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition with RMC-7977 for 7 days had similar effects, including an increase in collagen deposition and αSMA+ myofibroblastic cancer-associated fibroblasts (Fig. 1G–J), which was consistent across subcutaneous and orthotopic models (Supplementary Fig. S3A–S3C). In summary, RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition with RMC-7977 promotes stromal changes across models similar to those observed with allele-specific inhibition of KRASG12D, suggesting that these changes are attributable to inhibition of RAS signaling in the cancer cells. Although we observe comparable changes to the tumor stroma and equivalent pERK suppression across all models, antitumor activity is increased in T cell–infiltrated tumors, suggesting there is an immune-mediated contribution.

To determine the effects of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition on immune cell biology, we performed flow cytometry and IF-based immunophenotyping of subcutaneous 6419c5 PDAC tumors treated with RMC-7977 or vehicle control. As expected, the abundance of tumor cells decreased after 3 doses of RMC-7977 (72 hours); importantly, the tumor cells that remained exhibited increased expression of MHC class I (Fig. 2A). Next, we investigated the effects of RMC-7977 on the myeloid compartment of 6419c5 subcutaneous tumors at the 72-hour timepoint by flow cytometry. Within total CD11b+ myeloid cells, we observed a decrease in both Ly6G+/Ly6Clo and Ly6G−/Ly6Chi subsets [previously shown functionally to be myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) in this model (37, 38)] in tumors treated with RMC-7977 compared with vehicle-treated tumors (Fig. 2B). As MDSC infiltration is known to be driven by oncogenic KRAS in PDAC (5, 38), this finding suggests that RMC-7977 mediates these effects by inhibiting oncogenic KRAS in cancer cells rather than through a direct effect on MDSCs. We also observed a decrease in CD103+ dendritic cells (DC; Fig. 2C), consistent with our previous findings with inhibition of KRASG12D (15).

We next characterized the composition and phenotype of tumor-infiltrating macrophages. Although flow cytometry did not reveal a significant effect of RMC-7977 on total CD11b+ F4/80+Ly6G- macrophages after 72 hours of treatment, the ratio of CD206loMHC-IIhi to CD206hi MHC-IIlo subsets was significantly increased (Fig. 2D). Using IF-based phenotyping, we observed an increase in F4/80+ macrophages in some tumors after 72 hours of treatment (Fig. 2E) and in virtually all tumors after 96 hours of treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4A), suggesting a time-dependent increase in macrophage abundance. Further characterization of these macrophages revealed an increase in expression of iNOS (Supplementary Fig. S4B) but not ARG1 (Supplementary Fig. S4C). RMC-7977 has been shown previously to selectively decrease the viability and increase the apoptosis of M2- but not M1-polarized bone marrow–derived macrophages in vitro (29). Taken together, these data suggest that RMC-7977 may preferentially increase the abundance of M1-polarized macrophages within the TME.

Next, we focused our attention on the T-cell compartment. We observed an increase in the abundance of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells after 72 hours of RMC-7977 treatment (Fig. 2F and G), consistent with the observed decrease in immunosuppressive myeloid cells. FoxP3+CD4+ T regulatory cells are often plentiful in PDAC tumors; however, the increase in CD4+ T cells upon RMC-7977 treatment was not attributable to this population (Supplementary Fig. S4D). CD8+ T cells in 6419c5 tumors treated with RMC-7977 expressed higher levels of granzyme B (Fig. 2H), a key enzyme involved in targeted cell killing by cytotoxic lymphocytes, as well as an increase in central memory (CD44+CD62L+) and effector memory (CD44+ CD62L−) CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2I). These results suggest that RMC-7977 relieves some of the inhibitory effects on T cells in PDAC tumors, ultimately leading to increased infiltration of effector and memory CD8+ T cells.

Given that RMC-7977 promotes antitumor macrophage and T-cell infiltration into T cell–low tumors, we next asked whether these immune cells retained proliferative activity in the setting of WT RAS inhibition. To test this, we costained for Ki-67 and immune cell markers in 6419c5 tumors treated with RMC-7977 for 72 hours. In contrast to the inhibitory effects of RMC-7977 on cancer cells, compound treatment resulted in an increase in Ki-67 staining in both F4/80+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells in the tumors (Supplementary Fig. S4E and S4F). Thus, RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition can potentiate macrophage and T-cell proliferation.

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that this early increase in proliferating immune cells might contribute to the compound’s antitumor effects. To test this, we first investigated macrophage and T-cell infiltration in PDAC tumors after 7 days of treatment with RMC-7977. T cell–low (6419c5) subcutaneous and orthotopic tumors had a significant increase in F4/80+ macrophages but not CD8+ T cells after 7 days (Supplementary Fig. S5A and S5B). By contrast, 4662G12C (T cell–intermediate) and 2838c3 (T cell–high) subcutaneous tumors had a significant increase in both F4/80+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells at the 7-day timepoint (Supplementary Fig. S5C and S5D). These results suggest that baseline tumor immune signatures influence the durability of antitumor immunity—particularly CD8+ T-cell infiltration—upon RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition.

To further assess the effect of RMC-7977 on T cells, we studied tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLN), a major site of T-cell priming. To evaluate this in both T cell–high and –low settings, mice bearing 2838c3 or 6419c5 subcutaneous tumors were treated for 7 days with RMC-7977, and TDLNs were harvested for analysis. In mice bearing T cell–high 2838c3 tumors, the abundance of total CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. S6A) and Ki-67+ CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. S6B) increased with 7 days of RMC-7977. Further analysis of the CD8+ T-cell population revealed increased expression of TNFα and TNFβ with RMC-7977 (Supplementary Fig. S6C and S6D), suggesting RMC-7977 may increase CD8+ T-cell effector function. In contrast, the TDLNs of mice bearing T cell–low tumors failed to exhibit significant increases in T-cell infiltration or markers of activity (Supplementary Fig. S6E–S6H). In summary, RMC-7977 treatment leads to an increase in proliferation and effector phenotypes of CD8+ T cells in a manner that correlates with the underlying makeup of the tumor-immune microenvironment.

To identify tumor-intrinsic mechanisms by which RMC-7977 may alter immune cell infiltration in the TME, we treated T cell–low or T cell–high tumor cells with vehicle or RMC-7977 for 48 hours in vitro. MHC-I expression was upregulated in tumor cells treated with RMC-7977 (Supplementary Fig. S7A and S7B). Cytokine arrays from conditioned media showed changes in tumor-derived factors with RMC-7977 treatment that were consistent across T cell–low and –high clones (Supplementary Fig. S7C–S7F). GM-CSF secretion, a cytokine known to promote MDSC recruitment in PDAC (38), was nearly undetectable after RMC-7977 treatment. M-CSF and CXCL10, factors known to facilitate macrophage and CD8+ T-cell migration (39, 40), respectively, were increased with RMC-7977. In addition, the CXCR2 ligands CXCL1 and CXCL2 were reduced with RMC-7977 treatment of T cell–low and T cell–high cells, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S7C and S7D). Given that CXCL1 is a determinant of low intratumoral T-cell infiltration (34), this result suggests that RMC-7977 treatment promotes T-cell infiltration in part by inhibiting the release of myeloid-recruiting chemokines. Similar changes to the tumor-secreted factors were seen by qPCR (Supplementary Fig. S7G), indicating that RAS(ON) inhibition by RMC-7977 leads to transcriptional modulation of their expression. Modulation of IL6, a key cytokine involved in PDAC progression (41), was not observed at the transcription level with RMC-7977 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S7H), and, furthermore, IL6 levels were undetectable on tumor cell conditioned media (Supplementary Fig. S7I and S7J).

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that T cells contribute to the antitumor response of RMC-7977. To test this, we subcutaneously implanted 2838c3 T cell–high cells, allowed tumors to reach 20 to 80 mm3, and depleted CD4+ and CD8+ T cells continuously starting 1 day prior to treatment with RMC-7977 or vehicle. T-cell depletion significantly blunted the response of 2838c3 tumors to RMC-7977 (Fig. 3A). Specifically, all tumors given isotype (nondepleting) antibodies exhibited regressions after 12 days of RMC-7977 treatment, with 5 of 7 mice exhibiting a CR. By contrast, only 3 of 7 tumors subjected to T-cell depletion exhibited evidence of regression, with no CRs detected (Fig. 3B). Thus, T cells can contribute to the depth of RMC-7977–induced tumor regression.

To determine the effect of T-cell depletion on the durability of response, we continued to administer T cell–depleting antibodies or isotype control antibodies after stopping RMC-7977. Under these conditions, T-cell depletion accelerated tumor outgrowth following discontinuation of RMC-7977 (Fig. 3C and D), suggesting that T cells contribute to the control of RAS-inhibited tumors even after cessation of therapy. To determine whether the T-cell effects depend on priming by DCs, we assessed the activity of RMC-7977 in Batf3−/− mice lacking conventional DCs necessary for antigen cross-presentation to CD8+ T cells (42). The response to RMC-7977 was blunted in Batf3−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. S8A), and tumors had significantly higher rates of relapse, similar to our results following T-cell depletion (Supplementary Fig. S8B).

We next assessed the role of T cells on RMC-7977 activity in the setting of reduced T-cell infiltration by treating mice bearing 6419c5 T cell–low tumors with RMC-7977 for 10 days in the presence or absence of T-cell depletion. In contrast to our findings with T cell–high tumors, we observed no difference in tumor growth between T-cell depletion and isotype control groups (Fig. 3E). Likewise, when we stopped RMC-7977 treatment in the setting of T cell–low tumors, we observed no effect of T-cell depletion on tumor outgrowth (Fig. 3F). These data suggest that T cells play a role in the response to RMC-7977 in T cell–high tumors but not T cell–low tumors.

Finally, we asked whether the antitumor activity of daraxonrasib—the RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitor currently under clinical evaluation—also depends on an intact T-cell compartment. Similar to our findings with RMC-7977, T-cell depletion blunted the response to daraxonrasib (Fig. 3G and H) and also reduced progression-free survival (Fig. 3I). Notably, CD8+ T-cell depletion alone was sufficient to dampen the response to daraxonrasib, suggesting that this T-cell subset is a key contributor to the antitumor activity of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition.

Given our interpretation from T-cell loss-of-function studies that T cells augment the response to RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition in T cell–high tumors, we next sought to characterize further the changes to the TME in these tumors using the investigational agent daraxonrasib. Analysis by flow cytometry revealed an increased proportion of tumor cells expressing MHC-I, MHC-II, and PD-L1 (Fig. 4A) as well as a decrease in CD11b+ myeloid cells and CD11b+ Ly6G+/Ly6Clo granulocytic MDSCs in 2838c3 subcutaneous tumors treated with daraxonrasib for 8 days (Fig. 4B). Investigation of the TME by NanoString showed an upregulation of T-cell exhaustion markers upon treatment with daraxonrasib, including PD-1, TIGIT, and CTLA-4 (Fig. 4C). These data suggest that daraxonrasib enhances antigen presentation and renders the TME more susceptible to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), which could further potentiate effects of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition in T cell–high tumors.

Given that checkpoint blockade inhibition with PD-1–blocking antibodies is validated in multiple clinical settings (43), combination of anti–PD-1 with daraxonrasib is a rational potential therapeutic strategy worth exploring. Thus, we treated subcutaneous T cell–high 2838c3 tumors with daraxonrasib alone or in combination with anti–PD-1. Mice treated with the combination of daraxonrasib plus anti–PD-1 exhibited more durable response compared with animals treated with vehicle or either compound (Fig. 4D–G), reflected in a greater response rate after 29 days of therapy (Fig. 4H). When therapy was discontinued after 59 days on therapy, 4 of 10 mice treated with the combination of daraxonrasib plus anti–PD-1 exhibited durable CRs as compared with 1 of 10 mice treated with daraxonrasib alone and 0 of 10 mice treated with anti–PD-1 alone (Fig. 4I). Combination of daraxonrasib with anti–PD-1 was well-tolerated as assessed by body weight measurements (Supplementary Fig. S9A). Although daraxonrasib synergized with anti–PD-1 in T cell–high tumors, this effect was not apparent in mice bearing T cell–low tumors from two clonal KPCY-derived cell lines, 6419c5 and 6694c2 (Supplementary Fig. S9B and S9C). Thus, RAS(ON) inhibition can synergize with anti–PD-1 therapy to give greater responses in T cell–high PDAC tumors.

Given that RAS(ON) inhibition can combine effectively with anti–PD-1 therapy in T cell–high models, we sought to test whether a combination of multiple T cell–directed immunotherapies could (i) further improve the depth of response and (ii) potentially sensitize T cell–low tumors to immunotherapy. To test this, we treated mice with the RAS(ON) inhibitor RMC-7977 alone or with combination CD40 agonist (F), anti–CTLA4 (C), and anti–PD-1 (P). Most PDAC tumors are unresponsive to immunotherapy, including with dual ICB with anti-CTLA4 and anti–PD-1(3). CD40 agonists can enhance T-cell priming and render refractory PDAC tumors responsive to ICB; however, animal models suggest that CD40 agonist alone provides little benefit to tumors with low baseline T-cell infiltration (34, 44), a conclusion recently borne out in clinical studies (45). Given our observation that CD103+ DCs decreased in T cell–low tumors in response to RMC-7977, we hypothesized the addition of CD40 agonist could boost T-cell priming, as demonstrated in prior studies using combination CD40 agonist with RAS pathway–targeting agents (46). Finally, as most patients with PDAC receive chemotherapy, we also treated a separate tumor-bearing cohort with a standard-of-care regimen of gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel, alone or in combination with RMC-7977.

We first treated mice bearing 2838c3 subcutaneous tumors with the indicated combinations. Whereas chemotherapy alone was ineffective in controlling tumor growth in the T cell–high 2838c3 model, as previously reported (34), FCP alone, RMC-7977 alone, and RMC-7977 combined with either chemotherapy or a combination of CD40 agonist (F), anti-CTLA4 (C), and anti–PD-1 (P) antibodies (FCP) all significantly reduced tumor growth after 10 days (Fig. 5A and B) or 48 days (Fig. 5A and C), with RMC-7977 + FCP conferring the best response.

Because FCP alone is quite effective in controlling growth of small T cell–high tumors (Fig. 5A), we tested therapeutic effects against tumors larger in size at enrollment. We treated mice bearing large (200 mm3) 2838c3 tumors with RMC-7977 in combination with CP, F, or FCP to determine the relative benefit of each immunotherapy component to the depth and duration of response. All agents successfully reduced tumor growth after 12 days of treatment (Fig. 5D and E); however, the combination of RMC-7977 with immunotherapy significantly improved the durability of tumor regression at 27 days compared with RMC-7977 alone (Fig. 5D and F). Of note, mice treated with RMC-7977 plus a CD40 agonist-containing regimen (F or FCP) exhibited better tumor control at day 27 compared with mice treated with RMC-7977 plus dual checkpoint inhibition (CP; Fig. 5F).

Next, we investigated the effect of combination immunotherapy and chemotherapy in the T cell–low tumor line 6419c5, which typically does not respond to FCP immunotherapy (34). Although the antitumor response to RMC-7977 is unaffected by T-cell depletion (Fig. 3G and H), we hypothesized that the early decrease in MDSCs and increased T-cell infiltration with RMC-7977 would provide a window of opportunity to potentiate an immune response with combination immunotherapy. FCP administered with RMC-7977 significantly augmented the depth of tumor regressions by 7 days and led to regressions in 10 of 10 mice (100%) at 25 days compared with regressions in 4 of 7 mice (57%) with RMC-7977 as single agent (Fig. 5G–I), whereas the combination of RMC-7977 with chemotherapy provided no added benefit (regressions in 3 of 8 mice at 25 days; Fig. 5I). Treatment with individual components of the FCP combination—i.e., agonist CD40 (F) alone or checkpoint inhibition (CP) alone—indicated that both components contributed to the response, with agonist CD40 making the dominant relative contribution (Fig. 5J–L). Taken together, these data suggest RMC-7977 sensitizes tumors to combination immunotherapy.

Complex treatment regimens such as those explored above increase the risk of therapy-associated toxicity. To assess the impact of these combinations on animal well-being, we measured animal weight, which can serve as a surrogate for poor health in rodents. We found no evidence of body weight loss in experiments involving either 2838c3 (Supplementary Fig. S10A and S10B) or 6419c5 (Supplementary Fig. S10C and S10D) tumors treated with RMC-7977 or RMC-7977 and combination immunotherapy, suggesting these regimens can be tolerated in preclinical murine models.

The autochthonous KPC/Y model of PDAC gives rise to heterogenous tumors that arise stochastically, resulting in a dense desmoplastic tumor stroma that reflects the TME present in human disease. As RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition with RMC-7977 has previously been shown to have potent antitumor activity in the KPC/Y model (23), we sought to determine whether combining RMC-7977 with chemotherapy or immunotherapy could have additional benefits in this setting. To this end, we followed KPC/Y mice for tumor onset by serial ultrasound and treated animals with either (i) chemotherapy (gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel), (ii) FCP, (ii) RMC-7977, (iii) RMC-7977 with chemotherapy, and (iv) RMC-7977 with FCP when tumors reached a size of 20 to 100 mm3. To determine the contribution of individual immunotherapy agents, we also included treated animals with combinations of RMC-7977 with CD40 agonist (F) or dual checkpoint inhibition (CP). Whereas chemotherapy or FCP alone failed to promote tumor regressions after 14 days of therapy (Fig. 6A), RMC-7977 monotherapy and all combinations effectively inhibited tumor growth, resulting in small areas of residual tumor (Supplementary Fig. S11A–S11I). Notably, whereas RMC-7977 alone prompted tumor regressions in 7 of 10 mice (Fig. 6B), the combination of RMC-7977 with either immunotherapy or chemotherapy prompted regressions in 10 of 10 mice after this timepoint, suggesting both combinations provide additional short-term benefit in the autochthonous setting.

Given that combination immunotherapy increased durability of tumor regression in the subcutaneous setting, we continued treating KPC/Y mice with RMC-7977 and FCP. In an additional study, the difference between RMC-7977 monotherapy and combination immunotherapy became more apparent after 21 days of treatment when 5 of 10 mice treated with RMC-7977 exhibited tumor regressions compared with 8 of 10 mice treated with RMC-7977 + FCP (Fig. 6C and D). With continuous treatment, 7 of 11 mice treated with RMC-7977 had progression on therapy compared with 5 of 11 mice treated with RMC-7977 + FCP (Fig. 6E). These data demonstrate that T cell–dependent immunotherapy can combine with and delay tumor progression in the autochthonous setting. There were notable differences in the stromal compartment of tumors treated with RMC-7977 or combination FCP compared with untreated KPC/Y controls at survival endpoint (Fig. 6F and G). Additionally, mice treated with RMC-7977 + FCP had significantly increased intratumoral immune cell infiltration at endpoint, including greater numbers of F4/80+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells compared with controls (Fig. 6H and I). These data demonstrate that RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition also acts to alter the TME in the autochthonous setting as it does in the subcutaneous setting.

The RAS pathway is a critical mediator of T-cell activation, and perturbation of this pathway negatively affects T-cell activation, proliferation, and effector function (24–28, 47). Thus, our finding that T cells contribute to the antitumor properties of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition—an effect that may be leveraged with T cell–directed immunotherapy—stands in apparent contrast to these prior reports. Hence, we further explored effects of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition on T-cell function both in vitro and in vivo.

CD8+ T cells were isolated from naïve mice and activated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 for 3 days while concurrently treating with varying concentrations of RMC-7977. Analysis by CellTiter-Glo revealed an IC50 value for inhibition of proliferation of primary T cells of ∼160 nmol/L, more than an order of magnitude higher than the sensitivity of 2838c3 and 6419c5 PDAC cell lines, which had IC50 values of ∼8 and ∼30 nmol/L, respectively (Fig. 7A). There was no change in basal levels of pERK in naïve mouse CD8+ T cells upon exposure to RMC-7977 for 2 hours at 1, 10, and 100 nmol/L (Fig. 7B), concentrations that inhibit ERK phosphorylation in multiple PDAC cell lines (Fig. 7C). At relevant concentrations (<10 nmol/L), i.e., that match the exposures associated with antitumor activity in mouse models, RMC-7977 had minimal effects on CD8+ T-cell viability and proliferative capacity, whereas at higher concentrations (>10 nmol/L), RMC-7977 had a modest effect on the expression of CD25, a T-cell activation marker (Fig. 7D). To assess the effect of RMC-7977 on T cells after activation, we isolated CD8+ T cells from naïve mice and activated them with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 for 3 days prior to treatment with RMC-7977 for an additional 3 days. Under these conditions, RMC-7977 had no effect on T-cell proliferation or activation status even at the highest concentrations (Fig. 7E).

In naïve human CD3+ T cells isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), there was no effect on viability at any dose and minimal effects on proliferation at doses less than 10 nmol/L (Supplementary Fig. S12A). At higher doses, RMC-7977 treatment lowered CD25 expression and expression of the T-cell activation marker IFNγ (Supplementary Fig. S12B); however, preactivation of human T cells with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 for 3 days prior to treatment abrogated any effect on viability, proliferation, or activation (Supplementary Fig. S12C and S12D). Thus, RAS(ON) inhibition has minimal effects on T-cell proliferation or activation at dose concentrations that otherwise inhibit the growth of KRAS-mutant cancer cells. Importantly, we were unable to detect any phenotypic change in preactivated murine or human T cells exposed to RMC-7977 even up to high concentrations, exceeding exposures associated with activity in vivo.

To discern whether RMC-7977 affects T-cell priming in vivo in response to an antigen-specific challenge, we vaccinated C57BL/6 mice subcutaneously with OVA protein plus CpG, a TLR9 agonist, as previously described (48). Nonvaccinated C57BL/6 and OT-I mice were used as controls. After 72 hours of treatment with vehicle or standard dose RMC-7977, starting at the time of vaccination, we quantified the abundance of CD8+ splenocytes positive for the OVA-specific H-2Kb:SIINFEKL tetramer. RMC-7977–treated mice exhibited an increase in tetramer-positive CD8+ T cells following vaccination compared with vaccinated vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 7F). Thus, in line with our in vitro data, RMC-7977 has no detectable negative effect on T-cell priming or expansion in vivo.

It is increasingly appreciated that some oncogenic programs hinder tumor immunity often through tumor non-intrinsic pathways (49) such that inhibiting these pathways therapeutically can render tumors more responsive to ICB (50), as demonstrated in recent studies with allele-specific KRASG12D inhibition (17). In this study, we found that RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition also exemplifies this paradigm. Using the investigational drug candidate daraxonrasib or the representative preclinical tool compound RMC-7977, we observed deep and durable responses to RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition in mice bearing PDAC that not only depended on T cells and cDC1 but were also augmented to achieve CRs in combination with immunotherapy.

T-cell depletion in vivo or interference with T-cell priming (i.e., in Batf3−/− hosts) blunted the rate, depth, and durability of tumor regressions to RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition. Implanted tumors with high baseline T-cell infiltration had deeper tumor regressions compared with tumors with low T-cell infiltration. Whereas CRs were limited to subcutaneous tumor models, RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition effectively sensitized PDAC tumors to immunotherapy in both subcutaneous and autochthonous settings. In particular, the T cell–low tumor clone 6419c5—which is otherwise refractory to combination immunotherapy—achieved deep and durable response to RMC-7977 consistent with CR when immunotherapy was added, particularly with regimens containing a CD40 agonist. Whereas combination chemotherapy provided added benefit to RMC-7977 in autochthonous tumors, there was minimal added benefit of chemotherapy across the T cell–low and T cell–high subcutaneous models, suggesting that, unlike immunotherapy, T-cell infiltration does not significantly influence response to combination chemotherapy regimens.

RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition reshapes the TME, creating permissive conditions for productive immunotherapy. Therapeutic effects of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition in these preclinical models were associated with an influx of intratumoral T cells and macrophages and a reduction in intratumoral myeloid-derived subsets, effectively reshaping the TME into a T cell–high phenotype. With RMC-7977 treatment, intratumoral T cells were more proliferative and exhibited higher prevalence of effector and central memory phenotypes. Changes in T-cell infiltration and phenotype were dynamic. For example, tumors which have higher levels of T-cell infiltration at baseline are likely to exhibit more durable increases in T-cell infiltration following RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition. The observed difference in response between T cell–low and T cell–high tumors may be explained by the lack of opportunity for T cells to encounter tumor antigens in T cell–low tumors prior to treatment (given their low abundance at baseline), whereas T cells in T cell–high tumors are present prior to treatment, allowing more time for activation and differentiation.

These preclinical data are in concordance with prior studies demonstrating that inhibition of mutant KRASG12D and KRASG12C can relieve myeloid cell immunosuppression and promote the infiltration of antitumor immune cells, including cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (2, 15, 17–19, 21, 51). Within the myeloid population, RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition effectively decreased intratumoral MDSCs, consistent with the well-characterized link between oncogenic KRAS signaling and MDSC recruitment in KPC tumors (38). Similarly, our finding that RMC-7977 promotes macrophage infiltration is consistent with prior studies that genetically or pharmacologically inhibit oncogenic KRAS signaling (29, 52, 53). RAS-MAPK pathway inhibition has been shown to selectively deplete protumorigenic M2 macrophages (29, 54), further supporting the observation that RMC-7977 modulates macrophage polarization.

Our observations with respect to immune checkpoint expression differ somewhat from those in a recent publication using the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133. Specifically, the authors of that study note LAG3 and 4-1BB but not CTLA4 as prominent immune checkpoints on MRTX1133-treated tumors using the inducible KPC model (17). Similar to our findings, we found that RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition induced the expression of multiple immune checkpoints, including PD-1 and PD-L1, providing a rationale for combining KRAS inhibition with PD-1 antagonists.

We also observed changes in nonimmune components of the TME, for example increased αSMA+ myofibroblasts and collagen deposition seen nearly universally, and greater tumor vasculature observed with more variability. As αSMA+ myofibroblasts and type I collagen have both been associated with tumor-constraining activity (55–57), these changes in the fibroblast composition may further enhance the antitumor activity of the compound. Importantly, many of these findings with RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition are similar to our observations with the G12D allele–specific KRAS inhibitor MRTX1133 (15), further supporting the notion that the effects of RAS-GTP inhibition on the TME are mediated indirectly by changes in cancer cell signaling rather than direct effects on stromal components.

On one hand, our data show that T cells contribute to the therapeutic effects of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition, but on the other hand, prior studies imply that T cells require KRAS signaling for activation, proliferation, and effector function (24–28, 47). To explore this apparent paradox, and using an in vitro assay for cell viability, we found that T cells were far less sensitive to RMC-7977 than PDAC cells with KRAS mutations. Similarly, T cells did not exhibit evidence that MAPK signaling (i.e., pERK levels) was inhibited at doses of RMC-7977 that significantly inhibit MAPK signaling in RAS-mutant PDAC cells. These findings thus suggest that concentrations of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitors that can attenuate proliferation of cancer cells carrying RAS mutations may spare normal immune cells.

It is also possible that RAS signaling has different effects at distinct stages of T-cell differentiation. For example, we observed that the proliferation and activation of naïve murine and human T cells were inhibited at high concentrations of RMC-7977. By contrast, T cells that were preactivated continued to divide and maintained their activated phenotype at the highest doses of RMC-7977. These findings suggest that RAS signaling is important for T-cell priming (as previously understood), whereas RAS may be relatively dispensable once cells have become activated. In the in vivo setting, RMC-7977 was administered once tumors were already established, potentially after T cells gained experience to tumor antigen and became activated.

In fact, RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition at the right time and concentration may enhance T-cell function. In our vaccination study using ovalbumin as a model antigen, RMC-7977 treatment led to an unexpected increase in antigen-specific T cells compared with previous findings (22). This result is in contrast with a prior observation that targeted inhibition of MEK, a downstream mediator of KRAS signaling, prevents the expansion of naïve CD8+ T cells in a syngeneic colorectal cancer model (58), which may suggest a difference between RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition and MEK inhibition. Despite its effects on priming, MEK inhibition protects tumor-infiltrating T cells from death due to chronic T-cell receptor stimulation and spares cytotoxic activity (58).

Although powerful, treatment with novel KRASG12C(OFF) selective inhibitors has been met with clinical resistance and suboptimal durability, and rationally designed combination therapies have been clinically impactful. As such, our preclinical observations regarding the immune mechanisms of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition may have important clinical implications. First, combination regimens of RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitors with agonist CD40 and/or immune checkpoint inhibitors are worth evaluation in the clinic. Immunotherapy provided combination benefit to RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition in both autochthonous and subcutaneous tumors. By comparison, combination chemotherapy provided additive benefit to RMC-7977 in autochthonous tumors similar to combination immunotherapy but not in subcutaneous tumors in which response was similar to that of monotherapy RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibition. As previously suggested, CD40 agonist may be essential for priming T cells and provided the dominant relative contribution to combination benefit observed with RAS(ON) inhibition in the subcutaneous models. Second, immune features of a tumor may inform patient selection for clinical trials combining KRAS inhibition with immunotherapy. T cell–high PDAC preclinical tumor models responded better than T cell–low PDAC models to monotherapy or combination immune therapy. Thus, selection of patients based on the extent of baseline infiltration may be an important factor in the design of clinical trials.
