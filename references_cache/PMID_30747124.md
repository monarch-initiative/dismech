[Article in English]

Jackson AC.

Rabies is an acute neurological infection of humans and animals, caused by 
rabies virus and usually transmitted by animal bites. After an incubation period 
usually lasting weeks or longer, a variety of prodromal symptoms may occur. 
Encephalitic rabies (80% of cases) is associated with episodes of generalised 
arousal or hyperexcitability and hydrophobia, in which spasms involve the 
contraction of inspiratory muscles with swallowing. Paralytic rabies (in 20% of 
cases) is characterised by progressive lower motor neuron weakness. Laboratory 
investigations are important for confirming an ante mortem diagnosis of rabies. 
Cardiopulmonary and many other complications, including multiple organ failure, 
are common in aggressively managed rabies patients in critical care units. No 
effective therapy is available for rabies and survivors are uncommon. In 
contrast, preventative measures for rabies are highly effective after recognised 
exposures.

rabies virus and usually transmitted by animal bites. After an incubation period 
usually lasting weeks or longer, a variety of prodromal symptoms may occur. 
Encephalitic rabies (80% of cases) is associated with episodes of generalised 
arousal or hyperexcitability and hydrophobia, in which spasms involve the 
contraction of inspiratory muscles with swallowing. Paralytic rabies (in 20% of 
cases) is characterised by progressive lower motor neuron weakness. Laboratory 
investigations are important for confirming an ante mortem diagnosis of rabies. 
Cardiopulmonary and many other complications, including multiple organ failure, 
are common in aggressively managed rabies patients in critical care units. No 
effective therapy is available for rabies and survivors are uncommon. In contrast, 
preventative measures for rabies are highly effective after recognised exposures.

Introduction
Although rabies is an ancient disease caused by infection 
with rabies virus (RABV), human rabies continues to be an 
important public health problem. Most of the estimated 
59,000 human deaths caused by rabies each year worldwide 
are in Asia and Africa. About 99% of cases are caused by 
the transmission of RABV from dogs, usually due to bite 
exposure, in places where canine rabies is endemic. Almost 
half of human rabies cases occur in children. Unfortunately , 
physicians may not recognise rabies in countries where it is 
not endemic owing to a lack of familiarity with the clinical 
manifestations, even when they are typical. It is probable 
that many fatal cases of rabies go unrecognised. Moreover, 
it is not unusual for a fatal acute neurological illness to 
lack a definite diagnosis, and the specific laboratory tests 
for rabies are not performed unless a diagnosis of rabies is 
under question. Other reasons that fatal rabies cases could 
go unconfirmed are that an autopsy may not be performed 
or the pathologist may not consider rabies as a cause  
of death.
Exposures and transmission of 
rabies virus
In most human rabies cases, transmission occurs through 
animal bites and, worldwide, transmission through dog 
bites is the most significant. In North America, many 
patients with bat-acquired rabies have no history of contact 
with bats and only a minority have a history of a bite or a 
scratch (1). These victims are likely to be unaware that they 
have been bitten. 
Aerosol transmission of RABV has been documented 
infrequently . It has occurred naturally in Frio cave in Texas, 
United States of America (USA), which contains millions 
of Brazilian free-tailed bats (2). Subsequent experiments 
performed by Denny Constantine showed transmission 
to animals kept in cages in the cave. Aerosol transmission 
has also occurred as a consequence of laboratory accidents 
(3, 4) leading to death or severe disease in a pre-immunised 
person. With aerosol transmission, RABV takes a direct 
pathway into the brain through an olfactory route.
Transmission of RABV may also occur with either tissue or 
organ transplantation. There are eight well-documented 
cases of the disease due to corneal transplantation (5). 
Transmission from organ transplantation was first 
documented in three organ recipients in Texas, together with 
a fourth case where a vascular conduit from the same donor 
was used for a liver transplant (6). Soon after, transmission 
occurred to another three organ recipients in Germany 
(7). Subsequently , in the USA, organ transplantation in 
Maryland (donor from North Carolina) resulted in a single 
case (8); in Kuwait, it resulted in four cases (9, 10); and 
there have been seven additional cases in the People’s 
Republic of China from three donors (11, 12, 13, 14). In all 
rabies cases transmitted by tissue or organ transplantation, 
infectious RABV is thought to be present in transplanted

Human-to-human transmission is extremely rare in

However, in a case in Ethiopia, in which two patients with

patients was through contact with family members who 
died of the disease (15). A 41-year-old female died of rabies

had bitten his mother’s little finger.) A second five-year-old 
boy developed rabies 36 days after his mother had died of 
the disease. The woman had repeatedly kissed her son on 
the mouth during her illness. There is no documented case 
of RABV transmission from a patient to a healthcare worker. 
Owing to a theoretical risk of transmission, healthcare 
workers and family members in contact with rabies patients 
should take appropriate precautions to minimise the risk 
of RABV transmission through saliva or other secretions 
(16). Patient exposures justify the initiation of rabies 
post-exposure prophylaxis. Only one documented case of 
transplacental transmission of RABV exists (17). 
Serum rabies virus-neutralising antibodies (VNAs) have 
been detected in some individuals, suggesting that they 
are likely to have had inapparent exposures and naturally 
acquired immunity without the development of clinical 
rabies (18). This has been observed in Canadian Inuit 
hunters (19), an aboriginal male who trapped foxes in Alaska 
(20), and Floridian raccoon hunters (21). Rabies VNAs 
were also found in residents of the Amazon region of Peru, 
who were at high risk of vampire-bat exposures and had not 
previously received rabies immunisation; four had a history 
of bat bites (22). Rabies VNAs were detected in 6.6% of 
226 unimmunised students and faculty members at a 
veterinary medical school at the beginning of a rabies 
vaccine trial (23). These studies indicate that humans 
may be naturally immunised to rabies without developing 
clinical disease.
Incubation period
The incubation period of rabies frequently lasts between 
20 and 90 days, but it may be as short as only a few days, 
and may last for a year or more. There are well-documented 
cases in immigrants from rabies-endemic areas with 
incubation periods of up to six years (24). In the case of a 
Vietnamese girl in Australia who developed rabies, RABV 
transmission had taken place at least five years earlier (25, 
26). Lengthy incubation periods could be the result of 
another unrecognised or forgotten exposure closer to the 
onset of the illness. In very short incubation periods, RABV 
is likely to travel to the spinal cord or brain directly through 
peripheral nerves. During most of the incubation period of 
rabies, the virus is located at a site close to that of viral 
entry (e.g. bite site). In muscle, RABV binds to the nicotinic 
acetylcholine receptor on the post-synaptic membrane of 
the neuromuscular junction. After crossing the synapse, the 
virus spreads in motor neurons by retrograde fast axonal 
transport to the spinal cord or to cranial motor ganglia 
in the brainstem, depending on the site of exposure. 
This spread occurs very quickly at up to approximately  
250 mm/day with delays for viral replication at synapses 
which form neuroanatomical connections.
Prodromal symptoms of rabies 
There are a variety of non-specific prodromal symptoms 
of rabies lasting for up to approximately ten days, which 
are similar to those of many other viral illnesses. These 
symptoms include fever, chills, malaise, fatigue, insomnia, 
anorexia, headache, anxiety and irritability (27). About half 
of patients develop pain, paraesthesia (abnormal sensation 
of tingling or pricking), and/or pruritus (itching), which 
usually occur close to the site of the bite; the wound may have 
completely healed by this time (28, 29). Local neurological 
symptoms are likely to be due to RABV infection in local 
peripheral sensory ganglia (30). These early symptoms may 
occasionally occur at a site distant from the bite. 
Clinical forms of rabies
There are two clinical forms of rabies in humans: encephalitic 
and paralytic. Encephalitic rabies occurs in about 80% of 
patients and paralytic rabies in 20%. In the encephalitic 
form, patients have episodes of hyperexcitability or 
generalised arousal that are separated by lucid periods 
(27). The pathophysiological mechanisms resulting in the 
two forms of disease remain uncertain (31). Spinal cord, 
nerve root and peripheral nerve involvement are likely 
to underlie paralytic rabies, whereas brain infection is of 
fundamental importance in encephalitic rabies. Spells 
may occur with agitation, confusion, hallucinations and 
aggressive behaviour, typically lasting for periods of one 
to five minutes (29, 32, 33). These spells may take place 
either spontaneously or be precipitated by sensory stimuli. 
Fever is usual and may be very high (over 42°C/107°F). 
Autonomic dysfunction is common and includes 
hypersalivation, sweating, piloerection (goose bumps), 
and priapism (persistent and painful penile erection). This 
dysfunction may be caused by direct infection of autonomic 
nervous system centres or autonomic pathways involving 
the hypothalamus, spinal cord and/or autonomic ganglia. 
Epileptic seizures may occur in rabies, but they do not 
usually occur early in the illness and they are not prominent.

The majority of patients with encephalitic rabies develop 
hydrophobia, which is a specific and characteristic feature 
of rabies. The term ‘hydrophobia’ is derived from the Greek 
word meaning ‘fear of water’. Initially , patients experience 
pain in the throat or difficulty swallowing. Subsequently , 
on attempts to swallow, they experience contractions of the 
diaphragm and accessory muscles of inspiration, typically 
lasting for five to fifteen seconds. There may be associated 
features, including the contraction of neck muscles, retching, 
vomiting, coughing, grimacing and convulsions (34). 
Cardiopulmonary arrest may occur during severe spasms 
without the initiation of supportive care measures (32). In 
order to avoid spasms, patients may avoid drinking and 
become dehydrated. The sight, sound, or even mentioning 
of water or liquids may trigger spasms due to reinforcement 
by conditioning (35) or they may occur spontaneously . 
A draught of air on the skin may also precipitate the spasms 
(aerophobia), and there are a variety of other precipitating 
stimuli (27). A fan test in which air is fanned across the face 
with observation of the patient for the occurrence of spasms 
has been used as a bedside diagnostic test for aerophobia 
(36). Hydrophobia is probably due to the infection of 
neurons inhibiting the inspiratory motor neurons in the 
region of the nucleus ambiguus (involved in swallowing 
and speech) in the medulla (27, 35), resulting in the 
exaggeration of normally protective defensive reflexes for 
the respiratory tract. Moreover, increased libido, priapism 
and spontaneous ejaculations have been reported in rabies 
patients (37, 38, 39, 40, 41). 
Some clinical features of rabies are more likely to be 
associated with certain RABV variants. Rabies acquired from 
dogs more frequently causes hydrophobia and aerophobia, 
whereas rabies from bats is more associated with symptoms 
at the exposure site, such as local sensory symptoms, tremor 
and myoclonus (quick involuntary muscle jerk) (42). 
Rabies is virtually always fatal with death occurring within 
two weeks of the onset of symptoms. The survival period 
depends on how aggressive the approach to therapy is, 
including critical care management.
Paralytic rabies is characterised by muscle weakness that 
usually begins in the bitten extremity and progresses to 
quadriparesis (weakness in all four limbs) and bilateral 
facial weakness. This begins after the incubation period 
and following prodromal symptoms. The mental state of 
the patient is usually normal in the early phase of paralytic 
rabies. There are normally no sensory deficits. Paralytic 
rabies may be misdiagnosed as Guillain–Barré syndrome, 
including both the acute inflammatory demyelinating 
polyradiculopathy (disorder in which the immune system 
attacks part of the peripheral nervous system) and the 
motor-sensory neuropathy of acute onset with predominant 
axonal involvement (called the axonal Guillain–Barré 
syndrome) (43, 44, 45).
In paralytic rabies, sphincter involvement resulting in 
urinary incontinence is common but it is uncommon in 
Guillain–Barré syndrome (46). Bulbar and respiratory 
muscles become weak in paralytic rabies as the disease 
progresses, which leads to a fatal outcome. Hydrophobia 
is not usually present in paralytic rabies; however, mild 
inspiratory spasms may occur (47). Survival in paralytic 
rabies is typically longer than in encephalitic rabies (31). 
Paralysis develops either in association with or after the 
onset of coma in encephalitic rabies, but this should not be 
confused with paralytic rabies, in which muscle weakness 
develops relatively early .
Medical complications of rabies
Many medical complications may develop in patients with 
rabies. Cardiopulmonary complications are very common 
and include sinus tachycardia (abnormally rapid heart 
rate originating in the sinus node), cardiac arrhythmias 
(abnormal heart rhythm), heart failure, hypotension (low 
blood pressure), hyperventilation, hypoxaemia (abnormally 
low oxygen concentration in the blood), respiratory 
depression (decreased ability to inhale and exhale), and 
aspiration with secondary pneumonia (29, 35). Cardiac 
manifestations may reflect infection involving the autonomic 
nervous system or myocardium (48, 49, 50, 51, 52). 
There may be either hyperthermia or hypothermia, which 
may reflect hypothalamic involvement in the infection. 
Gastrointestinal haemorrhage, especially haematemesis, 
is a common complication (53). Endocrine complications 
include inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone 
and diabetes insipidus (secretion of or response to the 
antidiuretic hormone is impaired) (29, 54). Progression to 
multiple organ failure is very common in patients who are 
aggressively managed in critical care units.
Investigations
Neuroimaging studies
Neuroimaging in rabies is typically performed to exclude 
other diagnostic possibilities. Computed tomography 
(CT) studies of the brain are usually normal (55, 56, 57). 
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies of the brain may 
also be normal (56, 58) or they may show lesions located 
in grey-matter areas of the brain parenchyma, including 
the brainstem (59, 60, 61, 62). Gadolinium (a compound 
used to improve contrast in MRI scans) enhancement 
of cervical nerve roots, medulla and the hypothalamus 
has been described in patients with paralytic rabies (63).

Laboratory investigations
The electroencephalogram is often normal or shows non-
specific abnormalities. Slow wave activity , periodic activity 
(65) and epileptiform activity may be observed. In paralytic 
rabies, electrophysiological studies have shown features 
of anterior horn cell and/or peripheral nerve involvement 
with findings of peripheral nerve demyelination (30), and 
a primary axonal neuropathy has also been reported in 
paralytic rabies (66, 67). 
Haematologic and biochemical tests are usually normal. 
Hyponatremia (low blood levels of sodium) may be due 
to the inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone. In 
addition, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies are frequently 
abnormal. In the first week of illness, CSF pleocytosis 
was observed in 59% of cases and in subsequent weeks 
in 87% (68). The white cell count is usually fewer than 
100 cells/μL with a preponderance of mononuclear cells; 
CSF protein may be mildly elevated, whereas CSF glucose is 
usually normal with occasional reports of low glucose levels 
(69, 70). 
Serum rabies VNAs do not usually develop in unimmunised 
rabies patients until the second week of the illness, and 
rabies may be fatal without the development of a detectable 
serum-neutralising antibody level (29, 68, 71). Rabies VNAs 
are present in the CSF later than in the serum and the CSF 
titre is lower. Very high titres of CSF rabies VNAs indicate 
evidence of rabies encephalitis in vaccinated patients (4, 
29, 72, 73, 74). Detection of RABV antigen in skin biopsies 
is an important diagnostic test for rabies. RABV RNA in 
saliva, skin biopsies, CSF or brain tissue may be amplified 
using the reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction 
(RT–PCR). This is a valuable diagnostic advance for rabies 
diagnosis. It should be emphasised that negative tests for 
the detection of RABV antigen or RNA can never exclude a 
diagnosis of rabies. If clinical suspicion is high and the tests 
are negative, then these tests should be repeated at one or 
more later time points.
Negri bodies are characteristic eosinophilic inclusion bodies 
in the cytoplasm of neurons and are a pathologic hallmark 
of rabies that may be observed on routine histological 
staining in the majority of human cases. The RABV antigen 
may be detected in brain tissue obtained post mortem at 
autopsy and also ante mortem by brain biopsy (75), or post 
mortem at autopsy or by needle (e.g. Vim-Silverman or 
trucut needle) aspiration with entry via the orbit or foramen 
magnum (76, 77, 78), and tested for viral isolation and/
or for the detection of RABV antigen or RNA. Diagnostic 
tests can be performed on post-mortem human tissues. 
There are societal, cultural and religious barriers that may 
impede the performance of an autopsy or the collection of 
post-mortem specimens to make an unequivocal laboratory 
diagnosis of rabies. For example, both Islam and Judaism 
object to autopsies because bodily intrusion violates beliefs 
about keeping the human body complete; there are also 
requirements for a rapid burial (79).
Differential diagnosis
Specific questioning may be needed to reveal an animal bite 
history and the disease may progress before such questioning 
can take place. In some cases there is no history of an animal 
bite. There may be a history of foreign travel to a country 
with endemic dog rabies. Owing to bizarre neuropsychiatric 
clinical features, rabies may be misdiagnosed as a psychiatric 
disorder, including an acute psychosis (80) or hysteria (81). 
Rabies hysteria, which is classified as a somatic symptom 
disorder, may occur in a patient with potential rabies 
exposure and can be distinguished from rabies by a shorter 
incubation period (hours to a few days), early inability to 
communicate, bizarre spasms, spitting out of water taken 
in the mouth without actual swallowing attempts, barking, 
biting, aggressive behaviour, lack of a fever or neurological 
signs, and a long clinical course with recovery (36). Other 
viral encephalitides, including herpes simplex encephalitis, 
may show behavioural disturbances, but hydrophobic 
spasms do not occur in these disorders. Herpesvirus simiae 
(B virus) encephalomyelitis, which is transmitted through 
monkey bites, has a shorter incubation period than rabies 
(e.g. three to five days); vesicles may develop at the site of 
the bite, and recovery may occur despite a high mortality 
rate (82). 
The neurotoxin from the bacterium Clostridium tetani causes 
tetanus and the disease may develop after a contaminated 
wound caused by an animal bite. The incubation period 
(usually three to 21 days) is shorter than in rabies, and 
tetanus is associated with sustained muscle rigidity 
affecting axial muscles, including paraspinal, abdominal, 
masseter (trismus), laryngeal and respiratory muscles, 
with superimposed brief recurrent muscle spasms (83). In 
tetanus, the mental state is not affected, there is no CSF 
pleocytosis, and the prognosis is more favourable than in 
rabies.
Anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (anti-NMDA) 
encephalitis is associated with and is characterised by 
behavioural changes, autonomic instability , hypoventilation 
and seizures, and occurs in young patients. This autoimmune 
disease rivals the viral causes of encephalitis (84). There 
is also increased recognition of other antibody-mediated 
disorders of the synapse with therapeutic approaches 
involving immunotherapy and other novel therapies (85). 
Post-vaccinal encephalomyelitis should be considered in 
the few remaining countries where patients continue to be

Rabies due to other lyssavirus 
species
In addition to RABV , which is lyssavirus species 1, there are 
15 other lyssavirus species; and six have been associated 
with cases of human rabies: Mokola lyssavirus (species 3) 
(110), Duvenhage lyssavirus (species 4) (111, 112, 113), 
European bat 1 lyssavirus (species 5) (113, 114, 115, 116), 
European bat 2 lyssavirus (species 6) (69, 115, 117, 118), 
Australian bat lyssavirus (species 7) (119, 120, 121), and 
Irkut lyssavirus (122). They are also known as ‘rabies-like’ 
or ‘rabies-related’ viruses. Moreover, the clinical features 
of infections caused by RABV and other lyssaviruses are 
identical. There are nine other lyssaviruses that have 
not been reported to be associated with human disease, 
including Lagos bat lyssavirus (species 2). It is very likely 
that significant under-reporting is taking place and a lack 
of recognition of human cases of rabies due to non-RABV 
lyssaviruses, owing to the lack of virus typing of most 
human cases.

(qui représente 80 % des cas) est associée à des épisodes d’agitation ou 
d’hyperexcitabilité et d’hydrophobie durant lesquels surviennent des contractions 
spasmodiques des muscles pharyngés lors de la déglutition. La rage paralytique 
(20 % des cas) se caractérise par une dégénérescence progressive des 
neurones moteurs inférieurs. Des analyses de laboratoire sont nécessaires pour 
obtenir confirmation ante-mortem d’un diagnostic de rage. Des complications 
notamment cardiopulmonaires ainsi que des défaillances de plusieurs organes 
sont fréquentes chez les patients atteints soumis à des traitements très lourds 
dans les services de réanimation. Il n’existe pas de traitement curatif efficace 
et les survivants sont rares. En revanche, les mesures préventives suite à une 
exposition avérée sont extrêmement efficaces.

La rabia desde el punto de vista médico
A.C. Jackson
Resumen
La rabia es una infección neurológica aguda que afecta a personas y animales, 
causada por el virus de la rabia y transmitida generalmente por la mordedura de 
un animal. Tras un periodo de incubación que suele durar semanas, o incluso más 
tiempo, pueden aparecer diversos síntomas prodrómicos. La rabia encefálica 
(un 80% de los casos) se acompaña de episodios de nerviosismo generalizado 
hiperexcitabilidad e hidrofobia, con espasmos que entrañan la contracción de los 
músculos inspiratorios con la deglución. La rabia paralítica (un 20% de los casos) 
se caracteriza por un debilitamiento progresivo de la neurona motora inferior. 
Los análisis de laboratorio son importantes para confirmar un diagnóstico  
ante mortem de rabia. Es frecuente que los pacientes tratados con terapias 
agresivas en las unidades de cuidados intensivos sufran complicaciones 
cardiopulmonares, entre otras muchas que pueden ir hasta la insuficiencia 
multiorgánica. No hay un tratamiento eficaz contra la rabia y son contadas las 
personas que sobreviven. En cambio, las medidas de profilaxis tras una exposición 
resultan muy eficaces.
