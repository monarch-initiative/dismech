# Genetic defects of brain immunity in childhood herpes simplex encephalitis.
**Authors:** Zhang SY, Casanova JL
**Journal:** Nature (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41586-024-08119-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08119-z)

## Content

Herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) encephalitis (HSE) is the most common sporadic viral encephalitis in humans. It is life-threatening and has a first peak of incidence in childhood, during primary infection. Children with HSE are not particularly prone to other infections, including HSV-1 infections of tissues other than the brain. About 8-10% of childhood cases are due to monogenic inborn errors of 19 genes, two-thirds of which are recessive, and most of which display incomplete clinical penetrance. Childhood HSE can therefore be sporadic but genetic, enabling new diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. In this Review, we examine essential cellular and molecular mechanisms of cell-intrinsic antiviral immunity in the brain that are disrupted in individuals with HSE. These mechanisms include both known (such as mutations in the TLR3 pathway) and previously unknown (such as the TMEFF1 restriction factor) antiviral pathways, which may be dependent (for example, IFNAR1) or independent (for example, through RIPK3) of type I interferons. They operate in cortical or brainstem neurons, and underlie forebrain and brainstem infections, respectively. Conversely, the most severe inborn errors of leukocytes, including a complete lack of myeloid and/or lymphoid blood cells, do not underlie HSE. Thus congenital defects in intrinsic immunity in brain-resident neurons that underlie HSE broaden natural host defences against HSV-1 from the leukocytes of the immune system to other cells in the organism.

Herpes simplex virus 1 encephalitis (HSE) is the most common sporadic (as opposed to epidemic) encephalitis due to a single identifiable virus1–3. It is also sporadic (as opposed to familial) in the genetic sense. According to the available epidemiological data, it has an annual incidence of 2–4/1,000,000, corresponding to a prevalence of about 1–2/10,000 HSV-1-infected individuals. In the United States of America, it accounts for about 50–75% of cases of confirmed viral encephalitis each year. HSV was first identified as a causal agent of encephalitis in 19414, and HSV-1 was distinguished from HSV-2 in 19775, HSV-1 being the causal agent of HSE in most cases at all ages with the exception of neonates. HSV-1 and its ancestors — double-stranded enveloped DNA herpesviruses — are among the oldest known viruses. They have co-evolved with humans and their ancestors for about 40 million years, and HSV-1 itself is the oldest known human pathogenic virus, with an estimated age of about 6 million years and an evolutionary origin dating back to the ancient apes6. Humans are the only known reservoirs of HSV-1, accounting for human-to-human transmission, although this virus can infect other primates, in which it is much more virulent6–8. HSV-1 typically enters the human body via the oral or nasal epithelium and infects peripheral neurons, subsequently establishing latency in the trigeminal (TG) sensory ganglia9. HSE is one of the very few types of viral encephalitis for which the route of infection is strictly neurotropic, rather than passing through the blood-brain barrier10. It therefore manifests as an isolated, brain-restricted disease, and can be seen as the prototypic viral encephalitis. Children with HSE are typically otherwise healthy, not even particularly prone to other clinical forms of HSV-1 infection. No dissemination of HSV-1 to the bloodstream or tissues other than the brain has been reported in patients before, during, or after HSE1,11. Other infections may be observed in adults and the elderly, but not in children with HSE1. No effective vaccine against HSV-1 is available, despite the relentless efforts of virologists and immunologists12, but acyclovir therapy was introduced in 198213,14. HSE is a devastating condition in all age groups, proving fatal in more than 70% of patients in the absence of treatment, and with mild-to-severe neurological sequelae in about 40–60% of acyclovir-treated survivors15,16.

More than 85% of adults worldwide have antibodies against HSV-1, which causes asymptomatic infection or benign, self-healing disease in most individuals, occasionally with gingivitis and stomatitis during primary infection, and more commonly herpes labialis during reactivation17,18. In about 1–2/10,000 infected individuals of all ages, HSV-1 invades the central nervous system (CNS), probably via the olfactory bulb, causing forebrain HSE (≈95% of cases) or, more rarely, it passes via the TG nerves, causing brainstem HSE (≈5% of cases)11,19–21 (Figure 1). The seroprevalence of HSV-1-specific antibodies increases steadily from infancy into early adulthood, whereas the prevalence of HSE peaks before the age of 20 years, with the highest prevalence rates recorded between the ages of six months and three years, and then again after the age of 50 years1,19,22–24. Infection with HSV-1 is, therefore, necessary but far from sufficient for the development of HSE, which is strongly affected by age-dependent human determinants. This rare, severe disease striking a single organ due to infection with a single, typically innocuous virus was not suggestive of a primary immunodeficiency (PID), defined as an inherited defects of leukocytes25,26, genetically deciphered since 1985, which are typically familial traits conferring predisposition to multiple, recurrent or chronic, opportunistic infections of various organs27. The five known rare “Mendelian infections” were more suggestive of PIDs, as they include two severe viral infections, due to Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and human papillomaviruses (HPVs)28,29, and are typically familial, suggestive of Mendelian inheritance (i.e. with complete penetrance), which had been reported in only four kindreds with HSE30–33. Indeed, HSE is almost always sporadic, not only at population level in the infectious (sporadic as opposed to epidemic) sense of the term, but also at the individual level in the genetic (sporadic as opposed to familial) sense, as it strikes people with no relevant family or population history. Moreover, children suffering from inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) affecting leukocytes (PIDs) and those on immunosuppressive treatments display no unusual susceptibility to HSE1,34–36. Childhood predisposition to HSE, thus, appeared to be not only age-dependent, but also specific to both HSV-1 and the brain. We review here the last two decades of work testing the hypothesis that childhood HSE results from monogenic, but non-Mendelian IEIs of brain-intrinsic immunity to HSV-1 with incomplete clinical penetrance.

HSE being a brain- and HSV-1-specific condition, “syndromic” cases of HSE in combination with other severe infectious disease are exceedingly rare. However, such cases turned out to be crucial in studies of the pathogenesis of HSE. Indeed, two unrelated children with HSE and mycobacterial disease were studied in the early 2000s37–40. One of these patients, who died from HSE, was found in 2003 to have an autosomal recessive (AR) complete deficiency of STAT137. In various cell types tested at the time or later on, including leukocytes and dermal fibroblasts as a proxy for brain-resident cells, cellular responses to the three types of IFN (IFN-α/β, IFN-λ and IFN-γ) were found to be profoundly impaired in children with AR complete STAT1 deficiency37,41–43. This finding contrasts with observations for patients with AD partial STAT1 deficiency, whose cells respond normally to IFN-α/β and IFN-λ40. In total, 24 patients with complete STAT1 deficiency have since been reported. All known children with AR STAT1 deficiency suffered from mycobacterial disease (due to impaired response to IFN-γ), while about half of them also suffered from viral diseases (due to impaired response to IFN-α/β and/or IFN-λ). At least five of them were infected with HSV-1 at the time of testing, and one of whom had HSE41,44. The impairment of STAT1-dependent responses to IFN-α/β and/or IFN-λ therefore underlies HSE or other viral diseases, with incomplete penetrance. In 2004, a second child with HSE and mycobacterial disease was found to carry a NEMO mutation resulting in a partial form of X-linked recessive NEMO deficiency38. Hypomorphic mutations of NEMO, encoding one of the three subunits of the IKK complex in the canonical NF-κB pathway, had previously been shown to underlie various immunological and infectious phenotypes45. The patient died from HSE, and it was later demonstrated that leukocytes from this patient had impaired IFN-γ production, accounting for the patients’ mycobacterial disease, whereas the patient’s fibroblasts displayed impaired IFN-α/β and IFN-λ production following stimulation with various synthetic agonists or viruses, including HSV-1, accounting for HSE38,46,47. No history of HSE has been reported for any of the other patients with hypomorphic NEMO mutations since diagnosed, again suggesting incomplete penetrance. Collectively, these observations provided proof-of-principle that HSE may result from a monogenic defect with incomplete penetrance, in at least some children with STAT1 or NEMO deficiency and mycobacterial disease.

The first gene conferring a predisposition to isolated HSE to be identified, in 2006, was UNC93B148, the human ortholog of the mouse Unc93b1 gene that Bruce Beutler and coworkers had shown earlier in the year to be allelic to 3d, a locus controlling exogenous antigen presentation and Tlr3, Tlr7, and Tlr9 responses in mice49. Human UNC93B1 is an endoplasmic reticulum protein that interacts with TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 via their transmembrane domains and is required for the trafficking of these proteins to the endosomal compartment, where they are stimulated by their agonists50. Two unrelated children with AR complete UNC93B1 deficiency developed HSE but remained otherwise healthy48. The production of IFN-α/β and IFN-λ was impaired in the blood cells of these patients following TLR7, TLR8 or TLR9 stimulation, and in their fibroblasts following TLR3 stimulation. AD TLR3 deficiency was identified just a year later in two unrelated children with HSE51, in studies testing the hypothesis that defects of the TLR3 signaling pathway might be involved in HSE pathogenesis because patients with AR IRAK4 or MyD88 deficiency, who differ from UNC93B1-deficient patients in having impaired TLR7–9 immunity but normal TLR3 immunity, did not display HSE52–55. Twenty years after the first description of IRAK-4 deficiency, no patient with IRAK-4 or MyD88 deficiency has ever displayed a particular susceptibility to HSE56. Eight more unrelated HSE patients with AD (6 patients) or AR (3 patients) TLR3 deficiency (57–59 and our unpublished data) have been identified since the initial report in 2007. Impaired TLR3-dependent IFN-α/β and -λ production in the fibroblasts of UNC93B1- or TLR3-deficient patients is associated with the massive replication of HSV-1 and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), another neurotropic virus, resulting in high levels of cell death57,58. These viral phenotypes were rescued in vitro by the addition of recombinant IFN-α or -β. These experiments of Nature provided proof-of-principle that isolated childhood HSE can indeed result from single-gene IEIs, while also suggesting that impaired brain tissue-resident cell-intrinsic TLR3-dependent IFN-α/β- and IFN-λ-mediated antiviral immunity might be the root cause of HSE pathogenesis, at least in some children.

Since 2010, IEIs of other key molecules of the TLR3-IFN induction pathway have been identified in other children with HSE60–63 (Figure 2, Table 1). These IEIs include AR or AD TRIF deficiency, AD TRAF3 deficiency, AD TBK1 deficiency, and AD IRF3 deficiency. TRIF is the only known adaptor of TLR364–69. TRAF3 is an important adaptor70–73, TBK1 a kinase and IRF3 a regulatory transcription factor at the crossroads of multiple IFN-inducing signaling pathways, including that of TLR374–76. Like TLR3- or UNC93B1-deficient cells, dermal fibroblasts bearing any of these TLR3 pathway deficiencies display complete (AR UNC93B1, TLR3 or TRIF deficiency) or partial but profound (AD TLR3, TRIF, TRAF3, TBK1 or IRF3 deficiency) defects of IFN-β and -λ induction upon stimulation with the TLR3 agonist poly(I:C). In addition to impaired TLR3-dependent IFN-β and -λ production, IEIs of TRIF, TRAF3, TBK1, and IRF3 also partially decrease the responsiveness of other pathways, including the IFN-inducing pathways involving DExD/H-box helicases (TRIF deficiency), RIG-I/MDA5-MAVS (TRAF3, TBK1 and IRF3 deficiencies), TNF (TRAF3 deficiency), TLR7–9, and cGAS (IRF3 deficiency), in peripheral leukocytes or dermal fibroblasts60–63. However, the consequences of the partial impairment of these TLR3-independent pathways were not immediately clear, as the patients did not seem to display unusual susceptibility to infectious diseases other than HSE. A rare heterozygous gain-of-function variant of WWP2, encoding an E3 ubiquitin ligase that regulates TLR3 signaling by increasing TRIF ubiquitination, has recently been reported in a single child with HSE, but additional evidence is require to demonstrate causality77. AD IEIs of TLR3, TRIF, and IRF3 have also recently been identified in patients with influenza pneumonia or SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia, a new viral phenotype also partly attributed to the impairment of TLR3-dependent type I IFN immunity78–80. AD and AR deficiencies of TLR3 itself remain the most common genetic defects underlying HSE, found in up to 5% of the patients studied58. Using a new genome-wide (GW) computational approach to search for physiological homogeneity based on whole-exome sequencing (WES) data at cohort level, we recently retrospectively confirmed that rare possibly damaging variants of 10 TLR3-IFN cluster genes were the highest ranked putative causes of HSE, accounting for about 20% of the cohort studied (≈10% for six proven HSE genes)81. This analysis confirmed the results of individual patient-based molecular genetic studies at cohort level while also suggesting the involvement of other genes from this pathway.

We serendipitously clarified the respective contributions of IFN-α/β (type I) and IFN-λ (type III) to anti-HSV-1 immunity in the human brain, by identifying a large homozygous deletion in the IFNAR1 gene, which encodes one of the two chains of the receptor of type I IFNs82–84, in a patient who died from HSE85 (Figure 2, Table 1). The IFNAR1/IFNAR2 receptor complex is the sole receptor for all 16 subtypes of human type I IFNs, including 12 IFN-α (encoded by 13 loci), one IFN-β, one IFN-ε, one IFN-κ, and one IFN-ω86. The responses to IFN-α2b and IFN-β were abolished in dermal fibroblasts from the patient, confirming AR complete IFNAR1 deficiency. Another unrelated HSE patient with a recessive partial IFNAR1 deficiency was identified more recently59. The finding of IFNAR1 deficiency underlying HSE highlights the crucial role of IFNAR1/IFNAR2-mediated IFN-α/β immunity to HSV-1 infection in the CNS. Consistently, the lack of HSE in the more than 30 reported patients with AR IL-10RB deficiency, whose cells respond to type I but not type III IFNs, suggests that type III IFN is not essential for brain immunity to HSV-187,88. However, this remains a tentative conclusion, as only two of the at least 26 patients with IFNAR1 or IFNAR2 deficiency reported had HSE59,89–91. Two unrelated patients with syndromic HSE were recently found to have AR complete TYK2 (mycobacterial disease) or STAT2 (other viral diseases) deficiency92,93. Moreover, two AR IRF9-deficient siblings had other severe viral diseases, whereas a third sibling who died from HSE was not genotyped for IRF9 and may well have had the same IRF9 genotype as her two siblings94. The known key operators of the core type I IFN signaling pathway other than the IFNAR1/IFNAR2 receptor complex include JAK1, TYK2, STAT1, STAT2, and IRF984. Fibroblasts from human patients with complete IRF9 or IFNAR2 deficiency display a profound impairment of IFN-α and IFN-β responsiveness, explaining the severe viral diseases in these patients95,96. Unless IFNAR2 or JAK1 are redundant in the human brain, we expect to find IEIs of these molecules in HSE patients in the future. During encephalitis, type I IFNs may cross the inflamed blood-brain barrier59,97. The finding that IEIs of the type I IFN response pathway can underlie HSE paradoxically suggests that treatment with commercially available IFN-α2 or IFN-β, in addition to acyclovir, might be beneficial in HSE patients whose cells respond to IFN-α/β.

HSV-1 is a double-stranded DNA virus that, like many other viruses, can yield double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) viral intermediates or by-products of its replication in cells98,99. Human studies in vitro and mouse studies in vivo have suggested that both DNA and RNA sensors within cells are involved in the HSV-1-induced production of IFN during infection100–102. Interestingly, a recent study showed that infection with HSV-1 induced the trafficking of 5S ribosomal RNA pseudogene 141 (RNA5SP141), a cell-endogenous agonist of RIG-I, from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and its release from the cellular proteins with which it interacts, enabling this molecule to bind to RIG-I and induce the expression of type I IFNs102. In a more recent study, while searching for the genetic cause of HSE in another patient with defective adaptive immunity, Filomeen Haerynck and her team identified compound heterozygous loss-of-function mutations of the GTF3A gene encoding transcription factor IIIA (TFIIIA), a component of the RNA polymerase III (POL III) complex103. Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing analysis showed RNA5SP141 to be a transcriptional target of TFIIIA. The patient’s fibroblasts and GTF3A gene-edited cells had low levels of RNA5SP141 expression and no RIG-I-mediated IFN-α/β and IFN-λ production upon HSV-1 infection. Moreover, fibroblasts carrying GTF3A mutations displayed enhanced HSV-1 replication, a phenotype complemented by exogenous wild-type GTF3A expression or the addition of exogenous IFN-α/β103. These findings suggest that AR TFIIIA deficiency underlies HSE due to the impairment of RIG-I-induced type I IFN production during HSV-1 infection (Figure 2, Table 1). A sibling of the patient with the same GTF3A genotype and a similar immunological phenotype did not develop HSE during a previous infection with HSV-1. Human TFIIIA deficiency thus predisposes individuals to HSE with incomplete penetrance, like other known etiologies of HSE, but it also underlies an adaptive immunodeficiency of higher, and perhaps complete penetrance, resembling the etiologies of syndromic HSE that also underlie mycobacterial disease.

The unique added value of human studies is that they make it possible to define the function of host defense genes in natura104–108. The identification of monogenic IEIs of the TLR3-IFN-α/β-IFNAR1 circuit underlying childhood forebrain HSE paved the way for studies with patient-specific cell models testing the hypothesis that CNS-specific cell-intrinsic immunity, rather than leukocyte-mediated immunity, is crucial for host defense against HSV-1. We initially tested this hypothesis with dermal fibroblasts as surrogate cells, subsequently switching to the use of more relevant human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived CNS- and peripheral nervous system (PNS)-resident cells for patients with forebrain HSE and mutations of TLR3-IFN pathway genes. TLR3 pathway-deficient human fibroblasts48,51,57,58,60–62 and hPSC-derived cortical neurons and oligodendrocytes109 were found to be much more susceptible to HSV-1 than control cells (Table 2). AR IEIs of IFNAR1 or STAT1 conferred the same viral phenotype in dermal fibroblasts and hPSC-derived cortical neurons43,85. This phenotype was rescued by adding exogenous IFN-α/β, but not IFN-λ, in TLR3-deficient, but not IFNAR1-deficient or STAT1-deficient cells43,85,109. IFNAR2-deficient dermal fibroblasts and hPSC-derived cortical neurons displayed the same HSV-1 viral phenotype as IFNAR1- or STAT1-deficient cells (our unpublished data). By contrast, in vitro-differentiated human UNC93B1-deficient astrocytes or neural stem cells (NSCs) and, more importantly, TLR3-deficient peripheral TG neurons had normal susceptibility to HSV-1 infection, neatly highlighting the exclusive responsibility of the CNS, rather than the PNS, in the pathogenesis of childhood HSE110. The contribution of other types of brain-resident cells, such as microglial cells, remains to be determined. The most recently developed hPSC-derived brain organoids are now providing new models for the further dissection of brain region- and cell type-specific antiviral immunity111,112. The available data from hPSC-based experiments clearly indicate that TLR3-dependent, IFN-α/β-mediated cortical neuron- and oligodendrocyte-autonomous anti-HSV-1 immunity, as opposed to the innate and adaptive immunity mediated by leukocytes and related cells, is crucial for host defense against HSV-1 in the human forebrain.

TLR3 is a sensor of dsRNA. It was therefore initially thought that the underlying molecular mechanism by which deficiencies of the TLR3 pathway impair cell-intrinsic immunity to HSV-1 in human fibroblasts and brain-resident cells would involve an impairment of IFN-α/β induction following the recognition, by TLR3, of dsRNA viral intermediates or by-products. However, we recently showed that human TLR3 controls the constitutive levels of IFNB mRNA and secreted bioactive IFN-β protein, and, thus, also mRNA levels for IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) in fibroblasts and hPSC-derived cortical neurons113. The basal levels of TLR3-dependent IFN-β and ISG production in cells may be triggered by one or more of the proposed endogenous ligands, such as mRNA or debris from necrotic cells114, non-coding self RNAs generated during cell damage115,116, the microtubule regulator stathmin117, and/or a newly identified cell-endogenous agonist, biglycan118. More generally, basal levels of IFN and ISG mRNAs are low in the fibroblasts of patients with various inborn errors of the TLR3-dependent IFN induction pathway and the IFNAR1/IFNAR2-dependent IFN-α/β response pathway, attesting to the cell-intrinsic autoregulation of the TLR3-IFN-α/β circuit. We have also shown that TLR3 restricts viral growth in human fibroblasts and cortical neurons in vitro, through the basal IFN-β immunity-mediated control of early viral infection rather than the virus recognition-triggered amplification of IFN-α/β production. This finding highlights the importance of cell-intrinsic and constitutive IFN and ISG immunity in antiviral defense, particularly during early stages of viral infection113,119,120. Consistently, a recent study demonstrated the importance of basal levels of type I IFN immunity in hPSC-derived human neurons driven by high levels of long 3’-UTR self RNAs that signal via TLR3 and MDA5121. However, these findings do not exclude the possibility of HSV-1-derived intermediates or by-products inducing IFN-α/β via TLR3 or other type I IFN-inducing RNA or DNA sensors, thereby also contributing to the control of HSV-1 infection in the human brain. The finding of AR TFIIIA deficiency impairing RIG-I-mediated IFN-α/β production during HSV-1 infection provided evidence for the crucial role of virus-induced type I IFN immunity. IEIs of TRAF3, TBK1, or IRF3 may also impair both basal and virus-induced IFN-α/β immunity. Future studies searching for IEIs of these and other key molecules/pathways of the type I IFN network may help to clarify their roles in viral infection of the human brain, including that of individual type I IFNs and of cGAS/STING-IFN and other DNA sensors122.

Investigations of IEIs underlying HSE and other viral diseases progressively led to recognition that the TLR3-type I IFN circuit may govern the first line of broad antiviral responses in some cell types (e.g. cortical neurons and pulmonary epithelial cells) and in some specific tissues and organs (e.g. the brain and the lung)78,79,113. As a relatively isolated organ, the human brain may also use other TLR3- and IFN-independent mechanisms to fend off HSV-1 infection. A GW analysis of WES data from patients with forebrain HSE published in 2023 led to the identification of an otherwise healthy child with recurrent HSE who is compound heterozygous for nonsense (R422*) and frameshift (P493fs9*) RIPK3 variants123. Receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3) is a ubiquitous cytoplasmic kinase regulating cell death outcomes, including apoptosis and necroptosis124–129. When tested in vitro in HT29 colorectal carcinoma cells, the R422* and P493fs9* RIPK3 proteins were found to impair cellular apoptosis and necroptosis following stimulation with TLR3, TLR4, or TNFR1, and ZBP1(DAI)-mediated necroptotic cell death following HSV-1 infection. The patient’s fibroblasts had no detectable RIPK3, and apoptotic or necroptotic cell death following TNFR1 or TLR3 stimulation was impaired. Despite normal basal and induced levels of antiviral IFN-β and ISGs following HSV-1 infection, the patient’s fibroblasts were found to support excessively high levels of viral growth. These cells also responded normally to type I IFN, which rescued the viral phenotype. Cell death was impaired in the patient’s hPSC-derived cortical neurons, resulting in enhanced viral growth following HSV-1 infection, as observed in isogenic RIPK3 knockout (KO) hPSC-derived cortical neurons. AR RIPK3 deficiency, thus, confers a predisposition to HSE by impairing the cell death-dependent control of HSV-1 in cortical neurons independently of type I IFN immunity (Figure 2, Tables 1 and 2). This study provided proof-of principle that RIPK3-dependent cell death-mediated antiviral immunity is non-redundant, at least against HSV-1 in the human brain.

The increasing accessibility of next-generation sequencing technologies since 2010 has driven the discovery of new antiviral mechanisms in the human brain. A first example is provided by the discovery of an IEI of snoRNA31 in 201943 (Figure 2, Tables 1 and 2). SNORA31 is a highly conserved gene that encodes a 130-nucleotide small non-coding RNA (snoRNA) of the H/ACA box class, snoRNA31, with only one predicted function: directing the isomerization of uridine residues to generate pseudouridine at position 218 of the 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and position 3,713 of the 28S rRNA130,131. A GW analysis of WES data from patients with forebrain HSE led to the discovery of rare monoallelic variants of SNORA31 in five unrelated HSE patients43. We showed that snoRNA31 was a hitherto unrecognized CNS neuron-intrinsic HSV-1 restriction factor, as CRISPR/Cas9-introduced biallelic and monoallelic SNORA31 deletions rendered hPSC-derived cortical neurons highly susceptible to HSV-1. Accordingly, CNS cortical neurons derived from the hPSCs of patients with SNORA31 mutations are highly susceptible to HSV-1, like those from TLR3- or STAT1-deficient patients. Interestingly, transcriptomic analyses of hPSC-derived cortical neurons with SNORA31 mutations showed that these cells had impaired antiviral immune responses to HSV-1 despite normal responses to TLR3 and IFN-α/β stimulation, suggesting that AD snoRNA31 deficiency impairs cell-intrinsic immunity via a distinctive mechanism potentially involving an as yet undiscovered HSV-1-sensing molecule/pathway48,51. Moreover, fibroblasts from patients with AD snoRNA31 deficiency displayed enhanced susceptibility to a few other neurotropic viruses tested — varicella zoster virus, measles virus, vesicular stomatitis virus, and encephalomyocarditis virus — but not poliovirus. This observation raised the possibility that snoRNA31 might act as a restriction factor for a range of neurotropic viruses. The discovery of an AD IEI of snoRNA31 underlying HSE also highlighted the role of snoRNA in host antiviral defense while providing a second example of an inherited condition due to snoRNA mutations132. Further investigations are now required to determine the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms by which snoRNA31 acts as a virus restriction factor in neurons.

In parallel to our studies of the pathogenesis of forebrain HSE, we tested the hypothesis that brainstem HSE and other types of brainstem viral encephalitis result from inborn errors of brainstem-intrinsic immunity. We discovered biallelic mutations of the gene encoding debranching enzyme 1 (DBR1), which is highly conserved in humans, in five children who developed brainstem viral encephalitis due to infection with HSV-1, influenza B virus, or norovirus42. Ten other children with known or new deleterious biallelic DBR1 mutations have since been identified. All had brainstem viral encephalitis due to various viruses, including influenza A virus, human herpesvirus 6, and SARS-CoV-2 (133 and our unpublished data). DBR1 is the only known RNA lariat-debranching enzyme in humans. It hydrolyzes 2’5’-phosphodiester linkages at the branch points of intron lariat RNAs, facilitating their rapid turnover. Remarkably, DBR1 protein levels are highest in the spinal cord and brainstem. No connection between DBR1 and host immunity to infection was initially suspected42,134–139. The mutations found in the patients with viral encephalitis are severely hypomorphic, with only 1–5% residual activity. Interestingly, one very recent paper reported a new, different, biallelic DBR1 missense in five children from Saudi Arabia who died from congenital ichthyosis, suggesting that this new mutation may be more deleterious or may act via a different molecular mechanism140. DBR1-deficient fibroblasts from the patients with viral encephalitis, whose TLR3 and IFN-α/β response pathways were intact, were found to contain high RNA lariat levels and to display enhanced susceptibility to HSV-1 and VSV, like TLR3- and STAT1-deficient fibroblasts42. HPSC-derived hindbrain neurons for DBR1-deficient patients were highly susceptible to viruses, including HSV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 (133 and our unpublished data). The accumulation of RNA lariats may impair virus recognition by host cells, particularly via RNA-binding antiviral sensors, such as PKR141, thereby damaging brainstem-specific and cell-intrinsic defenses against viral invasion. Alternative molecular mechanisms, such as potential interference with the processing of some cellular protein-coding or non-coding RNAs or that of viral RNA lariats, cannot be excluded142–149. Remarkably, investigations of IEI underlying brainstem viral encephalitis revealed that DBR1 acts as a gatekeeper against viral infection in this small, specific, and critical region of the brain (Figure 2, Tables 1 and 2).

The discovery of IEIs of snoRNA31 and DBR1 provided proof-of-principle that completely new antiviral mechanisms also operate in the human brain, and that such mechanisms can be revealed by the search for new IEIs underlying HSE. In another recent study, a GW analysis of WES data from patients with forebrain HSE led to the identification of deleterious biallelic variants of the TMEFF1 gene encoding tomoregulin-1 (transmembrane protein with EGF-like and two follistatin-like domains 1, TMEFF1), in two children with sporadic HSE150. The TMEFF1 gene is highly conserved in humans and other animal species. However, since the first identification of its ortholog Tmeff1 in Xenopus laevis in 1996151, the function of TMEFF1 proteins in animals and humans has remained unclear. There was no evidence to suggest a role for TMEFF1 in antiviral immunity. Interestingly, in Xenopus laevis, as in mice, the production and function of Tmeff1 appear to be CNS-specific151–153. Likewise, the human TMEFF1 protein and mRNA are most abundant in the brain, and are, indeed, present almost exclusively in this organ. Detailed characterization of human TMEFF1 and its HSE patient-specific variants demonstrated that TMEFF1 is a cell-surface protein preferentially expressed on brain cortical neurons. It interacts with the main HSV-1 receptor, NECTIN-1, preventing HSV-1 gD-human NECTIN-1-mediated fusion between the virus and the cell, thereby blocking viral entry independently of type I IFN. AR TMEFF1 deficiency enables HSV-1 to enter patient-specific or CRISPR/Cas9-mediated TMEFF1 KO hPSC-derived cortical neurons rapidly, thereby enhancing HSV-1 translocation to the nucleus and replication. Human TMEFF1 is, thus, a restriction factor for HSV-1 entry into cortical neurons150,154. The constitutively high abundance of TMEFF1 in cortical neurons protects these cells from HSV-1 infection, whereas inherited TMEFF1 deficiency renders them susceptible to this virus, thereby underlying HSE (Figure 2, Tables 1 and 2). The antiviral action of TMEFF1 through interaction with NECTIN-1 suggested that TMEFF1 might be a specific viral restriction factor for the very small number of alphaherpesviruses (HSV-1, HSV2, VZV) that use NECTIN-1 as an entry receptor155,156, or possibly even only for HSV-1 in cortical neurons, opening up new possibilities for disease prevention and treatment in HSV-1-infected patients.

From AR STAT1 deficiency, the first IEI underlying syndromic HSE to be discovered, to AR TMEFF1 deficiency, the most recently discovered IEI underlying isolated HSE, the 19 HSE-predisposing gene defects identified (including plausible defects of IFNAR2, JAK1 and IRF9) display complete cellular penetrance, but incomplete clinical penetrance. Indeed, genetically affected relatives of HSE probands who are HSV-1-seropositive but have never had HSE have often been observed37,43,48,51,60–63,85,92,93,103. The only apparent exceptions are AR TLR3 and TRIF deficiencies, and AR RIPK3 deficiency, which display complete penetrance for HSE. However, the very small number of patients with these defects reported to date makes it difficult to interpret the findings clearly42,57,123. Similarly, IEIs of the TLR3 and type I IFN response pathways underlie a narrow range of other severe viral diseases, also with incomplete penetrance78,79,85. The precise clinical penetrance of these deficiencies for HSE and other viral diseases is difficult to estimate, given the small number of patients with each of the genetic defects, the lack of population-level viral serological data, and the sporadic nature of each infectious condition. The TLR3 P554S variant may be the only recurrent genotype underlying HSE in some children and viral pneumonia in others51,78,79. It has been found in three HSE-affected families containing nine heterozygous carriers, three of whom developed childhood HSE. Further studies are now required, to develop new computational and experimental tools for estimating the penetrance of physiologically connected rare gene defects for any given infection. Nevertheless, the incomplete penetrance of the known IEIs underlying HSE is consistent with the sporadic nature of HSE. Both host (age at infection, modifying genes) and viral (viral load, virus strain) factors may govern clinical penetrance. The 20 years of research on single-gene IEIs underlying HSE have also provided a new model of human genetic determinants of sporadic infectious diseases, with monogenic, but non-Mendelian predisposition to severe infectious consequences triggered by a specific pathogen under natural conditions of infection27,157.

The identification of human genetic determinants of childhood HSE have not only led to the new notion that a specific infection of a specific organ can be due to IEIs disrupting pathogen- and/or organ-specific defense mechanisms (Box 1), but have also raised new questions about HSE for investigation in future studies: 1) What other IEIs will be discovered in HSE patients? Future research may lead to discoveries of new mechanisms governing HSV-1-specific immunity, involving the receptors/pathways that may or may not relate to IFNs. 2) Could mouse models help decipher HSE pathogenesis? Some forward and reverse genetic approaches could advance our understanding of brain-intrinsic antiviral immunity (Box 2). 3) Could autoimmune phenocopies of some IEIs, including autoantibodies neutralizing type I IFNs, underlie HSE (Figure 3)? Since 2004, autoantibodies against cytokines have been shown to phenocopy the corresponding IEIs, underlying the same infectious phenotypes158. The most recent discoveries include the detection of autoantibodies against type I IFNs in 40% of adult patients with West Nile virus encephalitis159 and 10% of adults with tick-borne virus encephalitis160. These autoantibodies might perhaps underlie HSE in some patients, particularly adults. 4) What host factors confer a predisposition to HSE in adults? Like most other types of viral encephalitis, HSE has an age-dependent ‘U’-shaped incidence curve, with the highest incidence rates recorded in young children and the elderly1,3,19,22–24,161,162. Both human genetic defects and autoimmune phenocopies of some IEIs may underlie HSE in adults. Accordingly, IEIs of MASP2, a protease of the lectin pathway, and of two autophagy genes (ATG4A and LC3B2), have each been reported in two adults (MASP2) with HSE or a single adult (ATG4A, LC3B2) with recurrent HSV-2 Mollaret meningitis163,164. Some IEIs may be more penetrant in adults following the reactivation of HSV-1 from latency. Autoantibodies against type I IFNs or other molecules may also be involved165. Finally, somatic gene mutations in brain-resident cells may also accumulate with age and impair brain antiviral immunity.

These studies also pave the way for new avenues of research extending beyond HSE: 1) Are there other phenotypes associated with the known HSE-causing genes? Could TLR3, as a rheostat of basal type I IFN immunity in several cell types, govern the first line of broad antiviral responses in various tissue-resident cell types, or might it play a broader role in human antiviral immunity, including but not limited to the brain? TLR3 has already been shown to underlie enterovirus encephalitis166 and severe viral infections of the lungs78,79,113. 2) Could childhood encephalitis due to infection with other viruses also be genetic? The search for human genetic and immunological determinants of other types of viral encephalitis would widen and deepen our understanding of the antiviral defense mechanisms of the human brain. As proof-of-concept, IEIs of TLR3 or MDA5 have been identified in children with isolated enterovirus encephalitis166; IEIs of POLR3A and POLR3C, another two components of the POL III complex containing TFIIIA, confer susceptibility to severe varicella zoster virus diseases of the brain and the lungs167; and DBR1 deficiency impairing cell-intrinsic antiviral immunity at least in hindbrain neurons has been identified in children with brainstem encephalitis due to different DNA and RNA viruses42,133. Overall, the discovery of IEIs underlying childhood HSE has established a solid base for further explorations of viral encephalitis and other tissue-specific infections by forward genetic means and with a mechanistic mindset.
