Propionic acidemia (PA) is a genetic metabolic disorder caused by mutations in 
the mitochondrial enzyme, propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC), which is responsible 
for converting propionyl-CoA to methylmalonyl-CoA for further metabolism in the 
tricarboxylic acid cycle. When this process is disrupted, propionyl-CoA and its 
metabolites accumulate, leading to a variety of complications including 
life-threatening cardiac diseases and other metabolic strokes. While the 
clinical symptoms and diagnosis of PA are well established, the underlying 
pathophysiological mechanisms of PA-induced diseases are not fully understood. 
As a result, there are currently few effective therapies for PA beyond dietary 
restriction. This review focuses on the pathophysiological mechanisms of the 
various complications associated with PA, drawing on extensive research and 
clinical reports. Most research suggests that propionyl-CoA and its metabolites 
can impair mitochondrial energy metabolism and cause cellular damage by inducing 
oxidative stress. However, direct evidence from in vivo studies is still 
lacking. Additionally, elevated levels of ammonia can be toxic, although not all 
PA patients develop hyperammonemia. The discovery of pathophysiological 
mechanisms underlying various complications associated with PA can aid in the 
development of more effective therapeutic treatments. The consequences of 
elevated odd-chain fatty acids in lipid metabolism and potential gene expression 
changes mediated by histone propionylation also warrant further investigation.

Propionic acidemia (PA, also known as OMIM# 606054 and ORPHA: 35) is an infrequent genetic disorder that follows an autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance. It arises due to a malfunctioning mitochondrial enzyme called propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC; EC 6.4.1.3), leading to the buildup of propionyl-CoA and its byproducts. In 1968, the initial instance of PA was documented, wherein the patient displayed significant metabolic acidosis owing to 5.4 mM of propionic acid found in their plasma (Hommes, Kuipers, Elema, Jansen, & Jonxis, 1968). The global incidence of PA is highly variable, with a range of 1:50,000 to 1:100,000 worldwide (Al-Hamed, et al., 2019; Desviat, et al., 2004). However, the prevalence of PA is notably greater in specific populations, such as the Inuit people living in Greenland (1:1,000). The utilization of tandem mass spectrometry for neonatal screening has led to the identification of a greater frequency of PA (Tajima, et al., 2021).

The PCC enzyme is present in the mitochondria and catalyzes the propionyl-CoA carboxylation to D-methylmalonyl-CoA with ATP. It comprises two subunits, α and β, with a total molecular weight of roughly 700,000 Da. The enzyme has an optimal pH range of 8.0–8.5 and is activated by potassium (Hsia, Scully, & Rosenberg, 1979). Within the PCC enzyme, the α subunit comprises the biotin carboxylase, biotin transfer, and biotin carboxyl carrier protein domains. Subdomains A, B, and C make up the biotin carboxylase domain, which carries out the function of the Mg2+-ATP-dependent carboxylation (H. R. Wang, et al., 2020).

Propionyl-CoA is a downstream metabolite of isoleucine, valine, threonine, methionine, odd-chain fatty acids, and side-chain cholesterol (He, Berthiaume, Previs, Kasumov, & Zhang, 2023; He, Wang, Xie, Barry, & Zhang, 2021; Rivera-Barahona, et al., 2018) (Figure 1). Additionally, gut bacteria fermentation can produce propionate (Thompson, et al., 1990), which is metabolized to propionyl-CoA by short-chain acetyl-CoA synthetase (Yoshimura, Araki, Maruta, Takahashi, & Yamashita, 2017). Mutations of PCC can lead to reduced enzyme activity, causing the buildup of propionyl-CoA and its corresponding metabolites (Figure 1). While the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the various complications associated with PA are not fully understood, several interventions have been shown to be effective in controlling the progression of complications and lowering the occurrence of metabolic decompensations. These interventions consist of limiting protein intake, administering L-carnitine supplements, and employing antibiotics.

Our comprehensive survey of the latest literature on both research and clinical reports offers an up-to-date review of PA, with emphasis placed on the different associated complications and their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.

PCC is a heterododecamer that is comprised of six PCCA (propionyl-CoA carboxylase α subunits) and six PCCB subunits (propionyl-CoA carboxylase β subunits). The PCCA and PCCB genes, encoding for the α and β subunits of the PCC enzyme, respectively, are located on chromosome 13q32.3 and chromosome 3q22.3 (Tian, Wang, Shi, & Bai, 2020). Within the PCCA gene lies a stretch of DNA spanning over 360 kb and consisting of 24 exons that vary in length from 37 to 335 bp, encoding a protein composed of 728 amino acids (NM_000282). In contrast, the PCCB gene includes 15 exons that encode a protein comprised of 539 amino acids (NM_000532) (H. R. Wang, et al., 2020). PCCA generates carboxybiotin by breaking down ATP and transfers CO2 to the propionyl-CoA attached to the β subunit (Kraus, et al., 2012). A lysine residue located in the sequence Ala-Met-Lys-Met, which is conserved in biotin-dependent carboxylases, serves as the biotin attachment site (Perez, et al., 2003). The expression of PCCA does not appear to be the limiting factor for PCC activity. However, mutations in PCCB may affect the enzyme’s active site and result in a reduction of PCC activity in fibroblasts (Al-Hamed, et al., 2019; Rivera-Barahona, et al., 2018). The amino terminus of both PCCA and PCCB subunits contain mitochondrial leader sequences that facilitate the translocation of the protein into the mitochondria.

Recent advancements in genetics techniques have led to the discovery of many more mutations in both PCCA and PCCB. The detailed variants are listed in Supplemental Table 1. Based on their consequences, mutations in the PCC enzyme can be categorized into three groups: (1) folding defects of the hetero-oligomeric assembly, which can potentially be treated with drugs that improve PCC folding (Chloupkova, Ravn, Schwartz, & Kraus, 2000; H. Jiang, Rao, Yee, & Kraus, 2005; Ohura, Kraus, & Rosenberg, 1989; Perez-Cerda, et al., 2003; Rivera-Barahona, et al., 2018); (2) mutations occurring in catalytic sites of enzyme; and (3) mutations resulting in protein destabilization (Rivera-Barahona, et al., 2018).

The identification of genotype-phenotype correlation is helpful for preventive treatment. Tracing propionate metabolism with 13C-labeled propionate demonstrated a strong correlation between propionate metabolism and clinical severity of PA (Barshop, et al., 1991). Null alleles result in the most severe phenotype, whereas missense mutations are commonly associated with a less severe phenotype (Desviat, et al., 2004; Hannah, et al., 2019). However, this observation is limited to patients who are homozygous or have functional hemizygosity (along with a second null allele). In certain cases, homozygous patients with particular mutations may exhibit a more severe clinical course (Kim, Ryu, Lee, Kim, & Hahn, 2002). The interpretation of the relationship between genotype and phenotype can be complicated by the presence of compound heterozygous mutations or rare missense mutations (Rivera-Barahona, et al., 2018). For instance, a patient with PA presented with mild symptoms and was found to have a catalytic mutation p.Q58P along with a null mutation that resulted in no PCC activity. On the contrary, a patient with a mild missense mutation (p.H534R) along with a null mutation in the PCCB gene presented with severe symptoms. The potential leak of splicing mutations can result in the generation of some normal PCC, which may account for the presence of a small amount of normal protein and the mild phenotype observed in some cases (Clavero, Perez, Rincon, Ugarte, & Desviat, 2004). In addition to gene mutations, other factors such as the minimum requirement of protein activity, other genes that affect PCC expression, and the dynamics of whole system can add complexity to genotype-phenotype correlations (Dipple & McCabe, 2000).

Most PA patients are diagnosed with PA at an early age, typically before 3 months (Al-Hamed, et al., 2019). PA exhibits highly heterogeneous clinical features, including poor feeding, dehydration, vomiting, failure to thrive, listlessness, tachypnea, low muscle tone, pancytopenia, seizures, acidosis, hyperammonemia, hyperglycinemia, bone marrow suppression, ketonuria, and hypoglycemia (Al-Hamed, et al., 2019; Kor, et al., 2019; Shchelochkov, Carrillo, & Venditti, 1993). Late diagnosis can lead to severe developmental delay and various complications, which are discussed in detail in the following sections (Kolker, Garcia-Cazorla, et al., 2015; Kolker, Valayannopoulos, et al., 2015; Wolf, et al., 1981). Failure to receive treatment for the disease can lead to rapid progression. Thus, early diagnosis and preventive therapy are key factors in improving the quality of life for patients with PA.

Biochemical features of PA include increased levels of propionylcarnitine, 2-methylcitrate (MCA),
propionylglycine, tiglyglycine, propionate, and 3-hydroxypropionate (3HPA). All of these metabolites are related to propionyl-CoA metabolism (Kolker, Valayannopoulos, et al., 2015; Wolf, et al., 1981), and their elevations are a direct consequence of low PCC activity. Some but not all PA patients present ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, hyperammonemia, hyperglycinemia, and elevated ketones (Yang, et al., 2004). Therefore, accurate diagnosis of PA can be achieved through measuring propionylcarnitine, MCA, and 3HPA levels in plasma or urine, in combination with gene mutation analysis.

PA is frequently associated with cardiac disease, and the most common cardiac complications are QT prolongation (Laemmle, et al., 2014; Peregud-Pogorzelska, et al., 2019; Tajima, et al., 2021), hypertrophy, and dilated cardiomyopathy (Grotto, et al., 2018; Hejazi, Hijazi, Al-Saloos, & Omran, 2022; Iwashima, Ishikawa, Ohzeki, & Endou, 2010; Kovacevic, et al., 2020; Laemmle, et al., 2014; Lee, Addonizio, Barshop, & Chung, 2009; Peregud-Pogorzelska, et al., 2019; Riemersma, et al., 2017; Romano, et al., 2010; Taniwaki & Misawa, 1988), which can lead to fatal cardiac arrhythmias (Della Rossa, Dixit, Shah, Hang, & Duong, 2022; Lucke, et al., 2004). Reports indicate that approximately 25% of PA patients develop cardiac disease, but conventional tests like left ventricular ejection fraction may underestimate this rate. The left ventricular global longitudinal strain parameter is reported to be a better parameter for diagnosing early cardiac diseases in PA patients (Kovacevic, et al., 2022). Therefore, it is crucial to carefully evaluate the potential cardiac risk in PA patients at an early stage to prevent the progression of severe cardiac dysfunctions.

Protein restriction and other treatments, such as liver transplant, can improve cardiac complications in PA patients. However, some patients may still develop cardiac disease despite these interventions (Hejazi, et al., 2022; Romano, et al., 2010). The likelihood of developing cardiac disease in PA patients increases with advancing age (Kovacevic, et al., 2020; Peregud-Pogorzelska, et al., 2019; Tajima, et al., 2021).

To date, no metabolic parameters have been identified as being linked to cardiac disease in PA. In fact, cardiac lesions can develop in PA patients regardless of the severity of metabolic derangement (Iwashima, et al., 2010; Laemmle, et al., 2014; Lee, et al., 2009; Lucke, et al., 2004; Romano, et al., 2010; Tajima, et al., 2021; Taniwaki & Misawa, 1988). However, metabolic information is often obtained from plasma or urine, which may not accurately reflect tissue concentrations (Kovacevic, et al., 2022). Therefore, the definitive mechanism by which PA causes heart disease is not yet fully understood. Nevertheless, numerous research studies have attempted to shed light on this topic, and their key findings are summarized below.

Propionyl-CoA metabolism primarily occurs in the mitochondria of the heart. A deficiency in PCC accumulates propionyl-CoA and its metabolites, including MCA, 3HPA, and maleic acid. These metabolites may inhibit various enzymes and proteins involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism, such as the pyruvate dehydrogenase, mitochondrial complex III, and oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (Hejazi, et al., 2022; Mizuguchi, Hoshino, Nagasawa, & Kubota, 2009; Roginski, et al., 2020). Mitochondrial dysfunction-mediated heart disease in patients with PA was suggested by the presence of hyperglycemia, hyperlactatemia, and a decrease in serum free carnitine levels (Mizuguchi, et al., 2009; Tan, Bajaj, Morel, & Singh, 2018). The impaired mitochondria is consistent with the reduced Pi levels observed in vivo using non-invasive magnetic resonance spectroscopy (Gancheva, et al., 2020). Propionyl-CoA accumulation may also cause CoA trapping, which alters fuel switching in heart tissue and reduces the efficiency of cardiac energy metabolism (Y. Wang, et al., 2018). In addition, maleic acid, one of the metabolites of propionyl-CoA, can cause disturbances in mitochondrial energy, CoA trapping, and calcium homeostasis. This may contribute to the development of cardiac diseases in PA patients (Roginski, et al., 2020).

Studies have demonstrated that propionate and its metabolites, propionylcarnitine and MCA, can modify the expression of subunits of potassium channels and affect their repolarizing currents. This may increase the risk of developing acquired long QT syndrome associated with PA (Bodi, et al., 2016; Tajima, et al., 2021). This disruption of K+ flow via the KvLQT1/KCNE channel not only causes long QT syndrome but may also contribute to hearing impairment in PA patients (Grunert, Bodi, & Odening, 2017).

Several miRNAs have been found to be elevated in both Pcca−/−(A138T) mice and patients with PA. These miRNAs are known to play critical roles in heart health and disease. The identification of these altered miRNAs in PA patients suggests that they may serve as potential
therapeutic targets and biomarkers (Fulgencio-Covian, et al., 2020; Rivera-Barahona, Fulgencio-Covian, et al., 2017).

The observation of CoQ10 deficiency in the cardiac failure of a pediatric PA patient suggests that this may be a mechanism contributing to cardiac complications. This is supported by findings from other studies (Baruteau, et al., 2014; Fragaki, et al., 2011). However, Kovacevic et al. report that coenzyme Q10 does not seem to prevent or treat cardiac diseases in PA (Kovacevic, et al., 2020).

A post-mortem examination of a patient with PA revealed a significant decrease in free carnitine in cardiac tissue and lack of NADH cytochrome c reductase in muscle tissues including heart (Mardach, Verity, & Cederbaum, 2005). Despite this finding, clinical studies have not consistently demonstrated the efficacy of carnitine supplement in providing beneficial effects for patients with cardiomyopathy related to PA (Bhan & Brody, 2001). Furthermore, the uptake of carnitine by the heart remains an area for further investigation, as cardiac carnitine levels remain low even with carnitine supplementation.

Inadequate thiamine intake from total parenteral nutrition can result in thiamine deficiency, which may lead to lactic acidosis and cardiomyopathy (Matern, et al., 1996).

No specific medical treatments exist yet for cardiac disease in patients with PA. First-line treatment for cardiac complications associated with PA typically involves the use of cardiac drugs such as ACE inhibitors, beta-adrenergic blocking agents, water pills, or digitalis. Early detection of cardiac disease in PA is critical for prompt intervention, which may improve patient outcomes. Despite advances in treatment aimed at limiting the accumulation of toxic compounds, patients with PA still face poor outcomes in the long run with potential various complications (Romano, et al., 2010). Although CoQ10 has been proposed as a potentially effective treatment for improving left ventricular function, no evidence is found to substantiate its application in this context (Kovacevic, et al., 2020). Studies have demonstrated the efficacy of liver transplantation (LT) in reversing cardiomyopathy, although there have been reports of patients developing cardiomyopathy after LT (Berry, et al., 2020; Hejazi, et al., 2022; Tajima, et al., 2021). Heart transplantation may be considered as a last resort when other treatments have failed (Hejazi, et al., 2022; Lotan, et al., 2023; Seguchi, Toda, Hamada, Fujita, & Fukushima, 2022).

Neurological disorders are highly prevalent in patients with PA (Liu, et al., 2022). Brain imaging examinations of PA patients have revealed various abnormalities, including bilateral basal ganglia damage, generalized brain atrophy, delayed myelination, white matter changes, cerebral atrophy, intracranial hemorrhage, cerebellar hemorrhage, medullary lesions, scattered lesions in cerebellar hemispheres, astrocytosis, oligodendrocytic loss, neuronal loss, frontal lobe encephalomalacia, spongiosis, vacuolization, visual hallucinations, subcortical diffusion restriction, vermian atrophy, changes in cortical volume (Akman, Imamoglu, Demirkol, Alpay, & Ozek, 2002; AlGhamdi, et al., 2018; Asconape, Challa, & Angelo, 1981; Brismar & Ozand, 1994; Broomfield, Gunny, Prabhakar, & Grunewald, 2010; Cao, et al., 2023; Dave, Curless, & Steinman, 1984; Feliz, Witt, & Harris, 2003; Haas, et al., 1995; Hamilton, Haas, Nyhan, Powell, & Grafe, 1995; Johnson, Le, & Palacios, 2009; Kandel, Amatya, & Yeh, 2013; Liu, et al., 2022; Pfeifer, Van Tassel, & Miller, 2018; Prasun, Bailey, & Kishnani, 2014; Sethi, et al., 1989; Shuaib, et al., 2012; Velasco-Sanchez, et al., 2009). In addition, optic nerve atrophy has been reported frequently following metabolic episodes (Arias, et al., 2014; Martinez Alvarez, Jameson, Parry, Lloyd, & Ashworth, 2016; Noval, et al., 2013), however, there are also reports of optic nerve atrophy occurring without an episode (Ianchulev, Kolin, Moseley, & Sadun, 2003; Komatsuzaki, et al., 2012; Martinez Alvarez, et al., 2016; Williams, et al., 2009). In one particular case, PA patients were found to have deficiencies in vitamins B1 and B6, and supplementation resulted in improved vision (Williams, et al., 2009).

Patients with PA exhibit a wide range of neurological manifestations, which include cognitive impairment, developmental delay, and intellectual disability (Surtees, Matthews, & Leonard, 1992), as well as structural defects, the disorder of neuro-psychomotor development, and metabolic decompensations (Sivananthan, Hadzic, Dhawan, Heaton, & Vara, 2021). Other neurological symptoms include cranial nerve abnormalities, infantile spasms (Aldamiz-Echevarria Azuar, Prats Vinas, Sanjurjo Crespo, Prieto Perera, & Labayru Echeverria, 2005), seizures (Yuan, 1991), agitation, chronic psychiatric symptom, hallucinations, panic, grossly disorganized behavior (Dejean de la Batie, et al., 2014; Johnson, et al., 2009; Karimzadeh, et al., 2014; Prasun, et al., 2014; Scholl-Burgi, et al., 2009; Schreiber, et al., 2012; Shuaib, et al., 2012), and acute aphasia generalized hypotonia (Haas, et al., 1995). When the metabolic demand is increased, these deteriorations occur.

Evidence associating PA with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has been widely documented in patients (Al-Owain, et al., 2013; Cotrina, Ferreiras, & Schneider, 2020; de la Batie, et al., 2018; Witters, et al., 2016) and in animal models (MacFabe, et al., 2007; MacFabe, Cain, Boon, Ossenkopp, & Cain, 2011). The current mechanism underlying PA-induced ASD is not yet understood. Mitochondrial dysfunction resulting from PA has been suggested to contribute to ASD, but further investigation is required to confirm its relevance to PA patients (Witters, et al., 2016).

Previously, it was thought that neurological disorders in PA were only attributed to acute metabolic crises. For instance, lowering plasma ammonia by peritoneal dialysis remarkably improved neurologic status (Akman, et al., 2002). However, patients with PA may exhibit neurological symptoms such as hypotonia and cognitive delay without experiencing life-threatening episodes (North, et al., 1995; Nyhan, Bay, Beyer, & Mazi, 1999), indicating a need for further research.

The exact pathophysiological mechanism underlying how PA leads to brain diseases remains unknown (Haas, et al., 1995). However, several possible mechanisms have been proposed based on various experimental models, mostly discussing the consequences of accumulating propionate and its corresponding metabolites. For instance, propionate has been shown to interfere with the regulatory effects of cAMP and type I protein kinase. Additionally, the changes of the phosphorylation of cytoskeletal proteins, glial fibrillary acidic proteins, and vimentins by the pathological level of propionate in PA patients could contribute to the neurological dysfunction observed in PA (de Almeida, Funchal, Gottfried, Wajner, & Pessoa-Pureur, 2006; de Almeida, et al., 2003; de Mattos-Dutra, et al., 1997). Furthermore, propionate inhibits brain lipid biosynthesis. The reduced lipid biosynthesis could cause demyelination that characterizes patients affected by PA (de Mello, Rubin, Coelho, Wajner, & Souza, 1997).

Studies have shown that propionate results in a marked decrease in all ganglioside species, except for an increase in GM2. They are connected to the surface of dendrites and other neural membranes and are linked to synaptogenesis. This can potentially play a role in the observed brain impairment in PA. (Trindade, et al., 2002). In addition, the glial metabolism of propionate suggests vulnerability of astrocytes in PA when intramitochondrial propionyl-CoA accumulates, and it has been suggested that propionyl-CoA may change histone acetylation and further alter gene expression in neuronal and glial cells (Nguyen, et al., 2007).

Glutamate dehydrogenase can be inhibited by MCA even at low concentrations of 0.5 mM. Additionally, MCA has been found to mediate mitochondria swelling and significantly decrease mitochondrial membrane potential. The harmful impact of MCA on mitochondria could potentially play a role in the onset of brain impairment in individuals with PA (Amaral, Cecatto, Castilho, & Wajner, 2016).

The capacity of ascorbic acid to avert the behavioral changes triggered by propionate demonstrates that oxidative stress may cause the development of neurological disorders (Pettenuzzo, et al., 2002). This is further supported by evidence of increased reactive oxygen species and protein or lipid peroxidation mediated by high levels of propionate (Colin-Gonzalez, et al., 2015; Fernandes, et al., 2011; Rigo, et al., 2006).

The significance of propionyl-CoA as an anaplerotic substrate in various tissues is still unclear. It is possible that the decrease of anaplerosis from propionyl-CoA in PA redirects anaplerosis from other substrates, such as glutamine and glutamate in the brain, which may indicate impaired aerobic respiration within brain tissue (Davison, et al., 2011). Additionally, long-term use of metronidazole in PA patients may potentially cause neurotoxicity (Diodato, et al., 2018).

While kidney diseases have been less commonly reported in PA patients (Vernon, Bagnasco, Hamosh, & Sperati, 2014) compared to methylmalonic acidemia, early onset kidney diseases (Bernheim, Deschenes, Schiff, Cussenot, & Niel, 2017; Caltik Yilmaz, Kilic, Buyukkaragoz, Tayfur, & Kocak, 2015a, 2015b; Kasapkara, et al., 2014) have been particularly infrequent. However, with the improvements in treatments for PA since its first occurrence, more patients are now surviving into adulthood, and as a result, new late-stage complications, including chronic kidney disease (CKD), are emerging (Lam, et al., 2011; Roginski, et al., 2022; Shchelochkov, et al., 2019; Vernon, et al., 2014).

The exact mechanism by which PA can result in CKD is still unclear, but various studies have examined the impact of maleic acid on kidney function. Maleic acid, a compound that PA patients excrete heavily, causes mitochondrial permeability transition and decreases mitochondrial redox, membrane potential, and energy production. In addition, maleic acid has been shown to have cellular toxic effects, increasing propidium iodide incorporation and decreasing the reduction of 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazoliumbromide (Roginski, et al., 2022). In a separate study, maleic acid was found to impair respiration complexes. Furthermore, maleic acid was discovered to inhibit several mitochondrial dehydrogenases (Roginski, et al., 2019). Apart from the two aforementioned research investigations, a 13C-tracing study using PCCA null HEK293 cells revealed abnormal TCA cycle flux and glucose to serine synthesis, indicating possible altered energy metabolism in PA kidney cells (Anzmann, et al., 2019). The aforementioned studies propose that maleic acid has the potential to disrupt the kidney’s energy balance, resulting in chronic kidney failure. However, further research is required to validate this hypothesis.

Immune deficiency with PA has been reported. Cytopenia has been previously reported to associate with organic acidemias. PA patients experience thrombocytopenia (Branski, Gale, Gross-Kieselstein, & Abrahamov, 1977; Sindgikar, Rao, Shenoy, & Kamath, 2013) or more specifically immune thrombocytopenic purpura (Bulan, et al., 2014), leucopenia (Branski, et al., 1977; Sindgikar, et al., 2013), neutropenia (Sindgikar, et al., 2013; Sipahi, Yilmaz, & Tavil, 2004), anemia (Sipahi, et al., 2004; Stanescu, et al., 2021) and pancytopenia (Aydin Koker, Yesilbas, Koker, & Sevketoglu, 2020; Kasapkara, et al., 2015; Stork, et al., 1986). Trilinegage myelodysplasia has also been found in a PA patient (Sipahi, et al., 2004).

In a retrospective study by Medina-Torres et al. (Medina-Torres, et al., 2021), PA patients had abnormally low counts of CD4, CD8, CD19, and CD16+. Low absolute values of these T and B lymphocytes make this population susceptible to immunodeficiency. The presence of bacterial (Werlin, 1993) and fungal sepsis (Sindgikar, et al., 2013) as well as some uncommon infections (Al Essa, et al., 1998) in PA patients supports the idea of immunodeficiency. Additionally, secondary Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis has been found in PA patients who experienced metabolic decomposition and pancytopenia in the neonatal period (Aydin Koker, et al., 2020; Kasapkara, et al., 2015) or various infections in teenage years (Sulaiman, et al., 2016). Secondary lymphohistiocytosis is caused by another associated disorder, such as severe viral infections or inborn errors of metabolism (Kasapkara, et al., 2015), which suggests that PA patients could have increased likelihood of acquiring as they are immunodeficient.

The mechanism by which PA can cause cytopenia is not yet fully understood. According to a retrospective study in Madrid, low protein intake and an imbalanced intake of branched-chain amino acids were associated with severe anemia (Stanescu, et al., 2021). The extremely high levels of propionyl-CoA and its metabolites can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and interfere with hematopoiesis. This may make PA patients especially susceptible to cytopenia, as mitochondrial dysfunction can inhibit bone marrow proliferation, shorten the lifespan of red blood cells, and arrest the maturation of hematopoietic precursors (Medina-Torres, et al., 2021). Buffered propionic acid was found to inhibit the growth of both mouse erythroid and human granulocytic cultures in a concentration-dependent manner, while other intermediates of propionate exhibited no impact (Stork, et al., 1986).

While relatively uncommon in children, acute pancreatitis can be associated with patients who have PA (Bultron, Seashore, Pashankar, & Husain, 2008; Choe, et al., 2019; Gilmore, Bock, & Nowicki, 2008). Imaging has revealed a diffusely edematous or swollen pancreas (Bultron, et al., 2008; Choe, et al., 2019). The exact mechanism by which PA triggers pancreatitis is still unclear, but there are multiple proposed theories. First, it is suggested that PA can alter enzyme activity, metabolic/oxidative stress on the pancreas, ketoacidosis, and deficiencies in carnitine and methionine. Second, accumulating odd-chain fatty acids could potentially increase the risk of developing pancreatitis. Third, cellular acidosis may activate pancreatic protease zymogens, which is an early event in pancreatitis. Finally, the elevated mitochondrial calcium is a potential risk factor for pancreatitis development (Bultron, et al., 2008; Choe, et al., 2019).

Propionate and propionyl-CoA are predominantly metabolized in the liver. The anaplerosis of propionyl-CoA is a significant source of metabolic precursors for gluconeogenesis in the liver (Martin-Requero, et al., 1983; Patel, Lapointe, & Olson, 1984). Disruptions in propionyl-CoA metabolism in PA can trigger liver complications. In a retrospective study, elevated levels of α-fetoprotein were found in 8 out of 16 PA patients and increased with age. Significantly increased levels of α-fetoprotein were observed in patients with neonatal onset. Twenty-five percent of patients showed permanent and moderate increases in γ-glutamyl transferase, another sign of liver disease. Liver ultrasounds confirmed moderate hepatomegaly and hyperechoic liver in PA patients, which are common abnormalities in this condition (Imbard, et al., 2018).

Although the current mechanism explaining how PA can cause liver disease is unknown, it is believed that mitochondrial energy deficiency might play a pathological role (Imbard, et al., 2018). Research has shown that elevated levels of protein propionylation are linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to a reduction in respiration driven by complex I in patient-derived fibroblasts. Furthermore, exposure to propionate levels found in PA patients has been shown to cause increased protein propionylation and decreased mitochondrial respiration in Fao liver cells and cultured fibroblasts (Lagerwaard, et al., 2021). Stable isotope analysis and liver perfusion studies have demonstrated that high levels of propionate and its metabolites can inhibit TCA cycle flux and cause significant decreases in energy production in perfused rat livers. These findings shed light on the potential pathological mechanism underlying liver diseases associated with PA (Wilson, et al., 2017).

PA patients frequently experience ketoacidosis, which often results in misdiagnosis of diabetes prior to being diagnosed with PA (Dweikat, et al., 2011; Joshi & Phatarpekar, 2011). However, some patients with PA, including those with pancreatitis, have reported developing diabetes and receiving insulin treatment (Hwang, et al., 2021). Insulin-resistant hyperglycemia has also been observed in a neonatal infant with PA, who responded positively to decreased glucose administration (Filippi, et al., 2009). Risk factors for diabetes development in patients with PA include toxic intermediate accumulation in the gland, disruptions in energy homeostasis leading to impaired hormone synthesis, and defects in complex molecules (Alfadhel & Babiker, 2018).

In some cases, opposite findings have been reported with respect to glucose metabolism. For instance, a 34-year-old PA patient exhibited increased glucose metabolism and low work capacity during exercise (Storgaard, et al., 2020). While diabetic complications have not been confirmed in animal models of PA, additional research is necessary to establish a definitive link between PA and diabetes.

Pregnant patients with PA have an elevated risk of experiencing metabolic decompensation ascribed to the stress and increased protein turnover associated with pregnancy, delivery, and the postpartum period (Scott Schwoerer, van Calcar, Rice, & Deline, 2016). As such, it is crucial for the delivery of PA patient’s pregnancies to be closely monitored by an interdisciplinary medical team, including specialists in cardiology, genetics, and dietetics, to ensure that the patient does not undergo metabolic decompensation (Schmeck & Beilin, 2014). For PA patients who exhibit cardiac abnormalities, careful monitoring is particularly important, as cardiac output increases throughout pregnancy, putting these patients at even higher risk of cardiac decompensation (Scott Schwoerer, et al., 2016).

Breastfeeding can be highly advantageous for infants with PA, as it promotes immune health and reduces the production of propionate from the gut. However, on-demand feeding can put these infants at risk of metabolic decompensation if not closely monitored. For instance, a 14-month old PA patient experienced two metabolic episodes while breastfeeding on-demand, presenting with symptoms such as vomiting, mild acidosis, hypotonia, and mild encephalopathy. After the two metabolic decompensations, it was recommended that the infant stop breastfeeding on-demand (Huner, Baykal, Demir, & Demirkol, 2005).

PA patients are susceptible to skin abnormalities induced by dietary and vitamin deficiencies. Zinc supplementation improved skin lesions in a girl who had PA with necrolytic migratory erythema (Al-Rikabi & Al-Homsi, 2004). Conversely, protein restriction can cause generalized exfoliative eruption, which may improve with dietary liberation, as observed in a male infant (Lane, Spraker, & Parker, 2007). Vesiculobullous dermatitis has also been observed in a PA infant with a protein restrictive diet that is specifically limited in isoleucine and valine (De Raeve, De Meirleir, Ramet, Vandenplas, & Gerlo, 1994). Therefore, for patients experiencing skin abnormalities, it is important to inspect their diet and vitamin levels to alleviate symptoms.

Anesthesia has the potential to induce metabolic acidosis in patients with PA, which may be exacerbated by factors such as fasting, dehydration, hypoxemia, and hypotension. Anesthetics that can metabolize to propionic acid, including succinylcholine, benzylisoquinoline neuromuscular blocking agents, and propofol, should be avoided. In addition, a combination of local and general anesthesia without opiates is recommended to avoid respiratory depression, as PA patients are sensitive to opiates (Sanchez-Rodenas, et al., 2005). Furthermore, maintaining adequate tissue perfusion, avoiding lactic acid-containing fluids, and providing intravenous dextrose, l-carnitine, and bicarbonate can help prevent metabolic episodes during anesthesia. These patients are also prone to respiratory distress in the postoperative period due to fatigue or upper airway obstruction. To minimize airway complications, tracheal extubation should only be performed once the patient has regained strength. Additionally, postoperative humidified oxygen should be provided to prevent respiratory complications (Harker, Emhardt, & Hainline, 2000).

Due to decreased mitochondrial function, the use of local anesthesia in PA patients should be approached with caution as it can hinder mitochondrial energy production. However, ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve blocks were successfully administered to a 22-year-old female undergoing left foot surgery without any reported nerve injury. This patient had also received local anesthesia for wisdom teeth removal without complications. Nonetheless, careful monitoring should be conducted during and after the administration of local anesthesia to avoid any potential adverse effects (Soberon, Elliott, Bland, & Weinberg, 2014).

Apart from the specific pathological mechanisms discussed above that are associated with the complications of PA, various general mechanisms have been proposed through different experimental models.

Chapman et al. discovered variances in gene expression in lymphoblastoid cell lines from PA patients, impacting crucial pathways like energy metabolism genes (IDH2, NDUFAB1, DNUFB6, PDK3, and SLC25A1), stress response genes (EIF4A1, GADD45, and MAT2A), and immune genes (CTNND1, PTK2B, GRLF1, and ROCK2). These findings suggest potential players in the pathophysiology of PA (Chapman, Bush, & Zhang, 2015). Moreover, accumulation of MCA inhibits citrate synthase, aconitase, and isocitrate dehydrogenases, potentially leading to mitochondrial energy metabolism dysfunction (Cheema-Dhadli, Leznoff, & Halperin, 1975). PCC deficiency has also been shown to impact mitochondrial mass, oxidative phosphorylation complexes, and cytochrome oxidase activity (de Keyzer, et al., 2009; Gallego-Villar, et al., 2016; Hayasaka, Metoki, et al., 1982). Reduced mitochondrial energy metabolism is indicated by the decreased resting energy expenditure observed in PA patients (Feillet, Bodamer, Dixon, Sequeira, & Leonard, 2000). This is further supported by studies on C. elegans with PCCA-1 or PCCB-1 deletion, which exhibit attenuated mitochondrial energy metabolism (Chapman, et al., 2018).

Another common finding of PCC deficiency is increased oxidative stress in various experimental models. For instance, in the C. elegans model of PA, PCC deficiency led to increased oxidative stress and impaired energy metabolism (Chapman, et al., 2018). Similar conclusions were drawn in other models, such as increased levels of H2O2 and O2.− in a PA mouse model (Gallego-Villar, et al., 2016), elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in fibroblasts with PCC mutation (Gallego-Villar, et al., 2013), and increased levels of lipid peroxidation products and protein oxidation products in plasma and urine from PA patients (Mc Guire, Parikh, & Diaz, 2009; Salmi, Leonard, & Lapatto, 2012). These findings highlight the potential benefits of antioxidant therapy.

The elevated odd-chain fatty acids in lipids is due to propionyl-CoA accumulation in patients with PA. This accumulation can also lead to decreased or missing eicosanoids in PA skin fibroblasts, potentially affecting cell membrane function (Giudici, et al., 1986). Furthermore, prolonged fasting has been found to increase fatty acid oxidation including odd-chain fatty acids, resulting in increased propionyl-CoA and its metabolites (Thompson & Chalmers, 1990). In addition, propionylcarnitine secretion into urine can cause secondary carnitine deficiency in the tissues of patients with PA (Chalmers, Roe, Stacey, & Hoppel, 1984; Di Donato, Rimoldi, Garavaglia, & Uziel, 1984).

One of the biomarkers of PA is elevated glycine. This increase in glycine is due to an impaired glycine cleavage system (Hayasaka, Narisawa, et al., 1982). However, not all PA patients exhibit elevated glycine levels, and the consequences of increased glycine in some PA patients remain unclear.

The published guidelines for PA have helped establish common standards and improve outcomes for PA patients (Baumgartner, et al., 2014; Chapman, Gropman, et al., 2012; Sutton, et al., 2012). The typical recommendations for patients with PA are low-protein/medical diet, high carbohydrate, no long-term fasting, and supplementing with l-carnitine, sodium bicarbonate, sodium benzoate, metronidazole, laxatives, biotin, blood transfusions, platelets, blood growth factor, plasma, immunoglobulin, antibiotics, vasopressors, anticonvulsants, pro-motility agents, and insulin infusion to reduce propionyl-CoA production (Chiu, et al., 2014; Rafique, 2014; Ribas, et al., 2010; Subramanian, et al., 2023; Sutton, et al., 2012). Early detection and intervention can provide significant long-term benefits (Scott Schwoerer, Clowes Candadai, & Held, 2018) and non-compliance with dietary treatment can result in severe disease or even death for PA patients (Thomas, 1994). The following sections will discuss common treatments for PA.

Restricting protein intake can reduce the production of propionyl-CoA and alleviate metabolic stress in PA. Inadequate protein and energy intake may contribute to the manifestation of PA symptoms, and improving nutrition may be critical for better outcomes. A medical food called Propimex-1 Amino Acid-Modified with Iron, which is composed of a mixture of human milk and whole egg (excluding propiogenic amino acids) along with l-carnitine, carbohydrates, fats, minerals, and vitamins, has been shown to be beneficial to growth and nutrition status in PA patients over a 6-month period (Yannicelli, et al., 2003). Additionally, supplementation with leucine may facilitate catch-up growth in patients undergoing protein-restricted diets (Satoh, et al., 1983). Although strict protein restriction is the primary treatment for PA, in some cases, it may not be sufficient to resolve all complications. Late onset complications may still occur despite strict diet management (Sethi, et al., 1989).

Propionate, a product of the gut microbiome, contributes a substantial amount of propionyl-CoA in addition to propiogenic amino acids. Studies have shown that antibiotic treatment can decrease propionylcarnitine levels (Bordugo, et al., 2021). In healthy subjects, propionate from the gut microbiome was assessed after treatment with ampicillin, clindamycin, and metronidazole. Results showed that clindamycin and ampicillin were more effective antibiotics in inhibiting propionate production (Prasad, Nurko, Borovoy, & Korson, 2004).

Carnitine acetyltransferase exhibits higher specificity towards propionyl-CoA than acetyl-CoA as a substrate. Propionyl-CoA can be effectively reduced by l-carnitine, while the D-isomer of carnitine can have adverse effects in clinical applications (Fujisawa, Shimatani, Yamada, & Hironaka, 1989; Kurczynski, Hoppel, Goldblatt, & Gunning, 1989). Administration of L-carnitine can alleviate the symptoms of PA (Roe, Millington, Maltby, Bohan, & Hoppel, 1984).

Propionyl-CoA accumulation leads to mitochondrial metabolic stress and impairs energy metabolism by depleting CoA levels. Allosteric activators of PANK, such as PZ-3022 and BBP-671, were found to increase tissue CoA levels and improve mitochondrial function by normalizing the intracellular propionyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA (Subramanian, et al., 2023). In addition, HST5040 was found to decrease propionyl-CoA levels in a dose-dependent manner by forming HST5040-CoA in primary hepatocytes and redistributing free and conjugated CoA pools (Armstrong, et al., 2021).

The high levels of propionyl-CoA are reported to inhibit N-acetyl glutamate synthase (NAGS) and carbamoylphosphate synthase (CPS), which disrupts urea synthesis and leads to hyperammonemia (Coude, Sweetman, & Nyhan, 1979). Elevated levels of MCA can also impair glutamine synthase function, which is essential for ammonia detoxification, and can result in increased ammonia levels and decreased glutamine concentrations. Allosteric activators of CPS, such as carglumic acid (Carbaglu) and N-acetylglutamate, have been found to reduce the number of emergency room admissions by enhancing ureagenesis and lowering plasma ammonia levels when used in combination with standard treatment over the long term with no reported side effects (Abacan & Boneh, 2013; Ah Mew, et al., 2010; Alfadhel, et al., 2021; Fernandez de Miguel, Gimeno Diaz de Atauri, Torres Peral, Fernandez Carrion, & Serrano Ayestaran, 2009; Filippi, et al., 2010; Kido, Matsumoto, & Nakamura, 2020; Kiykim, et al., 2021; Schwahn, Pieterse, Bisset, Galloway, & Robinson, 2010; Soyucen, Demirci, & Aydin, 2010; Tuchman, et al., 2008; Tummolo, et al., 2018; Valayannopoulos, et al., 2016). In the case of acute hyperammonemia in newborns, carbaglu should be administered to remove the toxic ammonia and improve long-term outcomes (Schwahn, et al., 2010). In addition, hemodialysis and sodium phenylacetate are also proved effective in reducing serum ammonia concentrations (Petrowski, et al., 1987).

An intestinal motility agent has been tested as a means of preventing the reabsorption of propionyl-CoA metabolites and increasing their secretion. In a clinical case study involving four patients with PA, the administration of the intestinal motility agent for seven days resulted in a significant decrease in PA biomarkers (Prasad, et al., 2004).

The reduced levels of CoQ10 in plasma has been observed in some patients with PA. To address this, ubiquinol supplementation was tested and found to be well-tolerated at 10 mg/kg/day. This supplementation restored CoQ10 levels and led to a significant reduction in the severity of PA. Given that 3HPA, another metabolite of propionyl-CoA, was found to be doubled after six months of ubiquinol administration, further investigation is necessary to determine the efficacy of ubiquinol treatment for PA (Stanescu, et al., 2022).

PCC is an enzyme that relies on biotin for its function. Mutations in PCC can affect the binding of biotin, as seen in some PA patients who respond positively to biotin supplementation (Barnes, Hull, Balgobin, & Gompertz, 1970; Bartlett & Gompertz, 1976; Hillman, Keating, & Williams, 1978). However, it should be noted that not all PA patients respond to biotin supplementation (Sethi, et al., 1989).

Oxidative stress is induced by the deficiency of PCC. To reduce the resulting damage in PA, antioxidants have been shown to be effective. For instance, tiron, trolox, resveratrol, and MitoQ have been found to significantly reduce ROS content in the fibroblasts of both PA patients and controls (Gallego-Villar, Perez, Ugarte, Desviat, & Richard, 2014). Studies have demonstrated that bee pollen can protect against oxidative damage and reactive oxygen species induced by propionate in rats (Al-Salem, et al., 2020). A similar study demonstrated that MitoQ and resveratrol, a natural polyphenol, improved cardiac hypertrophy and decreased oxidative stress in the hearts of PA mice (Rivera-Barahona, Alonso-Barroso, et al., 2017).

PCC deficiency reduces the anaplerotic contribution of propionyl-CoA. Increasing anaplerotic flux from other substrates has been proposed as a potential therapy. Citrate, but not 2-ketoglutarate, succinate, or fumarate, has been shown to reduce hospitalizations in PA patients by increasing anaplerosis (Longo, et al., 2017). The therapeutic effect of citrate has been confirmed in another case where oral administration of citrate prevented severe disturbances in energy metabolism (Siekmeyer, et al., 2013).

PA progressively develops various complications without curative treatment. LT may be considered a viable long-term treatment option for patients with PA who experience progressive complications that are unresponsive to medical therapy (Arrizza, et al., 2015). LT has been shown to result in clinical improvements in patients with PA (Barshes, et al., 2006; Berry, et al., 2020; Nagao, et al., 2013). To completely evaluate the effectiveness and safety of LT, it will be necessary to have additional experience and a meticulous long-term monitoring of transplanted patients (Chapman, Summar, & Enns, 2012).

Many types of LT for PA have been reported including living-donor LT (Yorifuji, et al., 2004; Zeng, et al., 2022), auxiliary LT (Meyburg & Hoffmann, 2005), and auxiliary partial orthotopic liver transplantation (Shankar, et al., 2019; Shanmugam, et al., 2019). Living-donor LT is another option when liver transplantation is required. A portion of the recipient’s liver is retained to aid the new graft in case of graft dysfunction. The portal steal could potentially result in graft atrophy in the long run, which limits its clinical application (Shankar, et al., 2019; Shanmugam, et al., 2019). Domino liver transplant is an emerging liver transplantation when the available grafts are limited. However, domino liver transplant carries the risk of introducing new diseases to the recipient (Moguilevitch & Delphin, 2018).

There are several important questions that require further investigation regarding LT as a treatment option for PA: (1) long-term management of various complications induced by the transplanted liver or graft; (2) whether diet restrictions are still required after LT (Critelli, et al., 2018); (3) chronic development of other complications since the deficiency of PCC in other tissues has not been resolved (Chu, et al., 2019; Vara, et al., 2011; Yorifuji, et al., 2004); (4) whether LT results in significant changes in some PA biomarkers including 3HPA and citrate remaining elevated in plasma and urine, despite most LT leading to reduced metabolic decomposition (Berry, et al., 2020; Kasahara, et al., 2012; Quintero, et al., 2018; Rela, et al., 2007; Silva, et al., 2017; Yorifuji, et al., 2000; Zeng, et al., 2022).

The use of gene therapy shows great promise as a potential treatment for PA. The ultimate objective of gene therapy is to address these genetic mutations and provide a long-term solution for PA. A mouse model of PA was created by Barry and colleagues, who introduced a mutant human PCCA A138T allele into PCC-knockout mice (Guenzel, et al., 2013). AAV8 or AAVh10 vectors expressing PCCA can lower the metabolites of propionyl-CoA in PCCA−/−(A138T) mice by a single intravenous injection. Nevertheless, the efficacy of expression was found to be diminished in females relative to males. Whether the sex-specific effects of AAV are present in human remains to be established (Guenzel, Collard, Kraus, Matern, & Barry, 2015). Gene therapy using AAV8-TTR-PCCA (liver) demonstrated superior effect compared to AAV1-MCK-PCCA (muscle), suggesting that it could be a promising option to LT (Chandler, et al., 2011; Guenzel, Hillestad, Matern, & Barry, 2014; Hofherr, et al., 2009). In addition, liver-specific transgenic expression of Pcca prevented the lethality of Pcca−/− mice and enabled normal growth (Miyazaki, et al., 2001).

Improved propionate metabolism was found with the injection of human PCCB cDNA into the nucleus or cytoplasm of fibroblasts from patients with PCCB defects. The results suggest that the full-length wild-type protein can correct the defect caused by the mutant protein in PA and that the mutant protein does not exert dominant negative effects on the wild-type transgene (Lamhonwah, Leclerc, Loyer, Clarizio, & Gravel, 1994).

Aminoglycosides and analogs could be potentially used for treating PA patients with nonsense mutations by promoting premature termination codon readthrough (Sanchez-Alcudia, Perez, Ugarte, & Desviat, 2012). Another promising therapy is the antisense morpholino oligonucleotides for PA patients with splicing mutations (Rincon, et al., 2007).

In a recent study, an enzyme replacement approach was presented for the treatment of PA using biodegradable lipid nanoparticles to deliver mRNAs of PCCA and PCCB. This approach successfully produced normal PCC protein and improved metabolic alterations without adverse effects for a 3- and 6-month repeat administrations. The study demonstrates the potential therapy with mRNA technique for PA and other genetic diseases (L. Jiang, et al., 2020). Furthermore, cell-penetrating peptides could replace the mutated enzyme by delivering normal PCC to mitochondria (Collard, Majtan, Park, & Kraus, 2018; Darvish-Damavandi, Ho, & Kang, 2016).

For carriers of PA, the likelihood of newborns with mutated PCC is increased. Prenatal testing provides families with information that can help decide whether to terminate their pregnancy or prepare for prompt care after birth. To identify PA during pregnancies, three different diagnostic methods can be utilized, including the identification of PA metabolic biomarkers in amniotic fluid, enzymatic activity assay in available cells, or DNA mutation analysis from fetal tissue (Bustamante-Aragones, et al., 2008; Perez-Cerda, et al., 2004).

Genetic screening of embryos prior to implantation provides a feasible alternative. A couple who were carriers for PA underwent preimplantation genetic diagnosis and successfully gave birth to healthy, unaffected twins, after their 2-month-old infant diet due to PA (Alberola, et al., 2011).

Total parenteral nutrition can be beneficial in improving nutrition during prolonged periods of poor metabolic control in patients with PA. Total parenteral nutrition can shift the body to an anabolic state, leading to a reduction in lipid breakdown and propionate production generated by anaerobic fermentation of ingested carbohydrates in the gut (Walter, Leonard, Thompson, & Halliday, 1990). In some cases, exchange transfusion and ventilatory support have been used to sustain life during the neonatal period (Figueras Aloy, et al., 1988). However, in other cases, despite two double-volume exchange transfusions, there was no significant effect on the patient’s condition.

PA is a metabolic disorder resulting from an inborn error, but early diagnosis made possible through advanced technology facilitates preventive treatments. These treatments include a protein-restricted diet, l-carnitine supplements, antibiotics, antioxidants, Carbaglu, and CoQ10, which slow disease progression and decrease the number of metabolic decompensation episodes, leading to improved health in patients with PA. Despite these benefits, there are still challenges in understanding the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of PA and developing new, effective treatment strategies for the disorder.

The enzyme PCC is crucial in the functioning of mitochondria, and its deficiency can cause metabolic disturbances primarily in this organelle. While energy metabolic inefficiency in the mitochondria has been suggested as a contributing factor to various complications associated with PA, it remains unclear whether it is the primary etiological factor. In vitro experimental models have proposed various mechanisms by which propionyl-CoA and its metabolites could interfere with mitochondrial function. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether these changes take place in vivo, and the therapeutic targets for most complications associated with PA are not well established. An exception is the use of Carbaglu, which targets N-acetyl glutamate synthase (NAGS) and helps alleviate hyperammonemia resulting from high levels of propionyl-CoA and propionyl glutamate in PA patients. Nonetheless, challenges remain in understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms of PA and developing new and effective treatment strategies for the disease.

While a protein-restricted diet and liver transplant can effectively prevent the progression of the disease, they do not fully address the issue of low PCC activity in tissues or extrahepatic tissues. As a result, late-onset (chronic) diseases may still develop even after undergoing dietary interventions or liver transplant. Based on the current progress made in PA research, the following research directions warrant greater attention in the future (see summary in Figure 2).

Further research is needed to understand how accumulation of propionyl-CoA and its metabolites affect mitochondrial energy metabolism and identify which mitochondrial dehydrogenases or respiratory chain complexes are inhibited by PCC deficiency. Additionally, exploring whether CoA trapping mediated by propionyl-CoA causes dysregulation of fatty acid metabolism is necessary. Further investigation is needed to examine the impact of elevated levels of odd-chain fatty acids in lipids on their signaling and biological functions.

Despite the association between oxidative stress and PA, the relationship between PCC deficiency and oxidative stress remains incompletely understood. Hence, additional research is required to elucidate the mechanisms underlying how PCC deficiency triggers oxidative stress.

While a low-protein diet and liver transplant may slow the development of heart complications in PA, they cannot fully halt its progression. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms of heart diseases in PA is crucial, as well as for other potential complications.

Recent research suggests that allosteric activators of pantothenate kinase activators could be a promising therapeutic approach for PA by increasing CoA synthesis and decreasing propionyl-CoA. Similar drug therapies could be developed by targeting PCC activation and propionyl-CoA synthesis and metabolism.

Personalized treatment strategies for PA based on genotype and other modifying factors are necessary. It is also essential to investigate which genetic, epigenetic, or environmental factors may modify the disease phenotype.

Gene therapy is an encouraging therapeutic solution for PA. However, its current tools face various challenges, such as controlled tissue expression, lasting transgene expression, and potential unknown side effects, which need to be addressed before it can become a widespread clinical practice.
