# Hypotension, unspecified: Uncharted Mortality Trends and Disparities in the United States, A CDC WONDER Analysis (1999-2025)
**Authors:** Palwasha Asghar, Muhammad Bilal Masood, Sahla Waqas, Wajeeha Iftikhar Shah, Fatima Fazal, Raghabendra Kumar Mahato
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-9541628/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9541628/v1)

## Content

Abstract
Introduction:
 Hypotension of unspecified etiology is life-threatening, with an annual prevalence 9% and up to 75% in severe cases. Delayed treatment may cause cardiogenic or septic shock with high mortality. This study examines racial and geographic disparities in adult mortality.
Methods
 CDC WONDER data (1999–2025) were used to calculate age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) per 100,000 adults ≥ 25 years. Trends and annual percent change (APC) were analyzed using joinpoint regression.
Results
 Overall, 305,933 deaths occurred (AAMR 5.7). Rates rose from 5.7(1999) to 6.2(2006; APC 1.50). Declined to 4.1 (2018; APC – 3.10), increased to 4.8 (2021; APC 5.35), then fell to 4.2(2025; APC-3.10). Adults ≥ 85 years had the highest mortality (59.4). Mortality was higher in men than women (men: 4.9 per 100,000 [95% CI: 4.8-5.0] vs. women: 3.7 per 100,000 [95% CI: 3.6–3.8] in 2025). The gender gap persisted throughout the study period, with men showing a sharper rise during 2018–2021 (APC 5.89% vs. 4.63% in women), NH Blacks (8.1), urban Southern residents, especially South Caroline (8.3).
Conclusion
 Hypotension-associated mortality disproportionately affects NH Black men in urban Southern areas, particularly those aged ≥ 85 years. Targeted interventions addressing medication safety in long-term care facilities and improved cardiovascular care access in rural Southern communities are warranted.
Limitations:
 ICD-10 code I95.9 may represent a terminal physiological state rather than a primary disease entity.

• We identify that while overall mortality declined from 2006-2018, a sharp rise occurred during 2018-2021, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic.

Hypotension is defined as abnormally low blood pressure, which is systolic blood pressure < 90mmHg or diastolic pressure<60mmHg. The etiology is not very well known, and it can be asymptomatic or symptomatic, like dizziness, lightheadedness, syncope, nausea, and exhaustion, etc.(1)(2). Approximately five million patients with life-threatening illnesses are admitted to the ICU or ER in the United States each year(3)(4). The pathophysiological mechanisms by which hypotension leads to mortality involve inadequate end-organ perfusion. When mean arterial pressure falls below the autoregulatory threshold of vital organs (typically 60–65 mmHg), compensatory mechanisms, including sympathetic activation and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system stimulation, become overwhelmed. Prolonged hypoperfusion results in ischemic injury to the brain, heart, and kidneys, potentially precipitating multi-organ dysfunction syndrome. In severe cases, this cascade can progress to cardiogenic or septic shock, with mortality rates of 35–56% and 40–60%, respectively (5,6). Most of these patients suffer significant hemodynamic instability using a 65mmHg absolute MAP threshold. The prevalence of hypotension was 9%, and 75% experienced severe hypotension. These differences are statistically significant (4). Additionally, hypotension must be diagnosed and treated early since shock can become severe if it occurs, and delayed treatment is linked to a higher mortality rate that is 35–56% for cardiogenic shock and 40–60% for shock associated with sepsis (5) (6). Importantly, ICD-10 code I95.9 (hypotension, unspecified) is non-specific and may represent a terminal physiological state rather than a primary disease entity. This limitation should be taken into account when interpreting mortality trends. It has been demonstrated that demographic, age, gender, race, and lifestyle show a significant association between hypotension and mortality risk, as age is highly interactive with hypotension, seen more commonly in elderly people of 90 + year lower systolic blood pressure as compared to people in their 60 years(7) and young female are more likely present with hypotension as compare to male(7) (1)

Considering other factors associated with hypotension and mortality, such as acute kidney injury, cardiovascular problems, obesity, and ICU admission, will show a significant increase in mortality both intraoperatively and postoperatively (8) (9). Understanding the effects of these variables on mortality trends is key to guiding targeted interventions. Despite advances in medical interventions and prevention strategies, the burden of hypotension-related death remains high, highlighting the need for a detailed understanding of its epidemiological trends (5). Recent decades have seen significant shifts in the epidemiology of hypotension, with rising prevalence attributed to aging populations, improved detection, and growing awareness of the condition. Moreover, advancements in treatments have transformed the management. (10) However, the extent to which these developments have translated into reduced mortality from hypotension remains unclear.

Previous epidemiological studies have established associations between hypotension and adverse outcomes in specific clinical contexts, such as intraoperative hypotension (9) and ICU-related hypotension (8). However, population-level mortality trends for unspecified hypotension remain unexplored. The extent to which demographic factors, geographic location, and urbanization influence hypotension-associated mortality has not been systematically characterized. This study addresses these gaps by: (1) analyzing 26-year national mortality trends for unspecified hypotension, (2) quantifying disparities across age, sex, race, and geography, and (3) identifying temporal patterns coinciding with major healthcare events, including the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, this study aims to analyze national data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database to evaluate overall mortality trends associated with unspecified hypotension and provide insights into geographic, racial, and sex-based disparities. A clear understanding of these trends is essential for identifying high-risk populations and guiding targeted preventive interventions.

In this study, we used detailed death certificate records from the CDC-WONDER (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiological Research) Multiple Cause of Death database to analyze significant trends and patterns in mortality related to unspecified hypotension among adults aged ≥ 25 years from 1999 to 2025. The International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes I95.9 were used to identify cases, regardless of whether they were listed as contributing or underlying causes of death. The data uses death certificates of US residents in all states and the District of Columbia(11). The age cutoff of ≥ 25 years was selected to focus on adult populations and reduce variability from pediatric cases, a standard approach in CDC WONDER-based mortality studies that improves comparability across long-term trend analyses (51). The STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines were adhered to(12), and the study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval due to the anonymity of data.

The retrieved data included overall mortality rate, states, place of death, census regions, and urban/rural classifications. Gender (male, female), race/ethnicity (White, Black or African American, Asian or Pacific Islander, Hispanic or Latino), and age categories (divided into ten-year intervals) were all considered. The census regions were grouped as Northeast, Midwest, South, and West in accordance with the US Census Bureau rules. Moreover, regions were classified as urban (large metropolitan ≥ 1 million, medium/small metropolitan 50,000-999,999) or rural (< 50,000) using the National Center for Health Statistics method. (13) Our analysis extends to 2025. However, urbanization data is only available until 2020 due to limitations in the CDC dataset.

Our analysis focuses on death rate by overall year, sex, race/ethnicity,2013 urbanization, census, states, and place of death. Trends and annual percent changes (APCs) were analyzed using the Joinpoint Regression Program version 5.0 (14). Maximum 5 joinpoints were allowed, with model selection using Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Permutation tests with 4,499 random permutations were used to identify significant inflection points (α = 0.05). Average annual percent changes (AAPCs) were calculated for the overall period. An APC or AAPC was considered significantly different from zero if the 95% confidence interval did not include zero. Black people's death rates were compared with those of Hispanic/Latino, non–Hispanic American/Alaska Native, Asian or Pacific, and white people using age-standardized death rates from 1999 to 2025(15), and the same trend has been observed for urbanization and age(25–85 + year) (16)(17). A p-value of less than 0.05 was statistically significant, which shows a separate linear trend for the segment before and after the inflection, indicating a period at which a trend transforms. The proportion of deaths attributable to hypotension was calculated with the Monte Carlo permutation test with 4499 randomly permuted data sets(18). AAPCs are regarded as significantly different from 0 at the α = 0.05 level; if 0 is within the 95% CIs, AAPCs are considered a constant trend; if both 95% CI limits are positive or negative, AAPCs are increasing or decreasing trends. (19)

From 1999 to 2025, 305,933 deaths were associated with unspecified hypotension among U.S. adults aged ≥ 25 years. The highest number of deaths occurred in medical facilities (69.02%), followed by nursing homes/long-term care facilities (21.47%). Mortality was highest among males, adults aged ≥ 85 years, Non-Hispanic Black individuals, and residents of rural areas in the Southern United States.

The age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) was 5.7 per 100,000 in 1999. It increased to a peak of 6.2 in 2006 (annual percent change [APC] + 1.50, p < 0.05), then declined steadily to 4.1 by 2018 (APC − 3.87, p < 0.05). A sharp rise occurred during 2018–2021, reaching 4.8 (APC + 5.35, p < 0.05), followed by a decline to 4.2 by 2025 (APC − 3.10, p < 0.05) (Fig.1a, Table 1).

Men consistently had higher AAMRs than women (2025 rates: 4.9 vs. 3.7 per 100,000). Both sexes followed similar temporal patterns: an initial increase to 2006 (men: APC + 1.02; women: APC + 0.94, both p < 0.05), a decline through 2018 (men: APC − 3.81; women: APC − 3.67), a sharp rise during 2018–2021 (men: APC + 5.89; women: APC + 4.63), and a subsequent decline through 2025 (men: APC − 3.26; women: APC − 2.80). The gender gap remained stable over the study period (Fig.1b, Table 1).

Crude mortality rates increased progressively with age, from 0.2 per 100,000 in adults aged 25–34 years to 59.4 in those ≥ 85 years (Table 2). For adults aged 55–64 years, mortality declined from 2006 to 2018 (APC − 4.1, p < 0.05) and then rose sharply during 2018–2021 (APC + 7.5, p < 0.01). The 65–74 year age group showed a significant decline from 2006 to 2017 (APC − 4.0, p < 0.05) and again from 2021 to 2025 (APC − 4.1, p < 0.01). Among the oldest adults (≥ 85 years), mortality increased from 1999 to 2005 (APC + 2.7, p < 0.01), declined through 2018 (APC − 3.4, p < 0.001), then showed a modest but significant increase from 2018 to 2025 (APC + 1.5, p < 0.05). Other age groups demonstrated stable or non-significant trends (Fig.2, Table 2).

Non-Hispanic Black individuals had the highest AAMR (8.1 per 100,000), followed by Non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native (6.3), Hispanic/Latino (5.1), Non-Hispanic White (4.8), and Non-Hispanic Asian/Pacific Islander (4.0). Non-Hispanic Black mortality declined significantly from 2005 to 2016 (APC − 5.1, p < 0.05) and then stabilized. Non-Hispanic White mortality followed the overall U.S. pattern: increase to 2005, decline to 2018, sharp rise 2018–2021, then decline through 2025. Hispanic/Latino mortality showed a significant decline from 2013 to 2016 (APC − 10.6, p < 0.05), followed by a rise during 2018–2021 (APC + 6.5, p < 0.05) and a subsequent decline (APC − 4.9, p < 0.05). Asian/Pacific Islander populations demonstrated the steepest long-term decline from 1999 to 2019 (APC − 4.7, p < 0.05). Non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native mortality showed no significant change over the study period (Fig.3, Table 3).

Substantial geographic variation was observed. The highest AAMR was in South Carolina (8.3 per 100,000), and the lowest was in Minnesota (2.8). States in the top 90th percentile included South Carolina, Texas, Mississippi, Alabama, West Virginia, and Hawaii; those in the bottom 10th percentile included Minnesota, Montana, Colorado, Maine, and New Mexico. The gap between the highest and lowest states was approximately 2–3 fold (Fig.4a,4b, Table 4).

The Southern region consistently had the highest AAMRs. It followed the national trend: increase to 2008, decline to 2018 (APC − 3.9, p < 0.05), increase to 2021 (APC + 5.0, p < 0.05), then decline to 4.5 by 2025 (APC − 4.1, p < 0.05). The Midwest increased to 2005 (APC + 3.2, p < 0.05), then declined through 2017 (APC − 4.1, p < 0.05), with stable rates thereafter. The Northeast declined from 2004 to 2012 (APC − 2.7, p < 0.05) and again from 2012 to 2016 (APC − 6.2, p < 0.05), followed by a non-significant upward trend. The Western region had the lowest AAMRs overall (range 3.5–5.9) with gradual fluctuations (Fig.5, Table 5).

In 1999, metropolitan areas had slightly higher AAMRs than non-metropolitan areas (5.9 vs. 5.1). By 2020, this pattern reversed, with rural areas having higher rates (5.1 vs. 4.2). From 1999 to 2006, urban areas increased modestly (APC + 1.0, p < 0.05), while rural areas increased more sharply from 1999 to 2003 (APC + 4.2, p < 0.05) before stabilizing. During the decline from 2006 to 2018, urban areas declined faster (APC − 4.0%) than rural areas (APC − 3.1%). The 2018–2020 period saw a sharp rise in rural areas (APC + 6.2, p < 0.05), while the urban increase was non-significant (Fig.6, Table 6).

From 1999 to 2025, the majority of deaths occurred in medical facilities (245,114 deaths; 69.02%). Nursing homes or long-term care facilities accounted for 21.47% of deaths, followed by decedents’ homes (6.83%) and hospice facilities (2.68%). Although medical facilities remained the predominant place of death, recent years showed a gradual shift toward increased deaths at home and in hospice settings (Fig.7, Fig.8, Table 7).

Our study highlights key trends in hypotension-associated mortality among US adults. A total of 305,933 such deaths occurred over the study period. The AAMR rose from 1999 to 2006, then steadily declined for over a decade until a rapid surge in 2018–2021, followed by a decline. Significant disparities affected males, adults aged ≥ 85 years (59.4 per 100,000), NH Black populations (AAMR 8.1), Southern census region residents, and non-metropolitan dwellers. Medical facilities dominated as the place of death (69.02%), though home and hospice deaths have risen recently.

The early rise from 1999 to 2006 aligned with increased antihypertensive use in an aging population. Guidelines then promoted aggressive blood pressure targets, boosting diuretics and multi-drug regimens, which likely raised iatrogenic hypotensive events in vulnerable patients (20–22). The 2006–2018 decline likely stemmed from: (1) updated guidelines favoring individualized targets, (2) greater orthostatic hypotension awareness in geriatrics, (3) better recognition of medication risks, and (4) evidence-based protocols for long-term care blood pressure management (23,24).

Mortality rose sharply with age; adults ≥ 85 years faced crude rates nearly 300 times higher than those aged 25–34 years. The notable 2018–2025 increase in this group, despite stability elsewhere, merits attention. Geriatric susceptibility arises via multiple mechanisms (25). Age-related changes impair BP regulation (26): blunted baroreflex sensitivity hinders heart rate and vascular tone adjustments upon standing, promoting orthostatic hypotension. Autonomic dysfunction from diabetes or Parkinson’s limits sympathetic vasoconstriction during postural shifts or stress (27). Reduced renin–aldosterone activity causes salt/water wasting and plasma volume contraction, dropping BP (28). Arterial stiffness and diastolic dysfunction reduce cardiovascular adaptability, precipitating syncope, falls, and decline in frail elders (29). Saz-Lara et al.'s analysis of 11 studies (10,611 adults) linked increased arterial stiffness to 40% higher orthostatic hypotension risk, with central aortic stiffness strongest (50% higher) (30). Polypharmacy with α-blockers, diuretics, levodopa, opioids, benzodiazepines, or antipsychotics further precipitates hypotension (31).

Male predominance persisted (men 4.9 vs. women 3.7 per 100,000 in 2025), echoing CDC WONDER data on geriatric orthostatic hypotension (32). Factors include biological sex effects on cardiac structure, signaling, and vascular responses; women's cardiovascular survival advantage wanes post-menopause due to estrogen's role (33). Men face more psychosocial stressors, loneliness, alcohol/cigarette use, financial burdens, social isolation, obesity, and fatty diets, fostering oxidative stress and vascular impairment (34–36). Gender differences also affect antihypertensive responses, side effects, and dosing—women often receive lower doses, potentially reducing iatrogenic risk (37,38).

Hypothesized Mechanisms: Racial Differences

NH Black individuals had the highest AAMR, nearly double NH Asian/Pacific Islanders', mirroring cardiovascular mortality patterns (39). Socioeconomic and biological factors contribute. Giza et al. found blunted renin-angiotensin responses in Blacks versus non-Hispanic whites during standing (13% vs. 295% renin rise; suppressed aldosterone), possibly from renal sodium retention (40). Salt restriction may benefit these patients with refractory supine hypertension (41,42). Healthcare access disparities leave more untreated cases, elevating mortality (43).

The South showed highest mortality, especially rural areas, aligning with elevated diabetes/obesity prevalence (44,45) and poverty/low insurance (46). Urban-rural reversal—from metropolitan dominance in 1999 to non-metropolitan lead by 2020—stems from demographic differences, rural education gaps, few cardiac specialists, and limited outreach (47).Most deaths in medical facilities suggest patients reach care yet succumb, possibly from over-medication in hypertensives, urging cautious management (48).

Hypothesized Mechanisms: Pandemic-Related Effects

The 2018–2021 surge temporally aligns with COVID-19, implying links to the virus, healthcare overload, or both. Koudelka et al. noted five frail elders developing post-COVID hypotension despite stable meds, possibly from SARS-CoV-2 degrading ACE2 and amplifying ACE inhibitor/ARB effects; hospitalization, immobility, and malnutrition worsened sarcopenia and vascular tone (49). Prolonged ICU stays heightened risk; Schuurmans et al.'s meta-analysis linked ICU hypotension to higher mortality (OR 1.45; 95% CI 1.12–1.88), severity-dependent (50). Post-2021 decline reflects better pandemic control and healthcare stabilization.

Our study provides a deep analysis of changes occurring in hypotension-associated mortality among U.S. adults over time, by using CDC WONDER data. Reliable detection of trends is ensured by the use of AAMRs, jointpoint modeling, and stratification of data according to age, gender, race, and region. Limitations are still present in this analysis due to the specific nature of CDC data, such that death certificates do not distinguish hypotension as a causative or contributory factor in mortality. A sensitivity analysis comparing underlying cause-only versus any-mention mortality was not performed because I95.9 is rarely coded as the underlying cause of death, resulting in unstable rates that would preclude reliable trend analysis. The dataset also lacks individual-level variables such as specific etiologies of hypotension (septic, cardiogenic, neurogenic, medication-induced), comorbid conditions beyond those coded, medication data, laboratory values, disease severity, healthcare access, and socioeconomic factors, which could change the interpretation. Moreover, variables that had unreliable values in any year have been removed, which therefore limits our analysis. Despite these limitations, CDC WONDER provides the largest, most representative sample of U.S. mortality data, enabling trend analysis over 26 years.

Our study shows that despite decreasing mortality rates in the past, hypotension-related mortality rose in this decade, and the increased number of deaths cannot be ignored. First, persistent demographic and geographic disparities need targeted interventions. Efforts should focus on improving the prevention and management of high-risk populations. Long-term nursing facilities especially need a careful protocol for administering anti-hypertensive medications (52). Telemedicine and outreach clinicals need to be expanded (53), and there should be equity and fairness in the provision of healthcare facilities to all areas and ethnic groups.

Mortality associated with unspecified hypotension increased notably during 2018–2021, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic. Older adults (≥ 85 years), males, rural populations, and Non-Hispanic Black residents of the Southern United States face disproportionately high mortality rates. These findings support targeted interventions including medication safety protocols in long-term care facilities, expanded telemedicine access in rural areas, and culturally tailored cardiovascular care for high-risk racial groups.
