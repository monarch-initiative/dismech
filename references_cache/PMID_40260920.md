Osteoglophonic dysplasia (OGD) is a rare skeletal disorder caused by certain 
variants in FGFR1. The FGFR1 gene encodes a receptor vital for osteogenesis in 
the axial and craniofacial skeleton. Key OGD features include craniosynostosis, 
craniofacial dysmorphism, impacted teeth, rhizomelic shortening, and 
nonossifying fibromas. Patients may have hypophosphatemia due to high FGF23 
levels. We report two OGD patients with the c.1141T > C FGFR1 variant 
[p.(Cys381Arg)], initially diagnosed with Pfeiffer syndrome. Both showed classic 
symptoms as well as signs not previously reported, including elevated frontal 
temperature and overlapping toes. This report emphasizes distinguishing OGD from 
similar disorders and expanding the clinical phenotype.

Osteoglophonic dysplasia (OGD; MIM #166250) is a rare autosomal dominant skeletal disorder, with only 22 known patients (from 17 different families) reported in the scientific literature, 12 of whom have molecular confirmation (Holder et al., 2017; Kelley et al., 1983; Marzin et al., 2020; Santos et al., 1988; Zou et al., 2022). The name “osteoglophonic,” meaning “hollowed out” in Greek, reflects the characteristic radiolucent lesions resulting from non-ossifying fibromas. The disorder was first described by Sir Thomas Fairbank in 1951 as “acrocephaly with abnormalities of the extremities” and renamed “craniofacial dysostosis with fibrous metaphyseal defects” by Theodore E. Keats in 1975. Later, Peter Beighton formally termed it “osteoglophonic dysplasia” in the 1980s (Beighton, 1989; Beighton et al., 1980; Fairbank, 1951; Keats et al., 1975). There are 5 distinct known causative FGFR1 variants associated with OGD (Table 1)

Classic features of OGD include multi-suture craniosynostosis with related craniofacial features (prominent forehead, proptosis, widely spaced eyes, low-set ears, midface retrusion, short nose, and prognathism), impacted teeth, and rhizomelic limb shortening. Radiographic findings typically show multiple lytic lesions in long bones, corresponding to non-ossifying fibromas (Othman et al., 2024).

Here, we describe two previously unreported, unrelated pediatric patients with OGD who were initially given a diagnosis of Pfeiffer syndrome, an allelic but distinct syndromic craniosynostosis disorder caused by other gain-of-function variants in FGFR1. Both patients presented with the classic clinical and radiological characteristics of the syndrome. They also exhibited unique findings, including elevated frontal temperature, heat sensitivity, and excessive sweating. Physical examination revealed a common bilateral toe positioning, where the second and fourth toes overlap or underride the third. These features are novel to this disorder and have not been previously documented in the literature. This report aims to highlight the importance of distinguishing OGD from allelic disorders (Pfeiffer syndrome and others) and expand upon the previously reported clinical phenotype of OGD, including possible genotype-phenotype correlations.

Patient 1 is an 8-year-old boy from Brazil. He was born at 38 weeks of gestation via Cesarean section with a birth weight of 3 kg. Prenatal ultrasounds revealed hypomineralization of the skull, raising suspicion of osteogenesis imperfecta. Postnatally, he spent 15 days in the NICU for workup, including head imaging, which identified multi-suture craniosynostosis. He traveled to the United States (US) for surgical management of craniosynostosis around the age of 1 year. During his first year of life, he had global developmental delays. He was unable to sit, crawl, or say any words. He snored at night with difficulty breathing, which worsened with any upper respiratory tract infection. He also had feeding problems with pharyngeal muscle weakness, leading to the inability to tolerate solid food. His diet consisted solely of milk for the first year of life. At age 1 year, he underwent cranioplasty with anterior subtotal cranial vault remodeling and posterior split skull cranioplasty resulting in significant improvement in development within 2 months post procedure. At the age of 4 years, a brain MRI showed changes in the optic nerve concerning for increased intracranial pressure. He underwent a second cranioplasty with posterior cranial vault remodeling with a successful outcome. He was initially diagnosed with Pfeiffer syndrome due to multi-suture craniosynostosis and an identified FGFR1 variant, despite the fact his genetic testing identified the heterozygous pathogenic variant c.1141T>C, p.(Cys381Arg) in exon 9 of the FGFR1 gene (NM_023110.3). The genetic variant was confirmed on testing performed both in labs in Brazil and the US and has been reported previously to be associated with OGD (Table 1). At age 3 years, he was evaluated by an orthopedic surgeon, and his diagnosis was corrected to Osteoglophonic dysplasia based on his clinical examination and skeletal survey findings.

He had classic features of OGD (Figure 1:A–D), including short stature, abnormal skull shape, midface hypoplasia, widely spaced eyes, proptosis, low-set ears, short nose with anteverted nares, depressed nasal bridge, long philtrum, prognathism, high-arched palate, delayed dental eruption, and overlapping toes bilaterally (2nd and 4th underriding 3rd on left and overlapping 3rd on right). Skeletal surveys revealed delayed ossification of epiphyses, lucent metaphyseal lesions, a beaten copper appearance of the skull, cloverleaf deformity, and multiple unerupted teeth (Figure 1: E–F). Starting at age 4, the patient developed multiple low-energy fractures, including the distal radius, proximal humerus, and femoral diaphysis, which occurred with gradually increasing frequency. These fractures, coupled with progressive bilateral femoral deformities and recurrent leg pain, led to wheelchair dependence. At age 45 months, his bone mineral density on the spine region, L1–4, was 0.355 gm/cm2, Z-score −2.5 below the mean for age and gender. At age 68 months it was 0.354 gm/cm2, Z-score −2.4; however, his height-adjusted Z-score corresponds to −1.25 SD (Zemel et al., 2011). At age 7, he underwent bilateral femoral osteotomies with bone grafting and telescopic rod placement. The osteotomies healed without complications, and he is now a household ambulator without discomfort. No additional fractures have been reported since the surgery. Bone histology showed normal bone on one end with cysts of varying sizes, and excessive fibrous tissue on the other end (Supplementary figure 1).

He had multiple episodes of high fevers following his recent femoral osteotomy. His infectious workup was positive for Clostridioides difficile infection. He also had fevers following a craniectomy. He sweats excessively, especially when sleeping. His family reports that his forehead frequently feels hot to the touch, and that frontal thermometer will indicate that he has a fever when other methods of checking for temperature show that he is afebrile.

Additionally, at age 8 years, the patient developed a mandibular growth that progressively enlarged over a short period of time (Figure 2). Radiology images of the jaw showed a large mandibular cystic mass. The mass was successfully surgically excised. Pathology reports showed large, central giant cell granuloma (7 cm) of the mandible. His other medical history includes short stature and failure to thrive. At age 6 years and 8 months his weight was 14.4 kg (average for a 3-year-old boy, corresponding to a Z-score of −3.94 SD when using data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC] growth charts) and his height was 94.5 cm (average for a 3-year-old boy, Z-score −4.81 SD) (Supplementary Figure 2). He also has oral and pharyngeal dysphagia, alternating exotropia, and unilateral cryptorchidism. Metabolic workup including vitamin D levels, calcium, phosphorus, PTH, ALP, and intact FGF23, were all within normal ranges. Specifically, his phosphorus level was 4.1 mg/dl (Reference range 3.7–5.9) and his intact FGF23 was 24 pg/ml (Reference value <=52).

Developmentally, the patient achieved significant motor improvements after his first craniofacial surgery. He started to sit, then crawl, stand and was able to walk at around the age of 17 months. However, his current mobility is limited due to bone pain and leg deformities. He uses assistive devices such as a wheelchair and walker for mobility. His speech is understandable to his parents but challenging for strangers. Despite language and social limitations, he attends school and participates in activities like riding a tricycle and playing basketball with a lightweight ball. He relies heavily on oral nutritional supplements due to oral and pharyngeal dysphagia, with limited success in improving his appetite through cyproheptadine. Speech therapy has also been recommended to help address feeding issues.

Patient 2 is a 3-year-old girl with a confirmed molecular diagnosis of osteoglophonic dysplasia (OGD) also due to the heterozygous pathogenic variant c.1141T>C, p.(Cys381Arg) in exon 9 of the FGFR1 gene, identified prenatally through amniocentesis. At 28 weeks of gestation, fetal MRI revealed a misshapen skull, mild over-sulcation of the temporal lobes, and features suspicious for multi-suture craniosynostosis with short long bones. These findings prompted a consultation with a prenatal genetics clinic, where FGFR2-related craniosynostosis syndromes including Pfeiffer syndrome were initially suspected. The patient was born full term with a birth weight of 2.785 kg (Z-score −1.8 SD below the mean for age and gender, based on data from the CDC Growth Charts), length of 44.45 cm (Z-score −3.4 SD) and head circumference of 33 cm (Z-score −2.81 SD). Postnatally, she was stable and discharged home at 3 days of life. Physical examination noted a cloverleaf-shaped skull, fused sagittal sutures, hypoplastic midface, and overlapping toes. Feeding, movement, and tone were normal during the neonatal period. At 2 weeks of age, a head CT confirmed multi-suture craniosynostosis (bilateral coronal, sagittal, superior lambdoid, and proximal metopic sutures) with unremarkable brain parenchymal structures. At age 2 months, she underwent elective cranial vault expansion without complications and was discharged after 3 days. Postoperative helmet therapy was initiated and continued until age 9 months.

At age 20 months, evaluation showed features consistent with OGD (Figure 1:G–I), including impaired growth (weight 9.3 kg, average for a 14-month-old girl, Z-score −12.97 SD below the mean for age and gender based on CDC Growth Charts; length 72.5 cm, average for an 11-month-old girl, Z-score −3.01 SD; Supplementary figure 3) an abnormal skull shape with closed fontanels, midface hypoplasia, widely spaced eyes, proptosis, low-set ears, a short nose with anteverted nares, a depressed nasal bridge, a long philtrum, prognathism, a high-arched palate, unerupted teeth with imaging confirming their presence, genu varum, and overlapping toes (2nd and 4th overriding 3rd) bilaterally, while thumbs and great toes were normal in size and shape. Skeletal surveys revealed severe craniosynostosis features, mild platyspondyly with anterior “bullet” deformities of the vertebral bodies, and multiple lytic lesions in the appendicular skeleton consistent with non-ossifying fibromas, all compatible with OGD (Figure 1:J–L). Metabolic workup, including vitamin D levels, calcium, phosphorus and intact FGF23, were all within normal ranges. Specifically, her phosphorus level was 5 mg/dl (Reference range 3.2–6.5) and her intact FGF23 was 36 pg/ml (Reference value <=52).

Gross and fine motor development has been normal, although she exhibits delayed speech. Parents reported persistent nasal congestion and loud snoring since birth, and she was subsequently diagnosed with severe sleep apnea. She underwent tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy which resulted in significant improvement of her quality of sleep and the chronic congestion. She has episodes of increased frontal temperature with increased sensitivity to heat and sweating. At her most recent evaluation at 3 years and 6 months, she reported leg pain. Radiologic imaging revealed increased cystic lucencies in the long bones, progression of lucent foci, and a left distal femur fracture. Fibrous lesions were also noted in the bilateral distal clavicles (Supplementary Figure 4). She remains under multidisciplinary care, receiving surgical intervention as needed and ongoing monitoring for craniofacial and skeletal complications.

Osteoglophonic dysplasia (OGD) is a rare skeletal dysplasia currently known to be caused by five distinct activating pathogenic variants in the FGFR1 gene, which encodes a fibroblast growth factor receptor essential for osteogenesis of the axial and craniofacial skeleton (Kuthiroly et al., 2017; Marzin et al., 2020; Othman et al., 2024). Fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) and their receptors (FGFRs) play vital roles in human skeletal development. While FGFR1 and FGFR2 are crucial for craniosynostosis syndromes, FGFR3 is primarily associated with short-stature syndromes like achondroplasia and hypochondroplasia. OGD, resulting from distinct monoallelic FGFR1 gain-of-function variants, bridges characteristics of both craniosynostosis and short stature syndromes (Kuthiroly et al., 2017; White et al., 2005). The five known missense variants in FGFR1 variants occur in highly conserved residues within the ligand-binding and transmembrane domains of FGFR1. FGFR1 is a receptor tyrosine kinase that includes extracellular ligand-binding domains, a single transmembrane domain, and an intracellular tyrosine kinase region. In both of our patients, molecular analysis of FGFR1 revealed the missense variant c.1141C>T in exon 9, located within the transmembrane domain, resulting in the substitution of cysteine 381 with arginine. This substitution creates an unpaired cysteine residue, potentially disrupting disulfide bonding essential for proper dimerization of FGFR1, highlighting the role for this receptor as a negative regulator of long-bone growth (Sow et al., 2010; White et al., 2005).

Both patients in this report were initially considered to have features suggestive of Pfeiffer syndrome, a related craniosynostosis syndrome typically associated with FGFR2 and some (other) FGFR1 variants. OGD and Pfeiffer syndrome share overlapping features, such as craniosynostosis, hypertelorism, ocular proptosis, and a flattened midface. However, significant clinical differences exist. OGD is characterized by profound short stature, delayed tooth eruption, overlapping toes, non-ossifying fibromas, increased frontal temperature, and, in some cases, FGF23-mediated hypophosphatemia. In contrast, Pfeiffer syndrome more commonly results from FGFR2 variants and is associated with less pronounced short stature, dental crowding, broad thumbs and great toes and partial syndactyly (Figure 3) (Katouni et al., 2023; Othman et al., 2024; Wenger et al., 1993).

OGD presents as a clinical spectrum, with symptoms ranging from mild cases involving only radiolucent bone lesions and tooth retention (Marzin et al., 2020) to severe presentations including multi-suture craniosynostosis, cystic bone lesions, osteopenia, bone pain, bowed legs, limb length discrepancy, fractures or diffuse lysis of a whole bone (Kumar et al., 2021). Patient 1 represents one of the most severe presentations described, with recurrent fractures, severe lower limb deformities, and wheelchair dependence before the age of ten. Patient 2 displayed severe craniosynostosis that was detected prenatally and her skeletal phenotype continues to progress.

Interestingly, both patients exhibited common novel features not previously reported in the literature: increased frontal temperature, heat sensitivity with excessive sweating, and a consistent bilateral overlapping toe pattern, with the second and fourth toes overlapping or under-riding the third (Supplemental File). To further investigate these features, we contacted the authors of prior publications. Information was obtained for three patients. One patient; OGD 14 (Sow et al., 2010) with an FGFR1 variant similar to our patient’s (p.(Cys381Arg)) was reported to have toe abnormalities, though the exact nature of the abnormality was not specified, and recurrent fevers or excessive sweating were not observed. Another patient; OGD 17 (Kuthiroly et al., 2017) with a different variant (p.(Cys341Tyr)) did not have overlapping toes, though fever history was unavailable. A third patient; OGD 22 (Zou et al., 2022) with a different FGFR1 variant (p.(Tyr374Cys)) did not exhibit overlapping toes, recurrent fevers, or excessive sweating. While definitive conclusions cannot be drawn given the limited available data, these findings suggest a potential genotype-phenotype correlation, where toe abnormalities may be more specific to patients with the p.(Cys381Arg) variant in FGFR1.

Another distinguishing feature of Patient 1 was the development and swift progression of a large central giant cell granuloma of the jaw, a finding previously reported in only three other OGD cases (Shankar et al., 2010; Sow et al., 2010; White et al., 2005). The now four known cases suggest an empirical risk of 16.7% to develop giant cell granuloma in OGD. This complication emphasizes the potential for OGD to involve aggressive osseous lesions in addition to its hallmark skeletal dysplasia. Despite the severe manifestations seen in Patient 1, both patients in this study benefitted from surgical interventions. Orthopedic procedures including bilateral femoral osteotomies corrected deformities and restored some mobility in our first patient, while cranial vault remodeling surgeries improved intracranial pressure and developmental outcomes in both patients.

Neither of our patients presented with hypophosphatemia, a feature reported in some cases of OGD. Current reports show that the p.(Asn330Ile) variant has been specifically associated with FGF23-mediated hypophosphatemia (Kuthiroly et al., 2017; White et al., 2005). This absence highlights the phenotypic variability of the disorder, which poses challenges for diagnosis and management. Evidence suggests that increased FGFR1 activity, rather than non-ossifying fibromas, likely drives FGF23 excess and the resulting phosphate wasting or rickets, although the precise intracellular mechanism remains unclear (Ovejero et al., 2023). In cases where FGF23-mediated hypophosphatemia is present, therapy could involve phosphate supplements, calcitriol, or monoclonal antibodies targeting FGF23 (Cherian & Paul, 2024). While treatment adherence can improve outcomes, persistent bony deformities in some patients may require surgical intervention. These complexities reinforce the importance of integrating genetic testing with clinical and radiological evaluations to guide tailored management strategies. Additionally, since OGD is caused by variants that activate FGFR1, targeted therapies that modulate receptor activity could be a potential treatment avenue. One example is infigratinib, an oral FGFR1-FGFR3 inhibitor currently being studied in clinical trials for achondroplasia, a condition driven by FGFR3 activation (Savarirayan et al., 2025). This raises the question of whether similar approaches could be beneficial for patients with OGD. As our understanding of FGFR-mediated skeletal dysplasias continues to evolve, the potential for precision therapies tailored to specific genetic variants may pave the way for more effective, individualized treatment approaches.

The prognosis of OGD is generally favorable. Patients typically have normal intelligence and a normal life span; however, life expectancy depends largely on the severity of craniofacial malformations, as these may lead to fatal respiratory or feeding issues. The hallmark of the disorder is multiple non-ossifying fibromas, which appear as lucencies in the metaphyses of long bones. These fibromas tend to increase in size and number until skeletal maturity, after which they gradually fill in, leading to abnormal remodeling and expansion of the metaphyses. Fractures in these areas can result in pseudarthrosis (Fairbank, 1951; Kelley et al., 1983). Other associated conditions, such as inguinal hernia and hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, have also been documented (Holder et al., 2017; Keats et al., 1975; Kelley et al., 1983).

In summary, these cases expand the known phenotypic spectrum of OGD and highlight the critical distinctions between OGD and Pfeiffer syndrome. Notably, our two patients exhibit previously unreported shared features associated with the same genetic variant. It remains unclear whether this represents a genotype-phenotype correlation or if these features were present but underreported in earlier cases. The identification of these novel features emphasizes the need for increased clinical awareness and further research into this rare disorder. Understanding the spectrum of OGD manifestations will aid in earlier diagnosis, more accurate differentiation from other craniosynostosis syndromes, and the development of tailored management strategies.
