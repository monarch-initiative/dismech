# Transcutaneous Auricular Vagus Nerve Stimulation in a Pacemaker-Dependent Child with Syndromic Drug-Resistant Epilepsy: A Case Report with Prospective 9-Month Follow-Up.
**Authors:** Al-Ghawanmeh R, Al-Momani H, Abed Alrahman R, Badaineh Y, Alarameen D
**Journal:** Case Rep Neurol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1159/crn/abyag011](https://doi.org/10.1159/crn/abyag011)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is an emerging noninvasive neuromodulation therapy for drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). However, evidence regarding its use in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices, particularly pacemaker-dependent children, remains extremely limited.

CASE PRESENTATION: We report a 6-year-old boy with genetically confirmed TAF6-related Alazami-Yuan syndrome, severe developmental impairment, complete atrioventricular block requiring permanent epicardial VVIR pacing, and syndromic DRE refractory to multiple antiseizure medications (ASMs). Auricular taVNS was initiated at the cymba conchae under multidisciplinary supervision using stimulation parameters of 25 Hz, pulse width 250 μs, intensity titrated to tolerance up to 1.7 mA, for 4 h/day. Treatment initiation was performed in the pediatric intensive care unit with continuous electrocardiographic monitoring for 5 days. The patient underwent prospective follow-up over 9 months using caregiver-maintained seizure diaries, serial electroencephalography (EEG), serial electrocardiography, repeated pacemaker interrogation, adverse event surveillance, and Pediatric Quality of Life Family Impact Module (PedsQL-FIM) assessments. No arrhythmias, pacing abnormalities, device malfunction, or clinically significant pacemaker parameter changes were observed during follow-up. A mild transient local skin irritation resolved with conservative care. Seizure burden decreased from 2 to 4 prolonged seizures daily at baseline to approximately two brief generalized tonic seizures weekly, with sustained improvement through month 9. Reduction of ASM burden began only after clinical improvement had already been observed. Follow-up EEGs remained markedly abnormal with only limited interval electrographic change. PedsQL-FIM improved progressively from 10.4 at baseline to 54.9 at month 3, 80.6 at month 7, and 84.0 at month 9.

CONCLUSION: Auricular taVNS was successfully administered in a pacemaker-dependent child under structured multidisciplinary monitoring without identifiable cardiac or pacemaker-related adverse events during 9 months of follow-up. Although efficacy and safety conclusions cannot be inferred from a single case, this report supports the clinical feasibility of combining noninvasive vagal neuromodulation with permanent cardiac pacing in carefully selected pediatric patients.

Drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) affects approximately one-third of children with epilepsy and is frequently associated with developmental impairment, genetic etiologies, and substantial caregiver burden [1]. Despite advances in pharmacotherapy, many patients fail to achieve adequate seizure control.

Neuromodulation has emerged as an adjunctive therapeutic strategy for DRE. Implanted vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) has demonstrated efficacy in selected patient populations [2, 3]. However, its use in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), particularly pacemakers, remains limited because of concerns regarding electromagnetic interference and possible effects on cardiac conduction.

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) represents a noninvasive alternative targeting the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, most commonly at the cymba conchae [4, 5]. Because taVNS avoids surgical implantation and pulse generator placement, interest in its use has increased substantially in recent years.

Nevertheless, pediatric taVNS literature remains limited and methodologically heterogeneous. A recent systematic review highlighted variability in stimulation parameters and the absence of standardized pediatric protocols or dedicated pediatric devices [6].

Similarly, reports describing coexistence of neuromodulation therapies and CIEDs are rare and predominantly involve adult patients with implanted VNS systems [7–9]. To our knowledge, prospective pediatric follow-up of auricular taVNS in a fully pacemaker-dependent child with DRE has not been previously described. Here, we report prospective 9-month neurological and cardiac follow-up of auricular taVNS in a fully pacemaker-dependent child with syndromic DRE.

This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines. The completed CARE checklist is provided as online supplementary Material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/crn/abyag011).

A 6-year-old boy with a complex neurodevelopmental disorder presented with severe developmental delay, hypotonia, bilateral congenital cataracts, cryptorchidism, and dysmorphic features. Genetic testing identified a homozygous pathogenic TAF6 mutation (exon 7 c.610_611delGC, p.Ala204fs), consistent with Alazami-Yuan syndrome. An older brother had similar clinical manifestations and died during adolescence without undergoing genetic testing or receiving a molecular diagnosis.

Seizures began at 6 months of age as myoclonic seizures and later evolved into mixed seizure types including generalized tonic seizures and focal impaired awareness seizures with tonic features. Despite treatment with multiple antiseizure medications (ASMs) including perampanel, levetiracetam, phenobarbital, topiramate, and clonazepam, seizure control remained poor. At baseline, seizure burden was approximately 2–4 prolonged seizures daily, some lasting 10–15 min and requiring emergency department visits and hospitalization.

At 18 months of age, the patient developed complete atrioventricular block requiring implantation of a single-chamber epicardial VVIR pacemaker (Medtronic Sphera SPSR01), after which he became functionally pacemaker-dependent. Baseline electroencephalography (EEG) demonstrated diffuse background slowing with frequent generalized and multifocal epileptiform discharges, consistent with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. Baseline caregiver-reported Pediatric Quality of Life Family Impact Module (PedsQL-FIM) score was 10.4 [10].

After multidisciplinary discussion involving pediatric neurology, pediatric cardiology, and electrophysiology teams, auricular taVNS was initiated using electrodes placed at the cymba conchae. Stimulation parameters included frequency 25 Hz, pulse width 250 μs, intensity titrated to tolerance up to 1.7 mA, and treatment duration 4 h/day. Parameter selection was based on previously published taVNS protocols [11, 12].

Treatment initiation was performed in the pediatric intensive care unit with continuous electrocardiography (ECG) monitoring for 5 days. A predefined clinical monitoring plan was established prior to taVNS initiation as part of structured multidisciplinary monitoring. Assessments were performed at baseline and at approximately 1, 3, 7, and 9 months. The originally planned 6-month follow-up assessment was conducted at month 7 because of scheduling constraints. During the first month, follow-up was performed weekly through clinic visits or telephone review.

Prospective follow-up included caregiver-maintained seizure diaries, serial EEG, serial ECG, repeated pacemaker interrogation, adverse event surveillance, and serial PedsQL-FIM assessments. Seizure frequency was recorded prospectively by the patient’s primary caregiver using daily seizure diaries reviewed during follow-up visits.

No adverse cardiac events occurred during treatment initiation or follow-up. Continuous ECG monitoring demonstrated stable ventricular-paced rhythm without pauses, tachyarrhythmias, oversensing, undersensing, or loss of capture. Clinical improvement was observed early after treatment initiation. By month 1, caregiver-reported seizure frequency had decreased to approximately 2–3 seizures weekly, predominantly brief generalized tonic events. This reduction was maintained through months 3, 7, and 9. At month 9, the patient continued to experience approximately two brief generalized tonic seizures weekly, each lasting only a few seconds.

A transient worsening in seizure frequency was noted during a period of incorrect device use; however, causal interpretation remains uncertain. Improvement was observed following caregiver retraining and correction of the stimulation procedure.

Reduction in ASM burden occurred after the initial clinical improvement. One ASM (phenobarbital) was discontinued at month 3 because of sustained seizure reduction. By month 7, a second ASM (levetiracetam) had been discontinued, with dose reduction of the remaining medications (topiramate and clonazepam), while the perampanel dose remained unchanged. Medication burden remained stable through month 9.

Follow-up EEGs remained markedly abnormal throughout follow-up, with persistent diffuse slowing and generalized/multifocal epileptiform discharges. Only limited interval electrographic change was observed despite sustained clinical improvement.

Caregiver-reported quality of life improved progressively throughout follow-up. PedsQL-FIM increased from 10.4 at baseline to 54.9 at month 3, 80.6 at month 7, and 84.0 at month 9. A mild local skin irritation occurred early during treatment and resolved with conservative skin care and adjustment of electrode placement. No additional treatment-limiting adverse effects were observed. Table 1 summarizes the longitudinal clinical, cardiac, and electrophysiological outcomes.

This report describes prospective 9-month neurological and cardiac follow-up of auricular taVNS in a pacemaker-dependent child with syndromic DRE. The principal clinical relevance of the case lies in the coexistence of noninvasive vagal neuromodulation and permanent cardiac pacing in a pediatric patient, a scenario that remains rarely described in the literature. Noninvasive taVNS was favored over implanted cervical VNS because of its lower procedural burden, reversibility, and the desire to minimize potential interaction with existing cardiac implantable hardware.

In this patient, taVNS was temporally associated with substantial reduction in seizure burden and marked improvement in caregiver-reported quality of life. As an uncontrolled single case observation, the reduction in seizure frequency should be interpreted as an observational finding rather than evidence of treatment efficacy and should not be generalized beyond this individual patient. The principal contribution of this report is the demonstration of the feasibility of administering taVNS in a pacemaker-dependent child under structured multidisciplinary neurological and cardiac monitoring. Importantly, reduction in ASM burden occurred only after clinical improvement had already been observed. To our knowledge, this is among the first reports describing prospective neurocardiac follow-up of auricular taVNS in a fully pacemaker-dependent pediatric patient.

The limited EEG evolution despite sustained clinical improvement is not unexpected in severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, in which electrographic abnormalities may persist despite clinically meaningful reduction in seizure burden and caregiver-reported improvement [13]. This electroclinical discordance may reflect the severity of the underlying developmental and epileptic encephalopathy.

Mechanistically, stimulation of the auricular branch of the vagus nerve at the cymba conchae may access central vagal pathways involved in modulation of cortical excitability and autonomic regulation [4, 5]. Previous studies have suggested that taVNS may represent a promising adjunctive therapy for DRE, although available pediatric evidence remains limited and heterogeneous [6, 12, 14]. Pediatric feasibility studies have generally reported acceptable tolerability and favorable preliminary safety profiles, although standardized stimulation protocols and long-term pediatric data remain limited [11].

Cardiac safety represented the primary concern in this case. Potential electromagnetic interference between external neuromodulation systems and CIEDs remains a theoretical concern, particularly in pacemaker-dependent patients. Implanted cervical VNS systems have been associated with oversensing or pacing inhibition under certain conditions, and published experience regarding the coexistence of neuromodulation therapies and CIEDs remains limited, consisting predominantly of adult reports involving implanted VNS systems [7–9]. In contrast, auricular taVNS uses substantially lower stimulation amplitudes and a more peripheral stimulation site, remote from intracardiac leads and pulse generators, potentially reducing the risk of electromagnetic interference [4, 5]. Nevertheless, because this patient was fully pacemaker-dependent and had a unipolar ventricular lead configuration, treatment initiation was performed under intensive cardiac monitoring with repeated device interrogation. Unipolar pacing systems may theoretically be more susceptible to electromagnetic interference than bipolar configurations because of their larger sensing circuit [15]. Using the stimulation parameters applied in this patient, no pacing abnormalities, sensing disturbances, arrhythmias, pacemaker malfunction, or other identifiable cardiac or pacemaker-related adverse events were observed during continuous ECG monitoring, serial ECGs, or repeated pacemaker interrogation.

Beyond potential device interactions, published pediatric experience suggests that taVNS is generally well tolerated, with adverse effects usually limited to mild local discomfort, tingling, erythema, or transient skin irritation at the stimulation site [6, 11]. Clinically significant cardiovascular adverse events have not been commonly reported in published pediatric taVNS studies; however, evidence remains limited, particularly in children with CIEDs or significant underlying cardiac disease. In our patient, only mild transient skin irritation occurred and resolved with conservative management without interruption of treatment.

Caregiver-reported quality of life improved substantially during follow-up. In pediatric epilepsy, caregiver burden is closely linked to seizure severity and overall disease burden, and improvements in seizure control are frequently associated with improved caregiver well-being and family functioning [16, 17].

This report has several limitations. It describes a single observational case without a control condition. Seizure outcomes were based primarily on caregiver-maintained diaries rather than prolonged video-EEG quantification. Formal provocation testing using high-output stimulation or testing under maximal pacemaker sensitivity settings was not performed. Therefore, although no clinically significant interaction was identified under the stimulation parameters used in this case, subclinical interference under extreme testing conditions cannot be completely excluded. In addition, ASM adjustments during follow-up may represent a potential confounder, although clinical improvement preceded medication reduction. Spontaneous fluctuation in seizure burden cannot be excluded. Larger prospective studies are required to better define both efficacy and cardiac safety of taVNS in patients with CIEDs.

Auricular taVNS was successfully administered in a pacemaker-dependent child with syndromic drug-resistant epilepsy under structured multidisciplinary monitoring without identifiable cardiac or pacemaker-related adverse events during 9 months of prospective follow-up. Although efficacy and safety conclusions cannot be inferred from a single case, this report supports the clinical feasibility of combining noninvasive vagal neuromodulation with permanent cardiac pacing in carefully selected pediatric patients.
