# 3-Hydroxyisobutyric acid dehydrogenase deficiency: Expanding the clinical spectrum and quantitation of D- and L-3-Hydroxyisobutyric acid by an LC-MS/MS method.
**Authors:** Sasarman F, Ferdinandusse S, Sinasac DS, Fung E, Sparkes R, Reeves M, Rombough C, Sass JO, Voit R, Ruiter JPN, Koster J, Waterham HR, Pasquini E, Donati MA, Marquardt T, Wanders RJA, Al-Hertani W
**Journal:** J Inherit Metab Dis (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jimd.12486](https://doi.org/10.1002/jimd.12486)

## Content

A deficiency of 3-hydroxyisobutyric acid dehydrogenase (HIBADH) has been recently identified as a cause of primary 3-hydroxyisobutyric aciduria in two siblings; the only previously recognized primary cause had been a deficiency of methylmalonic semialdehyde dehydrogenase, the enzyme that is immediately downstream of HIBADH in the valine catabolic pathway and is encoded by the ALDH6A1 gene. Here we report on three additional patients from two unrelated families who present with marked and persistent elevations of urine L-3-hydroxyisobutyric acid (L-3HIBA) and a range of clinical findings. Molecular genetic analyses revealed novel, homozygous variants in the HIBADH gene that are private within each family. Evidence for pathogenicity of the identified variants is presented, including enzymatic deficiency of HIBADH in patient fibroblasts. This report describes new variants in HIBADH as an underlying cause of primary 3-hydroxyisobutyric aciduria and expands the clinical spectrum of this recently identified inborn error of valine metabolism. Additionally, we describe a quantitative method for the measurement of D- and L-3HIBA in plasma and urine and present the results of a valine restriction therapy in one of the patients.

1 | INTRODUCTION
The three branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) leucine,
isoleucine and valine are essential neutral amino acids,
each containing a methyl side chain. In humans, the
BCAAs are catabolized within the mitochondrion via a
series of enzymatic reactions, the first two of which are
common to these three amino acids (reviewed recently
1).
The first reaction, transamination, involves the BCAA
transaminase that generates the corresponding 2-oxo/keto
acids, followed by oxidative decarboxylation, carried out by
the branched-chain 2-keto-acid dehydrogenase complex,
which produces isobutyryl-CoA, 2-methyl-butyryl-CoA and
isovaleryl-CoA from valine, isoleucine and leucine, respec-
tively. From this point on, however, the degradative path-
ways of the BCAAs diverge with regard to the enzymes
involved, and also the sequence and type of reactions.
In the valine catabolic pathway, following hydrolysis of
3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA to 3-hydroxyisobutyric acid
(3HIBA) by 3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase (a.k.a.,
deacylase; HIBCH), 3HIBA undergoes dehydrogenation by
3-hydroxyisobutyric acid dehydrogenase (HIBADH). This
leads to the formation of methylmalonic semialdehyde, the
substrate for methylmalonic semialdehyde dehydrogenase
(MMSDH), which generates the common intermediary
metabolite propionyl-CoA. It is noteworthy that this path-
way is unique with regard to the conversion of 3-
hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA to propionyl-CoA, which proceeds
via free acids rather than the typical acyl-CoA esters.
Recently, it has been demonstrated that in addition to a
deficiency of MMSDH [MIM: 614105], primary 3-
hydroxyisobutyric aciduria can result from a deficiency of
HIBADH
2; 3-hydroxyisobutyric aciduria is also a recog-
nized secondary finding in a subgroup of mitochondrial dis-
eases. Pathogenic variants in ALDH6A1 [MIM: 603178]
encoding MMSDH have been identified to date in five
patients presenting with highly variable clinical phenotypes
that range from severe developmental delays and MRI
findings to virtually asymptomatic, and abnormal metabo-
lites including markedly elevated urinary 3HIBA, milder
but variable elevations of 3-hydroxypropionic acid, beta-
aminoisobutyric acid, beta-alanine and methylmalonic
acid.
3–6 A deficiency of HIBADH resulting from a homozy-
gous pathogenic variant in HIBADH [MIM: 608475] has
been recently described in t wo siblings with increased
urinary excretion of 3HIBA, one of whom presented with
psychomotor retardation and low average total IQ values,
while the other was asymptomatic.
2
Here we present three patients from two unrelated
families (including the two cases briefly mentioned previ-
ously in a book chapter by Wanders et al
7), who present
with marked 3-hydroxyisobutyric aciduria and a range of
clinical phenotypes. We report novel homozygous vari-
ants in HIBADH that are private to each family, and
show evidence for their pathogenicity, including reduced
HIBADH enzymatic activity in cultured patient fibro-
blasts. Additionally, we describe a liquid chromatogra-
phy–tandem mass spectrometry (LC –MS/MS) method for
the quantitative measurement of 3HIBA in plasma and
urine and present the results of a valine restriction ther-
apy in one of the patients.

3.2 | Molecular genetic analysis and
variant pathogenicity studies
Given the genetic heterogeneity of 3-hydroxyisobutyric
aciduria, which includes both primary and secondary cau-
ses, WES analysis was performed in Patient 1. This led to
the identification of a homozygous variant in the HIBADH
gene (c.302C > G, p.Thr101Arg), coding for the mitochon-
drial 3-hydroxyisobutyrate dehydrogenase enzyme. Sanger
sequencing of HIBADH confirmed this finding, and further
revealed a common polymorphism in the coding sequence
(c.18G > T, synonymous sequence variant). The only other
variant identified by WES in Patient 1 was a homozygous
missense substitution in the SZT2 gene (c.8768C > T, p.
Thr2923Ile; [MIM: 615463]), classified as a variant of
uncertain significance (VUS). The protein encoded by the
SZT2 gene (previously known as TIGR and KIAA0467)i s
expressed in the brain and localizes to the peroxisome,
where it is believed to play a role in resistance to oxidative
stress.
10 Variants in this gene have been identified in
patients with developmental and epileptic encephalopa-
thy-18, DEE18 [MIM: 615476], characterized by early onset
of refractory seizures, lack of psychomotor development
apparent from birth and thick corpus callosum.

3.4 | Therapeutic trial of valine
restriction
Given the confirmed diagnosis of an enzymatic deficiency
in the valine catabolic pathway, a therapeutic trial of
valine restriction was initiated in Patient 1 at 8 months of
age. In order to monitor the levels of 3HIBA while on
treatment, an LC –MS/MS method was developed for
accurate metabolite quantitation. Both the D- and L- ste-
reoisomers of 3HIBA were quantitated in plasma and
urine, in patients and controls (Supporting Information
Table S2).
The L-stereoisomer of 3HIBA was shown to accumu-
late specifically in the patient's plasma (pre-treatment:
527 μM; control range: 3.4 –24.1) and urine (pre-treat-
ment: 5231 mmol/mol creatinine; control range: 0.2 –
21.3). Upon restriction of valine intake and monitoring,
plasma levels of L-3HIBA and valine were found to be
directly proportional (Figure
4), while levels of D-3HIBA
varied slightly, between 0.7 –4.5 μM (control range: 0.4 –
14.7). The lowest level of plasma L-3HIBA measured in
the patient while on treatment was 86.5 μM (control
range: 3.4 –24.1); however, the corresponding plasma
valine concentration at this point was significantly below
the lower reference limit for age (58 μM; ref. range: 99 –
371). Thus, further treatment was aimed at maintaining
valine concentration at approximately 100 μM. Urine
excretion of L-3HIBA also decreased during this treat-
ment regimen, and the lowest measured value was
1264 mmol/mol creatinine (control range: 0.2 –21.3),
while excretion of D-3HIBA varied slightly, between 8.3 –
44.9 mmol/mol creatinine (control range: 3.5 –35;
Figure
5).
While there was a clear biochemical response to die-
tary valine restriction therapy in this patient, the clinical
benefits were equivocal and adherence has been
inconsistent.
4 | DISCUSSION
In this study, we present molecular and biochemical find-
ings confirming that pathogenic variants in HIBADH are
an underlying cause of primary 3-hydroxyisobutyric
aciduria. Initially believed to represent a single disorder
of valine catabolism, 3-hydroxyisobutyric aciduria has
now been shown to comprise a heterogeneous group of
disorders that includes distinct, primary enzymatic defi-
ciencies of the valine catabolic pathway in addition to
being a secondary finding in a subgroup of mitochondrial
diseases. Given that HIBADH requires NAD
+ as a sub-
strate, it is conceivable, as suggested previously, 13,14 that
3-hydroxyisobutyric aciduria could arise secondarily from
impaired oxidation of NADH resulting from a respiratory
chain defect. This possibility is further supported by the
finding of lactic acidosis in approximately half of the
reported cases of 3-hydroxyisobutyric aciduria.
7
Although the five HIBADH-deficient patients
reported to date had a variable clinical presentation with
limited overlap (Table

with marked and persistent elevations of 3HIBA. In four
of the five cases, there was an additional elevation of 2-
ethyl-3-hydroxypropionic acid present, mild elevations of
this metabolite having now also been confirmed in the
two previously published patients. Thus, the finding of a

substantial and persistent elevation of urine 3HIBA, with

hydroxypropionic acid but with no other metabolite
abnormalities warrants consideration of HIBADH defi-
ciency, regardless of the clinical presentation. This is in
contrast with some of the MMSDH-deficient patients,
where the biochemical abnormalities appear variable and
fluctuating, thus making suspicion of the correct diagno-
sis even more difficult in the context of a variable clinical
presentation.
4
Based on the patients described to date, both
HIBADH and MMSDH deficiencies present with variable
clinical findings including asymptomatic cases. This is in
contrast with HIBCH deficiency [MIM: 250620], the
enzyme immediately upstream of HIBADH, where all
patients have MRI features suggestive of Leigh syndrome
and either a Leigh-like presentation,
15–19 or a movement
disorder with dystonia and ataxia. 20 The clinical presen-
tation is generally more severe in HIBCH deficiency, a
finding that has been attributed to the toxicity of the
accumulating methacrylyl-CoA. 16,18,21 Among several
possible pathogenic mechanisms, methacrylyl-CoA can
react with, and deplete, cysteine pools leading to
decreased glutathione and thioredoxin, impairment of
mitochondrial enzymes containing Fe-S clusters, all
resulting in oxidative damage.
16 Although 3HIBA as a
free acid does not accumulate in HIBCH deficiency, there
are significant elevations of its CoA ester, resulting in
transesterification with carnitine and elevated hydroxy-
C4-carnitine in most patients.
15
It is not obvious how a deficiency of HIBADH or
MMSDH can result in the spectrum of clinical presenta-
tions described to date, or how identical variants can lead
to different presentations (including asymptomatic) in
members of the same family. It would be tempting to
speculate that the toxicity of the accumulating 3HIBA
and/or related metabolites could play a role, and that the
severity of the clinical presentation may be proportional
to the concentration of the toxic compound. To that
effect, it is noteworthy that Patient 3, who presented with
lower levels of accumulating 3HIBA and no evidence of
elevated 2-ethyl-3-hydroxypropionic, had a milder pre-
sentation than his sibling, Patient 2. This comparison,
however, is not straightforward, as during their teenage
years, the levels of 3HIBA were actually higher in the
asymptomatic sibling; moreover, the levels of 3HIBA in
patient 2 at this point could have been influenced to
some degree by dietary therapy, even if adherence had
been limited. Another argument against the toxicity of
3HIBA being responsible for the observed phenotypic
spectrum is the level of urine 3HIBA in the asymptomatic
Patient 5 reported previously, which was measured with
the method described here and found to be comparable
to the pre-treatment level in Patient 1 (4879 mmol/mol
creatinine; control range 0.2 –21.3; Supporting Informa-
tion Table S2b). One possible explanation for the clinical
variability in these patients could be the presence of com-
mon genetic variants that can influence the presentation
of disorders typically considered monogenic, as demon-
strated recently.
22 Alternatively, HIBADH deficiency may
represent a biochemical trait, rather than a clinical phe-
notype, with symptomatic cases reflecting ascertain-
ment bias.
With respect to treatment, valine restriction and a
low-protein diet resulted in lower levels of 3HIBA in
plasma and/or urine in Patient 1 and Patient 4, the symp-
tomatic patient described previously.
2 Upon valine
restriction in Patient 1, plasma levels of L-3HIBA were
quantified with a novel LC –MS/MS method and found to
be directly proportional with plasma valine levels; a find-
ing that brings forth an easily accessible monitoring
parameter. Whether this treatment resulted in an
improvement of the clinical parameters is less clear. Of
note, in the sibling Patients 2 and 3, the levels of 3HIBA
excretion decreased with age even when adherence to a
low protein diet was scarce, raising the possibility of a
gradual decrease of 3HIBA levels with age in HIBADH
deficient patients, even in the absence of (or with limited)
therapeutic intervention. However, at least in part, this
could be due to an increase in muscle mass, and therefore
of creatinine excretion, with age. Additional longitudinal
data in patients and controls are necessary to answer this
question. Detoxification of the accumulating 3HIBA can-
not occur through conjugation with carnitine in these
patients, as generation of acylcarnitine derivatives
requires transesterification of the corresponding CoA
ester, and in HIBADH deficiency the accumulating spe-
cies is the free acid. However, other conjugates such as
those with glycine, for instance, might be useful in the
detoxification of 3HIBA, and this possibility may be
worth further investigation.
In conclusion, we present findings that confirm
HIBADH deficiency as one of the causes for primary 3-
hydroxyisobutyric aciduria and expand the clinical spec-
trum of this newly described disorder of valine catabo-
lism. Overall, the findings to date warrant inclusion of
HIBADH deficiency in the recently curated nosology of
inborn errors of metabolism
23 (IEM) under the category
of well-characterized IEM described in the literature.
While clearly an extremely rare IEM, a diagnosis of
HIBADH deficiency should be considered in any patient
with an essentially isolated, marked and persistent eleva-
tion of urine (and plasma) 3HIBA, with or without an
accompanying elevation of 2-ethyl-3-hydroxypropionic
acid. A valine- and protein-restricted diet may be benefi-
cial to patients with proven HIBADH deficiency.
