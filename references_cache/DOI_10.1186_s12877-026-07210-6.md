# Effect of multi-domain interventions delivered via a smart-care platform on intrinsic capacity in older adults: a randomized controlled trial protocol
**Authors:** Tingting Huang, Yulong Wang, Haoran Huang, Shuxin Xu, Nana Chen, Muwei Zhu, Xianping Tang

In the context of ageing characterized by increased longevity but declining health, enhancing older adults’ intrinsic capacity, the composite of an individual’s physical and mental capacities, has emerged as a key strategy for promoting healthy ageing. Multi-domain interventions have been shown to effectively improve intrinsic capacity in this population. However, with the rapid growth of the elderly population, traditional intervention models face challenges such as resource imbalances and inefficiencies. Integrating multi-domain interventions with digital platforms offers a promising approach to enhance the intrinsic capacity of older adults efficiently and sustainably.

To evaluate the effectiveness of a multi-domain intervention delivered via a smart-care platform in improving intrinsic capacity among older adults.

This study protocol proposes a non-blinded randomized controlled trial. A total of 130 older adults with declining intrinsic capacity will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or control group. The intervention group will receive a 12-week multi-domain program delivered via a smart-care platform (including cognitive training, physical exercise, nutritional guidance, and psychological support), while the control group will receive routine health guidance (including health assessments and health counseling). Primary outcomeswill encompass multiple domains, including cognitive capacity, psychological capacity, vitality, and locomotor capacity. Secondary outcomes will include self-care ability, quality of life, and eHealth literacy. Participants’ data will be collected at the initial baseline (week 0) assessment, with follow-ups scheduled for weeks 4, 8, and 12.

This proposed study will evaluate the effectiveness of multi-domain interventions delivered via a smart-care platform in improving intrinsic capacity in older adults. If proven effective, this intervention could provide a scalable, highly cost-effective care model for resource-constrained settings, thereby optimizing care resources and advancing equitable healthy aging.

This trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) on September 17, 2025, registration number ChiCTR2500109335.

Global life expectancy continues to rise, with the number of people aged 60 and above projected to reach 1.4 billion by 2030 [1]. However, due to the effects of ageing, older adults continually face chronic illnesses and physical functional impairments, resulting in the phenomenon of “living longer but not healthier” [2]. In 2015, the World Health Organization released the “World Report on Ageing and Health” [3] to guide responses to population ageing. The report defines healthy ageing as the process of developing and maintaining functional ability that enables well-being in older age. Functional ability, in turn, is a composite of an individual’s intrinsic capacity (IC), the characteristics of their relevant environment, and the interactions between them. Within the framework of healthy ageing, the concept of IC has subsequently emerged, highlighting its central role in this process.

IC refers to the composite of all physical and mental capacities that an individual can draw upon and encompasses five domains: cognition (e.g., memory, executive function), locomotor function (e.g., gait speed, balance), vitality (nutritional status), psychological well-being (e.g., mood, motivation), and sensory function (vision and hearing) [4,5]. As individuals age, their physical and mental functions gradually decline, leading to reductions in IC reserves. This decline primarily results from the long-term, synergistic interaction between individual physiological factors and external environmental influences [6,7]. Research indicates that in older adults, impaired IC increases the risk of adverse outcomes, including accelerated ageing, falls, hospitalizations, disability, and even mortality [8]. Other studies have also clearly indicated that visual and hearing impairments can lead to negative emotions such as loneliness and depression among older adults, severely affecting their physical and mental well-being [9,10]. Impairments in IC directly result in declines in both health status and functional ability, hindering the process of healthy ageing and increasing the economic and resource burdens on social care systems. Therefore, enhancing IC in older adults is essential for preventing adverse health outcomes, improving quality of life, and promoting healthy ageing, while simultaneously alleviating the societal burden of elder care.

To enhance older adults’ IC, the World Health Organization has issued relevant guidelines [4,5] that provide evidence-based strategies for managing IC decline. These guidelines encourage researchers to design personalized, multi-domain interventions that are informed by screening assessment results and target specific IC domains to improve functional status. Concurrently, routine monitoring is recommended to evaluate intervention effectiveness. Multi-domain intervention is typically defined as an approach targeting at least two of the five intrinsic capacity (IC) domains. The core components of such interventions commonly include nutritional supplementation, cognitive stimulation therapy, psychological interventions, chronic disease management, and multimodal exercise training [4,5].

Accumulating research evidence suggests that multi-domain interventions can effectively prevent functional decline and enhance IC among older adults. For instance, Ferrara [11] implemented the +AGIL Barcelona multidomain program, which significantly improved the IC of community-dwelling older adults. Similarly, Liang [12] conducted a nationwide cluster randomized controlled trial delivering a multi-domain intervention that combined physical exercise, cognitive training, nutritional counseling, and chronic condition management, resulting in notable improvements in participants’ cognitive function and vitality. However, as global ageing accelerates, the number of older adults experiencing IC decline is expected to increase [1,13]. Traditional intervention models face substantial challenges, including inequitable distribution of healthcare resources across regions and socioeconomic groups. Moreover, healthcare delivery often becomes inefficient due to constraints in human and material resources, time, and geographic accessibility [14,15]. These challenges highlight the limitations of conventional models and underscore the need for technological innovation to transform intervention delivery and scalability.

In recent years, as healthcare has progressively transitioned toward digital transformation, numerous digital health platforms integrating Internet of Things ( IoT), big data, and artificial intelligence technologies have emerged, offering novel pathways for managing the health of older adults. Evidence suggests that digital health services not only provide convenience for older adults with varying health conditions but also effectively and precisely enhance their overall health [16,17]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Tandon et al. [18] utilized a mobile platform to deliver real-time monitoring, personalized reminders, and accessible health information for a large population of individuals with chronic diseases, thereby improving both quality of life and mental health. Moreover, growing research indicates that digital healthcare constitutes a cornerstone for improving healthcare efficiency among older adults, demonstrating the feasibility of leveraging digital medical technologies to promote healthy ageing [19,20]. Collectively, this evidence underscores that, compared with traditional intervention models, the integration of digital tools not only enhances the effectiveness of conventional interventions but also broadens their reach to a broader population. Digital health interventions help mitigate barriers such as time constraints, limited resources, and workforce shortages, offering greater flexibility and efficiency [21,22]. Furthermore, by enabling personalized health management based on individual data and needs, digital platforms may facilitate early detection and timely intervention for potential health issues or functional declines. Such technology-driven approaches hold promise for strengthening older adults’ IC and fostering healthy ageing.

This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a multi-domain intervention delivered via a digital platform in enhancing multiple domains of IC among older adults.

H1: We hypothesize that, following the 12-week intervention, the total IC scores of the experimental group will be significantly higher than those of the control group.

H2: We hypothesize that, compared with the control group, the experimental group will show significant improvements across all domains of IC—including cognitive, locomotor, psychological, vitality, and sensory domains—after the intervention.

This study will implement a 12-week, non-blinded randomized controlled trial involving older adults with declining IC. Eligible participants who provide informed consent will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or control group. The IC of participants will be assessed at four time points: at the initial baseline (week 0), with follow-ups at weeks 4, 8, and 12 (Table1). The study flowchart is shown in Fig.1.

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital (KYFY2025-SL398-01) and registered with the China Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500109335). The trial protocol will follow the SPIRIT(Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) statement. Furthermore, this study will report its intervention in line with the CONSORT-EHEALTH (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials of Electronic and Mobile Health Applications and Online Telehealth) guidelines [23]. Adherence to these internationally recognized standards will enhance the methodological transparency, reproducibility, and overall scientific credibility of the research.

This study will recruit participants from the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, China. Researchers will collaborate with hospital nurses to identify eligible older adults through referrals and poster advertisements. Initial interactions with potential participants will involve a preliminary assessment of IC using the WHO-ICOPE screening tool [4], which facilitates the rapid identification of individuals at risk of IC decline. Following a comprehensive understanding of each participant’s background, further screening will be conducted. For those meeting the inclusion criteria, researchers will provide a detailed explanation of the study procedures and obtain written informed consent. Participants and investigators will also exchange contact information to ensure timely communication in case of any questions or concerns.

Participants eligible for this study will be those aged 60 years or older, who have a WHO-ICOPE screening tool score of one point or higher (a score of ≥ 1 indicates a potential decline in IC), are fully conscious, able to understand instructions, and capable of cooperating in the completion of competency tests and functional assessments. In addition, must be able to use a smartphone or tablet and be willing to provide written informed consent.

Participants will be excluded if they are in an unstable medical condition or in the active phase of a mental disorder; have severe illnesses that significantly impair IC (e.g., osteoarthritis, cancer cachexia, or traumatic brain injury–related dementia); are unable to complete questionnaires due to factors such as acute illness, cognitive impairment, blindness, deafness, or limited proficiency in Chinese, or they refuse to participate in the study.

Based on results of similar published studies [24], we hypothesize that the mean IC scores for the intervention and control groups post-intervention will be 6.68 and 5.87, respectively, with standard deviations of 1.60 and 1.28. Using conventional Cohen’s effect size criteria, a benchmark effect size of d = 0.56 was determined. With a preset al.pha level of 0.05 and statistical power of 0.8, G*Power version 3.1.9.7 calculated the minimum required sample size per group to be 52 participants. Accounting for a potential 20% dropout rate, the final sample size was determined to be 65 participants per group, a total of 130 participants.

A total of 130 eligible participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the control group (65 participants per group). Randomization will be conducted using a computerized random number generator. Each participant will be assigned an identification number, and a random number will be generated for each participant using the RAND function in Excel, paired with their identification number. All random numbers will be sorted in ascending order, with the top 65 numbers assigned to the intervention group and the bottom 65 numbers assigned to the control group. To ensure allocation concealment, a sealed envelope with sequential numbering will be employed. Envelopes will be prepared and maintained by an independent data custodian not involved in the trial. Upon completion of participant enrollment, researchers will contact the custodian by telephone in numerical order to obtain each participant’s group assignment.

Due to fundamental differences in intervention format and delivery between the multi-domain digital platform used in this study and the control condition, blinding of intervention providers and participants was not feasible. To minimize measurement and analytical bias, outcome assessors and statisticians will remain blinded to the allocation sequence generation and group assignments and will not be involved in intervention implementation.

Researchers will develop corresponding intervention plans for each of the five domains of IC. After finalizing these plans, an expert meeting will be convened to collectively review and refine the protocols. The expert panel will comprise three geriatric physicians, two geriatric nurses, one nutritionist, and one rehabilitation therapist. Following the establishment of the multi-domain intervention plan for older adults’ IC, personalized adjustments will be implemented based on participants’ specific IC status. Prior to the official launch of the platform, we conducted a small-scale pilot test. By gathering user feedback and simultaneously optimizing the platform through iterative updates, we validated and enhanced its usability.

The first step of this study will involve using the WHO-ICOPE screening tool to conduct preliminary assessments of older adults, enabling rapid identification of those at risk of IC decline. The second step will entail conducting more in-depth evaluations across all IC domains for individuals with abnormal screening results to understand their specific circumstances. Following the assessment phase, personalized multi-domain intervention plans will be formulated for each participant. In the fourth step, professional interventions will be delivered via a smart-care platform. In the fifth step, community members and family caregivers will be encouraged to participate in the care process.

The Smart-Care program will be developed by our research team within the WeChat ecosystem. Users will be able to access the program by following and registering through the WeChat app on their smartphones. The core functional modules were designed based on extensive relevant literature and qualitative assessments of need of target users. The platform will include eight functional modules: Health Records, Health Assessment Form, Health Monitoring, Risk Alerts, Daily Check-ins, Disease Knowledge Guidance, Home Care, and Health Consultations, providing users with comprehensive health services. The control group will receive routine health management services, which will include assessing the health status of older adults and providing corresponding health guidance and education. Monthly telephone follow-ups will be conducted to provide additional guidance and support.

The intervention group will receive personalized, multi-domain intervention plans via a smart-care tailored to their individual health conditions (Table2). The implementation of personalized services is based on a set of rules. First, the platform identifies declining domains of IC and types of chronic diseases from completed health records, assigning corresponding labels. By recognizing these specific labels, the platform automatically pushes relevant health information to participants. Simultaneously, researchers use the platform to monitor participants’ health status in real time, enabling them to freely combine and adjust intervention measures as needed. After assisting participants with initial registration on the smart-care platform, researchers will provide each participant with a guidebook for platform usage and an intelligent monitoring wristband.

Researchers will train participants to ensure their proficiency in utilizing all features of the smart-care platform and the intelligent monitoring wristband. Training will be delivered through either in-person group sessions or individualized online instruction. Upon completion of the training, participants will undertake multi-domain interventions comprising cognitive training, physical exercise, and nutritional guidance. The intervention team will include geriatric physicians, geriatric nurses, nutritionists, the principal investigator, and researchers. Participants will be required to check in on the platform and report their progress after each training session, receiving feedback from the platform’s medical personnel. Each intervention session will last a minimum of 45 min, be conducted two to three times per week, and will be implemented in conjunction with the smart-care platform. Participants will upload their health and dietary data to the platform weekly, allowing researchers to promptly modify intervention plans as necessary.

To encourage family members and community members to join and create a supportive environment for participants, the platform includes an “Add Member” function that allows participants to authorize family accounts. Family caregivers can conveniently view participants’ basic health status to provide necessary supervision and reminders. Additionally, the platform features a “Care Services” module where certified community workers can post local community events and interact with participants online.

According to international exercise guidelines for older adults, individuals should engage in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, supplemented with strength and balance training [25]. Recent evidence indicates that multi-component exercise is the most effective approach to maintaining and enhancing health among older adults [26]. Accordingly, this study will design a multicomponent exercise program based on the Asia-Pacific Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Frailty [27], incorporating resistance, endurance, balance, and flexibility training. Participants will engage in exercise sessions twice per week, each lasting at least 60 min. The program will include the following components: (a) a 10-minute warm-up (e.g., marching in place, shoulder and neck mobility exercises); (b) 20 min of resistance training (e.g., resistance band exercises); (c) 10 min of aerobic exercise (e.g., brisk walking, jogging); (d) 10 min of balance training (e.g., single-leg stance, toe–heel stand, or Tai Chi); and (e) 10 min of full-body stretching (e.g., shoulder-to-foot and upper back stretches). This exercise program will be tailored to individual participants’ abilities, with the type and intensity of training adjusted as necessary. Participants will be required to wear the provided smart wristbands during exercise sessions, enabling medical personnel to monitor their physiological status in real time through the digital platform. In addition, relevant exercise videos and instructional materials will be uploaded to the platform for access by both participants and their family members, encouraging family engagement in assisting and supervising the older adults’ exercise routines.

According to the 2019Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE)guidelines, cognitive decline can be mitigated or reversed through cognitive training and social engagement [4]. In this study, cognitive training content will be developed based on previous research [12,28], incorporating activities such as word association exercises, numerical calculations, and brain-stimulating games (e.g., chess, puzzle solving). These materials will be made available on the wellness platform for older adults to practice twice weekly, with each session lasting 15–30 min.

In terms of nutrition, participants will be encouraged to upload photos of their daily meals to the platform at least twice per week. Based on theHealthy ageing and Longevity Dietary Strategiesguidelines [29], medical professionals on the platform will provide individualized dietary recommendations tailored to each participant’s health condition and eating habits. Each nutritional consultation will last approximately 10–15 min.

Drawing on previous studies [30,31], the psychological intervention component will include one to two group-sharing sessions per week, each lasting approximately 30 min, during which participants can discuss personal interests or seek health-related advice. In addition, mindfulness practice videos and audio materials—such as breathing exercises and guided meditations—will be uploaded to the platform to support emotional well-being. The platform’s social interaction features, including sharing, commenting, and “liking,” are designed to encourage communication and mutual support among older adults.

This questionnaire will be used to collect participants’ demographic information, including age, sex, marital status, educational level, occupation, living conditions, and economic status.

The 2019 World Health Organization (WHO)Integrated Care for Older People(ICOPE) assessment tool, developed based on the theoretical framework of theInternational Classification of Functioning,Disability and Health(ICF) [4], evaluates five domains of IC. The tool consists of nine screening items that assess physical and mental function, including the chair rise test, unintentional weight loss, decreased appetite, disorientation, memory impairment, depressed mood, loss of interest, and impairments in vision or hearing. Each domain is assigned one point, with the sensory domain encompassing both vision and hearing, resulting in a total possible score ranging from 0 to 9. A score of ≥ 1 indicates a potential decline in IC.

The ICOPE tool provides a brief and practical approach for evaluating functional ability in older adults. Its simplicity, time efficiency, and feasibility make it particularly suitable for use in primary healthcare settings, where it serves as a rapid screening instrument to identify early declines in functional capacity.

IC composite score serves as the primary outcome indicator, but its current assessment will be based on the five domains proposed by the WHO in 2019: cognition, mobility, vitality, psychology, and sensation [4]. The most common method for calculating the comprehensive score for IC involves assigning values to scores in each domain and then summing them [32]. Each domain is directly scored (0 points = severely impaired, 1 point = mild to moderate impairment, 2 points = normal), followed by summation [33]. The total score ranges from 0 to 10 points: 9–10 points indicate stable IC, 6–8 points indicate mild to moderate decline, and ≤ 5 points indicate severe decline. The following scales measure each domain of IC, and their scores are used to calculate the IC composite score.

The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) will be used for cognitive assessment. Developed by Folstein et al. in 1975 [34], this scale evaluates cognitive function and monitors progression in patients with cognitive impairment. The MMSE comprises five domains and 30 items, with a maximum score of 30 points. Higher scores indicate better cognitive performance, and scores below 24 are considered indicative of cognitive impairment. The Chinese version of the MMSE demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.833 [35], indicating good test-retest reliability and internal consistency. It also offers a brief assessment time, relatively straightforward content, and ease of administration.

The 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) will be utilized to evaluate the psychological status of older adults. Originally developed by Yesavage and colleagues in 1982 [36], the GDS-15 consists of 15 dichotomous (yes/no) items designed to screen for depressive symptoms among older populations. The total score ranges from 0 to 15, with scores of 0–5 indicating normal mood and scores of ≥ 5 suggesting the presence of depressive symptoms. Among Chinese older adults, the scale has demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.793 [37]. The GDS-15 is brief, easy to administer, and features simple, age-appropriate language, making it a practical and reliable tool for use in geriatric assessments.

We will employ the Short-form Mini-nutritional Assessment (MNA-SF) to evaluate the functional capacity domain. Developed by Rubenstein and colleagues in 2001 to streamline the original MNA scale [38], the MNA-SF substantially reduces assessment time. This six-item questionnaire includes dietary changes, weight fluctuations, stress, neuropsychiatric conditions, mobility, and body mass index. The maximum score is 14, with higher scores indicating better nutritional status. Scores between 8 and 11 suggest a risk of malnutrition, whereas scores below 8 indicate malnutrition. Among Chinese older adults, the scale has demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.703 [39]. The MNA-SF is simple to administer, time-efficient, and exhibits high accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, making it an appropriate tool for use in older adults.

To assess physical function, we will use the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). Developed by Guralnik and colleagues in 1994 [40], the SPPB consists of three components: a balance test, a walking speed test, and a chair rise test. The total score is calculated as the sum of the three component scores, ranging from 0 to 12 points, with higher scores indicating better physical performance. The scale demonstrates a sensitivity and specificity of 0.88 [41]. As an instrument for evaluating physical function in older adults, the SPPB provides a comprehensive assessment and has demonstrated good validity and reliability.

When assessing the sensory domains (vision and hearing), we will employ a self-report format. Measurement will be conducted using two questions: (a) participants who experience difficulties in daily activities due to vision loss will be classified as having visual impairment, and (b) participants who experience difficulties in daily activities due to hearing loss will be classified as having hearing impairment.

The Activities of Daily Living [42] scale will be used to assess participants’ ability to perform daily activities independently. Developed by Lawton and Brody in 1969 in the United States [43], the scale comprises two components: the Physical Self-Maintenance Scale (PSMS) and the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (IADL), for a total of 14 items. Scores range from 14 to 56, with higher scores indicating greater impairment in ADL. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.843 among Chinese older adults [44].

The World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) will be used to assess participants’ quality of life [45]. This instrument comprises four domains: physical health, psychological functioning, social relationships, and environment, with a total of 26 items. Standardized scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. The scale demonstrates high reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.90 [46] and strong construct validity.

The eHealth Literacy Scale (eHealth) will be used to assess participants’ ability and confidence in using information technology to access health-related information. Developed by Norman and Skinner in 2006 [47], the scale consists of eight items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater eHealth literacy. The scale demonstrates good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.913 among Chinese older adults [37].

Data collectors will explain the study objectives, questionnaire instructions, precautions, and confidentiality principles to participants prior to data collection. They will administer the questionnaires item by item, ensuring that participants understand each question and respond accurately and truthfully. During the survey, data collectors will complete the questionnaires objectively and accurately. Any omissions or obvious errors will be promptly corrected or supplemented, with confirmation from the participant. Each completed questionnaire will undergo on-site verification to ensure data integrity and accuracy. To maintain high-quality follow-up, appointment reminders will be sent to participants two weeks in advance to confirm follow-up times. Follow-up will be conducted either in person or via online video, depending on participants’ circumstances and preferences, to optimize data collection. All data will subsequently be entered independently by two individuals using EpiData3.1 software.

Statistical analysis will be performed using SPSS version 22.0. Baseline characteristics will be summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables with normal distribution will be presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables will be described using median and interquartile range, with intergroup comparisons conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables will be represented by frequency and percentage, with intergroup comparisons performed using the chi-square (χ²) test.

Primary and secondary outcome measures will be analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). The 2 (intervention group, control group) × 4 (T1, T2, T3, T4) mixed design will be employed to test for interaction effects between group and time. A significant interaction effect indicates differing trends between groups, suggesting the intervention is effective. Before analysis, Mauchly’s sphericity test will be conducted. If data violate sphericity assumptions (p< 0.05), the Greenhouse-Geisser correction will be applied. To control Type I errors from multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni correction will be implemented. All hypothesis tests will employ two-tailed tests at a significance level of α = 0.05, wherep< 0.05 indicates statistically significant differences.

As the global population ageing accelerates, living longer but less healthily has emerged as a core public health challenge [3]. In recent years, a growing body of scientific research has demonstrated that IC not only influences the quality of life and independence of older adults [8] but also serves as a significant predictor of adverse health outcomes [48,49]. Although most previous studies have demonstrated that multi-domain interventions can effectively improve the intrinsic capacity (IC) of older adults [26], traditional intervention models face significant challenges in the context of a rapidly growing elderly population, limited healthcare resources, and the diverse health needs of older adults [1,14]. These challenges primarily affect the accessibility, efficiency, and sustainability of conventional multi-domain interventions.

To address the challenges above, this study will draw on the experience of completed and ongoing multi-domain intervention studies conducted internationally [28,30] to develop a tailored multi-domain intervention program for older adults. The intervention will be implemented through a smart-care platform developed by our research team. This platform integrates eight functional modules, including health monitoring, personalized health guidance (covering diet, exercise, and cognition), daily check-ins, social interaction support, and consultation feedback. These digital features enable multi-domain interventions targeting older adults’ intrinsic capacity (IC), effectively addressing issues such as uneven resource allocation and low intervention efficiency associated with traditional approaches, thereby improving overall care effectiveness. Compared with conventional models, the smart-care platform offers a distinctive advantage through its capacity for continuous health data monitoring. This allows dynamic adjustments to intervention strategies based on real-time user health status to enhance precision. Previous studies have also shown that continuous monitoring of physiological parameters facilitates early detection of abnormalities and timely modification of care plans, ultimately improving chronic disease management outcomes [16,20]. Additionally, the platform’s integration of health guidance, daily check-ins, and consultation functions enables remote, continuous health management, effectively reducing labor and medical costs while mitigating regional disparities in healthcare resources. Research further indicates that digital remote interventions foster sustained interaction between patients and healthcare teams, decreasing the frequency of in-person visits [50]. This approach is particularly beneficial for individuals residing in remote areas or with limited mobility. Ultimately, the digital service model enhances the scalability and sustainability of interventions—an advantage that became especially evident during the COVID-19 pandemic [18,50]—and lays a solid foundation for large-scale implementation and adoption in the future.

Although digital health tools have the potential to enhance intervention effectiveness, extensive evidence indicates that older adults face substantial digital exclusion, which limits the equity and accessibility of digital health services [21,51]. Barriers such as inadequate digital skills and low self-efficacy contribute to the “digital divide,” preventing individuals with limited digital literacy from benefiting from these technologies and potentially exacerbating existing health inequalities [51,52]. Within this context, establishing the intrinsic efficacy of the intervention requires implementation under feasible and controlled conditions. Accordingly, this study focuses on older adults who are able to use smart devices, with the primary aim of evaluating the core effectiveness of a multi-domain digital intervention in improving intrinsic capacity (IC). In parallel, e-health literacy is included as an outcome measure to assess whether the intervention enhances IC by strengthening participants’ ability to access and utilize digital health resources.

Current research leveraging digital tools to empower multi-domain interventions for enhancing older adults’ IC remains limited. Existing studies demonstrate that multi-domain interventions can improve older adults’ IC, with particularly significant effects observed in psychological and cognitive domains [28,53]. However, previous studies have primarily relied on centralized, offline implementation models, which inherently constrain their scalability. Moreover, the duration and frequency of interventions have often been limited by human resources, time, and geographic factors. However, the present study leverages digital technologies to overcome these temporal and spatial barriers, thereby enhancing the accessibility, continuity, and sustainability of interventions. It is anticipated that this research will extend beyond the limitations of earlier studies, contributing innovative and impactful insights to healthy ageing.

The study also has certain limitations. First, as a single-center study, the sample was drawn from one tertiary hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the conclusions to populations across different regions and levels of medical care. In addition, the inclusion criterion requiring participants to be able to use smart devices was implemented to ensure intervention feasibility, which inevitably excluded individuals with limited digital proficiency. Future studies will expand sample diversity through multicenter collaboration and further optimize the platform to improve the representativeness and equity of the findings. Secondly, the acceptance and usage adherence of older adults toward the smart-care platform may present challenges, potentially leading to participants withdrawing from the study and attrition may affect experimental outcomes. To mitigate this issue, platform usage will be monitored weekly. Participants who fail to use the platform for one week will receive reminder notifications and be invited to provide feedback. Based on this feedback, the platform will be continuously optimized, and dedicated personnel will be assigned to assist participants in resolving technical difficulties. In addition, family members will be encouraged to participate in supervision to further reduce attrition rates. Such advancements will enable the continuous optimization of digital platforms, reducing operational difficulties for older adults and enhancing their confidence and engagement with digital health tools. Second, greater emphasis should be placed on strengthening community engagement and resource integration. Leveraging digital technologies to establish social support networks for older adults and promoting intergenerational collaboration can further enhance their acceptance of digital tools and improve their IC. Furthermore, to address limitations related to resource constraints and workforce shortages, it is essential to optimize training processes through digital solutions, thereby improving efficiency and reducing labor costs. Finally, continued efforts from researchers are required to refine these approaches, expand their scope of application, and validate their long-term effectiveness in diverse populations and settings.

This study protocol proposes a study that combines a smart-care platform with a multi-domain intervention to examine its effectiveness in enhancing IC of older adults. This approach enables personalized, efficient health management and is expected to positively impact IC of older adults while reducing the burden on caregivers and the healthcare system. Should this intervention model demonstrate effectiveness in this study, future work will focus on expanding its coverage and accessibility, particularly in underserved or rural areas.
