# Mutational spectrum in a Chinese cohort with congenital cataracts.
**Authors:** Liu HL, Zhang DW, Hu FY, Xu P, Zhang SH, Wu JH
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.2196](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.2196)

## Content

BACKGROUND: To identify the mutational spectrum in a Chinese cohort with congenital cataracts.

METHODS: Probands (n = 164) with congenital cataracts and their affected or unaffected available family members were recruited for clinical examinations and panel-based next-generation sequencing, then classified into a cohort for further mutational analysis.

RESULTS: After recruitment (n = 442; 228 males and 214 females), 49.32% (218/442) of subjects received a clinical diagnosis of congenital cataracts, and 56.88% (124/218) of patients received a molecular diagnosis. Eighty-four distinct variants distributed among 43 different genes, including 42 previously reported variants and 42 novel variants, were detected, and 49 gene variants were causally associated with patient phenotypes; 27.37% of variants (23/84) were commonly detected in PAX6, GJA8 and CRYGD, and the three genes covered 33.06% of cases (41/124) with molecular diagnosis. The majority of genes were classified as genes involved in nonsyndromic congenital cataracts (19/43, 44.19%) and were responsible for 56.45% of cases (70/124). The majority of functional and nucleotide changes were missense variants (53/84, 63.10%) and substitution variants (74/84, 88.10%), respectively. Nine de novo variants were identified.

CONCLUSION: This study provides a reference for individualized genetic counseling and further extends the mutational spectrum of congenital cataracts.

Congenital cataract, a unilateral or bilateral disease associated with lens abnormality, occurs in the early stage of life and during vision development (Foster, 1993; Gilbert & Foster, 2001; Jain et al., 1983). Serious vision loss caused by congenital cataracts accounts for 7.4%–15.5% of all childhood blindness (Gilbert et al., 1993), and the incidence was reported to be 7.43/10,000 in Asia (Wu et al., 2016). Although the disease can be caused by hereditary or nonhereditary factors (Medsinge & Nischal, 2015), genetic mutation remains the common cause, and the global prevalence of inherited cataracts was reported to be 22.3% (Wu et al., 2016). Hereditary pediatric cataracts may be associated with various diseases, including metabolic diseases, renal diseases, musculoskeletal diseases, dermatological diseases, and craniofacial anomalies. However, nonhereditary pediatric cataracts are attributed to trauma, congenital infections, drugs, radiation, or teratogen exposure (Rahi et al., 1995).

Reddy et al. (2004) first described the cosegregation of inherited cataracts with the Duffy blood group locus in 1963 [OMIM accession number 110700]. The ensuing improvement of advanced molecular biological techniques has facilitated the identification of disease‐associated variants. According to the data released in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD, https://apps.ingenuity.com) and Cat‐Map database (https://cat‐map.wustl.edu/), previous studies have reported more than 400 distinct genes (updated April 1, 2022) linked to inherited and age‐related forms of cataracts in humans, mice, and other vertebrates, including various crystallin genes, connexin genes, transcription factor genes, and others (Reis & Semina, 2019; Shiels & Hejtmancik, 2015, 2017). Mutations detected in the abovementioned genes may cause abnormalities in crystallin, lens membrane proteins, lens cytoskeletal elements, etc., thereby causing the occurrence of disease.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the clinical and genetic characteristics in this large Chinese cohort, which may further extend the mutational spectrum. Overall, determining the detailed genetic characterization in a Chinese cohort is highly beneficial for ophthalmologists, as it allows clinicians to better understand congenital cataracts and to offer individualized genetic counseling. Meanwhile, for women with planned pregnancies, the genetic diagnosis of congenital cataracts in a family would be helpful in avoiding risks. Moreover, the identification of the mutational spectrum in congenital cataracts is an important step towards understanding the molecular etiology, which may lead to the development of a medical therapy to slow lens opacification (Haddad et al., 2006; Pichi et al., 2016).

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University. This study conformed to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their guardians.

All of the patients had Chinese descent and were enrolled from the Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University between October 2016 and December 2021. One hundred and sixty‐four patients with congenital cataracts, also called “probands,” were initially examined in the hospital. All subjects (n = 442), including 164 probands and their affected or unaffected available family members, were recruited for clinical examination and panel‐based next‐generation sequencing to improve the rate of molecular diagnosis. All probands and their affected family members were classified as a cohort for detailed genetic characterization.

The following information of all subjects was recorded: age, sex, geographic distribution, family history of congenital cataract, and family history of consanguineous marriage. All subjects were clinically diagnosed by an experienced ophthalmologist according to the clinical symptoms and ophthalmologic examinations, including best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and slit‐lamp biomicroscopy.

Specialized panel (Beijing Genomics Institute, Shenzhen, China)‐based next‐generation sequencing containing 792 causative genes [See Additional File 1] involved in common genetic eye disease was performed on all subjects. First, genomic DNA was acquired from the venous blood of recruited subjects. Then, as in previous studies (Gao et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2019), the genetic information of the recruited subjects was further processed.

In brief, a high‐throughput target enrichment approach was crafted to capture the exons and untranslated regions (UTRs) of causative genes. The distinct parts of genomic DNA, including the coding exons, flanking intronic areas and promoter regions, were captured utilizing the Agilent SureSelect Target Enrichment Kit (Agilent Technologies, Inc., USA). Meanwhile, the MGISEQ‐2000 platform (BGI, Inc., Shenzhen, China) was used for the deep sequencing of enriched genomic libraries in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA, http://bio‐bwa.sourceforge.net/) was utilized for mapping the obtained sequences against the reference human genome (hg38).

Finally, genetic variants were further interpreted. Overall, four information bases for clarification of the identified variations were as follows: 1000 Genomes Project (http://browser.1000genomes.org/), dbSNP (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/SNP/), ESP6500 (http://evs.gs.washington.edu/EVS/), and ExAC (http://exac.broadinstitute.org). Variations with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.1% were further determined to be potentially deleterious. Moreover, the following three online websites were utilized to predict potential pathogenic variations: Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant (SIFT, http://sift.jcvi.org/), Polymorphism Phenotyping v2 (PolyPhen‐2, http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), and MutationTaster programming (http://www.mutationtaster.org/). Then, the pathogenicity of the screening variations was further identified according to the released data on the following three online websites: ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), HGMD (Human Gene Mutation Database, http://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php), and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, http://www.omim.org/). In accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) and genomics guidelines, the remaining variants were classified as pathogenic variants, likely pathogenic variants, variants of uncertain significance (VUS), benign variants, and likely benign variants. The genetic diagnosis of the patients was determined according to the clinical phenotype and genetic variant interpretation.

The genes detected in this study were also classified into the following groups based on gene function: crystallin genes (CRYAA, CRYAB, CRYBA1, CRYBB1, CRYBB2, CRYBB3, CRYGC, CRYGD, and CRYGS); connexin genes (GJA1, GJA3, and GJA8); transcription factor genes (PAX6, FOXE3, HSF4, MAF and PITX3); cytoskeletal genes (BFSP1 and BFSP2); and various other genes.

A total of 442 subjects (228 males and 214 females) were included in this study, including 54 unrelated families and 110 sporadic cases. The majority of probands were from eastern China (104/164, 63.41%). In addition, a positive family history of congenital cataract was found in 32.93% of the probands (54/164), and the pedigree charts in the familial cases with congenital cataract are presented in Additional File 2; a history of infection during pregnancy occurred in 3.05% of the probands (5/164); consanguineous marriage was observed in 2.44% of the probands (4/164).

The detailed information of each subject is shown in Additional File 3. Overall, 49.32% of subjects (218/442) were classified into the Chinese cohort with congenital cataracts, and 50.68% of subjects (224/442) showed normal lens phenotypes. The basic information of each patient is presented in Additional File 4. The average age of the patients was 17.03 ± 18.81 years (n = 218), ranging from 1 to 93 years old. The majority of patients were younger than 10 years (123/218, 56.42%), followed by those between 20 and 40 years (57/218, 26.15%).

A total of 82.57% of patients (180/218) received a presumptive clinical diagnosis of binocular cataract, and 17.43% of patients (38/218) were clinically diagnosed with monocular cataract. Other common conditions related to the eye included amblyopia, strabismus, and nystagmus, which could be secondary to untreated cataracts. In addition, 56.88% of patients (124/218) received a molecular diagnosis. Specifically, 65.00% of patients (117/180) with bilateral cataracts received a molecular diagnosis; 18.42% of patients (7/38) with monocular cataracts received a molecular diagnosis; one patient with a history of infection (1/5) received a molecular diagnosis; 81.48% of patients (44/54) with a family history received a molecular diagnosis; and 33.64% of patients (37/110) without a family history received a molecular diagnosis. Among these, the clinical diagnosis of five patients was finally corrected into bilateral spherophakia (Patient No. 85, 86 and 101) or bilateral ectopia lentis (Patient No. 170 and 197) according to the molecular diagnosis.

As shown in Additional File 4, a total of 168 variants were detected among the patients, including 84 distinct variants, and detailed information on each variant and its in silico functional assessment is presented in Additional File 5. Fifty percent of variants (42/84) were reported previously, and 50.00% of variants (42/84) were novel variants. One mutation found in CRYGD (NM_006891.3: c.70C>A) was identified as a high‐frequency mutation in families, which was detected in 2.44% of families (4/164, Family No. 56, 77, 89, and 108). According to the classification of variants in three databases (HGMD/ClinVar/InterVar), 44.05% of variants (37/84) were classified as disease‐causing mutations (DMs) in HGMD, which means that these variants have been reported in at least one study as causing disease; 29.76% of variants (25/84) were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in ClinVar; 21.43% of variants (18/84) were both classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic in ClinVar and DM in HGMD. In accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) and genomics guidelines, 49 gene variants were causally associated with patient phenotypes in this study, and the remaining variants were classified as VUS. The inheritance of the annotated variant gene was reported as autosomal dominant (57/84, 67.86%), autosomal recessive (10/84, 11.90%), X‐linked (6/84, 7.14%), or autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive (11/84, 13.10%) in OMIM.

All variants were classified in accordance with the standard gene variant nomenclature provided by the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS, http://www.HGVS.org/varnomen) and the Sequence Ontology (SO, http://www.sequenceontology.org/). The majority of variants were classified as missense (53/84, 63.10%). In addition, there were frameshift (13/84, 15.48%), nonsense (9/84, 10.71%), splice_donor (3/84, 3.57%), deletion (2/84, 2.38%), inframe_deletion (1/84, 1.19%), splice_acceptor (1/84, 1.19%), missense+splice_site (1/84, 1.19%), and inframe_deletion+splice_site (1/84, 1.19%) mutations. The majority of nucleotide changes were substitutions (67/84, 79.76%), followed by deletions (11/84, 13.10%), insertions (4/84, 4.76%), and duplications (2/84, 2.38%).

Eighty‐four distinct variants were detected in 43 genes. Mutations found in four genes, ADAMTS17, CYP1B1, TSC1 and TSC2, were not previously associated with cataracts in either the HGMD database or the Cat‐Map database. It was found that 27.37% of variants were commonly detected in PAX6 (10/84, 11.90%), GJA8 (8/84, 9.52%), and CRYGD (5/84, 5.95%), which covered 33.06% of cases (41/124) with molecular diagnosis. Moreover, 87.50% of GJA8 variants (7/8) were identified in exon 2, 80.00% of CRYGD variants (4/5) were detected in exon 2, and 50.00% of PAX6 variants (5/10) were detected in exon 5.

The cataract‐related variants identified in this study were shown in Table 1. And these genes were classified into the following five groups according to a previous study (Messina‐Baas & Cuevas‐Covarrubias, 2017) and the gene–phenotype relationships released on the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, http://www.omim.org/). The majority of genes were classified as genes involved in nonsyndromic congenital cataract (19/43, 44.19%) and responsible for 56.45% of cases (70/124) with molecular diagnosis, followed by genes involved in congenital cataracts with other eye defects (7/43, 16.28%), genes associated with syndromic congenital cataracts (13/43, 30.23%), genes responsible for syndromic congenital cataracts, which can also result in isolated congenital cataracts (3/43, 6.98%), and genes responsible for congenital cataracts with other eye defects, which can also result in isolated congenital cataracts (1/43, 2.33%). Based on gene function, the majority of genes were classified as crystallin genes (7/43, 16.28%), followed by connexin genes (2/43, 4.65%), transcription factor genes (4/43, 9.30%) and cytoskeletal genes (1/43, 2.33%). Crystallin genes were responsible for 32.26% of cases (40/124) with molecular diagnosis.

Forty‐two novel variants, including missense (22/42, 52.38%), frameshift (10/42, 23.81%), nonsense (5/42, 11.90%), deletion (1/42, 2.38%), inframe_deletion (1/42, 2.38%), splice_acceptor (1/42, 2.38%), missense+splice_site (1/84, 2.38%), and inframe_deletion+splice_site (1/84, 2.38%), were found in 38 patients, 81.58% of whom (31/38) were in a heterozygous state, while 10.53% were homozygous (4/38) and 7.89% were hemizygous (3/38). There were uncertain‐significance variants (20/42, 47.62%), likely pathogenic variants (17/42, 40.48%) and pathogenic variants (5/42, 11.90%). The uncertain‐significance variants mainly included missense variants (18/20, 90%), and the pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants mainly included frameshift (10/22, 45.45%), nonsense variants (5/22, 22.73%) and missense variants (5/22, 22.73%). Five rare unpublished missense (PM2) variants (GJA3 c.332 T>C; NHS c.74A>G; CRYGS c.1076 T>G; NHS c.176A>C; PAX6 c.3014G>A) received a classification of likely pathogenic with a combination of de novo (PS2).

Among patients with molecular diagnosis, 92.74% of patients (115/124) were in a heterozygous state, 4.84% (6/124) were homozygous and 2.42% (3/124) were hemizygous. A total of 8.06% of patients (10/124) presented with ≥2 disease‐associated variants, and 91.94% of patients (114/124) presented with one disease‐causing allele. In addition, 9 de novo variants were identified in this study, and detailed information on those variants is shown in Table 2.

The incidence of congenital cataracts depends on regional development and has been reported to be 1–6/10,000 in industrialized countries and 5–15/10,000 in economically weak countries (Apple et al., 2000). A great deal of similar work has been previously undertaken to determine the underlying mutations and different morphologies of hereditary cataracts. Ionides et al. (Ionides et al., 1999) classified cataract phenotypes into eight morphologies. Similarly, genetic analysis was previously performed in various cohorts, however, with limited cases. For example, the mutation profiles of congenital cataract genes were determined in 21 northern Chinese families (Zhang et al., 2018). Thirteen novel variants were also identified in the Swiss cohort (Rechsteiner et al., 2021), with 37 cases diagnosed with bilateral congenital cataracts. Researchers in Brazil (Santana et al., 2009), Britain (Berry et al., 2020, 2021; Gillespie et al., 2014), America (Reis et al., 2013) and Spain (Fernández‐Alcalde et al., 2021) also reported limited genes that were associated with congenital cataracts cases. Although the causes of congenital cataracts differ, this form of disease is classified into two main groups: hereditary and nonhereditary congenital cataracts. It was found that 8%–25% of congenital cataracts corresponded to inherited cataracts (Pichi et al., 2016). This study contained a Chinese cohort with congenital cataracts (n = 164), and a total of 442 subjects received genetic analysis and clinical interpretation. We mainly draw the following conclusions. First, the positive rate of genetic testing in this Chinese cohort was determined to be 56.88%, and this figure was consistent with the data presented by a team of Spanish researchers (49%) (Fernández‐Alcalde et al., 2021); however, it was an obviously lower figure compared with that reported by a team of British researchers (75%) (Gillespie et al., 2014). This finding reflected the combined effects of the panel and the various nonhereditary factors. Whole‐exon sequencing may further improve the positive rate of genetic testing. Moreover, the certain positive rate of genetic testing in a cohort of phenotypically normal subjects may be related to the incomplete penetrance of the gene (Reis et al., 2013), thus demonstrating a potential need for in‐depth research on the changes, specific genes, predicted effects on the protein products and lens, and inheritance patterns in the families. Meanwhile, the prevalence of variants in unaffected individuals probably also relates to noncausative changes. An intron or structural variant may occur in the other strand, thus potentially showing that the heterozygous state in some causes a recessive disease; this is a limitation of panel‐based next‐generation sequencing. Second, the positive rate of genetic testing showed a large difference between patients with binocular congenital cataracts and patients with monocular congenital cataracts (65.00% versus 18.42%), in agreement with the previous data reported by Rahi et al (Rahi et al., 1995). They found that 27% of Indian children with bilateral congenital cataracts had a genetic basis compared with 2% of those with unilateral cataracts. Meanwhile, it was found that there was a geographic bias for families with congenital cataracts. Obviously, most families in this study were from economically developed areas, which may be related to various factors, including unknown infectious and traumatic causes.

In general, hereditary cataracts comprise syndromic and nonsyndromic cataracts. If the lens and other various organs are implicated in the pathological process, it is defined as syndromic cataracts. In this study, 43 distinct genes were identified, and the majority of genes directly caused nonsyndromic congenital cataracts, which was also observed in the British cohort (Gillespie et al., 2014). A total of four genes that were not previously associated with cataracts in two databases were found in this study, perhaps as “unknown significance” or mutations in unknown cataract genes. The high‐frequency genes were PAX6, CRYGD, and GJA8, accounting for 27.37%. In general, mutations identified in distinct genes encoding the main cytoplasmic or developmental proteins of the human lens play crucial roles in the various morphologies of cataracts. Many reviews (Kannabiran & Balasubramanian, 2000; Shiels & Hejtmancik, 2013) have summarized genes associated with congenital cataracts, including genes encoding crystallins, lens‐specific connexins, aquaporin, cytoskeletal structural proteins, paired‐like homeodomain transcription factor 3, avian musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma, and heat shock transcription factor 4. The PAX6 protein, a developmental protein required for the eye, is commonly present in the neural tube, areas of the developing forebrain and hindbrain, eye, olfactory epithelium, pituitary, and cerebellum. Mutations in PAX6 caused 9 distinct diseases, and a total of 155 variants have been identified in PAX6 (ending October 12, 2021) thus far. Aniridia (Traboulsi, 2010) is a syndrome that includes cataracts, followed by iris hypoplasia, absence of fovea, and associated abnormalities. In this study, it was found that the majority of family cases of PAX6 mutations were accompanied by iris coloboma. In addition, two patients were accompanied by corneal dystrophy, which was ultimately diagnosed as cataracts with late‐onset corneal dystrophy. The CRYGD protein was encoded by the crystallin gene. It is well known that crystallin proteins, accounting for more than 90% of total lens proteins, ensure the best clarity and sharpness presentation of the human lens. To date, 119 variants have been identified in CRYGD, and mutations in 10 crystallin genes have been identified as hereditary causes of congenital cataracts (Berry et al., 2020). In this study, the majority of genes were classified as crystallin genes (16.28%), which is not entirely consistent with other similar studies from different populations (5%–38.46%) (Devi et al., 2008; Hansen et al., 2009; Kumar et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2011). Unlike the above two genes, GJA8 was classified as a connexin gene. Connexins usually function as gap junction channels, which makes intercellular communication possible. In addition to the above conclusion, we also found that the majority of nucleotide and amino acid changes were substitutions and missense, respectively.

There were certain limitations in this study. Considering that some patients underwent surgery for congenital cataracts, we failed to characterize the detailed clinical features of pediatric cataracts. Regretfully, patients without any molecular diagnosis were not subjected to more detailed testing with whole exome sequencing. Then, this large cohort of congenital cataracts was limited to the Chinese population and cannot be applied to other populations.

In conclusion, a total of 84 distinct variants were identified in this study, including 42 reported variants and 42 novel variants. These variants were identified in 43 distinct genes and commonly detected in PAX6, CRYGD, and GJA8, accounting for 27.37%. The majority of genes were classified as crystallin genes. In total, nine de novo variants were identified. This study further extended the mutational spectrum of congenital cataracts by performing detailed genetic characterization in a Chinese cohort, which may provide a genetic map for individualized genetic counseling.
