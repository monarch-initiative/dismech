# Cytokine production by bone marrow mononuclear cells in inherited bone marrow failure syndromes.
**Authors:** Matsui K, Giri N, Alter BP, Pinto LA
**Journal:** Br J Haematol (2013)
**DOI:** [10.1111/bjh.12475](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjh.12475)

## Content

Fanconi anaemia (FA), dyskeratosis congenita (DC), Diamond-Blackfan anaemia (DBA), and Shwachman-Diamond syndrome (SDS) are characterized by the progressive development of bone marrow failure. Overproduction of tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) from activated bone marrow T-cells has been proposed as a mechanism of FA-related aplasia. Whether such overproduction occurs in the other syndromes is unknown. We conducted a comparative study on bone marrow mononuclear cells to examine the cellular subset composition and cytokine production. We found lower proportions of haematopoietic stem cells in FA, DC, and SDS, and a lower proportion of monocytes in FA, DC, and DBA compared with controls. The T- and B-lymphocyte proportions were similar to controls, except for low B-cells in DC. We did not observe overproduction of TNF-α or IFN-γ by T-cells in any patients. Induction levels of TNF-α, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, IL-10, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor in monocytes stimulated with high-dose lipopolysaccharide (LPS) were similar at 4 h but lower at 24 h when compared to controls. Unexpectedly, patient samples showed a trend toward higher cytokine level in response to low-dose (0·001 μg/ml) LPS. Increased sensitivity to LPS may have clinical implications and could contribute to the development of pancytopenia by creating a chronic subclinical inflammatory micro-environment in the bone marrow.

Inherited bone marrow failure syndromes (IBMFS) are a group of rare genetic
disorders that are associated with increased risks of developing myelodysplastic
syndrome, acute myeloid leukaemia, and solid tumours (Shimamura & Alter 2010). The group includes Fanconi anaemia
(FA), dyskeratosis congenita (DC), Diamond-Blackfan anaemia (DBA), and
Shwachman-Diamond syndrome (SDS). Diverse sets of mutated genes with some functional
overlap have been identified for these disorders (Shimamura & Alter 2010). The biological functions of these genes
involve DNA repair (FA), ribosomal biogenesis (DBA, SDS, and DC
[DKC1 gene]), and telomere maintenance (DC) (Farrar & Dahl 2011; Savage & Bertuch 2010; Shimamura 2006; Shimamura & Alter
2010). Despite the differences in the affected biological functions,
these syndromes are characterized by high risks of multilineage bone marrow failure
(Shimamura & Alter 2010). This
includes DBA, which is classically a pure red cell aplasia (Shimamura & Alter 2010), but has been reported to develop
multilineage-defects in a small subset of patients (Giri et al, 2000). Patients with an IBMFS usually have
markedly hypocellular bone marrow, even when pancytopenia is absent.

Unlike acquired aplastic anaemia, IBMFS patients usually do not respond to
immunosuppressive treatment (Al-Rahawan et
al, 2006; Bagby et
al, 2004; Maciejewski &
Risitano 2003). However, subtle immunological alterations that are often
subclinical have been reported in some patients with IBMFS. Studies in FA have shown
low numbers of B- and Natural Killer (NK)-cells, reduced proliferative capacities of
T- and B-lymphocytes, and decreased cytotoxic activities in T- and NK-cells from
peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (Castello
et al, 1998; Fagerlie & Bagby 2006; Myers
et al, 2011). Overall, the levels of immunoglobulins
are generally not affected in FA patients (Myers
et al, 2011; Roxo
et al, 2001). An increase in tumour necrosis factor
-α (TNF-α) and γ-interferon (IFN-γ) production was
reported in activated bone marrow T-cells, and this overproduction was proposed as
one of the key mechanisms underlying haematopoietic stem cell (HSC) aplasia (Dufour et al, 2003). In
addition, peripheral blood monocytes from one FA patient showed a higher level of
TNF-α production in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Anur et al, 2012). Similar to
FA, defects in T- and B-cells from PBMCs have been reported in patients with SDS
(Dror 2005; Dror et al, 2001; Khan et al, 2008; Shah et al, 2009). These defects include low
IgG levels, low numbers of circulating T-, B-, and NK-cells, and proliferative
defects in T- and B-cells. Similar immune defects have also been reported for DC and
DBA (Cossu et al, 2002;
Jyonouchi et al, 2011;
Khan et al, 2011). These
immunological alterations may have important consequences for patients with IBMFS,
who may be more prone to environmental insults and are at increased risk of
developing cancer. However, because the diseases are rare, the sample sizes in the
aforementioned studies were relatively small, and the reported alterations were
heterogeneous and inconsistent among the patients. Further studies are required to
better understand the changes that occur in the immune system and the effect of
these alterations in patients with IBMFS.

Prior studies of the immune system in patients with IBMFS were mostly
performed on PBMCs; comprehensive studies on bone marrow cells have not been
reported in the literature. Bone marrow is the major site of haematopoiesis, and
B-cells and monocytes develop in this compartment. The bone marrow also contains
plasma cells and memory T-cells. The bone marrow is an important tissue for immunity
as it can contribute and provide support to the functions of innate and adaptive
immunity (Mercier et al,
2012; Zhao et al,
2012).

Taking advantage of the IBMFS Study at the National Cancer Institute, we have
conducted a comparative study of cytokine production by in
vitro-stimulated bone marrow mononuclear cells from patients with FA, DC,
DBA, or SDS. The main goal of our study was to examine whether T-cells and monocytes
from patients with IBMFS overproduce cytokines when compared with healthy
individuals. Published studies have shown that TNF-α inhibits the growth of
and causes apoptosis of HSCs (Broxmeyer et
al, 1986; Dybedal et
al, 2001). In addition, other studies have shown that
T-cells and monocytes in FA patients overproduce IFN-γ and/or TNF-α
(Anur et al, 2012; Dufour et al, 2003).
Therefore, we hypothesized that cytokine production may be dysregulated in patients
with FA and other IBMFS. Simultaneous comparison of the immune function of bone
marrow mononuclear cells from patients with FA, DC, DBA, or SDS showed that
intracellular production of IFN-γ and TNF-α by activated T-cells was
not higher in the syndrome groups. However, there was a trend toward enhanced innate
immune responses to low-dose LPS by monocytes in patients with FA, DC, DBA or
SDS.

Participants were recruited under the protocols approved by the
Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Institute (NCI Protocol
02-C-0052; NCT00027274). Written informed consent was provided in accordance
with the Declaration of Helsinki. Bone marrow aspirates from the posterior iliac
crest were collected from March 2002 to January 2012. Bone marrow mononuclear
cells from 16 FA (median age 16 years, range 3-59), 20 DC (median age 17 years,
range 3-69), 21 DBA (median age 11 years, range 3-47), and 7 SDS (median age 12
years, range 6-19) patients were used in the first series of experiments (Figs 1-3). For controls, bone marrow aspirates from 14 healthy adult volunteers
(median age 26 years, range 20-45) were obtained from Lonza (Walkersville, MD)
in April and May of 2012. The number of cells obtained from patients was limited
because of marrow hypocellularity; only 9 FA, 12 DC, 12 DBA, and 7 SDS patient
samples from the initial experiments had sufficient numbers of cells for testing
in the second series of experiments (Figs
4-5).

Low-density mononuclear cells were isolated using centrifugation through
Ficoll-Hypaque (1.077, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO. USA). Isolated cells were
resuspended in media with L-glutamine, penicillin/streptomycin, 12.5%
heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (all from
the American Type Culture Collection [ATCC], Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were
frozen using a controlled-rate-freezer (Kryosave, Integra, Planer plc,
Sunbury-on-Thames, UK), and immediately transferred into and maintained in the
vapour phase of a liquid nitrogen tank.

Reagent resources were as follows: Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA),
ionomycin, LPS: Sigma-Aldrich; Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated
anti-CD45 and phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-CD14 Simultest: BD Biosciences
(San Jose, CA, USA); Brefeldin A solution (1000×), 10×
permeabilization solution, antibodies to CD19 (PE-cyanin 7 [PC7]) and
IFN-γ (Alexa Fluor 488 [AF488]): eBioscience (San Diego, CA, USA);
Allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-CD34 and -CD14, PE-conjugated
anti-TNF-α and -TLR4 antibodies: BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA); and
Phycoerythrin-Texas Red (ECD)-conjugated CD3: Beckman Coulter (Brea, CA,
USA).

Polypropylene tubes (12 × 75 mm; BD Falcon, San Jose, CA, USA)
were used in the flow cytometry experiments, and samples were tested once within
each experiment. Bone marrow mononuclear cells were thawed in a 37°C
water bath, washed, and resuspended in AIM-V serum-free media (Invitrogen, Grand
Island, NY, USA). For surface staining, the cells were washed with wash buffer
(phosphate-buffered saline with 1% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum), and
incubated with mouse IgG (Chemicon, Temecula, CA, USA) for 10 min at 4°C,
then stained with anti-CD45 (FITC), -CD14 (PE), -CD3 (ECD), -CD34 (APC), and
-CD19 (PC7) antibodies for 30 min at 4°C. In other experiments, the cells
were stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD14 and PE-conjugated toll-like receptor
(TLR) 4 antibodies for 30 min at 4°C. For the intracellular IFN-γ
and TNF-α staining, the cells were incubated in AIM-V, 10 ng/ml of PMA
plus 0.5 μg/ml ionomycin, or 1 μg/ml of LPS for 4 h at
37°C. For the dose titration experiment, intracellular cytokine staining
was performed on cells stimulated with 1.0, 0.1, or 0.001 μg/ml of LPS
for 4 h. Brefeldin A was added at the start of the incubation. After washing,
the cells were incubated with mouse IgG for 10 min, and stained for CD3 (ECD),
CD14 (APC), and CD19 (PC7), or for CD14 (APC), at 4°C for 30 min. The
samples were washed, fixed (Optilyse C solution, Beckman Coulter) for 15 min on
ice, washed with wash buffer and permeabilization solution, followed by a 30-min
incubation with antibodies to IFN-γ (AF488) and TNF-α (PE). All
samples were collected using Cytomics FC500 cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and
data analysis was performed with FCS Express (De Novo Software, Los Angeles, CA,
USA).

In a 96-well tissue culture plate, 0.1 × 106
mononuclear cells/well were incubated with or without three different
concentrations (1.0, 0.1, or 0.001 μg/ml) of LPS in a final volume of 200
μl at 37°C for 4 or 24 h. Each sample was tested once. The plate
was centrifuged and 160 μl of supernatant was transferred into a new
96-well plate and stored at − 80°C.

To determine the concentration of cytokines, we employed the
Milliplex® Map Human Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel Immunoassay
Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). We tested for IFN-γ, TNF-α,
interleukin (IL)-10 (IL10), IL-1β (IL1B), IL-6 (IL6), granulocyte
colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF, CSF3) and granulocyte-macrophage
colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, CSF2), which are known to be secreted by
LPS-stimulated monocytes (Rossol et
al, 2011). In brief, the samples and standards were
tested in duplicates of 25 μl in an assay-specific 96-well plate. Assay
buffer (25 μl) was added to the sample wells and 25 μl AIM-V to
the standard, followed by 25 μl of a cocktail of antibody beads that
recognize the cytokines. The plate was protected from light and incubated at
4°C overnight on a shaker. The next day, after washing, 25 μl of
detection antibody was added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature
for 1 h on a shaker. Without washing, an additional 25 μl of
streptavidin-phycoerythrin was added, incubated on a shaker for 30 min, washed,
and resuspended in 150 μl of buffer before the concentrations were
measured using the Bioplex 200 System (Bioplex Manager 6.1, Bio-Rad, Hercules,
CA, USA).

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP (SAS, Cary, NC, USA). The
statistical significance between IBMFS and the controls was determined using the
two-tailed, Mann-Whitney non-parametric test with an alpha level of 0.05.

B cells and monocytes develop in the bone marrow from CD34+
HSCs (Mercier et al,
2012; Zhao et al,
2012). The bone marrow also contains memory T-cells (Di Rosa & Pabst 2005; Masopust & Picker 2012; Wood et al, 2007). We
assessed the proportions of HSC, monocyte, T-cell, and B-cell populations in
bone marrow mononuclear cells from 16 FA, 20 DC, 21 DBA, and 7 SDS patients, and
14 healthy adult controls by flow cytometry. The percentages of HSCs were
significantly lower than controls in FA, DC, and SDS, but not in DBA (Fig 1A). The percentages of monocytes were
below those of controls in FA, DC and DBA, but not in SDS (Fig 1B). The only difference in the lymphocyte populations
was a lower proportion of B-cells in DC than in controls (Fig 1C-D). The low proportions of HSCs in FA, DC, and SDS
were consistent with published studies (Ceccaldi
et al, 2012; Dror 2005; Dror & Freedman
1999; Dror et al,
2001; Goldman et al,
2008). The low percentage of monocytes in DBA suggests that a lineage
defect downstream of HSCs may occur in these patients (Giri et al, 2000). Thus, the proportions
of HSCs and monocytes were generally low in IBMFS, while T- and B-cells were
generally comparable to those in controls.

Increased production of TNF-α and IFN-γ by stimulated
T-cells has been reported following pharmacological stimulation of bone marrow
mononuclear cells from patients with FA; this overproduction was proposed as a
key mechanism of bone marrow aplasia (Dufour
et al, 2003). To test whether T-cells in our
patients with FA and in those with other IBMFS overproduce these cytokines, we
stimulated the bone marrow mononuclear cells with PMA plus ionomycin (P+I) and
measured intracellular TNF-α and IFN-γ by flow cytometry. Both
cytokines were produced in all groups, including the control, after 4 h of
stimulation (Fig 2A). FA, DC, and SDS were
comparable to the control, while DBA had lower proportions of
IFN-γ–positive and TNF-α–positive T-cells (Fig 2B-C).

Peripheral blood monocytes from one FA patient have been reported to
overproduce TNF-α (Anur et
al, 2012). Therefore, we tested the ability of
CD14+ bone marrow monocytes to produce TNF-α and
IFN-γ in response to LPS. In addition, we also examined the production of
these cytokines by B-cells, because they could potentially respond to LPS.
Mature human B-cells from healthy individuals generally do not respond well to
LPS; however, TLR 4, a receptor responsible for responding to LPS (Rossol et al, 2011), is
upregulated in patients with inflammatory diseases (Ganley-Leal et al, 2010). Because
immunological alterations have been reported in patients with IBMFS, we wanted
to test whether CD19+ cells in the bone marrow could respond to this
stimulus. TNF-α (Fig 3A) or
IFN-γ (Fig S1)
was not detected after 4 h of stimulation with 1 μg/ml LPS in B-cells.
The cells responsible for producing TNF-α were monocytes (Fig 3B) and, as expected, they did not
produce IFN-γ (Fig
S1). Only a minimal amount of TNF-α was detected in the
no-stimulation samples from any group (Fig
3B). We found that, within the monocyte population, the percentages
of TNF-α–producing CD14+ cells were lower in FA and DBA
than in the controls (Fig 3C). Our data
showed that monocytes were the major producers of TNF-α in response to
LPS. Together with the data in Fig 2, we
found that T-cells or monocytes from patients with IBMFS do not overproduce
IFN-γ or TNF-α.

Because LPS is a more specific stimulus than P+I, and because it signals
through TLR4 (Rossol et al,
2011), we surmized that the LPS-related differences in cellular
response were likely to be more physiologically relevant. To further ascertain
whether the response of monocytes in FA or DBA patients was weaker than the
controls, we conducted dose-response experiments to determine whether cytokines
secreted in response to varying doses of LPS differed between the IBMFS and the
control groups. We also examined the production of intracellular TNF-α
and IFN-γ. All samples were tested for the measurement of cytokines in
response to all doses of LPS (1.0, 0.1, or 0.001 μg/ml) at 4- and 24-h
time points. Because the number of cells available for analysis was limited, not
all patient samples were tested in the intracellular staining experiment. In
some samples, we were able to test only the highest and the lowest doses.

We detected TNF-α–producing monocytes in response to 1
μg/ml of LPS in all groups, and the proportions were comparable between
each syndrome and the control group (Fig
4A-B). In this experiment, there were no significant differences
between FA or DBA and controls, while TNF-α–producing monocytes
were significantly lower in Fig 3C. The
different results between these two experiments are probably due to the smaller
sample size in Fig 4B. Stimulation with 0.1
μg/ml of LPS showed cellular responses similar to those stimulated with 1
μg/ml of LPS (Fig 4A-B). However,
intracellular TNF-α was detected in response to 0.001 μg/ml of LPS
in some patient samples (Fig 4A) with
levels that were significantly greater in FA, DBA, and SDS than in the controls
(Fig 4B).

We hypothesized that the expression level of TLR4 could be responsible
for the differential response observed in these two experiments. However, when
we examined the median fluorescent intensity (MFI) of TLR4 on unstimulated
monocytes, we did not find any difference between the groups (Fig 4C), suggesting that the expression level
of TLR4 was not responsible for the increased sensitivity to LPS in monocytes
from patients with IBMFS.

To determine the amount of cytokines secreted in response to LPS, we
employed the multiplex Luminex technology to measure the concentrations of
IFN-γ and TNF-α in the cell culture supernatants at 4- and 24-h
time points. IFN-γ was not detected, even after 24 h of stimulation (data
not shown). In contrast, we detected substantial amounts of TNF-α in all
groups after 4 h of stimulation with high doses (1 and 0.1 μg/ml) of LPS
(Fig S2). The
concentrations of TNF-α detected for these 2 doses were generally similar
within each group and between the syndrome groups. However, the concentrations
were lower in IBMFS patient samples than in the controls (Fig S2), a result
possibly attributable to the lower number of monocytes in patients (Fig 1B). Despite the low numbers, higher
concentrations of TNF-α were detected in some patient samples when the
cells were stimulated with low-dose (0.001 μg/mL) LPS. In contrast, only
minimal amounts of TNF-α were detected in the controls (Fig S2). A similar
pattern of results was observed at the 24-htime point, except that the
concentrations were higher than those from the 4- time point (Fig S2).

To minimize the difference in the number of monocytes, we calculated
stimulation indexes (fold-increase over no-stimulation controls) to evaluate the
induced level of TNF. At the 4-h time point, TNF-α was greatly increased
in response to the high doses of LPS in all the groups, and the levels of
induction were comparable between the groups (Fig
5A). Also, the levels of induction were similar for these 2 doses of
LPS; however, after low-dose stimulation, the control group showed minimal
induction of TNF-α versus higher induced levels in FA,
DC, and SDS (Fig 5A). Although the
induction level was further increased in all groups at all doses at 24 h, it was
lower in IBMFS than in controls at the high doses. At the low-dose, we observed
a trend toward higher TNF-α induction in FA, DC, and SDS (Fig 5A).

In addition to IFN-γ and TNF-α, we also measured the
concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10, G-CSF, and GM-CSF, and examined the
induction levels of these cytokines using the stimulation index. While
moderate-to-strong inductions were observed for IL-6 and IL-1β, IL-10,
G-CSF, and GM-CSF were minimally detected at 4 h (Fig S3).

We found patterns and trends of induction that were similar to those for
TNF-α. At the high doses, the induction levels of IL-6 and IL-1β
were comparable among different groups at 4 h, except for SDS, which showed
higher responses than the control (Fig 5B).
The levels of the majority of the cytokines in IBMFS were generally below those
of the controls at 24 h (Fig 5C). As in our
data with TNF-α, stimulation with the low-dose LPS led to the secretion
of cytokines from patient samples, and there was a general trend toward higher
induction in FA, DC, and SDS (Fig 5B-C).
This was particularly prominent for SDS and FA at both time points. In contrast,
DBA and the control showed minimal inductions at both time points (Fig 5C). Collectively, our data showed that
the cells from IBMFS bone marrows produced the expected cytokines in response to
LPS. More important, the data in Figs 4 and
5 showed that monocytes in IBMFS are
more sensitive to low-dose LPS, and an overall trend toward greater cytokine
induction was observed.

We present here the first study of cytokine production in the bone marrow of
patients with one of the four major inherited bone marrow failure disorders (FA, DC,
DBA, SDS). There are two major findings: (1) T-cells did not overproduce either
TNF-α or IFN-γ, and (2) monocytes were more sensitive to low-dose LPS
stimulation in patients with IBMFS. Contrary to our expectation, bone marrow
lymphocyte percentages were comparable to those in controls. Nevertheless, the
absolute cell numbers could still be lower in patients with IBMFS because of the
bone marrow hypocellularity. Additionally, because the T-cell precursors originate
from HSCs in the bone marrow, development of new naive T-cells in the thymus may be
affected in patients with IBMFS. Because we used only CD19 as our B-cell marker, we
could not differentiate recirculating mature B-lymphocytes from those that were
going through B-cell development. To address these questions, absolute cell numbers
and the percentages of different cell populations should be measured in future
samples at the time of collection. Flow cytometry can be used to distinguish
subpopulations of B-cells (Jackson et
al, 2008). Furthermore, T-cell receptor excision circle
analysis should provide useful information on the presence of newly selected T-cells
(Somech 2011).

Based on the previous study that showed T-cells overproduce TNF-α
(Dufour et al, 2003),
etanercept, a TNF-α inhibitor, was administered to patients with FA in a
clinical trial to ameliorate bone marrow failure (Mehta et al, 2012), a study which failed to demonstrate
patient benefit. Our data, which showed no evidence of TNF-α overproduction
by activated T cells, were consistent with that null result. Moreover, we observed
that the levels of serum TNF-α in patients with IBMFS, including FA, were
comparable to those in controls (unpublished observations). However, we cannot rule
out the existence of functional alterations in T-cells in IBMFS. From the current
study, it is difficult to assess whether cytokine production is affected, because we
employed a non-physiological stimulus. We used P+I, a pharmacological stimulus,
because the previous study also used a similar pharmacological stimulus to stimulate
T-cells. Perhaps, the use of anti-CD3 antibody or antigens might better reveal
functional alterations in T-cells. In DBA, haploinsufficiency in ribosomal function
may be related tosuboptimal cytokine production that cannot be overcome even by the
potent P+I stimulus.

We have concentrated our efforts on studying the innate immune responses
that are mediated through TLR4. Dose titration of LPS revealed the hypersensitive
nature of bone marrow CD14+ monocytes in IBMFS. A similar finding has
been reported (Anur et al,
2012), in which monocytes purified from PBMCs of one FA patient produced
more TNF-α than the control cells in response to 0.1 ng/ml of LPS. In that
study, however, dose titration was not reported. Our data provide further support of
that observation, and extend the observation of hypersensitivity to other IBMFS.

The high doses of LPS provided a maximal level of activation, and the cells
probably reached their saturation point in responding to the stimulus. However,
under limiting conditions, cells from IBMFS patients were more sensitive to LPS than
those from controls. It was intriguing that the amount of TNF-α secreted by
cells of DBA patients was minimal in response to the low-dose LPS, yet we were able
to detect an intracellular level of this cytokine. In monocytes, the release of
membrane-bound TNF-α is mediated by tumour necrosis
factor-α-converting-enzyme (TACE)(Black
et al, 1997). It may be that TACE is under-expressed
in DBA cells or that these cells have inefficient protein trafficking and/or
secretory pathway. To investigate these potential mechanistic questions, future
studies using quantitative polymerase chain reaction, biochemical analysis, and
microscopy are needed. The cells of FA, DC, and SDS patients showed a similar
tendency toward higher cytokine induction at the low-dose LPS. Considering the
various biological functions of the genes that are mutated in these syndromes, there
are no obvious explanations for this hypersensitivity. Other than SDS and DC with
mutation in the DKC1 gene, the functions of these gene products are
not directly involved in protein synthesis. For SDS, this finding is
counterintuitive, because the maturation of the 60s ribosomal protein is compromised
in this syndrome (Dokal & Vulliamy 2010;
Parikh & Bessler 2012). It is also
unclear why these different syndromes show similar functional alterations. One
possible explanation is that cells of these syndromes may have alterations in p53
(TP53). These IBMFS have been reported to show dysregulation of p53 (Bellodi et al, 2010; Ceccaldi et al, 2012; Chin et al, 1999; Elghetany & Alter 2002; Narla & Ebert 2010; Zhang & Lu 2009). Genome instability that leads to a
quantitatively and/or qualitatively elevated level of p53 might initiate a
compensatory/feedback mechanism in cells to upregulate a survival factor, such as
NF-κB (NFKB1). As NF-κB is an important transcription factor for the
expression of proinflammatory cytokines, its enhanced level and/or activity could
potentially explain why different syndromes with different gene mutations and
different molecular pathways exhibit a similar functional phenotype. It would be
worthwhile to examine whether treatment of cells with a p53 inhibitor, such as
pifithrin, or with an RNA interference agent could reduce the production of
cytokines.

Furthermore, whether the mutated genes and altered pathways are directly
responsible for this hypersensitivity is unclear. At least for TNF-α in FA,
constitutive overproduction of this cytokine by EBV-transformed human lymphoblast
mutant cell lines of FANCA and FANCC genes was
corrected after the cells were reconstituted with the wild-type genes (Anur et al, 2012; Briot et al, 2008).
Overproduction of TNF-α has also been observed in other cell lines that
contain mutations in FANCB, BRAC2 (previously termed
FANCD1), FANCF and FANCG
genes (Briot et al, 2008;
Rosselli et al, 1994).
In some of the mutant cell lines, NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase
pathways were aberrantly activated (Anur et
al, 2012; Briot et
al, 2008). These pathways may be dysregulated in FA, SDS,
and DC. Similar experiments to those performed on FA, such as establishment of
mutant cell lines and reconstitution with the respective wild type genes, combined
with biochemical analysis, could certainly provide insights into molecular defects
that are associated with the other syndromes. These experiments in combination with
microarray technology could possibly identify dysregulated genes and altered
pathways. Another possibility is that monocytes in some patients may have been
presensitized by IFN-γ. Such sensitized cells have been reported to show
enhanced production of TNF-α following LPS stimulation (Hayes et al, 1995). This may explain why the
hypersensitivity in the syndrome groups is heterogeneous. Perhaps microarray
analysis could potentially address this question. Alternatively, heterogeneous
response may be the result of polymorphism in the TLR4 gene. In
IBMFS patients, TLR4 may have mutations that lead to enhanced
response. Single nucleotide polymorphism analysis can help shed light on the status
of TLR4, as well as other TLR and
TP53 genes, in these patients.

The majority of the patient samples we tested were from children (<
18 years of age), while the control bone marrow samples were obtained from adult
volunteers. The literature indicates that the immune responses in healthy children,
including those of the innate immune cells, are not elevated when compared with
adults (Baehner & Johnston 1972; Buck et al, 2002; Chipeta et al, 1998; Gardner et al, 1981; Hartel et al, 2005; Kollmann et al, 2012). Thus,
this age difference is probably not the major reason for the hypersensitivity. As in
the reported studies on IBMFS, the sample size in each syndrome group was relatively
small, and the responses were heterogeneous in our study. Additionally, because this
was a cross-sectional study, longitudinal studies may help us understand the changes
that occur in cells over time and clarify their potential role in the development of
bone marrow failure.

Overall, our data are consistent with the notion that the immunological
alterations in patients with IBMFS are subtle and largely subclinical. A low-level,
chronic adverse environmental exposure could potentially trigger a response from
monocytes in patients with IBMFS and incrementally contribute to the bone marrow
failure. While a single event may not be sufficient to acutely modulate

Our data do not permit defining specific therapeutic implications at
present. While our findings are intriguing, additional research using complementary
approaches is required to more precisely define the underlying molecular mechanisms
of this intrinsic hypersensitivity, and to delineate its implications for the
treatment of bone marrow failure. We believe that focusing on identifying
therapeutic targets within TLRs and the signalling pathways that are involved in
secretion of proinflammatory cytokines might be more beneficial than directly
targeting cytokines. Because of the heterogeneity of responses by different
patients, multiple different proteins may need to be targeted for an immunologically
targeted therapy to be effective. As more data become available on whether
reconstitution of mutated genes in IBMFS will ameliorate the overproduction of
cytokines, gene therapy could be considered as an option for patients with
IBMFS.

In this study, we only examined the cellular response to LPS, which signals
through TLR4; however, these cells can respond to pathogens, such as viruses,
through other TLRs. Dysregulation of TLR8-mediated production of TNF-α has
been reported in FANCA– and
FANCC–mutant cells (Anur
et al, 2012), suggesting that innate immune
responses through other TLRs may also be dysregulated in monocytes in patients in
with IBMFS. Further studies that use ligands specific for each TLR are required to
characterize whether innate immune responses through other TLRs are altered in
patients with IBMFS.
