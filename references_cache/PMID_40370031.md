BACKGROUND: Laryngeal dystonia (LD) is an isolated focal dystonia causing 
involuntary spasms in the laryngeal muscles that selectively impair speech 
production. LD is characterized as a functional and structural neural network 
disorder; however, the mechanistic aspects of network dysfunction in dystonia 
remain unknown.
OBJECTIVE: We hypothesized that iron-induced abnormal metabolic processes may 
underlie microstructural neuronal damage, contributing to altered neural 
activity within the dystonic network and, subsequently, the development of the 
dystonic state.
METHODS: We used 7 Tesla magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at ultra-high field 
resolution for quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) of iron content, 
multi-echo multi-band resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) of brain activity and 
functional connectivity, positron emission tomography with [11C]flumazenil 
radioligand of GABAA neuroreceptor availability, and immunohistochemistry of 
postmortem brain tissue to investigate iron metabolism in LD patients and 
healthy controls.
RESULTS: The QSM analysis found increased iron content in primary sensorimotor 
and premotor cortices, inferior frontal, middle frontal, and middle temporal 
gyri, middle cingulate cortex, superior and inferior parietal lobules, insula, 
putamen, and cerebellum. Histopathology substantiated the neuroimaging findings 
by showing focal clusters of iron precipitates in these regions. Increased iron 
content in the supplementary motor area and middle cingulate cortex was 
associated with altered neural activity, while increased iron in the middle 
cingulate cortex, premotor cortex, and putamen had associations with GABAA 
receptor availability in LD patients.
CONCLUSION: Abnormal iron accumulations are likely to contribute to the 
imbalance of excitatory and inhibitory signaling within the dystonic neural 
network, leading to altered network dynamics that ultimately contribute to LD 
development. © 2025 International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society.

Laryngeal dystonia (LD), a frequent form of isolated focal dystonia, is characterized by involuntary spasms of the laryngeal muscles, causing voice stoppages and strained, strangled, or breathy voice quality primarily during speaking. While the exact pathophysiology of the disorder is unclear, advanced neuroimaging studies have defined a complex, large-scale brain network disorganization in patients with LD, where alterations in the basal ganglia, thalamic, cerebellar, and cortical sensorimotor and parietal regions represent key changes of dystonic functional and structural connectomes.1-8 A similar pattern of altered brain network connectivity has also been described in other focal dystonias9-12 as well as inherited forms of dystonia.13-15 Furthermore, vulnerable functional connectivity of sensorimotor and parietal cortical regions in LD has been linked to the polygenic risk of focal dystonia16 and found to be influenced by external risk factors that likely trigger dystonic voice symptoms in susceptible individuals.17 Together, these studies were fundamental to our understanding of isolated focal dystonia including LD. However, the mechanistic aspects of dystonic network dysfunction remain largely unknown.

In previous years, a few studies began to address the potential neural mechanisms underlying the pathophysiologically abnormal dystonic network in focal dystonias. One of the first studies combining diffusion-weighted imaging with postmortem neuropathology in LD patients has shown that abnormally increased water diffusivity in the putamen and cerebellum is associated with abnormal accumulation of iron in the parenchyma of these regions.18 More recent R2*-relaxometry and susceptibility-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies corroborated this initial finding by reporting elevated iron content in the globus pallidus and cerebellum in patients with cervical dystonia19,20 as well as in the putamen of patients with X-linked dystonia-parkinsonism.21 Another relevant study found correlations between dystonia severity and iron accumulations in the substantial nigra, putamen, and globus pallidus in patients with Rett syndrome.22

The equilibrium of iron metabolism is of biological importance for electron exchange and oxidation–reduction reactions. Iron accumulations are known to potentiate the generation of free radicals and enhance lipid peroxidation, which causes oxidative stress, cell membrane damage, ferroptosis, and subsequent damage to neuronal function.23-27 27 Iron-induced oxidative processes have been shown to reduce GABAergic inhibition28 and cause degeneration of dopaminergic neurons.29 Relevant to the pathophysiology of focal dystonias, including LD, both the loss of inhibition due to GABAergic deficits and abnormal dopaminergic modulation due to decreased nigrostriatal dopamine release have been documented.30-34 When synthesized with the findings of abnormal iron accumulation,18-20 these studies offer potential mechanistic insights into the contributing factors underlying neural network dysfunction in focal dystonias. However, prior studies investigating iron metabolism were limited by their exclusive focus on the basal ganglia and cerebellum, notwithstanding the known presence of alterations in both the cortical and subcortical regions in dystonia.11 Other methodological limitations included small cohorts, selected dystonia populations, and imaging protocols that were highly dependent on MRI field strength and had limited dynamic range due to confounding water content.35

We used a multimodal, cross-disciplinary approach to determine iron metabolism and its relationship with functional activity and network connectivity, GABAergic neurotransmission, and disorder symptomatology in LD patients compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 1). Specifically, we used 7 Tesla (7 T) MRI at ultra-high field resolution for in vivo quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) of iron content, multi-echo multi-band resting-state functional MRI (MEMB rfMRI) of brain activity and functional connectivity, positron emission tomography (PET) with [11C]flumazenil radioligand for GABAA neuroreceptor mapping, and qualitative (immuno)histo-chemistry of postmortem brain tissue for iron precipitates. We hypothesized that iron-induced abnormal metabolic processes may underlie microstructural neuronal damage, contributing to altered neural activity within the dystonic network and, subsequently, the development of the dystonic state.

A total of 36 LD patients and 36 healthy controls (HC) were recruited for this study. Among these, 16 LD patients (mean age 51.9 ± 15.7 years, 11 females/5 males) and 16 age- and sex-matched HC (mean age 53.5 ± 12.3 years, 9 females/7 males) participated in the 7 T QSM and rfMRI studies. Eighteen LD patients (54.6 ± 11.0 years, 12 females/6 males) participated in the PET study.

All participants were native English speakers, right-handed based on the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, and had normal cognitive function based on the Mini-Mental State Examination or Montreal Cognitive Assessment. None of the participants had any past or present history of laryngeal, neurological (other than LD and co-occurring voice tremor), or psychiatric disorders. LD diagnosis was determined based on the case history, laryngeal and neurological examinations, and perceptual analysis of voice and speech recordings. LD patients were fully symptomatic at the time of study participation, and those who received botulinum toxin injections participated at least 3 months after their last injection when fully symptomatic. None of the participants were on any centrally acting medications before or during study participation.

All participants gave written informed consent prior to study participation, which was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, and Mass General Brigham.

The MRI/fMRI study used a 7 T Siemens whole-body MR scanner (Magnetom; Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 32-channel head coil. The imaging protocol included an enhanced multi-echo T2*-weighted three-dimensional (3D) gradient-echo (GRE) sequence processed to extract QSM images of brain iron content. QSM uses advanced MR phase imaging for in vivo measurements of tissue magnetic susceptibility and provides higher specificity and sensitivity of detecting iron in gray matter than any other neuroimaging-based iron measures, for example, R2* relaxometry.36,37 Images were acquired using TR = 60 ms, number of echoes = 8, TE1/inter-echo interval/TE8 = 10/3.98/37.84 ms, flip angle = 10°, voxel-size = 0.9 × 0.9 × 2 mm3.

MEMB rfMRI was acquired to examine functional activity and network connectivity using TR = 1850 ms, number of echoes = 4, TE1/TE4 = 8.5/53 ms, voxel-size- = 2.5 mm isotropic. During the scanning session, participants were instructed to lie still and keep their eyes closed without falling asleep or thinking about anything in particular. Whole-brain T1-weighted images were acquired for anatomical reference using a 3D-MP2RAGE sequence with TR = 6000 ms; TE = 5.14 ms; number of inversion times (TI) =2, TI1 = 1050 ms, TI2 = 3000 ms, voxel size = 0.7 mm isotropic.

PET [11C]flumazenil data were acquired on a GE Advance PET scanner in the morning or early afternoon (between 11:34 AM and 2:09 PM) to control for possible diurnal variations in GABAergic neurotransmission. All patients were asked to abstain from alcohol for a week, caffeine for 24 hours, and to fast for 3 h. Patients were instructed to lie still in the scanner with closed eyes without falling asleep in an environment with dimmed lights and reduced ambient noise. An 8-min transmission scan was obtained using a 68Ge source for attenuation correction of emission data. [11C]flumazenil was administered as a 1-min bolus injection through a catheter placed in the antecubital vein, and images were acquired for the subsequent 59 min. A dynamic PET scan in the 3D scanning mode with septa retracted was initiated at the start of [11C]flumazenil injection, generating 21 time-frames of 30 s to 5 min epochs over 60 min (FOV = 148 mm, reconstructed resolution = 6 mm in all directions). Accounting for decay, the effectively injected [11C]flumazenil activity over the 60-min PET scan was 20.13 ± 0.45 mCi.

The Susceptibility Tensor Imaging Suite v3.0 and Analysis of Functional NeuroImages (AFNI) software packages were used to calculate the QSM maps for the in vivo brain iron assessment. First, the magnitude image was used to extract the brain tissue masks using the AFNI 3dSkullStrip command. Then, raw phase images were unwrapped using a Laplacian-based phase unwrapping, followed by the calculation of the normalized phase.38 These images were averaged to determine the frequency shift to remove the background frequency using spherical-mean-value filtering (V_SHARP algorithm).39 Using the mean signal from each brain as a susceptibility reference, QSM images were calculated using the STAR-QSM (STreaking Artifact Reduction for QSM) method and registered to the AFNI standard Talairach–Tournoux space.

Three LD patients and three HC were excluded from statistical analysis because of QSM artifacts or excessive motion affecting QSM estimations. The final analysis was conducted in 13 LD patients (mean age 52.4 ± 16.1 years, 8 females/5 males) and 13 HC (mean age 52.8 ± 12.3 years, 8 females/5 males). Between-group comparisons were performed using a two-sided two-sample t-test with a significance set at a cluster-corrected P ≤ 0.05, with a minimum cluster size of 300 voxels.

MEMB rfMRI data were analyzed using AFNI afni_proc.py pipeline, which integrates multi-echo independent component analysis (ME-ICA v2.5 beta6). Briefly, functional images from all TEs were temporally concatenated, corrected for slice timing and motion artifacts, normalized to the AFNI standard space, and used as inputs for ME-ICA. The ICs were categorized as BOLD and non-BOLD based on their respective weightings measured by Kappa and Rho values, as described previously.40-42 Denoised volumes were smoothed with a 4-mm full width at half maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel, and the voxel signal was scaled to the global 4D mean of 100. Regression analysis censored motion (≥0.35 mm) between successive time points as well as time points where at least 10% of the brain voxels were computed as outliers. The final images were used in the association analysis as described below.

Head motion-induced artifacts were corrected using the registered attenuation correction method as described earlier.34 The decay- and motion-corrected images were averaged over frames 7–11 corresponding to the [11C] flumazenil peak uptake using PMOD Technologies software. The final averaged image was registered to the individual T1-weighted MR image.

Using a two-step simplified reference tissue model (SRTM2),39 whole-brain parametric maps of voxelwise [11C]flumazenil binding potential (BPND)43 was computed within a gray matter mask, which was obtained by segmenting the individual T1-weighted images using SPM software. The pons was manually drawn on individual images and used as a reference region devoid of GABAA receptors.44 BPND images were then spatially smoothed with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian filter, normalized to the AFNI standard Talairach–Tournoux space, and used for the association analysis with in vivo iron content.

To investigate the associations between iron, resting-state brain activity, and GABAergic neurotransmission in LD (Fig. 1), we performed voxelwise Spearman’s correlation analyses using statistically significant regions of increased brain iron content as identified using QSM. The QSM versus [11C]flumazenil BPND correlation analysis was conducted in 10 LD patients who had data available from both imaging modalities. The relationship between voxelwise QSM values and clinical characteristics of the disorder, including the age of LD onset, disorder duration, and symptom severity based on the Burke–Fahn–Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale, was conducted in all 13 patients. Statistical significance for all association tests was set at P ≤ 0.01, corrected by the total number of modalities and clinical scores used for correlations, with a minimum cluster size of 40 voxels.

Regions that showed significant correlations between QSM values, imaging measures, or clinical features were used as seeding regions of interest (ROIs) to investigate the impact of iron on the functional network organization in LD. The 3dInstaCorr program of AFNI software was used to map network-wide differences in functional connectivity between LD patients and HC. The seed ROIs were defined as 4-mm spheres centered at the coordinates of the maximum t statistics of each significant cluster showing increased iron content in LD patients. Single-subject correlation maps were generated by calculating the Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the average BOLD time course from each seed ROI and the time course from all voxels in the brain. Between-group differences in functional connectivity for each seeded network were assessed using two-sample t-tests at a voxelwise threshold of P ≤ 0.01 with a minimum cluster size of 40 voxels.

Postmortem brain tissue from six LD patients (5 females/1 male; 78.3 ± 13.1 years old at the time of death) and seven neurologically and cognitively healthy individuals (5 females/2 males; 64.4 ± 19.8 years old at the time of death) were obtained for this study from our private collection and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) NeuroBioBank. The LD diagnosis in patients and the absence of major neurological or psychiatric conditions in HC were confirmed antemortem, where applicable, and further validated for each case by reviewing available medical records at the time of tissue retrieval. Among LD patients, four were diagnosed with isolated focal LD, one had concurrent voice tremor, and one had concurrent essential tremor. All subjects provided consent for brain donation per the Declaration of Helsinki.

All brains were fixed in 10% formalin solution and cut into 2 cm-thick coronal slices (Fig. 2B). We conducted a targeted postmortem brain tissue procurement from regions that were found to show abnormal iron content using QSM imaging. However, due to the rare accessibility of postmortem brain specimens from patients with focal dystonia, the brain tissue available for staining varied across patients (Table 2). The control tissue was matched to the patient tissue samples from the same brain regions. All tissue blocks from patients and HC were embedded in paraffin, cut into 5 μm-thick sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate cell morphology and the presence of nuclear and cytoplasmic inclusions. Special stains were used to examine the presence of iron precipitates (Prussian blue staining), calcium deposits (Alizarin Red S staining), and phosphates (von Kossa staining) in tissue samples from both patients and HC. Additionally, all tissue sections were screened for neurodegenerative processes, including microglial/macrophage activity (CD68, Clone KP1; Agilent, Sant Clara, CA, USA), alpha-synuclein (1:100; Oncogene Research Products, San Diego, CA, USA), tau (1:1500, Clone Tau-2; Chemicon International Inc., Temecula, CA, USA), neurofilaments (clone RT-97 + NR-4, LSBio Inc., Shirley, MA, USA), ubiquitin (ab7780, Abcam Inc., Cambridge, UK), and beta-amyloid (ab2539, Abcam Inc.). Sections were incubated with a refined avidin-biotin kit (LSABþ System-HRP; Dako) for reaction product visualization. All tissue sections were evaluated using light microscopy (Axio Imager A2, Carl Zeiss Microscopy, LLC) for the qualitative classification of precipitates and inclusions. As described previously,45 occasional yet definitive pathological changes in the region were classified as mild; significant changes encompassing 10% to 50% of the region were classified as moderate, and a total or subtotal destruction of the region was defined as severe.

The QSM analysis found statistically significant focal clusters of increased iron content in LD patients compared with HC in the left primary motor cortex (M1, area 4), extending to premotor cortex (area 6) and inferior frontal gyrus (area 44); left primary somatosensory cortex (S1, areas 2 and 3b); right supplementary motor area (SMA); left middle cingulate cortex; left superior parietal lobule (SPL, areas 7A and 7PC); bilateral inferior parietal lobule (IPL, left areas PF and PFt; right area hIP3) extending to right SPL; right middle frontal gyrus (area 47); right middle temporal gyrus (area 21); right insula; left putamen; and left cerebellum (lobules VIIa/VIII/Crus 1) (Fig. 2A, Table 1).

The evaluation of postmortem brain tissue in LD patients and HC found no gross abnormalities (Fig. 2B). H&E stains showed normal cellular morphology in all available postmortem regions of interest in LD patients and HC. However, all six LD cases showed scattered patches of focal mineral accumulations in the parenchyma and occasionally in the vessel walls of the primary sensorimotor cortex, SMA, IPL, insula, putamen, caudate nucleus, pallidum, and cerebellum (Fig. 2B, Table 2). The follow-up Prussian blue stains confirmed the presence of mild to moderate patches of iron precipitates in all LD patients but none in HC (Fig. 2B, Table 2). Specifically, mild iron deposits were identified in the primary sensory cortex in all three available LD cases, the IPL in two of six available cases, the cerebellum in two of five available cases, and the insula in one of two available cases. Clusters of iron accumulation in the striatum and pallidum were moderate in four LD cases and mild in the remaining two cases. Mild iron precipitates were also found in the SMA and inferior frontal gyrus in tissue available from one LD case. No iron deposits were identified in the thalamus in any of the four available LD cases, which aligns with the normal iron content in this region as determined by the QSM analysis.

Accumulations of calcium (Alizarin Red stain) and phosphate (von Kossa stain) were less prominent than iron deposits in LD patients, and none were found in HC (Table 2). Mild calcium and phosphate deposits colocalized with iron were found in the primary sensory cortex in two of four available LD cases, the inferior frontal gyrus in one available case, the IPL in two of six available cases, the cerebellum in four of five cases, and in the striatum and pallidum in five of six available cases. The insula and SMA, which were stained positively for iron, did not show any calcium or phosphate precipitates in LD patients. The thalamus, which was negative for iron precipitates, was also negative for calcium and phosphate in LD patients.

Regions in the primary sensory cortex, IPL, insula, striatum, thalamus, and cerebellum in LD patients but not HC showed a mild to moderate increase in microglial activity (CD-68, Clone KP1 stain), likely associated with mineral deposits in these regions in patients. No accumulations of abnormal proteins, such as tau, ubiquitin, alpha-synuclein, and beta-amyloid, were found in HC or LD patients, except for one LD case with mild scattered non-Alzheimer’s disease-specific beta-amyloid inclusions in the sensorimotor, insular, and SMA regions. LD and HC cases were negative for the presence of neurofibrillary tangles.

Statistically significant positive correlations between QSM-based brain iron content and resting-state neural activity were found in the right SMA (Rs = 0.78, P = 0.005) and the left middle cingulate cortex (Rs = 0.86, P = 6 × 10−4) (Fig. 3A; Table 1). Statistically significant relationships between iron content and GABAergic neurotransmission were found in the left premotor cortex (area 6, Rs = 0.79, P = 0.009), middle cingulate cortex (Rs = −0.94, P = 2 × 10−16), and putamen (Rs = 0.82, P = 0.007) (Fig. 3B, Table 1). Between-group functional connectivity analysis showed a significant difference in the left SPL (P ≤ 0.01, peak Z = 2.8), which was part of the functional network seeded from the left middle cingulate cortex (Fig. 3C). There were no statistically significant correlations between the brain iron content and clinical features of LD, including severity, duration, and age of onset.

Using ultra-high field resolution multimodal neuroimaging and postmortem brain immunohistochemistry, this study identified cortical and subcortical brain regions of abnormal iron accumulations that are associated with altered functional brain activity, network connectivity, and GABAergic neurotransmission in LD patients. The presence of focal iron deposits in the sensorimotor, parietal, and prefrontal cortical regions in these patients is a novel finding that may be specific to the pathophysiology of LD, which is known to involve widespread alterations of cortical regions controlling speech production.1,2,46,47 Furthermore, the left lateralization of iron accumulations may be related to the lateralization of the neural network controlling speech motor output,48-50 which is particularly affected in LD patients.51 Conversely, increased iron content in the basal ganglia and cerebellum may represent a common structural pathology across different forms of dystonia.18,19

Iron plays a significant role in multiple biological processes in the central nervous system, including neuronal development, neurotransmission, and myelin synthesis. The existing literature identifies iron accumulations as a potential contributor to neuronal damage and abnormal brain metabolism, alongside other processes such as mitochondrial dysfunction, glutamate excitotoxicity, and neuroinflammation.52-54 Abnormal iron content has also been associated with the aggregation of proteins and metals, generation of free radicals, oxidative stress, glutathione dysfunction, and conversion of dopamine and other catechols to potentially neurotoxic semiquinone radicals.55-58 We may rule out the iron dyshomeostasis in misfolding and aggregation of toxic proteins in LD given that our neuropathological evaluation did not identify tau and amyloid proteins or neurofibrillary tangles that are a hallmark of neurodegenerative syndromes.59-62 However, the finding of the significant association between abnormal iron content and GABAergic function favors iron dyshomeostasis as a potential mechanism underlying abnormal neurotransmission in this disorder. Specifically, abnormal iron content found in the regions implicated in LD pathophysiology may induce oxidative processes and lead to the damage and loss of inhibitory neurons in these regions,28,29 contributing to abnormally reduced GABAergic inhibition and the subsequent imbalance between inhibition and excitation within the basal ganglia, cerebellar, and cortical circuitries.30-32,34,63

Altered iron metabolism may further impact neural network organization in LD, as revealed by significant positive and negative associations between abnormal iron content and functional activity and connectivity. The relationship between iron and brain functional connectivity has been previously described in healthy adults as well as neurological patients as a possible preclinical marker and neural signature of neurodegenerative processes in Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases.64,65 Our study demonstrated that iron accumulation is linked to altered functional connectivity in LD, particularly between the cingulate and parietal cortical regions, the latter playing a key role in LD pathophysiology.33 The IPL/SPL have been shown to influence whole-brain network activity during speech production via projections to the primary sensorimotor, prefrontal, insular cortices, and putamen.66,67 Changes in the parietal cortex have been related to genetic and environmental risks for the development of LD and proposed as one of the possible neural markers for the diagnostic classification of these patients from healthy individuals.16,17,67 We postulate that different mechanisms, including excitotoxicity and compensatory processes, may underlie these associations. Excitotoxicity plays an important role in the genesis of iron and calcium deposits.68-72 The histopathological findings of the present study, showing an overall greater accumulation of iron than calcium and phosphate, suggest that altered iron metabolism may play a more central role in disrupting neuronal signaling and homeostasis in LD, while the accumulation of other minerals appears to be a secondary phenomenon in the cascade of processes potentially leading to neuronal damage.73 Alternatively, mineral accumulations within the nervous system may carry a compensatory function to improve metal availability for physiological processes in hyperactive regions.55 Future studies are warranted to further disambiguate the mechanistic aspects of iron’s contribution to the pathophysiology of this disorder.

The absence of significant correlations between iron content and clinical features of LD is notable. However, this finding suggests that iron metabolism may represent an endophenotypic trait of the disorder rather than a compensatory feature attributable to symptom severity or disease progression. As focal dystonias, such as LD, are not associated with progressive neurodegeneration, focal iron accumulations may point to pathophysiological vulnerability linked to altered function and neurotransmission of the neural network, independent of symptom representation.

As a limitation of this study, we acknowledge that in vivo QSM and postmortem data originated from different cohorts. It is, however, evident that the conduct of these studies in the same human subjects is not feasible, given their nature. Likewise, animal models of LD do not exist, which could have been used for in vivo and ex vivo recordings. Therefore, the analysis of in vivo imaging data in conjunction with rarely available postmortem brain tissue from patients with LD presented an unprecedented opportunity to gain insights into the neuropathology underlying neuroimaging findings in this disorder.

In summary, our novel approach of multimodal ultra-high field neuroimaging combined with postmortem brain immunohistochemistry determined a reproducible pattern of cortical and subcortical abnormal iron accumulation in LD patients compared to healthy individuals. We further showed that iron dyshomeostasis in LD is associated with neural network dysfunction and abnormal GABAergic neurotransmission. It is plausible that abnormal iron precipitates are related to the imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory signaling within the dystonic neural network, leading to altered network dynamics that ultimately contribute to LD manifestation (Fig. 4).
