Case report

Accepted 2019 Dec 11; Collection date 2019.

We present the case of an infant with prenatal diagnosis, at 32 weeks gestation, of Ebstein’s anomaly without anterograde flow from right ventricular to pulmonary atresia (PA)—functional PA with flow reversal in the ductus arteriosus. Prostaglandin E1 was started after birth. Chest X-ray showed severe cardiomegaly and echocardiogram confirmed Ebstein’s anomaly with a thickened non-opening pulmonary valve without anterograde flow but with mild regurgitation. Multidisciplinary team decision was to progressively reduce prostaglandins and have an expectant attitude. Peripheral oxygen saturation above 85% was maintained and serial echocardiograms documented progressive reduction of the ductus arteriosus and the opening of the pulmonic valve cusps, with the development of anterograde flow. The newborn was discharged at day 19 of life without the need for any intervention, and at last follow-up remains asymptomatic, with anterograde normal flow in the pulmonary valve.

Keywords:congenital disorders, materno-fetal medicine, cardiovascular medicine

Ebstein’s anomaly represents 1% of all congenital heart diseases.1Described by Wilhelm Ebstein,2this disease is a right ventricular (RV) myopathy characterised by anomalous tricuspid valve (TV) formation.1Findings include apical displacement of the annular insertion point of the TV leaflets, particularly the septal leaflet, redundancy of the anterior leaflet, adhesion of the leaflets to the underlying myocardium, dilation of the RV atrial portion and dilation of the TV annulus.1These changes are extremely variable, but usually result in mild to severe tricuspid regurgitation and dilation of the right atrium.1Other malformations usually present are patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect in 80%–90% of cases and obstruction of the RV outflow tract due to stenosis or atresia of the pulmonary valve.3Atrial and ventricular dilation, as well as accessory atrioventricular pathways, cause a suitable substrate for the presence of arrhythmias.4

The clinical presentation is variable and depends on anatomical severity, ranging from symptomatic newborns to adulthood presentation.5In the fetus, severe TR may lead to cardiomegaly, hydrops and arrhythmia, with demise rates as high as 48%.6Risk factors for perinatal mortality include absence of anterograde flow in the pulmonary valve, reversal of flow in the ductus arteriosus and fetal distress.7Newborns may present with cyanosis and heart failure due to tricuspid regurgitation and right-to-left shunting at the atrial level.1Older children, adolescents and adults may exhibit dyspnoea, fatigue, cyanosis or palpitations as early symptoms.1

Treatment depends on the morphology and presentation. Medical therapy is preferred in the neonatal period, but refractory heart failure, marked cyanosis, significant dilation of the right heart or dependence of prostaglandins for maintenance of pulmonary perfusion are indications for surgery.8 9Patients should be submitted to a univentricular correction if there is not a reasonable anterograde pulmonary blood flow.9In the biventricular repair, the TV is repaired and the atrial septal defect is partially closed.8The TV repair is most often of the monocusp type and is based on the presence of a satisfactory anterior leaflet.8Bostonet alreported their outcomes on 32 neonates and infants submitted to biventricular repair: early survival was 78.1% (25/32) and 15-year survival estimate was 74%±8%.8Right atrial reduction can be performed to increase lung volume and improve its compliance.1

In children and adults, operation is advised for symptoms of cyanosis, fatigue, paradoxical embolism, decreasing exercise performance, progressive increase in heart size on chest radiography, progressive RV dilation or reduction of systolic function by echocardiography and/or appearance of atrial or ventricular arrhythmias.3A biventricular repair is the rule in this age group.1Operative management of Ebstein anomaly has advanced, and the most contemporary repair—the cone repair—provides a near anatomic result.10The results of the Mayo Clinic seem promising: there was only one early death (0.4%) and overall survival at 6 years was 98%.11Early reoperation was required in 14 patients (5.9%), freedom from late reoperation was 97.9% at 6 years.11The authors suggest that if the results of cone repair are proven durable, there may be a paradigm shift towards earlier operation.11

A 32-week female fetus was referred to fetal echocardiography due to cardiomegaly. Ebstein’s anomaly was diagnosed (figures 1 and 2), with severe tricuspid regurgitation, dilation of the right atrium, marked cardiomegaly and absence of anterograde flow through the pulmonary valve with reversal of flow in the ductus arteriosus. Delivery was at 38 weeks with birth weight of 2625 g. The newborn presented respiratory depression with bradycardia at 2 min of life and required invasive ventilation. Apgar index was 5, both at the first and fifth minutes of life. She was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) where prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) was initiated at 30 ng/kg/min. Chest X-ray showed marked cardiomegaly and left pleural effusion (figure 3). The echocardiogram confirmed Ebsteins’s anomaly with dysplastic TV, incomplete leaflet coaptation, RV normal dimension and severely dilated right atrium (figure 4,video 1); ostium secundum atrial septal defect with right-to-left shunt; a thickened pulmonary valve with an 8 mm annulus without anterograde flow, but with mild regurgitation (figures 5 and 6); patent ductus arteriosus with left-to-right shunt. After left thoracentesis and diuretics (furosemide 1 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours), resolution of pleural effusion and reduction of cardiomegaly were observed, allowing extubation on the fourth day of life and transference from the NICU to a ward. The case was discussed in multidisciplinary meeting, and progressive reduction of PGE1 and expectant attitude was decided. As the documentation of a mild pulmonary valve regurgitation with a good size annulus showed patency and potential effectiveness of the pulmonary valve, this option seemed appropriate. A reduction in pulmonary artery pressures with the progressive ductus closure would be sufficient to allow anterograde flow. During the next few days, PGE1 dose was tapered. Serial echocardiograms confirmed the progressive ductus arteriosus closure and the gradual improvement of anterograde RV flow to the pulmonary artery (figure 7) and showed an improvement of the tricuspid regurgitation. The patient maintained adequate peripheral oxygen saturations (>90%) in room air and chest radiographs documented a progressive reduction of cardiac index. PGE1 was discontinued at 15 days of age, with consequent total closure of the ductus arteriosus. The newborn was discharged at the 19th day of life and was referred to the outpatient clinic, waiting complete correction.

At the last follow-up the infant aged 16 months, was asymptomatic, showed good weight progression and peripheral oxygen saturation was 93%. Echocardiogram showed right heart dilation with severe tricuspid regurgitation, and anterograde normal flow through the pulmonary valve (figure 8).

This was a challenging case. The prenatal morphology with marked cardiomegaly, absence of anterograde flow in the pulmonary valve and flow inversion in the ductus arteriosus, indicated a ductus-dependent congenital heart disease with poor prognosis. The early neonatal period was complicated by congestive heart failure and PGE1 infusion was required for the maintenance of pulmonary perfusion. However, a suitable 8 mm pulmonary valve annulus and mild regurgitation indicated that there was no true anatomical atresia. Therapeutic options such as Blalock-Taussig (BT) shunt or percutaneous dilatation of the pulmonary valve increased the risk of severe pulmonary regurgitation. Eventually, with PGE1 reduction and a decrease in pulmonary vascular pressure, anterograde flow began to appear in the pulmonary valve.

This situation was described in a retrospective study of 29 newborns with Ebstein’s anomaly without anterograde pulmonary valve flow.12The authors began by stating that newborns with Ebstein’s anomaly may not present anterograde flow at the pulmonary valve level, even if their morphology is normal, and considered that this was due to a mismatch between RV contractility and afterload. PGE1 infusion for patency of the ductus arteriosus may perpetuate this condition by increasing the RV afterload. Thus, closure of the ductus arteriosus may be necessary to allow the development of anterograde flow and avoid a damaging circular shunt. The distinction between these patients and those who have a non-functioning pulmonary valve is essential, but may prove difficult since both conditions are characterised by the same aspects: pulmonary valve without leaflet excursion and absence of anterograde flow. The authors concluded that the only factor significantly associated with a normal pulmonary valve was the diameter of its annulus.12

Ebstein’s anomaly has a wide spectrum of presentation, with morphology strongly influencing management and outcome.

Both true anatomical or functional pulmonary valve atresia may be present.

The presence of regurgitation and the size of pulmonary annulus are determinant in the management and in the distinction of these entities.

The management of functional pulmonary atresia includes the closure of the ductus arteriosus to allow the development of anterograde flow.
