# A Heterozygous Gain-of-Function Variant in IKBKB Associated with Autoimmunity and Autoinflammation.
**Authors:** Sacco K, Kuehn HS, Kawai T, Alsaati N, Smith L, Davila B, Bundy V, Kuhns DB, Dobbs K, Delmonte O, Notarangelo LD, Rosenzweig SD, Keller MD
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-022-01395-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-022-01395-2)

## Content

PURPOSE: Biallelic loss-of-function variants in IKBKB cause severe combined immunodeficiency. We describe a case of autoimmunity and autoinflammation in a male infant with a heterozygous gain-of-function (GOF) IKBKB variant.

METHODS: Case report and review of the literature. We performed in silico variant analysis, measurement of plasma soluble biomarkers associated with immune activation, functional stimulation of patient peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and functional validation of variants transduced in Jurkat cells.

RESULTS: A patient with two heterozygous IKBKB variants (E518K and T559M) presents with previously undescribed autoimmune cytopenias and autoinflammation. He had decreased TNF-α-induced IkBα degradation in vitro, and had increased serum biomarkers associated with macrophage recruitment and activation. Jurkat cells transduced with the IKKb T559M variant showed increased basal levels of phosphorylation of IKKα/b and p65, and higher degradation of IkBα suggesting a GOF mechanism. No significant changes were observed in Jurkat cells transduced with the E518K variant.

CONCLUSIONS: A GOF variant in IKBKB may associate with autoinflammation and autoimmunity highlighting a novel clinical phenotype.

Biallelic loss-of-function variants of the IKBKB gene, encoding for the inhibitor kappa B kinase 2 (IKK2 or IKKβ) protein, cause autosomal recessive severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) with relatively normal T and B cell counts [1]. Heterozygous missense gain-of-function (GOF) variants in the same gene cause an autosomal dominant immunodeficiency disorder characterized by recurrent sinopulmonary infections, immune dysregulation, inflammation, epithelial defects, and lymphopenia [2, 3]. Here, we report a full-term infant with a GOF variant in IKBKB who clinically presented with features of autoinflammation and organ-specific immunity. Functional studies identified one clinically relevant GOF variant.

The patient was referred to Children’s National Hospital, Washington, DC. Whole blood samples were acquired from patient, their father, and adult healthy volunteers. All participants provided informed consent. The following clinical laboratory tests were obtained—hemoglobin, platelet count, total leukocyte count, serum immunoglobulins, inflammatory markers, and lymphocyte subsets. The patient was diagnosed by targeted gene panel sequencing. In silico structural and rotamer analysis was performed in UCSF ChimeraX [4, 5].

Human embryonic kidney 293 T cells (HEK293T; ATCC, CRL-3216) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, L-glutamine, and penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and Jurkat T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, penicillin 100 U/ml, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

Analysis of soluble biomarkers was performed on plasma obtained from patient and family members. Blood samples were centrifuged, and plasma samples were immediately frozen at − 85 °C before analysis. Levels of soluble biomarkers in plasma were measured as previously described [6]. Depending on the nature of the analyte, measurements were obtained using the V-PLEX Human Cytokine 30-Plex Kit (Meso Scale Discovery) and analyzed on a MESO Quick-Plex SQ 120 reader (Meso Scale Discovery) or using a customized, magnetic bead-based, multiplex assay (R&D Systems), according to the manufacturers’ specifications for standards and dilutions, and the magnetic beads were analyzed on Bio-Plex 3D instrumentation (Bio-Rad). The patient’s data was compared to a pooled sample of healthy pediatric controls.

Total PBMC or Jurkat T cells were stimulated with TNF-a (PeproTech, 100 ng/ml) for 10 and 30 min. Cells were washed with cold PBS once and lysed in the lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, and 0.5% NP40 and protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail [Sigma, PPC1010]). Cell lysates were prepared and separated by NuPAGE® Novex® 4–12% Bis–Tris Protein Gels (Life Technology) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer system (Bio-Rad). The membranes were incubated with NF-kB pathway antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, 9936), followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated second antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). b-actin used as a loading control (Cell Signaling Technology, 4970S). The target proteins were developed by use of SuperSignal™ West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34,076). The images were acquired and analyzed with Image Lab (Bio-Rad) or C-Digit scanner using Image Studio Software (Li-Cor).

Total PBMCs (100,000/100 ml) were stimulated with LPS (Invivogen, LPS-EK standard, 100 ng/ml) for 24 h. Cell-free supernatants were saved and analyzed for indicated cytokines using a Human XL Cytokine Luminex Performance Panel (R & D System) on the Luminex® 200 System (Luminex).

HEK293T cells growing in 12-well (2 × 105 cells/well) plates were co-transfected with pGL4.32-NF-kB (100 ng/well), pTL-RK (Renilla luciferase, 50 ng/well), and indicated pLVX-EF1a-IKBKB expression vector (150 ng/well) using Effectene (QIAGEN). Next day, luciferase activity was measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system and GloMax plate reader according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega).

For generation of IKBKB knocked out Jurlat E6–1 clones, two pairs of DNA oligos for guide sequences were designed to bind the target regions of exon3 of IKBKB gene using CRISPER/Cas9 technology. The sequences of the DNA oligos are as follows: sgrna1 5′; “CACCGAGATTGCCATCAAGCAGTGC” and sgrna3′rc; “AAACGCACTGCTTGATGGCAATCTC.” This pair of DNA oligo was annealed and cloned into either the PX458 (Addgene, Cambridge, MA, USA) plasmid digested by BpiI. Both of the plasmids were transfected into Jurkat clone E6–1 using AMAZA™ P3 primary cell 4D-Nucleofector™ X kit S (Lonza, Köln, Gemany) following the manufacture’s protocol. At the 24 h after transfection, the GFP positive cells were isolated and dispersed into single cells using limiting dilution. A few weeks thereafter, single clones were collected and Sanger sequencing was performed to identify the IKBKB knocked out Jurkat clone with compound heterozygous single base insertion mutations in exon3 of IKBKB gene. The loss of IKBKB protein expression of the IKBKB knocked out clone was confirmed by immunoblotting performed as indicated previously.

For reconstruction of WT or the mutant IKBKB expression to the IKBKB knocked out Jurkat E6–1 clone, the WT or mutant IKBKB cDNA was cloned into the pLVX-EF1alpha-IRES-mcherry Vector (TAKARA Bio USA, CA, USA). Lenti-X 293 T cells (TAKARA Bio USA) were plated into a T75 flask at a density of 9.4 × 106 cells overnight. The cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacture’s protocol with 10 μg of the pLVX-EF-1alpha IKBKB-IRES-mcherry vector or 10 μg of the pLVX-EF-1alpha IRES-mcherry vector, and two helper plasmids as 10 μg of psPAX2 plasmid (Addgene) and 1 μg of pMD2.G plasmids(Addgene). At 48 h after transfection, viruses were collected from the supernatants and concentrated by 100-fold using Leni-X™ Concentrator (TAKARA Bio USA). The IKBKB knocked out Jurkat E6–1 clones were infected with 10 μl of the concentrated viruses in RPMI containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 10 μg/ml of polybrene by spinning down the cells at 32 °C for 90 min.

The proband is a full-term Hispanic male with no significant prenatal or neonatal history born to non-consanguineous parents. Beginning at 6 weeks of age, he developed recurrent self-limiting episodes of rash, fever, and auto-immune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). Symptoms recurred at 7 weeks and he required packed red cell transfusion for acute anemia (hemogloblin 6.5 g/dL, supplemental table 1). During this episode, he developed erythematous papules coalescing into plaques scattered on the arms, trunk, and cheeks but most notably on the bilateral legs that are also studded with pinpoint pustules with sparing of palms, soles, groin, conjunctiva, and mucosal membranes (Fig. 1A and supplemental Fig. 1). Punch biopsy of the rash from the left leg showed superficial and deep perivascular mixed inflammatory cell dermal infiltrate with numerous eosinophils, neutrophils, and monocytes. Skin interstitium had predominantly neutrophilic inflammation with neutrophils being myeloperoxidase positive on immunohistochemistry. Histologic findings were suggestive of Sweet’s syndrome. The rash resolved gradually with topical emollient therapy.

He was found to have anti-D antibodies, in spite of sharing the same maternal blood type (O positive Rh-positive), and anti-neutrophil antibodies, suggesting an autoimmune process. He underwent a bone marrow biopsy, which showed normal hematopoiesis and no evidence of malignancy. After receiving his 2-month vaccines (second dose hepatitis B, and first doses of diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, Haemophilus influenzae type b, inactivated polio vaccine, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine [PCV13], and rotavirus), he had a third self-limiting episode of fever, rash, and anemia. At 5 months, he developed acute fever and was found to be positive for rhinovirus/enterovirus/RSV on PCR of nasopharyngeal swab. All episodes of AIHA were mild to moderate in severity, and responsive to packed red cell transfusions. Of note, his father had an history of painful urticaria-like lesions that began around age 4. The rash affected around 15% of his body surface area and was distributed over his lower abdomen, thighs, and shins. He had no associated fever, arthralgias or oral ulcers. The rash resolved by age 5 and he did not undergo medical evaluation. His mother had acute hepatitis B at age 6 with complete resolution. Both parents are otherwise healthy. The patient’s 5-year-old brother was also healthy.

At his initial visit at age 6.5 months, his hematopoietic cell counts and serum immunoglobulins were within reference ranges (Supplemental table 1). Peripheral blood lymphocyte phenotyping showed markedly decreased absolute T cells (565 cells/ml), decreased CD4 + T cells (411 cells/ml), and decreased CD8 + T cells (71 cells/ml); however, CD4:CD8 ratio was preserved. Absolute CD19 + B cells were increased (1891 cells/ml). Absolute natural killer (NK) cells (CD3-CD16 + CD56 +) were within a reference range (263 cells/ml) (Supplemental table 1). Antibody levels to five of 13 pneumococcal strains were ≥ 1.3 mg/ml; however, none of the titers was greater than 2.4. A targeted 207-gene primary immunodeficiency gene panel identified two IKBKB variants in trans (IKBKB: c.1552G > A, p.E518K; c.1676 C > T, p.T559M). The c.1676 C > T, p.T559M variant has a minor allele frequency (MAF) of 5.24e-5 in gnomAD [7] and a CADD-PHRED score of 18.6, while c.1552G > A, p.E518K has a MAF of 8.43e-6 and a CADD-PHRED score of 25.6. Both the E518K and T559M are located between the leucine-zipper and NEMO-binding regions (UniProt Entry ID: O14920) [8] (Supplemental Figs. 2A and 2B). We performed in silico structural and rotamer analysis in UCSF ChimeraX [4, 5].

The analysis predicted a loss of a hydrogen bond between adjacent alpha changes with the T559M variant; however, there was no structural change observed with the E518K variant [9, 10] (supplemental Fig. 2C–E). Co-segregation analysis revealed that the patient’s mother carried the E518K variant and father carried the T559M variant. Both parents had no pertinent history of severe and/or recurrent infection, autoinflammation, or autoimmunity.

He was started on prophylactic intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) 0.5 g/kg infusions at 9 months for infection prophylaxis given his history and genetic findings suggestive of combined immunodeficiency in the literature [1], and further live and inactivated vaccines were held. His skin rash improved and he had decreased frequency of the aforementioned febrile flares with AIHA. IVIG was discontinued by age 3 since he had no pertinent infectious history and had no hypogammaglobulinemia. He had no further episodes of autoinflammation after age 3.

Increased cytokine generation (IL-6 and TNF-α) was observed in PBMCs stimulated with LPS at 6.5 months of age (Supplemental Fig. 1B). Serum soluble biomarker analysis showed an increase in pro-inflammatory chemokines associated with macrophage recruitment and activation (Fig. 1). Expression of proteins along the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling was quantified using Western blot analysis. The patient’s PBMCs (drawn from blood at age 6.5 months) showed increased baseline phosphorylation of IKKα/b and p65 compared to that in healthy controls by 2.6-fold and 2.4-fold, respectively (after normalization with the total IKKb or p65 protein, respectively) (Fig. 2). IkBα degradation was also slightly higher (~ 20%) in the baseline but minimal degradation was observed upon TNF-α stimulation in the patient’s cells. On the other hand, phosphorylation of IkBa was decreased in the patient upon stimulation (Fig. 2). Testing of paternal PBMCs similarly showed decreased baseline IkBa and minimal phosphorylaytion of IkBa with TNFa stimulations (Supplemental Fig. 1C). Paternal serum soluble biomarkers showed elevated IL-16 and IL-18 despite being asymptomatic (Supplemental Fig. 3).

To further investigate into the mechanism underlying the variants, we generated Jurkat cell lines transduced with IKKb WT or the variants in IKKb−/− Jurkat cells using the CRISPR method. While NF-kB signaling of Jurkat T cells transduced with IKKb E518K was comparable to the WT transduced cells, the cells transduced with IKKb T559M variant showed increased basal levels of phosphorylation of IKKα/b and p65 and higher degradation of IkBα, suggesting that the T559M behaves as GOF (Fig. 3A–B). Decreased TNFa-induced IkBα phosphorylation was also observed in T559M transduced cells, probably due to the reduced IkBα expression at baseline. To further validate the effect of the mutant IKKb T559M on NF-κB activation, we used the NF-κB luciferase reporter construct in combination with either WT or the mutant constructs (Fig. 3C). The mutant T559M showed about 1.5-fold increase compared to the IKKb WT control, while E518K showed similar NF-κB activity to the WT control. Our immunoblotting and the luciferase activity data suggest that the T559M variant behaves as a GOF variant with incomplete penetrance.

The IKK kinase complex in NF-κB canonical signaling pathway is essential for innate and adaptive immunity. The IKK complex consists of two kinases (IKKα and IKKβ) and a regulatory subunit, NEMO/IKKγ [11]. Inborn errors of immunity with both LOF and GOF variants in IKBKB have been described (Supplemental Table 2). In an initial series of 4 patients (2 females, 2 males), homozygous null mutations in IKBKB (encoding IKKβ) were shown to cause SCID with normal numbers of peripheral B and T cells but very low levels of immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, and IgM) [1]. All patients had oral thrush, two had Escherichia coli sepsis, and two had parainfluenza pneumonia. NK cells were decreased in three patients. The patient’s fibroblasts showed impaired phosphorylation of IκBα in response to TNF-α stimulation. A similar phenotype was described in 4 patients from 2 different consanguineous families having homozygous nonsense mutations in IKBKB (R286X) [12]. Another study reported an 18-month-old female of first-degree consanguineous parents who presented with omphalitis, delayed umbilical cord separation, chronic diarrhea, disseminated BCGosis, and recurrent Candidiasis, who then developed Salmonella sepsis at age 3 months [13]. The patient had normal peripheral CD3+ T cells, low CD19+ B cells, decreased class-switched memory B cells (CD19+IgD−CD27+), increased transitional B cells (CD19+IgM++CD38++), and absent plasmablasts (CD19+CD38++). Additionally, a Chinese patient born to consanguineous parents presented with recurrent respiratory tract infections, chronic diarrhea, and failure to thrive, and functional testing validated a novel homozygous IKBKB (c.1183 T > C) LOF variant [14].

A subsequent study described a cohort with a de novo heterozygous missense GOF mutation in IKBKB (c.607G > A) leading to a relatively mild form of combined immunodeficiency in two kindreds [2]. The patients with GOF IKBKB variant presented with lymphopenia, hypogammaglobulinemia, recurrent respiratory infections, immune dysregulations, inflammation among others. A heterozygous GOF mutation in IKBKB (c.512A > G) has been described in an adult male with recurrent respiratory infections, cutaneous abscesses, granulomatous uveitis, cutaneous fibrotic reticulation, and severe bronchiectasis. The patient had lymphopenia and died from progressive pulmonary failure [3].

Our patient had increased levels of the biomarkers of monocyte/macrophage activation (Fig. 1). Activation of canonical NF-κB signaling induces expression of proinflammatory MIP-1α (CCL3), MDC, and MCP-1 (CCL2) which promotes monocyte/macrophage trafficking and inflammation [15]. The increase in pro-inflammatory S100A8 is likely related to increase NF-κB activation. Both S100A8 and S100A9 are NF-κB gene targets in murine and human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines [16]. Furthermore, S100A8 acts in a positive feedback loop to activate NF-κB and NLRP3 [17, 18]. Our patient’s symptoms resolved with age, suggesting that the role of IKKb T559M in promoting autoinflammation may become redundant with age.

To our knowledge, our case is the first to demonstrate a clinical phenotype of autoinflammation with organ-specific autoimmunity due to a GOF of the IKKb T559M variant. Canonical NF-κB signaling interacts with TNF-α, IL-1β, and NLRP3 signaling, and monogenic defects in these pathways may present with autoinflammation [19]. Our patient has elevated serum markers associated with macrophage recruitment and activation supporting an autoinflammatiory phenotype; however, the mechanisms leading to these findings are as yet unclear. This case expands the potential phenotypes in patients with IKBKB variants and highlights the need for further studies to understand genotype–phenotype correlations in this disease.
