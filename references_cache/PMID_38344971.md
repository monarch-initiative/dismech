# Immune responses to SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination in people with idiopathic CD4 lymphopenia.
**Authors:** Rocco JM, Boswell KL, Laidlaw E, Epling B, Anderson M, Serebryannyy L, Narpala S, O'Connell S, Kalish H, Kelly S, Porche S, Oguz C, McDermott A, Manion M, Koup RA, Lisco A, Sereti I
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaci.2023.10.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2023.10.012)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The immunogenicity of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) mRNA vaccines is variable in individuals with different inborn errors of immunity or acquired immune deficiencies and is yet unknown in people with idiopathic CD4 lymphopenia (ICL).

OBJECTIVE: We sought to determine the immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines in patients with ICL with a broad range of CD4 T-cell counts.

METHODS: Samples were collected from 25 patients with ICL and 23 age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers (HVs) after their second or third SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccine dose. Anti-spike and anti-receptor binding domain antibodies were measured. T-cell receptor sequencing and stimulation assays were performed to quantify SARS-CoV-2-specific T-cell responses.

RESULTS: The median age of ICL participants was 51 years, and their median CD4 count was 150 cells/μL; 11 participants had CD4 counts ≤100 cells/μL. Anti-spike IgG antibody levels were greater in HVs than in patients with ICL after 2 and 3 doses of mRNA vaccine. There was no detectable significant difference, however, in anti-S IgG between HVs and participants with ICL and CD4 counts >100 cells/μL. The depth of spike-specific T-cell responses by T-cell receptor sequencing was lower in individuals with ICL. Activation-induced markers and cytokine production of spike-specific CD4 T cells in participants with ICL did not differ significantly compared with HVs after 2 or 3 vaccine doses.

CONCLUSIONS: Patients with ICL and CD4 counts >100 cells/μL can mount vigorous humoral and cellular immune responses to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination; however, patients with more severe CD4 lymphopenia have blunted vaccine-induced immunity and may require additional vaccine doses and other risk mitigation strategies.

The rapid development of messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines against the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) spike protein has been a remarkable success of the coronavirus disease-2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.1 These vaccines are safe and generate high-titer anti-spike (anti-S) IgG antibodies as well as T-cell specific responses against SARS-CoV-2.2–4 However, vaccine immunogenicity is variable in people who are immunocompromised due to inherited, acquired, or iatrogenic causes. Multiple studies have attempted to define the B- and T-cell responses after mRNA vaccination in people with inborn errors of immunity, malignancies, or autoimmune diseases with the lowest anti-spike (anti-S) antibody production consistently occurring in those receiving B-cell depleting therapies or with underlying antibody deficiencies.5–10

Idiopathic CD4 lymphopenia (ICL) is a rare immune deficiency defined by a persistent CD4 T-cell count <300 cells/μl and an increased risk of autoimmunity, malignancies, and opportunistic infections such as cryptococcosis, non-tuberculous mycobacterial, and human papilloma virus (HPV)-related diseases.11–13 The degree of immunodeficiency in these patients is variable but associates with the severity of CD4 lymphopenia. Due to the rarity of this condition, no prior studies have reported on the efficacy of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination in this population, and the risk of severe COVID-19 in ICL remains unknown.

In people with HIV which also causes depletion of CD4 T-cells, it has been reported that those with CD4 counts <200 cells/μl have suboptimal anti-S antibody production and poorly detected T-cell responses after mRNA vaccination.14, 15 In contrast, individuals with well-controlled HIV on antiretroviral therapy (ART) have vaccine-induced humoral immunity comparable to the general population, emphasizing the importance of CD4 T-cells in both humoral and cellular immune responses.16, 17 Substantial immunologic and clinical differences exist, however, between ICL and people with HIV that could affect vaccine immunogenicity.18

To determine the SARS-CoV-2 specific immunity induced by mRNA vaccination in ICL, we used a well-characterized, longitudinal cohort and evaluated anti-spike and receptor binding domain (RBD) antibody levels as well as spike-specific T-cell responses after two and three doses of SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination. These findings were compared to age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers that were vaccinated at similar timepoints.

Twenty-five patients with ICL were previously enrolled in an institutional review board approved observational, prospective study (NCT0086726) on the natural history of ICL. All patients were evaluated at the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The diagnosis of ICL was confirmed by a total CD4 T-cell count ≤300 cells/μl or percentage <20% of total T cells on at least two occasions that were 6-weeks apart.11 All participants were enrolled, evaluated and those with genetic and acquired causes of lymphopenia were excluded. Details of the protocol and cohort have been recently published.18 ICL participants received SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination (BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273) by primary providers. Serum and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected 4–12 weeks after the second vaccine dose for 20 patients and between 4–7 months after a third dose for 7 patients. Two individuals with ICL were sampled twice, after their second and third dose of the vaccine. Serum and PMBCs from 23 healthy volunteers (HVs) were also obtained at similar timepoints post-second and third mRNA vaccine dose (NIH IRB-approved protocols NCT00001281 and NCT04411147). Four HVs were sampled after their second and third vaccine. PBMCs that were collected after recovery from acute COVID-19 in 5-individuals were used for the analysis of the TCR repertoire (NIH IRB-approved protocol: NCT04401436). Clinical characteristics and infection severity of these individuals is reported in Table-E1. All participants signed informed consent and procedures were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

SARS-CoV-2 WA1 and variant specific anti-S quantitative immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody responses were measured using the 10-plex Meso-Scale Discovery electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (MSD-ECLIA) as previously described (10-Plex Panel 19 and Panel 23).19 Anti-S and anti-RBD IgG, IgM, and IgA antibody responses were also measured using an enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA).20 A threshold of 3-standard deviations above the mean of the negative controls for both the spike and RBD was determined to have a specificity of 100% for a positive result and therefore was considered the threshold for positivity.

Frozen PBMCs were used to perform T-cell stimulation assays with SARS-CoV-2 wild-type pooled spike protein as previously described.20 Two peptide pools were used to measure spike-specific responses: spike pool A (peptides 1–160; residues 1–651) and spike pool B (peptides 161–316; residues 641–1273); responses from the 2 pools were summed to determine the response to full-length spike. See the Supplementary Methods for assay details and flow cytometry gating strategy in Figure E1. Spike-specific T cell frequencies were background subtracted using a DMSO only condition. Boolean gating applied in FlowJo was used to determine polyfunctional responses for CD4 T-cells and figures were generated by using Pestle (version 2) and SPICE (version 6.1).21

Complementarity determining regions (CDR)3 of T-cell receptor (TCR) β-chains were characterized using high-throughput sequencing with the immunoSEQ assay. The resulting CDR3 sequences were collapsed and filtered to quantify the absolute abundance and frequency of each unique TCRβ CDR3 region with the Adaptive Biotechnologies pipeline22 whose output was obtained from the immunoSEQ Analyzer tool of Adaptive Biotechnologies. TCR repertoire statistics computed by the Immunarch package in R (version 0.7.0) and immunoSEQ Analyzer, were compared across samples groups using the compare means function of ggpubr package (version 0.4.0.999) and lm function of the stats package (version 4.1.1) in R. SARS-CoV-2-specific depth was computed using the approach described by Snyder et al., which was further refined (see supplemental methods) to account for the systematic T cell count differences between the ICL and non-ICL samples, based on the SARS-CoV-2-specific CDR3 amino acid sequences previously reported in the ImmuneCODE and VDJdb databases.22–24 The reported depth values represent the abundance of SARS-CoV-2-specific clonotypes normalized by the total number of clones in each repertoire sample. The TCR repertoire diversity was quantified as the number of unique productive rearrangements averaged over 50 rounds of down-sampling (to minimum sequencing depth) to control for differences in overall T-cell counts (see supplemental methods).

Analyses of antibody levels and T-cell responses were performed using medians and interquartile range (IQR) and comparisons across three groups were made with the Kruskal-Wallis with statistically significant (p <0.05) differences further evaluated by Wilcoxon test and Holm correction. Two group comparisons were made using the Wilcoxon rank-sum (unpaired) and Wilcoxon signed-rank (paired) test where appropriate. Associations were evaluated with Spearman correlation analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.1) or GraphPad Prism (version 9).

For access to the original data, requests should be directed to the corresponding and co-corresponding authors.

We evaluated 25 patients with ICL, 12 women (48%) and 13 men (52%), followed at the NIH Clinical Center after SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination between 2020–2022 (Table-1). All patients were medically stable at the time of vaccination. No individuals had untreated infections or autoimmune conditions at the time of sampling, and none were receiving systemic chemotherapy for active malignancies. Three people with ICL were on stable, moderate doses of prednisone for chronic inflammatory diseases. No other ICL patients received any immunosuppressive therapy prior to or during the SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination series. A summary of opportunistic infections, autoimmune conditions, and other comorbidities are summarized in Table-E2.

Twenty participants had samples collected at a median of 39-days after their second mRNA vaccine and 7-individuals were sampled at a median of 192-days after their third dose. Median age at vaccination was 51 years (IQR: 44–62). Thirteen individuals received BNT162b2 and 12 received mRNA-1273. ICL patients were primarily White (n=21, 84%) consistent with the described demographics of this population. Twenty-three otherwise healthy persons were included as a matched control population. These HVs had a median age of 54 years (IQR: 43–60) with 13 women (56.5%) and 16 people of White ancestry (69.5%). Fourteen had samples collected at a median of 38-days after their second mRNA vaccination and 13 at a median of 119-days after their third vaccination. Seven volunteers were vaccinated with BNT162b2 and 16 received mRNA-1273. No participants had prior COVID-19 or positive anti-nucleocapsid antibodies after two doses of vaccine. However, two individuals (1-ICL and 1-HV) sampled after the third vaccine dose were found to have positive anti-nucleocapsid antibodies suggesting prior COVID-19 before their third vaccination.

The median CD4 T-cell count in participants with ICL was 150 cells/μL (range: 4–286) at the time of vaccination, and 11 individuals (44%) had a CD4 count ≤100 cells/μL. Additional lymphocyte subset analysis demonstrated a median CD8 count of 167 cells/μL (IQR: 127–457) and a B-cell count of 119 cells/μL (IQR: 92–265), respectively. Immunoglobulin levels were normal with IgG 932 mg/dL (IQR: 759–1159), IgM 87 mg/dL (IQR: 58–132), and IgA 136 mg/dL (IQR: 113–197). Vaccination was well-tolerated without significant adverse events.

Although determining the clinical efficacy of SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination in ICL was not feasible in this study, we separately reviewed all documented cases of acute COVID-19 on our natural history protocol of people with ICL (NCT0086726) which enrolled 108 individuals and after exclusion of participants with identified genetic or acquired cause for CD4 lymphopenia, maintained on study 91 individuals with ICL.18 In this larger parent cohort, we identified 25 episodes of acute COVID-19, and all but three cases occurred in vaccinated individuals. The median age of ICL patients who developed acute COVID-19 was 44-years old (range: 24–72). Fourteen (56%) were female and the median CD4 T-cell count was 52 cells/μL (range: 22–263). All individuals with ICL that developed acute COVID-19 had mild disease with none requiring high-flow supplemental oxygen, intubation, or intensive care. Based on treatment availability at the time of infection, patients received no treatment (n=9), nirmatrelvir-ritonavir (n=10), monoclonal antibody therapy (n=3), or remdesivir (n=1). The clinical details of each case of acute COVID-19 in people with ICL are summarized in Table-E3.

Quantitative SARS-CoV-2 anti-S IgG antibody responses were significantly lower in patients with ICL (anti-S: IgG 9056 AU/mL, IQR: 512–42110), compared to HVs (50799 AU/mL, IQR: 47927–59756) after 2-doses of mRNA vaccine by MSD-ECLIA (p=0.001). There was also variability within the ICL cohort, with the lowest anti-S levels observed in patients with CD4 ≤100 cells/μL (448 AU/mL, IQR: 43.5–6038) (Figure 1A–B). There was no detectable difference in anti-S IgG between HVs and those with ICL and CD4 >100 cells/μL (38454 AU/mL, IQR: 10950–54516).

Patients with ICL had a significant increase in anti-S IgG production when compared to their pre-vaccination timepoint and there was a strong correlation between anti-S IgG and CD4 count at the time of vaccination (R=0.55, p=0.0055 by Spearman’s correlation) (Figure 1D–F). There were no significant correlations between anti-S IgG and B-cells, total IgG, CD3 T-cells, or CD8 T-cells (Figure-E3) although a significant correlation between B-cells and total IgG levels (R=0.66, p<0.001) was observed (Figure-E2). Variant-specific (beta, gamma, delta, lambda, mu) anti-S IgG levels were also evaluated by MSD-ECLIA and showed a similar pattern across the ICL cohort with a consistent decrease in quantitative anti-S IgG titers compared to the HV population (Figure-E4A).

Seven patients with ICL had antibody testing after boosting with a third mRNA vaccine dose. No difference was observed in anti-S IgG levels in those with ICL and CD4 ≤100 compared to those with CD4 between 100–300, although only 2-individuals had CD4 less than 100 cells/μL in this subgroup. However, anti-S IgG antibody levels in ICL patients remained lower than those in HVs: 39726 AU/mL (IQR: 15074–50738) vs 64474 AU/mL (IQR: 50385–65423), respectively (Figure 1C, p=0.037). Of note, patients with ICL were sampled later after their third vaccination than HVs (192-days vs 119-days, p=0.001), suggesting the difference in antibody levels between the two groups could have been even smaller after the third dose. There were also overall small differences in variant-specific anti-S IgG levels after a third mRNA vaccine (Figure-E4B). In the two individuals with ICL (baseline CD4 T-cells of 75 and 188, respectively) that were sampled after both the second and third vaccine dose, anti-S IgG levels did not significantly increase after the third dose (Figure-E5).

Antibody responses were further evaluated using ELISA to determine SARS-CoV-2 anti-S and anti-RBD IgG, IgM, and IgA. Again, there were significant differences in anti-S and anti-RBD IgG antibody levels in those with ICL and CD4 ≤100 cells/μL when compared to HVs (Figure 2). No significant differences were found in anti-S or anti-RBD IgM post-vaccination. Anti-S and anti-RBD IgA levels were lower in ICL with CD4 ≤100 cells/μL, and although the IgA response was lower even in those with baseline CD4 >100 cells/μL, this did not reach statistical significance.

In 9 patients with ICL that had available PBMCs 4–10 weeks collected after the second mRNA vaccination, T-cell stimulation assays were performed and compared to 7 matched HVs at the same timepoint. T-cell stimulations were also done using pre-vaccination PBMC samples for 7 patients with ICL. After stimulation with SARS-CoV-2 spike peptides, levels of activated (CD154+CD69+) CD4 T-cells did not differ between HVs and ICL patients (Figure-3A). Activated CD4 T-cells producing IFNγ, any Th1 cytokine (IFNγ+, TNFα+, or IL2+), or IL4+IL13+ were also not significantly different between the groups (Figure 3B–D). Although there was a trend of increased frequencies of spike-specific CD4 T-cells in HVs, the variability in both cohorts and the smaller sample size limited the identification of statistically significant differences. Patients with ICL and CD4 counts ≤100 cells/μL consistently had the lowest CD4-specific responses, however, specific comparisons between those with CD4 T-cells ≤100 compared to those with CD4 between 100–300 cells/μL were not feasible due to the small sample size of each group.

T-cell stimulations were repeated in 13 HVs and 7 ICL patients after a third mRNA vaccine dose. Activation induced markers and cytokine producing CD4 T-cells did not differ between the groups (Figure 3E–H). T-cell subsets showed a trend toward an overall greater percentage of SARS-CoV-2 specific central memory CD4 T-cells in HVs vs an increased percentage of effector memory CD4 T-cells in ICL. This pattern was consistent in those that received two- or three- doses (Figure-E6), however it was not statistically significant due to low numbers of SARS-CoV-2 specific T-cells overall, and predetermined thresholds (2-times greater than background levels and at least >20 cells) were not met in all individuals, decreasing the power of this comparison. CD4 T-cells from ICL patients expressed more PD-1+ and Ki67+ than HVs. SARS-CoV-2-specific CD8 T-cells were poorly detected in both HVs and ICL patients as has been described post-mRNA vaccination in other cohorts of healthy or immunocompromised individuals (Figure-E7).5, 25 Similar trends were observed after two or three doses of mRNA vaccines.

Compared to pre-vaccination, ICL patients did have a significant increase in SARS-CoV-2 activated (CD154+CD69+) CD4 T-cells after two doses of mRNA vaccination (Figure 4A) (p=0.038). There was also an increase in Th1 cytokine-producing CD4 T-cells, but this was not statistically significant (Figure 4B–C). Activated spike-specific CD4 T-cells correlated with baseline CD4 T-cell count (r=0.56, p=0.026 by Spearman’s correlation) (Figure 4D). Polyfunctional CD4 T-cell cytokine responses after two or three doses of mRNA vaccine did not show a marked difference between the groups (Figure 4E).

T-cell receptor (TCR) profiling for SARS-CoV-2 spike-specific T-cell clonotypes demonstrated lower spike-specific TCR depth, which was quantified as the clonotype abundance normalized with respect to the total number of cells with productive repertoire sequences (controlling for differences in overall T-cell counts), in those with ICL after two mRNA vaccinations compared to HVs (Figure 5). A similar pattern was seen for clonotypes predicted to be restricted by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class-I and class-II. TCR repertoire diversity was evaluated as the number of unique productive rearrangements after down-sampling to the minimum sequencing depth across all samples (rearrangement counts averaged over 50 rounds of down-sampling) again to control for differences in absolute CD4 T-cell counts, and this also showed those with ICL have TCR repertoires with lower diversity (Figure-5C). Since age and sex can impact TCR sequencing results, this analysis was further refined using linear regression by adjusting for age and sex and although the trend was similar the differences were no longer statistically significant potentially due to the small sample size (Table-E4). Comparatively, HVs post-vaccination and unvaccinated persons who had recently recovered from COVID-19 (within 4–6 weeks) showed similar spike-specific TCR depth, and both were greater than people with ICL post-vaccination (Figure-E8). After three doses of mRNA vaccine, the difference in SARS-CoV-2 specific TCR depth was not significantly different between these three groups although there was a trend towards reduced SARS-CoV-2 normalized clonotype abundance and TCR richness in ICL (Figure 5D–F). Findings remained similar after controlling for age and sex with linear regression.

ICL is a rare immune deficiency characterized by low CD4 T-cells and opportunistic infections including HPV-related diseases and cryptococcosis.18 In contrast to other immunodeficiency syndromes such as common variable immunodeficiency (CVID), severe and/or recurrent bacterial or viral respiratory infections are rarely described in ICL. In fact, none of the individuals with ICL that developed acute COVID-19 on our natural history protocol experienced severe disease. Additionally, to our knowledge no cases of severe COVID-19 in ICL have been reported in the literature to date. Increased risk mitigation strategies, early treatment, and vaccination likely contributed to this protection, and people with ICL often have immunoglobulin levels in the normal range.18 However, since the mRNA vaccine platform is novel, and immune responses in immunocompromised patients are extremely variable, we explored vaccine-elicited immunity to SARS-CoV-2 post-mRNA vaccination in a cohort of people with ICL.6–9

We found that the vaccines were well-tolerated, and although ICL is associated with increased rates of autoimmunity,26 flares in underlying immune dysregulation did not occur as has been described in other autoimmune conditions.27–29 Most ICL individuals mounted proper antibody and T-cell responses, and in participants with CD4 >100 cells/μL, these responses were in fact indistinguishable from an otherwise healthy, age-matched population.

Anti-S IgG levels correlated strongly with CD4 count in ICL patients similar to findings in people with HIV.15 T-cell lymphopenia and low CD4 T-cell counts have been associated with poor anti-S IgG antibody responses in both inherited and acquired immunodeficiencies.7, 14, 15 A third ‘booster’ dose did appear to be beneficial in people with ICL increasing anti-S IgG and SAR-CoV-2 TCR specific depth consistent with findings described in other immunocompromised populations.30–32 After three doses, there were no observable differences based on CD4 T-cell count (≤100 vs 100–300 cells/μL) within the ICL cohort, however only 2-individuals with ICL had baseline CD4 ≤100 cells/μL limiting statistical comparisons.

Mechanisms contributing to poor antibody responses in ICL patients with lower CD4 T-cells are unclear; total B-cells were quantitatively lower in ICL compared to HVs, potentially accounting for this association, however total immunoglobulins were normal, and IgG levels did not differ between ICL and HVs. Additionally, there was no correlation with anti-S antibody levels and B-cell counts or total IgG as was seen with CD4 T-cells. Priming of CD4 T-cells, preexisting cross-reactive CD4 T-cells, and T-follicular helper cells are all involved in developing adequate humoral immune responses.33–35 Therefore, it is possible that the mere quantitative, or rather more complex qualitative dysfunction in the CD4 T-cell subset, or both, may contribute to the lower antibody titers found in more severely lymphopenic people with ICL.

There was no statistically significant difference in activation-induced markers or cytokine-producing SARS-CoV-2 specific T-cells when compared to HVs but the observed differences seemed primarily driven by low numbers of circulating T-cells which also resulted in the poor antibody responses in individuals with a CD4 <100 cells/uL. This has been identified in people with HIV and CD4 T-cells <200, where humoral and cellular immune responses post-mRNA vaccination are difficult to detect14 and suggests that peripheral CD4 counts may represent a predictor of immunogenicity of mRNA vaccine platforms in different clinical contexts.

Cellular immunity has been found to play an essential role in immune protection from severe disease, and vaccine-induced T-cell responses against SARS-CoV-2 are necessary, as they have a long half-life and demonstrate preserved immune protection across new variants.36 However, due to the difficulties in standardizing functional T-cell assays, immune correlates of protection for cellular immunity have not been established. We utilized multiple modalities, including T-cell stimulation assays and TCR sequencing, to evaluate SARS-CoV-2 specific T-cell responses in our cohort. Although patients with ICL have an inherent cellular immune deficiency, we were able to detect SARS-CoV-2 specific T-cells in all individuals tested. The correlation between the CD4 counts, anti-spike antibodies, and spike specific T-cell responses further emphasizes the dependence of mRNA vaccine response on preserved CD4 T-cell number and function.

This study is limited by its relatively small sample size, however ICL is a rare immune deficiency, and systematic sampling of a larger cohort was difficult during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although we performed a detailed analysis on SARS-CoV-2 vaccine immunogenicity, we were unable to determine its efficacy in preventing severe COVID-19 and death as no cases of severe disease have been reported in ICL patients. We do provide a quantitative description of immune responses post-mRNA vaccination, and although immune correlates of protection are continuously debated, our study utilized multiple well-established measures of immune protection including quantitative anti-S antibodies, T-cell stimulation assays, and TCR profiling. Finally, since participants were not systematically sampled longitudinally, the duration of these responses remains yet unknown.

Overall, we conclude that people with ICL and CD4 T-cells >100 mount expected humoral and cellular immune responses to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. Those with the lowest CD4 T-cell counts have lower antibody levels and SARS-CoV-2 specific T-cells, but these responses are still present and utilizing additional boosters in this more vulnerable population is likely required. Continued work is needed to define the pathogenesis of ICL as it pertains to vaccine immunogenicity and specific pathogen susceptibility.
