# Efficacy of a K(+) Channel Agonist, XEN1101, For Preserving Contractility in Mouse Models of Hypokalemic Periodic Paralysis.
**Authors:** Chanchykov V, Iype S, Quinonez M, Wu F, Cannon S
**Journal:** Muscle Nerve (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mus.70159](https://doi.org/10.1002/mus.70159)

## Content

INTRODUCTION/AIMS: Effective management remains lacking for recurrent episodes of acute weakness in hypokalemic periodic paralysis (HypoPP). We assessed the efficacy of a second-generation potassium channel agonist, XEN1101, to prevent and abort the low-K+ induced loss of force in mouse models of HypoPP.

METHODS: An ex vivo contractility assay was used to interrogate the efficacy of XEN1101 for preserving contractile force and for enhancing recovery of force in the setting of a low-K+ challenge for HypoPP mice carrying the sodium channel NaV1.4-R669H or the calcium channel CaV1.1-R528H mutations.

RESULTS: The acute loss of force for HypoPP muscle, triggered by a 2 mM K+ challenge, was prevented by low micromolar XEN1101, with an effective concentration of 0.30 μM for 50% protection. Application of 1 μM XEN1101, after the onset of 2 mM K+ induced weakness, restored the peak contractile force (70%-100% of baseline).

DISCUSSION: The KV7 potassium channel agonist XEN1101 is effective as both a prophylactic agent and as abortive therapy for management of low-K+ induced weakness in murine models of HypoPP. XEN1101 is more potent than the first-generation Kv7 agonist, retigabine, in our murine models of HypoPP and is also better tolerated in patients. These improvements provide a rationale for future clinical trials of XEN1101 in HypoPP patients.

Hypokalemic periodic paralysis (HypoPP) is a rare ion channelopathy of skeletal muscle that presents with recurrent attacks of weakness lasting for tens of minutes to hours [ 1 , 2 ]. Weakness often occurs in association with hypokalemia (K+ < 3.5 mEq/L), and episodes may be triggered by actions that lower serum K+ such as carbohydrate ingestion, stress, or diarrhea. Symptomatic management by lifestyle changes, oral K+ supplements, and K+‐sparing diuretics (e.g., eplerenone) is only partially effective [ 3 ]. The response rate is only about 50% for prophylactic management by chronic use of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors [ 4 ] (e.g., acetazolamide or dichlorphenamide), which frequently cause paresthesia and have a four to sevenfold increased risk of developing renal calculi [ 5 ]. A recent survey revealed that half of 49 HypoPP patients reported their daily lives were challenged by attacks of weakness [ 6 ].

Strategic approaches to improve the therapeutic management of HypoPP have been guided by advances in understanding the pathomechanisms for episodic attacks of weakness [ 7 ] and the development of validated animal models for preclinical studies [ 8 , 9 ]. HypoPP is caused by missense mutations of CaV1.1 calcium channels or of NaV1.4 sodium channels that produce an anomalous leakage current that, in turn, renders the muscle fiber susceptible to low‐K+ induced depolarization of the resting potential and refractory loss of excitability. Based on this mechanism, one interventional approach has been to promote hyperpolarization of the fiber resting potential by pharmacologic activation of potassium channels. Early studies on muscle fiber bundles from HypoPP patients showed KATP channel agonists (pinacidil or cromakalim) improved contractility during a low‐K+ challenge [ 10 ]. Clinical use of KATP agonists was not viable, however, because of the high supratherapeutic concentrations required (100 μM) which would cause intolerable vasodilation and hypotension [ 11 ]. Another K+ channel agonist, retigabine (ezogabine) acting on KV7 channels, was approved for adjuvant therapy in partial seizures [ 12 ], but then removed from distribution with a black‐box warning of pigmentary changes in skin and retina [ 13 ]. The potassium channel targets for retigabine (KV7.2—KV7.5) are primarily neuronal, and unexpectedly the drug also suppressed muscle fiber after‐discharges in myotonia [ 14 , 15 ]. Based on this new functional demonstration that skeletal muscle is a target, we used our genetically engineered mouse models of HypoPP (CaV1.1‐R528H and NaV1.4‐R669H) to show retigabine (1–2 μM) effectively prevents low‐K+ induced loss of force [ 16 ]. Second‐generation KV7 agonists have greater target specificity and fewer side effects, as supported by a Phase2b RCT for XEN1101 [ 17 ]. We now test the efficacy of XEN1101 for both the prevention and abortive rescue from low‐K+ induced loss of force in our mouse models of HypoPP.

This study was approved by the David Geffen School of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Genetically engineered mice with targeted knock‐in mutations of the NaV1.4 sodium channel were used for models of HypoPP (corresponding to human NaV1.4‐R669H) and HyperPP (corresponding to human NaV1.4‐M1592V), as previously described [ 8 , 18 ]. We also studied a second knock‐in mouse model for HypoPP that recapitulates the calcium channel mutation, CaV1.1‐R528H [ 9 ], which is the most common cause of HypoPP in humans. Based on our prior studies with these mouse models [ 8 , 9 ], the optimal paradigm for observing the low‐K+ dependent loss of force is to use the soleus muscle for NaV1.4‐R669H and the extensor digitorum longus (EDL) muscle for CaV1.1‐R528H, with both preparations obtained from homozygous mutant mice. For the HyperPP model, however, the viability to full term of homozygous mutant animals is very low [ 18 ], and so the soleus muscle was obtained from heterozygous NaV1.4‐M1592V mice. Mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, and soleus or EDL muscles were dissected out from the hind limbs.

The force of isometric contraction was measured in response to direct field stimulation by a pair of wire electrodes (Myobath, World Precision Instruments, Sarasota FL). A single shock or tetanic burst stimulation (80 mA, 40 pulses of 0.4 ms duration at 100 Hz for soleus; 30 pulses at 250 Hz for EDL) was applied every 2 min. All bath solutions contained 0.25 μM D‐tubocurarine (Sigma‐Aldrich) to prevent any contribution to muscle excitation from motor nerve endings. The bath was continuously bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2, maintained at 36°C, and contained 118 mM NaCl, 4.75 mM KCl, 2.54 mM CaCl2, 1.18 mM MgSO4, 1.18 mM NaH2PO4, 24.8 mM NaHCO3, and 10 mM glucose. The low‐K+ (2 mM) and high‐K+ (10 mM) solutions were prepared by replacement with NaCl to maintain a constant osmolality of 290 mOsm. A generic source of XEN1101 (Xenon Pharmaceuticals), N‐[4‐(6‐fluoro‐3,4‐dihydro‐1H‐isoquinolin‐2‐yl)‐2,6‐dimethylphenyl]‐3,3‐dimethylbutanamide, CAS No. 100934403305 AbaChemScene, was prepared as a 100 mM stock solution in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then diluted to the appropriate working concentration with the bath solution.

The improvement of contractility for XEN1101‐exposed muscle was quantified as the relative preservation of peak force during a low‐K+ challenge. An internal control was measured for each muscle preparation (loss of force in low‐K+ without drug) because the minimum force in 2 mM K+ is variable (40% to 80% of control). This ratio (loss_no_drug–loss_XEN1101)/loss_no_drug, was computed using the minimum force when spontaneous oscillations occurred for NaV1.4‐R669H, as reported previously [ 16 ]. The significance for differences in force observed in the pair‐wise comparison in no‐drug versus drug was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (socsistatistics.com). The experimentally observed size effect of XEN1101 was large and the throughput for the assay was low (one animal per test [XEN1101]), and therefore the number of observations at a single [XEN1101] is only 3–4 in the dose–response curve. This pragmatic limit on the number of observations restricts the minimum testable p‐value to ≤ 0.01.

The time course of our assay to quantify the efficacy of XEN1101 included an internal control for each muscle, as shown in Figure 1B. The peak tetanic force was measured every two minutes, with a time‐average (0–10 min) of the initial baseline response in 4.75 mM K+ used to normalize the amplitude of all subsequent measurements. A 2 mM K+ challenge was then applied for 30 min as the control to determine severity for the loss of force, which spontaneously oscillated through slow cycles of weakness and partial recovery for HypoPP NaV1.4‐R669H soleus muscle [ 8 ] (Figure 1B) or declined to a steady loss for HypoPP CaV1.1‐R528H EDL muscle [ 9 ] (Figure 2B). For muscles dissected from mice with either HypoPP mutation, the minimum force in 2 mM K+ is variable (40%–80% of control), which is why an internal control (i.e., no drug) is required to subsequently quantify the XEN1101 effect, compared to the preservation of ≥ 90% peak force during a 2 mM K+ challenge in WT. A return to control K+ of 4.75 mM was then used to confirm full recovery of peak force (40–50 min interval, Figure 1B). The bathing solution was then exchanged with 4.75 mM K+ plus XEN1101 to allow pretreatment, which was then followed by a second 2 mM K+ challenge in the continued presence of XEN1101. The representative trial in Figure 1B shows the loss of force for HypoPP NaV1.4‐R669H in 2 mM K+ was fully prevented by 3 μM XEN1101.

We tested the efficacy of pretreatment with 1 μM XEN1101 to attenuate the K+‐dependent loss of peak tetanic force for two knock‐in mouse models of HypoPP (NaV1.4‐R669H [ 8 ] and CaV1.1‐R528H [ 9 ]) and one model of HyperPP (NaV1.4‐M1592V [ 18 ]). The representative data in Figure 2 show the K+‐induced loss of force in a control trial (black symbols) and the improvement observed when the same muscle was tested in the presence of 1 μM XEN1101. There was nearly full protection from loss of force for both the NaV1.4 and the CaV1.1 HypoPP models (Figure 2A,B), but limited protection of only about 40% for the HyperPP model (Figure 2C). This difference was consistently observed for additional assays at 1 μM XEN1101 in all three mouse models (Figure 2D, p < 0.002 R669H vs. M1592V, p < 0.001 for R528H vs. M1592V, AVOVA n = 3 for each), and so further studies were only performed in the HypoPP models.

The dose‐dependent efficacy for XEN1101 was quantified by calculating the relative reduction for the severity in the loss of force in the presence of varying concentrations of drug during a K+‐challenge. With this prophylactic usage, XEN1101 was equally effective in both models of HypoPP (NaV1.4‐R669H measured for the soleus muscle EC50 = 0.30 ± 0.68 μM, and CaV1.1‐R528H measured for extensor digitorum longus muscle, EC50 = 0.28 ± 0.092 μM), as shown in Figure 3. The prophylactic effect of XEN1101 was statistically distinguishable from no drug for concentrations ≥ 0.1 μM (p < 0.05), and even more so for ≥ 0.3 μM (p < 0.01). By comparison, the relative protection of only 0.3 in 1.0 μM XEN1101 for the HyperPP NaV1.4‐M1592V mouse (Figure 2C) is equivalent to a fivefold lower potency.

The ability to abort an attack of weakness and hasten recovery was tested by application of XEN1101 after the onset of the loss of force during a low‐K+ challenge. The two paired muscles dissected from the same animal were tested simultaneously, in separate muscle bath chambers. A 2 mM K+ challenge was applied to both muscle chambers, with the control remaining drug‐free and 1 μM XEN1101 applied to the test chamber after confirming a loss of force. As shown by the two representative experiments for each HypoPP model in Figure 4, the temporal pattern for the loss of force was initially very similar for the two muscles from the same animal, but then diverged with substantial recovery in XEN1101 even while the bath K+ remained low at 2 mM.

We now show that a second‐generation Kv7 channel agonist, XEN1101, is effective to improve contractility during a low‐K+ induced loss of force in two mouse models of HypoPP (NaV1.4‐R669H and CaV1.1‐R528H). Our ex vivo contraction studies show XEN1101 has an EC50 of 0.30 μM for prophylactic suppression of low‐K+ induced loss of force in HypoPP susceptible muscle, which is threefold more potent than we reported for retigabine [ 16 ]. Pharmacokinetic studies in humans show this concentration is achievable with 20 mg/day dosing [ 13 ]. Like retigabine, XEN1101 also rescues the recovery of force in the continued presence of low K+, and thereby supports the possible use as abortive therapy in HypoPP patients. Importantly, the consistency for the beneficial effects of retigabine and XEN1101 further supports the view that KV7 channels are the primary site of action, as previously implied by abolishing the retigabine effect with a low concentration (10 μM) of the KV7 inhibitor XE991 [ 16 ].

Potassium channel agonists are less effective for mitigating the high‐K+ induced loss of force in HyperPP. The KATP channel agonists (pinacidil and cromakalim at 100 μM) were unable to prevent the loss of force in human HyperPP fibers exposed to 9 mM K+, and 300 μM pinacidil was required for recovery in 6 mM K+ [ 11 ]. Herein, we found XEN1101 had a 5‐fold lower potency for preventing loss of force in HyperPP NaV1.4‐M1592V soleus muscle compared with low‐K+ challenges for either of the two HypoPP mutant lines. The reduced efficacy for both classes of K+ channel openers in HyperPP may be a consequence of the depolarized shift for the K+ Nernst potential (EK) during ictal hyperkalemia, which in turn would attenuate the beneficial hyperpolarization of the resting potential. For example, a doubling of external K+ from 4.5 to 9 mM will depolarize EK by 18 mV at 35°C. Another possible cause for the difference in efficacy may be due to the anomalous depolarizing current to be overcome by a K+ channel opener [ 20 ]. In HypoPP, the causative gating pore leakage current is relatively low amplitude that can be fully compensated by a modest increase of the muscle fiber conductance to K+. For HyperPP, however, the NaV1.4 gain of function defects produce a much larger pathogenic increase in the persistent Na+ current that requires a commensurately larger enhancement of a K+ current to prevent depolarization‐induced weakness.

This preclinical study, based on muscle contractility in mouse models of periodic paralysis, provides proof‐of‐principle that XEN1101, a second‐generation KV7 agonist, may be effective for prevention and abortive therapy against acute attacks of weakness in patients with HypoPP resulting from mutations in either CaV1.1 or NaV1.4 channels. XEN1101 is more potent than retigabine for preserving muscle contractility in a provocative challenge with low K+, and XEN1101 is not associated with the adverse pigmentation changes that led to removal of retigabine. Taken together, the potent efficacy of XEN1101 for symptomatic management of HypoPP in this preclinical study and the safety of XEN1101 in clinical trials provide support for moving forward with clinical trials in patients with periodic paralysis.
