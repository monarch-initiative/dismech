# Gene Therapy for Inherited Bleeding Disorders
**Authors:** Valder R. Arruda, Jesse Weber, Benjamin J. Samelson-Jones
**Journal:** Seminars in Thrombosis and Hemostasis (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1055/s-0041-1722862](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0041-1722862)

## Content

AbstractDecades of preclinical and clinical studies developing gene therapy for hemophilia are poised to bear fruit with current promising pivotal studies likely to lead to regulatory approval. However, this recent success should not obscure the multiple challenges that were overcome to reach this destination. Gene therapy for hemophilia A and B benefited from advancements in the general gene therapy field, such as the development of adeno-associated viral vectors, as well as disease-specific breakthroughs, like the identification of B-domain deleted factor VIII and hyperactive factor IX Padua. The gene therapy field has also benefited from hemophilia B clinical studies, which revealed for the first time critical safety concerns related to immune responses to the vector capsid not anticipated in preclinical models. Preclinical studies have also investigated gene transfer approaches for other rare inherited bleeding disorders, including factor VII deficiency, von Willebrand disease, and Glanzmann thrombasthenia. Here we review the successful gene therapy journey for hemophilia and pose some unanswered questions. We then discuss the current state of gene therapy for these other rare inherited bleeding disorders and how the lessons of hemophilia gene therapy may guide clinical development.

Keywords:gene therapy, hemophilia, inherited bleeding disorders, AAV, lentiviral, factor IX, factor VIII

Inherited bleeding disorders are a heterogeneous group of genetic diseases that cause spontaneous and/or excessive hemorrhage after trauma.1–8Several features of these diseases make them amenable to gene therapy. Foremost, they are mostly well-defined monogenic disorders with a clear genotype–phenotype relationship. Second, the clinical experience with replacement therapies provides the rationale that correcting the deficiency is sufficient to ameliorate the bleeding phenotype. Gene therapy has the potential to address many of the limitations of current protein- and cell-based replacement therapies, including limited availability, frequent intravenous (IV) administrations, and sawtooth pharmacokinetics (►Fig. 1). The goal of gene therapy is to supply a continuous uninterrupted amount of transgene within the therapeutic window (►Fig. 1), potentially avoiding periods of supra-and subtherapeutic levels, and removing the need for frequent IV administrations.

Hemophilia A (HA) and hemophilia B (HB) have been on the cutting-edge of gene transfer clinical development.9–13These X-linked bleeding disorders result from an absence or deficiency of coagulation factor VIII (FVIII) or factor IX (FIX), due to mutations in eitherF8orF9, respectively. Clinical trials of early approaches including adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors targeting skeletal muscle for HB and ex vivo plasmid DNA electroporation of skin fibroblasts or systemic administration of retrovirus vectors for HA were initiated over 20 years ago. Only AAV liver-directed gene transfer continued clinical development with ongoing pivotal clinical trials for HA and HB (►Table 1). Other viral or nonviral vector strategies are also being investigated to increase the options for populations that likely require distinct approaches, such as young children and patients with neutralizing antibodies against AAV. Because of the promising safety profile in humans, longevity of expression, and well-established production methods, AAV has become the preferred vector in gene therapy clinical trials. In 2017, the first AAV gene therapy drug was approved in the United States. This AAV serotype 2 (AAV2) vector contains a RPE65 transgene for the treatment of the inherited degenerative retinal disease Leber congenital amaurosis.14More recently, an AAV9 vector encoding a survival motor neuron 1 transgene for the treatment of pediatric patients with spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)15was also approved. The hallmark of these AAV drugs is their ability to safely and durably ameliorate the disease phenotype after a single vector administration.13

Gene-replacement therapies for genetic disease require long-term expression of the therapeutic transgene (►Fig. 1) without toxicity. There are two main strategies: (1) the use of nonintegrating vectors targeting non- or slowly dividing cells and (2) the use of integrating vectors in dividing cells. Both strategies have been developed for hemophilia with the former now in late-phase clinical trials (►Table 1) and the latter in recently initiated clinical studies targeting hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) for expression of FVIII in platelets.16,17

AAV is a replication-deficient virus that requires another virus for natural infection. AAV vectors rely on the transduction of nondividing cells to establish their permanence with the episome acting as an extrachromosomal piece of DNA that is continuously transcribed. This is ideal for hemophilia since both FIX and FVIII are naturally made in the liver, though the cell compartments for each protein is different (►Table 2). FIX synthesis is restricted to the hepatocyte, which is the target cell of current AAV gene therapy strategies. However, FVIII synthesis is from endothelial cells (ECs), mostly within the liver sinusoidal ECs (LSECs).18,19Thus, FVIII expression in hepatocytes is ectopic, which raises concerns discussed below.

There are two manufacturing systems used for the production of AAV vectors in ongoing clinical trials for hemophilia.20,21In the mammalian system, cells are adhesive; whereas in the insect cell system, the cells are in suspension, which facilitates the scale-up required for clinical trials. Naked plasmids (mammalian system) or baculovirus expression vectors (insect cell system) introduce the therapeutic transgene flanked by AAV inverted terminal repeats as well as the AAVcapandrepgenes needed for capsid formation. Although the final products are both AAV vectors that transduce human liver cells in vivo, these vectors are not identical, as discussed below with regard to emerging differences in gene therapy trial for hemophilia (►Table 1).

AAV episomes can stably express their transgenes for the lifetime of the cell, shown in nonhuman primate (NHP) neurons for more than 15 years22and HA and HB dog hepatocytes for more than 7 years.23However, when a transduced cell divides, the episome is not replicated and will be diluted in rapidly dividing cells. This is a major obstacle for liver-directed approaches in young children where episomal dilution will lead to lower therapeutic transgene expression. Integrating vectors, like retroviral and lentiviral (LV) vectors, are frequently used for ex vivo genetic modifications. Limited data in large animal models of hemophilia have used LVs for ex vivo modifications of HSCs16and in vivo delivery to the liver.24LVs insert their transgene into the genome of transduced cells. This means that every time the transduced cell divides, the daughter cells will carry along the novel genetic information, preventing dilution of the transgene. LV transduction of LSECs has the potential for long-term expression of FVIII, which has been suggested in murine studies,25,26but large animal study models are needed to determine the translation potential of this approach. The integration event itself is a stochastic process, which does have the potential for oncogenesis.27Vector-related oncogenesis was observed in an early clinical trial testing a retroviral vector for severe combined immunodeficiency disorder. Treatment resulted in improvement of the disease phenotype, but also drove leukemia in several subjects.28,29However, the current generation of LV vectors has incorporated several technologies to limit oncogenesis30with several ex vivo clinical trials for genetic disease including sickle cell anemia and thalassemia showing promising results.11

Activated FIX (FIXa) is a serine protease that requires the cofactor activity of FVIIIa to efficiently activate factor X (FX), which is the rate-limiting step of sustained coagulation. HA and HB affect approximately 1:5,000 or 1:30,000 live-born males worldwide, respectively. Approximately, a third of patients have de novo mutations. The bleeding phenotype is highly dependent on the residual factor activity. Patients with severe disease (<1% of normal factor activity) benefit from prophylactic IV factor infusions to prevent spontaneous bleeding.31Prophylactic therapy is highly efficacious in preventing bleeds, establishing the therapeutic rationale for gene transfer. However, prophylaxis is both expensive32and time-consuming with limited adherence.33Neutralizing alloantibodies against FIX or FVIII, termed inhibitors, develop in up to 10% of HB patients34,35and 20 to 30% of severe HA patients.36The hope is that gene therapy would provide a cost-effective easy-to-use long-term therapy supplanting the need for frequent prophylactic IV infusions.

The complementary DNA (cDNA) of F9 is approximately 1.6 kb, which is easily accommodated within the approximately 4.7kb packing capacity of AAV. Because of the small cDNA size and low risk of inhibitor formation, HB was an early target for AAV-based gene therapy. In 2000, a dose-escalation clinical trial for HB evaluated intramuscular administration of an AAV2 vector into multiple sites of skeletal muscle.37,38When this study was initiated, targeting skeletal muscle, rather than hepatocytes, was considered a more conservative approach, given the lack of experience in parenteral injection of AAV in humans. Previous in vitro39and preclinical40studies had demonstrated that ectopic expression in skeletal muscle could produce functional FIX. Safety assessments showed that the therapy was well tolerated without toxicity, there was limited systemic spread of the vector, and there was no vector germline transmission. However, only minimal circulating FIX levels were observed. Nevertheless, local FIX expression or vector-derived sequences were sustained for at least 3.7 and 10 years, respectively.41,42Based on the reassuring safety data of this first in-human AAV muscle-directed gene therapy trial, a liver-directed gene therapy strategy for HB was initiated. The rationale to target the liver was based on (1) hepatocytes being the endogenous site of FIX synthesis and (2) for a given vector dose, there may be an advantage of FIX expression from the liver compared with skeletal muscle. This is salient for clinical development because, for a given gene therapy strategy, the safety profile is directly correlated to the vector dose used.

In 2006, a clinical trial reported on the safety and efficacy of AAV2 liver-targeted FIX gene therapy administered via hepatic artery delivery.43This was the first in-human AAV-based liver-directed gene therapy. The study used a dose-escalation design with three dosing cohorts: a low-dose of 8.0 × 1010vector genomes per kilogram body weight (vg/kg), an intermediate dose of 4.0 × 1011vg/kg, and a high dose of 2.0 × 1012vg/kg. Reassuringly, there were no safety concerns after treatment of low- and mid-dose cohorts, though FIX levels remained <1% of normal. In the first subject of the high-dose cohort (subject E), FIX levels peaked at 11% of normal, but unexpectedly, the expression was transient.

The clinical course of these subjects revealed major immunological obstacles that limit the efficacy of systemic AAV gene transfer (►Fig. 2). Foremost was the identification of an anticapsid cellular immune response hypothesized to be responsible for the loss of FIX levels in subject E. This was clinically apparent in a transient increase of liver transaminase levels, which coincided with the decline of FIX transgene levels, suggesting a correlation between hepatocyte damage and FIX loss. This hypothesis was supported by the identification of cytotoxic lymphocytes specific for AAV2-capsid peptides, but not for FIX, occurring concomitantly with elevated transaminase levels (►Fig. 2middle panel).43Second, the presence of pre-existing AAV-neutralizing antibodies impedes effective transduction after systemic delivery43(►Fig. 2, bottom panel), which was not observed after intramuscular administration of AAV2-FIX.38These AAV-neutralizing antibodies develop within 2 weeks of AAV vector delivery and persist in large preclinical models and clinical trial subjects for a long period of time (longer than years) in a dose-dependent manner.13

The first gene therapy clinical trial that demonstrated sustained therapeutically relevant transgene levels was reported in 2011, using a liver-directed AAV8 vector expressing FIX in HB subjects.44,45FIX levels have been maintained between 2 and 6% of normal in a vector-dose-dependent manner throughout the study, with up to 8 years of follow-up eluded to in a review.10In the high-dose cohort (2 × 1012vg/kg), the anticapsid cellular immune response was identified in four of six subjects, which confirmed the vector-dose dependency of this complication. This trial showed that a promptly initiated steroid-based immunosuppressive therapy mitigates transduced hepatocyte damage by AAV-capsid-specific T cells (►Fig. 2, middle panel). Steroid immunosuppression has since then been adapted for many clinical trials of AAV, as a means to potentially circumvent the loss of transgene expression due to capsid-reactive T cells. However, in some HB gene therapy trials steroids have been insufficient to control the immune response.46

These results posed the question of how best to decrease the AAV vector dose to limit the capsid-directed cellular immune responses (►Fig. 2) while still achieving clinically relevant FIX levels. In 2009, we described the naturally occurring X-linked thrombophilia FIX-Padua where the R338L substitution resulted in an eightfold increase in specific activity compared with wild-type (WT) FIX.47The underlying mechanism of FIX-Padua is due to an enhanced interaction with FVIII/FVIIIa, and not due to abnormal activation or inactivation.48The rationale for using FIX-Padua in gene therapy was that the transgene hyperactivity would allow for a lower dose of AAV vector while maintaining or increasing the FIX activity level. The lower vector dose could limit vector-dose-dependent complications, including the anti-AAV capsid cellular immune response. In preclinical gene transfer studies, FIX-Padua resulted in higher FIX activity levels compared with FIX-WT, without an increased risk of immunogenicity or thrombosis, the latter consistent with its mechanism of hyperactivity.49–53

The enhanced efficacy of transgene FIX-Padua compared with FIX-WT was demonstrated in 2017 (►Table 1).54In this clinical trial, 10 severe HB subjects were administered an AAV8 vector encoding for FIX-Padua at a single dose of 5 × 1011vg/kg.54Subjects demonstrated sustained FIX activity levels at approximately 25% of normal without any serious adverse effects. Although two of the 10 subjects demonstrated evidence of AAV-capsid mediated cellular immune responses, they were rapidly initiated on steroids and maintained therapeutic FIX activity levels. Compared with the earlier trial using AAV8 FIX-WT,44,45the use of transgene FIX-Padua resulted in about fivefold increased FIX activity levels at a fourfold lower AAV8 vector dose, which resulted in a decreased rate of subjects needing steroids from 60% (4 of 6)44to 20% (2 of 10).54

Similar benefits of using transgene FIX-Padua55compared with FIX-WT56were reported in the clinical development of an AAV5 vector for HB. This AAV5 vector was manufactured using an insect cell system, compared with the earlier AAV2 and AAV8 vectors that were manufactured using a mammalian system.20,21In the initial approach, an AAV5 FIX-WT vector was administered to HB subjects at doses of 5 × 1012vg/kg(low dose) or 2 × 1013vg/kg (high dose).56There was a clear vector dose-dependent rise in FIX activity, with the high-dose subjects achieving a mean FIX activity level of 7% of normal. Notably, about a 10-fold higher AAV5 vector dose, compared with AAV8 vector, was required to achieve comparable FIX levels (►Table 1), likely in part because of the superior hepatocyte transduction of AAV8 compared with AAV5. Subsequently, FIX-Padua was substituted for FIX-WT in this AAV5 vector and HB subjects (n= 3) received 2 × 1013vg/kg, which resulted in an average FIX activity of 47% of normal. FIX-Padua compared with FIX-WT transgene resulted in the expected sevenfold higher FIX activity levels, in the otherwise same AAV5 vector at the same dose,55,56consistent with the hyperactivity of FIX-Padua protein.48However, this AAV5 vector dose was still over an order-of-magnitude greater than other AAV vectors encoding for FIX-Padua (►Table 1).

Three of the 10 HB subjects that received AAV5 FIX-WT (one in the low-dose and two in the high-dose cohort) were treated with steroids for elevated transaminase levels,56but there is no clear evidence that steroids provided a benefit. Subsequently, none of the three HB subjects that received 2 × 1013vg/kg of AAV5 FIX-Padua exhibited concerning elevated transaminase levels and none received steroids for gene-therapy-related indications.55These findings of AAV5-FIX, increased transaminase levels without identification of T cell-mediated toxicity, do not follow any of the potential outcomes shown in ►Fig. 2. Thus, it is possible that the underlying mechanism of elevated transaminase levels may depend on the AAV serotype, vector manufacturing, vector dose, the therapeutic transgene (see below discussion on HA gene therapy), or distinct combinations of each of these elements.13

Currently, utilizing FIX-Padua is the gold standard for current gene therapy trials of HB. FIX-Padua is an example of a disease-specific breakthrough technology that accelerated gene therapy for HB. While initial trials considered even a modest increase of 1 to 5% of FIX to be a success, current trials reaching levels of 30% and higher have redefined what patients can expect in the outcome of such treatments.

TheF8cDNA is approximately 7 kb, which exceeds the packaging capacity of AAV vectors. The solution to fitF8within an AAV vector was the result of basic biological studies of FVIII. Researchers found that the entire B-domain of FVIII (encompassing 40% of the protein) could be replaced by a linker and still maintain hemostatic activity,46,57designated B-domain-deleted FVIII (FVIII-BDD). This modification reduced the cDNA to approximately 4.4k. FVIII-BDD is another example of a disease-specific technological breakthrough that is essential for AAV-based gene therapy for HA.

BioMarin Pharmaceutical Inc. (Novato, CA) reported the first promising gene therapy treatment for severe HA (n= 15) using a hepatocyte-directed AAV5 vector that encoded FVIII-BDD in 2017 and 2020.58,59They utilized four cohorts: 6 × 1012vg/kg (cohort 1,n= 1), 2 × 1013vg/kg (cohort 2,n= 1), 6 × 1013vg/kg (cohort 3,n= 7), and more recently 4 × 1013vg/kg (cohort 4,n= 6). The doses used in first two cohorts were similar to that of AAV5 vector employed in HB gene therapy trials discussed above.55,56While no serious adverse reactions were observed, all 7 subjects of cohort 3 and 4 out of 6 subjects from cohort 4 exhibited asymptomatic transient elevated liver transaminase levels and received steroids therapeutically or prophylactically. However, as was observed in the AAV5 FIX-WT clinical trial discussed above,56the etiology of these elevated liver transaminase levels remains unclear, as there was no evidence of an anti-AAV capsid cellular immune response.13

By the end of the first year, 6 of the 7 cohort 3 subjects had normal or near-normal FVIII activity levels, whereas subjects in the lower cohorts had no or only minimally increase levels58that eventually decreased to <1% of normal. In a 3-year follow-up, though the FVIII levels all remained in a therapeutic range, the FVIII levels consistently declined approximately 50% for the cohort 3 and 30% for the subjects from cohort 4 (►Table 1), though the etiology of this decrease is not understood.59These data suggest that the liver-directed AAV5 gene therapy that expresses FVIII-BDD is safe and efficacious, though long-term durability remains an open question. Other AAV liver-directed gene therapy trials for HA are evaluating a variety of vector serotypes, manufacturing processes, and B-domain-deleted FVIII variants (reported in meeting abstracts).9,10,13

The published descriptions and the ongoing pivotal clinical trials that have demonstrated therapeutically relevant factor levels with the resulting amelioration in the bleeding phenotype after AAV-based FVIII and FIX gene therapy without major safety concerns suggest that AAV drugs for HA and HB will likely achieve regulatory approval. However, several questions have yet to be definitively answered (►Table 3), such as durability, need for immunosuppression, acute and long-term safety concerns, and inclusion of inhibitor patients, pediatric, and women with severe hemophilia.

The durability of hepatocyte-expressed FVIII in humans remains an open question with the declining levels listed in ►Table 1. This decline has not been observed in HB gene therapy clinical trials, long-term preclinical studies in HA dogs,23or in preliminary results reported in meeting abstracts of other HA gene therapy trials,9,10albeit the latter is with much a shorter follow-up. The features of this AAV drug that contribute to the loss of FVIII levels remain undefined. It remains to be determined whether the vector serotype, manufacturing process, FVIII expression in hepatocytes, episomal structural changes, or other unknown characteristics contribute to this observation. The declining FVIII levels also raise the question of what durability hemophilia patients should expect for approved gene therapy drugs (►Table 3). In our opinion, the stable FIX levels in HB subjects (►Table 1) and FVIII levels in hemophilia canine models >7 years after gene transfer set reasonable expectation for sustained therapeutic transgene levels.

The optimal immunosuppression regimen to control the cytotoxic T cell response is also not defined.60What features of the AAV drug contribute to a steroid-responsive cellular response versus a steroid-resistant response are not known. It is also important to identify in preclinical studies the appropriate timing of immunosuppression if more intense therapy is contemplated.60,61

The risk of integration and genotoxicity after AAV administration is likely low, though how low remains debated.62However, both the likelihood of a cytotoxic T cell response and genotoxicity increase with increasing vector dose. Although these vector-dose-dependent risks are not well quantified, it does raise the question of how to balance these risks against the diminishing marginal hemostatic benefit of higher factor levels.

Lastly, access to gene therapy needs to be expanded to all hemophilia patients, including those in developing countries as well as patients currently excluded from clinical trials such as inhibitor patients, children, and females. We have recently reviewed the data and rationale about the potential for using gene therapy to treat inhibitor patients.36To date, all gene therapy trials for hemophilia have only enrolled adult male subjects. Preclinical studies to support the inclusion of symptomatic females with factors levels compatible with severe hemophilia need to be initiated. These cases arise from nonrandom inactivation of the unaffected allele (lyonization), X-chromosomal abnormalities such as Turner syndrome, mosaicism, and rarely biallelic mutations. It is estimated that there are 80,000 hemophilia carriers in the United States based on the assumption that 95% of the mothers of hemophilia men are carriers and likely that four to five carriers related to a single hemophilia man.63,64Over the last decade, the care of hemophilia carriers using the hemophilia treatment centers has increased by more than 60%.64This will provide the opportunity to identify those symptomatic carriers that may benefit from novel therapies for hemophilia, including gene therapy. In a retrospective study, the prevalence of inhibitors to FVIII in severe HA females in the United States was 15% (2 out 15 patients); both inhibitors were high titers and detected early in life (<24 months).65However, the numbers of patients are too small to allow firm conclusions.

Two issues need to be addressed to facilitate inclusion of females in hemophilia gene therapy clinical trials. First, sex differences in the efficacy of AAV liver gene therapy needs to be systematically investigated. Murine studies with a variety of transgenes have consistently demonstrated higher transgene expression levels in male mice compared with females after AAV liver-directed gene therapy in a serotype-independent manner.66–68Limited data in HB dogs69and fetal NHPs70similarly suggest higher transgene FIX levels after AAV liver gene therapy in male compared with female recipients. However, additional large animal studies are required to definitively determine if sex is a modifier of liver-directed expression by AAV vectors. Second, the risk of female germline transmission needs to be properly addressed in animal models for reproductive toxicology. Together these data will provide critical information on the AAV gene therapy for females with hemophilia and notably for those who are commonly affected with autosomal disease as well (see below).

FVII deficiency is the most common autosomal recessive bleeding disorder, occurring in approximately two per million individuals.8Most patients with FVII <2% of normal have a severe bleeding phenotype, encompassing approximately 20% of FVII-deficient patients. Conversely, most patients with FVII >20% of normal are commonly asymptomatic.1Menorrhagia and epistaxis are the most common bleeding symptoms, though life-threatening intracranial bleeds occur, especially in infants.2As concentrates of zymogen FVII are not available, recombinant FVIIa (rFVIIa) is the recommended replacement therapy.2The dosing of rFVIIa for replacement therapy for FVII deficiency is lower than the doses used for bypassing therapy. Though based on limited evidence, long-term secondary prophylaxis has been recommended for FVII-deficient patients with a severe bleeding phenotype.71,72Prenatal diagnosis and primary prophylaxis (before the onset of bleeding) have also been advocated for infants with a strong family history of catastrophic bleeding.73

FVII has the shortest half-life of the coagulation factors (~5 hours), which is only modestly longer than the half-life of FVIIa (~2 hours). Prophylaxis regimens are typically one to three IV administrations per week,71,72though the short half-life results in plasma troughs without measurable drug (►Fig. 1).71Inhibitors against FVII/FVIIa develop in approximately 3% of patients.74The efficacy of protein prophylaxis, being less than optimal because of the subtherapeutic troughs, establishes the rationale for FVII gene therapy. However, the therapeutic level needs to better defined, though >2% of normal FVII levels is a minimal goal given the preponderance of bleeding symptoms below this threshold.

FVII is a vitamin K-dependent coagulation factor. Like FIXa, FVIIa is a serine protease which requires its cofactor tissue factor (TF) to efficiently activate FX. Uniquely among coagulation factors, 1% of plasma FVII circulates as FVIIa, though the mechanism of this activation remains undefined.75Importantly, human FVII/FVIIa does not bind mouse TF; thus, the hemostatic efficacy of human FVII/FVIIa therapeutics cannot be evaluated in murine models.76

AAV liver-directed FVII gene transfer has produced promising results in large-animal models. Administration of a relatively low dose (4 × 1011vg/kg) of AAV5 with human FVII to adult NHPs (n= 6) resulted in 7% of normal FVII levels, which are likely therapeutically relevant.77Interestingly, no difference in expression between female (n= 3) and male (n= 3) NHPs was appreciated, which is in contrast to other studies that have suggested higher transgene levels in male compared with female recipients after AAV liver-directed gene transfer,66–70as discussed above with regard to hemophilia. As such, additional large-animal studies examining sex differences in transgene expression after AAV liver-directed gene therapy are required before designing a clinical trial with both male and female subjects.

Subsequently, administration of an AAV8 vector encoding canine FVII zymogen to FVII-deficient male dogs (n= 4) also resulted in sustained FVII levels without safety concerns.78There was a vector-dose-dependent increase in plateau FVII levels with clinically relevant vector doses (6 × 1011–2 × 1012vg/kg) resulting in FVII levels >10% of normal. Canine models of coagulation deficiencies have been highly informative for anticipating dosing in clinical trials for gene therapies,79suggesting that this approach has translational potential for adults with FVII deficiency.

Given the severe consequences of intracranial hemorrhage in children with FVII deficiency (with an estimated prevalence of 5–16%),2,8,80FVII gene transfer in pediatric preclinical models has also been investigated. Delivery of AAV8 vector with mouse FVII to FVII-deficient mice at day 35 of life rescued the high mortality of this fragile model: 100% (n= 19) of treated mice survived to day 67 after administration while only 43% (n= 23) of untreated animals survived (p< 0.001), with the expired animals uniformly demonstrating intracranial hemorrhage.77

An alternative gene therapy approach to FVII deficiency could be delivery of transgene FVIIa, rather than zymogen FVII. This would be analogous to using rFVIIa protein for FVII deficiency. Preclinical studies in HA and HB dogs have demonstrated that AAV8 delivery of canine FVIIa can improve the bleeding phenotype.81The potential advantage of this approach would be that the same gene therapy product could be used to treat HA and HB with and without inhibitors as well as theoretically FVII deficiency. Unfortunately, the canine study used high AAV8 vector doses (2 × 1013–1 × 1014vg/kg) that are associated with an elevated risk of AAV-capsid-mediated immune responses in humans. One strategy to potentially reduce the AAV vector dose is use of a hyperactive FVIIa variant transgene with increased protein-specific activity (reviewed in Samelson-Jones and Arruda46), analogous to the use of FIX-Padua for HB gene therapy. Studies using an AAV8 expressing a gain-of-function FVIIa (VEAY) transgene was superior to murine FVIIa-WT without the formation of antibodies. Though there were no immunogenicity concerns in this murine study, careful assessment of the immunogenic risk of FVIIa variant transgenes would be necessary for further development, especially given the history of two distinct gain-of-function FVIIa variant proteins triggering neutralizing cross-reactive antibodies to endogenous FVII-WT in patients with hemophilia.82–84In silico studies may help inform on the immunogenic risk.84Preclinical studies using canine FVIIa transgenes in FVII-deficient dog models will help define the translational potential of this approach.

Zymogen FVII levels that are likely therapeutic or near-therapeutic have been expressed with translational doses of AAV vectors in adult large-animal models. However, since FVII deficiency is an autosomal disease, more preclinical studies are needed to address sex as a modifier of AAV liver gene therapy efficacy. The majority of FVII deficiency cases1and the canine model are the result of missense mutations79. Early HB-directed intramuscular injection of AAV-FIX gene therapy studies37,38only included subjects withF9missense mutations based on preclinical canine data suggesting an increased risk of antitransgene FIX antibodies with canineF9nonsense mutations.40,85,86However, subsequent liver-directed AAV gene therapy trials included HB subjects regardless of underlying HB mutation without transgene immunogenicity concerns.43–45,54As such, we would anticipate similarly including FVII deficiency patients regardless of the underlying mutation for AAV liver-directed gene therapy despite being limited to preclinical evaluations in models only with a missense mutation. Furthermore, in contrast to HB,34inhibitor development in FVII deficiency is not associated with allergic reactions making it less of a safety concern.74The accumulating experience of treating rare disease with gene transfer, especially in infancy,15should energize studies for severe FVII deficiency.

Congenital VWD encompasses a spectrum of genetic diseases due to defective or deficient von Willebrand factor (VWF) due to mutations in the VWF gene.3,4Patients with a severe bleeding phenotype typically have Type 3 VWD (T3VWD) with a complete absence ofVWFdue to biallelicVWFmutations.1VWD is widely described as the most common inherited bleeding disorder, though the estimated prevalence of T3VWD is one in a million.3,4Treatment is focused on restoring VWF activity, which can be achieved with desmopressin in patients with low levels of functional VWF87or with VWF concentrates.3,4Between 5 and 10% of patients with T3VWD will develop alloantibodies to VWF.88Patients with severe disease benefit with decreased bleeding rates from prophylactic therapy with VWF concentrates,89,90which establishes the therapeutic rationale of VWF gene transfer.

Circulating VWF is multimeric glycoprotein responsible for initiating platelet aggregation at the site of injury as well as carrying most circulating FVIII.91,92VWF is synthesized primarily in EC as well as megakaryocytes. Multimerization underlies the hemostatic potency VWF. VWF occurs intracellularly throughout the secretory pathway and results in VWF concatemers that are heterogeneous in length ranging from 2 to >100 subunits chained together in an end-to-end fashion.93Successful VWF gene transfer likely requires not only translation of the VWF monomer, but also sufficient multimerization for the transgene-VWF to adequately restore hemostasis.

The inherent complexity of the VWF protein and its large cDNA size pose challenges for gene transfer. At 8.4 kb, the cDNA of the VWF limits the use of AAV vectors, though not LV, which is more suitable for dividing cells such as ECs. Multiple studies have demonstrated expression of functional VWF after nonviral hepatocyte-directed gene transfer in VWD mice, but the ratio of high-molecular-weight to low-molecular-weight multimers of hepatocyte-expressed VWF is consistently lower than endogenous-expressed VWF in WT mice, which reduces its hemostatic efficacy.94–97Similar results were observed after in vivo hepatocyte-directed LV-based VWF gene transfer in VWD mice.98This is speculated to be secondary to hepatocytes lacking the necessary cellular machinery for efficient multimer assembly.

Given the problems of inefficient multimerization of ectopically expressed VWF, alternative targets for VWF gene transfer are attractive. An early proof-of-concept study demonstrated successful ex vivo gene transfer of functional VWF using a LV vector into blood outgrowth ECs (BOECs) from T3VWD dogs.99In vitro studies demonstrated that these transduced BOECs secreted a full range of VWF multimers, though in vivo studies have not been reported. An analogous approach for FVIII gene transfer in HA dogs resulted in circulating FVIII levels, but also the development of anti-FVIII antibodies.100Recently, in vivo LV LSEC-directed FVIII gene transfer in HA mice resulted in therapeutic FVIII levels as well as immune tolerization in mice with preexisting inhibitors.25,26Combined, the promising multiple endothelial-targeted approaches for FVIII gene transfer may reinvigorate this approach for VWF.

The cDNA of VWF greatly exceeds the packaging capacity of AAV vectors, though this obstacle in not unique for VWF and several strategies to allow for AAV-based gene transfer of large genes have explored.101One application of a dual AAV vector system forVWFtransfer worked in vitro, but failed in vivo.98

The large cDNA of VWF limits the vectors available for VWD gene therapy. In contrast to FVIII-BDD,46there are no truncated variants of functional VWF. LV and dual-vector AAV systems both have potential forVWFtransfer. Murine studies also suggest that the hepatocytes cannot sufficiently multimerize VWF for full normal hemostasis. This is consistent the increasing appreciation of potential disadvantages of ectopic protein expression including hepatocyte expression of FVIII. This observation has renewed interest in targeting EC for FVIII gene transfer,25,26which may help guide a similar approach for VWF gene transfer. As megakaryocytes are an endogenous site of VWF synthesis in humans, platelet-targeted VWF gene transfer may also be an alternative strategy. Again, the experience of FVIII platelet-targeted gene transfer17may help guide the approach for VWF.

GT is an inherited qualitative platelet disorder due to dysfunctional or absent αIIbβ3integrin that is an instructive example of gene therapy for a primary platelet disorder.5,102GT is recessively inherited with pathological variants occurring in either the neighboring αIIb(ITGA2B)or β3(ITGB3)genes on chromosome 17. Integrin αIIbβ3is essential for platelet plug formation by mediating activation and cross-linking of adjacent platelets through ligand binding. Patients present in infancy with severe mucocutaneous bleeding. GT has an estimated prevalence of one in a million, but is higher in some ethnic communities. Bleeding is treated with platelet transfusions or rFVIIa.6The development of antiplatelet alloantibodies is a major risk of platelet transfusions occurring in approximately 30% of all patients, but much higher (up to 80%) in patients with a complete αIIbβ3deficiency.7Allogenic bone marrow transplantation is curative, including for patients with alloantibodies, supporting the rationale for gene therapy.6

The αIIbβ3integrin is the most abundant protein on the surface of platelets with ligands including fibrinogen, VWF, and fibronectin. Signals originating inside the cell during platelet activation induce a conformational change in αIIbβ3that enhances ligand binding (inside-out signaling); conversely, ligand binding results in intracellular signal transduction (outside-in signaling) mediating multiple cellular processes for hemostasis.5

A limited number of preclinical studies have demonstrated the feasibility of gene therapy for GT by administration of ex vivo LV-transduced HSC after bone marrow conditioning. As platelets are an anuclear cell fragment, gene therapy must target their HSC progenitors. In conditioned β3knockout mice, administration of transduced HSCs resulted in circulating platelets with approximately 10% of normal αIIbβ3levels, which improved ex vivo platelet aggregation and improved in vivo hemostasis.103However, two out the four recipients developed antiplatelet antibodies after gene therapy.. Similar hemostatic improvements and immunogenicity concerns were observed after a comparable gene approach therapy in αIIb-deficient dogs.104Canine GT recapitulates the bleeding phenotype of the human disease as well as the risk of platelet alloantibodies.79Four dogs received bone marrow conditioning and then LV-transduced HSC with the human αIIbgene. One dog died of intracranial hemorrhage. The three surviving animals demonstrated sustained expression of human/canine αIIbβ3hybrid on approximately 10% of circulating platelets at approximately 10% of normal receptor density, throughout the length of the study, up to 5 years.104Despite this low level of expressed αIIbβ3, the animals exhibited an ameliorated bleeding phenotype and an improved response to hemostatic challenges. However, two of the three animals exhibited antiplatelet antibodies after gene therapy. The catastrophic bleeding in this study highlights the requirement for a better hemostatic plan to circumvent early bleedings and to allow long-term expression of the defective gene.16

Although the limited preclinical studies of ex vivo HSC-directed LV gene therapy have demonstrated a phenotypic improvement in GT models, they have also exposed substantial obstacles including major bleeding complications during bone marrow conditioning and the risk of an immune response against the transgene. Notably, however, the phenotypic improvements were evident even with a low level of αIIbβ3expression per platelet on only a minority of platelets, suggesting that αIIbβ3gene transfer will have a clinical impact if these obstacles can be addressed. Strategies being investigated to enhance FVIII HSC-directed gene transfer may inform on how to avoid the problems with conditioning in GT gene therapy, including nongenotoxic bone marrow conditioning regimens105and in vivo LV HSC-directed approaches.106A better understanding the immune response to transgenes after gene therapy36,107may similarly inform on the risk of transgene αIIbβ3. The availability of small-and large-animal models of GT will facilitate the adoption of gene therapy breakthroughs for GT gene therapy. Species-specific transgenes may help address the risk of immunogenicity after gene therapy, especially given the experience with HSC transplant that generally eradicates pre-existing platelet alloantibodies.

The increasing number of approved gene therapy products and the likely approval of AAV-based gene transfer drugs for HA and HB have engendered optimism that similar treatments could be developed for other monogenic bleeding disorders. Though the rationale for gene therapy of other rare inherited bleeding disorders is well established by the benefits of replacement therapies, and especially prophylactic treatment, development of translational gene therapy for these diseases will likely require additional breakthroughs.

The cellular target needs to be carefully considered. Early skeletal-muscle-directed studies of FIX gene transfer established the safety of AAV vectors in human subjects.37,38The field has subsequently moved to hepatocyte-directed AAV approaches,44,45,54in a large part because of the ease of IV administration. AAV-based gene transfer for FVIII has also focused on hepatocyte-directed approaches, mostly because of the existing technologies that allowed for successful FIX hepatocyte-directed gene transfer, even though FVIII is endogenously expressed in EC. However, the declining FVIII levels seen in hepatocyte-directed gene therapy clinical trials (►Table 1)58,59and an evolving appreciation for the cellular stress induced by FVIII expression46,108have fostered interest in targeting alternative tissues, especially EC for FVIII-transgene expression.25,26The hypothesis is that the endogenous source of a protein may have unique cellular machinery that facilitates sustainable expression. Likewise, multiple murine studies have found that ectopic hepatocyte-expressed VWF has limited hemostatic efficacy, likely because of reduced multimerization. These observations raise the question of whether EC or megakaryocytes could better multimerize transgene-VWF.

The risk of an immune response to the transgene in another issue. Alloantibodies are described in all the discussed inherited bleeding disorders (►Table 2). However, accumulating preclinical studies suggest that AAV gene therapy, especially liver-directed approaches, may be protolerogenic compared with protein therapy.36

Gene therapy for HA and HB have both greatly benefited fromvariant transgenes, FVIII-BDD, and FIX-Padua, respectively, that were identified by studying the basic biology of FVIII and FIX, respectively.46Comparable breakthroughs may be required to overcome current obstacles for FVII deficiency, VWD, and GT gene therapy. Gene therapy as a field is also rapidly advancing. All these disorders will benefit from clinical development strategies for rare diseases with prevalence of one in a million, including modular platforms that could allow the facile substitution of similar transgenes. Recently, FIX-Padua was successfully substituted for FIX-WT in uniQure NV (Amsterdam, The Netherlands) trials for HB gene therapy product with predictable increases in FIX activity levels and without restarting clinical development.109,110

Likewise, the experiences of systemically administered AAV in infants with SMA15and Crigler–Najjar will inform on treatment of infants in other diseases, including FVII deficiency. A better understanding of differences between transgene expression in male and female subjects after AAV liver-directed gene therapy will also be critical for autosomal disorders.

With the anticipated very high costs of gene therapy, more quality-of-life outcomes need to be collected to understand the cost–benefit of such approaches.111Ultimately, gene therapy has the potential to both increase the accessibility of treatments for bleeding disorders but also normalize quality-of-life outcomes.
