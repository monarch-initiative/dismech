# Blood biomarker changes in response to low-dose oral ketamine treatment in adults with major depressive disorder (MDD) and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)
**Authors:** Braxton AM, Driver C, Hermens DF, Quigley BL
**DOI:** [10.64898/2026.08.12.26360216](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.12.26360216)

## Content

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are prevalent, chronic, and disabling mental health conditions which are difficult to treat. Ketamine has demonstrated effect for improving depression and PTSD symptoms independently but reports often overlook focused comorbid improvement of these symptoms within individuals. To address this, we assessed blood-based biomarker and psychological changes following low-dose oral ketamine treatment for adults with MDD alone (n=14) and comorbid MDD+PTSD (n=21). Before treatment, the MDD clinical group presented with more severe depression, lower serotonin levels and higher kynurenine levels than the MDD+PTSD group. Post-treatment there were no detectable differences in the biological response between clinical groups, with combined analysis revealing common decreases in circulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF-A). Additionally, post-treatment, both clinical groups showed improvements in anxiety, stress, social functioning, suicidal ideation, and general well-being measures, as well as individual improvements in depression scores and PTSD symptoms in the MDD- and PTSD-containing groups, respectively. Collectively, this study presents additional evidence that low-dose oral ketamine treatment can be effective for MDD and MDD+PTSD, individually and comorbidly, and that both MDD and PTSD clinical groups responded in the same biological manner. <h4>Highlights</h4>  Low dose oral ketamine can be effective in treating MDD and PTSD concurrently Blood biomarkers BDNF and VEGF decreased with symptom improvement No detectable difference in oral ketamine treatment response by clinical diagnosis

1. Introduction 40
Major depressive disorder ( MDD) and post-traumatic stress disorder ( PTSD) are 41
prevalent, chronic, debilitating, and disabling affective and trauma/stressor-related disorders, 42
respectively, which are difficult to treat and have high rates of morbidity and mortality. 43
Diagnosis of these disorders contributes to worldwide disease and economic burden, negatively 44
affecting personal relationships , employment, psychosocial functioning, and quality of life  45
(Koenen et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2024) . Despite psychotherapeutic and pharmacotherapeutic 46
interventions, failure to respond to t herapy occurs in 30 -50% of people with MDD  or PTSD 47
(Semmlinger et al., 2024; Sicras -Mainar et al., 2012) . Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors 48
(SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are first-line treatments for 49
MDD, and SSRIs are the only United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 50
approved pharmacotherapeutics for PTSD . SSRIs and SNRIs have shown only moderate  51
efficacy in both MDD and PTSD, may have delayed therapeutic actions often resulting in early 52
discontinuation, and adverse side-effects with long-term use (Nierenberg et al., 2008; Van Etten 53
and Taylor, 1998). This has created a critical need for urgent clinical advancements of novel , 54
rapid, robust, and efficacious pharmacotherapeutic treatments with transdiagnostic potential. 55
Ketamine is a  non-competitive N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, 56
which began showing promise as a rapid and robust antidepressant  in people with MDD 57
twenty-six years ago (Berman et al., 2000; Driver et al., 2022). Since then, multiple controlled 58
trials have demonstrated ketamine’s rapid antidepressant response in individuals with MDD, 59
treatment-resistant depression , and suicidal ideation  (Glue et al., 2024; Kryst et al., 2020; 60
Parikh et al., 2024; Seraj et al., 2025) . In parallel, evidence has accumulated that similar 61
positive treatment outcomes can be achieved with ketamine for the treatment of other disorders, 62
such as PTSD (Ragnhildstveit et al., 2023). Ketamine is highly liposoluble and multiple routes 63
of administration may be used, with various bioavailability ranges: intravenous (IV) (100%), 64
intramuscular (93%), subcutaneous (90-95%), intranasal (45-50%), sublingual (20-30%), and 65
oral (17-29%) (Gibula-Tarlowska et al., 2025; McIntyre et al., 2021). IV ketamine is currently 66
the most widely studied and utilised, providing a rapid, but often short-lived therapeutic effect 67

In addition to assessing clinical outcomes from ketamine treatment , biomarkers may 81
play a key role in understanding whether individuals with MDD and/or PTSD will improve 82
with ketamine therapy (Meshkat et al., 2023), due to underlying neurobiological mechanisms 83
and changes that are common among these disorders. Beyond the traditional immune markers, 84
putative ketamine-related biomarkers ha ve included the  neurotrophic factors , brain-derived 85
neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF), as well as the 86
neurotransmitter/hormone, serotonin. BDNF plays a vital role in neurogenesis, neuroplasticity 87
and neuroprotection, which is essential for learning and memory  (Bathina and Das, 2015) , 88
while VEGF supports vasculogenesis, angiogenesis, neurogenesis, and neuroprotection 89
(Wiszniak and Schwarz, 2021). It has been suggested that BDNF and VEGF work together to 90
promote neuroplasticity and neuroprotection and that reduced or abnormal levels of either one 91
or both of these neurotrophic factors may be involved in the development and maintenance of 92
psychiatric disorders (Matsuzaka and Yashiro, 2025) . Recent meta-analysis of BDNF levels 93
from multiple psychiatric conditions suggest that BDNF levels appear reduced in MDD but 94
elevated in PTSD (Zou et al., 2024), while other reviews/studies report elevated VEGF levels 95
in people with MDD or PTSD compared with healthy controls (Matsuzaka and Yashiro, 2025; 96
Quigley et al., 2025b). Alternatively, serotonin is an important neurotransmitter and peripheral 97
hormone which is responsible for the regulation of stress responses, mood, behaviour, sleep, 98
memory, and appetite (Kanova and Kohout, 2021). Serotonin is synthesised from tryptophan, 99
with tryptophan metabolism believed to be involved in the pathophysiology of 100
neuropsychiatric disorders due to the synthesis of both serotonin and kynurenine, with the 101

2. Materials and methods 118
2.1 Study participants 119
The present study is a subset analysis of participants enrolled in clinical trials at the 120
Thompson Institute, University of the Sunshine Coast (UniSc), Queensland, Australia (Clinical 121
Trials Registry of Australia and New Zealand ACTRN12621000429853 and 122
ACTRN12618001965291; (Quigley et al., 2025a)). All participants included in the study had 123

Supplementary Table 1). 131

Participants physical health was evaluated with a physical examination, pathology 144
blood tests, and review s of individual medical history. Throughout the treatment and follow-145
up phase, participants continued taking regular ly prescribed medications as directed by 146
individual treating physicians. Thus, most participants within these studies were taking 147
concurrent pharmacotherapeutics, such as SSRIs, SNRIs, mood stabilisers or antipsychotics. 148
Demographic dat a, concurrent pharmacotherapeutics, and comorbid medical conditions  are 149

2.3 Blood collection 157
A phlebotomist collected non-fasting whole blood samples at all timepoints. Serum-separator 158
tubes (SST)  were used for serum . Collection tubes remained at room temperature for a 159
minimum of 30 minutes to allow for clottin g. Tubes were then centrifuged at 2465 x g for 15 160
minutes at 4°C. Samples were all processed within four hours of collection, with serum 161
separated, aliquoted, then stored at −80°C. 162

2.5 Clinical (psychological) scales data collection 175
The following clinical scales  were assessed at baseline  and the follow-up timepoint:  176
Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) (Lovibond and Lovibond, 1995), a 21-item-177

measure for the MDD group utilised HAM-D (Hamilton, 1960), a 17-item clinician-rated scale 184
used to measure the severity of depressive symptoms , whilst the PTSD group  utilised the 185
Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) (Montgomery and Asberg, 1979), a 186
10-item clinician-rated scale used to measure the severity of depressed symptomology; and the 187
Post-traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) (Blevins et al., 2015), a 20-item 188
self-report assessment used to measure severity of PTSD symptoms.  189

2.6 Statistical analysis 191
R Statistical Software (v4.3.3) was used for statistical analyses (R Core Team, 2024). The study 192
used two independent groups (MDD and MDD+PTSD), often with non -normal distributions 193
as determined by Shapiro-Wilk test (Shapiro and Wilk, 1965), and had small unequal sample 194
sizes. As such , the Welch two-sample t-test (Welch, 1947) or the Whitney U/Wilcoxon test 195
(Mann and Whitney, 1947) was used as appropriate to determine overall differences between 196
groups and to make multiple pairwise comparisons, with corrected p-values. The combination 197
of the Welch two-sample t-test and Whitney U/Wilcoxon test with continuity correction was 198
used to compare differences in baseline and the follow-up timepoint for all study parameters  199
(BDNF, VEGF-A, tryptophan, serotonin, and kynurenine; DASS-21-subscales of depression, 200
anxiety and stress , SOFAS, BSS , WHO-5, PCL-5, MADRS, and HAM-D). Comparison of 201
study groups by sex was completed using the Fisher Exact test  (Fisher, 1934) , while 202
comparisons of study groups by age was completed using the student’s t-test (Student, 1908). 203

4. Discussion       277
The current study has contributed to the growing evidence base for the use of low-dose 278
oral ketamine as a viable treatment for individuals with a standalone diagnosis of MDD, and 279
comorbid MDD+PTSD, and supports its efficacy . Additionally, analysis showed that oral 280
ketamine appeared to have the same biological effect on blood biomarkers in both MDD and 281
MDD+PTSD participants (i.e., regardless of clinical diagnosis). Our integrated analysis found 282
baseline differences in serotonin and kynurenine biomarkers between clinical groups, and 283
combined treatment analysis revealed shared reduced BDNF and VEGF-A levels across both 284
groups. As well,  robust improvements in both clinician-administered and self -reported 285
psychological measures were reported , with analyses showing  significant improvements in 286
severity of symptoms, overall functioning, and psychological wellbeing. These findings align 287
with previous studies on oral ketamine administration  which show significant improvements 288
in depressive symptoms in MDD  (Glue et al., 2020; Glue et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2024; 289
Veraart et al., 2023) and PTSD symptoms in PTSD (Quigley et al., 2025a). A novel finding that 290

The baseline data analysis for this study cohort detected a significant difference in age 294
between the two clinical groups in this study, with  MDD participants being younger. 295
Interestingly, Australian statistics currently suggest that both MDD and PTSD have higher rates 296
in younger adults compared to older adults (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2020-21; Phoenix 297
Australia, 2020). The older age of participants in these studies with PTSD may have reflected 298
a differing prevalence and/or need in the local community ( southeast Queensland, Australia), 299
where recruitment was open to the public and participants were  self-selecting. This 300
demographic difference would be interesting to pursue in future studies. 301
There were significant differences detected in serotonin and kynurenine levels at 302
baseline between the MDD and the MDD+PTSD groups, with lower serotonin and higher 303
kynurenine levels seen in the former. This reciprocal relationship was not surprising, given the 304
shared tryptophan precursor pathway for serotonin and kynurenine, and that lower serotonin 305
levels have been consistently associated with MDD (Colle et al., 2020; Correia and Vale, 2022). 306
The only significant difference within the psychological scale scores between clinical 307
groups at baseline was the DASS-21 depression subscale, with participants from the MDD 308
group reporting more severe depression symptoms compared to the MDD+PTSD group. This 309
contradicts reports investigating the severity of depression alone or co -morbid with PTSD in 310
military veterans, which found that MDD+PTSD patients reported more severe depression 311
compared to MDD patients (Campbell et al., 2007). Whether the outcome reflects a difference 312
inherent to our large civilian study cohort or simply a unique characteristic of this group of 313
individuals remains to be further explored.  314
Biomarker changes following ketamine treatment at the combined sample level showed 315
BDNF and VEGF-A levels significantly reduced post oral ketamine treatment. This outcome 316
contrasts with IV ketamine studies that report increases, particularly in relation to BDNF levels 317
during successful treatment of MDD (Haile et al., 2014; Kang and Vazquez, 2022; Zheng et 318
al., 2021). Whether the difference in BDNF change in the MDD group is related to the route of 319
ketamine administration (oral verses IV; leading to different primary and secondary active 320
ketamine metabolites) is a question for future pharmacokinetic studies. Additionally, in clinical 321
groups where MDD (characterised by lower BDNF levels) is comorbid with PTSD 322
(characterised by higher BDNF levels) (Zou et al., 2024) , it is still unclear whether increases 323
or decreases in overall BDNF levels would be most beneficial. As such, we cannot definitively 324

2022).  344
There were limitations of this analysis that should be acknowledge . The small sample 345
size, particularly for the MDD group, may have underpowered parts of the study by limiting 346
the variability and representativeness of the cohort studied and impacted the statistical power. 347
Additionally, different primary clinical depression scales were used for the MDD group (HAM-348
D) and the MDD+PTSD group (MADRS), which limited direct comparisons between groups. 349
Moreover, both trials were open label with no placebo group. Finally, most study participants 350
were on concurrent medications that may have affected the ketamine treatment -related 351
outcomes. A review by (Veraart et al., 2021), showed that prescribed medications for depression 352
are often combined with ketamine to prevent relapse, with most published evidence analysing 353
IV administration with a range of antidepressant medications which suggest synergistic effects, 354
except for benzodiazepines, which may reduce the antidepressant effects of ketamine. Despite 355
these limitations, the outcomes from our analysis largely align with published literature, offer 356

5. Conclusion 360
This study  assessed the changes in biological and psychological parameters for MDD  and 361
comorbid MDD+PTSD participants in low-dose oral ketamine treatment trials. There is urgent 362
need for clinical advancements of rapid, robust, and efficacious pharmacotherapeutics  for 363
mental health  disorders. Low-dose oral ketamine offers great potential as a rapid -acting 364
translational therapeutic treatment for MDD and MDD+PTSD . Our results suggest an 365
etiological and biological overlap between MDD and PTSD  and overlapping benefits for 366
treatment of these disorders separately, and concurrently. Additionally, blood-based biomarkers 367
have a place in understanding the treatment response to ketamine and further study with larger 368
cohorts across longer time periods are needed. Overall, our study continues to support evidence 369
that low-dose oral ketamine is efficacious and provides rapid, robust significant psychological 370
improvements for challenging to treat disorders such as MDD and MDD+PTSD.  371
