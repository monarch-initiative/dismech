attention for their roles in disease and maintaining physiological homeostasis.
Historically, these immune cells were known to mediate allergic responses and
defend against parasitic infections. Recent evidence further links eosinophils
to several eosinophil-associated disorders, including eosinophilic esophagitis,
eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, and hypereosinophilic syndrome;
however, their pathogenic contribution appears to be disease-specific and
remains incompletely defined in some contexts. The advances in immunology and
molecular biology have yielded targeted biologic therapies against eosinophils,
significantly improving outcomes for patients with eosinophilic disorders.

population comprising distinct subtypes that differ in surface protein
expression, tissue localization, and functional properties. Specifically,
resident eosinophils (rEos) reside in tissues for local maintenance and immune

and tissues. Recognizing distinct eosinophil subsets is crucial for
understanding their contributions to disease progression. This review highlights
recent discoveries in eosinophil diversity and explores therapeutic implications
of selectively targeting specific eosinophil subsets in clinical practice.

FACTS
• The specific molecular mechanisms that define the functional dichotomy between homeo static
rEos and iEos in different human tissues and diseases remain a major subject of research.
• The existence and clinical relevance of the rEos and iEos subsets defined in mice must be
conclusively validated in human pathophysiology to translate preclinical findings.
• A key therapeutic challenge is the development of subset-specific biologics that selectively deplete
pathogenic iEos while sparing the homeostatic functions of rEos.
• A major gap is confirming whether the rEos/iEos paradigm established in mice defines
functionally distinct and clinically relevant populations in humans.
• Eosinophil heterogeneity may define disease endotypes, where the relative abundance of rEos and
iEos could explain differential treatment responses.


OPEN QUESTIONS
How can we therapeutically target pathogenic iEos while preserving the homeostatic functions of
rEos to advance the treatment of eosinophil-associated diseases?

Introduction
Granulocytes in peripheral blood were first formally delineated in 1846 by Jones [1] and Brewer
[2], whose morphological and functional characterization laid the groundwork for subsequent
hematological and immunological research. Because of the lack of effective staining techniques at
the time, they could not accurately distinguish the granulocyte subtypes. This limitation was later
overcome in 1879, when Paul Ehrlich introduced an aniline dye -based blood -staining method
using coal-tar dyes such as eosin  [3]. He successfully identified eosinophils and named them by
their unusual affinity for the eosin dye . These spherical cells, measuring 12 –17 micrometers in
diameter, are characterized by a bilobed nucleus and cytoplasm that stains pink due to their
acidophilic properties and strong affinity for eosin. Functionally, they are classified as nonspecific
cytotoxic cells that participate in destructive processes. Their primary origin lies in pluripotent
hematopoietic stem cells within the bone marrow, which differentiate through defined progenitor
stages into the mature eosinophilic lineage. In some cases, however, these cells may also develop
in the thymus or spleen [1]. Furthermore, he not only characterized the distinct staining properties
of eosinophil granules but also examined their distribution across various species and tissues. From
these studies, he hypothesized that eosinophils develop in the bone marrow– a prescient insight
consistent with current understanding of eosinophil hematopoiesis.
Identifying the precursor of the eosinophil took several more decades. In 1960, Rytömaa observed
that eosinophils were especially abundant in the bone marrow, reinforcing the idea that their origin
lies there. It was not until 1984 that Fischkoff et al. provided definitive evidence of a common
progenitor: they demonstrated that eosinophils and neutrophils arise from a common precursor cell
using the promyelocytic cell line HL-60 as a model [4]. This finding established that eosinophils
share a developmental lineage with other granulocytes. Molecular advances in the late 20th century
further clarified eosinophil lineage commitment. The genetic and epigenetic regulation of
eosinophils, with particular emphasis on the critical roles of the transcription fact or GATA-1 and
interleukin (IL)-5, is under active investigation, highlighting important therapeutic implications
[5]. In 1998, a gene selectively expressed in developing eosinophils, EOS47, was identified; its
promoter region contains binding sites for key  hematopoietic transcription factors such as Myb -
Ets, C/EBP, and GATA [6], which are crucial for lineage specification. Consistent with this,
subsequent studies showed that eliminating a high -affinity GATA-1 binding site in the GATA -1

Parallel research uncovered how the immune system regulates eosinopoiesis (the production of

and prolong their survival by inhibiting apoptosis [9].
Collectively, these landmark discoveries have shaped the current understanding of eosinophil
biology. Each breakthrough – from the first identification of eosinophils to the elucidation of their
molecular regulators – has provided fundamental insights into how eosinophils develop and
function. These insights now form the basis for modern clinical perspectives, guiding research and
therapy in diseases where eosinophils play a prominent role.
Granule Dynamics and Exocytosis
Eosinophils are granulocytes with numerous cytoplasmic granules that store a variety of proteins,
cytokines, chemokines, enzymes, and lipid mediators essential to their function [10]. A defining
feature of eosinophils is the pres ence of four major cationic proteins within their secondary
granules: major basic protein 1 and 2 (MBP1, MBP2), eosinophil peroxidase (EPO), eosinophil
cationic protein (ECP), and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (EDN). When released, these granule
proteins can induce significant tissue damage , bronchoconstriction, mucus hypersecretion,  and
dysfunction [11]. The accumulation of eosinophils in tissues and their detection in biofluids are
strongly correlated with the severity of eosinophilic disease.
Each of these eosinophil-specific granule proteins has potent biological activities. ECP and EDN
are ribonucleases with antiviral properties; notably, ECP can form voltage -insensitive toxic ion
pores in target cell membranes, potentially facilitating the entry of other cytotoxic molecules [12].
In addition to its cytotoxic actions, ECP also exhibits several immunomodulatory effects, including
suppression of T-cell proliferation, inhibition of B-cell immunoglobulin production, induction of
mast cell degranulation, and stimulation of airway mucus secretion and fibroblast
glycosaminoglycan production [13]. MBP, another abundant granule protein, can alter smooth

triggers the degranul ation of mast cells and basophils [14]. EPO constitutes roughly 25% of the

and nitric oxide to generate highly reactive oxygen species (e.g. , hypohalous acids) and reactive
nitrogen species like peroxynitrite, which in turn oxidize nucleophilic targets in proteins [15, 16].
These oxidative reactions promote cellular oxidative stress and can lead to target cell death via
apoptosis or necrosis [15, 16].
Upon appropriate stimulation, eosinophils can release their granule contents by four primary
mechanisms: classical exocytosis, compound exocytosis, piecemeal degranulation, and cytolysis.
In classical exocytosis, individual granules fuse directly with the plasma membrane, releasing their
entire contents into the extracellular space. Compound exocytosis is a variant wherein multiple
granules first fuse together inside the cell, and then this larger composite granule fuses with the
plasma membrane to secrete its contents [17]. Piecemeal degranulation (PMD) is a form of
regulated secretion in which granule-derived mediators are selectively mobilized in small packets
via transport vesicles rather than full -granule release, allowing for a gradual and cont rolled
secretion of specific substances [17].
The fourth mechanism, eosinophil cytolysis (ECL), involves lytic cell death and rupture of the
eosinophil via planned non -apoptotic cell death [ 18, 19]. There is evidence that eosinophil
degranulation and ECL play a role in human disease from studying the ultrastructure of tissue
biopsies, staining for extracellular granule proteins, and finding soluble granule proteins in
different clinical samples [19, 20]. Although PMD and ECL make up most of the protein released
from granules in patients, ECL also triggers inflammatory signals via chromatolysis, extracellular
dsDNA traps, and pro -inflammatory galactin -10-containing Charcot –Leyden crystals [ 21, 22].
During cytolysis, the cell undergoes chromatolysis (disintegration of nuclear chromatin) followed
by plasma membrane rupture, leading to the release of intact, membrane -bound granules into the
extracellular space – these released granules are known as free  eosinophil granules (FEGs) [23].
Cytolysis is often accompanied by the formation of eosinophil extracellular traps (EETs) [21].
EETs are extracellular fiber networks composed of DNA strands decorated with granule proteins
such as MBP and ECP [18]. These DNA-based traps can also incorporate intact granules (FEGs)
[16] and are sometimes associated with distinctive eosinophil sombrero vesicles (EoSVs) [23],
which are large tubular vesicles derived from eosinophils. Additionally, recent studies have shown

One hallmark of eosinophil-rich inflammation involving cytolytic degranulation is the presence of

Dynamics of Eosinophil Movement
Under normal conditions, eosinophils migrate from the bone marrow to specific organs, primarily
the gastrointestinal tract. Most eosinophils are found in the intestine (specifically in the segments
beyond the esophagus). Other organs that normally harbor eosinophils include the uterus and
mammary glands of young women, the thymus, adipose tissue (body fat) , and the lungs. These
locations represent the homeostatic distribution of eosinophils in healthy tissues.
Historically, eosinophils were mainly associated with responses to parasitic worm (helminth)
infections and with allergic diseases.  However, it is no w recognized that their functions vary
widely depending on the tissue environment. Studies in mice have shown that, under stable (non-
inflammatory) conditions, eosinophils can play important homeostatic roles in different tissues.
For example, in steady state conditions , eosinophils contribute to the following tissue functions:
Intestine: They support immunoglobulin A (IgA) antibody responses and stimulate mucus
production [35]. Mammary glands: They appear to aid in normal mammary gland development
[36]. Adipose tissue:  They help regulate insulin sensitivity and promote the “browning”
(conversion to thermogenic brown fat) of white adipose tissue [35]. In this way, eosinophils
contribute to normal physiolo gical processes in these organs beyond their traditional role in
pathogen defense.

Because eosinophils primarily reside in tissues, they normally make up less than 5% of circulating
leukocytes in the blood  [35] (Figure 1A). In vivo studies have shown that eosinophils remain in
the bloodstream only briefly – on the order of 8–10 hours on average (with a range of about 3 to
24 hours) before migrating out of circulation [37, 38]. In contrast, once eosinophils enter tissues,
they can persist much longer. Their half-life is roughly 36 hours in the lung and up to 5–6 days in
the intestine, thymus, and uterus under steady-state conditions [37].
The extended survival of eosinophils in tissues appears to be related to tissue -specific cellular
programs and local survival cues. Notably, CD11c expression has been reported on murine tissue-
resident eosinophils in the intestinal lamina propria, thymus, and uterus, whereas circulating
eosinophils and lung eosinophils generally show low or ab sent CD11c expression under steady -
state conditions [109,110]. In intestinal eosinophils, surface CD11c expression appears to be
dynamically regulated and may reflect tissue adaptation or local eosinophil plasticity rather than
serving as a universal marke r of resident eosinophils [110,111]. Whether CD11c directly
contributes to eosinophil survival or instead marks tissue conditioning remains unresolved . In
addition, eosinop hil longevity is supported by the cytokine IL-5, which can inhibit eosinophil
apoptosis (programmed cell death) and thereby prolong the cells’ survival in tissues [37, 39].
Orchestration of Eosinophil Activity
The recruitment of eosinophils from the blood into tissues is primarily driven by chemoattractant
molecules of the eotaxin family . Chief among these is eotaxin-1, also known as CCL11, a
chemokine produced by epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and monocytes in response
to type 2 inflammatory signals such as IL-4, IL-13, and TNF-α [40]. The three eotaxins – CCL11
(eotaxin-1), CCL24 (eotaxin-2), and CCL26 (eotaxin-3) – have overlapping but distinct roles over
the course of an immune response. CCL11 is the most potent chemoattractant for eosinophils [41].
It likely acts early in an inflammatory response to rapidly recruit eosinophils, whereas CCL24 and
CCL26 may help sustain eosinophil accumulation and inflammation over longer periods. CCL11’s
strong affinity for the receptor CCR3 on eosinophils triggers efficient signaling that leads to rapid
and robust eosinophil migratio n into tissues [42]. The importance of CCR3 is highlighted by
studies showing that loss of CCR3 – the principal receptor for eotaxin-1 [43] – results in defective
homing of eosinophils to certain tissues (particularly the intestine), while eosinophil numbers in
the lung and thymus remain normal in the absence of CCR3 [44]. This finding suggests that

In addition to chemokines like eotaxins, certain interleukins contribute to eosinophil trafficking
under normal, non-diseased conditions. IL-5 and IL-13, secreted by Th2-polarized T lymphocytes
and type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2) [9, 46], can promote eosinophil migration into tissues
even in the absence of overt inflammation [47]. However, their chemotactic influence is
considerably weaker than that of eotaxin -1. IL -13 mainly serves to enhance the production of
CCL26 by local tissue cells [46], while IL-5 primarily acts on eosinophil precursors and mature
eosinophils: it stimulates the bone marrow production of eosinophils, increases their sensitivity to
eotaxin-1, and helps maintain their survival once they enter tissues [9].
IL-5 is the cytokine most specific to the eosinophil lineage and is essential for normal eosinophil
development in the bone marrow. Accordingly, the presence of IL -5 in local tissues is crucial to
varying degrees for sustaining rEos populations in different organs. For exa mple, eosinophil
homing to tissues under steady -state conditions is independent of IL-5 in the lungs , partially IL-
5–dependent in the gastrointestinal tract and uterus, and entirely IL-5–dependent in adipose tissue
[47, 48]. In other words, local IL-5 production is absolutely required to maintain eosinophils in fat
tissue, whereas other factors can compensate for the absence of IL-5 in the lungs.
In allergic and other reactive inflammatory conditions, eosinophils themselves become important
sources of cytok ines and chemokines that amplify type 2 inflammation. Eosinophils have been
identified as significant producers of IL -5, IL-13, IL-25, and CCL26 during allergic responses,
thereby contributing to a strongly Th2 -skewed immune environment and subsequent eosi nophil-
rich inflammation [49]. Of the eotaxin family members, CCL26 actually displays the weakest
chemotactic activity for eosinophils, despite also binding to CCR3. It has a lower binding affinity
for CCR3 and induces only a relatively modest chemotactic response compared to CCL11 or
CCL24. Notably, CCL26 is upregulated by IL -13 and is predominantly expressed by airway
epithelial cells during allergic inflammation. Although its role in eosinophil recruitment is more
limited, CCL26 is thought to contribute to the pathology of asthma [50]. In the allergic lung, local
IL-4 and IL-13 further facilitate eosinophil infiltration by acting on the endothelium and
epithelium: these cytokines increase the adhesiveness of endothelial cells by upregulating  the
adhesion molecule VCAM-1 [51], and they stimulate bronchial epithelial cells to secrete CCL11

Eosinophil survival in tissues during inflammation is also prolonged by cytokines. IL-5, IL-3, and
GM-CSF (granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor) act as crucial hematopoietic signals
that regulate eosinophil development, maturation, and longevit y [10]. IL-3 primarily drives the
early expansion of eosinophil progenitor cells in the bone marrow, while IL -5 is essential for the
terminal differentiation of these progenitors into mature eosinophils [46]. GM-CSF supports the
maturation of eosinophil pr ecursors and enhances the survival of both developing and mature
eosinophils. Mechanistically, IL-3 exerts its effects via the IL-3 receptor (IL-3R), which consists
of a specific α subunit (IL-3Rα) paired with a β-common (βc) chain that is shared with the receptors
for IL-5 and GM-CSF [52]. Engagement of IL-3R by IL-3 triggers multiple intracellular signaling
pathways – including JAK/STAT, MAPK, and PI3K – which act in synergy with IL -5 and GM-
CSF signaling to promote eosinophil growth and resist apoptosis [53]. When IL -3 or GM -CSF
signaling is dysregulated, it can lead to eosinophilic disorders characterized by excessive
eosinophil accumulation, tissue damage, and chronic inflammation [52, 54].
Beyond their role in inflammation, eosinophils actively participate in regulating immune
responses. Eosinophils promote humoral immunity by priming B cells, which can enhance
antibody production [35]. They also play a central role in shaping type 2 immunity: eosinophils
can function as antigen-presenting cells to activate CD4+ T cells, and they secrete an array of type
2 cytokines from their granules – notably IL-4, IL-5, and IL -13 – which influence the immune
environment [35]. Through these actions, eosinophils help orchestrate the balance between Th1
and Th2 immune responses, closely regulating the shift toward Th2 immunity when appropriate
[55].
Molecular Triggers of Eosinophil Function
Eosinophils are terminally differentiated granulocytes that develop in the bone marrow and
circulate briefly in the bloodstream before migrating into tissues. These cells are characterized by
their bilobed nucleus and large cytoplasmic granules filled with  cytotoxic proteins and enzymes.
In parasitic infections, eosinophils degranulate and release potent molecules such as major basic
protein and eosinophil peroxidase that directly damage helminths and other parasites. However, in
allergic airway diseases like atopic asthma, the same degranulation process can injure host tissues.
Elevated eosinophil counts in the bronchial mucosa of asthmatic patients correlate with epithelial

damage and disease severity [56]. This cytotoxic function is complemented by the ability of
activated eosinophils to extrude mitochondrial DNA and granule p roteins into the extracellular
space, forming EETs. These net -like structures immobilize pathogens but can also contribute to

priming with interferon-γ and high concentrations of IL-5 increases the propensity of eosinophils

remain an important area for future research [26].
Beyond their cytotoxic role, eosinophils serve as immunomodulatory cells. They synthesize and
secrete a surpr isingly broad spectrum of cytokines, chemokines, and lipid mediators [ 64]. Upon
activation, eosinophils release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, IL-12,

eotaxin-1, and lipid mediators like platelet -activating factor and leukotriene  C4 [60]. These
mediators regulate adhesion molecule expression, cell trafficking, vascular permeability, mucus
secretion, and smooth -muscle contractility, thereby amplifying type  2 inflammation and airway
hyper-responsiveness. Eosinophi ls can also initiate antigen -specific immune responses by
functioning as antigen -presenting cells. Under appropriate activation conditions, eosinophils
express major histocompatibility complex class  II molecules and co-stimulatory proteins such as
CD86 and CD30 ligand [66]. This allows them to present antigenic peptides to T lymphocytes and
modulate adaptive immunity, illustrating their role as a bridge between innate and adaptive
responses [61].
Epithelial-derived cytok ines are critical regulators of eosinophil activation and survival. The
“alarmins” TSLP, IL -25 and IL -33 are released by damaged airway epithelium and stimulate
type 2 innate lymphoid cells to produce IL -5, thereby promoting eosinophil differentiation,
recruitment, and survival [62]. TSLP and IL -33 can act directly on eosinophils by engaging the
TSLP receptor and ST2 (the IL -33 recep tor), inhibiting apoptosis and extending eosinophil
lifespan [63]. These signals not only amplify Th2 responses but also enhance eosinophil function
by increasing cytokine secretion and responsiveness to chemokines.

Revisiting Eosinophil Diversity
In recent years, multiple studies have proposed that eosinophils comprise distinct subtypes. A
central unresolved question is whether these states reflect a single lineage at different activation or
maturation stages, or, by analogy with Th1 vs. Th2 lymphocytes, represent intrinsically distinct
populations generated in the bone marrow with stable functional programs. A pivotal murine study
by Mesnil et al. [63] addressed this issue in an asthma model, delineating lung resident,
homeostatic/physiological eosinophils (rEos) versus allergen -recruited inflammatory eosinophils
(iEos). This framework is influential but should be interpreted alongside evidence that eosinophil
phenotypes and functions differ substantially across tissues. Across complementary experiments
in lung and blood , the investigators demonstrated clear, reproducible differences between
eosinophil populations in allergic asthmatic mice and healthy controls. In mice, the rEos –iEos
distinction is supported by nuclear morphology, membrane pro tein expression, and tissue
localization. rEos display canonical eosinophil features , including red-staining granules rich in
MBP and EPO —and co-express CCR3, Siglec-F, and CD125  (the IL-5 receptor α subunit) [ 35,
39, 64]. Depending on the tissue, rEos can also express CD11b (intestine, thymus, adipose tissue),
F4/80 (mammary gland, lung, adipose tissue), and activation/adhesion molecules such as CD69
and CD44 (intestine, thymus) [39, 63, 64]. Most tissue rEos possess a segmented nucleus and are
CD11c positive [12, 59].
The lung is a notable exception. Lung rEos resemble resting blood eosinophils, exhibiting a ring-
shaped nucleus, CD62L expression, intermediate Siglec-F, and CD11c negativity [63, 65]. In mice,
a ring-shaped nucleus often indicates cellular immaturity [66], suggesting that lung rEos retain an
immature phenotype after dissemination to the airways. Despite this, they undergo gradual
degranulation and retain phagocytic capacity, indicating preserved effector functions.
Importantly, during allergic airway inflammation , the number, localization, and
morphological/phenotypic/transcriptomic characteristics of lung rEos remain stable and distinct
from iEos. By contrast, iEos are robustly recruited to the lungs upon allergen exposure and are
defined as SiglecFhi CD62L-CD101hi cells with a segmented nucleus; CD101 functions as an iEos
marker and is not expressed on lung rEos. These observations support a true rEos-iEos dichotomy
and further suggest that analogous subsets exist in the circulation of asthmatic mice, implying that
differentiation may occur prior to tissue recruitment. Consistent with this cross-species framework,
Mesnil et al. [63] also compared human samples, showing that parenchymal rEos in non-asthmatic

Divergent functions of eosinophil subsets in airway disease
The concept of iEos and rEos is relatively new. Evidence regarding their mechanisms and roles
across diseases remains limited, and key clinical contexts are still unexplored. To date, no studies
have specifically eva luated these phenotypes during exacerbations, nor in patients treated with
monoclonal antibodies other than mepolizumab and benralizumab. Moreover, their functional
roles have not yet been published and thus remain speculative. This evidence gap has been
explicitly highlighted by the EAACI task force on new molecular insights and clinical functions
of eosinophils [76], which calls for focused research on this topic in the coming years.
Even with these limitations, several hypotheses emerge regarding potential clinical utility. One is
that a threshold proportion of iEos , potentially in the 8-10% range, could suffice to classify an

Harnessing Eosinophils for Anti-Tumor Strategies
Both myeloid and lymphoid malignancies have well -documented associations with eosinophilia.
In myeloid neoplasms (such as chronic myelogenous leukemia, chronic eosinophilic leukemia, and
acute myeloid leukemia)  [77], eosinophilia arises directly from the clonal prolife ration of
malignant myeloid progenitor cells. By contrast, in lymphoid neoplasms associated with
eosinophilia, the eosinophil expansion is typically driven indirectly by cytokine secretion [78]. T-
cell neoplasms are most frequently linked to eosinophilia (e.g., cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, adult
T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, angioimmunoblastic T -cell lymphoma), although certain B -cell
malignancies (notably Hodgkin lymphoma and B -lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma) have also
been implicated [79, 80] (Figure 4).
In practice, unexplained eosinophilia in peripheral blood or bone marrow usually prompts
pathologists to search for an underlying myeloid or lymphoid neoplasm, since reactive (non -
neoplastic) eosinophilia provides limited diagnostic information. Recent public ations offer
comprehensive reviews of this topic, including molecular -level diagnostic approaches [ 81].
Additionally, eosinophilia has been observed in some non -hematologic malignancies (such as
breast, pancreatic, and lung cancer), where it is sometimes regarded as a paraneoplastic syndrome
[82].
Pharmacotherapy in Eosinophil-Driven Diseases
Glucocorticoids have traditionally been the cornerstone treatment for eosinophilic diseases, as they
decrease eosinophil levels and broadly reduce inflammation [ 83]. Corticosteroids—administered
systemically or by inhalation—remain the gold standard therapy, primarily by suppressing type 2
(T2) inflammation and reducing eosinophil-promoting mediators like IL-5 and eotaxin. However,
glucocorticoids are associated with si gnificant side effects and often prove inadequate for
controlling severe disease.
In recent years, a major therapeutic advance has been the development of biologic medications
that specifically target eosinophils or the pathways governing their recruitment  and survival.
Monoclonal antibodies against IL-5 (e.g., mepolizumab, reslizumab) or against the IL -5 receptor

Another promising target is Siglec -8, an inhibitory receptor expressed on eosinophils and mast

modulation may have context -dependent clinical effects and underscore the need to distinguish
eosinophils as pathogenic drivers, biomarkers of tissue inflammation, or participants in broader
immune circuits.
Patient selection for eos inophil-targeted therapies has largely been based on peripheral blood
eosinophil counts [ 87]. However, blood eosinophil levels have important limitations: elevated
blood eosinophils are not always reliable indicators of eosinophilia, correlate with disease severity,
or predict therapeutic responsiveness [88]. Indeed, even in patients with high baseline eosinophil
counts and clear eosinophil reductions on therapy, clinical benefits have sometimes been only
modest. For example, in eosinophilic esophagitis (Eo E), treatments like benralizumab (anti -IL-
5Rα) or Siglec-8 blockade have yielded limited improvement despite effectively depleting tissue
eosinophils [ 89]. Similarly, while anti –IL-5/IL-5Rα therapies help many patients with severe
eosinophilic asthma, a su bset of patients fails to respond despite exhibiting a pronounced
eosinophilic inflammatory signature [90].
This variability in treatment response may reflect several factors. For instance, some patients may
have parallel non–type 2 inflammatory pathways driving disease [91] or genetic variants that alter
T2 cytokine signaling [92]. In other cases, disease activity might be sustained by alternative, tissue-
specific eosinophil survival mechanisms [ 93] or by therapy -resistant eosinophil subsets residing
in particular anatomical niches [94].
Importantly, eosinophil activity (such as degranulation) may be a more accurate indicator of
disease severity and treatment response than eosinophil counts alone. Conditions with similar
degrees of eosinophil infiltration can exhibit markedly different levels of eosinophil degranulation

warrants further investigation.
Beyond agents that directly target eosinophils, several other biolo gics attenuate eosinophilic
inflammation indirectly by suppressing upstream T2 immune pathways or epithelial “alarmin”
signals. For example, dupilumab (which blocks IL -4Rα and thus interferes with IL -4 and IL -13
signaling) and the anti -TSLP antibody tezepe lumab can reduce eosinophilic inflammation in
asthma and other conditions [62, 83]. Biologics targeting IL-33 or its receptor ST2 are also in late-
stage clinical development [ 100]. In addition, the oral investigational drug dexpramipexole has
shown the ability to markedly deplete blood and tissue eosinophils, although its precise mechanism
of action remains unknown [101].
A persistent challenge across all eosinophil -modulating therapies is the unpredictability of
individual patient responses. This uncertainty has prompted efforts to discover new biomarkers
that can guide the selection and tailoring of treatments. Potential approaches include detailed
transcriptomic and proteomic profiling of eosinophils from patient samples, advanced imaging
techniques to assess the spatial distribution and activation state of eosinophils in affected tissues,
and integrated clinical–molecular frameworks that combine biomarker data with imaging findings
and traditional clinical parameters. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of eosinophil heterogeneity
and function in different disease contexts will be essential for optimizing personalized treatment
strategies and improving patient outcomes (Figure 5).

Limitations of Single-Cell Eosinophil Profiling
Single-cell RNA se quencing (scRNA-seq) has become a powerful approach to dissect immune
cell heterogeneity, but eosinophils presen t unique technical obstacles [1 02]. These granulocytes
are fragile and easily activated during isolation, which can introduce artifactual changes in their
gene expression profiles [102]. Moreover, eosinophils are packed with ribonucleases (RNases) in
their granules , which can rapidly degrade RNA if the cells become stressed or lyse during
processing [1 03]. Consequently, eosinophils have ofte n been underrepresented in single -cell
studies because they may be lost or transcriptionally altered by the time of analysis.
To minimize these artifacts, researchers have developed gentle, rapid handling protocols for
eosinophil isolation. For example, di rectly encapsulating freshly isolated eosinophils into
microfluidic scRNA -seq platforms can reduce activation time and preserve the cells’ native
transcriptomic state [104]. Specialized “stress-free” processing methods likewise aim to prevent
degranulation and RNA degradation, yielding higher -quality eosinophil transcriptomes [ 105].
Alternatively, single -nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA -seq) can be employed to profile
eosinophils: by capturing messenger RNA from isolated nuclei, this approach bypasses the
cytoplasmic RNases and can provide transcriptomic insights even when whole-cell sequencing is
problematic. Using these strategies, researchers are increasingly able to obtain reliable single-cell
data for eosinophil phenotyping.
Spatial transcriptomic methods profile cells in situ  within intact tissues, preserving the spatial
context that is lost during cell isolation [ 106]. This histology-based approach avoids the need to
physically isolate eosinophils and thus circumvents some issues of cell fragility. Recent  
technological advances have made high-plex spatial transcriptomics feasible. For example, several
platforms — such as 10x Genomics Visium and Xenium, NanoString GeoMx and CosMx, and
Vizgen’s MERFISH -based MERSCOPE — can generate transcriptome -wide gene ex pression
maps directly from tissue sections at near -cellular resolution [1 07]. These technologies enable
investigators to pinpoint eosinophil-specific gene expression signatures within their native tissue
microenvironments.
However, analyzing spatial transcriptomic data presents its own challenges. Delineating individual
eosinophils in crowded tissue sections and handling the enormous volume of data generated
requires sophisticated computational tools [1 07]. Recent advances in spatial statistics and

In addition, researchers are integrating spatial transcriptomics with multiplexed imaging

maps can be co -registered with immunofluorescence or mass -spectrometry-based imaging (such
as MALDI or imaging mass cytometry) to correlate eosinophil gene expression with protein

Together, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic tools are providing unprec edented insights into
eosinophil subsets and their functions in disease. By capturing eosinophils ’ gene expression
either in suspension or within intact tissues, researchers can distinguish different activation states
(e.g., homeostatic vs. inflammatory) and observe how local micro-environmental cues influence
eosinophil behavior. Mapping eosinophils in the context of surrounding cells and signals is
especially valuable for identifying which eosinophil phenotypes drive pathology versus aid in
tissue repair. Ultimately, these advanced phenotyping methods will refine our understanding of
eosinophil heterogeneity in conditions like asthma, EOE, and other inflammatory diseases
(including certain cancers).
Conclusion and future directions
Eosinophils are granulocytic cells that have long been regarded as purely inflammatory and
defensive, primarily in the context of pathology. This view has shifted as accumulating evidence
reveals homeostatic roles and cooperative interactions with other immune cells. A key advance is
the recognition of phenotypically distinct eosinophil states, including, but not limited to, iEos and
rEos, and their putative links to tissue homeostasis, host protection, disease activity, and treatment
response. Yet fundamental question s remain unresolved and limit translation. Do iEos and rEos
represent stable lineages or activation/maturation states within a single lineage? Are they specified
in the bone marrow  or diversified after tissue entry ? Do they function equivalently  upon
stimulation, and do they share genomic/epigenomic programs? Can they serve as biomarkers to
guide the use of monoclonal antibodies in asthma and COPD? Historically, most studies have
treated eosinophils as a homogeneous population, which complicates interpretat ion and cross -
study comparisons.

Moving forward, subtype -aware research is essential. We advocate prospective, multi -center
studies that (i) predefine iEos and rEos by harmonized cytometric panels (for humans, e.g., Siglec-
8, CD62L, CD101, CCR3, CD11b, CD125) with standardized pre -analytics (anticoagulant,

include functional assays (chemotaxis, degranulation, cytokine secretion, phagocytosis) aligned to
clinical phenotypes. Longitudinal sampling across stable state , exacerbation, and post-
exacerbation is needed to define temporal dynamics and test whether iEos ex pand during
exacerbations in asthma or COPD. In parallel, lineage tracing and bone-marrow progenitor studies
should clarify ontogeny and plasticity.
Clinically, the field should evaluate whether iEos frequency (rather than total eosinophil count
alone) imp roves endotyping and treatment selection . Subtype -specific metrics could identify
patients with low peripheral eosinophil counts but high iEos proportions who nonetheless display
Th2-high biology  and may benefit from anti-IL-5/IL-5R strategies. Conversely,  patients with
airway eosinophilia but low circulating iEos may align better with upstream pathway blockade
(e.g., TSLP). Such frameworks require rigorous validation against hard outcomes (exacerbations,
lung function, FeNO, symptom scores) and on-treatment trajectories  under currently approved
biologics.
Finally, we recommend establishing reporting standards  for eosinophil -subtype studies
(definitions, gating, controls, data sharing) and exploring clinically actionable thresholds for iEos
that are reproduc ible across platforms. Addressing these priorities will refine the biological
understanding of eosinophil heterogeneity, enhance patient stratification , and accelerate
personalized medicine for eosinophil-associated diseases.

Figure 1.  Hierarchical tree of eosinophil progenitor formation in the bone marrow. CMPs give rise
to MEPs and GMPs, with eosinophil commitment regulated by C/EBP, GATA-1, and modulated by
PU.1, FOG, and Id1. Eosinophil progenitors expand under GM-CSF, IL-3, and IL-5. Later on, these
cells downregulate CD34 and leave the bone marrow for the circulation. Notably, human eosinophil
progenitors arise directly from CMPs, whereas in mice they develop via GMPs, which are bipotent
for eosinophils and neutrophils.

Figure 2. Phenotyping of blood eosinophil subtypes in mouse and human. These subsets reflect
homeostatic versus inflammatory functions across species —rEos: resident eosinophils , iEos:
inflammatory eosinophils.

Figure 3. The different roles of eosinophil subgroups in chronic inflammatory diseases .  
Elevated levels of  pro-inflammatory cytokines in inflamed tissues drive the proliferation and
differentiation of eosinophil progenitors (EoP) into distinct resident (rEOS) and inflammatory
(iEOS) eosinophil subpopulations before they enter  the bloodstream. Chemokines recrui t these
eosinophils to sites of inflammation, where upregulated adhesion molecules on the vascular
endothelium slow their transit and facilitate tissue infiltration. Within this microenvironment,
eosinophils become activated and release cytokines and granu le proteins. These mediators
exacerbate inflammation and tissue damage, contributing to the pathogenesis of chronic
inflammatory diseases such as CRSwNP, asthma, EoE, and EGPA. CRSwNP: chronic
rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, EGPA: eosinophilic granulomat osis with polyangiitis, EoE:
eosinophilic esophagitis, EoP: eosinophil progenitors, iEOS: inflammatory eosinophil, rEOS:
resident eosinophil.

Figure 4. Eosinophil recruitment and anti -tumor functions.  
Tumor-derived cytokines and alarmins (e.g., IL-5, IL-33) recruit and activate eosinophils at tumor
sites. Activated eosinophils exert direct cytotoxic effects through degranulation (MBP-1, RNases)
and indirect effects by recruiting NK cells, CD8 ⁺ T cells, and dendritic cells, and by polarizing
macrophages tow ard an M1 phenotype. They also modulate tumor vasculature, increasing
leakiness and promoting necrosis. Abbreviations: IL, interleukin; CCL, CC -chemokine ligand;
CXCL, CXC-chemokine ligand; TNF-α, tumor necrosis factor-α; CCR, CC-chemokine receptor;
