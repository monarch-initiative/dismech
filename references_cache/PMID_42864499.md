# Micronuclear ecDNA constrains tumor cell fitness and is associated with favorable patient survival.
**Authors:** Brückner L, Xu R, Tang J, Gnanasekar A, Herrmann A, Wong IT, Zhang S, Tu F, Pilon M, Kukalev A, Pardon K, Sidorova O, Atta J, Yu Q, Montuori G, Pradella D, Ilić M, Suina K, Novais-Cruz M, Kaltenbach S, Treue D, Giurgiu-Kraljič M, Herzog S, Hollinger AS, Wittstruck N, Fernandez M, Wolozyn N, Becker F, Louma VR, Yan X, Schmargon R, Dörr JR, Gamlin D, Lehmann A, Gürgen D, Richter M, Dubois F, Simeoni F, Pennycook BR, Hamilton A, Lindemann RK, Dawes C, Balmus G, Hero B, Fischer M, Bafna V, Verhaak R, Wahl G, Liu Y, Koche RP, Papathanasiou S, Medema R, Spanjaard B, Ventura A, Pombo A, Huang W, Scheer M, Werner B, Chang HY, Mischel PS, Henssen AG

**DOI:** [10.1038/s41588-026-02782-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-026-02782-w)

## Content

Extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) contributes to cancer genome instability by enabling high-copy oncogene amplification and genetic intratumoral heterogeneity. Micronuclei (MNs) are common in chromosomally unstable cancers, yet their role in ecDNA-driven tumors remains unclear. Here we investigate how ecDNA damage, mitotic segregation and MN formation are linked. We find that ecDNAs are a major source of MNs following their damage, particularly under replication stress. Replication stress promotes mitotic ecDNA clustering, asymmetric inheritance, mis-segregation into MNs and reduced transcriptional output. Single-MN sequencing reveals that multiple distinct ecDNA species coalesce within individual MNs. Cells inheriting ecDNA+ MNs display impaired proliferative capacity and are more likely to undergo cell death. In patients with MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma, higher frequencies of ecDNA+ MNs at diagnosis are associated with improved overall survival. Together, these findings identify ecDNA mis-segregation as a mechanism that limits tumor fitness, highlighting mitotic ecDNA retention as a potential therapeutic vulnerability.

heterogeneity. Micronuclei (MNs) are common in chromosomally unstable

are linked. We find that ecDNAs are a major source of MNs following their

Extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) has emerged as a major form of cancer
genome instability, driving high-copy oncogene amplification, intratu-
moral heterogeneity, rapid tumor evolution and therapy resistance1,2.
Across diverse cancer types, the presence of ecDNA (ecDNA+) corre-
lates with poor outcomes compared to tumors with linear chromo-
somal amplifications or other types of genome instability3. ecDNAs
are relatively large (approximately 1–2 Mb)4, chromatinized, circular
DNA structures that are enriched for oncogenes as well as regulatory
and immunomodulatory elements 5–7. They are observed predomi-
nantly in cancer cells and are distinct from extrachromosomal circular
DNA, which comprises smaller, typically non-clonal circular DNA ele-
ments that rarely encompass full genes and can also be detected in
normal cells8–10. The probable absence of centromeres on ecDNA11,12
permits uneven segregation during mitosis, fueling heterogeneity and
accelerating tumor evolution through non-Mendelian inheritance11.
Chromosomal tethering mechanisms are thought to promote ecDNA
transmission during cell division13,14.
MNs are small, extranuclear bodies initially enclosed by nuclear
envelopes that arise at the end of mitosis and contain mis-segregated
chromosomes or chromatin fragments 15,16, distinguishing them
from other extranuclear structures such as exclusomes 17. They are a
hallmark of chromosomal instability. Chromatin in MNs can undergo
chromothripsis 18–20 and epigenetic remodeling 21–23, resulting in
long-term genomic and transcriptional alterations. MNs can serve
as intermediates for ecDNA generation20, suggesting reciprocal links
between ecDNA and MN biology. Treatment of ecDNA-bearing cells
with hydroxyurea (HU) or other DNA-damaging agents increases MN
frequency and decreases oncogene levels, implying that damaged
ecDNA may seed MN formation 24–26. However, the mechanisms of
MN generation in ecDNA+ tumors, their molecular composition and
functional consequences remain unknown.
Using cell lines, engineered mouse models, patient tumor samples,
single-MN sequencing and live-cell imaging, we set out to define how
micronucleation arises from ecDNA and how this process shapes cancer
cell fitness and patient outcome.

Replication stress promotes ecDNA micronucleation
Given that ecDNAs lack centromeres, they can rely on chromosomal
tethering to ensure inheritance during mitosis11,14,30. MN formation can
occur when ecDNAs fail to tether to chromosomes during cell division
(Fig. 2a), as also evidenced by recent reports14,31. If ecDNAs detached,
mis-segregated stochastically and remained independent, individual
ecDNA molecules would be expected to segregate into separate MNs
and be inherited by both daughter cells. Instead, live-cell imaging of
unperturbed COLO 320DM cells expressing fluorescently labeled
MYC ecDNA (tetR–mNeonGreen) and chromatin (H2B–emiRFP670)30
revealed that ecDNA frequently detach from chromatids as clusters
during mitosis and are subsequently encapsulated on average into
single MNs in daughter cells at the end of mitosis (Fig. 2a,b and Sup-
plementary Video 1). The frequency of detached clusters positively
scaled with the abundance of ecDNA in cells (Fig. 2c–d), suggesting
that although individual detachment events may occur stochastically,
ecDNA coalescence and subsequent mis-segregation are governed by
non-random processes.
ecDNAs are frequently damaged and prone to replication stress29,32,
prompting us to test whether DNA damage can promote clustered
detachment and micronucleation. Consistent with these prior findings,
significantly higher signs of replication stress and DNA damage were
observed on ecDNA compared to HSRs (Supplementary Fig. 3a–f).
Treatment with HU, which induces replication stress and causes DNA
damage by depleting nucleotides, increased signs of replication stress,
especially on ecDNA (Supplementary Fig. 3a–f). Nanopore-based
fork progression analysis confirmed reduced nucleotide incorpora -
tion and disproportional replication fork slowing on ecDNA, both in
untreated conditions and upon HU treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4).
This resulted in substantially more untethered, γH2AX+ ecDNA+ clusters
during anaphase in COLO 320DM cells compared to their isogenic HSR
counterparts as well as in non-isogenic cell line pairs (Fig. 2e–g and
Supplementary Fig. 5). As a result, ecDNA+ cells exhibited increased MN
frequency, and MNs were significantly enriched for ecDNA oncogene
sequences (oncogene-positive MNs) to an extent exceeding expecta-
tions from random segregation (Supplementary Fig. 6a,b). The increase
in MN abundance was rescued by transfection of nucleotides, con -
firming that HU-induced nucleotide depletion promoted MN forma-
tion (Supplementary Fig. 6c,d). No enrichment of MNs for oncogene

MN formation, we analyzed adult neural stem cells (aNS cells) from

Distinct ecDNAs co-segregate into single MNs
Up to 25% of ecDNA+ tumors harbor more than one ecDNA species30.
Given that damaged ecDNAs cluster before the end of mitosis, indi-
vidual MNs might contain oncogenes from distinct ecDNA species. At
the same time, DNA damage could, in principle, generate chromosomal
fragments that co-segregate into MNs together with attached ecDNA.
T o determine the genomic content of MNs in cancer cell lines with mul-
tiple ecDNA species, we developed a laser microdissection method to
isolate single MNs and their corresponding primary nuclei, followed
by paired-end sequencing (Fig. 3a). This approach was applied to TR-14
cells, which harbor multiple ecDNA species, each carrying a distinct
oncogene (for example, MYCN, MDM2 or CDK4) alongside additional
passenger genes. The circular structure of these ecDNAs was previously
assessed by sequence reconstruction27, and their extrachromosomal
presence, oncogene content and copy number per cell were confirmed
by metaphase-FISH (Fig. 3b). After HU treatment to induce replication
stress, 70% of MNs were strongly enriched in all ecDNA species (Fig. 3c
and Supplementary Fig. 9a,b), consistent with collective micronuclea-
tion of separate ecDNAs. Notably, the majority of ecDNA+ MNs (92.3%)
lacked detectable linear chromatin fragments, arguing against frequent
co-segregation of chromosomal fragments together with ecDNA and
indicating that ecDNA mis-segregates more readily than chromosomal
DNA under replication stress (Fig. 3d,e and Supplementary Fig. 9c).
Importantly, ecDNA was markedly enriched relative to linear chromo-
somal DNA in MNs compared with the corresponding primary nuclei, a
pattern that would not be expected if ecDNA mis-segregated stochasti-
cally, in which case similar relative enrichments would be anticipated in
both compartments. Copy number proportions among ecDNA species
within individual MNs mirrored those in the primary nucleus (Fig. 3f
and Supplementary Fig. 9d), and enrichment of different ecDNA spe-
cies correlated positively in MNs (Fig. 3g–j), in line with the collective
micronucleation of structurally distinct ecDNAs.
T o test whether damage to one ecDNA species can result in collec-
tive micronucleation of other ecDNA species, we introduced CRISPR–
Cas9-mediated double-strand breaks on MYC  ecDNA of DMS273
cells, which also harbor a separate ecDNA species encoding MYCL
(Supplementary Fig. 10a). Targeted cleavage increased γH2AX foci on
MYC and promoted its presence in MNs, while concurrently enriching
MYCL ecDNA in the same MN (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 10b–f).
Taken together, these findings suggest that ecDNAs mis-segregate
into MNs en masse in cells with elevated ecDNA damage and are largely
independent of linear chromatin fragments, raising the possibility
that this impacts ecDNA inheritance patterns (Supplementary Fig. 11).
Micronucleation constrains ecDNA function
Live-cell imaging revealed that ecDNA clusters, both under basal
conditions and following HU treatment, segregated on average
into a single MN and one of the two daughter cells, suggesting that
micronucleation introduces asymmetry into ecDNA inheritance
(Supplementary Fig. 12a,b and Supplementary Videos 2 and 3). This
suggests that ecDNA micronucleation skews previously observed
random inheritance patterns 11, which would have implications for
tumor evolution. T o quantify this effect, we measured ecDNA copy
number in paired daughter cells at cytokinesis using DNA FISH after
HU treatment. Compared to untreated controls, HU treatment mark-
edly increased the degree of ecDNA imbalance between daughter cells
(Supplementary Fig. 12b), confirming that replication stress promotes
asymmetric segregation through micronucleation.

T o further investigate the consequence of asymmetric segrega-
tion for the inheritance of ecDNA, we developed a computational
model (Supplementary Fig. 12c). Assuming a probability q  of asym-
metric segregation and probability 1 − q  of random segregation, the
resulting ecDNA distribution in daughter cells shifted from unimodal
(random segregation) to bimodal or trimodal patterns as q  and p 1
varied (Supplementary Fig. 12d). Using an approximate Bayesian com-
putation framework, we inferred optimal parameter estimates for
p1 and q (Supplementary Fig. 12e–i). Applying this procedure to our
experimental data from pairs of daughter cells with and without HU
treatment, we observed increased biased segregation post-treatment
(Supplementary Fig. 12j–l). Therefore, replication stress enhances
ecDNA segregation asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 12).
Linear chromatin fragments incorporated in MNs remain epige-
netically and transcriptionally silenced21–23. We set out to test whether
ecDNA is also repressed in MNs following replication stress (Fig. 4a).
During mitosis, ecDNA retained high H3K27Ac and low H3K27me3,
unlike chromosomal DNA, which showed the expected inactive chro-
matin patterns (Fig. 4b,c ). However, ecDNA in MNs had significantly
reduced H3K27Ac and stably low H3K27me3 compared to ecDNA in
mitosis and the primary nuclei following HU treatment (Fig. 4d,e).
Chromatin immunoprecipitation with sequencing confirmed reduced
H3K27ac at ecDNA loci, including MYC  (Fig. 4f ). MNs also exhib -

transcriptional activity in MNs (Fig. 4g,h). Indeed, intron RNA FISH
further confirmed that most MNs in ecDNA-containing cell lines
lacked detectable oncogene expression, independent of the onco -
gene encoded (Fig. 4i). RNA sequencing confirmed the decreased
expression of ecDNA-encoded genes, including MYC , alongside
reduced MYC-driven transcriptional programs (Fig. 4j–l). Collec-
tively, these results show that unlike its high expression in the nucleus
and throughout mitosis 30,31, ecDNA within MNs is transcriptionally
silenced, suggesting that micronucleation restrains the oncogenic
potential of ecDNA.

Discussion
This study elucidates the role of ecDNA mis-segregation into MNs and
its implications for oncogene regulation. Our results support the view
that faithful inheritance of ecDNA depends on chromosomal tether-
ing during mitosis13,14,35. When this process fails, oncogene-bearing
ecDNAs can coalesce into clusters that segregate asymmetrically and
are sequestered into MNs, a process that is potentiated by replica -
tion stress-induced damage. This asymmetric accumulation of ecDNA
within MNs is associated with decreased oncogene output and impaired
daughter cell fitness, and it corresponds to improved survival in chil-
dren with MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma. T ogether, these findings
link ecDNA+ MN formation to reduced tumor fitness, including at the
patient level.

micronucleation (Fig. 1). This observation suggests that its acentric
structure stochastically predisposes ecDNA to mis-segregation. This
conclusion is in line with recent reports that identified molecular

Our study also uncovers a previously unrecognized link between
ecDNA micronucleation and cancer cell fate, with direct implications
for patient outcomes. We demonstrate that the ability of cancer cells
to faithfully retain ecDNA during mitosis is a critical determinant of
cellular fitness. Elevated rates of ecDNA micronucleation impair pro-
liferative capacity and increase cell death, thereby attenuating cancer
cell aggressiveness (Fig. 5 ). The observation that sequestration of
oncogene-containing ecDNA into MNs is associated with improved
event-free and overall survival in children with neuroblastoma high-
lights the functional relevance of ecDNA-containing MNs and suggests
their potential utility as biomarkers for patient stratification and for
therapeutic strategies aimed at selectively damaging ecDNA.
Even though the exact mechanism through which MNs affect
cancer cell fates remains to be determined, we provide evidence for at
least one potential contributor. We show that although ecDNA retains
marks of active chromatin in mitosis30, micronucleation triggers epi-
genetic remodeling, similar to what has been shown for linear chromo-
some fragments in MNs 21,22. ecDNA primarily loses H3K27ac in MNs
while retaining mitotic H3K27me3 levels, potentially distinguishing
ecDNA from linear acentric fragments. This process is accompanied
by reduced oncogene transcription, effectively reducing the onco -
genic output of ecDNAs (Fig. 4). As cells are addicted to high oncogene
levels, such reduced oncogene output could explain the decreased
proliferative capacity of cells with ecDNA in MNs. Even though ecDNA
silencing may contribute to altered cancer cell fitness, it probably
does not represent the only mechanism through which ecDNA+ MNs
impact cell physiology.
In summary, our findings provide functional and clinical evidence
that ecDNA segregation during mitosis is more than a curious cel -
lular phenomenon but rather represents a biologically and clinically
relevant process. By establishing ecDNA micronucleation as both a
hallmark of ecDNA-driven cancers and a potential biomarker of patient
outcome, our work opens avenues for its clinical translation. More
broadly, the discovery that ecDNA micronucleation can shape tumor
fitness highlights a vulnerability of ecDNA-bearing cancer cells and
suggests that targeting ecDNA segregation could represent a tractable
therapeutic strategy.

Methods
Patient samples and clinical data access
This study comprised the analysis of tumor and blood samples from
patients diagnosed with neuroblastoma between 1991 and 2022.
Patients were registered and treated according to the trial proto -
cols of the German Society of Pediatric Oncology and Hematology
(GPOH). Patients were registered in GPOH trials NB90 (ref. 37), NB97
(ref. 38), NB2004 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00410631 ) and
NB2016-registry39 (German Clinical Trials Register: DRKS00023442).
All intervention studies (NB90, NB97, NB2004) have completed recruit-
ment and the planned follow-up period for their primary endpoints.
The NB2016-registry is an ongoing prospective neuroblastoma registry
rather than an interventional clinical trial. All protocols were approved
by the ethics committees of the Universities of Cologne as leading
institutions, and approval was confirmed by the local ethics commit-
tees (application numbers 9764, 04-049, 16-432). Informed consent
was obtained from the participants at the local treatment site and was
a prerequisite to register in the respective trials. Trial participation was
a requirement for inclusion in this analysis. This study was conducted
in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Hel-
sinki (2013) and good clinical practice; informed written consent was
obtained from all patients or their guardians. The collection and use of
patient specimens and clinical data were approved by the institutional
review boards of Charité–Universitätsmedizin Berlin and the Medical
Faculty of the University of Cologne. Patients were enrolled in the Ger-
man neuroblastoma clinical trials NB90, NB97 (ethics approval 9764;
ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00017225 ), NB2004/NB2004-HR
(ethics approval 04-049 for NB2004-HR; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier:
NCT00410631) or the NB2016-registry (ethics approval 16-432; German
Clinical Trials Register: DRKS00023442). Specimens and clinical data
were archived and made available by Charité-Universitätsmedizin Ber-
lin or the National Neuroblastoma Biobank and Neuroblastoma Trial
Registry (University Children’s Hospital Cologne) of the GPOH. The
MYCN copy number was determined as a routine diagnostic method
using FISH. DNA and total RNA were isolated from tumor samples with
at least 60% tumor cell content as evaluated by a pathologist.

status of MNs was classified as high or low relative to the median

Clinical data analysis
For the analysis, follow-up information was evaluated to match tumor
samples with the respective patients. The endpoints were overall sur-
vival and event-free survival. Overall survival was defined as death from
any cause, and event-free survival was defined as disease progression
or relapse, or death from any cause. Patients without these events were
censored at last follow-up. Overall survival and event-free survival
were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier estimator 53. The log-rank
test was used for comparison. A Cox proportional hazards regression
was conducted for ecDNA + MN percentage. Statistical analysis was
conducted using SPSS 29. Despite availability, only clinical data from
samples collected at first diagnosis before the start of therapy were
included to eliminate a potential bias owing to therapy-related changes
in tumor genetics.

To determine replication fork speeds, we only retained complete forks (forks running off the end of a read were excluded) from the forkSense
output. We then overlapped the remaining forks with the Decoil amplicon reconstruction for CHP212 and classified them as either circular
(overlapping with ecDNA regions) or linear (overlapping with any other region). To compare ecDNA forks with linear forks of similar chromatin
context, we further filtered forks which overlapped with active regions, by H3K27ac ChIPseq peaks. Bed files for peak calls were taken from
and downloaded from GEO (GSE90683) and converted from hg19 to hg38 using the UCSC liftover tool (https://github.com/
ucscGenomeBrowser/kent). Fork speeds were calculated by dividing the length of each detected fork by the treatment time beginning with

Reads were basecalled using Guppy (v6.4.6, dna_r9.4.1_450bps_hac model, ONT) and aligned to hg38 using minimap (v2.30). We used
DNAscent (v3.1.2) to investigate replication fork dynamics. We applied the DNAscent detect function to detect EdU and BrdU incorporation
on nanopore reads in batches of around 4000 reads. The forkSense function was then used to classify replication forks based on the order
EdU and BrdU incorporation.
To determine replication fork speeds, we only retained complete forks (forks running off the end of a read were excluded) from the forkSense
output. We then overlapped the remaining forks with the Decoil amplicon reconstruction for CHP212 and classified them as either circular
(overlapping with ecDNA regions) or linear (overlapping with any other region). To compare ecDNA forks with linear forks of similar chromatin
context, we further filtered forks which overlapped with active regions, by H3K27ac ChIPseq peaks. Bed files for peak calls were taken from
and downloaded from GEO (GSE90683) and converted from hg19 to hg38 using the UCSC liftover tool (https://github.com/
ucscGenomeBrowser/kent). Fork speeds were calculated by dividing the length of each detected fork by the treatment time beginning with
the start EdU treatment until the start of the thymidine chase out (Untreated: 15 min; HU: 16.4 min).
