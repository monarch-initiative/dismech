# Human iPSC-derived glutamatergic neurons with pathogenic KCNQ2 variants display hyperactive bursting phenotypes.
**Authors:** Sundberg M, Shum C, Norabuena EM, Makhortova NR, Chen C, Yu L, Wightman EV, Kim K, Han SY, Howe J, Poduri A, Buttermore ED, Scherer SW, Sahin M
**Journal:** Neurobiol Dis (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.nbd.2025.107126](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nbd.2025.107126)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in the KCNQ2 gene, which encodes a potassium channel subunit, are associated with neonatal seizures, epileptic encephalopathy, intellectual disability, and autism. Although the consequences of disrupted KCNQ2 channel function have been studied in the past, the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying the development of neurological phenotypes remain unclear, and neuronal models of specific patient variants are lacking. We generated patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from fibroblasts from three individuals with distinct KCNQ2 pathogenic variants. We corrected the KCNQ2 variants using CRISPR-Cas9 editing to create isogenic controls and differentiated these iPSCs into glutamatergic neurons to study the effects of each variant on neuronal function. The three KCNQ2 variants were: 1) KCNQ2 c.875_877delTCCinsCCT, L292_L293delinsPF, 2) KCNQ2 c.766G > T, G256W, and 3) KCNQ2 c.821C > T, T274M. Our data revealed longer neurite outgrowth in two patient lines (T274M and G256W). Transcriptional profiling showed that all three KCNQ2 lines co-expressed genes enriched in synaptic transmission/signaling, cell adhesion, and GTPase signal transduction. Functional analyses of neuronal networks revealed increased burst duration in all three KCNQ2 lines compared with their isogenic controls. Furthermore, neurons from the L292_L293delinsPF and T274M lines displayed increased network connectivity associated with increased density of synaptic markers. Finally, we detected hyperexcitable neuronal networks in the G256W line with electrical stimulation of the neural networks on a high-density microelectrode array, and this phenotype was rescued with retigabine. These disease-related phenotypes for each of the KCNQ2 pathogenic variants can be used for drug screening to identify treatment options for the patients.

Pathogenic variants in the KCNQ2 gene are associated with the development of neonatal seizures, developmental epileptic encephalopathy (DEE), and autism spectrum disorders (ASD) (Dirkx et al., 2020; Miceli et al., 2022). DEE has been associated with severe neurodevelopmental deficits in patients, especially when the seizures are drug resistant and occur soon after birth (Auvin et al., 2016; Dirkx et al., 2020). Recent studies have also implicated the gain of function variants in KCNQ2 with ASD without the occurrence of neonatal seizures (Miceli et al., 2022).

The KCNQ2 gene is located on chromosome 20q13.33 and it encodes a member of the potassium voltage-gated potassium channel subfamily Q, Kv7.2 protein subunits (Wang and Li, 2016). Tetrameric channels comprised of KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 shuttle potassium into and out of neurons and regulate M-currents (Brown and Adams, 1980). KCNQ2 is primarily localized at the axon initial segment and nodes of Ranvier, but is also found at synaptic terminals of neurons, including in dendrites and the soma (Dirkx et al., 2020; Simkin et al., 2021; Wang and Li, 2016). In addition to ion channel dysfunction, recent studies have shown that KCNQ2 plays an important role in neurodevelopment. In typically forming networks, KCNQ channels regulate somatic excitability via neurotransmitter secretion, including bursting of neurons throughout neuronal networks (Wang and Li, 2016). In addition, KCNQ2 channels may affect the regulation of synchronous firing properties of neuronal networks (Piccinin et al., 2006). Thus, a key role of KCNQ2 channels is to regulate neuronal firing, which may also explain why specific epilepsies are affected by variants in KCNQ channels (Gardiner and Lehesjoki, 2000).

There are still uncovered aspects about the mechanism by which KCNQ2 channels impact neuronal excitability. Loss-of-function variants leave neurons in a depolarized state after an action potential leading to increased repetitive firing of glutamatergic neurons (Niday and Tzingounis, 2018). The most significant phenotype that is common between loss-of-function variants of KCNQ2 (or KCNQ3) in humans is recurrent neonatal seizures, which can start upon birth or after 2–3 days of birth (Abreo et al., 2025; Grinton et al., 2015; Millichap et al., 2016). Gain-of-function variants can also cause hyperexcitable glutamatergic neuron networks although the exact mechanisms have not been fully discovered. It has been shown that gain-of-function variants results in overactive potassium channels in interneurons, which leads to reduced inhibition of the neuronal networks and increased activity of the excitatory glutamatergic neuron networks and hyperexcitability (Miceli et al., 2015). This implies that alterations of the whole network signaling rather than intrinsic properties of individual glutamatergic neurons can cause neuronal hyperexcitability in the case of gain-of-function variants. Thus, both increasing and decreasing potassium channel activity, through loss- or gain-of-function variants respectively, can result in the same outcome of neuronal network hyperexcitability(Niday and Tzingounis, 2018).

Previously established mouse models with loss-of-function variants of KCNQ2 p.T274M and p.G256W displayed neuronal hyperexcitability and reduction in M current density/conductance (Abreo et al., 2025; Biba-Maazou et al., 2022). In the mice with p.T274M variant these changes and seizures were affected by the developmental time of the animal and were mainly present during neonatal period (Biba-Maazou et al., 2022). Also, the mice with G256W variant died prematurely due seizures, and the hippocampal CA1 pyramidal cells in the brain slices of the G256W/+ mice were hyperexcitable (Abreo et al., 2025).

In this study, we aimed to understand how three different pathogenic KCNQ2 variants predispose the development of seizures and neurodevelopmental deficits by studying patient-specific and isogenic control iPSC-derived glutamatergic neurons in vitro. We reprogrammed patient fibroblasts from individuals with pathogenic KCNQ2 variants p. L292_L293delinsPF, p. G256W, and p.T274M, into iPSCs and established isogenic control lines by correcting these variants using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. Variant-bearing and isogenic control iPSCs lines were differentiated into excitatory glutamatergic neurons, and we compared their cellular phenotypes through gene expression profiling, morphological measurements, and functional electrophysiological analysis. We identified common and distinguishable cellular disease phenotypes in the three different patient variants in vitro that may reflect differences in the location and effects of each variant. In the future, these functional human neuronal network models and phenotypic data can be used for development of personalized therapies for patients with different KCNQ2 variants.

To generate human iPSC lines from patients with pathogenic variants in KCNQ2 for phenotypic characterization in vitro, we collected fibroblasts from three patients with heterozygous single nucleotide variants in KCNQ2 and reprogrammed these cells into iPSCs using non-integrating Sendai virus (Fusaki et al., 2009; Takahashi et al., 2007). All three patients had presented with epilepsy: tonic seizures and spasms in the child with KCNQ2 variant: L292_L293delinsPF; tonic-clonic seizures and spasms in the patient with KCNQ2 variant: G256W; and focal motor seizures in the patient with KCNQ2 variant: T274M (Supplemental Table 1). The location of these variants are also displayed in the schematic view of the KCNQ2 channel in the Fig. 1A: (1) KCNQ2 variant L292_L293delinsPF is located at transmembrane in the S6 pore domain; (2) KCNQ2 variant G256W is located in the extracellular domain near the S5-S6 pore loop, and; (3) KCNQ2 variant T274M is located in the pore domain.

We used CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to generate isogenic pairs of iPSCs carrying corrected KCNQ2 gene (WT) or KCNQ2 variant, as described in the materials and methods. Each cell line was named as shown in Fig. 1B. To verify the presence of the variants or corrected WT site, we picked clones that were propagated and Sanger-sequenced (Supplemental Fig. 1A). After iNGN2-transductions, we also karyotyped the cell lines with the G-band karyotyping, and showed that the cells persisted normal karyotypes after viral transductions (Supplemental Fig. 1B). Pluripotency of the iPSC lines was characterized with staining of the stem cell colonies with pluripotency markers: OCT-4, NANOG, SSEA-4, and TRA1–60 (Supplemental Fig. 2). After the CRISPR-Cas9 editing, we performed genome sequencing to verify that the CRISPR-Cas9 editing did not introduce any off-target variants to the generated iPSC lines (Shum et al., 2023). We completed whole genome sequencing for the edited cell lines and discovered that one of the corrected iPSC-clones (isoL292_293) expressed a nonsynonymous variant in CACNA1E (Shum et al., 2023) that was not present in the original patient-derived cells (Shum et al., 2023). According to this whole genome sequencing data the other CRISPR-corrected and non-corrected clones also expressed unintended variants that were not present in the original patient-derived cells (Shum et al., 2023), but there is insufficient evidence for the association of these variants with ASD, benign familial neonatal seizures (BFNS) or DEE.

To induce glutamatergic neuron differentiation, we used the previously published protocol by Zhang et al. (2013), with minor modifications. The timeline and sample collection points are shown in the schematic presentation on the Fig. 1C. To characterize the neurite outgrowth phenotypes on the patient cell lines and their isogenic corrected control lines, we plated the iNGN2 neurons at day 6 days of differentiation onto 96-well plates and performed time-lapse live imaging in Incucyte platform (Fig. 2, Supplemental Fig. 3). We detected significantly increased neurite length after 24 h and 72 h of plating on two patient cell lines: T274M/+ and G256W/+ compared to their isogenic controls (Fig. 2B-C). Interestingly, the third patient cell line L292_L293delinsPF/+ did not show an increase in neurite length compared to the isogenic control line (Fig. 2B-C). After 7 days of plating (day 13 of total differentiation time), we still detected longer neurites in the G256W/+ neurons, whereas the two other KCNQ2 variant lines did not show significant differences at this later timepoint (Fig. 2D).

We assessed the differentiation and maturation status of glutamatergic neurons derived from KCNQ2 variant and isogenic control lines by RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) profiling of major neuronal markers, synapse markers and NMDA and AMPA receptor subunits (Supplemental Table 2). Neuronal markers MAP2 and TUBB3 were found to be expressed in both variant-expressing and control neurons (Fig. 3A). Variant and control neurons expressed synapse markers SYP, SYN1, SYT1, DLG4, and NLGN2, NLGN3, and NLGN4X (Fig. 3A). Variant and control neurons also expressed AMPA and NMDA receptor subunits GRIA1, GRIA4, GRIN1, and glutamate transporter SLC17A6, consistent with the maturation of glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 3A). The absence of NANOG, POU5F1, SOX2, PAX6, FOXG1, GFAP and AQP4 expression indicated the absence of stem cells, neural stem cells and astrocytes in our neuronal cultures (Fig. 3A). These data demonstrate that KCNQ2 variants do not affect the differentiation and maturation of iPSCs into induced glutamatergic neurons.

After the removal of genes with low expression, we compared the Pearson correlation coefficients between all samples and found that samples clustered based on differentiation batch and site. After surrogate variable analysis to adjust for batch and site effects, samples did not show a clear cluster based on genotype or CRISPR status (Fig. 3A). Based on this analyses, we did not find evidence that KCNQ2 variants have a major impact on basal neuronal gene expression.

Altered potassium channel gene expression has previously been reported in iPSC-derived neurons generated from a participant with a heterozygous KCNQ2 variant (c.1742G > A)(Simkin et al., 2021). Therefore, we examined the genes encoding voltage-gated BK and IK/SK channel subunits (KCNMA1, KCNMB1–4, KCCN4/KCNN1–3) and the expression of genes encoding other ion channel subunits (KCND2, KCNT1, KCNA1, KCNA2, KCNA4, KCNQ2, HCN1, HCN2, SCN8A and ANK3). We observed increased levels of KCNN1 (potassium calcium-activated channel subfamily N member 1) in the two patient-derived cell lines with L292_L293delinsPF/+ (Fig. 3B, p = 0.005); and T274M/+ (Fig. 3B, p = 0.01) compared to their isogenic controls. We also observed reduced levels of KCNQ5 (potassium voltage-gated channel subfamily Q member 5) in the patient-derived cell line with T274M /+ compared to its isogenic control (Fig. 3B, p = 0.001). Finally, we found that levels of HCN1 (hyperpolarization activated cyclic nucleotide gated potassium channel 1) were reduced in the patient-derived cell line with G256W/+ compared to its isogenic control (Fig. 3B, p = 0.0008). We found that KCNQ2 variant and control neurons did not differ in the expression levels of other voltage-gated BK and IK/SK channel subunits or ion channel subunits.

We identified 142 genes that were differentially expressed between L292_L293delinsPF/+ and its isogenic control (FDR < 0.05 and log2FC > 1), of which 69 were upregulated, and 73 were downregulated. We also identified 126 genes that were differentially expressed between G256W/+ and its isogenic control, of which 86 were upregulated, and 40 were downregulated (Fig. 3C). Finally, we identified 198 genes that were differentially expressed between T274M/+ and its isogenic control, of which 128 were upregulated and 70 were downregulated (Fig. 3C, and Supplemental Fig. 4). A comparison of these three lists of genes showed an overlap of 15 differentially expressed genes between cell lines L292_L293delinsPF/+ and G256W/+, an overlap of 17 differentially expressed genes between cell lines L292_L293delinsPF/+ and T274M /+, an overlap of 10 differentially genes between cell lines G256W/+ and T274M /+, and an overlap of 9 differentially expressed genes between all three cell lines (Supplemental Fig. 4). These 9 genes were CHCHD2 (coiled-coil-helix-coiled-coil-helix domain containing 2), DNAH9 (dynein axonemal heavy chain 9), REC8 (REC8 meiotic recombination protein), IFTAP (intraflagellar transport associated protein), ZNF562 (zinc finger protein 562), SHISA6 (shisa family member 6), LINC00654 (long intergenic non-protein coding RNA 654), CCDC144NL-AS1 (CCDC144NL antisense RNA 1), and PCDHGB4 (protocadherin gamma subfamily B, 4).

We then explored gene set and pathway enrichment from the lists of differentially expressed genes in each KCNQ2 variant line compared with their isogenic controls. Several different gene ontology (GO) terms and pathways were associated with positive fold change, including potassium channels and GABA-B receptor activation. Similarly, several other GO terms and pathways were associated with negative fold change, including MHC class II antigen presentation and endosomal/vacuolar pathway. However, these differences did not survive multiple comparison corrections.

We performed WGCNA of the KCNQ2 variant and isogenic control lines to reveal how different variants may regulate the expression of common gene sets or pathways. When batch was used as a blocking factor, we identified 11 modules of coexpressed genes that were significantly correlated to cell line, 7 modules of coexpressed genes that were significantly correlated to CRISPR status, and 2 modules of coexpressed genes that were significantly correlated to both cell line and CRISPR status. Based on gene ontology enrichment analysis, the functions that were most significantly correlated with cell line and CRISPR status were identified in one of the modules and included synaptic transmission/signaling, cell adhesion and GTPase signal transduction (Fig. 3D). The genes in this module were input to STRING analysis of protein-protein interactions and visualized by Cytoscape version 3.10.2. This revealed 32 clusters of protein interactions, with the main clusters involving USP9X (ubiquitin specific peptidase 9 X-linked), GRIK1 (glutamate receptor kainate type subunit 1), and CAMK2A (calcium/calmodulin dependent protein kinase II alpha) (Fig. 3E).

To follow the RNAseq findings of neuronal differentiation, we validated the presence of TBR1-positive cortical neurons and MAP2-positive neurons in our iNGN2 cultures after 30 days of differentiation (Supplemental Fig. 5). We then investigated synaptic marker expression in these KCNQ2 variant neurons since the WGCNA indicated that synaptic transmission/signaling modules were significantly correlated to cell lines and the CRISPR status (Fig. 3D). We analyzed the presence of synaptic puncta in the neurons with SYN1/PSD95 staining on MAP2-positive neurites (Fig. 4A-B). We detected significantly increased number of synaptic puncta positive for both SYN1 and PSD95 expression on MAP2-positive neurites at day 30 of differentiation on the two patient cell lines: L292_L293/+ and T274M/+ compared to their isogenic controls (Fig. 4B). The third patient cell line G256W/+ did not show any significant difference in the synaptic puncta expression compared to its isogenic control line (Fig. 4B).

Since we identified synaptic transmission/signaling modules through WGCNA and detected altered synaptic marker expression in the KCNQ2 variant neurons at the protein levels, we next investigated whether these deficits also affected to the functionality of the neuronal networks. First, to characterize the functional phenotypes of the KCNQ2 variant cortical neurons, we completed a time series of recordings from the KCNQ2 variant and isogenic control neuronal networks on LD-MEA. As expected, we detected an increase in the number of active electrodes in all studied cell lines during days 12 to 31 of differentiation (Supplemental Fig. 6A). After days 33–35 of differentiation, the number of active electrodes remained consistent in three lines, reduced in one line, and increased slightly in two cell lines (Supplemental Fig. 6 A). At days 30–31, there were no significant differences in the number of active electrodes between cell lines (Supplemental Fig. 6C). In addition, we measured network viability with weighted mean resistance, which was consistent in all the cell lines during the time course of recordings (Supplemental Fig. 6B). Moreover, there were no significant differences in the weighted mean resistance between cell lines at days 30–31 (Supplemental Fig. 6 D). Thus, we chose the data from time point days 30–31 for detailed functional characterization of the KCNQ2 variant neurons and isogenic control neurons. We detected slightly increased weighted mean firing rate in all KCNQ2 variant lines and significantly increased burst duration in all the KCNQ2 variant patient cell lines compared to their isogenic controls (Fig. 5). The number of spikes per burst, the number of network bursts and the network bursting frequency and synchrony index were also increased in two patient-derived KCNQ2 variant neuron lines L292_L293/+ and T274M/+ compared to their isogenic controls. This deficit may be explained by the afore mentioned increase in the SYN1 and PSD95 synaptic puncta on these two patient cell lines compared to their isogenic control lines (Fig. 4). Interestingly, the third patient cell line G256W/+ did not show alterations in these phenotypes compared to its isogenic control line (Fig. 5), which again may reflect the lack of difference in the synaptic marker expression profile compared to its isogenic control line (Fig. 4). Furthermore, inter burst interval was longer in G256W/+ neurons compared to its isogenic control.

For the characterization of neuronal network activity and single neuron activity in the KCNQ2 variant and CRISPR-Cas9 corrected iPSC-derived cortical neurons, we used HD-MEA. Interestingly, we detected increased network burst duration in two patient derived cortical neurons compared to their controls (L292_L293delinsPF/+ and T274M/+, Fig. 6), which was consistent with the data from the LD-MEA recordings (Fig. 5).

To identify the activity of single neurons on the network, we used the axon tracking tool in the HD-MEA software and spike-sorting to identify of individual neurons and their functional ‘footprints’ in the networks (Fig. 6B-F, Supplemental Fig. 8, and Supplemental Movies 1–2). First, the neuron firing rate was not significantly different between lines (Fig. 6C). Neuron conduction velocity was increased in G256W/+ and decreased in T274M/+ (Fig. 6D), compared to their isogenic controls. We used the axon tracking tool to quantify the total neurite length on HD-MEA chips. Total neurite length was significantly increased in one of the patient lines G256W/+ compared to its isogenic control at day 30 of differentiation (Fig. 6E). This aligns with the imaging-based analyses of the increased neurite outgrowth from day 7, 9 to day 13 of the G256W/+ line compared to its isogenic control (Fig. 2). In addition, the amplitude at the initiation site was significantly decreased in T274M/+ neurons compared to its isogenic controls (Fig. 6F). This spike sorting and detection of individual neurons from these patients derived neuronal networks on HD-MEA revealed at the first time the complexity and structure of functional neurites and their signaling direction and speed in a dish.

Finally, since the previous animal studies have indicated that G256W/+ neurons may be hyperexcitable(Abreo et al., 2025), we decided to study hyperexcitability in our human G256W/+ neuron networks by stimulating the cortical neuron networks on HD-MEA. We stimulated the neuronal networks with incremental stimulation steps of 100 mV, from 100 mV up to 900 mV. We detected significantly increased number of total spikes after 900 mV stimulation of G256W/+ networks compared to its isogenic control line (Supplemental Fig. 7E). Retigabine treatment (10 μM) reduced the total number of spikes during stimulation with 900 mV. After 24 h washout of the drug, the total number of spikes increased back to pre-treatment level with the 900 mV stimulation in G256W/+ networks (Supplemental Fig. 7E). The evoked activity peak after 900 mV stimulation was also increased in G256W/+ networks. Evoked activity peak was reduced with Retigabine treatment in G256W/+ networks and restored to increased levels after 24 h post wash of the drug (Supplemental Fig. 7F). We also verified that during the 900 mV stimulation, the active area of the networks in the chips were comparable between isogenic control line isoG256W and KCNQ2 variant line G256W/+ prior to retigabine treatment and post-wash of retigabine treatment (Supplemental Fig. 7G).

To characterize KCNQ2 dysfunction, we generated iPSCs from the fibroblasts of three patients with pathogenic variants in KCNQ2 who had had neonatal seizures and epilepsy associated with ASD. We characterized the iPSC-derived glutamatergic neurons with various genetic, morphological, and physiological methods and detected disease relevant phenotypes specific for each variant (summarized in Supplemental Table 3).

KCNQ2 has been shown to be involved in neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth in PC12 cells and mouse cortical neurons (Zhou et al., 2016). However, previous studies have not investigated neurite outgrowth phenotypes in iNGN2 glutamatergic neurons or 3D embryoid body based neuronal cells with KCNQ2 variants (Filip Rosa et al., 2024; Simkin et al., 2021). Thus, here we investigated whether structural and functional changes occur in human glutamatergic neurons harboring KCNQ2 variants. We report here for the first time altered neurite outgrowth in neurons with G256W variant that was first detected at 24 h after plating with live cell imaging, and persisted up to day 30, when it was detected with functional neurite tracking method on spike sorted neurons on the HD-MEA platform. This implies that morphological changes may happen during development in the patient derived neuronal networks when they carry the specific variant G256W in the KCNQ2 channel. There is also previous evidence of potassium channel involvement in the regulation of neurite outgrowth and neurodevelopment(Arcangeli et al., 1993; Bortolami and Sesti, 2023). Furthermore, KCNQ2 may modulate the resting membrane potential to regulate neuronal differentiation (Zhou et al., 2016). Here we generated glutamatergic neuronal networks with induced overexpression of NGN2 in the iPSCs. It is possible that the use of overexpression of NGN2 by-passes the regular stages of neural progenitor differentiation and obscures the effects of KCNQ2 on differentiation efficiency. Thus, studies of neural stem cells or neural progenitors with these KCNQ2 variants may reveal distinct developmental phenotypes. Nevertheless, our findings provide evidence for a role of KCNQ2 in neurite outgrowth, and suggest that study of KCNQ2 variants impact on membrane depolarization and the control of membrane potential in neural progenitors or immature neurons is warranted in the future.

Transcriptional profiling showed that all KCNQ2 variant neurons and isogenic controls expressed neuronal and synapse markers and AMPA and NMDA receptor subunits, suggesting that the variants investigated in this study did not impair neuronal differentiation by exogenous iNGN2 expression. Surprisingly, principal component analysis (PCA) did not show a clear cluster by genotype or CRISPR status in studied cell lines. Several studies have shown that PCA of RNA-Seq samples from isogenic iPSC lines with genetic alterations such as highly penetrant familial Alzheimer’s disease variants cluster based on genotype (Kwart et al., 2019; Volpato et al., 2018). Our data suggest that the KCNQ2 variants in this study may not have a significant impact on basal gene expression in iNGN2 neurons. Alternatively, subtle genotypic effects may affect neurons in a cell-type specific way and be masked by RNA-seq of bulk populations. Recent single-cell transcriptomic studies of iNGN2 neurons revealed four transcriptionally distinct cell populations and showed that genotypic effects are masked by cell-type compositional heterogeneity and aggregated molecular data from multiple laboratories (Lin et al., 2021; Volpato et al., 2018). Thus, future studies may further dissect the impact of KCNQ2 variants on specific neuron types by single-cell assays.

As KCNQ2 interacts with other potassium channels and other channels to regulate neuronal excitability, we examined their expression levels in detail from the RNA-Seq dataset. Increased levels of KCNN1 were observed in neurons with the L292_L293delinsPF/+ and T274M/+ variants whereas reduced levels of KCNQ5 was observed in neurons with the T274M/+ variant. These changes in gene expression were also observed in iPSC-derived neurons from a participant with a heterozygous KCNQ2 variant (R581Q)(Simkin et al., 2021). In contrast, we observed reduced levels of HCN1 in iPSC-derived neurons with the G256W variant only. The change in expression levels of KCNN1, KCNQ5 and HCN1 correlated with some of the morphological and functional phenotypes assessed in this study (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). Namely, neurons with the L292_L293delinsPF, T274M from our study and R581Q variants (Simkin et al., 2021) showed increased levels of KCNN1, increased number of network bursts and increased bursting frequency compared to isogenic control neurons. Whereas neurons with the G256W variant had reduced levels of HCN1 and longer neurite outgrowth, no difference in the expression of synaptic puncta compared to isogenic control neurons. Thus, KCNQ2 variant neurons show differential expression of selected potassium channels that correlate with some of the morphological and functional phenotypes detected in this study. KCNN1 encodes a voltage-independent calcium-activated channel and is a member of the KCNN family of potassium channel genes. It has been implicated in temporal lobe epilepsy (Oliveira et al., 2010). HCN1 encodes a hyperpolarization-activated cation channel expressed in cortical and hippocampal pyramidal cell dendrites(Shah et al., 2010). HCN1 has been shown to be involved in seizures and epileptogenesis (Huang et al., 2009). Future studies on additional KCNQ2 variants and more in-depth phenotypic characterization may reveal how these variants affect different aspects of neuronal morphology and function.

With RNA-Seq analyses, we also detected common DEGs among the KCNQ2 neurons that include members of the PCDHA-PCDHB-PCDHG gene cluster (Supplemental Fig. 4B). This gene family encodes cadherin-related transmembrane proteins that are expressed in the brain and localized to axons and synapses(Kohmura et al., 1998) (Wu and Maniatis, 1999) (Phillips et al., 2003). These proteins have been implicated in dendrite arborization, neuronal survival(Wang et al., 2002) (Lefebvre et al., 2012), excitatory synapse and inhibitory synapse development and maturation (Mancini et al., 2020; Molumby et al., 2017; Steffen et al., 2021), and neurodevelopmental deficits (Mancini et al., 2020; Steffen et al., 2021) and epilepsy (Lamers et al., 2022). We had also observed differential expression of genes in this gene cluster in neurons derived from isogenic iPSC lines that had been engineered to knock out 10 ASD-risk genes, including KCNQ2(Deneault et al., 2018). Furthermore, downregulation of several genes in this cluster have been observed in SHANK3+/− iPSC-derived organoids(Wang et al., 2022) and Suv39h2-deficient mice (Balan et al., 2021). Previous mouse study has also indicated that loss of γPcdh led to anxiety like phenotypes and social interaction deficits (Steffen et al., 2021) and reduced maturation of inhibitory synapses (Steffen et al., 2021), which may also lead to hyperexcitability of glutamatergic neurons. In addition, one of the PCDH genes, PCDH19, has been associated to developmental epileptic encephalopathy, behavioral deficits and cognitive problems (Lamers et al., 2022). These data and ours indicate that members of the PCDHA-PCDHB-PCDHG gene cluster are involved in the hyperexcitability phenotype and ASD. Mechanistically, these common DEGs among the KCNQ2 neurons indicate shared biological processes or pathways, such as axon or dendrite arborization, synapse development and neuronal function, that may be relevant to the physiological and behavioral deficits, such as hyperexcitability, observed in DEE and ASD (Supplemental Fig. 9).

In support of this, WGCNA and gene ontology enrichment analysis showed that cell line and CRISPR status were most significantly correlated with gene sets related to synaptic transmission/signaling, cell adhesion and GTPase signal transduction. Cell adhesion was also enriched in RNA-Seq and pathway analysis of neurons derived from isogenic iPSC lines that had been engineered to knock out 10 ASD-risk genes, including KCNQ2(Deneault et al., 2018). We explored the network of coexpressed genes further by Cytoscape visualization of STRING analysis of protein-protein interactions. This revealed main clusters of protein interactions involving USP9X, GRIK1 and CAMK2A. Although the expression levels of these genes were not significantly different in KCNQ2 variant neurons compared to their isogenic controls (data not shown), these findings suggest a relationship between KCNQ2 variants and these gene modules. USP9X, GRIK1 and CAMK2A have been implicated in various aspects of neuronal development and synaptic plasticity (Contractor et al., 2001) (Stegeman et al., 2013; Stephenson et al., 2017) (Yoon et al., 2020) and may relate to the increased burst duration observed in all KCNQ2 variant neurons examined in this study. In summary, transcriptome and enrichment analysis revealed common DEGs and shared biological pathways in KCNQ2 neurons. These data suggest that glutamatergic neurons with different KCNQ2 variants have convergent changes in the PCDHA-PCDHB-PCDHG gene family and neuronal hyperexcitability (Supplemental Fig. 9). However, the causative nature of these correlations is not clear and mechanistic experiments are needed in the future. Clinically, these may serve as potential targets for therapeutic intervention or be used as diagnostic markers for precision medicine.

To study neuronal network development further, here we measured the density of synaptic puncta with Synapsin1 and PSD95 staining on MAP2-positive neurites in variant-bearing and corrected cells. We detected an increased number of synaptic puncta on two of the studied KCNQ2 variant cell lines L292_L293delinsPF and T274M. Neurons with KCNQ2 variant G256W, which displayed increased in neurite outgrowth, had no significant change in the synaptic puncta per neurite length. Altered synapse formation affects network functionality and has been reported previously in studies with other neurodevelopmental disorders(Sundberg et al., 2021). To investigate in detail the neuronal network functionality, we recorded the KCNQ2 variant neurons on MEA plates in both low-density MEA(Winden et al., 2019) and high-density MEA platforms(Bakkum et al., 2013; Ballini et al., 2014; Frey et al., 2010; Obien et al., 2014; Sundberg et al., 2021). We detected significantly increased burst durations in all of the KCNQ2 variant patient cell lines; L292_L293delinsPF, T274M, and G256W, compared to their isogenic controls. Previous studies have also shown increased burst length and number of spikes per bursts in another human iPSC derived iNGN2 neuron line with a different KCNQ2 variant, R581Q(Simkin et al., 2021). Similarly in mice, a knockout of Kcnq2 from neocortical neurons caused increased neuronal activity of layer 2/3 (L2/3) somatosensory pyramidal neurons (Niday et al., 2017). In addition, a knock-in mouse model with the KCNQ2 variant T274M had spontaneous seizures at early stages of life. Electrophysiological recordings showed that this variant led to reduction in M current density and hyperexcitability of the neurons, which were dependent on the developmental stage of the neurons as this phenotype diminished during aging (Biba-Maazou et al., 2022). We also detected an increased number of spikes per burst, number of network bursts, and network bursting frequency and increased synchrony index in L292_L293delinsPF; and T274M glutamatergic neuron networks. This deficit may be explained by the increase in the SYN1 and PSD95 synaptic puncta that we detected in these two patient lines compared to their isogenic control lines at the same time point. The third patient cell line G256W did not display deficits in these functional phenotypes, which may reflect the lack of differences in the synaptic marker expression and increased neurite length compared to its isogenic control line.

Next, we were interested to study in more detail the effects of G256W/+ variant in the functional phenotypes of iNGN2 glutamatergic neurons. In clinic, the patient with G256W/+ variant presented with tonic-clonic seizures and spasms. In line with this, a previous study described a patient with KCNQ2 G256W heterozygosity, who had neonatal-onset epilepsy and developmental impairments(Abreo et al., 2025). In that study, the authors also created KCNQ2 variant-expressing G256W/+ mice that had changes in pyramidal cells, where the KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 expression were detected in the neuronal soma, rather than in the axon initial segments (Abreo et al., 2025). Furthermore, hippocampal pyramidal neurons were hyperexcitable in G256W/+ brain slices and these mice died prematurely due to epilepsy(Abreo et al., 2025). To correlate our functional characterizations to these previous findings, we decided to study excitability of the G256W/+ networks and performed stimulation series on HD-MEA. We detected significantly increased number of total spikes after 900 mV stimulation in G256W/+ networks compared to its isogenic control, and Retigabine reduced this effect, suggesting a role for KCNQ2 in the hyperexcitability. Retigabine is anti-seizure medication drug that enhances KCNQ2/KCNQ3 channel activation in neurons. We also detected that G256W cortical neurons had increased neuron conduction velocity and decreased amplitude at the initiation site in the individual spike sorted neurons on HD-MEA. Interestingly, another study has recently discovered loss-of-function phenotypes and reduced M-current and neuronal firing rates in two other KCNQ2 variants (R325G and G315R patient iPSC derived iNGN2 neurons) and reduced burst duration in KCNQ2 mutant murine neurons (Filip Rosa et al., 2024). Consistent with these studies, various genetic variants in potassium channels are linked to intellectual disability and can contribute to both loss-of-function and gain-of-function properties and are dependent on developmental stage(Biba-Maazou et al., 2022; Kessi et al., 2020; Miceli et al., 2015; Niday and Tzingounis, 2018). Thus, these functional differences detected in our study between the three different KCNQ2 variant neurons and their networks may be explained by differences in the location of these variants in the protein structure and the different effects of these variants in the KCNQ2 channel functionality.

One limitation of this study is that here we were solely focused on investigation of the iNGN2-induced glutamatergic excitatory neurons with KCNQ2 variants. We did not differentiate the patient derived iPSC-lines into other types of neurons, e.g. GABAergic neurons, due to time and cost limitations, and since the scope of our study was focused on the excitatory cortical neuron disease phenotyping. However, we are aware that inhibitory GABAergic neurons express KCNQ2/3 channels (Cooper et al., 2001), and KCNQ2 and Kv7/M channels also control interspike interval in the hippocampal interneurons (Lawrence et al., 2006). Previous studies have also shown that GABAergic neurons display disease phenotypes associated to KCNQ2 disorders (Soh et al., 2018). In Pvalb-Kcnq2-null mice the GABAergic neurons show reduced inhibition due the lack of Kcnq2 channel function that led to hyperactivation of the excitatory neuron networks and increased seizure occurrence (Soh et al., 2018). Other studies have described that GOF KCNQ2/3 can lead to either hypoexcitability or hyperexcitability of pyramidal neurons in mice depending on their location in the brain (Varghese et al., 2023). In the future, these disease phenotypes will be interesting to study also with human iPSC-derived GABAergic neuron models and co-culture platforms in vitro.

In summary, here we discovered novel functional disease phenotypes in the KCNQ2 variant neuronal networks that were shared among different KCNQ2 variant neurons. Specifically, increased burst duration in all three iPSC-derived cortical neurons with KCNQ2 variants compared to their respective controls. Synchrony index and number of network bursts and network burst frequency were significantly increased in L292_L293delinsPF and T274M neurons compared to their controls underlying increased network connectivity in these two KCNQ2 variant lines that was associated with the increased synaptic marker expression. Distinct phenotypes were detected in G256W line, with the increased total neurite outgrowth length that was significantly increased at day 7, 9 and day 13 during neural differentiation, and at day 30 in spike sorted individual neurons. G256W/+ neurons also had increased neuron conduction velocity, and their networks were hyperexcitable upon stimulation, and the KCNQ2 channel enhancer Retigabine rescued this effect. Transcriptional profiling revealed differentially expressed genes and co-expressed genes that were common between cell lines, as well as differentially expressed genes that were unique to each cell line. These different neuronal disease phenotypes are associated with unique genetic variations across these patient lines and may also reflect the variable clinical phenotypes. In addition, these new functional human-derived neuronal network models and disease phenotypes revealed PCDHA-PCDHB-PCDHG gene family as a potential target for drug-screening. In the future, these neuronal networks will facilitate systemic investigation and development of therapeutic interventions for patients with specific pathogenic KCNQ2 variants.

Human iPSCs were differentiated into induced neurogenin2 (iNGN2) cortical neurons using a modified protocol (Zhang et al., 2013). Briefly, human iPSCs were maintained in vitronectin coated plates (1:100 dilution, ThermoFisher, # A14700) and fed with Stemflex medium (ThermoFisher, # A3349401). For iNGN2 differentiation the iPSCs were treated with 8 μg/ml of polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, # TR-1003-G) and transfected with TetO-Ngn2-Puro and FUW-M2rtTA lentiviruses (produced by the Viral Core at Boston Children’s Hospital using plasmids from Addgene: #52047 and #20342 respectively). After transduction, the cells were expanded to generate a master cell bank. Upon starting the differentiation, the cells were expanded to reach about 80 % confluency and incubated with Accutase (Innovative Cell Technologies catalog # AT104–500) at room temperature to achieve single-cell dissociation. Cells were resuspended into Stemflex medium supplemented with 10 μM ROCK-inhibitor Y-27632 (Cayman catalog # 10005583). Cells were seeded at a density of 89,000 cells/cm2 onto Geltrex coated plates (a1:100 dilution, ThermoFisher # A1413301). Stemflex medium was removed 18 h after and switched to “N2 differentiation medium” that consisted of DMEM/F12 (ThermoFisher, # 11320082); 100× N2 supplement (ThermoFisher #17502048) and 100× non-essential amino acids (ThermoFisher, 11140050). N2 medium was supplemented with 2 μg/ml of doxycycline (Clontech, # 631311); 10 ng/ml of BDNF (Peprotech, # 450–02) and NT3 (Peprotech, # 450–03) on day 0. On day 1, a full media exchange was performed with N2 medium supplemented with 2 μg/ml doxycycline and 1 μg/ml puromycin (InVivoGen, catalog # ant-pr-1), 10 ng/ml BDNF and NT3. On day 2, cells were switched to B27-medium that consisted of Neurobasal-A-medium (ThermoFisher # 10888–022); 50× B27 (ThermoFisher catalog # 17504–044) and 100× Glutamax (ThermoFisher # 35050–061). B27-medium was supplemented with 2 μg/ml doxycycline, 1μg/ml puromycin, 10 ng/ml BDNF and NT3. On day 3 a full media exchange was repeated as described on day 2, and included 2 μM Ara-C (Sigma-Aldrich, # C1768). At day 6, cells were dissociated using Papain (Worthington Biochemical, # LK003178) and DNAseI (Worthington Biochemical, # LK003172) and seeded onto 96-well plates for neurite outgrowth assays, 6-well plates for RNA-Seq sample collections, 24-well plates for synaptic marker analysis in co-cultures with human astrocytes (Ncardia), and low density multi-electrode array (LD-MEA) and high-density multi-electrode array (HD-MEA) plates for functional assays in co-cultures with human astrocytes (Ncardia), as described in detail below.

iNGN2 neurons were plated at day 6 of differentiation in 6-well plates pre-coated with 100 μg/ml poly-D-lysine solution in borate buffer (ThermoFisher) and laminin 10 μg/ml (Sigma), at a density of 1.5 × 106 neurons/well (SickKids) or in plates pre-coated with 25 μg/ml poly-ornithine solution in borate buffer and laminin 10μg/ml (BCH). 24 days post-plating, total RNA was extracted from each well using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen). RNA samples were checked on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 RNA Nano chip for RNA integrity (RIN > 7) and quantified using the Qubit RNA HS Assay. RNA library preparation was performed following the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Preparation protocol to generate stranded data. In brief, 200 ng of total RNA was used as the input material and enriched for poly-A mRNA using magnetic oligo d(T) beads, fragmented into the 200–300-bases range for 10 min at 94 °C and converted to double stranded cDNA. cDNA proceeded to library preparation and dual-index Illumina adapters were added through PCR. Final libraries were validated on the Fragment Analyzer using the High Sensitivity NGS Kit or on the Bioanalyzer 2100 DNA High Sensitivity chip (Agilent Technologies) to check for size and absence of primer dimers and quantified by qPCR using Kapa Library Quantification Illumina/ABI Prism Kit protocol (KAPA Biosystems). The quality of the sequences in FASTQ format was assessed using the FastQC (v.0.11.5) tool. Adapter removal and trimming of read ends with a Phred quality score of less than 25 were carried out using Trim Galore (v.0.5.0) and Cutadapt (v.1.10). Trimmed reads less than 40 bp in length were excluded from downstream analysis. The reads were screened for the presence of rRNA and mtRNA sequences using FastQ-Screen (v.0.10.0). RSeQC package (v.2.6.2) was used to assess read distribution, positional read duplication, gene body coverage, junction saturation and to confirm the strandedness of the reads. The reads were aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38.p13) with Gencode v44 annotations using the STAR aligner (v.2.6.0.c). STAR alignments were processed to extract read counts for genes using htseq-count (v.0.6.1p2).

Differential gene expression results were obtained from two separate programs, DESeq2 (v.1.26.0 s) and edgeR (v.3.28.1), using R (v.3.6.1). The surrogate variable analysis (SVA) method, implemented in the sva R package (v3.34.0), was used to adjust for batch effects and identify unknown variables present in the data. Six surrogate variables were discovered and were applied as covariates in the differential expression analysis. Minimal initial filtering of 50 read counts per gene in at least five samples was applied to the dataset. For the DESeq2 analysis, the default median of ratios method was used to generate normalized counts. For the edgeR analyses, read counts were normalized with the scaling factors from the Trimmed means of M-values (TMM) method. The glmLRT functionality in edgeR was used for differential analysis. The resulting p-values from DESeq2 and edgeR results were adjusted using Benjamini and Hochberg’s method for controlling the false-discovery rate (FDR).

The log2 fold changes and adjusted p-values were used to select of differentially expressed genes from DESeq2 and edgeR results. Genes with an adjusted p-value/FDR ofless than 0.05 and fold changegreater than 1.0 were considered significantly expressed.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed as described previously (Zhang and Horvath, 2005). Briefly, a correlation matrix of all expressed genes was calculated and taken to the power of 6 to approximate scale-free topology, resulting in an adjacency matrix. A topological overlap matrix was calculated, and hierarchical clustering was performed. Modules were identified using the dynamic tree-cut algorithm. Module eigengenes (MEs) were determined by obtaining the first principal component of the scaled expression data within a module. ME-based connectivity was determined by calculating the correlation between the expression of a gene and the ME (Dong and Horvath, 2007). Gene ontology overrepresentation analysis (ORA) was performed using a one-sided Fisher exact test with a custom gene-set collection including the gene ontology annotations provided by the R package org.Hs.eg.db (v3.17.0) filtered to include sets with annotated 100–1000 genes, and Reactome and Biocarta pathways (downloaded from their respective websites on 20230818) filtered to include sets with 15–500 annotated genes. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied when noted. An ANOVA test, which included batch as a blocking factor, was used to investigate the relationship between the module eigengenes and either CRISPR status or cell line. 5–7 replicates of each cell line were used for RNA sequencing analysis and has been described in the figure legend for Fig. 3.
