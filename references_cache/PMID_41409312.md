# Identification of Novel and Recurrent FBN1 Gene Mutations in Two Unrelated Turkish Families with Isolated Ectopia Lentis: A Case Report with Insights from a Literature Review.
**Authors:** Heidargholizadeh S, Sharifi S, Palanduz S, Nazemi A
**Journal:** Mol Syndromol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000549172](https://doi.org/10.1159/000549172)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: This study aimed to identify the potential genetic defects underlying familial clustering of lens dislocation in two unrelated Turkish families, consistent with the clinical features of isolated ectopia lentis (IEL). The investigation seeks to determine whether the detected findings overlap with those observed in other syndromic conditions that present with lens dislocation. Additionally, a focused review of IEL within the scientific literature was conducted to contextualize the molecular and phenotypic spectrum associated with lens abnormalities. The results of this study are expected to contribute to a deeper understanding of the molecular basis of IEL and to enhance diagnostic precision, genetic counseling, and clinical management strategies for affected individuals.

CASE PRESENTATION AND CONCLUSION: Comprehensive family histories and clinical evaluations revealed lens dislocation and associated ocular manifestations without systemic abnormalities or extraocular features suggestive of connective tissue disorders. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) in affected individuals identified two heterozygous FBN1 missense variants. The first variant, ENST00000316623.5:c.2920C>T, which has been previously reported in the literature, is located within the TB5 domain and was identified in ten affected members of family 1. The second variant, ENST00000316623.5:c.7018T>C, located within the TB9 domain, was identified in a single affected individual from family 2 and is reported here for the first time in association with IEL. Segregation analysis demonstrated that both variants co-segregated with the ectopia lentis phenotype and were completely absent in unaffected family members as well as in WES data from 200 ophthalmologically normal in-house controls. Besides, our study focused review of the literature on FBN1-associated IEL revealed that approximately 66.7% of reported variants involve missense substitutions affecting cysteine residues. Our study further reinforces this pattern by identifying two rare FBN1 missense variants that co-segregate with the phenotype, thereby expanding the known mutational spectrum of IEL. These findings also underscore the phenotypic heterogeneity of IEL, as reflected by the variable age of onset among affected individuals, and emphasize the critical role of domain-specific cysteine-altering mutations in the pathogenesis of IEL.

Pathogenic variants in FBN1 gene constitute the primary genetic etiology of isolated ectopia lentis (IEL). The majority of IEL-associated variants are missense mutations that disrupt cysteine residues. While certain FBN1 mutations are observed in both IEL and Marfan syndrome, a subset appears to be restricted to IEL, accounting for approximately 11.8% of all reported FBN1 variants. Notably, mutations localized to the first 15 exons of FBN1 are more commonly associated with IEL than with Marfan syndrome.

We identified two FBN1 missense variants associated with IEL, ENST00000316623.5:c.2920C>T and ENST00000316623.5:c.7018T>C, the latter representing a novel IEL-associated mutation previously documented in ClinVar with an uncertain phenotype. Additionally, our literature review demonstrated that IEL-associated pathogenic variants in FBN1 extend beyond exon 15, suggesting an expanded mutational spectrum and highlighting that IEL may involve a broader range of FBN1 disruptions than previously recognized. Beyond this, our results also suggest the presence of a potential novel mutational hotspot specific to IEL. Considering the pleiotropic effects of FBN1 mutations, long-term clinical follow-up is strongly recommended for carriers across all age groups.

Ectopia lentis (EL) is a rare inherited disorder characterized by lens displacement as a result of the congenital deficiency of the suspensory ligament. EL, occurring at 6.4 per 100,000 people in a Danish study [1], can cause blindness due to complications like high myopia, glaucoma, astigmatism, and retinal detachment. EL frequently occurs with cardiovascular, skeletal, and nervous system disorders, commonly in syndromic diseases, but can also present as isolated ectopia lentis (IEL; MIM #129600) [2].

IEL is primarily linked to FBN1 mutations, which encode fibrillin-1 protein. In a study of 175 EL patients, 83.4% of clinically relevant variants were in FBN1 [3]. Despite ∼3,000 identified variants, only a few are associated with IEL, with a reported frequency of 11.8% [4].

These insights are crucial for distinguishing IEL from overlapping FBN1-related syndromes and for emphasizing the importance of early and longitudinal clinical surveillance to detect potential systemic involvement. In this study, whole-exome sequencing (WES) was conducted to investigate the genetic basis of lens dislocation in two Turkish families exhibiting familial clustering. The findings were interpreted in the context of previously reported data to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying pathology.

This study investigated two unrelated Turkish families affected by IEL. Family 1 spanned five generations and included 23 affected individuals (14 males and 9 females). Due to sample availability, genetic analysis was conducted on 10 affected and 3 unaffected members (Fig. 1), whereas family 2 comprised three generations and included two affected individuals; an index case (a male) and his deceased mother. Genetic analysis was performed on the one affected (index case) and three unaffected first-degree relatives of the index case (Fig. 2). The diagnosis of IEL was established in accordance with the revised Ghent criteria. All affected individuals which we analyzed exhibited bilateral crystalline lens subluxation. Among the affected individuals in family 1, additional ocular findings were noted beyond lens dislocation. Specifically, one individual (III-2) presented with astigmatism and myopia, while another (III-4) exhibited glaucoma. In family 2, the index patient (II-2) was diagnosed with cataract in addition to lens dislocation. The age of onset ranged from 8 to 24 years in family 1, while the affected individual in family 2 manifested the phenotype at 50 years of age. The principal presenting symptom in both families was progressive visual deterioration, most commonly blurred vision.

Given the well-established overlap between IEL and Marfan syndrome, all affected individuals underwent detailed systemic evaluation for Marfan-related manifestations. Notably, neither family demonstrated cardiovascular abnormalities such as aortic root dilation, nor skeletal features including arachnodactyly or pectus deformities, excluding systemic connective tissue involvement and confirming the isolated nature of the phenotype.

In view of the clear familial aggregation, an inherited etiology was hypothesized, and WES was performed on the five individuals from the two families. After comprehensive analysis of more than 20,000 genes, FBN1 emerged as the most plausible candidate gene, demonstrating a genotype fully concordant with the observed phenotype.

In family 1, WES identified a heterozygous missense variant (ENST00000316623.5:c.2920C>T) within FBN1, which exhibited complete cosegregation with the disease phenotype and was detected in 10 affected individuals but absent in all unaffected family members, as confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Table 1, Fig. 1). In family 2, WES revealed a heterozygous missense variant, ENST00000316623.5:c.7018T>C, in one affected individual. This variant was likewise absent in unaffected relatives and validated by Sanger sequencing (Table 1, Fig. 2). Both variants were not present in WES data from 200 ophthalmologically confirmed in-house controls, underscoring their rarity and potential pathogenicity.

Computational pathogenicity assessment using multiple in silico meta-predictors and variant annotation databases consistently classified both nucleotide substitutions as deleterious (Supplementary Data; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549172). According to ClinVar, the FBN1 variant identified in family 1 has been previously associated with IEL, familial thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection, and classic Marfan syndrome. Conversely, the variant identified in family 2 had been reported in ClinVar in an individual with an indeterminate phenotype. Its novel identification in a patient with IEL in the present study provides new evidence implicating this FBN1 variant in IEL pathogenesis.

A limited number of studies have described FBN1 variants in patients with IEL [5, 6]. Given the incomplete understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations in IEL, systematic exploration of FBN1 variants remains essential. Our study integrated clinical and genetic data from two Turkish families with an extensive literature-based comparative analysis, underscoring its dual nature as both an original investigation and a comprehensive literature review.

Through this integrative approach, we cataloged 30 pathogenic FBN1 variants previously associated with IEL (Table 2), 18 of which were exclusively linked to IEL. The rarity of IEL cases indicates that FBN1 variants underlying IEL represent a minor fraction of all reported FBN1 mutations [4]. Importantly, the mutational spectrum of IEL is dominated by missense mutations, particularly those involving cysteine residues. Consistent with previous observations, an international cohort of 1,013 individuals with Marfan syndrome harboring FBN1 mutations revealed that cysteine substitutions or creations markedly increase the risk of EL [5]. In agreement with these data, 27 of 30 IEL-associated variants (90%) identified in our review were missense, and 18 (66.7%) specifically affected cysteine residues. These substitutions disrupt disulfide bond formation, destabilize fibrillin-1, and ultimately impair microfibril integrity, thereby providing a molecular basis for lens zonule fragility observed in IEL. Building on this mutational profile, previous studies have proposed a preferential clustering of IEL-associated FBN1 variants within the first 15 exons, in contrast to the more widespread distribution seen in Marfan syndrome (p < 0.005) [7]. However, our comparative analysis, incorporating both familial and literature-derived data, indicates that FBN1 mutations are not confined to this region. We observed pathogenic variants extending beyond exon 15, suggesting an expanded mutational spectrum and highlighting the genetic heterogeneity underlying IEL (Fig. 3). Further supporting this heterogeneity, the variants identified in our two Turkish families are located within the TB5 and TB9 domains of the fibrillin-1 protein, distinct from the exon clustering described above. Alterations within the TB5 domain may disrupt its interaction with heparan sulfate, leading to impaired microfibril assembly and potentially reducing fibrillin-1 secretion or incorporation into the extracellular matrix. Such molecular disturbances can compromise microfibrillar architecture and tissue elasticity, triggering aberrant activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway, a recognized hallmark of IEL pathophysiology [8]. In line with this mechanism, elevated TGF-β levels in the aqueous humor of EL patients have been shown to correlate with the severity of lens dislocation (r2 = 0.379; p = 0.003), reinforcing the pathogenic role of TGF-β and matrix metalloproteinases in IEL [9]. Although the variants identified impair lens zonule integrity, they appear not to affect the extracellular matrix of other organs, explaining the isolated nature of the phenotype. Finally, integrating the molecular, structural, and clinical evidence from both our families and the existing literature demonstrates that EL is not restricted to any specific FBN1 domain. Missense variants affecting cbEGF domains, representing approximately 59% of all EL-associated mutations, show no discernible clinical differences from variants located in other regions of the gene [5]. Collectively, these findings indicate that IEL can arise from mutations dispersed throughout the FBN1 gene, independent of the affected domain, and can manifest in both isolated and syndromic contexts.

The FBN1 variant identified in family 1, along with 12 additional variants summarized in Table 2, has previously been reported in association with both Marfan syndrome and IEL. This observation highlights the pronounced phenotypic heterogeneity of FBN1-related disorders, likely influenced by genetic background, modifier loci, that modulate disease expressivity. This variability is further exemplified by differences in age of onset among affected individuals. In family 1, lens dislocation manifested before the age of 10, consistent with earlier reports [15], whereas in family 2 and several literature cases, the condition manifested at a more advanced age. Such discrepancies underscore the progressive and heterogeneous nature of FBN1-associated EL and emphasize the importance of long-term clinical surveillance for carriers of FBN1 variants across all age groups. Given the potential for subclinical or delayed systemic involvement, early molecular diagnosis remains indispensable for individuals with suggestive ocular findings or a positive family history, facilitating timely monitoring, intervention, and genetic counseling. Collectively, our findings broaden the mutational and phenotypic spectrum of FBN1-associated IEL through the identification of a novel variant, while reinforcing the pathogenic significance of cysteine residue substitutions and domain-specific perturbations in the molecular architecture of fibrillin-1. These insights underscore the value of comprehensive genomic evaluation in IEL families, establishing a foundation for enhanced diagnostic precision, prognostic assessment, and personalized clinical management in FBN1-related connective tissue disorders.
