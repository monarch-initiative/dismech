# DICER1-Related Tumor Predisposition: Identification of At-risk Individuals and Recommended Surveillance Strategies.
**Authors:** Schultz KAP, Nelson AT, Mallinger PHR, Harris AK, Kamihara J, Baldinger S, Chen KS, Pond D, Hatton JN, Dybvik AG, Mitchell SG, Perrino MR, Ben-Ami T, Kachanov D, Su Y, Duan C, Olson DR, Watson D, Field AL, Harney LA, Garrity Carr A, Frazier AL, Schneider DT, Wilson DB, MacFarland SP, Schoettler PJ, Bauer AJ, Dehner LP, Hill DA, Stewart DR, Messinger YH
**Journal:** Clin Cancer Res (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-24-1532](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-24-1532)

## Content

PURPOSE: DICER1-related tumor predisposition increases risk for a spectrum of benign and malignant tumors. In 2018, the International Pleuropulmonary Blastoma (PPB)/DICER1 Registry published guidelines for testing- and imaging-based surveillance of individuals with a known or suspected germline DICER1 pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variant. One of the goals of the Registry is to continue to refine these guidelines as additional data become available.

EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN: Individuals were enrolled in the International PPB/DICER1 Registry, the International Ovarian and Testicular Stromal Tumor Registry, and/or the NCI Natural History of DICER1 Syndrome study.

RESULTS: Review of participant records identified 713 participants with a germline DICER1 P/LP variant from 38 countries. To date, 5 cases of type I and 29 cases of type Ir PPB have been diagnosed by surveillance in enrolled individuals. One hundred and three individuals with a germline P/LP variant developed a primary ovarian Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor at a median age of 14 years (range: 11 months-66 years); 13% were diagnosed before 8 years of age, the current age of onset of pelvic surveillance. Additionally, 4% of Sertoli-Leydig cell tumors were diagnosed before 4 years of age.

CONCLUSIONS: Ongoing data collection highlights the role of lung surveillance in the early detection of PPB and suggests that imaging-based detection and early resection may decrease the risk of advanced PPB. DICER1-related ovarian tumors were detected before 8 years of age, prompting the Registry to recommend earlier initiation of ovarian surveillance with pelvic ultrasound beginning at the time of detection of a germline DICER1 P/LP variant.

DICER1-related tumor predisposition (MONDO: 0100216, OMIM *606241) is an autosomal dominant, pleiotropic disorder caused by heterozygous germline variants in the DICER1 gene. The prevalence of germline DICER1 loss-of-function variants is estimated at approximately 1 in 6,099 to 8,416.[1] DICER1-related tumor predisposition is characterized by increased risk for a spectrum of malignant and benign neoplasms as well as lung cysts, thyroid nodules, and macrocephaly.[2–4] Penetrance is incomplete, and many individuals are entirely healthy or have isolated manifestations. In a study of non-proband DICER1 heterozygotes, the penetrance for a neoplasm diagnosis (excluding lung cysts/type Ir PPB and thyroid nodules) by age 10 years was approximately 5% and by age 50 was approximately 19% (females, 26.5%; males, 10.2%).[5] The most common phenotypes include multinodular goiter and lung cysts; more aggressive neoplasms such as advanced pleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB), rhabdomyosarcoma and aggressive central nervous system (CNS) tumors occur, but are rare.

The DICER1 gene (NM_177438.3) is located on chromosome 14q32.13 and encodes an RNase III-family endonuclease responsible for double-stranded RNA and precursor microRNA processing.[6–8] Dysregulation of microRNAs has a pro-oncogenic effect; thus, DICER1 plays an integral role in tumorigenesis by post-transcriptional microRNA modulation of gene expression of protein-coding genes.[9] Neoplasms arising in the context of DICER1 typically have biallelic pathogenic variants, often a germline loss-of-function variant in one allele and a second, tumor-specific, variant within the RNase IIIb domain (“hotspot” region, e.g. E1705, D1709, D1713, G1809, D1810, and E1813) or, more rarely, the RNase IIIa domain. Although many individuals with a DICER1-related tumor have a predisposing germline loss-of-function pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variant, some DICER1-related tumors are characterized by biallelic pathogenic variants limited to tumor tissue. A small subset of individuals, estimated to account for ~10% of individuals who develop PPB, are found to have somatic mosaicism for a DICER1 variant.[10] Individuals with mosaic DICER1 RNase IIIb missense variants have been described with severe phenotypes.[11, 12] In contrast, individuals with mosaic loss-of-function variants tend to have similar-to-less severe phenotypes compared with individuals with germline heterozygous loss-of-function variants.[10]

Germline loss-of-function variants in DICER1 were first described in 2009 in the study of PPB.[13] The International PPB (now PPB/DICER1) Registry was established in 1987 to study this rare tumor and continues to enroll and longitudinally follow individuals with known germline DICER1 loss-of-function variants and/or known or suspected DICER1-associated neoplasms. The International Ovarian and Testicular Stromal Tumor (OTST) Registry was established in 2011 to further investigate the clinicopathologic and genetic features of sex cord-stromal tumors, including the relationship between DICER1 variation and sex cord-stromal tumors.[14] The National Cancer Institute (NCI) Natural History of DICER1 Syndrome study (NCI DICER1 study) also began in 2011 to study the phenotype of individuals with germline DICER1 P/LP variants.

The International PPB/DICER1 Registry published the first iteration of DICER1 surveillance guidelines and testing recommendations for at-risk individuals in 2018.[15] These guidelines for at-risk individuals have facilitated standardized care and early detection of DICER1-related neoplasms.[16] In 2021, the Host Genome Working Group of the European branch of the International Society of Pediatric Oncology (SIOP-E) and CanGene-CanVar project published a core set of recommendations with extended options for consideration.[17] Herein, we provide an interim report and data-driven update to the prior Registry surveillance guidelines based on 6 additional years of ongoing data collection and evolving knowledge of DICER1-related tumor predisposition.

Individuals were enrolled in the International PPB/DICER1 Registry (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03382158), the International OTST Registry (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01970696) and/or NCI DICER1 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01247597). This study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Study procedures were approved by Children’s Minnesota institutional review board (protocols #1111-112 and #1611-130). Written informed consent (including assent when applicable) was obtained before any study procedures. Individuals with known germline DICER1 P/LP variants were selected for this analysis. Variants were classified by type, with large deletions defined as exon-level deletions. The proband was defined as the individual enrollee through whom each family was ascertained. Non-probands were defined as enrolled family members of the proband found to have the familial DICER1 variant. Individuals with mosaicism for loss-of-function variants or RNase IIIb missense variants were excluded.

For all participants, medical records were reviewed and tumor and/or other DICER1-associated conditions (i.e. thyroid nodules) were systematically abstracted by organ system. Demographics were recorded including biological sex, age at enrollment and follow-up, and self-reported race and ethnicity. Outcomes were ascertained at least annually until participants were lost to follow up. Available pathology was centrally reviewed.

The data generated in this study are not publicly available, in compliance with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, as the data contains potentially identifying information. The data are available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author in a redacted format.

Review of participant records identified 713 participants with a germline DICER1 P/LP variant (574 participants in the International PPB/DICER1 Registry and/or International OTST Registry and an additional, non-overlapping, 139 participants in the NCI DICER1 study database). Median age at enrollment was 9 years (range: 0–77 years), and median age at last known follow-up was 15 years (range: 0–80 years) (Table 1). Median follow-up from date of enrollment was 35 months (range: 0–466 months). Females made up 61% (437/713) of enrollees. The majority (79%, 562/713) of patients are from the United States with 38 countries represented. Most patients (76%, 544/713) self-identified as white/Caucasian and 73% (523/713) identified as non-Hispanic or Latino. Overall, 61% (435/713) of participants were considered probands: 435 unique families were represented in this cohort. There were 118 probands with at least one other relative enrolled. Nonsense variants were the most prevalent (72%, 513/713) reported variant type. A small portion (6%, 41/713) of enrolled participants are deceased. PPB was the most common (54%, 22/41) cause of death.

Among participants with a documented germline DICER1 P/LP variant, 267 cases of PPB (type I=76, type II=73, type III=60, and type Ir=59) were identified (Fig. 1A). Type I PPB was diagnosed primarily in children under the age of 2.5 years with a median age of 8 months (range: 0 months–7.9 years); 95% occurred by the age of 3.1 years. Type II PPB was diagnosed at a median age of 2.8 years (range: 4 months-13.3 years) and type III at a median age of 3.5 years (range: 1.8–11.8 years). Type Ir PPB, which lacks a malignant component, was detected at a median age of 2.4 years with a wide range of age at diagnosis (range: 4 months-24.3 years). One patient was found to have a thoracic Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor (SLCT).[18]

Overall, 5 cases of type I and 29 cases of type Ir PPB have been identified by surveillance and confirmed by pathology in asymptomatic individuals with known germline DICER1 P/LP variant, history of other DICER1-related condition, and/or family history of DICER1-related tumor predisposition. One patient with history of type Ir PPB detected via surveillance was diagnosed with a contralateral type II PPB at 2 years of age, 11 months following their initial type Ir diagnosis. An additional patient was found to have bilateral lung cysts by surveillance imaging at 18 months of age (not resected). Subsequent computed tomography (CT) at 42 months of age showed that the right-sided lung cyst had increased from 9 to 16 mm and the left-sided cyst remained unchanged at 6 mm. Subsequent imaging at 4 years of age showed a 3.5 cm solid lesion at the site of the previously-identified right-sided cyst, resected and centrally reviewed as type III PPB.

DICER1-related renal tumors were found in 73 individuals (cystic nephroma (CN) = 62, Wilms = 7, and anaplastic sarcoma of the kidney (ASK) = 4). The median age at diagnosis of CN was 2.0 years (range: 4 months-15.3 years) with 90% occurring by 5 years of age (Fig. 1B). The ages at diagnosis for each case of ASK were 1, 6, 9, and 23 years. Median age at diagnosis for Wilms tumor was 4.2 years (range: 7 months–11.8 years). Overall, 7 CNs and 1 Wilms tumor were diagnosed by surveillance. For individuals without a history of CN, ASK, Wilms, or other renal tumor, 11% (32/286) had a reported, generally simple, renal cyst on an imaging report (CT, ultrasound (US), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)). Median age of detection, based on first imaging report available, of a renal cyst was 27 years (range: 1–73 years).

Among participants with germline DICER1 P/LP variants, 103 were diagnosed with ovarian SLCT at a median age of 14 years (range: 11 months–65 years) (Fig. 2A). Of these, 11 developed a second primary ovarian SLCT in the contralateral ovary (metachronous) at a median of 45 months (range: 10–156 months) from first SLCT diagnosis. Metachronous SLCT was defined as intrinsic to contralateral ovary and/or differing DICER1 RNase IIIb “hotspots” when “hotspot” testing was available, independent of the time interval between diagnoses. Importantly, 13% of primary SLCTs were diagnosed under the age of 8 years, the current age of initial pelvic surveillance. Additionally, 4% were diagnosed before the age of 4. Four patients with a germline DICER1 P/LP variant and ovarian sarcoma were identified in our cohort with a median age of 7 years (range: 4–10 years); 2 (50%) of these were diagnosed before age 8 (4 and 5 years, respectively). When considering all primary ovarian tumors in patients with germline DICER1 P/LP variants, 15% occurred before 8 years of age. Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS) of the female reproductive tract was identified in 23 participants with a median age of 11 years (range: 4 months-49 years).

When reviewing all individuals enrolled since the publication of the 2018 guidelines, 87 participants with SLCT were identified (43 with confirmed germline DICER1 P/LP variant), 10 of whom had SLCT diagnosed by surveillance imaging prior to symptom onset.[15] Of those, 90% (9/10) were resected as International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage IA; none of these 9 received adjuvant chemotherapy. One (1/10) was found as stage IIA and received adjuvant chemotherapy. When comparing the remaining 77 cases diagnosed due to presenting signs and symptoms, 55% (42/77) were resected as IA, and 44% (34/77, of which 12 were stage IA) received adjuvant chemotherapy. Thus, asymptomatic detection via surveillance resulted in significantly more cases resected as stage IA (90% vs 55%, p=.032) and fewer receiving chemotherapy (10% vs 44%, p=.038). Surveillance also detected lesions at a smaller size. The median largest tumor dimension for those found via surveillance was 6.6 cm (range: 2.4–22.0 cm) compared to 13.0 (range: 1.8–33.0 cm) for those who presented symptomatically (p=.041).

Sixteen cases of primary central nervous system (CNS) tumors were identified (pineoblastoma=7, primary intracranial sarcoma, DICER1-mutant (PIS DICER1)=5, medulloblastoma=2 and undifferentiated round cell spinal tumor and astrocytoma n=1 each). One individual with pineoblastoma was detected asymptomatically after opting for a baseline MRI that demonstrated an ill-defined enhancing pineal region mass. The pineal mass was followed with imaging for 4 years prior to resection and enlarged from 14 mm to 27 mm in largest dimension during that time. The remaining individuals presented with signs and symptoms. These individuals were diagnosed from early childhood through early adulthood, with a median age of 10 years (range: 3–26 years) (Fig. 2B). The undifferentiated round cell spinal tumor had an RNase IIIb “hotspot” variant on tumor testing. Tumor DICER1 testing and central pathology review was unavailable for both medulloblastomas and the astrocytoma.

In total 41 cases of thyroid carcinoma were recorded. Median age of diagnosis was 13 years (range: 6–45 years); 3 cases were diagnosed prior to 8 years of age (Fig. 2B). Most were female (85%, 34/40). Nearly all (95%, 39/41) were differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC). Most (67%, 12/18) presented with multinodular goiter. All cases of DTC with information available (n=36) were American Joint Committee on Cancer stage I. Tumor sequencing identified an RNase IIIb “hotspot” variant in 80% (4/5) of tested tumors

Two individuals with poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC), bothwith a confirmed tumor RNase IIIb “hotspot” variant, were identified in our cohort, diagnosed at 9 and 22 years of age.

Additional tumors seen in the context of DICER1 include ciliary body medulloepithelioma (CBME), nasal chondromesenchymal hamartoma (NCMH), and peritoneal PPB-like sarcoma (Fig. 1B and 2B). Seven patients with CBME were identified in our cohort at a median age of 7 years (range: 4–9 years). NCMH, a benign nasal tumor, was identified in 15 patients with a median age at diagnosis of 10 years (range: 6–25 years). Five patients with peritoneal PPB-like sarcoma were identified in our cohort with a median age at diagnosis of 13 years (range: 3–13 years).

Studies to date suggest that more than 70% of individuals diagnosed with PPB have a germline DICER1 P/LP variant.[10, 16, 19] PPB is characterized by an age-related progression from a multiseptated cystic (type I) to a high-grade multipatterned sarcoma with either a mixed cystic/solid (type II) or purely solid (type III) pattern. The goal of lung surveillance is to detect PPB in its earliest form, type I PPB, which is associated with the most favorable prognosis. Most clinically significant PPB occurs in children under the age of 7. Type I is associated with 3-year event-free survival 93.4% compared to 66.2% for type II and 56.1% for type III PPB.[16, 19] In addition to worse outcomes, disease-specific morbidity is increased in type II and III PPB. Type I PPB is treated with surgery with a subset of individuals, approximately 40%, receiving chemotherapy, whereas type II and III PPB are treated with surgery, intensive chemotherapy and in some cases, radiation therapy.[16, 19]

Not all PPBs progress; regressed PPB (type Ir) is an architecturally multicystic or unilocular cyst that lacks a primitive small cell population that is hypothesized to represent regressed or non-progressed type I PPB. Type Ir PPB has been histologically confirmed in individuals up to age 45 years.[16]

Importantly, only 2 cases of advanced PPB were detected via surveillance in this cohort. The first, a type II PPB, was detected following the resection of a type Ir; the second, a type III PPB progressed from a cystic lung lesion detected by surveillance imaging and observed without surgical intervention. Overall, in most cases, surveillance has facilitated the detection and resection of cystic PPB prior to progression to advanced PPB.

Lung cysts, hypothesized to represent type Ir PPB, were frequently detected by imaging and represent a significant challenge in DICER1 surveillance. Data from a separate cohort showed lung cysts in 39% of individuals with a germline DICER1 P/LP variant whose first chest CT was performed at 12 years of age or older.[20] Further studies of the mechanism of non-progression or regression of DICER1-related lung cysts in childhood are needed to guide judicious surgical intervention and inform decision making for cystic lesions in young children.

Individuals with a germline DICER1 P/LP variant are at risk for the development of CN, ASK, and Wilms tumor. CN, a benign, typically multicystic tumor, is one of the most common manifestations of DICER1-related tumor predisposition. Progression from CN to ASK has been described.[21, 22] The median age of diagnosis of CN is considerably younger than for ASK, 2 years for CN in this series vs. 8.7 years for ASK in the largest reported series.[23] The age differential suggests that CN may develop far in advance of malignant progression. Additionally, a case report has described the initial transition of a CN to ASK in a 7-month-old.[21]

Wilms tumor has been noted in individuals with germline DICER1 P/LP variants; however, Wilms tumor is more commonly non-DICER1 related. The goal of renal screening is early detection to preserve renal function through less extensive surgery, especially in cases of bilateral disease, and to prevent progression to ASK.

Renal cysts have been reported as a feature of Dicer1 mouse models. A prior study of individuals with a germline DICER1 P/LP variant did not detect a significant excess of individuals with renal cysts compared to controls.[24] As simple-appearing renal cysts may not always be mentioned in imaging reports, we expect our reported prevalence to be an underestimate. Although renal cysts are common in other inherited conditions and their prevalence increases with age in the general population, further study is needed to assess renal cyst formation in individuals with germline DICER1 P/LP variants. The finding of otherwise unexplained renal cysts in children and adolescents should prompt consideration of DICER1 testing.

Individuals with germline DICER1 P/LP variants and female reproductive organs are at increased risk for the development of SLCT (including gynandroblastoma) and other gynecologic tumors. Although SLCT is the most common ovarian tumor associated with germline DICER1 P/LP variants, other ovarian tumors including DICER1-related ovarian sarcoma and JGCT have been reported.[25, 26] Additional gynecologic tumors seen in the context of DICER1 include ERMS of the gynecologic tract (including cervix, uterus, and vagina), neuroendocrine tumors, and Müllerian adenosarcomas.[27, 28]

For SLCT, JGCT, and sarcomas, stage at diagnosis impacts both treatment and prognosis.[29, 30] In SLCT, tumor markers are not uniformly elevated at diagnosis, and only 35% present with symptoms of virilization.[29] Therefore, US is more sensitive than biochemical surveillance and should be the primary surveillance method for gynecologic malignancies. Surveillance also informs the surgical approach; pre-operative suspicion of SLCT can guide complete staging based on FIGO criteria and careful resection to avoid rupture, which is associated with higher stage and worse prognosis.[29] We also note that logistical barriers of extended surveillance (time, cost, risk of surveillance fatigue) must be balanced against the potential dramatic impact of diagnosis at stage IA with favorable outcomes and typically decreased need for chemotherapy.

This analysis also demonstrates that beyond the time period of surveillance for recurrence, an individual with a known or suspected germline DICER1 P/LP variant and an ovarian tumor diagnosis should continue gynecologic imaging for detection of metachronous disease.

When an individual presents with a second SLCT diagnosis, it is critical to distinguish metachronous (second primary) from recurrent disease, as prognosis and treatment vary dramatically. Tumor testing on both tumors is recommended, as differing RNase IIIb “hotspot” variants in the tumors will confirm metachronous tumors. Tumors with the same “hotspot” variant, on the other hand, could represent somatic mosaicism, recurrence, or more rarely metachronous tumors.

In DICER1-related tumor predisposition, metastatic type II or III PPB is the most common CNS finding. Primary CNS tumors are rare but seen throughout childhood and early adulthood. Pineoblastoma is a rare primitive neuroectodermal tumor most frequently diagnosed in the first two decades of life, with primary presenting symptoms of vomiting, headaches, and altered mental status.[31] Pineoblastoma pathogenesis diverges from the typical acquisition of a tumor-specific somatic missense RNase IIIb “hotspot” variant since loss of heterozygosity of the wild-type DICER1 allele is the usual somatic event.[31] Pituitary blastoma is a rare tumor of the anterior pituitary that generally occurs in children aged 2 years and younger and presents with Cushing syndrome, ophthalmoplegia, and/or diabetes insipidus.[32] Nearly all cases of pituitary blastoma with documented germline testing have germline DICER1 P/LP variants.[32] PIS DICER1 has been described in multiple series and is primarily diagnosed in the first two decades of life.[33–35] Although some cases of primary PIS DICER1 have been reported in the context of a germline DICER1 P/LP variant, in many cases, DICER1 alteration is confined to the tumor. ETMR-like infantile cerebellar embryonal tumor, a more recently described manifestation of DICER1 germline variation, has been reported in infancy.[36] Medulloblastoma and astrocytoma, both reported in our cohort (although without central pathology review), have not previously been described in the context of DICER1-related tumor predisposition. Tumor testing was unavailable for the respective cases, and it remains unclear if a second, tumor-specific, variant occurred.

The utility of screening in CNS lesions remains unknown, given the rarity and age range at diagnosis. Given that most primary CNS lesions in the context of DICER1-related tumor predisposition occur in the first two decades of life, sedation may be needed for MRI for some patients. It is also unclear if early detection alters prognosis in these rare tumors. For individuals with type II and III PPB, surveillance for CNS metastasis is indicated.

Thyroid nodules and DTC are among the most common manifestations of germline DICER1 variation. Cumulative incidence of multinodular goiter or thyroidectomy is 32% in women and 13% in men by the age of 20, with a 16- to 24-fold increased risk of thyroid cancer compared with National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program over an individual’s lifetime.[4]

The majority of participants had low-risk DTC limited to the thyroid; however, two individuals were diagnosed with PDTC. The low-invasive potential of DICER1-associated DTC has been previously reported.[37–43] PDTC has also been reported in the context of DICER1 with mixed data in regard to poor outcome.[44, 45] PDTC has the potential to be more invasive than DTC, however additional factors such as oncogenic drivers, mitotic figures and extent of angioinvasion and capsular invasion impact outcome.[45, 46] Thyroblastoma, a high-grade embryonal thyroid neoplasm with unique cytological and histological characteristics, has also been described in the context of tumor DICER1 alterations.[47–49]

Current surveillance recommendations include initiation of thyroid US at 8 years of age with continued surveillance every 3 years if no nodules are found on the initial US.[15, 17, 50] Based on the majority of patients having benign thyroid nodules or low-risk invasive DTC, there is ongoing discussion regarding the optimal age to initiate thyroid US, balancing expeditious diagnosis for patients with invasive thyroid neoplasms while limiting invasive procedures for patients with benign disease or indolent DTC. The high rate of indolent DTC highlights the importance of careful attention to sonographic features to determine which nodules should undergo fine needle aspiration with combined use of the nodule number and laterality with cytological data to optimize surgical planning. Ongoing efforts to assess the benefits of early surveillance and better inform DICER1-related thyroid management is ongoing. Clinicians are referred to the currently published pediatric and adult guidelines for further information on management of thyroid disease in children and adults.[15, 17, 51–53] Additionally, as suggested below, germline testing is recommended for individuals with thyroid nodules or carcinoma in which a somatic DICER1 alteration is detected.

Most cases of CBME in the context of DICER1-related tumor predisposition have been reported prior to the age of 10, but a case diagnosed at 18 years of age has been reported.[54, 55] No imaging studies are indicated in asymptomatic individuals, but patients and caregivers should be counseled on the risk for ophthalmologic manifestations of DICER1-related tumor predisposition. Annual dilated ophthalmologic exam with visual acuity screening from 3 years of age to at least 10 years of age is recommended.

In NCMH, most individuals present with signs and symptoms of nasal obstruction. No surveillance imaging is routinely recommended, although patients and caregivers should be counseled on potential symptoms of NCMH. Otolaryngologic evaluation and imaging should be considered for persistent nasal or sinus symptoms.

Peritoneal PPB-like sarcoma has been reported in a small number of individuals, generally arising from the fallopian tube or pelvic sidewall with histologic characteristics parallel to PPB in the lung (type Ir, I, II, and III peritoneal PPB-like sarcoma).[56] To date, there is no indication for separate surveillance for peritoneal PPB-like sarcoma, though abdominal or pelvic US, recommended for surveillance of renal and gynecologic tumors, may theoretically facilitate early detection.

Based on the above data, we recommend modification of our previously published testing criteria to include the conditions listed in Table 2 and provide a testing schema for individuals suspected to have a germline DICER1 P/LP variant due to family history and/or presenting with a known or suspected DICER1-associated neoplasm and/or somatic testing showing a DICER1 variant (Fig. 3).[15] We recommend genetic counseling and consideration of testing for individuals with a history of any of the listed conditions, when feasible. For individuals identified with a germline DICER1 P/LP variant, genetic education, counseling, and testing in first-degree relatives (parents and siblings) are recommended. Further identification of at-risk relatives can be made after determination of maternal versus paternal (or de novo) inheritance, for which cascade genetic education, counseling, and testing may then be indicated. It is estimated that 87% of germline DICER1 P/LP variants are inherited.[10]

Germline DICER1 testing should include deletion/duplication analysis, which can be done together with initial sequencing or after, if initial sequencing is otherwise negative. Additionally, as recent studies have detected deep intronic pathogenic variants, additional sequencing and splicing assays and/or RNA analysis may be indicated if the suspicion of a DICER1 variant is high, but initial testing is uninformative.[57]

Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) present a unique challenge. The ClinGen DICER1 and miRNA-Processing Gene Variant Curation Expert Panel (https://clinicalgenome.org/affiliation/50050/) has developed individualized germline sequence variant curation guidelines that have helped reduce the number of VUS.[58] VUS status should be revisited intermittently. In the context of clinical concern, we recommend consideration of RNA sequencing to aid in determining variant pathogenicity. Concurrent DNA and RNA sequencing has been shown to improve diagnostic yield.[59] RNA sequencing is a helpful tool to measure expression across the transcriptome and connect that information to the variant of interest.

The presence of biallelic somatic (tumor) variants and negative germline testing may indicate no heritable or subsequent risk. However, the possibility of mosaicism should be considered, especially in individuals with multiple manifestations of DICER1-related tumor predisposition. Mosaic DICER1 loss-of-function variants have been described, although they tend to be associated with fewer foci of disease, in accordance with fewer cells at risk for a “second hit.”[10] Individuals with somatic mosaicism for a loss-of-function variant should follow standard germline DICER1 screening recommendations.

Individuals with somatic mosaicism for one of these constitutional mosaic “hotspot” variants have earlier onset of disease and higher frequency of multisite disease, potentially owing to the risk of acquiring a loss-of-function second hit.[11] Testing of tissues from multiple sites may help confirm RNase IIIb mosaicism when the same “hotspot” is identified. Given the incidence of bilateral neoplasms, decisions on resection can be complicated due to the need for organ preservation. At a minimum, individuals with known or suspected mosaicism for an RNase IIIb missense variant should be offered surveillance in concordance with standard germline DICER1 screening recommendations. Expert consultation and individualized decision-making are advised.

Some individuals with negative germline testing may have an underlying DICER1 variant not detected by initial testing. Historically, analysis for large deletions or duplications was not widely available. Additionally, deep intronic variants have more recently been shown to impact splicing and tumor risk.[57] Tumor testing, if available, may help guide additional testing. If tumor testing is not available or only an RNase IIIb “hotspot” variant is detected, further testing is indicated. Prior testing should be reviewed to determine if deletion/duplication testing was done; if not performed previously, deletion/duplication testing should be completed. Intronic testing with splice site and/or RNA analysis should also be considered.

As screening recommendations begin in infancy, testing at-risk individuals soon after birth is recommended. The high likelihood of good outcomes in individuals undergoing surveillance should be considered. Pretest counseling should carefully consider the significant difference between inheritance (50% chance that the child of an individual with a germline DICER1 P/LP variant will inherit the same variant) versus penetrance (low likelihood that an individual with a P/LP variant will develop a manifestation with poor prognosis such as advanced PPB).

The most common manifestations of DICER1-related tumor predisposition are lung cysts and thyroid nodules, which are both associated with a favorable prognosis.[4, 20] More serious manifestations such as advanced PPB, CNS tumors, and advanced ovarian tumors are rare, especially in individuals undergoing surveillance. Surveillance commences at the time of detection of a germline DICER1 P/LP variant for most individuals. Learning/developmental differences are not generally a part of the DICER1 phenotypic spectrum and when present should prompt consideration of additional evaluation.

We recommend third-trimester US with attention to lung and kidneys for women whose fetus(es) are at risk for a loss-of-function germline DICER1 P/LP variant (due to parental germline variant or potential germ cell mosaicism). Pending these results, chest radiograph at birth (prior to return of genetic testing results) may be considered.

Updated surveillance recommendations are displayed in Table 3, with a discussion of updates below. Patient, family, and clinician education remain the cornerstone of surveillance. We highlight the family education document available through the SIOP-E as well as educational resources available from the International PPB/DICER1 Registry and other sources (www.PPBregistry.org).[17]

We recommend standardization of the chest radiograph interval to every 6 months (from every 4–6 months) in accordance with renal US in childhood and pelvic US for individuals with female reproductive organs and in accordance with SIOP-E guidelines.[17] Registry recommendations for chest CT at age 3 months and age 2.5 years, prior to the peak age at diagnosis of types II and III PPB, remain unchanged.

We recommend initiation of gynecologic surveillance at the time of detection of a germline DICER1 P/LP variant. This is earlier than our previously published guidelines that suggested initiation of gynecologic screening between 8 and 10 years of age.[15] This change is based on analysis of the age at diagnosis of gynecologic tumors and the impact of surveillance in facilitating early detection of ovarian tumors.

DICER1-related CNS tumors are rare. Whether to offer CNS surveillance by MRI for asymptomatic individuals remains a subject of ongoing discussion and longitudinal data collection. For now, shared decision-making and family education regarding signs and symptoms and neurologic examination at each available opportunity with urgent MRI for any signs or symptoms of CNS disease are recommended.

Recommendations for thyroid surveillance remain unchanged, however, there are on-going efforts to assess the benefits versus risks of early surveillance well as to identify clinical markers that may be used to delineate invasive tumor types (PDTC) from the more common DTC and benign disease.

When an individual is diagnosed at or above 12 years of age, baseline chest radiograph, abdominal US, thyroid US, and ophthalmologic exam are recommended. If these exams are normal, thyroid imaging continues every 3 years. If thyroid nodules are detected, further evaluation and imaging intervals should follow thyroid association guidelines.[51, 52] Pelvic US for individuals with female reproductive organs is recommended every 6 months until at least age 40. Age- and sex-based cancer screening as recommended by clinical guidelines (e.g., routine age- and family-history-related colon cancer screening) should be considered and is not specifically addressed within these surveillance guidelines.

There are additional tumors, seen more rarely, associated with DICER1-related tumor predisposition that are not discussed.[60] Importantly, these guidelines are not intended to address tumor-specific disease monitoring. Additional and/or more frequent surveillance may be indicated for tumor staging and disease surveillance. For example, an individual with a recent diagnosis of PPB generally requires more frequent chest imaging for disease-specific surveillance than recommended here. These data are also not intended to represent incidence or risk of specific neoplasm development in DICER1-related tumor predisposition, as the cohort is inherently subject to ascertainment bias due to enrollment driven in part by individual or family history of the tumor. For instance, as PPB was a major reason for ascertainment, these data should not be construed to represent the numerical risk for PPB for an individual with germline DICER1 P/LP variant. Also, the compliance of each individual with surveillance recommendations cannot be feasibly determined. Thus, as in nearly all studies of rare cancer predisposition, the denominator of unaffected individuals undergoing surveillance cannot be fully defined. Additionally, this report does not address the number of, or complications of surgical interventions. Also, as the International PPB/DICER1 Registry was founded in 1987, DICER1 testing results are not available for all enrollees; only those with available genetic results were included in this analysis unless explicitly stated. Nonetheless, these guidelines represent data from the largest-ever-assembled cohort of individuals with germline DICER1 P/LP variants. These guidelines present testing and surveillance recommendations based on 40 years of longitudinal data on age-related cancer risk, with refinement of the existing guidelines based on additional data collection over the last 6 years.

In this report, we provide updates following the first published DICER1 testing and surveillance guidelines in 2018.[15] Based on 6 additional years of enrollment and data collection as well as evolving knowledge of DICER1-related tumor predisposition, we present new testing and surveillance guidelines. Importantly, the PPB surveillance recommendations have been shown to detect disease in its most curable form, resulting in reduced morbidity and mortality. In addition, screening of the female reproductive tract has aided in early detection of ovarian tumors, primarily SLCT, and helped guide surgical management. Continued data collection has identified the need for earlier initiation of surveillance for tumors of the female reproductive tract. Ongoing global collaboration with patients, families, clinicians, and researchers is encouraged.
