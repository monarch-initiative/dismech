# Triggering mechanisms of acute thunderstorm asthma: epithelial barrier disruption and immune dysregulation.
**Authors:** Xiao Z, Shi Y, Zhao D, Wang Y, Gu Y
**Journal:** Respir Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12931-026-03532-6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03532-6)

## Content

Thunderstorm asthma (TA) refers to a phenomenon characterized by sudden onset, large-scale outbreaks, and potentially fatal acute exacerbations of asthma. Despite accumulating epidemiological evidence, its cellular and molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Recent studies have proposed a core framework involving "environmental triggers-epithelial barrier damage-immune dysregulation." During thunderstorms, high humidity and strong convection can cause pollen to hydrate and rupture into sub-pollen particles (SPPs) smaller than 2.5 μm, which may further combine with other pollutants such as particulate matter ≤ 2.5 μm(PM2.5) and ozone (O3) to form bioaerosols capable of penetrating small airways. These factors can lead to damage of the airway epithelial barrier, with sequential cellular and molecular pathophysiological changes including downregulation of various tight junction proteins in the epithelial barrier, imbalance of mucociliary clearance function, and upregulated secretion of epithelial alarmins such as interleukin (IL)-25, IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP). This results in increased disease severity through activation of innate and adaptive immunity (e.g., type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2)/T helper 2(Th2) axis activation leading to immunoglobulin E(IgE) upregulation, eosinophil activation, and mast cell degranulation; Th17-mediated neutrophilic inflammation; and toll-like receptor(TLR)-mediated innate immune processes and mucosal inflammation) and enhancement of intrinsic susceptibility factors (e.g., TLR gene polymorphisms and abnormal expression, DNA methylation and histone modifications, as well as microbiome-host interactions). According to research in meteorology, exposomics, and molecular immunology, we believe that airway epithelial barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation play significant roles in TA. Future translational directions primarily involve establishing a population stratification and early warning system through combinations of meteorological factors with allergens/pollutants, thereby enhancing public protection and health management efforts to improve the early warning, prevention, and clinical management of TA.

Bronchial asthma (asthma) is a chronic respiratory disease and a major global public health challenge. It affects approximately 300 million people worldwide and is associated with around 1,000 daily deaths, predominantly in low- and middle-income countries where many cases are preventable. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Strategy for Asthma Management and Prevention 2025, while asthma prevalence has plateaued in some regions, climate change may further increase both its prevalence and disease burden [1,2,3,4]. In recent years, the phenomenon of acute asthma exacerbations induced by specific meteorological conditions, commonly referred to as “TA”, has attracted significant attention within the medical community [5,6]. The 2016 TA event in Melbourne, Australia, shows the possible risks of such events. Within 30 h of the storm, local emergency departments saw a big increase of 3,365 visits (a 672% rise) due to breathing problems, including 476 more asthma admissions (a 992% rise), leading to 10 deaths [7]. This event shows how quickly TA can start, how it affects many people, and how it can be deadly.

Current studies have shown that the unique meteorological conditions during thunderstorms, such as high humidity and downdrafts, can cause pollen grains in the air to rupture, releasing a large number of submicron-sized (< 2.5 μm) allergens that can penetrate deep into the lower respiratory tract. This may induce airway epithelial cells to release pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-8, impair the function of the airway epithelial barrier, and increase the risk of allergic reactions [7,8,9]. Although the epidemiological characteristics of TA and its associations with meteorological parameters, bioaerosols (pollen, fungal spores), and pollutant concentrations have been preliminarily elucidated [10,11,12,13], the core mechanistic network of how it precisely drives airway epithelial barrier dysfunction (including tight junction disruption, abnormal mucociliary clearance, and alarmin release) and the subsequent systemic immune dysregulation cascade (such as elevated IgE, altered macrophage polarization, and Th2/Th17 immune imbalance) [14,15,16,17,18,19] remains to be elucidated.

On one hand, during thunderstorm events, pollen, fungal spores, and air pollutants (such as PM2.5and O₃) often exhibit synchronous changes [11,12]. The mechanisms by which the potential additive, synergistic, or antagonistic effects among these factors collectively act on airway epithelial cells and immune cells, thereby synergistically driving the acute exacerbation of asthma, remain unclear. On the other hand, the molecular basis of individual susceptibility is central to understanding the selectivity of TA: this involves the complex interplay between genetically predisposing genes (such as polymorphisms in the GSDMB/ORMDL3 locus at 17q21 and epithelial barrier-related genes FLG/SPINK5), epigenetic regulation (such as dynamic changes in DNA methylation), and airway microbial dysbiosis (such as decreased microbial diversity and dysregulation of short-chain fatty acid(SCFAs) metabolism) with environmental exposures, the specific mechanisms of which still require in-depth exploration [20,21,22,23,24,25]. This has severely hindered the construction of precise risk prediction models based on the pathophysiological mechanisms of TA, as well as the development of novel preventive and therapeutic strategies targeting key cascade steps.

In light of this, this review systematically integrates the latest evidence from meteorology, immunology, respiratory medicine, and molecular biology. We anchor our synthesis in the expanded epithelial barrier hypothesis proposed by Akdis et al. [11,187]. This hypothesis posits that the rise in epithelial barrier-damaging agents linked to industrialization, urbanization, and modern life underlies the global increase in allergic and autoimmune diseases, thereby providing a critical theoretical lens through which to view TA pathogenesis. The framework identifies the airway epithelial barrier as a critical interface that mediates crosstalk between environmental triggers and the immune system, where exogenous stimuli (e.g., allergens, pollutants) disrupt epithelial integrity to initiate inflammatory cascades. While this offers a foundational understanding of barrier–immune interactions in allergic disease, it does not delineate the specific molecular and cellular cascades unique to TA—particularly how thunderstorm-specific environmental co-exposures (e.g., hydrous pollen-derived sub-pollen particles, PM2.5, O3bioaerosols) drive epithelial barrier dysfunction (tight-junction disruption, mucociliary impairment, alarmin release) and subsequent systemic immune dysregulation.

To address this gap, we propose and elaborate a “environmental trigger–epithelial barrier disruption–immune dysregulation” tripartite pathophysiological framework (Fig.1). This framework refines and extends Akdis’s theory by delineating TA‑specific trigger‑barrier‑immune interaction pathways. Our aim is to provide a solid theoretical foundation for a comprehensive understanding of TA—a unique and severe clinical phenotype—and to promote the development of more effective public health interventions, individualized management strategies, and ultimately, alleviation of the health burden of this climate‑sensitive disease.

Pathophysiological framework of TA

The definition of TA is the occurrence of various manifestations such as acute asthma attacks or bronchospasm immediately after a thunderstorm [26]. Globally, the reporting frequency of TA events is on the rise, especially in regions experiencing significant climate change. Analysis of events recorded worldwide since 1983 (summarized in Supplementary Table S1) delineates its core characteristics: occurrences primarily during high pollen/spore seasons, affecting individuals often with seasonal allergic rhinitis (SAR), and triggered by specific meteorological shifts (e.g., sudden increases in humidity, temperature drops, and strong winds) frequently synergized by air pollutants.

Our synthesis of the registry yields key epidemiological insights:

Geographic and climatic patterningreveals that severe outbreaks occur where high pollen seasons overlap with convective thunderstorms (e.g., Melbourne, Ahvaz, Hohhot). Trigger profiles vary by climate: temperate zones with grass pollen (e.g., ryegrass) are linked to IgE-mediated type 2 inflammation (T2), while arid zones with drought-tolerant pollens (e.g.,Artemisiain Hohhot [49],Salsolain the Middle East) and dust/pollutant co-exposures may involve mixed or non-T2 phenotypes, a hypothesis requiring future biomarker validation.

Temporal trendsshow a clear increase in both the frequency and scale of TA events since the 2000 s (8 major events in 1983–1999 vs. 17 in 2000–2024), a progression that parallels climate change-driven increases in extreme convective weather, extended pollen seasons, and enhanced pollen allergenicity.

High-risk populationsare clearly delineated. TA is more pronounced in individuals with SAR, who constituted up to 87% of emergency presentations in Melbourne (2016) and 79.35% in Hohhot [49,51]. Epidemiological studies quantify the acute risk: thunderstorms increase asthma attack risk by 1.24 times (95% CI 1.13–1.36) [52], with each 1 g/m2/s increase in daily precipitation raising children’s hospital visit risk by 27.0% (RR = 1.270, 95% CI: 1.056, 1.527), and each 1 °C temperature drop increasing the risk among adults under 65 by 1.4% (RR = 1.014, 95% CI: 0.998, 1.029) [11]. The elderly do not show the same elevated acute risk, highlighting the need for age-targeted interventions.

Management and susceptibility implicationsemerge from these patterns. Despite wider implementation of grass pollen allergen immunotherapy (AIT) in high-income countries, severe outbreaks persist, often among untreated or inadequately managed individuals. This suggests that the acute, high-dose exposure to sub-pollen particles during a storm may exceed the protective threshold of conventional AIT. Furthermore, the geographic clustering of severe events in certain ethnic groups (e.g., individuals of Asian/Indian ethnicity in Melbourne 2016) indirectly underscores a role for host genetic factors in susceptibility.

In conclusion, the synthesis of global TA data indicates that: (1) risk peaks at the intersection of pollen seasons and convective storms, with trigger profiles varying by climate zone; (2) the increasing trend aligns with climate change; (3) AIT alone is insufficient, necessitating integrated environmental and pharmacological strategies; and (4) climatic factors, regional aeroallergens, and host susceptibility jointly shape TA risk and phenotype, calling for a precision public health approach.

Fluctuations in weather conditions, such as temperature, humidity and air pressure, influence asthmatic attacks. According to a review study in Hong Kong, China covering 22 years (1998–2019), the drop in ambient temperature to the 5th percentile (14.6 °C) from the median (24.8 °C), correlated with an increase in the risk for hospitalization of asthma patients (cumulative relative risk ARR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.05–1.15). On the other hand, asthma hospitalization rates didn’t show any significant relationship with relative humidity or rainfall, the research found [53].

A recent study [54] found that PM2.5and PM10both had a positive link with asthma hospital visits (r = 0.205 and 0.217,P< 0.05), especially in winter. Humidity and rain affect breathing health in an indirect way because they change how pollutants in the air spread and settle. In summer, heat and humidity together, shown by the Humidex, had a strong link with asthma hospital visits (R2= 0.956). Also, the model included wind speed, and it showed that some health effects appear later after the weather changes. These results show that weather and pollution—like temperature, humidity, wind speed, rain, PM2.5, and PM10—play a big part in how asthma develops. So, we should watch these factors and use them in early warning systems, and this can help medical care and public health.

TA is a phenomenon of acute exacerbation that is strongly influenced by specific meteorological conditions, with significant differences in its frequency and severity between urban and rural areas. Urban areas are generally at higher risk for TA due to factors such as population density, elevated air pollution levels, and higher concentrations of airborne allergens. In contrast, rural regions, with their greater microbial diversity and more frequent exposure to animals, which help mitigate the risk of allergies and asthma, have seen an increased asthma burden among urban migrants due to rural-to-urban migration [55].

During crop harvesting seasons in rural areas, significant amounts of pollen and other biological allergens are released into the air. For individuals with allergic asthma living in these regions, thunderstorms can trigger severe asthma attacks, particularly when they coincide with the pollen season [56]. Consequently, while the risk of TA varies between urban and rural areas, both settings necessitate increased awareness and proactive measures to address the health impacts of thunderstorm events.

During thunderstorms, both the composition and morphology of airborne pollen particles undergo significant alterations. Research has demonstrated [9,57] that thunderstorms induce the rupture of larger pollen particles (typically 10–100 μm in diameter) due to convective processes, including high humidity, strong winds, and electric fields, resulting in the release of substantial amounts of sub-pollen particles (SPPs) with diameters less than 2.5 μm. These smaller particles, typically at the micron scale, can more easily penetrate deeper into the human airways, thereby increasing the risk of acute TA attacks.

A study of the 2016 Melbourne thunderstorm [58] revealed a 250% increase in the number of ruptured grass pollen particles during the event, strongly correlated with abrupt climatic conditions, relative humidity, and drastic temperature fluctuations. The physical rupture of pollen grains released substantial amounts of bioactive SPPs, which can penetrate deeply into the lungs, significantly exacerbating asthma symptoms by triggering intense IgE-mediated immune responses.

Thunderstorm conditions also induce significant alterations in the concentration and composition of airborne particulate matter (PM), further exacerbating airway irritation and inflammatory responses. Synergistic interactions between pollutants and fragmented pollen can compromise epithelial barrier integrity and enhance allergen sensitization, significantly increasing the risk of acute asthma attacks [188]. During such events, the levels of chemical tracers linked to pollen and fungal spores—such as fructose and mannitol—significantly increase in PM2.5. These changes are closely tied to meteorological factors, including rainfall, humidity, and temperature [57]. Consequently, the shifts in PM concentration and composition during thunderstorms pose substantial challenges for asthma patients, underscoring the need for clinicians to account for these environmental variables in asthma management.

The airway epithelium, a complex multi-layered structure, serves as the first line of defense in the respiratory tract.Airway barrier function is dependent on the tight junctions (TJs)’ integrity. Tight junction proteins can be influenced and altered due to the effect of airborne particles and allergens brought upon by thunderstorms. These also alter the intracellular signal transduction pathways. For instance, proteases originating from environmental allergens, in particular, house dust mite proteases have been shown to cleave tight junction proteins, notably occludin and claudins, directly [59]. Studies employing in vitro models, such as the Calu-3 airway epithelial cell line, have demonstrated that proteases present in pollen can degrade tight junction proteins, including epithelial (E)-cadherin, claudin-1, occludin, and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1). This degradation compromises epithelial barrier integrity, increases its permeability, and may thereby facilitate allergen penetration into the submucosa and subsequent interaction with immune cells [14].

Moreover, during thunderstorms, epidermal cells are likely to release various alarmins like IL-25, IL-33 and TSLP. These agents may induce type 2 immune responses by triggering dendritic cells (DCs) and type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) and differentiating Th2 cells and eosinophilic infiltration, thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses in the airways. This process shows that the environment can make an acute asthma attack worse [60,61]. This occurs via disruption of epithelial barrier function and dysregulation of immune responses.

As airway epithelial damage advances, the impairment of barrier function allows more external irritants, such as allergens, pollutants, and pathogens, to submucosa infiltrate [60,62]. There can be immune responses from such invasions which leads to re-infections and worsening airway inflammation which is a vicious circle. Repeated bouts of exacerbation can result in not only acute exacerbations but also a persistent long-term airway remodeling which worsening the disease severity in patients who have asthma. Air pollution, such as PM, has been shown to lower the levels of tight junction proteins, leading to epithelial barrier dysfunction and increased inflammatory response [63]. Therefore, concentrating on the protection and repair of the airway epithelium which releases pathways for future immune activation and inflammatory processes may be an essential approach to TA management.

The mechanism behind mucociliary clearance is necessary for the maintenance of the airway. Impairment of this may lead to various respiratory illnesses. People who suffer from TA usually have strange mucus production due to the malfunction of airway epithelial cells. Research shows that during asthma this epithelial cell makes a lot of mucus. However, the level of mucin MUC5AC increases significantly (the healthy airway makes mostly MUC5B, asthma suffers show a shift from MUC5B to MUC5AC) [64]. An increase in mucin production is closely linked to the Th2-type inflammatory responses, especially mediated by IL-13 along with other Th2-type cytokines. The IL-13 treatment attracts goblet cell hyperplasia through the STAT6-SPDEF pathway, resulting in mucus plugs and airflow obstruction [65].

The irregular secretion of mucus activates many signaling pathways at the molecular level. Studies have shown [66] that higher levels of calcium ions in the cell can boost reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which can also strengthen ROS-related pathways of PM2.5-induced mucus secretion. The cause of this is the presence of ions in PM2.5 which disrupt the calcium balance and elevate the generation of ROS. Also, upregulation of the transcription factor NF-κB directly aids overproduction of MUC5AC PM2.5 activates the NF-kB pathway via the IRE1a/NOD1 pathway, leading to its translocation into the nucleus, which in turn promotes transcription of MUC5AC mRNA and its protein secretion. The beneficial development of excess airway mucus occurrence is an essential component of pathology. This emphasizes the requirement for regulating the secretion of mucus which happens in TA.

The mucociliary clearance is essential with the right function of the cilia. Researchers have demonstrated [67] that dysfunction of cilia, either primary or secondary, can lead to antigen accumulation in the airway epithelium. The accumulation activates pro-oxidative enzymes like DUOX1 and releases IL-33 which induces damage and inflammation in the airways. By preventing the clearance of antigens and increasing oxidative stress as well as airway inflammation, this may contribute to asthma development. Also, impaired cilia function is associated with increased mucus secretion and changes to the structure of the airway epithelium.

Patients suffering from asthma have noticeable changes in the structure and function of cilia of their airway epithelial cells. The fine particulates in the air, especially PM2.5, which remain in the air after thunderstorms, may alter the movement of airway ciliary axonemes by affecting cAMP-PKA signaling and increasing the chances of respiratory infections. Laboratory studies have proven [68] that PM2.5can affect the ciliary beat frequency (CBF) of the bovine airway cells depending on concentration and time. A brief exposure to low levels (6.25–25.25 μg/mL) for a short time (10 min) causes the CBF to increase by as much as 10% (4.09 ± 0.08 Hz at 12.5 μg/mL) via cAMP-PKA activation. Conversely, short exposure at high concentrations (100 μg/mL) or any concentration (6.25–100.25 μg/mL) for prolonged exposure (1 h) lead to decrease in CBF (all levels below control 3.75 ± 0.10 Hz,p< 0.05) by damaging mechanisms of axonemal structure such as loss of outer dynein arms and reduced activity of signaling pathway. When CBF is disrupted, it impairs mucociliary clearance, which can contribute to respiratory diseases.

When CBF changes, it may take more effort to get mucus out. This could worsen breathing problems. Therapies that improve this process such, as drugs that boost ciliary activity, might offer new ways to treat asthma.

The release of epithelial alarmins (IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP) is a thunderstorm-specific pathological hub that bridges environmental triggers and acute asthma exacerbations. Unlike general asthma—where alarmin release is often driven by chronic allergen exposure—TA is characterized by rapid, storm-induced activation of airway epithelial cells by synergistic environmental stimuli: hydrous sub-pollen particles (SPPs < 2.5 μm), PM10, O3, and fungal spores [9,57,58]. IL-25, IL-33 and TSLP together can cause asthma-related type 2 inflammation indirectly and directly[69,70,71,72]. They can bind to type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) alone or in combination, activating them and inducing production of type 2 cytokines like IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 (for example, IL-33 promotes ILC2 function by enhancing expression of OX40L/PD-L1). Not just Th2, but ILC2s also produce these cytokines that participate in the class switching of IgE in B cells. Aside from this, TSLP also activates DCs to produce OX40L, which aids in differentiating naïve T cells into Th2 cells. The IL-33 factor directly stimulates cytokine production by CD4 + T cells. The factor IL-25 enhances the potential of Th2 memory cells to release cytokines. The targeting of these three alarmins decreases allergic airway inflammation substantially, further supporting their important involvement in the inflammation seen in asthma.

ILC2s are crucial in the development of allergic disorders such as asthma, as their activation is closely associated with the release of alarmins. The activation of these immune cells is particularly relevant in TA, where it is potently driven by alarmins secreted by the airway epithelial cells in response to thunderstorm factors, like IL-25 or IL-33 and TSLP [69,73]: In particular, IL-33 acts on ILC2s through the ST2 receptor, further amplifying OX40L/PD-L1 expression,and directly activates ILC2s, promoting substantial IL-5 and IL-13 release. On the other hand, IL-25 promotes the production of chemokines by ILC2s and, when combined with IL-2, stimulates their proliferation. TSLP activates ILC2s and enhances their activity via DCs through TSLPR-IL-7Rα complex. These alarmins collectively compel Th2 cells to create IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 which causes the Th2 inflammation to amplify (IL-4). This will attract eosinophils (IL-5). This will increase mucus production and raise airway sensitivity (IL-13). This will worsen the process of airway inflammation and allergies.

Environmental influences associated with thunderstorms play an essential role in inducing asthma attacks due to dysfunction of the airway epithelial barrier. This involves three interconnected processes: loss of TJs, failure of mucociliary clearance and secretion of alarmins from epithelial cells (IL-33, TSLP and IL-25). Mechanisms of airway epithelial barrier dysfunction induced by thunderstorm-associated factors

Thus, the storm-induced surge in epithelial alarmins serves as a pivotal link between environmental exposure and the acute, widespread type 2 immune activation characteristic of TA outbreaks. The impairment of the airway epithelial barrier and alarmin release connect environmental triggers to airway immune inflammation. The process starts a chain reaction that can disrupt the immune response, mainly resulting in the Th2 response. This kind of response will cause increased IgE levels along with eosinophils infiltration that finally leads the asthma attacks to become severe.

During thunderstorms, pollen grains can rupture because of osmotic pressure. This causes the release of allergenic particles which are small enough to be inhaled. A person can suffer a pronounced IgE-mediated allergic response when people inhale high concentrations of these allergens [74]. Critically, thunderstorms generate a high-concentration, acute exposure to inhalable SPPs, PM, O3, nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and fungal spores [9,57,58], a distinct exposure scenario central to TA pathogenesis. The process involves the activation of B cells by the allergens. The class switch to produce IgE antibodies for the allergens. The activation of B cells and production of IgE antibody are modulated by immune regulatory factors such as progranulin (PGRN) that inhibits B-cell activation and IgE production through hindering IFITM3-STAT1 signaling pathway [75,76]. On the other hand, over-activation of this pathway may increase IgE responses. This may explain the higher IgE levels seen in TA episodes, which are likely driven by the intense allergen load characteristic of these events.

In the acute setting of TA, the storm-induced surge in allergen-specific IgE is considered a central driver of the attack. The elevated levels of allergen-specific IgE mediate acute symptoms through several key, interrelated mechanisms. The IgE antibodies are mainly responsible for the acute attacks of asthma via binding to high-affinity IgE receptors (FcεRI) on mast cells and basophils, leading to cell activation and release of inflammatory mediators upon exposure to thunderstorm-generated allergens [76]. The high density of thunderstorm-generated aerosols, such as SPPs, other pollen fragments, and fungal spores, would be expected to promote widespread cross-linking of IgE receptors, providing a mechanistic basis for the rapid onset and severity of acute bronchospasm in TA.

The process of type 2 inflammatory response is characterized by increased production of cell types such as eosinophils in the airways. This immediate release of inflammatory mediators is central to the acute bronchospasm characteristic of TA. The infiltration and activation of these eosinophils are largely independent of direct IgE-mediated activation, but are further facilitated indirectly by IgE—specifically, IgE-driven inflammation promotes the production of type 2 cytokines such as IL-5, which is the key mediator of eosinophil recruitment and activation [76]. Thus, the intense early-phase, IgE-mediated reaction in TA can initiate a robust inflammatory cascade, driving significant eosinophilic airway inflammation. Ultimately, these cells will continuously release leukotrienes that will worsen airway inflammation and cause epithelial damage.

Moreover, IgE has a direct effect on the airway epithelium (through its interaction with FcεRI/CD23 receptors or modulation of alarmins such as TSLP) as well as an indirect one (activation of mast cells and basophils to release inflammatory mediators and recruit eosinophils) [76,77]. In the context of TA, these IgE-mediated actions synergize with the initial storm-induced epithelial injury caused by SPPs [9], promote goblet cell hyperplasia (with resultant mucus overproduction), and induce airway smooth muscle cell contraction [76,77]. This combination of immune and direct environmental insults likely contributes to the profound epithelial barrier disruption and severe clinical presentation of TA. Collectively, this cascade of IgE-driven events, superimposed on the direct epithelial insult from storm aerosols, culminates in the severe acute asthma attacks typifying TA.

The innate immune system is the bodily defense mechanism against foreign invaders and harmful microorganisms. TA flare-ups occur due to the inappropriate activation of the respiratory center. The unique conditions of a thunderstorm, including strong convection, humidity shifts, and electrical activity, dramatically alter the bioaerosol profile. PM from dust storms and other thunderstorm-related weather events lead to an influx of pollen, microbials and aerosols to the lungs. These particles are sensed by airway epithelial and immune cells, which mount an innate immune response causing airway inflammation and asthma attacks. Macrophages and DCs are essential components of the innate immune response that contribute to airway inflammation during thunderstorms.

This storm-specific innate immune activation is expected to drive profound inflammatory changes. Indirect evidence for the scale of such activation comes from children with acute asthma exacerbations, who show increased expression of innate immunity-related genes in peripheral blood [78]. Of the 852 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified in peripheral blood of children with acute asthma exacerbation, 648 are upregulated. These upregulated DEGs are predominantly enriched in innate immune-related pathways, including neutrophil degranulation, neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) signaling, phagosome formation, IL-1 family signaling, toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, and pyroptosis. The pathways associated with neutrophil degranulation and IL-1/TLR signaling indicate an increase in inflammatory activity, while the increase in phagosome formation pathway suggests greater cellular migration. The activation of these innate immune pathways can enhance macrophage activation (a process that depends on TLR/IL-1R signaling and pyroptosis-related inflammatory effects) and facilitate the maturation of DCs (also TLR-dependent) [78].

In the context of TA, TLRs are critically engaged by the complex mixture of storm-generated aerosols. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) act as pattern recognition receptors in the innate immune system and help in quick detection and signaling of contextual components like microbes and particulate materials [79,80]. Bacterial lipoproteins, the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria, CpG DNA and PM are airborne microbial constituents. They can activate TLR during thunderstorms. Subtypes like TLR2 which distinguishes bacterial lipoproteins, TLR4 which recognizes LPS and TLR9 which identifies CpG DNA all exhibit this behavior [81,82,83]. The TLRs start the downstream signaling pathways, MyD88-dependent pathway activates NF-κB, which induces the release of several inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 [81,82,84].

The TLR activation that occurs impacts local inflammatory processes in the airways, and also influences dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation, which contribute to the initiation of adaptive immune responses [79]. People with asthma frequently have TLR polymorphisms and irregular expressions, so it is worth mentioning here. The genetic causes of an innate immune imbalance may interact with the environmental conditions associated with thunderstorms, including the dispersal of allergens and microbes, to significantly drive thunderstorms trigger acute asthma attacks [85,86]. This gene-environment interaction may be pivotal in determining individual susceptibility to TA. Intriguingly, while geographic variations in TLR polymorphisms are well-documented and influence susceptibility to other inflammatory diseases[189,190] [13,14], direct evidence linking these genetic variations to asthma or TA remains scarce. It is plausible that region-specific environmental exposures (e.g., endemic microbial flora and dominant allergen profiles) interact with TLR polymorphisms to modulate TA risk, and this hypothesis warrants validation through cross-regional genetic epidemiology studies.

Mouse studies have demonstrated that TLR4 gene polymorphism amplifies inflammatory responses [86]. This mechanism may be particularly relevant in the context of TA, where thunderstorms facilitate the dispersal of both allergens and Gram-negative bacteria carrying LPS. TLR4 D298G/N397I variant mice (analogous to human D299G/rs4986791 and T399I/rs4986790) are reported to have an exaggerated inflammatory response to house dust mite exposure. The eosinophilia in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) reached 72%, compared to 30% in wild-type mice (p< 0.0001). Furthermore, the HDM-specific IgG1 increased 2.4-fold while the response to LPS was attenuated, and IL-4 was increased by 50%. Thunderstorms improve airflow, which facilitates the dispersal of allergens (dust mites, fungi, etc.) and increases concentrations of Gram-negative bacteria (which contain LPS). Consequently, those with a TLR4 polymorphism may have an amplified inflammatory response to these environmental stimuli. The abnormal expression of TLR5 and TLR7 impairs the defense mechanisms in human airways. Studies indicate [85,86] that in patients with severe asthma, there is a 40% reduction of TLR5 expression in the central airways. There is also a 60% decrease in the total TLR7 expression (p< 0.001). This impairment leads to a reduced clearance of bacterial flagellin (TLR5 ligand) and viral single-stranded RNA (TLR7 ligand). Besides, TLR7-mediated inhibition of Th2 inflammation is weakened. Rain and wind during thunderstorms can help distribute various bacteria and viruses through the air. Patients with lower TLR5/7 levels face difficulties in clearing inhaled pathogens, thereby increasing their airway inflammation. Thus, in TA, pre-existing TLR dysregulation may compromise host defense against storm-dispersed microbes, further exacerbating acute inflammation and TA exacerbations.

The pathophysiology of TA may involve dysregulation of immune responses relevant to Th2 and Th17 pathways, with storm-specific environmental factors potentially exacerbating these imbalances to drive disease severity. Th2 and Th17 cells are essential in developing allergy diseases like asthma. During thunderstorms, there is an enhanced Th2 cell-mediated allergic inflammatory response. During these events, air currents break pollen into PM2.5size particles, which aggregate with dust and other pollutants like PM2.5and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) to activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) in airway epithelial cells [87]. This stimulation causes the epithelium to release pro-inflammatory substances such as TSLP and IL-33. The response depends on the activation of AhR by house dust mite allergen Dermatophagoides farinae (Der f) and benzo(a)pyrene (B(a)P) in human bronchial epithelial cells [87]. It is important to note that Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (Der p) is another major and clinically relevant HDM allergen. Moreover, its core components, Der p 1 and Der p 2, constitute globally prevalent triggers for allergic asthma and rhinitis, alongside Der f [191][15]. Thus, the storm-driven co-exposure to both allergen-derived and pollutant-derived AhR ligands could create a synergistic impetus for epithelial alarmin release in TA. Pollutants reacting during thunderstorms aggravate the situation and lead to the shedding of pro-inflammatory substances; these agents drive the differentiation of naïve T cells into Th2 cells which then produce IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 cytokines. IL-5 is very effective in attracting and activating eosinophils [55,87]. Eosinophils comprised 72% of the BALF of mice exposed to dust mites and B(a)P. The percentage was a mere 30% in wild-type mice, which led to more intense airway inflammation [87].

The pathophysiology of TA may additionally involve dysregulation of the Th17 arm of immunity. This consideration arises because Th17 responses are often associated with severe and steroid-resistant asthma phenotypes. The AhR promotes the formation of Th17 cells by inhibiting Stat1 signaling to enhance Th17 differentiation [87]. By extension, the AhR-activating pollutants present in thunderstorm aerosols could concurrently favor Th17 differentiation. PM and pollution during thunderstorms affect airway epithelial barrier via AhR which may be further impaired by vigorous winds thus disrupting barrier integrity. The situation triggers more entry of storm-borne allergens, which constantly activate the Th2 inflammatory pathway. Collectively, this leads to a detrimental cycle of barrier damage, increased exposure to the allergen, Th2 activation, and further-enhanced inflammation [55,87]. In TA, this cycle may be initiated and accelerated by the acute, high-intensity exposure to storm-derived particulates and pollutants. Understanding this imbalance, particularly how it is triggered by thunderstorm-specific co-exposures, is therefore key to developing targeted therapeutic strategies for TA.

Acute asthma attacks brought on by thunderstorms have largely been attributed in the scientific literature to Th2 cells and type 2 inflammatory responses driven by ILC2. Still, very few researchers carried out work on immune profiles specifically associated with TA. Research on asthma more generally indicates that smokers, obese patients with asthma and patients with moderate-to-severe glucocorticoid-resistant asthma are more likely to have non-type 2 features[88-91]. The phenotype involved is usually associated with an inflammation dominated by neutrophils, activation of the IL-17/IL-6 pathway, and involvement of alarmins and cytokines released by epithelial cells, such as TSLP, IL-33 and IL-25, which may heave and interact with traditional Th2 processes[92]. These observations suggest that similar non-T2 inflammatory processes, potentially driven by thunderstorm-specific environmental exposures, may also occur in some vulnerable populations experiencing TA, and could play an important role in the damage of the airway epithelial barrier and immune system dysregulation characteristic of TA outbreaks.

Under the condition of thunderstorms, PM2.5enhances IL-6 and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8) expression via epithelial TLR4/NF-κB pathway, signaling the recruitment of neutrophils [93,94]. Similarly, O3exposure, another common pollutant during thunderstorms, is dependent on NF-κB1, activities transcriptional inflammatory. IL-6 is a critical contributor to O3 exposure-induced inflammatory response in the lungs [95]. Moreover, LPS can induce secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 from the bronchial epithelium via TLR4–NF-κB [96]. These findings delineate a mechanism by which an NF-κB-driven pro-inflammatory network in epithelial cells may be activated during thunderstorms. After activation, neutrophils can release enzymes like neutrophil elastase (NE) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) and produce NETs [97], which may be particularly harmful to the epithelial barrier and produce increased mucus in acute TA. Recent studies show that NETs play an important role in neutrophilic asthma [98], a phenotype potentially relevant to severe or steroid-resistant TA cases. In addition, neutrophils comprise more than 50% of sputum in this disease and are associated with markedly high IL-17A and IL-8 levels [90]. IL-17A is secreted by Th17 cells. The receptor of IL-17A is IL-17RA/RC. It activates the NF-κB/MAPK pathway and produces mediators like chemokines (e.g., IL-8), prostaglandins, and leukotrienes. These recruit neutrophils and aggravate airway inflammation and hyperreactivity [99]. Preclinical evidence from experimental asthma models further confirms that Th17-dominated non-T2 inflammation drives airway barrier dysfunction and steroid resistance via two complementary mechanisms, one involving proteolytic imbalance from elevated matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-8/MMP-9) and reduced tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMP-3/TIMP-4) that degrades structural collagens and weakens epithelial integrity, and the other entailing induction of necroptosis in bronchial epithelial and subepithelial cells via the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway to release pro-inflammatory damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and perpetuate neutrophilic infiltration [192] . Research shows an imbalance between Th17 and regulatory T cells (Treg) cells may cause corticosteroid-resistant neutrophilic asthma [91], suggesting a potential mechanism for poor steroid response in some TA presentations. In conclusion, the thunderstorm-associated factors create a distinct non-type 2 inflammatory pathway through the activation of epithelial NF-κB signaling, potentiation of the Th17/IL-17 axis, and driving of neutrophil responses (Fig.3).

Non–type 2 inflammation in TA

In addition, some TA patients may have an inflammatory profile that is mixed. They may exhibit features of eosinophilic infiltration and neutrophilic infiltration together with biomarkers reflecting T2 as well as Th17/IL-17 pathways [100]. As a result, future strategies to prevent and manage TA should consider the underlying mechanisms of the T2 and non-T2 inflammatory responses together.

The 17q21 locus is a genomic region strongly associated with asthma susceptibility. Specific genetic polymorphisms within this region may not only influence general susceptibility but also confer an increased risk of acute asthma exacerbations following thunderstorms. This area is regarded as the main genetic locus associated with asthma in children. The variants present play an important role in altering the levels of gasdermin B (GSDMB) and ORMDL Sphingolipid Biosynthesis Regulator 3 (ORMDL3) in determining asthma susceptibility. The interaction of these genes with environmental factors like viruses and pollutants, for example during thunderstorms, raises the risk of an acute episode of asthma [101,102,103].

Significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the 17q21 region, including rs72163891 and rs7216389, switch the IFN class by increasing mucosal GSDMB levels. The result is a decreased amount of type I/III interferons needed for antiviral defense along with an increased amount of type II interferon (IFN-γ) and stimulation of cytolytic responses by natural killer (NK) cells and CD8⁺ T cells. As a result, airway epithelial barrier dysfunction and decreased viral resistance will occur [101]. The effects are further amplified during thunderstorms as the presence of respiratory viruses such as human rhinovirus (HRV) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) would activate the MAVS-TBK1 signaling pathway that is downstream of GSDMB, with the consequential release of inflammatory mediators such as RANTES and OASL. Moreover, harm to the epithelial barrier by environmental pollutants like PM2.5and PAHs leads to genetic vulnerability to viruses and inflammation due to pollution [102,103].

Certain SNPs in the 17q21 region (such as rs7216389 and rs2305480) are risk variants for pediatric asthma. Children with the risk allele exhibit elevated ORMDL3 expression in the airway epithelium, which disturbs intracellular calcium homeostasis due to associations with calcium pump protein SERCA2. This interaction promotes epithelial cell autophagy (1.8-fold increase in the LC3B-II/LC3B-I ratio) and cell death (1.5-fold increase in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release), which weakens the barrier’s protective ability against allergens and pollutants. Moreover, ORMDL3 changes sphingolipid metabolism to enhance the contraction of airway smooth muscles and the production of mucus, which aggravates asthma [103,104].

The airway inflammatory characteristics have a connection to the 17q21 variant. People who have this allele of greatest risk have significantly higher blood eosinophils and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) [101]. People who have a genetic/epigenetic predisposition to asthma are at increased risk for asthma exacerbations under conditions present during a thunderstorm due to the effect of pollution on eosinophil accumulation. Thus, treatment strategies should be directed towards this interaction.

Thunderstorm weather increases the prevalence of airborne allergens (like pollen, dust mites), and pollutants (like PM2.5) and microbes. These exposures can trigger acute asthma attacks through epigenetic alterations (e.g. DNA methylation and histone modification) in the airway epithelial and immune cells, which in turn drive downstream changes in gene expression, protein profiles, and cellular metabolism, as elucidated by multi-omics studies. DNA methylation, catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), is an epigenetic mechanism that influences TA by regulating promoters or enhancers [105]. In asthma, hypomethylation of Th2-related genes (e.g., IL-5RA, IL-13) enhances their expression and eosinophil activity, while demethylation of the IL-4/IL-13 promoters facilitates Th2 cell differentiation via STAT6/GATA3 binding [106]. Thunderstorm-associated allergens may exacerbate this pro-inflammatory epigenetic pattern. In addition to allergens, exposure to particulate matter present in both thunderstorm aerosols and urban pollution can modify the epigenetic landscape. Independent studies on prenatal exposure indicate that traffic-related air pollution is associated with differential DNA methylation at specific CpG sites in umbilical cord blood, such as cg14547404 (annotated toARHGAP22) and cg06517429 (annotated toCASP7), genes involved in immune regulation and apoptosis, respectively [17,107]. This evidence underscores the potential for environmental pollutants to induce epigenetic alterations relevant to immune pathways. A comprehensive epigenome-wide association study revealed a principal component (PC7), which explains 4.28% of clinical variance and is strongly correlated with elevated eosinophil and IgE levels, was associated with 204 CpG sites. Of these, 190 were previously linked to asthma, revealing a stable epigenetic signature for eosinophilic inflammation and IgE dysregulation [18,108]. This molecular framework may help explain the rapid eosinophil-driven responses characteristic of TA.

Histone modifications, such as acetylation, represent a key epigenetic mechanism that regulates chromatin structure and gene expression. In asthma, epigenomic studies have directly linked enhancer activity in critical cell types (including CD4 + T cells, ILC2s, and bronchial epithelial cells) to disease susceptibility. These regulatory regions, dynamically marked by histone modifications, frequently colocalize and interact with genetic risk loci identified through genome-wide association study (GWAS) [109]. This synergy establishes an integrated epigenomic-genetic network, supporting the hypothesis that thunderstorm events may exacerbate asthma by disrupting these well-defined regulatory circuits. Histone deacetylases (HDACs), especially HDAC2, repress inflammatory gene expression. In severe asthma, diminished HDAC2 activity is linked to poor corticosteroid response. Environmental exposures such as air pollutants, whose levels can surge during thunderstorms, reduce HDAC2 expression, disrupting histone acetylation balance and promoting pro-inflammatory gene (e.g., Th2 cytokine) expression. This epigenetic dysregulation establishes histone acetylation as a key link between environmental triggers and asthma pathogenesis, highlighting HDAC activity as a potential therapeutic target for thunderstorm-associated exacerbations [19,110].

Given that TA is triggered by acute, high-dose environmental exposures, the resulting epigenetic alterations are likely to initiate extensive molecular cascades. Therefore, integrating multi-omics technologies (e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics) is essential for comprehensively mapping the TA-specific molecular landscape from epigenetic regulation to the final immune and inflammatory outputs [160,173]

TA might be regulated by the airway microbiome. The microbial quantity and diversity of airways, in turn, significantly modulate immune balance and inflammatory pathways in the airways. In healthy people, a different microbiome in the airways helps maintain balance by stopping pathogens growing and also regulating immune cells [111]. However, the thunderstorms are expected to increase the levels of airborne allergens like pollen as well as pollutants like PM2.5and microbial agents. This can further disturb the already disrupted airway microbiota among patients with asthma. Studies using induced sputum samples show that asthmatics (especially during acute exacerbations) have a significant reduction in airway bacterial diversity and also have a microbiota profile that is different from healthy individuals. For instance, the increase in potentially inflammatory bacteria Atopobium and Alloscardovia coupled with a decrease in beneficial bacteria Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium is found in obese asthma patients [112]. Moreover, contamination due to thunderstorms such as PM2.5, can support the growth of harmful bacteria like Haemophilus, Streptococcus, and Moraxella in the airways of asthma patients. These bacteria can activate TLR4 pathway in airway epithelial cells that produce IL-1β and TNF-α. This aggravates allergic reactions inside the airway during thunderstorms [111,112].

The gut microbiome may indirectly exacerbate TA through the gut-lung axis and this effect may be mediated by SCFAs [24,113]. SCFAs, especially butyrate, propionate, and acetate, are produced in the gut by the fermentation of dietary fibre. Butyrate strengthens the intestinal barrier and reduces systemic inflammation via inhibition of HDAC and promoting Treg differentiation [24,113]. Pollutants such as PM2.5during thunderstorms can change the gut microbiota composition. It has been reported that these pollutants reduce the concentrations of short chain fatty acids in the blood and airways of asthmatics. This happens because they reduce SCFA-producing bacteria such as Faecalibacterium and Roseburia [24,113]. The reduction of SCFAs can prevent Treg from controlling Th2 inflammation. This will lead to an increase in Th2 cytokines (such as IL-4 and IL-13) and can worsen thunderstorms. According to this research, the SCFA levels in asthma patients’ sputum were negatively correlated with the severity of airway inflammation. Research has found that lowering eosinophils and FeNO during TA can be achieved by prebiotic supplementation for growth of butyrate SCFA gut bacteria or directly by butyrate. Moreover, lung function medicals such as forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁) can also be improved [24,113]. By adjusting the airway microbiome to enhance SCFA levels, we may be able to manage asthma and its acute exacerbations.

Monitoring biomarkers is critical for TA risk stratification, inflammation evaluation, and management guidance. TA individuals often have strong Th2-type inflammation activation. Rye grass pollen specific IgE (RGP-sp IgE) levels correlate with the severity of TA. The median RGP-sp IgE level is 27.4 kU/L in H-TA (hospitalized patients due to severe TA episodes). In contrast, the I-SAR (patients with only SAR symptoms and no TA history) group had a median RGP-sp IgE level 1.8 kU/L. Peripheral blood eosinophils (≥ 0.3 × 10⁹/L) (univariate OR = 3.7, multivariate OR = 2.59) and increased levels of FeNO (median 31.0 ppb in H-TA) may be useful non-invasive markers of Th2 inflammation which indicate a risk of the acute exacerbation of TA [114]. Epithelial alarmins (TSLP, IL-33) and Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) are also upregulated in TA airways, holding potential for future precision immune profiling and targeted therapies.

To elucidate the pathophysiological framework of TA, spanning from environmental trigger-induced epithelial barrier disruption to immune dysregulation, a multifaceted assessment of acute airway alterations is indispensable. A study conducted on 454,921 adults in the UK consisting of 48,896 people with asthma found that for every IQR increase in exposure to PM coarse, PM10, NO₂ and NOₓ, asthma patients had a decrease of 5.143 ml, 7.614 ml, 13.266 ml and 9.440 ml in FEV₁ and a decrease of 11.744 ml, 15.637 ml, 13.041 ml and 9.063 ml in forced vital capacity (FVC), respectively [115]. Acute TA episodes often involve small airway dysfunction and inflammation, which may not be fully captured by standard spirometry. Specialized tests such as multiple-breath washout (MBW) and impulse oscillometry (IOS) are more sensitive for detecting early ventilation heterogeneity and increased airway resistance; these abnormalities can occur up to 2.8 weeks prior to FEV₁/FVC deterioration and thus may help predict exacerbations [116]. Furthermore, the refinement of IOS parameters (R5-R20, AX reactance area, X5) into composite scores may enhance the prediction of TA-specific small airway dysfunction [117]. Imaging modalities like high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) directly visualize key pathological features such as airway narrowing and mucus plugging [118], and quantitative CT (QCT) parameters derived from such imaging hold promise for predicting the risk of future lung function decline and asthma attacks [119].

Advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques, such as those utilizing hyperpolarized gas to measure ventilation defect percent (VDP), have been shown to forecast the incidence of asthma attacks over two years [120]. Techniques like lung ultrasound (LUS) offer rapid, bedside assessment, proving particularly useful in pediatric emergency settings [121]. In conclusion, these assessments will further our understanding of the mechanisms underlying acute TA exacerbations and promote the advancement of precision medicine.

As TA events have increased in frequency and magnitude, robust environmental monitoring and effective early warning systems are indispensable. Integrating weather data with allergen concentration tracking to generate credible risk models enables timely TA alerts, supporting public and healthcare preventive actions.

To improve the accuracy of TA early warning models, meteorological data must be integrated with spatially refined allergen data. As demonstrated in a multi-city analysis, reliance on single-site pollen monitoring is insufficient for assessing area-wide risk. Crucially, incorporating local environmental characteristics, such as land cover of pollen-producing grasslands and deciduous trees, can significantly improve the prediction of TA risk at distances far from pollen monitors [122]. Technological advances, such as automated bioaerosol sensors (e.g., Rapid-E) coupled with dispersion models, enhance real-time tracking of allergen sources like starch particles—implicated in TA outbreaks [123]. Further, smart health management system creation and use is a significant support tool for patients and public health authorities [124]. Digital health innovations, such as wearable sensors (e.g., smartwatches), help track personal health parameters such as heart rate, sleep, and physical activity levels [125]. A prominent example is the Southeast Asia Community Observatory and Health and Demographic Surveillance Site (SEACO HDSS), which evaluates sensor-based digital technologies for monitoring climate-health interactions, focusing on health issues like asthma [126]. Its aims include generating exposure–response data, testing tool acceptability for broader surveillance, and informing protective policies for vulnerable regions [126].

Amid global climate change-driven increases in severe weather and environmental allergens, continuous refinement of early warning systems and environmental monitoring is imperative. Future advancements should prioritize integrating multi-source data (e.g., meteorological, pollen, air quality), expanding monitoring site density, and conducting AI-powered risk assessments[25,125,127-130]. Additionally, key strategies to reduce TA incidence and related fatalities include enhancing public risk awareness through education and delivering targeted early warnings to vulnerable groups via smart platforms.

Core strategies for TA prevention focus on individual protection, policy intervention, and cross-sectoral collaboration, aligned with mitigating environmental trigger-epithelial barrier interactions and reducing baseline inflammatory burden [127,131]. Targeted health education for vulnerable groups (e.g., allergic rhinitis/asthma patients) enhances self-protection skills (e.g., avoiding outdoor exposure during high-risk storms) and emergency response capabilities, effectively lowering TA incidence [131]. Countries like Australia and China have integrated TA early warning systems into healthcare infrastructure, combining weather monitoring with real-time pollen analysis to issue preemptive public alerts [50,127].

Policy-level actions include strengthening air quality standards to reduce pollutant (PM2.5, O3, NO₂) emissions and implementing strategic urban greening, which mitigates airborne allergen (pollen) concentrations and protects the airway epithelial barrier [50,127,132,133]. A cross-departmental framework facilitating information sharing among meteorological, public health, and medical institutions ensures rapid response to TA-triggering events [50,127,134].

Vulnerable and disadvantaged communities require tailored interventions, as socioeconomic factors (income gaps, education levels) and community violence modulate TA susceptibility by amplifying the impact of environmental triggers [135]. Community health education also emphasizes proper use of controller medications to minimize acute exacerbations induced by barrier disruption and immune dysregulation [136,137]. Collectively, this multi-layered approach reduces TA risk by addressing environmental triggers, strengthening public health resilience, and promoting health equity.

Pharmacological management of TA targets two core pathophysiological links: protecting the airway epithelial barrier and modulating dysregulated immunity, with therapies stratified by disease severity and inflammatory phenotype [138,139,140,141]. ICS and bronchodilators remain first-line treatments. Regular ICS use stabilizes epithelial barrier function, reduces airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness, and with consistent use, decreases the risk of severe acute flares, although its anti-inflammatory effects peak over days to weeks [138,139,140,141]. Bronchodilators provide rapid relief from acute bronchospasm [141]. For patients with T2-high inflammation not controlled by dual therapy (ICS/LABA), triple therapy (ICS/LABA/LAMA) provides added benefit [142,143].

Prominent biological targets linked to type 2 inflammation include IgE IL-5/5R IL-4R TSLP etc. These components have proven useful in relieving asthma attacks, improving lung functioning, and enhancing the quality of life of patients [144]. Omalizumab, an anti-IgE monoclonal antibody, reduces exacerbations and improves lung function in moderate-to-severe allergic asthma refractory to standard therapy by binding free IgE and downregulating FcεRI on mast cells and basophils, thereby attenuating allergen-triggered responses [145,146]. Anti–IL-5/IL-5Rα agents, including mepolizumab, benralizumab, and the ultra-long-acting depemokimab, suppress eosinophilic inflammation by inhibiting eosinophil survival, maturation, and recruitment [147]. Dupilumab, an anti-IL-4Rα monoclonal antibody, reduces Th2 immune response and mucus hypersecretion by simultaneously blocking IL-4 and IL-13, demonstrating efficacy in improving small airway function and reducing airway hyperresponsiveness [143,148-150]. These biologics target the immune dysregulation in asthma, particularly in severe, therapy-resistant cases, demonstrating favorable efficacy and safety [142,144,151].

AIT is a disease-modifying treatment crucial for the long-term management of TA, alongside biologics. It is administered via three main routes: subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT), sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT), and oral immunotherapy (OIT) [152]. SCIT is often considered superior to SLIT and nasal immunotherapy for house dust mite allergy, particularly in terms of providing long-term relief from symptoms and reduced IgE levels. Although, it requires longer treatment duration (three to five years) and has a higher rate of local side effects (swelling and itching at the injection site) and systemic reactions (hives and mild anaphylaxis) [152,153]. SLIT is characterized by a favorable safety profile, typically with only mild local reactions, and improved patient adherence due to reduced clinic visits, making it particularly suitable for children and older adults [152,154].Animal studies indicate that while SCIT downregulates Th2 inflammation and promotes allergen-specific neutralizing antibodies (IgG1/IgG2a), SLIT primarily mitigates airway hyper-responsiveness; moreover, vitamin D₃ can augment the immunomodulatory activity of SCIT [155].

Oxidative stress is a central driver of TA, with urban PM exacerbating airway injury by driving oxidative and inflammatory responses. Consequently, therapeutic strategies are increasingly targeting oxidative stress reduction and barrier function restoration [156,157]. Vitamin D has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects. Furthermore, it is also observed to stimulate the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway, reduce PM-induced airway inflammation, and inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and nerve growth factor (NGF) thereby providing protective benefit to the airways [157]. Early-life administration of vitamin D can benefit offspring airway immunity by modulating both airway immune cells and microbial communities [158]. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting oxidative stress and barrier repair, warranting future clinical exploration.

In summary, pharmacotherapy for TA is evolving to target its core pathophysiology, namely epithelial barrier disruption and immune dysregulation. Continuous research must validate these targeted strategies, particularly biologics and immunomodulators, within the unique context of acute, high-dose environmental exposure that defines TA.

Present-day biomedical research leverages multi-omics technologies to unravel complex diseases like asthma [159,160,161,162,163]. For TA, multi-omics integration is critical to delineate the complete pathway from the initial epigenetic disruptions caused by thunderstorm exposures (e.g., SPPs, PM2.5) to the downstream inflammatory responses. Transcriptomic studies indicate that during acute asthma exacerbations, airway epithelial cells show dysregulation in inflammatory, stress, and immune activation pathways [159]. In children with asthma, these transcriptomic features further interact with immune cell inflammation (e.g., eosinophils) to drive pathogenesis [162].

The integrated application of multi-omics technologies, including combinations such as genetics with proteomics [168], epigenetics with proteomics [166], and metagenomics with proteomics [169], provides a powerful framework for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of asthma and forms an informational foundation for novel therapeutics [164,166,168]. These approaches reveal protein regulatory networks that could form the pathological basis for acute TA. Specifically, genetic studies identify plasma proteins with potential causal links to asthma risk, such as IL-4R, LTB [164], and ST2 [166], alongside broader pathogenic target ensembles [168], which may act as critical regulatory nodes under acute environmental stress. Concurrently, mass spectrometry analyses define sputum protein signatures linked to imminent exacerbations [165] and heterogeneous airway inflammatory proteomes [167], representing likely downstream molecular phenotypes triggered by thunderstorm exposure. Thus, thunderstorm events may trigger TA by acutely disrupting this multi-omics-defined protein network, ultimately driving severe inflammatory responses.

Metabolomic signatures associated with air pollution exposure have been identified, linking pollutants like PM2.5and NOxto an elevated risk of chronic respiratory diseases (including asthma). This association is partly mediated by systemic inflammation and involves perturbations in pathways such as amino acid and energy metabolism [170]. The functional dimension of metabolomics is demonstrated in cough variant asthma (CVA), where sputum profiling revealed dysregulated metabolic pathways, inflammation-linked metabolites, and clinically informative biomarkers [171]. This approach can pinpoint pathway disturbances and their functional consequences. A critical future direction is to apply integrated metabolomic profiling to TA, to define its acute metabolic signature and biomarker potential. SCFAs (e.g. butyrate, acetate) in the feces of asthmatic children show a negative correlation with serum IgE levels, and their metabolic configurations in blood and feces can distinguish allergic rhinitis from asthma (areas under the curve (AUC): 0.732 & 0.799) [172].

Combining metabolomics with transcriptomic and proteomic data is essential. Metabolomics may serve as a “research roadmap” for future in-depth elucidation of the complex pathological mechanisms of TA [173]. The multi-omics approach will help in identifying personalized therapeutic targets like inhibitors of metabolic pathways, thus supporting precision medicine [174].

Severe global climate change is increasing thunderstorms in number and strength which has an adverse impact on public health, especially among the asthmatic population [5,49]. Temperatures and storms impact the rainfall pattern and both affect air quality and pollen. This increases your risk of having asthma attacks [30,32,34,36]. High levels of pollen present a major environmental trigger of sudden asthma attacks. The accurate monitoring and forecasting of pollen are essential ways in which asthma sufferers can protect themselves against these hazards [175].

Anticipated warming and changes in humidity due to climate change will extend pollen seasons and increase pollen levels over the coming decades. The likelihood of thunderstorms happening at the same time as higher pollen count may be on the increase too. These changes will impact the health of asthma sufferers directly by changing how and when people are allergic to them. As such, we will need a strong global model of pollen concentrations by accounting for climate change factors so that we can better anticipate future risks of allergenic pollen exposure and its contribution to asthma attacks [176]. It will help to better understand asthma.

We need public health strategies that addresses asthma due to climate change. The first thing that health policies should do is create awareness among health care providers and the public regarding the impact that climate change and pollen will have on the incidence of asthma, understanding that differences in pollen can trigger asthma. For instance, advanced pollen prediction technologies (e.g. multi-parameter prediction models developed on the basis of machine learning) can enhance early warning systems for severe weather events (e.g. thunderstorms) at high pollen levels. This will help keep asthma patients informed. They will receive alerts to stay indoors due to high pollen counts [159,160]. Moreover, it is essential for authorities and health bodies to increase funding for pollen monitoring systems in places with high asthma rates and considerable pollen fluctuations. Through real-time monitoring, both the patients and the healthcare will get accurate data to help take treatment and allergen avoidance decisions. Actions should also be taken to assist asthma patients in forming solidarity networks that enable them to access medical advice and psychological support during unexpected pollen levels due to climate change. In conclusion, effective public health strategies should be developed based on the effects of climate change on pollen in order to prevent asthma attacks and its consequent health hazards.

Improving asthma care requires tailored management strategies and collaborative efforts across disciplines, especially for managing acute flare-ups linked to thunderstorms. When healthcare providers use risk assessment to individualize prevention and treatment, it helps them to create specific management plans for the type and severity and stage of asthmas. New guidelines stress the need for tailored pathways to treatment based on an assessment of factors such as management of symptoms, risk of exacerbations, type 2 airway inflammatory markers, allergic conditions, adherence to treatment and co-morbidities, to enhance asthma control and reduce the risk of attacks [1,137,177]. Machine learning technology is an important force behind personalized management innovations. Using large datasets to assess each patient’s medical history plus type 2 inflammatory markers (blood eosinophils and FeNO), lung function (FEV₁) as well as environmental triggers (pollen and air quality), allows accurate forecasting and adjustment of severity asthma exacerbation, thereby improving the precision of asthma prevention and treatment [178,179].

The best asthma care requires a team of specialists from many fields. Pulmonologists make a diagnosis, formulate treatment plans and create a tailored strategy; while allergists help with allergens and immunotherapy. Environmental influences on health are considered and the community educated. The collaboration of these professionals allows for a smooth overlap of clinical resources and public health measures to manage issues comprehensively. Collaboration needed by pulmonologists, allergists and otolaryngologists to enhance teamwork in diagnosis, treatment planning and follow-up in cases of severe eosinophilic asthma and chronic sinusitis as a consensus of European experts show [180]. Furthermore, collaborative philosophies for managing complex airway diseases, including chronic sinusitis with asthma and aspirin-induced respiratory reactions, have been shown to yield substantial benefits to patients while also minimizing the waste of medical resources [181,182]. In practice, a highly-effective way to improve overall asthma management is to set up multidisciplinary outpatient clinics, create consultation teams, and hold joint diagnostic and treatment sessions [183,184].

According to the 2025 guidelines from the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) the long-term management of asthma should be personalized through an ‘Assess-Adjust-Review’ cycle in a news article [1]. Initial review should ideally coincide with the initiation of controller therapy, at 2–3 months after a clinic visit. Thereafter, timing may be tailored based on symptom control and risk factors. Assessment of lung function is recommended at the time of diagnosis, 3–6 months after initiation of treatment and at regular intervals thereafter, e.g. every 1–2 years. Patients who do not achieve adequate control with standard therapy may benefit from evaluation of type 2 inflammatory biomarkers such as FeNO and blood eosinophils which could aid in understanding inflammatory phenotypes. Education on self-management is vital during follow-up. The A2BCD framework acknowledges “patient education and self-management skills training” as essential elements in asthma care [185]. Education is crucial as it raises awareness around the disease, thereby promoting tailored treatment [185]. Furthermore, randomized controlled trials show that an educational programme, designed following the theory of planned behaviour (TPB), whereby there are five home visits over three months, significantly improves asthma control (100% of the intervention group moved from uncontrolled to well controlled, whereas only 20% of the control group moved from uncontrolled to well controlled) and medication adherence (Morisky score improved from 2.37 to 7.50 in the intervention group, whereas there was no improvement in the control group) [186].

TA Management: Personal Risk Assessment, Collaborative Medical Input and Continuous Long-Term Management and Assessment Would Probably Work Well for a Successful Strategy. Using high-end technology such as machine learning for decision support, synergy with RM, allergy, ENT specialist etc., continuous education and adaptive management of patients can improve asthma management, decrease frequency of acute episodes, improved quality of life and health outcome of the patients.

The rising incidence of TA presents a significant climate-sensitive health challenge. This review synthesizes evidence supporting a core pathophysiological framework: TA is initiated by thunderstorm-driven generation of respirable bioaerosols (e.g., sub-pollen particles, pollutant mixtures), which cause acute airway epithelial barrier disruption. This breach, characterized by tight junction damage, mucociliary dysfunction, and alarmin release (IL-25, IL-33, TSLP), triggers a pronounced immune dysregulation. The response dominantly involves a robust type 2 (T2) inflammation (ILC2/Th2 axis, IgE, eosinophils), but non-T2 pathways (Th17/neutrophils, IL-6/IL-17) also contribute, particularly in severe or steroid-resistant cases, and may explain heterogeneous clinical phenotypes. Underlying genetic, epigenetic, and microbiome-host interactions further modulate individual susceptibility.

These insights carry direct clinical and public health implications. First, they underscore the urgency of developing integrated early warning systems that combine real-time meteorological, pollen, and air quality data to protect vulnerable populations. Second, management strategies must evolve beyond conventional asthma therapy. This includes promoting barrier-protective agents, tailoring biologic therapies (e.g., anti-IgE, anti-TSLP, anti-IL-5/5R, anti-IL-4R) based on inflammatory endotypes, and reconsidering the timing and efficacy of allergen immunotherapy in the context of acute, high-dose exposures. Third, a precision medicine approach is essential, guided by biomarkers (e.g., FeNO, eosinophils, specific IgE) and advanced lung function assessments to stratify risk and personalize interventions.

For future research, priority should be given to: 1) delineating the precise molecular cascades linking specific storm-related co-exposures to epithelial damage and immune activation, using integrated multi-omics approaches; 2) prospectively validating clinical biomarkers and inflammatory phenotypes (T2 vs. non-T2) in TA cohorts to guide targeted treatment; and 3) elucidating the long-term respiratory consequences of repeated TA episodes. Furthermore, multidisciplinary collaboration among pulmonologists, allergists, climatologists, and public health experts is crucial to translate mechanistic understanding into effective prevention and policy.

In summary, TA epitomizes the critical intersection of environmental change, epithelial biology, and immunology. Addressing this growing threat requires a dual strategy: advancing translational research to unravel its unique pathophysiology and implementing coordinated public health actions to mitigate risk. As climate change increases the frequency and intensity of conducive weather events, building resilience against TA becomes an increasingly urgent component of global respiratory health.
