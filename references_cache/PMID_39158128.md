# Phenotypic spectrum and tumor risk in Simpson-Golabi-Behmel syndrome: Case series and comprehensive literature review.
**Authors:** Nisbet AF, Viswanathan A, George AM, Arias P, Klein SD, Nevado J, Parra A, Pascual P, Romeo DJ, Tenorio-Castaño J, Taylor JA, Zackai EH, Lapunzina P, Kalish JM
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.a.63840](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.a.63840)

## Content

Simpson-Golabi-Behmel syndrome (SGBS) is a rare congenital overgrowth condition characterized by macrosomia, macroglossia, coarse facial features, and development delays. It is caused by pathogenic variants in the GPC3 gene on chromosome Xq26.2. Here, we performed a comprehensive literature review and phenotyping of known patients with molecularly confirmed SGBS and reviewed a novel cohort of 22 patients. Using these data, we characterized the tumor risk for Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma to suggest appropriate screening for this patient population. In addition, we discuss the phenotypic overlap between SGBS and Beckwith-Wiedemann Spectrum.

Simpson-Golabi-Behmel syndrome (SGBS) (OMIM: 300037, HGNC: 4451) is a rare congenital X-linked overgrowth syndrome that was initially described by J. Simpson in 1975 and further characterized by A. Behmel and M. Golabi in 1984 (Behmel et al., 1984; Golabi & Rosen, 1984; Simpson et al., 1975). The phenotype of SGBS is thought to be caused by pathogenic variants of GPC3, which is located at chromosome Xq26.2 and encodes for Glypican-3 (Pilia et al., 1996). Patients with SGBS may present with macroglossia, both prenatal and postnatal overgrowth, macrocephaly, organomegaly, genitourinary anomalies, and a unique facial gestalt characterized by hypertelorism, frontal bossing, anteverted nares, and high-arched or cleft palate (Klein et al., 2023). Occasionally, more severe presentations include congenital heart defects, diaphragmatic hernias, and brain malformations (Fiandrino et al., 2022; Halayem et al., 2016; Reischer et al., 2021). Patients with SGBS have also been reported to have increased predisposition for embryonal tumors such as Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma (Klein et al., 2023), although the exact incidence of these tumors have not been fully characterized in the literature. Due to the phenotypic variability in SGBS, there exists a need to precisely delineate the clinical presentation of this syndrome.

Clinical features of SGBS overlap significantly with those of Beckwith-Wiedemann Spectrum (BWSp), a congenital overgrowth disorder also associated with increased predisposition to embryonal tumors and characterized by features such as macroglossia, macrosomia, lateralized overgrowth, hyperinsulinism, and organomegaly (Verloes et al., 1995; Wang et al., 2019). The similarities between the two conditions have led to misdiagnosis of patients, which has been previously reported in the literature (Higashimoto et al., 2024; Knopp et al., 2015; Punnett, 1994) and highlights the need for additional investigation.

Our study presents a cohort of 22 patients with molecularly confirmed SGBS, 21 of whom have not been previously reported in the literature. Additionally, we completed a comprehensive literature review of patients with molecularly confirmed SGBS to better characterize the phenotype and cancer risk associated with this condition. Together, these contributions allow our study to provide additional diagnostic guidance in differentiating between SGBS and BWSp in both molecular and non-molecularly diagnostic settings.

On May 15th, 2024, all entries returned from the search term “Simpson-Golabi-Behmel” from the National Library of Medicine’s PubMed database were saved and serially given a unique identifier code. Each case study was identified and reviewed for phenotypic data. From the case studies reviewed, a total of 125 unique individuals with molecular confirmation of SGBS were phenotyped. We also included data from a cohort of 42-molecularly confirmed patients reported by Cottereau et al., for which individual phenotype data were available (Cottereau et al., 2013). We did not include the case series from Brioude et al. in 2019 that referenced 18 cases identified in their laboratory due to lack of individual phenotype data necessary for analysis (Brioude et al., 2019). Females with strong clinical suspicion and molecular confirmation of SGBS were included in the cohort. Cases of misdiagnoses, identified through follow up publications, were removed from the analysis. Cases clinically diagnosed as SGBS but molecularly confirmed to have isolated variants in GPC4 and PIGA were also excluded from the study. Fetuses and infantile deaths were excluded from cancer incidence calculations but included in phenotype frequency calculations. Non-molecularly confirmed cases were reviewed for phenotype data when available, but these data were not included in the literature or total cohorts. Supplementary Figure 1 details the methodology of the literature search and cohort inclusion.

In addition to patient data obtained from the literature, we collected data on 22 patients with molecularly confirmed SGBS, of whom 14 were either evaluated or had records reviewed at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia and eight were evaluated or had records reviewed as part of the Spanish Overgrowth Syndrome Registry Initiative. Patient “Spain8” was previously reported in the literature by PL (Lapunzina et al., 1998), but genetic confirmation of SGBS has subsequently been acquired and thus this patient has been included in our novel cohort. Charts were reviewed and data extraction was performed via IRB13–010658, for patients at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. The BWSp clinical score was calculated for all patients to highlight the phenotypic overlap between SGBS and BWS using previously established methodology (Brioude et al., 2018). For patients with adequate data stored in their medical chart, BWS index of macroGlossia (BIG) scores were retrospectively assigned to assess severity of macroglossia using previously established methodology (Romeo et al., 2024). Data on the Spanish patients were abstracted from their electronic health records by the Spain-based research team under CEIC-HULP-PI446, Hospital Universitario La Paz. Informed consent, where applicable, was obtained from all participants in accordance with the protocols approved by the respective IRBs.

There were 16 unique genetic variants of GPC3 identified in our novel cohort of 22 patients, of which there were 21 males and 1 female. Macroglossia (86.4%), macrostomia (73.7%), and frontal bossing (57.1%) were among the most common facial features observed. Additional high-prevalence features included macrosomia (81.8%), organomegaly (63.6%), renal abnormalities such as hydronephrosis and renal dysplasia (63.6%), and developmental delay (66.7%). One male patient in the cohort, “CHOP3,” was diagnosed with a hepatoblastoma at 17 months of age. Additionally, we included the case of “Spain8,” who presented with a hepatocellular carcinoma and died at 3 years of age. The complete genotype and phenotype of all patients in the novel cohort are reported in Table 1. Figure 1 highlights some of the facial characteristics of patients in our cohort.

Phenotype data was also collected from 167 patients reported in the literature with molecular confirmation of Simpson-Golabi-Behmel syndrome. A list of these variants and their classifications are reported in Supplementary Table 1. Cases reported as molecularly confirmed that lacked specific genotype information such as breakpoints or variant details were not included in this list. Within the literature cohort, 91.6% of patients were male, 6.6% were female, and 1.8% were not reported. The frequencies of macroglossia, organomegaly, cryptorchidism, and developmental delay were strikingly similar to findings from the novel cohort. Skeletal features (65%), including polydactyly (15%), and skin manifestations such as supernumerary nipples (61.2%) were prevalent. Furthermore, genitourinary findings (67.2%), including cryptorchidism (41.4%), and renal abnormalities (56.5%), were also common. Abdominal wall defects such as diastasis recti or umbilical hernia occurred in 32% of individuals. Frequencies of SGBS features are reported for the novel, literature, and total cohorts in Table 2. A total of 23 tumors were identified from the literature, including eight cases of Wilms tumor and seven cases of hepatoblastoma (Tables 3A and 3B). In addition to the cases of Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma, eight other cases of tumors were identified from the literature in patients with molecularly confirmed SGBS. These included pancreatoblastoma (Hasan et al., 2024), neuroblastoma (Xu et al., 2022), an unspecified leukemia (Cottereau et al., 2013), medulloblastoma (Thomas et al., 2012), ovarian tumor (Gurrieri et al., 2011), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Sakazume et al., 2007), pancreatic carcinoma (Rodriguez-Criado et al., 2005), and an adrenal neuroblastoma which was initially reported as a Wilms tumor (Hughes-Benzie et al., 1992; Hughes-Benzie et al., 1996; Lapunzina et al., 1998).

The tumor incidence of Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma are 5.1% and 5.1% respectively among patients with molecularly confirmed SGBS in the total cohort. The average age of diagnosis for cases of Wilms tumor was 18.06 months while the average age of hepatoblastoma diagnosis was 17.25 months (Table 3). These figures are based on data available from the literature, and it is likely that not all patients diagnosed with these embryonal tumors received appropriate screening measures. Furthermore, Cottereau et al. reported the presence of a Wilms tumor in a male fetus with molecularly confirmed SGBS (Cottereau et al., 2013). One patient in our novel cohort, “CHOP3,” was diagnosed with hepatoblastoma at 17 months of age. At 16 months, elevated AFP levels were noted on quarterly screenings, and ultrasound revealed a small liver mass. Further imaging and liver biopsy confirmed the mass as a hepatoblastoma. The patient underwent tumor resection followed by postoperative chemotherapy and has since recovered well with no reoccurrence after two years of follow-up. Five additional cases of Wilms tumor and one additional case of hepatoblastoma were identified from the literature in patients who had strong clinical suspicion of SGBS, but lacked molecular confirmation (Andrysiak-Mamos et al., 2019; Li et al., 2001; Lindsay et al., 1997; Paludetti et al., 2003; Verloes et al., 1995).

In addition to the incidence of tumors, developmental delays were also notable, with a frequency of 57.7% observed in the total cohort. In our novel cohort, developmental delays were predominantly observed in speech and language, as well as fine motor skills. Speech delays were in some cases exacerbated by macroglossia, which led to difficulty with articulation. Additionally, several patients exhibited psychomotor delays, often associated with low muscle tone, and one patient, “CHOP4,” was diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder.

Alongside these observations, mild to moderate macroglossia was seen in 86.4% of the patients in the novel cohort. Recently we published a phenotypic severity index to provide greater nuance in discussions surrounding macroglossia in BWS: the BWS index of macroGlossia (BIG) (Romeo et al., 2024). This anatomic scoring system enables providers to quickly categorize patients as either having no macroglossia (BIG0), mild macroglossia (BIG1), moderate macroglossia (BIG2), or severe macroglossia (BIG3), with each category being associated with meaningful clinical outcomes. We retrospectively applied this scoring system to the present cohort of patients with SGBS and found that all with macroglossia were assigned either BIG1 or BIG2 scores, and that only one patient, “CHOP3,” with a BIG score of 2 required a tongue reduction surgery. This patient presented with a primary concern of upper airway obstruction, was referred to our plastic surgery team, and ultimately deemed a candidate for tongue reduction surgery. Our research group has previously shown that tongue reduction surgery can reliably improve sleep disordered breathing in children with macroglossia (Cielo et al., 2018; Wagner et al., 2023). The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient has already demonstrated meaningful improvement.

We also aimed to investigate the phenotypic similarities of SGBS and BWSp by calculating the BWSp clinical score for patients in our novel cohort. The BWSp clinical score, a diagnostic tool comprising of cardinal and suggestive features, was designed to assist in the diagnosis of BWSp (Brioude et al., 2018). The mean BWSp score of the novel cohort was 4.41, with all but 3 patients presenting with a cardinal feature of BWSp (a score of 4 is required for a clinical BWSp diagnosis). In total, 16 out of 22 patients satisfied criteria for a diagnosis of clinical BWSp in the cohort. The BWSp clinical scores along with the number of cardinal and suggestive features for each patient in the novel SGBS cohort are reported in Table 4.

One patient from our novel cohort initially presented with concerns for BWSp, “CHOP5.” First seen at 1 month of age, his exam was notable for macrocephaly, macroglossia, wide spaced nipples, palpable liver edge, and multiple hemangiomas. At four months, subtle lateralized overgrowth of his right leg was noted clinically, and BWSp was suspected. However, his extremities were symmetric in subsequent evaluations. In early childhood, the patient was also noted to have developmental delays as well as hypotonia, and his head circumference increased to 98th percentile. Hypertelorism and coarse facial features were also present. At the time, chromosomal studies were normal, and a definitive diagnosis could not be made. The patient presented to our clinic again at 33 years of age when his nephew, “CHOP1,” was diagnosed with SGBS. Additional genetic testing at that time showed a 428kb familial duplication on Xq26.2 that encompasses the first two exons of GPC3, confirming a diagnosis of SGBS for “CHOP5.”

This study presents a large novel cohort of patients with SGBS, allowing for further characterization of the incidence of common SGBS phenotypes – in particular cancer risk, developmental delay, and macroglossia. We contextualize these data within our institutional experience with both SGBS and BWSp to guide providers in differentiating between these common overgrowth syndromes with and without resource-intensive molecular testing. As a consequence of small sample size and limited clinical information from the literature, genotype-phenotype correlations could not be conducted but remain an area of future research.

The tumor risk in SGBS is 5.1% for Wilms tumor and 5.1% for hepatoblastoma (Tables 2 and 3), both exceeding the commonly accepted threshold for tumor screening (Brioude et al., 2018; Kalish et al., 2017). Therefore, we recommend that care plans for patients with SGBS adopt the updated screening protocols proposed for screening for Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma in patients with BWSp (Kalish et al. Clinical Cancer Research under review). These include measuring serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels every three months until the 3rd birthday, obtaining complete abdominal ultrasounds every three months until the 3rd birthday, and renal ultrasounds every three months from the 3rd to the 7th birthday (Table 5). AFP testing has the added advantage of also detecting hepatocarcinoma and pancreatoblastoma in some cases. Hasan reported a case of pancreatoblastoma in a patient with SGBS whose tumor diagnosis was aided in part by elevated AFP levels at 5 weeks of age (Hasan et al., 2024) and extremely elevated AFP levels were also noted in a patient with SGBS and hepatocarcinoma prior to confirmation of the tumor via biopsy (Lapunzina et al., 1998). These reports marked the first cases of pancreatoblastoma and hepatocarcinoma in patients with SGBS, but also underscores the potential utility of AFP screening for detection of these additional tumor types.

Speech and language delays have been previously reported in SGBS (Cottereau et al., 2013; Halayem et al., 2016; Van Borsel et al., 2008). The frequency of developmental delay in the cohort suggests the need for careful monitoring of speech and cognitive development in SGBS patients. Macroglossia in patients with SGBS may contribute to early speech delays, such as difficulty with articulation, but delays have also been reported in patients who have SGBS without macroglossia, suggesting additional contributory mechanisms (Cottereau et al., 2013). In addition, delays in fine motor skills have also been previously reported and were found in the cohort, and these delays may be associated with low muscle tone (Mariani et al., 2003; Rodriguez-Criado et al., 2005). Overall, early intervention services, including evaluations by developmental pediatricians and other provider teams such as speech, physical, and occupational therapy, can help improve outcomes for developmental delays and are imperative to a holistic treatment plan for patients with SGBS.

We report the first case of tongue reduction in a patient with SGBS, “CHOP3.” We speculate that the relatively low incidence of tongue reduction surgery in those with SGBS may be related to presence of macrostomia in these patients, which allows increased oropharyngeal space for the tongue to grow. Still, patients with SGBS and macroglossia should be monitored by a multidisciplinary team including speech pathologists, orthodontists, pulmonologists, and plastic surgeons to monitor macroglossia longitudinally. To better describe associations among macroglossia, facial anatomy, genotype, and incidence of surgery, our team is now prospectively collecting both morphometric (Romeo et al. under review) and BIG score data to conduct future studies and determine best clinical practices.

Due to the X-linked pattern of inheritance in SGBS, males are disproportionately affected compared to females. In the present study, we included a few female patients with strong clinical suspicion and molecular confirmation of SGBS. While the mechanism of GPC3 variants in causing the SGBS phenotype females is not well-characterized, our data suggests that these patients may still present with features and tumors characteristic of SGBS. The study of females with SGBS across the range of clinical features remains an area of future investigation.

Distinguishing SGBS from other overgrowth conditions like BWSp can be a challenge due to their overlapping features (Figure 2). Both conditions manifest with macroglossia, macrosomia, enlarged organs, and a predisposition to Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma (Knopp et al., 2015; Vuillaume et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019). Abdominal wall defects and polyhydramnios can occur in either syndrome. Furthermore, our data suggest that patients with SGBS often satisfy the requirement for a clinical diagnosis of BWSp via the BWSp clinical scoring system (Brioude et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019).

This phenotypic overlap can lead to potential misdiagnosis, which has been extensively reported in the literature (Higashimoto et al., 2024; Knopp et al., 2015; Punnett, 1994). The case of initial misdiagnosis in our cohort supports this trend and further highlights the overlap of features between BWSp and SGBS. Due to the shared features of both syndromes, clinical management is similar in many ways, such as screening for Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma and clinical management of macroglossia. However, careful clinical evaluation is requisite for accurate diagnosis as both BWSp and SGBS can present with unique features respective of their syndrome. Patients with SGBS often exhibit a characteristic coarse facial appearance encompassing frontal bossing, down slanting palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, anteverted nares, macrostomia, thick lips, and a high-arched or cleft palate with prognathism (Figure 1) (Brioude et al., 2019; Cottereau et al., 2013). These craniofacial features are not typically observed in BWSp. Furthermore, supernumerary nipples and developmental delays are characteristic of SGBS but are not typically seen in BWSp (Hughes-Benzie et al., 1996; Klein et al., 2023). Conversely, BWSp is frequently associated with lateralized overgrowth and some patients may exhibit prolonged hyperinsulinism, which have not been in reported in patients with SGBS (Duffy et al., 2019). Many of these unique features require targeted care and counseling that may be missed in cases of delayed diagnosis.

Obtaining accurate diagnosis of SGBS from BWSp is important due to the unique implications of the syndrome. Accurate diagnosis of SGBS can facilitate early interventions from developmental pediatricians and speech therapists for possible developmental delays that may present in these patients. Additionally, SGBS is inherited via an X-linked pattern of inheritance while BWSp is typically caused by epigenetic causes and not passed on to future offspring. Thus, genetic counseling, particularly in the context of family planning, is an important resource to offer for families with a diagnosis of SGBS.

This comprehensive review of the literature and analysis of a novel cohort highlight the diverse phenotypic spectrum present in patients with SGBS. Incidence of Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma in SGBS was identified based on available data and warrants tumor screening in these patients. Distinguishing SGBS from similar overgrowth conditions like BWSp remains a challenge without molecular diagnosis but is critical for effective clinical management, particularly regarding early intervention strategies and appropriate genetic counseling.
