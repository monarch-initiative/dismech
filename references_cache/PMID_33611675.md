# A novel splicing variant in DNAH8 causes asthenozoospermia.
**Authors:** Zhou Z, Mao X, Chen B, Mu J, Wang W, Li B, Yan Z, Dong J, Li Q, Kuang Y, Wang L, Wu L, Sang Q
**Journal:** J Assist Reprod Genet (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10815-021-02116-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10815-021-02116-1)

## Content

PURPOSE: To identify the genetic factors responsible for asthenozoospermia, which is a major cause of male infertility characterized by immotile and malformed spermatozoa.

METHODS: Whole-exome sequencing was performed in two brothers from a family with asthenozoospermia to identify pathogenic variants. The functional effect of the identified variant was investigated in HEK293T cells using a minigene assay.

RESULTS: We identified a novel homozygous splicing variant c.6311-2A>G in DNAH8 from two affected brothers belonging to the same consanguineous family. The splicing variant altered a consensus splice acceptor site of DNAH8 intron 44, which led to the deletion of exon 45 and resulted in a frameshift and a predicted truncated protein (p.G2104Efs*19). Although most spermatozoa from the patients presented with reduced sperm motility, intracytoplasmic sperm injection was able to overcome the inability of the spermatozoa to reach the ovum and thus produce a healthy child for the proband.

CONCLUSIONS: This finding expands the mutational spectrum of DNAH8, making it a potential genetic diagnostic marker for those suffering from primary male infertility.

Keywords: Male infertility, Asthenozoospermia, Variant, DNAH8

It has been estimated that 8–12% of couples worldwide are affected by infertility, and in 20–25% of these couples, the problem can be attributed to the male partner [1, 2]. Low sperm count, poor sperm motility, and abnormal sperm morphology are the most common causes related to male infertility. One of the important male deficiencies is asthenozoospermia, which is a decrease in sperm motility (total motility <40% and progressive motility <32%) that results from various genetic and environmental factors [3]. Until now, only a small number of genetic factors have clearly been proven to cause asthenozoospermia [4, 5], and there are likely to be other genetic determinants that remain to be elucidated. For some affected individuals, the application of intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) can overcome the deficiencies of asthenozoospermia and result in healthy offspring [6], while for others, such treatment is ineffective. The relationship between successful ICSI attempts and genetic factors is unclear, and thus an understanding of the underlying genetic basis of idiopathic asthenozoospermia is of great significance for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of male infertility.

Axonemal dynein motors, which can be subdivided into inner and outer dynein arms, are multiprotein ATPase complexes that interact with the peripheral microtubules in the axonemes of cilia and flagella, and ATP hydrolysis makes the microtubules slide past each other causing the cilia and flagella to move [7]. Axonemal dynein motors contain one to three non-identical heavy chains that are responsible for force production and ATPase activity. DNAH8 (MIM* 603337) encodes an outer dynein arm axonemal heavy-chain component that is expressed in sperm cells but not in ciliated airway epithelial cells [8]. Previously, a homozygous variant in DNAH8 (c.1768C>T, p.Arg590*) was identified in a patient with primary ciliary dyskinesia (MIM# 244400) [9], a genetic disease characterized by abnormal ciliary structure resulting in chronic respiratory tract infections and abnormal flagellar structure resulting in abnormal sperm motility [10], but this variant is still classified as a variant of unknown significance because its contribution to primary ciliary dyskinesia has not been confirmed. Recently, it has been reported that bi-allelic variants in DNAH8 cause the complete or almost complete absence of DNAH8 proteins in the spermatozoa, which is responsible for multiple morphological abnormalities of the flagella, a severe form of asthenozoospermia [11, 12]. These previous studies show that there is a causal relationship between DNAH8 variants and asthenozoospermia.

In our present work, we identified a homozygous splicing variant c.6311-2A>G in two patients with idiopathic male infertility from a consanguineous family. The splicing variant might be responsible for male infertility because it likely disrupts the in vivo processing of axonemal dynein motors. This finding expands the spectrum of pathogenic DNAH8 variants that are responsible for asthenozoospermia and male infertility.

The two affected brothers diagnosed with asthenozoospermia from a consanguineous family were recruited from the Ninth Hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University. We obtained informed consent from all human subjects prior to the onset of the study, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Medical College of Fudan University and the Reproductive Study Ethics Committee of the hospital.

Genomic DNA samples were extracted from peripheral blood using standard methods, and whole-exome sequencing was performed to detect new rare DNAH8 variants according to GRCh37. Homozygosity mapping was carried out with HomozygosityMapper (http://www.homozygositymapper.org) after obtaining the exome data in consanguineous families. All candidate variants in DNAH8 were prioritized based on the following filtering criteria: (1) a minor allele frequency of the variants of less than 1% in the Exome Aggregation Consortium database (ExAC, http://exac.broadinstitute.org/) and the genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD, http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org) as well as in our in-house exome database; (2) in silico prediction to be damaging. The candidate variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing of the affected proband as well as his affected brother using the sequencing primers shown in Supplemental Table 1.

The c.6311-2A>G variant was located at the acceptor splice-site of intron 44. Due to the large size of intron 44 and intron 45, we were unable to clone the full sequence from exon 44 to exon 46 into the minigene vector. Thus, we integrated exon 44, exon 45, exon 46, and the sequences 200 bp before and after every exon by PCR and cloned the integrated fragment into a modified pcDNA3 plasmid. The control and c.6311-2A>G plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells. After incubation for 36 h, total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and cDNA was obtained with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Osaka, Japan). The primers for constructing the minigene vectors and for detecting alternative splice sites are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

The affected siblings in consanguineous family 1 had been diagnosed with asthenozoospermia and primary infertility for several years, characterized by abnormal sperm morphology and motility. Normal PB1 oocytes could be retrieved from their female partners, but they all suffered from recurrent early embryonic arrest in ICSI attempts. The family pedigree is shown in Fig. 1a, and the clinical characteristics of the proband’s semen are summarized in Table 1. Proband II-1 from family 1 was 33 years old and had undergone seven ICSI attempts. Seventeen ovulated oocytes were retrieved from his female partner in these seven cycles (Table 2), and all of these were fertilized normally. These zygotes with 2PN cleaved normally, but 15 were arrested at the 4–7 cell stage after cultivation for 3 days. Only two morphologically normal embryos were acquired and frozen, leading to a successful pregnancy after implantation. Individual II-1 had one younger brother II-3 who suffered from male infertility but had a child through assisted reproductive technology, but the specific clinical information for his brother was not available.

The two affected individuals in family 1 underwent whole-exome sequencing. A recessive model of inheritance for variant filtering was applied because of the consanguineous family history, and homozygosity analysis was performed to establish the homozygous regions (Fig. 1b). Based on the criteria described in the methods, we identified a homozygous splicing variant c.6311-2A>G in DNAH8, which altered a consensus splice acceptor site of DNAH8 intron 44. The DNAH8 homozygous variant was subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing of DNA samples from the two affected individuals (Fig. 1a). The minor allele frequency of variant c.6311-2A>G was 3.19 × 10–5 in the gnomAD database, and the MutationTaster software (http://mutationtaster.org/MutationTaster/) predicted that it might be disease causing (Table 3).

The residue affected by the splicing variant is conserved among different species (Fig. 2b), but no mRNA analysis or immunostaining could be performed on sperm cells from the patients due to the lack of biological samples. In order to study the effect of the splicing variant c.6311-2A>G, a minigene assay was performed. Agarose gel electrophoresis showed a different size band for c.6311-2A>G DNAH8 compared with wild type, indicating the abnormal splicing isoform of this variant (Fig. 2c). The cDNA sequence analysis of the band in the c.6311-2A>G lane is shown in Fig. 2d. We found that the splicing variant led to the deletion of exon 45, which resulted in a frameshift and a predicted truncated protein (p.G2104Efs*19) (Fig. 2d). The parental samples were not available for family 1, and thus the inheritance pattern of family 1 was unclear.

In this study, we identified one novel homozygous splicing variant c.6311-2A>G in DNAH8 in a consanguineous family suffering from male infertility. The patients from the family shared similar infertility phenotypes, including abnormal sperm morphology and motility. The c.6311-2A>G splicing variant was verified in vitro to affect the normal alternative splicing of intron 44, thus leading to aberrant DNAH8 transcripts (Fig. 2c and d). We assume, therefore, that DNAH8 function was lost in family 1.

Previous studies have suggested that affected men harboring bi-allelic DNAH8 variants have diminished sperm motility and malformed sperm flagella [11]. In this study, the patient carrying the homozygous splicing variant c.6311-2A>G also exhibited poor sperm motility (Table 1). However, this patient showed a high percentage of sperm with abnormal heads rather than tail defects (Table 1). Such phenotypic variability might depend on the different effects of DNAH8 variants. In addition, it was reported that ICSI treatment could help infertile Dnah8-knockout male mice obtain healthy pups [11]. In our results, the proband underwent seven ICSI attempts characterized by early embryonic arrest with only two viable embryos obtained, which led to a successful pregnancy. It is worth noting that the DNAH8 variant caused early embryonic arrest even though fertilization failure could be overcome by ICSI treatment. These results suggest that DNAH8 might play an essential role in early embryonic development.

There are limitations to this study. First, because of the lack of parental samples, the inheritance pattern for the two affected individuals is not clear. Second, detailed morphology of the spermatozoa, the localization of DNAH8 in the spermatozoa, and the ultrastructural defects in the sperm flagella from the men harboring the homozygous DNAH8 variant are not available due to the lack of frozen semen samples.

In conclusion, we have identified a novel homozygous variant in DNAH8, which is an important gene related to sperm flagella formation. This finding confirms the previous study suggesting an essential role for DNAH8 in sperm morphology and motility, and we have expanded the mutational spectrum of DNAH8, which might be a potential genetic diagnostic marker for those suffering from asthenozoospermia and recurrent ICSI failure.
