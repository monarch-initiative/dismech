# Inhaled granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (molgramostim) as host-directed therapy for pneumonia-related acute respiratory distress syndrome: a multicentre, randomised, placebo-controlled phase 2a trial (GI-HOPE).
**Authors:** Herold S, Welte T, Zacharowski K, Meybohm P, Bauer M, Wilhelm J, Walmrath HD, Tello K, Vadász I, Hecker M, Malainou C, Kuznetsova I, Matt U, Trauth J, Seeger W, Lohmeyer J
**Journal:** ERJ Open Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1183/23120541.01795-2025](https://doi.org/10.1183/23120541.01795-2025)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) enhances pulmonary host defence and promotes re-establishment of alveolar barrier function. We investigated the safety, feasibility and efficacy of nebulised recombinant human (rh)GM-CSF (molgramostim) in pneumonia-related acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

METHODS: In this multicentre, randomised, double-blind, parallel-group, placebo-controlled, investigator-initiated phase 2a trial, patients were randomised to receive nebulised low-dose (150 μg) or high-dose (450 μg) rhGM-CSF or placebo for 3 days. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed before the first dose and after dosing. The primary outcome parameter was the composite GI-HOPE score, representing expression changes of CD80, CD86, CD206 and human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR on alveolar macrophages after dosing compared to baseline. Secondary outcomes included oxygenation, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores and clinical end-points at day 28.

RESULTS: 46 participants were randomised, 43 completed treatment (n=15 placebo, n=16 low dose and n=12 high dose), and BAL was performed on 38 participants before and after treatment. Although the composite biological GI-HOPE score did not reach significance (p>0.05), high-dose treatment caused upregulation of HLA-DR and CD206 on alveolar macrophages, indicating deposition in the alveolar compartment and beneficial macrophage activation (HLA-DR: p=0.0406 versus low dose, p=0.1841 versus placebo; CD206: p=0.009 versus low dose, p=0.0179 versus placebo). High-dose rhGM-CSF treatment revealed a favourable profile of oxygenation at the end of analysis compared to time-points before inhalation (p=0.095 and p=0.0175) or compared to the low-dose and placebo groups (p=0.0063 and p=0.0198; visit 13), and was associated with decreased SOFA scores in the high-dose group over time (p=0.0013 and p=0.00023 compared to low dose and placebo). Importantly, inhaled rhGM-CSF did not increase alveolar or systemic inflammation.

CONCLUSION: Inhalation of 450 μg rhGM-CSF was safe, and promoted a favourable profile regarding alveolar macrophage activation, oxygenation and SOFA scores over time; however, it did not lead to differences in the GI-HOPE score.

Severe viral and bacterial pneumonia may progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), characterised by a dysbalanced pulmonary inflammation and damage to the alveolar epithelium with loss of barrier integrity, resulting in lung oedema evidenced by bilateral infiltrates on chest imaging not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload [1]. Depending on oxygenation, ARDS is defined as mild (arterial oxygen tension (PaO2)/inspiratory oxygen fraction (FIO2) 200– ≤300 mmHg), moderate (PaO2/FIO2 100– ≤200 mmHg) and severe (PaO2/FIO2 <100 mmHg) [2]. According to the LUNG-SAFE study, 28-day mortality is 35% and increases with the severity of hypoxaemia, with rates >40% in severe ARDS [1]. Despite advancements in our understanding of ARDS pathophysiology and the establishment of subphenotypes by plasma biomarkers with different clinical outcomes and intervention responses [3], effective targeted treatment strategies are still missing. Given the compartmentalised inflammation and injury observed particularly in pneumonia-related ARDS (p-ARDS) [4], local delivery of host-targeted therapies by inhalation or nebulisation represents a valid strategy to increase local drug concentrations and efficacy while minimising systemic side-effects.

One such therapeutic avenue is the use of inhaled recombinant human (rh)GM-CSF, a cytokine with multifaceted roles in regulating the immune response. While GM-CSF has been explored in other clinical contexts, its application in p-ARDS offers a particularly intriguing prospect. The rationale behind this approach lies in its ability to beneficially modulate the pulmonary immune environment by promoting innate and adaptive lung immunity through increased phagocytosis, antibacterial, antiviral and antifungal defence in pre-clinical models and patients [5–12], and by mitigating uncontrolled inflammation, e.g. by supporting neutrophil efferocytosis [13, 14], while simultaneously driving lung repair by a direct targeting of the alveolar epithelium to restore barrier function [15]. It is released from the alveolar epithelium to provide the instructive signal that differentiates monocytes into homeostatic tissue-resident alveolar macrophages (TR-AMs) within the alveoli in the first days of life [16–19] and is required for differentiation of recruited bone marrow-derived monocytes/macrophages (BMDMs) into homeostatic TR-AMs after their depletion upon severe inflammatory insults, to re-establish local host defence and return to alveolar homeostasis [20]. This process is of utmost importance to resolve inflammation and to repair the injured lung [16, 21–23]. Furthermore, GM-CSF instructs a macrophage subset that supports euplastic repair of the alveolar epithelium after virus-induced lung injury [24]. In COVID-19 pneumonia, GM-CSF suppressed alveolar hyperinflammation via differentiation of BMDMs into TR-AMs [12].

Alveolar macrophage phenotypes and functions are related to defined expression patterns of surface molecules. CD206 (the mannose receptor) has been identified as a marker for differentiated TR-AMs [25] and upregulation indicates differentiation from recruited, more pro-inflammatory BMDMs to homeostatic TR-AMs [12, 18]. CD206 recognises mannose from pathogen surfaces, aiding their clearance, is increased after direct GM-CSF stimulation in macrophages, and was shown to act in an anti-inflammatory manner by actively degrading inflammatory proteins and internalising myeloperoxidase [26–30]. Human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR is another well-known GM-CSF response marker and mediates antigen presentation to T-cells. Low expression of HLA-DR on monocytes/macrophages is considered to reflect impaired local and systemic host defence, and was associated with aberrant, pro-inflammatory activation in COVID-19 and with compensatory anti-inflammatory response syndrome-related immunosuppression in sepsis patients [31–33]. CD80 and CD86 are alveolar macrophage-expressed co-stimulatory molecules that present antigen and indicate pro-inflammatory activation [34, 35]. Given our own data [36] and data from others on the putative beneficial potential of inhaled rhGM-CSF in pneumonia and ARDS [12], we sought to test whether adjunct nebulised molgramostim (Molgradex) is safe, targets alveolar macrophages and might improve clinical parameters in patients with p-ARDS.

Eligible patients had a diagnosis of p-ARDS (according to the 2012 Berlin definition [2]) and requirement for positive pressure ventilation for >72 h in total with FIO2 ≥50% (or less when on additional extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) therapy) and not longer than 14 days, and were 18–75 years of age, inclusive. Underlying pneumonia (bacterial or viral) was diagnosed based on clinical data (history, vital signs, laboratory parameters and chest radiography/computed tomography scan) with or without pathogen detection from sputa, tracheal aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). Detailed eligibility criteria are listed in table 1.

GI-HOPE was a randomised, double-blind, parallel-group, multicentre, phase 2a pilot study comparing inhaled molgramostim (high dose and low dose) versus placebo, conducted at five centres across Germany (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02595060). The trial was approved by the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (Bundesinstitut für Arzneimittel und Medizinprodukte (BfArM)) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines, with an independent data monitoring board review after a safety run-in phase (further details are provided in the supplementary material). The enrolment span from July 2016 to February 2021, and included several recruitment pauses due to a data and safety monitoring board evaluation after the safety run-in phase and the sequential inclusion of further study sites, sponsor changes and several protocol amendments. Clinical practices and standards of care, however, had not changed during enrolment. After informed consent was obtained, eligible patients were randomised in a 1:1:1 ratio (according to a randomisation list that was computer generated by an independent contract research organisation) to receive either placebo, 150 μg molgramostim or 450 μg molgramostim using in-line PARI eFlow vibrating mesh nebulisers (PARI, Starnberg, Germany) at standardised ventilator settings during a nebulisation period of 2–3 min once per day for 3 consecutive days. BAL was performed according to predefined standard operating procedures 1 day before and 1 (or 2) days after the dosing. Serum samples were additionally taken 24 h before the first dose and 24 h after the last dose, and 60 min before and 120 min after each dosing, to assess systemic GM-CSF levels. The trial design scheme is provided in figure 2a.

Trial outcomes included safety and efficacy regarding fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-based analysis of expression of host defence parameters HLA-DR, CD80 and CD86, and of the TR-AM homeostasis marker CD206 on BALF macrophages in response to treatment (analysed for each single marker and within a composite score, the GI-HOPE score, as a surrogate marker and primary end-point). The GI-HOPE score was calculated by adding up log10 values of the x-fold (post- versus pre-treatment) increase in median fluorescence intensities (MFIs) of single host defence markers pre- and post-treatment (visit 6 versus visit 2) according to protocol. Safety data (adverse events, vital signs, routine laboratory clinical chemistry, haematology and haemostasis parameters) including local and systemic inflammatory state (leukocyte subsets and cytokines in BALF, C-reactive protein, and differential white blood counts) were analysed daily. Secondary outcomes included oxygenation (PaO2/FIO2), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II scores, and GM-CSF levels in serum. The protocol included analysis of Extravascular Lung Water Index (ELWI) using the PiCCO (pulse index continuous cardiac output) system (Getinge, Rastatt, Germany) that allows monitoring in patients without extracorporeal circulation device only (continuous venovenous hemofiltration, ECMO), excluding ELWI analysis in the majority of patients. Concomitant medication was documented at each visit. Days alive, days on ventilator/ECMO, days on vasoactive drugs and all-cause mortality were monitored over a 28-day period (supplementary table S2). All other aspects of care were provided according to general standards of critical care, which did not change throughout the recruitment period. Project management, monitoring and the trial statistician were provided by the Coordinating Center for Clinical Trials (Koordinierungszentrum für Klinische Studien (KKS)) of the University of Giessen (Giessen, Germany).

Sample size and power calculations were based on the analysis of the primary efficacy end-point and previous analyses on a similar cohort [36]. The sample size was determined with PASS 2008 sample size and power software (www.ncss.com), revealing a minimal group size of n=15 patients for the likely more efficient high-dose group (power of 80% at a two-sided significance level of 2.5% to detect a difference in means of 25%). The primary efficacy analysis is based on the per-protocol population. The GI-HOPE score was analysed by a two-sample Mann–Whitney test at a two-sided significance level of 2.5%, to detect significant differences in the high-dose or low-dose group compared to placebo, according to the protocol, with Bonferroni correction. Data were analysed in Prism 10 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA), and the applied statistical tests are provided in the figure legends. For treatment group comparisons, one- or two-way ANOVA with Tukey's honestly significant difference were performed. Intraindividual pre- and post-treatment effects were analysed by the Wilcoxon paired signed-rank test, or paired t-test in normally distributed data. Survival analyses were analysed using the Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test. SOFA score progressions were analysed and compared by a linear mixed model using R version 4.1.1 (www.r-project.org). Linear mixed models were fit with the package lmerTest [37] with visit number (numeric) and treatment group (categorical, three levels) as well as their interaction as predictor variables and a random intercept for subject. The statistical significance of the time×treatment interaction was assessed with type III ANOVA with Satterthwaite's method. Residual diagnostic plots did not reveal any relevant violations of the model assumptions (linearity, homoscedastic and normal distributed residuals). Detailed information on handling of missing data is provided in the supplementary material. Outcomes were analysed by summary measures of the empirical distribution (mean with standard deviation, minimum–maximum and median (interquartile range)) for comparison between groups. Data are provided for the modified intention-to-treat population.

458 patients were screened for eligibility between July 2016 to February 2021, of whom 412 were excluded (of these, 22 patients died before study start, 15 had a do-not-resuscitate order, 362 did not meet inclusion or met exclusion criteria and 13 were excluded for other reasons) (figure 1). Eligibility criteria are detailed in table 1. 46 patients were randomised, with 15, 17 and 14 allocated to the placebo, low-dose and high-dose treatment groups, respectively. Out of these, three were never treated, resulting in a modified intention-to-treat population of 43 patients (15, 12 and 16, respectively). One participant did not receive complete treatment, resulting in a per-protocol analysis population of 42 patients (figure 1). Baseline patient characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, weight, vital signs and laboratory parameters) were similar in the treatment groups. The majority of patients (33/43 (77%)) had virus-induced ARDS (severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 or influenza A virus), and a bacterial pathogen (Streptococcus pneumoniae or Legionella pneumophila) was detected in only 3/43 (7%) patients. No pathogen was documented in 7/43 patients (16%) (table 2). All patients depended on invasive ventilation, and 29/43 (67.4%) patients needed additional ECMO therapy at baseline (34/43 (79.1%) throughout the observation period). Upon inclusion, 1/43 (2.3%) patients was classified as mild ARDS, 10/43 (23.3%) patients were classified as moderate ARDS and 32/43 (74.4%) patients were classified as severe ARDS according to oxygenation [2]. SOFA and APACHE II scores reflected high disease severity, without significant differences in the three treatment groups at baseline (table 3). Clinical outcomes at visit 14 are reported at individual and aggregated level in supplementary table S4a and b, with the study being underpowered for detecting significant differences in these parameters. At visit 14, 63% (27/43) of patients were alive, 16% (7/43) were weaned, 58% (25/43) were without vasoactive therapy and 16% (7/43) were discharged from the intensive care unit (ICU). We did not detect significant differences in overall survival, days alive, days on ventilator, days in the ICU and days on vasoactive treatment between treatment groups. Supplementary figure S3 depicts survival probabilities according to treatment in the modified intention-to-treat population (supplementary figure S3a) and in the same population excluding n=4 patients with death reported unrelated to p-ARDS (supplementary figure S3b).

There were no significant differences in primary or secondary end-point analyses when data were stratified post hoc according to diagnosis of COVID-19-related ARDS versus p-ARDS due to other or unknown pathogens (not shown).

We recorded a total of 269 adverse events: 109 (41%) in the placebo group, 76 (28%) in the low-dose group and 84 (31%) in the high-dose group. There were no significant differences in the overall frequencies of events or at the level of MedDRA (Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities) System Organ Classes (table 4). 33 serious adverse events were documented: 13 in the placebo group (n=4 moderate and n=9 severe), 11 in the low-dose group (n=1 moderate and n=10 severe) and nine in the high-dose group (n=9 severe), with similar incidences in the treatment groups (details listed in supplementary table S5a). No serious adverse events were reported related to the study drug.

Given that systemically applied GM-CSF has been in clinical use to mobilise myeloid cells from bone marrow in neutropenic patients (Leucomax) and that endogenous GM-CSF has been ascribed pro-inflammatory roles, e.g. in COVID-19 infection [38], which might cause side-effects, we particularly addressed alveolar and systemic inflammation in response to study drug. No significant changes in the proportion of lymphocytes, neutrophils or macrophages in BALF in response to study drug were observed at visit 6 compared to visit 2 (supplementary figure S4a). Cytokine quantification in BALF revealed significant reduction of interferon (IFN)-γ-induced protein 10 (IP-10; CXCL10) concentrations in all three treatment groups at visit 6, whereas interleukin (IL)-6 and CCL2 concentrations were only reduced in the high-dose treatment group, suggesting a beneficial effect of GM-CSF with respect to cytokines that were recently reported to be associated with severity and worse outcome of p-ARDS, particularly in COVID-19 (supplementary figure S4b) [39, 40]. Further BALF cytokines analysed (IL-1β, IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra), CCL5, IL-8, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IL-18, CCL3, IL-10 and IFN-λ2) did not differ between treatment groups or between visits, and type I IFNs (IFN-α and IFN-β) were not detectable at the indicated visits (supplementary figure S4c).

We detected a minor and transient spill-over of inhaled GM-CSF into the systemic circulation (serum) in the high-dose group at 2 h post-inhalation compared to 1 h before inhalation at dosing days (mean±sem: 16.8±4.2 pg·mL−1 at visit 3, 16.3±3.7 pg·mL−1 at visit 4 and 12.1±3.3 pg·mL−1 at visit 5), which was no longer detectable at 21 h later (supplementary figure S5a). We recorded parameters of systemic inflammation (total leukocyte and differential counts in peripheral blood, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin) besides standard laboratory parameters daily from visit 2 to visit 13 and did not reveal any significant changes between placebo and verum groups, nor any trend towards organ toxicity. Total leukocyte counts and C-reactive protein are depicted in supplementary figure S5b and c, and selected laboratory parameters (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, γ-glutamyl transpeptidase, lactate dehydrogenase, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, creatinine, urea nitrogen, creatine kinase, haemoglobin, differential white blood cell and platelet counts, and prothrombin time) are listed in supplementary table S5b. Accordingly, we did not observe any significant increase of selected serum cytokines CCL2, CCL3, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-1ra, IP-10 and TNF-α in response to GM-CSF inhalation in the post- versus pre-dosing analysis. IFN-α, IFN-β, IFN-λ2, and IL-1β were not detected at the given time-points, and CCL5 and IP-10 concentrations were significantly reduced at visit 6 compared to visit 2 in high-dosed patients (supplementary figure S5d). Our data indicate that GM-CSF inhalation in p-ARDS patients at the indicated dosing regimens did not cause organ toxicity or increase parameters of alveolar or systemic inflammation.

We provide evidence that rhGM-CSF (molgramostim) applied via inhalation is a safe and well-tolerated adjunctive host-directed therapy in patients with p-ARDS, in our study predominantly classified as severe. Although the primary end-point (significant increase in the GI-HOPE score at visit 6 versus visit 2 in treated patients versus placebo) was not met, we revealed that molgramostim applied once daily over 3 days dose-dependently targeted the alveolar macrophage compartment to induce increased HLA-DR and CD206 expression, and improved oxygenation as well as SOFA scores over time, with a marked difference in outcomes between the high-dose (450 µg) and low-dose (150 µg) groups. Although homogeneous application to distal lung areas might be difficult to achieve in ventilated ARDS patients and remains challenging, we chose this route of application as we had demonstrated that epithelial- but not myeloid-expressed GM-CSF protects the lung in viral lung injury, and that GM-CSF-deficient mice that at the same time specifically overexpress GM-CSF in the alveolar epithelium are highly protected from infection and lung damage, suggesting high GM-CSF concentrations in the alveolar lining fluid driving protection [9, 15]. As such, rhGM-CSF was administered directly into the lung via ultrasonic nebulisation, after careful adjustments of ventilator settings, to direct the drug to the site where endogenous GM-CSF acts to defend alveolar integrity. Our previous data further suggested that GM-CSF might be particularly beneficial in situations where endogenous release from alveolar epithelial cells is compromised due to lung damage [12, 15, 41]. Correspondingly, we detected only very low concentrations of (endogenous) GM-CSF in BALF in our cohort at pre-treatment (visit 2; median: 14.0 pg·mL−1; range: 2.2–36.4 pg·mL−1), with median levels post-treatment (visit 6) ranging at 6.0 pg·mL−1 in the placebo group, 48.2 pg·mL−1 in the low-dose group and 299.6 pg·mL−1 in the high-dose group (data not shown). Furthermore, inhalative administration was considered to limit systemic exposure to the drug, potentially reducing side-effects, and would be easy to administer in outpatient settings.

GM-CSF has been widely recognised as a pro-inflammatory cytokine that might even worsen the patient's inflammatory state; however, our data clearly challenge this paradigm. In our study, inhaled GM-CSF was associated with transient and minor elevations in plasma in the high-dose group, yet these effects were not accompanied by signs of increased systemic inflammation, nor did inhalative deposition cause alveolar inflammation, as evidenced by serum and BALF cytokine analyses, C-reactive protein, and leukocyte differential counts in BALF and blood. High-dose GM-CSF even decreased IL-6 and CCL2 levels in BALF. Accordingly, concerns that GM-CSF may cause or accelerate hyperinflammation motivated phase 2 clinical studies of neutralising endogenous GM-CSF in COVID-19 patients; however, gimsilumab or otilimab did not improve patient outcomes [42, 43]. Tazawa
et al. [44] and Trapnell
et al. [45, 46] reported benefits and safe application of inhaled molgramostim or sargramostim in patients with pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, with daily inhalations of 300 µg molgramostim or 250 µg sargramostim even upon long-term application of 48 weeks.

Several recent studies add to our findings and highlight rhGM-CSF's (sargramostim) capacity to improve gas exchange and support alveolar macrophage function in hypoxaemic COVID-19, with a beneficial safety profile [12, 47]. This suggests a compartmentalised role of lung-deposited GM-CSF in the alveoli, consistent with its known function in re-establishment of epithelial integrity at mucosal and alveolar surfaces [15, 48], as well as in maintaining and restoring alveolar macrophage functions and tissue-homeostatic phenotype [21], the latter supported by our finding that high-dose rhGM-CSF increased the expression of CD206 on alveolar macrophages. Increased mannose receptor expression is not only associated with improved pathogen clearance, but also with an inflammation resolution macrophage phenotype [26–30]. Notably, we recently identified that CD206high but not CD206low alveolar macrophages drive lung epithelial repair after influenza virus-induced lung injury [49].

Despite the encouraging signals observed in the high-dose group, our study has several limitations. First, a non-clinical surrogate parameter (GI-HOPE score) is certainly far less predictive of a therapy response compared to a clinical end-point, which would have required a much higher number of patients. Our rationale to include a composite biological response score as the primary efficacy parameter was to 1) evaluate whether the drug reached the target compartment (alveolus) by inhaled application in ARDS patients, i.e. caused a local quantifiable cellular response, and 2) if so, to analyse whether it causes beneficial reprogramming of macrophages. Second, a notable proportion of patients in this treatment group were found to be non-responders with regard to macrophage marker change and oxygenation improvement, suggesting pre-selection of GM-CSF responders by suitable biomarkers as a future strategy. In this context, HLA-DR, a classical GM-CSF response gene downregulated on monocytes in patients with dysregulated inflammation or infection [31–33], might be of interest. Given that rhGM-CSF restored reduced HLA-DR expression in post-surgery patients and sepsis-associated immunosuppression [32, 50], and that patients with a HLA-DR increase on alveolar macrophages revealed a trend towards improved survival (p=0.17) in our pilot trial, HLA-DR expression on mononuclear phagocytes might become a clinically useful biomarker to stratify patients that particularly benefit from rhGM-CSF inhalation in pneumonia and ARDS. Moreover, due to the inclusion criteria (being ventilated for >72 h with FIO2 ≥50% despite standard of care), and recruitment during the COVID-19 pandemic with many patients suffering from severe ARDS and requiring ECMO, our study population comprised predominantly patients with advanced respiratory failure and high mortality risk, which may have limited the potential to observe improvements in clinical outcomes within the 28-day period, and reduces the generalisability of the findings. These factors underscore the need for well-defined stratification approaches and possibly earlier, pre-emptive interventions aimed at preventing ARDS progression in pneumonia in follow-up trials.

In conclusion, our findings support further investigation of inhaled rhGM-CSF as host-directed therapy in pneumonia and ARDS. Beneficial safety profiles in our and other studies on inhaled rhGM-CSF even in patients with severe lung infection and damage will allow for prolonged dosing regimen, which might increase its efficacy. Optimising application schemes and patient selection will be key to exploit its therapeutic potential in larger follow-up trials, and to demonstrate efficacy in clinical end-points.
