OBJECTIVE: Body dysmorphic disorder is common, distressing, and often severely 
impairing. Serotonin reuptake inhibitors appear efficacious, but the few 
existing pharmacotherapy studies were short term (≤4 months), and no relapse 
prevention studies or continuation phase studies have been conducted to the 
authors' knowledge. The authors report results from the first relapse prevention 
study in body dysmorphic disorder.
METHOD: Adults (N=100) with DSM-IV body dysmorphic disorder received open-label 
escitalopram for 14 weeks (phase 1); 58 responders were then randomized to 
double-blind continuation treatment with escitalopram versus switch to placebo 
for 6 months (phase 2). Reliable and valid outcome measures were utilized.
RESULTS: In phase 1, 67.0% of treated subjects and 81.1% of subjects who 
completed phase 1 responded to escitalopram. Body dysmorphic disorder severity 
(in both the intent-to-treat and the completer groups) and insight, depressive 
symptoms, psychosocial functioning, and quality of life significantly improved 
from baseline to end of phase 1. In phase 2, time to relapse was significantly 
longer with escitalopram than with placebo treatment (hazard ratio=2.72, 95% 
CI=1.01-8.57). Phase 2 relapse proportions were 18% for escitalopram and 40% for 
placebo. Among escitalopram-treated subjects, body dysmorphic disorder severity 
significantly decreased over time during the continuation phase, with 35.7% of 
subjects showing further improvement. There were no significant group 
differences in body dysmorphic disorder severity or insight, depressive 
symptoms, psychosocial functioning, or quality of life.
CONCLUSIONS: Continuation-phase escitalopram delayed time to relapse, and fewer 
escitalopram-treated subjects relapsed than did placebo-treated subjects. Body 
dysmorphic disorder severity significantly improved during 6 additional months 
of escitalopram treatment following acute response; more than one-third of 
escitalopram-treated subjects experienced further improvement.

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), an often-severe disorder, consists of distressing or impairing preoccupations with nonexistent or slight defects in appearance (e.g."scarred” skin"thinning” hair) plus repetitive behaviors performed in response to appearance concerns (e.g., mirror checking, excessive grooming). DSM-5 classifies BDD as an Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorder. Nationwide epidemiologic studies indicate a current prevalence of 1.7%–2.4% (1–4). BDD usually causes substantial impairment in social and academic/occupational functioning (5) and is associated with high rates of suicidality (6).

However, very few BDD pharmacotherapy studies have been conducted, and all contained relatively small samples (in the largest study, only 34 patients received active medication [7]). Controlled monotherapy studies are limited to one double-blind placebo-controlled trial (N=67) in which fluoxetine was more efficacious than placebo (7), and a blinded cross-over trial (N=29) in which the SRI clomipramine was more efficacious than desipramine (8). Four methodologically rigorous open-label trials (N=15–30) also found that SRIs are often efficacious for BDD (9–12). Two particularly notable gaps in the BDD pharmacotherapy literature are: 1) no prospective relapse prevention studies have examined risk of relapse following discontinuation of efficacious medication, and 2) no studies have examined medication response during the continuation phase (following acute treatment); all pharmacotherapy studies have been acute-phase studies lasting only two to four months. Such studies are needed because many patients with BDD receive pharmacotherapy (most often, SRIs) (13), and because BDD is often chronic, necessitating longer-term treatment (13).

Two studies that examined relapse after treatment with an SRI were limited by small sample sizes, lack of a placebo arm, unblinded evaluation of treatment outcome, and imprecise definition of relapse (using only the Clinical Global Impressions Scale [CGI] [14]). In one – a small chart-review study from a clinical practice – 83% (N=31) of patients who discontinued an effective SRI experienced subsequent relapse of BDD (15). In the other – a 16-week open-label fluvoxamine study – six responders who were subsequently treated in a clinical practice discontinued fluvoxamine; all six patients relapsed within four days to about two months (11). In both studies, subjects were assessed only at clinic visits, thus precluding precise assessment of time to relapse, and no secondary outcomes were assessed.

Here we report results from the first prospective pharmacotherapy relapse prevention study in BDD. In Phase 1, subjects were treated with 14 weeks of acute open-label escitalopram and assessed for treatment response. Responders were then randomized in Phase 2 to continue treatment with escitalopram versus switch to placebo, and were followed under double-blind conditions for six months. The study's primary aim was to compare time to relapse (and relapse rates) in Phase 2; we hypothesized that escitalopram responders who continued escitalopram for six additional months would have a longer time to relapse and a lower relapse rate than subjects switched to placebo. Secondary/exploratory hypotheses pertaining to Phase 2 were: 1) Subjects randomized to six months of continuation escitalopram treatment would perform better than placebo-treated subjects on measures of BDD-related insight/delusionality, depressive symptoms, psychosocial functioning, and quality of life; and 2) BDD symptoms would significantly improve with continued escitalopram treatment over six additional months.

Phase 1 (acute open-label phase) outcomes were also of interest, because few BDD pharmacotherapy studies have been done, and all contained relatively small samples (7–12). Furthermore, no study has examined BDD remission (as opposed to response/improvement) with pharmacotherapy. Phase 1 hypotheses were: 1) BDD symptoms, BDD-related insight, depressive symptoms, functioning, and quality of life would significantly improve after 14 weeks of open-label escitalopram; and 2) Subjects with delusional BDD would be as likely as those with nondelusional BDD beliefs to respond to escitalopram (we also explored additional variables as predictors of response to acute open-label escitalopram treatment).

This outpatient study was conducted at two academic sites: Butler Hospital (subsequently Rhode Island Hospital)/Brown University and Massachusetts General Hospital. Inclusion criteria were age ≥18; DSM-IV BDD (including delusional BDD) for ≥6 months; baseline total score ≥24 on the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale Modified for BDD (BDD-YBOCS) (16); score of at least moderate on the CGI Severity scale (14); ability to communicate meaningfully with the investigators; and competency to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were any of the following: current clinically significant suicidality or a suicide attempt within the past year; need for inpatient or partial hospital treatment; current or past DSM-IV bipolar disorder or psychotic disorder; DSM-IV alcohol or drug dependence or abuse in the past three months or a positive urine drug screen for any illicit substances of abuse; mental retardation, dementia, brain damage, or other cognitive impairment that would interfere with study participation; body image concerns accounted for primarily by an eating disorder) or weight concerns (i.e., BDD criteria not met if weight concerns were excluded); use of psychotropic medication or other herbal preparations with alleged behavioral effects during the study for two weeks (six weeks for fluoxetine) before the baseline assessment; use of any investigational medication within three months or a depot neuroleptic within six months of baseline; previous allergic reaction to escitalopram or citalopram; current cognitive-behavioral therapy; presence of significant and/or unstable medical illness; history of a seizure disorder; females who were pregnant, breast-feeding, or sexually active and not using adequate contraception; and presence of behavior that would interfere with cooperation with the protocol.

The protocol and informed consent documents were approved by the hospitals’ institutional review boards. After complete description of the study to the subjects, written informed consent was obtained. Oversight was provided by an independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board.

During screening, psychiatric and medical histories (using the BDD Form [e.g., 7] and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Patient Version [SCID-P and SCID-II] [17–19]), a urine pregnancy test, and urine toxic screen were obtained.

Phase 1: Participants received open-label escitalopram monotherapy for 14 weeks: 10 mg/day for weeks 1–3, 20 mg/day for weeks 4–6, and 30 mg/day thereafter. A lower dose was allowed to improve tolerability. Phase 1 assessments (below) were conducted at baseline, weekly during weeks 1–4, and biweekly during weeks 6–14. Escitalopram response was defined as ≥30% reduction in BDD-YBOCS score from baseline that persisted for at least two consecutive assessments and through the end of Phase 1 (or last visit, for early terminators).

Phase 2: Nonresponders were removed from the study at the end of Phase 1. Subjects who completed Phase I, responded to escitalopram, and agreed to continue study participation entered Phase 2. Phase 2 participants were randomly assigned to six additional months of treatment with: 1) continuation monotherapy with escitalopram, or 2) discontinuation of escitalopram (via tapering by 10 mg/day a week) and substitution with pill placebo. Randomization was stratified by BDD-related insight and presence of major depressive disorder. Phase 2 assessments were conducted at the randomization visit and biweekly thereafter through study week 40.

Throughout Phase 2, subjects randomized to continuation therapy received the escitalopram dose prescribed at the end of Phase 1. However, those taking only 10–20 mg/day at the end of Phase 1 could increase to 30 mg/day during Phase 2 if BDD symptoms worsened before relapse; conversely, if problematic side effects occurred at the 30 mg/day dosage, the dose could be lowered to 20 mg/day.

Relapse of BDD was defined as: 1) 50% or greater loss of pre-randomization BDD-YBOCS improvement, plus 2) BDD-YBOCS score >20 (which corresponds to full-criteria BDD), plus 3) much or very much worse on the CGI since randomization. Relapse criteria needed to be met for two consecutive weeks, although for safety reasons subjects could be withdrawn after meeting relapse criteria for only one week (and were considered to have relapsed). Relapsing participants were referred for doctor’s choice rescue treatment by a psychiatrist not otherwise involved in the study. Additional psychotropic medication was limited to zolpidem 5–10 mg/hs for insomnia and sildenafil up to 100 mg 3 times a week for patients with treatment-emergent sexual dysfunction.

Independent evaluators who did not provide study treatment administered the following reliable and valid measures. Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale Modified for BDD (BDD-YBOCS), a 12-item semi-structured clinician-administered scale adapted from the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (16), rated past-week BDD severity. Clinical Global Impression Scale (CGI), a global rating scale that ranges from 1 (very much improved) to 7 (very much worse) (14), assessed BDD symptoms (CGI-BDD) and overall symptoms (CGI-global). The CGI was a secondary outcome measure in Phase 1 and determined relapse in Phase 2. Brown Assessment of Beliefs Scale (BABS), a 7-item semi-structured clinician-administered scale, assessed past-week BDD-related insight/delusional beliefs both dimensionally and categorically (20). It also classified false beliefs (for example, “I look like a monster”) as delusional or non-delusional. Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (17 item) assessed current severity of depressive symptoms (21). LIFE-RIFT (Range of Impaired Functioning Tool) assessed psychosocial functioning; it consists of 5- to 7-point rater-administered sub-scales for work, household duties, student work, relationships with family and friends, recreation, overall life satisfaction, and global social adjustment (22). Q-LES-Q Short Form assessed quality of life: social, leisure, household, work, emotional well-being, physical, and school (23). Psychiatric Status Rating Scale for BDD (BDD-PSR), a 7-point scale, reflects whether BDD symptoms currently meet full DSM-IV BDD criteria or are in full or partial remission (13,24). It was used in Phase 1 to examine remission from BDD, and in Phase 2 to examine further improvement (by one or more points) during continuation treatment with escitalopram.

Measures were administered at all visits, except that the CGI severity, LIFE-RIFT, and Q-LES-Q were administered only at the Phase 1 baseline and termination visits and at Phase 2 mid-point and termination visits. Interrater reliability on the BDD-YBOCS and BABS was examined; intraclass correlations for all scale items and total score were >0.9.

At all visits, vital signs were obtained, and medication compliance was monitored by patient inquiry, returned pill count, and a drug accountability form. Patients were asked whether they had experienced any adverse physical symptoms since the last visit, which were rated for severity, action taken, outcome, and seriousness. A urine drug screen and pregnancy test were repeated at the end of Phase 1.

Analyses were conducted using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and OpenEpi and employed a two-sided alpha=0.05 type I error rate. Major variables were screened for inconsistent or abnormal values, and continuous measures were assessed for skewness and outliers. Transformations to improve normality were applied. Differences in baseline covariates were tested using Fisher’s exact test (categorical) and Student’s t-test (continuous). Phase 1: Estimated power was >95% to detect a medium effect size for change in BDD-YBOCS score over time. Change scores were compared to a null value using a series of Student’s t-tests. Secondary analyses quantified the proportion (and 95% Wald confidence intervals [CIs]) of subjects who were much or very much improved (CGI improvement score=1 or 2) and who attained BDD remission (PSR score=1 or 2) by week 14 or early termination visit. Cox’s proportional hazards regression examined predictors of response to open-label escitalopram, with estimated power being 76%-≥90% to detect medium to large effect sizes. We planned to enroll enough subjects in Phase 1 to obtain 58 responders to be randomized in Phase 2. Phase 2: For the primary aim examining group differences in time to relapse (and proportion relapsing), Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated, and Cox’s proportional hazards regression was employed (with the likelihood ratio p-value reported). In Phase 2, data from assessments made after a patient entered rescue treatment were excluded from analyses. A priori, we determined that randomization of 58 subjects to Phase 2 would yield 80% power to detect a hazard ratio (HR) as small as 3.0 over six months of follow-up. For secondary analyses of change in BDD-YBOCS score, as well as changes in secondary outcome measures (BABS, HAM-D, LIFE-RIFT, and Q-LESQ), we employed generalized linear regression models and regressed the change score (baseline to last visit) against treatment group. For large effect sizes (d=0.8), estimated power for a two-tailed test alpha=0.05 was ≥74% for the BDD-YBOCS, and for the other secondary outcome measures, using a Bonferroni-adjusted alpha=0.0125, our estimated power was 31%-74% to detect medium to large effect sizes. During six months of continuation escitalopram treatment, we estimated the proportion (and 95% Fisher’s exact CIs) of subjects who further improved by one or more points on the BDD-PSR; power to detect significant improvement in 40% or more of subjects was 99%. We also examined site differences in the effect of treatment by including site and the interaction between treatment and site in regression models. Within the escitalopram and placebo groups, Student’s t-tests were used to compare the change in BDD-YBOCS total over Phase 2 to a null value.

Across the two sites, 173 subjects were enrolled (Figure 1). Seventy-three subjects did not pass screening. One-hundred subjects received initial open-label escitalopram treatment in Phase 1. Demographic characteristics are shown in Table 1; no significant site differences existed.

A total of 74.0% (N=74) of subjects completed 14 weeks of escitalopram treatment in Phase 1. BDD symptom response was achieved by 67.0% [57.8%, 75.7%] (N=67) of all enrolled subjects (in the intention-to treat [ITT] population), and by 81.1% [72.2%, 90.0%]) (N=60) among the 74 Phase 1 completers. Based on the CGI-BDD improvement score, 71.1% [62.1%, 80.2%] (N=69) of the 97 subjects with a post-baseline assessment were much or very much improved by their last Phase 1 visit. Full remission from BDD was attained by 20.0% [12.2%, 27.8%] (N=20) of ITT subjects, and by 25.7% [15.7%, 35.6%] (N=19) of Phase 1 completers. Median time to first response was 7.9 weeks [6.9, 8.9]. The mean escitalopram dose at the last Phase 1 visit was 26.2 ± 7.2 (range=5–30) mg/day.

Significant improvement from baseline to the last Phase 1 visit was attained on the BDD-YBOCS, BABS, HAM-D, LIFE-RIFT, and Q-LES-Q (all p's<0.0001). Mean BDD-YBOCS scores decreased from 32.7 ± 5.4 at baseline to 16.9 ± 10.2 at the last Phase 1 visit among the 97 subjects with a post-baseline assessment (average decrease of 48.7%) and to 14.9 ± 9.3 at week 14 in the completer population (average decrease of 54.4%).

Based on the CGI global improvement score, 67.0% [57.7%, 76.4%] (N=65) of subjects were much or very much improved by their last post-baseline Phase 1 visit. Response rates did not significantly differ between subjects with delusional versus nondelusional BDD, but there was a trend for those with nondelusional BDD beliefs (N=26) to be more likely to respond to escitalopram (N=74) (70.3% versus 57.7%, HR=0.58 [0.33, 1.04], p=0.054), respectively. The only baseline variable that predicted BDD response was presence of a personality disorder (HR=0.58 [0.36–0.94], p=0.030). Response to acute open-label escitalopram did not significantly differ by baseline BDD severity, BDD duration, depressive symptoms, gender, or minority status.

Fifty-eight participants were randomized to double-blind continuation treatment with escitalopram or discontinuation of escitalopram and substitution with placebo (Figure 1). Table 1 shows demographic characteristics at randomization; no significant differences existed by treatment. Phase 2 was completed by 89.3% (N=25) of escitalopram-treated subjects and 80.0% (n=24) of placebo-treated subjects (p=0.47).

Time to relapse of BDD was significantly longer with escitalopram than placebo (HR=2.72 [1.01, 8.57], p=0.049) (Figure 2). By the end of Phase 2, relapse proportions were 40% for placebo versus 18% for escitalopram. There were no statistically significant between-group treatment differences on secondary outcome measures and no significant site differences.

Among the 28 subjects randomized to continuation escitalopram, BDD-YBOCS scores decreased significantly during Phase 2 (mean=4.1 points, p=0.036). Among escitalopram-treated subjects, 35.7% (N=10) (95% CI [18.0%, 53.5%]) had a further decrease in BDD-PSR score. The mean escitalopram dose at the end of Phase 2 participation was 28.7 ± 4.6 (range 7.5–30.0) mg/day.

Phase 1: Overall, 89% (n=89) of ITT subjects experienced at least one treatment-emergent (i.e., post-baseline) adverse event (regardless of relationship to treatment). Adverse events occurring in at least 5% of subjects and considered at least possibly related to study medication were: fatigue (N=37), nausea (N=30), sexual dysfunction (N=28), dry mouth (N=25), insomnia (N=25), headache (N=22), appetite changes (N=20), agitation (N=10), flatulence (N=7), hypersomnia (N=6), sweating (N=6), dizziness (N=5), yawning (N=5), and irritability (N=5). Phase 2: Overall, 25.0% (N=7) of escitalopram-treated subjects versus 46.7% (n=14) of placebo-treated subjects experienced at least one adverse event considered at least possibly related to study medication (Fisher’s exact p=0.11). No serious adverse events occurred during either treatment phase.

Among those subjects who responded to acute-phase escitalopram treatment, continued treatment with escitalopram significantly delayed time to relapse of BDD compared to treatment with placebo, thus confirming our primary hypothesis. Moreover, more than twice as many placebo-treated subjects relapsed compared with escitalopram-treated subjects. These findings are particularly relevant clinically because BDD is often chronic (13,24).

The only published relapse data – from a chart-review study in a clinical practice – found that discontinuation of an effective SRI was followed by relapse (much or very much worse on the CGI) in 83% (n=31) of cases (15), a considerably higher rate than in the current study (40%). However, we assessed relapse over six months, whereas the chart-review study assessed relapse between the time an SRI was started and the last clinic visit, which varied considerably among subjects (mean follow-up duration was not reported). Furthermore, the two studies had methodologic differences, including different outcome measures and blind assessments every two weeks in the current study versus less frequent, unblinded assessments in the chart-review study. The prior study’s higher relapse rate may also be attributable, in part, to patients’ knowledge that active medication was being discontinued, unlike the current study.

Our secondary/exploratory hypothesis concerning changes in clinical symptoms, functioning, and quality of life during Phase 2 was not confirmed. Studies with larger samples are needed to determine whether group differences might emerge over a longer period following medication discontinuation.

Consistent with our hypothesis, during the six months of Phase 2 there was a statistically significant improvement in BDD-YBOCS score from randomization to study termination with escitalopram treatment; 35.7% of subjects improved by at least 1 point on the 7-point BDD-PSR scale. This finding is consistent with clinical observations that patients may improve further with SRI continuation following acute response (25). Further improvement with continuation phase SRI treatment has also been found in studies of OCD, depression, and other disorders (26,27).

Our open-label Phase 1 results confirm findings from all prior BDD treatment studies, indicating that acute SRI treatment is efficacious for a majority of patients. The current study’s ITT response rate (67%) and degree of improvement on the BDD-YBOCS is similar to that reported in prior, smaller acute phase studies (n=15–34 SRI-treated subjects), which had ITT response rates of 53% to 83% (7,9–12).

Consistent with our hypothesis and prior research, BDD-related insight, depressive symptoms, psychosocial functioning, and quality of life also significantly improved with open-label escitalopram (9–12). The mean Phase 1 escitalopram dose (26.2 ± 7.2 mg/day) is higher than doses often used to treat anxiety disorders and depression but similar to doses usually recommended for OCD and typically used for BDD (25,28,29). No dose-finding studies have been conducted in BDD; however, clinical observations and retrospective data from an observational study suggest that relatively high SRI doses (sometimes higher than those used in the current study) are often needed to effectively treat BDD (25,30).

Our finding that individuals with delusional BDD beliefs were as likely as those with nondelusional BDD beliefs to respond to escitalopram is consistent with all prior SRI studies (7–11). It is also consistent with data from a range of diagnostic validators indicating that delusional BDD and nondelusional BDD constitute the same disorder (31,32), as in DSM-5. However, unlike prior studies, there was a trend for those with nondelusional BDD beliefs to be more likely to respond to escitalopram.

Study limitations include statistical power that was too limited to examine predictors of relapse after escitalopram discontinuation or to test treatment differences for some other reported results (e.g., within type of adverse event). Additionally, analysis of multiple outcomes in Phase I may have inflated type I error. Also, while use of placebo is a “gold-standard” approach that has many methodologic advantages, it does not mimic what actually occurs in clinical practice, where patients know whether they have discontinued medication. Knowledge of medication discontinuation may possibly be associated with a higher relapse rate than was found in this study. Furthermore, our findings may not be fully generalizable to certain clinical settings or populations. For example, suicidality and co-occurring substance use disorders appear common in individuals with BDD (6,33), yet we excluded individuals with a current/recent substance use disorder from this efficacy study. We also excluded more highly suicidal individuals due to potential risks of discontinuing efficacious medication, and we excluded more severely ill patients who required concomitant psychotherapy or a higher level of care. Study strengths include the fact that this is the first relapse prevention study in BDD; contained the largest sample of any BDD treatment study; and used randomization, placebo, and blind and rigorous assessment of outcomes at specified intervals. Additional studies with larger samples and longer follow-up are needed.
