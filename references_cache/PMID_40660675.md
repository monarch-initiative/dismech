# BCS1L-Associated Disease: 5'-UTR Variant Shifts the Phenotype Towards Axonal Neuropathy.
**Authors:** Orbach R, Maio N, Butterfield RJ, Foley AR, Silverstein S, Li Y, Chao K, Lehky TJ, Potticary A, Rouault TA, Donkervoort S, Bönnemann CG
**Journal:** Ann Clin Transl Neurol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/acn3.70108](https://doi.org/10.1002/acn3.70108)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: To investigate the consequences of a pathogenic missense variant (c.838C>T; p.L280F) and a 5'-UTR regulatory variant (c.-122G>T) in BCS1L on disease pathogenesis and to understand how regulatory variants influence disease severity and clinical presentation.

METHODS: Deep phenotyping, research-based whole genome sequencing, biochemical characterization of identified variants, and studies in patient-derived fibroblast cultures were applied to uncover the underlying genetic cause and molecular defects in siblings with a genetically uncharacterized complex neurologic condition.

RESULTS: Genome sequencing identified a paternally inherited missense variant (c.838C>T; p.L280F) and a maternally inherited 5'-UTR variant (c.-122G>T) in BCS1L in two affected siblings. Although the missense variant disrupts complex III assembly, the 5'-UTR variant allows residual wild-type BCS1L expression, likely mitigating disease severity. Biochemical studies in patient-derived fibroblasts confirmed the pathogenicity of both variants and demonstrated a moderate in vitro response to a coenzyme Q10 analog.

INTERPRETATION: This study expands the clinical spectrum of BCS1L-related disorders to include a comparatively milder phenotype with central and peripheral nervous system involvement. Our findings demonstrate that the 5'-UTR variant modulates disease severity by enabling residual wild-type BCS1L expression, partially mitigating the pathogenic effects of the missense variant. These insights underscore the importance of evaluating both protein coding and regulatory variants in mitochondrial disease diagnostics and pathogenesis.

Eukaryotic cells primarily rely on oxidative phosphorylation for efficient energy production. Complex III (CIII), also known as cytochrome bc1 complex or ubiquinol‐cytochrome c reductase, plays a central role in mitochondrial respiration. Pathogenic variants in genes encoding CIII subunits or assembly factors are associated with several mitochondrial diseases [1]. The most common are biallelic variants in BCS1L, which encodes a translocase that facilitates the integration of the Rieske iron–sulfur (Fe‐S) subunit, UQCRFS1, into CIII [2]. The two primary phenotypes linked with disease‐causing variants in BCS1L are GRACILE [3] and Björnstad syndromes [4]. GRACILE, presenting perinatally, is characterized by growth retardation, aminoaciduria, cholestasis, iron overload, lactic acidosis, and early death. Björnstad manifests with sensorineural hearing loss and pili torti, a distinctive hair abnormality [4]. The increasing availability of genetic testing has broadened the phenotypic spectrum associated with BCS1L variants [5, 6] to now include milder clinical presentations [5] and previously unrecognized features, such as cardiomyopathy [7].

Here, we describe two siblings with a complex neurological phenotype but led by an axonal, predominantly motor peripheral neuropathy, without overt features of either GRACILE or Björnstad syndrome. Biochemical assays performed in patient‐derived fibroblasts confirmed the pathogenicity of the variants and their impaired ability to assist in CIII assembly. Our study expands the clinical and genetic spectrum of BSC1L‐related disorders, underscores the role of variants in untranslated regions as potential disease modifiers, and offers insights into potential tailored treatment strategies.

Patients were referred to the Neuromuscular and Neurogenetic Disorders of Childhood Section program for undiagnosed patients by their neurologist. Ethical approval was obtained via the Institutional Review Board of the National Institutes of Health (protocol 12‐N‐0095). Medical history and clinical evaluations, including imaging and histopathology, were conducted as part of the standard diagnostic procedures.

Quartet (affected siblings and parents) exome and genome sequencing was performed on DNA isolated from whole blood; human whole transcriptome sequencing was performed on RNA isolated from fibroblasts cultured from a skin biopsy performed on patient 1 (P1). Sequencing and data processing were performed by the Genomics Platform at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard [8]. Variant validation and segregation studies were performed through targeted variant testing via a CLIA‐certified laboratory.

Immortalized dermal fibroblast cell lines for the siblings and their father and Hela cells (ATCC #CCL‐2) were grown in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (4.5 g/L glucose), 2 mM glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum (Life Technologies), and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Life Technologies) in 5% CO2 at 37°C.

To study genotype–phenotype correlations and perform biochemical characterization of the patient variant (L280F) as well as the most common disease‐causing missense variants associated with GRACILE (S78G) and Björnstad syndromes (R183H), the human BCS1L ORF (NM_004328) was cloned into the pCMV6‐Entry vector (OriGene) using MluI and SgfI restriction sites and expressed with a C‐terminal Myc‐DDK tag. Site‐directed mutagenesis was performed with a QuikChange II Site‐Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies). Transient transfection of BCS1L wild‐type or variants in HeLa cells was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were harvested and processed 24 h post‐transfection.

The native PAGE Novex Bis‐Tris gel system (ThermoFisher Scientific) was used to assess activities and levels of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes, with the following modifications: only the Light Blue Cathode Buffer was used; 20 μg of membrane protein extracts were loaded per well; the electrophoresis was performed at 150 V for 1 h and 250 V for 2 h.

In‐gel Complex I activity assay was performed as previously described [9, 10, 11]. After resolution of the respiratory complexes by BN‐PAGE, the gel was incubated with 0.1 M TrisCl, pH 7.4, containing 1 mg/mL nitrobluetetrazolium chloride (NBT) and 0.14 mM NADH at room temperature for 30–60 min.

CIII activity was measured using a commercially available kit (Abcam #ab287844) that assesses cytochrome c reduction at 550 nm, following the manufacturer's protocol.

For the native immunoblots, polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) was used as the blotting membrane. The transfer was performed at 25 V for 4 h at 4°C. After transfer, the membrane was washed with 8% acetic acid for 20 min to fix the proteins and then rinsed with water before air‐drying. The dried membrane was washed 5–6 times with methanol (to remove residual Coomassie Blue G‐250), rinsed with water, and then blocked for 2 h at room temperature in 5% milk, before incubating with the desired antibodies diluted in 2.5% milk overnight at 4°C. In order to avoid stripping and reprobing of the same membrane, which might allow detection of signals from the previous immunoblots, samples were loaded and run in replicates on adjacent wells of the same gel and probed independently with different antibodies.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) in patient‐derived and paternal cells were assessed with CM‐H2DCFDA (ThermoFisher Scientific #C6827), a chloromethyl derivative of H2DCFDA. Briefly, 2 x 106 cells in 100 μL PBS were incubated with 10 μL H2DCFDA for 20 min at 37°C on a shaker. Following 2 washes with cold PBS, fluorescence was measured with FACSMelody cell sorter (BD Biosciences).

Mitochondrial respiration was determined using a Seahorse Flux Analyzer XF96 (Agilent), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 25,000 cells were seeded in each well of a Seahorse 96‐well plate the day prior to the assay. On the day of the assay, cells were incubated for 1 h in 180 μL of XF‐based medium without phenol red, pH 7.4 containing 111 mM NaCl, 4.7 mM KCl, 2.5 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 5 mM HEPES. For measurements, cells were allowed to equilibrate for 1 h in a non‐CO2 incubator immediately before analysis of the metabolic flux. Oxygen consumption rates (OCR) were measured three times before sequential injection of 1 μM oligomycin A (Agilent, 3 time points), 1 μM FCCP (Agilent, 3 time points), and a mixture of 0.5 μM rotenone and antimycin A (Agilent, 3 time points). OCR values for each well were normalized according to the number of cells determined by the Cytation 1 reader (BioTek, Agilent). No significant changes in cell size were observed in the different cell lines and under the different conditions. MitoQ (Mitoquinone mesylate) was purchased from Cayman Chemical (#29317) and used at concentrations of 100 or 200 nM, as indicated, for 7 days prior to OCR, oxidative stress marker analysis, and ROS measurements.

Oxidative stress damage in patient‐derived and paternal cells was assessed as follows: Lipid peroxidation was measured using the 4‐HNE assay kit (Abcam, #ab238538) and the MDA assay kit (colorimetric, Abcam, #ab118970), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein carbonylation was evaluated with the protein carbonyl content assay kit (Abcam, #ab126287), following the manufacturer's instructions.

Patient 1 (P1) was born at 37 weeks of gestation following an uncomplicated pregnancy and initially showed normal development. Concerns emerged at 16 months when he began experiencing motor regression, manifested by frequent falls and coordination difficulties, which were initially addressed by physical therapy. By age 3 years, weakness, clumsiness, and balance issues became more prominent, leading to referrals to specialists and a neurological evaluation that did not yield a specific diagnosis. Over the years, P1 experienced a gradual decline in motor function, including episodic focal dystonia in his right hand. By age 8 years, he required a manual wheelchair for long‐distance ambulation because of increasing weakness, low stamina, and clumsiness. By age 11 years, he transitioned to a motorized wheelchair as his symptoms continued to progress. At his last assessment at age 12 years, he was still able to walk short distances without support, climb stairs using a handrail, and arise from a seated position on the floor. However, he reported increased weakness in his arms and bulbar symptoms, including episodes of choking and difficulties chewing and swallowing hard‐textured foods. His right hand has become gradually non‐functional because of contraction in a fixed, fisted‐thumb adduction position. He has been prescribed a cough assist device to help with airway clearance and non‐invasive ventilation (NIV) in the form of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) for nocturnal de‐saturations due to obstructive sleep apnea. Additional medical issues have been life‐long challenges with gaining weight, daily migraines, and learning disabilities. Despite extensive diagnostic evaluations, he remained without a genetic cause for his symptoms.

Patient 2 (P2), P1's older sibling, was born via C‐section at term (40 weeks) following a normal pregnancy. He spent 5 days in the NICU because of respiratory distress from meconium aspiration, requiring antibiotics and CPAP support. Early motor milestones were achieved on time, with independent ambulation by 12 months of age. Aside from recurrent ear infections and minor respiratory illnesses in early childhood, he was generally healthy. In retrospect, around age 2 years, his parents began to notice frequent falls, heavy and loud steps, and unusual wear on his shoes. However, these concerns were minimal and did not prompt medical attention. As he entered elementary school, it became apparent that he struggled both physically and academically. By the 4th grade, he was diagnosed with depression, anxiety, and ADHD. A neurological evaluation was requested only around the time of the diagnostic workup for his younger brother, when the neurologist noted similarities between the siblings, including poor balance, coordination difficulties, muscle weakness, muscle cramping, and failure to gain weight. By his last assessment at age 15 years, P2 reported progressive weakness, increased difficulty walking, frequent falls, and “locked fingers”, (leading to difficulties with fine motor tasks). Although he did not require NIV or a cough assist device, he reported frequent choking episodes. His medical history also includes learning disabilities, laser surgery for retinal lattice degeneration in both eyes, and moderate low‐frequency mixed hearing loss (revealed as part of detailed phenotyping).

Physical examination of the siblings at ages 12 (P1) and 15 (P2) years revealed a slim body habitus, prominent scapulae (Figure 1A), and distal muscle atrophy (Figure 1B). P1 presented with a fixed, fisted right‐hand posture (Figure 1C). P2 had pronounced high‐arched feet and hammer toe deformities (Figure 1D). Both siblings had predominantly distal muscle weakness. Sensory examination was normal in P1, and near‐normal in P2 with only mildly reduced vibration sensation. Both siblings demonstrated a positive Romberg sign and were unable to perform tandem gait. Their baseline gait was notably wide‐based and with a steppage quality. Additional physical examination findings are provided in Table S1. Electrodiagnostic studies revealed a primary axonal neuropathy, predominantly affecting motor function, with moderate progression over time. In P1, the peroneal nerve compound muscle action potential at the extensor digitorum brevis muscle declined from 2.9 mV at age 8 years to 1.3 mV at 11 years. Similarly, in P2, it decreased from 1.5 mV at age 11 years to 0.8 mV at age 15 years (Table S2). Muscle ultrasound revealed diffuse streaky echogenicity in upper and lower extremity muscles (Figure 1E–G.), with fasciculations in proximal and distal muscles, consistent with acute and chronic denervation changes. A muscle biopsy of P1's vastus lateralis (age 8 years) showed moderate, acute, and chronic neurogenic atrophy (Figure 1H–J). P1's brain MRIs were notable for a stable expansion of right posterior fossa extra‐axial CSF (Figure 1K) and a small focus of signal change within the white matter (Figure 1L). P2's brain MRI was normal. Additionally, P2 had moderate, bilateral mixed low‐frequency hearing loss, whereas P1's auditory evaluation at age 12 years was normal. Summary of pertinent symptoms, examination findings and workup are provided in Table S1.

P1 underwent extensive genetic testing at age 7 years, which was initially unrevealing. Research‐based quartet exome sequencing identified a paternally inherited variant in BCS1L (ENST00000359273.8, NM_001079866.2: c.838C>T p.Leu280Phe) (Figure S1A), hereafter referred to as p.L280F, without any reportable findings on the maternal BCS1L allele. Subsequent research‐based quartet (P1, P2, and parents) genome sequencing (GS) revealed that the mother is heterozygous for a 5′‐UTR BCS1L variant (ENST00000359273.8 NM_001079866.2: c.‐122G>T), and confirmed that both affected siblings are compound heterozygous for biallelic changes in BCS1L, carrying the paternally inherited missense variant p.L280F and the maternally inherited 5′‐UTR variant (c.‐122G>T). No additional shared pathogenic variants in other known disease‐causing genes, including the mitochondrial DNA, were identified. BCS1L variants segregated as shown in the pedigree; the unaffected brother carries the wild‐type BCS1L genotype, whereas the sister is heterozygous for the 5′‐UTR variant (Figure 2A). The p.L280F variant is classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar (VCV000423549.3) with an allele frequency of 1.590 × 10−6 in the gnomADv4 database and no reported homozygous individuals. It resides in exon 6 (Figure 2B,D) and the leucine to phenylalanine substitution is predicted to be deleterious by multiple in silico tools. The 5′‐UTR variant, c.‐122G>T, is classified as a variant of uncertain significance in ClinVar (VCV003385355.1) with an allele frequency of 8.1 × 10–5 (gnomADv4) and no reported homozygous individuals. The c.‐122G>T variant resides in a regulatory region of the 5′‐UTR (Figure 2B,C). In silico tools such as SpliceAI and Pangolin predicted no changes in splicing, and this was further confirmed by MINTIE v0.4.1. MINTIE analysis of patient RNA sequencing data, compared to 10 GTEX control fibroblast samples, did not identify novel splice isoforms in the patient samples (Figure S1B).

BSC1L inserts the Rieske protein, UQCRFS1, into a CIII assembly intermediate that forms following the acquisition of its Fe‐S cluster by UQCRFS1 (Figure 3A) [10]. The highly conserved L280 residue is situated near the critical Walker B motif residue, E282, essential for ATP binding and hydrolysis (Figure 3B–E). Substitution of phenylalanine at position 280 is predicted to introduce steric hindrance with E282 (Figure 3E), which is predicted to disrupt the ATPase activity of BCS1L. Because BCS1L facilitates the insertion of UQCRFS1 into complex III, we investigated whether the L280F variant retains this function by overexpressing C‐terminally FLAG‐Myc–tagged BCS1LL280F in HeLa cells. Co‐immunoprecipitation (co‐IP) assays revealed that the L280F variant fails to interact with UQCRFS1 (Figure 3F), supporting its loss of function and confirming its pathogenicity.

To further understand the functional implications of the variants identified in our study, we performed a genotype/phenotype correlation and biochemical characterization of three BCS1L variants: p.S78G and p.R183H (previously associated with GRACILE [3, 5] and Björnstad [4] syndromes, respectively), and p.L280F (identified here), by expressing them in HeLa cells. Co‐immunoprecipitation assays showed that p.L280F exhibited markedly reduced interaction with UQCRFS1, similar to the severe GRACILE‐associated S78G variant, whereas p.R183H retained partial binding (Figure 3G). These results indicate that the p.L280F variant is functionally deleterious, and the milder phenotype observed in our patients is likely modulated by the co‐occuring 5′‐UTR variant.

Biochemical analysis of fibroblasts generated from P1 and P2, compared to their unaffected father who carries the p.L280F variant (fibroblasts from the mother were not available), revealed decreased BCS1L levels and slightly elevated LYRM7 levels in both patients' cells (Figure 4A,B). LYRM7, a mitochondrial chaperone involved in the insertion of the [Fe2S2] cluster into UQCRFS1 [10], is likely upregulated as part of a compensatory mechanism, potentially aimed at assisting CIII assembly in response to the reduced availability of functional BCS1L. A complex III assay confirmed lower activity in patient‐derived cells (Figure 4C), stemming from defective CIII assembly, as assessed by native immunoblot (IB) against a CIII core subunit, UQCRC1 (Figure 4D). Loss of complex III impaired the incorporation of complex IV (CIV) into supercomplexes, resulting in elevated levels of free CIV (Figure 4D, IB to MTCO1). Notably, mass spectrometry (MS) analysis detected several wild type BCS1L peptides covering the region spanning the L280 amino acid residue, which were present in both patient‐derived and paternal cells (Figure S2A,B). This finding indicates that the 5′‐UTR variant allows for some residual production of wild‐type BCS1L protein, thereby contributing to the observed biochemical profile and milder clinical presentation in the affected siblings.

GRACILE syndrome is associated with iron overload [3]. However, in our patients, liver function tests and iron‐related blood parameters—including transferrin, transferrin saturation, serum iron, and ferritin—were all within normal reference ranges (Table S1). Despite normal total iron levels in patients and paternal cells (Figure 4E), P1 cells exhibited an elevated labile iron pool (LIP) (Figure 4F) without corresponding changes in the levels of iron regulatory proteins (IRPs) (Figure S3A,B). The lack of expected downregulation of IRPs in the presence of elevated LIP suggests dysregulated iron sensing, where the impaired regulatory feedback mechanism may contribute to increased intracellular levels of ROS [13].

P1 and P2 cells showed reduced basal OCR and spare respiratory capacity (Figure 5A–C), consistent with the loss of CIII activity and overall levels (Figure 4C,D). The reduced OCR and spare respiratory capacity reflect a compromised mitochondrial function, indicating the inability of the patient‐derived cells to efficiently generate energy, which is a direct consequence of the underlying CIII defect. Dysfunctional CIII contributes to an imbalance in electron transport, likely resulting in the production of ROS, which in turn exacerbates cellular stress, leading to damage to key cellular components, including DNA, lipids and proteins [14, 15]. Accordingly, oxidative stress markers were elevated in patient cells (Figure 5D,E), presumably due to impaired mitochondrial respiratory chain function [15, 16]. In an effort to mitigate ROS damage, Mitoquinol mesylate (MitoQ)—an antioxidant designed to be efficiently targeted to mitochondria [17]—was administered in vitro at concentrations of 100–200 nM. MitoQ modestly improved mitochondrial respiration, with a notable increase in P2 cells' spare respiratory capacity (Figure S4A–C). However, it did not significantly reduce oxidative stress markers (Figure S4D,E), indicating that MitoQ was unable to resolve the underlying respiratory chain defect due to CIII loss. Clinically, the administration of ubiquinone supplementation along with B‐complex vitamins yielded no significant improvement in the patients' symptoms, likely reflecting the complexity of the mitochondrial dysfunction in the patient‐derived cells. These findings suggest that restoring or stabilizing BCS1L protein levels could hold therapeutic potential, although further studies are needed to fully characterize the contributions of allelic dosage and mitochondrial background to the observed phenotype.

We report here two male siblings with progressive axonal lower motor neuropathy as the leading feature but with concomitant central nervous system involvement. Initially, both siblings presented with a regression in motor function, followed by progressive muscle weakness, along with behavioral and cognitive symptoms. P1 developed dystonia and migraines, whereas P2 exhibited hearing loss and retinal lattice degeneration. Their diagnostic journey came to resolution following a research‐based genome sequencing analysis, integrated with deep phenotyping and tailored biochemical characterization of patient‐derived cell lines, which revealed biallelic BCS1L variants and confirmed their pathogenicity.


BSC1L is associated with recessive phenotypes, including GRACILE [3, 18] and Björnstad syndromes [4]. Classic GRACILE syndrome presents in utero (growth restriction) or at birth, with expected mortality within the first months of life [3]. A retrospective study involving 33 patients with biallelic pathogenic BCS1L variants highlighted a phenotypic spectrum that may represent a continuum of clinical features, with onset under 1 month of age generally correlating with poorer prognosis [5]. Reported CNS features included movement disorders, seizures, sensorineural hearing loss, ADHD, and brain MRI abnormalities. Notably, lower motor neuropathy, a distinctive finding in our siblings, has not previously been reported in association with BSC1L‐related disorders, possibly masked by the severity of the systemic involvement.


BCS1L 5′‐UTR variants have been associated with disease in a few cases. The first reported GRACILE syndrome case [19] involved a patient with biallelic intronic variants, including a splicing variant (c.321G>T), leading to a truncated protein, and a 5′‐UTR variant (c.‐588 T>A) [3]. Another patient with the same 5′‐UTR variant and the p.R56X substitution was diagnosed with classic GRACILE syndrome. Additionally, three neonates with GRACILE syndrome were found to carry the p.R56X variant along with a distinct 5′‐UTR single nucleotide variant, which altered splicing to create a 19‐nucleotide deletion in the 5′‐UTR region [5, 20]. This combination led to decreased BCS1L mRNA and protein levels. Unlike those cases, which presented with early metabolic symptoms without CNS or PNS involvement, our patients predominantly exhibited neurological symptoms without typical metabolic signs.

Functional assays of the p.L280F variant demonstrated a profound defect in its interaction with the Rieske protein, preventing its proper insertion into complex III, similar to the common GRACILE‐causing p.S78G variant [3]. The c.‐122G>T variant, located in the 5′‐UTR of the BCS1L gene, resides within a highly conserved regulatory region, which likely modulates translation efficiency and protein expression levels, rather than affecting transcription [21]. Such variants often pose challenges in genetic analyses: they are frequently missed by exome sequencing, filtered out during data analysis, and difficult to classify because of the complexities in predicting or determining their consequences on protein synthesis [22].

The milder phenotype observed in our patients likely stems from their compound heterozygous state. The 5′‐UTR variant allows for limited production of functional wild‐type BCS1L, partially mitigating the severe defect of the p.L280F variant. Additionally, our RNA‐seq data suggest that alternative transcription initiation sites may be used, resulting in transcripts that bypass the c.‐122G>T variant and support the production of functional BCS1L. Multiple lines of evidence support the variants' pathogenicity and elucidate the underlying disease mechanism. Patient cells exhibited decreased BCS1L protein levels, reduced complex III levels, and diminished activity relative to control fibroblasts and paternal cells. Since the father and siblings share the L280F variant, whereas only the siblings carry the 5′‐UTR variant, we infer that the 5′‐UTR variant impairs protein translation, resulting in reduced BCS1L protein levels, as supported by MS data, and decreased CIII activity in the patient cells. This impairment results in reduced OCR in BCS1L‐deficient patient cells. Notably, both basal and spare respiratory capacity measurements aligned with the clinical observation that P1 presented earlier than P2 and with a more severe phenotype.

Collectively, these findings highlight the pathogenicity of the identified BCS1L variants and offer insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying our patients' clinical symptoms. The variability in disease severity between the siblings points to the interplay of genetic and non‐genetic factors in disease pathogenesis. These molecular insights, particularly our characterization of the specific variants and their functional consequences, provide a foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. Potential approaches include targeting the 5′‐UTR region with small molecules to enhance translational efficiency [23, 24], or utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 technology for gene editing and epigenetic modulation [25, 26]. In our study, we evaluated Mitoquinone mesylate (MitoQ) [27], a widely used over‐the‐counter mitochondria‐targeted antioxidant, to reduce oxidative stress linked to impaired CIII function. MitoQ can effectively scavenge ROS generated by excessive iron accumulation or oxidative stress secondary to respiratory chain dysfunction in mitochondria [28, 29]. In vitro MitoQ treatment showed dose‐dependent effects, with a modest improvement in mitochondrial respiration and a notable increase in spare respiratory capacity in P2's cells at 200 nM.

This study has several limitations. First, our conclusions are drawn from observations in only two affected individuals, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, maternal fibroblast samples were unavailable, precluding a direct biochemical evaluation of cells heterozygous for the BCS1L 5′‐UTR variant and a wild‐type allele. This also limited our ability to assess potential additive effects from maternally inherited mitochondrial DNA variants, though no pathogenic mtDNA variants were detected in patient cells. Third, although we confirmed the presence of wild‐type BCS1L peptides in patient‐derived cells by mass spectrometry—suggesting that the 5′‐UTR variant permits some residual protein expression—the underlying mechanism by which this variant influences translation efficiency remains speculative. Finally, our therapeutic exploration was limited to the use of MitoQ, a mitochondria‐targeted antioxidant, which showed only modest functional rescue. Future studies will be required to explore additional therapeutic strategies, including approaches aimed at enhancing BCS1L expression from the affected allele or correcting the translational defect.

Despite these limitations, our study provides important insights into the clinical and molecular consequences of BCS1L 5′‐UTR variants and lays the groundwork for future mechanistic and therapeutic investigations. Overall, our findings establish axonal motor neuropathy as a clinical manifestation of BCS1L‐related mitochondrial disorders, expanding the spectrum of motor neuropathies to be considered in differential diagnoses. Our report underscores the challenges and significance of identifying disease‐causing variants in the 5′‐UTR region, which can modulate disease severity. It also highlights the critical role of comprehensive genetic analyses and functional studies in elucidating the full spectrum of mitochondrial disease phenotypes and uncovering mechanistic insights that may guide the development of targeted therapeutic approaches.
