# High Altitude Pulmonary Edema Response to Continuous Airway Positive Pressure: A Randomized Controlled Trial: The HAPER CAPER Trial.
**Authors:** Bolotin T, Subedi S, Dahal S, Walker C, Chung K, Martz A, Gemmel D, Chopra Q, Donley C
**Journal:** Acad Emerg Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/acem.70126](https://doi.org/10.1111/acem.70126)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Morbidity and mortality in cardiogenic pulmonary edema have been reduced by noninvasive positive-pressure airway maneuvers. The value of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) for the treatment of high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is uncertain. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the efficacy and speed of CPAP for HAPE resolution.

METHODS: A prospective, investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, double-blind controlled trial of high-flow oxygen with CPAP versus sham CPAP with high-flow oxygen (oxygen-only group) was conducted. Sixty-four dyspneic adults with maximum oxygen saturation < 85%, recent arrival at high altitude, and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema on chest radiography were randomized to receive CPAP treatment plus usual care or usual care (oxygen-only) delivered through a sham CPAP mask. The primary endpoint was the clinical resolution of HAPE.

RESULTS: HAPE resolution in the CPAP plus high FiO2 group and the high flow oxygen alone group was similar. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to this study being slightly underpowered. There was no significant difference in time to resolution (CPAP ~158 min vs. oxygen ~178 min, p = 0.297). Both therapies were effective, with a mean time to resolution of HAPE of approximately 2.5 to 3 h. No treatment-related adverse outcomes, intubations, or mortality were observed in either group.

CONCLUSION: Both CPAP with high-flow oxygen and high-flow oxygen alone are highly effective for the treatment of HAPE. CPAP conferred no improvement in the time to resolution of HAPE. High-flow oxygen therapy requires significantly less resource utilization for similar benefits in the treatment of HAPE.

Cardiogenic pulmonary edema is a high‐mortality condition associated with impaired oxygenation and respiratory failure secondary to elevated hydrostatic pulmonary venous pressure caused by elevated cardiac filling pressure [1]. Emergency department (ED) treatment consists of supplemental oxygen, noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, fluid redistribution (through the use of vasodilators, inotropes, and diuretics), hemodynamic support with vasopressors, and airway and arrhythmia management as required [1, 2]. High‐altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a life‐threatening, noncardiogenic form of pulmonary edema induced by hypobaric hypoxia [3]. HAPE occurs in nonacclimatized individuals who ascend to altitudes above 2500 m [3]. HAPE is rare in most EDs as it improves with descent and is typically managed at high altitude [3, 4]. The reported incidence of HAPE ranges from 0.2% to 15% depending on ascent profile and altitude achieved [3]. If left untreated, HAPE has a reported mortality of 50% [3]. Significant altitude gain without acclimatization, particularly when coupled with strenuous low‐temperature exercise, increases susceptibility to HAPE [5]. Modern transportation, including ski lifts, allows for ascent from sea level to an altitude of 3964 m within 5 h [6]. The Colorado Rockies are a popular ski tourism destination that attracts millions of visitors annually, many of whom rapidly ascend from low altitudes [7]. In Colorado, the incidence of HAPE has been estimated at 1 per 10,000 recreational skiers, and typically affects young, healthy adults with minimal comorbidities [3, 8, 9]. Many Colorado ski villages are located at altitudes in excess of 2500 m, with summits surpassing 3900 m [9, 10]. The number of high‐altitude visitors is increasing, along with the prevalence of HAPE‐related ED presentations [7, 11].

In response to hypobaric hypoxia, a pathophysiologic cascade may occur, including elevated pulmonary arterial pressure and nonuniform pulmonary vasoconstriction [12]. This process can precipitate capillary stress failure, causing noncardiogenic pulmonary edema [13, 14, 15]. Randomized trials have demonstrated continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) benefits in cardiogenic pulmonary edema [16, 17]. CPAP treatment has been shown to reduce intubation rates [18]. A Cochrane meta‐analysis of 24 studies with 2664 participants found that CPAP may reduce in‐hospital mortality [19]. CPAP mechanisms include increased airway diameter with reduced resistance, recruitment of collapsed alveoli with improved oxygenation, improved functional residual capacity enhancing lung compliance, reduction in the work of breathing, and increased transmural pressure across alveolar walls, improving ventilation‐perfusion matching [20, 21].

The mainstay of HAPE treatment is descent and supplemental oxygen [22, 23]. Adjunctive treatment includes pulmonary vasodilation with nifedipine [24]. The value of nifedipine in treating HAPE is limited; while one small case series suggested benefit, a separate cross‐sectional analysis did not confirm this finding [25, 26]. The 2024 Wilderness Medicine Society guidelines make a weak recommendation for CPAP as an adjunct to supplemental oxygen, particularly in cases of refractory HAPE [24]. Evidence supporting this recommendation is based on case series as well as observational studies [27, 28]. Various methods of positive pressure ventilation for HAPE, such as auto‐PEEP, pursed‐lip breathing, CPAP helmet, and EPAP devices, have been described in the literature [27, 28, 29, 30]. These methods suggest efficacy in improving oxygen saturation, work of breathing, heart rate, and HAPE resolution [31]. There are no trials comparing supplemental oxygen with any form of positive pressure ventilation in the treatment of HAPE. The purpose of this study was to evaluate CPAP for the resolution of HAPE, compared to supplemental high‐flow oxygen in a prospective randomized placebo‐controlled trial.

After receiving Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval by Catholic Health Initiatives Institute of Research and Innovation Institutional Review Board (Project 1389613), a randomized‐controlled trial of CPAP versus high‐flow oxygen in HAPE patients was performed. The study was designed using CONSORT guidelines and registered with the clinical trials protocol registration system [32]. Funding was provided through a monetary grant from Lake Erie College of Osteopathic Medicine (Erie, Pennsylvania) along with study equipment and IRB registration fees from Pulmodyne Incorporated (Indianapolis, Indiana). Neither granting entity had any control over experimental design, data collection, analysis, or manuscript production. Subjects were recruited at three participating high‐altitude EDs: Keystone, Colorado (2828 m); Breckenridge, Colorado (2926 m); and Copper, Colorado (2950 m). These EDs are combined urgent care and emergency departments. They are American Trauma Society Level V Trauma Centers located at the base of ski resorts that primarily treat ski‐related trauma and altitude illness.

The initial identification of potential trial subjects was performed by the triage nurses. Inclusion criteria were age 18 years or greater, oxygen saturation below 85%, recent arrival to high altitude, dyspnea or cough, and chest radiograph revealing pulmonary edema. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, incarceration, age less than 18 years, respiratory failure requiring immediate intubation, altered mental status impairing decision‐making capacity, history of congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or asthma, active pneumonia, and facial hair growth preventing CPAP mask application. Of 83 patients approached for randomization, 19 declined to participate. Sixty‐four subjects were randomized, but six were excluded from analysis due to two protocol violations and four withdrawals. A total of 627 patients were seen for HAPE at the three EDs during the study period. The majority were not approached for recruitment due to inadequate research staff availability in busy community EDs (Table 1).

While awaiting recruitment and randomization, all patients were briefly placed on high‐flow oxygen using a non‐rebreather mask to reduce hypoxemia prior to enrollment. Due to their profound hypoxia, patients were seen and randomized immediately. Oxygen saturation and vital signs were recorded at the time of arrival and at the time of study initiation. Patients were randomized to either CPAP or sham CPAP masks (oxygen‐only) at time zero. CPAP masks were either an O2 Max Trio CPAP system providing 10 cm of water pressure and an FiO2 of 90% or an identical system with a modified pressure valve that delivered no positive pressure and an FiO2 of 90%. All CPAP equipment was provided by Pulmodyne Incorporated (Indianapolis, Indiana). In accordance with the 2019 Wilderness Medical Society guidelines, all nonhypotensive patients were treated with 30 mg nifedipine extended‐release orally. After the protocol termination, patients were either discharged or admitted to a lower altitude hospital at the discretion of the treating physician, without direction from the study protocol. Except for CPAP application, all other interventions were equivalent for the participants in both groups. Treating physicians were not precluded from any additional therapies they felt were appropriate. Any such treatment decisions occurred after randomization.

Thirty minutes after CPAP application, subjects were switched to nasal cannula oxygen at 6 L/min. Oxygen saturation was measured using a Nellcor Portable SpO2 Patient Monitoring System, PM10N, made by Medtronic Incorporated (Minneapolis, Minnesota). The same treating nurse assessed heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and apparent work of breathing (determined by the subjective impressions of the nurse and patient) off CPAP. If subjects could tolerate 6 L/min of oxygen without significant dyspnea while maintaining an SpO2 of 92%, FiO2 was decreased to approximately 28%, or a flow rate of 2 L/min of oxygen. Patients were ambulated in the ED while wearing a pulse oximeter and nasal oxygen. If they maintained a SpO2 of ≥ 92%, the protocol was terminated, and patients were eligible for discharge with portable oxygen. If they desaturated below 92% or experienced profound dyspnea with ambulation, they were placed back on CPAP or oxygen only for an additional 30 min. This process was repeated every 30 min until patients were able to maintain SpO2 of ≥ 92% or had completed 120 min of treatment. Data were recorded on a standard paper data collection form.

Primary outcome: Time from the initiation of CPAP or oxygen‐only to able to ambulate on 2 L/min of nasal oxygen with an SpO2 of ≥ 92% (this was the a priori definition of clinical HAPE resolution) at which time patients were reassessed and dispositioned. The time of entry of the disposition order was defined as the HAPE resolution time. Secondary outcomes included improvements in vital signs and SpO2.

After obtaining written informed consent, the subjects were randomized using the sealed envelope method with opaque mask packets. Packets contained either an O2 Max Trio CPAP mask system set to 10 cm of water pressure or an identical system in which the pressure valve was modified to deliver no positive pressure. Both systems provided FiO2 of 90%. Packets were labeled with a computer‐generated random number sequence and stored as mixed control and experimental packets in a designated location at each ED. Only the primary investigator had access to the key to unmask blinding.

The sample size was calculated to detect a 20% difference in time to HAPE resolution, with 80% power (α = 0.05). The projected sample size of 264 subjects was not achieved due to equipment expiration. Enrollment was paused during the COVID‐19 pandemic, and while recruitment resumed afterward, full enrollment was not possible before the study equipment expired.

Data were analyzed by group (CPAP vs. oxygen‐only). To ensure similar groups, baseline demographics, tobacco use, and native altitude were compared for ratio‐level data. Mean and standard deviation and independent sample Student t‐test were used to compare groups. Numbers and percentages along with chi‐squared analysis were used to compare groups. Mean and standard deviation of time to disposition, as well as pulse oximetry, breaths/min, heart rate, and mean arterial blood pressure over time were tabulated by group and displayed using box and whisker plots. Differences by group were assessed using repeated measures analysis. All analyses were based on cases with complete data; significance was assumed at p ≤ 0.05.

Patient characteristics in both the control (n = 27) and CPAP (n = 31) intervention groups are described in Table 2. Both the control and CPAP groups were similar in terms of age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and tobacco use. Native altitude by group was also similar. Among CPAP patients, average native altitude was 482 ± 710 ft, compared to 371 ± 326 ft in the standard of care group (t = 0.747, p = 0.458). Days from altitude arrival to dyspnea onset were also similar by group (t = 0.116, p = 0.908). Among CPAP patients, average days from altitude arrival to dyspnea onset were 1.29 ± 0.97 days, compared to 1.26 ± 1.06 days among the standard of care patients. Likewise, days from dyspnea onset to emergency department presentation were similar by group, 1.70 ± 1.12 vs. 1.78 ± 1.05 days in the CPAP and standard of care patient groups, respectively (t = −0.270, p = 0.708). BMI in the control group had a mean of 29.6 compared to the CPAP group's 29.3 (t = −0.265, p = 0.431). Of note in both groups, average age was young (35‐38), tobacco use was infrequent (< 13%), and patients were overwhelmingly male (93%–97%). Cough as a presenting symptom was also similar by group (p = 0.303).

Indicative of HAPE, vital signs were atypical. Physiological derangement in both groups at baseline was similar (all p > 0.05) (see Table 3). In general, patients were remarkably desaturated on room air pulse oximetry at baseline, which was 67% ± 13%. Respirations/min were slightly elevated, ~21 ± 3 breaths/min, in both groups on average. Both groups also exhibited mild tachycardia at ED presentation; the average heart rate was 105 ± 17 beats/min. Arrival mean arterial blood pressure was also elevated, 110.39 ± 13.91 mmHg, and similar in both groups.

Recovery from acute HAPE presentation, in terms of pulse oximetry, respirations, heart rate, and mean arterial blood pressure, was similar by group, suggesting no benefit of CPAP over standard of care. For example, mean SpO2 improved from ~80% on arrival to ~94% at 2 h in both groups (see Figures 1, 2, 3, 4). Repeated measure analyses for all physiological assessments demonstrated recovery from HAPE ED presentation (Greenhouse–Geisser p < 0.05), but no statistically significant difference by group (p > 0.05). In addition to a per protocol analysis of all patients with complete data an intention to treat analysis also revealed no statistically significant benefit from CPAP.

Average time in the ED from arrival to discharge did not vary by group (t = −1.053, p = 0.297), although time to discharge was 20 min less in the CPAP group. Among CPAP patients, average time to disposition was 158 ± 62 min; average time to disposition was 178 ± 86 min in the standard of care group (see Figure 5).

Both high‐flow oxygen therapy and CPAP are effective treatments for HAPE. Although not evidence‐based, CPAP with high‐flow oxygen is a locally common practice in Colorado high‐altitude emergency departments for HAPE patients. This practice probably originated from clinician experience with the treatment of cardiogenic pulmonary edema with CPAP. This study sought to evaluate the efficacy of CPAP compared to the standard therapy of high‐flow oxygen treatment. CPAP has theoretical therapeutic advantages for HAPE, as it does in cardiogenic pulmonary edema. However, no statistically significant decrease in time to clinical recovery or physiological assessment was noted in this trial. Although terminated early due to COVID‐19 and underpowered, the pattern of response between groups was similar. Quicker resolution of HAPE with CPAP would likely be clinically insignificant since time to HAPE resolution with high‐flow oxygen was rapid. These findings suggest that discontinuation of CPAP use for standard HAPE patients would be a reasonable choice, as it could limit ED resource utilization. In an austere environment where oxygen and nifedipine are unavailable for use as pulmonary vasodilators, CPAP would still be a reasonable option if available; however, this scenario was not within the scope of this trial. No ED fatalities or intubations occurred in the 464 unrecruited patients treated for HAPE. This investigation did not include patients with imminent ventilatory failure. Those individuals could potentially benefit from CPAP to reduce the intubation rate, as supported by a recent case report [33]. The patients studied in this trial do not provide objective data to draw such a conclusion, but it is reasonable to propose that CPAP may decrease intubations in HAPE, as in cardiogenic pulmonary edema by decreasing the work of breathing through positive pressure. In review of all interventions, one patient in the control group and two patients in the CPAP group received dexamethasone. All doses of dexamethasone were administered orally within 15 min of the patient discharge and were unlikely to have any significant effect on the study results. One control patient received intravenous ondansetron and three CPAP patients received oral acetaminophen. Again, these interventions were unlikely to influence results. Two patients in the CPAP group received a liter of intravenous crystalloid during the study period.

CPAP requires more resources compared with high‐flow oxygen [34]. The type of CPAP administered in this trial required large amounts of oxygen because positive pressure was derived from oxygen flow rates. This CPAP system is commonly used in prehospital care. CPAP delivered by a conventional hospital‐based CPAP machine would have significantly lower oxygen supply requirements but would necessitate the presence of both an appropriate device and additional specially trained personnel [35]. Case reports describe the use of CPAP machines without supplemental oxygen to treat HAPE in austere environments [28]. In this study, patients received both positive pressure and high‐flow oxygen, making it difficult to predict the utility of CPAP without an additional oxygen source.

In this trial, HAPE was predominantly observed in young, healthy males. Female sex may be protective against HAPE. This study did not examine any potential mechanisms underlying this observation. Animal studies have shown that estradiol causes prostacyclin release and increased nitric oxide production in the pulmonary vasculature, reducing pulmonary vasoconstriction [36, 37, 38]. Additional research has demonstrated estradiol‐mediated inhibition in the production of hypoxia‐induced endothelin‐1, a biochemical initiator of HAPE pathophysiology [39, 40]. Thus, the presence of estradiol may be protective against HAPE due to decreased hypoxia‐induced pulmonary vasoreactivity. However, this has limited clinical utility in the ED, as animal studies on estradiol supplementation involved pretreatment for at least 1 week before assessing pulmonary artery response to hypoxia [40]. The prothrombotic effects of exogenous estrogen further complicate its use as a potential intervention, making additional research in this area less appealing [41].

Future research in high‐resource settings should explore adjunctive pharmacologic treatments for HAPE. While high‐flow oxygen therapy is rapid and effective, hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction responds to nifedipine and phosphodiesterase inhibitors [23]. A prospective study investigating the use of phosphodiesterase inhibitors as adjunctive therapy for HAPE, particularly in comparison to nifedipine, would be valuable.

In retrospect, including a group that received CPAP with a lower FiO2 would have provided valuable insight into its utility in low‐resource environments where it may be the only available treatment. Lightweight, battery‐powered CPAP units capable of delivering 20 cm H2O of positive pressure are now commercially available [42].

Consistent with previous findings, HAPE was more prevalent in our population from December to April [43]. This seasonal variation in HAPE incidence might be attributable to ambient temperature, a known risk factor for HAPE [5]. However, regional sporting activities vary by season. During winter months, individuals ascend rapidly to mountain summits via ski lifts, whereas in warmer months, they typically reach summits by hiking. The additional time required for hiking may promote acclimatization and reduce HAPE incidence. Given the mean time from altitude arrival to symptom onset in our study exceeded 1 day, we are unable to draw any meaningful conclusions with regard to this phenomenon.

Our study has multiple limitations. Most significantly, the current study was underpowered, and the projected sample size was not obtained owing to equipment expiration and COVID‐19. This may have led to an underestimation of the true effect of CPAP due to a Type II error. The study was initiated in November 2019 and suspended from March 2020 to July 2022 due to potential CPAP aerosolization of viral particles. Low recruitment was primarily due to the experiment being conducted in busy community emergency departments with limited personnel for research. Although a preplanned interim data analysis was intended at 50% sample recruitment, this was not performed due to the low enrollment and early termination of the study. The universal application of oxygen to experimental subjects limits the generalization of study results to lower resource settings where CPAP might be available, but without oxygen. CPAP alone without exogenous oxygen was not tested. All three study sites are within the same hospital system, which may affect external validity. However, sex‐specific incidence and seasonal effects in the current data and the existing literature from the same geographic location were similar [9, 34]. The majority of the subjects were recruited from one of the three facilities, leading to potential site selection bias. This facility's location has the largest number of visitor accommodations, many of which are at a higher elevation than the resort base elevation, likely leading to a higher incidence of HAPE. Unmasking of blinding could have occurred based on the physical appearance as well as the sensation of positive pressure despite identical masks. One patient in the CPAP group did refuse reapplication of the CPAP mask after the initial titration to nasal cannula oxygen. This study also lacks a measure of patient satisfaction with the treatments, making it impossible to comment on patient preference. It is possible there was a subjective reduction in dyspnea while on CPAP that is not reflected in our data. The lower incidence of HAPE during warmer months raises the possibility of a disparity in the populations present at different times of the year, potentially introducing selection bias.

In summary, both CPAP with high‐flow oxygen and high‐flow oxygen alone are highly effective in treating HAPE. CPAP did not produce an observable reduction in clinical resolution time. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the study's limited power. High‐flow oxygen therapy achieves similar benefits while requiring significantly fewer resources and less oxygen. HAPE is more prevalent in males and has a higher incidence during colder months.
