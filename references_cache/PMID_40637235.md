# FASTKD5 processes mitochondrial pre-mRNAs at noncanonical cleavage sites.
**Authors:** Antonicka H, Vučković A, Weraarpachai W, Hong S, Brischigliaro M, Ahn A, Barrientos A, Hillen HS, Shoubridge EA
**Journal:** Nucleic Acids Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1093/nar/gkaf665](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkaf665)

## Content

The first post-transcriptional step in mammalian mitochondrial gene expression, required for the synthesis of the 13 polypeptides encoded in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), is endonucleolytic cleavage of the primary polycistronic transcripts. Excision of the mtDNA-encoded transfer RNAs (tRNAs) releases most mature RNAs; however, processing of three noncanonical messenger RNAs (mRNAs) not flanked by tRNAs (CO1, CO3, and CYB) requires FASTKD5. To investigate the molecular mechanism involved, we created knockout human cell lines to use as assay systems. The absence of FASTKD5 produced a severe OXPHOS assembly defect due to the inability to translate two unprocessed noncanonical mRNAs and predicted altered folding patterns specifically at the 5'-end of the CO1 coding sequence. Structural features 13-15 nt upstream of the CO1 and CYB cleavage sites suggest FASTKD5 recognition mechanisms. Remarkably, a map of essential FASTKD5 amino acid residues revealed RNA substrate specificity; however, a key, putative active site residue was required for processing all three noncanonical pre-RNAs. Mutating this site did not significantly alter the binding of any client RNA substrate. A reconstituted in vitro system showed that wild-type, but not mutant, FASTKD5, was able to cleave client substrates correctly. These results establish FASTKD5 as the missing piece of biochemical machinery required to completely process the primary mitochondrial transcript.

Mammalian mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) codes for 13 polypeptides that are essential structural subunits of the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complexes, and the 22 transfer RNAs (tRNAs) and 2 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) required for their translation on specialized ribosomes in the mitochondrial matrix. Transcription of mtDNA produces two polycistronic transcripts from the so-called heavy and light strands of mtDNA that must be processed by endoribonucleases to release the mature RNA species. Maturation of most mitochondrial RNAs occurs by cleavage of tRNAs at the 5′ end by mitochondrial RNase P and at the 3′ end by mitochondrial RNase Z, a concept known for decades as the tRNA punctuation model [1]. Mitochondrial RNase P lacks an RNA component and is rather composed of three protein subunits [2]. A cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the enzyme bound to RNA showed that two of the subunits (TRMT10C and SDR5C1) form a platform that binds RNA, positioning the catalytic subunit PRORP to cleave the tRNA substrate at the precise 5′ end [3]. ELAC2, the RNase Z that processes tRNA 3′ ends, is targeted to both the nucleus and mitochondria [4] and recent cryo-EM structures show that the same accessory subunits used by RNase P are also required for the complete 3′ end processing of mitochondrial tRNAs with degenerate structures [5–7].

The molecular machinery required to mature messenger RNAs (mRNAs) that are not flanked by tRNAs remained unknown until we demonstrated that depletion of FASTKD5, a member of the FASTK-family of proteins, resulted in the accumulation of unprocessed transcripts from precisely those noncanonical open reading frames: the 5′ end of cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (MT-CO1), the junction between the bicistronic MT-ATP8/6 mRNA and MT-CO3, and the junction between the MT-ND5 and MT-CYB mRNAs [8]. These results were subsequently confirmed in another cell line [9]. It is not known whether FASTKD5 complexes with or recruits other proteins, or whether it possesses endoribonuclease activity itself.

The FASTK (Fas-activated serine-threonine kinase) family, which is comprised of six proteins: FASTK and FASTKD1-5, appeared early in the evolution of Metazoa and phylogenetic analysis shows that expansion of the family occurred in the vertebrate lineage. FASTK has a cytosolic and mitochondrial isoform, but all others localize exclusively to the mitochondrial matrix (reviewed in [10]). While all FASTK family members appear to have specific roles in mitochondrial RNA transactions, the precise molecular mechanisms of action remain largely obscure [9]. FASTK, FASTKD1, FASTKD2, and FASTKD5 co-localize with markers of mitochondrial RNA granules, which are centers of posttranscriptional processing and modification of mitochondrial RNAs [8], the others appear more broadly distributed in the matrix. All contain a predicted mitochondrial matrix targeting sequence (MTS), two FAST domains (FAST1, FAST2, of unknown function), and a so-called RAP domain (RNA-binding domain enriched in apicomplexans) at the extreme C-terminus. The RAP domain of FASTKD4 was suggested to adopt an endonuclease fold similar to that found in PD-(D/E)-XK nucleases [11], but a recent study has shown that FASTKD4 does not possess a nuclease activity, but rather its function was adapted to ensure a proper maturation and polyadenylation of the MT-ND3 mRNA [12]. All mammalian FASTK family members also contain a variable number of heptatricopeptide (HPR) repeats which overlap with the FAST1 domain. These repeats are reminiscent of octotricopeptide repeat domains (OPR) [13] and the PPR domains of pentatricopeptide repeat proteins [14], both of which are RNA-binding domains.

Here we have investigated the biochemical consequences of FASTKD5 loss, mapped the amino acid residues essential for its function using a null cell line as an assay system, characterized the pre-mRNA structural determinants required for FASTKD5-mediated processing, and demonstrated, using an in vitro reconstituted system, that FASTKD5 alone can process specific pre-mRNA substrates.

The 143B and HEK293T cell lines, derived knock-out (KO) clones and derived cell lines expressing FASTKD5 constructs were grown in high glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2. The medium for the KO clones and derived cell lines expressing FASTKD5 constructs was supplemented with 50 μg/ml of uridine. Cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

For protein stability experiments, selected cell lines were grown in six-well plates in regular medium supplemented with 10 μg/ml cycloheximide for 0, 8, 16, 24, 32, and 48 h, after which cells were collected by trypsinization for downstream analysis.

FASTKD5 KO cell lines were generated by CRISPR/Cas9–mediated gene editing using a gene-specific target sequence (sgKD5 sequence is indicated in Supplementary Table S1) cloned into pSpCas9(BB)-2A-Puro (PX459) V2.0 [15] for 143B cells or Synthego Corporation (Redwood City, CA, USA) guide RNAs (gRNAs) for HEK293T cells. The plasmid was transfected into 143B cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following day, transfected cells were selected by addition of puromycin (2.5 μg/ml) for 2 days. HEK293T cells were transfected with gRNAs and then plated into 96-well plates. Single cell clones were screened for loss of FASTKD5 and MT-CO1 protein by immunoblotting with a protein specific antibody (Sigma, Supplementary Table S1) and frameshift mutations were confirmed by genomic sequencing. Two 143B derived clones and one HEK293T clone were selected for downstream analysis.

143B and the KO cell lines were reconstituted with FASTKD5 constructs by retroviral infection with a virus produced in Phoenix cells transfected with pBABE-3xFLAG-Puro plasmids as described previously [16]. Briefly, retroviral constructs were transiently transfected into the Phoenix packaging cell line using the HBS/Ca3(PO4)2 method. 143B and KO cells were infected 48 h later by exposure to virus-containing medium in the presence of 4 μg/ml of polybrene as described [17].

The wild-type (WT) FASTKD5 cDNA in pDONR221 [18] was cloned into Gateway modified pBABE-3xFLAG-Puro plasmid [19] using Gateway technology (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The single point mutations and FASTKD5 deletion mutants were engineered using QuikChange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent) with primers indicated in Supplementary Table S1 according to manufacturer’s instructions.

For recombinant protein expression, the sequence coding for FASTKD5 lacking the predicted N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (Δ1–27) was polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplified from human complementary DNA (cDNA). The sequence was cloned into 14-C (for bacterial expression; kind gift from Dr Scott Gradia; Addgene plasmid #48309) and 438-C (for insect cell expression; kind gift from Dr Scott Gradia; Addgene plasmid #55220) [20] vectors in frame with an N-terminal 6xHis tag followed by a tobacco etch virus (TEV) cleavage site. The mutant variant of FASTKD5-D429N was generated by site-directed mutagenesis using Round-the-Horn PCR amplification of WT FASTKD5 in the 14-C vector as the template.

The sequences of all plasmids were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Images were processed in Fiji [21].

Briefly, pelleted 143B, KO, and over-expressing cells were resuspended in PBS at a final concentration of 5 mg/ml. Digitonin was added to the cells at a ratio of 0.8 mg digitonin/mg of protein, and the samples were incubated on ice for 5 min, diluted to 1.5 ml with PBS, and centrifuged for 10 min at 10 000 × g, at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended in a buffer containing 75 mM Bis–Tris (pH 7.0), 1.75 M aminocaproic acid, and 2 mM EDTA at a final protein concentration of 2 mg/ml. One-tenth volume of 10% n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside was added to the samples, and the samples were incubated on ice for 20 min, followed by centrifugation at 20 000 × g for 20 min, at 4°C. Protein concentration in supernatants was determined by Bradford assay.

SDS–PAGE was used to separate whole cell extracts. A total of 20–50 μg of protein was run on either 10% or 12.5% polyacrylamide gels. BN-PAGE was used to separate individual OXPHOS complexes. Twenty micrograms of mitoplasts were run on native 6%–15% polyacrylamide gradient gels as previously described [23]. Separated proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane and immunoblot analysis was performed with indicated antibodies (Supplementary Table S1).

In case of HEK293T cells, whole-cell extracts were prepared by solubilizing the cells in RIPA buffer, and equal amounts of total cellular protein were loaded in each lane and separated by SDS–PAGE using a 17.5% polyacrylamide gel. Spectrophotometric assays of whole cell extracts were used to measure enzyme activities as described previously [25, 26]. Briefly, cells were grown on 10 cm plates to about 90% confluency, washed twice with PBS and scraped in 1 ml ice-cold PBS. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 10 000 × g for 1 min at 4°C. Cell pellets were resuspended in 50 mM triethanolamine pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA and homogenized with motorized pestle for 15s. Citrate synthase activity was measured as an increase in absorbance at 412 nm at 30°C in 100 mM Tris–HCl pH7.4, 25 μM acetyl-coenzyme A, 200 μM Ellman’s reagent (DTNB), 0.1% Triton X-100 with or without 70 μM oxaloacetic acid (pH 7.4) as a substrate. Cytochrome c oxidase (COX) activity was measured as a decrease in absorbance at 550 nm at 30°C in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1% n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside and 50 μM reduced cytochrome c as a substrate. COX activity was normalized to citrate synthase activity.

Two 15-cm dishes of WT or HEK293T FASTKD5-KO cells, respectively, grown at 80% confluency, were inoculated into one liter of FreeStyle 293 expression medium supplemented with 50 ml FBS, 50 μg/ml uridine, and 10 ml of 100× antibiotic/antimycotic. Cells were grown at 37°C under 5% CO2, spinning at 63 RPM for 4 days. Mitochondria were isolated as previously described [27] and resuspended in 1 ml of STE buffer (0.32 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris–HCl, and 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4). Three micrograms of freshly prepared mitochondria were pelleted by centrifugation at 10 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. For one set of experiments, the pellet was resuspended in 150 μl of Na-cacodylate in organello translation buffer (300 mM Na-cacodylate pH 8.0, 100 mM mannitol, 10 mM sodium succinate, 80 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM Na-phosphate, 60 mg/ml of each of the 20 amino acids including methionine, 5 mM ATP, 20 mM GTP, 6 mM creatine phosphate, and 60 mg/ml creatine kinase) that had been preheated to 37°C and incubated for 5 min at 37°C. A freshly prepared and preheated mixture of 150 μl of the Na-cacodylate translation buffer plus 3 μl of dimethyl sulfate was added to the mitochondria, resulting in a final DMS concentration of 1%. The samples were then incubated for 5 min at 37°C for DMS labeling. To quench the DMS reaction, 180 μl of β-mercaptoethanol were added to the mitochondria, the solution was mixed, and the samples were centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The mitochondrial pellets were washed twice in 1 ml of glycerol wash buffer (10% glycerol, 0.15 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.8), following the same centrifugation conditions indicated above and resuspended in 1 ml of TRIzol for RNA extraction.

RNA was extracted following the TRIzol manufacturer’s specifications. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, and an equal volume of 100% isopropanol and 3 μl of glycogen were added to precipitate the RNA. The samples were incubated at −80°C for 1 h and centrifuged at 15 000 × g for 20 min, at 4°C. RNA was resuspended in 50 μl of RNase-free water and quantified by measuring absorbance at a wavelength of 260 nm with Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Ten micrograms of RNA was DNase-treated using the TURBO DNase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After treatment, the RNA was purified using RNA Clean and Concentrator − 5 kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNase treatment and column purification were repeated two additional times to ensure the complete removal of all DNA.

One microgram of DNase-treated RNA per reaction was used as the input for the rRNA depletion. First, 1 μl of the rRNA subtraction oligo mix (500 nM mitochondrial rRNA oligonucleotides, and 250 nM cytosolic rRNA oligonucleotide) and 2 μl 5× hybridization buffer (1× concentration: 200 mM NaCl, 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4) were added to each reaction, and the final volume was adjusted with RNase-free water to 10 μl. The samples were denatured at 95°C for 2 min, and then the temperature was reduced by 0.1°C/s until the reaction reached 22°C to allow annealing. Next, a mix of 2 μl RNase H buffer, 2 μl thermostable RNase H (NEB), and 6 μl of water was added. The samples were incubated at 65°C for 5 min. Free DNA oligos were degraded by adding a mix of 2.5 μl TURBO DNase, 5 μl TURBO DNase buffer, and 22.5 μl of water, and incubating at 37°C for 30 min. The RNA was cleaned with RNA Clean and Concentrator − 5 kit (Zymo Research), following the manufacturer’s instructions for size-selection of RNA molecules > 200 nt, and eluted in 12 μl of RNase-free water.

Sequencing libraries were generated using the xGen Broad-range RNA library preparation kit from IDT, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with the following modifications. The RNA fragmentation step was conducted by combining 1 μg of RNA with 1 μl of reagent F1, 4 μl of F3, and 2 μl of F4, and incubating at 95°C for 2 min. The samples were immediately placed on ice and combined with a mixture of 1 μl Induro Reverse Transcriptase (NEB), 1 μl R1 reagent, and 1 μl 0.1 M DTT. This mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature before adding 2 μl of reagent F2, and then placed in a thermocycler for 10 min at 20°C, 10 min at 42°C, and 60 min at 55°C. Then, 1 μl of 4M NaOH was added, and the samples were incubated at 95°C for 3 min. At this point, the sample was removed from the thermocycler and placed on ice, and 1 μl of 4M HCl and 27 μl of IDTE (10 mM Tris, 0.1 mM EDTA) were added. After the indexing PCR, the indexed libraries were resolved on an 8% TBE gel for 55 min at 180 V and size-selected for products ∼300 nt. The products were sequenced using the NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina) with PE 150, 250M reads per sample.

MitoDMS-MapSeq data were analyzed using SEISMIC-RNA v0.20.0 for resolving RNA secondary structures (https://github.com/rouskinlab/seismic-rna) [28]. Specific parameters were applied as follows: Positions with a mutated fraction >15% were excluded (–max-fmut-read 0.15). Only reads aligned to the heavy strand of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) were included in the analysis (–sep-strands). A mutation ratio corresponding to the 95th quantile was used for normalization (-q 0.95). The maximal base-pairing distance was set to 350 bases (–fold-md 350).

Read depth across the mitochondrial genome and ΔDMS reactivity were visualized using Circos (https://circos.ca) [29]. Read depth per position was normalized by the average depth across all positions. ΔDMS reactivity values were calculated as the average over a 10-nucleotide sliding window. To minimize biases due to low read counts, tRNA, rRNA, and other noncoding RNAs were excluded from the plots. A small nonzero value (0.001) was added to mutation ratios to allow calculation of the log2 fold difference. Data generated by SEISMIC-RNA were processed using custom Python scripts for visualization. RNA secondary structure arc plots were generated using VARNA (https://varna.lisn.upsaclay.fr) [30].

The cleavage assays were carried out in 10 μl reactions containing RNA cleavage assay buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 4 mM DTT), 200 nM 3′-end Cy3 or Cy5 labeled RNAs (Supplementary Table S1) and 0.3 μM FASTKD5 (identical results were obtained with protein purified from insect cells or E. coli). For the cleavage assays, the RNA cleavage assay buffer and FASTKD5 protein were first mixed and incubated at 30°C for 5 min. RNA was added, and reactions were incubated at 30°C for a further 60 min. The reactions were quenched with an equal volume of 2 × RNA loading buffer (6.4 M urea, 2 × Tris-borate-EDTA buffer, 50 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and 1.6 U of recombinant proteinase K (New England Biolabs) was added. The reactions were incubated at 30°C for 60 min or at 50°C for 10 min and stored at −20°C until analysis by urea–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). Before urea–PAGE, the reactions were thawed and incubated at 95°C for 3 min. A 10 μl portion of each reaction was loaded onto 7 M urea, 10% polyacrylamide (acrylamide to bis-acrylamide ratio 19:1) denaturing gels in 1 × Tris–borate–EDTA buffer. After electrophoresis, gels were imaged using an INTAS imaging system (INTAS Science Imaging, Germany). microRNA marker (NEB) and Low Range ssRNA ladder (NEB) were used to estimate the size of the RNA bands. Cy3- and Cy5-labeled RNA migrates at an apparent size of ∼+3 and +4 nucleotides, respectively. The size of RNA indicated in the figures is the actual RNA size.

This assay was based on the method described [34]. First, an RNA cleavage assay was carried out in 10 μl reactions containing RNA cleavage assay buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 4 mM DTT), 200 nM 3′-end Cy5 labeled RNAs (Supplementary Table S1) and 0.3 μM FASTKD5. The RNA cleavage assay buffer and FASTKD5 protein were first mixed and incubated at 30°C for 5 min. RNA was added, and reactions were incubated at 30°C for a further 60 min. FASTKD5 was denatured at 95°C for 5 min and 3 μl of the reaction was either left untreated [incubated in 1× rCutSmart buffer (New England Biolabs)] or treated with either Quick CIP (New England Biolabs, in 1× rCutSmart buffer) or T4 PNK (New England Biolabs, in 1× rCutSmart buffer + 5 mM DTT) at 37°C for 30 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 80°C for 5 min. Next, a universal linker DNA oligonucleotide 5′-phospho-ATG TGA GAT CAT GCA CAG TCA TA-3′-NH2 was ligated to the RNA using T4 RNA ligase 1 (New England Biolabs) at 37°C for 3 h, followed by inactivation of RNA ligase at 65°C for 15 min. The One-step RT-PCR kit (QIAGEN) was then used for the amplification of the resulting products using a gene-specific forward primer and the anti-linker reverse primer. PCR products were separated on 8% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels and visualized using SYBR™ Gold stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer sequences used for the mitochondrial polyadenylation tail (MPAT) assay are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Previous investigations showed that FASTKD5 was required for the complete processing of noncanonical pre-mRNAs (not flanked by tRNAs) in the primary mitochondrial polycistronic transcript [8, 9]. To investigate the precise molecular basis for FASTKD5 activity, we first sought to create and characterize a cell line lacking FASTKD5, which we could then use as an assay system to assess the functionality of specific domains/amino acid residues in the FASTKD5 protein. We used CRISPR/Cas9 editing to create a knockout (KO) line in 143B cells and propagated them in medium containing pyruvate and uridine, which supports the replication of cells with deficient OXPHOS activity. We isolated and characterized two independent KO clones (sgKD5/1 and sgKD5/2). Immunoblot analysis of the steady-state levels of mtDNA-encoded polypeptides showed specific decreases in MT-CO1 (CO1) and MT-CYB (CYB) (for simplicity, in all figures and the subsequent text, the prefix MT- was omitted), both encoded by noncanonical pre-mRNAs, but not ND1 or ATP6 (Fig. 1A). Retroviral expression of a FLAG-tagged WT FASTKD5 construct rescued this phenotype, demonstrating the specificity of the CRISPR/Cas9 editing. Immunofluorescence analysis using an antibody specific for CO1, showed complete loss of CO1 at the single cell level in the FASTKD5 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B) and rescue of the phenotype only in those cells expressing the FLAG-tagged WT construct (Fig. 1B).

We next proceeded to investigate the effects of the FASTKD5 KO on RNA processing by Northern blot analysis (Fig. 1C) which showed near complete loss of the mature, processed forms of the CO1, CO3, and CYB mRNAs, a phenotype rescued by expression of the FLAG-tagged WT construct. The lack of processing resulted in a decrease of total mRNA levels (processed and unprocessed) for the CO1 transcript as determined by RT-qPCR analyses (Supplementary Fig. S1C), but not any other transcript. We conclude that the complete loss of FASTKD5 function specifically affects the processing of only the noncanonical pre-mRNAs in the primary polycistronic transcript.

We subsequently examined the effects of the FASTKD5 KO on the translation of mtDNA-encoded polypeptides by pulse-labeling with [35S]-Met/Cys in the presence of emetine, an inhibitor of cytosolic translation (Fig. 1D). Quantification of these results (Supplementary Fig. S1F) showed that the translation of CO1 and CYB was specifically decreased, to near zero, consistent with the results of the Northern blot analysis (Fig. 1C) demonstrating that the presence of 5′-UTRs on these transcripts prevents translation on mitochondrial ribosomes. The translation of CO3 was, however, not affected in the KO cells, although the level of the mature CO3 mRNA was virtually undetectable by Northern blot. This directly demonstrates that CO3 encoded by a tricistronic mRNA (ATP8/6-CO3) can be synthesized by the mitochondrial ribosome as has been suggested previously [35].

Analysis of the assembly of the OXPHOS complexes by BN-PAGE demonstrated that complexes III and IV failed to assemble, as expected from the mitochondrial translation experiments (Fig. 1E). However, we also observed a near complete lack of fully assembled complex I even though the mtDNA-encoded subunits of this complex were translated at near normal levels. The complex I assembly defect can be ascribed to the complete absence of complex III, whose presence is necessary for the biogenesis/stability of complex I [36, 37]. The maximal enzyme activity of complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) was also virtually undetectable as expected from the BN-PAGE results (Fig. 1F).

To investigate the impact of the deletion of FASTKD5 on the steady-state levels of other proteins involved in posttranscriptional regulation of mitochondrial gene expression, we performed immunoblot analyses for a wide spectrum of proteins that localize to the mitochondrial RNA granule [8] and are involved in RNA processing, the pseudouridine synthase module [38] as well as proteins involved in mitochondrial transcription and translation (Supplementary Fig. S1A). None of these proteins were altered by deletion of FASTKD5 or by re-expression of the FLAG-tagged construct. The levels of mtDNA were also not significantly altered by the FASTKD5 KO (Supplementary Fig. S1E), and neither was the appearance or number of mitochondrial nucleoids and RNA granules (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

Finally, to test whether the OXPHOS assembly defects in the FASTKD5 KO cells made the cells uridine auxotrophs, we omitted uridine from the medium and observed that the growth rate was severely depressed, an effect that was rescued by expression of a WT FASTKD5 construct (Fig. 1G).

While we have recently reported the mature mRNA structurome in WT HEK293T cells [39], the folding of nascent human mtRNAs before processing into mature transcripts remains largely unexplored. Specifically, it is not known whether particular conformations in precursor transcripts might facilitate their recognition by FASTKD5 at noncanonical processing sites. To address this question, we employed the mitoDMS-MaPseq approach [39], which permits mapping unpaired, accessible sites on mRNA to predict RNA folding, using mitochondria isolated from WT and FASTKD5 KO cells. For this we generated and characterized a FASTKD5 KO HEK293T cell line (labeled FASTKD5-KO, to discriminate from FASTKD5 KO in 143B cells). Consistent with observations in the 143B background, the FASTKD5-KO cells exhibited profound reductions in the steady-state levels of CO1 and CYB (Supplementary Fig. S2A) due to the inability of mitochondrial ribosomes to translate the corresponding unprocessed transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S2B). To enhance the original mitoDMS-MaPseq method, we made slight modifications by labeling actively translating, energized mitochondria using an in organello translation buffer that included all 20 amino acids (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section).

Using mitoDMS-MaPseq on WT and FASTKD5-KO mitochondria, we achieved high sequencing coverage (>100K reads) across the entire mitochondrial transcriptome (Fig. 2A and B). The samples had high signal-to-noise ratios in the mRNAs from both strands, and the DMS reactivity results were highly reproducible between independent WT and FASTKD5-KO biological replicates (R2 > 0.95). Although the normalized average read count for most mature transcript regions was comparable between WT and FASTKD5-KO samples, it was markedly decreased for CO1 and slightly increased for CO2, CYB, and ND5 (Fig. 2A and B), suggesting altered transcript stability, in agreement with the Northern-blot data in 143B KO cells (Fig. 1C). As expected, the number of reads before and across the noncanonical processing sites in the 5′ end of CO1, the ND5-CYB intergenic region, and the ATP8/6-CO3 junction was strikingly increased in FASTKD5-KO samples (Fig. 2C), corroborating the essential role of FASTKD5 in generating the corresponding mature transcripts. When correlating population average DMS reactivities between the WT and KO for each individual transcript, we observed a moderate correlation for the L-strand ND6 transcript (R2 = 0.7892) between the WT and FASTKD5-KO, while the coding regions of all H-strand mt-mRNAs showed an excellent correlation for (R2 = 0.9248–0.9740) (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). Among the H-strand transcripts, the reactivities for CO1 showed the most scattered correlation and lowest R2 (0.9248). Notably, we detected spikes of noncorrelating DMS reactivities at the 5′ ends of CYB and CO1, which remain unprocessed in FASTKD5-KO mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. S3A). These discrepancies arise from the low levels of unprocessed transcripts that accumulate in WT mitochondria. Despite the overall good correlation in reactivities, the DMS signal across the CO1 coding sequence is lower in FASTKD5-KO mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. S3A), suggesting that the unprocessed transcript may be less structured.

We next examined the unprocessed transcripts accumulating in FASTKD5-KO mitochondria for potential folding patterns. Whereas the ATP8/6-CO3 mRNA junction is unstructured, the unprocessed 5′ regions of CO1 mRNA and CYB mRNA exhibit several hairpin structures starting 13–15 nt upstream of the processing site (Fig. 3B). Although similar structures were modeled in WT mitochondria, the modeling had low confidence due to the low steady-state levels of unprocessed transcripts, which resulted in insufficient data (Fig. 2C). Remarkably, inspection of the sequence between the structured region and the processing site at the 5′ end of CO1 and CYB, revealed a particular identity, including a CCA triplet just before the cleavage site (Fig. 3C). These findings support the hypothesis that FASTKD5 may use a combination of sequence and structural information to process CO1 and CYB mRNA precursors with specificity and fidelity.

To investigate how FASTKD5 functions, we first examined the role of individual functional domains in the processing of noncanonical pre-mtRNAs. We suspected that all domains in FASTKD5 would be necessary for function; however, we formally investigated this by deleting them individually and testing whether any of the deletion mutants would restore function in FASTKD5 KO cells. Deletion of any of the domains (MTS, heptatricopeptide repeats, FAST1, FAST2, and RAP domains) resulted in complete loss of function as analyzed by immunoblot analysis or immunofluorescence at the single cell level (Supplementary Fig. S4).

We hypothesized that the heptatricopeptide repeats likely contain RNA-binding/recognition properties and that the region containing the endonuclease-like fold may contain the active site. We constructed an extensive series of single amino acid mutants, guided by evolutionarily conserved residues in FASTKD5 homologues across species and other FASTKD family members. Additionally, we included several variants of unknown significance identified in subjects with potential FASTKD5 deficiency.

We introduced a total of 53 amino acid substitutions, primarily replacing conserved residues with alanine (Supplementary Table S1), and assessed their ability to rescue FASTKD5 function using immunoblotting and immunofluorescence with an anti-CO1 antibody (Fig. 4A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S5). Figure 4A and B shows typical results from a dispensable and an essential amino acid residue. We confirmed that the residues we mapped as essential all affected pre-mRNA CO1 processing (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S6A). We modeled all the profiled variants on the AlphaFold-predicted model of FASTKD5 (Fig. 4D), creating a map of 21 essential and 32 dispensable amino acids for the expression of the CO1 polypeptide. Northern blot analysis of processing of ATP8/6-CO3 and ND5-CYB transcripts surprisingly showed that some amino acid residues that were essential for processing of CO1 were dispensable for processing of CO3, and further, some residues essential for processing of the latter were dispensable for processing of CYB (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S6A). Rescue of CYB processing resulted in the rescue of CYB synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S6B) and CYB steady state levels (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. S5). Maps of the essential vs. dispensable residues for the three different processing events are shown on AlphaFold-predicted structures (Fig. 4G). Remarkably, only seven of the residues required for CO1 processing were necessary to process CYB, and no residues dispensable for CO1 processing appeared as essential for CYB processing. We conclude that there is a hierarchical dependence of FASTKD5 function in processing noncanonical pre-mRNAs that is context (pre-mRNA)-dependent, consistent with the RNA structure data (Fig. 3).

Immunoblot analysis of the expression of individual variants identified three variants that were expressed significantly less than the WT FASTDK5 or the other variants, suggesting that they are important for protein stability. To formally address this, we measured the half-life of these variants (Y125C, D249A, E317A) and compared it to the WT protein and the D589A and D429A variants (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. S5C) following inhibition of cytosolic translation with cycloheximide. The half-life of Y125C, D249A, and E317A variants was 3–6 h, compared to 37 h for the WT protein, demonstrating that these are critical for the stability of FASTKD5 protein and interestingly they all cluster together in the heptatricopeptide repeat domains (Fig. 4I). The D589A variant also had a shorter half-life (14 h), while the D429A variant was even more stable than the WT protein (50 h, Fig. 4H).

Our mutational analysis identified four aspartate/glutamate residues that are essential for processing of all three pre-mRNAs (D249, E317, D429, D589); however, they do not cluster in one domain of the protein (Supplementary Fig. S7A). In all four cases, the D/E residues were mutated to alanine (A), a substitution that might lead to a disruption of polar interactions within the protein, leading to destabilization of the protein, as we saw in case of E317A and D249A, and partially for D589A. For this reason, we mutated these amino acids to asparagine/glutamine (N/Q) and assessed their ability to rescue noncanonical processing in FASTKD5 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Like the D/E to A mutants, the D249N and E317Q mutants were still unstable, as evidenced by their low expression in all cell lines. All four mutants were deficient in rescuing CO1 expression. However, the D589N, D249N, and E317Q mutants restored CYB expression, while the D429N mutant did not. These data suggest that the D429 residue of FASTKD5 plays a critical role in the processing of both CO1 and CYB pre-mRNAs. As several essential positively charged residues (Fig. 4D and G) that are predicted to bind RNA cluster mostly near D429, we speculate that this region contains the RNA binding/recognition site and possibly the active cleavage site.

To test whether FASTKD5 on its own can accurately process pre-mRNA substrates we purified FASTKD5 protein (lacking the MTS) and tested whether it could induce specific cleavage on synthetic RNA substrates. To this end, we used synthesized single-stranded RNAs, that were either potential substrates or downstream sequences, tagged at the 3′ end with the fluorophore Cy3 or Cy5. FASTKD5 processed all three substrates at the expected sites, producing a band running at 24 nt (Fig. 5A), with no cleavage observed for an unspecific transcript. For all three noncanonical substrates, other smaller cleavage products in much lower quantities were also observed (Fig. 5A). The fraction of processed RNA was FASTKD5 dose-dependent (Fig. 5B). We further examined the cleavage of a pre-mRNA CO1 sequence with a longer 5′-end (Fig. 5C) and showed that it was cleaved as efficiently as the shorter sequence. We conclude that FASTKD5 possesses an intrinsic activity that enables it to correctly process client RNA substrates. Its inability to process control (unspecific) RNA sequences outside of target 5′ UTRs (Fig. 5A and B) indicates that this activity is not due to nonspecific nuclease function. However, the presence of additional, unexpected cleavage products on authentic substrates suggests that other factors may be necessary to ensure specificity or enhance cleavage efficiency.

Our RNA structure analysis identified similarities in the sequence pattern for two FASTKD5 substrates, CO1 and CYB (Fig. 3C). We tested whether a CCA triplet just preceding the cleavage site is necessary for processing (Fig. 5D and F). Mutation of CCA to CCG diminished the processing of CO1 and practically abolished the processing of CYB pre-mRNA. Interestingly, the insertion of a CCA motif into the unspecific mRNA (Fig. 5E and F, CCU→CCA) resulted in a product of the expected size, albeit the processing was not very efficient. These data imply that the CCA motif in the CO1 and CYB pre-mRNAs is important but not sufficient for FASTKD5 recognition of the cleavage site. As several mitochondrial transcripts utilize AUA as their start codon (ND5, ND1, and ND3), we further tested whether the substitution of AUG with AUA in the CO1 and CYB pre-mRNAs would affect the processing by FASTKD5 (Fig. 5D and F). We observed that although some accurate processing produced a band at 24 nt, additional cleavage products were also observed for both substrates. A processing defect has also been observed in cells where the AUG in the CYB transcript was mutated to AUA (Dr Michal Minczuk, personal communication). These results underscore the importance of a sequence fidelity that is pre-mRNA specific for pre-mRNA processing by FASTKD5.

A recent study by Clemente et al. [40] showed that processing of noncanonical mitochondrial transcripts (especially ATP8/6-CO3 and ND5-CYB) resulted in the formation of 3′ phosphates, which are further hydrolyzed by the phosphatase activity of ANGEL2. To determine whether pre-mRNA processing by FASTKD5 produces 3′-phosphates/2′-phosphates (and 5′-OH) or 5′-phosphates (and 3′-OH), we utilized a modified MPAT assay (Fig. 5G), that requires a ligation of a linker to a free 3′-OH group that would result in a PCR product of 40 bp for all three substrates. We show (Fig. 5H) that such products were not present for any substrate, and only appeared after treatment with PNK, a T4 polynucleotide kinase, which hydrolyses phosphomonoester bonds or 2′,3′-cyclic phosphodiester ends. In the case of the 5′ end-CO1, treatment with CIP that hydrolyses phosphomonoester bonds, also resulted in a PCR product of 40 bp. These data suggest, that FASTKD5 processing of noncanonical pre-mRNAs results in products with a 3′/2′-phosphate or a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphodiester.

Our mutational analysis identified D429 as a crucial amino acid in FASTKD5 for processing noncanonical pre-mRNAs. To test the effect of this mutation on RNA cleavage in an in vitro assay, we generated a recombinant D429N FASTKD5 variant and compared its activity to WT FASTKD5 (Fig. 5I). Consistent with findings from the FASTKD5 KO model, the D429N variant showed markedly diminished processing activity for all three noncanonical transcripts. To determine whether this mutant could still bind RNA, we performed fluorescence anisotropy measurements (Fig. 5J). The results indicate that both WT FASTKD5 and the D429N mutant can bind substrate RNAs with comparable affinities (estimated dissociation constant Kd of 0.08–0.17 μM). Taken together, our data suggest that D429 of FASTKD5 plays a crucial role in the processing of noncanonical junctions, but it does not abolish binding of the protein to the RNA.

This study demonstrates that the loss of FASTKD5 results in a severe deficiency in the assembly of multiple OXPHOS complexes resulting from an inability to process the noncanonical pre-mRNAs within the primary polycistronic mitochondrial transcript, extending our previous observations [8]. This defect prevents the translation of at least two mRNAs, encoding CO1 and CYB, supporting observations that mRNAs with 5′-UTRs generally fail to efficiently load onto mitochondrial ribosomes to form an initiation complex [35, 41]. Aside from the two known bicistronic transcripts (ATP8/6 and ND4L/ND4), one exception to this rule is the CO3 mRNA, which can be loaded on to mitochondrial ribosomes and translated as part of the tricistronic ATP8/6-CO3 transcript [35]. Despite undetectable levels of the mature CO3 mRNA in FASTKD5 KO cells, CO3 mRNA translation proceeds normally downstream of the ATP8/6 overlapping reading frames. Notably, the total steady-state levels of CO1 and CYB mRNAs were uniquely reduced in the FASTKD5 KO cells, likely reflecting increased turnover due to their inability to associate with the mitochondrial ribosome. This observation was further supported by the pulse translation experiment, which demonstrated that CO1 and CYB were the only proteins whose synthesis was significantly reduced, reaching almost undetectable levels in the KO cells.

These mitochondrial transcript processing defects resulted in a complete failure to assemble Complexes III and IV, and consequently, a near-total loss Complex I due to its structural dependency of Complex III [36, 37]. The loss of OXPHOS capacity rendered the KO cells uridine auxotrophs, as pyrimidine biosynthesis is intimately coupled to a functional mitochondrial respiratory chain.

A previous study investigating FASTKD5 function in haploid Hap1 cells using CRISPR/Cas9 editing to disrupt FASTKD5, did not show defects in CYB translation or assembly of Complexes III/I [9]. This discrepancy may partly reflect cell type-specific responses to FASTKD5 loss. However, in that study KO cells retained residual FASTKD5 protein, which could partially compensate for processing defects. Similarly, our earlier study in 143B cells, where FASTKD5 protein levels were decreased by siRNA (short interfering RNA)-mediated knockdown [8], did not show defects in ND5-CYB processing, implying that even a small amount of FASTKD5 protein may be sufficient to process CYB pre-mRNA correctly.

To gain insight into the determinants of substrate recognition by FASTKD5 in vivo and to assess whether precursor and mature RNAs fold differently, we utilized the mito-DMS-MaPseq approach to examine the folding landscape of precursor RNAs that accumulate in FASTKD5-KO mitochondria. Although research on how RNA structure influences and evolves during processing is still emerging, evidence in nonmitochondrial systems suggests that RNA structure changes at various stages of a molecule’s lifecycle. For instance, substantial differences between nuclear and cytosolic RNA structures have been observed in Arabidopsis, indicating notable transitions from precursor RNA to mature transcript [42]. In humans, however, the dynamics of structural changes during transcript processing remain less clear, raising questions about the consistency and predictability of such refolding events. Given that RNA structure influences interactions with regulatory RNA-binding proteins and other nucleic acids, transcript refolding during processing may have significant downstream effects on transcript stability and gene expression. Our research on FASTKD5-KO mitochondria indicates that among the three precursor transcripts that remain unprocessed, only the coding region of CO1 exhibits clear structural deviations compared to its mature counterpart. This variation could be influenced by the heavily structured 5′ end of the unprocessed transcript. Noteworthy, the unprocessed CO1 did not only exhibit a distinct folding pattern but was also unstable. These two properties are probably related as the unstable mature CO1 transcript that accumulates in the absence of the mRNA stability factor LRPPRC has also a more relaxed folding than in WT cells [39].

Whether FASTKD5 possesses endonuclease activity or recognizes its substrates and recruits other proteins with such activities to mature the noncanonical substrates, has been a subject of speculation [8–10]. We demonstrate unequivocally, using an in vitro reconstituted system, that FASTKD5 possesses intrinsic activity sufficient to correctly process three different synthetic pre-mRNA substrates of the primary polycistronic mitochondrial transcript. Thus, complete maturation of the primary mitochondrial transcript requires the concerted activity of three different RNA processing factors: RNase P, RNase Z, and FASTKD5. Pre-mRNAs flanked by tRNAs adopt characteristic tertiary structures, and both RNase P and RNase Z utilize two additional subunits (TRMT10C and HSD17B10) as RNA recognition platforms to process their substrates accurately [5–7,43]. While PRORP (MRPP3), the catalytic component of RNase P, can process some pre-mRNAs independently, its processing rate is 10 times slower without these co-factors, and not all substrates are efficiently cleaved [43]. We used an endpoint assay to assess FASTKD5 activity and so it is not possible to estimate turnover. It is possible that FASTKD5 may similarly require auxiliary factors for optimal activity, particularly in the context of pre-CO3 and pre-CYB processing, where multiple cleavage products were observed. In our previous studies we used immunoprecipitation experiments [8] and BioID proximity interaction analysis [18] to identify FASTKD5 interactors and showed that FASTKD5 interacts with proteins of the RNA granule, including FASTKD family members FASTKD4 and FASTKD1 and RNase P subunits TRMT10C and PRORP.

Among the FASTKD family members, FASTKD4 has also been implicated in processing one noncanonical pre-mRNA, the ND5-CYB junction [9, 11]. However, this processing event is only partially abrogated in FASTKD4 KO cells and pulse translation experiments revealed no observable deficiency in the synthesis of any of the mtDNA-encoded polypeptides. Double KO (FASTKD4/5) Hap1 cells exhibit more severe ND5-CYB processing defects than the single KO cells, but with no apparent defect in the translation of CYB mRNA. Our data confirm that FASTKD5 is essential for the processing of the pre-mRNA ND5-CYB junction and that endogenous FASTKD4 cannot compensate for its absence. Whether FASTKD4 somehow collaborates with FASTKD5 in this process will require further investigation. Interestingly, while FASTKD4 has also been shown to bind the poly(A) tail of the ND3 transcript, and its loss destabilizes ND3 mRNA in HAP1 cells [12], our data show that FASTKD5 KO cells exhibit increased ND3 transcript levels (Supplementary Fig. S1C) without a corresponding rise in ND3 polypeptide synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S1F), suggesting additional layers of post-transcriptional regulation.

The idea that RNA processing occurs co-transcriptionally implies that primary transcript maturation events occur with relatively uniform kinetics. Recent estimates from the Churchman lab [35] indicate transcription of the polycistronic transcripts proceeds at <1 kb/min, and that the half-lives of the pre-mRNAs (canonical and noncanonical) range from 1 to 39 min, consistent with the idea that the two processes are coupled. We have previously shown that tRNA processing occurs in a defined sequence: 5′-end processing by RNase P precedes 3′ cleavage by RNase Z [7]. If, as suggested above, FASTKD5 uses a platform on which to process its substrates, its nature and regulation may determine the order in which canonical and noncanonical cleavage events occur. Notably, an in vivo tissue-specific KO of PRORP showed that the noncanonical pre-mRNAs were not processed when 5′-tRNA cleavage was absent [44], suggesting that tRNA excision is a pre-requisite for FASTKD5 function. Whether this dependency is direct or results from disruptions in canonical RNA processing remains an open question.

Several studies on nonmitochondrial RNA systems have identified transcriptome-wide patterns of RNA structures in nascent RNAs and highlighted their potential impact on RNA processing, particularly during splicing, and gene expression [45–48]. Our data suggest that in mitochondria, noncanonical RNA processing may vary between the ATP6-CO3 junction and the other sites, suggesting multiple mechanisms of RNA recognition to optimize FASTKD5 activity and specificity. Notably, the 5′ ends of unprocessed CO1 and CYB exhibit distinct structures 13–15 nt upstream of the processing site, which follows a CCA triplet. This observation is reminiscent of the RNase BN/RNase Z from E. coli, which cleaves various substrates, including tRNAs, after a CCA motif [49, 50]. The relevance of RNA structural motifs has also been demonstrated for bacterial RNase E, which recognizes RNA structures through an RNA duplex binding surface to position its active site relative to a single-stranded substrate [51]. By analogy, FASTKD5 may preferentially interact with stem-loop structures at the 5′ ends of unprocessed CO1 and CYB, facilitating access of its catalytic domain to the precise processing site. Alternatively, the mRNA structures could limit FASTKD5 scanning to facilitate precise cleavage. The importance of both the CCA motif prior to the cleavage site of CO1 and CYB, as well as the AUG start site sequence suggests that FASTKD5 might also recognize a specific primary sequence for adequate processing.

We revealed that pre-mRNA cleavage by FASTKD5 produces 5′-OH and 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate (for ND5-CYB and ATP6-CO3 substrates) or 3′/2′ monophosphate (for 5′ end-CO1) in vitro. All metal-ion independent RNases generate 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate products, whether protein enzymes or ribozymes [52]. These RNases process single stranded RNA substrates that are extended and distorted at the cleavage site to expose the scissile phosphate to 2′-OH attack. The sequence recognition as well as the active sites of these RNases vary extensively; however, they all require a general base, a general acid and positively charged amino acids in their active site [52]. FASTKD5 does not require a metal ion for RNA processing in vitro and the proposed active site contains several positively charged essential amino acids. Thus, we can speculate that FASTKD5 belongs to a family of metal-ion independent RNases. The processing of noncanonical pre-mRNAs has been suggested to produce 3′-phosphates in vivo, that are removed by ANGEL2 to allow for polyadenylation of the ND5 and ATP8/6 transcripts [40]. However, ANGEL2 has been shown to hydrolyze 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate and 2′-phosphate, but not 3′-phosphate in vitro [40, 53]. Interestingly, ANGEL2 has been shown to interact with FASTKD4 in Drosophila [40] as well as in human cells [18], but not with FASTKD5. Although highly speculative, it is possible, that FASTKD4 mediates an interaction between FASTKD5 and ANGEL2 to facilitate the maturation of 3′-ends after noncanonical processing.

A surprising outcome of our FASTKD5 mutational analysis is that while many of the tested amino acid residues are required for pre-CO1 processing, fewer are required for pre-CO3 and even fewer for pre-CYB. Moreover, several additional or alternative cleavage products were observed for CO3 and CYB in the in vitro assay. FASTKD5 was able to bind CO1 and CO3 pre-mRNAs with similar affinities in vitro, confirming our previous results in vivo, where RNA-immunoprecipitation experiments showed that FASTKD5 was able to bind to CO1, CO3, and CYB mRNAs [8]. Substrate processing times also differ among substrates. For instance, CYB processing generally occurs co-transcriptionally, while CO1 processing can take up to 15 min [35]. In contrast, the ATP8/6-CO3 junction, which lacks the structural features characteristic of CO1 and CYB, is the slowest, taking over 20 min [35]. Given that the tricistronic ATP8/6-CO3 transcript remains translation-competent [54], it is plausible that the translation of ATP6 on mitochondrial ribosomes may impact the processing kinetics of the ATP8/6-CO3 junction. These data suggest that FASTKD5 does not process all substrates identically, and that other proteins may contribute to cleavage recognition site and specificity through substrate-specific mechanisms.

To gain some insight into the nature of the endonuclease active site of FASTKD5, we mutated all evolutionary conserved aspartate and glutamate residues. We identified D429 as the only residue essential for processing all noncanonical pre-mRNAs in our cellular model and confirmed its crucial role in an in vitro cleavage assay. D429 is adjacent to a cluster of essential positively charged amino acids, which may together form the FASTKD5 active site. Our in vitro binding data suggest that the mutation of D429 to asparagine does not abolish RNA binding, suggesting that this mutant can bind noncanonical RNA substrates but has impaired cleavage activity. While these data provide strong functional evidence, solving the structure of substrate-bound FASTKD5 will be necessary to definitively characterize its active site and elucidate the precise mechanism by which it processes noncanonical pre-mRNAs in the primary mitochondrial polycistronic transcript.
