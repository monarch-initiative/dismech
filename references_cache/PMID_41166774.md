# Nationwide Study of the Epidemiology and Clinical Features of X-Linked Adrenoleukodystrophy in Denmark.
**Authors:** Videbæk CS, Dunø M, Lindquist SG, Larsen VA, Kjær MW, Melgaard L, Nielsen JE, Grønborg SW, Lund AM
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgme.2025.109265](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgme.2025.109265)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVE: This nationwide, single-cohort study provides a comprehensive analysis of the epidemiology of X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD) in Denmark. We examined incidence, age at symptom onset, and sex-stratified survival outcomes to explore differences in symptom development. Findings were compared with international cohorts to contextualize the Danish results.

METHODS: We included all individuals with genetically confirmed ALD residing in Denmark, with no age restrictions. Patients were born between 1911 and 2020. Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from medical records. Where available, dried blood spots collected at birth and stored in the Danish National Biobank were analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry to quantify C26:0-lysophosphatidylcholine (C26:0-LPC). Cumulative incidence functions were used to estimate the risk of developing specific ALD phenotypes over time.

RESULTS: A total of 113 individuals (49 males, 64 females) met inclusion criteria. The point prevalence of ALD was 1.42 per 100,000, and the average birth incidence from 1932 to 2023 was 1.81 per 100,000 (males: 1.7; females: 1.9). Thirty-four distinct pathogenic ABCD1 variants were identified, with c.1679C > T, p.(Pro560Leu) being the most common (21 cases). By age 60, 76 % of males had developed adrenomyeloneuropathy (AMN), 44 % had developed cerebral ALD (cALD), and 44 % had adrenal insufficiency. Among females, 80 % had developed AMN by the same age. AMN was associated with the longest diagnostic delay, averaging 8 years in males and 9 years in females.

DISCUSSION: This study offers a rare, nationwide view of ALD spanning over a century of births alongside measurement of C26:0-LPC in historical, neonatally collected dried blood spot cards from 14 individuals in this cohort. While the birth incidence aligns with some other natural history studies, it remains lower than in countries with universal newborn screening. The high frequency of AMN in both sexes highlights the need for greater clinical awareness. Notably, our findings confirm that the majority of women with ALD will develop neurological symptoms over time, challenging the long-standing perception of female carriers as largely asymptomatic. The relatively low detection of cALD and adrenal insufficiency in adults suggests possible underdiagnosis and calls for improved long-term follow-up, especially in adult neurology and endocrinology.

1. Introduction
X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD) is caused by pathogenic var -
iants in the ABCD1 gene located on the X chromosome, leading to 
impaired beta-oxidation and accumulation of very long-chain fatty acids 
(VLCFA) [ 1 ]. The reported incidence of ALD varies greatly between 
studies. In older population-based studies, incidences of 0.5 – 3.6/ 
100,000 males [ 2 – 8 ] have been described (Table A.1), whereas newer 
studies based on newborn screening (NBS) programs found birth prev -
alences as high as 1/4845 (20.6/100,000) [ 9 ]. With improved tech -
nologies and the introduction of NBS for ALD in several countries, early 
diagnosis has become more feasible, but predicting individual disease 
course remains a major challenge.
The phenotype of ALD varies and spans from asymptomatic patients 
to the onset of fatal cerebral ALD (cALD) in early childhood. Almost all 
male patients will eventually develop Addison ’ s disease and a myelop -
athy/peripheral neuropathy (adrenomyeloneuropathy (AMN)) 
throughout their lives [ 10 ]. Some patients develop cALD, a fatal, central 
demyelination that can present in childhood (31 – 35 %), adolescence 
(4 – 7 %), or adulthood (2 – 5 %) [ 1 ]. Addison ’ s disease is treated with 
hormone substitution, and cALD can be treated with hematopoietic stem 
cell transplantation (HSCT), or gene therapy if treatment is initiated in 
early disease stages; otherwise, treatment is only supportive [ 11 , 12 ]. 
Female carriers have traditionally been described as asymptomatic, but 
newer research indicates that up to 80 % of female carriers will even -
tually develop symptoms of AMN [ 10 , 13 ].
No finite predictive measure of phenotype has been established to 
predict disease course. C26:0-lysophosphatidylcholine (C26:0-LPC) is 
currently being investigated as a potential predictive biomarker of 
phenotype [ 14 , 15 ]. Neurofilament light polypeptide is also being 
explored as an early indicator of cALD [ 16 – 20 ], but as of today, the 
disease course of patients who are diagnosed pre-symptomatically 
through family cascade testing or NBS cannot be predicted, and 
instead, they are followed up in a systematic surveillance program 
which includes assessment of adrenal function every three to 12 months 
and cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans every six to 12 
months [ 12 ]. Females are only followed up clinically, and routine MRI 
and screening for adrenal insufficiency for females with ALD is not 
reccomened [ 12 ].
This study explores the epidemiology of ALD in Denmark, with a 
focus on comparing incidence rates, genotype, and phenotype distri -
bution in the Danish population against published international data. 
We further investigated age at symptom onset and survival in both males 
and females with ALD, alongside an analysis of C26:0-LPC as a potential 
biomarker for assessing disease severity.
2. Methods
2.1. Study design
This nationwide single-cohort study was performed according to the 
STROBE statement [ 21 ]. Data was collected from May 2023 to July 2025 
from the patients ’ medical records. All data was collected and stored 
using RedCap Database electronic data capture tools hosted at Copen -
hagen University Hospital Rigshospitalet [ 22 , 23 ].
2.2. Study population
We aimed to include all individuals diagnosed with ALD in Denmark. 
Inclusion criteria were male and female subjects with genetically 
confirmed ALD (defined as likely pathogenic and pathogenic variants in 
the ABCD1 gene) and Danish residency. There were no age limitations.
According to current national guidelines, pediatric patients with ALD 
should be followed at Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet, 
and follow-up of adults with ALD is limited to a few specialized centers, 
but is mainly carried out at Copenhagen University Hospital, 
Rigshospitalet [ 24 , 25 ]. Patients were identified through their ICD-10 
code (E713A) or OMIM code (300100) at Copenhagen University Hos -
pital, Rigshospitalet, specifically within the pediatric, neurological, and 
genetic departments. Additionally, the genetic department cross- 
checked their internal results database to identify individuals with 
pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the ABCD1 gene. As part of 
this process, we also cross-checked with laboratory data system at the 
national center for inborn errors of metabolism. This provided an 
overview of all diagnoses made at the center as well as those registered 
by the hospital ’ s genetic department.Furthermore, we contacted all 
pediatric, neurological, endocrinological, and genetic departments in 
Denmark to enquire if they followed patients with ALD. We provided 
them with the ICD-10 code for ALD to search their patient registries. We 
also inquired if the endocrinological departments had Addison ’ s disease 
patients who could be relevant to test for ALD andchecked whether 
genetic counseling and testing of relevant family members had been 
offered in affected families.
2.3. Study variables
Study variables included demographic information (birthday, sex, 
survival status) about the patient; data related to their ALD-diagnosis 
(including age at first symptoms and diagnosis, whether the patient 
had been diagnosed due to symptoms or cascade testing, the patient ’ s 
genotype and phenotype, family history of ALD, and VLCFA concen -
trations); results from the latest neurological, MRI, and endocrinological 
evaluation; if treatment had been initiated we included data related to 
this; and data on social demographics including educational status. For 
all patients with an MRI available, we obtained a Loes score, which is an 
imaging-based severity score used to quantify cerebral involvement in 
cALD [ 26 ].
Patients were categorized into ALD phenotypes as described by 
Moser et al. [ 27 ] Cerebral involvement was characterized as a Loes score 
above 0.5 with contrast enhancement and progression after six months. 
The diagnosis of cALD had to be given by a neurologist or a pediatric 
neurologist. For patients where scans were not available (older medical 
records), it was agreed that a description of this based on evaluation by a 
neuroradiologist and the diagnosis given by a neurologist/pediatric 
neurologist was sufficient. Adrenal insufficiency was characterized as an 
inadequate rise in cortisol (defined as ≤ 420 nmol/L) after adrenocor -
ticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation test and the introduction of 
hormone substitution treatment. If results from the ACTH stimulation 
test were not available, it was agreed that the criteria were met if it had 
been described by an endocrinologist/pediatric endocrinologist in the 
medical records. For the very few patients for whom no stimulation test 
had been described, it was deemed sufficient if the diagnosis had been 
confirmed by a senior endocrinologist/pediatric endocrinologist in the 
medical charts, and the patient was being monitored and treated for 
adrenal insufficiency.
2.4. Ethical approvals
The study was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee in the 
Capital Region of Denmark (H-23007728). A waiver of consent was 
given for this study.
2.5. Analysis of VLCFA
Analysis of C22:0, C24:0, and C26:0 was performed in EDTA-plasma 
using gas chromatography – mass spectrometry (GC – MS). Prior to 2013: 
C22:0, C24:0, and C26:0 were converted to their methyl esters, quan -
tified, and ratio-calculated employing heneicosanoic acid (C21:0) and 
heptacosanoic acid (C27:0) as internal standards. For diagnostic evalu -
ation, only the C26:0/C22:0 and C24:0/C22:0 ratios were utilized.
From 2013 onwards: C22:0, C24:0, and C26:0 were quantified and 
ratio-calculated using isotope-labeled internal standards in accordance

2.6. Analysis of C26:0-LPC
Dried blood spot samples were retrieved from the Danish National 
Biobank for individuals in the cohort born after 1982. The blood spots 
were taken 48 – 72 h after birth for samples taken after 2009 and 4 – 9 
days for samples before 2009, with a heel prick, and the blood was 
transferred to a filter paper card. Sample preparation was performed 
using the CE certified Revvity Neobase 2 kit (Waltham, MA, US), with 
the only exception being that the extract was diluted tenfold. Samples 
were analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry 
(LC-MS/MS) using a Thermo Scientific Quantiva MS/MS instrument 
(Waltham, MA, US) coupled to a high-performance liquid chromatog -
raphy (HPLC) Thermo Scientific Vanquish system (Waltham, MA, US). 
The column was from Thermo Scientific (Hypersil Gold 3 UM 10 ×
2.1MM, Cat. 25,003 – 012101 set in a UNIGUARD Holder Cat. 852 – 00). 
Samples were run under isocratic conditions, 0.5 mL/min of the kits flow 
solvent for 2 min. Transitions monitored was 636.5 to 104.1 Da for 
C26:0-LPC and 640.5 to 104.1 Da for the deuterated standard. Both with 
a RF-lens of 137.8 V and collision energy of 27.4 V.
2.7. Statistic analysis
We used cumulative incidence functions to estimate the risk of 
developing different ALD phenotypes, accounting for competing risks. 
The only competing event considered was death before onset of the 
phenotype.
The analysis was performed in R version 4.3.0 (2023-04-21 ucrt) 
using the Prodlim package, with age as the underlying time scale. Time 
started at birth. For each phenotype (cALD, adrenal insufficiency, or 
AMN), individuals were classified into three outcome groups: 
I. Phenotype development: Individuals who developed the pheno -
type of interest. We used the age at the earliest recorded mention 
of symptoms. If this was unavailable but the patient had symp -
toms at the time of diagnosis, we used age at diagnosis.
II. Competing risk (death): Individuals who died before developing 
the phenotype. We used age at death.
III. Censored (asymptomatic): Individuals who did not develop the 
phenotype of interest during the observation period. We used age 
at data collection as the censoring time.
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize cohort characteristics, 
with medians and ranges reported for continuous variables and per -
centages for categorical variables. Missing values were left as NA. For 
the cumulative incidence analysis, patients were excluded if event status 
or follow-up time was missing, if neither time of symptom onset nor time 
of diagnosis was available, or if phenotype status was unknown.
3. Results
We identified 113 patients (49 males, 64 females) with genetically 
confirmed ALD ( Table 1 ). Three patients were from Århus Uni -
versitetshospital, Skejby, the remaining were identified at Copenhagen 
University Hospital, Rigshospitalet. Clinical data to establish the pa -
tients ’ phenotype were available for 41 males and 36 females. 85 pa -
tients were alive at the time of data collection (32 males and 52 females; 
survival status unknown for one female). 17/49 males had passed away 
at the time of data collection. Of these, 8/17 had passed away due to 
cALD. The median age of death due to cALD was 10 years (range 5 – 49). 
The cause of death was unknown for 8/17 males, and one passed away 
due to cancer. In the group of patients with an unknown cause of death, 
the median age of death was 42 years (range 39 – 60). The oldest patient 
was born in 1911, and the youngest in 2020. 10 females in our cohort 
had passed away. The median age of death was 74 (range: 61 – 88), and 
the cause of death was unknown for seven. One died due to sepsis, one 
due to pneumonia, and one due to liver failure.
3.1. Prevalence and incidence
Inhabitants in Denmark were 5,995,628 as of 01.12.2024. This 
means that the point prevalence of ALD in Denmark is 1.42 per 100.000 
inhabitants. The average birth incidence from 1932 to 2023 was 1.81/ 
100,000 for both male and female patients (1.7/100,000 for males and 
1.9/100,000 for females). Table 2 provides an overview of the evolution 
of the birth incidence from 1931 to 2020.
3.2. Genetic data
We identified 34 different pathogenic variants, the most common 
being c.1679C > T, p.(Pro560Leu), which was identified in 21 in -
dividuals from three different families. See Table 3 for a list of identified 
variants and Table A.2 for a classification of variants that have not 
previously been described.
3.3. Biochemical data
Measurements of C22:0, C24:0, and C26:0 were available for 22 
patients (14 males), see Table 4 . C26:0/C22:0 and C24:0/C22:0 ratios 
were available for 52 patients (32 males) and were elevated (ref: C26:0/ 
C22:0 > 2.2 and C24:0/C22:0 > 104) in all but one male. This patient 
had symptoms of AMN, adrenal failure, a known pathogenic variant in 
the ABCD1 gene, and elevated C24:0/C22:0. F or seven patients, it was 
noted in the patient chart that VLCFAs were elevated, but the result of 
measurement was not provided. For 19 individuals, their dried blood 
spot card was available for analysis of C26:0-LPC; of these, 14 had both 
phenotype and time of symptom onset available. A comparison of these 
factors can be seen in Fig. 1 .

41 (44 %) male patients had adrenal insufficiency; of these, three (7 %) 
patients only needed supplementary cortisol when sick or in stressful 
situations, and 5/41 patients had no other symptoms than Addison ’ s

3.4.2. Adrenal insufficiency
18 patients in our male cohort had adrenal insufficiency. Of these, 
five patients had no other manifestations of ALD than adrenal insuffi -
ciency at the time of their latest clinical assessment. 8 patients presented 
with adrenal insufficiency as their first manifestation of ALD. The cu -
mulative incidence plot ( Fig. 2 B) illustrates the increasing risk of adrenal 
insufficiency with age. From this analysis, we see that at age 60, 44 % 
(95 % CI: 26 %–61 %) will have developed adrenal insufficiency.
3.4.3. AMN
Males with the AMN phenotype were the largest phenotypic group. 
3/19 had presented with adrenal failure and had thus already been 
diagnosed at the time of onset of neurological symptoms. 1/19 had been 
diagnosed due to cascade screening. The remaining patients presented 
with symptoms related to AMN. The percentage of AMN patients in our 
cohort is 48 %, but from the cumulative incidence analysis, it becomes 
apparent that a large part of the patients who do not have AMN are 
young, and most of the older, male ALD population has developed AMN. 
The most common symptom among AMN patients was spasticity in the 
lower (17/19) extremities. Followed by reduced muscle strength in the 
lower extremities (15/19) and urge incontinence (7/19). In Fig. 2 C, we 
have provided the cumulative incidence curve for AMN in the male 
cohort, which shows that at age 58, 76 % (only one patient was still in 
the analysis at age 58 for this group, so 95 % could not be calculated) of 
patients will have developed AMN.
AMN was the only phenotype identified in females with ALD in our 
cohort. 23/64 females had received an AMN diagnosis and were 
symptomatic at the time of data collection. Of these, 15 had been 
diagnosed due to cascade testing, and 8 had been diagnosed due to 
symptom onset and were the index cases in the family. The median time 
from symptom onset to diagnosis in the group that had not been diag -
nosed due to cascade testing (8/23) was 9 years (see Table 1 ). Fig. 2 D 
illustrates the increasing risk of AMN among females with age. From the 
cumulative analysis of females with a known phenotype, we see that at 
age 60, 80 % (95 % CI: 64 %–95 %) of females will have developed AMN. 
Reproductive decisions.
Of the females included in this study, four chose preimplantation 
genetic testing (PGT-M). 10 underwent prenatal testing for ALD during 
pregnancies. The first prenatal diagnosis of a fetus was carried out in 
1990, and the most recent in 2022. No patient chose to continue a 
pregnancy with a male fetus hemizygous for a pathogenic ABCD1 
variant; in one case with a female fetus heterozygous for the family’s 
ABCD1 variant, pregnancy was terminated.
4. Discussion
In this nationwide ALD study, we explored the characteristics of the 
Danish ALD population. We found the point prevalence of ALD in 
Denmark to be 1.42 per 100,000 inhabitants and a high rate of AMN 
compared to any other phenotype.
The actual birth incidence of ALD has become more uncertain 
following the implementation of NBS for ALD [ 9 ]. Studies using NBS 
data have reported incidences ranging from 1/4845 to 1/51,081 [ 9 ]. In 
contrast, our study found an average birth incidence of 1.81/100,000 
and a point prevalence of 1.42/100,000. However, it is important to 
note that our birth incidence estimate spans a long period (from 1932 to 
2023), which may introduce ascertainment bias. This potential bias is 
further reflected in the fluctuating birth incidences over the years, 
peaking at three per 100,000 during the 1960s. The higher incidence 
observed in NBS programs may partly reflect the detection of screen 
positives with ABCD1 variants of unknown significance (VUS). Since the 
introduction of NBS, the number of reported VUS in the ABCD1 database 
has increased, with 62 % of NBS-identified variants classified as VUS
29
. 
Individuals with these variants often show borderline C26:0-LPC levels 
and lack a family history of ALD, adding diagnostic uncertainty [ 31 ]. 
This was illustrated in California, where raising the second-tier C26:0- 
LPC cut-off led to a significant decrease in birth incidence and a drop in 
percentages of VUS cases [ 32 ]. Higher incidence rates in NBS studies 
may also reflect ascertainment bias, as NBS may be more effective at 
detecting milder or even subclinical ALD phenotypes than clinical 
evaluation alone. However, this is challenged by the fact that all families 
in our study underwent extensive cascade testing, which should have 
identified even mild cases. The differences between incidences found in 
natural history studies and NBS studies highlight the need for careful 
interpretation of screen positives in NBS studies to ascertain whether 
they have ALD or not, especially when dealing with VUS.
We observed a significant drop in incidence after 2000 ( Table 2 ), 
likely due to several factors. Notably, incidence among females was 
particularly low from 2000 to 2020. In Denmark, carrier screening is not 
routinely offered to healthy females under 18, meaning some at-risk 
girls in known ALD families remain untested. Additionally, from the 
1990s onward, families with a known ALD diagnosis increasingly 
adopted family planning strategies to avoid passing on the condition. An 
increased interest in prenatal diagnosis was also seen in this study, as has 
been documented previously [ 33 , 34 ] and likely contributed to the 
decline in birth incidence. Finally, as shown in this study, diagnoses may 
be delayed in patients with de novo ABCD1 variants or in families not yet 
identified as affected.
The phenotype distribution in our study differs notably from previ -
ous natural history studies (see Table 5 ). We observed a lower propor -
tion of patients with cALD and a higher proportion with AMN (see 
Table 5 ). Our cumulative incidence analysis for cALD onset shows the 
biggest increase from age five to 17, aligning with earlier reports that 
state that the risk of cALD is highest in childhood [ 10 ]. About 27 % 
developed cALD during this period, which is slightly lower than the 
historically reported 33 % before the age of 12 [ 30 ]. This discrepancy 
may be due to the smaller number of older males in our cohort, leading 
to wider confidence intervals and greater uncertainty. Another 
contributing factor could be the frequency of monitoring: in Denmark, 
children receive MRIs every six months until age 12 and annually 
thereafter, while adults are scanned only when clinically indicated. 
Consequently, silent, stable lesions in adults may go undetected. This 
lack of data also h the need for specific clinical guidelines for adult 
patients with ALD. Although comprehensive guidelines exist for the 
pediatric ALD population [ 12 ], it is our impression that specific guide -
lines for adult patients, taking into account age, overall health status, co- 
morbidity, HSCT eligibility, and AMN disease stage, would be helpful to 
ensure optimal follow-up.
11/16 patients with cALD had symptoms of cALD at the time of their 
diagnosis, and only one patient unambiguously met the criteria for HSCT 
[ 42 ] and underwent successful presymptomatic transplantation after 
family screening. This patient remains symptom-free post-transplant (2- 
year observation period). Another patient, transplanted after symptom 
onset, experienced disease progression and died shortly after, which is 
consistent with prior findings [ 42 , 43 ] and shows the importance of early 
detection. In our cALD cohort, we observed an average diagnostic delay 
of one year, which often precludes timely treatment of this fatal 
phenotype and stresses the critical value of newborn screening. This is 
further supported by a recent French study that emphasized both the 
clinical and economic burden of childhood cALD and the importance of 
early diagnosis [ 44 ].
The proportion of patients with isolated adrenal insufficiency in our 
cohort is consistent with previous studies (see Table 5 ). However, the 
overall number of patients who developed adrenal insufficiency was 
lower than expected. We saw no increase in risk after the age of 41, at 
which point the cumulative risk reached 44 %. This is notably lower 
than prior estimates, suggesting that nearly all males with ALD even -
tually develop adrenal insufficiency [ 10 ]. These findings may reflect

underdiagnosis, loss to follow-up in adulthood, or differences in cohort 
characteristics, and emphasize the need for continued monitoring of 
adrenal function, particularly in older patients.
AMN was the most prevalent phenotype among males and the only 
phenotype identified among females. Spasticity and lower limb weak -
ness were the most common symptoms in our AMN cohort. A recent 
patient-reported study [ 45 ] found that balance and mobility issues had 
the greatest impact on daily life and quality of life. As these symptoms 
are both prevalent and burdensome, these findings highlight that they 
should be routinely addressed in the clinical management of AMN pa -
tients. In our female ALD cohort, 64 % had developed symptoms of 
AMN, and our analysis suggests that approximately 80 % will eventually 
be affected. However, clinical data were available for only approxi -
mately half of the females. This lack of information was largely due to 
limited contact with the healthcare system, which may suggest that 
these women were asymptomatic. However, it is also possible that 
symptoms were managed in primary care or that patients opted out of 
regular monitoring. In a few, older cases, medical records were no 
longer accessible. Previous studies have reported lifetime risks of up to 
80 % [ 13 , 29 ], indicating that our findings are in line, although influ -
enced by data availability and follow-up intensity. Furthermore, females 
in our cohort died younger than the average Danish female life expec -
tancy of 83.2 years (2018 – 2019) [ 46 ], emphasizing the need for 
heightened awareness of the clinical implications of ALD in females, 
who have otherwise historically been categorized as asymptomatic 
carriers.
C26:0-LPC has been proposed to be associated with disease onset and 
severity in patients with ALD [ 14 , 15 ]. This was not evident in our 
dataset. It is, however, noticeable that all of our male patients had 
C26:0-LPC levels far above the cut-off levels that are being used in NBS 
screening protocols [ 9 ].
While our cohort size was limited, the centralized healthcare system 
and follow-up of patients with ALD in Denmark enhance the reliability 
and application of our findings to similar populations. There was no 
difference in the number of male and female patients, but there was a 
significant difference regarding clinical data to establish phenotype, as 
there were more data available for males than for females. The data for 
this study were collected retrospectively, which leads to issues such as 
incomplete data, varying record quality, and a lack of standardization. 
The retrospective design can also lead to bias, as methods, clinical 
practices, data recording, and treatment options may have changed over 
time. This is also important to keep in mind because the ABCD 1-gene 
was not described until 1993 [ 47 ]. Although there was no predefined 
age requirements for patients to meet to be included in the study, pa -
tients would have had to be alive at this time to be genetically tested. 
Any patients that passed away from ALD or other reasons before this 
time will be missing from this study. In several of the descriptions of the 
families from the genetic departments, it was noted that an older family 
member had symptoms resembling multiple sclerosis or that a child 
passed away with symptoms that resembled cALD. Our results should be 
seen in the light of this.
The strength of this study is that although Denmark is a small country 
(5,995,628 inhabitants as of 01.12.2024) there is a well-organized, free 
healthcare system that is almost exclusively public. There are strict 
nationwide guidelines stating that pediatric patients with ALD should be 
followed at a single center, with adult follow-up limited to only a few 
centers. Furthermore, a comprehensive search strategy, including 
outreach to relevant departments and pedigree analysis, was used to 
minimize missed cases and spectrum bias. However, it is possible that 
some cases have been missed, which could be due to that (I) a few de -
partments never responded to our inquiry despite several attempts of 
contact. Because all genetic departments did respond to our inquiry, we 
could make sure that all patients diagnosed with a pathogenic variant in 
the ABCD1- gene were accounted for. Furthermore, despite our encour -
agement to look up patients based on their diagnostic code, we cannot 
guarantee that they have, and some might have answered based on 
recollection. (II) Patients with de novo pathogenic variants that have not 
yet developed symptoms have yet to be identified, which could lead to 
some spectrum bias. This is important to remember regarding female 
probands. In our cohort, 8 females were the index patients in their 
families, and we also saw that this patient group had the longest diag -
nostic delay across all phenotypes.
In this study, we provide an overview of the Danish ALD population, 
offering insight into prevalence, phenotype distribution, and diagnostic 
trends. Our findings emphasize the importance of addressing diagnostic 
delays, under-recognition in adults, particularly females, and the critical
