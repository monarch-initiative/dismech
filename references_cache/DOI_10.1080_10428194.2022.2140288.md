# Cost-effectiveness of azacitidine and ivosidenib in newly diagnosed older, intensive chemotherapy-ineligible patients with <i>IDH1</i> -mutant acute myeloid leukemia

Issue date 2023 Feb.

Ivosidenib + azacitidine (IVO/AZA) is approved in the United States for newly diagnosed, older or intensive chemotherapy-ineligible patients withIDH1-mutated acute myeloid leukemia. We created a partitioned survival analysis model to evaluate the health economic implications of this approval. Model outputs were used to calculate the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of IVO/AZA versus AZA. One-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted.

In the base case scenario, IVO/AZA and AZA resulted in life-time costs of $403,062 and $161,887, respectively. With an incremental gain of 0.95 QALYs, the ICER of IVO/AZA was $252,782/QALY. In sensitivity analyses, only a reduction in the price of IVO by 59.3% lowered the ICER to below $150,000/QALY and 99.95% of model calculations yielded ICERs of >$150,000/QALY. In a model in which all patients received IVO monotherapy after progression on AZA monotherapy, the ICER was $155,453/QALY and various model inputs that would make IVO/AZA cost-effective were identified.

Keywords:acute myeloid leukemia, AML, azacitidine, ivosidenib, cost-effectiveness

Approximately 5–10% of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cases harbor mutations in isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1).1,2Ivosidenib (IVO) is an oral inhibitor of mutant IDH1 that resulted in complete remission (CR) plus CR with partial hematologic recovery (CRh) in 42.4% and 30.4% of older/intensive therapy-ineligible patients with newly diagnosed and relapsed/refractory (R/R),IDH1-mutant AML, respectively.3,4While not tested in a randomized clinical trial, these studies led to the approval of IVO as monotherapy for both indications by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

Following encouraging results from a phase Ib trial of IVO in combination with the hypomethylating agent (HMA) azacitidine (AZA),5a randomized, placebo-controlled, phase III trial of IVO + AZA vs placebo + AZA (AGILE trial) was conducted and showed improved event-free survival (EFS; hazard ratio [HR] 0.33; 95% confidence interval [95% CI] 0.16–0.69; p=0.002) and overall survival (OS; HR: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.27–0.73; p=0.001) with the combination.2Based on these results, the combination of IVO + AZA was approved in the US for the treatment of newly diagnosed, older (≥75 years) or intensive chemotherapy-ineligible patients withIDH1-mutant AML. The external validity of using the AGILE trial results for a US approval has been criticized for not including a contemporary control arm of AZA + venetoclax, which was not approved in Europe until May of 2021, or adopting a cross-over design to allow control arm non-responders access to IVO salvage therapy, a clinically effective monotherapy in the R/R setting approved in the US, but not in Europe. Furthermore, the health economic implications of the combination of IVO + AZA are unknown.

We conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis based on the phase III AGILE trial.2In line with the original trial, patients with newly-diagnosedIDH1-mutant AML entered our partitioned survival model at age 75 years and received either IVO + AZA or AZA monotherapy. We used recreated patient-level data derived from the Kaplan-Meier curves and at-risk tables for EFS and OS from the AGILE trial for the IVO + AZA arm to fit loglogistic parametric survival distributions (Figure 2).2,6,7Using the HRs for EFS and OS reported by the AGILE trial,2these curves were used to derive the EFS and OS curves for the AZA arm, respectively. The 95% CI for the HRs of EFS and OS from the AGILE trial were used in sensitivity analyses to evaluate how changes in the comparative clinical efficacy would affect the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). With a median duration of follow-up of only 12.4 months in the AGILE trial, data on long-term outcomes with IVO + AZA are immature and the impact of post-progression therapy on OS and utilities is difficult to assess.2Additionally, prior studies of AZA monotherapy suggest that it is not considered a curative therapeutic modality in the absence of a subsequent allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant.8,9The majority of patients in either treatment arm of the AGILE trial did not receive any subsequent anti-cancer therapy (80.6% in IVO + AZA and 78.4% with AZA monotherapy). The remainder of the patients received antimetabolites, venetoclax (n=4 in the IVO + AZA arm and n=7 in the AZA monotherapy arm) or IVO monotherapy (n=2 in the AZA monotherapy arm).2As all parts of the Kaplan-Meier curve influence the parametrization equally, but the tails of the survival curves are associated with high uncertainty due to the small numbers of patients at risk, we used the time to median OS and EFS to reconstruct the survival curves for the base-case scenario to minimize the influence of an immature tail of the survival curves on the model. The entire OS and EFS curves were used in a sensitivity analysis.

Medication costs were derived from the average sales price (ASP) for AZA and cytarabine listed by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) or the average wholesale price (AWP) for IVO and venetoclax, respectively.10,11Medicare Part B reimbursement is based on the ASP method, which is based on manufacturers’ sales reports and includes discounts, rebates, and a 6% reimbursement surcharge.12As ASP ranges between 26–30% below the AWP, we used a 28% discount off the AWP for the base-case scenario, and varied this discount during sensitivity analyses between 18% and 38%.13–15Costs for the subcutaneous administration of drugs (procedure code: 96401) were based on the National Payment Amount published in the physician fee schedule by CMS.16Costs and practice patterns of treatment discontinuation or treatment hold due to adverse events, subsequent treatments, supportive and terminal care, and incidence of complications were derived from the original trial or published literature (Table 1).2,17–20We assumed that all serious adverse events would result in hospital admission and that cost and duration of such admissions would be similar to those of patients receiving other forms of lower-intensity AML treatment.17As the median time to complete remission in the AGILE trial was 4.3 months with IVO + AZA and 3.8 months with AZA, we assumed that patients would achieve transfusion independence after 4 months at the percentages reported in the AGILE trial.2

Clinical variables, costs, and utilities included in model

Model assumptions were derived preferentially from the AGILE trial.2If not available, costs and utilities were derived from the literature reporting cost and treatment patterns from a US perspective. All costs were adjusted for inflation to 2021 USD. Costs and utilities were varied by 50% and 10%, respectively, during sensitivity analyses and as shown in the table.

Costs were adjusted for inflation to 2021 US dollars using the personal consumption expenditure health index.21Utilities were derived from the literature as the AGILE trial only reported changes in the quality of life measured as mean least squares from an unknown baseline.2,22As the quality of life assessments were not statistically significantly different for both arms, we assumed equal utilities for the base case scenario with differences in utilities favoring IVO + AZA in sensitivity analyses. Costs and utilities were modeled over a 30-year time horizon and discounted by 3% annually as recommended by the second panel on cost-effectiveness in health and medicine.23Utilities were measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Model outputs were used to calculate the ICER for IVO + AZA, which represents the cost in 2021 US dollars (USD) of each additional QALY gained compared to AZA.

One-way sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the impact of individual parameters on the overall model. Utility values were varied with a 10% range and all other variables were varied across a 50% range. The ranges of the various variables used in sensitivity analyses are shown inTable 1. Results from one-way sensitivity analyses are presented in a tornado diagram showing the 10 variables with the greatest influence on the ICER.

Various alternative scenarios were modelled to assess for the influence of 2ndline treatment with IVO, differences in utilities, and utilization of the full EFS and OS curves reported in the AGILE trial. Specifically, we conducted a model in which all patients in the AZA monotherapy arm would receive IVO monotherapy as 2ndline treatment, which is in line with the US FDA-approved indication of IVO, while patients progressing on first-line IVO + AZA received 2ndline treatment as reported by the AGILE trial.2In the absence of data reporting on the efficacy of IVO monotherapy in patients previously treated with AZA, this scenario only included an adjustment in the treatment costs and not the OS curve or changes in utility with 2ndline IVO. To account for potential changes in the efficacy with 2ndline IVO following AZA, we conducted one-way sensitivity analyses to identify thresholds in model parameters including the HR for the OS curve that would make upfront IVO + AZA cost-effective. We also modelled the influence of an improvement in utilities by 0.1 with IVO + AZA compared to AZA to account for the differences in quality-of-life assessments in a subset of timepoints reported by the AGILE trial.2One-way sensitivity analyses in which utility values were varied with a 10% range and all other variables were varied across a 50% range were conducted for each of the aforementioned alternative scenarios as well.

Finally, we performed probabilistic sensitivity analyses, in which we described each parameter using a distribution and performed 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations, each time randomly sampling from the distributions of model inputs. Beta distributions were used to describe probabilities and utilities, while gamma distributions were used for costs. Results of probabilistic sensitivity analyses are shown as cost-effectiveness acceptability curves.

In the base case scenario, IVO + AZA and AZA resulted in life-time costs of $403,062 and $161,887, respectively, for an incremental cost of $241,175 with IVO + AZA. With an incremental gain of 0.95 QALYs with IVO + AZA (IVO + AZA: 1.30 QALYs; AZA: 0.35 QALYs), the ICER of IVO + AZA compared to AZA was $252,782/QALY.Table 2provides key model outputs from the base case model as well as 95% confidence intervals that were derived from the probabilistic sensitivity analysis.

Finally, we considered several alternative scenarios. In a first scenario, we used the full EFS and OS curves from the AGILE trial to reconstruct and parametrize the EFS and OS curves used in our model, we found an ICER of $185,024/QALY (IVO + AZA: life-time costs: $652,088; effectiveness: 3.10 QALYs; AZA: life-time costs: $242,828; effectiveness: 0.89 QALYs). In one-way sensitivity analyses, none of the variables included in the model for this scenario would lead to a reduction of the ICER to below the WTP threshold of $150,000/QALY (Supplemental Figure 1)

In another alternative model in which all patients who were initially treated with AZA monotherapy would receive IVO monotherapy as second-line therapy at the time of progression or after failing to achieve a response to AZA monotherapy, the ICER was $155,453/QALY. In one-way sensitivity analyses of this alternative model, several scenarios would make a strategy of using IVO + AZA in the first-line setting cost-effective (Figure 4). Among these we found that a HR for OS of <0.40 would make IVO + AZA in the first-line setting cost-effective compared to AZA monotherapy first followed by IVO salvage treatment in the second-line setting.

Although quality of life assessments in the AGILE trial were comparable for most timepoints between study arms, we evaluated a third alternative scenario in which patients treated with IVO + AZA experienced an incremental utility of 0.1 compared to AZA. In this model, the ICER remained above the WTP threshold at $229,141/QALY and none of the variables included in the model reached the WTP threshold of $150,000/QALY in one-way sensitivity analyses based on this model.

While IVO + AZA improved EFS and OS compared to AZA in the AGILE trial resulting in FDA approval,2our model shows that this combination is unlikely to be cost-effective under the current pricing model for the treatment of older patients with newly diagnosedIDH1-mutant AML. With expanding therapeutic options, questions related to the sequencing of these strategies must be considered. For example, AZA + venetoclax has shown improved OS when compared with AZA in a similar, but genetically unselected population of older patients with newly diagnosed AML in the VIALE-A study.24Additionally, patients withIDH-mutant disease appear to have a higher rate of response to AZA + venetoclax compared to patients withIDHwild-type disease.25However, randomized studies comparing the combination of IVO + AZA with AZA + venetoclax have not been and may never be conducted. Therefore, it is unclear what the benefits of IVO after AZA + venetoclax or of AZA + venetoclax after IVO + AZA are to inform optimal sequencing of these agents. In terms of health economic considerations, we have previously shown that AZA + venetoclax is also not cost-effective in a model based on the VIALE-A trial.26Whether this would also apply to patients withIDH-mutant disease treated with AZA + venetoclax is unclear and requires additional study. We were unable to construct a model comparing IVO + AZA with AZA + venetoclax due to the difference in patient populations enrolled in the trials and absence of detailed outcomes in theIDH1-mutant patient subset.2,24

As IVO has been approved for the treatment of R/RIDH1-mutant AML in the US, we considered an alternative scenario, in which all patients treated with AZA monotherapy would receive IVO as the second line of therapy. Since it was only the two US-enrolled patients (2.7%) in the AZA + placebo arm of the AGILE trial who subsequently received IVO monotherapy, the influence of 2ndline treatment with IVO on OS and utilities is uncertain. However, in the model of IVO as 2ndline therapy in all patients after AZA monotherapy, one-way sensitivity analyses identified a threshold for the HR of OS that would make upfront treatment with IVO + AZA cost-effective compared with AZA monotherapy. Reflecting an increase in costs with 2ndline IVO after AZA monotherapy, we found that a HR of OS of <0.40 of IVO + AZA compared to AZA monotherapy first followed by IVO salvage treatment in the 2ndline setting would be necessary to make IVO + AZA in the first-line setting cost-effective compared to AZA. The cost-effectiveness of azacitidine + venetoclax or IVO + AZA when compared with AZA monotherapy in younger, intensive chemotherapy-eligible patients is also unknown, but may soon be relevant to study pending the results of several ongoing or planned randomized trials.

Potential limitations of our study include the differences in practice patterns and patient characteristics among patients enrolled in clinical trials and real-world practice. Additionally, the data for EFS and OS reported in the AGILE trial are still immature as the study was prematurely terminated based on recommendations by the independent data and safety monitoring board, which limits the robustness of long-term outcomes. Our initial parametrization based on the complete EFS and OS curves reported in the AGILE trial yielded a 5-year OS of >25% with IVO + AZA, which substantially exceeded clinical expectations in this patient population.8Therefore, we used the time to median OS and EFS to reconstruct the survival curves for the base-case scenario to minimize the influence of an immature tail of the survival curves on the model.2Repeating the analysis once updated, more mature survival curves are available, would reduce this uncertainty regarding long-term outcomes. Secondly, the absence of IVO use after progression in the AZA monotherapy arm in the AGILE trial limits its applicability to clinical practice and our model. As IVO monotherapy is approved by the US FDA for patients with R/R,IDH1-mutant AML, we modelled an alternative scenario in which all patients received IVO monotherapy at progression on AZA monotherapy. To account for potential changes in the efficacy with 2ndline IVO following AZA, we conducted one-way sensitivity analyses and identified thresholds in model parameters including the HR for OS that would make upfront IVO + AZA cost-effective reflecting the increased life-time costs if IVO is used as salvage therapy. Thirdly, we used the discounted AWP in our model, which might lead to an overestimate of the drug costs of IVO. However, as our threshold analyses required a 59.3% price reduction to reach the WTP threshold of $150,000/QALY, it is unlikely that routine discounts will be as substantial to meet this threshold. Fourthly, we did not have access to patient-level data on health care resource utilization from the original AGILE trial and had to rely on estimates from the literature. While this potentially introduces uncertainty to the model, several variables included in our model (e.g., AML-related hospitalizations, monthly transfusion rates) are reflecting health care resource utilization and varying these variables by 50% in one-way sensitivity analyses did not impact the ICER substantially. Finally, our study was conducted from a US payer perspective limiting its generalizability to other settings with different health care expenses, practice patterns, and medication availability.

In conclusion, we conducted a partitioned survival analysis to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of IVO + AZA in newly diagnosed, older or intensive chemotherapy-ineligible patients withIDH1-mutant AML. With an ICER of $252,782/QALY, IVO + AZA is not cost-effective compared to AZA alone when applying a WTP threshold of $150,000/QALY. In various sensitivity analyses, only a reduction in the price of ivosidenib by 59.3% lowered the ICER to below $150,000/QALY.
