Details</a></p></div></div><div><div><h2>Continuing Education Activity</h2><p>Intellectual disability is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by deficits in intellectual and adaptive functioning, with onset during the developmental period, and is frequently accompanied by psychiatric, neurologic, developmental, and medical comorbidities. This activity reviews contemporary approaches to recognizing intellectual disability and global developmental delay, conducting comprehensive histories and physical examinations, differentiating potentially mimicking conditions, and applying diagnostic criteria that emphasize adaptive functioning rather than IQ alone. Participants will examine tiered evaluation strategies, including sensory assessment, neuroimaging when clinically indicated, genetic and metabolic testing, and identification of treatable or preventable causes. The activity also addresses individualized management through educational, behavioral, vocational, family, and interprofessional support, as well as careful management of comorbid conditions and medication-related risks. This activity for healthcare professionals is designed to enhance the learner's competence in identifying intellectual disability, performing the recommended evaluation, selecting appropriate supervision and functional planning, recognizing adverse effects and polypharmacy concerns associated with pharmacotherapy, and implementing an appropriate interprofessional approach when managing this condition.</p><p>
<b>Objectives:</b>
</p><ul><li><div>Identify the diagnostic criteria for intellectual disability.</div></li><li><div>Implement tiered diagnostic strategies to identify underlying causes of intellectual disability.</div></li><li><div>Evaluate comorbidities that may influence individualized management of intellectual disability.</div></li><li><div>Collaborate with interprofessional team members to improve care coordination and outcomes for patients affected by intellectual disability.</div></li></ul><p>
<a>Access free multiple choice questions on this topic.</a>
</p></div><div><h2>Introduction</h2><p>Individuals with intellectual disability have neurodevelopmental deficits characterized by limitations in intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior. These disabilities originate at or around the time of birth and, according to the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities (AAIDD) definition, manifest before the age of 22. Intellectual disability may be associated with a considerable number of related and coexisting conditions, including mental health disorders (eg, depression and anxiety), neurodevelopmental disorders (eg, autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder), neurological conditions (eg, infantile cerebral palsy), and medical conditions (eg, postnatal meningitis) resulting in intellectual disability.</p><p>The term "global developmental delay" is preferred for children younger than 5 years with impairments in intellectual and adaptive functioning who fail to meet expected developmental milestones. Global developmental delay serves as a provisional diagnosis until standardized testing can be completed. Rosa's Law (S. 2781) amended federal health, education, and labor statutes to replace the term "mental retardation" with "intellectual disability."[US Federal Register. <a>Rosa's Law</a>. 2017]</p><p>
<b>Intellectual Functioning</b>
</p><p>Intellectual functioning, also called intelligence, includes a wide range of mental activities, eg, logical reasoning and practical intelligence (problem-solving), the ability to learn, verbal skills, and so on. Intelligence manifests as numerous capabilities, behaviors, thoughts, and emotions. In other words, intellectual functioning is the global ability that enables an individual to understand and interact with reality. Intellectual functioning is commonly measured by the intelligence quotient (IQ), which is a total score obtained from standardized tests (IQ tests) designed to evaluate human intelligence. IQ test scores have a median of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. A score of 70 or below (2 standard deviations below the median) is generally considered to represent intellectual limitation in combination with clinical judgment.</p><p>
<b>Adaptive Behavior</b>
</p><p>Adaptive behavior disabilities are expressed as a lack of competence in social, conceptual, and practical skills. Social skills include interpersonal skills, social responsibility, self-esteem, naivety, the ability to resolve social problems, and the ability to follow society's rules. Conceptual skills include the ability to understand time, finance, and language. Practical skills include using tools, performing activities of daily living, and interacting with others. All these skills are learned throughout development and performed in response to common problems and simple/complex tasks, as well as expectations from our community and society. Adaptive behavioral responses become progressively more complex with age. Several validated tools are useful for assessing limitations in adaptive behavior.</p></div><div><h2>Etiology</h2><p>Although many causes of intellectual disability remain unknown, its etiology can be broadly categorized into genetic abnormalities and environmental exposures (see <b>Image</b>. Intellectual Disability Etiologies). Genetic abnormalities include single-gene mutations, copy-number variants, and chromosomal abnormalities that can result in inborn errors of metabolism, neurodevelopmental defects, and neurodegeneration.</p><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Figure 1</a></h4><p>Intellectual Disability Etiologies. The etiologies of intellectual disability largely remain unknown; however, known causes can be broadly categorized into genetic abnormalities and environmental exposures. Contributed by L Keun, MD </p></div></div><p>Environmental exposures can include maternal exposure to toxins or infectious agents, uncontrolled maternal medical conditions, delivery complications, and postnatal trauma or exposure to toxins or infectious agents. Among known preventable or environmental causes of intellectual disability, fetal alcohol syndrome represents the most common. Down syndrome represents the most common chromosomal cause, while Fragile X syndrome represents the most common genetic cause.</p><p>
<b>Genetic Etiologies</b>
</p><p>Genetic abnormalities may cause inborn errors of metabolism, neurodevelopmental defects, or neurodegeneration. Inborn errors of metabolism can produce intellectual disability through multiple mechanisms, including the accumulation of toxic metabolites, energy deficiency, impaired neurotransmitter function, and disrupted metabolism of complex molecules. These mechanisms can ultimately result in neuronal injury.</p><p>
<b>Phenylketonuria</b>
</p><p>Phenylketonuria (PKU) represents an inborn error of metabolism that affects approximately 0.01% of newborns. The exact incidence varies by geographic region and population, ranging from 1 in 2,600 in Turkey to 1 in 200,000 in Finland.<a>[1]</a> PKU most commonly results from an autosomal-recessive defect involving phenylalanine hydroxylase, which prevents the liver from converting phenylalanine to tyrosine (p-tyrosine). PKU can also result from defects involving tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) synthesis or recycling, a form referred to as "BH4-responsive PKU."</p><p>Although the precise mechanism of neuronal damage in PKU remains incompletely characterized, the consequent accumulation of phenylalanine and reduction in tyrosine levels are thought to produce "alterations in protein synthesis, oxidative stress, changes in bioenergetics and white matter disturbances," along with impaired neurotransmitter synthesis.<a>[2]</a> Neurological damage associated with PKU generally remains irreversible but can be prevented. Consequently, newborn screening for PKU remains mandatory in the United States and other countries because early screening followed by prompt initiation of a low-phenylalanine diet, preferably within the first days of life, may prevent intellectual disability.<a>[3]</a></p><p>
<b>Lesch-Nyhan syndrome</b>
</p><p>Lesch-Nyhan syndrome is an X-linked inborn error of metabolism caused by a mutation in the <i>HPRT1</i> gene, resulting in deficiency of the hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) enzyme.<a>[4]</a> HGPRT deficiency causes uric acid accumulation, which produces intellectual disability and severe self-mutilating behavior, including self-biting involving the mouth and fingers.<a>[5]</a> Other recognized inborn errors of metabolism associated with intellectual disability include Niemann-Pick disease, Hurler syndrome (MPS I), Hunter syndrome (MPS II), maple syrup urine disease, Hartnup disease, homocystinuria, and galactosemia.</p><p>
<b>Fragile X syndrome</b>
</p><p>Fragile X syndrome represents a neurodevelopmental defect and the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability. The condition results from a single-gene mutation involving the <i>FMR1</i> gene (Xq27.3). In most cases, the CGG repeat within the <i>FMR1</i> gene expands to more than 200 repeats, triggering hypermethylation of CpG islands in the <i>FMR1</i> promoter region and silencing <i>FMR1</i> transcription. The <i>FMR1</i> gene encodes FMRP (Fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein 1), an RNA-binding protein that regulates translation of mRNAs essential for synaptic plasticity. Loss of FMRP disrupts synaptic function and can produce intellectual disability, behavioral disturbances, and seizures.<a>[6]</a></p><p>
<b>Neurofibromatosis type 1</b>
</p><p>Neurodevelopmental defects also occur in neurofibromatosis type 1, also known as von Recklinghausen syndrome. This autosomal dominant condition results from mutations in <i>NF1</i>. Characteristic manifestations include abnormal neural cell migration, café-au-lait spots, movement disorders, and varying degrees of intellectual disability.<a>[7]</a></p><p>
<b>Rett syndrome</b>
</p><p>Rett syndrome represents another neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by regression associated with arrested neuronal development and features of neurodegeneration. Rett syndrome follows an X-linked dominant inheritance pattern and occurs almost exclusively in females as a consequence of mutations in the <i>MECP2</i> gene, which encodes the MeCP2 protein. MeCP2 functions as an important modulator of gene expression throughout the CNS; consequently, patients with Rett syndrome experience widespread disruption of neuronal maturation and synaptic function.</p><p>Generalized brain atrophy commonly occurs. Melanin-containing neurons in the substantia nigra are notably reduced in patients with Rett syndrome, leading to abnormalities in the dopaminergic nigrostriatal pathway and resulting in parkinsonian features. Clinicians should therefore use caution when prescribing antipsychotic medications because patients with Rett syndrome may have theoretical vulnerability to worsening parkinsonian symptoms from dopamine antagonism. Symptoms typically emerge between 6 and 18 months of age.<a>[8]</a></p><p>
<b>Other genetic syndromes</b>
</p><p>Alterations in IQ can also occur as part of the clinical presentation of other genetic syndromes. In many cases, the intellectual deficit remains limited and may emerge at different stages of neurodevelopment, with or without cognitive decline. For example, chromosome 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, historically described as DiGeorge syndrome or velocardiofacial syndrome, is among the most common multiple-anomaly syndromes in humans. The condition usually presents with borderline intellectual function or mild intellectual disability during early childhood and may involve cognitive decline during adulthood.<a>[9]</a></p><p>
<b>Environmental Etiologies</b>
</p><p>Environmental exposure during pregnancy can contribute to intellectual disability and includes maternal exposure to toxins, infectious agents, uncontrolled maternal medical conditions, and birth complications.</p><p>
<b>Fetal alcohol syndrome</b>
</p><p>Alcohol represents a common toxic substance capable of causing intellectual disability following fetal exposure. Fetal alcohol syndrome includes intellectual disability along with other developmental abnormalities.<a>[10]</a> Acetaldehyde, the oxidative metabolite of ethanol, competitively inhibits retinaldehyde dehydrogenase, thereby reducing biosynthesis of retinoic acid, an essential signaling molecule involved in nervous system development. No amount of alcohol has been established as safe during any trimester, and binge drinking represents the most harmful pattern of exposure.<a>[11]</a> Exposure to opioids, cocaine, and teratogenic medications may also contribute to intellectual disability.</p><p>
<b>Infectious agents</b>
</p><p>Common, well-recognized infectious agents associated with intellectual disability include rubella and HIV. The risk of congenital defects following maternal rubella infection is highest when infection occurs during the first 12 weeks of gestation, reaching 85% to 90%, and declines to 25% by week 26. Immunization prevents maternal rubella infection.<a>[12]</a><a>[13]</a> HIV can undergo vertical transmission from mother to infant. Infants with untreated perinatal HIV may develop encephalopathy, seizures, and intellectual disability, often accompanied by microcephaly and advanced immunosuppression, with <i>Pneumocystis jirovecii</i> pneumonitis (PCP) infection serving as a marker of advanced immunosuppression.<a>[14]</a></p><p>Viral proteins, eg, gp120 and secondary neuroinflammatory processes, overstimulate the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDA-R) system, producing excitotoxic neuronal injury. Maternal antiretroviral therapy and newborn prophylactic treatment with zidovudine have significantly reduced HIV transmission.<a>[14]</a> Other fetal infectious exposures associated with intellectual disability include congenital cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection, syphilis, and toxoplasmosis.</p><p>
<b>Maternal medical conditions</b>
</p><p>Uncontrolled maternal medical conditions can also contribute to fetal intellectual disability. Hypertension during pregnancy, asthma, urinary tract infection, pre-pregnancy obesity, and pregestational diabetes have been shown to significantly increase risk.<a>[15]</a><a>[16]</a> Malnutrition and obstetrical complications that result in fetal anoxia, including placenta previa, placental abruption, and umbilical cord prolapse, may also cause intellectual disability.<a>[17]</a></p><p>
<b>Other acquired environmental etiologies</b>
</p><p>Intellectual disability can also emerge or become acquired during early childhood due to conditions including infection (particularly encephalitis and meningitis), head trauma, and asphyxia and associated anoxic brain injury. Other acquired etiologies include intracranial tumors (either through direct effects of the tumor or iatrogenic effects related to perioperative and postoperative complications and chemotherapy), malnutrition, and exposure to toxic substances.<a>[18]</a></p><p>Studies indicate that environmental lead exposure can produce a permanent pattern of cognitive dysfunction. Lead poisoning during the first several years of life and in-utero lead exposure may adversely affect infant neurodevelopment, as measured at 24 months, independently of postnatal blood lead levels. Higher blood lead levels have been associated in a dose-dependent manner with lower IQ and lower socioeconomic status at age 38 years, despite adjustment for maternal IQ, child IQ, and childhood socioeconomic status.<a>[19]</a>[CDC. <a>Lead Exposure Symptoms and Complications</a>. 2026] However, evidence remains mixed and requires further elucidation. A large Japanese cohort involving more than 80,000 children found no significant association between prenatal blood lead levels and impaired early neurodevelopment.<a>[20]</a></p></div><div><h2>Epidemiology</h2><p>The prevalence of intellectual disability in developing countries is estimated to range from 10 to 15 per 1000 children; of those, about 85% have a mild intellectual disability. From 1 to 3% of the Western population is estimated to have an intellectual disability. Incidence is challenging to calculate accurately, as mild disabilities may be underrecognized until later in childhood. Intellectual disability is 1.5 times more prevalent in males than in females and peaks in child and adolescent populations.<a>[21]</a> This latter finding is thought to be because school pressures facilitate identification of intellectual disability and, later in life, the earlier mortality of individuals with more severe forms. In addition, identification of intellectual disability in adulthood is often more rigorous. The risk factors for intellectual disability include low level of maternal education, poverty, advanced maternal age, advanced paternal age, and preterm birth or low birthweight.<a>[22]</a></p></div><div><h2>History and Physical</h2><p>
<b>Clinical History</b>
</p><p>Maintaining daily functions can pose significant challenges for individuals with varying degrees of intellectual disability, as impairments in activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental activities of daily living (iADLs) commonly occur. These impairments may manifest as difficulty feeding themselves, using the bathroom, dressing, interacting appropriately with family and friends, communicating effectively, maintaining impulse control, and performing academically.</p><p>Regarding the clinical history, symptoms of intellectual disability typically begin during childhood or adolescence and should prompt appropriate screening. Delays in language or motor skills may become apparent by age 2. However, many children with mild intellectual disability may not receive identification until they reach school age, when increasing academic, social, and functional demands make their difficulties more apparent. A comprehensive history of patients with intellectual disability must include the following:</p><ul><li><div>Information about the mother’s pregnancy, labor, and delivery</div></li><li><div>Mother’s use of substances or medications during pregnancy</div></li><li><div>Mother had an uncontrolled medical condition</div></li><li><div>Was the delivery preterm?</div></li><li><div>Was any delivery issue present?</div></li><li><div>Patient’s sensory, social, language, and motor development to detect any developmental delay</div></li><li><div>Patient’s exposure to infection, trauma, and toxins</div></li><li><div>Patient’s medical conditions</div></li><li><div>Patient’s psychiatric conditions</div></li><li><div>Medications that the patient is taking</div></li><li><div>Patient’s family history of psychiatric and medical conditions</div></li><li><div>Patient’s living situation, caretaker</div></li><li><div>Patient’s behavior toward caretaker, parents, and siblings</div></li><li><div>Patient’s academic performance at school</div></li><li><div>Patient’s behavioral disturbance reported at school</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Physical Examination</b>
</p><p>Physical examination is vital to differentiate intellectual disorders from other conditions. A full neurological exam should be performed to identify any deficit that may mimic intellectual disability and comorbid symptoms. Visual and hearing tests are particularly important, as abnormal vision or hearing can make communication difficult, leading to delays in the development of language and social skills and closely mimicking intellectual disability. Motor dysfunctions, eg, spasticity, hypotonia, hyperreflexia, and involuntary movements, are commonly present in individuals with intellectual disabilities, particularly when comorbid with a neurologic disorder, eg, cerebral palsy.<a>[23]</a> Some physical characteristics are closely associated with a specific diagnosis as follows:</p><ul><li><div><b>Down syndrome</b>: slanted eyes, flat nasal bridge, protruding tongue, small chin, and single crease on the palm</div></li><li><div><b>Fragile X syndrome</b>: long and narrow face, prominent forehead and jaw, large ears, and large testicles in males</div></li><li><div><b>Fetal alcohol syndrome</b>: smooth philtrum, thin vermilion, and small palpebral fissures</div></li><li><div><b>Prader-Willi</b>: obesity secondary to compulsive eating behavior, hypogonadism, small hands and feet</div></li><li><div><b>Cat’s cry (Cri-du-chat) syndrome</b>: microcephaly, hypertelorism, low-set ears, and micrognathia</div></li><li><div><b>Phenylketonuria</b>: hypopigmentation (fair skin/hair, blue eyes), eczema, musty/mousy body odor, bizarre movements, eg, twisting hand mannerisms, poor motor coordination, and perceptual difficulties</div></li></ul></div><div><h2>Evaluation</h2><p>
<b>Diagnostic Criteria</b>
</p><ul><li><div>According to the <i>Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision</i> (<i>DSM-5-TR</i>), the diagnosis of intellectual disability requires deficits in intellectual and adaptive functioning with onset during the developmental period; according to the AAIDD, onset is defined as before the age of 22.[Psychiatry Online. <i>DSM-5-TR</i>. <a>Neurodevelopmental Disorders</a>. 2022][AAIDD. <a>Defining Criteria for Intellectual Disability</a>. 2026] <i>DSM-5-TR</i> (2022) renamed the disorder "intellectual developmental disorder (intellectual disability)" to align with ICD-11, retaining "intellectual disability" in parentheses.</div></li></ul><p>The IQ test was widely used to assess individuals' intellectual functioning and to assign severity grades before the <i>DSM-5-TR</i> (see <b>Table 1</b>). An IQ between 65 and 75 was historically considered diagnostic of intellectual disability.</p><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Table</a></h4><p><i>Table 1</i>. Historical IQ Levels and Their Definitions. </p></div></div><p>However, current practice no longer classifies intellectual disability based solely on IQ scores. Since the publication of the <i>DSM-5</i> in 2013, clinicians have assessed severity primarily by adaptive functioning, while the role of IQ scores in determining severity has diminished.</p><p>The <i>DSM-5-TR</i> defines the diagnostic criteria for intellectual disability as follows:</p><ul><li><div>Deficits in intellectual functioning confirmed through clinical evaluation and standardized IQ testing</div></li><li><div>Deficits in adaptive functioning that result in failure to meet developmental and sociocultural standards for personal independence and social responsibility</div></li><li><div>Onset of deficits during the developmental period <a>[24]</a></div></li></ul><p>All 3 criteria must be met to establish a diagnosis of intellectual disability. Severity classification additionally uses the categories of "mild," "moderate," "severe," or "profound," based on adaptive functioning across the "conceptual," "social," and "practical" domains.</p><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Table</a></h4><p><i>Table 2</i>. Adaptive Functioning Domains and Corresponding <i>DSM-5-TR</i> Definitions. </p></div></div><p>Adaptive functioning is also influenced by "intellectual capacity, education, motivation, socialization, personality features, vocational opportunity, cultural experience, and coexisting other medical conditions or mental disorders."[Psychiatry Online. <i>DSM-5-TR</i>. <a>Neurodevelopmental Disorders</a>. 2022]</p><p>The Adaptive Behavior Assessment System (ABAS-3) can measure adaptive functioning as a "General Adaptive Composite" score along with 3 individual scores corresponding to the above domains.<a>[25]</a> Adaptive function is measured across various aspects, including literacy, mathematics, judgment, social participation, and independent living (see <b>Table 3</b>). The ABAS-3 is validated from birth to 89 years, with a dedicated parent/caregiver form for ages 0 to 5 years and a teacher/daycare form for ages 2 to 5.</p><p>
<b>Global developmental delay</b>
</p><p>For children younger than 5 years, when reliable assessment of intellectual function cannot be performed (and the severity of intellectual disability cannot be assessed), the term global developmental delay (GDD) is used instead.<a>[26]</a> GDD is characterized by failure to achieve developmental milestones expected for a given age range in 2 or more of the following domains:</p><ul><li><div>Gross or fine motor skills</div></li><li><div>Speech and language</div></li><li><div>Cognition</div></li><li><div>Personal-social</div></li><li><div>Activities of daily living</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Unspecified intellectual disability</b>
</p><p>The <i>DSM-5-TR</i> also has “unspecified intellectual disability” (intellectual developmental disorder) to describe individuals older than 5 suspected of having an intellectual disability who have difficulty completing required tests. Limiting factors can include sensory or physical impairments (blindness and deafness), communication or locomotor difficulty, behavioral problems, or concurrent psychiatric disorders.</p><p>
<b>Additional Evaluation Considerations</b>
</p><p>The goals of evaluation are to determine comorbidities, prognosis, and future expectations for the family. If the symptoms appear to be related to a syndrome, targeted evaluation is necessary. Inborn errors of metabolism are sometimes treatable causes of intellectual disability, and focused metabolic testing is warranted if a condition is suspected based on the history and physical. Considerations include developmental regression, episodic decompensation, organomegaly, coarse facies, or consanguinity. Genetic testing might have implications for risk of recurrence in future pregnancies and the need for genetic counseling.</p><p>
<b>Tiered Evaluation Approach</b>
</p><p>The modern evaluation of unexplained intellectual disability/GDD in children uses a tiered approach.<a>[27]</a> The protocol recommended by the AAP (2025) is the following tiered framework.</p><p>Every evaluation should include a thorough patient and family history, along with a comprehensive physical and dysmorphology examination. Hearing and vision evaluations should also be completed. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) should be considered when the phenotype supports neuroimaging, while clinicians should recognize that MRI frequently requires sedation or anesthesia. Clinical features supporting brain MRI include:</p><ul><li><div>Abnormal head circumference</div></li><li><div>Focal neurological signs</div></li><li><div>Seizures</div></li><li><div>Increased tone</div></li><li><div>Developmental regression</div></li></ul><p>Neurology or genetics evaluation should be considered when developmental regression has occurred. Single-gene or panel testing may also be appropriate when the phenotype suggests a monogenic disorder or a defined group of disorders. When the initial evaluation does not establish a diagnosis, clinicians should proceed with tiered genetic and metabolic testing.</p><p>
<b>Tier 1 testing</b>
</p><p>Tier 1 evaluation begins with pretest genetic counseling. Counseling should address variants of uncertain significance, the potential for incidental findings, and the possibility of nonmaternity or nonpaternity. Additional genetic findings may include benign, likely benign, pathogenic, and likely pathogenic variants. The ACMG SF v3.2 list, cited by the AAP 2025 report and subsequently updated to v3.3, identifies 81 genes that may be incidentally detected and that are associated with increased risk of various conditions, including cardiomyopathy, cardiac conduction disorders, cancer, metabolic disorders, and connective tissue disorders.<a>[28]</a> The diagnostic yield of genome or exome sequencing improves when samples from the proband and both parents are submitted together as a trio.</p><p>Targeted genetic testing should follow when a specific syndrome is suspected.<a>[29]</a> Testing may include genome sequencing, exome sequencing performed either sequentially or concurrently, and chromosomal microarray. When tier 1 testing does not establish a diagnosis, evaluation should proceed to tier 2 testing.</p><p>
<b>Tier 2 testing</b>
</p><p>Tier 2 testing includes Fragile X screening. Fragile X screening was previously considered a tier 1 test; however, its diagnostic yield remains only 1% in both males and females. The AAP now classifies Fragile X screening as tier 2 because of its low diagnostic yield. Exome sequencing, genome sequencing, and chromosomal microarray do not detect CGG expansions within the 5' untranslated region of the <i>FMR1</i> gene.</p><p>Tier 2 evaluation also includes screening for the following treatable inborn errors of metabolism:</p><ul><li><div><b>PKU</b>: plasma amino acids</div><ul><li><div>Increase phenylalanine</div></li></ul></li><li><div><b>Homocystinuria</b>: plasma amino acids</div><ul><li><div>Increased methionine</div></li><li><div>Increased homocysteine</div></li></ul></li><li><div><b>Organic acidurias</b>: urine organic acids, plasma acylcarnitines, ammonia</div></li><li><div><b>Urea cycle disorders</b>: ammonia, urine organic acids (which may also show increased orotic acid), abnormal citrulline and/or arginine levels</div></li><li><div><b>Creatine deficiency syndromes</b> (SLC6A8, GAMT, and AGAT): urine creatine to creatinine ratio, plasma creatine, plasma and urine guanidinoacetate</div></li><li><div><b>Lesch-Nyhan</b>: plasma uric acid and urine purines/pyrimidines</div></li><li><div><b>Mucopolysaccharidoses</b> (eg, Hurler): urine glycosaminoglycans</div></li><li><div><b>Biotinidase deficiency</b>: biotinidase enzyme activity (sometimes included in newborn screening tests)</div></li></ul><p>If no diagnosis is found, clinicians should proceed to tier 3 testing.</p><p>
<b>Tier 3 testing</b>
</p><p>Tier 3 evaluation includes genome sequencing when testing has not already been performed. Additional testing should assess imprinting disorders, trinucleotide repeat disorders, and balanced chromosome disorders. Mitochondrial DNA testing should be performed when not previously completed. Repeated analysis of exome and genome sequencing data can identify variants that may have become clinically interpretable as knowledge and variant databases evolve.</p><p>Higher-resolution chromosome microarray testing should also be considered, including specific exon-level coverage of disease-associated genes. Additional metabolic evaluation may be warranted based on the clinical phenotype and previous test results, including:</p><ul><li><div>

<b>Additional Testing Considerations</b>
</p><p>Chromosomal microarray has largely replaced standard karyotype and fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) subtelomere studies when etiology is unknown.<a>[30]</a> FISH is useful for identifying microscopic chromosomal deletions.<a>[31]</a></p><p>Cell-free fetal DNA (cfDNA) testing has emerged as a highly sensitive noninvasive prenatal screening test for trisomy 21, detecting around 99% of cases and reducing reliance on invasive testing.<a>[32]</a> However, a positive cfDNA result requires confirmation by diagnostic karyotype from chorionic villous sampling or amniocentesis.</p><p>Metabolic testing is now considered second-tier, given its very low yield (estimated 1%–5%) in nonsyndromic GDD/ID, unless there are specific clinical indications of a metabolic disorder.<a>[27]</a> Urine and blood tests evaluate enzyme activity and detect inborn errors of metabolism, including PKU, Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, galactosemia, and Hurler syndrome, using advanced techniques, eg, tandem mass spectrometry and gas chromatography.<a>[33]</a> Blood tests for thyroid disorders and creatine kinase levels can be a good initial test for muscular dystrophy. Blood lead, iron, and B12 testing help to determine environmental causes of intellectual disability.</p><p>Computerized tomography (CT) or MRI has been useful in identifying causes when microcephaly, cerebral developmental delay, and cerebral palsy are noted.<a>[34]</a> However, MRI is the preferred modality because it provides substantially more information than CT on gray/white matter and posterior fossa detail.</p><p>Functional MRI and diffusion tensor imaging are being researched to identify abnormalities in neural circuit pathways. For example, Fragile X syndrome with aberrant frontal-striatal pathways is associated with deficits in attention and inhibition, which result in symptoms that mimic ADHD. Electroencephalography (EEG) is used when seizures or paroxysmal events are suspected, which often accompany intellectual disability. <b>Allen Cognitive Levels</b>
</p><p>The Allen Cognitive Levels (ACL) are derived from Claudia Allen's <i>Cognitive Disability Theory</i> and describe functional cognition, which is how cognitive capacity translates into everyday task performance. The levels span from 1 through 6 (lowest to highest), typically scored in 0.1 increments with the leather-lacing Allen Cognitive Level Screen (ACLS/LACLS).</p><p>The following are the 6 global levels:</p><ul><li><div><b>Level 1</b>: Automatic actions</div><ul><li><div>Minimal response to stimuli</div></li><li><div>Near-total dependence for ADLs</div></li></ul></li><li><div><b>Level 2</b>: Postural actions</div><ul><li><div>Gross body movements present</div></li><li><div>Assists with movement/positioning but disoriented</div></li></ul></li><li><div><b>Level 3</b>: Manual actions</div><ul><li><div>Uses hands on objects</div></li><li><div>Can do repetitive ADL steps with cueing but limited awareness of results</div></li></ul></li><li><div><b>Level 4</b>: Goal-directed actions</div><ul><li><div>Completes familiar, concrete tasks step-by-step</div></li><li><div>Relies on visible cues</div></li><li><div>Poor anticipation of problems or safety hazards</div></li></ul></li><li><div><b>Level 5</b>: Exploratory/independent learning</div><ul><li><div>Learns by trial-and-error</div></li><li><div>Can problem-solve concretely but may not anticipate consequences of new situations</div></li></ul></li><li><div><b>Level 6</b>: Planned actions</div><ul><li><div>Normal cognition and abstract reasoning</div></li><li><div>Plans ahead and anticipates consequences</div></li></ul></li></ul><p>The ACL is used predominantly in occupational therapy to guide discharge planning, supervision needs, and task adaptation. In intellectual disability, the ACL is used as a supplementary functional-cognitive measure to evaluate features defined in the Practical and Conceptual adaptive domains. This helps inform supervision needs, task adaptation, and support planning rather than establishing the diagnosis itself.</p><p>In one study of patients with severe cognitive and adaptive impairment in schizophrenia, ACL scores correlate strongly with basic ADL performance (eg, r ≈ 0.66 against the Functional Needs Assessment) and appear culturally unbiased across 3 ethnic groups (White, Mexican-American, and African-American).<a>[42]</a></p><p>However, a scoping review found that while ACL scores reliably track "in-the-moment task performance," they do not consistently correlate to real-world performance in the community.<a>[43]</a> In addition, data about ACLs' predictive ability remain mixed: one retrospective inpatient study linked higher ACL scores on admission to discharge to an independent-living setting, whereas a larger acute-inpatient study found no significant association between ACL and nursing-assistance needs or discharge disposition.<a>[44]</a><a>[45]</a> The first study noted that the admission ACL score was associated with independent living before admission, and that independent living before admission had the strongest correlation with independent living on discharge, with the second-strongest association being higher ACL scores.</p></div><div><h2>Treatment / Management</h2><p>Most causes of intellectual disability are not curable; however, if they are treatable (eg, hypothyroidism or phenylketonuria), they should be addressed promptly. A range of targeted therapeutic approaches is now in use or under development, ranging from dietary manipulation and nutritional supplementation to drugs that target the underlying molecular mechanism, enzyme replacement therapy, and gene therapy.<a>[46]</a> Management goals include preventing further deterioration, minimizing disability, and improving quality of everyday life. When initiating therapy, a clinician must be aware of the various avenues of treating intellectual disability to orchestrate an interprofessional approach.</p><p>
<b>Educational Support</b>
</p><p>Educational support is a crucial component of the management of intellectual disability. Upon diagnosing intellectual disability in children, healthcare practitioners should contact the school promptly to arrange special education services. This typically involves referring the child to the locally designated early intervention or special education pathway, recognizing that the responsible agency, entitlement, and age eligibility vary by country. Most high-income systems follow the same underlying model of screening, referral, interprofessional evaluation, and then an individualized plan delivered in the least restrictive setting, though the structures vary. The following are some educational support strategies utilized:</p><ul><li><div>In the United States, this means either a state early intervention program (ages 0–3) or a referral to the school district's special education evaluation team (eg, a committee on special education) under IDEA (ages 3–21, inclusive).</div></li><li><div>Portugal has a national statutory system (SNIPI) coordinating health, education, and social care for children up to age 6.</div></li><li><div>German-speaking countries (Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, and South Tyrol) use "Frühe Hilfen" early-help programs.</div></li><li><div>The United Kingdom provides support through Special Educational Needs and Disability (SEND) services in England, and Additional Support for Learning in Scotland, coordinated by SENCOs.</div></li><li><div>Australia combines individualized funding through the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) with education-based special education.</div></li><li><div>Canada administers special education at the provincial or territorial level rather than under a single national statute.</div></li></ul><p>In many low-income and middle-income settings across Africa, Asia, South America, and Mexico, formal entitlement-based pathways are often absent, so care relies more on health worker–led detection and referral and on community-based early intervention programs, with access shaped heavily by awareness, resources, workforce, and cross-sector coordination.</p><p>Special education can also vary among schools, but it typically provides comprehensive support ranging from academic modifications to transition planning from childhood to adulthood, with a focus on promoting self-sufficiency. Special education also provides teaching on how to seek assistance, along with behavioral, vocational, communication, functional living, and social skills education, based on individual needs. In addition, as noted above, the least restrictive learning environment for students is usually targeted, which may include placing them in general classrooms as much as is tolerated, with the intent to optimize inclusion and interaction with typically developing peers.<a>[47]</a></p><p>Monitoring the child's progress in school is important because education in classroom settings alone may not be sufficient. If an individual with an intellectual disability requires assistance beyond what is available at school, recruiting the family to meet the child's needs at home, through family education or other outside resources, can be critical. For example, a student with an intellectual disability who needs extra attention with social skills may be referred to participate in Special Olympics programs, which have been shown to improve social competence among individuals with intellectual disabilities.<a>[48]</a></p><p>Lastly, as noted previously, other causes of learning difficulties must be ruled out through appropriate hearing and vision screenings.</p><p>
<b>Behavioral Interventions</b>
</p><p>Behavioral interventions are another important aspect of intellectual disability management and can take several forms. Behavioral therapy aims to encourage positive behaviors and discourage undesirable ones. Providing positive reinforcement and benign negative-punishment/behavior-reduction procedures (eg, time-outs) is an effective method of behavioral training. Other supplemental methods may include avoiding triggers of negative behavior, shunning misconduct, and redirecting to prevent or curtail any troublesome behavior.</p><p>Cognitive-behavioral approaches have shown benefit for aggression in eligible intellectual disability patients, with emerging evidence showing benefit for anxiety.<a>[49]</a><a>[50]</a> Cognitive-behavioral therapy is based on the principle that one's behavior, emotions, and cognitions are connected, and it aims to correct negative behaviors by identifying and adjusting negative thoughts and emotional stress. Other cognitive-behavioral approaches that have shown benefit include anger management and positive behavior support for short-term treatment of aggression.</p><p>The intervention with the most robust intellectual disability-specific evidence base is personalized applied behavior analysis (ABA), which uses individualized functional assessment to strengthen adaptive behaviors and reduce challenging ones through techniques, eg, differential reinforcement and functional communication training. Group parent-training programs (eg, Stepping Stones Triple P, Incredible Years) are similarly well-established and reduce child behavioral problems by roughly "a third of a standard deviation."<a>[29]</a><a>[51]</a></p><p>Although many other approaches to behavioral intervention exist, the implementation of behavioral therapy, cognitive therapy, or a combination of the 2 is the most widely used method shown to be an effective behavioral intervention for patients with intellectual disability.</p><p>
<b>Vocational Training</b>
</p><p>Vocational training helps teenagers and young adults acquire the skills needed to enter the labor market. In vocational training, patients carry out prescheduled activities under the supervision of an interprofessional team comprising a social worker, an occupational therapist, a teacher, a counselor, and a psychologist. Patients learn independent living skills (hygiene and appropriate clothing), job-specific skills, and how to fulfill their responsibilities. One study found that patients who underwent vocational training had lower support requirements than their peers.<a>[52]</a>[Journal of Policy and Practice in Intellectual Disabilities. <a>Effects of Vocational Training on a Group of People with Intellectual Disabilities</a>. 2016]</p><p>
<b>Vocational training models</b>
</p><p>Notably, different models of vocational training have yielded varying outcomes. Supported employment, sheltered work, and class-based teaching differ chiefly by setting, wage, and effectiveness.<a>[53]</a><a>[54]</a></p><p>Supported employment is an integrated model developed in the United States in the 1980s and now available in many other countries. Supported employment begins by placing a person with an intellectual disability in a paid job in the open labor market, then providing individualized, ongoing on-the-job support from a trained job coach. The position is matched to the person's abilities and paid at or above minimum wage, on terms equal to those of nondisabled workers.</p><p>Sheltered work (which includes sheltered workshops, day centers, or vocational rehabilitation centers) is employment in a segregated facility where most workers have disabilities, providing supervised, often contract-based tasks that are easy to learn, either as a stepping-stone toward community work or as a long-term substitute for it. Sheltered work is frequently authorized to pay sub-minimum wages and has not been shown to increase (and may actually impede) open-labor-market employment.</p><p>Class-based teaching refers to classroom-based or center-based instruction of vocational skills delivered to a group in a segregated or simulated setting away from a real workplace. One systematic review found that "supported employment increased employment in the open labor market," whereas sheltered work did not.<a>[55]</a> Class-based teaching and sheltered work were actually barriers to employment, as skills learned out of context transfer less effectively than those learned with direct experience and job-coach support.</p><p>
<b>Family Education</b>
</p><p>Family education represents another essential component of management. Family members should first receive education regarding intellectual disability, including its definition, management, and prognosis. The family should then receive assistance with placement decisions, including caregiver training and referrals to appropriate services and equipment.</p><p>Families should receive recognition both as an important source of patient support and as individuals who may experience substantial stress. Common psychosocial concerns within families include a desire for respect, experiences of stigma, feelings of helplessness, and symptoms or diagnoses of depression and anxiety.<a>[56]</a></p><p>The healthcare team should also connect families with local disability advocacy organizations, case managers, and educational advocates. In the United States, nationally available resources include the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities (AAIDD), The Arc of the United States, and Family-to-Family Health Information and Education Centers. In the United States, case management or social work support can also help families access state developmental disability agencies and Medicaid Home- and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers, which provide major housing and community supports.<a>[57]</a> Funding varies significantly across states, while international resource availability similarly varies by country.</p><p>Establishing strong family support can, in turn, foster a caring home environment for the patient. Caregiver or parent skills-training programs improve child development and behavior while also strengthening parenting skills and knowledge, caregiver psychological well-being, and family relationships.<a>[58]</a> Family-centered care, which explicitly incorporates family education, shared decision-making, and social and emotional support, improves family well-being and quality of life.<a>[59]</a></p><p>Patients with intellectual disabilities may also qualify for Supplemental Security Income (SSI). SSI represents a means-tested program that requires both a qualifying disability, which often involves marked and severe functional limitations, and low family income and assets. For children, the family's income and assets contribute to eligibility determinations. After age 18, eligibility is determined solely by the individual's own income and assets. In most states, SSI eligibility also confers Medicaid eligibility.</p><p>United States government resources addressing education, employment, food security, and housing are listed under the "Disability Resources" section of the United States Department of Labor website. Other federal supports in the United States can include HUD/Section 8 housing, SNAP, and ABLE accounts, as well as SSDI and Medicare during adulthood.[DOL. <a>Disability and Employment</a>]</p><p>
<b>Psychopharmacologic Interventions</b>
</p><p>Medications are not the main component of intellectual disability treatment, but they play a significant role in treating behavioral abnormalities associated with intellectual disorders and comorbid conditions. Studies have shown that patients with intellectual disabilities are at risk for polypharmacy, and clinicians should be cautious when prescribing medications, as some patients may be at a higher risk of adverse effects and may need lower dosages.<a>[60]</a> Treatment of comorbid conditions, including aggression, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), depression, and movement disorders, is an important aspect of adequate psychopharmacologic therapy.</p><p>
<b>Aggression</b>
</p><p>Aggressive behavior is not uncommon among individuals with intellectual disabilities and causes admission to institutional settings. Risperidone is an atypical antipsychotic well-documented to treat disruptive, aggressive, and self-injurious behaviors in children with intellectual disabilities, with good safety and tolerability profiles.<a>[61]</a> However, pharmacotherapy should also be considered second-line to nonpharmacologic interventions. The American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Practice Parameter (2020) recommends considering risperidone only after assessing whether nonpharmacological means may address aggression or irritability, given that risperidone can cause increased appetite and weight gain, asymptomatic hyperprolactinemia, somnolence, and headache.<a>[29]</a> Extrapyramidal symptoms were shown to be comparable to placebo.</p><p>Aripiprazole is another atypical antipsychotic that is used to manage aggression, which works via D2 partial agonism/antagonism, as opposed to full antagonism via conventional atypical and typical antipsychotics. Aripiprazole is FDA-approved for irritability in autistic disorder for children ages 6 through 17. However, no antipsychotic is FDA-approved specifically for intellectual disability. Aripiprazole has a weaker evidence base compared to risperidone. Regarding adverse effects, aripiprazole is associated with less weight gain and prolactin elevation compared to risperidone but an increased risk of akathisia, sedation, and tremor.<a>[62]</a></p><p>A network meta-analysis in 2025 found that risperidone, aripiprazole, risperidone plus buspirone, omega-3 fatty acids, arbaclofen, and risperidone plus palmitoylethanolamide all significantly reduced agitation compared with placebo in ASD or intellectual disability. All were generally well tolerated, with "no significant increase in all-cause dropouts compared to placebo."<a>[63]</a></p><p>
<b>ADHD</b>
</p><p>Medications for ADHD should be considered adjunctive. The Society for Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics Clinical Practice Guideline (2020) recommends multimodal school- and community-based interventions, eg, special education, behavioral interventions, parent/caregiver training, alongside pharmacotherapy for children with ADHD and ID.<a>[64]</a></p><p>The literature provides support for methylphenidate, α-2 agonists (clonidine and guanfacine), and atomoxetine to reduce ADHD symptoms in patients with intellectual disability. Methylphenidate has the strongest evidence base and is generally considered first-line. Two RCTs in children with intellectual disability plus a meta-analysis showed efficacy with a moderate effect size.<a>[29]</a> Effect size was smaller in children with intellectual disability compared to children with typical development. In a 1990 study of 12 children, methylphenidate improved attention, on-task behavior, and work output; however, these attentional improvements did not translate into improved learning or social interaction.<a>[65]</a> This dissociation between attention and learning was later corroborated by a 2004 study of 24 children in a placebo-controlled, double-blind, crossover trial.<a>[66]</a></p><p>Evidence for atomoxetine is more limited than methylphenidate but still significant. Atomoxetine has shown benefit for ADHD symptoms in intellectual disability with higher doses across multiple studies (one study average dose of 1.22 mg/kg/day, and another ranging between 25 to 60 mg per day).<a>[67]</a></p><p>Evidence for α-2 agonists (clonidine and guanfacine) is more limited than for both methylphenidate and atomoxetine.<a>[67]</a> Clonidine showed "statistically significant improvements in conduct and impulsive-hyperactive symptom scores" in a double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover study in 10 children with ADHD and intellectual disability. 8-µg/kg/day clonidine dosing showed response in 7 out of 10 children. Of note, dose-dependent adverse effects were observed: drowsiness occurred in 5 children, and a dose-dependent, asymptomatic drop in systolic blood pressure of approximately 9% was reported in all children receiving 8 µg/kg/day. No discontinuations occurred. On the other hand, guanfacine showed "significant symptomatic improvements in ABC hyperactivity score only, with both parents (36%) and teachers (56%)" in a double-blind, crossover, placebo-controlled RCT of 11 children ages 5 through 9. Doses of up to 3mg/day were used. Of note, all of these trials were small, and most were short crossover studies, limiting the strength of the evidence.</p><p>
<b>Depression</b>
</p><p>Depressive symptoms can be easily overlooked in individuals with intellectual disabilities when other behavioral problems are prominent; this requires careful evaluation. Diagnosis of depression is also challenging in this population, as criteria may need to emphasize observational data over others.<a>[68]</a> Some important criteria include "sadness, irritability, decreased social interaction, regression of skills, sleep disturbances, diurnal variation, and aggression."</p><p>Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), eg, fluoxetine, paroxetine, and sertraline, have shown the potential to help with depressive symptoms in patients with intellectual disability.<a>[69]</a><a>[68]</a><a>[70]</a> Most current studies are open-label and naturalistic. However, SSRIs do have the potential to interact with other drugs commonly used in adults with intellectual disability, and adverse effects can include "increased aggression, self-injury, and repetitive behavior."<a>[71]</a> These adverse effects may also indicate a potential manic switch. Of note, in a pediatric meta-analysis of the general pediatric population (not necessarily children with intellectual disability), a similar risk of adverse events was noted with SSRIs/SNRIs, and the SSRI/SNRI–placebo difference in depressive disorders was small, partly driven by a high placebo response.<a>[72]</a></p><p>Involuntary movements, repetitive self-stimulatory behaviors, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms may be harmful to the patients. Antipsychotic medications have been anecdotally reported to diminish these symptoms, although there was no observation of improvement in adaptive behavior.<a>[73]</a> SSRIs have been used for aggression, self-injurious behavior, and obsessive-compulsive/stereotyped behaviors, with a systematic review finding improvement in fewer than half of cases on average.<a>[74]</a> The response was most pronounced when an underlying anxiety or obsessive-compulsive disorder was present, though the evidence base is weak and adverse effects were once again a recognized concern.</p><p>
<b>Special populations</b>
</p><p>Trofinetide is a synthetic analog of the amino-terminal tripeptide (GPE) of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1). In March 2023, trofinetide became the first FDA-approved treatment for Rett syndrome (disease-modifying, not a cure). Trofinetide is indicated for adults and pediatric patients aged 2 years and older, and its mechanism is not fully established but is thought to improve neuronal morphology and synaptic function.<a>[75]</a><a>[76]</a> Of note, effect sizes were modest (Cohen d ≈ −0.5 for the Rett Syndrome Behavior Questionnaire, and −0.6 for the CGI-I in the pediatric phase 2 study), and patient follow-up in the trials was only 1 to 3 months.</p><p>Ganaxolone is a neuroactive steroid that modulates both synaptic and extrasynaptic GABA-A receptors (positive allosteric modulation) and was FDA-approved as of March 2022 for seizures associated with cyclin-dependent kinase-like 5 (CDKL5) deficiency disorder (CDD) in patients 2 years and older.<a>[77]</a> CDD is a rare "X-linked, developmental and epileptic encephalopathy" associated with severe intellectual disabilities.<a>[78]</a> Ganaxolone is also a synthetic analog of allopregnanolone, distinguished by a 3β-methyl substitution along with its extrasynaptic (tonic inhibition) activity. Separately, ganaxolone has been studied in tuberous sclerosis-related epilepsy, status epilepticus, and focal epilepsy with variable results, and postpartum depression without any demonstrated benefit.</p><p>
<b>Additional Considerations</b>
</p><p>The internet, social media, and mobile apps have expanded accessibility for people with intellectual disability or limited literacy, offering simplified text, easier-to-navigate platforms, and access to health information for patients and families.<a>[79]</a> Advances in artificial intelligence further support automated language simplification and assistive reading technologies.<a>[80]</a> However, most consumer tools currently have a limited evidence base, and a persistent digital divide (driven by device access, cost, digital literacy, and the need for support) constrains benefit, particularly in low-resource settings.</p><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Table</a></h4></div></div></div><div><h2>Differential Diagnosis</h2><p>The following diseases or conditions can be mistaken for improves.</div></li><li><div>However, severe environmental deprivation, neglect, and abuse are associated with (and can also cause) pervasive and often persistent cognitive deficits and intellectual disability.</div></li></ul></li><li><div><b>Cerebral palsy</b>: This can present with poor muscle coordination, vision, hearing, and speech problems, which may itself mimic symptoms of intellectual global impairment across verbal and nonverbal domains.</div></li></ul></li></ul></div><div><h2>Prognosis</h2><p>The prognosis of intellectual disability depends on the severity of adaptive functioning impairment, co-occurring conditions, and the supports provided.<a>[82]</a> With adequate support, individuals with mild intellectual disabilities may achieve sufficient language and social skills to live independently and raise their own families. Independence levels can vary; population follow-up data show heterogeneous real-world outcomes. In one cohort (mean age of 36), 44% were employed at some point, and 56% were never employed; most needed societal support.<a>[83]</a> The AAP also notes that some individuals with mild intellectual disabilities may reach a sixth-grade academic level.</p><p>Individuals with moderate intellectual disabilities may achieve elementary-level academic skills. They can acquire language to communicate their needs, form relationships, and may be employed in jobs requiring minimal communication and cognitive demands, with ongoing support and teaching.</p><p>Individuals with severe intellectual disabilities usually have difficulty acquiring language, so they have trouble with communication and social functioning. They may be able to develop alternative communication skills and recognize critical words for functioning. Individuals with severe intellectual disabilities require substantial daily support, which can be provided in community-based living arrangements with appropriate services, and supervision is often required for them to perform any work-related tasks. Individuals with profound intellectual disabilities present with very limited symbolic communication and typically require around-the-clock support for all activities.</p></div><div><h2>Complications</h2><p>About a third of individuals with intellectual disabilities have diagnosed comorbid psychiatric disorders.<a>[84]</a> One study suggests a point prevalence of any psychiatric condition and intellectual disability of up to 76%.<a>[85]</a> Depression can occur across all severity levels; more severe intellectual disabilities may present atypically as irritability, aggression, or self-injury rather than reported sadness, and is often underdiagnosed because of communication barriers and diagnostic overshadowing. Other frequently observed comorbidities include ADHD/hyperactivity, anxiety disorders, autism spectrum disorder, and self-injurious and repetitive stereotypic behaviors. (Please refer to the <b>Treatment</b> section for more information on management of complications.)</p></div><div><h2>Deterrence and Patient Education</h2><p>Among the most challenging aspects of intellectual disability are deficits in social skills and the resulting risk of social isolation. Family education plays a critical role in helping patients develop social competence and strengthen self-esteem. Families can support these goals by encouraging positive social interactions, facilitating participation in appropriate community activities, and providing opportunities to develop and practice interpersonal skills. The Special Olympics International program represents 1 example of a structured program that can promote social interaction, friendship development, and improved self-esteem.<a>[86]</a> Individuals with disabilities benefit from ongoing opportunities and structured support that encourage meaningful social participation and inclusion.</p><p>Families of individuals with intellectual disabilities should also recognize their increased vulnerability to experiences of failure and rejection and should provide a consistently encouraging and supportive environment. Positive reinforcement, emotional support, and opportunities for successful social engagement can help promote confidence and resilience. Family therapy may also provide valuable support by addressing feelings of guilt, despair, or anger that may arise within family relationships and by helping family members strengthen communication, coping strategies, and mutual support.</p></div><div><h2>Enhancing Healthcare Team Outcomes</h2><p>Intellectual disability is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by deficits in intellectual and adaptive functioning that begin during the developmental period. Clinical evaluation should include a comprehensive history, physical and neurologic examination, assessment of adaptive functioning, hearing and vision testing, and targeted genetic, metabolic, or neuroimaging studies when indicated. Management focuses on identifying treatable causes, addressing psychiatric, neurologic, and medical comorbidities, and providing individualized educational, behavioral, family, vocational, and pharmacologic interventions. Careful medication selection, monitoring for adverse effects, and reduction of unnecessary polypharmacy can help prevent medication-related harm.</p><p>The management of patients with intellectual disabilities is best achieved by an interprofessional team. Communication barriers can make history-taking and shared decision-making difficult. This may inadvertently lead to patients being excluded from decisions or pressured into treatments. Therefore, the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities emphasizes the inclusion of patients as much as possible in treatment decisions.<a>[87]</a> Physicians, primary care clinicians, and advanced practitioners can coordinate evaluation, referral, treatment, and follow-up with psychologists, psychiatrists, neurologists, speech-language professionals, nurses, social workers, pharmacists, educators, patients, and families. Pharmacists provide valuable review of the medications during reconciliation, help select medications for comorbidities, and verify dosing. Pharmacists can also educate patients and their families about the medications they are using and help significantly with deprescribing (as in the STOMP initiative in the UK).<a>[88]</a></p><p>Effective communication and accurate documentation support continuity of care, timely escalation, and avoidance of redundant testing or preventable hospital use. Nurses monitor treatment response and adherence, while pharmacists support medication reconciliation, dosing, patient and family education, deprescribing, and communication of adverse events. Shared decision-making should accommodate communication barriers and promote meaningful patient participation in treatment decisions, thereby improving care coordination, medication safety, and patient-centered outcomes.</p></div><div><h2>Review Questions</h2><ul><li><div>
