# Renal Sarcoma: A Population-Based Study.
**Authors:** Alevizakos M, Gaitanidis A, Korentzelos D, Basourakos SP, Burgess M
**Journal:** Clin Genitourin Cancer (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.clgc.2022.07.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clgc.2022.07.012)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Renal sarcomas are exceedingly rare and lack a prognostic stage classification. We thus aimed to investigate the contemporary clinicopathologic characteristics and outcomes of renal sarcomas at a national level.

PATIENTS AND METHODS: We utilized the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database to extract data on patients with renal sarcoma diagnosed between 2004 and 2015. We estimated median, 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) probabilities via Kaplan-Meier curves and used multivariable regression to compare OS between different patient groups.

RESULTS: We identified 365 patients; at diagnosis, 104 patients (28.5%) had stage I disease (T1N0M0), 133 patients (36.4%) patients had stage II disease (T2-4N0M0), and 117 patients (32.1%) patients had stage III disease (any T, N1, or M1). Median survival was 105 months (interquartile range [IQR], 29 - not reached) for stage I disease, 46 months (IQR 14-118 months) for stage II disease, 8 months (IQR 3-28 months) for stage III disease, and 32 months (IQR, 8-116 months) for the entire cohort. Patient age (hazard ratio [HR] for death [per year] 1.02, 95% confidence interval [95% CI] 1.00-1.04), stage (II vs. I: HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.00-2.92; III vs. I: HR 4.93, 95% CI 2.68-9.05), grade (grade 3 vs. grade 1: 3.07, 95% CI 1.18-8.00; grade 4 vs. grade 1: HR 3.66, 95% CI 1.41-9.49), and possessing medical insurance (HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.16-0.94) were independently and significantly associated with OS. Performance of nephrectomy also trended towards independently improving OS (HR 0.23, 95% CI 0.05-1.09).

CONCLUSION: A novel staging classification for renal sarcomas into a 3-stage system based on Tumor Node Metastasis (TNM) criteria produces distinct survival curves, although further studies are needed to robustly assess its validity.

Renal sarcomas are exceptionally rare, accounting for approximately 1% of all primary kidney malignancies.1,2 The kidney is the second most common site of genitourinary sarcomas, which collectively represent 2% of all soft tissue sarcomas.3 They constitute a wide spectrum of malignancies as they comprise various histologies,4 of which leiomyosarcoma and liposarcoma are the most common.3,5 Given their rarity, contemporary data on these malignancies exists only in form of case reports and case series with a low number of patients.3,4,6 Consequently, concrete epidemiologic data as well as a prognostic stage classification are currently lacking.7–10 Additionally, management of these tumors is suboptimally based on either the aforementioned data or extrapolation from other renal cancers or sarcomas of different sites.7–9

To delineate the clinical and histologic characteristics and outcomes of patients with renal sarcomas at a national level, we queried the National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. This allowed us to more accurately define outcomes of this disease in a recent patient cohort as well as examine association of outcomes with potential predictors at the national level.

We accessed a cohort of patients with the diagnosis of primary renal sarcoma from the National Cancer Institute’s SEER database. This database contains high quality data from 18 cancer registries and covers approximately 34.6% of the total US population based on the 2010 census.11 All patient data are de-identified and available to the public and thus Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was not required for the conduct of this study.

We applied the following criteria to identify eligible cases with defined sarcoma histologies and excluding sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma: (a) microscopically confirmed tumor arising in the kidney or renal pelvis, (b) ICD-O-3 histology codes 8800 to 8935, 8964, 8980 to 8991,12 (c) tumor diagnosed between January 1, 2004 and December 31, 2015, (d) reported tumor being primary by the International Association of Cancer Registry rules,13 (e) age at diagnosis equal to or greater than 18 years. Specifically, the ICD-O-3 codes pertained to sarcomas not otherwise specified, fibromatous malignant neoplasms, malignant fibrous histiocytomas, liposarcoma neoplasms, myomatous malignant neoplasms, rhabdomyosarcomas, clear cell sarcomas of kidney, carcinosarcomas, and malignant mesenchymomas.12 We accessed the aforementioned study period as this was the one utilizing the latest SEER coding schema and had the most available data.

We extracted data on patient age at diagnosis, gender, ethnicity, and insurance status. We also accessed data on tumor histology, size, extension, and grade at diagnosis, as well as whether nephrectomy had been performed. We additionally recorded length of observation, overall survival (OS, measured in months) and cause of death for all patients. We used the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system 10 for TNM group staging. Notably, since there is no prognostic staging grouping for renal sarcomas currently specified by AJCC,10 we developed the following staging schema: Tumors that were confined to the kidney and < 7 cm (T1N0M0) were considered stage I disease, larger primary tumors that had not metastasized to regional lymph nodes or distant sites were considered stage II disease (T2N0M0, T3N0M0, T4N0M0), and tumors that had metastasized to regional lymph nodes or distant sites were considered metastatic or stage III disease (any T, N1, or M1). We included lymph node metastasis to our metastatic classification because nodal involvement is independently and substantially associated with increased mortality in soft tissue sarcomas,14 and N1 disease is classified as stage IV for sarcomas of the trunk and the extremities.10,15

We subsequently developed Kaplan-Meier curves to determine median and 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS probabilities and their respective 95% confidence interval (95% CI)16 and used the reverse Kaplan-Meier method to calculate the median follow-up time. To evaluate associations of known parameters with OS, we generated pre-specified regression models with all covariates chosen based on background knowledge and theoretical considerations.17 This was done in order to avoid the inherent inferential limitations associated with univariate comparisons or stepwise procedures.17 We thus performed multivariable Cox regression to identify the effect of age, ethnicity, insurance status, sarcoma stage, grade, and histology, and performance of nephrectomy in OS. Since we had grouped together nodal and distant metastatic disease in our staging classification, we also performed the log-rank test to compare OS between N0 versus N1 disease as well as M0 versus M1 disease.16 We used the STATA v13 software package (STATA Corporation, College Station, TX) for the statistical analysis and the alpha level of statistical significance was set at 0.05.

We identified a total of 365 patients with renal sarcoma and median follow-up was 71 months (interquartile range [IQR] 33–108 months). Median age at diagnosis was 61 years (IQR 51–72 years) and median tumor size was 10 centimeters (IQR 6–16 centimeters). The most common histology was leiomysarcoma (113 patients, 31%), followed by liposarcoma (95 patients, 26%). At diagnosis, 104 patients (28.5%) had stage I disease, 133 patients (36.4%) patients had stage II disease, and 117 patients (32.1%) patients had stage III disease; disease stage was unknown in 11 (3%) of patients. The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS probabilities for the entire cohort were 71% (95% CI 65%−75%), 48% (95% CI 42–53%), and 42% (95% CI 36–48%) respectively and the median survival was 32 months (IQR, 8–116 months). Median OS was 105 months (IQR 29 – not reached) for stage I disease, 46 months (IQR 14–118 months) for stage II disease, and 8 months (IQR 3–28 months) for stage III disease. OS probabilities by stage are listed in Table 2 & Figure 1.

In multivariable analysis, increasing age (hazard ratio [HR] 1.02, 95% CI 1.00–1.04), stage (stage II vs. stage I: HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.00–2.92; stage III vs. stage I: HR 4.93, 95% CI 2.68–9.05), and grade (grade 3 vs. grade 1: 3.07, 95% CI 1.18–8.00; grade 4 vs. grade 1: HR 3.66, 95% CI 1.41–9.49) were significantly and independently associated with worse OS. Additionally, possessing medical insurance was associated with improved OS (HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.16–0.94) and performance of nephrectomy trended towards independently improving OS (HR 0.23, 95% CI 0.05–1.09). Gender (female vs. male, HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.70–1.63) and ethnicity (black vs. white, HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.25–1.79; other [American Indian, Alaska Native, Asian, Pacific Islander] vs. white, HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.48–2.15) were not significantly associated with OS. Sarcomas of the kidney are exceedingly rare malignances with limited current epidemiologic and prognostic data. In our study, we accessed a multi-institutional registry and analyzed the largest recent cohort of patients with renal sarcomas, characterizing the epidemiologic, clinical, and pathologic characteristics of this group of malignancies. This allowed us to investigate prognostic factors and to evaluate a novel staging classification.

In our analysis, we observed that median age at diagnosis was 61 years, somewhat higher than what has been historically reported. 1–3,18 Median tumor size was 10 cm, in accordance with prior reports,2,3,18 whereas the male predominance noted in these older studies 2,3 was not reaffirmed in our analysis, where the number of male and female patients was roughly equal. Additionally, leiomyosarcoma and liposarcoma represented the majority of renal sarcomas, in accordance with the literature.1–3 Staging data in the literature are scarcer as most studies record renal sarcomas as either localized or metastatic2,3 and there is no recommended prognostic stage grouping currently endorsed by the AJCC for this group of malignancies.10 In our study, we identified that approximately a third of patients presented with low-volume disease confined to the kidney (T1N0M0), a third of patients presented with larger primary tumors (T2–4N0M0), and another third had nodal or distant metastatic disease at initial staging (any T, N1, or M1).

Importantly, we found that the above classification had substantial prognostic implications. Specifically, OS was significantly different in the various stages of diseases, with median survival of approximately 9 years for stage I disease, 4.5 years for stage II disease, and only 0.7 years (8.4 months) for disease with synchronous nodal or distant metastases. These outcomes are unfortunately worse than the ones reported in a historic Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) cohort of 131 patients with genitourinary sarcomas from 1977 to 2003, which reported median disease-specific survival of 10.7 years and 1.4 years for non-metastatic and metastatic disease respectively.3 Although the aforementioned study had included all genitourinary sarcomas, kidney origin of tumor was not shown to be correlated with survival.3 The reason for the observed survival difference thus potentially highlights the importance of seeking specialized and centralized care for such rare tumors 19 or alternatively it may reflect publication bias.16 Notably, in our study nodal metastasis was considered sufficient for inclusion to the metastatic group, since it is independently and substantially associated with increased mortality in soft tissue sarcomas.14,15 In our analysis, we found that both N1M0 and N0M1 disease were independently associated with markedly worse OS compared to N0M0 disease, with similar survival curves in both comparisons, thus supporting our classification.

Furthermore, in our study we identified several other effectors of survival. Increasing age and tumor grade were independently associated with worse survival, overall in accordance with the available literature for soft tissues sarcomas in general.20 In contrast, leiomyosarcoma or liposarcoma histologies were not found to lead to different survival, also in agreement with prior reports.3 Interestingly, we also found that lack of medical insurance was independently associated with increased mortality. This observation adds to a growing number of evidence that links uninsured status with decreased cancer survival as a result of suboptimal care.21,22 Furthermore, performance of nephrectomy trended towards a significant improvement in survival. Nephrectomy remains the cornerstone of management for stage I kidney tumors23 and our inability to show a significant survival benefit from this modality in our multivariable analysis was likely secondary to inadequate power and potentially inclusion of metastatic disease. Indeed, surgery has remained the cornerstone of management of operable renal sarcomas the period of observation and to the present day.24, 25

Regarding study limitations, tumor grade was only based on tumor differentiation since data on mitotic count and tumor necrosis, which are required for the commonly used FNCLCC (National Federation of French Cancer Centers) histologic grade,10 were unavailable. Additionally, SEER coding does not specify multifocal involvement of the primary site. Thus, we could not identify any T4 cases, leading to a more conservative T staging and potentially limiting our ability to discern survival differences across T subgroups. However, we found that T2 to T3 tumors, which would be classified along T4 tumors as stage II disease, were associated with worse survival than T1 tumors, and nodal and distant metastatic disease was associated with worse survival regardless of T stage. Furthermore, the SEER database does not record data on systemic treatments, thus their effect on the observed outcomes could not be assessed. However, all our patients were diagnosed between 2004 and 2015, allowing for some homogeneity in the administered systemic regimens which have not yet provided substantial benefit in advanced disease with response rates of 10% to 20% with use of multi-agent chemotherapy and targeted agents, such as pazopanib. To this date, substantial investigation continues into novel agents as well as systemic immunotherapy.24

In conclusion, renal sarcomas constitute a heterogeneous group of diseases, for which prognosis is strongly dependent on stage at presentation. A novel disease classification into stage I, stage II, and stage III (metastatic) groups, with N1 disease included in the metastatic group, provides well-separated survival curves which can help inform patient counseling and possibly management. Given the paucity of data, further studies are certainly warranted to assess the validity of this model and the efficacy of employed interventions; however our model can provide clinicians with valuable prognostic and therapeutic information.
