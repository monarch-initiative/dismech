# Decreased mitochondrial activity in the demyelinating cerebellum of progressive multiple sclerosis and chronic EAE contributes to Purkinje cell loss.
**Authors:** Atkinson KC, Desfor S, Feri M, Sekyi MT, Osunde M, Sriram S, Noori S, Rincón W, Bello B, Tiwari-Woodruff SK
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2421806122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2421806122)

## Content

In multiple sclerosis (MS), cerebellar gray matter atrophy, white matter demyelination, and Purkinje cell (PC) loss have been linked to tremors, impaired motor control, and loss of coordination. Similar pathologies have been observed in the mouse model of MS, experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE). This study hypothesized that inflammatory demyelination of the cerebellum alters overall mitochondrial function and is a contributor to axon degeneration and PC loss. Postmortem cerebellar tissue from MS patients, particularly those with secondary progressive MS, showed decreased mitochondrial complex IV (COXIV) activity and significant PC loss. Inflammation, PC axon demyelination, axon degeneration, and parallel fiber loss were also evident. These findings were mirrored in late-stage EAE mice, which also showed increased inflammation and demyelination, reduced PC COXIV activity, and overall PC loss. Further analysis of EAE mice revealed altered mitochondrial structure, modified mitochondrial respiration, and reduced levels of mitochondrial genes involved in energy production. These findings indicate that both human MS and mouse EAE share similar cerebellar changes linked to mitochondrial dysfunction. Thus, late-stage EAE is a valuable model for studying MS-related cerebellar pathology, and mitochondria may be a potential therapeutic target for MS treatment.

Multiple sclerosis (MS) affects 2.3 million individuals globally, characterized by autoimmune-driven inflammation leading to central nervous system (CNS) demyelination and chronic disability. About 80% of MS patients experience cerebellar inflammation-associated demyelination, manifesting as tremors, impaired motor control, and coordination loss throughout their lives (1–3). Cerebellar gray matter atrophy occurs in later MS disease stages (4). However, studies suggest a correlation between cerebellar cortex demyelination and earlier mortality in primary progressive MS (PPMS) and secondary progressive MS (SPMS) patients (2, 5). Despite cerebellar dysfunction being prevalent in MS, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Purkinje cells (PCs), myelinated gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic neurons, are the sole output neurons of the cerebellar cortex, integrating signals from the brain and spinal cord to modulate motor activity (6). PCs receive input from climbing fibers (from the inferior olive) and parallel fibers (from granule cells) and send inhibitory signals to the deep cerebellar nuclei, which then influence motor pathways (7). PCs, with their extensive dendritic arbors and glutamatergic inputs, necessitate high metabolic activity for efficient signal transmission to deep cerebellar nuclei (8). Postmortem analysis reveals inflammation, demyelination, and PC loss within cerebellar MS lesions (5, 9, 10), alongside evidence of PC fusion and heterokaryon formation possibly for neuronal repair initiation (11). MS-afflicted PCs, influenced by demyelination and increased energy demands, also exhibit abnormal expression of Nav1.8, a voltage-gated Na+ channel typically found in unmyelinated nociceptive fibers, potentially leading to increased spontaneous firing and disruption of CNS circuitry (12). This also likely contributes to exacerbated MS-associated cerebellar dysfunction (13).

Mitochondria, crucial for energy production via oxidative phosphorylation, are implicated in MS neurodegeneration (14). The CNS’s high metabolic rate makes it particularly vulnerable to even slight aberrations in energy supply. Under pathological conditions such as oxidative stress, excessive Ca2+ influx can trigger mitochondrial fission and/or fusion, yielding fragmented/swollen and dysfunctional mitochondria and contributing to cell apoptosis and subsequent neurodegeneration (15, 16). PCs in the cerebellum are among the most energy-demanding cells in the human body, with each PC using approximately 1.24 × 1010 adenosine triphosphate (ATP) molecules per second (17), making them exceptionally susceptible to mitochondrial dysfunction. Indeed, PC demyelination, PC loss, and ataxia are prominent in primary mitochondrial diseases (18, 19).

Postmortem studies of MS CNS tissue demonstrated a compromised neuronal ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation and suppressed mitochondrial complex IV (COXIV) activity in demyelinated axons and neurons (20–22). Given that neuronal mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated in MS pathogenesis (22–25), understanding mitochondrial dynamics, especially in PCs, could be pivotal in deciphering cerebellar symptoms in MS.

Previous research from our group and others have demonstrated inflammatory demyelination in the cerebellum and PC loss during the late stages of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), a mouse model of MS (14, 26). Mitochondrial dysfunction has been observed as early as the asymptomatic phase of EAE, preceding the onset of clinical symptoms (27, 28). Evidence suggests that mitochondrial stress is closely associated with neuronal loss (28). Additionally, during CNS inflammation in EAE, metabolic disruption arises due to increased energy demands that demyelinated neurons cannot meet. This is attributed to altered mitochondrial morphology, reduced mitochondrial activity, and changes in the expression of genes involved in mitochondrial function (20, 29).

Our hypothesis suggests that changes in mitochondrial dynamics within the cerebellum play a role in progressive PC loss and worsening cerebellar dysfunction in MS and its experimental model, EAE. To explore mitochondrial dysfunction in the MS cerebellum, we conducted a pathological analysis of cerebellar tissue, primarily from postmortem SPMS patients and healthy controls, obtained from the NeuroBioBank and the Cleveland Clinic. To further investigate the mechanisms and progression of cerebellar dysfunction, we utilized the chronic EAE model, which mirrors critical aspects of MS, such as inflammation, demyelination, and neurodegeneration (26, 30). At various stages of EAE progression, we assessed behavior, pathology, mitochondrial activity, and mitochondrial protein expression and performed NanoString ribonucleic acid (RNA) analysis. Findings from both MS and EAE studies revealed significant pathological changes and PC death. Notably, the loss of COXIV in demyelinated PC cells suggests a link between PC death and mitochondrial dysfunction. Unraveling the pathophysiology of MS symptoms holds promise for developing improved treatments and enhancing the quality of life for individuals living with MS.

The cerebellar cortex is a major site for demyelination with nearly 38.7% of the cerebellar cortex being affected in a cohort of PPMS and SPMS patients (5, 9, 10). To establish MS-induced cerebellar pathology, a cohort of MS cerebellar sections from primarily a SPMS cohort with an expanded disability status scale (EDSS) of 6 to 9 (details in SI Appendix, Table S1) was compared to “normal” control cerebellar sections using immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Fig. 1 A, i and SI Appendix, Table S2A). The MS cerebellar sections (Fig. 1A) showed significant white matter (WM) demyelination using proteolipid protein (PLP) immunostaining as compared to normal control sections (t = 8.774, df = 14, P = 0.0001) (Fig. 1 A, ii–iv). Additionally, increased inflammatory lesions which extended from the WM into the adjacent granule cell layer (GCL), molecular layer (ML), and the meningeal layers were identified with the presence of Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP)+ activated astrocytes (WM+GCL: t = 4.295, df = 13, P = 0.0009; ML: t = 3.250, df = 13, P = 0.0063) and leukocytes (CD45) (WM+GCL: t = 2.601, df = 13, P = 0.0219; ML: t = 2.188, df = 13, P = 0.0476) (Fig. 1 B, i–iii). Parallel fibers, the axons of granule cells, run perpendicular to PC dendrites. They serve as a major pathway for transmitting sensory and motor information to PCs. A significant loss of parallel fibers (immunostained with NF200) in MS is accompanied by axon damage (t = 2.617, df = 14, P = 0.0101, Fig. 1 C, i–iii).

To determine whether PC counts are altered in the MS cerebellum, IHC for Calbindin, a calcium-binding protein expressed in PCs, was performed. There was a significant decrease in Calbindin+ PCs in the MS postmortem tissue compared to the control postmortem tissue in every lobule (Fig. 2 A, i and ii; t = 2.882, df = 13, P = 0.0128). To determine whether these pathological changes were coupled with alterations in mitochondria activity, overall cerebellar COXIV expression was assessed (Fig. 5A). There was a regional change in the distribution of COXIV during EAE. Compared to normal, EAE D21 showed a significant decrease in overall expression, specifically in the GCL as compared to the PCL [F(3, 16) = 30.27, P < 0.0001] (Fig. 5 A, i and iii). Interestingly, in EAE D40, COXIV staining appeared to rebound, and by EAE D60, there was no significant difference as compared to the normal group (Fig. 5 A, iii). This change in COXIV expression was not in the PC layer but the GCL and WM of the cerebellar lobules (Fig. 5 A, i and iii). Next, the number of PCs was counted and COXIV puncta were quantified in individual delineated PC somas (Fig. 5 B, i and ii). Analysis revealed a significant decrease in PC numbers at EAE D40 and EAE D60 compared to normal [Fig. 5 B, iii, F(3, 32) = 4.084, EAE D40: P = 0.0144; EAE D60: P = 0.0143]. This also coincided with a decrease in COXIV puncta at EAE D60 compared to normal [Fig. 5 B, ii–iv, F(3, 113) = 3.906, EAE D60: P = 0.0031]. The decrease in PC COXIV puncta was contrary to the overall increase in cerebellar COXIV expression at EAE D60 in Fig. 5 A, i and iii and indicated dysfunctional mitochondria in PCs. To investigate PCs and axonal mitochondrial pathology at a higher resolution, we conducted transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of normal and EAE D40 cerebellar sections (Fig. 5C). As compared to normal, the cerebellar WM of EAE D40 sections indicated loss of myelin (Fig. 5 C, i). Compared to normal groups, no differences in basal respiration, coupled respiration, uncoupled respiration, and spare respiratory capacity (SRC) were observed between EAE D60 and normal mice (Fig. 6 A, ii).

To further assess mitochondrial changes in the cerebellum, a comprehensive analysis of oxidative phosphorylation was performed (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Table S2B). Mitochondria homogenates of normal, EAE D21, EAE D40, and EAE D60 groups were compared with subunits of all 5 components of the electron transport chain (OXPHOS), using voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) as a loading control. Decreased protein levels of the mitochondrially encoded cytochrome c oxidase I (MTC01) subunit of COXIV in cerebellar mitochondria at EAE D40 and EAE D60 was observed compared to normal mice [Fig. 6 B, i and ii, H(4) = 7.138, P = 0.0303, EAE D40: P = 0.0313, EAE D60: P = 0.0116]. There were no significant differences in any of the other complexes between normal and EAE groups. To determine whether mitochondrial transport is altered in EAE longitudinally, we measured syntaphilin, an axonal protein that is used as a measure of anchored mitochondria (31) (Fig. 6 B, ii). EAE D40 and EAE D60 mice had decreased syntaphilin expression compared to normal mice [H(4) = 14.50, P = 0.0023, EAE D40: P = 0.0088, EAE D60: P = 0.0067].

To quantify the expression of genes involved in core metabolic processes and immunometabolism in normal and EAE D40 cerebellum, NanoString gene analysis was performed (Fig. 7). The nCounter “Metabolic Pathways Panel” quantifies 768 genes that are involved in essential pathways related to cell metabolism, signaling, and stress (Dataset S1). A heat map shows changes in all genes from the Metabolic Pathways panel (Fig. 7 A, ii). A volcano plot was used to demonstrate genes that are differentially expressed during EAE (Fig. 7 A, iii). Genes that were significantly upregulated in EAE D40 such as Col4a1 and Col4a2 are components of type IV collagen and important in the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. Other upregulated genes such as Aspa, Nat8l, and Slc16a6 have been shown to be important in amino acid synthesis and transport. Genes that were significantly downregulated in EAE D40 were related to different components of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system, such as COXI, COXIV, and COXV. Past studies have shown significant changes in associated genes during EAE in the CNS (32, 33). Cerebellar pathology in MS can lead to various forms of ataxia, but directly linking symptoms to specific cerebellar damage, such as PC loss, remains challenging. Postmortem cerebellar tissue analyzed in this study came from patients with severe motor disability (EDSS 6 to 9). Similarly, EAE mice exhibited significant motor impairment, as indicated by high EAE scores and rotarod performance deficits (34, 35). Gait analysis revealed a persistent decrease in stride length from early EAE (D13) through the chronic stage, while other gait parameters remained unchanged (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (36, 37). Although not solely indicative of cerebellar dysfunction, the reduced stride length suggests impairment within cerebellar circuitry in EAE mice.

Ongoing research seeks to better understand the mechanisms by which MS affects the reduction in PC numbers, including the roles of specific inflammatory pathways, mitochondrial dynamics, and neurodegeneration. Identifying these mechanisms may help develop targeted therapies to protect cerebellar function and alleviate the motor deficits associated with MS.

After 10 to 15 y of MS, most patients transition to SPMS, characterized by gradual brain and spinal cord atrophy due to myelin loss and cumulative neuronal damage. In the cerebellum, PCs and other neurons are progressively damaged, contributing to symptoms like ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremors, and nystagmus (3, 5). PCs are particularly vulnerable due to MS-related oxidative stress, demyelination, and inflammation, which disrupt mitochondrial function. This dysfunction leads to energy deficits, increased reactive oxygen species production, and impaired calcium handling, all of which can negatively affect PC survival (22).

Previous studies have shown significant cerebellar inflammation and demyelination in MS, but findings on PC loss vary. Howell et al. used 14 SPMS female samples and quantification revealed no significant difference in the PC density between controls and the SPMS samples (10). Similarly, Redondo et al. (9) used 6 MS 40 to 50-y-old female samples and compared them to 80-y-old normal male tissue. Their results showed PC pathology in MS but did not show an overall loss in PC numbers throughout the cerebellum. PC loss was specifically found in the lesioned areas similar to what was observed earlier by Kutzelnigg et al. (5). However, Kemp et al. (11) found a significant loss of PCs using varied samples from eight patients with MS that included 1 RRMS, 1 PMS, and 5 SPMS (mostly female patients) compared to six young, control male patients with no pathology. Significant PC loss was observed in some MS cases, including the Charcot and Marburg variants, linking it to cerebellar demyelination (38). Interestingly, PC loss is also seen in essential tremor (39), suggesting a potential common pathological mechanism underlying ataxia.

Here, we used a cohort of mostly SPMS (n = 9), along with RRMS n = 1 and PMS n = 1 cerebellar tissue controlled for age, sex, duration of disease, and EDSS scores, and compared them to normal (n = 5) cerebellar tissue. Our study findings revealed significant demyelination, inflammation, and parallel fiber loss, accompanied by a significant reduction in PCs and a decrease in COXIV puncta within PC bodies in the MS cerebellum as compared to the normal group. These results unequivocally suggest the connection between inflammatory demyelination and mitochondrial dysfunction in PCs.

Our study is the first to quantify a reduction in mitochondrial function (COXIV IHC) specifically in PCs somas in the MS brain. Previous research has reported decreased COXIV expression in pattern III MS lesions, affecting axons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes (40). Additionally, COXIV expression was reduced in axons at the rim of chronic active lesions, whereas axons in inactive lesions showed increased COXIV activity (22), indicating distinct mitochondrial alterations across different lesion types. Other studies have also reported an overall reduction in COXIV expression in postmortem MS brains (22). Further investigations, including genomic spatial profiling of MS postmortem tissue, are needed to correlate decreased COXIV in PCs with demyelinating white matter lesions, inflammation, and broader cerebellar pathology (41).

The second phase of our study examined whether the inflammatory demyelination, PC loss, and mitochondrial changes observed in SPMS patients could be studied over time in the EAE mouse model. Human postmortem studies are limited by factors like patient variability and treatment history, making animal models crucial for understanding disease progression and the molecular mechanisms driving mitochondrial dysfunction and PC degeneration.

Pathological changes observed in SPMS postmortem tissue were similarly assessed in EAE mice at peak (D21), late (D40), and chronic (D60) stages. Previous studies have reported significant cerebellar pathology in EAE, including inflammation, axon damage (42, 43), and PC loss (26). In this study, Iba-1+ cell increases were noted at all EAE stages, consistent with findings in other CNS regions (30, 44, 45). While Iba-1 is not a definitive inflammation marker, further analysis of immune cell involvement will enhance comparisons with human MS pathology. Inflammation and demyelination were observed as early as EAE D14 (26, 42, 43, 46), with axon damage appearing as a hallmark feature of both EAE and MS (47, 48). Some studies suggest that axon degeneration may precede demyelination due to oxidative stress (24) and sodium channel alterations (49). This study confirms white matter axon damage and reduced parallel fibers in EAE mice, further supporting its relevance as a model for MS-related cerebellar pathology.

Mitochondrial dysfunction, fragmentation, and impaired trafficking are more pronounced in spinal cord axons near perivascular inflammatory cells and are associated with increased phosphofructokinase-2 expression in activated astrocytes and reduced mitochondrial complex I activity, preceding demyelination or axonal degeneration (28). ATP production is essential for neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity, and demyelination increases neuronal energy demands, requiring mitochondria to redistribute accordingly (50, 51). PCs have a high mitochondrial density to support their elevated energy needs (52, 53). In this study, EAE D60 mice exhibited decreased COXIV levels in PC somas and increased PC loss, suggesting that COXIV reduction may precede PC degeneration. A prior study of MS autopsy tissue found a positive correlation between mitochondrial content in demyelinated axons and COXIV deficiency in dorsal root ganglia neuronal cell bodies (51). COXIV expression in the EAE cerebellum fluctuated between EAE D21 and D60, likely due to mitochondrial redistribution from cell bodies to axons. High COXIV intensity in granule cells and PCs made quantification in cerebellar white matter challenging. Future studies using two-photon microscopy in MITOtag-YFP mice will further investigate these mitochondrial dynamics. Ultrastructural analysis revealed increased mitochondrial number and size in demyelinating PC axons and morphologically distinct mitochondria in PC somas at EAE D40.

The decrease in coupled respiration at EAE D21 may result from inflammation, active demyelination, and cellular stress affecting oligodendrocytes, neurons, and their progenitors. This decline aligns with the reduction in COXIV observed via IHC at peak disease. By EAE D60, COXIV levels (except in PC somas) rebounded, corresponding with restored coupled respiration. However, Western blot analysis revealed an overall decline in COXIV protein in late and chronic EAE, mirroring the COXIV decrease in PCs. While a link between reduced COXIV in PCs and impaired respiration is possible, further cell-specific studies are needed. Previous research has shown that deleting mitochondrial ATPase in PCs leads to PC loss and decreased COXI and COXIV expression (54). Although no significant difference in ATP5A protein expression was found in EAE D21 mice, nuclear-encoded ATP Synthase genes were downregulated in SPMS postmortem tissue (23) and EAE D40 RNA analysis. The decline in COXIV protein in late-stage EAE suggests that early disease events influence long-term cellular health.

Syntaphilin is crucial for anchoring mitochondria to microtubules, ensuring proper distribution within axons to meet local energy demands. Its role becomes especially important in demyelinated regions, where axons experience increased metabolic stress due to myelin loss. In MS, elevated syntaphilin levels in chronic spinal cord lesions suggest that mitochondria in demyelinated axons become relatively immobile, potentially contributing to disease pathology (22, 31, 55). This reduced mitochondrial mobility may contribute to the accumulation of mitochondria within demyelinated axons, potentially playing a role in the pathophysiology of chronic MS lesions (22). However, in EAE, syntaphilin levels in the cerebellum decrease significantly at EAE D40 and D60, indicating impaired mitochondrial anchoring, which may promote neuronal degeneration. Alternatively, this reduction could reflect changes in mitochondrial positioning within demyelinated axons. In a healthy CNS, syntaphilin supports mitochondrial function, but during demyelination, increased syntaphilin may have harmful effects. This is supported by findings in shiverer mice, where syntaphilin deletion improved survival, preserved PCs, and reduced axonal damage (55). These results suggest syntaphilin as a potential therapeutic target for demyelinating diseases.

During EAE D40, several mitochondrial genes were significantly downregulated, including members of the NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase family, such as Ndufa6, which is essential for COXI activity and assembly (56). Similar gene downregulation (Ndufa6, Ndufb8) has been observed in the MS motor cortex (23). In EAE cerebellum, reductions were also noted in cytochrome c oxidase genes (Cox4i1, Cox5a, Cox5b, Cox6a1, Cox7c) and ATP synthase genes (Atp5k, Atp6v1f), mirroring changes in the MS motor cortex (23, 33). However, these mitochondrial alterations are complex and influenced by the diverse cellular environment of the cerebellum, including astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons. The interplay of astrogliosis, microgliosis, neurodegeneration, and immune infiltration in EAE and MS suggests that mitochondrial changes occur in a cell-specific manner. Future research will investigate early MS (RRMS) to assess initial PC responses and compare findings to SPMS. Additionally, region-specific mitochondrial studies using brain slice punches and Seahorse XFp analysis (57), as well as cell-specific investigations with Mitotag transgenic models (58), will be crucial for understanding mitochondrial dynamics in demyelinating diseases.

In the current study, we highlight MS cerebellar pathology in a new largely SPMS population that have minimal postmortem autolysis duration controlled by age, sex, and EDSS scores. Here, we focused on longitudinal EAE-induced cerebellar pathology with a focus on mitochondria pathology, function, and morphology and their relationship to PC health. The EDSS is the most used tool to assess disability in MS, ranging from 0 to 10 in 0.5-unit increments that indicate increasing levels of disability, based on a subjective examination by a neurologist. EDSS scores from 1.0 to 4.5 apply to individuals with MS who can walk without assistance. The postmortem cerebellar brain sections analyzed in our study had EDSS scores between 6 and 9, indicating a loss of ambulatory ability.

There are no behavioral tests that exclusively assess cerebellar function without spinal cord and motor cortex involvement. EAE mice underwent gait testing (36), which may reflect cerebellar dysfunction alongside spinal cord and motor cortex impairments. Mice with an average EAE score of 2.5 showed decreased stride length early in disease progression, persisting through EAE D60 coinciding with cerebellar demyelination, inflammation, and neurodegeneration.

Our findings indicate that cerebellar pathology in MS may result from mitochondrial dysfunction, which, alongside significant demyelination, contributes to the loss of myelinated PC axons, nonmyelinated parallel fibers, increased inflammation, and PC loss (Fig. 8). To further investigate these changes, we examined the EAE model’s potential for studying cerebellar pathology and disease progression in MS. While EAE does not fully replicate the complexity of MS, its pathological similarities, and comparable mitochondrial alterations to those observed in the MS cerebellum suggest that it serves as a valuable model. When combined with postmortem MS brain tissue analysis, EAE can help advance our understanding of MS and other neurodegenerative diseases.
