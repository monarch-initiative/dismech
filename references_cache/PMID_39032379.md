# Genotype-Phenotype Correlations and Sex Differences in ZC4H2-Associated Rare Disorder.
**Authors:** Peters S, Sportiello K, Mandalapu S, Nguyen A, Carrier R, Dickinson C, Paciorkowski A, Bearden D
**Journal:** Pediatr Neurol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.pediatrneurol.2024.06.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pediatrneurol.2024.06.009)

## Content

BACKGROUND: ZC4H2-associated rare disorder (ZARD) is caused by pathogenic variations in the ZC4H2 gene on the X chromosome. This gene codes for a zinc finger protein involved in neural development. ZARD is characterized by highly variable symptoms, potentially influenced by the sex of the individual.

METHODS: The ZC4H2-Associated Rare Disorder Natural History Study is a prospective natural history study conducted among individuals with ZARD that consists of standardized interviews, developmental assessments, and neurological examinations conducted every six months for two years. In this article, we present data from baseline visits with 40 participants, the largest ZARD cohort studied thus far, focusing on genotype-phenotype correlations and sex differences. Fisher exact, maximum likelihood χ2, and Mann-Whitney tests were utilized.

RESULTS: Males tended to have maternally inherited ZC4H2 pathogenic variations, whereas females tended to have de novo variations (P < 0.001). Female participants were more likely to have contractures at birth (P < 0.01), arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (P < 0.001), spasticity on examination (P < 0.1), and lower limb muscle atrophy (P < 0.05). Male participants were more likely to have seizures (P < 0.1), intermittent pain (P < 0.01), severe vision impairment (P < 0.05), dysphagia for solids (P < 0.01), and generalized muscle atrophy (P < 0.05).

CONCLUSIONS: Our study suggests there is significant overlap in severity and range of symptoms between males and females, although several symptoms are more common in one sex than the other. Further analysis is needed to better understand how pathogenic variation type affects phenotype.

ZC4H2-Associated Rare Disorder (ZARD), also known as Wieacker-Wolff syndrome and Miles-Carpenter syndrome, is a rare condition resulting from pathogenic variations in the ZC4H2 gene. ZARD has typically been described as an arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (AMC) disorder with global developmental delays. AMC is characterized by congenital joint contractures and reduced fetal movement.1 While there are many similarities among people with ZARD, the condition is associated with highly variable phenotypic expression. A variety of neurological, ophthalmic, craniofacial, respiratory, musculoskeletal, and endocrine symptoms have been described in this condition.2–8 While on average males tend to have more severe clinical presentations, female patients with loss-of-function ZC4H2 sequence variations can present with severity similar to that of males.3,9,10

ZC4H2, located on the long arm of the X chromosome, encodes a protein that is expressed in the fetal brain, spinal cord, and other tissues.2,11 The protein contains a zinc-finger domain with four cysteine residues and two histidine residues, as well as a coiled-coil domain. Prior research on mice and zebrafish suggests an important role in nervous system development. Mouse hippocampal neurons with altered Zc4h2 function have a lower density of dendritic spines than wild-type neurons.2 Zebrafish with knockout zc4h2 have abnormal movements and decreased numbers of GABAergic interneurons in the midbrain and V2 interneurons in the spinal cord.11

The ways in which ZC4H2 protein influences neurodevelopment are not fully clear, but prior studies suggest that ZC4H2 acts as a stabilizer of RNF220, an E3 ubiquitin ligase involved in neural patterning via the Sonic hedgehog (SHH) signaling pathway.12 When stabilized and in association with RNF220, ZC4H2 plays a role in targeting progenitor factors for degradation and enables full activation of SHH signaling and therefore proper neural patterning.12,13 ZC4H2 also stabilizes Smad proteins, shown to enhance bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling and neural patterning in Xenopus amphibians and stem cell differentiation in mammalian cells.14 In addition, loss of function of ZC4H2 results in aberrant development of spinal motor neurons as well as disorganized neuromuscular junction endplates, potentially contributing to the arthrogryposis seen in this disorder.2 Furthermore, ZC4H2 appears to be necessary for the development of central noradrenergic neurons in the locus coeruleus of mice.15 This noradrenergic system has important roles in cognition, attention, mood, homeostasis, and other higher brain functions.16 It has important functions outside of the nervous system as well, as it positively modulates TRPV4, a mechanosensitive channel involved in vascular endothelial function, bone density, and bladder function.17

While previous studies, including two case series and several case reports, have highlighted the broad range of symptom presentations of ZARD, there has not yet been a study that follows the natural history of the disorder. The goal of this natural history study is to provide information to clinicians and families about the characteristics, prognosis, and management of ZARD. To our knowledge, this study involves the first prospective cohort description of ZARD and the largest cohort studied thus far. We are particularly interested in learning more about potential genotype-phenotype differences between sexes, common comorbidities, effects of various treatments, and how symptoms change over time. This paper will focus on presenting features, genotype-phenotype correlations, and phenotypic differences between male and female participants.

This is a nonrandomized, nondrug, and single-center study at the University of Rochester Medical Center. Participants with ZARD are enrolled with follow-up approximately every six months for two years. We have completed the baseline visit and preceding screening and enrollment visit for the 40 participants (13 males and 27 females) currently enrolled. Sex was defined as the sex defined at birth. The baseline visits took place from December 2022 to December 2023. The supplementary table S1 details the elements of the completed visits. Data were collected and managed using REDCap.18,19 All visits with participants and their families occurred via televideo except visits for two participants who were seen in person at the University of Rochester Medical Center. Two of the children included in this study were deceased at the time of study enrollment, so the parents of these children took part in only the interview portions of the visit protocol and provided medical records.

An electronic consent form was provided to participants and each participant’s legally-authorized representative (LAR) for review prior to enrollment. For each of our participants, the LAR, who provided consent, was a parent. The screening and enrollment visit allowed any questions to be answered and involved a “talk-back” approach to ensure that the purpose and risks of the study were well-understood. During the screening and enrollment visit, we also requested copies of the child’s genetic testing results and a recent note from a neurologist or primary care physician. Once baseline visits began, primary data was provided by each participant’s parent/caregiver via a structured interview on Zoom. Additional information was requested from the caregiver after the baseline visit. This information included imaging reports, EEG and/or EMG reports, current medications, and appointment notes from specialists.

Known patients with ZARD were informed of the study and invited to participate. The ZC4H2 Foundation also assisted with referring patients to us, and information was posted on the ZC4H2 Foundation website (www.zc4h2foundation.org) as well as a private Facebook support group dedicated to families with ZARD. Patients were recruited remotely only if they reached out to the principal investigator and expressed interest in the study.

Individuals were eligible for participation if the following criteria were met: 1) the participant must be aged 0–21 years at enrollment, 2) the participant must have a confirmed pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in the ZC4H2 gene as determined by the PI, 3) their legally authorized representative (LAR) must be able to understand the elements of the informed consent process, and 4) the LAR must be willing to comply with the scheduled visits and study procedures. Exclusion criteria included language proficiency below the level needed to understand and complete the consent process and study procedures, as well as any condition that, in the judgment of the principal investigator, may interfere with the assessment of ZARD and that is clearly not related to their ZARD diagnosis. Each patient whose family contacted us was found to be eligible for participation, with the exception of three patients for whom we were not able to receive genetic information. All patients approached for participation agreed to participate in the study. One patient withdrew from the study after further genetic testing revealed that his symptoms are likely not due to the ZC4H2 variation; this patient is not included in the data below. Four of the female participants have had X-inactivation studies done.

Interviews with parents, genetic testing reports, and physical examinations were utilized. In the “Results” section below, as well as in Tables 3–5, we specify the source of the particular data described. Interviews were conducted in English, Spanish, or Portuguese according to family preference by a child neurologist fluent in that language.

Descriptive statistics are provided for each group. Our statistical analysis was conducted using the Stata/SE 18.0 software by StataCorp. The two study groups (male and female participants) are of unequal size.20 For nominal data, we opted to use the Fisher’s Exact test for all 2×2 designs and the maximum likelihood ratio χ2 test for all other designs (i.e., where choices included yes, no, and unknown) in order to avoid Type I and Type II errors due to small sample size.20,21 The p-value used to determine significance was the Fisher’s exact value or, for maximum-likelihood ratio χ2 tests, the probability ratio. For all of the nominal data, we calculated Cramér’s V to provide a measure of the correlation strength.22 The two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann-Whitney) test was utilized for ordinal ranked data that is not normally distributed, therefore comparing the median instead of the mean. Due to the small sample size and therefore low power, a p-value equal to or less than 0.1 was considered significant.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Rochester (approved on 12/12/2022; reference number 7737). All procedures complied with relevant laws and institutional guidelines. Verbal and written informed consent were required from the parents (legally-authorized representatives) of all participants in order to enroll in the study.

A total of 40 participants (27 females and 13 males) from ten countries were enrolled, including two deceased participants with information provided by their parents. The youngest participant was enrolled on the first day of life, while the oldest was 21 years old. Table 1 provides demographic information of the participants. Table 2 describes the genetic information, presenting symptoms, and selected features of each participant, grouped by variation type. Figure 1 provides the locations of participants’ missense, nonsense, and frameshift variations on the ZC4H2 gene.

In tables 3–5, the total n and percentages shown are with regard to the total number of participants who answered each question or had available information. Tables 3a and 3b provide genetic and family information stratified by sex. Sixty-one percent of male participants had maternally inherited pathogenic sequence variations in the ZC4H2 gene, while the majority of female participants (70.37%) had de novo pathogenic sequence variations (Cramér’s V = 0.61, maximum likelihood ratio χ2 = 15.87, probability ratio = 0.001****). In addition, it was more common for male participants to have affected family members with the same ZC4H2 variant (58.33% of males vs. 11.54% of females, Cramér’s V = −0.49, Fisher’s exact = 0.005***). Females more commonly had deletions, frameshift, or nonsense variations, whereas males were more likely to have missense variations (see table 3b). Because males and females presented with different variation types, overall genotype-phenotype correlations were challenging to determine. However, we noted that some of the mildest phenotypes were seen in girls with missense variations and splice site variations (see table 2), while the single boy with a complete gene deletion was severely affected and died shortly after birth. As shown in table 2, girls with deletions in the ZC4H2 gene tended to have a more severe motor phenotype with variable cognitive function, while severe cognitive impairment was seen mostly in boys with missense variations. Almost all patients were noted to have a significant gap between expressive and receptive language skills, with the majority of participants having less impairment in receptive language than expressive speech (82% of participants of each sex).

Of the four female participants who have had X-inactivation testing, participant 21 (number assigned in table 2) had a random X-inactivation pattern (64:36 ratio) while the other three had a skewed X-inactivation pattern. Of the three girls with a skewed X-inactivation pattern, participant 23 had a 100:0 ratio, participant 18 had a 17:83 ratio, and participant 24 had an unspecified ratio. Comparing these participants using the data in table 2 does not elicit any clear association between X-inactivation patterns and disease severity.

Table 4, with information gathered from parents, describes sex differences with regard to the prevalence of various ZARD symptoms and comorbidities. We found that female participants were significantly more likely to have contractures at birth (96.3% of females vs. 53.85% of males, Cramér’s V = 0.52, Fisher’s exact = 0.003***) and a diagnosis of arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (92.59% of females vs. 41.67% of males, Cramér’s V = 0.56, Fisher’s exact = 0.001****). Male participants, on the other hand, were statistically more likely to experience seizures (33.33% of males vs. 22.22% of females, Cramér’s V = 0.39, maximum likelihood ratio χ2 = 5.79, probability ratio = 0.06*), dysphagia for solids (75% of males vs. 29.63% of females, Cramér’s V = −0.42, Fisher’s exact = 0.01***), difficulty with pain or irritability (83.33% of males vs. 48.15% of females, Cramér’s V = 0.33, Fisher’s exact = 0.08*), and, more specifically, intermittent periods of pain (75% of males vs. 29.63% of females, Cramér’s V = −0.42, Fisher’s exact = 0.01***).

Table 5 describes sex differences in ZARD symptom prevalence based on the physician’s examination. While there was no sex difference regarding the prevalence of abnormal muscle bulk, there were sex differences regarding the specific distributions of muscle atrophy. Isolated lower limb muscle atrophy was more common in females (61.54% of females vs. 18.18% of males, Cramér’s V = −0.40, Fisher’s exact = 0.03**), while a generalized distribution of muscle atrophy was more common in males (54.55% of males vs. 15.38% of females, Cramér’s V = 0.40, Fisher’s exact = 0.04**). Males were more likely to be blind or have severe visual impairment (25% of males vs. 0% of females, Cramér’s V = −0.43; Fisher’s exact = 0.03**). Spasticity was more frequently seen in females (100% of females vs. 81.82% of males, Cramér’s V = 0.37; Fisher’s exact = 0.09*).

Table 6 includes the Mann-Whitney test results for motor, cognitive, and language functions (as reported by parents) of participants. Girls had better expressive speech than boys (Z = 2.682, p-value = 0.007***), although when other means of expressive language were included, such as signing or the use of communication devices, there was not a significant difference in expressive language function between the two sexes. Receptive language and cognitive function were not significantly different between males and females, nor was gross motor or fine motor function.

Of the 40 participants, 37 had brain MRIs performed. The most common brain MRI findings were white matter abnormalities (in 12 participants) and global atrophy (in 7 participants). Neither of these findings was associated with motor function or cognitive function. Spine MRI was performed for 24/40 participants. The most common finding by far was tethered cord or imaging features suggestive of tethered cord. Only three participants for whom spine MRI was performed did not have tethered cord or imaging findings suggestive of tethered cord. There was not an association between tethered cord/imaging features suggestive of tethered cord and reduced motor function.

Consistent with previous findings, we found that male participants were statistically more likely to have maternally inherited pathogenic variations, while de novo pathogenic variations were statistically more common in female participants.11 This is consistent with our additional findings that male participants were more likely to have family members with the same ZC4H2 variant as well as family members with similar symptoms. Unexpectedly, we found that female participants were more likely to have contractures at birth, a diagnosis of arthrogryposis multiplex congenita, spasticity on exam, and lower limb muscle atrophy, while males were more likely to experience seizures, intermittent periods of pain, severe vision impairment, dysphagia for solids, and a generalized distribution of muscle atrophy. Of note, a phenotype characterized by much greater impairment in expressive speech than receptive language was extremely common in both males and females.

An analysis by Zanzottera et al. (2017) suggests that many female carriers of a ZC4H2 variation have an X-inactivation pattern that preferentially inactivates the affected X chromosome. That being said, many studies have not found a correlation between X-inactivation patterns and phenotypic severity.4,10,11,23 Our study did not detect this correlation, but only four participants in this study had X-inactivation studies done, which strongly limited our power to detect any association.

Due to the challenges of recruiting sufficient numbers of patients with ZARD at a single center, this study was conducted almost entirely via televideo visits with parents of individuals affected by ZARD, supplemented by medical record review. While this choice provided access to participants around the world, it potentially limited the depth and accuracy of the physical and neurological examinations. We tried to decrease the chance of this by instructing the parents on maneuvers and positions that help with the examination, as well as by focusing on aspects that we were confident we could reliably assess over televideo.

Another limitation of this study was the relatively small number of participants representing certain imaging findings on brain/spine MRI, which reduced our power to detect associations between imaging findings and cognitive or motor function. In addition, recruitment through the Facebook support group for ZARD and the webpage of the ZC4H2 Foundation likely did create selection bias regarding who enrolled in the study. For example, mildly symptomatic or asymptomatic patients with ZC4H2 variants might be less likely to be involved in the patient community. Conversely, severely affected patients might not participate due to the burden of illness. In addition, patients without computer or internet access would not have been able to participate in the study, and the Facebook group was English-speaking, which limited our ability to recruit families who did not speak English.

Another limitation of this study was the disproportionately small number of males enrolled, which may have limited our ability to explore the full spectrum of the ZARD phenotype in males. Nevertheless, we think that results of the current study are likely generalizable to the wider ZARD population.

Previous studies found that male patients with ZARD tend to have more severe clinical presentations but that female patients with loss-of-function variations can present with severity similar to that of males.10,16,17 In contrast, our study suggests, without taking variation type into consideration, that female and male children with ZARD experience a similar severity and variety of symptoms. While our study found some significant sex-based differences in symptom prevalence, the vast majority of the features included in our study did not differ significantly between males and females. As the ZARD natural history study continues, we will evaluate developmental and symptom trajectories over time and further evaluate optimal treatments for children living with ZARD. Given the level of impairment in gross motor function and expressive speech noted in this population, interventions to improve communication, treat spasticity, and optimize motor function are needed.
