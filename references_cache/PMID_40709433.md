Cronkhite-Canada syndrome (CCS) is a rare, non-hereditary disorder characterized 
by diffuse, non-neoplastic gastrointestinal polyposis along with ectodermal 
abnormalities, such as alopecia, skin hyperpigmentation, and nail dystrophy. It 
typically affects individuals aged >50 years and can lead to severe 
complications, including protein-losing enteropathy, chronic diarrhea, and 
malnutrition, if not diagnosed early. We report the case of a 68-year-old male 
who presented with a 2-month history of diarrhea, weight loss, and hair loss. 
Endoscopic and histopathological examinations revealed diffuse polyposis 
throughout the gastrointestinal tract, consistent with CCS findings. The patient 
was treated with a combination of corticosteroids and azathioprine, which led to 
marked clinical and endoscopic improvements. No recurrence was observed during 1 
year of follow-up period. This case highlights the importance of recognizing the 
typical clinical and diagnostic features of CCS. Owing to its nonspecific 
presentation, diagnosis may be delayed, potentially worsening the prognosis. 
Therefore, CCS should be considered in the differential diagnosis of middle-aged 
or older patients who present with multiple gastrointestinal polyps accompanied 
by ectodermal symptoms. Early recognition and appropriate immunosuppressive 
therapy are essential for improving patient outcomes and preventing 
complications. Long-term surveillance is necessary because of the potential for 
polyp recurrence or malignant transformation.

Cronkhite-Canada syndrome (CCS) is a rare, non-hereditary disorder characterized by diffuse gastrointestinal polyposis accompanied by chronic diarrhea; hypoproteinemia; and ectodermal abnormalities, such as skin hyperpigmentation, alopecia, and nail dystrophy.1-4 CCS primarily affects middle-aged and older adults, typically aged >50 years, with a slightly higher prevalence in males than in females.5 The disease involves both the upper and lower gastrointestinal tracts; however, esophageal involvement is uncommon.6

CCS was first described in 1955, and the term “Cronkhite-Canada syndrome” was later coined by Jarnum and Jensen in 1966.7,8 To date, approximately 500 cases have been reported worldwide, with approximately 75% originating in Japan.9 The etiology and pathogenesis remain unclear, but hypotheses include autoimmune dysregulation, malabsorption, and infectious triggers.10

Diagnosis is based on a combination of clinical history, physical examination, endoscopic findings, and histopathological evaluation.11,12 The presence of multiple gastrointestinal polyps along with characteristic ectodermal changes provides important diagnostic clues. However, owing to its nonspecific clinical presentation, diagnosis is often delayed, which contributes to a poor prognosis. The 5-year mortality rate is reported to be approximately 55%.10,13

Currently, there are no established standard treatments for CCS. Management primarily involves supportive care with high-protein nutritional supplementation and glucocorticoids to control gastrointestinal inflammation.14,15 Recently, combination therapies involving immunosuppressants and biological agents have shown promising results in several case reports.16

In this report, we present the case of a 68-year-old male who was diagnosed with CCS based on clinical, serological, endoscopic, and histopathological findings. We describe the patient’s clinical course of treatment and favorable response over 1 year. Through this case and literature review, we aimed to enhance the clinical awareness of CCS and provide insights into its diagnosis, management, and prognosis.

A 68-year-old male presented with a 2-month history of diarrhea, weight loss, and hair loss.

On presentation, the patient had a slender physique, with a height of 170.0 cm and weight of 67.2 kg. His vital signs were stable: blood pressure, 120/90 mmHg; pulse rate, 80 bpm; and body temperature and respiratory rate within the normal range. Physical examination revealed onycholysis and extensive hyperpigmentation of hands and feet. Generalized alopecia included scalp and eyebrows (Fig. 1).

Serological testing revealed hypoalbuminemia with normal inflammatory markers: Hemoglobin, 15.0 g/dL; total protein, 4.8 g/dL; albumin, 3.0 g/dL; C-reactive protein, 0.45 mg/dL; erythrocyte sedimentation rate, 2 mm/h. Tumor markers were within the normal ranges: carcinoembryonic antigen, 2.2 ng/mL; carbohydrate antigen 19-9, 10.3 U/mL. Autoantibody results were as follows: antinuclear antibodies, 1:40; antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, negative; and anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae antibodies (IgA/IgG), negative/positive. Tests for infectious causes, including Interferon-Gamma Release Assay; tests for human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis A virus, hepatitis B virus, and hepatitis C virus; and Venereal Disease Research Laboratory test, were negative. Fecal calprotectin was markedly elevated at 1,254 mg/kg, whereas stool culture, ova, and occult blood tests were negative.

Abdominal computed tomography revealed several hepatic cysts and prostate enlargement but no other specific findings.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed diffuse erythematous nodular lesions throughout the stomach and multiple erythematous, sessile polyps with smooth surfaces along the greater curvature of the gastric body. The duodenum also showed multiple nodular erythematous polyps in the bulb and second portion. The esophagus showed unremarkable findings (Fig. 2).

Colonoscopy revealed multiple erythematous, elevated mucosal lesions throughout the colon, with smooth-surfaced polyps of various sizes. Some had a partial strawberry-like appearance (Fig. 3).

The histopathological examination revealed no evidence of cellular dysplasia or malignancy. Biopsies of the stomach and duodenum specimens revealed chronic inflammation. Colonic biopsy revealed mucosal thickening with scattered, elongated crypts. Although the number of crypts did not increase, infiltration of inflammatory cells, including eosinophils and plasma cells, was observed. These findings were consistent with those of hyperplastic colonic polyps accompanied by cystically dilated glands and inflamed, edematous stroma.

Additional biopsies of the sigmoid colon and rectum specimens revealed chronic colitis with stromal edema and chronic inflammation with erosion, respectively. The eosinophil count was <35 per high-power field in the sigmoid colon and <10/high-power field in the rectum (Fig. 4).

Infectious causes, including tuberculosis and viral and bacterial infections, were excluded. Based on the clinical presentation and laboratory, endoscopic, and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of CCS was established.

Treatment with prednisolone (30 mg/day) was initiated, followed by a tapering regimen and addition of azathioprine for maintenance immunosuppressive therapy.

The patient showed gradual improvement in diarrhea and alopecia, with restoration of appetite (Fig. 5). After 4 months, follow-up endoscopy revealed a decrease in polyp size and number. At approximately 1 year, upper and lower endoscopies confirmed marked improvement, with significantly fewer and smaller polyps than before. Capsule endoscopy did not reveal any remarkable findings (Figs. 6–8). Three polyps were resected from the ascending colon by endoscopic mucosal resection and polypectomy. Pathologic evaluation revealed hyperplastic polyps with cystically dilated glands and eosinophil-rich stroma, consistent with previous findings. In two of the three polyps, focal low-grade dysplasia was identified (Fig. 9).

The prednisolone dose was tapered to 5 mg per week, and eventually, it was discontinued. The azathioprine dose was increased from 25 mg/day to 75 mg/day. Mesalazine (2 g/day) and lansoprazole (15 mg/day) were added as maintenance therapy. The patient remains under regular outpatient follow-up.

This case represents a classic presentation of CCS with characteristic clinical, laboratory, endoscopic, and histopathological findings. The patient responded favorably to a combination of corticosteroid and azathioprine therapy.

CCS remains a rare and poorly understood condition, with approximately 500 cases reported worldwide. More than 80% of cases occur in individuals aged >50 years, and a male predominance has been noted. The majority of known cases have been reported in Japan.10 Its etiology remains unclear, although nutritional deficiencies, mucosal barrier dysfunction, autoimmunity, and infection have been proposed.10 Supporting the autoimmune hypothesis, anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae antibody (ASCA)―a marker commonly associated with Crohn’s disease―has been reported to be positive in some CCS patients. Previous studies have identified ASCA IgG positivity in a subset of cases, suggesting a possible immune- mediated mechanism.17

Diagnosis relies on the integration of clinical symptoms with endoscopic and histologic evaluations.11 Its hallmark features include widespread polyposis of the gastrointestinal tract, alopecia, hyperpigmentation, nail dystrophy, diarrhea, weight loss, and malnutrition. Polyps are typically found throughout the stomach and colon and, in approximately 50% of cases, in the small intestine (particularly the duodenum and terminal ileum), whereas esophageal involvement is rare.

Owing to complications such as malabsorption, gastrointestinal bleeding, and recurrent infections, the reported 5-year mortality rate is as high as 55%.10

Currently, there are no universally accepted treatments for CCS. Therapeutic strategies described in previous reports include nutritional support, antibiotics, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, cromolyn sodium, and H. pylori eradication therapy.14 In addition, azathioprine has been reported in previous literature as a useful steroid-sparing agent for maintaining remission and preventing relapse, particularly during tapering of corticosteroids.14 In our case, azathioprine was introduced early in the course of treatment to allow for timely steroid tapering and reduce the risk of relapse, based on the increasing evidence supporting its role in long-term disease control. The patient tolerated azathioprine without any adverse events; therefore, NUDT15/TPMT testing was not deemed necessary. Recently, favorable outcomes have been achieved with biological agents, such as infliximab and cyclosporine.16,18,19

Owing to its nonspecific symptoms, the diagnosis of CCS can be delayed.20 However, CCS should be considered as a differential diagnosis of middle-aged and older patients who present with multiple gastrointestinal polyps and ectodermal changes. Early recognition and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy are essential for improving the prognosis. Long-term follow-up is necessary to monitor the recurrence and potential malignant transformation of polyps. Therefore, regular endoscopic surveillance should be considered to enable early detection of dysplasia or malignancy, as recommended in previous long-term outcome studies.4,21
