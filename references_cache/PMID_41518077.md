# CRISPR-Cas9-Generated TXNDC15 c.560delA Homozygous Mouse Model Exhibits Meckel-Gruber Syndrome Phenotype.

To determine whether TXNDC15 variation causes Meckel-Gruber syndrome (MKS), we 
assessed the pathogenicity of the frameshift variant c.560delA. A CRISPR-Cas9 
generated mouse model carrying the equivalent Txndc15 c.512delA mutation was 
analyzed at embryonic day 15.5. Homozygous Txndc15mt/mt embryos displayed the 
complete MKS phenotype-fetal lethality, exencephaly, omphalocele, post-axial 
polydactyly, and polycystic kidneys-together with markedly reduced TXNDC15 
protein in brain, liver, and kidney. These findings confirm TXNDC15 as a bona 
fide MKS disease gene.

Our institutional series includes a non‐consanguineous couple with four consecutive pregnancies complicated by Meckel–Gruber syndrome (MKS) (Deng and Xie 2024; Xu et al. 2024). The 36‐year‐old gravida 4, para 0, abortus 3 woman had no living children. 2014: spontaneous abortion at 5 weeks; no karyotype obtained. 2015: detailed ultrasound at 22 weeks disclosed occipital encephalocele, bilateral post‐axial polydactyly of the feet, and bilateral polycystic kidneys; pregnancy terminated without genetic analysis. 2017: spontaneous abortion at 7 weeks; no karyotype performed. 2021: first‐trimester scan at 12 weeks revealed NT 4.0 mm, 5 mm encephalocele, 7 × 4 mm omphalocele, and markedly enlarged cystic kidneys (left 11 × 7 mm, right 12 × 6 mm) containing multiple anechoic cysts—classic for MKS.

Trio‐exome sequencing of chorionic villi identified a novel homozygous single‐adenine deletion in TXNDC15 (NM_024715.3: c.560delA, p.Asn187IlefsTer4) inherited from both heterozygous parents. The frameshift introduces a premature termination codon predicted to elicit nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay and abolish protein function (Lindeboom et al. 2019). The constellation of encephalocele, omphalocele, polycystic kidneys, and post‐axial polydactyly in two separate fetuses confirms the MKS phenotype and establishes biallelic TXNDC15 loss as its cause. To date, the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database has confirmed that both homozygous and compound heterozygous mutations in the TXNDC15 gene (OMIM 617778) are responsible for causing MKS subtype 14 (MKS14; OMIM 619879). The homozygous TXNDC15 c.560delA (p.Asn187IlefsTer4) variant fully segregates with the classical Meckel–Gruber phenotype observed in this fetus and is therefore classified as the definitive pathogenic lesion underlying the recurrent fetal disease in this family.

MKS is a lethal autosomal‐recessive ciliopathy characterized by profound developmental defects and an estimated worldwide incidence of 1:135,000 live births (Auber et al. 2007). It is defined by a triad of occipital encephalocele (or other posterior‐fossa malformations), bilateral cystic renal dysplasia with markedly enlarged kidneys, and hepatic developmental defects manifested by ductal‐plate malformation, periportal fibrosis, and biliary cysts (Salonen and Paavola 1998). A frequent ancillary finding is bilateral postaxial polydactyly, reported in 70%–80% of cases (Fraser and Lytwyn 1981; Hsia et al. 1971; Majewski et al. 1983; Salonen 1984). Less common anomalies, encountered in 25%–40% of fetuses, comprise long‐bone bowing and shortening, male genital malformations, micro‐ or anencephaly, cleft lip/palate and other craniofacial defects, congenital heart disease, pulmonary hypoplasia, and midline defects such as omphalocele (Fraser and Lytwyn 1981; Majewski et al. 1983; Salonen 1984; Salonen and Paavola 1998). Truly rare features (< 20%) include cystic dysplasia of the lung or thyroid, retinal dysplasia, and assorted visceral malformations (Lowry et al. 1983; Majewski et al. 1983; Moerman et al. 1982; Paavola et al. 1995). Central nervous system involvement is an obligate component of MKS and manifests variably as complete craniorachischisis, partial agenesis of the corpus callosum, Dandy–Walker malformation (Przylepa et al. 1996), or most frequently—occipital encephalocele (Ahdab‐Barmada and Claassen 1990).

MKS results from mutations in genes encoding primary ciliary proteins. To date, 14 genes—MKS1, TMEM216, TMEM67, CEP290, RPGRIP1L, CC2D2A, NPHP3, TCTN2, B9D1, B9D2, TMEM231, KIF14, TMEM107, and CSPP1—have been definitively implicated in MKS (Teebi and Teebi 2005). Pathogenic variants in three additional genes (C5ORF42 (Shaheen et al. 2013), CSPP1 (Shaheen et al. 2014), and CEP55 (Bondeson et al. 2017)), have been associated with either classic MKS or MKS‐like phenotypes, while variants in SEC8 (also known as EXOC4) (Shaheen et al. 2013) and EXOC3L2 (Shaheen et al. 2016) are considered potential, albeit less well‐established, causes of MKS‐related presentations.


TXNDC15 is a less‐characterized member of the thioredoxin (TRX)‐like protein family. It is a type I transmembrane (TM) protein composed of 360 amino acids, featuring an N‐terminal luminal or extracellular region containing a TRX‐like domain with a putative, atypical Cys–Arg–Phe–Ser (CRFS) active site, a TM domain, and a short C‐terminal cytoplasmic tail. TXNDC15 encodes protein disulfide isomerase 15, which contains a thioredoxin domain and plays a role in ciliogenesis, primarily localizing to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Yamazaki et al. 2024). TXNDC15 was initially identified in a CRISPR‐based screen for Hedgehog (Hh) signaling defects (Breslow et al. 2018). TXNDC15 has been implicated in only five additional, unrelated Meckel–Gruber kindreds beyond the family described here, beginning with the initial report that first proposed TXNDC15 as a candidate disease gene for MKS (Radhakrishnan et al. 2019; Ridnõi et al. 2019; Shaheen et al. 2016). Previous studies have demonstrated that pathogenic TXNDC15 variants disrupt the proper localization of the ciliary receptor TMEM67 to the transition zone, resulting in a reduction of ciliated cells and aberrant ciliary morphology (Shaheen et al. 2016). Breslow et al. further showed that Hedgehog (Hh) signaling is markedly impaired in Txndc15‐knockout mouse cells (Breslow et al. 2018). Collectively, these findings underscore the essential role of TXNDC15 in transition‐zone assembly and in the targeting of ciliary membrane proteins, and suggest that its influence on ciliary function may be mediated indirectly via the ER (Yamazaki et al. 2024).

Despite the above insights, no animal model of TXNDC15‐associated MKS has yet been described. The gene is absent from ClinGen's curated gene–disease list and lacks corroborating entries in the Human Gene Mutation Database, gnomAD, the 1000 Genomes Project, and other population or disease repositories. Consequently, the contribution of TXNDC15 to MKS remains to be formally established.

Using CRISPR–Cas9, we generated a mouse line homozygous for the human‐relevant TXNDC15 c.560delA variant and determined whether this frameshift mutation is sufficient to recapitulate MKS. Biochemical analyses were integrated to quantify the impact of the deletion on TXNDC15 protein expression and stability, thereby providing definitive evidence for its pathogenicity and clarifying the molecular basis of disease in the index family.

Mice were maintained in a specific‐pathogen‐free facility (22°C ± 2°C, 40%–70% relative humidity) on a 12‐h light/dark cycle. Experimental animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation under protocols approved by the Southern Medical University Animal Ethics Committee (Guangzhou, China) (the animals will be made available to the research community upon request, subject to ethical approval and availability).

The mouse Txndc15 locus on chromosome 13 contains five exons; the ATG initiator is in exon 1 and the TAA terminator in exon 5. Mouse and human TXNDC15 share > 95% amino‐acid identity. The patient variant c.560delA (p.Asn187Ilefs4; NM_024715.4) corresponds to c.512delA (p.Asn171Thrfs4) in the Txndc15‐201 transcript (ENSMUST00000021959; NM_175150.3), which was therefore selected for modeling. On a C57BL/6J background, the c.512delA mutation was introduced by CRISPR–Cas9‐mediated homology‐directed repair. A 1.5‐kb single‐stranded oligodeoxynucleotide donor was designed with CRISPOR (crispor.tefor.net). Guide RNAs: gRNA‐A1: AAGGGAGATGGACTAAGAGC‐GGG; gRNA‐A2: GCACCTCATAATATTAAGCG‐TGG; gRNA‐B1: TGGACTAAGAGCGG GTGCCC‐AGG; gRNA‐B2: CTACCACGCTTAATATTATG‐AGG.

To establish the Txndc15 c.512delA mouse line, we assembled a CRISPR–Cas9 plasmid encoding gene‐specific gRNAs and Cas9, linearized the vector, and micro‐injected it into C57BL/6J zygotes. F0 founders carrying the heterozygous Txndc15 c.512delA allele were back‐crossed to wild‐type C57BL/6J mice for three generations to dilute potential off‐target effects and stabilize the genetic background. Heterozygous F3 progeny were then inter‐crossed to generate homozygous Txndc15 c.512delA/c.512delA animals (F4). All procedures were performed by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Guangzhou).

Embryos were harvested from pregnant mice at embryonic day (E) 15.5, rinsed in ice‐cold PBS, and examined under a stereomicroscope; morphology and developmental stage were documented in detail. Where required, the brain, liver, and kidneys were micro‐dissected, transferred to the appropriate culture medium, and processed immediately or snap‐frozen for downstream analyses.

Tail biopsies were collected from the embryos for genotyping. The primer sequences are as follows: F: 5′‐TGGTGGTGCTGTCAGT‐3′; R: 5′‐CCATCCTCACGGAGTAAC‐3′.

Brain, liver, and kidney tissues were harvested from mouse embryos. The tissue samples were washed 2–3 times with pre‐cooled PBS, lysed in lysis buffer on ice by centrifugation at 4°C for 13 min. The protein extracts were resolved by 8%–15% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were probed with primary antibodies, followed by peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐mouse or anti‐rabbit secondary antibodies (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). The antigen–antibody reaction was visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence assay. Quantitative analysis of the Western blot results was performed using ImageJ software. Antibodies used: TXNDC15 (Novus, NBP1‐69614); β‐actin (Tiandeyue, TDY051).

The dissected organs were fixed in 4% formaldehyde overnight at room temperature and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Sections with a thickness of 4 μm were prepared for immunofluorescence (IF) and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The samples were heated at 60°C for 4 h, followed by deparaffinization through three 10‐min extractions in 100% xylene. This was succeeded by rehydration in descending concentrations of alcohol (100%, 95%, 80%, and 70%) for 5 min each in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). H&E staining was carried out on the paraffin sections following standard protocols.

Previous research on the TXNDC15 gene has predominantly focused on case reports involving families with MKS. Excluding the cases presented in this study, the gene has been reported in three publications involving five MKS families. Shaheen et al. (2016) reported a family with a homozygous deletion variant c.672_686del in the TXNDC15 gene. The fetus primarily presented with encephalocele, polydactyly, and polycystic kidneys. The second family had a homozygous variant c.103+1G>A, involving a newborn who died shortly after birth. The manifestations included encephalocele, postaxial polydactyly and syndactyly, polycystic kidneys, low‐set ears, micrognathia and retrognathia, thoracic deformities, widely spaced and hypoplastic nipples, bilateral microphthalmia, ambiguous genitalia, phallus absence, and absence of vaginal opening. The third family involved monozygotic twin fetuses, both with a homozygous mutation c.956dup in the TXNDC15 gene. One fetus presented with occipital encephalocele, polydactyly, polycystic kidneys, low‐set ears, micrognathia, hypertelorism, bilateral talipes equinovarus, and absence of the uterus. The other fetus exhibited occipital encephalocele, polydactyly, polycystic kidneys, and hepatic fibrosis. Ridnõi et al. (2019) reported a 12‐week fetus with a compound heterozygous variant c.211dup/c.635T>C in the TXNDC15 gene, presenting with encephalocele, polydactyly, polycystic kidneys, and increased nuchal translucency thickness of 4.1 mm. Radhakrishnan et al. (2019) reported a family with a homozygous variant c.844C>T in the TXNDC15 gene. At 14 weeks of gestation, the fetus presented with micrognathia, anteverted nares, hypertelorism, short neck, holoprosencephaly, bowed long bones of the legs, and a single ventricle heart with a common atrioventricular canal. Kuroda et al. (2025) reported a 1‐year‐old girl carrying a compound heterozygous variant c.485A>G/c.786dup in the TXNDC15 gene. She exhibited hypoplasia of the cerebellar vermis with a molar tooth sign, mild holoprosencephaly, and cortical abnormalities. She had severe developmental delay and epilepsy, with overlapping phenotypes of Joubert syndrome (JS) and MKS, including the molar tooth sign, cortical dysplasia, and mild holoprosencephaly.

MKS is now recognized to encompass 14 distinct subtypes, each arising from a single‐gene defect. The implicated genes overwhelmingly encode structural or membrane‐associated components of the primary cilium; pathogenic variants disrupt ciliary function and converge on the classical triad of polycystic kidneys, occipital encephalocele, and post‐axial polydactyly. In addition to these cardinal features, individuals with MKS1 may develop hepatic manifestations such as periportal fibrosis or bile‐duct proliferation. Systematic histological examination of H&E‐stained liver sections from our mouse model revealed no evidence of fibrosis.

In our homozygous mouse model, gross examination at E15.5 revealed occipital encephalocele in 37.7% (10/27) and post‐axial polydactyly in 25.9% (7/27) of embryos. Prior functional work has shown that the pathogenic Txndc15 variant prevents the ciliary receptor Tmem67 from being correctly docked at the transition zone, resulting in fewer and morphologically abnormal cilia (Shaheen et al. 2016). Although TMEM67 (MKS3) is one of the most frequently mutated genes in MKS, comparative studies indicate that both polydactyly and, to a lesser extent, encephalocele are significantly less penetrant in MKS3‐linked families than in those harboring MKS1 or MKS2 mutations (Consugar et al. 2007; Morgan et al. 2002).

The Jackson Laboratory has reported that polycystic kidney phenotype emerges in mouse embryos at embryonic day 16 (E16) (Cook et al. 2009). Du et al. observed that renal cysts first appear in TMEM67‐homozygous mutant mice at embryonic day 15.5 (Du et al. 2013). In our cohort, cystic kidneys were first detectable at E15.5, with a penetrance of 20%—a figure that most likely reflects the early gestational stage examined. No cystic lesions were observed in wild‐type or heterozygous littermates at this time point.

Using CRISPR/Cas9, we generated the first Txndc15c.512delA homozygous mouse model. To our knowledge, this is the inaugural report of a Txndc15‐null allele in mice. Homozygosity was embryonic‐lethal: no Txndc15
mt/mt pups were recovered at birth. At E15.5, however, mutant embryos displayed the Meckel–Gruber spectrum—encephalocele, omphalocele, post‐axial polydactyly, and polycystic kidneys—while wild‐type and heterozygous littermates were morphologically normal. Immunoblotting of E15.5 brain and kidney lysates showed an almost complete loss of TXNDC15 protein in Txndc15
mt/mt embryos. These data establish that biallelic loss‐of‐function of TXNDC15 is sufficient to produce MKS, formally implicating the gene in the syndrome and validating the pathogenicity of the c.512delA variant.

Collectively, our findings establish TXNDC15 as a novel disease gene for MKS and identify the homozygous c.560delA variant as the most likely cause of the fetal malformations in this family. In a subsequent pregnancy cycle, pre‐implantation genetic testing was performed, and a single TXNDC15
wt/wt embryo was selected for transfer, culminating in the birth of a healthy baby boy.
