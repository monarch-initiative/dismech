PURPOSE: Angiosarcomas (AS) are rare vascular sarcomas. Secondary AS (sAS) arise
from DNA-damaging factors such as radiotherapy and UV radiation (UV-AS) or due
to chronic lymphedema. The prognosis for advanced AS is poor, with limited
treatment options. Immune checkpoint inhibition is not approved for AS, but high
intratumoral T-cell density and frequent mutations in sAS may support efficacy.
PATIENTS AND METHODS: This prospective, single-arm, multicenter phase II trial
assessed the efficacy and safety of cemiplimab (350 mg, intravenously every 3
weeks) in patients with locally advanced or metastatic sAS using a Simon's
two-stage design. The primary outcome was the best overall response rate within
24 weeks of treatment. Secondary outcomes included time to response, duration of
response, progression-free survival, overall survival, and predictive biomarkers
for treatment response.
RESULTS: Eighteen patients (12 with AS from radiotherapy, 3 with UV-AS, and 3
with AS due to chronic lymphedema) were treated with cemiplimab. The best
overall response rate was 27.8% (4 partial responses, 1 complete response), with
a time to response of 2.6 months and a duration of response of 6.9 months. The
median progression-free survival was 3.7 months, and the median overall survival
was 13.1 months. Grade ≥3 immune-related adverse events occurred in 33.3% of
patients. High tumor mutational burden was observed in three patients with
UV-AS, two of whom showed a response. High intratumoral CD3+ (P = 0.019), CD4 (P
= 0.046), CD8+ (P = 0.026), and FoxP3+ (P = 0.026) T-cell densities; low
platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (P = 0.026); and Colidextribacter abundance were
associated with response.
CONCLUSIONS: Cemiplimab shows promising effectivity in sAS and warrants further
investigation. Promising predictive blood and tissue biomarkers were identified,
indicating potential for improved patient selection.

Although sarcomas are generally considered immune-cold tumors and unlikely to respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), our prospective study highlights that specific secondary angiosarcoma (AS) subgroups may benefit from ICI treatment. Extensive biomarker analysis revealed the predictive value of high intratumoral T-cell density, low platelet–lymphocyte ratio, and gut microbiome abundance of Colidextribacter for treatment response to ICI. For the first time in AS, ctDNA was shown to be a predictor of treatment response as early as 3 weeks after treatment initiation. This study paves the way for the treatment of patients with secondary AS with immunotherapy and biomarker-driven clinical trials in rare cancers by identifying patient subgroups with possible susceptibility to immunotherapy.

Angiosarcomas (AS) are rare and aggressive soft-tissue sarcomas with endothelial characteristics that can be subdivided into primary AS (de novo; pAS) and secondary AS (sAS; refs. 1–3). sAS arise due to DNA-damaging factors such as previous radiotherapy (RT-AS) and UV radiation (UV-AS) or due to chronic lymphedema (Stewart-Treves AS; refs. 1, 3). The prognosis of locally advanced and metastatic AS is very poor, with a median overall survival (OS) of 5 to 10 months (4, 5). Treatment primarily consists of palliative chemotherapy, predominantly with paclitaxel or doxorubicin (6). The ANGIOTAX study reported a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 4.0 months and OS of 7.6 months in patients with unresectable AS treated with paclitaxel (7). Pazopanib, an oral multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor, is the only noncytotoxic drug approved for treating AS, with a response rate of 20% and a median PFS of 4.6 months (8). In the past decade, survival for AS has not improved, highlighting the urgent need for new treatment strategies (4, 9).

The development of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) was a major breakthrough in the treatment of many solid cancers, but not sarcomas. Sarcomas are generally considered immune-cold tumors with a poorly immune-infiltrated tumor microenvironment (TME), low tumor mutational burden (TMB), and low PD-L1 expression, all of which are potential biomarkers of response to ICI treatment (10, 11). Despite an overall immune-cold phenotype, small case series have shown that a subgroup of AS might benefit from treatment with ICI (12, 13). Florou and colleagues (12) retrospectively reported a response rate of 71% in seven patients with predominantly cutaneous AS. These patients were treated with various ICIs, both off-label and in clinical trials. D’Angelo and colleagues (13) showed a clinical response in three out of eight patients with AS treated with nivolumab and the CD122-preferential IL-2 agent bempegaldesleukin.

We and others have shown that AS is a heterogeneous group of malignancies with significant differences between pAS and sAS in clinical behavior, genetic background, and immunologic landscape (1, 14–16). sAS are characterized by a more T cell–infiltrated TME, including higher densities of CD4+ Th cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells compared with pAS (1, 17). In sarcomas, several studies have indicated a possible correlation between T- and B-cell infiltration of the TME and response to ICI (10, 13). Furthermore, a high TMB [TMB-H; TMB ≥ 10 mutations/megabase (mut/Mb)] is reported in more than 50% of UV-AS (1, 16, 18). sAS are also characterized by the frequent presence of mutations in genes involved in the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway (1). Alterations of the DDR pathway are known to induce genomic instability and increased TMB and are associated with an activated TME, indicating possible susceptibility to ICI (19).

However, unambiguous biomarkers predicting response to ICI are lacking. Promising biomarkers associated with response to ICI or survival, such as ctDNA levels, the gut microbiome, IFN-γ levels, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), have not yet been investigated in AS. Patient selection is essential to avoid unnecessary treatment and the subsequent risk of toxicity for nonresponders to ICI. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to identify possible predictive biomarkers for ICI response in sAS.

Given the complex genetic background of sAS, characterized by UV-mediated DNA damage and frequent MYC expression, sAS show some similarities to cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC; ref. 20). The PD-1 inhibitor cemiplimab has demonstrated favorable results in the treatment of CSCC, with an objective response rate of 40% to 50% in patients with locally advanced or metastatic disease (21). We aimed to investigate the efficacy of cemiplimab in sAS with a translational focus on predictive biomarkers for treatment response to ICI.

The CEMangio trial is an investigator-initiated, open-label, multicenter, single-arm, phase II clinical trial of cemiplimab monotherapy across three academic sarcoma expertise centers in the Netherlands. Adult patients with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 to 2 and histologically confirmed locally advanced or metastatic sAS were included. Patients unfit for chemotherapy in the first line of systemic treatment and patients in advanced lines of treatment were included. Adequate hepatic, renal, and bone marrow function was required. At least one measurable baseline lesion per RECIST version 1.1 was mandatory.

Key exclusion criteria were active autoimmune disease requiring systemic immunosuppressive treatments, prior treatment with ICI, continuous corticosteroid treatment, a history of pneumonitis in the past 5 years, and anticancer treatment other than radiotherapy within 30 days of cemiplimab. Detailed eligibility criteria can be found in the study protocol (appendix).

This study protocol and all amendments were approved by the appropriate medical ethical board (CMO Arnhem-Nijmegen, 2021–12959) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines of the International Conference on Harmonization for Good Clinical Practice. There are currently no formal guidelines like CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials; randomized trials), STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology; observational trials), or PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses; systematic reviews and meta-analyses) for single-arm interventional trials. However, we used applicable elements of CONSORT where possible. All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. This clinical trial is registered under the trial number NCT04873375.

Patients were treated with cemiplimab 350 mg intravenously every 3 weeks for up to 96 weeks or until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or withdrawal of consent. Tumor response evaluations were scheduled at baseline, after 6 and 12 weeks, and every 12 weeks thereafter. Adverse events (AE) were graded according to the NCI Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (RRID:SCR_010296). Treatment interruptions were applied based on the guidance of the summary of product characteristics, as registered with the European Medicines Agency.

The primary endpoint was the best overall response (BOR) rate (BORR) within 24 weeks of cemiplimab treatment, according to RECIST 1.1 or daylight photography. BORR was defined as the proportion of patients who had a partial response (PR) or complete response (CR) to therapy. Secondary endpoints included median time to response, defined as the time between treatment initiation and the first date of an objective response; median duration of response (DOR), defined as the time between the first measurement of CR or PR and the first date of recurrent or progressive disease (PD) or death; PFS, defined as the time between the start of treatment and the first date of recurrence or PD or death from any cause; and OS, defined as the time between the start of treatment and death from any cause. Other secondary outcomes included safety, sAS subtype responses, and translational endpoints.

Secondary outcomes included associations between tumor characteristics and treatment response. A detailed description of the materials and methods for the translational research component of this study is provided in the supplementary materials and methods. In summary, tumor biopsies, stool samples, and blood samples were collected at prespecified time points. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) and multiplex IHC (mIHC) were performed on tumor biopsies. The gut microbiome was analyzed using 16S rRNA sequencing. Peripheral blood was used for the determination of IFN-γ levels, NLR, PLR, ctDNA analysis, and flow cytometry of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC). Responders were classified as patients with either a CR or PR as BOR. Nonresponders were classified as patients with either stable disease or PD as BOR.

Sample selection for NGS was conducted with the criterion of having at least 30% tumor cell content to ensure precise analysis of TMB and microsatellite instability. DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue, subsequently precipitated, and quantified. Library preparation and sequencing were performed as described previously (22, 23). Coverage tables and a variant call file were generated for both single- and multiple-nucleotide variants, including the number and percentages of variant alleles. Genomic variants were filtered by excluding (i) variants outside exon and splice-site regions, except those in the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter region; (ii) synonymous variants, unless they were located in a splice-site region; (iii) variants with a frequency >0.1% in the control population represented in the Exome Aggregation Consortium (RRID:SCR_004068) version 0.2; and (iv) variants with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of less than 5%. Candidate variants were validated using Alamut Visual Plus version 1.7.1. Variants were manually analyzed and classified into five categories based on predicted pathogenicity: class 1 (not pathogenic), class 2 (unlikely pathogenic), class 3 (variant of unknown significance), class 4 (likely pathogenic), and class 5 (pathogenic). Classes 4 and 5 were considered potentially clinically relevant and are referred to as (likely) pathogenic in this article. Pathogenicity interpretations for variants in tumor suppressor genes (TSG) utilized three prediction tools [sorting intolerant from tolerant (RRID:SCR_012813), PolyPhen-2 (RRID:SCR_013189), and Align-Grantham Variation Grantham Deviation (RRID:SCR_010772)], and for both TSG and oncogenes, various knowledge-based resources [ClinVar (RRID:SCR_006169), OncoKB (RRID:SCR_014782), InterVar] were used. Therapeutic targeting of TSGs typically necessitates the inactivation of both alleles. Thus, for class 4/5 variants in TSGs, we assessed whether these affected one or both alleles by examining relative coverage and/or VAFs of the variant and adjacent SNPs. Gene amplification was evaluated as previously described based on median coverage normalization, with a relative coverage of ≥3 indicating amplification (22). The number of gene copies was estimated by adjusting the relative coverage for the percentage of tumor cells in the sample. TMB analysis was based on nonsynonymous variants (total TMB), with a threshold of 10 mut/Mb considered TMB-H. Mutational signatures were analyzed in tumors with a TMB ≥10 mut/Mb using the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (RRID:SCR_002260) mutational signature version 3 (22, 24). Responders were classified with either a CR or PR as BORR. Nonresponders were classified as patients with either stable disease or PD as BORR.

Blood samples were collected at prespecified time points. Blood samples (30 mL per sample) were collected in EDTA tubes and processed within 2 hours using two centrifugation steps: the first at 2,000 × g for 10 minutes to isolate plasma and the second at 16,000 × g for 10 minutes to remove cellular debris. Plasma was stored at −80°C until further processing.

Isolation of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) from 4 to 10 mL of plasma was performed manually using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit or automated using the QIAsymphony instrument (QIAGEN). DNA concentrations were measured using the Qubit High Sensitivity kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 50 ng of cfDNA input was used. Targeted NGS was performed using an in-house developed NGS test (25). The NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina; RRID:SCR_016387) was used for sequencing, producing 2 × 150 base pair paired-end reads. Analysis was conducted as described previously by Hofste and colleagues (25) and Tolmeijer and colleagues (26). Variants were filtered by excluding (i) variants outside exons and splice-site regions, (ii) synonymous variants, (iii) variants present with a frequency >0.1% in the control population represented in the Exome Aggregation Consortium (0.2; RRID:SCR_004068) database, (iv) variants with less than five variant reads, (v) variants with only alternative reads on the forward or reverse read, and (vi) mutations with low confidence according to the Mutect2 (RRID:SCR_026692) quality filter. Only variants that were detected in the tissue were retrieved. The number of mutant molecules per milliliter of plasma was calculated by using the mean mutant VAF, the volume of plasma used for isolation, and the total number of cfDNA molecules (26).

In the cases in which a MYC amplification was detected in tissue, copy-number analysis was performed as described by Tolmeijer and colleagues (26). Per sample, the coverage per probe was divided by the overall median coverage of the sample to obtain a normalized coverage per probe. The normalized coverage was divided by the median normalized coverage of healthy cfDNA samples (n = 22), processed similarly, to obtain a relative normalized coverage per probe. Per gene, the median relative normalized coverage was calculated. Amplification was defined as a median relative normalized coverage >0.3.

Sections of 4 µm thickness were cut from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded biopsies before treatment and, if available, at 12 weeks of treatment. Samples belonging to one patient were mounted on one glass slide. mIHC was performed on the Bond RX system (RRID:SCR_025548) using a panel to detect cytotoxic T cells, regulatory T cells, Th cells, B cells, and NK cells, as described previously (1, 27). An antibody against erythroblast transformation–specific related gene, a transcription factor expressed in endothelial cells, served as a tumor marker (clone EPR3864, Abcam; RRID:AB_11156852). Whole tissue multispectral imaging was performed with the PhenoImager HT (Akoya Biosciences; RRID:SCR_023772) at 20× magnification using the Vectra Polaris (RRID:SCR_025508) software (version 1.0.13, Akoya Biosciences). All areas containing tissue were selected for inForm batch processing with Phenochart (RRID:SCR_019156, version 1.2.0, Akoya Biosciences). Different Phenochart annotations were made to separate before-treatment samples from after-treatment tissue. Representative images were selected with Phenochart to train an algorithm with inForm image analysis software (RRID:SCR_019155, V.2.6.0, Akoya Biosciences) for spectral unmixing of Opal fluorophores, removal of autofluorescence signal, and tissue segmentation. For tissue segmentation, the algorithm was trained to recognize tumor, stroma, and background based on the presence of erythroblast transformation–specific related gene, DAPI, and autofluorescence. This algorithm was used to batch process whole tissue images. Cell identification was performed with ImmuNet (28). Immune cell data were saved into Flow Cytometry Standard files, and subsequent phenotyping and localization of lymphocyte cell subsets were performed with FlowJo (RRID:SCR_008520, version 10, Tree Star Inc.). Immune cells were classified as CD3+ general T cells (CD3+), CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CD3+, CD8+), FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (CD3+, FOXP3+), CD4+ Th cells (CD3+, CD8−, FOXP3−), CD20+ B cells (CD20+), or CD56+ NK cells (CD56+). Infiltration of lymphocyte subsets was expressed in cell density by dividing the absolute cell counts by the surface area (cells/mm2) of the tissue region in consideration (total tissue, tumor, or stroma).

For flow cytometry, PBMCs were isolated and cryopreserved at baseline and, if available, at 12 weeks of treatment. PBMCs were thawed and stained first with a fixable viability dye eFluor 780 (65-0865-12, eBioscience). After washing with autoMACS staining buffer (130-091-221, Miltenyi Biotec), cells were stained with the following antibodies: CD8 FITC (555366, BD Biosciences, RRID:AB_395769), TIM-3 PE (119704, BioLegend, RRID:AB_345377), CD20 PERCP (130-113-376, Miltenyi Biotec, RRID:AB_2726144), CD3 PE-Cy7 (25-0038-42, Invitrogen, RRID:AB_1582253), CD56 APC (130-113-305, Miltenyi Biotec, RRID:AB_2726084), CD4 APC-R700 (564975, BD Biosciences, RRID:AB_2744418), PD-1 BV421 (329920, BioLegend, RRID:AB_10960742), LAG-3 Super Bright 600 (63-2239-42, Invitrogen, RRID:AB_2688188), and PD-L1 BV711 (329722, BioLegend, RRID:AB_2565764). The stained cells were washed twice with staining buffer, and fluorescence was analyzed with the BD FACSLyric. A positive control was consistently used. A second experiment was conducted due to the steep decrease in PD-1–positive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells at 12 weeks. The second experiment included PBMCs from baseline and 12 weeks of three patients. Two conditions were tested per time point. The first condition comprised a culture with RPMI and human serum and served as the control. The second condition included RPMI, human serum, and 10 μg/mL cemiplimab. After an incubation period of 66 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2, flow cytometry was conducted as described above. Data analysis was performed in BD FACSuite software version 1.5.0.925 (BD Biosciences, RRID:SCR_025927).

OM-200.100 kits (OMNIgene•GUT, DNA Genotek) were used to collect and stabilize DNA for quantitative gut microbiome profile analysis. Illumina 16S rRNA marker-gene amplicon libraries were generated and sequenced at BaseClear B.V. on an Illumina MiSeq paired-end 300 system. The paired-end sequencing data were split using the demultiplexing tool bcl2fastq (version 2.20) in order to generate FASTQ read sequence files. Preprocessing of reads was performed with the Illumina chastity filter, and reads containing the PhiX control signal were removed. The remaining reads were adapter-trimmed to a minimum length of 50 bp. A custom QIIME2-based Nextflow pipeline (version 23.10.1) was used for processing the 16S marker-gene sequences (29, 30). First, reads were primer-trimmed with cutadapt (RRID:SCR_011841; forward: CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG, reverse: GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC) to a minimum length of 20 bp, and untrimmed reads were discarded (31). Trimmed paired-end reads were then merged into single reads via paired-end read merger (RRID:SCR_003776; ref. 32). Reads are then imported in QIIME2 (RRID:SCR_021258) via “qiime2 tools import” as “SingleEndFastqManifestPhred33V2” format (30). Denoising is performed via “qiime2 dada2 denoise-paired” set at default settings except for the discard of forward reads with number of expected errors higher than 3 (default 2), chimera method is set to “consensus,” and the fold change versus the abundance of the sequence being tested is set to 2 (default 1; ref. 30). The latter entails that sequences that are being tested as chimeric should be more abundant than the parent sequence. The construction of a phylogenetic tree was done using “qiime2 phylogeny align-to-tree-mafft-fasttree” (30). Classification of representative sequences was accomplished by “qiime2 feature-classifier classify-consensus-vsearch” via VSEARCH alignment against the SILVA 138 database (Ref NR 99; i.e., nonredundant 99% identity) as a 16S reference database for taxonomic classification (30, 33).

RStudio Integrated Development Environment (version 2023.12.1) with an R base (version 4.3.3) was used for data analysis. First, the obtained rooted phylogenetic tree from “qiime2 phylogeny align-to-tree-mafft-fasttree” and the table with taxonomy and their counts in Biological Observation Matrix format were loaded into R (30). The data were used to select the “Bacteria” domain, and total sum scaling normalization was performed by dividing, for each sample, the counts for each taxon by the total counts for that respective sample. Alpha diversity metrics by the Shannon index were computed after rarefaction using “qiime2 diversity core-metrics-phylogenetic” with default settings (30). Tabular formats were data-wrangled and visualized using “tidyverse” and “patchwork” in R. Principal coordinate analysis with weighted unique fraction (UniFrac) metric distances was computed by the R package “rbiom.” All visualizations were achieved via data wrangling with the R packages “tidyverse” and “patchwork.” Permutational multivariate ANOVA was applied to the weighted UniFrac distances to compute differences in β diversity between selected study groups (34). The fold change of log2 bacterial expression between two groups was tested with the Mann–Whitney test with continuity correction in the normal approximation for the P value (35). Enrichment of operational taxonomic units was compared between responders and nonresponders. In the end, we yielded an average of 17,165 ± 3,312 (SD) reads on which taxonomic classification was performed (the lowest sample had 12,942 reads). At the taxonomic level, 1,882 amplicon sequence variants were identified in the bacterial domain.

Plasma samples were collected using EDTA tubes for IFN-γ analysis. Samples were stored at −80°C. The MILLIPLEX Human Cytokine/Chemokine/Growth Factor Panel A kit (Merck) was used. The IFN-γ detection limit was ≥5.01 pg/mL. Analysis was performed using a FLEXMAP 3D with xPONENT software (RRID:SCR_025653, Luminex).

Primary and secondary clinical efficacy outcomes were assessed according to the intention-to-treat principle among enrolled patients. All patients who received at least one dose of cemiplimab were assessed for safety. The data cutoff date was October 18, 2024. A Simon’s two-stage design was used (36). The null hypothesis that the true response rate is 10% was tested against a one-sided alternative. In the first stage, 13 patients were accrued. In the case of one or fewer responses in the first 13 patients, the study would be stopped. Otherwise, five additional patients would be accrued for a total of 18 patients. The null hypothesis would be rejected if four or more responses were observed in 18 patients. This design yielded a type I error rate of 10% and a power of 90% when the true response rate is 34%. This 34% was in concordance with the trial conducted by Migden and colleagues (37), treating patients with locally advanced or metastatic CSCC with cemiplimab. Patient characteristics were summarized using means or medians as applicable. Crude survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with survival differences between patient subgroups assessed through log-rank testing. Values were found to be statistically significant with a P value < 0.05. For patients with a response, without disease progression, or who did not die, censoring was applied at the time of their last contact with the study team for DOR and PFS analysis. For OS, patients without a survival event were censored at the time of last known survival. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 22.0.0.1, and R Studio, version 3.6.2 (RRID: SCR_016479).

The Binary Alignment Map and corresponding (annotated) Variant Call Format files of the patients cannot be publicly shared due to Institutional Review Board restrictions, as the patients did not consent to share raw sequencing data beyond the scope of research and clinical terms. All variants assessed as (potentially) clinically relevant are included in the article. Data requests will be processed according to national and institutional guidelines upon reasonable request to ingrid.desar@radboudumc.nl.

Study enrollment started in January 2022. After the inclusion of 13 patients, in concordance with Simon’s two-stage design, an interim analysis was conducted. The prespecified criteria were met, and five additional patients were accrued until December 2023, for a total number of 18 patients. All patients were included for the analysis of the primary endpoint. The baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. All enrolled patients received at least one dose of cemiplimab. The median follow-up was 11.0 months. The median age of the patients was 72 years (range 51–94). Patients enrolled included 12 with RT-AS (66.7%), 3 with UV-AS (16.7%), and 3 with Stewart-Treves AS (16.7%). All patients with RT-AS were women, previously treated for breast cancer, and all those with UV-AS were located in the head and neck area. Two patients with Stewart Treves AS were located in the lower extremities, and one was located in the pelvic area. The proportion of patients with locally advanced disease and distant metastases were 27.8% and 72.2%, respectively. Cemiplimab was first-line systemic treatment in 10 patients (55.6%), second-line systemic treatment in six patients (33.3%), and third-line systemic treatment in two patients (11.1%). An overview of patient representation can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Baseline demographics and disease characteristics of 18 patients with AS.

The BORR within 24 weeks was 27.8%, with four patients (22.2%) achieving a PR (2 with RT-AS, 2 with UV-AS) and one patient (5.6%) achieving a CR (RT-AS; Fig. 1A). One patient with a PR (UV-AS) at 24 weeks developed a CR at week 60. The null hypothesis would be rejected if four or more responses were observed in 18 patients. Therefore, with five objective responses, the primary endpoint of this study was met. Two patients report an ongoing CR at the data cutoff date, whereas both are no longer on treatment. One patient reached the maximum treatment duration at 96 weeks, and the other patient stopped due to immune-related dermatitis. One patient with a PR developed early disease progression within weeks after the radiological response. The median time to response was 2.6 months (range 1.2–5.4), and the median DOR was 6.9 months (range 0.43–not reached). The median PFS was 3.7 months (range 1.1–not reached; Fig. 1B), and the median OS was 13.1 months (range 2.1–not reached; Fig. 1C). An AE of any kind was reported by all patients, with 10 patients (55.6%) experiencing a grade ≥3 AE (Supplementary Table S2). Grade 3 or higher immune-related AEs occurred in six patients (33.3%). Serious AEs (SAE) were reported in six patients (33.3%; Supplementary Table S3). Treatment was stopped in three patients due to immune-related AEs: one patient with grade 3 hepatitis, another with grade 4 hepatitis, and one patient due to grade 2 dermatitis. One patient died after a rapid clinical deterioration followed by multiorgan failure, including liver and kidney failure, of unknown origin and without a typical immune-related pattern. Because causality could not be fully excluded, a possible relationship with the study drug was reported.

Of the 18 patients included, 11 were eligible for ctDNA analysis based on the presence of a mutation or amplification in tumor tissue that is detectable with the targeted ctDNA-NGS panel.

A specific mutation was detected in the tumor tissue of five patients. One patient was excluded from the ctDNA analysis because of low coverage. In the four remaining patients, 15 mutations in total were detected in the tumor tissue, of which 13 were also detected with ctDNA-NGS at baseline, resulting in a true-positive rate of 87% for small variant detection (Supplementary Table S4).

In one of the four patients, the TP53 mutation identified in the tumor tissue (p.Gln317*, Supplementary Table S4) was only detected at baseline in ctDNA after manual inspection of the Binary Alignment Map file. The other tumor-derived variant that was present in tissue was not detected in ctDNA at any time point. The locus-specific coverage of both tumor-derived variants was sufficient to reliably detect them in ctDNA at each time point, suggesting that missing those variants was likely the result of low ctDNA shedding. In this phase II study, cemiplimab showed promising efficacy in the treatment of locally advanced or metastatic sAS. The CEMangio trial prospectively studied the use of anti–PD-1 ICI monotherapy, reporting a BORR of 27.8%, a median PFS of 3.7 months, and a median OS of 13.1 months. Two patients (11%) have an ongoing CR. Furthermore, one patient experienced stable disease for 8.1 months, whereas two other patients had stable disease according to RECIST for up to 5 months. In aggressive diseases like AS, in which the median OS reported in literature is 5 to 10 months, a prolonged period of stable disease can be considered a favorable outcome. Our findings align with the reported ORR of 25% in a prospective cohort of 16 patients with pAS and sAS treated with a combination of ipilimumab and nivolumab by Wagner and colleagues (38). Furthermore, two other recent retrospective cohort studies reported similar response rates, ranging from 20% to 25%, in cohorts treating both pAS and sAS with ICI-based regimens (39, 40). These findings collectively suggest comparable efficacy across different studies and treatment protocols, highlighting the potential of ICI as a viable therapeutic option for sAS. A grade 3 or higher immune-related AE occurred in six patients (33.3%). SAEs were reported in six patients (33.3%). In two cases, the SAE was reported as related to cemiplimab treatment, and in one case, a possible relationship was reported. These results are comparable with those previously reported by Migden and colleagues in the trials evaluating cemiplimab in CSCC, in which an SAE or treatment-related AE of grade 3 or higher was reported in 36% to 44% of the cases (21, 37).

Particularly notable is the high response rate observed in UV-AS. We documented an ORR in one out of three (67%) patients with UV-AS, with all UV-AS tumors exhibiting the single-base substitution signature 7a, indicative of UV-induced damage. Wagner and colleagues (38) reported an objective response in 60% of the scalp/face AS in their study and in one out of two RT-AS, whereas no response was found in any of the pAS. Similarly, a 60% response rate in 14 scalp/face AS in a retrospective study involving pembrolizumab has been reported (39). In a retrospective study, including several subgroups of AS, a response rate of 43% was reported in the 14 cutaneous AS of the head and neck treated with an ICI-based regimen (40). This might be indicative that especially UV-AS are susceptible to ICI therapy, possibly due to the TMB-H associated with UV-AS (1, 16, 18). In our study, all patients with TMB-H were UV-AS, with the two patients reporting the highest TMB showing a PR and CR, respectively. In a cohort of 143 sarcomas, 26% exhibited TMB-H, with 63% of these cases occurring in AS of the head and neck (16). Previously, we also reported a TMB-H in 43% of UV-AS (3/7; ref. 1). However, three of the responders did not exhibit a TMB-H, highlighting that although TMB is a well-known biomarker, it does not unambiguously predict response to ICI. Other biomarkers, including the TME and gut microbiome, might be important in predicting response to ICI. None of the Stewart-Treves AS responded to treatment in our study. Interestingly, in contrast to our results, Rosenbaum and colleagues (40) suggested potential sensitivity to ICI in Stewart-Treves AS, reporting clinical benefit with a PR in both patients in their retrospective cohort. It must be remarked that both studies only included small numbers of Stewart-Treves AS, limiting the ability to draw rigid conclusions.

In our study, (likely) pathogenic mutations were identified in 88.2% of the tumors. Many of these mutations are druggable targets, for example, BRCA1, CHECK2, IDH1, NOTCH1, PALB2, and TSC2. Deficiencies in the DDR pathway are known to induce genomic instability and tumor evolution. DDR pathway mutations impair DNA repair and thereby increase TMB and upregulate PD-L1 expression. This leads to enhanced immunogenicity in tumors due to the increased presence of neoantigens; consequently, tumors with DDR pathway mutations are associated with an immune-infiltrated microenvironment and may be more susceptible to ICI (19, 41). Several clinical trials are currently investigating combination therapies using DDR pathway inhibitors, such as PARP inhibitors, along with ICIs (41, 42). We did not find a significant association between the presence of DDR pathway mutations and response to ICIs. This might be the result of the small numbers included. However, the high incidence of DDR pathway mutations in sAS and reports suggesting potential synergy with combination strategies involving ICIs and DDR inhibitors indicate an interesting opportunity for future research in AS.


MYC amplifications, which are typically considered a hallmark of RT-AS, were observed in only 50% of RT-AS and did not correlate with treatment response (1, 15). CRKL amplifications have often been identified in AS, with frequencies ranging from 6% to 27% (1, 12, 40). We only detected a CRKL amplification in two patients without a response to cemiplimab. Overexpression of CRKL proteins is associated with poor prognosis in various solid tumors (43). CRKL plays an essential role in T-cell migration, NK cell expansion, and cell growth. Xie and colleagues (44) reported that CRKL overexpression impedes CD8+ T-cell infiltration by mobilizing tumor-associated neutrophils, thereby limiting ICI effectiveness in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma. The frequent occurrence of MYC and CRKL amplifications warrants further research in larger cohorts of patients with AS treated with ICI as our findings might be influenced by the small numbers, whereas MYC and CRKL amplifications are emerging as potential druggable targets (45, 46).

ctDNA is increasingly recognized as a potential early response predictor to ICI in several tumor types, including sarcomas, but it has never been investigated in AS before (47, 48). We showed that a decline in ctDNA, as early as 3 weeks after treatment initiation, accurately predicted therapy response in responding patients, preceding a radiological response. We also demonstrated a clear increase in ctDNA in patients with evolving radiological disease progression. Real-time monitoring by means of ctDNA could enable clinicians to assess the effectiveness of ICI sooner than radiological imaging, which is often conducted after 12 weeks of treatment, allowing for timely adjustment of the treatment plan. This highlights the potential of ctDNA as a valuable tool in optimizing patient management (49).

We found significantly higher intratumoral CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, and FoxP3+ T-cell density in baseline tumor samples of responders to ICI. The tumor immune microenvironment and, in particular, the presence of CD8+ T cells have been reported as potential predictive biomarkers for treatment response to ICI (13, 50, 51). CD8+ T-cell infiltrates correlated with improved response in AS treated with nivolumab and bempegaldesleukin (13). Moreover, a high T-cell density was an independent prognostic factor for survival in soft-tissue sarcoma (51). Interestingly, in our study, the patient who exhibited a CR had a relatively low T-cell density. This highlights a limitation in using intratumoral lymphocyte density as a sole predictive biomarker, possibly explained by the heterogeneous nature of the TME with sampling bias.

An early increase in PD-1+CD8+ peripheral T cells has been reported as a possible predictive biomarker for ICI response in solid tumors (52). We found no association between baseline and 12-week peripheral immune cell composition and treatment response. In our study, after 12 weeks of treatment, the immune cell composition in peripheral blood showed almost no PD-1+ T cells. A second experiment confirmed that this drop in detection of PD-1–positive T cells was due to therapeutic binding of cemiplimab, as was also previously reported for nivolumab and pembrolizumab in other studies with solid tumors (52). In the peripheral blood of patients with HIV, cemiplimab PD-1 receptor occupancy of more than 70% correlated with undetectable PD-1 expression, which was achieved at levels of 1 µg/mL cemiplimab concentration (53). To gain a more comprehensive insight, in future studies, peripheral T-cell analysis during the first couple of weeks of treatment could be a valuable next step.

Inflammatory biomarkers detected in peripheral blood could offer an easily accessible and minimally invasive method for predicting treatment response to ICI. Key inflammatory cells, such as neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, interact with the TME and may help cancer cells evade immune surveillance (54). Indicative of an inflammatory and pro-oncogenic state, we report a significantly higher PLR in nonresponders. PLR has been correlated with PFS and OS in patients with gastric cancer treated with ICI (55). In contrast to previous studies, NLR and IFN-γ were comparable between responders and nonresponders in our trial (56, 57). We did observe a significant increase in IFN-γ levels in a patient who, at that time point, developed ICI-induced hepatitis. An increase in IFN-γ levels has been suggested as an indicator of immune-related AEs (58). Mixed signals on NLR and IFN-γ and the association between low PLR and response to ICI warrant further exploration to determine their role as predictive biomarkers.

The gut microbiome may influence the response to ICI. Our results are consistent with previous reports, showing a significant association with the response to ICI and enrichment of Colidextribacter (59). Furthermore, we identified nonsignificant changes in the relative abundance in responders of Agathobacter, Ruminococcus, and Streptococcus, genera that have been frequently associated with ICI response (60, 61). The presence of R. gnavus and Pseudoflavonifractor was significantly associated with nonresponse to ICI, in concordance with earlier studies (62). Escherichia Shigella was nonsignificantly enriched in nonresponders in our study, as also shown by He and colleagues (63), who investigated the influence of the gut microbiome on ICI response in patients with non–small cell lung cancer. Of all bacterial genera, Akkermansia is most often associated with response to ICI (60). Akkermansia was present in 40% of the responders in our study, compared with 20% of the nonresponders (nonsignificant difference), including the single patient who showed a CR at 24 weeks. There is increasing evidence that antibiotic use limits the benefit of ICIs (60). Interestingly, all four patients in our study who received antibiotic treatment in the 3 months prior to treatment were nonresponders, emphasizing the importance of appropriate antibiotic use in close proximity to ICI treatment.

Our study has some limitations. We included only 18 patients in a single-arm design, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, despite limiting inclusion to sAS, the cohort remains heterogeneous, comprising UV-AS, RT-AS, and Stewart-Treves AS. However, despite the small sample size, this cohort provides valuable insights for sAS treatment. Importantly, the study’s extensive translational research component has allowed us to deepen our understanding of this rare disease in the context of ICI and provided important leads for future research in AS. Future research could focus on combining ICI with chemotherapy as a first line of systemic therapy for metastatic or locally advanced sAS, as ICI combined with paclitaxel showed promising results in AS of the head and neck in the Alliance A091902 trial. Furthermore, the NADINA trial recently showed the value of neoadjuvant ipilimumab–nivolumab in melanoma (64). Neoadjuvant ICI, as monotherapy or combined with chemotherapy, could be a valuable next step to investigate in resectable sAS in order to reduce recurrence rates and improve long-term survival.

Cemiplimab shows promising effectivity in sAS, particularly in UV-AS and RT-AS. We showed that a T cell–infiltrated TME, TMB-H, low PLR, and Colidextribacter abundance are potential biomarkers for treatment response to ICI in sAS. Furthermore, this study highlights the potential added value of ctDNA in early response prediction. Future prospective clinical trials are needed to validate our findings in a larger cohort to determine the place and optimal regimen of ICI in sAS.
