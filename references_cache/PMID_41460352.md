# DDRGK1 preserves intervertebral disc development through ufmylation.
**Authors:** Lu M, Zhou T, Yang X, Liu K, Rong K, Ma P, Qin A, Zhao J
**Journal:** Cell Mol Life Sci (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00018-025-06034-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-025-06034-8)

## Content

UFMylation, similar to ubiquitination, is a unique post-translational modification which is indispensable in hematopoiesis, neurogenesis and chondrogenesis. However, its role in intervertebral disc development remains unclear. In this study, we focused on DDRGK domain containing protein 1 (DDRGK1), a pivotal component involved in UFMylation, and generated Ddrgk1fl/fl; Acan-CreERT2 (Ddrgk1cKO) mice to explore DDRGK1's regulatory function in the nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate. We found that Ddrgk1 conditional knockout led to severe retardation of spinal growth, disruption of disc cellularity and initiation of disc degeneration during early postnatal phase. Furthermore, Ddrgk1 conditional knockout in late postnatal phase resulted in profound degeneration of mouse discs, mainly characterized by substantially reduced thickness of cartilage endplate. In addition, Ddrgk1cKO mice exhibited exacerbated disc degeneration compared to the WT mice after the lumbar spine instability surgery. RNA sequencing of disc cells from Ddrgk1cKO mice showed upregulation of genes related to apoptosis, matrix metalloproteinase activation, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) unfolded protein response after Ddrgk1 conditional knockout. Immunohistochemical analysis further verified increased apoptosis, ECM disruption and ER stress in both nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate after Ddrgk1 conditional knockout. In summary, this study demonstrated that DDRGK1 preserves the normal cellularity and structure of intervertebral discs by regulating the cell fate of nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate cells, maintaining the ER homeostasis and regulating the metabolic balance of ECM.

The intervertebral disc is a fibrocartilaginous tissue located between adjacent vertebrae [1]. It permits limited movement and distributes compressive loads across adjacent vertebrae [2]. The intervertebral disc comprises three key components: nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus and cartilage endplate [3, 4]. The nucleus pulposus is a highly hydrated and gelatinous tissue composed of nucleus pulposus cells and abundant ECM [5]. The ECM secreted by nucleus pulposus cells is crucial for withstanding compressive loads and maintaining a stable cellular microenvironment [6]. The annulus fibrosus surrounding the nucleus pulposus constrains the position and shape of nucleus pulposus during axial compression and distributes stress uniformly across the vertebral body [7]. The cartilage endplate, a hyaline cartilage layer approximately 0.6–1 mm thick, covers the superior and inferior surfaces of the nucleus pulposus [8, 9]. Chondrocytes are the only cell type in the cartilage endplate which secrete ECM, mainly composed of collagen Ⅱ and proteoglycans, to maintain the structure and function of the cartilage endplate [10–12]. The cartilage endplate connects the vertebral body and nucleus pulposus, serving as a mechanical barrier and molecular channel from nearby blood vessels to the avascular intervertebral disc [13–15].

Intervertebral disc degeneration is a common degenerative disease of the musculoskeletal system [7, 16]. As a multifactorial disease, the pathogenesis of intervertebral disc degeneration remains incompletely understood. Notably, balanced interactions between the ECM and disc cells play a critical role in the homeostasis of intervertebral disc [17, 18]. Thus ECM degradation has been considered as an initiating and pivotal event in the intervertebral disc degeneration [19, 20]. In addition, the apoptosis of nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate cells is closely associated with the progression of intervertebral disc degeneration [14, 21–24]. Cartilage endplate calcification has also been highlighted as a key contributor which alters mechanical properties and impairs nutrient supply, ultimately exacerbating intervertebral disc degeneration [25, 26]. Furthermore, ER stress, a condition occurs when a large number of misfolded proteins accumulate in the ER, could also disrupt the metabolic balance of the ECM, trigger the apoptosis of the nucleus pulposus cells and ultimately lead to the aggravation of intervertebral disc degeneration [27–29].

Ubiquitin folding modifier 1 (UFM1) conjugation system is a recently characterized ubiquitin-like modification system. This modification, known as UFMylation, is catalyzed by the sequential action of the ubiquitin activating enzyme 5 (UBA5), the UFM1 conjugase 1 (UFC1) and the UFM1 ligase 1 (UFL1), and plays a critical role in maintaining ER homeostasis and embryonic development [30–32]. DDRGK1 is an essential component of UFMylation which forms a stable complex with UFL1 and contributes to the covalent attachment of UFM1 to substrate proteins [30, 33]. Embryonic knockout of Ddrgk1 has been confirmed to cause hematopoietic defects, leading to embryonic death by E12.5 due to anemia [34, 35]. Furthermore, DDRGK1 depletion reduces p53 stability, prevents p53 accumulation under DNA damage conditions and promotes tumor growth, underscoring the critical role of DDRGK1 in maintaining genomic integrity and determining cell fate [36]. Notably, DDRGK1 has been shown to be indispensable in cartilage development. Loss-of-function mutations in DDRGK1 have been proven to be linked to severe chondrodysplasia through its regulation of SOX9, a key transcription factor in chondrogenesis [34, 37]. Conditional knockout of Ddrgk1 in mouse mesenchymal cells led to severe limb shortening, joint abnormalities and growth plate development disorders characterized by shortened proliferative region and enlarged hypertrophic region [37]. In addition, DDRGK1 may modulate ER homeostasis and chondrogenesis by regulating the stability of inositol requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α) via UFMylation [38, 39]. However, no study has verified whether DDRGK1 regulates the survival and development of nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate cells through UFMylation. To address this, we employed Ddrgk1 conditional knockout mice to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Ddrgk1fl/fl mice and Acan-CreERT2 mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China. Upon arrival, the mice were housed in an SPF-grade laboratory animal facility, maintained at a temperature of 26–28℃ and a humidity of 50%−65%. They were fed a standard diet and had free access to fresh water. Apart from necessary experimental procedures, no additional disturbances were made to their environment. Experimental manipulations were conducted at least one week after their arrival to allow the mice to adapt to the new living conditions. In this study, Ddrgk1fl/fl mice were first crossed with Acan-CreERT2 mice to generate Ddrgk1fl/+; Acan-CreERT2 heterozygous mice. These heterozygotes were then crossed to produce Ddrgk1cKO mice. The mice were fasted overnight prior to the experimental procedures, with free access to water. All experimental procedures involving mice in this study strictly adhered to the Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals issued by State Scientific and Technological Commission of China, and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All mice were on a C57BL/6J background.

Tamoxifen was dissolved in corn oil at 12.5 mg/ml and administered at 75 mg/kg i.p. to induce Acan-CreERT2 expression. For the early-postnatal group, administration occurred on postnatal days 1 to 3, and for the late-postnatal group, it was given for 5 consecutive days at one month of age of mice. Upon reaching the specified time point, the mice were euthanized. Approximately 0.5 cm of the mouse tail tissue was collected for genotype identification. The dorsal skin was incised with tissue scissors to isolate the spine. Then the spine was either immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution or the intervertebral discs were isolated for RNA extraction and sequencing analysis.

To isolate primary mouse chondrocytes, neonatal Ddrgk1fl/fl and Ddrgk1cKO mice (3–5 each, not injected with tamoxifen) aged 3 days were euthanized and immersed in 75% ethanol for 15 min. Subsequently, the knee joint was isolated from both hind limbs of the mice after the skin was removed. The synovium and muscle tissue were stripped carefully. The cartilage samples were then minced into pieces (0.5–1 mm in diameter) and digested in DMEM/F12 with 1% collagenase II at 37℃ for 4 h. After centrifugation (300 × g for 5 min), the cells were resuspended in complete medium (DMEM/F12 with 5% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptoycin).

The primary mouse chondrocytes were cultured in complete medium with 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS) cell culture supplement at 37℃ and 5% CO2. The medium was refreshed every 3 days. 4-hydroxytamoxifen was added to the medium at 4µM after the first passage for 3 days to induce the conditional knockout of Ddrgk1 in the cells.

To evaluate chondrocyte differentiation, 1.5 × 105 primary chondrocytes were resuspended in 10 µl medium and then seeded in 24-well plates. After 1 h of adhesion at 37℃, 500 µl of DMEM/F12 medium with 1% ITS cell culture supplement, 5% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptoycin was added into each well. After a 24-hour incubation 37℃ and 5% CO2, cells were treated with or without 6.25nM thapsigargin (Tg) and 0.6µM kira6. The medium was refreshed every 2 days. Following 7 days of incubation, the cell masses were fixed and staining with Alcian blue for 24 h at room temperature.

Primary mouse chondrocytes were cultured for 7 days, and total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors. The extracted protein was quantified using BCA method and incubated with loading buffer at 95℃ for 10 min. Equal protein amounts were loaded onto a 4–20% SurePAGE™ gel for electrophoresis and then transferred to a 0.22 μm PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in TBS-Tween buffer (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Following TBST washes, it was then incubated with the primary antibodies (Table 1) at 4℃ overnight. The next day, membranes were washed with TBST for 3 times and incubated with anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Protein bands were then detected using a LI-COR Odyssey fluorescence imaging system.

The construction of the LSI surgery model closely replicates the process of human intervertebral disc degeneration, including degenerative manifestations such as reduced disc height, decreased vacuoles in the nucleus pulposus, ectopic bone formation in the cartilage endplate, and rupture of the annulus fibrosus [40]. The model was constructed as follows: 3-month-old WT and Ddrgk1cKO mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed in a prone position on the operating table. Then, lower back skin of mouse was prepared and sterilized. After the L5 vertebra was located, a longitudinal incision was made to expose the lumbar vertebrae. The posterior paraspinal muscles were dissected to reveal the L3-L5 spinous processes, which were resected along with the supraspinous and interspinous ligaments. Following suturing and sterilizing the skin, the mice were returned to their original environment and observed on alternative days. At the conclusion of the experiment, spinal tissue was obtained using the aforementioned method for further experimentation.

At the time of euthanasia, the spines in mice were isolated and fixed in 4% PFA solution for 1 day. Spine samples were then decalcificated in 10% ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid solution for 2–3 weeks, dehydrated by graded ethanol and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were then stained by H&E or Safranin O-Fast Green staining solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the sections were mounted, images were captured using the slide scanner for further analysis.

Cartilage endplate thickness was calculated as the average thickness of five equal parts of endplate along the sagittal plane of spine. The cartilage proportion in endplate was determined using Image J software in Safranin O-Fast Green stained sections. The mean value from obtained from 3 to 5 intervertebral disc segments per mouse sample served as a data point.

Intervertebral disc height change was assessed by the disc height index. Specifically, the height of intervertebral disc and adjacent vertebral bodies were measured at the midline and 25% of the disc width bilaterally. The disc height index was then determined as the average of three disc height measurements divided by the average height of the adjacent vertebral bodies.

TUNEL staining was employed to identify apoptotic signals in intervertebral discs. Briefly, paraffin sections of spinal tissue were treated with xylene and graded ethanol to dewax and rehydrate. Then the sections underwent proteinase K treatment for 30 min at 37℃, followed by three PBS washes. After permeabilized by Triton X-100 for 20 min, the sections were incubated with a TdT-dUTP reaction mixture (2:29 ratio) for 2 h at 37℃. To block endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were immersed into a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 15 min. Then converter-POD solution was added for 30 min incubation at 37℃. After treated with DAB working solution, cells exhibiting TUNEL-positive nuclear staining were identified as undergoing apoptosis. The apoptosis rate at specific region was determined by dividing the number of apoptotic cells by the total cell count.

To evaluate the importance of DDRGK1 in intervertebral disc development early postnatally, we generated Ddrgk1cKO mice to induce the knockout of Ddrgk1 in nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate cells after birth (Fig. 1A, Figure S1). We administered Ddrgk1cKO and wild-type (WT) mice with tamoxifen (75 mg/kg, i.p., P1-P3) and examined the structural changes from 5 d to 38 d (Fig. 1B). Safranin O-Fast Green staining images showed significant abnormalities in the spinal structure of Ddrgk1cKO mice (Fig. 1C). First, the WT mice exhibited a continuous increase in vertebral height over time. Initially, there was no notable difference between Ddrgk1cKO and WT mice at 5 d and 8d. However, the vertebral growth in Ddrgk1cKO mice was greatly decelerated from 12 d and almost stagnated at 18 d and 22d. Significant reductions were all detected in vertebral height of Ddrgk1cKO mice at 12 d, 22 d and 30 d compared to the WT group (Fig. 1, D, G and J). Second, Ddrgk1cKO mice exhibited structural similarities with WT mice at 5 d and 8 d, with no notable macroscopic alterations in the nucleus pulposus, cartilage endplate and annulus fibrosus. However, the nucleus pulposus in Ddrgk1cKO mice began to deform at 12 d, transitioning from an oval to a rectangular at 22 d and eventually to an irregular shape at 30d. Additionally, the cartilage endplate displayed structural distortion and disordered cartilage layers in Ddrgk1cKO mice from 12 d (Fig. 1C). Nevertheless, no significant differences in disc height were detected at 12 d, 22 d and 30 d between Ddrgk1cKO and WT mice (Fig. 1, E, H and K). Moreover, the disc height index of Ddrgk1cKO mice significantly increased that of WT mice at the same time points considering the changes in vertebral height (Fig. 1, F, I and L). To validate the RNA-seq findings, we isolated primary chondrocytes from WT and Ddrgk1cKO mice to verify changes in gene transcription and protein expression via RT-qPCR and WT assays (Figure S5 and Figure S6). Statistical analysis showed that Ddrgk1cKO mice exhibited significantly lower positive rates of IRE1α (Fig. 8D). For instance, the IRE1α positive rates were 51.07 ± 5.51% and 22.58 ± 5.84% in nucleus pulposus cells as well as 46.40 ± 8.79% and 18.68 ± 3.33% in cartilage endplate cells of WT and Ddrgk1cKO mice at 30d. The expression of ATF4 was significantly increased in the nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate cells at all time points after Ddrgk1 conditional knockout (e.g., 7.39 ± 3.38% and 18.07 ± 5.36% in nucleus pulposus, 6.36 ± 1.65% and 15.74 ± 2.60% in cartilage endplate of WT and Ddrgk1cKO mice at 22 d) (Fig. 8E). Except no significance of BiP expression was found in the nucleus pulposus after LSI modeling, significant increases of BiP expression were detected in other time points of nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate in Ddrgk1cKO mice (e.g., 6.89 ± 2.92% and 21.03 ± 2.35% in nucleus pulposus, 2.28 ± 0.56% and 9.06 ± 3.08% in cartilage endplate of WT and Ddrgk1cKO mice) (Fig. 8F). These findings indicated that conditional knockout of Ddrgk1 could reduce the stability of IRE1α and induce ER stress during intervertebral disc development and degeneration.

 Our study demonstrated that DDRGK1 as a critical component of UFMylation is essential for preserving the tissue structure and cellular phenotype of the cartilage endplate and nucleus pulposus as well as supporting vertebral growth during the developing phase. We found that Ddrgk1 conditional knockout in both early or late postnatal phase could lead to severe structural abnormalities in the cartilage endplate and nucleus pulposus and significant disc degeneration in adulthood (Figure 1-3). Notably, significant structural abnormalities were observed in both nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate inDdrgk1cKO mice in early postnatal phase, but no significant reduction was detected in disc height in Ddrgk1cKO mice (Figure 1 and 2). However, distinct cartilage endplate defect emerged as a key contributor to the significant decrease in disc height of Ddrgk1cKO mice in the late postnatal phase along with pronounced degenerative phenotype (Figure 3). RNA sequencing indicated the upregulation of apoptosis-related genes after Ddrgk1 conditional knockout (Figure 5E). Immunohistochemical staining confirmed that Ddrgk1 conditional knockout reduced cell proliferation (Ki67) and increased apoptosis (TUNEL) in nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate cells (Figure 6). Interestingly,Ddrgk1 conditional knockout did not reduce cell density in nucleus pulposus at 12d and in cartilage endplate during early postnatal phase, but did so significantly in later phase (Figure S7); meanwhile, significant changes in ECM indicators were observed in both phases (Figure 7 and Figure S8), which implied that DDRGK1 may regulate cell fate and matrix turnover via distinct pathways. The deficiency of the components in UFM1 system has been shown to impede cartilage development. Homozygous loss-of-function mutations inDDRGK1 or heterozygous mutations in UFM1-specific protease 2 gene (UFSP2)could lead to spondylo-epi-metaphyseal dysplasias (SEMD), a chondrodysplasia characterized by abnormal development of spine, epiphysis and metaphysis [34, 47, 48]. Patients with SEMD may present with scoliosis, spinal compression fractures, flattened vertebral bodies, and insufficient mineralization of long bones [49]. Furthermore, a loss-of-function mutation in UFSP2 has also been reported to be associated with Beukes hip dysplasia, characterized by proximal femoral deformities and severe osteoarthropathy [50]. Further researches revealed that conditional knockout of Ddrgk1 in mesenchymal cells leads to progressively limb shortening and joint abnormalities, while mice with Ddrgk1 conditional knockout in osteoblasts did not show apparent phenotype of limbs [34, 37]. Collectively, these studies highlight the critical role of the UFM1 system in regulating cartilage development.

 ER stress and the activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) play crucial roles in the abnormal development and accelerated degeneration of intervertebral discs caused by Ddrgk1 knockout. Our results showed significant enrichment of gene sets related to the ER unfolded protein response in intervertebral disc cells after Ddrgk1 knockout. Immunohistochemical staining revealed significantly increased levels of ATF4 and BiP, indicating ER stress induction and UPR pathway activation following Ddrgk1 knockout. Interestingly, we found that while the transcription level of Ire1 was upregulated afterDdrgk1 knockout (Figure S4C), its tissue protein level was significantly decreased compared to WT mice (Figure 8D), which was further validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot assays in primary chondrocytes in vitro (Figure S5B and Figure S6A). Besides, CHX assay indicated that proteasome-dependent pathway could be the principal pathway for IRE1α degradation (Figure S9). We also observed that modulation of ER stress through IRE1α pathway could partially alleviate the degenerative phenotype afterDdrgk1 conditional knockout in vitro (Figure S10). These findings suggested reduced IRE1α protein stability triggered ER stress following Ddrgk1conditional knockout.

 Current studies believe that the transient and moderate activation of IRE1α/XBP1s can reduce ER stress and mitigate cell damage, playing a positive regulatory role in cell survival [51]. By contrast, persistent ER stress and excessive activation of IRE1α can trigger pathways such as RIDD or JNK, inducing cell apoptosis [52]. Liu et al. found that DDRGK1 can regulate IRE1α stability by interacting with its kinase domain. They also found that loss of DDRGK1 in cancer cells and hematopoietic stem cells can accelerate IRE1α degradation and PERK activation, leading to apoptosis [39]. Additionally, in our previous studies, we found that DDRGK1 deficiency in chondrocytes has been shown to reduce IRE1α UFMylation, increase ubiquitin-mediated IRE1α degradation, decrease XBP1s expression, and activate the PERK/ATF4/CHOP-induced apoptotic pathway [38]. This study further investigated the alteration at both transcriptional and protein levels of IRE1α in discs after Ddrgk1 conditional knockout, while the XBP1 activity in disc cells was inferred by the activation of IRE1α/UPR signaling pathway and our prior work without direct quantification in this study. However, the mechanism of interaction between DDRGK1 and IRE1α remains controversial. Cai et al. reported upregulation of IRE1α and significant activation of the XBP1s pathway following Ddrgk1 knockout in intestinal cells [53]. Furthermore, in B cells, Ddrgk1 knockout has been found to significantly increase the expression and activation levels of IRE1α/XBP1 [54].

 SOX9, a member of the SRY-related high-mobility group (HMG) box transcription factor family, is a unique master transcriptional factor expressed in the process of embryonic mesenchymal condensation for cartilage development and bone formation [55, 56]. SOX9 binds to the HMG sites in DNA to activate target genes such as Col2a1 and Acan, which encode important components of the cartilage ECM [57, 58]. Notably, SOX9 is subjected to degradation by the ubiquitin-proteasome system [59, 60]. Egunsola et al. reported UFMylation mediated by DDRGK1 inhibited the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of SOX9, thereby enhancing its stability [34]. Our immunohistochemical staining showed a significant decrease of SOX9 expression following Ddrgk1 conditional knockout, which was validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot assays in primary chondrocytes from Ddrgk1cKOmice. Besides, up-regulation of MMP3, MMP9, MMP13, COL1A1 and COL10A1, coupled with down-regulation of Aggrecan and COL2A1, consistently indicated abnormal cartilage catabolism and matrix phenotype disruption after Ddrgk1 conditional knockout (Figure 7, S5, S6 and S8). These findings indicated that the absence of DDRGK1 disrupted the UFMylation of SOX9, thereby diminishing its stability and impairing chondrogenic activity and cell maintenance in vivo.

 Admittedly, this research had several limitations which warrant further investigation. First, the present work focused mainly on the role of DDRGK1 in nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate. However, we also observed some changes in the annulus fibrosus after Ddrgk1 conditional knockout that cannot be fully explained based on current findings. Thus, we planned to conduct further investigations into the specific role of DDRGK1 in the annulus fibrosus in future studies. Second, while RNA sequencing revealed transcriptional changes in disc cells, it lacked the spatial resolution to distinguish among the nucleus pulposus, cartilage endplate, and annulus fibrosus. Therefore, we planned to employ spatial transcriptomics to further analyze the intervertebral disc region of Ddrgk1cKO mice.

 In summary, our work provides detailed insights into intervertebral disc development and degeneration inDdrgk1cKOmice at the histological, transcriptional and protein levels. Conditional knockout ofDdrgk1 induces ER stress, accelerates ECM degradation and triggers apoptosis in nucleus pulposus and cartilage endplate cells, thereby profoundly exacerbating disc degeneration in both early and late postnatal phase. Collectively, this study underscores the pivotal role of DDRGK1 in preserving the normal cellularity and structure of intervertebral discs and offers a theoretical foundation for DDRGK1-targeting in treating degenerative disc diseases.
