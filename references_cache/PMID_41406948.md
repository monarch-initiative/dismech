# Plasma growth-associated protein 43 correlates with synaptic loss in Alzheimer's disease.
**Authors:** Lan G, Li B, Wang M, Barve A, Colin M, Buée L, Zhang L, Jiang M, Yang J, Li A, He Z, Zhou X, Zhu Y, Cai Y, Sun P, Liu L, Li J, Chen L, Fang L, Wang Y, Li M, Chen X, Shi D, Ye C, Fan X, Wang Q, Zhou L, Liu Z, Han Y, Wang L, Cheng G, Guan Y, Ni R, Richetin K, Xie F, Guo T
**Journal:** Cell Rep Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xcrm.2025.102508](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2025.102508)

## Content

Synaptic loss is a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease (AD) but lacks robust blood-based biomarkers. We investigate growth-associated protein 43 (GAP-43), previously identified as a synaptic candidate in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Postmortem proteomic profiling of brain-derived extracellular vesicles (n = 21) highlights GAP-43 as a central hub within synaptic protein networks co-depleted in AD and closely linked with proteins enriched in immune-, metabolic-, and synaptic-related modules. In two well-characterized Chinese AD cohorts (n = 785), we measure plasma GAP-43, including subgroups with CSF biomarkers (n = 72), SV2A-PET (positron emission tomography) (n = 85), tau-PET (n = 280), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (n = 595). Plasma GAP-43 correlates with CSF GAP-43, neurofilament light, and both baseline and longitudinal synaptic PET. Elevated plasma GAP-43 is associated with greater tau aggregation, faster brain atrophy, and accelerated cognitive decline, particularly among cognitively unimpaired individuals. These findings support plasma GAP-43 as a promising biomarker of early synaptic degeneration and a potential tool for identifying individuals at risk of AD progression.

Dementia is the leading cause of disability and death among older adults and currently affects over 55 million people worldwide.1 Approximately 60%–70% of dementia cases are attributed to Alzheimer’s disease (AD), marked by the deposition of extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and intraneuronal tau tangles composed of phosphorylated tau (p-tau), and then dementia with Lewy bodies and frontotemporal lobar degeneration.1 Studies of postmortem and animal models implicate synapses in disease progression due to the spread of disease-associated pathological proteins, such as aggregated tau2,3 and α-synuclein seeds.4,5 On the other hand, the abnormal accumulation of toxic proteins in synapses can directly trigger neuronal and synaptic dysfunction or degeneration, resulting in progressive cognitive decline.6,7,8 Evidence from the brains of AD patients suggests that synapse loss is most strongly correlated with reduced cognitive function.9,10,11

Developing robust biomarkers of synaptic phenotypes can directly contribute to understanding the role of synaptic degeneration in AD and neurodegenerative disorders. Ongoing clinical trials and studies aimed at identifying synaptic biomarkers primarily focus on distinct synaptic proteins in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and positron emission tomography (PET) ligands that bind to presynaptic vesicles (SV2A) or postsynaptic receptors (mGluR5).5,8,12 With advances in detection techniques, robust blood-based measurements of pathological proteins have now been considered scalable tools for evaluating neuropathological alterations in several neurodegenerative diseases, as CSF collections and PET scans are invasive and expensive.13,14,15,16 Nevertheless, blood levels of common synaptic proteins, such as neurogranin17 and synaptosome-associated protein 25 (SNAP-25),18 have not been corroborated as reliable indicators of brain pathology due to peripheral confounds and detection limits. Recently, Oeckl19,20 reported a novel mass spectrometry-based assay capable of quantifying β-synuclein in blood closely related to neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment in patients with AD and frontotemporal lobar degeneration. However, no study has provided direct evidence for the relationship between biofluid synaptic proteins and brain synaptic degeneration.

In previous works, we21,22,23 and others24,25 have reported that CSF growth-associated protein 43 (GAP-43), a presynaptic membrane protein highly expressed in the brain (www.proteinatlas.org), is a promising biomarker of synaptic degeneration, closely associated with microglial reactivity, tau accumulation, brain atrophy, glucose metabolism, and cognitive deficits. These observations suggest that CSF GAP-43 may be a powerful tool for predicting disease progression in clinical trials. However, the role of blood GAP-43 has not yet been investigated in AD and related dementias. To address this gap, the present study provides a large-scale evaluation of plasma GAP-43 in two independent and well-characterized Chinese aging cohorts: the Greater-Bay-Area Healthy Aging Brain Study (GHABS) and the Chinese Preclinical Alzheimer’s Disease Study (Huashan CPAS). We first depict enriched protein interaction networks for GAP-43 in brain-derived extracellular vesicles (BDEVs) from AD patients and controls, exploring biological pathways linked to GAP-43 dysfunction. By integrating multimodal fluid and imaging biomarkers, we then comprehensively characterize plasma GAP-43, examining its associations with synaptic loss, AD-related pathological features, brain atrophy, and cognitive decline. Our findings suggest that plasma GAP-43 may serve as a cost-effective and clinically feasible biomarker of early synaptic degeneration, providing complementary information to established AD biomarkers.

To investigate the cerebral origin and biological underpinnings of GAP-43-related alterations in AD, we analyzed BDEVs isolated from the prefrontal cortex of 4 healthy controls (HCs) and 17 patients with AD. We classified them into different Braak stages: I–II (n = 3), III–IV (n = 4), V–VI (n = 10).

To further determine the correlation between plasma GAP-43 and AD pathologies, we subsequently measured plasma GAP-43 concentrations and other AD-related biomarkers in 785 older adults from two well-characterized observational cohorts (Table 1). To study the GAP-43-related alterations in the brain of AD patients, we analyzed BDEVs isolated from the prefrontal cortex of HCs and neuropathologically confirmed AD patients across Braak stages I–VI (Figure 1A). BDEVs reflect molecular cargo actively released by neurons and glial cells and are increasingly recognized as carriers of disease-specific signatures. Quantitative proteomic profiling of BDEVs revealed significant alterations associated with AD (Figures 1B and S1). In particular, multiple synaptic proteins were markedly downregulated in AD patients at Braak V–VI stages relative to controls, notably GAP-43 and other synaptic vesicle components. Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated a pronounced depletion of pathways related to synaptic function in AD-derived BDEVs, alongside enrichment of pathways linked to immune regulation, mitochondrial and metabolic activity, extracellular matrix organization, and endoplasmic reticulum function (Figure 1C). These findings indicate a disease-associated shift in BDEV cargo, characterized by the loss of synaptic elements and the concurrent gain of proteins related to immune, metabolic, and structural processes. Furthermore, functional network-based analysis identified GAP-43 as a central hub within the dysregulated BDEV proteome (Figure 1D). Positively correlated proteins formed a synaptic module that was coordinately downregulated in AD. In contrast, negatively correlated proteins segregated into distinct clusters, which were enriched for immune, metabolic, and structural functions. Across all the individuals, the BDEV levels of GAP-43 were significantly reduced in AD patients compared to those in controls (p = 0.001) (Figure 1F). Moreover, GAP-43 abundance negatively correlated with BDEV levels of amyloid precursor protein (APP) (r = −0.54, p = 0.012) and positively correlated with pathological markers tau (MAPT) (r = 0.76, p < 0.001) and α-synuclein (SNCA) (r = 0.63, p = 0.002) (Figure 1G). Likewise, GAP-43 showed strong positive correlations with multiple key synaptic vesicle proteins, including syntaxin-1 (STX1; r = 0.75, p < 0.001), complexin-2 (CPLX2; r = 0.74, p < 0.001), AP2B1 (r = 0.64, p = 0.002), and SNAP-25 (r = 0.57, p = 0.007) (Figure 1H). Together, these results indicate that GAP-43 is markedly depleted in prefrontal cortex BDEVs from AD patients and that its reduction is tightly associated with a broad loss of synaptic protein cargo and elevated levels of AD-related pathological markers, reflecting a defining feature of synaptic dysfunction in AD.

To evaluate plasma GAP-43 as a potential peripheral biomarker of brain synaptic degeneration, we first assessed its relationship with two well-established synaptic biomarkers, CSF GAP-43 and SV2A-PET, in a pilot study.

In a subset of the GHABS cohort comprising 12 CU, 17 MCI, and 43 dementia individuals with paired CSF samples (Table S1), log10-transformed plasma GAP-43 levels were positively correlated with CSF GAP-43 (Pearson’s r = 0.44, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A), which remained significant within MCI (Pearson’s r = 0.59, p = 0.014) and dementia (Pearson’s r = 0.47, p = 0.002) subgroups (Figure S2). We then examined the correlation between plasma GAP-43 and brain synaptic density, indexed by SV2A-PET, in a subset of the Huashan cohort, including 30 CU, 24 MCI, and 31 dementia individuals. Across PET Braak-like stages, higher plasma GAP-43 levels were inversely correlated with SV2A-PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) in Braak I–II regions (Spearman’s rho = −0.28, p = 0.010; standardized β (βstd) = −0.29 [95% confidence interval (ci), −0.49 to −0.08]) (Figure 2C). Voxel-wise analysis further localized this negative correlation to the temporal cortex (Figure 2D). The results of voxel-wise analysis did not survive multiple-comparison corrections, which may be due to the limited sample size of the SV2A PET study. Among 41 participants having longitudinal SV2A-PET imaging with a mean (SD) follow-up of 1.4 (0.5) years, elevated plasma GAP-43 at baseline was significantly correlated with greater declines in SV2A-PET signal over time in Braak I–II regions (Spearman’s rho = −0.43, p = 0.005; βstd = −0.48 [95% ci, −0.76 to −0.21]) (Figure 2E). After stratifying by clinical diagnosis, these correlations with baseline and longitudinal SV2A-PET remained significant only in CU participants (p = 0.005 and 0.001, respectively). No significant correlation was observed in Braak III–IV or V–VI regions (Figure S3).

Together, the significant association between plasma GAP-43 and synaptic loss, as reflected by two well-established synaptic biomarkers (CSF GAP-43 and SV2A synaptic PET), supports the notion that plasma GAP-43 may be a potential blood biomarker for detecting synaptic loss.

To understand the characteristics of plasma GAP-43 as well as how it relates to AD core pathologies, we first investigated the relations of plasma GAP-43 concentrations with demographics and plasma levels of Aβ42/Aβ40, p-tau, brain-derived tau, N-terminal tau, NfL, GFAP, and sTREM2 in two large cohorts.

Demographic variables, including age, gender, APOE-ε4 status, education, and clinical diagnosis, were included as predictors in one multivariate model. In the GHABS cohort, plasma GAP-43 levels were positively associated with age (βstd = 0.47 [95% ci, 0.40–0.54], p < 0.001) and male gender (βstd = 0.46 [95% ci, 0.32–0.61], p < 0.001) (Figures 3A and 3B). Compared to CU participants, plasma GAP-43 levels were higher in patients with MCI (βstd = 0.23 [95% ci, 0.05–0.41], p = 0.013) and dementia (βstd = 0.26 [95% ci, 0.01–0.52], p = 0.042) (Figure 3C). Similar trends were replicated in the Huashan cohort (Figures 3D–3F), where plasma GAP-43 increases were associated with older ages (βstd = 0.54 [95% ci, 0.43–0.66], p < 0.001), male gender (βstd = 0.34 [95% ci, 0.11–0.57], p = 0.004), MCI (βstd = 0.35 [95% ci, 0.06–0.64], p = 0.005), and dementia (βstd = 0.24 [95% ci, 0.01–0.46], p = 0.035). No significant differences in plasma GAP-43 levels were observed between the MCI and dementia groups in either cohort. The effects of age and gender persisted across different diagnostic groups, except for the male effect in dementia (Table S2). When correlating different log10-transformed plasma biomarkers with each other, GAP-43 showed significant positive correlations with all biomarkers except for Aβ42/Aβ40. The strongest correlation was observed with NfL in both GHABS (Pearson’s r = 0.47, p < 0.001) and Huashan (Pearson’s r = 0.55, p < 0.001) cohorts (Figures 3G and 3H). These strong correlations between plasma GAP-43 and tau, as well as neurodegeneration biomarkers, further support the notion that plasma GAP-43 is a potential synaptic biomarker. Additional receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses to identify CI participants revealed that plasma GAP-43, when combined with established plasma biomarkers such as GFAP and p-tau217, exhibited improved predictive performance compared to individual biomarkers alone in both cohorts (Figure S5).

To understand the relationship between plasma GAP-43 and AD core imaging biomarkers, we subsequently examined the correlations between plasma GAP-43 and Aβ and tau PET imaging. No significant correlation was observed between plasma GAP-43 and Aβ-PET in either cohort (Figure S7). In contrast, higher plasma GAP-43 levels were positively correlated with tau-PET SUVR in Braak I (Spearman’s rho = 0.25, p = 0.002), Braak III–IV (Spearman’s rho = 0.28, p < 0.001), and Braak V–VI (Spearman’s rho = 0.17, p = 0.046) regions in the GHABS cohort (Figure 5A). Ultimately, we want to determine whether plasma GAP-43 can predict neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. To end this, we assessed the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between plasma GAP-43 and brain structure and cognitive function (Figure 6), as summarized in Tables S3 and S4. Across 68 FreeSurfer-defined brain regions, plasma GAP-43 positively associated with cMD in 32 cortical areas, predominantly in the temporal and occipital lobes (Figure 6B). Similarly, in the Huashan cohort, higher plasma GAP-43 was associated with lower HCV (βstd = −0.25 [95% ci, −0.41 to −0.10], p < 0.001) and temporal-metaROI cortical thickness (βstd = −0.29 [95% ci, −0.44 to −0.13], p < 0.001). The significant associations between plasma GAP-43 and microstructural and macrostructural alterations in gray matter, as well as cognitive decline, support plasma GAP-43 as a promising blood synaptic biomarker for detecting and monitoring neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.

Despite extensive efforts to identify alternative peripheral biomarkers reflective of brain pathology, no robust blood-based biomarker has yet been established to capture synaptic degeneration. Although previous studies have reported associations between plasma p-tau181 or GFAP and SV2A-PET measures of synaptic density,26,27 these plasma biomarkers primarily reflect tau pathology or astrogliosis and their elevations are most pronounced in AD. Thus, they may not accurately reflect synaptic-specific alterations, particularly during the early stages of the disease. This study focused on GAP-43, a presynaptic membrane protein detectable in CSF as a presynaptic biomarker. Our postmortem proteomic profiling of BDEVs revealed reduced GAP-43 levels in AD brains, showing strong correlations with APP, tau, and multiple synaptic vesicle proteins, indicating broad synaptic compromise. Notably, plasma GAP-43 positively correlated with worsening synaptic loss, as measured by SV2A-PET and CSF GAP-43, and with axonal damage, indexed by CSF and plasma NfL. In two well-characterized independent cohorts, we observed consistent associations between plasma GAP-43 and age and gender, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally. Plasma GAP-43 elevations were further related to early tau accumulations and predicted disease progression, including brain atrophy and cognitive decline, especially among CU participants. Together, this study suggests the possibilities for plasma GAP-43 as a promising blood biomarker for early synaptic degeneration, indicating potential utility in high-risk screening and disease monitoring, contributing to the ongoing establishment of a reliable blood-based biomarker framework for AD.

Our proteomic analyses of postmortem BDEVs provide valuable insights into the biological relevance of GAP-43 in AD. Although GAP-43 abundance showed only modest changes in advanced AD, pathway enrichment analysis revealed widespread synaptic dysregulation. Functionally grouped gene sets uncovered a marked downregulation of synaptic regulation modules in AD-derived BDEVs, alongside upregulation of modules related to immune response, endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, extracellular matrix, and peptidase. Although GAP-43 was not a leading-edge gene in these modules, it showed strong positive correlations with key synaptic proteins—such as SYT7, SNAP-25, STX1B, CPLX2, SNCA, SNCG, and AP2B1—all of which have been previously linked to synaptic pathology in AD. Conversely, GAP-43 exhibits negative correlations with proteins enriched in immune/metabolic modules (e.g., APP, APOD, CSTB, FLNA, FTL, ANXA1, and VCAN), further reinforcing its involvement in AD pathophysiology. These findings align with previous reports showing reduced neuronal exosomal GAP-43 levels in AD patients.28,29,30 For instance, Goetzl28 reported lower GAP-43 levels in plasma-derived neuronal EVs from patients with AD. At the same time, Jia and Eitan29,30 independently demonstrated reductions in circulating neuronal exosomal GAP-43 in preclinical and symptomatic AD. Animal and in vitro models further support these results. Cai31 demonstrated reduced GAP-43 levels in brain tissue and extracellular vesicles (EV) from 5×FAD mice, with EV-mediated delivery of GAP-43 mRNA improving cognition. Moreover, Hong32 reported longitudinal associations between plasma exosomal GAP-43 levels and clinical progression in Parkinson’s disease, suggesting its broader relevance across neurodegenerative disorders. These data indicate that GAP-43 is a key component of a coordinated synaptic remodeling process in AD. Despite moderate expression differences, GAP-43’s strong co-expression with synaptic proteins supports its role as a functionally important marker of early synaptopathy. These findings reinforce the utility of GAP-43 in tracking synaptic alterations and encourage its consideration in future biomarker panels for AD and related disorders.

We and others have previously shown that CSF GAP-43 is a presynaptic biomarker whose increase follows early tau pathology (i.e., soluble p-tau in CSF) and predicts disease progression, characterized by more aggressive hypometabolism, brain atrophy, and cognitive decline.21,22,33 Nevertheless, the association between plasma GAP-43 and neuropathology remained unclear. The first objective of this study was to determine the relationship between plasma GAP-43 and synaptic degeneration in the brain. Intriguingly, our data suggested a moderate positive correlation between GAP-43 in paired plasma and CSF samples. More importantly, in vivo measurements of synaptic density with SV2A-PET imaging provided robust evidence for the associations between plasma GAP-43 and both baseline and longitudinal synaptic alterations, particularly in entorhinal and hippocampal areas (Braak I–II). It is to be expected, as numerous postmortem works have indicated that the hippocampus was the site with the most significant neuronal and synaptic degeneration during AD progression.34,35 There was a 20%–44% reduction of synapses in the outer molecular layer of the dentate gyrus in patients with early AD,36,37 as well as a reduction of 55% in mild AD and 18% in MCI in the cornu ammonis 1 (CA1) region.38 This aligns with previous studies reporting the earliest and most significant reduction in hippocampal SV2A binding in MCI and AD patients.39,40,41 Most notably, the correlation between plasma GAP-43 and synaptic loss was only observed in CU participants, suggesting its utility in preclinical disease stages. The strongest correlation between plasma GAP-43 and the typical axonal marker NfL further supported its pathophysiological relevance. These findings suggest that plasma GAP-43 may be an indicator of neuropathological processes involved in early synaptic loss preceding overt neurodegeneration.

Another important finding of the current study was that plasma GAP-43 demonstrated significant associations with brain atrophy and cognitive decline. For neurodegenerative measures, plasma GAP-43 was most strongly associated with cMD, a sensitive indicator of capturing microstructurally isotropic changes in gray matter.42,43 Our recent work showed that elevated mean diffusivity of cortical gray matter was superior to cortical thinning and gray matter atrophy in detecting early brain structural alterations in AD and suspected non-Alzheimer’s pathophysiology.44 Elevated cMD is hypothesized to reflect the breakdown of microstructural barriers, perhaps due to the loss of synaptic and cellular integrity.45,46,47 These findings further increase the likelihood of plasma GAP-43 as an early biomarker for detecting synaptic loss in the brain. In contrast to cMD, there was an age-dependent effect of the cross-sectional associations between plasma GAP-43 and downstream macrostructural gray matter indicators (e.g., HCV and cortical thickness). Although plasma GAP-43 levels increased modestly with age, our multivariate modeling analysis indicated that they could predict more rapid hippocampal atrophy and cognitive decline over time after adjusting for age, suggesting that GAP-43 reflects synaptic pathology rather than normal aging. The predictive effect was primarily maintained in CU participants, implying involvement of GAP-43-associated synaptic changes in downstream neurodegeneration and cognitive decline during the early stage. Future studies in larger, independent cohorts will be necessary to compare the predictive effects of plasma GPA-43 and other plasma biomarkers, determining whether it provides additive predictive value beyond current plasma biomarker frameworks.

In addition, our study also demonstrated that plasma GAP-43 increases were strongly associated with older age, cognitive impairment, and male gender. Subtle synaptic dysfunction and loss, rather than neuronal loss, are early hallmarks of brain aging closely linked to age-related cognitive decline in human and animal models.48,49 Notably, although differences in age between diagnostic groups may confound interpretation, our multivariate analyses highlighted the independent effect of cognitive impairment. This suggests that there are both age-dependent and disease-related alterations in plasma GAP-43 levels. It supports the potential utility of GAP-43 as a biomarker of disease stage beyond normal aging, while also underlining the importance of considering age in future GAP-43 studies. However, the strong association between GAP-43 and age raises important considerations for its utility as a diagnostic biomarker. Although older ages are associated with synaptic loss, as reflected by CSF GAP-43 and SV2A PET, as well as plasma GAP-43, age-related increases in plasma GAP-43 may overlap with disease-related changes, reducing the discriminative value of plasma GAP-43 when employed alone, particularly in older populations. This implies that plasma GAP-43 may be most informative when interpreted in age-adjusted models or in combination with complementary biomarkers that are less affected by age. Additionally, while no human studies have reported gender-specific differences in plasma GAP-43, animal studies demonstrated that gender hormones may play a role in regulating GAP-43 expression. For example, male rats showed higher GAP-43 mRNA expression across several brain regions compared with females, and hormonal treatments indicated that estrogen reduced, while androgens increased, GAP-43 mRNA levels.50,51 Furthermore, testosterone in male AD mice could enhance synaptic density and protein expression in the hippocampus.52,53 These findings suggest that circulating gender hormones may modulate synaptic function and GAP-43 expression, thereby contributing to the observed gender-related differences, but further studies in diverse cohorts are warranted. Moreover, the absence of a significant increase from MCI to dementia suggests that the rise in plasma GAP-43 may primarily occur in the pre-dementia or early pathological stages, reaching a plateau before dementia onset.

One proteomic-based study identified a plasma signature of synaptic proteins that tracked with tau accumulations and dementia progression.49 Previous works on GAP-43 also strongly imply its relevance to tau-related synaptic dysfunction and loss.21,22,25 CSF GAP-43 elevations were associated with greater Aβ-related tau accumulation and spread in AD.25 This aligns with the positive correlation between plasma GAP-43 and tau-PET in the early tau-accumulating entorhinal cortex in this study. The correlation was preserved only in CU participants, which may be attributed to the fact that plasma GAP-43 levels already tended to plateau in MCI cases. Thus, our data support that tau pathology may be closely linked to early GAP-43-related synaptic degeneration, which may precede irreversible neuronal loss. Interestingly, we also observed a significant gender effect on baseline and longitudinal changes of plasma GAP-43 in CU participants. Although the underlying mechanism remains unclear, this may reflect gender differences in synaptic plasticity or resilience, which could potentially contribute to the observed gender differences in AD incidence. Given GAP-43’s role in axonal growth and synaptic remodeling,54,55 further investigation is warranted.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that plasma GAP-43 may serve as a sensitive marker of subtle synaptic degeneration, particularly within early vulnerable brain regions, possibly linked to brain aging and tau-relevant toxicity. Plasma GAP-43 provides a cost-effective alternative to CSF or PET synaptic biomarker tests in clinical trials and research frameworks for AD or other neurodegenerative diseases. This study offers a deeper understanding of how synaptic loss contributes to neuropathological changes, thereby underscoring the importance of developing synapse-targeted therapeutic strategies to prevent downstream neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.

The major strength of this study is characterizing plasma GAP-43 levels in two large and well-characterized Chinese AD cohorts, encompassing both community-based and memory clinic-recruited populations. Together, our data comprehensively demonstrate the relationships between plasma GAP-43 and cerebral synaptic loss, Aβ and tau accumulations, neurodegeneration, and cognitive function. To the best of our knowledge, no study has directly investigated the association between plasma synaptic protein and PET-assessed synaptic loss in living humans. However, several limitations should be noted here. Study limitations include the relatively small sample size of participants with PET imaging and follow-up data, which may limit statistical reliability. Future studies that collect plasma near death and perform neuropathological examinations in matched postmortem brains would help elucidate the links between plasma GAP-43 and neuropathological alterations in normal aging and neurodegenerative diseases. This plasma biomarker should be further validated in larger and independent populations, especially those with non-AD neurodegenerative dementia and psychiatric disorders that may affect synaptic function and integrity. Moreover, the fact that two primary cohorts in this study consisted of Han Chinese aging adults may limit the generalizability of our findings to people of other races or ethnicities, given that plasma biomarkers may exhibit population-specific variation.56,57 Efforts are underway to replicate and validate our findings in more diverse cohorts, including European and North American populations. Last, as this study is observational in nature, causal relationships cannot be inferred based on these findings.

The GHABS cohort (NCT06183658) is a prospective and longitudinal study initiated in 2021, consisting of community-dwelling older adults. GHABS aims to track early pathological features and evolution patterns of sporadic Alzheimer’s disease using biofluid and neuroimaging biomarkers. The participants are enrolled via posters and lectures in the local community around the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater-Bay-Area of southern China. Details on recruitment, exclusion, and inclusion criteria of the GHABS cohort have been reported previously.59 All GHABS participants aged between 55 and 90 years were classified as CU, MCI, or dementia, according to the standard protocol of the ADNI cohort.60 Briefly, CU participants had a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 0 with normal MMSE (the Chinese version), logical Memory recall, and Activity of Daily Living Scale (ADL). Participants with MCI had unimpaired MMSE but showed impairment in the Logical Memory test.61 Their CDR score was 0.5, with a mandatory requirement that the memory box score be 0.5 or greater; however, their ADL was normal. Dementia had a CDR of 1 or greater with abnormal MMSE, logical memory, and ADL. All participants underwent comprehensive neuropsychological assessments and blood sampling at baseline and annually. Some participants also underwent baseline and longitudinal MRI, Aβ-PET, and tau-PET scans at baseline and every two years. In this study, we identified 554 participants from the GHABS cohort with plasma biomarker measures, with a mean (SD) age of 67.1 (7.8) years. Of these participants, 355 (64.1%) were female and 377 (68.1%) were CU. Longitudinally, 113 had repeated plasma GAP-43 tests, 157 had longitudinal structural MRI scans, and 199 had longitudinal cognitive assessments.

The Huashan CPAS cohort is a longitudinal multicenter memory clinical study that is designed to track the development of AD and other dementias in the Chinese population. All participants were recruited from the memory clinics of Huashan Hospital, Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, and Ruijin Hospital in the Shanghai area of eastern China, following the standard criteria previously reported.62,63 The participants were classified into CU (CDR = 0), MCI (CDR = 0.5), or dementia (CDR ≥1). Meanwhile, MCI must meet the criteria proposed by Jack and Bondi64: (1) impairment in both tests within any single cognitive domain (with a deficit greater than one standard deviation adjusted for age) or (2) impairment in one test across three different cognitive domains. All participants underwent neuropsychological testing and blood sampling, while some underwent neuroimaging scans. Herein, we collected plasma biomarker measures from 231 participants in the Huashan cohort, with a mean (SD) age of 70.0 (8.2) years. Among them, 143 (61.9%) were female, and 154 (66.7%) had CI, as defined by either MCI or dementia. Longitudinally, 51 had repeated structural MRI scans and 128 had repeated cognitive assessments.

We took 1.5g of brain tissue and gently cut it into smaller pieces. These samples were further incubated in a Papain/Hibernate-E solution to gently digest the tissue and release interstitial fluid by avoiding cell lysis. Further, we performed differential centrifugation of digested material at 300 x g for 10 min, 2,000 x g for 10 min, and 10,000 x g for 4°C to remove cells, membranes, and debris, and consequently, the supernatant from 10,000 x g centrifugation was stored at −80°C until EV isolation was performed. We employed the procedures and various controls outlined in the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV) guidelines for the BDEV isolations from human interstitial fluid. However, the procedure described above for recovering BD fluids may still result in some cell lysis and intracellular contamination. The presence of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) in the preparations cannot be fully excluded. We loaded 500 μL of BD fluid on top of a size exclusion chromatography (SEC) column (10 mL column, CL2B Sepharose, pore size 75 nm, Millipore). A mean of 7.94 x 1010 (±3.36 x 109) vesicles/g of tissue in F1-4 (n = 36 samples) was recovered for each sample. Isolation was performed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a flow rate of 36–48 s/mL. The first 3 mL was removed, and the following 220 fractions were recovered (with 500 μL per fraction).

Fractions F1–4 were processed using a modified version of the iST protocol, referred to as the miST method. Briefly, 50 μL of each PBS-dissolved sample was mixed with 50 μL of miST lysis buffer (containing 1% sodium deoxycholate, 100 mM Tris (pH 8.6), and 10 mM DTT) and then heated at 95°C for 5 min. The mixture was diluted 1:1 with water, and disulfide bonds were alkylated by adding one-fourth the volume of 160 mM chloroacetamide (final concentration: 32 mM) and incubating at 25°C for 45 min in the dark. After adjusting the samples to 3 mM EDTA, digestion was performed with 0.5 μg of a trypsin/LysC mix (Promega #V5073) at 37°C for 1 h, followed by a second 1-h digestion using a fresh aliquot of the same enzymes. To remove sodium deoxycholate and desalt the peptides, isopropanol containing 1% TFA (in a 2:1 ratio to sample volume) was added, and the mixture was processed through a strong cation exchange (SCX) plate (Oasis MCX, Waters) via centrifugation. Peptides were eluted using 250 μL of a solution containing 80% acetonitrile, 19% water, and 1% ammonia. The resulting tryptic peptides were analyzed using an Ultimate RSLC 3000 nanoHPLC coupled via a nanospray Flex source to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Peptides were first trapped on a PepMap100 C18 microcolumn (20 mm × 100 μm, 5 μm) and then separated on a 45 cm custom-packed C18 column (75 μm ID, 1.8 μm particles) using a gradient of 4–90% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid over 140 min. MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 120,000 using a data-dependent acquisition strategy (top-speed mode, 2 s cycle) that targeted precursor ions with charge states ranging from 2+ to 5+. Fragmentation was performed using higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD) at 30% energy and 15,000 resolution. A 1.6 m/z isolation window was applied, and fragmented precursors were excluded from re-analysis for 60 s.

The obtained data files were analyzed with MaxQuant 1.6.14.0, which incorporates the Andromeda search engine. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was selected as a fixed modification, whereas methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation were specified as variable modifications. The sequence database and a “contaminant” database containing the most common environmental contaminants and enzymes used for digestion (keratins, trypsin, etc.). The mass tolerance was 4.5 ppm for the precursors (after recalibration) and 220 ppm for the MS/MS fragments. Both peptides and protein identifications were filtered at a 1% false discovery rate relative to hits against a decoy database built by reversing protein sequences. Proteins were annotated for several databases, including the Gene Ontology (GO) Human database, which is further categorized into cellular component (CC), biological process (BP), and molecular function (MF), as well as the MISEV guidelines for EV-associated and potential contaminant proteins.

Before further omic analysis, we preprocessed the data of all identified proteins, along with their respective riBAQ scores for each sample group. All the quantified proteins were subjected to statistical analysis using Perseus software to identify proteins that were significantly differentially expressed. Significant p-values were determined via false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted grouped Student’s t test with (Alzheimer’s disease Braak I-VI vs. healthy controls). We used the volcano plot analysis to calculate the log2(fold change) in protein expression using the following set parameters: FDR <0.05 and S0 = 0.1. The results obtained were visually demonstrated in a Volcano plot using the Prism version.

GSEA enables the detection of modest but coordinated changes in the expression of protein-encoding genes with similar functional properties. We used GOSemSim and clusterProfiler packages to perform the GSEA analysis on R. We provided our genelist with fold change values and used different gse functions for various databases, including the gseGO, gseKEGG, gseWP, and gsePathway. We selected the best enriched term and manually classified them according to the similarity in their functional properties into different functional modules such as: Synaptic Regulation, Immune Response and Regulation, Peptidase Activity, Extracellular Matrix and Adhesion, Mitochondria and Metabolism, Endoplasmic Reticulum and Secretory Pathway. We calculated an aggregated Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) for each module and average-log10 (FDR value) for the significance of the enrichment scores.

We extracted the top 5 leading-edge terms from the ranked list in each module. Among these leading terms, the annotated proteins and their ribaq scores were further used for correlation with GAP-43. We selected proteins that exhibited significantly high correlations (>±0.6) with GAP-43 from the leading edges to develop the network using the igraph and ggraph packages in R (v4.1.1). In the network, we place GAP-43 at the center, connecting it to all the proteins with which it is correlated. Red nodes represent positive correlation with GAP-43 expression, and blue nodes represent negative correlation with GAP-43 expression. These nodes (proteins) are further connected with their associated functional terms, with edges representing enrichment scores. The blue edges show negative NES scores, and the red edges show positive NES scores. All the aesthetic changes were further performed on Cytoscape 3.10. Pearson’s r was employed to evaluate the correlations between GAP-43 and other proteins.

Plasma and CSF GAP-43 concentrations were measured using a commercial Human GAP-43 ELISA kit (cat: abx250779, lot: E2212283A, Abbexa LTD, Cambridge, UK) on a Biotek Synergy Neo2 microplate reader, with a detection range between 156 and 10000 pg/mL. During sample runs across 14 ELISA plates, plasma quality control (QC) aliquots were analyzed in duplicate on each plate. The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV) were 0.7–6.4% and 8.9%, respectively. Importantly, both GHABS and Huashan cohorts were processed using identical assay protocols and QC procedures to ensure harmonization across cohorts. Plasma and CSF concentrations of Aβ42, Aβ40, NfL, GFAP, and p-tau181 were determined by the fully automated Simoa HD-X Analyzer (Quanterix) using the commercial NEURO 4-PLEX E (N4PE) and pTau-181 V2 Advantage kits, following the protocol of the manufacturer. Plasma concentrations of p-tau217, p-tau231, BD-tau, and NT1-tau were quantified using in-house Simoa immunoassays developed at the University of Gothenburg and Harvard Medical School.65,66,67 sTREM2 concentrations in plasma and CSF were measured using an MSD platform-based electrochemiluminescence assay developed by the Haass group.23,68 Slopes of plasma GAP-43 were calculated for all participants with longitudinal plasma GAP-43 measurements in the GHABS cohort, using linear mixed-effects (LME) models (lme4 package), adjusting for age and gender, and including a random time slope and intercept.

Detailed descriptions of MRI and PET imaging procedures in two cohorts have been previously reported.59,62 Structural MRI T1-weighted imaging was obtained to calculate HCV and cortical thickness using FreeSurfer (version 7.2.0).21 HCV was adjusted by the estimated total intracranial volume using the approach reported by Jack.69 Slopes of adjusted HCV and cortical thickness were computed for all the participants with longitudinal MRI data, using LME models, adjusting for age and gender, and including a random time slope and intercept. The diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) data were processed to calculate the mean diffusivity (MD) metric using open-source algorithms from MRTrix3 (v. 3.0.3), FSL (v. 6.0.3), and Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs, v. 2.4.3), as previously described.44 Briefly, we used a boundary-based algorithm and warped the MD metric to co-register the averaged b0 image to the corresponding skull-tripped 3D T1. Then, the volume MD metric for each participant was sampled at the midpoint of the cortical ribbon and projected to each cortical surface obtained during the FreeSurfer step to create a surface map of the cMD. cMD-metaROI was a composite ROI, including parahippocampal, insula, isthmus cingulate, and rostral anterior cingulate regions. The positivity of hippocampal and cMD atrophy was defined as an adjusted HCV ≤6973 cm3 and a cMD-metaROI <1.199, as previously reported.44,70

Aβ-PET imaging was performed with FSP or FBP tracers in the GHABS (FSP and FBP tracers) and Huashan (FBP tracers) cohorts, respectively. Aβ-PET SUVR was calculated using the mean uptake in the brainstem for FSP71 and the whole cerebellum for FBP72 as the reference region. Cortical Aβ plaques were evaluated by a composite Aβ-PET SUVR of AD summary cortical regions, including the bilateral frontal, cingulate, parietal, and temporal lobes. [18F]-SynVesT-1 SV2A-PET imaging was acquired in the Huashan CPAS cohort, using the whole cerebellum as the reference region.12 AV1451 and MK6240 tau-PET imaging were performed in the GHABS and Huashan cohorts, respectively. Tau-PET SUVR was calculated using the inferior cerebellar cortex as the reference tissue.73 According to previous studies74 the SUVRs of SV2A-PET and tau-PET were calculated in three predefined composite regions representing the Braak stages I-II, III-IV, and V-VI, respectively. Notably, the cortical region corresponding to the Braak II stage was removed for the tau-PET study due to the off-target binding of the AV1451 tracer. No partial volume correction (PVC) was applied to the PET imaging in this study.

Global cognitive function was assessed using MoCA and MMSE scores in the GHABS cohort, and memory and MMSE scores in the Huashan cohort. Memory scores were calculated by adding the Auditory Verbal Learning Test-Delayed Recall and Recognition (AVLT-N5 & N7). Slopes of MoCA, MMSE, and Memory were computed for all the participants with longitudinal cognitive data in each cohort, using LME models, adjusting for age and gender, and including a random time slope and intercept.

All analyses were performed using R (v4.1.1, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing), SPM12, Perseus, and GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2). The demographics and clinical characteristics are presented as numbers (%) or means (SD) in Table 1. Cross-sectional biofluid biomarkers were log10-transformed before parametric analyses. A two-sided p < 0.05 significance threshold was applied, unless otherwise noted.

In a subset of GHABS and Huashan cohorts, we evaluated the correlations of plasma GAP-43 with paired CSF biomarkers using Pearson’s r, and with regional SV2A-PET across different PET-like Braak stages using Spearman’s rank correlation and generalized linear model (GLM). Voxel-wise regression analyses were conducted using SPM12. Whole-brain SV2A-PET SUVR maps were included as the dependent variable, and plasma GAP-43 was included as the independent variable. Given the limited sample size and exploratory nature of the analyses, we applied a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.005 (uncorrected), without additional covariables, to minimize model complexity and avoid overfitting. Then, we examined the independent associations between plasma GAP-43 (outcome) and demographic variables (predictors), including age, gender, APOE-ε4 status, education, and clinical diagnosis, in a single multivariate GLM. The p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg approach with an FDR of 0.05. Pearson’s r was calculated to evaluate the correlations among plasma biomarkers, followed by the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR corrections. Moreover, we assessed the longitudinal changes in plasma GAP-43 using LME models with interaction terms (time × predictor), including age, gender, APOE-ε4 status, education, and plasma biomarkers. Each predictor was assessed in a separate model, and significant effects were further entered into multivariate models. All models were adjusted for age and gender.

Spearman’s rank correlation was calculated to assess the correlations of plasma GAP-43 with Aβ-PET and tau-PET across Braak-like stages. We applied GLM models to examine the associations between plasma GAP-43 and neurodegeneration (HCV, cortical thickness, or cMD) and cognitive function (MoCA, Memory, or MMSE). Age and gender were included as covariates, with additional adjustment for education in cognitive analyses. ROC analyses were conducted to assess the performance of plasma biomarkers in predicting positivity of HCV and cMD-metaROI cMD in the GHABS cohort. DeLong tests were used to compare the differences in AUCs between plasma models.
