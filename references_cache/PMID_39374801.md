The limited understanding of the mechanisms underlying human discogenic low back 
pain (DLBP) has hampered the development of effective treatments. While there is 
much research on disc degeneration, the association between degeneration and 
pain is weak. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify pain-inducing 
molecular mechanism to facilitate the development of mechanism-specific 
therapeutics. This scoping review aims to determine the current knowledge of 
molecular mechanisms associated with human DLBP. A systematic search on CENTRAL, 
CINAHL, Citation searching, ClinicalTrials.gov, Embase, Google Scholar, MEDLINE, 
PsycINFO, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and World Health Organization was 
performed. Studies with human DLBP as diagnosed by discography or imaging that 
analyzed human disc tissues and reported pain-related outcomes were included, 
and those on predominant radicular pain were excluded. The search returned 6012 
studies. Most studies did not collect pain-related outcomes. Those that included 
pain assessment relied on self-report of pain intensity and disability. Six 
studies qualified for data extraction and synthesis. The main molecular 
mechanisms associated with DLBP were the expressions of nociceptive 
neuropeptides and cytokines, particularly TNF-αdue to its strong association 
with pain outcomes. Activation of NF-κB signaling pathway, alterations in 
adrenoceptor expressions, and increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) were 
also associated with DLBP through regulation of pro-inflammatory factors and 
pain-related neuropeptides. Current evidence converges to TNF-α, NF-κB 
signaling, and ROS-induced pro-inflammation. Major weaknesses in the current 
literature are the focus on degeneration without pain phenotyping, and lack of 
association of molecular findings with pain outcomes. PERSPECTIVE: This scoping 
review identified TNF-α, NF-κB signaling, and ROS-induced pro-inflammation as 
relevant mechanisms of human discogenic low back pain. Major weaknesses in the 
current literature are the focus on degeneration without pain phenotyping, and 
lack of association of molecular findings with pain outcomes.

Chronic low back pain (CLBP) is a predominant challenge in global health, which significantly impairs quality of life and represents a leading cause of disability worldwide1. Among the various etiologies of CLBP, pain that originates from the intervertebral disc (IVD) or the cartilage endplate, referred to as discogenic low back pain (DLBP), has received much attention in research and clinical practice 2. The notion of DLBP is supported by many animal and human studies that have identified nociceptive fibers from the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) innervating disc structures 3–6 as well as evidence showing ingrowth of nerve fibers into the inner layer of degenerative discs 7,8. Epidemiological studies that have investigated the association of IVD degeneration with low back pain have found that individuals with MRI evidence of degeneration are 2–3 times more likely to experience CLBP 9–13. The process of disc degeneration includes chronic inflammation, annulus fibrosus rupture, extracellular matrix degradation, vascularization, and hyperinnervation 2,5,6,14,15. However, degeneration is frequently asymptomatic 16–18. Despite considerable research into disc degeneration 19–21, the fact that this condition can be asymptomatic, and the high prevalence of CLBP without disc degeneration indicate that mechanisms other than degeneration contribute to pain from the disc 14,22–27.

The limited understanding of the underlying causes driving nociception within the disc have hampered the development of effective treatments for DLBP, resulting in reliance mainly on opioids and surgery when conservative treatment fails 2,28–31. This scoping review aims to synthesize the available evidence on the molecular mechanisms associated with human DLBP and identify areas of future research to better understand the mechanisms of DLBP. This knowledge is essential to facilitate the development of mechanism-based therapeutics.

This scoping review adheres to The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist, and the Arksey & O’Malley framework were used to conduct this review 32,33.

Investigations that studied patients with axial low back pain and suspected discogenic pain by either discography or imaging, that analyzed human disc tissues, and included patient-reported outcomes of pain or function were included.

The exclusion criteria were: participants experiencing predominantly radicular pain, case reports, editorials, or letters to the editor, studies without clear details on molecular pathways, focused solely on surgical or pharmacological interventions without discussing molecular mechanisms, or that relied on animal models for analysis, unless human data that could directly contribute to the understanding of human LDBP were included.

A systematic search on CENTRAL, CINAHL, Citation searching, ClinicalTrials.gov, Embase, Google Scholar, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and World Health Organization were performed. Keyword searches pertaining to discogenic low back pain, were conducted in each database, as well as use of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms) where possible. A Health Sciences Research Librarian at University of Washington was consulted for database selection and search strategy development prior to beginning the search. Grey literature was not searched unless it was indexed in the databases that were searched for this review. The search was initially run from inception to November 30, 2023, to ensure that as many relevant publications as possible could be included. Appendix A in the supplemental file described our full search strategy utilized across all databases.

All records were imported into the electronic platform Covidence (Covidence, 2023) and duplicates were removed. Screening was completed independently by three reviewers (AC, JL and KG). Title and abstract screening were carried out, followed by full-text screening. Any conflicts that arose during the screening process were discussed and resolved by a fourth reviewer (PB). Any studies that satisfied the inclusion criteria were carried forward for data extraction. Data extraction was carried out independently by one researcher (AC) and checked for accuracy by two others (PB and MC). A data extraction template was developed to guide the extraction of the following information: study characteristics (author, year of publication, country, study design), participant characteristics including demographics (sex, age, BMI) and diagnoses, pain evaluation, details of molecular analyses (techniques used, biomarkers studied), main findings related to the molecular mechanisms of DLBP, and conclusions as well as implications for future research (appendix B).

Each paper was evaluated based on 7 components. 1) It was determined whether each study implemented specific inclusion and exclusion criteria for screening and recruitment of study participants, and 2) whether adjustments were made for demographic confounding factors, unless statistical analysis indicated no significant difference between groups. 3) The source of tissue samples was evaluated to ensure they were obtained from patients diagnosed with axial low back pain associated with degenerative disc without other spinal disorders that could cause different types of pain, and the diagnoses must have been confirmed by radiological images and/or discography. 4) For studies that involved patients with different pain types, it was evaluated whether disc samples from patients with different pain phenotyping were categorized into groups before analysis. 5) Furthermore, the evaluation included whether the studies conducted separate analysis on nucleus pulposus (NP) and annulus fibrosus (AF), 6) collected lumbar spine radiological imaging for analysis, and 7) performed and reported the results of correlation analyses between molecular and clinical findings.

After establishing a comprehensive summary of each study based on the data extraction template, data synthesis was performed and compiled to the results section of our review by one researcher (AC) and verified by two others (PB and MC).

The search process yielded 6,012 studies, from which 5 duplicates were removed. Many studies analyzing human disc tissues did not collect pain-related patient-reported outcomes and were thus excluded, as it was not possible to identify which molecular mechanisms were relevant to pain. After title, abstract, and full-text screening using our inclusion and exclusion criteria, six studies qualified for data extraction and final synthesis (figure 1). A comprehensive overview of the included study characteristics is presented in table 1.

All six studies evaluated pain intensity using either the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) or Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), with three recording also back pain-specific measurements such as the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) 34–36 and the Japanese Orthopedic Association Back Pain Evaluation Questionnaire (JOABPEQ) 36. Among them, two studies identified a group of patients with DLBP based on both radiological imaging and clinical phenotyping 34,37, while the other ones did not delineate participant characteristics clearly, potentially including patients with other types of pain such as radicular or referred leg pain 35,36,38,39. Additionally, three studies did not ensure a balanced representation of the demographic profiles such as sex, age, and BMI 34,35,37. Despite the data collection of pain evaluation in all six studies, one did not perform an analysis of the correlation between molecular findings in discs and pain outcomes 39.

Table 1 provides an overview of included studies, table 2 the results of the critical appraisal, and table 3 of the limitations of the studies.

Schroeder et al. 37 investigated the patterns of the precursor and active forms of substance P (pro-SP and SP), TNF-α, IL-1α, MMP-3, and TGF-β protein expressions in discs collected from patients with DLBP and radiologically-confirmed degenerative disc diseases, acute low back pain due to disc herniation, and scoliosis with no pain. Pain intensity was measured using VAS. The results highlighted distinct patterns of cytokine and SP expressions between the AF and NP in painful degenerative discs. Significantly different patterns of cytokines and neuropeptides expressions were also found between degenerated and herniated discs, with AF tissue of painful discs expressing more SP than the extruded AF materials of acute painful herniated discs. Comparing non-painful discs to those from DLBP patients, changes in the levels of pro-SP and TGF-β expression in AF were opposite to those in NP. The expression of active SP and MMP-3 was more pronounced in NP than in AF, while the expression of TNF-α and TGF-β was more enhanced in AF than in NP. Moreover, the study found a significantly positive correlation between the level of TNF-α expression and pain intensity as well as duration of pain.

Aripaka et al. 35 measured the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 gene expressions in painful degenerative discs from 34 patients diagnosed with severe foraminal stenosis. Pain and disability were assessed using VAS and ODI, respectively. The authors observed higher levels of TNF-α and IL-6 expressions in AF than in NP. Additionally, they found significantly positive correlations between TNF-α and IL-1β in AF, TNF-α and IL-6 in NP, between pain intensity and TNF-α expression, and between disability and IL-6 expression in both AF and NP. The different correlations between clinical parameters and cytokine expressions were suggestive of a possible discrepancy in molecular mechanisms driving pain and those leading to disability.

Sianoh et al. 39 correlated cytokine expressions with severity of disc degeneration based on Pfirrman gradings, and with predominant clinical symptoms of either back or leg pain. The study participants included 55 patients with lumbar disc herniation (n=12), spinal stenosis (n=30), DLBP (n=9), and lumbar spondylolisthesis (n=7), who presented moderate to severe disc degeneration in radiological findings, excluding those with any previous spine surgery or who were taking potential confounding medications such as steroids. Pain intensity was evaluated by the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), and the study cohort was divided into groups based on the primary symptom of back pain (n=27) or leg pain (n=31) as determined by their NRS scores. No correlation analysis was made between pain intensity and molecular findings. The study found a rise in IL-6 in moderate (grade 4) to severe (grade 5) degeneration, a decrease in NGF and an increase in TNF-α in the most severely degenerated discs. Notably, the increase in the levels of TNF-α was more pronounced in patients with predominant back than leg pain.

Ahmed et al. 34 investigated the association between NF-κB signaling and the expression of pain-related neuropeptides, and between these molecular findings and DLBP severity. Disc tissues were obtained from 18 pain-free postmortem controls and 40 DLBP patients with predominant back pain lasting over a year, excluding those diagnosed with other painful conditions such as disc herniation and fibromyalgia. Pain intensity and disability were measured by VAS and ODI on a 0–100 scale, indicating moderate DLBP (VAS=32.01 ± 19.98), disability (ODI=31.59 ± 12.25), and minimal referred leg pain (VAS=4.71 ± 9.31). The study found that painful degenerative discs exhibited increased NF-κB activation and levels of TRPV1, and increased CGRP protein expression contrary to its decreased gene expressions. Co-expression of CGRP and SP was identified and positively correlated with expressions of NF-κB and TRPV1. Besides a negative correlation between TAC1 expression and back pain severity, the authors only observed a weak association between the molecular findings and pain outcomes including pain intensity and disability. It was concluded that NF-κB served as a potential mediator in peripheral pain mechanism through regulating the expressions of pain-related neuropeptides in painful degenerative discs.

Zheng et al. 36 focused on reactive oxygen species (ROS) and examined their role in mechanisms driving DLBP using human NP tissues, cell culture, and DLBP rat models. Human disc samples were collected from 30 patients diagnosed with DLBP, which had lasted at least 6 months, associated with disc degeneration, with or without radicular pain resulting from disc herniation. Disability and other back pain related clinical symptoms were evaluated by ODI and the Japanese Orthopedic Association Back Pain Evaluation Questionnaires (JOABPEQ). Pain intensity was assessed by VAS. The findings from human data suggested that SP expressions in NP tissues of painful discs correlated with DLBP severity. ROS stimulation, both in living rats and human NP cell cultures, promoted SP expression in a dose-responsive manner. Combining all data, the authors confirmed ROS as a primary inducer of SP release in painful discs.

Brenneis et al. 38 explored the changes in the patterns of adrenoceptor expressions in the sympathetic nervous system. A total of 43 human disc samples were collected from DLBP patients diagnosed with disc degeneration with or without Modic changes. Additionally, potential confounding factors, such as presence of adjacent segment diseases, uses of nicotine, NSAID, and β-blocker, were also recorded. The expressions of α1A, α2A, and β2 adrenoceptors positively correlated with the severity of both endplate and disc degeneration. Notably, the level of α1A-adrenoceptor expression was lower in samples from severe than low to moderate pain groups. Furthermore, β-blockers markedly reduced the level of α1A-adrenoceptors expression, while NSAIDs increased the level of β2 adrenoceptors expression, and patients with adjacent segment diseases exhibited reduced α1A-adrenoceptor expression.

The four studies 34–37 that performed separate molecular analyses in different anatomical locations found notable differences in the molecular mechanisms between AF and NP. In the AF, there were increased expressions of the precursor of SP (pro-SP), TGF-β, MMP-3 37, IL-6 35,39, and TNF-α, which not only showed positive correlations with IL-1β but were also consistently associated with pain intensity 35,37,39 and duration 37 across multiple studies. Furthermore, significant increases in the activation of the NF-kB signaling pathway and the expression of TRPV1 were associated with a positive correlation with the levels of CGRP and SP, with a pattern of co-expression observed between the two 34.

In contrast to the findings in the AF, significant decreases in both pro-SP and TGF-β were found in the NP 37. The increase in ROS levels in the NP tissues was suggested to promote the release of SP in both human samples and rat models 36. In addition, the expression of IL-6 was positively correlated with the level of disability and the expression of TNF-α 35. Consistent with the findings in the AF, the increase in TNF-α 35,37,39 was positively correlated with pain 35,37,39. Lastly, both AF and NP showed a decrease in α1A-adrenoceptor expression in the sympathetic nervous system, which was positively associated with the level of disc degeneration and pain intensity 38.

Although many studies have conducted analyses on clinical data and molecular findings in disc tissues, they often did not restrict enrollment to patients diagnosed with axial low back pain due to degenerative disc diseases, and did not exclude other spinal disorders. Most studies did not implement comprehensive pain phenotyping, account for confounding factors, perform separate analyses on NP and AF tissues within the discs, or collect and analyze imaging data (table 2).

The included studies have found following molecular mechanisms as potentially relevant in DLBP: upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically TNF-α and nociceptive neuropeptides such as CGRP and SP 34–37,39, possibly mediated by the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway 34; increase in ROS 36; upregulation in extracellular matrix degrading enzymes such as MMP-3 37; and changes of adrenoceptors expressions 38. The mechanisms are illustrated in figure 2.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines, their signal transduction, and associations with pain-related neuropeptides have been implicated in various chronic pain conditions 40,41. Among the studies included in this review, Schroeder et al. and Aripaka et al. highlighted the different expression patterns of cytokines and neuropeptides between AF and NP tissues 35,37, as well as between chronic degenerative and acute herniated discs 37. Accordingly, an animal study demonstrated that systemic inflammation induced by cytokine overexpression promoted cell death and immune cell infiltration in AF with no change in NP, implying that pro-inflammatory cytokine expressions may predispose the discs to herniation rather than directly contributing to degeneration in NP tissues 42. These findings underscore the importance of pro-inflammatory cytokines in driving distinct pathogenesis of DLBP in AF compared to NP tissues.

TNF-α expression in AF and NP tissues consistently showed significantly positive correlations with patient-reported outcomes, such as pain intensity, duration, and disability 35,37,39. AF tissues from patients with predominantly back pain exhibited a more significant increase in TNF-α levels than those with predominantly leg pain 39. A study identified a correlation between endplate abnormalities, inflammation, and axon growth induced by TNF-α, further supporting its significance in painful degenerative discs and potential as therapeutic target 43. Inhibition of TNF-receptor (TNFR)-1 mediated cellular senescence in human AF cells extracted from herniated discs reactivated cell proliferation and promoted repair of degenerative discs through improving TNFR-2 modulated pro-reparative response 44. Despite the above data, the clinical relevance of TNF-α and the efficacy of anti-TNFα therapy in treating DLBP are still uncertain 45,46.

Inflammatory pathways have been associated with the pathogenesis of disc degeneration 47. Studies included in this review confirmed their roles in triggering nociception within degenerative discs 34,35,37,39. The NF-κB signaling can promote peripheral nociceptive mechanisms through neuropeptide release, and a pattern of co-expression of CGRP and SP was identified in degenerative discs expressing an abundance of TRPV1 34. This finding, together with findings of sprouting of primary afferents to normally non-innervated regions, suggests that neurogenic inflammation may play a role in both degeneration and nociception through activation of TRPV1 and the subsequent release of pain-related neuropeptides such as CGRP and SP 48,49. Recent animal studies also demonstrated that the therapeutic effect of most analgesic and anti-inflammatory agents originated from inhibiting TRPV1 activation and regulating the expressions of NF-κB and TNF-α 50, or through direct bindings to NF-κB, CGRP, and SP receptors 51. Despite no direct evidence associating pain relief of DLBP with effective NF-κB targeted treatment, studies suggest that all-trans retinoic acid can prevent postoperative pain associated with epidural fibrosis after lumbar spine surgery through the inhibition of NF-κB signaling; furthermore, glucocorticoids may modulate pain after single level lumbar fusions by targeting inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB 52. These findings suggest a potential role of NF-κB-targeted therapeutics for painful degenerative discs 53.

Upregulation of oxidative stress, resulting from accumulation of ROS and downregulated antioxidants efficiency, is a known contributor to degeneration 47,54. In addition, ROS can drive DLBP by enhancing the release of pain-related neuropeptides 36. A recent study introduced a previously unknown pain-inducing mechanism mediated by NP cells in degenerative discs under stress induced by ROS without the presence of immune cells 55. Collectively, the role of ROS in DLBP, whether through inflammatory elements or direct impact on NP cells, highlights a new hope of therapeutic strategies for DLBP.

Enhanced extracellular matrix degradation, due to upregulation of catabolic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), is associated with loss of disc structural integrity, vascularization, nerve ingrowth, pyroptosis, and other inflammatory responses 56. Given the correlations between expressions of different MMP isoforms 57 and increase in MMP-3 levels 37, also other MMPs (e.g., MMP-2 and MMP-9) may be involved in nociception within degenerated discs (see recent review on MMPs 58). Along with their interactions with other inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, TGF-β, IL-6, and IL-1β, as well as their downstream signaling pathways, MMPs may contribute to both extracellular matrix degradation and pain in degenerated discs 59–63.

One study 38 reported a significant correlation between adrenoceptor expressions and severity of disc and endplate degeneration, aligning with previous findings of a link between abnormalities in sympathetic nervous system and intensity of pain and disability in chronic LBP 64. Another study using porcine IVDs found a relationship between sympathetic innervation and intensity of experimentally-induced DLBP 65. An in-vitro study further provided insight into the role of sympathetic nervous system in the pathogenesis of IVD degeneration through changes in adrenoceptor expression and activation of downstream signaling pathways by norepinephrine stimulation 66. The use of specific adrenoceptor agonists or antagonists in modulating pain and inflammation suggested potential therapeutic avenues 67–69.

The above-mentioned changes in molecular and behavioral profiles of AF and NP cells, driven by environmental stressors associated with degeneration 2,70, may lead to a pathological disc microenvironment predisposing to DLBP 6,13,14,71. The findings of this review are suggestive of a pathway that may begin with ROS accumulation, possibly due to mitochondrial dysfunction or extracellular matrix degradation promoted by catabolic enzymes such as MMP-3. In response to environmental stress, degenerative AF and NP cells may activate the inflammasome, releasing cytokines such as IL-1β. This activates downstream inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB, thereby promoting the synthesis of inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, TGF-β, and IL-6. This collective inflammatory response can sensitize ion channels such as TRPV1 on primary afferent fibers that sprout into degenerative disc tissue, releasing pain-related neuropeptides such as substance P that initiates a cascade of nociceptive transduction. DLBP may be maintained by a loop involving the interaction between inflammatory responses and nociceptive 6,41,49 (figure 2).

Limitations are outlined in table 3. The main one is the frequent failure to address the heterogeneity by recording clinical characteristics and comprehensive phenotyping 35–39. No measures of somatosensory function have been part of the pain assessment. The inclusion of patients with diagnoses other than degenerative disc diseases 35–39, unadjusted demographic characteristics 34,37, and improper categorization of acute versus chronic pain 37 may have limited the robustness of the findings. Moreover, most studies analyzed only specific disc regions or entire discs, limiting insights into cytokine variations between AF and NP tissues 34,36,38,39. In one study, no correlation analysis between clinical phenotyping and molecular findings was performed 38.

This review focused on studies that specifically addressed the molecular mechanisms underlying nociception. Intentionally, a considerable number of studies that only investigated molecular mechanisms of disc degeneration but did not include pain-related outcomes or other relevant clinical data were excluded. This approach can lead to an oversight of significant discoveries in the molecular mechanisms of discogenic pain. However, because of the discrepancy between degeneration and pain, the goal was to provide a clearer understanding of specific nociceptive processes in degenerative discs. One major strength is the structured approach to the existing literature, involving selection of studies specifically focusing on nociceptive mechanisms, pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, critical appraisal and synthesis.

This review found very few studies that addressed the molecular mechanisms of human DLBP, with most studies focusing on disc degeneration without recording pain outcomes. A major weakness is the lack of comprehensive pain phenotyping, which fails to address the heterogeneity of low back pain.

The main molecular mechanisms of DLBP identified are centered around the expressions of nociceptive neuropeptides and cytokines, particularly TNF-α, due to its correlation with pain phenotypes. Activation of NF-κB signaling, alterations in adrenoceptor expressions, and increase in ROS are involved in painful disc degeneration through regulation of pro-inflammatory factors and pain-related neuropeptides.

Future research should focus on patients predominantly experiencing axial low back pain, incorporating assessments of pain outcomes such as pain interference, physical function, and assessment of neuropathic components using questionnaires and/or quantitative sensory testing. The analyses should adjust for potentially confounding factors. It is essential to perform tissue analyses on different sites to consider distinct molecular expressions across different anatomical regions of disc tissues. Integration of multi-omics approaches such as metabolomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and genomics may be considered, as they can provide additional insights on the molecular mechanisms of DLBP.

## Table 3. Limitations of the included studies.

| Authors & year | Study limitations |
| Schroeder et al. (2013) | ○ Age and BMI mismatch between groups, and a skewed sex ratio within DDD group and pain-free scoliosis patient groups ○ Difficulty differentiating DLBP from radicular pain due to similar pain levels in DDD patients for back and leg pain ○ Misclassification of acute pain subjects, with three herniated disc patients reporting pain durations exceeding three months, including one case lasting 36 months. ○ No reported pain intensity data in the disc herniation group, except for mentions of acute pain increases 1–21 days before surgery. ○ Lack of identification and adjustment for potential confounding variables in the analysis. |
| Aripaka et al. (2021) | ○ Absence of pain-free controls and unreported BMI values; unbalanced gender distribution among participants. ○ Inability to distinguish between types of pain due to mixed symptoms of leg and back pain from varying diagnoses ○ Lack of identification and adjustment for potential confounding variables in the analysis |
| Sainoh et al. (2017) | ○ No control group and a very small cohort of DLBP patients. ○ No differentiation in the analysis between different regions (AF and NP) of the whole disc samples. ○ Limited use of pain outcomes for identifying predominant symptoms without any correlation analysis with molecular findings. ○ Lack of adjustment for potential confounding variables in the analysis. |
| Ahmed et al. (2019) | ○ Post-mortem disc samples as controls with limited sample size, disproportional sex ratio, and significant difference in BMI compared to DDD patients ○ Analysis restricted to AF tissue without consideration of regional differences in painful discs. ○ No adjustments made for the potential confounding effects of medication history and pre-operative analgesic administration on the expression of pain-related neuropeptides. ○ Inability to adequately explain weak and negative correlations observed between molecular findings and clinical data. |
| Zheng et al. (2021) | ○ No control group and a very small cohort patients with mixed diagnoses other than DDD ○ Lacking detailed clinical characteristics and pain phenotype data for participants ○ Most major findings relied on in vitro NP cells and in vivo DLBP rat models, with human tissues utilized only for qualitative and semi-quantitative imaging analyses ○ Lack of adjustment for potential confounding variables in the analysis |
| Brenneis et al. (2022) | ○ A small cohort size of high BMI and an uneven sex ratio without a control group and clear diagnoses, inclusion and exclusion criteria for study participants. ○ Whole disc samples analyzed without considering regional differences in adrenoreceptors expressions between AF and NP. ○ Pain intensity data was discretized, limiting effective correlation analysis with molecular findings. ○ No adjustments for the confounding effects of drugs affecting the sympathetic nervous system given their significant impact on adrenoreceptors expressions in the discs |
