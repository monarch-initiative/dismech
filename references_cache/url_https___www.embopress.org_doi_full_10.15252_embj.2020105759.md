<section><div><h2>Abstract</h2><div><p>Parvalbumin‐positive (PV<sup>+</sup>) fast‐spiking interneurons are essential to control the firing activity of principal neuron ensembles, thereby regulating cognitive processes. The high firing frequency activity of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons imposes high‐energy demands on their metabolism that must be supplied by distinctive machinery for energy generation. Exploring single‐cell transcriptomic data for the mouse cortex, we identified a metabolism‐associated gene with highly restricted expression to PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons: <i>Cox6a2</i>, which codes for an isoform of a cytochrome <i>c</i> oxidase subunit. <i>Cox6a2</i> deletion in mice disrupts perineuronal nets and enhances oxidative stress in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons, which in turn impairs the maturation of their morphological and functional properties. Such dramatic effects were likely due to an essential role of COX6A2 in energy balance of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons, underscored by a decrease in the ATP‐to‐ADP ratio in <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons. Energy disbalance and aberrant maturation likely hinder the integration of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons into cortical neuronal circuits, leading to behavioral alterations in mice. Additionally, in a human patient bearing mutations in <i>COX6A2</i>, we found a potential association of the mutations with mental/neurological abnormalities.</p></div></div></section><section><div><h2>Synopsis</h2><div>
<div><figure><div><div><picture><img/></picture></div></div></figure></div>
<p>Whether brain neurons firing at high frequency depend on distinct metabolic machineries to satisfy their increased energy demands remains unclear. Here, combined genetic, functional and behavioural analyses reveal a selective role for the oxidative phosphorylation in stress protection of interneuron by safeguarding their energy supply.</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p>Mitochondrial complex IV subunit isoform Cox6a2 is enriched in parvalbumin‐positive (PV<sup>+</sup>) interneurons in the adult rodent, rhesus monkey, and human brain.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>PV<sup>+</sup> interneuron‐specific Cox6a2 ablation decreases cellular ATP levels and increases oxidative stress in mice.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Cox6a2 loss impairs transcriptional and morphological maturation during postnatal stages.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Adult Cox6a2‐depleted PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons are hyperexcitable and defective in repetitive firing.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Cox6a2 depletion results in hyperactivity <i>in vivo</i>.</p>
</li>
</ul>

<section><div><h2>Introduction</h2><div><p>Gamma‐aminobutyric acid‐containing (GABAergic) interneurons are the major source of inhibition in the mammalian brain (Hu <i>et al</i>, <a>2014</a>). Parvalbumin‐positive (PV<sup>+</sup>) interneurons are the largest class of cortical GABAergic interneurons and have been shown to regulate plasticity during learning and memory (Fishell &amp; Rudy, <a>2011</a>; Hu <i>et al</i>, <a>2014</a>; Tremblay <i>et al</i>, <a>2016</a>). PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons are also known as fast‐spiking interneurons due to their high‐frequency firing, i.e., generating up to hundreds of action potentials per second, that is required to synchronize oscillations of principal neurons (Cardin <i>et al</i>, <a>2009</a>; Sohal <i>et al</i>, <a>2009</a>). Such generation of brain oscillations underlies sensory information processing, working memory, attention, learning, and social behavior (Hu <i>et al</i>, <a>2014</a>). PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons also play a major role in the regulation of the excitatory/inhibitory balance in the cortex that is often impaired in psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia and autism (Marin, <a>2012</a>). The contribution of PV<sup>+</sup> interneuron dysfunction to psychiatric disorders is highlighted by alterations in gamma oscillations in schizophrenia patients (Cho <i>et al</i>, <a>2006</a>; Minzenberg <i>et al</i>, <a>2010</a>) and incomplete or retarded maturation of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons in patients (Gandal <i>et al</i>, <a>2012</a>) and in mouse models (Cabungcal <i>et al</i>, <a>2019</a>; Vasistha <i>et al</i>, <a>2019</a>).</p><p>The cellular properties of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons such as high‐frequency firing, short single spikes, and fast membrane time constant must impose high‐energy demands and require an elevated mitochondrial activity (Kann <i>et al</i>, <a>2014</a>). Although these metabolic characteristics ensure the normal functioning of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons, they also render PV<sup>+</sup> cells more sensitive to oxidative stress (Kann, <a>2016</a>). Indeed, genetic and pharmacological manipulations of the redox system lead to alterations in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons that do not affect other types of neurons (Cabungcal <i>et al</i>, <a>2019</a>). The high vulnerability of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons to oxidative stress has been also demonstrated in a number of genetic and environmental animal models of mental disorders (Steullet <i>et al</i>, <a>2017</a>). Such vulnerability to oxidative stress, which is especially pronounced during brain maturation (Behrens &amp; Sejnowski, <a>2009</a>), could lead to a selective dysfunction of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons in mental disorders (Chung <i>et al</i>, <a>2016</a>; Steullet <i>et al</i>, <a>2017</a>; Vasistha <i>et al</i>, <a>2019</a>). Therefore, in the healthy brain, PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons must benefit from an optimized metabolism accounting for their high‐energy demands, which simultaneously preserve their cellular properties and protect them against oxidative stress. However, in spite of being one of the most studied neurons in the brain, it is still unknown how PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons sustain energy demands for high‐frequency firing.</p><p>Since the main source of energy in neurons is oxidative phosphorylation (Sokoloff, <a>1960</a>), PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons might have optimized the oxidative phosphorylation machinery to produce ATP according to their demands. To test this, we leveraged single‐cell (sc) transcriptomic data to cross‐compare the expression of metabolic genes between PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons and all other types of neurons in the mouse cortex. We observed that one gene, <i>Cox6a2</i>, showed highly specific expression in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons. <i>Cox6a2</i> codes for the isoform 2 of the subunit COX6A, which integrates cytochrome <i>c</i> oxidase, also known as complex IV (CIV), in the oxidative phosphorylation pathway (Kadenbach <i>et al</i>, <a>1982</a>). We showed that the specific expression of <i>Cox6a2</i> in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons in the brain is highly conserved across mammals. <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons exhibit reduced expression of parvalbumin and a lower number of synaptic contacts onto principal neurons. Furthermore, <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> increases oxidative stress in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons and decreases the density of the perineuronal nets (PNNs) surrounding these cells, which in turn can affect the function of the local cortical circuits. Indeed, the maturation of the functional and morphological properties of <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons is perturbed, and <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> mice exhibit behavioral alterations associated with hyperactivity. Altogether, we show that COX6A2 is essential for the proper maturation and function of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons and <i>Cox6a2</i> deletion disrupts the unique cellular properties of fast‐spiking interneurons leading to behavioral abnormalities.</p></div></div></section><section><div><h2>Results</h2><div><h3>Identification of genes associated with energy production that are enriched in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons</h3><p>To identify genes that might contribute to sustain the high metabolic demands of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons, we explored the sc transcriptomic dataset produced for the mouse visual cortex (Tasic <i>et al</i>, <a>2016</a>). Such unique functions of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons have been hypothesized to rely on a distinct metabolism optimized for high‐frequency firing. Our data from mice demonstrate that PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons express <i>Cox6a2</i>, a gene that encodes one of the two isoforms of COX6A subunit, which integrates CIV in the oxidative phosphorylation pathway. <i>Cox6a2</i> had been previously identified only in the heart and skeletal muscles (Kadenbach <i>et al</i>, <a>1982</a>; Taanaman <i>et al</i>, <a>1993</a>). Importantly, in the brain, <i>Cox6a2</i> expression is largely limited to PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons, being virtually absent in other types of neurons and glia. In contrast, the gene encoding the other isoform of COX6A, <i>Cox6a1,</i> is ubiquitously expressed in the brain. PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons lacking <i>Cox6a2</i> show lower parvalbumin expression, higher oxidative stress, and aberrant PNNs. <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons exhibit impaired firing properties, form fewer synaptic contacts onto excitatory neurons, and show an abnormal morphology. Furthermore, in a key experiment, we showed a shift in energy balance of <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons to lower ATP‐to‐ADP ratio. Altogether, these effects result in behavioral alterations in mice.</p><p>The unique fast‐spiking properties of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons require a complex and coordinated work of the ion transport system that restores the intracellular ion concentrations after each action potential. This complex process must heavily rely on ATP. Importantly, COX6A2 is an ideal candidate to regulate ATP synthesis in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons, since COX6A2 but not COX6A1 is able to adjust the catalytic activity of CIV (Anthony <i>et al</i>, <a>1993</a>; Frank &amp; Kadenbach, <a>1996</a>). Thus, studies on reconstituted COX6A2‐containing CIV from the heart have shown that ADP stimulates CIV activity and low ATP‐to‐ADP ratio increases the proton transport efficiency of CIV (Anthony <i>et al</i>, <a>1993</a>; Frank &amp; Kadenbach, <a>1996</a>). In contrast, COX6A1‐containing CIV lacks ADP‐dependent stimulation and changes in the ATP‐to‐ADP ratio do not affect the efficiency of COX6A1‐containing CIV (Huttemann <i>et al</i>, <a>1999</a>). Strikingly, we found that PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons lacking <i>Cox6a2</i> also exhibit a decreased expression of <i>Cox6a1</i>. Since the ubiquitously expressed COX6A1 isoform would normally substitute COX6A2 in CIV (Taanaman <i>et al</i>, <a>1993</a>), reduced expression of <i>Cox6a1</i> in mutant PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons could lead to the formation of fewer CIV, further disrupting the activity of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway.</p><p>We show that COX6A2 is essential for the maturation of the functional properties of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons over the period from P22 to P45. Interestingly, the impairment in the functional properties of <i>Cox6a2</i><sup>−/−</sup> PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons was minor before ~ P30 and became more pronounced afterward. Thus, it seems that the effect of COX6A2 absence might accumulate over maturation, eventually hampering the normal development of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons. Such maturational delay resembles the impairment of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons that is observed in mental disorders (Gandal <i>et al</i>, <a>2012</a>) and could lead to functional alterations in the neural networks controlled by PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons, thereby contributing to their pathophysiology.</p><p>Selective expression of COX6A2 in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons also seems to protect them against oxidative stress. Reduced activity of CIV due to loss of COX6A2 has been shown to increase ROS production in the mitochondria (Quintens <i>et al</i>, <a>2013</a>). Moreover, in this study, we observed higher oxidative DNA damage in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons lacking <i>Cox6a2</i> expression. In addition, it has been proposed that PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons are more vulnerable to oxidative stress than other neurons possibly due to the higher metabolism required to sustain their electrophysiological properties (Steullet <i>et al</i>, <a>2017</a>). Thus, higher oxidative stress in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons in mouse models of mental disorders is associated with decreased expression of parvalbumin and GAD67, and loss of integrity of the PNNs that enwrap them (Cabungcal <i>et al</i>, <a>2006</a>, <a>2013b</a>; Behrens <i>et al</i>, <a>2007</a>). Interestingly, <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons also show lower expression levels of parvalbumin, <i>Gad1</i> (GAD67) and <i>Gad2</i> (GAD65). Furthermore, <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> affects the integrity of PNNs surrounding PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons. Thus, in addition to being essential for the maturation of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons, COX6A2 likely also protects them from increased ROS production and elevated oxidative stress.</p><p>Our animal behavior experiments showed that <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> contributes to hyperactivity, whereas social and acoustic startle behaviors that are often affected in animal models of mental disorders such as schizophrenia (Meechan <i>et al</i>, <a>2009</a>; Steullet <i>et al</i>, <a>2017</a>) were not changed in <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> mice. However, we analyzed behavior only in females and thus could not assess sex‐specific differences in <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup>. In addition, based on limited human data, <i>COX6A2</i> mutations might lead to cognitive abnormalities. One of the plausible explanations could be that the behavioral phenotype in <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> mice is rather related to attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). The low processing speed index that we identified for the patient with <i>COX6A2</i> mutations was also previously associated with the ADHD phenotype (Cook <i>et al</i>, <a>2018</a>). However, more comprehensive animal behavior and identification and investigation of a larger cohort of human patients bearing <i>COX6A2</i> mutations are necessary to connect <i>COX6A2</i> mutations to human neurodevelopmental disorders.</p><p>We found that the selective expression of <i>Cox6a2</i> in PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons <i>in vivo</i> depends on two regulatory sites present in the <i>Cox6a2</i> promoter: MEF2‐ and E‐box2‐binding sites, which have been previously shown to regulate the expression of <i>Cox6a2</i> in myoblasts and fibroblasts <i>in vitro</i> (Wan &amp; Moreadith, <a>1995</a>). Interestingly, MEF2 also regulates parvalbumin expression and both MEF2 expression and parvalbumin expression are activity‐dependent (Flavell <i>et al</i>, <a>2006</a>). Recent studies have shown that PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons exhibit different roles in memory acquisition depending on their high or low expression of PV, which under basal conditions correlates with the levels of MEF2 (Donato <i>et al</i>, <a>2013</a>, <a>2015</a>). Selective excitation/inhibition of high or low PV‐expressing interneurons results in specific plasticity changes such that the former are recruited during memory acquisition, whereas the latter mediate memory consolidation. These MEF2‐dependent effects might be mediated by adjusting <i>Cox6a2</i> expression according to the needs of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons in order to be integrated into the cortical and hippocampal networks.</p><p><i>Cox6a2</i> is a striking example of how regulation of gene expression can be adjusted not only according to the tissue needs but even to a particular cell type. Interestingly, the co‐expression of <i>Cox6a2</i> and PV is conserved across mammals. Thus, it might be that during evolution the expression of energy‐associated genes was optimized in fast‐spiking interneurons, which shows how exceptionally important fast‐spiking interneurons are for mammalian brain function.</p></div></div></section><section><div><h2>Materials and Methods</h2><div><h3>Human subjects</h3><p>The tissue samples from Brodmann areas 9 and 10 (BA9 and BA10), from 59‐year‐old man, and 69‐year‐old woman (both Caucasian, cause of death cardiovascular disorders), were provided by the Newcastle Brain Tissue Resource, and the immunohistochemical study was approved by the Danish Regional Ethics Committee.</p><p>Two patients with mutations in <i>Cox6a2</i> were identified in a previous study (Inoue <i>et al</i>, <a>2019</a>). PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons were identified in slices based on EGFP expression. The time period of recordings (P22–P45) corresponds to the late maturational period of PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons (Okaty <i>et al</i>, <a>2009</a>; Miyamae <i>et al</i>, <a>2017</a>). The recorded cells were later split into two equal parts of maturational period, P22–P33 and P34–P45; for explanations, see the <a>Results</a> section.</p><p>On the day of the experiment, mice were deeply anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (MSD, 100 mg/kg body weight) and xylazine (ScanVet, 10 mg/kg body weight) and transcardially perfused with cooled (4°C) choline chloride artificial cerebrospinal fluid (cc‐aCSF; 110 mM choline chloride, 25 mM NaHCO<sub>3</sub>, 1.25 mM NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 2.5 mM KCl, 0.5 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 7 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 25 mM glucose, 11.6 mM ascorbic acid, 3.1 mM sodium pyruvate; all from Sigma‐Aldrich) saturated with carbogen (95% O<sub>2</sub>, 5% CO<sub>2</sub>). The head of the mouse was cut off as soon as the perfusion was completed and submerged in cool cc‐aCSF saturated with carbogen. The brain was extracted, and the cerebellum and olfactory bulbs were removed. The caudal side of the brain was glued to a metal stage and placed in the slicing chamber filled with cool cc‐aCSF saturated with carbogen. Brains were sliced into 300‐μm‐thick coronal sections using a VT1200 vibratome (Leica Biosystems, Germany) and subsequently transferred to regular aCSF (125 mM NaCl, 25 mM NaHCO<sub>3</sub>, 1.25 mM NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 2.5 mM KCl, 0.5 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 7 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 25 mM glucose; all from Sigma‐Aldrich) saturated with carbogen at 37°C for 5 min. The slices were then maintained at RT until recording.</p><p>Brain slices were placed in a recording chamber under the objective of an upright BX51WI microscope (Olympus Life Science Europa, Germany). The chamber was continuously perfused with aCSF saturated with carbogen and heated to 30<sup>–</sup>32°C using a bath controller V heating system (Luigs &amp; Neumann, Germany). Patch electrodes (resistance of 2–9 MΩ) were pulled on a P‐87 flaming brown pipette puller (Sutter Instruments Co., USA) and were filled with 122 mM K‐gluconate, 5 mM Na<sub>2</sub>ATP, 2.5 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 0.0003 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 5.6 mM Mg‐gluconate, 5 mM K‐Hepes, 5 mM H‐Hepes, 1 mM EGTA, 10 mM biocytin (all from Sigma‐Aldrich), and 0.01 mM Alexa Fluor 568 hydrazide (Invitrogen; pH 7.4). The patch pipette was positioned on a three‐axis motorized micromanipulator (Luigs &amp; Neumann, Germany) connected to a CV‐7B Headstage (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale CA, USA). EGFP‐labeled cortical PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons were identified by epifluorescence and were recorded with the whole‐cell configuration patch‐clamp technique in current‐clamp mode. Recordings were made using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale CA, USA). Data were sampled at 10 kHz, sent to a computer via a 1440A digitizer (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale CA, USA), and displayed by means of Clampex software (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale CA, USA).</p><p>The input resistance was calculated as the slope of the linear fit of the current–voltage relationship between −10 and +10 mV. The spike threshold was determined as the first positive peak above 10<sup>12</sup> mV/s<sup>3</sup> present on the third derivative of the voltage trace in a 1‐ms time window preceding the peak of the action potential (Henze &amp; Buzsáki, <a>2001</a>). The upstroke of action potential was calculated as the maximum of the first derivative of the membrane potential occurring in a 1‐ms time window before the peak. The maximal firing rate was determined by applying incremental 2‐s depolarizing current pulses. For all cells tested, we observed that the frequency reached a plateau value. The ability of interneurons to sustain repetitive firing was checked by applying 30‐s depolarizing current pulses of increasing intensities.</p><p>Data analysis was performed using Matlab 2016a (MathWorks, USA), Python 3.6 (Python Software Foundation), and Clampfit 10.7 (Molecular Devices, USA). Statistics and figures were done with Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, USA) and OriginPro 2017 (OriginLab, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation of the mean (SD). Normality was tested using Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed samples were compared with the two‐tailed independent <i>t</i>‐test. Samples that did not pass the normality test were compared using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney test.</p><h3>Reconstruction of neuronal morphology</h3><p>Once the electrophysiological recordings were completed, PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons were filled with biocytin (Sigma‐Aldrich). Briefly, the sections were blocked and permeabilized with 1% BSA, 1% NGS, and 0.4% Triton X‐100 for 1 h at RT, followed by incubation with the rabbit anti‐EGFP antibody ON at 4°C. A total of 24 WT PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons and 19 <i>Cox6a2</i><sup><i>−</i>/<i>−</i></sup> PV<sup>+</sup> interneurons were reconstructed and analyzed. After 90 min of the incubation at 42°C, the plate was taken out of the thermocycler, and 0.4 μl of TSO (12.5 μM, /5Biosg/AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGTACATrGrG+G/3/) was added to each sample and incubation continued for another 12:20. Female mice were chosen due to economic reasons and since sex‐specific differences were not the major focus of the study. The estrus cycle was not checked. At the time of the first behavioral test, mice were 5–6 months old. The cohort was selected from littermates derived from heterozygous <i>Cox6a</i><sup><i>+</i>/<i>−</i></sup> breedings. The following behavioral paradigms have been implemented: the OFT, the SIT, and the PPI of the acoustic startle response test. C57BL/6J female mice have been shown to exhibit a stable behavior throughout the four phases of the estrous cycle in several behavioral tests (Meziane <i>et al</i>, <a>2007</a>). Therefore, they represent an adequate population of animals for the purposes of the study. To reduce potential external stressors, mice were not disturbed for 3 days before the start of a training session/test. All training sessions/tests were performed during the light phase.</p><p>Mice were submitted to the OFT and SIT sequentially in the same arena, which consisted of a brightly lit 120‐cm‐wide circular arena surrounded by 60‐cm height walls. In the OFT, mice were recorded for 10 min, which provided us information about their locomotor activity and level of anxiety‐like behavior. Importantly, the OFT was also used to habituate test mice to the arena, reducing novelty‐related locomotor hyperactivity that could potentially affect social interaction in the SIT that was performed right after the OFT. Mice have a natural tendency to interact with an unfamiliar rodent over a novel inanimate object. Thus, in the SIT, two identical cylindric containers that allowed visual, olfactory, and tactile interactions between the test and stranger mice were placed in the center of the arena. A female stranger mouse of approximately the same age and body weight as the test mouse was placed in one of the cylinders. The other container remained empty. Test mice activity was analyzed for 5 min. To assess their level of sociability, we measured the time test mice spent interacting, both directly and indirectly, with the stranger mouse and the empty cylinder. By indirect interaction, we refer to the time the animal's nose was within 2 cm of the cylinders and pointing toward them. A piezoelectric accelerometer transduced the displacement of the tubes in response to movements of the animal. Mice were acclimatized in the tube for 5 min before sessions started. The session started and finished with five startle trials, each of which consisted of a stimulus of 120 dB and 40 ms of duration. Then, 35 trials were delivered in a semi‐randomized order: 10 trials of 120 dB; 20 trials of 120 dB preceded by a prepulse of 72, 74, 78, or 86 dB (five of each type; denoted PPI72, PPI74, PPI78, and PPI86, respectively); and five trials with white background noise only. Tube movements were registered for 100 ms after the start of the acoustic stimulus. The average response over the 100 ms of each trial was calculated, and the average response for the trials of the same level of prepulse (e.g., the five startle trials preceded by a prepulse of 72 dB) was obtained. These values were used to calculate the PPI, which is defined as the percent reduction in the acoustic startle reaction after presenting a prepulse compared to the acoustic startle response to the 120‐dB noise pulse without the preceding prepulse: %PPI = 100 − [(Average response to Prepulse + 120 dB noise pulse)/Average response to 120 dB noise pulse] × 100.</p><h3>Cognitive assessment of the human subject carrying <i>Cox6a2</i> mutations</h3><p>The evaluation of the cognitive abilities and intelligence of the human patient carrying mutations in <i>Cox6a2</i> was performed using the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS)‐III (Wechsler, <a>1997</a>). This intelligence quotient (IQ) test provides scores for verbal IQ and performance IQ. Verbal IQ is assessed by verbal comprehension and working memory tasks, while performance IQ is evaluated by tasks related to perceptual organization and processing speed. The total score obtained is called full‐scale IQ.</p><h3>Statistics</h3><p>Statistical analysis of the data collected from the immunohistochemistry experiments, reconstructed neurons, and behavioral tests were performed with Prism 7.0 (GraphPad software). Normality of data distribution was analyzed by D'Agostino and Shapiro–Wilk's tests. When comparing two populations, normally distributed data were analyzed by two‐tailed Student's <i>t</i>‐test, whereas non‐normally distributed data were analyzed by two‐tailed Mann–Whitney test. More than two populations were compared using one‐way ANOVA. When comparing two parameters between two or more populations, a two‐way ANOVA with Sidak or Tukey's correction was carried out. <i>F</i> test was used to compare the variances. Females and males were analyzed together and the graphs represent joint sex data, except for the behavioral tests, where only female mice were used. For detailed statistical information, see <a>Appendix Table S5</a>.</p></div></div></section>
