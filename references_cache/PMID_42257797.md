# Predictors of in-hospital mortality in severe leptospirosis: a retrospective cohort study (São Paulo, 2017-2025).
**Authors:** de Freitas JJA, Nardotto DDMP, Netto LC, Dias SL, Ho YL
**Journal:** Infection (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s15010-026-02845-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s15010-026-02845-4)

## Content

PURPOSE: Although advances in intensive care have improved survival of patients with severe leptospirosis, it remains with high mortality. We aimed to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality among patients with severe leptospirosis admitted to a tertiary intensive care unit.

METHODS: We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults with leptospirosis admitted to the ICU of a tertiary referral hospital in Brazil, between January 1, 2017, and March 3, 2025. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and intensive care support variables were collected. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify independent predictors of death.

RESULTS: Forty-four patients were included; median age was 42 years, and 95.5% were male. In-hospital mortality was 20.5% (9/44). Severe multiorgan dysfunction was common; 56.8% of patients required renal replacement therapy and invasive mechanical ventilation in 43.2%. In the clinical multivariable model, alveolar hemorrhage (aOR 7.91, 95% CI 1.07-58.61) and arrhythmia (aOR 13.82, 95% CI 2.06-92.64) were independently associated with death. In the laboratory-based model, urea > 180 mg/dL (aOR 9.01, 95% CI 1.36-59.65) and platelet count < 45 × 10³/mm³ (aOR 14.07, 95% CI 1.40-141.88) were independently associated with mortality.

CONCLUSION: In this contemporary ICU cohort, mortality in severe leptospirosis was primarily driven by respiratory and cardiac complications rather than isolated renal dysfunction. Although renal failure remained a marker of severity, its prognostic impact appeared attenuated by timely renal replacement therapy.

Abstract
Purpose Although advances in intensive care have improved survival of patients with severe leptospirosis, it remains with
high mortality. We aimed to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality among patients with severe leptospirosis
admitted to a tertiary intensive care unit.
Methods We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults with leptospirosis admitted to the ICU of a tertiary referral
hospital in Brazil, between January 1, 2017, and March 3, 2025. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and intensive care sup -
port variables were collected. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Multivariable logistic regression models were
used to identify independent predictors of death.
Results Forty-four patients were included; median age was 42 years, and 95.5% were male. In-hospital mortality was 20.5%
(9/44). Severe multiorgan dysfunction was common; 56.8% of patients required renal replacement therapy and invasive
mechanical ventilation in 43.2%. In the clinical multivariable model, alveolar hemorrhage (aOR 7.91, 95% CI 1.07–58.61)
and arrhythmia (aOR 13.82, 95% CI 2.06–92.64) were independently associated with death. In the laboratory-based model,
urea > 180 mg/dL (aOR 9.01, 95% CI 1.36–59.65) and platelet count < 45 × 10³/mm³ (aOR 14.07, 95% CI 1.40–141.88) were
independently associated with mortality.
Conclusion In this contemporary ICU cohort, mortality in severe leptospirosis was primarily driven by respiratory and car-
diac complications rather than isolated renal dysfunction. Although renal failure remained a marker of severity, its prognostic
impact appeared attenuated by timely renal replacement therapy.

In Brazil, leptospirosis is an endemic disease of manda -

periods, particularly in large urban centers. In 2023, 3,428
cases were confirmed nationwide, with 288 reported deaths

Clinically, leptospirosis often presents as a non-specific
febrile illness, making early recognition difficult. The dis -
ease is classically biphasic, with an initial leptospiemic
phase characterized by fever, headache, chills, and myalgia,
followed by a transient improvement and an immune-medi-
ated phase that may include aseptic meningitis or uveitis. In
approximately 5–10% of symptomatic cases, leptospirosis
progresses to a severe multisystem form known as Weil’s
disease, which is associated with mortality rates of up to
15%. Hallmark manifestations include acute kidney injury
(AKI), jaundice predominantly due to conjugated hyper -
bilirubinemia, and severe pulmonary involvement, most
notably alveolar hemorrhage [4]. Acute respiratory distress
syndrome (ARDS) may also occur, sometimes indepen -
dently of severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, with imaging
typically demonstrating bilateral infiltrates consistent with
alveolar hemorrhage, ARDS, or pulmonary edema [5].
Pulmonary involvement is the major determinant of prog-
nosis. A prospective observational study reported severe
pulmonary disease in approximately 4% of patients with
high microscopic agglutination test (MAT) titers, with mor-
tality approaching 70% among those who developed respi -
ratory complications [6]. Coagulation abnormalities further
contribute to disease severity, with documented activation
of the coagulation cascade, thrombocytopenia, and elevated
D-dimer levels, which may progress to disseminated intra -
vascular coagulation and life-threatening hemorrhage [7].
Renal dysfunction is another central feature of severe
leptospirosis. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis
found that approximately 50% of patients develop AKI,
nearly 15% require renal replacement therapy, and mortal -
ity among those with renal involvement is approximately
12.5% [8]. Importantly, a substantial proportion of survivors
subsequently develop chronic kidney disease, highlighting
the long-term consequences of severe infection.
Supportive strategies in the intensive care unit (ICU)
appear to play a critical role in outcomes. A prospective
observational study involving patients with leptospirosis
complicated by AKI and ARDS demonstrated significantly
lower mortality among those who received early, daily dial-
ysis compared with late, intermittent dialysis [ 9]. Although
limited by its non-randomized design, these findings suggest
that timely and intensive organ support may favorably influ-
ence outcomes.
Despite these advances, leptospirosis continues to cause
substantial morbidity and mortality. Identifying predictors
of death and clarifying the role of advanced supportive ther-
apies remain essential to optimize the management of criti -
cally ill patients. Accordingly, this study aims to describe
the clinical, sociodemographic, and supportive care charac-
teristics of patients with severe leptospirosis admitted to the
ICU and to identify risk factors associated with mortality.
Materials and methods
Study design
We conducted a retrospective observational study with
the aim of identifying factors associated with in-hospital
mortality among patients with severe leptospirosis admit -
ted to the intensive care unit (ICU), as well as describing
the advanced supportive strategies used in this setting. The
study was conducted in a tertiary university hospital (Hospi-
tal das Clínicas da Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade
de São Paulo—HCFMUSP) in Brazil, a national referral
center for the management of severe leptospirosis.
Participants
Patients admitted between January 1, 2017, and March 3,
2025, with a diagnosis of leptospirosis were assessed. Cases
were identified through leptospirosis notification records
from the hospital’s Epidemiological Surveillance Unit and
were subsequently confirmed by detailed medical record
review.
The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years at admission;
“highly probable” or “confirmed” cases of leptospirosis –
diagnoses were considered confirmed when serological tests
(MAT/ELISA) or serum polymerase chain reaction (PCR)
were positive; cases were classified as “highly probable”
when confirmatory tests were not performed, but clinical
and laboratory findings were compatible and no alternative
diagnosis was identified; ICU admission for at least 24 h.
Patients were excluded if essential clinical or laboratory
data were unavailable or incomplete, precluding adequate
analysis.
Procedures
The data were collected by the authors JJAF and DDMPN.
Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and ICU advanced sup -
port-related variables were collected. Demographic and
severity variables included age, sex, exposure risk, and

severity scores (APACHE II and SAPS 3), as well as the
presence of comorbidities. Sex was recorded as assigned at
birth based on medical records; gender identity data were
not available. Exposure risk was classified as high when
there was a report of contact with floods or standing water,
high-risk occupational activity (such as contact with sewage
or waste collection) and/or contact with rodents; low when
there was documentation that such contact did not occur; or
indeterminate when no description was available.
Clinical variables encompassed baseline and follow-up
parameters. At baseline, time intervals from symptom onset
to first medical attendance, to first hospital admission, and
to admission at HCFMUSP were recorded, as were clini -
cal manifestations. Regarding antimicrobial therapy, the
interval between symptom onset and the first dose of anti -
microbial and treatment duration were collected. During
hospitalization, the occurrence of new organ dysfunction
(renal, respiratory, cardiovascular, hepatic, among others)
and the timing of its onset were recorded.
At ICU admission, hemodynamic and physiological data
were recorded, as well as respiratory parameters, includ -
ing PaO₂ and the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio. The need for mechanical
ventilation at ICU admission and at any time during hospi -
talization was also documented. During hospitalization, we
recorded the occurrence of oliguria, the need for dialysis,
and new-onset arrhythmias, defined as rhythm disturbances
first documented during the hospital stay, including atrial
fibrillation, paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, ven -
tricular tachyarrhythmias, and bradyarrhythmias.
Laboratory data were collected at hospital admission and
at ICU admission. These included hemoglobin, hematocrit,
leukocytes, and platelets; coagulation profile (INR); urea,
creatinine, sodium, and potassium; arterial blood gas analy-
sis (pH and bicarbonate); liver enzymes, total bilirubin and
fractions; and creatine phosphokinase (CPK). Respiratory
assessment included the need for mechanical ventilation,
PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio at ICU admission, the worst PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio
observed during hospitalization, and duration of mechanical
ventilation. For renal replacement therapy, when indicated,
parameters immediately before dialysis initiation were
recorded (including urea, creatinine, sodium, potassium,
calcium, and urine output), as well as cumulative fluid bal -
ance in the first 3 ICU days. Additionally, the maximum
values of AST, ALT, direct bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, and
lipase during hospitalization were extracted.
The ICU advanced supportive strategies assessed
included hemodynamic support (use of vasoactive drugs),
ventilatory support (PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio in admission, the worst
PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, need for mechanical ventilation and dura -
tion of mechanical ventilation), renal replacement therapy
(renal replacement therapy modality and renal parameters/
urine output before dialysis), and use of blood products
(packed red blood cells and platelets). Alveolar hemorrhage
was defined by the presence of diffuse bilateral pulmonary
infiltrates associated with reported hemoptysis and/or aspi -
ration of bloody secretions through the endotracheal tube.
Outcome
The primary outcome was hospital mortality. The second -
ary outcome was to determine the risk factors for mortality
and comparison between the patients who received dialysis
versus those who did not.
Statistical analysis
Data were initially entered into a Microsoft Excel spread -
sheet and subsequently analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics
(version 21). A 5% significance level (p < 0.05) was adopted,
with estimates presented with 95% confidence intervals
(95% CI). Normality of continuous variables was assessed
using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data with a normal distribution
were expressed as mean ± SD, while non-normally distrib -
uted data were expressed as median and interquartile range
(IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and
percentages. Binary clinical variables and interventions,
including oliguria, dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, arrhyth -
mia, need for mechanical ventilation, and dialysis, were also
evaluated.
In univariable analyses, the association between cat -
egorical variables and mortality was assessed using the
chi-square test or the Fisher exact test, as appropriate and
crude odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% CIs were
estimated. Quantitative variables were compared using Stu-
dent’s t test for normal distributions or the Mann–Whitney
test for non-normal distributions.
Some continuous variables were subsequently catego -
rized for association analyses with the outcome, based on
clinical cutoffs or according to the best accuracy point on
the ROC curve, defined by the Youden index for prediction
of death: APACHE II (> 17), platelets (< 45,000/mm³), urea
(> 180 mg/dL), creatinine (> 5.0 mg/dL), bicarbonate (< 20
mEq/L). The presence of oliguria, dyspnea, cough, hemop -
tysis, arrhythmia, and the need for mechanical ventilation
(at admission and during hospitalization) and dialysis,
among others, were also analyzed as dichotomous variables.
Given the limited number of events, a parsimonious
approach was adopted for multivariable model building
to reduce the risk of overfitting. Variables with p < 0.05 in
univariable analysis and/or considered clinically relevant
were selected as candidates for logistic regression. A for -
ward stepwise approach was used to assess the stability of
the model (including standard errors), the width of confi -

Results
A total of 44 patients were included. Median age was 42
years (IQR 26.8–58.3), and 95.5% were male, most of whom
had no previously known comorbidities. Nearly all patients
were classified as having a high exposure risk (79.5%). At
ICU admission, disease severity was moderate to high, with
a mean APACHE II score of 17.3 (SD 7.77) and a mean
SAPS 3 score of 65.18 (SD 15.89) (Table 1).
The median time from symptom onset to first medical
attendance was three days (IQR 1–6), five days (IQR 4–6.8)
to first hospital admission, and 6.5 days (IQR 5–8) to admis-
sion to the tertiary hospital. The most frequent presenting
symptoms were jaundice (100%), myalgia (90.9%), fever
(81.8%), dark urine (70.5%), calf pain (65.9%), and oliguria
(61.4%), whereas respiratory symptoms were less common
(Table 1).
At hospital admission, laboratory tests showed ane -
mia and marked thrombocytopenia (median hemoglobin
10.55 g/dL [IQR 10.0–12.3]; median platelet count 44,500/
mm³ [28,250–76,750]). Renal dysfunction was prominent
(mean urea 165.9 mg/dL [SD 66.0] and mean creatinine
4.94 mg/dL [SD 2.21]), accompanied by metabolic acidosis
(median pH 7.36 [IQR 7.31–7.40]). Biochemical evidence
of cholestasis was common, with marked hyperbilirubi -
nemia (total bilirubin 13.98 mg/dL [IQR 8.36–22.72])
(Table 1).
Antimicrobial therapy was initiated at a median of 5.5
days (IQR 4–7) after symptom onset and was maintained for
a median of seven days (IQR 7–8). Hemorrhagic complica-
tions were frequent, including alveolar hemorrhage (43.2%),
bleeding at puncture sites (22.7%), and upper gastrointesti -
nal bleeding (11.4%), with high transfusion requirements
for packed red blood cells (43.2%) and platelets (29.5%).
Invasive mechanical ventilation was required in 43.2%
of patients and vasopressors in 52.3%. Mean PaO₂/FiO₂
at ICU admission was 325.8 (SD 157.69), and the mean
worst PaO₂/FiO₂ during hospitalization was 168.7 (SD
73.9). Renal replacement therapy was required in 56.8% of
patients, most commonly on a daily schedule (38.6%), with
a median time to initiation of one day of hospital admission
(IQR 0–1) and a median duration of five days (IQR 3–14).
Cumulative fluid balance over the first three ICU days was
− 46 mL (IQR − 2021 to 1666). Cardiac arrhythmia occurred
in 25% of patients. Overall in-hospital mortality was 20.5%
(Table 2).
Patients requiring renal replacement therapy differed
from those who did not in baseline characteristics, severity,
organ dysfunction, and ICU support (Table 3). They were
significantly older (median age 52 years [IQR 37.5–59.5]
vs. 32 years [IQR 24–48]; p = 0.018) and exhibited greater
severity at admission, with higher APACHE II (20.24
vs. 13.42; mean difference 6.88, 95% CI 2.48 to 11.16;
p = 0.003) and SAPS 3 scores (71.12 vs. 57.37; mean differ-
ence 13.75, 95% CI 4.85 to 22.65; p = 0.003). Additionally,
oliguria was more frequent (80.0% vs. 36.8%; p = 0.005),
and they had more severe renal and metabolic abnormalities
at admission, including higher urea (188.4 vs. 136.3 mg/dL;
p = 0.008), higher creatinine (5.9 vs. 3.68 mg/dL; p < 0.001),
and higher potassium (4.29 vs. 3.53 mmol/L; p = 0.006), and
greater severity of metabolic acidosis (Table 3).
Hemorrhagic complications were more frequent among
patients who required dialysis, including alveolar hemor -

ture sites (36.0% vs. 5.3%; p = 0.027), requiring more blood
component transfusions. These patients also more frequently
required invasive mechanical ventilation (64.0% vs. 15.8%;
p = 0.002) and vasopressors (76.0% vs. 21.1%; p = 0.001).

Discussion
This cohort was composed predominantly of young adult
men with high exposure risk, consistent with Brazilian and
international reports of severe leptospirosis. This marked
male predominance likely reflects greater occupational,
environmental, and social vulnerability-related exposure to
contaminated water, mud, soil, sewage, and waste, particu -
larly during floods and heavy rainfall events. In our setting,
these exposures commonly affect men working in waste col-
lection, agriculture, and other outdoor or manual occupa -
tions, as well as people experiencing homelessness or living
in vulnerable urban areas.
The low prevalence of chronic comorbidities suggests
that disease severity is driven primarily by the intensity of
the infectious insult and the host inflammatory response
rather than by underlying chronic disease [10]. The high fre-
quency of classical clinical features of Weil’s disease com -
bined with severe laboratory abnormalities indicates delayed
presentation, with patients already exhibiting advanced dis-
ease at the time of admission to tertiary care. This is sup -
ported by the substantial interval between symptom onset,
first medical contact, and referral hospital admission, which
likely contributed to greater severity at ICU admission.
Despite the inclusion of exclusively ICU-admitted
patients with high severity scores, the observed mortality
rate of 20.5% was relatively low. Mortality rates reported in
the literature vary widely across settings and periods, sug -
gesting that prognosis reflects the interaction between base-
line severity and timely access to advanced supportive care.
Earlier hospital-based studies reported case fatality rates of
15–18% [11, 12]. In contrast, Delmas et al. demonstrated
that a protocol advocating immediate ICU transfer after ini-
tial evaluation was associated with a marked reduction in
ICU mortality to 6%, reinforcing the importance of early
intervention and ICU availability in modulating outcomes
[13].
Renal and metabolic abnormalities were prominent at
ICU admission. Median urea and creatinine levels were con-
sistent with AKIN stage 3 acute kidney injury, and bicarbon-
ate levels indicated severe metabolic acidosis. Nevertheless,
43% of patients did not require renal replacement therapy
(RRT). Even among those managed without dialysis, renal
dysfunction remained significant, with median urea and cre-
atinine levels of 138 mg/dL and 3.68 mg/dL, respectively,
corresponding to at least AKIN stage 2, along with marked
thrombocytopenia. These findings raise important questions
regarding the timing and indications for dialysis in Weil’s
disease.
Historically, acute kidney injury—particularly oliguria—
has been a major prognostic determinant in severe lepto -
spirosis, and hyperkalemia has also been associated with
mortality [11, 12]. In contemporary ICU cohorts, however,

the prognostic impact of renal failure may be attenuated by
timely access to RRT. In the cohort reported by Delmas et
al., neither creatinine levels nor the need for RRT was asso-
ciated with mortality, suggesting that adequate renal support
may mitigate the lethal consequences of AKI [ 13]. None-
theless, the concept of “early dialysis” in Weil’s disease
remains poorly defined.
In the clinical trial by Andrade et al., intensive daily
dialysis was associated with lower mortality compared with
a conservative strategy. However, all included patients had
advanced renal dysfunction and concomitant respiratory
failure requiring mechanical ventilation, underscoring the
coexistence of multiorgan dysfunction [9]. Similarly, in our
cohort, the indication for dialysis was influenced not only
by renal and metabolic parameters but also by dysfunction
in other organ systems, including oliguria, alveolar hem -
orrhage, and the need for vasoactive support, resulting in
particularly high severity scores. Notably, RRT use was not
associated with mortality in univariable analysis, support -
ing the notion that dialysis decisions should be guided by
overall clinical context, multiorgan involvement, and vol -
ume status rather than isolated laboratory thresholds. Once
indicated, RRT should be initiated promptly, with modal -
ity and intensity tailored to the degree of renal dysfunction,
metabolic derangement, and fluid overload [9, 14].
Respiratory involvement emerged as a key determinant
of outcome. The finding of alveolar hemorrhage as an inde-
pendent risk factor for death reinforces the prognostic rel -
evance of respiratory failure in severe leptospirosis. Unlike
renal dysfunction, respiratory failure remains particularly
challenging to manage, especially in resource-limited set -
tings. Previous studies have consistently linked pulmonary
manifestations—including dyspnea, crackles, and alveo -
lar infiltrates—to worse prognosis [ 11, 12, 15, 16]. In the
cohort of Delmas et al., refractory ARDS was present in
most fatal cases, suggesting that progression to severe pul -
monary failure may be associated with a narrow window of
reversibility [13].

The use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
(ECMO) has increasingly been reported in patients with
ARDS secondary to Weil’s disease. A recent narrative
review encompassing 43 cases reported an overall survival
rate of 84%, indicating that ECMO is feasible and poten -
tially beneficial in selected patients [ 17]. In our series, all
three patients who required ECMO survived.
Leptospirosis has also been investigated as a poten -
tial contributor to chronic kidney disease, particularly in
endemic regions such as Latin America, where recurrent or
severe infection may contribute to the burden of renal dis -
ease [8]. Although nearly half of the patients in our cohort
required renal replacement therapy during hospitalization,
none of the survivors remained dialysis-dependent after
discharge. However, because long-term follow-up and post-
discharge serum creatinine measurements were not system-
atically available, we were unable to determine the incidence
of chronic kidney disease in this cohort. Future prospective
studies should assess long-term renal outcomes after severe
leptospirosis, particularly among patients requiring renal
replacement therapy during the acute phase.
We observed an association between arrhythmia and
mortality, which may reflect myocardial involvement as
well as the effects of electrolyte disturbances and acidosis.
Previous studies have identified myocarditis and arrhyth -
mias as independent risk factors for severity and death in
Weil’s disease [18–20].
This study has limitations, including its retrospective
design, limited sample size, and small number of deaths,
which reduce statistical power and limit multivariable
adjustment. These constraints preclude causal inference and
increase the risk of type II error. In addition, the use of corti-
costeroids or other immunosuppressive agents as adjunctive
therapy aimed at modulating the host immune response was
not systematically evaluated; therefore, we could not assess
their efficacy or association with clinical outcomes.
Future studies should better define optimal timing and
intensity of RRT using detailed pre-dialysis data, and sys -
tematically evaluate ventilation strategies in patients with
severe pulmonary involvement, ideally in prospective
cohorts adjusted for disease severity.
In summary, our findings suggest a shift in the prognos -
tic hierarchy in the ICU era. Renal dysfunction remains a
key marker of severity, particularly when accompanied by
oliguria, but its impact may be mitigated by timely support-
ive care and RRT. In contrast, severe respiratory failure has
emerged as the principal driver of mortality in critically ill
patients with leptospirosis, underscoring the need for early
recognition and aggressive management of pulmonary
complications.
