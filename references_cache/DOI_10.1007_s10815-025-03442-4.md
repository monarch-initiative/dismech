Abstract
Purpose To highlight the successful use of assisted reproductive technologies in achieving pregnancy and healthy childbirth 
in a woman with Swyer syndrome, a rare disorder of sex development characterized by gonadal dysgenesis, elevated 
gonadotropin levels, oestrogen insufficiency, tall stature, delayed puberty, and primary amenorrhoea. This case highlights 
the efficacy of reproductive treatments for individuals with nonfunctional gonads resulting from complete gonadal dysgenesis.
Methods This report describes a woman diagnosed with Swyer syndrome at 18 years, presenting with primary amenorrhoea 
and delayed puberty. Following diagnosis, the patient underwent bilateral gonadectomy due to the increased risk of germ 
cell neoplasia and began hormone replacement therapy to address oestrogen insufficiency. At 31, the patient pursued fertility 
treatment, undergoing in vitro fertilisation (IVF) with donated oocytes.
Results The IVF treatment using donor oocytes led to a successful pregnancy without complications. The pregnancy 
culminated in the delivery of a healthy baby via caesarean section, marking a successful outcome for a patient with Swyer 
syndrome.
Conclusion Assisted reproductive technologies, particularly in vitro fertilisation (IVF) using oocyte donation, provide a 
feasible option for individuals diagnosed with Swyer syndrome to achieve parenthood. This case adds to the growing research 
on reproductive success in women with complete gonadal dysgenesis, emphasizing the potential for positive outcomes 
through specific reproductive approaches.

Introduction
Swyer syndrome, also known as 46,XY gonadal dysgenesis, 
is a disorder in which patients have a feminine phenotype but 
a male genotype. The diagnosis is confirmed based on clini-
cal findings, hormone profiling, karyotyping, genetic analy-
sis, and histological examination of the gonads [1 ]. This 
syndrome was first described by Swyer in 1955 [2]. The inci-
dence of this rare condition is estimated to be approximately 
1 in 80,000 births [3 , 4]. Most patients present with tall 
stature, delayed puberty, and primary amenorrhoea. A nor -
mal vagina and fallopian tubes are usually found, along with 
a hypoplastic uterus and streak gonads. The inability of the 
streak gonads to function as normal ovaries typically leads to 
hypergonadotropic hypogonadism [5–7]. In addition, streak 
gonads carry a 30% risk of developing gonadoblastoma and 
a 50–60% higher risk of germ cell malignancies, primar -
ily dysgerminomas [1 ]. Therefore, patients with gonadal 
dysgenesis and a 46,XY karyotype should be referred for 
bilateral gonadectomy [6 ]. It is also recommended that the 
fallopian tubes be removed during surgery to reduce cancer 
risk and significantly lower the chance of ectopic pregnan-
cies in women using donor oocytes for future pregnancies 
[8]. Despite common infertility issues due to aplastic gonads 
and the inability to undergo gametogenesis, oocyte donation 
(OD) is an effective fertilisation method. This allows preg-
nancy to be achieved and, in hereditary cases, avoids pass-
ing the condition to offspring [1, 7, 9]. Herein, we present a

Case report
A 31-year-old woman was referred to a specialised fertility 
centre for possible treatment options. Primary infertility had 
lasted for 2 years, and the patient’s partner was 39 years old 
and healthy. At this time, the patient was taking, in her own 
words, “pills for cycle regulation” and was unaware of the 
reason for the cycle disorder. Her medical history included 
surgical intervention for a deformed sternum during child-
hood, scoliosis of the spine several years later, and a pelvic 
laparoscopic procedure at the age of 18 years. Treatment 
with pills was initiated immediately after pelvic surgery. 
Menstruation began at 19 years of age. The patient did not 
report any other significant health issues.
To ensure accuracy, medical data were verified using 
reliable medical documentation: an 18-year-old patient was 
referred to a gynaecologist because of primary amenor -
rhoea. At the time of admission, she was 165 cm tall and 
weighed 48 kg (BMI 17.63 kg/m 2). Puberty was assessed 
using the Tanner scale and assigned as stage 1 for breasts 
and stage 4 for pubic hair. The external genitalia were 
female. Transabdominal ultrasonography revealed a hypo-
plastic uterus (31 × 8 × 10 mm) with a thin endometrium; 
however, no ovaries were identified. The hormonal profile 
revealed hypergonadotropic hypoestrogenism (FSH > 170 
IU/L, LH 60 IU/L, estradiol <73.4 pmol/L) and slightly 
increased testosterone (2.24 nmol/L). The karyotype of 
peripheral blood leukocytes was 46,XY. Although the diag-
nosis was not formulated clearly (the working diagnosis was 
“gonadal dysgenesis”), laparoscopic bilateral gonadectomy 
was performed. A rudimentary uterus was observed during 
the procedure, along with the fallopian tubes on both sides, 
holding a piece of tissue resembling a streak gonad. Histo-
pathological examination confirmed the presence of streak 
gonads. Postoperative hormone replacement therapy (HRT) 
was initiated. The patient was lost to follow-up during the 
subsequent 13 years.
At the current visit, the patient was 180 cm tall and 
weighed 62 kg (BMI 19.1 kg/m 2). She had Tanner stage 3 
breasts and stage 5 pubic hair. Transvaginal ultrasonography 
revealed a normally developed retroverted uterus (53 × 24 
× 34 mm) with an endometrial thickness of 5 mm (Fig.  1).
A rechecked hormonal profile confirmed hypergonado-
tropic hypoestrogenism, with normal testosterone levels. 
A diagnosis of pure gonadal dysgenesis 46,XY, or Swyer 
syndrome, was established. The patient was referred for

additional genetic counselling because of potential SRY and

Upon understanding her diagnosis, the patient consulted a 
psychologist. She was referred for long-term psychotherapy. 
In vitro fertilisation with oocyte donation was recommended 
for the management of infertility. An ART procedure using 
vitrified donated oocytes from the local gamete bank was 
performed 1 year later. Ten vitrified oocytes were thawed, 
and nine were injected with the husband’s sperm via intra-
cytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). Fertilization was suc-
cessful in all nine injected oocytes, resulting in a fertili-
zation rate of 100%. On day 5 of culture, four blastocysts 
were observed, corresponding to a blastulation rate of 44%. 
The morphological qualities of the blastocysts, assessed 
according to the Gardner grading system, were 4Aa, 3Ab, 
3Ab, and 4Bb, respectively. The blastocyst with the high-
est morphological quality (4Aa) was selected for transfer, 
while the remaining three blastocysts were vitrified for 
cryopreservation.
Endometrial preparation was initiated with oral oestradiol 
at a total daily dose of 6 mg (administered as 2 mg three 
times daily). After 15 days of treatment, adequate endome-
trial development was confirmed by transvaginal ultrasound, 
which revealed a trilaminar endometrium with a thickness 
of 8.9 mm. Progesterone supplementation was subsequently 
introduced, consisting of 25 mg/day of subcutaneous pro-
gesterone and 600 mg/day of vaginal progesterone. On the 
sixth day of progesterone administration, a single blasto -
cyst was transferred into the uterine cavity under ultrasound 
guidance.
On the 10th day following blastocyst transfer, early serum 
human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) levels were quantified 
at 73 IU/L. A viable intrauterine pregnancy was confirmed 
4 weeks later. Hormonal treatment with oestradiol and pro-
gesterone was discontinued entirely at 13 weeks of gesta-
tion. The pregnancy proceeded without any complications. 
An elective cesarean section was performed at 39 weeks of 
gestation after premature rupture of membranes. The indica-
tion was a history of surgical spinal fixation for scoliosis. A 
healthy 3465-g baby was born. The postpartum period was 
uneventful, and the infant was fed pumped breast milk for 
6 months. After the completion of breastfeeding, hormone 
replacement therapy was resumed.
Discussion
Swyer syndrome is a rare condition affecting sexual develop-
ment. In most cases, the aetiology of this disorder remains 
unknown. It can be inherited in an autosomal-dominant or 
recessive manner. Sometimes, spontaneous mutations may 
occur, or inheritance may be associated with sex [3 –5]. 
Deletions in the DNA-binding region of the SRY  gene have 
been observed in 10–20% of women with Swyer syndrome. 
In the remaining 80–90% of cases, SRY remains intact, sug-
gesting that mutations in other factors responsible for testis 
determination may be involved [9 –11]. Approximately 4% 
of individuals with Swyer syndrome have family members 
with the same sexual development disorder [12]. Our patient 
was tested for mutations in the SRY and AR genes, but none 
was detected.
Owing to the many challenges in diagnosing this syn-
drome, patients typically discover its presence around the 
age of 16 years. Patients with Swyer syndrome usually pre-
sent with tall stature, elevated gonadotropin levels, delayed 
puberty, and primary amenorrhoea. Hypoestrogenism 
results in low bone mineral density, leading to osteopenia 
or osteoporosis [ 5–7]. The vagina and fallopian tubes usu -
ally are normal, the uterus is hypoplastic, and the gonads are 
streaked. Since streak gonads carry a 30% risk of developing 
gonadoblastoma, it is important to note that gonadoblastoma 
can produce gonadal steroids, such as oestrogen and testos-
terone, which can induce pubertal development. Therefore, 
Swyer syndrome may have been diagnosed later [1]. Given 
the increased risk of malignancy in individuals with intersex 
conditions who possess Y chromosomes, gonadectomy and 
salpingectomy are recommended [8]. Our patient had a tall 
stature with normal external female genitalia. The labora -
tory results indicated hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. The 
patient underwent bilateral gonadectomy. Histopathology 
confirmed streak gonads, and HRT was initiated. HRT is 
crucial for developing secondary sexual characteristics, pre-
venting uterine hypoplasia, managing osteoporosis, address-
ing cardiovascular risks, and regulating the menstrual cycle 
[9]. Following this treatment, our patient experienced men-
strual bleeding, normalisation of uterine size, and progres-
sion of breast development to Tanner stage 3.
Although individuals with Swyer syndrome generally 
face infertility due to underdeveloped gonads and an inabil-
ity to produce gametes, egg donation offers an effective solu-
tion [1, 7]. The first reported pregnancy in a woman with 
Swyer syndrome due to OD was documented in 1988 [13]. 
In extremely rare cases, unassisted pregnancies have been 
reported; however, the exact mechanism remains unknown 
[12]. Patients with 46,XY gonadal dysgenesis who achieve 
pregnancy through OD face an elevated risk of obstetric 
complications, such as preeclampsia and pregnancy-induced 
hypertension. These risks arise from hormonal and structural 
characteristics of the uterus in this condition, which may 
impair trophoblast invasion. Hormonal imbalances or inad-
equate endometrial preparation for pregnancy could con -
tribute to this impairment, increasing the risk of placental 
pathologies [14]. Additionally, the use of oocyte donation 
and assisted reproductive technologies (ART) is associated 
with a higher incidence of hypertensive disorders during 
pregnancy and an increased rate of caesarean section [15].

Two reports described triplet pregnancies: one involving 
two developing foetuses and a hydatidiform mole, and the 
other reporting a successful triplet pregnancy without docu-
mented complications (lacking detailed information) [16, 
28]. Most patients (22) in the published series delivered at 
term [5, 7, 13, 14, 17, 19, 20, 22, 23, 27, 29, 30, 32, 34, 36, 
37], while 18 cases resulted in preterm deliveries [9, 14, 18, 
21, 24, 26–29, 31, 33, 35]. Preeclampsia was diagnosed in 
ten of the published cases, with two cases being classified 
as severe [14, 26, 28–30, 33, 35]. All patients with preec-
lampsia underwent caesarean section. In nine cases, preterm 
neonates were delivered at 28–36 weeks of gestation [9, 14, 
26, 28, 29, 33, 35]. Caesarean section is the predominant 
mode of delivery in patients with Swyer syndrome, with 
indications varying across cases (Table  1). The preference 
for caesarean delivery is based on the hypothesis that vaginal 
delivery may be unsuccessful due to potentially impaired 
uterine contractility [9, 27]. However, this hypothesis lacks 
robust supporting evidence, as successful vaginal deliver -
ies have been documented in patients with Swyer syndrome 
(Table 1). The primary indications for caesarean section in 
patients with Swyer syndrome can be categorized into four 
groups. The first includes pregnancy complications such as 
preeclampsia, multiple gestation, and foetal malpresentation. 
The second involves failed induction of labor, potentially 
linked to anatomical differences and altered receptor char -
acteristics in Swyer patients. The third is an androgynous 
pelvic shape, which may impact the feasibility of vaginal 
delivery. Lastly, elective caesarean section is performed due 
to maternal health concerns or upon maternal request [14] 
(Table 1). To date, six successful vaginal deliveries have 
been reported, although one case resulted in stillbirth at 34 
weeks of gestation [14, 29, 36]. In the analysed case, the 
patient conceived following the transfer of a single blasto-
cyst. The pregnancy progressed uneventfully, without com-
plications. An elective caesarean section was performed due 
to a history of surgical spinal fixation for scoliosis, as recom-
mended by an orthopaedic specialist.
Two cases of uterine rupture during pregnancy have been 
reported in patients with Swyer syndrome. In one case, uterine 
rupture occurred at 20 weeks of gestation, leading to mas -
sive haemorrhage and necessitating an abdominal hysterec-
tomy [23]. In the other case, uterine rupture was diagnosed 
at 28 weeks of gestation in a twin pregnancy. The foetuses 
were delivered alive via hysterotomy, and a 3-cm uterine rup-
ture was surgically repaired [14]. In the latter case, severe 
preeclampsia was also diagnosed [14]. These cases do not 
fully exclude the potential presence of specific anatomical and 
receptor-related uterine characteristics in Swyer syndrome.
A major challenge in treating patients with Swyer syndrome 
is addressing their psychological and emotional well-being. 
The initial shock for women comes from their diagnosis — the 
realization that genetically they are male, despite the absence 
of male physical traits. This is followed by the heightened risk 
of gonadal tumors, requiring surgical removal of the gonads. 
While these structures do not affect long-term health, the patient 
must psychologically prepare for the intervention. The final 
challenge is infertility, with some patients reporting suicidal 
thoughts upon diagnosis, as noted in a Danish study [38]. 
Consequently, the involvement of psychologists, psychiatrists, 
and psychotherapists is essential. In the case analysed, the 
patient required long-term psychotherapy.
Conclusions
Swyer syndrome should be considered in women with 
delayed puberty, primary amenorrhoea, or elevated gonado-
tropin levels. Due to the risk of gonadal cancer, adnexectomy 
is recommended, followed by HRT to support puberty, bone 
health, and cardiovascular risk management. Pregnancy can 
be achieved using OD, and it also helps prevent the condition 
from being passed on to offspring. Caesarean section is the 
most common method of delivery.
