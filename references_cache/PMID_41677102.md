# Highly purified cannabidiol (CBD) in CDKL5 deficiency disorder (CDD): Open-label prospective study.
**Authors:** Perulli M, De Gioia A, Ruggiero F, Ascione F, Porto C, Musto E, Massaroni V, Picilli M, Gambardella ML, Quintiliani M, Contaldo I, Veredice C, Battaglia DI
**Journal:** Epilepsia Open (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/epi4.70213](https://doi.org/10.1002/epi4.70213)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: CDKL5 deficiency disorder (CDD) is an early-onset developmental and epileptic encephalopathy characterized by frequent drug-resistant seizures, cerebral visual impairment, motor dysfunction, and sleep and gastrointestinal disturbances. Preliminary evidence suggests that highly purified cannabidiol (CBD) may reduce seizure frequency, but data on its effects on comorbidities are lacking. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of CBD in individuals with CDD.

METHODS: We conducted a prospective, open-label, single-center study including patients with CDD aged >1 year. Outcomes included motor seizure frequency, caregiver- and clinician-rated Clinical Global Impression (CGI), and changes in sleep, motor abilities, and EEG at 3, 6, and 12 months. CBD plasma levels were measured with High-Performance Liquid chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (HPLC-MS).

RESULTS: Eight of nine patients (all females; median age 10 years, range 1-24) completed the study, with a retention rate at 12 months of 8/9 (89%). One discontinued at 6 months due to a skin rash. A > 50% seizure reduction was observed in 8/9 patients at 3 months, 6/9 at 6 months, and 1/8 at 12 months. Seven patients showed some degree of vigilance improvements, three in motor performance, and two in sleep and constipation. All caregivers reported at least minimal overall improvement (CGI score 3) at 3 months, and three reported marked improvement (CGI score 2), with a peak at 3 months. Five patients showed adverse events during the trial, but none were considered serious. The median CBD dose at all time-points was 15.6 mg/kg/day (IQR 10.0-18.9) corresponding to a plasma dose of 69.9 ng/mL (IQR 29.8-114.6) and the median concentration/dose ratio was 4.7 (IQR 2.7-6.8).

SIGNIFICANCE: The safety and efficacy of highly purified CBD in CDD were consistent with previous reports in the literature, with possible benefits beyond seizure control. Further studies are warranted to assess non-seizure outcomes and compare long-term efficacy across treatment options.

PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY: We studied nine girls with CDKL5 deficiency disorder who had frequent, hard-to-treat seizures. They received cannabidiol for up to 1 year, added to their usual medicines. Most children had fewer seizures in the first months of treatment. Some families also noticed better alertness, eye contact, movement, sleep, or constipation. Side effects were usually mild and manageable. Although seizure frequency often returned to baseline by the end of the study, most families chose to continue cannabidiol. Because this was a small study without a placebo group, these results are preliminary, and larger controlled trials are needed.

CDKL5 deficiency disorder (CDD) is one of the most common early‐onset monogenic developmental and epileptic encephalopathies with an estimated incidence of 1 per 42 400 live births.
1
 It is caused by pathogenic variants in the CDKL5 X‐linked gene, which encodes cyclin‐dependent kinase‐like 5, a serine–threonine kinase involved in neuronal maturation, dendritic growth, and synaptic plasticity.
2



CDD primarily affects females due to male lethality in many cases and typically manifests within the first months of life
2
 with epileptic spasms and often sequential seizures that show poor response to antiseizure medications and are associated with severe hypotonia.
3
 In addition to drug‐resistant epilepsy, individuals with CDD present with global developmental delay and, later, often severe intellectual disability, stereotypies, cerebral visual impairment, and a spectrum of motor, gastrointestinal, and sleep disturbances.4, 5, 6, 7


Given the complexity and severity of the disorder, management remains challenging and primarily symptomatic. While new targeted therapies are under investigation, current treatment options aim to reduce seizure burden and improve quality of life. Among emerging treatments, highly purified plant‐derived cannabidiol (CBD; Epidyolex®) has shown promise in other monogenic developmental and epileptic encephalopathies.
8
 There is also preliminary evidence specifically regarding seizure reduction in CDD, although almost half of the patients discontinued the drug before 48 weeks.
9
 A very recent study tried also to give a qualitative report on the effects of CBD beyond seizure reduction in a group of 13 subjects with CDD, although in a retrospective way.
10



This study aimed to evaluate prospectively the efficacy, both in terms of seizure and non‐seizure outcomes, and the safety of hpCBD in individuals with CDD.

This was an exploratory single‐center prospective non‐profit open‐label trial (EudraCT number 2021‐001959‐13) carried out at the Pediatric Neurology Unit of Fondazione Policlinico Universitario A. Gemelli IRCCS in Rome, between June 2022 and December 2024; the last patient was enrolled in December 2023.

Patients were included in the study if they respected inclusion criteria: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of CDKL5 deficiency disorder (CDD), including a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in CDKL5, (2) age ≥1 year and ≤30 years and (3) at least four countable motor seizures per month in the 6 months prior to enrollment, with a 12‐week baseline countable seizure frequency before screening. Exclusion criteria included: (1) investigational drug or device use within 30 days prior to the initiation of the present study, (2) consumption of galenic formulations of cannabis‐derived products, and (3) contraindication to CBD (according to the summary of product characteristics), including hepatic impairment and suspected hypersensitivity to CBD, sesame oil, or any ingredient in the product. Patient consent was collected at the screening visit.

The protocol included a screening visit in which inclusion and exclusion criteria were assessed and baseline data were collected including: seizure frequency, detailed medical history indicating age at epilepsy onset, antiseizure medications (ASMs) history (prior and concomitant ASMs number and type); baseline scores of CGI‐I, baseline EEG, ECG, vital signs, weight, blood test results (including transaminases, bilirubin, plasma concentration of concomitant ASMs), clinical, neurological and neurovisual evaluation. Moreover, at the baseline visit, countable motor seizures were defined with parents as all the seizures with motor phenomena lasting at least 1 s; in the only patient where spasms were the only countable seizure, the number of clusters of spasms was counted. Moreover, at each visit, families were asked to report significant improvements after the beginning of CBD treatment in the main features of CDD (alertness and visual contact, motor coordination, sleep, and constipation). Significance was defined as a change that has an effect on daily function and care (i.e. no more need for laxatives and fewer injuries due to falls). Amelioration of features that could be tested also by physicians (alertness and visual contact, motor function) was reported only if these were observed also by the attending physician. Highly purified CBD in a 100 mg/mL solution (Epidyolex®) was dispensed to patient's families after a positive screening and subsequent enrollment in the study. A typical titration scheme was: starting dose of 5 mg/kg/day in two divided doses for 1 week and up‐titration in weekly increments of 2.5 mg/kg twice daily to a maximum maintenance daily dosage of 20 mg/kg. The titration could be slower to minimize adverse effects and maximize tolerability. Patients were asked to agree to attend follow‐up visits at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after enrollment, with a 2‐week window, to fill seizure diaries and communicate adverse events. CBD plasma levels were measured with high‐performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HPLC‐MS). The sample was collected on the morning of the visit before the drug administration.

The study was approved by the local ethical committee as a pilot study with no formal sample size calculation. Statistical analysis was not performed due to the small sample size. Data are presented as median and interquartile ranges due to the non‐normal distribution of the sample.

We enrolled nine subjects out of nine screened, all females (Table 1). Median age at enrollment was 9.8 years (IQR 2.1–18.0), and median age at onset of epilepsy was 1 month (IQR 0–5 months). They had failed a median of 5.5 antiseizure medications (IQR 3.8–8.0), and they were taking a median of 3.0 antiseizure medications (IQR 1.0–4.0), with 5/9 taking an ASM with sodium channel blockage as one of the mechanisms of action. Sodium valproate was the most common concomitant ASM at baseline (5/9), followed by clobazam (3/9). One patient (PT5), after failing 4 ASMs, was only on a ketogenic diet and was the only patient who was enrolled in monotherapy, as the family was reluctant to start any other ASM. The most common seizure type at baseline was spasms (7/9 subjects), although this was considered a countable motor seizure only in one patient (PT8) for whom the family was able to reliably report the number of clusters of spasms. The most common countable motor seizures were tonic seizures (6/9). Seizure frequency was weekly in five subjects and daily in 4.

In terms of neurodevelopment, all subjects were non‐verbal except for one (PT6), who was able to speak single words. 6/9 subjects were non‐ambulant, although two were below the age range of beginning of independent walking; the age of independent walking for the others was 18, 28, and 48 months. They all had variable degrees of central visual impairment, troubles with hand coordination, behavior, and stereotypies.

Figure 1 summarizes the timing of the hpCBD increase, plasma level, and concentration/dose (C/D) ratio. There was a great variability in the speed of increase of hpCBD, which did not correlate directly with efficacy in seizure reduction. The median hpCBD dose at all time‐points was 15.6 mg/kg/day (IQR 10.0–18.9) while at the steady state it was 18.8 mg/kg/day. The median hpCBD plasma concentration at all time‐points was 69.9 ng/mL (IQR 29.8–114.6), while the median C/D ratio was 4.7 (IQR 2.7–6.8).

Results on seizure reduction are summarized in Table 2. The peak efficacy was at 4 months, when 8/9 subjects had a >50% seizure reduction while 3 had a >75% seizure reduction. The median time with >50% seizure reduction was 4 months. In five patients, we were able to withdraw another ASM (two vigabatrin, one phenytoin, one valproate, and one lamotrigine); one patient was able to decrease the dose of concomitant therapy; two patients were on single add‐on while one patient was on monotherapy. There were no signs of correlation between efficacy and CBD dose or plasma levels nor with concomitant therapy or type of seizures or epilepsy.

Results on non‐seizure outcomes are summarized in Figure 2B. At 1 month, 8/9 families reported a minimal improvement (CGI 3) and one family a marked improvement (CGI 2). At 3 months, a CGI 3 and 2 were reported respectively by 6 and 3 families. At 6 months, six continued to have GCI 3 while CGI 2 was reduced to 2 patients. The “no change” group increased from 1 to 3 at 12 months, while 6 families could still notice a minimal improvement (CGI 3) compared with baseline.

Non‐seizure outcomes: (A) as Clinical Global Impression reported by caregivers, and (B) as improvements at different levels with effects on daily function and care observed by caregivers and clinicians.

Increased alertness and visual contact were the most common reported benefits of therapy (7 subjects at 1 and 3 months, 6 subjects at 6 months and 4 at 12 months), which was confirmed by clinicians during follow‐up visits. A clear improvement in motor coordination was seen in three subjects and confirmed by clinicians, with one of them losing this change with a subsequent increase in seizures. Finally, two different families reported a marked improvement in constipation and sleep. These changes seemed to have only a partial correlation with seizure frequency, with many subjects showing stable improvement despite loss of benefit in seizure control.

Three patients showed mild somnolence, which led to slower titration (one in monotherapy, one with concomitant valproate at a low dose, one with concomitant valproate, lacosamide, and clobazam). One patient had a mild skin rash after 6 months since the beginning that was resolving with corticosteroid therapy, but together with the family, the decision was made to withdraw from the trial for safety. One patient had a mild elevation of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 1.6 times the upper limit, that resolved after discontinuation of sodium valproate.

CDD is a condition with a substantial treatment gap. A multicenter study on 172 patients showed that the median seizure frequency was 2 per day despite treatment with two or more ASMs in most patients.
11
 The only ASM that is specifically approved for CDD is ganaxolone, which showed a modest improvement compared with placebo (24% vs. 10% of >50% seizure reduction).
12
 In the most recent international consensus developed through a Delphi process, CBD achieved the highest level of expert agreement (92.5%) among ASMs recommended for the treatment of CDD, second only to ganaxolone, which reached full consensus (100%).
13



Retrospective data on large cohorts suggest that CBD could be relatively effective and safe in a heterogeneous group of DEEs.
14



The first evidence on the efficacy of CBD in CDD was provided by a prospective study published in 2018 that explored efficacy and safety in a study that also included patients with Aicardi, Dup15q, and Doose syndromes. The >50% reduction in seizure frequency was 41% at 12 weeks and 57% at 48 weeks, but the retention was only 11/17 (65%) and 10/17 (59%), respectively. In our study, the 12‐month retention rate was much higher (89%), which might be explained by the relatively lower mean doses used in our protocol (16.4 ± 4.3 mg/kg/day vs. 26.2 ± 10.1 mg/kg/day).

A very recent retrospective study on CBD use in CDD (n = 13) and MECP2‐related encephalopathy (n = 14) found a > 50% reduction in seizure frequency in 9/13 (69%) after a median duration of treatment of 14 months (IQR 8.5–20). This higher rate of efficacy can be explained by selection bias with exclusion of patients with short negative trials due to inefficacy or adverse events.
10
 With all the limitations of a retrospective study, this was also the first report of the efficacy of CBD on non‐seizure outcomes in CDD (attention and reactivity, sleep quality, motor function), but data are not separated between CDKL5 and MECP2. In the present study, we found an effect on non‐seizure outcomes which might explain the high retention rate compared with the relatively short duration of the efficacy. Moreover, we did not record seizure intensity change but qualitatively some patients reported shorter and less intense seizures despite the frequency being back to the baseline. Finally, there is no evidence of the long‐term efficacy of any drug in CDD, making it difficult to define what can be considered a good outcome.

Monitoring plasma concentration of CBD showed a substantial interindividual and intraindividual variability in concentration/dose ratio. Recent evidence on clinical pharmacokinetics suggests that both formulation (CBD‐enriched oil compared to purified CBD) and administration with food affect CBD exposure, advising for therapeutic drug monitoring.
15
 A recent study shows similar heterogeneity, without a clear effect of other ASMs on CBD levels.
16
 Moreover, age has been proposed as a driver of heterogeneity, with younger patients reaching lower plasma doses per weight‐adjusted oral dose.

Our study has several limitations, the main being the lack of a randomized placebo, which would help account for the placebo effect. At the same time, the families of these patients have tried several medications without effect or with only very limited effect over time and therefore tend to have a generally more critical view of new therapies compared with other conditions. Another limitation is the lack of scales—or better, objective biomarkers—capturing the beneficial effects on non‐seizure outcomes. Therefore, our trial relies on subjective observations. At the same time, however, some of these improvements, such as in alertness and motor function, were observed not only by parents but also by the physicians who followed the patients throughout the trial. Finally, the investigators were allowed to reduce concomitant therapies, which limits attribution of non‐seizure outcome positive effects to CBD. The small sample size makes it a feasibility pilot study.

In this exploratory prospective open‐label trial, we suggest that CBD may be an effective and relatively safe therapeutic option in patients with CDD, which warrants further investigation through randomized controlled trials. CBD may be associated with effects not only on seizures but also on awareness, sleep, and motor functions. Finally, plasma concentration monitoring could allow for better personalization of treatment.
