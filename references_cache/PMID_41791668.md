# Prothrombotic PROC variant rebalancing hemostasis in severe hemophilia A with attenuated bleeding risk.
**Authors:** Ramanan R, Van Thillo Q, Lavend'homme R, Van Laer C, Thys C, De Wispelaere K, Baert S, Kint C, Verhamme P, Vanassche T, McFadyen JD, Perkins AC, Tran HA, Labarque V, Jacquemin M, Freson K
**Journal:** J Thromb Haemost (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jtha.2026.02.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtha.2026.02.014)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Severe hemophilia A (HA), defined by a clotting factor [F]VIII level of <1%, leads to a severe bleeding tendency. A pathogenic F8 variant p.His300Lysfs∗41 accounted for <1% FVIII levels in a patient with HA; however, he exhibited a discordantly mild-to-moderate bleeding phenotype.

OBJECTIVES: To identify and characterize the hemostatic impact of co-inherited genetic modifiers in a HA patient exhibiting a clinical phenotype discordant with their FVIII level.

METHODS: Multigene sequencing to detect possible genetic modifiers identified a heterozygous PROC variant (p.Trp414Arg). Plasma protein C (PC) activity and antigen were measured. Recombinant wild-type and mutant PC were produced using Expi293F cells with function and quantity measured by chromogenic activity assays and competitive ELISA. Protein structure was predicted with AlphaFold 3. Thrombin generation assays in FVIII-deficient plasma with varying PC concentrations were used to mimic PC deficiency in HA.

RESULTS: PC activity in the patient's plasma was 58% (clotting-based) and 53% (chromogenic), and PC antigenic concentration was 59%. Mutant PC in transfection supernatant demonstrated concordant marked reduction in PC activity (0% vs 90%) and quantity (4% vs 100%) compared with wild-type PC. Thrombin generation assays demonstrated increases in endogenous thrombin potential and peak height upon addition of thrombomodulin in PC-deficient vs PC-replete FVIII-deficient plasma.

CONCLUSION: The partial quantitative PC deficiency in the patient resulted from a heterozygous pathogenic PROC p.Trp414Arg variant. Quantitative reduction in PC improved thrombin generation in FVIII-deficient plasma upon addition of thrombomodulin. Thus, this PROC variant was predicted to attenuate bleeding severity in HA by improving thrombin generation. Multigene panel sequencing permits the identification of genetic modifiers that result in naturally rebalanced hemostasis in inherited bleeding disorders.

Introduction 1 
Haemostatic control involves a delicate balance between procoagulant and 2 
anticoagulant factors. In patients with haemophilia A (HA), deficient clotting factor VIII 3 
(FVIII) results in a bleeding diathesis, with severity depending on the degree of FVIII 4 
deficiency. Patients with a FVIII activity below 1% are considered to have severe HA1.  5 
Severity may be attenuated in those with HA who have a concomitant thrombophilia 6 
variant present 2,34. High throughput sequencing allows multiple genes involved in 7 
haemostasis and thrombosis to be sequenced in parallel and thus allows the detection 8 
of concomitant variants which may modify phenotype. The concept of genetic 9 
modifiers in inherited bleeding d isorders has underpinned the advent of rebalancing 10 
agents via inhibition of endogenous anticoagulants5. 11 
 12 
Protein C (PC) is a vitamin K -dependent6 anticoagulant serine protease zymogen 13 
encoded by the PROC gene. When activated to activated protein C (APC) by thrombin 14 
complexed to TM, it inactiva tes FVa and FVIIIa 7, and thus reduce s thrombin 15 
generation. Variants in PROC have been associated with a genetic predisposition to 16 
thrombosis8,9 While reports of PROC variants in patients with HA have been published 17 
within case series of HA with concurrent thrombophilia 2,3, an extensive 18 
characterization of the clinical and laboratory phenotype of these patients has not yet 19 
been performed. We hypothesised that a concomitant genetic variant in PROC 20 
(c.1240T>C, p.Trp414Arg) attenuated the bleeding severity in a patient with severe 21 
HA. We performed clinical and functional characterisation of this PROC variant in the 22 
context of severe HA. 23

Results and Discussion 5 
Patient clinical characteristics  6 
A now 41-year-old male patient first presented at our centre at the age of 2 2, with a 7 
diagnosis of moderate HA made in childhood, with no former FVIII levels available. He 8 
had been lost to follow-up at his previous treating centre and was referred to our centre 9 
in the setting of a planned dental extraction.  Re-evaluation demonstrated FVIII:C 10 
levels below 1% by both chromogenic and one-stage assays, characterising him as 11 
severe, rather than moderate, HA . He had no history of an inhibitor. He had a single 12 
target joint in the right ankle , consequent to recurrent pro voked bleeds after playing 13 
basketball with features of haemophilic arthropathy  (narrowed joint space in the 14 
tibiotalar joint on x -ray) demonstrated on imaging. Previously, he had used plasma -15 
derived concentrate on demand  every two to three weeks for these bleeds. He had 16 
also experienced occasional provoked bleeds in the left ankle, and on one occasion, 17 
had suffered prolonged bleeding after a dental procedure without prior treatment.  18 
 19 
We subsequently proposed prophylactic treatment with a standard half -life product 20 
(octocog alpha, Advate ) 40-50 IU/kg every three days, which resulted in complete 21 
bleed prevention. However, within four years, the patient had self-ceased prophylaxis 22 
because of the lack of bleeds after discontinuing basketball, with factor usage limited 23 
to 1-6 times a year, mostly for minor traumatic joint bleeds. He was commenced on 24 
emicizumab when this medication became available at our centre, to eliminate all 25 
bleeds, and has reported no further bleeding events to date. 26

The milder than expected bleeding phenotype in this patient with only provoked bleeds 28 
despite FVIII levels <1% prompted us to screen for an underlying thrombophilia  by 29 
performing high-throughput sequencing using our thrombosis-haemostasis multigene 30 
coagulation panel. This confirmed both the presence of a pathogenic 31

FVIII, and a variant of uncertain significance (VUS)  in PROC, c.1240T>C 33

Molecular modelling 5 
Protein modelling was performed using AlphaFold 3 and visualised in PyMOL. The 6 
variant p.Trp414Arg (Figure 2a) did not appear to be in close proximity to the catalytic 7 
triad (p.His253, p.Asp299, p.Ser402) in the AlphaFold 3 predicted structure 8 
(Figure 2b). This suggested that the variant was unlikely to direc tly interfere with the 9 
enzymatic activity of PC. However, the mutated residue was located near the 10 
disulphide bond between p.Cys183 and p.Cys319, which links the light and heavy 11 
chains of mature and activated PC (Figure 2b). A hydrogen bond is present between 12 
p.Trp414 and p.Cys319 (Figure 2c), raising the possibility that the variant arginine 13 
could disrupt the critical disulphide bond between the cysteines, leading to protein 14 
instability. Analysis of the variant amino acid's rotamers revealed potential stru ctural 15 
issues disrupting the local environment, namely  steric clashes with nearby residues 16 
(Figure 2d). The AlphaMissense prediction for the p.Trp414Arg yielded a pathogenicity 17 
score of 0.699, predicting the variant to be likely pathogenic. The heatmap (Figure 2e) 18 
visually illustrates various hotspot regions for predicted pathogenic variants, one of 19 
which (outlined in black) includes the p.Trp414Arg variant. 20 
 21 
This study investigated the clinical and functional impacts of the PROC c.1240T>C 22 
(p.Trp414Arg) variant in a patient with severe HA, focussing on its role in modifying 23 
the haemostatic phenotype. Our findings, namely the absence of detectable PC in the 24 
supernatant of Expi293 cells transfected with mutant PROC, coupled with plasma PC 25 
levels of approximately 50–60% in the patient heterozygous for the mutation, suggest 26 
that the PROC p.Trp414Arg variant is associated with a quantitative deficiency in 27 
circulating PC. This was predicted to be secondary to disruption of the disulphide bond 28 
linking the light and heavy chains of PC based on analysis of predicted wild-type and 29 
mutant protein structures. An alternative possibility is that the p.Trp414Arg variant 30 
results in an unfolded protein, which causes intracellular stress and selective death of 31 
actively expressing cells. By artificially recreating the phenotype of this patient with 32 
FVIII-deficient plasma and quantitative PC deficiency, we demonstrated that thrombin 33

The effects of genetic modifiers in people with  haemophilia have been described in 4 
small case series 15–20. The bleeding phenotype in severe HA may be attenuated in 5 
those with FV Leiden21,18 and the G20210A prothrombin gene mutation 22, though the 6 
small size of these study populations precludes robust statistical confirmation of a 7 
modifying effect. Based on these observations, inhibitors of the natural anticoagulants, 8 
the so -called rebalancing agents, are being evaluated in the management of 9 
haemophilia5. These include fitusiran23–25 (antithrombin inhibitor), concizumab26,27 and 10 
marstacimab28 (TFPI inhibitors), and VGA039 (PS inhibitor)29. Our data  provides 11 
proof-of-concept support for the ongoing evaluation of rebalancing agents specifically 12 
targeting the PC pathway in patients with inherited bleeding disorders. One example 13 
includes SerpinPC, an activated PC inhibitor30,31, however further development of the 14 
drug was recently halted by the manufacturer citing commercial reasons. 15 
 16 
Evaluation of genetic modifiers is currently descriptive and case -based, with broader 17 
analysis limited by the rarity of inherited bleeding disorders and co -occurring 18 
pathogenic variants. For the concept to be more widely representative, large pedigrees 19 
or large numbers of kindreds are necessary to perform statistical analysis of effect 20 
size. However, this poses a significant challenge due to the rarity of inherited bleeding 21 
disorders, compounded by the even greater rarity of co-occurring pathogenic variants 22 
in other genes.  Our study was also limited by the lack of stored patient plasma 23 
uncontaminated with haemostatic therapy and lack of a bank of severe HA PPP 24 
samples to serve as controls for comparing TGA parameters to better represent in vivo 25 
haemostasis. However, the benefit of artificially recreated samples reduces the pre -26 
analytic issues of stored patient plasma, which is often frozen and thawed multiple 27 
times. It also focuses the question to the sole eff ect of PC deficiency in severe FVIII 28 
depleted plasma. 29
