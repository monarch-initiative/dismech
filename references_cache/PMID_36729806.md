PURPOSE: The purpose of this study was to report functional and anatomical
outcomes of anti-VEGF treatment in eyes with autosomal recessive
bestrophinopathy (ARB) presenting in the first decade of life.
METHODS: The study included case series of four eyes from two siblings with
compound heterozygous mutations in the BEST1 gene who were treated with eight
monthly intravitreal bevacizumab injections. Response to treatment was analyzed
using color fundus photography, fundus autofluorescence, optical coherence
tomography, OCT angiography, and microperimetry.
RESULTS: Patient 1 (male, age 9 years) had visual acuity of 20/20 in the right
eye and 20/50 in the left eye. Patient 2 (female, age 10 years) had visual
acuity of 20/25 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye. All eyes had
multifocal subretinal deposition of lipofuscin and subretinal fluid, and three
eyes had choroidal neovascularization (CNV). Lipofuscin material reabsorbed in 2
of 4 eyes, the CNV regressed in 3 of 3 eyes, a bacillary detachment resolved in
1 of 1 eye, but the subretinal fluid did not change. Functional improvement in
visual acuity was noted, but MP showed scattered areas of reduced retinal
sensitivity. No ocular or systemic side effects were detected.
CONCLUSION: Anti-VEGF treatment of choroidal neovascularization in eyes with ARB
resulted in anatomical changes that were only clinically significant in the eye
with decreased visual acuity. The hyporeflective subretinal material remained
unchanged suggesting a nonexudative cause. These findings provide new insights
into the management of ARB, especially in pediatric subjects with CNV.

Autosomal recessive bestrophinopathy (ARB) is a rare retinal dystrophy first described in 2008 by Burgess et al1 as the null phenotype of the BEST1 gene, with the total absence of function of the bestrophin-1 protein. This protein is typically expressed in the basolateral plasma membrane of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and is responsible for the Ca2+-activated Cl− channels.1 The estimated prevalence of ARB is 1:1,000,000; although the age of onset is between 4 and 40 years, most cases begin in childhood.1,2 Clinically, ARB presents with multifocal subretinal yellowish deposits involving the posterior pole corresponding to marked hyperautofluorescence on fundus autofluorescence (FAF). Other abnormalities such as accumulation of subretinal fluid, cystoid macular edema, and choroidal neovascularization have been described.3 In addition, ARB patients usually have other ocular associations, including hyperopia, short axial length, and shallow anterior chambers with an increased risk of acute angle-closure glaucoma.4,5 Unfortunately, most patients with ARB end up with low vision because of choroidal neovascularization or RPE atrophy.2,5 In an era where multimodal imaging and genetic testing allows early diagnosis, early intervention could alter the unfortunate fate of photoreceptors affected by this disease. Therefore, in the present study, we report anatomical and functional outcomes of anti-VEGF treatment in four eyes with ARB and discuss the dilemma of anti-VEGF intervention in the absence of proven therapies for this blinding disorder.

Two siblings were referred to the Ophthalmic Genetics clinic at the University of Kentucky for further evaluation of retinal abnormalities. Patient 1 was a 9-year-old boy who had failed school vision screening. Patient 2 was a 10-year-old asymptomatic girl, but examination revealed similar retinal abnormalities.

Both patients underwent complete ophthalmic evaluation, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) on Snellen, intraocular pressure (IOP) measured by Goldmann applanation tonometry, axial length (Lenstar, Hagg Streit Diagnostics, Switzerland), wide-field color fundus photography (CFP), and FAF (FAF Optos; CA, Dunfermline, United Kingdom), optical coherence tomography (OCT Spectralis; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), OCT angiography (OCTA Plex Elite 9000; Carl Zeiss Meditech, Dublin, CA), full-field ERG was performed following ISCEV standard protocols after 20 minutes of dark-adaptation time using Espion E3 (Diagnosys LLC, Lowell, MA) and microperimetry (MAIA, Macular Integrity Assessment System, CenterVue SpA, Padova, Italy). Fluorescein angiography was attempted in both subjects, but it was successful only on Patient 2.

Because of the presence of subretinal fluid in all eyes and CNV detected in OCTA in three of four eyes and after an extensive discussion of the risk, benefits, and alternatives of treatment with the parents, the four eyes were treated with off-label intravitreal bevacizumab (IVB) (1.25 mg/0.05 mL) injection; each patient received bilateral injections under general anesthesia for a total of eight injections per eye (from July 2020 to May 2021). Imaging studies were repeated 1 week before the subsequent treatment to evaluate the changes in subretinal lipofuscinous material, subretinal fluid, and CNV.

To quantify the response of subretinal fluid and material to intravitreal bevacizumab, we performed semi-automatic segmentation (from the outer border of the outer plexiform layer to RPE) of the 8 × 8 mm posterior pole volumetric OCT scan. This approach was taken to eliminate the bias in delta calculations because of inner retinal layer artifacts present in some OCT B-scans.

Both subjects had compound heterozygous mutations in the BEST1 gene defined as c.422G>A (p.Arg141His) and c.122T>C (p.Leu 41Pro); these pathogenic variants were determined to be in-trans after testing the parents.

A 9-year-old boy was referred for evaluation of abnormal macular findings in both eyes detected on eye examination after failing school vision screening. He had no complaints, but his father noticed that he frequently covered his left eye to see well. His examination revealed a baseline BCVA of 20/30-3 in the right eye and 20/50-1 in the left eye. Refraction was +0.25 +0.50 × 074 in the right eye and +0.25 +0.25 × 141 in the left eye. Intraocular pressure was 13/14 mmHg in the right eye and left eye, respectively. Ocular motility, pupillary examination, and anterior segment examinations were unremarkable in both eyes. Anterior chamber depth (ACD) was 3.44 mm in the right eye and 3.17 mm in the left eye. Axial length was 22.4 mm in the right eye and 22.8 mm in the left eye. Retinal examination showed abnormal foveal reflex, confluent subretinal yellowish deposits inside the arcades of the right eye greater than the left eye, and multiple areas of focal subretinal fibrosis in both eyes (Figure 1, A and F). Full-field ERG demonstrated abnormal responses as follows: decreased rod-specific b-wave amplitude (38.9 μV in the right eye and 43 μV in the left eye in the dark-adapted 0.01 step); normal combined response in both eyes (amplitudes of −128/112 in the right eye and −170/122 in the left eye for a/b waves in microvolts for the dark-adapted 3.0 step); subnormal cone responses (−53/47 in the right eye and −74/59 in the left eye in microvolts for a/b in the light-adapted 3.0 step), and normal but weak for age cone-flicker (64 and 82 μV peak amplitude). The FAF showed well-delineated areas of highly hyperautofluorescent subretinal material confined to the posterior pole (Figure 1, C and I) of the right eye greater than the left eye. OCT showed evidence of deposition of homogenous hyperreflective material between the photoreceptor layer (PRL) and RPE. In addition, OCT showed thickening of the outer photoreceptor segments in the subfoveal region, subretinal fluid (Figure 2, B, C, H, I ), and areas of apparent subretinal fibrosis. Interestingly, a bacillary detachment with overlying thickening of the ELM (Figure 3 left) was detected by OCT on his left eye. Finally, subfoveal CNV was detected on OCT in both eyes, and the corresponding vascular net was visualized on OCTA (Figure 4, B and G).

A 10-year-old girl, the sister of Patient 1, was referred for similar macular findings in the absence of symptoms. Her examination revealed baseline BCVA of 20/25-1 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye. Refraction was +3.00 +0.75 × 062 in the right eye and +2.75 +0.75 × 128 in the left eye, and IOP was 17/18 mmHg in the right eye and left eye, respectively. Slit-lamp examination revealed shallow anterior chamber, with ACD of 3.00 mm in the right eye and 2.92 mm in the left eye. Axial length was 21.05 mm in the right eye and 21.04 mm in the left eye. Retinal examination showed an abnormal foveal reflex, subretinal yellowish deposits extending beyond the arcades nasal to the optic disc in both eyes, and subretinal fibrosis near the superior-temporal arcade in the right eye (Figure 1 L, R). The ff-ERG demonstrated abnormal responses as follows: decreased rod-specific b-wave amplitude (105.4 μV in the right eye and 93.5 μV in the left eye in the dark-adapted 0.01 step); normal combined response (amplitudes of −79/106 in the right eye and −78/67 in the left eye for a/b waves in microvolts for the dark adapted 3.0 step); subnormal a-wave but normal b-wave amplitudes in the cone responses (−19/110 in the right eye and −16/91 in the left eye in microvolts for a/b in the light adapted 3.0 step) and normal cone-flicker (109 and 101 μV peak amplitude). The FAF outlined areas of hyperfluorescent subretinal material in the right eye greater than the left eye and demonstrated areas of extension beyond the arcades and nasal to the optic nerve (Figure 1 O, U). OCT showed deposition of homogenous hyperreflective material between PRL and RPE, thickening of the outer segments of the photoreceptors, which was more evident in the subfoveal region, and shallow subretinal fluid in both eyes (Figure 2 N, O, T, U). Extramacular CNV was detected in the left eye by OCTA on wide-field scans of 12 × 12 mm. The CNV was superior-nasal to the vascular arcade corresponding to the area of subretinal fibrosis on examination (Figure 4, K–N).

Given the significant amount of hyperreflective subretinal material in all eyes, associated with choroidal neovascularization in three of them, a decision was made to treat with intravitreal bevacizumab. Because of the significant improvements noted, we continued treatment at 4-week to 6-week intervals. After administration of eight intravitreal injections of bevacizumab 1.25 mg/0.05 mL, the BCVA of Patient 1 markedly improved in the left eye to 20/25 (from 20/50) and remained stable in the right eye (20/25). In Patient 2, the final BCVA was 20/20 in both eyes and remained stable. Interestingly, FAF showed decreased intensity and size of hyperfluorescent areas in response to treatments in three eyes (Figure 1). The decrease in subretinal lipofuscinous material was also noticed on OCT after the first dose and continued to improve throughout the treatment (Figure 2).

In contrast, the subretinal fluid in the four eyes did not significantly change. Qualitative observation of OCT B-scans show modest improvement in some areas but no definitive change globally. The shallow cuff of subfoveal fluid persisted throughout treatment. Quantification of central foveal thickness showed fluctuation over time but no significant change (Figure 5). The bacillary detachment in the left eye of Patient 1 resolved entirely after the first IVB treatment (Figure 3).

OCTA confirmed the presence of and highlighted the vasculature of CNV in three eyes (all but right eye of Patient 2). In addition, OCTA allowed us to detect changes over time. A noticeable decrease in the CNV area and vascular CNV blood flow was noted in both eyes of Patient 1. No changes in CNV were noticed in Patient 2 (Figure 4).

The above-referenced structural changes in our patients reached a point of stability after eight injections. Given the good visual acuity present in all eyes, lack of further changes and the cost and risks of procedures under anesthesia, we discussed continued treatment versus observation and settled on observation. During the additional follow-up period, no worsening was observed.

We performed microperimetry on the last visit (55 weeks) to assess the retinal sensitivity at different retinal loci. Both patients showed areas of good retinal sensitivity but also areas of decreased sensitivity in areas that had hyperautofluorescence at baseline visit (Figure 6).

We present the largest case series of pediatric eyes with ARB treated with several bevacizumab intravitreal injections with a detailed multimodal imaging and functional evaluation. Our report provides 16 months of follow-up with sequential FAF, OCT and OCTA. With these, we demonstrate changes to the choroidal neovascularization and, interestingly, to lipofuscin material. Our article presents the novel finding of a bacillary detachment of the photoreceptor layer that had an excellent outcome to anti-VEGF treatment. In addition, for the first time in ARB literature, we use OCTA to monitor neovascular networks over time and microperimetry to describe functional outcomes. Despite these positive changes, the hyporreflective subretinal material did not change, suggesting this is probably not subretinal fluid but rather a separation of the RPE from the neurosensory retina as a result of inadequate RPE pump function.

The amount of hyperautofluorescent lipofuscin material decreased with anti-VEGF treatment. The four eyes in this report had a significant amount of lipofuscin subretinal material (Figures 1 and 2). The material was widespread with multifocal conglomerates, consistent with previously described cases of ARB.3 In this study, we show partial reabsorption of this subretinal material after anti-VEGF therapy, a finding not reported elsewhere in the literature. This reabsorption was most impressive at the site of densest accumulation (Figure 1, arrows). Although the exact mechanism for this lipofuscin material reabsorption is unknown, there is evidence that the accumulation of lipofuscin material between the RPE and photoreceptor outer segments is because of impaired clearance of these byproducts. A “sick” RPE cell incapable of clearing lipofuscin runs into a vicious circle because the lipofuscin has been shown to cause cellular damage.6 This toxic lipofuscin, and especially A2E, generate upregulation of cytokines and VEGF.7 We hypothesize that anti-VEGF treatment reduces the excessive VEGF levels and thus provides better homeostasis for the RPE cells, allowing them to reabsorb the excess of lipofuscin. Although reabsorption of this material has also been noted to occur as part of disease natural history in autosomal dominant Best disease, the reabsorption noted in our cases seemed to occur at a rate significantly faster than one would expect to see in patients without any intervention.8

The intraretinal fluid improved, but the hyporeflective subretinal material thought to be subretinal fluid fluctuated over time with a tendency to increase with greater duration between injections (Figure 5). Overall, a persistent shallow cuff of subfoveal fluid persisted. Our findings correlate with those of Iannaccone et al,9 who found that in a 5-year-old child with ARB and associated unilateral CNV, eight bilateral IVB treatments incompletely resolved SRF/IRF while attenuating the CNV. Similarly, Madhusudhan et al10 reported that intraretinal and subretinal fluid persisted in a 26-year-old patient who received three intravitreal ranibizumab treatments for CNV. The source of the IRF was unknown and they postulated that it may not be directly related to the CNV. Madhusudhan et al10 also found that in ARB, the IRF never resolved and even appeared to increase after the first injection. The authors postulate that this was caused by significant RPE disease. We postulate the possibility that this hyporeflective characteristic, noted on OCT, may not be subretinal fluid but rather the “outer layer opacification” described by Witsberger et al.11 This is a hyporeflective band localized between the thickened photoreceptor outer segments and the subretinal material giving the impression of subretinal fluid. This could certainly be a possibility in the left eye of Patient 2, where the entire OCT scan showed a hyporeflective band resembling subretinal fluid that was not improved with anti-VEGF injections. Moreover, fluorescein angiography showed no pooling or leakage, and in FAF, there was no hyperautofluorescence in the hyporeflective area on OCT, thereby supporting the notion that areas of ORL hypofluorescence may not be SRF. This hypothesis is supported by Guziewicz et al,12 who found RPE-neuroretinal separations caused by impaired cone-associated microvillar ensheathment and compromised insoluble interphotoreceptor matrix in an ARB canine model.11

Another finding in our study was the bacillary layer detachment noted in the most symptomatic eye in this case series (Figure 3). A bacillary layer detachment (BALAD) is an intraretinal split at the photoreceptor inner segment myoid level that has classically been linked to inflammatory conditions such as Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada syndrome13 but recently has been shown to be present in other retinal conditions such as central serous retinopathy14 and age-related macular degeneration.15 A recent review showed >20 underlying etiologies—all are associated with subretinal exudation.16,17 In our patient, the bacillary detachment resolved after the first dose of IVB with reattachment and subsequent improvement of visual acuity.

Our work also includes OCTA monitoring of choroidal neovascularization changes over time (Figure 4). The utilization of OCTA is of particular importance because choroidal neovascularization might be difficult to visualize with fluorescein angiography because of overlying lipofuscin material; thus, OCTA could be a good outcome marker in clinical trials of patients with ARB complicated with choroidal neovascularization. This imaging allowed us to track changes over time such as thinning of the CNV network and decreased flow at the CNV lesions. In particular, Patient 1 had a dense CNV in the central macula in both eyes, but the CNV was not obvious on OCT (Figure 2). Zhang et al18 report an 8-year-old patient with bilateral CNV, shown with OCT and OCTA, that did not respond to bevacizumab injections. The patient had significant subretinal and intraretinal fluid since the age of 2 years and received six injections in the right eye and four in the left eye over a period of 5 years. Unfortunately, the final visual outcome was poor with visual acuity of 20/100 in the right eye and 20/70 in the left eye, but the large interval between injections precludes us from judging the effectiveness of treatment. Our study aimed to provide injections between 4 and 6 weeks in consideration of the bevacizumab half-life.

A novel contribution of the current manuscript is the microperimetry performed in these young subjects. Although this could be a difficult test to perform in children, our patients performed well and had similar results in two different sessions. As seen in Figure 6, the microperimetry showed subnormal sensitivities in areas with hyper-FAF and CNV but showed normal retinal sensitivity (>24 db) in areas with subretinal fluid or photoreceptor outer segments edema. Similarly, Parodi et al19 demonstrated preserved retinal sensitivity in 32 patients with vitelliform macular dystrophy. In their study, the retinal sensitivity was preserved in subclinical and vitelliform stages and only significantly affected in pseudohypopyon and atrophic stages. Our findings demonstrate subnormal retinal sensitivity at many retinal loci despite good visual acuity, supporting microperimetry as an important subclinical outcome marker to consider in natural history or interventional studies.

Our study is limited by the small sample size but expands on the findings of previous case reports on this rare condition. The timing of the injections was not regular because of complexity of coordinating operating room timing with the patients' school schedules.

Our work may help with decision making on the treatment dilemma physicians face while managing ARB with or without CNV. Because ARB has a childhood onset, it is difficult to predict its speed of progression. Intervention is likely to be most effective if delivered early in the disease course and certainly before vision-limiting complications such as macular atrophy, macular fibrosis, or progressive visual loss occur. Vision loss occurs at a slow rate unless other complications develop.20 With the proven safety profile of intravitreal anti-VEGF from the retinopathy of prematurity studies21 in younger age groups,22 it may be inappropriate to recommend observation alone in ARB patients with confirmed active CNV and decreased vision, but in the absence of CNV, the subretinal fluid could be observed avoiding unnecessary injections.

Although the treatment of these patients resulted in favorable anatomical and structural changes, the majority and most visually significant changes (the resolution of the bacillary detachment, the visual acuity improvement, and the lipofuscin reabsorption) occurred after the first injection. By the end of the study period, the subretinal fluid, photoreceptor edema, and subretinal material remained to some degree. Taken together, these findings suggest that the disease course of ARB is more complex than the exudative process found in age-related macular degeneration; a dual process of CNV and diffuse RPE dysfunction seems logical in ARB. Although longer therapy may be required to fully control the exudative process in ARB, this becomes difficult to achieve in children requiring general anesthesia to receive treatment. Now that we have some reports on the effectivity of bevacizumab, other anti-VEGF agents such as aflibercept may be considered in future cases to compare responses. This also brings emphasis to the need for the development of other therapies such as gene therapy to attempt to cure the disease.

In conclusion, our results advocate for treatment of CNV in ARB cases with foveal active choroidal neovascularization affecting visual acuity. With the scarcity of ARB, a multicenter collaboration to increase the sample size and establish injection frequency will be of extreme importance.
