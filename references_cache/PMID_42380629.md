# Male reproductive phenotype in cystic fibrosis: comparison of existing animal models.
**Authors:** Boursier A, Cottart CH, Balde-Camara A, Vialard F, Barbotin AL
**Journal:** Lab Anim (NY) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41684-026-01763-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41684-026-01763-0)

## Content

In men, cystic fibrosis (CF) leads to infertility in over 95% of cases, due to early and progressive atresia of the vas deferens, resulting in obstructive azoospermia. The advent of highly effective modulator therapy has enabled more men with CF to consider parenthood, raising important questions regarding the impact of these treatments on male fertility. Animal models provide major insights into the mechanism of CF-related reproductive tract abnormalities, yet high interspecies variability complicates model selection. Mouse models display heterogeneous phenotype: knock-in or partial knockout models usually remain fertile, whereas complete knockouts may develop vas deferens atresia with aging. Among currently studied models, CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR)-knockout rats more closely reproduce the human phenotype, showing bilateral absence of the vas deferens and epididymal hypoplasia, although they exhibit more pronounced hypospermatogenesis than observed in men. In larger animal models, including ferrets, pigs, sheep and rabbits, absence of the vas deferens duct and epididymis is frequently observed, often with normal testicular histology; however, most studies in these species have mainly focused on neonatal stages, leaving long-term reproductive outcomes uncharacterized. No single model fully replicates human male reproductive pathology in CF. Combining rodent and large-animal models is probably essential to elucidate the mechanisms of vas deferens absence and to assess the long-term effects of CFTR modulators on male spermatogenesis and reproductive outcomes.

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations 
in the CFTR gene, which encodes the CF transmembrane conductance 
regulator (CFTR) protein, an anion channel responsible for chloride 
and bicarbonate ion transport across epithelial cell membranes1. CFTR 
is widely expressed in various organs, including the lungs, pancreas, 
intestines and reproductive tract. In men with CF , impaired or absent 
CFTR function leads to the accumulation of abnormally thick mucus 
in the affected organs. This accumulation results in chronic inflamma-
tion and progressive tissue damage, which are hallmark features of CF 
pathophysiology2.
The male reproductive tract is particularly sensitive to CFTR dys-
function. More than 95% of men with CF present with bilateral absence 
of the vas deferens3. Current evidence suggests that CFTR-related absence 
of the vas deferens most often results from progressive obstruction and 
secondary degeneration of the vas deferens during fetal life rather than 
true congenital agenesis
4.
These alterations result in a complete absence of spermatozoa in the 
ejaculate (that is, obstructive azoospermia), precluding natural conception. 
When men with CF wish to conceive, sperm can be surgically retrieved 
(testicular or epididymal extraction) and used for assisted reproductive 
technologies, which offer favorable outcomes in this context
5,6. In rare 
cases, men with CF present with intact vas deferens. This phenotype is 
more commonly associated with mild CF-causing variants, such as the 
3849 + 10 kb C → T splice variant, whereas other variants, such as R117H, 
are strongly associated with bilateral absence of the vas deferens7. A French 
study reported that one-fourth of 48 men with CF who achieved biological 
fatherhood did so through natural conception, with the proportion being

It is noteworthy that structural differences exist between the human 
epididymis and rodent models. In rodents, the proximal epididymis 
contains a well-differentiated initial segment. By contrast, the human 
epididymis lacks a clearly defined initial segment; its proximal region is 
formed in continuity with the efferent ducts and shows less pronounced 
compartmentalization. Furthermore, rodents exhibit a well-developed 
distal cauda epididymis with substantial sperm reservoir capacity. While 
the intraluminal diameter increases along the epididymal tubule in 
rodents, the human epididymal lumen remains relatively small throughout 
the organ, possibly contributing to the faster sperm transit observed in 
humans (reviewed in ref. 23).
Male mice carrying human CFTR mutations (F508del, G551D or 
G480C) are generally fertile, with intact epididymides and vas deferens24–28. 
In the study by Reynaert et al.28, the timing of sperm appearance in the 
vas deferens was delayed in mutant animals: spermatozoa were detected 
from day 40 after birth in wild-type animals, but only from day 50 in 
CFTR-F508del mice, with persistence at later stages. The study also 
showed that vas deferens lumina in these mutants were normal up to day 
40. After that, the luminal diameter decreased and secretions accumulated, 
although no additional structural differences compared with wild-type 
animals were reported
28.
Regarding CFTR models with residual function, they do not exhibit 
macroscopic reproductive tract abnormalities or infertility 29,30. In one 
study, only a single Cftrtm1Hgu male mouse was found to have increased 
mucin content in the vas deferens30.
By contrast, complete CFTR KO models display heterogeneous and 
age-dependent reproductive phenotypes. While several studies have 
reported fertile males without macroscopic or histological alterations 
of the reproductive tract20,31,32, others, most notably those investigating 
older (3–15 months) Cftrtm1Unc mice bred to congenic C57BL/6J, describe 
infertility, with bilateral absence of the vas deferens in 8/10 males; vas 
deferens was visible in only 2/10 males aged 3–5 months33. Histology and 
semen analysis in these older mutant animals revealed reduced epididy-
mal sperm counts and fewer late spermatids in the seminiferous tubules 
compared with wild-type animals 33. Studies extending to 6  months in 
CFTR KO animals with mixed genetic background also reported reduced 
seminiferous tubule diameter and decreased expression of protamine-2, 
a specific marker of spermatids and spermatozoa
34.
Overall, most investigations have been conducted in mice younger 
than 3 months, while reproductive tract alterations seem to emerge at later 
stages in complete CFTR KO models.
Finally, some investigators have suggested a delay in puberty in CF 
mice, presumably because of their reduced body weight35, which is con-
sistent with the delayed appearance of sperm in the vas deferens reported 
in both F508del and CFTR KO mice28.
Rat models
The first CFTR KO rat model was generated in 201436,37 and subsequent 
F508del knock-in models have since been developed37,38. Rat models more 
closely recapitulate key features of human CF , including some aspects 
of pulmonary disease and pancreatic involvement 21. They represent a 
more physiologically relevant alternative to mouse models, combining 
improved disease fidelity with ease of handling and relatively affordable 
housing costs.

In humans, CFTR is highly expressed in the fetal male reproduc-
tive tract, notably in Wolffian-derived structures, such as the rete testis, 
epididymis and vas deferens
4. CFTR is also highly expressed in the effer-
ent ducts, which derive from the mesonephric tubules9,10. Although these 
structures have not been specifically described in patients with CF , their 
role in fluid reabsorption and sperm concentration suggests that functional 
impairment in CF cannot be excluded.
Bilateral absence of the vas deferens in CF is associated with absence 
of the seminal vesicles in approximately 50% of cases and less consistently 
with epididymal anomalies11,12. The underlying mechanism is believed 
to involve progressive obstruction and atresia of the vas deferens due 
to abnormal intraluminal secretions. Fetal studies have demonstrated a 
morphologically normal reproductive tract up to 22 weeks of gestation, fol-
lowed by lymphoid infiltrates, epithelial destruction and mucinous plugs 
from 26 weeks onward, consistent with a secondary degenerative process3,4. 
Ultrasound studies in children with CF further support this theory, as 
pediatric patients exhibit fewer urogenital tract anomalies than adults
13.
Spermatogenesis is maintained at least at a moderate level in men 
with CF , although some studies have reported lower testosterone levels, 
suggesting possible testicular involvement
14. In a 2025 publication, we 
reported lower rates of epididymal sperm retrieval in patients without 
CFTR residual activity compared with those with residual activity, sug-
gesting testicular impairment in the absence of CFTR function6.
The advent of CFTR modulators has transformed the clinical land-
scape and substantially improved quality of life for patients with CF , ena-
bling more individuals to consider and pursue parenthood. Consequently, 
fertility has become a central concern in CF management. The widespread 
use of highly effective modulator therapy raises new questions regarding 
its potential impact on reproductive health. Indeed, early use of CFTR 
modulators may have implications for male reproductive outcomes 15. 
Because CFTR modulators improve mucus clearance and are administered 
earlier in life, including during pregnancy and early childhood, the key 
question is whether these treatments can preserve the vas deferens and 
epididymal structures, possibly by promoting the fluidification of intralu-
minal secretions. This possibility is supported by two recent case reports 
describing clinical outcomes in boys exposed in utero to elexacaftor/
tezacaftor/ivacaftor. In the first report, deferential structures appeared 
normal in a boy at 2 weeks and 3 months of age
16, whereas in the second 
report, normal reproductive anatomy was observed in a boy at 8 weeks 
and 6 months of age17. Because placental transfer of CFTR modulators has 
been documented, questions remain regarding the potential long-term 
consequences of an early and prolonged exposure to CFTR modulators, 
which may begin as early as the prenatal or neonatal period18. While these 
therapies may support the preservation of the epididymis and vas deferens, 
their long-term effects on testicular development and spermatogenesis 
remain to be elucidated.
In this context, evaluation of male reproductive outcomes across 
existing CF animal models is essential. Multiple animal models of CF are 
available, showing interspecies variability, including in the reproductive 
tract19. Comparative analysis of reproductive tract abnormalities and 
fertility endpoints is critical to identify the models that most accurately 
replicate the human phenotype and are best suited for studies on male 
fertility in CF . The aim of this Review is therefore to compare reproduc-
tive phenotypes across male CF animal models, highlight differences 
from human pathophysiology and identify the most relevant models for 
studying male fertility in CF .

Male reproductive phenotypes in CFTR KO rats seem to be closer 
to the human CF phenotype, as adult animals constantly exhibit bilateral 
absence or hypoplasia of the vas deferens36–38. McCarron et al.37 addition-
ally reported hypoplasia of the epididymis and seminal vesicles, along 
with testicular histological alterations consistent with hypospermato -
genesis (that is, reduced testicular sperm production). These alterations 
included decreased seminiferous tubule diameter, disorganization of the 
seminiferous epithelium and a reduced number of germ cells, particularly 
mature spermatids, in CF animals. Consequently, no sperm were detected 
in the cauda epididymis of these animals37. A milder degree of testicular 
dysfunction is suspected in patients with CF , although it has not yet been 
formally demonstrated. Although breeding of CFTR KO male rats has not 
been attempted, they are probably infertile due the combined absence of 
vas deferens and defective spermatogenesis.
F508del rats display a more heterogeneous phenotype: Dreano 
et al.38 reported variable presence of one or both vas deferens, although 
the frequency of each phenotype remains unknown. McCarron et al. 37 
observed that F508del rats display a phenotype similar to that of CFTR 
KO rats, with hypoplasia of the vas deferens and epididymis as well as 
hypospermatogenesis. However, in contrast to CFTR KO males, F508del 
rats exhibited normal to moderately reduced epididymal sperm counts 
in the cauda epididymis, although sperm cells exhibited morphological 
abnormalities such as head–tail disjunction37. This discrepancy between 
F508del and CFTR KO rats may be explained by residual CFTR function 
in F508del animals, as demonstrated by Dreano et al. 38, which could 
mitigate the severity of reproductive tract abnormalities.
Rabbit models
The first CFTR KO (CFΔ1) rabbit model of CF was established in 2021 
by Xu et al.39. These animals exhibited some manifestations of CF , such 
as gastrointestinal obstruction and growth restriction, but they did not 
spontaneously develop lung disease. Four adult males were reported to 
be infertile and exhibited a complete absence of both vas deferens and 
epididymis. No further investigations were conducted regarding testicular 
histology or spermatogenesis39.
Ferret models
In 2008, Sun et al. developed the first CFTR KO ferret, which repro -
duced key features of the human pathology, including ileus meconium, 
pancreatic involvement and early airway inflammation40. In a cohort of 
five male kits, one male exhibited complete absence of the vas deferens, 
while the remaining animals showed segmental or unilateral absence of 
the deferens. By contrast, heterozygous CFTR +/– kits had consistently 
normal vas deferens41. At that kit stage, epididymal morphology, sper -
matogenesis, histopathological abnormalities and long-term evolution 
of deferential structures were not studied. Another study from the same 
group confirmed these findings, reporting the absence of epididymis and 
vas deferens in four CFTR KO kits42.
The team also generated a CFTR-G551D knock-in model and con-
ducted a detailed investigation of the CFTR-G551D male reproductive 
tract. CFTRG551D homozygotes displayed the same phenotype as KO kits, 
characterized by the absence of epididymis and vas deferens (observed in 
three CFTR-G551D kits). Remarkably, when pregnant jills were treated 
with VX-770 (ivacaftor), their CFTR
G551D/G551D offspring were born with 
both epididymal and deferential structures42. This report represents the 
first documented case of in utero vas deferens preservation by CFTR 
modulator therapy in animal models.

Pig models
In 2008, Rogers et al.44 developed the first CFTR KO pig model, leverag-
ing the anatomical similarities between porcine and human airways to 
address the limitations of mouse models, which fail to develop sponta-
neous lung disease. Initial observations in 20 CFTR−/− piglets suggested 
that the vas deferens was normal at birth44. Subsequent studies, however, 
demonstrated that the majority of CFTR KO piglets exhibited absence 
of the vas deferens and epididymis, characterized by rudimentary caput 
epididymides with reduced tubular structures, while the testes remained 
normal45. A second CFTR KO pig model generated using bacterial artificial 
chromosomes (BAC) vectors displayed a similar phenotype, with absent 
or hypoplastic vas deferens and a rudimentary caput epididymis with a 
reduced ductular network
46.
Evaluation of the CFTRF508del/F508del model revealed a highly similar 
pattern of male reproductive tract abnormalities, with 17 of 19 piglets 
showing absence or hypoplasia of the vas deferens and an isolated rudi-
mentary caput epididymis
45.
Interestingly, when studying the potential mechanisms underlying the 
absence of vas deferens and epididymis in the CFTR KO and CFTR-F508del 
pig models, Pierucci-Alves et al.45 found no differences in mucus accumula-
tion within the reproductive tract compared with wild-type pigs, suggesting 
that the reproductive tract abnormalities develop independently of mucus 
inspissation or luminal obstruction. Proposed contributing mechanisms 
include (1) enhanced transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ) signaling, 
which can promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and fibrotic tissue 
remodeling; (2) reduced deferential luminal flow, leading to increased local 
prostaglandin levels, cellular stress and apoptosis; and (3) decreased circulat-
ing insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), as observed in CFTR KO piglets45 
and known to be associated with reproductive tract defects in male mice.
So far, no studies have assessed the progression of these abnormalities 
into adulthood or evaluated spermatogenesis in CF pigs.
Identifying the most relevant models
Evaluation of male reproductive phenotypes across CF animal models 
reveals high interspecies variability in the timing, severity and mechanisms 
underlying reproductive tract abnormalities (Table 1). While >95% of men 
with CF present with bilateral absence of the vas deferens, no single animal 
model fully recapitulates the human reproductive phenotype. Overall, CF 
mouse models, particularly CFTR knock-in or KO models with residual 
function, fail to reproduce the severity of the male reproductive phenotype 
observed in humans, as they often remain fertile with intact vas deferens 
and epididymides. This preservation of the reproductive tract may be 
partly explained by specific epididymal features in mice, including greater 
segmentation and a proportionally wider luminal diameter, which may 
reduce susceptibility to mucus inspissation and secondary obstruction23. 
Another hypothesis is that, similarly to the lungs and pancreas, which 
do not develop the characteristic CF symptoms observed in humans, 
alternative chloride channels may be present in the murine reproductive 
tract, potentially compensating for CFTR loss of function and thereby 
preventing obstruction and secondary atresia
47.
CFTR KO mice display age-dependent phenotypes ranging from 
preserved reproductive tract in young animals to progressive vas deferens 
atresia, accompanied by spermatogenic alterations. Further investiga -
tions, particularly in older CFTR KO mice, are warranted to confirm 
the phenotype described by Durie et al.33, which suggests the occurrence 
of age-related progressive vas deferens atresia. If validated, this model 
could provide a valuable tool for studying the mechanisms of secondary 
vas deferens atresia, although its onset might be delayed compared with 
humans. Caution is warranted, as most studies have focused on younger 
animals maintained on mixed backgrounds, making it unclear whether 
the phenotype reported by Durie et al.
33 reflects the congenic C57BL/6 
background, a later onset of the reproductive phenotype in mice, or 
both. Knock-in mouse models, meanwhile, do not appear to display any 
reproductive tract abnormalities.
CFTR KO rats, by contrast, more consistently display absence of 
bilateral vas deferens with epididymal hypoplasia, more closely mim -
icking the human phenotype. However, they frequently exhibit more 
pronounced testicular alterations, including hypospermatogenesis and 
absence of epididymal sperm, which seem more severe than those cur -
rently described in men with CF . Notably, in CFTR KO rats, vas deferens 
regression does not appear to be consistently associated with intraluminal 
obstruction, suggesting that mechanisms other than inspissated secretions 
may be involved48. A similar observation has been reported in CF pigs, in 
which Wolffian structure regression occurs without luminal obstruction 
or mucus accumulation45. While luminal obstruction remains a plausible 
explanation for vas deferens atresia in men, particularly given the nar -
rower luminal diameter23, some evidence suggests that alteration of the 
proximal epididymal compartment may also contribute to this phenotype. 
In men with absence of the vas deferens, the caput epididymis is typically 
present but frequently exhibit pathological changes 49,50. Human caput 
epididymal organoid models have shown that CFTR inhibition disrupts 
epithelial fluid transport and induces a reduction in the size of epididymal 
structures49. Moreover, studies in CFTR-deficient rodent models further 
indicate that CFTR participates in epithelial proliferation and differen-
tiation through interactions with the Wnt/β-catenin pathway48 and tight 
junction complexes51. Loss of CFTR function in these rodent models is 
associated with altered epithelial differentiation, increased proliferation 
and cytoarchitectural disorganization of epididymal structures. Together, 
these findings suggest that intrinsic epithelial dysfunction in proximal 
epididymal structures may contribute to subsequent vas deferens regres-
sion and partial epididymal absence.
Further studies in F508del rats are required to assess the penetrance 
of vas deferens alterations and their impact on spermatogenesis in adult 
males. The mechanisms underlying the absence of the vas deferens in CF 
are probably multifactorial.
Rats could represent a relevant and practical model for studying the CF 
reproductive phenotype. Housing costs are relatively low, and larger sam-
ples sizes than in mice facilitate the visualization and analysis of vas deferens 
and epididymal structures. While early studies have raised uncertainty 
regarding the responsiveness of the CFTR-F508del protein in rats to modu-
lator therapies52, emerging evidence now suggests that this model is at least 
partially responsive. Dreano et al.38 demonstrated significant restoration of 
CFTR-dependent chloride transport in CFTR-F508del rat primary nasal 
epithelial cells using the VX-809 + VX-770 combination. More recently, 
Reyne et al.53 reported in vivo correction of nasal CFTR function following 
treatment with the triple combination elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor in 
homozygous F508del rats. These findings suggest that rat CFTR-F508del is 
at least partially responsive to modulator therapies, supporting the utility 
of this model for evaluating their potential to prevent vas deferens atresia. 
Further studies are warranted to define the extent and tissue specificity of 
the modulator response in rats. An alternative approach could be the use 
of humanized rat models, such as rats expressing hCFTR-G551D, recently 
reported to respond to ivacaftor. However, the reproductive phenotype in 
this model has not yet been evaluated
54.
Among large model animals, ferrets, sheep and pigs consistently 
display absence of vas deferens but typically show normal testicular histol-
ogy. However, the extent of epididymal alteration differs across species, 
ranging from partial to complete absence. No explanation has yet been 
provided for this variability
39,41–46. CF is associated with high lethality in 
these models, mainly due to meconium ileus21. In addition, keeping large 
animals alive until adulthood incurs substantial costs 55. Consequently, 
most analyses have focused on the neonatal stage, and no data are cur -
rently available regarding the reproductive phenotype in adult males. In 
pigs, the mechanisms driving Wolffian structures regression seem distinct

Overall, these findings emphasize the need for complementary use 
of murine and large-animal models to investigate the mechanisms of 
reproductive tract abnormalities in CF . The CFTR KO rat model seems to 
be the most consistent model for the absence of the vas deferens, although 
the associated testicular alterations warrant caution. Ferrets and pigs reli-
ably mimic the absence of vas deferens observed in humans. However, 
the mechanisms observed in pigs differ from those described in humans.
Future research should focus on longitudinal evaluation of repro-
ductive outcomes extending into adulthood, particularly in large-animal 
models for which current data are largely restricted to the neonatal period. 
Such studies should assess not only reproductive tract anatomy but also 
spermatogenesis and fertility over time. The effects of aging, which remain 
largely unexplored in most models, should also be specifically addressed. 
This issue is becoming increasingly relevant, as highly effective modulator 
therapy has substantially improved life expectancy in individuals with CF , 
potentially leading to the emergence of age-related clinical challenges.
In addition, the impact of early and prolonged exposure to CFTR 
modulators, including in utero and during early childhood, warrants 
specific investigation. Confirming that pharmacological restoration 
of CFTR function can prevent vas deferens atresia, and assessing the 
long-term consequences on testicular development, germ cell maturation 
and adult fertility, represent critical priorities. Finally, detailed analyses 
of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying reproductive tract 
alterations across species will be necessary to determine to what extent 
these models faithfully reflect the human disease process.
