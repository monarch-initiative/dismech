# Mice heterozygous for a null mutation of CPE show reduced expression of carboxypeptidase e mRNA and enzyme activity but normal physiology, behavior, and levels of neuropeptides.
**Authors:** Fricker LD, Lemos Duarte M, Jeltyi A, Lueptow L, Fakira AK, Tashima AK, Hochgeschwender U, Wetsel WC, Devi LA
**Journal:** Brain Res (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.brainres.2022.147951](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brainres.2022.147951)

## Content

Carboxypeptidase E (CPE) is an essential enzyme that contributes to the biosynthesis of the vast majority of neuropeptides and peptide hormones. There are several reports claiming that small decreases in CPE activity cause physiological changes in animals and/or cultured cells, but these studies did not provide evidence that neuropeptide levels were affected by decreased CPE activity. In the present study, we tested if CPE is a rate-limiting enzyme in neuropeptide production using CpeNeo mice, which contain a neomycin cassette within the Cpe gene that eliminates enzyme expression. Homozygous CpeNeo/Neo mice show defects found in Cpefat/fat and/or Cpe global knockout (KO) mice, including greatly decreased levels of most neuropeptides, severely impaired fertility, depressive-like behavior, adult-onset obesity, and anxiety-like behavior. Removal of the neomycin cassette with Flp recombinase under a germline promoter restored expression of CPE activity and resulted in normal behavioral and physiological properties, including levels of neuropeptides. Mice heterozygous for the CpeNeo allele have greatly reduced levels of Cpe mRNA and CPE-like enzymatic activity. Despite the decreased levels of Cpe expression, heterozygous CpeNeo mice are behaviorally and physiologically identical to wild-type mice, with normal levels of most neuropeptides. These results indicate that CPE is not a rate-limiting enzyme in the production of most neuropeptides, casting doubt upon studies claiming small decreases in CPE activity contribute to obesity or other physiological effects.

Neuropeptides and peptide hormones belong to a large class of cell-cell signaling molecules that perform a number of diverse functions (Fricker, 2012). Most neuropeptides and peptide hormones are produced from precursors by a series of peptidases. Initially, one or more endopeptidases cleave the precursor at specific sites that contain basic amino acids, and these residues are subsequently removed by a carboxypeptidase (Fricker, 2012). Carboxypeptidase E (CPE) is the major peptide-processing carboxypeptidase (Fricker, 1988a). CPE is enriched in the regulated secretory granules of neuroendocrine cells and becomes activated as the pH decreases during granule maturation (Greene et al., 1992). Mice lacking CPE activity show a dramatic reduction in the levels of the mature forms of peptides and an increase in processing intermediates containing C-terminal basic residues, relative to wild-type (WT) mice (Fricker et al., 2006; Lim et al., 2006; Naggert et al., 1995; Srinivasan et al., 2004; Zhang et al., 2008)

Despite the importance of CPE in the production of many neuropeptides and peptide hormones, mice lacking CPE activity are viable, albeit with the physiological and behavioral phenotypes described below (Naggert et al., 1995). There are three reasons that can account for the viability of these mice. First, peptides located on the C-terminus of their precursors do not require a carboxypeptidase to produce the mature forms of these peptides. Second, some of the peptide-processing intermediates that are substrates of CPE are biologically active even with C-terminal Lys or Arg residues attached. Third, carboxypeptidase D (CPD) contributes to the processing of peptides in the trans-Golgi network (TGN) and immature secretory granules (Song and Fricker, 1995; Varlamov and Fricker, 1998). CPD has a similar substrate-specificity as CPE, and although CPD is not thought to be present in mature secretory granules, peptides derived from precursors that are initially cleaved in the TGN or immature secretory granules are only partially affected by the absence of CPE whereas peptides derived from precursors that are primarily cleaved in secretory granules show >90% reduction in mice lacking CPE (Fricker, 2010).

A naturally occurring mouse mutation, named ‘fat’ due to the obese phenotype (Coleman and Eicher, 1990), was mapped to the Cpe gene (Naggert et al., 1995). These mice have a point mutation that eliminates CPE activity due to a folding defect. Subsequently, the Cpe gene was disrupted in Cpe knockout (KO) mice (Cawley et al., 2004). Both Cpefat/fat and Cpe KO mice show similar phenotypes including greatly impaired fertility and adult-onset obesity (Cawley et al., 2004; Naggert et al., 1995). Mutations in the human CPE gene have been reported, including several that truncate the protein and eliminate critical catalytic residues (Alsters et al., 2015; Bosch et al., 2021; Durmaz et al., 2021). Individuals homozygous for these severe mutations are morbidly obese and have hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. Mice lacking CPE activity show elevated anxiety-like and depressive-like behaviors, and impaired spatial memory (Rodriguiz et al., 2013; Woronowicz et al., 2008).

Conditional Cpe KO mice (i.e., CpeFlx/Flx mice) were recently generated and tested using Cre recombinase under the Pomc promoter which targeted the knockout to cells expressing proopiomelanocortin (POMC) (Fricker et al., 2021). The disruption of Cpe in POMC cells had a significant effect on the levels of POMC-derived peptides such as α-melanocyte stimulating hormone (αMSH) but did not affect body weight. To generate the CpeFlx/Flx mice, an intermediate line (named CpeNeo) was produced. CpeNeo mice contain a neomycin cassette which was removed using Flp recombinase expressed under a germ-line promoter to produce CpeFlx/Flx mice (Figure 1A). Homozygous CpeNeo/Neo mice were noted to have greatly reduced fertility during breeding, raising the possibility that they did not produce CPE. Often, the presence of a neomycin cassette blocks normal exon splicing, generating a de facto “knock-out first” allele (Skarnes et al., 2011; Testa et al., 2004). Removing the targeting cassette by Flp recombinase usually restores wild-type levels of gene expression.

The initial goal of this study was to characterize CpeNeo/Neo mice and compare them to WT and CpeFlx/Flx mice (Figure 1A). After confirming that homozygous CpeNeo/Neo mice are Cpe-null, we performed a detailed characterization of heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice. These mice have levels of Cpe mRNA that are ~50% of those in WT mice, and also show greatly reduced enzyme activity. Therefore, these heterozygotes are a suitable model to study the consequences of reduced CPE enzyme activity. A number of studies have claimed that CPE inhibitors produce biological effects (Bommer et al., 1989; Cao et al., 2017; Höring et al., 2012; Liew et al., 2014; Plum et al., 2009; Przewlocka et al., 1986; Vannini et al., 2020), but these studies did not confirm that CPE was inhibited in vivo by demonstration of reduced neuropeptide levels. If small changes in CPE activity produce physiological effects, then CPE must be a rate-limiting enzyme. To test this idea, we used a peptidomics approach to quantify levels of individual neuropeptides in heterozygous CpeNeo mice, compared to their levels in wild-type mice and CPE-null mice. We also investigated other physiological parameters (body weight, fertility) and behaviors (anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors) in CpeNeo heterozygous mice. Our finding that CpeNeo heterozygous mice are indistinguishable from wild-type mice raises questions about studies associating small changes in CPE activity with biological effects.

CPE-like enzymatic activity was measured in frontal cortex using dansyl-Phe-Ala-Arg (Figure 1B). To distinguish CPE from lysosomal enzymes that also cleave the substrate, the reaction was performed in the presence of 1 mM CoCl2 or 1 μM guanidinoethylmercaptosuccinic acid (GEMSA), with the difference in activity reflecting metallocarboxypeptidase CPE and CPD (Fricker, 1995). To minimize the contribution from CPD, the tissue was centrifuged and the soluble fraction was assayed for metalloCP activity. The majority of CPE activity is soluble whereas the majority of CPD in brain is membrane bound (Song and Fricker, 1996; Supattapone et al., 1984). However, some CPD is present in the soluble fraction and is detected by the assay (Song and Fricker, 1996). Thus, the small amount of enzyme activity detected in the CpeNeo/Neo mice (Figure 1B) is likely due to CPD. The level of residual enzymatic activity in the CpeNeo/Neo mice (Figure 1B) is comparable to the activity found in Cpefat/fat mice when assayed under similar conditions (Naggert et al., 1995). CPE-like activity is fully restored upon removal of the neomycin cassette by Flp recombinase (CpeFlx/Flx mice, Figure 1A–B). Mice with one functional allele and one mutant allele (i.e., heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice and heterozygous CpeFlx/Neo mice) have significantly reduced levels of CPE-like activity relative to WT mice (Figure 1B).

To assess CPE mRNA levels in the various genotypes, mRNA was prepared from a coronal slice through the hypothalamus, which included the hippocampus, cortex, and other brain regions. Relative levels of CPE mRNA were determined by qPCR using sets of primers that spanned exons 1 & 2, exons 2 & 3, and exons 3 & 4 (Figure 1C). Only the latter set was predicted to be affected if the neomycin cassette was not removed during RNA splicing, but often mRNAs that are incorrectly spliced are rapidly degraded. The qPCR analysis shows the neomycin cassette greatly decreased the levels of CPE mRNA, regardless of the primer set used, with ~99% reduction in CpeNeo/Neo mice, relative to WT controls. Removal of the neomycin cassette with Flp recombinase (i.e., F/F group) restored CPE mRNA to levels comparable to WT mice. Importantly, levels of CPE mRNA in the heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice and the mixed CpeFlx/Neo mice were reduced to ~50% of WT mice (Figure 1C). The large reduction in levels of CPE mRNA in some groups did not cause an increase in levels of CPD mRNA (Figure 1C).

Homozygous CpeNeo/Neo mice are subfertile (Table 1). Nine female homozygous CpeNeo/Neo mice were mated either with five WT, two heterozygous CpeNeo/+, or two homozygous CpeNeo/Neo mice, typically starting at 7–8 weeks of age and maintained for several months. None of the nine breeding pairs produced viable offspring that survived until weaning. One of the breeding pairs of CpeNeo/Neo females mated to a WT male gave birth to a litter of 7 pups (at 118 days), another breeding pair gave birth to a litter of 2 pups (at 90 days). While the pups appeared healthy at birth, they did not survive until weaning. These two breeding pairs failed to produce additional litters over several months, and the other seven breeding pairs with female CpeNeo/Neo mice did not produce any litters. Three breeding pairs of male CpeNeo/Neo mice mated with heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice only produced one litter of 6 pups, all of which were viable (4 CpeNeo/+ and 2 CpeNeo/Neo). This breeding pair was mated when the mice were very young (5 weeks) and the female became pregnant when the CpeNeo/Neo male was 6.4 weeks old. This pair did not produce any additional litters. The two infertile breeding pairs were mated when the male CpeNeo/Neo mice were 8 and 13 weeks.

In summary, young male and female CpeNeo/Neo mice are subfertile. Female CpeNeo/Neo mice are unable to produce pups that survive until weaning. Male CpeNeo/Neo mice are subfertile when mated at a young age, and infertile when mice >7 weeks are mated. This is similar to results with Cpefat/fat mice (Srinivasan et al., 2004) and Cpe KO mice (Cawley et al., 2004). In contrast, heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice were fertile. Twenty six breeding pairs produced 74 litters, with a mean litter size of 6.1 pups born and 5.6 weaned. The male/female ratio from breeding was 1.02 and the frequencies of Cpe+/+, CpeNeo/+, and CpeNeo/Neo pups (0.31, 0.54, and 0.15) were close to the expected Mendelian distribution, albeit with a slight decrease in viable CpeNeo/Neo pups.

Removal of the neomycin cassette with Flp recombinase restored fertility (Table 1). Two breeding pairs of homozygous CpeFlx/Flx mice mated to heterozygous CpeFlx/+ mice produced 44 pups in 7 litters, of which 43 pups survived until weaning (6.1 viable pups/litter). When both parents were homozygous, 89 pups were produced in 17 litters from 6 breeding pairs, for a mean of 5.2 pups per litter, of which 80 pups survived until weaning (4.7 pups per litter). The male/female ratio from breeding was 1.29.

CpeNeo/Neo mice show adult-onset obesity (Figure 2), as previously reported for Cpefat/fat mice and Cpe KO mice (Cawley et al., 2004; Naggert et al., 1995). The CpeNeo/Neo males were significantly heavier than all other groups at 18–26 weeks (p≤0.001), and at 16 weeks were significantly heavier than the WT, CpeNeo/+, and CpeFlx/Neo mice (p≤0.05). CpeNeo/Neo females were significantly heavier than all other genotypes from 12–26 weeks (p<0.03), and from 8–10 weeks were significantly heavier than the WT and CpeFlx/+ mice (p≤0.03). Body weights of male and female CpeNeo/+, CpeFlx/+, and CpeFlx/Flx mice were normal compared to the WT controls (Figure 2).

Previous studies with Cpefat/fat mice found that mature adult mice exhibited anxiety-like behavior in a variety of assays: elevated zero maze, light/dark box, and open field (Rodriguiz et al., 2013). However, anxiety-like behavior was not observed in young adult Cpefat/fat mice (Rodriguiz et al., 2013). To test if the CpeNeo/Neo mice showed a similar age-dependent increase in anxiety-like behavior, we used the open field assay (Figure 3A, B). Locomotor activities were comparable among young adult mice (6–11 wks) of all genotypes and they spent similar amounts of the time (~10%) in the center of the open field. However, the >20 wk CpeNeo/Neo mice showed a large decrease in locomotion and spent very little time (~3%) in the center zone (Figure 3A, B). This finding is similar to previous results with Cpefat/fat mice (Rodriguiz et al., 2013). Importantly, mature adult heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice (>20 wks) showed no difference in total distance traveled or time in center compared to either WT or CpeFlx/Flx genotypes (Figure 3A, B), indicating that reduced CPE levels had no impact on anxiety-like behavior seen in the Cpe null mice.

Previous studies observed depressive-like behavior in Cpefat/fat mice using both the tail suspension and forced swim assays (Rodriguiz et al., 2013). To determine whether the CpeNeo/Neo mice and other genotypes displayed this behavior, we used the forced swim test. For these studies, only young pre-obese adult mice (7–12 wks) were tested to avoid the complication of measuring swimming/floating in obese mice. As with the other behavioral tests, males and females in roughly equal proportion were tested, and because there were no significant differences between sex, the results were pooled. The CpeNeo/Neo mice showed a dramatic increase in the time spent immobile (Figure 3C). WT mice spent approximately one third of the time immobile, while the CpeNeo/Neo mice spent nearly two thirds of the 5 minute test period immobile. The other genotypes were not statistically different from the WT group, and the CpeNeo/Neo group was significantly different from all other genotypes (Figure 3C).

Taken together, the CpeNeo/Neo mice display anxiety-like and depressive-like behavior as previously reported with the Cpefat/fat mice (Rodriguiz et al., 2013). Mice in which the neomycin cassette was removed with Flp recombinase from birth (i.e., CpeFlx/Flx mice) were indistinguishable from WT mice. Heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice are not significantly different from WT controls in either assay (Figure 3A–C).

Quantitative peptidomics of whole brain was performed to examine the relative levels of peptides in each genotype. Peptides were extracted from whole mouse brain that had been heated to 80°C to inactivate peptidases prior to further processing of the tissue—this step is necessary to eliminate postmortem degradation of peptides and proteins (Dienel, 2021). Tissue extracts were labeled with one of five isotopic tags, filtered through a 10 kDa membrane to remove proteins, purified on C18 resin to remove salts, and analyzed by liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC/MS). The isotopic tags involved reductive methylation with heavy and light forms of formaldehyde. This procedure allows 5 samples to be compared in each LC/MS run. Each run included 2–3 WT mice, and 2–3 mice from other genotypes. Altogether 6 LC/MS runs were performed with 5–13 replicates of each genotype (Figure 4). Hundreds of distinct peptides were detected in one or more of the LC/MS runs (see Supplemental Table S1). Of these, 22 peptides were detected in all 6 runs, as well as in previous studies comparing Cpefat/fat and WT mouse brain (Lim et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2008). Two of these 22 peptides are shown in Figure 5, Panels A-D, and all 22 peptides are summarized in Panels E-F. Data for each of these peptides is provided in Figure 6 and Supplemental Table S1.

The proenkephalin-derived peptide SPQLEDEAKELQ is greatly decreased in CpeNeo/Neo mice, relative to WT mice (Figure 5A). Conversely, the immediate precursor of this peptide that contains Lys-Arg on the C-terminus is dramatically elevated in CpeNeo/Neo mice; levels are not detectable in WT mice and are clearly present in the CpeNeo/Neo mice (Figure 5B). Similarly, the mature form of this peptide was previously found to be greatly decreased in Cpefat/fat mice relative to WT mice (Figure 5C), while the precursor was elevated (Figure 5D).

The 22 peptides found in each of the 6 LC/MS runs and in previous studies comparing Cpefat/fat and WT mice can be grouped into 3 categories based on the importance of CPE in their formation. The first category of peptides is defined as those that are primarily produced by CPE. Seventeen of the 22 peptides were decreased >50% in the CpeNeo/Neo mice, relative to WT mice (Figure 5E), and the magnitude of decrease previously reported in Cpefat/fat mice (Lim et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2008) is similar to the magnitude of change found in the present study on CpeNeo/Neo mice (Figure 5F). These 17 peptides were not significantly altered in the peptidomic analyses comparing either heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice or CpeFlx/Flx mice with the WT mice (Figure 5E). The second category of peptides was defined as those in which CPD plays an important role, based on the presence of consensus sites for endopeptidase cleavage by furin or related enzymes in the TGN or immature secretory granules. Two of the 22 peptides fit into this category—these proSAAS-derived peptides are cleaved at a furin-consensus site (RLRR-AV) and then trimmed by CPD to produce the peptides ending in RL, and both of these were only partially decreased in the CpeNeo/Neo mice (Figure 5E). However, this decrease was not statistically significant (Figure 6). The third category is defined as peptides that do not require CPE because they are located on the C-terminus of their precursors. Three peptides fit this category (Figure 5E, 5F, and Figure 6). While none of these were significantly decreased in either the CpeNeo/Neo or the Cpefat/fat mice, two were significantly increased in both of these genotypes, relative to WT mice (Figure 6); these peptides represent the C-termini of proSAAS (i.e., BigLEN) and protachykinin A (i.e., peptide ALN…NYE). None of the C-terminal peptides are significantly altered in the CpeFlx/Flx or CpeNeo/+ mice, relative to WT mice (Figure 6).

Relative levels of mRNAs encoding several neuropeptide precursors were examined by qPCR, using the same mRNA/cDNA samples used to examine Cpe and Cpd mRNA levels in Figure 1. Despite the significant increase in C-terminal peptides derived from proSAAS and protachykinin A, the mRNAs encoding these proteins were not elevated in the CpeNeo/Neo mice relative to WT mice (Figure 7). Proenkephalin mRNA was also examined because the C-terminal peptide (i.e., ‘heptapeptide’) showed a slight decrease, albeit non-significant, in the peptidomics analysis of CpeNeo/Neo mice. As with proSAAS and protachykinin mRNA, the levels of proenkephalin mRNA were also similar between CpeNeo/Neo and WT mice (Figure 7).

One major finding of the present study is that CpeNeo/Neo mice are very similar to the previously characterized Cpe null mice (i.e., Cpefat/fat or Cpe KO). On a molecular level, CpeNeo/Neo mice have a large decrease in CPE expression and enzyme activity, whereas mice in which the neomycin cassette was removed with Flp recombinase (i.e., CpeFlx/Flx mice) show WT levels of CPE expression and enzyme activity. Peptidomics analyses of mouse brain confirmed that the mature forms of most neuropeptides were greatly decreased in the CpeNeo/Neo mice, whereas the levels in the CpeFlx/Flx mice with the Neo cassette removed were similar to those in WT mice. The extent of the neuropeptide changes in CpeNeo/Neo mice found in the present study is comparable to the extent of those previously found in Cpe null mice (Cawley et al., 2010; Lim et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2008). Physiologically and behaviorally, CpeNeo/Neo mice are also similar to Cpe null mice; these parameters include impaired fertility (Srinivasan et al., 2004), adult-onset obesity (Naggert et al., 1995), and expression of anxiety-like and depressive-like behaviors (Rodriguiz et al., 2013). All of these physiological and behavioral defects were normalized by removal of the Neo cassette using Flp recombinase. Taken together, CpeNeo/Neo mice are a CPE null mouse line that complements the conditional CpeFlx/Flx mouse line. Treatment of CpeFlx/Flx mice with Cre recombinase leads to elimination of CPE enzyme activity, while treatment of CpeNeo/Neo mice with Flp recombinase leads to expression of CPE activity on a null background. These two lines can be used to identify the cells and neuropeptides that contribute to the phenotypes of Cpe null mice, using CpeFlx/Flx mice and selective Cre expression or using CpeNeo/Neo mice and selective Flp expression. In addition, these two mouse lines will be useful in providing information on peptides in cells that are affected by the presence/absence of CPE, thereby identifying potential therapeutic targets. A key feature of the peptidomics approach is the relative levels of mature forms (i.e., fully processed) versus the Lys/Arg-extended intermediate precursor forms (i.e., CPE substrates). The C-terminally-extended peptides can be efficiently purified on affinity columns, permitting the detection of relatively low abundance peptides in extracts from small areas of Cpe-null mouse brain (Fricker et al., 2021).

Another major finding of the present study is that CpeNeo/+ heterozygous mice have essentially normal levels of most neuropeptides, despite greatly reduced Cpe expression and enzymatic activity. While heterozygous Cpe null mice (e.g., heterozygous Cpefat or Cpe KO mice) were known to be fertile, other parameters were not previously reported including whether peptide levels were altered in heterozygous mice. In the present study, only one peptide (proSAAS derived SLSAASAPLVETSTPL) showed a significant difference in levels between CpeNeo/+ and WT mice. None of the other peptides were affected by ~50% reduction in CPE in the CpeNeo/+ mice. Thus, CPE is not a rate-limiting enzyme in the production of most neuropeptides. This result confirms previous predictions based on the extremely low levels of peptides with C-terminal basic residues in WT mice, with a few exceptions such as α-neoendorphin which ends with Pro-Arg and is inefficiently cleaved by CPE due to the penultimate Pro (Chen et al., 2001).

The present finding that greatly reduced CPE levels do not alter the levels of most peptides raises questions about studies that claim to inhibit CPE activity and produce biological effects (Bommer et al., 1989; Cao et al., 2017; Höring et al., 2012; Liew et al., 2014; Plum et al., 2009; Przewlocka et al., 1986; Vannini et al., 2020). Many of these studies used GEMSA, an arginine analog that binds CPE in vitro with a Ki of 9 nM (Fricker et al., 1983). However, it is unlikely that GEMSA can significantly inhibit CPE in cells or animal tissues because GEMSA is not predicted to enter dense core secretory vesicles where CPE is located. While peptides of 5 or more amino acids with multiple arginine residues and a net positive charge can penetrate cells (Milletti, 2012), GEMSA is below this size requirement and has a net negative charge of −1 at physiological pH. Furthermore, cell-penetrating peptides enter the cell cytosol but are not known to enter secretory granules where CPE is located. Despite these theoretical concerns, GEMSA was reported to reduce levels of enkephalin by 50% following 3 days of twice-daily injection into rat brain (Bommer et al., 1989). In the Bommer study, other peptide products of CPE were found to be unaltered or to increase by 75%, thereby raising doubts about the mechanism of the reported changes in enkephalin levels (Bommer et al., 1989). Additional studies have claimed that GEMSA injections into animals produce biological changes, but none of these studies demonstrated that CPE was directly inhibited or that peptide levels were altered (Cao et al., 2017; Höring et al., 2012; Przewlocka et al., 1986; Vannini et al., 2020). For example, Vannini and colleagues injected GEMSA into the brain of tetanus neurotoxin-treated mice and found changes in electrical activity within 5 minutes; from this result they concluded that “CPE inhibition reduces indicators of seizure activity in epileptic mice” despite no evidence that GEMSA penetrated the cellular and secretory granule membranes to gain access to CPE, or that the 5 minute treatment altered peptide levels (Vannini et al., 2020).

Several studies have observed 10–50% decreases in CPE levels and suggested that these changes were responsible for physiologic effects, without providing evidence that peptide processing was altered (Liew et al., 2014; Plum et al., 2009). Based on the present finding that CPE is not a rate-limiting enzyme in the production of the vast majority of neuropeptides, it is doubtful that small changes in CPE enzymatic activity contribute to biological effects in the studies cited above. However, it is possible that CPE has non-enzymatic roles, and if so, then small changes could potentially have consequences. For example, CPE has been claimed to function as a chaperone for the sorting of neuropeptide precursors into secretory granules (Cool et al., 1997) although three other laboratories tested this and concluded that CPE was not involved in prohormone sorting (Irminger et al., 1997; Kemppainen and Behrend, 2010; Varlamov et al., 1997). CPE was also proposed to function as a neurotrophic factor, named neurotrophic factor-α1 (Cheng et al., 2014; Chougule et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2019), and an alternatively spliced form of CPE lacking the N-terminal signal peptide domain (CPE-ΔN) was reported in the cell nucleus where it was claimed to induce tumor growth and serve as a marker for metastatic cancer (Lee et al., 2011). However, this publication and another by the same laboratory have been retracted (Lee et al., 2019; Murthy et al., 2020). Recently, two studies by Liang et al reported that shRNA mediated knock-down of Cpe mRNA caused changes in neuronal migration, dendrite morphology, and microtubule glutamylation (Liang et al., 2019; Liang et al., 2022). The mechanism of some of these effects was proposed to be non-enzymatic, based on the finding that cytosolic expression of a 10-residue peptide corresponding to the C-terminus of CPE produced the same effect (Liang et al., 2019). These studies assumed CPE to be a transmembrane protein based on a publication (Dhanvantari et al., 2002). However, the sequence of CPE does not contain a traditional transmembrane-spanning domain (Fricker et al., 1986), and numerous studies have shown that CPE is released from membranes at neutral pH with an intact C-terminus (Fricker, 1988b; Fricker et al., 1990; Parkinson, 1990; Parkinson, 1992). The pH-dependent release suggests that CPE is a peripheral membrane protein bound to membranes through a C-terminal amphipathic helix (Fricker et al., 1990). Thus, the effect that Liang and colleagues observed when they expressed the C-terminal region of CPE in the cytosol is not physiological. Taken together, the literature on non-enzymatic functions of CPE is inconsistent between different laboratories and further studies are necessary to resolve the discrepancies.

The mature forms of all neuropeptides are not completely eliminated in the absence of CPE because of the contribution of CPD, which has a similar substrate specificity as CPE. One main difference between these enzymes is that CPD resides in the Golgi, TGN, and immature secretory vesicles but is removed from secretory granules as they mature (Varlamov et al., 1999). By contrast, CPE is inactive in the Golgi and activated by acidification and elevated Ca2+ levels that occur in maturing secretory granules. CPD processes peptides cleaved by furin in the TGN and/or immature vesicles (Han et al., 2020). Some peptide precursors contain furin consensus sites, such as several proSAAS-derived peptides (Fricker et al., 2000; Sayah et al., 2001; Wardman and Fricker, 2014). These peptides are only partially reduced in the CpeNeo/Neo mice in the present study, consistent with previous findings using Cpefat/fat mice (Lim et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2008). Some peptides were not unaffected by the absence of CPE in the CpeNeo/Neo mice; most of these represent cytosolic protein degradation fragments (Table S1). Although these cytosolic peptides may have important functions in cells (Ferro et al., 2014), they are not produced in the secretory pathway and therefore are not CPE substrates or products. Peptides derived from the C-terminus of secretory pathway proteins were not significantly decreased in the CpeNeo/Neo mice. These C-terminal peptides showed either a small non-significant decrease in Cpe null mice (heptapeptide from proenkephalin) or a significant increase (peptides located at the C-terminus of proSAAS and protachykinin A). The reason for the altered levels of these peptides does not appear to be due to changes in gene expression based on the similar levels of mRNAs encoding the precursors proSAAS, proenkephalin, and protachykinin A (Figure 7). Cpd mRNA levels were also found to be unaltered in the heterozygous or homozygous CpeNeo mice (Figure 1C), indicating no upregulation of CPD mRNA expression in these mice; examination of CPD protein levels was not performed. CPE and CPD are the only carboxypeptidases known to participate in neuropeptide production, with CPE performing the major role.

Previously Cpe null mice have been found to show age-dependent changes in some physiological/behavioral parameters, with anxiety, infertility, and obesity developing during the transition from young adult to mature adult (Cawley et al., 2004; Naggert et al., 1995; Rodriguiz et al., 2013; Srinivasan et al., 2004). Other phenotypes have been detected as early as 7 weeks, such as depressive-like behavior and subfertility (Cawley et al., 2004; Naggert et al., 1995; Rodriguiz et al., 2013; Srinivasan et al., 2004; Woronowicz et al., 2008). All of these phenotypes were found in young CpeNeo/Neo mice in the present study. The CpeNeo/Neo mice show similar age-related changes as previously reported for Cpe null mice in terms of body weight, fertility, and anxiety. To address this point, future studies can use tamoxifen-inducible Flp CpeNeo/Neo mice to selectively turn on CPE expression at various ages. Alternatively, tamoxifen inducible Cre CpeFlx/Flx mice can be used to selectively disrupt CPE expression at various ages.

In conclusion, the CpeNeo/Neo mice represent a novel model of global CPE-null mice. Heterozygous CpeNeo/+ mice have reduced expression of CPE mRNA and enzyme activity but show normal body weights and behavioral activities, and levels of most neuropeptides are similar to WT mice.

Mice were euthanized by decapitation and the frontal cortex dissected, frozen in dry ice, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Frozen tissue was homogenized in 0.5 mL of 100 mM NaAc buffer, pH 5.5, using a mechanical tissue disruptor (Brinkmann Polytron; Kinematica, Bohemia, NY) and centrifuged at 15,000 × g in a microfuge for 20 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and aliquots were assayed for enzyme activity using the fluorescent substrate dansyl-Phe-Ala-Arg as described (Fricker, 1995), with modifications to adapt the assay to a 96-well plate-reader fluorometer (Fricker et al., 2021). Fluorescence was determined using a 96-well plate spectrofluorometer: excitation 355 nm; emission 538 nm, top read, filter cutoff 455 nm. Metallocarboxypeptidase activity was determined from the difference between activity in the presence of CoCl2 (which activates CPE) and GEMSA (an in vitro inhibitor of CPE), assayed in triplicate for each biological sample. Activity was divided by the mass of the tissue. The number of biological replicates is indicated in the figure legend.

Mice for peptidomic studies were euthanized by decapitation and their heads were placed in a standard microwave oven and irradiated for 5 sec to bring the internal temperature to ~80°C, as described (Che et al., 2005). This heat-treatment is necessary in order to inactivate tissue enzymes which would otherwise degrade cellular proteins and greatly increase the background signal from non-neuropeptides (Che et al., 2005; Fridjonsdottir et al., 2018; Svensson et al., 2003). After cooling, the brain was removed, frozen in dry ice, and stored at −70°C until peptide extraction. Prior to extraction, the tissue was transferred to a −20°C freezer. Cold water (1.5 mL) was added to the sample immediately prior to homogenization using a tissue disruptor (Brinkmann Polytron). Homogenates were incubated in a 70°C water-bath for 20 min, cooled to room temperature, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C in a microfuge. The supernatant was removed and stored at −70°C until proceeding.

The isotopic labeling was performed using reductive methylation as described (Dasgupta et al., 2018; Tashima and Fricker, 2018). In brief, an aliquot of brain supernatant (100 μL) was combined with 10 μL of 1 M sodium acetate buffer pH 6.0 and mixed. In a fume hood, 64 μL of freshly prepared 4% formaldehyde solution was added. One of three formaldehyde solutions was used: light (e.g., natural isotopic form), intermediate (D2CO), or heavy (D213CO) according to the chart below (D represents deuterium, 2H). One of two different reducing agents was added: 64 μL of 0.6 M NaBH3CN or NaBD3CN (see chart below) and the samples were mixed. After incubation for 18 hr at room temperature, the reaction was quenched with 200 μL of freshly made 4% ammonium bicarbonate. Tubes were mixed and incubated for 2 hr at room temperature. The mixture was acidified with 200 μL of 10% formic acid in a fume hood (the reaction may produce hydrogen cyanide) and incubated 10 min at room temperature. Samples for each set were combined (e.g., the five individual tissue samples labeled with different isotopic tags to be compared in the peptidomics analysis; see scheme in Figure 4).

Peptides were separated from proteins using microfiltration with a 10 kDa cutoff dialysis membrane (Ultracel-10 K units; Amicon, Miami, FL), then purified on C18 spin columns (Pierce Chemical, Dallas, TX). The general procedure has been recently described (Dasgupta et al., 2018; Fricker et al., 2021; Tashima and Fricker, 2018). In brief, the samples were applied to the ultrafiltration devices that had been rinsed three times with water to remove potential contaminants. Samples were centrifuged at 2,300 × g until very little liquid remained in the samples (typically 30 min). The flow-through contained peptides. This fraction was acidified with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to a final concentration of 0.5% and applied to a C18 spin column (PierceChemical) that had been activated as described by the manufacturer. The sample was applied to the spin column in aliquots of 400 μL (the maximum capacity of the spin columns), until the total sample was loaded onto the column. The columns were washed 3 times with 200 μL of equilibration buffer (0.5% TFA containing 5% acetonitrile) and peptides were eluted with 200 μL of 0.5% TFA containing 70% acetonitrile. These eluates were dried in a vacuum centrifuge and stored at −70°C until MS analysis.

The liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC/MS) experiments were performed on a Synapt G2 mass spectrometer coupled to a nanoAcquity capillary nano-LC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Dried peptides were dissolved in 20 μL of 0.1% formic acid and the peptide mixture (5 μL) was desalted online for 5 min at a flow rate of 8 μL/min of phase A (0.1% formic acid) in a trap column (Symmetry C18 Trap Column, 100 Å, 5-μm particles, 180 μm × 20 mm; Waters). The mixture of trapped peptides was subsequently separated by elution with a 7–35% gradient of phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) through a capillary analytical column (BEH 130 C18 column, 1.7-μm particles, 75 μm × 200 mm; Waters) over 90 min. The data were acquired in the data-dependent mode and the MS spectra of multiple-charged protonated peptides generated by electrospray ionization were acquired for 1 sec from m/z 400–1600. The three most intense ions exceeding a base peak intensity threshold of 2500 counts were automatically selected and dissociated by 15- to 60-eV collisions with argon for 0.6 sec. The typical LC and electrospray ionization conditions consisted of a flow rate of 275 nL/min, a capillary voltage of 3.0 kV, a block temperature of 100 °C, and a cone voltage of 40 V. The dynamic peak exclusion window was set to 45 sec.

MS spectra were analyzed using the MassLynx 4.1 software (Waters). Peak groups representing peptides labeled with different isotopic labels were identified by the regular mass difference profiles of consecutive peaks. The relative intensity of each monoisotopic peak was entered into the spreadsheet and adjusted to correct for isotopic overlapping of labeled peaks as described (Dasgupta et al., 2018; Tashima and Fricker, 2018). To identify peptides, MS/MS data were analyzed using the Mascot search engine (Matrix Science Inc., Boston, MA) and the NCBIprot database, specifying Mus musculus (house mouse) which consisted of 172780 sequences. No cleavage site was specified. Modifications included the reductive methyl labels and N-terminal protein acetylation, methionine oxidation, and C-terminal amidation species. All search results were manually interpreted to eliminate false positives, as described (Morano et al., 2008; Wardman and Fricker, 2011). In brief, the criteria included: (i) an observed mass within 40 ppm (preferably 20 ppm) of the theoretical mass; (ii) the number of Me tags observed matched with the number of free primary amines available (i.e., Lys residue and N-terminus if not acetylated); (iii) the observed charge state(s) of the peptide was consistent with the expected number of positive charges; and (iv) ≥80% of the major fragments observed in the tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) matched b or y ions, with a minimum of five matches. In addition, due to the small 2 Da difference between isotopic tags, we used a wider mass range for the initial MASCOT search and then manually confirmed the monoisotopic assignments, as described (Hsieh et al., 2010). Mass spectrometry data have been submitted to the MassIVE repository with the dataset identifier MSV000087742 (ftp://MSV000087742@massive.ucsd.edu), which can be accessed using the username: MSV000087742_reviewer; Password: ldfcpe2021.

In addition to analyzing the data from the present study, selected peptides were compared to previous studies performed with Cpefat/fat mice (Lim et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2008). These analyses were performed to determine if the changes seen in the present study were comparable to those seen in previous studies with Cpe-null mice.

Most of the results regarding fertility were from breeding pairs initiated when the mice were 7–9 weeks of age and maintained for 3–4 months. To investigate subfertility in the CpeNeo/Neo mice, some of the breeding pairs were initiated when the mice were ~5 weeks of age based on previous reports that Cpe null mice occasionally produced litters when mated at a young age (Srinivasan et al., 2004). Some CpeNeo/Neo breeding pairs were maintained together until the mice were 6 months of age. In all cases, breeding pairs were monitored 3 times per week and the number of newborns recorded and compared to the number of pups that survived until weaning at 3 weeks of age.

Mice were weighed every 2 weeks from 6–26 weeks of age. Mice used for some behavioral experiments (e.g., open field) were included in the body weight study following the testing.

Mice were placed in 50 × 35 cm plastic boxes with opaque walls and motor responses were video recorded for >15 min using a ceiling mounted camera. Recordings were subsequently analyzed using AnyMaze software (version 5.33; Stoelting Company, Wood Dale, IL) with the open field protocol. The center zone was defined as 24 ×18 cm, which represents 25% of the total area of the box. In addition to monitoring time spent in center area, the number of line crossings, and the total distance traveled was determined for the first 15 min in the open field.

For each genotype, the following numbers of male and female mice were used. Young adult (6–11 weeks of age): WT 3 males, 7 females; CpeNeo/+ 5 males, 12 females; CpeNeo/Neo 4 males, 7 females; CpeFlx/Flx 4 males, 1 female. Mature adult (20–33 weeks of age): WT 5 males, 15 females; CpeNeo/+ 5 males, 4 females; CpeNeo/Neo 9 males, 13 females; CpeFlx/Flx 4 males, 7 females; CpeFlx/Neo 6 males, 2 females.

Mice were placed in 27°C water in a glass cylinder 12.5 cm diameter, 18.0 cm high, with 13.0 cm water height. An observer blinded to the genotype recorded immobility over a 5 min period in increments of 1 min. Following the 5 min swim, the mice were removed, dried with a paper towel, and monitored for 10 min.

The results are presented as means ± standard errors of the mean. For each experiment, the statistical tests and the number of mice in each group are indicated in the figure legends. Some of the statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.02. Analyses of the data depicting the body weight over time were conducted with RMANOVA using SS-IV because not all mice had body weights for every time point. Bonferroni corrected pair-wise comparisons were used as the post-hoc tests for the significant RMANOVA terms. The RMANOVA tests were run using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). In all cases p<0.05 was considered significant. No animals were excluded from any of the physiological or behavioral studies reported in this study.
