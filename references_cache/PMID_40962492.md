# Heterozygous TBX2 frameshift variants cause a novel syndromic hearing loss with incompletely penetrant nystagmus.
**Authors:** Hua W, Wang Y, Li X, Xiong W, Wang L, Chen M, Bu F, Liu L, Chen F, Zhong M, Lu Y, Liu Z, Cheng J, Yuan H
**Journal:** J Med Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1136/jmg-2025-110997](https://doi.org/10.1136/jmg-2025-110997)

## Content

BACKGROUND: A substantial fraction of hereditary hearing loss (HL) remains unexplained by known HL genes. Tbx2 is a developmental transcription factor critical for inner ear hair cell differentiation in mice, while its pathogenic role in genetic HL in humans has yet to be documented. Here, we identified heterozygous TBX2 frameshift variants that cause human HL, establishing a previously unrecognised genetic link.

METHODS: Linkage analysis combined with whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was applied to identify the causative gene in two unrelated Chinese families with autosomal dominant progressive sensorineural HL (SNHL) accompanied by incomplete penetrance nystagmus. Functional evaluation of TBX2 variants was performed through protein expression, localisation and transcriptional activity analysis in vitro, phenotypic analysis and mechanism study in knockout and knock-in mice model in vivo.

RESULTS: Linkage analysis in Family 1 mapped SNHL to chr17q23.2 (maximum logarithm of odds=3.01), WGS identified two rare heterozygous TBX2 variants (c.977delA, p.Asp326Alafs*42 and c.987delC, p.Ala330Argfs*38) each segregating with the phenotype in a separate family. Affected individuals exhibited isolated auditory and oculomotor phenotypes, without additional syndromic features seen in previously described TBX2-associated disorders. In vitro assays demonstrated that the truncated TBX2 proteins maintained normal expression and nuclear localisation but exhibited 80% reduction in transcriptional activity. In vivo, heterozygous Tbx2 knockout mice (Tbx2+/- ) developed progressive HL and transient postnatal misexpression of outer hair cell marker in inner hair cells, supporting haploinsufficiency as the pathogenic mechanism.

CONCLUSION: These findings establish TBX2 as a novel gene for syndromic HL, defining a new autosomal dominant disorder characterised by progressive HL with variable nystagmus. This discovery expands the spectrum of T-box transcription factor disorders and informs molecular diagnosis and genetic counselling in hereditary HL.

Tbx2 is a transcription factor critical for inner hair cell specification and maintenance during murine cochlear development. However, its pathogenic role in genetic hearing loss (HL) in humans has yet to be documented.

This study identifies heterozygous TBX2 frameshift variants as the cause of a novel autosomal dominant disorder characterised by progressive HL and variably penetrant nystagmus. Functional assays demonstrate that these variants result in stable but transcriptionally impaired proteins, with mouse models supporting a haploinsufficiency mechanism.

These findings expand the phenotypic and pathogenic spectrum of T-box transcription factor disorders and highlight TBX2 as a clinically relevant gene for genetic diagnosis of hereditary HL. The study supports incorporating TBX2 into diagnostic gene panels and suggests new avenues for investigating dosage-sensitive mechanisms in inner ear development.

 Tbx2 (MIM:600747), a member of the Tbox transcription factor family, orchestrates inner hair cells (IHCs) fate specification during murine cochlear morphogenesis.13 In murine cochlear progenitors, Tbx2 is initially broadly expressed throughout the otocysts, and this expression is gradually reduced in the lateral regions of the developing organ of Corti, while remaining persistently high in the IHCs and their associated supporting cells.24 Conditional knockout of Tbx2 in these progenitors blocks IHC development and induces transdifferentiation into outer hair cells (OHCs), whereas ectopic Tbx2 expression in nascent OHCs drives IHC-like differentiation.5 Recent work has uncovered a Tbx2–Casz1–Gata3 regulatory cascade in which Tbx2 hierarchically maintains Casz1 to stabilise IHC fate.6 While Tbx2 is essential for murine cochlear development, its pathogenic role in human genetic hearing loss (HL) remains undocumented.

In humans, TBX2 is expressed across multiple tissues including the lungs, arterial system and bladder.7 Pathogenic variants in TBX2 have been implicated in diverse syndromes involving conotruncal heart defects,8 vertebral anomalies and variable endocrine and T-cell dysfunction (MIM:618223),9 as well as osteochondrodysplasia.10 11 Notably, these conditions have been reported in only three sporadic occurrences and a single autosomal dominant trio, and none have featured HL. Although disruption of the long non-coding RNA TBX2-AS1 has been associated with hearing impairment,12 no TBX2 coding variants have yet been linked to sensorineural HL (SNHL). Within the Chinese Deafness Genetics Consortium (CDGC) cohort, targeted sequencing of 201 known deafness genes failed to explain approximately 49% of cases,13 indicating undiscovered genetic causes.

In this study, we reported two unrelated autosomal dominant families from the CDGC cohort, with monoallelic frameshift variants in TBX2 characterised by progressive bilateral SNHL combined with incomplete penetrance of nystagmus. Furthermore, based on the results from in vitro and in vivo studies in heterozygous Tbx2 knockout mice, it is suggested that the mechanism by which TBX2 causes HL might be related to haploinsufficiency.

The CDGC project, launched in September 2013, which encompassed 22 125 patients from 20 665 affected families. Family 1 and Family 2 were recruited from mainland China. All participants underwent various audiological tests, such as pure-tone audiometry (PTA), acoustic impedance, auditory brainstem response (ABR), distortion product of otoacoustic emission and otolaryngological examination. PTA was employed to determine hearing threshold levels (dB HL) for air and bone conduction at multiple frequencies (250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000 and 8000 Hz). The severity of HL was categorised based on the average thresholds across specific frequencies (500, 1000, 2000 and 4000 Hz), with classification criteria across the range of normal (<20 dB), mild (20–40 dB), moderate (41–70 dB), severe (71–95 dB) and profound (>95 dB) deafness. Selected individuals from Family 1 underwent comprehensive assessments including CT scan of the temporal bone, vestibular testing, ophthalmic assessments such as full-field electroretinogram (ERG), optical coherence tomography (OCT) and slit-lamp biomicroscopy, circulatory evaluation through cardiac colour Doppler ultrasound with left ventricular function quantification, computerised multilead electrocardiography, complemented by haematological profiling and lipid panel analysis; endocrine investigation featured thyroid colour Doppler imaging coupled with functional assessments; immunological profiling comprised C reactive protein measurement, absolute T-lymphocyte subset enumeration, immunoglobulin quantification and complement component analysis; gastrointestinal evaluation integrated structural visualisation through electronic gastroscopy and colonoscopy, supplemented by Helicobacter pylori detection testing; renal-metabolic assessment incorporated urine sediment analysis, urinary calcium, urinary phosphorus and urine analysis; neurological evaluation using brain MRI. Additionally, physical examinations, including head and neck palpation and interviews with affected individuals or their parents, did not reveal additional symptoms affecting multiple organs. Body weight and height, motor and neuronal development, as well as behaviour and cognition, were within normal range.

Genomic DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood samples obtained from 33 available members of Family 1 using a MagPure Tissue & Blood DNA LQ Kit (Magen, China). Genotyping and whole-genome linkage analysis were carried out using an Illumina Infinium Asian Screening Array MD-24 (Illumina, USA; Guoke Biotechnology, China), encompassing 690 000 high-density SNP markers. Multipoint parametric linkage analysis was conducted in Merlin V.1.1.2, assuming a dominant inheritance model for the disease phenotype. SNPs with a call rate below 95% and those displaying Mendelian inconsistencies were excluded from the analysis. The logarithm of odds (LOD) was computed with a mutant allele prevalence frequency of 0.0001 and penetrance values of 0.01, 0.90 and 0.90 for wild-type (WT) homozygotes, mutant heterozygotes and mutant homozygotes, respectively.

WGS was conducted on 24 members of Family 1 (including 13 individuals with HL) and 4 members of Family 2, comprising the proband, her parents and elder sister using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) with paired-end 150 base reads. Reads were mapped along the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) with the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (V.0.7.15), variants were called using the Genomic Analysis Tool Kit, and Variant Effect Predictor was used for variant annotation.

A stepwise filtering strategy was employed to identify causative variants in patients. Variants meeting criteria of reading depth≥6, genotype quality≥20 and minor allele frequency thresholds of 0.005 in public databases were selected for further analysis. Subsequently, variants within coding and non-coding regions are predicted to affect protein function. As not all patients with HL have accompanying nystagmus, a separate analysis was conducted on Family 1 with nystagmus (IV:22, IV:23, V:20, V:21, V:22) as an independent phenotype using the aforementioned filtering strategy based on WGS analysis under both autosomal recessive and autosomal dominant patterns, respectively. Previously reported variants in known HL and nystagmus genes were identified through public databases such as Deafness Variation Database, ClinVar, OMIM (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man), HPO (Human Phenotype Ontology) and literature. A systematic literature review was conducted on all mutant genes to ascertain their potential connection with HL and nystagmus, considering the patient’s phenotype and the effect of candidate genes on inner ear function based on in vivo or in vitro experiments. To mitigate misinterpretation due to variant types or sequencing quality, the Integrative Genomics Viewer V.2.8.11 was used to manually review diagnostic variants.

DNA samples from all available family members (38 from Family 1; 10 from Family 2) underwent Sanger sequencing. The DNA sequence containing TBX2 candidate variants was amplified by PCR using the following primers: forward 5′-ACAACAACCCGTTTGCCAAG-3′; reverse 5′-CCTTCCTGGCCTTTCAGGTT-3′. The PCR products were sequenced bidirectionally using the ABI 3500xL Dx Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Variant genotypes were confirmed through sequence analysis using Chromas.

Human embryonic kidney 293 cells and HeLa cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, Virginia, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (SH30022.01B, HyClone, Logan, Utah, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (26140079, Gibco, USA). A plasmid expressing WT TBX2, N-terminally attached to Flag, was synthesised from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The TBX2 c.977delA, p.Asp326Alafs*42 and c.987delC, p.Ala330Argfs*38 were generated by site-directed mutagenesis KLD reactions (M0554, New England Biolabs, NEB), a unique of blend of Kinase, Ligase and DpnI enzymes. The cells were harvested for TBX2 expression analysis by western blot after transfecting for 48 hours. The antibodies used included anti-Flag (F1804, Sigma-Aldrich) and GAPDH (Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase, D190090, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). GAPDH was used as the protein loading control. For immunofluorescence staining, HeLa cells were transfected with TBX2 plasmids for 24 hours. The antibodies included anti-Flag (F1804, Sigma-Aldrich), Alexa Fluor 555 Phalloidin (A34055, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 secondary antibody (A48289, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescence images were captured by confocal microscopy (STELLARIS 5, Leica Microsystems). For the dual luciferase transcription reporter assay, we constructed a reporter plasmid based on pGL3-promoter (HG-VQP0124, Promega) that contained four palindromic T-box binding site sequences (AATTTCACACCTAGGTGTGAAATT), pRL-TK (E2231, Promega) was used as the internal reference reporter plasmid. Transcriptional activity assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions of Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910, Promega). Each assay was performed in technical duplicates and repeated at least three times.

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2020134A), and all experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with institutional and national animal welfare regulations. Tbx2+/− mice in the C57BL/6 background were created using CRISPR/Cas9, as described in a previous study. Two small-guide RNAs (sgRNA): 5′- TGACCCGCCGTAAGGGCCTG-3′ (sgRNA-1) and 5′-AGGCTCCGAGGCGCCGACGT-3′ (sgRNA-2) were combined with Cas9 mRNA and injected into the cytoplasm of C57BL/6J zygotes. To confirm the sequence of the mutant allele, Founder 0 (F0) mice were identified through genotyping PCR. Subsequently, accurately sequenced F0 mice were bred with C57BL/6 WT mice to produce F1 mice, which underwent genotyping PCR and were used for the functional investigations in this study. The Tbx2D326Afs*/+ knock-in model was also generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 approach with sgRNA (5′- AGGGTCGCAGGACGAGGCAT-3′). The mutant allele was confirmed through genotyping PCR and Sanger sequencing. The genotyping primers for these mouse strains are provided in online supplemental table 5.

ABR analysis was carried out at frequencies of 4 kHz, 5.6 kHz, 8 kHz, 11.3 kHz, 16 kHz, 22.6 kHz and 32 kHz in all anaesthetised mice. Mice were deeply anaesthetised via intraperitoneal injection of 480 mg/kg chloral hydrate and placed on a heating pad to maintain a body temperature of 37°C. Three subdermal needle electrodes were positioned: one at the vertex (active electrode), another at the right mastoid region (reference electrode) and the third at the left shoulder (ground electrode). ABR measurements were controlled using BioSigRZ software. The simulation involved tone-burst short pure tone signals lasting 3 ms, with a 1 ms rise and fall time, delivered at a frequency of 20/s. Recordings were conducted for 15 ms and averaged 400 times. Sound intensity ranged from 90 dB SPL (Sound Pressure Level) to 0 dB SPL, decrementing by 5 dB.

The dissection and immunofluorescent staining of inner ear sample from P7, P14, P42 and P140 were followed the previously reported protocol. Primary antibodies used included goat polyclonal anti-Prestin (sc-22692, Santa Cruz), rabbit polyclonal anti-vGlut3 (135203, Synaptic Systems) and mouse monoclonal anti-Otoferlin (ab53233, Abcam). Immunofluorescence images were acquired using Nikon C2 (Nikon, Japan), TiE-A1 plus (Nikon, Japan) or NiE-A1 plus (Nikon, Japan) confocal microscopes.

A detailed scan of three segments of the cochlear duct was performed by using a ×10 lens with confocal microscope. The Image J software was employed to draw lines between IHCs and OHCs to calculate the total length of these three segments. Following that, each cochlea was evenly divided into three equal-length portions. These portions were categorised as the basal, middle or apical turns based on respective locations. We conducted a scan of the entire cochlea using a confocal microscope with a ×60 lens to quantify the proportion of IHCs expressing Prestin (Prestin+IHCs), and total IHCs that express vGlut3 (vGlut3+IHCs). The percentage of Prestin+IHCs was calculated by normalising numbers of all Prestin+IHCs to total IHCs.

The nystagmus in mice is monitored and recorded using the Mouse Vestibular Ocular Reflex (aVOR and OVAR) Test System (GT-MVOR03, Giant(Ju'an), Shenzhen). An open field test was conducted in a 25 cm × 20 cm cage to evaluate vestibular function through behavioural metrics including circling, head bobbing, swing and exploratory behaviour. For further analysis, a 30 min video was captured at 50 frames per second during the experiment. Pixel-to-centimetre calibration was performed by measuring the cage length in pixels from the captured images and comparing it to the actual physical cage length determined using a ruler. Mouse locomotion was tracked using a customised neural network-based software, which was trained to automatically label the mouse’s head centre (A1) and body centre (A2) in each video frame. Subsequently, the mouse’s centroid position was derived by calculating the mean coordinates of A1 and A2. The movement trajectory was reconstructed by sequentially connecting the centroid positions across consecutive frames.

Data were analysed using GraphPad Prism V.8.0. Experiments were analysed using an unpaired Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA. A significance level of p<0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Family 1 includes 37 individuals across five generations with bilateral SNHL (figure 1A). Onset typically manifests during the first or second decade of life, ranging from 4 to 40 years of age (table 1). Hearing impairment was mild in early childhood and progressed gradually to moderate levels by adulthood. Audiograms displayed a characteristic sloping configuration, with high-frequency thresholds declining first, followed by involving multiple frequencies (figure 1C and online supplemental figure 1). Longitudinal audiometry over 10 years confirmed a gradual progression (figure 1B). Audiologic evaluations demonstrated normal immittance testing and equivalent air-conduction and bone-conduction thresholds, consistent with SNHL. Severely affected members reported tinnitus and vertigo (III:2, III:12, IV:14, IV:19, IV:22, IV:23, IV:31, IV:36, table 1), but temporal bone CT showed no structural inner ears or vestibular anomalies (III:7, IV:23, IV:31, online supplemental figure 2).

Nine individuals (IV:22, IV:23, IV:27, IV:31, IV:33, IV:36, V:20, V:21, V:22) developed involuntary horizontal nystagmus in early childhood (onset<6 years), often preceding HL (table 1). The nystagmus was characterised by involuntary horizontal oscillations lacking discernible slow–fast phase pattern (online supplemental video 2), differing from the typical central and peripheral forms. Fundus examination, including full-field ERG, OCT and slit-lamp biomicroscopy, showed no ocular abnormalities. Vestibular assessments, including video head impulse testing and caloric testing, revealed normal horizontal canal function and symmetric responses, excluding peripheral vestibular dysfunction (online supplemental figure 3). Cranial MRI demonstrated normal gross neuroanatomy but lacked the resolution to evaluate key vestibulo-ocular reflex pathways such as the vestibular nuclei and medial longitudinal fasciculus (online supplemental figure 4). The precise aetiological mechanisms remain uncertain.

Family 2 presented a similar clinical picture. This three-generation family has four members with progressive HL and occasional nystagmus (figure 1D). The proband (III:6) manifested severe bilateral SNHL requiring hearing aids by age 3, while her mother (II:6) developed mild, late-onset HL in her 30s, consistent with the phenotype observed in Family 1. Notably, nystagmus was observed in the mother (II:6) at age 5, preceding HL onset, similar to Family 1 (table 1). The temporal bone CT scan of the proband was also normal.

To uncover the genetic cause underlying the major phenotype of HL, we performed genome linkage analysis in Family 1 (33 individuals including 22 HL affected), which revealed a maximum LOD score of 3.01 on chromosome 17q23.2. WGS of 13 affected and 10 unaffected members identified a single rare variant in this interval, a heterozygous TBX2 frameshift variant (NM_005994.4, c.977delA, p.Asp326Alafs*42) that cosegregated with the HL phenotype, confirmed via Sanger sequencing (figure 2A–C and online supplemental table 1). Additionally, WGS performed on two patients (II:6, III:6) and two unaffected members (II:5, III:5) in Family 2 revealed a heterozygous TBX2 frameshift variant (NM_005994.4, c.987delC, p.Ala330Argfs*38) segregating with SNHL, confirmed via Sanger sequencing (figure 2C and online supplemental table 4).

To investigate whether the nystagmus could stem from another genetic cause, we performed a focused analysis of WGS data for the nystagmus-affected individuals (IV:22, IV:23, V:20, V:21, V:22). No plausible alternative variants or loci were identified (Autosomal recessive inheritance pattern in online supplemental table 2; Autosomal dominant inheritance pattern in online supplemental table 3), suggesting that the TBX2 frameshift alone is responsible for both HL and variably penetrant nystagmus in this family. Notably, aside from the auditory and oculomotor features described, thorough clinical evaluations of Family 1 found no cardiac, skeletal, immune or endocrine abnormalities, distinguishing this disorder from previously characterised TBX2-associated syndromes.

Both TBX2 variants identified in these families lie in exon 5 and introduce premature termination codons. They are predicted to produce truncated proteins that lack the C-terminal transcriptional repression domain and a portion of the activation domain, while leaving the DNA-binding T-box domain intact (figure 2D), distinct from previously reported TBX2 mutations, which cluster in the N-terminal region.911

To evaluate the functional impact of the TBX2 frameshift mutations, we performed in vitro assays. Western blotting and immunofluorescence in transfected cells showed that the mutant TBX2 proteins are expressed at normal levels and correctly localised to the nucleus, similar to the wild type (WT) (figure 3A,B). However, their transcriptional activity was severely compromised. In a dual-luciferase reporter assay, the TBX2 mutants retained only 18–21% of WT activity (p<0.001), corresponding to approximately 80% loss of function (figure 3C), and hinted that the pathogenic mechanism might primarily be TBX2 haploinsufficiency.

We next investigated TBX2 dysfunction in vivo using a mouse model. CRISPR/Cas9 was applied to generate Tbx2 knockout mice (figure 4A), and homozygous Tbx2−/− mice were embryonic lethality due to cardiac insufficiency consistent with prior studies.14 Heterozygous Tbx2+/− mice exhibited normal ABR thresholds at postnatal day 70 (P70), but by P100, they began to show HL starting at higher frequencies (11.3 and 22.6 kHz) and by P150 they exhibited significant HL across multiple frequencies compared with WT littermates (figure 4B). The progressive nature of the deficit in Tbx2+/− mice closely mirrored the human phenotype of delayed-onset, gradual SNHL. At the cellular level, we found that Prestin, a motor protein normally expressed in OHCs, is ectopically expressed in IHCs of the Tbx2+/− knockout mice. This ectopic expression is detectable during early postnatal development (P7–P14); however, it gradually diminished by P42 and disappeared by P140, with no significant alteration in the total number of IHCs throughout this period (figure 5 and online supplemental figures 5–7). Specifically, the reduced Prestin immunostaining signal from P7 to P42 reflects a decrease in Prestin protein levels within individual IHCs, rather than IHC degeneration or death. Further, quantitative tracking revealed the number of Prestin+ IHCs was more prevalent in the apical turn, less so in the basal turn and rare in the middle turn (figure 5H–K). By adulthood (P90 and P160), cochlear morphology in Tbx2+/− mice appeared normal on histology in the organ of Corti, stria vascularis and spiral ganglia (online supplemental figure 8). This transient misexpression of an OHC marker in IHCs suggests that partial loss of Tbx2 disrupts IHC identity during a critical developmental window, potentially rendering those cells more vulnerable and leading to HL later in life. Notably, Tbx2+/− mice did not display spontaneous nystagmus or vestibular dysfunction on detailed behavioural testing (including circling, head bobbing and open-field analyses of motor activity and exploration patterns) up to P160 of age (onlinesupplemental videos 2  3 and online supplemental figure 9). Given that only 25–37.5% of individuals with TBX2 variants manifest nystagmus, the absence of a mouse ocular motor phenotype likely reflects interspecies differences or the same incomplete penetrance observed in humans.

To distinguish whether the human TBX2 mutants have any dominant-negative activity (as opposed to simple haploinsufficiency), we generated a Tbx2 knock-in mouse model (Tbx2D326Afs*/+) carrying the precise c.977delA variant from Family 1 via CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing (online supplemental figure 10A). Heterozygous Tbx2D326Afs*/+ knock-in mice were viable, but no homozygous mutant pups were obtained, a phenotype consistent with embryonic lethality that parallels Tbx2−/− mice. Notably, in contrast to Tbx2+/− mice, the Tbx2D326Afs*/+ mice showed no signs of hearing impairment, the ABR thresholds of these mice at ages up to 8 months remained indistinguishable from WT littermates (online supplemental figure 10B).

In this study, we identified two frameshift variants of TBX2 in two unrelated autosomal dominant Chinese families with late-onset progressive HL and nystagmus with incomplete penetrance. In mice, carrying one allele with the frameshift mutation did not cause HL unless the entire allele was knocked out, reinforcing that TBX2-related HL is due to reduced gene dosage rather than a toxic effect of the truncated protein. In aggregate, our findings support TBX2 haploinsufficiency as the pathogenic mechanism in these families. The TBX2 variants cause a severe loss-of-function at molecular level, and the mouse models confirm that one functional Tbx2 allele is insufficient to maintain normal hearing.

The phenotype we report for TBX2 (progressive HL with/without nystagmus) is markedly milder and more tissue-specific than previously described TBX2 mutation syndromes. Earlier studies documented TBX2 missense or nonsense variants, predominantly localised to the N-terminal DNA binding domain, causing severe developmental anomalies such as congenital heart defects, craniofacial and limb abnormalities, immune dysregulation and osteochondrodysplasia.911 In contrast, the two frameshift mutations in our families truncate the distal portion of TBX2, eliminating the C-terminal transcriptional repression domain and part of the activation domain while preserving the DNA binding T-box domain. We surmise that this difference allows carriers to largely avoid major birth defects due to partial retention of TBX2 function in embryogenesis, yet it leads to a later-onset, sensory-specific disorder in the cochlea due to the reduced transcriptional activation capacity. TBX2 frameshift variants define a previously unrecognised autosomal dominant syndrome of progressive HL with incomplete penetrant nystagmus. Although these symptoms co-occur as minor features in over 300 syndromes from HPO,15 OMIM,16 Orphanet17 and DECIPHER,18 they have not previously been recognised as core manifestations of a distinct genetic disorder.

The more severe hearing impairment in proband III:6 of Family 2 may arise from prenatal antiviral drug exposure,19 potential genetic modifiers or epigenetic mechanisms. Genetic modifiers, known to regulate the expressivity of primary pathogenic mutations, are well-established in HL and can underlie phenotypic heterogeneity. For example, a MYO7A promoter variant and ATP2B2 polymorphisms have been shown to exacerbate phenotypic severity.2024 Future studies, including epigenetic profiling of this family and targeted sequencing of candidate modifier genes, will help delineate the contributions of these factors.

Similarly, incomplete penetrance of nystagmus in affected individuals also implies the involvement of genetic modifiers or epigenetic influences. One plausible mechanism is allelic imbalance-preferential expression of the WT or mutant allele, which has been implicated in other haploinsufficient disorders such as Lynch syndrome and MYBPC3-linked hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.25 26 Variability in TBX2 expression among individuals or across tissues may influence the threshold required for maintaining ocular motor circuit integrity. Additionally, epigenetic regulation and compensatory transcriptional networks in the nervus or vestibulo-ocular pathway could mitigate the functional impact of TBX2 deficiency in certain individuals.

Functionally, we show that the mutant TBX2 proteins are stable, correctly localised, but severely impaired in transcriptional activity, consistent with a hypomorphic loss-of-function allele. The absence of a dominant-negative phenotype in Tbx2D326Afs*/+ knock-in mice carrying the exact human variant supports a haploinsufficiency mechanism. Unlike Tbx2+/− mice, these knock-in animals retain normal hearing, indicating that the truncated mutant protein retains partial function and, in the presence of one WT allele, provides sufficient TBX2 dosage to maintain hearing. The essential non-redundant role of TBX2 and the strict dosage dependence are underscored by the embryonic lethality observed in both Tbx2−/− and Tbx2D326Afs*/D326Afs* homozygous knock-in mice. This suggests that the D326Afs* protein itself is not toxic but hypomorphic and that pathology results from overall insufficiency of functional TBX2 rather than dominant-negative effects. Together, these results reinforce the notion that TBX2-related HL arises from reduced gene dosage, with the precise level of functional TBX2 determining the phenotypic outcome.

In Tbx2+/− mice, the transient expression of the OHC-specific protein Prestin in IHCs observed across all cochlear turns, with a higher prevalence in the apical region during early postnatal development (P7–P14), suggests temporary fate instability in the sensory epithelium. This aberrant expression, which gradually diminishes by P42, coincides with the onset of hearing27 28 and is likely to disrupt normal cochlear maturation. Although the misexpression resolves by adulthood without apparent histological abnormalities, its occurrence during a critical developmental window may contribute to the late-onset, progressive HL observed in these mice, which becomes evident around P100. We propose that the early postnatal presence of Prestin in IHCs disrupts the functional maturation of the cochlea, although this aberrant expression subsides over time, its deleterious effects accumulate progressively, ultimately contributing to the observed progressive hearing deficits. The lack of persistent ectopic expression in adulthood indicates that sustained activation of OHC-specific genes in IHCs may require continuous transcriptional input that the mature cochlear environment fails to provide. Supporting this, Casz1, a downstream effector of Tbx2 essential for stabilising IHC identity, is a transient developmental regulator and is not maintained at significant levels in the mature cochlea.6 The lack of persistent Tbx2–Casz1 signalling in adult hair cells likely prevents prolonged misexpression of OHC markers in IHCs. As cochlear hair cells become terminally differentiated shortly after birth and lose plasticity, any Tbx2-related cell fate disruption appears limited to a critical early developmental window and is later stabilised, which is consistent with the delayed-onset, progressive HL observed in Tbx2+/− mice.

In summary, our findings identify TBX2 as a previously unrecognised deafness gene underlying an autosomal dominant disorder characterised by progressive HL and variably penetrant nystagmus. This study broadens the phenotypic spectrum of T-box transcription factor disorders and underscores the role of dosage-sensitive mechanisms in auditory pathophysiology. It also highlights the clinical significance of tissue-specific manifestations in monogenic diseases, with important implications for genetic counselling and precision medicine.
