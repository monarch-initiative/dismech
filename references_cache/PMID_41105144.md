# TUBA8 promotes neuronal dendrite development through its 40th alanine.
**Authors:** Shao F, Xue H, Chang B, Liu Y, Cao S, Li J, Zhang X, Diao L, Bao L
**Journal:** J Mol Cell Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/jmcb/mjaf036](https://doi.org/10.1093/jmcb/mjaf036)

## Content

The functional specificity of tubulin isotypes has been demonstrated by various neurological diseases caused by an increasing number of mutations in tubulin isotypes. TUBA8 is specifically localized in cerebellar Purkinje cells, which exhibit the most elaborate dendritic trees in the central nervous system. However, the role and related molecular mechanism of TUBA8 in regulating neuronal dendritic morphology remain poorly understood. Here, we report that TUBA8 is required for neuronal dendrite development. As the most divergent member in α-tubulin isotypes, the expression of TUBA8 in Purkinje cells starts at P0, plateaus at P10, and persists into adulthood. Loss of TUBA8 in Purkinje cells induces global dendritic height defects in multiple lobules during development and aging. Meanwhile, TUBA8 deficiency causes age-dependent decreased locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior. In contrast to TUBA8, TUBA4A, another tubulin isotype highly expressed in Purkinje cells, is not required for dendrite development. Furthermore, the 40th alanine, which differs with any other α-tubulin isotype and cannot be modified by acetylation, methylation, or lactylation, mediates the promoting effect of TUBA8 in neuronal dendrite development. This study reveals a specific role of TUBA8 in regulating neuronal dendritic morphology and highlights the importance of the 40th amino acid in implementing functions of α-tubulin isotypes.

The cerebellum is a well-defined brain region that plays vital roles in motor function, as well as cognition, emotion, and autonomic regulation (Klein et al., 2016; Gill and Sillitoe, 2019; Beeraka et al., 2022). The cerebellar cortex consists of three distinct laminae: the molecular layer, the Purkinje cell layer, and the granule cell layer (Sathyanesan et al., 2019). Purkinje cells are the sole output neurons that develop the most elaborate dendritic trees around postnatal 3 weeks and form thousands of synapses with parallel fibers of granule cells to establish the main cerebellar circuitry (Napper and Harvey, 1988; van der Heijden and Sillitoe, 2021). Therefore, the dendrite development and maintenance of Purkinje cells are essential for cerebellar neuronal circuitry formation and function. Moreover, the unique dendritic morphology and special developmental characteristics of Purkinje cells make them a good cell model for studying the mechanisms and principles of dendrite morphogenesis (Hasegawa et al., 2022).

Shaping the development and maintaining the function of dendritic arbor require the involvement of microtubules (Conde and Cáceres, 2009). The diverse functions of microtubules are attributed to various tubulin isotypes, a large combination of tubulin post-translational modifications (PTMs), and the interactions with a variety of microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs) (Moutin et al., 2021). Up to now, lines of evidences have indicated that PTM- and MAP-induced changes in microtubule dynamics have distinct effects on dendrite growth and branching (Yau et al., 2014; Ori-McKenney et al., 2016; Kahn et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2024), while the effect of tubulin isotypes on neuronal dendrites has been less studied (Panda et al., 1994; Diao et al., 2022). In vertebrates, various tubulin isotypes have differing abundances in tissues during development. This leads to varying ratios of functionally distinct tubulin isotypes assembling into microtubules, enabling diversification of microtubule dynamics and function both within and between cell types. Among the α-tubulin isotypes, TUBA8 was the latest to be described in 2000 (Stanchi et al., 2000; Diggle et al., 2017), and very little is known about its function in the central nervous system. A previous study using in situ hybridization with an optimized Tuba8 RNA probe showed that a very low level of Tuba8 was expressed in the developing cerebral cortex and specifically localized in cerebellar Purkinje cells (Braun et al., 2010). However, the spatio-temporal expression and function of TUBA8 in the dendrites of Purkinje cells remain unclear.

In this study, by re-analyzing the previously published RNA sequencing data of Purkinje cells (Clifford et al., 2019), we found that Tuba8 and Tuba4a were most dramatically increased among the α-tubulin isotypes during dendrite development, which was confirmed at the protein level. With Pcp2-driven conditional knockout (cKO) of TUBA8 and TUBA4A in Purkinje cells, we found that loss of TUBA8 resulted in developmental defects in dendritic morphology and function, while loss of TUBA4A did not affect dendrite development. Furthermore, by using the model of cultured hippocampal neurons and in utero electroporation (IUE) of Purkinje cells, we found that the role of TUBA8 in promoting dendrite development was mainly controlled by its 40th alanine. This study complements the effect of tubulin isotypes on neuronal dendrite growth and branching and provides a new molecular mechanism for the development of the dendrites in Purkinje cells.

To investigate the major α-tubulin isotypes expressed in cerebellar Purkinje cells, we re-analyzed the previously published RNA sequencing data obtained from the developing Purkinje cells by laser capture microdissection (Clifford et al., 2019). The data analysis showed that the most abundant α-tubulin isotypes were Tuba1a, Tuba1b, Tuba4a, and Tuba8 (Figure 1A). In the developmental process of Purkinje cells, the main progressively increased α-tubulin isotypes were Tuba8 and Tuba4a, the top two isotypes exhibiting the highest amino acid sequence divergence of α-tubulin (Figure 1A; Supplementary Figure S1A and B). The expression proportion of these two isotypes in total α-tubulin pool was increased from nearly undetectable at postnatal day 0 (P0) to ∼30% at P21, respectively (Figure 1A). During the dendrite development of Purkinje cells, Tuba1a exhibited a progressively decreased expression (Figure 1A), implying its minimal contribution to dendritic morphogenesis. Meanwhile, Tuba1b showed a modestly increased expression (Figure 1A), indicating its restricted function from this limited upregulation. Thus, TUBA8 and TUBA4A were further investigated in Purkinje cells during dendrite development.

Due to the lack of a specific commercial antibody for TUBA8, we generated the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) knockin mice (EGFP-TUBA8 mice) by combining the androgenetic haploid embryonic stem cell technique with the CRISPR/Cas9 system to label endogenous TUBA8 at the N-terminus (Figure 1B and C; Zhong et al., 2016). Immunoblotting with GFP antibody showed that TUBA8 was expressed in a very restricted set of tissues, including the cerebellum, olfactory bulb, heart, and stomach (Figure 1D), at the age of 1 month (1 M), which was generally consistent with previous studies on the distribution of Tuba8 (Stanchi et al., 2000; Braun et al., 2010). Further immunostaining on whole brain slice of adult EGFP-TUBA8 mice demonstrated that TUBA8 was significantly enriched in Purkinje cells of the cerebellum and sporadically expressed in other brain regions, including the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and olfactory bulb (Figure 1E). In Purkinje cells, TUBA8 was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm and dendritic tree and also present in the axon (Supplementary Figure S1C). Co-labeling of EGFP-indicated TUBA8-positive cells with the Purkinje cell marker calbindin showed that the expression of TUBA8 started at P0 and spread over all Purkinje cells at P10 (Figure 1F; Supplementary Figure S1D), coinciding with their dendrite development. Corresponding immunoblotting also confirmed that TUBA8 appeared at early postnatal stages, such as P7, and continued to be expressed into adulthood (Supplementary Figure S1E).

In contrast, there is a commercially available antibody for TUBA4A with high specificity (Supplementary Figure S1F). Using this antibody, we demonstrated that TUBA4A was expressed in all tissues except the testis and particularly abundant in the nervous system (Figure 1G). Whole brain staining with the TUBA4A antibody showed that TUBA4A was widely expressed throughout the brain, such as the olfactory bulb, cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum (Figure 1H). Co-labeling of TUBA4A-positive cells with calbindin showed that TUBA4A was highly expressed in cerebellar nuclei but barely expressed in Purkinje cells at P0 (Figure 1I). As development progressed, TUBA4A expression in Purkinje cells increased and was clearly co-labeled with calbindin at P10 (Figure 1I; Supplementary Figure S1D). Collectively, these results suggest that TUBA8 and TUBA4A are almost synchronously and robustly expressed in postnatal cerebellar Purkinje cells.

To investigate the potential functions of TUBA8 in postnatal development of Purkinje cells, we constructed Pcp2-Cre/Tuba8flox/flox mice (Pcp2-Cre/Tuba8fl/fl mice, also named as TUBA8 cKO mice) by crossing Tuba8fl/fl mice with Pcp2-Cre mice, in which exon 2 of Tuba8 was specifically deleted in the Pcp2 promoter-driven Purkinje cells from P6 onward (Supplementary Figure S2A). To verify the knockout effect of TUBA8 in Purkinje cells, we generated a homemade polyclonal TUBA8 antibody targeting the amino acids 35–45 (TFGTQASKIND) of murine TUBA8 (Diggle et al., 2017), which is distinct from other α-tubulin isotypes (Supplementary Figure S1B). The homemade TUBA8 antibody was able to specifically recognize overexpressed TUBA8 but not other α-tubulin isotypes (Supplementary Figure S1F) and could be blocked by the antigen peptides (Supplementary Figure S2B and C), indicating its good specificity without cross-reactivity with other α-tubulin isotypes. Immunostaining of the cerebellum from wild-type (WT) and EGFP-TUBA8 mice showed that the TUBA8 antibody could specifically recognize the endogenous TUBA8 in Purkinje cells, and the EGFP tag did not affect the expression level of endogenous TUBA8 (Supplementary Figure S2D). Immunostaining of cerebellar vermis slices from the control and TUBA8 cKO mice showed that the expression of TUBA8 was eliminated from P7 to adulthood in TUBA8 cKO mice (Supplementary Figure S2E), which further validated that the signals detected in Purkinje cells using the homemade TUBA8 antibody were indeed specific to TUBA8. Immunoblotting of adult cerebellum tissue extracts also confirmed that TUBA8 was completely ablated in homozygous TUBA8 cKO mice (Supplementary Figure S2F). The TUBA8 cKO mice, from birth to adulthood, were virtually indistinguishable from age-matched Tuba8fl/fl mice in body weight, cerebellar weight, and area (Supplementary Figure S2G–J). We then focused on Purkinje cells themselves. Immunostaining and quantitative data showed that the height of Purkinje cells, including the cell bodies in the Purkinje cell layer and the dendrites in the molecular layer, had a significant reduction in multiple lobules at P7, P10, and P21 in TUBA8 cKO mice (Figure 2A–D), whereas the number of Purkinje cells in each lobule was not affected (Supplementary Figure S2K and L), indicating that loss of TUBA8 only impairs the dendrite development of Purkinje cells. Specifically, 4 and 6 lobules were affected at P7 and P10, respectively (Figure 2B and C), while 2 lobules were reduced and 1 lobule was increased at P21 (Figure 2D). At the age of 1 M, only 2 lobules of Purkinje cells were significantly higher in TUBA8 cKO mice than in the control mice (Figure 2E), implying that effective compensation occurs at 1 M. These data indicate that TUBA8 deficiency impairs the dendrite development of Purkinje cells, especially at early stages.

To explore the morphological changes in individual Purkinje cells in vivo, we used the transient IUE assay to sparsely label Purkinje cells (Nishiyama et al., 2012; Takeo et al., 2015, 2021). We electroporated a plasmid encoding yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) into Tuba8fl/fl and TUBA8 cKO Purkinje cells at embryonic day 12.5 (E12.5) and measured the dendritic morphology of Purkinje cells in lobule IV/V at P10. Immunostaining and quantitative data showed that loss of TUBA8 reduced the maximal height of the dendritic tree expansion in Purkinje cells without affecting their maximal width (Figure 2F and G), accompanied by a reduction in the total length of the dendrites (Figure 2H). Furthermore, Sholl analysis showed that the number of intersections between distal dendrites and concentric circles of Purkinje cells in TUBA8 cKO mice dramatically decreased (Figure 2I; Supplementary Figure S2M), indicating that loss of TUBA8 reduces the morphological complexity of dendritic tree. Collectively, these data support that TUBA8 promotes the dendrite development of Purkinje cells.

The dendrite of Purkinje cells in the vertebrate brain matures into a highly elaborate fan-shaped tree at P21, after which it remains largely unchanged. To explore whether loss of TUBA8 affects the dendrite maintenance in Purkinje cells, we measured and analyzed the dendritic morphology of Purkinje cells in adult TUBA8 cKO mice and age-matched control mice. Immunostaining and quantitative data showed that the number of lobules with reduced dendritic height in TUBA8 cKO mice progressively increased from 2 M to 19 M compared to that in age-matched Tuba8fl/fl mice (Figure 3A–E), indicating that loss of TUBA8 affects dendrite maintenance of Purkinje cells during aging. Furthermore, we performed Golgi staining to sparsely label Purkinje cells in 13 M mice to detect the morphology of individual Purkinje cells. Golgi staining showed that Purkinje cells from TUBA8 cKO mice had the reduced maximal height of the dendritic tree expansion (Figure 3F and G), total dendritic length (Figure 3H), and dendritic arborization (Figure 3I), as well as a longer distance between the soma and the first dendritic branch point (Figure 3J). Meanwhile, the width of the dendritic tree (Figure 3F and G) and the density of distal dendritic spines (Supplementary Figure S2N and O) were similar in Purkinje cells from TUBA8 cKO and Tuba8fl/fl mice. These data suggest that TUBA8 is required for dendrite maintenance of Purkinje cells.

Given the important function of TUBA8 in regulating dendritic morphology of Purkinje cells, we wondered whether loss of TUBA8 in Purkinje cells impairs mouse behavior. The open field test is a widely established paradigm for assessing locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior (Seibenhener and Wooten, 2015; Kraeuter et al., 2019). With increasing age, the distance traveled in the open field by TUBA8 cKO mice was significantly reduced compared to that by age-matched Tuba8fl/fl mice (Figure 3K and L), indicating the impaired locomotor activity of aged TUBA8 cKO mice. Meanwhile, the time in the center zone by 12 M and 18 M TUBA8 cKO mice was significantly reduced compared to that by Tuba8fl/fl mice (Figure 3M), implying that aged TUBA8 cKO mice display anxiety-like behavior. The rotarod test is widely used to assess motor performance (Shiotsuki et al., 2010; Brooks et al., 2012), such as motor balance, motor coordination, and motor learning. The time of TUBA8 cKO mice spent in the rotarod test was not significantly changed compared to that of the control mice (Figure 3N and O), implying that their ability of motor balance and coordination is unaffected. Taken together, these data suggest that loss of TUBA8 in Purkinje cells impairs the locomotor activity and causes anxiety-like behavior in aged mice.

Given that the dendritic height of Purkinje cells in TUBA8 cKO mice was not affected much at 1 M and 2 M (Figures 2E and 3A), we further explored whether these phenomena were compensated by other α-tubulin isotypes, such as TUBA4A. Immunoblotting showed that the expression of TUBA4A in TUBA8 cKO mice at 1 M was higher than that in Tuba8fl/fl mice, without altering the expression of total α-tubulin (Supplementary Figure S3A and B), providing a possible evidence for the compensation of TUBA4A expression after TUBA8 knockout. Since the expression pattern of TUBA4A in Purkinje cells during development was similar to that of TUBA8, we next investigated the function of TUBA4A in the dendrite development of Purkinje cells. We also generated TUBA4A cKO mice (Supplementary Figure S3C) that had similar body weight and cerebellar weight as their age-matched control mice (Supplementary Figure S3D and E). Immunostaining demonstrated that TUBA4A was completely knocked out in Purkinje cells, while the expression of TUBA4A was not only in Purkinje cells but also in other cell types of the cerebellar cortex, such as Bergmann glial cells (Supplementary Figure S3F). Meanwhile, we found that the height of Purkinje cells in each lobule of TUBA4A cKO mice was similar to that of Tuba4afl/fl mice, except for a slight increase in lobule VI at P10 and P21 (Figure 4A–D). Interestingly, loss of TUBA4A led to a compensatory increase in TUBA8 expression, while the expression level of total tubulin was not changed (Supplementary Figure S3G and H), implying that the slight promotion of dendrite development of Purkinje cells in TUBA4A cKO mice may be caused by an increase in TUBA8 expression. Collectively, TUBA4A is not required for dendrite development of Purkinje cells, and it does not account for the compensatory dendrite defects in 1 M TUBA8 CKO mice.

We then generated TUBA8 and TUBA4A double cKO mice (Pcp2-Cre/Tuba8fl/fl/Tuba4afl/fl mice, also named as DcKO mice), in which both Tuba8 and Tuba4a were specifically deleted in Purkinje cells (Supplementary Figure S4A). Immunostaining showed that TUBA4A was completely knocked out, while there was 35%–45% residual TUBA8 in the cell body of Purkinje cells at P10–1 M and <10% at 9 M and 13 M (Supplementary Figure S4B and C). Furthermore, immunostaining and quantitative data showed that the dendritic height of Purkinje cells in multiple lobules of DcKO mice at P10 and P21 was significantly reduced compared to that of Tuba4afl/fl/Tuba8fl/fl mice (Figure 4E–G), while the dendritic height of Purkinje cells in lobules of 1 M DcKO mice was similar to that of Tuba4afl/fl/Tuba8fl/fl mice (Figure 4H). Due to the residual TUBA8, the dendritic height defect of DcKO mice was slightly alleviated compared to that of TUBA8 cKO mice, further supporting that TUBA4A is not required for the dendrite development of Purkinje cells. However, DcKO mice exhibited earlier and more severe dendritic height defect compared with age-matched TUBA8 cKO mice at 9 M and 13 M (Figure 4I and J; Supplementary Figure S4D). It is possible that due to insufficient expression of Cre in the early stage of DcKO mice, TUBA8 was not completely knocked out in the developing Purkinje cells, while persistent Cre expression led to almost complete TUBA8 knockout of DcKO mice at 9 M (Supplementary Figure S4C, E, and F). Meanwhile, DcKO mice exhibited earlier onset of reduced locomotor activity at 8 M in the open field test compared with age-matched TUBA8 cKO mice (Figure 4K). More severe defects of dendrites and behavior in aged DcKO mice imply other complicated mechanisms that need to be further investigated. Altogether, these results further support that TUBA8 promotes the dendrite development and maintenance of Purkinje cells.

Since Purkinje cells are difficult to be enriched, cultured, and transfected, we then used hippocampal neurons as a cell model to study the underlying mechanism of TUBA8 in promoting dendrite development. First, we constructed CAG promoter plasmids with nucleotides of hemagglutinin (HA) peptides (YPYDVPDYA) at the N-terminus of the four tubulin isotypes (Tuba1a, Tuba1b, Tuba4a, and Tuba8) that were expressed in Purkinje cells. All of the overexpressed tubulin isotypes could be well incorporated into microtubule network (Supplementary Figure S5). We then transiently expressed YFP, HA-TUBA1A, HA-TUBA1B, HA-TUBA4A, or HA-TUBA8 into hippocampal neurons and cultured them for 5 days to examine the effect of increasing the proportion of a particular α-tubulin isotype on dendrite development. Immunostaining and quantitative data showed that elevating the amount of TUBA8 but not YFP or other tubulin isotypes was able to increase the total dendritic branch length (TDBL) and the total dendritic branch tip number (TDBTN) in cultured hippocampal neurons (Figure 5A and B). Thus, the function of TUBA8 in promoting dendrite development is not unique in Purkinje cells but also in hippocampal neurons.

In order to explore the molecular basis in regulating dendrite development, we analyzed the amino acid sequence of TUBA8 and found two regions with distinct sequences from other α-tubulin isotypes, amino acids 32–45 and the C-terminal tail (Supplementary Figure S1B). In particular, the 40th alanine (A40) and 449th phenylalanine (F449) in TUBA8 were unique. Previous studies have shown that α-tubulin acetylation at lysine 40 (K40) is involved in axon development and the C-terminus of α-tubulin detyrosination cycle affects the dynamics of microtubules (Akella et al., 2010; Wei et al., 2018; Ziak et al., 2024). We then generated two chimeric TUBA8 constructs with the amino acids 32–45 or C-terminal tail of TUBA4A (TUBA8TUBA4A(32–45) and TUBA8TUBA4A(437–449)) and two TUBA8 point mutations (TUBA8A40K and TUBA8F449Y) (Supplementary Figure S5) and expressed them in hippocampal neurons. We found that the TDBL and the TDBTN in neurons expressing TUBA8TUBA4A(32–45) and TUBA8A40K for 5 days were significantly reduced compared to that in neurons expressing TUBA8 (Figure 5C and D). Meanwhile, the neurons expressing TUBA8A40K exhibited more significant reduction in TDBTN compared the neurons expressing TUBA8TUBA4A(32–45) (Figure 5D). These findings imply that the 40th alanine plays a critical role in promoting dendritic arborization and the microenvironment formed by the amino acids flanking the 40th residue in TUBA8 exerts a slight negative effect on dendritic branching. Furthermore, the TDBL of neurons expressing TUBA8F449Y and TUBA8TUBA4A(437–449) was larger than that of neurons expressing YFP but smaller than that of neurons expressing TUBA8, and the TDBTN of neurons expressing TUBA8F449Y and TUBA8TUBA4A(437–449) was similar to that of neurons expressing TUBA8 (Figure 5D), indicating that the C-terminal tail of TUBA8 slightly contributes to the effect of TUBA8 on dendrite development. Collectively, these data suggest that TUBA8 promotes neuronal dendrite development mainly through the 40th alanine.

To validate the impact of the 40th alanine in TUBA8 on the dendrite development of Purkinje cells in vivo, we employed IUE assay to overexpress HA-TUBA8 or HA-TUBA8A40K in E12.5 Purkinje cells of TUBA8 cKO mice. Immunostaining and quantitative data showed that overexpression of HA-TUBA8 rather than HA-TUBA8A40K in TUBA8-deficient Purkinje cells rescued the defects in dendritic tree expansion and total length (Figure 5E and F) and partially restored the deficits in dendritic arborization at P10 (Figure 5G), implying that TUBA8 promotes dendrite development in Purkinje cells mainly through the 40th alanine. Additionally, we observed that reintroducing TUBA8A40K into TUBA8-deficient Purkinje cells exacerbated dendrite defects, similar to that observed in hippocampal neurons, further indicating that the amino acids flanking the 40th residue of TUBA8 negatively affect dendrite development. Taken together, these results reveal a critical role of the 40th alanine in TUBA8 in dendrite development.

Spatio-temporal distribution and modulation of tubulin isotypes is essential for regulating the dynamics of microtubules and the function of the nervous system. Here, we report that the tubulin isotypes TUBA8 and TUBA4A are distributed and enriched in Purkinje cells and both of their expression increases with the development of Purkinje cells. TUBA8, but not TUBA4A, is essential for the dendrite development of Purkinje cells in neonatal mice. Furthermore, the promoting effect of TUBA8 on dendrite development is not unique in Purkinje cells but also in hippocampal neurons, which is mainly mediated by its 40th alanine. These findings reveal the function and mechanism of TUBA8 in the development of Purkinje cells, which lays a foundation for the role of specific tubulin isotypes.

In vertebrates, more than seven genes encode the α-tubulin isotypes, which are highly conserved but still differ in amino acid sequence and vary widely in expression, distribution, and function (Breuss and Keays, 2014; Diao et al., 2022). Previous studies showed that Tuba8 was abundantly expressed in the testis, heart, and skeletal muscle, with lower levels in the developing cerebral cortex, but specifically localized to the cerebellar Purkinje cells (Stanchi et al., 2000; Braun et al., 2010; Diggle et al., 2017). In this study, using the EGFP-labeled endogenous TUBA8 knockin mice, we found that TUBA8 was mainly expressed in the cerebellum and heart at comparable levels and also observed in the olfactory bulb, cerebral cortex, and hippocampus at 1 M. Interestingly, TUBA8 is indeed specifically enriched in Purkinje cells, with the expression beginning at P0, plateauing at P10, and sustained into adulthood.

A previous study reported that TUBA8 global knockout mice appeared similar to WT mice, without significant abnormal defects observed (Diggle et al., 2017). In this study, we generated TUBA8 cKO mice to eliminate the expression of TUBA8 in Purkinje cells from P6. As a consequence of acute TUBA8 deficiency, the developmental defects became increasingly apparent from P7 to P10. Afterwards, the Purkinje cells exhibited robust compensatory capacity, possibly by the increased expression of other tubulin isotypes except for TUBA4A or the upregulation of microtubule function resulted from unknown mechanism, so that the effects of TUBA8 deficiency were gradually restored from P10 to 1 M. However, aging drives a decline in cellular compensatory capacity, reducing the ability to compensate for deficits. The loss of redundancy ultimately unmasks the essential physiological role of TUBA8 in promoting dendritic complexity, which leads to dendrite defects in aging mice. Notably, although the presence of TUBA8 promotes dendrite development, its functional loss in early developmental stage can be compensated by various alternative mechanisms, suggesting that differences in tubulin isotypes seem to be dispensable in dendrite development. Nevertheless, once the cellular compensatory capacity is impaired by aging, these isotype differences prove biologically indispensable. Collectively, the present study highly supports the physiological role of TUBA8 in dendrite development and maintenance of Purkinje cells.

TUBA8 was first identified in 2000 and has the most divergent sequence among all α-tubulin isotypes (Stanchi et al., 2000), in particular amino acids 32–45 and the C-terminal tail. A recent study showed that Tuba8 was expressed transiently in cortical progenitors at E11.5 and drove the differentiation of radial glial progenitors into apical intermediate progenitors through its C-terminal tail phenylalanine (Ramos et al., 2020). In the present study, overexpression of TUBA8 and various TUBA8 mutants into cultured hippocampal neurons provides the evidence that the 40th alanine of TUBA8, but not its C-terminal tail phenylalanine, is essential for promoting dendrite development.

Except for TUBA8, lysine 40 of α-tubulin can be modified by acetylation, methylation, or lactylation, which is involved in the growth and branching of neuronal axons and dendrites (Akella et al., 2010; Park et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2024). The tubulin with an alanine mutation at the 40th position is not able to be modified by acetylation, methylation, or lactylation (Kearns et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2024). Previous studies showed that decreasing the methylation level of α-tubulin lysine 40 inhibited the branching of neuronal dendrites (Koenning et al., 2021), increasing the expression level of α-tubulin lactylation promoted the total number of dendrites and axons (Sun et al., 2024), and decreasing the expression level of α-tubulin acetylation facilitated both dendrite and axon growth (Jenkins et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2018; Koenning et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2024), implying that these modifications on lysine 40 of α-tubulin have distinct roles in regulating neuronal morphology. How does the 40th alanine of TUBA8 affect neuronal development in vertebrates? In this study, the A40K mutant of TUBA8 eliminated the promoting effect of TUBA8 on the total dendritic length and branching in cultured hippocampal neurons. Meanwhile, loss of TUBA8 caused a decrease in the lobule height and complexity of dendritic tree in Purkinje cells, and overexpression of HA-TUBA8 rather than HA-TUBA8A40K in TUBA8-deficient Purkinje cells rescued dendrite defects. These results all indicate that the 40th alanine of TUBA8 promotes dendrite development. This impact may stem from the intrinsic alanine structure or/and the absence of PTMs. Additionally, emerging studies have shown that some tubulin isotypes have distinct influences on microtubule properties, such as dynamics and structure (Vemu et al., 2016; Ti et al., 2018; Diao et al., 2022, 2023a, b). Whether TUBA8 with the 40th alanine possesses the unique role in regulating microtubule property and thus neuronal dendrite development will be interesting and meaningful topics for further study.
