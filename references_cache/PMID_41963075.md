INTRODUCTION: The onset of sarcoidosis is likely the result of a complex
genetic-environment-immunological interaction. This systematic review and
meta-analysis aimed to identify occupational toxic particles associated with an
increased risk of developing pulmonary sarcoidosis.
METHODS: Publications in English, published from January 2000 to January 2025,
were searched in PubMed, Scopus and JSTOR. The risk of bias was assessed for all
included studies. Sensitivity analyses stratified by study quality were
conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings and potential bias. To
assess publication bias, a funnel plot was used when more than six studies were
included in the analysis.
RESULTS: Five categories of toxic particles were identified to be associated
with increased risk of the onset of sarcoidosis: chemicals, inorganic dusts,
metals, mixed dusts and fumes and organic dusts. The quantitative analysis
includes data from 13 studies. The results suggested that occupational silica,
pesticides, mould/mildew and World Trade Center ((WTC) dust exposures were
associated with increased odds of pulmonary sarcoidosis. Sensitivity analysis
confirmed the robustness of the association for WTC dust and mould, whereas the
risk associated with silica appeared attenuated in high-quality studies.
However, gold exposure was identified as a protective factor.
CONCLUSION: Pulmonary sarcoidosis is associated with occupational silica,
pesticides, WTC dust and mould. Future research should prioritise
gene-environment interactions and granuloma mineralogy to refine preventive
strategies and disease management.

ABSTRACT
Introduction The onset of sarcoidosis is likely the
result of a complex genetic- environment- immunological
interaction. This systematic review and meta- analysis
aimed to identify occupational toxic particles associated
with an increased risk of developing pulmonary
sarcoidosis.
Methods Publications in English, published from January
2000 to January 2025, were searched in PubMed, Scopus
and JSTOR. The risk of bias was assessed for all included
studies. Sensitivity analyses stratified by study quality
were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings
and potential bias. To assess publication bias, a funnel plot
was used when more than six studies were included in the
analysis.
Results Five categories of toxic particles were identified
to be associated with increased risk of the onset of
sarcoidosis: chemicals, inorganic dusts, metals, mixed
dusts and fumes and organic dusts. The quantitative
analysis includes data from 13 studies. The results
suggested that occupational silica, pesticides, mould/
mildew and World Trade Center ((WTC) dust exposures
were associated with increased odds of pulmonary
sarcoidosis. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness
of the association for WTC dust and mould, whereas the
risk associated with silica appeared attenuated in high-
quality studies. However, gold exposure was identified as a
protective factor.
Conclusion Pulmonary sarcoidosis is associated with
occupational silica, pesticides, WTC dust and mould. Future
research should prioritise gene- environment interactions
and granuloma mineralogy to refine preventive strategies
and disease management.
INTRODUCTION
Sarcoidosis is still regarded as a disease
of unknown aetiology. 1 2  Over the past 20
years, this paradigm has gradually shifted,
with more rapid progress during the last
decade. It is a multisystem chronic inflam-
matory disease that affects multiple organs in
the body, including lymph nodes, eyes, skin
and, most commonly, lungs. 3 The American
Thoracic Society has established three criteria
for diagnosing sarcoidosis: (1) characteristic
clinical and radiological presentation; (2)
evidence of non- caseating granulomas in one
or more tissue samples; and (3) exclusion of
alternative causes of granulomatous disease. 1
WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
 ⇒ The aetiology of pulmonary sarcoidosis remains
largely unknown, but the prevailing hypothesis sug-
gests a complex interplay between genetic suscep-
tibility and environmental triggers.
 ⇒ A plethora of occupational exposures has been sus-
pected of being risk factors. Nevertheless, the evi-
dence from individual studies has been fragmented,
inconsistent and often lacks quantitative synthesis.
WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
 ⇒ This systematic review and meta- analysis synthe-
sises data from 2000 to 2025, confirming significant
associations between pulmonary sarcoidosis and
exposure to silica, pesticides, mould/mildew and
World Trade Center (WTC) dust.
 ⇒ It provides a nuanced risk assessment, revealing
that while WTC dust and mould associations are
robust, the risk attributed to silica may be overesti-
mated in lower- quality studies.
 ⇒ The analysis identifies a novel and potentially pro-
tective association with gold exposure, contrasting
with the risk posed by other inorganic particles.
HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH,
PRACTICE OR POLICY
 ⇒ Clinicians should incorporate a targeted occupa-
tional anamnesis focusing on pesticides, silica and
organic dusts for all patients presenting with sar -
coidosis to aid in potential aetiology identification.
 ⇒ These findings reinforce the need for stricter re-
spiratory protective equipment and exposure limits
in the agricultural, construction and remediation
sectors.
 ⇒ Future research must pivot from general association
studies to investigating gene- environment interac-
tions and the mineralogical analysis of granulomas
to define causal mechanisms.

Worldwide prevalence varies significantly across different
regions, reflecting diverse epidemiological factors. It was
2–80 per 100 000 people, and incidence is 1.0–35.3 per
100 000 people per year.4
As the lungs, skin and eyes directly contact the envi-
ronment, external factors crucially influence sarcoid-
osis phenotype. 5 While specific exposure links remain
debated,6 the disease likely results from gene- environment
interactions7 driving a TH1- predominant immunopa-
thology with potential TH2/TH17 shifts.8 Under normal
circumstances, antigen clearance leads to self- regulation
and resolution of the immune response. 9 While genetic-
environmental interaction is crucial in understanding
sarcoidosis, some researchers argue that the role of
specific pathogens or environmental exposures may be
overstated, suggesting that further research is needed
to clarify these relationships. 8 10  Online supplemental
Figure S1 illustrates the wide range of factors that have
been proposed to influence the aetiology of sarcoidosis.
Recent epidemiological studies have provided evidence
suggesting causal associations between occupational
exposure to specific agents and sarcoidosis. 2 Pathogen-
esis has been more clearly defined, including the role of
gene- exposure interactions. Certain occupations such
as firefighters 11 and military service 12 have been associ-
ated with an increased risk of sarcoidosis. Occupational
exposure to toxic particles like silica, 13 metal dust 14 and
pesticides,15 among others, has been associated with an
increased risk of developing granulomatous lung disease.
Blanc et al reported that the proportion of sarcoidosis
attributable to occupational exposure ranged from 0%
to 54%, with an overall attributable fraction of 30% (95%
CI 17% to 45%).16
Evidence linking occupational exposure to sarcoidosis
remains inconsistent. While Huntley et al recently iden-
tified risks regarding silica, pesticides and mould, 7 this
study extends those findings through updated literature
and more granular exposure classification. We aimed to
identify specific toxic particles associated with pulmo-
nary sarcoidosis risk through a systematic review and
meta- analysis.
METHODS
This study was designed and reported in accordance with
the guidelines of Preferred Reporting Items for System-
atic reviews and Meta- Analyses (PRISMA)17 and has been
registered in the International Prospective Register of
Systematic Reviews PROSPERO databases under the
identification number: CRD420251030872.
Information sources and search strategy
A comprehensive literature search in English was
performed from January 2000 to January 2025 was
conducted in these databases: PubMed, Cochrane
Library, Scopus and JSTOR. Additional studies were also
identified using the references of the reviewed publi-
cations. We included studies published from January
2000 onwards to improve methodological consistency,
reflecting more standardised sarcoidosis diagnostic
criteria and advances in occupational exposure assess-
ment. Older studies often used less precise exposure clas-
sification or outdated diagnostic definitions, which could
increase heterogeneity and reduce comparability.
The search strategy was built using Medical Subject
Headings terms and keywords related to pulmonary
sarcoidosis and occupational exposure.
The following bibliographic query was used: (“Sarcoid-
osis” OR “Sarcoid- like” OR “Pulmonary sarcoidosis”)
AND (“Occupational exposure” OR “Workplace expo-
sure” OR “Occupational hazard” OR “Work related expo-
sure” OR “Occupational disease”). Keywords relevant to
the study topic were searched in the “All Fields” category
in each database to ensure comprehensive retrieval.
The retrieval procedure was conducted by two inde-
pendent researchers.
Eligibility criteria
Only cohort and case–control studies were included to
ensure methodological robustness and appropriate assess-
ment of exposure- outcome relationships. Cross- sectional
studies and case series were excluded due to their limi-
tations in establishing temporality. Online supplemental
Table S1 summarises the inclusion and exclusion criteria
according to the PICOS framework (ie, populations/
participants, interventions and comparators, outcomes
of interest and study designs/types).
Exclusion criteria included experimental studies,
case reports, reviews, cross- sectional studies and ecolog-
ical studies, studies using non- standardised diagnostic
methods or having a high risk of assessment inaccuracies,
and studies with inadequate data for meta- analysis, such
as insufficient information on relative risk and CIs.
Data collection
Data were extracted independently by two authors (AK
and NK) with any discrepancies resolved through consul-
tation and discussion with authors FD and WF.
Studies were categorised based on the reported occu-
pational exposure to toxic particles and study design. If a
study included multiple exposures, all were considered.
Demographic information, including gender, age and
ethnicity, as well as the diagnostic criteria for sarcoidosis,
was collected. The OR for specific occupational toxic
exposures was extracted or calculated using available
data for case–control and cohort studies.
The following criteria were applied for data extraction:
 ► Preference for fully adjusted risk estimates: The most
thoroughly adjusted risk estimates in each article
were preferred over less adjusted or crude estimates.
 ► When the hierarchy of exposure variables is used, the
risk rate associated with the highest level of exposure
is selected for analysis.

Quality analysis
The quality of case–control and cohort studies was
assessed using the Newcastle- Ottawa Scale. Studies with
a score of more than seven were classified as high- quality
research, those with a score of between four and six were
classified as medium- quality research, and studies with a
score of three or less were classified as low- quality research
(online supplemental Tables S2 and S3). To assess the
robustness of our findings and evaluate the influence of
study quality on the pooled risk estimates, we performed
sensitivity analyses stratifying the studies by quality level
(high- quality vs medium- quality).
Statistical analysis
The compiled data classifies all studies according to
reported occupational exposures. A meta- analysis was
performed when two or more studies reported an OR
for a specific occupational exposure. Data included
the frequency of pulmonary sarcoidosis cases linked to
occupational exposure to toxic particles, as well as the
prevalence of occupational exposures in pulmonary
sarcoidosis cases. In addition, studies using mineralogical
analysis techniques on sarcoidosis are also described.
The meta- analysis was carried out using RevMan V.5.4.1
software. A 95% CI and relative risk were chosen as the
statistics to assess effect. The significance level was set at
0.05. Inter- study heterogeneity was estimated using the
I² test. When I² ≤50%, this was interpreted as a sign of
homogeneity, and a fixed- effects model was used for anal-
ysis. When I² ≥50%, heterogeneity was considered signifi-
cant, and a random effects model was used.
Subgroup analyses were performed to explore poten-
tial sources of heterogeneity. In addition, a sensitivity
analysis was performed using the ‘leave- one- out’ method,
whereby each study was individually excluded in order
to assess the impact of each study on the results. This
procedure was used to test the robustness of the results
by comparing the effects before and after the elimination
of a study.
To assess publication bias, a funnel plot was used when
more than six studies were included in the analysis.
RESULTS
A total of 21 studies meeting the research criteria were
selected. Among them, 13 studies were included in the
quantitative analysis (online supplemental Figure S2).
Qualitative studies
The compendium of occupational exposure to toxic
particles associated with pulmonary sarcoidosis and
the summary of each individual study included in the
compendium are presented in online supplemental
tables S4 and S5.
Five categories of occupational toxic particles were
identified to be associated with pulmonary sarcoid-
osis: chemical agents (5 studies), inorganic dusts (10
studies), metals (10 studies), mixed dust/fumes (6
studies) and organic dusts (5 studies).
Research most often focused on the following toxic
particles: silica (8 studies), aluminium (5 studies), chro-
mium (5 studies), cobalt (3 studies), gold (3 studies),
nickel (3 studies), pesticides (4 studies), World Trade
Center (WTC) dusts (3 studies) and vegetable dust (3
studies).
Meta-analyses
The quantitative analysis includes data from 13 studies,
among them 8 case–control studies and 5 cohort studies.
The characteristics of the studies included in the meta-
analysis are presented in table 1.
Silica exposure showed a modest primary associ-
ation with pulmonary sarcoidosis (OR 1.12; 95% CI
1.01 to 1.23; I 2=44%; Figure 1 and online supple-
mental Figure S1- S4). However, sensitivity anal-
ysis revealed significant subgroup heterogeneity
(p=0.007): risk was elevated in medium- quality
studies (OR 1.26; 95% CI 1.13 to 1.42) but attenuated
and non- significant in high- quality studies (OR 1.05;
95% CI 0.99 to 1.13) (online supplemental Figure
S3).
Similarly, occupational exposure to pesticides (OR
1.44; 95% CI 1.21 to 1.72, I 2=30 %) (figure 2). The
sensitivity analysis revealed that the overall signifi-
cant association was largely driven by the high- quality
study (Newman et al 15). While the pooled estimate
for medium- quality studies did not reach statistical
significance (OR 1.21; 95% CI 0.91 to 1.60), the high-  
quality evidence showed a strong and significant asso-
ciation (OR 1.62; 95% CI 1.29 to 2.03), contributing
57.4% of the weight to the overall analysis (online
supplemental Figure S5).
Furthermore, occupational exposure to mould or
mildew was significantly associated with the develop-
ment of pulmonary sarcoidosis (OR 1.59; 95% CI 1.33
to 1.90), showing perfect consistency across studies
(I²=0%) (figure 3 and online supplemental Figure
S6). Importantly, risk estimates for mould exposure
were highly consistent across study quality levels
(high- quality OR 1.62 vs medium- quality OR 1.54),
reinforcing the validity of this finding. Conversely,
results for vegetable and animal dusts exhibited
significant heterogeneity (I 2 >90%), largely attrib-
utable to differences between older case–control
studies and recent cohort data.
The risk of developing pulmonary sarcoidosis was
not significantly associated with occupational expo-

I2=9%). Furthermore, occupational exposure to
gold was associated with a significant reduction in

DISCUSSION
This systematic review and meta- analysis identified
occupational exposures to silica, mould/mildew,
pesticides and WTC dust as being associated with an
increased risk of developing pulmonary sarcoidosis.
In contrast, occupational exposure to gold was asso-
ciated with a reduced risk of pulmonary sarcoidosis.
Evidence remains inconclusive regarding the poten-
tial causal relationship between exposure to vege-
table dust, animal dust and certain metals, including
aluminium, chromium and nickel.
Occupational toxic particles significantly contribute to
sarcoidosis onset and progression. Inhalation triggers an

Pesticides
Pesticides, known to trigger autoimmun e pathologies
sharing pathways with sarcoidosis, 23–25 were confirmed
as a significant risk factor in our analysis. Crucially, our
results substantiate these epidemiological observations.
Regarding pesticide exposure, our sensitivity analysis rein-
forces the validity of the association. Unlike silica, where
higher quality studies showed attenuated risks, the link
between pesticides and sarcoidosis was most pronounced
in the highest quality study included (Newman et al 15).
This suggests that the association is not an artefact of
bias in lower- quality studies but rather a robust finding
supported by rigorous methodology.
Biologically, pesticides impair alveolar epithelium,
driving inflammation and reactive oxygen species (ROS)
accumulation, a hallmark of sarcoidosis. 26 Furthermore,
interactions with susceptibility genes like BTNL2 likely
modulate this risk.27
Silica
There is growing evidence linking occupational exposure
to respirable crystalline silica (RCS) with an increased inci-
dence of sarcoidosis, placing workers in mining, construc-
tion and iron- steel industries at higher risk.28 Biologically,
silica is a plausible trigger as its inhalation can induce
an immune response leading to the formation of non-
caseating granulomas—the hallmark of sarcoidosis—in
genetically predisposed individuals. 29–32 Consistent with
previous reviews,2 33 34 our primary analysis identifies RCS
as a compelling occupational risk factor. However, our
sensitivity analysis adds a crucial nuance regarding the
magnitude of this risk: stratification by quality suggests
that medium- quality studies may overestimate the asso-
ciation due to potential recall bias. High- quality cohorts
(eg, Iversen et al,35 Jonsson et al36) which employ rigorous
exposure assessment demonstrate a persistent but more
modest association.
Metals
In our review, equipoise persists for aluminium, nickel
and general metal dust exposure with sarcoidosis. Metals
possess properties that stimulate antigen- specific cellular
immune responses, which have potential to induce similar
clinical and pathological conditions to sarcoidosis. 37
Importantly, our sensitivity analysis indicates that this
lack of association is robust and not due to the dilution
of effects by lower- quality studies. The consistency of find-
ings across varying quality scores suggests that current
evidence does not support a major role for broad metal
exposure in sarcoidosis aetiology, except for specific
isolated findings (eg, gold) that warrant further high-
quality investigation.
Exposure to metals can lead to immunological sensi-
tisation, resulting in the activation of CD4+T cells and
the release of inflammatory cytokines such as interleu-
kin- 12, interferon-γ and tumour necrosis factor-α, which
promote granuloma formation.32 38
Aluminium, copper, titanium and zirconium have all
been associated with formation of non- caseating granu-
loma in the lungs. 39 Iron, copper, cadmium, chromium
and nickel possess the ability to produce reactive radicals,
most notably ROS.40
Immunoreactivity to metals such as aluminium, beryl-
lium and zirconium was significantly higher in patients
with sarcoidosis compared with controls, suggesting these
antigens may be involved in the pathogenesis of a distinct
subgroup of patients with sarcoidosis.41
The lymphocyte proliferation test, used in diagnosing
CBD, is suggested for identifying patients with sarcoidosis
with inorganic antigenic triggers.29
Metals like tungsten and cobalt have been found in
lung tissues of patients with ILD, indicating their role in
forming fibrotic lesions through macrophage phagocy-
tosis and interaction with cytotoxic T lymphocytes.42
Mineralogical detection of relevant elements within
granulomas supports a causal role for inhaled parti-
cles.18 21 Conversely, we observed an unexpected inverse
association with gold exposure. While biologically plau-
sible given gold’s immunomodulatory history, 43 this
finding relies on limited data (n=2) and may reflect bias.
It should be considered hypothesis- generating rather
than definitive.
Organic dusts
Organic dusts, such as those from vegetable and animal
sources, contain a variety of microbial and non- microbial
antigens that can trigger an immune response in genet-
ically susceptible individuals. 44 Among these exposures,
the specific link between mould/mildew exposure and
sarcoidosis appears particularly robust. Unlike other
exposures where study quality influenced the magnitude
of risk, the association with mould remained stable and
significant regardless of the study’s quality score or design.
This consistency across different cohorts and methodol-
ogies (Kucera et al; Newman et al) strongly supports an
aetiological role for fungal antigens in sarcoidosis.
Agricultural organic dust exposure correlates with
specific phenotypes 45 and a 1.5- fold increased risk. 15
Similarly, mould and ‘musty’ environments are signifi-
cant triggers. Mechanistically, microbial cell walls likely
induce granulomatous hypersensitivity in susceptible
individuals,46 47  a hypothesis reinforced by sarcoidosis
clusters in water- damaged buildings.48
The strongest occupational association is in agricul-
tural workers (farmworkers, raising birds, cotton ginning,
exposure to organic/vegetable dust or insecticides) and
workers in industries related to organic dust exposure,

Mixed dusts and fumes
Studies suggest that exposure to mixed dusts and fumes,
such as those encountered by military personnel and fire-
fighters, may increase the risk of developing sarcoidosis.47
The presence of inorganic particles, such as those from
building activities, further supports the hypothesis that
airborne inorganic dust may contribute to sarcoidosis.18
New York firefighters exposed to the complex WTC
dust cloud (cement, silica, asbestos, metals and combus-
tion by- products) showed significantly elevated sarcoid-
osis incidence. 52 Our quality- stratified analysis provides
statistical confirmation of this observation. The strong
risk observed (OR 2.75) is derived exclusively from high-
quality studies involving large cohorts of firefighters and
rescue workers (Izbicki et al; Weber et al). The perfect
consistency (I 2=0%) between these high- quality studies
reinforces the conclusion that intense exposure to mixed
dusts at the WTC site is a definitive trigger for sarcoidosis.
In contrast, the lack of association in the lower- quality
study is likely attributable to limited statistical power or
exposure misclassification.
Beyond the exposure itself, a significant link has been
observed between the development of sarcoidosis after
exposure to the WTC dust and certain variants of the
HLA- DQB1 gene. Although the WTC event highlighted
the influence of environmental factors in triggering
sarcoidosis, the precise biological mechanisms and the
exact causative exposures remain unclear.53
Mineralogical studies
Mineralogical studies provide a biological link between
occupational exposures and sarcoidosis. Inorganic parti-
cles and metals—including silica, aluminium, titanium,
nickel, iron, zinc and steel- related particles—have been
identified in granulomas or respiratory samples of
sarcoidosis patients, reflecting exposures typical of high-
risk occupations such as construction, mining, metal-
working and firefighting.
Catinon et al reported significantly higher levels of steel
particles in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from patients
with sarcoidosis, together with increased construction-
related occupational exposure, supporting our meta-
analytical findings linking building- related dusts and
silica exposure to sarcoidosis risk.18
Similarly, the detection of silica and metals such as
nickel, chromium, cobalt and iron in lung tissues aligns
with epidemiological evidence of increased sarcoidosis
risk among workers exposed to respirable crystalline
silica and metal dusts. The presence of these particles
within granulomas supports the hypothesis that persistent
inhaled inorganic antigens may trigger granulomatous
inflammation in genetically susceptible individuals.18 54
Our findings should be interpreted in the context of
the recent systematic review and meta- analysis by Huntley
et al, which reported increased risks of sarcoidosis associ-
ated with occupational exposure to silica, pesticides and
mould/mildew.40 While our results are largely concor -
dant with these key associations, important methodolog-
ical differences distinguish the two studies and justify the
added value of the present analysis.
In contrast to Huntley et al, who primarily analysed
broader occupational exposure categories, our study
employed a more detailed exposure- specific approach
and conducted separate meta- analyses for individual
agents, including metals and mixed dusts such as WTC
dust. This allowed us to highlight areas of equipoise for
certain metals, as well as to identify a potentially inverse
association with gold exposure, a finding not previously
reported.
Furthermore, our review incorporated additional
studies published after the search period of Huntley et al
and explicitly integrated mineralogical and mechanistic
evidence to strengthen biological plausibility. Taken
together, these methodological differences support the
complementary nature of our findings and reinforce the
robustness of the associations observed across indepen-
dent analyses.
Strengths, limitations and implications of this review
This meta- analysis represents one of the earliest compre-
hensive efforts to systematically examine the link between
occupational exposure to toxic agents and the devel-
opment of sarcoidosis. Conducted in accordance with
PRISMA guidelines, the review followed a rigorous and
transparent methodology for study selection, screening
and data reporting. Moreover, the inclusion of a wide
spectrum of occupational exposures including chem-
icals, inorganic dusts, metals and organic particles
provides a broad and integrative perspective on potential
environmental contributors to the disease. The evidence
compiled in this review is intended to guide and support
future research into environmental factors involved
in sarcoidosis, given the ongoing clinical uncertainty
surrounding its aetiology.
Our meta- analysis has several limitations. First, most
studies relied on indirect exposure assessments (eg,
job- exposure matrices (JEMs), retrospective reviews).
The resulting heterogeneity in exposure definitions and
potential misclassification may bias estimates. Conse-
quently, design- specific subgroup analyses were feasible
only for silica.
Second, the diversity of exposure types and the lack of
granular data on dose, duration and latency prevented
detailed dose- response analyses. Findings may also be
influenced by the healthy worker effect, diagnostic vari-
ability and the inherent limitations of observational
designs in establishing causality. Furthermore, due to the

for clinical risk prediction. However, from a population
perspective, even small increases in relative risk can trans-

the exposure is highly prevalent.
Occupational exposures such as silica and metal fumes
remain widespread globally. As a result, the population-
attributable risk associated with these exposures may be
substantial despite modest individual- level effect esti-
mates. This distinction between individual- level clin-
ical relevance and population- level impact is central to
occupational and environmental epidemiology, where
prevention and exposure reduction are key objectives.
Consequently, small effect sizes, when applied to large
exposed populations, may still support preventive strat-
egies and regulatory measures aimed at reducing the
overall burden of respiratory disease.
CONCLUSION
The onset of pulmonary sarcoidosis was associated with
occupational silica, pesticides, mould or mildew and WTC
dust exposure. Equipoise persists regarding the potential
causal relationship between exposure to vegetable dust,
animal dust and certain metals, including aluminium,
chromium and nickel. However, gold exposure was iden-
tified as a protective factor.
The onset is far more likely the result of a complex
genetic- environment- immunological interaction. Future
studies should examine the potentially complex relation-
ship between genetic factors and airborne occupational
exposures and the mineralogical composition of sarcoid-
osis granulomas. Understanding these environmental
triggers is crucial for developing preventive strategies
and improving disease management.
