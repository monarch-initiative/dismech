# Association of variants in GJA8 with familial acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome.
**Authors:** Dong S, Zou T, Zhen F, Wang T, Zhou Y, Wu J, Nagata T, Matsushita I, Gong B, Kondo H, Li Q, Zhang H
**Journal:** Eur J Hum Genet (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41431-023-01503-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41431-023-01503-9)

## Content

Congenital acorea is a rare disease with the absence of a pupil in the eye. To date, only one family and two isolated cases with congenital acorea have been reported. The gene associated with acorea has not been identified. In this study, we recruited a Chinese family acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome. By analyzing the whole-exome sequencing (WES) data of this Chinese family, we revealed the association of a novel heterozygous variant, NM_005267.5:c.137G>A (p.G46E) in the gap junction protein alpha 8 (GJA8) gene encoding connexin 50 or CX50, with familial acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome. Additionally, another variant, NM_005267.5:c.151G>A (p.D51N) in GJA8, was identified to co-segregate with this syndrome in an unrelated Japanese family. Ectopic expression of p.G46E and p.D51N mutant GJA8 genes in cultured cells caused protein mislocalization, suggesting that the p.G46E and p.D51N mutations in GJA8 impaired the function of the gap junction channels. These results established GJA8 as the first gene associated with familial acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome.

Subject terms: Diseases, Medical genetics

The normal development of the eyes is a highly coordinated process requiring the orchestrated actions of a variety of genes and factors [1]. Dysregulation of this process leads to anophthalmia, microphthalmia, or coloboma, which are congenital eye anomalies collectively referred to as AMC [2]. Patients with anophthalmia are born without one or both eyes. Microphthalmia refers to eyes that are smaller than normal or eyes with a decreased axial length (<19 mm at one year of age or <21 mm as an adult) [3]. Coloboma is due to defects in the closure of the optic fissure that may affect the iris, lens, retina, and/or optic nerve. AMC causes severe visual impairment and cosmetic problems.

Microphthalmia can be caused by variants in genes that regulate the development of the eye [4]. Microphthalmia is often accompanied by congenital cataracts [5], which are early-onset symptoms resulting from defects in lens development. Over 110 genes have been associated with congenital cataracts [6]. Variants in genes encoding connexins account for around 16% of all congenital cataract cases with a known genetic cause [6]. Connexins are transmembrane proteins that oligomerize to form a hexameric complex or connexon to allow ions and small solutes (less than 1 kD) to cross the plasma membrane [7]. Connexons can dock with their counterparts in neighboring cells to form gap junction channels [8], which facilitate the direct exchange of small molecules between cells. The dysfunction of gap junction channels leads to the abnormal development of lens and congenital cataracts [8].

Microphthalmia is also commonly associated with abnormal pupil development, such as small pupils or microcoria. By definition, microcoria is a pupil size smaller than 2 mm in diameter [9, 10]. The gene that causes microcoria was initially mapped to the chromosomal locus of 13q31-32 [9, 11]. Later, the deletion of GPR180 was revealed to be the cause of microcoria [12]. Another more severe form of pupil anomaly is the congenital absence of a pupil, or acorea. Acorea patients display no pupil or slit-like openings upon topical administration of mydriatic drugs. Familial acorea, a rare disease with autosomal dominant inheritance, was first reported by Kondo et al. in 2013 [13]. To date, only one family in Japan and two isolated cases of acorea in India have been reported since its description [13–15]. Individuals with acorea may also exhibit microphthalmia, congenital cataracts, and iridocorneal dysgenesis [13]. A genetic study mapped the candidate genes to chromosomes 1, 5, 8, 11, and 17 [13]. The associated gene has not yet been identified. Without information on the causative genes for acorea, the mechanism underlying acorea remains unknown.

In this study, we report a Chinese family with congenital acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome. By using whole-exome sequencing followed by Sanger sequencing, a novel variant (NM_005267.5:c.137G>A, p.G46E) in GJA8 (encoding connexin 50, or CX50) was found in affected family members but not in unaffected family members or 400 unrelated healthy controls. Interestingly, another known cataract-associated variant, c.151G>A (p.D51N) in GJA8, was also identified to co-segregate with patients from the reported Japanese family [13], implicating the association of GJA8 with familial congenital acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome. The in vitro expression of recombinant proteins in cultured cells showed that the p.G46E and p.D51N mutations cause protein mislocalization.

The study involved one Chinese family (family CN) and one Japanese family (family JP) with familial congenital acorea, microphthalmia, and cataract syndrome. A Chinese family, including seven members without a history of consanguineous marriage, was enrolled in this study. The Japanese family had been previously reported [13]. The pedigrees of the families are shown in Fig. 1. The pedigree of family CN included three affected individuals and four normal individuals. Pedigree analysis suggested that the inheritance mode of this family was autosomal dominant. The proband (II-2) was diagnosed with congenital acorea, microphthalmia, and cataracts (Fig. 2A, B, and D), which were similar to the clinical symptoms manifested by patients in family JP [13]. The proband’s son (III-1) of family CN was diagnosed with the same symptoms in infancy. The son underwent iridectomy (performed in a different hospital) to treat acorea and cataracts in infancy. As a result, a significant portion of the irises and the lenses in both eyes were removed, which enabled the imaging of the fundi (Fig. 2C). The fundi appeared normal, indicating that the acorea and cataracts did not affect the development of the retina.

To identify the gene associated with congenital ocular anomalies in these families, the genomic DNA of individuals II-1, II-2, and III-1 of family CN were subjected to whole-exome sequencing. A total of 72,339 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and indels were obtained from whole-exome sequencing. To search for the genetic variants associated with the disease, we first filtered out SNPs and indels with high minor allele frequencies > 0.0002 against the database of the 1000 Genomes Project database (http://grch37.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens/Info/Index), the dbSNP database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/), and the gnomAD database (http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), as congenital cataracts are rare diseases with a global incidence rate of 2.2/10,000 [16]. We further filtered out the variants located at the introns, 5′ UTR, and 3′UTR, which usually do not alter gene function. The pool of candidate variants was further narrowed down by excluding synonymous SNPs and non-frameshift indels. As the patients from family CN shared nearly identical phenotypes with family JP, in which the candidate disease-associated gene was mapped to chromosomes 1, 5, 8, 11, and 17 [13], the candidate gene for family CN could also be linked to these chromosomes. Given that the disorder was inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern in family CN, the remaining variants were narrowed down to eight candidate genes: TRIM45, GJA8, ARNT, IL20, LZTS1, JMJD6, FBXO43, and SPAG5. In the same manner, the candidate genes in family JP were narrowed down to nine genes: GJA8, SLC39A1, CCDC15, SLC35A4, PCDHB16, PKNOX2, NEK8 USP36, and CEP295NL. GJA8 was the only gene shared by these two families. Thus, GJA8 was considered the top candidate gene. Ultimately, a heterozygous variant NM_005267.5:c.137G>A (p.G46E) in GJA8 was identified in individuals II-2 and III-1 of family CN, and a different heterozygous variant NM_005267.5:c.151G>A (p.D51N) was identified in individuals III-3 and IV-3 of family JP. In contrast, the proband’s wife (II-1) of family CN, who exhibited normal eye and pupil sizes, did not carry this variant. These two variants were verified using direct Sanger sequencing (Fig. 3). Direct sequencing also showed that variants c.137G>A (p.G46E) and c.151G>A (p.D51N) in the GJA8 heterozygous variant were present in all other affected individuals but not in unaffected individuals in family CN and JP, respectively, indicating the co-segregation of the heterozygous variants c.137G>A (p.G46E) and c.151G>A (p.D51N) in GJA8 with the respective familial disorders.

Because GJA8 has been linked to congenital cataracts [8, 17], the novel variant c.137G>A (p.G46E) in GJA8 was considered the candidate variant that was associated with ocular problems in family CN. Additionally, this variant was not present in our in-house database containing 400 healthy controls. Therefore, the disease phenotype in family CN could potentially be attributed to c.137G>A (p.G46E) in GJA8. The variant c.151G>A (p.D51N) was previously reported as a genetic variant associated with congenital cataracts and microphthalmia [5, 18]. Given that all affected members exhibited congenital cataracts along with other abnormalities in the eye, c.151G>A (p.D51N) in GJA8 can, therefore, be considered the disease-associated variant in this family.

Together, the NM_005267.5:c.137G>A (p.G46E) and NM_005267.5:c.151G>A (p.D51N) variants in GJA8 co-segregated with the same disease phenotype in two unrelated families, respectively, proving that GJA8 is the gene associated with familial congenital acorea, microphthalmia, and cataract syndrome in these two families. To the best of our knowledge, GJA8 is the first gene to be associated with acorea.

GJA8 encodes the connexin 50 (CX50) protein containing 433 amino acid residues. While the c.137G>A variant causes the substitution of the glycine by glutamic acid at position 46, c.151G>A leads to the replacement of the aspartic acid by asparagine at position 51. Multiple sequence alignment of GJA8 showed that both mutants occurred in a highly conserved region (Fig. 4A). Moreover, G46 and D51 are also highly conserved across species in CX46 (GJA3) and CX26 (GJB2) corresponding to G45 and D50 in CX26, respectively, but in CX43 (GJA1), G46, but not D51, is conserved (Fig. 4B–D). D51 is replaced by A51 in CX43. In addition, the alignment of all human connexins showed that G46 of CX50 is conserved in all other connexins, whereas D51 in CX50 is less conserved, varied from D to A, E, S, M, R, K, and G [19]. These sequence alignments suggest that while both G46 and D51 are functionally important for gap junction channels, G46 is particularly important. G46 and D51 are located in transmembrane domain 1 (TM1) and extracellular loop 1 (ECL1), respectively [20] (Fig. 4E). According to the crystal structure of CX26 and cryo-EM structure of CX50 [20, 21], G46 (equivalent to G45 in CX26) is a pore-lining residue, whereas D51 (equivalent to D50 in CX26) is close to the entrance of the hemichannel pore. Thus, two residues could be involved in gating control of the pore.

To evaluate the effects of G46E and D51N mutations on protein localization, human lens epithelial cells (HLECs) were transfected to express WT CX50, G46E, and D51N without any tags. Co-labeling of the cells with anti-β-catenin and anti-CX50 showed that WT CX50 could be targeted to the plasma membrane, whereas G46E and D51N mutant proteins were localized to the perinuclear area, and barely observed in the plasma membrane (Fig. 5), indicating that G46E and D51N prevented targeting of CX50 to the plasma membrane. These results suggest that the G46E and D51N variants could cause CX50 dysfunction, possibly in part due to protein mislocalization.

In this study, we identified a novel heterozygous GJA8 missense variant, c.137G>A (p.G46E), in a Chinese family with congenital acorea, microphthalmia, and cataract syndrome. Additionally, we identified another variant, c.151G>A (p.D51N), in GJA8 associated with the same ocular disorder in a Japanese family. To date, 74 variants of GJA8 have been reported to be associated with congenital cataracts, cataracts, congenital cataract–microcornea syndrome, glaucoma, microphthalmia, and defective irises and pupils. These consist of 68 missense variants, 2 small deletions, and 4 small insertions, but not acorea (http://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php). Our study is the first to link GJA8 variants to acorea.

Acorea is a rare disease. Prior to this study, only one family and two isolated cases have been reported [13–15]. All the reported familial acorea cases, including the family in this study, consistently exhibited congenital cataracts, suggesting that acorea in these patients could be a secondary effect of a certain type of congenital cataract. Here, we demonstrated GJA8 was associated with acorea. A wide spectrum of variants in GJA8 has been associated with congenital cataracts, and congenital cataracts caused by variants in G46, such as p.G46V and p.G46R, have been documented, although some mutant proteins, such as p.G46V, may form functional gap junctions [22]. Thus, the novel p.G46E variant, predicted to damage the protein function, also likely causes congenital cataracts, which is in agreement with the clinical manifestation of congenital cataracts exhibited by the patients in family CN [23, 24]. A previous study mapped the candidate gene for familial acorea, microphthalmia, and cataract syndrome to chromosomes 1, 5, 8, 11, and 17 in family JP [13]. Interestingly, GJA8 is located on chromosome 1. This suggests that GJA8 could be a candidate gene for the Japanese family. Here, we validated c.151G>A (p.D51N) in GJA8 as the disease-associated variant. Although c.151G>A (p.D51N) in GJA8 was previously identified in patients who exhibit congenital cataracts but not acorea, given that there are diverse additional symptoms apart from congenital cataracts caused by GJA8 variants, it is not surprising that the same variant in GJA8 could give rise to the acorea phenotype, which may depend on the actions of other genetic modifiers whose identities require further research to reveal. The association of congenital cataract with defective pupils is not new. Five reported mutations have been reported to cause cataracts accompanied by pupil problems ranging from persistent pupillary membrane [5, 25], pupil defects [26], peripupillary iris atrophy [27], to non-dilating pupil [28]. Interestingly, two out of these five mutations occur in GJA8 [5, 26]. In particular, one of these mutations is p.D51N in GJA8 [5].

GJA8 is located on chromosome 1q21.1, including two exons. GJA8, encoding a transmembrane connexin protein GJA8, namely CX50, is a member of the connexin family that is pivotal in the formation of gap junction channels [29, 30]. CX50 is involved in the embryological development of the lens [31]. CX50 is commonly expressed in mature lens fibers and epithelial cells. Like other connexins, CX50 includes four transmembrane domains (TM1, TM2, TM3, and TM4), two extracellular loops (ECL1 and ECL2), one N-terminal helix (NTH) domain, one intracellular loop (ICL), and one C-terminal domain [20]. G46 is located in TM1 and close to ECL1, whereas D51 is located with ECL1. The importance of this region was manifested by previous studies showing that several variants at positions 42–73 of CX50 fail to form functional homotypic or heterotypic gap junctions and severely impair the connexin function [32–34].

The cryo-EM structure of CX50 reveals that G46 is located in the TM1 helix and close to the entrance of the hemichannel pore. As CX50 forms channels with preference for conductance of cations, the amino acids with negative charge such as glutamate (E) lining the pore represent lower energetic barriers for the cations to permeate [20]. Thus, when G at position 46 is mutated to E, cations need to overcome lower energetic barriers to enter the channel, which may result in leaky channel currents. A previous mutagenesis and function study showed that G46E in CX50 increased the unitary channel conductance from 201 to 293 ps, but with the increased probability of the fully closed state and decreased open dwell time [35]. The importance of G46 is also exemplified by a study showing that the mutation of G45 in CX26 (equivalent to G46 in CX50) to E increased hemichannel activity and caused the lethal form of keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome [36].

In contrast, the negatively charged D at position 51 is located in ECL1 and close to the extracellular entrance of the hemichannel pore, a region with high K+ ion density in the cryo-EM structure of CX50/CX46 intercellular channels [20], suggesting it facilitates the conductance of cations. When D51 is mutated to N with neutral charge, it is expected to reduce the channel conductance. Indirect evidence comes from a previous in vitro study showed that the substitution of D51 in CX46 with C, a residue with neutral charge, resulted in a decreased unitary conductance [37]. D51 is highly conserved between CX50 and CX46 as well as CX26. The syndromic deafness-linked D50N in CX26 (equivalent to D51N in CX50) showed reduced unitary conductance [38]. In addition, the negatively-charged D at the pore entrance is important for pore gating regulated by calcium [39]. Similarly, CX26D50N loses its inhibition by extracellular calcium [38]. Thus, D51N in CX50 may influence the channel properties of CX50 in a similar way. Although G46E and D51N may have oppositive effects on channel conductance, the patients harboring these variants included in this study exhibit nearly identical dominant symptoms. The dominant effect of the mutants could be because the gap junction channels are multisubunit proteins, and in mutant cells, the channels are composed of both wild-type and mutant subunits, which could cause dysfunction of all CX50-related gap junction channels in cells. Additionally, both mutants prevented targeting of the protein to the plasma membrane, resulting in a decreased number of channels. Since CX50 is mainly expressed in the lens cells, how the malfunction of the cells exactly causes the absence of the pupil requires further research to elucidate.

In conclusion, we identified the association of c.137G>A (p.G46E) and c.151G>A (p.D51N) in the GJA8 gene with familial acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome from two unrelated families. GJA8 is the first identified gene linked to this syndrome. The results enrich the variant spectrum of the GJA8 gene and the symptom spectrum related to the variants of GJA8. Functional analysis may help to further elucidate the pathogenic mechanism of congenital acorea, microphthalmia, and cataract syndrome.

A Chinese family with the congenital acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome, consisting of three affected patients and four unaffected individuals, was recruited at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, China. The Japanese family with familial acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome was reported earlier [13].

The clinical data for the Japanese family were reported previously [13]. The clinical data or medical records of the Chinese family were obtained from all participants. Clinical examinations included A-scan and B-scan ultrasonography, slit-lamp examinations, and ultrasound biomicroscopy. Fundus photography was also conducted for the proband’s son after iridectomy. Fundus and slit-lamp examination were performed for all the healthy controls.

For the Chinese family, ~3 ml of peripheral blood was drawn from six participants (individual I-3 decided not to donate her blood sample) and stored temporarily in EDTA tubes at 4 °C, followed by the extraction of genomic DNA using a blood DNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd.). The resultant genomic DNA was stored at −20 °C till use. For the Japanese family, DNAs were isolated from the blood samples of eight family members including all affected individuals.

The DNA from the proband (II-2), and individuals II-1 and III-1 of the Chinese family, and the proband and the proband’s father of the Japanese family was subjected to whole-exome sequencing. The libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq6000 (Illumina, USA), resulting in yielding 150 bp of paired-end sequence. The annotation of mutants and sequencing data analysis were as described previously [40]. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform (HiSeq X-10, Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. The whole exome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Sanger sequencing was performed on six members of the Chinese family and eight members of the Japanese family to confirm the co-segregation of the candidate variants with the phenotypes of congenital ocular anomalies. Genomic DNA fragments encompassing candidate pathogenic variants were amplified using the following primers: GJA8-F: CGCGTTAGCAAAAACAGA; GJA8-R: TCGTAGCAGACGTTCTCG. The PCR products were purified and sequenced on an ABI sequencer.

The human GJA8 cDNA was cloned into a pEGFP-N1 vector (Clontech, USA) plasmid to replace the EGFP. Briefly, the human GJA8 cDNA was amplified from the cDNAs reverse-transcribed from the total RNAs prepared from the white blood cells of a healthy donor. The primers used to amplify the GJA8 cDNA were hGJA8-F (5′GCCACCATGGGCGACTGGAGTTTCCTG3′) and hGJA8-R (5′TACGGTTAGATCGTCTGACCTG3′). The primers used to amplify the pEGFP vector were EGFP-GJA8-F (5′CAGGTCAGACGATCTAACCGTAGTGAGCAAGGGCGAGGAGCTG3′) and EGFP-GJA8-R (5′CAGGAAACTCCAGTCGCCCATGGTGGCGACTGCAGAATTCGAAGCTTG3′). The amplified GJA8 cDNA was cloned into the EGFP vector using the NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Cloning Kit (New England Biolabs, USA) following the manufacturer’s instruction. The resulting construct was verified by sequencing. The construct would express the recombinant GJA8 without a GFP tag. The point mutations of GJA8 were created by a Q5® Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England Biolabs, USA) and verified by sequencing. Primers G46E-F (5′AGGATGAGCAATCCGACTTCG3′) and G46E-R (5′CCCACACGAACTCTGCGGCCG) were used for generating the G46E mutant; D51N-F (5′AACTTCGTGTGCAACACCCAG3′) and D51N-R (5′GGATTGCTCATCCCCCCACAC3′) for D51N.

The primary HLECs were obtained from the ScienCell Research Laboratories, USA. The HLECs were cultured in the EMEM medium (Hyclone, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. The empty vector plasmid or the recombinant plasmids wild-type or mutant GJA8 plasmids were used to transfect cells with the FuGene transfection reagent (Promega, USA). Transfection for each construct has been done in triplicate.

HLECs were transfected with the empty vector, wild-type, and mutant GJA8 plasmids, respectively. One day (24 h) after transfection, the cells were washed twice with 1xPBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Sigma, USA) for 15 min at room temperature, and washed 10 min x2 times with 1xPBS. The cells were permeabilized and blocked with 5% NDS (normal donkey serum) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA) containing 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma, USA) in 1xPBS. For immunostaining, the fixed cells were incubated with mouse anti-β-catenin (1:200, Proteintech, China) and rabbit anti-CX50 (1:200, Proteintech, China) at 4 °C overnight. The secondary antibodies were Alexa488-conjuated goat-anti-mouse (1:300, ThermoFisher, USA) and Alexa594-conjugated goat-anti-rabbit (1:300, ThermoFisher, USA). The cells were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). The fluorescent signals were imaged on a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).
