# Alveolar echinococcosis.
**Authors:** Stojković M, Junghanss T, Gehling KG
**Journal:** Curr Opin Infect Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1097/QCO.0000000000001226](https://doi.org/10.1097/QCO.0000000000001226)

## Content

PURPOSE OF REVIEW: Alveolar echinococcosis is a wildlife zoonotic disease. Clinically and radiologically, it presents in a manner similar to hepatic malignancy. The aim of our review is to summarize the current standard of clinical management of alveolar echinococcosis. We also want to raise awareness and promote an integrated approach for this neglected tropical disease (NTD).

RECENT FINDINGS: Data suggest increased prevalence of alveolar echinococcosis in some parts of the world. This may be due to intensified surveillance, spread of the disease, decreasing control efforts or a combination of these. Development of new diagnostics and treatment modalities is very slow, constrained by lack of investment into research.

SUMMARY: Treatment options for alveolar echinococcosis have not changed in decades. Cure is only available for early and limited hepatic disease amenable to R0-surgical resection. Advanced disease, ineligible for surgery, is treated with benzimidazoles with a parasitostatic effect on Echinococcus multilocularis. The prognosis of alveolar echinococcosis ranges from excellent to poor depending mainly on the extent of hepatopathy induced by irreversible damage to the biliary and vascular system of the liver. Benzimidazole intolerance dramatically worsens prognosis, as parasitic growth cannot be suppressed in inoperable patients.

Alveolar echinococcosis is an infection of the liver and very rarely other organs with the larval stage (metacestode) of Echinococcus multilocularis. It is endemic in the Northern Hemisphere with more than 90% of cases occurring in China. Above average annual incidence rates have been observed in Central Asia and Eastern and Central Europe [ 1▪ ]. Establishing the diagnosis can be challenging because radiological characteristics of alveolar echinococcosis are nonspecific and alveolar echinococcosis lesions must be differentiated from malignant and benign lesions. Diagnosis is usually confirmed by serology or, less frequently liver biopsy with histopathology and/or PCR. Long-term medical treatment with benzimidazoles is highly effective in suppressing parasitic growth and, hence, the progression of disease. Prognosis is excellent if no major pathology of the biliary and vascular system and/or impairment of liver function has occurred. At present, cure can only be achieved by R0-resection of alveolar echinococcosis lesions, alternative treatments, that is, by interventional radiology have not been formally evaluated. The risks and benefit of surgery versus benzimidazole treatment must be weighed very carefully. In this review, we are summarizing the clinical manifestation, diagnostic procedures, treatment modalities, follow-up, prognosis and prevention for alveolar echinococcosis.

The incubation period varies widely, from several years to decades. In the liver, the main target of the parasite, manifestations range from spontaneously calcified abortive to large infiltrative lesions. The latter presents with jaundice or clinical features secondary to occlusion of blood vessels. Most alveolar echinococcosis liver lesions are discovered in patients assessed for right upper quadrant pain, other nonspecific general complaints or raised liver enzymes. In a smaller proportion of patients, alveolar echinococcosis is diagnosed during work-up of suspected malignant disease. In advanced alveolar echinococcosis, patients may present with fatigue, night sweats and weight loss, mimicking malignant disease or tuberculosis. Painless jaundice is a sign of infiltration of the liver hilum and central biliary vessel involvement. Less frequently, patients present with symptoms and signs of inferior vena cava obstruction or occlusion such as significantly reduced physical capacity and lower limb oedema.

Neurological signs and symptoms due to secondary alveolar echinococcosis of the central nervous system are very rare and mainly observed in severely immunocompromised patients due to underlying illnesses (e.g. hematological malignancy) or immunosuppressive drugs, which seem to favor alveolar echinococcosis growth. In alveolar echinococcosis cohorts of tertiary referral centers, which are biased towards patients with advanced disease, a significant proportion of alveolar echinococcosis patients have concomitant malignancy (e.g. breast cancer, renal cell carcinoma, cholangiocellular carcinoma), either detected prior to alveolar echinococcosis or during follow-up.

Primary extrahepatic presentation of alveolar echinococcosis is rare and is usually diagnosed through tissue biopsy.

Recurrent cholangitis, cholangiosepsis or liver abscess can occur even if alveolar echinococcosis-related stenoses of central bile ducts are treated with stenting and balloon dilation by repeated endoscopic retrograde cholangiography (ERC). If the biliary system communicates with a necrotic cavity, debris can cause biliary obstruction, which may need cholangioscopy and flushing of central bile ducts and the necrotic cavity to mobilize obstructing material.

Alveolar echinococcosis-related portal hypertension can develop due to posthepatic pathology, intrahepatic pathology and prehepatic pathology. Alveolar echinococcosis-related infiltration of the hepatic segment of the inferior vena cava causes chronic Budd–Chiari syndrome and progressive damage to liver tissue and liver cirrhosis. Alveolar echinococcosis-related recurrent and chronic biliary obstruction leads to secondary biliary sclerosis and ultimately cirrhosis (Fig. 1). Alveolar echinococcosis-related infiltration or compression of the portal vein causes portal vein thrombosis, sometimes with cavernous portal vein transformation.

Rate and severity of impairment of liver function due to alveolar echinococcosis-induced pathology of the biliary and vascular system are crucial for the long-term prognosis of patients. Furthermore, bacterial complications such as recurrent cholangitis or bacterial infection of necrotic cavities can cause further damage to the liver parenchyma and accelerate impairment of liver function depending on the extent and frequency. Steady deterioration of liver function hampers benzimidazole tolerance and suppression of alveolar echinococcosis growth. Complicated hepatic alveolar echinococcosis benefits greatly from interdisciplinary clinical management including cancer screening.

Radiologically, alveolar echinococcosis manifestations are morphologically diverse and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of malignant and benign liver lesions. Cystic and solid components with a nonenhancing central part of the lesion with calcifications, microcystic appearance of active infiltrative components and thin ring enhancement, which can, however, be discontinuous or absent are the leading radiological findings in alveolar echinococcosis (Fig. 2).

Abdominal ultrasound is the most widely available imaging modality. Familiarity with the ultrasound features of alveolar echinococcosis is of high differential diagnostic value. Alveolar echinococcosis lesions can be hyperechogenic on ultrasound and are therefore an important differential diagnosis of hemangioma. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound differentiates the two causes, however, with very high diagnostic accuracy. Typical calcifications can be seen in both ultrasound and computed tomography (CT) (Figs. 1c, 2f). The latter best depicts calcifications. Contrast-enhanced CT in arterial and portal venous phase is the modality of choice for adjacent vascular liver structures. MRI is the most sensitive imaging tool to visualize the microcystic character of alveolar echinococcosis manifestations in T2-weighted sequences. Furthermore, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is helpful in identifying biliary complications.

Several radiological classification systems have been suggested for the assessment of alveolar echinococcosis: ultrasound-based: the Echinococcus multilocularis National Health Commission Classification-Ultrasound (EMNHCC-US) by the National Health Commission of the People's Republic of China and Echinococcus multilocularis Ulm classification (EMUC) [ 3 ]. The EMUC-CT classification defines multiple patterns of calcification and five primary morphologies. The MRI-based Kodama classification distinguishes five lesion types and depicts microcystic components best, informing on parasite activity. The classifications are descriptive and are not designed for clinical management decisions [ 2 ].

From a clinical management perspective, vascular and biliary involvement and hepatic function must be systematically investigated, assessing the feasibility of R0 resectability on one side and conservative management with drug treatment and accompanying measures to preserve liver function on the other side (see section ‘Treatment’). A recommendation for structured radiological reporting of alveolar echinococcosis has been published [ 4 ].

Regarding standardized evaluation of diagnostic and therapeutic measures, the World Health Organization Informal Working Group on Echinococcosis (WHO-IWGE) suggested the PNM classification, derived from the T (primary tumor size) N (regional lymph node involvment) M (distant metastases) staging for malignant tumors and reflecting the malignancy-like growth of alveolar echinococcosis manifestations. It describes the extension of the parasitic mass in the liver (P), the involvement of neighboring organs (N) and metastases (M) [ 5 ]. Distant metastases are most common in the lung but can be found in every organ. For osseous alveolar echinococcosis involvement, the spine seems to be the most common site. Primary evaluation of alveolar echinococcosis patients includes radiological staging by abdominal MRI or CT, CT-chest and cCT/cMRI.

The quality of serological tests used for further workup of patients is decisive. Highly sensitive ELISA tests with purified or recombinant Echinococcus antigens (EgHF, Em2, Em2+, recEm18-ELISA) are used as the first step in a two-step-procedure. In a second step, positive ELISA tests are confirmed by immunoblotting, which is more specific. Differentiation from cystic echinococcosis requires imaging in addition to serology. This is particularly important for alveolar echinococcosis presenting with cysts or pseudocysts (Fig. 3a–c and Fig. 4a and b). Misclassifying alveolar echinococcosis as cystic echinococcosis is a major pitfall, which can lead to catastrophic outcomes if cyst-sterilizing techniques are applied (consequences of erroneous instillation of a protoscolicidal agent into a alveolar echinococcosis lesion can be seen in ref. [ 5 ], Fig. 3 [ 6 ]. In cases of false-negative serologic results, the diagnosis is usually established by a biopsy, usually in a work-up for malignancy. Echinococcus serology is also used to screen populations at risk for alveolar echinococcosis [ 7 ].

If the suspicion of alveolar echinococcosis remains and/or important other differential diagnoses must be considered (i.e. malignancy) after a serologically negative, equivocal or only partially positive result, liver biopsy is indicated. Differentiation of E. multilocularis and Echinococcus granulosus may be challenging for pathologists. Histopathological presentation of alveolar echinococcosis is characterized by necrotic areas and the detection of laminated layers on Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) reaction. Microscopic differentiation between E. multilocularis and E. granulosus is based on the thickness of the laminated layer. In alveolar echinococcosis, protoscolices are very rarely detected, while in cystic echinococcosis, they are usually abundant. If the diagnosis including species subtyping cannot be obtained by light microscopy, immunohistochemistry and PCR may be required [ 8▪ ].

Recently, histomorphological alveolar echinococcosis viability criteria based on hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining underpinned by monoclonal Antibody Em18 (mAbEm18) immunostaining were described. This provides a basis for better pre-interventional and post-operative (resection margins) assessment in the future [ 9 ].

In lesions presenting with microcystic components, the regression of microcysts is a radiological sign of treatment response and declining activity (Fig. 2a and c).

F-18 fluorodeoxyglucose PET (FDG-PET)/CT or MRI is used to evaluate metabolic activity by measuring the extent of perilesional metabolic activity or fibroinflammatory reaction as an indirect parameter for parasitic activity. Lack of measurable FDG-PET metabolic activity indicates suppressed parasite activity but is not a sign of parasite death. Delayed FDG-PET imaging and whole-body dynamic FDG-PET/CT parametric imaging may increase sensitivity and accuracy in metabolic assessment of alveolar echinococcosis [ 10 , 11 ]. FDG-PET/CT is not a standardized tool for monitoring treatment; this is still subject to further studies.

Surgical treatment is the only treatment that can cure alveolar echinococcosis. It is usually only indicated if curative (R0) resection of all alveolar echinococcosis lesions can be achieved (Fig. 3 e). The surgical approaches range from open to laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgery depending on the localization and size of the alveolar echinococcosis lesion(s) and the expertise of the center involved. Histopathological evaluation of resection margins must be evaluated as in malignancy. According to the current expert consensus, patients receive postoperative benzimidazole treatment for a duration of 2 years.

Other therapeutic approaches adapted from oncology (e.g. radiofrequency) are applied to alveolar echinococcosis liver lesions on a case-by-case basis and deserve formal evaluation.

Rarely, palliative surgery is indicated in patients with complications that cannot be controlled by other means. Physicians caring for alveolar echinococcosis patients must bear in mind that alveolar echinococcosis is not a malignancy in the strict sense. In patients with uncomplicated disease, who tolerate benzimidazole treatment well, risks of radical surgical approaches must be weighed against risks and benefits of lifelong parasitostatic benzimidazole treatment.

Liver transplantation is a last resort in patients of otherwise incurable disease. Residual lesions of alveolar echinococcosis are not considered a contraindication for liver transplantation [ 12 ].

Drug treatment is used for patients in whom hepatic alveolar echinococcosis lesions cannot be resected with curative intent or for whom drug treatment is a safer alternative to surgery. In selected cases, resectability of hepatic lesions should be re-evaluated during drug treatment.

Presently, benzimidazoles are the only proven effective compounds for treating alveolar echinococcosis. Due to the better bioavailability and simpler drug intake regimen, albendazole is the drug of first choice. The recommended daily dose is 10–15 mg/kg divided in two doses. Systemic availability is increased by administration with a fatty meal. Mebendazole is used if albendazole is not available or not tolerated. Mebendazole is given at a dose of 40–50 mg/kg/day divided into three doses and also administered with a fat-rich meal. In clinical practice of alveolar echinococcosis treatment, benzimidazoles are given continuously, without interruption. Therapeutic drug monitoring is strongly recommended to measure albendazole sulfoxide (active metabolite) and should be obtained at 4, 8 and 12 weeks after the start of treatment, after dose adjustment and at follow-up visits thereafter (for long-term follow-up see section 'Follow-up' below). For this purpose, plasma samples are taken 4 h after drug intake.

During benzimidazole treatment, patients need to be monitored for adverse reactions. Frequently, a rise of liver enzymes is observed under albendazole and less so under mebendazole treatment. In general, liver enzymes and complete blood count (CBC) are checked before starting treatment and monitored on day 5, day 10 and in biweekly intervals, then monthly and trimonthly in the further course (for long-term follow-up see section 'Follow-up' below). Aminotransferase levels three times above normal and evidence of bone marrow suppression require immediate discontinuation of benzimidazoles [ 13 ].

Most frequently, albendazole treatment must be discontinued due to hepatotoxicity. Once liver enzymes are back to normal, treatment can be restarted. This is usually done by slow and gradual increase of the albendazole dose over several weeks or months and can ‘overcome’ toxicity in some patients. If albendazole is still not tolerated, mebendazole with a gradually increasing dosing scheme can be tried. Reintroduction of benzimidazoles after hepatotoxicity or bone marrow toxicity needs very careful monitoring of CBC, liver enzymes, bilirubin and International Normalized Ratio (INR) as well as drug levels during up-dosing of treatment.

Biliary infiltration and obstruction are treated by repeated ERCP with balloon dilatation or stenting until bile flow is restored [ 14 ].

Vascular involvement requires careful and regular assessment, e.g. obstruction of the inferior vena cava or portal vein needs evaluation for esophageal varices. Nonselective beta blockers and follow-up endoscopies should be considered if indicated. Depending on liver vessels involved, benefits and risks of anticoagulation need to be considered [ 4 ].

The psychological burden of alveolar echinococcosis patients is high and comparable to that of cancer patients. Psychological evaluation should be offered to patients on initial assessment and during follow-up [ 15 ].

After surgical resection of alveolar echinococcosis, a baseline imaging of the liver, is recommended 3 months postoperatively, then at yearly intervals for 10 years. In our experience, MRI is the best choice for baseline imaging. Postoperatively, antirecEm18 antibodies fall rapidly, and seroconversion correlates well with curative resection.

Follow-up intervals for patients under benzimidazole treatment are 3–6 monthly at the beginning, depending on the severity of disease. Once disease is stable, 6–12-month follow-up intervals are sufficient. Ideally, imaging is combined with antirecEm18 antibodies (tested in parallel with serum of the previous follow-up) as a marker of disease activity.

Negative FDG PET/CT or MRI results combined with negative serology (recEm18 ELISA) are used to guide ending benzimidazole treatment in alveolar echinococcosis patients [ 10 ]. There are no generally accepted criteria for ending treatment, and the decision is made on an individual basis. The decisions should always be discussed with expert centers.

Alveolar echinococcosis is acquired through ingestion of contaminated (primarily raw) food and water. Dogs can passively transmit E. multilocularis after contact with infected canine (e.g. fox, coyote) feces. Dogs can also function as definitive host passing E. multilocularis eggs. Hygiene, including hand hygiene, is essential. Echinococcal eggs are inactivated by cooking but not by conventional freezing in household freezers (−70 to −80 °C is needed). Serological screening of people at risk may be indicated for early detection and treatment of infection. Pet dogs should be dewormed monthly [ 13 ].

Advanced alveolar echinococcosis is a complex and severe, rare and neglected disease. It needs close cooperation of specialists from infectious diseases, radiology, gastroenterology, surgery, oncology and psychology to achieve appropriate clinical care.

No new drugs have been developed for nearly half a century leaving patients with advanced disease and intolerability to the only drug available with few options. Alveolar echinococcosis is one of the most severe chronically disabling parasitic diseases affecting young and middle-aged patients and can shorten their life span significantly when untreatable. Vertical disease silos should be abandoned, and alveolar echinococcosis management should follow a horizontal approach to diagnostics, treatment, prevention and health education [ 16 ]. Interdisciplinary cross-fertilization is a very powerful clinical development tool.
