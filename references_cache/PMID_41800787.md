# Is high insulin protective or detrimental? Mathematical modeling reveals the base of the iceberg.
**Authors:** Yang B, Ha J, Sherman AS
**Journal:** Am J Physiol Endocrinol Metab (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1152/ajpendo.00414.2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.00414.2025)

## Content

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) progression involves complex interactions over time, yet longitudinal human data remain limited. Mathematical modeling enables reconstruction of individualized disease trajectories and mechanistic insights from sparse clinical observations. We developed a series of obesity-diabetes progression models, incorporating proposed mechanisms of glucose-insulin regulation and identified the best-performing framework using individualized longitudinal data from Southwest Native Americans. This population, with high rates of hyperinsulinemia and T2D progression, offers a good opportunity to investigate whether hyperinsulinemia is a primary factor in T2D. The model captured heterogeneous trajectories of glucose, insulin, insulin sensitivity, and beta-cell function, under the assumption that hyperinsulinemia reflects compensation for insulin resistance rather than a primary defect. We extended the model to study a subset of the population carrying an ABCC8 loss-of-function variant linked to primary rather than compensatory hyperinsulinemia. The simulations reproduced the observed earlier diabetes onset in heterozygous carriers by incorporating chronic calcium stress, which accelerates beta-cell failure when combined with insulin resistance. In contrast, homozygous carriers exhibited severe early-onset diabetes even without insulin resistance. These findings suggest that high insulin secretion alone does not initiate diabetes unless it is extreme but may exacerbate progression in the presence of metabolic stress caused by insulin resistance. Importantly, the model predicts that Ca2+-reducing therapies in all the genotypes can mitigate beta-cell dysfunction if applied sufficiently early and at appropriate intensity. This study addresses controversial questions in diabetes pathogenesis and provides a platform for personalized disease stratification and therapeutic development.NEW & NOTEWORTHY This study presents the first obesity-diabetes model validated against four-dimensional, individualized longitudinal data, capturing heterogeneous disease trajectories and quantifying key mechanisms of progression. Uniquely, it investigates the transition from primary hyperinsulinemia to beta-cell failure in ABCC8 loss-of-function carriers and highlights the potential of early, appropriately dosed Ca2+-reducing therapies to delay disease progression, providing new genotype- and stage-specific insights.

The pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes (T2D) involves complex interactions between insulin secretion and action. While understanding its progression is critical for developing prevention and treatment strategies, long-term human studies remain scarce due to the challenges of extended follow-up, resulting in most research relying on cross-sectional data. Moreover, prospective longitudinal studies have demonstrated that individual disease trajectories often differ substantially from population averages (1, 2), highlighting the need for alternative approaches to studying personalized disease development. Mathematical modeling potentially offers a cost-effective tool to investigate such complex biological systems. The classic Minimal Model, which defines insulin sensitivity (SI) and beta-cell function (AIRg) through an ordinary differential equation (ODE) framework, has been widely applied but captures only the metabolic state during a single intravenous glucose tolerance test. Although such models can be applied at multiple points in time, models incorporating long-term dynamics, such as those developed by Topp et al. (3) and De Gaetano et al. (4), link the states at different time points by integrating mechanisms such as beta-cell mass regulation and insulin secretory capacity to better characterize disease progression.

Despite these advances, further improvements are possible by incorporating additional physiological mechanisms. One key example is lipotoxicity, in which excess free fatty acids (FFAs) contribute to both insulin resistance and beta-cell failure (5, 6). More recent models have incorporated these additional mechanisms: Yang et al. introduced obesity-related effects into a Topp-like model to better capture longitudinal glucose dynamics in Southwest Native Americans (7); Yildirim et al. (8) extended the work of Ha et al. (9) to include interactions among glucose, insulin, FFAs, inflammation, insulin sensitivity, and beta-cell function; De Gaetano et al. (10) incorporated lifestyle factors such as exercise and diet; and Islam et al. (11) modified the Topp model to study the role of FFAs and physical activity in the pathways to T2D and prevention. Although these models provide valuable mechanistic insights, most have been calibrated using either mean longitudinal data or individualized data for a single variable. Validation against multi-dimensional individualized longitudinal datasets, essential for capturing the system’s complexity, remains largely unaddressed. Here, we close this gap with such validation, a critical step toward modeling T2D progression.

One intriguing challenge in understanding the progression of diabetes is disentangling the respective roles of insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia in disease pathogenesis (12, 13). While insulin resistance has long been viewed as the primary driver, several studies have reported that individuals with primary hyperinsulinemia, characterized by apparent elevated insulin secretion in the absence of insulin resistance, are at increased risk of progressing to diabetes (14, 15). Moreover, high insulin can itself induce insulin resistance (16, 17). These findings raise three fundamental questions that have been debated in the literature (12, 13, 18): (1) is hyperinsulinemia primary or secondary? (2) Is high insulin protective or detrimental? and (3) What drives the transition from insulin hypersecretion to beta-cell failure and hyposecretion during disease progression?

To address these questions, we leveraged a four-dimensional longitudinal T2D dataset from Southwest Native Americans (SWNA), a population that exhibits pronounced hyperinsulinemia during disease progression. Our modeling framework relied primarily on repeated fasting plasma glucose and insulin measurements, with OGTT-derived indices used for model parameterization. We developed a mathematical modeling framework to identify an optimal obesity-diabetes progression model capable of capturing the typical trajectory of diabetes progression in SWNA individuals and to test hypotheses underlying the disease process. Building on this framework, we extended the model to investigate a SWNA subgroup harboring a specific loss-of-function ABCC8 variant (14), which is clinically associated with primary hyperinsulinemia, i.e., heightened insulin secretion independent of insulin requirements. This integrative approach reveals the dynamic interplay among insulin secretion, insulin sensitivity, and obesity in diabetes pathogenesis and allows us to test the proposed role of Ca2+-dependent inhibition in reversing secretory failure in type 2 diabetes (19).

We first develop a modeling framework to study the natural diabetes progression and then propose a model to investigate accelerated progression in individuals carrying the pathogenic ABCC8 gene variants.

The first part of this study is a secondary analysis of clinical data from SWNA individuals previously analyzed by Ha et al for a different purpose (20). These individuals had 2-hour OGTT data available from at least three visits at the Clinical Center of the Phoenix Epidemiology and Clinical Research Branch., NIH. Prior to each admission, these individuals were determined to be generally healthy by medical history, physical examination, and no medication use. The 2-hour OGTT measures were used to document that all study participants were free of diabetes before enrollment in a study (21). Both BMI and body composition (assessed by dual X-ray absorptiometry) were assessed at each visit. Details of the clinical examinations can be found in (14).

For the current analysis of T2D progression, individuals were selected from the dataset who had both normal glucose tolerance and normal fasting glucose (NGT/NFG) at baseline and developed prediabetes or diabetes during a subsequent visit (Table 1). The selection of individuals with prediabetes and diabetes is based on crossings of the 2-h glucose thresholds (140 mg/dl and 200 mg/dl, respectively); the participants in the study had a low incidence of impaired fasting glucose with normal glucose tolerance compared to impaired glucose tolerance with normal fasting glucose, as previously reported for this population (21).

The prediabetes progression dataset comprises 63 individuals who had normal glucose tolerance (FPG < 100 mg/dl [5.6 mmol/L], 2h-PG < 140 mg/dl [7.8 mmol/L]) at baseline and developed prediabetes (2h-PG ≥ 140 mg/dl) during a follow-up period of 5.6±2.5 years, with an average of 6 OGTTs per individual, obtained at discrete clinical visits over the follow-up period. The T2D progression dataset includes 18 individuals who transitioned from euglycemia to diabetes (2h-PG ≥ 200 mg/dl) during 6±2.7 years of follow-up, with an average of 6.5 OGTTs per individual. One individual was excluded from this dataset due to incomplete data.

Topp et al proposed a pioneering mathematical model (3) with the simplest structure capable of capturing the main features of the glucose-insulin regulation system: fast negative feedback by insulin to control glucose on a time scale of hours, slow negative feedback by beta-cell mass to compensate for reduced insulin sensitivity, and progression to diabetes when the slow negative feedback tips over into positive feedback. Most subsequent modeling efforts on T2D progression have been built by modifying this influential work, reinforcing the paradigm of glucotoxicity as the dominant driver of pathogenesis. However, certain parameter values in the Topp model deviate from physiological data (22–27), and the simplified character of the model restricts its ability to represent clinical observations or experimental results of diabetes (7, 28).

Our procedure for constructing the mathematical models is to begin with a simple conceptual model and then progressively introduce complexity until the model can account well for the data. We initially refined the model by carefully adjusting its parameters to align with physiological data (see Supplemental Method File S1 for details of all modeling procedures, including Figures M1–M10, Tables M1–M9, and Models 1–5.2.1).

When assessed using the SWNA T2D progression dataset, the re-calibrated Topp model had limited ability to capture individual trajectories (see model reports in Supplementary Results File S3). This motivated the development of a new modeling framework to improve performance. We then constructed a base model with minimal structural changes to the Topp model: the insulin sensitivity function was revised to account for elevated free fatty acids (29), and the beta-cell mass equation was adjusted to limit the rates of expansion and apoptosis (30). We evaluated this model using the same T2D progression dataset and found that it still lacked sufficient mechanistic detail to capture key data patterns (see model reports in Supplementary Results File S3). A model incorporating additional physiological mechanisms is necessary to capture individualized disease progression.

Recent clinical findings provide valuable mechanistic insights for guiding model extensions. These include insulin resistance induced by chronically high insulin levels (16, 17), reduced insulin clearance (31, 32), coexistence of positive and negative effects of FFAs on beta-cell function (6, 18, 33). Based on this, we propose nine alternative formulations for eight components of the base model to improve performance (Fig. M8 or S10). However, incorporating all mechanisms simultaneously would significantly increase model complexity. Therefore, we identify the optimal subset of these components to strike a balance between physiological realism and model parsimony (Fig. 1).

The T2D progression dataset was employed to select the optimal model for the investigation of disease development, while the prediabetes progression dataset was used to validate the optimal model by refitting individual-specific parameters within the same model structure. The data variables used in the models are age, percent body fat (PFAT), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), fasting plasma insulin (FPI), the Matsuda index, and a form of the insulinogenic index (IGI), defined as AUCI/AUCG, the ratio of the area under the curve of insulin to that of glucose during the three-hour OGTT. We observed substantial variability in individual trajectories and addressed it by applying a nonlinear mixed-effects modeling approach, in which all individuals were fitted simultaneously, with selected parameters allowed to vary between individuals. Detailed methods for model fitting and selection are provided in Supplemental Method File S1, Section 3. Model code files are available in Supplemental Code File S4.

The contribution of individual physiological mechanisms to overall model performance was systematically assessed using a stepwise model building framework guided by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (34). The most substantial improvement in performance was achieved by incorporating the effect of glucotoxicity on β-cell secretory capacity (σ), yielding a marked reduction in BIC. Integration of additional physiological mechanisms progressively lowered BIC (Fig. M8 or S10). Only small further decreases in BIC were obtained by changes in the fifth model generation (Fig. 1). Specifically, while a 47% reduction in BIC was observed between the first- and second-generation models, the maximum variation among fifth-generation models was limited to 2%, with Model 5 and Model 5.2 yielding the two lowest BIC values (Fig. M8 or S10).

Model 5 and Model 5.2 differ only in their assumptions about the impact of elevated free fatty acids (FFAs) on beta-cell secretory capacity (σ): Model 5 assumes a biphasic effect (both stimulatory and inhibitory), whereas Model 5.2 assumes a purely inhibitory (lipotoxic) effect. Although Model 5.2 exhibited a marginally lower BIC value, Model 5 was selected as the optimal model based on prior clinical evidence supporting the biphasic effect of FFAs on β-cell function(18). Therefore, Model 5 was deemed the final optimal model for characterizing typical diabetes progression in SWNA.

This optimal model retains the core concept of previous diabetes progression models in (9, 28), in which a decline in insulin sensitivity leads to glucotoxicity that impairs beta-cell mass and function, ultimately resulting in diabetes. The major difference between the present model and the models in (9, 28) lies in the incorporation of individualized FFA profiles (X) into the regulation of insulin sensitivity and beta-cell function, which permits characterization of diverse individual trajectories. In Eq. 1, HGP represents hepatic glucose production rate. The term SGG denotes insulin-independent glucose uptake, while S(X,AGE,I)IG represents insulin-dependent glucose uptake. The function S(X,AGE,I) represents insulin sensitivity, following the concept introduced with the minimal model of Bergman, Cobelli and colleagues (35), and takes into account the impact of excessive free fatty acids (quantified by X) and insulin-induced insulin resistance.

Insulin secretion from beta-cells is assumed to be stimulated by elevated glucose levels and mediated by cytosolic calcium concentrations (Eq. 2). The rate of insulin clearance is denoted by K(I), reflecting the reduced rate of insulin clearance when the insulin level is high (32). The beta-cell mass is determined by a logistic growth equation with a glucose-dependent growth rate (Eq. 3). That rate is postulated to be a bump function, reflecting the toxic effect on hyperglycemia on beta-cell mass (3).

The steady-state value σ∞ reflects a stimulatory effect at low elevations of glucose or low X, and a detrimental effect at high elevations of glucose or high X (Eq. 5). The product of σ and β represents the total capability of beta-cells to respond to glucose stimulation, and because σ and β cannot be independently identified from the available data, this combined quantity was fitted to the OGTT-derived insulinogenic index (AUCI/AUCG).

In the SWNA population, a subgroup was identified with primary hyperinsulinemia due to loss-of-function variant in ABCC8 (14). The ABCC8 gene encodes the SUR1 subunit of the ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channel in pancreatic beta-cells, which plays a key role in regulating glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. Upon glucose uptake, rising ATP/ADP ratios induce closure of the KATP channels, leading to membrane depolarization, an increase in intracellular calcium levels, and subsequent insulin release (36).

Loss-of-function (LOF) variants in ABCC8, which impair the channel’s ability to close properly in response to increased ATP/ADP levels, disrupt this regulatory mechanism, leading to inappropriate insulin secretion relative to the glucose level. The clinical presentation varies by zygosity. Heterozygous carriers often exhibit milder, transient, or subclinical hyperinsulinemia due to partial preservation of KATP channel function (37, 38). In contrast, homozygous variants typically result in more severe dysregulation, manifesting as congenital hyperinsulinism (CHI) with persistent hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia during infancy (39, 40). Notably, both groups have been reported to carry an elevated long-term risk of beta-cell dysfunction and progression to insulin-deficient diabetes later in life. However, the precise physiological mechanisms linking early hypersecretion to eventual beta-cell failure remain unclear.

A similar transition from early beta-cell overstimulation to later secretory failure is also observed in typical type 2 diabetes progression due to glucotoxicity. Impairment of first-phase insulin secretion occurs even when glucose levels are minimally raised and becomes aggravated as glucose levels escalate (41). However, in cases involving ABCC8 LOF variants, glucose levels are initially lower than normal, so there must be an additional mechanism driving the accelerated progression to T2D in these individuals. Similarly, hypersecretion of insulin is unlikely to be the sole explanation because it is inherently self-limiting – insulin decreases as beta-cell function deteriorates. Following the mechanism proposed by Nichols et al. (42), we suggest that the key mediator of enhanced diabetes risk is Ca2+-dependent downregulation, wherein persistently high cytosolic calcium downregulates insulin secretion and increases the risk of beta-cell apoptosis in response to metabolic stress (36, 43, 44). Crucially, in the presence of LOF ABCC8 variants, cytosolic Ca2+ can remain high even when glucose is low or insulin secretion is impaired, placing continuous stress on beta-cells and accelerating their failure.

While preserving the general structure of the core model, we introduced targeted refinements to reflect the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms associated with ABCC8 variants. In particular, we incorporated Ca2+-dependent downregulation in Eq. 5 for β-cell function (σ) and evaluated its role in the increased diabetes risk observed in ABCC8 variant carriers. We further simulate the predicted effect of Ca2+-reducing therapy with a KATP channel opener on diabetes progression across different genotypes in SWNA. To facilitate long-term simulation and reduce model complexity, we implemented several structural simplifications in the model for ABCC8 variant carriers, such as modifying the HGP function and omitting age-dependent effects on insulin sensitivity, that do not alter model behavior relevant to the Ca2+-dependent downregulation mechanism. These adjustments were introduced to streamline the analysis and ensure model tractability across different genetic backgrounds.

Application of the optimal model to the T2D progression dataset reproduced the long-term trends of all measured variables, as shown in the observation-versus-prediction plots (Fig. 2). Despite substantial inter-individual variability in the four-dimensional dataset, the model reliably captured the overall dynamics (full fitting results provided in Supplemental Results File S3). These results suggest that the model effectively captures the essential features of the glucose-insulin regulatory system.

In the data partitioning analysis, BIC values obtained when re-estimating only random effects on the test subsets were comparable to those obtained by full re-estimation of both fixed and random effects (Table M8 in Supplemental Method File S1), suggesting stable predictive performance across partitions. The good concordance between observed and predicted data generated by applying Model 5 to the prediabetes progression dataset (Fig. 3) further supports the model’s robustness and its ability to capture the longitudinal dynamics across glucose tolerance states.

We next apply Model 5 (Fig. M8 or S10) to provide insights into the pathogenesis of T2D in the SWNA population. Comparison of estimated individual parameter distributions revealed that only susceptibility to glucotoxicity (σ6) differs significantly between prediabetes and diabetes progression groups (Fig. S1), suggesting that impaired beta-cell function plays the major role in driving progression from prediabetes to diabetes in this population. Insulin resistance, which is severe in this population, also plays an important role, but in the progression from normoglycemia to prediabetes (45). Put another way, if there was no insulin resistance to set the stage, beta-cell function would not decline.

The SWNA population exhibits marked hyperinsulinemia throughout disease progression, and the relative contributions of insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia to T2D pathogenesis have long been debated. The standard model is that insulin resistance is the primary defect, leading to compensatory hyperinsulinemia and then T2D when compensation fails or is inadequate due to beta-cell failure. An alternative hypothesis posits that beta-cell hypersecretion is primary and that the consequent hyperinsulinemia induces insulin resistance and ultimately beta-cell failure (1, 12, 17, 46). Model 5 is based on the standard model and, as shown in Figs. 2 and 3, can account the patterns of glucose and insulin in the hyperinsulinemic SWNA population. However, multiple mathematical models built upon the standard disease progression theory may be able to fit a given dataset, and the following empirical observations are often cited to argue for the alternative model that hyperinsulinemia drives hyperglycemia rather than resulting from it: (1) insulin concentrations during glucose tolerance tests in prediabetes can be twice those of euglycemic lean individuals, and (2) fasting glucose remains largely unchanged during early prediabetes despite substantial increases in basal insulin (12, 46, 47).

Nevertheless, these observations demonstrate correlation, not causation. We use our simplest model (the base model, section 4.1.1 of the Supplemental Method File S1), to illustrate quantitatively how hyperinsulinemia can arise in response to glucose elevations too small to detect clinically.

First, we demonstrate that a rapid decrease in insulin sensitivity results in significant elevations in both glucose and insulin. Figures 4a and 4b show changes in basal glucose and insulin following a stepwise 30% reduction in insulin sensitivity on day 10, which is restored on day 12 (Fig. S2d). Both insulin and glucose rise rapidly as beta-cell mass cannot immediately compensate for the abrupt loss of sensitivity (Fig. S2c).

In contrast, when insulin sensitivity declines gradually over years, more representative of natural disease progression, the rise in glucose is minimal (Fig. 4c) and diminishes over time (Fig. S3a), while basal insulin still increases by 42%. In this scenario, beta-cells have sufficient time to compensate, preventing substantial glucose elevation (Fig. S3c). The slower the decline in insulin sensitivity, the smaller is the glucose elevation, while the long-term increase in insulin is almost unaffected. These simulations demonstrate that even minor, subclinical elevations in glucose can trigger substantial compensatory insulin secretion when the slow feedback system is activated. In the next section we apply the model to a subset of the SWNA population in which primary hypersecretion is undoubtedly present and study its interaction with primary insulin resistance.

The SWNA subgroup carrying the heterozygous ABCC8 variant R1420H exhibits a twofold increased risk of T2D and a 7-year earlier onset despite being leaner than non-carriers (14). The analyzed individuals had a mean BMI exceeding 34 kg/m2, and diabetes incidence increased with age. We extend the optimal model by incorporating hypersecretion in the equation for insulin and Ca2+-dependent downregulation in the equation for beta-cell function (σ) and use it to simulate a hypothetical heterozygous ABCC8 carrier individual and a non-carrier individual, both experiencing gradual but substantial weight gain. The model successfully reproduces the observed 7-year earlier diabetes onset (Fig. 5a). We now use the simulated trajectories to provide an integrated view of basal-state glucose-insulin dynamics throughout disease progression for both cases.

Figures 5a and 5b show that the heterozygous R1420H carrier initially exhibits mild hyperinsulinemia and slightly lower fasting glucose compared to the control without the KATPchannel variant. However, this primary hyperinsulinemia cannot be sustained over time. Persistent calcium elevation (Fig. S4a), driven by both insulin resistance and reduced KATP channel function, gradually impairs insulin secretion. Although we applied the same FFA profile to both individuals, beta-cell failure occurs earlier in the heterozygous case. These findings align with the observations in (14) that individuals heterozygous for R1420H displayed reduced insulin responses after age 15 compared to controls. Although no data were presented on insulin secretion at younger ages, the variant was associated with higher birth weight, suggestive of in utero exposure to high insulin (14).

Figures 5c and 5d further illustrate the loss of the initial beta-cell hyperactivity in the heterozygous carrier. Early hyperinsulinemia suppresses glucose levels, offsetting the increased calcium influx from KATP dysfunction. This glucose suppression signals beta-cells to limit mass expansion and secretory capacity, which prevents hypoglycemia but reduces their ability to compensate as insulin resistance progressively increases. Once insulin secretion becomes insufficient to control glycemia, intracellular calcium levels rise further, initiating Ca2+-mediated downregulation of insulin secretion. This establishes a self-reinforcing cycle wherein hyperglycemia drives additional calcium accumulation, further impairing beta-cell function. After approximately 20 years, plasma glucose rises rapidly.

In contrast, although the non-carrier initially exhibits higher glucose, the lower intracellular calcium affords beta-cells the temporal and functional capacity to respond to the glycemic load by increasing both mass and function. This compensation helps to suppress glucose levels and limits intracellular calcium concentration (Fig. S4a), postponing the synergistic effects of glucotoxicity and Ca2+-dependent suppression of insulin secretion. Consequently, the heterozygous carrier experiences accelerated beta-cell failure and earlier onset of diabetes.

We next examine whether the ABCC8 LOF variant alone can cause diabetes without obesity-induced insulin resistance by varying the rate of rise of excess FFA (denoted X in Fig. 6b). We compare baseline simulations (Figs. 5, 6a) with model trajectories generated with less FFA rise (Figs. 6c, d). The FFA conditions correspond to high (Fig. 6a), moderate (Fig. 6c), and low insulin resistance (Fig. 6d).

Under moderate insulin resistance, the non-carrier remains normoglycemic, while the heterozygous carrier still progresses to diabetes, though at a slower rate (Fig. 6c). When insulin resistance is lowered further, the carrier maintains slightly elevated insulin and suppressed glucose levels over time (Fig. 6d), with no progression to diabetes. Thus, the ABCC8 variant decreases the ability to compensate for insulin resistance but is benign if insulin resistance is modest.

While the previous simulations explored how the heterozygous ABCC8 variant interact with insulin resistance to accelerate diabetes progression, individuals with homozygous ABCC8 variants often present a distinct and more severe phenotype (14, 39, 40). Clinically, these individuals experience hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia in infancy, which may transition into beta-cell failure and overt diabetes already in childhood. To capture this more extreme disease trajectory, we next simulate a homozygous ABCC8 variant scenario.

We modeled the homozygous ABCC8 variant by increasing cytosolic calcium concentration and amplifying Ca2+-dependent downregulation, reflecting severely impaired or nonfunctional KATP channels. A virtual individual was simulated with high insulin sensitivity, low baseline glucose, and elevated baseline insulin secretion, consistent with clinical phenotypes (40). As shown in Fig. 7a, b, this individual exhibits persistent hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia in infancy.

To mitigate hypoglycemia, beta-cells downregulate both mass expansion and secretory capacity over time (Fig. 7c, d). As a result, the hyperinsulinemic and hypoglycemic state resolves with age. However, sustained intracellular Ca2+ levels (Fig. S4a) continue to impair β-cell function, eventually leading to insulin deficiency in the absence of insulin resistance.

In the simulation, impaired fasting glucose emerges at age 7 (Fig. 7a), aligning with reported childhood presentation in homozygous ABCC8 carriers (39). This result suggests that under conditions of extreme insulin hypersecretion, Ca2+-dependent downregulation alone is sufficient to drive β-cell failure and diabetes, independent of external metabolic stressors such as insulin resistance.

Nichols et al. (19) investigated the role of KATP channel activity in the transition from hyperinsulinism to diabetes and proposed an inverse-U relationship (their Figure 1) between beta-cell membrane excitability and secretory response: moderately reduced KATP activity leads to hypersecretion and hyperinsulinism, whereas severely reduced KATP density results in paradoxical undersecretion and the development of diabetes. Our simulated trajectories of beta-cell function vs Ca2+ levels support this inverse-U relationship (Fig. S4b). In the non-carrier, where Ca2+ levels rise from a normal baseline, beta-cell function shows a marked initial increase before declining, highlighting the stimulatory effect of moderate Ca2+ elevation. In contrast, the heterozygous carrier, whose Ca2+ is already elevated, exhibits only a modest rise before beta-cell function decreases. The homozygous carrier, starting with supernormal Ca2+, displays a direct and pronounced decline in beta-cell function as Ca2+ rises further.

Nichols et al. (19) also suggested that inhibition of Ca2+entry might help reverse secretory failure in type 2 diabetes, consistent with the counterintuitively beneficial effects observed with diazoxide treatment (48, 49). To examine this, we simulated Ca2+-reducing therapies across genotypes in SWNA, comparing moderate and intensive reduction (higher doses) initiated at different disease stages.

Figure 8 illustrates the predicted effect of intensive Ca2+-reducing therapy with a KATP channel opener on diabetes progression in a hypothetical heterozygous ABCC8 carrier and a non-carrier, with treatment starting at year 21.5 as the carrier crosses the prediabetes threshold. Both individuals exhibit a transient rise in fasting glucose due to a short-term drop in insulin from enhanced beta-cell KATP activity. As Ca2+ levels fall, beta-cell function recovers via relief of Ca2+-dependent downregulation (Fig. 8d), because, in this case, the benefit of reduced Ca2+ outweighs the harm from transiently elevated glucose. Consequently, insulin halts its decline and rises in both individuals. With lower Ca2+ levels (Fig. S4c), the non-carrier sustains high secretion (Fig. 8b) to counterbalance rising insulin resistance. In contrast, the carrier’s secretion later declines, and deficiency re-emerges as insulin resistance worsens, since Ca2+ remains above normal despite therapy. Overall, the carrier’s progression is delayed by 13.5 years relative to no treatment (Fig. 5), while the non-carrier completely avoids diabetes (Fig. 8a).

When intensive therapy is delayed until diabetes onset (year 22.5; Fig. S5), or when only moderate Ca2+ reduction is applied at prediabetes onset (Fig. S6), the reduction in Ca2+ is insufficient to counteract the adverse impact of the transient glucose elevation in the heterozygous carrier, leading to slightly accelerated disease progression. In the non-carrier, the prediabetes threshold is not reached before year 25, and all three of the above intervention regimens prevent diabetes onset. Notably, in both genotypes, only intensive Ca2+ reduction initiated at the prediabetes stage provides substantial long-term benefit, whereas initiation after diabetes onset slightly worsens progression (Fig. S7).

The importance of both timing and dosage is further highlighted in the homozygous carrier simulations. Moderate Ca2+ reduction initiated at year 5 (before prediabetes onset) accelerates diabetes progression by 8 years compared with no treatment (Fig. 9a vs. Fig. 7a). In contrast, intensive therapy initiated at the same time lowers Ca2+ by an additional 50%, restoring it to the normal range (Fig. S4d). This normalization triggers a sustained rebound in beta-cell function and an increase in insulin secretion without provoking hypersecretion, thereby maintaining glucose within the normal range. When treatment is initiated even earlier, at year 4 (Fig. S8), both moderate and intensive Ca2+ reduction fully restore beta-cell function and preserve euglycemia.

Longitudinal studies of type 2 diabetes (T2D) have revealed marked variability in individual disease trajectories, with wide differences in the onset, rate, and magnitude of changes in glucose and insulin levels (1, 2). Such heterogeneity challenges conventional cross-sectional analyses, underscoring the importance of individualized, mechanism-based approaches. Given the difficulty of obtaining dense longitudinal data in large populations, mathematical modeling offers a powerful alternative to reconstruct full disease trajectories and interrogate the complex dynamics driving diabetes progression.

In this study, we developed a series of diabetes progression models that incorporate multiple physiological mechanisms and alternative assumptions regarding the regulation of glucose and insulin homeostasis. The modeling framework systematically extends the classical Topp model (3), with each component formulated based on experimental and clinical data to ensure physiological relevance. In common with other models in the Topp family, progression to diabetes entails a transition from negative feedback, which maintains glucose homeostasis, to positive feedback driven by glucotoxicity that accelerates beta-cell dysfunction and hyperglycemia. A comprehensive summary of the model formulation and interpretation is provided in the Supplementary Material File S2.

Through iterative model refinement and calibration against longitudinal data from Southwest Native Americans (SWNA), we identified an optimal model that captures the diverse and highly variable trajectories of glucose, insulin, insulin sensitivity, and beta-cell function observed across individuals. Comparison of estimated individual parameter distributions between individuals with prediabetes and diabetes revealed a significant difference only in one parameter, which is related to how much glucotoxicity impairs beta-cell function. This suggests that while insulin resistance is necessary, the transition to T2D is critically dependent on impaired beta-cell function, with individual susceptibility to glucotoxicity playing a central role in determining progression rates.

The major distinction between our modeling framework and previous diabetes progression models (such as those in (9, 28, 50)) lies in the explicit incorporation of individualized free fatty acid (FFA) profiles (X) into the regulation of insulin sensitivity and beta-cell function. This extension enables the model to capture heterogeneity in lipid-driven effects that cannot be represented by population-averaged formulations and provides a mechanistic basis for the wide diversity of disease trajectories observed across individuals (Fig. 2, 3).

Having established the modeling framework, we used it to address a critical debate in the diabetes field, whether hyperinsulinemia is a compensation for insulin resistance (13, 28) or a primary initiating event in the pathogenesis of T2D (12, 46, 47). First, we demonstrated that our base model, in which insulin resistance is primary and hyperinsulinemia is compensatory, can account for a substantial rise in insulin without a meaningful rise in glucose when the development of insulin resistance is slow (Fig. 4). The slower the onset of insulin resistance, the smaller the rise in glucose. This has also been shown with the even simpler Topp model (3), indicating that this is a fundamental property of this class of models. Moreover, faster declines in insulin sensitivity elicit stronger beta-cell responses but result in a lower disposition index (Fig. S3e), revealing that elevated insulin levels can coexist with impaired regulatory function. Thus, the presence of elevated insulin levels in the absence of hyperglycemia during early stages does not, by itself, support the primary hyperinsulinemia hypothesis.

Clinical data have shown that a rise in glucose can occur without apparent changes in insulin sensitivity (1). This observation challenges the assumption that insulin resistance is always necessary for the progression of typical T2D. However, the trajectories of glucose, insulin, and excessive free fatty acids (denoted X) in hypothetical non-carriers (Fig. 6a, b), reveal that the upsurge in fasting glucose occurred after FFA and thus insulin resistance had reached the plateau. This suggests that prior exposure to insulin resistance had already disrupted the glucose regulatory system, such that no additional trigger was required to drive disease onset.

We then extended the optimal model to study an SWNA subgroup carrying an ABCC8 loss-of-function variant associated with undisputed primary hyperinsulinemia. The extended model successfully recapitulated the clinically observed 7-year earlier onset of T2D in R1420H carriers (14) (Fig. 5). The key addition to the model was the assumption that chronic elevation of cytosolic calcium downregulates beta-cell function. Without this assumption, the hyperinsulinemia caused by the variant was insufficient by itself to accelerate T2D onset or cause a transition from hypersecretion to hyposecretion (not shown). This was the case in spite of the inclusion of insulin-induced insulin resistance in the model, another important element of the primary hyperinsulinemia hypothesis (17, 46). Insulin-induced insulin resistance did improve model performance as assessed by the BIC, but its contribution was quantitatively modest and not essential for fitting the data (Fig. M8 or S10).

The model further predicts that the heterozygous ABCC8 variant is largely benign in the absence of insulin resistance, as insulin secretion can maintain normoglycemia under low metabolic demand. However, when insulin resistance is present, the limited compensatory capacity of beta-cells under chronic calcium stress leads to earlier beta-cell failure and accelerated hyperglycemia (Fig. 6). In contrast, the homozygous ABCC8 variant, which causes severe calcium dysregulation, can drive diabetes onset even without insulin resistance, as demonstrated in the simulation of early-onset diabetes (Fig. 7). Collectively, these findings suggest that insulin hypersecretion may worsen dysglycemia but is not sufficient by itself to initiate diabetes; rather, the interplay between insulin resistance and beta-cell vulnerability to glucotoxicity determines disease outcome.

This work extends understanding from mouse models of LOF KATP channel variants and KATP channel knockouts, distinguishing between metabolic stress from high glucose metabolism (termed hyperstimulation) and calcium stress from low KATP activity (termed hyperexcitability) (19, 36). Hyperexcitability induces Ca2+-dependent downregulation of insulin secretion and transition from hypoglycemia to hyperglycemia. We have quantified these effects with a model for the first time, with or without insulin resistance, showing plausible mechanisms underlying the previously described inverse-U relationship between β-cell excitability and insulin secretion (19). The simulations also support Ca2+-reducing therapies, but their efficacy depends critically on both timing and dosage. Only sufficiently intensive reduction of Ca2+ prior to diabetes onset yielded durable benefits in the simulations, as the protective effect of relieving Ca2+-dependent downregulation needs to outweigh the harm of elevated glucose. Once diabetes is established, however, reducing Ca2+ alone is unable to disrupt the self-reinforcing cycle of glucotoxicity and beta-cell dysfunction. Supplying exogenous insulin might help in that case.

The heterogeneous responses predicted under Ca2+-reducing therapies mirror the mixed clinical results observed with diazoxide in people with diabetes (51–53). Notably, the model suggests that early intervention in euglycemic ABCC8 variant carriers (Figs. S8, S9) may prepare beta-cells to better withstand the progressive insulin resistance of later life. At the same time, the optimal dosage and timing of Ca2+-reducing therapy remain challenging to determine in clinical practice, and our simulations are not intended to predict individual patient outcomes, which depend on many additional physiological and environmental factors beyond the scope of the model. Rather, these results provide a mechanistic rationale for genotype-guided, stage-specific interventions that verbal reasoning alone cannot establish.

Strengths of our study include the use of a longitudinal data set and a systematic model development approach balancing fidelity to the data and parsimony. Key regulatory factors often omitted in prior models, including free fatty acids (FFAs), dynamic hepatic glucose production (HGP), and reduced insulin clearance, were incorporated and contributed meaningfully to model performance. Modeling insulin sensitivity as a function of both adiposity and age allowed the model to account for inter-individual variability more effectively than treating insulin sensitivity as an independent function of time as in other models. Incorporating dynamic HGP regulation by insulin and FFAs provided a physiological link between peripheral and hepatic insulin resistance, while modeling insulin clearance as a function of insulin concentration further enhanced model precision.

We were limited to use of fasting glucose and insulin, as inclusion of the rapid dynamics during the OGTTs in long-term longitudinal simulations proved computationally challenging. We did, however, take the OGTT data into account partially in estimating insulin sensitivity with the Matsuda index. We did not model individual glucose rate of appearance, and we did not have sufficient data to include incretin or glucagon effects. In the ABCC8 simulations we did not have access to individual time series, only the group means. The SWNA data set used to establish the model was invaluable for showing the acceleration of progression to T2D in those who had the variant in ABCC8 but lacked data supporting the model finding that individuals transitioned from hypoglycemia in infancy to hyperglycemia later in life. However, there are numerous other reports in the literature of this sequence of events in people with ABCC8 variants; see (37, 39, 40) and the references in (54). Finally, while we believe that the general findings from the SWNA data will be broadly applicable, they may not generalize in detail to other populations. These aspects warrant further investigation and remain important directions for future work.

In conclusion, the present work represents a significant step forward in quantitatively dissecting the heterogeneous natural history of T2D. By systematically integrating multiple physiological mechanisms, including factors often overlooked in previous models, and validating the framework against rich individualized longitudinal data, we provide a mechanistic platform capable of capturing individualized disease trajectories. The model results enhance understanding of the complex interplay between insulin resistance, beta-cell function, and genetic predisposition. Notably, they cast doubt on the hypothesis that primary hypersecretion is important in typical obesity-related T2D pathogenesis or, when clearly present as in LOF ABCC8 variants, is capable of causing T2D in the absence of insulin resistance unless it is severe. The approach also offers a flexible framework for future integration with pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic frameworks to support therapeutic development and personalized intervention strategies.
