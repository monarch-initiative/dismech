# TUBA1A-related tubulinopathy associated with the infantile epileptic spasms syndrome and atypical absence seizures.
**Authors:** Ng AC, Scantlebury MH
**Journal:** Epileptic Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/epd2.70072](https://doi.org/10.1002/epd2.70072)

## Content

Patients with TUBA1A pathogenic variants may present with complex brain malformation, intellectual disability, and epilepsy. The epilepsy phenotype is varied, ranging from mild to severe, with epileptic spasms and focal seizures being the most common seizure types. We report on an infant with a TUBA1A variant presenting with an evolving epileptic encephalopathy. Initially, this patient presented with infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, with the eventual appearance of atypical absence seizures before the age of 1 year. We discuss the initial diagnostic uncertainty that was raised by the finding of early onset absence epilepsy and review the other TUBA1A-related seizure types reported in the literature.

Tubulinopathy refers to a heterogeneous group of disorders due to pathogenic variants in genes encoding tubulin and is characterized by complex brain malformations and other neurological conditions. The family of tubulin genes includes alpha, beta, gamma, delta, and epsilon. At least 15 and 12 genes encode tubulin alpha and beta, respectively. Alpha and beta tubulin assemble into heterodimers to form microtubules, which are part of the cytoskeletal fibers essential for mitosis, axonal transport, and neuronal proliferation and migration. Although TUBA1A, located on chromosome 12q13.12, is the most common mutated gene in tubulinopathies, pathogenic variants in TUBA8, TUBB2A, TUBB2B, TUBB3, TUBB5, and TUBG1 have also been associated with complex brain malformations.
1



Brain malformations in TUBA1A‐related tubulinopathy include lissencephaly (agyria‐pachygyria), polymicrogyria, agenesis or dysgenesis of the corpus callosum, dysmorphic basal ganglia, and cerebellar hypoplasia.
1
 Craniofacial dysmorphism, severe intellectual disability, and epilepsy are also common. In terms of the expression of the epilepsy phenotype, seizure type, electroencephalography findings, and response to treatment are highly variable. Here, we report a patient with a TUBA1A pathogenic variant presenting with an evolving epileptic encephalopathy, characterized by epileptic spasms and atypical absence seizure. We present MRI and electroencephalographic findings and treatment responses. The patient's family provided informed consent for the generation of this report.

The index patient was born to non‐consanguineous parents of Caucasian descent. The pregnancy was conceived by in vitro fertilization by the intracytoplasmic sperm injection method. The patient's mother was a 37‐year‐old, G2P0 woman and has a history of polycystic ovarian syndrome. The first pregnancy ended in a spontaneous loss at 18 weeks' gestation. The patient received extensive prenatal diagnostic investigations. Fetal magnetic resonance imaging at 31 weeks of gestation showed mild ventriculomegaly and suspected agenesis of the corpus callosum. Growth parameters met criteria for large for gestational age (abdominal circumference 97th percentile, head circumference 55th percentile, estimated fetal weight 90%–95% percentile). The delivery at 38 weeks and 4 days was via caesarean section due to breech presentation positioning. APGAR scores were 6 and 7 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. Birth weight and head circumference were 3.34 kg (64.2 percentile) and 33 cm (25.7 percentile), respectively.

Brain magnetic response imaging on day 1 of life confirmed agenesis of corpus callosum with accompanying colpocephaly (Figure 1). Furthermore, basal ganglia appeared dysmorphic with small thalami. There was no evidence of malformation of cortical development.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging at 1 day of age. T2 axial sequences demonstrate fused and dysmorphic basal ganglia and thalami, agenesis of corpus callosum, and colpocephaly.

At 2 months of age, the patient developed flexion epileptic spasms that clustered. Routine electroencephalogram at 5 months of age showed abundant multifocal epileptiform discharges that were predominant over the left posterior head region with medium to high amplitude background slowing. Interictal EEG findings are consistent with a developing epileptic encephalopathy but did not meet the criteria of hypsarrhythmia (BASED score of 3).
2
 No epileptic spasms were captured on routine EEGs. Physical examination demonstrated normal alertness, non‐dysmorphia features, hypotonia, absence of visual tracking, and no organomegaly. She met the criteria of infantile epileptic spasms syndrome due to the presence of epileptic spasms and developmental regression.
3
 Vigabatrin treatment improved epileptiform burden and abated epileptic spasms. However, by 10 months of age, EEG worsened and was characterized by near‐continuous, independent bilateral occipital epileptiform discharges. EEG background demonstrated left posterior predominant disorganized high amplitude slowing (Figure 2A). Vigabatrin dose was further increased. The family declined initiating prednisolone therapy. At 11 months of age, the patient developed atypical absence seizures characterized by brief (8–13 s) staring spells, subtle eye roll, and with eyelid flickering. EEG captured atypical absence seizures characterized by rhythmic, high amplitude, 2.4 Hz left frontal predominant bilateral spike and wave discharges and interictal high amplitude posterior predominant spikes and background (Figure 2B).

(B) EEG performed at 11 months of age demonstrating an asymmetric atypical absence seizure. Given the development of atypical absence seizure in an infant, a diagnosis of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome was initially entertained. Valproic acid was started at 9 months of age, leading to a reduced burden of atypical absence seizures. Ketogenic diet (3:1) was initiated at 13 months of age, which led to a complete resolution of seizures 4 months later. We were unable to start ethosuximide due to the high sugar content of the liquid formulation, which would negatively affect ketonemia induced by the ketogenic diet. The ketogenic diet however did not lead to normalization of the EEG, which still showed multifocal epileptiform abnormalities over the bilateral occipital head regions, slow background, and resolution of electroclinical seizures (Figure 2C).

Genetics studies revealed a normal microarray. A comprehensive epilepsy panel covering 474 genes revealed a TUBA1A heterozygous pathogenic variant c.641G>A, p.(Arg214His). The patient was also found to possess a SMARCA2 c.3017T>C, p.(Met1006Thr), a variant of uncertain significance. The patient tested negative for the SLC2A1 pathologic variant, known to cause GLUT1 deficiency syndrome and early onset absence seizures.

We presented a case of a TUBA1A‐related tubulinopathy presenting with a constellation of symptoms that included atypical absence seizures, a finding that raised diagnostic uncertainty. Clinically, absence seizures can be difficult to detect in infants due to the subtle nature of behavioral arrests in this age group. The patient's parents reported a definite resolution of epileptic spasms upon commencing vigabatrin, only to report new absence seizures characterized by brief eyelid fluttering and nystagmus, associated with slow, rhythmic spike‐wave on the EEG. No specific epilepsy syndrome is attributed to all tubulinopathies.
4
 Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome is the most common epilepsy syndrome in tubulinopathies and manifests in the first year of life.
5
 Focal seizure is also a common seizure type due to tubulinopathies, and the most frequent type of epileptogenic lesion is likely lissencephaly and polymicrogyria.
5
 The severity of epilepsy may range from mild to drug‐resistant, with TUBA1A often demonstrating a more severe phenotype than TUBB2B.
4
, 
5
 Overall, 64.7% of patients with TUBA1A demonstrate drug‐resistant epilepsy.
5



Our patient presented with epileptic spasms and electroencephalogram findings characterized by high amplitude background slowing and bilateral independent occipital spikes consistent with infantile epileptic spasms syndrome. However, the interictal findings did not meet the spike burden to fit the definition of classical hypsarrhythmia.
2
 Nevertheless, the presence of epileptic spasms and developmental regression enabled us to make the diagnosis of infantile epileptic spasms syndrome.
3
 Although epileptic spasms resolved with vigabatrin treatment, a new seizure type, characterized by slow (2 Hz) rhythmic spike wave runs associated with behavioral arrest and eye roll was seen. These were best characterized as atypical absence seizures. Absence seizures are rare in tubulinopathies, appearing in only 5.0% (2 in 40) of patients in one series of TUBA1A patients and none (0 in 11) of TUBB2B patients.
5
 Another series reported absence seizures in 6% of patients with TUBA1A.
4
 However, the age of onset of absence seizures in these series is unknown, and it is unclear whether these were typical or atypical absence due to a lack of detailed description of electroclinical phenotypes. Generation of absence seizures is thought to involve abnormal thalamocortical interactions. Mechanistic studies indicate that absence seizures are likely driven by a cortical focus. Dysfunctional communication between the cortex and thalamus is necessary for the spread, synchronization, and prolongation of the discharges leading to a seizure.
6
 Interestingly, subtle cortical brain malformations have been reported in some patients with absence seizures that may be causative.
7
, 
8
, 
9
 It is also possible that the dysmorphic thalami may contribute to the generation of atypical absence seizures in our patient. The combination of brain and thalamic structural abnormalities may account for the early age of onset of atypical absences in our patient.

The extremely early onset of absence seizures initially raised the possibility of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome as 12% of patients under the age of four presenting with absence seizures are found to have GLUT1 deficiency syndrome.
10
 However, no pathogenic variant was found in the SLC2A1 gene, and cerebrospinal fluid testing was not performed due to the invasiveness of this test.

The ketogenic diet was the treatment that resulted in the resolution of her electroclinical absence seizures and the improvement of the epileptiform abnormalities of EEG. The ketogenic diet, a high‐fat, low‐carbohydrate diet, has been used to treat drug‐resistant epilepsy. Multiple mechanisms of action have been implicated in the efficacy of the ketogenic diet, including direct antiseizure effects of ketone bodies, enhancing GABA inhibitory neurotransmission, improving mitochondrial function, and reducing inflammation.
11
 These diverse effects synergize to reduce seizures and epileptogenesis, as well as provide neuroprotection. Whether the ketogenic diet interacts directly with the pathogenesis of tubulinopathy is unknown.

The TUBA1A c.641G>A p.Arg214His is a recurrent variant found in patients with a range of brain malformations. The Arg214 residue is located in the intermediate domain of the TUBA1A protein, and the Arg214His variant is predicted to negatively affect interactions of microtubule protofilaments.
12
, 
13
 A pathologic study on a 23‐week gestational fetus with the TUBA1A c.641G>A variant from a terminated pregnancy revealed central polymicrogyria and complete agenesis of the corpus callosum.
14
 Another patient with the same variant presented with agenesis of the corpus callosum, Dandy‐Walker variant syndrome, cerebellar vermis hypoplasia, ventricular dilatation, dysgenesis of the basal ganglia, and unilateral optic nerve hypoplasia.
13
 This patient had focal to bilateral tonic‐clonic seizures with onset at 23 months of age. Therefore, the same c.641G>A variant can manifest with phenotypic pleiotropy.

In summary, we described a patient with TUBA1A‐related tubulinopathy presenting with infantile epileptic spasms syndrome and atypical absence seizures. To our knowledge, this is the first report of an infantile‐onset, atypical absence seizure in a patient with tubulinopathy. Thus, neuroimaging and genetic testing may be important in patients with early onset absence seizures to identify tubulinopathies. Finally, our findings suggest that a ketogenic diet may be effective in patients with epilepsy related to tubulinopathies.
