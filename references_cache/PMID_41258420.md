# Paired tumor-normal sequencing provides insights into the CDKN2A-associated tumor spectrum.
**Authors:** Breen KE, Mehta N, Ravichandran V, Mehine M, Gao C, Fiala E, Mukherjee S, Gao M, Arora K, Kemel Y, Roth S, Arnold AG, Kennedy J, Sheehan M, Murphy F, Ptashkin R, Lin A, Walsh M, Ceyhan-Birsoy O, Tap WD, Rolston V, Murciano-Goroff YR, Latham A, Stadler ZK, Offit K, Mandelker D
**Journal:** NPJ Precis Oncol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41698-025-01155-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41698-025-01155-6)

## Content

Germline pathogenic variants (PV) in CDKN2A are characterized by an increased risk for melanoma, pancreatic cancer, nervous system tumors, and additional cancer types. We sought to define the tumor and clinical characteristics in a cohort of cancer patients with CDKN2A PV. Amongst 71,868 patients, 69 (0.1%) had a CDKN2A PV. Of these, 87.0% (n = 60), 7.2% (n = 5), and 5.8% (n = 4) had a PV impacting p16INK4A, p14ARF, or both p16INK4A and p14ARF, respectively. Biallelic inactivation of CDKN2A was observed in 33/47 (70.2%) of tumors assessed. Most tumors (28/38, 73.7%) in patients with p16INK4A PV demonstrated biallelic inactivation, including non-classic tumors such as lung cancer and osteosarcoma. Approximately half (5/9, 55.6%) of tumors in patients where p14ARF was impacted demonstrated biallelic inactivation, including peripheral nervous system tumors. This analysis supports a potential role of CDKN2A in the development of tumors beyond melanoma and pancreatic cancer and may have important implications for surveillance recommendations.

CDKN2A (cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A) is a tumor suppressor gene involved in cell cycle regulation. Germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants (PV) in CDKN2A that disrupt the p16INK4A protein cause Familial Atypical Multiple Mole Melanoma (FAMMM) syndrome, associated with an increased risk for cutaneous melanoma, dysplastic nevi, and pancreatic cancer. While the penetrance varies between individuals and families with CDKN2A PV, cumulative risks are estimated to be 58–92%3 and 17–19%4,5 for melanoma and pancreatic cancer, respectively. Due to this significantly elevated risk, individuals with FAMMM are recommended to have regular dermatologic examinations and consider undergoing pancreas cancer surveillance, with acknowledgment of its current limitations6.

While the majority of PV in CDKN2A are predicted to impact p16INK4A, less frequent PV in CDKN2A that disrupt both p16INK4A and p14ARF protein have been identified as a predisposition to melanoma and neural system tumors, which has been referred to as Melanoma-Astrocytoma Syndrome7–9. The spectrum of reported tumors in p14ARF PV carriers includes melanoma but is otherwise not well-defined10.

Recent reviews have described a wide spectrum of tumors in CDKN2A PV carriers and subsequently inferred genotype-phenotype relationships9,11,12. However, prior studies have not comprehensively interrogated the tumor data in individuals with CDKN2A PV, limiting the ability to distinguish between CDKN2A germline-driven tumors and sporadic tumors in CDKN2A PV carriers. Consequently, there are no clear consensus guidelines on recommended surveillance for individuals with various CDKN2A PV. Here, we report the tumor and clinical characteristics in a pan-cancer cohort of patients found to harbor CDKN2A germline PV via cancer-agnostic paired tumor/normal testing.

Amongst a total of 71,868 patients who underwent paired tumor/normal sequencing, 69 (0.1%) had a PV in CDKN2A. Most patients (60/69, 87.0%) had a PV predicted to impact only p16INK4A function. However, 7.2% (5/69) of patients had a PV predicted to impact only p14ARF, and 5.8% (4/54) of patients had a PV predicted to impact both p16INK4A and p14ARF. Over half of patients were female (n = 38, 55.1%), and most of the cohort was of European ancestry (n = 57, 82.6%). The most common type of alteration were missense variants (n = 33, 47.8%) followed by null variants (n = 17, 24.6%). Gross deletions were only observed when p14ARF (n = 1, 1.4%) or both p14ARF and p16INK4A (n = 3, 4.3%) were impacted. The median age of cancer diagnosis, which was considered the age at diagnosis of the earliest sequenced tumor, and smoking history were only available for Cohort 1. Median age of cancer diagnosis for Cohort 1 was 59 years, ranging from age 2 to 76. Approximately two-thirds of patients in Cohort 1 (n = 32/54, 59.3%) were never-smokers. A total of 31 unique variants were identified in 69 patients upon utilizing the 2015 classification framework of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) that was modified to reflect nuances of the CDKN2A gene (Supplementary Table 2). In the context of p16INK4A, 16 variants were classified as pathogenic, and 11 variants were classified as likely pathogenic. In the context of p14ARF, 4 variants were classified as pathogenic, and 5 variants were classified as likely pathogenic. Note that 3/4 gross deletions were considered pathogenic in both isoforms and 1 deletion was considered pathogenic and likely pathogenic in p16INK4A and p14ARF, respectively. Variants, classification, and evidence codes are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Using our CDKN2A-specific variant classification framework, a total of 5/24 (21%) variants were reclassified into a different tier. Two of these variants – c.58G>C/p.(Ala20Pro) and c.143C>G/p.(Pro48Arg) in p16INK4A carried by one patient each – would have otherwise been considered variants of uncertain significance (VUS) per standard ACMG/AMP guidelines with updated recommendations by the ClinGen SVI. The additional evidence not fully captured by the standard recommendations for classification were primarily clinical features and tumor data of multiple index patients. Specifically for p.(Ala20Pro), there were multiple probands with pancreatic cancer and a strong family history of pancreatic cancer and melanoma, which was considered supportive evidence. For p.(Pro48Arg), there were numerous probands with pancreatic cancer, some of whom also had a family history of pancreatic cancer and/or melanoma. Additionally, there was at least one pancreatic tumor that demonstrated loss of heterozygosity (LOH), and the variant was located in the functionally important ankyrin 2 domain.

Of the 73 tumors in 69 patients, 37.0% (27/73) were FAMMM-associated tumors: pancreas (n = 19/73, 26.0%) or melanoma (n = 8/73, 11.0%), while 6.8% (n = 5/73) were peripheral/central nervous system tumors (PNS/CNS): PNS (n = 3/73, 4.1%) or CNS (n = 2/73, 2.7%) tumors (Supplementary Table 1). The majority of the pancreatic tumors or melanomas (n = 26/27, 96.3%) occurred in individuals with a variant impacting p16INK4A. All of the PNS tumors occurred in individuals with variants predicted to impact p14ARF or both isoforms, while the two CNS tumors both occurred in individuals with variants impacting p16INK4A only. The remaining tumor types in the overall cohort (n = 41/73, 56.2%) were not classically associated with either CDKN2A phenotype. Notably, sarcomas constituted 8.2% of the overall cohort (n = 6/73).

Acquired CDKN2A alterations were identified in two samples (n = 2/73, 2.7%). Of the 47 tumors that could be assessed for both LOH and acquired alterations, 70.2% (n = 33) demonstrated biallelic inactivation (Fig. 2). Most tumors in the p16INK4A-only cohort (73.7%, n = 28/38 tumors with available data) and the cohort with both isoforms impacted (75%, n = 3/4) demonstrated biallelic inactivation compared to two tumors in the p14ARF-only cohort (40%%, n = 2/5) (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). Most of the pancreatic tumors (85.7%, n = 7/8), all of the melanomas (100%, n = 5/5), nerve sheath tumors (100%, n = 3/3), and CNS tumors (n = 2/2) demonstrated biallelic inactivation; one patient’s glioblastoma (Cohort 1.33) acquired two somatic alterations (c.304del/p.(Ala102Argfs*44) and c.80_82del/p.(Glu27del)) in p16INK4A, the former of which is considered likely oncogenic and represents biallelic inactivation. Sarcomas are not classically associated with germline CDKN2A PV, with the exception of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST). Of the sarcomas, 3/4 (2 osteosarcomas and 1 myxofibrosarcoma) had LOH in the tumor, while one soft tissue sarcoma did not. Additional non-classic tumor types that demonstrated biallelic inactivation included lung (n = 4/4), prostate (n = 3/5), uterine (n = 2/2), and stomach (n = 1/1) cancers. Thus, most of the FAMMM-associated or PNS/CNS tumors (n = 17/18, 94.4%) had biallelic inactivation, and almost half of the non-classic tumors (n = 16/29, 55.2%) had biallelic inactivation. CDKN2A PV were collectively enriched in cancer cases versus Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) v2 non-cancer controls (OR = 4.017, 95% CI: 2.447–6.838, p < 0.0001) and gnomAD v4 controls (OR = 2.641, 95% CI: 2.002–3.442, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table 5A). Tumor-specific analysis was sufficiently powered for pancreatic cancer, prostate cancer, sarcoma (osteosarcoma, soft tissue sarcoma, and MPNST), and breast cancer (Supplementary Table 5B). CDKN2A PV were enriched in pancreatic cancer (gnomAD v2: OR = 16.720, 95% CI: 8.454–32.704, p < 0.0001; gnomAD v4: OR = 11.105, 95% CI: 6.589–17.653, p < 0.0001), prostate cancer (gnomAD v2: OR = 5.340, 95% CI: 1.771–13.584, p = 0.0020; gnomAD v4: OR = 3.358, 95% CI: 1.222–7.398, p = 0.0105), and sarcomas (gnomAD v2: OR = 9.058, 95% CI: 2.679–24.536, p < 0.0001; gnomAD v4: OR = 6.835, 95% CI: 2.488–15.068, p = 0.0003). However, CDKN2A PV were only enriched in breast cancer when compared to the gnomAD v2 non-cancer cohort (OR = 3.885, 95% CI: 1.149–10.515, p = 0.0150) but not the gnomAD v4 cohort (OR = 2.442, 95% CI: 0.787–5.765, p = 0.0583).

Overall, 34/54 (63%) patients in Cohort 1 had a personal history of a FAMMM-associated or a PNS/CNS tumor, either as the primary tumor analyzed or in the reported medical history (Supplementary Table 1). Of the 29 patients with a personal history of a FAMMM-associated phenotype, 26 (90%) had a variant impacting p16INK4A only. Both of the patients with CNS tumors had a variant impacting p16INK4A only. All 3 patients with a PNS tumor had a variant impacting p14ARF only or both isoforms. Of the 20 patients without a personal history consistent with either of these phenotypes, 17 (85%) had a reported family history of a FAMMM-associated or PNS/CNS tumor.

Overall, 80.8% (n = 42/52) of patients had a family history of a FAMMM-associated or a PNS/CNS tumor. The majority of patients (n = 31/35, 88.6%) with a family history of a FAMMM-associated tumor had a variant impacting p16INK4A. A little over half of patients (n = 4/7, 57.1%) with a family history of a PNS/CNS tumor had a variant impacting p14ARF only or both isoforms. Almost 10% (n = 5/54, 9.3%) of patients did not have a personal or family history of CDKN2A-associated tumors, and all had variants impacting p16INK4A only.

While a broad range of cancers have been reported in individuals with germline pathogenic variants in CDKN2A, it was unclear what additional tumor types may be driven by CDKN2A13–15. This is the first known study to utilize paired tumor/normal sequencing and assessment of biallelic inactivation to demonstrate that 61.8% of tumors that arise in CDKN2A PV carriers outside the FAMMM spectrum (including sarcomas, lung cancer, and possibly prostate cancer) have evidence of being driven by the germline alteration. Furthermore, of the evaluable tumor types in the association analyses, CDKN2A PV were statistically enriched in patients with pancreatic cancer, prostate cancer, and sarcomas. We therefore propose a renaming of both FAMMM and Melanoma-Astrocytoma Syndrome to a more encompassing nomenclature: CDKN2A-Associated Tumor Syndrome.

Sarcomas constituted ~11.0% (8/73) of the tumors in our overall cohort. Two of the sarcomas were MPNST, a tumor that has previously been associated with Melanoma-Astrocytoma Syndrome phenotype7–9. Notably, the 2 osteosarcoma cases both had variants impacting p16INK4A-only and demonstrated LOH (previously reported16), as did the myxofibrosarcoma in an individual who also had MPNST with a variant impacting both isoforms. A recent study identified an enrichment of germline CDKN2A variants predicted to only impact p16INK4A (n = 12, 1.2%) in a large cohort of individuals with osteosarcoma17. CDKN2A germline variants have also been identified in individuals across eight families affected with various types of sarcomas, including multiple osteosarcomas, and LOH was confirmed in an osteosarcoma with the germline c.194T>C/p.(Leu65Pro) variant18. Our findings in concert with recent literature suggest a role for germline CDKN2A variants in driving osteosarcoma tumorigenesis. Therefore, individuals with a personal or family history of sarcoma should consider germline testing for CDKN2A as part of a hereditary cancer susceptibility panel testing, and unaffected individuals with a CDKN2A PV may consider participating in a surveillance program including whole-body magnetic resonance imaging (WBMRI) for sarcoma screening, particularly in the setting of a family history of sarcoma. While there are limitations to detecting these tumor types using these imaging modalities, long-term surveillance has demonstrated clinical utility in patients with other high-risk hereditary cancer syndromes19–21.

Interestingly, the two CNS tumors in our cohort that were likely driven by CDKN2A were identified in individuals with p16INK4A variants. This finding is supported by multiple reports in the literature of glioblastoma in families with germline variants impacting p16INK4A only, as well as in families with impact to both p16INK4A and p14ARF, with ages of diagnosis ranging from 14 to 70 years9,10,12,22. Additional LOH data on these tumors and more robust case/control studies will be critical in addressing the obvious challenge of when to recommend surveillance (brain MRI) for unaffected for individuals harboring PV impacting p16INK4A only, but findings from our study suggest that these tumors are unlikely to be sporadic in origin. CNS tumors remain a risk with loss of both isoforms and support a role for surveillance, regardless of family history.

This study is one of the first to clarify which non-classic tumors are likely to be sporadic in CDKN2A PV carriers. We note that previous studies have suggested an association with breast cancer9,23,24. However, only 2/5 breast tumors with next-generation sequencing (NGS) and Fraction and Allele-Specific Copy Number Estimates from Tumor Sequencing (FACETS) available in this study demonstrated LOH, and one of those patients with a PV impacting p14ARF only also harbored a PV in BRCA1, with the tumor demonstrating a homologous recombination deficient mutation signature. Interestingly, CDKN2A PV were also enriched in breast cancer cases when compared to non-cancer controls in gnomAD v2, but this association did not replicate when compared the larger gnomAD v4 control cohort. While it does not seem that there is enough evidence to provide increased breast cancer risk management based solely on a CDKN2A PV, larger studies will be informative in confirming this finding. Some non-classic tumors such as two uterine cancers and a stomach cancer demonstrated LOH in this study, but more robust research with larger cohorts is needed to establish a clear risk. Lastly, other studies have reported a higher risk for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and lung cancer, especially among smokers10,11. We did not identify any HNSCC in our cohort, which may be due to the low number of individuals undergoing germline testing for these cancer types at our institution. However, counseling regarding these smoking-related diseases in CDKN2A carriers is recommended given previous study data.

For individuals with p16INK4A-only variants, standard dermatologic and pancreas surveillance measures are recommended3,6,25,26. However, if there is a family history of sarcoma and/or glioblastoma, additional screening with whole-body and brain MRI could be considered with acknowledgement of its current limitations. For individuals with p14ARF-only variants, although melanoma was the only statistically significant association in one study10, it is reasonable to consider WBMRI based on more recent reports of individuals with peripheral and central NST (sarcoma, multiple neurofibromas, schwannomas, and a brain tumor diagnosed in adolescence)12. Furthermore, the individual with a p14ARF-only variant from this cohort had an MPNST exhibiting LOH and two prior nerve sheath tumors diagnosed in the 30 s. Therefore, the NST phenotype may not be exclusive to individuals with variants impacting both isoforms, although penetrance may vary. For individuals with both isoforms impacted, dermatologic and pancreas are reasonable, as well as whole-body MRI regardless of family history given the established risk for central and peripheral NST. Longitudinal surveillance should incorporate reassessment of family history over time, co-segregation of the variant, and confirmed pathologies of reported cancers in the family. CDKN2A is one of a few genes with completely distinct, clinically relevant isoforms that function in different signaling pathways, and there are no gene-specific guidelines for variant classification (https://cspec.genome.network/cspec/ui/svi/). Given the potential for different phenotypic presentation based on whether a variant affects the function of p16INK4A, p14ARF, or both isoforms, we proposed an adapted framework to the ACMG/AMP guidelines, such that variants could be separately interpreted in the context of each isoform. Notably, we allowed for the counting of specific but different cancer cases towards PS4_strength depending on the isoform(s) affected, we incorporated biallelic inactivation in the specified tumors when available, and we defined critical functional regions for each isoform. The CDKN2A-modified framework resulted in upgrading the classification of two variants that would otherwise have been considered non-actionable. In addition, we demonstrated that variant classifications were typically discordant between isoforms, suggesting that an isoform-specific framework is more informative. The curation framework utilized here may help establish a standardized variant classification methodology for this gene that ultimately could have implications for surveillance recommendations.

Limitations of this study include the small number of individuals carrying a CDKN2A PV impacting p14ARF. Furthermore, the patient population was collected from a cohort of affected patients undergoing matched tumor/normal sequencing at a single cancer institution, and therefore the cohort is limited primarily to individuals with advanced cancers and primarily of European ancestry. Additionally, where available, patients’ histories of prior diagnoses and their family histories of cancer are largely self-reported and unconfirmed by pathology. Short-read sequencing also has the limitation of being able to phase germline and somatic alterations if they do not occur within ~100 bp, such as seen in one patient in our cohort with a glioblastoma (Cohort 1.33). In addition, undetectable somatic alterations in tumors contributing to biallelic inactivation cannot be excluded based on the technical limitations of the assay27. Additionally, an alternate driver cannot be ruled out. For example, some melanoma cases in demonstrated an ultraviolet mutational signature (Cohorts 1.5 and 1.40), a neurofibroma had two somatic NF1 drivers (Cohort 1.54), a homologous recombination deficient ovarian cancer was more likely to be primarily driven by a germline RAD51D PV (Cohort 1.4), and a squamous cell carcinoma of the conjunctiva acquired an FGFR1 amplification (Cohort 1.17). However, multiple drivers often contribute to tumorigenesis28–30. Finally, the association analyses relied upon publicly available data for controls. Participants who underwent exome sequencing from the gnomAD v2 cohort were largely included in the gnomAD v4 cohort, but the precise amount of overlap between v4 and the v2 non-cancer subset is unclear. Therefore, differences in the OR between the v2 non-cancer and the v4 total cohorts may be due to differences in their compositions and/or the robustness of the larger v4 cohort, which may provide a better estimate of the prevalence of pathogenic variants in the general population. In addition, differences in sequencing methodologies and/or bioinformatic pipelines between the sequenced populations may limit the reliability of these findings.

Challenges remain in identifying which patients with CDKN2A variants are at risk for which tumor types, due to varying penetrance, unclear genotype/phenotype correlations, and small study numbers. However, this study utilized matched tumor/normal sequencing analysis to support the role of CDKN2A in the development of tumors beyond those classically associated with FAMMM, including nerve sheath tumors and sarcomas. These findings may have important implications for screening and management recommendations. Furthermore, patients with germline or somatic CDKN2A alterations may be eligible for targeted therapies, such as CDK4/6 inhibitors, which have been approved for use in some hormone receptor-positive breast cancers (regardless of CDKN2A status) (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2023/207103s017s018lbl.pdf, https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2023/209092s016lbl.pdf, https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2024/212526s009lbl.pdf) and investigated in preclinical and early trials for pancreatic cancer, sarcomas, melanoma, and others31–35. This study, in addition to previous reports, supports the need for greater awareness of the broader spectrum of disease and the isoform impact when interpreting a genetic testing report.

Smoking histories were self-reported and abstracted from the medical record. Patients were considered to have a positive history if they smoked greater than or equal to 100 cigarettes in their lifetime10. Segregation data and detailed family histories were collected during genetic counseling consultations (if completed).

Cohort 2: De-identified patients with a germline PV in CDKN2A were retrieved from 25,266 patients with 28,543 solid tumors evaluated by NGS via MSK-IMPACT (consented to IRB 12-245) between April 2015 and June 2021. Clinical data was limited to the tumor sequenced and biological sex assigned at birth.

Tumor pathology was defined based on the OncoTree classification system37. FAMMM phenotype was defined as a history of pancreatic cancer, melanoma, and/or dysplastic nevi. Nervous system phenotype was defined as a history of peripheral nervous system (PNS), including malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST), or central nervous system (CNS) tumor. Sex was defined as biological sex assigned at birth.

Genetic ancestry was inferred from clinical MSK-IMPACT sequencing panel data using over 3000 common single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers, as previously described38. Briefly, ADMIXTURE v1.339 was run in supervised mode using the 1000 Genomes Project cohort40 as a reference to infer ancestral proportions of African, European, East Asian, Native American, and South Asian populations. Patients with an ancestral fraction of >0.8 for any single population were assigned that population label; otherwise, they were considered admixed. Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry was inferred using 282 additional SNP markers captured by MSK-IMPACT, as previously described38 and used to discern Ashkenazi Jewish Europeans from non-Ashkenazi Jewish Europeans.

Tumor and germline analysis included sequencing of 341–505 and 76–94 cancer-associated genes, respectively, depending on assay version. Details about capture protocol, sequencing, quality control, read alignment, and variant calling for the MSK-IMPACT assay have been previously described27,36. All identified variants were compared to the GRCh37/hg19 reference build, and germline variants were classified separately in the NM_000077.5 (MANE Select) and NM_058195.4 (MANE Plus Clinical) transcripts41,42. Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) by the Fraction and Allele-Specific Copy Number Estimates from Tumor Sequencing (FACETS) algorithm43 was assessed to infer if a tumor was driven by a germline CDKN2A PV. If LOH was not present, the presence of acquired CDKN2A variant(s) in the tumor was noted.

In Cohort 1, germline variants were originally classified according to the guidelines from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP)44. All curations were then updated and evaluated from the perspective of both the p16INK4A and p14ARF isoforms. Additional modifications were applied as recommended by the Clinical Genome Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group (ClinGen SVI) (https://clinicalgenome.org/site/assets/files/3459/svi_criteria_nomenclature_recommendation_v1.pdf, https://clinicalgenome.org/site/assets/files/5182/pm2_-_svi_recommendation_-_approved_sept2020.pdf)45–47. CDKN2A-specific modifications included 1) defining unique phenotypes based on trends seen in prior publications10–12, 2) separating functional data based on isoforms evaluated in the assay48–50, 3) reviewing potentially critical functional domains/regions in the context of each isoform, and 4) incorporating an acquired CDKN2A alteration or LOH, provided the variant allele fraction of the germline alteration was enriched in the tumor, as supporting level evidence46 (Supplementary Table 2).

In Cohort 2, patients with a CDKN2A variant in the blood/saliva at a variant allele fraction of greater than 10% were identified. Then, variants that had an allele count of >50 in Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) v4.1.0 were removed, which was based on the allele counts of known pathogenic variants. Literature was briefly reviewed for all remaining variants using the Human Genome Mutation Database51 and Mastermind52 to identify variants with relevant germline cases and/or functional data. Splicing algorithms were additionally reviewed for synonymous and intronic variants. Complete manual curation, as described for Cohort 1, was done for any variant with a high suspicion of pathogenicity.

Association analyses were performed to identify enrichment of CDKN2A PV carriers 1) across all cancers and 2) by tumor type. Allele frequencies of CDKN2A PV carriers with cancer from this study (Cohorts 1 and 2) were compared with PV carriers in non-cancer controls from the gnomAD v2.1.1 cohort and in all controls from gnomAD v4.1.0 and gnomAD SVs v4.1.053,54. PV in the gnomAD cohorts were defined by the ClinVar classification of “Pathogenic”, ”Likely Pathogenic”, and “Pathogenic/Likely Pathogenic”, the “LOFTEE LC pLOF” flag (i.e., stop-gained, splice-disrupting, and frameshift variants), and/or a gross deletion of one or more exons in the gene. The gnomAD v2 allele frequencies were derived from analysis of sequence variants only in a non-cancer population, whereas the gnomAD v4 allele frequencies were derived from analysis of sequence and structural variants in a presumed non-cancer population. Therefore, in the association analyses, only CDKN2A sequence variants were compared against gnomAD v2 while sequence variants and copy number variants were compared against gnomAD v4 frequencies. Total allele numbers in controls were averaged across all entries in the gene within the respective gnomAD cohorts and then summed for sequence variants and structural variants for the gnomAD v4 analysis.

In the tumor-specific analysis, CDKN2A PV carrier cases were grouped by tumor type (n ≥ 4 for sufficient power). Cases with multiple primary tumor types were only counted in one tumor group. The total allele count for cases was based on the number of patients who consented to paired tumor and germline NGS via MSK-IMPACT for each tumor group, limiting our evaluation to Cohort 1 only. Cases were compared to PV carriers in non-cancer controls in gnomAD v2 and all controls in gnomAD v4 as described above.

For the association analyses, odds ratio (OR), 95% confidence interval (CI), and p value (Fisher’s Exact test, two-tailed) were calculated using R Statistical Software (v4.3.2; R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2023, https://www.R-project.org/). Fisher’s exact test was selected to assess differences in allele frequencies between groups, given the low number of PV carriers. P value was considered statistically significant if ≤0.05. Case and control counts can be reviewed in Supplementary Table 5.
