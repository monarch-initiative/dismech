# Infantile-onset Pompe disease entering adulthood: Insights from 2 decades of enzyme replacement therapy experience.
**Authors:** Regmi N, Kenney-Jung D, Stafford G, Malinzak M, Spiridigliozzi GA, Boggs T, Koch RL, Smith PB, Case LE, Young SP, Jones HN, Kishnani PS
**Journal:** Genet Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.gim.2025.101590](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gim.2025.101590)

## Content

PURPOSE: This study details the long-term clinical outcomes in adult participants with CRIM-positive infantile-onset Pompe disease treated with enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), initially reported in 2012 (n = 11).

METHODS: Medical records were reviewed for multisystem involvement and biomarker trends. Central nervous system involvement was evaluated using a Modified Fazekas Score to grade white matter hyperintensities on brain magnetic resonance imaging.

RESULTS: Of the initial 11 participants, 8 had survived to adulthood (median age 19.6 years), and 3 died (2 of arrhythmia, 1 of status epilepticus). All survivors began ERT between 0.2 to 6 months of age (7 at 20 mg/kg biweekly; 1 at 40 mg/kg biweekly), with subsequent escalation to 40 mg/kg/week of alglucosidase alfa between ages 8 to 15 years. None of the participants received immune modulation. Cardiac hypertrophy was resolved in all; 2 developed arrhythmias requiring intervention. None of the participants required invasive ventilation. Two participants were ambulatory; 6 used wheelchairs. Flaccid dysarthria (8/8), ptosis (4/8), and sensorineural hearing loss (6/8) were common. White matter hyperintensities were present in all but remained mild to moderate on Modified Fazekas Score. Cognitive function remained stable.

CONCLUSION: Long-term ERT preserves cardiac and respiratory function in adult infantile-onset Pompe disease survivors; however, multisystem morbidity persists, highlighting the need for earlier diagnosis and better therapies targeting muscle and other tissues including the central nervous system.

Pompe disease (PD, OMIM 232300) is a rare, progressive neuromuscular disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the acid α-glucosidase (GAA, HGNC:4065) gene, leading to a deficiency of acid α-glucosidase (GAA), a lysosomal enzyme responsible for glycogen breakdown.1 Glycogen accumulates in the skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscles, as well as in the central and peripheral nervous system.2–4 Infantile-onset Pompe disease (IOPD) presents within the first days to weeks of life with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, progressing to cardiorespiratory failure and death within the first 2 years of life without treatment.2,5,6

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with recombinant human GAA (rhGAA, alglucosidase alfa) has significantly improved survivorship and cardiovascular, respiratory, and motor function outcomes.7 However, its response varies because of several factors, including age at ERT initiation, dose of ERT, high sustained antibody titers, and cross-reactive immunologic material (CRIM) status.8–10 CRIM-negative (CRIM−) IOPD lack the ability to synthesize any GAA and represent the most severe form of the disease spectrum, whereas CRIM-positive (CRIM+) IOPDs can synthesize a nonfunctional form of GAA.11 CRIM− and a subset of CRIM+ individuals can develop high sustained antibody titers, necessitating immune tolerance induction (ITI) therapy alongside ERT for optimal outcomes.8,11–17

We previously described a cohort of 11 CRIM+ IOPD survivors, the oldest being 12 years at publication.9 Since then, 3 have died at ages between 10 to 16 years (2 from cardiovascular causes and 1 from neurological cause). The 8 surviving participants have now reached adulthood, making them among the oldest living individuals with IOPD. Long-term survivorship with IOPD children and adolescents has revealed involvement of musculoskeletal apparatus, speech, swallowing, hearing, vision, smooth muscles,9,18 and the central nervous system (CNS).19,20 Yet, there is limited information in the literature regarding the clinical course and outcomes of participants surviving beyond 18 years of age. Herein, we detail the evolving long-term clinical presentations of 8 CRIM+ adults with IOPD who were considered good responders to ERT.

The participants were from a previously published cohort who had reached adulthood (age ≥ 18 years).9 All were enrolled in institutional-review-board-approved research protocols (Duke University Health System Institutional Review Board: Pro00010830, Pro00001562,Pro00072329).

Longitudinal clinical and research data were collected every 6 to 12 months from July 1999 to July 2024. Assessments included cardiac, pulmonary, neurological status, motor function and mobility, ophthalmological findings, speech, language, neuropsychological, and physical therapy evaluations. All assessments were conducted by specialists with expertise in PD. Childhood data were previously published,9 and this study extends the analysis to the most recent assessment (MRA). Data were collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture tools hosted at Duke University.21

Annual echocardiograms were reviewed for left ventricular mass index and ejection fraction. Serial electrocardiograms were assessed for PR, QRS, QT, and QTc intervals.

Pulmonary function tests, including forced vital capacity (FVC) in supine and upright positions, were assessed longitudinally.

Walking endurance was measured using the 6 Minute Walk Test.22 The predicated 6 Minute Walk Test distance was calculated using age-appropriate established formulas.23,24 Gross motor function was assessed using the Gait, Stairs, Gowers Chair, Quick Motor Function Test, and Gross Motor Function Measure scores.25–27 Additional data included residual muscle weakness, grip and lateral pinch strength,28 use of orthoses/assistive devices, the capacity to walk long and short distances, perform functional transfers and sit independently.

Annual assessments included evaluation for ptosis, refractive errors, and corrective lens use.

Motor speech was evaluated by licensed, certified speech-language pathologists using a standard clinical protocol including determination of involvement of speech subsystems of respiration, phonation, articulation, and resonance. In some participants, a velopharyngeal functioning evaluation was also completed (velopharyngeal functioning/resonance, nasometry and nasopharyngoscopy). Hyponasality and hypernasality were rated using a 0 to 4 scale (0 indicates absence; 4 indicates severe).

Similarly, language assessments were performed using the Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals-Fifth Edition (CELF-5), generating core language scores, receptive language index, and expressive language index standard scores.

Swallowing status was determined based on Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS) score,29 history/current use of feeding tube, and the presence of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD).

Longitudinal audiologic evaluations assessed severity of sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) and use of hearing aids. Classification of hearing loss severity was based on the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association guidelines defined as follows: normal hearing: −10 to 15 dB; slight: 16 to 25 dB; mild: 26 to 40 dB; moderate: 41 to 55 dB; moderately severe: 56 to 70 dB; severe: 71 to 90 dB; profound: >90 dB.

Neurological exams assessed muscle strength, tone, reflexes, cranial nerves, and coordination. Manual muscle testing focused on distal groups and hip extensors.

Cognition was assessed yearly whenever possible and by the same clinical psychologist (G.A.S.). Initially, 5 participants (ID 1, 2, 5, 7, and 8) completed age-appropriate Wechsler scales, as detailed by Korlimarla et al30 (2020). Two participants with Spanish-speaking parents (ID 3 and 4) and significant speech articulation difficulties completed the Leiter International Performance Scale as a nonverbal measure of cognition. Beginning in 2018, all completed the Wechsler scales and Leiter-3 during each visit.

Longitudinal brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were performed on participants aged age 5 years and older using Siemens Prisma (Munich, Germany) 3.0T MRI Scanner (MAGNETOM Trio) with MR XA30A software and an 8-channel head coil. Noncontrast T1, T2, and T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences were obtained and assessed for white matter hyperintensities (WMHI) using the Modified Fazekas Score (MFS). The MFS, which evaluates 9 brain areas in the superficial and deep white matter regions, similar to the original Fazekas Score published by our group,30 provides more precise quantification and allows the inclusion of additional brain regions, as well as qualitative volumetric assessment of cortical atrophy (included as addendum “V” to the total sum score). Please refer to Supplemental Table 1 for details.

IgG antibodies to ERT (rhGAA) titers, urine glucose tetrasaccharide (Glc4) levels,31 and serum creatine kinase (CK) levels were trended.

Linear regression was performed using STATA 18 to correlate MRI scores (MFS) with cognitive scores, clustering by participant. Descriptive statistics and data visualization were conducted in R, GraphPad Prism, and Excel.

Of the 11 previously described CRIM+ IOPD participants, 3 died during follow-up: 2 from suspected cardiac arrhythmias at ages 10 and 13 years (ID 2 and ID 4), and 1 from refractory status epilepticus at age 16 years (ID 10) (communication from treating physicians).9,32 Clinical chart review revealed that all 3 were on ERT with dose escalations: ID 2 to 40 mg/kg biweekly at age 11 years, ID 4 to 40 mg/kg/week at age 9 years, and ID 10 with stepwise increases reaching 40 mg/kg/week by age 9.5 years. All 3 experienced clinical decline before death, including progressive cardiorespiratory insufficiency, invasive ventilatory needs, and motor decline. Based on the local treating physician, the likely cause of demise for ID 2 and 4 was life threatening arrhythmia. ID 10 exhibited new-onset seizures at age 15, progressing to refractory epilepsy and death by 16 years. The clinical course of ID 10 has been detailed previously (ID 2 of corresponding study).19 Baseline demographic features and genetic variants for the remaining 8 participants (6 males, 2 females; ages 18–24 years) are shown in Table 1. Two (IDs 1 and 8) were diagnosed prenatally via amniocentesis and initiated ERT at 2 and 1 months, respectively—the earliest in this cohort. As a result, their baseline cardiac and neuromuscular involvement was less severe than typically seen in symptomatic IOPD, underscoring both the rapid progression of the disease and the benefits of early diagnosis.

All 8 began ERT with alglucosidase alfa between 0.2 to 6 months of age, with a standard dose of 20 mg/kg every 2 weeks (except ID 3: 40 mg/kg/biweekly). Dose escalation to 40 mg/kg/week occurred between ages 8 to 15 on a case-by-case basis with reference to clinical status, biochemical markers, and physical therapy evaluations, with decisions also influenced by insurance coverage policies in the United States. At MRA (median age 19.6 years; range 18.0–24.5 years), all participants were receiving 40 mg/kg/week of alglucosidase alfa. A subset had recently transitioned to a next-generation ERT,33 although data on these transitions were excluded because of insufficient longitudinal follow-up.

All participants had normal left ventricular mass index at MRA, and pre-ERT cardiomegaly remained resolved. Two participants had documented arrhythmias early in life: ID 5, previously diagnosed with atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia at age 7 years, underwent successful ablation with no recurrence noted through MRA. ID 6, previously diagnosed with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome with preexcitation on electrocardiograms at age 8 years, continued to experience recurrent supraventricular tachycardia episodes despite ablation, and remained well controlled on oral Sotalol at MRA. No new arrhythmias were observed during the extended follow-up in other participants of the cohort.

Longitudinal respiratory status monitoring revealed low but stable FVC values across the cohort. Supine FVC was consistently lower than upright FVC, reflecting varying degrees of diaphragmatic involvement (Table 2). None required invasive ventilation up to MRA.

Mild sleep-disordered breathing, initially characterized as obstructive sleep apnea, was reported in 2 participants at the time of prior publication (ID 2 and ID 5). ID 2 subsequently died at age 10 years from suspected arrhythmia. In the surviving participant (ID 5 in prior report; ID 4 in this study), repeat polysomnography showed progression of obstructive sleep apnea with nocturnal hypoventilation and was initiated on nocturnal bilevel positive airway pressure at age 10 years (apnea/hypopnea index[AHI] 9.9–10.6 at the time of initiation). Bilevel positive airway pressure settings have been stable through MRA. No new findings were observed in the remaining participants during follow-up.

At the time of prior report, 5 of 8 participants were independently ambulatory and 3 (IDs 3, 4, and 7) used assistive mobility devices. At MRA, 5 out of 8 were nonambulatory (4 using power wheelchairs, 1 using a manual wheelchair), and 1 used power wheelchair for community mobility but remained ambulatory indoors with crutches and orthoses. The remaining 2 participants were fully ambulatory. Six of 8 were independent in transfers, and 7 of 8 were independent in sitting (Figure 1, Supplemental Table 2).

Functional testing showed marked mobility limitations. Two participants completed the 6-minute walk test, with a mean predicted distance of 69.1% (Supplemental Table 3). Three completed the 10-m walk test with a mean speed of 0.78 m/sec. Gross motor function (Gross Motor Function Measure, n = 7) averaged 53.9% (range: 15.6%−99.4%). The Quick Motor Function Test (n = 4) yielded a mean score of 31.4 (range: 2–59). Gait, Stairs, Gowers Chair scores (n = 8) averaged 20.4 (range: 5–27).

All demonstrated weakness in core and proximal/distal limbs. Grip and lateral pinch strength were consistently reduced (Supplemental Tables 2 and 4). One participant (ID 6), although lacking formal motor assessments, retained sufficient upper extremity strength for operating power wheelchair and cell phone use. Postural abnormalities were observed in all, including varying degrees of scoliosis, thoracic kyphosis/lumbar lordosis, and fixed contractures at the hips, knees, and plantar flexors.

Ptosis was present in 5 of 8 (bilateral in 2). Among those with bilateral ptosis, one (ID 4) required repeated surgical intervention, although recurrence was noted post-procedure. All had refractive errors, with myopia being the most prevalent (6/8) and 2 of 8 had hyperopia. Astigmatism was present in 3 of 6 myopic participants. All 8 were using corrective glasses at MRA.

In our previous report, dysarthria and hypernasality were observed in the majority (10 out of 11). On longitudinal follow-up, flaccid dysarthria persisted in all who underwent serial motor speech evaluations (6 of 8), with severity classified at MRA as mild (n = 3), moderate (n = 2), or severe (n = 1) as shown in Table 2.

Additionally, 3 of the 8 participants received a velopharyngeal functioning evaluation; 2 of 3 had hypernasality scores of 4 (severe). All 3 participants produced high-pressure consonants with weak oral pressure, especially high-pressure sibilant sounds (participant 3, whose dysarthria was mild, had less severe findings). Consistent with the overall myopathic picture, all 6 participants who received motor speech examinations were diagnosed with mild to severe flaccid dysarthria. Resonance imbalances, including hypernasality and cul-de-sac resonance, were noted in all 6 of these long-term survivors. In participants 3, 4, and 5, detailed velopharyngeal assessments confirmed hypernasality through both nasopharyngoscopy and nasometry (Supplemental Table 5).

Lastly, language assessments using the CELF-5, done in 7 participants, showed stable language abilities in 4 participants (average to above-average performance), whereas 3 participants demonstrated persistent moderate impairments in expressive and/or receptive domains with core language scores standard scores within −1.5 to −2 SDs below the mean. Based on receptive language index standard scores, 3 of 7 participants had above normal/normal receptive language skills, 2 of 7 had moderate receptive language impairments with standard scores within 11.5 to −2 SDs below the mean, and 2 of 7 had severe receptive language impairments with standard scores > −2.0 SDs below the mean. Based on expressive language index standard scores, 4 of 7 participants had above-average/average expressive language skills, 1 of 7 had mild expressive language impairment with standard scores −1 to −1.5 SDs below the mean, and 2 of 7 had moderate expressive language impairments with standard scores −1 to −1.5 SDs below the mean.

In our previously reported study, 3 participants were receiving mixed oral and gastrostomy tube (G-tube) feeding, whereas the remainder maintained exclusive oral intake. Over time, 2 participants had their G-tubes removed around ages 10 and 11 years of age and now feed entirely orally. One of the 3 participants (ID 8 of prior report, corresponding to ID 5) continues to have a G-tube in situ but has consistent oral intake of food or liquid (FOIS 3). In addition, 1 participant (ID 5 in the prior report, corresponding to ID 4 in the current study) required G-tube placement at age 11 years and remains gastrostomy-dependent with minimal attempts of food or liquid (FOIS 2) at MRA. The remaining 6 followed a total oral diet without restrictions (FOIS 7). GERD as diagnosed by video fluoroscopic swallow exam was present in 2 of 8 participants (Table 2). In this cohort, although most participants maintained or regained full oral feeding ability, one ultimately required escalation to gastrostomy support during long-term follow-up.

SNHL was previously reported in 9 of 11 participants, including 2 with unilateral involvement. Upon rereview, ID 1—previously reported as having SNHL—was confirmed to have normal hearing on all assessments except for a single evaluation at the time of the earlier publication. The 2 participants who previously had normal hearing in the earlier report (ID 4 and 9) developed bilateral moderate to severe SNHL; 2 with prior unilateral SNHL (ID 6 and 7), progressed to bilateral mild SNHL. The remaining 3 had stable bilateral moderate to severe SNHL (Table 2). Thus, although hearing loss progressed in some participants, it remained stable in others, and 1 individual (ID1) maintained normal hearing throughout follow-up.

In our previously reported study, most participants exhibited hypotonia, reduced muscle bulk, distal muscle weakness, and hyporeflexia, without evidence of CNS signs. Longitudinal follow-up revealed persistent neuromuscular findings across the cohort, with variability in severity and gradual progression of weakness, particularly affecting distal and hip extensor muscles. Postural abnormalities, including scoliosis and contractures, also became more pronounced over time. Deep tendon reflexes remained diminished or absent in all participants. Importantly, no new CNS signs such as spasticity, seizures, or pyramidal tract findings emerged in this cohort.

In our previously reported study, cognitive abilities were generally preserved, with no significant impairments noted in most participants. Longitudinal assessments revealed variability in cognitive profiles among long-term survivors, but no evidence of cognitive regression. Scores on the Wechsler and Leiter scales for each participant are plotted in Figure 2. Although composite scores vary slightly across different versions of the Wechsler scales, those composites measuring verbal comprehension and expression skills, perceptual reasoning, working memory, and visual processing speed were examined in addition to the Leiter Nonverbal IQ scores. There was a great deal of variability within and across participants in their composite scores. Full Scale IQ scores of 2 participants were in the average or above-average range, whereas 5 scored below average in comparison with same-aged peers; this pattern was also observed in Leiter Nonverbal IQ scores, which tended to be higher than Wechsler full scale IQs. There was no evidence of a loss of skills over time in this cohort of long-term survivors.

Baseline MRIs obtained between ages 2 and 15 years for participants 1 to 5 showed minimal WMHI, with MFS of 0 or 1 (out of 45), all limited to superficial white matter. On longitudinal follow-up, MFS remained stable in 4 of these participants, whereas participant 4 exhibited progressive WMHI, with scores increasing from 0 to 9 by age 15 years, and subsequently to 15 by age 20 years (Figure 3). The progression remained confined to superficial white matter regions, specifically the periventricular white matter at the centrum semiovale, corona radiata, and juxtacortical U fibers. Participants 7 and 8, who had baseline MRIs at ages 12 and 15 years respectively, each demonstrated extensive superficial WMHI (MFS of 19). Participant 7 remained stable over time, whereas participant 8 showed further progression involving deep white matter structures, including the corpus callosum, cerebral peduncles, and posterior limb of the internal capsule. No significant correlation was seen between MFS and cognitive performance.

All 8 participants initially had elevated Glc4 levels, which decreased within the first month of ERT. Urinary Glc4 levels decreased over time, although values remained above the reference range of 3 mmol/mol creatinine. At MRA, the median Glc4 values ranged from 7.7 to 12.2 mmol/mol creatinine (Figure 4).

Similarly, CK levels were elevated at baseline in all participants and decreased over time but remained above the reference limit (<270 U/L), with MRA values ranging from 266 to 1037 U/L.

All participants were seronegative for anti-rhGAA IgG at treatment initiation. Time to seroconversion ranged from 0.9 to 15 months (median: 1.8 months), with peak titers (median: 1:1600; range: 1:100 to 1:6400) occurring within the first year of ERT. None received ITI and remained immunotolerant at MRA.

This study reports long-term outcomes of a cohort of CRIM+ IOPD participants into adulthood. Treatment paradigms have changed significantly since these participants began ERT. All were initially started on the standard dose of ERT, with dose escalation occurring later—between ages 8 and 15 years—after some degree of irreversible tissue injury may have already taken place (Supplemental Figure 1). This is a key consideration when interpreting long-term outcomes in this cohort because earlier and higher-dose ERT strategies yield improved results.10,34 Moreover, in the past, CRIM—patients had poorer outcomes due to lack of immune tolerance strategies; today, ITI has enabled better responses in all IOPD cases, including those who are CRIM negative.13,15 With Pompe disease added to the US newborn screening panel in 2015, infants with IOPD are now diagnosed and treated earlier than this cohort—often within the first month, when even days can affect outcomes.34,35 Two participants in this cohort (IDs 1 and 8) were diagnosed prenatally and began ERT shortly after birth. Both had milder cardiac and neuromuscular finding, reflecting the evolving IOPD phenotype with early detection and treatment. However, it is possible that earlier initiation of higher-dose ERT may have further optimized outcomes. In addition, ERT protocols have changed since 2006. Although this cohort began on 20 mg/kg biweekly, the current standard is for a higher dose (40 mg/kg week),10 and next-generation therapies, such as avalglucosidase alfa, have shown improved biomarker and functional outcomes, which may shape future adult phenotypes.33,36

Despite immune tolerance and long-term ERT, multi-system involvement remained prominent. Although cardiac hypertrophy resolved in all, 2 participants developed arrhythmias—highlighting that normalized structure does not preclude conduction abnormalities. This finding is consistent with prior reports of arrhythmias in ERT-treated IOPD, likely due to myocardial fibrosis or residual glycogen in the cardiac conduction system.37–41

Additionally, CNS involvement is increasingly recognized in IOPD. This is consistent with our recent work describing the neurological course of 6 IOPD participants (1 CRIM− and 5 CRIM+) who developed seizures and/or encephalopathy during longitudinal follow-up (between ages 9–15 years), including the deceased participant described above.19 Although next-generation ERT may improve glycogen clearance in skeletal muscle, there is no evidence for CNS penetration of any current therapies. This cohort has been followed with serial brain MRI (using the MFS, specifically developed for this purpose), which revealed that most participants (5/7) maintained stable low MFS of 0 to 4 over time. The remaining 2 had an increase in MFS, although all scores remained below 20. In the previously adapted Fazekas scoring system, a score of 15 (midpoint of 30) was used to define severe white matter disease.42 For the MFS, this threshold was adjusted to 20 (out of 45) based on expert neuroradiology consensus during longitudinal imaging review to reflect the expanded scoring system in our MFS and better align with the broader distribution of scores observed in this cohort. Because previous studies have documented significant neurological sequelae in participants with elevated Fazekas scores,19 this finding will bear further monitoring.

There was considerable variability among the participants in their overall scores on cognitive testing. The participants also showed a great deal of variability among the domains measured. Although overall IQ scores are within the lower end of average or below average for some individuals, the majority have earned significant milestones, including a high school diploma and/or getting employed. Notably, 2 individuals have earned college degrees, underscoring their remarkable accomplishments. Importantly, there was no evidence of a loss of cognitive skills over time within this cohort. Future directions include seeking a better understanding of the impact of IOPD on the CNS and elucidation of risk factors for development and progression of WMHI, along with the relationship of WMHI to the CNS deterioration seen in some IOPD participants.

In addition to skeletal muscle pathology, signs of smooth muscle involvement were evident in this cohort, such as GERD, potentially indicating weakness of distal esophageal sphincter.43 Additionally, the ophthalmological findings are consistent with involvement of ocular smooth muscles (ciliary body and iris), alongside extraocular muscles.44 Similarly, urinary incontinence has also been noted in IOPD, supporting the need to monitor smooth muscle systems as part of comprehensive care.45,46 Lastly, these participants required various interventions for pulmonary involvement. Pulmonary function tests were consistent with varying degrees of diaphragmatic involvement, consistent with our previous study showing persistent diaphragmatic weakness in participants treated with ERT.47

Although SNHL was common, 1 participant (ID 1) maintained normal hearing throughout, and another (ID 8) had only mild, stable hearing loss—both received early ERT. These outcomes suggest variable involvement of the CNS and a potential protective effect of early ERT on auditory function. This observation aligns with findings from newborn screening cohorts, including a study by Hsueh et al,48 which reported preserved hearing in individuals with classic IOPD who were treated promptly after birth.

Overall, all participants demonstrated a myopathic neurological examination without upper motor neuron findings and exhibited a gradual decline in gross motor function over time. Most participants relied on a wheelchair for mobility as they entered adulthood. Notably, participants starting with a higher functional level showed less decline and had better preservation of ambulation than did more severely impaired participants.

Biochemically, most participants had persistently elevated Glc4 and CK levels despite clinical stability, suggesting incomplete glycogen clearance. Notably, the 2 independently ambulatory participants had the lowest biomarker levels, reinforcing their utility not only for tracking biochemical response to ERT but also as predictors of long-term motor outcomes. In contrast, CK levels showed fluctuations over time. Along with clinical evidence of myopathy, this suggests ongoing muscle involvement, possibly related to inability of current ERT to adequately access certain muscle fibers/compartments. Next-generation therapies with better targeting have shown improved glycogen clearance, resulting in improved biomarkers and clinical outcomes.33,49,50 To some extent, the observed increase in CK levels after 12 years of age may be attributed to the natural increase in muscle mass associated with the transition into adolescence and later adulthood. However, this pattern could also reflect persistent muscle damage. Current drug development efforts have demonstrated normalization of previously elevated biomarkers in participants with IOPD and later-OPD treated with first generation ERT (alglucosidase alfa).33

In analyzing the factors contributing to the overall good outcomes observed in this cohort, several key elements seem to contribute. All showed favorable longitudinal biomarker trends and low antibody titers. All participants had ERT doses individually titrated, with half of them receiving 40 mg/kg/week from the age of 10 for at least 10 years. Furthermore, the comprehensive care provided by multidisciplinary teams has ensured regular and meticulous follow-up, allowing for adjustments in ERT dosing as needed.

Limitations include the small sample size and retrospective design, as well as exclusion of CRIM− participants limiting the generalizability of the findings. Since ITI became common use in 2009 for CRIM− IOPD, more CRIM− participants are expected to enter adulthood, which will permit such comparisons.

In conclusion, although long-term ERT has markedly improved survival and preserved cardiac and respiratory function, adults with IOPD continue to face multisystem challenges. Cardiac conduction abnormalities were prominent, as were comorbidities in respiratory, neurological, ophthalmologic, speech, hearing, and musculoskeletal systems. Multidisciplinary care remains crucial for optimizing the health and quality of life of individuals with IOPD. Further research is needed to explore additional therapeutic strategies, particularly for improved tissue penetration including the CNS.
