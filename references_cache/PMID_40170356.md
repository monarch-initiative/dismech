# Severe Joubert syndrome in family with homozygous POC1B p.Arg106Pro variant is due to a co-inherited deep-intronic mutation in the neighboring CEP290 gene.
**Authors:** Betz C, Reusch B, Langmann T, Habbig S, Beck BB, Bolz HJ
**Journal:** HGG Adv (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xhgg.2025.100429](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xhgg.2025.100429)

## Content

"En bloc" inheritance of point mutations in adjacent genes has rarely been described. We have previously reported a family with severe, mostly early-lethal Joubert syndrome (JBTS) with early-onset severe retinal dystrophy (EOSRD) and polycystic kidney disease (PKD), which at that time had been attributed to a homozygous pathogenic missense variant, p.Arg106Pro (c.317G>C), in the ciliary POC1B gene. Because this and other POC1B variants were, in subsequent studies, only reported in patients with non-syndromic childhood or early-adult-onset macular dystrophy, we have now reassessed our index patient by long-read high-fidelity (HiFi) whole-genome sequencing (LR-WGS). We identified a homozygous deep-intronic variant, c.2818-657T>G, in CEP290, a JBTS/Meckel syndrome-associated gene on chromosome 12q21, only 1.28 Mb from the N terminus of POC1B. cDNA analysis revealed aberrant splicing with the frame-shifting inclusion of 37 bp from CEP290 intron 25, predicting the loss of CEP290 function. EOSRD and PKD can fully be ascribed to this CEP290 variant, whose effect outshines the "background" non-syndromic POC1B retinopathy and co-segregates with the severe syndromic phenotype. Our novel findings in this family no longer justify POC1B as a JBTS gene. This co-inheritance of two ciliopathies, with the clinically decisive variant hidden deep in an intron, exemplifies the importance of WGS for achieving the complete diagnosis in challenging cases.

Joubert syndrome (JBTS) is a rare, mostly autosomal recessive developmental disorder with a characteristic midbrain-hindbrain malformation (“molar tooth sign”), ataxia, psychomotor delay, oculomotor apraxia, and an altered respiratory pattern in the neonatal period. Depending on the genetic subtype, many organs apart from the CNS may be affected: retinal degeneration (as early-onset severe retinal dystrophy [EOSRD] or retinitis pigmentosa [RP]) is most common, and many patients also present with cystic kidney disease (typically as nephronophthisis [NPHP]). Liver fibrosis and skeletal involvement (e.g., polydactyly) may also occur.1 JBTS proteins play a role in the formation or function of primary cilia and/or associated structures, such as the basal body and the centrosomes. Pathogenic variants in more than 40 genes are known to cause JBTS.

We have formerly reported the identification of an assumingly disease-causing mutation in POC1B (MIM: 614784), a gene encoding a ciliary basal body protein, in a JBTS family with EOSRD and atypical polycystic kidney disease (PKD).2 While data from poc1b knockdown in zebrafish appeared to be compatible with a syndromic phenotype,3
POC1B mutations were otherwise only reported as a cause of autosomal recessive non-syndromic cone-rod dystrophy (CORD20; MIM: 615973) but not of EOSRD in severe JBTS resembling Meckel syndrome (MKS).4 We, therefore, reassessed our JBTS family and conducted long-read HiFi whole-genome sequencing (LR-WGS) of the index patient. Our findings reversed our previous molecular diagnosis, with an in
cis deep-intronic CEP290 mutation that co-segregates with the known POC1B mutation, finally removing POC1B from the JBTS candidate gene list.

Blood samples for DNA extraction were obtained with written informed consent. All investigations were conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study was approved by the institutional review board of the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Cologne. Because the index patient (V:12) and his family (Figure 1) were extensively described in our initial publication,2 the details given here are confined to the most important aspects and updates. Patient V:12 was born to consanguineous Iraqi parents. Oligohydramnios and massively enlarged kidneys had already been noted prenatally. After birth, renal function was severely compromised but improved with conservative management. He received a living-donor kidney transplant at the age of 5 years. He briefly required chronic hemodialysis due to graft loss from chronic rejection for 10 months before a second deceased-donor kidney transplantation was performed at the age of 17 years. Patient V:12 is now 20 years old, with status post-second kidney transplantation, severe congenital visual impairment compatible with EOSRD, severe neurocognitive impairment, and MTS clearly visible in cranial MRI. A brother (V:13) born in the meantime presented with oligohydramnios at gestational week 17 and showed enlarged cystic kidneys and cerebellar vermis hypoplasia. He was born at gestational week 26, received palliative care, and died on the day of birth. At least four additional, similarly affected individuals with massive polycystic kidney disease (PCKD) died from lung hypoplasia and/or end-stage renal disease (ESRD) as newborns or at early school age. Blindness, ataxia, and intellectual disability were reported in two girls (IV:1 and IV:6) who died at the ages of 6.5 and 9 years, respectively. Two affected boys (V:1 and V:2) died before blindness could have been recognized (1 and 2 days after birth). Lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) were generated from venous blood samples of the index patient’s father (III:10) and a control. 5 mL of venous blood and 45 mL of erythrocyte lysis buffer (155 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, and 0.1 mM Na2-EDTA [pH 7.4]) were mixed, incubated for 10 min at room temperature, and centrifuged for 10 min at 3,000g. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet resuspended. After additional incubation and centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in 2 mL of culture medium containing Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) virus particles. The suspension was incubated at 37°C at 5% CO2 for 1 h and then filled up to 5 mL with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20% FCS, penicillin/streptomycin, amphotericin B, and cyclosporine (2 μg/mL) and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2. The cell culture was expanded over several weeks. A patient fibroblast cell line was generated from a postmortem skin biopsy of V:13. Biopsy fragments were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 (DMEM with 10% FCS, penicillin/streptomycin, and amphotericin B) until larger colonies were recognized. Fibroblasts were then trypsinized and plated on dishes for further expansion.

DNA of the index patient (Figure 1, V:12) was extracted from peripheral blood on a QIAsymphony instrument (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). After removing short DNA fragments by precipitation (SRE kit, PacBio, Menlo Park, CA), DNA was sheared to approximately 16 kb by robot liquid handling (Hamilton NGS STAR; Hamilton, Bonaduz, Switzerland). Whole-genome library preparation and polymerase binding were done according to the SMRTbell prep kit 3.0 protocol (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA). Sequencing was performed on a PacBio Revio system (1 sample per SMRT cell, 300 pM adaptive loading concentration, 24 h movie time), aiming at a HiFi read output of at least 80 Gb.

Sequence data were processed with our in-house bioinformatic pipeline, which contains long-read-specific tools (HIFICNV for copy-number variations [CNVs], PBSV for structural variants, and TRGT and TRVZ for repeat expansions) provided by PacBio (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA), giving additional information on phasing, methylation status, and repeat expansions. Main callers for single-nucleotide variants (SNV) were DeepVariant5 and FreeBayes (https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.3907v2). The identified variants (SNVs and CNVs) were matched to our internal database (BiFreq) and external databases (gnomAD,6 ClinVar,7 and HGMD Professional8). In addition, in silico analyses and scoring of the variants were performed with regard to functional relevance (e.g., SIFT,9 PolyPhen2,10 MutationTaster,11 and PROVEAN12), conservation (GERP,13 SIPHY,14,15 phyloP,16 and phastCons16), and splicing (Alamut17 [integrates results from NNSplice,18 GeneSplicer,19 MaxEntScan,20 and SpliceSiteFinder-like21], SpliceAI,22 and MMSplice23). Basic threshold values for variant minor-allele frequencies (MAFs) were <0.1% for dominant inheritance and <1% for recessive diseases. LR-WGS of the index patient, V:12, resulted in a genome-wide coverage of approximately 37.7-fold, a yield of 119 Gb, and a mean read length of 15.5 kb. In addition to the previously known and reported homozygous pathogenic POC1B variant, we identified a homozygous deep-intronic variant, c.2818-657T>G (rs895573161; g.88103668A>C [GenBank: NC_000012.12] [GRCh38]), in intron 25 of CEP290 (GenBank: NM_025114.4) (Figure 2C). Pathogenic CEP290 variants are associated with (among others) JBTS (JBTS5, MIM: 610188) or, at the lethal end of the spectrum, MKS (MKS4, MIM: 611134). The variant has not been reported as disease-associated, nor is it annotated in the general population (gnomAD v.2.1.1 [hg19] or v.4.1.0 [hg38]). Alamut17 (which integrates results from NNSPLICE, SpliceSiteFinder-like, MaxEntScan, and GeneSplicer) predicted the generation of a novel acceptor splice site by the variant. Sanger sequencing of cDNA PCR amplicons derived from fibroblast RNA (patient V:13) and LCL RNA (patient V:12) revealed completely aberrant splicing in this tissue with the inclusion of a 37 bp poison exon from intron 25 (r.2817_2818insguaauguuugccuauuauacagcaacuuaagagaacu), due to the homozygous CEP290 variant, c.2818-657T>G (Figures 3A–3C). On the protein level, a translated aberrant transcript would contain nine unrelated residues before truncation due to a novel stop codon (p. Lys939_Glu940insValMetPheAlaTyrTyrThrAlaThr∗). Both this heavy truncation and a complete lack of protein due to nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) would correspond to loss of CEP290 function. Not surprisingly, in view of the proximity to POC1B and in line with a phenotype compatible with JBTS5 (and partially MKS4), the CEP290 variant co-segregates with the phenotype in all patients whose samples were still available (Figure 1).

Patients with CEP290-associated JBTS, JBTS5 (MIM: 610188), often present with early severe retinal and renal involvement. In our family, CORD20 due to the POC1B variant c.317G>C (p.Arg106Pro) is outshone by the JBTS5-inherent EOSRD in both age of onset and severity. The CEP290 variant can be regarded as solely responsible for the disease in the family, rectifying the clinically predominant diagnosis as JBTS5. As a consequence, POC1B cannot be considered a JBTS gene any longer.

The co-inheritance of Mendelian disorders due to the dysfunction of genes from the same locus usually occurs in the context of autosomal dominant contiguous gene syndromes (CGSs). In CGS, in cis CNVs affect consecutive genes whose haploinsufficiencies jointly contribute to the phenotype. Non-CGS-related co-inherited recessive disorders due to the dysfunction of two genes from the same locus (due to independent pathogenic variants in both genes) are probably extremely rare. They appear conceivable almost exclusively in a homozygous state of such mutations due to parental consanguinity.

Generally, parental consanguinity results in an elevated risk for (even rare) recessive disorders in the offspring. This may result in not only one but two or more simultaneous recessive disorders in the offspring due to the carriership of the parents for the same diseases (and respective disease-causing homozygosities in the child).25 If the mutant loci are inherited independently from each other, the a priori risk for each associated phenotype is 25%. In contrast, two recessive point mutations in closely neighboring genes are unlikely to be separated by recombination events, resulting in a 25% risk for en bloc inheritance of both conditions. One of the few reported examples is the co-occurrence of two rare conditions, a complex MFRP-related ocular phenotype and SLC37A4-associated glycogen storage disease type 1b (GSD-1b), in an Omani family.26 The awareness not only of both conditions but also their coupled inheritance is crucial for medical management and genetic counseling. In the case of our family, however, the very severe and early disease manifestations can be attributed to the CEP290 mutation. Thus, the POC1B variant, which would have manifested much later as a milder retinopathy (CORD20), is very likely clinically irrelevant for the patients with this biallelic en bloc inheritance of the pathogenic variants in POC1B and CEP290, respectively. Variants that are in linkage disequilibrium with the decisive variant and lead to erroneous initial molecular diagnoses may also reside in the same gene: we have previously reported a JBTS family in whom the phenotype turned out not to result from a homozygous C-terminal frameshift variant but from a more N-terminal missense mutation in AHI1.27 Similar examples have been described in a study of a large consanguineous cohort for the genes LAMB3, CYP21A2, and PDHX.28

Parental consanguinity may provide a shortcut to the identification of (mostly homozygous) disease-causing mutations in diagnostic and research-oriented whole-exome sequencing (WES)—provided the variant does localize in the protein-coding sequence. On the other hand, homozygous deep-intronic mutations usually escape detection by WES, preventing a complete genetic diagnosis. Unrelated or only partially causative exonic variants may then erroneously be held responsible for the phenotype: in the JBTS family reported herein, the presence of the exonic pathogenic POC1B variant in a mapped candidate locus (which also contained the “hidden” but critical CEP290 mutation) was particularly misleading. Thus, the possibility of co-inheritance of (two) recessive conditions should be taken into account if a formally “convincing” candidate variant (here, Arg106Pro in POC1B) co-segregates with an incongruous phenotype in several similarly affected patients from a consanguineous family. If such a second pathogenic variant is a deep-intronic splice site mutation, then this represents a particularly challenging pitfall.

Our study highlights a likely underrated cause of inherited (complex) phenotypes in countries with high rates of consanguinity, like the Greater Middle East. In both diagnostic and research settings, WGS should be preferred over WES: it enables the detection of disease-causing mutations in non-coding regions and may fully utilize the advantages of filtering for rare homozygous variants. Short-read WGS would very likely have safely revealed the deep-intronic variant in the reported family as well. WGS with long reads, however, allows for extensive haplotype phasing and immediate awareness of an in cis constellation of two pathogenic recessive variants. In view of its superior quality, we expect long-read WGS to replace “conventional” short-read WES and WGS in the near future.29
