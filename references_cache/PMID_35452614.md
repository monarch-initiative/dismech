# Ribosome-associated quality-control mechanisms from bacteria to humans.
**Authors:** Filbeck S, Cerullo F, Pfeffer S, Joazeiro CAP
**Journal:** Mol Cell (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.molcel.2022.03.038](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2022.03.038)

## Content

Ribosome-associated quality-control (RQC) surveys incomplete nascent polypeptides produced by interrupted translation. Central players in RQC are the human ribosome- and tRNA-binding protein, NEMF, and its orthologs, yeast Rqc2 and bacterial RqcH, which sense large ribosomal subunits obstructed with nascent chains and then promote nascent-chain proteolysis. In canonical eukaryotic RQC, NEMF stabilizes the LTN1/Listerin E3 ligase binding to obstructed ribosomal subunits for nascent-chain ubiquitylation. Furthermore, NEMF orthologs across evolution modify nascent chains by mediating C-terminal, untemplated polypeptide elongation. In eukaryotes, this process exposes ribosome-buried nascent-chain lysines, the ubiquitin acceptor sites, to LTN1. Remarkably, in both bacteria and eukaryotes, C-terminal tails also have an extra-ribosomal function as degrons. Here, we discuss recent findings on RQC mechanisms and briefly review how ribosomal stalling is sensed upstream of RQC, including via ribosome collisions, from an evolutionary perspective. Because RQC defects impair cellular fitness and cause neurodegeneration, this knowledge provides a framework for pathway-related biology and disease studies.

Either errors occurring during gene expression or the action of damaging agents can lead to the synthesis of proteins with various forms of aberrations, from point mutations to truncations and extensions that are not coded by native open reading frames. In addition to losing function, aberrant proteins can gain function, such as aggregation or dominant-negative behavior. It is thus not surprising to find that cells ensure the quality of the proteome already during protein synthesis (e.g., Deuerling et al., 2019; Inada, 2020; Steiner and Ibba, 2019)(Howard and Frost, 2021; Joazeiro, 2019; Sitron and Brandman, 2020; Yip and Shao, 2021). Several quality control machineries survey translation at multiple steps, and handle defective proteins by mediating their folding, sequestration into compartments, or proteolysis via the ubiquitin-proteasome or autophagy pathways (e.g., Hipp et al., 2019; Kwon and Ciechanover, 2017; Pilla et al., 2017; Pohl and Dikic, 2019; Reinle et al., 2022).

A source of aberrant proteins that has received much attention during the past decade is the stalling of ribosomes during translation. One of the model systems for studying ribosome stalling consists of reporter constructs encoding aberrant mRNA lacking stop codons (non-stop mRNA). Translation of non-stop mRNA was shown to cause ribosome stalling at the mRNA poly(A) tail, and mRNA decay (Frischmeyer et al., 2002). It was later found that proteins encoded by non-stop mRNA were also degraded (Ito-Harashima et al., 2007). Although the precise proteolytic mechanism was not determined, as misfolded and other forms of aberrant proteins had long been known to be targeted for degradation through cytosolic or ER-associated quality control machineries, non-stop proteins were assumed to undergo a related process. A shift in paradigm took place with the identification of the first component of this degradation pathway, the E3 ubiquitin ligase Ltn1/Listerin (Bengtson and Joazeiro, 2010). Yeast Ltn1 was being studied as a way to obtain clues into the molecular mechanism of neurodegeneration caused by mutation of its mouse ortholog (Chu et al., 2009). The work uncovered that Ltn1 acted in targeting non-stop proteins produced by ribosome stalling for ubiquitylation and degradation. Moreover, Ltn1 was shown to be associated with the large ribosomal subunit, and in its absence the ribosome stalling products were found to accumulate on ribosomes. The findings placed both the E3 ligase and its substrate on ribosomes, defining the translational quality control pathway known as Ribosome-associated Quality Control (RQC) and opening the door to its molecular dissection (Bengtson and Joazeiro, 2010). Subsequent work has revealed that RQC is also found in prokaryotes (Lytvynenko et al., 2019). However, with the ubiquitin system being absent in these organisms, an alternative proteolytic-tagging mechanism is utilized compared to eukaryotic RQC.

Here we review RQC mechanisms across evolution, emphasizing more recent findings on bacterial and mammalian RQC, and discuss how studies of less complex organisms have enlightened our understanding of the general logic and mechanisms underlying this conserved process. For coverage on additional aspects of the topic we refer the reader to recent reviews (Howard and Frost, 2021; Inada, 2020; Joazeiro, 2019; Sitron and Brandman, 2020; Yip and Shao, 2021). We adhere to the view that RQC begins after ribosomal subunit splitting, with its hallmark being the proteolytic tagging of aberrant nascent-chains for degradation while they are still associated with the large ribosomal subunit. We note that the term ‘RQC’ is sometimes used in eukaryotic studies to refer to processes happening already on stalled 80S ribosomes, such as the ubiquitylation of ribosomal proteins by the E3 ligase Hel2/ZNF598. However, as we discuss below, (1) ribosome stalling is not the only mechanism upstream of RQC, and (2) ribosome stalling elicits multiple responses other than RQC in both bacteria (most notably the SsrA/tmRNA pathway) and eukaryotes. We therefore encourage utilization of the more precise definition of RQC adopted in this review.

The substrates of RQC are dissociated large ribosomal subunits obstructed with a nascent-chain-tRNA conjugate (see further below), which are recognized by the ribosome- and tRNA-binding protein NEMF and its orthologs in yeast and bacteria, Rqc2 and RqcH, respectively. Here we discuss both established and potential sources of such obstruction.

The best characterized source of obstructed large ribosomal subunits is translational stalling. Ribosome stalling has been estimated to take place in 0.4% of the translation events in Escherichia coli (Moore and Sauer, 2007) and can occur for various reasons, for instance due to the presence of suboptimal codons on the mRNA, damage of the mRNA or rRNA, the absence of stop codons in ‘non-stop’ mRNA, the synthesis of elongation-inhibiting peptide sequences, or the action of translation elongation-inhibiting antibiotics (Joazeiro, 2017; Moore and Sauer, 2007). In mammalian cells, studies with reporter genes harboring candidate ribosome stalling signals have shown that, as for yeast cells, poly(A) translation can act as a potent stalling signal (Juszkiewicz and Hegde, 2017; Sundaramoorthy et al., 2017). An abundant source of poly(A) tail translation is non-stop mRNA resulting from the premature cleavage and polyadenylation within mRNA coding sequences, a prevalent error during gene expression that occurs at least 1% of the time in the human liver transcriptome (Ozsolak et al., 2010) and as often as ~50% of the time for yeast CBP1 (Mayer and Dieckmann, 1989). Ribosomes stall during translation of poly(A) sequences because ‘AAA’ codes for Lys, and polybasic peptides electrostatically interact with the ribosomal exit tunnel causing translation to slow down (Lu and Deutsch, 2008). In addition, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies have revealed that concomitant with this slowdown of translation, the poly(A) mRNA adopts an rRNA-stabilized non-canonical conformation, resulting in a reconfiguration of the decoding center that sterically hinders recruitment of aminoacyl-tRNAs and thus further contributes to translational stalling (Chandrasekaran et al., 2019).

In addition to producing aberrant nascent-chains, ribosome stalling would rapidly deplete cells of ribosomal subunits available for new rounds of protein synthesis if stalled ribosomes were not targeted for recycling (also referred to as ‘ribosomal rescue’; Moore and Sauer, 2007). Accordingly, dedicated mechanisms have evolved to sense stalled ribosomes and elicit rescue and other damage-containing responses, such as proteolytic tagging, in organisms from bacteria to humans.

An emerging picture is that the ribosome stalling location on an mRNA specifies how stalling is sensed and ribosomes are rescued (Fig. 1).

Ribosomes stalled at the 3’ end of an mRNA, e.g., generated when an endonuclease cleaves an mRNA within coding sequences (see, e.g., Cue2 below), are recognized by the Pelota-HBSIL complex (Dom34-Hbs1 in yeast) (Doma and Parker, 2006; Pisareva et al., 2011; Shoemaker and Green, 2011) (Fig. 1A). Mammals have an additional Pelota-interacting HBS1L ortholog, GTPBP2, that has been linked to the response to ribosome stalling caused by a tRNA mutation in mice (Ishimura et al., 2014). Cryo-EM analyses have revealed that Dom34-Hbs1 senses an unoccupied mRNA entry channel in the ribosome as proxy for mRNA 3’ end stalling—the GTPase Hbs1 binds to the ribosome near the entrance of the mRNA channel, while Dom34 binds to an empty ribosomal A-site and inserts a β-loop into the unoccupied mRNA entry channel (Hilal et al., 2016). Dom34/Pelota next recruits the AAA ATPase Rli1/ABCE1 to split the ribosomal subunits (Nürenberg-Goloub and Tampé, 2019). Pelota and HBS1L are paralogs of the canonical translation termination factors, eRF1 and eRF3, respectively. However, unlike eRF1, Pelota does not require a stop codon for binding to ribosomes and lacks peptidyl-tRNA hydrolase activity. As uncleaved peptidyl-tRNA is unable to passively slide out of the ribosomal exit tunnel from either end, the splitting of stalled ribosomes by the concerted action of Pelota, HBS1L and ABCE1 produces 60S subunits that are still obstructed. Even though the large subunit is not immediately recycled in this process, the lasting association of nascent-chains with dissociated 60S subunits is advantageous as it enables nascent-chains to be marked for degradation prior to being released, through the action of RQC (Shao et al., 2013).

Ribosomes stalled internally within an mRNA form complexes that have distinct structural features from ribosomes stalled at the 3’ end of an mRNA (Fig. 1B). In particular, the mRNA-occupied A-site and mRNA entry channel render those ribosomes less well accessible to Pelota-HBS1L. Rather, ribosomes stalled within an mRNA are sensed by virtue of their collision with trailing elongating ribosomes (Ikeuchi et al., 2019; Juszkiewicz et al., 2018; Meydan and Guydosh, 2021; Simms et al., 2017). Upstream of RQC, collided disomes are recognized by the E3 ligase ZNF598 (Hel2 in yeast) (Ikeuchi et al., 2019; Juszkiewicz et al., 2018). ZNF598 recruits downstream effectors in a ubiquitylation-dependent manner (Garzia et al., 2017; Ikeuchi et al., 2019; Juszkiewicz et al., 2018, 2020a; Matsuo et al., 2017, 2020; Sundaramoorthy et al., 2017). One such effector is the ASCC3 helicase (Slh1 in yeast), which utilizes ATP to separate the ribosomal subunits (Juszkiewicz et al., 2020a; Matsuo et al., 2020). Since no peptidyl-tRNA hydrolase activity is involved in this process, obstructed large subunits are produced, just as for 3’ end stalling (Juszkiewicz et al., 2020a; Matsuo et al., 2020).

Rescue mechanisms for ribosomes stalled internally or at 3’ ends of mRNAs can intersect (Fig. 1). In yeast and worms, another effector of Hel2-mediated ubiquitylation is the mRNA endonuclease Cue2 (NONU-1 in worms and N4BP2 in mammals) (D’Orazio et al., 2019; Glover et al., 2020). Cue2 is specifically recruited to ubiquitylated collided ribosomes, in part via its ubiquitin-binding CUE domains (Donaldson et al., 2003), and cleaves the mRNA between the stalled and trailing ribosomes, thereby providing a link between the two rescue pathways: in addition to facilitating exonuclease-mediated mRNA degradation (D’Orazio et al., 2019; Glover et al., 2020; Kögel et al., 2022; Zinoviev et al., 2020), Cue2-mediated mRNA cleavage causes trailing ribosomes to stall in the newly-generated 3’end, producing the canonical substrate for rescue by Dom34-Hbs1 (Fig. 1). Conversely, it is conceivable that ribosomes stalled at an mRNA 3’ end, if not rescued by Pelota/Dom34, will eventually collide and be targeted by the ZNF598/Hel2 pathway. The latter notion is supported by recent findings in bacteria, which are discussed next.

As the name suggests, SsrA/transfer-messenger RNA (tmRNA) is a hybrid tRNA-mRNA molecule. SsrA, found in all eubacteria, rescues stalled ribosomes and simultaneously targets the nascent-chains produced by stalling for proteolysis, through trans-translation (Fig. 2A) (Keiler et al., 1996; Moore and Sauer, 2007). The SsrA tRNA-like domain, together with the SmpB cofactor, structurally mimics tRNAAla. The charged SsrA-SmpB complex is delivered by elongation factor Tu (EF-Tu) to the empty A-site of ribosomes stalled on mRNA 3’ ends. In addition, the SmpB C-terminus, together with an SsrA stem-loop, sense an unoccupied mRNA entry channel (Rae et al., 2019). As in canonical translation, the nascent-chain is transferred to the Ala residue of SsrA in the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) and translation resumes on the open reading frame (ORF) of the SsrA mRNA-like domain. The ORF is followed by a stop codon, which enables canonical translation termination and ribosome recycling, and encodes a peptide, the SsrA tag, that becomes fused to the nascent-chain C-terminus and signals its degradation (Keiler et al., 1996)(Moore and Sauer, 2007).

For more than two decades after the discovery of SsrA’s function (Keiler et al., 1996), it remained the only known bacterial system responsible for targeting ribosome stalling products for degradation. This understanding has been broadened with the finding that the NEMF ortholog, RqcH, mediates a primitive RQC pathway in Bacillus subtilis (Fig. 2B) (Lytvynenko et al., 2019). RqcH is present in approximately 30% of sequenced bacterial species, including the vast majority of firmicutes, but notably has been lost in most bacteroidetes and actinobacteria as well as in proteobacteria such as E. coli (Burroughs and Aravind, 2014; Cerullo et al., 2022). Prior to the discovery that RqcH assumes a central role in B. subtilis RQC, its bacterial orthologs had been characterized as virulence factors for several pathogens, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae PavA (Holmes et al., 2001; Kodama et al., 2018; Osanai et al., 2013; Torelli et al., 2012). RqcH orthologs were proposed to reside on the bacterial surface and mediate host invasion by binding to fibronectin present on the surface of mammalian cells (hence, RqcH is also known as the fibronectin-binding protein PavA, Fbp54, Fbp62, FbpA, Fnm, EfbA or FBPS, depending on the species)(Holmes et al., 2001; Kodama et al., 2018; Osanai et al., 2013; Torelli et al., 2012). RqcH can bind to fibronectin in vitro (e.g., Musyoki et al., 2016), but evidence for its extracellular localization is controversial and, indeed, RqcH orthologs have no canonical secretion signal or known extracellular anchorage motif. It thus remains to be verified whether the effects of rqcH deletion on bacterial adhesion are indeed direct, or whether defective RQC can account for the phenotypes of rqcH-null bacteria.

The discovery of a bacterial RQC pathway has raised the question of what the source of RQC substrates is. In bacteria, ribosomes stalled at the 3’ end of non-stop mRNA are preferentially targeted by SsrA (Fig. 2A). While RqcH was able to target proteins produced by non-stop mRNA in cells lacking SsrA, it remained unknown how bacterial cells split ribosomes stalled in the 3’ end of those transcripts to generate obstructed 50S subunits for RqcH binding. It was also unclear whether RqcH generally acts as a fail-safe pathway for SsrA. Recent work has shed light onto these open questions, with the answer residing on ribosome collisions (Cerullo et al., 2022) (Fig. 2).

The eukaryotic SMR domain-containing RNA endonucleases Cue2 and NONU-1 specifically act on stalled ribosomes upstream of RQC (D’Orazio et al., 2019; Glover et al., 2020) (Fig. 1). Based on this knowledge, MutS2—the only SMR domain-containing protein in B. subtilis—was recently investigated as a candidate for a function upstream of RqcH in bacteria (Cerullo et al., 2022). MutS2 is a paralogue of the DNA repair proteins of the MutS family, with which it shares an ATPase/clamp domain, but MutS2 lacks the canonical DNA-binding domain of MutS proteins and there is no evidence supporting a direct role in either DNA repair or a DNA-related process (Burby and Simmons, 2017; Pinto et al., 2005). On the other hand, as predicted by the hypothesis that MutS2 plays a direct role in translational quality control, cells lacking MutS2 (now renamed RqcU, for RQC-upstream factor) showed increased sensitivity to inhibitors of translation elongation, and RqcU bound to ribosomes in co-immunoprecipitation and sucrose gradient centrifugation experiments (Cerullo et al., 2022). Moreover, cryo-EM analysis of endogenous ribosomal complexes co-purified with RqcU revealed that an RqcU homodimer selectively binds to collided disomes, suggesting that it acts as a sensor of ribosome stalling and collisions (Cerullo et al., 2022).

As for the MutS paralogues involved in DNA repair, the RqcU ATPase and clamp domains are shaped like a tweezer, which holds to the leading (stalled) ribosome by the central protuberance, adjacent to the ribosomal inter-subunit interface (Cerullo et al., 2022). In MutS proteins, ATP binding induces conformational changes that generate power strokes driving structural changes in associated domains or macromolecules (Groothuizen and Sixma, 2016). Likewise, two RqcU conformational states could be identified that are consistent with ATP-driven rearrangement of one monomer relative to the other. These observations led to the hypothesis that, following ribosome collision sensing, RqcU separates the subunits of the stalled ribosome in an ATPase-dependent manner, both for ribosome recycling and for eliciting RQC (Cerullo et al., 2022) (Fig. 2). Consistently, RqcU deficiency led to a defect in RQC and this defect could not be rescued by RqcU ATPase domain mutants.

Further linking RqcU and RqcH, whereas each gene is present in only ~30% of ~10,000 bacterial genomes sequenced, they strongly co-occur (Cerullo et al., 2022). In E. coli, where both genes are absent, two small SMR domain-containing proteins are encoded, SmrA and SmrB, neither of which is associated with an ATPase domain. Like B. subtilis RqcU, E. coli SmrB was found to associate with collided ribosomes generated through biochemical reconstitution (Saito et al., 2022). Cryo-EM analyses showed that SmrB recognizes a composite interface formed by both ribosomes and the connecting mRNA segment. The mRNA endonuclease activity of SmrB ultimately leads both to mRNA decay and to 3’ end stalling of ribosomes, generating substrates for SsrA (Saito et al., 2022). Although RNA endonuclease activity of the RqcU SMR domain has not been directly verified, RqcU is also likely to cleave mRNA and generate SsrA substrates, while simultaneously generating RQC substrates via the ATPase domain.

Therefore, ribosome collisions in both eukaryotic and bacterial cells are resolved following a strikingly similar logic, through the action RNA endonucleases and ATP-dependent ribosome splitting factors, with a key difference being that eukaryotes separated the sensor and effector activities, including ubiquitin as an adapter presumably to allow a higher degree of regulation.

The findings that, like eukaryotes, bacteria rely on ribosome collisions for sensing and responding to translational stalling (Cerullo et al., 2022; Saito et al., 2022) suggests that collided disomes are a conserved mechanism of stress sensing across the kingdoms of life. Whether this is also true for archaea remains to be demonstrated, as the sensing and rescue of stalled ribosomes remains poorly characterized in these organisms (Nürenberg-Goloub and Tampé, 2019). Moreover, as SMR domain-containing proteins are mostly absent in archaea, a mechanistic model is lacking for how ribosome collisions would be directly handled in those organisms. Archaea encode for Pelota and ABCE1 homologs that in diverse species are syntenic with RqcH homologs (aRqcH) (Lytvynenko et al., 2019), but how ribosomes stalled on mRNA 3’ ends—the preferred substrates of Pelota—are generated is not known either.

It is also evident that RQC is a deeply conserved mechanism for proteolytic tagging of the products of ribosome stalling and collisions. The phylogenetic distribution of RqcH homologs indicates that an early form of the RQC pathway was already present in the Last Universal Common Ancestor (LUCA) that gave rise to bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes (Burroughs and Aravind, 2014; Lytvynenko et al., 2019), with some bacterial phyla having lost rqcH during the bacterial divergence. We speculate that faulty translation by primitive ribosomes in the LUCA and its ancestral cells was likely to have been even more frequent than by modern ribosomes, which have evolved for an additional ~3 billion years. Under such scenario, primitive ribosomes would have virtually behaved as ‘toxic machines’ as a result of their increased levels of aberrant protein production, likely creating strong selective pressure early in the origins of life for mechanisms to develop that could handle the proteotoxic stress while enabling ribosomes to further evolve.

In addition to eliciting RQC, collided disomes are emerging as major signaling hubs for various other damage-control responses (Kim and Zaher, 2022; Meydan and Guydosh, 2021). In eukaryotes, those include stimulation of mRNA decay (Ikeuchi et al., 2019), activation of stress signaling (ribotoxic stress, inflammation and the integrated stress response) (Kim and Zaher, 2022; Pochopien et al., 2021; Vind et al., 2020; Wan et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2020; Yan and Zaher, 2021), prevention of new initiation on the stalling-associated mRNA (Hickey et al., 2020; Juszkiewicz et al., 2020b; Sinha et al., 2020), and prevention of frameshifting by the collided ribosome (Houston et al., 2021). Some of these responses appear to be mutually regulated, by mechanisms that are just being uncovered (Houston et al., 2021; Sinha et al., 2020). Evidence suggests that at least some of these responses are conserved as well—the stringent response signaling factor Rel was identified as a major component co-immunoprecitating with both RqcU and with bL9, a marker of collided disomes in B. subtilis (Cerullo et al., 2022); and bL9 itself has been implicated in suppressing frameshifting resulting from ribosomal collisions (Smith et al., 2019).

In addition to ribosome stalling, obstructed large subunits can also be produced as a result of aborted translation. As defined here, aborted translation is mechanistically distinct from ribosome stalling as it does not involve the action of molecular rescue factors for ribosomal subunit splitting. The paradigmatic example of aborted translation is the rapid separation of ribosomal subunits with energy input from heat shock (e.g., Korber et al., 2000). This process is distinct from effects of heat shock on ribosome stalling due to overwhelmed capacity of the chaperones implicated in co-translational protein folding and translation elongation (e.g., Shalgi et al., 2013) and from the action of the GTPase HflX as a heat shock-induced splitting factor for vacant ribosomes (Zhang et al., 2015). Another cause of aborted translation is the structural instability of ribosomes, e.g., under low intracellular Mg2+ concentrations (as mimicked in vitro by the classical effect of EDTA on ribosomal splitting observed in sucrose gradient centrifugation experiments; Utsunomiya and Roth, 1966) or with the loss of ribosomal protein bL31 (Chadani et al., 2017) (Fig. 3).

Aborted translation has yet to be characterized as a source of RQC substrates. Nonetheless, it is relevant to briefly mention the finding that obstructed 50S subunits produced by heat shock in E. coli are sensed by Hsp15, a heat shock-inducible protein (Jiang et al., 2009). Hsp15 keeps the nascent-chain-linked tRNA in a canonical P-site conformation. A plausible model requiring further validation is that Hsp15 binding thereby enables the action of a release factor on the P-site tRNA (Jiang et al., 2009). Notably, whereas E. coli does not have an RQC pathway, B. subtilis RqcP, the Hsp15 homolog, functions as an integral component of RQC by binding to a P-site tRNA and facilitating the translocation step in RqcH-mediated C-terminal tailing (see below; (Filbeck et al., 2021; Crowe-McAuliffe et al., 2021). These observations are thus consistent with the possibility that heat shock may elicit RQC in bacteria that are equipped with RqcH.

The first step in RQC is the sensing of an obstructed large subunit. Early structural work had suggested how this is accomplished (Lyumkis et al., 2014). Yeast Rqc2 binds to the inter-subunit surface of the 60S subunit (Fig. 4A) in a manner that is mutually exclusive with the 40S subunit, thereby specifically targeting free 60S particles while avoiding normally elongating ribosomes. Rqc2 also binds to the obstructing P-site tRNA, which stabilizes the complex. Thus, the simultaneous binding of Rqc2 to the free 60S and P-tRNA informs that the 60S subunit is obstructed. Another important feature of RQC highlighted by Rqc2’s binding mode is its independence of the nature of the associated nascent-chains. This enables RQC to broadly target the enormous diversity of nascent-chain sequences and lengths that can be produced by ribosome stalling or aborted translation (Lyumkis et al., 2014). These key principles of RQC function as a protein quality control mechanism are conserved from bacteria to mammals (e.g., Filbeck et al., 2021; Shao et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2015).

Once bound to the large subunit, eukaryotic NEMF orthologs promote proteolytic tagging by stabilizing the binding of the LTN1 E3 ligase to the ribosomal complex (Defenouillere et al., 2013; Lyumkis et al., 2014; Shao et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2015; Fig. 4A). Moreover, and more broadly across evolution, NEMF orthologs promote nascent-chain proteolysis by modifying them with C-terminal peptides (‘tails’), with diverse functions having been described for the different organisms where this has been studied, as discussed below.

NEMF orthologs synthesize C-terminal tails by extending the nascent-chains obstructing the large ribosomal subunit in the absence of mRNA, the small subunit, and canonical translation elongation factors (Shen et al., 2015; Osuna et al., 2017; Lytvynenko et al., 2019; Filbeck et al., 2021; Crowe-McAuliffe et al., 2021). This is a remarkable ability, given how small and architecturally simple NEMF orthologs are (Fig. 4B) compared to small ribosomal subunits—RqcH (65 kDa in B. subtilis) is comprised of a basic core of NFACT-N, coiled coils, NFACT-M and NFACT-R domains, where NFACT stands for ‘domain found in NEMF, FbpA, Caliban, and Tae2’ (FbpA and Tae2 are presently referred to as RqcH and Rqc2, respectively). An additional NFACT-C domain with unknown function is present in archaeal and eukaryotic orthologs (Fig. 4B).

C-terminal tailing is characterized by NEMF ortholog-mediated iterative recruitment of aminoacyl-tRNA to the large subunit A-site, peptidyl transfer and tRNA translocation to the P-site. Recent structural and biochemical work on bacterial RqcH (Fig. 4C) has provided information on detailed reaction steps underlying ‘Ala tailing’ (Filbeck et al., 2021; Crowe-McAuliffe et al., 2021; Takada et al., 2021). The initial binding of RqcH to the P-site tRNA is non-selective, mediated by unspecific electrostatic interactions between the NFACT-N domain and the anticodon arm of the P-site tRNA, presumably after the tRNA has been stabilized in a P-site conformation by the cofactor RqcP (for P-site tRNA-binding; introduced above under ‘Aborted Translation’) (Fig. 5A).

In the next step, as suggested by direct RNA sequencing experiments, RqcH selectively recruits tRNAAla(UGC) for making the C-terminal tail (Lytvynenko et al., 2019). tRNAAla(UGC) recruitment takes place via NFACT-N domain-mediated tRNA anticodon reading, in which the H-bond pattern mimics base-pairing between mRNA codon and tRNA anticodon in canonical translation elongation. During this ‘decoding step’, the tRNAAla anticodon nucleotides are accommodated in pockets of the NFACT-N domain (Fig. 5B), while the bulk of the tRNA backbone is coordinated by basic patches of RqcH, in conjunction firmly positioning tRNAAla in the A-site of the complex (Filbeck et al., 2021). RqcH may also bind to tRNAAla(UGC)
via the same principles described above prior to associating with the obstructed large ribosomal subunit.

RqcH residues Asp97 and Arg98 of the NFACT-N domain are crucially involved in establishing the H-bond pattern between the tRNA anticodon nucleotide G35 and the NFACT-N domain (Fig. 5B) and thus are essential for tRNAAla selectivity (Filbeck et al., 2021; Crowe-McAuliffe et al., 2021). This explains the conservation of equivalent residues among NEMF orthologs, which in turn suggests that some constraint is imposed on what tRNAs can be selected for C-terminal tailing across evolution. Accordingly, in vivo RNA crosslinking studies with human NEMF revealed that tRNAAla was strongly enriched in comparison to all other tRNAs (Thrun et al., 2021), although mass spectrometry analysis of a ribosome stalling reporter has been interpreted as indicating that alternative amino acids, such as Thr, Tyr and Gly, can be minor components of Ala tails in humans as well (Udagawa et al., 2021). In S. cerevisiae, it is well established that Rqc2 recruits tRNAAla and tRNAThr to make the original C-terminal Ala and Thr tails (‘CAT tails’) (Shen et al., 2015), which from a structural perspective could be achieved by maintaining selective reading of G35 in those tRNAs via the conserved Asp98 and Arg99 residues, but at the same time altering the binding pocket for the tRNA anticodon nucleotide N36 such that pyrimidines (C36 in tRNAAla, U36 in tRNAThr) can insert while purines are sterically excluded. Although more cases need to be studied, the evolutionary distribution suggests that polyalanine was the primordial form of C-terminal tail, and therefore Thr was likely incorporated secondarily as a major tail component during yeast evolution, which we discuss further below. Finally, Drosophila Caliban was crosslinked to tRNAAla, tRNAThr and tRNASer, but mass spectrometry analyses have suggested that more degenerate tails may be synthesized, including Cys, Glu and Tyr residues (Wu et al., 2019). Notably, the tail composition identified in flies is not consistent with their synthesis through an untemplated process in which amino acids are randomly incorporated. For example, while one 36-residue long tail had only Ala and Thr residues, in approximately equal contribution, another 25-residue long tail had 14 Ala and 8 Ser residues, but no Thr residues. These observations raise concerns with potential artifacts associated with the mass spectrometry analysis of low-abundance, heterogeneous peptides.

Following the decoding step, a conformational change in RqcH drives the A-site tRNA to a hybrid A/P-site conformation, coupled with peptidyl transfer (Fig. 5C) (Filbeck et al., 2021; Crowe-McAuliffe et al., 2021). This mimics the forward ratcheting of ribosomal subunits in canonical elongation, but how it is triggered remains unclear. Finally, driving the A/P hybrid tRNA to a P-site conformation, as in the reverse ribosome subunit ratcheting during canonical translation, requires the Hsp15/RqcP cofactor, which binds to the 50S subunit and forms a highly basic composite groove together with the NFACT-N domain to accommodate and stabilize the peptidyl-tRNA in a classical P-site conformation (Fig. 5A) (Filbeck et al., 2021; Crowe-McAuliffe et al., 2021). Whether RqcH remains stably associated with the large ribosomal subunit throughout the entire Ala tailing reaction or dissociates and rebinds with each reaction cycle remains to be investigated. In any case, the binding of RqcH is mutually exclusive with binding of canonical elongation factors EF-G and EF-Tu (Filbeck et al., 2021), consistent with results of biochemical studies in yeast and bacteria indicating that translational GTPases are not required for C-terminal tailing (Osuna et al., 2017; Takada et al., 2021). Thus, the ATP utilized for tRNA charging appears to drive the reaction (Filbeck et al., 2021).

The remarkable overall similarity of RQC complexes from bacteria and eukaryotes (Fig. 4) and the conservation of key RqcH residues involved in tRNA recruitment suggest that key aspects of the tailing mechanisms are conserved. However, differences are also apparent in addition to tRNA selectivity discussed above. In particular, Hsp15/RqcP homologs are only found among bacteria. Although other factors (or the NFACT-C domain) could perform the role of Hsp15/RqcP in archaea and eukaryotes, it is also conceivable that specific reaction requirements are distinct in these organisms.

In bacteria, Ala tails act as C-terminal degrons that drive the degradation of modified nascent-chains once released from the 50S subunit (Lytvynenko et al., 2019). Notably, the SsrA tag is also a C-terminal degron and resembles an Ala tail, ending in ALAA in most bacteria (Moore and Sauer, 2007). Consistent with this similarity, both the SsrA tag and Ala tails are recognized directly by the proteasome-like ClpXP protease (Lytvynenko et al., 2019; Moore and Sauer, 2007). Recent cryo-EM structures of an SsrA-tagged substrate in complex with ClpXP reveal the basis for degron recognition and explain the ALAA motif’s critical role in binding to the ClpX adaptor (Fei et al., 2020).

In eukaryotes, C-terminal tails have diversified their functions and, with the emergence of the ubiquitin system, work together with E3 ligases in signaling proteolysis. The best characterized example is that of LTN1, which is an ancient E3 ligase found across eukaryotes. Eukaryotic NEMF orthologs have evolved an additional proteolytic function that is C-terminal tailing-independent but that can nonetheless be C-terminal tailing-stimulated. This function is mediated by NEMF’s expanded M domain (Fig. 4B), which binds directly to LTN1 to stabilize its association with obstructed 60S subunits (Fig. 4A) (Defenouillere et al., 2013; Shao et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2015). In the canonical RQC pathway (RQC-L, for LTN1), LTN1 can then ubiquitylate nascent-chains while they are still 60S-associated (Fig. 6). However, LTN1 faces a challenge that is intrinsic to protein quality control E3 enzymes, namely how to successfully ubiquitylate vastly heterogeneous substrates (Joazeiro, 2019). Given the wide variety of nascent-chains that can obstruct the 60S subunit, Lys residues (which serve as ubiquitin acceptors) may sometimes not be properly exposed for ubiquitylation by LTN1. In this case, nascent-chain elongation at the C-terminus proves to be of great assistance, by exposing random nascent-chain Lys residues buried in the ribosomal exit tunnel (Kostova et al., 2017). Moreover, for ribosomes stalled in the poly(A) tail of non-stop mRNA, Lys residues encoded by the stalling sequence might also serve a role as ubiquitin acceptors, if C-terminal tails can be extended long enough to expose them.

The analysis of ribosome stalling reporters in mammalian cells has led to the surprising finding that NEMF was also competent to promote reporter degradation in the absence of LTN1 (Thrun et al., 2021). Furthermore, this process required NEMF’s Ala-tailing activity. It was found that, in case LTN1 fails to act, the release of nascent-chains from the 60S subunit exposes the Ala tail modification, which is then recognized as a degron by the Pirh2 or CRL2KLHDC10 E3 ligases (Fig. 6) (Thrun et al., 2021). Of relevance, CRL2KLHDC10 had been previously characterized as an E3 ligase that targets C-terminal degrons in the C-end rule pathway (Koren et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2018). These findings thus reveal the convergence of mammalian RQC and C-end rule proteolytic pathways via Ala tailing in the alternative RQC-C pathway (for C-end rule), and the deep evolutionary conservation of Ala tails as a proteolytic signal in RQC. The relative contributions of RQC-L and RQC-C across different cell types, under stress conditions, and towards different classes of RQC substrates remain to be investigated.

It has been suggested that CAT tails made in yeast by Rqc2 may also mediate Ltn1-independent proteolysis (Sitron and Brandman, 2019). However, only a small subset of engineered Ala and Thr combinations in tail sequences appeared to have such function (Sitron and Brandman, 2019) suggesting that CAT tails, which are heterogeneous in nature regarding both sequence and length (Shen et al., 2015), are likely to be inefficient degrons. An E3 ligase, Hul5, was identified as required for the degradation of some CAT tails in yeast (Sitron and Brandman, 2019). Hul5 is proteasome-associated and has been implicated in the degradation of misfolded proteins in general (Fang et al., 2011; Finley, 2011). It is thus conceivable that destabilizing CAT tails may act directly at the level of the proteasome. To rationalize these findings, a model has been proposed in which CAT tails (and conceivably mammalian Ala tails as well) may initiate protein unfolding at the proteasome (Thrun et al., 2021), thus facilitating degradation as described for certain C-terminal sequences (Prakash et al., 2004; Tomita and Matouschek, 2019).

So, what is the function of Thr residues in yeast CAT tails? Notably, LTN1 prefers ubiquitylating Lys residues, and does not appear to ubiquitylate Thr residues as reported for some other E3 ligases (Kostova et al., 2017; Yonashiro et al., 2016). Rather, Thr seems to act synergistically with Ala in increasing the amyloid-aggregation propensity of C-terminal tails (Choe et al., 2016; Defenouillère et al., 2016; Yonashiro et al., 2016), raising the possibility that CAT tails may function in promoting protein aggregation to sequester and/or enable elimination of the aberrant proteins via aggrephagy as a fail-safe pathway. Moreover, CAT tail aggregates activate a stress signaling response through Hsf1 (Shen et al., 2015). However, despite having potentially benefitial roles, CAT tail aggregates in excess can be toxic, in particular by sequestering components of mitochondrial chaperone and translation machinery, thus causing mitochondrial toxicity (Izawa et al., 2017).

It turns out that Ala tails in mammals can promote nascent-chain aggregation as well (Udagawa et al., 2021), presumably if RQC substrates are expressed at high enough levels to saturate RQC-C. Experiments carried out with Ala tail-coded reporters show that tails composed of 15 consecutive Ala residues are prone to aggregate in a comparable way to CAT tails, and that LTN1 failure can lead to an accumulation of Ala tail-mediated aggregates. These aggregates were shown to cause cytotoxicity, caspase-3-dependent apoptosis and impaired neuronal morphogenesis (Udagawa et al., 2021). These observations with yeast and mammalian C-terminal tails might provide a basis for neurodegeneration in LTN1-mutant mice (see below).

LTN1 provides a paradigmatic example for how specialized protein quality control enzymes function (Joazeiro, 2019). LTN1 acts broadly against its diverse protein quality control substrates yet selectively, by leaving the vast excess of nascent-chains associated with normally elongating ribosomes untouched. Two key mechanisms underlie these features. Mediating its broad action, LTN1 binds directly to the 60S subunit and utilizes it as an adaptor, thus functioning without regard to features of the nascent-chain substrates. On the other hand, conferring selectivity to its action, the binding of LTN1 to the 60S is mutually exclusive with the 40S subunit, so LTN1 is unable to bind to 80S particles.

The binding of LTN1 to the 60S subunit is weak and does not directly sense the 60S obstruction (Defenouillere et al., 2013; Lyumkis et al., 2014; Shao et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2015). NEMF and its orthologs compensate for these limitations by stimulating LTN1-60S binding, via a direct interaction between NEMF’s M domain and LTN1’s conserved N-terminal domain (Doamekpor et al., 2016; Lyumkis et al., 2014; Shao et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2015). NEMF orthologs also stimulate LTN1-60S binding indirectly, by preventing reassociation of free 40S subunits to obstructed 60S particles (Lyumkis et al., 2014). The relevance of NEMF’s anti-association function is well illustrated for yeast RQC. A yeast cell is estimated to have ~3,000 free 60S particles, a comparable number of free 40S particles and ~8,000 Rqc2 molecules, but only ~200 Ltn1 molecules (Lyumkis et al., 2014). Therefore, Rqc2 can compete against 40S particles to drive RQC forward more effectively than Ltn1 (Lyumkis et al., 2014). However, the effect may not be so striking for mammalian cells, as the number of NEMF molecules in HEK293T cells has been estimated to be at 105 copies/cell, only twice as many as Listerin (https://opencell.czbiohub.org).

Lastly, LTN1 binds to 60S subunits with the E3-catalytic RING domain placed adjacent to the opening of the nascent-chain exit tunnel (Fig. 4A) (Lyumkis et al., 2014; Shao et al., 2015) and appears to have a narrow window of ~12 amino acids outside the tunnel for targeting Lys residues for ubiquitylation, at least with the reporter substrates examined (Kostova et al., 2017). As discussed above, in case Lys residues are not available within this window, NEMF orthologs can also assist LTN1 by pushing exit tunnel-buried Lys residues through C-terminal tailing (Kostova et al., 2017).

Nascent-chain degradation requires additional components of a yeast RQC complex containing the 60S subunit, Ltn1, Rqc2 and Cdc48 and cofactors (Brandman et al., 2012; Defenouillere et al., 2013). Rqc1 binds to 60S subunits independently of Ltn1 and Rqc2 (Defenouillere et al., 2013) and plays a role in some step either before or during nascent-chain extraction from the 60S, but without affecting overall ubiquitylation of RQC substrates (Brandman et al., 2012; Defenouillere et al., 2013). Biochemical analyses later found that the human Rqc1 ortholog, TCF25, ensures that LTN1 preferentially forms poly-ubiquitin chains linked via ubiquitin’s Lys48 residue, by an unknown mechanism (Kuroha et al., 2018). In absence of TCF25, ubiquitin chains with all linkage types were formed instead. It is thus plausible that, across eukaryotes, TCF25 guides the synthesis of ubiquitin Lys48-linked chains that in turn recruit the AAA ATPase VCP (Cdc48 in yeast) for extracting nascent-chains and delivering them to the proteasome for degradation (Fig. 6).

The length of C-terminal tails appears to vary among organisms, which may be related to their respective functions, discussed above. For example, with reporter proteins that have been examined, a tailing-dependent band or smear above the unmodified reporter band is obvious in western blots of mammalian and yeast, but not of B. subtilis lysates. Consistent with the possibility that B. subtilis Ala tails are predominantly short, hard-coding only 6 Ala at the C-terminus of GFP sufficed to target the reporter for proteolysis (Lytvynenko et al., 2019).

How C-terminal tail length is specified remains to be understood. Termination of tail synthesis is thought to be mediated by the release of the nascent-chain from the P-site tRNA. However, an entirely stochastic process specified by kinetics of release factor binding would make it difficult to explain the observation that tail-modified nascent-chains in yeast and mammals sometimes show up as a discrete band with somewhat defined length (e.g., Thrun et al., 2021). Therefore, an open question is whether mechanisms exist for slowing down or halting the reaction once C-terminal tails reach a certain length, for example via tail interactions with the ribosomal exit tunnel. Conversely, it is not understood how nascent-chains are kept from being released prematurely—i.e., either before a tail that is long enough to function effectively is synthesized, or before the nascent-chain is ubiquitylated by LTN1. That C-terminal tailing per se is not essential for nascent-chain release is indicated by the fact that the phenomenon can also be observed in cells deficient for NEMF and its orthologs.

Nascent-chain release from the P-site tRNA is best understood for eukaryotic RQC (Fig. 6). One mechanism is tRNA cleavage by the ANKZF1 endonuclease (Vms1 in yeast), which releases the polypeptides still linked to the 3’-terminal CCA nucleotides that are universal to all tRNAs (Izawa et al., 2017; Kuroha et al., 2018; Su et al., 2019; Verma et al., 2018; Yip et al., 2019; Zurita Rendón et al., 2018). From the tRNA perspective, this produces a fragment that undergoes either tRNA quality control steps leading to either repair so it can be aminoacylated again, or degradation (Yip et al., 2019).

Biochemical studies suggest that cleavage by human ANKZF1 preferentially takes place after nascent-chains are ubiquitylated by LTN1 (Kuroha et al., 2018). In the absence of LTN1, nascent-chain release was mediated instead by Ptrh1, a homolog of the bacterial peptidyl-tRNA hydrolase Pth that is found across eukaryotes (but is absent in archaea). Like a canonical release factor and differently from ANKZF1, Ptrh1 acts by hydrolyzing the peptidyl-tRNA ester linkage (Kuroha et al., 2018).

The alternative mechanisms to release nascent-chains from obstructed 60S subunits described above may have distinct functional consequences, and seem to address the specific requirements of RQC-L and RQC-C pathways, although this hypothesis has yet to be tested experimentally. On one hand, the 3 tRNA nucleotides left linked to the Ala tail C-terminus by ANKZF1-mediated release would prevent Pirh2 and CRL2KLHDC10 from binding (Thrun et al., 2021). This is consistent with this release mechanism preferentially taking place with nascent-chains that are already ubiquitylated by LTN1 (Kuroha et al., 2018). On the other hand, nascent-chains released free of nucleotides by Ptrh1 in the absence of LTN1 (Kuroha et al., 2018) are competent to engage in RQC-C. How LTN1 signals to ANKZF1 has yet to be determined. Conceivably, Cdc48/VCP may play a role in coordinating this response via pulling action on ubiquitylated nascent-chains, conferring a unique conformation to the nascent-chain-bound tRNA on the P-site which may either render the peptidyl-tRNA ester linkage less accessible to Ptrh1 or simply favor ANKZF1 association. Alternatively, the RQC complex itself may block Ptrh1 binding and recruit ANKZF1.

In bacterial RQC, the nascent-chain release mechanism has not been elucidated. Release factors have been identified that act on the products of ribosome stalling, such as ArfA in E. coli and BrfA in B. subtilis protein (Müller et al., 2021), but those appear to act selectively on 70S ribosomes rather than dissociated 50S subunits, as their binding requires contacts with the mRNA entry channel (Huter et al., 2017; James et al., 2016; Shimokawa-Chiba et al., 2019). Based on the high degree of RQC conservation from bacteria to eukaryotes, an obvious candidate release factor for bacterial RQC is Pth, referred to above.

Much has been learned about RQC mechanisms over the past few years, thanks in part to cross-fertilization of research using model systems from bacteria to humans, enabled by the large extent of conservation of the pathway. Yet, as we have indicated throughout this review, several aspects of RQC remain incompletely understood. Moving forward, the field also needs to push more strongly towards the understanding of the function of RQC in biology and disease, especially given the indications of its relevance to pathomechanisms ranging from bacterial virulence to neurodegeneration.

The conservation of RQC and the Ala degron mechanism from bacteria to humans, the involvement of multiple E3 ligases on the mammalian pathway and the existence of the redundant SsrA mechanism for proteolytic tagging in bacteria all indicate that the aberrant nascent-polypeptides produced by ribosome stalling must be eliminated for all cells to function properly. An example of toxic gain-of-function caused by the accumulation of ribosome stalling products is that associated with translation of prematurely polyadenylated non-stop mRNA in eukaryotes. The poly(A) tail-encoded poly-Lys tract can serve as a nuclear localization signal and engage the nuclear transport machinery (Lang et al., 2018), accumulating in nucleoli and disrupting nucleolar homeostasis (Davis et al., 2021).

Furthermore, RQC is also involved in the final step in recycling of large ribosomal subunits produced by translation defects; the failure to release nascent-chains from obstructed large subunits, in addition to having a detrimental effect on protein synthesis capacity, is expected to interfere with protein import into organelles as ribosome stalling can also occur in association with ER or mitochondrial translocons. For these and other molecular consequences of defects in RQC that have been reviewed elsewhere (e.g., Howard and Frost, 2021; Joazeiro, 2019; Sitron and Brandman, 2020; Yip and Shao, 2021) it is not surprising that RQC defects have a negative impact on cellular fitness. For example, although LTN1 is not essential for the viability of HeLa, HEK-293 or human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPS) in culture in rich media (Thrun et al., 2021; Udagawa et al., 2021), the knockout of mouse LTN1 or deletion of its RING domain caused an early embryonic lethality phenotype (Chu et al., 2009). Furthermore, a random mutagenesis-induced hypomorphic mutation in LTN1 caused Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)-like disease in mice (Chu et al., 2009). This finding is consistent with LTN1’s known function in RQC, as defects in protein quality control are a hallmark of neurodegeneration (e.g., Luh and Bertolotti, 2020). The subsequent elucidation of RQC mechanisms predicted that mutations in NEMF might lead to a similar phenotype. Indeed, mutations in mouse Nemf were more recently reported to cause neurodegeneration as well. Moreover, two studies have reported mutations in human NEMF co-segregating with neuromuscular disease in several families (Martin et al., 2020; Ahmed et al., 2021). However, exactly how LTN1 or NEMF dysfunction causes neurodegeneration remains unclear. The knowledge on RQC mechanisms reviewed here provides an important framework for studies involving the pathway dysfunction. Among other things, we have learned that developing tools to analyze endogenous readouts of RQC, as well as to monitor Ala-tailing in disease tissues (e.g., Cerullo et al., 2022) will be a critical next-step.
