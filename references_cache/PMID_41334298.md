Many benign and malignant diseases can present with intraocular inflammation as the part of their clinical spectrum leading the physician to misdiagnose them as uveitis. The term “masquerade syndromes” is used to describe these entities.1

Retinoblastoma (Rb) as the most common primary intraocular malignancy of the childhood is usually diagnosed before the age of five; however, cases presented in older age are usually misdiagnosed primarily due to the atypical signs and symptoms.2 Although the delay in correct diagnosis might result in an advanced disease and the need for enucleation,23 with recent advances in the targeted therapy of Rb, some of these eyes might be salvageable.4

Hereby, we report the case of late-onset Rb masquerading as pars planitis in a 13-year-old boy which was managed with intra-arterial and intravitreal injection of chemotherapeutic agents.

A 13-year-old boy was referred to our clinic, with a 8-month history of progressive visual loss in his right eye (RE). His past medical history was unremarkable. Prior to his referral to our center, he had undergone ophthalmic examination and multimodal imaging in another center and diagnosis of pars planitis vs. organized vitreous hemorrhage (RE) had been made based on observance of diffuse vitreous opacities on fundus photography and optical coherence tomography (OCT). There was no visible mass in the first fundus photography before his referral [Figure 1]. He had been treated conservatively with no improvement after 6 months of follow-up.

(c) Optical coherence tomography discloses intra- and preretinal hyperreflective material accompanied with diffuse vitreous opacities

Upon arrival at our clinic, the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was hand motion in the RE and 20/20 in the left eye (LE). Intraocular pressure was 13 mmHg and 14 mmHg in his RE and LE, respectively. There was no evidence of iris neovascularization, iris atrophy, or ectropion uvea. Fundus examination of the RE revealed a large white mass at the posterior pole measuring 14 mm × 10 mm × 7 mm associated with diffuse vitreous seeding in the three quadrants [Figure 2a]. Based on our clinical examination, the diagnosis of late-onset Rb with the International Classification of Rb of Group D was made, and the patient was scheduled for three sessions of intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC) with melphalan (5 mg) and topotecan (1 mg) in his RE. Meanwhile, systemic examination by pediatric oncologist and comprehensive screening workup, including complete blood count, lumbar puncture, bone marrow biopsy, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), revealed no local invasion or distant metastasis. Despite the regression of primary tumor after 8 session of IAC, recalcitrant vitreous seeding did not respond to treatment. Therefore, we decided to start intravitreal chemotherapy (IVC) with melphalan (20 μg/0.1 cc) and topotecan (30 μg/0.1 cc). After four sessions of IVC, the tumor and vitreous seeds regressed completely [Figure 2b] which was confirmed by multimodal imaging [Figure 3], and BCVA in RE improved to 20/80; however, due to extensive retinal atrophy, significant visual field defect was present.

Rb is usually diagnosed based on the examination under general anesthesia and is supported by ancillary testes such as orbital computed tomography scan and MRI. However, the diagnosis of late-onset Rb can be challenging considering its atypical presentations.5 The classical signs and symptoms of Rb include leukocoria and strabismus in early childhood and decreased vision and floaters in older children. On the other hand, inflammatory disease is the presenting sign in 1%–3% of Rb cases which is usually seen in older children with no obvious calcified mass and increased retinal thickness as the sole indicator of malignant transformation.6 Presentation as a uveitis in this type of Rb can be misleading, as pars planitis may also present with the same features and is a much more common in older children. Of note, that the presumable diagnosis of pars planitis in our case was also a matter of debate. Intermediate uveitis consists of 1%–26% of all uveitis cases. It has an insidious onset with slow progression and usually bilateral involvement in patients aged between 23 and 31 years,3 which does not correlate with the presenting signs and symptoms in our patient. Moreover, the nonremarkable history of blunt trauma in our patient resulted in further dilemma in the diagnosis. The vitreous opacities were considered as organized vitreous hemorrhage; however, after retrospective evaluation of fundus photos taken at other center, it was proved to be vitreous seeding associated with Rb.

Furthermore, careful assessment of baseline imaging could have raised a suspicion of masquerade syndrome earlier. Certain atypical features may be suggestive of Rb rather than uveitis, such as: (1) mammillation-like hyperreflective deposits on OCT representing vitreous seeding, (2) whitish appearance of vitreous opacities on fundus examination, and (3) condensation of deposits predominantly at the posterior pole rather than the inferior vitreous where typical snowballs and snowbanking of pars planitis occur. Awareness of these subtle differences could assist in the earlier diagnosis and management.

The delay in the diagnosis of a masquerade syndrome with underlying malignancy can be catastrophic. Many of these Rb cases undergo enucleation due to the advanced disease.24 However, our patient was lucky as the tumor has been remained confined to the posterior segment. Although the results of new treatment modalities for the management of Rb have not been extensively studied in older children, we decided to conduct standard IAC followed by IVC for our patient. Fortunately, the tumor and vitreous seeds regressed completely and BCVA improved to 20/80 at the final visit albeit with extensive visual field defect. The older age of our patient allowed us to perform multimodal imaging to investigate the possible causes of the suboptimal visual acuity. As evident by fundus autofluorescence and OCT, the patient had developed extensive chorioretinal atrophy. These findings can be attributed to drug toxicity and the previous presence of the tumor itself. Chorioretinal atrophy is a well-known side effect of IAC7 and electroretinogram (ERG) disturbances secondary to photoreceptor damage have been described following IVC.8 Nevertheless, it should be noted the main goal in the management of Rb as a life-threatening malignancy is patient’s survival, while globe and vision salvage are secondary and tertiary goals.

In conclusion, Rb, as a masquerading syndrome should be considered in differential diagnosis of patients with atypical presentation of uveitis or traumatic vitreoretinopathy. Although these cases usually result in enucleation, with strict follow-up of these patients and on-time referral to ocular oncologists, patient’s life and even the globe and the vision can be salvaged despite the initial delay in the diagnosis.
