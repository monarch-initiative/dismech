# Therapeutic targeting of syndecan-1 axis overcomes acquired resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy in gastrointestinal cancers.
**Authors:** Theardy MS, Takeda M, Sorokin A, Chen S, Yang Z, Wang X, Kanikarla P, Coker O, Nguyen P, Wei Y, Yao J, Yan L, Jin Y, Cai Y, Paku M, Chen Z, Li KZ, Citron F, Tomihara H, Gao S, Deem AK, Zhao J, Wang H, Hanash S, DePinho RA, Maitra A, Draetta GF, Ying H, Kopetz S, Yao W
**Journal:** Cell Rep Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xcrm.2025.102253](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2025.102253)

## Content

The therapeutic benefit of recently developed mutant KRAS (KRAS∗) inhibitors remains limited by the rapid onset of resistance. Here, we aim to delineate mechanisms underlying acquired resistance and identify actionable targets for overcoming this clinical challenge. Previously, we identified syndecan-1 (SDC1) as a key effector for pancreatic cancer progression whose surface expression is driven by KRAS∗. By leveraging both pancreatic and colorectal cancer models, we show that surface SDC1 expression initially diminishes upon KRAS∗ inhibition but recovers in tumor cells that bypass KRAS∗ dependency. Mechanistically, we reveal that YAP1 activation drives the recovery of SDC1 surface localization to enhance macropinocytosis-mediated nutrient salvaging and activation of multiple receptor tyrosine kinases for tumor maintenance, promoting resistance to KRAS∗-targeted therapy. Overall, our study provides a strong rationale for targeting the YAP-SDC1 axis to overcome resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition, thereby revealing promising therapeutic opportunities for improving the clinical outcome of patients with KRAS∗-mutated cancers.

KRAS is the most frequently mutated oncogene in human cancers.1 Mutant KRAS (KRAS∗) plays a critical role in disease initiation across various cancer types, such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Accordingly, it is detectable in early neoplastic lesions and remains functional in invasive and metastatic disease. Due to its high prevalence and central role in tumor progression, effective and specific targeting of KRAS∗ has been a long-standing priority in anti-cancer drug development. However, efforts to inhibit KRAS∗ and other key components of the RAS-mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/RAS-phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway have historically yielded limited clinical response, often due to compensatory feedback activation or high drug toxicity. Notably, recent advances have led to the development of highly selective KRASG12C inhibitors, including adagrasib (MRTX849)2 and sotorasib (AMG510),3 which have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of KRASG12C-mutated NSCLC. Despite this progress, therapy responses remain short-lived due to the rapid development of acquired resistance. As agents targeting other KRAS mutants, such as KRASG12D, enter clinical trials, there is an urgent need to better understand the molecular mechanisms driving acquired resistance to KRAS∗-targeted therapies and to develop viable strategies that can broaden the therapeutic potential of KRAS∗ inhibitors.

Recent studies using preclinical models have indicated that acquired resistance to KRASG12C inhibition may be mediated by the activation of multiple receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) regulators, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR), insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R), human epidermal growth factor 2 (HER2), and Src homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase 2 (SHP2), as well as downstream RAS effectors such as MYC and mTOR.2,4,5,6,7,8 Using a genetically engineered mouse (GEM) model of PDAC driven by inducible KRASG12D, we recently demonstrated that activation of the YAP1 oncogene drives resistance to genetic inactivation of KRAS∗, a finding further confirmed in KRAS∗-driven colorectal cancer (CRC).9,10,11 However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the re-activation of the RTK-RAS signaling pathway, as well as the contribution of YAP1 activation to the development of resistance to KRAS∗ inhibitors, remain to be elucidated. Clarifying the effector pathways that contribute to KRAS∗ inhibitor resistance will offer opportunities to enhance the efficacy and duration of KRAS∗-targeted therapies.

Recently, we identified syndecan-1 (SDC1), a cell surface proteoglycan, as a key effector downstream of KRAS∗ and demonstrated that KRAS∗-driven SDC1 membrane expression plays a critical role in PDAC progression.12 In this study, we aimed to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the role of SDC1 in the regulation of KRAS∗ dependency. We discovered that surface SDC1 expression was tightly correlated with acquired resistance to both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of KRAS∗ in preclinical models of PDAC and CRC. Mechanistically, we identified that the YAP1 oncogene is a major driver for SDC1 reactivation in cells resistant to KRAS∗ inhibition. Furthermore, our findings elucidated a critical role for the YAP1-SDC1 axis for activating multiple RTKs to establish acquired resistance to KRAS∗ blockade. Our findings highlight the translational potential of targeting the YAP1-SDC1 axis to overcome resistance to KRAS∗-targeted therapies.

Previously, we demonstrated that spontaneous tumor relapse following KrasG12D extinction in doxycycline-inducible KRASG12D-driven (iKras) GEM models of PDAC was driven by either KRAS∗-independent mechanisms (KRAS∗-Escapers; E−) or oncogene reactivation (KRAS∗-reactivated; E+).9 Interestingly, while KrasG12D extinction resulted in a dramatic downregulation of cell surface SDC1,12 membrane SDC1 levels were comparable between E+ and E− tumors in the iKras model or tumor-derived primary cultures, despite E− tumors exhibiting significantly weaker MAPK activity than E+ tumors (Figures 1A and 1B). These findings suggest that the recovery of membrane SDC1 in E− cells likely involves mechanisms beyond the reactivation of KRAS∗-MAPK signaling. The recovery of membrane SDC1 in these KRAS∗-bypass cells prompted us to characterize the dynamic changes of SDC1 membrane expression upon KRASG12D inactivation in iKras tumor cells. As we previously reported, KrasG12D inactivation upon doxycycline withdrawal led to rapid and continuous downregulation of surface SDC1 levels during the first week.12 However, surface SDC1 expression gradually re-emerged following long-term KRAS∗ extinction (Figures 1C and S1A). To investigate the role of surface SDC1 in KRAS∗-driven tumors in a clinically relevant context, we evaluated SDC1 surface expression upon pharmacological inhibition of KRAS∗ or its downstream MAPK pathway. Overall, we observed a bidirectional change in surface SDC1 expression consistent with that observed upon genetic KrasG12D extinction in iKras tumor cells. Specifically, inhibition of MAPK signaling using the MEK inhibitor, trametinib, led to initial depletion followed by restoration of surface SDC1 in both mouse iKras tumor cells and human PDAC cell lines, including MIA PaCa-2, AsPC-1, and HuP-T4 cells (Figures 1D and S1C). This pattern was also observed upon treatment with the KRASG12C inhibitor, AMG510, in KRASG12C-mutant PDAC (MIA PaCa-2) and CRC (SW837 and LIM2099) cells (Figures 1E and S1D) but not in AMG510-treated, non-responsive AsPC-1 (KRASG12D) or HuP-T4 (KRASG12V) cells (Figures S1E and S1F). To further validate the induction of surface SDC1 expression during adaptation to KRAS∗ inhibition, we generated AMG510-resistant (AMG510-R) cell lines from PDAC (MIA PaCa-2) and CRC (SW837 and LIM2099) cells that were chronically treated with AMG510 (Figure S1G and data not shown). Notably, surface SDC1 levels were significantly upregulated in AMG510-R cells compared to their parental counterparts (Figures 1F and S1H and data not shown), further supporting the association between surface SDC1 expression and the emergence of KRAS∗ independence.

To assess the role of SDC1 in KRAS∗ bypass, we first tested the capacity of enforced SDC1 expression to enable KRAS∗-independent tumor growth. Using two independent iKras tumor cell lines, we found that iKras cells transduced with a green fluorescent protein expression vector (GFP; negative control) failed to generate tumors in the absence of doxycycline (i.e., without KRAS∗ transgene expression), whereas iKras cells transduced with either KrasG12V (positive control) or SDC1 expression vectors formed tumors without doxycycline (Figure 2A). Notably, iKras cells expressing soluble SDC1 (lacking the cytoplasmic and transmembrane domains) were unable to support tumor growth, indicating that membrane localization of SDC1 is critical for bypassing KRAS∗ (Figure 2A). Correspondingly, SDC1 depletion using short hairpin RNA (shRNA) in primary cultures derived from KRAS∗-inactivated, SDC1-expressing iKras cells (SDC1-bypass [SB] cells; Figures S2A–S2C) abolished clonogenic activity (Figures 2B and S2D). Similarly, genetic depletion of SDC1 by shRNA abolished the clonogenic activity in both E− tumor cells and E+ cells derived from relapsed iKras tumors (Figure S2E), suggesting that SDC1 is essential for in vitro growth of both KRAS∗-independent and KRAS∗-dependent cancer cells. Consistent phenotypes were obtained by CRISPR-mediated deletion of SDC1 in mouse escaper cells (Figure S2F). Building on these findings, we next sought to assess whether SDC1 depletion could sensitize KRAS∗-driven tumors to targeted therapy against KRAS∗ or MAPK signaling. SDC1 knockdown using shRNA significantly sensitized mouse iKras tumor cells to trametinib in vitro and synergized with trametinib to significantly prolong survival in orthotopic xenograft models implanted with iKras tumor cells (Figures S2G–S2I). Similarly, SDC1 depletion sensitized human PDAC cells to trametinib or AMG510 in vitro (Figures S2J–S2K). MIA PaCa-2 cells were rendered resistant to AMG510 (AMG510-R) through chronic exposure to 500 nM AMG510. These AMG510-R cells exhibited sustained resistance, showing no growth inhibition regardless of the presence of the drug (Figure S2L). Furthermore, this resistance phenotype persisted even after 2 weeks following drug withdrawal (Figure S2M), indicating a stable, drug-independent adaptation. As observed in E− cells derived from relapsed iKras tumors (Figure S2E), SDC1 depletion markedly impaired colony formation and viability of AMG510-R PDAC and CRC cells treated with AMG510 (Figures 2C and S2N–S2Q). In the subcutaneous xenograft model, parental MIA PaCa-2 cells were highly sensitive to AMG510 treatment, whereas the AMG510-R cells remained completely resistant in vivo (Figures 2D and 2E), displaying striking SDC1 expression and high proliferation capacity, as indicated by Ki-67 staining (Figures 2F and 2G). Importantly, SDC1 knockdown in combination with AMG510 treatment significantly blunted the growth of AMG510-R tumors and led to complete tumor regression (Figures 2D–2G). Collectively, our findings underscore an integral role for SDC1 in enabling emergence of resistance to KRAS∗ genetic extinction or pharmacologic inhibition, providing a rationale for the development of SDC1-targeted therapies in tumors with acquired resistance to KRAS∗-targeted therapy.

To gain mechanistic insights into SDC1-mediated bypass of KRAS∗ dependency, reverse phase protein array (RPPA) profiling was conducted on primary cancer cell lines derived from SB tumors, as well as their parental GFP- or SDC1-expressing iKras cancer cells cultured in the presence or absence of doxycycline for 24 hours. Unsupervised clustering revealed that the protein expression profiles of SB cells derived from two individual iKras cells lines were distinct from those of the parental KRAS∗-driven cells (Figure 3A). While MAPK signaling components, such as p-ERK and c-RAF, were downregulated in SB cells, these cells exhibited features of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), including downregulation of epithelial markers E-cadherin and β-catenin, along with upregulation of mesenchymal markers such as Snail, N-cadherin, and collagen (Figure 3A). These patterns are reminiscent of the downregulation of MEK/MAPK signaling and induction of EMT observed in E− tumors9 (Figure S3A). Notably, many proteins highly overexpressed in SB cancer cells were involved in growth factor signaling, including multiple RTKs such as EGFR, PDGFRβ, c-MET, HER2, and IGF1R (Figure 3A). These RPPA findings were further validated by phospho-RTK array and western blot analyses, which showed hyperactivation of multiple RTKs, downregulation of MAPK signaling, and upregulation of AKT signaling in SB cells compared to parental iKras cells (Figures 3B and S3B). Similar RTK hyperactivation was also observed in E− cells from the iKras model (Figure S3C) and AMG510-R cells (Figure 3C). Although the specific RTK activation profiles vary between models, KRAS∗ inhibition broadly led to pronounced activation of multiple RTKs (Figure S3D), coinciding with the recovery of cell surface SDC1 expression (Figures 1C–1E and S1A), highlighting the dynamic nature of RTK signaling and its alignment with the temporal regulation of SDC1 upon KRAS inhibition. Importantly, SDC1 depletion in SB cells (Figure S3D) and AMG510-R cells (Figure S3E) significantly suppressed the activation of multiple RTKs as well as downstream AKT-mTOR signaling (Figures 3C–3E and S3F–S3H). ARF6 activity is tightly regulated by guanine-nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), which activate ARF6 activity by facilitating GDP-to-GTP exchange, and GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), which inactivate ARF6 activity by catalyzing GTP hydrolysis. We previously identified PSD4, an ARF6 GEF, as a key regulator of ARF6 activity in KRAS∗-driven PDAC cells, with its transcription suppressed upon KRAS or MEK inactivation.12 Interestingly, PSD4 expression remained low in KRAS∗-bypass cells (Figures 4C and S4C), indicating that PSD4 is unlikely to be responsible for ARF6 activation and surface SDC1 recovery. To further investigate the mechanisms driving ARF6 reactivation during resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition, we mined the expression profiles of E− and E+ cells9 for ARF6-specific GEFs and GAPs. Our data revealed significant downregulation of ARF6-specific GAPs, including ASAP2, ARAP2, and ARAP3, in E− compared to E+ cells (Figure 4D). This was further validated by western blot analysis, which showed that the downregulation of ASAP2 and ARAP2 closely paralleled the recovery of surface SDC1 in mouse iKras cells and human MIA PaCa-2 cells during acquired resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition (Figures 4C and 4E). A similar decrease in GAP expression was observed in human PDAC cells following long-term MEK inhibition (Figure S4C). Importantly, reconstitution of ASAP2 expression in AMG510-R cells significantly diminished surface SDC1 levels (Figures 4F and 4G), underscoring the critical role of ASAP2 downregulation in SDC1 recovery during acquired resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition.

We further sought to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying ASAP2 downregulation during acquired resistance to KRAS inhibition. We had previously identified YAP1 activation, through either gene amplification or activation of a non-canonical WNT pathway, as a major driver for bypassing KRAS dependency in PDAC.9,11 Interestingly, in iKras PDAC mouse cells or MIA PaCa-2 cells following long-term genetic or pharmacological KRAS∗ inactivation, as well as in human PDAC cells upon long-term MEK inhibition, YAP1 protein accumulation coincided with a decrease in ASAP2 and ARAP2 expression (Figures 4C, 4E, and S4C). This was associated with decreased YAP phosphorylation, leading to YAP1 protein stabilization and nuclear translocation, thereby activating the YAP oncogenic signaling pathway (Figures S4D and S4E). Accordingly, YAP downstream effectors CTGF and CYR61 were concomitantly upregulated in both MIA PaCa-2 and SW837 cell lines upon AMG510 treatment (Figure S4E). While YAP1 knockdown had no effect on ARAP2 or ASAP2 levels, or on the clonogenic activity of KRAS∗-dependent cells, its depletion dramatically upregulated ARAP2 and ASAP2 expression, reduced surface SDC1 levels, and suppressed the clonogenic activity of KRAS∗-independent cells (Figures 4H–4J, S4F, and S4G). These findings were further confirmed using iKras cells with CRISPR-Cas9-mediated Yap1 knockout, which demonstrated that Yap1 deletion abolished downregulation of ASAP2 expression (Figure 4K) and blocked the recovery of surface SDC1 levels (Figure 4L) following KRAS∗ inhibition. Accordingly, YAP deletion fully abrogated ARF6 activity, as ASAP2 levels remained unrepressed, and knocking down ASAP2 thereby restored ARF6 activity, indicating that YAP1-mediated repression of ASAP2 is essential for ARF6 activation and for SDC1 recycling to the membrane in KRAS∗-resistant cells (Figure S4H). Importantly, YAP1 ablation significantly diminished the proliferative and clonogenic activity of KRAS∗-independent, but not parental KRAS∗-dependent, cells (Figures 4M and S4I). These data suggest that YAP1 directly or indirectly mediates the downregulation of ARF6 GAPs to promote SDC1 surface expression during acquired resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition.

Our study further demonstrates that the adaptive resistance mechanism through the YAP1-SDC1 axis is conserved across multiple KRAS∗-resistant models, including resistance to pharmacological inhibition of KRASG12D. Following prolonged MRTX1133 treatment, we observed a recovery of SDC1 expression that coincided with YAP1 upregulation and RTK activation in the patient-derived cell culture, PATC66, harboring KRASG12D (Figures S5A and S5B). Suppression of SDC1 in PATC66-derived MRTX1133-resistant (MRTX1133-R) cells significantly abrogated colony formation ability and attenuated RTK activation (Figures S5C–S5F). Moreover, YAP1 knockdown in MRTX1133-R cells led to derepression of ARF6-GAPs, resulting in reduced surface localization of SDC1 and further loss of clonogenic growth (Figures S5G–S5I). These findings establish the YAP1-SDC1 axis as a conserved and functionally relevant mechanism of resistance across different KRAS mutants.

The ability to stimulate macropinocytosis, a regulated form of endocytosis, is a distinctive feature of KRAS∗ activation,13 and PDAC cells harboring KRAS∗ rely on increased levels of macropinocytosis for nutrient salvaging to sustain uncontrolled cell growth.14 Our previous study demonstrated that the surface localization of SDC1, driven by KRAS∗ activation, plays a crucial role in maintaining macropinocytosis in PDAC.12 To better understand the function of SDC1 and the requirement of macropinocytosis in acquiring resistance to KRAS∗ inhibitors, we examined macropinocytic activity in KRAS∗-bypass tumors. Although extinction of KRAS∗ leads to a rapid reduction in SDC1 surface expression and macropinocytosis,12 macropinocytosis levels in E− tumor cells were comparable to those in E+ cells and iKras cells (Figures 5A and 5B), suggesting that macropinocytosis is recovered in these escapers through a KRAS∗-independent mechanism. The elevated macropinocytic activity in the context of KRAS∗ bypass was confirmed in MIA PaCa-2 AMG510-R-resistant cells. Our data suggest that macropinocytosis is recovered in E− cells, possibly through KRAS∗-independent but SDC1-dependent mechanisms. Indeed, ablation of SDC1 abolished macropinocytic activity in both E− cells and AMG510-R MIA PaCa-2 cells (Figures 5E–5H, S6A, and S6B), indicating that SDC1 is required for macropinocytosis in these KRAS∗-bypass cells. These findings, together with the observation that YAP1 mediates the downregulation of ARF6-GAPs to promote SDC1 surface localization, suggest that YAP1 may drive SDC1-dependent macropinocytosis in cells resistant to KRAS∗ inhibition. The small GTPase Rac1 plays a crucial role in initiating membrane ruffle formation and macropinocytosis.15 Our previous study showed that RAC1 activity is mediated by SDC1 to promote macropinocytosis.12 Here, we found that RAC1 activity is elevated in E− and AMG510-R cells compared to iKras cells and parental MIA PaCa-2 cells, respectively (Figures 5I and S6C). In AMG510-R cells, ectopic expression of dominant-negative RAC1 (RAC1(T27N)) suppressed macropinocytic activity without affecting SDC1 expression (Figures 5J, 5K, S6D, and S6E). Together, these data indicate that the SDC1-Rac1 axis is required for macropinocytosis in tumor cells resistant to KRAS∗-targeted therapy.

To assess the applicability of our findings in clinically relevant models, we evaluated responses to AMG510 in a cohort of CRC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models harboring KRASG12C. Tumors were stratified as sensitive (B8026, C1047) or moderately sensitive (C1177, F3008) based on tumor growth rates under AMG510 treatment (Figure S8A). To analyze the expression and function of the YAP1-SDC1 axis during acquired resistance to KRASG12C inhibition, we developed resistant models (B8026-R, C1177-R, and F3008-R) from AMG510-sensitive CRC PDX tumors following long-term AMG510 treatment (Figures S8B–S8D). While 70%–90% of PDAC cases exhibit high SDC1 expression,12 parental CRC PDXs exhibited only low SDC1 expression (Figure S8E). In contrast, both SDC1 and YAP1 are highly induced in PDX tumors with acquired resistance to AMG510 (Figures 7A, S8F, and S8G), thus underscoring activation of the YAP1-SDC1 axis during acquired resistance to KRAS∗-targeted therapy. While the recent development of small-molecule inhibitors specific for oncogenic KRAS has revolutionized the treatment landscape for KRAS∗-driven cancers, the efficacy and applicability of these therapies have been limited by the rapid onset of acquired resistance. For instance, AMG510 treatment in patients with KRASG12C-driven NSCLC achieved a response rate of over 30%,18,19 but all patients eventually developed therapeutic resistance, limiting long-term survival.19 Similarly, our previous work using an inducible KrasG12D-driven PDAC model demonstrated spontaneous tumor relapse in the majority of animals following genetic extinction of KrasG12D.9 Such acquired resistance to KRASG12D inhibition is also expected to emerge in ongoing clinical trials of multiple KRAS-targeted therapies. Vertical reactivation of the RAS pathway elements has been observed in most samples collected from patients who developed resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors.4,8,20,21 One major route leading to such recovery of RAS activity is the activation of RTKs through either genetic (e.g., gene amplification or fusion) or non-genetic mechanisms.2,4,5,6,7,8,22,23,24,25 Our study similarly revealed the activation of multiple RTKs in AMG510-R tumors upon characterization of AMG510-R tumors from mouse iKras-escaper mice and CRC PDX models, consistent with a recent study using human cancer cell lines.6 This finding suggests that targeting multiple RTKs rather than a single RTK is likely required to overcome acquired resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition. Previously, inhibition of multiple RTKs through adaptors like SOS and SHP2, which function downstream of RTKs to promote RAS activation, has shown promise in preclinical studies aimed at mitigating acquired resistance to KRAS inhibition.7,22,26 However, concerns regarding the therapeutic window and potential toxicity limit this approach. Furthermore, early studies have shown that activation of FOXO transcription factors or downregulation of MYC can trigger feedback activation of RTK transcription following inhibition of the PI3K/AKT or MEK pathways,27,28 but these adaptive mechanisms are challenging to therapeutically target. Here, we focus on SDC1, a transmembrane proteoglycan composed of a membrane-embedded core protein and covalently linked glycosaminoglycan chains, primarily heparan sulfate (HS) and chondroitin sulfate. The HS chain of SDC1 has been shown to act as a co-receptor for growth factors, promoting the activation of several RTKs, such as EGFR, c-MET, FGFR, and IGF1R.29,30,31,32 Accordingly, our data indicate that SDC1 functions as a common upstream regulator for the activation of multiple RTKs in tumor cells with acquired resistance to oncogenic KRAS inhibition. Additionally, SDC1 is localized to the cell surface and is dispensable for normal tissue homeostasis.33 Together, these findings suggest that SDC1 may serve as an actionable target to suppress the activation of multiple RTKs and overcome resistance to KRAS∗-targeted therapy.

Previously, we demonstrated that SDC1 plays a critical role for oncogenic KRAS-mediated macropinocytosis,12 a nutrient salvage process that supports oncogenic growth.14 Here we show that, while macropinocytosis and surface SDC1 levels rapidly decreased upon inhibition of oncogenic KRAS, SDC1-mediated macropinocytosis recovers in tumor cells upon acquiring resistance to KRAS inhibition. Macropinocytosis has also been implicated in resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors and standard-of-care agents, such as gemcitabine and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU).34,35 Together, these findings suggest that SDC1-mediated macropinocytosis may represent a general mechanism underlying therapy resistance.

Localization of SDC1 to the plasma membrane is critical for its function in both RTK activation and macropinocytosis. Our previous study indicates that ARF6 activity downstream of oncogenic KRAS signaling controls SDC1 surface localization in PDAC cells.12 Building upon this discovery, our current study demonstrates that SDC1, a critical downstream surrogate of oncogenic KRAS, can reestablish its membrane localization independently of KRAS-MAPK signaling during the development of resistance to KRAS inhibition. In the iKras GEM model, acute extinction of mutant KRAS results in downregulation of SDC1 expression, coinciding with suppression of MAPK signaling, as evidenced by reduced ERK phosphorylation.12,17 However, in resistant tumors that emerge following prolonged KRAS extinction, SDC1 expression is restored despite sustained MAPK suppression. This observation suggests that the recovery of SDC1 expression over time is independent of MAPK reactivation. Although partial reactivation of the MAPK pathway has been reported in KRAS-inhibited human PDAC cells due to compensatory feedback mechanisms,36 not all downstream effectors of MAPK signaling are uniformly restored. Previously, we demonstrated that surface localization of SDC1 is regulated through the MAPK-PSD4-ARF6 axis, wherein MAPK activation induces PSD4 expression, leading to ARF6 activation and subsequent SDC1 trafficking to the membrane.12 Genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of KRAS disrupts this pathway, resulting in decreased surface SDC1 expression. Notably, in the current study, PSD4 expression remains suppressed upon KRAS inhibition, irrespective of MAPK signaling status. Instead, we identified a compensatory mechanism in which ARF6 activity, and consequently surface SDC1 localization, is restored via the downregulation of ARF6 GAPs, mediated by adaptive activation of YAP1. This alternative route allows ARF6 to become reactivated despite the persistent loss of PSD4, thereby restoring membrane SDC1 in the absence of MAPK signaling. This selective restoration of signaling outputs supports the existence of adaptive rewiring under KRAS suppression. These findings are in line with recent work characterizing the complex and dynamic signaling changes following KRAS inhibition, particularly the delayed emergence of adaptive and compensatory mechanisms that contribute to acquired resistance.37 Together, our data reveal a YAP1-dependent bypass mechanism that uncouples SDC1 regulation from canonical MAPK signaling, highlighting the plasticity of oncogenic signaling networks in response to targeted KRAS inhibition. Further studies are needed to further elucidate mechanisms by which YAP1 transcriptionally regulates ARF6 GAPs.

The YAP1 oncogene has been well established as one of the major drivers of bypassing oncogene addiction and promoting acquired resistance to targeted therapies in several cancer types, including pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, colorectal cancer, melanoma, and glioblastoma.9,10,38,39,40 Here, we show that, while YAP1 is not directly involved in the KRAS signaling cascade, it indirectly functions to sustain KRAS downstream signaling during acquired resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition by maintaining plasma membrane localization of SDC1 for the activation of multiple RTKs and the induction of macropinocytosis. Notably, while both YAP1 and its downstream effector CYR61 exhibit basal level of expression in KRAS-dependent PDAC cells, we show that depletion of YAP1 does not affect ASAP2/ARAP2 expression in these cells when KRAS remains active. These findings align with accumulated evidence that YAP1 is dispensable for maintaining advanced KRAS-driven tumors, which are primarily sustained by oncogenic KRAS.41,42 However, previous studies have demonstrated that YAP1 signaling is essential for the initiation of KRAS-driven PDAC.43 These findings suggest the context-dependent role of YAP1 in tumor progression. Our findings further reveal that YAP1 regulates ASAP2/ARAP2 expression specifically under KRAS-inhibited condition, underscoring the context-dependent function of YAP1 and the dynamic regulation of its target genes during PDAC progression and maintenance. Furthermore, although YAP1 is essential for embryonic development,44 genetic studies in various mouse models suggest that YAP1 is dispensable for the homeostasis of normal cells in adults.45 Together, these data underscore the potential of therapeutically targeting YAP1 as an innovative strategy for overcoming the acquired resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition. Indeed, depletion of YAP1 with YAP1-ASOs sensitized CRC PDX models to AMG510 treatment without exerting overt toxicity in animals. Because the YAP1-ASOs used in our study are currently under phase 1 trial for advanced solid tumors (NCT04659096), and multiple YAP1 inhibitors are under clinical development, we are confident that our findings have the potential for rapid translation into the clinic for combination trials with iExosomes46 (NCT03608631) or small molecular inhibitors targeting oncogenic KRAS.

In summary, our study uncovers a previously unrecognized mechanism of resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition, revealing the YAP1-SDC1 axis as a critical and druggable mediator of KRAS∗ independence in gastrointestinal cancers. While coordinated activation of multiple RTKs has been a persistent clinical challenge in targeted cancer therapy, our findings demonstrate that SDC1 plays a central role in coordinating the activation of multiple RTKs, thereby driving resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition. This positions SDC1 as a promising therapeutic target to broadly counteract RTK-mediated resistance. This mechanistic insight provides a compelling rationale for targeting the YAP1-SDC1 axis as a previously unrecognized therapeutic vulnerability in KRAS∗-driven tumors. The translational relevance of these findings is especially significant for PDAC, where KRAS is the most prevalent oncogene and effective treatment options remain scarce. Although the development of mutant-selective and pan-RAS inhibitors marks a major advance, resistance remains a substantial barrier, underscoring the need for complementary strategies. Our study offers foundational insight into adaptive responses to both G12C and G12D inhibition and identifies a unifying mechanism of resistance with potential applicability across multiple KRAS-mutant tumor types. While we acknowledge the limitations of in vitro system and in vivo preclinical models, future studies in clinical specimens will be essential to further validate the YAP1-SDC1 axis. Nevertheless, our findings establish a strong rationale for developing therapeutic strategies targeting YAP1 and SDC1, with the potential to improve the durability and effectiveness of KRAS-targeted therapies in PDAC and other malignancies.

While our work has established the YAP1-SDC1 axis as a key adaptive mechanism to KRAS∗ inhibition, several important limitations warrant further investigation. Firstly, given the known heterogeneity of acquired resistance to KRAS∗ inhibition, additional studies should characterize other oncogenic drivers of surface SDC1 levels independent of YAP1 activation and KRAS∗ reactivation. Secondly, the precise mechanism by which YAP1 suppresses ARF6 GAP expression remains to be fully elucidated, particularly whether this occurs in a direct or indirect manner. In addition, while this study primarily focused on ASAP2 for the regulation of ARF6 activity, the potential functional redundancy among other ARF6 GAP family members requires additional investigation. Lastly, future work is needed to develop other therapeutic modalities directly targeting surface SDC1.
