Inherited erythromelalgia, small fibre neuropathy and paroxysmal extreme pain disorder are caused by gain-of-function mutations in the voltage-gated sodium channel Nav1.7. It remains unknown how different mutations in the same channel enhancing electrogenesis in sensory neurons results in such distinct disease presentations.

Most of the work analysing the impact of these mutations on electrophysiological properties has used overexpression systems in cell lines and rodent sensory neurons, which might differ from the natural context. We have differentiated sensory neurons from induced pluripotent stem cells derived from patient samples that have the Nav1.7 A1632G mutation.

This strategy reveals changes in electrophysiological properties, not previously observed in cell lines, that might be important for disease presentation. Furthermore, using CRISPR/Cas9, we corrected this mutation, which reduced the underlying hyperexcitability, providing a path for personalized medicine to treat these disorders, and we introduced the mutation into control induced pluripotent stem cells, which generated hyperexcitability, providing causality.

Induced pluripotent stem cell sensory neurons are a robust, scalable and relevant model to study the effects of gain-of-function mutations in ion channels in pain-related disorders.

Keywords:Nav1.7, inherited erythromelalgia, small fibre neuropathy, paroxysmal extreme pain disorder, gain-of-function mutations, voltage-gated sodium channels

Different gain-of-function mutations in the sodium channel Nav1.7 give rise to distinct phenotypes. To help identify the specific changes that drive this variability, Shimet al.develop a new pipeline for evaluating gain-of-function mutations in sodium channels, using patient-derived iPSCs differentiated into sensory neurons.

Gain-of-function mutations in genes that encode voltage-gated sodium channels, notablyScn9a(Nav1.7),Scn10a(Nav1.8) andScn11a(Nav1.9), cause a spectrum of painful diseases, including small fibre neuropathy, inherited erythromelalgia (IEM) and paroxysmal extreme pain disorder.1-6Distinct mutations throughout these genes can result in drastically different patient diseases, yet the associated mutations do not cluster in specific domains as is the case in most other inherited diseases.3,7For example, the F1449V mutation causing IEM lies close on the third intracellular loop (L3) to the I1461T mutation causing paroxysmal extreme pain disorder.7,8In fact, even distinct mutations at the same residue can result in distinct symptoms; A1632G mutations are associated with IEM, whereas A1632E mutations have a phenotype mixed between IEM and paroxysmal extreme pain disorder.9,10This suggests that the specific effects of the mutation on the channel biophysical properties are crucial to determining the disease mechanism and therefore the therapeutic strategy.

Previous efforts to characterize the effects of these mutations at the channel and cell levels have focused on overexpression of the mutant channel using either genetically modified immortalized cell lines or cultured rodent dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons.10-12Although this has been foundational for understanding the biophysical consequences of different mutations, it presents several limitations, including a lack of neural identity, electrical activity and morphological motifs when using immortalized cell lines, and the injury status of dissected and cultured rodent DRGs, in addition to the challenge of obtaining high numbers of cells for a high-throughput analysis.13

Several protocols have been developed that enable the differentiation of patient-derived fibroblasts into sensory or sensory-like neurons (SNs).14-17For modelling the effects of gain-of-function mutations, this has the distinct advantages of having the genetic background of the patient, a neural status similar to sensory neuronsin vivo, and that induced overexpression of the channel is not needed. Furthermore, using gene-editing strategies, such as Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)/Cas9, the mutation can be corrected for a direct confirmation of its effects.

We present here a pipeline to study gain-of-function sodium channel mutations, using a specific A1632G mutation in Nav1.7 as a proof of principle for the effectiveness of this strategy. Interestingly, we observe changes in this model system previously not seen with cell lines and overexpression systems, including alterations in the resting membrane potential (RMP) and multi-spike firing. Furthermore, using CRISPR/Cas9, when we correct the mutation we observe a restoration of normal activity, providing direct evidence that this mutation causes the pathological changes in neural activity. This is, therefore, a powerful and robust approach for determining the pathogenicity of ion channel mutations in sensory neurons.

Patients provided informed consent to participate in this study and to derive induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from their blood samples. The study was approved by the Human Investigation Committees at Yale University and the Veterans Affairs Medical Center, West Haven, CT, USA.

Cas9 target sites were identified using an online CRISPR tool (https://www.zlab.bio/resources), and guide RNAs were designed accordingly and ordered from Synthego. To knock in the desired mutations, we used a CRISPR ribonucleoprotein complex strategy. The guide RNA and Cas9 protein were combined to prepare the ribonucleoprotein complex, which was then delivered into cultured healthy control iPSCs along with the single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide template (180 bp, Integrated DNA Technologies) via nucleofection. Given that this approach does not introduce foreign DNA into the cells (unlike a plasmid-based approach), the ribonucleoprotein complex is degraded over time, which reduces the probability of off-target effects. The nucleofected cells were then transferred to a six-well plate with StemFlex medium containing 10 µM Y-27632. Medium was exchanged the next day with StemFlex medium. Two days after nucleofection, cells underwent single-cell dissociation with Accutase and were seeded at a density of 3000 cells per 10 cm plate. One week later, colonies were picked into a 96-well plate. To check which colonies were edited successfully to carry the point mutation, the gene was amplified by PCR and sent out for Sanger sequencing. Successfully engineered colonies were isolated and expanded. iPSCs were maintained in StemFlex medium and passaged using phosphate buffered saline and ReLeSR enzyme-free reagent. Medium was replaced every 2–3 days, and passaging was conducted when wells/plates were ∼80% confluent.

Differentiation was carried out according to a modified protocol originally described by Chamberset al.14iPSCs were passaged using Accutase and seeded as single cells on six-well culture plates at a density of ∼300 000 cells per well. Alternatively, cells were also seeded on 10 cm Petri dishes at a density of ∼1.5 million cells per dish. All plates and dishes were coated with Geltrex, and the cells were seeded in StemFlex medium containing 10 µM Y-27632. One day after single-cell dissociation, medium was replaced with pure StemFlex. When iPSCs reached ∼80% confluence in the well/dish, a 5-day course of dual-SMAD inhibition was initiated to induce neuralization. The two SMAD inhibitors were LDN-193189 (100 nM) and SB431542 (10 µM). Three additional small molecules inhibitors [CHIR99021 (10 µM), DAPT (10 µM) and SU5402 (10 µM)] were added on Days 6–10. Inhibitors were delivered in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/F12 media containing 20% KnockOut serum replacement, 1% non-essential amino acids (100×), 1% GlutaMax (100×) and 2-mercaptoethanol (55 mM). From Day 11 to replating on Day 50–60, cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and supplemented with five growth factors: BDNF, GDNF, NGF, Neurotropin-3 (NT-3) and ascorbic acid (all at 10 µg/ml). Cultures were treated with mitomycin-C on Day 15 to remove proliferating, non-neuronal cells; afterwards, the medium was changed three times per week.

For sequencing, iPSC sensory neurons were differentiated as above, allowed to mature for 4 weeks, then replated to a density of 10 000 cells per well and maintained for 2 weeks before isolation of RNA with RLT Buffer with β-mercaptoethanol and stored at −80°C. Samples were shipped to the UCLA Neuroscience Genomics Core, where sequencing was performed with the HiSeq-4000. Samples were initally pooled and barcoded, and the library was prepared using TruSeq Stranded RNA (100 ng) + RiboZero Gold. Preparation included 69 bp paired-end reads. Reads were aligned to the human_hg38 reference genome using the STAR spliced read aligner. Total counts of read fragments aligned to known gene regions within the hg38 Ensembl gene annotation were used as the basis for quantification of gene expression. Five total samples were sequenced, each a replicate differentiation. Genes exhibiting low expression were filtered and normalized by Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM). For calculation of the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), unknown batch effects were corrected by the Remove Unwanted Variation Using Residuals (RUVr) method and then values were normalized for gene length.

Mature iPSC-derived sensory neurons were replated onto 35 mm dishes for fluorescent-dye calcium imaging. Dishes were coated with poly-D-lysine, washed three times with autoclaved water, and subsequently coated with laminin, all prior to cell seeding. Neurons were maintained for 3 days prior to imaging. On the day of imaging, Fura-2 dye (4 μg/ml) was added to the medium and kept in relative darkness for 45 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed three times with standard external solution (SES). In our 7-min-long imaging protocol, five different stimuli were perfused for 15 s each at the start of minutes 2–5. The stimulants used were, in order: α,β-methylene-ATP (10 µM); menthol (500 µM); allyl isothiocyanate (AITC200 µM); capsaicin (10 µM); and potassium chloride (40 mM). SES was perfused in the 5 min 45 s when stimulants were not being applied. All imaging was carried out at 37°C. Recordings were made using the software Nikon NIS Elements.

Regions of interest were drawn within the Nikon NIS Elements software and extractedF340/F380ratio measurements were exported into Microsoft Excel for analysis and visualization. Fluorescence ratios were normalized to the 30 s period immediately prior to the application of ATP, hence why the generated graph begins at 0.5 min. NormalizedF340/F380ratio ≥ 1.1 during the 15 s stimulant application periods was designated as activation of the cell to the respective stimulus, based on the criteria established by Waingeret al.18Only regions of interest that exhibited a response to the positive control KCl were included in analysis.

iPSC-SNs were thawed and maintained in neuronal growth medium (NGM) consisting of DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, growth factors (NGF, BDNF, GDNF and NT-3; 10 ng/ml) and 35 μg/ml ascorbic acid at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2incubator.

Voltage-clamp recordings were performed on isolated iPSC-SNs using an EPC-10 double amplifier and Patchmaster software (HEKA Electronik). Recordings were obtained at 22°C ± 1°C, >48 h after plating, in alignment with prior investigations studying the A1632G mutation.10Recordings were started 5 min after establishing the whole-cell configuration, sampled at a rate of 50 kHz and filtered at 2.9 kHz. For recording of sodium currents, the following solutions were used (millimolar): 140 NaCl, 3 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 1 CaCl2, 10 HEPES, 5 CsCl, 20 TEA-Cl, 0.1 CdCl2and 1 4-aminopyridine, with pH adjusted to 7.3 with NaOH (332 mOsm/l). The pipette solution contained the following (millimolar): 140 CsF, 10 NaCl, 1 EGTA and 10 HEPES, with pH adjusted to 7.3 with CsOH (310 mOsm/l). Patch pipettes were fire polished and fabricated from borosilicate glass (World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA). Series resistance was compensated 80%–85% and held at −100 mV. Linear leak subtraction was applied before the test pulse using P/8 leak subtraction.

To isolate somatic currents and avoid space-clamp artefacts, a voltage prepulse protocol was used to inactivate distal sodium channels as previously described.19The prepulse (4 ms, −40 to −10 mV) was followed by a repolarizing interpulse (1 ms, −120 to −70 mV), which preceded the regular test pulse (40 ms, −80 to +40 mV in 10 mV steps). Prepulse and interpulse voltage and duration were adjusted for each cell individually to eliminate space-clamp artefacts and obtain optimal voltage-clamp conditions. Two batches of differentiation were analysed with similar properties.

Whole-cell current-clamp recordings were obtained using an Axon MultiClamp 700B amplifier and Digidata 1440a. Protocols were defined and executed using pClamp v.10.6 software. The data were filtered at 5 kHz and acquired at 50 kHz. Electrodes used for the recordings had a resistance of <1.5 MΩ when filled with the internal solution, which consisted of (millmolar): 140 KCl, 5 HEPES, 0.5 EGTA, 3 Mg-ATP and 20 dextrose 20; pH 7.3, 295–300 mOsm. The external recording solution contained (millmolar): 140 NaCl, 3 KCl, 10 HEPES-NaOH, 2 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, and 15 dextrose, pH 7.3, ∼320 mOsm. Only cells with a stable membrane potential (<10% variation) were chosen for analysis. The RMP was defined as the membrane potential in the absence of any current stimulation. The action potential (AP) firing rate was determined by quantifying the number of action potentials generated in response to 500 ms incremental depolarizing current steps applied at RMPs. Current threshold was established by injecting depolarizing 200 ms current steps in 5 pA increments.

Acquired data were analysed using pClamp v.10.6 (Molecular Devices), Origin 2018 (OriginLab Co.) and Excel (Microsoft Co.). Unless stated otherwise, data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean;nrefers to the number of cells. All datasets were subjected to normality tests and subsequent appropriate statistical analyses using Origin 2018 (OriginLab Co.) and SPSS v.24 (IBM SPSS Statistics). Individual tests used for each dataset are stated in the Results section. Two batches of differentiation were analysed with similar properties.

Statistical tests were performed as described in the main text and figure legends. All statistics were performed with GraphPad Prism v.10.

We generated induced sensory neurons (iSNs) using a modified version of the protocol introduced by Chamberset al.14previously characterized by Younget al.17(see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). The key changes in the protocol include shortening the amount of incubation time with nociceptor-promoting inhibitors and the addition of NT-3 and ascorbic acid to the medium during maturation. In addition, we matured iSNs in culture for >45 days before testing.

Bulk RNA sequencing and calcium imaging were performed to confirm the similarity in the identity of these neurons to those generated by the protocol of Younget al.17The RNA sequencing confirmed the presence of voltage-gated sodium channels, including robustScn9aexpression and its associated β-subunits, but littleScn10aorScn11a(Fig. 1A). Other sodium channels important in DRG electrogenesis in specific physiological settings, such asScn3aandScn8a, were also present. In addition, we found expression ofScn2aandScn7a, which are present in general neuronal and glial populations but are notably absent from DRGs.

To gain further insight into the genetic identity of the iSNs, we examined the expression of known marker genes of specific DRG populations, including A- and C-fibres, somatostatin (SST) neurons, transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M (melastatin) member 8 (TRPM8) neurons, C-low-threshold mechanoreceptor neurons and proprioceptors. The iSNs expressed high levels of Aβ-fibre marker genes, includingRet,Ntrk2,Slc17a7andNefh20(Fig. 1B). Markers of C-fibres were also present, includingRunx1,Adra2a,Sstr2andDcn, but notably, expression ofPou4f3,Calca,Bmpr1bandOprk1was absent. The markers of other DRG neuronal cell types were not observed, with the exception of SST, which displayed some of the highest FPKM values. This suggests that we produced a mixed population of Aβ-fibre, C-fibre and SST-like sensory neurons, similar to that found by Younget al.17Examination of the expression of somatosensory transducing receptors confirmed this, with the iSNs expressingPiezo1andPiezo2, in addition to high expression ofP2rx3andTrpv2(Fig. 1C).Trpv1was also expressed, albeit at low levels.

Using the cell-permeable calcium indicator Fura-2 we characterized the response properties of iSNs to a variety of chemical stimulators, including αβ-MeATP (a P2RX3 receptor agonist), menthol (a TRPM8 receptor agonist), AITC (a TRPA1 receptor agonist) and capsaicin (a TRPV1 receptor agonist) (Fig. 1D). Out of the 250 neurons recorded from, the majority (48%) were unresponsive to these chemical stimulations, hypothetically being mechanosensors; 31.6% were responsive to αβ-MeATP, in line with the high expression of the P2RX3 receptor, 10.8% were responsive only to capsaicin, 0.4% only to menthol, and none only to AITC (Fig. 1E). The remaining neurons were responsive to a variety of the agonists, in order of highest percentage to lowest including 4.8% responsive to both capsaicin and αβ-MeATP, 1.6% to capsaicin, αβ-MeATP and AITC, 1.2% to αβ-MeATP and AITC, 0.8% to all, 0.4% to capsaicin and AITC, and 0.4% to αβ-MeATP, menthol and AITC (Fig. 1E). This demonstrates that this protocol produces a mixed population of primary sensory-like neurons, including both Aβ-fibre- and C-fibre-like neurons.

A 39-year-old female and her daughter presented with IEM, with symptoms beginning during childhood (age 7–8 years).10We previously performed Sanger sequencing ofScn9aand found a heterozygous (c.4895C>G) mutation within the family, which leads to a change from alanine to glycine in the amino acid sequence at position 1632. Classic symptoms of IEM were present in both patients, including reddening and pain in the hands and feet, which were triggered by exercise and changes in temperature. The maximal level of pain during some episodes reached 8 on the pain numerical rating scale. To assess pain characteristics and severity over time, the patients completed a daily pain diary over a 6-week period (Table 1). The number, duration and severity of painful attacks, in addition to incidences and duration of awakening at night were captured. Furthermore, we assessed their common triggers to ascertain what underlies the attack onset.

As detailed inTable 1, both patients exhibited multiple pain attacks throughout the 6-week period, with at least one per day, often with the total attack time exceeding 1 h. Average self-reported pain scores during an attack were usually between 3 and 4, whereas pain scores between attacks were rated between 1 and 2. For both patients, the pain disrupted sleep, with one of the patients affected more than the other, waking up 27 times with an average duration of time awake of 13 min.

In both cases, there were common sets of triggers, with activity making up most of the triggering events (Table 1). This included walking, sitting down, yardwork, typing on a computer and driving a car. Exposure to heat was the second highest trigger, followed by showering, suggesting that changes in temperature are an important driver of pain attacks. Lastly, ≤17% of the pain attacks appeared spontaneously. This demonstrates both quantitatively and qualitatively how IEM drastically impacts the quality of life.

The location of the A1632G mutation is at the S4–S5 linker in domain III of Nav1.7. The S4–S5 linker transduces the outward movement of S4 upon depolarization of the cell membrane, which induces the opening of the activation gate of the channel.21The A1632G substitution in the S4–S5 linker is predicted to allow greater structural flexibility, which is consistent with the altered channel gating. Studies in HEK293 cells and rodent DRGs have observed a hyperpolarizing shift in activation, coupled with increased spontaneous and evoked firing and a decreased current threshold.10Given the potential limitations of using non-neuronal cell lines (HEK293) and overexpression of channels in rodent DRGs, we wondered whether patient-derived induced sensory neurons would reveal new electrophysiological alterations and whether correction of the mutation could restore electrical activity to normal.

Using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing we corrected the 4895G>C, reverting Gly back to an Ala at position 1632, then differentiated both the patient A1632G line and the corrected line into iSNs. Owing to neurite outgrowth of iSNs in culture and space-clamp artefacts, insufficient voltage control resulted in distorted voltage-clamp measurements. To investigate the voltage dependence of the activation of the corrected clone, we therefore used a prepulse voltage protocol that allows for somatic sodium channel activation without space-clamp artefacts.19

We then assessed the passive and active membrane properties of iSNs derived from the A1632G patient, in addition to two clones of a CRISPR-corrected line. We found pronounced and consistent differences in excitability between mutation-bearing iSNs and CRISPR-corrected sensory neurons. In contrast to the previous data from rodent DRG neurons overexpressing the A1632G channel, the average RMP from the A1632G patient iSNs was significantly more depolarized than that of CRISPR-corrected lines (Fig. 3A). A1632G-bearing iSNs also had a much lower current threshold (Fig. 3BandC). The more depolarized RMP and lower current threshold indicate that the A1632G-expressing iSNs are highly likely to be more excitable than the CRISPR-corrected neurons. Indeed, when graded suprathreshold 500 ms depolarizing current steps were applied to the iSNs, the A1632G-bearing neurons fired a significantly higher number of action potentials than the corrected neurons (Fig. 3DandE). In contrast, the A1632G neurons typically fired multiple action potentials, whereas the CRISPR-corrected neurons usually generated only one action potential in response to any depolarizing current step (Fig. 3EandF).

To confirm that the effect of A1632G is not attributable to polygenic differences in patient background, we performed a functional validation using CRISPR/Cas9 to knock in A1632G into cells with a wild-type (WT)/healthy control background. Examination of these iSNs neurons revealed pronounced differences in excitability. We found that the RMP of A1632G knock-in (KI) iSNs was on average ∼4 mV more depolarized than that of WT iPSC-SNs (Fig. 4A; A1632G KI = −53.4 ± 1 mV, WT = −57.5 ± 1 mV;t= 2.3,P =0.03, two-tailed unpairedt-test). In addition, the current threshold was also significantly lower (Fig. 4BandC; A1632G KI = 76 ± 14 pA, WT = 147 ± 16 pA;t= 3.4,P =0.002, two-tailed unpairedt-test). In addition, we found that the iSNs bearing the A1632G mutation fired a significantly higher average number of action potentials in response to 500 ms depolarizing current steps, as shown inFig. 4DandE(F =5.9,P =0.02, repeated-measures one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni corrections), and the number of neurons that fired multiple action potentials following the stimulation was substantially increased in A1632G KI neurons compared with WT (Fig. 4F). We conclude that the A1632G mutation in Nav1.7 is, by itself, sufficient to cause hyperexcitability, which will drive the clinical pain phenotype.

Gain-of-function mutations in the voltage-gated sodium channels Nav1.7, Nav1.8 and Nav1.9 cause small fibre neuropathy, IEM and paroxysmal extreme pain disorder.1,2,7,8Evaluating new variants requires functional testing, and with thousands of variants of unknown significance, achieving a proper diagnosis and therapeutic regimen remain challenging. Previous functional studies have introduced mutated channels into immortalized cell lines or overexpressed them in rodent DRG neurons, but both methods have potential drawbacks, including a lack of neural identity and a non-specific genetic background, on the one hand, and evaluating the effects of overexpression of an ion channel in an injured neuron setting, on the other. Furthermore, it is now recognized that there are species-specific differences in the biophysical and pharmacological properties of ion channels, hence studies in human cells will provide more clinically translatable data.22Our method of differentiating sensory neurons directly from a patient provides several advantages, including a neural identity, the preserved genetic background of the affected patient, the ability to correct pathogenic mutations using gene-editing technologies and the capability to produce large numbers of neurons for testing potential effective therapeutics. This approach has been used in both sensory neuron and other neurological diseases.23-26

Although our differentiation protocol produces sensory neurons, it is a mixture of populations, with a large proportion having an Aβ-fibre-like identity. Many of the iSNs produced by this protocol express high levels ofNefhand other Aβ-fibres markers. Nociceptor-like neurons constitute the second largest population, with clear expression ofAdra2a,Sstr2,Runx1andDcn. This is line with our calcium-imaging data, showing that 31.6% of the neurons are responsive to αβ-MeATP (probably through P2RX3) and 10.8% to capsaicin (probably through TRPV1). In comparison to the protocol of Chamberset al.14this protocol produces fewer neurons responsive to αβ-MeATP (50% versus 31.6%), slightly more that are responsive to capsaicin (1%–2% versus ∼10%) and many putative mechanoreceptive neurons based on mechanoreceptor gene expression (Ret,Nrtk2,Slc17a7andNefh). Others have also observed an A-fibre-like staining in induced sensory neurons, but our protocol appears to produce them in higher abundance.27,28Our protocol shares similarities with that of Zhuet al.,29which might explain the generation of A-fibre-like neurons. It has been well documented that culture conditions can drive the variability in sensory neuron differentiation.30Given the evidence that NT-3 and TrkC/TrkB signalling affect proprioceptive/mechanoreceptive afferent development, it might be that the addition NT-3 was crucial for a shift towards a putative mechanoresponsive population.20,31,32

The high levels of Nav1.7 expression in the iSNs makes this protocol well suited to studying the consequences of putative pathogenic mutations inScn9a(Nav1.7). As a proof of principle, we differentiated iSNs derived from an IEM patient who harboured a Nav1.7 A1632G mutation, which in HEK293 cells and DRG overexpression systems leads to a hyperexcitable phenotype.10Indeed, we observed that iSNs from this patient displayed a significant leftward shift in activation, reduced current threshold and depolarized RMP. Furthermore, the firing of multiple action potentials following a single stimulus was significantly increased. Some Nav1.7 mutations cause a depolarized RMP, but there are exceptions, such as Nav1.7 F1449V, A1632E and I228M.9,33,34Previous work found that the Nav1.7 A1632G mutation caused only a slight and non-significant decrease in RMP.10It is notable that in all these cases, the mutant channels were expressed in rodent DRG neurons. The decrease we recorded in the patient-specific iSN and in the iSN KI was ∼4 mV, and given that depolarizing the RMP by ∼5 mV can reduce current threshold by ∼25%–50%, it suggests that this change might be of significance in determining the phenotype.35This approach is therefore advantageous in uncovering new, clinically relevant phenotypes that need to be evaluated when designing therapeutic interventions.

As the accessibility of gene editing therapeutics increases, the need to verify pathogenic mutations becomes essential. In prior studies, iPSC-SNs have provided a platform that facilitated the pinpointing of pain-resilience genes, and we wondered whether a similar system could be beneficial.36,37We used this system to study whether correcting the A1632G mutation rescued the hyperexcitability phenotype. Indeed, correcting the patient mutation reversed the hyperexcitability, alterations in RMP and probability of multiple action potentials being fired after a given stimulus, and it shifted the activation threshold back to a normal range. In addition, introducing the A1632G mutation into a WT Nav1.7 channel recapitulated the hyperexcitability phenotype, revealing that this mutation indeed underlies the IEM in this patient. Using this platform to test the pathogenicity of putative sodium channel mutations opens the door, therefore, to targeted genomic-based therapies.
