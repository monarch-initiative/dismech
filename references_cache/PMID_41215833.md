# Crouzonodermoskeletal syndrome requires individualized surgical management: Scoping review of a rare complex craniofacial syndrome.
**Authors:** Reisert HD, Goynatsky M, Cantor Y, Zhang S, Segal S, Cohen JM, Liriano G, Behbahani M, Kobets A
**Journal:** JPRAS Open (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jpra.2025.09.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpra.2025.09.013)

## Content

Crouzonodermoskeletal syndrome (CDSS) is a rare condition characterized by premature fusion of the coronal sutures, midface hypoplasia, beaked nasal deformity, mandibular prognathism, and proptosis. A specific mutation in the Fibroblast Growth Factor 3 (FGFR3) gene causes CDSS (Ala391Glu) and thus provides a diagnostic marker for early identification. Beyond the Crouzon syndrome-like craniofacial appearance, the CDSS phenotype includes acanthosis nigricans, choanal stenosis/atresia, hydrocephalus, Chiari malformation, and achondroplasia, although presentations vary substantially throughout the literature. In this article, we consolidate and summarize features of previously reported cases of CDSS, and we report the presentation and management of an infant with CDSS at our center. Prior reports of CDSS were sought using a combination of relevant keywords. Selection was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines. Publications reporting patient-specific data for at least one case of CDSS were included. We identified 74 cases of CDSS among 32 publications from 1985-2023. Most patients were identified at birth with craniofacial abnormalities. Many developed acanthosis nigricans in early childhood. Patients underwent multiple surgeries, including cranial vault reconstruction, cerebrospinal fluid diversion, airway maintenance, and nutrition management. Our patient presented at 4-months-of-age with hydrocephalus, choanal atresia, tracheostomy dependence, proptosis, Chiari malformation, and multi-suture craniosynostosis. Genetic analysis identified the heterozygous FGFR3 Ala391Glu variant diagnostic for CDSS. Over an eleven-month period, he underwent numerous surgeries to address his head shape and associated comorbidities. There was significant variability in clinical presentations, treatments, and outcomes across cases in our review. Early diagnosis of CDSS is essential to anticipate complex medical needs, develop individualized treatment plans, and ultimately to improve patient outcomes. Our review emphasizes the importance of a collaborative, patient-centered approach between plastic surgery, neurosurgery, ophthalmology, otolaryngology, and pediatric general surgery teams to manage the complexities of patients with CDSS.

Crouzonodermoskeletal syndrome (CDSS), also known as Crouzon syndrome with acanthosis nigricans, is a rare craniofacial syndrome. As the name suggests, CDSS is associated with a Crouzon syndrome-like phenotype, including multi-suture craniosynostosis, midface hypoplasia with a beaked nose, and shallow orbits causing proptosis.1 However, CDSS is distinct from classical Crouzon syndrome (CS); the clinical manifestation of CDSS is more medically complex, involving abnormalities in numerous organ systems that should suggest this alternative diagnosis.2 Likewise, CDSS is markedly more rare than CS, affecting one in 1,000,000 people and affecting females over twice as frequently as males.2

CDSS is further distinguished from classical CS by its association with a distinct fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) mutation.3 As such, CDSS is diagnosed by detection of the c.1172C>A (p.Ala391Glu) nucleotide substitution through direct sequencing analysis of genomic DNA extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes. Although most cases of CDSS arise de novo, autosomal dominant inheritance is possible. Genetic analysis confirming the presence of the characteristic mutation can enable the family and care team to anticipate the clinical features, medical complexity, and multidisciplinary management required for affected individuals. Patients with CDSS can experience a multitude of complications that would be atypical for classical CS, including hydrocephalus, choanal atresia, odontogenic tumors resulting in mandibular prognathism, and vertebral abnormalities (e.g., achondroplasia, spinal stenosis, or scoliosis).2 Additionally, patients exhibit acanthosis nigricans in early childhood, which presents as hyperkeratotic velvety plaques in flexural areas of folded skin (e.g., the neck), as well as surgical scar hypopigmentation.4 These medical complexities require collaborative management between numerous medical and surgical specialties, with treatments conducted in an individualized rather than standardized order to optimize the patient’s clinical condition and outcome.5

Given the rarity of this syndrome, its uniquely challenging clinical manifestation, and the diversity in medical and surgical management of those affected, there is a need to consolidate data from described cases in the literature. In the present study, we sought to expand upon the 2007 review by Arnaud‐López and colleagues; we conducted an updated scoping literature review and describe our recent management of a patient with CDSS. In our review, we sought to consolidate features of smaller reports in the literature. Our goal was to provide a comprehensive overview of the syndrome that can assist treatment teams in their ability to recognize and accurately diagnose CDSS when an affected patient is born, anticipate each patient’s complex needs, and best support patients and their families as they grow.

A search was conducted in PubMed using the terms ([“Crouzon] AND [“acanthosis nigricans”]) OR (“Crouzonodermoskeletal”). This yielded 68 publications which were imported to Covidence (Cochrane, London, UK). All publications were screened by two authors (H.R. and M.G.), first by title and abstract for relevance and then by full text for eligibility. Publications reporting at least one case of CDSS were included. Publications of the wrong study design were excluded (i.e., did not describe at least one case of CDSS and/or did not provide case-specific details).

Ultimately, 32 publications were eligible for inclusion (Table 1). Data was extracted from all included publications by two authors (H.R and M.G). Collected data included patient characteristics and clinical features about the presentation, treatment course, and prognosis/outcome. Any disagreements during screening and data extraction were resolved through discussion with a senior author (A.K.). This review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines.6

Our scoping review identified 74 cases of CDSS throughout 32 publications. Data from these cases were synthesized with data from a recent case at our center, for a total of 75 cases included in the review (Table 1).

Initial surgical optimization included bilateral temporary tarsorrhaphy, endoscopic choanal atresia evaluation, and tracheostomy and gastrostomy replacement. Next, he received an Ommaya reservoir to enable cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) removal as needed; an Ommaya reservoir was chosen instead of an indwelling shunt at this time to avoid the risk of shunt damage during the patient’s upcoming cranial surgeries. The patient then underwent an open cranial vault reconstruction (CVR) for craniosynostosis correction, using a Marchac bandeau construct and split calvarial graft mosaic.13 The highly irregular bone on the undersurface of the skull was densely adherent to the dura, requiring meticulous dissection. This anomalous anatomy increased the risk of dural breech and CSF leak, which is atypical of routine cranial remodeling procedures. Prior to discharge, he received a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt and underwent permanent bilateral tarsorrhaphy, due to worsening corneal exposure and eye globe subluxation.

He returned to our center at 15-months-of-age and was noted to have a left eye cataract from corneal drying. He had hyperpigmented skin near his tracheostomy insertion with hypopigmented scarring, likely his initial focus of acanthosis nigricans. During this admission, he experienced a hypoxic cardiac arrest secondary to tracheostomy dislodgement and was successfully resuscitated. He underwent a bi-parietal CVR to address residual skull base and posterior compression, including a suboccipital craniectomy for Chiari malformation. The midline keel was drilled to reveal the dura, spanning 1 cm laterally on either side of the midline and 2 cm up to but safely away from the confluence. Ultrasound was used to confirm tonsillar decompression and excellent movement and pulsation of CSF spaces. The patient was flipped supine, and an extensive craniectomy was performed, circumferentially barrel staving the surrounding bone to successfully decompress the brain. Two years after discharge, now 3.5-years-old, the patient maintains improved cranial contour (Figure 3). He exhibits global developmental delay, is non-verbal, and is non-ambulatory. He is attentive to his family and can follow simple commands. He is dependent upon a gastrostomy tube for feeding and breathes room air through his tracheostomy tube. He has patches of dry, hyperpigmented, and thickened skin on his neck, abdomen, and around his mouth, suggestive of acanthosis nigricans. He has not required additional surgical management since discharge from our center. His parents expressed interest in pursuing additional elective craniofacial advancements to correct his midface hypoplasia, with a long-term goal of enabling the patient to breathe and eat by mouth.

CDSS is an extremely rare genetic syndrome with significant medical complexities and comorbidities, as demonstrated by our collation of 74 cases from the literature and description of a recent case at our center. In addition to clarifying the major clinical features of and surgical interventions for CDSS, we demonstrate that the female predominance of CDSS may be less extreme than previously reported (1.8:1 in the present study vs 2.4:1 as reported by Arnaud-López and colleagues in 2007).2 Given the broad clinical impact of CDSS, early and accurate diagnosis is crucial to optimize patient outcomes.

Conveniently, CDSS is known to be caused by a distinct FGFR3 Ala391Glu mutation, which allows for definitive molecular confirmation. As such, testing should be expediently pursued if a child presents with appreciable features at birth or in early childhood, including multi-suture craniosynostosis, hydrocephalus, choanal atresia, or acanthosis nigricans. Moreover, if abnormal head shape is identified on prenatal ultrasound, and chorionic villous sampling is pursued, genetic analysis should investigate the FGFR3 variant for CDSS in addition to variants associated with other multi-suture craniosynostosis syndromes.14 If the causal variant is identified, the genetic basis of the patient’s clinical symptoms and presentation can be confirmed. Furthermore, the medical team can proactively anticipate the complex surgical management that may be required, including CSF diversion, airway management, and CVR.

While all patients with CDSS have the same FGFR3 variant, there were a wide variety of phenotypes and functional abilities observed amongst the cases in the literature. Whereas some cases had severe functional impairments (e.g., developmental delays, intellectual disabilities, tracheostomy dependence) and required more extensive medical support, others maintained relatively preserved function. Three publications reported cases of a parent and child both affected by CDSS, suggesting that some patients were able to lead independent lives and ultimately raise children.7,9,15 To that end, although most cases presented with symptoms at birth, some patients were not identified until adulthood; two cases were only evaluated and diagnosed in the context of management for their newborn children, suggesting that the clinical presentations for these child-bearing adults were relatively mild.9,15 The broad range of clinical severity suggests that modifier genes, environmental factors, or epigenetic influences may play a role in determining disease severity. Future research investigating these additional influences is necessary to elucidate more effective treatments, tailored to specific symptoms. The diversity of function among patients affected by CDSS further highlights the importance of personalized treatments to work towards patient-specific goals.

Our review demonstrates that management of CDSS is multifaceted, with patients receiving numerous surgeries throughout their treatment. Prior studies have reported that the surgical sequences differ for each patient, although airway management regularly takes precedence in the order of surgical management for those with choanal stenosis/atresia.5 Our review further demonstrates this individuality. In our review, the most common surgeries among the reviewed cases were for CVR (n = 124) followed by CSF diversion (n = 54) and airway management (n = 46), with most patients requiring more than one surgery. Management seems to be dictated by the patient’s clinical development and comorbid medical complexities, namely hydrocephalus and elevated ICP. As such, each patient’s surgical sequence will differ according to their medical/surgical needs, clinical stability, and personal cosmetic preferences. Moreover, surgical management may be complicated by the patient’s comorbidities, such as when CVR is attempted for a patient with an indwelling CSF diversion catheter. Despite the many advancements in treatment, the most crucial factor remains the early identification of CDSS. Physicians should have a low threshold for pursuing genetic testing for CDSS in a child with characteristic features to enable prompt treatment initiation and symptom alleviation.

While the timing and sequence of interventions may play a critical role in minimizing the number of surgeries required, it is essential to recognize that prognostic outcomes are also influenced by the severity of clinical manifestations of each case. Given the complexity and variability of these presentations described in our review, treatment strategies must be addressed individually. In prior investigations, patients who underwent cranial expansion earlier in life tended to exhibit better prognostic outcomes and were less likely to experience the negative effects of hydrocephalus.5 Future prospective and multi-center studies may be beneficial to investigate factors that may influence the optimal timing and order of surgical intervention for patients with CDSS.

Limitations of the present study include the heterogeneity in data reporting across our sample of individual case publications or small case series over a 38-year-period. This study is further limited by publication bias and thereby may include a disproportionate percentage of patients with more severe presentations who receive comprehensive care and may be missing patients with milder presentations who may not seek care or be formally diagnosed. Collaboration across institutions can help promote research given the rarity of this syndrome, as much of the current literature on CDSS includes individual case reports. Such endeavors can help inform management of patients with CDSS to optimize patient outcomes.

Crouzonodermoskeletal syndrome (CDSS) is a rare genetic syndrome with a markedly more complex clinical manifestation than classical Crouzon syndrome. Through our synthesis of 74 cases in the literature and a recent case from our institution, we describe the most common features and highlight the diversity in patient presentation, management, and outcomes. Genetic testing for the diagnostic FGFR Ala391Glu mutation should be initiated for patients with craniofacial abnormalities at birth, especially if the patient presents with choanal atresia and hydrocephalus, which are features more suggestive of CDSS than classical Crouzon syndrome. Accurately diagnosing a patient with CDSS can enable complex medical needs to be better anticipated. While phenotypes can strongly correlate with genetic variant for some syndromes, CDSS is less predictable in terms of functional outcomes. As such, a personalized, multidisciplinary surgical approach to treating patients with CDSS is crucial.
