# Novel insights into the phenotypic spectrum and pathogenesis of Hardikar syndrome.
**Authors:** Strong A, March ME, Cardinale CJ, Liu Y, Battig MR, Finoti LS, Matsuoka LS, Watson D, Sridhar S, Jarrett JF, Cannon I, Li D, Bhoj E, Zackai EH, Rand EB, Wenger T, Lerman BB, Shikany A, Weaver KN, Hakonarson H
**Journal:** Genet Med (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.gim.2024.101222](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gim.2024.101222)

## Content

PURPOSE: Hardikar syndrome (HS, MIM #301068) is a female-specific multiple congenital anomaly syndrome characterized by retinopathy, orofacial clefting, aortic coarctation, biliary dysgenesis, genitourinary malformations, and intestinal malrotation. We previously showed that heterozygous nonsense and frameshift variants in MED12 cause HS. The phenotypic spectrum of disease and the mechanism by which MED12 variants cause disease is unknown. We aim to expand the phenotypic and molecular landscape of HS and elucidate the mechanism by which MED12 variants cause disease.

METHODS: We clinically assembled and molecularly characterized a cohort of 11 previously unreported individuals with HS. Additionally, we studied the effect of MED12 deficiency on ciliary biology, hedgehog, and yes-associated protein (YAP) signaling; pathways implicated in diseases with phenotypic overlap with HS.

RESULTS: We report novel phenotypes associated with HS, including cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, and vascular anomalies, and expand the molecular landscape of HS to include splice site variants. We additionally demonstrate that MED12 deficiency causes decreased cell ciliation, and impairs hedgehog and YAP signaling.

CONCLUSION: Our data support updating HS standard-of-care to include regular cardiac imaging, arrhythmia screening, and vascular imaging. We further propose that dysregulation of ciliogenesis and YAP and hedgehog signaling contributes to the pathogenesis of HS.

Hardikar syndrome (HS, OMIM #301068) is an X-linked dominant disorder characterized by cleft lip/palate, pigmentary retinopathy, aortic coarctation, intestinal malrotation, hepatobiliary disease and genitourinary malformations with preserved neurocognition (1, 2). MED12 (HGNC ID 11957; OMIM #300188) was recently identified as the causal gene for HS (3). Somatic, predominantly missense variants in MED12 are associated with uterine leiomyomas, while hemizygous and heterozygous constitutive missense MED12 variants are associated with syndromic and non-syndromic intellectual disability in males and females (4 – 6). HS is specifically associated with de novo nonsense and frameshift MED12 variants (3). The mechanism by which MED12 variants cause disparate disease is unknown.

MED12 encodes mediator complex subunit-12, a component of the kinase domain of the mediator complex (7). Mediator is a multi-subunit complex that plays a critical role in transcriptional regulation through coordination of transcription factors and RNA Polymerase II (8). Core mediator is composed of a head, middle and tail domain, which are key determinants of mediator activity (9). Pathogenic variants in various core mediator components are associated with intellectual disability syndromes (10, 11).

The mediator kinase domain of which MED12 belongs is a dissociable module of mediator believed to play a regulatory role in mediator activity (9). The kinase domain is composed of 4 subunits: CCNC, CDK8, MED13 and MED12. MED13 facilitates the interaction between the kinase domain and core mediator, CCNC connects CDK8 to the kinase domain, and CDK8 is the active kinase module (12). MED12 interacts with chromatin and transcription factors to modulate chromatin architecture and transcriptional activity (13 – 15).

Similar to HS, ciliopathy syndromes are characterized by retinopathy, biliary dysgenesis, cystic or fibrotic liver disease, skeletal dysplasia, endocrinopathies and intellectual disability (16). Ciliopathy syndromes are caused by dysfunction of the primary cilium, a mechanosensory organelle that exists atop most cell types and plays a critical role in organogenesis and organ maintenance and patterning (17). Primary ciliopathies are distinct from motile ciliopathies, which are caused by dysfunction of the motile cilium and characterized by impaired pulmonary clearance, bronchiectasis, situs abnormalities and infertility (18). Though the transcriptional program that coordinates development of the motile cilium is relatively well understood, a transcriptional program that coordinates primary ciliogenesis has not been described (19).

Hippo/YAP signaling is another critical developmental pathway with physiological relevance to the HS phenotype that acts both independently and in coordination with the primary cilium (20). Disrupted YAP signaling is implicated in cancer, fibrosis, cardiomyopathy, orofacial clefting, and angiogenesis, also features seen in HS (21 – 23). Mediator complex has been implicated in YAP signaling, though the role of dysregulated YAP signaling in the pathogenesis of mediator-related disease is unknown (24).

Here we describe 11 new cases of HS with molecularly-confirmed MED12 splice, nonsense and frameshift variants, bringing the total number of HS cases in the literature to 22. With this cohort, we expand the phenotype of HS to include cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, and vascular defects and we expand the molecular basis of HS to include splice MED12 variants. We further demonstrate that loss of MED12 decreases cell ciliation and hedgehog and YAP pathway activity. Our data are consistent with a model in which MED12 acts as a positive regulator of ciliary biology and hedgehog and YAP signaling, and that dysregulation of these signaling pathways may drive the Hardikar phenotype.

Our previously-published HS cohort highlighted core features of HS, including orofacial clefting, aortic coarctation, intestinal malrotation, ectopic ureters, biliary abnormalities, and cat’s paw retinopathy (patchy retinal pigmentation). We identified 11 additional individuals with HS found by exome sequencing to have heterozygous splice, nonsense, or frameshift MED12 variants (Figure 1). These cases highlight the previously described core features of HS (1–3; Supplemental References 1 – 3), but also highlight previously underappreciated complications, including non-aortic vascular malformations primarily involving head, neck, cardiopulmonary and abdominal circulation (9/21; 43%), cardiomyopathy (7/22; 32%), congenital diaphragmatic hernia (6/22; 27%), Meckel’s diverticulum (6/22; 27%), and arrhythmia including atrial ectopy, ventricular fibrillation, and elongated QT interval (3/22; 14%) (Table 1; Table 2; Supplemental Material; Supplemental Table 1).

HS has many overlapping features with ciliopathies, most notably retinopathy, biliary dysgenesis, and situs abnormalities. To evaluate a role for MED12 in ciliogenesis, retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, an established cell model for the study of ciliopathy-spectrum disease, were serum starved for 24 hours to promote ciliogenesis or were grown in complete medium (control) and MED12 expression was evaluated by quantitative PCR (qPCR). AURKA (HGNC ID 11393; OMIM #603072) and PLK1 (HGNC ID 9077; OMIM #602098) expression were used as treatment controls (Supplemental Figure 1a – b). All gene expression was normalized to ACTB (HGNC ID 132; OMIM #102630). Serum-starved RPE cells undergoing ciliogenesis had a 2-fold increase in MED12 expression compared to cells treated with complete medium (Figure 2a), supporting a role for MED12 in ciliogenesis.

To elucidate a role for MED12 in ciliary disassembly, RPE cells were serum-starved for 48 hours to promote ciliogenesis and then treated either with fresh, serum-free medium to promote continued quiescence and ciliary maintenance or with complete medium to promote cell cycle entry and ciliary disassembly. AURKA and PLK1 expression were used as treatment controls (Supplemental Figure 1c – d). RPE cells treated with serum and undergoing ciliary disassembly had a 50% reduction in MED12 expression, supporting a role for MED12 in the inhibition of ciliary disassembly (Figure 2b).

To further explore the role of MED12 in ciliogenesis, we assessed ciliogenesis in cells deficient in MED12. Despite multiple attempts to generate MED12 deficient RPE cells via CRISPR, we were only able to obtain in-frame single or double amino acid deletions. Clonal cell lines demonstrated normal MED12 mRNA and protein expression (data not shown), highlighting the critical role of MED12 in cell survival. We therefore used siRNA to knockdown MED12 expression. Optimized conditions allowed for ~80% reduction in MED12 message and protein at 72 hours post-transfection (Figure 2c).

siRNA-mediated MED12 knockdown in RPE cells reduced percent ciliated cells by 15% without affecting ciliary length (Figure 2d – g). To determine whether this reduction in percent ciliated cells reflects increased cell division and mitosis, RPE cells were transfected with control versus MED12 siRNA and cell proliferation rates were compared via counting assays. Cells transfected with MED12 siRNA had a ~50% reduction in cell proliferation, suggesting that the decrease in percent ciliated cells is not due to increased proliferation (Figure 2h).

The primary cilium houses multiple signaling pathways, most notably the hedgehog pathway. Hedgehog signaling can be queried using SAG, a pharmacological smoothened agonist (26). To determine whether SAG-induced hedgehog signaling is dependent on an intact cilium, we treated serum-starved (ciliated) and serum-fed (unciliated) RPE cells with SAG versus DMSO (control) and assessed GLI1 (HGNC ID 4317; OMIM #165220) induction via qPCR. Unciliated cells did not respond to SAG (Supplemental Figure 1e). To further assess the role of the cilium in the SAG response, siRNA was used to knock down the ciliogenesis factor TTBK2 (HGNC ID 19141; OMIM #611695) in RPE cells and the SAG response was assessed. TTBK2 knockdown reduced percent ciliated cells by 34% and abrogated the SAG response in RPE cells, supporting a critical role for the cilium in the SAG response (Supplemental Figure 1f – h).

To determine whether the reduction in percent ciliated cells with MED12 knockdown translates to a reduction in ciliary signaling, control versus siRNA-mediated MED12 knockdown RPE cells were treated with SAG or DMSO (control) and induction of hedgehog signaling was assessed through measurement of GLI1 mRNA. Cells treated with control siRNA had a robust induction of GLI1 expression in response to SAG; MED12 deficiency abrogated the response (Figure 2i).

Many of the features of HS can be explained by disrupted ciliary signaling; however, many of the novel features identified in our expanded cohort of Hardikar patients are not typical of ciliopathy spectrum disease, most notably the cardiac and vascular complications. Hippo/YAP signaling has recently been implicated in the pathogenesis of ciliopathy-spectrum disease and plays a known role in angiogenesis, cardiomyopathy and orofacial clefting (21 – 23).

To elucidate a role for MED12 in YAP signaling, we queried expression of the YAP downstream targets CTGF (HGNC ID 2500; OMIM #121009) and CYR61 (HGNC ID 2654; OMIM #602369) via qPCR in MED12 knockdown RPE cells. siRNA-mediated MED12 knockdown reduced CTGF and CYR61 expression by ~40% and ~20%, respectively (Figure 3a,b).

As a complementary approach, YAP signaling was queried in MED12-deficient RPE cells via luciferase assay using a TEAD-responsive luciferase (25). siRNA-mediated MED12 knockdown decreased TEAD.Luciferase expression by ~35% (Figure 3c). These reductions in YAP signaling occurred independent of changes in YAP (HGNC ID 16262; OMIM #606608) mRNA and protein: siRNA-mediated MED12 knockdown was associated with an increase in YAP mRNA (Figure 3d) and decreased phosphorylated-YAP (Figure 3e–h), suggesting attempted cellular compensation through increased YAP mRNA production and decreased phosphorylation and YAP turnover with MED12 deficiency.

HS is a female-specific, X-linked dominant, multiple congenital anomaly syndrome caused by nonsense and frameshift variants in MED12, a component of mediator complex with known roles in transcriptional regulation (1 – 3). Due to its rarity and recent gene discovery, the molecular and phenotypic spectrum of HS is incompletely characterized. Here we describe 11 additional individuals with HS, and reinforce the core phenotypes of orofacial clefting, aortic coarctation, genitourinary malformations and biliary dysgenesis (Supplemental Figure 1, Table 1, Table 2; Supplemental Table 1). We additionally identify 3 novel MED12 splice variants and 2 recurrent MED12 variants now identified in 2 probands each. Of note, despite identical variants, these individuals have overlapping but not identical features, suggesting heterogeneity in clinical presentation that may relate to X-inactivation or genetic background. We further expand the phenotype of HS to include arrhythmia (atrial ectopy, ventricular fibrillation, elongated QT interval), cardiomyopathy, and non-aortic vascular anomalies involving most notably the carotid vessels, posterior cerebral artery, vertebral arteries, pulmonary artery and vein, and renal artery (Table 2). Importantly, no affected individual had total body vascular imaging performed (total body CTA or total body MRA); vascular abnormalities were detected by targeted imaging obtained due to clinical concern (portal hypertension, global hypertension, jejunal atresia, surgical planning) or via direct visualization during a surgical procedure, meaning that other vascular anomalies may be present in HS patients that have not been ascertained.

Patient 10 represents the third HS patient reported with a mosaic causal MED12 variant, a nonsense variant present at 6% allele frequency in blood. It is unclear whether this low level is related to a late-occurring mutation event or due to differential cell proliferation/survival over time. This case highlights the importance of considering mosaicism and manually reviewing next generation sequencing data and employing deep exome sequencing if there is strong clinical suspicion for HS.

The mechanism by which nonsense, frameshift, and splice MED12 variants cause disease is unknown. Here we show that loss of MED12 decreases cell ciliation and impairs hedgehog and YAP signaling. We propose a model in which loss of MED12 interferes with the transcription of genes critical for ciliogenesis and also impairs YAP pathway activation, leading to the severe developmental abnormalities characteristic of HS (Figure 4).

Of note, cardiac-specific MED12 deletion in mice caused dilated cardiomyopathy and a missense MED12 variant was identified in an individual with dilated cardiomyopathy (27,28). MED12 was recently shown to be a critical regulator of gene expression in the heart, specifically with regards to genes involved in calcium homeostasis (27). This biology may also be contributing to the pathogenesis of heart disease in HS independent of ciliary and YAP signaling effects.

Though ciliopathy syndromes have classically been linked to structural and functional components of the primary cilium, it has become increasingly appreciated that pathogenic variants in genes without clear functional associations with the cilium can produce ciliopathy-spectrum phenotypes. Transcription factors and global transcriptional regulators have been previously implicated in ciliopathies: heterozygous pathogenic variants in the transcription factor FOXJ1 (HGNC ID: 3816; OMIM #602291) cause primary ciliary dyskinesia (29), biallelic pathogenic variants in NPHP7 (HGNC ID: 29450; OMIM #611498), which encodes a zinc-finger protein involved in transcriptional regulation causes nephronophthisis (30), and biallelic pathogenic variants in INTS13 (HGNC ID: 20174; OMIM #615079), which encodes a subunit of integrator complex, causes the ciliopathy oral facial digital syndrome (31).

Previous studies support a role for MED12 in hedgehog signaling, a pathway intricately linked to the cilium. Individuals with missense MED12 variants have phenotypes that reflect defective hedgehog signaling such as multiple oral frenula, polysyndactyly, and neural tube defects (6, 32). MED12 has been reported to inhibit hedgehog signaling (33,34). This contrasts with our data, which suggests that MED12 promotes hedgehog signaling. One possibility is that our experiments were done under serum-free conditions in which cells were ciliated. It is possible that MED12 has a ciliary-independent effect on hedgehog signaling queried by previous studies and not ours. It is possible that cell type differences account for these discrepancies, with previous studies done in lymphoblastoid cell lines or neuron-related cell types. Most importantly, previous studies were performed in patient-derived cell lines or using patient-derived MED12 variants identified in individuals with X-linked intellectual disability and not HS (33,34). It is well established that MED12-related disease has a wide phenotypic breadth, including syndromic and non-syndromic X-linked intellectual disability syndromes caused predominantly by missense MED12 variants (5, 6). This contrasts to HS, which is caused predominantly by nonsense/frameshift MED12 variants and is characterized by normal neurocognition (3). It is possible that MED12-associated intellectual disability is a gain-of-function phenotype and HS is a MED12-loss of function phenotype, which would explain the seemingly discrepant data. Future studies are required to more completely explore these possibilities: most impactful would be interrogation of YAP signaling, ciliogenesis, and ciliary signaling and unbiased RNA-sequencing and proteomics studies in fibroblast cell lines derived from unaffected relatives, individuals affected by HS, and individuals with MED12-related intellectual disability. Also informative would be functional studies of YAP signaling, ciliogenesis, and ciliary signaling in MED12-deficient systems rescued with wild-type MED12 versus MED12 variants associated with HS and intellectual disability. We demonstrate that siRNA-mediated MED12 knockdown decreases YAP signaling in RPE cells. It is unclear whether this dysregulation occurs independently of MED12’s effect on the cilium. Our data are consistent with a role for MED12 in promoting YAP signaling. This is particularly intriguing, as MED1 (HGNC ID: 9234; OMIM #604311), a component of core mediator, is believed to be integral to effective transcription of YAP downstream targets by facilitating release from transcriptional pausing (24).

MED12 may promote YAP signaling by preventing its inhibitory phosphorylation and degradation, promoting its nuclear translocation, or by directly promoting transcription of YAP downstream targets. Our data demonstrated no effect on total YAP abundance and decreased YAP phosphorylation with MED12 deficiency. These data are most consistent with a model in which MED12 directly enhances YAP/TEAD-mediated gene transcription. It is unknown whether this is achieved through interactions of core mediator, effects on chromatin accessibility, or interference with transcription factor binding. It is also unknown whether this function of MED12 is dependent on its role in the mediator kinase domain.

The mediator kinase domain is composed of 4 subunits: CCNC, MED12, MED13 and CDK8 (12). Heterozygous variants in CDK8 (HGNC ID: 1779; OMIM #603184) and MED13 (HGNC ID: 22474; OMIM #603808) are associated with autosomal dominant intellectual disability syndromes, but do not typically include the severe visceral manifestations characteristic of HS (35,36), suggesting that MED12 may have additional functions independent of its role in the mediator complex. A kinase-domain independent role for MED12 has been suggested previously in TGF beta signaling and stem cell biology (37). It is unclear whether this function is involved in transcriptional regulation or whether MED12 has a non-transcriptional and possibly even non-nuclear function.

Based on our updated phenotyping and functional data, we propose the following updates to the clinical care of patients with HS. We previously recommended based on our patient cohort that individuals with HS be screened for orofacial clefting and velopharyngeal insufficiency, retinopathy, congenital heart disease, aortic coarctation, carotid and aortic dilation, cerebral vascular malformations, biliary disease, intestinal malrotation, genitourinary abnormalities, vesicoureteral reflux, and hearing loss. Based on our expanded cohort and recent reports, we additionally recommend 1) annual echocardiogram to screen for cardiomyopathy, 2) lifelong annual EKG and 24-hour Holter monitor to screen for arrhythmia, and 3) baseline total body MRA with and without contrast (gadolinium) with repeat imaging at least every 2 years to screen for global vascular malformations (subsequent scans may be performed without gadolinium if baseline scans are normal to avoid gadolinium-associated toxicity38).

Though our study provides important insights into the phenotypic spectrum and pathogenesis of HS, it has several important limitations, including incomplete vascular imaging in a significant portion of our cohort, leading to a potential underestimation of the location and prevalence of vascular malformations, lack of serial vascular imaging data to determine whether vascular malformations are progressive, the young age of the cohort, which limits our ability to offer anticipatory guidance about disease progression during adulthood, and the inability to more completely characterize the molecular basis of disease due to lack of patient-derived cell lines and MED12-deficient cellular models.

In summary, our results suggest a role for MED12 in modulating ciliary biology and YAP and hedgehog signaling. We additionally describe novel phenotypes associated with HS including severe vascular and cardiac disease. It has not escaped our notice that YAP and TGFB signaling are powerful modulators of angiogenesis and cardiomyopathy. YAP and TGFB are attractive therapeutic targets in fibrotic and oncologic disease with active development of precision therapeutics. It will be critical to better understand the dysregulation of YAP and TGFB signaling in HS to potentially repurpose these drugs to treat HS patients.
