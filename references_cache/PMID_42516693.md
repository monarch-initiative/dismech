# A Case of High Pressure and High Stakes: Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension Emergence in an Infant With Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia.
**Authors:** Ahmed A, Brown AM, Eagle SJ, Brittain EL, Hemnes AR, Sullivan RT, Austin ED
**Journal:** Pulm Circ (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/pul2.70375](https://doi.org/10.1002/pul2.70375)

## Content

Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) is an autosomal‑dominant vascular disease characterized by telangiectasias and visceral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), commonly in the lungs, liver, and brain. HHT is linked to mutations in the genes Activin A Receptor Like Type 1 (ACVRL1), Endoglin (ENG), and SMAD Family Member 4 (SMAD4). The ACVRL1 gene encodes the receptor ACVRL1 (Activin A Receptor Like Type 1), also known as ALK1 (Activin Receptor-Like Kinase 1), and belongs to the Transforming Growth Factor-Beta (TGFβ) Superfamily of receptors. Approximately 20% of patients with ACVRL1-associated HHT develop pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). [1] In pediatric PAH cohorts, ACVRL1 mutations account for nearly 10% of identified genetic variants. [2] The reduced penetrance of PAH among ACVRL1 mutation carriers suggests that additional modifiers account for PAH development. [3]. Given the relevance of the bone‑morphogenetic‑protein (BMP) pathway to the pathogenesis of PAH, and the intimate relationship of TGFβ superfamily signaling partners including ACVRL1 and BMPR2, genes which modify BMP-TGFβ signaling balance may contribute to PAH development in children with ACVRL1‑positive HHT. One such gene is SMAD9 (Receptor Regulated SMAD 9); the protein product of SMAD 9 (SMAD8) which mediates downstream TGFβ signaling. Herein, we report what we believe to be the youngest reported case of PAH in ACVRL1‑mediated HHT, in a patient who co‑harbors a SMAD9 mutation.

A 4.5‑month‑old term male, weight 9th percentile and height 5th percentile, was referred for evaluation of a new systolic murmur [1, 2, 3]. The patient had no prior history of hypoxemia, epistaxis, or telangiectasias. Initial evaluation and echocardiography showed suprasystemic pulmonary arterial pressure with right‐ventricular dilation, and a small atrial septal defect (right‑to‑left shunt). Chest CT angiography demonstrated dilated main and branch pulmonary arteries with tortuous distal branches. The main pulmonary artery was 1.6 cm in size, and the pulmonary‑trunk‑to‑aortic ratio was 1.5 (Figure 1a). Patchy ground‑glass foci suggested small areas of pulmonary hemorrhage. No apparent arteriovenous malformations were found. Right‑heart catheterization revealed a mean pulmonary‑artery pressure 76 mmHg, mean systemic arterial pressure 79 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance indexed to body surface area (PVRi) 28.3 WU·m2, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 7 mmHg with left atrial pressure 6 mmHg, and Qp/Qs: 0.86; there was no acute vasodilator response by standard criteria [4]. The distal pulmonary arteries appeared engorged without clear AV connections. Severe precapillary pulmonary arterial hypertension was diagnosed.

Genetic testing identified a heterozygous germline mutation in ACVRL1 c.1450 C > T (p.Arg484Trp) and a heterozygous germline variant in SMAD9 c.1270 G > A (p.Ala424Thr). The ACVRL1 mutation was deemed pathogenic. The SMAD9 variant, while reported in PAH, is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

The patient was started on PH‐specific therapy that escalated to a phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitor, endothelin receptor antagonist, and intravenous treprostinil. At 4 years of age, he underwent a modified Potts Shunt to reduce right ventricular stress. At 5.5 years he was taken off IV treprostinil and put on oral selexipag to reduce the burden of continuous IV therapy [5].

Repeat catheterization was deferred due to parental preference. The patient has followed every 3 months for continued PH specialty care. At 8 years of age, CT angiography revealed a main pulmonary artery that measured 3.5 cm, enlarged branch pulmonary arteries (R 1.6 cm, L 1.4 cm), and distal branches that showed a tortuous course with small aneurysmal appearance, though without definitive presence of pulmonary AVMs (Figure 1b). New centrilobular enhancing nodules with ground‑glass opacities were found bilaterally in the lungs, associated mostly with the dilated distal pulmonary arteries. The patient also had hypertrophied bronchial arteries, a common finding in long‑standing PAH. At 8.5 years the child is active (WHO‑FC III), shows mildly depressed RV function, and continues oral triple therapy without evidence of clinically significant AVMs.

PAH in patients with HHT is an uncommon but debilitating comorbid condition. The factors driving the development of clinically manifest PAH amongst HHT patients remain incompletely understood. The BMP‐TGFβ signaling axis is particularly susceptible to mutations in ACVRL1, ENG and SMAD9. In this case, our patient has a known pathogenic mutation in ACVRL1, as well as a SMAD9 variant of uncertain significance. We hypothesize that this SMAD9 variant may act as a “second hit” within the BMP signaling pathway, contributing to the unusually early and severe PAH phenotype observed in this patient.

Loss‑of‑function mutations in ACVRL1 are a known cause of HHT and are associated with increased risk of PAH [6]. Reported pathogenic variants are found in the vital kinase domain, specifically exons 6–10 [7]. This site of our patient's pathogenic variant, p.Arg484Trp, occurs in exon 10 of ACVRL1. Exon 10 variants were reported in patients previously, and one‑third of those mutations have been linked to PAH. The presence of the mutation in our patient reinforces that ACVRL1 mutations could be associated with an increased likelihood that an HHT carrier will progress to PAH [7]. However, presentation with PAH in infancy is rare for those with HHT. SMAD9 variants account for only a few cases of heritable PAH but are seen to lower the age of onset when present with other BMP‑pathway defects [8].

In vitro studies show that ACVRL1 loss of function alone leads to endothelial proliferation, and isolated loss of SMAD9 favored endothelial overgrowth as well. This suggests combined ACVRL1 and SMAD9 variants may have synergistic effects that intensify vascular remodeling and contribute to PAH [9, 10].

This case report demonstrates how early and severe PAH can emerge in the setting of ACVRL‐mediated hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia. The coexistence of a rare variant in SMAD9 of uncertain significance may be a contributor to the child's phenotype, amplifying the severity with subsequent earlier presentation. If true, multilocus interactions between the ALK1 receptor (ACVRL1) and downstream BMP pathway mediators such as SMAD9 may be one example of synergistic disruption of endothelial signaling at multiple points in the TGFβ signaling pathway.

Ongoing genomic studies of PAH patients and their family aim to clarify modifiers of disease. This case underscores the potential role that multilocus genetic interactions have in accelerating disease onset and severity in PAH, highlighting the need for early detection of risk and subsequent surveillance when appropriate.
