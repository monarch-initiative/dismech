# Setting the Stage for Treatment of Aminoacyl-tRNA Synthetase (ARS)1-Deficiencies: Phenotypic Characterization and a Review of Treatment Effects.
**Authors:** Hoytema van Konijnenburg EMM, Rohof J, Kok G, van Hasselt PM, van Karnebeek CD, Muffels IJJ, Fuchs SA
**Journal:** J Inherit Metab Dis (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jimd.70017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jimd.70017)

## Content

Aminoacyl-transfer RNA (tRNA) synthetases (ARSs) are key enzymes for protein translation. The number of identified patients with recessive ARS1 deficiencies is rapidly increasing. Initially, only supportive care was available, but in recent years beneficial effects of targeted amino acid supplementation have been described. To allow early treatment and prevention of symptoms, rapid recognition is necessary, as well as insight into the natural history to evaluate treatment effects. We performed a scoping literature search for clinical characteristics and treatment effects of patients with ARS1 deficiencies. Symptoms were matched to Human Phenotype Ontology terms. We identified 438 patients with 20 different ARS1 deficiencies. Overall mortality was 22%. Neurological symptoms were most prevalent across all ARS1 deficiencies (in 87% of patients), including neurodevelopmental disorder (79%), microcephaly (50%) and seizures (46%). Growth issues and ophthalmological symptoms were also prevalent in many ARS1 deficiencies. Two distinct phenotypical clusters were seen: one with multisystemic disease including liver- and lung disease and another with a predominantly neurological phenotype. Supplementation with cognate amino acids was described in 21 patients, with beneficial effects (e.g., improvements in growth, development, liver and lung disease) in the majority. Treatment did not alleviate the most severe phenotypes. Specific symptoms relate to (a cluster of) specific ARS1 deficiencies; the mechanism is not yet understood. Multi-organ involvement should trigger inclusion of ARS1 genes in the diagnostic work-up. Treatment with cognate amino acids is promising, but it remains challenging to distinguish treatment effects from natural history. Synopsis: Treatment with cognate amino acids in ARS1 deficiencies is promising, but it remains challenging to distinguish treatment effects from natural history.

Protein translation is the process of translating the information encoded in mRNA to a protein, using amino acids corresponding to a certain sequence of nucleotides [1]. Many different enzymes and proteins are involved in this process, in which aminoacyl‐transfer RNA (tRNA) synthetases (ARSs) play a key role. ARSs are a group of highly conserved enzymes that are responsible for the correct coupling of amino acids to the corresponding tRNA, resulting in an aminoacylated, or charged, tRNA [1, 2, 3]. For each amino acid, there is a specific ARS, that is, IARS (for isoleucine) and LARS (for leucine). Protein translation takes place in the cytosol and the mitochondria using separate ARSs encoded by nuclear genes (nomenclature ARS1 for cytosolic ARSs and ARS2 for mitochondrial ARSs). Two ARSs function in both the cytosol and the mitochondria: GARS1 (glycine) and KARS1 (lysine). Besides protein translation, ARSs have many non‐canonical functions, including roles in immunological response, gene expression regulation, and RNA splicing [1, 3]. ARSs are essential for life, and therefore, genetic variants that impair ARS function can have detrimental effects. Due to rapid diagnostic advances, almost all ARS genes have now been associated with human disease. Most cases present with recessive disease. Some ARS1 genes are also associated with monoallelic (dominant) disease, generally resulting in a Charcot–Marie–Tooth peripheral neuropathy phenotype [3].

A previous review by our group provided a first overview of symptoms described in patients with recessive ARS1‐deficiencies [2]. Common symptoms in many patients with different ARS1 deficiencies included central nervous system dysfunction, failure to thrive, and feeding/gastrointestinal problems, but more distinct symptoms such as liver disease in patients with IARS1, LARS1, and MARS1 deficiency were seen as well [2]. It remains unclear why different ARS1 deficiencies cause distinct symptoms and to what extent the relative contributions of alterations in canonical and non‐canonical functions contribute to pathophysiology [1, 3].

Initially, only supportive care was available for patients with recessive ARS1 deficiencies. In recent years, however, beneficial effects of supplementing amino acids corresponding to the specific ARS1 deficiency have been described (among others by our group) [4, 5, 6, 7]. In addition to some obvious effects on growth and specific symptoms, these therapies may also help by preventing symptoms or halting progression of disease. However, it is difficult to objectively establish treatment effects in these rare and only recently described diseases for which the natural disease course is still relatively unknown. Unfamiliarity with the clinical phenotype makes it difficult to rapidly recognize ARS1‐deficiencies to allow early treatment and prevention of symptoms. Currently, ARS1‐deficiencies are mainly diagnosed via exome sequencing rather than targeted molecular genetic or biochemical testing, supplemented with quantification of residual enzyme activity. However, comprehensive genomic testing also generates many new variants of unknown significance, requiring functional testing for further validation. Genotype–phenotype correlation for this relatively new group of diseases has not been established, making it difficult to predict disease course.

To gain insight into the natural clinical phenotype and disease course, and to allow for early recognition and optimal care for patients with ARS1 deficiencies, this review aims to provide a comprehensive summary of clinical symptoms and reported treatment courses in all patients with recessive ARS1 deficiencies described in the literature.

This scoping review is an update and expansion of an earlier review in which 112 patients with 13 ARS1 deficiencies are described. The literature was reviewed to collect all the available data on clinical features of each recessive ARS1 deficiency. We included articles in English, or in other languages if enough information was provided in English for inclusion or the article or abstract could be translated. Reports lacking basic demographic data, clinical features, or genetic confirmation of ARS1 deficiencies were excluded. We cross‐compared all available information to prevent duplicate inclusions. We included all patients from our previous review and performed an additional search up to July 2024. Our search strategy was based on searching PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar using keywords corresponding to ARS1 deficiencies in the titles or abstracts of articles (see Data S1 for more information on the literature search and Figure S1 for a flowchart). We supplemented our search by using hand searches and snowballing techniques to identify other relevant studies from the references cited in and citing the retrieved articles, including any relevant article.

In total, we included 438 patients, and for each individual patient described in an article, we extracted the gene, age, gender, mortality, symptom descriptions, treatment, and observed treatment effects. We classified symptoms according to the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO [8]) terms to facilitate accurate comparison between articles and potential future studies.

We then summarized our findings of specific HPO terms to more general, overarching HPO terms. For example, patient descriptions stated various kinds of epileptic events, which we collected under the HPO term ‘Seizure’. Additionally, for the category abnormality of the nervous system, we created our own term for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abnormalities since this was described in many reports, but no overarching HPO term was available. We identified 136 articles describing clinical phenotypes of 438 patients (Figure 1) with 20 different ARS1 deficiencies. Patient numbers varied between 2 (TARS1‐deficiency) and 81 (KARS1‐deficiency).

Both the nervous system and the musculoskeletal system (in these patients mainly reflecting neurological symptoms as well) are most frequently affected across different ARS1 deficiencies. All ARS1 deficiencies are associated with neurological symptoms. Of the 438 described patients, 381 patients (87%) suffer from neurological symptoms and 276 (63%) from musculoskeletal symptoms (mainly hypotonia and hypertonia). The most prevalent neurological symptom is neurodevelopmental abnormality (348 patients, 79%), which includes neurodevelopmental delay (and global developmental delay), intellectual disability, and neurodevelopmental regression. Microcephaly is another common symptom that is described in 220 patients (50%) across almost all ARS1 deficiencies. All WARS1‐deficient patients suffer from microcephaly, and additionally, in IARS1‐, NARS1‐, QARS1, VARS1‐, and YARS1‐deficiency, microcephaly is a very frequent symptom (77%–96%). Microcephaly was rare in patients with DARS1‐, GARS1‐, LARS1‐, and MARS1‐deficiency (12%–25%) and was not reported in TARS1‐deficiency (n = 2). Seizures are also very common overall (prevalence 46% for all ARS1‐deficiencies), and especially frequent in AARS1‐, LARS1‐, NARS1‐, QARS1‐, SARS1‐, and VARS1‐deficiencies (67%–86%). MRI abnormalities of the central nervous system are described in 55% of all patients and across all ARS1 deficiencies except TARS1 deficiency (n = 2). A variety of CNS MRI abnormalities are described; the most prevalent are: abnormal myelination (n = 91, 21%) and abnormal cerebral white matter morphology (n = 58, 13%); cerebral atrophy (n = 80, 18%), cerebellar atrophy (n = 31, 7%) and CNS atrophy/degeneration (n = 40, 9%); cerebral calcification (n = 32, 7%) and abnormal corpus callosum morphology (n = 24, 5%). Ataxia is mentioned in 59 patients (13%) across different ARS1 deficiencies; other movement disorders are rarely reported.

Growth abnormalities (encompassing failure to thrive and small for gestational age among others) were common in many (AARS1‐, CARS1‐, FARSA‐, FARSB‐, IARS1‐, LARS1‐, MARS1‐, QARS1‐, VARS1‐, WARS1‐ and YARS1‐deficiency; prevalence 50%–100%) but not all ARS1 deficiencies. In total, 52% of ARS1‐deficient patients suffer from growth abnormalities, but prevalence in DARS1‐, NARS1‐, RARS1‐, and TARS1 deficiency is 0%–14%.

Overall, 33% of ARS1‐deficient patients suffer from a range of ophthalmological symptoms. Morphological abnormalities of the eyes are especially prevalent in FARSA deficiency (70%) and nystagmus (presumably reflecting a neurological symptom) is very common in RARS1 deficiency (75%). There were no ophthalmological symptoms described in IARS1‐ and TARS1‐deficient patients.

Encephalopathy was found to be rare within the cohort, except for AARS1‐, LARS1‐, and VARS1‐deficiency. In AARS1‐ and VARS1‐deficiency, early‐onset epileptic encephalopathy is frequently reported (54% and 50%), while in LARS1‐deficiency, acute transient encephalopathic episodes are described in 38%, often triggered by infections and associated with liver disease.

Neurodevelopmental regression was seen in 58 patients across 9 different ARS1 deficiencies and was relatively frequently reported in AARS1‐, DARS1‐, EPRS‐, and KARS1‐deficiency (35%–67%).

Intrauterine growth retardation/small for gestational age was very prevalent in CARS1‐, IARS1‐, and LARS1‐deficiency (50%–90%), and much less or not at all described in other ARS1 deficiencies.

Premature birth is rare overall but is reported in 64% of LARS1‐deficient patients.

Respiratory symptoms, mainly consisting of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (interstitial lung disease), are extremely common in FARSA‐, FARSB‐, and MARS1‐deficiency (86%–100%) and much less common or not at all in other ARS1‐deficiencies.

Liver disease is very common in patients with CARS1‐, FARSA‐, IARS1‐, LARS1‐, MARS1‐, and YARS1‐deficiency (50%–100%), and much less or not at all in other ARS1 deficiencies.

Cardiovascular symptoms are rare overall but are present in all (4) GARS1‐deficient patients: cardiomyopathy (2), ventricular wall hypertrophy (1) and atrial septal defect and pulmonic stenosis (1).

Anemia (not always further specified, but when reported, mostly microcytic) is very common in FARSB‐, LARS1‐, and YARS1‐deficiencies (47%–82%). However, it is much less frequent or absent in all other ARS1‐deficiencies.

Hypoalbuminemia is very common in FARSA‐, IARS1‐, and LARS1‐deficient patients (60%–90%) and relatively common in YARS1‐deficient patients (35%).

Otological symptoms, mainly sensorineural hearing impairment, are very common in KARS1‐deficient patients (78%), relatively common in HARS1‐, SARS1‐, WARS1‐, and YARS1‐deficiency (25%–60%) and much less common or not at all in other ARS1‐deficiencies.

Facial dysmorphias are common in FARSA‐ and YARS1‐deficient patients (70%); in FARSA‐deficient patients, dysmorphias of the fingers are common as well (70%).

Brittle hair (part of abnormality of integument) is rare overall but is described in all (4) of the CARS1‐deficient patients and all (2) of the TARS1‐deficient patients.


Immune system involvement is relatively rare overall, but chronic inflammation is common in FARSA deficiency (50%), as well as in IARS1 deficiency (70%), together with recurrent infections.

Symptoms that are triggered by infection are rare or not reported for the majority of ARS1 deficiencies, but are more common in EPRS‐, HARS1‐, LARS1‐, and SARS1 deficiencies (33%–44%).

We performed clustering analyses to identify phenotypically similar ARS1 deficiencies (Figure 3). UMAP revealed two distinct disease clusters: FARSA‐, FARSB‐, IARS1‐, LARS1‐, MARS1‐, and YARS1 deficiency, sharing a high prevalence of growth abnormalities, liver disease, hypoalbuminemia, and interstitial lung disease; AARS1‐, CARS1, QARS1‐, RARS1‐, VARS1‐, and WARS1 deficiency, sharing a high prevalence of CNS MRI abnormalities, seizures, and microcephaly. In addition, HARS1‐, KARS1‐, and SARS1 deficiency share a relatively high prevalence of hearing impairment. DARS1‐, EPRS‐, GARS1‐, NARS1‐, and TARS1 deficiency have a lower prevalence of these symptoms.

Several reports mentioned targeted treatment strategies (in addition to supportive treatments).

An infant with failure to thrive, liver failure, interstitial lung disease, anemia, and developmental delay caused by MARS1 deficiency received TPN at the age of four months. After five months, anemia and liver failure improved gradually, although she remained oxygen‐dependent and experienced motor (but not cognitive) delays and growth delays at the age of 3.5 years [14]. In a cohort of LARS1‐deficient patients, liver crisis and anemia spontaneously improved with age, although one of the patients was treated with TPN during acute liver failure triggered by illness and clinically improved [7]. Also, it was also noticed that the improvement of symptoms started once intake was established, similar to another patient with LARS1 deficiency, who experienced improvement in symptoms on a high‐caloric diet [15].

Specific amino acid supplementation has been described in 21 patients; protocols and outcomes differ. Duration of treatment differed as well as dosing. See also Table S2 for a summary of reports of treatment with specific amino acids. An infant with LARS1 deficiency with hypoalbuminemia was given a one‐month trial of leucine supplementation (300 mg/day) together with a high‐protein‐containing diet without effect on albumin levels (also no side effects were noted) [7]. Another study reported on a patient with IARS1 deficiency who was started on isoleucine supplementation (200 mg/kg/day) after which feeding, development, and susceptibility to infections improved [15]. Based on a study in yeast demonstrating that attenuated MARS1 activity could be rescued with methionine supplementation [16], an infant with MARS1 deficiency suffering from interstitial lung disease, failure to thrive, developmental delay, and liver disease was treated with methionine supplementation (dose not mentioned). Subsequently, respiratory symptoms, growth, and development improved [17]. Another study described two siblings with MARS1 deficiency of which the youngest child suffered from severe interstitial lung disease and liver disease, while the eldest child showed a subtle phenotype. Based on anecdotal evidence from patients with MARS1 deficiency indicating a benefit from the consumption of large amounts of eggs, the youngest child was treated with methionine supplementation (50–150 mg/kg/day) (together with intravenous immunoglobulins) which resulted in clear improvement in pulmonary symptoms and growth. Following this, the older child, who already had a preference for a high‐protein diet, was treated as well [6]. The effects of L‐methionine supplementation in patients with MARS1 deficiency were also evaluated in another study [18]. Four patients received L‐methionine supplementation (start dosage on 80 mg/kg/day, with further increase based on plasma concentrations) and were compared to a historical cohort with the same genetic variants. Methionine supplementation was well tolerated and clearly improved respiratory status, nutrition, inflammation, and liver symptoms. Treatment effects were better in the two patients who started treatment earlier (6 months vs. 21–35 months of age). In a separate report by the same research group, successful lung transplantations were performed in three patients with MARS1 deficiency and interstitial lung disease, who were treated with methionine supplementation (62 mg/kg/day) with no recurrence of lung disease post‐transplantation (follow‐up period 2.5–4.7 years). An earlier transplantation in a similar patient who was not treated with methionine had a fatal outcome due to lung disease recurrence (disease recurrence diagnosed at 2 years post‐transplantation, death at 3 years post‐transplantation) [19].

In an earlier study by our group, four patients with IARS1‐, LARS1‐, FARSB‐, and SARS1‐deficiency were treated with protein fortification and isoleucine (35–70 mg/kg/day), L‐leucine (35–100 mg/kg/day), L‐phenylalanine (40–100 mg/kg/day) and L‐serine (85.7–97.5 mg/kg/day), respectively [4]. All patients tolerated the treatment well, although the patient with FARSB‐deficiency, with a severe phenotype, died relatively soon after starting the treatment due to complications of pre‐treatment liver failure. With cognate amino acid supplementation, a higher number of clinical improvements were observed than with protein fortification alone. Improvements were most consistent in growth, development, and coping with infections, but also for liver function and respiratory symptoms. In another study, a patient with FARSA‐deficiency and progressive interstitial lung disease received a protein‐rich diet and phenylalanine substitution (dosage not mentioned), but no improvement in the disease course was observed [20].

In a patient with YARS1 deficiency and a severe, multisystem phenotype, supplementation with tyrosine did not improve symptoms (100–200 mg/kg/day) [21]. In another paper, a patient with QARS1 deficiency and epilepsy experienced aggravation of seizures with glutamine supplementation (dose not provided) and returned to baseline when glutamine was stopped [22].

Finally, two patients with KARS1 deficiency were treated with lysine supplementation [23]. The first patient, whose older sibling had died from KARS1 deficiency at 7 weeks of life, presented with a severe neurological and hematological phenotype and was started on lysine supplementation (20–30 mg/kg/day) in addition to protein fortification at 10 months of age. After treatment initiation, the patient's clinical phenotype declined and they died at 14 months of age. The second patient presented with a milder neurological phenotype and was started on lysine supplementation (20–30 mg/kg/day) in addition to protein fortification at 5 years of age. After 3 months, a previously essential anticonvulsant could be discontinued and subjective improvements in development, behavior, and sleep were noticed by the family.

A ketogenic diet was tried in a patient with QARS1 deficiency and severe epilepsy, progressive microcephaly, and developmental delay [24], with persisting improvements in alertness, interaction, development, and seizure frequency (clinical improvements were already noticed before seizure frequency was reduced). In another patient suffering from developmental delay, deafness, and neurological regression (not seizures) due to KARS1 deficiency, a ketogenic diet, together with vitamin supplementation (B1, C, E, biotin, carnitine and CoQ10), was started, after which the developmental regression ceased and some symptoms improved [25].

Anti‐inflammatory treatments have been trialed in several patients with MARS1‐, DARS1‐, and FARSA‐deficiency, with varying effects [18, 26, 27].

Methylcobalamin, vitamin B1, and cytophosphocholine sodium were given to an adult patient with DARS1deficiency in an attempt to promote myelin repair, with a positive effect on limb motor strength [28]. Riboflavin and idebenone were started in a KARS1‐deficient patient with mild psychomotor delay, cardiomyopathy, muscle weakness, and easy fatigability, after which a marked improvement in fatigability was seen, together with a slight increase in muscular strength [29].

Here, we gathered information on 438 patients with genetic variants in 20 different ARS1 genes from the literature. Our results show that nervous system abnormalities and musculoskeletal symptoms are common among all different ARS1 deficiencies. The most frequent nervous system abnormality was developmental delay. Growth issues and ophthalmological symptoms are also common across many different ARS1 deficiencies. Interestingly, certain symptoms are exclusively observed in a specific subset of ARS1 deficiencies and not in others.

One cluster of remarkably similar, multisystemic phenotypes comprises FARSA‐, FARSB‐, IARS1‐, LARS1‐, MARS1‐, and YARS1‐deficient patients, who present with growth abnormalities (intra‐uterine as well as later in life for IARS1‐ and LARS1‐deficiency), liver disease, hypoalbuminemia, and interstitial lung disease. Neurological symptoms are common as well, although not as common as in many other ARS1‐deficiencies. Earlier work similarly suggested that LARS1‐ and MARS1‐deficiencies share many similarities [30]. Another cluster of similar ARS1‐deficiencies presents with a predominantly neurological phenotype (AARS1‐, CARS1‐, QARS1‐, RARS1‐, VARS1‐ and WARS1‐deficiency), with a high prevalence of CNS MRI abnormalities, seizures, and microcephaly, and with a lower frequency of growth abnormalities. KARS1‐deficiency similarly presents with a neurological phenotype; in addition, sensorineural deafness is often seen. In RARS1‐deficiency, nystagmus is often present as well. A limitation is that the common phenotypes of CARS1‐, GARS1‐, HARS1, and TARS1‐deficiency have been understudied since only a handful of patients have been described for these diseases.

We do not know how the difference in phenotype between the clusters and distinct other symptoms between ARS1 deficiencies can be explained by similar underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Previously, we hypothesized that defects in the multisynthetase complex (MSC, formed by DARS1, EPRS, IARS1, KARS1, LARS1, MARS1, QARS1 and RARS1, facilitating amino acid charging and subsequent delivery to the protein synthesis machinery) may explain clustering of disease subtypes [31], but that does not corroborate with our current findings. Other possible explanations for the variety in symptoms between the ARS1 deficiencies could be related to differences between the abundancy of different amino acid transporters; alternative splicing; (unknown) moonlighting function of ARSs that differ per ARS; local/tissue amino acid demands in combination with ARS protein expression; differences in the severity of defective misacylation or interaction of ARS proteins with the immune system.

In some patients with LARS1‐ and FARSB‐deficiency, aminoacylation deficiency in fibroblasts worsened during higher temperatures, which could be reflective of clinical deterioration during infections [4, 5]. Indeed, in EPRS‐, HARS1‐, LARS1‐, and SARS1‐deficient patients, febrile illness is regularly (although not always) described as a trigger; however, this is much less common in other ARS1‐deficiencies. Possibly, fever‐induced deterioration is underreported in certain studies. Another common hypothesis in the field is that symptoms worsen early in life due to higher translation demands during rapid growth. Indeed, for many patients, symptoms improved spontaneously with advancing age. Additionally, when patients did die, it was often at an early age, typically due to liver or respiratory failure, especially in cases of FARSA, LARS1, MARS1, and YARS1 deficiencies. On the other hand, developmental regression over time and disease progression were also common, especially for the ARS1‐deficiencies presenting with a predominantly neurological phenotype. Patients with a predominantly neurological phenotype also died much later in life. However, for many case reports, survival was only mentioned up until a certain age, leading to a high amount of censored data, which could have potentially introduced bias. A valuable next step for ARS1‐deficiencies would be the systematic collection of detailed natural history data with a longer follow‐up time, which would help to differentiate treatment effects from the natural evolution of disease.

Other than supportive therapy, several different disease‐modifying treatments have been reported in ARS1‐deficient patients, of which supplementation with cognate amino acids is most frequently described. Several patients with MARS1‐ or LARS1 deficiency seemed to benefit from increased intake or TPN, although liver crises and anemia spontaneously improved with age as well, so it remains difficult to distinguish these effects from natural history and to establish relative effects of increased proteins versus increased calories.

Most reported patients benefited from specific amino acid supplementation. Especially in MARS1 deficiency, all patients in whom amino acid supplementation was reported showed clear benefits after treatment [6, 17, 18, 19]. For 7 patients, underlying genetic variants were similar to those of a larger, well‐documented historic cohort, and therefore, the clear differences in outcomes are convincing of a real treatment effect [18, 19]. For other patients, particularly those with a severe phenotype, amino acid supplementation was insufficient to improve symptoms or halt disease progression. Most likely, amino acid supplementation is unable to reverse pre‐existing (neurological) damage. In addition, some patients with severe forms of ARS1 deficiency can probably only benefit from disease‐modifying treatment at a very early disease stage, which emphasizes the need for early diagnosis and research into other treatment options, including gene therapy.

Overall, as all reports on treatment effects are generally observational and we cannot exclude publication bias, unambiguously establishing treatment effects in this only recently recognized group of diseases with a heterogeneous and fluctuating (sometimes trigger‐dependent) natural history remains difficult. Therefore, it would be worthwhile to perform n‐of‐1 and other controlled trials in selected patients, with standardized treatment protocols. Histidine supplementation is currently evaluated for patients with HARS1 deficiency (causing Usher syndrome Type III) [32, 33].

In conclusion, this review provides a comprehensive overview of the currently available reported data on symptoms and treatments of recessive ARS1‐deficiencies. Although some symptoms (neurodevelopmental disorder, growth abnormalities) are common to all ARS1‐deficiencies, we found two phenotypical clusters that are clearly distinct from the other ARS1‐deficiencies: one with multisystemic disease, including liver and lung disease and severe growth issues, and another with a predominantly neurological phenotype, including seizures and microcephaly, in addition to some specific symptoms in individual ARS1‐deficiencies. Supplementation with cognate amino acids has been described in 21 patients (mainly with a multisystem phenotype) with improved outcomes reported in most (but not all) patients. The results of this review can serve as a reference to enhance diagnostic and therapeutic care and trial readiness for patients with (suspected) ARS1‐deficiencies.
