# CGGV:assertion_8a4a40fe-471c-4e9a-a28b-7db3a21cffa6-2026-06-02T160000.000Z  GCK / monogenic diabetes

**CGGV:assertion_8a4a40fe-471c-4e9a-a28b-7db3a21cffa6-2026-06-02T160000.000Z** - GCK / monogenic diabetes (Definitive)

## Evidence summary

GCK was first reported in relation to autosomal dominant (AD) monogenic diabetes in 1992 (Froguel et al., PMID: 1545870). This disease entity is often referred to as maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) 2 (MODY2) and GCK -MODY, as well as GCK -associated hyperglycemia. GCK encodes the glucokinase protein, which acts as a glucose sensor that regulates insulin secretion in response to rising blood glucose levels. GCK -related hyperglycemia is characterized by lifelong, stable, mild fasting hyperglycemia that falls in the impaired fasting glucose to low diabetic range (HbA1c: 5.6-7.6% and fasting glucose: 100-144 mg/dL) and generally neither responds to nor requires treatment. Furthermore, individuals exhibit a normal response to a glucose load, and generally have a much lower prevalence of the vascular complications that are often associated with diabetes (PMID: 24430320).

We have curated the inheritance pattern for GCK -related monogenic diabetes as semidominant because hyperglycemia due to GCK deficiency follows a dosage-dependent pattern. Individuals harboring a heterozygous pathogenic loss of function variant display the mild fasting hyperglycemia phenotype described above, whereas the presence of biallelic pathogenic loss of function variants results in permanent neonatal diabetes mellitus (PNDM). PNDM is a more severe presentation characterized by insulin-requiring hyperglycemia with onset occurring within the first six months of life and is often associated with intrauterine growth restriction and low birth weight. It is often found in families of individuals with PNDM, that upon closer evaluation, the parents of the affected individual are found to have mild fasting hyperglycemia, consistent with GCK -related hyperglycemia. As such, a semidominant mode of inheritance was selected for this curation, and evidence from families displaying both autosomal dominant and recessive forms of inheritance were included. The mechanism for both GCK -related hyperglycemia and PNDM is loss-of-function. Of note, there are also some reports of individuals harboring biallelic GCK variants that are diagnosed with diabetes outside the neonatal period (PMID: 24001579, 25015100, 31197960).

Numerous variants in GCK have been reported in association with GCK -related hyperglycemia and PNDM, including missense, nonsense, frameshift, and splicing. Evidence supporting the association of GCK with monogenic diabetes includes case level data, segregation data, and experimental data. The aggregated score for genetic evidence, including case-level and segregation data is 12 points. For the purposes of this summary, 25 variants in GCK in 24 probands from 16 publications have been curated: 11 with AD GCK-related hyperglycemia,10 with AR PNDM, and 3 with AR non-neonatal diabetes (PMIDs: 1570017, 11372010, 14578306, 15644838, 18571549, 21104275, 21518409, 23085272, 24001579, 25015100, 25555642, 25850297, 26587058, 27269892, 27289208, 31197960). Additionally, literature describing GCK segregation data within families was evaluated (PMIDs: 1545870, 1570017, 11372010, 14578306, 18571549, 21104275, 21518409, 23085272, 26587058, 27269892). More evidence is available to support the gene-disease association in the literature, but as the maximum score for genetic evidence (12 points) has been reached, this literature has not been curated exhaustively. This gene-disease relationship is also supported by experimental evidence (total 6 points), including expression studies, animal models, and in vitro functional assays (PMIDs: 7665557, 1354782, 1557365, 17353190, 23715323, 24309898).

In summary, GCK is definitively associated with semidominant monogenic diabetes, including AD GCK -related hyperglycemia and AR PNDM. This has been repeatedly demonstrated in both the research and clinical diagnostic settings and has been upheld over time.

Per criteria outlined by the ClinGen Lumping and Splitting working group, we found differences in molecular mechanisms, inheritance patterns, and phenotypic variability underlying the GCK -related disease entities. autosomal dominant maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) 2 (MIM:125851) and autosomal recessive permanent neonatal diabetes mellitus (MIM:606176) have been lumped together into the current curation because of the overlapping underlying molecular mechanism (loss-of-function GCK variants. Familial hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, type 3 (MIM:602485) has been split into a separate curation due to the difference in underlying molecular mechanism (heterozygous activating GCK variants) and phenotype. Type 2 diabetes (polygenic; MIM:125853) will not be curated since the contribution of GCK in these instances is the result of common variants with a small effect.
