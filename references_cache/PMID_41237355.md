# Natural History of Children and Adolescents With Dravet Syndrome: A 24-Month Follow-Up.
**Authors:** Sullivan J, Wirrell EC, Knupp KG, Ciliberto M, Ziobro J, Chen DY, Flamini JR, Zafar M, LaVallee N, Stepanians M, Ventola P, Chavan TS, Wang F, Parkerson KA, Ticho B
**Journal:** Neurology (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1212/WNL.0000000000214388](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214388)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Dravet syndrome (DS) is an intractable developmental and epileptic encephalopathy caused primarily by pathogenic variants in the voltage-gated sodium channel α subunit 1 (SCN1A) gene. Patients with DS experience refractory seizures and significant cognitive and behavioral deficits. Longitudinal studies using standardized assessments are needed to systematically document patient outcomes over time. The BUTTERFLY study aimed to assess changes in adaptive functioning and neurodevelopment over 24 months in patients with DS.

METHODS: BUTTERFLY was a US-based multicenter, longitudinal observational study involving patients with genetically confirmed DS who were receiving standard-of-care treatment. Patients aged 2-18 years with a confirmed DS diagnosis were included. Exclusion criteria included gain-of-function SCN1A variants, current sodium channel blocker treatment, or other disorders per investigator discretion. The primary outcome measures-adaptive functioning and neurodevelopment-were assessed using Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale, Third Edition (Vineland-3); Bayley Scales of Infant Development, Third Edition (BSID-III); and Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence, Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV). Assessments were conducted at baseline and at 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months across 15 sites. Mixed models for repeated measures were used to analyze disease progression, and comparisons were made with published standardized scores for population norms.

RESULTS: Of the 36 patients enrolled, 21 completed the study. At baseline, the mean (SD) patient age was 10.8 (5.2) years, and 61.1% were female. Disease progression modeling indicated no significant improvements in 4 of the 6 Vineland-3 subdomains analyzed over 24 months. Receptive Communication (+7.24; 95% CI 1.30-13.19; p = 0.02) and Coping Skills (+4.61; 95% CI 1.14-8.08; p = 0.01) subdomains showed significant improvements. However, for Receptive Communication, this improvement translated to a magnitude of only approximately 3 months in developmental progression over the 24-month period. BSID-III and WPPSI-IV subtests did not show any significant improvement.

DISCUSSION: Despite a relatively small sample size and high withdrawal rate, BUTTERFLY data reveal a widening developmental gap between patients with DS and children with typical development over 24 months. These findings reinforce the critical need for disease-modifying therapies that address the underlying genetic cause of DS to improve long-term outcomes.

Dravet syndrome (DS) is an intractable developmental and epileptic encephalopathy primarily caused by pathogenic variants in the voltage-gated sodium channel α subunit 1 (SCN1A) gene, resulting in haploinsufficiency and reduced expression of NaV1.1 sodium channels. 1 These channels are primarily found in GABAergic inhibitory interneurons, and in some non-GABAergic excitatory neurons. 2 The loss of function of Nav1.1 channels leads to highly refractory seizures and significant cognitive and behavioral impairments. 3-6

DS affects an estimated 1:15,700 individuals in the United States, or approximately 20,000 individuals. 7 The disorder typically manifests in the first year of life, marked by recurrent and prolonged convulsive seizures often triggered by changes in temperature. 8 The spectrum of nonseizure symptoms associated with DS emerges as patients age and includes intellectual disability, behavioral disturbances, ataxia/motor abnormalities, speech impairment, autism, growth defects, and a higher risk of sudden unexpected death compared with other forms of epilepsy, creating a complex clinical landscape. 9-11

Owing to the highly refractory and prolonged nature of seizures in DS, use of multiple antiseizure medications (ASMs) is common and associated with significant treatment-related burden such as frequent medication switches and adverse events. Currently, there are no disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) for DS. Valproate, clobazam, stiripentol, and fenfluramine are recommended first- or second-line therapies as per international guidelines 12,13 and primarily focus on reducing seizures. However, a majority of patients with DS continue to experience both seizure and nonseizure symptoms. 13 Parents and caregivers emphasize the importance of addressing both types of symptoms when developing future therapeutic interventions, necessitating a comprehensive and mechanism-based approach to disease management. 14

To address this need, understanding the evolution of DS-associated nonseizure symptoms using prospective, systematic data from childhood to adolescence is imperative. Existing literature on DS indicates a period of normal development followed by plateauing or slowing; however, studies using standardized measures are needed to document outcomes over the course of the disease. 15

The goal of the BUTTERFLY natural history study was to assess changes in adaptive functioning, neurodevelopment, and other nonseizure symptoms over 24 months in patients with DS who used regular standard of care (SOC) treatment with best-available ASMs. Previously published 3-month and 12-month interim analyses demonstrated the validity of using Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Third Edition (Vineland-3); Bayley Scales of Infant Development, Third Edition (BSID-III); and Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence, Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV) to assess adaptive functioning and neurodevelopment in patients with DS. 16,17 The data presented here represent the final outcomes of BUTTERFLY after 24 months.

The BUTTERFLY study was a US-based multicenter, longitudinal, prospective observational study conducted in patients aged 2–18 years with a genetically confirmed DS diagnosis and uncontrolled seizures despite ASM use. The study comprised a baseline assessment and a 24-month observation period. Eligible patients were assessed for adaptive functioning, neurodevelopmental status, executive functioning, ambulation, and overall clinical status with an emphasis on cognition. The number of patients was sufficiently sized to provide exploratory and descriptive results. Assessments were performed at baseline and at 3-, 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-month visits. There was no additional follow-up period.

Investigators from 15 participating sites in the United States recruited eligible patients (eTable 1). Key inclusion criteria included an age of 2–18 years, a confirmed diagnosis of DS, a documented pathogenic variant, likely pathogenic variant, or variant of uncertain significance in SCN1A, and ≥2 convulsive seizures in the 4 weeks prior to screening (eFigure 1). Key exclusion criteria included gain-of-function variants of SCN1A, current treatment with sodium channel blockers, or any other disorder, which in the opinion of the investigator, would place the patient at risk. A comprehensive list of criteria is presented in eFigure 1. There was no restriction on the use of concomitant medications. Treatment with any investigational drug or device (unless in an open-label study) was not permitted.

Data from the 3-month and 12-month interim analyses have been published elsewhere. 16,17 This study presents the data upon completion of the study through Month 24. A summary of the study assessments conducted at baseline and every subsequent visit is shown in eFigure 2A.

The main objective of BUTTERFLY was to evaluate the course of adaptive functioning and neurodevelopmental status in patients with DS over 24 months while they were receiving SOC treatment. The following assessment scales were used for the primary outcome measures: Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Third Edition (Vineland-3): The Vineland-3 measures adaptive behavior through 4 key domains: Communication (Receptive, Expressive, and Written subdomains), Daily Living Skills (Personal, Domestic, and Community subdomains), Socialization (Interpersonal Relationships, Play and Leisure, and Coping Skills subdomains), and Motor Skills (Gross Motor and Fine Motor subdomains). This assessment was administered to the patients' parent/caregiver as a semistructured interview by a trained and certified rater at the specified time points. Raw scores from the Vineland-3 were converted to growth scale values (GSVs), which are reflective of the patient's absolute level of performance on an equal interval scale and are useful for measuring change over time. The Vineland-3 was administered to the parents/caregivers of all patients regardless of the patient's chronological or developmental age. Bayley Scales of Infant Development, Third Edition (BSID-III): The BSID-III assesses the development of infants and toddlers through a series of developmental play tasks. The BSID-III assesses Cognitive, Communication, and Motor domains, and was administered to the patients by a trained and certified rater at the specified time points. BSID-III raw scores were converted to growth score equivalents (GSEs), which are on an equal interval scale, similar to Vineland-3 GSVs, making them useful for assessing change over time. Although the BSID-III is intended for use in patients up to the age of 42 months, in BUTTERFLY it was administered based on the patient's chronological age and baseline test performance (eFigure 2B). Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence, Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV): The WPPSI-IV is an early childhood intelligence test that is divided into 2 distinct age bands (2–3 years and 11 months; 4–7 years and 7 months) corresponding to different subtest batteries which measure cognitive ability and development. The WPPSI-IV was administered to patients by a trained and certified rater at the specified time points based on the patients' chronological age and baseline test performance (eFigure 2B). Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence, Second Edition (WASI-II): The WASI-II is designed to assess verbal and nonverbal intellectual functioning across a wide range of ages (6–90 years and 11 months). The WASI-II was administered to patients by a trained and certified rater at the specified time points based on the patient's chronological age and baseline test performance (eFigure 2B). WASI-II results are not discussed further in this manuscript due to very few available results, reflective of the developmental age of patients with DS in this study.

The following assessment scales were used for secondary outcome measures: Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function—Preschool Version (BRIEF-P): The BRIEF-P is a 63-item scale intended to assess executive functioning in preschoolers (2–5 years and 11 months) and designed to be completed by parents, teachers, or caregivers. It contains 5 subscales—Inhibit, Shift, Emotional Control, Working Memory, and Plan/Organize—to assess executive functioning in different domains. These subscales are used to calculate 3 indexes, namely Inhibitory Self-Control Index (ISCI), Flexibility Index (FI), and Emergent Metacognition Index (EMI). A Global Executive Composite (GEC) score is generated from these indexes. In this study, the BRIEF-P was completed by the parents/caregivers of all patients, regardless of the patient's chronological or developmental age. Convulsive seizure frequency: Parents/caregivers of BUTTERFLY patients recorded countable convulsive and nonconvulsive seizures experienced by patients in a paper diary. Seizures were recorded for the first 4 weeks after screening for baseline and then for 4-week periods before each study visit. Countable convulsive seizure types included hemiclonic, focal with motor signs, focal to bilateral tonic-clonic, generalized tonic-clonic, tonic, atonic, and clonic. Gillette Functional Assessment Questionnaire (FAQ): The Gillette FAQ is a parent- or caregiver-reported measure that includes a 10-level classification of the patient's ambulatory function, and 22 functional locomotor activities rated on a Likert difficulty scale. Parents/caregivers of all patients completed the Gillette FAQ regardless of the patient's chronological or developmental age. Clinical and Caregiver Global Impression of Change (CGI-C and CaGI-C): The CGI-C and CaGI-C are clinician- and caregiver-rated assessments of the patient's overall cognitive functioning. They include domains such as attention, language/communication use, language understanding, memory, and academic skills. Change was scored on a 7-point scale with improvement ranging from “very much improved” (scored as 1) to “very much worse” (scored as 7). Clinicians and parents/caregivers completed the CGI-C and CaGI-C, respectively, for all patients regardless of chronological or developmental age.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4. For each clinical endpoint, by-patient spaghetti plots and mean score plots were used to evaluate linearity of progression over time. For all assessments, scores were excluded from analyses if they were performed outside of visit windows. WPPSI-IV data were excluded because of floor effects if a patient had a BSID-III score. Disease progression modeling for the primary outcome measures was performed using repeat measurements from multiple patient visits. Modeling included Vineland-3 GSVs, BSID-III GSEs, and WPPSI-IV raw scores. A p value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Modeling for secondary outcome measures was performed using BRIEF-P composite and index scores, convulsive seizure frequency, Gillette FAQ total scores, and CGI-C and CaGI-C scores. Disease progression was modeled using mixed models for repeated measures (MMRM) with random effects for intercept and slope (coefficient of time in months), allowing for patient heterogeneity while also providing an estimate of the average disease progression. 18 Patients were included in disease progression models if they completed a baseline assessment and at least 1 postbaseline assessment. Only observed cases were used in the analysis, as missing data were handled by MMRM, which uses a likelihood-based approach under the assumption that data are missing at random. Patients' age at screening, age at convulsive seizure onset, sex, body mass index, SCN1A genotype, baseline assessment score, and baseline convulsive seizure frequency were tested as covariates in the mixed-effects models. Covariate transformations (e.g., natural log) were considered for improving model fit. For each clinical endpoint, models were compared using Akaike information criterion corrected for finite sample size goodness-of-fit tests to select the best fitting models and prevent overfitting. Covariates were considered significant if p < 0.10. Relevant normative tables from Vineland-3, BSID-III, and WPPSI-IV manuals were used to compare development with population norms. 19-21

Caregivers of patients enrolled in the study provided written informed consent. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the appropriate Institutional Review Board or Independent Ethics Committee at each participating institution. The study protocol followed strict procedures for conducting, evaluating, and documenting the research and ensured that both the sponsor and investigators complied with the International Council for Harmonisation Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice for pharmaceuticals used in human studies. Compliance with these regulations and guidelines aligns with the ethical principles set out in the most recent Declaration of Helsinki (2013).

Anonymized data not published within this manuscript will be made available on reasonable request.

Of the 40 patients who provided informed written consent, 36 were enrolled in the BUTTERFLY study. Among the 4 patients who did not enroll, 2 did not meet the criteria for DS and 2 failed the screening. There were 12 patients in each of the 3 age groups (2–7 years, 8–12 years, and 13–18 years). Overall, 21 patients completed the study, while 15 patients withdrew before end of study (noncompleters) (Figure 1). One patient withdrew because of a coexisting illness and 14 patients withdrew for other reasons, including 8 patients who transferred to the MONARCH phase 1/2a study (NCT04442295). Of note, data from patients, including noncompleters, were included in disease progression models if they completed a baseline and at least 1 postbaseline assessment. Patients enrolled in BUTTERFLY (2–18 years) had a mean (SD) age of 10.8 (5.2) years (Table 2). Among the enrolled patients, 61.1% were female, 94.4% were White, and 83.3% were non-Latino. The mean (SD) age at seizure onset was 5.1 (2.0) months. Age at initial onset of clinically recognized developmental delay ranged 6–60 months with a mean (SD) of 20 (12.4) months. The mean (SD) baseline convulsive seizure frequency per 28 days was 14.3 (15.6). All enrolled patients reported ≥1 current countable convulsive seizure type, with generalized tonic-clonic seizures being the most common (reported by 86% of patients). At baseline, patients reported a mean (SD) of 3.7 (1.5) ongoing seizure medications or therapies (range 1–9). Clobazam was the most reported ongoing ASM (69%), followed by fenfluramine (44%), valproic acid (39%), stiripentol (39%), and cannabidiol (33%). The mean (SD) baseline Vineland-3 adaptive behavior composite was 44.3 (18.9).

Noncompleters (n = 15) were broadly similar to all patients, both in terms of baseline characteristics and baseline clinical profiles (eTable 2). At enrollment, the mean (SD) age of the noncompleters was 11.8 (5.2) years, and they completed a median of 4 visits before discontinuation. The mean (SD) baseline convulsive seizure frequency was 14.8 (16.8) seizures per 28 days, and their Vineland-3 adaptive behavior composite score was 43.5 (21.0).

Parents/caregivers completed all or part of Vineland-3 for 36 patients at baseline and for 14–28 patients at different postbaseline visits (Table 1). Disease progression modeling was completed for 6 of the 11 subdomains of Vineland-3. These subdomains (and domains) were Receptive and Expressive Communication (Communication), Personal Skills (Daily Living Skills), Coping Skills (Socialization), and Gross Motor and Fine Motor Skills (Motor Skills). These subdomains were selected to represent parts of all 4 core domains of Vineland-3 to assess multiple functional skill sets across the disease; of note, the Domestic, Community, and Written subdomains were specifically not selected because these are intended for an age-equivalence of 3 years and older.

The disease progression modeling for selected Vineland-3 subdomains included 21–32 patients who completed both an assessment at baseline and at least 1 postbaseline visit (Table 1). Disease progression modeling performed using individual subdomain GSVs indicated a statistically significant improvement in Receptive Communication (+7.24; 95% CI 1.30–13.19; p = 0.02) and Coping Skills (+4.61; 95% CI 1.14–8.08; p = 0.01) through Month 24 (Table 3). Data indicated that improvement from baseline approached statistical significance for Expressive Communication (+3.81; 95% CI −0.12 to 7.75; p = 0.06) and Fine Motor Skills (+6.93; 95% CI −0.63 to 14.49; p = 0.07). Despite statistically significant changes in certain subdomains, data suggested a substantial diversion in the progression of BUTTERFLY patients from the expected progression of population norms of the same mean developmental age-equivalent level; this diversion was observed across all modeled subdomains (Figure 2). The estimated developmental age-equivalence of BUTTERFLY patients was 19–28 months based on individual baseline GSVs for Vineland-3 subdomains. The estimated gain in age-equivalence over 24 months was 3 months for Receptive Communication. An accurate calculation of age-equivalence gain over 24 months was not possible for the Coping Skills subdomain because most patients performed below floor when converted to age-equivalent level at baseline. Overall, baseline score or the natural log of the baseline score was a significant covariate and a positive predictor of change across the analyzed Vineland-3 subdomains. Baseline seizure frequency was not a significant covariate across any of the analyzed Vineland-3 subdomains.

All or part of the WPPSI-IV was completed by 13 patients with valid scores (raw scores >2 on all subtests) at baseline, and by 4–8 patients across different postbaseline visits (eTable 2).

WPPSI-IV raw scores were used for modeling disease progression because conversion to an ability-based equal-interval scale was not available. Modeling across different subtests included 9–11 patients as they were assessed both at baseline and at least 1 postbaseline visit (eTable 3, eFigure 3). Disease progression modeling for WPPSI-IV subtests in BUTTERFLY patients indicated no statistically significant change across any of the subtests through Month 24 (Information: +2.36; 95% CI −3.23 to 7.95; p = 0.35; Similarities: +3.99; 95% CI −4.79 to 12.78; p = 0.34; Block Design: +2.83; 95% CI −3.60 to 9.26; p = 0.32; Matrix Reasoning: −4.02; 95% CI −9.24 to 1.21; p = 0.12; Picture Memory: −2.09; 95% CI −8.19 to 4.00; p = 0.47; Bug Search: +6.42; 95% CI −4.08 to 16.91; p = 0.19) (eTable 2). The estimated developmental age-equivalence of BUTTERFLY patients who completed the WPPSI-IV was 40–54 months as per baseline WPPSI-IV raw scores.

Parents/caregivers completed the BRIEF-P scale for 35 patients at baseline and for 15–31 patients at various postbaseline visits (eTable 4). As previously reported, patients—particularly those in the middle age group—demonstrated high BRIEF-P composite scores at baseline indicating difficulty in executive function. 17 Disease progression modeling of the composite and index scores included the 25–33 patients who were assessed both at baseline and at least 1 postbaseline visit. Neither the composite nor the indexes of BRIEF-P showed a statistically significant change (GEC: +1.27; 95% CI −17.04 to 19.57; p = 0.89; ISCI: +1.29; 95% CI −6.90 to 9.47; p = 0.74; FI: +0.17; 95% CI −6.58 to 6.92; p = 0.95; EMI: −0.87; 95% CI −9.72 to 7.98; p = 0.84) (eTable 4).

The mean (SD) convulsive seizure frequency per 28 days was 14.3 (15.6) at baseline (n = 26) (Table 2). Data regarding the change in convulsive seizure frequency was obtained from 18–31 patients across visits from baseline through Month 24 (Table 1). Modeling included 23 patients who were assessed for convulsive seizure frequency at baseline and at least 1 postbaseline visit (eTable 5). Disease progression modeling indicated no significant change in convulsive seizure frequency through Month 24 in BUTTERFLY patients (+10.61; 95% CI −32.97 to 54.19; p = 0.63) (eTable 5, eFigure 4). This indicates an ongoing high seizure burden despite the use of multiple ASMs and despite the option to optimize the ASM regimen per SOC throughout the study.

The Gillette FAQ assessment was completed by 14–34 caregivers or parents across visits from baseline through Month 24 (Table 1). Disease progression modeling included data from the 32 patients with assessments completed at baseline and at least 1 postbaseline visit. Modeling indicated no statistically significant change through Month 24 in the Gillette FAQ total scores (+0.81; 95% CI −2.13 to 3.76; p = 0.57) (eTable 5, eFigure 5).

Clinicians completed the CGI-C for 19–33 patients and caregivers completed the CaGI-C for 22–34 patients across visits (Table 1). CGI-C and CaGI-C disease progression modeling included 14 and 17 patients, respectively, who completed at least 2 visits. Modeling for the CaGI-C, but not CGI-C, indicated a statistically significant change (p = 0.04 for CaGI-C; p = 0.30 for CGI-C) through Month 24. The mean CGI-C and CaGI-C scores at Month 24 were 3.26 and 2.91, respectively (eTable 5, eFigure 6). In these assessments, a lower score corresponds to better clinical status, and a score of 3 corresponds to “slightly improved.” Baseline convulsive seizure frequency was a positive predictor for CaGI-C, suggesting that patients with a high baseline convulsive seizure frequency are likely to experience less improvement as rated by parents/caregivers in their overall status.

The BUTTERFLY observational study contributes valuable insights into the natural history of DS. Adaptive functioning and neurodevelopment (primary outcome), as assessed using Vineland-3, BSID-III, and WPPSI-IV, showed a general plateau at a developmental age of approximately 2 years despite the use of recommended SOC ASMs, including valproic acid, clobazam, stiripentol, fenfluramine, and cannabidiol.

We used a statistical approach, MMRM, to create disease progression models for estimating changes in patients' abilities over time. This allowed us to create an “average” progression curve while accounting for individual differences such as age and initial severity of symptoms. This approach provides a clearer picture of how DS affects patients over time and highlights areas where statistically significant changes occur. Disease progression modeling has been previously used to assess changes across different clinical measures over time in diseases characterized by complex clinical landscapes, including Alzheimer and Parkinson disease. 22-24

In our study, patient trajectories for all assessments, including Vineland-3 subdomains that showed significant improvements, deviated substantially from those expected in children with typical development. In addition, baseline data indicated that two-thirds of the patients scored at or below floor in the Coping Skills subdomain based on age-equivalent values. Although this subdomain represents Socialization, future modeling could explore subdomains without minimum age-equivalence (Interpersonal Relationships or Play and Leisure) which may offer improved measurement sensitivity. Alternatively, changes in raw scores or GSVs could be used. Ultimately, the relevance of subdomain content in relation to the developmental level of the patient population should be carefully evaluated when selecting end points for future studies. Although most patients demonstrated developmental age estimates around 2 years based on Vineland-3, some were able to complete WPPSI-IV, indicating cognitive variability and suggesting that a subset of patients with DS exhibit relatively higher developmental functioning. Developmental age of patients estimated using these assessments suggests a general plateau in adaptive functioning and neurodevelopment in patients with DS. These findings are consistent with observations from the 3-month and 12-month analyses and highlight the persistent challenges experienced by patients with DS and the increasing divergence from children with typical development as patients age. 16,17

The refractory nature of DS-related seizures is evident from our findings because BUTTERFLY patients experienced a high baseline convulsive seizure frequency that did not show a statistically significant change over the 24 months despite patients receiving SOC treatment and providers having the opportunity to optimize treatment throughout the study. Clinicians and caregivers generally rated the patients' overall clinical status as slightly improved, although only the caregiver-reported CaGI-C showed a statistically significant change over the study period. This discrepancy may be due to the subjective nature of these assessments and the fact that caregivers interact with patients more frequently. This highlights the need to consider multiple perspectives when evaluating patient progress.

Analyzing covariates is essential for identifying prognostic factors for future studies; these factors can be integrated into clinical trial design to evaluate potential treatments. In our analysis, baseline assessment score (or its natural log) was a significant covariate across most disease progression models, and it was a positive predictor, suggesting that patients with higher baseline scores tend to acquire the assessed skillset faster than those with lower baseline scores. Baseline convulsive seizure frequency was not a significant covariate for any of the analyzed Vineland-3 subdomains, but it was significant for several BSID-III and WPPSI-IV subtests, and for the CaGI-C assessment. For BSID-III and WPPSI-IV, baseline convulsive seizure frequency was a negative predictor, suggesting that patients with a higher baseline convulsive seizure frequency tend to acquire the assessed skillset at a slower rate. It was a positive predictor for the CaGI-C, suggesting that patients with a higher baseline convulsive seizure frequency are assessed as showing fewer improvements over time by caregivers. Although age at screening was a significant covariate for some subtests, this was not highly evident based on individual trajectories, as most patients across the age groups were generally stable over 24 months (eFigure 7). Future interventional studies will be crucial in evaluating the ability of patients across the age range to regain their developmental trajectory with targeted interventions.

Overall, the BUTTERFLY study provides valuable insights into the evolution of DS and emphasizes the need for a multidimensional approach to address the various clinical manifestations of this syndrome. These types of longitudinal observational studies will play an important role in evaluating the efficacy of potential DMTs for DS. SCN1A haploinsufficiency underlies the neuronal dysfunction that results in seizures and cognitive impairments in DS. 3-6 Thus, therapies addressing this genetic deficit may positively alter developmental trajectories of patients by alleviating both seizure and nonseizure outcomes. Early data from DS 25 and other rare neurologic diseases, including Angelman syndrome 26 and spinal muscular atrophy, 27 show that interventions targeting the underlying disease pathology have the potential to improve wider manifestations, including cognition.

This observational study was subject to certain limitations. Discontinuation of patients before the end of the study led to a substantial decrease in sample size by Month 24. Although the extent of missing data is recognized as a key limitation, the statistical models used to estimate disease progression included all patients with baseline and at least 1 postbaseline assessment by implicitly imputing the missing data under a missing at random assumption. Baseline characteristics of noncompleters were broadly similar to those of all enrolled patients, suggesting that disease progression trends for noncompleters could reasonably be expected to follow those of completers and supporting the modeling approach. Another limitation is that the protocol did not include any guidelines for patient enrollment, and consequently, the possibility of investigator-introduced selection bias cannot be excluded. Furthermore, several assessments, including seizure diaries, BRIEF-P, and Gillette FAQ, relied on the caregiver's ability to recall patient-specific events, leading to the possibility of recall bias. The use of assessments outside validated age ranges may have also affected the accuracy of results though they were specifically used in consideration of developmental age of patients with DS. Similarly, the changes detected here may have been confounded by the absence of baseline ASMs as a covariate. The lack of a dedicated control group of neurotypical peers makes contextualizing the observed trajectories more challenging. Finally, because this study was conducted in patients with DS who had uncontrolled seizures, the findings may not be generalizable to the very small subset of patients with DS who do not experience ongoing seizures. Despite these limitations, the data reported here provide an important resource for guiding future research and clinical strategies aimed at improving outcomes in DS.

In conclusion, these data align with previous findings and show that development in patients with DS lags substantially behind that of children with typical development, with the gap widening over time despite the use of best-available ASMs. 28-32 In this context, DMTs that target the underlying cause of DS, specifically SCN1A haploinsufficiency, have the potential to alleviate both the seizure and nonseizure burden associated with DS.
