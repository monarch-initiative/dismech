PURPOSE: PDE6A-associated retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a rare inherited retinal 
disease leading to severe vision loss and blindness, with no available 
treatment. This study assessed the safety and vision outcomes of a gene therapy 
using an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector encoding PDE6A (AAV8.hPDE6A).
METHODS: In an open-label, non-randomised controlled phase I/IIa trial, nine 
patients with biallelic PDE6A variants received a single subretinal injection of 
AAV8.hPDE6A. Doses were either 1.0×10¹⁰ (n=6) or 5.0×10¹⁰ (n=3) total vector 
genomes. Safety was the primary endpoint, assessed via clinical examinations, 
laboratory analyses and optical coherence tomography imaging. Secondary outcomes 
included changes in visual function, such as best corrected visual acuity 
(BCVA), contrast sensitivity, colour perception, dark adaptation thresholds, 
visual fields, patient-reported outcomes and chromatic pupil campimetry over 1 
year.
RESULTS: The mean patient age was 40.1 years, with baseline BCVA ranging from 40 
to 82 letters (0.9-0.1 logMAR). No systemic adverse events occurred, and most 
ocular events resolved without treatment. Persistent adverse events included 
small peripheral atrophic areas (n=2), disturbed colour discrimination (n=3), 
cataract (n=1), slight central retinal thinning (n=5) and moderate visual acuity 
loss (n=2, 1 in each dose group). BCVA, full-field stimulus thresholds and other 
visual function measures showed statistically non-significant changes, with a 
trend towards worsening of retinal sensitivity in the treated eyes.
CONCLUSION: Subretinal gene therapy with AAV8.hPDE6A did not improve visual 
function over 1 year and posed risks, including central retinal thinning and 
visual acuity decline. This is in contrast to the safety and efficacy profile 
established in preclinical models.

PDE6A- associated retinitis pigmentosa (RP) 
represents a rare form of inherited retinal disease 
(IRD), characterised by nyctalopia, visual field 
defects and significant loss of visual acuity. The 
disease primarily affects the rod photoreceptors, 
followed by secondary loss of cones.
1 Nyctalopia 
often presents as the initial clinical manifestation, 
followed by progressive concentric mid-
 peripheral 
visual field defects. V

DISCUSSION
The results from the first human phase I/IIa clinical trial assessing 
supplemental gene therapy for PDE6A-
 associated RP indicate 
that subretinal administration of AA
V8.hPDE6A in patients with 
advanced disease could not improve or stabilise photoreceptor 
visual function or retinal morphology within the observed time 
frame of 1 year. Although the treatment was generally well toler-
ated with no or only minimal signs of local inflammation, the 
subretinal injection of AAV8.hPDE6A was associated with local 
adverse effects and, in some cases, moderate (based on recom-
mendations of drug agencies)
24 deterioration of visual function in 
the TE compared with the untreated eye. It cannot be discerned 
to what extent average worsening of secondary endpoints and 
retinal thinning indicate loss of photoreceptor function and/or 
acceleration of disease progression. No clear relation between 
retinal thinning and BCVA or FST could be established. These 
findings were somewhat unexpected following the evident 
benefit of treatment with AAV8.hPDE6A in preclinical studies. 
Proof-
 of
-
 principle studies in mutant mice and dog models had 
reported 
improved retinal function and morphology at compa-
rable and higher dose levels. 14 15  In dogs, dose levels up to 
5.9×1011 vg (ie, one log unit higher than the high dose used in 
the clinical trial) were well-
 tolerated. Similarly
, in healthy NHPs, 
no adverse events were noted up to a dose of 5×10 11 vg. 25 
The fact that higher doses led to positive effects in the animal 
models begs the question of whether the dose levels chosen in 
the clinical trial were below the minimally effective dose. The 
allometric scaling across species takes into account differences in 
anatomical factors and the potential number of target cells in the 
treated area to calculate the equivalent multiplicity of infection 
or number of infectious particles (AAV). This poses the challenge 
of predicting the bleb size and position during and after surgery 
and predicting the cell density within the context of progressive 
IRD. Other potential confounding factors that could explain 
the discrepancy between the preclinical findings and the clinical 
study results include interspecies differences in terms of vector 
transduction efficacy, immune response and degeneration status 
at the time of treatment, as well as the actual rate of progression 
of degeneration, which is much slower in patients than in mouse 
and dog models of PDE6A-
 
RP .
Notably, preclinical studies were carried out in animals 
of very young age. Studies in mice, investigating the effect 
of the treatment time point of retinal degeneration, have 
shown that early treatment in gene therapy is paramount for 
success.

mice were injected at postnatal (PN) day 14 and dogs at week 
4. In contrast to the morphological baseline findings of our 
trial patient cohort, which all featured advanced disease, the

The Pde6a:Asp670Gly mutant mice at day 14 have a slightly

27 28 Although it is 
difficult to assess the number of remaining rods and cones in 
vivo, the significant thinning of the outer nuclear layer outside 
the central preserved region indicates that our patients likely 
began the trial with a substantially reduced number of rod 
photoreceptors at baseline. This would limit the potential of 
any clinical benefit, as the treatment was specifically targeted 
at rod photoreceptors, aiming at their functional and morpho-
logical preservation to prevent secondary degeneration of the 
remaining cone photoreceptor population. However, even 
in the only two patients with measurable rod function in 
the CPC at baseline, no improvement was noted, and CPC 
responses 1 year after treatment indicated a loss of measurable 
rod function.
With the potential for a treatment effect reduced a priori, the 
risk/benefit ratio quickly becomes challenging. Although there is 
evidence that short-
 lived iatrogenic 
detachment of healthy retina 
can be well-
 tolerated, 
photoreceptor degeneration is a well-
 
known consequence of retinal detachment even in otherwise 
healthy 
eyes.29 30 In the preclinical large animal studies, a signif-
icant loss of retinal cells was not observed, but the advanced 
disease stage in our patient cohort at baseline may make the retina 
more susceptible to additional damage and increase the risk for 
further cell loss due to the surgical intervention. Only one study 
using the same breed of Pde6a mutant dogs but a different AAV 
vector has reported the adverse event of retinal atrophy in vector 
TEs. Unfortunately, the study did not include a comparison with 
sham-
 injected eyes, leaving the question open to what degree the 
detachment of a retina with 
PDE6A-
 associated RP is causative 
for these adverse events.16
The postoperative loss of CRT and the transient functional 
impairment (first visit at week 2) with slow and incomplete 
resolution suggest that very early, if not immediate, effects of 
the surgical procedure may be the main cause of the adverse 
events observed. While this could be explained by an inca-
pacity of the degenerated retina to recover from the detach-
ment, the procedure itself was similar to other subretinal 
gene therapies. Therefore, undesired effects of the vector 
or a subclinical immune response must also be considered. 
Immune responses to AAV-
 mediated subretinal gene therapy
 
have been described previously, though in general at doses 
above the high dose of this study.
31
The findings of this study should be interpreted in light 
of its limitations, including the open-
 label, non-
 randomised
 
controlled trial design, the small sample size (nine patients 
total) and the relatively short duration of follow-
 up.
In summary
, subretinal gene therapy with AAV8.hPDE6A 
did not result in functional improvements in our patient cohort 
at the dose level applied. We did not observe preservation of 
cone photoreceptor function or functional rescue of rods, 
but in some patients, we recorded a decline in visual func-
tion and morphological alterations of photoreceptors that did 
not completely resolve over the observation period of 1 year. 
It is important to note that due to the slow rate of progres-
sion of PDE6A-
 RP
, which typically exhibits annual BCVA 
decline rates of 0.015 to 0.037 logMAR, 1 2 32  a follow-  up of 
12 months would likely not be sufficient to detect a slowing 
down of disease progression. It remains open whether a 
different dose or an optimised vector with broader transduc-
tion efficiency and/or the capability to spread laterally beyond 
the bleb margins, enabling subretinal treatment without foveal 
detachment, would lead to more positive efficacy outcomes.
