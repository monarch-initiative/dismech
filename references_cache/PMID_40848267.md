Varicella zoster virus (VZV) is a neurotropic member of the Herpesviridae family 
that causes varicella in primary infection and zoster during reactivation but in 
rare cases can lead to severe neurological complications, such as encephalitis. 
To identify inborn errors of immunity and unravel pathways involved in VZV 
central nervous system (CNS) immune responses and pathogenesis, we performed 
whole exome sequencing on a cohort of 38 children with neurological 
manifestations, including VZV encephalitis, cerebellitis, or stroke. We 
identified a total of 46 rare potentially pathogenic variants predicted to be 
deleterious, including variants in innate antiviral immunity, inflammation, cell 
stress responses, and autophagy. Collectively, these findings represent a 
knowledge base for further functional studies, provide new insights into the 
genetic landscape of VZV CNS infections, and highlight potential genetic defects 
that may compromise host defense, enabling new avenues for diagnosis and 
personalized treatment strategies for VZV CNS infections.

Varicella zoster virus (VZV) is a 125-kbp double-stranded 
DNA (dsDNA) virus belonging to the Herpesviridae family, 
and is one of 9 herpesviruses known to infect humans [1 ]. In 
an immunological VZV-naive community, the virus typically 
causes mild disease in most individuals, although with geo -
graphical differences. Most commonly, the virus is acquired 
during early childhood through the respiratory system. VZV 
replicates in T cells, leading to viremia and subsequent dissem
-
ination to various organs. This systemic spread causes varicella 
(chickenpox) [
2] with an annual global incidence of at least 140 
million new cases per year. Following the resolution of clinical 
symptoms, VZV establishes latency in dorsal root sensory neu-
rons and cranial nerves, with approximately 80% of adult 
individuals showing seropositivity [3]. Reactivation of the virus 
later in life occurs in roughly one-third of these individuals, 
presenting clinically as shingles (herpes zoster) [4 ]. In rare 
cases, VZV may spread to the central nervous system (CNS) 
during primary infection or reactivation, resulting in severe 
neurological conditions, including meningitis or encephalitis. 
Currently, there are vaccines available against VZV: a live at -
tenuated vaccine given in childhood to prevent primary infec-
tion/varicella and a subunit vaccine for middle-aged and 
elderly individuals to prevent reactivation/zoster [
5].
Although the immune system comprises multiple innate and 
adaptive defense mechanisms, VZV can, in rare cases, infect the 
CNS during either primary infection or reactivation from the 
trigeminal ganglion, and thereby lead to meningitis or enceph
-
alitis, which can be fatal [1
–3, 6–8]. VZV encephalitis is defined 
as an inflammation of brain parenchyma, together with cere -
brospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis (
>5 cells/μL), detection of 
VZV by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or intrathecal anti -
body test, and associated neurologic dysfunction. This rare 
but serious disease occurs in about 1 of 10 000 infected individ
-
uals [6, 9]. Patients present with meningism and altered level of 
consciousness and may experience impaired cognition, dys -
phasia, seizures, cranial neuropathies, and/or motor or sensory

Severe VZV phenotypes manifest primarily in vulnerable pop -
ulations (ie, infants, elderly, immunocompromised due to medi -
cations, malignancy, or transplantation) [ 11 , 12 ]. However, 
recent developments in the field have revealed that interindividual 
differences in disease severity can be attributed to genetic defects 
in the host immune responses and, to a lesser extent, potentially to 
aspects related to VZV virulence and inoculum [ 13–16 ]. 
Especially when severe VZV CNS infection is reported in children 
or young and otherwise healthy individuals, this may suggest a po -
tentially underlying host genetic basis. Such defects are termed in -
born errors of immunity (IEI) and are defined by mutations in 
genes regulating host antiviral immunity and/or defects in genes 
causing pathology through dysregulated or excessive inflamma -
tion. Over the last years, several IEIs have been identified and as -
sociated with severe infection triggered by VZV or other 
herpesviruses. These include defects in GATA2 [ 17 , 18 ], DOCK2 
[ 14 , 19 ], DOCK8 [ 20 ], and IFNGR1 [ 21 ]. We previously identified 
IEIs in genes of RNA polymerase III including POLR3A and 
POLR3C underlying VZV CNS infection in children and adults 
[ 22–25 ]. Additionally, several studies identified variants in the 
type I interferon (IFN) pathway (eg, IRF7, STAT4, JAK2), along -
side other potentially disease-causing gene variants in adult pa -
tients with acute retinal necrosis triggered by VZV, herpes 
simplex virus (HSV) type 1, or HSV-2 reactivation [ 26 , 27 ]. 
These findings establish a strong cause for IEIs as underlying fac -
tors for failing host immunity in VZV CNS infection. However, 
the genetic variants so far identified only explain the minority of 
cases, and much remains to be understood regarding the patho -
genesis and pathophysiology of this severe infectious CNS disease. 
The aim for this study was to perform a detailed investigation of a 
relatively large cohort of children with VZV CNS infection from 
Belgium, to discover potentially new IEIs and compare these find -
ings to the previously published cohorts from other geographical 
regions.
METHODS
Patient Cohort
Patients were retrospectively recruited using databases from 
hospitals and pediatric practices in Belgium in accordance 
with local regulations and with the approval of the institutional 
review board (IRB) of the Antwerp University Hospital. 
Informed consent was obtained in accordance with local 
regulations and with IRB approval. The cohort included 38 pa -
tients with primary VZV infection (chickenpox) (median age, 7 
years [range, 9 months to 13 years]), diagnosed with meningi -
tis/meningoencephalitis (n = 6), encephalitis (n = 10), cerebel -
litis (n = 9), or stroke (n = 13). Patients were identified as 
having severe VZV-related CNS complications if (1) severe 
clinical course led to hospitalization, (2) the complication oc -
curred after onset of chickenpox and no other reasons for the 
complication were found, or (3) CSF collected after the compli -
cation tested positive for VZV DNA using PCR. Patients were 
diagnosed according to standard criteria, including neurologi -
cal symptoms and detection of VZV in the CSF either by PCR 
or serological analysis.
DNA Isolation and Sequencing
Genomic DNA was isolated according to standard protocols as 
further described in the Supplementary Methods .
Whole Exome Sequencing Analysis
Sequencing reads were preprocessed using standard tools, and 
variants were filtered using minor allele frequency (MAF) and 
combined annotation dependent depletion (CADD) scores as 
described in the Supplementary Methods . A schematic repre -
sentation of the filtering process is displayed in Figure 1 . The 
pathogenicity of the translational impact of missense mutations 
has been additionally verified using the AlphaMissense tool [ 28 ]. 
Several of the proteins encoded by the identified genes have 
functions in multiple pathways of which some have no known 
relation to viral infection and/or antiviral immunology. As the 
focus of this report is VZV CNS infections, pathways beyond 
viral infection, immunology, and CNS functions were excluded 
from the analysis.
Functional Evaluation of Patient Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells
Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by 
standard Ficoll density gradient centrifugation and subse -
quently stimulated or infected. RNA was then isolated and 
reverse-transcription PCR performed on housekeeping and 
target genes. A detailed description of the procedure is provid -
ed in the Supplementary Methods .

NF-κB Signaling and Inflammatory Cytokines
Accounting for around 24% of the identified variants (11/46), 
the NF-κB pathway was highly represented in this cohort. 
Typically sequestered inactively in the cytoplasm, NF-κB tran-
scription factors are bound to inhibitory proteins, I
κBs, which 
upon stimulation are phosphorylated and degraded, leading to 
the release and activation of NF-κB–induced inflammation, cell 
survival, and proliferation [33]. Several variants in molecules 
acting upstream of I
κB phosphorylation were identified in 
our cohort, including variants in TRAF2 and MAP3K7/TAK1, 
additonally a variant affecting the noncanonical NF-κB activa-
tion pathway was identified in the gene encoding CARD14/

Another group of genes identified are encoding proteins of the

cellular stress signals, leading to the formation of a phagophore, 
which engulfs the targeted material from the cytoplasm and ex-
pands, thereby forming the autophagosome that fuses with a ly-

NFATC3 and ZNF660

DISCUSSION
This study sheds light on the genetic underpinnings of suscept-
ibility to VZV CNS infections by identifying rare variants in 
immune-related genes from a cohort of childhood VZV CNS 
infection. Through WES, we discovered novel potentially 
disease-causing variants in VZV-induced CNS infection, to
-
gether with previous publications from us and others [ 13, 26] 
providing insight into host factors that may compromise im -
mune defense mechanisms against this neurotropic virus, 
thereby providing a foundation for understanding the genetic 
basis of susceptibility to severe VZV infections. Our data did 
not reveal any correlations between specific variants or path
-
ways nor particular clinical subtypes of VZV CNS infection, 
when clustering the variants to the respective disease categories 
(
Supplementary Table 6). Therefore, we hypothesize that IEIs, 
such as the putative newly identified ones, lead to an impaired 
immune response, and the pathological manifestation might 
depend on the extent of impaired antiviral immunity versus in-
flammation and immunopathology and may also depend on 
other factors (eg, other digenic variants, sex, pathogen load). 
Overall, our results confirm previous studies and show that al
-
though often clustering to the same biological/immunological 
pathways, only a few individual gene variants are represented 
in several patients, suggesting immunological and biological 
homogeneity of affected pathways but limited genetic homoge
-
neity. Interestingly, some patients harbored more than one var-
iant across the different immune pathways examined.
The first line of protection against viral infections is viral 
sensing and the activation of innate immune responses. 
Among these, we identified variants in RNA polymerase III 
and PACT. RNA polymerase III is involved in sensing cytoplas
-
mic DNA of viral origin and inducing type I IFN responses [10, 
23, 24]. The recurrent detection of RNA polymerase III in this 
and other cohorts suggests that impaired viral DNA sensing 
may be a significant factor in VZV CNS infection. Similarly, 
PACT plays a key role in detecting (ds)RNA, which is generated 
during viral replication [31]. This highlights the critical need for 
proper RNA sensing in antiviral defense, and disruptions in

VZV CNS invasion. However, it may appear surprising that 
no variants in other DNA or RNA sensors were identified.
A substantial proportion of variants in genes mapped to 
NF-κB signaling, which is central to inflammation and immune 
regulation. Several variants in NF-κB activators, such as TRAF2, 
CARMA2, CARD9, and TAK1, were identified, supporting the 
idea that dysregulated NF-κB signaling contributes to VZV path -
ogenesis. Disruption in this pathway may impair the host’s ability 
to mount a robust immune response, facilitating viral persistence 
in the CNS, or alternatively in the case of gain of function result -
ing in CNS inflammation. Additionally, a subset of variants in 
autophagy-related genes, including ATG4 and ATG7, were iden -
tified. Autophagy has been recognized as a key antiviral defense 
mechanism, by supporting the degradation of viral particles, en -
hancing antigen presentation, and controlling infection [ 34 ]. 
Dysfunction in these pathways may result in ineffective clearance 
of VZV from infected cells. As some viruses, including herpesvi -
ruses, have been demonstrated to inhibit autophagy to establish a 
chronic infection [ 44 , 45 ], impaired autophagy could lead to viral 
persistence and reactivation, as well as hyperinflammation and 
pathology. The role of autophagy in neuronal homeostasis fur -
ther underscores its importance in preventing neurological com -
plications of VZV infections, as autophagic dysfunction in 
neurons could exacerbate CNS damage [ 16 , 46 ].
The detection of variants in the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) 
pathway (eg, MAP2K7) is also notable, as these kinases are activat -
ed upon cellular stress and inflammatory cytokines, both of which 
are prominent in viral infections. The JNK pathway mediates 
stress responses, apoptosis, and the transcription of inflammatory 
genes, which are critical during viral infections [ 47 ]. VZV specif -
ically requires JNK for replication, and inducing both JNK expres -
sion and phosphorylation in all cell types tested, including 
neurons [ 48 , 49 ], suggesting a proviral role of JNK in VZV path -
ogenesis [ 50 ]. Hence, increased susceptibility to severe VZV infec -
tion would be predicted from either gain-of-function protein 
variants in JNK signaling or alternatively relate to proteins that 
provide negative feedback to the JNK pathway. In line with this, 
some of these genes are related to cell stress or cell death pathways, 
the disturbance of which might disrupt clearance of infected cells 
and in this manner lead to hyperinflammation and pathology.
Even though only one of the patients showed a phenotype of 
increased VZV replication in PBMCs, most patients showed el -
evated levels of IL-6 production, potentially contributing to the 
hyperinflammation phenotype during disease development. 
Follow-up studies will be useful to verify our findings and to 
identify other proteins involved in antiviral immunity to 
VZV infection. Finally, functional analyses are mandated to 
validate the pathogenicity of each variant. This point is espe -
cially important when considering the existence of redundancy 
between different antiviral mechanisms in different immune 
and non–immune cell subsets and tissues.
As VZV encephalitis is a rare disease, we hypothesized that 
pathogenic variants are equally rare, therefore disqualifying 
gene variants that are highly prevalent in the general popula -
tion. This is why a frequency of ≤0.01% in the general popula -
tion was chosen as a strict criterium. In each of the tables on 
genetic variants we have shown the MAF of variants in this 
gene in the general population, in all cases being very rare. 
Beyond this, it is very difficult to establish the presence of these 
rare variants in children who did not develop neurological 
complications of VZV. However, it is well established that 
IEI caused by monoallelic variants display incomplete pene -
trance, reflected in the fact that family members carrying the 
same variants do not necessarily always experience the same 
complication or disease severity.
Limiting factors are that the identified variants are selected 
based on bioinformatical predictions. Moreover, we used 
WES as opposed to whole genome sequencing, thereby missing 
potentially disease-causing variants in noncoding regions or 
variants leading to complete absence of transcription. In addi -
tion, the focus on DNA sequencing has the implication that 
posttranslational modifications cannot be recognized. 
Additionally, good sequencing quality, high CADD score, 
and low frequency were strict inclusion criteria, as this study fo -
cused on predicted highly damaging variants. However, this 
might have led to a loss of identification of some possibly im -
portant variants, as the identified genes only represent 26 of 
the 38 patients. However, we would not anticipate a well- 
defined genetic lesion for each patient. Thus, the identified lists 
of potentially disease-causing variants should serve as a knowl -
edge base for further detailed studies to eventually classify them 
into IEI underlying severe VZV CNS infection.
Collectively, this study highlights the complexity of host im -
mune defenses against VZV, with the potentially pathogenic 
variants suggesting contributions from innate viral sensing, 
NF-κB signaling, autophagy, stress pathways, and a minor 
component from adaptive immune functions. The identifica -
tion of novel genetic variants suggests that rare IEIs likely 
play an important role in VZV susceptibility and CNS compli -
cations in a fraction of patients, thereby providing important 
knowledge on VZV CNS infection pathogenesis and immunity. 
This has potential clinical implications, as identifying patients 
with these genetic predispositions could lead to targeted treat -
ments, such as VZV vaccination, immunomodulatory medica -
tions, or gene therapies for individuals with severe or recurrent 
VZV infection in the CNS.
