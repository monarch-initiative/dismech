Both primary and secondary breast angiosarcoma (AS) are characterized by 
multifocal presentation and aggressive behavior. Despite multimodality therapy, 
local and distant relapse rates remain high. Therefore, neoadjuvant chemotherapy 
(NACT) is employed to improve the R0 resection rates and survival, but its 
benefits remain controversial. Herein, we investigate pathologic and molecular 
correlates to NACT-induced histologic response in a group of 29 breast AS, 4 
primary and 25 radiation-associated (RA). The two NACT regimens applied were 
anthracycline- and non-anthracycline-based. The pathologic response grade was 
defined as: I: ≤ 50%, II: 51%-90%, III: 91%-99%, and IV: 100%. An additional 45 
primary AS and 102 RA-AS treated by surgery alone were included for survival 
comparison. The genomic landscape was analyzed in a subset of cases and compared 
to a cohort of AS without NACT on a paired tumor-normal targeted DNA NGS 
platform. All patients were females, with a median age of 31 years in primary AS 
and 68 years in RA-AS. All surgical margins were negative in NACT group. The 
NACT response was evenly divided between poor (Grades I-II; n = 15) and good 
responders (Grades III-IV; n = 14). Mitotic count >10/mm2 was the only factor 
inversely associated with pathologic response. By targeted NGS, all 10 post-NACT 
RA-AS demonstrated MYC amplification, while both primary AS harbored KDR 
mutations. TMB or other genomic alterations did not correlate with pathologic 
response. All four patients with Grade IV response remained free of disease. The 
good responders had a significantly better disease-specific survival (p = 0.04). 
There was no survival difference with NACT status or the NACT regimens applied. 
However, NACT patients with MYC-amplified tumors showed better disease-free 
survival (p = 0.04) compared to MYC-amplified patients without NACT. The overall 
survival of NACT group correlated with size >10 cm (p = 0.02), pathologic 
response (p = 0.04), and multifocality (p = 0.01) by univariate, while only size 
>10 cm (p = 0.03) remained significant by multivariate analysis.

Angiosarcoma (AS) of the breast is a highly aggressive malignant vascular neoplasm exhibiting endothelial differentiation. Clinically, breast AS can be primary, occurring in younger females within the breast parenchyma, or secondary to prior radiation or lymphedema and centered in the skin of older patients. The incidence of radiation-associated (RA) AS has increased in recent decades as a result of preferred breast-preservation modality, including lumpectomy and radiation, over the traditional mastectomy for mammary cancer.1,2 Although genetically heterogeneous, displaying complex karyotypes, most AS show upregulation of endothelial receptors and angiogenic genes, including TIE1, TEK, KDR, and FLT4.3,4 In addition, as a result of the wide-application of DNA-based next generation sequencing (NGS) and other molecular tools, certain genomic signatures have emerged distinguishing RA-AS from other AS subtypes.5,6 Thus, most RA-AS harbor high-level MYC gene amplifications (at 8q24), which is only rarely seen in other AS types.7–9 Based on the high prevalence of MYC amplification in RA-AS, FISH testing or immunohistochemistry to evaluate for MYC overexpression have emerged as highly sensitive tests to support the diagnosis.7,10 Moreover, a recent molecular study focusing on breast RA-AS showed that MYC amplification was associated with worse prognosis than the small subset of cases lacking MYC amplification.11

As most breast AS have an ill-defined or multifocal growth, surgery alone may not be amenable in achieving negative margins. Therefore, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is often clinically utilized to potentially facilitate a better local control (i.e., to improve rates of R0 resection and reduce risk of local recurrence), but the actual benefits of this intervention as well as its effect on survival are not well defined. Herein, we undertake an in-depth analysis of the pathologic response in breast AS patients subjected to NACT and evaluate its potential correlations with certain molecular alterations and impact on survival. We also include for comparison a cohort of localized breast AS patients without NACT to evaluate for potential differences in outcome.

ASs of breast, primary or secondary, localized at diagnosis and treated with NACT were retrospectively retrieved from the Department of Pathology files and the Prospective Surgical Sarcoma database at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC), between 2005 and 2023 (IRB# 02-060, 12-245). For secondary AS, all patients had been previously treated with lumpectomy and adjuvant radiotherapy for breast cancer. We only included patients who underwent mastectomy or radical resection subsequent to completion of NACT. The use of NACT was determined on a case-by-case basis by the treating medical oncologist after multi-disciplinary consultation with the Sarcoma Disease Management Team at MSKCC. However, NACT clinical inclusion criteria generally included large, size >5 cm, multifocal tumors, preserved organ function and appropriate performance status. As there is no widely accepted standard of care chemotherapy regimen for NACT in breast AS, the treatment regimen was individualized for each patient depending on patient age, comorbidities, and performance status. A separate search for breast AS without NACT matched for stage (localized at diagnosis) and treated upfront with surgery was performed to select patients for a control group for survival analysis comparison.

Among both cohorts, cases tested by MSK-IMPACT between 2013 and 2023 were selected. Clinical findings including symptoms, multifocal presentation, latency interval from radiation therapy to diagnosis, NACT regimens applied, either anthracycline (liposomal doxorubicin or doxorubicin plus ifosfamide [AIM]) or non-anthracycline-based (gemcitabine plus docetaxel or paclitaxel), tumor size (largest nodule as determined radiographically or grossly), and margin status and disease status were collected from medical records.

Hematoxylin and Eosin and immunohistochemical slides were available for review in all cases. Among pathologic findings, the following were recorded: tumor size, architectural patterns (vasoformative, solid, papillary, blood lakes), cytomorphology (epithelioid), mitotic activity (per mm2), and presence of coagulative necrosis. For multifocal disease, the size of the pre-NACT largest nodule was recorded based on radiologic measurement, while the size of the post-NACT residual tumor was measured grossly and/or microscopically. Following grading of NACT pathologic responses in other sarcomas,12 we used a 4-tier grading scheme as follows: Grade I: ≤50%, Grade II: 51%–90%, Grade III: 91%–99%, and Grade IV: 100%. The pathologic treatment response was defined as a combination of necrosis, fibrosis, histiocytic infiltrate, and hemosiderin deposition. Coagulative necrosis alone, intermixed with large areas of viable tumor, was not considered pathologic response. The pathologic responses were further grouped into poor responders (Grades I–II) and good responders (Grades III–IV).

FISH was performed on interphase nuclei from 4 μm FFPE tissue sections using custom probes made from bacterial artificial chromosomes (BAC) flanking MYC (RP11-440N18; 8q24.21:128 596 756–128 777 986) in 87 cases (7 primary, 80 RA with 9 treated by NACT) and FLT4 genes in 74 cases (11 primary, 63 RA with 8 underwent NACT) as previously described.13,14 Slides were examined by using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axioplan, Oberkochen, Germany) and Isis 5 software (Metasystems). For interpretation, an amplification was defined as >10% of tumor cells with a MYC to control centromeric probe ratio >10, or the presence of tight clustered signals characteristic of homogeneously staining regions.13,14

MSK-IMPACT is a comprehensive molecular profiling assay that involves hybridization capture and deep sequencing of all exons and selected introns of up to 505 oncogenes and tumor-suppressor genes, allowing the detection of point mutations, small and large insertions, deletions, and rearrangements. In addition, the assay captures >1000 intergenic and intronic single-nucleotide polymorphisms, interspersed homogenously across the genome, aiding the assessment of genome-wide copy number. The assay uses paired tumor/normal DNA for sequencing and analysis. Details of the MSK-IMPACT assay have been previously published.15

Statistical and data analysis were performed using R software (version 4.3.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical data were evaluated using Fisher exact tests, and continuous data was analyzed using the Wilcoxon test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier method were used to compare the survival data between groups. Disease-free survival (DFS) was calculated as the time from initial diagnosis to the first event of either recurrent/metastatic disease or last clinical follow-up. Disease specific survival (DSS) was defined as the time from initial AS diagnosis to related death or last follow-up, while overall survival (OS) as the time from initial diagnosis to death from any cause. All p-values were based on a two-sided hypothesis, and a value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Mutations and gene-level copy number alterations were visualized and summarized using the R package ‘ComplexHeatmap’ version 2.8.0.16

A total of 29 post-NACT breast ASs (4 primary and 25 RA) from 28 patients were selected. All patients were females. The patients diagnosed with primary AS (median 31 years, range 29–69) were significantly younger than those with RA-AS (median 68 years, range 47–88, p = 0.03). Symptoms at presentation included skin thickening, erythema, bruise and palpable mass. A palpable mass was mostly found in primary AS and a subset of RA-AS, while skin thickening, erythema, and bruise were the predominant findings in RA-AS. The mean latency from prior radiation to RA-AS diagnosis was 8.6 years (range: 2–35 years) and the total radiation dose ranged from 4250 to 6080 cGy. Twelve (48%) RA-AS patients had multifocal lesions at presentation, but none showed distant metastatic disease. The size of the largest nodule pre-NACT ranged from 1.3 to 30 cm (mean 8 cm), as documented radiographically. Post-NACT, the tumor size decreased (p < 0.01) and ranged from 0 to 26.4 cm (mean 3.2 cm), as measured grossly and microscopically. The median change in tumor size after NACT was −3.4 cm. All surgical margins were negative. The follow-up duration after surgical removal ranged from 7 to 167 months (mean: 35 months) (Table 1).

Slides of pretreatment biopsy were available in 22 cases, showing a variety of histologic features, including: solid pattern (n = 15 cases, 68.2%), vasoformative (n = 14 cases, 63.6%), blood lakes, and epithelioid features in four cases each (18.2%). Histologic grading was only applied in the primary AS, as RA-AS were considered high grade by definition. All except one tumor was classified as high grade, which was defined as displaying solid growth (>10% of the tumor) and necrosis. The only lesion classified as low grade was a primary breast AS, which was defined by focal solid areas (≤10% of the tumor), up to 10 mitoses/mm2 (score 1 or 2), and lack of necrosis.17 Mitotic figures for the entire group ranged from 1 to 17/mm2. Pretreatment (spontaneous) tumor necrosis was identified in five cases (Table S1) (Figure 1A,C,E,G).

We selected 147 localized breast AS cases (45 primary AS, 102 RA-AS) from our files, which were managed by surgery without NACT. All were females. The primary AS patients (median, 41 years old) were younger than RA-AS patients (median, 69 years old, p < 0.01). The mean latency from radiation therapy to RA-AS development was 8.5 years. The tumor size ranged 0.3–22.9 cm (mean 5.1 cm).

Compared to the NACT group, the mean tumor size (largest nodule) at diagnosis in the non-NACT group was smaller (5.1 vs. 8 cm, p = 0.02). However, 35 (23.8%) cases (14 primary AS, 21 RA-AS) in this cohort were resected with positive margins. A significant difference was found in marginal status between NACT and non-NACT group (0% vs. 23.8%, p < 0.01). The mean follow-up duration was 53.4 months (ranged 0.5–283.6 months) (Table 1).

Twelve AS (2 primary, 10 RA) were treated with anthracycline-based NACT, while 17 (2 primary, 15 RA) received non-anthracycline-based NACT. Two patients (both RA-AS) who initially received anthracycline-based NACT experienced disease progression and subsequently switched to non-anthracycline-based NACT. One of them further received Pembrolizumab and Lenvatinib without clinical response.

The NACT response was graded on a four-tier system (Grades I–IV) as described above. Overall, the pathologic responses were evenly divided between poor responders (Grades I–II; n = 15) and good responders (Grades III–IV; n = 14). There were 10 cases showing Grade I response, 5 cases Grade II, 10 cases Grade III, and 4 cases Grade IV. Grade IV patients had a smaller mean tumor size at diagnosis than other groups (3 cm vs. Grade I–III: 8.1–9.3 cm, p = 0.02). Histologically, the residual viable tumor had heterogeneous growth patterns, including: vasoformative (n = 23, 79.3%), blood lakes (n = 9, 31%), solid (n = 9, 31%), and papillary (n = 6, 24.1%). Epithelioid features were seen in seven cases (24.1%). The predominant histologic pattern in residual viable areas of good responders was the vasoformative growth (all Grade III cases), while solid, papillary, and/or blood lakes patterns were only found in poor responders (Figure S1). The four patients with Grade IV response were all RA-AS. All four primary AS treated with NACT showed a poor pathologic response, with three being Grade I and one a Grade II. There was no difference between the morphologic patterns noted in the pre-therapy biopsy and treated samples. Mitotic figures >10/mm2 in pre-NACT biopsies was correlated with poor pathologic response (p = 0.04), being only identified in poor responders (p = 0.04) (Table S1) (Figure 1B,D,F,H).

Two primary AS and 10 RA-AS were analyzed by MSK-IMPACT. Both primary AS cases harbored KDR mutations, at exon16 p.T771R (c.2312C>G) or exon15 p.R725C (c.2173C>T). One also demonstrated hotspot of NRAS exon2 p.G13D (c.38G>A), HIST1H1C missense mutation and SRSF2, H3-3B, CXCR4 amplifications, while the other had an SLX4 missense mutation. (Figure S2) All RA-AS showed MYC amplification, which was also confirmed by FISH in four cases. Two cases showed FLT4 gene alterations, including one FLT4 amplification and one FLT4 mutation. Other recurrent alterations were uncommon, including: ETV6, TP53, and UPF1 mutations being identified in two cases each. All cases showed a low tumor mutation burden (TMB) of <10 mt/Mb. Other mutations are listed in Figure 2.

In an additional cohort of AS cases (not investigated by NGS), tissue was available for FISH analysis for MYC and FLT4 gene alterations. In eight additional RA-AS cases FISH showed MYC amplification. Overall, 18/25 RA-AS treated with NACT were MYC-amplified, the remaining 7 were not tested. Moreover, 7 additional RA-AS were tested by FLT4 FISH and none of them showed amplification. Thus, only one RA-AS showed MYC and FLT4 co-amplifications based on NGS. In the remaining eight cases, FISH for FLT4 gene alterations was not performed.

A total of 35 breast AS cases (10 primary, 25 RA-AS), localized at diagnosis and treated with surgery alone, were tested by MSK-IMPACT. The most common genetic alterations among primary breast AS were KDR alterations (60%, 3 exon16 p.T771R (c.2312C>G), 1 exon16 p.T771K (c.2312C>A), 1 exon14 splicing variant p.E685fs and 1 amplification), followed by 5 PIK3CA missense mutations (50%). Moreover, TP53 was identified in three cases (30%), ATM mutations and PDGFRA were found in two cases (20%). Detailed profile of primary AS mutation is shown in Figure S2.

The most common alterations in the RA-AS group were MYC amplifications (21/25, 84%), followed by FLT4 gene alterations (8/25, 32%), including two amplifications, three missense mutations, one in-frame deletion, one amplification and deletion, as well as one amplification and missense mutation. All FLT4 alterations cases were coamplified with MYC. In 14 cases, the MYC amplification was also confirmed by FISH. The next common recurrent genetic alterations were KMT2D mutations in five (20%) cases. HRAS mutations were identified in four (16%) cases, including three missense mutations, each in exon 3, (two p.Q61L (c.182A>T)), one p.A59T (c.175G>A) and one in-frame insertion. Four KDR mutations were detected (16%, exon16 p.T771R (c.2312C>G), exon15 p.C737F (c.2210G>T), exon14 p.C688Y (c.2063G>A), and exon16 in-frame insertion). Other alterations included three (12%) CRKL amplifications and mutations in NRAS, PIK3CA, FAT1, FOXO1, PTPRD, and RAF1 were found in two cases each (Figure 2). In four patients, sequential profiling from both the primary tumor and recurrence/metastasis tumor was performed. Additional acquired mutations were identified in recurrence/metastatic tumor, including FLT4, SETDB1, FOXP1, PRKD1, ERBB4, and 15q15.1 deletion in single cases. All cases showed a TMB of <10 mt/Mb. There was no difference in TMB or other genomic alterations between pathologic response or outcome in NACT and non-NACT group.

Additional FISH analysis was performed for MYC and FLT4 gene alterations. Of the five additional primary AS cases tested for MYC FISH, only one showed amplification. No FLT4 amplifications were detected by FISH in the 11 primary AS cases tested. In contrast, 54 additional RA-AS were tested by MYC FISH, 50 (92.6%) showed amplification and 4 were non-amplified. Forty-eight additional RA-AS were tested by FLT4 FISH, 5 (10.4%) showed amplification, being all coamplified with MYC. Adding the two cohorts together (NGS plus FISH), a total of 71 (89.9%) non-NACT RA-AS cases showed MYC amplifications, and 8 (10.1%) were non-amplified (Table S2). Overall, 9 (12.7%) non-NACT RA-AS showed FLT4 and MYC co-amplifications.

There was no significant difference in pathologic response grade, local recurrence, distant metastasis, or survival between the two NACT regimens applied. The good responders had better DSS than poor responders (p = 0.04) (Figure 3A). Additionally, good responders had a lower metastatic rate (7.1% vs. 26.7%) and there was a trend toward higher OS (Figure 3B) compared to poor responders, although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.06). Notably, the four patients with Grade 4 response remained NED.

Clinically, 11 patients developed local recurrence (37.9%) (all RA-AS), 5 distant metastases (17.2%) (3 primary AS, 2 RA-AS), to axillary lymph node, lung, liver or bone, and 8 died of disease (27.6%) (6 RA-AS, 2 primary AS). The presence of distant metastases correlated significantly with primary AS compared to RA-AS (75% vs. 8%, p = 0.01). There was no correlation between pathologic response and MYC amplification (p = 0.49). OS was associated with tumor size >10 cm (p = 0.02), pathologic response grade (p = 0.04), and multifocality (p = 0.01) by univariate analysis. However, only tumor size >10 cm showed significance (p = 0.03) for OS by multivariate analysis (Table 2 and Figure S3).

Sixty-three (42.9%) patients had local recurrence, 56 (38.1%) developed metastases and 54 (36.7%) died of disease. While local recurrence rate was higher in RA-AS than in primary AS (51% vs. 24.4%, p < 0.01), primary AS was more prone to distant metastasis (64.4% vs. 26.5%, p < 0.01). There was no survival difference between primary and RA-AS. Patients with positive margins showed an inferior OS compared to those with negative margin (p = 0.04) (Figure 4A). However, the positive margin was not associated with local recurrence (p = 0.12) or distant metastasis (p = 0.55). By univariate analysis, age (p = 0.02) and tumor size (p = 0.02) were associated with DFS. By multivariate analysis, only tumor size significantly correlated with DFS (p = 0.03). The OS was related to age, tumor size, and positive margin by both univariate (p = 0.02, p < 0.01, p = 0.04, respectively) and multivariate (p = 0.02, p < 0.01, p < 0.01) (Table 2). In the non-NACT group, patients with MYC amplified tumors were associated with older age (median 70.3 vs. 61.6 years old, p = 0.04) and higher metastasis rate (29.6% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.43) than MYC non-amplified patients (Table S2). The OS showed borderline significance of p = 0.05 between MYC-amplified and non-amplified patients.

Patients without NACT showed higher rates of distant metastasis (38.1% vs. 17.2%, p = 0.05) compared to NACT group. There were no significant differences in DFS and OS between patients with/without NACT in both primary AS (DFS p = 0.8, OS p = 0.2) and RA-AS group (DFS p = 0.1, OS p = 0.8). Among MYC-amplified tumors, patients treated with NACT showed a lower local recurrence rate (38.9% vs. 52.1%, p = 0.43), a lower metastatic rate (11.1% vs. 29.6%, p = 0.14) compared to patients without NACT and a significant better DFS (p = 0.04) (Table S2 and Figure 4B). There was no survival difference with FLT4 status either in NACT or non-NACT group. There were no differences in pathologic response grade and survival with any other genetic alterations.

Breast AS, whether primary or secondary, are often characterized by multifocal presentation and a highly aggressive behavior. Primary AS typically occur within the breast parenchyma of young women, presenting as a palpable mass, while RA-AS in the setting of prior radiation or chronic lymphedema in older women, often presenting as skin thickening, erythema or bruise. Over decades, breast-conserving therapies have largely replaced radical mastectomy as the preferred treatment for breast cancer.1 Due to this treatment shift incorporating external beam radiation and the subsequent prolonged survival for breast cancer patients, there was also an increase in the incidence of radiation-associated sarcomas, in particular ASs.2 For patients with localized AS disease, the standard treatment is surgical resection with negative margins. However, despite the radical mastectomy employed, the local recurrence and distant metastatic rates remain high, ranging from 20% to 40%.18–20 This is also observed in our AS cohort in the non-NACT group who underwent upfront surgery, with 24.4% of primary AS and 51% of RA-AS developed local recurrence, while 64.4% of primary AS and 26.5% of RA-AS developed distant metastasis. Therefore, multimodality treatment is essential for achieving better clinical outcomes. Although NACT has been employed in the appropriate surgical context to achieve negative margins, especially in the setting of large tumors and multifocality, its potential survival benefit has not been yet proven based on the prior studies.18,21–24 To assess the impact of NACT on tumor margins and survival, Saleh et al. studied 44 patients with primary right-sided heart sarcomas, 32 of them received NACT. Among these cases, 30 were AS with 24 of them receiving NACT. The patients were treated with a combination of doxorubicin and ifosfamide before surgery. An alternative regimen of gemcitabine and docetaxel was administered for patients with renal dysfunction or those who could not tolerate ifosfamide. The NACT increased the R0 resection rate to 46.9% of cases, compared to 33.3% with surgery alone. The median OS of patients who received NACT was double that of patients who did not (2 vs. 9.5 months, p = 0.417).22 In keeping with these results, all patients in our cohort from the NACT group had negative margins on the definitive resection, whereas 35 patients from the non-NACT group had an R1 resection (0% vs. 23.8%, p < 0.01). Moreover, the patients with positive margins exhibited an inferior OS compared to those with negative margin (p = 0.04).

Several retrospective studies focusing on AS have evaluated the impact of NACT on clinical outcomes and survival. However, these studies were limited by the small study group, unspecified site-related subgrouping or lack of comparison survival analysis among breast AS with/without NACT.18,23,24 Oxenberg et al. studied 23 non-metastatic cutaneous/soft tissue AS with/without NACT. Among the 10 patients who received NACT (gemcitabine plus docetaxel or paclitaxel), 3 patients had a complete response (2 gemcitabine plus docetaxel, 1 paclitaxel). There was no difference in margin status, local recurrence, or survival between NACT/non-NACT groups. Among the entire AS cohort included, there were nine breast AS (four primary, five RA-AS), with six breast (two primary, four RA-AS) AS patients treated with NACT (three gemcitabine plus docetaxel, three paclitaxel). One patient developed distant metastatic disease while on paclitaxel. The median change in tumor size after NACT was −3 cm. Microscopic positive margin was identified in one of the primary breast AS post NACT. However, the study did not include analyses of NACT pathologic response or survival specific to breast AS.23 Another retrospective study included 22 breast AS patients (11 primary AS, 11 RA-AS) who were managed with multimodality therapy. Ten patients received NACT (five primary, five RA-AS), they were allocated between anthracycline-based (three primary, two RA-AS) and non-anthracycline-based (two primary, three RA-AS) chemotherapy regimens. Five patients (50%, three primary, two RA-AS) had a partial pathologic response, three patients (30%, one primary, two RA-AS) had a complete response and no response in two (20%, one primary, one RA-AS) patients. Response rates were not associated with AS type or other clinical, pathologic, or treatment factors. However, there was no survival analyses performed between NACT/non-NACT subgroups due to limited case number.24 One study reported on 161 AS patients, including 57 breast AS, of which 27 had history of breast cancer (radiation-associated or lymphedema). Surgical resection was the first treatment in 121 (75.2%) patients, with only 9 (5.6%) being treated with NACT. No subgroup analysis or detailed clinicopathologic data regarding breast AS with/without NACT was provided.18

In addition, a handful of case reports described the clinical outcome of seven breast AS post NACT, including four primary and three RA-AS.25–31 The NACT regimens applied for primary AS included the following combinations: ifosfamide plus vincristine and dactinomycin, gemcitabine plus docetaxel, taxanes, and cisplatin plus doxorubicin and paclitaxel. Among the four primary AS, three showed complete pathologic response and one showed reduced tumor volume by 50% (on imaging). During the follow-up period of 6–24 months, all patients were NED.25–28 The three RA-AS received gemcitabine-based NACT regimens, with one showing complete response, while the pathologic responses of the other two cases were not recorded. All three patients remained NED at 12–31 months follow up.29–31

In our cohort, a total of 29 breast AS were subjected to NACT, with all four primary AS showing a poor histologic response (two AIM, one gemcitabine plus docetaxel, one paclitaxel). In contrast, RA-AS had a relatively even distribution between poor (n = 11) and good responders (n = 14). Among them, all four patients with Grade IV response (no residual viable tumor) were free of disease (three gemcitabine plus docetaxel and one doxorubicin). Moreover, there was no difference between good or poor responders among the anthracycline-based or non-anthracycline-based regimens. This likely is consistent with a clinical trial that noted equivalent efficacy in the GEDDIS trial that compared doxorubicin to gemcitabine plus docetaxel in the metastatic setting.32 The residual viable tumor foci showed only a vasoformative pattern in Grade III; while solid, papillary, blood lakes and epithelioid features were only observed in poor responders (Grades I–II). Although the tumor from the pretreatment biopsies showed a higher percentage of solid (60% vs. 42.8%, p = 0.07), blood lakes (20% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.29) and epithelioid features (20% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.29) in the poor compared to good responders, no statistical difference was found. Mitotic figures >10/mm2 in the pre-therapy biopsies was the only adverse factor associated with poor pathologic response grade. However, the limitation of performing the mitotic count on a small biopsy sample remains a caveat due to the occasional scant tissue for a full mm2 count or tumor heterogeneity bias. Overall, patients receiving NACT demonstrated lower rates of recurrence (37.9% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.84) and distant metastasis (17.2% vs. 38.1%, p = 0.05) compared to those without NACT. Additionally, good responders demonstrated better DSS than poor responders. Despite lower metastatic rates (7.1% vs. 26.7%, p = 0.33) in patients with good pathologic response, the difference was not statistically significant, likely due to the limited number of cases.

Although most AS exhibit genetic heterogeneity with complex karyotypes, by gene expression, not surprisingly, an upregulation of endothelial receptors and angiogenic genes is typically found, including TIE1, TEK, KDR, and FLT4.3,4,6 Large NGS studies have illustrated that RA-AS are frequently associated with MYC, FLT4, CRKL, PTPRB, PLCG1, HRAS, and KMT2D,5,6,33,34 whereas primary AS commonly harbor KDR, PIK3CA, TP53, ATM, and ATRX alterations.3,4,17,34 Other less common mutations, including KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, NOTCH, PTEN, EGFR, AKT, CTNNB1, and TERT, have also been reported.5,33 In our cohort, there was no difference in the genetic alterations observed between the NACT and non-NACT groups. In the combined cohort, KDR (n = 8/12, 66.7%) mutations were the most common genetic alteration in the primary breast AS cases, with five cases showing the hotspot mutations at p.T771 codon. PIK3CA mutations were detected in five (n = 5/12, 41.7%) primary AS, with three patients showing concurrent KDR mutations. The third most common alteration was TP53 mutation identified in three (n = 3/12, 25%) cases. Only one primary breast AS showed MYC amplification as detected by FISH; this low frequency of MYC amplification in primary AS being well-documented.7–9 In the combined RA-AS subgroup (n = 97 patients), comprising both NACT and non-NACT, in which material for NGS/FISH was available, MYC amplification (n = 89, 91.8%) was the most common alteration, followed by FLT4 (n = 10/74, 13.5%) amplifications. Among the 35 RA-AS cases tested by IMPACT, the most recurrent genetic alterations included: KMT2D (n = 6, 17.1%), HRAS (n = 5, 14.3%), KDR and CRKL (n = 4, each 11.4%), and ETV6, NRAS, TP53, and UPF1 (n = 3, each 8.6%). As previously described,11
MYC-amplified RA-AS in our series occurred in older females (median 70 years old vs. 59 years old, p = 0.05), more prone to distant metastasis (29.6% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.43) compared to non-amplified patients. There was no correlation between pathologic response and MYC amplification (p = 0.49). The OS showed borderline significance (p = 0.05) in MYC-amplified compared to non-amplified group. Previous studies have shown inconsistent results regarding the impact of MYC amplification on survival. Fraga-Guedes et al. reported that MYC-amplified RA-AS had a significantly worse OS in the 20/37 positive cases detected by IHC and FISH.35 There was also a trend toward poor DFS in MYC-amplified cases compared to non-amplified cases, although the difference did not reach statistical significance.35 In a study from The Netherlands, 110 cases of AS tested by IHC and FISH revealed that 16 (37.2%) primary AS and 40 (59.7%) secondary AS had MYC amplification,9 but no significant difference in median OS was detected between patients with and without MYC amplification.9 However, no subgroup survival analysis focusing specifically on RA-AS and MYC status was performed. To date, no comprehensive study assessed the correlation between MYC status, NACT pathologic response and outcome. In our cohort of 89 patients with MYC-amplified tumors, including 18 who undergone NACT, there was no difference between MYC amplification status and pathologic response. The NACT MYC-amplified cases showed lower metastatic rate (11.1% vs. 29.6%, p = 0.14) and significant higher DFS (p = 0.04) compared to MYC-amplified patients without NACT. Similar to MYC, FLT4 amplification usually occurs in the context of secondary AS, being co-amplified with MYC.13,14 Previous studies suggested that FLT4 amplification may represent a second hit in the progression of secondary AS.13 In addition, it was reported that secondary AS patients with MYC and FLT4 co-amplified tumors had a shorter OS.14 In this study, a total of 10 cases showed MYC and FLT4 co-amplifications, however, there was no survival difference with FLT4 status either in NACT or non-NACT group. Additionally, no differences were found in pathologic response grade and survival associated with any other genetic alterations.

This study is associated with certain limitations related to the retrospective nature and its inherent biases. Moreover, although our study is larger than many prior studies of NACT in this disease, the numbers remain small, that is, limited power to deduce differences in outcome between different chemotherapy regimens and between molecular subgroups. Prospective studies of NACT in AS are needed to better study its potential risks and benefits.

In conclusion, our study represents the largest investigation to date focusing on the clinicopathologic and molecular correlates of NACT in breast AS. The findings are also contrasted to a control group of breast AS patients who did not receive NACT, being treated at the same tertiary center and using a similar clinically-validated targeted NGS panel. Results showed that all patients subjected to NACT had a decrease in the median tumor size and had an R0 resection. Although no difference was found among the pre-therapy morphologic patterns and pathologic response, mitotic activity >10/mm2 emerged as the sole adverse factor associated with poor pathologic response. Moreover, all patients with a complete pathologic response (Grade IV) remained NED and good responders had better DSS than poor responders. Of note, there was minimal histologic response present in primary breast AS, which correlated with a high distant metastatic rate in this subset. In NACT group, tumor size >10 cm, pathologic response grade, and multifocal were associated with OS by univariate analysis. However, only large tumor size >10 cm remained significant by multivariate analysis for OS. Additionally, among the MYC-amplified tumors, patients treated with NACT showed a reduced metastatic rate and better DFS. In contrast, in the non-NACT group, age and tumor size were associated with DFS by univariate, while only tumor size remained significant by multivariate. The OS was significantly correlated with age, tumor size, and margin status by univariate and multivariate.
