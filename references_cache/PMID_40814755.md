# Biallelic Variants in the DARS2 Gene as a Novel Cause of Axonal Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease.
**Authors:** Estévez-Arias B, Sarv S, Bonello-Palot N, Carrera-García L, Ortez C, Expósito-Escudero J, Yubero D, Muchart J, Delmont E, Õiglane-Shlik E, Meren T, Puusepp S, Murumets Ü, Salomons GS, Udd B, Väli L, Cantarero L, Bönnemann CG, Nascimento A, Ramón-Maiques S, Õunap K, Hoenicka J, Natera-de Benito D, Palau F
**Journal:** Ann Neurol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ana.78005](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.78005)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) disease is a heterogeneous group of genetic neuropathies, with >90 genes identified. Several aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases have been linked to CMT. DARS2, encoding the mitochondrial aspartyl-tRNA synthetase, has been typically associated with leukoencephalopathy with brainstem and spinal cord involvement and lactate elevation. This study aimed to investigate the association between biallelic DARS2 variants and axonal CMT.

METHODS: We investigated 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families with axonal CMT and biallelic DARS2 variants. Functional studies in fibroblasts assessed their effects on DARS2 expression, localization, and mitochondrial function. Enzymatic activity was evaluated in HEK293 cells.

RESULTS: The 5 individuals, including 4 adults, presented with childhood-onset progressive axonal CMT. None had leukoencephalopathy, but one showed central nervous system involvement, with intellectual disability and epilepsy. Genetic analysis identified compound heterozygous DARS2 variants: family A, p.Ser238Phe and p.Arg336Cys; family B, p.Ser238Phe and p.Ile25Thrfs*38; family C, c.492+2T>C and p.Pro503Leu. Functional studies revealed reduced DARS2 protein levels, mitochondrial network abnormalities, and impaired mitochondrial function. p.Ser238Phe behaves as a hypomorphic allele, whereas p.Pro503Leu reduced DARS2 enzymatic activity by 75%.

INTERPRETATION: Our findings expand the DARS2-related disease spectrum, establishing a novel association with axonal CMT. Hypomorphic variants, such as p.Ser238Phe, when paired with more deleterious variants, result in isolated axonal CMT, whereas more severe combinations-although not as deleterious as those seen in leukoencephalopathy with brainstem and spinal cord involvement and lactate elevation-result in axonal CMT with central nervous system involvement, albeit without leukoencephalopathy. These observations raise the possibility that DARS2-associated diseases form a continuum rather than representing strictly distinct central or peripheral nervous system disorders. Here, we present 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families with recessively inherited DARS2 variants and an axonal CMT phenotype, without leukoencephalopathy. The discovery of hypomorphic variants, inherited in combination with a missense or a truncating variant, expands the phenotypic spectrum of DARS2‐related conditions and broadens the molecular etiologies of axonal CMT.

We investigated 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families, all presenting with axonal CMT. Two pairs of siblings were followed up at Hospital Sant Joan de Déu (HSJD), Barcelona, Spain, and Timone Hospital, Marseille, France, respectively, whereas the fifth individual was at Tartu University Hospital, Tartu, Estonia. Clinical records were reviewed, and a new comprehensive neurological examination was carried out on each affected individual. Blood samples were collected from affected individuals and their asymptomatic parents for DNA testing. Clinical data, electrophysiological studies, and brain and spinal cord MRI scans were collected and analyzed following the ethics guidelines of the institutions involved (protocol PIC‐147‐23). Written informed consent and age‐appropriate assent for study participation and biopsies were obtained by qualified clinicians.

DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using standard techniques. Individuals A1 and A2, a 23‐year‐old man and his 21‐year‐old sister of European Spanish descent, were born to non‐consanguineous parents with no family history of neuromuscular disorders. Both had unremarkable prenatal and early psychomotor development, but developed gait difficulties around the age of 7 years. Physical examination at the 9 years (A1) and 8 years (A2) revealed distal muscle weakness and atrophy, predominantly in the lower limbs, with absent patellar and Achilles reflexes. At age 9 years (A1) and ages 14 and 19 years (A2), nerve conduction studies revealed reduced compound motor action potentials amplitudes with preserved conduction velocities. Sensory nerve responses were absent, except for the radial nerve in A1, which showed reduced amplitudes. Electromyography detected in both siblings the presence of positive waves and fibrillations, leading to an axonal motor and sensory neuropathy diagnosis.

Over the years, the distal weakness and muscle atrophy progressed. By their most recent evaluations at ages 21 (A1) and 19 years (A2), both showed distal muscle weakness, with MRC scale grades of 3/5 in the peroneus and tibialis anterior muscles, and 4/5 in the hand extensors, hand interossei, and tibialis posterior muscles (Figure 1). Deep tendon reflexes remained absent in both siblings. Both had difficulty walking on toes and were unable to walk on heels, with mild instability on tandem gait testing and the Romberg test. Cognitive function was normal, and both were pursuing university degrees (Table 1).

At ages 21 (A1) and 19 years (A2), various laboratory tests were performed, including measurements of amino acid levels in blood, ammonia (A1: 23 μmol/l; A2: 18 μmol/l; normal: 12–50), lactate (A1: 0.54 mmol/l; A2: 0.63 mmol/l; normal: 0.5–2.2), GDF15 (A1: 195 pg/ml; A2: 265 pg/ml; normal: 200–540), and FGF21 (<30 pg/ml in both; normal: 0–300). All results were within the normal range. Brain and spinal cord MRI scans at ages 21 (A1) and 19 years (A2) were normal (Supplementary Figure S1). Repeated nerve conduction studies at age 21 years on the medial plantar, medial popliteal, lateral popliteal, and ulnar nerves of individual A1 once again confirmed reduced amplitude of compound muscle action potentials with normal velocity, whereas sensory nerve conductions remained inexcitable. Electromyography recordings of the tibialis anterior and vastus medialis were consistent with previous assessments and showed spontaneous activity, as well as deficits indicative of a neuropathic pattern and prolonged and increased amplitude. The overall phenotype in both siblings supports a diagnosis of axonal motor and sensory neuropathy.

Individuals B1 (man, 48 years old) and B2 (man, 50 years old) are siblings of European French descent, with no known family history of neuromuscular disorders. Both developed gait abnormalities and difficulty running from childhood, around the age of 10 years, with a slowly progressive course (Table 1).

On the most recent examination, individual B1 remained able to walk independently, but required a splint due to bilateral steppage. Physical examination revealed a complete absence of deep tendon reflexes in all 4 limbs, hammer toe deformities, cavus feet, and severe muscle atrophy affecting the feet, legs, and hands. Distal muscle weakness was prominent, with a muscle strength grading of 0/5 in the foot muscles, 2+/5 in the triceps suralis, 3+/5 in the interosseous and finger extensor muscles, and 4/5 in the psoas and quadriceps. Additionally, hypoesthesia was present below the knees and in the hands. Neurophysiological studies performed at age 27 years revealed intermediate nerve conduction velocities. Cognitive deficits, spasticity, and other signs of neurological involvement were not observed.

Individual B2 showed a remarkably similar phenotype, characterized by distal weakness that began in the lower extremities from childhood, accompanied by muscle atrophy and absent reflexes. Similar to individual B1, no cognitive deficits, spasticity, cerebellar ataxia, or other neurological symptoms were noted.

Individual C1 is an 11‐year‐old European British‐Estonian boy from unrelated parents, with no family history of neuromuscular disease. His pregnancy was uneventful, but a developmental delay was observed from his first year of life, achieving head control at 4 months, turning at 6 months, crawling at 8 months, sitting at 15 months, and walking at 3 years. By 3.5 years, developmental and speech delays were apparent, and physical examination revealed distal weakness and muscle atrophy, particularly in the lower limbs. Electroneurography of the upper limbs showed motor nerve responses with normal latencies, but low amplitudes and mildly slowed conduction velocities; no responses were detected in the lower limbs. Sensory nerve electroneurography showed absent sensory potentials in both upper and lower limbs. Electromyography revealed positive waves and fibrillations, confirming a diagnosis of axonal motor and sensory neuropathy (Table 1).

At the age of 7 years, C1 was diagnosed with moderate intellectual disability (cognitive age of 3 years 4 months to 3 years 11 months, as assessed by the Griffiths III subscale A and the nonverbal index of the Kaufman ABC test). Although he could still walk independently, his gait difficulties progressively worsened. Distal weakness persisted without signs of spasticity.

At the age of 7 years, muscle MRI showed bilateral fat infiltration, primarily affecting the calf muscles compared with the thigh muscles, including the gastrocnemius, soleus, and peroneus longus, with less involvement of the gluteus minimus and vastus lateralis (Figure 1). Muscle biopsy performed at the age of 8 years on the vastus lateralis showed significant fibrosis, fatty infiltration, numerous groups of atrophic muscle fibers, and a uniformity of type 2 fibers, compatible with large fiber type grouping. The atrophic fibers expressed neonatal myosin heavy chain. The fiber diameters ranged from 5 μm to 60 μm. Modified Gömöri trichrome staining revealed cytoplasmic bodies in some fibers. COX/SDH double staining revealed no COX‐negative fibers (Figure S2). Muscle ultrasound showed fasciculations characteristic of neurogenic damage.

At the age of 9 years, he developed epilepsy with myoclonus (both typical and negative) and atypical absence seizures. By the age of 10.5 years, his condition had worsened, with increased weakness and muscle atrophy in both upper and lower limbs. He was able to walk a few steps, but preferred assistance, and his gait was ataxic. Recent brain and spinal cord MRI showed no changes compared with previous scans, except for the thinning of the corpus callosum, which had not worsened (Figure S1). There was no elevation in lactate levels or involvement of the white matter, and no cortical abnormalities were observed.

Segregation analysis confirmed that affected siblings (A2, B2) carried the same compound heterozygous variants, and unaffected parents of all families had single heterozygous variants (Figures 2A and S3).

According to the American College of Medical Genetics guidelines,
29
 p.Ser238Phe and p.Arg336Cys are classified as variants of uncertain significance, p.Ile25Thrfs*38 and p.Pro503Leu are likely pathogenic, and c.492+2T>C is pathogenic (last access July 2025). In silico studies supported the pathogenic or damaging role of these variants (Table 2): p.Ile25Thrfs*38 variant is absent from population databases, while p.Ser238Phe, p.Arg336Cys, p.Pro503Leu and c.492+2T>C have allelic frequencies below 0.001 (gnomAD v4.0.0).
30
 All missense variants affect highly conserved residues of the protein.

Arg336 is positioned at the interface between the catalytic and insertion domains of the protein, where its side chain engages in electrostatic interactions that stabilize the hinge region (Figure 2C). The substitution with cysteine disrupts these interactions, leading to increased flexibility of the insertion domain, as observed in molecular dynamics simulations (Figure S4). The insertion and catalytic domains form a cleft where the acceptor stem of the tRNA binds, directly interacting with the flipping loop (Figure 2C).

Residue Ser238 is adjacent to this flipping loop (Figure 2C), and its substitution with a bulky, hydrophobic phenylalanine is predicted to alter intersubunit interactions with the C‐terminal region of the adjacent subunit, as observed in molecular dynamics simulations (Figure S4). These changes could impact the correct orientation of the tRNA active site.

Pro503 is located in a loop within the active site (Figure 2C). Together with the flipping loop, it contributes to the proper closure of the tRNA‐binding pocket. Its substitution with leucine could alter the loop conformation, preventing its correct positioning and affecting tRNA recognition and binding.

Variant c.492+2T>C alters splicing, as verified through RNAseq analysis (Figure S5), and produces an in‐frame deletion (p.Met134_Lys165del) that alters the connection between the anticodon binding and the catalytic domains of DARS2. RNAseq, which was performed for diagnostic purposes, identified only 1 expression outlier: ENTPD5 (ectonucleoside triphosphate diphosphohydrolase 5; Figure S6). ENTPD5 is involved in nucleotide metabolism and protein N‐glycosylation; however, it was not considered clinically significant, suggesting that this overexpression is likely an incidental finding. Exome sequencing performed in trio in family C revealed no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in other genes that could explain the clinical presentation.

Quantitative analyses were conducted to assess the impact of p.Ser238Phe and p.Arg336Cys variants on DARS2 gene and protein expression levels. We found no differences in mRNA levels of DARS2 between the samples of affected individuals and controls (Figure 3A). Western blot analysis showed a substantial reduction (approximately 32%) in DARS2 protein levels in fibroblasts carrying the variants compared with control cells (control (CT) vs A1, p = 0.0001; CT vs A2, p < 0.0001; Figure 3B). However, it is not possible to determine whether 1 variant is more responsible for this reduction than the other, or if both contribute equally.

To clarify this, we quantified the DARS2 fluorescence intensity specifically within TOMM20‐positive regions—this is, within the mitochondrial network—and calculated a mitochondrial‐to‐cytosol intensity ratio (Figure 5A, lower graph). This ratio was significantly lower in individuals A1 and A2 compared with controls (CT vs A1, p = 0.0001; CT vs A2, p = 0.0012). Importantly, this reduction was primarily driven by a significant decrease in mitochondrial DARS2 signal, while cytoplasmic intensity remained unchanged.

We further explored whether variants had any impact on the overall mitochondrial network morphology. Remarkably, fibroblasts harboring the p.Ser238Phe and p.Arg336Cys variants showed disrupted mitochondrial networks compared with control cells (Figure 5B). On the one hand, the mitochondrial mass in variant‐carrying cells, defined as the percentage of cytosol occupied by mitochondria, was significantly reduced (CT vs A1, p = 0.0013; CT vs A2, p = 0.0330; Figure 5B, left panel). On the other hand, the cells of affected individuals showed a less fragmented appearance compared to the control network (CT vs A1, p = 0.0216; CT vs A2, p = 0.0042; Figure 5B, right panel).

For an in‐depth analysis of mitochondrial function, we measured mitochondrial respiration in live fibroblasts with the Seahorse XF Analyzer (Figure 5C). Under basal conditions, the oxygen consumption rates were similar between control and patient A1 fibroblasts, indicating no significant difference in basal levels of respiration. Similarly, proton leak and ATP‐linked respiration were not affected in patient cells compared with controls, indicating that coupling efficiency and ATP production under resting conditions remained intact. However, the mitochondrial respiration rate of patient A1 fibroblasts showed deficient oxidative phosphorylation activity. We found a decreased maximal respiratory level after carbonyl cyanide‐p‐trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone treatment. Consistent with this, the spare respiratory capacity was also significantly decreased. These results indicate that patient A1's fibroblasts had a reduced ability to respond to bioenergetic stress and that mitochondrial function was impaired.

We describe a novel phenotype of axonal CMT associated with biallelic variants in the DARS2 gene in 3 unrelated families, thereby expanding the phenotypic spectrum of DARS2‐related disorders. Unlike previously reported cases of LBSL, the individuals in our cohort showed a phenotype primarily characterized by progressive distal muscular weakness, atrophy, and areflexia, supported by neurophysiological studies confirming axonal neuropathy. Notably, comprehensive cerebral and spinal MRIs ruled out white matter abnormalities and lactate elevation, distinguishing this phenotype from classical LBSL. In fact, the brain clinical phenotype in individual C1 is not associated with white matter pathology.

Although mild neuropathy has occasionally been reported in individuals with LBSL, it has never been the initial or predominant symptom. Instead, LBSL typically presents in early childhood with leukoencephalopathy, and peripheral neuropathy, when present, is a secondary feature. Given the severity of their disease, individuals affected by LBSL are likely to be examined at a young age, before the peripheral neuropathy has had sufficient time to develop. In contrast, all 5 individuals in our study first showed distal muscular weakness, with electrophysiological studies confirming axonal CMT. Although we cannot completely exclude the possibility of future leukoencephalopathy development, our findings suggest that DARS2 variants can cause a pure axonal neuropathy, representing a distinct clinical presentation.

A key finding in our study is the potential hypomorphic nature of the p.Ser238Phe variant, identified in individuals A1, A2, B1, and B2. This variant is located in the catalytic domain and has not been previously associated with DARS2‐related LBSL. Structural analyses suggest that p.Ser238Phe disrupts several residue contacts, but allows the formation of new interactions, potentially enabling partial retention of enzymatic function. We hypothesize that this variant does not completely abolish DARS2 dimerization or function. In both families A and B, p.Ser238Phe is in trans with more deleterious variants: in family A, it co‐segregates with p.Arg336Cys, which disrupts electrostatic interactions in a hinge region critical for protein stability; in family B, it is paired with a truncating variant (p.Ile25Thrfs*38). Structural studies further indicate that both p.Ser238Phe and p.Arg336Cys may alter tRNA binding due to their location within the catalytic domain and hinge region, respectively.

Very recently, Huang et al explored mitochondrial function in fibroblasts from individuals with LBSL.
45
 Interestingly, these individuals showed more pronounced mitochondrial deficiencies than those observed in individual A1. Although basal mitochondrial activity was impaired and ATP production reduced in fibroblasts of LBSL individuals, proband A1 showed normal basal activity, but decreased maximal respiration, suggesting a limited capacity of the electron transport chain under increased energy demand. These observations raise the possibility that differences in mitochondrial respiratory function may contribute to the clinical variability seen across the DARS2‐related disease spectrum. In this context, our findings further support p.Ser238Phe as a hypomorphic allele.

Similarly, the c.492+2T>C variant, which affects splicing and leads to exon 5 skipping, generating an in‐frame deletion, is expected to impair DARS2‐tRNA interactions. The deleted residues encompass part of the AC‐binding domain and hinge region. This variant has been previously reported in LBSL in combination with the common splice‐site variant in intron 2, but not with p.Pro503Leu. Functional studies demonstrated that p.Pro503Leu reduces DARS2 enzymatic activity by 75%, which aligns with the phenotype observed in individual C1. Although less severe than the classic DARS2‐related LBSL phenotype, C1 presents a more complex clinical picture than the 4 individuals from families A and B, who showed a pure axonal CMT. Unlike them, C1 also showed central nervous system involvement, characterized by intellectual disability and epilepsy, although without leukoencephalopathy. Notably, the initial presentation at 3 years‐of‐age was axonal CMT, highlighting peripheral neuropathy as an early feature.

The specific combinations of variants identified in these families may explain the relatively mild and isolated peripheral nervous system involvement in DARS2‐related CMT compared with classical LBSL. We hypothesize that the presence of a hypomorphic variant, such as p.Ser238Phe, allows for residual enzymatic function, thereby modulating the severity of the phenotype. In contrast, when more damaging variants are present, as in individual C1, neuropathy is accompanied by central nervous system involvement, albeit without leukoencephalopathy. Although none of the affected individuals have developed leukoencephalopathy to date, its future emergence cannot be entirely excluded. This possibility may support the notion that DARS2‐associated diseases lie on a continuum, rather than discrete entities affecting only the central or peripheral nervous system.

Variants in several aminoacyl‐tRNA synthetase genes have previously been associated with isolated peripheral neuropathy. In this context, our finding of disrupted mitochondrial network morphology is particularly relevant.
