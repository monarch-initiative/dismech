Abstract
Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is one of the most frequent cancer 
types and is responsible for the majority of cancer-related deaths 
worldwide. The management of NSCLC has improved considerably, 
especially in the past 10 years. The systematic screening of populations 
at risk with low-dose CT, the implementation of novel surgical and 
radiotherapeutic techniques and a deeper biological understanding 
of NSCLC that has led to innovative systemic treatment options have 
improved the prognosis of patients with NSCLC. In non-metastatic 
NSCLC, the combination of various perioperative strategies and 
adjuvant immunotherapy in locally advanced disease seem to enhance 
cure rates. In metastatic NSCLC, the implementation of novel drugs 
might prolong disease control together with preserving quality of life. 
The further development of predictive clinical and genetic markers will 
be essential for the next steps in individualized treatment concepts.

both sexes, with the incidence and mortality for men decreasing since 
around the 1990s, whereas these decreases only started to occur around 
the 2000s for women8. The reduction in lung cancer mortality is likely 
related to a reduction in incidence along with treatment advances9,10. 
In the European Union (EU), predictions for 2024 report lung cancer 
as the main cause of cancer-related mortality for both sexes. How -
ever, lung cancer mortality among men has decreased over the past 
two decades, in contrast to the increased mortality among European 
women. This difference is mainly due to earlier smoking cessation in 
men than in women11.
Furthermore, epidemiological data suggest the existence of 
sex-based disparities. Women with lung cancer are younger, have fewer 
comorbidities and are more likely to be never-smokers than men 12. 
These data emphasize the importance of considering these differences 
in future clinical trials and public health policies.
The median age of patients with lung cancer is 71 years, with fewer 
than 10% of cases diagnosed in individuals younger than 55 years. 
Additionally, there is a higher prevalence of male patients with lung 
cancer13. Currently, approximately half of the patients are diagnosed 
at an advanced stage, in part owing to nonspecific early symptoms and 
the currently low implementation of low-dose CT (LD-CT) screening 
programmes worldwide8.
NSCLC accounts for the majority of lung cancer cases annually, and 
adenocarcinoma (50% of cases) and squamous cell carcinoma (20–30% 
of cases) are the most prevalent subtypes of NSCLC14,15. Of note, the his-
tological subtype often correlates with the patient’s smoking history, 
and both factors (histology and smoking pattern) are associated with 
the incidence of druggable genomic alterations (usually more com -
mon in adenocarcinoma and in patients with a never or light smoking 
history)14,16 (Fig. 2).
Introduction
Lung cancer has traditionally been classified into two primary histo-
logical subtypes: small-cell lung cancer and non-small-cell lung cancer 
(NSCLC). NSCLC is the more common subtype, accounting for ~85% of 
lung cancers1. NSCLC is classified into three main histological subtypes 
distinguished by appearance on histology — adenocarcinoma (originat-
ing from the mucus gland cells), squamous cell carcinoma (originating 
from the airway squamous epithelium) and large cell carcinoma (a het-
erogeneous group of undifferentiated epithelium-derived tumours), 
although rare cases of adenosquamous (a mixture of squamous and 
gland-like cells) and sarcomatoid (a mixture of carcinoma and sarcoma 
cells) tumours also occur. T obacco smoking remains the most common 
risk factor for lung cancer worldwide. Despite the decreases in smoking 
prevalence over the past two decades, in 2020 more than 20% of the 
global population were smokers, with a prevalence of 15.4% among 
the younger population (15–24 years of age)2. However, the global inci-
dence of lung cancer in non-smokers is on the rise, underscoring the 
need for further research in this area. Exposure to carcinogens from 
tobacco use and from other sources results in genomic alterations, 
which drive unregulated growth and malignancy.
In the past two decades, the field of thoracic oncology has pro -
gressed remarkably, particularly in the management of NSCLC. This 
progress spans several aspects, from lung cancer screening to the 
introduction of innovative therapeutic options, including immune 
checkpoint blockers (ICBs) and personalized targeted therapies for 
oncogene-addicted NSCLC (the presence of a single driver muta -
tion necessary for tumour survival). Notably, both types of systemic 
treatment strategy are now transitioning from use for metastatic 
NSCLC to earlier stages of the disease, positively affecting patient 
outcomes across all stages of the disease. Moreover, in the context of 
oncogene-addicted NSCLC, growing understanding of the underly -
ing mechanisms of acquired resistance to targeted treatments has 
led to the development of new drugs designed to overcome acquired 
resistance, thereby expanding the scope for further lines of treatment 
for selected patients. Local treatment strategies have also improved, 
with refined surgical techniques contributing to a reduction in mor-
bidity after surgery3. Similarly, advances in radiotherapy techniques 
have resulted in higher conformality of radiation fields targeting 
tumours, thereby reducing the risk of severe toxicities 4. This pro -
gress has enabled the use of local ablative treatment, particularly in 
patients with advanced NSCLC with oligometastatic disease. However, 
it is important to note that, globally, not all patients with lung cancer 
enjoy these benefits, as inequalities exist in the approach to NSCLC 
treatment, which contribute to considerable geographical variation 
in disease burden5.
In this Primer, we review the epidemiology, pathophysiology, 
diagnosis and screening of NSCLC and provide an overview of current 
therapeutic approaches for patients with early, locally advanced or 
metastatic NSCLC, with an emphasis on the role of predictive biomark-
ers in guiding treatment decisions. Finally, we discuss quality of life 
(QOL) of patients with NSCLC, as well as current knowledge gaps and 
potential future treatment strategies.

Risk factors
T obacco smoking remains the most important risk factor for lung can-
cer, with a relative risk of lung cancer 10–30 times higher (depending on 
daily smoked amount and pack-years) compared with never-smokers17. 
The global prevalence of tobacco use has declined from 32.7% in 2000 to 
22.3% in 2020. The largest drop in tobacco use occurred in lower-middle 
income groups (from 44% in 2000 to 24% in 2020), whereas the smallest 
and slowest drop was in the upper-middle income groups (from 28% 
in 2000 to 24% in 2020)2. Thus, stronger anti-tobacco policies are still 
necessary, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Smoking 
cessation is highly effective in reducing lung cancer incidence and 
lung cancer-related mortality17–19. In addition, the potential impact 
of e-cigarette use, which is increasing among younger people (under 
25 years of age), on the risk of lung cancer remains unknown20. Beyond 
tobacco, cannabis is the most widely used inhaled product globally, 
and its consumption is often under-reported. This is a concern, as the 
co-consumption of cannabis and tobacco may lead to more severe 
forms of lung cancer21.
Of note, up to 25% of all NSCLC cases globally occur in lifelong 
never-smokers, a population predominantly composed of women 
across all age groups, races and ethnicities 22. Several risk factors 
have been proposed, such as outdoor air pollution 23,24; occupa -
tional exposure (for example, asbestos and arsenic) 25; household 
air pollution26; radon, considered one of the leading causes of lung 
cancer in never-smokers 27; obstructive lung diseases, pulmonary 
fibrosis or pulmonary infections (such as tuberculosis)25; pathogenic 
germline variants (in TP53, ATM, CHEK2, EGFRT790M for example) in some 
individuals28,29; and genetic ancestry30. Furthermore, the incidence of 
lung cancer in non-smokers is increasing globally, particularly among 
women and younger age groups. Indeed, lung cancer in never-smokers 
is estimated to be the fifth most common cause of cancer-related 
deaths worldwide in 2023, preferentially occurring in women and Asian 
populations31. This stresses the need for more research in this area (on 
aspects such as tumour characteristics and risk factors related to this 
disease) as well as the role of lung cancer screening in this population31.
Mechanisms/pathophysiology
Malignant neoplasms are characterized by invasion into their surround-
ings, metastasis to other locations, sustained angiogenesis and evasion 
of the immune system. Inherent in these characteristics is uncontrolled 
cell proliferation, which is independent of normal tissue homeostasis 
and is unbalanced by the capacity to induce cell loss (cell death by apop-
tosis and necrosis)31–34. A fundamental characteristic of malignancy is 
alteration of the normal genome and its expression, leading to altered 
cellular morphology and abnormal cell behaviour through altered 
protein expression35,36.

in never-smokers, and the most common druggable genomic alterations are 
mutations in KRAS and BRAF. Finally, squamous cell tumours are genetically

Squamous cell carcinomas mostly arise in the central bronchial epi-

dysplasia, then squamous cell carcinoma in situ, leading to invasive 
squamous cell carcinoma37. Several carcinogens may induce these 
processes38. Only a very small proportion of progenitor lesions progress 
to invasion and, consequently, a lung with a single invasive carcinoma 
may show broader regions containing premalignant lesions, referred to 
as field change39. Once invasive, malignant tumours continue to evolve 
through further genomic alterations, driving subclonal development 
and, usually, more aggressive disease40–42 (Fig. 2). Studies, especially in 
the past decade, have begun to describe the genomic alterations associ-
ated with the evolution and progression of lung cancers, including the 
interaction between precursor lesions and the immune system40–42.
The spatial and temporal progression of lesions, with increas -
ing nuclear and cytological aberration, is driven by accumulation of 
genomic alterations. Clinically apparent invasive lung cancers vary 
greatly in the amount of genomic change, referred to as the tumour 
mutational burden (TMB), which ranges from fewer than 10 to almost 
1,000 broadly defined mutations per megabase of exonic DNA43. Dupli-
cation (polysomy, gene amplification) or reordering (chromothripsis, 
rearrangements and/or inversions) of genomic material is frequent. 
Parts of chromosomes can also be deleted, leading to loss of heterozy-
gosity (LOH) for some genes. Epigenetic changes, including changes 
to gene methylation, microRNA levels, regulation of wild-type genes 
and post-translational protein modifications, also influence gene 
expression44. These mechanisms can result in major disruption and 
malfunction of cell signalling pathways. It has been suggested that for 
a malignant cell clone to evolve, 3–12 crucial alterations must occur, 
in sequence, in the same cell population, which is a statistically highly 
unlikely event45. However, given the potential for millions of genomic 
events in billions of epithelial cells45–47, this is possible.
Some of the earliest changes found in pre-invasive disease involve 
inactivation of tumour suppressor genes (TSGs) through LOH, gene 
methylation or mutation. Genomic analysis of precursor lesions and 
invasive tumours that are assumed to have arisen from such lesions indi-
cate that alterations that drive the neoplastic clone, and mechanisms 
that facilitate immune escape, occur quite early in this process 48–52. 
Activating mutations in oncogenic driver genes such as EGFR, KRAS, 
BRAF and ERBB2 have also been described in atypical adenomatous 
hyperplasia and adenocarcinoma in situ51,53–56.
In most lung cancers, especially in those related to tobacco use, 
genomic complexity is high, and several genomic alterations contribute 
to tumour growth — a phenomenon known as oncogene expedience57. 
These lung cancers frequently show spatial heterogeneity owing to 
clonal evolution, with mutations (termed branch mutations) found in 
some parts of the tumour but not others. By contrast, in some adeno-
carcinomas, genomic diversity is low and a single activating genomic 
alteration referred to as a driver oncogene is present in all tumour cells 
and is essential for tumour cell survival (termed oncogene addiction). 
This is a considerable oversimplification, as there is still variation in 
genomic complexity in oncogene-addicted lung cancer, and this varia-
tion probably influences sensitivity to targeted therapy58,59 (see below). 
Genomic studies show differences in tumour molecular landscape 
according to smoking history58,60. These data might help to elucidate 
more about the molecular background of lung carcinogenesis, includ-
ing differences related to the putative causative carcinogen; detailed 
discussion is beyond the scope of this Primer.
Therapeutically important genomic alterations
NSCLC can harbour many oncogenic alterations, which can affect 
oncogenes and TSGs16,61,62. Most therapeutically relevant oncogenic 
drivers are kinases activated by mutation, including receptor tyrosine 
kinases (RTKs) such as EGFR, ERBB2 (also known as HER2) and MET; 
or by forming fusion proteins in which the kinase domain is consti-
tutively activated, such as fusions involving ALK, RET, ROS1, NRG1, 
NTRK1, NTRK2 or NTRK3. Most of these driver mutations and fusions 
are not associated with tobacco carcinogens; for example, promotion 
of growth of lung epithelial cells harbouring EGFR mutations might be 
related to air pollution24. Oncogene addiction in NSCLC has prompted 
the development of targeted therapies, predominantly tyrosine kinase 
inhibitors (TKIs), which are typically small molecules that bind to the 
ATP-binding pocket of the active kinase domain. Other oncogenic 
alterations (for example, KRAS and BRAF mutations) activate signal-
ling pathways downstream of the membrane-bound RTKs (for example, 
RAS–RAF–MEK or PI3K–AKT–TSC1/TSC2–mTOR) and are more com-
mon than the above-mentioned oncogenic drivers in patients with a 
smoking history63. Regardless of the smoking pattern, these oncogenic 
alterations most commonly occur in adenocarcinoma and are rarely 
observed in squamous cell carcinoma64.
TSGs that are commonly altered in lung cancer include TP53, RB1, 
STK11, KEAP1, ATM, CDKN2A and CTNNB1 (refs. 16,61,62). In contrast to 
driver oncogenes, TSGs have not typically served as direct therapeu-
tic targets. However, they might be crucial in regulating aspects of 
the malignant phenotype, including proliferation, error-prone DNA 
replication, altered cell metabolism, evasion of immune surveillance 
and the ability to withstand oxidative stresses 63. In KRAS-mutated 
tumours, the occurrence of co-mutations (mutations in two or more 
genes in the same cell at the same time) in STK11 and KEAP1 (refs. 65,66) 
or a baseline mutation in the TSG SMARCA4 can reduce the efficacy of 
ICBs67. Similarly, co-mutations in TP53 reduce the effectiveness of TKIs 
in tumours with KRAS or EGFR mutations or with oncogenic fusions68–70.
Resistance to drugs targeting oncogenic drivers
Although the introduction of TKIs has changed the treatment landscape 
and prognosis of oncogene-addicted NSCLC, most tumours become 
resistant to these drugs. Resistance is acquired through three major 
mechanisms. First, acquired on-target mutations result in alterations 
in the driver oncogene, such as changes in the kinase domain that block 
binding of a TKI. For example, resistance to first- and second-generation 
EGFR-targeting TKIs is often due to acquisition of the EGFRT790M muta-
tion; tumours treated with third-generation EGFR TKIs, which also 
target EGFRT790M, develop other resistance mutations such as EGFRC797S 
(ref. 71). On-target mutations have been described for every NSCLC 
driver oncogene for which targeted therapies are available. Second, 
in bypass track resistance, the selective pressure of drug-induced 
signalling pathway blockade results in tumour cell clones adopting 
alternative signalling pathways to grow. Many bypass tracks exist; for 
example, in EGFR-positive NSCLC that progresses on EGFR-targeting 
TKIs, amplification of MET and upregulation of HER3 can occur and can 
be used as treatment targets72. Third, in histological transformation, 
the initial adenocarcinoma undergoes a lineage shift (that is, a change 
in cellular differentiation) that is accompanied by loss of addiction to an 
oncogenic driver; for example, the resistant tumour might transform 
to small-cell, squamous or sarcomatoid histology, or might undergo 
epithelial–mesenchymal transition73–75. Transformation to a small-cell 
histology is more common in tumours harbouring baseline TP53 and 
RB1 co-mutations76. Of note, more than one resistance mechanism can

Factors involved in response to immunotherapy
The fact that a neoplastic cell clone acquires many mutations, some 
possibly encoding neo-antigens, yet becomes a clinically apparent 
tumour, alludes to mechanisms that allow a nascent tumour cell clone 
to avoid elimination by a competent immune system — these are the 
concepts of immune surveillance and immune escape as they affect 
tumour evolution 78 (see above). One such important mechanism  
is tumour cell adoption of inhibitory immune checkpoints, whereby 
receptor–ligand interactions between tumour cells and immune cells 
(and between different immune cell types) effectively switch off an 
otherwise available antitumour immune response 79,80. Other factors 
may also negate a potential immune response to the tumour, includ-
ing, among others, molecular (cytokines and monokines), cellular 
(inhibitory T regulatory cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells) 
and metabolic (for example, enhanced lactate secretion or deple -
tion of key amino acids) elements in the tumour microenvironment 
(TME)81,82.
The most frequently used immunotherapies in NSCLC target pro-
grammed cell death 1 (PD1) or its ligand PDL1; an important immune 
checkpoint. The amount of PDL1 expressed on tumour cell membranes 
is positively correlated with the probability of drug response, and PDL1 
expression is used as a selective biomarker for many indications 82. 
High TMB may predict greater (neo)antigenicity and seems to predict 
therapy response but not patient survival benefit from ICBs82. Finally, 
the presence of a tumour-directed immune response — an inflamed 
tumour — might predict sensitivity to ICBs82.
Tumour genomic alterations also influence the response to 
immuno therapy. Patients with adenocarcinomas and driver oncogenes,  
such as EGFR mutations or ALK or RET fusions, typically have a poor 
response to ICBs83–85, due in part to their low TMB and low PDL1 expres-
sion, although the precise mechanisms remain unclear86. It is likely that 
predictive biomarkers need to be multifactorial to capture the complex 
interplay between tumour-intrinsic characteristics and the TME.
Diagnosis, screening and prevention
Diagnosis
There are no specific symptoms that are pathognomic for lung cancer, 
but further investigations should be performed in patients with cough-
ing that does not go away or worsens over time, haemoptysis, weight 
loss or dyspnoea (shortness of breath). For each patient with a suspicion 
of lung cancer, a complete medical history with comorbidities, weight 
loss, performance status and physical examination must be recorded, 
and laboratory tests must be performed (Box 1). The minimum require-
ment for imaging is a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest and upper 
abdomen (including liver, kidneys and adrenal glands). For all patients 
with NSCLC who are potentially eligible for curative-intent therapy,  
a 2-fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose (FDG)-PET scan should be performed to 
detect occult metastases not observed with the CT scan, as FDG-PET 
is superior to CT scan in detecting metastases87. Brain imaging, prefer-
ably by MRI, should be performed in most patients, as the incidence of 
brain metastases is high and this diagnosis can change the treatment 
plan, although the specific recommendations vary across countries 
and guidelines87–91. Other imaging modalities are recommended only 
if the results obtained influence the treatment strategy. Current stag-
ing is performed according to the 8th edition of the American Joint 
Committee on Cancer (AJCC)–Union for International Cancer Control 
(UICC) cancer staging system92–94.
The TNM classification is composed of T (representing tumour 
size or organ involvement by the primary tumour), N (representing 
the regional lymph nodes affected by the disease) and M (metastases). 
Based on these components, NSCLC can be classified into four differ-
ent stages, from I (very early disease) to IV (metastatic), which have 
prognostic value and are relevant in treatment decisions94.
In all patients, tumour tissue should be obtained for patho -
logical confirmation of the diagnosis, histological subtyping of the 
lung cancer and tests to evaluate potential predictive biomarkers 
(for example PDL1 expression and genomic analysis) 87,95–98. DNA and 
RNA derived from tumour tissue is the standard source for genomic

Screening
Obtaining a diagnosis at an earlier stage of lung cancer is an impor -
tant approach to reduce lung cancer mortality. Several single-arm 
and rand omized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating lung cancer 
screening have been performed103 (Table 2). All trials included patients 
at high risk of lung cancer (that is, smokers with ≥20 pack-years and  
age ≥45 years), although specific eligibility criteria were different across 
trials. Furthermore, although all trials incorporated LD-CT in their 
interventional arm, the frequency and timing of CT scans varied, and 
the comparator arm consisted of either no screening or chest radio-
graphy. The two largest trials, NLST and NELSON, showed a significant 
reduction in lung-cancer-related mortality (relative reduction 20%  
(95% confidence interval (CI) 6.8–26.7; P = 0.004) for NLST and cumula-
tive rate ratio at 10 years 0.76 (95% CI 0.61–0.94; P = 0.01) for NELSON), 
but only NLST showed a decrease in all-cause mortality (6.7% reduc-
tion (95% CI 1.2–13.6; P = 0.02))104,105. Furthermore, in the NELSON trial, 
the data indicated sex differences in mortality, with a cumulative rate 
ratio for lung cancer mortality at 10 years of 0.76 for men and 0.67 for 
women104. However, as only a small subset of the included patients 
were female and the study was not powered to evaluate sex-specific 
differences, more research is needed to clarify these potential dif -
ferences. A meta-analysis, with 94,921 participants from nine RCTs, 
showed that low-dose CT screening resulted in a 16% relative reduction 
in lung-cancer-related mortality and 3% relative reduction in all-cause 
mortality106. Of note, this decrease in mortality is caused by a decrease in 
NSCLC-related mortality, as screening does not improve small-cell lung 
cancer-related mortality107. Active lung cancer screening programmes 
are not in place in most countries, with the USA, Japan, South Korea, 
Croatia, Canada and the UK having active programmes, although pilot 
projects or clinical trials are ongoing in several countries108,109.
Among the non-smoker population, the TALENT study conducted 
in Taiwan reported a lung cancer detection rate of 2.6% in a lung cancer 
screening programme110, and the ongoing FANSS study assesses LD-CT 
screening in Asian female non-smokers (NCT05164757). However, the 
role of screening in never-smokers is still controversial16,111.
The challenges to optimize lung cancer screening are to identify 
and reach the highest-risk population, as well as to reduce the stress and  
potential harm for the patient and the cost and burden for the 
health-care system. Although age and smoking history are included 
in almost all risk models 112, the risk factors for lung cancer may vary 
between geographical areas, especially those with high incidence of 
lung cancer in never-smokers, suggesting that more refined models 
need to be developed 113. Ongoing screening trials take into account 
risk-based criteria and aim to refine them, for example, the 4-IN THE 
LUNG RUN trial. Furthermore, research is necessary to optimize screen-
ing in lung cancer survivors, as the risk of a second primary lung cancer 
is 8.4% at 10 years114.
Another challenge is to reduce the risk of unnecessary inva -
sive interventions for false-positive findings or indolent cancers in 
LD-CT screening; thus, non-invasive screening techniques need to be 
improved. Initial data suggest that deep-learning-based algorithms can 
detect lung nodules on CT scans and classify them as malignant versus 
benign115–117. Furthermore, several studies have evaluated blood or 
exhaled breath-based biomarkers118–134. If refined and further validated, 
these biomarkers can potentially be used either to select individuals 
for LD-CT screening or to further aid in stratifying nodules as high risk 
or low risk. Moreover, evidence suggests that a family history of lung 
cancer predisposes some people to lung cancer, especially in multiplex 
families (families in which multiple individuals are affected by a specific 
disease) and never-smokers135. These risk factors need to be taken into 
account in future research.

Prevention
The major prevention strategy to reduce NSCLC prevalence is the 
banning of tobacco. Implementing tobacco control policies such 
as nicotine-replacement therapies, smoking-related cancer aware-
ness campaigns and a nationwide ban on tobacco use in public places 
can help to decrease the prevalence of smoking and as a result lung 
cancer137,138. Use of plain packaging and graphic pictorial warnings 
on nicotine products decrease the attractiveness of these products. 
Increasing the price of tobacco products can also decrease their use137,138. 
In addition to smoking cessation, starting a screening programme can 
help to reduce long-term cancer-related mortality5. However, as other 
factors such as air pollution are also associated with a risk of developing 
NSCLC24, definition and application of strict international policies to 
reduce air pollution worldwide are urgently needed.
Management
Resectable early-stage NSCLC
Surgery remains the cornerstone treatment for patients with early-stage 
NSCLC. For those who are not eligible for surgery, for example, owing 
to compromised pulmonary function, radiotherapy is generally the 
local treatment of choice90. Radical surgery with lymph node dissec-
tion, aiming for a complete resection, is essential to ensure the best 
possible prognosis139 (Fig. 3).
Surgical strategies.  In the 1990s, video-assisted thoracoscopic 
surgery (VATS) for lung resections was introduced as a less-invasive 
approach than traditional open thoracotomy 140. In two RCTs, VATS 
resulted in improved outcomes with less postoperative pain, better 
QOL and greater self-reported physical functioning than for open 
thoracotomies 141,142. Based on these results, VATS is the preferred 
surgical approach for lobectomy in stage I NSCLC.
In 2000, robot-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) was intro-
duced to overcome the technical limitations of VATS, showing clear 
technical advantages including better dexterity, tremor filtration and 
3D view143,144.
A RCT published in 2021 that compared RATS and VATS 
lobectomies145 showed similar perioperative outcomes but a signifi -
cantly higher lymph node yield by RATS (median number of lymph 
nodes sampled six versus four with VATS; P = 0.0002). These results 
were confirmed by a larger trial, concluding that both RATS and VATS 
are safe and feasible in treating NSCLC146.
Nodal staging is crucial for better stage-specific survival, and it 
improves prognosis, including when lung-sparing approaches are used. 
A study comparing limited lobar resection (anatomical segmentectomy 
(removal of a specific lung segment, for example, lateral segment of 
a lower lobe)) or wedge resection (removal of the tumour and some 
normal lung tissue) with complete lobectomy found overall worse 
oncological outcomes for the limited approach but better survival 
using the limited approach when the two trial groups were matched 
by the number of lymph nodes removed147.
Resection in T1N0 disease.  Since 1991, complete lobectomy has 
been the gold-standard treatment for stage I NSCLC148, and anatomi-
cal segmentectomy was indicated in patients with compromised lung 
function, old age, cardiac comorbidities or pure ground glass opacity 
(GGO) tumours <2 cm. Two RCTs demonstrated similar outcomes after 
limited lobar resection (one trial evaluating segmentectomy only149, the 
other wedge resection or segmentectomy150) and lobectomy for NSCLC 
tumours ≤2 cm149,150. In one of these RCTs149, segmentectomy resulted 
in better overall survival than complete lobectomy, owing to reduced 
mortality for other causes, despite an increased rate of local recurrence.
Postoperative radiotherapy. The role of postoperative radiotherapy 
(PORT) has been controversial for years. Two phase III RCTs enrolled 
patients with completely resected pathological stage IIIA (N2) NSCLC 
who had or had not received neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy.  
In both the LungART and PORT-C trials, PORT did not improve 
disease-free survival (DFS) 151,152. In LungART, 3-year DFS was 47%  
(95% CI 40–50%) in patients receiving PORT compared with 44% (95%  
CI 37–51%) in those without PORT (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.68–1.08; 
P = 0·18)151. Similar progression-free survival (PFS) results have been 
reported in PORT-C152. Of note, these studies were performed before 
the introduction of ICBs and TKIs in the management of early-stage 
NSCLC, so whether similar results would be obtained with current treat-
ment strategies is unknown. Indeed, these trials were conducted with 
radiotherapy techniques that would not be considered the standard 
of care (SOC) nowadays.
Systemic therapies. The recommendation regarding the use of post-
operative (adjuvant) chemotherapy is based on a large pooled analysis 
of five RCTs, and this approach is recommended for resected NSCLC 
with tumour size ≥4 cm or lymph node involvement, provided there 
are no medical contraindications 153. The analyses demonstrated a 
5.4% improvement in 5-year overall survival with adjuvant chemo -
therapy compared with no adjuvant treatment (HR of death 0.89, 95% 
CI 0.82–0.96; P = 0.005).
Systemic treatment compliance is higher when chemotherapy 
is given as an induction (neoadjuvant) treatment than as an adjuvant 
therapy. Importantly, neoadjuvant therapy enables evaluation of path-
ological complete response (pCR) after surgery, which is a relevant 
surrogate outcome for patient survival154.
In patients with completely resected early-stage NSCLC, two rand-
omized phase III clinical trials have established the role of adjuvant  
ICBs. In the IMpower010 trial155, adjuvant atezolizumab for 1 year after 
adjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved DFS compared with 
best supportive care (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.88; P = 0.039) in patients 
with completely resected stage II–IIIA NSCLC and tumour PDL1 expres-
sion >1%. The benefit was mainly driven by tumours with high PDL1 
expression (PDL1 ≥50%) 155,156. On the basis of these results, the FDA 
approved adjuvant atezolizumab in PDL1 ≥1% stage II–IIIA NSCLC, 
whereas the EMA restricted the approval to tumours with high PDL1 
expression (PDL1 ≥ 50%). In the PEARLS trial (KEYNOTE-091), 1 year 
of adjuvant pembrolizumab significantly improved DFS compared 
with placebo (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.63–0.91; P = 0.0014), in patients with 
completely resected stage IB–IIIA NSCLC treated with or without adju-
vant chemotherapy 157. Surprisingly, adjuvant pembrolizumab did 
not improve DFS in PDL1 ≥50% tumours 157. T o date, adjuvant pem-
brolizumab is approved by the FDA and the EMA regardless of PDL1 
expression, but the EMA approval requires that patients have received 
previous treatment with adjuvant chemotherapy, as the study’s sub-
group analysis indicated that those who did not receive adjuvant 
 chemotherapy did not benefit from adjuvant pembrolizumab.

several phase III RCTs were launched to assess this strategy in patients

with PDL1 ≥1% NSCLC. T o date, in contrast to CheckMate 816, all other

incorporated an adjuvant ICB component (that is, a perioperative 
strategy)161,162,164,165. The pCR percentages in the CT–ICB arms in these 
four trials are similar to those reported in CheckMate 816 (~20%) and, 
although cross-trial comparisons are difficult, HRs for event-free sur-
vival are also similar (reducing the risk of progression in up to 40% 
of patients). An overall survival benefit with perioperative ICB was 
shown for the first time in the phase III RCT KEYNOTE-671, which com-
pared induction pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy with induction 
chemotherapy plus placebo, with subsequent surgery and adjuvant 
pembrolizumab or placebo for 1 year (overall survival HR 0.72, 95% 
CI 0.56–0.93; P = 0.00517), leading to both FDA and EMA approval165.  
In this perioperative strategy, durvalumab has also been approved (in 
2024) based on the results of the AEGEAN trial by the FDA164. For other 
phase III RCTs testing perioperative ICB, overall survival data are not 
yet available.
The development of predictive biomarkers to identify patients 
who benefit from adjuvant ICBs after neoadjuvant treatment is of 
paramount importance to avoid unnecessary patient-related toxicity 
as well as financial toxicity. The assessment of minimal residual disease 
(MRD) status by postoperative circulating tumour DNA evolves as one 
promising marker, which correlates with the risk of recurrence164–167. 
The negative predictive value of the currently used tests is suboptimal, 
and more sensitive tests are needed before MRD can be used in daily 
clinical practice (reviewed elsewhere 168). In addition, although the 
prognostic role of MRD is accepted, the potential predictive role of 
MRD status remains unknown and dedicated trials are needed.
Based on these data, all cases of early-stage NSCLC should be dis-
cussed in a multidisciplinary team to define the best type of treatment 
as well as the treatment sequence. In addition, testing for oncogenic 
drivers and PDL1 should become SOC in early-stage NSCLC (Table 3). Of 
note, the definition of resectable versus unresectable stage III has not 
always been clearly documented in clinical trials (although all current 
clinical trials with results reported mandated up-front resectability) 
and is not consistent across centres169. Therefore, the European Organi-
sation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) has provided 
a consensus definition to reduce discrepancies across centres and 
provide a uniform definition applicable in clinical trials170.
Similarly, the International Association for the Study of Lung 
Cancer (IASLC) issued a consensus recommendation about the perio-
perative strategy with ICB, with the aim of providing practical guid -
ance for the patient’s journey based on the latest clinical evidence171. 
Finally, an important challenge is the role of up-front neoadjuvant 
chemo-immunotherapy in unresectable tumours and whether neoad-
juvant chemo-immunotherapy may overcome these tumours to resect-
able. This concept is currently being evaluated in the MDT-BRIDGE 
clinical trial (NCT05925530)172.

The standard radiotherapy dose-fractionation of 60 Gy in 2 Gy 
fractions was established more than three decades ago 175. Neither a 
higher dose of radiotherapy nor the addition of cetuximab improved 
survival in a seminal phase III RCT176. Importantly, the study highlighted 
the contribution of the radiation dose to the heart to poor survival177.
Platinum-based doublet chemotherapy is delivered concurrently 
with radiotherapy and, to date, no specific regimen has demonstrated 
superiority178. Furthermore, the addition of induction or consolidation 
chemotherapy to concurrent chemoradiotherapy does not improve 
survival179,180, and survival rates remain poor, with only 15–30% survival 
5 years after concurrent chemoradiotherapy.
The addition of 12 months of consolidation treatment with dur -
valumab after concurrent chemoradiotherapy has changed the treat-
ment landscape174. The long-term results of the PACIFIC trial show 42.9% 
survival at 5 years in the durvalumab group compared with 33.4% in 
the placebo group181. The benefit for durvalumab was consistent in all 
subgroups analysed, with the exception of patients with tumours with 
PDL1 <1% or with EGFR mutations, revealed in a post hoc analysis. This 
result led to the controversial decision from the EMA to only license 
the drug for patients with tumours with PDL1 ≥1% NSCLC in Europe182. 
In the LAURA trial, lifetime osimertinib after chemoradiotherapy in 
unresectable EGFR-mutated NSCLC resulted in improved DFS com -
pared with placebo (HR 0.16, 95% CI 0.10–0.24; P < 0.001). However, 
it is too soon to obtain overall survival data183. Osimertinib has not yet 
been approved by regulatory agencies for this indication but has been 
granted priority review by the FDA.
Many patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC are not suitable 
for concurrent chemoradiotherapy owing to poor performance status 
and comorbidities184. For these patients, sequential chemoradiother-
apy or radiotherapy alone is considered, but local control and survival 
rates are inferior compared with concurrent chemoradiotherapy 173. 
The safety profile of ICB is similar in patients receiving sequential 
chemoradiotherapy to that of patients receiving concurrent chemora-
diotherapy, despite patients receiving sequential chemoradiotherapy 
being frailer and older, with the caveat that most of these patients still 
had a WHO (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG)) perfor -
mance status of 0 (asymptomatic; normal activity) or 1 (symptomatic 
but ambulatory) and that data for performance status 2 (symptomatic;  
bed-ridden <50% of the day) are largely lacking185,186. A single-arm phase II  
trial (NEJ039A) showed that daily low-dose carboplatin concurrent  
with radiotherapy followed by durvalumab is feasible in older patients 
(in this trial older was defined as ≥75 years) and/or patients with perfor-
mance status 2, as is durvalumab after radical radiotherapy in patients 
unfit for chemotherapy187,188. In addition, the optimal sequencing of 
immunotherapy and combination with chemoradiotherapy in unre -
sectable locally advanced NSCLC remains to be defined, as does the 
optimal duration of immunotherapy treatment. Building on the results 
of PACIFIC, several ongoing studies are evaluating various ICBs alone 
or in combination with immune modulators (such as TLR and CD40 
agonist antibodies) in addition to concurrent chemoradiotherapy.
Early use of a combination of ICBs together with concurrent 
chemoradiotherapy is another strategy to optimize the immunosen-
sitizing effect of radiotherapy. Encouraging efficacy results have been 
observed for this approach in exploratory phase II trials such as KEY-
NOTE-799 or Nicolas189,190; however, the increased risk of pulmonary  
toxic effects needs to be monitored carefully. Moreover, the first  
phase III RCT evaluating this concept, PACIFIC-2, failed to show benefit in 
either PFS (primary end point) or overall survival (secondary end point)  
when evaluating durvalumab or placebo concurrent with chemo -
radiotherapy followed by maintenance durvalumab or placebo 191. 
Results from several other phase III trials are awaited. Finally, there 
is an unmet need to identify which patients are most likely to benefit 
from chemoradiotherapy (either concurrent or sequential) and ICB 
combinations. Similar to the perioperative setting, the presence of 
MRD following curative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy assessed 
by circulating tumour DNA is associated with worse outcomes 192–194. 
However, most of the published studies are from single institutions, 
and larger prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings. 
Important open questions remain regarding the use of immunotherapy 
combined with radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy and associated 
clinical trials (Supplementary Table 2).
Metastatic NSCLC
Oncogene-driven NSCLC.  The discovery of targetable oncogenic 
drivers heralded a major paradigm shift in treatment and diagnosis 
of advanced NSCLC. Up-front targeted treatment in oncogene-driven 
NSCLC substantially improves patient outcome 195 (Fig.  3d), which 
was first reported in patients whose tumours harboured sensitizing 
EGFR mutations. Based on several phase III RCTs in which a significant 
improvement in response rate and PFS196 was found for monotherapy 
with EGFR TKI compared with chemotherapy, EGFR TKI monother -
apy became the preferred first-line treatment approach. Since then, 
third-generation TKIs, such as osimertinib, with improved efficacy 
compared with earlier-generation EGFR TKIs, have become standard of 
care197. Combination strategies involving a third-generation EGFR TKI 
(osimertinib or lazertinib) with, respectively, chemotherapy or ami -
vantamab (a bi-specific monoclonal antibody to EGFR and MET)198,199 
have been compared with third-generation EGFR TKI monotherapy. The 
goal of adding chemotherapy to EGFR TKI is to eliminate tumour clones 
with cell cycle abnormalities (for example, those with co-mutations 
detected) and other clones refractory to EGFR TKIs. Conversely, the 
combination with amivantamab aims to prevent the occurrence of MET 
amplification, which is a mechanism of acquired resistance. Both com-
bination treatment strategies resulted in a significant PFS improvement 
compared with third-generation EGFR TKI monotherapy (FLAURA2: 
HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.49–0.79; MARIPOSA: HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58–0.85), 
with outcomes for overall survival still pending198,199. However, these 
combination treatments have an increased risk of toxic effects 195,196. 
Future research should identify those patients who benefit most from 
combination strategies.
In other druggable genomic alterations, such as ALK fusions or 
RET fusions, TKIs also demonstrate improved outcomes compared 
with platinum-based chemotherapy196,200. The phase III LIBRETTO-431  
trial strongly supports up-front treatment with selpercatinib (a RET TKI)  
versus SOC, with a superiority in PFS (24.8 versus 11.2 months; HR 0.46,  
95% CI 0.31–0.70; P  < 0.001)200. Of note, similar to EGFR -mutated 
tumours, in NSCLC with ALK fusions, up-front treatment with a second- 
generation ALK TKI (for example, alectinib, brigatinib or ensartinib196) 
and especially a third-generation ALK TKI (for example, lorlatinib201,202) 
resulted in improved response rates and PFS, as well as central nerv -
ous system protection, compared with the first-generation ALK TKI 
crizotinib. Lorlatinib is also effective in patients whose disease has 
progressed following treatment with a second-generation ALK TKI203.
For other genomic alterations, such as BRAF V600E, mutations 
resulting in MET exon 14 skipping, or ROS1 fusions and NTRK fusions, 
TKIs have not been directly compared with first-line chemotherapy.

improvement in overall survival for sotorasib; overall survival has not

Other targeted approaches also have a role in the treatment of 
metastatic oncogene-driven NSCLC; for example, antibody–drug 
conjugates (ADCs) or bi-specific monoclonal antibodies. ADCs are 
monoclonal antibodies targeted against antigens expressed on the can-
cer cell surface and linked to, for example, chemotherapeutic agents, 
which they deliver following internalization by the cancer cell. T o date, 
the only ADC with regulatory approval is trastuzumab–deruxtecan for 
HER2-mutant NSCLC209,210. In addition, treatment with amivantamab in 
combination with chemotherapy has resulted in improved response 
rates and PFS compared with chemotherapy only as first-line treatment 
in NSCLC with EGFR exon 20 insertion 211; this combination has been 
approved by the FDA and the EMA for this population (Fig. 4).
Unfortunately, diverse resistance mechanisms to these targeted 
therapies almost inevitably lead to disease relapse. Both tissue and 
liquid-based biopsies have become increasingly important and have 
been adopted to delineate the mechanism of resistance 212. Develop-
ment of novel biomarkers and expanded knowledge about resist -
ance mechanisms might enable tailored treatment strategies that are 
matched to a patient’s anticipated disease trajectory.
Non-oncogene-driven NSCLC.  Historically, SOC first-line treat -

regimen with or without pemetrexed (a folate antimetabolite) main-
tenance therapy based on histology (pemetrexed should be used only 
in non-squamous histology)213.

Quality of life
Depending on the location and stage of the tumour, patients can have 
lung-cancer-associated symptoms, such as cough, haemoptysis, dysp-
noea, pain, fatigue and weight loss. Palliative radiotherapy can achieve 
symptom control for various clinical scenarios and is recommended in 
clinical guidelines, for example, for haemoptysis, superior vena cava 
syndrome or painful bone metastases95. These symptoms can nega-
tively affect QOL. The EORTC QLQ-C30, lung cancer EORTC QLQ-LC13 
(ref. 240), Euro QoL EQ-5D241 and the Lung Cancer Subscale (LCS) of the 
Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy — Lung (FACT-L)242 question-
naires are specifically designed to capture these symptoms and to evalu-
ate patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and QOL in lung cancer clinical 
trials. In the metastatic setting, chemotherapy improves QOL when com-
pared with best supportive care243–245. ICBs, either alone or in combina-
tion with chemotherapy, also improve or maintain QOL compared with 
chemotherapy221,246–249. Importantly, ICBs have a unique, immune-related 
adverse event profile that is different from that of chemotherapy (for 
example, endocrine and rheumatological toxic effects), and up to 40% 
of patients develop chronic immune-related toxicities250. Thus, the 
EORTC is evaluating whether a new health-related QOL questionnaire 
(QLQ-ICI) needs to be developed specifically for patients treated with 
ICBs. Targeted therapies also improve QOL compared with chemotherapy 
in patients with metastatic NSCLC and a targetable oncogenic driver251–253. 
Although, in general, high-grade toxicity does not occur often, patients 
can have low-grade toxicity and, as treatment duration is often prolonged, 
this toxicity can also influence QOL. Thus, the EORTC is currently updat-
ing the EORTC QLQ-LC13 questionnaire with items specifically related to 
targeted therapies (such as skin, nail and eye problems)254.
PROs can also be used to monitor patients’ well-being. The use of 
a web-mediated follow-up algorithm based on PROs resulted in earlier 
relapse detection compared with regular CT scans, and even improved 
survival255 and, in another trial, improved the QOL 256. Monitoring of 
PROs can also aid survivorship care by, for example, detecting late 
toxicities or, in palliative care, by proactively addressing symptoms 
at the end of life257.
Outlook
The fascinating and rapid developments in the management of NSCLC 
in the past decade have truly converted the stigma of this cancer as an 
untreatable disease with a poor prognosis into a model of innovation 
and new therapies. Identification of treatable oncogenic alterations and 
related targeted therapies, as well as therapeutic concepts to overcome 
tumour-mediated immunosuppression and opportunities for local 
therapies have led to new perspectives in long-term disease control and 
even potential cure. For the first time, surveillance data from the USA 
show a decrease in mortality for NSCLC in recent years (2013–2016)10. 
These observations are confirmed by results from European countries. 
Of note, young patients showed the strongest fall in mortality, prob-
ably related to changes in smoking prevalence together with smoking 
cessation strategies258.
Looking forward, new developments in key areas should drive 
further improvement in outcomes.
Low-dose CT screening
T o improve the efficacy of LD-CT screening, future research must opti-
mize selection scores for high-risk candidates, individualized screen-
ing intervals and the implementation of artificial intelligence in the 
detection of pulmonary nodules. In addition, large programmes are 
ongoing to explore the complementary contribution of circulating 
biomarkers to radiology-based screening119,120,259.
Perioperative management of patients with resectable NSCLC
The integration of ICB alone or in combination with chemotherapy 
in the perioperative setting has led to a new level of disease con -
trol and survival. However, multiple approaches have been investi-
gated and treatment selection remains difficult. Development and 
validation of new predictive biomarkers, customization of different 
treatment approaches to the individual patient, evaluation of new 
combinations together with implementation of local therapies such 
as radiotherapy might lead to improved efficacy and survival260.
Management of locally advanced, non-resectable NSCLC
After years of stagnation, the addition of consolidation ICB after chemo-
radiotherapy paved the way to long-term improvements of outcomes. 
The potential use of targeted therapies and new immunotherapy combi-
nations together with innovations in radiotherapy have the scope to lead 
to clinically meaningful improvements in outcomes183,261. Furthermore, 
trials are needed to better define the role of surgery in this setting172.
Oligometastatic NSCLC
Although two small randomized phase II trials262,263, completed before 
the introduction of ICBs and next-generation TKIs, improved outcomes 
(PFS and in one trial also overall survival) for patients with synchronous 
oligometastatic NSCLC, NRG-LU002 (ref. 264), the first randomized 
phase II/III trial for non-oncogene-driven oligometastatic NSCLC with 
almost all patients treated with ICBs was negative for its primary end 
point, PFS. This is in contrast to the phase III SINDAS trial 265, which 
evaluated the addition of radiotherapy to a first-generation TKI in 
EGFR-mutated synchronous oligometastatic NSCLC, which was posi-
tive for its primary end point, PFS, as well as its secondary end point, 
overall survival. These differences could be attributed to differences 
in staging, selection criteria and treatment, but also to differences in 
biology. A deeper understanding of both the biology and the optimal 
management of oligometastatic NSCLC is needed.

In addition to the continuously growing number of targetable onco-
genic alterations and associated therapies, real-world clinical experi-
ence reveals differential efficacy compared with that in clinical trials.

Non-oncogene-driven metastatic NSCLC
The use of immunotherapies alone or in combination with chemo -
therapy in the first-line setting of metastatic NSCLC has led to major 
improvements in long-term survival for selected patients. Unselected 
addition of new agents without supportive biomarkers is unlikely to 
further improve efficacy and will affect tolerability. The integration 
of new immunomodulatory approaches into sequential treatment 
concepts, adjusted by appropriate biomarkers for patient selection, 
is a promising avenue. These biomarkers could include combinational 
signatures identified using artificial intelligence, such as those explored 
in the European I3-Lung project268. In addition, understanding and 
overcoming the complex scenario of resistance following immuno-
therapy is a considerable clinical challenge, which is currently being 
investigated in numerous clinical trials269. Owing to negative results 
from multiple phase III RCTs, ongoing and future research will deter-
mine the potential role of multiple ADCs in NSCLC treatment. Despite 
these agents offering attractive, conceptual treatment opportunities, 
they also have new, potentially harmful adverse events, which might 
limit the potential outcome benefit270.
In summary, progress in the key areas described above has been 
rapid, highlighting the dynamic landscape in lung cancer diagnos -
tics and treatment, achieved by leveraging a better understanding of 
diverse disease biology. Historically, most patients with NSCLC have 
been treated with palliative intent, but current advances are important 
steps towards NSCLC becoming a treatable, chronic disease. Such a 
goal relies on the close interaction between clinicians and scientists 
in the best interest of patients.
