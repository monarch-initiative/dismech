# Evaluating Cochlear Implantation Outcomes in Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease: A Case Series Analysis of Genetic Profiles and Intervention Timing.
**Authors:** Hughes SE, Mehta AH, Stucken EZ
**Journal:** Otol Neurotol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1097/MAO.0000000000004815](https://doi.org/10.1097/MAO.0000000000004815)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMT) is a hereditary neuropathy that may present with sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) or auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder (ANSD). Cochlear implants (CIs) have been used to rehabilitate hearing loss in CMT, but their effectiveness remains poorly understood due to the central and peripheral neurological complexities of the disease.

STUDY DESIGN: Clinical capsule report.

PATIENTS: Two adult CMT patients and 2 pediatric CMT patients.

INTERVENTION: Cochlear implantation.

MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Preoperative clinical profiles and postoperative auditory performance.

RESULTS: Case 1: A 60-year-old woman with bilateral ANSD and comorbid multiple sclerosis (MS) achieved significant speech perception improvement (CNC: 8%-68%, AzBio: 64%) 1 year post-CI. Case 2: A 20-month-old boy with GJB1 -associated CMT1X and developmental delays exhibited rapid auditory gains (MLNT: 100%) following CI. Case 3: A 12-year-old boy with AIFM1 -related ANSD and mitochondrial dysfunction achieved moderate speech recognition (CNC: 56%, AzBio: 75%) but remained limited in noisy environments. Case 4: A 32-year-old man with PRPS1 -associated CMTX5 and profound prelingual SNHL showed improved sound awareness (20-30 dB HL) post-CI but had no speech perception gains due to prolonged auditory deprivation.

CONCLUSIONS: Cochlear implantation in CMT patients demonstrates variable but meaningfully beneficial auditory outcomes, with early intervention yielding the most favorable results. A personalized approach incorporating genetic profiling, tailored rehabilitation strategies, and setting realistic patient expectations is essential to optimizing outcomes.

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMT), a prevalent inherited neuropathy affecting 1 in 2,500 individuals, is associated with progressive motor and sensory impairments secondary to peripheral nerve dysfunction.1 CMT is characterized by distal muscle weakness and atrophy, pes cavus, distal sensory loss, areflexia, and gait disturbance.1 Hearing loss, reported in 60-85% of individuals with CMT,2-5 is often unrecognized due to complex auditory phenotypes like auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder (ANSD),3 which disrupts neural synchronization between the inner hair cells and auditory nerve, compromising speech perception despite normal pure tone thresholds (PTA).3,4,6-8

CMT’s genetic diversity,9 driven by mutations in over 30 genes inherited through autosomal dominant, recessive, and X-linked patterns, results in varied clinical presentations.9 For instance, GJB1 (CMTX1) mutations impair myelin integrity and auditory signal conduction, while PRPS1 (CMTX5) causes congenital profound hearing loss within a multisystem neuropathy.10 These genetic variations influence the type and severity of hearing loss through demyelination and axonal dysfunction.11,12

The effectiveness of cochlear implants (CIs) in CMT-related hearing loss remains poorly understood.5,13-15 Emerging evidence suggests electrical stimulation can improve outcomes in select patients, especially with early intervention.5,14,16,17 Outcome variability highlights gaps in understanding the factors affecting CI performance in this population.14

This case series examines CI outcomes in four patients with genetically confirmed CMT. By analyzing genetic mutations, auditory phenotypes, and clinical outcomes, this study aims to clarify the benefits and limitations of CIs in this population.

Four patients with genetically confirmed CMT and hearing loss received CIs. A 60-year-old woman with progressive bilateral sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) and ANSD was diagnosed with CMT at age 40. She had a strong family history (11 affected siblings) and comorbid multiple sclerosis (MS). Previous genetic testing identified an unknown mutation.

Preoperative audiometry showed a PTA of 38.3 dB HL in the right ear and profound speech discrimination deficits, with a Consonant-Nucleus-Consonant (CNC) word recognition score of 8% in quiet. At one-year post-CI, her CNC improved to 68%, and AzBio scores reached 64%, with reported significant quality-of-life improvements.

A 20-month-old male with developmental delays, bilateral ANSD, family history of CMT was found to have a GJB1 variant (c.221T>C), linked to X-linked CMT1X, which disrupts connexin 32 and peripheral myelin integrity.18,19 Newborn hearing screening showed normal auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds, but prolonged interpeak latencies, consistent with ANSD. Preoperative audiometry revealed a right-sided PTA of 25 dBHL and a left-sided PTA of 15 dBHL.

With minimal auditory responsiveness and delayed speech, he underwent right cochlear implantation at age 2. One-year post-activation, auditory-based communication skills, measured by the Little Ears Questionnaire20, improved from 8/35 preoperatively to 31/35. By age 4, he achieved 100% score on the Multisyllabic Lexical Neighborhood Test (MLNT).20,21

A 12-year-old boy with asymmetric progressive hearing loss, was diagnosed at age 8 with ANSD based on abnormal ABR waveforms at suprathreshold levels (70–90 dB), indicating poor neural synchrony. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous variant in AIFM1, associated with X-linked CMT and mitochondrial dysfunction, likely contributing to his auditory phenotype.22 Preoperative audiometry showed a PTA of 41.3 dBHL (left) and 21.3 dBHL (right).

Unaided speech testing revealed significant deficits—NU-6 scores in quiet (left=36%, right=44%) and in noise, +10 SNR (left=0%, right=56%). Binaural hearing aids showed limited benefits (Aided MLNT scores of 32% (quiet) and 0% (noise), AzBio scores in quiet= 26%. He received a CI in the left ear; post-activation, sound detection thresholds improved to 25–35 dB HL, with speech recognition gains including CNC scores of 56%, AzBio scores of 75% in quiet, and 62% in noise (+10 SNR).

A 32-year-old male presented with congenital, pre-lingual profound bilateral SNHL and progressive vision loss. Genetic testing confirmed a PRPS1 mutation linked to CMTX5 and severe sensory neuropathy.23 He received minimal auditory benefit from hearing aids. Audiological testing revealed aided detection thresholds of 85-90 dB HL and 0% AzBio scores in quiet and noise.

Post CI (left ear), he had improved sound detection thresholds to 20–30 dBHL. He did not achieve meaningful speech perception, consistent with expectations given his prolonged auditory deprivation.24 Gains in environmental sound awareness meaningfully enhanced his quality of life, in the setting of his progressive vision loss, and he has elected to pursue a second-sided CI. The patient experienced frequent falls due to his peripheral neuropathy, vision loss, and distal muscle weakness, and he had some challenges with processor retention related to frequent falls and head impacts. This was managed with stronger magnets and alternative retention strategies.

Cochlear implantation in patients with CMT and ANSD poses unique challenges due to the convergence of genetic, neural, and clinical factors. By integrating genetic findings with audiometric outcomes, this case series provides valuable insight into the variability of implantation outcomes and the factors influencing success.24

In this case series, the outcomes for CMT patients were aligned with expectations for the broader category of patients with ANSD. CIs are hypothesized to improve neural synchronization due to their impulsive electrical stimulation, almost like a superimposed gross neural synchrony, though this is not confirmed. In certain CMT phenotypes, partial demyelination contributes to temporal hearing deficits, with CIs potentially aiding in synchrony of activation to compensate for timing uncertainties.

Case 1 features a 60-year-old woman with an indeterminate CMT subtype and multiple sclerosis (MS), whose post-lingual hearing loss was unresponsive to hearing aids due to a profound loss of speech recognition.25 Cochlear implantation enabled meaningful gains by overriding disrupted neural pathways, thus restoring temporal coding necessary for speech discrimination. This underscores the importance of identifying patients whose speech perception deficits stem from neural timing issues rather than peripheral hearing loss, and reflects the benefits of CIs in post-lingual hearing loss, where existing linguistic frameworks support auditory rehabilitation.

A different narrative unfolds in a 20-month-old boy with GJB1-related CMT1X, where early intervention proved pivotal.18,26,27 His significant improvements in speech perception following cochlear implantation reflect the synergy between neuroplasticity and early treatment. Preservation of cochlear nerve integrity in GJB1 mutations likely contributed to his progress. GJB1 encodes for connexin 32 which is expressed in Schwann cells of the auditory nerve but not in cochlear hair cells. Connexin 32-related demyelination may initially spare hair cell-to-nerve synaptic input and spiral ganglion cell viability. This highlights the value of integrating genetic findings with auditory phenotyping to optimize care.

In Case 3, a 12-year-old boy with AIFM1-related mitochondrial dysfunction, is discussed in the context of known variability in CI outcomes with mitochondrial disorders. Though the literature on CI outcomes in patients with mitochondrial disorders is limited, outcomes range from excellent open-set speech perception to minimal benefit. These differences are thought to reflect variable involvement of the auditory nerve, spiral ganglion cells, and central auditory pathways, as well as broader neurologic comorbidities in mitochondrial disorders. 22,28 The preoperative speech performance for the patient described in case 3 showed notable improvements following implantation, suggesting intact central auditory pathways despite mitochondrial dysfunction. This variability emphasizes understanding the interaction between genetic mutations and auditory phenotypes when evaluating CI candidates. This case also highlights the complexity of auditory processing in patients with mitochondrial dysfunction, where deficits in temporal precision and neural synchrony may vary significantly between individuals.28

Case 4 presents a 32-year-old man with PRPS1-associated CMTX5, facing the challenges of CI in prelingual profound hearing loss and prolonged auditory deprivation.23,29 Regardless of the underlying etiology, these patients often have limited speech outcomes due to diminished neuroplasticity and underdeveloped central auditory pathways. Although speech perception didn't improve postoperatively, enhanced environmental sound awareness meaningfully impacted his quality of life amidst progressive vision loss. This case highlights the need to tailor rehabilitation goals to patient-specific capabilities and priorities, focusing on functional improvements over speech outcomes, particularly for those with additional sensory impairments.

These cases highlight the varied outcomes of CI in CMT: early implantation (Cases 2 and 3) leverages neuroplasticity, while post-lingual loss (Case 1) benefits from established linguistic frameworks. Pre-lingual profound loss (Case 4) outlines the challenges of prolonged deprivation yet affirms the value of functional improvements. Recent case series in CMT30,31 report similarly variable CI outcomes, with genetic subtype and baseline hearing influencing performance, consistent with our observations.

The retrospective nature of this study, coupled with a small cohort size and variability in genetic mutations and intervention timing, limits its generalizability. However, these findings lay the groundwork for exploring the intricate relationship between genetic factors and CI outcomes in CMT.

Cochlear implantation remains a valuable intervention for patients with CMT, offering improvements in speech perception, environmental awareness, or both. A personalized approach—integrating genetic profiling, comprehensive auditory assessments, and tailored rehabilitation—is essential to optimize outcomes as well as manage outcome expectations in patients. Future research should explore how genetic subtypes and central auditory deficits influence CI performance. Longitudinal studies examining neuroplasticity and comorbidities will be critical to advancing care for this complex population.

Cochlear implantation in patients with CMT and hearing loss including ANSD can yield meaningful auditory improvements. Early intervention enhances outcomes, and genetic profiling provides critical insights into clinical variability. Long-term follow-up is essential to monitor CI performance and address device-related challenges, considering CMT’s progressive nature.
