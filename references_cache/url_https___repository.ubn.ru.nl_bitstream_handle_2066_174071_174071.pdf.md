Abstract
COX5A is a nuclear-encoded subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV (cytochrome
c oxidase). We present patients with a homozygous pathogenic variant in the COX5A gene. Clin-
ical details of two affected siblings suffering from early-onset pulmonary arterial hypertension,
lactic acidemia, failure to thrive, and isolated complex IV deﬁciency are presented. We show that
the variant lies within the evolutionarily conserved COX5A/COX4 interface domain, suggesting
that it alters the interaction between these two subunits during complex IV biogenesis. In patient
skin ﬁbroblasts, the enzymatic activity and protein levels of complex IV and several of its subunits
are reduced. Lentiviral complementation rescues complex IV deﬁciency. The monomeric COX1
assembly intermediate accumulates demonstrating a function of COX5A in complex IV biogene-
sis. A potential therapeutic lead is demonstrated by showing that copper supplementation leads
to partial rescue of complex IV deﬁciency in patient ﬁbroblasts.

complex IV, COX5A, cytochrome c oxidase, OXPHOS, pulmonary arterial hypertension
1 INTRODUCTION
Mitochondrial complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) is the terminal
enzyme of the respiratory chain. It couples the electron transfer from
cytochrome c to molecular oxygen and allows the proton transloca-
tion across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Thereby, the electro-
chemical gradient is generated that serves as driving force for the ATP
production via complex V (ATP synthase).
Human complex IV is embedded into the inner mitochondrial mem-
brane. It is composed of 14 structural subunits and several prosthetic
groups including two copper centers (Balsa et al., 2012; Tsukihara et al.,
1996). Three complex IV subunits are encoded by the mitochondrial
genome and 11 by the nuclear genome. The mitochondrial-encoded
subunits COX1, COX2, and COX3 form the highly conserved structural
core of the enzyme, in which the redox reaction takes place. The 11
nuclear-encoded subunits are placed around the core of the enzyme.

The exact function of most of these proteins has not been resolved.
It has been suggested that they are required for either assembly,
stability, or functional regulation (Fornuskova et al., 2010). Most
complex IV subunits are hydrophobic proteins embedded into the
inner mitochondrial membrane and contain transmembrane domains
anchoring them. The exceptions are three peripheral hydrophilic
subunits, which adhere to the holo-complex only by interaction with
other subunits. These include COX6B, which faces the intermembrane
space, and COX5B and COX5A, which face the mitochondrial matrix
(Tsukihara et al., 1996).
Complex IV biogenesis takes place via sequential steps, in which
subunits and cofactors are integrated into distinct assembly inter-
mediates that eventually build the fully assembled holo-complex
(Fornuskova et al., 2010; Nijtmans, T aanman, Muijsers, Speijer, & Van
den Bogert, 1998; Stiburek, Hansikova, T esarova, Cerna, & Zeman,
2006). This process is facilitated by several so-called assembly fac-
tors, which aid or regulate the biogenesis of complex IV but are not
part of the holo-enzyme itself (Antonicka et al., 2003; Baertling et al.,
2015; Huigsloot et al., 2011; Ostergaard et al., 2015; Papadopoulou
et al., 1999; Tiranti et al., 1998; Valnot et al., 2000; Weraarpachai
et al., 2009). Complex IV assembly begins with the membrane integra-
tion of COX1, which constitutes the ﬁrst assembly intermediate, also
termed S1. The second intermediate named S2 is formed by an asso-
ciation of COX1 with a preformed heterodimer of COX4 and COX5A
(Fornuskova et al., 2010). This is followed by the insertion of COX2
and COX3 to complete the structural core. Subsequent integration of
the remaining subunits leads to the formation of the fully assembled
complex.
Holo-complex IV is incorporated into so-called supercomplexes,
which consist of complex I, a complex III dimer, and one or multiple
copies of complex IV (I/III/IV n supercomplexes). Further supercom-
plexes consist of only complex IV and a complex III dimer (III2/IV super-
complex) or complex I and a complex III dimer without complex IV (I/III2
supercomplex) (Cogliati et al., 2016; Gu et al., 2016; Letts, Fiedorczuk,
& Sazanov, 2016; Schagger & Pfeiffer, 2000).
Mitochondrial complex IV deﬁciency (MIM# 220110) leads to
severe early-onset disease with a mostly neuromuscular pheno-
type. Manifestations are variable and include, among others, Leigh
syndrome, encephalomyopathy, cardiomyopathy, lactic acidemia,
and metabolic acidosis (Shoubridge, 2001). In complex IV-deﬁcient
patients, the vast majority of disease-causing variants have been
detected in genes encoding complex IV assembly factors and in
mitochondrial-encoded subunits for which the ﬁrst pathogenic vari-
ants have already been described nearly 20 years ago (Clark et al.,
1999; Comi et al., 1998; Hanna et al., 1998; Shoubridge, 2001; Tiranti
et al., 1998). However, mutations in nuclear-encoded complex IV
subunits appear to be rare and have only been discovered in recent
years (Abdulhag et al., 2015; Hallmann et al., 2016; Indrieri et al., 2012;
Massa et al., 2008; Pitceathly et al., 2013; Shteyer et al., 2009; T amiya
et al., 2014).
In the present study, we demonstrate two siblings with pulmonary
arterial hypertension (PAH), failure to thrive, and lactic acidemia and
pathogenic variants in the gene encoding the nuclear-encoded com-
plex IV subunit COX5A (MIM# 603773). In patient ﬁbroblast studies,
we analyze the cellular phenotype of COX5A deﬁciency and offer a
therapeutic lead.
2 MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1 Whole-exome sequencing
Exome sequencing was performed essentially as described before
(Wortmann, Koolen, Smeitink, van den Heuvel, & Rodenburg, 2015). In
short, exome enrichment was performed using the SureSelect Human
All Exon 50 Mb Kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), covering∼21,000 genes.
The exome was sequenced on a HiSeq2000TM sequencer (Illumina,
San Diego, CA). Color space reads were iteratively mapped to the
hg19 reference genome and the variants were annotated using an in-
house annotation pipeline (Hoischen et al., 2010). The selection of vari-
ants was based on the following criteria:<5 variant reads, nonsynony-
mous coding, and splice variants, a frequency of<0.5% in the following
databases: dbSNP (v.137), the genome of the Netherlands, (Genome
of the Netherlands Consortium, 2014), Exome Variant Server (ESP)
(http://evs.gs.washington.edu/EVS/), and Exome Aggregation Consor-
tium (ExAC) data (http://exac.broadinstitute.org). Because of the clin-
ical and biochemical phenotype, variants in mitochondrial candidate
genes were selected. Based on a recessive inheritance model, only
a single homozygous candidate variant remained, which was subse-
quently validated by Sanger sequencing.
2.2 Three-dimensional modeling of protein
structures
A homology model of the COX5A structure was created using the mod-
eling algorithm in the YASARA (Krieger, Koraimann, & Vriend, 2002)
and WHAT IF (Vriend, 1990) Twinset with standard parameters. We
used the bovine cytochrome c oxidase subunit COX5A, which can be
found in the PDB ﬁle 2Y69 (Kaila et al., 2011), as a template for model-
ing. The sequences of human and bovine COX5A share 96% sequence
identity over residues 46–150. In order to analyze the effect of muta-
tions in their biological context, we superposed the newly created
model on the complex in PDB-ﬁle 2Y96.
2.3 Cell culturing and enzymatic analysis
Patient-derived skin ﬁbroblasts were cultured in a humidiﬁed atmo-
sphere with 5% CO 2 at 37 °C using supplemented M199 medium:
M199 medium (Pan Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) with 10% v/v
fetal calf serum (GE Healthcare) and 1% v/v penicillin/streptomycin
(Gibco/Life T echnologies, Grand Island, NY). The activity of the oxida-
tive phosphorylation (OXPHOS) enzymes and citrate synthase was
measured as previously described (Janssen et al., 2007; Rodenburg,
2011). All values were calculated relative to the activity of citrate
synthase.

3 RESULTS
3.1 Case reports
The two patients are female siblings of consanguineous parents (sec-
ond cousins) of Arabic decent from Oman. The patients are the parents’
second and third child, their ﬁrst-born male child was healthy.
The pregnancy of the ﬁrst patient was unremarkable. Her birth was
complicated by meconium aspiration with APGAR scores of 3 and 6 at
1 and 5 min, respectively. She recovered quickly and did not require
mechanical ventilation. Her birth weight was 2,900 g, body length
was 48 cm, and her head circumference was 34 cm (all parameters
between the 25th and 50th percentile). However, in the ﬁrst months of
life, growth stagnated and she was admitted to a hospital at the age
of 3 months with failure to thrive: her weight at this time was 3,000
g, her body length was 52 cm, and her head circumference was 37
cm. Additional symptoms and laboratory ﬁndings at this time included
coughing and vomiting along with mild hepatomegaly, slightly elevated
transaminases and lactate levels. Ammonia and creatine kinase lev-
els were normal. Gastroesophageal reﬂux disease was then diagnosed,
and she was discharged on a high calorie diet applied via nasogas-
tric tube feeding. However, weight gain remained poor. At 7 months
of age, her growth parameters were below the third percentile with
a body weight of 3,500 g, body length of 53 cm, and head circumfer-
ence of 39 cm. Furthermore, she only reached developmental mile-
stones with delay. At this time point, she displayed social smile, was
able to follow objects, and started to have partial head support. At the
age of 7 months, the patient was admitted to our clinic because she
had developed progressive lethargy with reduced oral intake and vom-
iting after each feeding. During physical examination, she appeared
irritable, distressed, and severely emaciated. Furthermore, signs of

3.2 Whole-exome sequencing identiﬁes pathogenic
variants in COX5A that lead to a loss of interactions in
the evolutionarily conserved COX5A/COX4 interface
By exome sequencing, a homozygous variant in the gene encod-
ing complex IV subunit COX5A was detected in both siblings:
chr15(GRCh37):g.75219127G>A; NM_004255.3 (GenBank reference
sequence):c.319C>T; p.(Arg107Cys). This variant was uploaded to
the public Leiden Open Variation Database and is available under the
following URL: http://www.lovd.nl/COX5A. The following independent
software analyses predicted this variant to be pathogenic, each
with the highest available category: MutationT aster (Schwarz, Cooper,
Schuelke, & Seelow, 2014) (URL: http://www.mutationtaster.org, build:
NCBI 37/Ensembl 69): “disease causing,” prob: 0.99999; PolyPhen-2
(Adzhubei et al., 2010) (URL: http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu>/pph2):
“probably damaging,” score: 1.000; AlignGVDV (Mathe et al., 2006;
T avtigian et al., 2006) (URL: http://agvgd.hci.utah.edu, version
18/09/2014): “most likely,” score: C65; SIFT (Kumar, Henikoff,
& Ng, 2009) (URL: http://sift.jcvi.org): “damaging,” score 0. SIFT
was used using the mode “SIFT Human Coding SNPs” and anno-
tation version “Homo sapiens GRCh37 Ensembl 63.” The variant
is not present in sequence variation databases (dbSNP build 149
[URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp], 1000 Genomes [URL:
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/variation/tools/1000genomes], ExAC
(Lek et al., 2016), in-house database). Of note, our in-house database
sufﬁciently covers sequences from individuals of Arab background
with over 1000 genomes including over 100 from Oman.
There were no other homozygous, compound heterozygous, or
autosomal-dominant genetic variants present in our data that were to
be considered pathogenic. The presence of the homozygous COX5A
variant was validated by Sanger sequencing. Pathogenic variants in
other genes known or predicted to cause mitochondrial complex IV
deﬁciency were excluded: there were no mutations in any of the 14
complex IV subunits, in all assembly factors known to be disease caus-
ing in humans or in any of the orthologues predicted to be complex IV
assembly factors.
The variant leads to an exchange of the large basic amino acid argi-
nine for the small neutral/polar amino acid cysteine in position 107 of
the amino acid sequence. The amino acid in position 107 is part of the
highly conserved COX5A/COX4 interface domain of the COX5A pro-
tein (Marchler-Bauer et al., 2015) (Fig. 1).

3.3 COX5A-deﬁcient patient ﬁbroblasts reveal
isolated complex IV deﬁciency
The second patient’s ﬁbroblasts were subjected to nondenaturing BN-
PAGE/immunodetection with n-dodecyl 𝛽-D-maltoside-lysed mito-
chondrial protein. The analysis revealed a clear reduction of the
levels of holo-complex IV (Fig. 2A) and normal levels of the other
OXPHOS enzymes. This observation was in line with additional SDS-
PAGE/immunodetection experiments using subunits representative of
each OXPHOS complex (Fig. 2B). Levels of mitochondrial-encoded
(COX1, COX2) and nuclear-encoded (COX4) complex IV subunit levels
were reduced to a similar extent. COX5A protein was still detectable
in low amounts in the patient cells.
3.4 COX5A deﬁciency leads to relative
accumulation of COX1
In order to analyze complex IV biogenesis in the COX5A-deﬁcient
patient ﬁbroblasts, we performed 2D-BN-PAGE/SDS-PAGE experi-
ments to detect speciﬁc assembly defects or accumulation of complex
IV assembly intermediates. The second dimension SDS-PAGE provides
separation and improved epitope availability for immunodetection of
the individual components of the multiprotein complexes separated in
their native state by the ﬁrst dimension BN-PAGE, including complex
IV assembly intermediates.
The experiment revealed an increase of monomeric COX1 protein
in relation to the amount of COX1 integrated into the holo-complex
(Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the additionally tested subunits COX2, COX4,
and COX5A were detectable at the height of holo-complex IV but not
at the height of this assembly intermediate. The composition of this
accumulating subassembly corresponds to the S1 assembly intermedi-
ate in complex IV biogenesis (Fornuskova et al., 2010; Nijtmans et al.,
1998; Stiburek et al., 2006).

complex IV levels in COX5A-deﬁcient patient

and there is a clear link between disturbed copper integration and
the occurrence of cardiopulmonary disease in complex IV-deﬁcient
patients. Therefore, we tested the effect of CuCl 2 treatment on the
patient ﬁbroblasyts.

4 DISCUSSION
We report the ﬁrst mutation in mitochondrial complex IV subunit
COX5A in two siblings with PAH, failure to thrive, and lactic acidemia.
Pathogenic variants in nuclear genes encoding mitochondrial com-
plex IV subunits have so far been described for six of the 11 nuclear-
encoded ones (Abdulhag et al., 2015; Hallmann et al., 2016; Indrieri
et al., 2012; Massa et al., 2008; Pitceathly et al., 2013; Shteyer et al.,
2009; T amiya et al., 2014). The spectrum of phenotypes is very hetero-
geneous: COX4 mutations lead to exocrine pancreatic insufﬁciency and
dyserythropoetic anemia (Shteyer et al., 2009) and pathogenic vari-
ants in COX6A1 cause Charcot–Marie–T ooth disease (T amiya et al.,
2014). COX8A deﬁciency leads to Leigh-like syndrome associated with
leukodystrophy and epilepsy (Hallmann et al., 2016) and variants in
NDUFA4 and COX6B cause severe encephalopathy (Abdulhag et al.,
2015; Massa et al., 2008; Pitceathly et al., 2013). Interestingly, muta-
tions in COX7B, which is the only X-chromosomal gene in this list, also
lead to early-onset PAH along with other symptoms such as microph-
thalmia and linear skin lesions (Indrieri et al., 2012).
While lactic academia and failure to thrive are very common fea-
tures of pediatric mitochondrial disease, PAH appears to be less fre-
quent. PAH is characterized by occlusion of the smallest pulmonary
arteries due to proliferative intimal lesions and vascular media smooth
muscle hypertrophy, which increases pulmonary vascular resistance
and eventually leads to heart failure (Loyd, 2011). It is often distin-
guished between primary and secondary PAH. The latter is a lot more
frequent and caused by a primary cardiac or pulmonary disease. Pri-
mary PAH has been deﬁned as increased mean pulmonary arterial
pressure without a primary cardiopulmonary disorder such as heart
disease, chronic thromboembolic disease, collagen vascular disease,
exogenous substance abuse, or HIV (Barclay et al., 2005; Rubin, 1993).
Primary PAH has been observed in children with mitochondrial dis-
ease due to variable causes including pathogenic variants in nuclear-
encoded genes such as NFU1 (Navarro-Sastre et al., 2011), SARS2
(Belostotsky et al., 2011), MFN2 (Ryan et al., 2013), and TMEM70
(Fassone & Rahman, 2012) and in mitochondrial-encoded tRNAs
including the m.3243A >G variant in MT- TL1 (Hung, Wang, Chung,
Hwang, & Ro, 2012) and the m.14709T>C variant in MT- TE(Van Hove
et al., 2008). Interestingly, primary PAH has also been described in
patients with isolated complex IV deﬁciency of unknown genetic cause
and in one of the few patients with pathogenic variants in a nuclear-
encoded complex IV subunit (Indrieri et al., 2012; Venditti et al., 2004).
In the present patients, a homozygous missense variant in the
COX5A gene was detected via whole-exome sequencing. Several lines

of evidence clearly indicate this variant to be the disease cause: iso-
lated complex IV deﬁciency was present and the clinical presenta-
tions were suggestive of a mitochondrial disease and consistent with
phenotypes previously observed in complex IV deﬁciency (Indrieri
et al., 2012; Venditti et al., 2004). There were no other variants in
any genes known or predicted to be complex IV associated except for
the COX5A variant, which was, furthermore, unanimously predicted to
be pathogenic by several independent mutation prediction softwares.
Additionally, lentiviral complementation with wild-type COX5A led to
an increase in complex IV levels in patient ﬁbroblasts.
Our BN-PAGE and SDS-PAGE analyses showed that the decreased
enzymatic activity of complex IV is caused by lower abundance of
holo-complex IV in patient ﬁbroblasts. COX5A protein levels were still
detectable in low amounts suggesting that the missense variant leads
to an instability of the subunit.
We further demonstrated that the missense variant lies in the highly
conserved COX5A/COX4 interface of the protein, and is likely to ham-
per the interaction between these two subunits. This interaction is
crucial for early complex IV biogenesis, in which COX5A and COX4
form a heterodimer that is consecutively bound to COX1 in order to
form the S2 assembly intermediate (Fornuskova et al., 2010; Nijtmans
et al., 1998; Stiburek et al., 2006). In the COX5A-deﬁcient patient
ﬁbroblasts, we showed that even though complex IV levels are clearly
reduced, the complex can still be fully assembled. However, we addi-
tionally demonstrated an accumulation of a complex IV subassembly,
which contains COX1 but not COX2, COX4, or COX5A, and, there-
fore, corresponds to the S1 assembly intermediate (Fornuskova et al.,
2010; Nijtmans et al., 1998; Stiburek et al., 2006). Our observation is
in agreement with previous studies that were performed with differ-
ent cell types using artiﬁcial genetic modiﬁcation: Fornuskova et al.
(2010) performed stable-down regulation of COX5A in HEK293 cells
and, thereby, demonstrated reduced holo-complex IV levels as well as
an accumulation of assembly intermediates containing only COX1 but
not COX2, COX4, or COX5A. Altogether, our data further substanti-
ate the role of COX5A in complex IV assembly and suggest an essential
role of COX5A in the formation of the S2 assembly intermediate.
Via supercomplex analysis, we showed that the decrease of com-
plex IV in the patient ﬁbroblasts exclusively affected monomeric com-
plex IV , whereas the levels of the complex IV containing high molecu-
lar weight supercomplexes remained unaffected. Similar observations
have previously been made in complex IV-deﬁcient patients with dif-
ferent underlying genetic defects (Baertling et al., 2015; Hornig-Do
et al., 2012; Kovarova et al., 2016). We also showed that the copper-
induced increase solely affected monomeric complex IV . This supports
the notion that the conservation of the supercomplexes is prioritized
over preserving monomeric complex IV , which appears to serve as a
“pool,” out of which the supercomplexes are assembled.
We showed that complex IV deﬁciency in COX5A-deﬁcient ﬁbrob-
lasts can be partially rescued by treatment with copper. Additionally,
in a model of complex IV breakdown, we observed complex IV levels
were higher in patient ﬁbroblasts simultaneously treated with CuCl 2.
On the one hand, this could suggest a copper-mediated stabilization
or a slower breakdown of holo-complex IV . On the other hand, a pool
of complex IV subunits and subassemblies is still present when CAP
treatment is started in this experimental setup. Therefore, an imme-
diate stabilization of existing complex IV subassemblies rather than a
stabilization of holo-complex IV is another possibility. This effect was
not present in healthy control ﬁbroblasts that were identically treated,
which supports previous observations showing that copper treatment
has no effect on the complex IV levels in healthy cells (Salviati et al.,
2002).
The positive effect of copper in our patient ﬁbroblasts is surpris-
ing, since there is no obvious connection between copper and COX5A.
Copper is integrated at two stages of complex IV assembly: into COX1
(CuB site) during the formation of the S2 assembly intermediate and
into COX2 (CuA site) as a prerequisite for the formation of the S3 inter-
mediate. COX1 and COX2 form the core of the fully assembled com-
plex, whereas COX5A is a peripheral matrix-facing subunit (Tsukihara
et al., 1996). Thus, a direct effect of copper on COX5A in the fully
assembled complex may seem rather unlikely. Copper treatment and,
thus, creating a copper excess has previously been shown to either par-
tially or fully rescue complex IV deﬁciency in ﬁbroblasts of complex IV-
deﬁcient patients in vitro (Baertling et al., 2015; Casarin et al., 2012;
Jaksch et al., 2001; Salviati et al., 2002). Despite the notion that this
can be explained by the fact that these patients mostly had defects in
copper delivery genes, the details of the mechanism behind this effect
remain unclear (Casarin et al., 2012; Ghosh et al., 2014; Salviati et al.,
2002). In the case of the presentCOX5A variant, it is possible that espe-
cially the formation of CuB site in COX1 is disturbed due to hampered
formation of the S2 assembly intermediate.
Despite the fact that copper treatment has been effective in com-
plex IV deﬁciency of different genetic causes, the maximum effect
as well as the doses and treatment times needed to reach it dif-
fered signiﬁcantly between different gene defects (Baertling et al.,
2015; Salviati et al., 2002). This suggests that the efﬁciency of copper
depends on the speciﬁc defect in the respective patient ﬁbroblasts.
Our results raise the obvious question of therapeutic application
of copper in patients, which seems to have been successful in single
cases of complex IV-deﬁcient patients (Freisinger, Horvath, Macmillan,
Peters, & Jaksch, 2004). However, this approach must be taken with
caution. Copper can have several deleterious effects, especially in high
concentrations, that is, inhibition of Krebs cycle enzymes (Salviati et al.,
2002). Its levels are closely regulated within the cell in order to keep
copper levels as low as possible and to avoid even the smallest excess
of copper (Rae, Schmidt, Pufahl, Culotta, & O’Halloran, 1999). Further-
more, the copper concentrations applied to the ﬁbroblasts in our study
would be lethal for patients (Salviati et al., 2002). Therefore, a direct
transfer of the results obtained in our and other cell culture models to
patient care is not trivial.
In summary, pathogenic variants in the COX5A gene can lead to
severe mitochondrial disease and should be considered in patients
with isolated mitochondrial complex IV deﬁciency.
