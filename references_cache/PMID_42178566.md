Febrile ulceronecrotic Mucha–Habermann Disease (FUMHD) is a rare and severe form of pityriasis lichenoides et varioliformis acuta. It presents rapidly with ulcerative and necrotic lesions, fever, and systemic symptoms. This condition is associated with significant morbidity and can be extremely dangerous. The exact cause of FUMHD is not well understood, but hypersensitivity reactions may trigger it.

We describe a 15-month-old Iranian boy who developed rapidly progressing ulceronecrotic skin lesions accompanied by high-grade fever and systemic involvement. A skin biopsy confirmed a diagnosis of FUMHD. Despite treatment with methylprednisolone, intravenous immunoglobulin, and a TNF-α inhibitor, the disease was fatal. To our knowledge, this represents the third reported fatal case of FUMHD in children and the youngest case documented to date.

This report presents the youngest known pediatric case of fatal FUMHD, highlighting the challenges of early diagnosis, risk of systemic infection, and limited treatment options. Early immunosuppressive and antimicrobial therapies are crucial, but prognosis remains poor in aggressive cases.

Febrile ulceronecrotic Mucha–Habermann disease (FUMHD) is a rare and aggressive subtype of a papulosquamous disorder known as Pityriasis lichenoides et varioliformis acuta (PLEVA)[1]. FUMHD is characterized by widespread erythematous macules and rapidly developing ulceronecrotic plaques covered with hemorrhagic crusts. Patients often experience high-grade fevers along with systemic manifestations such as lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, respiratory distress, and mucosal involvement [2].

While PLEVA and FUMHD share histopathological features, several authors suggest that FUMHD may represent the most aggressive end of a disease spectrum. In this proposed continuum, PLEVA progresses in rare cases toward widespread necrosis, ulceration, and systemic involvement due to exaggerated immune activation, T-cell clonal proliferation, and immune-mediated vasculitis. This transition underscores the importance of close monitoring of severe PLEVA cases for early signs of systemic progression.

Fatal cases of FUMHD in children are exceptionally rare. To date, only two pediatric fatalities have been reported in the literature. López-Estebaranzet al. [3] described a child with fulminant ulceronecrotic lesions complicated by systemic involvement, while Mezianeet al. [4] reported another pediatric case that progressed to multiorgan failure despite intensive treatment. Our report of a 15-month-old Iranian boy represents the third fatal pediatric case and the youngest patient described to date.

A 15-month-old Iranian boy from Ahvaz, southwest Iran, was previously healthy and fully vaccinated. He presented with respiratory distress, hypoxia, and extensive ulceronecrotic skin lesions and was admitted to Namazi Hospital, Shiraz, Iran. The illness began with a short prodrome of mild malaise, rhinorrhea, and sporadic cough lasting three days, without any history of recent drug exposure, vaccination, or infection as a clear trigger. Within a week, the child developed high-grade fever (39 °C), irritability, and widespread pleomorphic skin eruptions. These lesions rapidly progressed over the next several days to ulceronecrotic plaques involving the trunk, extremities, perianal region, oral mucosa, and genital area. The patient was hospitalized at Namazi Hospital for a total of 36 days, during which time he required supplemental oxygen, immunosuppressive therapy, and broad-spectrum antimicrobials. Despite initial stabilization, the disease progressed with hematologic abnormalities, recurrent fevers, and sepsis, ultimately resulting in death due to multiorgan failure (Fig.1).

The patient was initially diagnosed with Stevens–Johnson syndrome due to extensive mucocutaneous involvement. However, the patient’s systemic condition worsened, with persistent high-grade fever, progressive respiratory distress, and hepatosplenomegaly, while the absence of drug exposure prompted further investigations. Peripheral blood smear revealed mild monocytosis, and bone marrow aspiration was normal. An immune deficiency workup was performed, including flow cytometry for T-cell (CD3, CD4, CD8), B-cell (CD19), and NK-cell (CD16/56) subsets, all of which were within normal ranges for age. Serum immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE) were also measured and found to be normal. A skin biopsy demonstrated features consistent with PLEVA, including lymphocytic infiltration, interface dermatitis, and necrotic vasculitis, leading to a diagnosis of FUMHD (Figs.2,3,4).

Multiple paraclinical abnormalities were observed, including anemia, thrombocytopenia, hypoalbuminemia, elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, ferritin, and D-dimer), and mild electrolyte imbalances. Upon admission, the patient was initially started on high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone at 30 mg/kg/day for immunosuppressive therapy, aiming to control the aggressive inflammatory skin lesions and systemic symptoms. This was followed by a tapering course of oral prednisolone. Concurrently, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a dose of 2 g/kg, aimed at modulating the immune response and addressing suspected immune dysregulation.

Given the extensive ulceronecrotic skin lesions and mucosal involvement, which posed a high risk for secondary infections, empiric broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy was initiated promptly on hospital day 1. This included intravenous vancomycin (15 mg/kg every 8 h) and meropenem (20 mg/kg every 8 h) to cover potential gram-positive and gram-negative bacterial pathogens, respectively. Antifungal prophylaxis with fluconazole (6 mg/kg daily) was started due to concern for fungal superinfection. In addition, antiviral prophylaxis with acyclovir (10 mg/kg every 8 h) was given to mitigate viral infections, considering mucosal lesions.

As the disease progressed, dexamethasone was added to further suppress systemic inflammation. Owing to poor response and ongoing progression, the TNF-α inhibitor etanercept was introduced on hospital day 10 at a dose of 0.8 mg/kg once weekly subcutaneously to target the suspected immune-mediated vasculitis and T-cell activation.

During the treatment course, initial mild clinical improvement was noted with reduction of fever and stabilization of respiratory distress. However, cutaneous ulceronecrotic lesions continued to worsen. Progressive hematologic abnormalities, including worsening anemia and thrombocytopenia, developed and coincided with recurrent high-grade fevers, suggesting both disease activity and possible treatment-related marrow suppression.

By hospital day 25, the patient developed acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and signs of septic shock, marked by respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation and hemodynamic instability requiring inotropic support. Blood cultures later revealed Acinetobacter baumannii infection resistant to multiple antibiotics but sensitive to colistin, prompting targeted antimicrobial therapy adjustment.

Despite advanced critical care interventions combining immunosuppression and infection control, the patient’s systemic condition deteriorated, culminating in multiorgan failure. The patient passed away on day 36 of hospitalization.

The pathogenesis of FUMHD remains unclear, with proposed mechanisms including hypersensitivity reactions, T-cell clonal lymphoproliferation, and immune-mediated vasculitis [2,5]. While these processes are described across age groups, pediatric patients may present distinct challenges. An up-to-date literature review of FUMHD found that about 36 pediatric cases are reported [2]. In children, the immature immune system may contribute to exaggerated inflammatory responses, rapid progression of skin necrosis, and hematologic abnormalities, as observed in our patient. Timely recognition of FUMHD is challenging because its presentation overlaps with several more common pediatric conditions. Extensive mucocutaneous involvement often leads to an initial misdiagnosis of Stevens-Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis, particularly in the absence of a clear drug trigger [6,7]. Similarly, viral exanthems such as Epstein–Barr virus or adenovirus infection may mimic early febrile rashes. In some cases, features such as hepatosplenomegaly and cytopenias raise concern for hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) or even sepsis-related skin manifestations, further complicating the diagnostic process. Rarely, the histopathology of FUMHD may resemble cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, which has been reported as a diagnostic pitfall in severe cases [5]. In our patient, the initial diagnosis of Stevens–Johnson syndrome illustrates how easily the disease can be mistaken for other pediatric dermatologic emergencies, resulting in delays in initiating appropriate immunosuppressive therapy.

In our case, the initial clinical impression was Stevens–Johnson syndrome due to extensive mucocutaneous involvement, which highlights the risk of misdiagnosis in children. Histopathological findings and immunohistochemical staining were ultimately essential to confirm FUMHD. Prominent hematologic abnormalities, including anemia and thrombocytopenia, suggest a possible overlap between pediatric FUMHD and hematologic dysregulation, a link that deserves further investigation [8].

Immunosuppressive therapy is the cornerstone of treatment for FUMHD, but outcomes remain variable. Corticosteroids, IVIG, methotrexate, cyclosporine, and mycophenolate mofetil have all been used with differing success [2]. Biologic therapies, particularly TNF-α inhibitors such as infliximab and etanercept, have shown transient benefit in some cases but inconsistent long-term remission [9]. In our patient, despite high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG, and etanercept, disease progression continued, suggesting that response rates in pediatric fulminant disease may be limited.

Several reports have highlighted that the early initiation of combined immunosuppressive and antimicrobial therapy may improve outcomes [10]. Case studies have also noted benefit from extracorporeal photochemotherapy and high-dose IVIG in refractory presentations [8]. However, due to the rarity of the disease, no standardized treatment protocol exists, and management remains empirical.

Prognosis in FUMHD depends not only on immune dysregulation but also on complications, particularly sepsis. The breakdown of skin and mucosal barriers predisposes patients to secondary infections, which represent a critical risk factor for mortality. This highlights the dual challenge of controlling immune-mediated inflammation while preventing opportunistic infections. The literature confirms that pediatric fatalities are exceptionally rare but often associated with septic complications [3,4], emphasizing the importance of aggressive infection surveillance and control alongside immunosuppression.

Given the lack of standardized therapy, experimental approaches are increasingly being explored. Novel biologics targeting IL-17 and IL-23 pathways, effective in other severe dermatologic and autoimmune conditions, may offer potential therapeutic avenues for FUMHD [3]. In addition, precision medicine frameworks, including single-patient (N-of-1) trials and digital twin simulations, have been proposed for rare diseases [10,11]. These approaches could enable real-time modeling of disease progression and individualized treatment strategies based on genetic, immunologic, and clinical parameters. Further research into cytokine profiling, genetic susceptibility, and immune dysregulation may provide insight into targeted therapies and improve long-term outcomes [9,10].

This report presents the documented pediatric fatal case of FUMHD, underscoring the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with this rare and aggressive disease. Early misdiagnosis and overlap with other severe dermatologic conditions can delay appropriate immunosuppressive treatment. Despite intensive multimodal therapy including corticosteroids, IVIG, TNF-α inhibition, and broad-spectrum antimicrobials, prognosis remains poor in fulminant pediatric cases complicated by systemic infection. Enhanced awareness, prompt diagnosis, and aggressive combined immunosuppressive and antimicrobial strategies are crucial to improving outcomes. Further research into targeted therapies and immune mechanisms is urgently needed to guide treatment and reduce mortality in this vulnerable population.
