# A clinical case of CACNA1S-related muscle weakness in a Holstein calf with congenital astasia diagnosed by a genotyping test of stored blood.
**Authors:** Inokuma H, Maezawa M, Miyazaki Y, Ogino A, Watanabe KI, Kobayashi Y
**Journal:** J Vet Med Sci (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1292/jvms.24-0308](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.24-0308)

## Content

A homozygous calf with CACNA1S-related muscle weakness, a new autosomal recessive congenital disorder in Holstein cattle, was identified by genotyping 195 stored blood samples from Holstein calves aged less than 12 months. The patient was an 8-day-old male calf with congenital astasia which presented to a university hospital in 2019. The patient was unable to maintain an upright position with assistance to stand. Congenital abnormalities in the central nervous system were suspected, but necropsy revealed no specific lesion, with no clear diagnosis. CACNA1S-related muscle weakness should be considered a cause of congenital astasia or weakness in calves. Genotyping is required for a definitive diagnosis because clinical and laboratory findings are non-specific.

Early onset muscle weakness syndrome (MW) is a new autosomal recessive congenital disorder in Holstein-Frisian cattle [1]. Mutations in the
CACNA1S gene cause insufficient muscle contraction due to voltage-dependent Ca channel dysfunction in skeletal muscle, resulting in movement disorders such as astasia [1]. In the United States, the genetic region associated with the recessive condition of calf recumbency was identified in 2022 [2], leading the Holstein Association USA to recognize MW as a genetic disease in February 2024 [3]. MW is currently listed among diseases in the
pedigrees. Semen from carrier bulls has been introduced in Japan, and the existence of carrier cattle in the country has been known. However, no clinical cases of abnormal homozygotes have been
reported, and clinical signs and test findings of MW largely remain unclear. In this study, we diagnosed MW for the first time in Japan based on the genotyping test of a stored blood sample from
a calf with congenital astasia. The cause of congenital astasia could not be determined by clinical examination or necropsy in 2019. Here we report an overview of the clinical and laboratory
findings and necropsy records of the calf diagnosed with MW.

This study used 195 peripheral blood samples collected from Holstein cattle patients aged less than 12 months that were stored at −30°C until May 2024. These samples were provided to Obihiro
University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine (OUAVM) for the purpose of definitive diagnosis between November 2014 and July 2019. All cattle were transferred to the university with consent
from cattle owners. Physical examinations, complete blood counts, blood chemical analysis, other examinations, and autopsies were performed under approval numbers 26–48 (2014), 27–66 (2015),
28–33 (2016), 29–38 (2017), 18–32 (2018), and 19–144 (2019) from the Animal Experiment Committee of OUAVM. The 195 patients had various diseases, including 44 with cardiac diseases, 7 with
respiratory diseases, 54 with digestive diseases, 10 with urogenital diseases, 28 with musculoskeletal diseases, 34 with neurological diseases, 5 with eye diseases, and 8 with skin diseases.
Ages of the 195 patients ranged from 1 to 347 days, with a median age of 40 days. There were 26 male and 169 female patients.

DNA extraction from blood and genotyping were performed at Maebashi Institute of Animal Science, Livestock Improvement Association of Japan. Genotyping was performed using multiple polymerase
chain reaction with sequence-specific primers (PCR-SSP), using fluorescent primers. PCR-SSP is based on SSPCE [4]; the detection length of elongated DNA
amplified using different sizes of fluorescence primers is matched or mis-matched with the 3’-terminus at the target SNP site.

Of the 195 patients, 1 classified as having a neurological disease was found to have a homozygous mutation in the CACNA1S gene. The sire of this calf was a US breeding bull,
which was confirmed to be a carrier. However, the genotype of the dam and siblings of the patient could not be traced. Five other calves, including 2 with a ventricle septal defect, 1 with
unilateral testicular enlargement, and 2 with congenital joint dysplasia, were found to have a heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1S gene. The remaining 189 patients were
determined to be wild-type. Fourteen patients, including the present patient, were recorded as being unable to stand when they were brought to OUAVM. Of these, causes in 1 patient each were
ventricle septal defect, severe pneumonia, joint malformation, hydrocephalus, cerebellar hypoplasia, vertebral fracture, and vertebral abscess. The remaining 7 patients were suspected to have
nervous system abnormalities, but no abnormalities were found on histological examination, and the cause of astasia remained unknown.

The patient, a Holstein male calf born in May 2019, was unable to stand from birth and was referred to a local veterinarian at age 3 days (day 1). The patient was treated with vitamin E and B1
and dexamethasone on days 1 and 2. On day 4, watery diarrhea and fever were recorded, and Ringer’s solution, sodium bicarbonate, oxytetracycline, and menbutone were administered. The
veterinarian determined the prognosis to be poor and referred the patient to the Veterinary Teaching Hospital at Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine on day 6 for further
examination. On physical examination upon arrival at the university hospital, the patient was conscious and able to take milk. As watery diarrhea and mild dehydration were noted,
Cryptosporidium infection was examined using a kit (DipFit Cryptosporidum sp, Bio-X Diagnostics S.A., Rochefort, Belgium); the result was negative. The patient was able to

In the present case, the inability to stand from birth led to clinical suspicion of congenital central nervous system abnormalities, such as spinal cord malformation and cerebellar hypoplasia,
but pathological autopsy revealed no such abnormalities. White muscle disease was considered as a differential diagnosis but was ruled out based on the absence of increased activity of enzymes
(AST, CK, and LDH) released from the muscles [7] as well as pathological autopsy results. Although the cause of congenital astasia was unknown at the time
of necropsy in 2019, genotyping analysis using DNA extracted from stored blood led to a definitive diagnosis of MW five years after death. Homozygotes for CACNA1S mutations were
included among cases of unexplained inability to stand. When encountering such cases with unknown causes, MW should be included as a differential diagnosis.

MW carriers are known to exist in Japan based on preliminary genetic analyses of random samples from slaughterhouses performed by Maebashi Institute of Animal Science, Livestock Improvement
Association of Japan (unpublished data). However, no clinical cases of MW have been reported to date, possibly because the clinical sign of MW is non-specific astasia. It is also difficult to
clinically diagnose this disease due to the lack of specific laboratory test findings. MW should be considered a cause of congenital astasia or weakness in calves, and genotyping should be
performed for a definitive diagnosis given the non-specific clinical and laboratory findings of MW.

Homozygous mutations in the CACNA1S gene in MW cattle are associated with myopathy and generally result in the loss of standing ability [1]. However, there is also a report of individuals with homozygous mutations that showed motor dysfunction as calves but did not subsequently show weakness or recumbency [1]. Clinical findings relating to CACNA1S mutations are generally lacking in the literature. Human CACNA1S gene mutations are
also associated with congenital myopathy [5, 8], but the phenotype of this myopathy is known to be diverse, ranging
from more severe forms with early fatal onset to mild to moderate forms with a more favorable clinical course [5]. To better understand the clinical
variation and pathological characteristics of MW in cattle, it is necessary to accumulate cases of definitive diagnosis based on genotyping. For this reason, it is considered important to
conduct genotyping tests for MW in a large number of cases. These tests are currently being entrusted to the Livestock Improvement Association of Japan.
