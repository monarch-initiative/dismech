# Requirements for the neurodevelopmental disorder-associated gene ZNF292 in human cortical interneuron development and function.
**Authors:** Kaushik K, Chapman G, Prakasam R, Batool F, Saleh M, Determan J, Huettner JE, Kroll KL
**Journal:** Cell Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.celrep.2025.115597](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2025.115597)

## Content

Pathogenic mutation of the zinc-finger transcription factor ZNF292 is a recently defined contributor to human neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). However, the gene's roles in cortical development and regulatory networks under its control were previously undefined. Here, human stem cell models of ZNF292 deficiency, resembling pathogenic haploinsufficiency, are used to derive cortical inhibitory neuron progenitors and neurons. ZNF292-deficient progenitors undergo precocious differentiation but subsequently exhibit compromised interneuron maturation and function. In progenitors, genome-wide occupancy and transcriptomic analyses identify direct target genes controlling neuronal differentiation and synapse formation that are upregulated upon ZNF292 deficiency. By contrast, deficiency in interneurons compromises ZNF292 genome-wide association with and causes downregulation of direct target genes promoting interneuron maturation and function, including other NDD genes. ZNF292-deficient interneurons also exhibit altered channel activities, elevated GABA responsiveness, and hallmarks of neuronal hyperactivity. Together, the results of this work define neurodevelopmental requirements for ZNF292, some of which may contribute to pathogenic ZNF292 mutation-related NDDs.

Transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression to control multiple aspects of human brain development. Accordingly, mutations in genes encoding TFs frequently alter brain development and function, centrally contributing to the etiology of multiple neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs).1–5 Among these, pathogenic variants in the gene encoding the zinc-finger (ZF) TF ZNF292 have emerged as a novel, uncharacterized contributor to NDDs.6 Mirzaa et al. recently characterized ZNF292 pathogenic variants in a patient cohort with intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), epilepsy, and brain structural abnormalities, including microcephaly.6 The Simons Foundation scores ZNF292 as a new syndromic ASD gene7 with a pLI (probability of loss-of-function intolerance) score of 1.0, indicating intolerance of this gene to loss-of-function mutation.

Annotated ZNF292 structural features include 16 C2H2-type ZFs that bind DNA, two nuclear localization signals, and three coiled-coil domains that may mediate homomeric or heteromeric protein complex formation to regulate gene expression. While originally described as an enhancer of growth hormone and prolactin gene expression in rat pituitary8 and suggested to play roles in tumorigenesis,9,10 ZNF292 requirements in neurodevelopment and the mechanisms by which it mediates these remained unknown. Most pathogenic mutations are de novo heterozygous nonsense or frameshift mutations that may result in haploinsufficiency, with heterozygous microdeletions, including ZNF292, also causing NDDs,11 supporting ZNF292 deficiency as a major contributor to shared clinical NDD phenotypes.6

While the developmental etiology of NDDs, including ASD, remains poorly understood, alterations in the balance of neuronal excitation versus inhibition (E/I) in the cerebral cortex are a likely a major contributor.12 Accordingly, neurodevelopmental processes that establish and maintain cortical E/I balance are sensitive to perturbations, which can cause NDDs. Among these processes, cortical interneurons (cINs), constituting approximately 20%–30% of cortical neurons, provide essential inhibitory activity to counterbalance that of excitatory pyramidal neurons and maintain E/I balance of neuronal circuits in the brain. cINs derive primarily from the medial and caudal ganglionic eminences (MGE/CGE) and migrate tangentially during fetal development from these transient ventral telencephalic structures to their final destinations in the cerebral cortex.13,14 Relative to cortical excitatory neuron development, cIN development involves a more protracted period encompassing much of human gestation,15 making cINs particularly sensitive to NDD-associated perturbation. Accordingly, we and others have utilized human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC) models of MGE-like progenitor specification and cIN-like neuronal differentiation to model this neurodevelopmental process and investigate how its disruption contributes to NDD-associated phenotypes.15–18 These models provide a robust, experimentally tractable experimental platform for studying roles of new NDD-associated TFs such as ZNF292 in cIN development.

Here, we began to characterize ZNF292 requirements during neurodevelopment, using CRISPRi-based hPSC knockdown models that reduce ZNF292 protein to levels resembling the haploinsufficiency predicted to result from NDD-associated loss-of-function ZNF292 mutations, in conjunction with hPSC models of cIN development. This work identified anomalies related to MGE-like neural progenitor cell (NPC) differentiation timing and capacity and altered cIN maturation and function. We also defined and integrated the transcriptomic consequences of ZNF292 deficiency with its altered genome-wide occupancy in NPCs and cINs to identify putative ZNF292 direct target genes with dysregulated expression that contributes to corresponding neurodevelopmental phenotypes at a cellular and functional level. These putative ZNF292 direct targets included other NDD genes, suggesting that ZNF292 acts as an upstream regulator of other NDD-contributory genes with essential roles in cortical development and defining how mutations in both ZNF292 and its NDD transcriptional targets may alter downstream regulatory networks to cause NDDs.

To investigate the role of ZNF292 in human cortical development, we made stable hPSC models of ZNF292 knockdown (KD), using plasmid constructs that constitutively expressed dCas9-KRAB with one of two guide RNAs (gRNAs; G1/G2) targeting the ZNF292 promoter to repress gene expression (Figure 1A). We also utilized control hPSC models that stably expressed dCas9-KRAB but no gRNA (control/KRAB). We performed standard quality control measures, finding that all three models had the expected hPSC colony morphology and a normal karyotype (Figures S1A and S1B).

Using a well-established protocol,17,18 we specified hPSCs as ventral telencephalic MGE-like NPCs by day (D) 15 and differentiated these into cINs by D30 (Figure 1B). During human brain development in vivo, ZNF292 is expressed in multiple brain structures, with expression peaking by 12 weeks post-conception but continuing throughout the lifespan19 (Figure S1C). Likewise, during differentiation in vitro, ZNF292 expression increased during NPC specification from hPSCs and persisted through cIN differentiation (Figure S1D). We next assessed the efficacy of ZNF292 KD, finding significant ZNF292 protein reduction in hPSCs (D0, 46%-G1; 37%-G2; Figures S1E–S1G), NPCs (D15, 60%-G1, 55%-G2; Figures 1C and 1D; Figures S1H and S1I), and cINs (D30, 55%-G1, 54%-G2; Figures S1J–S1L) versus controls, respectively, with 80%–90% mRNA level reduction upon KD at these time points. Therefore, these models resemble the pathogenic ZNF292 deficiency found in some subjects with NDDs with heterozygous loss-of-function mutation or deletion.

We began to assess phenotypes from D11 during NPC specification, after moving D10 NPC spheres from 3D suspension to 2D culture, with KD spheres exhibiting significantly reduced outgrowth versus controls (Figures 1E and 1F). While sphere size did not differ between control and KD (Figures S2A and S2B), KD spheres exhibited reduced outgrowth through D15 (Figures 1G and 1H; Data S1), indicating a ZNF292 requirement for regulating early aspects of interneuron development. As reduced outgrowth may involve alteration of several cellular processes, we assessed whether KD affected NPC adhesion, cell count (Figures S2C and S2D), or 5-Ethynyl-2’deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation into replicating cell DNA (Figures S2E and S2F). At D11, we observed no significant differences in cell adhesion but a trend toward reduced cell proliferation, suggesting D11 onset of altered KD NPC properties. Consistently, we found that KD reduced the proportion of adherent D15 NPCs (Figure 1I), while D15 KD NPCs also exhibited reduced proliferation (Figure 1J). Together, these results suggested that ZNF292 deficiency affects both cell adhesion and proliferation during NPC specification.

To define the molecular basis of these cellular phenotypes, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in control and KD NPCs. All three sample types clustered independently in principal-component analyses (Figure 1K), with 2,267 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; 966 upregulated, 1,301 downregulated) present in both KD versus control comparisons (Figure 1L; Figure S2G; Data S2A–S2E). We next performed gene ontology (GO) analysis on these gene sets. KD-upregulated genes were enriched for terms associated with synaptic development and TF activity, suggesting dysregulation of transcriptional mechanisms controlling neuronal differentiation, which may enable NPCs to precociously upregulate genes associated with synaptic development and function (Figures 1M; Data S2F). By contrast, KD-downregulated genes were enriched for general GO terms associated with earlier developmental processes (Figure 1N; Data S2G), including signaling, morphogenesis, and cell adhesion, consistent with our prior finding of reduced KD NPC adhesion (Figure 1I). Together, these data supported precocious upregulation of genes related to neuronal differentiation, synapse organization, and function.

To elucidate how KD could affect cIN development, we next assessed the temporal expression of each upregulated DEG, using transcriptional profiles we previously defined for wild-type hPSC cIN differentiation from D0 to D6018,20 to identify four distinct expression profile clusters (Figure 2A; Data S2H). Most upregulated DEGs were in clusters 1 and 2 and most highly expressed in differentiated D35–D60 cINs, consistent with our hypothesis that ZNF292 deficiency causes precocious neuronal differentiation-related gene expression. These DEGs included the cIN markers GAD1/2, required for GABA production; genes related to gated channel activity, including SCN11A; and seven potassium channel genes (Figure 2B). Some cluster 1–2 genes were also expressed in D15 progenitors, including DLX1/2/5 and LHX6, TFs that regulate interneuron differentiation and/or migration.21,22 Interestingly, upregulated DEGs most highly expressed in D15 progenitors also included NKX2.1, ASCL1, SATB1, and WNT5B, which mark and contribute to progenitor specification (cluster 3, Figure 2C). These results suggest that ZNF292 transcriptionally controls both NPC specification and interneuron differentiation.

To more directly assess whether KD affected NPC proliferation, we assessed EdU incorporation, finding that KD reduced the EdU-immunopositive D15 NPC fraction (Figures 2D and 2E), compatible with our previous findings that KD caused premature NPC differentiation. Next, we validated our transcriptomic findings, performing immunocytochemistry for MGE-lineage (NKX2–1), ventral telencephalic (ASCL1), and neuronal (TUJ1) markers in NPCs. KD caused a significant increase in the fraction of cells immunopositive for all three markers, congruent with precocious NPC differentiation (Figures 2F–2K). These findings were also evident at D11 but more subtle (Figures S2H–S2M). Together, these data indicate that ZNF292 is required to regulate neuronal differentiation timing and/or capacity, with deficiency causing premature differentiation and upregulation of neuronal- and synapse-related genes.

We also assessed how expression of KD-downregulated DEGs changed during D0–D60 specification and differentiation, finding that many downregulated DEGs were also most highly expressed in interneurons (D35/D60, Figure S3A; Data S2I). However, these genes were involved in distinct processes versus the upregulated DEGs, with major categories involving cell-cell and plasma membrane adhesion molecules. KD-downregulated genes with peak expression in NPCs were also associated with regulation of Notch signaling (Data S2I). Diminished Notch signaling delays the G1/S-phase transition to enhance NPC commitment to neurogenesis, facilitating precocious differentiation.23 Inducing early cell exit by inhibiting Notch signaling in NPCs can also promote SST+ interneuron production.24 Interestingly, KD in NPCs caused SST upregulation at both RNA and protein levels (Figures S3B–S3D), accompanied by increased proneuronal TF expression. These data indicate that ZNF292, either indirectly or directly, modulates molecular programs controlling the transition from cycling NPCs to differentiated cINs.

While KD caused precocious neuronal gene expression in NPCs, this could involve either direct regulation of these genes by ZNF292 or ZNF292-dependent regulation of intermediate TFs. Therefore, we investigated the mechanisms underlying this phenotype by defining ZNF292 genome-wide occupancy in both control and KD NPCs, as described (see STAR Methods), identifying 7,777 sites of ZNF292 occupancy sensitive to KD (“control unique,” Figures 3A and 3B; Data S3A and S3B). We also identified 2,349 ZNF292 binding sites that were maintained upon KD in NPCs, suggesting that these peaks are less sensitive to ZNF292 reduction to ~50% of wild-type levels; these may constitute higher-affinity ZNF292 peaks. Our subsequent analysis focused on the 7,777 control-specific peaks lost upon KD with either gRNA.

Annotation of these ZNF292 binding events demonstrated that a majority occurred near gene promoters (38%), while many were also in intronic (28%) or intergenic regions (14%) or candidate cis-regulatory elements (CREs; 5.4%), suggesting that ZNF292 may function both at promotors and at more distal regulatory elements (Figure 3C). GO analysis of genes associated with these control-unique ZNF292-bound peaks indicated that ZNF292 most frequently associates with genes involved in neuronal differentiation and function, including neuron projection development, synapse organization, and axon development (Figure 3D; Data S3C). Together, these results indicate that ZNF292 binds near promoters of key genes involved in neuronal differentiation and maturation, through which it may regulate genes controlling cIN differentiation.

While the ZNF292 TF binding site (TFBS) is unknown, we used these data to investigate enrichment for known TFBSs under control-unique ZNF292-associated sites. This approach defined an enriched ZF TFBS (KLF14: RGKGGGCGKGGC) that may, among known motifs, most closely resemble the ZNF292 recognition motif and was present in more than half of identified ZNF292 peaks (Figure 3E). Based upon TFBS co-enrichment, basic helix-loophelix (bHLH) (present in 64% of peaks), ZF (54%), homeodomain (HD; 39%), MAD (37%), and SRY-related high mobility group-box (SOX/HMG) (21%) family TFs are other TF classes that may co-bind these regions (Figure 3E; Data S3D). We also assessed the chromatin context in which ZNF292 binding occurred, intersecting our chromatin state atlas (ChromHMM genome partitioning from D0 to D60, based on H3K4me3, H3K27me3, H3K27ac, and H3K9me3 histone modification enrichment20; Figure S3E) with ZNF292-bound peaks in NPCs. This analysis demonstrated that 67.9% of ZNF292-bound peaks were in active/open chromatin enriched for H3K27ac and/or H3K4me3 (Figure 3F; Data S4A). Of the peaks, 21.5% were also associated with bivalent chromatin co-enriched for H3K4me3 and H3K27me3, commonly associated with developmental gene maintenance in a poised but transcriptionally repressed state that can resolve rapidly to an active or repressive state in response to developmental cues (Figure 3F). Thus, ZNF292 frequently associates with both transcriptionally active chromatin and sites of bivalent, developmental gene repression, generally supporting a role in modulating expression of the associated genes.

Finally, examining TFBS enrichment of ZNF292-bound peaks associated with active versus bivalent chromatin and comparing these to TFBS enrichment under the full control-unique peak set revealed both overlapping TFBS and sites recognized by shared TF classes. These included the same ZF binding motif and motifs recognized by TFs in the bHLH, HD, and MAD families, while ZNF292-associated sites in active chromatin were also enriched for motifs recognized by Ets (ETS) and helix-turn-helix (HTH) factors, and sites in bivalent chromatin were also enriched for forkhead (FH) family TFs, respectively (Figures 3G and 3H; Data S3E and S3F). As some members of these TF families play known roles in modulating neurodevelopment, it will be interesting in the future to assess whether they co-regulate transcription with ZNF292 at these sites during neurodevelopment.

We next identified ZNF292-associated genes that were also differentially expressed upon KD, which represent putative direct targets in progenitors (Data S4A). This defined 1,063 ZNF292-bound and -regulated genes, including 405 KD-upregulated and 658 KD-downregulated DEGs (Figure 4A), suggesting that ZNF292 may act as both a transcriptional activator and a repressor during progenitor specification. Consistent with our prior analyses, enriched GO terms associated with ZNF292-bound and KD-upregulated genes were related to neuronal differentiation and acquisition of synaptic function (Figures 4B; Data S4B). Together, these findings indicate that ZNF292 may restrain the onset of neuronal differentiation in progenitors by directly controlling the expression of suites of neuronal genes, congruent with the precocious differentiation observed upon KD. ZNF292-bound and downregulated DEGs were instead enriched for terms related to cell adhesion and signaling receptor binding, which are also associated with cell state changes during early development and neurodevelopment (Figure 4B; Data S4C); these findings suggest a potentially direct role for ZNF292 in regulating the expression of a suite of genes that modulate intermediate developmental processes, such as altered cellular adhesion and signaling needed for progenitor specification, migration, and signaling interactions with the local environment during neurodevelopment.

We next examined the normal expression trends of ZNF292-associated and KD-upregulated genes during D0–D60 of interneuron development, finding that a majority were expressed at peak levels in differentiated and mature cINs (D35/D60; Figure 4C, clusters 1 and 3). Among these, cluster 1 and 3 genes were key regulators of synapse organization and signaling (e.g., NRXN2, SYN3, and GRIP1), gated channel activity (KCNH8 and KCNB1), and axon guidance (EPHA7, SLIT2, NRG3, and PTPRO). These results suggest that ZNF292 restrains expression of these genes to control differentiation timing and capacity, while KD causes their upregulation, accelerating the transition toward differentiated and/or functional cINs (Figure 4D, clusters 1 and 3). However, a substantial minority of ZNF292-bound, upregulated genes were most highly expressed at D15 (Figures 4C and 4D, cluster 4). These included ASCL1,25 SATB1,26 and several other genes involved in both progenitor fate acquisition and differentiation, further supporting a direct role for ZNF292 in regulating neuronal differentiation (Figure 4D, cluster 4; Data S4D). As SATB1 KD reduces neuronal differentiation,26 SATB1 upregulation upon KD is expected to promote differentiation and increase production of TUJ1-expressing neurons, as is seen here. KD also upregulated genes related to WNT signaling (AMER1, AMER2, NOG, and FRZB, Figure 4D, clusters 1 and 4), which regulate cytoskeleton remodeling to modulate neuronal morphogenesis and synaptogenesis.27 In summary, ZNF292 associates with, and KD upregulates, suites of genes that promote progenitor differentiation, neuronal morphogenesis and axon guidance, and synapse and ion channel organization and signaling. Browser tracks for several ZNF292-bound peaks associated with upregulated DEGs are shown in Figures 4E–4L. Peaks were enriched in control but not KD samples and were co-enriched with histone modifications associated with active and accessible chromatin or bivalent chromatin (Figures 4E–4L), consistent with overall trends (Figure 3F). ZNF292 was also associated with genes with diminished expression upon KD, and many of these were also normally expressed most highly in differentiated neurons (Figure S3F, clusters 1 and 4). These genes were predominantly related to growth factor binding (e.g., PTN, TGFBR3, IGFBP3, LRP2, and EPHA3), signal receptor binding (ERBB4, PDGFB, ACVR1, ITGB3, KIT, COL4A1, IGFBP7, IL1R1, and IL6R), and synaptic signaling (NTRK2, PLCB1, DLG2, CNTN4, and NTKR2), supporting additional roles for ZNF292 in promoting intermediate neurodevelopmental processes, including neuron cell-substrate adhesion, migration, and morphological changes accompanying differentiation (Figure S3F; Data S4E).

To examine whether ZNF292 deficiency affected later aspects of interneuron development, neurospheres were made from the control and KD models at D15, as differentiation was initiated.28 Differentiating spheres were seeded on Matrigel/laminin-coated dishes on D20 and assessed the following day (D21), and significantly greater outgrowth from plated KD versus control spheres was observed, suggestive of increased neuronal migration (Figures 5A and 5B; Data S5A). We assessed if this correlated with other altered neuronal characteristics by examining morphology at D23, finding that KD neurons exhibited increased cell soma size and generated significantly higher numbers of primary and secondary neurites versus controls, indicating that their precocious differentiation was accompanied by altered neuronal morphology (Figures 5C–5F; Data S5B).

To assess whether KD affected other aspects of neuronal differentiation and maturation, we used immunocytochemistry to assess expression of the mature neuronal and cIN markers MAP2 and CALB1. Interestingly, despite the prior hallmarks of precocious differentiation, a significantly reduced fraction of KD neurons was immunopositive for MAP2 and CALB1 at D30, suggesting that KD compromised neuronal maturation and/or acquisition of cIN identity (Figures 5G–5I). We likewise found that expression of the cIN marker SST was downregulated at the protein level in D30 KD cINs (Figures S4A and S4B). To comprehensively assess how KD affected the cIN transcriptome, we conducted RNA-seq analysis at D30, identifying significant transcriptomic differences between control and KD samples (Figure 5J; Data S6A–S6C) and defining 1,857 genes that were significant DEGs in both G1 and G2 KD versus control cINs (Figure S4C), with a greater number of KD-downregulated (1,120) versus -upregulated (737) DEGs (Figure 5K; Data S6D–S6F). In this analysis, both KD-up- and -downregulated DEGs were associated with GO terms related to neuronal development, identity, and function, including synapse-related genes (Figure 5L; Data S6G and S6H). However, several key neuronal and cIN markers, including MAP2, CALB1, and GAD2, were downregulated DEGs at D30. This suggested that ZNF292 deficiency led to precocious NPC differentiation into cINs but subsequently impaired their maturation and cIN identity. Mirroring our previous analyses, we examined the normal expression of KD-up- and -downregulated genes during D0–D60 of cIN differentiation. Congruent with enrichment for synapse-related GO terms in both KD-up- and -downregulated DEGs, many DEGs in both comparisons had peak expression in differentiated D35–D60 neurons (Figures S4D and S4E; Data S6I and S6J). These data suggested that ZNF292 deficiency dysregulates the expression of genes responsible for synapse formation and organization, which may alter later interneuron maturation, identity, or function.

To distinguish between ZNF292 direct and indirect transcriptional targets in neurons, we defined its genome-wide occupancy in both control and KD cINs, with peak centers visualized in Figure 6A and numbers under control and G1 or G2 KD conditions shown in Figure 6B (Data S7A). This analysis identified 7,969 control-unique binding sites lost upon KD and 6,448 sites bound by ZNF292 in both control and KD samples, the latter of which may constitute higher-affinity binding sites less sensitive to KD. We focused on KD-sensitive control-unique binding sites, which were associated with 5,826 genes, and annotated associated gene features. By comparison with ZNF292 genome-wide occupancy in progenitors, a substantial but reduced fraction of sites in cINs was also associated with gene promoters (27.4%). However, the peak fraction in intronic (34.2%), intergenic (20.6%), and annotated CREs (7.3%), all of which may constitute ZNF292-bound cIN stage-specific putative CREs, increased substantially (Figure 6C; Data S7A). TFBS analysis under these bound peaks demonstrated enrichment for TFBSs recognized by the same TF families enriched under ZNF292 binding sites in NPCs. Among these were ZF TFBSs and bHLH, HD, and MAD family TFBSs, suggesting that ZNF292 has a relatively canonical ZF binding site and may function with other TF co-regulators to regulate transcription in cINs (Figure 6D; Data S7B).

We next intersected these cIN ZNF292-binding sites with our ChromHMM chromatin state data from D0 to D60 as before20 (Figure S3B), finding that 78.48% of peaks were co-enriched with H3K27ac and H3K4me3, a 12% increase in association with these active histone modifications versus that observed in NPCs. This was concordant with a reduced number of ZNF292-binding sites associated with bivalent peaks (14.2% in cINs versus 22% in NPCs) (Figure 6E; Data S7A). Likewise, ZNF292 binding sites were rarely associated with repressive chromatin (9.4%) or chromatin unmarked by the four modifications assessed here (8.6%) (Figure 6E). ZNF292 peaks co-enriched with active chromatin were enriched for the same ZF consensus motif at 61% of peaks and TFBSs recognized by the bHLH, HD, MAD, and ETS TF families, reminiscent of our prior findings in NPCs (Figure S4F; Data S7C). The small fraction of bivalent ZNF292 peaks instead was enriched for TFBSs recognized by factors in the nuclear receptor, CCAAT binding, ZF, and ETS TF families (Figure S4G; Data S7D). Together, these results support a similar cis-sequence composition and TFBS enrichment at ZNF292 peaks in NPCs and cINs, while more frequent association of ZNF292-bound sites with an active chromatin state, in combination with our other data, suggests that ZNF292 may promote expression of direct targets associated with cIN neuronal maturation and function.

To define putative direct targets both bound and regulated by ZNF292 in cINs, we intersected genes associated with control-unique ZNF292 peaks (Figure 6B), with significant DEGs found in both G1 and G2 KD versus control comparisons. This identified 572 ZNF292-bound genes in cINs that were also DEGs, including 101 KD-upregulated and 459 downregulated DEGs (Figure 6F; Data S6E). The preponderance of KD-downregulated DEGs in this putative direct target gene analysis, in conjunction with ZNF292’s strong association with active chromatin in wild-type cINs, suggests that it may predominantly activate these direct targets in cINs. Relatively few high-confidence ZNF292-associated, KD-upregulated genes were identified; these were mostly related to voltage-gated ion channel genes (Data S7F). By contrast, GO terms enriched among bound and KD-downregulated genes were related to neuron morphogenesis, signaling receptor or cytoskeletal protein binding, cell-cell junction, and post-synapse (Figure 6G; Data S7G). In addition, during wild-type cIN differentiation from D0 to D60, most ZNF292-associated and downregulated genes were most highly expressed in D35–D60 cINs (Figure S4H; Data S7H). Accordingly, these genes were predominantly involved in synapse organization and signaling (e.g., CALB1, SYNDIG1, and DGKB), cell-extracellular matrix interactions (CDH18 and FMN1), axonogenesis (GAP43 and NTNG2), neurotransmitter binding (NTRK2 and NTRK3), and cytoskeletal protein binding (ANK2, ANK3, and MAP2) (Figure 6G; Figures S4I and S4J). Interestingly, the reduced fraction of MAP2- and CALB1-immunopositive KD cINs that we observed previously correlated with direct ZNF292 association with and regulation of these neuronal genes. Downregulated expression of these putative ZNF292 direct targets in KD interneurons would be expected to disrupt synapse formation or function or other aspects of interneuron maturation and acquisition of normal functional properties.

Finally, we assessed the extent to which the genes directly regulated by ZNF292 in NPCs and cINs overlapped. We identified 208 ZNF292-bound DEGs common to NPCs and cINs and compared their expression in NPCs and cINs under control versus KD conditions (Data S7I; Figure S5A). Cluster 1 contained genes that are most highly expressed in control NPCs. These genes were consistently downregulated under ZNF292-KD conditions and were related to “insulin-like growth factor binding” (e.g., ITGB3, IGFBPL1, and LRP2), “growth factor binding” (ERBB4, ITGB3, and IGFBPL1), “cytoskeletal protein binding” (SHROOM2 and PALLD), “PDZ-domain binding” (GRIA1, FZD7, and GJA1), and “neuropilin binding” (SEMA3A and SEMA3C) (Data S7J; Figures S5B and S5C). Cluster 2 genes increased in expression during control NPC to cIN differentiation, while KD consistently decreased their NPC and cIN expression. These genes play essential roles in modulating synapse organization, signaling, and transmission and brain-derived neurotrophic factor binding (Data S7J; Figures S5B and S5C), suggesting that most common NPC and cIN putative direct targets were downregulated upon KD and regulate neuronal maturation and functional properties.

Next, we determined which bound and KD-downregulated genes were also high-confidence ASD- or epilepsy-contributory genes, since these are co-morbidities seen in individuals with ZNF292 mutations (Simons SFARI database,29 04–03-2023 release, Data S7K, and Epilepsy Genes,30 05–22-2023 download, Data S7L). Forty-eight ZNF292-bound and KD-downregulated genes were ASD genes (Figure 6H; Data S7M), while 11 were also epilepsy-contributory genes. These genes are predominantly involved in synapse organization and neurotransmitter receptor and channel-related activity, which could contribute to impaired neuronal maturation and function. Nineteen ASD-associated genes were also ZNF292 bound and regulated in both NPCs and cINs (Data S7N), namely, NAV2, GRIN2B, RFX4, MEIS2, ITGB3, NCKAP5, LRP2, NRP2, CNTN4, GRIA1, ZNF292, CNR1, GPR85, DPP6, CNTNAP3, NTRK2, NTNG2, GPC4, and FGF13. Browser views of several neuronal (MAP2) and/or NDD-contributory genes (ANK2/3 and ITGB3) bound by ZNF292 and downregulated upon KD are shown in Figures 6I–6L. Together, these findings suggest that ZNF292 directly regulates the expression of multiple ASD-associated genes, especially those encoding cell adhesion, scaffolding, and signaling molecules that modulate synapse and channel structure and function during synaptic transmission, potentially contributing to NDDs.

During development, changes in ion-channel gene expression modulate intrinsic membrane properties of neurons to control action potential firing frequency and amplitude. Mutations in voltage-gated sodium channels are linked to altered neuronal excitability, including disorders involving epilepsy and pain.31,32 Here, we identified two well-known voltage-gated sodium channels, SCN4B and SCN9A, which are involved in neurological disease31 and were significantly downregulated upon KD (Figure S6A). Therefore, whole-cell voltage and current clamp recordings were obtained from cINs differentiated from control and KD models. Voltage steps from −80 to 0 mV evoked a fast peak of inward current mediated by tetrodotoxin-sensitive sodium channels, followed by a steady-state outward current mediated by voltage-gated potassium channels (Figure 7A). In control and KD model comparisons, KD neurons had significantly less inward sodium current, while raw sodium current measured at the peak of inward current or at 0 mV, as well as current density normalized by cell capacitance, were both reduced in KD cINs (Data S8; Figures 7B and 7C). We also found that KD cINs exhibited reduced expression of potassium-channel-activity-related genes, which may affect potassium current properties (Figure S6B). Accordingly, KD neurons exhibited a slightly less negative reversal potential for steady-state current (Vm for 0 K current) versus control cINs (Data S8; Figure 7D). Despite the significant difference in sodium current, recordings under current clamp revealed no significant differences in action potential properties between control and KD cINs, although minor differences in threshold, peak amplitude, half-width, rheobase, and firing frequency were observed (Data S8; Figure 7E).

In addition to voltage and current steps, we also challenged cINs with exposure to the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA and non-NMDA glutamate receptor agonist kainate, as several GABA-associated genes were either up- or downregulated in KD cINs (Figure S6C). As shown in Figure 7F, ZNF292-KD neurons displayed significantly larger currents evoked by GABA and smaller currents activated by kainate, relative to controls (Figures 7G–7I). Spontaneous inhibitory synaptic currents (sIPSCs) were also recorded in both control and KD cINs (Figure 7J), and both sIPSC frequency and amplitude were significantly higher in KD cINs (Figures 7K and 7L). Together, these data suggested that ZNF292-deficient cINs exhibited altered functional properties, with hallmarks of sustained immaturity, high responsiveness to GABA neurotransmitter, and neuronal hyperactivity. This could contribute to the etiology of clinical phenotypes in individuals with pathogenic ZNF292 deficiency or mutation.

ZNF292 pathogenic variants are a newly defined contributor to human NDDs.6 Therefore, here, we first assessed ZNF292 requirements for neurodevelopment, utilizing hPSC KD models resembling the deficiency predicted to result from heterozygous deletion or pathogenic loss-of-function mutation and a protocol we and others established to specify MGE-like progenitors and differentiate these into mature, functional cIN-like neurons.18,33,34 This model recapitulates key aspects of human cIN development in vivo, including progenitor specification, proliferation, and differentiation; neuronal migration and maturation; and acquisition of function. This work revealed ZNF292 requirements in progenitors, with deficiency causing precocious progenitor cell-cycle exit, differentiation, and upregulation of suites of direct target genes controlling neuronal differentiation, synapse organization, and channel activity; TFs that regulate neuronal differentiation; and cIN markers. Together, these data support a role for ZNF292 in directly restraining expression of these gene suites in NPCs to modulate differentiation timing. Disruption of the balance between progenitor proliferation and differentiation has also been implicated as a contributor to NDDs stemming from multiple other genetic etiologies.35–41 These findings support imbalanced proliferation-differentiation timing or capacity as a convergence point that may be a shared contributor to NDD etiology across mutations in multiple genes.

cINs are generated in the ventral telencephalon and migrate tangentially to their final destinations, forming synaptic connections with projection neurons in the neocortex,42 while coordinated differentiation and migration is also a common feature of other neurodevelopmental programs.43 Here, ZNF292-deficient newborn interneurons exhibited elevated migration and increased expression of DCX, a migratory neuronal marker.14 Mutation or altered expression of regulatory molecules that affect neuronal migration can also change neuronal morphology or complexity.44 Accordingly, ZNF292 deficiency increased neuronal complexity and numbers of primary and secondary neurites, suggesting premature initiation of programs controlling later neurodevelopment, both anomalies that could contribute to NDD etiology. Acquisition of mature cIN identity and function temporally follows and/or may be interconnected with regulation of these earlier events disrupted in ZNF292-deficient models.

Interestingly, despite initial increases in neuron differentiation and synapse-related gene expression and increased neurite branching in newborn cINs, maturing cINs exhibited consistent downregulation of cell adhesion, morphogenesis, synaptic organization, and signaling genes, suggesting altered maturation and function. This was concordant with transcriptomic, ZNF292 genome-wide occupancy, and immunostaining analyses in cINs, which defined putative ZNF292 direct targets involved in synapse formation, organization, and signaling and other NDD-associated genes with essential roles in neurodevelopment and function.45–50 Notably, behavioral and functional phenotypes in mouse and hPSC deficiency models of several of these NDD targets, including ANK2 and KCNQ3, were pharmacologically reversible.48,51,52 Therefore, our results reveal a downstream network of NDD genes that may mediate some consequences of ZNF292 deficiency and provide leads for developing patient interventions. Finally, as ZNF292 directly and indirectly regulates cIN expression of sodium and potassium channel genes and regulators of GABAergic neuronal activity, we assessed functional alterations, identifying reduced sodium and potassium current production and characteristics of cIN hyperexcitation and immaturity, including increased GABA responsiveness and sIPSC frequency and amplitude. As these functional changes could also be related to other co-occurring alterations not evaluated here, including altered input resistance,53 phosphorylation of voltage-gated sodium54 or potassium channels,55 or redistribution of voltage-gated potassium channels, further studies are required to define underlying contributors to altered function.

In conclusion, this work investigated ZNF292 requirements for neurodevelopment, providing insight into its putative direct targets and molecular mechanisms that may underpin ZNF292 mutation-related NDDs and linking consequences of ZNF292 deficiency with disrupted regulation of other NDD-associated gene targets. The cellular, molecular, and functional deficits resulting from ZNF292 deficiency during cIN differentiation provide a foundation for further studies of both temporal and cell-type-specific requirements in the developing and adult brain and further assessment of its mechanisms of action, which will facilitate the development of therapeutic interventions to ameliorate NDD phenotypes in individuals with ZNF292 mutation.

This study defined consequences of ZNF292 deficiency, with the anticipation that some pathogenic variants may involve loss-of-function mutations and haploinsufficiency. However, as ZNF292’s terminal exon encodes most of the protein, some mutant transcripts may escape non-sense-mediated decay, with pathogenicity potentially involving either haploinsufficiency or production of truncated protein forms with altered functionality. Comparing the consequences of haploinsufficiency and a range of pathogenic variants will enable identification of shared and variant-specific consequences and altered transcriptional mechanisms. Another limitation is that this study primarily focused on evaluating ZNF292 requirements for cIN development, necessitating similar future studies focused on elucidating ZNF292 requirements and consequences of pathogenic mutation in other neuronal and glial cell types.
