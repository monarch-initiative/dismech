BACKGROUND: Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) is a genetic disorder that
causes emphysema from lack of the alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) serpin antiprotease,
leading to protease-antiprotease imbalance. Weekly intravenous AAT therapy
(augmentation) is the only specific treatment available. Alvelestat is an oral
inhibitor of neutrophil elastase (NE) in development as a novel approach to AATD
therapy. Here, we tested the safety and mechanistic efficacy of alvelestat in
severe AATD.
METHODS: We conducted two complementary, double-blind, randomised,
placebo-controlled, 12-week trials, incorporating two doses of alvelestat in
AATD. ATALANTa investigated 120 mg twice daily, including a subset of
participants also receiving augmentation; ASTRAEUS tested 120 and 240 mg twice
daily without augmentation. Primary and secondary end-points were the change in
blood NE (the putative target) and its activity in AATD (Aα-Val360 and
desmosine/isodesmosine) as well as safety and tolerability.
RESULTS: We enrolled 161 participants (63 in ATALANTa and 98 in ASTRAEUS). Blood
NE was significantly suppressed in both studies at both doses, with the greatest
effect (>90% suppression) at alvelestat 240 mg twice daily. There was no effect
of alvelestat 120 mg on disease activity biomarkers, while 240 mg demonstrated
significant reduction in Aα-Val360 and desmosine. The most common adverse event
was headache, particularly at the 240 mg dose. No safety signals of concern were
detected.
CONCLUSIONS: Alvelestat effectively suppressed NE and its activity at both
doses, but only the 240 mg twice-daily dose demonstrated relevant efficacy
compared to placebo on disease activity biomarkers with a favourable safety
profile. These findings support progression of the 240 mg twice-daily dose into
a clinical end-point study.

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) is a genetic defect affecting more than 235 000 individuals worldwide [1] and increases susceptibility to emphysema-predominant COPD. Alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) is encoded by the SERPINA1 gene; mutations causing absence or misfolding of the protein result in reduced levels in the plasma and lungs [2]. The genotypes most associated with increased risk for COPD are Pi*ZZ, Pi*ZN (N=null) and Pi*NN, having severe deficiency, with very low or absent AAT, respectively [3]. Compared to non-AATD COPD, affected individuals have an earlier onset emphysema and a more rapid decline in lung function [4], leading to substantial loss of productivity and societal contributions, and increases in respiratory morbidity and mortality [5, 6].

The management of AATD-associated lung disease is similar to non-AATD COPD, apart from weekly intravenous, plasma-derived AAT “augmentation” therapy [7]. Augmentation is indicated for severe AATD genotypes with serum AAT level <11 µM in individuals with evidence of emphysema and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) 35–65% predicted [3]. Augmentation slows the loss of lung parenchyma measured by computed tomography (CT), but does not definitively affect lung function decline, symptoms, exacerbations or survival [7], potentially reflecting the impractical number of participants required to power these clinical outcomes in a rare disease [8, 9]. As augmentation therapy is not used in all patients with severe AATD, and is not universally available in European countries and Canada, we designed the studies with a placebo comparator.

The disease mechanism implicated is based primarily on protease–antiprotease imbalance [10]. AAT is a major inhibitor of neutrophil serine proteinases, particularly neutrophil elastase (NE). In deficient individuals, AAT-buffering capacity is exceeded in the presence of activated neutrophils, resulting in excessive lung tissue destruction through elastin degradation, initiating a cascade of inflammation, mucus overproduction and further lung damage [11]. Consequently, NE inhibition is an attractive therapeutic target.

Alvelestat is an oral, selective, NE inhibitor with elastase-binding affinity similar to AAT and known lung penetration [12, 13]. Daily dosing may reduce the metabolic drop-off that occurs prior to each weekly augmentation [14]. Therefore, alvelestat represents a potential oral disease-modifying treatment for AATD.

Study duration and sample sizes for assessing traditional COPD end-points are impractical for early-phase trials in a rare disease and there is no reference standard for assessing disease activity in AATD [8, 9]. Thus, biomarkers offer an intermediate measure of the proposed causal pathway. Blood biomarkers of NE amount and its activity in vivo, Aα-Val360 [15, 16] and desmosine/isodesmosine [17, 18], enable feasible study designs as they have been investigated in AATD, have specificity for NE-mediated changes (Aα-Val360), relate to lung damage and show treatment response over periods of weeks [19–22].

We report results from two complementary phase 2 trials applying biomarker end-points, in individuals with severe AATD, including overlapping and distinct genotypes, as well as in those receiving or naïve to augmentation therapy. The trials were designed to confirm mechanistic effects, explore dosing, and determine alvelestat safety and tolerability in relevant AATD populations.

A coordinated approach was used enabling assessment of alvelestat across the studies. ASTRAEUS (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03636347) and ATALANTa (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03679598) were randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blind, multicentre trials, with a 4-week screening period, 12-week treatment period and 4-week safety follow-up (supplementary figures E1 and E2).

Investigated alvelestat 120 mg, 240 mg or matched placebo, twice daily, randomised 1:1:1.

Individuals ≥18 years with severe AATD (Pi*ZZ, Pi*NN or other rare genotypes and AAT levels <11 µM/57.2 mg·mL−1). Patients with the Pi*SZ genotype and those on augmentation were excluded. Other key exclusion criteria were lung disease other than COPD, chronic liver disease, immunodeficiency disorders or recent cardiovascular events. Eligibility criteria are detailed in supplementary table E1a.

Primary efficacy end-points: change from baseline in serum NE activity, plasma Aα-Val360 and desmosine/isodesmosine compared to placebo to week 12.

Key secondary efficacy end-point: proportion with serum NE activity below limit of quantitation (<0.5 ng·mL−1) compared to placebo to week 12.

Exploratory end-points included spirometry, St George's Respiratory Questionnaire-COPD (SGRQ-C) [23], and biomarkers of inflammation and proteases.

The supplementary material lists all end-points (supplementary table E2a) and biomarker methods (supplementary table E3).

Following recruitment and retention challenges during the COVID-19 pandemic, ASTRAEUS objectives were revised to signal seeking, with a reduced sample size considered adequate for mechanistic efficacy and safety data, enabling development decisions despite loss of power. The 240 mg dose was prioritised, and recruitment to 120 mg terminated once an adequate number had been recruited for dose exploration (supplementary figure E1 and supplementary table E4).

ASTRAEUS was originally designed with 55 participants per group, to detect a between-treatment-group difference of 6.5% in desmosine (80% power, two-sided α=0.05), based on the effect size reported for augmentation [20, 22]. In line with reduced recruitment, the protocol was amended to reflect signal detection using one-sided testing (α=0.05) for desmosine reduction. Considering early dropouts due to COVID-19 restrictions and adverse events of headache, the Per Protocol Set, comprising participants completing 12 weeks of treatment, was chosen as the appropriate primary analysis set for the mechanistic signal-seeking objectives. The Safety Set comprised all patients who received at least one dose of study drug. Detailed statistical methods are outlined in supplementary table E4.

Tested alvelestat 120 mg or matched placebo twice daily, randomised 1:1.

Individuals ≥18 years with AATD genotypes Pi*ZZ, Pi*NN or other rare genotypes associated with known risk of lung disease. Unlike ASTRAEUS, patients with the Pi*SZ genotype and those on augmentation were included. Key exclusion criteria were lung disease other than COPD, chronic liver disease, immunodeficiency disorders or recent cardiovascular events. Eligibility criteria are detailed in supplementary table E1b.

Primary efficacy end-point: within-group percentage change in desmosine from baseline at week 12.

Secondary efficacy end-points: change from baseline in serum NE activity, plasma Aα-Val360, and plasma biomarkers of inflammation and proteases at week 12.

Exploratory end-points: spirometry, patient-reported outcomes (SGRQ-C and COPD Assessment Test (CAT)), modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea score and Shortness of Breath Questionnaire (SOBQ)).

The supplementary material lists all end-points (supplementary table E2b) and biomarker methods (supplementary table E3).

Following ASTRAEUS read-out showing limited effect of the 120 mg dose, the ATALANTa protocol was amended allowing recruitment of patients on augmentation, to investigate the effects of alvelestat in combination.

The study was powered for within-group percentage change from baseline in plasma desmosine/isodesmosine at 12 weeks. A sample size of 30 per group provided >90% power to detect a 15% difference using a two-sided paired t-test at α=0.05. The Full Analysis Set (subjects receiving at least one dose of study drug, with a post-baseline desmosine result) was used, with a prespecified analysis by augmentation status. The Safety Set comprised all patients who received at least one dose of study drug. Detailed statistical methods are outlined in supplementary table E4.

Safety assessments in both studies were adverse events, monitoring of laboratory parameters, vital signs and ECGs. Adverse events of special interest were infections requiring antimicrobial therapy, liver function changes, ECG abnormalities and neutropenia (supplementary table E5).

Aligned statistical methods were used for the studies. The mixed model for repeated measures was used to generate least squares means (LSM) and LSM differences between groups. No adjustment for multiplicity was made. LSM±se are presented unless stated otherwise.

The study was conducted across 26 sites in Canada, Europe, UK and USA (supplementary table E6). Between October 2018 and March 2022, 189 patients were screened; 99 eligible individuals were randomised to alvelestat 120 mg (n=22), alvelestat 240 mg (n=40) or matched placebo (n=36) twice daily. Of the 99 randomised participants, one withdrew consent prior to dosing. 22 participants discontinued; eight out of 22 (36%) from the 120 mg group, 11 out of 40 (27.5%) from the 240 mg group and three out of 36 (8.3%) from the placebo group (supplementary figure E3a). 11 withdrawals were a result of COVID-19 restrictions. Demographic and baseline characteristics were similar between groups (table 1, and supplementary tables E7 and E8) and as expected in AATD, apart from a female predominance.

Alvelestat 120 mg reduced serum NE in non-augmentation users (−19.6±4.9 ng·mL−1; p=0.0013). Similar effects were observed in participants using augmentation, with a numerical reduction of −14.9±6.9 ng·mL−1 (p=0.058). Neither were significant compared to placebo (p=0.185 on augmentation and p=0.33 non-augmentation).

There was no significant percentage change in Aα-Val360 from baseline compared to placebo by augmentation status (supplementary table E13a).

Alvelestat 120 mg had no effect on desmosine/isodesmosine by augmentation status (supplementary table E13b).

There were no consistent changes in proteinase 3, cathepsin B or inflammatory parameters (supplementary table E14).

However, in exploratory analyses in the subpopulation not receiving augmentation therapy, we observed numerical improvements in the SGRQ-C Total score of −4.7±2.8 points (p=0.10) and the SGRQ-C Activity domain score of −10.21±3.75 points (p=0.011) at 12 weeks of alvelestat 120 mg compared to placebo.

Headache was the most frequent adverse event (supplementary table E15), 15 events occurring in 32 participants on alvelestat compared to seven of 31 on placebo, with two discontinuations in the alvelestat group (table 3). COPD exacerbations were the second most frequent event, occurring more often on placebo (n=7 events) compared to alvelestat (n=1 event). Infection events were also more frequent on placebo (n=12 events compared to n=6 events on alvelestat). There were no events affecting liver or QTc. No serious adverse events were reported.

We report our experience from two, coordinated phase 2, randomised, placebo-controlled trials in 161 participants with severe AATD, using a biomarker-targeted approach to test the hypothesis that an oral inhibitor of NE may be an effective therapeutic strategy in this population.

The on-target effect of alvelestat was confirmed; both the 120 and 240 mg doses significantly inhibited serum NE activity by over 80% and 90%, respectively, with 240 mg being more effective in suppressing NE below the limit of detection. NE activity biomarkers also demonstrated a dose effect, with only the 240 mg dose reducing desmosine/isodesmosine and Aα-Val360 compared to placebo. The lack of effect of 120 mg on disease activity biomarkers in the face of significant suppression of serum NE likely reflects the requirement for higher lung concentrations, considered the main site generating Aα-Val360 [15] and desmosine/isodesmosine [22]. Sputum alvelestat levels are lower than in plasma. Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analysis predicts the 240 mg dose is required to achieve the target concentration for NE suppression of 300 nM in the lung [13]. There may be other possibilities to explain the lack of disease-activity biomarker response for the 120 mg dose of alvelestat, including non-linear dose–response effects or a compensatory negative feedback activation of the NE-mediated inflammatory pathway; these can be explored in future studies.

The clinical translatability of the biomarker effects has yet to be tested. However, Aα-Val360 and desmosine directly associate with lung function, CT density and respiratory health status in AATD [15, 16, 18, 20–22]. It is encouraging that the effect on these biomarkers at 240 mg was favourable to that observed with augmentation, with reduction in desmosine/isodesmosine of −0.028 ng·mL−1 at 12 weeks on alvelestat compared to −0.013 ng·mL−1 at 12 weeks on augmentation [20] and Aα-Val360 reduction of 4.8 nM at week 12 compared to a reduction from 10.63 nM at baseline to 8.53 nM after 6 months of augmentation [21].

AAT is the physiological inhibitor of two serine proteases, NE and proteinase 3, implicated in the pathogenesis of lung disease in AATD [10, 24, 25]. Alvelestat selectively inhibits NE, yet appears to have a similar effect on elastin breakdown as augmentation therapy, which affects both NE and proteinase 3. It is possible that NE is the predominant driver for elastolysis, as elastin has more NE binding sites, which dissociates less easily than proteinase 3 [26]. Alvelestat's inhibition of NE may also liberate the small amounts of AAT present in most patients with severe AATD to inhibit proteinase 3, as demonstrated in an ex vivo experimental model [27]. However, we did not observe a reduction in plasma proteinase 3 amount in the present studies using immunoassays, although future studies could use more specific tests of activity such as Aα-Val541 [24].

We made interesting observations from the subgroup analysis based on augmentation status in the ATALANTa study. Participants who were not receiving augmentation had less severe airflow obstruction and showed the clearest improvements in patient-reported outcomes. These findings are hypothesis generating, raising the potential benefit of intervention prior to significant respiratory impairment as a therapeutic strategy [28]. The post-hoc finding of an association between improvement in SGRQ-C and reduction in biomarkers supports a mechanistic effect through NE suppression. Improvements in SGRQ-C and other patient-reported outcomes may have utility as efficacy end-points, although these findings need further testing in a prospective manner. The lack of effect on lung function is not surprising in a 12-week trial, the relatively short follow-up being insufficient to determine long-term disease progression as reflected in FEV1 decline in AATD [8].

The safety profile of alvelestat was consistent with previous experience [13, 29, 30]. Selective inhibition of NE has not to date increased infection risk. Alvelestat has rarely been associated with reversible increases in liver transaminases. Patients with Pi*ZZ AATD are susceptible to liver disease because of sequestered misfolded AAT [31], and it is reassuring that no adverse trends in liver function were observed. Adverse events were more frequent at the 240 mg dose, driven by headache. Headache may result from off-target inhibition of the adenosine transporter [12], causing adenosine accumulation and cerebral vasodilation [32–34]. Experience from drugs with a similar mechanism has shown dose escalation to be effective [34]. Ongoing alvelestat studies support that a starting dose of 60 mg enables successful dose escalation to 240 mg [35].

The strengths of the research were the robustness of double-blind, placebo-controlled designs. The aligned treatment periods and eligibility criteria (outside of planned differences to expand the populations assessed) enabled comparison between the studies. This provided complementary data in a wider range of genotypes and exploration of alvelestat in combination with augmentation than would have been possible in a single study.

The limitations include the relatively small sample sizes to detect meaningful differences in secondary outcomes, although the results will be informative for phase 3 studies. Additionally, there were no statistical adjustments for multiplicity and the results should be treated with appropriate caution. A high dropout rate occurred in ASTRAEUS and results for 12-week completers were used, with risk of bias.

A biomarker-based strategy was utilised to investigate mechanistic proof of concept, dose finding and safety of alvelestat through two complementary 12-week trials in patients with severe AATD. Alvelestat achieved target engagement with >90% suppression of serum NE at 240 mg. The magnitude of change in disease-related activity biomarkers was comparable to that reported for augmentation therapy, suggesting relevance for alvelestat in AATD. These paired studies demonstrate the potential of workable, short-term clinical trials in a rare disease. The efficacy data support progression of alvelestat 240 mg twice daily into clinical end-point trials. The side-effect of headache at time of drug initiation is expected to be managed through a dose-escalation approach.
