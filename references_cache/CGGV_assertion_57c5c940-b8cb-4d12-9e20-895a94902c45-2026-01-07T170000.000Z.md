# CGGV:assertion_57c5c940-b8cb-4d12-9e20-895a94902c45-2026-01-07T170000.000Z  MYCN / megalencephaly-polydactyly syndrome

**CGGV:assertion_57c5c940-b8cb-4d12-9e20-895a94902c45-2026-01-07T170000.000Z** - MYCN / megalencephaly-polydactyly syndrome (Moderate)

## Evidence summary

The MYCN gene is located on chromosome 2 at 2p24.3 and encodes MYCN , a basic helix–loop–helix leucine zipper (bHLH-LZ) transcription factor belonging to the MYC family. MYCN plays a critical role during embryonic development, particularly in the developing nervous system, where it regulates neural progenitor proliferation, cell-cycle progression, ribosome biogenesis, metabolism, and neuronal migration. MYCN was initially associated with Feingold syndrome, an autosomal dominant disorder caused by MYCN loss-of-function variants, characterised by microcephaly, digital anomalies, gastrointestinal atresia, and intellectual disability. More recently, gain-of-function (GoF) variants in MYCN have been identified as the cause of a distinct megalencephaly–polydactyly syndrome, which represents a mirror phenotype to Feingold syndrome. This condition is characterised by megalencephaly or macrocephaly, postaxial polydactyly, cortical thickening, abnormal neuronal migration, developmental delay, and congenital anomalies.

Nishio et al. (2023, PMID: 37710961) and Kato et al. (2019, PMID: 30573562) independently reported affected individuals with heterozygous MYCN missense variants clustering within the N-terminal phosphodegron region, including c.173C>T (p.Thr58Met) and c.179C>T (p.Pro60Leu). Five missense variants that have been reported in 5 probands in 2 publications (PMIDs: 37710961, 30573562) and ClinVar (SCV006277950.1, SCV005391319.1) are included in this curation.

MYCN protein levels are tightly controlled through phosphorylation-dependent ubiquitin–proteasome degradation, primarily mediated by sequential phosphorylation at Ser62 and Thr58 and recognition by the SCF-FBW7 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. Functional studies in non-patient cells demonstrated significantly reduced Thr58 phosphorylation and increased MYCN stability for both p.Thr58Met and p.Pro60Leu compared with wild-type MYCN , while transcriptional activation of canonical MYCN targets such as CCND1 and CCND2 was preserved or enhanced. These findings support a gain-of-function disease mechanism driven by prolonged MYCN activity rather than increased intrinsic transcriptional potency. Additional clinical support comes from individuals with nearby missense variants affecting the same regulatory region, including p.Pro44Thr and p.Pro60Ser, reported in ClinVar and diagnostic laboratories, with overlapping phenotypes of macrocephaly, developmental delay, and craniofacial anomalies. Although functional data are limited for these variants, their clustering within the degradation-regulatory domain supports a shared pathogenic mechanism. Experimental evidence further supports this gene–disease relationship including biochemical function, expression, animal model, and functional alterations in non-patient cells (PMIDs: 28969385, 24705253, 30573562, 37710961)

In summary, there is moderate evidence to support this gene–disease relationship. While more evidence is needed to establish this relationship definitively, no convincing contradictory evidence has emerged.
