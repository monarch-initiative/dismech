Acute flaccid myelitis (AFM) is a serious neurological illness first recognized
in the United States in 2014, with subsequent outbreaks every two years.
Following extensive etiologic testing by multiple laboratories of hundreds of
specimens collected from patients diagnosed with AFM, no consistent cause of AFM
has been identified. However, viruses, including enteroviruses, have been
implicated through detection in non-sterile site specimens and antibody studies.
Cytokines and chemokines play important roles in the modulation of the innate
and adaptive immune response to pathogens. In the current study, we measured
levels of cytokines and chemokines in serum and CSF collected from confirmed AFM
patients and non-AFM control patients, to identify unique biomarkers as
potential hallmarks of AFM pathogenesis. Analysis of ratios of cytokines and
chemokines in the CSF compared to the serum indicate that the pro-inflammatory
cytokines/chemokines IP-10 and IL-6 were significantly elevated in AFM patients
compared to non-AFM patients. These results may provide additional insight into
potential etiologies, pathogenic mechanisms, and treatments for AFM.

First recognized in 2014, acute flaccid myelitis (AFM) is a severe neurological illness in which patients display acute flaccid limb weakness in one or more limbs. Clinical cases are defined by the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists as ‘confirmed’ when magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrates predominantly gray matter lesions spanning at least one or more segments of the spinal cord, and lesions extending to multiple levels are common [1]. During the 2014 outbreak, 120 patients in 34 states were confirmed as having AFM [3,4], with a median patient age of 7.1 years. In these cases, fever (64%) or respiratory symptoms (81%) often preceded limb weakness [3,4]. Additional outbreaks occurred in 2016 and 2018, with 153 and 238 confirmed cases, respectively [4–6]. During the most recent outbreak in 2018, median patient age was 5.3 years and 96% of patients had symptoms consistent with a viral illness in the week before limb weakness onset [6]. Extensive etiologic testing of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), stool, serum, and respiratory specimens from AFM patients has not identified a consistent cause for AFM. However, enteroviruses (EV) have been detected in non-sterile site specimens (nasopharyngeal swabs and stool) from almost 50% of cases, suggesting viral involvement in some stage of the pathogenesis of AFM [3,4,6–8]. In addition, two different peptide array approaches have demonstrated higher frequency of enterovirus antibodies in CSF of AFM patients compared to control patients, though the controls were not well-matched and additional confirmatory studies are needed [7,8].

Cytokines and chemokines are a diverse group of intercellular immune signaling molecules with roles in cell trafficking, immune surveillance, and regulation of inflammatory responses in nearly all cell types in the body. Pattern recognition receptors such as toll-like receptors, nod-like receptors, and RIG-like receptors, are critical to the innate immune response to injury and infection, typically resulting in the release of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines [9]. While cytokines can play an important role in infection control and tissue repair, they can also induce cell death through activation of apoptosis and other receptor-mediated processes, thus playing a role in disease pathogenesis.

Local production of cytokines in cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) has been observed to increase significantly after CNS injury, in autoimmune disorders, and in viral infections. During the neuroinflammatory response to spinal cord injuries, interleukin (IL)-8, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IL-1β, IL-10 and IL-6 are produced systemically [10]. Cytokines have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune disorders involving the CNS, including multiple sclerosis (MS) and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM). Elevated levels of TNF-α, interferon (IFN)-γ, and IL-10 in CSF of MS patients were shown to be associated with clinical exacerbations but were reduced during clinical remission [11]. In CSF from MS patients, levels of MIP-1β, IL-6, and CXCL10 (IP-10) have also been observed to be significantly higher compared to control patients with non-inflammatory neurological disease [12]. Patients with ADEM have been shown to express elevated levels of many cytokines in CSF including TNF-α, IP-10, IL-4, G-CSF, IL-6 and IL-1RA [13]. Conversely, reduced levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 have been associated with an improved neurological outcome after spinal cord injury (SCI) [14–16]. West Nile virus infections can induce IL-1β and TNF-α, which can cause neurotoxicity depending the levels produced and course of expression [17]. A recent study analyzing CSF samples from patients with enteroviral meningitis reported high levels of IL-2, IL-6, and IL-8 when compared to CSF from non-enteroviral meningitis cases, as well as high levels of IL-1β, IL-10, interferon-γ, and TNF-α in those patients demonstrating CSF pleocytosis [18].

In this study, we examined the expression profile of cytokines and chemokines in serum and CSF collected from confirmed AFM and non- AFM patients, to identify unique biomarkers as potential hallmarks of AFM pathogenesis. Elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines in CSF unique to confirmed AFM cases was identified. These unique biomarkers could not be solely attributed to general limb weakness or diagnosis of enterovirus infection of the central nervous system.

Cases included in this study were submitted to CDC as potential AFM cases or for laboratory testing of suspected Enterovirus infections. Patients meeting the clinical case criteria for AFM (acute onset of flaccid limb weakness) were classified using the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists case definitions of “confirmed” (magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with spinal cord lesions largely restricted to gray matter and spanning ≥1 spinal segment), “probable” (CSF pleocytosis, > 5 white blood cells per mm3), and “not a case” (non-AFM) (Table 1) [19]. We tested the paired CSF and sera of 70 confirmed AFM patients and 47 patients classified as non-AFM based on MRI and chart review to quantify levels for a panel of cytokines and chemokines. Cases classified as probable were excluded. All specimens were stored at − 80 °C until they were tested. The study protocol was reviewed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Human Research Protection Office and determined that CDC was not engaged.

Cytokines were detected and quantified using the Milliplex 38-plex human cytokine and chemokine magnetic bead assay (Supplementary Table 1) following the manufacturer’s recommended methods (EMD Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA). Specimens were vortexed for 10 s and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min prior to testing and all samples were run in duplicate.

Cytokine and chemokine levels in samples from patients included in this study were analyzed according to three patient classifications: 1) confirmed AFM versus non-AFM, 2) Limb weakness (patient exhibited some form of limb weakness that was suspected to be AFM) versus No limb weakness, and 3) enterovirus/rhinovirus PCR-positive (EV/RV- Positive) and EV/RV-negative (typical real-time RT-PCR tests cannot distinguish between the closely related enteroviruses and rhinoviruses, so the results are often reported as EV/RV-positive or EV/RV-negative). The concentration values for each cytokines were transformed to the log scale to facilitate statistical analysis of differences in cytokine levels between groups [20]. Concentration values at the limits of detections were assigned the limit of detection concentration for analysis and subsequent CSF:serum ratio calculations. Intrathecal synthesis was inferred using a ratio of a cytokine concentration in CSF to the serum. Outlier analysis was performed on the time intervals between CSF and serum collection to eliminate patients with long gaps between CSF and serum sample collections from intrathecal analysis. Normality test was performed on the data and, because the assumption of normal distribution was violated, we analyzed the data using a nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test in the SAS NPAR1WAY procedure. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.2 (SAS Institute), with p-value < 0.05 considered significant.

The patient metadata are summarized in Table 1. The cases included in the analysis of cytokines and chemokines had onset dates of clinical symptom presentation ranging from 2001 to 2018, with the largest proportion of patients having a reported onset of clinical symptoms in 2016 (n = 45, 44%). Cases included in our analysis with onset dates before 2014, are all non-AFM cases. For patients categorized into AFM or non-AFM included in our analysis, serum and CSF specimens were collected a median of 3.5 (AFM) and 5 days (non-AFM) (p = 0.0110) and 7.4 (AFM) and 8.1 days (non-AFM) (p = 0.0690) post-onset of clinical symptom presentation, respectively. The average age at onset for patients included in our analysis was 10.4 and 12.7 years for the AFM and non-AFM cases, respectively (p = 0.8790). For patients categorized into Limb weakness or No limb weakness included in our analysis, serum and CSF specimens were collected a median of 4.8 (Limb weakness) and 4.2 days (No limb weakness) (p = 0.7430) and 6.2 (Limb weakness) and 4.8 days (No limb weakness) (p = 0.4350) post-onset of clinical symptom presentation, respectively. Ages of patients with no limb weakness were not available. For patients categorized into EV/RV-positive and EV/RV- negative included in our analysis, serum and CSF specimens were collected a median of 3.7 (EV/RV-positive) and 5.1 days (EV/RV- negative) (p = 0.1290) and 5.4 (EV-RV positive) and 6.3 days (EV-RV negative) (p = 0.6110) post-onset of clinical symptom presentation, respectively. The average age at onset patients included in our analysis was 7.8 and 12.0 years for the EV/RV-positive and EV/RV-negative cases, respectively (p = 0.2260). The interval between the serum and CSF specimen collections ranged from 0 to 20 days for all patients. Patients with serum and CSF collected 5 days or more from one another were excluded from intrathecal analysis. No significant differences were observed in the collection intervals for all comparisons.

Cytokines unique to either AFM or non-AFM patients were identified in serum and CSF. Both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines were measured at significantly different levels in the CSF of confirmed AFM cases compared to non-AFM cases (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 2). Levels of IL-10, IL-1RA, IL-5, IL-6, sCD40 L and IL-7 were significantly higher in CSF from confirmed AFM patients compared to that of non- AFM patients. However, TGF-α and TNF-β concentrations were significantly lower in CSF from confirmed AFM patients as compared to that of non-AFM patients. In serum, IL-15, IL-5, IFN-γ, MCP-3, IL-12p40, IL- 12p70, IL-13, sCD40 L, IL-17A, IL-1α, IL-2, IL-7, and TNF-β were expressed at higher levels in confirmed AFM cases compared to non- AFM cases (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 3). No cytokines were identified as uniquely elevated in the serum of non-AFM cases compared to confirmed AFM cases.

To determine if the observed cytokine expression signature was unique to AFM patients, cases were re-categorized into limb weakness (which includes confirmed AFM cases and cases exhibiting some form of limb weakness that did not meet the strict AFM case definition), and non-limb weakness cases, which included encephalitis, aseptic meningitis, or other neurological illnesses not associated with limb weakness (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Table 3). For patients with limb weakness, CSF had statistically significantly higher levels of IL-5, MCP-3, IL-13, sCD40 L, and IL-7 compared to patients with no limb weakness. In serum, IL-10, IL-1RA, IL-5, FGF-2, GM-CSF, IFN-A2, IFN-γ, MCP-3, IL-12p40, IL-12p70, IL-13, sCD40 L, IL-17A, IL-1α, IL-9, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-7 and TGF-α were higher in limb weakness cases compared to patients with no limb weakness.

Because recent peaks of AFM incidence have correlated temporally with “enterovirus season” (Summer-Fall in the United States), we categorized the AFM and non-AFM cases into those that were EV/RV PCR- positive or PCR-negative [21,22] to determine if the cytokine expression profile differed based on virus positivity (Fig. 1C). For cases positive for EV/RV, the CSF had significantly higher levels of Eotaxin, G-CSF, IL-10, IL-6, IL-8, IP-10, MCP-1, GM-CSF, fractalkine, GRO, and IL-4 compared to patients who were negative for EV/RV (Supplementary Table 2). In the serum, IP-10 was significantly higher in EV/RV-positive cases compared to EV/RV-negative cases (p = 0.0226), while fractalkine and IL-7 levels were significantly lower in the EV/RV-positive cases compared to the EV/RV-negative cases (Supplementary Table 3) (p = 0.03 and p = 0.0336, respectively).

Intrathecal synthesis of cytokines was inferred for AFM and non-AFM patients by comparing the ratio of CSF concentration to serum concentration, with a ratio of 1 used as the baseline value and values greater than 1 indicative of intrathecal synthesis (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4). Patients with outlier collection intervals were excluded from analysis. The CSF:serum cytokine ratio IP-10 (p = 0.0017) was significantly elevated in AFM cases compared to non-AFM cases, while IL-5 (p = 0.0038), IL-12p70 (p = 0.0189), IL-12p40 (p = 0.021), IL-13 (p = 0.0135), sCD40 L (p = 0.0012), IL-17A (p = 0.0113), IL-2 (p = 0.0008) and TNF-β (p = 0.0003) were significantly lower in AFM cases compared to non-AFM cases.

Intrathecal synthesis of cytokines was analyzed in cases categorized as limb weakness and non-limb weakness cases, which included encephalitis, aseptic meningitis, or other neurological illnesses not associated with limb weakness (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 4). The CSF: serum cytokine ratio of IL-3 (p = .0.0021) was significantly elevated in limb weakness cases compared to no limb weakness cases, while IL-15 (p = .01050), IL-5 (p <0.0001), GM-CSF (p = 0.0128), MCP-3 (p = 0.0717), IL-12p40 (p = 0.0020), IL-13 (p = 0.0180), sCD40 L (p <0.0001), IL-17A (p <0.0001), IL-1α (p = 0.0174), IL-9 (p <0.0001), IL-2 (p <0.0001), and TNF-β (p = 0.0003) were significantly lower in limb weakness cases than in cases with no limb weakness. These analyses suggest that high levels of intrathecal synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokine IP-10 are unique to cases of AFM, and not more broadly attributable to other conditions associated with limb weakness.

Intrathecal synthesis of cytokines was also compared in EV/RV- positive and EV/RV-negative patients (Fig. 4). CSF:serum cytokine ratios were significantly higher for IL-8 (p = 0.0072), MCP-1 (p = 0.0160), FLT-3 L (p = 0.0341), fractalkine (p = 0.0051), IFN-γ (p = 0.0381), GRO (p = 0.0142), and IL-7 (p = 0.0234) in EV/RV-positive cases compared to EV/RV-negative cases. There were no cytokines whose CSF:serum ratios were significantly lower in EV/RV-positive cases compared to EV/ RV-negative cases.

In the AFM and non-AFM patients analyzed, 19 of the 38 cytokines were expressed at higher levels in the CSF and serum of AFM patients while only 2 were expressed at lower levels in the CSF of AFM patients when compared to non-AFM patients (and none at lower levels in serum). In the current study, we measured cytokine expression levels in CSF and serum samples from confirmed AFM patients and compared them to those of patients with other neurological diseases (i.e. non-AFM) to determine whether there were differences between the two groups in cytokine response within the CNS.

This study reports several cytokines that were observed to be increased in the CSF of AFM-positive patients compared to non-AFM cases which was unique to those samples positive for EV/RV and limb weakness. Elevated cytokine expression in CSF from patients with enteroviral meningitis with pleocytosis have shown increased levels of IL-6, IL8, IL-10, and IFN- γ [18], while other studies have shown high levels of IFN- γ and IL-6 in the CSF of patients with EV-A71 brainstem encephalitis, autonomic nervous system dysregulation and pulmonary edema [23]. However, those studies did not indicate if cytokines were produced intrathecally. Along with IL-6 and IP-10, IL-8 and MCP-1 were observed to be increased in the CSF of patients with encephalitis associated with aseptic (viral) meningitis, acute bacterial meningitis, and autoimmune etiologies, suggesting that while IL-8 and MCP-1 have increased production in the CNS, they may not be specific to infectious etiologies [24]. In our analysis, the CSF:serum ratios for the pro-inflammatory cytokine IP-10 was significantly higher level in samples from AFM patients compared to non-AFM patients. In the central nervous system, IP-10 has been associated with acute aseptic meningitis induced by viral infections [24,25]. Fortuna et al. showed that the expression of IP-10 in CSF is significantly higher in patients with viral and bacterial infections of the CNS such as West Nile Virus, enterovirus, HIV and tuberculosis [26]. In the CNS, IL-6 is produced at low levels under normal physiological conditions [27]. However, increases in IL-6 expression in the CNS are associated with neurological disorders including autoimmune diseases such as neuromyelitis optica [28,29]. Elevation of IL-6 in CSF relative to serum levels has been observed with viral infection of the CNS, including with enterovirus A71 [30] and Zika virus [31].

Our analysis of CSF and serum samples collected from patients during the AFM investigation had several limitations. The timing of sample collection from suspected AFM cases has not been optimal, as suspected AFM cases are communicated to public health authorities approximately 18 days after the initial event that triggered paralysis [6]. In addition, concurrent sampling of CSF and serum was a challenge; samples used in this analysis were convenience samples collected for etiological testing, such as viral PCR tests. Ratios of concurrent CSF to serum cytokine and chemokine levels were used to infer intrathecal synthesis; it is possible that elevated systemic levels of cytokines and chemokines in both CSF and serum could mask intrathecal synthesis. Paired CSF and serum samples submitted to CDC for enterovirus diagnostics were used as a control group, in addition to samples submitted from patients under investigation for AFM who were later determined to be non-AFM cases based on the strict case definition. Therefore, controls were not necessarily well-matched for age, gender, or potential exposures. Future investigations will include prospective testing to accumulate additional controls and include retrospective specimens to address these shortcomings. An ongoing natural history study, sponsored by NIH, could address the timing of sample collection limitation by ensuring collection close to the onset date and concurrent CSF and serum collection [32].

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study reporting cytokines as potential biomarkers of clinical cases of AFM. Such biomarkers may provide an additional parameter for diagnosing suspected AFM cases, possibly through a multiplex ELISA or other simplified assay format. Identification of pattern recognition receptors responsible for the elevated cytokine or chemokine response in the central nervous system could provide insight into a potential pathogenic mechanism for AFM. In addition, these cytokine biomarkers could be potential drug targets using monoclonal antibodies to treat confirmed AFM patients by modulating the activity of inflammatory cytokines in the central nervous system; however, this should be considered with caution due to the potential for immune suppression and increased susceptibility to infection [33–36].
