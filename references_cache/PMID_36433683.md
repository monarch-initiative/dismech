Deletion of 17p13.3 has varying degrees of severity on brain development based 
on precise location and size of the deletion. The most severe phenotype is 
Miller-Dieker syndrome (MDS) which is characterized by lissencephaly, dysmorphic 
facial features, growth failure, developmental disability, and often early 
death. Haploinsufficiency of PAFAH1B1 is responsible for the characteristic 
lissencephaly in MDS. The precise role of YWHAE haploinsufficiency in MDS is 
unclear. Case reports are beginning to elucidate the phenotypes of individuals 
with 17p13.3 deletions that have deletion of YWHAE but do not include deletion 
of PAFAH1B1. Through our clinical genetics practice, we identified four 
individuals with 17p13.3 deletion that include YWHAE but not PAFAH1B1. These 
patients have a similar phenotype of dysmorphic facial features, developmental 
delay, and leukoencephalopathy. In a review of the literature, we identified 19 
patients with 17p13.3 microdeletion sparing PAFAH1B1 but deleting YWHAE. 
Haploinsufficiency of YWHAE is associated with brain abnormalities including 
cystic changes. These individuals have high frequency of epilepsy, intellectual 
disability, and dysmorphic facial features including prominent forehead, 
epicanthal folds, and broad nasal root. We conclude that deletion of 17p13.3 
excluding PAFAH1B1 but including YWHAE is associated with a consistent phenotype 
and should be considered a distinct condition from MDS.

The human genome includes structural alterations such as duplications, deletions, inversions, and insertions which allow for genetic heterogeneity (Pinto et al., 2007). The consequence of these alterations may cause differing abnormal phenotypes resulting in a recognizable syndrome. With microdeletions, the phenotype is the result of multiple genes being absent; however, the contribution of the loss of each individual gene to the phenotype is not fully understood.

Miller–Dieker syndrome (MDS) is a rare disorder described in 1963 and 1969 as classical four‐layered lissencephaly, microcephaly, epilepsy, growth failure, developmental delay, and dysmorphic features which include micrognathia, thin upper lip, and high forehead (Schwartz et al., 1988). Over the decades, genetic studies have revealed that MDS is caused by a recurrent chromosome 17p13.3 microdeletion including YWHAE, PAFAH1B1 (also known as LIS1), CRK, MYO1C, and ABR which is associated with variable phenotype (or expression). Classic lissencephaly is a neuronal migration condition causing absent or reduced gyration of the cerebrum, known as agyria and pachygyria, respectively. Patients with lissencephaly have severe global developmental delay, intellectual disability, intractable epilepsy, and reduced lifespan (Di Donato et al., 2017). Haploinsufficiency of PAFAH1B1 is the most common Mendelian etiology of lissencephaly (Iefremova et al., 2017). PAFAH1B1 encodes for LIS1 which impacts cell division and motility in many periods of neurodevelopment (Fry et al., 2014).

Nagamani et al. (2009) reported five patients with 17p13.3 deletion involving YWHAE but not PAFAH1B1 revealing a different phenotype of dysmorphic features (prominent forehead, broad nasal root, epicanthal folds, and retrognathia), developmental delay, and growth restriction. These patients did not have the characteristic lissencephaly noted previously with MDS. With retention of PAFAH1B1, researchers could evaluate the role of YWHAE copy loss in the phenotype. Since this publication, at least 14 additional cases have been reported with 17p13.3 microdeletion involving YWHAE but excluding PAFAH1B1 (Table 1). Many questions have been raised regarding the role of each gene in this region and how it affects the phenotype.

Here, we report the phenotype of four individuals with chromosome 17p13.3 deletion including YWHAE but sparing PAFAH1B1. Two of these individuals were previously described and are here documented after an 11‐year follow up (Tenney et al., 2011). Because these two patients are now 16‐years‐old and have been followed since they were neonates, we are able to evaluate the natural history to adolescence for YWHAE haploinsufficiency. These four patients, together with the 19 previously reported patients, allow the phenotype of 17p13.3 deletion including YWHAE but sparing PAFAH1B1 to be expanded and confirmed as a distinct entity compared to MDS.

All the individuals were diagnosed with 17p13.3 microdeletion syndrome with PAFAH1B1 spared via chromosomal microarray. The families of these individuals provided consents for publication including photographs.

Individuals 1–3 were diagnosed with 17p13.3 microdeletion syndrome by microarray analysis at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center between 2008 and 2018. Individual 1 was analyzed in April 2018 using the Illumina CytoSNP‐850Kv1.1 BeadChip platform. This chip contains approximately 843,390 genome‐wide markers. Individuals 2 and 3 were analyzed in April 2010 and October 2008, respectively, using the Illumina Human610‐Quad v1.0 DNA Analysis BeadChip platform. This chip contained approximately 610,000 SNP markers and additional copy number variation (CNV) content. Microarray analysis was performed with the Illumina Infinium® Assay as described by the manufacturer (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). B‐allele frequency and log2
R ratio were analyzed with Illumina GenomeStudio analysis software. Individual 4 had microarray analysis performed in July 2018 by an outside laboratory (ARUP, Salt Lake City, UT, USA), using an Affymetrix oligo‐SNP array (Cytoscan).

Individual 1 is a 4‐year‐old female who was admitted at 6 weeks of age due to jaundice. She was born to a 22‐year‐old G2P1011 female at 37 weeks 6 days, weighing 2.52 kg (5%), and measuring 44.45 cm long (<2%). Her mother had a spontaneous abortion a year prior to the patient's birth. During initial evaluation, the individual was mildly dysmorphic with a triangular face, thin upper lip, and micrognathia. Due to the individual's dysmorphic features, weight loss, hypotonia, and jaundice, a SNP microarray was ordered which revealed a 1.4 Mb deletion of chromosome 17p13.3. Familial studies were performed which confirmed the deletion was de novo. Her deletion encompasses part of the MDS critical region but not the entire region omitting 26 Online Mendelian Inheritance of Man (OMIM) genes including YWHAE and CRK but not PAFAHB1 (Figure 2).

Due to her micrognathia and parental report of snoring, a sleep study was performed which revealed moderate obstructive sleep apnea for which she started on nocturnal oxygen. She then underwent a supraglottoplasty at 7 months of age treating her obstructive sleep apnea.

In genetics clinic at 2 years and 4 months, her weight was 19.8 kg (99%), height was 85 cm (22%), and head circumference 50.6 cm (97%). She was dysmorphic with broad forehead, short palpebral fissures, mild telecanthus, tented upper lip, round face, and mild micrognathia (Figure 1a). From a developmental perspective, she has global developmental delay which has improved with weekly therapies. She remained mildly delayed but was able to communicate more effectively with approximately 20 words, walk without support (working on stairs which is more difficult due to her hypotonia), and drink with a sippy cup. She is enrolled in the early intervention program which has continued to assess her needs. Her cognitive ability has not been assessed. Individual 1's brain MRI performed at 2.5 years of age revealed confluent areas of T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the white matter of the periventricular regions and in the deep white matter (Figure 1b). This patient has had only one febrile seizure but no unprovoked seizures.

Individual 2 is a previously reported (Tenney et al., 2011) now 16‐year‐old female who was initially seen in genetics clinic at 1 year 2 months for global developmental delay, short stature, and poor weight gain. She was born to a 31‐year‐old G3P3 at 36 weeks gestation via induction due to poor fetal growth. Her birth weight was 2.069 kg (<1%). She required neonatal admission for 2 weeks due to feeding difficulties requiring tube feeds. Head ultrasound at birth was normal. At her genetics clinic visit at 14 months, she was 7.3 kg (<3%), length of 66.8 cm (<3%), and head circumference of 46 cm (50%). She was noted to have arched eyebrows, mildly prominent nose, mildly downturned corners of the mouth, deeply set eyes with bilateral epicanthic folds, increased soft tissue in the infraorbital region, and simple ears (Figure 1c). The remainder of the exam was notable for dimples on her elbows and knees as well as hypotonia and global developmental delay. She was only able to stand with support and unable to push up while prone. She was cooing but not babbling and was unable to do pincer grasp. Due to the global developmental delay, short stature, poor weight gain, and dysmorphic features, a karyotype was performed which revealed 46,XX,del(17)(p13.3). This result was confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis using a probe specific for the 17p subtelomere.

A brain MRI at 14 months was then performed revealing patchy confluent areas of T2 hyperintensity and T1 hypointensity within the central white matter (frontal, parietal, and occipital lobes) with sparing of subcortical white matter. There also was slight delay in age of myelination, mild to moderate lateral and third ventricular enlargement, and mild prominence of the extra‐axial CSF spaces (Figure 1e).

Three months later (17 months of age), she was noted to have seizures including startles and jerking, head drops, throwing her arms forward and shaking, and becoming pale and limp. EEG revealed generalized epileptiform discharges lasting 1–2 s. The patient had some head drops with arm extension during the tracing which correlated with the EEG findings. Keppra was initiated which decreased her seizure activity.

At 3 years 8 months, she continued to have seizures, hypotonia, and global developmental delay. Microarray analysis detected a terminal deletion of 2.3 Mb 17p13.3. Eight genes (ABR, YWHAE, CRK, MYO1C, SKIP, PITPNA, SCARF1, RILP, PRP8, and SERPIN1) were deleted; however, the PAFAHB1 gene was not affected (Figure 2).

At age 4 years 3 months, she was seen by endocrinology due to her short stature and poor weight gain. Bone age revealed an estimated age of 48 months (3.1 SD below the mean). She was started on growth hormone replacement for 2 years with minimal improvement of her growth. Her height and weight are still at <3%, and her head circumference remains at 50%.

From a neurological perspective, she has intractable seizures with her last EEG performed at 8 years of age. EEG revealed occasional burst of arrhythmic spikes and polyspikes with a left frontal predominance, and slow waves were noted independently in the left temporal and right temporal head regions lasting less than 1 s. She was on multiple anti‐seizure medications with minimal improvement of seizure control; therefore, she had a Vagal Nerve Stimulator placed at 10 years of age which decreased the number and severity of her seizures. Her brain MRI also at 10 years old had progressed and revealed extensive area of T2 and FLAIR hyperintensity throughout large portions of both cerebral hemispheres involving the deep, periventricular, and some areas of subcortical white matter. Furthermore, innumerable cystic spaces also are seen within the cerebral white matter (Figure 1f,g).

Developmentally, as a 16‐year‐old, she is in the ninth grade and has an Individualized Education Program in place. She is able to walk and run with a crouching gait. She is seen by physical medicine and rehabilitation clinic to help with stretching and splinting as she has mild hypertonia in her lower extremities. She has limited speech, cognitive abilities, and is followed by multiple subspecialties including neurology, genetics, developmental pediatrics, and physical medicine and rehabilitation.

Individual 3 is a previously reported (Tenney et al., 2011) now 16‐year‐old female who was initially seen in genetics clinic at 2 months of age for macrocephaly and ventriculomegaly. She was born to a 19‐year‐old G1P1 at 33 weeks gestation via vaginal delivery. Prenatal ultrasound revealed intrauterine growth restriction, ventriculomegaly, and a 2‐vessel cord. Her mother reported having an amniocentesis with normal karyotype. Her birth weight was 1.995 kg (<1%) and length was 46.5 cm (3%). She was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit secondary to feeding difficulties requiring tube feedings. Head ultrasound at 25 days of life revealed mild to moderate ventriculomegaly with concerns of resolving germinal matrix hemorrhage on the right and a tiny choroid plexus cyst. Due to a sacral dimple, a spinal ultrasound was performed which revealed a low lying conus medullaris of the sacral spine. She presented at 2‐months‐old to genetics clinic which revealed growth parameters of weight of 3.9 kg (10%), length of 51.3 cm (5%), and head circumference of 39.5 cm (98th%). Physical exam revealed dysmorphic features including prominent eyes with slight downslanting palpebral fissures, synophrys, slight triangular‐shaped nose, posteriorly rotated ears, small mouth, widely spaced nipples, sacral dimple, 5th finger clinodactyly, and mild hypotonia (Figure 1h,i). A karyotype was performed which revealed 46,XX at 650 band level. At 6 months, brain and spinal MRI revealed mild lateral and third ventricular enlargement with no signs of tethered cord. Her development at that time was appropriate.

At age 3 years old, she was noted to have seizures with approximately five episodes per day characterized by a brief head drop with occasional falls. EEG was performed showing generalized epileptiform discharges; she was started on Keppra. She had a decrease activity to approximately two drop seizures a day. Repeat brain MRI showed diffuse T2 hyperintensity with deep white matter and subependymal regions as well as continued lateral and third ventriculomegaly (Figure 1j). She also was noted to have developmental delay with walking at 18 months and limited speech. Therefore, an expanded microarray was performed which showed a 156–242 kb deletion at 4p16.3 and 1.8–1.9 Mb deletion at 17p13.3. The 4p16.3 deletion is thought to be benign as it has been found in phenotypically normal individuals. The 17p13.3 deletion includes both the YWHAE and CRK gene but not PAFAHB1 (Figure 2).

From a neurologic perspective, she continues to have intractable seizures on multiple anti‐seizure medications. Her last EEG at 11‐years‐old revealed epileptiform discharges seen in the central midline head region with diffuse spike/polyspike and wave discharges recorded. Furthermore, electroclinical tonic and myoclonic seizures were recorded which were not well localized. Epilepsy surgery was offered but her mother deferred surgery at that time. She has had multiple brain MRIs with the most recent at 12 years of age showing confluent areas of T2 and FLAIR hyperintensity involving the periventricular white matter of both cerebral hemispheres that are associated with many small cystic spaces (Figure 1k,l). She continues to have mild enlargement of third and lateral ventricles along with narrowing of the craniocervical junction. Spinal MRI at 15‐years‐old revealed small syrinx at the cervicothoracic junction, mild platyspondyly from C3 to C5, and low lying conus medullaris.

Individual 4 is a 9‐year‐old male who was seen at our genetics clinic for epilepsy and developmental delay. He was born to a 23‐year‐old G1P1 mother at 33 weeks 1 day with pregnancy complicated by alcohol and methamphetamine use. His birth weight was 1800 g (25%), length 41 cm (15%), and head circumference 32 cm (90%). He had a prolonged neonatal intensive care unit stay due to prematurity, respiratory distress syndrome, and hyperbilirubinemia. He was noted to have dysmorphic facial features including small palpebral fissures, flat midface, and flat nasal bridge. Family history is notable for his mother having learning concerns.

His seizures include intractable drug resistant absence seizures with eyelid myoclonus (Jeavons syndrome) with photosensitivity and generalized tonic clonic seizures requiring multiple anti‐seizure medications. He was noted to have seizure‐like activity at age 3‐years‐old which was complicated by history of motor vehicle accident with traumatic brain injury at 2 years 10 months. He had repeated EEGs performed at age 5 years and 8‐years‐old that revealed frequent bursts of spike and sharp wave activity arising independently from the left frontocentral and right frontocentral head regions with spread diffusely bilaterally, generalized epileptiform discharges as well as photo‐convulsive and photo‐paroxysmal responses. Brain MRI at 4‐years‐old and 8‐years‐old revealed small nonspecific gliosis in genu and body of corpus callosum, possibly representing white matter shear injury given the history of motor vehicle accident (Figure 1n,o). Furthermore, there was bilateral frontal white matter signal abnormality. MRI of lumbar spine revealed low‐lying conus medullaris terminating at the upper margin of L3.

From a developmental perspective, he has global developmental delay. He started walking at 18 months and speaking at 2 years. He has had neuropsychology testing revealing his full‐scale IQ in the upper 50 s. He receives speech, occupational, and physical therapy multiple times throughout the week. In our genetics clinic, he was noted to be dysmorphic with flat and broad nasal bridge, hypertelorism, and mildly arched palate (Figure 1m).

Due to his developmental delay, seizures, and macrocephaly, a microarray was performed revealing a likely pathogenic 140 kb deletion of 17p13.3 including YWHAE, TRARG1 (a.k.a.TUSC5), and BHLHA9, but not CRK or PAFAHB1 (Figure 2). No parental testing was performed to evaluate whether the deletion was inherited or de novo.

Copy number variants including deletions and duplications are connected to many genetic syndromes causing many symptoms and anomalies. MDS is one of these chromosomal deletion syndromes with a microdeletion of 17p13.3. It is associated with lissencephaly, epilepsy, microcephaly, growth failure, developmental delay, and dysmorphic features which include micrognathia, thin upper lip, and high forehead (Schwartz et al., 1988). Throughout the years, advancements have been made in the understanding of multiple genes in this microdeletion including YWHAE and PAFAH1B1 and how they impact the phenotype (Barros Fontes et al., 2017; Blazejewski et al., 2018; Bruno et al., 2010; Cardoso et al., 2003; Chen et al., 2018; Mignon‐Ravix et al., 2010; Romano et al., 2020; Schiff et al., 2010; Shimojima et al., 2010; Tenney et al., 2011). Here, we describe four individuals with 17p13.3 microdeletions that includes YWHAE with the retention of PAFAH1B1 further increasing the evidence that loss of YWHAE has detrimental effects on brain physiology.

Nagamani et al. (2009) described five patients with 17p13.3 microdeletions including deletion of YWHAE but not PAFAH1B1. Their phenotypes included dysmorphic facial features (prominent forehead, epicanthal folds, broad nasal root, and retrognathia), growth restriction, and developmental delay. Brain imaging on all these patients was abnormal with results revealing prominent Virchow‐Robin spaces and abnormal T2 weighted intensities in the periventricular regions in the deep white matter. Schiff et al. further reported four additional patients with white matter abnormalities, intellectual disability, and growth restrictions. The authors elucidated that a 285 kb region that includes YWHAE plays an integral role in this phenotype. Noor et al. also described a patient with only YWHAE affected presenting with seizures and abnormal brain MRI including Chiari 1 malformation and thin corpus callosum without white matter abnormalities providing evidence of how critical YWHAE is in brain development. Romano et al. presented a patient with a 730 kb deletion of 17p13.3 which deleted YWHAE and CRK. Phenotypically, the patient had dysmorphic facial features, developmental delay, infantile spasm, and T2 and FLAIR hyperintensities in subcortical regions of the brain. Results from our four individuals suggest haploinsufficiency of YWHAE may contribute to white matter abnormalities including leukoencephalopathy and cystic changes, likely representing dilated perivascular spaces. Interestingly, the spaces increased and became more prominent over time. Common dysmorphic facial features seen in these individuals include broad forehead, epicanthal folds, hypertelorism, broad nasal root, and tented upper lip as well as macrocephaly, global developmental delay, and intellectual disability. Furthermore, three out of the four cases had epilepsy requiring multiple anti‐seizure medications. These patients provide further evidence that YWHAE plays an impact on neuronal development and function.

Emrick et al. (2019) discussed six patients with 17p13.3 microdeletion that did not include YWHAE, CRK, or PAFAH1B1 resulting in white matter changes and dysmorphic facial features. These patients did not have intellectual disability, developmental delay, or seizures (Emrick et al., 2019). This phenotype suggests that a different set of genes may contribute to the white matter changes on brain MRI, while YWHAE affects brain development and function (Table 1 reviews the phenotypes).

Interestingly, loss‐of‐function (LoF) variants in YWHAE are underrepresented in healthy controls (gnomAD) and may not be tolerated in healthy individuals. In fact, gnomAD only contains three LoF point mutations, and they are all in the last exon of YWHAE, meaning they might escape nonsense‐mediated decay. The gnomAD “pLI” metric equals 0.98 for YWHAE which is high (>0.90), meaning that YWHAE LoF variants are restricted in healthy populations and are predicted to be pathogenic. On the other hand, the four genes distal to YWHAE (TRAG1, BHLHA9, ABR, and TIMM22) are not predicted to be haploinsufficient (Table 2). Therefore, 17p13.3 deletions encompassing YWHAE and no more than these four distal genes are predicted to be pathogenic due to haploinsufficiency of YWHAE alone. No similar deletions are found in healthy controls (DGV Gold Standard Variants) providing evidence of haploinsufficiency of YWHAE (Figure 1). Proximal to YWHAE lies CRK, a gene that is predicted to be haploinsufficient based on population data (Table 2). Therefore, deletions that extend proximally from YWHAE may have phenotypic effects related to the haploinsufficiency of CRK as well as that of YWHAE.

In vivo studies have started to elucidate the role of these genes in the phenotype. YWHAE encodes 14‐3‐3ɛ which is part of the 14‐3‐3 protein family. This group of proteins functions in a multitude of cellular processes including cell cycle control and signal transduction by hindering phosphatases and working with over 200 proteins through phosphothreonine and phosphoserine binding motifs. 14‐3‐3ɛ role in the brain is a negative regulator of neurite formation. It attaches to doublecortin (Dcx), a microtubule binding protein, through phosphorylation allowing for DCx levels to increase. This rise in DCx interrupts microtubule assembly in the lamellipodia which is the first stage of neurite formation. Therefore, a loss of YWHAE may result in a decrease in 14‐3‐3ɛ, which would change neurite formation in the brain significantly (Cornell et al., 2016). In addition, 14‐3‐3ɛ binds to a phosphorylated NUDEL and continues to keep NUDEL in a phosphorylated stage. NUDEL regulates dynein in the cytoplasm in neuronal migration in a similar pathway as PAFAH1B1. YWHAE deletion has been correlated with increase neuronal apoptosis, hippocampal defects, cortical thinning, and decrease neuronal migration (Nagamani et al., 2009).

To review the phenotypes in this cohort, individual 1 is now 3‐years‐old with dysmorphic facial features, macrocephaly, and mild developmental delay. Individuals 2 and 3 who are now 16‐years‐old have macrocephaly, small stature, dysmorphic facial features, global developmental delay, and intractable epilepsy. These two individuals have intellectual disability requiring an individualized learning plan. Individual 4 has macrocephaly, dysmorphic facial features, global developmental delay, and epilepsy.

Reviewing the imaging in this cohort, individuals 2 and 3 who are 16‐years‐old have abnormal white matter changes along with innumerable cysts on brain MRI. The hyperintense white matter changes developed after 2 years of age and were static. The innumerable cysts in the cerebrum noted on MRI occurred after 5 years of age and increased over time. Individual 1 is now 3‐years‐old with abnormal white matter changes; however no cystic changes were noted on brain MRI. Individual 4, who is now 8 years of age, has MRI changes including atypical Virchow‐Robin space versus gliosis in right frontal white matter though the patient did also have a history of trauma. Reviewing the literature, 6 out of 17 patients had cystic changes noted as early as 18 months old. This further gives evidence that YWHAE haploinsufficiency affects neurodevelopment through white matter injury with developing enlarged perivascular spaces that may represent progressive brain volume loss.

Other genes also play a role in this phenotype but not as much information is known. More research needs to be done to evaluate what each gene codes for and how each gene impacts phenotype. Mignon‐Ravix et al. evaluated multiple gene expression in human tissue in this region which revealed that TRARG1 is not expressed in the brain while BHLHA9 was not expressed in the 25 human tissue samples evaluated. Furthermore, CRK is thought to be involved in growth while TRARG1 is hypothesized to be a tumor suppressor gene associated with fat metabolism (Noor et al., 2018; Romano et al., 2020). Once the roles of these genes are further elucidated, the genotype–phenotype relationship will be more clearly understood.

Deletion of 17p13.3 has implications on brain development, dysmorphic facial feature, and growth depending on the size of the deletion and the genomic content. PAFAH1B1 deletion has the most severe phenotype with lissencephaly and life expectancy of approximately 2 years of age. Deletion of YWHAE is also associated with altered brain development and increased likelihood of epilepsy; however, patients have survived into adolescence and adulthood. Other signs and symptoms associated with YWHAE deletion include brain cystic changes, short stature, and particular dysmorphic facial features that may include prominent forehead, epicanthal folds, and broad nasal root. CRK is another gene in this region near YWHAE, and its deletion also seems to increase the probability of growth failure (Noor et al., 2018).

Patients that have 17p13.3 microdeletion including YWHAE should have close follow up to evaluate developmental progress as well as for the possible development of seizures. If the patient develops seizures, it appears that they are at high risk of developing drug resistant epilepsy. Therefore, neurologists' input are very beneficial in managing these patients. Furthermore, two of our patients had innumerable microcysts which evolved and enlarged over time. Therefore, serial brain MRIs may be beneficial especially if there is worsening of seizure activity including intractable, drug nonresponsive epilepsy or developmental decline.

Further studies need to be performed in vivo with different point mutations of these genes to evaluate the mechanism of how the brain's formation is changed. Our cohort continues to provide evidence that multiple genes within 17p13.3 are required for normal brain development, specifically YWHAE. This cohort, together with the cases from the literature, make clear that 17p13.3 deletion encompassing YWHAE but sparing PAFAH1B1 results in a definable phenotype distinct from MDS.
