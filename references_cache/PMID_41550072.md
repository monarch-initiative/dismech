# Efficacy of Retigabine in Treating Weakness in a Mouse Model of Hypokalemic Periodic Paralysis.
**Authors:** Denman K, Rich MM
**Journal:** Muscle Nerve (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mus.70158](https://doi.org/10.1002/mus.70158)

## Content

INTRODUCTION/AIMS: Hypokalemic periodic paralysis (HypoKPP) is an ion channelopathy causing episodic skeletal muscle weakness triggered by hypokalemia. Reduced inward rectifier K+ (Kir) channel activity contributes to membrane depolarization and paralysis, suggesting that pharmacologic activation of muscle K+ channels may restore excitability. The Kv7 channel agonist retigabine previously mitigated low-K+ weakness in HypoKPP models. Here, we tested whether this effect persists under conditions producing sustained, severe weakness.

METHODS: Extensor digitorum longus (EDL) muscles from mice homozygous for the SCN4A R669H mutation were studied by isometric twitch force recording at 34°C, with or without 20 U/L insulin. Weakness was induced by reducing extracellular K+ to 1.0 mM. Retigabine (10 μM) was applied to the bath, and twitch force was analyzed by paired or unpaired t tests (n = 4-7 per group).

RESULTS: Baseline twitch force in 4.75 mM K+ was ~50% lower in HypoKPP than wild-type muscle (p = 0.01). Force declined further after 1 h in 4.75 mM K+ (p = 0.016) and was completely lost at 1.0 mM K+ with insulin. Retigabine significantly reduced loss of force at both 4.75 and 1.0 mM K+ (p = 0.047 and p = 0.007, respectively).

DISCUSSION: Kv7 channel activation by retigabine preserved contractile force even during sustained depolarization from severe hypokalemia. These findings extend prior work and support development of K+ channel agonists as a therapeutic approach for HypoKPP.

Hypokalemic periodic paralysis (hypoKPP) is an ion channelopathy characterized by episodic skeletal muscle weakness during periods of low serum K+ [ 1 , 2 ]. Current treatments including K+ supplementation, K+‐sparing diuretics, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors often provide incomplete relief [ 3 ].

HypoKPP results from mutations in skeletal muscle Na+ or Ca2+ channels that generate an aberrant “gating‐pore” current, allowing monovalent cation leak and sustained depolarization [ 4 , 5 ]. The resulting depolarizing current suppresses inward‐rectifier K+ (Kir) conductance under low extracellular K+, causing paradoxical depolarization and excessive K+ uptake by muscle fibers [ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ]. This depolarization inactivates Na+ channels, rendering fibers inexcitable and producing muscle weakness [ 3 ].

Because loss of Kir current contributes to the pathogenesis of hypoKPP, pharmacologic activation of K+ channels represents a logical therapeutic approach. Several K+‐channel agonists are already clinically available. The K_ATP opener pinacidil, approved for hypertension [ 10 ], and the related agent cromakalim improved resting potential and force in hypoKPP muscle biopsies [ 11 , 12 ], although adverse effects limited their clinical use [ 13 ]. Retigabine (ezogabine), a Kv7 (KCNQ) channel agonist previously approved for epilepsy [ 14 ], has shown particular promise: it reduced myotonia in mouse models of myotonia congenita [ 15 , 16 ] and lessened weakness in two mouse models of hypoKPP [ 13 ].

The aim of the present study was to determine whether the Kv7 channel agonist retigabine attenuates severe weakness induced by reduction of extracellular K+.

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the policies of the Animal Care and Use Committee of Wright State University and were conducted in accordance with the United States Public Health Service's Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (IACUC, Assurance Number: D16‐00383, protocol number 1239). Mice were euthanized using CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. Mice homozygous for the R669H Nav1.4 mutation were used to model hypoKPP (MMRRC cat #36984). Wild type mice from the breeding colony were used as controls. Both male and female mice were used for all experiments. There was no difference in weakness between male and female mice.

Dissection and preparation were done at 22°C. During experiments, bath temperature was adjusted to 34°C using dual‐channel temperature controller (TC‐344C, Warner Instruments). The solution used for experiments contained (in mM), 118 NaCl, 4.75 KCl, 1.5 CaCl2, 0.7 MgSO4, 26.2 NaHCO3, 1.7 NaH2PO4, and 5.5 glucose and maintained at pH 7.3–7.4 by aeration with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Osmolarity of solution containing 1 mM KCl was maintained by addition of NaCl.

Isometric force recordings were obtained from extensor digitorum longus (EDL) muscles isolated from mice homozygous for the R669H mutation (hypoKPP model) [ 17 ]. Wild‐type littermates served as controls. Single 2‐ms stimuli were delivered once per minute through platinum electrodes placed in the bath.

Each EDL muscle was dissected free, with the proximal tendon tied to a fixed post using 6–0 silk suture and the distal tendon attached to a force transducer (Aurora Scientific) in a custom recording chamber. The muscle was stimulated directly through two parallel platinum electrodes oriented perpendicular to the fiber axis. Stimuli were generated by a Dagan S‐900 pulse generator under CED Spike2 control, and signals were digitized at 25 kHz. Optimal muscle length was determined by adjusting resting tension to maximize twitch force.

Muscles were first equilibrated in normal extracellular K+ solution (4.75 mM) for up to 60 min. To induce weakness, perfusate K+ was reduced from 4.75 to 1.0 mM. In some experiments, insulin (20 U/L) was included in the perfusate to parallel previous studies [ 18 , 19 ]. Force in 1 mM K+ solution was recorded for up to 135 min, followed by a 25‐min return to normal K+ to assess recovery. Single twitches were evoked once per minute throughout.

Excessive stimulation intensity can elicit force independent of action potentials [ 20 ]. To verify that recorded force reflected excitation–contraction coupling, two wild‐type muscles were treated with 1 μM tetrodotoxin (TTX) to block Na+ channels and restimulated; no force was observed, confirming dependence on action potentials (data not shown). Experiments were performed at 34°C. For drug treatment, retigabine (10 μM) was present in the perfusate throughout each recording.

Preliminary experiments used trains of 40 pulses at 100 Hz alternating with single‐pulse contractions. The percentage decrease in force after K+ reduction was comparable under both stimulation paradigms (n = 3; data not shown); therefore, subsequent experiments employed single‐pulse stimulation only. This approach was selected to facilitate future studies correlating force loss with intracellular voltage recordings, as single‐action‐potential stimulation avoids confounding depolarization and mechanical movement associated with 100 Hz trains. The EDL, a predominantly fast‐twitch muscle, was chosen over soleus to extend prior work and to simplify future intracellular recording.

We tested whether brief, high‐frequency stimulation could accelerate the onset of low‐K+‐induced weakness. Muscles received 12 trains of 45 pulses at 100 Hz every 30 s to mimic exercise, after which single stimuli were delivered once per minute to monitor force recovery. Weakness was triggered by lowering of K+ to 1 mM at 34°C in Ringer with 20 U/L insulin. Stimulation of muscle did not speed development of weakness, so all experiments were performed without trains of repetitive stimulation (Figure S1).

All statistics were performed using Origin software (OriginLab, Northhampton MA, USA). For comparisons of hypoKPP muscle before and after reduction of K+ to 1 mM, the paired t‐test was used. For comparisons between wild type and hypoKPP, the unpaired students t‐test was used. All data is presented as mean ± SD. p < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Baseline twitch force in 4.75 mM K+ solution was significantly reduced in EDL muscles from hypoKPP mice compared with wild type (Figure 1A,B; p = 0.01, two‐sample t‐test; n = 4 WT, 5 hypoKPP). In initial experiments in the absence of insulin reduction in force in hypoKPP muscles was variable (Figure 1C p = 0.037; n = 4). All further experiments were performed with inclusion of insulin. During preincubation in 4.75 mM K+ in the presence of insulin, there was reduction of force (Figure 1A,D p = 0.016, paired t‐test, n = 5). Subsequent incubation in 1 mM K+ caused complete loss of force (Figure 1D p = 0, paired t‐test, n = 5). The complete loss of force was preceded by oscillations in force similar to those observed previously in the soleus muscle [ 13 , 17 ] (Figure 1D). Wild‐type muscles also exhibited a modest reduction in force during perfusion with 1 mM K+ (Figure 1E; p = 0.036, paired t‐test; n = 4).

Responses varied somewhat among individual muscles (Figure 2B), but the overall effect was consistently beneficial. Unexpectedly, addition of 10 μM retigabine in 4.75 mM K+ solution also enhanced twitch force (Figure 2C).

In this study, we established experimental conditions that reproducibly triggered sustained, severe weakness in the EDL muscle of a mouse model of hypoKPP. Under these conditions, the Kv7 channel opener retigabine effectively reduced weakness both at normal extracellular K+ and under severe hypokalemia (1 mM K+).

Previous studies of the Nav1.4‐R669H mouse model of hypoKPP described oscillations in force during reduction of extracellular K+ [ 13 , 17 ], whereas similar oscillations were absent in the Cav1.1 model [ 13 , 21 ]. To achieve reproducible and sustained weakness, we lowered extracellular K+ to 1 mM and included insulin in the perfusate. Although transient oscillations in force persisted, complete and stable loss of force developed over time at 34°C.

Insulin likely exacerbates weakness in hyopKPP via two mechanisms. The first is that inclusion of insulin in the perfusate reduces Kir current [ 22 ]. The second is promotion of cellular uptake of K+. In patients, insulin‐glucose infusion has long been used to precipitate attacks of weakness by lowering extracellular K+ [ 23 ]. Although perfusate K+ was tightly controlled, interstitial K+ within the EDL likely fluctuates as K+ moves into or out of muscle fibers. In hypoKPP muscle, there is inappropriate K+ uptake during low‐K+ exposure [ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ]. The addition of insulin may amplify this process, precipitating the self‐reinforcing decline in extracellular K+ that underlies weakness. Both effects of insulin that promote weakness would be expected to be antagonized by retigabine; consistent with the observation that retigabine reduced weakness when extracellular K+ was normal.

The beneficial effects of retigabine against depolarization‐induced weakness are mediated through activation of KCNQ (Kv7) channels, which are expressed in skeletal muscle [ 24 ]. Retigabine has been shown to alleviate myotonia in mouse models of myotonia congenita [ 15 , 16 ] and to significantly hyperpolarize the membrane potential of hypoKPP fibers in low extracellular K+ [ 13 ]. By enhancing K+ efflux when extracellular K+ is low, Kv7 channel opening may counter both membrane depolarization and systemic hypokalemia, effectively compensating for the loss of Kir conductance that drives hypoKPP pathophysiology.

A potential limitation of this therapeutic strategy is that, although retigabine effectively reduces myotonia in vivo, it did not improve motor performance in mice with myotonia congenita [ 16 ]. This discrepancy may reflect adverse central nervous system effects or other systemic factors. A similar gap between in vitro efficacy and in vivo functional improvement could occur in hypoKPP. Future in vivo studies using insulin and glucose administration to lower serum K+ [ 17 , 21 ] will be needed to assess whether Kv7 activation can improve motor strength during provoked attacks.

Retigabine was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as adjunct therapy of focal‐onset epilepsy in 2011 but later, following reports of dermatologic and ocular pigmentation, it was voluntarily withdrawn in 2017 because of limited clinical use [ 14 ]. Accordingly, these findings are not immediately translatable to clinical practice. Nevertheless, the data highlight Kv7 channel activation as a promising therapeutic direction, motivating development of newer Kv7 agonists with improved safety profiles.

This study extends prior work by demonstrating that K+‐channel agonists can reduce weakness in hypoKPP muscle both at normal and severely reduced extracellular K+. Moreover, retigabine was effective in the predominantly fast‐twitch EDL muscle, supporting further exploration of Kv7 channel activators as potential therapeutic agents for hypoKPP.
