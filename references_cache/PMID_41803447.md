# Efficacy of vaginal 17β-estradiol on the urinary storage symptoms in postmenopausal women: a randomized double-blind, placebo-controlled study.
**Authors:** Harncharoenkul P, Wattanayingcharoenchai R, Pongchaikul P, Aimjirakul K, Chinthakanan O, Manonai J
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-43359-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-43359-1)

## Content

This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluated the efficacy of vaginal 17β-estradiol (10 mcg) in alleviating storage symptoms of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in postmenopausal women. A total of 86 participants were randomized to receive either vaginal 17β-estradiol or placebo for 12 weeks, with both groups undergoing standardized behavioral modifications. The primary outcome, improvement in storage symptoms assessed using ICIQ-FLUTS, showed no significant difference between groups at 12 weeks (IRR: 1.28, 95% CI: 0.57-1.98; p = 0.441). However, urgency significantly improved in the intervention group at 4 weeks (mean difference: -0.47, 95% CI: -0.90 to -0.04; p = 0.033) but was not sustained. Urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) showed a consistent benefit, with significant improvement at both 4 and 12 weeks (p = 0.013). Secondary outcomes showed trends favoring the intervention group. PGI-I scores were significantly better in the intervention group at 4 weeks (p = 0.035) but were comparable to the placebo group at 12 weeks. Urethral maturation index (UMI) improved at 4 weeks (p < 0.001) and remained higher than the placebo group at 12 weeks (p = 0.007). Vaginal pH significantly decreased at both time points (p < 0.001) in the intervention group. The treatment was well tolerated, with minimal adverse effects. While vaginal 17β-estradiol may provide short-term urgency relief and sustained UUI improvement, further studies are needed to confirm long-term efficacy.

The lower urinary tract (LUT) is responsible for the storage and elimination of urine. The control of this phenomenon is attributed to the innervation of the autonomic, somatic, and sensory systems. Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) encompass a variety of frequently co-occurring issues pertaining to storage symptoms. Storage symptoms include frequency, urgency, nocturia and urinary incontinence (stress UI, urgency UI, and mixed UI) 1 . According to the International Continence Society (ICS), overactive bladder (OAB) which is considered the most troublesome subset of LUTS, is characterized by a sense of urgency to urinate, with or without involuntary leakage, sometimes accompanied by increased frequency of urination and waking up at night to urinate. Based on the provided definition, urgency is a fundamental element in the diagnostic criteria for OAB, serving as the primary symptom that influences the manifestation of other OAB symptoms 2 , 3 , and 4 . The overall prevalence of storage symptoms in women was about 16% in the US and Europe. The prevalence of UUI increased with age from 2.0% to 19%, with a marked increase after 44 years of age 5 , 6 . In Thailand, the prevalence of OAB in women was 21.3% and urgency, the main symptom, was found to occur in 32.8% 7 .

The process of ageing and impacts of reduced estrogen levels during menopause are widely recognized as significant determinants of the anatomical and functional changes that occur in the pelvic floor and lower urinary tract. Estrogen plays an important role in the genital and lower urinary tract functions, with estrogen receptors found in the bladder, urethra, vagina, and pelvic floor musculature. Epidemiological research suggests that menopause plays a significant role in the increased likelihood of developing pelvic floor disorders such as pelvic organ prolapse (POP), overactive bladder (OAB), and urinary incontinence (UI). Furthermore, the severity of urinary symptoms associated with these disorders tends to escalate following menopause 2 .

Estrogen has the potential to provide therapeutic advantages in managing irritative voiding symptoms, such as urgency, frequency, and UUI. The precise mechanism by which the medication induces the reversal of urogenital atrophy or its direct impact on the LUT remains uncertain. Estrogens may disrupt SUI by increasing the number of periurethral vessels, which account for one-third of urethral pressure. Furthermore, estrogens can diminish the frequency and amplitude of detrusor contractions, raising the bladder’s sensory threshold and promoting detrusor muscle relaxation 4 , 8– 12 .

A systematic review evaluated the effects of vaginal estrogen compared to placebo or no treatment on storage symptoms in LUTS, including eight studies. Vaginal estrogen was administered through ovules (2 studies), tablets (2 studies), and cream (3 studies), while one study did not specify the method of application. The findings suggest that vaginal estrogen may effectively reduce OAB and UI symptoms, which are components of storage symptoms in LUTS 4 .

To date, no prior investigations have been conducted employing robust methodological approaches to assess the efficacy of 17β-Estradiol at a dosage of 10 mcg in the treatment of LUTS in postmenopausal women, particularly in comparison to a placebo control group. This notable gap in the existing literature underscores the need for a comprehensive and rigorously designed study to investigate the potential therapeutic benefits of 17β-Estradiol in alleviating LUTS among postmenopausal women, while concurrently establishing its superiority or non-inferiority relative to a placebo intervention. This study evaluated the efficacy of vaginal 17β-estradiol treatment in alleviating storage symptoms of LUTS in postmenopausal women through a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. The study focused on assessing symptom severity and bother using the validated Thai version of the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Female Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (ICIQ-FLUTS). Secondary outcomes included improvements in quality of life, patient-reported global improvement, and urethral maturation index.

Postmenopausal women who had suffered from storage symptoms of LUTS were eligible if they presented with a storage phase symptom score ≥ 1 on the ICIQ-FLUTS in at least one of the following items: nocturia, urgency, daytime frequency, UUI, or SUI. The study was conducted at the outpatient clinic of the University Hospital between April 2024 to October 2024. Inclusion criteria included being in natural or surgical menopause for more than one year, absence of urinary tract infection or other identifiable causes, no use of hormone replacement therapy or any form of estrogen within the prior four weeks, and a willingness to adhere to the research protocol and scheduled follow-up appointments.

Exclusion criteria included contraindications to estrogen therapy, such as prior thromboembolic events or gynecologic/genitourinary malignancies, anterior compartment pelvic organ prolapse stage III or IV, recent antibiotic use within seven days, and the use of anti-muscarinics, β3-adrenoceptor agonists, or vaginal energy-based devices within the prior two weeks. Patients with a history of allergic reactions to the study drug or any condition deemed by the investigator to increase the risk of adverse outcomes were also excluded.

Based on these criteria, potential participants were consecutively screened to determine their eligibility before proceeding with the study procedures. The informed consent process was conducted by the principal investigator (PH) prior to screening. Individuals with at least one storage symptom of LUTS presenting to the clinic received a detailed explanation of the study in Thai language, including procedures, risks, and potential benefits, in a private setting. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before entering the study.

This study is a two-arm, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial investigating the effects of vaginal 17β-estradiol (Femiest® Haupt Pharma Munster GMBH, Muenster, Germany; estradiol hemihydrate 0.0103 mg, pH 6.94) compared with placebo, both combined with behavioral modifications. Participants randomized to the intervention group received 17β-estradiol 10 mcg intravaginally. The dosing regimen involves one vaginal tablet daily for the first two weeks, followed by one tablet twice a week for 10 weeks. To maintain blinding, the placebo was formulated to be physically indistinguishable from the 17β-estradiol tablet, matching its color, shape, weight, hardness, dimensions, and disintegration profile. The tablet was white, round, biconvex, and imprinted with an “E.” Its composition consisted of agglomerated lactose, microcrystalline cellulose PH102, and magnesium stearate, with no active hormonal ingredient. The placebo exhibited a pH of 7.18 and was odorless. Participants in the placebo group received an identical placebo with the same administration schedule. All participants were informed on potential adverse effects, including breast pain, peripheral edema, and postmenopausal bleeding.

The clinical study is a two-arm, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of postmenopausal women who suffer from storage symptoms of LUTS (ClinicalTrials.gov registration: NCT 06508944, 18/07/2024) and has been approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (COA MURA 2024/10). All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians.

The sample size was calculated using two independent population proportions formula. According to Eriksen PS, Rasmussen H (1991) 13 , after 12 weeks, 62.8% of the women with urological symptoms underwent a change for the better in the estradiol (Vagifem®) group compared to 32.4% in the placebo group. (p < 0.001). With sample size (n) equal to 39 participants per group and added drop out at rate of 10%, which is equal to 43 participants per group. The total number of sample size was 86.

Participants were randomized into two groups, each comprising 43 individuals. One group received vaginal 17β-estradiol combined with behavioral modifications, while the other group was assigned a placebo combined with behavioral modifications for 12 weeks. Randomization was performed using a block randomization technique with a 1:1 ratio. The randomization sequence was generated using Stata version 18 under the supervision of a senior statistician from the Clinical Epidemiology and Biostatistics section at Ramathibodi Hospital, ensuring no investigator interference. All participants received their allocated interventions as per the study protocol. Drug allocation remained blinded to both investigators and participants, with containers labeled with unique alphanumeric codes to mask the treatment. Data analysis was conducted by an independent statistician unaware of group assignments, and a sealed randomization list was maintained as a contingency.

Screening procedures included a pelvic exam, pelvic organ prolapse quantification system (POP-Q) staging, a supine empty bladder stress test, gynecologic ultrasonography with post-void residual (PVR) measurement, urinalysis, and a three-day bladder diary documenting micturition timing and volume, fluid intake, incontinence episodes, urgency, and associated activities.

During the second visit, data from the screening process was evaluated alongside additional assessments, including bladder diary interpretation, ICIQ-FLUTS and ICIQ-LUTSqol questionnaires, vaginal pH measurement and the urethral maturation index (UMI). Participants successfully enrolled received a Thai-translated behavior modification guidebook developed by the International Urogynecological Association (IUGA). This guidebook, distributed by research assistants, aimed to standardize participant knowledge and behaviors throughout the trial.

At Third Visit (4-Week Follow-Up), participants underwent a clinical evaluation, vital signs assessment, and a review of their three-day bladder diary, which they were reminded to complete prior to the visit. Additional assessments included the ICIQ-FLUTS and ICIQ-LUTSqol questionnaires, the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I), vaginal pH measurement and the urethral maturation index (UMI).

At Fourth and Final Visit (12-Week Follow-Up) or eight weeks after the third visit, participants underwent a final evaluation, including a pelvic exam, and review of their bladder diary. Additional assessments included the ICIQ-FLUTS and ICIQ-LUTSqol questionnaires, PGI-I, vaginal pH measurement and the urethral maturation index (UMI).

Any discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by blinded assessors, ensuring data accuracy.

Vaginal pH was assessed using MQuant® pH-indicator strips placed on the lateral vaginal wall until moistened. A pH of 4.6 or greater indicates vulvovaginal atrophy (VVA), which is commonly associated with postmenopausal changes. Subsequently, vaginal pH served as both a surrogate marker for urethral pH and a measure of compliance with treatment.

Cytohormonal urethral analysis is a definitive diagnostic method for evaluating urogenital atrophy, as estrogen plays a critical role in urethral epithelial maturation. The UMI is calculated based on the ratio of parabasal, intermediate, and superficial cells from a sample of 100 cells. A reduction in estrogen levels, typical of menopause, is reflected in higher proportions of parabasal and intermediate cells. To obtain samples, the distal urethra was gently swabbed with a wet cotton bud coated in normal saline solution, and the collected cells was prepared on slides using Papanicolaou staining. An independent cytologist examined these slides under a microscope, assessing six randomly selected high-power fields and calculating a mean cell ratio.

The primary objective of this study was to determine the efficacy of 17β-estradiol vaginal treatment in alleviating storage symptoms of LUTS compared to placebo. Secondary objectives included evaluating the efficacy of 17β-estradiol vaginal treatment on the urethral maturation index and vaginal pH in comparison to placebo, as well as assessing the impact of the treatment on overall quality of life and the patient’s perception of global improvement (PGI).

The primary outcome of the study was the improvement in storage symptoms, assessed using the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Female Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (ICIQ-FLUTS), a validated Thai version of the questionnaire 14 , 15 . The ICIQ-FLUTS consists of five items, each addressing a specific storage symptom: nocturia (item 2a), urgency (item 3a), daytime frequency (item 5a), urinary urgency incontinence (UUI, item 9a), and stress urinary incontinence (SUI, item 11a). Each item includes two sub-categories: severity and bothersome effects. Severity scores range from 0 (no severity) to 4 (highest severity), with a cumulative score range of 0 to 20 across all items. For bothersome effects, scores range from 0 (no bother) to 10 (highest level of bother), with a cumulative score range of 0 to 50. Improvement in storage symptoms is defined as a post-treatment ICIQ-FLUTS score lower than the pre-treatment score, while no improvement is indicated by a post-treatment score equal to or higher than the pre-treatment score.

The subgroup analysis evaluated the distribution of participants experiencing specific symptoms, including nocturia, urgency, daytime frequency, UUI, and SUI, based on responses to the ICIQ-FLUTS questionnaire. Participants with symptom severity scores of 1–4 were included, while those with a score of 0 (indicating no symptoms) were excluded from the subgroup analysis.

Secondary outcomes included both subjective and objective assessments. Subjective assessments focused on quality of life and global improvement. The impact of urinary storage symptoms on participants’ quality of life was evaluated using the Thai version of ICIQ-LUTSqol 16 , 17 . This questionnaire comprises 20 items, with scores ranging from 19 to 76, where higher scores indicate a greater impact on quality of life. The ICIQ-LUTSqol is a validated Thai adaptation of the King’s Health Questionnaire (KHQ). Global improvement will be assessed using the Thai version of the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I), which evaluates participants’ subjective perceptions of their improvement. Objective assessments included cytological/histological changes and a change in quality of life following treatment.

Participants characteristics were summarized using means (± standard deviation) or medians (interquartile range) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were conducted using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data and t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous data, as appropriate. Fisher’s exact test was specifically applied for small sample sizes.

To assess improvements in outcomes or compare categorical data before and after the intervention at the 4-week and 12-week follow-ups, multilevel mixed-effects Poisson regression was used, with results reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Mean scores or continuous data were analyzed using multilevel mixed-effects linear regression. Subgroup analyses for each item of the primary and secondary outcomes were conducted using the same statistical methods.

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 18.0, and a per-protocol analysis was applied. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 90 postmenopausal women were screened for eligibility, with four excluded—two for not meeting the inclusion criteria and two for declining participation. The remaining 86 participants were randomized into two groups: the intervention group, which received vaginal 17β-estradiol combined with behavioral modifications (n = 43), and the placebo group, which received a placebo combined with behavioral modifications (n = 43). All participants in the placebo group and 41 participants in the intervention group completed the study, with two participants in the intervention group lost to follow-up at the 4-week evaluation. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar between the two groups, as shown in Table 1. The mean age was 65.56 ± 7.72 years in the intervention group and 65.65 ± 6.12 years in the placebo group (p = 0.953). Body mass index (25.42 ± 5.71 kg/m² vs. 25.70 ± 4.65 kg/m², p = 0.805) and menopausal age (49.02 ± 5.60 years vs. 50.49 ± 4.03 years, p = 0.175) were comparable. Most participants were at POP-Q stage I-II for the anterior compartment, with similar distributions between groups (80.49% vs. 79.07%, p = 0.872).

As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 2, at the 4-week follow-up, the improvement incidence for ICIQ-FLUTS symptom severity was 0.59 ± 0.13 in the intervention group and 0.43 ± 0.12 in the placebo group, with an incidence rate ratio (IRR) of 1.36 (95% CI: 0.41–2.31; p = 0.460). By 12 weeks, the rates increased to 0.68 ± 0.13 for the intervention group and 0.53 ± 0.11 for the placebo group, with an IRR of 1.28 (95% CI: 0.57–1.98; p = 0.441). Although no statistically significant differences were observed, the intervention group demonstrated consistent trends toward improvement compared to the placebo group.

Fig. 2 Improvement in urinary storage symptoms, quality of life and urethral maturation value.

Bothersome symptoms showed a similar trend, with the intervention group achieving an improvement incidence of 0.68 ± 0.14 at 4 weeks, compared to 0.43 ± 0.12 in the placebo group (IRR: 1.56, 95% CI: 0.50–2.62; p = 0.300). By 12 weeks, the intervention group further improved to 0.78 ± 0.14, while the placebo group remained at 0.47 ± 0.10, with an IRR of 1.68 (95% CI: 0.74–2.62; p = 0.156).

For quality of life, as measured by ICIQ-LUTSqol, the intervention and placebo groups demonstrated comparable improvement incidence at 4 weeks and 12 weeks (p > 0.05).

As shown in Table 3, the subgroup analysis evaluated the distribution of participants experiencing specific symptoms, including nocturia, urgency, daytime frequency, UUI, and SUI. The results showed no statistically significant differences between the intervention and placebo groups for any of the symptoms analyzed. These findings indicated that the intervention did not result in significant symptom improvement compared to the placebo.

The analysis of urinary symptoms and quality of life, as measured by ICIQ-FLUTS and ICIQ-LUTSqol scores, revealed notable trends favoring the intervention group over the placebo group at both 4-week and 12-week follow-ups. For symptom severity scores, the intervention group showed significant improvement in urgency scores at the 4-week follow-up (mean difference: -0.47, 95% CI: -0.90 to -0.04; p = 0.033). By 12 weeks, the improvement in urgency scores persisted, but the difference was no longer statistically significant (mean difference: -0.26, 95% CI: -0.65 to 0.12; p = 0.178). Other symptom severity measures, including nocturia and daytime frequency, showed trends of improvement in the intervention group but did not reach statistical significance at either follow-up.

For bothersome scores, the intervention group demonstrated significant improvement in UUI scores at both 4 weeks (mean difference: -1.99, 95% CI: -3.55 to -0.43; p = 0.013) and 12 weeks (mean difference: -1.79, 95% CI: -3.21 to -0.37; p = 0.013). Improvements in other bothersome measures, such as nocturia and stress urinary incontinence, were observed but did not achieve statistical significance.

In terms of quality of life, as measured by the ICIQ-LUTSqol, the intervention group consistently demonstrated lower mean scores compared to the placebo group, reflecting better quality of life. Although these differences were not statistically significant, the results suggest a trend toward improved quality of life in the intervention group.

At the 4-week follow-up, participants in the intervention group reported better PGI-I scores (mean: 2.75 ± 0.15) compared to those in the placebo group (mean: 3.20 ± 0.15). A score of 2 represents “much better,” while a score of 3 indicates “a little better,” highlighting that they perceived greater improvement in their condition. The difference between the groups was − 0.45 (95% CI: -0.87 to -0.03; p = 0.035), indicating a statistically significant improvement in the intervention group.

By the 12-week follow-up, the intervention group maintained slightly better PGI-I scores (mean: 2.54 ± 0.14) compared to the placebo group (mean: 2.74 ± 0.13). However, the difference was no longer statistically significant.

As shown in Table 4 and illustrated in Fig. 3, the urethral maturation index also showed improvements in the intervention group, particularly in the proportion of superficial cells. At 4 weeks, the mean proportion of superficial cells in the intervention group was 9.77 ± 1.23, significantly higher than the placebo group (1.39 ± 1.31), with a mean difference of 8.38 (95% CI: 4.86 to 11.89; p < 0.001). This improvement persisted at 12 weeks, with the intervention group showing a mean proportion of 4.76 ± 1.12 compared to 3.05 ± 1.09 in the placebo group, with a mean difference of 1.71 (95% CI: -1.35 to 4.77; p = 0.007).

Fig. 3 Comparison of urethral maturation index between intervention groups.

Vaginal pH decreased significantly in the intervention group at both 4-week and 12-week follow-ups. At 4 weeks, the mean vaginal pH in the intervention group was 5.82 ± 0.18, compared to 6.71 ± 0.18 in the placebo group, with a mean difference of -0.89 (95% CI: -1.38 to -0.39; p < 0.001). This trend continued till at 12 weeks after treatment.

Adverse outcomes were minimal and comparable between the intervention group and the placebo group at both 4-week and 12-week follow-ups. At 4 weeks, vaginal discharge was reported by 5.88% of participants in the intervention group and 13.33% in the placebo group, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.407). Vaginal discomfort was reported by 5.88% of the intervention group and 6.67% of the placebo group at 4 weeks (p = 1.000) and by 7.32% and 4.65% at 12 weeks (p = 0.672), respectively. Vaginal bleeding was observed in 5.88% of the intervention group and none of the placebo group at 4 weeks (p = 0.494), and it decreased to 2.44% in the intervention group and remained absent in the placebo group at 12 weeks (p = 0.488). No serious adverse events were reported, and no participants discontinued due to adverse effects.

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial assessed the efficacy of ultra-low-dose 10 mcg 17β-estradiol vaginal tablet in alleviating storage symptoms associated with LUTS in postmenopausal women. The findings indicate that while the intervention group demonstrated trends toward improvement across various outcome measures, most differences between the 17β-estradiol and placebo groups did not reach statistical significance. Previous studies have reported significant benefits of vaginal estrogen therapy (ET) in different dosages and forms for urogenital symptoms assessed by a variety of measures for both objective and subjective outcomes 4 . Eriksen et al. (1991) 13 found that after 12 weeks of 25 mcg of 17 beta-estradiol vaginal tablet, 62.8% of participants in the treatment group experienced symptom improvement compared to 32.4% in the placebo group. Similarly, Simunic et al. (2003) 18 demonstrated a significant improvement in urinary atrophy symptoms in the vaginal ET group compared to baseline in a large multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled study involving 1,612 patients with urogenital complaints.

Although the improvement of ICIQ-FLUTS symptom severity was equivalent in the intervention and the placebo groups at 12 weeks, the improvement incidence observed in the placebo group was higher than that reported in previous studies. This discrepancy may be attributed to the inclusion of behavioral modifications as part of our study protocol, which could have contributed to symptom improvement independent of estrogen intervention.

Notably, urgency symptoms showed significant improvement in the intervention group at 4 weeks, but this effect was no longer statistically significant by 12 weeks. This early but transient improvement suggests that ultra-low-dose 17β-estradiol vaginal tablet may provide short-term relief for urgency, which could be clinically relevant for patients seeking immediate symptom alleviation. Meanwhile, UUI demonstrated a more sustained benefit, with significant improvement at both 4 weeks and 12 weeks, indicating that 10 mcg of 17β-estradiol vaginal treatment may offer prolonged symptomatic relief in women with bothersome UUI. Additionally, subjective patient-reported outcomes, as assessed by the PGI-I score, support these findings. At 4 weeks, participants in the vaginal estrogen group reported significantly greater perceived improvement compared to those in the placebo group. Moreover, the observed improvements in UMI and vaginal pH provide a biological rationale supporting the potential role of 17β-estradiol in managing LUTS in postmenopausal women. Taken together, these findings suggest that ultra-low-dose 17β-estradiol vaginal tablet, particularly when combined with behavioral modifications, may serve as a viable therapeutic option for postmenopausal women experiencing urgency and UUI, with its effects being more prominent in the early treatment phase.

Beyond the estrogen intervention, our study underscores the importance of behavioral modifications in managing storage LUTS in postmenopausal women. Both the vaginal estrogen plus behavioral modifications group and the placebo plus behavioral modifications group demonstrated overall symptom improvement. Nonetheless, a closer examination of individual symptoms suggests that 17β-estradiol vaginal treatment may provide early-phase benefits in particular symptoms relief. Importantly, this study reinforces that behavioral modifications remain a valuable strategy for alleviating storage LUTS in postmenopausal women, regardless of hormonal treatment. To ensure consistency and effectiveness, we utilized a standardized, globally recognized behavioral modification protocol from the International Urogynecological Association (IUGA) Patient Information Leaflets and incorporated evidence-based recommendations from the 2024 European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines. These guidelines emphasize bladder training, lifestyle modifications, pelvic floor muscle training, and cognitive behavioral therapy as first-line conservative therapies for LUTS 2 , 19 . Active follow-up by the research assistants also ensured compliance with the standardized behavioral modification protocol, reducing variability in adherence and strengthening the reliability of our findings.

This study addresses a critical knowledge gap by demonstrating that the 10-mcg-dose of vaginal estradiol offers comparable efficacy to the more commonly studied 25 mcg dose while minimizing the risk of systemic absorption. The treatment was well tolerated, with a favorable safety profile and minimal adverse effects, including vaginal discharge, discomfort, and postmenopausal bleeding, all of which occurred at low rates and did not lead to treatment discontinuation. Importantly, there were no significant differences in adverse effects between the intervention and placebo groups, and no serious adverse events were reported. These safety profiles align with Eugster-Hausmann et al. (2010) 20 , who reported that the 10-mcg dose minimizes estradiol absorption to an appropriate level, highlighting its safety and efficacy as a therapeutic option for managing vaginal atrophy in postmenopausal women. Ulrich et al. (2010) 21 reported the endometrial safety of 10 mcg estradiol in postmenopausal women with vaginal atrophy, further supporting the potential of this lower dosage in managing symptoms effectively while maintaining patient safety.

The present study confirms that vaginal estrogen therapy improves urethral epithelial health, similar to its effects on vaginal tissue. The vaginal estrogen group demonstrated significant improvements in vaginal pH and superficial cell proportions at both 4 and 12 weeks, representing enhanced vaginal and urethral epithelial function. To provide direct evidence of urethral epithelial response, we specifically collected maturation index data from the distal urethral epithelium, further supporting the pathophysiological link between estrogen depletion and LUTS. These findings align with previous studies which reported significant benefits of vaginal estrogen in alleviating urinary symptoms and reversing urogenital atrophy markers 4 , 8– 13 , 18 . In alignment with our findings, Zhang et al. (2024) 22 demonstrated a positive correlation between estrogen supplement and urinary frequency, as well as an increase in Collagen III deposition and altered mechanoreceptor activity (Piezo1, TRPV4) in bladder tissue. Their results suggest that estrogen treatment helps restore bladder function by reducing collagen accumulation and normalizing sensory protein expression. Furthermore, the observed urethral maturation improvements and vaginal pH reductions align with a previous study in reinforcing the estrogenic mechanism in restoring tissue health and alleviating LUTS 23 . Taken together, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of vaginal 17β-estradiol treatment in improving both urethral and vaginal epithelial integrity, supporting its role in LUTS management for postmenopausal women.

This study possesses several strengths that enhance the reliability and validity of its findings. The rigorous design—incorporating randomization, double-blinding, and placebo control—minimizes bias and ensures robust results. A multidimensional evaluation was conducted by assessing both subjective outcomes (ICIQ-FLUTS, ICIQ-LUTSqol, and PGI-I scores) and objective outcomes (vaginal pH and urethral maturation index), providing a comprehensive perspective on the efficacy of vaginal 17β-estradiol. The effective randomized allocation in creating balanced groups and the low dropout rate, preserved statistical power and reinforced the robustness of the findings. Participant compliance was carefully monitored, with blinded assessors verifying adherence through pill counts and bladder diaries collected three days before each follow-up visit. This process helped align questionnaire responses, particularly regarding LUTS severity in the ICIQ-FLUTS, with recorded bladder activity.

Despite its strengths, this study contained several limitations that should be acknowledged. The lack of ethnic diversity in the study population may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader demographic groups. Additionally, the 12-week study duration precludes the assessment of long-term efficacy and safety of ultra-low-dose 17β-estradiol vaginal treatment. Extended follow-up periods, such as 6 to 12 months, would provide valuable insights into sustained therapeutic benefits, adherence patterns, and potential long-term adverse effects. Furthermore, the study did not include objective measures to assess the direct impact of behavioral modifications, making it difficult to quantify their contribution to symptom improvement. Finally, although trends toward symptom improvement were observed, the lack of statistically significant differences in primary outcomes suggests that the effect size of vaginal 17β-estradiol treatment may be smaller than previously hypothesized. Future research should explore the long-term effects of low-dose vaginal estradiol on LUTS recurrence rates and further investigate the interplay between behavioral modifications and estrogen treatment to optimize therapeutic strategies.

This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial demonstrated the efficacy of ultra-low-dose vaginal 17β-estradiol in alleviating urgency symptom at 4 weeks and urgency urinary incontinence at both 4 and 12 weeks in postmenopausal women compared with placebo, suggesting that vaginal estrogen may provide short-term relief for urgency and sustained benefits for UUI. However, given that this was a well-powered study, the overall magnitude and duration of benefit were modest, and behavioral modifications appear to account for a substantial portion of symptom improvement in both groups. The approximately 10% difference between estradiol and placebo should therefore be interpreted cautiously in terms of clinical meaningfulness. Importantly, both the intervention and placebo groups showed symptom improvement, which may be attributed to the inclusion of standardized behavioral modifications in both arms. This finding underscores the importance of behavioral interventions as an effective non-pharmacologic approach to LUTS management, with or without hormonal therapy. The safety profile of 17β-estradiol was favorable, with minimal adverse effects. These findings support the clinical application of the ultra-low-dose vaginal estrogen regimen for postmenopausal women with bothersome LUTS, particularly for those seeking early symptom relief.
