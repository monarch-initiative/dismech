# Inherited Predispositions to Myeloid Neoplasms: Pathogenesis and Clinical Implications.
**Authors:** Liu YC, Eldomery MK, Maciaszek JL, Klco JM

Author information:

Myeloid neoplasms with and without preexisting platelet disorders frequently 
develop in association with an underlying germline predisposition. Germline 
alterations affecting ANKRD26, CEBPA, DDX41, ETV6, and RUNX1 are associated with 
nonsyndromic predisposition to the development of myeloid neoplasms including 
acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic syndrome. However, germline 
predisposition to myeloid neoplasms is also associated with a wide range of 
other syndromes, including SAMD9/9L associated predisposition, GATA2 deficiency, 
RASopathies, ribosomopathies, telomere biology disorders, Fanconi anemia, severe 
congenital neutropenia, Down syndrome, and others. In the fifth edition of the 
World Health Organization (WHO) series on the classification of tumors of 
hematopoietic and lymphoid tissues, myeloid neoplasms associated with germline 
predisposition have been recognized as a separate entity. Here, we review 
several disorders from this WHO entity as well as other related conditions with 
an emphasis on the molecular pathogenesis of disease and accompanying somatic 
alterations. Finally, we provide an overview of establishing the molecular 
diagnosis of these germline genetic conditions and general recommendations for 
screening and management of the associated hematologic conditions.

Heterozygous germline RUNX1 pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) variants cause familial platelet disorder with predisposition to myeloid malignancies in an autosomal dominant manner, which was first described in 1999 (1). Most patients with germline RUNX1 P/LP variants present with thrombocytopenia, whereas a smaller percentage of patients have impaired platelet function but no significant thrombocytopenia. Significant phenotypic heterogeneity has been described among patients with familial platelet disorder which is likely due to incomplete penetrance. The lifetime risk of developing myeloid malignancies is estimated to be roughly 40% and can occur within a wide age range (median age 29 years, 2-72 years; Figure 1) (2; 3). The most common malignancies include myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), although lymphoid malignancies such as T-lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) and B-lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) have also been reported (Table 1). Recent studies showed that AML patients with germline or acquired RUNX1 P/LP variants had different somatic alteration profiles. AMLs associated with germline RUNX1 P/LP variants are more likely to demonstrate somatic alterations involving GATA2 and the second allele of RUNX1; these AMLs are also less likely to have somatic alterations associated with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) and epigenetic regulation such as in DNMT3A (4). Although a high frequency (up to 30%) of germline RUNX1 P/LP variants were once reported in patients with RUNX1-altered AML (5), other studies have suggested much lower frequencies of germline RUNX1 P/LP variants in AML patients with somatic RUNX1 alterations (8% to 10%) (6-8).

Runt-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1, also known as AML1, CBFA2), located on chromosome 21q22, encodes the alpha subunit of core binding transcription factor (CBF). CBF is a heterodimeric transcription factor essential for hematopoiesis and is composed of RUNX1 (CBFα) and CBFβ subunits. RUNX1 contains a Runt homology domain (RHD) at the N-terminus, a transcriptional activation domain (TAD), and an inhibitory domain (ID) at the C-terminus. RUNX1 binds to DNA via the RHD domain that also mediates heterodimerization with the CBFβ subunit and contains a nuclear localization signal. The interaction with CBFβ strengthens the DNA affinity of RUNX1 (9). RUNX1 can activate or repress transcription depending on the context and interact with a variety of co-activators and co-repressors via the C-terminal TAD and ID. Transcription from two different promoters with alternative splicing generates three different major RNA isoforms. Isoform 1B and 1C, which contain the three major domains are functionally similar; but isoform 1C, transcribed from the distal promoter, has 32 extra amino acids at the N-terminus. The shorter isoform 1A lacks the C-terminal TAD and ID domain. Albeit playing an important role in regulating its own target genes, the expression of RUNX1 itself, with these RUNX1 RNA isoforms, is tightly controlled and depends on the cellular context in a specific temporal manner.

RUNX1 is especially important in the maturation of megakaryocytes. Conditional Runx1 knockout in mice leads to a remarkably decreased platelet count (10). RUNX1, through regulating multiple megakaryocyte developmental-related genes (including Spi1, Selp, and Itga2b), controls the direct differentiation of MK-HSCs (a subpopulation of hematopoietic stem cells with a megakaryocyte differentiation potential while maintaining self-renewal capability) to megakaryocytes (11). RUNX1 is also known to impact adult hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) activity. Increased HSCs were found in adult mice with conditional deletion of Runx1 (12). Expression of the full length RUNX1 1b isoform decreases HSC competitive repopulation, whereas expression of the short RUNX1 1a isoform lacking the C-terminal with a presumptive dominant negative effect, has the opposite effect (13).

Germline RUNX1 P/LP variants are loss-of-function or dominant negative variants that disrupt the function of RUNX1 and can be of various types, including missense, nonsense, splice-site variants, and in-frame as well as frameshift insertions/deletions (indels) (4). According to a recent study, splice-site variants affecting the canonical splicing donor and acceptor sites between exons 4 and 5 are the most frequently observed germline RUNX1 P/LP variants. Missense variants are enriched in the RHD domain whereas truncating variants (nonsense and frameshift indels) are identified throughout the protein (4).

Heterozygous germline P/LP variants in ANKRD26 result in familial thrombocytopenia in an autosomal dominant manner, which was first described in 2011 (14; 15). Patients usually show thrombocytopenia with normal platelet size; bleeding can be mild or absent. Platelet aggregation studies are usually within the normal range. The lifetime risk of developing hematologic malignancies is estimated to be 8% and can occur with a wide age range (1-84 years) (3; 14-16). The most common malignancies include MDS, AML, chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML), and less frequently lymphoid malignancies such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). The somatic alteration profile of the hematopoietic neoplasms with germline ANKRD26 P/LP variants is not well-characterized.

Ankyrin Repeat Domain 26 (ANKRD26), located on chromosome 10p12.1, encodes a protein containing N-terminal ankyrin repeats that is critical in megakaryopoiesis. The known germline P/LP variants in ANKRD26 are generally single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels located within the 5′ untranslated region (5’UTR) of the gene within the promoter. These P/LP variants can be missed by targeted sequencing if they are not specifically designed to cover the 5’UTR and may not be identified by whole exome sequencing (WES) depending on the coverage. These promoter variants disrupt the binding of transcriptional repressors such as RUNX1 and FLI1 (17), which normally occurs during late stages of megakaryopoiesis and results in silencing of ANKRD26 expression. Persistent ANKRD26 expression in megakaryocytes is associated with activation of the thrombopoietin/myeloproliferative leukemia virus oncogene (TPO/MPL) signaling through different downstream pathways. This includes MAPK and ERK 1/2 (17), which are known to prevent platelet formation, likely driving the observed thrombocytopenia in these patients.

Heterozygous germline P/LP variants in ETV6 lead to familial thrombocytopenia with predisposition to hematologic malignancies in an autosomal dominant manner, which was first described in 2015 (18; 19). Most patients show thrombocytopenia with high penetrance. They also show normal platelet size, decreased α granules, and abnormal platelet aggregation studies. The lifetime risk of developing hematologic malignancies is estimated to be 30% with a wide age range (2-82 years) (3). The most common malignancies include B-ALL, mixed-phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL), and sometimes CMML, MDS, and AML. P/LP germline ETV6 variants can be found in roughly 1% of pediatric B-ALL patients. Approximately 70% of B-ALL cases with P/LP germline ETV6 variants demonstrate a hyperdiploid genotype with recurrent alterations in NRAS, KRAS and PTPN11. The remaining 30% of cases with diploid karyotype often demonstrate deletions of PAX5 and ETV6 with a gene expression profile reminiscent of B-ALL with ETV6::RUNX1 fusion transcript (20).

ETS Variant Transcription Factor 6 (ETV6, also known as TEL), located on chromosome 12p13, encodes a transcriptional repressor in the Erythroblast Transformation-Specific (ETS) transcription factor family that is essential for hematopoiesis and megakaryopoiesis. ETV6 protein comprises a pointed N-terminal (PNT) domain at the N-terminus, a central linker domain, and an ETS DNA-binding domain at the C-terminus. ETV6 binds to the highly conserved 5’GGA(A/T)3’ motif sequences in the promoters of its target genes via the ETS DNA-binding domain. The PNT domain, also termed helix loop helix (HLH) or sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain, mediates dimerization or oligomerization of ETV6 itself or with other transcription factors such as FLI1; this multimerization is required for high affinity DNA-binding and essential for its transcriptional repression activity. The transcriptional complex composed of ETV6 and corepressors such as HDAC3, NCOR, and Sin3A, can also affect histone acetylation and chromatin condensation at target promoters, which subsequently regulates gene expression (21).

ETV6 is important in megakaryopoiesis. Transcriptional targets of ETV6 include genes encoding for megakaryocyte/platelet glycoprotein 1bα and glycoprotein IX (22). In addition, ETV6 inhibits the transcriptional activity of FLI1, another ETS transcription factor essential for platelet formation and megakaryopoiesis, likely through direct interaction (22). Conditional knockout of Etv6 in mouse megakaryocyte erythroid progenitor cells showed increased megakaryocyte colony forming cells with thrombocytopenia, suggestive of impaired terminal megakaryocyte maturation (23; 24). Through its capacity to maintain adult HSCs, ETV6 may also impact cells of other hematopoietic lineages. Disruption of Etv6 in adult mice resulted in significantly decreased HSCs in the bone marrow, but short-lived progenitor cells were relatively not affected (23).

Germline ETV6 P/LP variants can be of various types. Although most are missense variants, nonsense, frameshift insertion/deletions, deletions, and structural variants are also found. The loss-of-function ETV6 variants, confirmed by in vitro functional assays, impair the proper nuclear localization of ETV6 and disrupt DNA binding through altering or truncating the DNA binding domain of ETV6 (20; 25).

Heterozygous germline P/LP variants in CEBPA lead to predisposition to AML in an autosomal dominant manner. CEBPA-associated familial AML is defined as an AML individual with a germline P/LP variant in CEBPA, and/or a family with a germline P/LP variant in CEBPA in which more than one member presents with AML. The risk of developing AML in individuals with germline CEBPA variants depends on the type and the location of the variants (26; 27). The penetrance appears to be higher in families with N-terminal variants when compared to families with C-terminal variants (90% vs. 50%). The diagnosis of AML associated with germline CEBPA P/LP variants occurs with a wide age range, but the age of onset in these patients is earlier than that seen in AML patients with sporadic CEBPA alterations. Acquired somatic alterations disrupting the remaining wide-type CEBPA allele are almost always detected in CEBPA-associated familial AMLs (26; 28). The cumulative incidence of relapse is estimated to be 50-60% at 10 years, but the recurrent AMLs are usually associated with independent clones carrying different somatic CEBPA alterations and are thus not true relapses (26). In addition, CEBPA-associated familial AML demonstrates better prognosis when compared with sporadic CEBPA-associated AML (29).

CCAAT Enhancer Binding Protein Alpha (CEBPA), located on chromosome 19q13.1, encodes a basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor essential for myeloid differentiation, especially in monopoiesis and granulopoiesis. The bZIP family transcription regulators, including CEBPA, CEBPB, CEBPE, CEBPG, and CEBPD, contain two transactivation domains (TAD) in the N-terminus and one bZIP domain in the C-terminus. All CEBP transcription factors share a similar C-terminal bZIP domain for DNA binding and dimerization (either homodimerization or heterodimerization among the bZIP transcription regulators), but differ in the two N-terminal TAD domains for transcription control and protein interactions (30). CEBPA expression in common myeloid progenitors facilitates granulocytic differentiation and blocks monocytic differentiation (31). Cebpa knockout mice demonstrate low neutrophil counts and low eosinophils, but relatively normal monocyte number (32; 33). CEBPA activates the myeloid transcriptional program through inducing the expression of its target genes such as CSF3R, CEBPE, and KLF5 (34; 35).

Germline and somatic CEBPA variants cluster at the N-terminus as well as the C-terminal bZIP region. The N-terminal CEBPA variants, predominantly composed of frameshift indels, generate a truncated p30 isoform using an alternative translation initiation codon that is 351 nucleotides downstream of the canonical AUG initiation codon (36-38). The p30 isoform is known to demonstrate a dominant negative effect and inhibit the function of the full-length CEBPA isoform (p42) (39). The C-terminal loss-of-function CEBPA variants, predominantly missense variants or in-frame indels, impact DNA binding as well as dimerization (28).

Heterozygous germline P/LP variants in DDX41 predispose to hematologic malignancies in an autosomal dominant manner and contribute to roughly 80% of myeloid neoplasms with germline predisposition occurring during adulthood (40). The lifetime risk of developing hematologic malignancies is roughly 50%, although the risk increases significantly after 40 years of age (40). Myeloid neoplasms are more frequent in males despite equal frequencies of the variants in females (40). Individuals with germline DDX41 P/LP variants may demonstrate mild cytopenia or macrocytosis before developing overt hematopoietic neoplasms. Compared with the other genes predisposing to hematologic malignancies, germline DDX41-associated neoplasms are late-onset and patients often have no significant family history (41). The most common hematologic malignancies associated with germline DDX41 P/LP variants include MDS, AML, and less commonly lymphoid neoplasms including non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), CLL, and ALL.

Normal karyotype, hypocellularity and erythroid dysplasia are often seen in AML associated with germline DDX41 P/LP variants (42). Somatic alterations disrupting the remaining wild-type DDX41 allele are seen in roughly half of the myeloid neoplasms with germline DDX41 P/LP variants. These somatic DDX41 alterations are mainly missense variants involving the helicase domain, including the p.R525H variant that accounts for 80% of the somatic DDX41 alterations (43). In addition to somatic DDX41 alterations, myeloid neoplasms associated with germline DDX41 P/LP variants demonstrate recurrent somatic variants in ASXL1, CUX1, TP53, DNMT3A, and TET2 (40; 41).

DEAD-Box Helicase 41 (DDX41), located on chromosome 5q35.3, encodes a protein that belongs to the DEAD-box helicase family. The diverse functions of DDX41 have not been completely elucidated but are likely mediated by its interaction with dsDNA and DNA:RNA hybrids. In the nucleus, DDX41 regulates pre-mRNA splicing and snoRNA processing via interacting with the spliceosome and facilitating excision of snoRNA-containing introns in ribosomal protein genes (44; 45). Within the cytoplasm, DDX41 can modulate the activity of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) to subsequently impact the innate immunity type I interferon (IFN) response via its dsDNA unwinding capabilities, thus altering the relative ratio of dsDNA-to-ssDNA (46). Monoallelic germline Ddx41 P/LP variants in mice demonstrate age-dependent myelodysplastic changes, whereas biallelic variants lead to impaired hematopoiesis at a younger age (45). Severe acute graft-versus-host disease after allogeneic stem cell transplantation has been reported in patients with deleterious germline DDX41 variants, suggesting that the presence of DDX41 variants in non-hematopoietic tissue may provide a microenvironment that stimulates donor-derived T cells (47; 48).

Germline DDX41 variants can be of various types but are considered loss-of-function. Although two-thirds of the variants are truncating, missense variants and deletions are also observed (40). Since most truncating variants (>95%) are of germline origin, individuals with such variants identified on somatic testing should be offered germline testing. Individuals with the start-loss variant c.3G>A, p.M1? should also be offered germline testing since this variant has been confirmed to be exclusively of germline origin. This start-loss allele often demonstrates variant allele frequency (VAF) lower than that of other germline variants (~30% rather than ~50%) due to a common polymorphism affecting the binding of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) primers.

Pathogenic variants in the RAS-MAPK pathway resulting in aberrant and unregulated activation are common somatic events in a variety of hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic tumors across the age spectrum. These commonly involve canonical P/LP missense variants in NRAS and KRAS, with NRAS variants being among the most common acquired alterations in myeloid tumors. In childhood, alterations in a variety of pathway members including PTPN11 (which encodes SHP2, a non-receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase that regulates signaling through growth factor receptors), NF1 (which encodes neurofibromin 1, a GTPase-activating protein that negatively regulates RAS/MAPK pathway activity by accelerating the hydrolysis of Ras-bound GTP), and CBL (which encodes for an E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase involved in cell signaling and protein ubiquitination) have been identified in juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML), an aggressive childhood malignancy characterized by a proliferation of myeloid cells in the peripheral blood, spleen, bone marrow, and other tissues (49). While the RAS-MAPK alterations in JMML are commonly somatic, similar alterations with associated myeloid proliferations are observed as germline P/LP variants as part of a constitutional syndrome, collectively referred to as RASopathies.

The most common RASopathy is Noonan syndrome, in which children frequently harbor a germline PTPN11 P/LP variant. In addition to syndromic phenotypic manifestations, children with Noonan syndrome can develop a JMML-like proliferation. This potentially mild myeloid cell phenotype contrasts with the more aggressive nature of myeloid neoplasms with somatic PTPN11 alterations, which commonly involve different amino acids within PTPN11 (50). In addition to PTPN11, other RASopathies are also driven by P/LP germline variants in CBL, NF1, NRAS, HRAS, SHOC2, and others (reviewed in (51)). However, there is variability in whether these syndromes will be associated with myeloid cell proliferations, which are common in patients with germline CBL P/LP variants in CBL syndrome or Noonan syndrome-like disorders, and NF1 variants in Neurofibromatosis type 1. Curiously these JMML-like proliferations can spontaneously regress, a feature that is most frequently observed in children with germline CBL P/LP variants (52). In some cases, such as with germline NF1 or CBL P/LP variants, progression to advanced disease is associated with duplication of the mutant allele through uniparental disomy (UPD) in the myeloid compartment. These UPD events are also associated with acquired alterations in SETBP1 and deletions of chromosome 7.

Down syndrome (DS) is one of the most common genetic diseases, at an incidence of 1.4 per 1000 live births (53). Children with germline trisomy 21 and Down syndrome have increased incidence of transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM), myeloid leukemia, and B-ALL. TAM occurs in at least 5-10% of newborns with DS. The patients usually present with cytopenia, variable degree of leukocytosis, organomegaly, and circulating megakaryoblasts morphologically and immunophenotypically indistinguishable from blasts seen in myeloid leukemia associated with DS (ML-DS). TAM usually occurs within the first week of life (almost always present by the first 2 months of life) and the majority resolves spontaneously within the first 3 months of life. Roughly 20-30% of newborns with a history of TAM develop ML-DS. The overall risk of developing ML-DS is estimated to be 0.5-2% in children with DS (54; 55). ML-DS usually occurs within the first 2-4 years of life. Regarding ALL, the overall survival in children with DS appears worse than those without DS (70% vs. 89%) (56). Increased treatment-related toxicity and infections, as well as the potentially less favorable genetic alterations associated with DS-ALL, may contribute to the worse prognosis. JAK/STAT alterations (48%) (57) and aberrant expression of CRLF2 (60%) are identified in a high percentage of children with DS-ALL (58). Compared with children without Down syndrome, children with Down syndrome have a significantly higher risk of AML before age 5 years (hazard ratio 399) whereas the largest hazard ratios are found for the risk of developing megakaryoblastic leukemia before age 5 years (hazard ratio 1500) (59). Children with Down syndrome also have significantly higher risk of acute lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma than other children (<5 years: hazard ratio 28, ≥5 years hazard ratio 21) (59).

The development of TAM and ML-DS likely results from multiple transforming events. Trisomy 21, the first genetic event, occurs prenatally in these patients and leads to increased proliferation of megakaryocyte progenitors in the fetal liver (60). The subsequent event is usually a somatic GATA1 alteration, identified in the vast majority of TAM and ML-DS patients, that can block megakaryocytic differentiation (61). GATA Binding Protein 1 (GATA1), located on chromosome Xp11.23, encodes a transcription factor containing zinc finger (ZF) that is essential for normal erythropoiesis and megakaryopoiesis. The majority of somatic GATA1 alterations are insertions, deletions, or duplications, residing in exon 2, with a smaller subset in exon 3. Truncated GATA1 protein generated by mutant GATA1 gene lacks the transactivation domain but retains both DNA-binding zinc finger domains (62); the mutant protein thus causes deregulation of target genes (61). In ML-DS, increased expression of the shorter RUNX1A isoform (lacking the transactivation domain) is also observed and together with the presence of truncated GATA1 protein, displaces the RUNX1C isoform from its binding sites and induces oncogenic programs through interacting with the MYC:MAX complex (63).

Germline P/LP variants in BLM lead to Bloom syndrome (BS) in an autosomal recessive manner (64). The syndrome, also called premature aging syndrome, is characterized by growth deficiency, photosensitive skin changes, immune deficiency, insulin resistance, and predisposition to cancers including hematologic malignancies. A high rate of sister chromatid exchanges is the pathognomonic feature of BS (65). Although a wide variety of cancer types can be identified in patients with BS, hematologic malignancies are the most common followed by colorectal cancers. The most common hematologic malignancies are mature non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL), AML, and less commonly lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma (ALL). The mean age at diagnosis for NHL, AML, and ALL is around age 20 years although the age range is wide. BS is more common in the Ashkenazi Jewish population, with ~1 in every 48,000 individuals of the Ashkenazi Jewish population having BS (66).

BLM RecQ like helicase (BLM), located on chromosome 15q26.1, encodes a DNA helicase that unwinds DNA using the energy from ATP hydrolysis. DNA helicases are involved in processes including DNA replication and repair, and RNA transcription. BLM comprises several protein domains including the helicase domain and the RecQ C-terminal domain that contains the Zn2+-binding motif and a winged-helix domain (RQC). The DNA unwinding activity requires the RQC domains serving as the dsDNA binding site and the helicase domain. BLM forms a complex with other proteins including topoisomerase IIIα, an enzyme that catalyzes DNA strand decatenation reactions that preferentially binds to ssDNA (67; 68). Through these activities, BLM protects the genome from damage and mediates DNA repair, signaling, DNA formation, and maintenance of telomeres. Constant oxidative stress with ROS overproduction in mitochondria has also been reported in cells of BS patients, which may contribute to the cellular damages (69).

Germline BLM P/LP variants can be of various types but are considered loss-of-function and include missense, nonsense, splice-site, and in-frame as well as frameshift indels. The NM_000057.4:c.2207_2212delinsTAGATTC (p.Tyr736LeufsTer5) frameshift variant, commonly known as blmAsh, is a well-established pathogenic founder variant in the Ashkenazi Jewish population (70). Missense variants involving the highly conserved residues within the helicase domain disrupt the ability to unwind the DNA.

Heterozygous germline P/LP variants in GATA2 were described in four different syndromes with predisposition to myeloid malignancies in an autosomal dominant manner; these syndromes include monoMAC (monocytopenia and mycobacterium avium complex infections), DCML (deficiency of dendritic cells, monocytes, and lymphocytes), familial MDS/AML, and Emberger syndrome (71-74). These four syndromes are now considered as a spectrum of the same disease termed GATA2 deficiency or GATA2 haploinsufficiency. Phenotypic heterogeneity has been described among patients that is at least partially due to incomplete penetrance. Individuals with germline GATA2 P/LP variants frequently demonstrate immunodeficiency and show susceptibility to opportunistic infections. Subsets of patients can present with neutropenia and aplastic anemia/bone marrow failure syndrome (75; 76). The risk of developing myeloid malignancies increases with age; it is estimated to be <10% at the age of 10 years, roughly 40% at the age of 20 years, and 70-80% at the age of 40 years (77). The most common malignancies include MDS, AML, CMML, and less likely B-ALL or T-ALL. Germline GATA2 P/LP variants account for 7% of all primary pediatric MDS cases (78). The most common cytogenetic abnormalities in the myeloid neoplasms associated with germline GATA2 P/LP variants are monosomy 7 or der(1;7)(q10;p10) that leads to deletion of 7q and trisomy 1q; germline GATA2 accounts for 37% of pediatric MDS cases with monosomy 7 (78). Biallelic variants likely are not a common mechanism of leukemogenesis in individuals with germline GATA2 P/LP variants since current studies do not show significant presence of somatic GATA2 alterations in the associated myeloid neoplasms, albeit only a small number of cases were analyzed (79).

Bone marrow examination in individuals with germline GATA2 P/LP variants usually shows hypocellularity that can be in the range of aplastic anemia with or without significant dysplasia (80). Flow cytometric analysis of the bone marrow may demonstrate significantly decreased monocytes, B-cells, NK-cell, and dendritic cells, which can be suggestive features of a germline GATA2 alteration (81).

GATA Binding Protein 2 (GATA2), located on chromosome 3q21.3, encodes a transcription factor essential for hematopoiesis. GATA2 comprises two conserved zinc finger domains (N-ZF and C-ZF), two transactivation domains (TAD), a nuclear localization signal (NLS), and a negative regulatory domain (NRD). The two ZF domains can bind to the specific motifs T/A(GATA)A/G in the promoters/enhancers of target genes and facilitate interaction with other proteins (82). GATA2 regulates the expression of target genes together with a network of other transcriptional regulators such as RUNX1, FLI1, and PU.1 (83; 84). The expression level of GATA2 itself is also tightly regulated throughout development with the highest expression in immature hematopoietic cells that decreases in more mature cells. This dynamic transition of GATA2 expression is critical for stem cell maintenance and lineage differentiation (83). Mouse models with complete Gata2 knockout are nonviable due to a collapse of hematopoietic development, (84), whereas Gata2 haploinsufficiency causes impaired hematopoietic stem cell production without development of myeloid malignancies (85; 86).

Germline GATA2 P/LP variants are considered as loss-of-function and can be of various types including truncating variants (nonsense, frameshift indels, and splice-site variants) prior to or within the C-ZF that lead to the loss of the C-ZF domain, as well as missense variants within the C-ZF domain. Noncoding variants within intron 4 affecting the regulatory region of GATA2 result in haploinsufficiency (78; 87).

Germline P/LP variants in SAMD9 and SAMD9L were first recognized in MIRAGE syndrome and Ataxia-Pancytopenia syndrome, respectively, which are two multisystem disorders associated with hematological abnormalities (88; 89). Since the initial description, germline P/LP variants have also been reported in patients with MDS, BMF, and AML, including cases that are associated with extra-hematopoietic phenotypes, as well as in patients with isolated myeloid abnormalities (76; 90-93). Patients with these germline P/LP variants commonly present at a young age and germline P/LP variants identified in adults are rare (94). The incidence in pediatric MDS ranges from 8-17% and is commonly associated with bone marrow hypoplasia, cytopenia (commonly thrombocytopenia), monosomy 7 or deletion of chromosome 7q (91; 93). Immune dysfunction has also been observed in syndromic patients, commonly severe bacterial or viral infections, while non-syndromic patients can also present with some immune cell abnormalities, including low B cell numbers (reviewed in (95)).

Both SAMD9L and SAMD9 are interferon responsive genes located at 7q21 in a region frequently deleted in myeloid tumors. Expression of the mutant proteins leads to a wide variety of cellular phenotypes, including a decrease in cell cycle progression, decreased protein translation, apoptosis, and variable effects on DNA damage pathways (96). These phenotypes have been observed when mutant SAMD9/SAMD9L are overexpressed in heterologous expression systems (e.g. 293T cells), primary murine or human hematopoietic cells, iPSC derived fibroblasts, and genetically engineered mouse models (93; 96-98). Recent data has also suggested that SAMD9 functions as an anticodon nuclease with specificity for tRNA-Phe and that the germline P/LP variants observed in children with MDS/BMF lead to constitutive nuclease activity (99), possibly explaining the protein translation phenotype.

Most alterations are missense variants, typically in the second half of the coding region. Patients commonly present with monosomy 7 or del7q involving the SAMD9/9L locus at 7q21. Extensive mutational profiling has demonstrated that the clonal population harboring the chromosome 7 deletion universally lacks the mutant allele. Thus, there is strong selective pressure to not express the mutant protein and the chromosomal deletion is a cellular adaptation (92; 93). The observed cellular phenotypes resulting from expression of the mutant protein (i.e. apoptosis, decreased cell growth, and decreased protein translation) provide a mechanistic explanation for this adaptation. Considering that SAMD9L and SAMD9 are interferon-inducible genes, it is tempting to speculate that inflammatory states likely exacerbate the known SAMD9/9L cellular phenotypes. This has been supported using in vivo mouse models in which inflammatory insults were shown to worsen cellular phenotypes and even result in chromosomal deletions involving the murine Samd9l locus on mouse chr6, recapitulating the chromosomal 7 deletions observed in children with germline SAMD9/9L P/LP variants (98). Importantly, chromosome deletions are not the only recognized “rescue” mechanism for patients with these germline P/LP variants. Acquired somatic alterations in cis to the germline variant have been observed and functionally demonstrated to correct the deleterious effect of the germline P/LP variant. Further, duplication of the wild-type arm of chromosome 7q resulting from UPD has also been observed in some patients (92; 93). The mechanism of correction is likely to have a significant impact on clinical phenotypes. The cis alterations and UPD events maintain two copies of chromosome 7, whereas the acquired chromosomal deletions result in haploinsufficiency of key genes on chromosome 7, such as CUX1, and potentially lead to a growth advantage in the appropriate cellular context. Progression to advanced MDS and AML has been associated with additional somatic alterations, including recurrent alterations in SETBP1, NRAS, PTPN11, and RUNX1 (91; 93). In contrast, spontaneous disappearance of the monosomy 7 clone has also been recognized.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are inherited multisystem disorders caused by genetic defects leading to short telomeres (100; 101). The spectrum of TBDs includes classic dyskeratosis congenita (DC), Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson, Revesz, and Coats Plus syndromes. Classic DC is characterized by the triad of abnormal skin pigmentation, nail dyskeratosis, and leukoplakia. Patients often develop other clinical manifestations such as BMF, pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) found in the head, neck, or anogenital area, along with other complications (101; 102). Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson, Revesz, and Coats Plus syndromes are clinical variants of classic DKC that are characterized by early severe onset and other manifestations. Clinical diagnosis of TBDs is complicated by variable phenotypic presentation due to incomplete penetrance, variable expression, and anticipation (103). Most DC patients experience bone marrow failure by age 30, potentially leading to MDS and AML (104-108).

In patients with clinical symptoms suggestive of TBD, a definitive diagnosis can be established through molecular testing of genes associated with TBD or by assessing telomere lengths (101; 109). Autosomal dominant TBD results from monoallelic P/LP variants in genes such as NAF1, RPA1, TERC, TINF2, and ZCCHC8, while autosomal recessive TBD arises from biallelic P/LP variants in genes including CTC1, NHP2, NOP10, STN1, WRAP53, and POT1. Certain genes, like ACD, PARN, RTEL1, and TERT, may exhibit either autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive inheritance patterns. The DKC1 gene exhibits an X-linked inheritance pattern. The measurement of telomere length is commonly performed on peripheral blood leukocytes and lymphocytes via flow- fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) (110; 111).

Telomeres are composed of TTAGGG repeats located at the chromosome ends and are bound with protein complexes known as shelterin (112). Telomere length maintenance is chiefly governed by two key components: the telomerase enzyme complex and shelterin proteins (112-114). TBDs emerge from genetic defects leading to impaired telomere maintenance and accelerated telomere attrition, with such genetic defects identified in 80% of cases (101; 115). Shortening of telomeres is a result of incomplete replication of the ends of linear chromosomes in each replication cycle, a phenomenon referred to as the ‘end replication problem’ (112; 116-118). Structurally, the telomeric DNA consists of a double-stranded DNA region extending several kilobases, terminating in a single-stranded 3’ overhang varying between 50 and 400 base pairs (115; 118). The length of telomeres varies according to tissue type and race and can be influenced by environmental factors, including smoking, and is inversely related to age (119; 120). The ‘end replication problem’ results in exposed DNA ends that can be erroneously identified as double-strand DNA breaks, thus leading to activation of DNA damage response and cellular arrest, senescence, and apoptosis (112; 116-118; 121-124). The bypass of cellular arrest through somatic alterations in genes like RB1 and TP53 accelerates telomere shortening, resulting in a telomere crisis characterized by genomic instability and tumor progression (112) (Sidebar: TP53 and its controversy). Conversely, an uncontrolled increase in telomere length can promote cellular immortality and proliferation, which also contributes to cancer progression (125-127). Therefore, the regulation and maintenance of telomere lengths are a key mechanism in tumorigenesis (112).

Telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein complex, plays a crucial role in the synthesis of telomeric DNA repeats and functions by adding the nucleotides to telomeres using its intrinsic RNA template (hTR) (128). Tissues with a capacity for self-renewal maintain telomerase expression; it is largely inactive in the majority of somatic tissues (129-132). The catalytic unit of telomerase includes a reverse transcriptase, encoded by the TERT gene, and the human telomerase RNA that serves as the integral RNA template, encoded by TERC (112; 128). Essential constituents including DKC1, NOP10, NHP2, NAF1, WRAP53, PARN, and ZCCHC8 play a crucial role in the assembly, trafficking, recruitment, and stabilization of the telomerase complex, as well as in the processing of hTR (133-140) .

The shelterin complex is an intricate assembly of six proteins encoded by specific genes: TRF1 and TRF2, encoded by TERF1 and TERF2 genes, respectively, bind to duplex telomeric sequences. The protein POT1, encoded by the POT1 gene, specifically localizes to the single 3′ telomeric overhang. TRF1, TRF2, and POT1 are further connected through TIN2 (encoded by TINF2), TPP1 (encoded by ACD), and RAP1 (encoded by TERF2IP), all of which play roles in the regulation of telomere length (100; 118; 141). The shelterin complex regulates telomere length through facilitating T-loop formation, inhibiting the DNA damage response, and recruiting telomerase to telomeric DNA.

Genetic defects in TINF2 and ACD are associated with unusually long telomeres, thereby increasing the risk for certain tumor types, or with short telomeres, which are linked to TBD (115; 142-146). Notably, inactivating heterozygous variants in POT1, including both truncating and missense variants, are correlated with extended telomeres and a predisposition to CLL, melanoma, angiosarcoma, and gliomas (147-150); while homozygous missense variants associated with elongated telomeres were reported in Coats plus syndrome, contrary to shortened telomeres associated with TBD (151). The establishment of genotype-phenotype correlations will necessitate extensive large-scale cohort studies and detailed functional analyses.

Fanconi anemia (FA) is a heterogenous multisystem group of genetic disorders and is recognized as the predominant cause of inherited bone marrow failure syndromes (IBMFS) (152; 153). The underlying pathophysiology involves defective repair of interstrand crosslinking (ICL) of DNA, resulting from defects in genes associated with the FA and/or BRCA pathways (154). The average prevalence of FA is approximately 1 in 136,000 newborns (154; 155). Individuals with FA often present with a constellation of physical abnormalities, including microcephaly, ophthalmic tract defects, short stature, skeletal anomalies of extremities, abnormal skin pigmentation, and genitourinary system defects (156). The age of clinical presentation varies with a median age of onset of 7 years (157). Initial hematological manifestations of FA include thrombocytopenia with macrocytosis and increased hemoglobin F, followed by anemia or neutropenia (157; 158). Cross-sectional studies report that the occurrence of MDS in individuals with FA ranges from 11% to 34% (159-162). Moreover, the cumulative likelihood of developing AML in individuals with FA is estimated to be between 10% and 37% by the age of 50, with the peak incidence of leukemia typically manifesting during the teenage years or early adulthood (159; 160; 163-165). Additionally, individuals with FA are at a high risk for developing solid tumor cancers, most notably SCC in head and neck, upper gastrointestinal, and low gynecologic tracts (163; 166).

FA diagnosis can be established by germline genetic testing or chromosomal breakage studies. Genetic testing can confirm the FA diagnosis driven by P/LP variants in 21 genes that are known to be associated with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (see Table 1), FANCB which is associated with X-linked recessive inheritance, and FANCR (RAD51) which is autosomal dominant. Alternatively, DNA extracted from FA patient’s lymphocyte are characterized by hypersensitivity to diepoxybutane (DEB) and mitomycin C (MMC) and demonstrate chromosomal breakage and radial chromosomes.

Cytogenetic studies performed on bone marrow aspirate smears aim to detect early clonal abnormalities that dictate patient management. Common cytogenetic abnormalities include gain of 1q and 3q, and loss of 7 or 7q (167). Additionally, frequent cryptic alterations in RUNX1/AML1 regions at the long arm of chromosome 21 were reported in high-risk patients developing MDS/AML (168).

Interstrand crosslinks (ICLs) are produced by both exogenous sources, like platinum or psoralens chemotherapeutics, and endogenous sources, including from metabolites of substances such as alcohol, smoking, and dietary fat (169; 170). The FA pathway collaborates with homologous recombination (HR), forming an intricate network of genes and proteins. This network functions synergistically to detect ICLs, facilitate bypass of these crosslinks, and repair DNA. Within the hematopoietic system, an inability to repair ICLs due to genetic defects in the FA pathway triggers a DNA damage response (169)that results in activation of the TP53 pathway, followed by cellular arrest and, ultimately, bone marrow failure (171; 172).

The core complex of the FA pathway is composed of several FANC proteins, namely FANCA, FANCB, FANCC, FANCE, FANCF, FANCG, FANCL, FANCM, FANCT, FAAP100, MHF1, MHF2, FAAP20, and FAAP24. FANCM with MHF1 (FAAP16) and MHF2 (FAAP10), and FAAP24 are integral to recognizing and guiding the FA core complex to the ICL (154; 173) whereas FANCM phosphorylates ATR to activate the DNA damage response. The FANCL gene encodes for an E3 ubiquitin ligase, while the FANCT (UBE2T) gene produces an E2 ubiquitin ligase and together they work within the FA core complex to monoubiquitinate the FANCD2-FANCI complex (174; 175). Once ubiquitylated, the FANCD2-FANCI complex interacts with SLX4/FANCP to recruit and activate various structure-specific endonucleases to unhook the ICL (176-179). The deubiquitylation of the FANCD2-FANCI complex is mediated by ubiquitin carboxy-terminal hydrolase 1 (USP1) with USP1-associated factor (UAF1), which is essential for the recycling of these proteins within the FA pathway (176).

The unhooking of DNA results in one of the DNA strands with tethered ICL, necessitating a bypass step for DNA replication to continue, while the opposite parental strand develops DNA double strand breaks (DDSB) (154; 180). Translesion synthesis in the strand with tethered ICL occurs predominantly by polymerase zeta (Polζ), which also includes FANCV/REV7 (181). This synthesized strand is then used as a template for HR to repair the broken strand during S phase (182; 183). Several FANC genes, such as FANCD1 (BRCA2), FANCJ (BRIP1), FANCN (PALB2), FANCR (RAD51), and FANCS (BRCA1), are implicated in HR (154).

Ribosomopathies are a heterogenous group of disorders resulting from defects that impair ribosomal synthesis and function, leading to distinct clinical manifestations. Inherited ribosomopathies associated with hematological manifestations include Diamond-Blackfan anemia (DBA), Shwachman-Diamond syndrome (SDS), and X-linked DC (184). DBA patients exhibit features including severe anemia, growth retardation, and congenital anomalies of the craniofacial region and thumbs. MDS and AML have been described in DBA (185). SDS, a BMF syndrome resulting from P/LP variants in the SBDS gene, leads to exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, hematologic abnormalities, notably neutropenia, neurocognitive impairment, and liver abnormalities (186). X-linked DC, associated with the DKC1 gene, manifests similar to other TBDs. Other conditions such as cartilage-hair hypoplasia (CHH), Treacher-Collins syndrome (TCS), Bowen-Conradi syndrome, and North American Indian childhood cirrhosis, are also included in the spectrum of ribosomopathies; however these are not associated with hematological manifestations (187). Additionally, somatic deletions in the RPS14 gene, as a part of 5q-syndrome, are associated with MDS (188).

Certain ribosomopathies, including DBA, SDS, X-linked DC, and TCS, are associated with stress response and characterized by activation of TP53 (189-191). In those disorders, a subset of defective ribosomal proteins interact with and inhibit MDM2, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that degrades TP53, to activate TP53 with subsequent cellular arrest and apoptosis (192; 193). Of note, no cancer risk or hematological manifestations have been reported in TCS. Additionally, activation of TP53 in X-linked DC by DKC1 is mediated by activation of DNA damage response due to shortened telomeres (see TBD sections).

The elongation factor-like GTPase 1 (EFL1) and its essential cofactor SBDS mediate the removal of the anti-association factor eIF6, which is required for ribosomal maturation and assembly (186; 194). The majority of SDS cases are caused by P/LP autosomal recessive variants in the SBDS gene that disrupt eIF6 eviction. Biallelic P/LP variants in other genes such as DNAJC21 and EFL1, and monoallelic P/LP variants in SRP54 have also been associated with SDS like-features (186; 195-199).

In DBA, P/LP variants in more than 20 ribosomal protein genes contribute to the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of the disorder (see Table 1). The most common P/LP variants are found in the RPS19 gene, accounting for approximately 25% of DBA cases (200). Additionally, X-linked inheritance patterns in GATA1, a gene encoding a critical transcription factor for erythroid differentiation (201), and TSR2, which has a role in ribosome biogenesis, have been associated with DBA (202).

Severe congenital neutropenia (SCN) is a rare disorder with an estimated prevalence of 3 to 8.5 occurrences per million people (203-205). It is characterized by a selective maturation arrest of myeloid progenitor cells with subsequent decrease in the number of neutrophils, which in turn, increases the risk for recurrent infections. The mortality rate from sepsis in patients with SCN has improved significantly with the administration of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) therapy. However, transformation to MDS or AML has been reported as a long-term risk of G-CSF (206), particularly with acquired truncating alterations in CSF3R (207; 208).

The causative genetic P/LP variants interfere with various stages of neutrophil development and function, leading to granulopoiesis arrest. The most well-studied genetic P/LP variant in SCN involves the ELANE gene, which codes for neutrophil elastase, a protease essential for neutrophil function (209). Autosomal dominant P/LP variants in ELANE disrupt the proper folding of the neutrophil elastase protein, leading to apoptosis and premature death of neutrophil precursors (210). Autosomal recessive HAX1 P/LP variants lead to impaired mitochondrial integrity and function, triggering apoptosis in myeloid cells (211). Other genes implicated in SCN have been also reported, including CLPB (reviewed in (203; 212)).

Identifying individuals who should be offered germline testing relies on assessment of family history, physical exam findings, age of malignancy development, and history of multiple cancers. A panel-based strategy with next generation sequencing (NGS) technology is usually used to identify germline P/LP variants accompanied by genetic counseling, although some centers are also employing more comprehensive sequencing strategies, including whole genome sequencing (213; 214). The assay should allow for the identification of both single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and large insertions/deletions/duplications since the P/LP variants cover all types. Germline testing is crucial in selecting suitable hematopoietic stem cell donors, especially among family members who may carry the same germline driver (215). Cultured fibroblasts from skin biopsy are the preferred specimen for germline testing, although the long turnaround time (3-6 weeks) is a major disadvantage. Samples such as peripheral blood or bone marrow should be avoided to identify germline P/LP variants that may predispose to hematologic neoplasms since they may be involved by the associated hematopoietic malignancies. Alternative samples such as hair bulbs and nail clippings have been used in some institutions although DNA quantity may be insufficient in a subset of samples (3). Saliva and buccal swabs may have contamination by leukocytes harboring somatic mutations.

With the broad use of somatic cancer gene panels for tumor screening, the presence of a variant suspicious of germline origin should trigger germline workup and genetic referral for the patient. Experts have suggested lists of genes in which further evaluations should be considered when variants with high allele frequencies are identified in tumors (216; 217).

Recommendations for management and monitoring of BMF involves an initial baseline complete blood count (CBC), bone marrow aspiration and biopsy, and cytogenetic studies including chromosomal analysis and FISH. The frequency of these evaluations is dependent on the severity of BMF, presence of clonal cytogenetic abnormalities, and disease progression. The two primary treatment options are hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) and androgen therapy. HCT is the only curative therapy for BMF. Androgen therapy is a viable option (100; 101) for patients with moderate BMF who lack a donor, those who are medically ineligible for HCT due to pre-existing organ dysfunction or ongoing infections, and patients who choose not to undergo HCT.

To evaluate for potential MDS progression in patients with P/LP germline variants with increased risks, follow-up bone marrow examinations at clinically indicated intervals are common practice. Bone marrow findings in individuals with germline P/LP variants in genes associated with cancer predisposition can include low cellularity and cytologic atypia, which are features that overlap with MDS. Comprehensive evaluation including clinical, morphological, and molecular findings are required to make the diagnosis and to avoid a premature diagnosis of MDS. As stated in the recently updated international consensus classification (ICC) (42), progression to MDS can be associated with progressive cytopenias, increased marrow cellularity, multilineage dysplasia, cytogenetic abnormalities, new genetic alterations, or increased blasts. Presence of MDS-defining genetic alterations including monosomy 7, monosomy 5, del(7q), del(5q), multi-hit TP53 alterations, and SF3B1 alteration favor a diagnosis of MDS, but genetic alterations should be interpreted in the context of the specific germline conditions.

Myeloid neoplasms with germline predisposition are a heterogeneous group of disorders caused by P/LP variants in genes affecting a wide range of pathways. This includes pathways involved in stem cell development and hematopoietic lineage differentiation, telomere biology, ribosome biogenesis, and helicase function, among others. While there are known genes associated with each of these conditions, further study of genotype-phenotype correlations continues to expand our knowledge of causal genetic aberrations related to these conditions and comprehensive NGS strategies continue to nominate new potential genetic causes. Management of conditions predisposing to myeloid neoplasms including AML, CML, CMML, JMML, and MDS, as well as IBMFS, is complex. HCT is the only curative therapy for the hematologic manifestations of these conditions. Continued studies of the somatic alterations associated with specific germline aberrations may lead to novel therapies and improved management of individuals with conditions predisposing to IBMFS and myeloid neoplasms.
