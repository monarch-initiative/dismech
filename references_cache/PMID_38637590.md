Despite affecting ~11-13% of women globally, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is
a substantially understudied condition. PCOS, possibly extending to men's
health, imposes a considerable health and economic burden worldwide. Diagnosis
in adults follows the International Evidence-based Guideline for the Assessment
and Management of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, requiring two out of three criteria
- clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and/or
specific ovarian morphological characteristics or elevated anti-Müllerian
hormone. However, diagnosing adolescents omits ovarian morphology and
anti-Müllerian hormone considerations. PCOS, marked by insulin resistance and
hyperandrogenism, strongly contributes to early-onset type 2 diabetes, with
increased odds for cardiovascular diseases. Reproduction-related implications
include irregular menstrual cycles, anovulatory infertility, heightened risks of
pregnancy complications and endometrial cancer. Beyond physiological
manifestations, PCOS is associated with anxiety, depression, eating disorders,
psychosexual dysfunction and negative body image, collectively contributing to
diminished health-related quality of life in patients. Despite its high
prevalence persisting into menopause, diagnosing PCOS often involves extended
timelines and multiple health-care visits. Treatment remains ad hoc owing to
limited understanding of underlying mechanisms, highlighting the need for
research delineating the aetiology and pathophysiology of the syndrome.
Identifying factors contributing to PCOS will pave the way for personalized
medicine approaches. Additionally, exploring novel biomarkers, refining
diagnostic criteria and advancing treatment modalities will be crucial in
enhancing the precision and efficacy of interventions that will positively
impact the lives of patients.

(PCOS) is a substantially understudied condition. PCOS, possibly 
extending to men’s health, imposes a considerable health and economic 
burden worldwide. Diagnosis in adults follows the International 
Evidence-based Guideline for the Assessment and Management of 
Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, requiring two out of three criteria — clinical 
or biochemical hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and/or 
specific ovarian morphological characteristics or elevated anti-Müllerian 
hormone. However, diagnosing adolescents omits ovarian morphology 
and anti-Müllerian hormone considerations. PCOS, marked by insulin 
resistance and hyperandrogenism, strongly contributes to early-onset 
type 2 diabetes, with increased odds for cardiovascular diseases. 
Reproduction-related implications include irregular menstrual cycles, 
anovulatory infertility, heightened risks of pregnancy complications 
and endometrial cancer. Beyond physiological manifestations, PCOS

diminished health-related quality of life in patients. Despite its high 
prevalence persisting into menopause, diagnosing PCOS often involves 
extended timelines and multiple health-care visits. Treatment remains 
ad hoc owing to limited understanding of underlying mechanisms,

pathophysiology of the syndrome. Identifying factors contributing 
to PCOS will pave the way for personalized medicine approaches. 
Additionally, exploring novel biomarkers, refining diagnostic criteria 
and advancing treatment modalities will be crucial in enhancing the 
precision and efficacy of interventions that will positively impact  
the lives of patients.

Epidemiology
Prevalence
Being the most common lifelong endocrinopathy in women, the criteria 
to diagnose PCOS has evolved from being a consensus Rotterdam cri-
teria25 to an evidence-based guideline criteria1. However, controversy 
surrounds the inclusion of polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) in 
diagnostic criteria and relates to the lack of specificity of PCOM, the con-
tinuum of follicle counts without a clear delineating cut point, changes 
with age, methodological limitations with evolving technologies,  
and challenges with reporting26.

Introduction
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a complex hormonal condition 
characterized by signs of elevated androgens, irregular menstrua -
tion and polycystic ovaries. PCOS is an understudied yet major health 
threat for women and, possibly, for men (Box 1). Worldwide, ~11–13% 
of women suffer from PCOS 1, resulting in a substantial health and  
economic burden globally2–4.
Based on the International Evidence-based Guideline for the 
Assessment and Management of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome1, PCOS 
diagnosis in adults requires at least two of the following three criteria —  
clinical hyperandrogenism and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism 
with elevated total testosterone, free androgen index, or bioavailable 
testosterone; ovulatory dysfunction with irregular or absent men -
strual cycles; or polycystic ovaries with multiple small antral follicles 
and/or enlarged ovaries and/or elevated anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) 
levels1. In adolescents, a definitive diagnosis requires hyperandrogen-
ism and irregular cycles but does not include ovarian morphology or 
AMH levels as they are a normal part of development in adolescents.
PCOS, hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance are strong con-
tributors to the development of early-onset type 2 diabetes mellitus 
(T2DM)5,6, with preceding hyperinsulinaemia and insulin resistance7. 
The prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors, including excess 
weight, impaired glucose tolerance, T2DM, dyslipidaemia, hyperten-
sion and metabolic syndrome 8–11, are consistently higher in women 
with PCOS, likely contributing to the increased odds for cardiovascular 
disease, including ischaemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, 
stroke and, potentially, cardiovascular mortality12–14.
In individuals of reproductive age, PCOS is the most common 
cause of irregular menstrual cycles and anovulatory infertility1. More-
over, PCOS causes considerably increased but poorly appreciated 
pregnancy complications, including early miscarriage 1, gestational 
diabetes, hypertension and pre-eclampsia, and is associated with a 
markedly increased risk of endometrial cancer15–17.

Male phenotype of PCOS
Although studies implicate that a male equivalent of polycystic 
ovary syndrome (PCOS) exists, especially among men with a mother 
and/or sister with the syndrome, no diagnostic criteria are available. 
Furthermore, the mechanisms causing observed endocrine and 
metabolic alterations in men are unclear291. The early onset of 
androgenic alopecia (before 35 years of age) has been observed 
in men with a family history of PCOS292. Moreover, men with early 
androgenic alopecia exhibit elevated circulating testosterone, 
dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, and luteinizing hormone and 
decreased follicle-stimulating hormone and sex hormone-binding 
globulin levels293. Reduced levels of follicle-stimulating hormone and 
sex hormone-binding globulin may be potentially linked to insulin 
resistance294 — features equivalent to those in women with PCOS.
Consistent with these findings, sons of women with PCOS 
have greater obesity than sons of women without PCOS with lipid 
dysfunction, suggestive of latent insulin resistance295. Moreover, 
brothers of women with PCOS have increased anti-Müllerian hormone 
levels, altered gonadotrophin and steroidogenic secretion296,297, and a 
metabolic phenotype characterized by dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance 
and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction as well as an increased cardiovascular 
disease risk298–301. Current evidence indicates that genetic risk factors for 
PCOS contribute to an increased likelihood of obesity, type 2 diabetes 
mellitus, cardiovascular disease and androgenic alopecia in men96, 
aligning with earlier observations of familial clustering of the metabolic 
phenotype in brothers of women with PCOS302.
Although genetic components likely play a role in the male 
PCOS phenotype, clinical observations suggest that maternal 
obesity and PCOS may also impact the development of male fetuses, 
predisposing them to reproductive and metabolic disorders later in 
life, as observed in their female siblings106,109.

PCOS exhibit geographic and ethnic variability in symptom presenta-
tion, for example, Hispanic and African American people with PCOS 
have greater insulin resistance than other ethnicities29,30. AMH levels 
have been shown to correlate with PCOM and are now recommended 
in international guidelines for adult PCOS diagnosis with moderate 
evidence certainty26,28. However, AMH levels have limitations similar 
to PCOM, with a lack of clear cut-off points for PCOS, changes with age, 
variable assays, and challenges in reporting owing to a lack of global 
standards and limited evidence across those of different ancestry.
Obesity exacerbates endocrine abnormalities and symptoms in 
individuals with PCOS30,31 and increases prevalence32. Moreover, one 
Chinese study suggests that the prevalence of PCOS increases with age, 
being ~10% in adolescents between 10 and 20 years of age and 17% in 
women 21–30 years of age29. Two population-based studies have also 
reported such high prevalence in adulthood27,28. Owing to the decline in 
ovarian reserve and androgen levels towards menopause, the number 
of women presenting with PCOS symptoms or findings diminishes, 
thereby lowering the prevalence of the syndrome with increasing 
age33. However, given that irregular menstrual cycles and PCOM are 
physiological in adolescents, evidence and guidelines recommend 
not including these features in adolescents to avoid overdiagnosis. 
Although the underlying syndrome persists, ageing women with PCOS 
may eventually get increasingly regular cycles and PCOM is lost, making 
diagnosis challenging in older women (>50 years of age)29. However, 
long-term data suggests that PCOS-related comorbidity persists to 
perimenopausal years and beyond 34,35. Hence, PCOS prevalence in 
fertile age likely reflects the true overall prevalence (Fig. 1).
Comorbidities
Obesity. Obesity is a common comorbidity in PCOS, affecting 20–80% 
of patients, where clinical cohorts tend to capture those with higher 
weight than non-selected populations26,31,36. In population-based stud-
ies, clinical measures of obesity include BMI, waist circumference and 
waist-to-hip ratio 37. Many of these indicators are elevated in PCOS, 
although no optimal indicator has been established 38. The develop-
ment of overweight in PCOS starts early, possibly before 5 years of age, 
supporting early onset of the syndrome39. Weight gain is particularly 
pronounced from puberty to adulthood, for example, 11% of individuals 
with PCOS have exhibited a high BMI trajectory, compared with 6.6% in 
controls without PCOS, resulting in a +2.6 kg difference over a 10-year 
follow-up31,40. However, the weight gap narrows towards menopause, 
as women without PCOS also gain weight. Ethnic differences exist in 
terms of weight gain associated with PCOS. For instance, American 
Hispanic people, African Americans and South Pacific Islanders show 
the highest weight, whereas Europeans and South or East Asians have 
low BMIs but more central obesity 41. Of note, different BMI cut-offs 
for overweight and obesity apply to Asian populations, warranting 
consideration when making cross-ethnic comparisons42.
Hyperandrogenism. Epidemiological data indicates that 40–80% of 
women with PCOS exhibit hyperandrogenism24. The clinical manifes-
tations of hyperandrogenism vary depending on ethnicity, obesity 
and age. Hirsutism (excess hair growth) affects ~70–80% of patients 
compared with 13% (range 8–20%) in the general population, with 
geographic variations24. Acne prevalence is ~43% (95% CI 41–45%) in 
individuals with PCOS compared with 21% (95% CI 19–22%) in those 
without PCOS, with a higher rate of acne in adolescents than in adults 
or those with hyperandrogenic PCOS, with some geographic varia -
tion43. Female pattern hair loss prevalence is estimated at 28% (95% 
CI 22–34%) in women with PCOS44. Importantly, these manifestations 
are associated with substantial psychological distress, emphasizing 
the need for comprehensive treatment of women with the syndrome45.
Metabolic dysfunction. Approximately 50–80% of women with PCOS 
exhibit insulin resistance46,47. These individuals face an elevated risk 
of pre-diabetes and up to fourfold increased risk for T2DM, with the 
condition developing at an earlier age than in women without PCOS35. 
Although T2DM risk is increased in individuals with PCOS regardless 
of BMI, variations in risk for insulin resistance and T2DM are noted in  
individuals with different BMI, geographical locations and ethnicities41,48.
Cardiovascular disease. Current analyses have strengthened the evi-
dence for increased risk for different cardiovascular disease outcomes 
in individuals with PCOS13,49. Large data sets now indicate potentially 
increased risk for major cardiovascular end points12,35, including myo-
cardial infarction, stroke, heart failure and cardiovascular mortal -
ity12,35,50. The analysis showed the hazard ratio (HR) for the composite 
end point (that is, when combining two or more end points of interest 
within a single variable) of 1.26 (95% CI 1.13–1.41) in a UK data set and 
2.33 (1.26–4.30, Rotterdam criteria) and 2.47 (1.18–5.17, NIH criteria) 
in population-based data from Finland35. Importantly, this risk seems 
to accumulate at a relatively young age (~30–35 years onwards), even 
before menopause. Although the absolute risk is low as the populations 
assessed have been relatively young (in most studies, women were 
aged <55 years), evidence from systematic reviews resulted in updated 
guideline recommendations, which call for regular screening and early 
prevention as well as research conducted in women in postmenopause1.
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. Studies 
have demonstrated a close link between PCOS and major risk factors 
for the development of liver disease, including obesity, dyslipidaemia, 
insulin resistance, cardiovascular disease and hyperandrogenism 51.

One meta-analysis indicates a 43% increased prevalence of meta -
bolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) 52  
(95% CI 35–52%) in women with PCOS53. Further analyses show that the 
increased MASLD prevalence in these women could potentially be medi-
ated by a high prevalence of metabolic syndrome, elevated homeostatic 
model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), free androgen index 
and circulating testosterone53. Although MASLD is highly prevalent in 
relatively young women with PCOS, absolute risk is still low and evidence 
is yet not strong enough to justify screening. Thus, further research into 
underlying aetiology and pathophysiology is needed.
Infertility. Infertility risk is 15 times higher in women with PCOS than 
in women without PCOS54,55, yet the likelihood of having at least one 
child is similar between both groups of women56. Counselling should 
emphasize the potential for pregnancy, though it may take time, often 
requiring fertility treatments, especially in individuals with excess 
weight or obesity54,55,57. Spontaneous ovulation can occur, necessitating 
contraception if pregnancy is not desired. As women with PCOS reach 
~35–40 years of age, regular cycles and possible ovulation can emerge, 
but the implications for later fertility remain uncertain.
Obstetric complications. Women with PCOS face a twofold to three-
fold increased risk of pregnancy complications, including miscar -
riage, pregnancy-induced hypertension, pre-eclampsia, gestational 
diabetes, prematurity and small-for-gestational-age offspring58. These 
risks underscore the long-term health implications in daughters and 
likely also sons born to women with the syndrome. Although gesta-
tional diabetes risk is independent of BMI, a higher BMI increases 
the risk in certain white populations 59,60. By contrast, pregnancy-
induced hypertension and pre-eclampsia seem to have stronger, 
BMI-independent effects 58.
Psychological disorders. PCOS is associated with a high prevalence 
of mental health disorders. Independent of obesity, diagnostic crite-
ria or geographical region, 36.6% of women with PCOS present with 
moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms and 41.8% present with anxi-
ety symptoms compared with 13.2% and 8.5%, respectively, in con -
trols8. Adolescents also have higher odds of depression (OR 2.26, 95% 
CI 1.36–3.76) than controls, although evidence is limited for anxi -
ety symptoms according to the updated evidence-based guideline24. 
Nevertheless, a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated 
that symptoms of both anxiety and depression are more prevalent 
amongst adolescents with PCOS than amongst adolescents without 
PCOS23. Moreover, the perinatal and postpartum periods may present 
an additional risk for depression 61. Individuals with PCOS also have 
increased odds of binge eating disorder (OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.29–1.82)24. 
PCOS-specific features that impact feminine identities, such as excess 
weight, hirsutism and acne, lead to increased body image distress, 
reduced sexual function and decreased sexual satisfaction24,62. Impor-
tantly, body image distress may be a mediator of anxiety and depressive 
symptoms in individuals with PCOS61.
Cancer.  PCOS is associated with a twofold overall increased risk  
for cancer63, with the strongest evidence supporting elevated endome-
trial cancer risk (fivefold) owing to anovulation, progesterone resis -
tance, hyperinsulinaemia, overweight and low-grade inflammation15,64,65.  
Although Mendelian randomization analyses have linked PCOS to 
breast cancer66,67, clinical evidence is lacking 13,68. Possible protective 
effects of anovulation on breast tissue have not been studied. Findings 
from some studies suggest an increased risk of ovarian cancer, although 
cervical cancer risk seems unaffected68,69.
Postmenopause, long-term morbidity and mortality. PCOS emerges 
as a risk factor for multimorbidity and chronic medication use, even 
at postmenopause34,63. Although gynaecological manifestations of 
PCOS decline with age, metabolic issues and psychological distress, 
especially depressive symptoms, persist13,45,70. Studies also reveal an 
increase in major adverse cardiovascular end points like myocardial 
infarction, stroke, heart failure and cardiovascular mortality beyond 
menopause12,35,49,71, and hyperandrogenic status, at any point, seems 
to drive metabolic morbidity risks14.
Mortality data in PCOS is mixed 72–74, but a large register study 
reports increased overall mortality in PCOS50, with contributions from 
cardiovascular outcomes and endometrial cancer.
Mechanisms/pathophysiology
Genetic factors
Family and twin studies provide robust evidence for the genetic under-
pinnings of PCOS75. The correlation for PCOS between monozygotic 
twin sisters was twice as high as the correlation in dizygotic twins; 
heritability of PCOS was estimated at >70% in this twin study76. Early 
candidate gene research provided limited replicable genetic associa-
tions77. High-throughput genome-wide association studies (GWAS) 
have advanced our understanding, and initial GWAS in Han Chinese 
and European women identified 16 loci78–81 (Table 1).
Since then, an international consortium assembled data from 
>10,000 PCOS cases and 103,000 controls82. This GWAS meta-analysis 
identified 14 loci, of which three (MAPRE1, ZBTB16 and PLGRKT) were 
novel. Regardless of diagnostic criteria (NIH, Rotterdam or self-reported 
(hyperandrogenism and irregular cycles)), 13 of the 14 loci were consist-
ently associated with PCOS. Subsequent GWAS in electronic health 
record-linked biobanks identified one locus (SOD2) 83. In Northern 
Europeans, two independent rare variants in CHEK2  and a common 
variant in MYO10 were identified 84. Overall, several genes near loci 
discovered by GWAS implicate neuroendocrine (FSHR, LHCGR and 
FSHB) and metabolic dysfunction (INSR, THADA and HMGA2) in the 
pathogenesis of PCOS, whereas the role of other genes remains to be 
identified. Of note, although loci are named based on genes in the vicin-
ity, functional studies are needed to establish the effector gene at each 
locus. Studies in ovarian theca cells suggest that PA2G4 and ZNF217 may 
be the effector genes at the 12q13.2 and 20q13.2 loci, respectively, and 
could be a potential target for the treatment of ovarian dysfunction85,86.
Large-scale GWAS results have enabled Mendelian randomization 
analyses to examine causality between PCOS and other phenotypes87. 
Mendelian randomization utilizes genetic variants associated with 
an exposure to assess the causality of an exposure with an outcome 
without being affected by confounders that influence epidemiological 
studies. Mendelian randomization has suggested that high BMI79,82,88–90, 
insulin resistance79, increased fasting insulin 5 and reduced levels of 
sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG)6,79 may cause PCOS. However, 
another Mendelian randomization analysis did not find a causal role 
for PCOS in T2DM, coronary heart disease or stroke, suggesting that 
PCOS and cardiometabolic disease share common underpinnings (for 
example, obesity) rather than a causal relationship91. Moreover, PCOS 
seems to be associated with genetic variants that postpone menopause 
in women79,82. Conversely, PCOS may increase the incidence of oestro-
gen receptor-positive breast cancer66,67 and decrease endometrioid 
ovarian cancer92.

The existence of PCOS subtypes has been proposed, including 
individuals with normal weight but an increased ratio of luteinizing 
hormone to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and individuals with 
overweight or obesity with insulin resistance 93. A study performed 
unsupervised hierarchical cluster analysis of anthropometric, repro-
ductive and metabolic traits in 893 individuals with PCOS94 and iden-
tified a distinct ‘reproductive’ cluster with high luteinizing hormone 
and SHBG levels and a ‘metabolic’ cluster with high BMI, glucose and 
insulin levels. A GWAS stratified by BMI found shared as well as distinct 
genetic architecture in a lean PCOS subtype versus an obesity and over-
weight PCOS subtype95. Delineating specific subtypes of PCOS in large 
prospective studies, with validation in independent cohorts, offers the 
potential for precision medicine in PCOS. Notably, one study indicated 
that men with high polygenic risk scores for PCOS had increased risk 
for developing obesity, T2DM, cardiovascular disease and male-pattern 
baldness, highlighting the impact of PCOS on both sexes96 (Box 1).
Despite progress, the mechanistic details of how genetic variants 
contribute to PCOS, including the specific genes involved and their 
altered functions, remain unclear. A deeper understanding of these 
mechanisms is crucial for translating genetic discoveries into effective 
PCOS therapies.
Environmental and epigenetic factors
Non-genetic factors may also be involved in the pathophysiology of 
PCOS97 and its inheritance could be attributed to genetic–epigenetic 
interactions98 (Fig. 2). An aberrant maternal–fetal environment can con-
tribute to epigenetic changes, potentially affecting fetal development. 
Such maternal factors could be weight gain during early pregnancy in 
women with PCOS99 and sustained high levels of androgens and AMH 
throughout gestation100, negatively impacting placental aromatase 
activity101,102. Although exposure to environmental pollutants, including 
pharmaceuticals and personal care products, microplastics, endo -
crine disruptors, and nanoparticles, could potentially increase the 
risk of PCOS in the offspring, the evidence is still scarce, warranting  
further research103.

hypothesis 104, offspring of mothers with PCOS exhibit elongated 
anogenital distance, an indicator of in utero androgen exposure 105.

increased likelihood of developing obesity and dyslipidaemia, sug -

Hyperandrogenism
Elevated adrenal production of dehydroepiandrosterone and dehy-
droepiandrosterone sulfate, which through circulation reach peripheral 
target tissues and are then converted to oestrogens or androgens, can 
contribute to hyperandrogenism in women with PCOS113,114. However, the 
characteristic hyperandrogenism — the primary reproductive pheno-
type of PCOS — results from excess ovarian androgen production (Fig. 3). 
The theca cells of the ovary are the primary site of production of andros-
tenedione and testosterone and cells isolated from patients with PCOS 
show an intrinsic increase in androgen production in culture115. In vivo, 
luteinizing hormone and insulin are the primary stimulators of androgen 
production in combination with an absolute increase in the number of 
theca cells, which can be a consequence of increased follicle numbers 
and increased theca cell thickness116. A previously unrecognized gene 
variant identified by GWAS, DENND1A78, has been recognized in PCOS as 
a significant stimulator of the androgen pathway with several microRNA 
variants also implicated in the pathophysiology117. The ovarian granulosa 
cell layer is responsible for the aromatization of androgens to oestrogens 
under the influence of FSH, and most patients with PCOS exhibit high 
circulating levels of androgens, with consequences on multiple organ 
systems, including the skin, brain, adipose tissue and liver. T estoster-
one and androstenedione are the commonly elevated androgens and 
are amplified by the resultant low levels of SHBG, leading to elevated 
free androgens accessible to tissue androgen receptors118. Moreover, 
women with PCOS have insulin resistance with hyperinsulinaemia, which 
further decreases SHBG production in the liver and synergizes the effect 
of luteinizing hormone on theca cells, thereby contributing to elevated 
circulating levels of androgens119.
Ovarian function
The ovary contains many small follicles with oocytes that initially 
develop under the control of intra-ovarian growth factors and later 
under the influence of FSH following the development of appropriate 
receptors. Small (<9 mm) antral follicles produce large quantities of 
AMH (whereas large ones do not), which regulates the passage of fol-
licular growth to preantral, larger follicles in association with androgen 
receptors preceding the expression of FSH receptors120 (Fig. 3). High 
levels of androgens, AMH and paracrine regulators tend to prevent 
the spontaneous growth of antral follicles that can ovulate; however, 
this blockage can be overcome by exogenous FSH administration dur-
ing fertility treatment. Elevated levels of follistatin and diminished 
concentrations of activin A have been proposed to potentially con-
tribute to the arrest of follicular development (that is, not growing

Endometrial dysfunction
Endometrial dysfunction occurs in women with PCOS and anovula -
tion owing to the absence of the corpus luteum, leading to low pro -
gesterone levels that compromise endometrial health over time 131 
(Fig. 4). Reduced progesterone allows unhampered oestrogen activity, 
increasing the expression of oestrogen receptor-α (ERα) and ERβ, 
which promote endometrial cell proliferation 132 and hyperandro -
genism via increased endometrial androgen receptor expression 133. 
Hyperinsulinaemia and insulin resistance decrease the expression of 
insulin receptor and glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) 134. Importantly, 
studies have shown compromised endometrial function during the 
secretory phase with altered steroid receptor expression and glu -
cose transportation — factors that are known to be involved in delayed 
decidualization, impaired placentation, endometrial hyperplasia and 
potential carcinogenic processes131,135. Moreover, reduced expression 
of uterine receptivity markers, for example, integrin, HOXA10 and 
IGFB1, and increased expression of inflammatory factors, such as TNF 
and NF-κB, contribute to increased cell proliferation in PCOS134,136,137. 
However, in analyses of receptivity markers in samples collected in the 
secretory phase, no discernible differences were observed between 
the endometrium of those with PCOS and that of those without PCOS 
with similar BMI138.
Despite this finding, global gene expression profiling reveals 
distinct patterns in endometrial stromal cells from women with PCOS 
with increased expression of SNCA, CDKN3, and CD58 and decreased 
expression of PIBF1 and MICB139, which are linked to endometrial cancer, 
endometriosis, delayed decidualization and increased inflammation140. 
Moreover, exposing endometrial stromal cells to dihydrotestosterone 
disrupts gene expression associated with cell energy metabolism, 
proliferation, decidualization and inflammation139. Only few studies 
exist on immune cell migration in the PCOS endometrium; however, 
the current data suggest some changes even in well-matched samples, 
thus warranting future studies141,142.
Altogether, these findings underscore the multifaceted effect 
of hormonal and molecular changes in the endometrium of women 
with PCOS, potentially predisposing them to adverse reproductive 
outcomes such as early spontaneous miscarriage, pre-eclampsia and 
endometrial cancer143–146. How specific cell types contribute to PCOS-
specific endometrial dysfunction remains to be delineated, and these 
insights will help identify therapeutic targets (Fig. 4).
Insulin resistance
Women with PCOS, regardless of age, body composition and BMI, 
exhibit insulin resistance, characterized by a substantial reduction 
(35–40%) in insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, similar to that observed 
in individuals with T2DM 147–149. This insulin resistance is accompa -
nied by compensatory hyperinsulinaemia, which strains pancreatic 
β-cells and reduces hepatic insulin clearance, in turn exacerbating  
hyperinsulinaemia150.
Skeletal muscle dysfunction. The molecular basis of insulin resist -
ance in PCOS is unique and tissue specific and involves increased 
insulin-dependent serine phosphorylation of insulin receptors, impair-
ing insulin signalling in skeletal muscle151, a distinguishing feature not 
present in other insulin-resistant conditions like obesity and T2DM152. 
Skeletal muscle insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1) is also increased151, 
leading to dysfunctional insulin signalling through the activation of 
MAPK pathways153.
Furthermore, patients with PCOS exhibit abnormal DNA methyla-
tion and gene and protein expression profiles in skeletal muscle154–157. 
These changes activate inflammatory response, lipid, glucose and 
fibrotic pathways, contributing to insulin resistance 154–157. Addition-
ally, PCOS-related muscle dysfunction includes a reduction in insulin-
sensitive type I muscle fibres and ectopic lipid accumulation158 as well 
as impaired mitochondrial function 154, further aggravating insulin 
resistance (Fig. 5).
Despite these insights, the precise mechanisms and implications 
of PCOS on skeletal muscle dysfunction require further investigation, 
including understanding the roles of specific cell types in driving 
insulin resistance.
Adipose tissue dysfunction
Abdominal adipose tissue volume (including subcutaneous fat and 
visceral fat), measured with MRI is comparable between women with 
PCOS and those without PCOS, matched for BMI149,159, and androgens 
exert no discernible impact on fat distribution160. Nevertheless, distinc-
tive morphological and functional abnormalities are evident within 
the adipose tissue of women with PCOS, with dysfunctional adipokine 
secretion resulting in increased circulating leptin and follistatin, and 
decreased adiponectin levels161,162.
Enlarged adipocytes and decreased adiponectin levels, which are 
potent drivers of insulin resistance, are distinct features of PCOS 149. 
Moreover, hyperandrogenism correlates with adipocyte hypertro -
phy163, inhibits adipose stem cell commitment to preadipocytes164 and 
hinders their differentiation into mature adipocytes 165. In addition, 
differentiated adipocytes from women with PCOS have increased 
lipid content than those from women without PCOS, correlating with 
androgen levels166,167.
PCOS-specific adipose tissue dysfunction extends to impaired 
insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, which is attributed to decreased 
GLUT4 expression168,169. Moreover, subcutaneous adipocytes of women 
with PCOS with normal weight are resistant to catecholamine-induced 
lipolysis due to a decreased number of β2-adrenergic receptors170,171. 
T ogether with diminished hormone-sensitive lipase and protein kinase A 
(PKA) regulatory components, altered lipolysis is a possible link to insu-
lin resistance170,172–174 (Fig. 6). Additionally, adipocytes in PCOS exhibit 
dysfunctional lipid storage, release and energy regulation175,176.
Increased intra-adipose production of testosterone and dihy -
drotestosterone in PCOS via increased expression of andro  gen-
activating enzyme aldo-ketoreductase 1 C3 (AKR1C3) drives lipid 
accumulation, resulting in systemic lipotoxicity 177 with ectopic 
lipid deposition in tissues such as skeletal muscle and liver158, leading 
to insulin resistance. Inflammation, macrophage accumulation, fibrosis 
and adipose tissue expansion further exacerbate insulin resistance in 
PCOS178,179 (Fig. 5).
These findings underscore the specificity of adipose tissue dys -
function in women with PCOS. Although various global protein, tran-
scriptional and epigenetic changes may contribute to PCOS-specific 
dysfunctions158,180,181, the precise roles of specific cell types within adi-
pose tissue, their molecular functions and inter-tissue communication  
are yet to be defined.

Cardiovascular dysfunction
Studies have reported structural alterations, such as increased left 
ventricular mass and increased left atrial diameter 191, endothelial 
dysfunction192, coronary and aortic calcification 193, and increased 
intima-media thickness of common carotid and femoral arteries194,  
in women with PCOS, especially in those with insulin resistance and 
hyperandrogenaemia. Moreover, increased levels of fibrinogen  
and plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI1) suggest a prothrombotic 
state in women with the syndrome 195. T ogether, these alterations 
may contribute to their increased risk of cardiovascular disease and 
mortality. In addition, cardiovascular dysfunction is associated with 
elevated muscle sympathetic nerve activity196. Of note, women with 
PCOS have increased muscle sympathetic nerve activity independ-
ent of BMI197–199 and exercise. Electrical stimulation and renal den -
ervation not only decrease muscle sympathetic nerve activity but 
also improve hyperandrogenism, insulin resistance and hyperten -
sion200–204. Yet, whether the mechanisms leading to impaired cardiac 
autonomic function in women with PCOS rely on direct or indirect 
effects of hyperandrogenism, hyperinsulinaemia and insulin resist-
ance205, arterial stiffness206, and/or lower baroreflex sensitivity with 
lower heart rate variability remains to be determined207,208. Understand-
ing the underlying mechanisms driving increased sympathetic output 
is critical as these could be promising therapeutic targets in women  
with PCOS.

Psychological disorders
The aetiology and pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders in women 
with PCOS likely involve a complex interplay of genetic and environmen-
tal factors98. Twin and familial studies suggest substantial heritability 
of psychiatric disorders211, and Mendelian randomization analyses 
indicate a causal link between PCOS and depression82. However, cur-
rent research has found no genetic correlation between PCOS and 
psychiatric disorders 212, and the association between depression 
and PCOS weakens when BMI is taken into account, emphasizing the 
role of obesity.
Non-genetic factors, such as an adverse maternal–fetal environ-
ment owing to elevated androgens, can adversely impact fetal brain 
development, leading to anxiety-like behaviour in the offspring111,213,214. 
Experimental rodent data indicate that androgen exposure during 
critical brain development stages215 can mediate anxiety-like behav-
iour via various factors, for example, ERα, serotonergic signalling, 
GABAergic signalling and activation of adrenoceptor α1B, in the 
amygdala and hippocampus213,214. In support of preclinical observa -
tions, maternal PCOS is suggested to be linked to increased autism 
spectrum disorder in offspring of either sex 216 and an increased risk 
of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in boys only217. Epidemio-
logical studies also suggest associations between maternal PCOS and 
various psychiatric disorders, such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity 
disorder, and autism spectrum disorder in children97,218,219. Moreover, 
anxiety disorders in mice can be transgenerationally transmitted in  
both sexes111.

Diagnosis, screening and prevention
Diagnosis
Since the 2000s, PCOS diagnosis has changed as the understanding 
of the condition has evolved from a crude description of macroscopic 
ovarian changes to a complex endocrine condition caused by polygenic, 
epigenetic and environmental factors224. Ultimately, in 2018, building 
on the American Society for Reproductive Medicine and the European 
Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology Rotterdam consensus 
criteria25, an international PCOS network with 40 organizations reach-
ing across six continents upgraded these criteria from consensus to the 
International Evidence-based Guideline Diagnostic Criteria225 (Fig. 7).
Changes to the International Guideline included a robust develop-
ment process with patient and health professional groups; comprehen-
sive evidence synthesis and refined individual diagnostic components, 
including increasing the cut-off for arrested ovarian follicle count 
(<9 mm) from >12 to ≥20 per ovary and/or the cut-off for enlarged 
ovaries to >10 cm3 (ref. 225) (Fig. 7). Separate criteria for adolescents 
were developed requiring the presence of both oligoanovulation and 
hyperandrogenism, excluding ultrasound in the diagnostic criteria of 
adolescents owing to a lack of specificity for PCOS226. Adolescents ‘at 
risk’ were recognized when one of the two aforementioned features was 
present, with reassessment recommended once they reach adulthood. 
These altered criteria for adolescents were validated in a well-character-
ized longitudinal cohort and dramatically reduced overdiagnosis at this 
life stage227. Diagnosis in adults and adolescents requires biochemical 
exclusion of thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinaemia, congenital 
adrenal hyperplasia, primary and secondary ovarian failure, and clini-
cal exclusion of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, as well as of other 
less common causes such as adrenal carcinoma or Cushing disease.
The International Guideline is updated every 5 years and, in 2023, 
the evidence in PCOS was reviewed, synthesized, and appraised for 
quality and integrity and a 6,000-page technical report was presented 
with improved evidence certainty and strength of recommendations24. 
Diagnostic criteria were expanded to include either ovarian ultrasound 
or AMH levels in adults only1,24 (Fig. 7). This update is now a living guide-
line, responsive to the rapidly evolving advances in research, called the 
‘International Guideline diagnostic criteria’ .
Another key advance in knowledge around diagnosis relates to 
subgroups nested under the umbrella of PCOS. Traditionally defined 
as phenotypes A–D, based on Rotterdam diagnostic criteria25,224, these 
original crude groups do not align to aetiology, genotype or patho -
physiology. Exploration of GWAS combined with sophisticated cluster 
analyses in data sets has suggested grouping around core endocrine fea-
tures across hyperandrogenaemia, hyperinsulinaemia, hypothalamic–
pituitary abnormalities and BMI19,94,224,228. Reproductive, metabolic and 
indeterminant subgroups have emerged and progress in understanding 
the underlying aetiology and pathophysiology is likely to nuance these 
distinctions further, similar to T2DM. T2DM has a similar complex poly-
genic aetiology to PCOS and extensive subgroups have been identified, 
including many subtypes of maturity-onset diabetes of the young 229 
and proposed subtypes of adult-onset diabetes.
Evolving diagnostic criteria and lack of clarity on the subtypes, 
alongside inadequate health professional education and patient 
information, contribute to delayed PCOS diagnosis and patient dis -
satisfaction18. Delayed diagnosis also limits screening, prevention and 
treatment of the diverse features of PCOS, with health implications. 
Screening for PCOS and prevention opportunities can improve  
outcomes, including reproductive health (Box 2).
Management
Metabolic dysfunction
Lifestyle modification. Lifestyle interventions are a cornerstone in 
managing PCOS, particularly in individuals with excess weight. Modest 
reductions in total body weight, as little as 5%, offer metabolic and pos-
sibly reproductive benefits57,230–232. A meta-analysis of 18 randomized 
controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrated that lifestyle interventions 
improve various parameters, including waist circumference, waist-
to-hip ratio, modified Ferriman–Gallwey score (which evaluates hir -
sutism), fasting insulin, total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol compared 
with minimal intervention in PCOS24. Although RCTs investigating the 
effect of lifestyle interventions in the general population suggest a 
prevention of T2DM in individuals at risk233, similar trials in PCOS are 
yet to be performed. Notably, studies involving lifestyle interventions 
resulting in weight loss demonstrated that women with PCOS achieve 
similar weight loss improvements as those without PCOS24.
Notably, diet composition, including macronutrient components, 
does not significantly impact weight loss or metabolic outcomes in 
women with PCOS or the general population in RCTs234–236. Currently, 
no optimal lifestyle intervention exists for PCOS, with general popu-
lation guidelines applicable in this condition, tailored to personal 
preference237. Similarly, intensive exercise interventions alone do 
not yield superior weight loss or metabolic improvements in PCOS24. 
Overall, evidence supporting any specific type or intensity of exercise 
for anthropometric, metabolic, hormonal, reproductive or psycho -
logical outcomes is lacking 238,239, although engaging in 150 min of 
moderate-to-vigorous exercise per week is recommended for overall 
cardiometabolic risk improvement.
Bariatric surgery. In individuals with BMI >35 kg/m2, lifestyle interven-
tions are not as effective over the long term to maintain improvement 
in weight and metabolic disease. In the general population, bariatric 
surgery or metabolic surgery has been demonstrated to provide sub-
stantial and sustainable weight loss with accompanying improve -
ment in metabolic health240,241. In a non-RCT comparing surgery, oral 
contraceptive and metformin management of PCOS, the greatest 
improvement in metabolic outcomes was observed with surgery, with 
regards to decreased weight, BMI, waist circumference, hip circumfer-
ence, fasting glucose, fasting insulin, triglycerides, LDL, haemoglobin 
A1c (HbA1c), total testosterone and free androgen index242. In studies 
comparing surgical outcomes between women with and without PCOS, 
similar improvements were observed in both groups concerning total 
weight loss, total testosterone, SHBG, AMH, total cholesterol, LDL, 
triglycerides or HDL242,243, although improvement in fasting glucose 
levels and BMI post-surgery was greater in women with PCOS than in 
women without PCOS242,243.
Medical treatment. Although no drugs are approved specifically for 
the medical management of metabolic dysfunction in PCOS, met -
formin, a biguanide used to treat T2DM, has been used extensively in 
the management of PCOS. In RCTs investigating metformin and pla-
cebo, metformin significantly improved HOMA-IR and fasting glucose 
and improved waist-to-hip ratio and lipid profiles244. In a meta-analysis 
of RCTs conducted in women with PCOS, use of glucagon-like peptide 1 
(GLP1) receptor agonists, such as liraglutide, yielded modest metabolic 
improvements over a 12-week period compared with metformin alone.

Hyperandrogenism
Suppression of ovarian androgen secretion.  In PCOS, common 
androgen-related features include hirsutism, acne and female pattern 
hair loss1. Low-dose ethinyl oestradiol combined oral contraceptive 
pills (COCPs) are the preferred treatment for hirsutism in both adults 
and adolescents as they increase SHBG levels and decrease lutein -
izing hormone, serum testosterone and androstenedione levels 249. 
Meta-analysis shows that cyproterone acetate and fourth-genera -
tion progestins result in greater reduction in serum androgen levels 
than earlier progestins 250. However, fourth-generation progestins 
demonstrated no differences in their effect on hirsutism 250. Given 
the increased risk for venous thrombosis associated with ethinyl 
oestradiol plus cyproterone acetate, these preparations are consid-
ered second-line therapy 1. Addressing the primary symptoms and 
considering cosmetic therapies are crucial. The choice of treatment 
should involve shared decision-making, addressing contraindications 
and side effects.
Combining COCPs with lifestyle management is more effective 
than COCP–metformin combinations 1. Although metformin and 
COCPs can improve hyperandrogenism, a meta-analysis showed that 
COCP treatment results in lower free androgen index, total and free 
testosterone, androstenedione, and dehydroepiandrosterone sul -
fate levels along with higher SHBG levels than metformin treatment;  
however, hirsutism did not differ between the treatments251.
Anti-androgens.  Anti-androgens could be considered to treat hir-
sutism in women with PCOS if suboptimal response is observed 
after a minimum of six months of COCP and/or cosmetic therapy 24.

Bariatric surgery could also be considered to improve hirsutism.

Cosmetic treatments. Mechanical laser and light therapies should be 
considered to reduce facial hirsutism and related depression, anxiety, 
and quality of life in women with PCOS. A greater number of laser treat-
ment sessions may be required in women with PCOS than in women with 
idiopathic hirsutism to achieve hair reduction24. Adverse effects seem 
limited in the hands of experienced and suitably qualified providers and 
women should be encouraged to seek hair reduction therapies from 
such providers. Wavelength and delivery of laser treatment varies by 
skin colour and hair colour. Moreover, laser is relatively ineffective in 
women with blond, grey or white hair. Finally, the addition of COCPs, 
with or without anti-androgens, to laser treatment may provide greater 
hair reduction and maintenance than laser alone1.
Endometrial protection
Progestin-containing regimens offer endometrial protection. In PCOS, 
COCPs are commonly used as they are considered first-line therapy 
to manage irregular cycles1. Most COCPs have continuous progestin 
dosing, effectively controlling endometrial proliferation and offering 
cycle regulation. Progestin-only preparations can also be considered, 
although they lack the beneficial effect of COCPs to control hyperandro-
genism. Levonorgestrel intrauterine devices are effective for endome-
trial protection, although some women may suffer from extrauterine 
levonorgestrel effects such as acne and hair loss253. Cyclic progestins, 
like medroxyprogesterone or norethisterone, are also used, usually for 
10–14 days to induce menstruation over 8–6 cycles per year. Different 
weight loss strategies and metformin also support endometrial health 
and should be considered in PCOS131,254. In cases where hyperplasia or 
cancer are suspected, endometrial biopsy is indicated.
Fertility
Medication. Fertility treatment in women with PCOS should start with 
preconception counselling and optimizing maternal health before 
pregnancy255. Preconception weight loss through lifestyle interventions 
or bariatric surgery may improve ovulation, enhance natural pregnancy 
rates242,256, and may increase ovulation rates with first-line treatments257. 
However, pregnancy in PCOS carries risks, including miscarriage, ges-
tational diabetes, pregnancy-induced hypertension, pre-eclampsia, 
preterm delivery and the need for a Caesarean section1,258. The fetus is 
at risk for intrauterine growth restriction, with small-for-gestational-
age babies and low birthweight at birth1,258,259. Given the elevated risks, 
the primary goal of fertility therapy should be a singleton pregnancy to 
avoid complications associated with multiple pregnancies.
The first-line treatment for anovulation in PCOS involves ovula-
tion induction with letrozole over several cycles, with dose escalation

Screening and prevention
Infertility, time to conception and inter-conception intervals, rate 
of fertility treatment and maternal age are all increased in women 
with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), with lower fecundity rate 
and fewer children than in women without PCOS54,56. A delayed 
diagnosis of PCOS adversely impacts fertility and family size and 
increases advanced maternal age in a condition where pregnancy 
complications are already increased. In this context, a reproductive 
life plan is recommended to focus on timely diagnosis, screening, 
prevention and family planning1. On the other hand, women should 
be informed about the chance of spontaneous pregnancies and that 
they are likely to have at least one child; however, this may require 
assisted reproductive technologies55,56,303.
Preconception offers additional screening opportunities. Nutritional 
status, prenatal vitamin use, smoking, alcohol, diet, exercise, sleep, 
and mental, emotional and sexual health as well as screening for 
comorbidities such as excess weight, type 2 diabetes mellitus and 
high blood pressure, are important to improve maternal–fetal health 
and overall health. Risks for gestational diabetes and hypertension 
during pregnancy are increased in women with PCOS58. Screening, 
monitoring and prevention of complications in pregnancy are strongly 
recommended in the International Guideline, including identifying PCOS 
status, high BMI, excess gestational weight gain, hypertension and type 2 
diabetes mellitus, with a focus on improving maternal–fetal outcomes1,58.
For endometrial protection, monitoring cycle regularity 
for those not on hormonal contraception is important to limit 
hyperplasia and endometrial cancer, especially in those with 
a high BMI. However, the absolute risk for cancer remains low 
and routine screening is not recommended unless clinically 
indicated1,24.
Given that anxiety and depressive symptoms are increased in 
70–80% of those with PCOS, screening for depression and anxiety are 
routinely recommended, using regionally validated and assessable 
tools. For eating disorders, screening is not routinely recommended; 
however, where these disorders are suspected, screening is 
recommended1,24. Other psychological features, including body 
image distress and self-esteem, should be considered for screening 
and treatment if clinically indicated. Individualized screening, 
identification and treatment of psychological features should be a 
priority to improve quality of life1,24. The frequency of psychological 
screening should be based on clinical assessment and considered 
when other intersectional factors or life events occur such as 
infertility or pregnancy304.
Cardiometabolic screening is recommended routinely in PCOS 
from the time of diagnosis. Weight, glucose levels, blood pressure 
and lipid profiles should be routinely and regularly screened to 
prevent complications1,24.

IVF and elective single embryo transfer. In vitro fertilization (IVF) has 
gained popularity as a second-line fertility treatment in PCOS. Several 
modifications have improved its risk-to-benefit profile for patients 
with PCOS. These modifications include enabling multiple follicular 
recruitment through gonadotropin stimulation whilst reducing the risk 
of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome by using less potent ovulation-
triggering agents such as a GnRH agonist. The elective freeze-all strat-
egy, where collected oocytes are fertilized and frozen for later embryo 
transfer, which allows the ovaries to recover from gonadotropin hyper-
stimulation, is also employed to prevent exposure to endogenous 
human chorionic gonadotropin265.
This approach lends itself to elective single embryo transfer of a 
thawed cryopreserved embryo during a frozen embryo transfer cycle, 
substantially lowering the likelihood of multiple pregnancies. However, 
a small chance (<2%) of monozygotic twins exists as a result of elective 
single embryo transfer 266. Although the freeze-all strategy reduces 
ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome risk and potentially improves 
pregnancy rates compared with fresh embryo transfer, it may introduce 
other risks such as an increased incidence of gestational hypertension 
or pre-eclampsia and large-for-gestational-age babies267. The hyperten-
sive problems may be avoided if frozen embryo transfer is performed in 
natural cycles with an intact corpus luteum268. Concerns about ovarian 
hyperstimulation syndrome risk and the excessive use of fertility drugs 
have prompted the development of in vitro maturation, where oocytes 
are retrieved after mild ovarian stimulation and matured in vitro before 
undergoing standard IVF procedures269,270.
Ovarian drilling. The original PCOS treatment by Stein and Leventhal, 
involving ovarian wedge resection (that is, removal of a wedge-shaped 
portion in the ovary), improved hyperandrogenism and restored ovu-
lation, enabling natural pregnancy271. Modified methods like ovarian 
diathermy or drilling provide sustained reproductive benefits272, with 
potentially lower multiple pregnancy rates than low-dose gonadotro-
pin therapy273. Proponents have developed outpatient ablative pro -
cedures to lower costs and increase availability 274. The invasiveness 
of this procedure and the risk of diminishing the ovarian reserve if the 
operator is inexperienced warrant caution.
Psychological disorders
First-line treatments for PCOS, such as COCPs or lifestyle management, 
show some improvement or no difference in depressive or anxiety symp-
toms, suggesting that traditional approaches, including psychological 
therapy and anti-depressants or anti-anxiety medications, should be 
used when indicated275,276. As depression, anxiety and eating disorders 
impact the ability of patients to effectively engage in lifestyle manage-
ment, screening for these disorders to individualize lifestyle recom-
mendations is needed24. Moreover, the use of cognitive behavioural 
therapy along with lifestyle management may be an important strategy 
to address weight management in those with PCOS and high depres-
sive scores276,277. A multidisciplinary holistic approach that provides 
education on long-term risks, lifestyle interventions, screening and 
management of PCOS features is an essential component of improving 
outcomes, including psychological well-being and HRQoL in patients278.
Quality of life
HRQoL scores related to hirsutism, menstruation and fertility are lower 
in women with PCOS than in the general population279,280. Identification 
of areas of key concern that affect quality of life in this multifaceted 
disorder is critical to prioritizing treatment strategies. However, current 
surveys do not capture all aspects of PCOS that impact quality of life, 
and data for adolescents with PCOS is limited. Disease-specific HRQoL 
questionnaires include questions specific to PCOS, such as menstrual 
dysfunction, obesity-related matters and how symptoms of hyperan-
drogenism affect quality of life, and include the validated PCOS health-
related QOL questionnaire (PCOSQ)281,282 or the PCOS Quality of Life 
Scale (PCOSQOL). Studies have shown that PCOSQOL measures the 
impact of PCOS on feminine identity and the negative impact of PCOS on 
family and friends and on the feeling of pressure to have children in more 
detail than earlier questionnaires and is sensitive in both clinical and 
research settings in adult women283. In addition, the Adolescents’ PCOS 
health-related quality of life Questionnaire (APQ-20) was developed for 
adolescents and has been shown to be easy to use, valid and reliable284.
PCOS-specific quality of life questionnaires capture the impact 
of PCOS-specific symptoms on HRQoL, and social, cultural and 
ethnic differences are observed in the responses 279. For example, 
excess body weight is reported as a common concern in most patient 
surveys and has a strong association with lower quality of life scores 
whereas infertility, hirsutism and menstrual symptoms impact qual-
ity of life in some studies 279. Moreover, surveys in different world 
regions show that, in some cultures, fertility is a major stigma and 
fertility-related quality of life scores are more affected; the same 
holds for hirsutism.
Outlook
Although commendable progress has been made in deciphering the intri-
cacies of PCOS in the past decades, considerable knowledge gaps remain. 
Our understanding of this complex syndrome is still incomplete and we 
identify knowledge gaps and urgent research questions (Box 3). Of note, 
the questions highlighted represent only a fraction of the multifaceted 
areas that require further investigation. Overall, long-term health, quality 
of life and ability to work should be at the core of future studies.
Management
Metabolic dysfunction. Currently, data on the impact of specific life-
style interventions that are effective in the management of increased 
weight and metabolic dysfunction in PCOS is limited. Preventive 
therapies to control weight gain in adolescence and peripubertal and 
periconception interventions are needed. As there is now a growing 
list of effective anti-obesity agents, the question of how these might 
be better utilized in weight management and investigation of the 
long-term metabolic impact of these agents are needed.
Additionally, an improved understanding of the cardiovascular 
outcomes associated with PCOS and the impact that metabolic dys -
function and its management may have on these outcomes is needed. 
An understanding of how metabolic dysfunction impacts reproductive 
function and vice versa is also needed.

Reproductive dysfunction. The genesis of PCOM is unknown and the 
role of hyperandrogenism in the accumulation of small antral follicles 
is unclear. Deciphering the intricate relationship between PCOM in the 
absence of hyperandrogenism and its impact on health and disease is 
important. Moreover, challenging conventional norms by assessing 
hyperandrogenaemia in cases where serum levels appear normative 
is needed.
The accumulation of small antral follicles leads to excessive granu-
losa cell production of AMH, which in turn increases GnRH and lutein-
izing hormone secretion, leading to ovarian hyperandrogenaemia 
and contributing to the development of PCOS; this mechanism needs 
further investigation. Whether PCOS is of neuroendocrine and/or 
ovarian origin is still debated, and the respective contribution of each 
component to the manifestation of the syndrome needs further deline-
ation. The efficacy of neuroendocrine manipulations, including inter-
ventions such as kisspeptin, in the treatment of anovulation associated 
with PCOS needs to be investigated.
Hyperandrogenism. Conducting extensive population-based studies 
is imperative to comprehensively capture the spectrum of side effects 
and risks associated with hyperandrogenism treatment in individuals 
with PCOS. T o ascertain the optimal COCP preparation, particularly in 
adolescents, large-scale comparative studies are essential, focusing on 
progestin types and dosages, whilst assessing efficacy on acne, hair loss 
and psychological outcomes. A thorough investigation into adverse 
events linked to COCP usage, encompassing aspects such as weight 
gain, metabolic effects, increased insulin resistance and psychological 
impact, is imperative. Exploration of the relatively uncharted terri -
tory of progestin-only preparations, including intrauterine systems,  
implants and pills, is warranted to enhance our understanding of 
their role in treating hyperandrogenism. The potential of anti-obesity 
drugs in addressing both biochemical and clinical hyperandrogenism, 
either in isolation or in conjunction with COCPs, necessitates scrutiny 
through adequately powered randomized trials.
Psychological disorders.  Current research strongly supports the 
integration of psychological well-being in the overall management of 
PCOS across the lifespan. Routine assessment of mental health condi-
tions in all adolescents and adults evaluated for PCOS is recommended. 
Further research in understanding the biological, genetic and envi -
ronmental determinants that impact emotional well-being is urgently 
needed to better formulate individualized treatment strategies in this 
high-risk population. Incorporation of strategies to routinely screen 
patients, incorporate educational materials and access resources 
might require a change in our current models of care delivery for this 
chronic condition.

Mechanisms
Although GWAS has unveiled >20 genetic variants linked to PCOS, crucial 
gaps persist in understanding the effector genes at these loci and in 
deciphering how the altered expression or function of each gene con-
tributes to PCOS pathophysiology. Although large-scale GWAS in PCOS 
are prevalent in European and Chinese cohorts, the genetic architecture 
in other populations, like African or Hispanic, remains inadequately 
explored. Investigating potential PCOS subtypes necessitates analysing 
larger cohorts for statistical robustness during sample subdivision. In 
the realm of inheritance, exploring the contribution of epigenetic pro-
cesses in longitudinal mother–child cohorts is crucial for understanding 
maternal–fetal factors in PCOS transmission. Utilizing PCOS-like mouse 
models offers insight into key drivers and disease transmission loci.
A deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms of com -
pensatory hyperinsulinaemia, insulin resistance, T2DM, systemic 
inflammation, cardiovascular disease and pregnancy complications 
in PCOS is urgently needed. Employing temporal and spatial single-
cell technologies for ovaries, endometrium, adipose tissue, skeletal 
muscle, liver, pancreas and brain has the potential to unveil complex 
cellular interactions, identify biomarkers for prediction and targeted 
treatment, and predict treatment response.
Epidemiology
Large data sets and epidemiological studies are central to understand-
ing the features and natural history of PCOS285. Epidemiological data, 
including routine clinical data, are also key to evaluating care and treat-
ment responses across health-care settings286. Yet, only few high-quality 
longitudinal data sets in unselected populations with intergenerational, 
early and later life event data are available. Future and existing data 
sets should identify PCOS status, including self-report, PCOS symp-
toms and, ideally, androgen and AMH measurement28. Data harmoni-
zation and linkage are also vital to address current gaps and explore 
heterogeneity and ethnicity287. Data harmonization, core outcome set 
integration, secure data sharing, federated data analyses, and the use 
of artificial or synthetic data, alongside advanced analytics to harness 
the power of data in PCOS are warranted.
Gaps identified by updated guidelines
Research priorities identified in the 2023 International Evidence-based 
Guideline for the Assessment and Management of PCOS1 focus on defin-
ing diagnostic cut-offs, developing prevention strategies, addressing 
psychological aspects associated with PCOS, and devising optimal mod-
els of care and novel medical and surgical treatments for the manage-
ment of PCOS in individuals with excess body weight. The international 
research priorities are based on surveys of thousands of health profes-
sionals and women with PCOS18,288 and the process involved analysis 
of 52 systematic reviews24 and consensus processes via the Grading of 
Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) 
framework289. Clinical expertise, consumer preference and evidence gaps 
were captured, and the final consensus was obtained across guideline 
experts from 6 continents, representing 39 engaged organizations1.
