# Efficacy, Safety, and Certainty of Evidence for Systemic Antineoplastic Therapies in Treatment-resistant Adult Malignancies: A Systematic Review, Exploratory Meta-analysis, and Grade Assessment
**Authors:** Rodrigues MS, Silva LA, Silva CEdM
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-10253153/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10253153/v1)

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>Background  Therapeutic resistance remains one of the main causes of treatment failure, disease progression, and reduced survival in oncology. Although systemic antineoplastic therapies have expanded substantially in recent years, the magnitude and certainty of their benefits in resistant, refractory, relapsed, or previously treated adult malignancies remain variable across tumor types and therapeutic classes. <h4>Objective:</h4> To synthesize contemporary randomized evidence on the efficacy, safety, and certainty of evidence for systemic antineoplastic therapies used in adults with treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, recurrent, advanced, metastatic, or previously treated malignancies. Methods  This systematic review, exploratory meta-analysis, and GRADE assessment was prospectively registered in PROSPERO under the number CRD420261386007 and conducted according to PRISMA 2020 recommendations. PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, SciELO, and Google Scholar were searched for randomized clinical evidence published between 2021 and 2026. Eligible studies included adults with malignant tumors treated with systemic antineoplastic therapies, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, molecular targeted therapy, antibody–drug conjugates, cellular therapy, endocrine-based regimens, antiangiogenic therapy, tumor treating fields, or combination strategies. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach. When clinically appropriate, exploratory random-effects meta-analyses were performed for time-to-event outcomes. The current manuscript reports PROSPERO registration, PRISMA 2020 conduct, RoB 2 assessment, GRADE evaluation, 18 included randomized clinical studies, and 19 high-certainty outcomes. Results  A total of 219 records were identified, and 18 randomized clinical studies were included. The studies evaluated a broad range of resistant or previously treated malignancies, including breast cancer, urothelial carcinoma, differentiated thyroid cancer, lymphoma, multiple myeloma, ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, lung cancer, and hematologic malignancies. The most robust evidence was observed for cabozantinib, ciltacabtagene autoleucel, axicabtagene ciloleucel, sacituzumab govitecan, palbociclib, and enfortumab vedotin. Exploratory pooled analysis suggested improved progression-free survival with systemic antineoplastic therapies, although heterogeneity was substantial. Overall survival was also improved in the pooled analysis, with moderate heterogeneity. The GRADE assessment identified 19 high-certainty outcomes, mainly involving progression-free survival, event-free survival, overall survival, objective response, complete response, hematologic toxicity, diarrhea, and grade 3 or higher adverse events. Conclusion  Contemporary randomized evidence indicates that selected systemic antineoplastic therapies can provide clinically meaningful benefits in treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, or previously treated adult malignancies. However, high-certainty evidence is concentrated in specific therapeutic contexts rather than uniformly distributed across all interventions. Exploratory pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously because of substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity. Treatment decisions should integrate survival benefit, tumor response, adverse events, risk of bias, and certainty of evidence.</p>

Cancer remains one of the most complex challenges in contemporary medicine because its clinical behavior is shaped by biological plasticity, genomic instability, clonal evolution, immune escape, metabolic adaptation, and dynamic interactions with the tumor microenvironment (1). These features allow malignant cells not only to proliferate and disseminate, but also to adapt under therapeutic pressure. As a result, even when initial treatment responses are achieved, many tumors eventually progress, relapse, or become refractory to subsequent lines of therapy (2,3).

Therapeutic resistance is a central determinant of treatment failure in oncology. It may be primary, when tumors fail to respond from the beginning of treatment, or acquired, when disease progression occurs after an initial period of clinical benefit (2,3). Resistance is not mediated by a single mechanism. It may involve alterations in drug targets, activation of compensatory signaling pathways, impaired apoptosis, enhanced DNA repair, drug efflux, epigenetic reprogramming, metabolic adaptation, and protective interactions with stromal and immune components of the tumor microenvironment (2–4). Intratumoral heterogeneity further intensifies this process, since distinct cellular subclones may coexist within the same tumor, respond differently to treatment, and be selectively expanded during therapy (4,5).

The concept of resistance has become especially relevant in the era of precision oncology. Molecular targeted therapies, antibody–drug conjugates, immune checkpoint inhibitors, endocrine-based combinations, antiangiogenic strategies, and cellular therapies have substantially expanded the therapeutic landscape of advanced malignancies. However, these interventions may also impose selective pressures that favor the emergence of resistant clones, bypass signaling, lineage plasticity, immune evasion, and cross-resistance to later treatment lines (3,6). Therefore, therapeutic innovation does not eliminate resistance; rather, it changes the biological and clinical context in which resistance emerges.

Resistance to immune-based therapies adds another layer of complexity. Immune checkpoint inhibitors and cellular therapies have produced durable responses in selected solid and hematologic malignancies, but a substantial proportion of patients either do not respond initially or develop progression after apparent benefit (7,8). Mechanisms of immune resistance include impaired antigen presentation, defective interferon signaling, exclusion of cytotoxic T cells from the tumor microenvironment, T-cell exhaustion, expansion of immunosuppressive cell populations, and adaptive tumor changes under immune selective pressure (7,8). These mechanisms reinforce that resistance is not a uniform entity, but a heterogeneous process that varies across tumor type, biomarker profile, therapeutic class, disease stage, and prior treatment exposure.

Despite its clinical relevance, therapeutic resistance is not consistently defined or measured across randomized clinical trials. In many studies, resistance is not evaluated as a direct biological endpoint, but is inferred from clinical contexts such as refractory disease, relapse after previous response, progression after systemic therapy, advanced disease after prior treatment, or enrollment in heavily pretreated populations. Its consequences are usually captured through outcomes such as objective response, complete response, tumor progression, progression-free survival, event-free survival, and overall survival. Standardized response criteria, including RECIST, and time-to-event endpoints therefore remain essential for evaluating treatment activity in resistant disease settings (9).

In recent years, randomized clinical trials have evaluated multiple systemic antineoplastic strategies in resistant, refractory, relapsed, recurrent, metastatic, advanced, or previously treated adult malignancies. These strategies include chemotherapy, molecular targeted therapy, immunotherapy combinations, antibody–drug conjugates, CAR-T cell therapies, endocrine-based regimens, antiangiogenic therapy, tumor treating fields, and multimodal systemic approaches across solid and hematologic tumors (19–36). Together, these studies illustrate both the therapeutic progress achieved in difficult-to-treat malignancies and the persistent uncertainty regarding which interventions provide clinically meaningful benefits with acceptable toxicity.

A rigorous synthesis of this evidence is necessary for two reasons. First, treatment effects in resistant malignancies may differ substantially according to tumor biology, treatment line, comparator, endpoint definition, and therapeutic mechanism. Second, statistical significance alone is insufficient to determine clinical value. Treatment decisions in this setting must consider not only survival benefit and tumor response, but also adverse events, discontinuation due to toxicity, risk of bias, precision of estimates, consistency across outcomes, and applicability to the target population (10,11). The GRADE approach is particularly useful in this context because it allows the certainty of evidence to be judged across clinically relevant outcomes by considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias (14,15).

Therefore, this systematic review, exploratory meta-analysis, and GRADE assessment aimed to synthesize contemporary randomized evidence on the efficacy, safety, and certainty of evidence for systemic antineoplastic therapies used in adults with treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, recurrent, advanced, metastatic, or previously treated malignancies. The primary outcomes were overall survival, progression-free survival, and event-free survival. Secondary outcomes included objective response, complete response, tumor progression, treatment-related adverse events, serious adverse events, discontinuation due to toxicity, and certainty of evidence according to GRADE.

This systematic review, exploratory meta-analysis, and GRADE assessment was conducted according to a predefined protocol and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420261386007. The review was designed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses — PRISMA 2020 statement, aiming to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and methodological rigor throughout study identification, selection, data extraction, synthesis, and interpretation (10,11).

The review focused on randomized clinical evidence evaluating systemic antineoplastic therapies in adult patients with treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, recurrent, advanced, metastatic, or previously treated malignancies. For the purposes of this study, therapeutic resistance was considered a clinically operational disease setting rather than a single uniform biological variable. Therefore, resistance was defined as disease progression during or after prior systemic therapy, refractory disease according to trial-specific criteria, relapse after previous response, recurrence after prior treatment, or enrollment in a previously treated population in which resistance or reduced treatment sensitivity was clinically inferred from prior treatment failure.

Because definitions of resistance, refractoriness, relapse, recurrence, and previous treatment exposure vary across tumor types and therapeutic classes, the disease setting was interpreted according to the eligibility criteria, baseline characteristics, and terminology used in each individual trial. This approach allowed the inclusion of clinically relevant randomized evidence while preserving transparency regarding clinical and methodological heterogeneity across oncologic contexts.

Studies were considered eligible when they met the following criteria: randomized clinical evidence; publication between 2021 and 2026; adult participants aged 18 years or older; diagnosis of malignant tumor of any histological type or primary site; evaluation of systemic antineoplastic therapy with direct anticancer intent; and reporting of at least one clinically relevant efficacy or safety outcome.

Eligible disease settings included treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, recurrent, advanced, metastatic, or previously treated malignancies, provided that the enrolled population represented a clinically relevant context of treatment failure, disease progression, relapse after previous benefit, or limited response to prior systemic therapy.

Eligible interventions included systemic antineoplastic therapies such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, molecular targeted therapy, antibody–drug conjugates, cellular therapy, endocrine-based regimens, antiangiogenic therapy, tumor treating fields, and combination strategies. Eligible comparators included placebo, standard of care, investigator’s choice chemotherapy, active systemic therapy, maintenance therapy without the experimental agent, salvage therapy, or alternative randomized treatment schedules when random allocation was preserved.

Eligible efficacy outcomes included overall survival, progression-free survival, event-free survival, objective response, complete response, disease control, tumor progression, relapse, and resistance-related outcomes. Eligible safety outcomes included treatment-related adverse events, grade 3 or higher adverse events, serious adverse events, hematologic toxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity, immune-mediated toxicity, CAR-T-specific adverse events, and treatment discontinuation due to toxicity.

Studies were excluded when they involved pediatric populations, benign or non-malignant tumors, non-systemic interventions, supportive or palliative interventions without direct antineoplastic intent, or therapies not directed at malignant disease. Non-randomized studies, observational studies, case reports, case series, editorials, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, background articles, protocols without results, and phase I trials were excluded. Studies published before 2021 were also excluded. Trials were further excluded when they did not provide sufficient data for the assessment of survival, response, progression, resistance-related disease setting, previous treatment exposure, or safety outcomes.

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials — CENTRAL, SciELO, and Google Scholar. The final search was performed on May 19, 2026. The search strategy was designed to identify randomized clinical evidence evaluating systemic antineoplastic therapies in adult patients with treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, recurrent, advanced, metastatic, or previously treated malignancies.

Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to neoplasms, drug resistance, antineoplastic agents, systemic therapy, randomized trials, treatment failure, refractory disease, relapsed disease, previous treatment, disease progression, and advanced or metastatic cancer. The PubMed/MEDLINE strategy combined terms related to “Neoplasms,” “Drug Resistance, Neoplasm,” “Antineoplastic Agents,” “randomized controlled trial,” “phase II,” “phase III,” “refractory,” “relapsed,” “previously treated,” “progression,” and “systemic therapy.” Search terms were adapted for each database according to its indexing structure and search interface.

Google Scholar was used as a supplementary source to identify potentially relevant records not retrieved in bibliographic databases. Reference lists of included studies and relevant trial reports were also manually screened to identify additional eligible randomized evidence.

Filters were applied for randomized clinical trials or clinical trial evidence, publication date from 2021 onward, and articles published in English or Portuguese. Full-text availability was assessed during eligibility evaluation and was not used as the sole criterion for excluding potentially relevant studies when bibliographic information and outcome data were otherwise available.

All records retrieved from the search strategy were imported into a reference management system, and duplicate records were removed before screening. Three reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Potentially eligible studies were then assessed in full text.

Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. When consensus could not be achieved, adjudication by an additional reviewer was planned. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded and grouped according to the main cause of exclusion, including wrong study design, wrong publication type, wrong population, background article, review article, insufficient data, wrong outcome, phase I design, or inadequate study duration.

When multiple publications from the same clinical trial were identified, the most complete and clinically relevant report was prioritized. Secondary, subgroup, biomarker, molecular, or exploratory reports were considered only when they provided additional data relevant to the outcomes of interest, such as long-term follow-up, subgroup-specific efficacy, molecular response, safety, or resistance-related findings.

Data extraction was performed using a standardized form developed specifically for this review. Before formal extraction, the form was piloted in a sample of eligible studies and refined to ensure consistency among reviewers. Data were extracted independently by two reviewers and checked by a third reviewer. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

The extracted variables included author, year of publication, trial name, study design, trial phase, tumor type, disease setting, number of participants, intervention and comparator arms, treatment regimen, dose, previous lines of therapy, follow-up duration, primary and secondary endpoints, efficacy outcomes, safety outcomes, and information related to resistance, refractoriness, relapse, recurrence, prior treatment exposure, or disease progression.

For time-to-event outcomes, hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were extracted when available. For dichotomous outcomes, event counts, proportions, risk ratios, odds ratios, or sufficient information to calculate comparative estimates were collected when possible. Tumor response and progression were interpreted according to the criteria used in each trial, including RECIST, hematologic response criteria, molecular response criteria, or trial-specific definitions when applicable.

The primary outcomes of interest were overall survival, progression-free survival, and event-free survival. These outcomes were prioritized because they provide direct measures of survival, disease control, treatment failure, or progression in resistant and previously treated oncologic settings.

Secondary efficacy outcomes included objective response, complete response, disease control, tumor progression, relapse, molecular response, minimal residual disease negativity, and treatment failure when reported. Safety outcomes included treatment-related adverse events, grade 3 or higher adverse events, serious adverse events, treatment discontinuation due to toxicity, treatment-related deaths, hematologic toxicity, neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, diarrhea, neuropathy, immune-mediated toxicity, cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, and other therapy-specific adverse events.

Because the included trials evaluated different tumor types, therapeutic classes, comparators, and outcome definitions, outcomes were interpreted within their specific clinical context before being considered for cross-study synthesis.

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool for randomized trials. Judgments were made primarily for the main efficacy outcome of each trial, prioritizing the prespecified primary endpoint when available. The five RoB 2 domains were assessed: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result.

Each domain was classified as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias. An overall risk-of-bias judgment was assigned according to RoB 2 guidance. Open-label trials were not automatically judged as high risk; however, lack of blinding was considered when outcomes were subjective, investigator-assessed, influenced by treatment adherence, or related to adverse-event reporting. Survival outcomes and centrally reviewed radiologic endpoints were considered less vulnerable to measurement bias than subjective tolerability or patient-reported outcomes.

A narrative synthesis was performed for all included studies and was structured according to tumor type, disease setting, therapeutic class, comparator, efficacy outcomes, safety outcomes, risk of bias, and certainty of evidence. Quantitative synthesis was performed only when at least two studies reported sufficiently comparable time-to-event outcomes and provided hazard ratios with corresponding standard errors or 95% confidence intervals.

For time-to-event outcomes, including overall survival, progression-free survival, and event-free survival, hazard ratios were transformed to the logarithmic scale and pooled using inverse-variance methods. Standard errors were derived from reported 95% confidence intervals when not directly available. Because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was expected across tumor types, treatment mechanisms, comparators, and disease settings, random-effects models were used for exploratory pooled analyses.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, tau², and the chi-square test. I² values above 50% were interpreted as substantial heterogeneity. Pooled estimates were interpreted cautiously, especially when studies differed substantially in tumor type, therapeutic mechanism, comparator, treatment line, endpoint definition, or baseline prognosis. Therefore, the meta-analyses were considered exploratory and were not interpreted as evidence of a uniform class effect across all systemic antineoplastic therapies.

When quantitative pooling was not appropriate, results were summarized narratively by considering direction of effect, magnitude of benefit or harm, precision of estimates, consistency across outcomes, clinical relevance, risk of bias, and certainty of evidence.

Reporting bias was planned to be assessed when a sufficient number of studies was available for a given outcome. Funnel plots were considered for visual inspection of small-study effects and asymmetry, and Egger’s test was considered when quantitative assessment was appropriate (18). The possibility of selective reporting was also considered during interpretation, particularly when outcomes described in trial methods were incompletely reported in the results or when only selected subgroup, biomarker, or exploratory outcomes were available.

Because the review included heterogeneous tumor types, therapeutic classes, and outcome definitions, formal assessment of publication bias was interpreted cautiously and was not used as a standalone determinant of certainty.

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach. The assessment considered five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias (14,15). The certainty of evidence was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low for each clinically relevant outcome.

High certainty was assigned when the evidence came from randomized clinical trials with acceptable risk of bias, direct applicability to the review question, precise estimates, clinically meaningful effects, and no serious concerns regarding inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, or publication bias. Certainty was downgraded when there were important limitations related to open-label design, missing data, indirectness of population or comparator, wide confidence intervals, inconsistent effects, small sample size, subgroup-only evidence, or concerns about selective reporting.

The GRADE assessment was used to support interpretation of efficacy and safety outcomes, including survival, disease progression, tumor response, complete response, serious adverse events, grade 3 or higher adverse events, hematologic toxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity, and treatment discontinuation due to toxicity.

Ethics approval was not required because this systematic review and exploratory meta-analysis used data from previously published studies and did not involve direct contact with human participants, collection of individual patient data, or access to identifiable personal information.

All data used in this review were extracted from published randomized clinical studies. Extracted data, including study characteristics, outcome estimates, risk-of-bias judgments, GRADE assessments, and variables used for exploratory meta-analysis, should be made available as supplementary material or upon reasonable request.

The search strategy identified 219 records from databases and supplementary sources. After the removal of 40 duplicate records, 179 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 132 records were excluded because they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. A total of 47 reports were sought for retrieval, of which 2 reports could not be retrieved. Therefore, 45 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility.

After full-text evaluation, 27 reports were excluded. The main reasons for exclusion were wrong study design, wrong publication type, wrong population, background article, review article, insufficient outcome data, wrong outcome, phase I design, or inadequate study duration. Finally, 18 randomized clinical studies were included in the systematic review. The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

The included studies evaluated systemic antineoplastic strategies across a broad spectrum of solid and hematologic malignancies, including platinum-resistant ovarian cancer, refractory or relapsed acute myeloid leukemia, advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumor after imatinib failure, radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, advanced non-small cell lung cancer, endocrine-resistant breast cancer, relapsed or refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, large B-cell lymphoma, multiple myeloma, metastatic colorectal cancer, urothelial carcinoma, hormone receptor-positive/HER2-positive breast cancer, hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer, and metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (19–36).

Most studies enrolled patients with advanced, metastatic, relapsed, refractory, resistant, recurrent, or previously treated disease, reflecting clinical contexts in which therapeutic resistance or reduced sensitivity to prior treatment was relevant. The interventions included chemotherapy, molecular targeted therapy, immunotherapy combinations, antibody–drug conjugates, CAR-T cell therapies, antiangiogenic strategies, endocrine-based combinations, tumor treating fields, and multimodal systemic regimens. Comparators varied according to disease setting and standard of care, including placebo, physician’s choice chemotherapy, standard systemic therapy, salvage chemotherapy followed by autologous stem-cell transplantation, maintenance therapy without the experimental agent, or alternative randomized treatment schedules (19–36).

The most frequently reported efficacy outcomes were progression-free survival, overall survival, event-free survival, objective response, complete response, disease control, and tumor progression. Safety outcomes included grade 3 or higher adverse events, serious adverse events, hematologic toxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity, immune-mediated adverse events, CAR-T-related toxicities, treatment discontinuation due to toxicity, and treatment-related deaths. Overall, the included randomized clinical studies showed acceptable methodological quality, although risk of bias varied across designs, interventions, and outcomes. Double-blind, placebo-controlled trials generally presented lower risk of bias, particularly when outcomes were objective, prespecified, and centrally assessed. This was especially relevant for radiologic or time-to-event outcomes such as progression-free survival and event-free survival (22,31,34).

Open-label trials were more vulnerable to bias due to deviations from intended interventions and measurement of subjective outcomes, particularly for investigator-assessed response, patient-reported outcomes, tolerability, and adverse-event reporting. However, open-label design was not considered sufficient by itself to classify a study as high risk of bias when randomization was appropriate, endpoints were prespecified, outcome assessment was structured, and survival outcomes were objective (21,26,28,32,33,35,36).

Most pivotal phase III trials had low risk or some concerns overall. Some concerns were commonly related to open-label design, treatment deviations, or outcome measurement. One study derived from an exploratory molecular analysis had higher concerns because the evidence was based on a subset of the randomized population and therefore was more vulnerable to missing outcome data, indirectness, and selective interpretation (25). Several systemic antineoplastic therapies showed clinically meaningful benefit in resistant, refractory, relapsed, or previously treated malignancies. The strongest efficacy signals were concentrated in selected therapeutic contexts rather than distributed uniformly across all interventions.

Cabozantinib demonstrated a marked progression-free survival benefit in patients with radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer previously treated with VEGFR-targeted therapy. In COSMIC-311, median progression-free survival was not reached in the cabozantinib group and was 1.9 months in the placebo group, with a hazard ratio of 0.22 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.13 to 0.36. Objective response was also higher with cabozantinib than with placebo, although complete responses were not observed (22).

In large B-cell lymphoma, axicabtagene ciloleucel showed substantial benefit compared with standard second-line therapy in early relapsed or refractory disease. In ZUMA-7, median event-free survival was 8.3 months with axi-cel and 2.0 months with standard care, with a hazard ratio of 0.40 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.31 to 0.51. Progression-free survival, objective response, and complete response also favored axi-cel, with complete response observed in 65% of patients in the axi-cel group and 32% in the standard-care group (26).

Ciltacabtagene autoleucel produced one of the largest efficacy effects among the included studies. In CARTITUDE-4, conducted in lenalidomide-refractory multiple myeloma, median progression-free survival was not reached with cilta-cel and was 11.8 months with standard care. The hazard ratio for progression-free survival was 0.26, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.18 to 0.38. Overall response, complete response or better, and minimal residual disease negativity also favored cilta-cel, indicating not only delayed progression but also deeper tumor response (28).

Among antibody–drug conjugates, sacituzumab govitecan showed a consistent and clinically relevant benefit in relapsed or refractory metastatic triple-negative breast cancer. In ASCENT, progression-free survival was 5.6 months with sacituzumab govitecan and 1.7 months with physician’s choice chemotherapy, with a hazard ratio of 0.41 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.32 to 0.52. Overall survival was also improved, with median overall survival of 12.1 months versus 6.7 months, corresponding to a hazard ratio of 0.48 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.38 to 0.59. Objective response was substantially higher with sacituzumab govitecan than with chemotherapy (35).

Enfortumab vedotin improved both overall survival and progression-free survival in previously treated locally advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma. In EV-301, median overall survival was 12.88 months with enfortumab vedotin and 8.97 months with chemotherapy, with a hazard ratio of 0.70 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.56 to 0.89. Progression-free survival was also improved, with a hazard ratio of 0.62 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.51 to 0.75. Objective response and disease control were higher with enfortumab vedotin than with chemotherapy (33).

Palbociclib added to anti-HER2 and endocrine maintenance therapy improved progression-free survival in hormone receptor-positive/HER2-positive advanced breast cancer after induction therapy. In PATINA, median progression-free survival was 44.3 months with palbociclib-based maintenance therapy and 29.1 months with anti-HER2 and endocrine therapy alone, with a hazard ratio of 0.75 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.59 to 0.96. Overall survival data were less mature and did not show the same magnitude of benefit at the time of analysis (32).

Some studies showed more modest, mixed, or uncertain efficacy. Ripretinib did not demonstrate superiority over sunitinib for progression-free survival in advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumor after imatinib failure or intolerance, although objective response was numerically higher in the KIT exon 11 subgroup and tolerability favored ripretinib (21). Tumor treating fields combined with weekly paclitaxel did not produce a clear overall survival advantage in the overall platinum-resistant ovarian cancer population, although subgroup signals were reported (19). Chemotherapy plus bevacizumab improved progression-free survival compared with chemotherapy alone in bevacizumab-pretreated platinum-resistant ovarian cancer, but the magnitude of benefit was smaller and overall survival remained uncertain (30).

Other interventions did not demonstrate clear benefit in their evaluated settings. In aggressive B-cell lymphoma, tisagenlecleucel did not improve event-free survival compared with standard salvage therapy followed by autologous transplantation in responders, with similar median event-free survival between groups (36). In advanced non-small cell lung cancer, biomodulatory therapy showed inferior progression-free survival compared with nivolumab, although overall survival was numerically longer and the study was limited by early termination and small sample size (23). In metastatic colorectal cancer, pembrolizumab, binimetinib, and bevacizumab did not meet its prespecified objective response endpoint, although disease control was observed in a subset of patients (29). An exploratory random-effects meta-analysis was performed for studies reporting progression-free survival hazard ratios suitable for pooling. The pooled estimate suggested improved progression-free survival with experimental systemic antineoplastic therapies compared with their respective comparators. The pooled hazard ratio was 0.56, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.37 to 0.83. The overall effect was statistically significant, with Z = 2.84 and P = 0.004.

However, heterogeneity was very high, with tau² = 0.34, chi² = 80.48, df = 8, P < 0.00001, and I² = 94%. This indicates substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity across studies. Therefore, this pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously and should not be understood as evidence of a uniform treatment effect across all systemic antineoplastic therapies or all resistant malignancies. Rather, it suggests that selected interventions may improve progression-free survival in specific disease and treatment contexts (21–23,26,28,30,32,33,35). An exploratory random-effects meta-analysis was also performed for studies reporting overall survival hazard ratios suitable for pooling. The pooled estimate favored experimental systemic antineoplastic therapies, with a hazard ratio of 0.72 and a 95% confidence interval of 0.62 to 0.84. The overall effect was statistically significant, with Z = 4.27 and P < 0.0001.

Heterogeneity was moderate to substantial, with tau² = 0.03, chi² = 26.12, df = 11, P = 0.006, and I² = 55%. Although heterogeneity was lower than that observed for progression-free survival, differences in tumor type, therapeutic mechanism, comparator, treatment line, subsequent therapies, crossover, and follow-up duration limit interpretation of this pooled estimate as a single class effect. The overall survival meta-analysis should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating (19,22,23,25,26,28,30–35). Safety findings were clinically important because several interventions associated with meaningful efficacy also produced substantial toxicity. The safety profile varied according to therapeutic class, mechanism of action, disease context, and comparator.

Cabozantinib was associated with increased grade 3 or 4 adverse events compared with placebo in radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer. Grade 3 or 4 adverse events occurred in 57% of patients treated with cabozantinib and 26% of those receiving placebo. Treatment-related serious adverse events were also more frequent with cabozantinib, occurring in 16% versus 2% of patients. Important toxicities included diarrhea, palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia, hypertension, fatigue, hypocalcemia, and treatment discontinuation due to toxicity (22).

CAR-T cell therapies showed distinct immune-mediated toxicity profiles. In ZUMA-7, grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 91% of patients receiving axi-cel and 83% receiving standard care. Grade 3 or higher cytokine release syndrome occurred in 6% of patients, and grade 3 or higher neurologic events occurred in 21% (26). In CARTITUDE-4, cytokine release syndrome occurred in 76.1% of patients treated with cilta-cel, although grade 3 or 4 cytokine release syndrome was uncommon. Immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, cranial nerve palsy, and CAR-T-associated peripheral neuropathy were also reported (28).

Sacituzumab govitecan was associated with clinically relevant hematologic and gastrointestinal toxicity. Grade 3 or higher neutropenia occurred in 51% of patients treated with sacituzumab govitecan and 33% of those receiving chemotherapy. Febrile neutropenia occurred in 6% versus 2%, and grade 3 or higher diarrhea occurred in 10% versus less than 1%. Despite these toxicities, adverse-event-related deaths were similar between groups and none were considered related to sacituzumab govitecan (35).

Palbociclib substantially increased grade 3 and grade 4 adverse events when added to anti-HER2 and endocrine maintenance therapy. Grade 3 adverse events occurred in 79.7% of patients receiving palbociclib-based therapy and 30.6% of those receiving maintenance therapy without palbociclib. Grade 4 adverse events occurred in 10.0% versus 3.6%, respectively. The toxicity profile was mainly hematologic, especially neutropenia, and was biologically consistent with CDK4/6 inhibition (32).

Enfortumab vedotin produced survival benefit with a clinically relevant adverse-event profile. Treatment-related grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 51.4% of patients treated with enfortumab vedotin and 49.8% of those receiving chemotherapy. Important adverse events included peripheral sensory neuropathy, maculopapular rash, diarrhea, and hyperglycemia. Treatment-related deaths occurred in both groups and were numerically more frequent with enfortumab vedotin (33).

Some interventions showed more favorable tolerability than their comparators. Ripretinib was associated with fewer grade 3 or 4 adverse events than sunitinib in advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumor, with grade 3 or 4 adverse events reported in 41.3% versus 65.6% of patients, respectively. Treatment-related grade 3 or 4 adverse events were also less frequent with ripretinib than with sunitinib (21). Conversely, taselisib plus tamoxifen showed limited clinical benefit and substantial toxicity, including higher discontinuation due to toxicity and frequent diarrhea, which weakened the overall therapeutic value of the combination (34). The GRADE assessment identified 19 outcomes or characteristics rated as high certainty. These high-certainty findings were concentrated in six major therapeutic contexts: cabozantinib in radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, ciltacabtagene autoleucel in lenalidomide-refractory multiple myeloma, axicabtagene ciloleucel in early relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma, sacituzumab govitecan in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, palbociclib in hormone receptor-positive/HER2-positive advanced breast cancer, and enfortumab vedotin in previously treated advanced urothelial carcinoma (22,26,28,32,33,35).

High-certainty efficacy outcomes included progression-free survival, event-free survival, overall survival, objective response, complete response, and depth of response. High-certainty safety outcomes included grade 3 or higher adverse events, hematologic toxicity, neutropenia, leukopenia, anemia, febrile neutropenia, and grade 3 diarrhea. These outcomes were rated as high certainty when evidence came from randomized phase III trials with direct applicability, acceptable risk of bias, precise estimates, clinically meaningful effect sizes, and no serious concerns regarding inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, or publication bias.

Cabozantinib contributed high-certainty evidence for progression-free survival in radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer after previous VEGFR-targeted therapy (22). Ciltacabtagene autoleucel contributed high-certainty evidence for progression-free survival, overall response, and deep response in lenalidomide-refractory multiple myeloma (28). Axicabtagene ciloleucel contributed high-certainty evidence for event-free survival, 24-month event-free survival, progression-free survival, objective response, and complete response in early relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma (26).

Enfortumab vedotin contributed high-certainty evidence for overall survival, progression-free survival, and objective response in previously treated advanced urothelial carcinoma (33). In this systematic review, exploratory meta-analysis, and GRADE assessment of 18 randomized clinical studies, selected systemic antineoplastic therapies demonstrated clinically meaningful benefits in resistant, refractory, relapsed, or previously treated adult malignancies. The strongest and most certain evidence was observed for cabozantinib, ciltacabtagene autoleucel, axicabtagene ciloleucel, sacituzumab govitecan, enfortumab vedotin, and palbociclib-containing maintenance therapy. However, therapeutic benefit was not uniform across all interventions, and several studies showed modest, uncertain, or absent efficacy.

Exploratory pooled analyses suggested improvements in progression-free survival and overall survival, but heterogeneity was substantial, especially for progression-free survival. Therefore, pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as exploratory summaries rather than definitive evidence of a generalized class effect. High-certainty evidence was concentrated in specific treatment contexts and included both efficacy benefits and clinically important toxicity signals.

This systematic review, exploratory meta-analysis, and GRADE assessment synthesized contemporary randomized evidence on systemic antineoplastic therapies used in adults with treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, recurrent, advanced, metastatic, or previously treated malignancies. Across 18 randomized clinical studies, the evidence showed that selected systemic therapies can provide clinically meaningful benefits in difficult-to-treat oncologic settings, particularly for progression-free survival, event-free survival, overall survival, objective response, and complete response (19–36). However, these benefits were not uniform across all interventions, tumor types, or treatment contexts.

The strongest and most consistent evidence was concentrated in specific therapeutic settings. Cabozantinib improved progression-free survival in radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer previously treated with VEGFR-targeted therapy (22). Axicabtagene ciloleucel improved event-free survival, progression-free survival, objective response, and complete response in early relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma (26). Ciltacabtagene autoleucel produced substantial progression-free survival and response benefits in lenalidomide-refractory multiple myeloma (28). Sacituzumab govitecan improved progression-free survival, overall survival, and objective response in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (35). Enfortumab vedotin improved overall survival, progression-free survival, and objective response in previously treated advanced urothelial carcinoma (33). Palbociclib added to anti-HER2 and endocrine maintenance therapy improved progression-free survival in hormone receptor-positive/HER2-positive advanced breast cancer, although with a marked increase in hematologic toxicity (32).

The exploratory pooled analyses suggested an overall benefit of experimental systemic antineoplastic therapies for both progression-free survival and overall survival. Nevertheless, the progression-free survival analysis showed very high heterogeneity, and the overall survival analysis showed moderate to substantial heterogeneity. Therefore, these pooled estimates should not be interpreted as evidence of a single uniform treatment effect across all resistant malignancies. Instead, they should be understood as exploratory summaries showing that benefit is possible and sometimes substantial, but highly dependent on tumor biology, therapeutic mechanism, comparator, treatment line, and endpoint definition.

The efficacy findings support the concept that therapeutic resistance does not imply therapeutic futility. Several included trials demonstrated that biologically rational systemic strategies can overcome or partially bypass resistance mechanisms in selected clinical settings. This is particularly evident for therapies directed at actionable molecular pathways, tumor-associated antigens, immune effector mechanisms, or lineage-specific vulnerabilities (22,26,28,33,35). These results are consistent with the current understanding of cancer as a dynamic evolutionary process shaped by clonal heterogeneity, therapeutic pressure, genomic instability, and adaptive resistance mechanisms (1–6).

However, the review also shows that therapeutic innovation alone is not sufficient to guarantee clinical benefit. Several interventions produced modest, uncertain, or absent efficacy. For example, ripretinib did not improve progression-free survival over sunitinib in the overall population with advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumor after imatinib failure or intolerance, although it showed a more favorable tolerability profile and subgroup signals (21). Tumor treating fields plus paclitaxel did not demonstrate a clear overall survival advantage in the overall platinum-resistant ovarian cancer population (19). Tisagenlecleucel did not improve event-free survival compared with standard salvage therapy in aggressive B-cell lymphoma, highlighting that efficacy among CAR-T strategies may vary according to product characteristics, trial design, disease kinetics, bridging therapy, manufacturing time, comparator performance, and timing of treatment delivery (36).

These findings reinforce the need to interpret resistant malignancies as biologically and clinically heterogeneous rather than as a single category. The term “treatment-resistant cancer” includes multiple disease states, such as primary refractory disease, relapse after previous response, progression after specific drug classes, resistance to endocrine therapy, platinum resistance, lenalidomide-refractory disease, post-immunotherapy progression, and heavily pretreated disease. Each of these contexts may involve distinct resistance mechanisms and different probabilities of response to subsequent therapy (2–8). Therefore, broad pooled estimates across resistant malignancies are useful only as exploratory summaries and must be interpreted alongside disease-specific evidence.

Safety findings were central to the interpretation of clinical value. Several interventions with meaningful efficacy were also associated with substantial toxicity. Sacituzumab govitecan improved survival and response outcomes in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, but increased clinically relevant hematologic and gastrointestinal adverse events, especially neutropenia and diarrhea (35). Enfortumab vedotin improved survival in previously treated urothelial carcinoma, but required attention to neuropathy, rash, hyperglycemia, diarrhea, and treatment-related adverse events (33). Cabozantinib produced a large progression-free survival benefit in radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, but increased grade 3 or 4 adverse events, treatment-related serious adverse events, diarrhea, palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia, hypertension, fatigue, and other toxicities (22).

CAR-T cell therapies illustrate the complexity of benefit-risk interpretation in resistant hematologic malignancies. Axicabtagene ciloleucel and ciltacabtagene autoleucel produced large efficacy benefits, but were associated with expected immune-mediated toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome, neurologic events, immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, cranial nerve palsy, cytopenias, infections, and other therapy-specific adverse events (26,28). These toxicities do not negate the clinical value of CAR-T therapy, but they emphasize the importance of patient selection, specialized centers, monitoring protocols, and supportive care infrastructure.

The review also identified high-certainty evidence of harm. Palbociclib added to anti-HER2 and endocrine maintenance therapy improved progression-free survival, but substantially increased grade 3 and grade 4 adverse events, mainly hematologic toxicity, especially neutropenia (32). This is clinically important because high certainty of evidence should not be understood only as high certainty of benefit. In the GRADE framework, certainty refers to confidence in the estimated effect, whether that effect represents benefit or harm (14,15). Therefore, high-certainty toxicity outcomes are as relevant to clinical decision-making as high-certainty survival or response outcomes.

The GRADE assessment identified 19 high-certainty outcomes or characteristics. These high-certainty findings were concentrated in six major therapeutic contexts: cabozantinib in radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, ciltacabtagene autoleucel in lenalidomide-refractory multiple myeloma, axicabtagene ciloleucel in early relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma, sacituzumab govitecan in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, palbociclib in hormone receptor-positive/HER2-positive advanced breast cancer, and enfortumab vedotin in previously treated advanced urothelial carcinoma (22,26,28,32,33,35).

This concentration of high-certainty evidence has two implications. First, it indicates that the most reliable evidence is not distributed evenly across all resistant malignancies. Some tumor types and therapeutic classes are supported by large, well-designed phase III trials with precise estimates and clinically meaningful endpoints, whereas others rely on smaller studies, subgroup analyses, exploratory designs, immature outcomes, or less direct evidence. Second, it shows that certainty of evidence must be interpreted outcome by outcome. A therapy may have high-certainty evidence for progression-free survival but lower certainty for overall survival, quality of life, long-term toxicity, or specific subgroups.

This is especially important in treatment-resistant malignancies, where treatment decisions often occur under uncertainty and with limited therapeutic alternatives. In such contexts, even modest improvements in progression-free survival may be clinically meaningful for selected patients, particularly when accompanied by improved response, symptom control, or delay in clinical deterioration. Conversely, statistically significant improvements may have limited clinical value when absolute gains are small, toxicity is substantial, quality-of-life data are limited, or the evidence is indirect.

The findings suggest that selected systemic antineoplastic therapies should be considered valuable options in specific resistant, refractory, relapsed, or previously treated malignancies when supported by direct randomized evidence and acceptable benefit-risk balance. The most clinically persuasive examples in this review were those in which efficacy benefits were large, internally consistent across endpoints, supported by precise estimates, and accompanied by high certainty of evidence (22,26,28,33,35).

However, the results do not support a generalized conclusion that all newer systemic therapies improve outcomes in treatment-resistant malignancies. Clinical decisions should not be based only on the presence of an experimental therapy or on statistical significance in a single endpoint. Instead, decisions should integrate tumor type, biomarker profile, prior treatment exposure, comparator relevance, magnitude of benefit, toxicity, patient performance status, comorbidities, preferences, access, feasibility, and certainty of evidence.

The findings also reinforce the need to distinguish between disease control and survival benefit. Progression-free survival was frequently improved, but overall survival benefit was less consistent and may be influenced by crossover, subsequent therapies, disease biology, follow-up duration, and post-progression management. Therefore, progression-free survival should be interpreted as an important but incomplete measure of clinical value, especially in heterogeneous resistant cancer populations.

This review has several strengths. It was prospectively registered in PROSPERO and conducted according to PRISMA 2020 recommendations, which supports transparency and reproducibility (10,11). The review focused on randomized clinical evidence published in a contemporary period, allowing synthesis of recent systemic therapies used in resistant or previously treated adult malignancies. It combined narrative synthesis, exploratory quantitative pooling, RoB 2 risk-of-bias assessment, and GRADE certainty evaluation, providing a broad interpretation of efficacy, safety, methodological quality, and confidence in the evidence.

Another strength is the explicit recognition of heterogeneity. Rather than presenting pooled estimates as definitive class effects, this review interpreted meta-analytic results cautiously and emphasized disease-specific and therapy-specific contexts. This approach is particularly important in oncology, where the same endpoint may have different clinical meanings depending on tumor type, treatment line, comparator, and expected prognosis.

This review also has important limitations. First, the included studies were clinically heterogeneous. They involved different tumor types, therapeutic classes, treatment lines, comparators, endpoint definitions, follow-up durations, and baseline prognoses. This heterogeneity limits the interpretability of pooled estimates and supports the classification of the meta-analyses as exploratory.

Second, therapeutic resistance was operationalized according to trial-defined clinical contexts, such as refractory disease, relapse, previous treatment, progression after therapy, or advanced/metastatic disease after prior exposure. Although this approach reflects real-world trial reporting, it does not capture resistance as a standardized biological endpoint. Molecular mechanisms of resistance, clonal evolution, biomarker-defined resistance, and acquired escape pathways were inconsistently reported across studies.

Third, some included evidence came from open-label trials, subgroup analyses, exploratory reports, or studies with small sample sizes. These designs may increase vulnerability to bias, indirectness, imprecision, or selective interpretation, particularly for subjective outcomes, response assessment, toxicity reporting, and subgroup-specific effects.

Fourth, quality-of-life outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, cost-effectiveness, treatment accessibility, and long-term survivorship outcomes were not consistently available across studies. This limits the ability to fully assess clinical value, especially for therapies associated with high toxicity, logistical complexity, or substantial financial burden.

Fifth, publication bias and small-study effects could not be fully assessed for all outcomes because of the limited number of studies within each tumor-specific or therapy-specific comparison. Although funnel plots and formal tests can be considered when enough studies are available, their interpretation is limited in the presence of substantial clinical heterogeneity (18).

Future randomized trials in treatment-resistant malignancies should use clearer and more standardized definitions of resistance, refractoriness, relapse, and prior treatment exposure. Whenever possible, trials should integrate molecular, genomic, immunologic, and longitudinal biomarker analyses to distinguish primary resistance, acquired resistance, adaptive tolerance, and cross-resistance. This would improve comparability across studies and help identify which patients are most likely to benefit from specific systemic therapies.

Future research should also prioritize endpoints that capture both efficacy and clinical value. Overall survival, progression-free survival, event-free survival, objective response, complete response, minimal residual disease negativity, symptom control, quality of life, treatment discontinuation, and severe toxicity should be interpreted together rather than in isolation. In resistant malignancies, where therapeutic options may be limited and toxicity may be substantial, benefit-risk assessment should be central to trial design and evidence synthesis.

Finally, future systematic reviews should consider disease-specific meta-analyses when enough homogeneous trials are available. Broad cross-tumor meta-analyses may be useful for exploratory purposes, but more clinically actionable conclusions require narrower comparisons by tumor type, therapeutic class, biomarker profile, treatment line, and comparator.

Overall, this review shows that selected systemic antineoplastic therapies can produce clinically meaningful benefits in treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, or previously treated adult malignancies. The most reliable evidence is concentrated in specific therapeutic contexts and includes both efficacy benefits and toxicity signals. Exploratory pooled analyses favored experimental therapies for progression-free survival and overall survival, but substantial heterogeneity limits interpretation as a generalized class effect.

Therefore, the main message of this review is not that systemic therapies uniformly overcome resistance, but that some therapies, in well-defined clinical settings, can meaningfully improve outcomes despite prior treatment failure. Treatment decisions should integrate magnitude of benefit, toxicity, patient context, risk of bias, and certainty of evidence.

Selected systemic antineoplastic therapies demonstrated clinically meaningful benefits in adults with treatment-resistant, refractory, relapsed, or previously treated malignancies. The most consistent and high-certainty evidence was concentrated in specific therapeutic contexts, particularly for cabozantinib, ciltacabtagene autoleucel, axicabtagene ciloleucel, sacituzumab govitecan, enfortumab vedotin, and palbociclib-containing maintenance therapy. Benefits were mainly observed for progression-free survival, event-free survival, overall survival, objective response, complete response, and depth of response.

However, therapeutic benefit was not uniform across all interventions, tumor types, or disease settings. Exploratory meta-analyses suggested improvements in progression-free survival and overall survival, but substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity limits interpretation as a generalized class effect. High-certainty evidence also included important toxicity outcomes, emphasizing that certainty of evidence applies to both benefit and harm.

Overall, treatment decisions in resistant malignancies should integrate magnitude of survival benefit, tumor response, toxicity, patient context, risk of bias, and certainty of evidence. Future trials should apply clearer definitions of resistance and incorporate biomarker-driven analyses to better identify which patients are most likely to benefit from specific systemic therapies.
