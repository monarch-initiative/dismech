Polycythaemia vera (PV) is a haematological malignancy in the myeloproliferative 
neoplasm family. PV is typically characterized by erythrocytosis and often 
leukocytosis and thrombocytosis1. Clinical features include reduced life 
expectancy due to hazards of thrombosis (often in atypical sites), haemorrhage 
and transformation to myelofibrosis and less frequently to a form of acute 
myeloid leukaemia called blast phase. Almost two decades ago, the JAK2V617F 
mutation in exon 14 of JAK2 was described, and is known to be present in more 
than 95% of patients with PV. Testing for the JAK2V617F mutation is used in the 
diagnosis of PV, and the quantity of the mutation (that is, the variant allele 
frequency) is linked to prognosis and the risk of complications. As such, 
reduction of JAK2V617F variant allele frequency is currently being evaluated as 
a treatment target. Recommendations for PV treatment include control of vascular 
risk factors, therapeutic phlebotomy and low-dose aspirin in all patients. 
Currently, patients at higher risk of thrombosis (aged over 60 years and/or with 
a history of thrombosis) are offered cytoreductive agents. Hydroxyurea or 
interferons remain the preferred first-line cytoreductive agents, with the JAK1 
and JAK2 inhibitor, ruxolitinib, currently approved for the treatment of 
patients who are resistant to, or intolerant of, hydroxyurea. Future 
recommendations might be to treat the majority of patients with these agents as 
long-term benefits of treatment begin to emerge.

overview of this complex disease.
Epidemiology
Incidence and prevalence
PV can occur in all age groups but is much more common in older 
populations12. According to the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End 
Results program data from the USA, the median age at PV diagnosis 
is 65 years13. Case ascertainment in epidemiological studies has been 
refined by the demonstration in 2005 that most patients (~95%) have 
the acquired (somatic) mutation JAK2V617F, indicating clonal disease14. 
As this mutation can be detected in peripheral blood, case confirmation 
is greatly facilitated in epidemiological studies.
Introduction
The Philadelphia chromosome-negative myeloproliferative neo -
plasms (MPNs), which include polycythaemia vera (PV), essential 
thrombocythaemia, and primary myelofibrosis, are chronic neo -
plastic blood disorders associated with excessive production of 
mature blood cells of the myeloid lineage 1. PV is characterized by 
an abnormal increase in red blood cell (RBC) mass, often accom -
panied by leukocytosis and thrombocytosis. Clonal proliferation 
of haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) is driven by activating muta -
tions in JAK2, which encodes Janus kinase 2, a non-receptor tyros -
ine kinase that has important roles in cytokine and growth factor 
signalling. These activating JAK2 variants result in haematopoietic 
cytokine-independent signalling, thereby promoting clonal expan -
sion and myeloid differentiation 2. Of note, the most frequent driver 
mutation in PV, JAK2V617F, is also commonly identified in a setting known 
as clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP). CHIP is 
characterized by the detection of somatic mutations in the peripheral 
blood of otherwise healthy people and occurs in at least 10% of adults 
over the age of 70 years. It is well established that people with CHIP 
have increased rates of haematological malignancy, increased risk of 
cardiovascular disease (CVD) and worse all-cause mortality compared 
with those without CHIP 3,4.
The natural history of PV is marked by increased risk of thrombo-
embolic events5, and in a small subset of patients, the disease evolves 
into myelofibrosis (an entity characterized by splenomegaly and tis-
sue fibrosis in the bone marrow, causing bone marrow failure, with a 
survival of ~5 years), while an even smaller proportion (<5%) develop 
an acute myeloid leukaemia called blast phase. Myelofibrosis and blast 
phase typically occur, on average, two or more decades after the diag-
nosis of PV. Patients with PV have reduced overall survival compared

The incidence varies greatly between studies, many of which were 
conducted in resource-rich countries, which could have influenced 
case identification. A systematic review and meta-analysis in 2015 
showed reported annual incidence rates for PV ranging from 0.01 to 
2.61 cases per 100,000 people. In this systematic review, the com -
bined annual incidence rate for PV was estimated to be 0.84 cases per 
100,000 people. This analysis included studies predominantly from 
Europe, but also from North America, Asia and Australasia15. An analysis 
of registries in the European Union found that the annual incidence 
of PV varies from 0.4 to 2.8 cases per 100,000 people 16. Sweden has 
comprehensive population-based registries with compulsory report-
ing, which show an age-standardized incidence of PV of 1.48 cases per 
100,000 person-years17.
As a chronic disease with prolonged survival, the prevalence of PV 
is likely to be much higher than the incidence, but in this regard there is 
little good quality information available. In the earlier noted systematic 
review, five studies found that the prevalence of PV ranged from 0.49 
to 46.88 cases per 100,000 people, so large variation is observed 15. 
A claims-based analysis in the USA showed a PV prevalence of 22 cases 
per 100,000 (ref. 18).
Mortality and morbidity
The natural history of PV is particularly marked by an increased risk of 
thrombosis, disease transformation (to myelofibrosis or blast phase) 
and early death19. Before any planned management for PV was cus-
tomary, a retrospective study showed that 50% of untreated patients 
with PV died within 18 months, mainly due to thromboembolic events. 
The life expectancy of patients with PV is still currently lower than 
the life expectancy observed in the age-matched and sex-matched 
general population11. The annual incidence of total major thrombosis 
is age-dependent: in patients aged 60, 70 and 80 years the incidence 
is 1.9%, 3.6% and 6.8% per year, respectively20. This finding is in line 
with a contemporary population in which the annual thrombosis rate 
after diagnosis in patients with PV was approximately 2.6% per year21.
The risk of transformation to myelofibrosis has been estimated at 
between 15% and 25% over a patient’s lifetime, depending on individual 
risk factors such as older age, higher JAK2 mutation allele burden and 
additional somatic mutations, such as in ASXL1 or SRSF2. These muta-
tions are associated with aggressive disease phenotypes and predict 
an increased likelihood of transformation22,23. PV can also transform 
into acute myeloid leukaemia (blast phase), or other leukaemias in 
2–14% of patients, with higher rates linked to mutational profiles (for 
example, oncogenic variants in TP53 ) and prior cytotoxic therapy 
exposure, including agents such as busulfan or alkylating agents, 
radioactive phosphorus and potentially hydroxycarbamide. Blast 
phase arising from PV is associated with a poor prognosis, with survival 
often measured in months24.
The natural history of PV is generally based on studies conducted 
in the older adult population, but small proportions of the teenage 
and young adult populations are also affected. In the past decade 
these populations have been well-described in several articles; some 
important points include the potentially increased risks of splanchnic 
vein thrombosis in younger patients and the necessary consideration 
of special situations, for example pregnancy25,26 (Box 1).
Causes and risk factors
Environmental, lifestyle, medical and other factors are associated 
with development of PV. The search for causes of MPN in general and 
specifically for PV is difficult, owing to the rarity of the disorders and 
the long time-course of the disease. Observed clusters of MPNs have 
prompted investigation for associated occupational exposures. For 
example, in rural Pennsylvania, USA, a cluster was observed in popula-
tions with occupational exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, 
although in this cluster there was no clear indication of an association 
with cumulative exposure to these compounds27.
Cohort studies in populations and case–control studies are the 
main implements to investigate any associations with MPN. A system-
atic search of the literature in 2011 highlighted known associations with 
MPNs. In particular, PV was associated with family history of MPN and 
Jewish ethnicity. In cohort studies, some occupations were associated 
with higher mortality from PV, including poultry workers, commercial 
pressmen and petroleum refinery workers, and in case–control stud-
ies, occupations such as rural sector jobs were associated with PV 28. 
Autoimmune conditions were associated with MPN; in particular, 
giant cell arteritis was linked with PV28. Intriguingly, the incidence of PV 
in active blood donors in both Stockholm and France was higher than 
expected29,30, but this association was not observed in later studies31.
The exploratory Myeloproliferative Neoplasms: An In-depth 
Case Control (MOSAICC) study identified several risk factors for MPN, 
including increasing household density, low childhood socioeconomic 
status and high number of pack-years smoking. Current smoking was 
only associated with JAK2-positive PV and alcohol intake was inversely 
associated with risk of PV32,33. A further, larger and definitive MOSAICC 
study is in progress and will report in the next few years, potentially 
providing further insights. In a cohort study in the general population in 
Denmark, daily and occasional or ex-smokers had a higher risk of having 
an MPN diagnosis, including PV, compared with never-smokers34. The 
association of MPN with smoking is a recurrent theme, and suggests 
a role for inflammation in the development of MPNs. This hypothesis 
is reviewed in a 2023 article; of interest, inflammation has also been

Mechanisms/pathophysiology
Driver mutations in JAK2
The most prevalent mutation in PV is the somatic JAK2 gain-of-function 
mutation, valine to phenylalanine at codon 617, located in exon 14, that 
is found in ~95% of patients2,38–40. Other heterogeneous insertions or 
deletions (that is, ‘indels’) occur at JAK2 exon 12, that accounts for PV in 
2–4% of patients41. The same JAK2V617F mutation is found in 50–60% of 
patients with essential thrombocythaemia and primary myelofibrosis, 
while exon 12 mutations are restricted to patients with PV alone. JAK2 
is one of the four genes in human genome encoding for JAKs: JAK1, 
JAK2, JAK3 and TYK2.
The JAKs have two kinase domains, of which one is catalytically 
active at the protein carboxy terminus and the other is a pseudokinase 
domain located upstream of the kinase domain that binds ATP, but 
has no or a very weak ability to directly phosphorylate substrates 42. 
The JAKs have a central role in many physiological processes, includ-
ing haematopoiesis, that involve cell-to-cell signalling through 
cytokines and the downstream intracellular signalling cascade. JAKs are 
non-covalently appended through an amino-terminal FERM domain to 
the cytosolic portion of type I and type II cytokine receptors, which are 
devoid of intrinsic catalytic activity. In particular, JAK2 associates with 
the cytoplasmic domain of thrombopoietin receptor (MPL), erythro-
poietin receptor (EPOR), granulocyte colony-stimulating factor recep-
tor, growth hormone receptor and the IFNγ receptor 2. Upon cytokine 
binding to its cognate receptor, induction or reorientation of receptor 
dimerization occurs, resulting in transphosphorylation-mediated acti-
vation of the JAKs themselves, which in turn phosphorylate key tyros-
ine residues of the receptor cytoplasmic tail. These phosphorylated 
docks on the receptor and the JAKs, become scaffolds for signalling 
molecules, particularly members of the signal transducer and activa-
tor of transcription (STAT) family, which once transphosphorylated by 
JAKs, dimerize and translocate to the nucleus to regulate gene expres-
sion. JAK activation also initiates other signalling pathways such as the 
MAPK, PI3K and mTOR pathways (Fig. 2). All these proteins, once acti-
vated by phosphorylation, intervene at different levels in the control 
of cell survival, proliferation and differentiation43. Cytokine-mediated 
activation of JAK2 is non-redundantly and critically required for nor-
mal haematopoiesis to proceed, as suggested by catastrophic effects 
(that is, embryonic lethality) of experimentally induced Jak2 knockout 
in mice. Abnormal JAK–STAT signalling has been implicated across a 
number of myeloid and lymphoid malignancies and solid tumours44.
Activation of the JAK–STAT pathway could also promote reshaping 
of the chromatin architecture and the profile of transcription factor 
binding through chromatin accessibility in normal cells44. The JAK2V617F 
mutation has been shown to directly impair, through phosphorylation, 
protein arginine methyltransferase 5-mediated histone methylation45. 
Furthermore, JAK2-V617F mediates phosphorylation of tyrosine resi-
due 41 on histone H3 (ref. 46), resulting in altered gene expression and 
contributing to enhanced myeloproliferation.

their renewal, in addition to megakaryocyte differentiation and platelet 
formation; impaired expression and trafficking of MPL in a preclinical 
model of PV was demonstrated several years ago in seminal studies55.
Germline variants in JAK2 and other genes (such as TERT, MECOM 
and SH2B3 (ref. 56)) that predispose with low penetrance to sporadic 
and familial MPN have also been described (reviewed elsewhere57). In 
particular, a germline JAK2 46/1 (also known as GGCC) haplotype that 
is common in the general population predisposes to the development 
of PV with a nearly fourfold increased risk58–60, and was also reported 
to be associated with splanchnic vein thrombosis61.
Additional pathogenic mutations
Beyond driver mutations in JAK2, patients with PV often harbour muta-
tions in epigenetic regulators (TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1, IDH1 and IDH2) 
and splicing regulators (SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1 and ZRSR2), other signal-
ling proteins (CBL), cell cycle regulators, transcription factors (TP53 
and NFE2), and others. These additional mutations occur in up to 53% 
of patients with PV62, particularly in TET2, ASXL1, SRSF2 and IDH2, which 
are also often found in age-dependent CHIP3.
The order of acquisition of mutations might critically contribute 
to disease phenotype; for example, in a mouse model, inducing a TET2 
mutation first promoted a phenotype resembling essential thrombo-
cythaemia and inducing a JAK2 mutation first induced a phenotype 
that was more PV-like 63. Furthermore, the combination of Jak2 V617F 
expression in mouse HSCs with concomitant Tet2 loss promoted the 
fitness of Jak2-mutated cells, by conferring a strong functional com -
petitive advantage over normal haematopoiesis, highlighting func -
tional consequences of a co-mutation profile in PV 64. Patients with 
PV who have a DNMT3A  mutation might be less responsive to IFNα 
therapy, and this mutation also selectively promoted the expansion 
of DNMT3A–JAK2V617F co-mutated subclones in patients65, although this 
finding is controversial66. Interestingly, such a resistance to IFN was 
overcome by combination therapy of IFNα with azacytidine in mice 
bearing both mutations in haematopoietic cells.
Combinatorial effects of additional mutations with JAK2V617F could 
also be involved in the promotion of progression to myelofibrosis67,68. 
Mutations in SRSF2, documented in 2% of patients with PV, are con -
sidered adverse mutations owing to their negative effect on survival 
in a multivariable analysis, together with older age, leukocytosis and 
thrombosis history23. As discussed above, ASXL1 mutations have also 
been associated with adverse outcomes. Interestingly, one hypothesis 
is that, in some patients at least, the PV clone might originate from a 
single HCS before acquiring the JAK2V617F mutation. This mechanism was 
suggested from data showing that acute myeloid leukaemia transfor-
mations in PV are frequently JAK2V617F mutation-negative69,70, yet share 
the JAK2 haplotype. This finding underscores the effect of additional 
mutations, and could have important future therapeutic impacts when 
considering, for example, mutation-targeted therapies 69. Of note, 
karyotype abnormalities are found in about 12% of patients with PV and 
include trisomy of chromosomes 8 or 9, alterations in chromosomes 
5 and 7, and loss of the Y chromosome, which have all been associated 
with reduced survival71–73.
Systemic effects
In PV, clonal dominance with the competitive advantage of the 
malignant myeloproliferative clone, progression to myelofibrosis 
and systemic manifestations, are all different aspects of a profound, 
chronic condition of hyperinflammation that is generated by the clone 
itself35 (Fig. 2). Sustained inflammation also contributes to the increased 
vascular risk and atherosclerosis of patients with PV74. Circulating lev-
els of acute phase inflammatory proteins (including high sensitivity 
C-reactive protein (CRP) and pentraxin-3) are chronically elevated in 
PV and these levels are correlated with increased JAK2V617F variant allele 
frequency and increased risk of thrombosis75. Multiple cytokines were 
found to be elevated in the serum of patients with PV and associated 
with phenotypic traits and shortened survival76; a distinctive profile in 
relation to that of myelofibrosis was also highlighted76,77.
Subtle differences in inflammatory signalling driven by the 
myeloproliferative clone might also exist, with IFNγ and STAT1-
directed gene expression being less prevalent in PV than in essen -
tial thrombocythaemia 78. Inflammation might result from a 
combination of cell-autonomous-induced (via JAK2) and non-cell-
autonomous-induced (including cytokines such as IL-1α, IL-1β and TNF , 
and non-malignant stromal cells) activation of the NFκB cascade 79. 
Moreover, ablation of NFκB signalling in in vivo mouse models of PV 
alleviated the myeloproliferative phenotype. NFκB could therefore 
represent a target for therapy (for example, with BET inhibitors and 
IRAK1–4 inhibitors)78. In particular, IL-1β-mediated inflammation in 
mice favoured the early clonal expansion of JAK2-mutant HSCs 79. Of 
note, elevated levels of IL-1β in the serum of patients with MPN, and IL-1 
receptors on their haematopoietic progenitors and HSCs, correlated 
with increased JAK2 variant allele frequency 80. A substantial part of 
the therapeutic activity of ruxolitinib (a JAK1 and JAK2 inhibitor) is 
considered to be mediated by inhibition of inflammatory cytokines81,82. 
Finally, the role of inflammation in the pathogenesis and progression 
of MPN is also exemplified by the link between cigarette smoke and the 
expansion of JAK2 variant allele frequency and TET2 myeloproliferative 
clones in preclinical models83.
Dysregulated iron metabolism
Another level of deranged regulation in the pathobiology of PV is repre-
sented by iron metabolism, that configures a condition of non-anaemic 
iron deficiency. Patients with PV at diagnosis are typically microcytic 
(RBCs smaller than normal) and iron-deficient (low serum levels of 
iron and the iron storage protein ferritin, and low saturation of the 
iron-transport protein, transferrin) due to increased utilization of iron 
by proliferating erythrocytes. Iron deficiency is further exacerbated 
by repeated therapeutic phlebotomies that are needed to maintain

In PV, evidence indicates that erythropoiesis continues at a high 
rate despite iron deficiency and is associated with inappropriate sup-

normal conditions, the serum levels of hepcidin are physiologically 
upregulated by increased circulating iron levels and mediators of 
systemic inflammation (particularly IL-6 and IL-1β)87, and are downregu-
lated by bone marrow erythropoiesis through the peptide hormone 
erythroferrone released by erythroblasts 88. Hepcidin restrains iron 
availability by negatively regulating the activity of ferroportin (an iron 
exporter channel expressed on duodenal enterocytes, hepatocytes 
and macrophages); by reducing the export of intracellular iron, func-
tional iron-deficient restriction of erythropoiesis occurs87. In spite of 
suppressed levels of hepcidin in PV, systemic iron deficiency does not 
resolve, implying dysregulated iron homeostasis that might originate 
from concurrent systemic inflammation, elevated circulating levels of 
erythroferrone and/or aberrant tissue hypoxia-induced signalling89.
Administration of exogenous hepcidin reversed erythrocyto -
sis in JAK2V617F mice, possibly by restricting erythropoiesis following 
increased iron sequestration in macrophages 90. Also, treatment of 
patients with PV with ruxolitinib led to normalization of iron-related 
parameters and increased serum hepcidin levels, possibly as a conse-
quence of drug-induced suppression of erythropoiesis85. Subtle differ-
ences might exist between JAK2V617F and JAK2 exon 12-mutated PV, as in 
mice expressing exon 12-mutated Jak2, hepatic hepcidin expression was 
significantly lower than in Jak2V617F-mutated mice, while erythroferrone 
expression was increased, with increased erythroferrone expression 
also confirmed in analysis of erythroblasts from human primary PV49. 
A large population genome-wide association study showed that a sin-

iron-deficient JAK2-mutant mice show increased platelet production 
at the expense of erythropoiesis through primary involvement of 
common pre-megakaryocyte–erythrocyte progenitors92.
Diagnosis, screening and prevention
Signs and symptoms
A diagnosis of PV can be made in three main circumstances. First, the 
diagnosis can be made incidentally based on a peripheral blood count 
performed for another reason or during a systematic check-up. Second, 
a blood count can be prescribed owing to signs and symptoms that are 
suggestive of PV, such as palpable splenomegaly (present in about 30% 
of patients at diagnosis), aquagenic pruritus, microvascular symptoms 
(visual disturbances including blurring and scotoma, dizziness, head-
aches and erythromelalgia) or unexplained fatigue. Although none of 
them is pathognomonic of PV, aquagenic pruritus and erythromelalgia 
(a sudden and transient erythema, with warmth or burning of digits) are 
characteristic93. Third, in approximately a quarter of patients, the diag-
nosis is made after a vascular event, either thrombotic or haemorrhagic. 
Unusual sites of thrombosis, such as splanchnic veins (Budd–Chiari 
syndrome, portal or mesenteric veins) or cerebral sinuses, should raise 
the suspicion for an underlying prothrombotic state, among which 
MPN, and PV in particular, is frequently found94.
PV diagnostic criteria
Two classification systems currently coexist for MPNs, both updated 
in 2022: WHO classification95 and the International Consensus Clas-
sification (ICC)1; however, these systems are similar and consider the 
same key parameters for PV diagnosis96 (Fig. 3). There are three major 
criteria in both classifications: evidence of an erythrocytosis, charac-
teristic histopathological changes in the bone marrow biopsy, and 
identification of a known mutation in the JAK2 gene.
Evidence of erythrocytosis. Unregulated production of RBCs leading 
to an absolute erythrocytosis is the distinctive feature of PV, among all 
MPNs. Once a diagnosis of PV is suspected, an elevated haemoglobin 
concentration (above 16.5 g/dl in men, above 16.0 g/dl in women) 
and/or haematocrit (above 49% in men, above 48% in women) are 
deemed sufficient to diagnose PV according to the WHO classification 
and the ICC (although local factors, such as comorbidities and altitude, 
should be accounted for), if the other criteria are fulfilled. The main 
change in these criteria compared with the previous versions is that 
RBC mass measurement was removed from the diagnostic criteria in 
the WHO classification, while it was maintained in the ICC. This test 
has become less accessible but it remains the only direct evidence of 
true erythrocytosis, defined by an increased RBC mass above 125% 
of the expected value. RBC mass is particularly useful in situations 
where there is a notable expansion of the plasma volume (resulting in a 
haemodilution that will induce an artificial decrease in the haematocrit 
value)97. For example, in one study, half of the patients with splanchnic 
vein thrombosis had an absolute erythrocytosis demonstrated by the 
RBC mass, while they did not reach the above-mentioned thresholds 
of haemoglobin or haematocrit for the diagnosis of PV98. In addition to 
enabling the diagnosis of PV, RBC mass can also help to set a therapeutic 
target in these patients with a haematocrit often spuriously lower than 
45%. It is important to be mindful of the limitations of relying on hae-
moglobin or haematocrit, especially in the presence of iron deficiency, 
which will mask elevations in these parameters.
Histopathological changes in bone marrow.  The second major 
criterion consists of specific findings in the bone marrow biopsy: 
an age-adjusted hypercellularity with trilineage proliferation 
(panmyelosis), including prominent erythroid and granulocytic pro-
liferation, and particularly an increase in pleomorphic, mature mega-
karyocytes without atypia (the latter being suggestive of myelofibrosis) 
(Fig. 3). Bone marrow biopsy findings can also help reclassify patients 
with PV who initially presented with apparently isolated thrombo -
cytosis and red cell parameters below the thresholds for defining 
overt PV99,100. Interestingly, the ICC and WHO classification tolerate 
avoiding bone marrow biopsy for diagnostic purpose in patients with 
very high RBC parameters (haemoglobin and haematocrit above 
18.5 g/dl and 55.5% in men, and above 16.5 g/dl and 49.5% in women) 
and JAK2 mutation. However, bone marrow biopsy at the time of PV 
diagnosis can offer important prognostic information, as the iden -
tification of low-grade fibrosis is correlated with an increased risk of 
unfavourable outcomes and evolution to post-PV myelofibrosis 101. 
Still, bone marrow biopsy analysis can sometimes be difficult and 
significant inter-pathologist and intra-pathologist variability has

JAK2 mutation, regardless of the mutant allele burden. Therefore, a 
qualitative assay detecting the mutation with a reasonable sensitivity 
(that should be less than 1%) is necessary and sufficient for the diagnosis 
of PV, and quantification of the mutant variant allele frequency is not 
required, although it is recommended, in this context by the ICC. Of 
note, JAK2 mutations can be detected in individuals without evidence 
of MPN or PV, referred to as CHIP; yet, this finding is associated with a 
risk of vascular events and later development of MPN3,109,110. However, 
a measurement of variant allele frequency might be useful at diag -
nosis for patient follow-up, as it can provide important prognostic 
information. For example, a high variant allele frequency above 50% 
has been correlated with an increased risk of disease evolution to 
myelofibrosis111,112. On the other hand, therapies that can reduce the 
JAK2V617F variant allele frequency are now available, including various 
formulations of IFNα113,114 and ruxolitinib115,116. Thus, an assessment of 
the evolution of variant allele frequency during such therapies might 
have relevance, as patients with PV who achieve a durable molecular 
response have been demonstrated to have significant improvements 
in their event-free, progression-free and overall survival116,117.

Overall diagnostic strategy
PV is suspected based on the presence of characteristic clinical fea -
tures or symptoms, or after a major thrombotic event (potentially in 
a rare territory, such as splanchnic veins or cerebral sinuses), along 
with an increase in haemoglobin or haematocrit values as described 
above. In a patient with suspected PV, the first step should include 
screening for JAK2 mutation and serum erythropoietin level, both 
tests being performed on a peripheral blood sample. In patients with 
clear erythrocytosis and a JAK2 mutation with a subnormal erythro -
poietin level, the diagnosis of PV can reliably be made. Importantly, 
a subnormal erythropoietin level alone is insufficient for diagnosis. 
Additional information can be collected in selected patients via bone 
marrow biopsy, quantitative evaluation of JAK2V617F variant allele fre-
quency and NGS for the screening for additional mutations. These 
additional tests that give prognostic information could potentially be 
proposed to patients diagnosed at a younger age, who will require a 
very long follow-up and potentially several lines of treatment. Perform-
ing routine cytogenetic testing has been recommended at diagnosis, 
although this testing is controversial as it does not currently affect 
treatment.
Management
The treatment of PV has traditionally focused on thrombosis risk 
reduction in the absence of ‘disease-modifying’ therapies capable 
of affecting the underlying disease biology 123 (Fig. 4). Patients are 
conventionally classified as low risk or high risk for thrombotic com-
plications based on age and thrombosis history (other prognostic 
models are included in Table 1 ). Patients younger than 60 years and 
without previous thrombosis are considered at low risk of throm -
bosis and standard management (as discussed in the next section) 
includes low-dose aspirin and therapeutic phlebotomy to maintain the 
haematocrit <45%, although this position might be changing towards 
more aggressive therapy as discussed below 124. High-risk patients 
are those aged ≥60 years and/or with a history of thrombosis, and 
typically are eligible for cytoreductive therapy, in addition to thera -
peutic phlebotomy and low-dose aspirin. Clinical practice guidelines 
do acknowledge special situations in which cytoreductive therapy 
might be indicated in low-risk patients, such as a frequent need for 
or poor tolerance of therapeutic phlebotomy, new thrombosis or 
disease-related major bleeding, progressive thrombocytosis and/or 
leukocytosis, splenomegaly or disease-related symptoms 125,126. Man-
agement of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors is often overlooked 
but is of paramount importance.
The definitions of low-risk and high-risk PV have traditionally 
included below and above age 60–65 years and/or the presence of prior 
disease-related thrombosis or haemorrhage. Additional factors under 
consideration are leukocytosis and JAK2 variant allele frequency. Stud-
ies have arrived at somewhat divergent conclusions as to the relative 
contribution of leukocytosis to thrombosis risk. The European Col -
laboration on Low-Dose Aspirin in Polycythemia Vera (ECLAP) study 
(n = 1,638) identified a significant increase in the risk of thrombosis 
associated with a white blood cell (WBC) count >15 × 109/l, compared 
with <10 × 10 9/l127. A time-dependent, multivariable subanalysis of 
the Cytoreductive Therapy in Polycythemia Vera (CYTO-PV) study 
examining WBC counts immediately preceding thrombotic events 
and organizing them into approximate quartiles found that the risk 
of thrombosis steadily rose in patients with WBC counts >7 × 10 9/l, 
becoming statistically significant when the WBC count was >11 × 109/l128. 
By contrast, however, a retrospective analysis that used group-based 
trajectory modelling in 520 patients with PV followed at US academic 
centres found that persistently elevated leukocyte trajectories were 
not associated with thrombotic risk, although they were associated 
with the risk of disease evolution129. In the past decade, the Prospec -
tive Observational Study of Patients with PV in US Clinical Practices, 
a large, prospective, non-interventional study (n  = 2,510) across the 
US community and academic practices, showed that a WBC count 
>11 × 109/l was significantly associated with the risk of thromboembo-
lism in both low-risk and high-risk patients with PV. The association 
remained statistically significant for WBC counts >12 × 10 9/l in the 
context of controlled haematocrit (≤45%)130. Elevated neutrophil-to-
lymphocyte ratios have been shown to predict venous thrombosis in 
PV and all cause mortality131,132.

Standard management
Given that the most common complication associated with a diagnosis 
of PV is a vascular event, it is logical that standard management should 
include assessment and control of cardiovascular risk factors. The 
seminal CYTO-PV study (n = 365) established a stringent haematocrit 
goal of <45% in all patients with PV, regardless of the therapeutic modal-
ity used136. In this study, despite an absolute difference of only 3.1% 
between the median haematocrit values in the stringent haematocrit 
(<45%) and less stringent haematocrit (45–50%) groups, a fourfold 
reduction was observed in the incidence of the primary end point 
(major thrombosis or cardiovascular death) after a median follow-up 
of 31 months in the stringent group. Currently, some experts prefer 
a lower haematocrit target of <42% in women, given the differences 
in normal range between men and women, as well as a lower target 
in patients with splanchnic vein thrombosis; however, these recom-
mendations are not evidence-based. Similarly, no current evidence 
base exists for the appropriate target haematocrit in patients with PV 
who live at altitude.
Of note, therapeutic phlebotomy to reduce haematocrit induces 
iron deficiency with its attendant adverse effects and also dysregulates 
iron metabolism. The frequency of phlebotomy is highly variable 
between patients but is usually most intense at the time of diagnosis. 
Frequency of monitoring via blood count is therefore individually 
tailored.
The ECLAP study established the efficacy and safety of daily 
low-dose aspirin in all patients with PV, unless contraindicated137.
Cytoreductive agents
Hydroxyurea . Although hydroxyurea is the most widely used 
cytoreductive agent in patients with PV, it has not been studied in 
a prospective, randomized controlled trial in a population with 
contemporarily defined (for example, by detection of JAK2 V617F) PV. 
However, a propensity score matching analysis of 1,042 patients 
from the ECLAP study, of whom 681 received hydroxyurea and 
342 therapeutic phlebotomy only, demonstrated that with similar 
follow-up (29.9 months for phlebotomy and 34.7 months for hydroxyu-
rea), the incidence of fatal or non-fatal cardiovascular events was 5.8 
versus 3 per 100 person-years, respectively (P = 0.002)138. A US Surveil-
lance, Epidemiology, and End Results–Medicare database study of 
820 older adults with PV found that 16.3% received neither phlebotomy 
nor hydroxyurea and this group had the worst outcomes in terms of 
both thrombosis rate and survival, compared with those who received 
therapeutic phlebotomy only, hydroxyurea only, or both139. Data are 
conflicting on whether the need for phlebotomy while receiving 
hydroxyurea affects thrombosis outcomes in PV140,141. In our practice, 
we routinely optimize the dosing of hydroxyurea or other cytoreduc-
tive therapy to minimize or eliminate the requirement for phlebot -
omy, in an effort to consistently maintain the haematocrit below 45%. 
While an enduring controversy, hydroxyurea has never conclusively 
been shown to increase the risk of leukaemic transformation 142–144. 
Exposure to hydroxyurea does, however, confer an increased risk of 
non-melanoma skin cancer145.
Interferons. IFNs have long been known to be associated with high 
rates of haematological and molecular responses in patients with PV 
(reviewed elsewhere146). Their specific mechanism of action is not fully 
elucidated but includes apoptosis, immune modulation and depletion 
of mutant HSC.
In 2019, ropeginterferon alfa-2b, a monopegylated IFNα 2b 
administered subcutaneously every 2 weeks, obtained regulatory 
approval in Europe for the treatment of PV, marking the first licen -
sure of an IFN formulation for the treatment of an MPN. US approval 
followed in 2021 (Table 2 ). The main appeal of IFNs lies in their 
disease-modifying potential. Long-term follow-up of the phase III 
PROUD/CONTINUATION-PV trial that compared ropeginterferon 
alfa-2b with hydroxyurea in patients with PV demonstrated a sig -
nificant event-free survival advantage for ropeginterferon alfa-2b147. 
The label for ropeginterferon alfa-2b is both risk-agnostic and line of 
therapy-agnostic, meaning for all patients deemed in need of therapy 
regardless of whether first-line or beyond. Results from the Low-PV 
trial, which compared therapeutic phlebotomy and aspirin, with 
phlebotomy and aspirin plus low fixed-dose ropeginterferon alfa-2b 
in patients with low-risk PV, showed superiority of the latter for the 
composite end point of haematocrit control and absence of disease 
progression, and could signal a paradigm shift in the management of 
low-risk PV148,149.

Novel agents in clinical development
Novel agents in clinical development for the treatment of PV include 
hepcidin-modulating agents (rusfertide, sapablursen and divesiran), 
the histone deacetylase inhibitor, givinostat, and the lysine-specific 
demethylase 1 inhibitor, bomedemstat. All of these agents are cur -
rently in active phase I–III clinical trials (Table  4). Hepcidin acts as 
the master regulator of iron availability, through inhibition of the 
iron transporter ferroportin expressed by enterocytes, hepatocytes 
and macrophages164. Serum levels of hepcidin are inappropriately 
depressed in patients with PV, and in a JAK2V617F mouse model of PV, 
administration of a mini-hepcidin mimetic resulted in normalization of 
haematocrit and splenomegaly165. This concept was then evaluated in 
a phase II trial of self-administered, weekly subcutaneous injection of 
rusfertide (PTG-300) in a dose-finding stage followed by a double-blind, 
randomized withdrawal period with placebo over 12 weeks, in 
70 patients with either low-risk or high-risk PV, approximately half of 
whom were receiving concurrent cytoreductive therapy166. A response, 
which was defined as haematocrit control and the absence of phle -
botomy, was attained in 60% versus 17% in the placebo arm (P = 0.002). 
Improvements in symptoms and iron indices were observed with rusfer-
tide but no improvements in leukocyte or platelet counts were noted, 
and changes in JAK2V617F variant allele frequency were not reported. An 
ongoing randomized phase III trial has completed accrual, and initial 
results are anticipated in 2025 (NCT05210790).
A number of other therapeutic interventions converging on the 
hepcidin pathway are also under early-phase clinical evaluation. The 
antisense oligonucleotide, sapablursen, targets TMPRSS6 mRNA (that 
encodes a transmembrane serine protease matriptase-2), which nega-
tively regulates the hepcidin coreceptor, hemojevulin. The net effect 
of sapablursen is upregulation of hepcidin expression, and this subcu-
taneous injectable is being evaluated in an open-label phase IIa trial in 
phlebotomy-dependent patients with PV (NCT05143957). Divesiran, 
an N-acetylgalactosamine conjugated 19-mer short interfering RNA of 
TMPRSS6, is also being evaluated in phlebotomy-dependent patients 
with PV in a phase I–II trial (NCT05499013).
Givinostat is an oral pan-histone deacetylase inhibitor that modu-
lates acetylation status of both histone and non-histone proteins (such 
as the heat shock protein 90 molecular chaperone); this drug reduces 
JAK–STAT signalling and selectively reduces JAK2-V617F protein levels 
in MPN cell lines and primary cells167. Givinostat has been extensively 
studied in a number of early-phase trials in PV as a monotherapy and 
in combination with hydroxyurea, with response rates that range from 
50% to 80% in controlling blood counts and improving symptoms168–171. 
Myelosuppression and gastrointestinal toxicity are well characterized 
class effects, seen in approximately 50% of treated patients and can lead 
to dose modifications and even discontinuation in some patients. The 
GIV-IN global, randomized, phase III trial in high-risk patients with PV 
within 3 years of diagnosis will compare givinostat and hydroxyurea 
with a primary end point of complete haematological remission and 
normal spleen size at week 48 (NCT06093672).

Quality of life
The constellation of challenges that patients with PV face includes 
a range of symptoms, quality of life (QoL) and health issues (Fig.  5). 
Indeed, even in Sir William Osler’s first descriptions of the disorder, 
there was recognition of plethora, reddening of the skin and nota -
ble pruritus, amongst other challenges. The first efforts to improve 
the quantification of these symptoms were driven by patient advo -
cates with MPN who approached investigators. Comprehensive 
cross-sectional survey research of patients with MPN demonstrated 
fatigue as the most common symptom and aquagenic pruritus as a clas-
sic symptom172. Challenging symptoms can be a direct consequence of 
erythrocytosis affecting vascular flow (for example, headaches, includ-
ing migraines with aura, or challenges with concentration), a conse -
quence of splenomegaly (in a subset of patients) with early satiety, pain 
and abdominal fullness, or metabolically derived (for example, drench-
ing night sweats). Other symptoms that can be noted in PV include 
weight loss, bone pain, fever and more advanced splenomegaly-related 
symptoms, and in a patient with PV these might signal progression to 
myelofibrosis.
Health-related QoL (HR-QOL) looks at QoL as affected by 
health, and can include symptoms from a disease, but also include 
medication-related toxicities, the financial burdens of therapy, the 
complexity and hassle of medical care, travel needed for medical care, 
anxiety over uncertain health, the effects of disease on activities of 
daily living or desired activities or travels, among others. Symptoms 
related to a disease such as PV can clearly affect HR-QOL, sometimes 
substantially and, if severe enough, can be the biggest factor (for exam-
ple, severe fatigue, pruritus or night sweats) that might dramatically 
affect an individual.
The myelofibrosis symptom assessment form (MF-SAF) was devel-
oped to quantify symptoms in patients with myelofibrosis 173, based 
on survey data gathered from patients with myelofibrosis, and has 
evolved to MF-SAF 4.0, the current standard. Recognizing early on that 
symptoms are present in essential thrombocythaemia and PV that are 
less relevant in myelofibrosis, a broader based MPN symptom assess-
ment form (MPN-SAF) 174 was developed, and has been validated in 
dozens of languages and used in numerous phase III clinical trials. These 
patient-reported outcomes (PROs) forms have been highly validated 
to be: first, understandable by patients; second, able to reproducibly 
measure individual symptoms; and third, able to measure change in 
response to therapy. Over time, we have also seen great consistency 
in the prevalance and ranking of symptoms across countries and lan-
guages, with some effects of culture on absolute symptom severity 
between groups (but with the ability to measure change preserved). 
Given that each patient acts as his or her own control (measuring the 
change compared with baseline in individual symptoms or in aggre -
gate), a total symptom score was developed. Responses in trials have 
been variable, ranging from the percentage of patients with a 50% 
reduction in total symptom score compared with baseline (typically a 
minimal evaluable baseline), or a measurement of change in the single 
most bothersome symptom(s).
The National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recom-
mend that PV symptoms be measured by the MPN-SAF at baseline and 
serially with management175. The presence of problematic symptoms 
is used in the guidelines as a threshold for initiation of cytoreduc -
tive therapy in those patients on phlebotomy and aspirin alone, or 
for changing cytoreductive therapy to a second or subsequent line.

Outlook
PV is an acquired myeloproliferative clonal disorder caused by a 
mutant JAK2 HSC clone. In this condition, the peripheral blood genetic 
mutation profile reflects that of the HSC and can be repeatedly sam-
pled; thus, we have made considerable progress in our understanding 
of the pathogenesis and progress of this condition. The description of 
JAK2-mutated CHIP3, and also elegant work showing the likely occur-
rence of mutations sometimes many decades before any diagnosis of 
disease109,110, highlight the likelihood that PV, or at least JAK2-mutated 
CHIP, will be increasingly identified. This possibility raises the potential 
requirement for treatment to prevent the development of PV, or at least 
to minimize the associated enhanced risk of vascular events. These 
potential future interventions will require substantial efforts to identify 
patients with high-risk CHIP and to develop a minimally toxic therapy.
Historical data from a retrospective study in the 1970s, before 
current treatment algorithms evolved, showed that 50% of patients 
with PV died within 18 months, mainly due to thromboembolic events19. 
Over time, PV management has evolved to therapeutic phlebotomy, 
aspirin and cytoreduction to reduce the risk of thromboembolic events, 
with little evidence that this strategy will affect disease progression or 
overall survival, until recently (as discussed above). Targets for such 
treatments were normalization of the surrogate of RBC mass, haema-
tocrit and, controversially, the leukocyte and platelet counts, although 
evidence for this approach has only just emerged. As discussed above, 
the MAJIC-PV study, published in 2023, suggested that therapy research 
should now also focus on some form of molecular response (probably 
at least a 50% reduction in JAK2 V617F variant allele frequency) 116. Fur-
thermore, whilst in contemporary practice, cytoreductive treatments 
have only been utilized for high-risk PV, conventional lower-risk PV 
is now increasingly recognized to have an adverse disease outcome. 
Evolving prognostication will better identify those patients with PV, 
possibly even all patients, who should probably be treated with the 
newer, less toxic, potentially more potent, cytoreductive agents that 
are currently available.
Challenges or indeed opportunities in the future include the 
need to understand how to prioritize therapy targets; that is, com -
plete haematological response or molecular response or both. Work 
is needed to harmonize our abilities to robustly measure changes in 
the JAK2V617F variant allele frequency. We also need to understand what 
factors affect attainment of molecular response and what happens to 
non-driver mutations. For example, we should investigate whether 
clinicians should aim to reduce the JAK2V617F variant allele frequency 
within a year or whether it is sufficient to treat with the aim of achiev-
ing a target of, say, 50% reduction within 3 years. Of note, in defining 
molecular response, a 50% reduction is a much more modest threshold 
than that currently used in other myeloid malignancies, such as chronic 
myeloid leukaemia. If the 50% reduction is achieved, then we could ask 
whether the aim should be for stabilization or further reduction. A bet-
ter definition of true minimal residual disease might be the sustained 
normalization of blood cell counts without the need for phlebotomies, 
a normal or virtually normal bone marrow biopsy, and low-burden 
JAK2V617F variant allele frequency (perhaps even lower than 10%, rather 
1–5% or <1%). In addition, whether additional mutations in genes such 
as TET2 and DNMT3A should also have a 50% reduction in variant allele 
frequency could be investigated. These aims all reflect important future 
research priorities.
As discussed above, agents targeting the hepcidin pathway could 
also prove useful adjuncts, as the burden associated with repeated 
phlebotomy is high. Many agents are currently being evaluated, and 
whether these will be used alone or as an adjunct to cytoreduction 
needs to be evaluated. Long-term risks and benefits of these treatments 
will also need to be carefully scrutinized. We also need to investigate 
what happens to patients when these agents are potentially discontin-
ued and whether there will be a treatment escape mechanism. In the 
past 5 years, interest has also focused on combination therapies; for 
example, the combination of IFN and ruxolitinib. Also under investiga-
tion is the combined use of cytoreductive agents for PV and a statin, 
as statins have anti-inflammatory and positive vascular effects; such a 
combination has had an intriguing effect on the JAK2V617F variant allele 
frequency177,178.

need to be identified and made available. The second very troubling 
but less prevalent symptom is pruritus; again, our understanding of 
the pathophysiology of this symptom is not clear. Whilst our current 
therapies are mostly successful in reducing symptoms, an improved 
understanding of the underlying cause(s) of these disease features 
would help identify better therapy.
After more than 70 years from the first description of MPNs by 
William Dameshek179 and the clonal origin of the disease180,181, priorities 
beyond the next 5–10 years include better stratification of patients to 
truly appreciate the risks of disease transformation, particularly to 
primary myelofibrosis and acute myeloid leukaemia, as well as under-
standing surrogate markers to indicate that treatments are reducing 
those risks. Furthermore, whilst a number of therapeutic options are 
available for patients with PV, these are not curative and have consider-
able adverse effects, especially the risk of non-melanoma skin cancers 
and, in the case of IFN therapy, mood and autoimmune disturbances. 
Newer treatments, such as those targeting mutant JAK2 or pseudoki-
nase domain-specific inhibitors should be evaluated and could offer 
the promise of less toxic and more targeted agents. For diseases such 
as PV, efforts must also include the collection of detailed prospective 
biological and clinical data, which will facilitate better informed clinical 
pathways in the future.
