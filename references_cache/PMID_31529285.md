# A Myriad of Scleroma Presentations: The Usual and Unusual.
**Authors:** Elwany S, Fattah HA, Mandour Z, Ismail AS, Abdelnabi M
**Journal:** Head Neck Pathol (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s12105-019-01075-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12105-019-01075-5)

## Content

To prospectively study a series of scleroma patients with a focus on socioeconomic status, clinical presentation, pathology, microbiology, and treatment. A total of 52 patients diagnosed with scleroma were included in the study. Demographic data of the patients were collected and clinical, radiographic, and histopathologic findings were analyzed. Biopsies from atypical patients were sent for electron microscopic examination. Patients were treated both medically and surgically with clinical follow-up to determine outcomes and the incidence of recurrence. Fifty-two patients were included in the study. There were 23 males (44.2%) and 29 females (55.8%). The highest incidence of scleroma was identified in individuals from poor, rural areas in the third to fourth decades of life. The nose was affected in all cases. Other affected sites included the nasopharynx, soft and hard palate, larynx and trachea, lacrimal passages, skin, and gingiva. Some patients had atypical presentations. Complete follow-up of all patients was hindered by non-compliance with clinic visits and the long duration of medical treatment. Recurrence or relapse of the granulomatous lesions after initial improvement occurred in 11 patients (21.2%) within 1 to 3 years. Scleroma is a stubborn, chronic, granulomatous bacterial disease. The diverse presentations may mimic neoplasms and other granulomatous conditions. We present cases with unusual presentations and demonstrate the transmission electron microscopic features of these lesions. Furthermore, we confirm the importance of achieving full eradication of the etiologic bacilli to prevent recurrent disease.

Keywords: Rhinoscleroma, Granuloma, Nose, Klebsiella, Tracheostomy

Scleroma is a chronic granulomatous disease of the upper respiratory tract caused by a gram-negative bacillus, Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis (Von Frisch bacillus) [1, 2]. In classic form, the disease affects the nose in nearly all cases hence the former name of rhinoscleroma. The disease is endemic in Egypt, Central and South America, South-East Asia, and areas of Eastern Europe [3, 4]. Despite affecting populations around the globe, epidemiologic data on the incidence and prevalence of scleroma in different regions of the world are lacking [2].

Scleroma frequently involves sites other than the nose. These include the pharynx, larynx, trachea, and bronchi [5, 6]. Atypical presentations of scleroma are occasionally reported in the literature. These include involvement of the sinuses, lacrimal apparatus, cervical lymph nodes, and cranial cavity as well as the development of malignancy in association with the disease [7–11].

Many of the mechanisms behind scleroma infection remain unclear including the mode of transmission and infection, possible genetic predisposition, etiologic factors of atypical presentations, and reasons behind the high recurrence rate [12]. These questions guided our study of a cohort of scleroma patients referred to our department over a 12-year period.

The present series included 52 patients with histologically confirmed scleroma who presented to our department from January 2005 to December 2017. The demographic data gathered from patients included age, sex, residence, occupation, and education. Evaluation of the patients included full history taking, complete otolaryngology examination, applicable endoscopic examination, and computed tomography scans. Biopsies were taken from all cases and sent for histopathologic examination. Diagnosis was based on the clinical and histopathologic data. Tissue samples from patients with atypical presentations were sent for transmission electron microscopic examination. Bacterial cultures were performed at follow-up to determine the results of treatment.

The antibiotics prescribed were ciprofloxacin (500 mg twice daily), rifampin (150 mg twice daily), or Levofloxacin (500 mg once daily) [2, 11, 13]. They were given for 12 weeks followed by another nasal punch biopsy. If the biopsy results showed persistent disease, the treatment was continued for an additional 12 weeks with repeated laboratory tests for liver and kidney functions.

The patients were treated with varying surgical interventions. The most frequent procedures were debulking of granulomatous tissues, lysis of adhesions, bronchoscopic dilatations with or without laser vaporization, and tracheostomy. The patients were seen every 2–3 months for 1–5 years according to the compliance of the patients.

All patients signed informed consents to participate in the study, and the work was approved by the local Committee of Medical Ethics (Project Number: 64-6-05).

The study included 52 patients with a biopsy confirming scleroma diagnosis. The demographic data of the patients are shown in Table 1. The patients were 23 (44.2%) males and 29 (55.8%) females. Their ages ranged from 15 to 64 years (mean = 35.8 ± 6.9 years) with the highest incidence in the third and fourth decades of life. Thirty-eight patients (73.1%) came from rural areas, and 32 patients (61.6%) were farmers or manual workers. Thirty-two (61.6%) patients were illiterate, and only 2 patients (3.8%) completed an education above secondary school.

The nose was affected in all patients (Table 2), and in 17 patients (32.6%), the disease affected more than one site beside the nose. Thirty-one patients (59.7%) were diagnosed in the granulomatous stage (Fig. 1). A combination of granulomata and atrophic nasal mucosa was identified in 17 patients (32.6%). Extensive fibrosis with stenosis or obliteration of the nasal cavity and external nasal collapse was seen in 4 patients (7.9%) (Fig. 2).

The second most common site affected was the nasopharynx which showed granulomata and/or crusts and fibrosis in 23 patients (44.2%). The soft palate was affected in 9 cases (17.3%), 3 of which exhibited the typical uvular sign of a fibrotic tilt [14].

The larynx was affected in 8 patients (15.4%), and the trachea was affected in 18 patients (34.6%). Laryngeal involvement was mainly subglottic with narrowing of the subglottic airway. The trachea usually showed a mixture of granulomatous lesions and atrophic mucosa with crusting. Concentric stenosis of the trachea was seen in 11 patients (21.1%).

In a patient with an atypical presentation, a 42-year-old female, the granulomata involved the nose, lacrimal sacs, and skin and soft tissues of the face (Fig. 3). The histopathology of the lesions is shown in Fig. 4. In another atypical case, the palate and gums were infiltrated by granulomata causing mobility of the teeth. In this patient, cryosurgery was used to freeze and debulk the lesions (Fig. 5).

Diagnosis was based on clinical and histopathologic data. Histopathologic confirmation of the disease was obtained in all cases; however, in 19 patients (36.5%), multiple biopsies were needed to retrieve Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis bacilli. In the cases evaluated by electron microscopic examination, the results were specific and demonstrated the classic findings of bacilli within large, foamy Mikulicz cells (Fig. 6).

Different surgical procedures were used according to the site and type of lesion. A significant limitation in our study was that many patients were non-compliant with visits or lost to follow up. This led to inaccuracies in assessment of antibiotic efficacy. Moreover, difficult isolation of the organism made confirmation of true cure uncertain in some cases. Clinical improvement was observed in 12 patients (23.1%) after 12 weeks of antibiotic treatment and in 22 patients (42.3%) after 24 weeks of treatment. The remaining patients did not continue medical treatment or were lost to follow up. Reappearance or relapse of the granulomatous lesions after initial improvement was observed in 11 patients (21.2%) within 1 to 3 years. Bacterial eradication was not confirmed in any of these patients.

Scleroma, previously termed rhinoscleroma, was first described by Hebra and Kohn in 1870 as a neoplastic growth. In 1872, Gerber first suggested the inflammatory nature of scleroma, and, in 1877, Mikulicz described the typical foamy cells identified in biopsy specimens. Von Frisch isolated the bacilli Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis in 1882 [15]. The name scleroma was suggested by the International Congress of Otolaryngology in 1932 to highlight the fact that the disease could also affect extra-nasal sites [14].

Scleroma has several clinical presentations: granulomatous, atrophic, or fibrotic. The most common is granulomatous, but patients may show a combination of presentations. In the atrophic form, the atrophied nasal mucosa lends a widened appearance to the nasal cavity (secondary atrophic rhinitis). In the fibrotic form, the nasal cavity is stenotic or completely obliterated by dense fibrous tissues.

The histopathology of scleroma is well described in the literature; however, few studies explore the ultrastructural features [16–18]. In the present study, we document usual and unusual presentations of scleroma and present the diagnostic histopathologic and electron microscopic features of this disease.

Scleroma may mimic inflammatory and neoplastic processes including, but not limited to, sarcoidosis, rhinosporidosis, chronic fungal infections, lethal midline granuloma, histiocytosis X, Wegener granulomatosis, and lymphoma. The diagnosis, in many cases, is difficult, and histopathologic confirmation is necessary.

The present findings demonstrate almost equal case distribution between the sexes with a slight female predilection. Most patients affected with the disease were of lower socioeconomic status and had poor nutrition. The majority of patients (67.3%) were between 25 and 50 years of age. These findings are congruent with other reports [3].

The nose was affected in all cases. In a review of the literature, Miller et al. found that the initial symptom in 94% of cases was nasal obstruction followed by nasal deformity in 32% of patients [19]. In the present series, bilateral, hard, deforming granulomatous lesions were the most common nasal presentation (57.9%). Fibrotic lesions, identified in 7.9% of patients, represented end stage disease. A combination of atrophic and granulomatous lesions was found in 32.6% of patients.

According to the literature, the soft palate is much more frequently affected than the hard palate in scleroma patients [20]. In our study, the soft palate was affected in 9 cases (17.3%), 3 of them showed the typical uvula sign of forward tilting and fibrosis [15]. The hard palate was infiltrated by granulomatous masses in 2 patients (3.4%). In all cases of palatal involvement, the upper lip was also affected by the granulomatous lesions. We did not have any cases involving the tonsils in our series.

The reported incidence of laryngeal involvement in scleroma varies from 10 to 80% [21–24]. This wide range is likely due to variability in diagnostic methods and/or quality of the health care system. In the present series, the trachea was affected more frequently than the larynx. This is in agreement with the report by Gafaar et al. describing laryngotracheal involvement in scleroma [3]. Ten patients (19.2%) needed tracheostomy to relieve airway obstruction.

Involvement of the nasolacrimal duct and sac was described by Badrawy in 1962, and he termed the presentation dacryoscleroma [25]. We did not find other reported cases of dacryoscleroma since that time. In the present series, we describe a case of extensive bilateral dacryoscleroma. The granulomatous process extended along the nasolacrimal duct towards the nasolacrimal sac. The lacrimal glands were not involved by the disease.

A main limitation of this study is incomplete follow-up data. Assessing the results of treatment was impossible in some cases due to non-compliance with clinic visits. Most patients needed 3 to 6 months of antibiotic treatment in order to achieve clinical or bacterial cure. Persistence of disease despite proper treatment is not uncommon and may be seen 30 to 40% of cases [11]. Notably, bacterial cure, as assessed by culture, was not achieved in all patients with persistent disease in this series. To our knowledge, this may be the first research that objectively demonstrated, by bacterial culture, the importance of achieving complete cure in order to avoid relapse of the granulomatous lesions.

Although scleroma was first described almost 150 years ago, many of the mechanisms of infection are yet unknown and the treatment is still challenging. The disease is endemic in crowded, rural areas with low socioeconomic and nutritional status. Improving the standard of living in these areas may decrease the incidence of the disease or allow for early diagnosis in stages more amenable to treatment. Gafaar et al. called for a worldwide project to overcome the problems of scleroma [4]. We agree that this will be a step in the right direction for eradication of this disabling, obstinate disease.
