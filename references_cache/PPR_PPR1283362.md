# Long-Term Outcomes of Entecavir Therapy in Treatment-Naive Patients with Chronic Hepatitis B
**Authors:** Eroğlu E

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>Background  Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) requires long-term antiviral therapy to prevent disease progression. This study evaluated the long-term efficacy and safety of entecavir (ETV) therapy and investigated predictors of partial virological response (PVR) in treatment-naive CHB patients. Methods  This retrospective study included 230 nucleoside-naive CHB patients treated with entecavir between January 2020 and December 2024. Virological response (VR) was defined as HBV-DNA < 20 IU/mL. PVR was defined as a > 1 log10 IU/mL reduction in HBV-DNA despite detectable viral DNA after at least 12 months of therapy. Clinical, biochemical, and virological parameters were analyzed during follow-up. Results  The mean age was 44 ± 18 years, and 54.7% were male. Baseline HBV-DNA was 6.8 log10 IU/mL. VR rates reached 66% at month 3, 86% at month 6, 89% at year 1, and 100% by years 4–5. ALT normalization improved progressively, while HBeAg seroconversion was highest during years 2–3. PVR occurred in 10.2% of patients. PVR was significantly associated with higher baseline HBV-DNA levels and failure to achieve virological response VR at week 24. No serious adverse events were observed. Conclusions  ETV therapy provides effective, durable viral suppression and excellent safety in treatment-naive CHB patients. Continued treatment in patients with PVR may still achieve complete virological suppression.</p>

Abstract
Background
Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) requires long-term antiviral therapy to prevent disease progression. This study
evaluated the long-term eﬃcacy and safety of entecavir (ETV) therapy and investigated predictors of
partial virological response (PVR) in treatment-naive CHB patients.
Methods
This retrospective study included 230 nucleoside-naive CHB patients treated with entecavir between
January 2020 and December 2024. Virological response (VR) was deﬁned as HBV-DNA < 20 IU/mL. PVR
was deﬁned as a > 1 log10 IU/mL reduction in HBV-DNA despite detectable viral DNA after at least 12
months of therapy. Clinical, biochemical, and virological parameters were analyzed during follow-up.
Results
The mean age was 44 ± 18 years, and 54.7% were male. Baseline HBV-DNA was 6.8 log10 IU/mL. VR
rates reached 66% at month 3, 86% at month 6, 89% at year 1, and 100% by years 4–5. ALT normalization
improved progressively, while HBeAg seroconversion was highest during years 2–3. PVR occurred in
10.2% of patients. PVR was signiﬁcantly associated with higher baseline HBV-DNA levels and failure to
achieve virological response VR at week 24. No serious adverse events were observed.
Conclusions
ETV therapy provides effective, durable viral suppression and excellent safety in treatment-naive CHB
patients. Continued treatment in patients with PVR may still achieve complete virological suppression.
Background
The hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection continues to represent a major global public health concern. Around
400 million people are estimated to live with chronic HBV infection worldwide, which substantially
increases the risk of progressive liver disease, including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)
(1). Turkey is classiﬁed as a moderately endemic area for HBV infection, with reported hepatitis B
surface antigen (HBsAg) prevalence ranging from 2% to 8%. The national HBsAg positivity rate is
approximately 3.9%, which corresponds to nearly 3 million individuals living with HBV infection (2, 3). The
primary aim of hepatitis B (HBV) treatment is to achieve long-term suppression of viral replication and
thereby prevent progression to cirrhosis, decompensated liver disease, end-stage liver failure,
hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and other related complications. Ultimately, this approach is intended
to enhance both quality of life and overall survival. Antiviral therapy plays a key role in reducing hepatitis
Page 2/11

B virus DNA (HBV-DNA) to minimal or undetectable levels (6). Treatment initiation criteria have been
comprehensively outlined in several international guidelines. For treatment-naïve patients with chronic
HBV infection, nucleoside analogues such as tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and entecavir (ETV) are
recommended as ﬁrst-line agents because of their potent antiviral activity and low risk of resistance
development (1, 7). ETV, a potent antiviral drug endorsed by both the American Association for the Study
of Liver Diseases (AASLD) and the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), was
introduced into clinical use for chronic hepatitis B management in 2005 in the United States and
subsequently in 2007 in Turkey (7, 8). Previous studies evaluating ETV have shown that, in nucleoside-
naive patients, the very low incidence of resistance together with its potent antiviral activity makes ETV a
highly effective and widely utilized therapeutic option. In clinical trials assessing 48-week ETV therapy
for CHB, serum HBV-DNA levels have been reported to decline to undetectable levels in approximately
67% of HBeAg-positive patients and up to 90% of HBeAg-negative patients (9–11). Accordingly, this
study primarily aims to assess the virological and biochemical effectiveness of ETV therapy in
nucleoside-naive patients with chronic hepatitis B in routine clinical practice. In addition, it seeks to
determine treatment response rates and to analyze the evolution of PVR during long-term follow-up.
Methods
Study Design and Population
In the present retrospective study, consecutive patients presenting to the Infectious Diseases Clinic
between January 2020 and December 2024 were included and evaluated. “Diagnoses and treatment
responses in all CHB patients were evaluated according to the criteria outlined in the 2017 EASL
guidelines for the management of HBV infection. All patients received ETV monotherapy at a dose of 0.5
mg once daily (11). Liver biopsies were performed in accordance with AASLD recommendations (7).
Inclusion criteria for this study included patients aged 18–65 years, HBsAg positivity persisting for at
least 6 months, HBV-DNA levels exceeding 2000 IU/mL, absence of compensated or decompensated
cirrhosis, non-pregnant and non-breastfeeding status, and a minimum of 3 months of entecavir
monotherapy.
All consecutive treatment-naïve chronic hepatitis B patients who fulﬁlled the eligibility criteria and were
initiated on entecavir therapy during the study period were enrolled in the study.The sample size was
determined by the inclusion of all eligible patients during the study period. No formal sample size
calculation was performed due to the retrospective design; therefore, the ﬁnal sample size consisted of
all eligible patients available in the institutional clinical database. Of 242 patients treated with entecavir
0.5 mg daily, 12 were excluded due to treatment duration of less than 3 months (n = 9) or voluntary
discontinuation of therapy (n = 3). Consequently, 230 patients were included in the ﬁnal analysis. This
study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was
approved by the Ethics Committee of Karatay University Faculty of Medicine, Konya, Türkiye (Date:
25/12/2025, Decision No: 2025/009).

Treatment and Follow-Up
All patients received ETV monotherapy at a dose of 0.5 mg daily (Bristol-Myers Squibb, Shanghai, China).
In addition to demographic variables such as age and sex, routine laboratory and clinical assessments
were performed at baseline and at 3–6 month intervals, including: (a) Ishak scoring of pretreatment liver
biopsy specimens [histological activity index (HAI) and ﬁbrosis stage], HBsAg, HBeAg, and anti-HBe
status; (b) serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and HBV-DNA
levels measured before treatment and at months 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24; (c) time to ALT normalization and
achievement of undetectable HBV-DNA levels; (d) HBeAg seroconversion (in HBeAg-positive patients)
and total duration of follow-up during therapy; and (e) creatine kinase (CK), blood urea nitrogen, and
creatinine levels. The diagnosis of cirrhosis was established based on either histological or
ultrasonographic evidence. At each follow-up visit, patients were thoroughly evaluated and questioned
regarding possible adverse effects. Cumulative virological response (VR) was deﬁned as complete viral
suppression, corresponding to serum HBV-DNA levels below 20 IU/mL (< 100 copies/mL) at week 48. In
contrast, partial virological response (PVR) was deﬁned as detectable HBV-DNA by polymerase chain
reaction (PCR) in patients with good adherence, accompanied by a reduction in viral load of more than 1
log10 IU/mL after at least 12 months of therapy.
Serological response was deﬁned as HBeAg seroconversion, characterized by loss of HBeAg and
development of anti-HBe antibodies in HBeAg-positive patients. Biochemical response was deﬁned as
normalization of ALT levels, speciﬁcally a decrease in ALT to below the upper limit of normal in patients
with elevated baseline ALT. Patients were considered adherent to therapy if they took their medication
once daily without interruption. Regarding adverse events, any clinical sign, symptom, or laboratory
abnormality that resolved after discontinuation of the drug was regarded as treatment-related. In
addition, an increase in serum creatinine above the upper limit of normal was considered indicative of a
drug-related renal adverse effect.
Instruments
HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, and anti-HBe tests were carried out using the macro ELISA method (Architect
System, Abbott Diagnostics, Wiesbaden, Germany, and Cobas E 601, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim,
Germany). However, serum HBV-DNA levels were determined using the Bosphore HBV Quantiﬁcation
(HBV-DNA) kit, a quantitative PCR method for HBV nucleic acids, on an Anatolia Geneworks Montania
4896 Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR) device (Sultanbeyli, Istanbul, Turkiye).
Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences for Windows,
version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard
deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. HBV-DNA
levels were analyzed after logarithmic transformation. For comparisons of continuous variables,
Page 4/11

Student’s t-test was applied. Categorical variables were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test or
Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Finally, logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors
associated with partial virological response during entecavir monotherapy, and a p-value < 0.05 was
considered statistically signiﬁcant. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis, and no
imputation was performed. Patients with missing values were excluded from the relevant analyses.
Results
A total of 230 patients were treated with 0.5 mg of ETV daily. One hundred and eight cases were deﬁned
to be HBeAg positive, and 122 to be HBeAg negative. Of the study group, 54.7% (n = 126) were male
patients, and 168 patients (73.0%) had a family history of hepatitis B infection. The mean HBV-DNA level
was 6.8 log₁₀ IU/mL. The patients had a mean age of 44 years, and the median follow-up duration for the
entire cohort was 36 months (Table 1).

Discussion
Compared with other available nucleos(t)ide analogues, entecavir demonstrates superior eﬃcacy in
reducing HBV-DNA levels. Due to its potent antiviral activity, it achieves substantial viral suppression in
both HBeAg-positive and HBeAg-negative patients. Findings from pivotal registration trials have also
shown that entecavir possesses a high genetic barrier to resistance development. Consistent with these
properties, it is recommended as a ﬁrst-line therapeutic option in current HBV treatment guidelines (10,
11).
In our study, among patients treated with entecavir, cumulative VR rates increased steadily at all
assessment time points throughout the 5-year follow-up period. By the end of the second year, HBV-DNA
levels had declined to undetectable levels in more than 90% of patients. The signiﬁcant reduction in
serum HBV-DNA levels and the marked increase in virological response rates observed at weeks 12 and
24 further support the strong antiviral eﬃcacy of entecavir. Similarly, ALT levels showed an overall
improving trend at all time points during the follow-up period. The rates of HBeAg seroconversion were
20.5% in the second year and 18.0% in the third year, respectively, and are in line with previously
published data (12–14). In a multicenter study from Turkey conducted by Köklu et al., the HBeAg
loss/seroconversion rate was reported to be 23.5%, which is comparable with the results of our study
(15). The ultimate goal of CHB treatment is to achieve HBsAg clearance; however, this endpoint is
infrequently attained with currently available antiviral therapies (11). According to the literature, the rate
of HBsAg loss during long-term antiviral therapy is approximately 2% (7, 16). Consistent with these data,
Page 8/11

our study demonstrated an anti-HBs seroconversion rate of 2.9% after 5 years of follow-up. These results
indicate that functional cure is rarely achieved with current treatment strategies and that HBsAg loss
occurs only in a limited subset of patients.
In our study, various baseline and viral factors that may affect antiviral eﬃcacy were investigated. In
patients developing PVR, virological response (VR) at year 1 was signiﬁcantly associated with baseline
HBV-DNA levels and VR at month 6, whereas gender, age, body mass index, hepatitis B family history,
baseline ALT level, and VR at month 3 had no signiﬁcant impact on VR at week 48. In other words,
patients with higher baseline HBV-DNA levels and those who achieved VR at month 6 were more likely to
achieve VR within the ﬁrst year. In light of these ﬁndings, it is suggested that treatment may be optimized
in patients developing PVR by considering the duration of HBV-DNA suppression and baseline viral load
(1, 17). In previous studies evaluating treatment success rates, it has also been emphasized that both
patient and viral characteristics are critical in determining treatment outcomes (18). In this context, our
study will allow the treatment regime to be optimized on an individual basis and more personalized
approaches to be developed.
In the treatment of ETV, it has been reported that in patients with detectable HBV-DNA at week 48,
especially those with low baseline viral load, continuing monotherapy may be appropriate, as the long-
term ETV treatment provides VR in the vast majority of patients (1).
In our study, 18 patients who still had detectable HBV-DNA levels at the end of the ﬁrst year continued
receiving ETV therapy. Among nucleoside-naive patients, virological response (VR) rates during follow-up
were 83.3% (10/12) in the second year, 100% (12/12) in the third year, and 100% (6/6) in the fourth year.
These ﬁndings indicate that if the treatment is continued for more than one year, VR can ultimately be
achieved in a large proportion of patients. Although patients without cirrhosis at baseline were included,
HCC development during follow-up was observed in a small subset, highlighting the importance of long-
term surveillance even in non-cirrhotic CHB patients.
Limitations
The small number of participants in our study is one of the limitations of our study. Furthermore, the fact
that our study was conducted in a single center can also be considered another limitation. Therefore, we
consider that multicenter studies including larger numbers of participants would be beneﬁcial for
elucidating the issue.
Conclusıons
Long-term treatment with ETV at a daily dose of 0.5 mg effectively reduces HBV-DNA levels to
undetectable levels over a 5-year period. In treatment-naïve patients who develop PVR after the ﬁrst year,
continuation of monotherapy may remain appropriate in the absence of signiﬁcant adverse effects,
considering the low long-term risk of serious side effects associated with entecavir. After 1 year of
Page 9/11

treatment, VR is achieved in the vast majority of patients. Furthermore, ETV is considered a safe and
effective antiviral agent for long-term use.
