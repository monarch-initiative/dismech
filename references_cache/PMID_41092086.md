# CBTRUS Statistical Report: Primary Brain and Other Central Nervous System Tumors Diagnosed in the United States in 2018-2022.
**Authors:** Price M, Ballard CAP, Benedetti JR, Kruchko C, Barnholtz-Sloan JS, Ostrom QT
**Journal:** Neuro Oncol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1093/neuonc/noaf194](https://doi.org/10.1093/neuonc/noaf194)

## Content

The Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States (CBTRUS), an aggregation of data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's National Program of Cancer Registries and the National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results program, is the largest population-based registry focused exclusively on primary brain and other central nervous system (CNS) tumors in the United States (US). As of this report, the CBTRUS database represents the entire US population. This report contains the most up-to-date population-based data on primary brain tumors and supersedes all previous reports in terms of completeness and accuracy. All rates are age-adjusted using the 2000 US standard population and presented per 100,000 population. Between 2018 and 2022, the average annual age-adjusted incidence rate (AAAIR) of all primary malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors was 26.05 per 100,000 population (malignant AAAIR=6.86 and non-malignant AAAIR=19.19). Incidence was higher in women compared to men (29.67 versus 22.23 per 100,000) and in non-Hispanic Black individuals compared to non-Hispanic White (27.40 versus 26.36 per 100,000), non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native (24.38 per 100,000), non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander (20.42 per 100,000), and Hispanic individuals of all races (24.69 per 100,000). Gliomas accounted for 22.2% of all tumors. The most commonly occurring malignant brain and other CNS histopathology was glioblastoma (13.7% of all tumors and 52.2% of all malignant tumors), and the most common non-malignant histopathology was meningioma (42.6% of all tumors [includes malignant meningioma] and 57.4% of all non-malignant tumors). Glioblastoma was more common in men, and meningioma was more common in women. In children and adolescents (ages 0-19 years), the incidence rate of all primary brain and other CNS tumors was 5.99 per 100,000 population. Overall, there were 88,186 total deaths attributed to  malignant brain and other CNS tumors between 2018 and 2022, representing an average of 17,637 deaths per year and an average annual mortality rate of 4.41 per 100,000 population. The five-year relative survival rate following diagnosis of a malignant brain or other CNS tumor was 34.8%, while it was 91.7% for a non-malignant brain or other CNS tumor.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States (CBTRUS) database, developed in collaboration with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the United States’ (US) National Institutes of Health, is the largest population-based registry focused exclusively on primary brain and other central nervous system (CNS) tumors in the United States. As such, CBTRUS represents the entire US population. As of January 2025, CBTRUS is an organization affiliated with the Society for Neuro-Oncology.The CBTRUS Statistical Report: Primary Brain and Other Central Nervous System Tumors Diagnosed in the United States in 2018−2022 contains the most up-to-date population-based data on all primary brain tumors available through the US surveillance system and supersedes all previous reports in terms of completeness and accuracy. All rates are age-adjusted using the 2000 US standard population and presented per 100,000 population. Accordingly, this report represents a current, comprehensive source for the descriptive epidemiology of primary central nervous system tumors.
Incidence: Newly Diagnosed Primary Brain and Other CNS Tumors, 2018−2022
Between 2018 and 2022, the average annual age-adjusted incidence rate (AAAIR) of all malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors was 26.05 per 100,000 population. The AAAIR of malignant brain and other CNS tumors only was 6.86 per 100,000 population versus 19.19 per 100,000 population for non-malignant tumors only.
The overall incidence rate was higher in females compared to males (29.67 vs. 22.23 per 100,000, respectively). Primary malignant tumors were more frequent in males than females (8.02 vs. 5.82 per 100,000, respectively).
Incidence was slightly higher among individuals who were non-Hispanic Black (27.40 per 100,000) compared with individuals who were non-Hispanic White (26.36 per 100,000), non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native (24.38 per 100,000), non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander (20.42 per 100,000), or Hispanic individuals of all races (24.69 per 100,000).
Approximately 26.3% of all brain and other CNS tumors were malignant and 73.7% were non-malignant, making non-malignant tumors more than twice as common as malignant tumors.
Gliomas accounted for 22.2% of all tumors. The most commonly occurring malignant brain and other CNS tumor histopathology was glioblastoma (13.7% of all tumors and 52.2% of all malignant tumors).
The most common non-malignant histopathology was meningioma (42.6% of all tumors [includes malignant meningioma] and 57.4% of all non-malignant tumors). Meningioma was more common in females than males.
In children and adolescents (ages 0-19 years), the AAAIR of malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors was 5.99 per 100,000 population.
An estimated 107,100 new cases of malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors are expected to be diagnosed in the United States in 2025. This includes an expected 26,480 malignant and 80,610 non-malignant tumors.
There are an estimated 13,930 new cases of glioblastoma expected to be diagnosed in the United States in 2025.
Time Trends in Primary Brain and Other CNS Tumors Incidence, 2004−2022
There was a slight but statistically significant decrease in overall incidence of malignant primary brain tumors from 2004-2017.
Overall, non-malignant brain tumors had a statistically significant increase in incidence from 2004-2008 and from 2008-2018, followed by a non-significant slight decrease in incidence from 2018-2022.
Mortality: Deaths Due to Primary Brain and Other CNS Tumors, 2018−2022
There were 88,186 deaths attributed to malignant brain and other CNS tumors. This represents an average annual mortality rate of 4.41 per 100,000 population and an average of 17,637 deaths per year (approximately 48 deaths per day).
Among children ages 0-14 years, brain and other CNS cancer was the most common cause of cancer death and the second most significant contributor to cancer death in adolescents and young adults ages 15-39 years.
Survival: From Diagnosis to One-, Five-, and Ten-Years Beyond, 2004−2021
The five-year relative survival rate following diagnosis of a primary malignant brain and other CNS tumor was 34.8%. Among malignant brain and other CNS tumors, the five-year relative survival rate was highest in individuals ages 0-14 years (75.7%) and ages 15-39 years (72.4%) as compared to those ages 40+ years (20.3%).
Among primary non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors, the five-year relative survival rate was 91.7%. Survival following diagnosis with a non-malignant brain and other CNS tumor was highest in individuals ages 15-39 years (98.3%) and ages 0-14 years (97.5%) as compared to those ages 40+ years (90.3%).
In 2018, a total of 379,695 years of expected life were lost due to early death attributed to brain and other CNS tumors. The majority of these deaths (96%) were due to malignant tumors.
INTRODUCTION
The objective of the CBTRUS Statistical Report: Primary Brain and Other Central Nervous System Tumors Diagnosed in the United States in 2018−2022 is to provide a comprehensive summary of the current descriptive epidemiology of all primary brain and other central nervous system (CNS) tumors in the US population. Primary brain and other CNS tumors include those tumors that originate from the tissues of the brain or other CNS sites. CBTRUS obtained the latest available population-based data on all newly diagnosed primary brain and other CNS tumors reported from the CDC’s National Program of Cancer Registries (NPCR), and the NCI’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program for diagnosis years 2018−2022. Incidence counts and rates of primary malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors are presented by histopathology, sex, age at diagnosis, race-Hispanic ethnicity, and geographic location. Mortality rates for malignant brain and comparison cancers were calculated using the National Center for Health Statistics’ (NCHS) National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) data from 2018−2022. Also presented are relative survival rates, median survival, and adjusted hazard ratios for selected malignant and non-malignant histopathologies calculated using NPCR data for the period 2004-2021.
BACKGROUND
CBTRUS is a unique professional research organization that focuses exclusively on providing high-quality statistical data on the population-based incidence of primary brain and other CNS tumors in the United States (for more information on CBTRUS see: http://www.cbtrus.org/about/ and Kruchko, et al.1,2). CBTRUS was incorporated as a nonprofit 501(c)(3) in 1992 following a study conducted by the American Brain Tumor Association to determine the feasibility of a population-based central registry focused on all reported primary brain and other CNS tumors in the United States. As of January 1, 2025, the Society for Neuro-Oncology (SNO) and the Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States (CBTRUS) entered into a formal strategic affiliation designed to enhance both organizations’ efforts in brain tumor research, education, and collaboration. This affiliation will foster an integrated strategy to improve efficiency, amplify shared resources, and further solidify CBTRUS as the premier resource for annual histology-specific statistical information for all primary brain and other CNS tumors in the United States.
This report represents the thirty-third (33 rd ) anniversary of CBTRUS and the twenty-eighth (28 th ) statistical report published by CBTRUS. For this fourteenth (14th) report published as a Supplement to Neuro-Oncology, the official journal of the Society for Neuro-Oncology (http://www.soc-neuro-onc.org), CBTRUS continues its past efforts to provide the most up-to-date population-based incidence rates for all reported newly-diagnosed primary brain and other CNS tumors by behavior (malignant and non-malignant), histopathology, age at diagnosis, sex, race-Hispanic ethnicity, selected brain molecular markers (BMM), and geographic location. These data have been organized by clinically relevant histopathology groupings that reflect the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumours of the Central Nervous System, including selected molecularly-defined histopathologies beginning in diagnosis year 2018.3,4 These data provide important information for allocation and planning of specialty healthcare services such as clinical trials, research activities, and disease prevention and control programs. These data may also stimulate research into the causes of this group of diseases, which often result in significant morbidity and mortality.
CBTRUS is currently the only population-based site-specific registry in the United States that works in partnership with a public cancer surveillance organization, the CDC’s NPCR5, and from which data are directly received through the NPCR–Cancer Surveillance System 6 under a special agreement. Collection of central (state) cancer data was mandated in 1992 by Public Law 102-515, the Cancer Registries Amendment Act.7 This mandate was expanded to include non-malignant tumors with the 2002 passage of Public Law 107–260, starting January 1, 2004.8 CBTRUS combines the NPCR data with data from the NCI’s SEER Program,9 which was established for national cancer surveillance in the early 1970s. All data from NPCR and SEER originate from oncology data specialists (tumor registrars) who adhere to the Uniform Data Standards (UDS) for malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors as directed by the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries (NAACCR) (http://www.naaccr.org). Along with the UDS, there are quality control checks and a system for rating each central cancer registry (CCR) to ensure that these data are as accurate and complete as possible. As a surveillance partner, CBTRUS reports high-quality data on brain and other CNS tumors with histopathological specificity useful to the communities it serves.
The CBTRUS database is comprised of the largest histopathology-specific aggregation of population-based data limited to the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and survival of primary brain and other CNS tumors in the United States, and it is likely the largest histopathology-specific aggregation of primary brain and other CNS tumor cases in the world. The CBTRUS database now includes survival data with information on biomarkers found in 2016 WHO Classification from 43 CCRs and incidence data from all 52 CCRs in the United States and Puerto Rico, which is the first presentation of these data from a population-based dataset. Aggregate information on all cancers from all 52 CCRs in the United States, including primary brain and other CNS tumors, is available from the NPCR’s United States Cancer Statistics (USCS).10
Risk Factors for Primary Brain and Other CNS Tumors
Many environmental, behavioral, and genetic risk factors have been explored as causative agents for primary brain and other CNS tumors. However, few have been proven to be associated with impacting a person’s risk of developing a tumor. Exposure to ionizing radiation (the type of radiation generated by atomic bombs, therapeutic radiation treatment, and some forms of medical imaging) has been shown to increase a person’s risk of developing a CNS tumor (particularly meningioma). Also, having a first-degree family member (including parents, children, and full siblings) diagnosed with a brain tumor has been shown to increase risk approximately two-fold. However, having a history of allergies or other atopic diseases (such as eczema, psoriasis, and asthma) reduces a person’s risk of developing a CNS tumor (particularly glioma). Several recent review articles have elaborated on the current state of risk factor research in primary brain and other CNS tumors.11-14
Data Collection and classification
CBTRUS does not collect data directly from patients’ medical records. Registration of individual cases (tumors) is conducted by oncology data specialists at the institution where diagnosis and/or treatment occur and is then transmitted to the CCR, which further transmits this information to NPCR, SEER, and/or NAACCR. Some CCRs also send their data to SEER; data from those CCRs are taken from the NPCR file to eliminate duplicate cases. As noted, data for CBTRUS analyses come from the NPCR and SEER programs. By law, all primary malignant and non-malignant CNS tumors are reportable diseases based on their site, and CCRs play an essential role in the collection process. Brain and other CNS tumors are reported using the site definition described in Public Law 107-260.8 These data are population-based and represent a comprehensive documentation of all reported cancers diagnosed within a geographic region for the years included in this report.
CBTRUS obtained de-identified incidence data from 52 CCRs (48 NPCR and 4 SEER) that include cases of malignant and non-malignant (benign and uncertain behaviors) primary brain and other CNS tumors. The population-based CCRs include 50 state registries, plus the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico (Figure 1). See the Supplementary Data Note for a complete list of registries.

NPCR provided data on 487,495 primary brain and other CNS tumors diagnosed from 2018 to 2022 (Supplementary Figure 1). An additional 14,490 case records for the period were obtained from SEER for primary brain and other CNS tumor case records from 2018 to 2022 for Connecticut, Hawaii, Iowa, and New Mexico only. These data were combined into a single dataset of 501,985 records for quality control. The final analytic dataset had 491,804 records, which included 489,718 records from the 50 state CCRs and the District of Columbia used in the analytic dataset, and an additional 2,086 records from Puerto Rico. A total of 10,181 records (2.0%) were deleted from the final analytic dataset for one or more of the following reasons (Supplementary Figure 1):
Records with ICD-O-3 behavior code of /2 (indicates in situ cases, which is not a relevant classification for brain and other CNS tumors).
Records with an invalid site/histopathology combination according to the CBTRUS histopathology grouping scheme.
Possible duplicate records that included a less accurate reporting source than microscopic confirmation, also referred to as histopathologic confirmation (e.g., radiographic versus microscopic confirmation), possible duplicate record for recurrent disease, or errors in time sequence of diagnosis.
Possible duplicate records for bilateral vestibular schwannoma or meningioma that were merged to one paired-site record.
Records from Puerto Rico are included only in a supplementary analysis (See Supplemental Material), and these cases are not included in the overall statistics presented in this report.
For the purpose of comparison with brain and other CNS tumor incidence rates, age-adjusted incidence rates per 100,000 population for the entire United States for selected other cancers were obtained from the USCS produced jointly by the CDC and the NCI.10 This database includes both NPCR and SEER data, and together the programs represent the entire US population.
De-identified survival data for all primary brain and other CNS tumors were obtained from NPCR15 for 43 CCRs (see Supplementary Data Note for list of included CCRs) for the years 2004 to 2021. This dataset provides population-based information for 87.5% of the US population for the years 2004 to 2021 and is a subset of the data used for the incidence calculations presented in this report. Survival information is derived from both active and passive follow-up.
Mortality data used in this report are from the NVSS16, which includes death certification data for individuals from all 50 states and the District of Columbia and represents the entire US population. NVSS data are not collected through the cancer registration system. These data represent the primary cause of death listed on each death certificate and, as a result, deaths in individuals with cancer may be recorded as non-cancer deaths. NVSS data are limited to deaths in which a primary malignant brain and other CNS tumor was listed as the primary cause of death on the death certificate. Comparison data on malignant brain and other CNS tumors and other comparison cancers were obtained using SEER*Stat.17
Anatomic Location of Tumor Sites
Various terms are used to describe the regions of the brain and other CNS. The specific sites used in this report are based on the topography codes found in the ICD-O-3. CBTRUS groups ICD-O-3 sites C71.8 (Overlapping lesion of the brain) and C71.9 (Brain, Not Otherwise Specified, NOS) into “Other brain” and C72.8 (Overlapping lesion of brain and CNS) and C72.9 (Nervous system, NOS) into ‘Other nervous system’ for display in figures. This report also presents counts and incidence for specific sites separately in its tables. See Table 1 for the CBTRUS primary site groupings. See Supplementary Methods for updates in cancer registry collection and reporting of sites.
Classification by Histopathology and Behavior
There are over 100 distinct types of primary brain and other CNS tumors, referred to as ‘histopathologies’, each with its spectrum of clinical presentations, treatments, and outcomes. These histopathologies can be broadly classified as non-malignant (ICD-O-3 behavior codes of /0 for benign and /1 for uncertain) or malignant (ICD-O-3 behavior code of /3) and are reviewed and updated periodically by expert neuropathologists. The World Health Organization (WHO) publishes classification updates for all cancer sites and utilizes the ICD-O-3 for assignment of histopathology, behavior, and site codes. CBTRUS is using histopathology groupings according to 2016 WHO Classification of Tumours of the Central Nervous System. See the Supplementary Methods for differences in classification and reporting of brain tumors.
The ICD-O-3 codes in this current CBTRUS grouping18 (Table 2) may include morphology codes that were not previously reported to CBTRUS.19 Gliomas arise from glial or precursor cells and include glioblastoma, astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, ependymoma, oligoastrocytoma (mixed glioma), and a few rare histopathologies. As there is no standard definition for gliomas, CBTRUS defines gliomas as ICD-O-3 histopathology codes 9380-9384 and 9391-9460, as starred in Table 2. It is also important to note that the statistics for lymphomas and hematopoietic neoplasms contained in this report refer only to those lymphomas and hematopoietic neoplasms that arise in the brain and other CNS ICD-O-3 topography codes (Table 1).

Classification by Brain Molecular Markers (BMM)
Primary brain and other CNS tumors are a highly heterogeneous group of diseases, and characterization of unique tumor histopathologies within this group has been refined over time. The development of technologies for characterizing DNA sequence, RNA abundance as a measure of gene activity, and biochemical alterations that affect gene expression (such as DNA methylation) have led to the discovery of several factors known as ‘biomarkers.’ Biomarkers can be used to more accurately classify these tumors than microscopic appearance alone. With the increased recognition of the value of biomarkers for specific brain tumor histopathologies in classification, the WHO Classification of Tumours of the Central Nervous System included biomarkers in its 2016 revision. However, implementing the collection of these markers in cancer registration is multi-faceted and includes an ongoing educational and training component.
As of 2011, SEER registries began collecting information on three validated biomarkers for primary brain and other CNS tumors as Site-Specific Factors (SSF): promoter methylation status of O-6-Methylguanine-DNA Methyltransferase (MGMT), deletion of 1p, and deletion of 19q.22 Starting with diagnosis year 2018, the broad US cancer registry system began collecting information on multiple brain and other CNS markers, including isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 (IDH1/2) mutation, 1p/19q codeletion, medulloblastoma molecular subtypes, and all biomarkers found in 2016 WHO classification using the variable Brain Molecular Markers Site-Specific Data Item (please see Supplementary Table 1). Additional molecularly-defined histopathologies from 2016 WHO were added using their new ICD-O-3 codes for which collection also began in 2018. These data were available to CBTRUS for the first time with the 2021 NPCR and SEER data releases, and survival data were available for cases diagnosed in 2019 through the NPCR for the first time in this year’s (2025) CBTRUS Report. As such, these data are for the 2018-2022 (2019-2021 for survival data) diagnosis years only. CBTRUS evaluated the completeness of these markers in their first year (2018) of collection (please see Iorgulescu, et al.23).
The collection of new molecularly defined histopathologies, introduced in the 2021 WHO classification, began with diagnosis year starting January 1, 2024 for the first time and will be available for analysis by CBTRUS in reporting year 2027.

STATISTICAL METHODS
The CBTRUS Statistical Report presents the following population-based measures: incidence rates, mortality rates, observed survival (median survival time and hazard ratios), expected cases, and relative survival rates. See the Supplemental Methods for more information on definitions of terms and measures in surveillance epidemiology used.
Variable Definitions
Age is reported using age groups by years including all ages, 0-19, 20+ years and four age groups (0-14, 15-39, 40-64, 65+ years, or 40+ years). Hispanic ethnicity was defined using the NAACCR Hispanic Identification Algorithm, version 2, data element, which uses a combination of cancer registry data fields (Spanish/Hispanic Origin data element, birthplace, race, and surnames) to directly and indirectly classify cases as Hispanic or non-Hispanic. Race and Hispanic ethnicity were combined and reported as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native, non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander, and Hispanic (all races) (hereafter “Hispanic”). Other race, unspecified, and unknown race or ethnicity are included in summary measures that are not race/ethnicity-specific.
Estimation of Incidence Rates, Incidence Rate Ratios, and Time Trends
AAAIR, incidence rate ratios (IRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using the CBTRUS analytic dataset24 per 100,000 population for years 2018−2022, were based on five-year age groups and were standardized to the 2000 US standard population for consistency with other US reporting agencies where this population is the standard used for age-adjustment.25 Age-adjusted incidence rates used formulas described by Tiwari et al to calculate CI.26 Population data for each geographic region were obtained from the SEER program website.27 IRRs used formulas described by Fay et al. to calculate p-values, which were considered statistically significantly different when the p-value was less than 0.05.28.
For comparison cancers, AAAIR were estimated using the same approach from the USCS data from 2018−2022.29 Sites were classified using the SEER site recode found at (https://seer.cancer.gov/siterecode/icdo3_dwhoheme/index.html).
Joinpoint 5.4.030,31 was used to estimate incidence time trends and generate annual percentage changes (APC) and 95% CI for diagnosis years 2004−2022 excluding 2020 as an outlier (see the impact of COVID-19 statement below). Modified Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) procedures included in Joinpoint were used to select the best-fitting model.
Estimation of Expected Numbers of Brain and Other CNS Tumors in 2025 and 2026
Estimated numbers of expected primary malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors were calculated for 2025 and 2026. To project estimates of newly diagnosed brain and other CNS tumors, age-adjusted annual brain tumor case counts were generated for malignant (diagnosis years 2000−2022) and non-malignant (diagnosis years 2006−2022) tumors. These were generated by histopathologic type. Joinpoint 5.4.0 was used to fit regression models to these case counts which were used to predict numbers of cases in future years using the parameter from the selected models. Modified BIC procedures included in Joinpoint were used to select the best fitting model. The overall totals presented are rounded to the nearest ten and based on total malignant and non-malignant incidence. Presented stratified counts may not add up to these totals due to rounding. Estimated numbers of cases are highly dependent on input data. Different patterns of incidence within strata can significantly affect the projected estimates, especially when the number of cases within a stratum is low. As these estimates are based on 16-22 years of observed data, projected totals may not be equal to average annual case estimates from the last five years of data. Caution should be used when utilizing these estimates.
Estimation of Mortality Rates, Relative Survival Rates, Overall Survival, and Adjusted Hazard Ratios
Average annual age-adjusted mortality rates (AAAMR) for deaths resulting from all primary malignant brain and other CNS tumors were calculated using the age at death in the mortality data available in SEER*Stat Online Database provided by NCHS from death certificates per 100,000 population. These data were available for 50 states and the District of Columbia only. In addition to the total age-adjusted mortality rate for the United States, age-adjusted mortality rates are presented by sex and state.
The one-, five-, and ten-year relative survival (RS) rates for primary malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumor cases diagnosed between 2004−2021 in 43 NPCR CCRs were estimated using SEER*Stat. Median survival time for all reported primary malignant brain and other CNS tumors diagnosed between 2004−2021 in 43 NPCR CCRs was calculated by histopathology as well as by three age at diagnosis groups (0-14 years old, 15-39 years old, and 40+ years old) using the Kaplan-Meier method. Second or later primary tumors, cases diagnosed at autopsy, cases in which either race or sex is coded as other or unknown, and cases known to be alive but for whom follow-up time could not be calculated, were excluded from survival data analyses.
Cox proportional hazard models were used to test associations between demographic factors and overall survival by histopathology for malignant brain and other CNS tumors diagnosed in 2004−2021. All models were adjusted for age at diagnosis group (0-14 years [reference group], 15-39 years, 40+ years), sex (male [reference group], female), and race-Hispanic ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White [reference group], non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native, non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander, and Hispanic all races). These models were used to estimate hazard ratios for each group with corresponding 95% CI and p-values. Adjusted estimates included all covariates (age at diagnosis, sex, and race-ethnicity) a priori, regardless of individual significance level. The proportional hazards assumption was tested separately by histopathology, and residuals were examined for all variables.
Statistical Software
Counts, means, medians, rates, ratios, proportions, and other relevant statistics were calculated using R 4.5.0 and/or SEER*Stat 8.5.0.2 Please see the Supplemental Methods for additional information on R packages used.
Suppression of Displayed Values
Rates were suppressed when counts were fewer than 16 within a cell, or when mortality and survival counts were fewer than 50 within a cell. Suppressed counts and rates were included in totals, except when data were suppressed from only one cell to prevent identification of the number in the suppressed cell. NOTE: Reported percentages may not add up to 100% due to rounding.
Impact of the COVID-19 Pandemic on 2020 Cancer Registry Data
Health care disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic have significantly affected cancer incidence data through delays in diagnosis, as well as new case abstraction.
The 2020 data are included in all calculated incidence, mortality, and survival rates. The 2020 data were excluded from time trends analysis as an outlier. See the Supplemental Methods for more information on how the pandemic impacted cancer incidence rates.
All trends and projections analyses are performed using data collected through diagnosis year 2022 but exclude 2020 as an outlier year. Please see the statement from the NCI’s SEER program on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, including a recorded webinar, at https://seer.cancer.gov/data/covid-impact.html.

The IDH1/2-mutant astrocytoma subtype had an incidence rate of 0.47 per 100,000, while IDH1/2-wildtype astrocytoma subtype had an incidence rate of 2.74 per 100,000. Median age of diagnosis for these subtypes was 38 and 65 years, respectively.
When stratified by WHO grade, 55.3% of WHO grade 2 astrocytoma were IDH1/2-mutant, while 50.7% and 2.3% of WHO grade 3 and 4 astrocytoma were IDH1/2-mutant (Figure 4).
The most common medulloblastoma subtypes were Sonic Hedgehog (SHH)-activated & TP53-wildtype (included in the SHH group) and non-wingless (WNT)/non-SHH medulloblastoma (including both group 3/group C and group 4/group D), which both had incidence rates of 0.03 per 100,000, and a median age of diagnosis of 20 and 8 years, respectively.

The SHH-activated, and TP53-mutant medulloblastoma subtype (included in the SHH group) was too rare to calculate incidence.
In children and AYA, the most common medulloblastoma subtype was SHH-activated and TP53-wildtype (19.6% and 30.9%, respectively; Figure 5).
In adults ages 40+ years, the most common medulloblastoma subtype was non-WNT/non-SHH (23.1%).
Molecular subtype data were missing for many medulloblastoma cases (Figure 5).
Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27M-mutant had incidence rates of 0.08 per 100,000 and a median age of diagnosis of 16 years.

Time Trends in Primary Brain and Other CNS Tumors
The COVID-19 pandemic caused instability in cancer incidence due to health care disruption, so CBTRUS does not include incidence data from 2020 in time trends analyses.
Incidence rates of cancer overall and many specific cancer histopathologies have decreased over time. Overall, changes in incidence rates of all primary brain and other CNS tumors between 2004 and 2022 have been small. There are many factors that affect reported incidence rates over time that are not related to ‘true’ changes in incidence of these tumors, such as demographic changes, changes in histopathology classification, and changes in cancer registration practices. The latter is especially applicable to the collection of non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors.
For an overview of the overall incidence time trends by histopathology, sex, race, and Hispanic ethnicity from 2004−2019 see the 2023 CBTRUS Statistical Report.32

Overall, the most common tumor site was the meninges, representing 42.9% of all tumors.

Distribution and Incidence Rates by Major Histopathology Grouping, Specific Histopathology, and Behavior
The distribution by brain and other CNS histopathologies is shown in Figure 8B and Table 5. AAAIRs overall by major histopathology grouping (Table 2), specific histopathology, and behavior are shown in Table 5 and Supplementary Figure 3.
Meningioma (42.6%) was the most frequently reported histopathology overall, followed by tumors of the pituitary (17.4%) and glioblastoma (13.7%).
Tumors of the pituitary and nerve sheath tumors combined accounted for slightly less than one-fourth of all tumors (25.4%), the vast majority of which were non-malignant.
Among CBTRUS major histopathology groupings, incidence rates were highest for tumors of the meninges (10.99 per 100,000), followed by tumors of the sellar region (5.00 per 100,000), diffuse astrocytic and oligodendroglial tumors (4.37 per 100,000), and tumors of the cranial and spinal nerves (2.08 per 100,000).
Among specific histopathologies, incidence rates were highest for meningioma (10.66 per 100,000), tumors of the pituitary (4.81 per 100,000, 99.5% non-malignant), glioblastoma (3.28 per 100,000), and nerve sheath tumors (2.08 per 100,000). Of all vestibular schwannoma tumors, 49.7% occurred in individuals ages 40-64 years.
After glioblastoma, the most frequently occurring malignant histopathologies were glioma malignant, NOS (0.58 per 100,000), diffuse astrocytoma (0.47 per 100,000), and lymphomas (0.43 per 100,000) (Table 5).

There was large variation in survival estimates for all ages depending upon tumor histopathology; five-year survival rates were 99.2% for nerve sheath tumors and 7.0% for glioblastoma.
Survival generally decreased with older age at diagnosis; children and young adults generally had better survival outcomes for most histopathologies.
Among predominantly non-malignant histopathologies, five-year survival was lowest for neoplasm unspecified, craniopharyngioma, and meningioma, which had five-year relative survival of 60.4%, 85.9%, and 88.5%, respectively.
Among predominantly non-malignant histopathologies, five-year survival was highest for nerve sheath tumors, which had five-year relative survival of 99.3%.
Although one-year relative survival for most tumor types was higher for AYA than children, five- and ten-year survival were usually higher for children as compared to AYA.

Strengths and Limitations of Cancer Registry Data
The CBTRUS, developed in collaboration with the CDC and NCI, is the largest population-based registry focused exclusively on primary brain and other CNS tumors in the United States and represents cases collected from the entire United States population. This report contains the most up-to-date population-based data on all primary brain and other CNS tumors available through the cancer surveillance system in the United States.
Registration of individual cases is conducted by oncology data specialists, formerly referred to as cancer registrars, at the institution where diagnosis (assignment of a specific histopathology) or treatment occurs and is then transmitted to the CCR, which further transmits this information to NPCR and/or SEER. Each CCR reports cases only for individuals who are residents of that particular state or jurisdiction, so duplicate records should not occur for individuals who may have traveled across state lines for treatment. As a result, the CBTRUS dataset is a complete recording of all cases submitted to CCR for the time period examined here (2018−2022), with minimal duplication.
The cancer registry system has no publicly available survival and outcomes data. Survival data used for this report are collected by NPCR for 43 of the 51 CCRs in the United States—primarily through linkage with death certificate and other administrative records—and by SEER for the remaining CCRs—through active and passive methods—and the feasibility of these data for use in survival studies has been evaluated38,39 and shown to produce reliable and robust estimates of cancer survival. Use of passive follow-up with record linkage may result in overestimation of survival in some populations, such as those whose members are more likely to leave the state or country.
No mechanism currently exists for central pathology review of cases within the US cancer registry system, and histopathology code assignment at case registration is based on histopathology information contained in the patient’s medical record. The WHO Classification of Tumours of the Central Nervous System has been revised in 1993, 2000, 2007, 2016, and 2021.3,40-43 As of 2018, the US cancer registry system uses the 2016 classification for data abstraction, but tumors included in this report may have been diagnosed using either the 2016 or 2021 classification system due to the variation in adoption of new standards by individual physicians and medical practices. Histopathologies are reflective of the prevailing criteria at the time of case registration. This means that despite changes that may occur over time, it is not possible, without additional variables, to go back and reclassify tumors based on the new criteria. In addition to changes in criteria over time, there is significant inter-rater variability in histopathological diagnosis of glioma.44,45 This also means that incomplete, incorrect, or alternatively stated diagnoses included in a pathology report or other medical record may result in an incorrect reporting of the details of an individual case.
Cancer registration in the United States requires the reporting of cases that are confirmed by using different types of diagnostic procedures, including both microscopic confirmation and radiographic confirmation. Microscopic confirmation alone (in some cases, supplemented by molecular testing) provides certainty when assigning specific histopathology and CNS WHO grade. Many tumors have unique characteristics that make them identifiable on imaging, which thereby qualifies as a valid type of diagnostic procedure, but it is important to consider the lower level of certainty in specifying the correct histopathology of these tumors.
TECHNICAL NOTES
Variable Completeness in Cancer Registration
Obtaining the most accurate and complete cancer registration data possible is essential to generate accurate population-level statistics to guide public health planning. Agencies such as NAACCR and IARC have developed stringent standards for evaluation of cancer registry data quality, and they evaluate each specific registry by multiple metrics before including it in analytic datasets. While many measures of quality and completeness are assessed across all cancer sites, some variables are pertinent only to specific sites and/or histopathologies and require special care. In the case of primary brain and other CNS tumors, variables such as CNS WHO grade are not relevant to certain histopathologies (e.g., many tumors of the pituitary), which are not assigned a CNS WHO grade. Similarly, the BMM variable is applicable only to specific histopathologies. Variables like CNS WHO grade or BMM may not be found in the patient record for those cases that had diagnosis confirmed via radiographic methods as compared to histopathological examination. This report evaluates the completeness of multiple variables, including CNS WHO grade (applicable to specific brain and other CNS sites and histopathologies only), BMM (applicable to specific histopathologies only), extent of surgical resection, and radiation treatment.
Data Interpretation
CBTRUS works diligently to support the broader surveillance efforts aimed at improving the collection and reporting of primary brain and other CNS tumors. CCR data provided to NPCR, to SEER and, subsequently, to CBTRUS vary from year to year due to ongoing updates to cases from all cancer diagnosis years, as well as changes in collection and data refinement aimed at improving completeness and accuracy. It is important to note that data from previous CBTRUS Reports cannot be compared to data in this current report. This current report supersedes all previous reports in terms of coverage of the US population and completeness with the most up-to-date population-based information available, making these data the most accurate and timely to reference. See the Supplemental Methods for more about factors potentially affecting data interpretation.
CBTRUS editing practices are reviewed, revised, and conducted yearly. These practices are aimed at refining the data for accuracy and clinical relevance and play a role in interpreting these report data. They also include the exclusion of site and histopathology combinations considered invalid by the CBTRUS consulting neuropathologists who revised the CBTRUS site/histopathology validation list in 2021. Editing changes, such as the Multiple Primary and Histology Rules issued in 2007 and revised in 2018, and adoption of Cancer PathCHART in 2024 also incorporate updates to the cancer registration coding rules that influence case ascertainment and data collection.
CONCLUDING COMMENT
The CBTRUS Statistical Report: Primary Brain and Other Central Nervous System Tumors Diagnosed in the United States in 2018−2022 comprehensively describes the most up-to-date (published October 2025) population-based incidence, mortality, and relative survival of primary malignant and non-malignant brain and other CNS tumors collected and reported by CCRs covering the entire US population. This report aims to serve as a useful resource for researchers, clinicians, patients, and families. CBTRUS continually revises its reports to reflect the current collection and reporting practices of the broader surveillance community in which it works, while integrating the input it receives from the clinical and research communities, especially from neuropathologists, when possible. In this way, CBTRUS facilitates communication between the cancer surveillance and the brain tumor research and clinical communities and contributes meaningful insight into the descriptive epidemiology of all primary brain and other CNS tumors in the United States. With CBTRUS’s new affiliation with the Society for Neuro-Oncology (beginning January 1, 2025), there will be more opportunities to facilitate communication between these interdependent communities and share our experiences more broadly globally.
