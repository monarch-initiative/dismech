PURPOSE OF REVIEW: Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) 
is a devastating heritable channelopathy that can lead to sudden cardiac death 
in children and young adults. This review aims to explore genetics, the cardiac 
and extracardiac manifestations of mutations associated with CPVT, and the 
challenges involved with managing phenotypically variable variants.
RECENT FINDINGS: The understanding of the genetics and mechanisms of CPVT 
continues to grow with recent discoveries including alternative splicing of 
cardiac TRDN and calmodulin gene variants. Additionally, there is an increasing 
recognition of the extra-cardiac manifestations such as epilepsy, 
neurodevelopmental delay, and glucose homeostasis abnormalities in RyR2 variant 
carriers. Advances in precision medicine, including the development of 
iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, are valuable models for developing targeted 
therapeutics.
SUMMARY: CPVT remains a complex disorder with cardiac and neurological 
manifestations impacting management. Early genetic testing and personalized 
treatment, including beta-blockers, flecainide, and ICDs, is important in 
improving outcomes. Ongoing research into the mechanism of each mutation will 
help in developing more effective, personalized therapeutics.

Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is a hereditary, malignant arrhythmogenic disorder characterized by exercise or emotion-induced syncope in the absence of structural heart anomalies. Classically, a bidirectional ventricular tachycardia or polymorphic premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) are seen on electrocardiogram (ECG) during an episode. Though rare, affecting 1 in 10,000 individuals, CPVT is lethal as it can lead to cardiac arrest and sudden cardiac death (SCD) at the initial presentation [1]. CPVT onset generally occurs in children and young adults, with a mean age of symptom onset of 11 years [2]. The most common clinical symptom at presentation is syncope, followed by aborted cardiac arrests, palpitations, and seizures [2]. After an initial episode, exercise stress testing is the primary diagnostic tool in CPVT[3]. The onset of monomorphic ventricular ectopy followed by complex ventricular arrhythmias on ECG with increasing levels of exercise is diagnostic of CPVT[3].

Since the first case of CPVT was reported, variants in at least 7 distinct genes have been linked to CPVT and many different presentations of the disease have been described in the literature. Most recently, extra-cardiac manifestations of CPVT such as epilepsy, neurodevelopmental effects, and diabetes mellitus have been reported. Here, we review the pathophysiology, genetics, cardiac and extra-cardiac clinical manifestations, and acute and chronic management of CPVT.

CPVT is caused by genetic variants that alter intracellular calcium (Ca2+) homeostasis in cardiomyocytes during beta-adrenergic stimulation. Under normal conditions, the cardiac action potential opens voltage-dependent L-type Ca2+ channel in the plasma membrane leading to an influx of Ca2+ ions that then trigger the release of Ca2+ for the ryanodine receptor type 2 (RyR2) into the cytoplasm. The increase in cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentrations leads to sarcomere contraction during systole. During diastole, RyR2 closes and Ca2+ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) through sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA2a) and Ca2+ is also extruded from the cytosol by the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger. In CPVT, generally speaking a genetic variant in one of the key Ca2+ handling proteins causes increased diastolic SR Ca2+ leak due to abnormal RyR2-mediated Ca2+ release or impaired Ca2+ reuptake or buffering within the SR following adrenergic stimulation. The aberrant Ca2+ leak causes activation the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger leading to delayed after depolarizations (DADs) which can trigger ventricular tachycardia.

CPVT types are classified by the gene that is affected by genetic variants (Table 1). The mechanism by which variants cause aberrant SR Ca2+ release following adrenergic stimulation depends upon the specific gene and variant, and have been previously reviewed [4]. Recent advancements in the field are summarized below:

Mutations in RYR2 cause diastolic SR Ca2+ leakage from RyR2 under beta-adrenergic stimulation and is the most common cause of CPVT [5]. Recent cohorts in Taiwan and China showed that 55% and 70% of cases who carry a pathogenic CPVT variant had missense mutations in RYR2 [6,7]. One potential mechanism for CPVT relates to a reduced binding affinity of RyR2-stabilizing subunit FK506-binding protein 12.6 (FKBP12.6) to mutant RyR2 channels [8,9].This observation led to the development of therapeutic concept that increasing FKBP12.6 binding to RyR2 by means of overexpression [10,11] or a small molecule that enhances their affinity can be used for the treatment of CPVT arrhythmias [12].

A majority of CPVT-associated RYR2 variants are autosomal-dominant gain-of-function missense mutations. We recently demonstrated in mice, that allele-specific disruption of mutant Ryr2 allele expression was sufficient to prevent stress-induced arrhythmia for at least 12 months without changes in contractility or baseline ECG intervals [13]. This study suggests that genome editing may also be evaluated in clinical trials as a novel and permanent treatment option for CPVT [14].

While CPVT is caused by gain-of-function variants in RyR2, it was recently recognized that loss-of-function variants in RyR2 cause a distinct clinical phenotype known as calcium release deficiency syndrome (CRDS) that can be undetectable until it leads to sudden cardiac death [15]. While exercise stress testing (the standard clinical diagnostic test for CPVT) can often induce ventricular arrhythmias characteristic of CPVT, this is not the case for patients with CRDS. A recent clinical study revealed that CRDS patients often exhibit a unique repolarization response on electrocardiograms after provocation by cardiac pacing with a brief tachycardia and a subsequent pause [15]. These findings need to be confirmed in larger cohorts before they can be utilized as a clinical diagnostic tool for suspected CRDS patients.

CASQ2 is a high capacity, low affinity Ca2+ binding protein in the cardiomyocyte SR that functions as a Ca2+ buffer. Loss-of-function of CASQ2 can cause CPVT due to the loss of Ca2+ buffering which can lead to erratic SR Ca2+ release events and DADs [16]. A recent case report identified a 10-year-old child with an autosomal-recessive nonsense variant [17]. At age 6, the patient presented with syncope and epileptic discharges on EEG, and the patient was started on anticonvulsants. The same genetic defect that causes cardiac arrhythmia can also impact brain function in some individuals, potentially causing seizures. This is because the Ca2+ handling proteins that are impacted by genetic variants causally linked to CPVT are expressed in both cardiomyocytes and neurons. The patient was event free for 4 years when anticonvulsants were stopped, whereafter the patient experience exercise-induced presyncope for 4 months before hospitalization and genetic testing confirmed CPVT [17].

TECRL is a protein in the SR that maintains Ca2+ homeostasis. Autosomal-recessive variants in TECRL can cause CPVT through elevated diastolic Ca2+ levels though the mechanisms are not well understood [18]. A recent case report found compound heterozygous mutations in TECRL in a 10-year-old child that experienced syncope while swimming [19]. A recent study of Tecrl knock-out mice demonstrated several differentially-expressed genes with enrichment of metabolic and Ca2+ handling pathways [20]. However, further studies are needed to better elucidate the mechanism of TECRL associated CPVT.

CaM1 is a highly conserved Ca2+-binding protein that acts as an important intracellular binding protein for Ca2+ ions. CaM1 regulates kinases, phosphatases, and ion channels including RyR2 and L-type Ca2+ channels (CaV1.2). Mutations in CaM1 can lead to CPVT through a disrupted interaction with RyR2 [21]. A recent computational model of CaM1’s interactions with CaV1.2 and RyR2 has shown that binding interactions between RyR2 and CaM1 in the C-Lobe of CAM1 are consistent with known mutations that cause CPVT [22]. Another study in zebrafish demonstrated that CAM1 variants that affect the CaM1 interaction with RyR2 have different phenotypic effects mimicking either bradycardia or ventricular tachycardia [23]. Further studies in mammalian models are needed to better understand the mechanism of each CAM1 variant.

Triadin, encoded by TRDN exons 1-8, is an integral component of the Ca2+ release complex and functions by anchoring calsequestrin to RyR2 [24]. A recent report revealed a long noncoding RNA, TRDN-AS, encoded by the antisense strand of the triadin gene between exons 9 and 11 that regulates the alternative splicing of TRDN [25]. Downregulation of Trdn-as in mice resulted in downregulation of cardiac triadin. Furthermore, Trdn-as knockout mice were susceptible to catecholamine-induced ventricular tachycardia. Mechanistically, Trdn-as was found to be essential for splicing factor recruitment, highlighting the emerging role of epigenetics in CPVT mechanisms.

Gomez-Hurtado et al.[26] identified CALM3 as another CPVT risk gene. A heterozygous mutation (p.Ala103Val) was identified in CALM3 in a 31-year-old woman clinically diagnosed with CPVT, who tested negative for other known CPVT genes. This patient experienced episodes of exertion-induced syncope since the age of 10, normal QT intervals, and ventricular ectopy during exercise-stress testing consistent with CPVT. The A103V mutation lowered calmodulin Ca2+-binding activity, but did not alter CaM binding to RyR2. In permeabilized cardiac myocytes, p.Ala103Val mutant CaM promoted spontaneous Ca2+ waves and spark activity, consistent with a cellular phenotype of RyR2 hyperactivity and CPVT [26].

Like CALM1 variants, CALM2 variants encoding calmodulin are associated with CPVT [27]. A recent case report found a c.136G>A variant responsible for CPVT in a 21-year-old female with bidirectional VT [28], representing the third case of this particular CALM2 variant according to the International Calmodulin Registry. The patient was managed with propanol and subcutaneous ICDs. Due to the rarity of these variants, reporting cases is important for gaining more insights into best treatment practices.

According to expert consensus recommendations, four main criteria exist in the diagnosis of CPVT [3]. CPVT can be difficult to diagnose as a resting 12-lead ECG is usually normal. A systematic review of 11 studies describing a total of 403 patients found that 66% of patients are symptomatic when a CPVT diagnosis was made, meaning many individuals are asymptomatic [29]. While syncope is the most common symptom at initial presentation, one-fifth of CPVT patients present with an aborted cardiac arrest at initial presentation [2]. In patients presenting with an aborted or sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) and normal heart structure, CPVT must be considered in the differential diagnosis. Importantly, other causes of arrhythmia such as electrolyte abnormalities and substance use should be excluded prior to initiating diagnostic work-up for CPVT. An exercise-stress test is generally the first diagnostic tool and will demonstrate ventricular ectopy on ECG which progresses into monomorphic PVCs followed by polymorphic PVCs and bidirectional or polymorphic VT on ECG, which are diagnostic of CPVT [3]. In patients who are unable to tolerate exercise (e.g., physically disabled, infants, immediately post-resuscitation), intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion is an alternative diagnostic tool involving incremental increases in epinephrine administration until polymorphic VT is triggered on ECG. Holter monitoring and implantable loop recorders are other options for CPVT diagnosis in these patients [3], although the former may not be adequate for diagnosis alone [30]. It is important to consider that in pediatric CPVT patients, ventricular arrhythmias are more likely to occur or be evoked in the afternoon and evening hours due to circadian arrhythmia patterns [31]. Once a diagnosis of CPVT is established, genetic testing to identify the specific pathogenic mutation is recommended in both the index patient and their relatives, even if asymptomatic [3].

Initially thought to mainly be a cardiac disease, newer research has revealed important extra-cardiac manifestations of CPVT. Aside from the heart, the neurologic system seems to be the most implicated in CPVT. Konomatsu et al.[32] described the case of a 17-year-old girl with recurrent unprovoked generalized convulsion and a family history of arrhythmia in her father and paternal grandfather but no history of SCD or epilepsy. She independently also experienced episodes of loss of consciousness with twitching after exercise. Genetic testing revealed a pathogenic RYR2 missense variant (c.11995A>G, p.Met3999Val) and separate diagnoses of CPVT and genetic generalized epilepsy (GGE) were made. However, this pathogenic RYR2 variant may actually represent a novel neurocardiac Ca2+ channelopathy as this isoform is abundantly expressed in cardiac muscle and the cerebral cortex. The increase in Ca2+ release from the SR due to an abnormal ryanodine receptor results in increased activity of the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger and DADs in cardiomyocytes and neurons [33]. In addition, leaky RyR2 channels can selectively strengthen excitatory, but not inhibitory, synapses, lowering the threshold for spreading depolarizations in the dorsal medulla, leading to cardiorespiratory collapse [34]. Moreover, RYR2 mutations already have been demonstrated to cause generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCS) in humans [35]. In 2019, Yap and Smyth [36] reported the case of a 32-year-old female presenting with unprovoked GTCS without arrhythmias who was found to have a heterozygous RYR2 missense mutation (c.229G>A, p.Ala77Thr) on genetic testing. Her brother, carrying the same mutation, was diagnosed with CPVT [36].

RYR2 mutations are also linked with benign epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (BECTS). Xing et al. [37] report the case of a 14-year-old boy who presented with recurrent syncopal episodes over nine years and sudden hemifacial convulsions spanning four years. On whole exome sequencing, a heterozygous RYR2 missense mutation (c.6577G>T, p.Val2193Leu) was identified. The patient received diagnoses of CPVT and BECTS, and was treated with metoprolol (12.5 mg) and levetiracetam (500 mg) twice daily. While levetiracetam helped to control this patient’s seizures, metoprolol did not effectively decrease his exercise-induced syncopal episodes.

Anecdotal case reports have suggested a link between de novo mutations in RYR2 and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). In 2019, Lieve et al. [38] concluded that 34/421 (8%) of patients with RYR2-variant CPVT had mild to severe intellectual disability, compared to 1-3% in the general population. Nine out of these 34 patients had additional neurodevelopmental disorders including communication disorders, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), specific learning disorder, or motor disorders. The link between RYR2 mutations and NDDs can be explained by the resulting dysregulation of intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis due to “leaky” RYR2 channels, which is crucial for neuron survival and function [39].

Several CALM1 and CALM2 missense variants with a cardiological phenotype of CPVT have also been associated with neurodevelopmental/ neurological features such as intellectual disability, ASD, ADHD, and developmental delay [28,40]. While the link between calmodulin mutations and neurodevelopmental disorders is not completely elucidated, a potential mechanistic paradigm to explain this relationship is a dysfunction in calmodulin’s Ca2+-binding affinity and Ca2+ sensing properties [41]. Since calmodulin has many neuronal targets and plays an important role in maintaining synaptic plasticity by regulating CaMKII [42], the effects of Ca2+ imbalance may manifest as neurodevelopmental features. CALM mutations may also interfere with a specific calmodulin/ protein binding interface, affecting downstream signaling pathways.

Santulli et al. [43] is the only study to report abnormal insulin secretion and glucose homeostasis in 27 CPVT patients with pathogenic heterozygous missense RYR2 variants [43]. A mean 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test value of 160.4 ± 25.3 mg/dL was found in this cohort. All patients with a missense variant specifically between amino acids 2246-2534 in the RYR2 gene had 2-hour OGTT values of 170 mg/dL or greater [44]. The role of RYR2 in mediating Ca2+ release in the pancreatic beta-cells to regulate glucose-mediated insulin secretion has been investigated. For example, gain-of-function defects in RYR2 result in increased intracellular Ca2+, which thereby augments insulin production in the pancreatic beta-cells [45].

A patient acutely presenting with polymorphic VT requires rapid termination of their arrhythmia. Because of the catecholaminergic nature of the arrhythmia in CPVT, however, standard resuscitation algorithms including the administration of bolus epinephrine and defibrillation may need to be avoided as they can trigger or exacerbate an electrical storm in CPVT patients [46,47]. Esmolol may be preferred in the acute setting due to its rapid onset and short half-life [48].

Beta-adrenoreceptor blockers are a cornerstone medication in the prophylactic management of CPVT patients with a documented stress-induced ventricular arrhythmia [3,48]. Nadolol, a long-acting non-selective beta-blocker, is preferred as it is associated with lower rates of arrhythmias during exercise stress testing compared to both β1-selective β-blockers and patients receiving no beta-blocker therapy [48–50]. In cases where breakthrough arrhythmic events occur despite beta-blocker therapy [49], the addition of flecainide, a class IC anti-arrhythmic, was associated with fewer adverse events including SCD, SCA, or arrhythmic syncope [51]. Clinicians must regularly counsel CPVT patients regarding strict compliance with their medications as non-adherence is high [52] and may lead to complications including SCD [53]. Of note, a fraction of CPVT patients cannot tolerate beta-blocker therapy due to unacceptable side effects, in whom intentional non-therapy may be safely pursued given a low-risk assessment [54]. Left cardiac sympathetic denervation (LCSD) is a surgical procedure that involves the denervation of the left stellate ganglion (T1) along with the thoracic ganglia from T2-T4 [55]. LCSD is recommended as an additional therapy in CPVT patients who are symptomatic despite optimal medical therapies (OMT) [55,56].

An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) is indicated in CPVT patients who have survived a SCA or experience arrhythmic syncope despite OMT (beta-blocker plus flecainide) and/ or LCSD (class I recommendation) [57]. Though ICDs were formerly a staple in the management of CPVT, recent research has demonstrated that ICD implantation is not associated with improved survival [58]. It is linked with a significant rate of both appropriate and inappropriate shocks [53,58], which may trigger a fatal electrical storm [59]. Therefore, ICDs should be programmed to minimize unnecessary shocks and to have long delays for shock delivery.

Catheter ablation of PVCs that trigger polymorphic VT is a novel procedure that may reduce syncope and exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias in patients on maximal beta-blocker therapy who are unsuitable candidates for flecainide treatment [60]. To that end, ablation does not fully eliminate arrhythmogenic substrates in CPVT patients, so continued treatment with beta-blockers is necessary [60]. In contrast, the ablation of the atrioventricular (AV) node may not be an effective strategy in preventing arrhythmias in CPVT patients [61].

The advent of precision medicine, specifically with human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), is an exciting novel avenue of treatment in CPVT and cardiac channelopathies in general. Patient-derived iPSC-cardiac myocytes have been utilized to serve as a disease model in a dish and to analyze the pathogenicity of variants of uncertain significance in long QT syndrome [62]. This helps to both determine a patient’s risk of experiencing SCD and perform phenotypic analysis to develop and test patient-specific treatments [63]. Recently, ruxolitinib, a Janus kinase inhibitor indicated for the treatment of myeloproliferative disorders, was demonstrated to prevent arrhythmias by inhibiting CaMKII hyperactivity in validated murine CPVT models [64]. However, more research on ruxolitinib involving human subjects is necessary, especially given CaMKII’s crucial role in maintaining long-term synaptic plasticity [42]. Our preliminary analysis of structure-activity relationship of second-generation tetracaine derivatives identified the lead compound MSV1302 as having the capacity to normalize aberrant RyR2 activity in CPVT mouse models without altering cardiac inotropy, chronotropy, or off-target effects on other ion channels [65]. MSV1302 or similar RyR2 modifying compounds may be strong candidates for future clinical studies to determine its efficacy in CPVT patients [65,66].

CPVT is a complex genetic disorder with significant cardiac and neurological implications. While current management strategies including beta-blockers, flecainide, and left cardiac sympathetic denervation have improved patient outcomes, emerging precision medicine approaches show promise. Genetic understanding continues to evolve, revealing new pathogenic mutations and potential extra-cardiac manifestations. Future research focusing on targeted therapies and personalized treatment strategies will be crucial in mitigating the potentially fatal risks associated with this rare, but potentially fatal, condition.
