# Dedicator of cytokinesis 8 interacts with talin and Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein to regulate NK cell cytotoxicity.
**Authors:** Ham H, Guerrier S, Kim J, Schoon RA, Anderson EL, Hamann MJ, Lou Z, Billadeau DD
**Journal:** J Immunol (2013)
**DOI:** [10.4049/jimmunol.1202792](https://doi.org/10.4049/jimmunol.1202792)

## Content

Recently, patients with mutations in DOCK8 have been reported to have a combined immunodeficiency characterized by cutaneous viral infections and allergies. NK cells represent a first-line defense against viral infections, suggesting that DOCK8 might participate in NK cell function. In this study, we demonstrate that DOCK8-suppressed human NK cells showed defects in natural cytotoxicity as well as specific activating receptor-mediated NK cytotoxicity. Additionally, compared with control NK cells, NK cells depleted of DOCK8 showed defective conjugate formation, along with decreased polarization of LFA-1, F-actin, and cytolytic granules toward the cytotoxic synapse. Using a proteomic approach, we found that DOCK8 exists in a macromolecular complex with the Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein, an actin nucleation-promoting factor activated by CDC42, as well as talin, which is required for integrin-mediated adhesion. Taken together, our results demonstrate an important role for DOCK8 in NK cell effector function and provide important new mechanistic insight into how DOCK8 regulates F-actin and integrin-mediated adhesion in immune cells.

The DOCK (dedicator of cytokinesis) 180 family of guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEF), of which there are 11 known genes, participate in the activation of Rho family proteins and contribute to multiple cellular processes including cell migration, phagocytosis and immune homeostasis (1–3). In 2009, mutations in the DOCK8 gene were found to be associated with cases of autosomal recessive hyper-IgE syndrome (4, 5). The disease showed an autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance, and all reported patients harbored homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations on both alleles leading to either null or non-functional expression of DOCK8. The main clinical symptoms of the patients were recurrent sinopulmonary and cutaneous infections, and severe allergies. Immunologically, patients showed high IgE immunoglobulin and eosinophil numbers in their sera, defects in generation of long-lasting humoral immunity, and T cells with limited cellular proliferation following activation (4–7).

Direct killing by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) or NK cells against target cells is required for clearance of intracellular pathogens including virus-infected or transformed cells (8–10). The observation that most DOCK8-deficient patients are susceptible to recurrent viral infections suggests the existence of a general deficiency of cytotoxic lymphocytes. Supporting this hypothesis, one group reported T cell lymphopenia and impaired T cell expansion in DOCK8-deficient patients (5). However, another clinical report found that T cell numbers in patients were within normal range (4), and a recent study reported that mouse CTLs harboring a mutation within the DOCK8 DHR2 (DOCK homology region 2) domain had no killing defect compared to control CTLs (7). Although NK cell numbers were within the normal range, it remains possible that impaired NK cell function might be responsible for the recurrent viral infections. Interestingly, TAP-deficient patients lack CD8+ T cells and do not show particular susceptibility to viral infections (11). It is also worthwhile to note that NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity is crucial for elimination of herpes simplex virus (HSV), the most common recurrent viral infection seen in DOCK8-deficient patients (4, 5, 12). Despite this, NK cell function has yet to be assessed in patients deficient in DOCK8.

Previous studies using B cells and T cells from DOCK8 mutant mice have shown that DOCK8 is involved in F-actin and integrin accumulation at the immune synapse (IS) (7, 13). Recent findings that DOCK8 has GEF-activity toward CDC42 (14) should also be noted considering an essential role of CDC42 in reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton as well as its regulation of cell polarity. Since F-actin polymerization is involved in integrin clustering and cytotoxic synapse formation leading to adhesion and killing (15), we decided to investigate the role of DOCK8 in human NK cells. Herein we show that DOCK8 knockdown results in diminished NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Consistent with previous studies in T cells (7), we find that suppression of DOCK8 affects F-actin accumulation at the cytotoxic synapse (CS), as well as diminished integrin recruitment leading to an overall decrease in cell-cell adhesion. Using proteomics, we show that DOCK8 interacts with the integrin regulator talin, as well as the CDC42 effector Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome protein (WASP) and the interaction with DOCK8 is involved in their accumulation at the CS. Taken together, our results provide new mechanistic information regarding DOCK8 cellular function in NK cells and contribute to the understanding of the known phenotypes observed in DOCK8-deficiency.

While DOCK8 is expressed in T and B cells (4–7), DOCK8 expression has not been examined in NK cells. Therefore, we prepared two human NK cell-lines, NKL and YTS, and primary human NK clones along with Jurkat T cell leukemia cell-line and primary human T cells, and then examined DOCK8 expression at the protein level (Fig. 1A). DOCK8 expression was detected in both NK cell-lines as well as primary human NK cells. Unexpectedly, DOCK8 was not expressed in Jurkat T cells, whereas we were able to detect its expression in primary human CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1A).

To determine whether DOCK8 is involved in NK cell cytotoxicity, we compared cytotoxic activity of YTS cells after depletion of DOCK8 using two different siRNA oligonucleotide duplexes. Seventy-two hours after transfection, we examined natural cytotoxicity using the 721.221 B lymphoblastoid cell-line (Fig. 1B). DOCK8-suppressed YTS cells showed less cytotoxicity compared to control siRNA-transfected cells in all examined effector:target (E:T) ratios, and this cytotoxicity defect was DOCK8 dosage-dependent (Fig. 1B, left and center). Lytic units (LU), a value representing relative cytotoxic activity, was also diminished in the DOCK8-suppressed cells compared to that of the control group, and again, the impact on LU depended on the level of DOCK8 expression (Fig. 1B, left and right). We also observed a similar decrease in lytic activity toward 721.221 cells by both DOCK8-depleted NKL cells and primary human NK clones (Fig. 1C and Fig. 1D, top). In addition to natural cytotoxicity, we examined receptor-mediated cytotoxicity using a reverse antibody dependent cellular cytotoxicity assay against P815 tumor target cells labeled with anti-FcR (anti-CD16) mAb or anti-NKG2D mAb. Suppression of DOCK8 consistently decreased redirected cytotoxicity through both the FcR and NKG2D receptors (Fig. 1D, bottom). Taken together, these data identify DOCK8 as a regulator of NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity.

NK cell cytotoxicity is a multi-step process where each step is regulated by specific molecules to carry out rapid and efficient target-recognition and killing (15). The initial step of NK cell cytotoxicity is binding of NK cells to target cells via adhesion molecules. Integrin-mediated firm adhesion to target cells allows NK cells to scan bound targets via various activating receptors (15, 24, 25). Additionally, previous studies using DOCK8-mutant T and B cells indicated a role for DOCK8 in integrin clustering at the IS (7, 13). To test whether DOCK8 was involved in initial target cell binding, we assessed and compared adhesion of control transfected and DOCK8-suppressed NK cells. Significantly, YTS, NKL cells and primary human NK clones depleted of DOCK8 showed decreased conjugate formation with target cells compared to control NK cells (Fig. 2A–2C). The defect in adhesion of DOCK8-suppressed NK cells was not due to reduction in surface expression of integrin, since we observed similar CD18 (β2 integrin) surface expression levels in both control transfected and DOCK8-depleted YTS cells and NKLs (Supplemental Figure 1). Therefore, DOCK8 is involved in conjugate formation between NK cells and target cells.

We next examined whether DOCK8 regulates key events during NK cell cytotoxicity: accumulation of LFA-1 and F-actin at the CS, as well as granule polarization. We first assessed LFA-1 accumulation at the CS in control or DOCK8-depleted YTS cells conjugated with 721.221 target cells. Whereas LFA-1 was highly polarized to the NK–target CS in most control-transfected cells, many DOCK8-depleted YTS cells failed to polarize LFA-1 to the interface; LFA-1 was either evenly distributed on the cell surface or weakly polarized (Fig. 3A). When we examined the target-contacting region of control transfected cells using 3D reconstruction, we were able to observe thick clustering of LFA-1 into ring-shaped peripheral supramolecular activation cluster (pSMAC) (Supplemental Video 1). However, DOCK8-suppressed YTS cells showed much less efficient accumulation of LFA-1 at pSMAC consistent with what has been observed in DOCK8 mutant B cells (13) (Supplemental Video 2). For quantification of accumulated integrin at the CS, we measured sum fluorescence intensity of NK-target interface and compared it to that of entire NK cell (Fig 3B). Fluorescence of integrin at the CS was significantly weaker in DOCK8-depleted YTS cells compared to that observed in control cells, and this was due to a decrease in both contact area and density of integrin at the interface (Supplemental Figure 2A).

Previous studies in DOCK8-mutant T cells have found diminished accumulation of F-actin at the IS (7). We therefore examined whether DOCK depletion in NK cells would affect F-actin accumulation at the CS. Consistent with what has been observed in T cells lacking DOCK8 (7), DOCK8-suppressed YTS cells showed defective polarization of F-actin to the CS (Fig. 3C and 3D). Again, this defective actin accumulation was due to smaller contact area and weaker density of F-actin at the CS (Supplemental Figure 2B). DOCK8-depleted NKLs also showed a similar decrease in F-actin and LFA-1 at the CS (data not shown). We next examined cytolytic granule polarization, since it was previously determined that integrin engagement in NK cells provides signals for granule polarization (26, 27). In this experiment, each conjugated YTS cell was placed into three groups depending on the position of granules from the NK-target cell interface; ‘front’, ‘middle’, and ‘back’. Cytolytic granules were clearly polarized just behind the NK–target interface (‘front’) in almost half of conjugated YTS cells, whereas DOCK8-suppressed YTS cells showed far fewer conjugates with polarized lytic granules (Fig. 3E and 3F). Granules were either not clearly polarized to the front (‘middle’) or found at opposite position (‘back’) from the NK-target interface in DOCK8-depleted YTS cells. These data suggest that DOCK8 not only regulates integrin clustering and F-actin accumulation at the CS, but also participates in the development of NK cell-mediated killing through its effects on lytic granule polarization.

Eleven members of the DOCK family of GEFs (DOCK1 – DOCK11) can be organized into four distinct subgroups (DOCK-A DOCK-D) based on domain composition and sequence homology (1). Members of DOCK-A and DOCK-B (DOCK1 – DOCK5) are able to interact with ELMO mainly through a highly conserved N-terminal SH3 domain and with Crk adaptor proteins via the proline-rich motif located at their C-terminus (2, 3, 28, 29). Interactions of DOCK-A and DOCK-B members with these binding partners are essential for their normal cellular processes. In the case of DOCK180 (DOCK1), interaction with ELMO has been reported to be critical for regulation of the DOCK180/Rac pathway (2, 3, 30). In contrast, DOCK8 and other DOCK-C subfamily members (DOCK6 and DOCK7), while possessing the DHR1 and DHR2 domains, do not contain other identifiable domains and therefore are not predicted to interact with ELMO and Crk proteins and are thus likely regulated by a different mechanism.

While our preceding data provide insight into the role of DOCK8 in NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, the mechanism by which DOCK8 contributes to cellular functions is lacking as a result of limited knowledge regarding the DOCK8 interactome. Therefore, we first examined whether DOCK8 exists in a multi-protein complex endogenously. YTS cell homogenate was separated via size-exclusion chromatography and DOCK8-containing fractions were identified by immunoblot (Supplemental Figure 3A). Interestingly, we observed that DOCK8 eluted at a much larger size (> 400 kDa) than expected (~240 kDa) indicating that DOCK8 might be constitutively integrated into macromolecular complexes. To identify potential DOCK8-interacting proteins we immunoprecipitated DOCK8 from the DOCK8-enriched fractions and analyzed associated proteins by mass spectrometry (MS), an approach we previously applied for identification of the WASH interactome (19, 31). Interestingly, we identified WASP as a candidate DOCK8-interacting protein by MS (Supplemental Figure 3B). WASP is the founding member of the WASP superfamily of F-actin nucleation promoting factors that stimulate the activity of branched F-actin via the ubiquitously expressed Arp2/3 complex (32–35). Furthermore, this potential interaction was very intriguing, since the activity of WASP requires binding of active CDC42 (CDC42-GTP) (36).

Recently, it was shown that DOCK8 functions as a CDC42-GEF via its DHR2 domain (14). Using an in vitro split luciferase assay (23), we also observed that the DOCK8 DHR2 domain has robust GEF activity toward CDC42 with limited activity toward RAC1 and no activity toward RHOA (Supplemental Figure 4). To confirm the interaction of DOCK8 with WASP, we transfected 293T human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells with plasmid encoding FLAG-only/FLAG-tagged DOCK8 along with hemagglutinin (HA)-only / HA-tagged WASP and observed that WASP co-immunoprecipitated together with DOCK8 (Supplemental Figure 3C). Importantly, we were also able to confirm that endogenous DOCK8 and WASP co-immunoprecipitate with each other in NKLs (Fig. 4A). As expected, WASP did not co-precipitate with DOCK8 in Jurkat cells, where DOCK8 is not expressed.

In addition to WASP, we also identified talin as a candidate-interacting partner of DOCK8 (Supplemental Figure 3B). Talin not only participates in integrin affinity maturation by mediating a conformational change of the integrin after cell activation (inside-out signaling), but also transmits activation signals from integrins (outside-in signaling) (37–41). Again, exogenous DOCK8 co-precipitated together with talin (Supplemental Figure 3D), and we could also confirm reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation of endogenous DOCK8 and talin in NKLs, but not in Jurkat cells (Fig. 4B). Interactions of DOCK8 with each protein do not seem to be dependent on the GEF activity of DOCK8, as we did not observe any change in the interaction between WT DOCK8 and GEF-deficient mutant (V1985A) (Supplemental Figure 3E and 3F).

To delineate the interacting region of DOCK8 with WASP and talin, we prepared three non-overlapping fragments of DOCK8 fused with glutathione S-transferase (GST) at the N-terminus (Fig. 4C). Using lysates from HEK293T cells and HEK293T cells expressing HA-tagged WASP, we mapped the interacting region of DOCK8 for each binding partner by pull-down experiments. WASP was mainly pulled down with the middle fragment of DOCK8, and weak interaction was also observed with the N-terminal fragment (Fig. 4D). On the other hand, talin was pulled down with the C-terminal fragment of DOCK8 (Fig. 4D). Taken together, our results demonstrate important molecular interactions of DOCK8 with the CDC42 effector WASP as well as the key integrin regulator talin.

From previous studies, it is known that both WASP and talin are recruited to the CS of NK cells and T cells (42–45). To determine whether DOCK8 mediates accumulation of WASP and talin at the CS, we assessed accumulation of each protein at the CS in control or DOCK8-depleted YTS cells conjugated with 721.221 target cells. In the case of WASP, whereas most of control YTS cells showed clear polarization at the CS, DOCK8 suppression reduced WASP accumulation at the CS (Fig. 5A and 5B). In addition, we observed either much weaker accumulation or non-polarization of talin at the CS in DOCK8-depleted YTS cells (Fig. 5C and 5D). The defects in accumulation of both proteins at the CS in DOCK8-suppressed NK cells were due to a decrease in both contact area and accumulated density of each protein (Supplemental Figures 2C and 2D). These data suggest that DOCK8 mediates CS accumulation of WASP and talin, two essential regulators of F-actin reorganization and integrin affinity maturation, respectively.

Susceptibility to recurrent infections, various types of allergies, as well as high IgE levels in the blood are main clinical symptoms observed in most DOCK8-deficient patients (4–7, 46). Since initial reports of DOCK8-deficient patients in 2009, research has begun to unveil why deficiency of DOCK8 leads to combined immunodeficiency in humans. Defects in long-lasting humoral responses as well as lack of memory B cells have been observed in both DOCK8-deficient humans and DOCK8-mutant mice (6, 13). In addition, DOCK8 has been shown to be essential for survival of peripheral T cells and memory CD8+ T cells (6, 7, 13, 46) suggesting a potential role of DOCK8 in generation of memory in lymphocytes. Recently, DOCK8-knockout mice have been generated (14), and dendritic cells (DCs) of these mice showed defective migration into lymph nodes (LN) after antigen priming, a process required for initiation of adaptive immune responses. Overall, low numbers of circulating naïve T cells, defects in their priming in the LN, and failure in qualitative enhancement of immune responses by memory B cells and T cells might collectively lead to recurrent infections seen in DOCK8-deficient patients.

Importantly, our current understanding does not clearly explain recurrent viral infections, which is most frequently observed in DOCK8-deficiency (4, 5, 7). Cytotoxic lymphocytes are essential for immune protection from viral infections and tumor cells (8–10). In this study, we have demonstrated that DOCK8 plays an essential role in mediating NK cell cytotoxicity using human NK cell-lines as well as primary human NK clones. Considering this novel role of DOCK8 in NK cells, functional defects of NK cells in DOCK8-deficient patients could be part of the cause for frequent occurrence of viral infections and cancer in these patients (4, 5, 7). In this regard, a recent study showing normal cytotoxic activity of CTLs from DOCK8-mutant mice is very unexpected and interesting (7). However, it is worthwhile to note that viral infections are much less commonly observed in patients lacking CD8+ T cells (11), and HSV is the most common recurrent viral infection observed in DOCK8-deficient patients (4, 5, 12), since NK cell-mediated killing is essential for eradication of HSV. Therefore, future studies focusing on DOCK8-dependent NK cell functions in humans or mice will be important in understanding clinical features observed in DOCK8-deficient patients.

Our data demonstrate the cellular role of DOCK8 in NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity is achieved in part through integrin-mediated adhesion to target cells, as well as polarization of F-actin and lytic granules at the NK cell CS. In the case of DOCK8-depleted NK cells, although defects in the accumulation of F-actin and polarization of lytic granules were assessed for well-conjugated NK cells only, this does not formally rule out the possibility that observed phenotypes might be due to weak adhesion in conjugated NK cells. Integrin activation alone in NK cells is known to mediate accumulation of F-actin at NK-target interface as well as polarization of lytic granules, supporting this possibility (26, 27, 47). Further studies focusing on roles of DOCK8 in integrin regulation of NK cells as well as specific NK-activating receptor-mediated signaling will provide clearly-defined cellular roles of DOCK8 in NK cells.

Recently, Harada and colleagues reported that DOCK8 is a CDC42-GEF via pull-down assay, in vitro GEF assay, and structure analysis of DOCK8 DHR2 domain (14). However, while they did not note GEF activity toward RAC1, another group reported RAC1 GEF activity of DOCK8 (48). Using an in vitro split luciferase assay that we have developed previously (23), we observed that the DHR2 domain of DOCK8 was most active toward CDC42, showed modest activity toward RAC1 and no activity toward RHOA. In addition, a GEF-deficient mutant (DHR2-V1985A, (49)) had no detectable GEF activity toward either CDC42 or RAC1. Therefore, the observed differences in DOCK8 GEF specificity may be a result of the sensitivity of the experimental conditions. Spatiotemporal activation of CDC42 in NK cells was recently suggested to be important in CS formation and cytotoxic activity (50, 51). These observations are consistent with the cytotoxicity defects that we observed in DOCK8-depleted NK cells. As an essential regulator for cell polarity in all eukaryotes (52), CDC42 has been implicated in MTOC polarization in T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs) (53–55). Consistent with these observations, we showed defective granule polarization toward the CS in DOCK8-suppressed YTS cells. This suggests that CDC42 activity in NK cells may be also critical for MTOC polarization toward CS, since polarization of cytolytic granules toward CS is achieved by MTOC polarization (15). In addition, CDC42 was reported to be responsible for conjugate formation between T cells and antigen-presenting cells (APC), as well as actin polarization at the contact site (55, 56). Since similar defects are observed in DOCK8-suppressed NK cells and T cells, one function of DOCK8 might be to generate a localized pool of activated CDC42 (CDC42-GTP) at the contact site in order to promote conjugate formation, F-actin generation via WASP, and participate in MTOC polarization (Fig. 6).

Previous studies using B cells and CD8+ T cells from DOCK8-mutant mice have demonstrated defects in integrin clustering and F-actin accumulation at the IS (7, 13). Our findings that both talin and WASP are DOCK8-interacting partners and that DOCK8 mediates their localization to the CS provide a mechanistic link to begin to explain the cellular phenotypes observed in T cells, B cells and NK cells, as well as provide an important missing piece of information explaining remarkable similarities between WASP- and DOCK8-deficiency. Significantly, both diseases share many clinical features such as recurrent infections, and high IgE levels in serum as well as defective cellular immunity (33–35, 57). NK cells from WASP-deficient patients demonstrate defects in cytolytic activity, integrin-mediated adhesion, F-actin reorganization and polarization of cytolytic granules to the CS similar to DOCK8-suppressed NK cells (43, 58, 59). Thus, the finding that DOCK8 is a GEF for CDC42 (14) and can interact with the CDC42 effector WASP, suggests a putative molecular link for localizing DOCK8 GEF activity in close proximity to its effector molecule (Fig. 6). It remains possible that the observed clinical features observed in DOCK8-deficient patients are in part a result of defective CDC42 activation along with diminished recruitment and activation of WASP. Future studies focusing on the ‘DOCK8-CDC42-WASP’ pathway and the molecular organization and regulation of the complex by DOCK8 will contribute to a better understanding of the role of this important protein in cellular immunity.

In addition, talin, another interacting partner of DOCK8, is a critical regulator of integrin affinity, and its importance in integrin activation has been firmly established from both in vivo and in vitro studies (37–41). Talin is composed of N-terminal globular head domain containing high-affinity binding region for integrin β-chains and C-terminal rod domain that contains binding sites for multiple cytoskeletal and signaling proteins such as F-actin and vinculin (60). Talin exists in an auto-inhibited conformation in the cytosol in resting cells, and multiple mechanisms have been suggested to relieve this inhibition as well as recruitment of talin to integrins (37, 60). We observed that DOCK8-depleted NK cells failed to efficiently localize talin toward the NK-target interface. It remains possible that defects in talin localization toward the interface might be an indirect effect due to failure to recruit LFA-1 to the cytotoxic synapse following DOCK8 suppression. However, our data suggest a possible role of DOCK8 in mediating localization of talin toward the plasma membrane. The localization of DOCK8-talin toward the plasma membrane might be regulated by the DHR1domain that binds to phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate (PtdIns(3,4,5)P3) (1–3) (Fig. 6). Then, binding of the talin head domain to the integrin β-chain disrupts the inhibitory interaction between integrin α- and β-chains leading to high-affinity integrin conformation. It is also very interesting to note that talin interacts with phosphatidylinositol phosphate kinase type Iγ (PIPKIγ) that produce PtdIns(4,5)P2 (PIP2) (60–62), as well as vinculin that associates with Arp2/3, actin nucleating protein (60, 63). Activation of WASP from its auto-inhibited state requires concurrent interaction with CDC42-GTP as well as PIP2 (36). The active form of WASP is then able to interact with Arp2/3 inducing generation of a branched F-actin network leading to integrin clustering (Fig. 6). Supporting our hypotheses, it was recently shown that talin is responsible for F-actin accumulation by recruiting Arp2/3, vinculin, and WASP to the LFA-1 ICAM-1 interface in NK cells (22, 47). Taken together, our results provide important clues toward understanding the cellular role of DOCK8 in the interaction between integrin and actin cytoskeleton networks leading to formation of CS in NK cells.
