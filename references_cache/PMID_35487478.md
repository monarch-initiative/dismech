AIM: This study investigated whether rare, deleterious variants in monogenic 
diabetes-genes are associated with early-onset type 2 diabetes (T2D).
METHODS: A nested case-control study was designed from 9712 Italian patients 
with T2D. Individuals with age at diabetes onset ≤35 yrs (n = 300; cases) or ≥65 
yrs (n = 300; controls) were selected and screened for variants in 27 monogenic 
diabetes-genes by targeted resequencing. Rare (minor allele frequency-MAF <1%) 
and possibly deleterious variants were collectively tested for association with 
early-onset T2D. The association of a genetic risk score (GRS) based on 17 
GWAS-SNPs for T2D was also tested.
RESULTS: When all rare variants were considered together, each increased the 
risk of early-onset T2D by 65% (allelic OR =1.64, 95% CI: 1.08-2.48, p = 0.02). 
Effects were similar when the 600 study participants were stratified according 
to their place of recruitment (Central-Southern Italy, 182 cases vs. 142 
controls, or Rome urban area, 118 vs. 158, p for heterogeneity=0.53). 
Progressively less frequent variants showed increasingly stronger effects in the 
risk of early-onset T2D for those with MAF <0.001% (OR=6.34, 95% CI: 1.87-22.43, 
p = 0.003). One unit of T2D-GRS significantly increased the risk of early-onset 
T2D (OR 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01-1.18; p = 0.02). This association was stronger among 
rare variants carriers as compared to non-carriers (p = 0.02).
CONCLUSION: Rare variants in monogenic-diabetes genes are associated with an 
increased risk of early-onset T2D, and interact with common T2D susceptibility 
variants in shaping it. These findings might help develop prediction tools to 
identify individuals at high risk of developing T2D in early adulthood.

The prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) is dramatically increasing worldwide, thus imposing a severe burden on health care systems, patients and their families [1]. Even though T2D typically occurs in middle-aged and older adults, its age of diagnosis has been recently decreasing, with a growing number of people being diagnosed in the first decades of life [2, 3]. The main concern with early-onset T2D is that it may have a more aggressive metabolic phenotype and that this, combined with the longer exposure to the diabetic milieu, may determine a higher risk of chronic diabetes complications [4, 5], including retinopathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular disease, and premature mortality, as compared to the common forms of T2D diagnosed in middle-aged patients [6–8].

Unfortunately, the intrinsic pathogenic signatures of early-onset T2D are not completely understood. As with later forms of the disease, several pieces of evidence suggest the contribution of environmental risk factors such as unhealthy diet, obesity, and sedentary habits, which have become increasingly prevalent also in the younger generations [9]. However, genetic factors also seem to play an important role. In this regard, recent large-scale association studies have shown that low-frequency variants in genes usually responsible for monogenic diabetes forms are associated with an increased risk of T2D [10, 11]. However, whether these variants also determine an earlier onset of the disease, and therefore explain a significant part of the etiology of early-onset T2D, remains to be clarified.

The aim of the present study was to investigate the role of rare and possibly deleterious variants in known monogenic diabetes-genes as determinants of an early onset of type 2 diabetes.

We had initially access to records and samples concerning a total of 9,712 adult individuals from five independent collections of Italian patients with T2D (Supplementary Table 1). Study subjects were all whites (as per self-report) and were recruited in outpatient diabetic clinics at research/academic hospitals from Central-Southern Italy (n=5,167) and from the Rome urban area (n=4,545). Of the patients from the Central-Southern area, 2,826 were recruited at the Scientific Institute Casa Sollievo della Sofferenza, San Giovanni Rotondo (Apulia), 682 at the Endocrine Unit of the University of Foggia (Apulia), 641 at the University Hospital of the Magna-Graecia University in Catanzaro (Calabria), 1,018 at the University Hospital of Pisa (Tuscany). The hospitals from which the patients from the Rome urban area were recruited are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Diabetes was defined according to American Diabetes Association criteria (1). Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or both, or the presence of antihypertensive treatment. The age at diagnosis of diabetes was based on self-reported information. Family history of type 2 diabetes, referred only to first degree relatives, was self-reported. All patients underwent physical examination and measurements of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), lipid levels, blood pressure, and body mass index as previously described (2, 3). Clinical characteristics of patients contributed by each center are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Of the 9,712 patients initially collected, 223 individuals were excluded because of lack of information on age at diabetes diagnosis. From the remaining 9,489 patients, a nested case-control study for early-onset T2D was set up by using the extreme-phenotype approach [12]. Accordingly, all patients from each single cohort who had age at diagnosis of T2D ≤ 35 years and for whom DNA was available and suitable for next generation sequencing (NGS) were included into the study as “cases”. For each of such cases, a control patient with age at diagnosis of T2D ≥ 65 years was randomly selected from the same population. A total of 600 individuals (i.e., n=300 cases and n=300 controls) were in the end included in the study.

The study protocol was approved by the local Institutional Ethic Committee and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The study was carried out in accordance to the Helsinki Declaration.

Targeted resequencing of 27 monogenic diabetes-genes (Supplementary Table 2) as well as read mapping and variant calling were carried out as previously described [13]. Sequencing data were then subjected to a bioinformatic filtering and prioritization pipeline. In detail, several quality filters were used to discriminate real variants from false positives. PASS filter variants with genotype score equal to 99 were retained while variants showing extreme strand bias, very high or low allele balance of heterozygous calls (e.g. >0.75 or < 0.25), depth of coverage <30X and mapping quality read values <30 were discarded. In addition, visual inspection of reads in the Integrated Genome Viewer [14] was carried out by two independent operators in dubious cases. High quality variants were then considered for subsequent analysis if: (i) were located in the coding region or in canonical splice sites (ii) were absent or rare (i.e., had minor allele frequency -MAF <1%) in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD, http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org) (iii) had a possible biological impact on protein function (i.e., being nonsense, frameshift, affecting canonical splicing, or possibly deleterious missense variants as estimated on the basis of a REVEL score ≥0.750 [15]).

Pathogenicity of the variants identified was assessed according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines for variant interpretation [16].

All variants retrieved after applying the bioinformatic pipeline were verified by Sanger sequencing. Briefly, polymerase chain reaction was used to amplify gene fragments that included the variants of interest. Primers were designed with Primer3 (http://primer3.ut.ee/) and their sequences are available upon request. All obtained amplicons were subjected to direct sequencing in both forward and reverse directions on an automated AB 3130XL Sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) using the ABI Prism BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems). Results were analyzed with the GeneScreen software (http://dna.leeds.ac.uk/genescreen/).

In a secondary analysis, we investigated whether T2D polymorphisms interact with rare variants in modulating their effect on early-onset disease. To this end, among 128 SNPs that were validated in a large meta-analysis of individuals of European ancestry [17], 17 with an OR ≥1.10 for T2D and an effect allele frequency ≥10% were selected, on the basis of a ready available genotyping assay design (Supplementary Table 3). Genotyping was carried out at LGC Genomics (United Kingdom) using KASP (competitive allelic-specific amplification) as reported [18]. Design genotyping assays was obtained for all the selected SNPs. Genotypes call was performed with KlusterCaller™ software (www.lgcgroup.com/genomics). Performance of each SNP assay was quality controlled on each individual plate using SNPViewer (www.lgcgroup.com/genomics). All genotypes were validated after manual inspection of each cluster. All SNPs had a missing call rate < 5% and did not significantly deviate from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE p value <0.05). Finally, the 17 GWAS-derived T2D SNPs were used to create an un-weighted genetic risk score (GRS), constructed by summing the number of risk alleles carried by each patient for each SNP.

Patients’ baseline characteristics were reported as means ± standard deviations and percentages for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Variables with skewed distributions were logarithmically transformed and then rescaled by their standard deviation. Comparisons between continuous variables refer to unpaired t-tests or Mann-Whitney for normally or non-normally distributed variables, respectively and for categorical variables refer to Pearson chi-squared. Deviation from HWE was investigated by exact χ2.

Associations between rare variants (collapsed together into a single burden variable) and early-onset T2D were analyzed by Fisher exact test. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity between the two groups of patients recruited in two different geographical regions was investigated by Cochrane Q-test.

Univariate logistic regression analysis, adjusted by study sample, was used to assess the association between the GRS and early-onset T2D in the whole sample.

The size of the study sample conferred an 80% power (alpha=0.05) to detect an effect of a rare variants on early-onset T2D equivalent to an OR of 1.7 - an effect similar in magnitude to that reported for the association of rare variants in monogenic diabetes-genes with T2D as a whole [10]. This sample size also provided 80% power (alpha 0.05) to detect at an OR of 1.05 per unit increment of the GRS.

All statistical analyses, including study statistical power, were performed using the SAS Software, Release 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Two-sided p-values<0.05 were considered as significant.

The clinical characteristics of the 600 study participants (300 cases with T2D onset ≤35 years, and 300 controls with T2D onset ≥65 years) are shown in Table 1. Due to the study design, the age of T2D onset was markedly different between cases and controls. Cases were also younger and heavier, had worse glycemic control, and were more likely to be on insulin treatment and to have a family history of T2D (Table 1). They also showed lower HDL cholesterol, while controls had a higher prevalence of hypertension, most likely because of older age, (Table 1).

The average read depth across the targeted gene regions in all sequenced samples was 1307.28 (± 1057.78) per base with ≥30 reads for 95.0% of bases, ≥20 reads for 95.7% of bases and ≥10 reads for 96.6% of bases. The fraction of the targeted regions (98.051 bp) covered by at least one read was 98.0%. The average quality of scores across all bases of all mapped reads was 35 (Sanger/Illumina 1.9 encoding) and the mean of mapping quality scores across the targeted gene regions for all samples considered together was 49.

Using our filtering/prioritization pipeline, a total of 51 unique variants were identified (in heterozygous or in homozygous state, Supplementary Table 4) in 85 individuals (51 cases and 34 controls). These included 42 non-synonymous, 6 nonsense, 2 frameshift, and one splicing variants in 18 out of the 27 screened genes (Supplementary Table 4). When all rare and possibly deleterious variants were considered as a single exposure, carriers of one or more variants had a significantly increased risk of early-onset T2D (OR, 95% CI=1.64, 1.08–2.48; p=0.02; Table 2, first line). Notably, no difference in this association was observed when the 600 study subjects were stratified according to the two geographical regions of recruitment: 324 subjects (182 cases vs. 142 controls) from Central-Southern Italy and 276 (118 vs. 158) from the Rome urban area (OR, 95% CI=1.85, 1.03–3.32 and 1.40, 0.72–2.59; p=0.27, respectively; p for heterogeneity=0.53). Among cases, the 51 patients carrying rare variants had lower BMI and higher HDL-cholesterol than non-carriers (Table 3). Interestingly, the enrichment of rare and possibly deleterious variants in cases was progressively greater (p for trend< 0.0001) for classes of variants with progressively lower MAF, up to a greater than a 6-fold increase in the risk of early-onset T2D for ultra-rare variants (MAF <0.001%; Table 2). Also in this case, no evidence of heterogeneity (p=0.843) was observed between subjects from Central-Southern Italy and those from the Rome urban area.

Among the ultra-rare variants, 8 (all found in MODY genes and carried by 9 cases) have been classified as Pathogenic/Likely Pathogenic (P/LP) according to ACMG/AMP guidelines [16] (Supplementary Table 4). This finding raises the possibility that these 9 individuals were in fact MODY cases misdiagnosed as T2D patients. Nonetheless, when these P/LP variants were not considered in the analysis, the association between ultra-rare variants (i.e., MAF<0.001%) and early-onset T2D remained statistically significant: OR, 95% CI=4.00, 1.12–14.93; p=0.03.

The mean ± SD of the 17 SNP-based GRS was 22.5 ± 2.1 (interquartile range 21–24) in cases and 22.0 ± 2.3 (interquartile range 20–24) in controls (p=0.02), with an OR of early-onset T2D of 1.09 (95% CI: 1.01–1.18; p=0.02) per GRS unit increment. No difference in this association was observed when the 600 study subjects were stratified according to the two geographical regions of recruitment (OR, 95% CI: 1.14, 1.02–1.26 and 1.03, 95% CI: 0.92–1.16 in individuals from Central-Southern Italy and the Rome urban area, respectively; p for heterogeneity=0.23)

A significant interaction was observed between SNPs and rare variants, with the allelic GRS being 1.45 (95% CI: 1.15–1.57) in rare variant carriers as compared to 1.06 (95% CI: 1.01–1.13) among non-carriers (p for interaction = 0.02).

In this study we evaluated both the influence of possibly deleterious rare variants in monogenic diabetes-genes and that of T2D-associated SNPs in increasing the risk of early-onset rather than late-onset forms of T2D. To this end, in order to maximize the study statistical power, we used the extreme-phenotype approach [12] by selecting and investigating among ~9500 T2D patients 300 individuals with age at diabetes onset ≤ 35 years (i.e., cases) and 300 counterparts with age at diabetes onset ≥65 years (i.e., controls). We found that, on average, each rare variant increased the odds of early disease manifestation by 64%, but this effect could be even stronger, given that the burden test that we utilized does not take into account the possible protective role of some variants, which may have biased results toward the null hypothesis. Not surprisingly, considering the known role of monogenic diabetes-genes on insulin secretion rather than insulin resistance, early-onset carriers had lower BMI and increased levels of HDL Cholesterol - two markers of increased insulin sensitivity - as compared to early-onset patients not carrying rare variants. Notably, most of the association was driven by ultra-rare variants (MAF<0.001%), each of which, on average, increased the risk of early-onset diabetes more than 6-fold. The increased risk conferred by the ultra-rare variants was only slightly attenuated when the P/LP variants were excluded from the analysis.

Data concerning the impact of rare variants on the age at T2D onset have been sparse and sometimes contradictory [10, 11]. In this context, our findings are quite consistent with those recently reported in a large number of individuals [11] indicating that rare and pathogenic variants in actionable MODY genes are associated with T2D anticipation. Conversely, our data seem to be in contrast with those from a previous report [10], in which no difference in age at T2D onset was observed between carriers and non-carriers of rare variants in monogenic diabetes-genes. A possible explanation for these discrepancies could be the fact that most of the cohorts examined in the latter study [10] comprised individuals whose age at diabetes diagnosis was, by selection, >35 years and/or <60 years. It is conceivable that exclusion of extreme values biased the association with age at diagnosis towards the null hypothesis, thus increasing the risk of false negative results. By contrast, the selection of subjects at the extremes of the age at onset phenotype may have improved the power to detect significant different signals in early vs. late-onset T2D in our study.

It has been recently reported that common variants (i.e. SNPs) known to affect T2D susceptibility are also involved in shaping the risk of disease anticipation [20]. Consistent with this observation, we found that each unit of a GRS based on 17 GWAS-derived T2D SNPs increased the odds of early-onset T2D by 9%. Remarkably, this effect seemed to be quite strong among individuals carrying rare variants while instead approaching a neutral effect in individuals with no rare variants; this suggests that the genetic background of T2D anticipation is based on a synergism of rare and common variants. This hypothesis is supported by a growing body of research, pointing to the possible interaction between common and rare variants in the modulation of the expression of several disease-phenotypes [21–24], including diabetes [24].

This finding, which needs to be confirmed in independent larger studies and possibly by the using of a GRS obtained from a larger number of SNPs, supports the tenet that a better understanding of the genetic background of T2D anticipation is instrumental for the identification of high-risk individuals.

Our study has several strengths. One is the high ethnic and environmental homogeneity of the five Italian cohorts, which may have helped reduce the risk of false negative findings. Another is the thorough clinical characterization of study participants, as indicated by the observation of differences in clinical characteristics between cases and controls that were similar to those reported in larger studies [8], with early-onset T2D being characterized by a more aggressive disease. Finally, the fact that the association of rare and ultra-rare variants in monogenic diabetes-genes with early-onset T2D showed no heterogeneity across geographical regions also make these data robust. However, our study had some limitations. Firstly, due to the relatively small sample size, additional, smaller effects of infrequent variants may have been missed. Secondly, although our findings were very similar in the two geographical strata, the study lacks a formal replication. Thirdly, we investigated rare variants in 27 genes previously associated with monogenic forms of diabetes. It is possible that some patients with early-onset T2D may carry rare variants in other genes that we did not study. Finally, only 17 loci hosting common variants associated with T2D were investigated in our study. This may have altered the real extent to which common variants affect disease anticipation.

In conclusion, our study suggests that rare variants in monogenic diabetes genes act together with common T2D susceptibility variants in determining the risk of an early-onset of T2D. This represents a novel finding which while waiting for replication in larger and independent samples, serves the important function of hypothesis generating.

Gaining further insights into the genes contributing to such predisposing background will further advance the goal of better predicting, and possibly preventing early-onset T2D.
