# SUN5 forms a regular protein lattice reinforcing the sperm head-tail junction.
**Authors:** Moecking J, Doroshev S, Leung MR, Zeev-Ben-Mordehai T
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2520626123](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2520626123)

## Content

Spermatozoa strongly rely on their streamlined morphology to successfully fertilize an oocyte. A striking example of a morphological defect resulting in infertility is acephalic spermatozoa syndrome, a rare but severe condition leading to detachment of the sperm head and tail. Among the most common genetic causes for this syndrome are mutations in the linker of nucleo- and cytoskeleton (LINC) complex component SUN5. LINC complexes typically reside in the nuclear envelope, the double-membrane surrounding the nucleus, where they establish a physical bridge between nucleus and cytoplasm. This localization allows them to transduce mechanical signals from the cytoplasm to the nucleus and to regulate nuclear morphology. In sperm, LINC complexes are essential for a multitude of morphological changes during sperm development, including the reshaping of the nucleus and establishing a stable head-tail junction. Here, using superresolution fluorescence microscopy, we find that sperm-specific SUN5 localizes to the base of the head in human, mouse, and boar sperm. By applying in situ cryoelectron tomography, we find an extensive hexagonal lattice in the nuclear envelope in this region. This lattice appears to maintain a consistent close apposition between the inner and outer nuclear membranes (ONM). Further structural analysis supports a model in which LINC complexes form this lattice by laterally interacting at the ONM. Overall, this study sheds light on nuclear envelope organization in the highly streamlined sperm cell, providing a structural basis for uniform nuclear envelope spacing maintained by a LINC lattice and rationalizing the disruptive effects of SUN5 mutations.

Sperm cells have developed a highly specialized and streamlined morphology, providing the optimal vehicle to deliver paternal DNA to the oocyte. Defects in sperm ultrastructure often have direct consequences for male fertility (1). A rare but severe morphological malformation is the so-called acephalic spermatozoa syndrome (ASS), with the primary phenotype being the presence of detached sperm heads and tails in the ejaculate due to a failure in establishing a stable head–tail junction (2). Because of this severe morphological defect, affected individuals are infertile and must undergo fertility treatment. The most common cause for ASS is mutations of the gene encoding for the protein Sad1 and UNC domain-containing protein 5 (SUN5), being responsible for approximately 30 to 50% of reported cases (1, 3–10).

SUN proteins typically localize to the double membrane surrounding the nucleus, the nuclear envelope (NE), where they are embedded in the inner nuclear membrane (INM). Together with Nuclear envelope spectrin repeat (Nesprin) proteins that are embedded in the outer nuclear membrane (ONM), they form linker of nucleo- and cytoskeleton (LINC) complexes (11). These complexes establish a direct physical connection between elements of the nucleoskeleton, like the nuclear lamina, and elements of the cytoskeleton, like microtubules, and are conserved in yeast, flies, fish, and mammals (11, 12). Their functions range from regulation of nuclear morphology, migration, and polarity to mechanotransduction (12). The fact that out of the five mammalian SUN proteins SUN3, SUN4, and SUN5 as well as the SUN1η isoform are testis-specific, suggests that LINC complexes have a significant role in adapting and maintaining the highly streamlined shape of the sperm nucleus (11, 13–19).

Indeed, SUN3-5 play critical roles in reshaping and positioning the nucleus during different stages of sperm maturation and in forming a stable connection between the sperm head and tail. The earliest developmental stage at which SUN5 is detected is in pachytene spermatocytes in mice (16). During late spermatogenesis, SUN5 localizes to the posterior part of the nucleus and retains this localization in ejaculated sperm (16, 20, 21). Both Nesprin2 and Nesprin3 can interact with SUN5, and the SUN5/Nesprin3 complex was shown to be important for the correct localization of the proximal centriole (PC) (9, 21, 22). Furthermore, knock-out of SUN5 in mice resulted in altered Nesprin3 localization and—similar to the ASS phenotype observed in patients presenting with SUN5 mutations—detachment of the sperm head and tail leading to infertility (16, 20–23).

In contrast to SUN5, SUN3 and SUN4 display different subcellular localization patterns. In mice, SUN3 is expressed during the postmeiotic stages of spermatogenesis, colocalizes with Nesprin1 in mouse spermatids, and interacts with Nesprin1 when overexpressed in HeLa cells (13). Immunofluorescence (IF) experiments showed that SUN3 localizes to the attachment sites between the NE and the manchette, a transient microtubule-based structure crucial for sperm head formation. SUN4 displays a similar expression and localization profile during early spermatogenesis. However, at later stages of spermatogenesis, SUN4 localizes to the posterior pole of the nucleus, whereas SUN3 localizes laterally along the nucleus (13, 17, 24). Moreover, in ejaculated human sperm, SUN4 localizes to the midpiece (25). Sperm from SUN3- and SUN4-knockout mice show loss of attachment between the nucleus and the manchette, resulting in severe malformation of the head and, consequently, infertility (15, 17, 24, 26).

The structure of LINC complexes has thus far mainly been investigated in vitro. Crystal structures of purified SUN domains from SUN1 and SUN2 revealed homotrimers with each protomer bound to a short C-terminal Nesprin peptide (27–29). The region before the SUN domain forms coiled-coils in SUN1 and SUN2 that act as regulators and are important for Nesprin recruitment (30, 31). Based on these findings, a model of a LINC complex was proposed. In this model, the SUN protein is attached to the nucleoskeleton, embedded in the INM, and reaches through the PNS with its trimeric coiled-coil and SUN domains. At the ONM, it binds the very C-terminal end of a Nesprin protein, which is embedded in the ONM and bound to the cytoskeleton with its N-terminal part (27–29). Intriguingly, purified SUN domain trimers can also form higher-order multimers, including dimers of trimers or trimers of trimers (32–34). Furthermore, fluorescence fluctuation spectroscopy analysis of SUN1 and SUN2 in U2OS cells suggests the formation of higher-order oligomers (35). The formation of 2D LINC lattices has been proposed and is supported by molecular dynamics simulations of SUN1 trimers, suggesting that lattice formation could be mediated by direct lateral interactions of SUN domains (27–29, 36). Yet, structures of sperm-specific LINC complexes are not available. Likewise, it has not been determined experimentally whether and how LINC complexes organize into higher-order assemblies in the NE.

Here, we combined superresolution fluorescence microscopy with in situ cryoelectron tomography (cryo-ET) to investigate SUN5 localization, organization, and structure in mature mammalian sperm. Fluorescence microscopy shows that SUN5 is the major LINC component of the NE at the head–tail junction. Cryo-ET of sperm cells thinned with cryofocused ion beam (cryo-FIB) milling reveals a remarkably uniform spacing of ~13 nm between the INM and ONM, at the base of the nucleus. We further find that this uniform membrane spacing is mediated by a regular protein lattice in the PNS. Finally, our in situ structure, combined with AlphaFold predictions, suggests that the lattice is formed by trimeric LINC complexes.

In mammalian sperm, the head and the tail are joined by an elaborate structure called the connecting piece, which consists of several cellular elements that ensure stable coupling while still maintaining a degree of flexibility (37). We used stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy to determine the localization of the testis-specific LINC components SUN4 and SUN5 in the connecting piece of human, boar, and mouse spermatozoa. We find that SUN5 fluorescence is clearly detected at the base of the sperm nucleus in all three species (Fig. 1A and Movie S1). Counterstaining with α-tubulin and DAPI allowed to precisely define the position of SUN5 to be between the PC and the nucleus. In contrast, SUN4 primarily localizes to the midpiece of human and boar sperm (Fig. 1B and Movie S2). This is surprising, as SUN proteins are typically localized to the NE, but is consistent with previous observations (25). In the context of the highly specialized sperm cell, SUN4 may form a noncanonical LINC complex, potentially involved in the correct positioning of mitochondria and stabilization of the midpiece. Interestingly, in mouse sperm, SUN4 signal localizes to the connecting piece around the PC like SUN5 (Fig. 1B). The apparent differential localization of SUN4 in the mouse compared to human and boar could be due to species-specific differences or may result from comparing different maturation stages as mouse sperm were collected from the cauda epididymis while human and boar sperm were collected from ejaculates. Overall, our fluorescence-based analysis strongly suggests that SUN5 is the main LINC complex component at the base of the nucleus in human and boar sperm.

To determine how SUN5-based LINC complexes are organized in the NE of human sperm, we used cellular cryo-ET. Imaging the sperm connecting piece with cryo-ET requires thinning by cryo-FIB milling, as this part of the cell is ~800 nm thick in human sperm (for details, see Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–C). Tomograms collected at the base of the head reveal common features of the sperm connecting piece, including the densely packed nuclear chromatin, the basal plate (BP) tightly aligning with the cytoplasmic side of the NE, as well as the segmented columns (SC) and parts of the PC (Fig. 2 A and B). The BP and SC consist of electron-dense material without distinct structural features, similar to previous observations (38, 39). Intriguingly, we observed prominent repetitive protein densities in the NE along the BP (Fig. 2B and Movie S3). Orthogonal views of the ONM reveal that the regular densities form a striking two-dimensional lattice at the perinuclear space (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). At the INM, a similar protein lattice is present, albeit less pronounced (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and F). A regular protein array at the NE of sperm from guinea pig and rat was previously described in freeze-fracture studies (40). Remarkably, the protein lattice we observe here is limited to the region at which the NE integrates with the sperm connecting piece, like the SUN5 fluorescence signal. At sites where the NE bends toward the postacrosomal sheath (PAS) region, we instead observe clusters of nuclear pore complexes (NPC), consistent with previous findings (41) (Fig. 2C and Movie S4). Furthermore, the lattice is not observed in the NE near the PAS region and acrosome (Acr) (Fig. 2 D and E). Within the PAS region, regularly arranged membrane protein densities occur outside the NE as was previously described for boar sperm (42).

Inspecting multiple tomograms capturing the NE along the base of the sperm nucleus indicated that the spacing between INM and ONM is strikingly uniform and tight (13.19 ± 1.25 nm) when the protein lattice is present (Fig. 3 A and F). Of note, the membrane spacing observed here is significantly shorter than the membrane spacing reported for other cell types, for example, Caenorhabditis elegans (~50 nm), Xenopus oocytes (~30 nm), and yeast (~25 nm) (43–45). Interestingly, a recent study reported even tighter membrane spacing in the human sperm NE (~9 nm) for areas distal from NPC and wider spacing (~27 nm) in areas closer to NPCs (41). In contrast to the membrane spacing observed at the base of the head, we find a spacing of ~5.5 nm near the PAS region and Acr (Fig. 2 D and E). This indicates that the LINC lattice is involved in maintaining a uniform membrane spacing of ~13 nm along the human sperm connecting piece. LINC complexes have previously been hypothesized to serve as “molecular rulers” and to stabilize uniform membrane spacing of the NE depending on the lengths of their coiled-coil domains (46–48). Functionally, the regulation of membrane spacing by LINC complexes is crucial for ensuring nuclear morphology in cells exposed to physical stress, as has been shown in force-bearing muscle cells in C. elegans (43). As the connecting piece of sperm cells must withstand forces exerted by flagellar beating, the protein array observed at the NE may play a role in maintaining nuclear morphology and attachment to the tail.

Interestingly, we also observed areas with irregular membrane spacing and bulging of the ONM in 23 out of 44 tomograms and vesicles were located on the cytoplasmic side near the bulging ONM in three tomograms (Fig. 3 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Consistent with the observation that the uniform membrane spacing coincides with the protein lattice, its presence is drastically reduced at sites of membrane bulging. Moreover, the BP was interrupted at sites where membrane bulging occurred, indicating that the lattice may also be tethering the BP to the NE. On the cytoplasmic side, we find filaments extending from the BP to the upper part of the SC, sometimes referred to as the capitulum (Fig. 3C), which suggests a molecular network coupling the SC, BP, and the nucleus.

As our fluorescence data indicated that SUN5 is also present in the connecting piece of boar and mouse sperm, we next imaged the nucleus along the connecting piece of sperm from these species. Like our in situ data from human sperm, the tomograms show a regular protein lattice along the NE that appears to maintain uniform membrane spacing (Fig. 3 D–F). Moreover, filaments like those observed in human sperm connecting the BP to the upper part of the SC are present in both boar and mouse sperm. This implies that despite the significant differences in head morphology between these three species, the molecular mechanism of head–tail attachment, including the LINC lattice in the NE, is conserved.

To analyze the organization of the lattice observed along the NE at the base of the sperm head, we used subtomogram averaging and resolved the structure of the LINC lattice in human sperm to 13.7 Å (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D; for details see Materials and Methods). Our structure reveals a regular protein network organized in a hexagonal pattern along the ONM (Fig. 4 A and B). Likewise, regularly spaced densities are also apparent at the INM, directly opposing the ONM densities. Similar structural analyses for boar and mouse sperm resulted in comparable hexagonal lattice structures along the ONM and INM, substantiating that the lattice is conserved in mammalian sperm (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). The 3D reconstructions of the lattice from the different species further show a spacing between ONM and INM of ~13 nm, consistent with the direct measurements in the tomograms (Figs. 3F and 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B).

According to the hypothesized LINC complex topology, the lattice observed at the ONM corresponds to SUN domains. Crystal structures of SUN1 and SUN2 in complex with a Nesprin C-terminus revealed the formation of SUN domain trimers as well as different oligomeric assemblies of these (32–34). This raises the question of which oligomeric state the SUN domain adopts in the context of the sperm NE. To determine the oligomeric state, we attempted fitting available crystal structures of the different SUN domain assemblies into our reconstruction from human sperm. Only a single SUN domain trimer produced a good fit for the ONM density (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). In contrast, dimeric or trimeric assemblies of SUN domain trimers are significantly larger than the resolved density, leading us to conclude that the lattice observed at the ONM is formed by SUN trimers (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C).

LINC complexes have previously been proposed to act as “molecular rulers,” dictating membrane spacing via their coiled-coil domains. To investigate which SUN proteins could promote membrane spacing of ~13 nm, we next used sequence analysis tools to identify the PNS-localized portion of the five human SUN proteins. Subsequently, this region was mapped to an AlphaFold3-predicted structure of the corresponding trimeric SUN protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–D). In agreement with previous findings suggesting that SUN1 and SUN2 maintain membrane spacing at 40 to 50 nm, predictions of SUN1 and SUN2 display a significantly longer coiled-coil region (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E) (47, 48). Fitting a model composed of just the PNS-localized portion of SUN5 into our subtomogram average demonstrates that the lengths of the SUN-domain trimer and coiled-coil match perfectly the observed spacing between INM and ONM (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). While similar AlphaFold3 predictions of SUN3 and SUN4 also fit with the observed membrane spacing, the fluorescence data clearly indicate that SUN5 is the major SUN protein found at the base of the sperm head.

As the lattice along the ONM also displays clear density extending toward the membrane (Fig. 4 A and B), we use AlphaFold3 to predict a trimeric model of the PNS-localized portion of SUN5, including the C-terminal SUN-binding part and transmembrane helix of Nesprin3 (residues 918 to 957) as well as fatty acids to mimic the ONM in the predicted structure (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). In the resulting model, the C-terminal peptide of Nesprin3 intercalates tightly into the SUN5 SUN domain trimer, consistent with observations from crystal structures of SUN1 and SUN2 with different Nesprins (Fig. 4C). Fitting multiple copies of this model into our subtomogram average shows that the ONM density matches the SUN5 SUN domain trimer well. It also demonstrates that SUN domain trimers can form the observed hexameric assemblies by direct lateral interactions (Fig. 4 D and E). Based on our structure, the intertrimer spacing is 7.2 nm (center-to-center), allowing individual SUN domains to directly interact. Moreover, the observed membrane spacing concurs with the length of the SUN5 coiled-coil (Fig. 4D). Taken together, our structural analysis reveals a conserved hexagonal lattice in the NE at the base of the mammalian sperm head, which is formed by trimerization of SUN domains that directly interact laterally.

Here, we identify a regular protein lattice along the NE of human sperm. The lattice is limited to the base of the sperm head, correlating with the fluorescence signal of the sperm-specific LINC component SUN5 (Figs. 1 and 2). Furthermore, we demonstrate that the LINC lattice is conserved in mouse and boar sperm and maintains a narrow spacing between INM and ONM of ~13 nm (Fig. 3). Using subtomogram averaging, we provide an in situ structure of a LINC lattice, showing that it is formed by trimeric assemblies arranged into a hexagonal lattice (Fig. 4). Considering the accepted role of LINC complexes in bridging the nucleo- and cytoskeleton, the LINC complex lattice in sperm seems to provide a bridge between the nuclear lamina and the BP in sperm cells (Fig. 5).

Interestingly, an earlier study using freeze-fracturing EM describes a lattice of “large particles” with a diameter of 15 to 25 nm along the ONM with a spacing of approximately 20 nm in guinea pig and rat sperm (40). A possible explanation for the discrepancy in the observed lattice parameters can be the harsh sample preparation procedure applied in freeze-fracturing EM. In agreement with our results, a recent preprint reports a hexagonal lattice in human sperm with similar spacing as observed in our structure, and also concludes that the lattice is formed by SUN5/Nesprin3 trimers (49). This study further combines molecular dynamics simulations with in situ cryo-ET and AlphaFold3-predicted structures to analyze the effect of the lattice on membrane bending and to further investigate the lateral interaction between SUN5 SUN domain trimers. Curiously, our AlphaFold3-predicted structure of full-length SUN5 showed an unstructured C-terminal region extending from the SUN domain, which might be involved in establishing the lateral interactions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D). However, these extensions are not resolved in our structure, and additional disruption or mutagenesis studies will be required to determine their structural role in lattice formation.

In our structure, three clear densities connect each SUN5 trimer to the ONM (Fig. 4). The resolution of our structure prevents an unambiguous assignment of the interacting protein. Nevertheless, we assigned these densities to Nesprin3, the most probable candidate to interact with SUN5 in ejaculated sperm, and thus in the context of the LINC lattice (9, 22). However, it should be noted that another study described the formation of a SUN5/Nesprin2 complex in the mouse and human during the meiosis stages of spermatogenesis (21). Binding of different Nesprin proteins to the same SUN protein has been observed before (33), yet how Nesprin binding to SUN5 is regulated during different stages of sperm maturation is unclear. Potentially, SUN5 binds to Nesprin2 in earlier stages of spermatogenesis for correct translocation and to Nesprin3 to form the lattice observed in mature sperm.

Apart from Nesprin3 and Nesprin2, other proteins have been reported to directly interact with SUN5, including CENTLEIN and Septin12. CENTLEIN is a protein that localizes to the proximal ends of centrioles and is required for centriole cohesion in U2OS and RPE-1 cells (50). Direct interaction between CENTLEIN and SUN5 was shown during spermatogenesis in the mouse to bridge the interaction with PMFBP1 (51). However, as CENTLEIN is a cytoplasmic protein and the direct interaction with SUN5 was only shown during spermatogenesis, it seems unlikely that CENTLEIN is directly involved in the NE lattice. Septin12, a testis-specific cytoskeletal GTPase that may restrict membrane protein diffusion at the NE, directly interacts with SUN5 in immunoprecipitation experiments and colocalizes with SUN5 in epididymal mouse sperm (23, 41). Septin12 depletion or mutation causes morphological defects in all subcellular compartments, resulting in infertility (52–54). Septin12 further localizes to the perinuclear space (41), where it may be part of the repetitive unassigned density at the INM observed in our structure, which appears to follow a similar hexagonal pattern as observed at the ONM. Alternatively, both Septin12 and CENTLEIN may be involved in regulating the correct translocation of SUN5/Nesprin3 complexes during the formation of the lattice.

Based on the observation that the LINC lattice is absent in areas displaying ONM bulging and interrupted BP (Fig. 3), we speculate that one function of the LINC lattice is in tethering the two nuclear membranes to each other like “molecular velcro.” Thereby the lattice maintains the stable, uniform and narrow membrane spacing of ~13 nm (Fig. 3). Moreover, our study supports the notion of an extensive molecular network spanning from the nuclear lamina through the nuclear envelope all the way to the SC (Fig. 5). The hexagonal 2D lattice observed along the NE would be at the center of this network, forming a robust connection between the nucleo- and cytoskeleton. On the nuclear side, the N-terminal region of the LINC complex connects to Lamin B1 (23, 55). The major portion of Nesprin (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B) would localize to the cytoplasm and could be part of the BP. Finally, filaments connect the BP to the upper portion of the SC. An interesting question is whether this interaction network mainly functions as a structural reinforcement of the sperm head–tail coupling apparatus or could also have a role beyond that, for example, in mechanotransduction. A possible role in mechanotransduction could be the induction of chromatin remodeling upon capacitation and subsequent changes in motility. Increased motility may lead to increased movement of the SC, which could be transmitted to the nucleus via the proposed network (37). Chromatin remodeling was observed previously in capacitated human sperm (56), however, the mechanism of how this remodeling is induced remains unknown. Further investigation of early developmental sperm stages, like spermatids, will be instrumental to untangle the precise molecular composition, assembly mechanism, and functional role of the proposed network in sperm development and potentially signal transduction.

In summary, our study reveals an in situ structure of LINC complexes assembled into a continuous hexagonal lattice. The data indicate that this lattice is formed by SUN5/Nesprin3 trimers, which interact laterally via the trimeric SUN domain. Considering the extensive interaction network described above, our results present an explanation on how SUN5 deficiency or mutations cause severe morphological malformations resulting in ASS and male infertility (1, 2, 10, 57).

Ejaculated human donor sperm was received fully deidentified either frozen in straws or fresh from the Utrecht University Medical Center (protocol number 21-754). Frozen sperm was stored in liquid nitrogen or nitrogen vapor until used and fresh sperm was used immediately. Frozen sperm was thawed for 30 s in a 37 °C water bath immediately before the start of an experiment. Subsequently, the sperm sample was layered onto a discontinuous two-step Percoll gradient (35%, 70%). Percoll dilutions were prepared in HBS buffer [20 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 137 mM NaCl, 10 mM glucose, 2.5 mM KCl]. The gradient was centrifuged for 10 min at 300 g followed by another centrifugation step for 20 min at 750 g to isolate motile sperm. The pelleted motile sperm was resuspended in DPBS, the cell concentration determined in a counting chamber and the cells diluted to the desired concentration.

Boar sperm was obtained commercially (Varkens KI Nederland) and received and stored at 18 °C in an extender solution. Processing of the sample was carried out within 24 h after delivery of the sample. Boar sperm was applied to a Percoll gradient and further processed as described for human sperm.

Mouse (Mus musculus) sperm was isolated from the cauda region of the epididymis of C57BL/6 mice as previously described (58). Caudal mouse sperm was kept in a modified Biggers, Whitten, and Whittingham media (BWW: 20 mM HEPES, 91.5 mM NaCl, 4.6 mM KCl, 1.7 mM D-glucose, 0.27 mM sodium pyruvate, 44 mM sodium lactate, 5 U/mL penicillin, and 5 µg/mL streptomycin, adjusted to pH 7.4 and an osmolarity of 300 mOsm/kg) and was washed by centrifugation at 400 g for 15 min through a 27% Percoll layer followed by another centrifugation (400 g, 2 min) to remove excess Percoll.

Human sperm samples were diluted to 10 to 20 × 106 cells/mL in DPBS or DPBS containing 5% glycerol. Mouse and boar sperm samples were diluted to 20 to 30 × 106 cells/mL. 3.0 to 3.5 µL of the diluted cell suspension was loaded onto a glow-discharged Quantifoil R2/1 or R2/2 200 mesh holey carbon grid. The grids were blotted from the back for 5 to 6 s before plunging into liquid ethane cooled to liquid nitrogen temperature. Plunge-freezing was carried out using a manual plunger (MPI Martinsried) or an EM GP2 Automatic Plunge Freezer (Leica). Afterward, grids were clipped into Autogrids (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for mechanical support and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Grids were loaded into an Aquilos (Thermo Fisher Scientific) cryofocused ion beam/scanning electron microscope. Grids were mapped and monitored during milling using the SEM at 2 kV and 13 pA. Grid maps were collected before and after deposition of platinum GIS for 6 to 8 s. Subsequently, grids were sputter coated with platinum (1 kV, 30 mA, 10 Pa, and 10 s). Targeting with the FIB was performed at 30 kV and 1.5 or 10 pA. Samples were milled at an effective milling angle of 11 to 12° in four consecutive steps: a rough milling step at 0.5 or 1 nA, including milling of microexpansions for stress-relief (59), an intermediate milling step at 0.1 or 0.3 nA, a fine milling step at 50 pA or 0.1 nA and a polishing step at 30 pA. Polished lamellae were stored in liquid nitrogen until imaging.

All data were collected on a 200 kV Talos Arctica (Thermo Fisher Scientific) microscope equipped with a postcolumn energy filter (Gatan) and a K2 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan). Data collection was carried out in zero-loss imaging mode with a 20 eV slit width using SerialEM (60). For lamellae from human sperm, images were acquired following a grouped dose-symmetric tilt scheme covering a range from −48° to 48° or −56° to 56° in 4° increments and a maximum total dose of ~100 e−/Å2. Initial overview tilt series were collected at a pixel size of 3.57 Å and a target defocus of −6 µm and tilt series for subtomogram averaging were collected at a pixel size of 2.17 Å and a target defocus of −3 to −5 µm.

Mouse and boar sperm data were collected similarly but with an effective tilt range of −56° to 56° or −54° to 54° with a 2° or 3° tilt increment, respectively. The defocus was set between −4 and −6 µm and frames were recorded at a pixel size of 2.17 Å (boar sperm) or 2.83 Å (mouse sperm).

For human sperm data, individual frames were aligned and tomograms reconstructed using AreTomo3 (2.2.2) (61). For segmentation and visualization, four times binned tomograms were subsequently denoised with cryo-CARE 0.3.0 (62). For mouse and boar sperm frames were aligned on-the-fly using WARP and tomograms were reconstructed using IMOD 4.12.62 by weighted backprojection and CTF-corrected using ctfphaseflip (63, 64).

Tomograms (bin4, pixel size 8.68 Å) of the human sperm connecting piece were imported into Dynamo and particles were seeded at the NE by creating a surface model along the ONM above the BP (~27 K particles). Subtomograms were extracted with a box size of 60 nm3 and an initial reference was generated by averaging 50 random particles without alignment. Subtomogram averaging was carried out using a spherical mask with a diameter of 54 nm and with a 30° cone aperture and a 160° azimuth rotation range. This revealed a striking hexagonal lattice particularly pronounced at the ONM. Subsequently, tomograms and aligned particle coordinates were imported into Relion 5.0 (65). Particles were re-extracted (bin2, pixel size 4.34 Å) with a similar box size and subsequently C6 symmetry expanded (~161 K particles). After recentering the particles on a SUN trimer, particles were re-extracted and duplicate particles removed setting the minimum interparticle spacing to 4.5 nm (51 K particles). Subsequently, particles were iteratively refined with C3 symmetry applied and bad particles removed with 3D classification using a mask focused on the ONM lattice and resulting in 15,488 particles for the final refinement. Before the final refinement, another round of duplicate removal (minimum interparticle spacing 6 nm) was carried out. Gold-standard FSC was determined using the PostProcess job of Relion 5.0 providing a similar mask as used for the final refinement, giving a resolution of 13.7 Å. The final map was sharpened with a b-factor of −100 for visualization.

Subtomogram averaging of mouse and boar sperm data was carried out in PEET (1.18.0) with missing wedge compensation (66, 67). Positions for subtomogram extraction were defined by manually placing two model points per particle, one at the ONM and one at the INM (bin4, pixel size 8.68 Å). The stalkInit function of PEET was used to generate initial motive list and rotation axis files, defining the initial particle orientation. Subtomograms of approximately 60 nm × 40 nm × 60 nm were extracted and computationally aligned. For the alignment, a cylindrical soft-edged mask with a height of ~30 nm and a radius of ~30 nm was applied and a similar masking strategy was applied during subsequent steps. Based on the known orientation of the particles relative to the two membranes, the initial alignment allowed for a search range of 120° around the y axis (defined to be perpendicular to the membranes) and 30° search ranges around the x and z axes. After initial alignments, particle positions were re-extracted and refined at a pixel size of 4.34 Å (boar) or 5.66 Å (mouse).

Gold-standard FSC was determined by splitting the dataset into even and odd particles, running separate alignments and using simpleFSC with the same mask applied as during the alignment. Further details on cryo-ET data collection and subtomogram averaging are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Generally, cryo-CARE denoised tomograms were used for segmentation and visualization. The nuclear envelope was segmented using Membrain v2 and the resulting segmentation manually cleaned in UCSF ChimeraX (68, 69). The BP was segmented manually using the drawing tools in IMOD and the resulting model was smoothened using mtffilter. To segment the nuclear envelope densities, the model files produced for particle extraction in subtomogram averaging after refinement were loaded in the ArtiaX plugin in ChimeraX and displayed as spheres (70). Segmentations were combined and visualized in ChimeraX. Representative tomogram slices were captured with IMOD.

Distances were measured in IMOD by placing two model points at the distance supposed to be measured. At least 20 measurement points per tomogram were placed to derive the intermembrane spacing directly from 43, 7 or 2 different tomograms for human, mouse or boar sperm, respectively. Intermembrane spacing from subtomogram averages was measured similarly, with a minimum of 8 measurements along the membrane. Points were placed directly on the PNS-facing lipid layer. Spacing of individual densities within the lattice was derived by placing pairs of points spanning from the center of one trimer to the center of the neighboring trimer for the ONM densities. Distances were obtained by running imodinfo on the corresponding model files and plotted using GraphPad Prism.

For SUN5, three copies of the full-length protein (1-379, UniProt: Q8TC36) were submitted for structure prediction by AlphaFold3, resulting in the successful prediction of a trimer (71). For structure prediction of Nesprin3, a single copy of the full-length protein (1-975, Uniprot: Q6ZMZ3) was submitted. DeepTMHMM was used to predict the transmembrane helices of all human SUN proteins (72). Based on the determined transmembrane helices AlphaFold3 was used to predict the structure of trimers of only the PNS-localized portion of all five human SUN proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The resulting models were fitted in ChimeraX, orienting the SUN domains based on the density at the ONM. For the prediction of the SUN5 trimer in complex with Nesprin3, only residues 121-379 (SUN5) and residues 918-975 (Nesprin-3) were submitted for prediction. This prediction job also included 50 molecules of each palmitic acid and oleic acid to mimic the ONM. As residues 365 to 379 appeared unstructured in the prediction of both full-length SUN5 and in the SUN5/Nesprin3 complex, they were omitted in Fig. 4 for visualization purposes. All models were visualized and assessed in ChimeraX.

For IF staining of sperm cells, #1.5 coverslips (CARL ROTH, 53586) were precoated with 200 µL poly-L-lysine (Sigma, P4832) and incubated for 15 min, then excess liquid was removed and the coverslips were allowed to dry. Sperm samples were thawed and washed, after which 100 to 200 µL of the cell suspension was pipetted onto the coated coverslips and incubated for 30 to 60 min to allow cell adhesion. Excess liquid was then removed, and fixation was performed by adding 200 µL of 4% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific, FB002), incubating for 15 min at room temperature (RT) under a fume hood, followed by two washes with 400 µL of DPBS. Permeabilization was carried out by incubating the coverslips in 300 µL of DPBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min at RT, followed by two additional washes with 400 µL of DPBS. Blocking was performed by incubating the coverslips in 400 µL of blocking solution (DPBS with 10% BSA) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibody staining was conducted by incubating the coverslips overnight at 4 °C in 300 µL of blocking solution containing the following primary antibodies: SUN5 (Proteintech, 17495-1-AP) at 1:100 for human and 1:50 for mouse, primary conjugated Alexa 488 alpha Tubulin Monoclonal Antibody (B-5-1-2) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 32-2500) at 1:250, and SUN4 (Proteintech, 19721-1-AP) at 1:100 for human and 1:50 for mouse. After primary antibody incubation, the coverslips were washed three times for 5 min each with 400 µL of washing solution (DPBS + 0.05% Tween-20) on a gentle shaker. Subsequently, coverslips were incubated for 2 h at RT with gentle shaking in 300 µL of secondary antibody solution, which included anti-rabbit Alexa 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21207) at 1:200 and DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #62248) at 1:1,000. After incubation, coverslips were washed three times for 5 min each with 400 µL of washing solution to ensure thorough removal of unbound antibodies. Coverslips were then carefully lifted and allowed to dry for a few minutes before mounting. A drop of mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P36965) was applied to a glass slide, and the dried coverslip was placed onto it, with the sample facing the mounting medium. Mounted samples were stored at 4 °C for 24 h before imaging.

All confocal and STED imaging was performed on a commercial Abberior Infinity Line easy3D STED system built on an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope with autofocus. Samples were imaged using a 60× 1.42 NA UPLXAPO60XO oil immersion objective. A 405 nm continuous-wave laser provided excitation for DAPI. For Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 594, excitation was provided by pulsed lasers at 488 nm and 561 nm, respectively, both operating at 40 MHz. STED imaging used a pulsed 775 nm depletion laser (2.75 W, 40 MHz). Fluorescence was detected with a spectral detection unit comprising a MATRIX detector and avalanche photodiodes, using appropriate emission windows for each dye. Imspector software (Abberior Instruments) was used to control the setup and acquire images.

For all fixed cell experiments, DAPI and Alexa Fluor 488 were recorded in confocal mode using 405 nm continuous wave and 488 nm pulsed excitation, whereas Alexa Fluor 594 was recorded in STED mode using 561 nm pulsed excitation together with the 775 nm depletion laser. Emission was collected between 415 nm and 478 nm for DAPI, 498 and 551 nm for Alexa Fluor 488, and 588 and 698 nm for Alexa Fluor 594. Time-gated detection was applied to the STED channel with a gate delay of 0.75 ns and a gate width of 8 ns. The pinhole was set to 1 Airy unit for all channels. Excitation powers were 1 to 5% (405 nm), 3 to 8% (488 nm), and 9 to 16% (561 nm), with depletion at 10 to 15% of the maximum output; powers were empirically adjusted to balance signal and photobleaching and held constant within each experiment. Images were acquired as two-dimensional optical sections with pixel sizes of 10 to 20 nm in the xy plane, pixel dwell times of 50 to 80 µs, and one to five line accumulations, with higher line accumulation used for the STED channel. Image acquisition was performed with Imspector, and images were exported as TIFF files.
