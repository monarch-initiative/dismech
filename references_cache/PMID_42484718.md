# Neuroimaging in FDXR-related mitochondriopathy.
**Authors:** Lam A, Beutler BD, Hegde SS, Barkovich MJ, Zamary AR, Torres FA
**Journal:** Pediatr Radiol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00247-026-06734-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00247-026-06734-8)

## Content

Ferredoxin reductase (FDXR)-related mitochondriopathy is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the FDXR gene. The clinical presentation is highly variable, ranging from isolated sensory neuropathies to severe infantile-onset encephalopathy with early demise. Neuroimaging is typically normal. However, oxidative stress in the setting of systemic infection can precipitate severe symptoms with distinct imaging manifestations, including confluent white matter signal abnormality, longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions, and optic nerve enhancement. We describe a previously healthy and asymptomatic 2-year-old female with genetically confirmed FDXR-related mitochondriopathy who was found to have a distinct pattern of diffusion restriction, rapid parenchymal atrophy, and optic nerve changes following a respiratory illness. Early recognition and management of this rare but potentially devastating entity can help prevent vision loss and improve functional outcomes.

Abstract
Ferredoxin reductase (FDXR)-related mitochondriopathy is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic 
variants in the FDXR gene. The clinical presentation is highly variable, ranging from isolated sensory neuropathies to 
severe infantile-onset encephalopathy with early demise. Neuroimaging is typically normal. However, oxidative stress in 
the setting of systemic infection can precipitate severe symptoms with distinct imaging manifestations, including confluent 
white matter signal abnormality, longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions, and optic nerve enhancement. We describe a 
previously healthy and asymptomatic 2-year-old female with genetically confirmed FDXR-related mitochondriopathy who 
was found to have a distinct pattern of diffusion restriction, rapid parenchymal atrophy, and optic nerve changes following a 
respiratory illness. Early recognition and management of this rare but potentially devastating entity can help prevent vision 
loss and improve functional outcomes.

Introduction
Ferredoxin reductase (FDXR)-related mitochondriopathy 
is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic 
pathogenic variants in the FDXR gene. The disorder is char-
acterized by a variable phenotype with optic nerve atrophy 
being the most distinct and consistent feature; developmen-
tal delay, hypotonia, ataxia, and sensorineural hearing loss 
may occur with variable severity and frequency [1 –4].The 
imaging features of FDXR-related mitochondriopathy have 
been incompletely characterized, with most reported cases 
showing either a normal brain MRI or nonspecific findings, 
such as cerebral volume loss and optic nerve atrophy [1, 2].
We present a case of genetically confirmed FDXR-related 
mitochondriopathy demonstrating the evolution from normal 
neuroimaging to Leigh syndrome-like basal ganglia involve-
ment that was precipitated by a viral respiratory infection.

A contrast-enhanced MRI of the brain and orbits 
demonstrated no abnormality. The patient returned at 
6 months of age. Neurologic examination revealed new 
central hypotonia, impaired balance, and diminished 
strength. Correlation of the clinical history and neurologic 
examination raised concern for an underlying neurogenetic 
or metabolic disorder, prompting comprehensive genetic 
testing. Whole exome sequencing subsequently identified 
a homozygous pathogenic missense variant, c.1156C>T 
(p.R386W), in the FDXR gene, confirming the diagnosis of 
FDXR-related mitochondrial disease (OMIM 619333). Both 
parents were confirmed heterozygous carriers.
Neurologic symptoms gradually progressed between 
6 months and 2 years of age, characterized by progressive feed-
ing intolerance, poor weight gain, constipation, urinary reten-
tion, and progressive visual decline. Ophthalmologic examina-
tions revealed severe, progressive optic nerve atrophy, resulting 
in complete blindness by 2 years of age. However, repeat MRI 
examinations of the brain during this interval remained nor-
mal. The patient developed new symptoms between 2 years 
and 2.5 years of age, including sleep disturbances, irritability, 
and episodes concerning for seizure activity.
At 2.5 years of age, the patient was hospitalized with 
acute respiratory failure following sequential COVID-19 and 
human metapneumovirus (HMPV) infections complicated 
by secondary methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus 
(MSSA) pneumonia. Intubation was required on the first 
hospital day for escalating respiratory distress and hypox-
emia. During the hospitalization, the patient demonstrated 
marked neurologic decline, characterized by progressive 
hypotonia, diminished deep tendon reflexes, and complete 
loss of the gag reflex. Differential considerations included 
hypoxic-ischemic injury, acute ischemic infarct, and toxic-
metabolic encephalopathy. An MRI of the brain with and 
without contrast was acquired to assess for an underlying 
cause of the rapidly worsening neurologic status.
The MRI showed a striking symmetric pattern of restricted 
diffusion with associated T2/FLAIR hyperintensity involving 
the globi pallidi and extending inferiorly through the cerebral 
peduncles into the substantia nigra of the midbrain (Fig. 1). 
Additional findings included marked atrophy of the optic 
nerves, optic chiasm, and optic tracts (Fig. 2), as well as mod-
erate diffuse cerebral volume loss that was new or markedly 
progressed compared with MRI performed 6 months earlier.

Discussion
FDXR‑related mitochondriopathy
FDXR-related mitochondriopathy is an autosomal recessive 
disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the FDXR 
gene, which encodes ferredoxin reductase, a mitochondrial 
flavoprotein located in the inner mitochondrial membrane 
[1]. Fewer than 50 cases have been reported worldwide 
[1–3]. The clinical spectrum ranges from isolated sensorial 
neuropathies to severe infantile-onset encephalopathy with 
early demise [2, 4].
The clinical phenotype of FDXR-related mitochondriopathy 
is highly variable. Optic nerve atrophy is the most consist-
ent feature and may be present in up to 89% of patients [3 ]. 
Notably, optic nerve atrophy represents a common downstream 
consequence of mitochondrial dysfunction and is not specific 
to FDXR-related disease, as similar findings are observed 
across multiple mitochondrial optic neuropathies, including 
Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and dominant optic atrophy.
The neuroimaging features of FDXR-related mitochon-
driopathy have been incompletely described in the literature. 
Individuals with genetically-confirmed FDXR-related mito-
chondriopathy have been shown to exhibit either a normal 
brain MRI or nonspecific findings, such as cerebral atrophy 
and optic nerve atrophy [1, 2]. Limited data and case series 
describe severe optic nerve atrophy, confluent white matter 
signal abnormality, and longitudinally extensive spinal cord 
lesions. To the best of our knowledge, our case is the first in 
which neuroimaging findings evolved from essentially nor-
mal to a distinct pattern of pronounced diffusion restriction

The clinical and imaging features of FDXR-related mito-

thies, including Leigh syndrome, POLG-related disorders, 
mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and 
stroke-like episodes (MELAS), and leukoencephalopathy 
with brainstem and spinal cord involvement and lactate 
elevation (LBSL) (Table 1).
Leigh syndrome
The hallmark neuroimaging feature of Leigh syndrome is 
symmetric T2/FLAIR-hyperintense signal involving the 
putamina with relative sparing of the caudate nuclei. Dif-
fusion restriction may be present in the hyperacute or acute 
phase. Progressive disease is characterized by T2/FLAIR 
signal abnormality extending to the globi pallidi, thalami, 
periaqueductal gray matter, tectal plate, and dorsal pons and 
pontomedullary junction. Cavitation can be seen in advanced 
cases. The imaging findings in the appropriate clinical con-
text are relatively specific. However, MR spectroscopy may 
be of value in equivocal cases and will classically reveal a 
markedly elevated lactate peak affecting the basal ganglia or 
brainstem. Genetic testing is required for confirmation [5 ].
POLG‑related disorders
The neuroimaging manifestations of POLG-related disorders 
vary based on the specific phenotype, which is determined by 
a combination of mutation-specific effects on mitochondrial 
DNA maintenance, inherited allelic combinations, and 
tissue vulnerability to mitochondrial dysfunction. Alpers-
Huttenlocher syndrome is classically characterized by a 
distinct pattern of T2/FLAIR signal abnormality involving 
the occipital cortices and thalami. Alpers-Huttenlocher 
syndrome is also associated with liver failure, and thus 
symmetric subthalamic T1 shortening involving the globi 
pallidi, a known manifestation of hepatic encephalopathy, 
is commonly observed. Neuroimaging features of adult-
onset POLG-related disorders are often more subtle and 
characterized by slowly progressive cerebellar-predominant 
neurodegeneration rather than acute lesions [6 ].
MELAS
The neuroimaging features of MELAS are unique among 
other mitochondriopathies. Acute-phase findings include 
cortical and subcortical expansile T2/FLAIR-hyperintense 
lesions in a predominantly parieto-occipital and parieto-
temporal distribution. However, MELAS demonstrates a 
distinct migratory pattern, characterized by lesion migra-
tion and evolution over days to weeks, leaving behind cor -
tical laminar necrosis following resolution. In addition, in

LBSL
The neuroimaging features of LBSL are virtually 
pathognomonic. A diagnosis can be established in the 
appropriate clinical context if the patient demonstrates a 
unique pattern of T2/FLAIR-hyperintense signal involving 
the periventricular and deep white matter, dorsal columns 
and lateral corticospinal tracts, and pyramids and/or 
decussation of the medial lemniscus. Diffusion restriction 
may be present along the margins of the white matter signal 
abnormality. The presence of profound cerebral atrophy in 
infancy with tortuous cortical vessels is also suggestive of 
underlying LBSL, but somewhat nonspecific [8].
Treatment
Treatment for FDXR-related mitochondriopathy remains 
largely supportive. Regular ophthalmologic monitoring 
for progressive optic atrophy and retinopathy, audiology 
assessment for sensorineural hearing loss, and neurologic 
follow-up for developmental regression and seizures are 
recommended [1–3].
In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of 
recognizing FDXR-related mitochondriopathy as a cause 
of Leigh-like basal ganglia involvement and documents 
the potential for infection-triggered acute neurologic 
decompensation with evolution from normal to abnormal 
neuroimaging. Serial neuroimaging may be valuable in 
patients with FDXR deficiency, particularly during acute 
illnesses, as normal baseline imaging does not exclude the 
possibility of future development of cytotoxic edema and 
neurodegeneration.
