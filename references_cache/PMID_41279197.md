# Indirect Deuterium Displacement Exchange Imaging for Noninvasive High-Resolution CSF Production Mapping.
**Authors:** De D, Pan S, Hosseini H, Bateman N, Daemi A, Garbow JR, Quirk JD, Strahle JM, Nazeri A
**Journal:** bioRxiv (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1101/2025.10.20.683579](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.10.20.683579)

## Content

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) production is central to brain homeostasis, yet existing measurement techniques are invasive, technically demanding, and confounded by intracranial perturbations. Here, we introduce indirect deuterium displacement exchange imaging (CSF-iDDxI), a noninvasive MRI approach for mapping CSF production in vivo. The method leverages intravenously infused deuterium oxide (D2O), which crosses the blood-CSF barrier, replaces existing H2O in CSF, producing concentration-dependent attenuation of proton (1H) signal within CSF spaces. Using high-resolution 3D balanced steady-state free precession MRI in rats, we demonstrate robust and spatially widespread D2O-induced CSF signal loss that is selectively suppressed by acetazolamide, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor known to suppress CSF production by the choroid plexus. Dynamic imaging during intravenous D2O infusion further revealed that cortical parenchymal signal changes were unaffected by acetazolamide, confirming specificity to CSF production. Exploratory kinetic modeling estimated rapid CSF water turnover (k ≈ 0.09 min-1) under physiological conditions, reduced to k ≈ 0.031 min-1 with acetazolamide suppression, consistent with prior isotope tracer studies. Together, these findings establish CSF-iDDxI as a sensitive, pharmacologically validated tool for quantifying CSF production and turnover.

Introduction: 41
 42
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) plays a central role in maintaining brain homeostasis by 43
supporting waste clearance and intracranial pressure regulation1. CSF is primarily 44
produced by the choroid plexus within the ventricular system, where a specialized 45
epithelium forms the blood–CSF barrier (BCSFB)2,3, with possible extra-choroidal 46
contributions from transcapillary and interstitial exchange3. Once secreted, CSF flows 47
from the ventricles into the subarachnoid space, where it circulates around the brain 48
and spinal cord. CSF penetrates the brain parenchyma via perivascular spaces, 49
facilitating glymphatic clearance of metabolic by-products and proteins1,2,4.  As the 50
initiating driver of this cascade, CSF production serves as a key regulator of neurofluid 51
homeostasis and waste clearance1,5–7. Disruption of this tightly regulated process has 52
been implicated in hydrocephalus8–11, aging and age-associated neurodegeneration 53
(e.g., Alzheimer’s disease)12–15, and idiopathic intracranial hypertension16,17.  54
Quantifying CSF production noninvasively thus represents a crucial step toward 55
understanding how its dysregulation drives brain pathology and how targeted 56
modulation might restore neurofluid homeostasis. Several methodologies exist for 57
measuring CSF production, each with inherent advantages and limitations18. Direct 58
measurements employing catheter-based quantification of CSF efflux from the lateral 59
ventricles following surgical occlusion of the fourth ventricle yield precise estimates of 60
ventricular choroidal CSF secretion13. Indirect methods typically involve infusing tracers 61
or contrast agents into the ventricular system and measuring their dilution over time18–20. 62
However, these approaches are susceptible to inaccuracies due to tracer leakage and 63
incomplete mixing across CSF compartments18. Furthermore, these approaches are 64

To address the limitations of existing methods, we developed indirect deuterium 75

intravenously infused deuterium oxide (D2O, heavy water; Fig. 1). This approach 77
provides the imaging analog of the water-exchange paradigm originally pioneered by 78
Bering in the 1950s24. As a stable isotopic analog of H2O, D2O is functionally equivalent 79
to water in its transport properties and distribution across physiological compartments 80
through aquaporins25,26, making it an ideal tracer for studying water dynamics in vivo. 81
D2O can be indirectly detected on conventional proton (1H) MRI through a 82
concentration-dependent signal loss caused by displacement of native tissue water27,28. 83
This principle has been previously demonstrated for assessing cerebral perfusion, 84
where intravascular D2O infusion led to measurable 1H signal attenuation in the brain 85
parenchyma27,28. In contrast to brain parenchyma, where signal changes arise from both 86
intravascular D2O and D2O that has crossed the blood–brain barrier (BBB), CSF lacks 87

Building on this foundation, we utilized D2O-induced signal loss for CSF labeling: 90
following infusion, D2O circulates systemically and crosses the BCSFB, resulting in 91
newly secreted CSF with a D2O concentration that closely reflects that of plasma. This 92
replacement of H2O by D2O enables visualization of CSF production as a progressive 93
reduction in 1H signal intensity within the ventricular and extra-ventricular CSF spaces, 94
proportional to the influx of D2O. Given the relatively small size of CSF spaces in 95
murine models, evaluation of D2O-induced signal attenuation with CSF-iDDxI requires 96
imaging techniques with high spatial resolution, strong signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and 97
insensitivity to flow-related artifacts. To address these constraints, we employ high-98
resolution three-dimensional balanced steady-state free precession (3D b-SSFP), which 99
provides sharp fluid–tissue contrast and is well suited for detecting subtle changes in 100
CSF composition without partial volume effects. We applied this approach in a rat model 101
and demonstrated that D2O infusion produced robust signal loss within the CSF. To 102
validate the approach, we used acetazolamide (ACZ), a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor 103
that suppresses CSF production. ACZ markedly attenuated D2O-induced CSF signal 104
loss, while cortical dynamics were unaffected, indicating that the effect reflects specific 105
suppression of CSF production rather than nonspecific consequences of diuresis or 106
perfusion. Exploratory kinetic modeling further confirmed that CSF water turnover is 107
rapid under physiological conditions and slows markedly with acetazolamide. Together, 108
these results establish a noninvasive framework for imaging CSF production and its 109
pharmacological modulation in vivo. 110

D2O-induced global CSF signal loss is attenuated by acetazolamide:  112
Using the CSF-iDDxI paradigm (Fig. 1), voxel-wise maps of CSF secretion were derived 113
by comparing pre- and post-infusion b-SSFP signal intensities within CSF spaces. D2O 114
was infused twice to confirm reproducibility of the CSF-iDDxI measurements across 115
repeated trials. Rats were assigned to three groups: D2O infusion, H2O infusion as 116
control, and D2O infusion following acetazolamide pretreatment (D2O/ACZ) to suppress 117
CSF production. Group-averaged signal loss maps showed widespread attenuation 118
across the CSF spaces with D2O, minimal change with H2O, and intermediate values 119
with D2O/ACZ (Fig. 2A). To quantify these effects, global CSF signal changes were 120
calculated across the entire CSF compartment. Global CSF signal decrease (averaged 121
across the CSF compartment) was larger in the D2O group (infusion 1: 9.3% ± 3.1%; 122
infusion 2: 6.4% ± 2.3%) compared to the H2O group (infusion 1: 1.2% ± 2.3%, p = 123
0.0023; infusion 2: 0.7% ± 2.4%, p = 0.0023; Fig. 2B). The D2O/ACZ group showed 124
intermediate signal loss (infusion 1: 5.5% ± 1.6%; infusion 2: 3.7% ± 1.3%), significantly 125

Discussion: 184
Our findings establish intravenously infused D2O combined with high-resolution b-SSFP 185
MRI as a noninvasive strategy for probing CSF production dynamics in vivo. We 186
demonstrate that systemic D2O administration produces robust and spatially 187
widespread attenuation of CSF signal, consistent with incorporation of D2O into newly 188
secreted fluid across the BCSFB. Critically, this effect was markedly suppressed by 189
ACZ, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that reduces choroid plexus secretion. To capture 190
temporal dynamics, we complemented high-resolution CSF-iDDxI with dynamic 191
deuterium displacement imaging during, tracking D2O-induced signal loss in both brain 192
parenchyma and the superior sagittal sinus (blood pool) during and after the first 193
infusion. In contrast, cortical D2O-induced signal changes were unaffected by ACZ, 194
confirming that the observed CSF effects reflect targeted suppression of secretion 195
rather than nonspecific alterations in perfusion or water distribution.  196
Heavy water (D2O) can be detected in vivo with MRI either directly with a dedicated 197
deuterium (2H) coil or indirectly with a conventional 1H coil. In the seminal studies direct 198
2H imaging was first applied in early studies of perfusion29 and has more recently been 199

Whereas earlier studies employed dynamic, low-resolution 2D acquisitions to track 205
perfusion-related signal changes, we employed 3D b-SSFP at 110 µm3 resolution for 206
CSF-iDDxI to enable high-resolution mapping of CSF signal changes. Such resolution is 207
essential in murine models given the small intracranial CSF spaces and the need to 208
minimize partial volume effects. In addition, signal changes in CSF-iDDxI represent 209
exchanged D2O, as vascular partial volume contribution is negligible within high 210
intensity CSF. This differs from prior indirect deuterium imaging studies, where signal 211
changes reflect a combination of intravascular D2O and D2O that had crossed the BBB 212
into interstitial and intracellular compartments. 213
3D b-SSFP is particularly well-suited for this application, combining rapid volumetric 214
acquisition and high SNR efficiency with relative insensitivity to flow-related artifacts that 215
typically confound CSF imaging. However, the long acquisition time (4–5 minutes per 216
phase advance, with at least two phases) precludes fully dynamic imaging at this 217
resolution, and the low parenchymal SNR limits detection of tissue signal changes. 218
However, for translation to larger animals and humans, where CSF spaces are larger, 219
whole-brain acquisitions at submillimeter resolutions can be achieved with temporal 220
resolutions <1 minute using advanced acceleration techniques33.  221

induced signal loss in CSF production by ~47%. The suppressive effect of ACZ on CSF 223
secretion is well established, thereby serving as a pharmacological validation for CSF-224
iDDxI. Previous studies using alternative approaches for measuring CSF production 225
(e.g., ventriculo-cisternal perfusion) have reported comparable ACZ-induced reductions 226
(40–57%) across various animal models34–37. ACZ-induced changes were confined to 227
CSF signal, as cortical parenchymal ROIs showed no difference between the D2O and 228
D2O/ACZ groups on dynamic deuterium displacement imaging. These findings indicate 229
that ACZ’s effect is specific to CSF production and not attributable to nonspecific diuretic 230
effects or perfusion-related changes. Overall, CSF-iDDxI establishes a platform for 231
quantitative measurement of CSF production in vivo and for evaluating drugs that 232
suppress or enhance secretion, as well as diseases with suspected disturbances in 233
CSF production. 234
Follow-up ROI analysis revealed no spatially distinct effects of ACZ. This presumably 235
reflects equilibration of water composition across CSF spaces within 10–15 minutes of 236
infusion. This is likely facilitated by mixing effects from cardiorespiratory-driven CSF 237
flow, which enhance effective CSF diffusivity38,39. Resolving potential choroidal versus 238
extrachoroidal sources of CSF production, or detecting spatial gradients, may require 239
higher temporal resolution or CSF-iDDxI performed during continuous intravenous D2O 240
infusion. Similarly, intra-arterial D2O administration could reveal lateralized as well as 241
choroidal versus extra-choroidal spatial gradients. CSF-iDDxI–based spatial mapping of 242
CSF production may have translational relevance, particularly in obstructive 243

Our exploratory estimates of CSF kinetics demonstrated CSF water turnover constant of 246
k ≈  0.09 min-1 under physiological conditions and k ≈  0.031 min-1 with ACZ-mediated 247
CSF production suppression. These values align with prior evidence from tritiated water 248
(HTO) studies in animals, which have shown rapid equilibration between plasma and 249
CSF within 5–10 minutes after bolus injection40,41, reflecting fast CSF turnover dynamics 250
comparable to those observed here. Of note, CSF water turnover rates derived from our 251
approach are not directly comparable to production rates reported with earlier methods. 252
For instance, Liu et al. reported a CSF production rate of 133.2 nL/min under isoflurane 253
anesthesia (assuming VCSF ≈  36 µL in mice; k = 0.0037 min-1)13. This estimate, however, 254
does not account for contributions from the fourth ventricle or extra-choroidal sources, 255
and more fundamentally represents net CSF efflux through a lateral ventricular cannula 256
rather than steady-state CSF water turnover rate. 257
Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the relatively long acquisition times of 258
high-resolution 3D b-SSFP preclude fully dynamic mapping of CSF production, limiting 259
the ability to resolve rapid fluctuations or compartment-specific gradients. Future large-260
animal studies leveraging advanced acceleration techniques may enable dynamic, high-261
resolution CSF-iDDxI with improved temporal resolution and translational relevance. 262
Second, in small-animal preclinical MRI, surface coil–related receive bias can heighten 263
the impact of minor head motion, leading to compromised signal uniformity and subtle 264
motion-related signal fluctuations. Securing the head with extra caution is therefore 265
essential in small animals, while the use of multi-channel coils in clinical scanners 266

Animals:  293
All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at 294
Washington University in St. Louis (Protocol ID: 24-0027). In total, 25 male Sprague–295
Dawley rats (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA), weighing between 550 and 296
650 g and aged 4–6 months, were used in this study.  297
Experimental Design:  298
Rats were randomly assigned to one of three groups: (i) D2O group (n = 7), which 299
received intravenous deuterium oxide; (ii) negative control H2O group (n = 8), which 300
received intravenous H2O as the vehicle; and (iii) D2O‐ ACZ group (n=10), which was 301

suppress CSF production44. All animals received two intravenous infusions of normal 303
saline prepared in either D2O or H2O. Each infusion was warmed to 35–37/i2 °C, 304
administered at 15/i2 mL/kg, and delivered over 12 minutes via tail vein using an infusion 305
pump. All animals receiving D2O remained viable for the entire study period until 306
planned euthanasia, with no premature mortality (4–6 months of follow-up).  307
