Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a complex 
neuroimmunological disorder characterized by disabling symptoms that are often 
difficult to manage. More recently, in the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection, 
potential pathophysiological overlaps and disease modulation have been 
hypothesized. Our successful case highlights the need to investigate novel 
therapeutic approaches, including plasma exchange.

ME/CFS is a debilitating neuroimmunological disorder characterized by persistent fatigue, exertional intolerance, and autonomic dysregulation. Post‐exertional malaise, a hallmark of the disease, represents a disproportionate worsening of symptoms after minimal exertion [1]. Disturbed vascular regulation and orthostatic intolerance have also been documented [2]. An autoimmune component has been proposed, particularly in post‐infectious cases, involving β2‐adrenergic receptor antibodies [3, 4]. This autoimmune hypothesis is further supported by the increasing incidence of ME/CFS following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection [5].

Raltegravir, a retroviral integrase inhibitor, has previously been explored in ME/CFS based on early hypotheses suggesting a potential role of retroviral elements, including xenotropic murine leukemia virus‐related virus (XMRV). Subsequent studies have not confirmed a causal association, and these hypotheses are no longer supported by current evidence [6]. However, more recent evidence has not substantiated this. Instead, it supports a multifactorial etiology of ME/CFS arising from a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers—particularly viral infections. This interaction is thought to initiate a cascade of pathophysiological processes, including immune dysregulation, chronic low‐grade inflammation, gut microbiome alterations, and metabolic disturbances [7].

Despite its relatively high prevalence, ME/CFS remains without universally accepted diagnostic criteria, which continues to hinder timely recognition and accurate diagnosis. Moreover, no standardized treatment guidelines are currently available; management is therefore primarily focused on long‐term symptom control, which remains challenging even with multimodal pharmacological approaches [7].

Two small proof‐of‐concept studies [4] and one prospective study [8] have highlighted the potential efficacy of IgG depletion via immunoadsorption in patients with post‐infectious ME/CFS. Further research is needed to clarify the role of plasma exchange in patients without evidence of prior infection at the time of ME/CFS diagnosis, as well as to better understand its potential association with symptom exacerbation following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.

A 35‐year‐old female with a 6‐year history of ME/CFS was referred for evaluation regarding plasma exchange therapy.

Her initial symptoms included persistent fatigue, exertional intolerance, vertigo, and diffuse myalgia. MRI findings of demyelination initially raised suspicion for multiple sclerosis, which was ruled out following negative cerebrospinal fluid analysis. Furthermore, the patient was diagnosed with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome. Her family history was unremarkable. Based on the clinical features and findings, a diagnosis of ME/CFS was established.

The patient's symptoms progressively worsened over time, particularly fatigue, diffuse myalgia, and vertigo, and she also began experiencing sudden episodes of nausea and migraine. These symptoms significantly impacted her ability to work and contributed to the development of depression. Conservative management, including physiotherapy and psychotherapy, was ineffective. A short‐term trial with amantadine did not result in any symptom relief.

Given the unfavorable clinical course, an extensive infectious and oncological workup was performed, revealing no abnormalities. MRI showed stable leukoencephalopathy. Serology revealed elevated M3/M4 muscarinic acetylcholine receptor and β1/β2‐adrenergic receptor IgG antibodies, further supporting the diagnosis of severe ME/CFS.

Subsequently, in 2020, off‐label raltegravir 800 mg/day was initiated, resulting in marked symptom relief after approximately 4 months of treatment. After SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in 2022, while still receiving raltegravir therapy, the patient experienced transient post‐exertional malaise along with persistent brain fog and concentration difficulties, prompting a dosage escalation. In the following months, the cognitive symptoms persisted. The patient reported weight gain and worsening of the symptoms during perimenstrual periods and in winter months.

A four‐week raltegravir discontinuation led to gradual fatigue recurrence and elevated antibody titers (Table 1), supporting an autoimmune mechanism and prompting plasma exchange initiation.

In January 2024, plasma exchange was initiated using the Terumo Optia system, with peripheral venous access. Each session consisted of a 1:1 plasma volume exchange with 5% albumin replacement (3500 mL) and citrate anticoagulation (Table 2). Regular blood gas analyses were conducted during the procedures, revealing recurrent hypocalcemia as a side effect of citrate anticoagulation, which was corrected with intravenous calcium. Fibrinogen levels remained within the normal range; therefore, transfusion of fresh frozen plasma was not required. Low‐dose intravenous immunoglobulin (OCTAGAM) was administered at the end of each cycle.

The therapy was well tolerated, with no reported side effects or complications.

Plasma exchange was initially performed every 2–3 months. The patient reported marked improvement in fatigue, concentration, and cognitive clarity. Over the following months, raltegravir was gradually tapered and discontinued. At 3 months and 6 months follow‐up, the patient reported no signs of symptom recurrence. Given the positive outcome and sustained symptom control, the interval between plasma exchange sessions was extended to 3 months. The patient remained stable, reporting improved stamina, weight loss, and disappearance of menstrual cycle–related symptom exacerbations.

After three plasma exchange sessions spaced 2 months apart, a significant decrease in muscarinic and adrenergic receptor antibodies was observed (Table 1, Figure 1). The patient continues maintenance plasma exchange at intervals of approximately 3 months, with a single session per cycle, maintaining stable symptom control without the need for additional pharmacotherapy.

Our case highlights the potential therapeutic role of plasma exchange (TPE) in ME/CFS with suspected autoimmune involvement. The patient experienced significant improvement in cognitive function, physical stamina, and overall quality of life following TPE, accompanied by a measurable reduction in muscarinic and adrenergic receptor autoantibodies. This parallel improvement supports the hypothesis that autoantibodies may contribute to disease pathophysiology in a subset of patients.

An important aspect of this case is the preceding SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. The COVID‐19 pandemic has brought renewed attention to chronic fatigue syndrome, as a considerable proportion of patients with long COVID present with clinical features overlapping with myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), including persistent fatigue, cognitive impairment, and post‐exertional malaise. Increasing evidence suggests that post‐COVID syndromes share pathophysiological features with ME/CFS, including immune dysregulation, autonomic dysfunction, and autoantibody production. This overlap may provide a valuable framework for advancing biomarker discovery and facilitating the development of targeted therapeutic interventions. In this context, the observed secondary loss of response to raltegravir in our case may reflect post‐infectious immune activation rather than primary treatment failure.

The initial clinical response to raltegravir, followed by diminished efficacy after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, further supports the heterogeneity of ME/CFS and the possibility that different disease phases may be driven by distinct mechanisms. Notably, integrase inhibitors such as raltegravir have been explored in the past based on hypotheses involving retroviral elements; however, these concepts, including the role of XMRV, are no longer supported by current evidence. Instead, contemporary research increasingly emphasizes autoimmune and post‐infectious mechanisms [7].

Compared with raltegravir, plasma exchange led to a more rapid and sustained clinical improvement in our patient. This may be explained by the direct removal of circulating autoantibodies and inflammatory mediators. An additional consideration in favor of plasma exchange in this case was its potential to remove not only circulating autoantibodies but also other pathogenic plasma components, such as pro‐inflammatory mediators and fibrin amyloid microclots, which have been increasingly implicated in post‐COVID and ME/CFS pathophysiology. In contrast, immunoadsorption primarily targets immunoglobulins and may not address these additional circulating factors [9]. Similar effects have been described in small studies investigating immunoadsorption in ME/CFS, further supporting the concept of antibody‐mediated disease mechanisms. Furthermore, the persistence of brain fog and fatigue during high‐dose raltegravir therapy suggests these symptoms may have been drug‐related side effects.

The clinical relevance of our findings lies in the potential identification of a treatable ME/CFS subgroup characterized by immunological abnormalities. However, this observation must be interpreted with caution. As a single case report, causality cannot be established, and spontaneous fluctuation of symptoms cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the influence of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection represents a significant confounding factor.

Due to the disabling nature of its symptoms—particularly cognitive and physical impairment exacerbated by exertion—ME/CFS imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden. A significant proportion of affected individuals are unable to maintain employment, while the frequent use of costly off‐label therapies further underscores the challenges in disease management and highlights the urgent need for standardized, targeted treatment strategies [10].

This case supports the hypothesis that a subset of ME/CFS patients with autoimmune features may benefit from antibody‐depleting therapies such as TPE or immunoadsorption. Further controlled studies are warranted to assess its efficacy, safety, and to better define appropriate patient selection criteria for this approach.
