# Bipolar Disorder.
**Authors:** Iyer V, Jain A, Mitra P

## Content

Bipolar disorder remains one of the most challenging and multifaceted conditions in psychiatry for several reasons. Bipolar disorder is a chronic mood disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of mania, hypomania, and depression, often interspersed with periods of relative euthymia. These episodic mood disturbances can significantly impair functioning across occupational, interpersonal, and cognitive domains. Globally, bipolar disorder affects approximately 0.5% to 2.4% of the population, depending on diagnostic criteria and the breadth of the bipolar spectrum considered. In the United States, epidemiologic surveys estimate that approximately 2.8% of adults experience bipolar disorder within a given year and roughly 4.4% meet criteria at some point in their lifetime. Large-scale epidemiologic studies further demonstrate that the burden of bipolar disorder has increased globally over the past 3 decades, with tens of millions of individuals affected worldwide and incidence rates continuing to rise. The disorder typically emerges in late adolescence or early adulthood and follows a recurrent, lifelong course marked by episodic mood instability, residual symptoms, and substantial psychosocial impairment. The clinical presentation is heterogeneous, often complicated, and ambiguous. Empirical studies demonstrate that mixed symptomatology is common, with systematic reviews showing that approximately one-third of bipolar depressive or manic episodes exhibit features of the opposite polarity. This coexistence may manifest, for example, as agitation, racing thoughts, or irritability during depressive episodes, or dysphoria and suicidal ideation during manic states. Such overlapping symptom clusters blur traditional diagnostic boundaries and complicate the differentiation between bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and other psychiatric conditions. Furthermore, patients frequently experience psychiatric and medical comorbidities, including anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, and cardiovascular disease, which further complicate management and worsen long-term outcomes. In addition, bipolar disorder is characterized by high relapse rates, incomplete treatment response, and chronic functional impairment, requiring long-term monitoring and interprofessional management. These factors collectively contribute to the complexity of diagnosing and treating bipolar disorder, highlighting the need for continued education and evidence-based clinical approaches.
