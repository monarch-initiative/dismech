Abstract
Background: Functional abdominal pain disorders (FAPD) are common in children and there is
heterogeneity in the available literature regarding the role of ﬁbre in FAPD and none so-far in functional
abdominal pain-not otherwise (FAP-NOS).
Aim: The study aimed to evaluate the eﬃcacy of psyllium ﬁber in pediatric FAPD including Irritable bowel
syndrome (IBS) and FAP-NOS.
Methods: In this prospective randomized placebo-controlled trial, children (5-18 years) diagnosed as
FAPD based on Rome IV criteria were randomized to psyllium ﬁber or placebo (maltodextrin) for 12
weeks. Post-treatment improvement of pain and quality of life (QoL) from baseline was compared
between the two groups.
Results: The mean age of 109 children (FAP-NOS: 85%; psyllium ﬁber 57, placebo 52) was 11.8 ± 3.9
years (58% boys). There was a signiﬁcant difference in pain improvement in terms of reduction in scores
for intensity (5.5 vs. 3.2), frequency (6.0 vs. 3.2), duration (5.3 vs. 3.3), and QoL (4.4 vs. 2.3) between the
psyllium and placebo group (P<0.001 in all). Responders (>50% reduction in pain) were seen in 86% of
the psyllium as compared to 60% in the placebo group (P=0.001). On multivariate analysis, use of
psyllium ﬁber was the only factor predictive of response (OR: 5.49, 95% CI: 2.05-14.72, P < 0.001). After
discontinuation of psyllium ﬁber, 81% had sustained response over a mean follow-up of 6.7 ± 4.0
months.
Conclusions: A 12-week trial of psyllium ﬁber improves pain in most and gives sustained relief in three-
fourths of children with FAP-NOS and IBS.
Trial registration: CTRI/2023/08/056772; Clinical trial registry website: http://ctri.nic.in
Introduction
Functional abdominal pain disorders (FAPD), now commonly referred to as disorders of gut-brain
interaction, are quite common in children. The estimated global pooled prevalence of FAPDs is 11.7%.1
FAPD in children consists of functional dyspepsia (FD), irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), functional
abdominal pain-not otherwise speciﬁed (FAP-NOS), and abdominal migraine (AM).2 Children with FAPDs
have lower health-related quality of life (HR-QoL).3
The pathophysiology of these disorders are multifactorial and include altered motility, visceral
hypersensitivity, and gut-brain interactions.4 Inadequate ﬁber intake is a potential dietary risk factor for
FAPDs.5
There is a lot of heterogeneity in the available literature regarding the role of ﬁber in FAPD in pediatric
patients. Though some previous studies showed its role in recurrent abdominal pain (RAP), recent
Page 2/14

studies are mainly in IBS. In our setting, we see predominantly FAP-NOS, and the role of psyllium ﬁber in
this subset (FAP-NOS) has not been studied so-far. Thus, in this prospective, randomized placebo-
controlled trial, we aimed to evaluate the eﬃcacy of psyllium ﬁber in children with FAPD (FAP-NOS and
IBS).
Methods
Ethical Statement
The study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval from the institutional ethics committee and
was registered with the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI: CTRI/2023/08/056772). This is a single
blinded trail conducted in accordance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice, the Declaration of
Helsinki, and the recommendations of the Rome foundation pediatric subcommittee for
pharmacological clinical trials in children with FAPD. Written informed consent from the parents and
assent from the children were obtained before enrolment. 
Study population: From September 2023 to May 2025, children (5 to 18 years), diagnosed with FAPD
(FAP-NOS and IBS) as per Rome IV criteria, were enrolled consecutively from the Pediatric
Gastroenterology outpatient services of Sanjay Gandhi Post Graduate Institute of Medical Sciences,
Lucknow, India, a tertiary health care centre.
Study design: The study consisted of a baseline observation period of 4-week followed by 12-week
treatment period after randomisation to either the psyllium ﬁber or placebo group.
Exclusion criteria: Children were excluded if there was suspected/proven organic disease (screened with
hemogram, total protein, albumin, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, c-reactive protein in all patients and
celiac screening in patients with IBS-Diarrhoea), functional dyspepsia and abdominal migraine as their
pathophysiology is different, already on ﬁber therapy in any form or psychiatric disorder and if they were
on anticonvulsants or antipsychotic medications. Children on laxatives such as Polyethylene glycol
(PEG), a washout period of 4 weeks (off laxative) was given before being included in the study.
Baseline assessment: Once the diagnosis was made, stressors, if any, were identiﬁed, and parents and
the child were individually counselled about the pathogenesis of the disease, keeping the brain–gut axis
in mind. There was no further counselling session during the trial. 
Sample size: Taking study conducted by Menon et al.6 as reference study and assuming the lower
effect-size in our study (Cohen-d: 0.8) for the difference among the two groups, at minimum two-sided
95% conﬁdence-interval and 95% power of the study, estimated sample size in each group was 42.
Sample size was estimated using software G*Power version 3.1.9.2.
Randomisation and intervention: Children recruited in the study were randomized by a computer-
generated random number table in the ratio of 1:1. The participants were blinded. Psyllium ﬁber
Page 3/14

(Telephone brand, Sidhpur Sat-Isabgol Factory Private Limited, Patan, Gujarat, India) and placebo
(Maltodextrin) were given (5g in 5-11 years old and 10 g in 12-18 years old) once a day for 12 weeks in
powder form after mixing with plain water (100 ml), followed by one glass of water (100 ml). Both
powders were provided in the form of opaque sachets, with each sachet containing 5 g of either of the
materials, to ensure patient blinding. Only intervention (psyllium or placebo) or analgesics (oral
acetaminophen or parenteral analgesic after physician’s advice) were allowed during the trial. Drug
therapy compliance was monitored monthly via telephone check-ins and veriﬁed through sachet counts
or diary reviews after 12 weeks of intervention.
Outcome assessment: The primary aim was to assess the eﬃcacy of psyllium ﬁber in terms of pain
relief in children with FAPD. Pain assessment was done using the pain score table, which assessed
duration, frequency, and intensity of pain. The same score has been used in our previously published
study and shown to be useful.7 Parent/child (>12 years) were trained to document each episode, its
duration and pain intensity for 4 weeks at baseline (before randomisation) and for 12 weeks after
randomisation. Visual analogue scale was used to describe the intensity of pain which ranged from 0 for
no pain to 10 for unbearable pain. A scale of 0-10 was also used each for pain frequency (number of
episodes of pain lasting for at least 10 minutes) and pain duration in hours. Thus, total score was
calculated out of 30 at baseline and at the end of intervention after 12 weeks (supplementary
information, S1). The outcome scores were documented and assessed by a single assessor (MK)
throughout the study. Responder is deﬁned as the reduction in total pain scores of > 50% of baseline
values following intervention. The secondary outcomes were Quality of life (QoL) and occasions
requiring analgesics (oral/parenteral) during pain episodes. QoL assessment was done by the American
Chronic Pain Association QoL rating scale just before randomisation (2nd visit) and after 12 weeks of
treatment (3rd visit) and scores were noted (supplementary information, S2).8 The adverse events (AEs)
were noted after 12 weeks of treatment. Missing drug for more than 10% of days (>9 days in 12 weeks of
treatment) was labelled as poor compliance and was excluded from the per-protocol analysis (PPA). 
Post-intervention follow-up: At the end of the trial period, the intervention and the placebo were
discontinued in patients with a complete response, and the patients were followed-up. Cases were
allowed to continue psyllium ﬁber based on the willingness of the parents. Control patients with no
response to placebo who came for follow-up were also started on psyllium ﬁber. After the treatment was
stopped, pain status was assessed by either direct interviewing at follow-up or through telephone and
the pain status was categorized as either sustained response if the condition remained the same or
improved compared with the status while on treatment, or relapse if there was worsening of pain or if
there was a recurrence of pain after at least 3 months of asymptomatic period. 
Statistical analysis: The study employed both an intention-to-treat (ITT) and a per-protocol  analysis
(PPA) to evaluate the primary outcomes. Normality of the continuous variables were tested and a
variable was considered normally distributed when Z-score of the variables were ranged between ± 3.29.
Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as
number (percentage). For the comparison between means of two paired groups/ unpaired groups, paired
Page 4/14

samples t-test / Independent samples t-test was used as appropriate. Chi-square test/ Fisher’s exact
test was used to compare the proportions/ test the association between two categorical variables.
Binary logistic regression analysis was applied to identify the independent predictors for responders. All
the results were considered as statistically signiﬁcant on P-value <0.05. Statistical analysis was
performed using Statistical package for social sciences version -23 (SPSS-23, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).
Results
From September 2023 to May 2025, a total 109 children with FAPD (FAP-NOS 93 and IBS 16 [IBS-
Constipation (8); IBS-Diarrhoea (5); IBS-Mixed (3)] were recruited in the study. Their mean age was 11.8 ±
3.9 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 63:46. Of the 109 randomized children, 57 were randomized to
the psyllium ﬁber group and 52 to the placebo, of which 47 from the ﬁber group and 49 from the placebo
group completed the study (Fig 1).
Baseline characteristics
The baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. There was no statistically signiﬁcant difference in
haematological parameters and inﬂammatory markers between the groups. The most common site of
pain was periumbilical (n=86, 79%), followed by multiple sites or diffuse pain (n=16, 14.6%) and
epigastric pain (n=7, 6%). Family history of functional gastrointestinal disorder, migraine, anxiety, or
depression was present in 17% (n=18; psyllium, 10; placebo, 8) of patients. History of stressors such as
academic pressure, death of a family member, parental conﬂicts, change of school or residence, etc.,
was found in 14% (n=15; psyllium, 7; placebo, 8) of patients. 
Primary eﬃcacy variables. 
The eﬃcacy outcome (primary and secondary) in the psyllium ﬁber group and the placebo group is
shown in Table 2. Assuming the same baseline scores in the post-treatment period for those children
who were lost to follow-up, ITT analysis was done in 109 children, and pain scores at baseline and post
12-week intervention were compared (Table 2). The mean pain score reduction was much greater in the
psyllium group than in the placebo group (15.7 vs. 9.4, P=0.048). Also, the effect size was more in
psyllium group as compared to the placebo (Cohen-d [calculated using mean difference with respect to
pooled SD]: 2.9 vs 1.4, P<0.001) (Figure 2). PPA also showed a signiﬁcant reduction in pain scores (16.8
vs. 9.7, P < 0.001) in the psyllium group as compared with the placebo (Table 3). 
Pain scores showed signiﬁcant improvement for IBS and FAP-NOS in the ﬁber group as compared with
placebo (Table 4). Signiﬁcantly higher proportion of patients responded to therapy (>50% reduction of
pain) with the psyllium as compared with the placebo [86% (n=49) vs. 60% (n=31), P=0.001]. Complete
relief of pain was also signiﬁcantly more in the psyllium group as compared with the placebo group
(n=20, 36% vs. n=6, 11%; P=0.006). Non-responders (<20% pain reduction) were fewer in number in the
psyllium group as compared with the placebo group, however, the difference was not statistically
signiﬁcant (n=5, 9% vs. n=11, 21%, P = 0.06). On multivariate binary logistic regression analysis to assess
Page 5/14

the parameters predicting responders, the only parameter that was predictive of pain response was the
use of psyllium ﬁber (adjusted OR: 5.49, 95% CI: 2.05-14.72, P<0.001) (Table 5). 
Post-intervention outcomes in psyllium ﬁber are shown as a ﬂow chart (Figure 3). After discontinuation
of psyllium ﬁber, 81.6% had sustained response over a mean follow-up of 6.7 months. Of the remaining 7
patients (18.4%) who had relapse of symptoms, 5 had mild symptoms without a need for analgesics or
any treatment, and 2 were seeking medical advice from elsewhere and were still on intermittent
analgesics. Of 20 patients who did not respond to the placebo, psyllium ﬁber was given to 11 patients
who came for follow-up and response was seen in 7 of them. Of 4 patients who did not show response
to ﬁber, 2 were treated with amitriptyline, with positive response. 
Secondary eﬃcacy variables
Quality of life (QoL) scores following treatment showed a signiﬁcant improvement in the psyllium group
as compared with the placebo group in PPA (4.4 vs. 2.3, P<0.001) (Table 3). Most children (psyllium:
98.3% and placebo: 96.5%) required analgesics at least once (either oral or parenteral) during the
baseline observation period. However, only 26.3% of children in the psyllium group and 58.2% of children
in the placebo group required analgesics during the intervention period. There was signiﬁcantly more
reduction in analgesic requirement in psyllium group compared to the placebo group (35.8% vs. 21.9%,
P=0.0001). Subgroup analysis of pain scores and QoL was done in the placebo group to look for the
placebo effect. There was a signiﬁcant reduction in the mean pain scores (20.5 vs. 11.1, P<0.001) and
improvement in QoL (3.9 vs. 1.8, P<0.001) in the placebo group as well from baseline to the end of
therapy. The requirement of analgesics was also signiﬁcantly lower during intervention in the placebo
group (36.1 vs. 14.1, P<0.001). 
Adverse events
There were no major adverse events. The incidence of minor adverse events was more in psyllium group
compared to placebo (11% vs. 2%, respectively, P=0.055). Adverse events (n=6) noted in the psyllium
ﬁber group were bloating (n=2), nausea (n=3) and vomiting (n=1). Discontinuation of therapy due to
adverse events (nausea) was reported in 2 cases after 2 weeks of starting therapy.  None had an
exacerbation of pain in the psyllium group. Three children found it diﬃcult to take psyllium, of whom 2
cases stopped medication after 4-week duration, after having a response. In the placebo group, one child
had minor adverse effect in the form of nausea and the medicine was discontinued.
Discussion
In this prospective, randomized, single-blinded, placebo-controlled trial, we found that there was a
signiﬁcant reduction in pain scores with 12-week trial of psyllium as compared with placebo (16.8 vs.
9.7, P<0.001). Also, the proportion of responders was signiﬁcantly higher in the psyllium group (86% vs.
60%; P=0.001). Though, there was signiﬁcant reduction in mean pain scores and improvement in QoL
scores in the placebo group at the end of study as compared to the baseline scores, however, the effect-
Page 6/14

size was more in psyllium group as compared to the placebo (Cohen-d: 2.9 vs 1.4, P<0.001). On
multivariate binary logistic regression analysis, the use of psyllium ﬁber was the only parameter
predictive of pain response. The role of ﬁber in IBS is well known both in adult and pediatric studies.6,9-11
Our study included patients with FAP-NOS as the majority. The role of ﬁber in this subset of FAPD has
never been described before. 
In a recent meta-analysis of the epidemiological data on FAPD, IBS was found to be the most
predominant type.1 On the contrary, FAP-NOS constituted the majority (63%) followed by IBS (17.4%) in a
recent Indian study.12 A similar spectrum was seen in our study with FAP-NOS diagnosed more
frequently than IBS (85% vs 15%). FAP-NOS was more common in younger age as compared to IBS (10.4
years vs 14.9 years, P=0.003). 
Psyllium, a soluble ﬁber, increases bulk of stool and its fermentation generates short-chain fatty acid
such as butyrate which provides energy to colonic epithelial cells and act as an anti-inﬂammatory
agent.13 RCTs done by Shulman et al. including 84 children of IBS (psyllium ﬁber: 37 and placebo
[maltodextrin]: 47 for 6 weeks) and Menon et al in 81 children of IBS (psyllium, n=43 and placebo
[maltodextrin], n=38 for 4 weeks) have shown a signiﬁcant reduction of pain frequency (8.2 vs. 4.1,
P=0.03) and total IBS-severity score system (IBS-SSS) [75 vs. 225; P<0.001] in the psyllium group
respectively.6,9 Previous studies by Jalihal et al and Prior et al failed to show any  signiﬁcant response in
pain relief.14,15 On the contrary, our study found signiﬁcant pain reduction in the ﬁber group as compared
to the placebo in IBS. A recent meta-analysis in children with FAPDs, including 5 RCTs (predominantly
IBS) found beneﬁcial effects of soluble ﬁbers, in particular psyllium.11 
The effectiveness of ﬁber in children with FAP-NOS remains uncertain due to a lack of substantial
evidence.14 Our cohort was different from other studies as it was constituted by FAP-NOS as the
majority (85%). Feldman et al. in an RCT of ﬁber in 52 RAP children showed a signiﬁcant reduction of
pain attacks (50% reduction was twice more in ﬁber than in placebo).16 In RCTs of 89 children of
abdominal pain-predominant FGID by Hovarth et al. (glucomannan with placebo) and 31 children with
RAP by Christensen et al. (ispaghula husks versus placebo) did not ﬁnd any difference in the pain
frequency between the groups.17,18 On the contrary, our study showed statistically signiﬁcant reduction
in pain in terms of number of episodes, intensity of pain, duration and total pain scores in the ﬁber group
(P<0.001 in all). Also, majority (89/93, 96%) of children in FAP-NOS group had normal stool pattern at
baseline (Bristol type 3 and 4). Four children who had abnormal Bristol (type 2: 1, type 5: 3) were equally
distributed in psyllium and placebo group. To our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst study to discuss the role of
ﬁber in pediatric FAP-NOS.
In our study, a placebo response was seen in 60% of the patients. In the previous studies and meta-
analysis done in children with AP-FGID, placebo response was seen in 41%-58% of the patients.19-21 High
placebo effect could be attributed to the concealment of group, high expectancy level of the children and
their parents getting enrolled in a trial, strong family-doctor bond and parents’ reassurance about the
Page 7/14

tertiary care physician’s knowledge and experience. The last statement can further be justiﬁed by the
observation, that 45% of patients had no pain in the baseline period (Figure 1).22 We explored factors
such as gender, age, FAPD subtype, stressor or family history of FGID by multivariate regression analysis
and found no effect in predicting response to the placebo as shown in past studies.23,24 However, mean
baseline pain scores in the placebo group were lower in responders as compared to non-responders
(18.9 vs. 22.6, P=0.023). Despite a 60% response in the placebo group, our study showed signiﬁcantly
more response in the psyllium group as compared to the placebo. One may argue that, like the placebo
group, responders in the psyllium group may also have lower mean baseline pain score as compared to
the non-responders, however, it was not statistically different (21.2 vs. 22.3, P=0.497). 
The strength of the study is its randomized design with blinding of patients. The majority of our cohort
comprised FAP-NOS, and the role of psyllium in this subset in children has not been discussed in
previous studies. The intervention duration was also longer in our study as compared to previous
studies. Also, the children with FAPDs were meticulously characterised using Rome IV criteria, including
FAPD subtype, family history and history of stressor, which we demonstrated were comparable between
the groups and are therefore less likely to contribute signiﬁcantly to the observed pain responses to
psyllium. Notably, objective measurement of daily pain scores using diaries, which are shown to be more
reliable in children than questionnaires, enhances the credibility of our data collection approach.25 
Our study has some limitations. The biggest limitation is the lack of double blinding and the assessment
of response by a single assessor throughout the study. However, it has helped to maintain the uniformity
in assessing responses, and also the objective pain score tables used in our study minimised the bias.
Assessment was done only at baseline (4 week run-in before randomisation) and week 12 (post
randomisation). The amount of dietary ﬁber was not pre-determined, and all patients were advised to
follow a regular diet, so the possibility of more ﬁber intake in the placebo group cannot be ruled out.
However, it applies to real-life scenarios. The associated symptoms, such as anxiety and depression,
were not assessed, which could have affected the outcome. A recent systematic review by Almutairi et
al. has questioned the validity of maltodextrin use as placebo in clinical trials as it induces various
modiﬁcations in gut microbiota conﬁgurations, and immunological factors.26  Also, it differs from
psyllium in texture, however, use of opaque sachets ensured blinding in our study. Ours is a single-centre
study, which raises the need for multicentric and blinded trials with a larger sample size to validate our
results. 
Conclusions
Psyllium ﬁber has similar eﬃcacy in the FAP-NOS and IBS with no signiﬁcant adverse effects. Proper
counselling of parent-child dyad about the pathophysiology of FAPD along with placebo could improve
pain in around half of children. A 12-week trial of psyllium ﬁber improves pain in most and gives
sustained relief in three-fourths of children with FAP-NOS and IBS. However, there is a need of more
randomized studies with double blinding to validate our results.
