# Non-Coding c.*6C>T Variant in RBM8A Associated With Thrombocytopenia-Absent Radius (TAR) Syndrome in Three Indian Patients.
**Authors:** Langeh N, Sharath R, Ansari MT, Palanichamy JK, Gupta N
**Journal:** Clin Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cge.70208](https://doi.org/10.1111/cge.70208)

## Content

Thrombocytopenia-absent radius (TAR) syndrome is a rare genetic disorder characterized by the absence of radius in the forearms and a decrease in platelet count. The molecular basis of TAR syndrome is linked to a heterozygous minimal deletion within the 1q21.1 region spanning 200 k bases (kb), resulting in a null allele and a nucleotide variation in RBM8A resulting in a hypomorphic allele. Previous studies have identified pathogenic variants in the coding regions of the RBM8A gene as the cause of TAR syndrome. However, the involvement of non-coding variants in disease pathogenesis remains largely unexplored. We investigated the association of a non-coding 3' UTR variant, c.*6C>T, in RBM8A with TAR syndrome in three individuals from two unrelated families of Indian origin. Our study provides evidence that this variant is associated with decreased stability of the transcript and is a hypomorphic allele with disease-causing impact when in trans with a null allele (1q21.1 deletion). The present work is the first application of an mRNA stability assay to directly detect RNA degradation in patients with non-coding RBM8A variants causing TAR syndrome.

Keywords: non‐coding variant, RNA‐binding motif protein 8A, TAR syndrome

A non‐coding 3′ UTR variant, c.*6C>T, in RBM8A in three affected individuals from two unrelated Indian families. Functional mRNA stability assay demonstrated that this non‐coding variant leads to decreased stability of the RBM8A transcript, showing increased degradation compared to the control. These findings provide the first application of a patient‐derived mRNA decay assay to establish that the c.*6C>T variant is a hypomorphic allele contributing to TAR syndrome when in trans with a null allele.

Thrombocytopenia‐absent radius (TAR) syndrome (MIM #274000) is a rare genetic condition clinically characterized by a triad of thrombocytopenia with bilateral absence of radii and the presence of thumbs [1, 2]. Individuals with TAR syndrome exhibit reduced megakaryocyte counts and often experience bleeding episodes during the initial year of life, which tend to decrease with age [3, 4]. Apart from these pathognomonic features, patients may also have cardiac, gastrointestinal, and urogenital defects [4, 5]. Approximately half of the patients have lower limb involvement, including hip dislocation, femoral and/or tibial torsion, genu varum, coxa valga, and absent patella. The most severe manifestation is tetraphocomelia [4, 5]. TAR syndrome is caused by a heterozygous, partial deletion in the 1q21.1 region causing a null allele and a single nucleotide variant in RBM8A, resulting in a hypomorphic allele. While the inheritance pattern resembles autosomal recessive inheritance, the compound heterozygosity of a null allele and a hypomorphic allele makes its inheritance unusual [6]. Variable expression and incomplete penetrance have been reported. The most frequent non‐coding variants in RBM8A include a 5′ untranslated region (UTR) variant (c.‐21G>A, rs139428292), and a variant in the first intron (c.67 + 32G>C, rs201779890) [4]. In addition, several other non‐coding variants have been reported, further expanding the mutational spectrum [7]. TAR syndrome is estimated to have a prevalence ranging from 1:100000 to 1:200000, but it may be more common in populations of African descent, attributed to the presence of the recurrent RBM8A hypomorphic allele (c.*6C>G) with a minor allele frequency of 14.8% in the African population [3, 4].

Previous research has primarily focused on coding variants within RBM8A, which encodes the RNA‐binding motif protein 8A. However, the contribution of non‐coding variants in TAR syndrome remains poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the potential association between a non‐coding variant, c.*6C>T, in the RBM8A and TAR syndrome in three Indian patients. This variant, occurring in the 3′ UTR region, a regulatory region influencing transcript stability, likely contributes to the observed reduction in gene expression.

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples of the affected individuals, and chromosomal microarray (CMA) was performed using Affymetrix microarray technology consisting of 750 000 markers. All coding exons and flanking regions of the RBM8A gene were sequenced using Sanger sequencing. The common variants in RBM8A (c.‐21G>A in the 5′ UTR and c.67 + 32G>C in the first intron) associated with TAR syndrome were ruled out. The validation of the identified copy number variant (CNV) and single nucleotide variant (SNV) was done by segregation analysis in the parents. The impact of the identified non‐coding, 3′ UTR variant on mRNA stability was performed in Family 2 by RBM8A mRNA stability assay using actinomycin which inhibits transcription thereby helping to quantify the rate of mRNA decay. The details of the mRNA stability assay are provided in the Supporting Information.

Further evaluation for Patient 1 could not be performed, as the parents declined additional testing after a prolonged diagnostic journey of nearly 6 years and did not wish to pursue further investigations.

Informed consent was obtained from the families following the Institute Ethics Committee (IEC) approval [IECPG‐563/26.09.2019]. CMA identified a recurrent pathogenic heterozygous null allele, 1q21.1 deletion, in all affected individuals (P1, P2, and P3) (Table 1). The CNV was inherited from the father in Family 1 and from the mother in Family 2.

Sanger sequencing of RBM8A [NM_005105.4] revealed a likely pathogenic, hypomorphic, splice donor region variant, c.*6C>G (rs12079762) in the 3′ untranslated region (UTR), in the affected individuals (P1, P2, and P3) (Figure 2). The variant is reported in ClinVar (VCV000095245.13) as likely pathogenic. The variant was found to be inherited from the mother in Family 1. The genetic status of the father in Family 2 was not available (not tested), and therefore, the inheritance of the variant in this family cannot be determined. No other pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants clinically correlating with the phenotype were identified.

To assess the effect of the hypomorphic allele on transcript stability, an mRNA stability assay was performed using quantitative PCR following Actinomycin D treatment (Supporting Information). In the unaffected control negative for the hypomorphic variant (shown as black line in Figure 2B), the RBM8A showed little degradation till 6 h after actinomycin treatment. In contrast, RBM8A in P2 and his affected brother (P3) carrying the hypomorphic allele degraded faster and reduced by ~50% at 6 h implying the reduced half‐life of RBM8A mRNA in these patients.

TAR syndrome results from compound heterozygosity involving a heterozygous 1q21.1 microdeletion, creating a null allele, together with a hypomorphic RBM8A variant. This unusual inheritance pattern combines a rare null allele with a relatively common regulatory variant [6]. Targeted deletion/duplication analysis and sequencing identify approximately 95% of cases [8]. Because 1q21.1 deletions may extend beyond the minimally deleted 200‐kb region, methods such as quantitative PCR, MLPA, or chromosomal microarray are preferred.

Approximately 75% of TAR syndrome cases inherit the 1q21.1 microdeletion from a parent, while the remainder arise de novo [6, 9]. The 1q21.1 region contains low‐copy repeats predisposing it to non‐allelic homologous recombination and recurrent rearrangements, likely explaining both the frequency of de novo deletions and variable copy number variant (CNV) sizes [6]. In our study, the microdeletion was inherited in both families. Family 1 carried a paternal 1394 kb deletion, whereas Family 2 showed a maternal deletion of 456 kb. The differing deletion sizes observed between parents and affected offspring further highlight the structural complexity of the 1q21.1 locus. Consistent with previous reports, clinically unaffected carriers may harbor large deletions or duplications in this region [10, 11].

Most individuals with TAR syndrome carry one of two common non‐coding RBM8A variants: the 5′UTR variant c.‐21G>A (rs139428292) or the intronic variant c.67 + 32G>C (rs201779890) [4, 6, 12]. Correlating the different genotypic variants with patient phenotype is challenging. The c.‐21G>A variant is associated with lower platelet counts and reduced hemoglobin levels, whereas individuals with c.67 + 32G>C tend to have milder hematological manifestations, with platelet counts stabilizing over time and normal hemoglobin values [4, 13].

Additional RBM8A non‐coding variants have also been implicated in TAR syndrome. The intronic variants c.206 − 13C>A and c.205 + 3_205 + 6del function as null alleles by disrupting normal splicing, resulting in premature truncation or exon skipping [3]. Although rs201779890 does not alter splicing, it is believed to affect transcription factor binding [9]. In both families in our study, we identified the non‐coding RBM8A variant c.*6C>G (rs12079762) in the 3′ UTR splice donor region. This variant acts as a hypomorphic allele and causes disease when present in trans with a null allele such as the 1q21.1 deletion [3, 14]. The allele is relatively common in individuals of African ancestry and was previously identified in 11 of 26 TAR syndrome patients, most of whom were of African descent, suggesting a founder effect [3]. Functional characterization using luciferase reporter assays demonstrated reduced RBM8A expression, consistent with its hypomorphic nature [3].

RBM8A encodes Y14, a core component of the exon‐junction complex involved in mRNA splicing, transport, localization, and nonsense‐mediated decay [15]. Because the identified variant lies within the 3′ UTR, a region important for transcript stability, we hypothesized that it might impair RBM8A mRNA stability. To test this, we performed an mRNA stability assay using actinomycin D [16]. In affected siblings from Family 2, RBM8A transcript levels declined to approximately three‐fifths of baseline within 6 h of treatment, whereas controls showed minimal degradation. These findings support the hypothesis that the c.*6C>G variant reduces transcript stability and increases RNA degradation.

To our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating altered RNA stability in TAR syndrome associated with a non‐coding RBM8A variant using an mRNA degradation assay, supporting the pathogenic role of c.*6C>G in individuals of non‐African origin. However, because both families were diagnosed by targeted Sanger sequencing, we could not assess the possibility of a founder effect within our population.

Regulatory RBM8A variants are relatively common in the general population and are pathogenic only when inherited in trans with a rare null allele such as a 1q21.1 deletion. Therefore, regulatory RBM8A variants may not warrant reporting as incidental findings. In contrast, identifying a null allele has important implications for genetic counseling and reproductive planning, including consideration of partner testing for RBM8A regulatory variants.

This study provides evidence for the association of the non‐coding 3′ UTR variant c.*6C>T in the RBM8A gene with TAR syndrome in Indian patients. These findings expand the genetic spectrum of TAR syndrome and highlight the importance of evaluating noncoding variants in rare genetic disorders.
