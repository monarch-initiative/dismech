# Genotypic and Phenotypic Characterization of Seven Individuals With Predicted Bone Morphogenetic Protein 2 (BMP2) Haploinsufficiency.
**Authors:** Stavrén-Eriksson E, Hammarsjö A, Lindstrand A, Nordgren A, Grigelioniene G, Pigg MH
**Journal:** Clin Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cge.14727](https://doi.org/10.1111/cge.14727)

## Content

Bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2), encoded by the BMP2 gene located in chromosomal region 20p12, is a signalling protein involved in formation of bone and cartilage and other developmental processes such as cardiac and neural development. Haploinsufficiency of BMP2 has been associated with distinct facial features, short stature, skeletal malformations and cardiac abnormalities. The degree of developmental delay is still controversial. We summarise clinical and genetic findings from seven individuals with BMP2 haploinsufficiency. The study participants were identified by genetic testing and their phenotypic data was collected retrospectively from medical records. One individual had a novel frameshift variant in BMP2, and six individuals had 1.3-3.7 Mb microdeletions, including BMP2. In our cohort, delayed language development (4/5) and secretory otitis media (4/5) were common. Our results, together with previous studies, suggest that individuals with sequence variants or small microdeletions can have mild developmental delay or delay in one area (e.g., verbal development or gross motor development). We propose that global developmental delay is either a rare part or not part of the phenotype. Based on our observations, we propose that evaluation of language development and regular controls of the middle ear should be included in the surveillance of these individuals.

Bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP‐2) is a signalling protein that belongs to the transforming growth factor beta (TGF‐β) superfamily [1, 2]. BMP‐2 is expressed in cardiac, skeletal and neural tissues [3, 4]. It is involved in formation of bone and cartilage [5] as well as in several other developmental processes such as craniofacial [6], cardiac [7] and neural development [3]. The OMIM database contains two diagnostic entities linked to heterozygous BMP2 variants: Brachydactyly, type A2 (BDA2; OMIM #112600), which occurs due to duplication of the regulatory element of BMP2, and short stature, facial dysmorphism and skeletal anomalies with or without cardiac anomalies 1 (SSFSC1; OMIM #617877), which occur due to BMP2 haploinsufficiency.

Haploinsufficiency of BMP2 is associated with short stature, skeletal malformations, hearing impairment, cleft palate and cardiac abnormalities including structural abnormalities and arrhythmias [2, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Previous studies reporting individuals with 566.4 kb to 10.7 Mb deletions or pathogenic single nucleotide variants involving BMP2 report phenotype similarities [2, 3, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13], indicating haploinsufficiency of BMP2 as the major determinant of the phenotype [6, 9], The association of developmental delay to BMP2‐associated conditions remains elusive. Tan et al. describe a phenotype ‘without neurological deficits’ [6] while Priestley et al. suggest that BMP2 variants ‘increase risk for neurodevelopmental delays’ [9].

Aiming to improve insights on clinical variability of BMP2‐related disorders, we present seven additional individuals with haploinsufficiency of BMP2.

The individuals were patients and family members that performed BMP2‐carrier testing. Clinical information was collected retrospectively through medical records. Auxology data z‐scores were calculated by WHO child growth standards [14] and for neonatal data using reference of Voigt et al. [15].

Whole genome sequencing was performed at Clinical Genomics, SciLifeLab (Stockholm, Sweden) on HiSeqX, HiSeq2500 or NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) with automated TruSeq DNA PCR‐free library preparation. All called and annotated variants were assigned a prioritisation score using a rank model and analysed in Scout, an interface for variant analysis, as previously described by Stranneheim et al. [16]. Exome sequencing was performed in Individual 5 by Blueprint Genetics.

Validation and segregation analysis of the variant were performed using Sanger sequencing for Family 2 (primer sequences available upon request). For larger deletions, validation and segregation were done using array comparative genomic hybridisation (array‐CGH 180K) (AMADID:031035, Oxford Gene Technology, Begbroke, Oxfordshire, UK) at the department of Clinical Genetics and Genomics at Karolinska University Hospital [17].

The variants identified in this study have been submitted to the ClinVar database.

All individuals were male and at the time of the study, their age ranged between 8 and 47 years, two individuals were adults and five were children or adolescents. All but one individual had short stature (6/7) with z‐score ranging from −3.8 to −1,6. Individual 6 is on the contrary tall, 185 cm (z‐score + 1.16). Individual 5 had partial, isolated, growth hormone deficiency with night growth hormone secretion peak value of 5.3 μg/L at 4 years and 2 months. He received treatment with growth hormone from 4 years and 9 months of age and when he was 8 years old, he was 130.9 cm (z‐score + 0.64). Skeletal anomalies were present in all individuals (6/6). All individuals had specific craniofacial features, traits present in all individuals were dental crowding (6/6), midface hypoplasia (7/7) of different extent and long philtrum (7/7). The most reported developmental delay in our cohort was delayed speech (4/5), including articulation. One of these individuals had no other delays (Individual 5), whereas two of them also had hypotonia in infancy (Individuals 4 and 7) and one had mildly delayed motor development (started walking at age 1 year and 9 months) and a WISC‐V (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children‐V) test indicating an intelligence below average (Individual 1). Individual 3 was reported to have learning difficulties, although no additional data was available. Intelligence was only formally quantified in two individuals, Individual 1 described above and Individual 4 with an average intelligence. The other individuals were subjectively evaluated to have normal intelligence since no information in their journals indicated otherwise, and Individual 6 had a university degree.

Four individuals had recurrent secretory otitis media and needed to go through myringotomy, all of them also had secondary conductive hearing impairment because of secretory otitis media. Three of them also had adenoid hypertrophy and underwent adenoidectomy.

While mild developmental delay, or delay in an isolated area, for example verbal or motor development, has commonly been reported [6, 9], previous studies have not definitively established whether global developmental delay is a core phenotype of BMP2‐haploinsufficiency. Individuals with larger 20p12 deletions, encompassing BMP2 and additional genes, sometimes have more extensive developmental and/or neurocognitive difficulties [2, 3, 13, 18, 19].

Tan et al. suggested that monoallelic BMP2 loss alone does not lead to major neurodevelopmental problems by presenting 12 individuals with normal intellect and minor or no developmental problems [6]. Conversely, Priestley et al. reported developmental data in 14 individuals, with four showing global developmental delay [9]. Notably, three of the four patients with global development delay also had additional genetic variants complicating interpretation [9].

In the literature most individuals (28/29) with BMP2 sequence variants or deletions encompassing only BMP2, and no other genetic variant conflicting interpretation, have no or mild developmental delay [2, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11]. In our cohort, only Individual 1 with a 3.7 Mb deletion presented with global developmental delay (although mild motor delay) and intelligence below average. We hypothesize that it is not the deletion including BMP2 that is responsible for this phenotype since his brother (Individual 2), with the same deletion, managed regular schooling and was not considered for intelligence testing. The remaining individuals in our cohort with isolated BMP2 variants, or smaller deletions, displayed no or mild developmental delay (primarily verbal delays), and they had no apparent intellectual disability. Our data is thus in agreement with the Tan et al. report, and we conclude that monoallelic BMP2 loss is unlikely to be responsible for significant neurodevelopmental problems while patients with larger 20p12 deletions may show more severe developmental delay [6]. However, the results presented by Priestley et al. makes this interpretation somewhat controversial [9].

Regarding speech development, four of the individuals in our study had speech delay including articulation difficulties. These findings align with Priestley et al. that reported three individuals with isolated speech delay [9]. Altogether these results indicate that the delayed speech development is not associated with low intelligence or general developmental delay. We speculate that individuals with BMP2 haploinsufficiency may suffer from articulation difficulties related to facial muscular hypotonia and conductive hearing loss. Middle ear pathology and conductive hearing loss has been reported in individuals with pathogenic variants/deletions in BMP2 [2, 6, 9]. Our findings support this association within the BMP2 phenotype. The conductive hearing impairment detected in four individuals (4/5) in our cohort resulted from repeated secretory otitis media.

Individual 5, who presented with partial growth hormone deficiency, responded well to growth hormone treatment. In the other four individuals where data was available, growth hormone levels were normal. Ahluwalia and Gelb presented a patient with a BMP2 sequence variant and short stature who received treatment with growth hormone between 8 and 13 years of age, though specific growth outcomes were not provided [10]. Additional reports are needed to conclude whether growth hormone deficiency is a part of the BMP2 haploinsufficiency phenotype or just a co‐incidental finding. Until then, we suggest that evaluation of growth hormone levels should be included in the medical investigation.

In conclusion, we suggest that individuals with BMP2 variants or small deletions generally have normal intelligence and that mild developmental delay is sometimes observed. We propose that global developmental delay is either a rare part or not part of the phenotype. Delayed speech, secretory otitis media and conductive hearing loss are common features that require medical attention. Based on our observations, we recommend that evaluation of language development and regular controls of the middle ear are included in the surveillance of these individuals.
