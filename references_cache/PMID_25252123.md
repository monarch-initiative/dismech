# A five generation family with a novel mutation in FOXC2 and lymphedema worsening to hydrops in the youngest generation.
**Authors:** Sargent C, Bauer J, Khalil M, Filmore P, Bernas M, Witte M, Pearson MP, Erickson RP
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2014)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.a.36736](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.a.36736)

## Content

We describe a five generation family with dominantly inherited lymphedema, but no distichiasis, in which 3/3 affected offspring in the fifth generation have died of fetal hydrops and related birth defects. Mutational analysis disclosed a novel mutation in FOXC2 (R121C) in affected members. We searched for possible genetic influences on the greater severity of lymphedema (hydrops) in the fifth generation. Karyotypes disclosed an extra band in Xp in one affected fetus, but this was also found in the mother. Copy number variation (CNV) studies on four members of the pedigree (mother of the three severely affected fetuses/infants; one severely affected; a full, and a half, unaffected sibs) did not detect the source of the Xp band or a possible influence on the severe phenotype. However, use of SNP arrays did allow identification of the portion of the maternal proximal Xp shared by a hydrops-affected daughter and son which was not shared by an unaffected daughter from the same sibship.

Lymphedema-distichiasis (LD[OMIM 153400]) is a dominantly inherited disorder of lymphatics and other tissues, especially eyelids where a double row of eyelashes (disitichiasis) is frequently found [ Erickson, 2008 ]. Of several inherited forms of lymphedema, LD is the one which has most frequently been found with other congenital abnormalities, including cardiac defects, cleft palate, and spinal extradural cysts. Infrequently it has presented as fetal hydrops.

The causative gene was cloned in 2000 and found to be a member of the fforkhead transcription factor family, FOXC2 [ Fang et al, 2000 ]. While identified by a translocation breakpoint in an affected child, it is of note that two children in one of the two initial families (both families showing a dominant inheritance pattern) presented with severe, in utero, fetal hydrops [ Fang et al, 2000 ]. Such severe fetal hydrops in families of dominantly inherited lymphedema is rare enough that it warrants single case reports [ Daniel-Spiegel et al, 2005 ]. Although large studies of congenital lymphedema have concluded that genetic causes are a rarity [ Bellini et al, 2009 ], a recent report identified mutations in known familial lymphedema genes in 25% of surviving cases of generalized in utero lymphedema (two in VEGFR3 and one in FOXC2) [ Ghalamkarpour et al, 2009 ].

We have recently identified a second family with a FOXC2 mutation and multiple cases of fetal hydrops. We identified a novel mutation in FOXC2 in affected family members and searched for causes of fetal hydrops. An extra cytogenetic band on Xp was identified in one of the affected children and the mother, and studies of CNV and inheritance patterns of SNPs did not identify a unique cause of the fatal form of hydrops but were compatible with Xp involvement.

Early generations of this family were briefly presented and illustrated [Family 1, Witte et al, 1998 ]. At that time five individuals (Individuals III 1,3,4 and IV 1,2) were examined with 5/5 having pre-pubertal onset of lymphedema and 4/5 with bilateral leg and genital (2/2 males) involvement. Four out of five of the cases were severe and all had recurrent cellulitis. The severe brawny swelling of the lower limbs was illustrated for Individuals III 2, 3 and 4, while lesser edema was exhibited for Individuals IV1 and 4 (then Individual IV 2) [ Witte et al, 1998 , Fig. 3]. Lymphangioscintigraphy in Individual III4 showed delayed tracer transport, lymphatic hypoplasia with extensive dermal diffusion, and lack of radiotracer in regional or retroperitoneal lymph nodes [ Witte et al., 1998 , Fig. 3]. Despite careful examination, none of the cases had distichiasis.

Since this earlier publication, Individual IV1 has a son with cleft palate and lymphedema (V1). Individual IV4 (previously IV2) has had two unaffected daughters (Individuals V2, 3) and three severely affected children who died of fetal hydrops and birth defects (Individuals V3-7; Fig. 1 shows the extended pedigree, Fig. 2 provides more recent illustrations of affecteds). Individual V 4 was born with tetralogy of Fallot, cystic hygroma, and hydrops and survived to 6 weeks. Individual V5 was terminated at 16 weeks due to cystic hygroma and lymphedema. Individual V6 was a “blighted ovum” which spontaneously aborted at 10 weeks gestation. Individual V7 was born with cleft palate and severe hydrops and died at 3 days of age. The family remains free of distichiasis.

Informed consent for both participation and collection of blood samples was obtained from all family members in accordance with the Institutional Review Board of the University of Arizona. DNA was extracted from either venous blood or buccal swabs by standard procedures. Sequencing of the single exonic, 70% GC rich FOXC2 gene was performed as previously described [ Dellinger et al., 2008 ]. The c.361C>T mutation found leads to a unique restriction site for Nci I in our PCR product. Thus, enzyme digestion was used to type many of the family members. The Nci I cutting created two smaller bands and left half of the larger band in the affected patients with no cutting in the unaffecteds.

Standard giemsa-banded karyotypes were performed on Individuals IV4 and V7 from the pedigree by a commercial lab.

First pass genome-wide genotyping and copy number analysis was carried out on Individuals IV4, V2, V3 and V4 using the IlluminaHumanCytoSNP-12 beadchip array (~300K SNPs from HapMap phases 1 and 2). This chip has a low SNP density on the X chromosome. A second pass analysis using Individuals IV 4, V3, V4, and V7 was performed with the IlluminaHumanOmni1-Quad (<106 SNPs from HapMap phases 1, 2, 3), which has a mean SNP density of 1/ 2.4Kb, with 27,500 markers on X (~1/ 5Kb).

Copy Number Variants for each individual were identified using the Genome Studio software, using CNV partition 3.1.6, where a CNV is called when three consecutive SNPs show an abnormal allele count. CNV calls with a confidence value of <35 were excluded. Large LOH intervals (2 alleles, but no heterozygosity, over 1Mb in length) were manually re-inspected to assess their validity. All CNV intervals have been compared against only the control samples catalogued in dbVar (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/dbvar: build 37) to determine if they were described previously. The size of each CNV interval, with an allele score of 3 or more, in samples from Individuals IV4 and V7 was scrutinized for the possible origin of the abnormal band on the X chromosome.

For analysis of potential X-chromosomal contributions to the phenotype, comparison of Individuals V3 and V4 (same paternal X chromosome) was performed in Genome Studio with genotype data from the IlluminaHumanCytoSNP-12, using the genome viewer, chromosome browser function. The differential interval on the X chromosome was confirmed as distal maternal Xp, and refined using SNP data from Individuals IV4, V3, V4 and V7, scored on the IlluminaHumanOmni1-Quad.

For the non-PAR region of the X chromosome, the genotype of Individual V7 was nominally assigned maternal allele 1 for each X-chromosomal locus (9960 markers for Xp). Maternal allele 2 was deduced from the genotype of the mother, IV4. The paternal X chromosomal alleles were imputed by comparing the genotypes of Individuals V3 and V4, as they have inherited the same allele for each locus from their father. By reconstructing the genotypes across the X chromosome, it was possible to determine if the full sibs, sisters V3 and V4, had inherited maternal allele 1 or 2 from the maternal X chromosomes. Data from the IlluminaHumanCytoSNP-12 were utilized to compare the half-sib V2 (2300 common alleles). Where Individual IV4 was heterozygous, but Individuals V2 and V4 were homozygous, it was possible to impute which maternal allele had been inherited by Individual V2. The alleles, as inherited by each offspring of Individual IV4 are summarized graphically in Figure 2.

Sequencing revealed a c.361C>T alteration which was confirmed by sequencing both strands. This results in a novel R121C substitution (mutations reviewed in Dellinger, et al, 2008 ; none further at HGMD.org) in the forkhead box of helix 3 and is a very highly conserved site. As indicated in Fig. 1, all affected members studied had the mutation.

Karyotyping disclosed an anomalous banding pattern in distal Xp in Individual V7 and his mother, IV4. Flourescence in situ hybridization (FISH) with probes for the subtelomeric region of the X short arm (Xp,Yp, Vysis) and Xp22.32, the second most distal band on the X short arm, (STS, Vysis) showed normal localizations.

From the CNV analyses, multiple large regions, >1Mb, of homozygosity (two alleles, monozygosity) were detected, and are presented separately since losses are more important (Table I, and Supplementary Table I in supporting information online) from areas of copy number variation (loss, copy 0-1; gain, allele copy 3-4: gains are not shown. No intervals detected through these analyses were unique to the pedigree under investigation: all had been reported in the control samples catalogued in dbVar.

With the IlluminaHumanCytoSNP-12 beadchip array, SNP studies showed that an unaffected daughter (V3) differed from her hydrops-affected sister (V4) by a portion of the mother's Xp from p11.3 to p22.2 (Fig 3A). Confirmation that this portion of the mother's X was shared by the hydrops affected son (V7) came from analysis of the Omni1-Quad data. Recombination breakpoints with respect to the maternal X chromosomes were identified from comparison of the genotypes of the offspring in the non-PAR region of Xp (Fig 3B). Individuals V3 and V4 share maternal markers on the X distal to rs5952572 (~44.2Mb): the breakpoint lies between markers rs1451511 and rs5952572, with the intervening SNP genotypes proving uninformative. The region of the maternal X chromosome common to individuals V4 and V7 extends from the PAR boundary to 22.9 Mb, with a breakpoint between markers rs5926147 and rs5926152. Its gene content is given in Supplementary Table II. The half-sibs V4 and V2 have inherited the opposite alleles in distal Xp, with a breakpoint between markers rs3764880 (12.9Mb) and rs5979833 (13.2Mb). If the abnormal cytogenetic pattern reported for V7 is diagnostic for the phenotype, and correlates with the defined maternal allele 1, then individual V2 should not carry this cytogenetic abnormality. We did not have cell lines to test whether the hydrops affected daughter (V5) also shared the maternal region in common with the other affected individuals, or has the cytogenetic anomaly observed in the hydrops affected son.

FOXC2 maps to 16q24.3 and lies in a cluster of three forkhead genes. FOXC2 produces a 2.2 kb transcript with a 1.5 kb single exon coding region that is highly (~70%) GC-rich [ Kaestner et al., 1996 ]. The FOXC2 protein contains 501 amino acids. The one known domain in the gene is the forkhead domain. One functional Foxc2 consensus forkhead domain–binding site has been defined in a reporter assay system [ Miura et al., 1997 ]. However, binding specificity in vivo is thought to involve both the conserved forkhead domain and flanking sequences [ Miura et al., 1997 ]. It up regulates Ang2 [ Xue et al, 2008 ] and integrin beta 3 [ Hayashi et al, 2008 ] and down regulates Pax3 [ Lagha et al, 2009 ].

The mutation found in the family is in the highly conserved DNA-binding, FOX box. Previously a mutation at the same amino acid site (R121H) was extensively studied by Berry et al [2005] . When FOXC2 with the mutation was expressed in cultured cells following plasmid transfection, the mutant protein was stable but showed decreased phosphorylation and was grossly defective in nuclear localization. Gel shift mobility tests and luciferase response expression showed defective binding to a FOXC2, DNA-targeting sequence [ Berry et al, 2005 ]. Amino acid 121 is located externally on the 3rd helix and the cysteine substitution would be accessible to disulfide linkage so different effects (no distichiasis) than other mutations might be explained by redox differences between tissues.

Karyotyping disclosed an apparent extra band on Xp which was not a telomeric duplication as studied with two probes. Xp has been strongly implicated as the location of gene(s) which are involved in lymphedema in Turner patients [ Boucher, et al, 2001 , Fig 4]. The most plausible candidate for the phenotype is VEGFD, the gene for a lymphatic growth factor, at ~15.4Mb.

The possible role for VEGFD variants as modifiers of lymphedema remains contentious. VEGFD is increased in the serum of patients with primary lymphedema [ Fink et al, 2004 ], and in acute Kawasaki disease with edema [ Ebata et al., 2011 ]: upregulation of protein expression also appears to correlate to increased lymphangiogenesis, increased rates of metastasis and poor prognosis in a number of studies of cancer patients [for examples, see Nakamura et al., 2003; Harris et al., 2011 ; Lin et al, 2011; Yanagawa et al., 2012 ]. In addition, over expression of VEGFD has been shown to promote neo-lymphangiogenesis in tumors, again supporting a role in human lymphangiogenesis [ Stacker et al, 2001 , Koch et al, 2009 ] but other work shows only VEGFC to have such a role ( Shibata et al, 2013 ]. It seems not to be a critical gene for lymphedema, at least in mice, since the mouse knockout of of VegfD has minimal effects on lymphangiogenesis [Baldwin et al, 2001] and the double knockout of VegfC and VegfD shows the same phenotype as the VegfC knockout [ Haiko et al, 2008 ]. However, expression of VegfD in skin (targeted by a keratin 14 promoter) can rescue the lymphatic dysplasia seen in VegfR3 heterozygous knockouts. This suggests functional redundancy of VegfC and VegfD. In a zebra fish model, VegfD activates Sox18 expression and plays a role in blood vasculogenesis as well [ Duong et al, 2014 ].

Initially, given the evidence for correlation of elevated expression of serum VEGFD and lymphedema, and the abnormal appearance of the X chromosome in this pedigree, we wondered if the sub PAR interval of the X chromosome containing VEGFD (aka FIGF) could be duplicated. Thus, we used CNV studies to search for such a possible duplication. However, there was no evidence for a CNV gain involving this locus, or any other region of Xp, that was large enough to explain the abnormal banding pattern observed by cytogenetic analysis. There were many large intervals of apparent monozygosity probably arising from low minor allele (or extended haplotype) frequencies in these regions of the genome. In all of the examples listed, other family members show substantial overlapping regions of monozygosity below the 1Mb interval size used as a cut-off value in the software. Furthermore, no regions of chromosomal gain were large enough (CNV size range 412bp to 23897bp) to explain the abnormal banding pattern of the maternal X inherited by her hydrops affected son.

There is still support for involvement of the abnormal X from analysis of the Illumina data. A difference was found between the affected and unaffected siblings: the hydrops affected infants shared a portion of the maternal Xp not inherited by an unaffected sibling. It is possible that this shared portion of Xp represents the portion of the chromosome involved in the cytogenetic differences, and covers the lymphedema critical regions that are causative for the fetal hydrops. Significantly this interval harbors the locus for VEGFD, the only gene between PAR and 22.9Mb currently known to be involved in lymphangiogenesis (See Supplementary Table II in supporting information online).

Although the additional band could be of autosomal origin, no region of the genome with a copy number gain was large enough to predict a cytogenetically visible alteration to the karyotype. Therefore, autosomal material can only be attributed to the cytogenetic abnormality if this reflects a balanced translocation which is not cytogenetically obvious. Finally, since the hydrops affected infants do share the same maternal X chromosome alleles, the banding pattern could reflect an intra-chromosomal rearrangement on Xp. This would not necessarily be detectable using SNP arrays, unless a structural rearrangement is accompanied by a substantial amplification or loss of neighboring genomic material. Unfortunately, all CNVs on the X chromosome are also seen in normal individuals, and do not appear to be unique to this family.

The same karyotypic finding for the X chromosome was present in the mother who did not show hydrops. However, there are possible scenarios to explain this and maintain its relevance: her chromosomal mutation could be somatic and include the germ-line or it might not have a zygotic effect, but an effect with maternal transmission. There may also be allelic variants of other genes which interact with VEGFD polymorphisms, of which we are unaware at this time, that have been inherited from the father of the affected offspring. Thus our CNV studies do not disclose a possible genomic cause of the increased severity of disease in this case from the fifth generation. Additionally, we are limited by not having cell lines and SNP analysis on more of the family. Currently, therefore, we do not have a definitive explanation for the occurrence of hydrops in three cases from this pedigree or for the two cases initially published [ Fang et al., 2000 ].
