# Pretreatment blood NfL indicates response to cellular therapies in cerebral adrenoleukodystrophy.
**Authors:** Weinhofer I, Ponleitner M, Rommer P, Bergner CG, Starke S, Gräfe D, Merkenschlager A, Forss-Petter S, Köhler W, Berger J, Kühl JS
**Journal:** Commun Med (Lond) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s43856-026-01525-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-026-01525-6)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The neuroinflammatory cerebral form of X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (CALD) is among the most severe neurological diseases affecting children. Early intervention by hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) or gene therapy halts CALD, justifying its inclusion in newborn screening programmes. Currently, eligibility for treatment is assessed using MRI-based Loes and neurological scoring; however, grading poorly differentiates advanced stages or atypical lesion patterns. We recently identified blood neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), indicative of axonal and glial damage, as valid biomarkers reflecting CALD activity.

METHODS: Here, we investigated how pre-treatment biomarker levels relate to outcome of HSCT or gene therapy in a retrospective cohort of 14 paediatric CALD patients aged 5-13 years. Patients were monitored longitudinally before and for up to 5.5 years following HSCT or gene therapy. Disease progression was assessed using MRI-based Loes scoring and neurological evaluations. Plasma NfL and GFAP were quantified using single molecule array (Simoa) technology.

RESULTS: Here we show that blood NfL and GFAP transiently increase due to myeloablative conditioning and gradually normalize post-treatment. Pre-treatment NfL ≤ 113 pg/ml, but not GFAP, correlate with CALD stabilization or, for Loes >9, only minor progression. In contrast, NfL >243 pg/ml associates with major progression irrespective of baseline Loes score. In three boys with atypical lesions or advanced disease, NfL outperforms Loes-scoring in predicting outcome.

CONCLUSIONS: Blood NfL can complement clinical decision-making, particularly in patients with advanced CALD or atypical lesions. These findings are especially relevant for clinical management in countries that have not yet implemented X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy into newborn screening.

X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (X-ALD, OMIM #300100) is the most common monogenetically inherited brain white matter disease and has been recently added to newborn screening programmes in several countries1,2. X-ALD is caused by ABCD1 mutations that lead to impaired peroxisomal degradation and accumulation of saturated very long-chain fatty acids3,4. The core syndrome of X-ALD in adulthood is adrenomyeloneuropathy (AMN), a slowly progressive dying-back axonopathy in the spinal cord5. In more than half of all male X-ALD patients, with peak onset in boyhood, yet unknown genetic, epigenetic or environmental triggers induce a life-threatening progressive leukodystrophy (cerebral ALD, CALD) that cannot be predicted based on the genotype6–8. CALD is characterized by rapidly advancing inflammatory destruction of myelin and axons with disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and infiltration of peripheral immune cells6. To detect the onset of CALD presymptomatically, patients undergo a burdensome, frequent MRI screening, often under sedation, to visualize inflammatory brain white matter lesions and opening of the BBB by extravasation of gadolinium (Gd)-based contrast agents9.

The standard of care for paediatric CALD is hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), either allogenic with a suitable donor or autologous with ex vivo lentiviral gene therapy10. However, the latter approach has recently been associated with an increased risk of hematologic cancer11. Exactly how HSCT halts CALD is not yet completely understood, but strong evidence points to the cerebral engraftment of donor-derived or corrected peripheral macrophages that replace dysfunctional microglial cells12. Following HSCT, restoration of the BBB is usually evident within 100 days13. In contrast, myelin destruction and axonal injury, as semi-quantitatively assessed by conventional MRI, may progress for up to 18 months after the HSCT intervention14. Accordingly, patients with higher pre-treatment MRI severity scores, indicating a more advanced disease status, generally show higher neurocognitive decline after transplant15.

Defining the eligibility of CALD patients for treatment is key to enable survival free of major functional deficits14,16. Currently, CALD progression is graded using MRI-severity Loes scoring, ranging from 0 to 34, whereby higher values correlate with an increasing number of demyelinated structures and atrophy17. However, this grading system poorly reflects the size and dynamics of demyelinating lesions or patients with atypical patterns18,19. Therefore, it may be challenging to decide on the Loes score alone who is eligible for cellular therapies, especially in countries without a newborn screening programme. Moreover, Loes' scoring was not intended to monitor treatment success due to its continued increase in successfully transplanted patients with local brain gliosis and subsequent atrophy10. As such, there is an unmet need for alternative biomarkers to assist clinicians in facilitating treatment decisions, predicting disease outcome, and monitoring disease activity.

We and others recently demonstrated that the blood-derived biomarkers neurofilament light chain protein (NfL), a neuronal scaffolding protein indicating axonal damage, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), indicating astrocyte activation or damage, reflect the inflammatory activity and progression of CALD20–23. So far, NfL shows the best correlation with neuroaxonal damage and brain white matter lesion volume in CALD patients, including decreasing levels upon halt of the disease by HSCT20–22,24,25. However, no data are available on how pre-treatment blood NfL associates with treatment outcome in CALD. Here, we investigated the prognostic potential of pre-treatment blood NfL and GFAP levels for post-treatment response and outcome in transplanted children with CALD. We show that pre-treatment blood NfL, but not GFAP, is associated with treatment response in children undergoing HSCT or gene therapy for CALD. Lower NfL levels are linked to subsequent disease stabilization, whereas higher levels identify patients who remain at risk of progression despite intervention. We also observe that NfL provides useful prognostic information in cases with atypical lesions or advanced MRI severity, outperforming Loes-scoring in these patients. Together, these findings suggest that pre-treatment blood NfL adds clinically relevant information beyond current imaging-based assessments and may support decision-making for children with CALD.

The longitudinal retrospective single-centre cohort study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of the Medical University of Vienna (EK1613/2019) and local biobanking by the Ethical Review Board of the University Hospital Leipzig (EK 370/21). Plasma samples were collected after informed consent was obtained from legal guardians and participants. With X-ALD being an X-linked disorder and CALD very rarely affecting females, we included male paediatric X-ALD patients (n = 18, median age = 8.5 years) who were diagnosed based on elevated plasma VLCFAs by gas chromatography—mass spectroscopy and/or (likely) pathogenic ABCD1 mutations. The longitudinal HSCT group consisted of 11 children (median age = 7 years; median Loes score = 4) and the gene therapy group of 3 children (median age = 9 years; median Loes score = 2). In addition, plasma samples from 4 CALD children up to 9.5 years post-allogeneic HSCT were assessed. Children of the gene therapy group received LentiD (bluebird bio) in the study ALD-102 (#12) or in the study ALD-104 (#13 and #14) with the respective conditioning protocol28.

For acquiring the scans, a 3.0-tesla MRI system (Prismafit; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) was used. The protocol included T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences covering all three dimensions. Gd was added as contrast enhancement at baseline examination and after treatment, at least until unequivocal resolution of Gd enhancement was confirmed in a follow-up scan.

Blood samples were collected at the University of Leipzig, Germany, into standard polypropylene EDTA collection tubes and processed at room temperature within two h. Plasma was generated by centrifugation at 3.500 × g for 5 min and stored aliquoted, pseudonymized at −80 °C. NfL and GFAP levels were assessed by a blinded investigator using Simoa® NF-light Advantage Kit (#103400, Quanterix), GFAP Simoa® Discovery Kit (#102336, Quanterix) and the Simoa® Neurology 2-Plex B (NfL, GFAP) assay (#103520) on an SR-X Analyzer (Quanterix).

Our longitudinal dataset consisted of plasma samples derived from 14 boys with CALD (median age = 7.5 years, median Loes score = 3.5) with samples taken 29–10 days (median = 12 days) before and at various time points up to 5.5 years after allogeneic HSCT (#1 to #11) or gene therapy (#12 to #14). In addition, plasma was collected from 4 CALD children (median age = 14 years) at 4–9.5 years after allogeneic HSCT (#15 to #18). In all patients, MRI analysis confirmed lesion progression, whereas Gd-enhancement using a standard single-dose condition was not observed in three (#1, #2 and #3) of the 14 longitudinal patients. Baseline characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

To explore whether pre-treatment NfL and/or GFAP could complement MRI in predicting treatment outcome for individual CALD patients, we determined NfL and GFAP before and longitudinally up to 5.5 years post allogeneic HSCT or gene therapy in plasma samples of 14 boys with CALD including 4 patients with uncommon lesion patterns (#7 and #8, additional brain stem lesion; #13, brain stem lesion only; #14, frontal involvement) and 3 with advanced demyelination (#7, 10 and #11, Loes scores ≥12, respectively) (Fig. 1). In contrast to NfL, pre-treatment GFAP levels were neither consistently higher in CALD patients when compared to the median GFAP value of the asymptomatic X-ALD group nor were these levels associated with treatment outcomes (Fig.1C, Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). Moreover, there was a better correlation between pre-treatment NfL and MRI severity score than for GFAP levels (Fig. 1B, D), thus confirming our previously reported data21.

Patients with advanced CALD have been described to deteriorate more rapidly under aggressive chemotherapeutic preconditioning27. Chemotherapy has also been associated with a transient increase in neurotoxicity in multiple sclerosis (MS) patients treated with HSCT29. Thus, we assumed that the CALD patients with marked acute axonal damage, as reflected by high plasma NfL levels (≥201 pg/ml; #8, #10, #11 and #14), may be especially vulnerable to the neurotoxic effects of the conditioning regimen. Accordingly, we compared the NfL levels of these more advanced patients before and 1 month after HSCT or gene therapy to those of earlier-stage CALD children with lower plasma NfL (≤113 pg/ml; #1 to #7, #9, #13, Fig. 2A, B). Intriguingly, we found a significant transient increase in NfL upon conditioning 1 month post-treatment relative to baseline only for the CALD group with relatively low pre-treatment NfL (Fig. 2A). No significant further elevation of NfL was observed 1 month post treatment in the CALD group already presenting high pre-treatment NfL levels (Fig. 2B). Similarly, plasma GFAP was significantly stronger increased 1 month post-treatment in the group of early-stage CALD when compared to the group of more advanced CALD children (Fig. 2C, D). With neuroinflammatory CALD being one of the most severe brain disorders affecting children, X-ALD has been added to newborn screening programmes in several countries. Lifesaving allogeneic HSCT or gene therapy can effectively halt CALD progression, but only if applied at early disease stages. Thus, prompt diagnosis of CALD onset and assessment of eligibility for treatment is crucial. We compared in the present study the prognostic value of pre-treatment blood NfL and GFAP, as measured in plasma samples using Simoa technology, in a cohort of 14 childhood CALD patients who received allogeneic HSCT or gene therapy. The data revealed for patients with baseline Loes score <9 that pre-treatment plasma NfL levels of ≤113 pg/ml correlate with stabilized CALD course post-treatment. In contrast, pre-treatment NfL of > 243 pg/ml was associated with major progression of the inflammatory cerebral demyelination, i.e. development of major functional deficits, independent of the baseline Loes score. Further, blood NfL only gradually normalized in CALD patients, a pattern similarly observed in MS29,30 but contrasting with the more rapid decline seen within 6 months of pharmacological fingolimod or tocilizumab treatment in MS or neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, respectively31,32.

Evaluating treatment eligibility through MRI-based Loes scoring is especially demanding in CALD children with atypical lesion patterns or more advanced disease stages. The latter aspect is particularly relevant for countries where X-ALD newborn screening has not been implemented, and CALD is often diagnosed at later symptomatic stages. With the limitation of a small sample set, we compared the prognostic value of pre-treatment plasma NfL levels and Loes scores for our paediatric CALD cohort. Applying Loes score ≤9 as threshold for treatment eligibility and pre-treatment NfL ≤113 pg/ml to indicate probable stabilization post-treatment, we found that higher pre-treatment NfL correlated with disease progression post-treatment in two CALD patients (#8 and #14) with more advanced disease status and unusual lesion pattern, despite seemingly favourable Loes scores 6.5 and 6, respectively. In contrast, lower pre-treatment NfL of 112 pg/ml in patient #7 with atypical lesions and a high Loes score of 12 resulted in only minor progression post-treatment.

Our finding that myeloablative conditioning using the chemotherapeutic agent busulfan, combined with either cyclophosphamide or fludarabine, transiently exacerbates axonal damage and astrocyte activation, elevating the systemic release of NfL and GFAP in paediatric CALD aligns with previous observations in adult patients with severe MS29,30. The potential relationship between this transient increase in neurotoxicity and neurocognitive impairment in the already compromised brains of CALD patients remains unexplored. Additionally, it remains unclear whether the accumulation of saturated very long-chain fatty acids, which have been previously linked to pro-inflammatory skewing of immune cells33,34, also potentiates the susceptibility of brain cells to the neurotoxic effects of chemotherapeutic conditioning. However, the transient increase in NfL may be in part be characteristic of busulfan, since paediatric leukemia patients conditioned with busulfan also display some elevation, albeit to a lesser extent (Weinhofer/Kühl, unpublished data). Therefore, reduced-intensity or non-myeloablative conditioning protocols omitting busulfan might attenuate the chemotherapy-associated neurotoxicity reflected by the transient rise in NfL and GFAP19,35. As all patients in our cohort received busulfan-based myeloablative regimens, we cannot compare biomarker dynamics between conditioning strategies. However, while myeloablative conditioning may be associated with further injury to the CNS in childhood CALD patients, correction of the defective monocyte/macrophage system by engraftment of the allogeneic donor or corrected autologous myelopoiesis is pivotal. Therefore, the benefits of myeloablation far outweigh the risks by preventing the devastating and ultimately fatal outcome of CALD progression. Furthermore, in early-stage patients, the transitory increase in plasma NfL or GFAP is without clinical correlation.

With X-ALD being a rare disorder (combined male and female incidence 1:14,700) and about 30% of boys below the age of 12 developing CALD5, the major limitation of our study is its small sample size, comprising longitudinal samples from 14 children with CALD including 3 patients with advanced demyelination, which reflects the rarity of paediatric CALD and the restricted availability of longitudinally sampled patients undergoing cellular therapies. Moreover, our study included only 3 patients who received gene therapy, while 11 children underwent allogeneic HSCT. It is possible that NfL levels indicating stabilization post-treatment differ between these two approaches, similar to how efficacy appears to be somewhat different36. Detailed neurocognitive follow-up data were not available for all patients, limiting our ability to relate chronic low-grade biomarker elevation to long-term functional outcomes. The persistent low-grade elevation of NfL and GFAP levels observed years after HSCT likely reflect residual effects of prior neuroinflammation but cannot be correlated with neurocognitive trajectories in our dataset. Further, the exhibited heterogeneity in lesion distribution, Gd-enhancement status and MRI severity within this small cohort of patients limits conclusions about biomarker performance across the full spectrum of CALD presentations. Therefore, we could not perform multivariable modelling to adjust for confounders such as baseline Loes score, lesion pattern, age, or conditioning regimen; thus, we cannot establish NfL as an independent predictor of treatment response. Accordingly, the limited cohort size restricts the generalizability and applicability of our findings, which may not fully capture variability in the relationship between pre-treatment NfL levels and treatment outcome in larger populations. Further, the retrospective, single-centre design introduces potential sources of selection and measurement bias, including non-uniform MRI timing and variability in clinical sampling intervals. As a consequence of these limitations, the proposed plasma NfL threshold ranges should be interpreted with caution and validated in larger, multicentre cohorts. Nevertheless, our data provide valuable insights relevant for the clinical management of childhood CALD, especially in countries that have not implemented X-ALD newborn screening and a relevant proportion of patients present with CALD in rather advanced stages, with questionable benefits of cellular therapies.
