# Expanding the phenotypic and genetic spectrum of ORAS with novel homozygous variant in OTULIN gene: a case series and literature review.
**Authors:** AlSaleem A, Assiri A, Alfadda R, Al-Jumaah S, Al-Mayouf SM
**Journal:** Pediatr Rheumatol Online J (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12969-025-01129-9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12969-025-01129-9)

## Content

Autoinflammatory diseases (AID), are a group of rare heterogeneous disorders, originate from dysregulation in the innate immune system. They represent an expanding array of conditions characterized by recurrent fever episodes and systemic inflammation, resulting from uncontrolled activation of proinflammatory cytokines and subsequent damage to host tissues. Over the past two decades, there have been significant advances in the genetics and genomics of AID. [1]. OTULIN, a highly conserved deubiquitinase, regulates linear polyubiquitin modifications produced by the linear ubiquitin assembly chain complex (LUBAC). OTULIN acts as a suppressor of the NF-kB signaling pathway, working in tandem with the LUBAC complex to maintain immune homeostasis at the cellular level and prevent the activation of NF-kB and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-induced cells [2]. A deficiency in OTULIN disrupts the NF-kB pathway, leading to upregulation of the TNF cytokine triggering inflammatory cascade [2–4]. A loss-of-function mutation in OTULIN is associated with OTULIN-related autoinflammatory syndrome (ORAS), also known as Otulopenia, a severe and potentially life-threatening AID. This condition is characterized by early-onset systemic inflammation, manifesting with recurrent fevers, failure to thrive, skin rash, lipodystrophy, musculoskeletal, and gastrointestinal inflammation [5, 6]. ORAS was first documented in 2016, with the initial report including four patients. All patients had neonatal-onset disease, recurrent fever, panniculitis, vomiting or diarrhea, and elevated serum inflammatory markers [5]. To date, ten patients with molecularly confirmed ORAS have been reported, the majority with biallelic loss-of-function mutation [5–9]. Recently, the first heterozygous dominant negative variant was reported in two patients from unrelated families [10]. Given the rarity of this condition, we aim to report the phenotype and genetic characteristics of the three cases with ORAS in Arab population and compare our data with previously published reports.


First patient is a 5-year girl first exhibited symptoms at 12 months of age, including persistent high-grade fever, respiratory distress, abdominal discomfort, vomiting, and diarrhea. She also developed a skin abscess on her right forearm and right cheek (Fig. 1a + b). Laboratory tests showed elevated white cell count (WBC) (63.7 [nl range 4.30–11.30 109/L]), platelet count (1108 [nl range 155–435 109/L]), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (105 [nl range 0–15 mm/Hr]), and CRP (300 [nl range < 1 mg/L]). However, her microbiological cultures were negative. She suffered recurrent fever episodes, accompanied by respiratory distress and gastrointestinal involvement. Along with skin abscesses, she developed multiple abscesses in the lungs and spleen (Fig. 1d). Her condition worsened, leading to critical care admissions and the need for invasive ventilatory support. Laboratory findings indicated significant leukocytosis, thrombocytosis, and increased inflammatory markers. All tests for potential infectious and immunodeficiency causes were inconclusive. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) revealed a novel homozygous pathogenic mutation in the OTULIN gene, c. 553G > T (p.Val185Phe). Familial segregation analysis showed heterozygous carrier status in both parents and siblings. She was initiated on a TNF inhibitor (Adalimumab) and corticosteroid regimen, which was gradually tapered and discontinued in a duration of 6 months. She demonstrated improved growth parameters and normal social and intellectual development. Her cytosis and inflammatory markers normalized. Second patient is a 16-year-old male who initially presented symptoms at 24 months of age included fever, skin abscess in the gluteal region, leukocytosis, and high inflammatory markers. He continued to experience recurrent episodes characterized by high fevers, fatigue, and skin abscesses. An abdominal ultrasound revealed multiple splenic abscesses (Fig. 1e). Cultures tested negative for pyogenic bacteria, mycobacterials, viruses, and fungi. Comprehensive immunological tests yielded unremarkable results. At the age of five, he suffered a severe episode included intense abdominal pain, vomiting, and diarrhea, along with recurrent fever and cutaneous manifestations. Laboratory results indicated a high WBC count (56.3 109/L), platelet count (1294 109/L), ESR (125 mm/hr), and CRP (300 mg/L). His clinical course was complicated by bowel obstruction, necessitating an urgent exploratory laparotomy. WES identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in the OTULIN gene, c. 553G > T (p.Val185Phe). He was managed with a three days course of pulse corticosteroids, followed by maintenance doses with a tapering plan, and initiated on Adalimumab. This resulted in clinical remission. Currently at 16 years of age, he is on Adalimumab as monotherapy, clinically asymptomatic with normalisation of his inflammatory markers and cell counts. He exhibits age-appropriate growth parameters and maintains an average academic performance in school.

Third patient is a 12-year-old boy presented at the age of four years with subtle dysmorphic features, abdominal discomfort, and a splenic abscess. He subsequently developed recurrent fever episodes associated with nodular lesions as well as skin and splenic abscesses (Fig. 1c + f). These episodes recurred every 2–3 months over the span of a year. A physical examination revealed cervical lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory tests showed normal cell counts but elevated inflammatory markers (ESR = 38 mm/hr, CRP = 28 mg/L), with a normal immunological workup. Cultures from the skin lesion tested positive for a fungal (candida) infection. The patient was initiated on anti-fungal therapy (Fluconazole), which led to a significant response, complete resolution of the splenic and skin abscesses, and normalization of his laboratory results. WES identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in the OTULIN gene, c. 553G > T (p.Val185Phe). He is currently on prophylactic anti-fungal treatment. Two years follow-up assessments have shown a stable clinical course with no further attacks, appropriate developmental milestones, and overall well-being.

In this report, we described three patients from three unrelated families with confirmed consanguinity from the Arab population diagnosed with ORAS. Typically, patients with classic ORAS present with neonatal-onset disease, characterized by panniculitis and gastrointestinal symptoms, with a subset developing sterile abscesses (Table 1) [5–10]. Interestingly, our patients had a later disease onset compared to previously reported cases. Specifically, patient III first noticed splenic and skin abscesses at the age of five years, unaccompanied by gastrointestinal or musculoskeletal symptoms. It remains uncertain whether the complete resolution of his condition was due to the therapeutic intervention with anti-fungal medication, as all cultures, including fungal, were negative. When comparing the clinical manifestations of ORAS patients in our series with those from other countries, it is noteworthy that two patients had lung disease. Despite our understanding of ORAS pathogenesis, optimal medical treatment to control disease activity remains elusive. Studies by Zhao et al. and Damgaard et al. have shown that TNF blockers, specifically Infliximab, are more effective in treating inflammatory disease than IL-1 blockade [5, 6]. Similarly, two of our patients exhibited a dramatic response to TNF blockers (Adalimumab), and corticosteroids were subsequently tapered and eventually discontinued. The most recent follow-up visit showed no active disease, improved growth parameters, and normalized inflammatory markers (Fig. 2). In conclusion, ORAS is a potentially life-threatening condition that necessitates early recognition and initiation of therapeutic intervention. Certain factors, such as early-onset sterile systemic inflammation affecting the skin and gastrointestinal tract, and the presence of hematopoiesis, can be considered as the most relevant clues to predict the suspicion of ORAS. We report three patients with a novel disease-causing genetic variant of the OTULIN gene. Our findings align with previous reports; all patients experienced recurrent multiorgan inflammation with diffuse sterile abscesses (skin, spleen, and lung), nonspecific gastrointestinal manifestations, hematopoiesis, and elevated inflammatory markers. Interestingly, none of our patients had an early or neonatal onset, nor did they exhibit panniculitis or lipodystrophy, which are considered as the most observed features in reported literature. We believe our study broadens the spectrum of ORAS phenotypic findings, highlighting considerable variability and atypical features in relation to novel genetic variants. Furthermore, this work underscores the critical need for early recognition and genetic diagnosis of such life-threatening inflammatory disorders, emphasizing the importance of early therapeutic intervention.
