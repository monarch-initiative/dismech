Angelman syndrome (AS), an early-onset neurodevelopmental disorder characterized
by abnormal gait, intellectual disabilities, and seizures, occurs when the
maternal allele of the UBE3A gene is disrupted, since the paternal allele is
silenced in neurons by the UBE3A antisense (UBE3A-AS) transcript. Given the
importance of early treatment, we hypothesized that prenatal delivery of an
antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) would downregulate the murine Ube3a-AS,
resulting in increased UBE3A protein and functional rescue. Using a mouse model
with a Ube3a-YFP allele that reports on-target ASO activity, we found that in
utero, intracranial (IC) injection of the ASO resulted in dose-dependent
activation of paternal Ube3a, with broad biodistribution. Accordingly, in utero
injection of the ASO in a mouse model of AS also resulted in successful
restoration of UBE3A and phenotypic improvements in treated mice on the
accelerating rotarod and fear conditioning. Strikingly, even intra-amniotic (IA)
injection resulted in systemic biodistribution and high levels of UBE3A
reactivation throughout the brain. These findings offer a novel strategy for
early treatment of AS using an ASO, with two potential routes of administration
in the prenatal window. Beyond AS, successful delivery of a therapeutic ASO into
neurons has implications for a clinically feasible prenatal treatment for
numerous neurodevelopmental disorders.

Prenatal gene therapy of somatic cells has the potential to treat severe disorders that can be diagnosed before birth, including neurodevelopmental diseases.1 Advantages of delivering a therapy to the fetus include preventing the onset of irreversible organ damage, accessing neurons during a period of increased blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, and inducing tolerance to new proteins in the fetal environment.2 Among gene therapy approaches, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are a safe and effective strategy to treat a range of disorders without the potential toxicities of genomic integration seen with viral vectors.3 ASOs can treat numerous conditions by regulating the alternative splicing, degradation, or translation of specific precursor mRNAs or mRNAs.3 Prenatal applications of ASOs have demonstrated efficacy in a mouse model of Usher syndrome.4,5 Given the potential applicability of ASOs to treat a broad range of severe, early-onset diseases for which prenatal therapy would be optimal, we sought to understand the safety, biodistribution, and efficacy of ASOs in a mouse model.

One of the most compelling indications for prenatal therapy is Angelman syndrome (AS), a single-gene disorder characterized by intellectual disability, sleep abnormalities, seizures, and ataxia.6,7 AS is caused by mutations of the 15q11–13 region, affecting multiple genes, most notably the imprinted gene UBE3A, which encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase protein 3a (UBE3A).8 Neuronal function is impaired if the maternal allele of the UBE3A is disrupted, since the paternal allele is usually silenced in neurons.9 This paternal UBE3A is silenced by a nuclear-localized long non-coding RNA, UBE3A antisense transcript (UBE3A-AS).10 Degradation of the UBE3A-AS transcript by a gapmer ASO can reactivate the intact paternal UBE3A allele, leading to a genetic rescue of AS.11,12

Several phase I and II clinical trials of ASOs are underway to treat pediatric patients with AS (NCT04428281, NCT05127226, and NCT04259281). However, research using transgenic mouse models in which maternal Ube3a expression can be temporally induced13 or deleted14 during critical developmental time windows has demonstrated the importance of maternal Ube3a expression during prenatal development to ameliorate specific behavioral phenotypes, supporting the need for early treatment. Prenatal diagnosis of AS is feasible since deletions in the 15q11–13 region that lead to AS are detectable on cell-free non-invasive prenatal tests.15 Moreover, our previous work on in utero enzyme replacement therapy indicates permeability of the BBB at mid-gestation in mice, which enables correction of neurons with systemic administration of a protein.16 We therefore hypothesized that prenatal delivery of an ASO, either by an IC or an IA fluid approach, can rescue the paternal Ube3a expression in a reporter mouse model. In this report, we demonstrate efficacy by Ube3a-AS downregulation, UBE3A protein reinstatement, and functional rescue in AS mice with IC delivery, supporting the promise of a prenatal therapy for AS. We also show broad systemic distribution and functional rescue in neurons when ASO is delivered to the amniotic fluid, with implications for minimally invasive treatment of various conditions in utero.

We designed a 20mer ASO to target the start of the Ube3a-AS mouse transcript. Once bound, the ASO-RNA heteroduplex induces RNase H degradation and subsequent transcriptional termination of Ube3a-AS (Figure 1A). We used a gapmer design, as previously reported for targeting this region in mouse and non-human primate studies,11,12 with locked nucleic acid (LNA) modifications added to the 2′ sugar moiety of base pairs at the 3′ and 5′ ends, flanking the target sequence designed to bind the Ube3a-AS transcription for RNAse H degradation. We bred wild-type (WT) female mice to Ube3a+/YFP males such that half of the fetuses inherit the paternal YFP+ allele (PATYFP) and successful ASO on-target activity can be readout as YFP+ neurons17 (Figure 1B). To assess our ability to deliver therapeutics to the fetal mouse CNS, we first performed pilot injections of fast green dye into the left ventricles of embryonic day (E)13.5–E14.5 mice and confirmed distribution in both ventricles (Figure 1C). We then measured survival to birth after in utero injection of various doses of ASO (Figure 1D) and observed excellent survival at lower doses, with a threshold effect reached at 1–2.5 μg, and poor survival with a 5-μg dose.

We then analyzed brains and spinal cords of IC-injected mice at postnatal day (P)14 for biodistribution of the ASO using miRNA scope in situ hybridization (ISH). We detected ASO signal throughout neurons of the brain and spinal cords with both the 1-μg and 2.5-μg doses, with higher ASO signal observed after injection of the higher dose (Figure 2A). We found ASO signal in the cortex, striatum, hippocampus, thalamus, and hypothalamus, as well as the cerebellum and medulla (Figures 2B–2E). Within the cortex of the medial cerebrum, ASO signal was primarily in cortical layers 2/3 and 5/6, regions predicted to be critical for AS treatment18 (Figure 2B). In the hippocampus, greater ASO intensity was primarily concentrated in the outer cell layers of the dentate gyrus and CA1, brain regions that play an important role in memory and neuroplasticity (Figure 2C).19 IC ASO injection also penetrated the spinal cord, with ASO signal detected in both the ventral and dorsal horns of the thoracic spinal cord (Figure 2F). To rigorously quantify regional uptake, we analyzed these sections by registering the 2D cross-sections to the 3D Allen Developing Mouse Brain Atlas20 (Figure 2G). Using a radial tree to illustrate the brain region hierarchy, beginning at the neural plate, and dividing into regions of the forebrain, we found that with both doses, ASO penetrated areas of the forebrain, notably the dorsal pallium (cortex) and medial pallium (hippocampus), with the higher dose resulting in higher ASO density (Figures 2H and 2I). At the 1-μg dose, ASO density correlated positively with caudal brain position (Figures 2J and S1). After confirming widespread ASO uptake in neurons, we compared UBE3A-YFP immunofluorescence in brains of injected mice with that of negative controls (mice treated with PBS [PATYFP + PBS]) and that of positive controls (mice in which the YFP is carried on the maternally inherited allele, injected with PBS [MATYFP + PBS]). We detected robust UBE3A-YFP expression (using a GFP-labeled anti-FYP antibody) throughout the brain after in utero injection of ASO at multiple doses (Figure 3A). In particular, we observed high YFP immunofluorescence in cortical layers 2/3 and 5/6 (Figures 3A and 3B) and the CA1 region of the hippocampus (Figure 3C), which correlated well with the regions with high ASO accumulation detected with miRNA ISH in the previous experiments (Figure 2C) and are regions thought to be important in the pathophysiology of AS.21 Quantification of staining in the cortex confirmed both the 1- and 2.5-μg doses increased the number of NeuN+/GFP+ cells to comparable levels (Figure 3D). We also analyzed whether in utero ASO delivery downregulates the Ube3a-AS transcript. We next wanted to determine whether the observed levels of UBE3A increase and Ube3a-AS decrease after in utero injection of a therapeutic ASO can rescue the cognitive and motor impairment phenotypes described in a mouse model of AS.22 We used the 1 μg dose, which had shown comparable activity in cells and regions of interest, but slightly higher survival in the reporter model, relative to higher doses. We bred Ube3a+/KO females to WT males such that one-half of the litter would be maternal Ube3a deficient (AS mice), performed IC injections in the fetal mice at day E13.5–E14.5, and analyzed the pups at 6–8 weeks of age (7–9 weeks after in utero injection) using a variety of established behavioral tests (Figure 4A). Survival to birth in AS mice after in utero ASO injection did not differ significantly from that in controls (Figure 4B); however, survival to P14 was lower in the in utero-treated AS mice, compared with WT mice (Figure 4B), leading to limitations in the numbers of affected mice that could be analyzed further. After 1 week of behavioral testing, mice were euthanized and their brain tissue was analyzed for UBE3A reinstatement by immunofluorescence. Notably, even 7–9 weeks after in utero injection, ASO-treated AS mice showed increased UBE3A in the brain both in the cortical and hippocampal neurons compared with negative controls, demonstrating a durable therapeutic effect after a single in utero dose (Figure 4C). The overall number of neurons with UBE3A in treated AS mice was similar to, albeit slightly lower than, WT mice (Figure 4D). Behavioral analysis showed a phenotypic deficit in AS mice compared with controls in the cue-based fear conditioning test that was partially rescued in ASO-treated mice (Figure 4E). We also analyzed cognitive and motor abilities via the accelerating rotarod test, evaluating latency to fall across three different rounds (training, round 1, and round 2), giving the mice a 20-min break between rounds. We again observed a phenotype in AS mice compared with controls, with a partial rescue in ASO-treated AS mice (Figures 4F and 4G). Context-based fear conditioning showed a phenotype in AS mice, which was not improved after prenatal ASO treatment (Figure S2A). The untreated AS mice did not exhibit phenotypes significantly different from WT on the following behavioral testing: open field, novel object recognition, and marble burying. To explore potential routes of entry into the CNS, we performed miRNA ISH to detect the ASO 5 days after in utero delivery (E18.5). We found widespread ASO distribution throughout the E18.5 embryo, including in the brain, with some variability between littermates (Figure 6A). We quantified ASO in each organ (both as density per area of tissue and by the percentage of ASO positive cells) (Figures 6B and 6C). The lung showed the greatest ASO density and percent of positive cells, consistent for all mice analyzed, followed by the liver and intestines (Figures 6B and 6C). To identify predictors of CNS penetration, we closely examined ASO staining intensity in regions that could be relevant for ASO uptake to the brain (Figure 6F), such as the nasal cavity, olfactory nerve layer, and cribriform plate. We found high intensity of ASO signal throughout the blood vessels of the lamina propria in the respiratory epithelium of the nasal cavity (Figure 6G). We also detected ASO in olfactory cilia of the nasal cavity and in the olfactory nerve layer (Figure 6H), suggesting the ASO could traverse the cribriform plate. ASO staining was robust in the cells of the choroid plexus, suggesting entry to the brain through the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Figure 6I). In contrast, mice in which these areas were not penetrated had low ASO signal in the brain (Figures S3A–S3D). In the heart, we detected ASO signal in the lumen and the ventricular wall (Figure 6J). Finally, ASO was present intracellularly in the placenta, with lower staining intensity observed in the decidua (Figure 6K). These findings suggest that IA ASO enters the CNS through multiple routes, possibly including respiratory, the CSF, and hematogenous.

After observing widespread biodistribution of the ASO at the E18.5 time point, we assessed ASO efficacy three weeks after IA injection (on day P14), using the YFP reporter mouse model. We detected intense staining for UBE3A-YFP protein throughout the cortex and hippocampus (Figure 7A). Both the cortical layers 2/3 and 5/6, and hippocampal region CA1, showed high UBE3A-YFP intensity (Figures 7B and 7C), albeit with some variability (as evident by images from four mice shown). The number of cortical neurons with functional rescue (NeuN+/GFP+) was significantly higher than in untreated animals (Figure 7D), with levels reaching 75% ± 11% of maternal allele expression. These numbers are higher than those observed with IC ASO delivery (Figure 3D) and would likely lead to similar improvements in the behavioral phenotype. Accordingly, we confirmed that ASO delivery to the amniotic fluid resulted in functional ASO activity in the brain, shown by significant downregulation of Ube3a-AS in cortical neurons compared with controls (Figure 7E). We performed a pathological analysis of the livers of pups and dams to detect ASO toxicity. A scientist blinded to the experimental groups did not observe any pathology in maternal or pup livers after in utero ASO exposure compared with controls (Figure S4A). We also performed miRNA ISH of fetal and maternal livers to detect ASO in livers of pups and dams. ASO was observed in the fetal liver at day E18.5 after IC and IA delivery, mainly concentrated in the interstitial space (Figure S4B). Interestingly, ASO was also found in the maternal liver after IA but not IC delivery (Figure S4B). Consistent with these data, we also observed significantly higher levels of ASO in the placenta and maternal decidua after IA injection compared with ICV injection (Figures S4C and S4D). To detect potential liver toxicity in the dams, we measured liver function tests in maternal serum 4–5 days after injection (E18.5). We observed an elevation in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in the dams of IC-injected pups, and a trend for AST elevation in dams of IA-injected pups compared with dams of PBS-injected controls; alanine transaminase, albumin, total protein, and alkaline phosphatase were similar between groups (Figures S4E–S4I).

Our study demonstrates that prenatal ASO delivery via IC or IA injection results in widespread delivery to neurons in the brain and spinal cord. We show that direct IC delivery of ASO elicits degradation of the Ube3a-AS transcript in cortical neurons, with subsequent increased UBE3A protein throughout the brain and improvement of multiple behavioral phenotypes. Furthermore, our experiments with amniotic fluid delivery demonstrate widespread systemic and CNS distribution, pointing to a less invasive delivery method that could be relevant for multiple early-onset disorders.

Our findings of UBE3A restoration throughout the brain are relevant for patients with AS.21 Loss of UBE3A affects neurons in all regions of the brain, particularly the cortex and hippocampus,21 resulting in severe functional impairments. Thus, our findings of widespread ASO distribution in the CNS, UBE3A reinstatement in critical brain regions with both IA and IC injections, and our behavioral testing results suggest a strong potential for therapeutic benefit in humans. Motor learning impairments, which are a severe symptom for individuals living with AS, are a well described phenotype in the AS mouse model25 and are likely secondary to cognitive impairments in learning ability.26 Our ASO was able to improve this phenotype (demonstrated in rotarod testing) and resulted in behavioral trends similar to those in WT mice, showing the potential therapeutic benefit of early ASO delivery.

We were encouraged to see that ASO delivery via an IA injection is even more effective as IC delivery in reinstating UBE3A protein and allows infusion of a higher dose. Our findings of broad systemic ASO distribution in the lung, liver, kidney, stomach, and intestines have significant implications for other severe genetic disorders that can be diagnosed before birth and are amenable to nucleic acid therapy, such as cystic fibrosis.27,28 In trying to understand routes of entry into the brain, we analyzed ASO distribution at an early time point and found correlations between brain ASO uptake and uptake in areas that could serve as potential routes to the brain. Based on these analyses, we hypothesize that there are several routes of CNS penetration, including through the nasal cavity into the olfactory bulb, through the bloodstream, and through the choroid plexus into the CSF.

The current progress in both fetal therapies and for postnatal ASO therapies (particularly for AS and spinal muscular atrophy [SMA]) is such that we see a feasible path to the clinic for a prenatal ASO therapy as well as compelling rationale for early intervention. Reinstatement of maternal Ube3a expression during fetal development improves behavioral phenotypes in transgenic mice.13 Based on this rationale, a recent report of using gene editing to unsilence paternal Ube3a used combined prenatal and early postnatal delivery time points to optimize benefit.29 For SMA, there is evidence of prenatal onset of damage to human spinal cord neurons as well as demonstration of improved benefits when treatment is given in the prenatal period.30 In terms of feasibility, ASO delivery in ongoing clinical trials in newborns with AS or SMA is intrathecal, for which the most appropriate prenatal correlate is direct intraventricular injection. There is a precedent for human prenatal intraventricular injections, most notably to place shunts for patients with congenital aqueductal stenosis.31,32 Given the widespread adoption of even more invasive operations (such as repair of myelomeningoceles),33,34 the risk-benefit profile could be acceptable in a patient with a devastating underlying condition such as AS.

Our data on brain penetration after IA delivery present another alternative for clinical translation. Accessing the amniotic cavity is common for diagnostic amniocentesis and has an extremely low complication rate in experienced hands.35 Infusion of a therapy into the amniotic fluid is performed for the treatment of X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (XLHED)36 as well as for serial amnio-infusions for patients with renal agenesis (NCT03101891).37 Although we observed variability in our mouse model due to the technical challenge of this type of delivery in a small mouse fetus, we do not anticipate this issue when translating to delivery to a human fetus. However, it is important to note that ASO penetration into the brain in humans is a higher bar than protein penetration into the skin and buccal cavity in patients with XLHED. Although there is a paucity of data on the timing of BBB formation in humans, Crouch et al.38 demonstrated a that critical elements of a functional BBB were diminished in the developing human brain at 17 weeks, a time window that is comparable with the timing of administration of fetal molecular therapies. Large animal experiments to map ASO biodistribution after IA or IC injection could shed light on whether the observed robust brain penetration with IA injection in mice can be replicated.

Our detailed examination of biodistribution in pups, placentas, and maternal livers yielded important insights into potential toxicities of this approach. We did observe ASO in the fetal livers and placentas of IC-injected fetuses, suggesting ASO leakage from the CNS to peripheral organs, even through an IC approach. We expect this finding is due to the permeability of the BBB at the gestational time frame of this study. Interestingly, we also detected ASO in maternal livers after IA injection, likely secondary to the high exposure of the dams when pups are injected with the high doses used with this route, as evidenced by the higher levels of ASO in the placenta and decidua with IA injection. Importantly, we also detected an increase in maternal AST levels with IC injection (even without evidence of ASO in the dam liver), albeit without any evidence of pathological changes in the liver. We think that maternal exposure in the mouse model with either route of injection (with at least 6–8 injected pups per litter) is higher than would be expected in large animals carrying a single gestation. Since maternal toxicity is a critical factor in patient safety, large animal studies that would more accurately mimic maternal exposure and transplacental transfer will be important to perform in the future.

Our study represents an important step forward for considering the safety and biodistribution of ASOs after in utero delivery. The strengths of the study include the testing of various doses and routes of administration in both a reporter model and a disease model. While the design of the reporter model allows accurate detection of the ASO function in neurons, our use of miRNA ISH allowed us to understand further details of biodistribution throughout the body. We were also able to examine biodistribution to maternal tissues and the placenta using this sensitive method. These data are an important addition to the previous report of IA delivery of ASO for the treatment of Usher syndrome,4 in which the authors examined biodistribution to the ear and other fetal organs by quantitative PCR and immunofluorescence. In our experiments, we were further able to quantify ASO after IC and IA delivery in specific regions of the brain by carefully mapping to a defined brain atlas to have a 3D understanding of brain ASO distribution with both routes of administration. Our model also allowed us to discern ASO function, even in a reporter model. Another relevant study5 tested IA delivery of a splice-switching ASO in the Usher syndrome mouse model and demonstrated that IA delivery was not as successful as direct injection into the ear cavity. These results demonstrate the importance of testing each strategy in the relevant disease model, since there are numerous unique variables (such as the optimal developmental time window for treatment, the percentage of corrected cells needed to discern a benefit, and the pharmacokinetics of the ASO formulation) for each disease.

One limitation to our study was that pre-weaning survival in the AS mice was low, which decreased the number of animals that could be generated for behavioral testing. To determine common AS mouse phenotypes, mouse behavioral testing is typically performed around 3–5 months of age.39 We chose an earlier time point for behavioral testing to obviate the need for postnatal redosing, but the lack of phenotype seen on some tests could be due to the young age of the mice in this study. Nevertheless, this time point allowed us to assess the continued presence of UBE3A protein in the brain up to 9 weeks of age, which underscores the long therapeutic effect of this approach.

Prenatal molecular therapies for early onset genetic diseases are gaining traction, with several ongoing clinical trials, and promising early data in several cases including infantile onset Pompe disease.1,40 However, in a recent study of prenatal gene editing in AS mice,29 long-term rescue was mainly a result of integration of the AAV vector used to deliver the nuclease, a finding that has some safety implications for use in the clinical setting. In this study, we chose to focus on ASOs to avoid potential long-term off-target effects of integrating vectors in fetuses. Since ASOs are given every 3 months, a single in utero dose could have a therapeutic benefit before the onset of irreversible organ damage, as part of a strategy that includes subsequent postnatal doses. In the near term, further studies are required to precisely translate this approach to clinical testing. Since the target sequence is different between mice and humans (and ASO toxicity can be dependent on length and sequence of ASO), a preclinical study will likely need to test a different sequence and dose in a large animal model.

In conclusion, our study supports the premise of using ASOs as a viable therapy for prenatal treatment in fetuses with AS, with two feasible routes of administration for effective CNS delivery. These findings could be extrapolated for prenatal therapy in other severe, early-onset neurological disorders that are diagnosable prenatally. Based on our observation of a broad distribution of the ASO in multiple other tissues, similar therapies could be developed for a broad range of early-onset disorders.

To quantify ASO distribution by region, we detected miRNAscope probes to ASOs across each tissue section, segmented sections by image registration to a brain atlas, and calculated ASO density for each brain region. For ASO probe detection, we identified individual cells and ASO levels using the digital pathology processing software, QuPath (v.0.4).44 First, we pre-processed images by identifying a representative region of interest and estimating staining vectors (“Estimate stain vectors” automated function). Since miRNAscope probes often coalesce into large clusters where individual probes are indistinguishable, we performed a relative quantification of probes using the QuPath positive cell detection pipeline, as recommended by the miRNAscope QuPath Analysis Guidelines. We detected individual cells using default parameters except for increasing the nucleus background radius to 12 to reduce false cell detections in the background, reducing the minimum area to 5 to detect smaller cells, and reducing the intensity threshold to 0.05 to increase sensitivity. For each detected cell, we scored its positivity using the optical density mean thresholds set to 0.1 for 1+, 0.2 for 2+, and 0.3 for 3+ cells, where cells below 1+ are considered negative, and higher positivity indicates higher ASO expression. To automate the pipeline, we scripted it using Groovy (v4.0) in Java (v17.0).

For brain region identification, we performed segmentation by atlas image registration for each section as implemented in our MagellanMapper (v1.6) open-source toolkit.20 The Allen Developing Mouse Brain Atlas series provides developmentally age-appropriate atlases for both the E18.5 and P14 timepoints in our study. Since these atlases were annotated sagittally and most of our samples are coronal, we used our 3D reconstructed versions of these atlases with extended coverage to the full brain and labels smoothed in 3D.45 For image registration, we employed the Elastix library46 in SimpleITK (v2.3).47 To reduce computational requirements during registration, we down-sampled each tissue section by 50×. For each section, we identified an appropriate atlas image plane, rotating the atlas in 3D to account for non-orthogonal sectioning if necessary. We further scaled and rotated the extracted atlas plane to ensure a match between images. For each transformation, we treated the atlas as the moving image and the tissue section as the fixed image, moving the atlas into the tissue’s coordinate space. The alignment employed default multi-resolution translation and affine rigid transformations (1,024 iterations each), followed by a b-spline deformable registration (512 iterations, size of 30–150 voxels) and replication of the full transformation to the atlas labels. In some sections, artifacts such as tears prevented close alignment. In these cases, we manually identified corresponding points between the atlas plane and sample to align through an additional b-spline transformation. For the whole embryo samples, we annotated masks of the brains to focus the alignment and preprocessed low-contrast images using contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization as implemented in the Scikit-image (v0.19) image processing library48 with a kernel size of 0.02. We also annotated other organs to compare ASO distribution throughout the body in these whole-embryo images using QuPath.

To assign the ASO detections by segmented region, we generated a density map of ASO probes by down sampling cell positions to the size of registered atlas labels and multiplying each cell by its positivity score. Next, we summed the ASO probes within all voxels defined by each atlas label to identify the ASO probe count and divided by the number of voxels to obtain ASO densities by region. For brain-wide distribution, we combined the ASO probe counts and volumes across sections within each sample brain for each region and background subtracted the matched samples from the same animal and section positions treated with negative control probes. We performed linear regressions in Pingouin (v0.5).49 We generated plots using Matplotlib (v3.6), Seaborn (v0.12), and Networkx (v3.0). To understand the phenotypic outcomes of ASO delivery in utero, we included the following four groups in our AS study: AS KO + scramble ASO (negative control) and/or PBS/no surgery, AS KO + targeting ASO (experimental), and WT + scramble ASO and/or PBS (positive control). A nontargeting scramble ASO was designed to act as a vehicle control. Three groups of age-matched littermates were generated, and mice were randomly assigned to each treatment group. All mice were 4–6 weeks old and individually housed at least 2 days, typically 7 days. All behavioral testing and scoring was performed by blinded experimenters to genotype and treatment. Behavioral tests were always run in the following order: day 1, accelerating rotarod test and marble burying test day 2, open field test and novel object test (1); day 3, novel object (2); day 4, fear conditioning (1); and day 5, fear conditioning (2).

Accelerating rotarod (4–40 rpm over 5 min, model of machine) was used to test motor function as well as learning. Mice were given three trials with 20 min between each trial. Performance was scored as latency to fall. The maximum duration of a trial was 5 min.

Open cages (13 × 7 × 5 inches) were filled with 2 inches of bedding material (material type) and 20 black glass marbles were placed in a 5 × 4 equidistant grid on the bedding. Mice were given access to the marbles without disturbance for 30 min. After the test, the mice were gently removed from the cage. Marbles were scored as buried if more than 50% of the marble was covered by bedding.

Mice were placed in the center of an open 40-cm × 40-cm square chamber (Kinder Scientific, Poway, CA) with no cues or stimuli and allowed to move freely for 10 min. Infrared photobeam breaks were recorded and movement metrics analyzed by MotorMonitor software (Kinder Scientific).

The novel object recognition task was performed according to the protocol previously described.51 During the habituation phase (day 1), mice could freely explore an empty arena for 10 min. During the training phase (day 2), two identical objects were placed in the habituated arena, and mice could explore the objects for 5 min. For the testing phase (day 3), one object was replaced with a novel object, and mice could explore the objects for 5 min. Time spent exploring each object was quantified using Smart Video Tracking Software (Panlab; Harvard Apparatus, Hollisten, MA, USA). Two different sets of objects are used. To control for any inherent object preference, half of the mice are exposed to object A as their novel object and half to object B. To control for any potential object-independent location preference, the location of the novel object relative to the trained object is also varied. The objects were chosen based on their ability to capture the animal’s interest, independent of genetic background or age. To determine percent time with novel object, we calculate (Time with novel object)/(Time with trained object + Time with novel object) × 100. In this preference index, 100% indicates full preference for the novel object, and 0% indicates full preference for the trained object. A mouse with a value of 50% would have spent equal time exploring both objects. Mice that did not explore both objects during the training phase were excluded from analysis.

To enable testing for the hippocampal-dependent contextual fear conditioning mice learned to associate the environmental context (fear conditioning chamber) with an aversive stimulus (mild foot shock; unconditioned stimulus [US]). The mild foot shock was paired with a light and tone cue (conditioned stimulus [CS]) to also assess amygdala-dependent cued fear conditioning. Conditioned fear was displayed as freezing behavior. Specific training parameters are as follows: tone duration 30 s; level 70 dB, 2 kHz; shock duration 2 s; intensity 0.6 mA. This intensity is considered not painful and can easily be tolerated, will generates an unpleasant feeling. More specifically, on day 1 (training day) each mouse was placed in a fear-conditioning chamber and allowed to explore for 2 min before delivery of a 30-s tone (70 dB) ending with a 2-s foot shock (0.6 mA). Two minutes later, a second CS-US pair was delivered. On day 2 (testing day), each mouse was first place in the fear-conditioning chamber containing the same exact context, but with no administration of a CS or foot shock. Freezing was analyzed for 1–3 min. One hour later, the mice were placed in a new context containing a different odor, cleaning solution, floor texture, chamber walls, and shape. Mice were allowed to explore for 2 min before being re-exposed to the CS. Freezing was analyzed for 1–3 min. Freezing was measured using a FreezeScan video tracking system and software (Cleversys, Inc).

Comparisons between more than two groups were evaluated using ANOVA with Tukey, Sidak, or Dunn’s multiple comparisons test as well as two-tailed t tests. For correlation comparisons a one-tailed Pearson correlation test was used. Data are represented as means ± SEM. A p value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Prism Version 9.2.0.; La Jolla, CA, USA)
