# Early Identification of Children at Risk for Complicated Parapneumonic Pleural Effusion: Development of a Prediction Model.
**Authors:** Servitje-Verdaguer B, Conti-Degiorgis R, Velasco R, Gelman-Bagaria A
**Journal:** Pediatr Pulmonol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ppul.71817](https://doi.org/10.1002/ppul.71817)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Parapneumonic pleural effusion (PPE) is a frequent complication of pediatric pneumonia. Some cases progress to complicated PPE (cPPE), which is associated with greater morbidity, longer treatment, and a higher likelihood of need for pleural drainage. Early identification of children at risk of progression remains difficult at presentation, and pediatric evidence on early predictors is scarce and inconsistent.

METHODS: We conducted a retrospective observational study including children aged <18 years admitted with PPE between 2015 and 2025. cPPE was defined through standard laboratory and/or imaging criteria. Twenty clinical, laboratory, and radiological candidate predictors available at initial evaluation were assessed. After univariate screening, variables with statistical association and clinical relevance were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. After model assessment, a simplified score was derived from the original model for bedside use.

RESULTS: A total of 122 children were included, 77 (63%) with cPPE and 45 (37%) with uncomplicated PPE. In univariate analyses, younger age, dyspnea, chest wall retractions, reduced lung sounds, C-reactive protein, lower hemoglobin, pleural effusion thickness, and mediastinal shift were associated with cPPE. In the final multivariate model, five variables remained independently associated: age (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.63-0.84), retractions (OR 3.23, 95% CI 1.16-8.95), C-reactive protein ≥176.5 mg/L (OR 2.83, 95% CI 1.02-7.82), pleural effusion thickness (OR 2.16, 95% CI 1.27-3.70), and mediastinal shift (OR 10.75, 95% CI 2.24-51.64). The model showed good discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.883 (95% CI 0.817-0.948). The optimal probability threshold was 0.49, yielding a sensitivity of 92.2% and a specificity of 71.1%. A simplified additive clinical score derived from the model demonstrated similar performance, allowing stratification into four risk groups with progressively increasing observed rates of cPPE.

CONCLUSIONS: Early risk stratification of pediatric PPE is feasible using a small set of readily available clinical, laboratory, and radiological variables. The proposed model and the adapted clinical score showed good discriminatory ability and may help identify children at increased risk of progression to cPPE who could benefit from closer monitoring and early optimized management. External validation is needed before routine clinical implementation.

Parapneumonic pleural effusion (PPE) represents one of the most frequent complications of community‐acquired pneumonia in children and is associated with substantial morbidity and healthcare utilization [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Although many cases follow a relatively benign course and resolve with medical therapy alone, a considerable proportion of patients progress to complicated PPE (cPPE), characterized by pleural infection with loculations or biochemical markers suggestive of empyema [5, 6, 7, 8]. Children with cPPE typically experience a more severe clinical course, including prolonged fever, longer antibiotic therapy and hospitalization, and a higher likelihood of invasive procedures [4, 6, 9, 10, 11].

Early identification of patients at risk of developing cPPE remains clinically relevant. Prompt recognition of severe disease may facilitate closer monitoring, guide early imaging evaluation, and support timely referral to centers with expertise in pleural drainage or surgical management [12, 13]. However, identifying which children will progress to complicated disease remains challenging at the time of presentation.

The available evidence on this matter remains scarce, as only a very limited number of studies have specifically addressed early predictors of cPPE in children [14, 15, 16, 17]. Most published studies have included relatively small cohorts [14, 16, 17] or focused primarily on disease severity and management rather than early risk prediction [15]. Although several clinical and laboratory predictors have been proposed, the reported findings have been heterogeneous and sometimes conflicting, and no models have undergone independent validation to our knowledge [14, 15, 16, 17].

The objectives of this study are therefore to identify independent early predictors of progression to cPPE in a pediatric cohort, to develop and internally validate a prediction model for estimating the risk of cPPE at the time of presentation, and to derive a simplified clinical risk score suitable for bedside use.

We conducted a retrospective observational study at our tertiary hospital, including all patients aged 0–17 years admitted with radiologically confirmed PPE between January 2015 and December 2025. Patients with pleural effusion of non‐infectious origin, severe immunosuppression, active malignancy, decompensated chronic cardiac, respiratory, renal, or hepatic disease, or severe neurological disorders were excluded from the study. Data were retrieved from an institutional database. Clinical, laboratory, imaging, management and outcome data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized data collection form. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Parc Taulí University Hospital (protocol code 2025/5070, approved in 23 October 2025).

PPE was defined as the presence of pleural effusion in the context of pneumonia identified by chest imaging (ultrasound, radiograph, or computed tomography). Effusions were classified as cPPE when there was evidence of loculations on imaging and/or pleural fluid biomarkers were consistent with empyema. Effusions not meeting these criteria were classified as uncomplicated PPE (uPPE).

Etiology was defined as the microbiological identification of a pathogenic bacterium in blood, pleural fluid and/or nasopharyngeal swab, either by conventional culture or polymerase chain reaction detection.

Radiologic resolution was defined as the time from admission to the first available chest radiograph without PPE‐related pathologic findings, as interpreted by a specialist pediatric pulmonologist.

A set of potential predictors was considered based on previous literature, clinical plausibility, and expert clinical judgment. Twenty candidate variables available at the time of initial evaluation were selected, including clinical, laboratory, and radiological parameters potentially associated with progression to cPPE. These variables were initially evaluated in univariate analyses, and those demonstrating both statistical significance and clinical relevance were considered for inclusion in the multivariate model.

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were reported as means with standard deviations (SD), whereas continuous variables without a normal distribution were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Differences between children with cPPE and uPPE were assessed using the Student's t test or Mann‐Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ
2 test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropiate. No relevant missing data were present for the variables included in the analyses, with the exception of serum procalcitonin levels, which has a high proportion of missing values because it was not routinely measured throughout the study period. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

To identify predictors associated with progression to complicated disease, univariate analyses were first performed for all candidate variables. Variables showing statistically significant association in these preliminary analyses were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model.

Multivariate logistic regression was used to estimate the independent association between selected predictors and the development of cPPE. Results were reported as an adjusted odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and expressed as the area under the curve (AUC). The optimal probability threshold for identifying patients at increased risk of cPPE was determined using the Youden index, and both sensitivity and specificity were calculated. A p‐value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Finally, to facilitate clinical applicability, a simplified clinical prediction score was derived from the final multivariate logistic regression model. Regression coefficients of the independent predictors were used to assign relative weights to each variable, creating an additive score that could be easily applied in clinical practice. The performance of the simplified score was also evaluated using ROC curve analysis and compared with the original one.

A total of 122 children with PPE were included in the study cohort. Among them, 77 (63%) fulfilled criteria for cPPE, while 45 (37%) were classified as uPPE. Children who developed cPPE were markedly younger than those with uPPE (median 3.5 vs. 6.3 years). Signs of respiratory compromise at presentation were also more frequent in the cPPE group, with higher rates of presence of dyspnea (66% vs. 47%), chest wall retractions (56% vs. 29%), and reduced lung sounds (84% vs. 69%). Other clinical findings did not reach statistical significance.

Radiological findings showed clearer differences between groups. Children with cPPE presented with larger pleural effusions on thoracic ultrasound (median 2.0 vs. 1.3 cm) and higher rates of mediastinal shift on chest radiography (38% vs. 9%). However, bilateral involvement did not show significant differences between groups.

Laboratory markers also revealed important differences, as our data reported higher values in children with cPPE regarding white blood cell counts (median 19.9 vs. 16.6·109/L), C‐reactive protein (CRP) levels (median 284 vs. 147 mg/L), and procalcitonin levels (median 9.8 vs. 0.9 ng/mL). Lower hemoglobin levels were also more frequent in patients with cPPE (mean 106 vs. 113 g/L).

Management strategies differed substantially between groups, as patients with cPPE underwent significantly more pleural fluid analysis (81% vs. 58%), pleural drainage (60% vs. 16%), and fibrinolysis (22% vs. 2%).

Consistent with greater disease severity, children with cPPE experienced a more protracted clinical course and worse short‐term clinical outcomes. Patients with cPPE experienced significantly longer fever duration (median 13 vs. 8 days), intravenous antibiotic regimens (median 15 vs. 9.5 days), and hospital stays (median 16 vs. 11 days). Necrotizing pneumonia occurred more frequently in the cPPE group (35% vs. 11%), while the rest of complications were rare. No long‐term pulmonary sequelae or deaths occurred in either group.

An initial exploratory analysis was performed to evaluate the association between a broad set of candidate variables and the development of cPPE as the dependent variable. In univariate logistic regression analyses, several of those variables were significantly associated with the development of cPPE. Age showed a strong inverse association with disease severity, with each additional year reducing the odds of cPPE (OR: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.76–0.91). Among clinical variables, markers of respiratory distress were the most informative: dyspnea (OR: 2.24, 95% CI: 1.06–4.76), chest wall retractions (OR: 3.11, 95% CI: 1.42–6.83), and reduced lung sounds (OR: 2.45, 95% CI: 1.01–5.91) were all significantly associated with cPPE, whereas other signs such as tachypnea, tachycardia, or hypoxemia did not show significant association.

Among laboratory parameters, CRP showed a modest but statistically significant association with cPPE (OR: 1.003, 95% CI: 1.000–1.006), while lower hemoglobin levels were also mildly associated with increased risk (OR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99). Other laboratory variables, such as leukocyte count, neutrophil count, platelet count, and natremia, were not significantly associated with disease severity.

Finally, radiological variables demonstrated the strongest associations with complicated disease. Initial pleural effusion thickness was a robust predictor, with each centimeter increase associated with more than a twofold increase in the odds of cPPE (OR: 2.18, 95% CI: 1.37–3.47). Similarly, the presence of mediastinal shift on chest radiography was associated with a markedly increased risk (OR: 6.19, 95% CI: 2.01–19.08).

A multivariable analysis was performed using logistic regression. The initial model included all variables showing an association with the development of cPPE in the univariate analysis, as well as potential confounders. For leukocyte count, neutrophil count, and CRP levels, optimal cut‐off values were determined though maximization of the Youden index, and these variables were subsequently entered into the multivariate model as dichotomized variables. The final model was selected using a backward stepwise approach and included age in years, chest wall retractions, CRP ≥ 176.5 mg/L, pleural effusion thickness in centimeters, and mediastinal shift (Table 3).

In the adjusted analysis, all five variables remained independently associated with the development of cPPE. Age continued to show a strong protective effect, as each year associated with a 27% reduction in the odds of cPPE. The presence of chest wall retractions was associated with a threefold increase in the odds of cPPE, as was a CRP level equal or higher than 176.5 mg/L. As for radiological variables, pleural effusion thickness remained a robust predictor, with each centimeter increase associated with a twofold increase in the odds of cPPE, whereas mediastinal shift increased the odds by approximately tenfold, although with a wider CI reflecting lower event frequency.

The performance of the multivariate prediction model was assessed in terms of discrimination and overall model fit. The model demonstrated a strong ability to distinguish between children who developed cPPE and those who did not, with an AUC of 0.883 (95% CI: 0.817–0.948) through ROC curve analysis, indicating an overall good discriminatory performance. Overall model fit was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test, which revealed that the model was highly statistically significant (χ
2 = 56.83, df = 4, p < 0.001).

The optimal probability threshold for classification, determined using the Youden index, was 0.49. At this cut‐off point, 71 cases of cPPE and 32 cases of uPPE were correctly identified, along with 6 false negatives and 13 false positives. Thus, the model achieved a sensitivity of 92.2% (95% CI: 84–96) and a specificity of 71.1% (95% CI: 57–82) for the prediction of cPPE, corresponding to a Youden index of 0.63.

Lastly, to facilitate rapid bedside stratification, we developed a simplified clinical prediction score based on the final multivariate logistic regression model, including the same five variables. Integer point values were assigned according to the relative magnitude of the regression coefficients, while preserving the ranking and relative contribution of each predictor. Continuous predictors (age and pleural effusion thickness) were categorized into clinically meaningful intervals, and points were assigned proportionally across categories. Using the Youden index, the best threshold for this clinical score was set at 18. At this cut‐off point, 74 cases of cPPE and 28 cases of uPPE were correctly identified, along with 3 false negatives and 17 false positives. The clinical score displayed a sensitivity of 96.1% (95% CI: 89–99) and a specificity of 62.2% (95% CI: 48–75) for the prediction of cPPE, corresponding to a Youden index of 0.63. Positive predictive value was 81.3% (95% CI: 72–88), while negative predictive value was 90.3% (95% CI: 75–97).

In this retrospective cohort of children hospitalized with PPE, we addressed the need for early identification of factors associated with progression to cPPE, which remains a frequent challenge in everyday practice. We identified five variables at presentation that were independently associated with progression to complicated disease, which are particularly attractive as they are objective, rapidly available, and broadly reproducible across centers. We then developed a simple multivariate model based on these five independent predictors, which demonstrated good discriminatory performance.

A simplified bedside score was also developed to facilitate early risk stratification and later internally validated. Although any simplification implies some loss of precision compared with the original regression equation, the score preserves the main structure of the model and offers an intuitive way to classify patients into risk groups. Such a tool may be especially helpful in emergency departments or general pediatric wards for an initial evaluation; however, we believe that this score should be understood as a support instrument for clinical judgment rather than a substitute for individualized assessment.

Our findings are broadly consistent with the limited pediatric literature on predictors of cPPE, while also extending current evidence. Previous studies have identified elevated inflammatory markers (CRP and ferritin), hypoalbuminemia, and neutrophilia as being associated with empyema or complicated pleural infection [14]. Likewise, larger pleural effusion size has consistently been associated with greater disease severity and worse clinical outcomes, such as a longer hospital stay [15]. Our results confirm the importance of systemic inflammation and pleural disease burden by identifying high CRP levels and pleural effusion thickness as independent predictors of cPPE. In addition, mediastinal shift emerged as the strongest radiological predictor, highlighting the contribution of mass effect as a marker of advanced pleural involvement. Unlike previous studies that failed to identify clinical features capable of distinguishing uPPE from cPPE [17], we found that chest wall retractions at presentation independently predicted progression to cPPE, suggesting that objective signs of respiratory distress may provide additional prognostic information when interpreted together with laboratory and imaging findings.

Only one previous pediatric study has proposed a clinical prediction score for complicated pleural infection [16]. Although that model demonstrated good discriminatory performance, it was derived from a small case‐control cohort study including only 30 cases of cPPE and was restricted to children aged 2–59 months. In addition, the study compared children with cPPE to those with severe pneumonia without significant PPE, rather than distinguishing between cPPE and uPPE, which represents a different and potentially more clinically relevant diagnostic challenge. Furthermore, the prediction model did not incorporate radiological variables despite the well‐established role of imaging in assessing pleural disease severity.

In contrast, our model was developed in a cohort of children with established PPE across the entire pediatric age range and is based exclusively on objective clinical, laboratory, and radiological variables that are routinely available at the time of presentation. Another important feature of our study is the choice of cPPE itself as the main marker of disease severity. We considered this outcome more robust than management‐related endpoints such as drainage procedures or PICU admission, which may be influenced by local protocols, physician preferences, or temporal changes in clinical practice. In contrast, the definition of cPPE relied on objective laboratory and radiological criteria that more directly reflect the intrinsic progression of pleural infection. This likely strengthens the clinical coherence of the model, even if some overlap inevitably exists between predictors and the definition of the outcome, particularly regarding imaging variables.

Still, our study has several limitations that we want to acknowledge. First, its retrospective single‐center design entails an inherent risk of selection bias and variability in clinical data and documentation. Second, the sample size, although reasonable for an exploratory model, remains limited, and the number of events may restrict the stability of some estimates, particularly for less common variables such as mediastinal shift. Third, the model was not tested in a different cohort, so performance may be optimistic and routine implementation cannot be recommended until external validation studies have been carried out. Fourth, because this was an observational study conducted over a long period, changes in microbiological epidemiology, imaging practices, and therapeutic strategies may have influenced the cohort in ways that are difficult to fully capture.

Despite these limitations, the study also has relevant strengths. It includes a well‐characterized pediatric cohort over a prolonged time, uses a clinically meaningful definition of cPPE, and focuses specifically on predictors available at presentation. In addition, the final model is intentionally simple and based on variables that are easy to obtain in routine practice, which enhances its translational potential.

Younger age, chest wall retractions, C‐reactive protein levels, pleural effusion thickness, and mediastinal shift were independently associated with progression to cPPE in children. A prediction model based on these five variables showed good discriminatory value and was further translated into a simplified clinical score for bedside use. The study's findings suggest that early risk stratification in pediatric PPE is feasible and may help identify children who require closer monitoring and early optimized management. Future studies should externally validate this model and assess its impact on clinical decision‐making and patient outcomes.
