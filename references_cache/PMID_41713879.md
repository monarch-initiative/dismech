# Detection of a Distinct Erythropoietin (EPO) Profile After Isoelectric Focusing in Patients With Familial Erythrocytosis.
**Authors:** Martin L, Idriss S, Maaziz N, Huerre S, Mekacher LR, Lahmek K, Girodon F, Gardie B, Ericsson M, Marchand A
**Journal:** Drug Test Anal (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/dta.70049](https://doi.org/10.1002/dta.70049)

## Content

Since the implementation of the first erythropoietin (EPO) detection method to fight the use of recombinant EPO (rEPO) for doping, the techniques used and identification criteria have evolved. The first technique involved iso-electric focusing (IEF-PAGE) to separate EPO isoforms by their charges. It can still be used for initial testing but is now largely replaced by electrophoretic separation based on molecular weight (SDS-PAGE or SAR-PAGE) to conclude decisively on the presence of rEPO. Recently, different mutations in the noncoding region of the EPO gene were identified in patients with familial erythrocytosis. Their blood EPO profiles after IEF-PAGE were very basic and in the same area as rEPO. To avoid any possible misinterpretation in an antidoping context, the goal of this work was to characterize urine and blood EPO profiles from these patients using all methods authorized in antidoping laboratories (IEF-PAGE, SDS-PAGE, and SAR-PAGE) and to compare them with wild-type (WT) EPO and with profiles obtained after the administration of rEPO Eprex. Results showed that SDS-PAGE and SAR-PAGE profiles were not modified in the patients compared with WT EPO and could not be mistaken for the presence of rEPO. In contrast, the IEF-PAGE profiles in urine were slightly more acidic than in blood and closer to the rEPO profile. However, they did not meet the identification criteria used when this technique was the only authorized method. It is highly unlikely that these EPO mutations, only identified in erythrocytosis patients yet, could lead to an adverse analytical finding.

Erythropoietin (EPO) is a key hormone regulating red blood cell homeostasis and the adaptation of the human body to changes in oxygen availability. Its production shifts from the liver, the main organ producing EPO during fetal life, to the kidney shortly after birth and during adulthood [1, 2, 3]. Once in circulation, EPO exerts pleiotropic effects by binding to its receptors (EPOR) expressed at the membrane of many cell types. Its erythropoietic activity is due to the activation of antiapoptotic signaling pathways in erythroid progenitors in the bone marrow [4]. EPO is a heavily glycosylated protein containing three asparagine (N)‐linked oligosaccharides and one serine (O) linked oligosaccharide (representing 40% of its total mass). Variations in the composition of these glycan chains, particularly in their sialic acid content, play a major role in EPO's circulating half‐life and its binding affinity to EPOR [5, 6].

Production of human EPO in cultured mammalian cells led to a potent treatment for correcting anemia at the end of the 1980s, but it also enabled large‐scale misuse for doping. At the turn of the year 2000, the first method designed to detect doping with recombinant EPO (rEPO) in urine was implemented [7]. It involved the electrophoretic separation of proteins by iso‐electric focusing (IEF‐PAGE): Proteins loaded on a polyacrylamide gel migrate in an electric field within a pH gradient until they reach the point where their net charge is zero. After blotting of the gel, the EPO isoforms are specifically detected using immunodetection. Interestingly, rEPO was characterized by a more basic profile than endogenous EPO detected in urine [8]. This was linked to subtle differences in glycan composition, due to differences in the glycosylation properties of mammalian cultured cells compared with endogenous renal cells. However, over the years, several sources of variability in endogenous EPO profiles, sometimes producing more basic profiles, were identified, including bacterial degradation in “active urine” [9], blood EPO profiles suggesting a selection of more acidic isoforms during renal filtration [10], and “effort urines” observed after exhaustive exercise [11].

Another method to identify doping with rEPO was validated and implemented in 2009: it used difference in molecular weight (MW) rather than isoelectric point. Indeed, due to the slight changes in glycosylation rEPO drugs display a slightly higher MW than endogenous EPO. This SDS‐PAGE or SAR‐PAGE method (depending on the migration buffer used, containing either sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS] or sarcosyl [SAR] [12, 13]) became mandatory in 2013 as a confirmation analysis for doping control samples whenever the presence of rEPO was suspected following IEF‐PAGE analysis. Indeed, the EPO profiles obtained by SDS/SAR‐PAGE is less subject to variations than the IEF EPO profile (endogenous EPO shows the same MW in blood and urine, is not affected by exercise, and in cases of “active urine” presents only a decreased MW). Today the SDS/SAR‐PAGE method is still the method of choice to detect doping with rEPO and with second‐ and third‐generation EPO drugs (NESP, CERA), even if the IEF‐PAGE method can still be used as an initial testing procedure or as a valuable additional analysis for a confirmation in some cases (e.g., in the presence of artifactual bands on SDS/SAR‐PAGE). However, recently, a rare polymorphism in the EPO gene coding sequence (EPOc.577del) found in heterozygous state in 0.5%–1% of East Asian populations and leading to a 26‐amino acid extended EPO protein (VAR‐EPO) [14], was identified as a potential source of false suspicion of rEPO in urine using SDS/SAR‐PAGE analysis. An additional level of control was made mandatory by the World Antidoping Agency (WADA) in its technical document TDEPO2024 [15]. It requires to test for the presence of VAR‐ EPO (in blood or using a specific anti‐VAR‐EPO antibody) and if needed to perform a sequencing of EPO gene to rule out the rare possibility of an athlete carrying this mutation before concluding to an adverse analytical finding.

Recently, the French antidoping laboratory was contacted by a medical research team looking for expertise on EPO profiling. The team had identified families with hereditary erythrocytosis, yet these patients had no mutations in the classic hypoxia‐pathway‐related genes and did not show elevated EPO concentrations in blood. The only mutations detected were in noncoding parts of the EPO gene (the c.−252C→T variant located in the main promoter, and the c.14–28T→C and c.14–26A→G variants located in intron 1), while the amino acid sequence of the protein was not affected, although regulatory elements appeared to be impacted. Initial evaluation using SDS/SAR‐PAGE analysis found no clear difference with wild‐type (WT) EPO. Unexpectedly, after IEF‐PAGE, EPO profiles from these patients presented a clear shift to the basic area where recombinant EPO is usually found. IEF EPO profiles also exhibited fewer isoforms than WT blood EPO profiles from healthy adults. This specific form of EPO was associated with a higher activation of EPOR signaling in vitro and could thus be involved in the development of erythrocytosis. Additionally, this basic EPO profile was highly similar to the EPO blood profile observed in premature newborns, suggesting a potential hepatic rather than renal origin [16].

To avoid any possible source of confusion or misinterpretation for the antidoping community, the goal of this work was to present for the first time analysis of urine from these patients in addition to blood using all the authorized methods for rEPO detection currently in use in antidoping laboratories (IEF‐PAGE, SDS‐PAGE, and SAR‐PAGE), and to discuss the interpretation of the results obtained.

Blood and urine samples were collected from erythrocytosis patients and Eprex‐administered subjects after the approval of both studies by local ethics committees.

Patients with familial erythrocytosis and mutations in noncoding sequences of the EPO gene were recruited from hospitals by Prof. Gardie and Prof. Girodon after investigation on a genetic origin of their diagnosed erythrocytosis. They provided written informed consent for additional analysis of their blood and urine samples (for more details, see [16]). The samples analyzed in this study were from three patients carrying the c.−252C>T variant located in the EPO gene main promoter (Patient 1: male, 42 year‐old (yo); Patient 2: female, 50 yo; Patient 3: female, 76 yo) and one patient carrying the c.14‐26A>G variant located in intron 1 of the EPO gene (Patient 4: male, 50 yo). These mutations did not introduce any change in the protein sequence.

These patients presented moderate to severe erythrocytosis conducting to high hematological values (males: hematocrit (%) 55.8–63.0; hemoglobin (HGB) (g/dL): 18.2–20.8; females: hematocrit (%) 54.0–55.0, HGB (g/dL) 17.7–18.1). They were treated by repeated phlebotomies every couple of months. EPO concentration in blood were in the low to normal range (2–15 IU/L).

Eprex administered subjects: six healthy volunteers were recruited and providing written informed consent to participate in an administration study of rEPO Eprex and biosimilar Hemax conducted at Tizi‐Ouzou hospital (Algeria). Blood and urine samples analyzed in this study were collected from one subject before and 24 and 48 h after subcutaneous administration of a microdose of Eprex (10 IU/kg).

Urine and blood from one healthy volunteer were part of a collection of human samples authorized by the bioethic cell of the French Government for antidoping research (ARDC‐2022‐4944).

EPO immunopurification from urine and plasma was performed using mAb anti‐EPO clone 9C21D11 linked to NHS‐activated magnetic beads. Urine samples (15 mL) needed to be concentrated before application onto the magnetic beads. Briefly, urine were neutralized by addition of 3.75‐M Tris/HCl buffer pH 7.4 and protease inhibitors were added before the ultrafiltration step using Amicon‐ultra centrifugal filters with a 30‐kDa cut‐off to obtain an ultrafiltrate down to 1 mL.

The urinary retentate and the plasma (1.0–1.2 mL) were applied to magnetic beads for 1 hour under rotating agitation at room temperature. The beads were washed four times with Tris buffer (50‐mM Tris‐HCl, pH 7.4) containing 0.3‐M NaCl. EPO was eluted using 150‐μL elution buffer (150 mM Tris/Glycine pH 2, 6‐M urea and 0.05% of Tween‐20) followed by shaking for 10 min on a Thermomixer (Eppendorf) at 1200 rpm. After several steps of washes and buffer exchange, the final eluate was adjusted to 45 μL in Tris buffer and separated to equal volume (15 μL) for IEF‐PAGE, SAR‐PAGE and SDS‐PAGE analysis.

SDS‐PAGE: 15 μL of the final eluate was used for analysis after supplementation with 2.25‐μL DTT 1 M and 5.6‐μL LDS sample buffer. After a denaturation step of 5 min at 95°C on a Thermomixer at 1200 rpm, cooling into a frozen Eppendorf IsoPack and short centrifugation, the eluates were loaded into NuPAGE 10% Bis‐Tris Midi Gel wells using MOPS‐SDS running buffer. Transfer onto a poylvinidylene membrane (PVDF) using a discontinuous buffer transfer system with CAPS buffer was performed as previously described [12].

SAR‐PAGE: Analysis was performed exactly like SDS‐PAGE analysis with the replacement of SDS by SAR in the sample buffer and electrophoresis running buffer and the change of the blotting buffer for the continuous Bjerrum system (48‐mM Tris, 39‐mM glycine, 1.3‐mM SDS, 10% ethanol).

IEF‐PAGE: All samples were analyzed on a pH 2–6 gel as previously described [17]; single blotting was performed using the discontinuous buffer system.

EPO detection: Following blotting, the membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with the biotinylated AE7A5 anti‐EPO antibody (0.5 μg/mL). After a 45‐min incubation with streptavidin‐HRP at room temperature, detection and amplification of the signal by chemiluminescence was performed using Immobilon ECL Ultra Western HRP substrate detection. Images were recorded with a CCD camera (Vilber).

Plasmas and urines were analyzed using the methods accredited in the French antidoping laboratory for EPO detection in doping control samples. The criteria from the WADA technical document TD2024EPO were considered for interpretation and conclusions. EPO profiles obtained after IEF‐PAGE, SDS‐PAGE, and SAR‐PAGE were compared with profiles from one healthy subject (wild‐type, WT) and from one Eprex‐administered subject.

Plasma EPO profile from all the patients showed a basic profile, with almost no signal in the endogenous area (defined as below the isoforms of rEPO BRP standard) and with the most intense isoforms in a high pH range (Positions 3–5 in the basic area). This profile is even more basic than rEPO BRP standard, where the most intense bands are placed in Positions 1–3, and in the basic area (Figure 1A). The specific basic profiles from patients were stable over time, remaining unchanged over several years (Figure 2). It clearly differed from WT blood EPO profiles (as exemplified in Figure 1A), which have the most intense isoforms in the transitional part between endogenous area and basic area of the gel (rarely above Position 1 in the basic area). It also differed from the profile observed after an administration of rEPO Eprex (main isoforms in Positions 1–3, and the right part of Figure 1A). However, considering the criteria from TD2024EPO [15] (“The IEF‐PAGE profile: (i) deviates from that of endogenous EPO; and/or (ii). in the basic area there must be at least three acceptable consecutive bands; and (iii) the two (2) most intense bands measured by densitometry shall be in the basic area.”), all of the patient samples would be considered suspicious for rEPO and would be submitted to a confirmation analysis using SDS/SAR‐PAGE.

For SDS‐PAGE and SAR‐PAGE, Dynepo standard (a recombinant EPO produced in human cells) was used to delimit the recombinant area from the endogenous area.

Urine EPO profiles of the patients were slightly more acidic than their blood profiles, as previously observed for WT EPO between blood and urine (in urine the most intense isoforms are always present in the endogenous area under normal conditions) [10]. This difference between urine and blood has been linked to the renal filtration process. A not yet fully elucidated differential filtration/reabsorption of some EPO isoforms produces an enrichment of acidic isoforms in urine. Interestingly, the proportion of the shift down the pH gradient for urine compared with blood (approximately one or two isoforms positions) was similar between EPO from the patients and WT EPO. This phenomenon was not impaired by the qualitative changes in EPO glycosylations observed in the patients. Due to this shift, the most intense isoforms in urine from the patients were Positions 2–4, and in the basic area, which were closer to the isoform distribution of rEPO standards such as BRP or Eprex (Positions 1–3). In a doping control context, such profiles would be highly suspicious for the use of rEPO, especially considering the market launch of EPO biosimilars since 2007, which can present basic profiles with isoform distribution different from the original rEPO drugs [18]. In any case, this would trigger additional analyses by SDS‐PAGE or SAR‐PAGE.

By SDS‐PAGE (Figure 1B) or SAR‐PAGE (Figure 1C), EPO profiles from the patients and the healthy subjects were very similar. One single EPO band was positioned in the endogenous area of the gel (defined as below a reference line using Dynepo, a rEPO produced in human embryonic kidney cells, as a marker). On the contrary, EPO profiles after Eprex administration presented a typical smear above the line, reflecting the presence of rEPO (Figure 1B, right part). There was no difference in the band position between urine and plasma profiles. However, contrary to the WT subject and to observations from multiple athletes samples (data not shown), the intensities of EPO signal from patients plasma (immunopurified from 1 to 1.2 mL) were much higher than the EPO signal after preparation from 15‐mL urine. Urines were stored refrigerated or frozen after collection and kept frozen until analysis to limit degradation (Patient 3 exhibited a second low‐MW band below main EPO signal, which could indicate partial degradation). So, the clear difference in EPO signal intensity between WT and patients, and between urine and blood, might reflect a lower quantity of EPO passing into patients' urine. This might be due to renal filtration/reabsorption mechanisms that preferentially retain basic isoforms.

SDS/SAR‐PAGE analysis of the patients' samples would not fulfill the criteria from TD2024EPO [15] to consider the presence of rEPO, even in the case of a mixed band of endogenous EPO and rEPO (“A diffuse or faint area of the band above the corresponding endogenous band, which extends beyond the band apex of Epoetin‐δ (Dynepo) or Epoetin‐α, is also indicative for the presence of rEPO”). Therefore, even if an athlete presented with a condition similar to that of these patients (despite its unlikelihood), there would be no risk of reaching an adverse analytical finding would occur.

Considering the results presented here, before 2013 and WADA TD2013EPO [19] where the SDS/SAR‐PAGE method was made mandatory for a confirmation of rEPO, a risk of incorrectly declaring the presence of rEPO (false positive) needed to be evaluated. However, the patients' IEF profile differed from the one observed after use of the original rEPO drugs (epoetins alfa and beta, see Eprex administration Figure 1A, right part). One of the main rEPO identification criteria from WADA technical documents (TD2004EPO, TD2007EPO, TD2009EPO) before 2013 was: “the two most intense bands measured by densitometry shall be in the basic area, shall be consecutive, and shall be bands “1” and “2” or “2” and 3” [20]. For the blood EPO IEF profile from the patients, the quantitative distribution of the two most intense bands (Bands 4 and 5) did not fulfill this identification criterion. The same applied for urines from the patients, with the two most intense bands in Positions 3 and 4. Since the implementation of mandatory SDS/SAR‐PAGE confirmation for any rEPO suspicion in athlete samples, no false positive case could have been declared.

If any doubt remained, the likelihood of having high‐level athletes carrying the same mutations is highly unlikely. These patients suffering from erythrocytosis caused by a mutation in the noncoding region of the EPO gene locus require regular medical follow‐up and bloodletting, which seems hardly compatible with the practice of high‐level sport. Combined with the dehydration resulting from strenuous effort, blood viscosity may lead to vascular complications, headaches, dizziness, and visual disorders. Therefore, it seems highly unlikely that an athlete could have been wrongly suspended for rEPO abuse due to a similar mutation.

Mutations in the noncoding regions of the EPO gene locus identified in patients with familial erythrocytosis probably affect regulatory elements and indirectly modify the mature EPO protein released into circulation. Differences in EPO glycosylation are the main explanation for the specific basic IEF profile and are probably involved in the high erythropoietic activity of these patients. The mechanism leading to this EPO production is still under investigation but could be related to EPO expression of outside of the kidney, most likely by the liver. The IEF‐PAGE method used for EPO detection in the antidoping context can now be considered as a valuable technique to identify and better understand the cause for some idiopathic erythrocytosis, and potentially additional renal or hepatic pathologies, that might be related to qualitative changes in EPO. In an antidoping context, the risk of finding an athlete with a suspicious but natural basic IEF EPO profile seems highly unlikely and would not lead to the declaration of an adverse analytical finding according to the WADA technical document in force. In any case of new findings of unexpected EPO profiles, the evaluation of the profile in blood and urine, and the evaluation of EPO profiles in different samples collected over time would be highly recommended. The consistency of a characteristic profile would be in favor of an athlete physiological particularity and against the momentary presence of an administered prohibited drug.
