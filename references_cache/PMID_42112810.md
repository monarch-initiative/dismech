# Early Life Disease Burden and Outcomes in Children Diagnosed With Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia in Infancy.
**Authors:** Kumar M, Gatt D, Zysman-Colman Z, Fontoura-Matias J, Fertuck D, Dell SD, Kritzinger FE, Shapiro AJ
**Journal:** Pediatr Pulmonol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ppul.71658](https://doi.org/10.1002/ppul.71658)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a genetically heterogeneous disorder causing chronic oto-sino-pulmonary disease from birth. Since diagnosis is often delayed into childhood or adulthood, early-life disease burdens remain poorly described.

METHODS: This retrospective study analyzed disease burdens until 2 years of age and outcomes at 6-7 years of age in children diagnosed with PCD near birth at two Canadian PCD centers.

RESULTS: Thirty-five infants (median diagnostic age 34 days) were included. All received daily chest physiotherapy, while 52% received daily inhaled hypertonic saline therapy (IHS) from diagnosis. By 2 years of age, 66% experienced outpatient respiratory exacerbations, 29% had inpatient respiratory exacerbations, 60% required emergency room visits for respiratory illness, and 14% had Pseudomonas aeruginosa in sputum cultures. Abnormal audiology occurred in 91% screened, and 49% required tympanostomy tubes. Despite early diagnosis and therapy, spirometry showed a trend towards obstruction (mean FEV₁/FVC ratio Z-score -1.43) by 7 years of age. Infants on daily IHS from birth (n = 18, mostly at one center) had significantly fewer Pseudomonas aeruginosa sputum isolates (0% vs. 29%, p = 0.02) but significantly increased inpatient respiratory exacerbations (44% vs. 12%, p = 0.04) compared to infants not on regular IHS (n = 17).

CONCLUSION: Despite early diagnosis, ongoing respiratory surveillance, and regular airway clearance, infants with PCD develop frequent respiratory infections and have high clinical care burdens. Infants have markedly increased risk of hearing loss, warranting universal screening and close follow-up. Inhaled hypertonic saline may influence early Pseudomonas acquisition and hospitalization risk, warranting prospective study of this commonly prescribed therapy.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a rare, heterogenous disorder caused by pathogenic variants in genes encoding components of motile cilia, leading to impaired ciliary movement or formation [1]. Over 50 genes have been implicated in PCD, resulting in organ laterality defects, neonatal respiratory distress, chronic respiratory infections with progressive lung damage, otorhinolaryngology complications, and subfertility [1, 2]. Although the estimated global prevalence of PCD is between 1 in 7,600 and 1 in 20,000 [3, 4], the true prevalence may be higher from under‐recognition and diagnostic challenges, especially in newborns and infants who have not had ample time to develop the characteristic phenotype of chronic cough and nasal congestion with recurrent respiratory infections [5].

Delayed PCD diagnosis is associated with worse clinical outcomes, including lower pulmonary function, poor nutritional parameters, and increased respiratory exacerbations [6, 7]. Infants with PCD also display early signs of airway disease, with chronic lobar atelectasis on radiography and obstruction on infant pulmonary function testing [8, 9]. Despite these associations, wide variability persists in mean diagnostic ages of PCD, ranging from 2.6 to 8.3 years [3, 10, 11]. As a result, very little is known about PCD disease burdens in early infancy, and there is scant data on treatments, complications, or outcomes in this initial period of life. This multicenter study is the first to investigate clinical care burdens and outcomes in the first 2 years of life for infants diagnosed with PCD near birth.

This retrospective study was conducted in the PCD clinics of the Montreal Children's Hospital (MCH), in Montreal, Quebec, Canada and The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids), in Toronto, Ontario, Canada. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards at both centers (MCH 2023‐9523, Sickkids 1000080423) and families provided written informed consent for inclusion of their clinical data in internal PCD databases at each site. These internal databases were searched for patients diagnosed with PCD within 3 months of birth and with at least 2 years of regular clinical follow up, during the time period of 2000–2023 at SickKids and 2011–2023 at MCH. Inclusion in the study required confirmation of diagnosis by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) demonstrating a Class 1 ultrastructural defect [12] and/or identification of biallelic, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in a gene associated with PCD (or a hemizygous pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant in a known X‐linked or autosomal dominant PCD gene).

Various clinical parameters and results were abstracted at one and 2 years of life, including demographics, clinical history, visits in respiratory medicine clinics, chronic therapies, respiratory exacerbations (defined by the treating physician) with number of antibiotic days per year for respiratory illness, sputum microbiology, spirometry, and hospitalization or emergency events. We also collected information on numbers of acute otitis media events treated within our institutions, audiology testing, presence of serous effusions, and pressure equalization tube placement, but we did not collect data on routine or acute visits in otolaryngology clinics or on community‐based clinic visits for acute otitis media. Diagnostic age for PCD was defined as the age when clinicians first suspected PCD and began appropriate PCD therapies. Nutritional status was assessed using World Health Organization (WHO) Z‐scores for weight and height [13]. Spirometry results, using Global Lung Initiative (GLI) race neutral values [14], were also collected at age 6 and 7 years, when children were at their clinical baseline, if they had achieved those ages by the time of database review. All patients were followed and treated per PCD Foundation consensus recommendations [15]. Clinical outcomes were compared between two cohorts: those who received regular inhaled hypertonic saline therapy (IHS) and controls receiving standard care without regular IHS. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the population, while Fisher's exact testing and the Mann–Whitney U test were applied for categorical and continuous data analysis, respectively. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The study included 35 infants diagnosed with PCD within 3 months of birth: 17 (49%) from MCH and 18 (51%) from SickKids. The median age at PCD diagnosis was 34 days (IQR 61 days), and females represented 49% of the cohort. The median age at PCD diagnosis was significantly earlier in the MCH cohort (5 days, IQR 15.5) than in the SickKids cohort (52 days, IQR 40, p = 0.0003). Premature birth (before 37 weeks gestational age) was present in 7 (20%) patients, and 3 (8.6%) met the National Institutes of Health definition of bronchopulmonary dysplasia [16]. A family history of PCD was known in 13 (37%) children. All infants had a history of neonatal respiratory distress. Organ situs included 16 (46%) with situs inversus totalis, 14 (40%) with situs solitus, and 5 (14%) with situs ambiguus. Congenital cardiac disease was identified in 12 (34%) infants. By 2 years of life, all of infants were receiving daily airway clearance with manual chest percussion, and 34 (97%) received this twice daily. Daily inhaled hypertonic saline (IHS) therapy (ranging from 3% to 7% concentration) was given to 18 (52%) infants (16 of 17 infants in the MCH center and 2 of 18 infants in the SickKids center), while 17 (48%) infants did not receive regular IHS therapy. With IHS therapy, pre‐medication with two puffs of an albuterol metered dose inhaler was frequently used. For those regularly using IHS, 16 (89%) used this twice daily, while 2 (11%) used this only once daily. Median age at start of IHS was 35 (IQR 120) days. Most of the infants receiving IHS were followed at the MCH clinic, and all patients on IHS were instructed to perform equipment sterilization after each treatment session. Prophylactic antibiotics were used in one infant with chronic amoxicillin for functional asplenia.

At 1 year of age, the mean Z‐score for weight was 0.3 (SD 1.11), which falls within the normal range for age. By the end of year two, the mean Z‐score for weight remained normal at 0.2 (SD 1.08). Height was also stable, with the mean Z‐score for height being −0.10 (SD 1.20) and −0.23 (SD 1.03) at one and 2 years of age, respectively (Table 2). However, height was significantly lower in the 3 patients with bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD, mean height Z‐score −1.15, SD 0.54) compared to those without BPD (−0.13, SD 1.03, p = 0.019).

4 (40%) inpatient exacerbations occurred at SickKids and 6 (60%) at MCH in the first 2 years of life. Diagnoses at inpatient hospitalizations included viral infection, bronchiolitis, pneumonia, PCD respiratory exacerbation, and atelectasis.

All 5 pseudomonas aeruginosa sputum cultures occurred at the SickKids site.

Healthcare utilization was substantial, with a mean of 6.5 (SD 2) outpatient respiratory clinic visits per patient across the first 2 years of life. Emergency room visits for respiratory illness occurred in 21 (60%) children in the first 2 years of life, with a mean of 1.3 (SD 1.4) emergent visits per child (Table 2). Emergency room diagnoses included 28 with viral bronchiolitis or “respiratory infection” per emergency physicians, 7 with pneumonia, 3 with respiratory distress, 5 with acute otitis media, 1 with periorbital cellulitis, and 3 with fever. Excluding pressure equalization tube placement, surgical procedures were required in one child for hydrocephalus and obstructive sleep apnea, in one infant with pyloric stenosis, and in one infant for drainage of a periorbital abscess. One infant required thickened feeds for chronic aspiration.

During the first year of life, 15 (43%) children experienced at least one outpatient respiratory exacerbation, resulting in a mean of 20.1 (SD 10.8) days of outpatient antibiotics. By the end of the second year, this increased to 23 (66%) children experiencing an outpatient respiratory exacerbation, with mean oral antibiotic exposure for outpatient respiratory exacerbations rising to 25 days (SD 13.9). The mean number of outpatient respiratory exacerbations per patient was 1.7 (SD 0.7) in year one, and 1.9 (SD 1) by 2 years of life, with 13 (37%) of infants having > 1 outpatient exacerbation. Inpatient respiratory exacerbations were less common overall, with 7 (20%) children requiring at least one hospital admission during the first year of life, increasing to 10 (29%) children over the 2‐year period. Among those admitted, the mean duration of inpatient antibiotic therapy over 2 years was 14.7 (SD 5.9) days, and no patients required intensive care support during their inpatient admissions (Table 2).

Ear‐related outcomes were also burdensome. Acute otitis media affected 10 (29%) children during the first year and increased to 14 (40%) children over the 2 year follow‐up. Serous ear effusions were documented on examination at least once in 30 (86%) children. Audiology testing was performed in 23 (66%) children overall, including 13 of the 21 who never had acute otitis media. Median age at audiology testing was 14 (SD 6.3) months. Audiology was abnormal in 21 (91%) children tested, including 11 (52%) infants who did not have documented acute otitis media by 2 years of age. Conductive hearing deficits were mild in 13 (76%) and moderate in 4 (24%), and none had sensorineural deficits. Tympanostomy tube (PET) placement was performed in 17 (49%) children, at a mean age of 15.4 (SD 5.1) months. Two children (6%) required more than one set of PET placement during the 2‐year period (Table 2).

Sputum surveillance cultures were routinely performed in this population, mainly with deep pharyngeal swab samples. The mean number of sputum cultures performed over the first 2 years was 3.6 (SD 1.9) per patient. By 2 years of age, Pseudomonas aeruginosa was isolated in the sputum of 5 (14%) patients, with a mean age at first isolation of 44 (SD 29.5) days. Pseudomonas aeruginosa was eradicated with 1 month of inhaled tobramycin in three cases, but this infection became chronic in two other cases despite inhaled and intravenous eradication attempts. The other most commonly isolated sputum organisms were Staphylococcus aureus and Haemophilus influenzae in 45% and 38% of samples, respectively. [Table 2]

Spirometry data were available for 14 (40%) patients by 6 and 7 years of age. At 6 years, the mean FEV₁ Z‐score was −0.51 (SD 1.31), the mean FVC Z‐score was 0.32 (SD 1.21), and the mean FEV₁/FVC ratio Z‐score was −1.47 (SD 0.90). At 7 years, spirometry showed a mean FEV₁ Z‐score of −0.66 (SD 0.78), a mean FVC Z‐score of 0.14 (SD 0.76), and a mean FEV₁/FVC ratio Z‐score of −1.43 (SD 0.59) (Table 3).

This multicenter analysis of infants diagnosed with PCD in the first 3 months of life reveals important insights into clinical heterogeneity and early‐life treatment burdens. Despite all patients being started on daily chest physiotherapy and approximately half receiving daily inhaled hypertonic saline, infants with PCD still have frequent respiratory complications. Outpatient respiratory exacerbations are commonplace, with two‐thirds requiring oral antibiotics for respiratory illness over the first 2 years of life, and recurrent exacerbations occurred in 37% of infants. These results are consistent with an observational study reporting an average of two oral antibiotic courses annually in older children with PCD [18]. Emergency room visits are also widespread in infants with PCD, as 60% of patients presented emergently for respiratory illness by 2 years of age. More severe respiratory exacerbations, necessitating hospitalization with intravenous antibiotics, occurred in nearly 30% by 2 years of life.

Ear disease is also widespread in this cohort of infants with PCD, including 40% with acute otitis media by 2 years of age. Despite most infants not having acute otitis media, two‐thirds were screened for hearing loss, per PCD Foundation recommendations. Alarmingly, 91% of children screened had abnormal audiology results, including half of the children who never had an episode of acute otitis media. This highlights the need for universal audiology testing in infants with PCD, regardless of their clinical otitis history, as chronic middle ear effusions are commonplace (found in nearly 90% of this population) and seem to underlie hearing loss outside of acute infections.

Nutrition is often adversely affected in infants with chronic respiratory disease, and direct consequences of poor nutrition have been linked to worsened pulmonary outcomes in bronchopulmonary dysplasia, cystic fibrosis, and more recently in PCD [7, 19, 20]. Outside of the CCDC39 and CCDC40 severe genotypes, children with PCD generally maintain weight and body mass index Z‐scores within normal ranges. Importantly, children diagnosed with PCD earlier in life may have slightly better growth trajectories compared to those diagnosed later, likely due to earlier optimization of clinical care and nutritional support [7, 21]. Our data showing normal growth is maintained in this early‐life cohort supports the benefits of early PCD diagnosis on nutritional outcomes. However, linear growth seemed slightly more limited than weight in our cohort, and this may result from 20% of our population being born prematurely, which is higher than the 10% prevalence of prematurity in the general population but similar to another PCD cohort diagnosed in infancy [22, 23]. In particular, patients with bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) had significantly lower Z‐scores for height at 2 years of age compared to patients without BPD. One patient with the FOXJ1 genotype and severe obstructive sleep plus hydrocephalus had height restriction, while another patient with the CCDC39 genotype had both height and weight restriction by 2 years of age.

In our cohort, acceptable spirometry was available in approximately 40% of patients at 6 and 7 years of age. Mean FEV₁ Z‐scores were in the lower range of normal at both time points (− 0.51 and −0.66, respectively), while mean FVC Z‐scores remained normal. Notably, FEV₁/FVC ratio Z‐scores demonstrated a more pronounced reduction (−1.47 and −1.43), indicating a tendency toward airway obstruction with age, despite early PCD diagnosis and appropriate therapy. Past multi‐center studies and meta‐analyses have reported similar patterns, with the greatest deficit often observed in the FEV₁/FVC ratio, reflecting underlying obstructive physiology [24, 25]. In this cohort, the lower FEV1 and FEV1/FVC values suggest that early diagnosis, close clinical surveillance, and standard therapies fail to preserve long‐term pulmonary function and underscore the need for closer monitoring and novel treatments in children with PCD [26, 27].

Lower airway bacterial colonization is common in PCD, and infants in this cohort showed repeatedly positive sputum cultures with organisms commonly expected in PCD, including Haemophilus influenzae and Staphylococcus aureus. However, Pseudomonas aeruginosa was isolated in 5 patients (14%) within the first 2 years of life. Though some of these early Pseudomonas infections were quickly eradicated with inhaled antibiotics, progression to chronic colonization in two cases underlies the persistent lower airway infection and inflammation occurring in infants with PCD. This finding supports obtaining surveillance sputum cultures multiple times annually in all infants with PCD, as suggested by the PCD Foundation [15]. As chronic Pseudomonas colonization in patients with PCD is associated with worse pulmonary function and disease progression, early eradication seems prudent in infants with PCD [28, 29, 30]. Fortunately, when Pseudomonas aeruginosa respiratory colonization is treated appropriately in PCD, children often achieve eradication, and adults maintain overall stable pulmonary function [31, 32].

The therapeutic role of inhaled hypertonic saline (IHS) in PCD remains unclear, and currently, there are no guidelines recommending its routine use in this population. A past randomized controlled trial of IHS in adults with PCD demonstrated statistically significant improvement in the QoL‐B Health Perception scale but no other significant clinical effects [33]. Despite this, daily IHS therapy is frequently used in children and adults with PCD, and it is usually well tolerated (with short‐acting beta‐agonists beforehand), even in the neonatal period [18, 34]. Approximately half of the infants in this cohort received regular IHS, and these patients showed a significant decrease in Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolation in sputum cultures, suggesting a potential benefit from IHS. However, infants on IHS also showed a significant increase in inpatient hospitalizations for respiratory exacerbations and a decrease in linear growth in our cohort. The reasons behind these dichotomous IHS associations are unclear, but they may represent differences in overall clinical cares between the two institutions, with 16 of 18 children on IHS being followed in the MCH center. In addition, the IHS cohort included three patients born at < 32 weeks' gestation who demonstrated abnormal height Z‐scores, which are expected during the first 2 years of life before catch‐up growth occurs. Overall, these results highlight the urgent need for further analysis of IHS efficacy in PCD. Unfortunately, spirometry data at ages 6 and 7 years were not available for enough patients on IHS therapy to allow for analysis.

Despite all patients being diagnosed early in life, the median age at PCD diagnosis was significantly younger at the MCH site compared to SickKids (median age of diagnosis at MCH 5 days (IQR 15.5) vs. SickKids 52 days (IQR 40), p = 0.0003). This difference is likely explained by the lack of an obstetric unit at SickKids, meaning patients born in a maternal care setting require external referrals for PCD investigations. In contrast, the MCH site hosts maternal deliveries and a neonatal intensive care unit within the same campus. Significantly fewer patients were diagnosed via TEM at MCH compared to SickKids (MCH 29% vs. SickKids 72%, p = 0.02), likely due to the earlier time period of PCD investigations at SickKids, when TEM was a first‐line test for PCD, and widespread genetic testing was less available. Despite these differences, genetic diagnoses remain similar between the two sites (MCH 100% vs. SickKids 78%, p = 0.10).

There are several limitations to this study. First, we present a small number of patients, who were definitively diagnosed with PCD in the first 3 months of life. Despite our multi‐center approach, results are limited by this. Our retrospective analysis cannot prove cause and effect between interventions and outcomes, and our findings of decreased Pseudomonas aeruginosa cultures and increased inpatient hospitalizations for infants on IHS may not be solely due to IHS administration. Indeed, more severe genotypes, including CCDC39, CCDC40, and CCNO, often contribute to worse clinical outcomes and faster disease progression, and 80% of these genotypes occurred at the SickKids site in our study. With our small population, these severe genotypes could not be factored into analyses, and some infants with Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection were also affected by these more severe genotypes. Additionally, most children receiving IHS therapy were followed at a single site (MCH), and other clinical practices at this site may have influenced outcomes. We did not collect granular information on other otolaryngology outcomes, including records of scheduled or emergent otolaryngology clinic visits, tympanometry reports, or middle ear microbiology. As many patients are followed in otolaryngology clinics exterior to the two academic centers, this data was not available in hospital records. Thus, our reported annual days of systemic antibiotics per infant with PCD are also likely underestimated, as these only include treatments for respiratory exacerbations and fail to capture antibiotic courses for acute otitis media in the community. Since many infants in our cohort remain young at the time of data analysis, they could not perform reliable spirometry for inclusion in this study, and lack of pulmonary function data, especially in patients on IHS therapy, may have altered our results at ages 6 and 7 years.

This is the first cohort to explore early‐life outcomes in infants with PCD diagnosed near birth. With regular clinical surveillance and therapies, infants with PCD maintain normal growth but have mild airway obstruction at school age despite these early interventions. Respiratory and ear disease are burdensome in the first 2 years of life, with many experiencing respiratory exacerbations and chronic otitis media with hearing loss, while some show early sputum colonization with Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In addition to standard cares, inhaled hypertonic saline therapy may play a role in early acquisition of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and respiratory‐related hospitalizations in infancy. Rigorous, prospective, multicenter, controlled trials in neonatal and infant populations are warranted to further explore early‐life outcomes in PCD.
