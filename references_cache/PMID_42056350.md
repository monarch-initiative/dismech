# Identification of a Novel De Novo Heterozygous SEC61A1 Variant in a Patient With Severe Congenital Neutropenia.
**Authors:** Wang Z, Jing H, Chen Y, Zhong L, Yang J
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.70217](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.70217)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Severe congenital neutropenia (SCN) is a heterogeneous hematopoietic disorder characterized by impaired granulocyte maturation and increased susceptibility to infections. Over 25 genes have been linked to SCN, yet the molecular pathogenesis remains unknown in 30%-50% of cases. This study aimed to expand the understanding of SEC61A1 variant-mediated diseases.

METHODS: Clinical data were collected from the patient. Whole-exome sequencing was performed, followed by Sanger sequencing for familial validation. Bioinformatics, conservation, and protein structural analyses were conducted to evaluate candidate variants.

RESULTS: We report an 11-year-old boy with decreased neutrophils and lymphocytes, and elevated proportions of monocytes and eosinophils. Bone marrow showed granulocytic hyperplasia with maturation arrest and nuclear abnormalities. Flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow cells is indicative of aberrant myeloid antigen differentiation and impaired granulocytic maturation. A novel de novo pathogenic SEC61A1 variant (c.1135 T > C; p.Trp379Arg) was identified. Structural analysis suggested that this variant may disrupt protein conformational stability.

CONCLUSIONS: The SEC61A1 c.1135 T > C (p.Trp379Arg) variant is considered likely pathogenic. This is the second reported SEC61A1 variant associated with SCN11, expanding the genetic and phenotypic spectrum of SCN.

Severe congenital neutropenia (SCN) is a heterogeneous hematopoietic disorder characterized by arrested granulocyte maturation at early juvenile granulocytes and an absolute peripheral blood neutrophil count < 0.5 × 109/L (Rout et al. 2025; Skokowa et al. 2017). These abnormalities increase patients' susceptibility to severe, recurrent infections, including skin infections, otitis, deep abscesses, sepsis and gingivitis. Over 25 genes have been linked to SCN (Borbaran Bravo et al. 2025; Makaryan et al. 2017; Olofsen and Touw 2020; Wang et al. 2022). Yet the molecular pathogenesis is unknown in 30%–50% of SCN cases (Skokowa et al. 2017).

The Sec61 complex, located on the mammalian endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane, consists of three subunits (Sec61α, Sec61β, Sec61γ) and plays a key role in transporting nascent and precursor peptides into the ER (Xin et al. 2025). It mediates the translocation of signal peptide‐containing precursor polypeptides across the ER membrane and forms ribosomal receptors and gating pores (Lang et al. 2017). Additionally, the Sec61 channel functions as a passive ER Ca2+ leak channel (Dong et al. 2023; Itskanov and Park 2023; Lang et al. 2011; Sicking et al. 2021). The SEC61A1 gene (NM_013336.4) encodes the α‐subunit of the SEC61 complex. Known SEC61A1‐related disorders include SCN (Van Nieuwenhove et al. 2020), common variable immunodeficiency disease type 15 (CVID15) (Schubert et al. 2018), autosomal dominant tubulointerstitial nephropathy‐5 (ADTKD5) (Bolar et al. 2016; Econimo et al. 2022; Espino‐Hernández et al. 2021; Mabillard et al. 2023; Sicking et al. 2022; Tanaka et al. 2025), cystic kidney disease (Bekheirnia et al. 2021), congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) (Rao et al. 2019), polycystic liver disease (Schlevogt et al. 2023; Weiand et al. 2024), autism (Yuen et al. 2016) and developmental disorders (DD) (Kaplanis et al. 2020). To date, 14 SEC61A1 variants have been reported to be related to disorders in 15 families (Bekheirnia et al. 2021; Bolar et al. 2016; Espino‐Hernández et al. 2021; Groopman et al. 2019; Kaplanis et al. 2020; Karpman et al. 2024; Rao et al. 2019; Schlevogt et al. 2023; Schubert et al. 2018; Tanaka et al. 2025; Van Nieuwenhove et al. 2020; Yuen et al. 2016). Among these, only the p.Gln92Arg variant has been associated with SCN (Van Nieuwenhove et al. 2020). The p.Val67Gly variant was identified in a family with late‐onset ADTKD, with two affected individuals also presenting with neutropenia (Bolar et al. 2016).

In this study, we reported a de novo heterozygous missense SEC61A1 variant in a Chinese boy with scalp abscess and recurrent respiratory infections. Bone marrow examination revealed granulocyte maturation arrest, leading to the identification of the second SEC61A1 variant associated with SCN11.

A two‐generation non‐consanguineous Chinese Han family was enrolled in this study, with three family members participating. Clinical evaluations were performed on all participating family members. The proband presented with severe neutropenia, whereas both of the proband's parents were clinically healthy and exhibited no relevant pathological manifestations. Peripheral blood samples were collected from all the enrolled subjects, following the acquisition of written informed consent from the participants themselves or their legal guardians. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Academy of Medical Sciences and Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital.

Genomic DNA was isolated from the whole blood of three family members (Proband and parents of proband). Exome capture was performed using the IDT xGen Exome Research Panel v2.0. Libraries were sequenced as 150‐bp paired‐end reads on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument. Reads were aligned to the Human Genome Reference Database (GRCh38/hg38) using the BWA MEM algorithm. Single‐nucleotide variants and small insertions and deletions were detected with GATK HaplotypeCaller, and copy number variants were analyzed with EXCAVATOR2 software.

Variant annotation and pathogenicity analyses of variants were performed as previously described (Jiang et al. 2024). Briefly, sequence variations were compared with the 1000 Genomes Project (http://www.1000genomes.org/), the GnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), and an internal whole exome sequencing (WES) database of 2114 Han Chinese records. Multiple in silico tools were used for analysis, including SIFT (http://sift.jcvi.org/), PROVEAN (http://provean.jcvi.org/), Polyphen‐2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), Mutation Taster (http://www.mutationtaster.org), and CADD (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu/snv). Variants were categorized as benign, likely benign, uncertain significance, likely pathogenic, or pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics. Variants were classified as being related to the proband phenotype (HP:0012234:Agranulocytosis; HP:0012135:Abnormality of cells of the granulocytic lineage; HP:0002719:Recurrent infections), and prioritized 171 disease‐causing genes for granulocyte‐related genetic disorders (Table S1).

Sanger sequencing was used to confirm the candidate pathogenic variant. Specific primers (F:5′‐AAGACCCGGTCCATGCAGTTGT‐3′ and R: 5′‐ACAGGCCCCAAAATGCGCTC‐3′) were used to amplify the region of interest. The PCR products (967 bp) were visualized on 1% agarose gel, followed by bidirectional sequencing using ABI PRISM 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.).

Amino acid sequence homology analysis was performed using HomoloGene (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/homologene). The 3D structures of wild‐type and mutant SEC61A1 proteins were modeled using the online protein model prediction server MODELLER (https://salilab.org/modeller/). Protein structure changes and amino acid interactions were analyzed using PyMOL (https://pymol.org/2/).

An 11‐year‐old boy, born at term via normal vaginal delivery with a birth weight of 3.3 kg, presented with recurrent infections and growth delay. His height (131 cm) and weight (25.4 kg) were both below the 3rd percentile for age and sex, with no history of perinatal asphyxia. At 9 years of age, he developed a scalp abscess requiring surgical debridement. One month later, he was hospitalized for right‐sided hydronephrosis with multiple renal calculi and underwent retrograde ureteral stent placement under general anesthesia. During this hospitalization, persistent neutropenia was first noted, with a total leukocyte count of 3.96 × 109/L and an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of 0.475 × 109/L (Figure 1A). The patient had no fever after surgery intravenous cefazolin was used for prophylaxis.

At 11 years of age, the patient presented with a 15‐day history of fever and was diagnosed with severe pneumonia requiring hospitalization. IV cefoperazone‐sulbactam was administered for infection control. Serial complete blood counts demonstrated persistent leukopenia (WBC: 2.45–3.81 × 109/L) and neutropenia (ANC: 0.438–0.541 × 109/L). Immunoglobulin quantification showed normal IgG, IgA, and IgM levels. Bone marrow cytology demonstrated active proliferation of nucleated cells, with granulocytic lineage accounting for 30.5% of the nucleated cells and a myeloid‐to‐erythroid (M:E) ratio of 1:1. The granulocytic lineage exhibited active proliferation with maturation arrest, dysplastic nuclei, and nuclear‐cytoplasmic asynchrony. The erythroid series displayed increased orthochromatic normoblasts and marked anisocytosis in mature erythrocytes, with no obvious dysplasia (Figure 1B). Flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow from the patient showed that total nucleated cells accounted for 62.22% of total events. Subsequent CD45‐PC7/SSC INT gating revealed a markedly reduced proportion of neutrophils (Neu, 23.25%), compared with the normal bone marrow neutrophil reference range (40%–60%) (Figure 1C). Immunophenotypic characterization of the gated neutrophil population demonstrated an abnormal granulocytic differentiation trajectory of CD13/CD33 and CD117/CD15, characterized by retained CD117 expression in mature neutrophil populations and dysregulated CD13/CD33 expression, indicative of aberrant myeloid antigen differentiation and impaired granulocytic maturation (Figure 1D). T‐cell subset analysis of peripheral blood revealed decreased CD4+ lymphocytes (407 cells/μL; reference range: 493–1191 cells/μL), with normal counts of CD8+, double‐negative, and double‐positive T cells. This case was treated with leucoson and traditional Chinese medicine preparations to increase neutrophils.

Trio‐whole exome sequencing (trio‐WES) of the proband and his parents identified a novel de novo heterozygous variant c.1135 T>C (p.Trp379Arg) in exon 10 of the SEC61A1. This variant was not found in the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database (dbSNP), GnomAD, and an in‐house exome sequencing variant database of 2114 healthy individuals. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the proband was heterozygous for the variant and his unaffected parents carried the wild‐type allele (Figure 2A). According to ACMG guidelines, the variant was categorized as a likely pathogenic variant (PS2_Moderate + PM2 + PP3 + PP4).

The p.Trp379Arg variant is located between TM8 and TM9 in the C‐terminal region, which is critical for maintaining membrane permeability during protein insertion. Evolutionary conservation analysis revealed that the mutant region protein is highly conserved across the SEC61A1 protein sequences of different species (Figure 2B).

Structural analysis of the mutant SEC61A1 protein showed that the p.Trp379Arg mutation substitutes the uncharged nonpolar amino acid tryptophan with the positively charged polar amino acid arginine. Wild‐type Trp379 forms hydrogen bonds with the protein residues Ser383, Val382, and Phe375, with bond distances of 3.1, 2.8, 3.2, and 2.9 Å, respectively. In contrast, the mutant forms three hydrogen bonds with Ser383 and Phe375, with bond distances of 2.9, 2.7, and 2.8 Å. These significant changes in amino acid properties, structure, and interactions likely lead to functional alterations in the SEC61A1 protein (Figure 2C).

SEC61α, the largest subunit of the SEC61 complex, contains ten transmembrane domains (TMDs). Transmembrane helices (TMs) 1–5 and 6–10 form the central pore plug‐like domains. Collectively creating a clam‐shaped pore with a central ring to facilitate protein translocation. On the luminal side, a short helix (TM2a) forms a displaceable plug domain that seals the pore of the Sec61 complex (Itskanov et al. 2023). In recent years, SEC61A1 variants have been closely linked to various genetic diseases. To date, 14 disease‐associated SEC61A1 variants have been reported, including 7 variants linked to ADTKD (Bolar et al. 2016; Econimo et al. 2022; Espino‐Hernández et al. 2021), 2 variants respectively to CVID and developmental disorders (Schubert et al. 2018), and 1 each to SCN, polycystic liver disease, and autism (Van Nieuwenhove et al. 2020) (Figure 2D and see Table 1).

This study reports a novel de novo heterozygous missense SEC61A1 variant, c.1135 T > C (p.Trp379Arg), in a patient presenting with persistent neutropenia, granulocytic dysplasia, CD4+ T lymphocytopenia, and recurrent infections. Bone marrow flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a continuous granulocytic differentiation pathway with preserved maturation, characterized by coexisting CD117+ immature precursors and CD15+ mature neutrophils, plus a band‐like distribution on the CD13/CD33 plot. This pattern reflects inefficient but intact differentiation, distinct from the disrupted, disorganized, or clonal maturation typical of MDS. Additionally, the patient's presentation, isolated severe neutropenia, absence of multilineage cytopenia, and lack of morphologic multilineage dysplasia support a diagnosis of congenital granulopoietic dysfunction rather than MDS‐like process or clonal evolution. Compared with classical SCN cohorts, this case had a relatively attenuated clinical course and was treated with leucoson and traditional Chinese medicine preparations instead of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF). Differential diagnosis limitations include the need to exclude cyclic and autoimmune neutropenia, requiring serial blood counts and testing for anti‐neutrophil antibodies or anti‐HNA. Given the known risk of clonal evolution and myelodysplasia in SCN, cytogenetic and molecular evaluation is essential.

The patient also had a history of urolithiasis and ureteral stenosis, suggesting possible renal involvement. Coexisting abnormal hematological indicators and renal abnormalities indicated multisystem involvement, highlighting that SEC61A1 function is not restricted to the hematopoietic system. Notably, ultrasound showed morphologically normal kidneys and preserved renal function. Based on the clinical phenotype and in silico structural modeling, the c.1135 T>C (p.Trp379Arg) variant is strongly associated with SCN and is likely pathogenic.

Two known neutropenia‐associated SEC61A1 variants (p.Val67Gly and p.Gln92Arg) localize to TM2. The plug domain between TM1 and TM2 interacts with the transmembrane helix bundle center to block and stabilize the pore. Nascent protein signal peptides intercalate between TM2 and TM7, displacing TM2 and opening the Sec61 complex's “lateral gate” (Bolar et al. 2016; Van Nieuwenhove et al. 2020). Both neutropenia‐associated variants disrupt protein translocation and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) calcium homeostasis (Lang et al. 2017). These alterations trigger ER stress and unfolded protein response (UPR) activation, which ultimately arrests granulopoiesis and induces apoptosis (Gogala et al. 2014; Lang et al. 2011). The Arg379 residue identified in this study is located in Loop 8 of SEC61A1, adjacent to the central pore periphery, a region critical for channel stability and lateral gate conformational transitions (Dong et al. 2023). Disruption of the hydrogen‐bonding network at this site is predicted to impair ER translocon function, suggesting a similar pathogenic mechanism. Future functional assays will validate this variant's pathogenicity.

Two SEC61A1 variants (p.Val85Asp and p.Glu381Ter) have been reported in two kindreds with CVID, with 13 affected individuals showing hypogammaglobulinemia and recurrent infections (Schubert et al. 2018). p.Val85Asp, p.Val67Gly, and p.Gln92Arg are located in the TM2 region of SEC61A1, while p.Glu381Ter and p.Trp379Arg are in Loop 8 of SEC61A1. The patients in these study had normal B‐cell and NK‐cell counts, with only CD4+ T lymphocytopenia (Schubert et al. 2018; Van den Berg et al. 2004). Previously, SCN11 caused by the pathogenic p.Gln92Arg variant was associated with increased plasmablast numbers and persistent hypergammaglobulinemia (Van Nieuwenhove et al. 2020). Despite affecting various leukocyte subsets, the B‐cell phenotype of p.Gln92Arg and p.Trp379Arg variants differs from that of CVID patients with SEC61A1 variants, indicating phenotypic heterogeneity in SEC61A1 pathogenic effects.


SEC61A1‐associated kidney disorders are extremely rare, with only seven families reported (Bekheirnia et al. 2021; Bolar et al. 2016; Espino‐Hernández et al. 2021; Karpman et al. 2024; Rao et al. 2019; Tanaka et al. 2025). Phenotypes are highly variable, including ADTKD, CKD and CAKUT, with histopathological findings of tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis and cystic dilatation of glomeruli. While the patients presented with chronic renal failure, anemia and hyperuricemia. Overt renal disease in SEC61A1 variant carriers often manifests later in life, so serial renal function monitoring is recommended for all affected individuals, regardless of initial clinical presentation. Recent studies suggest associations between SEC61A1 and developmental disorders, autism spectrum disorder and diabetes (Lloyd et al. 2010; Xu et al. 2024). Certain SEC61A1 variants may increase the risk of Alzheimer's disease (Lang et al. 2017; Sprenkle et al. 2017). However, limited case numbers prevent establishing clear correlations, requiring further research to clarify these associations and the pathogenic mechanisms of SEC61A1 variant‐related diseases.

In conclusion, we report a de novo heterozygous SEC61A1 c.1135 T>C (p.Trp379Arg) variant in a Chinese boy presenting with SCN11, which is the second SEC61A1 variant associated with this disorder. Our findings expand the phenotypic and genetic spectrum of SEC61A1‐related diseases. Given the late onset of clinically evident renal disease in SEC61A1 variant carriers, we recommend serial monitoring for renal dysfunction in these patients.
