# Impact of nutrients on the function of the chlamydial Rsb partner switching mechanism.
**Authors:** Kuwabara S, Landers ER, Fisher DJ
**Journal:** Pathog Dis (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1093/femspd/ftac044](https://doi.org/10.1093/femspd/ftac044)

## Content

The obligate intracellular bacterial pathogen Chlamydia trachomatis is a leading cause of sexually transmitted infections and infectious blindness. Chlamydia undergo a biphasic developmental cycle alternating between the infectious elementary body (EB) and the replicative reticulate body (RB). The molecular mechanisms governing RB growth and RB-EB differentiation are unclear. We hypothesize that the bacterium senses host cell and bacterial energy levels and metabolites to ensure that development and growth coincide with nutrient availability. We predict that a partner switching mechanism (PSM) plays a key role in the sensing and response process acting as a molecular throttle sensitive to metabolite levels. Using purified wild type and mutant PSM proteins, we discovered that metal type impacts enzyme activity and the substrate specificity of RsbU and that RsbW prefers ATP over GTP as a phosphate donor. Immunoblotting analysis of RsbV1/V2 demonstrated the presence of both proteins beyond 20 hours post infection and we observed that an RsbV1-null strain has a developmental delay and exhibits differential growth attenuation in response to glucose levels. Collectively, our data support that the PSM regulates growth in response to metabolites and further defines biochemical features governing PSM-component interactions which could help in the development of novel PSM-targeted therapeutics.

Introduction
The type and abundance of nutrients and metabolites present during bacterial growth fluctuate (Zhang et al. 2021). Accordingly, bacteria have evolved to possess a broad range of mechanisms to sense and respond to environmental and intracellular compound fluxes. These mechanisms include multi-protein modules consisting of a sensor and a relay partner or partners that transmit the input signal into an output response altering the physiology of the bacterium including growth rate and cellular division (Wang and Levin 2009, Beaufay et al. 2021). Output responses typically manifest through modulation of transcription, protein abundance, or protein function from post-translational modifications, or altered protein-protein/protein-target interactions (Gerosa and Sauer 2011, Hengge et al. 2016, Mijakovic et al. 2016, Gottesman 2019, Schramm et al. 2020). Developing successful prevention and treatment methods requires the ability to manipulate bacterial growth and functionalities; this makes understanding the mechanisms of signal receipt, transduction, and output responses in bacteria essential.
In many Gram positive bacteria (Benson and Haldenwang 1993, Moy and Seshu 2021) and a limited number of Gram negative bacteria including Synechocystis, Bordetella, Vibrio, and Chlamydia (Shi et al. 1999, Kozak et al. 2005, Hua et al. 2006, Morris and Visick 2010), protein phosphorylation-based partner switching mechanisms (PSMs) are used to respond to a variety of signals including nutrient availability or various stressors. Signal receipt leads to alterations in bacterial virulence, development, growth, or induction of stress specific response pathways depending upon the bacterium and PSM module (Bouillet et al. 2018). In most of these bacteria, the PSM is nominally comprised of a sensor phosphatase (SP), an anti-sigma factor (ASF) that possesses serine-kinase activity, and an anti-anti-sigma factor (AASF) that is phosphorylated by the ASF and dephosphorylated by the SP. The Bacillus subtilis PSM, regulator of σB (Rsb), is the most extensively studied and is composed of RsbU (one of multiple SPs in B. subtilis), RsbV (AASF), and RsbW (ASF) with σB as the binding partner for RsbW (Dufour and Haldenwang 1994, Voelker et al. 1995, Wise and Price 1995). In the absence of RsbU activity, RsbV is in a phosphorylated state from RsbW kinase activity leaving RsbW free to sequester σB with the output being repression of σB responsive promoters. Upon activation of RsbU, RsbV becomes dephosphorylated and binds to RsbW freeing σB to direct the RNA polymerase core (RNAP) to σB promoters. Consequently, the output of PSMs is largely controlled by the dominant SP and ASF enzymatic components, which are in turn regulated by environmental or intracellular conditions.
Chlamydia spp. are obligate intracellular bacterial pathogens that infect a wide variety of animals with Chlamydia trachomatis, a human restricted pathogen, being the leading cause of reportable bacterial sexually transmitted infections world-wide and the leading cause of infectious blindness (Collingro et al. 2020, Kreisel et al. 2021, Solomon et al. 2022). All Chlamydia undergo a biphasic developmental cycle transitioning between the infectious elementary body (EB) and the replicative reticulate body (RB) (Abdelrahman and Belland 2005). During infection, the bacteria reside within a host-derived parasitophorous vacuole termed the inclusion (Moore and Ouellette 2014). Upon infection, an EB differentiates into an RB within the inclusion, RBs undergo division, and then a pool of RBs will differentiate back into EBs which exit the host cell via cell lysis or inclusion extrusion (Hybiske and Stephens 2007). While antibiotic therapy is available for chlamydial infections, the preferred antibiotics are broad-spectrum, treatment failures occur, and not all cases receive treatment due to a high rate of asymptomatic infections (Detels et al. 2011, Borel et al. 2016). These problems highlight the need for improved therapies and preventive measures such as vaccination.
Chlamydia, like other obligate intracellular bacteria, possess a reduced genome (∼1 Mbp) concomitant with the loss of a number of metabolic pathways that produce essential compounds (Stephens et al. 1998). Subsequently, Chlamydia are auxotrophic for glucose (in the form of glucose-6-P), the nucleotide triphosphates ATP, GTP, TTP, and UTP, and numerous amino acids and other metabolites including tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) intermediates (reviewed in (Omsland et al. 2014)). Prior studies have shown that modulation of host cell carbon pools (glucose, TCA intermediates), nucleotides, and amino acid levels and types results in reduced production of infectious EBs supporting the inability of Chlamydia to synthesize these compounds de novo (Moulder 1970, Gill and Stewart 1970a, Hatch 1975, Coles et al. 1993, Harper et al. 2000, Iliffe-Lee and McClarty 2000, Gehre et al. 2016). Further corroborating parasitism of the host cell by Chlamydia, glycogen, glucose, and NTP pools in the host cell are altered upon infection and host glucose transporters GLUT1 and GLUT3 are upregulated (Ojcius et al. 1998, Wang et al. 2017, Maffei et al. 2020). Interestingly, prior literature has shown that Chlamydia do not appear to respond to changes in carbon sources at the transcriptional level; however, infectious progeny production drops by multiple logs when grown under low glucose conditions or when grown with TCA intermediates as carbon sources which are inferior carbon sources to glucose (Iliffe-Lee and McClarty 2000, Nicholson et al. 2004). The absence of a transcriptional response to alterations in carbon sources would suggest Chlamydia either reside in a stable environment that hasn't necessitated the need to acquire or maintain carbon stress response pathways, alternative non-transcriptional response pathways are present, or the conditions tested did not provide the required stimulus to provoke a transcriptional response. And in regard to stability of the intracellular niche, even if cellular resources are moderately stable after reaching homeostasis post-infection, the ratio of host-sourced macro- and micro-nutrients per bacterium most certainly shifts as RBs divide resulting in hundreds of progeny competing for a set amount of nutrients.
A conserved PSM system is encoded by all Chlamydia and is comprised of two SPs, RsbU and CTL0852 (putative), which contain three domains: a periplasmic sensor, a HAMP signal transduction region, and a cytoplasmic PP2C-type protein phosphatase (Hua et al. 2006). The PP2C domain of CTL0852 is missing aspartic acid residues critical for catalytic function along with other conserved residues found in PP2C homologs, but enzyme activity may still warrant consideration as atypical PP2Cs have been identified (Shi 2004, Rajagopalan et al. 2018). Alternatively, CTL0852 may interact with and regulate RsbU activity or sequester phosphorylated RsbV1/RsbV2 (Hua et al. 2006). RsbW serves as the ASF, and RsbV1 and RsbV2 comprise the AASFs. Prior work has found all of the PSM genes are expressed during the RB stage of development, RsbW phosphorylates RsbV1 and RsbV2 on S56 or S55, respectively, and that RsbU can dephosphorylate RsbV1 (Douglas and Hatch 2000, Belland et al. 2003, Hua et al. 2006, Thompson et al. 2015). A significant role for the PSM in regulating chlamydial growth and development is supported by the severe reduction in bacterial replication and infectious EB production in cell culture of an RsbU-null mutant and moderate reductions in infectious EB production (one-log) for an RsbV1-null strain (Thompson et al. 2015, Soules et al. 2020a). However, the exact mechanism by which the PSM feeds into chlamydial growth remains unknown. RsbW can bind the housekeeping sigma factor in Chlamydia, σ66 (two alternative sigma factors are also encoded by Chlamydia, σ28 and σ54), σ66 genes are differentially transcribed when PSM components are overexpressed or deleted, TCA intermediates α-ketoglutarate, malate, and oxaloacetate are ligands for the sensor region of RsbU, and RsbW interacts with RsbV1 and RsbV2 in an ATP-dependent manner (Thompson et al. 2015, Soules et al. 2020a). These latter points support a hypothesis by which the PSM ultimately regulates growth and development via transcription. Transcriptional regulation would occur by modulating the ratio of RNAP in complex with σ66, σ28, and σ54 based on the amount of RsbW::σ66 versus RsbW::RsbV1/V2 and σ66. Shifts in RNAP alternative sigma factor complexes by sequestering the house keeping sigma factor have been reported for Escherichia coli in stationary phase through the activity of the anti-sigma factor Regulator of Sigma D (Jishage and Ishihama 1998). RsbU and RsbW enzymatic activities would be controlled by carbon sources and energy-levels (ATP), which allows monitoring of both the host cell and bacterial metabolic rates with chlamydial growth responding in kind via differential gene expression and/or expression levels. However, this model is not in concert with the prior carbon-source transcriptional data. To further refine the PSM model and to flesh-out novel aspects of the chlamydial PSM, we used a mix of in vitro and in vivo approaches to: (i) identify the RsbV2 phosphatase and to assess the impact of metalation on RsbU function, (ii) query the NTP donor preferred by RsbW in an effort to further delineate the energy sensing potential of the PSM, and (iii) define the role of the PSM in glucose-associated attenuation of chlamydial IFU production.
Materials and methods
Cell culture conditions
HeLa cells and L2 mouse fibroblast cells were routinely passaged in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), high glucose (4.5 g L−1) (HyClone) supplemented with GlutaMAX (Gibco) and 10% FBS (HyClone). Cells were grown at 37°C with 5% CO2. L2 mouse cells were used for C. trachomatis transformations and plaque assays while HeLa cells were used for infection assays. For low glucose experiments, HeLa cells were adapted to low glucose medium (1 g L−1) DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with GlutaMAX and 10% FBS. HeLa cells were serially passaged in 75% high glucose, 50% high glucose, 25% high glucose, and then 0% high glucose (100% low glucose) medium and then maintained in low glucose medium. Cell stocks were stored in cryoprotective medium (Lonza) in liquid nitrogen vapor; PCR was used to test for mycoplasma contamination of cell lines.
Bacterial culture conditions
Escherichia coli strains were grown in Lysogeny broth (LB)-Lennox or on LB-Lennox agar plates at 37°C. When required, strains were grown with 100 µg mL−1 ampicillin (Fisher BioReagents) or 20 µg mL−1 chloramphenicol (Fisher BioReagents). Escherichia coli DH5α, NEB 10-beta (New England BioLabs), or XL-1 Blue (Stratagene) were used for vector construction and vector propagation and E. coli BL21(DE3) or NEB Express (New England BioLabs) were used for protein expression. Escherichiacoli stocks were stored in 15% glycerol at −80°C. Bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Table S1.
Chlamydia trachomatis culture and infection assays
Chlamydia trachomatis L2 434/Bu (Thomson et al. 2008) was the parent strain for the recombinant strains used in infection experiments and it served as the source of genomic DNA used for cloning of the PSM genes (NCBI Reference Sequence: NC_010287.1). C. trachomatis stocks were prepared and stored as described in Scidmore using HeLa cells (Scidmore 2005). Stocks were titered using the inclusion forming unit (IFU) assay and GFP expressed from the pBOMB3-bla recombinant cryptic plasmid to denote inclusions (Weber et al. 2016, Illingworth et al. 2017). For routine culturing and IFU assays, infected HeLa cells were grown in high glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 200 ng mL−1 cycloheximide, and non-essential amino acids (HyClone). Chloramphenicol was used at 0.8 µg mL−1 (plasmid maintenance) for all strains and spectinomycin was used at 500 ng mL−1 for DFCT34 and 35 (rsbV1::GII(aadA) marked, (Lowden et al. 2015)).
Time course infection assays were performed in 24 well tissue culture plates. HeLa cells were grown until confluent and infected at an MOI of 3 using centrifugation (545 g for 1 hour). HeLa cell numbers at confluency were determined by performing cell counts on random wells. C. trachomatis strains were diluted in low or high glucose medium supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMAX, and 0.8 µg mL−1 chloramphenicol without cycloheximide or nonessential amino acids. Wells were infected in duplicate and two independent experiments were performed. At the designated times, medium was collected from the wells and centrifuged at 13 000 g for 5 minutes at 4°C to collect free EBs. Sterile water (500 µL) was then added to the cells which were incubated for 5 minutes followed by scraping to collect EBs. Samples were added to the respective Eppendorf tubes used for medium collection and the EBs were similarly pelleted by centrifugation. Pellets were suspended in 1 mL SPG and stored at −80°C for titering by IFU assay. Genomic DNA was harvested at times matching EB harvests. EBs were collected from the medium using centrifugation and the infected cells were then collected in 400 µL PBS using scraping to dislodge attached cells. Samples were frozen at −80°C until used for genomic DNA extraction.
Quantification of gDNA equivalents
Genomic DNA was extracted from frozen time point samples with the GeneJet Whole Blood Genomic DNA purification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quantified using a Take3 plate (BioTek Synergy HT). Fifty ng of DNA was used for qPCR with Maxima SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix and primers targeting the chromosomal gene omcB (0.3 µM each) and annealed at 59.5°C. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2. qPCR was performed on a Bio-Rad MiniOpticon Real-Time PCR machine. Genomic DNA obtained from purified EBs was used to create a standard curve for quantifying chlamydial DNA present in each infection sample.
Escherichia coli vectors and strain construction
Primers and vectors were designed using Clone Manager (Sci Ed Software), primers and the gBlock were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies, and vectors were sequence verified using Sanger sequencing performed by Psomagen using commercial sequencing primers for pGEX-6P-1 (GE Healthcare) and pLATE52 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) vectors. Sequence alignments were performed using BioEdit 7.2 (Hall 1999). Genes were PCR amplified using Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and C. trachomatis L2 434/Bu genomic DNA. The vector designs for making N-terminal GST-tagged RsbW and GST-tagged RsbU (PP2C domain only) were based on (Thompson et al. 2015). Both rsbV1 and rsbV2 were cloned into pLATE52 to generate N-terminal His-tagged constructs. PCR products and restriction enzyme digested PCR products and vectors were agarose gel purified and extracted using the GeneJet Gel Extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The rsbV1 and rsbV2 pLATE52 clones were obtained using ligation independent cloning as directed (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The pGEX-6P-1 clones were obtained through ligation of same-digested PCR products and vectors using T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Vectors were transformed into E. coli DH5α or XL-1 Blue and transformants were selected on LB agar plates with ampicillin. Individual clones were used for plasmid extractions with the GeneJet Plasmid kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and insert sequences were confirmed via Sanger sequencing. Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix was used with the corresponding primers for site-directed mutagenesis to construct rsbV1 and rsbU missense mutants. Sequence-verified vectors were then transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) (pLATE52 vectors) or NEB Express (pGEX vectors) for protein production.
Recombinant protein expression, purification, and GST-tag cleavage
Strains were grown in LB with ampicillin at 37°C until reaching an OD600nm of 0.6 to 0.8. Cultures were then shifted to the induction temperature and induced as listed in Table S3. Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation, frozen at −20°C, and lysed via sonication using buffers supplemented with EDTA-free protease inhibitor tablets (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (buffers listed in Tables S3 and S4). Lysed samples were centrifuged to remove debris, filter sterilized using 0.45 µm syringe filters, and proteins were then purified using affinity chromatography with Glutathione Agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for GST-tagged proteins or HisPur Cobalt Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for His-tagged proteins. Purification and storage buffers are provided in Supplementary Table 4. The eluted protein fractions were exchanged into Buffer F and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filters (MilliporeSigma). Purified proteins were quantified using the Bio-Rad Protein Assay Dye Reagent (Bio-Rad) and all purified proteins were aliquoted and stored at −20 °C. Protein purity was confirmed by running Laemmli (always included β-mercaptoethanol) prepared samples on SDS-PAGE gels followed by staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Fig. S1). Data reported reflects results from at least two independent protein purifications.
The GST-tag on the recombinant RsbU PP2C constructs was cleaved using PreScission protease (GE Healthcare). Purified, tagged-protein was incubated with PreScission protease in Buffer G and rotated at 4°C overnight. Cleaved GST was removed using Glutathione Agarose and supernatants were retained for concentration in Buffer F. Cleavage was confirmed by running samples on SDS-PAGE gels (Fig. S1C).
Trypsin proteolysis assays with recombinant PSM proteins
Limited trypsin proteolysis assays were used to assess gross structural changes in recombinant proteins after introducing amino acid point mutations. The method was adapted from (Claywell et al. 2018). Briefly, 2.1 µg of trypsin (Gibco) was mixed with 27 µg of recombinant His-RsbV1, His-RsbV1 mutants (L15F and S56A), GST-RsbU PP2C, or GST-RsbU PP2C mutants (D461A, P562S and R599C) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) in a total volume of 450 µL. At time points of 0, 5, 15, and 30 minutes, 100 µL samples were removed and mixed with 6X Laemmli buffer and heated at 95°C for 5 minutes to terminate the reactions. Protein digestion was confirmed by running samples on 20% SDS-PAGE gels for His-RsbV1 or 15% SDS-PAGE gels for GST-RsbU. Digestion patterns were visualized using Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Fig. S2).
RsbW kinase and RsbU phosphatase assays
The protocols for kinase and phosphatase assays were adapted from (Thompson et al. 2015). All reactions were terminated by the addition of 6X Laemmli buffer followed by heating at 95°C for 5 minutes. Phosphorylation status of substrates was assessed by running samples on 15%–18% Phos-tag SDS-PAGE gels containing 10 µM to 50 µM Phosbind Acrylamide (Wako/APExBIO) and proteins were detected using Coomassie Brilliant Blue. Kinase assays were performed with a molar ratio of 1 (RsbV1 or RsbV2) to 0.19 (GST-RsbW kinase) in Buffer H with 1 mM ATP and incubated at 30°C unless otherwise specified. To confirm the phosphorylation site for His-RsbV1, kinase reactions were run with a 1 to 1 molar ratio of His-RsbV1 or His-RsbV1 mutant (S56A) and GST-RsbW and were incubated for 2 hours (Fig. S3A). For phosphate donor testing, kinase assays were done against His-RsbV1, and the reaction was initiated by adding either 1 mM ATP or GTP or by adding different concentrations of ATP or GTP. To prepare phosphorylated substrates for phosphatase assays, His-RsbV1, His-RsbV1 L15F, or His-RsbV2 were used in kinase reactions run overnight with ATP. GST-RsbW was removed with Glutathione Agarose. The supernatants were then concentrated with Buffer F as described for protein purification, and phosphorylation was confirmed by using Phos-tag SDS-PAGE gels (Fig. S3B).
General RsbU PP2C phosphatase activity was assessed using a molar ratio of 1 (phosphorylated RsbV1) to 0.4 (RsbU PP2C) in Buffer I supplied with 10 mM MnCl2 and incubated at 30 °C unless stated otherwise. To test metal specificity, substrate preference, and activity of RsbU D461A, a 1 to 1 molar ratio of RsbU PP2C and phosphorylated His-RsbV1 or His-RsbV2 was initially used to account for potential reduced activity of RsbU towards RsbV2 or RsbU D461A towards either substrate. Subsequent experiments were performed with the 1 to 0.4 molar ratio. For metal specificity, samples were incubated with 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MnCl2, or mixtures of 10 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM MnCl2 or 10 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM MnCl2.
Western blot detection of PSM proteins
HeLa cells were infected at an MOI of 12 with DFCT28 or DFCT34 with chloramphenicol (800 ng mL−1) in 6 well plates with high or low glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Infections were halted at various times by the addition of Laemmli buffer with EDTA-free protease inhibitor tablets. Samples were heated at 95°C and run on 15% SDS-PAGE gels. Protein was then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk Tris-buffered saline (mTBS). Membranes were then probed with either mouse monoclonal IgG anti-MOMP (Abcam ab41193, 1:1000), rabbit polyclonal anti-RsbV1 (this study, peptide sequence for antibody production KVFDSVNEALQALAKENS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:200), or polyclonal anti-RsbV2 (this study, peptide sequence for antibody production DKVITIRKAEQESFSDF, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:200) diluted in mTBS. Validation of the rabbit polyclonal antibodies was performed with purified proteins and C. trachomatis lysates (Fig. S4). Primary antibody-probed blots were washed with 0.05% Tween 20 Tris-buffered saline (TTBS) and then probed with either a goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 32260, 1:1000) or a goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (MilliporeSigma, AP124P, 1:1000) diluted in mTBS. Finally, blots were washed with TTBS followed by TBS and incubated with Immobilon western chemiluminescent HRP substrate (MilliporeSigma) to detect antibody presence. For quantitative analysis of RsbV1 and RsbV2 protein levels at 50 hours post infection, blots were cut in half to separate low molecular weight proteins (RsbV1 and RsbV2) from high molecular weight proteins (MOMP). The blots were then probed with anti-RsbV1 or anti-RsbV2 antibodies and anti-MOMP antibody (1:2500 for RsbV1-paired blots and 1:5000 for RsbV2-paired blots) followed by secondary probing with the respective HRP-conjugated antibodies. Blots were then reassembled and imaged at the same time using chemiluminescence. Blots were imaged on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP system and data were processed using Bio-Rad Image Lab software. Three independent infection experiments were performed for protein quantification blots.
Chlamydia trachomatis vectors and strain construction
Generation of pBOMB3-bla plasmid: The pBOMB3 plasmid was kindly provided by Dr Ted Hackstadt (National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases) (Weber et al. 2016). Primers were designed to flank the bla ORF and leave a blunt cutter site (NaeI) in its absence. Amplification of the pBOMB3 backbone was performed via PCR with Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix and 10 ng pBOMB3. The PCR product was run on a 0.8% agarose gel, extracted using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit, and self-ligated using T4 DNA Ligase overnight at 4°C. Escherichia coli NEB 10-beta were transformed with the pBOMB3-bla ligation product and selected on LB agar with chloramphenicol. A single clone was used for plasmid purification with the GeneJET Plasmid Miniprep Kit and sequence verified via Sanger sequencing of the entire vector.
Generation of pBOMB3-bla + rpoBp-rsbV1 plasmid: A gBlock containing the rpoB promoter and rsbV1 ORF and pBOMB3-bla were digested using the restriction enzymes NheI and Acc651 (both from Thermo Fisher Scientific). The products were then agarose gel purified and ligated with T4 DNA ligase overnight at 4°C. The ligation reaction mixture was used for transformation of E. coli NEB 10-beta. Transformants were selected with chloramphenicol and a clone was selected for plasmid purification and Sanger sequencing of the insert.
Transformations: Chlamydia trachomatis L2 434/Bu (wild type or DFCT15 [rsbV1::GII(aadA)](Lowden et al. 2015)) was transformed as previously described with pBOMB3-bla or pBOMB3-bla::rpoBp-rsbV1 (Johnson and Fisher 2013). At 18 hours post infection, the medium was exchanged with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, nonessential amino acids, 200 ng mL−1 cycloheximide, and 200 ng mL−1 chloramphenicol. At 48 hours post infection, cells were lysed using glass beads and scraping. The EBs were then used to infect L2 cells via centrifugation and transformants were selected with 400 ng mL−1 chloramphenicol. Transformants were passaged two more times with chloramphenicol increased to 800 ng mL−1 and then 1.6 µg mL−1 for the final passage. Transformants were confirmed by chloramphenicol resistance, the presence of green-fluorescent inclusions (GFP-positive), PCR with primers specific for the recombinant plasmid, and anti-RsbV1 western blotting (Fig. S4C). Clones were obtained using the plaque assay as previously described with 400 ng mL−1 chloramphenicol and 500 µg mL−1 spectinomycin to select for the vector (cat) and the rsbV1::GII(aadA) marker, as appropriate (Johnson and Fisher 2013).

Results and Discussion
ATP is a preferred phosphate donor over GTP towards RsbV1
The reduced genomes of Chlamydia have led to a requirement on the host cell for a number of critical metabolites including select TCA cycle intermediates, glucose-6-P to initiate glycolysis or divert into glycogen synthesis, and ATP to supplement ATP produced by Chlamydia ((Stephens et al. 1998) and reviewed in (Omsland et al. 2014)). Consequently, we argue that these obligatory intracellular bacteria have a vested interest in monitoring both host cell and bacterial metabolite reservoirs. The bacteria would then be predicted to alter growth rate and developmental progression in-step with changes in metabolite abundance to maximize RB replication balanced with optimal timing of RB to EB differentiation. Prior literature indicates that the RsbW-RsbV1/RsbV2 interaction is ATP-dependent and that the RsbU sensor domain can sense TCA intermediates including α-ketoglutarate, malate, and oxaloacetate (Thompson et al. 2015, Soules et al. 2020a). And while the impact of TCA ligand binding on RsbU has not been discerned (activation or repression of PP2C activity), we predict that abundant levels of RsbU ligands and ATP result in occupation of RsbW in phosphorylating RsbV1/V2, which are then dephosphorylated by ligand-bound RsbU. In this situation, the RsbW target σ66 (supported by direct binding experiments and alterations of σ66-dependent genes when PSM component levels are altered (Thompson et al. 2015)) is free to transcribe genes involved in RB growth and replication. As access to the host-derived RsbU ligands and ATP are reduced over time owing to the increase in number of RBs, the enzymatic activities of RsbU and RsbW would be reduced as would binding of RsbW to RsbV1/V2 since interactions are ATP-dependent. Consequently, there would be less σ66 available (bound by the pool of RsbW) leading to changes in the abundance of RNAP holoenzymes containing σ66, σ54, or σ28 which would result in alterations in transcription profiles. These transcriptional changes could affect growth rate and lead to initiation of developmental progression in conjunction with other differentiation factors. Note that when RsbW is free to bind σ66 it is unlikely that all of the sigma factor pool would be sequestered and the remaining σ66 would likely direct RNAP/σ66 holoenzymes to consensus promoters. The remaining RNAP core complexes would be free to interact with σ54 or σ28 and be directed to their respective promoters which are associated with late genes involved in RB to EB differentiation or EB proteins required for early infection (Yu and Tan 2003, Soules et al. 2020b).
GTP, like ATP, can be used as a high energy molecule in certain enzymatic reactions and GTP can also be parasitized from the host cell via nucleotide transporters and C. trachomatis is sensitive to disruption of host cell GTP synthesis (Tipples and McClarty 1993, Tjaden et al. 1999, Rother et al. 2018). In addition, GTP may be produced by the chlamydial TCA cycle instead of ATP during the conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate and RsbU enzymatic activity may be regulated by TCA intermediates (Soules et al. 2020a). Owing to these metabolic parallels to ATP and the RsbU link with the TCA, we sought to determine if RsbW could use GTP as a phosphate donor allowing the PSM to also sense and regulate chlamydial growth in response to alterations in intracellular GTP abundance. To assess GTP as a phosphate donor, a GST-tagged RsbW was used to phosphorylate His-tagged RsbV1. RsbW activity and specificity was confirmed using ATP as the donor and either wild-type RsbV1 or an RsbV1 S56A point mutant which lacks the known RsbV1 phosphorylation site. As expected, only the wild-type RsbV1 was phosphorylated as determined by running protein reactions on Phos-tag gels (Fig. S3A). Next, RsbW and RsbV1 were incubated with either ATP or GTP using differing concentration of phosphate donor and different reaction times to determine if GTP could act as a phosphate donor and whether the RsbW kinase reaction showed a donor preference (Fig. 1). While RsbW was able to phosphorylate RsbV1 using GTP, phosphorylation of RsbV1 via ATP (Fig. 1A) was faster and required lower concentrations than observed for GTP (Fig. 1B). As ATP concentrations in Chlamydia trachomatis exceed GTP (∼3.6-fold in RBs and 10.4-fold in EBs, (Tipples and McClarty 1993)) and ATP could act as a donor at a 20-fold lower concentration than GTP (5 µM vs 100 µM), ATP is the more likely physiological phosphate donor used by RsbW. This would make ATP the more relevant nucleotide triphosphate for regulating PSM signaling. While GTP was a poorer donor than ATP, the ability of RsbW to utilize GTP is not a universal feature of ASFs as the homologous protein Slr1861 (a SpoIIA-like protein from Synechocystis sp. Strain PCC 6803) cannot use GTP to phosphorylate its RsbV-homologs Slr1856 and Slr1859 (Shi et al. 1999). A similar failure to utilize GTP was also found for the B. subtilis RsbW versus RsbV (Delumeau et al. 2002).

RsbU dephosphorylation of RsbV2 is metalation sensitive
Prior work from Thompson et al. concluded that RsbW preferentially interacts with RsbV1 over RsbV2 (Thompson et al. 2015). Similarly, that study also determined that RsbU could only dephosphorylate RsbV1 and not RsbV2. In an interesting parallel to the Synechocystis PSM which like Chlamydia encodes two RsbV paralogs, the ASF prefers one AASF (Slr1856) over the other (Slr1859) and the sensor phosphatase (IcfG, Slr1860) appears to only dephosphorylate Slr1856 (Shi et al. 1999). Our work with CppA, a chlamydial PP2C phosphatase, determined that activity towards the generic substrate pNPP and peptide substrates P-Ser, P-Thr, and P-Tyr was metal-dependent and that substrate preference was impacted by metalation status with differential activity observed when using Mg2+ versus Mn2+ (Claywell and Fisher 2016). Like CppA, RsbU belongs to the PP2C phosphatase class, but the RsbU-types act as sensor phosphatases possessing three domains instead of one including the sensor, a signal transducing HAMP domain, and the PP2C phosphatase domain (Zhang and Shi 2004, Pereira et al. 2011). In RsbU, the PP2C phosphatase domain is at the C-terminus and is predicted to reside in the cytoplasm while the sensor domain is exposed to the periplasm where it presumably binds to TCA ligands (Hua et al. 2006, Soules et al. 2020a). An alignment of the group II PP2Cs (sensor phosphatase types) and group I PP2Cs (CppA class) was performed focusing on the PP2C region (chlamydial RsbU numbering, 415–650) to highlight conserved residues and motifs along with the divergent ‘flap region’ involved in substrate binding (Fig. 2) (Schlicker et al. 2008, Gilmartin et al. 2014, Kerk et al. 2015). The ‘flap region’ is truncated and highly specific for group II PP2Cs and longer and more promiscuous for group I PP2Cs (Levdikov et al. 2012, Kerk et al. 2015, Walker and Pakrasi 2022). The eight conserved residues including the four aspartic acids which coordinate the divalent metals required for mediating water-based nucleophilic attack on the phosphorylated substrate are present in PP2C-type phosphatases along with the 11 conserved secondary structural motifs (Jackson et al. 2003, Shi 2004, Levdikov et al. 2012, Bradshaw et al. 2017).
Based on the metalation-sensitive substrate preference found with CppA (Claywell and Fisher 2016), we re-evaluated the substrate specificity of RsbU versus phosphorylated RsbV1 and RsbV2 using Mg2+ or Mn2+ alone or in mixtures (Fig. 3). As previously shown, RsbU could dephosphorylate RsbV1 in the presence of a 10:1 mM mixture of Mg2+/Mn2+, but failed to dephosphorylate RsbV2 (Thompson et al. 2015). However, RsbV2 could be dephosphorylated in the presence of Mn2+ alone or a 1:1 mixture with Mg2+/Mn2+ (Fig. 3A). These results confirm that RsbU can dephosphorylate both RsbV1 and RsbV2. Similar to prior observations regarding RsbW preferring RsbV1 over RsbV2 (Thompson et al. 2015), phosphorylated RsbV2 was also an inferior substrate for RsbU compared to phosphorylated RsbV1 under the conditions tested which may not necessarily reflect the in vivo scenario. These in vitro results suggest that RsbV1 and RsbV2 may play unequal roles in the PSM or that RsbV2 may have additional or alternative partners, which we are exploring. While our manuscript was in revision, Rosario and Tan reported similar findings regarding the impact of Mg2+/Mn2+ on the ability of RsbU to dephosphorylate P-RsbV2 using 32P-labeled RsbV2 to measure substrate dephosphorylation rather than Phos-tag gels (Rosario and Tan 2022).

The metalation sensitivity of both RsbU (group II) and CppA (group I) PP2Cs in reference to substrate choice suggests that experiments assessing activity of PP2Cs versus potential substrates should be performed with different metals used in isolation as combinations or the incorrect metal may mask phosphatase activity. This may explain the reported activity differences observed with the Synechocystis sensor phosphatase/AASFs (Shi et al. 1999). While RsbU could dephosphorylate RsbV1 using either metal type, time course experiments revealed that activity is stimulated by Mn2+ over Mg2+ with complete substrate dephosphorylation occurring within five minutes for Mn2+ compared to partial dephosphorylation at 20 minutes for Mg2+ (Fig. 3B). Phosphatase experiments were also performed with a D461A catalytic mutant to confirm the essentiality of the PP2C conserved aspartate for phosphatase activity. Neither phosphorylated RsbV1 or RsbV2 could be dephosphorylated by the RsbU D461A mutant (Fig. 3C).
The fact that metalation alters the substrate specificity of two chlamydial protein phosphatases raises the question as to whether bacterial levels of Mg2+ and Mn2+ fluctuate during infection and might serve as a regulatory mechanism impacting the phosphorylation status of CppA and RsbU substrates. While much attention has been given to the role of iron in chlamydial physiology, less is known about other metals (Pokorzynski et al. 2017). A putative Mn2+ transporter has been identified in YtgABCD although the YtgA metal binding component shows a preference for iron and it is induced under iron starvation (Raulston et al. 2007, Miller et al. 2009, Luo et al. 2019). Furthermore, the level of Mn2+ in EBs has been quantified, but similar data for Mg2+ concentrations in EBs or the concentrations of either metals in RBs are lacking. Alteration of bacterial metabolism and virulence in response to alterations in metal abundance and types is common amongst pathogens (reviewed in (Waters 2020)) and sequestration of metals is a well-recognized innate immune mechanism {reviewed in [Murdoch and Skaar 2022]}. In this case, we speculate that Chlamydia may shift the phosphoproteome composition in both a general (CppA) and PSM-specific manner (RsbU) in response to metal levels altered through either host cell abundance or bacterial processes. The consequences of a shift in the general phosphoproteome are unclear, but metalation changes affecting RsbU would alter the abundance of dephosphorylated RsbV1/V2, with increased dephosphorylation in the presence of elevated Mn2+, and hence the amount of free RsbW and σ66.
Mutations decreasing RsbU activity are associated with reduced bacterial fitness
Work from Soules et al. demonstrated that an RsbU null mutant containing a premature stop codon obtained using chemical mutagenesis is severely attenuated for growth (Kokes et al. 2015, Soules et al. 2020a). IFU production for the mutant in cell culture required a 72 hour harvest interval as opposed to the normal 48 hours to obtain infectious progeny. Consistent with this observation, our lab was unable to generate an insertional rsbU mutant using mutant selection and passaging conditions that relied on 48 hour harvesting intervals. The results from Soules et al. suggest that RsbU activity, and the PSM in general, is critical for normal chlamydial growth and development (Soules et al. 2020a). Along with the RsbU nonsense mutant reported by Kokes et al. (Kokes et al. 2015) and characterized by Soules et al. (Soules et al. 2020a), additional growth attenuated strains were obtained that encoded rsbU alleles harboring missense mutations. RsbU mutants with amino acid substitutions occurring at key locations within the PP2C domain (Fig. 2) were selected for activity testing. Phosphorylated RsbV1 was used as a substrate for the RsbU R599C (located in the α4 motif, Fig. 2) and RsbU P562S (resides in the substrate binding flap region, Fig. 2) point mutants. Interestingly, the strain carrying the RsbU P562S mutant allele also had a missense mutation in RsbV1 producing an RsbV1 L15F mutant. As the L15F mutation is positioned on the same face as the RsbV1 phosphorylation site (S56, Fig. 4A) and Phe is a much bulkier residue than Leu, we assessed whether RsbW could still phosphorylate RsbV1 L15F. Phosphorylation kinetics for RsbW versus RsbV1 and RsbV1 L15F were similar (Fig. 4B).

We next assessed phosphatase activity of the RsbU point mutants by measuring the time needed to completely dephosphorylate RsbV1. The RsbU P562S point mutant (Fig. 5A) showed reduced activity towards both wild type RsbV1 and the RsbV1 L15F mutant compared to wild type RsbU (Fig. 5B). As the P562S mutation is found within the flap region which is used for substrate recognition (Schlicker et al. 2008, Gilmartin et al. 2014), we infer that docking between phosphorylated RsbV1 and RsbU P562S is negatively impacted resulting in reduced substrate turnover. In contrast to the RsbU P562S mutant that only showed a minor reduction in activity (on the order of minutes), the RsbU R599C was significantly impaired and required over three hours to completely dephosphorylate RsbV1 (Fig. 5C). The reduced phosphatase activity observed for the RsbU P562S and R599C mutants supports that these mutations could have yielded the attenuated plaquing phenotype of the respective mutant strains. However, it is important to note that the strains do contain multiple mutations and restoration of normal growth through complementation with wild-type RsbU or growth attenuation by replacing the wild-type RsbU with the mutant versions would be required to directly link the RsbU mutation to attenuation. In conjunction with the report from Soules et al. (Soules et al. 2020a), the data do support a significant role for RsbU and the PSM in chlamydial physiology.

Alterations in medium glucose levels has a greater impact on infectious progeny production than bacterial numbers
To more directly assess a role for the PSM in regulating growth in response to nutrient availability, we assessed the growth of C. trachomatis L2 434/Bu recombinant strains grown under high or low glucose conditions. As Chlamydia are auxotrophs for glucose-6-P, the levels of glucose-6-P in the host cell dictate the amount of glucose available for the bacterium and in turn impact the rate of glycolysis and substrate level phosphorylation of ATP (Stephens et al. 1998, Iliffe-Lee and McClarty 1999, Gehre et al. 2016). It should be noted that glucose availability, as a primary carbon source for the cell, will also impact the abundance of metabolites including TCA cycle intermediates and ATP within the host cell and some of these metabolites would be taken up by Chlamydia (Omsland et al. 2014). Either pBOMB-bla (designated as the empty vector) or an rsbV1-expressing vector, pBOMB3-bla::rpoBp-rsbV1, were transformed into C. trachomatis L2 434/Bu or a C. trachomatis L2 434/Bu RsbV1-null strain (rsbV1::GII(aadA)) to create a parental recombinant strain (DFCT28, empty vector), an RsbV1-null strain (DFCT34, rsbV1::GII(aadA) plus empty vector), the rsbV1-complemented strain (DFCT35, rsbV1::GII(aadA) plus rpoBp-rsbV1), and an RsbV1-overproduction strain (DFCT36, rpoBp-rsbV1). The RsbV1 and RsbV2 phenotypes of each strain was confirmed via anti-RsbV1 or anti-RsbV2 western blotting (Fig. S4C).
The recombinant strains were used to infect HeLa cells grown with high (Fig. 6A) or low glucose (Fig. 6B) medium and samples were processed at 10 hour intervals for IFU analysis (infectious EB production) and quantification of gDNA (total bacteria). As seen previously, in high glucose medium the RsbV1-null strain (DFCT34) showed a trend of approximately a one log reduction in IFUs compared to the parental strain (DFCT28) at 30, 40, and 50 hours post infection (EB production would be expected to commence within the 20 to 30 hour interval) (Thompson et al. 2015). The complemented strain (DFCT35) exhibited IFU production more similar to DFCT28 than to DFCT34. Both the complemented strain and the RsbV1 overproducing strain (DFCT36) yielded significantly more IFUs than DFCT34 at 40 and 50 hours post-infection. While similar trends were observed under low glucose conditions regarding attenuated IFU production for DFCT34 compared to all strains and increased IFUs for DFCT35 and DFCT36 (30 hour time point), there were two divergent findings from the high glucose conditions. Firstly, the overall IFU yields were reduced by ∼two logs for DFCT28, DFCT35, and DFCT36 or ∼one log for DFCT34 when comparing the two glucose conditions. Focusing on IFU production for DFCT28 and DFCT34 under high and low glucose conditions (Fig. 6C), we calculated a 60-fold reduction in IFUs at the 50 hour time point for DFCT28, but only a ten-fold reduction in IFUs at 50 hours post infection for DFC34. Secondly, the IFU production differences between DFCT34 and the other strains in low glucose was not as divergent as under high glucose conditions, i.e. DFCT34 appeared less attenuated compared to the parental strain when grown with reduced levels of glucose. We speculate that a properly functioning PSM is required for maximizing IFU yields. In this scenario, the high glucose condition provides a higher ‘ceiling’ in regard to IFU production compared to the low glucose condition. The strains with a complete PSM are able to take advantage of the increased glucose levels whereas the RsbV1-null strain is less in tune with the environment and has a lower IFU potential under both high and low glucose conditions. Under low glucose conditions, IFU yield is constrained and even with a functional PSM the differences between the RsbV1-competent strains and the null strain are repressed. At a molecular level, the RsbV1-null strain would be anticipated to have a higher concentration of RsbW in complex with σ66 unless RsbV2 is able to partially compensate for the loss of RsbV1. We are currently exploring whether RsbV2 is redundant or increases the breadth or number of regulatory checkpoints for the PSM.

The reduced IFUs observed for all strains grown under low glucose conditions and the reduced IFUs produced by DFCT34 compared to the other strains under both high and low glucose conditions could be attributed to fewer overall bacteria (less RBs present leading to fewer EBs), fewer EBs being produced at the time points sampled owing to alterations in developmental progression, or production of a higher ratio of non-infectious to infectious EBs. Note that these outcomes are not mutually exclusive. Prior studies have also observed reduced IFUs in response to glucose levels without linking IFUs to bacterial numbers (Gill and Stewart 1970b, Harper et al. 2000, Iliffe-Lee and McClarty 2000, Maffei et al. 2020). To discern whether the differences in IFUs under high or low glucose conditions or between strains was due to simple differences in bacterial numbers, we quantified gDNA as a marker for all bacteria (RBs, EBs, non-infectious EBs). Differences in IFU production under high and low glucose conditions were significant for DFCT28 with a ten-fold greater yield or more for DFCT28 in high versus low glucose (Fig. 6C). For DFCT34, IFU differences trended in a similar direction as seen for DFCT28 (higher yield obtained under high glucose conditions), and IFU fold yields were less overall for DFCT34 than for DFCT28. The IFU fold yield differences for DFCT34 under high and low glucose were lower than that observed for DFCT28, again supporting a nutrient-dependent IFU production limit in the absence of RsbV1 due to an altered PSM. In contrast to the fold yield differences observed for IFUs, the differences in fold yield for gDNA was approximately two-fold (Fig. 6D) for both strains across both glucose conditions. At the 40 and 50 hour time points, gDNA was significantly reduced for each strain under low glucose conditions. As seen for IFUs, DFCT34 yielded significantly lower gDNA than DFT28 at 40 and 50 hours post infection. Collectively, the IFU and gDNA data indicate that glucose levels and disruption of normal PSM signaling through the loss of RsbV1 may have a more profound impact on infectious EB production (IFUs) than in total bacterial numbers as measured by gDNA. It is important to note that an exact correlation between IFUs and gDNA equivalents as a surrogate for bacterial numbers is not known. Electron microscopy and the assessment of EB stage specific proteins would help to further address the gDNA and IFU data in terms of absolute bacterial numbers and alteration in developmental stages within the inclusion.
Finally, we assessed the relative levels of RsbV1 and RsbV2 over time using western blotting of samples obtained under both high and low glucose conditions. As glucose levels change, we predict that PSM signaling would be altered leading to differential phosphorylation status of RsbV1 and RsbV2 as RsbU and RsbW activities are impacted by TCA intermediates and ATP abundance. Prior work from our lab identified phosphorylated RsbV1 and RsbV2 in EBs, but not in early stage RBs (15 hours), in a Chlamydia caviae phosphoproteome study supporting differential phosphorylation of the AASFs at least under the condition tested (Fisher et al. 2015). An alternative to using RsbV1 and RsbV2 phosphorylation status to regulate RsbW availability would be to alter protein abundance via transcription or through protein turn-over. Western blots to measure RsbV1 and RsbV2 levels in DFCT28 and DFCT34 grown under high or low glucose conditions did not reveal significant differences in protein levels supporting the idea that phosphorylation status is likely the major regulating factor for these PSM components versus protein abundance (Fig. S5). We are pursuing methods to measure the change in RsbV1 and RsbV2 phosphorylation status during infection. Mimicking the gDNA data which showed that bacterial numbers are more similar under high and low glucose conditions (∼two-fold, Fig. 6D) than IFUs (ten-fold or more, Fig. 6C), MOMP (found in RBs and EBs) levels appeared equivalent under the different media conditions.
Summary
As auxotrophs for a number of metabolites, it would seem paramount that Chlamydia have mechanisms to parasitize the host cell and monitor metabolite abundance within both the host as well as the bacterium to optimize growth potential and progeny (EB) production. PSMs are widely employed by bacteria to respond to environmental signals and these modules can feed multiple signals into the same output pathway while allowing for stochastic and graded responses across a population (Locke et al. 2011, Young et al. 2013). The stochastic nature of PSMs would allow for differential responses of RBs throughout the inclusion which could manifest in the unequal conversion of RBs to EBs observed during development. However, the exact role of the PSM in growth or developmental processes remains unclear. The chlamydial PSM appears capable of sensing TCA intermediates (via RsbU) derived from the host cell along with intracellular ATP (via RsbW) derived from the host or bacterium (Thompson et al. 2015, Soules et al. 2020a). In addition, the phosphatase activity of RsbU has been reported to be inhibited by phosphoenolpyruvate, a stage II glycolytic intermediate (Rosario and Tan 2022). These findings would appear to support that Chlamydia sense and respond to carbon-source levels, which remains difficult to reconcile with prior reports that Chlamydia lack an adaptive carbon response (Iliffe-Lee and McClarty 2000, Nicholson et al. 2004). Here, we offer further support for the PSM as an energy sensing module coordinating development in response to glucose levels (in vivo data) and ATP and metal content (in vitro data). Collectively, the growing body of data on the chlamydial PSM supports that it is highly relevant for chlamydial physiology linking nutrition to growth and development making it critical for infection and highlighting this module as a novel therapeutic target.
