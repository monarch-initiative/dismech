# COQ2-Associated Primary Coenzyme Q10 Deficiency Presenting With Proteinuria: A Case Report and Literature Review.
**Authors:** Yue Y, Zhao F, Chen Q
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.70221](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.70221)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Primary coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) deficiency (PCOQ10D) is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in the CoQ10 biosynthetic pathway, including PDSS2, COQ2, COQ6, and COQ8B/ADCK4. Among these, pathogenic variants in the COQ2 gene impair oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial biogenesis in podocytes, often leading to encephalopathy and nephropathy.

METHODS: Clinical data were collected from a pediatric patient with proteinuria caused by COQ2 gene variants, who was admitted to the Children's Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing Medical University in June 2025. Relevant examinations were completed, and whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed to screen for potential genetic variants in the patient's genomic DNA. Pathogenicity assessment of the identified variants was conducted in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines and online bioinformatics tools. Additionally, a systematic literature review on COQ2-associated nephropathy was carried out in this study.

RESULTS: The patient initially presented with global developmental delay accompanied by neurological lesions and developed proteinuria at 6 months of age. Genetic testing revealed two pathogenic variants: c.368G>A, p.(Arg123His) and c.908A>G, p.(Tyr303Cys). After oral administration of high-dose CoQ10 (85 mg/kg/d) combined with enalapril maleate (0.80 mL/kg/d), the patient achieved complete remission of proteinuria and maintained stable renal function.

CONCLUSIONS: PCOQ10D exhibits marked phenotypic heterogeneity, characterized by variations in age of onset, organ involvement, and clinical severity, as well as significant interindividual differences in treatment responses to CoQ10 supplementation. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of the disease. Moreover, the therapeutic outcomes suggest that all diagnosed patients require long-term supplementation with adequate doses of CoQ10, which is of great significance for delaying disease progression.

Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) deficiency is broadly categorized into two types: primary and secondary. Primary Coenzyme Q10 deficiency (PCOQ10D) is caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in CoQ10 biosynthesis, whereas secondary deficiency arises from other genetic defects or extrinsic factors (such as certain medications or nutritional deficiencies), which lead to decreased CoQ10 levels in the body. Clinically rare, primary CoQ10 deficiency is the main focus of this study (Yubero et al. 2018).

PCOQ10D is a group of autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorders caused by defects in the CoQ10 biosynthetic pathway (Yubero et al. 2018; Hsu and Lee 2023). As an endogenously synthesized lipid‐soluble compound, CoQ10 acts as an indispensable electron carrier in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, mediating oxidative phosphorylation and promoting the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). In addition, this compound also plays a crucial role in fatty acid β‐oxidation, pyrimidine biosynthesis, and cellular antioxidant defense (Hsu and Lee 2023; Liu et al. 2020).

PCOQ10D exhibits an extremely broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from isolated steroid‐resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) to multisystem involvement disease, with an age of onset spanning from infancy to adulthood. To date, pathogenic variants in at least nine known genes have been identified as causative factors for this disease, including COQ2, COQ4, COQ5, COQ6, COQ7, COQ8A (ADCK3), COQ8B (ADCK4), and COQ9. Variations in any of these genes may lead to decreased CoQ10 levels or functional impairment in the body (Hsu and Lee 2023; Liu et al. 2020; Malicdan et al. 2018; Nam et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2024).

Among these genes, variants in COQ2, COQ4, COQ6, and COQ9 mostly manifest in infancy or early childhood (Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, et al. 2022). In contrast, variants in COQ8B tend to have a relatively late onset, with a reported median age of onset of 9.8 years (Mantle et al. 2023). Furthermore, pathogenic variants in genes such as COQ2, COQ6, and COQ8B/ADCK4 can disrupt podocyte metabolism and damage the glomerular filtration barrier, with SRNS being their hallmark renal manifestation (Tan and Airik 2021).

In recent years, reports on COQ2‐associated PCOQ10D characterized predominantly by steroid‐resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) have been relatively scarce, and there exist considerable interindividual variations in treatment responses among patients. In this study, we report a pediatric case who initially presented with global developmental delay complicated by neurological involvement and subsequently developed massive proteinuria at 6 months of age. The patient achieved clinical remission after treatment with high‐dose CoQ10 supplementation combined with an angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI). Beyond presenting this case, we also conduct a literature review to discuss the relevant genotype–phenotype correlations.

A 6‐month, 16‐day‐old female infant was admitted to our hospital. She was the third child from a G3P3 pregnancy. Her eldest sibling had died of unknown causes at over 2 months of age, while the second was a healthy female (see pedigree chart, Figure 1A). The patient was delivered at full term via cesarean section due to a scarred uterus, with a birth weight of 2.53 kg. Her parents were 35 (father) and 34 (mother) years old at the time of her birth, with no history of consanguineous marriage or family history of inherited metabolic or infectious diseases. Prenatal ultrasound had revealed left ventricular dilatation. Brainstem auditory evoked potentials were normal, and cerebral MRI showed no significant abnormalities.

At 3 months of age, she was referred to the Rehabilitation Department due to poor head control and reduced vocalization. Physical examination revealed head lag, an inability to roll over, and absent voluntary grasping. The Gesell Developmental Assessment Scale indicated scores of 85 (adaptability), 71 (gross motor), 82 (fine motor), 109 (language), and 91 (personal‐social), leading to a diagnosis of global developmental delay (GDD). With regular rehabilitation, her head control improved and stabilized, and no motor regression was observed. Further auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing performed revealed bilateral elevated wave V thresholds at 80 dBpeSPL (equivalent to 45 dBHL), indicative of possible mild hearing loss. Additionally, bilateral wave I latencies were prolonged under 120 dBpeSPL stimulation, necessitating regular follow‐up. However, the patient did not undergo a routine ophthalmologic examination.

At 4 months of age, she was diagnosed with epilepsy and treatment with sodium valproate (Depakine) was initiated. Seizure control was achieved and confirmed by therapeutic drug monitoring. She also had a history of hypothyroidism, which was managed with oral levothyroxine (Euthyrox). During hospitalization, echocardiography showed patent foramen ovale, with no other structural or cardiac function abnormalities detected.

At 6 months of age, the infant was admitted to the Nephrology Department for recurrent proteinuria persisting for over 20 days. Laboratory tests revealed proteinuria (2+), a markedly elevated urine protein‐to‐creatinine ratio (UPCR) of 10.46 mg/mg, hypoalbuminemia (albumin: 28.70 g/L; reference: ≥ 30 g/L), and a blood pH of 7.31. Renal function was within the normal range. Immunological workup showed an elevated IgM level (1.21 g/L; reference: 0.31–0.85 g/L), while complement factors (C3, C4), other immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG), autoantibody panels, and hepatitis B markers were all normal or negative. Renal ultrasound indicated slightly increased echogenicity of the bilateral renal parenchyma. A renal biopsy was not performed.

With written informed consent from the legal guardian of the infant, approval from the Hospital Ethics Committee, and strict adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki, 4 mL of EDTA‐anticoagulated peripheral venous blood was collected from the infant and both parents, respectively. Whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was performed using the IDT xGen Exome Research Panel v6.0. The sequencing data were analyzed on a precision diagnosis cloud platform for genetic diseases, which integrates functional modules including molecular annotation, biological interpretation, inheritance pattern analysis, and clinical phenotype correlation analysis.

Detected variants were filtered and classified using a bioinformatics pipeline that leveraged multiple databases (including pathogenic mutations, population genomics, and clinical features of 4000 genetic diseases) and gene‐specific algorithms. Variant interpretation adhered to a three‐tier system and the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. Candidate variants were subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing with PCR amplification on an ABI 3730 analyzer, and the results were analyzed using dedicated software.

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted across two major electronic databases: PubMed and the Wanfang Medical Database, to identify previously reported cases of COQ2‐associated nephropathy. The search strategy utilized the following terms: (“COQ2” OR “Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency‐1”) AND (“nephropathy” OR “nephrotic syndrome” OR “proteinuria” OR “renal failure”).

The inclusion criteria were: (1) pediatric patients defined as < 18 years of age; (2) genetically confirmed pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the COQ2 gene; and (3) presence of clear renal involvement. The exclusion criteria were: (1) cases with incomplete clinical or genetic data; and (2) non‐human studies (e.g., animal models or in vitro studies).

The search was restricted to articles published from 2006 to the present. Articles published in English and Chinese were included. The reference lists of the retrieved articles were also manually screened to identify any additional relevant studies.

WES of the proband and her parents identified two heterozygous mutations in the COQ2 gene. The first variant, c.368G>A, p.(Arg123His) (Figure 1B), was paternally inherited (Figure 1C), with no corresponding maternal variant (Figure 1D). It was classified as likely pathogenic based on ACMG criteria, supported by the following evidence: (PM5) a distinct pathogenic missense change at the same residue (c.367C>T, p.(Arg123Cys)) has been reported; (PP3_Moderate) REVEL prediction indicated a deleterious effect; (PM3_Strong) the variant was observed in trans with pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in multiple patients with related phenotypes (Xu et al. 2018; Kurata et al. 2022), as well as in our in‐house database; and (PM2_Supporting) a low frequency in population databases (0.0001). The second variant, c.908A>G, p.(Tyr303Cys) (Figure 1E), and with no paternal variant detected (Figure 1F), was maternally inherited (Figure 1G). It was preliminarily classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), supported by: (PM2_Supporting) absence in population databases; (PM3 (Trans)) occurrence in trans with the likely pathogenic c.368G>A variant; and (PP3_Moderate) a deleterious REVEL prediction. Of note, the proband's unaffected sister was not genetically tested.

The infant was started on oral enalapril maleate (0.80 mL/kg/d) to reduce proteinuria, and CoQ10 (85 mg/kg/d) was administered after the gene sequencing results came out. After 2 weeks of treatment, notable improvement was observed: UPCR fell to 3.37 mg/mg and plasma albumin increased to 36.90 g/L. At 3 months, proteinuria further improved (UPCR 1.60 mg/mg) and renal function remained stable. The dynamics of microalbuminuria are shown in Figure 2, while proteinuria recurred due to an upper respiratory tract infection at 8 months of age. Long‐term monitoring continues.

To date, more than 60 patients with COQ2 mutations have been reported, whereas descriptions of COQ2‐related nephropathy remain relatively scarce. Based on our defined search strategy and criteria, a total of 15 pediatric patients (< 18 years) with genetically confirmed COQ2 mutations and complete clinical data were identified from 2006 to the present (Table 1). The age of onset varied widely, ranging from the neonatal period to adolescence: two cases presented in the neonatal period, 11 in infancy, and two during adolescence. Among the cohort, four patients had neurological involvement, one had cardiomyopathy, and one exhibited optic atrophy. Nine children received CoQ10 supplementation, of whom six responded favorably. The remaining three showed no significant benefit and experienced rapid progression to end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) or death. Notably, eight individuals (including the above‐mentioned three children and the five children who did not receive CoQ10 replacement therapy and had poor prognosis) carried homozygous COQ2 mutations, and four of them developed neurological disorders.

The child presented with early‐onset global developmental delay complicated by neurological lesions and developed proteinuria at 6 months of age. Considering the family history, genetic testing was promptly performed, which revealed compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in the COQ2 gene. The patient exhibited a favorable response to oral CoQ10 supplementation, confirming the diagnosis of primary CoQ10 deficiency type 1 (PCOQ10D1). This disorder has a broad clinical spectrum, which can manifest as isolated renal involvement (e.g., nephrotic syndrome, renal failure) or multisystemic manifestations such as neonatal renal failure, hyperlactatemia, and severe neurological involvement (Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, Emma, et al. 2022). Therefore, for pediatric patients with a definite family history, early disease onset, multisystem involvement, and unknown etiology, early genetic testing is recommended to facilitate a definitive diagnosis.

Geographical variations exist in the distribution of CoQ10 deficiency‐related pathogenic variants, with a notably higher incidence reported in Asian populations (Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, et al. 2022). For instance, Park et al. found that PCOQ10D accounts for 7% of all pediatric SRNS cases and 16% of those with a genetic diagnosis (Park et al. 2017). Similarly, two multicenter studies in China revealed COQ8B variants in 5.7%–6.7% of pediatric SRNS cases (Wang et al. 2017; Rao et al. 2019), and Zhang et al. identified the Han Chinese as a high‐risk group for COQ8B‐related SRNS (Zhang et al. 2024). In contrast, an international cohort study identified only 9 PCOQ10D families among 526 genetically confirmed SRNS cases (Sadowski et al. 2015). These findings collectively indicate that PCOQ10D‐associated glomerulopathy is more prevalent in Asians.

PCOQ10D exhibits marked clinical heterogeneity, with an age of onset ranging from infancy to adulthood. The genotype–phenotype correlation of this disease is complex and poorly predictive, posing considerable challenges to disease diagnosis and prognostic assessment (Hsu and Lee 2023; Alcazar‐Fabra et al. 2018, 2021; Fontaine et al. 2025). However, specific correlations have been established in experimental models, such as cell lines and yeast. These functional models have demonstrated that mild mutations in the COQ2 gene (which preserve partial enzyme activity) are more likely to cause an isolated renal phenotype. In contrast, severe variants (resulting in profound loss of function) are predominantly associated with early‐onset, multisystemic disease. Despite these mechanistic insights, phenotypic overlap remains a significant clinical challenge. Pathologically, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) and podocyte mitochondrial abnormalities are common findings (Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, et al. 2022).

Beyond the COQ2 gene, variants in genes such as COQ6 and COQ8B can also cause PCOQ10D and SRNS. This conclusion is supported by relevant mouse model experiments: mice deficient in the coq6 or adck6 (coq8b) gene develop symptoms such as proteinuria, podocyte foot process effacement, and glomerulosclerosis, which closely recapitulate the phenotypic features of human patients (Heeringa et al. 2011; Gee et al. 2014; Widmeier et al. 2019).

A cohort study by Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, et al. (2022) revealed genotype‐dependent variation in disease progression. Among pediatric‐onset cases, 50% of patients with COQ2 or COQ6 variants developed renal failure before age 5, whereas COQ8B‐related disease typically manifested during adolescence. By adulthood, however, renal survival was similarly poor across all three genetic groups, with COQ2 patients showing the worst overall survival. Extra‐renal manifestations were most frequent and severe in the COQ2 cohort and least common in COQ8B‐related disease. Furthermore, COQ6 variants cause Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency‐6 (PCOQ10D6), which is characterized mainly by SRNS and sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), reinforcing the link between COQ6 dysfunction and renal‐audiological phenotypes (Nam et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2024; Wongkittichote et al. 2023). Notably, among siblings carrying the same COQ6 variant, the presence of the COQ8B p.(Arg174) allele was associated with milder renal involvement compared to the p.(His174) allele, suggesting that COQ8B polymorphisms may act as phenotypic modifiers (Guery et al. 2003).

The COQ2 gene, located on chromosome 4, encodes p‐hydroxybenzoate‐polyprenyl transferase, which catalyzes the attachment of the benzoquinone ring to the decaprenyl side chain during CoQ10 synthesis (Tan and Airik 2021). Silencing the coq2 homolog in drosophila led to slit diaphragm and mitochondrial defects, along with increased autophagy and mitophagy—pathologies reversible by CoQ10 supplementation or human wild‐type COQ2 expression (Zhu et al. 2017). To date, 16 predominantly missense COQ2 mutations have been reported, most frequently c.973A>G, p.(Thr325Ala). Here, we identify a novel heterozygous missense mutation in COQ2. As shown in the table, its core renal phenotype is SRNS, typically manifesting in infancy or early childhood. Renal involvement varies individually, from childhood proteinuria to rapid ESKD progression, potentially influenced by variant‐dependent residual COQ2 function (Desbats et al. 2016). Early onset, homozygous mutations, and neurological lesions further predict severe outcomes (Wu et al. 2019), as seen in Table 1 patients 2–4, 6, 7 and 11, most of whom have poor prognosis.

Oral CoQ10 supplementation serves as the mainstay treatment for PCOQ10D, compensating for biosynthetic defects and improving symptoms including proteinuria, neurological manifestations, and hearing impairment (Tan and Airik 2021; Alcazar‐Fabra et al. 2018; Fontaine et al. 2025; Shalata et al. 2019; Ashrafi et al. 2022). In the present case, administration of high‐dose CoQ10 (85 mg/kg/d) combined with enalapril maleate (0.80 mL/kg/d) for 3 months resulted in resolution of proteinuria and stabilized renal function. Treatment efficacy, however, varies considerably depending on genotype, disease stage, organ involvement, timing of initiation, and dosage (Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, et al. 2022; Mantle et al. 2023; Tan and Airik 2021; Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, Emma, et al. 2022). Nearly half of the patients summarized in the table experienced poor outcomes despite CoQ10 treatment, likely due in part to subtherapeutic initial dosing. Drovandi et al. reported that 6% of COQ2 patients attained complete remission with CoQ10 doses up to 60 mg/kg/d, aligning with our findings. Early intervention before significant structural damage to podocytes or tubular cells is crucial for optimal renal outcomes (Sun et al. 2024; Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, Emma, et al. 2022).

Therapeutic responses also reflect genotype–phenotype correlations: patients with COQ6 variants typically achieve higher proteinuria remission rates (Nam et al. 2022; Drovandi, Lipska‐Zietkiewicz, Ozaltin, Emma, et al. 2022), whereas those with COQ8A or COQ7 mutations often show limited response (Hsu and Lee 2023; Rebelo et al. 2023). Hearing outcomes also differ by genotype; carriers of c.686A>C exhibit poorer responses compared to those with c.189_191delGAA or c.782C>T variants (Mantle et al. 2023).

Several factors constrain treatment success, including poor CoQ10 bioavailability, uneven tissue distribution, and irreversible disease progression (Mantle et al. 2023; Tan and Airik 2021; González‐García et al. 2025). These limitations have motivated the investigation of alternative agents such as 4‐hydroxybenzoic acid, a CoQ10 precursor that has shown promise in preclinical models for restoring cellular CoQ10 levels, though clinical evidence remains limited (González‐García et al. 2025).

PCOQ10D1 is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. The proband carries compound heterozygous variants inherited from each parent, indicating that both parents are obligate carriers, with a 25% recurrence risk for subsequent pregnancies. The unexplained death of the affected sibling at 2 months of age in this family underscores the critical importance of timely and comprehensive genetic counseling.

Detailed genetic counselling was provided to the parents, emphasizing the genetic nature of the disease, the carrier status, and the recurrence risks. For future family planning, prenatal testing via chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis, as well as preimplantation genetic testing (PGT), were highly recommended to prevent the recurrence of the disease. Furthermore, ethical considerations regarding cascade testing must be addressed. While the proband's second sibling is currently a healthy female, targeted genetic screening for this asymptomatic minor could be considered. Although testing minors for adult‐onset conditions is generally discouraged, early identification of carrier status or presymptomatic disease in early‐onset actionable conditions (such as PCOQ10D, which is highly responsive to early CoQ10 supplementation) holds significant clinical benefit. Shared decision‐making with the parents is essential in navigating these ethical complexities.

In summary, while oral CoQ10 remains the cornerstone therapy, its variable efficacy underscores the need for early diagnosis, adequate dosing, and novel strategies like precursor‐based treatments to improve prognosis.
