Ossicular Fusion and Cholesteatoma in Auriculo-Condylar Syndrome:
In Vivo Evidence of Arrest of Embryogenesis
Evan J. Propst, MD; Bo Y . Ngan, MD; Richard J. Mount; Daniel Martin-Munoz, MD; Susan Blaser, MD;
Robert V . Harrison, PhD; Sharon L. Cushing, MD; Blake C. Papsin, MD
Auriculo-condylar syndrome (ACS) is a rare condition affecting first branchial arch structures. The types of hearing loss
and temporal bone findings in ACS have not been reported. We describe a 14-year-old male with constricted pinnae, mandib-
ular dysostosis, glossoptosis, a high-arched palate, hearing loss, and cholesteatoma. Computed tomography imaging demon-
strated malleoincudal joint ankylosis. The fused malleoincudal complex was removed during mastoidectomy for cholestea-
toma. Electron microscopy and histopathology of the joint suggested the fusion was congenital. This is the first report of
ossicular fusion and cholesteatoma in ACS and the most detailed in vivo evidence of disruption of embryogenesis during mal-
leoincudal joint formation.
Key Words: Auriculo-condylar syndrome, question mark ears, constricted ears, congenital cleft auricle, malleoincudal
joint fusion, ossicular fusion, electron microscopy, cholesteatoma.
Laryngoscope, 123:528–532, 2013
INTRODUCTION
Auriculo-condylar syndrome (ACS, OMIM 602483)
is a rare autosomal dominant condition with phenotypic
variability and incomplete penetrance.
1,2 ACS was first
described by Jampol et al. 1 in 1998 in a five-generation
family with a marked constriction between the lower and
middle thirds of the pinna (‘‘question mark ear’’) and
abnormalities of the temporomandibular joint and man-
dibular condyle with microstomia. A recent review of 56
individuals with ACS described additional features of
macrocephaly , mild developmental delay , auricular pits/
tags, stenotic external auditory canals, hearing loss, pto-
sis, facial asymmetry and/or clefting, prominent cheeks,
abnormal palate, micrognathia, microglossia, glossopto-
sis, respiratory distress, feeding difficulties, and
hypotonia.
2 ACS is believed to be due to an embryologic
abnormality in the development of the first branchial
arch, because the first branchial arch is believed to give
rise to the mandible, premaxilla, maxilla, zygomatic
bone, part of the temporal bone, muscles of mastication,
and parts of the malleus and incus in the middle ear.
3
Despite the fact that hearing loss has been reported in
42% of patients with ACS,
2 only the presence or absence
of hearing loss has been described, and there have been
no descriptions of temporal bone findings to suggest the
cause of hearing loss in affected individuals.
1,2
CASE REPORT
We report the case of a 14-year-old white male with
Robin sequence and mandibular dysostosis, recurrent
right draining ear, and a worsening conductive hearing
loss (Fig. 1). The right pinna was constricted at the junc-
tion of the inferior and middle two-thirds immediately
superior to the lobule, and the helix and superior anti-
helical crura were underdeveloped. Otoscopy revealed a
stenotic right external auditory canal with a tympanic
membrane perforation with keratinous debris in the
middle ear space. Two-dimensional computed tomogra-
phy (CT) axial imaging (Fig. 2) revealed a funnel-shaped
external auditory canal narrowing medially with opacifi-
cation of the external auditory canal, middle ear space,
and mastoid cavity . The malleus and incus were in nor-
mal position, but the malleoincudal joint appeared to be
partially ankylosed laterally . The head of the malleus
was smaller than usual for the patient’s age, but the
body of the incus was complete. The periantral air cells
were eroded with a resultant large cavity . Three-dimen-
sional CT imaging (Fig. 3) revealed a small tympanic

DISCUSSION
We report the case of a 14-year-old male with man-
dibular dysostosis, constricted pinnae, and conductive
hearing loss—features consistent with ACS. We further
describe the presence of ossicular fusion at the malleoin-
cudal joint, a feature that has not previously been
described in ACS, and hypothesize that this is likely the
primary cause of conductive hearing loss in this syn-
drome. Fusion of the malleus and incus has been
described in patients with various syndromes, including
branchio-oto-renal syndrome.
5 In the normally hearing
ear, ossicular development begins at 4 to 5 weeks of
embryogenesis, as neural crest mesenchyme from the
first and second branchial arches differentiates into indi-
vidual ossicles.
3 The first branchial arch (mandibular
arch) is believed to give rise to the head of the malleus
and the short crus and body of the incus.
3 The second
branchial arch (hyoid arch) is believed to give rise to the
manubrium of the malleus, long process of the incus, the
stapes, and the tympanic surface of the footplate.
3 At 8
to 9 weeks’ gestation, the malleus and incus, which were
previously one collection of cells, appear to separate by
the formation of the malleoincudal joint.
6 Electron mi-
croscopy of the joint in our patient (Fig. 4C, 4D)
confirmed that it was only formed through half of the
specimen with the cleft on the ventral surface and the
fusion along the dorsal aspect. This provides evidence
for arrest of embryogenesis resulting in joint fusion. In
mice, cells in the incudomalleal joint region actually do
not move away but rather downregulate expression of
chondrogenic markers (Type II collagen) and take on a
joint fate, expressing genes such as Gdf5.
7 Deep histopa-
thologic sections through the joint region (Fig. 5)
demonstrated underdeveloped and asymmetric articular
cartilages with an irregularly shaped articular disc with
evidence of ground substances. This suggests uncoordi-
nated and uneven downregulation of chondrocytes
throughout the joint space. Examination of the area
where the joint had not formed showed mineralization
from the inside outward, providing proof of endochondral
ossification of the malleus and incus that is believed to
begin at 15 weeks’ gestation.

Our patient had a draining right ear with pro-
gressive hearing loss and was found to have
histopathologically confirmed cholesteatoma. We do not
believe the joint fusion to be due to inflammation second-
ary to cholesteatoma because in our experience, ossicular
fusion has never before been observed in the presence of
cholesteatoma in the absence of another syndrome involv-
ing middle ear ossicular formation. We therefore
hypothesize that the conductive hearing loss was likely
due primarily to ossicular fusion secondary to ACS, which
progressed due to the addition of cholesteatoma. We previ-
ously reported temporal bone anomalies in children with
cholesteatoma and proposed two theories to explain the
association.
8 The first is a mechanical theory , whereby
cochleovestibular anomalies are part of a greater temporal
bone anomaly involving the middle ear that predisposes
to cholesteatoma formation. Based on this theory , aber-
rant ossicular and ligamentous formation may have led to
anomalous aeration pathways leading to cholesteatoma
formation. The second is a developmental theory , whereby
a third variable such as genetic mutation leads to both
cochleovestibular anomalies and cholesteatoma. One
theory of congenital cholesteatoma formation involves
aberrant contact between ectoderm of the first branchial
cleft with endoderm lining the first pharyngeal pouch dur-
ing 8 weeks’ gestation with epithelial cells derived from
the meatal plate becoming pinched off medially by meso-
dermal elements.
9 The tympanic ring starts as a single
condensation in the region of the first branchial arch and
is formed by endomembranous ossification.
10 Given that
three-dimensional CT imaging in our patient revealed a
small tympanic ring, it is possible that aberrant formation
during embryogenesis led to both temporal bone anoma-
lies and cholesteatoma formation.
CONCLUSION
ACS is a rare autosomal dominant condition affect-
ing mainly first branchial arch structures. Even though
approximately one half of these individuals have hearing
loss, the cause remains unknown, and there have been
no descriptions of temporal bone anomalies in these
patients. This is the first report of ossicular fusion and
cholesteatoma in a patient with ACS and the most
detailed histopathologic study providing in vivo evidence
of disruption of embryogenesis during formation of the
malleoincudal joint.
