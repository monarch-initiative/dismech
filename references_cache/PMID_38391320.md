Angiosarcoma (AS) is a rare malignant tumor that functionally and morphologically recapitulate features of normal endothelium and comprise less than 5% of all soft tissue sarcomas.[1]They have a marked predilection to occur in the skin and soft tissue. They are also known to involve rare sites such as breast, liver, bone, spleen, heart, orbit, and oral cavity. As the presentation and behavior of these tumors depend on the location, they are divided into several clinical groups: cutaneous angiosarcoma of the usual type unassociated with lymphedema; cutaneous angiosarcoma associated with lymphedema; angiosarcoma of the breast; radiation-induced angiosarcoma; and angiosarcoma of deep soft tissue. Of these, the most common is cutaneous AS unassociated with lymphedema, which primarily affects the head and neck area of elderly patients. Diagnosis of these tumors is often difficult due to several reasons—small size of the biopsies, rarity of the tumor, and wide morphologic spectrum often leading to confusion with benign vascular tumors at one end to carcinomas and sarcomas at the other end. This study aimed to describe the clinicopathological and immunophenotypic features of a series of head and neck AS with special emphasis on the differential diagnosis and pitfalls in the diagnosis of this rare tumor.

This Was A Retrospective Study Of 11-Year Duration From January 1, 2006, To December 31, 2017. Following Approval By The Institutional Review Board At Our Center, Case Records And All The Available H And E And Immunohistochemical Slides Of 36 Cases Of Angiosarcoma During The Study Period Were Retrieved From The Archives. Clinical Characteristics, Treatment Received, And Follow-Up Data Were Obtained From The Case Files. Histomorphological Features Assessed Were Growth Patterns, Cell Types, Cellular Atypia, Mitotic Activity, Presence Of Intracytoplasmic Luminae, Tumor Giant Cells, Necrosis, Hyaline Globules, And The Presence Of Any Additional Findings, Which Were Recorded. Patterns Of Immunohistochemical Staining And Their Utility In Resolving Differential Diagnoses Were Assessed.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using Ventana BenchMark XT automated immunostainer. The primary antibodies used were CD31 (DAKO, clone JC70A, dilution 1:50), CD34 (DAKO, clone QBEnd-10, dilution 1:50), CK (DAKO, clone AE1/AE3, dilution 1:100), LCA (DAKO, clone 2B11 + PD7/26, dilution 1:500), S100 (DAKO, Polyclonal, RTU), Desmin (DAKO, D33, 1:100), P63 (NeoBiotechnologies, clone TP63/1423R, RTU), and HMB45 (PathnSitu, clone HMB45, RTU).

The study group comprised 22 females and 14 males. The age ranged from 43 years to 84 years with a mean age of 65 years. The most common anatomic location was the scalp (n= 27). Other sites involved were face (n= 4), maxillary sinus (n= 2), submandibular region (n= 2), and neck (n= 1). Clinically, most patients presented with a history of enlarging erythematous pruritic patches, which progressed to swelling, ulceration, and bleeding [Figure 1aandb]. Two patients with a mass involving maxillary sinus presented with epistaxis. The duration of symptoms ranged from one month to 12 months (median duration of five months). Satellite lesions were noted in seven cases, and lesions were multifocal in five cases. The majority of the biopsies showed superficial squamous epithelium with a subepithelial Grenz zone and an infiltrative neoplasm involving the entire dermis and variably involving the subcutis. The superficial epithelium was normal in most cases but at times showed hyperplasia, mild-to-moderate reactive atypia, and ulceration. The most common histopathological pattern observed was the presence of a combination of vasoformative and solid components, which was noted in 22 cases (61%). The proportion of each component varied with the vasoformative component predominating in 14 cases and solid foci predominating in eight cases. The vasoformative areas were often seen to merge with solid areas in most cases and could be identified as dissecting and anastomosing slit-like spaces filled with red blood cells and lined by plump endothelial cells amid solid sheets of malignant cells, except in one case where the vasoformative component was seen juxtaposed to the solid undifferentiated area. While 13 cases (36%) showed a purely vasoformative pattern with the absence of solid areas, the pure solid pattern was found in only one case (3%). Vasoformative component varied in morphology from capillary and cavernous-sized blood vessels to retiform slit-like vessels [Figure 2a-c]. In one case, the neoplastic component was composed exclusively of capillary-sized blood vessels lined by mildly atypical endothelium, which could be identified as malignant only by its lack of circumscription and infiltrative nature into the subcutis. Lobular (n= 1), trabecular (n= 2), and pseudoglandular (n= 1) growth patterns were focally noted adjacent to typical angiosarcomatous areas. One case with an exclusive solid pattern had a spindle cell sarcomatous appearance [Case 21;Figure 3e].

In the vasoformative areas, the neoplastic cells appeared as plump atypical endothelial cells lining the vessels often with hobnailing, which could be easily identified at low power magnification. The cells were also seen forming tufts and papillary projections into the vessel lumen with hyalinized cores [Figure 2d]. In solid areas, the tumor cells were either exclusively epithelioid (n= 12) [Figure 2e], spindly (n= 2), or mixed epithelioid and spindly (n= 9). Epithelioid morphology was characterized by cells with copious amounts of dense amphophilic cytoplasm and round/ovoid nuclei with fine chromatin and prominent central eosinophilic nucleoli [Case 20;Figure 3a]. Occasional cells with intracytoplasmic luminae containing red blood cells were noted in both epithelioid and spindle cells in 14 cases and extracellular hyaline globules were noted in four cases. In three cases, the neoplastic cells showed prominent cytoplasmic vacuolation and signet ring cell morphology [Case 6;Figure 3candd], and in one case, there was rhabdoid morphology [Case 15;Figure 3b]. The cytological atypia varied from mild (n= 9) to moderate (n= 11) and marked (n= 16), with tumor giant cell formation in five cases. Perineural invasion was identified in two cases.

Solid areas were seen punctuated by necrosis (n= 12), which was an extensive geographic type in two cases. A notable stromal feature identified in four cases was the presence of dense sclerosis with neoplastic cells in cords and trabeculae seen dissecting through them [Figure 2f]. Other findings were the presence of lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, blood lakes, and hemosiderin-laden macrophages.

CD31 was positive in all 36 cases (100%), while CD34 was positive in only 40% of the cases (10/25). Pan-keratin (AE1/AE3) was done in 15 cases and was positive in one case [Figure 3f-h]. Twenty-nine patients received treatment at our center. Sixteen patients underwent surgical excision. A total of 13 patients were treated with radiation therapy, with one of them receiving chemotherapy in addition. Two patients refused therapy, and five patients were lost to follow-up before initiating therapy. Clinical follow-up was available for 31 patients and ranged from one month to 59 months (median follow-up of 13 months). Local recurrence developed in 12 patients; one patient had parotid recurrence; and 12 patients developed metastasis. Sites of metastasis were lymph nodes (n= 9), lung (n= 1), liver (n= 1), and bone (n= 1). Twenty-five patients died of disease, on an average of 24 months after diagnosis; four patients are alive with disease; and two patients are alive with no evidence of disease at the last follow-up.

AS of the head and neck primarily affect the scalp of elderly patients and are known to have varied presentations. The majority of the patients in this series presented with slow-growing nodular erythematous swellings and less often infiltrative plaque-like or ulcerative lesions. At presentation, they were often misdiagnosed for an unrelated inflammatory, infective, or dermatologic condition before being referred to our center. The mean age of the patients was 65 yrs and the most common site was scalp and face, which was comparable to other previous studies.[2,3]

In the majority of biopsies, we had a primary diagnosis of AS by morphology alone. The superficial location of the neoplasm, infiltrative nature, and histological evidence of vasoformation, which ranged from evident well-formed vessels to subtle cytologic features such as intracytoplasmic lumens with red blood cells merging with spindled/epithelioid morphology of tumor cells, pointed to the diagnosis. 96% of the cases in our series showed the presence of at least focal vasoformative areas. Previous studies on head and neck AS by Austet al.[3]and oral and salivary gland AS by Fanburg-Smithet al.[4]showed vasoformation in all the cases included in their studies, but the identification of high-grade undifferentiated solid foci of neoplastic cells, particularly in a limited biopsy, often leads to a range of differentials.

A significant proportion of our cases were referred to our center with a histologic diagnosis other than AS. The differential diagnosis considered included acantholytic squamous cell carcinoma in one case, poorly differentiated carcinoma/melanoma in two cases, large-cell lymphoma in one case, spindle cell carcinoma/sarcoma in one case, and rhabdomyosarcoma in one case. Acantholytic squamous cell carcinoma is a rare histologic variant of squamous cell carcinoma occurring in sun-exposed areas of the head and neck. It is a known histologic mimicker of AS.[5,6]In our case, pan-keratin and p63 were focally expressed in the tumor cells, but intense positivity for CD31 and CD34 and typical angiosarcomatous areas aided in reaching the diagnosis.

Not many studies have described the pattern of cytokeratin (CK) and P63 expression in cutaneous angiosarcoma. In a study by Linet al., they described the coexpression of CK and p63 in epithelioid angiosarcoma of the parotid gland.[7]Kallenet al. reported that nuclear p63 expression might be seen in 20%–30% of malignant vascular tumors, including AS, epithelioid angiosarcoma, and epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (EHE).[8]The study by Donghiet al. on cutaneous AS found none of their cases to be positive for CK.[9]The study by Al-Abbdi showed one case (3%) positive for CK of 33 cases of AS in various organs.[10]

AS with prominent epithelioid morphology is known to cause confusion with other epithelioid-appearing tumors. These include poorly differentiated carcinoma, melanoma, anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL), epithelioid sarcoma, and other epithelioid vascular tumors such as EHE and rarely pseudomyogenic (epithelioid sarcoma-like) hemangioendothelioma (PHE).[11]EHE typically has a prominent myxochondroid or hyaline stroma, while PHE has spindle cells with deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm sometimes mimicking rhabdomyoblasts, which is not a feature of AS.[12]Proximal-type epithelioid sarcoma can rarely involve the head and neck region of elderly patients and can mimic angiosarcoma. A hemorrhagic angiomatoid variant has been described for epithelioid sarcoma, which can closely simulate angiosarcoma.[13,14]Melanoma cells can show spindling and epithelioid features and the presence of clear cells, signet ring cells, bi- or multinucleation, and erythrocyte phagocytosis, all of which overlap with that of AS. In the present series, two cases were confused with poorly differentiated carcinoma/melanoma and one case with rhabdomyosarcoma. Features of vasoformation, amphophilic grayish cytoplasmic staining, presence of intracytoplasmic luminae, and positivity for vascular endothelial markers along with negativity for epithelial, skeletal muscle, melanoma, or lymphoma markers supported the diagnosis.

Spindle cell predominant AS needs to be distinguished from other cutaneous spindle cell neoplasms. This includes atypical fibroxanthoma, sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma, and sarcomas. A targeted IHC panel must be used and interpreted in the right clinical context.[15]In our series, only one case had an exclusive spindle cell sarcomatous appearance.

Rare morphologic features, which we encountered in the present series, were signet ring cell morphology, prominent cytoplasmic vacuolation, and rhabdoid morphology. Several case reports have described similar unusual histological findings in AS previously.[16,17]

Given the wide differential diagnosis of AS, a high index of suspicion and the application of an appropriate panel of IHC markers that includes CD31, CD34, CK, EMA, S100 protein, Melan A, HMB45, CD30, CD45, and other relevant markers will aid in the diagnosis. CD31 is known to be the most sensitive marker for vascular differentiation. However, 10% of AS can be negative and other nonvascular tumors such as atypical fibroxanthoma can express CD31.[11]Also, the expression of CD31 in intratumoral macrophages and plasma cells of vascular and nonvascular tumors is known.[19]CD34 is less sensitive and specific and stains a variety of soft tissue tumors and hematopoietic neoplasms.[18]Our study showed similar results with 100% of cases being positive for CD31, while only 40% (10/25) were positive for CD34. More recent markers of utility in vascular tumors include ERG and FLI1, and though not 100% specific, they help in identifying AS.[20,21]

AS affecting the head and neck region are rare aggressive vascular tumors characterized by a broad morphologic spectrum raising challenges in morphologic interpretation and diagnosis. Evidence of vasoformation is the sine quo none of these tumors, and a diligent search for their identification is necessary for any superficial tumor in the head and neck area of elderly patients. At the same time, morphology should be interpreted in the right clinical context along with the judicious use of a panel of IHC markers to rule out specific differentials.
