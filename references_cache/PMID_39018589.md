BACKGROUND: Brigatinib is a next-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI)
targeting ALK and ROS1. The Barossa study is a multicenter, phase II basket
study of brigatinib in patients with ROS1-rearranged solid tumors. ROS1
TKI-naive patients with ROS1-rearranged non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) were
enrolled in cohort 1, and ROS1-rearranged NSCLC patients treated previously with
crizotinib were enrolled in cohort 2. Patients with ROS1-rearranged solid tumors
other than NSCLC were enrolled in cohort 3.
PATIENTS AND METHODS: Eligible patients received brigatinib at the dose of 180
mg once daily with a 7-day lead-in period at 90 mg. The primary endpoint was the
objective response rate (RECIST 1.1) assessed by independent central review in
cohorts 1 and 2.
RESULTS: Between July 2019 and June 2021, 51 patients were enrolled into the
study. Of the 51, 47 patients had ROS1-rearranged NSCLC; 28 and 19 of these
patients were enrolled in cohort 1 and cohort 2, respectively. The remaining
four patients had other ROS1-rearranged solid tumors, including rectal, brain,
and pancreas tumor in one patient each, and primary unknown tumor in one
patient. The confirmed objective response rate was 71.4% [95% confidence
interval (CI) 51.3% to 86.8%] in cohort 1 (TKI-naive NSCLC patients) and 31.6%
(95% CI 12.6% to 56.6%) in cohort 2 (NSCLC patients treated previously with
crizotinib). The median progression-free survival was 12.0 months (95% CI
5.5-22.9 months) in cohort 1 and 7.3 months (95% CI 1.3-17.5 months) in cohort
2. None of the patients in cohort 3 showed any treatment response. Pneumonitis
was observed in 9.8% of all the patients.
CONCLUSIONS: Brigatinib was effective in TKI-naive patients with ROS1-rearranged
NSCLC. The safety profile of brigatinib was consistent with that reported from
previous studies.

ROS1 proteins are members of the insulin receptor subfamily and are proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinases involved in cell proliferation and differentiation. ROS1 rearrangements are identified mainly in patients with non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), especially adenocarcinoma. They have been also reported, however, in patients with gastric, colorectal, bile duct, and ovarian cancers, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, and angiosarcoma.1,2 ROS1 rearrangement is estimated to occur in ∼2% of patients with lung adenocarcinoma.3

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) first approved crizotinib for the treatment of advanced NSCLC harboring ALK rearrangement (ALK-rearranged NSCLC) in 2011. An expansion cohort of the phase I study of crizotinib demonstrated an objective response rate (ORR) of 72% and median progression-free survival (PFS) of 19.2 months in patients with advanced NSCLC harboring ROS1 rearrangement (hereinafter, ROS1-rearragend NSCLC),4 which led to crizotinib being approved for ROS1-rearranged NSCLC in 2016. Crizotinib has gastrointestinal toxicities, such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, and shows poor penetration into the central nervous system (CNS), which is the most common site of progressive disease in patients with ALK or ROS1-rearranged NSCLC.5

Brigatinib is a next-generation ALK inhibitor and potentially inhibits ROS1, insulin-like growth factor receptor 1 (IGF-1R), and specific epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutants. A preclinical study demonstrated that brigatinib inhibited ROS1 with potency like that of ALK. Brigatinib had activity against ROS1 with a secondary mutation at the gatekeeper residue (L2026M), although the potency of brigatinib against ROS1 was equivalent to crizotinib.6 A phase III study of brigatinib and crizotinib demonstrated a more prolonged PFS and intracranial PFS in the brigatinib arm compared with the crizotinib arm in patients with ALK-rearranged NSCLC.7 In a previous phase I/II study of brigatinib in patients with NSCLC and other malignancies which included three patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC, one crizotinib-naive patient with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC showed partial response; in the remaining two patients who had previously received crizotinib, one showed stable disease, and the other showed progressive disease.8 The efficacy of brigatinib in ROS1-rearranged solid tumors, however, including NSCLC, has not yet been adequately investigated. Based on this background, we conducted a phase II study (the Barossa study) (JapicCTI-194851) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of brigatinib in patients with ROS1-rearranged solid tumors.

The major eligibility criteria for inclusion in this study were: patients with advanced solid tumors harboring ROS1 rearrangement, aged 20 years or older, and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0-2. Other criteria included adequate organ functions (serum total bilirubin ≤1.5 times the upper limit of normal, serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ≤2.5 times the upper limit of normal (≤5 times the upper limit of normal in case of abnormal hepatic function due to malignant tumor), serum creatinine ≤2 times the upper limit of normal, serum lipase ≤1.5 times the upper limit of normal, peripheral blood neutrophil count ≥1500/μl, platelet count ≥50 000/μl, and hemoglobin ≥9.0 g/dl). Presence of ROS1 rearrangement was confirmed in the tissue or blood sample using RT–PCR or next-generation sequencing validated by central laboratories (LC-SCRUM-Asia or GI/MONSTAR-SCREEN-JAPAN). In cohorts 1 and 2, the diagnosis made by the AmoyDx ROS1 gene fusions detection kit (AmoyDx, Singapore), Oncomine Dx Target Test Multi-CDx system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profiling (Foundation Medicine, Cambridge, MA), or Guardant 360 (Guardant, Palo Alto, CA) was acceptable; in cohort 3, diagnosis made by the AmoyDx ROS1 gene fusion detection kit, gene testing certified by Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments or quality-assured test carried out by a central laboratory was acceptable. ROS1 rearrangement was validated by central laboratory test using another next-generation sequencing, Oncomine Comprehensive Assay (OCA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), when residual tissue sample was available.

The key exclusion criteria were: ALK fusion gene-positive; history of chemotherapy within 2 weeks before enrollment; history of radiotherapy (other than palliative therapy) within 2 weeks before enrollment; surgery within 2 weeks before enrollment; symptomatic brain metastasis; interstitial pneumonia diagnosed by computed tomography (CT) of the chest; and active concomitant malignancy. All patients were required to provide written informed consent before their participation in this study. The protocol was approved by the institutional review board (K0843 at National Cancer Center), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonization guidelines for Good Clinical Practice.

Barossa is a multicenter, phase II basket study carried out in three cohorts. ROS1 inhibitor-naive ROS1-rearranged NSCLC patients were enrolled in cohort 1, and ROS1-rearranged NSCLC patients previously treated with crizotinib were enrolled in cohort 2; patients with ROS 1-rearranged solid tumors other than NSCLC were enrolled in cohort 3 (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642).

Patients received brigatinib 90 mg orally once daily for 1 week, followed by 180 mg orally once daily; each cycle consisted of 28 days. Treatment of the patients was continued until they were detected to have progressive disease, developed unacceptable toxicity, or required dosing interruption for >6 weeks due to poor tolerability or other discontinuation criteria. At the investigator’s discretion, brigatinib treatment could be continued even after disease progression. Dosing interruption or dose reduction were permitted for treatment-related adverse events. Adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. Disease assessment according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 included CT imaging of the chest and abdomen and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain with contrast injection unless it was contraindicated regardless of whether brain metastasis was suspected or not. Assessment was carried out at screening, every 8 weeks through cycle 12, and then every 12 weeks until the end of treatment. If the treatment was discontinued in the patients for any reasons other than disease progression, CT assessment was continued until detection of progression of the underlying disease or initiation of subsequent therapy for the underlying disease. Brain MRI assessment was continued every 12 weeks until intracranial progression was detected, even if subsequent therapy was initiated.

The primary endpoint was the ORR as confirmed by independent central review (ICR) in patients with ROS1-rearreanged NSCLC (cohorts 1 and 2). The secondary endpoints included PFS, overall survival (OS), duration of response, disease control rate (DCR), intracranial PFS, time to intracranial progression, and safety. The exploratory endpoints included ORR in patients with ROS1-rearranged solid tumors other than NSCLC (cohort 3). PFS was defined as the interval from enrollment in the study to establishment of objective evidence of disease progression, or death from any cause. OS was defined as the interval from enrollment in this study to death or the date of the final follow-up visit.

A single-stage binomial design was used for cohorts 1 and 2. For crizotinib-naive ROS1-rearranged NSCLC patients, ORRs of crizotinib, ceritinib, and lorlatinib in crizotinib-naive patients were 71.7%, 67%, and 61.5%, respectively.9, 10, 11 As the aim of cohort 1 was to assess whether response to brigatinib was similar to these agents, the sample size in cohort 1 was determined as 28, with a one-sided alpha of 5%, the power of 80%, and expected and threshold ORRs of 75% and 50%, respectively. According to the LC-SCRUM-Asia database, the ORR of patients with advanced ROS1-rearranged NSCLC to platinum-based chemotherapy was 41% and that to single-agent chemotherapy was 21% (unpublished data). The reported ORR to lorlatinib in patients previously treated with crizotinib was 26.5%.10 Based on these results, the sample size in cohort 2 was determined as 19, with a one-sided alpha of 5%, the power of 80%, and expected and threshold ORRs of 50% and 20%, respectively. The aim of cohort 3 was to assess response to brigatinib in an exploratory manner. Considering the rarity of ROS1-rearrangement in the other solid tumors, target sample size in cohort 3 was set as up to 5.

Efficacy and safety were assessed in all the enrolled patients who had received at least one dose of brigatinib. For centrally assessed ORRs, the 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Clopper and Pearson method. A statistical significance in each cohort was planned to be declared if the lower limit of the 90% CI exceeded the threshold value of centrally assessed ORR. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. We did all analyses with SAS (version 9.4).

Plasma samples were collected at the baseline and at disease progression or study discontinuation. Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from the plasma and the extracted cfDNA was subjected to next-generation sequencing using PGDx elio™ plasma focus (Personal Genome Diagnostics Inc, Baltimore, MD).

A total of 51 patients from 11 institutes in Japan were enrolled in the study between July 2019 and June 2021. Out of these 51 patients, 28, 19, and 4 patients were enrolled into cohort 1 (ROS1 inhibitor-naive NSCLC patients), cohort 2 (crizotinib-pretreated NSCLC patients), and cohort 3 (patients with solid tumors other than NSCLC), respectively (Figure 1). The median age of the patients was 61 years. The majority of the patients were female (57% in total) and had never smoked (59%). Twenty-nine percent and 74% of the ROS1 inhibitor-naive and crizotinib-pretreated NSCLC patients had brain metastasis at the baseline as assessed by ICR (Table 1). The sites of the primary tumor in cohort 3 included the rectum, brain (oligoastrocytoma), and pancreas; the site of primary tumor was unknown in one patient (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642). ROS1 rearrangement was diagnosed using next-generation sequencing in 24 patients, multiple PCR panel in 13 patients, and RT–PCR assay in 39 patients (includes some duplications). Residual tissue sample was available in one of the four patients with solid tumors (cohort 3). ROS1 rearrangement in a patient with rectal cancer was not validated by OCA analysis. A total of 14 patients (74%) required treatment interruption, and 6 patients (32%) required brigatinib dose reduction. The median RDI was 78.7% (range, 41.4% to 100%). Seven patients (37%) received brigatinib treatment even after diagnosis of progressive disease. The confirmed ORR by ICR in the crizotinib-pretreated patients (cohort 2) was 31.6% (90% CI 14.7% to 53.0%; 95% CI 12.6% to 56.6%), with a DCR of 57.9% (95% CI 35.5% to 79.7%) (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642). Six patients had measurable brain metastases; of these, four patients showed partial response, one showed stable disease, and the remaining one patient showed evidence of progressive disease. Therefore, the intracranial ORR was 66.7% (95% CI 22.3% to 95.7%) (Table 2). Eighteen patients progressed as of the data cut-off date. Among these, eight patients showed intracranial progression (brain metastases in seven patients and carcinomatous meningitis in one patient). The median follow-up durations for the PFS and OS were 25.8 and 42.8 months, respectively. The median PFS was 7.3 months (95% CI 1.3-17.5 months) (Figure 2C) and the median intracranial PFS was 9.3 months (95% CI 3.2-17.5 months) (Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642). The 1-year cumulative incidence rate of intracranial progression was 27.6% (95% CI 9.4% to 49.6%) (Supplementary Figure S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642). The median OS was 12.2 months (95% CI 3.4-39.6 months) (Figure 2D).

All four ROS1-rearranged patients with cancers other than NSCLC needed discontinuation of the protocol treatment due to disease progression. The median treatment duration was 72.5 days (range, 29-113 days). None of the four patients showed response to the treatment (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642).

The most common toxicity was elevated serum levels of enzymes, such as creatine phosphokinase (CPK), AST, ALT, amylase, lipase, and alkaline phosphatase, followed by hypertension (Table 3). Pneumonitis was observed in 11.8% (6 of 51) of the patients. Three patients who had not previously received crizotinib developed grade 3 pneumonitis on days 4, 20, and 51, respectively, and the protocol treatment needed to be discontinued in all three. The pneumonitis improved with steroid therapy in all three patients. Two patients developed grade 2 pneumonitis, of whom one patient had not previously received crizotinib (cohort 1) and the third had previously received crizotinib (cohort 2). The protocol treatment needed to be interrupted for 42 days in the previous patient; the patient received steroid therapy and the brigatinib treatment was resumed when the pneumonitis improved. This patient was continuing to receive brigatinib 90 mg as of the data cut-off date. In the latter patient, the protocol treatment needed to be interrupted for 4 weeks. The patient received steroid treatment; however, she developed pneumocystis pneumonia as a complication of steroid therapy. One month later, the brigatinib treatment was resumed, although 2 months later, the pneumonitis flared up, and the protocol treatment had to be discontinued. One patient who had not previously received crizotinib developed grade 1 pneumonitis on day 1203. After 2 weeks of interruption of brigatinib treatment, the pneumonitis resolved spontaneously, without steroid therapy, and the brigatinib treatment was still being continued as on the data cut-off date. Adverse events necessitated dose reduction in 18 patients (36%), dosing interruption in 27 patients (53%), and treatment discontinuation in 6 patients (12%). Plasma samples were collected at the baseline and analyzed in all of the 51 enrolled patients. Of the 51, 10 (19.6%) plasma samples were positive for ROS1 fusion at the baseline. As shown in Supplementary Figure S8A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642, the median PFS of the patients whose plasma samples were positive and negative for ROS1 fusion were 5.4 and 9.3 months, respectively (hazard ratio = 2.38, P = 0.03). The most common concomitant genomic alteration at the baseline was TP53 mutation (n = 11, 21.6%), and the presence of the TP53 mutation tended to be associated with a shorter PFS (median PFS, 6.4 and 9.0 months) (Supplementary Figure S8B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642). The ROS1 G2032R resistance mutation was detected in one NSCLC patient who was pretreated with crizotinib (cohort 2) at the baseline. Of the 51 patients, post-treatment plasma samples were obtained from 42 patients (disease progression, n = 33; study discontinuation, n = 9). Of the 42 samples, additional genomic alterations were found in 11 patients (26.2%) (Supplementary Figure S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103642), including ROS1 G2032R mutation (n = 2), MET amplification (n = 2), and ALK fusion (n = 1).

This was the first study conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of brigatinib in patients with ROS1-rearranged solid tumors, mainly NSCLC. The ORR in the ROS1 inhibitor-naive patients was 71.4% (90% CI 54.3% to 84.9%). The lower limit of the CI of the ORR exceeded the threshold of 50%; therefore, the primary endpoint was met in cohort 1. The median PFS, however, was 12.0 months (95% CI 5.5-22.9 months), which was a little shorter than expected, because previous phase II studies of crizotinib in patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC reported a median PFS in the range of 5.5 to 22.8 months.4,9,12, 13, 14, 15 A phase III study comparing brigatinib and crizotinib in patients with ALK-rearranged NSCLC demonstrated that PFS was significantly longer in the brigatinib arm than in the crizotinib arm [24.0 versus 11.0 months, hazard ratio: 0.49 (95% CI 0.35-0.68)].7 Brigatinib may exert less potent inhibitory activity against ROS1 than against ALK.

The ORR in the crizotinib-pretreated patients, however, was 31.6% (90% CI, 14.7% to 53.0%). The lower limit of the CI of the ORR could not exceed the threshold of 20%. The absolute value of 31.6% itself is promising, when compared with the response rate of 26.5% in the phase II study of lorlatinib in patients previously treated with crizotinib.10 Expectation of an ORR of 50% might have been too high and the number of patients too small. As a result, the number of patients might have been improperly set. The efficacy of brigatinib in patients previously treated with crizotinib warrants further investigation.

ROS1-rearrangement is extremely rare in patients with solid tumors other than NSCLC. Four patients with ROS1-rearranged cancers other than NSCLC were enrolled in our study for 2 years. The sites of the primary tumors were the rectum, brain, and liver, with the primary site unknown (cancer of unknown primary) in the remaining one patient. Unfortunately, no treatment response was observed in any of the patients with ROS1-rearranged solid tumors other than NSCLC. A tissue sample from a patient with rectal cancer was investigated by central laboratory using another next-generation sequencing, OCA; however, ROS1 rearrangement was not confirmed. ROS1 rearrangement was detected using FoundationOne CDx Cancer Genomic Profiling before study enrollment in this patient. This ROS1 rearrangement might have a fusion partner not covered by the OCA. The frequency of ROS1 fusion partner breakdown has been reported to vary depending on the primary cancer site. The most common fusion partners of ROS1 are CD74, EZR, and SDC4 in patients with NSCLC, while the most common ROS1 fusion partner in patients with glioblastoma is GOPC.16 The biological characteristics of ROS1-rearranged solid tumors might differ depending on the primary tumor site, and further investigations are warranted.

At the time our study was initiated, entrectinib had not yet received approval for the treatment of ROS1-rearranged NSCLC. Entrectinib was approved in August 2019 by the FDA and in February 2020 by the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency in Japan. An integrated analysis of three studies on entrectinib in a total of 168 patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC revealed an ORR of 68%, median PFS of 15.7 months, and median OS of 47.8 months. The intracranial ORR was 80% in 25 patients with measurable baseline CNS metastases, and the median intracranial PFS was 8.8 months. The intracranial ORR, however, was 14% in seven patients with measurable CNS-only progression after previous crizotinib treatment.17 Recently, a phase I/II study of repotrectinib, a next-generation ROS1 tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), reported an ORR of 79% and median PFS of 35.7 months (95% CI 27.4 months to not estimated) in ROS1-TKI-naive ROS1 fusion-positive NSCLC patients. The ORR, however, was 38% and the median PFS was 9.0 months (95% CI 6.8-19.6 months) in patients who had previously received one ROS1-TKI but never received any chemotherapy.18 In our study, 90% of the patients had received prior cytotoxic chemotherapy in cohort 2, which consisted of patients who had previously received treatment with crizotinib. The ORR was 31.6% and the median PFS was 7.3 months in the crizotinib-pretreated patients. These data are almost comparable with those reported from the aforementioned repotrectinib study, although it is not known if prior cytotoxic chemotherapy might affect the efficacy of brigatinib.

Pneumonitis was observed in 14% (4/28) patients who had not previously received crizotinib and 5.3% (1/19) patients who had previously received crizotinib. Grade 3 pneumonitis (n = 3) was observed only in ROS1 inhibitor-naive ROS1-rearranged NSCLC patients. A randomized controlled study comparing brigatinib and crizotinib in patients with ALK-positive NSCLC (ALTA-1L) reported the development of pneumonitis in 6% (8/136) patients in the brigatinib arm and 2% (3/137) patients in the crizotinib arm. Grade 3 or 4 pneumonitis occurred in 3% (4/136) and 0.7% (1/137) of patients in the two treatment arms, respectively.7 Although Japanese patients were not enrolled in the ALTA-1L study, any-grade pneumonitis was observed in 7% of Asian patients (4/59), and grade 3/4 pneumonitis in 3% of patients (2/59). A Japanese phase II study of brigatinib (J-ALTA) had two cohorts, an ALK TKI-refractory cohort and a TKI-naive cohort.19 Six of 72 patients (8.3%) who had previously received ALK-TKI developed pneumonitis (grade 1, n = 1; grade 2, n = 4; grade 3, n = 1). A total of 4 of 32 patients (12.5%) who were ALK-TKI-naive developed pneumonitis (grade 1, n = 4).20 The frequencies of pneumonitis in our study cohorts were comparable with those in this previous Japanese study of brigatinib in patients with ALK-rearranged NSCLC.

The detection rate of ROS1 fusion using plasma ctDNA in this study was similar to a previous report,21 suggesting the challenges in implementing the current ctDNA-based analysis for ROS1 fusion screening. The method of PGDx elio plasma focus is DNA-based target capture sequencing using ctDNA, similar to Guardant 360. We previously reported the challenge of fusion detection by ctDNA-based next-generation sequencing (Guardant 360) compared with tissue RNA-based next-generation sequencing, especially ROS1 fusion (positive percent agreement: 18%), which is almost similar to this study. Therefore, low detection rate of ROS1 fusion in this PGDx assay is mainly due to the difficulty of fusion detection by the current available ctDNA-based assay. The spectrum of acquired-resistance genomic alterations detected in this study was consistent with in a previous report,22 and a clinical trial of taletrectinib, a new-generation ROS-TKI is under way (TRUST-II, NCT04919811) to overcome resistance associated with the ROS1 G2032R mutation.23

There were several limitations in our study. First, the sample size of cohort 2, which consisted of crizotinib-pretreated ROS1-rearranged NSCLC patients, was too small to allow reliable evaluation of the activity of brigatinib (n = 19). Second, the sample size of cohort 3, which consisted of patients with ROS1-rearranged solid tumors other than NSCLC, was also small (n = 4). Third, tissue samples were not collected at the time of diagnosis of progressive disease after crizotinib pretreatment (cohort 2) or brigatinib treatment to analyze the mechanisms of development of resistance to crizotinib and brigatinib.

Brigatinib was effective in ROS1 inhibitor-naive patients with ROS1-rearranged NSCLC. Our results failed to suggest any activity of brigatinib in patients with ROS1-rearranged solid tumors other than NSCLC. The safety profile of brigatinib was consistent with that reported from previous studies.
