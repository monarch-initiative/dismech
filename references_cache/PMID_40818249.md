Neuropsychiatric manifestations of systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE) occur in
more than half of patients with SLE, with symptoms including fatigue, anxiety,
depression, pain, psychosis, and cognitive dysfunction. These symptoms are often
present at the time of diagnosis of SLE and can be insidious. Patients consider
these to be among their most debilitating symptoms which most diminish their
quality of life, but recognition and attribution of these frequently non-acute
symptoms can be difficult, resulting in underassessment, underdiagnosis, and
poor understanding of the underlying pathogenic mechanisms and how to treat
them. In this review, we highlight findings from neuroimaging studies which have
been critical in proving the existence of structural and functional brain
alterations that associate with symptomatology, and pathophysiological findings
from animal models of diffuse NPSLE, focusing principally on blood brain barrier
injury and brain-reactive autoantibodies. Most importantly, we demonstrate that
mechanistic progress can be made with respect to these manifestations of NPSLE
and that this progress leads to therapeutic strategies which can be tested in
clinical trials.

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease marked by autoantibody production and inflammation causing significant morbidity and mortality. The disease course can be highly variable; most commonly individuals experience both remission and relapse of symptoms. The etiology of SLE involves genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors that lead to altered function in both the innate and adaptive immune system. Genetic studies include findings of a 30 % concordance for disease among identical twins, and a 20-fold increased probability over the normal population of a first degree relative of a patient with SLE also having SLE [1-3]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified over 100 different susceptibility loci in European, African, and Asian populations [4]. The polymorphisms of risk alleles are primarily found in regulatory regions, which implies an effect on gene expression [3]. Approximately 90 % of patients with SLE are female [5]. While hormones are thought to contribute to the female predominance, recent studies also demonstrate that alterations in the function of Xist, a long non-coding RNA involved in X inactivation in individuals with two X chromosomes, are associated with SLE, with incomplete X inactivation as a risk factor [6,7]. (Fig. 1)

Up to 95 % of patients with SLE experience nervous system-related symptoms during the disease course, termed Neuropsychiatric Lupus (NPSLE) [8]. In 1999 the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) defined 19 syndromes that could be termed NPSLE if no other etiologic explanation was present [9]. These syndromes include peripheral nerve dysfunction, as well as focal and diffuse manifestations within the central nervous system (CNS). Diffuse CNS NPSLE symptoms vary in severity and character and include cognitive impairment, mood disorders, psychosis, depression, and chronic pain and fatigue. Pain, fatigue, depression, and cognitive dysfunction are reported by patients as the symptoms of SLE that most diminish quality of life [10-12]. While there is a high prevalence of NPSLE symptoms, brain pathology can also be subclinical. Neuroimaging of patients with SLE often shows abnormalities without concurrent NPSLE diagnosis or even notable impairment on cognitive or behavioral assessments [13,14].

Interestingly, most of the risk alleles associated with SLE do not specifically indicate risk for NPSLE. In one study, polymorphisms in TREX1 (DNASE III), a 3 ′ repair exonuclease, associated with NPSLE, although the particular reason for this association is not clear [15]. A meta-analysis looking for potential NPSLE susceptibility genes identified polymorphisms in pathways of immune complex engagement, such as FcgRIIIa, FcgRIIIb, and integrin alpha M genotypes [16]. It is unknown whether there are other genetic associations with NPSLE, whether any risk alleles associate with specific NPSLE syndromes, and whether any polymorphisms might affect tissue susceptibility rather than immune system activation. Etiologic mechanisms are not thought to originate within the CNS but rather to be secondary to systemic abnormalities such as autoantibodies that target the CNS, cytokines, and other soluble mediators. Cognitive dysfunction is one of the most prevalent NPSLE symptoms and notably does not correlate with disease activity [17,18], indicating a chronic neuropathology that is presumed to be triggered by systemic immune abnormalities, but once triggered is independent of peripheral disease activity.

The purpose of this review is to highlight mechanistic insights underlying diffuse CNS symptoms of NPSLE, focusing primarily on brain-reactive autoantibodies as a causative agent for cognitive impairment, mood disorders, and fatigue and to consider potential therapeutic avenues for improving patient quality of life.

Diffuse CNS complaints were often minimized by clinicians or thought not to be attributable to SLE. Advanced neuroimaging techniques offer objective quantifications of CNS abnormalities which assist in identifying physiological correlates to symptoms and may provide insight into their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms (Table 1). Recent advances in the breadth and resolution in neuroimaging allow for associations between regions of brain abnormality and impairments in specific domains of brain function.

Conventional magnetic resonance imaging can be used to identify structural and volumetric changes, indicating injury or cerebral atrophy. Decreased hippocampal and amygdala volume have been found in patients with SLE; hippocampal atrophy correlated with anti-NMDAR antibody titer and severity of cognitive dysfunction [1]. Functional MRI (fMRI) evaluates regional brain function based on blood flow and oxygen uptake and can be used to assess the brain at both resting state and during the performance of specific cognitive tasks. Patients with SLE display altered functional connectivity even at rest in multiple regions, including the hippocampus, which correlate with both clinical disease severity and cognitive impairment [49-51,36]. During task-based fMRI, patients with SLE display impairments in attenuation of the default mode network and in sustained attention, as well as recruitment of additional atypical neuronal pathways to complete cognitive tasks [11,50,57-59]. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) quantifies neuronal metabolites such as N-acetylaspartate (NAA, a marker of neurons). Patients with NPSLE display focal decreases in NAA that may be transient or chronic, indicating neuronal loss or damage [28,47]. Positron Emission Tomography (PET) assesses regional brain metabolism and neuronal function based on glucose uptake and has shown resting hypermetabolism in several areas which associates with poorer performance in cognitive testing in individuals with SLE [68,29,69]. Several techniques can assess blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity; impaired integrity can be seen in SLE, often in the hippocampus [36-38]. These techniques connect neuropsychiatric manifestations with structural and functional CNS alterations and all demonstrate abnormalities in patients. None are specific for SLE and none define specific manifestations of NPSLE.

There have been many studies of brain dysfunction in mouse models of SLE. A large proportion of these have focused on the MRL/lpr model which has numerous important differences from the human disease. These mice, in particular, have an abnormal brain from the earliest time of investigation, perhaps due to the activation of the maternal immune system that is present during gestation. Thus, it is critical to test murine models against data derived from human patients. For further descriptions of different murine models and their caveats and considerations, please reference Table 2.

Cognitive dysfunction is exhibited by many patients with SLE, at frequencies ranging from 15 % to 79 % [118]. The variability in reported prevalence is likely due to different definitions of impairment and different study populations [118]. Symptoms of cognitive dysfunction are often subtle and do not necessarily correspond with peripheral disease activity, complicating recognition and attribution to SLE and resulting in underassessment and underdiagnosis [119].

The ACR defines cognitive dysfunction in SLE as a significant deficit in any or all cognitive domains, which include simple or complex attention, reasoning, executive skills, memory, visual-spatial processing, language, and psychomotor speed [9]. Learning, memory, and attention have been found to be the domains most frequently affected [120]. Assessments of cognitive dysfunction can include neuropsychological battery tests such as the ACR neuropsychological battery (ACR-NB), Modified Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE), Automated Neuropsychological Assessment Metric (ANAM), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), as well as self-reported cognitive questionnaires such as the Cognitive Symptoms Inventory (CSI) and the Perceived Deficits Questionnaire (PDQ) [120] (Table 3). The ACR-NB is considered to be the gold standard, but its high administrative burden and time requirement limit its clinical use [118]. Self-assessments have been found to correlate poorly with objective testing, and frequently reflect patients’ concurrent symptoms of depression, anxiety, fatigue, and pain, which make self-assessments unreliable [118,121]. Patients frequently describe cognitive dysfunction as “brain fog,” and cite difficulties concentrating, keeping up with conversations, multitasking, planning, finding words, and remembering the location of objects or travel routes [23,122].

Psychiatric symptoms are described at higher rates in individuals with SLE compared to the general population [148], yet causative factors have been historically understudied, with many in the field attributing elevated anxiety and depression in patients to difficulties coping with chronic disease. A review of clinical studies containing a minimum sample size of 20 patients found that psychiatric symptoms occur in a majority of patients [149]. Up to 39 % of patients with SLE experience depression and up to 24 % experience anxiety [149]. In a resting-state fMRI study comparing 32 patients to 18 healthy controls, anxiety was linked to faster hemodynamic responses in the right amygdala and reduced prefrontal connectivity, while depression showed altered ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) dynamics and decreased intrinsic connectivity in the medial orbitofrontal cortex [150]. A one-year longitudinal study of 65 patients with SLE revealed that anxiety symptoms tend to worsen over time independent of disease activity. While antidepressants had little effect on symptom progression, anxiolytics improved depression outcomes, and treatment with methotrexate or plaquenil significantly reduced both depression and anxiety symptoms [151]. In patients with SLE without major neuropsychiatric manifestations, mild depression and anxiety were highly prevalent [152]. A cross-sectional study similarly found that depression and anxiety are more common among individuals with SLE with more severe disease activity [153]. It must be noted that determination of prevalence and severity of psychiatric symptoms relies heavily on the types of assessments used (summarized in Table 3).

Acute psychosis in patients with SLE is comparatively rare, with an incidence of 1.5–2.5 % [154-156]. Psychosis in patients with SLE typically occurs within 3 years of SLE diagnosis, and is associated with young age of diagnosis, male sex, African ancestry, and prior NPSLE events [157]. Psychosis is often associated with global SLE disease activity.

Fatigue is one of the most frequent symptoms of SLE; 67–90 % of patients report experiencing abnormal fatigue and over half of patients consider fatigue their most debilitating symptom [1]. Similar to many other manifestations of NPSLE, the attribution of fatigue is difficult. The fatigue reported by patients with chronic disease is described as differing from the ‘usual’ fatigue in severity, lack of clear cause, lack of resolution with rest, unpredictability, and as being ‘profound’ and ‘overwhelming’ [121,122]. There are several questionaries and scores that measure fatigue (Table 3), but no clear gold standard exists [121]. Fatigue is a hallmark of depression, another common symptom of patients with NPSLE. While patients report experiencing fatigue without depression and vice versa, they frequently co-occur [158]. An understanding of fatigue attributable to direct effects of SLE is critical as it might well require different treatment from fatigue derived from other sources, such as from effects of medication [159].

Inflammation is likely a common cause of fatigue in SLE. SLE patients have reported that fatigue often relapses and remits with other SLE symptoms [121,159]. Fatigue scores have been reported to be increased in patients with active SLE compared to patients with inactive disease [159,160]. A plausible mechanism for this is the sickness behavior paradigm, in which inflammatory cytokines, especially Type I IFN, IL-1b, and TNF, induce fatigue, anhedonia, social withdrawal, and depression [161]. Mice administered IL-1b or TNF exhibit social withdrawal, decreased food and water intake and decreased physical activity, as well as alterations in sleep, diurnal rhythms, pain sensitivity, and cognition [161]. While circulating levels of proinflammatory cytokines are higher in patients with SLE than in healthy controls, higher levels do not necessarily correspond with increased fatigue [121], and disease remission does not always associate with an absence of fatigue [162]. Trials of immunomodulatory drugs exhibit mixed results on fatigue; abatacept, which inhibits T cell costimulation, reduced fatigue at 12 months [163], while infliximab, an anti-TNF mAb, improved fatigue but did not reach clinical significance by Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) measures [164]. The B cell activating factor (BAFF) antagonist tabalumab did not reduce fatigue, though the BAFF antagonist Blisibimod did [165,166]. Clinical trials of belimumab, another anti-BAFF agent, found improvements in physical component scores of fatigue in the BLISS-52 trial in participants receiving 10 mg/kg belimumab compared to placebo, but not in the BLISS-76 trial at 52 weeks in which patients received the same dose of belimumab [167]. Schwarting et al. (2019) found in their study of 426 patients with SLE that fatigue severity correlated with anti-NMDAR antibody titers; interestingly, a subset of these patients (82) treated with belimumab had decreased anti-NMDAR titers and decreased fatigue scores at 6 + months following treatment [22].

Fatigue in SLE may also be related to poor sleep [168]. Patients with SLE experiencing insomnia exhibited significantly higher levels of perceived stress, higher rates of neuropsychiatric symptoms, and even flares of systemic disease [169,170]. NZB/W lupus-prone mice subjected to sleep deprivation developed earlier onset disease [171]. Interestingly, in a different study of sleep deprivation in mice, chronic sleep restriction was associated with decreased expression of tight junction proteins including claudin-5, ZO-2, and occludin and loss of BBB integrity [172]. Microglia display an activated morphology and increased inflammatory markers including increased production of complement component C3 with sleep loss [173]. Several days of sleep restriction in mice induced increased synaptic pruning of neurons by astrocytes and microglia [174]. Studies of sleep deprivation in other animal models have shown reduced synaptic plasticity and decreased dendritic complexity in the hippocampus, resulting in memory impairments and anxiety behaviors [175]. These studies of patients and murine models suggest that poor sleep is both a consequence of the systemic disease and a contributor to CNS manifestations of disease, including fatigue and cognitive dysfunction.

The BBB is a specialized vascular barrier which protects the brain by limiting the access of substances from the peripheral circulation to the brain parenchyma [176], including blood-borne pathogens as well as peripheral immune cells and soluble substances that can be damaging to the CNS [177]. The BBB is comprised of endothelial cells with intercellular junctions characterized by tight junction and adherens junction proteins, distinct receptors and transporter proteins, and an absence of cellular fenestrations [177,178]. The endothelium is surrounded by pericytes which regulate cerebral blood flow, and astrocytes have end-feet that encircle endothelial cells and release effector molecules which maintain barrier function [177,178].

Disruption of the BBB is frequently implicated in NPSLE development. The presence of serum components in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), such as albumin, is frequently used as a marker of BBB permeability, as albumin is not produced in the CNS and an intact BBB does not permit its entrance into the CSF [179]. CSF levels of albumin as well as haptoglobin, beta-2 microglobulin, and alpha-2 macroglobulin have been shown to be significantly increased in patients with NPSLE [179]. Many studies indicate that BBB permeability allowing brain-reactive autoantibodies into the CNS is a key event in initiating neuropsychiatric involvement. As BBB compromise is required for antibodies to access brain parenchyma, the association of BBB dysfunction and NPSLE symptoms suggest that antibodies may play a causative role. Numerous mediators of decreased BBB integrity (Table 4) have been identified and associated with increased NPSLE symptoms.

The presence of autoantibodies in the CSF including anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor 2 (NR2), anti-ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1), anti-microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2), and anti-suprabasin (SBSN) has also been found to associate with NPSLE [196]. Conversely, serum increases of proteins such as neurofilament light (NfL), a neuronal protein, S100B, produced primarily by astrocytes, are also used as indicators of BBB permeability [196]. Serum NfL, an indication of neuronal damage, is increased in NPSLE, and correlates with increased total CSF volume, indicating cerebral atrophy [196]. A number of autoantibodies, including anti-phospholipid antibodies (aPL), have been suggested to alter brain endothelial cell junctions (Table 4).

Complement component C5a, also elevated in active disease, has also been shown to cause BBB permeability in an in vitro model and endothelial cell apoptosis in vivo in MRL/lpr lupus-prone mice [197]. Matrix metalloproteinases, which are upregulated by proinflammatory cytokines and degrade extracellular matrix proteins, contribute to BBB permeability. Their presence is increased in the serum of patients with NPSLE and is associated with cognitive dysfunction and abnormalities on MRI [196]. TNF-like weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK), a TNF family cytokine which binds the receptor Fn14 expressed on fibroblasts and endothelial cells, has been shown in the MRL/lpr model of NPSLE to increase CSF IgG and albumin [198]. TWEAK was also shown to induce IL-6, IL-8, and ICAM-1 expression in human endothelial cells in vitro [199]. While many mediators of BBB disruption facilitate the entry of soluble molecules into the brain, some permit cellular infiltration. Immune complexes, which are elevated in the plasma of patients with SLE, can activate the NF-KB pathway in endothelial cells and induce cytokine production and adhesion molecule expression to facilitate infiltration of immune cells [200].

BBB disruptions may also originate from within the CNS. Serum S100B is elevated in SLE and more so in NPSLE, with higher levels associated with cognitive dysfunction [179,196]. Overproduction of S100B has been shown to promote BBB permeability as well as neuronal loss [154]. The acute-phase reactant protein lipocalin-2 (LCN2), which is produced at basal levels by neurons, microglia, astrocytes, and endothelial cells, is upregulated in neuroinflammation, and has been shown to be upregulated in the CSF of patients with SLE as well as in patients with depression and memory impairments [93]. LCN2 is able to bind to endothelial cells to induce activation and tight junction disruption leading to BBB permeability [93]. LCN2 deficiency has no impact on systemic manifestations of SLE including autoantibody titers, splenomegaly, and renal pathology; thus, it has a CNS autonomous role in modulating neuropsychiatric symptoms [93].

Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) is used to assess BBB function by measuring contrast leakage into the brain. Studies of patients with SLE without a history of NPSLE demonstrate increased BBB permeability compared to healthy individuals, especially in the hippocampus [38]. Extensive damage to BBB integrity has been associated with decreased cerebral grey matter volume and decreased cognition scores [37].

While CSF measures of increased BBB permeability give no information as to the region of disruption, different mediators can cause regional changes in permeability leading to specific NP manifestations. Linking specific inducers of BBB damage and DCE-MRI findings to specific NP symptoms should allow for a better mechanistic understanding of the molecular and cellular underpinnings of these manifestations and allow for more targeted diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Numerous brain-reactive antibodies have been reported in patients with SLE (Table 4). We describe two in detail to illustrate that mechanisms of autoantibody-mediated brain injury can be determined and that many pathways may be involved.

Autoantibodies that bind the excitatory neuronal NMDAR, termed anti-NMDARs, associate with NPSLE manifestations. One of the more widely studied anti-NMDAR antibodies binds GluN2A/B and cross-reacts with dsDNA (DNRAb: dsDNA- and NMDAR-Reactive Antibody). These antibodies are found in approximately 30 % of patients with SLE, and in a higher percentage of patients with NPSLE and function as positive allosteric modulators of NMDAR activation [201,109,202]. The presence of DNRAbs in the CSF is associated with diffuse CNS manifestations [201,203].

DNRAb-containing SLE serum injected intravenously into mice followed by lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced BBB breach leads to neuronal injury in the hippocampus with corresponding spatial memory impairment, while the same serum selectively depleted of NMDAR reactivity showed no hippocampal damage or behavioral impairment [204].

Our laboratory identified the binding specificity of these antibodies as a consensus sequence DWEYS, present in GluN2A and GluN2B, DWEYS in GluN2A and EWDYG in GluN2B [109]. Non-spontaneously autoimmune mice immunized with the DWEYS peptide multimerized on a polylysine backbone produce DNRAbs (DNRAb+ mice), while mice immunized with just the polylysine backbone, do not (DNRAb-). DNRAbs in peripheral circulation do not access the CNS without a BBB breach; the region of permeability impacts the antibody-dependent phenotype. For example, when LPS is administered to mice, antibody is found selectively in the hippocampus and DNRAb+ mice display impaired cognition, specifically deficits in spatial memory [203]. Alternatively, when epinephrine is administered to mice, antibody is found in the lateral amygdala and DNRAb+ mice exhibit an alteration in fear learning behavior [205]. Thus, the same autoantibody can have different behavioral consequences depending on the region of BBB permeability- a concept with significant implications for understanding diverse symptoms present in patients with NPSLE.

Deeper investigation into the DNRAb-dependent hippocampal phenotype in mice reveals an acute and a chronic self-sustaining phase of antibody-mediated damage. Interestingly, this mirrors the complexity of CNS disease in patients, in which chronic neuropsychiatric symptoms do not necessarily resolve at the end of a flare and their presence does not correlate with disease activity. The acute phase, which terminates at one week post-LPS administration, is characterized by dose-dependent DNRAb-mediated excitotoxicity leading to death in pyramidal neurons in the hippocampus [206]. The chronic phase, evident at 8 weeks and lasting at least 1-year following LPS injection, is characterized by decreased dendritic arborization in surviving pyramidal neurons of CA1 region of the hippocampus and microglial activation assessed by both morphology and transcriptional profiling [204,207-210]. This phenotype is established by neuronal secretion of high molecular group box 1 (HMGB1), a chromatin protein that can act as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) when in the extracellular space. HMGB1 from the neurons binds both the GluN2B subunit of the NMDAR and the complement component C1q to form a molecular bridge that targets synapses for elimination by microglia. HMGB1 also activates microglia through RAGE-signaling [209,210]. The chronic phase of antibody-mediated damage in the hippocampus associates with impaired spatial memory in mice. Patients with SLE with serum titers of DNRAbs and cognitive impairment have a spatial memory deficit, supporting the validity of this mouse model [203].

DNRAb-mediated amygdala damage after epinephrine also shows acute neuronal apoptosis with abnormal neuronal morphology and altered fear memory [205]. Notably, patients with SLE and DNRAb titers have increased damage in the amygdala by calculated apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) on MRI [14]. This again validates the use of this mouse model to study aspects of NPSLE. Further investigation into the chronic phase of DNRAb-mediated amygdala damage may provide insight into mechanisms underlying mood and anxiety disorders which are experienced by patients with NPSLE and DNRAb.

NPSLE is not the sole example of NMDAR-mediated autoimmune psychiatric syndromes. Anti-NMDAR receptor encephalitis was first described in 2007 as a paraneoplastic syndrome associated with ovarian teratoma, but need not be paraneoplastic [211]. Characterized by production of IgG autoantibodies that target the GluN1 subunit of NMDARs, anti-NMDAR encephalitis typically leads to psychiatric symptoms like psychosis and agitation [212-215]. Mechanistic in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that antibody binding results in internalization of the receptor without changing the number of synapses, dendritic structure, or overall survival of the cell [216]. These GluN1-binding antibodies are not present in patients with NPSLE, suggesting that the NMDAR is not the immunogen in SLE.

Appreciating the significant burden that cognitive impairment poses on patients with NPSLE, we have investigated therapeutic solutions to counteract inflammatory mechanisms identified in the DNRAb-mediated murine model of NPSLE. Angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors have been identified as effective in both preventing and treating the DNRAb-mediated hippocampal phenotype. The renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS) regulates sodium and water resorption and vascular tone. Within this system, ACE is involved in two separate pathways: (1) ACE catalyzes the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II and (2) ACE facilitates the breakdown of bradykinin. ACE inhibitors, a class of FDA-approved medications used for hypertension, decrease angiotensin II and downstream aldosterone production and increase levels of bradykinin. RAAS also has functions unrelated to blood pressure and vascular homeostasis, namely in the CNS, where angiotensin II and bradykinin are purported to have a role in modifying microglial function. AT1R, a receptor for angiotensin II, is a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed on neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and microglia in the cortex, hippocampus, and basal ganglia in both animal and human brain tissue. AT1R activation has been shown to play a role in microglial activation through regulation of NAPDH oxidase activity [217]. Interestingly, ACE is expressed in the CNS, including on pyramidal neurons of the hippocampus [218]. Importantly, an ACE polymorphism associated with elevated serum ACE is an SLE risk allele [219-221]. ACE inhibition in DNRAb+ mice leads to a reduction in microglial activation, restoration of neuronal dendritic arborization, and improvement of spatial memory [222]. In the DNRAb-mediated model of NPSLE, telmisartan, an angiotensin-receptor blocker (ARB) that crosses the BBB and inhibits angiotensin II binding to AT1R, can replicate the effects of ACE inhibition. The success of these therapeutic interventions shows that altered intercellular interactions and not loss of neurons compromise cognitive function. An alternate mechanism for ACE inhibitor mediated neuroprotection is the degradation of bradykinin, a peptide in the kallikrein-kinin system. Bradykinin has been shown to have anti-inflammatory effects in cultured microglia through bradykinin receptor B1 signaling [223].

While a retrospective, observational study did not find that ACEi/ ARB use was associated with the absence of cognitive dysfunction, the study had many limitations including the fact that patients were primarily prescribed ACEi/ARBs for hypertension, which itself is associated with irreversible cognitive dysfunction [224]. Randomized controlled trials are needed to properly evaluate the effect of ACEi/ARBs in neuroinflammation-driven cognitive dysfunction in NPSLE.

One of the first reports of autoantibodies that bind ribosomes in serum of patients with SLE was published in 1965 by Sturgill and Carpenter [225]. The next several decades of work identified anti-ribosomal P (anti-P) antibodies in patients with SLE that react with P antigen, found intracellularly in the ribosome. A literature review and metanalysis of anti-P antibodies in SLE showed an overall prevalence of 10–47 % [182]. Anti-P antibodies associate with diffuse CNS NPSLE, specifically with depression, and psychosis [182]. Interestingly, both anti-P and DNRAbs correlate with cognitive dysfunction, but each antibody associates with a distinct impairment: anti-P with deficits in attention and DNRAbs with deficits in spatial planning [226].

The cellular target of anti-P antibodies is an 11 amino acid sequence in the C-terminal domain of phosphoproteins P0, P1, and P2 of the large ribosomal subunit [227]. In addition to intracellular ribosomal protein binding, anti-P antibodies have been shown to cross react with a neuronal membrane antigen, neuronal surface P antigen (NSPA). NSPA is an integral membrane protein expressed exclusively by neurons in areas of the brain relating to cognition, emotion, and memory like the neocortex, hippocampus, bed nucleus of the stria terminalis, and amygdala. NSPA is involved in NMDAR and AMPAR receptor stability. It is essential for ubiquitination of the tyrosine phosphatase PTPMEG, which at high levels leads to decreased GluN2A and GluN2B subunits at post-synaptic densities [183]. NSPA-deficient mice, like DNRAb+ mice with hippocampal lesions, have impaired spatial memory but also impaired recognition memory [228]. Anti-P antibodies purified from serum induce neuronal apoptosis in vivo and in vitro, and accordingly allow calcium entry into cortical neurons in culture [229], reflecting a similar immediate excitotoxicity of penetration into the brain as is seen with DNRAb.

IV injection of patient-derived anti-P antibodies followed by LPS administration in mice leads to memory impairment assessed by Morris water-maze task [230]. Similarly, mice immunized with ribosomal P epitope, to produce anti-NSPA antibodies exhibit impaired memory performance after a breach of BBB integrity [230]. Intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection of affinity-purified anti-P antibodies from patient sera induced depression-like behavior in mice [231]. These ICV-injected antibodies were found in the piriform cortex, hippocampus, and cingulate cortex of the mouse brains [231], again suggesting that the region of antibody penetration determines the behavioral or cognitive phenotype.

Targeting of NSPA in multiple regions of the brain results in spatial memory impairment and depression-like behaviors in mice. While direct mechanisms for the role of anti-P antibodies in psychosis have not been demonstrated due to the difficult nature of studying psychosis in animal models, it is plausible that similar mechanisms of NSPA modulation are at play in a different circuit network. Additionally, functional alterations in areas of cognitive and emotional regulation on rsFMRI were found in patients with SLE with anti-P antibodies [232].

It was tempting to hypothesize that one type of antibody would associate with one type of symptom in NPSLE, but both DNRAbs and anti-P antibodies have been shown to cause more than one NPSLE symptom- including cognitive impairment, altered anxiety, and depression. Both antibodies target antigens that are widely distributed on neurons in the CNS, and therefore have potential to impact an enormous range of behaviors. The antibody-mediated symptoms then are at least partially determined by the region of antibody penetration, which circuits they disrupt, and the factors that permit region-specific permeability. This paradigm now explains why some studies find no association between anti-P or DNRAbs and NPSLE and cognitive impairment because a significant BBB permeability event is necessary for antibody-mediated CNS pathology [233]. It is important to note again that both of these antibodies have brain reactivity and consequent pathology due to cross-reactivity from their initial peripheral immunogen, and that the brain antigens here (NR2 subunits and NSPA) are not the immunogen.

Patients with SLE often have multiple autoantibodies with a variety of antigenic specificities. It is possible that some patients may have multiple anti-brain antibodies, and that multiple pathways downstream of antibody binding can be affected. Without direct investigation, it is difficult to predict whether the effects of two distinct antibodies would lead to synergistic, antagonistic, or combinatorial effects on brain function.

A focus on antibodies as causative agents of NPSLE suggests a therapeutic potential of B cell depletion. In early clinical studies, CD-19-targeting CAR T cell therapy resulted in disease remission, normalization of serological markers, and improvement in organ function for patients with severe SLE, though neuropsychiatric involvement was not assessed [234-236]. Notably, CAAR T cells engineered to recognize NMDAR-specific B cells have demonstrated selective elimination of these pathogenic cells and a sustained decrease in anti-NMDAR autoantibody levels in preclinical models of NMDAR encephalitis, paving the way for targeted therapies that could minimize off-target effects and relapses in NPSLE [237]. This precision immunotherapy represents a novel and potentially transformative strategy for patients with autoantibody-mediated neuropsychiatric lupus.

While much of the pathogenesis and resulting clinical course of NPSLE can likely be directly attributed to autoantibody effects, other mediators like cytokines, chemokines, and metabolites have all been linked to NPSLE.

Cytokines encompass a broad category of small proteins secreted by a wide variety of cells involved in pathways of the innate and adaptive immune system. Dysregulation of cytokines has been described in NPSLE, where increased cytokine levels have been observed in the CSF of patients with NPSLE [238,239]. Cytokines access the CNS through BBB transport or local production in the CNS, where they can be synthesized or secreted by neurons, glia, endothelial cells, and choroid plexus cells [240].

Type I interferons (IFNs) include IFNα and IFNβ and are typically associated with immune responses to viral infections. Increases in Type I IFNs and IFN-induced genes have been widely implicated in systemic aspects of SLE pathophysiology. They likely contribute to NPSLE as well. The impact of type I IFN on the brain can be seen in Type I interfer-onopathies, caused by mutations that increase IFN production or amplify its signaling. These inflammatory diseases are often associated with neuroinflammation and cognitive dysfunction [241]. The use of recombinant IFN as a therapy for hepatitis C and some cancers have provided further insight into the role of IFN in neuropsychiatric disease [242]. A subset of patients who receive IFN therapy develop autoimmune diseases like SLE [241]. More commonly, IFN treatment can cause cognitive deficits like memory loss and lack of concentration, or psychiatric symptoms like anxiety, depression, mania, and psychoses [243, 244]. Several studies demonstrate abnormalities on EEG and MRI in patients who have received IFN therapy [245-247]. These problems do not always resolve after cessation of therapy. Patients with NPSLE can have increased IFNa in the CSF and blood or alternatively substances such as nucleic acid-containing immune complexes that induce IFN production in cells ex vivo [239,248].

One possible mechanism of type I IFN-induced cognitive dysfunction in patients with NPSLE is the involvement of IFN in the kynurenine/tryptophan (KYN/TRP) metabolic pathway. IFN induces the enzyme indoleamine 2,3 dioxygenase (IDO) which converts TRP into KYN, which is further metabolized into quinolinic acid (QA) or kynurenic acid (KA). High interferon-stimulated gene expression in blood cells associates with elevated KYN/TRP ratios in patients with SLE [249]. QA and KA have opposing effects on neurons within the brain: QA acts as an NMDAR agonist and induces excitotoxicity, while KA acts as an NMDAR antagonist and has a protective role against excitotoxicity.

The ratio between QA and KA is posited to affect neurocognitive abilities, with higher QA/KA ratios associating with neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity [250]. Elevated QA/KA ratios are also associated with depression and suicidal ideation as well as cognitive dysfunction in patients receiving IFNa treatment, as well as cognitive dysfunction [251-256]. Levels of QA in the CSF of patients with NPSLE are notably higher than levels of QA in patients with neuropsychiatric dysfunction of other etiologies or healthy controls [257]. In patients with SLE, QA associates with impaired cognitive performance [258]. Patients with SLE are also reported to have decreased serotonin levels, which may also relate to increased IDO activity [256].

Interleukin-6 (IL-6), a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is presumed to play a role in acute NPSLE. Serum and CSF levels of IL-6 are elevated in patients with NPSLE and correlate with disease activity [248,259,260] Presence of IL-6 in the CSF can be attributed to either its direct penetration of the BBB or its production within the CNS, where notably IL-6 mRNA is upregulated in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex of patients with NPSLE [261]. IL-6 production can also occur in neurons through prolonged NMDAR activation, which in SLE may be a consequence of DNRAb or elevated QA levels [262,263]. Interestingly, knocking out IL-6 in the MRL/lpr mouse model of NPSLE ameliorates memory deficits, although the cellular and molecular basis for improvement is yet incompletely understood [264].

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) is a pro-inflammatory cytokine with significant impact in autoimmune diseases. TNF in the CNS is predominantly produced by microglia, but also by neurons and astrocytes [265]. TNF can exacerbate glutamate excitotoxicity in neurons through several mechanisms: reduction of glutamate uptake in astrocytes, upregulation of AMPA and NMDA receptors on neurons, and decrease in protective and inhibitory GABA receptors [265]. Thus, TNF can contribute to neuronal loss and neuronal secretion of HMGB1.

Chemokines are a group of small proteins that interact with G-protein coupled receptors on leukocytes, promoting leukocyte movement from the circulation into tissue during injury [266]. While typically below levels of detection in CNS, their expression can increase significantly during neuroinflammation. In the context of NPSLE, the most extensively studied chemokines are CXCL10 and CCL2.

CXCL10 and CCL2 are both produced in the CNS by glia and neurons. Both chemokines are elevated in the CSF of patients with NPSLE [266]. Further research into consequences of these chemokines in the CNS and the cell types recruited by their presence is needed.

Recent advances have suggested a role for T cells in the MRL/lpr mouse model of NPSLE [267]. T follicular helper (Tfh) cells in this model infiltrate the choroid plexus (ChP) and brain parenchyma in MRL/lpr mice, where they secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-21 [268]. How well the MRL/lpr model reflects the human disease is not known. In general, the chronic symptoms of NPSLE are not associated with increased cells in the cerebrospinal fluid or brain parenchyma of patients, although the data on ChP is limited.

NPSLE presents a complex clinical course characterized by fluctuating symptoms that, despite periods of remission, often progressively worsen over time. This symptomatology is likely often mediated by pathogenic antibodies whose entry into the CNS is contingent upon a breach in the BBB, along with production of inflammatory cytokines and disruptions in key metabolic indices such as the KA/QA balance. Once antibodies penetrate the CNS, they can initiate long-term neuroinflammation and maladaptive homeostatic changes that may contribute to sustained neural dysfunction, independent of systemic disease activity.

The majority of NPSLE-associated autoantibodies appear to target neurons specifically. This neuron-specific targeting may be a consequence of the BBB that restricts peripheral exposure to neuronal antigens, thus allowing neuron-reactive B cells to escape tolerance checkpoints. In contrast, antigens expressed by glial cells such as microglia are similar to those on macrophages, and B cells reactive to these antigens are potentially subject to immune tolerance mechanisms. Most anti-brain antibodies still lack a clearly defined mechanistic link to clinical symptoms or neuropathological changes. Interestingly, evidence from neuroimaging studies indicates that significant brain abnormalities can exist even in patients with SLE who have not been diagnosed with NPSLE. This implies that subclinical brain pathology may be more common than currently appreciated. Several factors may contribute to this under-recognition, including the limitations of current diagnostic tools and the frequent misattribution of psychiatric symptoms such as depression and anxiety to idiopathic psychiatric disorders rather than to SLE pathophysiology. Advanced neuroimaging analyses reveal that compensatory neural network reorganization may preserve cognitive performance in affected individuals, effectively masking underlying functional brain impairments during routine neuropsychological assessments. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating CNS integrity in SLE patients even during quiescent phases of the disease.

Experimental data from animal models of NPSLE suggest that early or ongoing brain pathology may be responsive to treatment, reinforcing the need for proactive neuropsychiatric assessment and intervention. Promising therapeutic strategies aimed at dampening neuroinflammation—such as modulation of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) activity, purinergic signaling, and ATP receptor pathways—highlight the potential for targeting specific inflammatory cascades to alleviate symptoms and reverse neural injury or functional impairment in NPSLE. In humans, causality of pathologic agents in SLE and downstream pathogenesis can only be determined in clinical trials, which are informed by similar states from different etiologies. Short of clinical trials, it is immensely useful to collect CSF alongside multiple modalities of neuroimaging, like PET, DCE-MRI, and rsFMRI. This permits a more comprehensive view of what is occurring in the brains of these patients and is the best approach to causality with our current tools. Together, these insights advocate for a reevaluation of how NPSLE is diagnosed, monitored, and treated. Greater emphasis should be placed on identifying subclinical neuroinflammatory damage, improving diagnostic sensitivity, developing better animal models, and implementing targeted immunomodulatory therapies to mitigate long-term neurological sequelae in affected patients.
