# Advanced modelling of RYR1-related myopathies using human iPS cells and 3D engineered skeletal muscles
**Authors:** Lucia Rossi, SungWoo Choi, Isobel Terri Olden, Aude Biehler, Lyn Healy, Francesco Muntoni, Giovanni Baranello, Anna Sarkozy, Valentina Maria Lionello, Francesco Saverio Tedesco

Abstract

                  Background
 Ryanodine receptor type 1-related myopathies (RYR1-RM) represent a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of congenital skeletal muscle disorders caused by pathogenic variants in the ryanodine receptor type 1 (
                  RYR1
                  ) gene, which encodes a calcium channel critical for skeletal muscle excitation-contraction coupling. Dysregulated calcium handling is a central feature of these conditions. However, little is known on the precise mechanisms by which specific mutations disrupt muscle development, maturation, and contractile function in human. Furthermore, the lack of physiologically relevant human models has hindered the development of effective therapeutic strategies. In this study, we investigated whether dominant pathogenic
                  RYR1
                  variants, leading to gain of function, affect myogenic capacity and excitation-contraction coupling during human skeletal muscle development and maturation in patient-derived iPSC platforms.
Methods
 We generated patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) carrying pathogenic gain of function
                  RYR1
                  variants in position R2452W and A4894P, followed by transgene-free, lineage-directed differentiation into skeletal myogenic derivatives. These
                  RYR1
                  pathogenic variants underwent morphological and functional characterisation in two-dimensional (2D) cultures and 3D engineered muscle tissues.
Results
 The
                  RYR1
                  gain-of-function variants R2452W and A4894P did not significantly impact reprogramming or exit from pluripotency. Differentiation of
                  RYR1
                  -mutant iPSCs recapitulated histological, disease-associated phenotypes of RYR1-RM, including altered myofibre size and alignment. Functional assessment of electromechanical coupling in 2D and 3D cultures revealed altered calcium dynamics and reduced force of contraction in RYR1-RM models vs. healthy controls, demonstrating that the disrupted excitation–contraction coupling associated with dysregulated RYR1 channel activity can be faithfully recapitulated
                  in vitro
                  in a humanised setting.
Conclusion
 Collectively, these findings establish a disease modelling platform providing the foundation for genotype–phenotype correlation studies, precision medicine and preclinical therapeutic evaluation for RYR1-RM.

Abstract
Background
Ryanodine receptor type 1-related myopathies (RYR1-RM) represent a clinically and genetically
heterogeneous group of congenital skeletal muscle disorders caused by pathogenic variants in the
ryanodine receptor type 1 (RYR1) gene, which encodes a calcium channel critical for skeletal muscle
excitation-contraction coupling. Dysregulated calcium handling is a central feature of these conditions.
However, little is known on the precise mechanisms by which speciﬁc mutations disrupt muscle
development, maturation, and contractile function in human. Furthermore, the lack of physiologically
relevant human models has hindered the development of effective therapeutic strategies. In this study,
we investigated whether dominant pathogenic RYR1 variants, leading to gain of function, affect
myogenic capacity and excitation-contraction coupling during human skeletal muscle development and
maturation in patient-derived iPSC platforms.
Methods
We generated patient-speciﬁc induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) carrying pathogenic gain of
function RYR1 variants in position R2452W and A4894P, followed by transgene-free, lineage-directed
differentiation into skeletal myogenic derivatives. These RYR1 pathogenic variants underwent
morphological and functional characterisation in two-dimensional (2D) cultures and 3D engineered
muscle tissues.
Results
The RYR1 gain-of-function variants R2452W and A4894P did not signiﬁcantly impact reprogramming or
exit from pluripotency. Differentiation of RYR1-mutant iPSCs recapitulated histological, disease-
associated phenotypes of RYR1-RM, including altered myoﬁbre size and alignment. Functional
assessment of electromechanical coupling in 2D and 3D cultures revealed altered calcium dynamics and
reduced force of contraction in RYR1-RM models vs. healthy controls, demonstrating that the disrupted
excitation–contraction coupling associated with dysregulated RYR1 channel activity can be faithfully
recapitulated in vitro in a humanised setting.
Conclusion
Collectively, these ﬁndings establish a disease modelling platform providing the foundation for
genotype–phenotype correlation studies, precision medicine and preclinical therapeutic evaluation for
RYR1-RM.

BACKGROUND
The type 1 ryanodine receptor (RyR1), encoded by RYR1, is the principal calcium (Ca²⁺) -release channel
of skeletal muscle. Assembled as a homotetramer of 5,038-residue subunits with a molecular mass of ~ 
2.2 MDa [1], it is the largest ion channel described to date [2]. RyR1 resides in the terminal cisternae of
the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), where it controls the release of stored Ca²⁺ into the cytosol and thereby
occupies a central position in excitation-contraction (EC) coupling. Skeletal muscle EC coupling is driven
by a direct, mechanically transmitted interaction between RyR1 and the voltage-sensing dihydropyridine
receptor (DHPR) located at the transverse-tubule membrane [3–5]. Membrane depolarisation is detected
by the DHPR, which undergoes a conformational change that is mechanically transmitted to RyR1,
triggering channel opening and the release of Ca²⁺ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to the sarcoplasm to
generate the Ca²⁺ transient that initiates muscle contraction. Consequently, genetic variants that perturb
RyR1 function uncouple membrane excitation from the tightly regulated Ca²⁺ signalling required for both
force generation and subsequent muscle relaxation [6].
Pathogenic variants in RYR1 are the most frequent cause of congenital myopathy (CMYO), collectively
termed RYR1-related myopathies (RYR1-RM), affecting approximately 1/90,000 individuals [7][8].
Dominant variants in RYR1 are also the commonest cause of malignant hyperthermia susceptibility
(MHS; MIM #145600), a potentially fatal pharmacogenetic reaction to volatile anaesthetics and
depolarising muscle relaxants, with an estimated population prevalence that could be as high as 1 in
2000 individuals [9]. The two conditions are not mutually exclusive: carriers of MHS variants may remain
asymptomatic until exposed to a triggering agent, and a subset harbours a concurrent CMYO [10].
RYR1-RM patients are clinically heterogeneous, with a spectrum ranging from fetal hypokinesia, early-
onset hypotonia and weakness, delayed motor milestones to scoliosis, joint contractures, facial
weakness, ophthalmoparesis, bulbar and respiratory involvement [11]. From a genetic point of view,
RYR1-RMs are caused by either dominant, recessive or de novo variants. Historically, diagnosis relied on
muscle biopsy histopathology, giving rise to clinical entities known as central core disease (CCD), multi-
minicore disease (MmD), core–rod myopathy, centronuclear myopathy and congenital ﬁbre-type
disproportion [12]. These histological hallmarks are neither speciﬁc nor stable over time, and the
recognised phenotypic spectrum has since widened to encompass King–Denborough syndrome,
exertional rhabdomyolysis and adult-onset presentations [13], prompting the proposal of "RYR1-related
disorders (RYR1-RD)" as a unifying nomenclature [14][15]. Interpreting this heterogeneity is compounded
by the size of RYR1: its 106 exons encode one of the largest human proteins, and while hundreds of
pathogenic variants have been catalogued, a far greater number of reported variants remain of uncertain
signiﬁcance (VUS)[16].
At the mechanistic level, RYR1-RM arise through several routes. Autosomal-dominant variants cluster
within three mutational hotspots [17,18] spanning the N-terminal, central, and C-terminal/pore regions of
the channel. The majority of these variants destabilise the closed state and produce a constitutive Ca²⁺
leak, characterised by elevated resting cytosolic Ca²⁺ and depleted stores. A subset of C-terminal/pore
Page 4/30

variants instead uncouple depolarisation from Ca²⁺ release in the absence of overt leak [19,20].
Autosomal-recessive variants, frequently truncated in one allele, reduce RyR1 protein abundance and
blunt voltage-gated Ca²⁺ release [21,22]. These mechanisms converge on dysregulation of intracellular
Ca²⁺ homeostasis, with secondary mitochondrial oxidative stress and redox modiﬁcation of RyR1
cysteine residues that are thought to contribute to progressive channel dysfunction and to the broad
phenotypic variability observed in patients [23].
RYR1-RM remain incurable, with no disease-speciﬁc treatment currently approved [24]. The large size of
RYR1 precludes gene replacement therapies using adeno-associated virus (AAV). One of the most
advanced drug candidates, Rycal S48168 (ARM210), has shown only preliminary tolerability in a small,
open-label phase 1 trial [25](NCT04141670) in adult patients. Three out of four participants who received
the highest dose reported decreased fatigue [25]. Other therapeutic approaches being explored include
the use of drugs to modulate RYR1 function and reduce calcium leak, such as dantrolene [26]. Other
possible therapeutic strategies include targeting oxidative stress with N-acetylcysteine (NAC)[27,28] and
enhancing the activity at the neuromuscular junction [29–32]. Much of our mechanistic understanding
derives from preclinical systems (e.g., transfected HEK293 cells, RyR1-null myotubes, lymphoblastoid
lines and primary myoblasts), alongside animal models spanning Drosophila, C. elegans, zebraﬁsh, and
mouse models [33]. These models nonetheless carry translational limits. Generating a dedicated animal
line for each of the several known RYR1 variants is not feasible; even if such models could be created,
their predictive clinical value could be limited, as recently observed in a related CMYO [34]. Patient-
derived primary myoblasts are more directly human-relevant, but are constrained by limited proliferative
capacity and by loss of differentiation potential during the expansion required for systematic screening.
To overcome these limitations, we have harnessed our expertise in human iPSC-based disease
modelling and muscle tissue engineering [35,36][37,38] to establish a human-relevant complement to
existing animal and cellular models for future therapeutic development in RYR1-RM. We generated and
characterised human iPSC-derived skeletal muscle models carrying two distinct gain-of-function RYR1
variants. We ﬁrst established patient-speciﬁc iPSC lines and derived myogenic progenitors and then
deﬁned morphological and functional phenotypes in cultured myotubes and 3D engineered muscle
tissues.
RESULTS
1.  Generation and characterisation of patient-speciﬁc RYR1-RM iPSC lines 
Two primary myoblast lines were obtained from patients with RYR1-RM, carrying heterozygous
pathogenic gain-of-function RYR1 variants: c.7354C>T p. R2452W (in exon 46; hereafter referred to as
R2452W) and c.14680G>C p. A4894P (in exon 102; hereafter referred to as A4894P) (Figure 1A). Both
patients presented symptoms from birth/early childhood and histopathological features consistent with
a diagnosis of CCD. Their family history was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Clinically,
the patient with the R2452W variant showed proximal and axial weakness, progressive respiratory
Page 5/30

weakness and scoliosis, while the patient with the A4894P variant had congenital bilateral hip
dislocation, but retained good motor function up to adult life with mild proximal and axial weakness and
ankle tightness. Both variants are also associated with MHS.
Presence of the pathogenic variants in heterozygosity for each line was conﬁrmed via Sanger
sequencing for the RYR1 exons of interest (Figure 1B). To assess the structural context of the
pathogenic variants, we mapped their positions onto the 3D reconstructed RYR1 protein structure using
ChimeraX. Structural modelling revealed that the A4894P variant is located within pore region, whereas
the R2452W variant is in the cytosolic domain of the RYR1 channel (Figure 1C).
RYR1-RM myoblasts were then reprogrammed into iPSCs using a non-integrating, mRNA-based
reprogramming protocol (Supplementary ﬁgure 1A). Two clones per patient-derived cell line were
selected and tested for genome integrity (Supplementary ﬁgure 1B). Copy number variation analysis
revealed that one clone per line was genomically stable (Supplementary ﬁgure 1C,D), hence further
analyses were performed with such clones. Successful reprogramming was conﬁrmed by expression of
the pluripotency-associated markers SOX2, NANOG, and OCT4 via quantitative PCR (Figure 1D,
Supplementary ﬁgure 1E) and immunoﬂuorescence analysis (Figure 1E). To functionally assess
pluripotency, RYR1-RM iPSCs were differentiated into derivatives of the three embryonic germ layers.
Differentiation into ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm was conﬁrmed by immunostaining for βIII-
tubulin (ectoderm), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; mesoderm), and GATA4 (endoderm), respectively
(Figure 1F). These results demonstrate that the newly generated patient-speciﬁc iPSCs carrying RYR1
pathogenic variants R2452W and A4894P respectively retain pluripotency and can differentiate into
derivatives of all three germ layers.
2.  Human iPSCs harbouring pathogenic RYR1 variants undergo eﬃcient skeletal myogenesis 
Having successfully generated RYR1-RM iPSCs, we differentiated them into myoblasts using an
established transgene-free, small molecule-based differentiation protocol mimicking human
developmental myogenesis in vitro [39], alongside a healthy control line (Supplementary Figure 2A,B).
The myogenic identity of the derived cells was conﬁrmed by immunostaining for the myogenic markers
MyoD and Desmin, demonstrating successful skeletal myogenic speciﬁcation and generation of
myoblast-like cells (Supplementary Figure 2C).
To investigate whether RYR1 pathogenic variants impact on complex processes of skeletal myogenesis
such as terminal differentiation, multinucleation and generation and maintenance of muscle
stem/progenitor cells, we quantiﬁed Myosin Heavy Chain (MYHC) positive multinucleated myotubes,
alongside Pax7-positive cells at day 6 of differentiation (Figure 2A).  No signiﬁcant difference was
observed in Pax7- (Figure 2B; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 3.33±2.08%, R2452W: 2.33±0.58%, A4894P:
2.00±1.73%) or MyoD-positive nuclei (Figure 2C; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 52.00±14.73%, R2452W:
36.00±4.58%, A4894P: 27.67±9.82%) between RYR1-RM and healthy control myotubes. Furthermore,
analysis of the differentiation index showed comparable number of nuclei in MYHC positive cells across
all lines (Figure 2D, E; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 78.97±9.25%, R2452W: 71.09±12.10%, A4894P:
Page 6/30

66.97±13.47%), indicating that RYR1 pathogenic variants do not signiﬁcantly impair myotube formation
during in vitro skeletal muscle differentiation. 
As RYR1 is predominantly expressed in mature skeletal myoﬁbres, we investigated its expression and
localization sequentially (days 0 to 5) throughout myogenic differentiation of iPSC-derived myoblasts.
RYR1 expression was ﬁrst detected at day 3 of differentiation, detecting a 100-fold increase vs. its
expression to day 0. To conﬁrm that RYR1 expression increased in parallel with myotube maturation, Titin
(TTN) gene expression was quantiﬁed. TTN expression followed a similar pattern to RYR1, with a 1000-
fold change increase when comparing day 3 to day 0 of differentiation (Supplementary ﬁgure 2D). No
signiﬁcant difference was observed in RYR1 or TTN gene expression between RYR1-RM and healthy
control cell lines. For both genes, the fold increase in expression from day 0 to day 3 of differentiation
was comparable across all three cell lines, after day 3 of differentiation the expression levels remained
stable (Figure 2F; Supplementary ﬁgure 2D,E).
To conﬁrm RYR1 protein production, healthy control and RYR-RM myotubes were analysed by
immunoﬂuorescence at day 6 of differentiation. RYR1 exhibited a punctate staining pattern in all Titin
and MYHC positive myotubes, consistent with its expected subcellular localisation in differentiated
skeletal muscle cells (Figure 2G). Overall, these data support the suitability of human iPSC myogenic
derivatives to study RYR1-RM in vitro. 
3.  RYR1-RM iPSC-derived skeletal myotubes exhibit altered morphology 
Individuals with RYR1-RM exhibit abnormalities in myoﬁbre size, with ﬁbre size disproportion
representing a characteristic pathological feature in muscle biopsies [40]. Therefore, we ﬁrst assessed
whether our in vitro model recapitulated this histological phenotype. Interestingly, quantitative analysis of
myoﬁbre dimensions at day 6 of differentiation revealed a signiﬁcantly higher proportion of larger
myotubes in the RYR1-RM lines compared with healthy control (Figure 3 A,B; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl
8.78±3.52 µm, R2452W: 16.76±3.99 µm, A4894P: 13.30±8.78 µm).
As myonuclear shape and positioning abnormalities are a feature of some congenital myopathies and
muscular dystrophies (e.g. MTM1- or LMNA- related forms), we next examined nuclear morphology using
a proprietary computational pipeline (Supplementary Figure 3A). Compared with healthy control, RYR1-
RM myotubes displayed rounder nuclei (Figure 3A,C; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 0.73±0.004, R2452W:
0.78±0.003, A4894P: 0.78±0.002, accompanied by a decrease in the major axis length (Figure 3 A,D;
Supplementary ﬁgure 3B; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 23.22±0.63 µm, R2452W: 20.96±0.11 µm, A4894P:
20.92±0.13 µm), suggesting that the disease-associated pathogenic variants may also affect nuclear
morphology. 
Looking further into myoﬁbre morphology, we could also notice a reduction in the amount of myotubes
per ﬁeld of view (Supplementary Figure 3C) and reduction in myotube alignment in the RYR1-RM lines
(Figure 3A). Therefore, we quantiﬁed myotube alignment using three parameters: the ﬁeld alignment
index (ranging from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect alignment), ﬁeld circular variance (reﬂecting the
Page 7/30

degree of dispersion within each ﬁeld), and circular standard deviation in degrees (representing the
spread of local spine tangent orientations as an axial angular standard deviation). RYR1-RM myotubes
exhibited a lower alignment index than healthy control myotubes (Figure 3E, Supplementary Figure 3D;
mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 0.91±0.02, R2452W: 0.85±0.008, A4894P: 0.86±0.01), together with signiﬁcantly
higher ﬁeld circular variance (Figure 3F; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 0.09±0.02, R2452W: 0.15±0.008,
A4894P: 0.14±0.01) and circular standard degree (Figure 3G; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 10.96±1.30,
R2452W: 15.11±0.36, A4894P: 14.34±0.80), indicating a greater degree of myotube disorganization.
Together, these measures demonstrate a signiﬁcantly higher level of disorganization in myotubes
carrying pathogenic RYR1 variants.  
4.  RYR1-RM iPSC-derived skeletal myotubes display functional alterations in calcium dynamics 
Given the central role of RYR1 in excitation–contraction coupling, we investigated whether the gain-of-
function R2452W and A4894P pathogenic variants altered calcium dynamics in iPSC-derived myotubes.
To monitor calcium transients with live imaging analysis, iPSC-derived myoblasts were transduced with a
lentiviral vector expressing the genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP under the control of the
muscle-speciﬁc MHCK7 promoter [41]. Following differentiation, both healthy control and RYR1-mutant
myotubes responded to caffeine-induced pharmacological activation (Figure 3H and Supplementary
Figure 3E) as well as to electrical stimulation (Supplementary Figure 3F,G), demonstrating functional
excitation–contraction coupling. Of note, RYR1-RM myotubes showed a trend towards reduced calcium
transients upon electrical stimulation (Supplementary Figure 3H), suggesting a diminished capacity for
depolarisation-induced calcium release.
To speciﬁcally assess RYR1 channel activity when directly activated, we analysed traces of healthy
control and RYR1-RM myotubes following stimulation with caffeine, an established RYR1 agonist [42,43].
We ﬁrst examined the ability of myotubes to collectively respond to caffeine stimulation, detecting an
increased calcium release in R2452W and A4894P pathogenic variants compared to the healthy control
line by analysing the whole cell culture well (Supplementary ﬁgure 3E). To further validate this ﬁnding at
higher resolution, we analysed calcium release in individual myotubes, detecting a trend towards an
increased response in R2452W and a signiﬁcantly enhanced response to caffeine in variant A4894P
(Figure 3H-K; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 0.20±0.08, R2452W: 0.28±0.14, A4894P: 0.37±0.15). Interestingly,
RYR1-RM myotubes displayed substantially greater heterogeneity in their calcium responses when
compared to the healthy control, with a large proportion exhibiting high-amplitude calcium transients
(ΔF/F0 > 0.5), and a higher standard deviation (SD: Healthy Ctrl: ±0.08, R2452W: ±0.14, A4894P: ±0.15),
consistent with increased variability in RYR1-mediated calcium release (Figure 3K). 
Together, these ﬁndings indicate that R2452W and A4894P iPSC-derived myotubes exhibit altered RYR1
channel activation in response to both electrical and pharmacological stimulation. The increased
sensitivity to activation upon caffeine administration may reﬂect increased channel sensitivity and
activity due to the underlying gain-of-function variants. 
5. RYR1-RM 3D engineered muscle tissues show myoﬁbre abnormalities and reduced contractile force
Page 8/30

To more faithfully recapitulate the architecture of native skeletal muscle, improve long-term culture
viability and enable functional evaluations beyond calcium dynamics, we engineered RYR1-RM 3D
skeletal muscle tissues (3D-EMTs). 3D-EMTs provide a platform to enhance muscle maturation and
enable functional assessment of contractile properties. iPSC-derived myoblasts were embedded within
ﬁbrin hydrogels between two ﬂexible polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) posts, which provide mechanical
tension to promote myoﬁbre alignment and maturation[35–37].
Healthy control and RYR1-RM 3D-EMTs were maintained in differentiation medium for 14 days.
Throughout this period, no overt macroscopic differences in tissue morphology were observed between
genotypes during differentiation (Figure 3A). Immunoﬂuorescence analysis conﬁrmed terminally
differentiated Titin-positive myoﬁbres in both control and RYR1-RM  3D-EMTs (Figure 3B). We further
evaluated sarcomeric organisation in both healthy and RYR1-RM  3D-EMTs and observed formation of
organised sarcomeric structures, characterised by the presence of titin-positive striations.
Immunoﬂuorescence analysis of differentiated 3D-EMTs at day 14, conﬁrmed detection of RYR1 in both
healthy control and RYR1-RM samples (Figure 4C). Closer examination of myotube organisation revealed
that healthy control myotubes were predominantly aligned along the principal axis of the 3D-EMTs. In
contrast, RYR1-RM  3D-EMTs exhibited reduced alignment along this axis and a greater degree of
dispersion compared with healthy controls (Figure 4D,E; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 7.67±1.77°, R2452W:
15.60±3.04°, A4894P: 14.94±2.91°). 
We then assessed functional EC readouts in RYR1-RM 3D-EMTs. Calcium transients were evaluated with
live imaging analysis, by monitoring GCaMP6 ﬂuorescence in response to electrical stimulation of the
3D-EMTs. At day 14 of differentiation, both healthy control and RYR1-RM 3D-EMTs responded to
electrical stimulation with calcium release (Figure 4F,G), demonstrating functional EC coupling. Notably,
calcium transient peak amplitude was reduced in RYR1-RM  3D-EMTs compared with healthy control
(Figure 4G; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 122.7±103.2, R2452W: 33.48±40.97, A4894P: 43.06±31.99),
consistent with a functional defect in response to electrical stimulation, validating our observations in
monolayer cultures.
Finally, we assessed force of contraction of RYR1-RM and control 3D-EMTs using a twitch protocol with
electrical stimulation at 1Hz and 5V. Although all samples responded to electrical stimulation with
measurable contractions (Figure 4H), RYR1-RM 3D-EMTs showed a signiﬁcant reduction in contractile
force when compared with the healthy control, both when stimulated with a 1 Hz twitch contraction
protocol (Figure 4I; mean ±SD: Healthy Ctrl 137.30±31.17 µN, R2452W: 35.50±7.15 µN, A4894P:
34.43±12.73 µN) and when stimulated with a 30 Hz tetanic contraction protocol (Figure 4J; mean ±SD:
Healthy Ctrl 145.6±50.12 µN, R2452W: 42.33±11.25 µN, A4894P: 38.00±13.84 µN). Taken together, our
results show that typical histopathological and functional RYR1-RM features can be modelled upon
hiPSC differentiation into skeletal myogenic derivatives in 2D and 3D cultures.
DISCUSSION
Page 9/30

In this study we generated, characterised and differentiated two patient-derived iPSC lines carrying
dominant pathogenic RYR1 variants associated with early onset myopathy [44,45] located in structurally
and functionally distinct regions of the RYR1 channel: R2452W in the cytosolic region, and A4894P within
the pore-forming domain. Together they sample two of the mutational hotspots along the length of the
RYR1 protein and let us ask whether variants in different channel domains produce convergent or
distinct cellular phenotypes. Both variants did not detectably impair skeletal myogenesis. Directed,
transgene-free differentiation yielded myogenic progenitors and myotubes at eﬃciencies comparable to
the healthy control, with no signiﬁcant differences in Pax7- or MyoD-positive progenitor cells, Desmin
expression, or myogenic differentiation capacity into myotubes. The temporal induction of RYR1 and TTN
transcripts during terminal differentiation was likewise indistinguishable between patient-derived and
control lines, and RYR1 protein showed the expected punctate distribution in maturing myotubes. The
cellular phenotypes we describe therefore arise downstream of a likely intact differentiation programme,
although variants associated with more severe, pre-natal presentations are likely to have a more tangible
impact on myogenic commitment or maturation.
Nonetheless, patient cultures showed reproducible morphological abnormalities. Myotubes carrying
either variant had an altered ﬁbre-diameter distribution. Myoﬁbre size abnormalities are a recognised
histopathological feature of RYR1-RM [40,45]; however, the increased myotube diameter we see in vitro
does not directly reproduce the small-ﬁbre predominance frequently reported in patient biopsies and
should be interpreted with caution. A speculative explanation of such ﬁnding might be that larger
myoﬁbres are produced during earlier stages of myogenesis [46] like those modelled using iPSCs, which
might in turn be more susceptible to pathological events downstream RYR1, and hence progressively
lost with time.
An intriguing structural ﬁnding was the disrupted myotube organisation. Cultures carrying either variant
were signiﬁcantly less well aligned than control cells, with a lower ﬁeld alignment index, greater circular
variance and increased angular dispersion, and this disorganisation persisted when the cells were
assembled into 3D-EMTs, where patient tissues again showed reduced alignment along the principal
axis of tension. Interestingly, myoﬁbre disorganisation and splitting has recently been described in
Drosophila larvae overexpressing Ryr1 [47] strengthening our ﬁnding in another model. The persistence
of the phenotype across culture and model formats argues in favour of an intrinsic defect in myoﬁbrillar
organisation as it has also been reported in RYR1-RM patient biopsies [48] and could contribute to the
contractile deﬁcits measured in the 3D-EMTs.
An iPSC platform is particularly well suited to model pathogenic variants occurring in RYR1. The gene
has 106 exons and a correspondingly large allelic spectrum with many reported variants of unknown
signiﬁcance. As generating a dedicated animal model for every variant is impractical, patient-speciﬁc
iPSC lines could provide a human substrate in which these variants can be examined functionally. The
R2452W variant has been reported in a family segregating CCD with MHS [49] and functionally
characterised in a heterologous HEK293 system, where cells expressing the variant released
signiﬁcantly more Ca²⁺ than wild-type when stimulated with the RyR1-speciﬁc agonist 4-chloro-m-cresol,
Page 10/30

in line with the hypersensitivity to direct RYR1 activation observed in our platforms for this variant [50].
A4894P affects a residue within the channel pore at which different amino acid substitutions have been
reported to produce divergent functional consequences (hyperactivation or inactivation of the channel,
alongside CMYO with uniform type 1 ﬁbres) [51].
An intriguing functional ﬁnding of our study is a dissociation between the calcium release triggered by
caffeine or electrical stimulation. When RYR1 was activated directly with caffeine, RYR-RM myotubes
generated calcium transients of greater amplitude than healthy control, with the strongest response in
the pore variant A4894P and an intermediate response in the cytosolic variant R2452W; RYR-RM
myotubes also showed greater heterogeneity, with an increased proportion of myotubes exhibiting high-
amplitude transients (ΔF/F₀ > 0.5). By contrast, depolarisation-evoked release, assessed by electrical
stimulation, trended lower in patient myotubes and was signiﬁcantly reduced for A4894P. The same
pattern held in the 3D-EMTs, where electrically-evoked transient amplitude was signiﬁcantly lower in both
RYR-RM patient lines when compared to the healthy control, overall suggesting impaired coupling
between RyR1 and the voltage-sensing DHPR [54].
The 3D-EMT platform also allows contractile performance to be measured, the readout most directly
related to the muscle weakness that deﬁnes RYR1-RM clinically. The detection of reduced force of
contraction in patient-derived tissues ﬁts both the blunted depolarisation-evoked calcium release and the
myoﬁbre misalignment described above, connecting the molecular and structural phenotypes to a deﬁcit
at whole tissue level.
Finally, in this study we compared two RYR1-RM patient lines with a healthy control line. Therefore, we
cannot exclude a contribution from inter-individual variation unrelated to the RYR1 variant. To address
this, future work will aim to expand the library of both control and mutant lines, including isogenic pairs
to further strengthen genotype–phenotype comparisons. The mechanistic basis of the calcium and
structural phenotypes will also need more direct interrogation, to separate the leak, store-depletion and
uncoupling contributions outlined above.
CONCLUSION
Overall, this patient-speciﬁc 2D and 3D platform models variant-speciﬁc calcium-handling and
morphological phenotypes in dominant RYR1-RM, alongside what has been seen in patient muscle and
in prior animal models. It shows that iPSC-derived myotubes and 3D-EMTs are a workable system to test
the functional consequences of RYR1 variants. It also provides a basis for deeper mechanistic
dissection, personalised medicine and scalable therapeutic testing, that RYR1-RM still lacks.

iPSC generation
Page 11/30

Two RYR1 patient-derived primary myoblasts lines, harbouring pathogenic gain-of-function RYR1
variants: c.7354C > T (exon 46; hereafter referred to as R2452W) and c.14680G > C (exon 102; hereafter
referred to as A4894P), were obtained from UCL MRC Neuromuscular Disease Biobank and
reprogrammed at The Francis Crick Institute (London) using a non-integrating mRNA-based protocol
(StemRNA™ 3rd Gen Reprogramming Kit, REPROCELL) that combines non-modiﬁed RNA (NM-RNA) and
microRNA technology.
Patient-derived primary myoblasts were seeded at a density of 5x104 cells/well in 2 wells of a 6-well
plate coated with iMatrix (Stemgent). On the ﬁrst day of reprogramming, day 0, the medium was
switched to NutriStem medium (Stemgent), and cells were transferred to a hypoxic incubator at 37°C, 5%
CO2 and 5% O2. Myoblasts were reprogrammed 1-day post-seeding using the StemRNA™ 3rd Gen
Reprogramming Kit (Stemgent) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Brieﬂy, the NM-RNA
cocktail was added to RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Gibco) and transfected into the cells for four
consecutive days with medium change 6 h post-transfection. iPSC-like colonies started to appear 9 days
following the ﬁrst transfections; they were manually picked and transferred to Matrigel-coated 6-well
plates with mTeSR1 medium (StemCell Technologies) containing 10 µM Y-27362. The medium was
changed after 24 h. Colonies were expanded by splitting at a 1:3 to 1:6 ratio every 4–6 days and
maintained in a hypoxic incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2 and 5% O2. Human cell work was conducted under
the approval of the NHS Health Research Authority Research Ethics Committee reference no.
13/LO/1826; IRAS project ID no. 141100.
iPSC cell culture
NCRM1 (https://hpscreg.eu/cellline/CRMi003-A) was used as healthy control human iPSC line and
cultured in parallel to the two patient-speciﬁc newly generated iPSC lines. iPSCs were cultured in mTeSR
Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies, 100–0276). Cells were grown on tissue culture plates coated in
Matrigel 0.5% (Corning, 354230/354231), in DMEM high glucose (DMEM HG, Sigma Aldrich, D5671) at
5% O2 and 5% CO2. iPSCs were detached for expansion and passaging with EDTA 0.5 mM
(ThermoFisher, J15694.AE). Trilineage differentiation to conﬁrm pluripotency was performed using
STEMdiff Trilineage Differentiation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, 05230).
Skeletal myogenic differentiation of iPSCs
Skeletal myogenic differentiation was performed using a small-moleculedifferentiation protocol adapted
from the literature[39]. Brieﬂy, on day − 1, iPSCs were dissociated in TrypLE Express (GIBCO, 12604-013)
and 3x105 cells/cm2 were seeded onto Matrigel-coated plates in mTeSR Plus supplemented with 10 µM
Y-27362 (Cell Guidance, SM02). On day 0–1, somite differentiation was initiated by culturing cells in
DMEM/F-12 Glutamax (Thermo Fisher, 10565018), supplemented with ITS 1X (Gibco, 41400045), CHIR 3
uM (Tocris Bioscience, 4423/10) and Penicillin-Streptomycin 0.5% (Gibco, 11548876). On day 2–3 cells
were cultured in DMEM/F-12 Glutamax, supplemented with ITS 1X, LDN193189 200 nM (STEMCELL
Page 12/30

Technologies, 72147), SB431542 10 uM (Bio-Techne, 1614/1) and Penicillin-Streptomycin 0.5%
(PenStrep, Gibco, 11548876). On day 4–5 cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 Glutamax, supplemented
with ITS 1X, CHIR 3 uM, bFGF 20 ng/ml (Gibco, PHG0026) and PenStrep 0.5% (Gibco, 11548876). From
day 6 and up to day 28 of differentiation, cells were cultured in DMEM HG, supplemented with 15%
Knockout Serum Replacement (KSR, Thermo Fisher, 10828028), HGF 10 ng/ml (Fisher Scientiﬁc, 100-
39H), IGF 2 ng/ml (Cambridge Bioscience, GFM5-10) and PenStrep 0.5%. Cells were fed with fresh
medium every day for the ﬁrst 6 days, and every 48 hours from day 6 onwards. On day 28, cells were
dissociated by incubating with Collagenase Type IV (STEMCELL Technologies, 7909) for 5 minutes at 37
oC, followed by incubation with TrypLE Express for 5 minutes. Cells were ﬁltered consecutively through
100 µm and 40 µm cell strainers to remove cell clumps and obtain a single cell suspension. Cells were
then plated 5000/cm2 in Skeletal Muscle Growth Media (Promocell, C-23060) supplemented with bFGF
20 ng/ml. After the ﬁrst cell passage, cells were cultured in Skeletal Muscle Growth Media without bFGF
supplementation. To induce terminal myogenic differentiation, myoblasts were passaged and plated in
Skeletal Muscle Growth Media, and once at 100% conﬂuence they were switched to a terminal
differentiation medium composed of DMEM/F-12 Glutamax, ITS 1x, N2 supplement 1x (Gibco,
17502048) and Pen Strep 0.3%. Cells were differentiated as monolayer cultures for 5–6 days.
Differentiating iPSCs and myoblast cultures were grown at 37°C under ambient O2 and 5% CO2.
Generation of human 3D engineered muscle tissues (3D-EMTs)
3D-EMTs were generated as previously described[37]. Brieﬂy, iPSC-derived myoblasts were pre-treated
with 10 µM Y-2736210 for 2 hours before detachment. Myoblasts were counted to have 1 million cells
per construct, pelleted and placed on ice. The hydrogel mix is composed of cells, mixed with 3.5 mg/mL
human ﬁbrinogen (Merck, F3879), 10% Matrigel (Corning) and heat-inactivated proliferation media
(inactivation by warming at 56°C for 30 minutes) was added to reach a total ﬁnal volume of 113 µl per
construct. To promote polymerisation, the hydrogel mix was combined individually for each muscle
construct with 7 µl of thrombin (Baxter), mixed well and then cast in a 2% UltraPure agarose in DPBS
(Invitrogen, 16500-500) mould containing the silicon posts (EHT Technologies/Dinabios GmbH
Hamburg). Constructs were placed at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 2 hours, after which DMEM high glucose was
added to each muscle to facilitate their removal from the mould. After 10 minutes of incubation, 3D
constructs attached to the silicone posts were lifted from the agarose moulds and placed in a new 24-
well plate containing fresh Skeletal Muscle Growth Media containing 33 µg/ml Aprotinin (Sigma Aldrich,
A3428-10MG) and cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2. After 48 hours, differentiation was induced with terminal
differentiation medium supplemented with 33 µg/ml Aprotinin, which was changed every other day.
Contractility assay
For muscle contraction experiments, 50 µl of hydrogel-cell suspension (comprising ﬁbrinogen, thrombin,
Matrigel and cells), prepared as described previously, was dispensed into each well of a 24-chamber
Page 13/30

polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) casting plate. Prior to use, the PDMS chambers were pre-treated with 1%
(w/v) Pluronic F-127 (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at room temperature, to minimize adhesion of the
hydrogel to the PDMS surface. The fabrication and design speciﬁcations of the 24-chamber PDMS plate
have been previously described[55]. Immediately after the hydrogel deposition, the CUORE Smartlid
system (Optics11 Life, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) was assembled onto the PDMS plate to support
tissue polymerization around the Smartlid cantilevers. The upper component of the Smartlid
incorporates an optical-ﬁbre plate equipped with 24 pairs of stainless-steel cantilevers, serving as
anchor points for the developing constructs. After allowing hydrogel polymerization for 2 hours at 37°C
with 5% CO2, 1 ml of Skeletal Muscle Growth Medium containing 33 µg/ml Aprotinin was added to each
well. After 48 hours, the engineered muscle tissues (3D-EMTs) were lifted from the casting PDMS plate,
and the 3D-EMTs now anchored to the cantilevers were transferred to a standard 24-well plate containing
terminal differentiation medium supplemented with 33 µg/ml Aprotinin, which was changed every other
day. Agrin (R&D Biosystems, 550-AG-100) was added to the terminal differentiation medium from day 4
of differentiation onwards. Additionally, the medium was refreshed immediately after each electrical
stimulation session, which was performed every 48 hours. Electrical stimulation was started at day 4 of
differentiation, when an additional component of 24 pairs of carbon electrodes was inserted in the
Smartlid system. Constructs were stimulated using a twitch protocol of 1 Hz 3 Volts for 30 seconds. On
day 6 of differentiation, constructs were subjected to square-wave pulses at 3 V and 1 Hz for 30
seconds, followed by another round of stimulations with square-wave pulses at 5 V and 1 Hz for 30
seconds. The same was done on day 8. At day 10 of differentiation, muscles were subjected
consecutively to square-wave pulses at 3 V and 1 Hz for 30 seconds, followed by another round of
stimulations with square-wave pulses at 5 V and 1 Hz for 30 seconds, and ﬁnally to a tetanic stimulation
with 5 V and 30 Hz for 10 seconds. The analysis was performed using the CUORE Viewer software.
Protein structure prediction
The RYR1 protein structure was reconstructed based on the available crystallographic structure of the
rabbit RYR1 protein using UCSF ChimeraX. The template structure (PDB ID: 8X48) was obtained from the
Protein Data Bank and visualised and reconstructed within the ChimeraX environment. To establish
correspondence between the human and rabbit RYR1 sequences, the human RYR1 protein sequence
(UniProt ID: P21817) was aligned with the rabbit RYR1 sequence (UniProt ID: P11716) using Protein
BLAST. This alignment enabled accurate identiﬁcation of equivalent amino acid positions between the
two species. Subsequently, the speciﬁc pathogenic variants identiﬁed in the human RYR1 protein were
mapped onto the reconstructed structure using the sequence visualisation tools available in UCSF
ChimeraX.

Cells were ﬁxed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Boster, AR1068) for 15 minutes at room temperature
and washed with DPBS (Gibco, 14190). Cells were blocked/permeabilized in permeabilization buffer,

X-100) overnight at 4°C on shaking plate. Primary antibodies were removed, and cells were washed at

primary antibodies and incubated with DAPI 1:1000 for 2 hours in the dark at room temperature.
Secondary antibodies were removed, cells washed at least twice for 10 minutes in PBS, and either
imaged immediately after or left in PBS and stored at 4°C until imaged.
3D-EMTs were immunolabelled as previously described[37]. Brieﬂy, muscles were ﬁxed alternatively
while still attached to posts in 4% PFA for 3 hours at 4°C or in 1% PFA overnight at 4°C, then washed with
PBS before being removed from posts and placed into blocking buffer (PBS, 10% FBS, 1% BSA, 0.5%
Triton-X-100) for 6 hours. Primary antibodies were incubated in staining buffer (PBS, 1% BSA, 0.5%
Triton-X-100) overnight on a shaker at 4°C. The next day, 6 washes were performed throughout the day
with 1x PBS on shaking plate at room temperature. Secondary antibodies were diluted in staining buffer
with DAPI 1:1000 and incubated overnight on a shaker at 4°C. The next day, muscles were washed 6
times over the day with 1x PBS before they could be mounted onto cavity slides (MERCK Life Science
Ltd. UK, BR475505-50EA) with mounting medium (SIGMA Aldrich, F4680-25mL). Primary antibodies
used in this study are listed in Table 2. Secondary antibodies used were all AlexaFluor (Life Technologies
1:500).

Analysis of myoﬁbre size and alignment
Fiber diameter was quantiﬁed using the measurement tool in Fiji. Five myotubes were randomly selected
within each image, and each selected ﬁber was measured six times across its whole length. The 6
diameter values obtained for each myotube were then averaged to obtain an average measurement of
the ﬁbre diameter for each myotube.
Fiber directionality was assessed by analysing the orientation of individual myotubes within each ﬁeld of
view. For each myotube, a mask was generated using a proprietary segmentation algorithm. For each
segmented myotube mask, a one-pixel-wide skeleton was generated, and the longest connected path
was taken as the myotube spine. Local tangent directions were computed for every consecutive pair of
pixels along the skeleton path. Tangent angles were weighted by skeleton step length and combined
using axial circular statistics, treating directions separated by 180 degrees as equivalent. The mean
resultant direction deﬁned myotube orientation, while the resultant vector magnitude quantiﬁed tangent
alignment and was used to derive circular variance and circular standard deviation. The ﬁgure reports
three related parameters derived from this analysis: Spine tangent circular variance – quantiﬁes the
dispersion of local spine tangent orientations along the myotube. A value of 0 indicates that all tangents
point in the same axial direction, while higher values reﬂect greater variability in orientation. Spine
tangent circular standard deviation (degrees) – expresses this same dispersion as an axial angular
standard deviation. Smaller values correspond to straighter myotubes with more uniformly oriented
segments. Spine tangent alignment index – a normalized measure (0–1) of directional coherence along
the spine, where 1 reﬂects perfect alignment of local tangents and values close to 0 indicate highly
disordered orientation. In addition, overall ﬁbre directionality within the 3D engineered muscle tissues
was independently quantiﬁed using the Directionality plugin in Fiji, with the resulting angular dispersion
values plotted in the corresponding graph.
