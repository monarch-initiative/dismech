# Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease in children.
**Authors:** Saylam E, Ramani PK, Duvuru R, Haley B, Veerapandiyan A
**Journal:** Ann Child Neurol Soc (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/cns3.20093](https://doi.org/10.1002/cns3.20093)

## Content

Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) disease represents a diverse group of inherited neuropathies with a broad spectrum of symptoms. It is the most prevalent inherited neuropathy, with an estimated prevalence ranging from 9.7 to 82 cases per 100,000 individuals. Despite this, CMT comprises only 118 of 853 inherited neuropathy entries in the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database. This comprehensive review offers a thorough examination of CMT's clinical features, subtypes, genetic underpinnings, and pathomechanisms in pediatric cases. CMT typically manifests as progressively worsening muscle weakness and atrophy, primarily affecting the distal extremities. Patients may also experience foot and ankle deformities, hand atrophy, and other systemic issues. To accurately diagnose CMT, a detailed family history, comprehensive clinical evaluation, nerve conduction studies, and relevant genetic testing are essential. Importantly, establishing a differential diagnosis is crucial during evaluation to rule out other conditions with similar presentations. This review aims to provide clinicians with a valuable resource for diagnosing and managing CMT, emphasizing the need for a streamlined and standardized approach considering advancements in genetic testing and the identification of various subtypes.

Charcot‐Marie‐Tooth (CMT) disease is a group of inherited neuropathies characterized by progressive muscle weakness and atrophy, primarily affecting the distal lower and upper extremities. As the most common inherited neuropathy, CMT involves various genetic pathogenic variants leading to dysfunction of peripheral nerves. Patients typically present with muscle weakness, sensory loss, and foot deformities such as pes cavus. The disease is heterogeneous, with different subtypes exhibiting distinct clinical features and progression.
1
 Accurate diagnosis often requires a combination of clinical evaluation, electrophysiological studies, and genetic testing to differentiate CMT from other neuropathies and guide appropriate management.

CMT commonly presents with distal, symmetrical muscle weakness, atrophy, and reduced deep tendon reflexes, typically affecting the lower extremities more than the upper. Infantile CMT forms can present with generalized hypotonia, hip dysplasia, poor sucking, and respiratory issues. In toddlers, early signs include motor delays, toe walking, frequent tripping, and falling.
1
 Older children often exhibit difficulties with running, clumsiness, and recurrent ankle injuries. Adolescents may first seek care from orthopedists or podiatrists for issues like pes cavus or pes planus. Upper extremity symptoms often become noticeable when children engage in tasks like writing or dressing. Sensory symptoms, such as cold feet, and hair loss are common but may go unnoticed unless severe. Muscle cramps are frequent in axonal subtypes, and neuropathic pain results from reduced A‐delta afferents and small fiber involvement. Abnormal gait and scoliosis may contribute to back pain.

Weakness affecting ankle plantar flexion, dorsiflexion, eversion, and inversion are common. Distal muscle weakness can make voluntary toe walking challenging unless heel cord contractures are present. Pes cavus and hammer toes are common, while pes planus is less frequent. Advanced cases may show atrophy in the anterior and posterior compartments of the legs. In the upper extremities, subtle wasting of the hypothenar and the base of the thenar muscles may be observed, sometimes preceding noticeable weakness. Positive sensory symptoms are not common. Impaired vibration and position sense is common. Sensory nerve dysfunction may lead to tremors and dry skin on the hands and feet. Reflexes are often diminished at the ankles and knees, though in axonal types, they can be normal or even brisk. In young children, the examination might appear normal except for difficulties with heel walking. Enlarged and excessively firm nerves are present in about 25% of patients, often visible in the arms.

In evaluating a patient for suspected CMT, the clinical features are essential for narrowing the diagnosis. Notably, positive sensory symptoms are generally absent in CMT. A detailed family history should include questions about any neuropathic symptoms among relatives. The neurological examination may reveal key signs such as pes cavus, distal muscle weakness, diminished or absent deep tendon reflexes, and abnormal sensory findings, indicating neuropathy. If CMT is suspected, genetic testing is recommended. Electrophysiological studies can aid in the diagnosis and help rule out other conditions. Serum creatinine kinase levels are typically normal.

In 1968, Dyck and Lambert identified two main categories of CMT based on the electrophysiologic findings: type 1 demyelinating (conduction velocity <38 m/s) and type 2 axonal (conduction velocity >38 m/s). Later, the intermediate form was identified with a conduction velocity of 35–45 m/s. Over time, with the advancement of genetic testing, CMT was classified into subtypes CMT‐1 to CMT‐7
2
 (Tables 1, 2, 3). With such advancements in genetic testing and several subtypes being identified, a simplified and standard approach to facilitate the identification of these disorders is needed. In 2015, Mathis and colleagues proposed an approach to include the gene name in the nomenclature of inherited neuropathies.
8
 The same group suggested using a different approach based on the survey results from several neurologists and scientists.

Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCS) are minimally invasive and valuable for differentiating between inherited and acquired neuropathies when used alongside clinical assessments.
1
 NCS can distinguish between demyelinating and axonal neuropathies. Demyelinating neuropathies are characterized by severe nerve conduction velocity (NCV) slowing, typically below 38 m/s in upper limb motor nerves. In contrast, the axonal type shows normal or only mildly reduced NCVs, generally above 38 m/s in median or ulnar motor nerves.
7
, 
10
 NCS are crucial for assessing the extent and pattern of conduction slowing, providing insights into myelin dysfunction. A decrease in the amplitude of compound muscle action potentials (CMAP) indicates axonal fiber loss. Both axonal and demyelinating CMT types lead to a reduction in CMAP and sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitudes due to axonal fiber loss.
10
, 
11



NCV slowing is uniform and diffuse in CMT due to widespread myelin and Schwann cell dysfunction. In contrast, acquired neuropathies exhibit asymmetrical slowing and partial conduction blocks.
11
 Asymmetrical findings are rare in some CMT subtypes. CMTX shows irregular conduction abnormalities across and within nerves, with notable excessive temporal dispersion and conduction blocks.
12
 In addition, males have lower NCV (25–45 m/s in upper limb nerves) than females, who usually have normal or mildly slowed NCV.
12
 Conduction blocks are also observed in CMT1B, often due to developmental issues in internode formation, nodal‐paranodal regions, and myelin thickness.

The normal structure and function of peripheral nerves depend on the close anatomical and physiological interactions between Schwann cells and axons. Abnormalities in the genes that regulate myelin assembly and axonal transport lead to primary demyelination and axonopathy, respectively. CMT may occur due to one of the following molecular and cellular mechanisms: defects in the myelin assembly, cytoskeletal structure abnormalities, endosomal sorting and cell signaling defects, mitochondrial genes involved in the respiratory chain, proteasome and program aggregation, transcription defects, calcium homeostasis defects, and cell fusion‐fission apparatus defects. Abnormal myelin formations including “outfoldings” and occasionally “infoldings” occur in patients with MTMTR2 pathogenic variants.
13
 Abnormal extensions of Schwann cell cytoplasm is a pathologic hallmark for SH3TC2‐related CMT.
14



CMT is a progressive and diverse group of disorders characterized by genotypic and phenotypic variability, as well as variable expressivity. Progression of CMT1A appears to be stable throughout early childhood and adolescence; however, other subtypes progress faster during early childhood than adolescence.
15
 In CMT1A, symptoms typically emerge within the first decade of life.
16
, 
17
 Electrophysiological abnormalities can be detected in infancy, with distal motor latency prolongation observable within the first few months, followed by additional abnormalities. However, conduction slowing remains stable after childhood and does not correlate with disease severity.
16
, 
18
 Research on the natural history of other CMT subtypes is limited. In CMT2A, onset during childhood is often a predictor of more severe disease, linked to a higher incidence of foot deformities and earlier loss of ambulation. CMTX typically begins within the first two decades of life in males and follows a progressive course, while female carriers may be asymptomatic or exhibit only mild symptoms.
10
, 
19
 Comorbidities such as diabetes, exposure to chemotherapeutic agents like vincristine, and concurrent neuropathies can exacerbate the severity of the disease and negatively impact outcomes.
10



Autosomal dominant demyelinating CMT primarily includes CMT1 and its subtypes. This category includes at least 70% of all inherited neuropathy patients. Seven subtypes are identified with five causal genes. In CMT1, motor and sensory NCVs are reduced in the demyelinating range typically between 15 and 38 m/s. Both distal motor latencies and F wave latencies are prolonged, indicating uniform conductive slowing along the length of the nerve. Conduction velocity slowing is also uniform along the individual nerves and between different nerves in an individual patient, distinguishing CMT1 from other acquired demyelinating neuropathies such as AIDP (acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy) or CIDP (chronic inflammatory demyelinating Ppolyradiculoneuropathy).

CMT1A is a typical childhood‐onset form with predominant distal dominant weakness, sensory loss, limb deformities, and occasional central nervous system manifestations. CMT1A is about 50% all CMT and about 70% CMT1.
20
 CMT1A is caused by pathogenic variants in the peripheral myelin protein 22 gene (PMP22), and many of these cases are associated with 1.5 MB duplication within chromosome 17p11.2. PMP22 is a membrane glycoprotein and a component of the compact myelin sheath. It is expressed in Schwann cells of the peripheral nervous system. Overexpression of PMP22 leads to abnormal synthesis and function of the myelin sheath and altered Schwann cell growth and maturation. However, the exact molecular mechanism is not clear.
21
, 
22
 CMT1A also occurs with partial or complete trisomy of the short arm of chromosome 17 as part of a multiorgan phenotype with development delay, craniofacial abnormalities, and skeletal and cardiovascular involvement. Deletion in PMP22 causes hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies (HNPP). It is characterized by recurrent, often temporary, episodes of nerve compression or damage, typically triggered by minor trauma or pressure. Common symptoms include sudden onset of weakness, numbness, or pain in affected areas, frequently occurring in the arms or legs. Symptoms typically resolve within weeks to months after relieving the pressure or cessation of the triggering activity. The recovery period can vary depending on the severity and duration of the nerve compression, as well as the individual's overall health and the effectiveness of any treatments.
23
 Point pathogenic variants in PMP22 (CMT1E) cause infantile‐onset severe demyelinating phenotype (reminiscent of Dejerine‐Sottas syndrome) as well as mild phenotype resembling HNPP. An association of CMT1A and deafness has been linked to Try28Arg, T23R, Val65Phe pathogenic variants, and four amino acid– deletion at the border between the third membrane and the extracellular domains.
6
 Pathogenic variants in the PMP22 gene that affect the fourth membrane domain and result in a premature stop codon (cys109stop) can lead to a highly variable phenotype, ranging from asymptomatic cases to severe symptoms. The apparent age of onset in CMT1A is variable based on awareness of early manifestations. Thomas and colleagues found that 85% of patients developed clinical evidence before 20 years of age.
24
 Other CMT1A‐associated manifestations such as prolonged QT,
25
 hypertelorism, intractable epilepsy, audiovisual hallucinations,
26
 impaired speech perception,
27
 and sleep apnea have been reported. Tremor occurs in about 25% of patients with CMT1A.

CMT1B, the third most common demyelinating neuropathy after CMT1A and CMTX,
1
 is associated with pathogenic variants in the MPZ gene on chromosome 1q22, which encodes a protein called myelin protein zero.
28
 Myelin protein zero is responsible for maintaining myelin compaction and stability. CMT1C is due to pathogenic variants in the lipopolysaccharide‐induced tumor necrosis factor (LITAF) gene, also called small integral membrane protein of the lysosome/late endosome (SIMPLE).
29
 CMT1D is due to pathogenic variants in the zinc finger domain‐containing transcription factors early growth factor response 2 gene (EGR2) on chromosome 10q21.1‐q22.1.
30
 The same is implicated in congenital hypomyelination neuropathy. CMT1E is caused by point pathogenic variants in the PMP22 gene.
31
 CMT1F is caused by defects in the NEFL gene located on chromosome 8, which codes for the neurofilament light chain protein. The defective NEFL protein is thought to disrupt the assembly of neurofilaments, reducing the diameter of the nerve fiber and subsequently impairing the transmission of nerve impulses.
32
 CMT1G is a less common subtype caused by a pathogenic variant in the PMP2 gene, located on chromosome 8Q21.13, which encodes a protein important for peripheral nerve myelin stability. There is predominant lower extremity involvement in this subtype.

Axonal CMT (CMT2) accounts for approximately 30% of all genetically confirmed CMT subtypes. CMT2 is characterized by reduced motor amplitudes, normal or near normal NCV, loss of large, myelinated nerve fibers, and clusters of regenerating nerve fibers on nerve biopsy, all pointing toward primary axonal involvement. Distinguishing between CMT2 and CMT1 based solely on history and clinical examinations can be challenging, often necessitating NCS and genetic testing. To date, 30 subtypes and 33 causative genes have been identified, with 10 genes associated specifically with the intermediate type.
33



CMT2A is the most prevalent axonal form, caused by pathogenic variants in the mitofusin 2 (MFN2) gene. This is the most progressive type among all CMT subtypes and accounts for 15%–20% of CMT cases. MFN2 is a membrane protein that plays a critical role in mitochondrial fusion. MFN deficiency reduces mitochondrial mobility and leads to insufficient axonal transport of mitochondria. Patients can also have optic atrophy, hearing loss, diaphragmatic weakness, and vocal cord paralysis. CMT2B causes prominent severe distal sensory loss leading to foot ulcers, and it is associated with pathogenic variants in RAB7. CMT2C is caused by pathogenic variants in the TRPV4 (a phosphoribosyltransferase) gene. Diaphragmatic and vocal cord paralysis have been reported. CMT2D is caused by pathogenic variants in the GARS gene and is characterized by severe involvement of the upper extremities. In some individuals, particularly in children, the condition can be quite severe. CMT2J is caused by pathogenic variants in the MPZ gene, which cause late‐onset axonal neuropathy, with the age of onset between 30 and 50 years with variable slowed NCVs and axonal loss. Despite advances in genetic testing, patients with classic axonal type do not have identifiable genetic variants. These patients tend to have an older age of onset, and increasing evidence suggests that multiple variants of uncertain significance in different genes account for “idiopathic neuropathy” or complex neuropathy phenotype.

Autosomal recessive forms of CMT are rare, accounting for less than 10% of cases.
34
 These forms can be demyelinating, axonal, or intermediate and include conditions such as congenital hypomyelination neuropathy and Dejerine‐Sottas syndrome. They are more common in regions with high rates of consanguineous marriages. Typical features include onset before 3 years of age, rapid progression, significant distal deformities, progressive weakness, atrophy, and loss of ambulation by the third decade. Dejerine‐Sottas syndrome is associated with pathogenic variants in several genes, including PMP22 and MPZ, with many being point pathogenic variants. CMT4, a recessive demyelinating subtype, includes CMT4A, caused by pathogenic variants in GDAP1. GDAP1 pathogenic variants cause axonal recessive forms, intermediate recessive forms, and dominant forms of CMT. Recessive GDAP1 is often secondary to reduced mitochondrial fusion activity, whereas dominant GDAP1 is secondary to impaired mitochondrial fusion.
35
, 
36
 CMT4B and CMT4C are associated with MTMR2 and SH3TC2 genes, respectively.

CMTX is the second most common type of CMT, accounting for 10%–15% of all CMT subtypes.
5
, 
37
 CMT1X accounts for 90% of CMTX patients and is caused by pathogenic variants in the GJB1 (gap junction protein beta 1) gene located on Xq13.1. GJB1 encodes connexin 32, which is crucial for forming gap junctions in noncompact myelin at paranodal regions and Schmidt‐Lanterman incisures, thereby supporting myelinated axon homeostasis in oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells. In male patients with CMTX, symptoms include progressive muscle atrophy, weakness, frequent falls, areflexia, sensory loss, and variable CNS involvement. Affected females often experience later onset and may be asymptomatic despite abnormal NCVs. NCS typically shows intermediate velocities, mildly prolonged distal motor and F wave latencies, and distal axonal loss. Nerve biopsy reveals less prominent onion bulbs compared with CMT1.

CMT5 (HMSN5) is associated with spastic paraplegia, now known as complicated hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP), and is marked by predominant pyramidal signs. CMT6 is associated with optic atrophy, while CMT7 is associated with retinitis pigmentosa. Currently, CMT5, CMT6, and CMT7 are less commonly emphasized in clinical practice due to the gene‐cause designations.

Distal hereditary motor neuropathies (dHMN) are considered part of the CMT spectrum. Bansagi et al. found that pathogenic variants in the same genes can lead to both dHMN and CMT phenotypes, suggesting that dHMN and motor CMT should be viewed as similar conditions rather than distinct entities. dHMN typically shows no sensory nerve involvement.
38



When diagnosing CMT in children, it is crucial to consider a range of other conditions that may present with similar clinical features. A thorough differential diagnosis ensures accurate identification and appropriate management (Table 4). It is crucial to differentiate demyelinating CMT from acquired neuropathies like CIDP. CIDP is characterized by weakness in both distal and proximal extremities, fluctuating course, nonhomogenous conduction slowing, conduction blocks, and temporal dispersion in NCS. On the other hand, CMT is characterized by a slow, progressive course with skeletal deformities, distal predominant weakness, and usually homogenous conduction slowing in NCS. Inheritance patterns are also helpful in differentiating two diseases. However, diagnosis remains challenging in sporadic cases or in the presence of limited family history. In CMT, cerebrospinal fluid protein is usually normal or mildly elevated, whereas it is markedly elevated in CIDP.
39
 Anti–GM1 antibodies and oligoclonal bands are often detected in patients with CIDP. Yet CIDP and CMT1 can entirely mimic each other, and phenotype, cerebrospial fluid, and histopathology findings may overlap. Molecular studies are extremely helpful in these cases, and it is crucial to follow treatment response with clinical and electrophysiologic findings. Chronic idiopathic axonal neuropathies can mimic CMT2. Early‐onset disease and foot deformities are suggestive of CMT2.

Spinal muscular atrophy can mimic CMT due to its presentation of progressive muscle weakness and atrophy. However, SMA primarily affects the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord, leading to proximal muscle weakness and diminished deep tendon reflexes with preserved sensation,
40
 whereas CMT typically involves distal muscle weakness and sensory abnormalities. Distal myopathies can resemble CMT due to the presentation of muscle weakness and atrophy predominantly affecting the distal extremities. Distal myopathies are a diverse group of disorders with varying genetic causes. Differentiation is facilitated by electrophysiological evaluation, as nerve conduction studies in myopathies are usually normal, while EMG reveals myopathic changes. Confirmatory genetic testing is essential for an accurate diagnosis. Pes cavus observed in CMT can also be seen in other genetic disorders, such as HSP, and in ataxic disorders, including Friedreich's ataxia and spinocerebellar ataxias. HSP primarily affects the upper motor neurons, causing progressive weakness and spasticity.
41
 Ataxic disorders present with gait and balance issues that may overlap with CMT symptoms. However, key distinguishing features include ataxia, dysarthria, and other signs of cerebellar dysfunction. Diagnostic evaluation involving neuroimaging, and genetic testing is critical for accurate differentiation and diagnosis.

There is currently no disease‐modifying treatment for CMT. Clinical trials focusing on treatment, including genetic therapies, are rapidly emerging. Current management focuses on alleviating symptoms through pain relief, muscle cramps management, rehabilitation, and surgical intervention for skeletal deformities.
42



Pain is a frequent symptom in patients with CMT.
42
 Pain can be managed using medications such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors; tricyclic antidepressants; anticonvulsants including pregabalin, gabapentin, and carbamazepine; and topical anesthetics. Fatigue is another common symptom. Modafinil demonstrated some effectiveness in a small group, although it carries significant risks.
43
 The efficacy of amantadine is not well established.
42
 Quinine has been somewhat effective in relieving leg cramps, but the risks associated with its use outweigh the benefits.
44
 Pediatric CMT guidelines recommend muscle stretching for the treatment of muscle cramps.
45



There is evidence that mild to moderate exercise, including muscle strengthening, stretching, and balance exercises, is beneficial in CMT and can increase strength and improve balance.
46



The clinical practice guideline provides guidance on the management of common clinical problems experienced by children with CMT.
45
 It advocates for improved access to multidisciplinary care. Management recommendations for weakness, maintaining and improving joint range of motion, impaired mobility and balance, muscle cramps, joint and spine deformity, sensory symptoms, fatigue, impaired upper limb function, and respiratory weakness are discussed.
45



Pulmonary function testing is recommended when it can be completed reliably in children with symptoms of sleep‐disordered breathing, recurrent lower respiratory tract infections (defined as more than two courses of antibiotics over 4 months or more than two hospital admissions for a respiratory tract infection in 12 months), and scoliosis with a Cobb angle of >40°, as well as nonambulatory children. Children at high risk for respiratory issues should be referred for evaluation. Additionally, a formal swallowing assessment is recommended for children with recurrent respiratory infections and/or a history of aspiration.
45



Approximately 20% of CMT patients undergo foot surgery to realign the foot and alleviate imbalance and pain. Surgery may be indicated in some patients following multidisciplinary evaluation and after nonsurgical treatments, such as bracing and stretching, have failed. Fixed deformities are more challenging to address, and potential treatments include osteotomies, joint fusions, and soft tissue surgeries. However, managing fixed deformities surgically is technically more demanding.
47
, 
48



Although there is no curative drug treatment for CMT at present, several clinical trials are underway. A phase 3, international, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study examined the efficacy and safety of high‐dose (6/0.70/210 mg) and low‐dose (3/0.35/105 mg) PXT3003 (baclofen, naltrexone, and D‐sorbitol) in treating patients with mild to moderate CMT1A. This study demonstrated statistically significant improvements in the Overall Neuropathy Limitations Scale and a favorable safety profile in the high‐dose group.
49
 Progesterone can increase in vitro expression of PMP22 and MPZ. The progesterone antagonist onapristone was found to be too toxic for humans, and new studies are ongoing to test another derivative of a progesterone antagonist.
50
 Neurotrophin 3 (NT‐3) was shown to augment axonal nerve regeneration in animal models.
51
, 
52
, 
53
 Eight patients with CMT1A received intradermal NT‐3 in a double‐blind, placebo‐controlled pilot trial and showed some improvements in sensory loss. Gene therapy delivery is still in the preclinical stage.
42
 Ascorbic acid is shown to reduce PMP22 expression by reducing cAMP levels. In a randomized controlled trial with 81 CMT1A patients, no significant difference was observed between the treated group and placebo groups.
54
 Therefore, ascorbic acid is not commonly used.
45
 Curcumin has been shown to decrease apoptosis and increase axonal size in clinical trials.
