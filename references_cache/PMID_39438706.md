Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC) is the most common malignant salivary tumor. A 
complete understanding of the high heterogeneity of MEC in histology and 
genetics would help in accurate diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, We evaluated 
the clinical features, treatment outcomes, and pathological parameters of 454 
MECs and analyzed their genomic features using whole-exome sequencing and 
whole-transcriptome sequencing. We found that MECs predominantly occurred in 
females and those in their 4th-5th decades. The parotid gland was the most 
frequently affected site. All patients underwent complete mass resection with 
lobectomy; 414 patients were alive without relapse at follow-up, after an 
average period of 62 months (1-116 months). The disease progressed after initial 
treatment in 40 patients. The lungs were the most common site of distant 
metastasis. For classical MECs, histologic gradings of the AFIP, modified 
Healey, and MSK systems were significantly associated with recurrence and lymph 
nodal metastasis; these gradings were significantly related to lymph nodal 
metastasis for the subtypes. Older age, minor salivary gland involvement, 
clinical symptoms, high TNM stage, high-grade tumor, and improper surgical 
modality were the main prognostic factors. BAP1 was the most frequently mutated 
gene in MEC. Mutations in CDKN2A, MET, and TP53 were more frequently found in 
aggressive tumor phenotypes. MAML2 rearrangement was observed in 42% of 
patients, and EWSR1 rearrangement in 8%. Specific genetic events (in TP53 and 
FBXW7) with CRTC1::MAML2 fusion superimposed might be associated with 
unfavorable prognosis. This study provides new insights into precision 
therapeutic strategies for MEC.

Salivary mucoepidermoid carcinomas (MECs) account for ~30–40% of all malignant tumors of the major salivary glands1. MEC is a highly heterogeneous neoplasm. Multiple variant forms of MEC, including oncocytic2, ciliated, sclerosing3, Warthin-like4, uni-cystic5, calcifying6, and mucoacinar7 types, have broadened the histologic spectrum of this cancer type. Histological grading is known to influence MEC prognosis8. Four histological grading systems are used to classify MEC and predict prognosis; these systems are broadly classified into two types: qualitative (the Healey grading system and the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSK) system) and quantitative (the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology (AFIP) grading system and the Brandwein system)9–12. The variations in histological features between types and the use of different grading systems make it difficult to summarize the effects of histological type on outcomes. In addition, owing to subjectivity in the grading criteria, a large retrospective study is needed to compare the value of the four grading systems in predicting the prognosis of classical MEC and MEC subtypes and to better guide treatment.

Advancements in understanding MEC pathogenesis have focused on genetic alterations such as the CRTC1::MAML2 translocation present in 50–70% of patients; the frequency of this change was higher in low- and intermediate-grade tumors. However, no significant correlation between translocation status and lymph node metastasis or recurrence was found13. Beyond CRTC1::MAML2 translocation, little is known about the MEC genomic profile. Further analysis of mutations using next-generation sequencing (NGS) techniques may help identify novel MEC prognostic markers.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the clinical and histological features and therapeutic sensitivity of salivary MEC and determine the prognostic factors related to lymph node metastasis and overall survival (OS). Furthermore, the relationships between genetic profiles based on whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS) and pathologic manifestations and clinical prognosis were explored.

Four hundred and fifty-four cases of MEC were identified among 1900 salivary carcinoma cases from 2010 to 2020. A total of 264 patients were female (58%), and 190 were male (42%). The male:female ratio was 1:1.39. The age at diagnosis ranged from 7 to 82 years (mean age: 42.65 years), with ~64% of these cases presenting in patients 40–50 years of old. In total, 43% of carcinomas were present in the major salivary glands; 49% of the carcinomas were in the minor salivary gland, and 8% were in the jawbones. The most frequently affected sites were the parotid gland (35%), palate (27%), and tongue (6%); 55% of the patients presented no overt clinical symptoms. The most frequent chief complaint was pain (27%), followed by rapid growth (6%). The MEC patients comprised 431 nonsmokers and 23 smokers (Table 1). A total of 414 patients were alive at follow-up without relapse or metastasis after an average period of 62 months (1–116 months). Forty (8.8%) patients experienced disease progression after the initial treatment, with an average follow-up period of 37 months (2–114 months). Nineteen (4.2%) patients developed local recurrence, and 21 (4.6%) died of distant organ metastasis, including metastasis to the lung (n = 10), brain (n = 5), neck (n = 3), liver (n = 2), and bone (n = 1). The 5- and 10-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates were 91.9% and 82.5%, respectively. The detailed RFS data for the four grading systems are shown in Fig. 2b–e. Of the classical MEC cases, 26 (9%) showed recurrence. Of the clear cell MECs, nine (15%) recurred. No recurrence was observed in oncocytic or calcified MECs. Of the 2/12 (17%) sclerosing MECs, 2/11 (18%) uni-cystic MECs and 1/31 (3%) Warthin-like MECs recurred. No significant differences in recurrence rate were observed between the histological subtypes (p = 0.213).

Upon comparing the clinical and treatment modalities between salivary MEC patients with and without recurrence, we found that age, TNM stage, tumor location, and surgical modality were significantly different between patients with and without recurrence, and postoperative management and sex were not significantly different between patients with recurrence (Table 2). In terms of the pathological features, recurrence was associated with a cystic component of <20% (p = 0.011), perineural invasion (p = 0.003), necrosis (p = <0.001), mitosis (p = <0.001), anaplasia (p = <0.001), lymphovascular invasion (p = <0.001), and bone invasion (p = 0.003) but not with invasive growth (p = 0.369) or T stage (p = 0.138). Furthermore, patients were grouped based on MEC type (classical MECs and MEC subtypes), and there was a significant difference in the number of cases with and without recurrence between patients grouped by AFIP, modified Healey, and MSK grade but not Brandwein grade. For the MEC histological subtypes, none of the systems showed significant difference in the number of cases with recurrence between groups. Patients were grouped based on MEC type (classical MEC and MEC histological subtypes), and there were significant differences in terms of the number of cases with and without lymph node metastasis for patients grouped by AFIP, modified Healey, and MSK grade but not by Brandwein grade (Table 3). To our knowledge, our sample size is comparable to or larger than those in previous studies14–17. In our series, most MECs occurred in patients 40–50 years old, slightly younger than the range reported by Chen et al.18. The incidence was slightly higher in the minor salivary glands (49%) than in the major salivary glands (43%). There was a lower percentage of patients with lymph node metastasis (10.6%), local recurrence (4.2%), and distant organ metastasis (4.6%) in this cohort vs. another MEC cohort, in which 22.2% of patients had lymph node metastasis, 19.5% had local recurrence, and 8.5% had distant organ metastasis19. Older age, minor salivary gland involvement, clinical symptoms, high TNM stage, and high AFIP grade were associated with recurrence and could indicate a need for more aggressive treatment.

In this study, we also described the most comprehensive genetic profile of patients with salivary MECs by WES and WTS. In previous studies, commercial array-based comparative genomic hybridization (arrayCGH) panels only explored a very limited part of the coding genomic DNA20,21. Through advancements in sequencing technology, whole-genome and transcriptome sequencing have emerged as comprehensive approaches to identifying novel mutations and chromosomal alterations. CRTC1::MAML2 translocation occurs in almost half of tumors and is a driver of MEC tumorigenesis22. Notably, we found no correlation between fusion status and tumor grade or survival (Table 4). In a previous study, CRTC1::MAML2 fusion was restricted to low- and intermediate-grade MEC, and fusion could be regarded as a biomarker of favorable prognosis23. However, our data and other retrospective studies have shown that translocation is present in high-grade MECs24. In this study, genetic events associated with CRTC1::MAML2 were identified in salivary MECs; these events might cause progression and are associated with an unfavorable prognosis. One MAML2 fusion-positive patient with a concurrent TP53 mutation (tumor sample 20) developed distal metastases to the brain and eventually died. CRTC1::MAML2 remains recognized a driver, and additional molecular abnormalities, such as those involving TP53, have emerged as codrivers. TP53 is the most frequently altered gene in human cancers and has been extensively studied. Abd-Elhamid and Elmalahy25 and Morita et al.26 demonstrated that 80% of primary and 100% of recurrent salivary MEC cases show positive immunostaining for p53, which indicates that aberrant p53 expression might be an early event in MEC carcinogenesis and may be correlated with local recurrence. Wang et al.21. demonstrated that TP53 is the most commonly mutated gene in MEC (28%, 5/18). Another MAML2 fusion-positive patient with concurrent FBXW7 mutation (tumor sample 29) had lymph node metastasis. FBXW7 encodes a member of the F-box protein family and functions as a tumor suppressor27. However, to date, no study has assessed the function of FBXW7 in salivary MEC. Future work is necessary to assess whether the FBXW7 mutation is a stochastic genetic mutation or plays a vital role in the tumorigenesis of MAML2 fusion-positive MEC patients. Other studies describing CDKN2A mutations in a subset of MAML2 fusion-positive MEC patients with poor outcomes have also been reported28. Moreover, an increasing number of precision oncology studies have demonstrated the presence of multiple codrivers in other solid cancers29. For example, RET fusions are an important oncogenic driver of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Among RET-rearranged NSCLCs, 41.1%–72.3% harbored concurrent alterations, and concomitant TP53 oncogenic alterations appeared most frequently (37.7%–42.5%)30. RET-rearranged NSCLC patients with concurrent TP53 mutations have relatively inferior OS31. Taken together, these results suggest that MAML2 fusion is not an independent prognostic factor in patients with salivary MEC. Analysis of the presence of codriver mutations and administration of a matched targeted agent in solid tumors might be crucial to increasing the success of targeted treatment.

The most frequently mutated gene identified in these analyses was BAP1, although its mutations occurred in low- and high-grade MEC tumors. We also identified mutations in MET, TP53, CDKN2A/2B, and RB1 in more than one tumor. BAP1 has important functions in tumor suppression32 and has been reported in various cellular processes, including the DNA damage response33, chromosome stability34, cell metabolism, cellular differentiation35, and immune regulation36. Of the four tumors with BAP1 mutations in our study, two were substitutions and two were truncations. BAP1 mutations have been previously reported in 20% of MEC cases, all of which were truncation mutations21. This finding was inconsistent with the results of one study wherein none of the MEC cases showed a loss of BAP1 expression by immunohistochemistry. Our NGS data can be used to confirm this negative result. The genetic profile of BAP1 differed not only in the truncation but also in the substitution. Moreover, some unknown epigenetic changes in BAP1 may have led to the restoration of BAP1 expression despite the BAP1 mutation.

An interesting finding of this study was that the BAP1 mutation did not often cooccur with the mutation of one tumor suppressor gene, CDKN2A. There was one case of advanced MEC that exhibited mutation of both drivers. The patient (tumor sample 19) experienced local recurrence postoperatively. CDKN2A is a major tumor suppressor gene that encodes p16. Dysregulation of CDKN2A is a frequent event in the development of solid tumors. In our series, CDKN2A losses/deletions occurred in 8% of salivary MECs, a frequency that was lower than that in other adenocarcinomas, such as lung adenocarcinoma (19.1%)37. Anzick et al.38. found CDKN2A deletions in 5/5 unfavorable MEC cases. In humans, BAP1 is a two-hit tumor suppressor gene; mutations are accompanied by monoallelic loss of 3p or biallelic deletions of the BAP1 locus (LOH), including broad deletions of 3p21, narrow deletions of several exons, or loss of the entire BAP1 allele39. The biological and clinical implications of the overlapping mutation of BAP1 and CDKN2A are currently unclear. Further large cohort studies are needed to assess the relationship between BAP1 and CDKN2A in clinical prognosis.

The other three patients with BAP1 mutations all had high-grade MECs. Two of these cases showed concomitant translocation of MAML2; of these patients, Patient 18 showed lymph node metastasis, while the other (Patient 6) did not. Another patient with a BAP1 mutation (Patient 30) harbored lymph node metastasis and was negative for MAML2 translocation. In this study, we demonstrated that MEC is a highly heterogeneous carcinoma. Among those with the same histological grade, MEC patients with different molecular alterations presented different prognoses.

Other mutated genes, including MET, POLE, and RB1, were also identified in our study and may be potential therapeutic targets for MEC patients. Clinical investigations have demonstrated that targeted agents directed against these driver and resistance mutations could improve the clinical outcomes of patients harboring these mutations. MET encodes the receptor tyrosine kinase c-MET and can stimulate the MAPK and PI3K pathways, thereby triggering cell migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis40. RB1, the first human tumor suppressor gene to be described, encodes the protein pRb, which plays a critical role in cell cycle progression, genomic instability, apoptosis, and metabolic reprogramming41. POLE encodes the catalytic and proofreading subunits of DNA polymerase and plays an important role in DNA replication and proofreading; however, its function in tumorigenesis is still controversial42. The role of these mutations in MEC tumorigenesis requires further basic and clinical research.

MECs also arise at sites other than the salivary glands, such as the trachea, bronchus, breast, pancreas, thyroid gland, and liver. Primary pulmonary mucoepidermoid carcinoma (PMEC) is a rare neoplasm that accounts for <1% of all lung carcinomas. CK7, Muc5Ac, p40, and p63 were positive in PMEC and salivary gland MEC, and MAML2 rearrangement was identified in 66%–100% of PMEC cases43,44. By a comprehensive genomic profiling analysis, CDKN2A, TP53, and PIK3CA alterations and PI3K pathway activation in high-grade PMEC tumors were found to be higher than their frequencies in low-grade tumors. EGFR gene abnormalities (exon 21) were detected in 25% of PMEC45. Primary MECs of the thyroid gland are extremely rare, accounting for 0.5% of thyroid malignancies46. Unlike salivary gland MEC, MAML2 translocations are not present in thyroid MEC. A few reports have noted the presence of BRAF p.V600E and TERT C228T mutations in thyroid MEC47. Primary hepatic mucoepidermoid carcinoma (HMEC) is also rare. Aside from the CRTC1::MAML2 fusion gene, WTS indicated germline mutations in Fanconi’s anemia genes (FANCA, FANCI, FANCJ/BRIP1, and FAN1) and somatic GNAS R201H mutations in primary HMECs48. Differences in the embryonic origins of MECs may be a confounding variable with unknown implications. The major salivary glands and most minor salivary glands are derived from the ectoderm. The mucous glands of the pulmonary bronchi, thyroid, and liver are derived from the entoderm49. The embryonic origins of MECs may be considered a reason for the unique molecular spectrum of MECs.

A major advantage of our study is that we comprehensively described the clinicopathological characteristics and the molecular characteristics of MECs in a large cohort. In addition, to investigate the biological features of MEC, all samples were sequenced by high-throughput sequencing methods, including WES and whole-transcriptome analysis. This technology possesses high throughput and high accuracy. At the same time, the association between gene expression profiles and the clinicopathological features of MEC patients was statistically analyzed. We found that the MAML2 fusion status had no impact on prognosis or histological grade. CRTC1::MAML2 remains a driver mutation in MEC, and additional molecular abnormalities emerge and act as codrivers, such as TP53. Gene comutations may contribute to cancer progression. This study also has several limitations. First, due to the limited number of samples available for molecular analysis in the present study, a future study with an expanded sample size and analysis of other public databases is needed for a comprehensive view of the gene expression profiles of MEC. Second, the molecular features of different histopathological subtypes (such as clear cell, Warthin-like, oncocytic, sclerosing, uni-cystic, and calcifying MEC) were not investigated. Third, the underlying mechanisms of these interactions between gene mutations and invasive biological behaviors need to be explored by more fundamental studies.

In conclusion, we analyzed a large series of salivary MECs by comprehensively assessing their clinicopathological features and molecular changes. High pathological grade based on the AFIP, Healey, and MSK grading systems was significantly associated with lymph node metastasis. The lung is the most common site of distant metastasis. Older age, minor salivary gland involvement, clinical symptoms, high TNM stage, and high AFIP grade were associated with recurrence and could indicate a need for more aggressive treatment. BAP1 was the most frequently mutated gene, and CRTC1::MAML2 was the most frequently mutated fusion gene in MEC. Mutations in CDKN2A, MET (8%), and TP53 (8%) were more frequently found in aggressive tumor phenotypes. Single MAML2 fusion had no impact on prognosis or histological grade. The cooccurrence of specific genetic events (affecting TP53 and FBXW7) with CRTC1::MAML2 fusion might cause MEC progression. Recognition of the high heterogeneity of MECs could be helpful in the formulation of personalized treatments.

All primary salivary gland malignancies at Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology were retrieved with institutional ethics approval (PKUSSIRB-201948111). The study was conducted according to the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients in this study provided written informed consent for sample collection and data analyses. H&E-stained slides were independently reviewed by two head and neck pathologists in a double-blinded manner. An MEC was defined as a distinctive salivary gland malignancy composed of mucinous, intermediate, and squamoid tumor cells forming cystic and solid patterns1. Metastatic MEC from other primary lesions was excluded. Among them, 28 fresh frozen tissues were subjected to WES and WTS.

Clinical characteristics were obtained from the electronic medical records, including age at time of diagnosis, sex, tumor site, histologic verification, surgical treatment (complete mass resection with lobectomy, with or without ipsilateral or bilateral neck dissection and adjuvant treatment, such as radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and/or I125 seed implantation). The tumors were staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual, 8th edition50. The status at the last follow-up was classified as follows: no evidence of disease (NED), alive with disease (AWD), and died of disease (DOD). Clinical follow-up information was available for all patients. DFS was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of first relapse, second primary malignancy or death resulting from any cause. RFS was defined as the follow-up interval calculated from surgery date to date of the first recurrence51.

MEC cases were classified as follows: classical, clear cell, Warthin-like, oncocytic, sclerosing, uni-cystic MEC, and calcifying MEC. In addition, the recently described ciliated and mucoacinar variants were included in the variant list. We defined the most predominant subtype as the histological subtype. The case was considered a classical variant when >50% of the tumor area showed classical MEC histology. OMEC and clear cells of MEC were required to have >50% oncocytic or clear cell elements52. Warthin-like MEC cases histologically consisted of the diversity of cystic lining oncocytic epithelium and dense lymphoid stroma, with epithelial components arranged in a papillary cystic pattern4. Uni-cystic MEC was proposed when the lesion was showing uni-cystic architecture and was composed of varying proportions of epidermoid cells, mucous cells, and intermediate cells5. Sclerosing MEC contains extensive hyalinizing sclerosis and lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate53. Calcifying MEC contains an amount of calcified material in the tumor mass54. All tumor cases were histologically graded according to the AFIP55, Brandwein10, modified Healey56, and MSK11 grading systems.

DNA and RNA were isolated from 28 frozen fresh MEC samples and paired adjacent normal salivary gland tissue samples using a QIAamp FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). A negative control copy number and somatic mutation baseline were constructed using normal salivary gland tissue obtained from patients. The samples were then subjected to NGS-based WES using OrigiMed (Shanghai, China). Genes were captured and sequenced to a mean depth of 800× using an Illumina Nova system. Gene fusions were detected using a pipeline developed in-house. Gene rearrangements were assessed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer. Gene fusions at the transcript level were detected using STAR fusion. The clinical and pathological information is listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Salivary MEC patients with lymph node, local recurrence or distal metastasis were classified into the “progression group (group 1: sample 2, 13, 18, 19, 20, 29, 30),” whereas the others were classified into the “non-progression group (group 2: sample 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 16, 17, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 28).” The gene expression level was compared between the progression group and non-progression group; differential expression analysis was performed with the “limma” package of “RStudio” software and DESeq2 v1.18.1. The heatmap for analyzing different genes was generated using the “pheatmap” package of “RStudio” software. GO and DAVID analysis of the selected differential genes was performed. The samples were subjected to principal component and clustering analyses based on unsupervised learning. Transcription factors of the target genes were generated from the ENCODE clustered transcription factor binding site database. GSEA was performed using GSEA v4.03 software (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp). Pre-ranked gene lists were selected from the Molecular Signatures Database v7.0, including hallmark gene sets, GO BP gene sets, and oncogenic signature gene sets. Significant enrichment was defined as NOM.p.val < 0.05 and df$FDR.q.val < 0.25.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac (version 26.0). The groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum, Chi-square, or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the t-test for discrete variables. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier, Mann–Whitney U test, log-rank, and Life Table methods. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant.
