INTRODUCTION 33 
YWHAG Syndrome , or Developmental and Epileptic Encephalopathy 56 (DEE56; OMIM 34 
#617665), is a n ultra -rare genetic condition presenting with  early-onset epilepsy, including epileptic 35 
encephalopathy (EE) and drug-resistant seizures, and accompanied by neurodevelopmental delays1-5. The 36 
Epi4K Consortium & Epilepsy Phenome/Genome Project study confirmed that de novo mutations in the 37 
YWHAG gene, encoding for the 14-3-3γ protein, are causative for YWHAG Syndrome1. Critically, little is 38 
known about the molecular mechanisms underlying YWHAG Syndrome, and no therapeutic interventions 39 
are available to patients.  40

The 14-3- 3γ protein ( Tyrosine 3- Monooxygenase/Tryptophan 5- Monooxygenase Activation 41 
Protein Gamma) is a member of the highly conserved 14-3-3 family of proteins. The 14-3-3s contribute to 42 
various biological functions, including  signal transduction mediation through binding to phosphorylated 43 
serine and threonine motifs on target peptides6. 14-3-3 dimers bind target proteins at a structural U-shaped 44 
groove consisting of nine alpha-helical structures7. Expression of the 14-3-3 protein family in the brain is 45 
ubiquitous, with isoforms varying across cell types and developmental stages 8. 14-3-3γ, encoded by 46 
YWHAG, is one of the predominant neuronal isoforms of 14-3- 3 during development9. 14-3-3γ is highly 47 
expressed during the embryonic stage in mice until P7, when expression gradually decreases, reaching a 48 
minimal level by P30 10. 14-3-3γ play s a crucial role in neuronal migration during this window of early 49 
cortical development, and Ywhag ablation results in delays in neuronal migration and morphological 50 
defects10,11. YWHAG also interacts with major neurodevelopmental signaling proteins , including 51 
components of the ROCK pathway, RAF1, and Protein Kinase C (PKC) 12,13, suggesting a role in neurite 52 
outgrowth and cytoskeletal rearrangements. Multiple missense poi nt mutations in YWHAG have been 53 
identified as disease-causing, with the most severe located within the binding groove of 14-3-3γ4,5. 54 
Here, we set out to address the molecular and cellular phenotypes resulting from the YWHAGR132C 55 
mutation using an isogenic model of human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). We differentiated and 56 
characterized NGN2 -derived cortical excitatory neurons, profiling changes to cytoskeletal stability, 57 
transcriptomics, calcium homeostasis, calcium event amplitude and frequency, and network excitability.  58 
We uncovered a YWHAGR132C neuronal phenotype of cytoskeletal instability and an elevated baseline 59 
calcium level, associated with reduced spike frequency and amplitude. We found that the ROCK pathway 60 
modulates a biphasic response to YWHAGR132C, first increasing and later suppressing the expression of focal 61 
actin genes. Critically, treatment with lovastatin stabilized the cytoskeleton and decoupled the calcium 62 
baseline from calcium spike amplitude and frequency in YWHAGR132C/+ neurons, supporting a link between 63 
cytoskeletal and calci um homeostasis. The characterization of cellular and molecular  YWHAGR132C 64 
phenotypes lays the groundwork for future mechanistic studies of  YWHAG function and YWHAG-65 
associated conditions.  66

Data and statistical analysis  191 
All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates  (defined as “batches”), 192 
in separate rounds of iPSC thawing, plating, culture, neuronal differentiation, and maintenance, unless 193 
noted. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio version 2024.09.1+394 and R (R Foundation 194 
for Statistical Computing,  version 4.4.2 ). To enable batch‑aware comparisons, all analyses explicitly 195 
tracked biological replicate (“batch”), well, site, and unique ROI identifiers. 196 
Calcium datasets were analyzed as follows: f or the calcium baseline analysis, the first frame of  each site 197 
and ROI was used to quantify intensity levels at the single ROI level for visualization, and the results were 198 
analyzed in aggregate at the well level . Baseline fluorescence levels were z-score normalized within each 199 
batch to account for batch-to-batch variability. To account for initial fluorescent decay and variability, an 200 
additional early -window metric was obtained by averaging frames 1 -5 and then used for downstream  201 
statistical testing. For analysis of event frequency and amplitude, the fluorescence time series was converted 202 
to ΔF/F using a rolling mean baseline . To reduce sensitivity to slow baseline drift, a rolling baseline f or 203 
each ROI, computed over a fixed window (30 frames), was established, and ΔF/F was calculated. Calcium 204 
events were then detected at the  ROI level by applying a rolling  median and median absolute deviation 205 
(MAD). Calcium events were identified as local maxima exceeding a threshold defined as rolling 206 
median + 4×MAD.  To prevent repeated event counting, we defined a minimum refractory period of 5  207 
frames. Calcium event frequency (in Hz) was defined as the total number of detected events divided by the 208 
30-second recording, and calcium event amplitude was defined as the median ΔF/F  value across detected 209 
events within each ROI. ROIs with no detected events were retained for frequency analysis but excluded 210 
from amplitude analysis.  211

YWHAG Syndrome develop early-onset seizures that eventually lead  to developmental abnormalities2-4. 257

but changes in calcium homeostasis, which modulate neuronal excitability, are frequently observed in 259 
developmental epilepsies24. To assess the functional phenotypes of differentiated YWHAGR132C/+ neurons, 260 
we performed calcium imaging using the Fluo- 4 AM calcium indicator  (Figure 2a). The YWHAG R132C/+ 261 
neurons exhibited a consistently elevated calcium baseline, evident as early as d.i.v. 18 in culture an d 262

DISCUSSION 371 
YWHAG Syndrome poses a challenge for disease modeling due to the limited availability of 372 
clinical samples and the small patient population. Further, the role of the 14-3-3 family, including 14-3-3γ, 373 
as scaffolding proteins during cytoskeletal assembly and cellular homeostasis makes it challenging to parse 374 
the effects of YWHAG mutations on neuronal function. In this study, to better understand the downstream 375 
effects of the YWHAGR132C mutation on neuronal function, growth, and maturation, we differentiated iPSC-376 
derived YWHAGR132C/+ cortical neurons  and paired isogenic controls.  NGN2-induced differentiation of 377 
YWHAGR132C/+ revealed maturation -associated phenotypes of cytoskeletal instability , with increased 378 
baseline calcium, and decreased event frequency and amplitude. Although most epileptic phenotypes can 379 
be associated with hyperexcitability, a higher calcium baseline can lead to network hypoexcitability, 380 
resulting in an epileptic phenotype, such as in the case of interneurons in SCN1A and loss-of-function (LOF) 381 
mutations in Dravet Syndrome33, and reduced excitability in cortical excitatory neurons in LOF SCN2A34.    382 
Transcriptomic analysis of both early and matured Y WHAGR132C/+ revealed a biphasic involvement 383 
of the ROCK pathway, initially as an enforcer of cytoskeletal rigidity, and later as a stabilizer in a 384 
compromised cytoskeletal state. Direct inhibition of the ROCK pathway further shifted the YWHAGR132C-385 
associated calcium baseline away from the control. However, treatment with lovastatin for 1  hour and 30 386 
minutes was sufficient to partially rescue the calcium baseline phenotype towards control, but did not 387 
restore the frequency or amplitude of calcium events. ROCK inhibition for 12 hours did not alter 14-3-3γ, 388 
cytoskeletal, or HSP70 protein levels, whereas  treatment with lovastatin slightly reduced 14 -3-3γ protein 389

Lovastatin is an inhibitor of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase and 394 
is known under the generic names Mevacor and Altoprev 35. It is an FDA -approved drug for 395 
hypercholesterolemia, but it also attracted  interest as a potential anti- seizure drug candidate when it was 396 
shown to decrease inflammatory markers during the epileptogenesis period in the hippocampal region of 397 
epileptic rats 36, suggesting a potential neuroprotective effect after status epilepticus. By inhibiting 398 
HMG‑CoA reductase, lovastatin reduces prenylation, the synthesis of isoprenoids like farnesyl and 399 
geranylgeranyl that are essential for the prenylation of small GTPases, includi ng the Rho family37-39. 400 
Finally, lovastatin has been shown to inhibit L -type calcium channels in smooth muscle cells, directly  401 
modulating calcium homeostasis 32. Lovastatin’s role in multiple cellular pathways beyond cholesterol 402 
synthesis and blood-brain barrier permeability has expanded its potential use beyond hypercholesterolemia 403 
to neurological and neurodegenerative diseases, including Fragile X syndrome and Parkinson’s disease40,41. 404 
Of particular note, in mouse models of Fragile X syndrome, lovastatin had a beneficial effect while 405 
Simvastatin did not show a marked effect42. We found that lovastatin modulates YWHAGR132C phenotypes, 406 
particularly calcium homeostasis, where it decouples the calcium baseline from calcium spike frequency 407 
and amplitude, and cytoskeletal stability. This modulation of both calcium and cytoskeletal phenotypes by 408 
lovastatin suggests potential druggable nodes that can be targeted for YWHAG S yndrome and broader 409 
conditions associated with YWHAG dysfunction.  410 
The broader effects of 14-3-3γ-dependent regulation of neural function are evident in conditions 411 
such as Down Syndrome (DS) and Williams Syndrome, in which direct involvement of 14-3-3γ has been 412 
established. Individuals with fetal DS exhibit significantly downregulated 14-3- 3γ expression in the 413 
cortex43, whereas aged DS patients show increased protein abundance in several brain regions, including  414 
the cortex 44. Williams Syndrome, a neurodevelopmental condition affecting multiple organs 45, presents 415 
atypically when the deletion of chromosome 7q11.23 i ncludes YWHAG46. Patients carrying the YWHAG 416 
deletion suffer from epilepsy, which is not a symptom in the typical clinical presentation47. Strikingly, there 417 
is also strong evidence of the 14 -3-3γ engagement in neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s and 418 
Parkinson’s. In mouse models, 14-3-3γ appears to promote α-synuclein aggregation48 and co-localizes with 419 
Lewy bodies 49; remarkably, there is an indirect link whereby lovastatin can al leviate α -synuclein 420 
aggregation50. In Alzheimer’s disease patients, the expression of the YWHAG protein is increased  in the 421 
cortex of aged patients44, while it also co-localizes with neurofibrillary tangles in the hippocampal region51. 422 
Nonetheless, the precise role of 14-3-3γ in those progressive conditions remains to be elucidated. 423 
In conclusion, we have described an isogenic iPSC-derived cellular model of YWHAG Syndrome, 424 
allowing for the study of functional and molecular outcomes associated with 14 -3-3γ mutations. We have 425 
found that YWHAGR132C/+ neurons have disrupted cytoskeletal homeostasis, which is maintained via ROCK. 426 
Inhibition of ROCK leads to a critical homeostatic failure, and this perturbed state can be stabilized by 427 
lovastatin. Further, stabilization with lovastatin decouples the calci um baseline from calcium event 428 
frequency and amplitude, indicating a YWHAG-associated, cytoskeletal -dependent, reconfiguration of 429 
calcium homeostasis. Together, these results establish testable molecular pathways and therapeutic targets 430 
for YWHAG Syndrome and YWHAG-associated disorders. 431
