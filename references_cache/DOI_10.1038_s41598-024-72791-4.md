# Establishment of human pluripotent stem cell-derived cortical neurosphere model to study pathomechanisms and chemical toxicity in Kleefstra syndrome

In the present study, we aimed to establish and characterize a mature cortical spheroid model 
system for Kleefstra syndrome (KS) using patient-derived iPSC. We identified key differences in the 
growth behavior of KS spheroids determined by reduced proliferation marked by low Ki67 and high 
E-cadherin expression. Conversely, in the spheroid-based neurite outgrowth assay KS outperformed 
the control neurite outgrowth due to higher BDNF expression. KS spheroids were highly enriched 
in VGLUT1/2-expressing glutamatergic and ChAT-expressing cholinergic neurons, while TH-positive 
catecholamine neurons were significantly underrepresented. Furthermore, high NMDAR1 expression 
was also detected in the KS spheroid, similarly to other patients-derived neuronal cultures, denoting 
high NMDAR1 expression as a general, KS-specific marker. Control and KS neuronal progenitors 
and neurospheres were exposed to different toxicants (paraquat, rotenone, bardoxolone, and 
doxorubicin), and dose-response curves were assessed after acute exposure. Differentiation stage 
and compound-specific differences were detected with KS neurospheres being the most sensitive to 
paraquat. Altogether this study describes a robust 3D model system expressing the disease-specific 
markers and recapitulating the characteristic pathophysiological traits. This platform is suitable for 
testing developing brain-adverse environmental effects interactions, drug development, and screening 
towards individual therapeutic strategies.

Rare neurodevelopmental disorders represent a field where the current animal models are very limited in 
recapitulating the human developing brain, thus seriously limiting our understanding of the vital interactions 
of the altered fetal genotype and adverse environmental effects during pregnancy potentially contributing to the 
post-natal severity of the syndromes. The overall aim of this study is to demonstrate that in vitro patient-derived 
cellular systems are suitable tools to provide valuable information filling the gap in the fetal-environmental 
interactions and potentially delivering insights on druggable targets and drugs in rare neurodevelopmental 
diseases.
Kleefstra syndrome (KS), initially called the 9q34 subtelomeric deletion syndrome is caused by loss-of-
function mutations in the euchromatin histone methyl transferase 1 (EHMT1) or the deletion of a specific region 
on chromosome 9 that includes EHMT1 leading to haploinsufficiency of this gene1,2. EHMT1, also known as G9a-

like protein (GLP), forms a heteromeric complex with G9a and is responsible for the mono- and di-methylation 
of histone H3lysine 9 (H3K9) conferring repressive marks that are non-permissive for transcription 3,4. KS is 
defined by distinct facial features, microcephaly, developmental delay, intellectual disability, low muscle tone, 
and communication difficulties often accompanied by psychiatric symptoms such as apathy, psychosis, autistic 
features, bipolar mood disorders, and regression in cognitive abilities 5–7. Despite several well-characterized 
animal models, our understanding of how EHMT1 deficiency causes KS is limited. In Drosophila melanogaster 
EHMT/G9a is responsible for regulation of learning and memory, which could be restored in the mutants 
during adulthood by re-expression of EHMT indicating that cognitive defects are reversible8. In a mouse model, 
Balemans et al.9 showed that Ehmt1(+/-) mice revealed reduced activity and exploration, with increased anxiety 
compared to wildtype mice, and that the hypoactivity is not caused by any motor dysfunction. Furthermore, 
the same research group showed that pyramidal CA1 hippocampal neurons of Ehmt1+/− mice had a significant 
reduction in spine density, number of mature spines, and dendritic arborization, indicating postsynaptic 
deficit10. Y amada et al. reported that aberrant microglia activation due to upregulation of proinflammatory genes 
and consequent neural inflammation might play a role in the molecular pathology of Ehmt1+/− mouse brain11.
The cellular consequences of EHMT1 deficiency in epithelial cells were identified in a recent paper by 
Iglesias-Ortega et al. using siRNA to downregulate EHMT1 in a retinal epithelial cell line (RPE1)12. Among the 
key changes identified were alterations in the morphology and distribution of different subcellular structures, 
such as the Golgi apparatus, the lysosomes, and different cell adhesion components accompanied by impaired 
cell proliferation and migration. Human-induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology13 and the subsequent 
neuronal differentiation allow us to investigate the pathophysiology of neurodevelopmental disorders on single-
cell neuronal network level 14. Neuronal networks of KS-patient-derived cells exhibit network bursting with a 
reduced rate, longer duration, and increased temporal irregularity compared to control networks mediated by 
NMDA receptor subunit 1 15.
The human iPSC-derived 3D models bridge the gap between traditional two-dimensional (2D) mono-layer 
cultures and animal models. The 3D cultures better replicate the structural complexity of tissues found in vivo, 
providing a more physiologically relevant environment for cells. This includes the spatial organization, cell-cell, 
and cell-matrix interactions16,17. Depending on the cell type of origin, the two main human iPSC-derived 3D 
CNS models are categorized as organoids and neurospheres18,19. The brain organoids are derived directly from 
hPSC-s, and following a multilayer patterning can reach great complexity over a prolonged time enabling the 
study of early neuronal differentiation and brain development20,21. 
Compared to organoids the neurospheres are less complex and relatively homogenous in their structures 
and among each other and with restricted cell types obtained from NPCs. As such, the neurospheres represent a 
more suitable platform for drug screening purposes and toxicology assays22.
Here, we established and characterized a cortical neurospheroid model system derived from low-passaged 
NPCs obtained from a KS-patient-derived iPSC, generated and characterized previously by our group carrying 
a heterozygous, de novo, premature termination codon mutation (Trp1138Ter) leading to nonsense-mediated 
mRNA decay23,24. We identified key differences in the growth behavior of KS spheroids determined by reduced 
proliferation and distinct cellular composition. In KS cortical neurospheres glutamatergic and cholinergic 
neurons were overrepresented while the catecholaminergic neurons were almost absent compared to the control.
Next, we addressed the question of how EHMT1 haploinsufficiency is affecting the drug toxicity sensitivity 
of KS patients. By using four different compounds, we have found significant differences in tolerance depending 
not only on the genotype but also on the differentiation status of the cells. Neuronal progenitor cells (NPC) 
were less sensitive to paraquat than their fully matured 3D counterparts, while rotenone, bardoxolone, and 
doxorubicin were more detrimental to the NPCs. KS spheroids showed extreme sensitivity to paraquat and 
manifested slightly decreased viability in response to the other compounds tested, pinpointing the importance of 
the developmental stage in toxicology tests. Overall, we report a well-characterized, robust model system suitable 
not only for neurodevelopmental studies of the KS pathophysiology but also for drug screening purposes.
Results
Mutation in EHMT1 causes reduced spheroid formation and growth arrest
To generate the 3D neurospheroid culture for the KS cell line we used our well-established protocol for toxicology 
assays in non-diseased, control cell lines 22. Although the spheroid formation has occurred in both cell lines, 
while the control spheroids manifested a progressive growth during the culture, the growth of the KS spheroids 
was overtly arrested and their size at the termination of the 3D culture (at day 57th ) was equal to the starting size 
(Fig. 1a-b). To measure EHMT1 activity in TD57 spheroids we detected the expression of its product H3K9me2 
using ICC (Fig.  1c-d). The lower H3K9me2 immunoreactivity detected in KS denotes EHMT1 deficiency and 
thus downregulated activity. To address the question if the smaller spheroid size of the KS line is the result 
of a lower proliferation rate and cell cycle arrest, we measured the gene expression of KI67, marker gene of 
proliferation, and CDKN1A, indicative of cell cycle arrest using qRT-PCR on TD57 day spheroids. We found 
that the expression of KI67 was significantly lower in KS than in the control (p = 0.004), while CDKN1A showed 
slightly higher but statistically non-significant expression in the KS (Fig.  1e). To identify whether the lower 
proliferation rate is evoked by the 3D culture system or is associated with impaired EHMT1 expression, we 
quantified the expression of both genes in the NPC stage as well. Figure  1f shows that although the trend is 
similar to the expression levels seen in 3D (lower KI67 expression and higher CDKN1A expression in KS) the 
difference is not significant. Similar changes were reported by Lee et al. 25 in lung cancer cell lines where they 
used siRNA to silence the EHMT1. The authors found that EHMT1 knockdown reduced spheroid formation 
in 3D culture and caused cell-cycle arrest by increasing CDKN1A expression. Another aspect of the spheroid 
formation the authors investigated was the expression of the CDH1 gene encoding E-cadherin responsible for 
cell adhesion and they have found that its expression decreases when EHMT1 is silenced accounting for the

The level of synaptophysin was slightly elevated, while V AMP2 was significantly overrepresented in KS spheroids

Neurite outgrowth assay shows growth advantage in KS neurospheres
To investigate the impact of EHMT1 deficiency in KS on neurite growth we performed a neurite outgrowth 
assay using TD57 spheroids by plating them on laminin-coated tissue culture plates and culture for three days 
and neurites were visualized by immunostaining of tubulin beta 3 (TUBB3). We were intrigued by the results as 
opposite to the growth behavior of the KS spheroids, the neurites of the KS were significantly longer (p = 0.002) 
and presented a more abundant branching than the control (Fig. 3a-c). To verify if the same difference was true 
for the spheroid, we measured TUBB3 expression on cryosections of TD57 spheroids. Similar to the results 
obtained with the neurite outgrowth assay, KS spheroids also contained significantly higher TUBB3 expression 
(p = 0.02) indicating longer neurites (Fig. 3d-e).
The growth and branching of neurites, among others, is determined by the neurotrophin brain-derived 
neurotrophic factor (BDNF) 26. To investigate if BDNF is responsible for the longer neurites in KS we used 
immunostaining to detect BDNF expression in the neurites of TD57 spheroids. We have found that indeed, 
BDNF expression is significantly higher in KS than in the control ( p = 0.008, Fig.  3f–g, Supp. Figure  1a) 
accounting for differences in the length and branching of neurites.

Finally, we used doxorubicin (DOX) as it is one of the most effective and widely used chemotherapeutic anti-
cancer drugs, crucial to the treatment of a range of neoplasms while causing severe neurotoxicity 33. In actively 
proliferating cells DOX inhibits topoisomerase II, leading to the formation of double-stranded breaks of DNA

differentiated cells its cytotoxic effect is conferred mainly by mitotoxicity, the formation of free radicals, and lipid

the difference is not significant and the EC50 value is 0.01 µM for both lines (Fig.  4a; Table 1). At 3D TD57 the 
two cell lines have similar dose-response curves, and overall, both cell lines show significantly higher tolerance

again is in line with the different proliferation rates of NPC vs. TD57 with NPCs proliferating at a much higher

Regarding the effect of PQ Naspolini et al. 35 showed that PQ exposure and the oxidative stress response 
resulted in decreased glucose transport into neural cells associated with a compensatory increase in lactate 
dehydrogenase (LDH) activity suggesting the importance of lactate as energy fuel. To address the question if 
EHMT1 haploinsufficiency impacts LDH expression we measured the transcript levels of the two subunits of 
LDH, LDHA which has a higher affinity for pyruvate, and LDHB with a higher affinity for lactate. Figure  5 
shows that the expression of LDHA is significantly downregulated in the KS line in both maturation stages tested 
(p = 0.03 for the NPC, and p = 0.002 for the 3D spheroid), while LDHB is not affected by the mutation.
Discussion
Patient-derived, 3D-based CNS modeling of neurodevelopmental diseases represents a valuable tool for 
personalized medicine and drug development as it recapitulates most of the phenotypic and functional aspects 
of various brain regions depending on the differentiation protocol applied 18,19,36. In this study, we established 
a 3D corticoid brain model for KS suitable for the identification of disease-related molecular and cellular 
changes during brain development and drug screening purposes. We found that although the spheroids form 
similarly to the control cell line, their growth is restricted reflected by the low expression of KI67, high CDH1, 
and moderately increased CDKN1A, which might be responsible for cell cycle arrest. Our result with a patient-
derived model is in line with the findings reported by Fear et al. who used CRISPR single-based gene editing

be responsible for the lower cell proliferation seen in KS cells. E-cadherin, a key cell-cell adhesion molecule,

EHMT1 and overexpression of EHMT1 is associated with tumor stage and lymph node metastasis by inhibiting 
E-cadherin40. In addition, G9a/GLP (the corresponding protein products of EHMT2/EHMT1) inhibition 
induced change in morphology in cell lines accompanied by the appearance of smaller neurosphere-like cells 
in SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cultures41. Whether these cellular defects contribute or not to microcephaly seen 
in more than 55% of the KS- patients 42 is not clear and needs further investigation. Our results together with 
the cited references demonstrate that EHMT1 has a universal role in basic cellular mechanisms like cell cycle 
regulation, cell proliferation, survival, and migration regardless of cell type. Furthermore, considering, that we 
and others showed that EHMT1 is implicated in regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition 37,40, it would be 
intriguing to assess the incidence and progression of cancer in KS patients, which might have been overlooked 
previously, due to the small group size of rare disease patients.
Conversely to reduced cell proliferation and growth arrest of KS cells, in the spheroid-based neurite outgrowth 
assay, we observed that the KS spheroid-derived neurites were significantly longer and more abundantly branched 
than in the control. This is in line with the findings of Benevento et al., who showed that BDNF levels were 
abnormally increased in the brain of Ehmt1+/− mice due to hypomethylation of the BDNF promoter43. Similarly, 
in Ehmt1+/− mouse embryonic stem cell-derived neurons Bdnf was repressed by NRSF/REST, and loss of Ehmt1 
by reducing the expression of NRSF/REST resulted in increased expression of REST-target genes, among them 
Bdnf44. We further investigated the synapthogenesis but the increase in neurite outgrowth did not lead to higher 
connectivity within neuronal networks as assessed by the lower expression of PSD-95 and SYP in the KS line.
Next, we aimed to determine the cellular composition of TD57 neurospheroids. While several lines of evidence 
indicate that reduced EHMT1 level accelerated neuronal differentiation during time course experiments37,44–46, 
we did not see a difference in the three main cell types (neuron, astrocyte, oligodendrocyte), as most probably 
by day 57 of differentiation not only the KS but also the control reached maturation. Instead, we identified 
major differences and shifts in neuron subtypes in favor of the excitatory neurons marked by excessive ChAT, 
VGLUT1/2, and NMDAR1 expression. Previously, our group reported that iPSC-derived KS neurons showed 
higher sensitivity of calcium responses to acetylcholine stimulation 24. In the current paper, we demonstrate 
that mature neuropsheroids from KS also express high ChAT levels contributing to the excitatory phenotype 
described by Frega et al. manifested in network bursting with a reduced rate, longer duration, and increased 
temporal irregularity compared to control networks 15. The authors show that these changes are mediated by 
the upregulation of NMDA receptor (NMDAR) subunit 1 encoded by the GRIN1 gene15. These results are in 
line with our findings regarding higher protein and transcript levels for NMDAR1 and GRIN1 in TD57 KS-
patient-derived spheroids. The molecular mechanism accounting for these observations relies on the role of 
EHMT1 regulating NRSF/REST (repressor element-1 silencing transcription factor/neuron-restrictive silencer 
factor) function. It has been shown that reduced EHMT1 activity elevated NRSF/REST-target gene expression 
during neuronal development 44 and REST is known to regulate the expression of ChAT, GRIN1, and other 
cholinergic genes47. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the glutamatergic neurons expressing VGLUT1/2 are 
also significantly enriched in KS spheroids. VGLUTs are responsible for releasing glutamate into the synaptic 
cleft and thus their aberrant overexpression renders them excitotoxic 48. Whether excitotoxicity due to high 
VGLUT1/2 and high ChAT expression is part of the pathomechanism of KS is not known, but some of the 
symptoms like hallucinations, epileptic seizures, and poor sleep seen in these patients are most probably caused 
and exacerbated by the hyperexcitability due to elevated glutamate, acetylcholine and NMDRA149. The effects of 
excitatory neurotransmitters are balanced out by serotonin, an inhibitory neurotransmitter involved in emotion 
and mood. The level of serotonin is regulated by the SLC6A4 protein that transports serotonin from synaptic 
spaces into presynaptic neurons thus decreasing serotonin availability. So far, there are no studies investigating 
the serotonin-associated gene expression pathways in KS but is well documented that KS patients manifest 
anxiety, depression, apathy5, and psychiatric disorders correlated with high SLC6A4 expression50.
Finally, we aimed to assess the chemical-induced oxidative stress and toxicity of the control and KS cell line 
using an ATP-based viability assay. Data obtained so far on stress tolerance of G9a mutant Drosophila suggests 
that G9a has a protective function in response to multiple types of stress 51. To establish the role of the EHMT1 
deficiency in the stress response of the KS-patient-derived cell line, we tested the cytotoxic effect of oxidative 
stress (induced by paraquat or rotenone), Nrf2 pathway activation (CDDO-Me), and a chemotherapeutic 
drug (doxorubicin) on neuronal progenitor cells and terminally differentiated neurospheres. EHMT1 is 
a negative regulator in both NF- κB- and type I interferon-mediated gene induction pathways 52 and thus its 
haploinsufficiency leads to aberrant microglia activation and consequent neural inflammation described in the 
Scientific Reports |        (2024) 14:22572 8| https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-72791-4
www.nature.com/scientificreports/

Ehmt1+/− mouse brains by Y amada et al.11. Bardoxolone-methyl, via inducing the Nrf2 pathway inhibits NF-κB 
leading to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, and could ameliorate the inflammation-related symptoms 
provoked by low EHMT1 expression. We show that bardoxolone-methyl exerts a toxic effect in the nanomolar 
range in the progenitor cell cultures of both cell lines while the differentiated neurospheres became less sensitive, 
with EC50 value in the micromolar range. We observed a similar trend with doxorubicin treatment. DOX 
is the first-line chemotherapeutic agent for many types of cancer, and although it poorly crosses the blood-
brain barrier it still penetrates the brain at cytotoxic doses leading to thinning of the brain cortex and reduced 
brain connectivity53. Our group has shown before that DOX had a differentiation stage-specific effect when 
neurospheroids at different maturity phases were exposed to the drug22. Here we demonstrated that the EC50 of 
DOX was 1500 fold lower for the undifferentiated progenitors compared to the fully matured neurospheres. The 
trend observed might be the consequence of the slower proliferation rate of neurospheres vs. NPCs which leads 
to less DOX intercalation within DNA thus less cell death. In general, it was shown that human iPSC-derived 
neurons during maturation acquire a complex anti-apoptotic network to protect against cell death and confer 
neuronal resilience against stress54 which explains the increased sensitivity of NPCs versus the neurospheres in 
response to the chemicals tested except PQ.
Concerning the effect of the ROS inducers, we found that PQ had the most deleterious effect on KS spheroids, 
while in the case of the other compounds, the difference in sensitivity was less profound. Our results are in line 
with the data published by Riahi et al. regarding the increased sensitivity of Drosophila where the loss of the 
epigenetic regulator G9a causes a shift in the transcriptional and metabolic responses to oxidative stress that 
leads to decreased survival time upon feeding with paraquat 51. Naspolini and colleagues in PQ-treated rats 
showed that due to energy failure, lactate dehydrogenase was upregulated which suggests the relevance of lactate 
as energy fuel35. Our findings revealed lower LDHA expression in the KS cell line which might contribute to the 
low ROS tolerance of this genotype. Furthermore, in healthy rat hippocampal neurons PQ exposure provoked 
cytotoxicity associated with decreased cell viability and increased glutamate excitotoxicity 35, which in our KS 
model is further exacerbated by the high VGLU1/2 and NMDAR1 expression. Moreover, the decreased LDHA 
level might be responsible for depression documented in some of the KS patients. The human prefrontal cortex 
is reported to be highly glycolytic at the resting state and further upregulates its glycolytic capacity upon brain 
activation, and alterations in the energy metabolism of this brain region are associated with depression 55. Y ao 
et al. showed that astrocytic LDHA regulates neuronal excitability and depressive-like behaviors through lactate 
homeostasis in mice 56. Additional research is needed to elucidate if LDHA downregulation seen on the RNA 
level translates to an energy deficit contributing to and exacerbating the psychopathology seen in KS patients.
LDHA also was demonstrated to negatively correlate with E-cadherin expression particularly in cancer biology. 
Several studies have investigated the relationship between these two molecules, suggesting that decreased LDHA 
levels correlate with increased E-cadherin expression inhibiting the epithelial-to-mecenchymal transition57.
Overall we summarize our findings combined with the published results in Fig.  6. In the KS cell line, the 
decreased EHMT1 activity and downregulated methylation of the CDH1 promoter results in elevated E-cadherin 
which inhibits cell proliferation, mainly via contact inhibition of proliferation and recruitment of the Hippo 
pathway39,58, migration, and higher sensitivity to oxidative stress by inhibiting the nuclear accumulation of Nrf2 
58. Next, the LDHA is downregulated in the KS cell line, which also weakens the cells’ survival and stress response 
mechanisms, especially during oxidative stress conditions induced by PQ treatment which, at least partially, 
could be mitigated by anaerobic glycolysis51. Furthermore, the low LDHA levels might enhance the E-cadherin 
expression, and its negative effects on cell survival and proliferation.
Overall, these observations revealed novel insights into the altered genetic pathways and their interactions in 
different developmental stages of the KS 3D neuronal network models, potentially revealing druggable targets 
and differential sensitivity to drugs and environmental or iatrogenic toxicants. Such observations would help 
pharmacologists, toxicologists, and physicians to understand better the potential risks and solutions in KS or 
other neurodevelopmental diseases caused by defects in the methylation of histones or DNA, such as Kabuki 
syndrome59.
It is important to note, that depending on the size of the deleted chromosome fragment, or the position of 
the mutation causing the EHMT1 haploinsufficiency the genetic and epigenetic modifications and the clinical 
severity of the syndrome varied broadly in these patients. Hereby we extrapolate our findings with one unique 
KS patient cell line to other EHMT1 mutations based on the comparison with published datasets, however, 
the clinical phenotype variability would point to the importance of investigating the cellular level changes on a 
patient-by-patient manner, ultimately also indicating the need for the personalized therapy approach.
In conclusion, we established a 3D model system expressing the KS-specific markers which recapitulate the 
characteristic pathophysiological traits and provide a suitable platform for neurodevelopmental studies of the 
KS pathophysiology, developmental neurotoxicity and also for drug screening and testing individual therapeutic 
strategies in this rare disease.

Cortical neurosphere culture
3D cortical neurosphere cultures were established as described before by our group 22. In brief, single-cell 
suspension of NPCs in passage number 9 was plated onto a low-adherent 96-well plate with 10,000 cells/well in 
neuronal maintenance medium (NMM), a mixture of 1:1 vol/vol of DMEM/F12 and Neurobasal-A medium,

Spheroids were cultured for 57 days (terminal differentiation day 57: TD57) with half NMM medium change

Cryosectioning, immunocytochemistry (ICC), and microscopy of 3D neurospheroids
3D samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (PBS) for 1 h at room 
temperature (RT) and washed 3 times with PBS. Afterward, the samples were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose 
(in PBS) containing 0.01% sodium azide at 4 °C until embedding in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound (Sakura). 
The 16 μm parallel sections were made using a cryostat (Leica CM 1860 UV , Leica Biosystems), mounted to 
Superfrost Ultra Plus Adhesion Slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and stored at -20 °C until use.
After 10  min air-drying, the sections were blocked for 1  h at RT with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) 
in PBS, supplemented with 0.01% TritonX-100. Primary antibodies were used overnight at 4  °C as follows: 
rabbit anti-TUBB3 (1:500, Abcam), chicken anti-MAP2 (1:2000, Abcam), goat anti-GFAP (1:400, Abcam),

(1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific), mouse anti-SYP (1:100, Abcam), rabbit anti- H3K9me2 (1:250, Thermo 
Fisher Scientific). Next day, sections were washed in PBS three times, and the immune reaction was completed 
by Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-chicken (1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-mouse 
(1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-goat (1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa 
Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit (1:600, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit (1:600, 
Thermo Fisher Scientific) applied for 1 h at RT. The sections were covered using Vectashield mounting medium 
containing DAPI (1.5 µg/ml; Vector Laboratories), which labeled the nuclei of the cells. Negative controls for 
the secondary antibodies were performed by omitting the primary antibodies. The highest diameter middle 
sections with immunoreactivity were analyzed using a Leica Stellaris-8 microscope (Leica Microsystems) with 
Leica Application Suite X software (Leica Microsystems). All images were further processed using the GNU 
Image Manipulation Program (GIMP 2.10.0). Quantification of the immunocytochemistry data was performed 
using NIH ImageJ analysis software (imagej.nih.gov/ij) according to Tieng et al. and Kobolák et al. 22,61. Briefly, 
confocal images were taken at ×200 magnification using the Leica Stellaris-8 system with a 20x dry lens (NA: 
0.75). Using NIH ImageJ, we applied an automatic threshold to the images to distinguish immunoreactive pixels. 
Pixels with intensity levels above the threshold were classified as white, while those below the threshold were 
classified as black. This thresholding ensures that even the darker parts of the spheroid are accurately accounted 
for. We measured the numbers of immunoreactive pixels for neuronal, glial, and synaptic markers, as well as 
DAPI, in the middle sections of the four largest neurospheres for each cell line. These measurements were 
normalized to the number of DAPI-positive nuclei and presented as marker/DAPI ratios ± SEM (p < 0.05).

random fields were chosen from four spheroids in such a manner to cover the full-length neurites excluding the

Toxicity profiling
NPCs were plated in NMM supplemented with 5ng/ml bFGF and 5ng/ml EGFat 30 000 cells / well in Nunc 
MicroWell 96-well, flat-bottom, optical coverglass base microplate (ThermoFisher), and triplicate wells were 
treated the following day in 100 µl NMM supplemented with graded doses of chemicals. For the TD57 spheroids, 
50% of the spent medium was exchanged with NMM containing the amount of chemicals to yield the specified 
treatment concentrations. Cell cultures were exposed to the following toxicants for 72 h: paraquat dichloride 
hydrate (Carbosynth, CAS # 75365-73-0), doxorubicin hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, CAS # 25316-40-9),

control was used as a regular medium supplemented with 0.1% DMSO. ATP release-based viability assay 
was performed using the CellTiter-Glo Cell Viability Assay kit for the NPCs (Promega) and with CellTiter-
Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay kit for the neurospheres (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The 
luminescence signal was recorded with a Thermo VarioScan Flash (Thermo Fisher Scientific) plate reader.
