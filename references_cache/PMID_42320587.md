ABSTRACT: Mild bleeding disorders (MBDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of 
inherited conditions characterized by clinically relevant bleeding symptoms 
despite largely normal or inconclusive results in routine hemostatic testing. 
These disorders account for a substantial proportion of referrals to specialized 
hemostasis clinics and include von Willebrand disease (VWD), inherited platelet 
function disorders, and mild coagulation factor deficiencies. Despite systematic 
diagnostic algorithms, many patients with MBDs remain without a definitive 
diagnosis and are classified as having bleeding disorder of unknown cause 
(BDUC), complicating clinical management and counseling. Conventional diagnostic 
approaches rely on structured bleeding assessment tools, detailed family 
history, and stepwise laboratory testing. Biological variability, assay 
limitations, and phenotypic overlap often result in inconclusive findings.
ABSTRACT: Recent advances in genetic analysis have begun to transform this 
diagnostic landscape. Targeted next-generation sequencing panels, whole-exome 
sequencing (WES), and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) enable identification of 
pathogenic variants across numerous hemostasis-related genes. In MBDs, genetic 
testing is valuable for refining diagnoses in qualitative VWD, confirming 
inherited platelet disorders, and identifying rare mild coagulopathies. In 
contrast, its diagnostic yield in type 1 and low von Willebrand factor (VWF) is 
modest, reflecting complex genetic architecture, modifier effects, and 
incomplete penetrance. In BDUC, genetic testing has revealed monogenic causes in 
some patients and multifactorial contributions in others.
ABSTRACT: Genetic testing should therefore be regarded as a complementary tool 
rather than a replacing conventional diagnostics. Integrated with clinical and 
laboratory findings and supported by expert variant interpretation, it can 
reduce diagnostic uncertainty and improve disease classification in patients 
with MBDs.

Introduction
Mild bleeding disorders (MBDs) comprise a heterogeneous
group of inherited conditions characterized by a clinically rele-
vant but typically non-life-threatening bleeding tendency.
1
Affected individuals commonly present with mucocutaneous
bleeding manifestations, including easy bruising, epistaxis, heavy
menstrual bleeding, and excessive bleeding following minor
trauma or surgical procedures. The term MBD is generally used
to describe bleeding disorders with a mild-to-moderate bleeding
phenotype, which can be difficult to distinguish from physio-
logical interindividual variability of hemostatic parameters at ini-
tial presentation. However, the diagnosis often becomes more
apparent over time, particularly after recurrent bleeding epi-
sodes or following significant hemostatic challenges such as sur-
gery or trauma.
Despite advances in laboratory diagnostics, establishing a
definitive diagnosis in patients with mild bleeding phenotypes
remains challenging as standard hemostatic investigations
frequently yield normal or equivocal results, leading to diagnostic
uncertainty or failure to identify an underlying cause. Patients
who exhibit a significant bleeding phenotype in the absence
of identifiable abnormalities on comprehensive laboratory evalua-
tion are classified a bleeding disorder of unknown cause (BDUC), a
diagnosis of exclusion defined after minimum laboratory assess-
ment. This should include, based on the recommendation of The
Scientific and Standardization Committee of the International
Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH), a complete blood
count, prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin
time (aPTT), thrombin time, von Willebrand factor (VWF) antigen,
VWF function, coagulation factors VIII, IX, and XI, and platelet

The limitations of current diagnostic strategies are reflected in

of bleeding in patients with BDUC is particularly challenging, as
the underlying etiology remains undefined. Notably, individuals
with BDUC may experience major bleeding events, including
postpartum hemorrhage, even when treated with nonspecific
hemostatic measures such as tranexamic acid.

the most frequently recognized MBDs included von Willebrand
disease (VWD), inherited platelet function disorders (IPFDs), and
mild coagulation factor deficiencies; however, in the last decade,
BDUC has emerged as the most commonly recognized subgroup
within the spectrum of MBDs.
6,7
In recent years, the emergence of molecular genetic testing —
including next-generation sequencing (NGS) — has begun to
reshape diagnostic pathways for inherited bleeding disorders. 8,9
These technologies offer the potential to un cover pathogenic or
likely pathogenic (LP) variants that may confirm a suspected
diagnosis, refine disease classification, or identify novel genetic
contributors to bleeding phenotypes previously labeled as BDUC.
However, important limitations remain, including low diagnostic
yield in BDUC, frequent identification of variants of uncertain sig-
nificance (VUS), and challenges in clinical interpretation. Given the
rapid evolution of genomic tools and growing evidence supporting
their clinical utility, in this review, we focus our attention on how
genetic analysis is influencing diagnostic accuracy, discuss the
strengths and limitations of available approaches, highlight practi-
cal considerations for integrating genetic analysis into clinical
decision-making and its future role in the assessment of MBDs.
Diagnosis and Diag nostic Challenges
Diagnostic Approach
Diagnostic evaluation of patients with suspected MBDs begins
with a comprehensive clinical assessment, including physical
examination and a detailed personal and family bleeding his-
tory.
10 Because bleeding symptoms are often intermittent and
context-dependent, standardized bleeding assessment tools
(BATs) have become integral to the diagnostic pathway.
9
The most widely used expert-administered tool is the ISTH
BAT, which provides a structured and quantitative evaluation of
bleeding symptoms.
11,12 A validated self-administered version
(Self-BAT) has also been developed to improve accessibility and
efficiency, and has shown good concordance with expert-
administered assessments, particularly in adults.
13,14
Following clinical assessment, first-line laboratory investiga-
tions include a complete blood count, PT, aPTT, fibrinogen levels,
and initial VWF testing. In patients with a prolonged aPTT,
additional single coagulation factor measurements are required.
These initial investigations establish a diagnosis in a subset of
patients referred for evaluation of mild bleeding symptoms, most
commonly corresponding to VWD or mild hemophilia Aand B.
4,15
For the remaining patients, additional specialized tests are
required to evaluate platelet function disorders, rare coagulation
factor deficiencies, and abnormalities of fibrinolysis. However,
the diagnostic yield of these second- and third-line investigations
remains limited, particularly in patients with mild or fluctuating
phenotypes, as many assays have suboptimal sensitivity and
specificity and are technically demanding, often requiring spe-
cialized laboratories and expert interpretation.
15–17
A further challenge is the substantial influence of pre-
analytical and physiological variables on hemostatic assays. VWF
levels and platelet function assays are particularly sensitive to
pre-analytical factors (sample handling and transport time), as
well as physiological factors (acute stress, inflammation, hor-
monal status, and pregnancy).
18,19 Consequently, repeat testing
is often necessary to confirm or reject borderline or inconsistent
findings. In some patients, progression to advanced testing is
delayed or precluded due to confounding factors such as ongoing
medication use (e.g., antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants) or
pregnancy. Table 1 summarizes the main features of MBDs.
20
Key Diagnostic Challenges
Diagnosing MBDs, including those classified as BDUC remains
challenging and may result in underdiagnosis, misclassification,
or delays in appropriate counseling and management:
Overlap of Clinical Phenotypes
One of the principal challenges in diagnosing MBDs is the consid-
erable overlap in clinical presentation. Mucocutaneous bleeding
manifestations — such as epistaxis, easy bruising, heavy menstrual
bleeding, and bleeding following dental or surgical procedures —
are common to both diagnostically established inherited bleed-
ing disorders and in patients classified as BDUC. Because these
symptoms are often mild and nonspecific, conventional labora-
tory assays may fail to detect subtle hemostatic abnormalities,
leading to diagnostic ambiguity.
2,5
Variability of Laboratory Results
Variability in laboratory results represents a major obstacle
to accurate diagnosis. VWF levels are known to fluctuate in
response to physiological stress, inflammation, exercise, hormonal
changes, and aging.
21–23 Similarly, platelet function tests exhibit
considerable intraindividual and interlaboratory variability. 24,25
These fluctuations may obscure mild hemostatic defects during ini-
tial assessment, potentially leading to misclassification or a provi-
sional assignment of a BDUC diagnosis when repeat testing might
reveal an underlying abnormality.
18,20,26,27
Lack of Standardization and Reproducibility
Many assays used to investigate platelet function and rare bleed-
ing disorders lack standardization across laboratories. Techniques
such as light transmission aggregometry, flow cytometry-based
platelet testing, and specialized coagulation assays require

Unclear Pathophysiology of BDUC
Perhaps, the most significant diagnostic challenge is the uncer-
tain biological basis of BDUC. The absence of identifiable abnor-
malities in standard hemostatic pathways suggests that BDUC
may represent a heterogeneous group of disorders, potentially
involving subtle defects in platelet signaling, endothelial func-
tion, fibrinolysis, or as-yet-unrecognized molecular pathways.
Emerging research has implicated alterations in fibrinolytic balance
and receptor-mediated signaling in selected patient subsets; how-
ever, these mechanisms are not routinely assessed in current clini-
cal diagnostics.
28,29 This lack of mechanistic clarity limits both diag-
nostic precision and the development of targeted therapeutic
strategies.
21,30 Importantly, this uncertainty is not exclusive to
BDUC. Similar limitations in understanding the biological determi-
nants of bleeding phenotype also exist in several established MBDs,
particularly type 1 VWD and mild platelet function disorders,
where incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, and weak
genotype–phenotype correlations remain common. Thus, the dis-
tinction between BDUC and other MBDs is often based more on
the current ability to establish a laboratory or molecular diagnosis
than on fundamentally different biological mechanisms.
These challenges underscore the need for improved diagnos-
tic tools, better standardization of assays, and the integration of
emerging approaches such as advanced functional testing and
genetic analysis into future diagnostic algorithms.
Role of Genetic Testing
Against this backgr ound of diagnostic uncertainty, molecular
genetic testing has emerged as a powerful complimentary tool,
particularly for patients with persistent bleeding symptoms and
inconclusive results in conventional investigations.
For MBDs as inherited bleeding disorders, advances in molec-
ular genetics have substantially reshaped the diagnostic
landscape,31,32 improving diagnostic accuracy and supporting
treatment decision-making and optimized control of bleeding
symptoms.
8,9
The clinical utility of genetic testing is well evidenced in
hemophilia. It provides essential clinical information related to
disease severity and is the only way to accurately identify female
carriers who are universally at higher risk of bleeding symptoms
compared with the normal population.
12,14,31 In addition,
genetic analyses may reveal identified mild combined deficiency
avoiding misclassification and better phenotype explanation.
33
A typical example is combined inheritance of pro- and anticoag-
ulant defects, which may modulate the clinical phenotype and
contribute to the variable or unexpected bleeding diathesis,
complicating both diagnosis and interpretation of laboratory
findings.
34,35
The diagnostic impact of genetic analysis is most evident
across three major groups of MBDs: VWD, inherited platelet
disorders, and other mild coagulation or fibrinolytic disorders.
Von Willebrand Disease
VWD is the most prevalent inherited bleeding disorder and
results from pathogenic variants in the VWF gene , which encodes
VWF, a key mediator of platelet adhesion and carrier protein for
factor VIII.
36 The marked genetic heterogeneity underlies the
broad phenotypic spectrum of VWD, ranging from mild mucocu-
taneous bleeding to severe hemorrhage. While genetic testing
has substantially improved the molecular basis of VWD, its diag-
nostic utility varies considerably across disease subtypes.
37
Qualitative VWD (type 2) and severe quantitative deficiency
(type 3), demonstrate strong genotype –phenotype correlations.
In these settings, genetic analysis is particularly valuable for
accurate subtype classification, differentiation from phenotypic

Large cohort studies have consistently shown that potentially

41–43 Individuals

47–49 Even when variants are detected, careful
pathogenicity assessment is required, as some variants occur in
asymptomatic individuals.
Consequently, the absence of a detectable VWF variant does
not exclude VWD, nor does the presence of a variant necessarily
confirm VWD. For this reason, routine genetic testing in type
1 VWD has historically been debated. Nevertheless, patients
with VWF levels below 30 IU/dL are more likely to harbor patho-
genic VWF variants, and in this subgroup, NGS-based testing may
support subtype classification, family counseling, and distinction
from BDUC or phenotypically overlapping disorders.
37,49–52
From a diagnostic perspective, genetic testing has become
increasingly valuable in: (1) refining VWD subtype classification,
especially within type 2 VWD; (2) distinguishing genocopies,
such as platelet-type VWD versus Type 2B VWD and mild hemo-
philia A versus Type 2N VWD; and (3) supporting family planning
and genetic counseling.
40,53,54
Hence, some limitations have to be kept on mind. Genetic
testing should not be used as an exclusionary tool in patients
with a convincing bleeding history and laboratory phenotype.
Rather, its value in mild VWD lies in selective application, espe-
cially when laboratory results are discordant, subtype distinction
affects management, or differentiation from BDUC is clinically
relevant.
6 In this context, in some cases with borderline reduc-
tions in VWF levels, the absence of supportive genetic findings
together with inclusive laboratory phenotypes may argue against
a monogenic form of VWF and help to prevent overdiagnosis of
VWD.
10,28,47 Despite technical and interpretive challenges —
including detection of gene conversions, handling of VUS, and
overlap of variants across VWD subtypes — molecular testing
remains a powerful tool when applied within a phenotype-driven
framework cautiously.
28 Current guidelines therefore recommend
selective use of genetic testing, particularly when laboratory and
clinical findings are discordant or when subtype confirmation influ-
ences management.14
Overall, genetic testing in mild VWD should be regarded
as a precision diagnostic instrument rather than a screening
test, complementing but not replacing careful clinical evaluation
and longitudinal laboratory assessment. A phenotype-driven,
expert-interpreted approach remains essential for translating
genetic findings into meaningful patient care.

findings with platelet phenotyping in routine clinical practice.

Other MBDs
Other MBDs include mild coagulation factor deficiencies (notably
factor XI deficiency) and rare disorders of fibrinolysis or platelet
granule biology. These conditions often display poor correlation
between factor levels, genotype, and bleeding severity, compli-
cating diagnosis.
60,61
Factor XI deficiency, caused by variants in the F11 gene, is a
paradigmatic example. Many affected individuals have near-
normal baseline hemostasis yet experience significant bleeding
following surgery or trauma.
62 Genetic testing allows definitive
diagnosis and identification of at-risk family members, although
bleeding risk prediction remains imperfect. 63,64
Similarly, rare conditions such as Quebec platelet disorder,
driven by duplications involving the PLAU gene, cannot be reli-
ably detected using standard coagulation or platelet function
tests and require molecular confirmation.
65 In patients with per-
sistent bleeding symptoms and normal results in conventional
testing, broad NGS panels or exome sequencing have demon-
strated incremental diagnostic yield, identifying pathogenic var-
iants in coagulation, platelet, or fibrinolytic genes previously not
considered.
3,8,30
Mild bleeding phenotypes may also arise from inherited disor-
ders of fibrinolysis, including plasminogen activator inhibitor-1
(PAI-1) deficiency and α2-antiplasmin deficiency, which lead to
premature clot breakdown and delayed bleeding manifestations.

Overall Impact on Diagnosis
Across all three groups, genetic testing has reshaped diagnostic
paradigms by providing molecular confirmation in ambiguous
cases, reducing misclassification and diagnostic delay, revealing
new disease mechanisms and expanding phenotypic spectra and
enhancing precision in counseling and clinical management.
Nevertheless, the overall prevalence of rare conditions such as
such as fibrinolytic disorders, or Quebec platelet disorder
contributes only marginally to the diagnostic yield in patients
with mild bleeding phenotypes. These conditions are exceedingly
rare and typically identified only in selected cases with suggestive
clinical features. While their recognition may have therapeutic
implications (e.g., antifibrinolytic therapy), routine screening is
not justified, and a targeted diagnostic approach is recom-
mended. Moreover, with advances in whole-exome and whole-
genome sequencing (WES/WGS), the diagnostic yield in patients
with MBDs remains limited. While these approaches have
enabled the discovery of novel genes (e.g., APOLD1, F3),
68,69
most variants are rare or of uncertain significance, and their con-
tribution to the bleeding phenotype is often unclear. Thus,
genetic testing alone cannot explain the majority of cases,
highlighting the need for integrative diagnostic approaches.

Sanger Sequencing
Sanger sequencing was the first method developed for detecting
genetic variants, generating DNA sequences one nucleotide at a
time.
74,75 It remains the gold standard for high-accuracy, tar-
geted analysis of individual genes or specific variants. In the con-
text of MBDs, Sanger sequencing is primarily applied for: (1) con-
firmation of variants identified by NGS; (2) cascade testing of
family members following identification of a pathogenic variant;
and (3) targeted investigation when there is strong clinical suspi-
cion of a single-gene disorder.
Despite its high analytical accuracy, Sanger sequencing has
notable limitations, including low throughput, high per-gene
cost, and limited efficiency in genetically heterogeneous condi-
tions such as inherited platelet disorders or BDUC
3,29,57
Consequently, in contemporary diagnostic practice, its role is
largely confined to variant validation, targeted familial testing,
and focused analysis in disorders with a well-defined mono-
genic basis.
Targeted NGS Gene Panels
Targeted NGS panels have emerged as the preferred first-line
genetic diagnostic approach for many MBDs. These panels simul-
taneously analyze multiple genes associated with coagulation
factor deficiencies, platelet function disorders, and fibrinolytic
abnormalities. Their advantages include: (1) high diagnostic yield
in inherited platelet disorders and rare bleeding disorders;
(2) cost-effectiveness and faster turnaround times compared
with broader sequencing approaches; (3) high depth of coverage
for clinically relevant genes; and (4) reduced incidence of inciden-
tal findings compared with WES or WGS.
8–11,32,49
Multiple studies have demonstrated diagnostic with suspected
inherited platelet disorders or unexplained bleeding phenotypes
using targeted panels. 51,52 However, limitations include depen-
dence on predefined gene content, inability to reliably detect
deep intronic or regulatory variants, and frequent identification
of VUS, particularly in mild phenotypes with incomplete pene-
trance.
41,52
Current expert consensus recommends targeted NGS panels
as the preferred genetic testing approach in patients with per-
sistent bleeding symptoms, equivocal standard laboratory
testing, or suspected inherited platelet or rare coagulation disor-
ders. Moreover, targeted gene panels are generally more cost-
efficient, whereas WES and especially WGS are substantially
more expensive and require more complex data analysis.
Whole-Exome Sequencing
WES enables analysis of all protein-coding regions and is particu-
larly valuable when targeted gene panels fail to identify a causa-
tive variant. WES allows: (1) identification of pathogenic variants
in genes not previously linked to bleeding disorders and (2) detec-
tion of oligogenic or complex inheritance patterns.
50,76,77
However, WES has uneven exon coverage, limited sensitivity
for CNV analysis and noncoding variants, and generates a
substantial number of VUS and incidental findings, necessitating
robust bioinformatics filtering and expert interpretation. 50,78
Cost and longer turnaround times may further limit its routine
clinical application.
Accordingly, WES is best positioned as a second-tier diagnostic
approach, particularly in patients with strong bleeding pheno-
types, suspected syndromic features, or unresolved BDUC after
targeted testing. However, recent WES data highlight both
the potential and the limitations of genetic testing in inherited
primary hemostasis disorders.
79 In a multicenter study of
170 patients, genotype –phenotype correlation was particularly
limited in BDUC, with 40% of cases lacking a candidate variant.
Importantly, 56% of detected variants were classified as VUS,
highlighting major interpretation challenges. Additionally, ∼11%
of patients carried variants in multiple genes, suggesting com-
plex or oligogenic contributions to the bleeding phenotype.
Together, these findings demonstrate that while WES can identify
rare and novel variants, its clinical utility in BDUC is limited by low
diagnostic yield, a high proportion of VUS, and increasing genetic
complexity, supporting a targeted and integrative diagnostic
approach.
Whole-Genome Sequencing
WGS offers the most comprehensive genomic assessment, cap-
turing coding and noncoding regions, structural variants, copy-
number changes, and deep intronic variants that may affect
splicing. In theory, WGS has the greatest potential to resolve
BDUCs and MBDs by identifying pathogenic mechanisms missed
by targeted approaches. In practice, widespread adoption of WGS
is limited by high cost, substantial data storage and analysis
requirements, and ongoing challenges in interpreting noncoding
variants with uncertain functional significance.
80,81 Currently,
WGS is best reserved for highly selected cases, unresolved
BDUC, or specialized centers integrating clinical diagnostics with
research settings.
Data Evaluation and Expert Interpretation
Irrespective of the sequencing method employed, expert inter-
pretation is essential for translating genetic findings into clinically
meaningful diagnoses that can be interpreted into a clear result
letter. Variant classification should follow standardized frame-
works such as the ACMG/AMP guidelines,
82 which categorize var-
iants as pathogenic (P), LP, VUS, likely benign, or benign (B).
However, these frameworks have limitations in MBDs character-
ized by variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance.
28,83,84
Interpretation must integrate bleeding phenotype (including
BAT scores), hemostatic laboratory results and family history
with segregation analysis. Multidisciplinary review, involving
hematologists, clinical geneticists, and specialized coagulation
laboratories, is critical to avoid misclassification, overdiagnosis,
and inappropriate clinical management.
5,30

Genetic testing has become an important complementary tool in
the diagnostic evaluation of MBDs. What were once considered

value of genetic testing in this setting lies not in replacing con-

standard diagnostic pathways fail to provide clear answers.
In routine clinical practice, targeted NGS-based gene panels
currently represent the most practical and cost-effective genetic
approach for selected patients with suspected inherited platelet
disorders, rare bleeding disorders, or unresolved bleeding pheno-

additional insights in unresolved cases but currently remain best
suited for specialized centers and research-integrated diagnostic
settings.
Ultimately, genetic data only gain clinical meaning when inter-
preted by experienced multidisciplinary teams that understand
both the biology of hemostasis and the nuances of bleeding phe-
notypes. Used thoughtfully, genetic testing has the potential to
reduce diagnostic uncertainty, improve patient counseling, and
refine individualized clinical management. Used indiscriminately,
however, it risks adding complexity without improving diagnostic
clarity. The future of diagnosing MBDs therefore lies not simply in
more sequencing, but in smarter, phenotype-driven integration
of genetic information into everyday clinical practice.
