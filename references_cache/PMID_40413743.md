# Identification of RMP24 and RMP64, human ribonuclease MRP-specific protein components.
**Authors:** Che R, Mirani B, Panah M, Chen X, Luo H, Alexandrov A
**Journal:** Cell Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.celrep.2025.115752](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2025.115752)

## Content

Human RNase MRP is a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) enzyme that processes precursor rRNA (pre-rRNA) at ITS1 site 2 and may have additional activities. It is an endonuclease related to RNase P, which processes pre-tRNAs and pre-tRNA-like substrates. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, these two RNPs utilize distinct catalytic RNAs with eight shared and one or two specific protein subunits. However, the human RNase MRP-specific protein subunits remain unidentified. Our genome-wide forward genetic screening identifies two poorly characterized human genes, which we name ribonuclease MRP subunit P24 (RMP24) and RMP64. We show that Rmp24 and Rmp64 are required for pre-rRNA ITS1 site 2 processing and associate with MRP RNA but are not required for RNase P activity and do not associate with RNase P-specific H1 RNA. Despite limited sequence homology, Rmp24 and Rmp64 exhibit predicted structural similarities to two RNase MRP-specific components in S. cerevisiae. Collectively, our functional screening and validation reveal two protein components unique to human nuclear RNase MRP.

Human ribonuclease P (RNase P) and MRP (RNase MRP) are ribonucleoprotein
(RNP) enzymes that employ catalytic RNAs. While RNase P is primarily responsible for
processing the 5′ leaders of pre-tRNAs1–4 and tRNA-like
structures5–7 and is conserved across all three domains of
life,8 nuclear RNase MRP is
found only in eukaryotes9,10 and is known for the initial processing of
precursor rRNA (pre-rRNA) at ITS1 site 2 in humans11 and ITS1 site A3 in yeast
Saccharomyces cerevisiae.12,13 Besides
processing pre-rRNA, RNase MRP is known to cleave additional RNAs, including the
5′ UTR of cyclin B214 and
CTS1 mRNA.15 Mitochondrial RNase
MRP, which is a distinct entity that shares its RNA component with nuclear RNase
MRP, is required for generating primers for mitochondrial DNA replication.16–18 Mutations in the RNA moiety of human RNase MRP and
mutations in proteins or promoter regions of RNase MRP components are associated
with genetic disorders such as cartilage-hair hypoplasia (CHH) and anauxetic
dysplasia (AD), both of which are classified as ribosomopathies.19–21 Whereas the structure of the human RNase MRP complex is
unknown, recent structures of yeast RNase P22 and RNase MRP,23,24 as well as human
RNase P25 (Figures 1A, 1B, and
1D), revealed their organization and
catalytic mechanisms,9 as well as
critical substrate recognition determinants, including the key roles of yeast RNase
MRP-specific protein subunits in recognition of the pre-rRNA substrate.23

In yeast, there are two such RNase MRP-specific protein subunits, Snm1 and
Rmp1, which are absent in RNase P. The first subunit, Snm1, assists in the folding
of the yeast RNase MRP RNA, NME1, to form the substrate-binding module.9,23,24 The second
subunit, Rmp1, together with Pop4 and Pop1, coordinates the single-stranded pre-rRNA
substrate and its characteristically flipped cytosine base, enabling RNase
MRP’s substrate specificity.23,24,32 The additional eight protein subunits of
yeast RNase MRP—Pop1, Pop3, Pop4, Pop5, Pop6, Pop7, Pop8, and Rpp1—are
all shared with RNase P.9,23,24,33 Furthermore, the trimer
Pop1-Pop6-Pop7 is also found in the yeast telomerase holoenzyme, where it is
required for the correct localization of the telomerase RNA, TLC1, as well as
holoenzyme assembly.34,35

In contrast, in humans, since the discovery of the RNase MRP over 35 years
ago,36,37 no RNase MRP-specific protein subunits have
been identified.11,33 It has therefore remained a mystery whether
such subunits exist,9,11 as the nine proteins known to associate with
human RNase MRP—Pop1, Pop5, Rpp14, Rpp20, Rpp25, Rpp29(Pop4), Rpp30, Rpp38,
and Rpp40—are also known to be components of RNase P33,38
(Figures 1C and 1D). The lack of known RNase MRP-specific protein
components has greatly hindered studies of human RNase MRP, including the
identification of its substrates and substrate specificity determinants,
understanding its function, and determining its structure, which remains
unknown.9,11

Using a genome-wide forward genetic screening that leveraged the requirement
of RNase MRP and RNase P for reporter translation, we identified two poorly
characterized yet highly enriched human genes, c18orf21 and
c3orf17, which we named ribonuclease MRP subunit P24
(RMP24) and RMP64, respectively. We
demonstrate that these two genes are specifically required for the activity of human
RNase MRP but not RNase P. The protein products of RMP24 and
RMP64 associate extensively and exclusively with the only known
human RNase MRP-specific component, MRP RNA, but not with the RNase P-specific H1
RNA. Our findings establish Rmp24 and Rmp64 as human protein components unique to
RNase MRP.

To identify factors affecting the essential human ribonuclear complexes
nuclear RNase MRP and RNase P, which are indispensable for translation due to
their established roles in processing the pre-rRNA ITS1 site 211 and pre-tRNA 5′-leader
sequences,1,3,11
we performed a genome-wide CRISPR sgRNA-based forward genetic screening that
detects translation defects using a dual-fluorescence reporter system. This
system can distinguish high-throughput defects in translation from defects in
transcription, which are otherwise indistinguishable, using a basic fluorescent
protein reporter, where a decrease in fluorescence could arise from either
defect.

As shown previously26
and illustrated for UPF1 in Figure 1F, the Fireworks cells (Figure S1A) exhibit a rightward
fluorescence shift upon the knockout of bona fide nonsense-mediated mRNA
degradation (NMD) components (Figures 1F
and 1G). In contrast, as observed
previously,26
translation defects, including those resulting from knockouts of
RMRP and RPPH1 (genes encoding the
catalytic components of RNase MRP and RNase P, respectively), induce a right and
downward fluorescence shift (Figures 1E,
1G, and S1C). This right and downward shift
occurs because the inhibition of translation reduces the susceptibility of the
premature termination codon (PTC)-containing RFP reporter (Figure S1A) to NMD, as NMD requires
active protein synthesis.27–31
Consequently, for the same-cell PTC-containing reporter, translation inhibition
and the resulting suppression of NMD lead simultaneously to (1) reduced
translation of the reporter and (2) an increase in its PTC-containing mRNA
available for translation. Together, these two opposing effects of translation
inhibition on the PTC-containing RFP Fireworks reporter result in an overall
increase in its fluorescence, whereas the fluorescence of the PTC-lacking GFP
Fireworks reporter decreases (Figures 1G,
S1A, and S1B). This response,
previously used to distinguish human bona fide NMD factors,26 enables efficient fluorescence-activated
cell sorting (FACS)-based isolation of cells with NMD-impacting defects in
translation, differentiating translation defects from those in both
transcription and NMD (Figure
S1B).

To minimize undesired enrichment of guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting NMD
factors in the forward genetic screen that detects translation defects via their
impact on NMD, we constructed a genome-wide lentiviral “omission”
CRISPR library consisting of 90,260 guide RNAs targeting 20,861 human genes and
deliberately lacking guide RNAs targeting known bona fide NMD factors (Figure 2A; Data S1).

We conducted three rounds of genome-wide iterative FACS-based forward
genetic screening (Figures 2B and S2) of the NMD omission
library (Figure 2A). During these screening
rounds, the Fireworks cells harboring guide RNAs that cause a right and downward
fluorescence shift (Figure 1G) were
enriched in the blue sorting gate (Figure
2B). The enrichment levels were 0.13%, 0.17%, and 1.48% (Figure 2B) after the first, second, and third
rounds, respectively (Figure
S2).

Deep sequencing of guide RNAs isolated from cells enriched after the
third screening round (Figure 2B) revealed
that guide RNAs targeting components shared by RNase P and RNase MRP, including
RPP20, POP5, RPP30,
RPP38, RPP29, and RPP14,
display some of the highest enrichment (Figure
2C; Data
S1). This high enrichment can be attributed to the concurrent disruption
of two pathways indispensable for translation—tRNA processing and
pre-rRNA processing—the combined effect of which would be expected to
produce a major two-pronged impact on translation and, consequently, NMD (Figure 2; Data S1).

Consistent with the enrichment of factors impacting translation, the
screening additionally enriched guide RNAs targeting tRNA synthetases, including
the highly enriched YARS (YARS1) and QARS
(QARS1); a component of the eukaryotic translation
initiation factor 3 (eIF-3) complex, EIF3I; components of the
small-subunit (SSU) processome, such as PWP2
(UTP1), IMP3, IMP4, and
RPS19BP1 (AROS); ribosome biogenesis
factors NOC4L (UTP19), NOL12,
and NOM1; ribosomal proteins RPS28,
RPS16, and RPS11; and numerous other
ribosome biogenesis factors (Data S1). Strikingly, guide RNAs targeting two poorly characterized
human genes, c18orf21 and c3orf17, exhibited
enrichment as high as that of components shared by both RNase MRP and RNase P
(Figure 2C; Data S1).

Individual FACS-based validation of guide RNAs targeting the
screening-enriched c18orf21 (RMP24) and
c3orf17 (RMP64), as well as nuclear RNase
MRP and RNase P components RMRP, POP5,
RPP14, RPP20, RPP29,
RPP30, RPP38, RPP40,
RPP21, and RPPH1, confirmed the right and
downward fluorescence shift (Figures 3A and
S3B) in the
Fireworks cell line (Figures
S1A and S1B), indicating a translation-mediated impact of their knockouts on NMD
(Figure 1G). Due to these similarities
and the strikingly high enrichment of c18orf21
(RMP24) and c3orf17
(RMP64), comparable to that of the components of nuclear
RNase MRP and RNase P (Figure 2C; Data S1), we assessed the
effects of the knockouts of c18orf21 (RMP24)
and c3orf17 (RMP64), obtained as shown in
Figure S3A, on the
activities of human nuclear RNase MRP and RNase P.

Unlike viability-based approaches, fluorescence-based enrichment of
knockout cells enables their efficient isolation well in advance of the onset of
lethality, facilitating the analysis of early knockout effects. As measured
using RT-qPCR across the ITS1 site 211 of the endogenous rRNA precursor, knockouts of
c18orf21 (RMP24) and
c3orf17 (RMP64), quantified in Figures 3D and S3B, led to approximately 6- and
12-fold increases, respectively, in the levels of the unprocessed pre-rRNA
precursor (Figure 3B, left graph). This
increase was consistent with an approximately 5- to 7-fold increase in the
levels of the ITS1 site 2-unprocessed pre-rRNA precursor resulting from the
knockouts of RMRP, POP5,
RPP14, RPP20, RPP29,
RPP30, RPP38, and RPP40
(Figure 3B, left graph; the knockouts
are quantified in Figures 3D, S3B, and S3C). To rule out off-target
effects of RMP24- and RMP64-targeting sgRNAs
on processing of the ITS1 site 2 of the endogenous pre-rRNA precursor, we
confirmed that additional sgRNAs toward RMP24 and
RMP64 replicated this effect (Figures 3B, right graph, and S3B). As negative controls,
knockouts of RPP21 and RPPH1, the unique
protein and RNA subunits of the human RNase P, respectively, produced no effects
on the cleavage of endogenous pre-rRNA ITS1 site 2 (Figure 3B).

In contrast to the impact of the knockouts of RMP24 and
RMP64 on the processing of pre-rRNA ITS1 site 2, the same
knockouts produced no increase in the levels of the endogenous RNase
P-unprocessed MALAT1-mascRNA precursor (Figure
3C, left graph). Similar to pre-tRNAs, the MALAT1-mascRNA precursor
requires RNase P for cleavage of its tRNA-like mascRNA structure.5,7 Unlike pre-tRNAs, which are transcribed by RNA
polymerase III (RNA Pol III) and whose expression depends on RNase P’s
function as an RNA Pol III transcription factor,39–41 the MALAT1-mascRNA precursor is transcribed by RNA Pol
II. Consistently, additional sgRNAs toward RMP24 and
RMP64 confirmed the lack of an increase (Figure 3C, right graph). To rule out detection limits
as a factor, we show that knockouts of the RNase P/MRP-shared and RNase
P-specific components POP5, RPP14,
RPP20, RPP29, RPP30,
RPP38, RPP40, RPP21, and
RPPH1 result in a 4.2- to 53-fold increase in the
intrinsically low levels of RNase P-unprocessed endogenous long non-coding RNA
(lncRNA) MALAT1 (Figure 3C). These findings
confirm the efficiency of the knockouts (quantified in Figures 3D, S3B, and S3C) and are consistent with the
essential roles of POP5, RPP14,
RPP20, RPP29, RPP30,
RPP38, RPP40, RPP21, and
RPPH19,25 in the activity of human
endogenous RNase P. Collectively, our data demonstrate that
RMP24 and RMP64 are specifically required
for the activity of human nuclear RNase MRP in pre-rRNA processing but are
dispensable for the cleavage of the endogenous tRNA-like MALAT1 mascRNA by RNase
P.

Since both RMP24 and RMP64 are
specifically required for the activity of human RNase MRP and not RNase P (Figures 3B and 3C), we examined whether Rmp24 and Rmp64 associate with MRP RNA in
human cells. We immunopurified these proteins from nuclear extracts and
quantified the amounts of MRP RNA and H1 RNA in the eluted fractions.

As shown in the top image of Figure
4A, FLAG purification of Rmp24 and Rmp64 resulted in 131- and
246-fold enrichment of MRP RNA, respectively, compared to that of the “no
ORF” (open reading frame) and Mettl1-FLAG42 negative controls. The enrichment of MRP
RNA in the Rmp24 and Rmp64 fractions exceeded that observed with the RNase
P-specific protein component Rpp21 (46-fold) and was comparable to the
enrichment seen with the RNase P/MRP-shared component, Rpp25 (191-fold).

By contrast, the same immunopurifications of Rmp24 and Rmp64 (Figure 4A, compare the bottom and top images)
did not enrich H1 RNA compared to that of the no-ORF negative control. In
comparison, immunopurification of the RNase P-specific protein component Rpp21
and the RNase P/MRP-shared component Rpp25 resulted in 640- and 331-fold
enrichment of H1 RNA, respectively, reflecting their known association with H1
RNA in the RNase P complex.

Together, these findings demonstrate a specific and preferential
enrichment of Rmp24 and Rmp64 with MRP RNA, the only known RNase MRP-specific
component, and no enrichment with H1 RNA, the RNase P-specific RNA component.
These results suggest that Rmp24 and Rmp64 represent previously unrecognized
protein components of human RNase MRP that are not shared with RNase P.

Whereas Protein BLAST44 for human Rmp64 yielded a negligible alignment score
(E-value of 0.61) with S. cerevisiae’s RNase
MRP-specific component Rmp1 (Figure S4A), the cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of
S. cerevisiae’s Rmp1 and the AlphaFold-predicted
structure of Rmp64 displayed the following similarities (Figures 4B and 4C): first, the overall arrangements of the four N-terminal α
helices (αI–αIV) comprising amino acids 5–123 in
yeast Rmp1 and 52–200 in human RMP64 are similar (Figure 4, compare 4B and 4C).
Second, twelve amino acids conserved between human Rmp64 and yeast Rmp1 (Figure S4A) exhibit
similar positions and orientations (Figures
4B and 4C, shown in white).
Third, several of these conserved amino acids—Asn72 (Asn25), Asn74
(Asn27), Arg78 (Arg30), Leu166 (Leu95), Glu168 (Gln97), Phe169 (Phe98), and
Leu172 (Leu101)—are located in the loops connecting α helices
αI and αII (loop I-II) and αIII and αIV (loop
III-IV) (Figures 4B and 4C). Critically, in the structures of yeast RNase
MRP,23,24 Rmp1 loops I-II and III-IV represent key
elements of substrate recognition: together with residues of Pop1 and Pop4, they
coordinate the ITS1 substrate backbone phosphates of C2 and
A3,23 enabling
the flipped state of the substrate nucleotide C4 (Figure 4B). Nevertheless, unlike yeast Rmp1, in which
Arg24 and Gln28 in loop I-II and Gln97 in loop III-IV are critical for
coordinating the flipped state of the substrate nucleotide C4 in the
yeast RNase MRP structure23
(Figure 4B), human Rmp64 carries Ser71,
Arg75, and Glu168 at the corresponding positions, potentially reflecting
species-specific requirements for recognizing and cleaving distinct pre-rRNA
ITS1 sequences.11,45

Although Protein BLAST44 for human Rmp24 produced a negligible alignment score
(E-value of 0.76) with S. cerevisiae’s RNase
MRP-specific component Snm1 (Figure S4B), the cryo-EM
structure of S.cerevisiae’s Snm1 and the AlphaFold-predicted
structure of Rmp24 displayed notable structural similarities: first, the
N-terminal regions of Snm1 and Rmp24 have an overall
αα-loop-βββ arrangement (Figure 4, compare 4D and 4E). Specifically, they
are comprised of two α helices, αI and αII, spanning amino
acids 5–46 in yeast Snm1 and 3–33 in human Rmp24, followed by a
single α-helical turn-containing loop formed by amino acids 47–92
in Snm1 and 34–54 in Rmp24, and three β-sheets
(βI–βIII) comprised of amino acids 93–117 in yeast
Snm1 and 55–114 in human Rmp24 (Figures
4D and 4E). Second, both human
and yeast proteins contain four conserved cysteines arranged in a CXXC …
CXXC motif (Figure
S4B). In the structure of yeast Snm1, these four cysteines coordinate
Zn+2, forming an experimentally determined C2C2-type zinc
finger23,46 (Figure
4D). AlphaFold predicts a similar zinc-finger arrangement for Rmp24
(Figure 4E). In both the cryo-EM
structure of yeast Snm1 and the AlphaFold-predicted structure of Rmp24, one
cysteine, C106 (C104), is located within βII, another (C109 in Snm1 and
C107 in Rmp24) is located in the βII-βIII turn, and the remaining
two cysteines (C61 and C64 in Snm1 and C43 and C46 in Rmp24) reside in the
αII-βI loop.

Consistent with our functional and pull-down analyses, the structural
similarities of human Rmp24 and Rmp64 to the only two known yeast RNase
MRP-specific (i.e., not present in RNase P) protein components, Snm1 and Rmp1
(Figures 4B–4E), further support the RNase MRP-specific roles of
human Rmp24 and Rmp64.

By leveraging the requirement of RNase MRP for reporter translation, we
uncovered two human RNase MRP-specific protein components, Rmp24 and Rmp64, whose
existence had long remained a puzzle.9,11,33 Their discovery greatly simplifies the study
of RNase MRP independently from RNase P, which was previously hindered by the shared
nature of all known RNase MRP protein subunits.

Supporting our finding of the role of RMP64 in RNase
MRP-mediated pre-rRNA ITS1 site 2 processing, Rmp64(c3orf17/Nepro47) is known to localize in the
nucleolus,48,49 and its mouse knockout exhibits
mis-localization of 18S rRNA.48
Mass spectrometry identified RNase P/MRP-shared subunits in Rmp64
preparations49 and,
separately, Rmp24, Rmp64, and RNase P/MRP components in preparations of monopolar
spindle-one-binder 3C (MOB3C).50 Mutations in RMP64 are linked to CHH and
AD (CHH-AD),51,52 rare genetic disorders also associated with
mutations in RMRP19 (RNase MRP-specific RNA) and POP153 (a shared RNase P/MRP component).
Notably, both reported pathogenic CHH-AD-associated RMP64
mutations, R94C and L145F, map to its N-terminal region51,52
(Figure S4A), predicted
to contain a four-helix bundle analogous to the four rRNA substrate-recognizing
α helices of yeast Rmp1 (Figures 4B and
4C). Genome-wide co-essentiality mapping
and supervised machine learning predicted the association of
RMP24(c18orf21) and RMP64
with RNase P/MRP complexes.54,55 Separately, recent genome-scale
computational analysis of perturbative maps of transcriptional and morphological
data also suggested RMP24’s involvement in the RNase MRP
complex.56

In addition to establishing the roles of RMP24 and
RMP64, our findings confirm that human
RPP29(POP4), RPP14,
POP5, RPP20, RPP30,
RPP38, RPP40, and RMRP are
required for pre-rRNA ITS1 site 2 cleavage (Figure
3B). We cannot draw such conclusions for POP1 due to
insufficient knockout efficiency (data not shown) or for RPP25,
which shows no effect (data not shown), possibly due to its redundancy with
RPP25L, as reported for RNase P.57,58
Consistent with the reported direct interaction between bacterially expressed
GST-tagged human Rpp21 and in-vitro-transcribed human MRP
RNA,59 we observe that
Rpp21-FLAG co-purifies a significant amount of MRP RNA—46 times that of the
negative controls (Figure 4A, top). While the
functional significance of this association remains unclear, in agreement with human
RPP21 being specifically required for activity of RNase P, we confirm that
RPP21 is not required for ITS1 site 2 cleavage (Figure 3B).

In summary, we demonstrate the existence of two human nuclear MRP-specific
protein components (Figure 4F) that have eluded
identification for over three decades. Our identification of RMP24
and RMP64 paves the way for studying substrate specificity
determinants of RNase MRP, uncovering its additional potential endogenous human RNA
substrates, further delineating its cellular function, and contributing to its
future structural studies.

Whereas the presented results establish the requirement of human
RMP24 and RMP64 for in
vivo pre-rRNA ITS1 cleavage and their association with MRP RNA, the
in vitro activity of the purified Rmp24- and
Rmp64-containing RNase MRP complex, as well as reconstitution of its activity
from heterologously expressed purified components, has yet to be demonstrated.
While the predicted structural similarities between human Rmp64 and yeast Rmp1
indicate that human Rmp64 similarly functions as a determinant of RNase MRP
substrate specificity, experimental confirmation of its role in substrate
recognition will require biochemical and structural analyses. Understanding the
specific molecular functions of Rmp24 and Rmp64 will greatly benefit from the
structure determination of the human RNase MRP complex.

at the corresponding locus in the reference human genome; and (iv)
“mutations” if they contained nucleotide substitutions not
