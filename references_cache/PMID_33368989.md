# Carpenter syndrome in a patient from Tanzania.
**Authors:** Lodhia J, Rego-Garcia I, Koipapi S, Sadiq A, Msuya D, Spaendonk RV, Hamel B, Dekker M
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.a.62015](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.a.62015)

## Content

Carpenter syndrome (acrocephalopolysyndactyly type II) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. It was clinically diagnosed in a female baby with polysyndactyly and craniosynostosis in a referral clinic in Northern Tanzania. In the RAB23 gene, a previously described homozygous variant c.82C>T p.(Arg28*) was detected that results in a premature stop codon. Both parents were demonstrated to be heterozygous carriers of this variant. Herewith, its pathogenicity is proved. A literature search suggests this is the first molecularly confirmed case of Carpenter syndrome in continental Africa.

1 | INTRODUCTION
Carpenter syndrome (CS; OMIM #201000) is a rare autosomal reces-
sive disorder. It is also known as “acrocephalopolysyndactyly type II ”
and was first reported by Carpenter in the beginning of the 20th
century. The cardinal features include craniosynostosis, poly-
syndactyly, obesity, umbilical hernia, and cardiac defects (Haye et al.,
2014). Other features that have been reported are brachydactyly,
molar agenesis, genu valgum, hypogenitalism, learning disability
(Jenkins et al., 2007), polysplenia (Alessandri et al., 2010), and coxa
valga (Islek, Küçüködük, Incesu, Selçuk, & Aygün, 1996). CS has a
wide clinical interfamilial and intrafamilial variability. It has an inci-
dence of one in one million live births. Abnormal head shape is pre-
sent in all individuals due to craniosynostosis with affected sagittal,
metopic, coronal, and lambdoid sutures in decreasing order of fre-
quency (Jenkins et al., 2007).
Pathogenic variants in the RAB23 gene have been identified
to be responsible for CS. It encodes a small GTPase of the Ras
superfamily involved in vesicle trafficking and acting as a
negative regulator of hedgehog (HH) signaling (Jenkins
et al., 2007). The variant c.434T>A encoding p.(Leu1145*) has
been reported at least in one allele in five families from Northern
Europe (three of them homozygous) (Jenkins et al., 2011). In a
haplotype study (Jenkins et al., 2007) a founder effect was
suggested. Apart from c.434T>A p.(Leu145*), in the same study,
three nonsense and on e missense variants have been identified.
There are other disorders associated with disturbed HH signal-
ing. It is not surprising to find polysyndactyly and brachydactyly,
mediated by sonic hedgehog (SHH) , and craniosynostosis (medi-
ated by Indian hedgehog [IHH]) (Twigg et al., 2012). Obesity,
however, is not yet explained b ased on this mechanism. Twigg
et al. described in 2012 Carpenter syndrome-2 (OMIM #614976)
with lateralization defects, like dextrocardia and situs inversus,
associated with variants in the MEGF8 gene.
Although there are patients of African origin (Jenkins et al., 2007),
we have not been able to find any clinical description of CS in Africa
in scientific literature, except for a family with genetically confirmed
CS from Comoros Islands, situated in the South-West Indian Ocean
(Alessandri et al., 2010). As far as published work concerns we hereby
describe the first genetically confirmed CS patient from continental
Africa.

2 | CASE PRESENTATION
A 7-month-old female baby and her mother visited the surgical outpa-
tient clinic of Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Center in Moshi, Tanzania.
This is a referral hospital in Northern Tanzania. She reported that her
child was born with an abnormal head size and shape with abnormal
fingers and toes, but otherwise developing well. It is the firstborn child
to the parents, born at term by an uncomplicated vaginal delivery and
with a birth weight of 4,000 g (above 90th percentile). The mother
had an uneventful pregnancy, but reported a miscarriage 2 years ear-
lier at 10-weeks of gestation.
On examination, the child was 10.5 kg (97th percentile) with a
length of 69 cm (47th percentile). Weight for length was above
98th percentile (indicating obesity). She was able to sit without
support, crawled, responded to visual, and acoustic stimuli, recog-
nized her parents and did not like to be held by others. She
followed objects, grabbed, and held them with her hands. Examina-
tion of the head showed an asymmetric skull with cloverleaf shape,
metopic ridge and temporal bulging, arched eyebrows, telecanthus,
epicanthal folds, down slanting palpebral fissures, and anteverted
nares (Figure 1). Upon abdominal examination, there was a reduc-
ible umbilical hernia of 2 cm in diameter (Figure 1). The external
female genitalia were normal. Musculoskeletal examination of the
limbs showed broad thumbs with brachydactyly, syndactyly, and
polydactyly. The feet showed broad halluces and bilateral talipes
equinovarus (Figure 1).

A plain chest X-ray was essentially normal with clear lung fields
and normal appearing cardiac shadow. Cardiac and abdominal –pelvic
ultrasonography studies showed no abnormalities. Plain X-ray of
hands and feet revealed postaxial polydactyly (Figure 1). Head CT-
scan showed hydrocephalus with dilated lateral and third ventricles
with posterior fossa crowding and craniosynostosis. Three-
dimensional (3D) virtual-reconstruction images showed the cloverleaf
shaped skull bone configuration (Figure 1).
No one else in the family was known to have similar abnormali-
ties. The child's father had polydactyly in both hands. The extra digits
were surgically removed when he was a child. This likely refers to the
ulnar sides but there was no documentation of this procedure to con-
firm. On inspection, the thumbs of the father were somewhat broad
and flat, but no scars were seen on radial and ulnar sides. X-rays of his
hands were normal.
The clinical diagnosis was CS. Venous blood was sampled from
the patient and both parents, and was sent to the Genome Diagnos-
tics Laboratory of Amsterdam University Medical Center, Amsterdam,
The Netherlands. Targeted DNA sequence analysis of the RAB23 gene
(Ref. Seq.: NM_016277.3) showed homozygosity for the variant
c.82C>T p.(Arg28*). That results in a premature stop codon, thereby
confirming the clinical diagnosis of CS. Targeted analysis of the
father's and mother's RAB23 gene revealed heterozygosity for this
pathogenic variant in both parents. The parents have been counseled
about the findings and its consequences in terms of management and
recurrence risks.
3 | DISCUSSION
Our patient has most of the characteristics present in CS, with four
out of five of those most consistently found in the literature
(Alessandri et al., 2010; Islek et al., 1996; Jenkins et al., 2007), all-
owing clinical diagnosis.
DNA analysis showed homozygosity for the variant c.82C>T p.
(Arg28*). This is a nonsense variant which has been reported only
once before in literature (Jenkins et al., 2011) in two Australian sib-
lings of Northern European origin. These siblings inherited two vari-
ants on the maternal allele, c.82C>Tp.(Arg28*) and c.434T>Ap.
(Leu145*), and one variant on the paternal allele, c.434T>Ap.
(Leu145*). Due to homozygosity of the pathogenic variant c.434T>A
p.(Leu145*), it was not clear whether the variant c.82C>T p.(Arg28*)
was pathogenic. Our patient proves pathogenicity of this variant.
The polydactyly of the father is most likely coincidental. Nowhere
in the literature had a parent an abnormality which could be hypothe-
sized as a heterozygous manifestation, while mosaicism due to a sec-
ond postzygotic variant seems very unlikely.
A multidisciplinary approach is crucial in the management of such
a complex entity as CS (Kadakia, Helman, Healy, Saman, & Wood-
Smith, 2014).
The initial diagnosis of CS in this patient was made by physical
examination and shows the importance of dysmorphology training as
an element in the medical curriculum, even in low resource settings.
