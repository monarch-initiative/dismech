# A Novel Frameshift Variant c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54) Leading to Extended Incorrect Protein C Termini in HOMER2 Causing Autosomal Dominant Nonsyndromic Hearing Loss.
**Authors:** Peng LT, Wang WQ, Huang SS, Liu M, Yang SY, Kang DY, Wang X, Gao X, Yuan YY, Xu JC
**Journal:** J Clin Lab Anal (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jcla.70137](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcla.70137)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Heterozygous variant in HOMER2 is associated with autosomal dominant nonsyndromic hearing loss (ADNSHL), designated as the locus of DFNA68. Only five pathogenic variants in HOMER2 have been identified to date.

AIMS/OBJECTIVES: The aim of this study is to investigate the molecular etiology of ADNSHL in a four-generation Chinese family.

MATERIAL AND METHODS: Whole exome sequencing analysis was conducted to detect the disease-causing variant. Co-segregation analysis was performed using Sanger sequencing.

RESULTS: The family exhibited autosomal dominant, progressive, post-lingual, nonsyndromic sensorineural hearing loss, similar to that observed in previously reported DFNA68 families. Unlike the initially high-frequency hearing loss observed in the previously reported families, the young proband in our study (IV:4, 18 years old) exhibited typical low-frequency hearing loss. A novel frameshift variant, c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54), in HOMER2 was identified, which co-segregated with the hearing loss phenotype in the family. The variant deletes 7 nucleotides, leading to an extended incorrect protein C terminus. Based on the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines, the variant c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54) is classified as likely pathogenic.

CONCLUSIONS AND SIGNIFICANCE: These findings may help expand the spectrum of pathogenic variants of the HOMER2 gene and provide a molecular interpretation for these patients with ADNSHL.

Hearing loss is one of the most common sensory disorders in humans. Genetic factors play an important role in hearing loss, contributing to approximately 60% of cases of congenital hearing loss. Autosomal dominant nonsyndromic hearing loss (ADNSHL) accounts for 15%–20% of hereditary hearing loss. To date, 64 genes have been implicated in ADNSHL (http://hereditaryhearingloss.org; Feb 19, 2025).

Given the genetic heterogeneity of hearing loss, the responsible genes encode a diverse array of functionally distinct proteins involved in various auditory processes. These include ion channel regulation, fluid homeostasis maintenance, synaptic transmission mediation, and hair cell bundle development/morphogenesis [1]. Disruption of any component within this complex biological cascade can lead to hearing impairment.

Deafness, autosomal dominant 68 (DFNA68, OMIM #616707) is a rare subtype of ADNSHL caused by heterozygous variants in the HOMER2 (MIM #604799, Homer Scaffold Protein 2, NM_004839.4) gene. In 2015, HOMER2 was first reported as the ADNSHL gene in a five‐generation European family [2]. Since then, another four dominant variants in HOMER2 have been reported worldwide associated with DFNA68. The HOMER2 gene includes 9 exons, encodes HOMER2 protein, a polypeptide of 343 amino acids with a mass of about 39.5 kDa. The protein primarily functions in regulating intracellular calcium homeostasis, organizing the actin cytoskeleton, and facilitating synaptic plasticity and signaling pathways in neurons [3, 4, 5]. The variants may affect the stability of the HOMER2 protein, leading to its degradation and loss of normal function [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. The hearing loss associated with HOMER2 variants is post‐lingual with an onset in the first or second decades of life and progression to profound hearing loss [2, 6, 7, 9]. Variants may disrupt the interaction between HOMER2 and calcium channels or other cytoskeletal proteins, thereby affecting the function of hair cells in the inner ear [2, 9]. Due to the limited number of cases and variants reported, establishing a precise genotype–phenotype correlation remains challenging.

Here we identified one Chinese family with ADNSHL, in which affected individuals present post‐lingual and progressive hearing loss. One disease‐segregating novel frameshift variant, c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54) in the HOMER2 gene, was detected by whole exome sequencing.

The study was approved by the PLA General Hospital Ethics Committees. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients or parents of the minor subject for participation in the clinical and genetic research study, and publication of their clinical data.

This family is a four‐generation Chinese family with post‐lingual nonsyndromic sensorineural hearing loss. DNA samples were obtained from 11 members of this family (II:3, II:4, II:5, II:7, III:2, III:6, III:7, III:10, III:12, IV:2, and IV:4). A detailed medical history was obtained through a questionnaire. Computed tomography (CT) of the temporal bone was performed in the index patient (IV: 4). A physical examination, otoscopy, and pure tone audiometric examination (at frequencies from 125 to 8000 Hz) were performed to identify the phenotype. The hearing loss range was described depending on the pure tone audiometry (PTA) parameters: low frequency, 125–500 Hz; mid frequency, 1000–2000 Hz; and high frequency, 4000–8000 Hz. Prelingual and post‐lingual hearing loss was classified according to the onset of prominent hearing loss. Prelingual hearing loss is present before speech develops and usually begins before 3 years of age, whereas post‐lingual hearing loss occurs after the development of normal speech [11].

According to pure‐tone audiometry (PTA) of the better ear, the average hearing threshold level at four air conduction frequencies (500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz) was used to define the severity of hearing loss. According to the 2021 WHO classification of hearing loss, 20‐ < 35 dB HL (decibels hearing level) was defined as mild, 35‐ < 50 dB HL was defined as moderate, 50‐ < 65 dB HL was defined as moderate to severe, 65‐ < 80 dB HL was defined as severe, and > 80 dB HL was defined as profound.

Whole exome sequencing (WES) genetic analyses were performed on 11 individuals, including five affected individuals (II:5, III:2, III:7, IV:2 and IV:4) and six unaffected individuals (II:3, II:4, II:7, III:8, III:11, and III:13). Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral venous blood according to the manufacturer's instructions (TianGen, Beijing, China). The details of the gene capture, sequencing and bioinformatics analysis methods have been described in detail previously [12]. The reference sequence of HOMER2 used in alignment was NM_004839.4. Sequence variants were checked in the GnomAD population databases (http://gnomad‐old. broadinstitute.org/). The pathogenicity of candidate variants was assessed using the REVEL (https://sites.google.com/site/revelgenomics/). Manual classification of the variants was conducted based on American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG)/Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) guidelines for genetic hearing loss [13, 14]. Potential pathogenic variants finally screened by these analyses were confirmed using Sanger sequencing.

This family was a four‐generation Chinese family with ADNSHL and included nine affected patients (Figure 1A). Five affected and six unaffected individuals of this family participated in the present study. The ages at onset of the affected individuals ranged from 17 to 24 years. The audiogram of the 18‐year‐old proband (IV:4) with an onset age of 17 years showed low‐frequency moderate hearing loss. The audiogram of IV:2 showed all‐frequency moderate to severe hearing loss; this individual had an onset age of 24 years, which was the latest in this family. Most affected individuals in this family complain of tinnitus but do not experience vertigo, and their intelligence and speech functions are normal. Tandem gait and Romberg tests did not reveal any symptoms of vestibular dysfunction in all family members. CT scans of the proband ruled out middle‐ and inner‐ear malformations (Figure 1D). The general physical examinations for all family members were normal.

The median sequencing depth of the exon target regions exceeded 100× and the sequencing depth of 98% of the target regions exceeded 30×. The variants remaining after filtering were manually assessed based on the frequency/presence in the known SNP databases, any previous association with disease, predicted functional impact, any nucleotide/amino acid conservation, and the potential detrimental biochemistry of any amino acid substitution observed. According to the autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, only variants that were heterozygous in the affected siblings were selected as candidates (Table 2).

Using Sanger sequencing, eleven participating family members (five affected and six unaffected) were genotyped to identify the candidate variants. One heterozygous frameshift variant c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54) (chr15:85,632,407–85,632,413del, GRCh38/hg38) in HOMER2 (NM_004839.4) remained after the filtering process and validation by Sanger sequencing (Figure 1B). The HOMER2 c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54) change occurs in an evolutionarily highly conserved region across different species in the C‐terminal region resulting in 2 amino acid changes at positions 342–343, and extending 52 amino acids (Figure 2). This variant has not been previously reported. According to the ACMG classification system, the c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54) is classified as a likely pathogenic variant (PM2, PM4, PP1, PP3).

In 2015, Richard et al. reported a five‐generation European family segregating post‐lingual progressive ADNSHL, and determined that the family's causal variant was c.554G>C (p.Arg185Pro) in HOMER2 using WES [2]. Variant in HOMER2 is considered a rare cause of hereditary hearing loss. To date, only five heterozygous variants in the HOMER2 gene have been reported worldwide in five isolated families of European, Chinese, Sicilian, Italian and Spanish origins [6, 7, 8, 9].

Hearing loss caused by a variant in the HOMER2 gene is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, suggesting that a single copy of the mutated gene is sufficient to cause the disease. Affected individuals with variants in HOMER2 exhibit bilateral post‐lingual progressive hearing loss that typically begins in the high frequencies during the first or second decade of life. The degree of hearing loss ranges from mild to severe hearing loss and the annual threshold deterioration is between 1.2 and 1.6 dB HL per year across all frequencies [2, 6, 7, 8, 9] (Table 1). It was accompanied with or without tinnitus [7]. Symptoms of vertigo, balance disorders, and vestibular system involvement were not reported. In the family investigated in this study, the age of onset of hearing impairment was 17–24 years, which progressed with age. It is noteworthy that, unlike the initially high‐frequency hearing loss observed in the other five previously reported families (Table 1), the young proband in our study (IV:4, 18 years old) exhibited typical low‐frequency hearing loss (< 1000 Hz), an ascending audiogram pattern. The older patients (43 years old) displayed flatter audiograms, indicating hearing loss across all frequencies. Accordingly, the different forms of auditory configuration may present different stages of the disease.

HOMER2 belongs to the HOMER family of scaffolded proteins, which is related to involved in Ca2+ homeostasis, cytoskeletal organization and synaptic plasticity [5, 15, 16]. HOMER2 is highly expressed in tissue and cell types that are critical for sensory perception, neuronal function, and cellular homeostasis, with particularly high levels in the inner ear's hair cells and the central nervous system [2, 17]. The HOMER2 protein contains two domains: a conserved N‐terminal Enabled Vasodilator‐stimulated phosphoprotein Homology 1 (EVH1) domain (1‐111aa) that interacts with proline‐rich sequences, and a C‐terminal coiled‐coil region (173‐317aa) with a CDC42‐binding domain (191‐283aa) and two leucine zipper motifs (LZA, 238‐296aa and LZB, 311‐339aa), responsible for multimerization between HOMER family members and interaction with the CDC42 small GTPase [4]. The multimerization and interaction capabilities of HOMER2 are critical for its role as a scaffolding protein in the regulation of various cellular processes [18, 19, 20, 21, 22].

Five reported pathogenic variants of HOMER2 associated with DFNA68 include p.Arg185Pro, p.Thr187_Thr188del, p.Pro267AlafsTer10, p.Met270HisfsTer9, and p.Ter344TrpfsTer10. As for the c.188 C>T (p.Pro63Leu) variant, although identified in a sporadic patient with hearing loss, no family history or clinical data were reported, and the variant classification is of uncertain significance [23] (Table 1). All five variants affect the C‐terminal coiled‐coil region, and three of these result in a premature stop codon, leading to the formation of truncated proteins or extended incorrect proteins. The HOMER2 c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54) identified in this study is classified as ‘likely pathogenic’. It is the third frameshift variant and the second variant that leads to an extended incorrect protein C terminus. This variant results in changes of the last two amino acids and the translation of an additional 52 abnormal amino acids, ultimately generating an extended HOMER2 protein. Extended incorrect terminus variants (EITs) have been previously observed in different disease‐associated genes. For example, a loss‐of‐function EIT was reported in the NR3C2 gene, leading to a marked reduction in expression, which could explain its functional impact [24]. On the other hand, it has been shown that a WT1 EIT can lead to a gain‐of‐function phenotype, promoting WT cell proliferation [25]. These data illustrate that EITs may not necessarily lead to a loss of function. Christel Vaché and colleagues identified a nonstop substitution (c.1064A>G, p.Ter344TrpfsTer10) in HOMER2, which converts the translational termination codon (TAG) into a tryptophan codon (TGG), predicting an extension of the protein by 10 amino acids. RNA analysis of the proband indicated that the mutant HOMER2 protein escapes the non‐stop decay pathway [9]. They inferred that the 10‐amino‐acid protein extension might inhibit the multimerization process, which typically occurs through the C‐terminal domains of the proteins, or it might compete with other partner proteins, resulting in a dominant‐negative effect. It is hypothesized that this variant could potentially cause hearing loss through a mechanism similar to that of the HOMER2 p.Ter344TrpfsTer10 variant, via a dominant‐negative mechanism that may involve inhibiting multimerization or competing for other partner proteins.

Two main hypotheses have been proposed to explain the molecular impact of the HOMER2 variants on hearing loss. The first suggests that HOMER2 regulates actin dynamics in stereocilia through its interaction with CDC42, a small GTPase that fine‐tunes actin turnover. The second hypothesis centers on HOMER2's role in cytoplasmic Ca2+ regulation, with the protein regulating Ca2+ handling proteins such as TRPC and PMCA channels. However, the exact molecular mechanism involved in the pathogenicity of the HOMER2‐associated hearing loss has not been determined. Overexpression of the mutant homer2 in zebrafish resulted in inner ear defects, and mouse mutants with a targeted deletion of Homer2 (Homer2

−/−
) exhibited early‐onset progressive hearing loss, similar to the phenotype observed in patients with the HOMER2 variant. Nevertheless, no differences in the number or morphology of cochlear hair cells and spiral ganglions were observed between wild‐type and Homer2

−/−
 mice. The exact mechanism underlying the hearing deficit in patients with the HOMER2 variant still needs to be elucidated [2].

In summary, we identified a novel c.1023_1029del (p.Asp342ArgfsTer54) frameshift variant in the HOMER2 gene that causes ADNSHL in a Chinese family. This variant marks the sixth pathogenic variant in the HOMER2 gene associated with hearing loss. The data from this study expand the spectrum of pathogenic variants in the HOMER2 gene, offer a molecular interpretation and diagnosis of these patients with ADNSHL, and contribute to the development of genetic counseling for inherited deafness. However, this study has limitations, particularly the lack of longitudinal audiometric data needed to fully characterize the progressive nature of hearing loss associated with this variant. Further studies should include long‐term audiometric follow‐up and functional investigations to elucidate HOMER2's precise role in auditory pathways and the mechanistic effects of this variant.
