# ERMA (TMEM94) is a P-type ATPase transporter for Mg(2+) uptake in the endoplasmic reticulum.
**Authors:** Vishnu N, Venkatesan M, Madaris TR, Venkateswaran MK, Stanley K, Ramachandran K, Chidambaram A, Madesh AK, Yang W, Nair J, Narkunan M, Muthukumar T, Karanam V, Joseph LC, Le A, Osidele A, Aslam MI, Morrow JP, Malicdan MC, Stathopulos PB, Madesh M
**Journal:** Mol Cell (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.molcel.2024.02.033](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2024.02.033)

## Content

Intracellular Mg2+ (iMg2+) is bound with phosphometabolites, nucleic acids, and proteins in eukaryotes. Little is known about the intracellular compartmentalization and molecular details of Mg2+ transport into/from cellular organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). We found that the ER is a major iMg2+ compartment refilled by a largely uncharacterized ER-localized protein, TMEM94. Conventional and AlphaFold2 predictions suggest that ERMA (TMEM94) is a multi-pass transmembrane protein with large cytosolic headpiece actuator, nucleotide, and phosphorylation domains, analogous to P-type ATPases. However, ERMA uniquely combines a P-type ATPase domain and a GMN motif for ERMg2+ uptake. Experiments reveal that a tyrosine residue is crucial for Mg2+ binding and activity in a mechanism conserved in both prokaryotic (mgtB and mgtA) and eukaryotic Mg2+ ATPases. Cardiac dysfunction by haploinsufficiency, abnormal Ca2+ cycling in mouse Erma+/- cardiomyocytes, and ERMA mRNA silencing in human iPSC-cardiomyocytes collectively define ERMA as an essential component of ERMg2+ uptake in eukaryotes.

In eukaryotes, Mg2+ is the most abundant divalent cation essential for cellular functions and is associated with several clinical conditions, including migraines, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cancer, preeclampsia, and obesity 1– 3 . Most intracellular Mg2+ (iMg2+) is bound with ATP, ribosomes, polyphosphates, and other molecules. Ligand-induced mobilization of the remaining unbound free iMg2+ can control several cellular functions 1 , 4– 9 . Intracellular ion mobilization is tightly regulated through organelle-specific ion channels and transporters. Since 1974, several findings alluded that iMg2+ has a second messenger role 4 , 7 , 10– 12 . The proposition of iMg2+ as a dynamic second messenger raises important fundamental question: How do Mg2+ transporters facilitate rapid Mg2+ influx when the intra- and extracellular [Mg2+] are nearly comparable? Recently, we revealed that the glycolytic end-product lactate induces rapid depletion of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) Mg2+ stores, followed by robust mitochondrial Mg2+ (mMg2+) uptake. This finding indicates that the iMg2+ is compartmentalized and implies the existence of Mg2+ transporters across the subcellular membranes. Indeed, we recently confirmed that Mitochondrial RNA splicing 2 (Mrs2) comprises the bona fide mMg2+ uptake machinery in mammalian cells 2 , however, none of these aforementioned Mg2+ transport candidates are ER-specific.

In general, the SERCA pump belongs to the family of P-type ATPases along with plasma membrane Ca2+ ATPase (PMCA), Na+/K+ ATPase, and H+/K+ ATPase 13– 17 . A notable feature of the sarcoendoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ ATPase (SERCA) pump and P-type ATPases, in general, is to couple the hydrolysis of ATP to the movement of ions across a biological membrane 14 , 18 . In vertebrates, three distinct genes encode for SERCA 1, 2, and 3 and produce more than 10 isoforms. These different isoforms of the SERCA pump are variedly expressed in various tissues and can be distinguished by their rate of Ca2+ uptake from the cytosol to ER lumen. Our recent finding indicated that lactate stimulates a rapid release of ERMg2+ followed by ER refilling via an unknown mechanism 2 . This observed phenomenon is similar to the cytosolic Ca2+ (cCa2+) clearance mechanism, wherein the SERCA pump takes up elevated cCa2+ into the ER lumen 19– 26 .

The topology of bacterial Mg2+-transporting P-type ATPase (MgtA) is thought to be analogous to SERCA 5 , 6 , 27 . Here we asked if such a protein exists on the ER membrane that is involved in ERMg2+ uptake. We employed bioinformatics approaches (blast search, topology predictions, and AlphaFold2 modeling), wherein we compared E. coli Mg2+ transporter MgtA/CorB characteristics against all human proteins. RNAi-mediated knockdown of several putative P-type ATPase candidates revealed TMEM94 (hereafter referred to as ERMA; Endoplasmic Reticulum Mg2+ ATPase) as a potential transporter of ERMg2+ uptake in metazoans. The embryonically lethal nature of ERMA deletion in a mouse model and multi-organ functional defects associated with human mutations in ERMA 28 . Thus, ERMA is a highly conserved ER Mg2+ P-type ATPase in eukaryotes.

Recent studies demonstrated that the free [Mg2+]i is compartmentalized indicating a tightly regulated iMg2+ transport mechanism exists 2 , 3 , 29 . Having established that lactate elicits ERMg2+ release, we next developed an assay that measures the ER release followed by refilling. Because the intracellular Mg2+ indicator Mag-Green cannot be specifically compartmentalized, here we generated an ER-targeted genetically encoded Mg2+ sensor, Mag-FRET-1, to monitor ER Mg2+ dynamics (Figure 1A) 2 , 30 . Hepatocytes were transduced with an adenoviral-delivered Mag-FRETER sensor, and the proper localization was confirmed using confocal microscopy (Figure 1A). Consistent with our previous work, L-lactate stimulation resulted in an ERMg2+ release as indicated by the loss of FRET signal (Figure 1B; circle 1); moreover, the subsequent uphill recovery of the FRET signal was an indication of ER refilling (Figure 1B; circle 2). After a steady state was reached, we added a bolus of MgCl2 (10 mM) to assess the ERMg2+ uptake driven by the plasma membrane Mg2+ entry (Figure 1B; circle 3) 2 , 29 . The apparent FRET efficiency (EA) was calculated using EA = B-A × β – C × γ/C. The FRET channel is denoted as B and its signal is derived from acceptor channel. Here β refers to donor cross-talk; β=B/A which generates donor only reference correction factor. γ refers to and corrects for acceptor cross-excitation; γ=B/C (Figure S1A–S1C) (see details in methods). The mean raw traces derived from Cerulean (Gray colour) and Citrine (red colour) signals display the dynamic changes of individual channel fluorescence upon lactate or MgCl2 addition (Figure S1A). Next, we calculated the non-normalized mean FRET trace derived from panel S1A (Figure S1B). Finally, we normalized FRET trace was derived from panel S1B after dividing basal FRET signal (Figure S1C). After the validation, we next tested the suitability of Mag-FRETER sensor for ERMg2+ uptake measurement. ER-targeted MagFRET sensor expressing hepatocytes were permeabilized and resuspended in intracellular medium with 50 μM Mg-ATP and 150 μM EDTA. After reaching the steady-state minimum baseline FRET, various concentrations of MgCl2 (0.15, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, and 10.0 mM) were added and FRETSE was calculated. Our data indicate that ER-MagFRET shows systematically increasing responses over the entire range of 150 – 10,000 μM extra-ER MgCl2 addition indicating the existence of ERMg2+ uptake machinery (Figure S1D). Thus, our Mag-FRETER assay is suitable for ERMg2+ uptake candidate identification in mammalian cells.

The comprehensive BLAST using bacterial MgtA as a search query identified over 30 candidates, including seven poorly characterized candidates and a previously undescribed human P-type ATPase like protein ERMA (NP_001308077.1; 1366 amino acids) (Figure 1C and Figure S1E) 5 , 6 . Although ERMA remains relatively unstudied, a report alluded that patients presenting with recessive truncating ERMA mutations manifest with neurodevelopmental disorders (global developmental delay and intellectual disability), congenital heart malformations, and facial dysmorphism 28 . Having this knowledge, we prioritized these seven candidates for loss-of-function screen using RNA interference (RNAi). Our targeted RNAi screen identified that RNAi silencing of ERMA significantly reduced ERMg2+ uptake following ERMg2+ depletion by lactate stimulation (Figure 1D and 1E). Suppression of ERMg2+ uptake was further confirmed with individual small interfering RNAs (siRNA) targeted against different regions of ERMA mRNA (Figure S1F–S1I). Note that the NCBI database analysis also highlighted that ERMA has a P-type ATPase like domain in the C-terminal region similar to MgtA, which mediates cation transport ATPase activity. These computational and experimental data further supported our hypothesis that ER Mg2+ uptake protein machinery exists in metazoans (Figure 1F).

P-type ATPases are integral membrane proteins found in all forms of life, where their primary function is to pump ions or lipids across the membrane in which they are situated 15 . All P-type ATPases transfer a phosphate released by ATP hydrolysis to a phosphorylation site in a DKTGTL motif conserved throughout evolution. During their pumping cycles, P-type ATPases oscillate between two major conformational states, termed E1 and E2, which can exist in both phosphorylated and non-phosphorylated forms 15 , 17 . Multiple sequence alignment predicted that there is a DKQGIL motif present in ERMA (Figure S2A and S2B). Having observed the reduction of ERMg2+ uptake by RNAi mediated ERMA knockdown (KD), we next examined ERMg2+ dynamics upon lactate stimulation in multiple human and mouse cell lines and primary hepatocytes expressing MagFRETER. Acute knockdown of ERMA (72 hours) in HepG2 cell line or knockout of Erma in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) resulted in low ER-Mag-FRET signal under resting conditions (Figure S3A and S3B). In order to compare the uptake rate between WT and knockdown cell lines, and KO MEFs, we normalized the FRET signal to basal values. We observed that siRNA-mediated knockdown of ERMA in HeLa, primary murine hepatocytes, HepG2, and C2C12 resulted in significant suppression of ERMg2+ uptake (Figure S3C–3J), while the scrambled siRNA treated cells showed normal ERMg2+ uptake. Thus, ERMA is required for ERMg2+ refilling in multiple mammalian cell types.

We next performed in silico prediction of ERMA topology. The consensus transmembrane domain prediction analysis revealed that ERMA is a multi-pass transmembrane protein similar to P-type ATPases (Figure 2A and 2B) 15 , 31 , 32 . In support, the AlphaFold2 prediction model also shows that ERMA has 10 transmembrane regions 33 . The phylogenetic analysis revealed that ERMA is widely distributed in eukaryotes (Figure 2C). Our sequence alignment data further show that it is highly conserved, and the human database reported that it is ubiquitously expressed across human tissues (Figure 2D and Figure S2A) 34 , 35 . Although computational analysis predicted bacterial MgtA is an orthologue of human ERMA, no clear homologue was identified in yeast. We next conducted RT-qPCR to determine the murine tissue distribution of ERMA and the transcript analysis showed that ERMA was differentially but ubiquitously expressed (Figure 2E). The GMN residues form a unique signature motif for the transport of Mg2+ in bacterial CorA, yeast mitochondrial Mrs2p, and mammalian mitochondrial Mg2+ transporter Mrs2 2 , 29 , 36– 39 . Importantly, upon sequence alignment of ERMA among metazoans, we observed the existence of a conserved GMN motif at the C-terminal region of ERMA (Figure S2A).

We next investigated the subcellular distribution of human and mouse ERMA by confocal imaging as well as subcellular fractionation. To visualize ERMA localization, COS-7 cells were transiently co-transfected with C-terminal mRFP-tagged human ERMA (HuERMA-mRFP) and Sec61β-GFP, and the localization was visualized using a confocal microscope (Figure 2F). HuERMA and Sec61β-mCherry colocalization and intensity profile analyses revealed a near-complete overlay of both fluorescence in the ER compartment (Figure 2F and 2G; Figure S4A and S4B top panels). Additionally, we co-transfected Sec61β-GFP and Adv-Sec61β-mCherry in COS-7 cells to confirm that these fluorescent-tagged proteins properly target the ER (Figure S4A and S4B bottom panels). To confirm the endogenous ERMA localization, we generated a polyclonal antibody against human ERMA C-terminal peptide. The C-terminal ERMA peptide sequence is fully conserved in human, mice, and several other species (Figure S2A). Primary murine hepatocytes were infected with adenovirus expressing ER marker Sec61β-mCherry, and cells were stained with ERMA primary antibody, and secondary antibody conjugated with Alexafluor488 for immunofluorescence. Similar to human ERMA, the mouse orthologue is also localized in the ER (Figure 2H and Figure S4C). Furthermore, we sub-fractionated C-terminal FLAG-tagged HuERMA expressing HEK293 cells into mitochondrial, microsomal, or cytosolic fractions and immunoblotted for ERMA localization. ERMA and SERCA2 were present in the ER fraction confirming that it is indeed an ER resident protein (Figure 2I).

We next performed a fluorescence protease protection (FPP) assay to determine whether ERMA is an ER integral membrane protein 40 . C-terminal mGFP-tagged mouse ERMA (muERMA-mGFP) and a single pass transmembrane ER localized N-terminal mCherry-tagged STIM1 (mCherry-N-STIM1) were transiently co-expressed in COS-7 cells (Figure S4D) 41 . Digitonin permeabilized cells were treated with proteinase K. Upon treatment, muERMA-mGFP signal was rapidly lost, while the ER-luminal STIM1 mCherry fluorescence intensity was unaffected (Figure S4E and S4F). After the complete disappearance of the GFP signal, permeabilized cells were subsequently treated with 1% Triton X-100, and a complete loss of mCherry signal was observed (Figure S4E and S4F). This result revealed that the GMN motif containing the C-terminal region is facing the cytosol. To further probe the N- and C-terminal topologies of ERMA, N- or C-terminal FLAG-tagged ERMA plasmids were expressed in HEK293 cells and ER fractions were treated with proteinase K and immunoblotted (Figure S4G). Both N-terminal and C-terminal ERMA FLAG tags were cleaved from the full-length protein, suggesting that both termini are exposed to the cytosol (Figure S4G). Collectively, the transmembrane prediction analyses and our experimental results suggest that ERMA is an ER integral membrane protein with ten transmembrane domains (Figure S4H).

To characterize the function of ERMA as a P-type ATPase, we utilized global Erma knockout (KO) mouse model. Since the global deletion of Erma in mice is embryonic lethal, we isolated WT and Erma+/− hepatocytes or WT, Erma+/− and Erma−/− MEFs for ERMg2+ uptake studies 28 . Our data indicate that a loss of a single allele exhibited haplo-insufficiency with a 50% reduction of Erma mRNA (Figure S5A); we next tested the Erma-mediated ERMg2+ uptake in murine primary hepatocytes wherein WT and Erma+/− hepatocytes were transduced with the ER MagFRET sensor. Upon lactate stimulation, while a rapid ER Mg2+ depletion was seen in both cell types, the ERMg2+ uptake was markedly reduced in Erma+/− hepatocytes (Figure S5B). Having observed an essential role for ERMA in ERMg2+ uptake, we next developed an assay to directly show that ERMA transports Mg2+ from the cytosol. Because of the lack of radiolabelled Mg2+ to determine the Mg2+ uptake in microsomes, we transduced our adenoviral ER-targeted MagFRET sensor along with the ER marker Ad5a-Sec61β-mCherry in primary WT hepatocytes. 48 hours post infection, microsomes were isolated for FRET analysis using confocal microscopy (Figure 3A inset). Freshly isolated microsomes were embedded in low melting agarose gel, made with Ca2+ free intracellular medium, that avoids the perturbation of microsome suspension upon stimulation. The gel-immobilized microsomal suspension was incubated with or without 50 μM Mg-ATP before a bolus of 25 mM MgCl2 addition. In the presence of Mg-ATP, the FRET signal was increased overtime, but it was absent in the ATP free condition, suggesting ERMg2+ uptake is an ATP-dependent active process (Figure S5C and S5D). This in vitro assay was adapted to directly measure ERMg2+ uptake in WT and Erma+/− murine hepatocyte microsomes. As expected, an increased ERMg2+ uptake was observed in WT microsomes, while it was markedly reduced in Erma+/− hepatocyte microsomes upon 25 mM MgCl2 stimulation (Figure 3A left and middle panels). Additionally, Erma+/− hepatocyte microsomes showed a significant reduction of ERMg2+ uptake rate (Figure 3A left and right panels). Erma+/− pregnant mice embryos (E9.5-E11.5) were used for MEFs isolation. WT and Erma−/− MEFs expressing Mag-FRETER were stimulated with 5 mM L-lactate, and ERMg2+ release and refill were measured (Figure 3B and 3C). L-lactate-induced ERMg2+ release was observed in both WT and Erma−/− MEFs (Figure 3C and 3D), but the refilling was markedly reduced, indicating that Erma is required for ERMg2+ uptake (Figure 3C–3E). To further confirm ERMA’s role in ERMg2+ uptake, human (Hu)ERMA-mRFP was ectopically reconstituted in Erma−/− MEFs (Figure 3B). Remarkably, HuERMA restored ERMg2+ uptake in Erma−/− MEFs potentially explaining the functional role of ERMA in ERMg2+ uptake (Figure 3C–3E). We next explored whether the loss of ERMA affects IP3R-mediated ERCa2+ release and SERCA-dependent Ca2+ uptake in Erma−/− MEFs. First, to distinguish the properties of ERMA from the SERCA pump, we investigated the ERCa2+ levels in Erma−/− MEFs. WT or Erma−/− MEFs were transfected with ER-targeted Ca2+ sensor R-CEPIA1er, and MEFs were stimulated with purinergic receptor agonist ATP (100 μM) to determine ERCa2+ depletion and subsequent SERCA-mediated Ca2+ refilling 42 , 43 . MEFs stimulated with ATP exhibited a rapid ERCa2+ depletion followed by ERCa2+ refill in both WT and Erma−/− that was indistinguishable among the genotypes (Figure 3F and Figure S5E and S5F; circles 1, 2, and 3 denote ER Ca2+ basal, release, and uptake). In parallel, we also tested the IP3R-mediated ERCa2+ release and SERCA-dependent Ca2+ uptake using a FRET-based ER-localized Ca2+ sensor D1ER 42 . Similar to R-CEPIA1er, the D1ER showed that ER Ca2+ release and SERCA-mediated uptake were unaffected in Erma−/− MEFs; further, SERCA protein abundance was unaltered (Figure S5G circles 1 and 2 denote ER Ca2+ release, and uptake and Figure S5H). Since [Ca2+]ER was unaffected by the loss of Erma, we next asked whether ERCa2+ depletion-induced plasma membrane Ca2+ entry (store-operated Ca2+ entry; SOCe) was intact 41 , 44– 47 . WT and Erma−/− MEFs were treated with thapsigargin (Tg) to elicit ERCa2+ depletion under extracellular Ca2+ free condition followed by a bolus of Ca2+ addition. Our data shows that the SOCe process was unaltered in Erma−/− MEFs, indicating that ERMA is not directly involved in SOCe (Figure S5I). These data reinforce that ERMA is directly associated with ERMg2+ uptake.

Although ERMA exclusively localized in the ER compartment, we investigated whether the loss of Erma alters Mrs2-mediated mMg2+ uptake in MEFs. Both WT and Erma−/− MEFs were transduced with either ER or mitochondrial-targeted MagFRET sensors. Although the ERMg2+ uptake was absent in Erma−/− MEFs, we found a similar or higher mMg2+ uptake in Erma−/− MEFs (Figure S5J–S5L). In parallel, we examined whether Mrs2 deletion impacts Erma-mediated ERMg2+ uptake. Hepatocytes isolated from WT or Mrs2−/− mice were transduced with ER MagFRET sensor 2 , 3 . Analysis of ERMg2+ dynamics revealed that an abrogation of Mrs2-mediated mMg2+ uptake did not affect ERMg2+ release, while the ERMA-mediated ERMg2+ uptake was normal (Figure S5M and S5N). Although ER-localized ERMA and the mitochondrial MRS2 channel are required for cytosolic Mg2+ dynamics, we speculate that both ER and mitochondria sequester cytosolic Mg2+ upon ERMg2+ release or plasma membrane-mediated Mg2+ entry. Intriguingly, the loss of ERMA exhibited an elevation of cytosolic Mg2+ upon L-lactate stimulation, indicating an absence of ERMg2+ uptake in Erma−/− MEFs (Figure S5O). Since cytosolic Ca2+ clearance is controlled by multiple mechanisms 48– 51 , our data indicates that the unknown plasma membrane Mg2+ efflux mechanism may be much slower than Ca2+ efflux components (Figure S5O). Since the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) is the driving force for Mrs2-mediated mMg2+ uptake, we measured ΔΨm and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in WT and Erma−/− MEFs. Although the ΔΨm and OCR were normal in Erma−/− MEFs (Figure S5P–S5S), we cannot preclude the perturbation of these parameters and lactate metabolism in other cell types. To distinguish the role of ERMA from the SERCA pump and plasma membrane Ca2+ entry, we next tested either inhibition of SERCA activity with Tg or extracellular nominal Ca2+ (Ca2+ free media) controls ERMg2+ release and uptake. Under these experimental conditions, ERMg2+ uptake was normal in WT MEFs, but it was absent in Erma−/− MEFs suggesting that the functional role of ERMA is distinct from SERCA activity (Figure S5T and S5U). Together, these results indicate that ERMA is an ER-resident protein involved in ERMg2+ uptake.

The presence of the P-type ATPase domain in this family of proteins is necessary for the activity 14– 17 . A similar P-type ATPase domain is present in ERMA (Figure S2B). The occurrence of frequent frame shift mutations between the N-terminal and ATPase regions of human ERMA 28 and the unique Mg2+-selective GMN motif prompted us to test the role of these regions in ERMg2+ uptake (Figure S2A). We mutated the ATPase domain and GMN residues in human ERMA and reconstituted in Erma−/− MEFs since the reconstitution of full-length human ERMA restored ER Mg2+ uptake in these knockout MEFs (Figure 4A and Figure 3B–3E). The ectopic expression of ERMAΔATPase mutant in both WT and Erma−/− MEFs exhibited a marked reduction of ERMg2+ uptake (Figure 4B and 4C). Similarly, the reconstitution of GMN mutant (ERMAIKL) failed to restore ERMg2+ uptake, indicating that these domains are required for ERMA activity (Figure 4D and 4E). However, the C-terminal GMN domain is conserved across species with the exception of the protozoan Toxoplasma gondii where asparagine is substituted with tryptophan. We next asked whether GMW also constitutes ERMg2+ uptake, and therefore we generated a Hu-ERMA GMW mutant and expressed it in WT and Erma−/− MEFs (Figure 4F). Surprisingly, Hu-ERMAGMW mutant did not suppress ERMg2+ uptake in WT MEFs (Figure 4G). In parallel, the reconstitution of the Hu-ERMAGMW mutant in Erma−/− MEFs fully restored ERMg2+ uptake, indicating that the asparagine alone within GMN is not a determinant of ERMg2+ uptake (Figure 4G). Collectively, our data show that the conserved ATPase and GMN motifs are required for ERMg2+ uptake.

The MgtA and MgtB bacterial Mg2+ ATPase pumps, conserved among prokaryotes and eukaryotes, are active even under low extracellular [Mg2+]. Physiologically, these pumps are greater than 1000-fold more selective for Mg2+ over Ca2+ at similar ion concentrations 6 , 52 . Our phylogenetic tree comparing mammalian SERCAs, PMCA1, Na+/K+ ATPases, and the two ERMA isoforms with MgtA and MgtB show a common clade shared only by the bacterial Mgts and ERMA (Figure 5A). To explore the potential Mg2+ binding mechanism of ERMA relevant to ion transport (distinct from the Mg2+ involved in autophosphorylation/dephosphorylation), we first generated an AlphaFold2 model of ERMA isoform 2 33 (Figure 5B). Structural alignment of the ERMA isoform 2 model with experimentally determined E1-Ca2+ SERCA1a 18 and E1-Na+ Na+/K+-ATPase 53 using TM-align 54 revealed that these proteins likely adopt similar folds (Figure 5B–5E). These E1 states represent the high-affinity ion binding states associated with transport. Interestingly, an E1-Mg2+ crystal structure has also been determined for SERCA1a 55 , although SERCA1a does not physiologically transport Mg2+, and not only does the 3D structural alignment of ERMA with E1-Mg2+ SERCA1a suggest a similar fold, but also reveals Tyr1142 as a potential ligand for Mg2+ binding in the ion transport pathway (Figure 5F and 5G). Consistent with the 3D structural alignment, multiple sequence alignment between the ERMA, MgtA, and MgtB reveals a sequence conservation of the ERMA Tyr1142 with the Tyr in the Mgts, critical for Mg2+ transport (Figure S6A). To validate the critical role of a conserved tyrosine residue for Mg2+ ion transport activity, we mutated HuERMA tyrosine 1142 and reconstituted in Erma−/− MEFs (Figure 5H; Figure S6B). The ectopic expression of HuERMAY1142A mutant in Erma−/− MEFs did not rescue the ERMg2+ uptake (Figure 5I and 5J). Collectively, our structural analyses suggest that ERMA is closely related to prokaryotic Mgts, with ERMA harboring a similarly conserved Tyr1142 on TM6, vital for ion transport activity only in Mg2+ ATPases.

The monomeric form of all mammalian ATPase family protein complexes transports ions and other metabolites 14 , 16 , 17 . However, it has been reported that oligomerization of P-type and F-type ion pumps occurs for stabilization 56– 58 . Since ERMA is a P-type Mg2+ ATPase and disease-related human frame shift mutations affect the ATPase domain and the unique Mg2+ selective GMN motif regions, we next investigated the importance of these regions for protein stability 28 . First, we performed size exclusion chromatography (FPLC) to determine the oligomeric nature of ERMA 59 . Our FPLC analysis showed that full-length ERMA was eluted exclusively as a higher-order oligomeric complex, which was confirmed under reducing SDS-PAGE (Figure 6A). To confirm whether endogenous SERCA2 exist as a dimer, these fractions were probed for SERCA2. Remarkably, we found that the SERCA2 forms a dimer indicating that both these ion transport ATPase family members form higher order oligomers (Figure S6C). In parallel, FPLC fractions were probed for endogenous ERMA oligomeric complex using ERMA antibody (Figure S6D). Our results indicate that ERMA exist as an oligomeric complex in situ. We extended the oligomeric analysis to human disease-causing ERMA mutant proteins. Although FPLC separation of ERMAΔ491–1110 exhibited higher order oligomer, the monomeric units were also seen in this truncated construct, indicating that amino acid residues between 490–1110 may not be strictly necessary for protein stability and higher-order oligomer formation (Figure 6B). Nevertheless, the truncated ERMAΔ491–1110 forms multimeric units to a lesser extent, possibly with the endogenous full-length ERMA (Figure 6B). Similarly, HEK293 cells expressing ERMA human disease and GMN mutants were separated by FPLC. ERMA mutantΔ265–321 also exhibited higher-order multimeric units, however, this mutant was more susceptible to degradation, indicating a possible role in disease development (Figure 6C). Since the GMN motif is crucial for ERMA-mediated ERMg2+ uptake activity (Figure 5D and 5E), we next asked whether the C-terminal GMN motif is essential for oligomer formation. Our FPLC analysis indicated that ERMAIKL mutant protein was unstable with increased levels of degradation. However, non-degraded ERMA exhibited higher-order oligomer formation (Figure 6D).

To further confirm and characterize higher order oligomer formation in situ, we performed cross-linking experiments. ER fractions obtained from HEK293 expressing full-length ERMA were subjected to immunopurification as well as paraformaldehyde cross-linking. Remarkably, ERMA was able to form trimeric complexes by immunoprecipitation or chemical cross-linking (Figure 6E). We next asked whether reconstitution of loss-of-function P-type or GMN ERMA mutants alter this trimeric ERMA complex. HEK293 cells were reconstituted with full-length ERMA-GST and FLAG-tagged ERMA WT or its mutants for immunoprecipitation (Figure 6F). The HEK293 cell lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation with FLAG antibody. Transfection of HEK293 cells with these tagged proteins showed considerable ectopic protein expression (Figure 6F; FLAG input). FLAG-tagged ERMA protein was immunoprecipitated using anti-FLAG antibody, while GST or GST-tagged ERMA alone was not pulled down, demonstrating antibody specificity (Figure 6F; IP-FLAG). Full-length ERMA-GST was able to interact with all FLAG-tagged full-length ERMA and its mutants (Figure 6F; IB GST), indicating that homomeric interacting regions reside outside the mutation sites. Together, these findings suggest that ERMA undergoes oligomer complex formation, possibly for stability and higher Mg2+ transport into ER compartment (Figure 6G).

One of the prominent features of ERMA human mutations is cardiac abnormalities, prompting us to evaluate the importance of ERMA in cardiac function 28 . We isolated adult cardiomyocytes from WT and Erma+/− mice since homozygotes are embryonic lethal. As iCa2+ cycling is essential for excitation-contraction coupling in the heart, we analyzed iCa2+ handling properties of the Erma+/− cardiomyocytes. Spontaneous and uniform Ca2+ transients were observed in WT cardiomyocytes, while these Ca2+ transients were perturbed in Erma+/− cardiomyocytes, (Fig. 7A–7C). Although the aberrant Ca2+ transients were generated by Erma+/− cardiomyocytes, the increased frequency was observed in Erma+/− (Fig. 7A and 7B). Furthermore, Erma+/− cardiomyocytes failed to display synchronized Ca2+ waves and iCa2+ cycling parameters as seen in WT myocytes (Fig. 7C and 7D). Since cardiac Ca2+ cycling is modulated by [Mg2+]i, our prediction is that the lack of clearance of cytosolic Mg2+ in Erma+/− cells might alter iCa2+ transients 60 , 61 (Figure S5O). To recapitulate the mouse Erma+/− cardiomyocyte phenotype, we next used a healthy control iPSC line to generate iPSC-cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CM) to investigate the consequence of ERMA deficiency. Although Ca2+ cycling is a well-established mechanism in cardiomyocytes, it is unknown whether cardiomyocytes exert spontaneous iMg2+ dynamics. iPSC-CMs were transfected with scrambled or ERMA siRNAs. 24 hours post-transfection, cardiomyocytes were transduced with an ER-targeted MagFRET sensor. After 72 hours, scrambled or ERMA siRNA-treated myocytes were imaged for iMg2+ dynamics. Our results show that iPSC-CMs generate spontaneous Mg2+ oscillations that was absent in ERMA siRNA knockdown (Figure S7A–S7C). We next asked whether the changes in iMg2+ dynamics caused Ca2+ cycling dysregulation. Remarkably, the spontaneous Ca2+ cycling was perturbed in ERMA siRNA-treated iPSC-CMs suggesting that suppression of ERMA-dependent iMg2+ uptake is likely leading to aberrant cardiac Ca2+ cycling and dysfunction (Figure S7D–S7E). These data suggest that sarcoplasmic Mg2+ is necessary for cardiomyocyte Ca2+ cycling.

We next evaluated the in vivo physiological changes of haploinsufficient mice. Echocardiography was performed on wild-type (WT) and Erma+/− 3–5-month (young) and 11–12-month-old (Old) mice (Fig. 7E–H and Table S1). Ejection fraction (EF) of younger and older WT mice were comparable. Although the younger Erma+/− mice exhibited lower EF, the comparison of EF between WT and Erma+/− cohorts was not statistically significant (p=0.12). However, the older Erma+/− mice had a lower EF compared to WT (53% versus 67%) (Fig. 7I). Parameters of diastolic function such as E wave velocity, E/A ratio, and E/E’ showed no significant difference between younger WT and Erma+/− mice. Interestingly, estimated LV mass was ~30% lower and the fractional shortening was also lower in the older Erma+/− mice (Fig. 7J and 7K). Strikingly, the E/A ratio significantly increased in older Erma+/− mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 7L). However, the heart rates of both genotypes were comparable (Fig. 7M). Collectively, these results suggest that Erma haploinsufficiency exhibited cardiac abnormalities, in particular, systolic and diastolic dysfunction over time compared to their littermate WT counterparts that correlates with abnormal Ca2+ cycling in Erma+/− cardiomyocytes.

In metazoans, the compartmentalization of divalent cations is essential for signal transduction and cell function. The subcellular compartmentalization of Mg2+ was a mystery until the identification of a ligand for ERMg2+ release from the ER and subsequent uptake by the mitochondria 2 . The comprehensive computational, biochemical and image-based analyses showed ERMA is a bona fide ER localized multi-pass transmembrane protein with Mg2+ uptake activity. Intriguingly, our proteolytic studies and AlphaFold2 model reveal that ERMA encompasses 10 transmembrane units that are like Na+,K+ ATPase, PMCA, and SERCA. ERMA not only has a P-type ATPase domain but also a unique Mg2+ binding GMN motif at the C-terminal region of ERMA that is absent in all other ATPase family members. The human mutations and genetic ablation of individual Na+,K+ ATPase subunits result in distinct pathological phenotypes. Similarly, genetic mutations and conditional deletion of SERCA and PMCA isoforms result in cytosolic Ca2+ clearance defects and organ dysfunction 21 , 62 . Similar to other P-type ATPases, global deletion of Erma in mice results in embryonic lethality. It is also important to highlight that patients presenting with recessive truncating ERMA mutations manifest neurodevelopmental disorders, congenital heart malformations, and facial dysmorphism, underscoring the importance of ERMg2+ homeostasis in cell function and organism development 28 .

The phylogenetic analysis revealed that Group III ATPase family members are involved in H+ or Mg2+ transport mechanisms in bacteria, protozoans, yeast, and plants. However, the multicellular organisms’ Group IIIB ATPases are yet to be identified 32 . Our biochemical and functional studies indicate that the previously undescribed ERMA is an intracellular Mg2+ transport ATPase that is closely related to bacterial MgtA, suggesting it possibly lies in Group III and expands the family. Although prokaryotic and eukaryotic P-type ATPases have been named after the conserved DKTGTL motif, the mammalian ERMA possess an atypical P-type loop (DKQGIL) (Figure S2B). The mutagenesis of DKQGIL motif in ERMA exhibited loss-of-function (Figure 4C), however, future studies are warranted to understand the functional role of non-canonical residues (T-Q or T-I) in the ERMA. Because bacterial MgtA and MgtB P-type ATPases are functionally similar to mammalian ERMA, it was expected that these cation ATPases would make use of the same molecular architecture to transport Mg2+ into the ER/SR. Indeed, our AphaFold2 model predicts that ERMA harbours a Tyr residue (Tyr1142) within TM6 critical for Mg2+ binding associated with ion transport, based on structural alignment with E1-Mg2+ SERCA1a 55 ; however, SERCA isoforms do not possess this Tyr residue, highlighted as critical for Mg2+ transport 52 . Our data reveals that the mutation of this tyrosine residue disrupted the Mg2+ transport mechanism (Figure 5). Similarly, mutagenesis of the unique GMN motif in ERMA reduced ERMg2+ uptake suggesting a functional significance (Figure 4E). It remains to be discovered whether these critical residues directly control Mg2+ transport into ER. It is also tempting to speculate on the GMN motif existence in the ERMA for moonlighting functions such as heteromeric interaction with regulatory subunits or iMg2+ sensing. Having established the role of MgtA as a bacterial Mg2+ ATPase, it is still unproven whether MgtA translocates protons as a counter charge 63 . Although ERMA is an essential component of ERMg2+uptake, it remains to be elucidated whether ERMA is an electrogenic or electroneutral P-type ATPase.

Our discovery demonstrates that ER is the intracellular Mg2+ compartment that releases robust Mg2+ upon lactate stimulation, indicating that ER could be the main reservoir for free Mg2+ ions 2 . Apart from the lumen of the ER, the protein synthesis machinery ribosomes also sequester a pool of Mg2+ ions for its stability as well as function 64 . Further biochemical, biophysical, and structural studies will shed light on the electrochemical properties of ERMA as a ERMg2+ P-type ATPase. Since ERMA is a major molecular component of the ER Mg2+ uptake machinery characterized here, we speculate that ER luminal Mg2+ is essential for various ER functions, including protein folding, glycosylation, and lipid synthesis, and its absence could lead to perturbation of signal transduction and detrimental physiological outcomes.

Even though the combination of integrative systems tools, targeted RNAi screen, as well as global deletion of ERMA by CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing in murine model revealed that ERMA is essential for ERMg2+ uptake, our study do not completely rule out whether auxiliary protein machinery are required for Mg2+ refilling in the ER. Our studies have not yet identified the existence of other redundant systems in metazoans. Since the ERMg2+ release mechanism is still unknown, identifying this candidate will determine the rate of ERMA mediated ERMg2+ uptake under resting and active states. Although SERCA are H+/Ca2+ ATPase pumps, our study did not reveal whether ERMA is likely a H+/Mg2+ ATPase in metazoans. Since we found embryonic lethality from the global deletion of Erma, conditional or inducible model systems will be necessary to address the critical role of ERMA for embryonic development and adulthood. These models will reveal the cause and effect of ERMA in cellular metabolism and correlate the human ERMA gene mutations associated with multi-organ functional defects. Although our experiments applying Mg2+ addition to microsomes under Ca2+ free conditions directly measured ERMg2+ uptake, we did not rule out whether ERMagFRET-1 sensor partly sensitive to ERCa2+. Since Erma knockout mouse model exhibited embryonic lethality, the influence of gender, sex, and age-related parameters are lacking in this study. Our identification of ERMA as an ERMg2+ uptake machinery will aid in understanding the role of ERMg2+ in cellular signal transduction and pathogenesis.

To assess the mitochondrial membrane potential, WT and Erma−/− MEFs were plated on 25-mm glass coverslips and loaded with ΔΨm indicators tetramethylrhodamine, ethyl ester (TMRE, ex/em 556/610 nm, 100 nM) and dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123 500/536 nm, 10μM), and stained with Hoechst 33342 in serum-free medium for 30 min (37°C). Confocal images were acquired using a 63x oil objective. ΔΨm was quantified using ImageJ software.

MEFs isolated from WT or Erma−/− (3 × 105 cells/well) were plated on gelatin-coated 96-well Seahorse XF Cell Culture Microplates (Agilent #101085-004). After 24 hours, the media was replaced with XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent # 103015-100) assay media supplemented with glucose, glutamine, and pyruvate and incubated in a non-CO2 incubator for 1h at 37°C. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured in an XF96 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, Agilent) at 37°C after calibration using Seahorse XF Calibrant solution (Seahorse Bioscience, Agilent) in a CO2-free incubator overnight, according to manufacture protocol. During the run, MEFs sequentially challenged with oligomycin, FCCP, and a mixture of antimycin A and rotenone at concentrations of 2 μM, 5 μM, and 1 μM, respectively, at the indicated time points. Data were collected using Agilent Seahorse Wave 2.6.1 software 2 .

ER fractions were prepared from HEK-293 cells expressing N or C-termini FLAG tag-ERMA and were incubated with different concentration gradients (0, 10, 50, and 100 µg/ml) of Prot-K for 40 min (37°C). This was followed by arresting the reaction by heating the samples at 70°C for 5 mins. Samples were then subjected to western blot analysis. Crosslinking analysis was performed on ER fractions, and IP purified full-length ERMA FLAG proteins were treated with or without 0.25% formaldehyde at room temperature for 30 mins, following which the reaction was arrested with Tris buffer (pH 7.5; final concentration 50 mM) and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, the proteins were separated under reducing conditions and western blotting was performed and probed with FLAG antibody to detect the cross-linked ERMA protein.

The human ERMA (UniProt ID: Q12767-3) structural model was generated using AlphaFold2 (v2.2.0) 33 on the Shared Hierarchical Academic Research Computing Network (SHARCNET: www.sharcnet.ca) of the Compute Canada/Digital Research Alliance of Canada. A total of 5 predictions were made (1 seed per model), using a maximum template date of 2022-11-01 and all available genetic databases. The highest confidence model based on predicted LDDT was taken for visualization and analysis. 3D structural alignments of the AlphaFold2 ERMA model with experimentally determined E1-2×Ca2+ (1T5T.pdb), E1-3×Na+ (7E1Z.pdb) and E1-1×Mg2+ (3W5B.pdb) P-type ATPase structures were done using TM-Align 54 . All structure images were generated using PyMOL (Version 2.4, Schrödinger, LLC).

All data are expressed as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analysis was done using a Two-tailed Student’s unpaired t-test for one or multiple samples and One Way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons or regression/correlation analysis when required. Statistically significant was defined as p<0.05. All experiments were conducted at least three times unless specified otherwise. GraphPad Prism was used for statistical testing. GraphPad Prism and Canvas 11 were used to generate graphs and plot the figures.
