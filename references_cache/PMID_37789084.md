# Two new patients with acromesomelic dysplasia, PRKG2 type-identification and characterization of the first missense variant.
**Authors:** Akgun-Dogan O, Díaz-González F, de Lima Jorge AA, Onenli-Mungan N, Menezes Andrade NL, de Polli Cellin L, Ceylaner S, Barcellos Rosa Modkovski M, Alanay Y, Heath KE
**Journal:** Eur J Hum Genet (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41431-023-01472-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41431-023-01472-z)

## Content

Acromesomelic dysplasia, PRKG2 type (AMDP, MIM 619636), is an extremely rare autosomal recessive skeletal dysplasia characterized by severe disproportionate short stature presenting with acromesomelia, mild metaphyseal widening of the long bones and mild spondylar dysplasia. To date, only four variants have been reported; one nonsense, one splice-site, and two frameshifts in five AMDP families. Here, we report the first missense variant and a second splice-site variant in PRKG2 in two patients with clinical and radiological features of acromesomelic dysplasia. Furthermore, functional studies of the novel missense variant, p.Val470Gly, revealed that it was unable to down-regulate FGF2-induced MAPK signaling and, thus, would be predicted to cause growth delay. Hence, this report expands the mutational spectrum in skeletal dysplasias associated with PRKG2 variants. In addition, we propose recognizable facial features with acromesomelic dysplasia, PRKG2 type.

Introduction

Biallelic variants in PRKG2 (protein kinase cGMP-dependent type II gene) cause acromesomelic dysplasia, PRKG2 type (AMDP, MIM 619636), a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by severe disproportionate short stature presenting with acromesomelia, mild metaphyseal widening of the long bones, and mild spondylar dysplasia [1]. To date, four homozygous loss of function PRKG2 variants have been reported in five families: a nonsense (p.Arg569*), splice-site (c.1635-1 G > A), and two frameshifts (p.Asn164Lysfs*2; p.Asp761Glufs*34) [1–3]. Affected individuals present with a similar skeletal phenotype in four of these families; however, three siblings with a homozygous frameshift variant encoding a larger protein presented with severe platyspondyly with anterior beaking and mild metaphyseal changes, but milder acromesomelic shortening. The phenotype may be misdiagnosed with spondylometaphyseal dysplasia [2].

PRKG2 encodes for the cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) dependent protein kinase II (cGKII), which acts as a downstream mediator of the C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP)/natriuretic peptide receptor-B (NPR-B) pathway. The role of PRKG2 in endochondral ossification and longitudinal growth has also been widely supported by several animal models developed postnatal dwarfism with shortened limbs and other skeletal anomalies, similar to those observed in AMDP patients [4–6]. The involvement of this signaling pathway in skeletal growth has been widely demonstrated as variants in both NPR-B and CNP cause several skeletal dysplasias and growth disorders [7]. Biallelic variants in NPR2 are responsible for Acromesomelic dysplasia, Maroteaux type (AMDM, MIM 602875), which represent the most severe phenotype; heterozygous variants in NPR2 and NPPC, encoding CNP, are associated with milder phenotypes [8–11].

Here, we report two additional novel variants, the first biallelic missense variant and a splice-site variant, in two children with AMDP connected via collaboration between growth disorders/skeletal dysplasia research groups from Turkey, Brazil and Spain. We present their clinical and radiological phenotype as well as functional studies confirming the pathogenicity of the missense variant.

Clinical cases and methods

Patient 1 (Turkey)

The proband is a 7-year-old girl referred to our pediatric genetics clinic at age two due to disproportionate short stature. She was the second child of first cousin’s parents. Prenatal history was uneventful, birth weight was 3000 g (−0.72 SDS), and height was 50 cm (0.28 SDS). Relative macrocephaly and slow linear growth were noted at 9 months old. At 2 years, she presented with a height of 76 cm (−3.37 SDS), a weight of 10.5 kg (−1.24 SDS), and her arm span to height (A/H) ratio was 0.947. Both parents had average stature, mother (160 cm, −0.1 SDS) and father (180 cm, 0.9 SDS), and the elder sibling had average growth. Physical examination revealed mildly disproportionate short stature with lordotic posture, mildly short fingers with fetal pads, and large halluces (Fig. 1A, C). Facial features included a prominent forehead, thick eyebrows, synophrys, broad nasal bridge, anteverted nares, and wide philtrum (Fig. 1A, C). Generalized hirsutism, widening of wrists, mild limitation in elbow extension, and interphalangeal joint laxity were also noted. Neurological examination was normal. Skeletal radiographs revealed platyspondyly with anterior beaking of thoracolumbar vertebral bodies, bowing of the radius and ulna, mild metaphyseal irregularities of the long bones, moderate brachydactyly and short and broad phalanges with cone-shaped epiphyses (Fig. 2). Karyotype analysis was 46,XX. Acromesomelic dysplasia, Maroteaux type (AMDM, MIM 602875), was suspected, but NPR2 sequencing was negative. At five years and ten months, height was 95 cm (−4.06 SDS), weight 15.5 kg (−1.97 SDS), and occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) 52 cm (0.67 SDS). GH treatment was started at the age of six. Singleton whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed.

Patient 2 (Brazil)

The second proband, an 8-year-old female, was enrolled into an ongoing prospective project where WES is used to evaluate cases of growth disorders of unknown cause. She was first evaluated by a local pediatric endocrinologist due to a severe short stature. She is the only child of a healthy non-consanguineous couple from a small isolated town in south Brazil (5000 inhabitants). Both parents have a borderline average height, the mother (155 cm, −1.2 SDS) and the father (162.8 cm, −1.8 SDS). There are three paternal half-siblings with normal growth, a maternal uncle with severe short stature (142 cm), and a similar phenotype. She was born at term (39 weeks) after an uneventful pregnancy, but small for gestational age for length: birth length 42 cm (−3.5 SDS), weight 2550 g (−1.5 SDS) and head circumference of 32 cm (−1.3 SDS). Her developmental milestones were compatible with her peers, and her school performance was adequate. At the first evaluation, the patient was prepubertal; her height was 101 cm (−5.1 SDS), weight 16 kg [body mass index (BMI) SDS: 0.3], her arm spam was 98 cm (−4.6 SDS), A/H ratio was 0.970, and her OFC was 51 cm (−0.5 SDS). Physical examination revealed small hands, hyperthricosis and mild facial dysmorphisms, including a prominent forehead, thick eyebrows, synophrys, broad nasal bridge, anteverted nose, long philtrum and large halluces (Fig. 1B, D). Routinely performed laboratory tests for short stature were normal, as was her karyotype (46, XX). The hormonal evaluation showed a normal IGF-1 (125 µg/L,−0.5 SDS) and an exaggerated GH response to the stimulation test (GH peak of 26.9 µg/L). Her bone age was advanced by 1 year (chronological age of 7.9 years and bone age of 8.9 years) with short metacarpals and phalanges with cone-shaped epiphyses. A skeletal survey showed mild mesomelia, thoracolumbar scoliosis, and subtle irregular metaphyseal changes. Reverse phenotyping following singleton exome sequencing confirmed the diagnosis of acromesomelic dysplasia, type PRKG2.

Methods

Genetic analysis

Written informed consent for genetics studies and publication of individual details and images was obtained. Whole exome sequencing in Patient 1 was performed in a commercial diagnostic laboratory (Intergen, Ankara, Turkey). Patient 2 was included in a research study approved by The Comite de Etica e Pesquisa do Hospital das Clínicas da Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de Sao Paulo (CAAE, 06425812.4.0000.0068).

DNA samples were extracted from peripheral blood. Exome sequencing was performed in the probands using TWIST comprehensive exome kit (TWIST Bioscience) and run on a NextSeq500 (Patient 1) or NovaSeq6000 (Patient 2) platform (Illumina). DNA sequences were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 reference. Variant calling and data analysis were performed using Varsome Clinical bioinformatics analysis software (Patient 1) or Franklin by Genoox interpretation engine (Patient 2). Allelic frequencies were assessed using GnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org). In silico prediction analysis included CADD V1.6 (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu), SIFT (https://sift.bii.a-star.edu.sg/), Polyphen-2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), and GERP (https://bio.tools/gerp) were used for missense variant pathogenicity predictions, whereas dbscSNV Ada/RF (http://www.liulab.science/dbscsnv.html), Splice AI (https://spliceailookup.broadinstitute.org/), Human Splice Finder (http://www.umd.be/HSF3/), NetGene2 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetGene2-2.42/), and Splice Site Prediction by Neural Network (https://www.fruitfly.org/seq_tools/splice.html) were employed for splice site variants. Sanger sequencing was performed for segregation studies in Patient 1. The variants were classified according to the ACMG standards and guidelines [12].

Functional studies

Functional characterization was performed for the missense variant, p.(Val470Gly) (V470G). The variant was introduced into the pCMV6-PRKG2-WT vector by site-directed mutagenesis [1] using the following primers, 5′- GGGTTCGGAAGAGTTGAGCTTGGTAAAGTAAAAAATGAGAATGTTGC-3 (mutated base underlined) and the reverse complement. Sequences were validated by Sanger sequencing. The previously reported p.Arg569* (R569*) mutant was employed as a positive control [1].

The effect of the V470G mutant on cGKII synthesis and its capacity to inhibit fibroblast growth factor (FGF)-induced MAPK pathway was evaluated by western blot of the cell lysates of transiently transfected HEK293T cells treated with cGMP analog (8 pCPT-cGMP) and fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2), as previously described [1]. A total of three biological replicas were performed.

Results

Genetic analyses

A homozygous PRKG2 missense variant, NM_006259.3:c.1409 T > G, p.(Val470Gly) was identified in Patient 1. This variant is absent from the gnomAD population database and affects a highly conserved amino acid in the catalytic domain of cGKII/PRKG2 close to the ATP-binding site (Fig. 3A). In silico analyses predict the variant to be deleterious (SIFT: damaging, PolyPhen2: probably damaging, CADD score: 32, GERP: 5.98). Since the variant is close to the splice junction, Splice AI and HSF were used to predict whether it affected splicing, however, no significant impact was detected. The variant was initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) using ACMG recommendations (PM2, PP3) [12]. However, after functional characterization, it was reclassified as pathogenic. Parents were found to be heterozygous for the variant. The variant was submitted to ClinVar database (SUB13315514).

WES analysis of Patient 2 resulted in the identification of a homozygous nucleotide variant in the exon 9 canonical splice donor site of PRKG2, NM_006259.3:c.1154+1 G > A, p.? (Fig. 3A). A large region of homozygosity (ROH) surrounding PRKG2 (chr4:56734700-89415395, 32 Mb) was observed. In-silico splice-site prediction tools predicted that the variant ablated the splice donor site. This variant was absent in GnomAD database. It was classified as likely pathogenic (PVS1, PM2) [12]. Segregation analysis could not be performed in parents and maternal uncle due to lack of DNA samples. The variant was submitted to ClinVar database (SUB13317430).

Functional characterization

We first evaluated the capacity of the missense variant, V470G, on the synthesis of the cGKII mutant protein by Western blot of cell lysates from transient transfections in HEK293T cells. The expression level was significantly reduced (≥70%) compared to the wild type, similar to that observed for the R569* positive control (Fig. 3B, C).

Next, we assessed whether the mutant could downregulate the FGF2-induced MAPK pathway by analyzing its effect on the phosphorylation of Raf-1 and ERK1/2. Wild-type cGKII phosphorylates Raf-1 at Ser-43, which consequently reduces the activation of ERK1/2 and thus, the MAPK signaling in a cGMP-dependent manner. In contrast, the V470G mutant variant, as with the R569* positive control, was unable to down-regulate the MAPK pathway, as seen by the failure to reduce ERK1/2 activation (Fig. 3D, E).

Discussion

PRKG2 encodes a 762 amino acid serine/threonine kinase cGKII enzyme with two main functional domains: an N-terminal regulatory domain and a carboxy-terminal catalytic domain. The N-terminal domain harbors two cyclic nucleotide-binding domains, CNB-A and CNB-B, with different affinities for cGMP binding (CNB-B > CNB-A) [13], along with other regions involved in the dimerization, auto-inhibition, and cellular localization of the protein [14]. The C-terminal or kinase active domain contains the ATP and the substrate-binding sites necessary for the phosphorylation of target proteins [15]. The binding of cGMP is required for the activity of the enzyme as it releases the substrate binding site, which, in its absence, remains inactive due to its interaction with the auto-inhibitory region located in the N-terminus [15]. Therefore, cGKII catalytic activity relies on both the integrity of these regions and the correct union of cGMP, ATP, and substrate to their binding sites.

To date, including our patients six homozygous PRKG2 variants have been reported in 10 individuals from seven different families, all predicted to cause alterations in protein length [1–3]. The nonsense variant p.Arg569*, identified in two unrelated individuals, and the frameshift p.Asn164Lysfs*2 variant, identified in an Indian girl, are located in the C-terminal and N-terminal domains, respectively. Both variants lead to prematurely truncated proteins partially or entirely lacking the catalytic domain, affecting the substrate-binding site and, thus, exhibiting no kinase activity. Likewise, the two splice-site variants are both predicted to affect the catalytic domain [3]. Whereas these variants generate or are predicted to generate shortened and catalytically inactive proteins, the p.Asp761Glufs*34 variant leads to an elongated protein due to the addition of 33 amino acids at the C-terminal. Although the integrity of the functional regions of the protein is conserved, the mutant protein has been demonstrated to be impaired [2]. Functionally, the three variants tested so far (p.Arg569*, p.Asn164Lysfs*2, and p.Asp761Glufs*34) have been shown to be; (1) unable to phosphorylate c-Raf1 at Ser43 and subsequently to downregulate MAPK signaling in response to FGF2, and (2) have dramatically reduced protein levels compared to the wild type cGKII protein [1, 2]. Here, we assessed whether those parameters were also altered in the p.Val470Gly variant. The mutant cGKII protein levels were severely reduced compared to wild-type cGKII. It also failed to phosphorylate c-Raf1 serine 43, the target of cGKII actions, and thus was unable to inhibit the FGF2-induce downstream ERK1/2 activation in a cGMP-dependent manner. Taken together, these data provide evidence that this missense variant causes a reduction in the availability of cGKII protein, which is catalytically inactive, thus confirming that it is the pathogenic cause of the skeletal anomalies and growth delay observed in the patient. Interestingly, the p.Val470Gly variant is the first missense variant reported in not only humans but also in animal models, as all studied variants have led to truncated abnormal proteins partially or entirely lacking the kinase domain and, thus, catalytically inactive proteins [5, 16, 17]. Even though the integrity of the functional domains of the protein remains intact, the p.Val470Gly variant appears to alter the cGKII native structure so that it cannot perform its function. One suggestion may be that as it is located close to the ATP binding site, it may affect the ATP union to cGKII and, thus, its ability to carry out its kinase activity.

The clinical and radiographic features of individuals with AMDP are presented in Table 1. The observed clinical characteristics mandate a comparison between AMDP and AMDM as both genes participate in the C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP)/natriuretic peptide receptor-B (NPR-B) pathway. The less severe vertebral, metaphyseal changes and brachydactyly in AMDP demonstrates a similar, yet milder phenotype than AMDM. The prominent forehead and short nose are shared facial features. The presence of large halluces, a common feature of AMDM, in our patients is also noteworthy. Our retrospective evaluation of clinical data and images of reported patients suggests a resemblance in facial features: thick eyebrows, synophrys, broad nasal bridge, anteverted nares, and pointed chin. Generalized hypertrichosis in the patient from Iran was also recognizable in both of our patients [2]. Thus, we suggest that a common facial phenotype may exist; however, the limited number of patients is currently insufficient to make a facial gestalt heatmap. Future reports from other ethnicities, will help determine if the facial gestalt and generalized hypertrichosis are components of this phenotype.

In summary, AMDP is a newly recognized autosomal recessive skeletal dysplasia with disproportionate short stature. Our results suggest that patients with missense or variants that cause prematurely truncated proteins have similar clinical and radiographic phenotypes, whereas an elongated protein result in more severe spondylar dysplasia but milder acromesomelic shortening. Future reports will contribute to our observation regarding an AMDP facial gestalt. Finally, we suggest that this extremely rare condition be considered in patients with acromesomelic dysplasia and the described facial features, especially when NPR2-related acromesomelic dysplasia has been molecularly excluded.
