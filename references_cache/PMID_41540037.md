# UQCRC1 deficiency impairs mitophagy via PINK1-dependent mechanisms in Parkinson's disease.
**Authors:** Li JL, Huang SY, Huang PY, Fu SJ, Tsao YJ, Chang W, Hong CL, Hung YC, Liu PH, Lai LC, Lin CH, Chiang WC, Chan CC
**Journal:** NPJ Parkinsons Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41531-026-01262-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41531-026-01262-6)

## Content

Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and mitophagy are functionally interconnected cellular processes, the defects of which are considered key driving forces behind the pathogenesis of Parkinson's disease (PD). UQCRC1, a core subunit of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex III, is a recently identified familial PD gene whose pathogenic mutations result in OXPHOS stress. Given its importance, however, the role of UQCRC1 in idiopathic PD as well as mitophagy has not been investigated. In this study, we collected 19 datasets comprising postmortem substantia nigra from 150 cases of non-disease controls and 185 cases of PD or incidental Lewy body disease (iLBD), and the meta-analysis of the UQCRC1 mRNA level showed reduced expression in idiopathic PD, suggesting the potential of UQCRC1 as a biomarker. Leveraging the SH-SY5Y cells and fly models, we showed that mitophagy was impaired upon UQCRC1 mutation or depletion. Notably, insufficiency of PINK1 mRNA was associated with UQCRC1 deficiency, and overexpression of Pink1 rescued the locomotion and mitophagy defects in the fly models with neuronal loss of uqcrc1. Treatment with two PINK1 activators, kinetin and MTK458, resulted in similar protective effects in the fly and cell models. Overall, we identified OXPHOS stress led by deficiency of UQCRC1 as an etiology of mitophagy defects in PD and PINK1 as a therapeutic target for UQCRC1-associated PD.

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide with increasing prevalence in the aging population 1 . PD is characterized by progressive bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra (SN). Accumulating genetic studies have deepened our understanding of PD pathogenesis. However, common genetic variants account for only approximately 22% of PD risk 2 , with most PD cases being idiopathic without definite genetic etiology. To date, treatments for PD have largely focused on symptomatic relief. Elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms may provide new insights for the development of disease-modifying therapies for PD pathogenesis.

The molecular pathogenesis of PD is intricate and heterogeneous. Notably, a crucial factor contributing to PD is oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) stress resulting from the dysfunctions of the mitochondrial respiratory chain 3 . This is exemplified by the neurotoxin 1-Methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP), whose metabolite disrupts the mitochondrial respiratory chain, leading to neuronal death and parkinsonism 4 . Genetic studies have further strengthened the role of OXPHOS dysfunction in PD pathogenesis. Mutations of several PD-associated genes have been shown to affect mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes. For instance, oligomerized mutant α-synuclein disrupts the respiratory chain complex I 5 , 6 ; the loss of CHCHD2 destabilizes complex IV 7 ; and the mutations of DJ-1 impair its ability to enhance the function of ATP synthase of complex V 8 . Taken together, studies using these toxin and genetic models supported that OXPHOS distress is central to PD pathogenesis.

In addition to the OXPHOS stress, the impairment of mitophagy is also critically linked to PD pathogenesis 9 . Mitophagy is a critical mitochondrial quality control mechanism that removes dysfunctional or superfluous mitochondria via autophagic sequestration. PINK1 and Parkin are two master enzymes responsible for the initiation of mitophagy. Upon mitochondrial stress, PINK1 kinase is stabilized and activated by auto-phosphorylation on the mitochondrial outer membrane, where it phosphorylates ubiquitin and Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase. The phosphorylated Parkin in turn ubiquitinates mitochondrial outer membrane proteins, resulting in the recruitment of ubiquitin-binding autophagy receptors and ultimately the autophagic removal of damaged mitochondria 10 . Several PD etiologies have been proposed to impact mitophagy, including excessive α-synuclein and LRRK2 mutations 11– 13 . Recently, a proteomic study using human postmortem midbrains of PD revealed that deficiency of the respiratory chain complexes was correlated with reduced expression of mitochondrial quality control proteins 14 . Moreover, it has been reported that lysosomal function is impaired in cells with chronic OXPHOS deficiency 15 . In contrast, enhanced mitophagy was found in neurons upon OXPHOS stimulation 16 . These observations imply that OXPHOS activity plays a regulatory role in mitophagy, but the detailed mechanisms are not yet clear.

UQCRC1, which encodes a core subunit of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex III, is recently identified as a familial PD gene from a multicenter East Asian population 17 . The PD-associated missense variant p.Y314S leads to reduced oxygen consumption and ATP production rate 18 . Mice with UQCRC1 p.Y314S mutation exhibited dopaminergic neuronal loss and locomotor defects 18 , and similar phenotypes were observed in Drosophila with uqcrc1 depletion 19 . Thus far, UQCRC1 mutants are the only PD-associated genetic variants that encode a component of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Elucidating whether UQCRC1 deficiency impacts mitophagy may provide insights into the specific mechanism by which the electron transport chain complex regulates mitophagy.

Here, we extended our study on the role of UQCRC1 from familial PD to idiopathic PD. We first conducted a meta-analysis using open-source datasets and revealed that low levels of UQCRC1 are associated with idiopathic PD. Using both cell and fly models, we demonstrated that mitophagy was impaired upon UQCRC1 mutation or depletion. Furthermore, we observed that UQCRC1-associated mitophagy impairment was accompanied by PINK1 reduction. Notably, enhancing PINK1 activity by PINK1 overexpression or treatments of PINK1 activators, such as kinetin and MTK458 20 , rescued the Parkinsonism-like defects in the UQCRC1-associated PD models, suggesting that the augmentation of mitophagy is a potential therapeutic approach for PD.

To elucidate the expression level of UQCRC1 in idiopathic PD, we searched the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database with the keywords “(Parkinson’s disease) AND “Homo sapiens”[porgn:__txid9606] AND (substantia nigra).” We filtered the 327 results with the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. S1), yielding a total of 19 datasets as summarized in Table S1 21– 32 . We then analyzed these datasets consisting of 150 cases of controls and 185 cases of PD or incidental Lewy body disease (iLBD) and found a reduction in UQCRC1 mRNA levels in the substantia nigra of PD patients. The estimated average log2-fold change of the UQCRC1 mRNA level was -0.2981 in PD substantia nigra compared to normal individuals based on the random-effects model (95% confidence interval (CI): -0.5204 to -0.0758, p = 0.0086) (Fig. 1A). Namely, there was approximately a 20% decrease in UQCRC1 mRNA level in PD. To examine the accountability of the outcome, we checked the heterogeneity and publication bias among the included datasets. There was no noticeable heterogeneity according to the Q-test (Q(18) = 14.3105, p = 0.7086, tau² = 0.00, I² = 0.00%), and the publication bias was not noted according to the rank correlation or regression test (p = 0.1284 and p = 0.1736, respectively) (Fig. S2A). To validate the meta-analysis findings, we obtained whole brain lysates of postmortem PD patients from two independent sources and performed a western blot analysis of UQCRC1. UQCRC1 protein levels were lower in the brains of PD patients compared with the non-disease controls (Fig. 1B). We also interrogated whether the protein level of UQCRC1 is affected in familial PD with the known UQCRC1 mutation 17 . By using the SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells with or without UQCRC1 p.Y314S mutant knocked in ref. 19 , we showed that UQCRC1 is reduced in mutant cells compared with the wild-type control cells (Fig. 1C). Taken together, UQCRC1 is decreased in both familial and idiopathic PD, supporting UQCRC1 deficiency may be a common phenomenon in PD.

Since mitophagy impairment is critically linked to PD, we examined whether mitophagy is reduced in the UQCRC1-associated PD models. We utilized two fluorescent reporters, 2xCOX8-EGFP-mCherry and mito-QC for monitoring the mitophagy flux 33 , 34 . Both reporters are composed of tandem EGFP and mCherry fused to different mitochondrial targeting sequences. The EGFP signal is quenched in acidic environments once the damaged mitochondria are fused with the lysosomes to form mitolysosomes. Thus, the EGFP + /mCherry+ and EGFP-/mCherry+ (red-only) fluorescence signals indicate non-acidified compartments and acidified mitolysosomes, respectively (Fig. 2A). In SH-SY5Y cells with the knock-in of the familial form of UQCRC1 p.Y314S, the number of red-only puncta (indicating mitolysosomes) at the basal condition was similar to that in the control cells. Upon mitophagy induction by the treatment with mitochondrial toxins oligomycin and antimycin A (OA), the mutant cells showed fewer mitolysosomes than the control cells (Fig. 2B), suggesting that UQCRC1 mutation blunts mitophagy flux. Since UQCRC1 deficiency is a common feature of idiopathic PD (Fig. 1), we examined whether mitophagy is also impaired upon the knockdown of UQCRC1. Depletion of UQCRC1 with three different shRNAs with good knockdown efficiency (Fig. S3) resulted in fewer mitolysosomes upon OA treatment (Fig. 2C). The similarities between the mutant cells and knockdown cells led us to use the two models interchangeably in the following cell experiments. In Drosophila, by using the dopaminergic neuron-specific driver TH-GAL4, we found mitolysosome decrease in cells expressing uqcrc1 RNAi (uq RNAi) compared to the control group (UAS-LacZ). The decrease was comparable to cells expressing RNAi of key autophagy factor Atg5 (Atg5 RNAi), suggesting that reduced UQCRC1 impedes mitophagy. PINK1 and PRKN are key regulators of mitophagy and are well-known familial PD genes. We performed another meta-analysis and found that PINK1 was also deficient in idiopathic PD midbrains (Fig. 3A), while PRKN was not (Fig. S4A). In the meta-analysis of PINK1, 17 datasets were enrolled (two datasets, GSE20333 and GSE43490, excluded for no PINK1 mRNA data). The estimated average log2-fold change of PINK1 mRNA level in PD substantia nigra compared to the control group based on the random-effects model was -0.3537 (95% CI: -0.5851 to -0.1224, p = 0.0027), equivalent to 22% reduction in PD brains. Among the datasets we included, there was no heterogeneity (Q(16) = 11.2919, p = 0.7911, tau² = 0.00, I² = 0.00%) and the analysis was not undermined by the publication bias (p = 0.9032 and p = 0.5561, respectively, according to the rank correlation and the regression test), giving good confidence of the results (Fig. S2B). With the aim of investigating the correlation between UQCRC1 and PINK1, we normalized the expression levels of both UQCRC1 and PINK1 from 0 to 1 in each dataset. Pooled results showed that there was a strong positive correlation (r = 0.6667, p < 0.0001) between UQCRC1 and PINK1 mRNA levels (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the correlation between UQCRC1 and PRKN was weak despite reaching statistical significance (r = 0.1805, p = 0.0017) (Fig. S4C).

To validate the above findings, we examined the levels of PINK1 and PRKN mRNA in control vs. UQCRC1 p.Y314S SH-SY5Y cells. The RNA-seq revealed that the PINK1 mRNA level was reduced in the mutant cells (Fig. 3C), while the PRKN mRNA level was not (Fig. S4D). These results are consistent with our meta-analysis outcome that the level of PINK1, but not Parkin, was reduced upon UQCRC1 deficiency.

Given the identification of PINK1 downregulation in UQCRC1-associated models, we next examined whether the molecular events associated with PINK1 activation are affected by UQCRC1 deficiency in SH-SY5Y cells. We monitored the mitochondrial recruitment of Parkin and the phosphorylation of ubiquitin, two signature molecular events of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy in SH-SY5Y cells with UQCRC1 knockdown. We expressed FLAG-Parkin in SH-SY5Y cells and performed immunofluorescent staining of mitochondrial protein ATP5A and FLAG-Parkin to evaluate the mitochondrial localization of Parkin during mitophagy induction. UQCRC1 knockdown reduced the extent of OA-induced mitochondrial recruitment of Parkin (Fig. 4A), indicating that UQCRC1 loss hampers Parkin activation during mitophagy. Western blot detection of phosphorylated ubiquitin at serine 65 (p-Ub) revealed a time-dependent increase in p-Ub following OA treatment in wild-type cells, but the accumulation of p-Ub species was dampened in UQCRC1-depleted cells (Fig. 4B). To sum up, our data showed that the initiation process of the PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy is compromised upon UQCRC1 deficiency, consistent with the observation of PINK1 downregulation in the UQCRC1 models of PD. Previous studies have shown that PINK1 overexpression is neuroprotective in PD models induced by MPTP or α-synucleinopathy 35 , 36 . We wondered whether PINK1 overexpression also provides a similar effect in the UQCRC1-associated PD models. First, we found that, in the Drosophila model with pan-neuronal (elav-GAL4) depletion of uqcrc1, the expression level of Pink1 was also reduced (Fig. 5A) and overexpression of Pink1 rescued the climbing defect of uqcrc1 RNAi flies (Fig. 5B). To evaluate whether Pink1 overexpression restored mitophagy, we used mito-QC and measured the mitolysosome numbers per brain area and the mitolysosome to mitochondria ratio as the indicators of mitophagy flux. Both parameters were reduced in fly brains with uqcrc1 deficiency (Fig. 5C–E), consistent with the findings in SH-SY5Y cells and fly dopaminergic neurons (Figs. 2C, D). The mitophagy impairment was reversed by Pink1 overexpression (Fig. 5C–E). These findings suggest that enhancing PINK1 function improves phenotypic defects in UQCRC1-associated PD. To verify the hypothesis, we tested whether the treatment with two known PINK1 activators, kinetin and MTK458, provided neuroprotective effects in uqcrc1 RNAi flies and UQCRC1 mutant SH-SY5Y cells. We found that MTK458 improved the climbing defects as well as the PPL1 cluster dopaminergic neuronal loss in flies with neuronal uqcrc1 deficiency (Figs. 6A, B). The improving effect was blunted by Pink1 null mutant (Fig. S5). In addition, we have previously shown that the UQCRC1 mutant leads to reduced neurite length in SH-SY5Y cells 17 . In UQCRC1 knockdown cells, the similar neurite shortening was observed. MTK458 rescued the defect in both models (Fig. 6C and S6A) and also improved mitophagy (Fig. 6D). Kinetin exhibited the similar benefit in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S6B). Collectively, these results demonstrated that the use of PINK1 activators may be a therapeutic strategy for treating PD in which UQCRC1 deficiency is featured. In this study, with meta-analysis of postmortem brain datasets, we found that UQCRC1 expression is reduced in PD. These data suggested that UQCRC1 is more than a gene associated with rare familial PD. Its expression level can also serve as a biomarker of PD with potential roles in PD pathogenesis. Our previous study showed that deficiency of UQCRC1 leads to cytochrome c release to the cytosol and induces apoptosis 19 . Here, we demonstrated that UQCRC1 deficiency also resulted in impaired mitophagy. We found that UQCRC1 expression level positively correlated with the level of PINK1 in human substantia nigra; this finding is further supported by our experiments in the fly and the cell culture models. We were able to show that overexpression of Pink1 rescued the locomotion and mitophagy defects of the uqcrc1-knockdown flies. Treatment with the PINK1 activator, kinetin and MTK458, provided beneficial effects in both the fly and cell culture models, suggesting the therapeutic potential of PINK1 activators in treating UQCRC1-associated familial and idiopathic PD.

The role of UQCRC1 in PD has been thought controversial. While the initial study identified a UQCRC1 p.Y314S mutant in a Taiwanese family 17 , subsequent larger cohort studies 37– 40 did not replicate an association of common or rare genetic variants with PD. However, more recent data from the UK Biobank identified PD cases with different UQCRC1 protein-truncating variants 41 , and several missense variants (p.E110D, p.I311L, p.G405V) have also been linked to PD 18 , 39 , 42 . These findings suggest that although UQCRC1 mutations may be rare, the gene does contribute to PD pathogenesis. Our study broadens this scope by demonstrating a widespread decrease in UQCRC1 expression in common idiopathic PD, providing further evidence for its involvement in PD pathogenesis.

Our study proposes OXPHOS stress as an etiology of impaired mitophagy in PD. As the brain ages, the expression levels of proteins related to the mitochondrial respiratory chain, OXHPOS, and mitophagy activities change over time 43– 45 . A recent study in mice revealed that, in the dopamine-producing nigrostriatal neurons, mitophagy flux was enhanced in old age 45 , suggesting an increased dependence on mitophagy to maintain neuronal integrity during aging. In other words, the aged neurons become more susceptible to mitophagy insufficiency. In this study, we showed that OXPHOS stress led by UQCRC1 mutation or deficiency undermined mitophagy, especially when there was an increased demand for mitochondrial turnover upon damage (Fig. 2B). While our results provide a crucial link between OXPHOS stress and PD pathogenesis, the complexity of this relationship calls for further investigation. Understanding the inter-regulations between OXPHOS, mitophagy, and aging will be crucial for developing targeted interventions for PD.

Our study has several limitations. First, while we analyzed brain lysates from two independent sources that provided consistent data regarding UQCRC1 levels in PD, the confidence in our findings would be significantly increased with more extensive patient enrollment and additional brain lysate analysis. Second, our work identified a correlation between UQCRC1 deficiency and PINK1 downregulation, but the detailed molecular mechanism directly linking the loss of UQCRC1 to PINK1 deficiency remains to be elucidated. Factors that have been experimentally validated to regulate PINK1 expression include ATF3 46 , p53 47 , sirtuins 48 , and NRF1 49 . Repressors like ATF3 and p53 are known to suppress PINK1. Interestingly, while p53 levels are reported to be upregulated in PD brains 50 , ATF3 mRNA is downregulated 51 , suggesting intricate and context-specific regulation in vivo. In contrast, sirtuins and NRF1 are the positive regulators of PINK1 transcription. Sirtuins are a class of NAD+-dependent deacetylase, comprising SIRT1-7 52 . FOXO3 deacetylation dependent on SIRT1 or SIRT3 is essential for PINK1 transcription 48 . In postmortem PD brains, concomitant reduction of SIRT3 and PINK1 levels have been found in some dopaminergic neurons 14 . NRF1 is a transcription factor responsive to mitochondrial stress 53 . Reduced NRF1 has also been reported in PD brains 54 , suggesting the downregulation of PINK1. It is not known whether the dysregulation of the SIRT3-FOXO3-PINK1 and NRF1-PINK1 axes contributes to the molecular etiology of the UQCRC1 model of PD or not. Further studies are required to pinpoint which regulatory axis is responsible for the observed PINK1 decrease.

PINK1 has long been considered a potential therapeutic target for PD 55 . Several activators have been developed, such as the adenine analogs kinetin 56 , kinetin riboside 57 , N 6 -benzyladenosine 58 , and MTK458 59 . Despite some concerns about their structural compatibility 60 or specificity 61 , importantly, they have been shown to activate PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy. Most of these activators were tested in vitro, while their roles in PD animal models are far less known. Kinetin has been tested in a fly model of heteroplasmic mitochondrial diseases and showed good responses in terms of climbing activity and dopaminergic neuron loss 62 . Kinetin has also been tested in a mice model of Alzheimer’s disease with promising responses 63 . However, in a mouse model of α-synucleinopathy, the kinetin failed to rescue both the behavior deficits and the dopaminergic neuronal loss in spite of the high dose treatment 64 . It is not known to what extent the mitochondria are damaged in their α-synuclein mouse model, and we hypothesized that PINK1 activators might be more effective in the PD models that featured evident OXPHOS deficits and mitophagy impairment instead of those associated with aggregates of neurotoxic peptides such as α-synuclein. Recently Chin, et al. showed that MTK458 provided beneficial effects in the mice with striatal injection of alpha-synuclein preformed fibrils (PFFs) 59 . In that study, they showed that the PFFs can induce OXPHOS dysfunction and mitophagy defects in primary neurons, thus explaining the promising outcome of MTK458. In the era of precision medicine, we anticipate that PINK1 activators can be used in patients showing OXPHOS defects, such as those with UQCRC1 deficiency.

In sum, we demonstrated that UQCRC1 deficiency is widely found in PD among different datasets. We also showed that UQCRC1 deficiency is linked to PINK1-dependent mitophagy impairment, broadening our understanding of the role of OXPHOS defects in PD pathogenesis. As a proof of concept, we showed that PINK1 activators elicit beneficial effects in improving phenotypic defects of UQCRC1-associated PD cellular and fly models. These observations suggest that PD patients with OXPHOS defects may benefit from treatments that activate PINK1, providing new insights into the precision treatment of PD in the future.

We conducted a systematic search on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) using Boolean keywords: (Parkinson’s disease) AND “Homo sapiens”[porgn:__txid9606] AND (substantia nigra) to obtain complete datasets. Datasets included in our analysis had to meet two specific criteria. First, the studies must involve the SN or laser-captured dopaminergic neurons from the SN of individuals diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease or incidental Lewy body disease (iLBD) compared to control groups. Second, the results must be analyzable with GEO2R. Any datasets that did not satisfy both criteria were excluded from the analysis. The searches were conducted up to September 2024.

All gene expression values were log2-transformed, with the exception of those already pre-processed (e.g., RMA signal intensity). We calculated the group-specific mean and standard deviation for both the control and PD groups within each dataset. The log2-based fold change was then derived by taking the difference between the control group’s mean and the PD group’s mean. This subtraction step served to normalize the PD group expression levels to the control group (i.e., setting the control to a baseline), facilitating the integration of expression data across diverse datasets.

The meta-analysis was conducted with Jamovi 2.3.28, a software package based on the R statistical language. The random effects model was used to analyze the differences in the expression levels of UQCRC1, PINK1, and PRKN between the control subjects and PD patients. The heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed with Cochrane’s Q test and I2 index. To analyze the publication bias the funnel plot was generated for visual inspection and Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation test and Egger’s regression intercept test were also used. To examine the correlation of the gene expression levels of UQCRC1 with the levels of PINK1 or PRKN, firstly the gene expression levels were normalized from 0 to 1 in each dataset. The normalized results were pooled together. Pearson correlation was then applied to describe the strength of the correlation of genes of interest.

Human whole brain lysates were obtained from Novus Biologicals (healthy subjects: NB820-59177 (male, age 82); PD patients: NB820-59407 (female, age 71)) and GeneTex (healthy subjects: cat# GTX28771 (male, age 77); PD patients: cat# GTX26602 (female, age 78)). Information about disease stage was not provided by the manufacturers.

Generation of the UQCRC1 p.Y314S knock-in (KI) mutation in human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells has been described in our previous study 17 . Both wild-type and UQCRC1 mutant cells were grown in DMEM/F12 medium (GIBCO, 12500062) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (GIBCO, A3160502) and kept at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The cells were subcultured upon reaching confluence, typically every 5 days.

Human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cell lines were used for mRNA next-generation sequencing. RNA extracted from SH-SY5Y cells were treated with the Ribo-Zero Gold kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to remove rRNAs and subsequently fragmented into 180 bp segments. The RNA fragments were reverse-transcribed to cDNA with TruSeq Stranded total RNA kit (Illumina). Specific adapters were ligated to both ends of the cDNA fragments to construct the sequencing library. Next, the sequencing library was hybridized with the oligonucleotide sequence on the surface of flow cells (Illumina), where each bound fragment underwent bridge amplification to form clonal clusters. Finally, all of the clusters on the flow cells were sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq 500 with paired-end 75 bp read length and 25 M reads per sample (Illumina).
