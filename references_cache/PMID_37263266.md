Diabetes prevalence continues to climb with the aging population. The latest 2021 figures estimate 536.6 million people world-wide have diabetes (10.5% prevalence),1 up from the 2019 estimate of 463 million (9.3% prevalence).2 Moreover, the 2021 report revised the projected 2045 prevalence upwards to 783.2 million (12.2%) from 700 million (10.9%) in the 2019 report. Type 2 diabetes (T2D), which constitutes around 95% of cases, is metabolically acquired and results from excess dietary intake, sedentary lifestyle, and a confluence of additional factors, such as genetics.3 As the body’s cells grow insulin resistant, hyperglycemia ensues. Additionally, excess fat intake causes obesity and dyslipidemia, which are highly comorbid with T2D. T2D occurs on a continuum of impaired metabolism; prediabetes, a state of insulin resistance and elevated fasting blood glucose (FBG) not meeting the formal criteria for diabetes, precedes frank T2D. Type 1 diabetes (T1D) occurs from autoimmune-mediated destruction of insulin-producing β-cells, leading to hyperglycmia.4

In addition to the growing burden of managing diabetes, both T1D and T2D patients are susceptible to developing secondary diabetic complications. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), the most common, is a length-dependent damage to peripheral nerves, first in the feet, progressing proximally, and then in the hands (Figure 1A).5 DPN is highly morbid, and T1D and T2D patients experience pain, loss of limb sensation, falls, and increased risk of foot ulcers and lower limb amputations. Although the prevalence is lower, prediabetic patients also develop peripheral neuropathy, which grows more severe upon transition to frank T2D.6

DPN pathogenesis is complex,7 and reflects a state of impaired metabolism and bioenergetics failure in the nerve, like T2D more broadly in metabolically active tissues, such as muscle and fat. This pathology occurs in the setting of the unique anatomy of the peripheral nervous system (PNS), which is comprised of extremely long axons that are sustained by interactions with supportive glia. This anatomical pattern and the nerve damage consequent to T2D underpins the distal-to-proximal symptomatology in clinically manifest DPN. Whereas earlier work focused on the contribution of hyperglycemia to nerve damage and bioenergetics failure, recent research and clinical evidence additionally suggest that obesity and dyslipidemia also promote disease pathogenesis.

This review discusses the risk factors and potential metabolic pathways that contribute to prediabetic neuropathy and DPN. First, the review outlines PNS anatomy, nerve bioenergetics under homeostatic conditions, and communication between axons and their supportive glia. Next, the review examines how hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia contribute to bioenergetics failure in DPN and promote painful DPN. Unfortunately, DPN, which is generally progressive, still lacks disease-modifying therapies and clinical care revolves around pain management,5 diligent foot care, and fall prevention strategies. Thus, the review ends by summarizing research on mechanism-based targets, unlocking novel roads to potential disease-modifying therapies.

Hyperglycemia and associated metrics, e.g., FBG, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and disease duration, are the traditional and principal risk factors for prediabetic neuropathy and DPN in patients with prediabetes and diabetes, respectively.5,6 Controlling diabetes slows DPN progression in patients with T1D, but only has a modest impact in patients with T2D,8 suggesting additional factors underlie DPN onset and progression. Indeed, although hyperglycemia remains an important risk factor,9 recent clinical and preclinical studies highlight the independent contribution of the metabolic syndrome (MetS) and its components,5 obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, which are frequently comorbid with prediabetes, T2D, and, increasingly, with T1D.10

A decade of research now consistently supports risk for prediabetic neuropathy and DPN from obesity [e.g., waist circumference, body mass index (BMI)], dyslipidemia, and hypertension in patients with prediabetes and T2D (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). Emerging evidence indicates that the MetS is a DPN risk factor in T1D as well. Overall, clinical studies suggest that the MetS and/or its components can enhance DPN risk, independent of glycemic status. These observations have important implications for the mechanisms of DPN pathophysiology and suggest that dyslipidemia and obesity, in addition to previously acknowledged hyperglycemia, injure the PNS and result in DPN.

Although impaired metabolism is the major DPN driver, interest has grown recently in potential genetic contributions, either at single loci or through polygenic risk. Single-loci studies have investigated genes linked to metabolism, cholesterol transport, mitochondrial uncoupling, oxidative stress defense, and vascular function but findings lack concordance across studies.11 The dawn of Omics approaches has facilitated genome-wide association studies, which have further identified DPN candidates.5 One study generated a polygenic risk for T2D, which correlated significantly, albeit weakly, with DPN.12 Presently, genome-wide analysis is still in the early stages of investigation, and implications for DPN pathophysiology are yet to be determined.

The PNS is comprised of 31 pairs of spinal nerves and 10 pairs of cranial nerves, with two pairs of cranial nerves, the optic and olfactory, considered part of the central nervous system (CNS). Peripheral nerves consist of neurons (cell bodies and axons), their supportive glia, the Schwann cells (SCs), and vascular and connective tissue. The axons of neurons serving the limbs, particularly the lower limbs, are extremely long and possess an aspect ratio in the thousands, with narrow diameters versus extremely long lengths. This configuration has important ramifications for energy distribution across axons. Peripheral nerves comprise sensory and motor neurons, which relay information afferently and efferently, respectively. Although sensory dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons are primarily damaged in DPN,7 increasingly, research indicates that motor neurons are also affected.13 Each sensory axon, also known as a nerve fiber, projects from one DRG neuron and typically carries electrical impulses. Nerve fibers are subdivided into unmyelinated and myelinated fibers (Figure 1B). Multiple unmyelinated axons, known as small fibers, organize into Remak bundles. Myelinated axons, designated as large fibers, are individually ensheathed by multilaminar SC myelin. By definition, a peripheral nerve most commonly contains bundles of unmyelinated and myelinated nerve fibers. Small unmyelinated and large myelinated fibers transmit differing sensory modalities at differing speeds, with faster saltatory transmission across nodes of Ranvier in myelinated fibers versus slower non-saltatory conduction in their unmyelinated counterparts.

Additionally, the PNS contains autonomic nerves, which regulate unconscious bodily functions, such as organ and glandular activity. Sensory autonomic nerves sense cues, e.g., blood oxygenation, glucose, and, in response, motor autonomic nerves innervate cardiac and smooth muscle and glandular cells. This review does not directly address injury to the autonomic nervous system, although research suggests shared underlying mechanisms of DPN and diabetic autonomic neuropathy.

Neurons require massive amounts of energy to function normally (Figure 2A). Even at rest, axons maintain a membrane potential, but consume even more energy upon firing to propagate signals and release and reuptake neurotransmitters. The constant and intensive energy demand renders neurons reliant on well-orchestrated bioenergetics, which requires both a continuous supply of energy substrates to the neuron cell body and axon, along with well-regulated substrate catabolism to generate ATP, the energy currency of cells. The former is accomplished by substrate transporters, directly importing energy substrates into axons7 and indirectly via SCs though SC-axon metabolic coupling. The latter, well-regulated substrate catabolism, is achieved by axonal cytoplasmic glycolysis and β-oxidation leading to mitochondrial energy conversion.

Glucose and fatty acids are the primary energy substrates imported into cells from circulation. Neurons mainly take up glucose by insulin-independent glucose transporter 3 (GLUT3)14 (Figure 2B). Imported glucose is first metabolized by glycolysis in the cytoplasm to pyruvate, which under aerobic conditions is transported into mitochondria, where it is broken down by the tricarboxylic acid cycle, which feeds substrates into oxidative phosphorylation, an oxygen requiring process (Figure 2C). Oxidative phosphorylation is driven by the mitochondrial membrane potential within the inner membrane. There is a net production of 32 ATP per glucose molecule during aerobic glycolysis. During limited oxygen availability, e.g., intense activity, anaerobic glycolysis, instead, breaks down glucose-derived pyruvate into lactate, which results in the net production of two ATP.

Although neurons favor glucose as an energy substrate, they also possess fatty acid transporters15 and may utilize fatty acids.16 Fatty acids are entirely metabolized within mitochondria (Figure 2C). First, acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member ligates the fatty acid to coenzyme A (CoA) producing a fatty acyl-CoA, which is imported into mitochondria.17 Next, β-oxidation breaks down the acyl-CoA into acetyl-CoA, which is funneled into the tricarboxylic acid cycle followed by oxidative phosphorylation.

Given the central role of mitochondria for energy generation, especially during periods of intense activity, e.g., rapid firing, neurons rely on well-regulated mitochondrial dynamics.18 Formation of new mitochondria, also known as mitochondrial biogenesis, occurs mainly in the neuron body. Mitochondria can then be trafficked over the entire length of the axon to areas of high energy consumption, e.g., nodes of Ranvier and synapses. The mitochondrial lifecycle ends with mitophagy of terminally injured mitochondria. Any disruptions to this carefully orchestrated process stresses and injures neurons.18,19

SCs are the principal PNS glia, and mainly metabolize glucose for their energy needs, while fatty acids are presumably reserved to synthesize myelin.20 To import glucose, SCs express insulin-independent glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1)21 (Figure 2B). SCs are known traditionally for their role in myelinating axons although most peripheral axons are unmyelinated. Non-myelinating SCs do not wrap around individual unmyelinated axons but rather hold axons together in Remak bundles (Figure 1B).7 These non-myelinating SCs clearly adopt a function distinct from electrical insulation of axons. Indeed, recent studies indicate SCs provide trophic support22–25 and transfer energy substrates to axons during periods of high activity.26,27 In this scenario, SCs take up glucose or utilize their glycogen reserves26 to generate pyruvate, which is converted into lactate within SCs. Lactate is shuttled via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) to energy-hungry axons, where it is converted back into pyruvate and funneled into mitochondria to fuel axonal bioenergetic needs (Figure 2B). A similar metabolic coupling model exists between CNS axons and oligodendrocytes,28–30 and was developed, by analogy, to the PNS. Thus, more PNS research is ongoing to fully understand if and how SC-axon metabolic coupling contributes to nerve health and function and under what conditions.

In addition to metabolic coupling, neurons and SCs communicate via multiple routes, including paracrine signaling,31 soluble factors, e.g., growth factors,32 and physical contact through gap junctions and myelin channels33 (Figure 1B). Attention has recently focused on communication through extracellular vesicles (EVs), which carry various cargo, e.g., microRNAs (miRNAs), metabolites, and proteins, between cells during health and injury.34 In the PNS, studies of SC-derived EVs have revolved around nerve repair following injury. Axons can internalize SC-secreted EVs, which promote axonal outgrowth after nerve crush.35,36 To our knowledge, no study has examined the influence of SC EVs on PNS bioenergetics and activity. CNS studies demonstrate that neurotransmitters trigger EV release from oligodendrocytes, which are internalized by neurons, enhancing metabolism37 and firing rates.38 Oligodendrocyte-derived EVs also influence axonal transport.39 Collectively, these results suggest that CNS glial EVs can support neuronal activity and nerve health. Similar events likely occur between SCs and PNS axons, although more research is needed.

Diabetes injures both small and large sensory fibers in a distal to proximal pattern, producing the classic stocking-glove impairment of multiple sensory modalities (Figure 1A, Figure 3A). However, DPN can also manifest as isolated small fiber dysfunction, i.e., damage only to small fibers. Small fiber dysfunction secondary to injury of small unmyelinated C and thinly myelinated Aδ fibers produces a variety of primarily painful symptoms, including prickling, burning, and electric shock-like sensations, and impaired temperature perception. Small fiber loss is assessed by measuring intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD) from a skin punch biopsy, most commonly from the distal leg of patients. Large fiber dysfunction reflects a combination of demyelination and axonal loss.40 Patients experience generalized numbness with loss of vibratory and position sense. Large fiber dysfunction is quantified by mild slowing of nerve conduction velocities (NCVs) coupled with absent or decreased sensory nerve amplitudes secondary to axonal loss.5 Temporally, small fiber dysfunction is thought to precede large fiber dysfunction in DPN,13 though this may not be the case based on symptom progression in a large recent study.41 Additionally, although DPN is progressive, the disease course is punctuated by attempted small nerve fiber regeneration, which ultimately succumbs to net degeneration.7

Insulin signaling and the consequences of insulin resistance in metabolically active tissues are well-established.42 Systemically, during T2D, growing insulin resistance in metabolically active tissues, like muscle, fat, and liver, leads to hyperglycemia and organ-dependent changes in lipid metabolism. Clinical studies confirm elevated plasma glucose and HbA1C along with abnormal plasma lipid profiles correlate with insulin resistance.43

By contrast, far less is known for peripheral nerves, although in vitro44 and ex vivo45 evidence suggests nerves develop insulin resistance during T2D. Both neurons and SCs express insulin receptors46 and insulin has multiple homeostatic PNS roles, including as a neurotrophic factor.47 While PNS glucose uptake occurs via insulin-independent GLUTs, insulin does regulate PNS metabolism more broadly through nutrient sensors such as mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1),45 which interact with energy sensors [e.g., AMP activated kinase (AMPK)]48 and regulators of mitochondrial biogenesis [e.g., proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1α)].48 During nutrient excess, insulin resistance can blunt insulin-mediated nutrient and energy responses,49 profoundly perturbing nerve metabolism, leading to nerve injury (Figure 3B).

Thus, while the core of complex DPN pathophysiology is impaired nerve metabolism and bioenergetics failure in the context of energy substrate overload, there remains major gaps in our understanding of the role of insulin signaling and insulin resistance on nutrient uptake and utilization in the PNS under normal and diabetic conditions. While research is ongoing to traverse these gaps, the ensuing sections describe the current understanding of how hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia influence mitochondrial bioenergetics and axonal trafficking. The picture that emerges during diabetes is one of a loss of mitochondrial trafficking and bioenergetics failure at the distal ends of extremely long peripheral axons in DPN, which triggers distal-to-proximal nerve damage in a pattern mirroring patient symptomology.

Historically, the description of DPN as a microvascular complication arose from damage to the nerve microvasculature. The diabetic nerve is characterized by endothelial dysfunction with endoneurial capillary abnormalities and thickening of the basement membrane,50,51 reduced endoneurial perfusion, and ischemia.52,53 Diabetic animal models also exhibit poor vasodilation of epineurial arterioles, impairing regulation of endoneurial blood flow.54,55 Indeed, the salutary effects of γ-linolenic acid on DPN in the context of T1D are attributed to its role as a vasodilatory prostaglandin precursor.56 Another microvascular facet of DPN is hyperglycemia-induced activation of protein kinase C (PKC), which impacts expression of vascular endothelial growth factor, promotes vasoconstriction, and induces hypoxia.57,58 Diabetes can also lower levels of neurotrophic and angiogenic factors, contributing to poor nerve health.59 Lastly, endothelial cells may be susceptible to excess nutrients, through hyperglycemia- and dyslipidemia-induced oxidative stress, further contributing to nerve damage during diabetes.60,61

Discussed in detail in our 2017 review,7 hyperglycemia mediated nerve damage through well-documented and classical pathways was previously considered the “sole” culprit underlying DPN. These pathways include polyol pathway activation, a shunting of glucose into sorbitol with consequent osmotic stress and impaired Na+/K+-ATPase activity and NADPH depletion, hexosamine pathway activation, with excessive glycolytic intermediates leading to PKC activation, and advanced glycation end products, which activate inflammatory responses (Figure 3B).7 An additional classical hallmark of the PNS under the framework of hyperglycemia is mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetics failure (Figure 3B).18,48 Persistent hyperglycemia first hyperpolarizes and then depolarizes neuronal mitochondrial membranes,62 lowering coupled and uncoupled respiration,63 and reducing ATP production.64
In vitro in DRG neurons, hyperglycemia (50 mM) does not appear to impair mitochondrial trafficking.64 However, elevated glucose (30 mM) does influence mitochondrial trafficking in CNS hippocampal neurons by modifying the trafficking adaptor protein Milton (also known as TRAK) in the kinesin machinery responsible for anterograde transport.65 The differences between mitochondrial trafficking in PNS and CNS axons remain understudied, albeit a clearer understanding could yield important insights into DPN pathogenesis.

Additionally, streptozotocin (STZ) T1D as well as T2D rodents have downregulated sciatic nerve expression and or activation of proteins regulating the cellular metabolic machinery in response to chronic hyperglycemia,66,67 including AMPK and PGC1α,48 which would have severe downstream effects on nerve metabolism and bioenergetics (Figure 3B). Underpinning the critical role of mitochondrial dysfunction in DPN, both knockout and overexpression studies of mitochondrial proteins and transcription factors influence nerve damage and resilience to nutrient overload.68 PGC1α knockout aggravates DPN, whereas overexpression of transcription factor A, mitochondrial protects STZ T1D rodents from DPN.68 Hyperglycemia may also perturb protein homeostasis and upregulate unfolded protein response proteins in the sciatic nerve of STZ T1D rats69 and in vitro in a SC line, RSC96,70 indicative of endoplasmic reticulum stress.

In addition to the classical pathways activated by hyperglycemia, recent research, prompted by clinical studies (Table 1), uses in vitro and in vivo model systems to address the role of dyslipidemia and obesity in DPN. In vitro in DRG neurons, the saturated fatty acid palmitate increases mitochondrial depolarization and significantly reduces the number and velocity of motile mitochondria in DRG axons (Figure 3B). These perturbations lead to insufficient mitochondria in the distal axon termini, inability to meet axonal energy demands, and distal axonal injury.64 The effects of fatty acids on mitochondrial dynamics are length- and saturation-dependent. Palmitate (16:0, carbon number:double bond number) and stearate (18:0) lower the fraction of motile and polarized mitochondria versus medium-chain laurate (12:0) and myristate (14:0) in DRG neurons.71 Palmitate and stearate also increase mitochondrial circularity and increase caspase 3/7 activity, while laurate and myristate have no effect. The monounsaturated fatty acid oleate (18:1) prevents palmitate-induced deficits in mitochondrial motility and membrane polarization.72

Live imaging of mitochondrial trafficking in situ in the saphenous nerve of a mouse model with high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity and prediabetes confirms in vitro findings of mitochondrial depolarization.73 HFD mice develop prediabetes and neuropathy, like humans, characterized by a drop in NCVs and IENFD loss.72,74,75 In these animals, axonal mitochondria are depolarized with an impaired ability to dissipate membrane potential in response to axonal energy demands, suggesting a lack of reserve capacity.73 In tandem, there is evidence of changes in mitochondrial morphology and enhanced PGC1α expression, possibly to compensate for loss in membrane potential and energy deficits.

Whole sural nerve bioenergetics, which represent viable axons ex vivo in the same HFD model, show significantly lower ATP production, coupling efficiency, and mitochondrial density,76 indicating severely impaired function with mitochondrial respiration uncoupled from ATP production. In contrast, proximal ex vivo DRG neurons from HFD animals ramp up basal respiration and ATP production, presumably to offset the distal axonal bioenergetic dysfunction in the sural nerve. These studies illustrate a distal-to-proximal breakdown in mitochondrial bioenergetics and energy production, which underlies DPN. Metabolomics and flux analysis of sciatic nerves from T2D mice with DPN indicate reduced β-oxidation and tricarboxylic acid cycle activity,77 confirming a bioenergetic crisis.

Currently, we propose that longer-chain saturated fatty acids result in lower mitochondrial ATP production secondary to less efficient β-oxidation.78 Simultaneously, dyslipidemia impairs mitochondrial trafficking, leading to a deficiency of mitochondria in the distal nerve. Lower ATP production coupled with fewer mitochondria causes bioenergetic failure, which triggers distal-to-proximal nerve damage that manifests clinically as DPN. In contrast, the monounsaturated fatty acid oleate, which is efficiently metabolized,78 can prevent palmitate-induced mitochondrial injury, presumably by enhancing β-oxidation and ATP production.

Importantly, while criteria for diabetes are not met, HFD mice have elevated FBG and impaired glucose tolerance.72,74,75 Glucose can affect mitochondrial bioenergetics in sensory axons62,63,64 although both in vivo73,76 and in vitro studies64,71,72 suggest the perturbations secondary to dyslipidemia are more disruptive to axonal mitochondria trafficking. Studies have also addressed the impact of HFD on protein homeostasis79 and lipid profiles in nerves of HFD mice,75,76 and on the mouse75,76 and human80–82 plasma lipidome. HFD in vivo and palmitate in vitro induces endoplasmic reticulum stress in DRG.79 Triacylglycerols accumulate in sciatic nerves of HFD animals75 while nerve lysophospholipids and plasmalogens are lowered.76 When comparing HFD mice from distinct genetic backgrounds, the ensuing neuropathy phenotypes infer a plasma and nerve lipid signature,76 comprising triacylglycerols, sphingomyelins, phospholipids, and various complex lipids, linked to DPN. Although the precise significance remains uncertain, it is possible specific lipids reflect the underlying degenerative process or possess bioactivity that influence nerve function. Indeed, swapping dietary saturated fatty acids for monounsaturated fatty acids in HFD mice improves nerve function without ameliorating basic plasma metabolic profiles,72 suggesting potential fatty acid-mediated effects within the local nerve milieu that contribute to neuropathy.

Oxidative stress, an imbalance between excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and clearance, holds a central role in DPN pathophysiology.83 Earlier research considered mitochondria the major source of oxidative damage (Figure 3B),62,84–86 but recent studies identify additional pro-oxidant enzymes like NADPH oxidases (Noxs) as potent ROS sources in DPN.87 Specifically, Nox288,89 and Nox490,91 are upregulated in diabetic nerves and contribute to structural and functional nerve damage via lipid, protein, and DNA oxidation, impairing axonal signaling92 and transport.93 SCs are more resilient to oxidative stress and initially respond to elevated ROS by activating their antioxidant response.94 However, long periods of ROS eventually produce mitochondrial dysfunction, Nox4-induced oxidative stress, and SC apoptosis.91,95 ROS can also activate downstream effectors like PKC96 and jun N-terminal kinase,97 which are independently associated with DPN pathology.7 Together, these factors induce pro-inflammatory mediators, which vice versa, results in a detrimental feed-forward loop of injury (discussed below).

At steady state, PNS-resident macrophages locally surveil the endoneurial space,98,99 and upon injury, are joined by infiltrating macrophages to initiate nerve repair.100,101 These repair macrophages transition to a more pro-inflammatory phenotype over time, accumulate and cause nerve damage102,103 a pattern seen in T1D and T2D rodent models103,104 and the skin of persons with painful DPN.105 Studies show that depleting macrophages102,106 or inhibiting their migration107 improves mechanical sensation102,106 and large fiber function.107 Accumulating macrophages trigger cytokine and chemokine release, including pro-inflammatory interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, chemokine ligand 2, and tumor necrosis factor-α.104,108 This highly inflammatory environment intensifies existing oxidative stress, further aggravating nerve damage.7 Future studies addressing the intersection of inflammation with nerve bioenergetics and resulting DPN pathology will be critical to identifying therapeutic avenues in DPN.

If SC-axon metabolic coupling is required for normal nerve function, disrupted coupling could injure axons and promote DPN. Mounting evidence suggests axonal energetic deficits can initiate programmed axon death,109,110 leading to the study of axon integrity in animal models with SC metabolic perturbations. The resulting animal models develop neuropathy with features characteristic of DPN.

Liver kinase B1 (LKB1) is a serine threonine kinase, which regulates cellular energy homeostasis by phosphorylating the master energy sensor AMPK along with other AMPK-related kinases and is important for nerve health.111 SC-specific LKB1 deletion generates a mouse model with early unmyelinated sensory axon degeneration followed by large, myelinated fiber damage,22 as observed in DPN. LKB1-deficient SCs exhibit profoundly dysregulated energy metabolism, including aberrant lactate production and diminished mitochondrial respiration. The earlier degeneration of small unmyelinated axons versus large, myelinated axons in SC LKB1 knockout mice suggests small fibers may rely more heavily on SC energetic support,22 which is anticipated based on their larger energy consumption.112 Alternatively, LKB1-deficient SCs may possibly release harmful, axon-destabilizing substrates, which could accumulate at higher concentrations in small diameter axons. Importantly, the early loss of small fibers in this SC LKB1 knockout animal model parallels findings in human DPN,13 further supporting the importance of SC-axon metabolic coupling in DPN pathogenesis.

There are inconsistent findings from studies investigating the transporter MCT1, which mediates lactate shuttling from SCs to axons, providing needed substrates for energy-requiring axons (Figures 2B and 3B). In one study, SC-restricted MCT1 knockout from mice triggered hypomyelination of sural sensory neurons, with slower tail sensory NCV with aging, without concurrent changes to sciatic motor neurons.24 In contrast, another study found SC-restricted MCT1 or MCT4 knockout did not affect tail sensory or sciatic motor NCV, axon myelination, or Remak bundle structure, although it compromised motor end-plate structure.25 It is possible that MCT1-mediated lactate transfer may only become essential to axons with aging or disease. Aligned with this idea, SC-restricted MCT1 deficiency slows peripheral nerve regeneration in mice sustaining sciatic nerve crush injury,113 and potentiates DPN in STZ-T1D animals with slower sensory and motor NCV, reduced action potential amplitudes, and demyelination compared to non-deficient mice.114 Overall, more research is needed to determine whether impaired SC-axon metabolic coupling through disrupted MCTs, with decreased shuttling of lactate from the SC to the axon as a source of energy, contributes to DPN.

Pyruvate kinase (PK) is the terminal enzyme in glycolysis, which dephosphorylates phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate. Together with the activities of the key enzymes lactate dehydrogenase and pyruvate dehydrogenase, PK governs cellular lactate production. Expressing the M2 PK isoform (PKM2) increases lactate production. Thus, PKM2 knockout from SCs may feasibly lower lactate production and lactate shuttling to axons. Indeed, SC-restricted PKM2 knockout in mice lowers axonal lactate levels upon electrical stimulation versus wild-type animals, which is offset by a relative increase in axonal glucose.23 Despite this increase in glucose, ATP production remains lower overall. Sciatic nerve myelin and NCV are unaffected in mutant PKM2 knockout mice, although motor axon terminals retract, and mitochondrial trafficking is slowed. Thus, although PKM2 knockout reduces axonal lactate, it does not lead to sensory neuropathy or a DPN-like phenotype. This may in part be due to demonstrated compensatory mechanisms in response to the PKM2 knockout, e.g., enhanced axon glucose uptake,23 which counters metabolic perturbations in SCs and SC-axon coupling, e.g., suppressed lactate transfer.

To what extent SC-axon metabolic coupling contributes to DPN remains an active area of investigation. The initiating events in DPN development may involve axonal toxicity from hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia, aggravated by deficient SC-axon metabolic coupling, further destabilizing axons and eventually leading to axon injury, dysfunction, and death. This idea is supported by a recently discovered SC-driven adaptation that occurs upon axon injury by enhancing SC-axon metabolic coupling to stabilize distressed axons.115 SCs distal to a nerve injury site rapidly upregulate glucose import and glycolytic activity to enhance MCT shuttling to distressed energy-starved axons, blocking their deterioration. This metabolic adaptation is driven by SC mTORC1. Injured nerves with mTORC1 deficient SCs degenerate more rapidly because SCs cannot upregulate glycolysis and MCT shuttling, as discussed above. These findings reveal a critical role for augmented SC-axon metabolic coupling via MCT shuttling to support perturbed axons (Figures 2B and 3B). Future research should investigate these intriguing possibilities and assess the contribution of SC-axon metabolic coupling, specifically from the perspective of DPN.

In addition to their supportive functions, SC-derived EVs can also trigger nerve injury in DPN. Hyperglycemia-treated SCs release pathologic EVs, which suppress axonal growth in vitro and accelerate DPN development in vivo post injection in db/db T2D mice.116 EV cargo contains elevated miR-28, miR-31a, and miR-130a, which reduce expression of target proteins, including DNA methyltransferase-3α and growth-associated protein-43.116 In contrast, homeostatic SC-derived EVs increase DRG neurite outgrowth in vitro and restore small and large fiber function upon injection into db/db mice.117 The underlying neuroprotective mechanisms involve increased miR-21, miR-27a, and miR-146a and lower target proteins like NF-κB and PTEN, which are potential pathogenic players in DPN.7 Another study reported similar results regarding the role of miR-21 in SC-derived EVs and their beneficial effects on neurite outgrowth in vitro.118 Although this is a nascent area of investigation, these results suggest that SCs under impaired metabolic conditions can release pathologic EVs, which promote nerve degeneration and DPN (Figure 3B), whereas homeostatic EVs may promote nerve repair (Figure 2B). Future research is needed to better understand the function of EVs in the context of DPN.

DPN pathophysiology is complex, so agnostic high throughput Omics approaches119 can improve understanding by revealing previously unanticipated pathways. Integrative methods combining two or more Omics datasets can identify novel associations in DPN. Computational methods can also be leveraged to identify drug targets.120. Towards this end, the past decade has seen a rapid rise in the number of Omics DPN datasets119 along with bioinformatics analysis pipelines.

Transcriptomics research analyzes gene expression changes in peripheral nerves of both DPN animal models and humans, consistently revealing immune response and inflammatory pathway involvement in neuropathy. There is an enriched inflammatory response in human sural nerve biopsies from diabetic patients with rapidly progressing versus more stable DPN.121 In tissue proximal to the site of nerve injury, aberrant inflammatory processes in DRG were present in a small group of patients with painful DPN.122 Complimenting human studies, transcriptomics of nerve tissues from T1D and T2D rodents also show dysregulated inflammatory responses during DPN.75,123–128 However, the direction in inflammatory gene expression changes, i.e., up- or downregulated, are rarely concordant in mouse and human,129 challenging clinical translation.

Microarray121,123,125 and bulk RNA-seq122,124 were previously the primary tools, but newer approaches, such as single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq)130–132 and spatial132 transcriptomics, allow a better understanding of cellular heterogeneity on DPN pathogenesis. scRNA-seq analysis of DRG from STZ T1D rats with painful DPN identified a novel peptidergic neuronal cluster, characterized by neurofilament and cytoskeletal gene expression, linked to mechanical allodynia.130 scRNA-seq of foot skin fibroblasts from human diabetic study participants with nonhealing (n=4) versus healing (n=4) diabetic foot ulcers found distinct clusters for various cell types, including immune cell profiles.131 A larger study made similar findings, further characterizing a fibroblast cluster associated with healing diabetic foot ulcers, enriched in matrix remodeling and inflammatory response gene.132 Spatial transcriptomics localized this fibroblast cluster at the wound bed versus the wound edge or unwounded skin.132 Additionally, differential polarization of M1 versus M2 macrophages was noted in healing versus nonhealing diabetic foot ulcers.

A few studies have investigated epigenetic dysregulation in DPN,133 finding evidence of differential DNA methylation in pathways related to immune response, PI3K-Akt signaling, and glycerophospholipid metabolism.134,135 Recent evidence also implicates altered miRNA expression in DPN development,136 such as miR-467b and miR-466a in dorsal horn of STZ-induced T1D mice, predicted to target tumor necrosis factor-α and IL1B.137

Lipids and metabolites are also important in DPN pathophysiological and are studied by metabolomics, either alone or in conjunction with other Omics data like transcriptomics. A recent integrative lipidomics-transcriptomics study of prediabetes and T2D animal models highlighted the crucial role of triglyceride metabolism in DPN, identifying a potential therapeutic target, diacylglycerol acyltransferase 2, a key enzyme in triglyceride synthesis.75

Overall, to date, DPN Omics studies have uncovered valuable insight. Moving forward, integrating the vast knowledge from Omics datasets, along with machine learning/deep learning, will be crucial in DPN research and to identify therapeutic targets.

Neuropathic pain is defined as “pain caused by a lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory system”.138 This is a common and disabling consequence of DPN, affecting between 25 to 50% of patients.139 In DPN, pain like sensory dysfunction is length dependent, predominantly affecting feet and occasionally also the hands. It remains a major challenge for the field to understand why some patients with DPN develop neuropathic pain and others do not. Neuropathic pain likely arises due to the interactions between injury to peripheral sensory neurons, genotype, and environment.140 Detailed case-control studies, including deep phenotyping of neuropathy, sensory profiles, and metabolic biomarkers, are providing insights into these pathophysiological factors.

Deep phenotyping suggests an association between neuropathic pain and DPN severity; participants with neuropathic pain have a greater degree of sensory loss (assessed by quantitative sensory testing) across a range of sensory modalities.141–143 A recent study using machine learning found poorer glycemic control (higher HbA1c) correlated with painful (versus painless) DPN.144 There is also an association between painful DPN and higher BMI,144,145 although there is no detailed analysis of lipid profiles.

How are these metabolic changes linked to the underlying neurobiological processes driving neuropathic pain? One emerging mechanism is post-translational modification by reactive metabolites of ion channels expressed in sensory neurons (Figure 4). Ion channels are critical for the initial transduction of sensory stimuli by nociceptors, action potential generation and propagation, and, finally, neurotransmitter release within the dorsal horn of the spinal cord.146 They are also important targets for drug discovery. Genetic variants in voltage-gated sodium channels (Nav1.7, Nav1.8, sodium channel β2 subunit) have been linked to the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain.147–149 However, the full ‘channelome’ is yet to be explored and, in some cases, rare channel variants do not segregate in painful versus painless DPN groups.150

Methylglyoxal is a glycolytic side product that is elevated in diabetes and linked to DPN risk151 and, particularly, to painful DPN.152 Methylglyoxal can modify Nav1.8152 and the ligand-gated ion channel TRPA1 (transient receptor potential cation channel, subfamily A, member 1), which responds to environmental irritants,153 enhancing channel activity. Since these ion channels are enriched in nociceptors, they result in hyper-excitability and pain. Indeed, recent evidence in humans shows painful TRPA1-dependent activation of nociceptors by methylglyoxal.154 A recent paper demonstrated a further potential interaction of methylglyoxal with hypomagnesemia on neuropathic pain.155

SUMOylation is the covalent modification of proteins by small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) polypeptides. Metabolic enzymes and ion channels are differentially SUMOylated in DPN.156 The ligand-gated ion channel TRPV1 (transient receptor potential cation channel, subfamily V, member 1) expressed by nociceptors and activated by noxious heat and capsaicin is key to peripheral pain sensitization.157 TRPV1 SUMOylation at lysine 324, a key residue of channel functionality, regulates excitability in T1D DPN.156 Mice expressing a SUMOylation defective TRPV1 do not acquire the expected thermal hyperalgesia early during the course of DPN but rather rapidly develop diabetic thermal hypoalgesia. At later DPN phases in mouse and human tissue, SUMOylated TRPV1 is decreased, contributing to the expected late phase thermal hypoalgesia in both murine and human DPN. TRPV1 is also subject to other post-translational modifications. For instance, it is sensitized in the context of hyperglycemia, through high mobility group box 1-mediated activation of the receptor for advanced glycation end products.158

More recently, interest has grown in hyperpolarization-activated and cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN)-2 channels, which are important ‘pacemaker’ channels in nociceptors that modulate firing frequency. The second messenger cAMP strongly shifts the voltage dependence of HCN2 activation. Although diabetes does not alter HCN2 expression, recent data show channel activity increases due to elevated cAMP levels, contributing to pain-related hypersensitivity in animal models.159 Further studies are needed to determine what metabolic or inflammatory factors raise cAMP levels in DRG, and if this elevation is directly attributable to diabetes. Nevertheless, HCN2 represents a potential therapeutic target worthy of more study. In addition to these channel modifications, changes in channel expression levels, such as potassium channels,7 are also associated with painful DPN.

Recent studies suggest changes in lipid metabolism influence stress pathways in sensory neurons and pain. Interestingly, HFD mice develop mechanical pain hypersensitivity associated with endoplasmic reticulum stress markers in nociceptors.79 Selectively ablating the liver X receptors, which play a crucial role in regulating cholesterol and fatty acid homeostasis, from nociceptors exacerbates endoplasmic reticulum stress and mechanical pain hypersensitivity. Conversely, a liver X receptor agonist reduces mechanical pain hypersensitivity. HFD mice with prediabetic neuropathy also exhibit increased calcium signaling in DRG nociceptors and fragmented mitochondria morphology before the onset of mechanical allodynia and small fiber degeneration.160 The contribution of calcium overload on mitochondrial dysfunction and pain was supported by selectively deleting the mitochondrial calcium uniporter, which restored normal mitochondrial morphology and reversed mechanical allodynia. Recent human genetic data also links mitochondria to neuropathic pain. Meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies of neuropathic pain found a genome-wide significant locus at chromosome 12q23.1, mapped to SLC25A3, which encodes a mitochondrial phosphate transporter critical for ATP production.161

Research on painful DPN has largely focused on neurons and their axons. However, a recent study found that ablating terminal SCs in mice reduced IENFD and triggered pain-like behaviors, which suggests that terminal SCs may maintain small fiber health and regulate nociception.162 Several studies also show that infiltrating macrophages and the pro-inflammatory mediators they release sensitize nociceptors and induce painful DPN.102,163 These results suggest a key role for macrophages during painful DPN and that targeting macrophages may represent a viable therapeutic strategy. Together, these results highlight the need to better understand how non-neuronal cellular mechanisms interact with sensory neurons to promote painful DPN.

Although the initial insult occurs to peripheral nerves, it is important to recognize that brain and spinal cord responses are also important to the development of neuropathic pain. Brain imaging indicates that painful DPN is associated with structural changes in key somatosensory regions, such as thalamus and somatosensory cortex, and altered functional connectivity in ascending pathways and cortical regions sub-serving pain as well as the descending pain modulatory system.164–166 Currently, how structural and functional brain changes linked to painful DPN relate to metabolic factors is incompletely understood. Multiple potential mechanisms may contribute, such as vascular injury, oxidative stress, osmotic changes, and glial and neuronal injury.167 Future studies are needed to determine how aberrant sensory afferent activity interacts with maladaptive plasticity within the CNS to initiate and maintain neuropathic pain. This will likely need large multi-modal cohorts with clinical, metabolic biomarkers, and imaging outcomes, including national level initiatives, such as UK-Biobank.168

Since bioenergetics failure is a DPN characteristic, many attempts have been made to enhance energy pathways in the PNS. These efforts span AMPK agonists67 and agonists of the transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR), which activates fatty acid uptake and catabolism.169,170,171 Preclinical and some clinical evidence suggest some salutary effects (Supplementary Table S2), warranting further studies of AMPK and PPAR signaling. An additional candidate is sterile alpha and TIR motif containing 1 (SARM1), a sensor of metabolic stress with NAD(P)ase activity and key mediator of axon Wallerian degeneration. Although Sarm1 knockout improves several nerve metrics,172,173 the role of SARM1 on DPN progression remains uncertain but an active research area. Mitochondrial uncoupling has also been examined as a potential approach to mitigate DPN by disengaging mitochondrial respiration from energy production, expanding the reservoir of NADH and FADH2 available for fatty acid oxidation and tricarboxylic acid cycle. However, the uncoupling agent niclosamide ethanolamine does not improve DPN in mice.174

Good glycemic control more effectively prevents DPN in patients with T1D versus T2D. A recent new direction is borne by a class of drugs called sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, which decrease blood glucose by enhancing glucose excretion by kidneys. However, despite the novel mechanism for regulating glucose levels, based on evidence to date, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors do not ameliorate DPN in the T2D context (Supplementary Table S2).175,176

Given the risk from dyslipidemia on DPN, one possible path forward for preventing DPN rests on managing cholesterol and triglyceride levels (Supplementary Table S2). Statins are HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, which block cholesterol synthesis, and could conceivably lower DPN risk by exerting lipid-lowering and pleiotropic effects, e.g., endothelial activation, anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects. Studies variably report salutary, marginal, or negative effects from statin use,177 possibly because too little cholesterol, an essential myelin component, may harm nerve health.178 Overall, the current state of research suggests statins are unlikely to prevent DPN but alternative lipid-lowering or multi-target strategies warrant further investigation.

The efficacy of general antioxidants for DPN, such as the long-studied α-lipoic acid, remain uncertain due to variable clinical trial results.179 Efforts have refocused on targeted anti-inflammatory, e.g., minocycline,180 or antioxidant agents, e.g., inhibitors of NADPH oxidases91,181 and xanthine oxidase.182 Results are promising and support further study (Supplementary Table S2).

Overall, for targeted candidates investigated to date, effectiveness in rodents has yet to be validated in humans. It is critical to note that mechanism-based targets are not entirely conserved across mouse and human DPN,129 challenging translation of potential therapies. Several candidates, despite their rational, mechanism-based design, fail to improve DPN in animal models for several possible reasons. Drugs are not tissue-specific and may differentially impact complication-prone tissues due to distinct underlying pathophysiology and metabolic states.77,183 This suggests it may be necessary to identify cellular and subcellular targets of gluco- and lipotoxicity to attain full efficacy. Additionally, drugs can exert pleiotropic actions independent of their energy-regulating or glucose- and lipid-lowering properties, which may be detrimental in the nerve. Finally, due to multifactorial DPN pathophysiology, it is possible single-targeting agents may not suffice to mitigate nerve damage.

DPN prevention and management has pivoted from a purely glucose centric to a multifactorial approach also encompassing obesity and dyslipidemia. The American Diabetes Association now recommends diet and exercise to manage T2D and the MetS as first-line treatment for DPN in T2D.184 Despite the recommendation, large randomized controlled trials are sparse, although early clinical evidence from mostly non-randomized studies suggest some benefits from exercise.185 Notably, DPN can improve with exercise independent of significant weight loss or betterment in glycemic status, suggesting mechanisms in the nerve microenvironment besides systemic changes.

Newer studies have examined more rigorous exercise regimens, such as progressive resistance training186 and high-intensity interval training,187 with variable benefits in humans (Supplementary Table S2). Regarding the effects of diet on DPN,185 preclinical research has tested dietary reversal,75 replacing fat calories from saturated to monounsaturated72 or polyunsaturated fatty acids,188 ketogenic diet,189 and supplementation with specific substrates, such as serine.190 Evidence underscores the importance of fatty acid intake composition, not just total calories, suggesting pleiotropic effects. Large, randomized studies of diet in humans are lacking. However, polyunsaturated fatty acid supplementation in T1D participants increased fiber length in a non-validated DPN metric, corneal fiber length.191 Besides diet, weight loss generally demonstrates varying benefits on DPN,185 such as medical weight-management by calorie restriction192 and surgical weight loss through bariatric surgery.193–195 Longer-term follow-up studies using validated DPN assessment methods are still needed.

Unfortunately, current pharmacological therapies for painful DPN are inadequate due to poor efficacy and tolerability.196,197 Guidelines have recently been published for treating DPN. First line pharmacological therapy includes tricyclic antidepressants, serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, gabapentinoids, and sodium channel blockers.198 A recent randomized controlled trial compared amitriptyline, duloxetine, or pregabalin monotherapy versus combined therapy in those that failed monotherapy. This trial showed additional benefit from combined versus monotherapy but efficacy did not differ between treatment pathways in terms of the exact choice of drugs.199 It is anticipated that future studies employing patient stratification by sensory profiles, IENFDs, genetic profiles, or other biomarkers, can improve targeting of therapies to patients most likely to respond.200

As well as improved targeting of existing therapies, the field desperately needs novel, non-addictive treatments for painful DPN. Selective small molecule blockers of several ion channels discussed, e.g., Nav1.7, Nav1.8, TRPA1, are under clinical development.201 There is interest in non-pharmacological therapies, such as neuromodulation with advanced DRG and spinal cord stimulation.202,203 Given growing evidence that metabolic factors are relevant to neuropathic pain per se in addition to neuropathy development, there is interest in therapeutic exercise-based lifestyle interventions. So far, there have been relatively few trials and the evidence-base is modest,204 but this is an exciting prospect for disease-modifying therapy.

The past decade of research has led to new perspectives in DPN pathophysiology. Whereas hyperglycemia was the primary focus, recent research has additionally elucidated the impact of dyslipidemia on nerve dysfunction, which converges on similar themes of bioenergetics failure within the unique PNS anatomy. Moreover, drawing parallels to the CNS, SC-axon metabolic coupling supports nerve health, and may breakdown during DPN, but more research is needed in this area. Additional lines of future inquiry include the impact of insulin signaling on nerve metabolism in the context of DPN, along with the contribution of EVs and mechanism-based inflammatory targets. Increasingly, computational methods may help shed light on the multifactorial and complex pathophysiology of DPN. Hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia trigger pain pathways, e.g., by modifying ion channels, and further investigation could yield tractable therapeutic targets. The involvement of both hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia in DPN pathophysiology is leading to a reoriented view on potential treatments, with implications for patients. Randomized clinical trials on dietary interventions and medical or surgical weigh loss along with exercise interventions are anticipated with great interest.
