# Natural disease course modeling of achondroplasia to evaluate the efficacy of recifercept in the absence of a placebo control arm in phase II study.
**Authors:** Qiao W, Boucher M, Slade A, Dawra VK
**Journal:** CPT Pharmacometrics Syst Pharmacol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/psp4.13143](https://doi.org/10.1002/psp4.13143)

## Content

While randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blinded clinical studies are the gold standard for evaluating the efficacy of investigational drugs, the use of placebo in children with achondroplasia should be limited because it provides no clinical benefit while exhausting study participants' treatment window. Recifercept is an investigational drug for treating children with achondroplasia aged 2-10 years. An alternative efficacy evaluation method, instead of a placebo control arm, was employed in the phase II study. Prior to participating in the phase II study, participants completed a natural history (NH) study. Based on the NH data, a multi-variate linear mixed effects natural disease course model of three anthropometric end points (standing height, sitting height, and arm span) was developed. The model was validated using published growth charts of children with achondroplasia. Subsequently, the model was used to simulate the natural growth trajectories (without any treatment) of the three anthropometric end points for the individuals enrolled in the phase II study. To quantify the efficacy of recifercept, the simulations were compared with the observations post-recifercept treatment in the phase II study. For all the tested doses of recifercept, at the individual level, the observations were comparable to the simulations at 6 and 12 months post-recifercept treatment, suggesting no treatment effect. The results contributed to the decision of terminating recifercept clinical development. This work delivers a framework that could eliminate the need for placebo in clinical trials yet provide sufficient evidence for evaluating the efficacy of an investigational drug.

Achondroplasia is the most common form of dwarfism that affects approximately 1/25,000 live births worldwide.
1
,
2
 The disease is caused by mutations in the transmembrane domain of fibroblast‐growth‐factor receptor‐3 (FGFR3) protein,
3
 which is expressed in chondrocytes and mature osteoblasts. The mutations lead to sustained activation of the downstream signaling pathways that suppress chondrocyte activation and proliferation, and bone elongation. Physical features of people with achondroplasia include but not limited to short stature, short limbs, and long torso relative to limbs. Achondroplasia drug development faces several clinical challenges. First, achondroplasia is a growth disorder, and disease‐modifying treatments need to be initiated early in life to ameliorate the physical features that impact the patients' quality of life and medical conditions. In fact, it was found that the growth rate of newborns with achondroplasia is close to normal in the first year,
4
 and it drops to approximately the third percentile of normal within the second year, and it remains at around the third percentile of normal throughout the rest of life, suggesting that treatment during infancy is ideal for preventing a reduction of the growth rate. Carefully balancing the benefits and risks is needed to design clinical trials for achondroplasia.
5
 While randomized, placebo‐controlled clinical studies are preferred to investigate the effectiveness of a new treatment, for ethical reasons, the use of placebo in children with achondroplasia should be limited because it provides no medical benefit while exhausting the children's treatment window. Second, achondroplasia is a rare disease that has a small impact on the patients' life expectancy or intelligence compared with normal. Thus, parents/guardians are cautious when deciding whether to enroll their young children in clinical trials of investigational drugs. In addition to the small population size, enrollment in clinical trials for children with achondroplasia is slow. As a result, there was not an approved drug for treating children with achondroplasia in the United States until November 2021 when vosoritide
6
 received the United States (U.S.) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval. Establishing growth charts of children with achondroplasia is necessary to assist clinical evaluation of investigational drugs for treating this population, as recommended by the U.S. FDA.
7



The need to establish growth charts of children with achondroplasia is well recognized. Nehme et al. conducted the first study on the topic in 1976.
8

It is challenging to justify matching characteristics between participants with achondroplasia in clinical trials and patients contributing to the retrospective NH data. Thus, the growth charts derived from retrospective NH studies have limited use as references to evaluate the the effectiveness of investigational drugs especially for international multi‐center trials. For rare disease drug development, in general, FDA encourages sponsors to conduct prospective NH studies using the same inclusion / exclusion criteria and end points as the planned interventional studies, so that data of the prospective NH studies may be utilized as external controls to evaluate the effectiveness of the investigational drugs,

Recifercept is an investigational drug for treating children with achondroplasia.
16
 During its clinical development, a NH study (NCT05659719) was conducted to understand the natural growth trajectories of children with achondroplasia aged 0–15 years. Eligible children from the NH study were subsequently enrolled in a phase II study (NCT04638153), which was conducted without a placebo cohort to primarily assess the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy of recifercept in pre‐pubertal children with achondroplasia aged 2–10 years. Herein, a multi‐variate linear mixed effect achondroplasia disease course model was developed with a focus on three anthropometric end points including standing height, sitting height, and arm span. The final model was used to extrapolate the natural growth trajectories of the three anthropometric end points of the participants who were enrolled in the phase II study, representing their growth trajectories in the absence of any treatment. Comparison between the individual extrapolated natural growth trajectories and the corresponding observations post‐recifercept treatments quantifies the efficacy of recifercept. This work highlights that in the clinical development of drugs for rare diseases, an associated prospective protocol‐driven NH study not only accelerates the conduct of an interventional study but the NH study data, with appropriate modeling, can also potentially replace the need for a placebo cohort in the interventional study.

This study uses data from a NH study (NCT05659719) of children with achondroplasia and a phase II study (NCT04638153) of recifercept. The clinical study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Boards/Ethics Committees of the study sites and all participants provided written informed consent before participation in the studies.

In the NH study, children with clinically confirmed achondroplasia and aged ≤15 years on the date of consent/assent were enrolled. Tanner Staging of puberty and anthropometric measurements of the participants were collected at the baseline and thereafter, every 3 months for a maximum of 5 years. The primary anthropometric measurements were, but not limited to, standing height/length, sitting height/crown: rump length, and arm span. Individual's participation was terminated as soon as he/she reached puberty completion (Tanner Stage 5).

The phase II study of recifercept is a randomized, dose‐finding study to determine the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy of recifercept in pre‐pubertal (Tanner Stage 1) children with clinically confirmed achondroplasia aged 2–10 years. Participants must have completed the NH study with ≥2 valid height/length measurements that are ≥3 months apart, and one of the timepoints must be within the 3 months prior to enrollment in the phase II study. Participants were randomized into one of the three recifercept subcutaneous dose groups: 1 mg/kg once a week (QW), 2 mg/kg twice a week (BIW), and 1.5 mg/kg once a day (QD). Tanner Staging of puberty was performed at the screening, and at every 3 months after the enrollment in the study along with the anthropometric measurements. The primary efficacy end point of the study is increase in standing height at 12 months after the first dose of recifercept.

To reduce the variability in anthropometric measurements, for each participant, measurements were performed by the same personnel, trained according to the contents of the study‐specific Anthropometric Measurement Manual.

The natural disease course modeling was focused on standing height, sitting height, and arm span of children with achondroplasia aged 2–11 years. The NH data of two participants without age information were excluded from further analyses or modeling. The longitudinal profiles of the three anthropometric end points for the rest of the participants were visually inspected. Datapoints with obvious measurement/reporting errors based on visual inspection were excluded. In addition, for each anthropometric end point, individuals with <2 datapoints were excluded from model development. The procedure of data processing is summarized in Figure S1.

A staged approach was taken to develop a natural disease course model for children with achondroplasia aged 2–11 years based on the NH study data (Figure 2). We first modeled each anthropometric end point independently to select variance models for random effects, and covariate models for fixed effects. Given the linear model structure, random effects may be modeled as variance in the intercepts, slopes, or both for individual's growth trajectories. As shown in Table S1, for each anthropometric end point, the model with random effects on both the intercept and the slope provided the lowest AIC and BIC. Thus, these models were used to select covariate models.

The NH dataset had information on participants' race, ethnicity, gender, and study center which were potential covariate candidates. However, distributions of ethnicity (76% White, 15% Asian, 1% Black or African American, and 21% Others), race (93% non‐Hispanic or Latino), and study sites (variable number of enrollments across 31 study sites) were skewed; gender (44% female) was the only factor evaluated as a covariate for population intercepts and slopes. Next, we sought to understand the correlation of the random effects of standing height, sitting height, and arm span based on the models with gender as a covariate for intercepts. Figure 3b illustrates that among the three anthropometric end points, the random effects on the intercepts (Spearman correlation coefficients >0.5) and the random effects on the slopes (Spearman correlation coefficients >0.5) were highly correlated, respectively. However, for each anthropometric end point, the random effect on the intercept and that on the slope were not significantly correlated.

In summary, for each anthropometric end point, a separate linear mixed effect model was developed with random effects on both intercept and slope. Gender was found to be a covariate on the intercepts for all three models. Among the three models, the random effects on the intercepts and the random effects on the slopes were highly correlated respectively.

To capture the correlation between the random effects in standing height, sitting height, and arm span, a multi‐variate linear mixed effect model was developed. As informed by the results of the uni‐variate models, the final multi‐variate model incorporated random effects in the intercepts and the slopes of each of the anthropometric end‐point models. In addition, gender was used as a covariate on the typical values of intercepts. As shown in Table 1, all the random effects were estimated with shrinkage values in the range of −21.5% to 19.0%. The diagnostic plots for each anthropometric end point present acceptable model fit (Figures 4 and S2). The fitted annualized growth rates of children with achondroplasia aged 2–11 years are 4.51, 2.58, and 4.18 cm/year for standing height, sitting height, and arm span, respectively.

Next, the natural disease course model was used to quantify the efficacy of recifercept by comparing individual's anthropometric measurements at 6 months and 12 months after the first recifercept treatment in the phase II study to the model simulations for the same timepoints representing the growth trajectories in the absence of any treatment.

Twelve out of 57 participants (21%) in the phase II study were excluded from the evaluation of recifercept efficacy. One participant was 11.03 years old at the time of enrollment into the phase II study, which did not satisfy the age‐based inclusion criterion; seven participants were under 2 years old when they were enrolled in the NH study and their natural growth models were not characterized; and four participants did not reach the milestone of 6 months after the first recifercept treatment. Individual's growth trajectories of the 45 participants who contributed to the evaluation of recifercept efficacy within 12 months after the first recifercept treatment are shown in Figure S3A. A total of 237 anthropometric datapoints collected from the 45 participants were available for evaluating recifercept efficacy, consisting of 79 datapoints per anthropometric end point. There were 13 (4 for male/9 for female) datapoints per anthropometric end point for age ≥10 years per anthropometric end point.

For the 45 participants who contributed to recifercept efficacy evaluation, simulated natural growth trajectories using the individual natural disease course model were compared with their measurements post‐recifercept treatment (Figure S3B). The summary results are shown in Table 2. For standing height, regardless of the recifercept dose/dosing regimen, the difference between the standing heights post‐recifercept treatment and the projected standing heights as per their natural disease course (without any treatment) was negative on average. The average differences ranged between −0.6 and −0.5 cm after 6 months of treatment and ranged between −1.1 and − 0.8 cm after 12 months of treatments, across the three recifercept treatment arms. The 80% CI of the differences included 0 cm, suggesting that there is no difference between the participants standing heights after recifercept treatment and their projected standing heights without any medical treatment. The comparison suggested that recifercept administered for up to 12 months was not efficacious in improving the growth of standing heights of children with achondroplasia aged 2–10 years. Similar results were observed for sitting height as well as arm span.

Overall, the multi‐variate natural growth model of children with achondroplasia was used to simulate the natural growth trajectories of individuals who were enrolled in the recifercept phase II study. Comparison between the model simulations and the observed individuals' anthropometric measurements 6 months and 12 months after recifercept treatment indicated that recifercept at the three tested dose levels was not efficacious in children with achondroplasia aged 2–10 years.

Achondroplasia is a genetic disorder that affects patients' growth. Treatment for achondroplasia should be initiated early in life. At the time of designing the NH study, there was no approved pharmacological treatment for children with achondroplasia except growth hormones. The NH study was conducted, prior to initiating the phase II study, to refine the choice of clinical measurements, identify sites and patients, and provide baseline growth trajectory for patients enrolled in the phase II study.

Although randomized, double‐blind, and placebo‐controlled studies are the gold standard for clinical research, implementation of the clinical trial design can be challenging for rare disease studies due to ethical and practical reasons, especially when the targeted patients are children with a rare disease that has a limited treatment window. Achondroplasia is such an example in which early treatment could help change the natural course of the disease. Thereby, during the development of recifercept, a prospective protocol‐driven NH study was conducted and eligible participants from the NH study were rolled over to a phase II study to evaluate the efficacy of recifercept without a placebo control. To quantify the efficacy of recifercept, a natural disease course model was developed to describe the growth trajectories of children with achondroplasia aged 2–11 years in the NH study. By comparing the projected individual natural growth trajectories to their growth trajectories post‐recifercept treatment, efficacy of the tested recifercept doses can be reliably quantified without a placebo control. The modeling and simulation results suggested no evidence of recifercept efficacy in children with achondroplasia aged 2–10 years, regardless of the dose level. Despite excluding the data of 21% participants, enrolled in the phase II study from the analyses for reasons such as (a) not meeting the age‐based inclusion criterion, (b) unavailability of individual natural growth models, or (c) short participation time in the phase II study, the conclusion based on the modeling and simulation results is consistent with the lack of dose–response in the phase II data. The observation period of 6–12 months post‐treatment should be sufficient given vosoritide had demonstrated significant increase in annualized growth velocity by 6 months post‐treatment start.

 It is worth noting that modeling the growth of children with achondroplasia aged <2 years was outside the scope of the current work. The estimated intercepts from the natural disease course model do not reflect the anthropometric measurements of the population at birth.

Further, the goal of the present study was to simulate individual growth trajectories that could be used to compare with the observed individual data in the phase II study to quantify recifercept efficacy at 6 months and 12 months post‐treatment start, and therefore the individual natural disease course model fitted to the NH data will be suitable for growth projection. Thus, datapoints with obvious measurement/reporting errors and participants with only one timepoint data were excluded from NH study for model development. For participants whose data were included in the model development, the duration of NH data collection per individual ranged between approximately 3 months and 4.26 years. While averages of triplicate measurements per timepoint were used for the model development to minimize technical variabilities in anthropometric measurements, theoretically occasional variabilities across the data collection period may compromise the accuracy of the individual natural disease course model, especially in rare cases when there were only two timepoints per individual that were approximately 3 months apart. In the presented study, there were only two individuals who contributed to recifercept efficacy assessment had their individual natural disease course models developed based on data with two timepoints, but their growth projection (extrapolation) recapitulated the observed data in the phase II study, suggesting suitability of the individual models for growth projection despite their limited NH data.

Comparison between the model simulations and Merker et al.'s data indicated that for certain anthropometric end points, the model slightly over‐predicted the published population average starting from age 8 years. However, the impact on evaluating recifercept efficacy should be minimal. Specifically, the evaluation period of recifercept efficacy for each participant was only 12 months in the phase II study. Each participant in the phase II study must have completed the NH study with ≥2 valid anthropometric measurements that were ≥3 months apart, and one of the timepoints must be within the 3 months prior to enrollment in the phase II study. Thereby on the individual level, the NH data used for model fitting were adjacent to the data from the phase II study. If a participant in the phase II study was approaching height plateau at puberty completion, the only potential nonlinear components in our study, the associated growth rate should be captured by the annualized growth rate fitted from the NH data that was also around the same age. In fact, most of the participants in phase II have completed the study during the linear growth phase before puberty onset.

While the growth of children with achondroplasia may differ depending on the races, ethnicities, and regions, the current study only evaluated gender as a covariate due to data limitation. Future studies could be conducted with balanced data collection for races, ethnicities, and regions to evaluate the association of these potential covariates with growth of achondroplasia.

Overall, this work highlights the impact of a NH study in drug development for rare diseases where biology, ethics, and small size of the patient population limit the application of placebo‐controlled studies in clinical research. It also highlights the opportunity of applying natural disease course/progression modeling to make efficient studies, saving resources and cost and, to make early unambiguous program‐level decisions.
