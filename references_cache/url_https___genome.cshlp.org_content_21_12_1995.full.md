<meta name="citation_abstract" content="&lt;p&gt;The biological basis for the development of the cerebro-cerebellar structures required for posture and gait in humans is poorly understood. We investigated a large consanguineous family from Turkey exhibiting an extremely rare phenotype associated with quadrupedal locomotion, mental retardation, and cerebro-cerebellar hypoplasia, linked to a 7.1-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 17p13.1–13.3. Diffusion weighted imaging and fiber tractography of the patients&#039; brains revealed morphological abnormalities in the cerebellum and corpus callosum, in particular atrophy of superior, middle, and inferior peduncles of the cerebellum. Structural magnetic resonance imaging showed additional morphometric abnormalities in several cortical areas, including the corpus callosum, precentral gyrus, and Brodmann areas BA6, BA44, and BA45. Targeted sequencing of the entire homozygous region in three affected individuals and two obligate carriers uncovered a private missense mutation, WDR81 p.P856L, which cosegregated with the condition in the extended family. The mutation lies in a highly conserved region of WDR81, flanked by an N-terminal BEACH domain and C-terminal WD40 beta-propeller domains. WDR81 is predicted to be a transmembrane protein. It is highly expressed in the cerebellum and corpus callosum, in particular in the Purkinje cell layer of the cerebellum. &lt;italic&gt;WDR81&lt;/italic&gt; represents the third gene, after &lt;italic&gt;VLDLR&lt;/italic&gt; and &lt;italic&gt;CA8&lt;/italic&gt;, implicated in quadrupedal locomotion in humans.&lt;/p&gt;" />

<div  class="abstract-wrapper section-title" id="abstract">

    <h3>Abstract</h3>


            <div><div class="abstract" xmlns:xhtml="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml"><p class="first" id="d382222e343">The biological basis for the development of the cerebro-cerebellar structures required for posture and gait in humans is poorly understood. We investigated a large consanguineous family from Turkey exhibiting an extremely rare phenotype associated with quadrupedal locomotion, mental retardation, and cerebro-cerebellar hypoplasia, linked to a 7.1-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 17p13.1–13.3. Diffusion weighted imaging and fiber tractography of the patients' brains revealed morphological abnormalities in the cerebellum and corpus callosum, in particular atrophy of superior, middle, and inferior peduncles of the cerebellum. Structural magnetic resonance imaging showed additional morphometric abnormalities in several cortical areas, including the corpus callosum, precentral gyrus, and Brodmann areas BA6, BA44, and BA45. Targeted sequencing of the entire homozygous region in three affected individuals and two obligate carriers uncovered a private missense mutation, WDR81 p.P856L, which cosegregated with the condition in the extended family. The mutation lies in a highly conserved region of WDR81, flanked by an N-terminal BEACH domain and C-terminal WD40 beta-propeller domains. WDR81 is predicted to be a transmembrane protein. It is highly expressed in the cerebellum and corpus callosum, in particular in the Purkinje cell layer of the cerebellum. <span class="italic">WDR81</span> represents the third gene, after <span class="italic">VLDLR</span> and <span class="italic">CA8</span>, implicated in quadrupedal locomotion in humans.</p></div></div>

  </div>

            <div><div class="article" xmlns:xhtml="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml"><hr></hr><p class="first" id="d1499529e355">Developmental abnormalities of the cerebellum are a rare and genetically heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by loss of balance and coordination. Identification of the genes responsible for these disorders provides mechanistic insights into the regulation of neuronal development, differentiation, morphogenesis, migration, and organization (<a href="#B8" data-rid="B8" id="xref-ref-8-1">Fogel and Perlman 2007</a>). These genes can be identified by exploiting targeted genomic sequencing in combination with linkage analysis and homozygosity mapping (<a href="#B32" data-rid="B32" id="xref-ref-32-1">Ropers 2007</a>; <a href="#B1" data-rid="B1" id="xref-ref-1-1">Bilguvar et al. 2010</a>). We applied this approach to the analysis of cerebellar hypoplasia and quadrupedal locomotion in an extended consanguineous family from southern Turkey.</p><p id="d1499529e366">Multiple families have been reported with cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and disequilibrium syndrome (CAMRQ) (<a href="#B39" data-rid="B39" id="xref-ref-39-1">Tan 2006</a>; <a href="#B42" data-rid="B42" id="xref-ref-42-1">Turkmen et al. 2006</a>, <a href="#B43" data-rid="B43" id="xref-ref-43-1">2009</a>; <a href="#B23" data-rid="B23" id="xref-ref-23-1">Moheb et al. 2008</a>; <a href="#B27" data-rid="B27" id="xref-ref-27-1">Ozcelik et al. 2008</a>; <a href="#B17" data-rid="B17" id="xref-ref-17-1">Kolb et al. 2010</a>). All the reported CAMRQ families are consanguineous with recessive inheritance of their condition. Clinical characteristics vary slightly among the families. In four families from Turkey and Iran, the condition is due to homozygosity for mutations in the <span class="italic">VLDLR</span> gene encoding the very low density lipoprotein receptor (CAMRQ1 [MIM 224050]). Each of these four families harbors a different <span class="italic">VLDLR</span> mutation. In a fifth family, from Iraq, the condition is due to homozygosity for a missense mutation in the <span class="italic">CA8</span> gene encoding carbonic anhydrase VIII (CAMRQ3 [MIM 613227]). In Family B, the first family described in the literature and also referred to as Uner Tan syndrome (<a href="#B39" data-rid="B39" id="xref-ref-39-2">Tan 2006</a>), homozygosity mapping revealed a 7.1-Mb interval on chromosome 17p13, containing 192 genes and at least 20 pseudogenes, that segregates with the disease (CAMRQ2 [MIM 610185]) (<a href="#B42" data-rid="B42" id="xref-ref-42-2">Turkmen et al. 2006</a>; <a href="#B27" data-rid="B27" id="xref-ref-27-2">Ozcelik et al. 2008</a>). In order to identify the mutation responsible for CAMRQ2 in Family B, we targeted and fully sequenced the 7.1-Mb genomic interval and evaluated all variation in the region.</p><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e406"></span><h2 class="section-title">Results</h2><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e409"></span><h3 class="section-title">Description of the affected family</h3><p id="d1499529e412">Family B came to medical attention because of the unusual form of locomotion in five of the 19 siblings. A detailed clinical description, including video recordings and genetic mapping, was published elsewhere (<a href="#B39" data-rid="B39" id="xref-ref-39-3">Tan 2006</a>; <a href="#B42" data-rid="B42" id="xref-ref-42-3">Turkmen et al. 2006</a>; <a href="#B27" data-rid="B27" id="xref-ref-27-3">Ozcelik et al. 2008</a>). Pedigree analysis suggested autosomal recessive inheritance. Linkage analysis and homozygosity mapping revealed a single locus on chromosome 17p between D17S1866 and D17S960. Illumina 300 Duo v2 BeadChip SNP genotype data of two of the affected individuals (05-984 and 05-987) revealed a single 6.8-Mb homozygous stretch between markers rs4617924–rs7338 (chr17: 114,669–6,917,703) and confirmed that chromosome 17p is the only region of interest (Supplemental Fig. 1).</p><p id="d1499529e423">The phenotype was further characterized by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and morphometric analyses (<a href="#F1" data-rid="F1">Fig. 1</a>). The most dramatic morphological differences were significant reductions in volume in the cerebellum and corpus callosum of the patient's brain (<a href="#F1" data-rid="F1">Fig. 1A</a>). Both the cortex and the white matter of the cerebellum were significantly smaller in the patients. In contrast, the volume occupied by the caudate nucleus was significantly larger. Significant structural differences were also detected in the motor areas precentral gyrus and BA6 (increased mean curvature and gray matter volume) and motor speech areas pars opercularis and pars triangularis (increased cortical thickness and mean curvature) (<a href="#F1" data-rid="F1">Fig. 1B</a>). A detailed account of the morphometric analyses is presented in Supplemental Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 1. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and fiber tractography revealed moderate to high atrophy in superior, middle, and inferior cerebellar peduncles (Supplemental Fig. 3).</p><div class="fig panel" id="F1"><div class="label">Figure 1.</div><div class="caption"><p class="first" id="d1499529e444">MRI-based morphological analysis of brain from affected and unaffected individuals. (<span class="italic">A</span>) Midsagittal MRI scans of a healthy control individual (<span class="italic">left</span>) and affected relative from Family B (<span class="italic">right</span>). WDR81 p.P856L was predicted to be “damaging” (SIFT score, 0) by SIFT (<a href="#B24" data-rid="B24" id="xref-ref-24-3">Ng and Henikoff 2001</a>), “probably damaging” (PSIC score difference, 2.724) by PolyPhen (<a href="#B38" data-rid="B38" id="xref-ref-38-3">Sunyaev et al. 2001</a>), and “under evolutionary constraint” (GERP score, 5.68) by GERP (<a href="#B2" data-rid="B2" id="xref-ref-2-2">Davydov et al. 2010</a>).</p><p id="d1499529e632">The function of WDR81 is unknown, but clues can be derived from its structure. The conserved region of WDR81 that includes P856 is flanked on the N-terminal side by a BEACH (Beige and Chediak-Higashi) domain at amino acids 352–607. BEACH proteins have been implicated in membrane trafficking (<a href="#B44" data-rid="B44" id="xref-ref-44-1">Wang et al. 2000</a>), synapse morphogenesis (<a href="#B16" data-rid="B16" id="xref-ref-16-1">Khodosh et al. 2006</a>), and lysosomal axon transport (<a href="#B22" data-rid="B22" id="xref-ref-22-1">Lim and Kraut 2009</a>). A BEACH domain is the major structural feature of neurobeachin, a scaffolding protein disrupted in a patient with autism (<a href="#B47" data-rid="B47" id="xref-ref-47-1">Volders et al. 2011</a>). WDR81 p.P856L lies in a major facilitator superfamily (MFS) domain, a region characteristic of solute carrier transport proteins (<a href="#B35" data-rid="B35" id="xref-ref-35-1">Saier et al. 1999</a>). The C terminus of WDR81 is composed of six WD-repeats that are likely constituents of a beta-propeller. Supporting the likelihood that WDR81 is a transmembrane protein is the observation that <span class="italic">WDR81</span> transcript expression is increased in membrane-associated RNA in contrast to cytoplasmic RNA (4.14 folds, <span class="italic">P</span> = 0.03, and 1.78 folds, <span class="italic">P</span> = 0.0002 in Gene Expression Omnibus [GEO] [<a target="xrefwindow" href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/" id="d1499529e665">http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/</a>] data set GSE4175) (<a href="#B5" data-rid="B5" id="xref-ref-5-1">Diehn et al. 2006</a>).</p><p id="d1499529e672">In order to assess a possible role for WDR81 in regulating motor behavior, we evaluated the expression profiles of human and mouse <span class="italic">WDR81/Wdr81</span> isoform 1 in the brain. Human <span class="italic">WDR81</span> isoform 1 transcript was expressed in all the tissues evaluated (Supplemental Fig. 5). In particular, all the brain tissues were positive for the transcript, with highest levels of expression in the cerebellum and corpus callosum (<a href="#F3" data-rid="F3">Fig. 3A</a>). In the mouse brain at post-partum day P7, <span class="italic">Wdr81</span> expression was observed in Purkinje cell layer in the cerebellum (<a href="#F3" data-rid="F3">Fig. 3B,C</a>). Despite these advances, demonstrating the causality for a mutation in the absence of two or more independent cases remains a challenge. This is particularly true when multiple variants, none of them with obvious effect on protein function, cosegregate with the phenotype in the family; the candidate gene encodes a previously uncharacterized protein with multiple isoforms, of which the critical mutation is on only one; and the candidate mutation is a missense. However, unique families and uncharacterized proteins exist, and precisely because of this reason, it becomes imperative to fully exploit genetics and genomics approaches to distinguish the causative mutation.</p><p id="d1499529e787">We describe here the discovery of a mutation associated with an extremely rare and genetically heterogeneous autosomal recessive phenotype in a unique consanguineous family (<a href="#B39" data-rid="B39" id="xref-ref-39-4">Tan 2006</a>). The putative causative mutation could be distinguished from previously unknown rare polymorphisms in the same genomic region by analysis of conservation at all candidate variant sites, by the presence of polymorphic stops in the critical region of another candidate gene, and by genotyping ethnically matched unaffected individuals who would not be expected to carry homozygous mutations at the mutant site. We conclude that the WDR81 p.P856L mutation is the cause of cerebellar hypoplasia associated with quadrupedal locomotion in Family B.</p><p id="d1499529e792"><span class="italic">WDR81</span> is an uncharacterized gene. It shows similarity with a host of genes, including <span class="italic">NSMAF</span> (neutral sphyngomyelinase activation associated factor), <span class="italic">NBEA</span> (neurobeachin), and <span class="italic">LYST</span> (lysosomal trafficking regulator). The <span class="italic">LYST</span> gene contains HEAT/ARM repeats, a BEACH domain, and seven WD40 repeats (<a href="#B45" data-rid="B45" id="xref-ref-45-1">Ward et al. 2000</a>). Nearly all reported <span class="italic">LYST</span> mutations result in protein truncation and lead to Chediak-Higashi syndrome (CHS), which is characterized by accumulation of giant intracellular vesicles leading to defects in the immune and blood systems (<a href="#B34" data-rid="B34" id="xref-ref-34-1">Rudelius et al. 2006</a>). Two patients with missense <span class="italic">LYST</span> mutations have been reported (<a href="#B15" data-rid="B15" id="xref-ref-15-1">Karim et al. 2002</a>). Interestingly, these patients presented with neurological symptoms without immunological involvement. The Lyst<sup>Ing3618</sup>/Lyst<sup>Ing3618</sup> mutant mouse harbors a missense mutation in the WD40 domain. Purkinje cell degeneration accompanied by age-dependent impairment of motor coordination without signs of lysosomal deficiency in immunological organs were characteristics of these animals (<a href="#B34" data-rid="B34" id="xref-ref-34-2">Rudelius et al. 2006</a>).</p><p id="d1499529e834">Expression of <span class="italic">WDR81</span> at high levels in the human cerebellum and corpus callosum and in the Purkinje cell layer of the mouse cerebellum is consistent with our observations of major structural abnormalities in these regions of the brain of affected individuals. Together, these observations suggest a possible role for WDR81 in motor behavior. Further work will be required to understand the normal biological function of WDR81 and the role of the mutation in causing cerebellar hypoplasia and quadrupedal locomotion. Genomic analysis of Family B demonstrates that WDR81 is highly likely to be critical to these developmental processes.</p></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e839"></span><h2 class="section-title">Methods</h2><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e842"></span><h3 class="section-title">Human subjects</h3><p id="d1499529e845">The institutional review boards of Bilkent, Hacettepe, Baskent, and Cukurova Universities approved the study (decisions: BEK02, 28.08.2008; TBK08/4, 22.04.2008; KA07/47, 02.04.2007; and 21/3, 08.11.2005, respectively). The remaining 14 participants were age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Sequence Search and Alignment by Hashing Algorithm (SSAHA) (<a href="#B25" data-rid="B25" id="xref-ref-25-1">Ning et al. 2001</a>) was used to determine probe uniqueness by NimbleGen (Roche NimbleGen). Sequence capture was conducted by the NimbleGen facility using 25 μg of input DNA. Captured DNA samples were subjected to standard sample preparation procedures for 454 GS FLX sequencing with Titanium series reagents. Four full 454 GS FLX runs were conducted for two affected individuals (05-985, 05-987) and their unaffected obligate carrier parents (05-981 father, 05-982 mother). Sequence data were initially mapped to human genome reference sequence and annotated using the GSMapper software package (Roche). Fold enrichment of the target region was calculated with the formula ∑REMTrm/STrm: ∑RMG/SG as described previously (REMTrm, number of reads mapped to target region; STrm, size of target region; RMG, number of reads mapped outside of the target region; SG, size of human genome) (<a href="#B30" data-rid="B30" id="xref-ref-30-1">Rehman et al. 2010</a>). Variants were identified with ALLDiff and more stringent HCDiff approaches (<a href="#B13" data-rid="B13" id="xref-ref-13-3">Hedges et al. 2009</a>). Annotation of variants was made by GSMapper software using the refGene table of the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Genome Browser (<a href="#B9" data-rid="B9" id="xref-ref-9-1">Fujita et al. 2010</a>). Ensembl 62 genome annotation data for hg19 human genome assembly were extracted using the BIOMART data-mining tool for further analysis of intronic and intergenic variants in terms of hypothetical genes and splicing variants (<a href="#B7" data-rid="B7" id="xref-ref-7-1">Flicek et al. 2011</a>). Novel variants were reported based on the SNPs included in the reference SNP database. For Illumina sequencing, a total of 6,184,539-bp-long unique probes were designed to target a 9-Mb genomic region spanning the disease locus (chr17:0–9,059,276; hg19) using a custom NimbleGen HD2 2.1M sequence capture microarray. Another affected individual was sequenced with the Illumina Genome Analyzer IIx. Illumina sequence data were mapped to the reference genome using MAQ tools (<a href="#B20" data-rid="B20" id="xref-ref-20-1">Li et al. 2008</a>), and single nucleotide variants were determined with Samtools (<a href="#B21" data-rid="B21" id="xref-ref-21-1">Li et al. 2009</a>). To determine indels, data were mapped with BWA (<a href="#B19" data-rid="B19" id="xref-ref-19-1">Li and Durbin 2010</a>) and analyzed with Samtools. Sequence data were visually analyzed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) (<a href="#B31" data-rid="B31" id="xref-ref-31-1">Robinson et al. 2011</a>).</p></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e880"></span><h3 class="section-title">Array based genotyping</h3><p id="d1499529e883">We conducted Illumina 300 Duo v2 BeadChip for two affected individuals (05-984, 05-987) according to the manufacturer's recommendations (Illumina). The image data were normalized, and the genotypes were called using data analysis software (Bead Studio, Illumina). Sex, inbreeding, and sibship were confirmed. The Mendelian compatibility of sequence variants was analyzed with PLINK (<a href="#B29" data-rid="B29" id="xref-ref-29-1">Purcell et al. 2007</a>).</p></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e888"></span><h3 class="section-title">DNA sequencing</h3><p id="d1499529e891">Confirmation of novel variants identified by next-generation sequencing was done with conventional capillary sequencing. The Primer3 software (<a href="#B33" data-rid="B33" id="xref-ref-33-1">Rozen and Skaletsky 2000</a>) was used to design PCR primers for the amplification of candidate variants (Supplemental Table 12). Products were analyzed via gel electrophoresis and were sequenced using forward and reverse primers on an ABI 3130 XL capillary sequencing instrument (Applied Biosystems). Sanger sequence trace files were analyzed with the CLCBio Main Workbench software package (CLCBio Inc.).</p></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e896"></span><h3 class="section-title">Population screening</h3><p id="d1499529e899">To distinguish the disease-causing variant from novel polymorphisms, a population screening approach was conducted for each candidate variant. Allele-specific PCR (AS-PCR) and restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analyses were performed (Supplemental Table 12) on 1098 chromosomes from a healthy control population. In addition, the first-, second-, and third-degree relatives of the affected family, amounting to 177 individuals, were sampled for genotype analysis. Sanger sequencing was performed to confirm all of the variants detected in the normal population using the above-mentioned methods. Racial distribution of the control group was 100% Caucasian, including 22% from southeastern Turkey.</p></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e901"></span><h3 class="section-title">Quantitative real-time RT-PCR analysis of <span class="italic">WDR81</span> expression</h3><p id="d1499529e907">First-strand cDNA was prepared from multi-tissue RNA panels (Clontech: 636567, 636643; Agilent: 540007, 540117, 540137, 540157, 540053, 540005, 540143, 540135) with RevertAid kit and random hexamer primers (Fermentas; K1622) after DNase I (Fermentas; EN0521) digestion. The PCR primers located in exon 1 and flanking the mutation site were designed using Primer3 software (Supplemental Table 13; <a href="#B33" data-rid="B33" id="xref-ref-33-2">Rozen and Skaletsky 2000</a>). SYBR Green real-time PCR were realized according to standard protocols (BioRad; 170-8882) with 100% PCR efficiency. Each assay included minus RT and nontemplate controls. C<sub>t</sub> values were normalized to <span class="italic">GAPDH</span> as an internal control. The data were analyzed using the Pfaffl method (<a href="#B28" data-rid="B28" id="xref-ref-28-1">Pfaffl 2001</a>).</p></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e921"></span><h3 class="section-title">In situ hybridization</h3><p id="d1499529e924">In order to examine the specific expression pattern of <span class="italic">Wdr81</span> gene in the mouse brain, probes that contain the mutated region in human patients were prepared by PCR amplification of the region from mouse genomic DNA and subsequent cloning into plasmids. The riboprobes were synthesized by using Dig-labeled NTPs, and in situ hybridization experiments were performed as described (<a href="#B40" data-rid="B40" id="xref-ref-40-1">Tekinay et al. 2009</a>). The Animal Ethics Committee of Bilkent University approved procedures for the tissue extraction and for in situ hybridization tests. Animals were group housed in a 12-h dark, 12-h light cycle. Embryo and P7 brain sections were prepared as described (<a href="#B11" data-rid="B11" id="xref-ref-11-1">Gong et al. 2003</a>). Twenty-micrometer sagittal sections were taken with a cryostat (Leica). The antisense probe was prepared by PCR amplification from the mouse genomic DNA and subsequent cloning into pCR4-TOPO vector (Invitrogen). A modified version of pSK vector was used for cloning the sense probe of the same region. Digoxigenin (Dig)-labeled riboprobe was transcribed using Dig-NTP in the transcription reaction. Riboprobes were purified with Mini Quick Spin DNA columns (Roche) prior to hybridization. Sections were incubated at 60°C overnight in hybridization buffer containing 50% formamide, 5× SSC, 5× Denhardt's reagent, 50 μg/mL heparin, 500 μg/mL herring sperm DNA, and 250 μg/mL yeast tRNA. Hybridized sections were washed for 90 min with 50% formamide and 2× SSC at 60°C. Probes were detected with anti-Dig Fab fragments conjugated to alkaline phosphatase and NBT/BCIP substrate mixture (<a href="#B40" data-rid="B40" id="xref-ref-40-2">Tekinay et al. 2009</a>).</p></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e938"></span><h3 class="section-title">Bioinformatics analyses</h3><p id="d1499529e941">Homozygosity mapping analysis was performed using HomozygosityMapper software (<a href="#B36" data-rid="B36" id="xref-ref-36-1">Seelow et al. 2009</a>). SIFT (<a href="#B24" data-rid="B24" id="xref-ref-24-4">Ng and Henikoff 2001</a>) and PolyPhen (<a href="#B38" data-rid="B38" id="xref-ref-38-4">Sunyaev et al. 2001</a>) tools were used to predict the functional impact of the variants. Genomic Evolutionary Rate Profiling (GERP) scores for each variant were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser allHg19RS_BW track (<a href="#B2" data-rid="B2" id="xref-ref-2-3">Davydov et al. 2010</a>). The PFAM protein domain search module of CLCMain Workbench V5.0 (CLCBio, Inc.) and ScanProsite (<a href="#B10" data-rid="B10" id="xref-ref-10-1">Gattiker et al. 2002</a>) tools were used to predict domains and possible effects of the variant on protein product. Membrane spanning domains were predicted using TMpred software (<a target="xrefwindow" href="http://www.ch.embnet.org/software/TMPRED_form.html" id="d1499529e959">www.ch.embnet.org/software/TMPRED_form.html</a>). Homology searches were performed with CLCMain Workbench using appropriate modules (reference sequence accession codes for WDR81 orthologs are <span class="italic">Ailuropoda melanoleuca</span>, XP_002918082; <span class="italic">Callithrix jacchus</span>, XP_002747874; <span class="italic">Danio rerio</span>, XP_001921778; <span class="italic">Equus caballus</span>, XP_001502383; <span class="italic">Gallus gallus</span>, XP_415806; <span class="italic">Monodelphis domestica</span>, XP_001371487; <span class="italic">Mus musculus</span>, NP_620400; <span class="italic">Oryctolagus cuniculus</span>, XP_002718930; <span class="italic">Pan troglodytes</span>, XP_523527; <span class="italic">Pongo abelii</span>, XP_002826860; <span class="italic">Rattus norvegicus</span>, NP_001127832; <span class="italic">Sus scrofa</span>, XP_003131868; <span class="italic">Taeniopygia guttata</span>, XP_002194363; <span class="italic">Tetraodon nigroviridis</span>, CAG08933; <span class="italic">Xenopus</span> [<span class="italic">Silurana</span>] <span class="italic">tropicalis</span>, XP_002937192). Published microarray data sets of E9.5, E11.5, and E13.5 mouse brain tissue (GSE8091) were downloaded from the GEO database (<a target="xrefwindow" href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/geo/query/acc.cgi" id="d1499529e1016">http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/geo/query/acc.cgi</a>) (<a href="#B12" data-rid="B12" id="xref-ref-12-2">Hartl et al. 2008</a>) and processed with GeneSpring GX V11.1 software (Agilent Technologies). Data sets were grouped within day groups, and standard quality control and filtering analysis were performed (<a target="xrefwindow" href="http://www.chem.agilent.com/cag/bsp/products/gsgx/manuals/GeneSpring-manual.pdf" id="d1499529e1022">http://www.chem.agilent.com/cag/bsp/products/gsgx/manuals/GeneSpring-manual.pdf</a>). Differentially expressed genes within the day groups were filtered using a one-way ANOVA test (Bonferroni-corrected <span class="italic">P</span> &lt; 0.001). Genes that correlated with <span class="italic">Wdr81</span> (<span class="italic">R</span> = 0.95 − 1.0) were obtained using the “Find Similar Entity Lists” module of the software. Functional annotation clustering was performed using the obtained gene list by DAVID tools (<a href="#B14" data-rid="B14" id="xref-ref-14-2">Huang et al. 2009</a>). <span class="italic">WDR81</span> differential expression in the GEO data sets was further investigated using the NextBio System, a web-based data-mining engine (<a href="#B18" data-rid="B18" id="xref-ref-18-1">Kupershmidt et al. 2010</a>), and the GSE4175 (<a href="#B5" data-rid="B5" id="xref-ref-5-2">Diehn et al. 2006</a>) data set was selected as a significant difference in membrane-associated RNA versus cytoplasmic RNA comparisons. Ensembl identifiers of the candidate genes and transcripts are as follows: <span class="italic">WDR81</span> [ENSG00000167716; ENST00000409644], <span class="italic">MYBBP1A</span> [ENSG00000132382; ENST00000254718], and <span class="italic">ZNF594</span> [ENSG00000180626; ENST00000399604].</p></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e1057"></span><h3 class="section-title">MRI data acquisition and structural analysis procedures</h3><p id="d1499529e1060">MRI data were acquired using a three Tesla scanner (Magnetom Trio, Siemens AG) with a 12-channel phase-array head coil. A high-resolution T1-weighted three-dimensional (3D) anatomical-volume scan was acquired for each participant (single-shot turbo flash; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm<sup>3</sup>; repetition time [TR] = 2600 msec; echo time[TE] = 3.02 msec; flip angle = 8°; field of view [FOV] = 256 × 224 mm<sup>2</sup>; slice orientation = sagittal; phase encode direction = anterior-posterior; number of slices = 176; acceleration factor [GRAPPA] = 2). DTI data were acquired using a single-shot spin-echo EPI with a parallel imaging technique GRAPPA (acceleration factor 2). The sequence was performed with 30 gradient directions, and the diffusion weighting b-factor was set to 800 sec/mm<sup>2</sup> (TR, 6400 msec; TE, 88 msec; in-plane resolution, 1 mm × 1 mm; slice thickness, 3.0 mm; 50 transverse slices; base resolution, 128 × 128). Structural analyses were performed with the Freesurfer image analysis package (<a target="xrefwindow" href="http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/" id="d1499529e1071">http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/</a>). The analyses involved intensity normalization, removal of nonbrain tissue, subcortical segmentation (<a href="#B6" data-rid="B6" id="xref-ref-6-1">Fischl et al. 2002</a>), and identification of the white matter/gray matter boundary upon which cortical reconstruction and volumetric parcellation were performed. The cortex was then registered to a spherical atlas and parceled into units according to the gyral and sulcal structure based on the Desikan-Kilinay Atlas (<a href="#B3" data-rid="B3" id="xref-ref-3-1">Desikan et al. 2006</a>) and the Destrieux Atlas (<a href="#B4" data-rid="B4" id="xref-ref-4-1">Destrieux et al. 2010</a>). Next, using the same software, we performed morphometric analyses of cortical thickness, mean curvature, surface area, and volume for each unit of parcellation and computed the group differences. Significant differences between the groups are determined using two-tailed unpaired <span class="italic">t</span>-tests at an alpha level of 0.05. Fiber tracking was performed in MedINRIA (<a href="#B41" data-rid="B41" id="xref-ref-41-1">Toussaint et al. 2007</a>). Fibers with FA &lt; 0.3 were excluded from the analysis. Region of interests (ROIs) were drawn manually over cross-sections of superior, middle, and inferior cerebellar peduncles, using the MRI Atlas of Human White Matter as a reference (<a href="#B26" data-rid="B26" id="xref-ref-26-1">Oishi et al. 2010</a>). ROIs were drawn at approximately corresponding locations for the patients and healthy controls. Fiber tracts were first limited to pass through these ROIs and were then subsequently refined using a recursive tracking technique (<a href="#B41" data-rid="B41" id="xref-ref-41-2">Toussaint et al. 2007</a>). T1-weighted images were coregistered with DWI data using FSL (<a href="#B37" data-rid="B37" id="xref-ref-37-1">Smith et al. 2004</a>; <a href="#B46" data-rid="B46" id="xref-ref-46-1">Woolrich et al. 2009</a>). Final tracts were manually overlaid onto high-resolution T1-weighted images for illustration purposes.</p></div></div><div class="section"><span id="d1499529e1103"></span><h2 class="section-title">Data access</h2><p id="d1499529e1106">Sequence data of the homozygous region has been deposited at the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ; <a target="xrefwindow" href="http://www.ddbj.nig.ac.jp/" id="d1499529e1108">http://www.ddbj.nig.ac.jp/</a>) under accession no.
