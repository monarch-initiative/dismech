A diagnosis of synchronous tumors is always questionable when they show similar histology. We present a case of synchronous tumors, i.e. melanoma (MM) arising from the scalp and angiosarcoma (AS) from the cheek in a patient of Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP). The presence of melanin in AS of the cheek led to a differential diagnosis and a possibility of transdifferentiation of malignant melanoma in the first instance. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was negative for melanoma markers (Melan A, SOX-10 & S 100) and positive for vascular markers (ERG, CD31 & CD34) ruling out transdifferentiation and concluding the cheek swelling as angiosarcoma. The presence of non-cutaneous malignancies is rare in XP and the presence of MM in a nearby site and the melanin pigment in the cutaneous malignancy mislead the diagnosis. The morphology of the tumor was the key which led to the further workup of the case.

Synchronous multiple primary tumors (SMPT) are defined as two or more primary malignancies diagnosed within a six-month period.[1]Genetic diseases are one of the common etiologies for the SMPT. Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP) is a genodermatosis characterized by a defect in nucleotide excision repair, leading to hypersensitivity to UV radiation and ultimately culminating in occurrence of cutaneous malignancies, the most common being squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and basal cell carcinoma (BCC). The incidence of melanoma in XP is only three percent and is shown to have PTEN mutation frequently rather than BRAF mutations inherent to them. Malignant melanoma is known to exhibit numerous morphological variations and aberrant expression of IHC markers.[2,3]It is also known to show differentiation toward different non-melanocytic elements, making it a great mimicker.[3]

Cutaneous angiosarcoma (AS) too is rarely associated with XP. Though there are no histological differences in these tumors as seen in normal populations, they do occur at an earlier age in XP. Angiosarcoma is of three types and is commonly associated with chronic lymphedema.

To date, to the best of our knowledge, only six cases of AS in association with XP have been reported.[4-9]In India, one case has been reported where the patient having a previous history of MM developed AS, but they were not synchronous in occurrence.[9]

We report a case of synchronous primary malignancies—MM and AS in a patient of XP which is the first of its kind and has the unique feature of the presence of melanin pigment in AS histology which led to the diagnostic workup of the case.

A 21-year-old male, with a known case of XP with history of consanguineous marriage in parents, presented with a brown to blackish pigmented ulcero-proliferative lesion on left cheek and a nodular mass over left parietal region on the scalp for 6 months. On examination, the cheek swelling measured 6 × 5.5 × 2.7 cm and the scalp swelling measured 5.4 × 4.8 × 1.8 cm [Figure 1]. The patient gave a significant past history of SCC and basal cell carcinoma on the forehead at 18 years of age. There was no significant family history.

Histopathology of incisional biopsies sent from both present lesions (cheek and scalp) showed poorly differentiated neoplasm with cells arranged in sheets. The individual cells were elongated with moderate eosinophilic cytoplasm, round nucleus, and prominent eosinophilic nucleoli. Occasional areas showed scant brown pigment in the cytoplasm. Additionally, a biopsy from the cheek showed focal areas with numerous vascular channels lined by pleomorphic tumor cells containing RBCs with few areas showing cellular tufts projecting into vascular channels. A diagnosis of malignant melanoma was given for scalp swelling and a diagnosis of cutaneous angiosarcoma with a differential diagnosis of MM was offered for the cheek swelling on biopsies. This was followed by wide local excision specimen of both masses. The histopathology showed features similar to the previous biopsies, i.e.,

1) Scalp lesion showed features of melanoma with solid sheets of elongated cells with occasional cells having melanin pigment.2) Cheek lesion showed solid sheets of tumor cells with focal areas showing vascular channels lined by pleomorphic cells. Occasional tumor cells with melanin were noted as in scalp lesion. Based on the morphological findings on histopathology, a preliminary diagnosis of MM with pseudoangiosarcomatous pattern was considered for the cheek swelling.

Further, a panel of immunohistochemistry markers HMB45, CD31, CD34, ERG, SOX-10, S-100, Melan-A, and PTEN was performed for both the cheek swelling and scalp swelling. The IHC results were as follows [Figures 2and3]:

PTEN expression was retained in both tumors. Additionally, qualitative RT-PCR for BRAF V600 mutation performed on both lesions was negative.

Considering the histomorphology, immunohistochemistry results, and BRAF analysis, a final diagnosis of angiosarcoma was rendered for cheek swelling and melanoma for the scalp swelling.

Xeroderma pigmentosum is a genodermatosis with a predisposition to the occurrence of cutaneous malignancies owing to its defective DNA repair mechanisms. SCC and BCC are frequently reported with less occurrence of malignant melanoma.[5]Non-cutaneous malignancies are rare, among which angiosarcoma is reported by few authors.[4-7]As per the literature, the presence of malignant melanoma and angiosarcoma as synchronous multiple primary malignancies in a case of Xeroderma pigmentosum is unusual and is the first time being reported from India. As the suspicion index of non-cutaneous malignancies is low in XP, the diagnosis of AS was a differential diagnosis in cheek swelling with MM as the primary diagnosis. The occurrence of melanoma in the vicinity (scalp), the presence of melanin pigment, and vascular channels prompted us to explore further the following possibilities for the above event -

1) Could it be a pseudoangiosarcomatous change in MM?[3]2) Is it aberrant immunohistochemical expression of vascular markers in MM?[10]3) Is it transdifferentiation of MM into AS, a phenomenon seen in melanoma?[11]4) Or is it de novo AS?

Hence, the decision to do a full immunohistochemistry panel was taken. The cheek swelling was completely negative for melanoma markers (Melan A, SOX-10 and S 100) ruling out aberrant immunohistochemical expression and strongly positive for vascular markers (ERG, CD31 and CD34) ruling out transdifferentiation and concluding a de novo AS. The scalp swelling was strongly positive for melanoma markers and completely negative for vascular markers. Additionally, molecular analysis was tried to confirm this finding. PCR for BRAF mutation (commonly seen in melanomas) was done which turned out to be negative in both tumors. On further literature review, we found that melanomas in XP frequently show PTEN mutations; hence, both lesions were analyzed for loss of PTEN. However, the results showed retained PTEN expression in both tumors. The complete molecular profiling with NGS could not be done in view of the expensive nature of the test. Therefore, we ruled out that both tumors do not have the usual genetic profile inherent to them by performing PCR for BRAF and immunohistochemistry for PTEN. Our final diagnosis was based on morphology and IHC results.

We report synchronous malignancies—AS and MM in patients with XP. The challenge in diagnosis was the peculiar histology of occasional tumor cells with melanin pigment in AS. The possibilities of transdifferentiation and aberrant expression of markers were explored and ruled out, making this case a first of its kind in India.

The treatment of AS includes surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, but in XP patients, the radiation therapy is contraindicated. Our patient underwent surgical resection and was doing well. He presented with a swelling on the cheek at the surgical site on a recent follow-up around two years post-surgery. He has been advised to take protective measures against sun exposure and has been asked to come for regular follow-up. Genetic counselling has also been advised.

Multiple synchronous primary malignant tumors are a frequent occurrence in XP. However, the exact diagnosis of each lesion may vary, and the possibilities of non-cutaneous malignancies should be kept in mind. Thorough workup is essential when the lesions are adjacent and with deceiving histology specially, when one of the lesions is melanoma with its great mimicking patterns. Transdifferentiation and aberrant IHC expression should be ruled out before reaching a conclusion because the treatment differs for each lesion.
