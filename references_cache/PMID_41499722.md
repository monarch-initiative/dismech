# HLA and T-Cell Receptor Investigations in Idiopathic and Paraneoplastic Opsoclonus-Myoclonus in Children.
**Authors:** Olivé-Cirera G, Muñoz-Sánchez G, Palou E, Ruiz García R, Fonseca EG, Luchoro JF, Aguilera-Albesa S, Maudes E, Martinez-Hernandez E, Sabater L, Dalmau JO, González A, Armangue T
**Journal:** Neurol Neuroimmunol Neuroinflamm (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1212/NXI.0000000000200541](https://doi.org/10.1212/NXI.0000000000200541)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome (OMAS) is a rare pediatric autoimmune neurologic disorder, with 40%-50% of cases occurring as a paraneoplastic syndrome in the setting of neuroblastoma or other neuroblastic tumors. The strong clinical similarities between idiopathic and paraneoplastic OMAS have led to the hypothesis that neuroblastoma may initially be present in idiopathic cases but spontaneously regress before OMAS onset. Both B- and T-cell mechanisms appear to contribute to OMAS autoimmunity, although their precise roles remain unknown. The aim of this study was to investigate similarities and differences between idiopathic and paraneoplastic OMAS by analyzing HLA profiles and T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoires in a Spanish pediatric cohort.

METHODS: Children with OMAS were enrolled in a nationwide prospective study (2013-2024). Neural autoantibody testing was performed in all patients. HLA genotyping was performed using next-generation sequencing (GenDx/Illumina) and compared with a reference Spanish population (n = 4,335). The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method was used to control for multiple comparisons across HLA antigens. TCR repertoires were analyzed in peripheral blood samples of 12 patients using the Adaptive Biotechnologies immunoSEQ assay. The bioinformatics tool Grouping of Lymphocyte Interactions by Paratope Hotspots (GLIPH2) was used to identify shared motifs within CDR3β-TCR sequences.

RESULTS: Among 25 children with OMAS (median age 22 months [interquartile range (IQR): 15-38]), 12 (48%) had neuroblastoma and 3 (12%) had neuronal antibodies (one with anti-Hu, one with anti-myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein, one against unknown neuronal surface antigens). Some HLA class II antigens (DRB1*08, DRB1*10, and DQB1*04) were consistently overrepresented in patients with OMAS compared with Spanish controls (all p-adjusted <0.05). Distinct HLA antigens were associated with idiopathic OMAS (HLA-DRB1*08, HLA-DQB1*04), while paraneoplastic OMAS was linked to HLA-DRB1*10 (all p-adjusted <0.05), suggesting subgroup-specific genetic risks. TCR analysis revealed that patients with paraneoplastic OMAS showed greater TCR repertoire similarity, whereas shared sequences were rare in idiopathic OMAS.

DISCUSSION: Despite similar clinical presentations, idiopathic and paraneoplastic OMASs appear to have distinct immunopathogenic mechanisms. In paraneoplastic OMAS, enrichment of specific HLA antigens and convergent TCR profiles supports antigen-driven T cell-mediated autoimmunity.

Opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome (OMAS) is a rare autoimmune disorder that typically affects previously asymptomatic children between 1 and 3 years of age,1 although it can also occur in adolescents and adults.2,3 It typically presents with abnormal eye movements, myoclonus, ataxia, irritability, and sleep disturbances. Approximately 40%–50% of pediatric cases have a paraneoplastic origin, usually neuroblastoma.1 Clinical similarities between paraneoplastic and idiopathic OMASs in young children have led to the hypothesis that neuroblastoma may have been initially present in idiopathic cases but spontaneously regressed before OMAS onset.4 Although autoimmune mechanisms are suspected in both forms of OMAS, the exact pathogenesis remains unknown.5 An antibody-mediated mechanism is supported by clonal B-cell expansion in patients' CSF and favorable clinical responses to immunotherapy, such as rituximab.6-9 Moreover, OMAS-associated neuroblastoma shows more prominent inflammatory infiltrates (CD20+ B cells and CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) than neuroblastomas of patients without OMAS.10,11 Although antibodies against intracellular or surface neuronal antigens (Hu, Ri, HNK1, glycine receptor) have been found in approximately 11% of patients with paraneoplastic OMAS (mainly adults),5,12,13 efforts to identify a common target antigen have been unsuccessful.14 Notably, no antibodies have been identified in idiopathic cases.

The high prevalence of autoimmune diseases among first-degree relatives of children with idiopathic or paraneoplastic OMAS15 suggests a genetic predisposition, potentially involving specific HLA haplotypes. Along with the lack of disease-specific autoantibodies, and T-cell involvement in anti-neuroblastoma immune responses,10,11,16 these findings support exploring antigen-driven T-cell mechanisms in OMAS.

In this study, we examined the HLA profile and T-cell repertoire (TCR) in children with paraneoplastic and idiopathic OMAS to assess the genetic contribution to autoimmunity and to identify similarities or differences that might indicate whether spontaneous tumor regression underlies idiopathic cases.

We included children (<18 years) with OMAS whose serum, CSF, and DNA samples were submitted by their physicians to our laboratory (IDIBAPS-Hospital Clinic, Barcelona, Spain) between 2013 and 2024. Recruitment was part of an ongoing prospective study involving 40 hospitals across Spain, investigating autoantibodies in children with suspected autoimmune encephalitis within 3 months of disease onset. DNA samples were also collected for genetic studies. Clinical information was obtained using structured questionnaires completed by treating physicians at disease onset, every 6 months for 24 months, and annually thereafter. Brain MRI studies at onset and during follow-up were centrally reviewed at IDIBAPS-Hospital Clinic. Treatment decisions were made at the discretion of the treating physicians.

Serum and CSF were screened for antibodies against intracellular or cell-surface neuronal antigens using rat brain tissue immunohistochemistry, as previously described.17 Positive samples were further tested using antigen-specific methods, including in-house cell-based assays (CBAs) with HEK293 cells expressing the target antigen or commercial immunoblots (PNS12, Euroimmun, Germany).17-19 In addition, all samples were systematically tested for antibodies against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) and glycine receptor GlyR using in-house live indirect immunofluorescence CBAs and against glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) using fixed CBAs.20

Peripheral blood samples were collected using standard 10-mL K2 EDTA Vacutainer tubes (BD, 366433). DNA was extracted, quantified, quality-checked with an Invitrogen Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, REF: Q32853, Waltham, MA), and stored at −80°C until analysis. HLA genotyping and TCR repertoire studies were performed at the Department of Immunology, Centre de Diagnòstic Biomèdic, Hospital Clínic de Barcelona, Spain.

HLA genotyping was performed by next-generation sequencing using the GenDx/Illumina platform as reported.21 HLA-A, B, C, DRB1, and DQB1 antigen frequencies in patients with OMAS were compared with those of the Spanish population (n = 4,335) from the Allele Frequency Net Database.22

TCR repertoire analysis was performed using the Adaptive Biotechnologies immunoSEQ assay, which applies bias-controlled multiplex PCR amplification and high-throughput sequencing to target rearranged TCR genes. In brief, the immunoSEQ TCR-B kit was used according to the manufacturer's instructions to amplify the CDR3 locus from genomic DNA extracted from peripheral blood. After verification of successful amplification and library preparation, sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform and analyzed using Adaptive Biotechnologies' ImmunoSEQ Analyzer software (V3.0), which identifies V, D, and J genes; filters out nonproductive sequences; and reports T-cell clonality.

We used the GLIPH2 tool, a bioinformatics platform that clusters similar TCR sequences to identify shared motifs, offering insights into immune responses and disease associations.23

Data are represented as counts and proportions for categorical variables, or median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Demographics and clinical and laboratory characteristics were compared using the Fisher exact test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. To account for multiple comparisons across HLA antigens (n = 63), the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied. Adjusted p values (p-adj) were calculated, and a threshold of p-adj <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.3 or Stata version 13.1.

For TCR repertoire analysis, quality control and summary statistics of CDR3 sequences (templates) were generated using the ImmunoSEQ Analyzer online portal provided by Adaptive Biotechnologies.24 Each sample was assessed for total absolute rearrangements, defined as the sum of unique detectable CDR3 sequences (or rearrangements) in the TCR-β chain.

To evaluate T-cell repertoire clonality, we calculated the Simpson Clonality Index, derived from the square root of the Simpson diversity metric.25 The Simpson Clonality Index,25 which measures the degree of TCR clonotype expansion, ranges from 0.002 to 0.44 in healthy individuals, with higher values indicating reduced TCR diversity due to clonal expansion.25 This measure of repertoire skewing is generally preferred over the Shannon Clonality Index because it is less sensitive to variations in the total number of sequences. Comparison between groups was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Written informed consent was obtained from patients or proxies. The study was approved by the Internal Review Boards of University Hospital Clinic of Barcelona.

Data will be available on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Among 25 patients with OMAS, 12 (48%) were paraneoplastic, all with neuroblastoma (6 female, 6 male), and 13 were idiopathic (4 female, 9 male). The median age at symptom onset was 22 months (IQR 15–38), with a median follow-up of 24 (IQR 12–48) months. Demographic information, neuronal antibody results, treatment, and outcomes are given in Table 1.

Neuronal antibodies were detected in 3 of 25 patients (12%). Two had paraneoplastic OMAS (one with anti-Hu, one with antibodies against an unidentified surface antigen), and one had idiopathic OMAS with MOG-IgG (titer 1/160). All 25 patients, including the MOG-IgG–positive case, had typical OMAS and normal brain MRI; no demyelinating features emerged in this patient after 24 months.

Several HLA class II antigens (DRB1*08, DRB1*10, and DQB1*04) were more frequent in patients with OMAS than in the general Spanish population (Table 2) (all p-adj<0.05). No differences were observed for HLA class I loci after adjustment for multiple comparisons compared with controls.

Comparison between paraneoplastic and idiopathic OMASs revealed no significant differences in any HLA loci (Table 2). However, when compared with the general population, idiopathic OMAS was associated with the presence of HLA-DRB1*08 or HLA-DQB1*04 antigens (5/13 [38%] vs 253/4,335 [6%] for HLA-DRB1*08, p-adj = 0.029; 5/13 [38%] vs 260/4,335 [6%] for HLA-DQB1*04, p-adj = 0.029, Table 2). By contrast, these haplotypes were not associated with paraneoplastic OMAS, which was instead associated with HLA-DRB1*10 (4/12 [33%] vs 121/4,335 [3%], p-adj = 0.029; Table 2).

DNA samples for TCR repertoire analysis were available from 7 patients with paraneoplastic OMAS and 5 with idiopathic OMAS. The median productive Simpson Clonality Index was 0.0139 (IQR 0.0126–0.0501) in the paraneoplastic OMAS group and 0.0275 (IQR 0.0149–0.0763) in the idiopathic group, with no significant differences (p = 0.431) (eFigure 1). Although both groups exhibited a broad range of clonality values, individual values in patients with paraneoplastic and idiopathic OMAS were in the range of clonality expansion observed in healthy individuals.25

To assess whether TCR sequences were shared within either the paraneoplastic or idiopathic OMAS groups, we searched for group-specific sequences present in at least half of the patients in one group but absent in the other. Six distinct CDR3β TCR sequences were identified in at least 4 of 7 patients with paraneoplastic OMAS (CASSLDSYEQYF, CASSRSYEQYF, CASSLGGYEQYF, CASSLGGNTEAFF, CASSLGETQYF, and CASRLNTEAFF) but were absent in all 5 patients with idiopathic OMAS, suggesting a convergent antigen-driven T-cell response in the paraneoplastic group (eTable 1). Only one CDR3β TCR sequence was detected in at least half of the patients (3 of 5) in the idiopathic OMAS group (CASSEGSNQPQHF), and it was absent in the paraneoplastic group (eTable 1).

GLIPH2 analysis revealed no shared motifs or CDR3β homology among the 6 TCR sequences from the 4 patients with paraneoplastic OMAS.

In this Spanish cohort of children with OMAS, we identified several noteworthy immunogenetic findings. First, HLA class II haplotypes (DRB1*08, DRB1*10, and DQB1*04) were more frequent in patients with OMAS than in the general population, supporting a predominant role for class II–mediated immune mechanisms in disease susceptibility. Second, while overall HLA distributions did not differ significantly between paraneoplastic and idiopathic OMASs, certain alleles appeared to confer group-specific risk, namely DRB1*08 and DQB1*04 in idiopathic OMAS and DRB1*10 in paraneoplastic OMAS. Third, patients with paraneoplastic OMAS exhibited greater TCR repertoire similarity, whereas shared sequences were infrequent in those with idiopathic OMAS.

Despite evidence of B-cell expansion in the CSF,7 associations with some antibodies,5,12 and genetic susceptibility,15 the mechanisms underlying OMAS remain poorly understood. Our findings using 2 independent yet complementary approaches (HLA association and TCR repertoire sharing) support T cell–mediated immune mechanisms in paraneoplastic OMAS. Given that HLA-DRB1*08, DRB1*10, and HLA-DQB1*04 antigens are relatively uncommon in the general Spanish population (≤6%), their significantly higher frequencies in the OMAS cohort suggest that these alleles may function as genetic risk factors of this disorder.

Several HLA class II alleles have been linked to autoimmune neurologic disorders driven by pathogenic autoantibodies.26-28 Our findings similarly suggest that humoral immune mechanisms targeting unidentified antigens may contribute to OMAS pathogenesis. The frequent response to rituximab further supports B-cell involvement, as in other forms of autoimmune encephalitis.6,8,9,29 The detection of MOG-IgG antibodies in an otherwise typical idiopathic OMAS case is unexpected and may represent an epiphenomenon, although overlap with other autoimmune disorders cannot be excluded, warranting long-term follow-up.

Our results differ from a preliminary multicenter European study that reported increased HLA-DRB1*01 frequencies in OMAS.30
By contrast, this allele was not enriched in our Spanish cohort, highlighting the genetic complexity of autoimmune diseases and the value of analyzing homogeneous populations. Together with the multiple HLA associations observed here, these findings suggest that OMAS, similar to anti-NMDAR encephalitis,31 exhibits more diverse HLA associations than IgG4-mediated disorders, such as anti-MuSK myasthenia gravis, anti-IgLON5 disease, or LGI1 encephalitis, in which consistent HLA class II associations have been reported across populations.26-28

No significant HLA differences were observed between idiopathic and paraneoplastic OMASs. However, compared with the general Spanish population, idiopathic OMAS was associated with HLA-DRB1*08 and DQB1*04, whereas paraneoplastic OMAS was linked to HLA-DRB1*10.

TCR repertoire analysis revealed immunologic differences between idiopathic and paraneoplastic OMASs. Shared TCR sequences were more frequent in the paraneoplastic group, indicating a more constrained immune repertoire. These findings argue against neuroblastoma regression in idiopathic OMAS and suggest 2 distinct pathophysiologic mechanisms. Nevertheless, occult neoplasia cannot be excluded individually, so comprehensive tumor screening, including repeat evaluation at 6 months, is recommended for all idiopathic patients, with further follow-up if clinical evolution is unfavorable.29

A limitation of our study is the small sample size. Strengths include a homogeneous Spanish cohort with a large control group, crucial for HLA and genetic analyses, and unbiased recruitment of idiopathic and paraneoplastic OMAS subgroups.

These findings reveal immunogenetic differences between paraneoplastic and idiopathic OMASs despite similar clinical presentations. Future studies with larger cohorts and advanced genetic approaches, such as whole-genome sequencing, are needed to clarify OMAS's genetic background. Further investigation of idiopathic vs paraneoplastic OMAS, combined with in vitro studies of TCR-peptide-HLA cross-reactivity with neuroblastoma proteins, may provide deeper insights into the disorder's pathogenesis.
