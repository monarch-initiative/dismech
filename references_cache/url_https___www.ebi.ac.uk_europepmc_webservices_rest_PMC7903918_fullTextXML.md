Here, we analyse mice carrying null alleles of <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic>, and of <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> together to investigate their roles in hearing. We found that <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">96</italic> heterozygous mice had normal hearing and homozygotes were completely deaf with abnormal hair cell stereocilia bundles and reduced numbers of inner hair cell synapses at 4 weeks of age. <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic> knockout mice developed normal hearing then exhibited progressive hearing loss. Our transcriptional analyses revealed significant changes in a range of other genes, but surprisingly there were fewer genes with altered expression in the organ of Corti of <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96</italic> null mice compared with our previous findings in <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mutants, which have a point mutation in the miR-96 seed region. This suggests that the more-severe phenotype of <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mutants compared with <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">96</italic> mutants, including progressive hearing loss in <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> heterozygotes, is likely to be mediated by the gain of novel target genes in addition to the loss of its normal targets. We propose three mechanisms of action of mutant miRNAs: loss of targets that are normally completely repressed, loss of targets for which transcription is normally buffered by the miRNA, and gain of novel targets. Any of these mechanisms could lead to a partial loss of a robust cellular identity and consequent dysfunction.</p></abstract><abstract abstract-type="precis"><p><bold>Summary:</bold> Our study BY</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec sec-type="intro" id="s1"><title>INTRODUCTION</title><p>The microRNAs (miRNAs) miR-96, miR-182 and miR-183 are expressed together on a single transcript in sensory cells, including the retina and the hair cells of the inner ear (<xref rid="DMM047225C108" ref-type="bibr">Weston et al., 2006</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C110" ref-type="bibr">Xu et al., 2007</xref>). Point mutations in <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> cause rapidly progressive hearing loss in the diminuendo mouse mutant [<italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> (<xref rid="DMM047225C56" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2009</xref>)] and progressive hearing loss with later onset in human families (<xref rid="DMM047225C69" ref-type="bibr">Mencia et al., 2009</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C93" ref-type="bibr">Solda et al., 2012</xref>), and the diminuendo mutation has also been shown to delay maturation of the central auditory system (<xref rid="DMM047225C89" ref-type="bibr">Schluter et al., 2018</xref>). In homozygous <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mice, most of the cochlear hair cells die by 28 days after birth. However, this is not the cause of the hearing loss; even before the onset of normal hearing, homozygote hair cells fail to mature both morphologically and physiologically, remaining in their immature state, and heterozygote hair cells show a developmental delay. miR-96 is thus thought to be responsible for coordinating hair cell maturation (<xref rid="DMM047225C13" ref-type="bibr">Chen et al., 2014</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C48" ref-type="bibr">Kuhn et al., 2011</xref>). Overexpression of the three miRNAs also results in cochlear defects and hearing loss (<xref rid="DMM047225C109" ref-type="bibr">Weston et al., 2018</xref>). The complete loss of all mature miRNAs from the inner ear results in early developmental defects including a severely truncated cochlear duct (<xref rid="DMM047225C29" ref-type="bibr">Friedman et al., 2009</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C94" ref-type="bibr">Soukup et al., 2009</xref>). miR-96, miR-182 and miR-183 have also been implicated in other diseases, including glaucoma (<xref rid="DMM047225C65" ref-type="bibr">Liu et al., 2016</xref>), ischemic injury (<xref rid="DMM047225C17" ref-type="bibr">Cui and Yang, 2013</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C22" ref-type="bibr">Duan et al., 2019</xref>) and spinal cord injury (<xref rid="DMM047225C63" ref-type="bibr">Ling et al., 2017</xref>).</p><p>MicroRNAs regulate the expression of many other genes by targeting specific sequences in their mRNAs, leading to transcript destabilisation or translational inhibition. Transcriptome analyses of the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> organ of Corti showed that many genes were misregulated in homozygotes, including several known to be important for hearing that appear to contribute to specific aspects of the diminuendo phenotype (<xref rid="DMM047225C13" ref-type="bibr">Chen et al., 2014</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C48" ref-type="bibr">Kuhn et al., 2011</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C57" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2016</xref>, <xref rid="DMM047225C56" ref-type="bibr">2009</xref>). However, the diminuendo mutation is a single base pair change in the seed region of the miRNA that is critical for correct targeting, and it is not clear to what extent the diminuendo mutant phenotype is the result of the loss of normal targets of miR-96, and how much is due to the gain of novel targets. We previously suggested that the progressive hearing loss was most likely caused by the loss of normal target repression because all three point mutations in mouse and human <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> lead to a similar phenotype, which seems unlikely if the gain of novel targets is the main mechanism involved.</p><p>The regulatory network generated from <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> expression data (<xref rid="DMM047225C57" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2016</xref>) includes a number of genes known to be involved in deafness – such as <italic toggle="yes">Ptprq</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Gfi1</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Kcna10</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Slc26a5</italic> – as well as new candidate genes. Manipulating this network could be a useful therapeutic approach to treating hearing loss due to hair cell dysfunction triggered by a broad range of factors, including genetic variants and environmental insults. For example, <italic toggle="yes">Trp53</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Hif1a</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Nfe2l2</italic> are in the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> network and are involved in cellular responses to stress (<xref rid="DMM047225C91" ref-type="bibr">Simmons et al., 2009</xref>). In order to focus our translational efforts, it is important to understand better the molecular basis of the network. For this reason, we have analysed a second mutation of <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> in this study, a double knockout of <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic>, as well as a knockout of the closely linked <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic> gene, generated through a mouse miRNA knockout programme (<xref rid="DMM047225C83" ref-type="bibr">Prosser et al., 2011</xref>). Although both new mouse mutants exhibit hearing loss, their phenotypes differ from the diminuendo mouse, with no sign of hearing loss in the heterozygotes, suggesting that the more-severe phenotype of <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mutants is likely to be mediated by the gain of novel target genes in addition to the loss of its normal targets.</p></sec><sec sec-type="results" id="s2"><title>RESULTS</title><sec id="s2a"><title><italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">96</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic> knockout mice</title><p>Two mouse lines were used in this study; a knockout of <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>tm1Hmpr/Wtsi</sup></italic>, hereafter referred to as <italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>ko</sup></italic>) and a double knockout of both <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">Mirc40<sup>tm1Hmpr/WtsiOulu</sup></italic>, hereafter referred to as <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic>), which are only 116 bp apart, making it technically challenging to generate two separate knockouts. The mice were generated and maintained on the C57BL/6N genetic background. C57BL/6 mice are known to have age-related hearing loss, partly due to the <italic toggle="yes">Cdh23<sup>ahl</sup></italic> allele (<xref rid="DMM047225C76" ref-type="bibr">Noben-Trauth et al., 2003</xref>). Higher frequencies are affected first, after 4 weeks of age, whereas the lower frequencies remain unaffected up to 6 months of age (<xref rid="DMM047225C58" ref-type="bibr">Li and Borg, 1991</xref>). We observed a similar pattern in wild-type mice from both the <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">96<sup>dko</sup></italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>ko</sup></italic> lines, which exhibited mild progressive hearing loss at 24-42 kHz from 8 weeks of age but retained good hearing sensitivity at frequencies between 3-12 kHz up to 6 months of age (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="DMM047225F1">Fig. 1</xref>).

In <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> homozygotes, there was no detectable expression of <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic> or <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic>. Likewise, in <italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>ko</sup></italic> homozygotes, there was no detectable expression of <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic> (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Fig. S1</uri>). The levels of expression in heterozygotes of both knockouts was variable, as was the expression of <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic> in <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> homozygotes, and <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> in <italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>ko</sup></italic> homozygotes. It is likely that this is because we lack a proper miRNA control for sensory tissue in the inner ear. We used <italic toggle="yes">Mir99a</italic>, which is expressed in almost all cell types in the cochlea, including hair and supporting cells (<xref rid="DMM047225C29" ref-type="bibr">Friedman et al., 2009</xref>), but because <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> are also expressed in hair cells, it is possible that the mutant alleles affect the expression of <italic toggle="yes">Mir99a</italic> in hair cells, making it an unreliable calibrator between wild type, heterozygote and homozygote. A better calibrator would be an miRNA expressed only in supporting cells and not in hair cells.</p></sec><sec id="s2c"><title>Impaired auditory responses in homozygotes but normal thresholds in heterozygotes</title><p><italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> homozygous mice were profoundly deaf, with most showing no response at the highest sound level tested [95 dB sound pressure level (SPL)] at any of the ages tested (14 days to 6 months old). By contrast, <italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>ko</sup></italic> homozygotes exhibited only a mild hearing loss starting at higher frequencies and progressing with age to lower frequencies (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="DMM047225F1">Fig. 1</xref>). We used a Pearson correlation to cluster genes across all 24 samples (<italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>ko</sup></italic>) and obtained 29 consensus modules (including the reserved ‘grey’ module, which consists of genes outside all the other modules) (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Table S4</uri>).</p><p>These consensus modules are groups of genes with highly correlated expression profiles. One module was highly correlated and clustered with the wild type versus <italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>ko</sup></italic> homozygote trait (salmon, <uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Fig. S16B</uri>).</p><p>We chose nine modules for further exploration (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Fig. S15</uri>, <uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Table S4</uri>). This approach highlighted multiple transcription factors that could be involved; however, it did not offer any links to miR-96 or the significantly misregulated genes.</p></sec><sec id="s2l"><title>Network analysis: an automated approach using publicly available regulatory data</title><p>In our previous study on genes misregulated in <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic>, we used regulatory interactions described in the literature to create an internally consistent network of regulatory interactions connecting miR-96 to as many of the misregulated genes as possible (<xref rid="DMM047225C57" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2016</xref>). Two misregulated genes were left out completely due to having no known upstream regulators in our compiled regulatory interactions (<italic toggle="yes">Ccer2</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Rn7s1</italic>) (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="DMM047225F6">Fig. 6</xref>A). We initially concluded that this was more likely to be due to haploinsufficiency than to the gain of novel targets, because humans heterozygous for different point mutations also had progressive hearing loss (<xref rid="DMM047225C69" ref-type="bibr">Mencia et al., 2009</xref>). However, heterozygous <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> mice have ABR thresholds and DPOAE responses resembling those of the wild type, and there is no difference in thresholds or wave 1 amplitudes between heterozygotes and wild types after noise exposure. Two recent studies of different knockout alleles targeting the entire miRNA cluster (miR-183/96/182) found that heterozygotes also exhibited normal hearing (<xref rid="DMM047225C28" ref-type="bibr">Fan et al., 2017</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C30" ref-type="bibr">Geng et al., 2018</xref>). It is possible that the more-severe phenotype seen in <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> heterozygotes is due to the acquisition of new targets by the mutant miRNA, or it could be an effect of the different background, as the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> allele was generated by N-ethyl-N-nitrosourea (ENU) mutagenesis on a C3HeB/FeJ background, in contrast to the C57BL/6N background of the <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> allele or the reported knockouts of the entire cluster, which were created in 129S2 (<xref rid="DMM047225C68" ref-type="bibr">Lumayag et al., 2013</xref>) or 129SV6 (<xref rid="DMM047225C28" ref-type="bibr">Fan et al., 2017</xref>) embryonic stem (ES) cells and then crossed onto the C57BL/6J background (<xref rid="DMM047225C28" ref-type="bibr">Fan et al., 2017</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C68" ref-type="bibr">Lumayag et al., 2013</xref>). Mice homozygous for the <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> allele showed no ABRs at all ages tested from 14 days onwards, and in this they resemble the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> homozygotes, in which the compound action potentials recorded from the round window of the cochlea were undetectable at 4 weeks of age (<xref rid="DMM047225C56" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2009</xref>).</p><p><italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> homozygotes circle by 3 weeks of age, whereas a milder phenotype of variable hyperactivity was observed in some <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> homozygotes, the prevalence increasing with age (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Fig. S5</uri>). Circling is a more-severe manifestation of vestibular dysfunction than hyperactivity. This could again be due to the C3HeB/FeJ background, because <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mice carry the <italic toggle="yes">Pde6b<sup>rd1</sup></italic> mutation causing retinal degeneration (<xref rid="DMM047225C80" ref-type="bibr">Pittler and Baehr, 1991</xref>) and are blind by adulthood. The <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> allele was generated on a C57BL/6N background, which lacks the <italic toggle="yes">Pde6b<sup>rd1</sup></italic> mutation but has the <italic toggle="yes">Crb1rd<sup>8</sup></italic> mutation, which leads to variably penetrant retinal dysplasia (<xref rid="DMM047225C71" ref-type="bibr">Moore et al., 2018</xref>). Other ocular abnormalities have also been observed in C57BL/6N mice, such as lens abnormalities and vitreous crystalline deposits (<xref rid="DMM047225C71" ref-type="bibr">Moore et al., 2018</xref>). However, no ocular abnormalities have been reported for these mice in the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium (IMPC) pipeline (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.mousephenotype.org/data/genes/MGI:3619440">https://www.mousephenotype.org/data/genes/MGI:3619440</uri>), so it is likely that they have sufficient vision to partially compensate for the lack of vestibular sensory input, reducing the severity of the observed phenotype. It is notable that mice lacking the entire miR-183/96/182 cluster (on a mixed background of C57BL/6J and either 129S2 or 129SV6) exhibit both persistent circling behaviour and retinal defects (<xref rid="DMM047225C28" ref-type="bibr">Fan et al., 2017</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C68" ref-type="bibr">Lumayag et al., 2013</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="s3b"><title>Stereocilia bundles and innervation in <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic>/<italic toggle="yes">96<sup>dko</sup></italic> mice and <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mice</title><p>The hair cells of <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> homozygous mice are present at 4 days old but appear abnormal, and by 28 days old have degenerated almost completely (<xref rid="DMM047225C56" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2009</xref>). In <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> homozygotes, however, some hair cell stereocilia bundles are still visible at P28, although they are severely disorganised (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="DMM047225F3">Fig. 3</xref>A). Some of the differences will be due to certain genes not being represented on the microarray assay when it was carried out, such as <italic toggle="yes">Ccer2</italic>, and some may be the result of the different genetic background. We aimed to minimise this by use of the sex-matched wild-type littermates as controls, but it is not possible to entirely eliminate the effect of genetic background; there are 83 genes that lack miR-96 seed region matches in one background compared with the other (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Table S1</uri>), any of which could be contributing to the phenotype. We used the gEAR dataset comparison tool (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://umgear.org/compare_datasets.html">https://umgear.org/compare_datasets.html</uri>, accessed July 2020) and data from mouse hair cells compared with the rest of the cochlear duct at P0 (<xref rid="DMM047225C11" ref-type="bibr">Cai et al., 2015</xref>) to identify which of the 21 direct targets were excluded from hair cells but present in the rest of the cochlear duct. We found eight genes that showed this pattern of expression: <italic toggle="yes">Snai2</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Zeb1</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Irs1</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Nr3c1</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Foxo1</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Alk</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Eln</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Rad51</italic>. The precise role of Irs1 in the function of the cochlea has not yet been elucidated, but because Zeb1 and Snai2 are known to be required for the development of non-sensory cells in the cochlear duct, it may be that miR-96 plays a role in establishing and/or maintaining the repression of non-hair cell genes in developing hair cells.</p><p><italic toggle="yes">Nr3c1</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Foxo1</italic> have the most downstream links of the 21 direct targets, predicted to regulate nine and 12 genes, respectively<italic toggle="yes">. From the microarray carried out on <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> P4 organ of Corti, we found 19 genes that were significantly downregulated in the mutant and that bore matches to the mutant seed region in their 3′UTR (<xref rid="DMM047225C56" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2009</xref>). Our approach should be able to detect both effects, but if a target is highly expressed in the non-sensory epithelial cells, the difference in expression between wild type and homozygote as a result of derepression in the hair cells might not be detectable. Our current transcriptome data, therefore, are more likely to highlight targets that are being buffered rather than targets that are completely repressed by miR-96 (<xref rid="DMM047225C38" ref-type="bibr">Hertzano et al., 2011</xref>). This may explain why likely target genes, such as <italic toggle="yes">Zeb1</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Foxo1</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Nr3c1</italic>, have not been found to be significantly misregulated in our transcriptome data. It may also explain the variability of the network genes we tested (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Fig. S11</uri>E,F) – in the absence of the miRNA buffering, transcriptional noise has increased. This effect would be exacerbated by a mutated seed region, such as in the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mutant; not only would the normal buffering effect be gone, but multiple other genes would be misregulated within the hair cell, further disrupting normal cell function, as indeed we observed in the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> transcriptome analyses (<xref rid="DMM047225C57" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2016</xref>, <xref rid="DMM047225C56" ref-type="bibr">2009</xref>).</p><p>We suggest that the consistent misregulation of <italic toggle="yes">Ocm</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Slc26a5</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Myo3a</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Sema3e</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Slc52a3</italic> observed in both the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> (<xref rid="DMM047225C57" ref-type="bibr">Lewis et al., 2016</xref>, <xref rid="DMM047225C56" ref-type="bibr">2009</xref>) and <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> homozygotes is the result of the lack of repression of genes that would usually not be expressed in hair cells at all; for example, <italic toggle="yes">Zeb1</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Foxo1</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Nr3c1</italic>. In the wild-type hair cell, miR-96 acts to reinforce hair cell fate by maintaining and/or reinforcing repression of these non-hair cell genes. The links between the direct targets of miR-96 and the consistently misregulated downstream genes have yet to be discovered, but we suggest that <italic toggle="yes">Ikzf2</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Fos</italic> are likely to be involved (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="DMM047225F7">Fig. 7</xref>A,B).

This has important implications for understanding the effect of mutant miRNAs in the human population. So far, all three reported human mutations in miR-96 have been point mutations, two in the seed region and one in the stem region of the pre-miRNA (<xref rid="DMM047225C69" ref-type="bibr">Mencia et al., 2009</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C93" ref-type="bibr">Solda et al., 2012</xref>), and although all the individuals carrying those point mutations have exhibited some degree of progressive hearing loss, the phenotypes differ between people, as do the phenotypes of the mice carrying the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mutation and the <italic toggle="yes">Mir183/96<sup>dko</sup></italic> allele. Determining the misregulated pathways common to all <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> mutations will be important not only for furthering our understanding of the role of this miRNA in hair cell development but also for developing therapeutic interventions, not only for people with <italic toggle="yes">MIR96</italic> mutations but also more generally for hearing loss associated with hair cell defects.</p></sec></sec><sec sec-type="methods" id="s4"><title>MATERIALS AND METHODS</title><sec id="s4a"><title>Ethics approval</title><p>Mouse studies were carried out in accordance with UK Home Office regulations and the UK Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act of 1986 (ASPA) under UK Home Office licences, and the study was approved by the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute and the King's College London Ethical Review Committees. Similarly, genes upregulated in <italic toggle="yes">Mir182<sup>ko</sup></italic> homozygotes with heptamers complementary to the miR-182 seed region in their 3′UTR were included as targets of miR-182 (<xref rid="DMM047225TB1" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>B). This resulted in a list of 97,062 unique links of the form &lt;gene A&gt; &lt;interaction&gt; &lt;gene B&gt;.</p><p>All potential links between the ultimate regulators (<italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic> alone, or <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic>) and the misregulated genes were then identified, and the direction of misregulation of intermediate genes predicted. Starting with the known misregulated genes, each upstream regulator was given a score based on the direction of regulation of the known misregulated genes (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Fig. S19</uri>). This process iterated until the gene(s) at the top of the cascade (in this case, <italic toggle="yes">Mir96</italic>, <italic toggle="yes">Mir183</italic> or <italic toggle="yes">Mir182</italic>) were reached. Consistent links were kept, the shortest paths between miRNA and misregulated genes identified, and the final network was written out in the simple interaction format (sif) and viewed using Cytoscape (<xref rid="DMM047225C90" ref-type="bibr">Shannon et al., 2003</xref>) (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Fig. S18</uri>). Further network analyses were carried out using the Cytoscape Network Analyser tool to calculate the degree and betweenness centrality of each node (gene). A node's degree is the number of edges connecting it to other nodes (in this case, interactions between genes and gene products), and its betweenness centrality measures how important it is for connecting distant parts of the network.</p><p>In order to test the PoPCoRN tool, we searched for studies that measured the misregulation of a set of genes in a system in which a specific upstream regulator was either induced or silenced, and that also identified and confirmed the misregulation of an intermediate gene (where necessary, the data for these intermediate genes were removed from the input prior to network creation). We found ten suitable studies from which we were able to create networks and test the predicted misregulation of 14 intermediate genes. The tool made correct predictions for seven of the genes and incorrect predictions for three of them (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Table S10</uri>). For the remaining four, it did not predict either up- or downregulation, which points to a deficiency of underlying data (<uri xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://dmm.biologists.org/lookup/doi/10.1242/dmm.047225.supplemental">Table S10</uri>). This will always be a problem for a tool based on existing data that does not attempt extrapolation.</p><p>Many similar approaches to network creation have been described previously (for example, <xref rid="DMM047225C15" ref-type="bibr">Chindelevitch et al., 2012</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C27" ref-type="bibr">Fakhry et al., 2016</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C46" ref-type="bibr">Krämer et al., 2014</xref>; <xref rid="DMM047225C82" ref-type="bibr">Pollard et al., 2005</xref>), but they address the case in which the upstream regulator or regulators remain unknown; for example, when comparing samples from healthy and diseased tissues, and one of their main aims is to identify potential upstream regulators. Our implementation asks a different question, exploring the downstream regulatory cascade of a known regulator. In the case of miR-96, and potentially many other regulators involved in disease, the genes involved in mediating their effect are candidate therapeutic targets.</p></sec><sec id="s4q"><title>Strain differences in seed region matches</title><p>The C57BL/6NJ genomic sequence is available from the Mouse Genomes Project (<xref rid="DMM047225C1" ref-type="bibr">Adams et al., 2015</xref>) via the Ensembl browser (<xref rid="DMM047225C111" ref-type="bibr">Zerbino et al., 2018</xref>), but the <italic toggle="yes">Mir96<sup>Dmdo</sup></italic> mice were made and maintained on the C3HeB/FeJ background, which is not one of the sequenced strains.
