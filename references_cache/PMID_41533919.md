# PRPF8 Mutation-Induced Defects in Human iPSC-Derived RPE Are Rescued by Adenine Base Editing.
**Authors:** Sun X, Liang Y, Liang Y, Li W, Chen H, Ding C, Duan C, Zhou Y, Zhang R, Xu H, Chen J
**Journal:** Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1167/iovs.67.1.21](https://doi.org/10.1167/iovs.67.1.21)

## Content

PURPOSE: The pathological effects of pre-mRNA processing factor 8 (PRPF8) mutations on the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) are not fully understood. We aimed to identify disease-specific cellular and molecular phenotypes in PRPF8 retinitis pigmentosa (RP) patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-RPE and to test whether adenine base editing (ABE), which corrects the PRPF8 mutation in iPSCs, can reverse abnormal RPE phenotypes.

METHODS: We obtained patient-derived iPSCs with the heterozygous PRPF8 (c.5792C>T) mutation and created an induced mutation iPSC line by introducing the same mutation into wild-type iPSCs using CRISPR/Cas9. These cells were differentiated into RPE cells. We measured PRPF8 expression, barrier integrity, and apicobasal polarity. Electron microscopy examined apical microvilli and pigment granules. RNA sequencing quantified splicing events and affected pathways. ABE corrected the PRPF8 mutation in patient iPSCs, and the corrected clones were re-differentiated into RPE cells for evaluation.

RESULTS: PRPF8-mutant RPE cells exhibited decreased PRPF8 mRNA and protein levels, weakened barrier function, and disrupted cell polarity. Ultrastructural analysis showed loss of apical microvilli and pigment granules. Transcriptomic analysis identified abnormal splicing events, with enrichment in cilium assembly and melanosome pathways. ABE correction restored PRPF8 expression, normalized barrier integrity, apicobasal polarity, and rescued the defects in apical microvilli and pigment granules.

CONCLUSIONS: PRPF8 mutations in patient-derived iPSC RPE cause functional and ultrastructural defects driven by splicing abnormalities. ABE correction of the PRPF8 mutation in iPSCs can restore PRPF8 expression and alleviate cellular and molecular defects in RPE and highlights the therapeutic potential of precise gene editing correction strategies for RP.

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of inherited retinal dystrophies characterized by the progressive degeneration of photoreceptors, leading to night blindness, visual field constriction, and eventual vision loss.1 RP affects approximately 1 in 3000 to 4000 individuals worldwide, with over 80 genes and loci identified to date; among these, 23 genes are associated with autosomal-dominant RP (adRP).2 Mutations in pre-mRNA processing factors (PRPFs), including PRPF3, PRPF4, PRPF6, PRPF8, and PRPF31, represent the second most common cause of adRP after mutations in rhodopsin.3,4

PRPF8, located on chromosome 17p13.33, encodes a key component of the spliceosome, a large ribonucleoprotein complex responsible for removing introns and ligating exons to generate mature mRNA.5,6 Although PRPF8 is ubiquitously expressed, its mutations predominantly cause retinal degeneration, highlighting the essential role of the spliceosome in maintaining retinal homeostasis and function.4 Moreover, dysregulation of PRPF8 may be implicated in tumorigenesis.7,8 Previous studies have shown that PRPF8 mutations impair spliceosome function. Prpf8 knock-in mouse models (Prpf8-H2309P) have only subtle RPE abnormalities (vacuoles in the RPE cells) of the Prpf8 knock-in mice by ultrastructural analyses.9 In another study, homozygous Prpf8 knock-in mice (p.Tyr2334Asn and p.Glu2331ValfsX15) revealed circular RNA (circRNA) deregulation in the cerebellum, but no abnormalities were found in the retina.10 This indicates that animal models have inherent limitations, highlighting the need to adopt alternative systems that more closely recapitulate human disease.11

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) share growth and differentiation abilities with human embryonic stem cells, making them useful for disease modeling, drug screening, and testing therapeutic strategies.12 Maruotti13 reported an automated platform that produces pure RPE cells without three-dimensional culture or manual dissection of pigmented clusters; this system enables efficient in vitro RPE modeling, crucial for a retinal stability environment. The main functions of RPE cells include the daily phagocytosis of shed photoreceptor outer segment discs, transepithelial transport of nutrients and ions, polarized secretion of growth factors, and regulation of the visual cycle.14,15 Arzalluz-Luque et al.16 found that the heterozygous mutation in PRPF8 (c.6974–6994del) disrupts the spliceosomal complex and ribosomal functions by generating patient-specific RPE cells carrying the PRPF8 mutation. However, this study did not further investigate the resulting pathological features or functional impairments of the RPE cells.

Considering the close correlation of RPE cell pathology in RP, especially PRPF RP diseases, we first used an established iPSC line from a RP patient with a heterozygous PRPF8 (c.5792C>T) mutation17 as the patient group and induced mutation in the iPSC line by introducing the same PRPF8 (c.5792C>T) heterozygous mutation into wild-type iPSCs via CRISPR/Cas9 technology as the PRPF8-induced mutation (IM) group.18 The iPSCs were differentiated into RPE cells, which were then analyzed morphologically, molecularly, and functionally using immunofluorescence, electron microscopy, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR), and transcriptome sequencing. Finally, using adenine base editing (ABE), we rescued the PRPF8 mutation (c.5792C>T) in patient-derived iPSCs (patient-ABE group) and observed repair in abnormal RPE cells. The study shows that iPSC-derived RPE cells reflect RP patient phenotypes and underscores the potential of precise gene editing.

The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changsha Aier Eye Hospital (KYPJ005). One patient-driven iPSC line was used in this study, and two genetically modified iPSC lines were used. Additionally, one urine-derived iPSC line was used as the control, obtained from Cellapy Biotechnology (Beijing, China). No animal experiments were involved in this study.

The iPSCs were cultured in six-well plates coated with Matrigel (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) and maintained in mTeSR Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) at 37°C in a 5% CO2. When the cells reached 75% to 85% confluence, they were dissociated with 0.5-mM EDTA (Cellapy Biotechnology) and passaged at a 1:6 ratio every 4 to 6 days.

RPE cells were derived from iPSCs following a previously described protocol (RP reference). In brief, iPSCs were cultured in the mTeSR Plus medium until approximately 80% confluence, then transitioned to RPE differentiation medium. The differentiation medium, used from day 0 to day 7, consisted of Gibco Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 20% knockout serum replacement (KSR; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× non-essential amino acids (NEAAs; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 50-µM Gibco β-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% Gibco penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10-mM nicotinamide (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). From day 8 to day 14, nicotinamide was gradually replaced with 100-ng/mL Activin A (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA), during which pigmentation became visible. On day 14, Activin A was removed, and 3-µM CHIR99021 (MedChemExpress) was added. On day 42, RPE cells were harvested by incubating them with Gibco TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes at 37°C, then seeded onto Matrigel-coated six-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. Meanwhile, the differentiation medium was replaced with RPE maintenance medium, consisting of DMEM, 4% KSR, 1× NEAAs, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 50-µM β-mercaptoethanol. At this point, cells were designated as passage 1 (P1). RPE cells at passage 2 (P2) were used for subsequent experiments.

RPE cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS. Cells were subsequently permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 minutes and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies diluted in 3% BSA were applied and incubated overnight at 4°C. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies (diluted at 1:1000 in PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1 µg/mL; Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 5 minutes. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (ZEISS LSM 880; Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany), and image processing was performed using ZEN 3.2 software. All antibodies used are listed in Table 1.

Total RNA was extracted from RPE cells using the Steady Pure Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer's protocols. Reverse transcription was performed with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to synthesize cDNA. The qPCR was conducted using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a LightCycler 96 PCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Each reaction had a final volume of 10 µL and was run for 45 cycles. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated with the 2−∆∆Ct method. Primer pairs were synthesized by TsingKe Biotech (Beijing, China), and all sequences are listed in Table 2. Each experiment included at least three biological replicates and three technical replicates per group.

Cells were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 20 minutes on ice. The lysates were centrifuged, and the total protein concentration in each sample was measured using the BCA Protein Quantification Kit (Solarbio), following the manufacturer's instructions. An equal amount of protein (20 µg per lane) was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies listed in Table 1. GAPDH or β-actin served as the loading control. The next day, membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IRDye 680RD or goat anti-mouse IRDye 800CW secondary antibodies (1:10,000; LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Protein bands were visualized using the Odyssey Fc Imager (LI-COR Biosciences), and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RPE cell shape regularity was quantified using the form factor (FF) as described by Bhatia et al.19 Zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1)-labeled RPE monolayers were imaged, and individual cell borders were segmented in ImageJ. Cell area and perimeter were obtained using the “Analyze Particles” function, and the FF was calculated for each cell using the formula FF = 4π × (area)/(perimeter2). An FF value of 1.0 represents a perfect circle, whereas a regular hexagonal RPE cell typically has an FF of approximately 0.8; lower FF values indicate increasingly irregular or elongated cell shapes.

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured to assess the integrity of tight junctions in RPE monolayers, as previously described. RPE cells were seeded onto 24-well Transwell inserts at a density of 1 × 103 cells per insert. TEER measurements were taken every 2 days using a Millicell ERS-2 Voltohmmeter (MilliporeSigma, Billerica, MA, USA) until values stabilized, indicating mature barrier formation. TEER values (Ω·cm²) were calculated using the following formula: TEER = (R_total – R_insert)/A, where R_total is the resistance of the cell-covered insert, R_insert is the resistance of a blank insert, and A is the surface area of the membrane.

Briefly, RPE cells were seeded onto glass slides placed in a 24-well plate. After 2 weeks, the slides were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Solarbio) for 2 hours at room temperature, followed by three gentle PBS washes. Post-fixation involved treating the samples with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4; Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA) for 1 hour at room temperature and then rinsing three times with PBS. The samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and twice with 100%), then treated with isoamyl acetate (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 minutes at each step. Finally, the samples were critical-point dried, coated with a conductive metal, and imaged using a scanning electron microscope (SU8100; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

RPE cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1-M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4), scraped from culture plates, pelleted by centrifugation, and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. Samples were stained with uranyl acetate, dehydrated through graded ethanol, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (60–90 nm) were cut, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined with a transmission electron microscope (HT7700; Hitachi).

RPE culture media were collected from both the upper and lower chambers of the Transwell plate. The secretion levels of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF) proteins were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, culture media were collected and centrifuged at 1000g for 10 minutes to remove debris. Samples and standards were added to the wells of a 96-well plate precoated with capture antibodies. After washing with the provided wash buffer, detection antibodies were added and incubated for the recommended time. The addition of enzyme-conjugated secondary antibodies and substrate solution followed this. The reaction was stopped using the stop solution provided in the kit. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy HTX; BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The concentration of each sample was calculated based on the standard curve generated from known concentrations.

Photoreceptor outer segments (POSs) were isolated from fresh porcine eyes and labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC; MedChemExpress) as previously described.20 Briefly, POSs were resuspended in 0.1-M sodium bicarbonate and incubated with FITC in DMEM for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. After labeling, POSs were washed three times with PBS and resuspended in DMEM. Confluent RPE monolayers were incubated with FITC-labeled POSs for 5 hours at 37°C. The medium was removed, and cells were washed four times with PBS to remove unbound POS. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, blocked, and then incubated overnight at 4°C with anti–ZO-1 antibody. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 minutes. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope, and the number of internalized FITC-labeled POSs per cell was quantified. For quantification, images from three independent experiments per condition were analyzed with ImageJ, and comparisons used Student's t-test. Data are shown as mean ± SD; P < 0.05 was significant.

Total RNA was extracted using Invitrogen TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) from the control and PRPF8 RPE cells. Each group included three biological replicates. RNA library construction and sequencing were performed by BGI Biotech (Wuhan, China). Raw sequencing reads were filtered with SOAPnuke 1.5.2 to remove low-quality reads. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 and aligned to the reference coding gene set with Bowtie2-2.2.5. Gene expression levels were measured with RSEM 1.2.12 and normalized as reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with the criteria of an adjusted P < 0.05 and fold change > 1. Heatmaps were created using the R package pheatmap 1.0.8 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using a hypergeometric test, with significance determined as adjusted P < 0.05.

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) and SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests, whereas multiple group comparisons were evaluated by one-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We identified a male patient presenting with typical clinical features of RP. Fundus photography revealed a mildly pale optic disc with bone-spicule pigmentation in the posterior pole and equatorial regions (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Optical coherence tomography showed RP-related changes, including cystoid macular edema (Supplementary Fig. S1A, white arrows) and ellipsoid zone disruption. (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Genetic analysis confirmed a heterozygous PRPF8 mutation (c.5792C>T), resulting in a threonine (Thr) to methionine (Met) substitution. Urine-derived cells from this RP patient were reprogrammed using our established protocol to generate iPSC lines (CSUASOi006-A), as described in our previous study,17 as a patient group (Fig. 1A). The qPCR and western blot analysis showed that PRPF8 mRNA and protein levels were significantly lower in patient iPSCs compared to control iPSCs (mRNA P < 0.001; protein P < 0.01) (Figs. 1C, 1E). Sanger sequencing of cDNA from patient iPSCs showed that this single-base change affects only the last codon of exon 36 without changing the downstream mRNA sequence (Supplementary Fig. S1D). To eliminate confounding background genetic variation, we generated an induced mutation iPSC line by introducing the same PRPF8 patient heterozygous mutation into control iPSCs using CRISPR/Cas9, as in our previous study.18 This cell line (PRPF8-IM) also shows significant downregulation of PRPF8 expression at both the mRNA (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1D) and protein (P < 0.05) levels (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Fig. S1C). The male wild-type iPSCs used as the control group were obtained from Cellapy Biotechnology. Together, these data confirm that the heterozygous PRPF8 c.5792C>T mutation causes RP. Both iPSC lines (patient, PRPF8-IM) exhibited significant PRPF8 downregulation at the mRNA and protein levels, providing an in vitro platform to study its pathology.

All of the iPSCs were differentiated into RPE cells following a previously reported protocol.21 Control, patient, and PRPF8-IM iPSC lines were cultured for the same duration. Under brightfield microscopy, control-RPE showed the characteristic pigmented, polygonal “cobblestone” morphology at P2. In contrast, pigmentation was decreased, and cell morphology appeared irregular in both the patient and PRPF8-IM groups (Fig. 2A). Immunofluorescence staining for the tight-junction protein ZO-1 further confirmed that the patient and PRPF8-IM RPE cells had disorganized cell borders and irregular shapes. Conversely, ZO-1 staining in the control group showed a typical polygonal morphology and well-organized tight junctions (Fig. 2B). Additionally, immunofluorescence showed that RPE-specific markers microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) and RPE65 were expressed in all groups (Fig. 2B). To further evaluate whether the PRPF8 mutation affected RPE cell size, we quantified the surface area of individual ZO-1–labeled RPE cells using ImageJ. The mean cell area was 193.4 ± 104.6 µm2 in the control group (n = 46), 184.0 ± 122.4 µm2 in the PRPF8-IM group (n = 50), and 186.4 ± 81.56 µm2 in the patient group (n = 47) (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The FF was calculated as 4π × area/perimeter2 from ZO-1–labeled RPE borders using ImageJ. The FF in control-RPE cells (0.817 ± 0.074) was significantly higher than in the patient (0.674 ± 0.174; P < 0.01) and PRPF8-IM (0.682 ± 0.119; P < 0.01) RPE cells, indicating that the control group more closely resembled the hexagonal structure of healthy RPE (Fig. 2C). Meanwhile, western blot analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in PRPF8 protein expression in both patient and PRPF8-IM RPE cells (P < 0.001) (Figs. 2D, 2E). To determine whether the PRPF8 mutation affects the expression of other spliceosomal components, we also quantified PRPF31 mRNA levels in all RPE lines. PRPF31 expression showed no significant difference among the control, PRPF8-IM, and patient groups (Supplementary Figs. S2B, S2C).

These findings demonstrate that the heterozygous PRPF8 c.5792C>T mutation (in both the patient and PRPF8-IM groups) leads to RPE abnormalities resembling the pathological features of RP. Compared with the control group, the PRPF8 mutation caused a marked reduction in PRPF8 protein expression, accompanied by irregular cellular morphology and disrupted tight junction integrity.

We assessed RPE barrier function by measuring TEER. RPE cells seeded on 24-well Transwell inserts formed confluent monolayers after 2 weeks. Control RPE exhibited TEER of 332.8 ± 26.5 Ω·cm2. In contrast, patient and PRPF8-IM RPE registered 185.4 ± 21.3 Ω·cm2 and 172.7 ± 26.4 Ω·cm2 (both n = 3, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A), To further assess the polarized RPE monolayer, the secretions of PEDF from the apical side and VEGF from the basal side were quantified. RPE cells were cultured on 24-well Transwell inserts for 2 weeks. Media samples were collected from the upper and lower chambers of all three groups. ELISA analysis revealed that the secretion levels of PDGF and VEGF proteins in the control-RPE group were lower compared to those in the patient and PRPF8-IM RPE groups (PDGF, P < 0.05; VEGF, P < 0.01) (Figs. 3B, 3C). Scanning electron microscopy demonstrated that control RPE exhibited abundant apical microvilli and robust intercellular contacts. In contrast, low-magnification images of patient and PRPF8-IM RPE showed irregular cellular morphology and enlarged junctional gaps, and high-magnification images revealed a paucity of microvilli (Fig. 3D). Collectively, PRPF8 mutations cause RPE cells to have significantly reduced barrier function, along with disrupted epithelial polarity, abnormally increased secretion of PDGF and VEGF, and a loss of apical microvilli as shown by ultrastructural analysis.

To assess how the PRPF8 mutation affects RPE function, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on control-RPE and patient-RPE cells. Principal component analysis showed a significant difference between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Pearson correlation confirmed high reproducibility among replicates (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Using a threshold of adjusted P < 0.05 and fold change > 1, we identified 1879 DEGs, with 976 upregulated and 903 downregulated (see volcano plot in Supplementary Fig. S3C). GO enrichment of the downregulated DEGs highlighted biological processes such as ion transport, transmembrane transport, ion transmembrane transport, and visual perception in the top 10 biological processes (Fig. 4A). KEGG pathway analysis showed significant downregulation of the cell cycle and p53 signaling pathways in the top 10 pathways (Fig. 4B). Heatmaps for visual perception and p53 pathway genes further illustrate these trends (Fig. 4D). The qPCR validation confirmed the RNA-seq findings; genes associated with visual perception, RGR (P < 0.01) and RPE65 (P < 0.01), were markedly diminished in patient-RPE relative to the control, whereas p53-related genes, CCNB1 (P < 0.01), CDK1 (P < 0.001), and p53 (P < 0.001), demonstrated significant upregulation in patient-RPE compared to the control (Fig. 4E). Based on the RNA-seq results, several downregulated biological processes were linked to ion transport. To explore this further, we created a Venn diagram of three related biological processes (ion transport, transmembrane transport, and ion transmembrane transport) (Fig. 5A). Nine overlapping genes were identified and displayed in a heatmap (Fig. 5B), which showed that the expression levels of ion transport–related genes were notably higher in the control-RPE group compared to the patient-RPE group. The qPCR validation confirmed the downregulation of CLCN4 (P < 0.05) and CLCN5 (P < 0.05) in the patient-RPE group compared to the control-RPE group (Fig. 5C). These data suggest that the PRPF8 mutation may disrupt ion transport in RPE cells, leading to abnormal localization and decreased Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, as well as the downregulation of ion transport gene expression.

Based on the role of PRPF8 as a splicing factor, we analyzed our RPE cell transcriptome data to identify splicing differences between the patient and control groups in RPE and between the patient and control groups in iPSCs. We detected 378 differential splicing events in patient-RPE versus 278 in patient-iPSCs. We then analyzed transcripts in all five types for splice event, including alternative 3′ splice sites (A3SS), alternative 5′ splice sites (A5SS), skipped exons (SEs), mutually exclusive exons (MXEs), and retained introns (RIs). All splice event types showed an increase in the patient-RPE group compared to the patient-iPSC group (Fig. 6A), with SE events comprising nearly half of all events (Fig. 6B). GO enrichment analysis of biological processes for splicing events indicated that patient-RPE cells showed significant enrichment in transcripts related to eye morphogenesis and ciliary functions (e.g., protein localization to cilium) (Fig. 6C). Cellular component analysis indicated enrichment in cilia, melanosomes, and pigment granules (Fig. 6D). We then labeled RPE cilia with ARL13B, revealing a reduced cilia incidence and length of cilia in the patient group (incidence P < 0.001; length P < 0.001) and PRPF8-IM group (incidence P < 0.005; length P < 0.001) (Figs. 6E, 6G, 6H), and used transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine RPE ultrastructure, which showed fewer melanosome in patient and PRPF8-IM RPE cells (P < 0.01) (Figs. 6F, 6I). These data suggest that a PRPF8 mutation disrupts alternative splicing in RPE cells, affecting specific biological processes underlying ciliary- and pigmentation-related cellular phenotypes.

In our previous study,22 we designed a single guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the heterozygous PRPF8 mutation (c.5792C>T; p.T1931M) (Supplementary Fig. S4A) and precisely corrected the PRPF8 mutation in patient-iPSCs by ABE. PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing confirmed successful correction of the mutation of PRPF8 (c.5792C>T) (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Unlike CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing, ABE did not introduce synonymous mutations.

We next investigated whether ABE correction of the PRPF8 mutation in patient-iPSCs can reverse the RPE defects. Quantitative PCR demonstrated a significant increase in PRPF8 mRNA expression in the ABE-corrected group (patient-ABE) compared to the uncorrected group (patient) in iPSCs and RPE (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7A). PRPF8 protein levels were markedly upregulated in control and patient-ABE RPE versus patient-RPE (P < 0.01) (Figs. 7B, 7C). The corrected RPE cells (patient-ABE) displayed the characteristic pigmented, polygonal cobblestone morphology (Fig. 7D). ZO-1 immunofluorescence revealed a regular monolayer and robust expression of RPE markers MITF and RPE65 in the patient-ABE group (Fig. 7E). TEER measurements showed restoration of barrier function in the patient-ABE group compared to the patient-RPE group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7F), and Na+/K+-ATPase polarity was reinstated in the patient-ABE group cells (Fig. 7G). To assess reversal of ultrastructural defects, TEM was used to examine the ultrastructure of RPE cells from the control, patient, PRPF8-IM, and patient-ABE groups. Notably, patient-ABE RPE cells no longer exhibited abnormal apical microvilli, displaying structures similar to those of the control, which indicates that the abnormal microvillar features were reversed by ABE correction (Fig. 7H). To determine whether PRPF8 mutations affect the phagocytic function of RPE, we performed in vitro POS phagocytosis assays using control, patient, and ABE-corrected iPSC-derived RPE cells. FITC-labeled porcine POSs were incubated with confluent RPE monolayers, and internalized POSs were quantified by immunofluorescence imaging. The results showed that patient RPE exhibited a significantly reduced POS uptake compared with control RPE (P < 0.01). Importantly, ABE-corrected RPE displayed a marked restoration of phagocytic capacity (Figs. 7I, 7G). These results demonstrate that ABE successfully corrected the PRPF8 c.5792C>T mutation in patient iPSCs and effectively restored key molecular, structural, and functional phenotypes in the patient-derived RPE cells.

PRPF8, as a key component of the spliceosome, is crucial for maintaining splicing accuracy and activating the spliceosomal machinery.5,23 Mutations in the PRPF8 gene cause RP, leading to irreversible vision loss.24 Although recent studies have begun to unravel the specific pathogenic mechanisms of PRPF8-RP,25 the precise role of PRPF8 in spliceosome function and mRNA processing within RPE cells remains poorly understood. Moreover, the high genetic heterogeneity of RP continues to hinder the development of targeted gene therapies.26 To fill these gaps, we generated a patient-derived iPSC line carrying a heterozygous PRPF8 mutation (c.5792C>T)17 and differentiated it into RPE cells. In parallel, we induced the same PRPF8 mutation (c.5792C>T) into wild-type iPSCs via CRISPR/Cas9,18 thereby eliminating inter-individual genetic variability. We demonstrated that this mutation induces abnormal RPE morphology and dysfunction, as well as widespread splicing dysregulation. And, notably, ABE-mediated correction of PRPF8 (c.5792C>T) in patient iPSCs reversed these cellular and molecular defects in patient iPSC-derived RPE. This finding highlights the therapeutic potential of ABE for PRPF8-RP.

 Compared to previously reported animal models of PRPF8-related RP, patient-derived iPSCs carrying PRPF8 mutations offer distinct advantages. They not only avoid the ethical concerns associated with animal experimentation but also overcome the limitations imposed by species differences and physiological traits.27 Although several PRPF8 knock-in mouse models, such as Prpf8-H2309P, p.Tyr2334Asn, and p.Glu2331ValfsX15, have been developed to mimic patient genotypes, none has successfully reproduced the severe retinal abnormalities seen in patients.9,10 These limitations highlight the value of in vitro models derived from patients with clinically confirmed diagnoses. In this study, the use of PRPF8 patient-specific iPSCs provided a genetically matched and physiologically relevant platform for exploring genotype–phenotype correlations in PRPF8-RP. The RPE is a monolayer of hexagonal, pigment-containing cells that plays a critical role in forming and maintaining the outer blood–retina barrier.28,29 In our study, PRPF8 mutations disrupted the highly organized cellular structure of the RPE, leading to irregular cell shapes and a significant decrease in barrier function, both of which are vital for maintaining RPE stability. Similar abnormalities have also been observed in other spliceosomal gene mutations, such as PRPF31 and PRPF6,30,31 suggesting that mutations in PRPF family genes may cause similar downstream defects in RPE through impaired pre-mRNA splicing.

Proper ion homeostasis is critical for retinal integrity; defects in ion channel genes or dysregulated ion transport are linked to various retinal conditions and degeneration.14,30 Notably, the PRPF8 mutation causes significant transcriptomic dysregulation in RPE cells, impairing ion transport and epithelial polarity. Similarly, Atkinson et al.32 showed that PRPF8 mutations disrupt epithelial polarity, as indicated by mislocalized ezrin and collagen IV markers. Furthermore, Buskin et al.33 found that PRPF31-mutant patients have reduced BEST1 expression. Another study showed that a PRPF6 mutation in RP lowers Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity in RP-iRPE cells.31 Together, these findings suggest that PRPF gene disruptions impair epithelial polarity in RPE cells. Unlike the findings of Arzalluz-Luque et al.,16 who reported no evident morphological or functional abnormalities in PRPF8-mutant RPE cells carrying an in-frame deletion (c.6974–6994del), our model harboring the missense variant c.5792C>T (p.T1931M) exhibited a marked reduction in PRPF8 mRNA and protein levels. Importantly, this mutation lies outside the canonical C-terminal Jab1 domain and affects a highly conserved residue. Variants at this position were first reported by Xiao et al.34 in a large Chinese adRP cohort. This discrepancy underscores the substantial allelic heterogeneity of RP, in which different mutation types within the same gene can yield distinct molecular consequences and phenotypic outcomes. This difference likely highlights the remarkable complexity of RP.

Additionally, GO analysis revealed significant downregulation of genes involved in the visual cycle, underscoring functional impairment of the RPE. KEGG pathway enrichment further identified upregulation of genes within the p53 signaling pathway. Previous reports have shown that PRPF8 knockdown induces p53 accumulation and activation, ultimately triggering apoptosis in human embryonic stem cells.35 Consistently, Yano et al.36 demonstrated that PRPF19, in concert with PRPF8 and PRPF3, regulates alternative splicing of MDM4 to activate the p53–p21 signaling pathway. Aberrant splicing may activate the p53 pathway and trigger cell death, potentially leading to retinal degeneration. PRPF8-mutant RPE cells exhibited increased secretion of both PEDF and VEGF. Elevated VEGF levels are commonly associated with RPE stress and metabolic imbalance,37 whereas increased PEDF levels likely represent a compensatory cytoprotective response aimed at preserving photoreceptor support.38 The simultaneous upregulation of these two key trophic factors, therefore, indicates disturbed secretory homeostasis and reflects cellular stress, consistent with the transcriptional activation of stress pathways observed in our model.

PRPF8 serves as a central protein within the spliceosome, orchestrating the precise excision of introns during pre-mRNA processing.6,39 To delineate the impact of PRPF8 mutations on splicing regulation, we quantified alternative splicing (AS) events in patient RPE cells. Notably, exon skipping is emerging as the most dominant category. Our finding is in concordance with those of Arzalluz-Luque et al.,16 who reported widespread AS dysregulation in RPE cells with PRPF8 mutations. Aberrantly spliced transcripts revealed significant enrichment for biological processes associated with cilium assembly and pigment granules. These findings align with our immunofluorescence staining and TEM analyses. Wheway et al.40 reported that mutations in PRPF8 or PRPF31 result in ciliary defects. Atkinson et al.32 also discovered a heterozygous PRPF8 (c.6926 A > C) mutation that demonstrated ciliary defects, and they also found that these mutations lead to photoreceptor-layer disorganization, ciliary defects, mitochondrial abnormalities, and widespread alternative or cryptic splicing affecting retina-relevant pathways. This finding complements our RPE-based observations and underscores the limited tolerance of retinal tissues to spliceosome dysfunction. In addition, we observed a significant reduction in POS phagocytosis in PRPF8 RPE cells. Given that impaired RPE phagocytosis can lead to secondary photoreceptor degeneration, this finding provides a functional link between RPE pathology and the retinal degeneration observed clinically in PRPF8-associated RP. Therefore, these studies reinforce the notion that PRPF8 splicing factors play convergent and indispensable roles in maintaining RPE homeostasis by regulating ciliogenesis. Gene and stem cell therapies represent promising strategies for treating RP and other inherited retinal degenerations.41,42 Among these, patient-specific gene correction using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing has become a widely explored approach,43 particularly in iPSCs, which can be differentiated into retinal cells for potential autologous cell replacement.44 However, the clinical translation of CRISPR/Cas9 remains hindered by its low homology-directed repair (HDR) efficiency and the risk of off-target double-strand DNA breaks.45 To address these limitations, in our previous work, we employed ABE to precisely correct the PRPF8 c.5792C>T point mutation in patient-derived iPSCs.22 Unlike conventional CRISPR/Cas9 systems, ABE enables targeted single-nucleotide conversion without introducing DNA double-strand breaks or relying on HDR,46 thereby offering a safer and more efficient genome editing strategy.47 Building on this, ABE-edited patient iPSCs were efficiently differentiated into RPE cells in the current study. We found that cellular and molecular defects in PRPF8 patient–derived iPSC RPE and key ultrastructural features were rescued by ABE-edited technology. These findings highlight the pathogenic impact of PRPF8 mutations on RPE and ABE as a promising therapeutic platform for the precise correction of pathogenic point mutations in RP and, potentially, other forms of inherited retinal disease, with superior safety and efficacy compared with traditional genome editing approaches.

In summary, our study provides new mechanistic insights into how PRPF8 mutations disrupt RPE structure and function, leading to dysregulation of splicing, disrupting polarity, and causing ciliary defects. Importantly, we demonstrated that adenine base editing corrected the PRPF8 mutation in patient-derived iPSCs, thereby rescuing the abnormal RPE phenotypes. Collectively, these findings offer valuable mechanistic insights into the pathogenesis of RPE associated with PRPF8 and underscore the therapeutic potential of precise gene correction methodologies.
