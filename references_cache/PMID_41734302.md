AIMS: The aim of this study was to investigate the differences in clinical and
ultrasound findings between alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS) and non-ARMS in
order to improve the accuracy of preoperative diagnosis of ARMS in children.
MATERIAL AND METHODS: A retrospective study of 33 children with pathologically
confirmed RMS (ARMS and non-ARMS groups) was realized.  Clinical features and
ultrasound parameters were compared between ARMS and non-ARMS using Fisher 's
exact test analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area
under the curve (AUC) were used to represent diagnostic performance.
RESULTS: Among the clinical features, there were statistically significant
differences between ARMS and non-ARMS groups in site (p=0.020), TNM stage
(p=0.007), IRS stage (p=0.009), risk grade (p=0.011), and distant metastasis
(p=0.020). There were statistical differences in necrosis (p= p0.039) and
central hyperechoic fiber bundles (p<0.001) between the two groups. The
combination of ultrasound and clinical characteristics demonstrated excellent
predictive ability (AUC was 0.964).
CONCLUSIONS: Children with ARMS more often present with poor prognosis, and
combined clinical and ultrasound features are helpful for preoperative
identification of ARMS and providing imaging evidence for accurate clinical
diagnosis and treatment.

Introduction
Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a rare malignant soft 
tissue tumor originating from striated muscle cells or im-
mature mesenchymal cells that are about to differentiate 
into striated muscle. It is the most common soft tissue 
sarcoma in children [1]. Its incidence is second only to 
neuroblastoma and Wilms’ tumor, accounting for approx-
imately 3% of all malignant tumors and 6.5% of soft tis-
sue sarcomas in children in China [2]. RMS is seen at any 
age in children, and its high incidence is in the age range 
of 1-4 years, and the incidence is higher in males than 
in females [3]. Because RMS originates from interstitial 
cells in the embryonic period, it can occur anywhere in 
the body, most commonly in the head and neck region 
and genitourinary tract, followed by involvement of the 
extremities, trunk, retroperitoneum, and biliary tract [4]. 
The clinical symptoms of RMS are related to the site of 
tumor growth, and most of the children present with rap-
idly growing soft tissue masses.
According to the pathological results, WHO divides 
RMS into four subtypes: embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma 
(ERMS), alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS), pleo-
morphic rhabdomyosarcoma (PRMS), and spindle cells/
sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma (SRMS/ScRMS) [5]. 
ERMS and ARMS are the most common in children, 
accounting for about 70-80%. RMS histomorphology 
is diverse, and its prognosis is associated with the age,

Primary RMS in bladder, prostate, limbs, trunk and other

is prone to hematogenous metastasis of regional lymph 
node metastasis due to poor tumor cell differentiation, 
suggesting a poor prognosis [9]. Previous studies have 
shown that PAX3/7-FOXO1 gene fusion exists in about

metastasis by activating downstream signaling pathways, 
and the five-year survival rate of children with positive 
fusion genes is significantly lower than that of negative 
fusion genes [10,11]. At the same time, mutations and 
abnormal miRNA expression  in ARMS are associated

surgical resection, maintenance chemotherapy for high-
risk RMS has been shown to improve survival outcomes

RMS lacks distinct imaging features and laboratory 
tests, and is difficult to differentiate from other solid tu-
mors due to the heterogeneity or differentiation of the 
tumor, especially large abdominal and pelvic tumors in 
young infants. The gold standard for defining the nature 
of the tumor is histopathological examination, but this 
invasive examination is usually accompanied by other 
complications. Therefore, for children with RMS, espe-
cially ARMS, preoperative determination of the nature of 
the tumor is essential for the treatment and prognosis of 
children. Conventional ultrasound (US) of RMS usually 
presents as a solid mass with infiltrative growth, which 
can involve grade adjacent tissue, and internal echoes 
also show diversity due to heterogeneous cellular distri-
bution within the tumor. RMS is commonly located in or 
around the muscle layer. Sheng et al found that calcifica-
tion, encapsulated vessels, and intraspinal tumor exten -
sion were less common in RMS children compared with 
neuroblastoma [14]. Franco et al analyzed computed to-
mography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) 
images of 14 children with RMS and found that their 
imaging features (such as density and heterogeneity) 
were not associated with its pathological subtypes [15]. 
According to previous studies, there have been no stud-
ies to definitively focus the diagnostic efficacy of US in 
RMS in children, especially ARMS with poor prognosis. 
The aim of this study was to investigate the differences in 
clinical and US features between ARMS and non-ARMS 
in order to improve the accuracy of preoperative diagno-
sis of ARMS in children and to provide imaging evidence 
for clinical treatment and intervention.
Material and methods
Study population
A retrospective analysis was conducted on 33 chil -
dren with pathologically confirmed RMS from January 
2019 to March 2025 at Xi’an Children’s Hospital. The 
institutional Ethics Committee approved the study of our 
hospital. Since this study was retrospective, all patients 
waived informed consent. The inclusion criteria were 
as follows: a) patients diagnosed as RMS by pathologi -
cal examination after surgery or biopsy; b) patients who 
underwent US examination 2 weeks before surgery. The 
exclusion criteria were as follows: a) patients with in-
complete clinical data; b) patients who had missing pre-
operative US images and poor image quality; c) patients 
with history of other malignant tumors at admission;  
d) patients who did not undergo histopathological exami-
nation.
US acquisition and analysis
US image was performed using a Philips EPIQ 7 
Color Doppler Ultrasound System with high frequency 
probe (linear array probe L12-5) and low frequency 
probe (convex array probe C5-1). First, low-frequency 
probe was used to determine the target lesion, evaluate 
its size, boundary and shape, high-frequency probe was 
used to observe the echo inside the lesion and its rela-
tionship with the surrounding tissues; color Doppler flow 
imaging (CDFI) was used to assess the blood flow of the 
lesion. 
US examinations were performed by a single sonog-
rapher with 3 years of experience in ultrasound. The fi-
nal analysis of ultrasound images was performed by two 
sonographers with more than 5 years of ultrasound ex-
perience in pediatric diseases. In order to maintain the 
objectivity of the image results, they were blinded to the 
pathological results of the children. We assessed the US 
characteristics of each RMS patient and extracted lesion 
characteristics from US images, including: site, maxi -
mum lesion diameter, echogenicity (iso-/hypo-/mixed-), 
shape (irregular/lobulated/regular), margin (clear/un -
clear), calcification (presence/absence), necrosis (pres-
ence/absence), central hyperechoic fiber bundles (pres-
ence/absence), invasion of theadjacent tissues (presence/
absence), and Adler flow grade (grade 0/I/II/III).
Diagnosis and pathological assessment
According to the international and domestic guide-
lines for the diagnosis and treatment of RMS in children 
and adolescents [2], we first performed clinical evalua -
tion in children with suspected RMS, then localization 
and qualitative diagnosis by imaging examination (in-
cluding US/CT/MRI), and finally confirmed the diagno-
sis and pathological classification by US-guided biopsy

We used the tumor node metastasis classification (TNM) 
staging system and the Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma 
Study Group (IRS) staging system to stage RMS [16,17]. 
TNM staging system is based on the primary location, 
size, lymph node metastasis or distant metastasis of the 
tumor and other conditions for staging. The IRS staging 
system is staged according to the extent of surgical resec-
tion and postoperative pathological microscopic results: 
stage I refers to the tumor showing localized lesions, 
which can be completely removed by surgery without 
regional lymph node metastasis; stage II refers to the 
flesh eye showing complete resection of the tumor, but 
also that there has been local tissue invasion or regional 
lymph node metastasis around, with or without micro -
scopic residual; stage III refers to the tumor that cannot 
be completely removed or only biopsy is performed, re-
sidual tumor tissue can be seen macroscopically, with mi-
croscopic residual; and stage IV refers to distant metasta-
sis already at the time of diagnosis. Combined with IRS 
clinical staging criteria and histopathological classifica-
tion, RMS was divided into three groups: low, intermedi-
ate, and high risk. 
Treatment 
The pathological nature of the tumor is determined 
through surgical resection or US-guided needle biopsy. 
Surgical resection is the preferred treatment for complete 
removal of the tumor. In cases where resection is diffi-
cult or where function needs to be preserved, adjuvant 
chemotherapy may be administered after confirmation by 
biopsy.
Statistical analyses
All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 statisti-
cal software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY , USA). The Kolm-
ogorov Smirnov test was used to assess the normality 
of continuous variables. Student’s test and Mann-Whit-
ney U test were employed for normally distributed and 
non-normally distributed continuous variables, respec-
tively. Results were reported as mean±standard deviation 
for normally distributed variables and as median with 
interquartile range for non-normally distributed vari-
ables. Categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-
square test. A two-tailed p-value of <0.05 was considered 
statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic 
(ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) were used 
to represent diagnostic performance.
Results
Clinical characteristics analysis and pathological 
type
Based on pathological results, 33 children with 
RMS were ultimately classified as either ARMS or non-
ARMS. Of the children in this group, 17 (51.5%, 17/33) 
were male, with age of onset of 1 month – 15 years, mean 
age of 5.3 ± 4.3 years, and a median age of 4.0 years 
(Table I). The pathological types and clinical stages of 33 
patients in this group are shown in table II. Among the 
clinical features, there were statistically significant dif-
ferences between ARMS and non-ARMS groups in site 
(p=0.020), TNM stage (p= 0.007), IRS stage (p= 0.009), 
risk grade (p=0.011), and distant metastasis (p=0.020).
Analysis of US characteristics of  
ARMS and non-ARMS
In US all 33 cases of RMS showed ill-defined borders 
and irregular shape. Due to large tumor diameter hypo- 
or mixed echogenicity was predominant. Grade II-III 
blood flow signals were more frequently found (84.8% 
vs 15.2%). Table III shows the comparison of US char -
acteristics between ARMS and non-ARMS. Compared 
with the non-ARMS group, the ARMS group more of-
ten showed mixed echoes (75.0% vs. 19.0%), and there 
were statistically significant differences between the two 
groups in necrosis (p=0.039) and central hyperechoic 
fiber bundles (p<.001) (fig 1-3). There were no statisti -
cally significant differences in border, shape, blood flow 
grade, calcification, and invasion of surrounding tissues.
Evaluation of diagnostic performance
We used the ROC curve to evaluate diagnostic per -
formance (fig 4), and the results showed that US features 
demonstrated good predictive ability. The AUC value 
for necrosis was 0.690 (95%CI: 0.497–0.884), with di-
agnostic sensitivity and specificity of 66.7% and 71.4%, 
respectively. The AUC value for central hyperechoic fi-
ber bundles was 0.845 (95%CI: 0.694–0.997), with di-
agnostic sensitivity and specificity of 83.3% and 85.7%, 
respectively. We combined all US features and all clin -
ical features separately and compared their predictive 
capabilities. The results showed that the diagnostic per -
formance of combining all US and clinical features was 
superior to that of using a single feature (AUC values of 
0.964, 0.845, and 0.865, respectively) (Table IV).
Treatment and Follow-up
In our study 7 children (7/33, 21.2%) underwent both 
surgical resection and chemotherapy, 22 children (22/33, 
66.7%) were assessed as inoperable and received chemo-
therapy alone, and 4 children (4/33, 12.1%) discontin -
ued treatment. Follow-up was conducted for 29 children 
who received treatment. Among them, 23 (23/29, 79.3%)

Discussion

RMS, and its site of growth and clinical presentation are 
diverse [18]. ARMS generally indicates  a poor progno-
sis and poor patient survival [19]. In this paper, we com-
pared the clinical and US characteristics of ARMS with 
non-ARMS, and the results showed that among the clini-
cal characteristics, ARMS group and non-ARMS group 
were statistically different in site, TNM stage, IRS stage, 
risk grade and distant metastasis. There were statistically 
significant differences between the two groups in the ne-
crosis and central hyperechoic fiber bundles. Combining 
all US and clinical features resulted in excellent predic-
tive ability (AUC=0.964).

non-skeletal muscles, most commonly in the head and 
neck region, and genitourinary tract. Of the 33 RMS pa-
tients in this study, 8 were primary in the maxillofacial 
region and 16 were primary in the pelvic cavity, consist-
ent with previous report [20]. The clinical symptoms of 
RMS vary according to the site of disease, and most ab-
dominal or pelvic tumors present with abdominal disten-

Research indicates that treatment protocols for chil -
dren RMS should include more detailed imaging guide-
lines to ensure uniformity in response assessment and 
better application in practice [24]. The US appearance of 
RMS in different locations is closely related to its growth 
site. For example, RMS in the abdominal or pelvic cav-
ity presents as a large mass similar to neuroblastoma 
or rhabdoid tumor, and the echogenicity of the tumor 
is closely related to the internal tissue. Tumors arising 
in the head and neck region may appear adjacent to the 
parotid gland, orbit, and cervical vessels, the borders of 
the tumor are not clear, and hypervascular tissues lead 
to CDFI showing abundant blood flow signals. Parates-
ticular RMS presents as a large solid mass encapsulating 
or invading the testis, and Doppler US shows increased 
vascularity outside the testis and scrotum [25]. 
We found that there were statistically significant dif-
ferences between the two groups in necrosis (p=0.039) 
and central hyperechoic fiber bundles (p<0.001). Because 
ARMS is a malignant tumor composed of primitive small 
round cells, it shows differentiated rhabdomyoblasts with 
abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and characteristic neo-
plastic multinucleated giant cells. Microscopically, it can 
be observed that the fibrous vascular bundles separate 
the tumor cells into nests, and the cell adhesiveness in 
the center of the cell nests is poor, while the peripheral 
cells are dense [21,26]. We could therefore observe hy-
perechoic fiber bundles in the center of the tumor rather 
than pseudocapsule located at the periphery of the tumor 
in US images. On the other hand, ARMS alveolization 
is formed because tumor cells accumulate at the edge 
of the fibrous septa and rhabdomyoblasts adhere to the 
monolayer. In the central part of this lesion, cells lose 
this connection and undergo necrosis and degeneration 
[26]. ARMS is more malignant than other types of RMS, 
and when the tumor grows too fast and newly generated 
blood vessels fail to provide enough oxygen and energy, 
it will cause ischemia and hypoxia in the tumor site and 
then lead to necrosis, which is generally manifested as 
local or irregular anechogenicity on US images.

This study is the first to systematically compare the 
US findings of ARMS versus non-ARMS in children 
from an imaging perspective, providing new evidence 
for noninvasive preoperative assessment of ARMS. Un-
like previous studies that only analyzed single imaging 
indicators or clinical parameters, this study innovatively 
combined multiple clinical features with US imaging pa-
rameters and verified their diagnostic efficacy by ROC 
curve, and the results showed that the combined model 
had significantly higher diagnostic accuracy in ARMS 
prediction and provided a more operable diagnostic strat-
egy for clinical practice. By clarifying the relationship 
between ARMS and poor clinical stage, distant metasta -
sis and high-risk grade, combined with US findings, this 
study provides valuable imaging support for early risk 
assessment, individualized treatment planning and prog-
nosis of ARMS in children. 
Immunohistochemically, ARMS expressed desmin, 
MyoD1 and myogenin positivity, especially MyoD1 and 
myogenin showed strong positivity at the edge of the al-
veolar structure or around blood vessels [27,28]. Clini -
cians usually identify the nature of the tumor by surgical 
specimens or needle biopsies. In early diagnosis of RMS, 
US-guided biopsy can effectively improve the accuracy 
of sampling. Core needle biopsies are more suitable than 
fine needle biopsies for assessing morphologic structures 
and obtaining adequate tissue for cytogenetic and mo-
lecular analysis, which may predict aggressive behavior 
or abnormal gene fusion of the disease [29]. Contrast-
enhanced US can further improve the accuracy of preop-
erative diagnosis of RMS because it can provide blood 
perfusion and microvascular imaging inside the tumor, 
while contrast-enhanced US-guided biopsy can also 
avoid false solid components of two-dimensional images 
[30]. Although multimodal treatment for RMS has be-
come more established, the overall survival rate is only 
about 20%. In recent years, immunotherapy and nano-
medicine have shown promising results in the treatment 
of RMS, improving overall survival and causing fewer 
side effects [31].
This study has some limitations. First, this is a small 
single-center retrospective study, and the sample size 
should be expanded for further validation in the future. 
Second, no prognostic indicator was included in this 
study because some patients were not treated completely 
or lost to follow-up during the course of the study; how-
ever, we tracked the recurrence and prognosis of hos-
pitalized patients. Therefore, the follow-up time should 
be prolonged to further verify the correlation between 
ARMS US images and prognosis in subsequent studies; 
third, our study is limited to conventional US and Dop-
pler US, and future studies are expected to obtain more 
information on the internal and molecular levels of the 
tumor preoperatively with the help of contrast-enhanced 
US and artificial intelligence to provide more imaging 
basis for clinical practice.
Conclusions
ARMS has atypical clinical features and poor clinical 
prognosis in children with ARMS, and combined clinical 
and US features are helpful for preoperative identifica -
tion of ARMS and providing imaging evidence for ac-
curate clinical diagnosis and treatment.
