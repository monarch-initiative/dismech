# DEGS1-Related Hypomyelinating Leukodystrophy: Four Individuals From Same Family and Review of Literature.
**Authors:** Grinberg M, Dale B, Ramachandrannair R, Tarnopolsky M, Whitney R, Ejaz R
**Journal:** Neurol Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1212/NXG.0000000000200334](https://doi.org/10.1212/NXG.0000000000200334)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: DEGS1-related leukodystrophy has been described in 33 individuals to date with varying degrees of neurologic symptoms and atrophy/hypomyelination on MRI brain. We describe a family of 4 individuals with DEGS1-related leukodystrophy with phenotypic heterogeneity both within the family and in previously reported individuals.

METHODS: Four related individuals were followed by the neurology, genetics, and neurometabolic teams with clinical examinations, MRI brain, and genetic/metabolic investigations. Trio exome sequencing (ES) was completed after initial investigations returned normal.

RESULTS: ES revealed homozygous pathogenic variants in DEGS1, c.110T>C (p.Met37Thr), for the proband with subsequent confirmatory testing of remaining affected family members. While the affected individuals share many similarities, there is notable variability in severity between them as well as with other 33 previously reported individuals, including 2 with the same pathogenic variant. This variability is highlighted in the presence of seizures, nystagmus, failure to thrive, microcephaly, MRI brain findings, and age at disease onset.

DISCUSSION: This report expands on the documented knowledge of DEGS1-related leukodystrophy and adds to the current understanding of the phenotypic range of affected individuals. Consideration of variability in the condition's natural history and outcome is key to appropriately counsel patients and families.

DEGS1-related leukodystrophy is a rare, autosomal recessive, neurodegenerative condition reported in 33 individuals. 1-8 DEGS1 encodes for protein C4-dihydroceramide desaturase, essential for ceramide synthesis via conversion of dihydroceramide into ceramide. The condition presents similarly to other leukodystrophies in developmental delay, regression, spasticity, dystonia, and seizures. 1 MRI brain findings include diffuse hypomyelination, T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) thalamic hyperintensities, thin corpus callosum, and cerebellar atrophy. 1

Variable expressivity has been reported depending on the amino acid change, with the initial impression that affected relatives shared similar phenotypes. 1 Subsequent reports described differences in individuals with the same DEGS1 variant and intrafamilial variability, suggesting that environmental and genetic factors influence severity.

We report 4 additional related individuals with DEGS1-related leukodystrophy and compare their presentations to 33 previously reported individuals. The current report expands this condition's genotype-phenotype correlations and helps to better understand its natural history.

Four children were seen by the genetic, neurometabolic, and pediatric neurology teams, with complete history and physical examinations. All 4 underwent MRI brain, and 3 underwent EEG. Trio exome sequencing (ES) was performed on the proband (P2) and parents at the Centre for Applied Genomics (Toronto, Canada) with genomic DNA extracted from whole blood. Confirmatory variant specific testing was performed for the 3 remaining individuals. The proband's affected sister underwent bloodwork for small molecule and organelle metabolic disorders, ophthalmologic consultation, and chromosomal microarray.

A review of literature was conducted using PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar with the final search on August 1st, 2025. Search terms included the following: (“DEGS1” or “dihydroceramide desaturase 1”) AND (“hypomyelinating leukodystrophy” OR “leukodystrophy” OR “white matter disease”). References in relevant articles were manually reviewed for additional eligible studies.

Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available on request from any qualified investigator.

Written informed consent was obtained from all guardians of participants in the study. This case report did not require Research Ethics Board approval as per institutional guidelines.

We report 4 individuals from a Syrian family; 3 are siblings with consanguineous parents, and the fourth is their cousin (Figure 1), all with varying neurologic symptoms. Antenatal histories were unremarkable. They have no notable dysmorphic features.

P1-P3 parents are both paternal first cousins and maternal second cousins. P4 is P1-P3's paternal first cousin and maternal second cousin twice removed. Owing to multiple loops of consanguinity, other similarly affected children have also been reported in the family.

The eldest child (P1), a 15-year-old boy, developed appropriately until 15 months when he began toe walking with subsequent motor regression. He became non ambulatory with spastic quadriplegia at age 2 years. He can roll but cannot sit and can speak several words. He has failure to thrive (FTT) (weight Z-score: −3.98, height Z-score: −3.5) and microcephaly (head circumference [HC] < 3rd percentile). He has never had seizures. MRI brain showed cerebellar atrophy and bilateral lateral ventricular dilation with periventricular white matter loss; MRS was normal (Figure 2A).

The second child (P2), the proband, a 12-year-old boy, initially walked, but at 15–16 months regressed to being non-ambulatory. He currently crawls and rolls with lower limb spasticity. He is verbal and speaks short phrases. He uses a phone and a tablet to search for videos. He has FTT (weight Z-score: −3.43, height Z-score: −3.41) with normal HC (8th percentile). He developed bilateral tonic-clonic and focal seizures at age 6 years, now well-controlled on levetiracetam, lacosamide, and valproic acid. EEG was normal. MRI brain revealed ventricular dilation with periventricular white matter involvement, thin corpus callosum and increased FLAIR changes in thalami, and peritrigonal regions; MRS was normal (Figure 2B).

The third child (P3), a 4-year-old girl, sat unsupported and crawled at 8 months. She never walked and regressed to being nonambulatory with poor head control by 24 months. She can only say “mama”. She has focal and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures on clobazam, significant irritability and sleep disturbance. She has FTT (weight Z-score: −3.63, height Z-score: −4.18) and microcephaly (HC < 3rd percentile). EEG showed frequent interictal discharges, excessive beta activity, and lack of posterior dominant rhythm and sleep features. MRI brain showed cerebellar atrophy, delayed myelination, increased FLAIR/T2 signal in the left hippocampus and bilateral thalami, and a thin corpus callosum; MRS was normal (Figure 2C).

The fourth child (P4), a 3-year-old boy, crawled and stood with support at 12 months but regressed to sitting with support, nonverbal, communicating by groaning and pointing. He had one bilateral tonic-clonic seizure; clobazam was started. EEG showed right parietal and mid-left central epileptiform discharges. He has dystonic episodes managed on baclofen. He has FTT (weight Z-score: −1.72) and normal HC (58th percentile). MRI brain demonstrated unmyelinated subcortical U-fibers and low creatine peak (Figure 2D). Urine creatine to creatinine ratio was elevated; genetic testing for SLC6A8 was negative.

Trio ES completed in 2023 on the proband, P2, revealed a previously reported homozygous pathogenic variant in DEGS1, NM_003676.4:c.110 T>C (p.Met37Thr). Follow-up targeted variant testing in the remaining affected individuals was positive for the same homozygous variant.

DEGS1/delta 4-desaturase is a part of the myelination pathway given its involvement in the final step of ceramide formation. 9 The resulting accumulation of dihydroceramide and other dihydrosphingolipid species have been implicated in exosomes and reactive oxygen species formation, as well as membrane composition changes, permeability, and membrane stability, leading to autophagy and apoptosis. 10,11 While these findings were initially observed in animal models, 33 individuals with DEGS1 deficiency have now been described with features consistent with hypomyelinating leukodystrophy. 1-8,12,13

Our cohort presented with clinical and radiographic features suspicious for hypomyelinating leukodystrophy, with classic features of white matter injury including regression, global developmental delay, seizures, and spasticity. Brain imaging revealed findings of hypomyelination including diffuse loss of gray and white matter differentiation in P1, and diffuse prominence of extra-axial CSF spaces and bilateral ventricles in P1 and P2, the latter suggesting white matter loss. In the younger patients, hypomyelination was suspected because of delayed myelination associated with small bilateral thalami in P3 and unmyelinated subcortical U-shaped white matter in P4.

We compared our patients with previously reported individuals with DEGS1-related leukodystrophy (Table). Notable features in our cohort include universal regression, compared with 9/33 previous cases with an average age at onset at 1.9 years. The onset of regression is variable ranging from shortly after birth to 2 individuals presenting at age 4.5 years and one outlier at age 10 years. 1,7 Enteral feeding and nystagmus were also absent in our cohort though common in published cases.

MRI brain findings are also reviewed in Table. 1-8 The most common findings are diffuse hypomyelination with more prominent white matter changes (22/33), atrophy of the corpus callosum (20/30) and cerebellum (12/33), and ventricular dilation (10/33). The basal ganglia was implicated with increased clinical severity. 1 However, our cohort lacked basal ganglia abnormalities and did not show any significant MRS changes, which have been previously reported in several patients. 1 Nonetheless, MRI brain can be a reliable diagnostic tool, even if done early in disease. 1

Predicting symptom severity is challenging because there is intrafamilial variability and lack of genotype-phenotype correlation. P1 has never had seizures unlike his siblings. P2 has some preserved language and ambulation while P3 is nonverbal with poor head control. Similarly, Dolgin et al. 2 described a family with one sibling who is a high school graduate and uses a wheelchair, while another sibling is nonverbal and is unable to sit. This variability makes prognostication difficult even in families with previously diagnosed members.

A limited comparison was made between our family to 2 individuals with the c.110T>C (p.Met37Thr) variant because of lack of detailed information. One of these 2 individuals was homozygous, and the other was a compound heterozygote: c.110T>C (p.Met37Thr) and c.878G>A (pTrp293*). 1,3 The individual with the same homozygous variant is nonverbal with spasticity, maximum motor ability of head control, and profound hypomyelination on MRI brain. 3 She has no seizures, FTT, or microcephaly in contrast to our cohort. Available information for the individual with the compound heterozygous variant is moderate motor delay, mild intellectual delay, and generalized hypomyelination on MRI brain. 1

In summary, we describe 4 related individuals with DEGS1-related leukodystrophy, further characterizing this rare disorder and underscoring the challenges in its counseling given the variable expressivity, even within families. Some individuals may be seizure-free with basic verbal communication, whereas severely affected individuals have profound regression, medically refractory epilepsy, and FTT requiring enteral feeds. These differences suggest a shared phenotype with variable severity, influenced by genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors. Further studies in genotype-phenotype correlation and additional factors influencing patient outcomes are needed.
