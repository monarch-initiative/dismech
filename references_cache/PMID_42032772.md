BACKGROUND: Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) shows a consistent sex bias, yet how
sex shapes de novo variant (DNV) risk across coding and noncoding sequence
remains unclear. I analyzed DNVs in > 41,000 parent–child sequenced trios from
three ASD family-based cohorts and compared DNV characteristics and enrichment
patterns in males and females. Notably, these trios included individuals with
ASD as well as those without ASD. I developed a new sex-aware DNV caller
(HAT-FLEX) and thoroughly evaluated each candidate DNV using an additional tool
introduced in this study, SNOW, to generate a high-confidence callset. RESULTS:
I identified enrichment of missense and loss-of-function (LOF) DNVs both overall
and within known ASD-related genes (i.e., SFARI genes). Gene-specific enrichment
analyses revealed twelve genes that were exome-wide significant and specific to
males, for significance, including FOXP1, SMAD6, AUTS2, CCDC168, PIEZO1, EML6,
ZNF84, IGSF23, OTOG, NHSL1, ADNP, and FREM3 and three genes that were specific
to females, for significance, including TAOK1, MECP2, and DDX3X within a variant
class. Direct comparisons of DNVs in males and females revealed MECP2 as the
only exome-wide significant gene; however, GABBR2 was also trending toward
enrichment in the direct males with ASD comparison to females with ASD.
Furthermore, probit analyses support a female protective effect and demonstrates
that damaging DNVs, particularly LOF variants, including potentially stronger
X-linked effects in females, are associated with increased ASD liability,
whereas synonymous variants are not. Finally, I analyzed promoters and
identified a single significant promoter region (p = 3.8 × 10−13), associated
with the WDR74 gene, with the signal driven by DNVs observed in males with ASD.
Surprisingly, the noncoding RNA gene RNU2-2 lies within this significant WDR74
promoter and accounted for most of the DNVs in the region. RNU2-2 DNVs were
present in 0.2% of individuals with ASD in comparison to 0.05% of individuals
without ASD. CONCLUSIONS: These findings show that ASD DNV risk differs by sex
at both the gene and liability levels, supporting a female protective effect
while highlighting sex-specific patterns in coding variation. They also identify
RNU2-2 as a noncoding contributor to ASD risk, expanding the landscape of
ASD-associated variation beyond protein-coding genes.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the overall prevalence of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is one in 31 individuals. Furthermore, ASD exhibits a sex bias with a prevalence in males of one in 20 individuals and in females of one in 70 individuals [1]. ASD is considered to be multifactorial with contributions from both the genome and the environment, which is modeled using a liability distribution. Under Falconer’s threshold model, this indicates that the rare class (females with autism) would require a higher threshold to develop ASD than the more common class (males with autism) [2,3]. More generally, sex bias in human phenotypes and disease is common and the reasons for this bias are important for understanding of both the underlying mechanisms and ultimately for more tailored future clinical care. Potential mechanisms underlying sex bias include effects from the genome, hormones, and other potential environmental interactions. Regarding the genome, the most obvious culprits are the (1) sex chromosomes, themselves, and the loci which they interact with in the autosomal genome and (2) the genomic loci interacting with hormones. There are several potential downstream effects of this including gene expression differences in males and females. Classical examples of genes related to ASD with effects from the X chromosome areFMR1repeat expansions in Fragile X Syndrome in males [4] and protein-affecting de novo variants (DNVs) inMECP2in Rett Syndrome [5]. Fragile X syndrome preferentially affects males and Rett syndrome preferentially affects females due to differences in underlying mechanisms. An example of an effect from the Y chromosome is the significant increase in ASD in individuals with XYY syndrome [6]. A recent example indicating hormonal involvement in ASD has shown a link between ASD and fetal estrogens [7].

With the advent of whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS), the study of rare variants in ASD genome-wide has expanded greatly. This has yielded several studies identifying an excess of rare and de novo variants (DNVs) in females with ASD as compared to males with ASD [8,9]. In ASD, this phenomenon has taken on the identity of the female protective effect [10] and fits well with the threshold model described above. More generally comparisons of rare variants have been expanded out beyond ASD to a broader set of individuals including those with developmental disorders [11]. This is important; however, it is also relevant to note that developmental disorders more broadly do not exhibit as substantial of a skewing towards males as in ASD [9]. The result of these studies has been the identification of several genes on the X chromosome with enrichment in females with developmental disorders with the most common beingDDX3X[12]. This gene is a gene on the X chromosome that escapes X inactivation and thereby is expressed at higher levels in females with ASD than males with ASD presumably providing a type of “buffered” expression.

Since the sex bias in ASD is higher than in general developmental disorders, the focus of this current study is on DNVs in large cohorts of families with ASD. A previous study has examined DNVs in ~ 23,000 trios with ASD and an additional ~ 30,000 individuals with developmental disorders [13]. A recent preprint reports assessment of DNVs in ~ 31,000 individuals with ASD [14], which included DNV calling in a subset of the individuals in the study and the integration of these with existing DNV calls from other cohorts. However, ongoing efforts to sequence more individuals with ASD has generated additional datasets. The most concerted of these efforts have come from the Simons Foundation through their cohorts including the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC) [15] and the Simons Powering Autism Research (SPARK [16]).

In this study, > 41,000 parent–child trios from families with ASD, that were sequenced by either WES or WGS through the SSC or SPARK, are assessed for DNVs in the protein-coding regions of the genome and a subset of > 9800 parent–child trios sequenced by WGS are assessed for DNVs in the noncoding regions of the genome. Importantly, the DNVs generated and analyzed in this current study are not integrated from different DNV callers but rather are from a uniform calling workflow as follows. First, I developed two modern computational tools for calling (i.e., HAT-FLEX) and optimizing DNV calls (i.e., SNOW) from WES and WGS data. Specifically, these sex-aware tools have been developed to handle DNV calling on autosomes, the pseudoautosomal regions (PARs), the non-PAR X chromosome, and the non-PAR Y chromosome. Second, I generated high-quality, sex-aware, DNVs using data from multiple sequencing technologies and characterized DNV patterns across three ASD family cohorts. Third, I evaluated gene-set and gene-specific enrichment of loss-of-function (LOF) and missense DNVs in males and females with or without ASD, including analyses involving the autosomes, the PARs, the non-PAR X chromosome, and the non-PAR Y chromosome. Fourth, I identified the rates of LOF and missense DNVs within genes and through a probit model identified importance of LOF and missense DNVs to ASD. Finally, I tested for enrichment of DNVs in promoter regions in males and females with or without ASD, respectively, leading to further followup that involved a noncoding RNA gene.

Three cohorts consisting of families with ASD were accessed from the Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI) Base. These included a total of 41,421 parent–child sequenced trios from the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC) [15] and the Simons Powering Autism Research (SPARK [16] cohorts.

I attempted to call DNVs on individuals from all 41,421 trios (Supplementary TableS1). In total, 85 samples failed DNV QC based on outlying DNV counts: < 40 or > 150 DNVs in WGS, or > 8 DNVs in WES. I removed an additional 220 individuals who belonged to monozygotic twin pairs, retaining only one individual per twin pair to avoid duplicate representation. This resulted in a total of 41,201 independent parent–child sequenced trios; of which, 31,268 trios were from WES data (17,114 males with ASD, 4867 females with ASD, 4528 males without ASD, and 4759 females without ASD), and 9848 trios (4808 males with ASD, 1021 females with ASD, 1928 males without ASD, and 2091 females without ASD) were from WGS data. A description of how I called DNVs is below.

For WGS data, family-level genome-wide variant call files (VCFs) were obtained from a previous study [17]. Briefly, these VCFs were generated from genotyping Genomic VCFs (gVCFs) generated by GATK HaplotypeCaller [18] and DeepVariant [19], respectively, with GLnexus [20]. For WES data, I downloaded per-individual gVCFs generated with DeepVariant from SFARI Base (accession: SPARK iWES_v3). For each family, I performed joint-genotyping using GLnexus (release v1.4.1-0-g68e25e5).

For both WGS and WES, I called DNVs using my newly developed tool called HAT-FLEX (version 1.0,https://github.com/TNTurnerLab/HAT-FLEX, full description inSupplementary Methods). Briefly, HAT-FLEX is a Python post-calling computational tool for identifying DNVs from parent–child trios using one or two genome-wide VCFs per trio (e.g., GATK, DeepVariant), with an optional two-caller intersection mode; it supports human and other diploid species, reads trios from a simple family file or standard pedigree file, and applies sex-aware haploid/diploid logic on X/Y including awareness of PARs, so that filtering and parent relevance depend on genomic context. It detects candidate DNVs via sex-aware genotype patterns (reference alleles in parent(s), alternate allele in child), optionally splits multi-allelic sites by allele with normalization, can mask user-provided regions, and then applies layered quality controls including required FORMAT keys, strand-bias checks, depth/GQ thresholds, parent “ALT leak” rules (allele depth and genotype-based), allele-balance thresholds (diploid and haploid), homopolymer handling, and a “haploid unusual” flag for heterozygous-like signals on haploid loci. Outputs include a sorted, normalized trio-only VCF with added INFO/FILTER annotations (e.g., DENOVO, SEXCTX, IRRELPARENT, HAPUNUSUAL, CLUSTER), optional TSV and metrics/manifest summaries, and automatic bgzip compression plus optional tabix indexing (with gzip fallback), along with logging, and verbosity controls.

For WGS data, the parameters of HAT-FLEX included –ped to input a standard pedigree, –caller1_vcf for the DeepVariant family VCF, –caller2_vcf for the GATK HaplotypeCaller VCF, –intersect_mode of allele to intersect the two callers by allele, –normalize_alleles set to true, –par_bed set to build 38 coordinates, –gq_value set to 20, –depth_value set to 10, –haploid_depth_value set to 5, –ab_min set to 0.25, –haploid_ab_min set to 0.85, –require_format_keys set to GT,DP,AD,GQ, –strict_format set to false, –max_homopolymer set to 10, –cluster_window_bp set to 100, and –cluster_min_count set to 2. For WES data, the same parameters were used except for –caller2_vcf and –intersect_mode, which were not used, since only DeepVariant was used in the HAT-FLEX calling on the WES data.

Post-calling of DNVs, I developed a computational tool called SNOW (Second-pass de Novo variant Offspring Workflow, version 0.1.0,https://github.com/tycheleturner/snow, (full description inSupplementary Methods) to further process the DNVs. First, I collapsed DNV calls usingsnow consolidate, which merges nearby child-only variants into single records when they met strict eligibility criteria. Variants were processed in genomic order per contig, requiring the child to carry an alternate allele while all other samples were homozygous reference, and restricting merging to high-confidence PASS sites annotated with the HAT-FLEX cluster flag. Eligible variants within 100 bp were grouped into clusters; for clusters with multiple variants, I defined the minimal spanning interval, retrieved the reference haplotype, and assembled the alternate haplotype by substituting the alternate allele at each site while disallowing overlaps. I required at least two reads to support the assembled (collapsed) haplotype.

Next, I interrogated alignment data for evidence of the alternate allele at candidate DNVs, usingsnow parentcheck, which scans parental and child reads around each variant annotated with the HAT-FLEX DENOVO flag. Using the child VCF and the child, father, and mother CRAM files, I counted ALT-supporting reads in each individual. Support was assessed at the haplotype level from aligned query-reference base pairs, with explicit handling of SNVs, MNVs, and INDELs; reads were required to anchor the reference position and, for INDELs, to match the expected inserted or deleted sequence. For variants annotated with SEXCTX = PAR, I also queried the homologous PAR on the partner sex chromosome to capture shared coverage. I required zero high-quality ALT-supporting reads in both parents and at least two high-quality ALT-supporting reads in the child.

Finally, I inferred parent-of-origin for DNVs in trios, usingsnow phaser, which combines phase-by-transmission generated on a genome-wide trio VCF with read-backed physical phasing from the alignment data of the child. I first identified informative transmission sites genome wide as high-quality biallelic SNVs where the child was heterozygous and all trio genotypes had GQ ≥ 20, excluding non-PAR X/Y in male probands. For each DNV, I collected child reads supporting the ALT allele and phased them to nearby informative SNVs within ± 40 kilobase pairs (kbp) by determining which parental allele those same reads carried. I summed paternal and maternal support across informative sites to assign parent-of-origin by majority vote (ties or insufficient evidence were reported as unknown), and quantified confidence using a binomial test reported as both a 0 to 1 score and a Phred-scaled value. For male probands, non-PAR X and Y calls were assigned deterministically (X assigned maternal, Y assigned paternal).

An important check of HAT-FLEX and SNOW is QC of the full DNV callset generated in this study, which I describe later. Here I discuss my use of publicly available WGS alignment and DNV call data from prior studies [21,22] to further benchmark the methods. Notably, these publicly available datasets were generated from blood-derived DNA rather than lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs), which are known to introduce cell line-specific artifacts that can confound DNV calling [23]. Therefore, this is an ideal testing dataset for benchmarking HAT-FLEX and SNOW. Since the source studies reporting DNV calls used the telomere-to-telomere (T2T) reference genome [24], I selected that dataset for benchmarking. I downloaded the DeepVariant-GLnexus callset derived from PacBio data, the DRAGEN callset derived from Illumina data, the PacBio BAM files for each individual, and the T2T reference genome. HAT-FLEX was applied to a single trio comprising child NA12877 and parents NA12889 and NA12890, using the DeepVariant-GLnexus and DRAGEN callsets as caller 1 and caller 2 in HAT-FLEX, respectively. All parameters were kept consistent with those used in the autism cohort analyses. Briefly, variants clustered on the same read were collapsed, additional allele-level validation was performed using the alignment data, and PacBio BAM files were used to phase the DNVs with SNOW.

The DNVs called in the ASD cohorts described above were annotated for their presence in the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (HPRC) [25,26]. First, the decomposed VCF from the HPRC was downloaded fromhttps://s3-us-west-2.amazonaws.com/human-pangenomics/pangenomes/freeze/freeze1/minigraph-cactus/hprc-v1.1-mc-grch38/hprc-v1.1-mc-grch38.vcfbub.a100k.wave.vcf.gz. Each DNV was tested for pangenome-graph membership usingsnow pangenomecheck, which queries each DNV against the pangenome VCF for presence or absence at the site and/or the allele level. DNVs present at the site-level in the pangenome VCF were removed from further analyses. QC of the DNVs was performed using acorn [27] (Supplementary FigureS1, Supplementary FigureS2, Supplementary FigureS3, Supplementary FigureS4, Supplementary FigureS5, Supplementary FigureS6). DNVs were than annotated with Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) [28] for predicted functional consequences. DNVs that are SNVs were summarized as an SBS96 mutational spectrum using deconstructSigs [29]. Single base substitution signatures were visualized by hierarchical clustering heatmaps (individuals × signatures) using heatmap.2 [30] with dendrograms for both rows and columns. For any analyses involving exome data, to ensure comparable callability across samples, analyses were restricted to DNVs in the targeted capture regions. To assess coverage across the capture regions, I used mosdepth [31] to calculate the mean sequencing depth for each individual in each region. I then aggregated the data to identify regions with sufficient coverage: at least 10 × coverage in more than 90% of individuals on the autosomes, at least 10 × coverage in more than 90% of females on the X chromosome, and at least 5 × coverage in 90% of males on each of the X and Y chromosomes.

Missense and LOF DNVs were assessed for gene set enrichment using the denovolyzeByClass function in denovolyzeR [32]. The analyses evaluated enrichment across eight gene sets including (1) all genes, (2) non-PAR genes on the X chromosome, (3) non-PAR genes on the Y chromosome, (4) PAR genes, (5) hormone receptor genes from the GtoPdb [33] accessed athttps://www.guidetopharmacology.org/GRAC/NHRListForward, (6) genes exhibiting sex-bias in gene expression in the brain [34], (7) genes present in the SFARI Gene database (release 2025 Q4) (https://gene.sfari.org/), and (8) 100 random genes exclusive of the SFARI gene list. For each gene set, enrichment was tested for synonymous DNVs, missense DNVs, and LOF DNVs.

I analyzed four DNV datasets stratified by proband sex (male/female) and affection status (affected/unaffected). After basic cleaning and filtering (including harmonizing gene symbols), each dataset was formatted for denovolyzeR for gene set enrichment tests. A gene set was considered significant if it passed Bonferroni correction for 24 tests (p< 0.002).

Since denovolyzeR does not stratify expected DNV counts by child sex, I adjusted expectations for non-PAR genes on the X and Y chromosomes using a parent-of-origin model with male:female germline mutation-rate ratio\(r={\mu }_{\text{male}}/{\mu }_{\text{female}}=3\)(i.e., paternal fraction\(=r/(1+r)=0.75\)). denovolyzeR’s baseline expectation is\(E\propto 2N{p}_{\text{DNM}}\), where\(N\)is the number of probands and\({p}_{\text{DNM}}\)is the per-gene, per-class probability from denovolyzeR’s internal table; let\(N={N}_{m}+{N}_{f}\)with\({N}_{m}\)males and\({N}_{f}\)females. Relative to the autosomal baseline\(\propto ({\mu }_{\text{male}}+{\mu }_{\text{female}})\), transmission implies for non-PAR loci: male X\(\propto {\mu }_{\text{female}}\)and male Y\(\propto {\mu }_{\text{male}}\), yielding multipliers\({x}_{\text{male}}=1/(1+r)\)and\({y}_{\text{male}}=r/(1+r)\); female multipliers are\({x}_{\text{female}}=1.0\)and\({y}_{\text{female}}=0.0\). For mixed-sex groups,\({x}_{\text{mult}}=({N}_{f}\cdot 1.0+{N}_{m}\cdot {x}_{\text{male}})/N\)and\({y}_{\text{mult}}=({N}_{m}\cdot {y}_{\text{male}})/N\). PAR genes were unadjusted (multiplier\(=1.0\)). Implementation-wise, I multiplied the numeric columns of denovolyzeR’s probability table by gene-specific multipliers and supplied the adjusted table to denovolyzeByClass via the probTable argument; genes without a specified multiplier were left unchanged (multiplier = 1.0).

Per-gene DNV enrichment was tested separately within each sex × affection stratum using denovolyzeR (denovolyzeByGene) for LOF, missense, and LOF + missense classes, with non-PAR X/Y expectations adjusted as described above. Genes with ap-value less than 2.5 × 10−6were considered exome-wide significant.

As a complementary analysis, I tested per-gene enrichment using two-sided Fisher’s exact tests on allelic variant counts for DNVs that were missense or LOF. For each gene and chromosomal category (autosomes, PAR, X non-PAR), I constructed four pairwise comparisons: (1) affected females vs affected males, (2) unaffected females vs unaffected males, (3) affected females vs unaffected females, and (4) affected males vs unaffected males. For each comparison, I formed a 2 × 2 table of variant alleles vs non-variant alleles between the two groups, where the group-specific denominator equaled the number of allele copies evaluated given cohort size and chromosome copy number (autosomes/PAR: 2N per group; X non-PAR: 2N in females and N in males). Non-PAR Y genes were not included in between-group comparisons. Genes with ap-value less than 2.5 × 10−6were considered exome-wide significant.

Child affection status was analyzed with child-level probit regression under a latent-liability framework (Additional details inSupplementary Methods). Briefly, DNVs were aggregated by child, mutation class, and genomic region, and grouped as LOF, missense, or synonymous (negative control) variants in autosomal and X non-PAR regions. Expected burden was derived from denovolyzeR gene-level mutation probabilities, with sex-specific adjustment for X non-PAR mutation rates. Primary predictors were observed-minus-expected excess burden terms for damaging and synonymous variant classes. Additive models included damaging burden, synonymous burden, and sex; interaction models additionally tested sex-by-burden effects.

Promoters were tested for enrichment of DNVs using fitDNM [35,36]. They were defined as 2000 bp upstream of the transcription start site of protein-coding genes from the RefSeq database downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser [37,38]. Due to the discovery, in this study, ofRNU2-2, in a genome-wide significant promoter I also tested for enrichment of DNVs in exonic coordinates of protein-coding and noncoding genes, respectively (see below).

I implemented a custom pipeline (based on previously described approaches [35,36,39]) to test for enrichment of DNVs in exonic regions of protein-coding and noncoding genes. Briefly, I (i) estimated gene-level mutability from trinucleotide-based substitution rates using a reference genome and RefSeq gene models, (ii) counted unique DNVs overlapping exons for each gene, and (iii) assessed enrichment by comparing observed counts to expectations under a Poisson model (\(\lambda =2\times {n}_{\text{adj}}\times \mu \), with\({n}_{\text{adj}}\)adjusted for sex-chromosome copy number). Enrichment was summarized using one-sided Poissonp-values and observed-to-expected ratios.

Throughout the remainder of the paper the following acronyms are used to represent the cohorts. “SSC WGS” refers to whole-genome sequencing data from the Simons Simplex Collection, “SPARK WGS” refers to whole-genome sequencing data from the SPARK cohort, and “SPARK WES” refers to whole-exome sequencing data from the SPARK cohort. For the SSC WGS cohort, I identified 277,345 SNVs, 41,369 INDELS (15,653 deletions, 25,716 insertions), and 2611 MNVs (Fig.1A, Supplementary TableS2). The mean ± standard deviation of length for the deletions were 4.48 ± 4.88 bp, for insertions was 5.03 ± 5.39 bp, and for MNVs was 14.05 ± 20.52 bp. For the SPARK WGS cohort, I identified 392,037 SNVs, 47,503 INDELS (20,294 deletions, 27,209 insertions), and 3241 MNVs (Fig.1A, Supplementary TableS3). The mean ± standard deviation of length for the deletions were 4.69 ± 5.16 bp, for insertions was 5.07 ± 5.67 bp, and for MNVs was 12.53 ± 18.87 bp. For the SPARK WES cohort, I identified 46,802 SNVs, 2517 INDELS (1015 deletions, 1502 insertions), and 312 MNVs (Fig.1A, Supplementary TableS4). The mean ± standard deviation of length for the deletions were 5.21 ± 5.12 bp, for insertions was 4.71 ± 3.64 bp, and for MNVs was 9.25 ± 12.63 bp (Fig.1A). Please note for any analyses of coding regions across SSC WGS, SPARK WGS, and SPARK WES, I used the subset of WES coordinates (Supplementary TableS5) with high coverage, across most of the samples, as shown in Supplementary TableS6. This resulted in the removal of 4677 DNVs that were in lowly covered exome regions (Supplementary TableS7).

Several metrics were considered for determination of high quality DNVs (Fig.1A). First, the number of DNVs per individual was calculated and was 76.82 ± 14.27 in SSC WGS, 78.16 ± 15.94 in SPARK WGS, and 1.59 ± 1.30 in SPARK WES. Second the Ti/Tv ratio was calculated and was 2.25 in SSC WGS, 2.27 in SPARK WGS, and 2.81 in SPARK WES. QC metrics on the DNV dataset show that HAT-FLEX and SNOW generate high-quality DNVs. To further assess the quality of these tools I pursued benchmarking of HAT-FLEX and SNOW on publicly available, high-quality WGS data, and found that both methods perform well (Supplementary TableS9). In one trio with NA12877 as the child, the previous publication [22] reported 72 DNVs identified across three sequencing technologies: PacBio, Illumina, and ONT. Since HAT-FLEX uses at most two callers, I used the publicly available genome-wide PacBio and Illumina VCFs as the two input callsets for intersection-level calling with HAT-FLEX and the PacBio alignment files as input for SNOW. Consequently, some DNVs reported in the previous study were not represented in the HAT-FLEX/SNOW input data because they were supported only by ONT. Of the 72 DNVs reported in the previous paper, HAT-FLEX/SNOW identified 54. Among the 18 DNVs not recovered, four were flagged as LowGQ by HAT-FLEX, three were absent from the publicly available Illumina genome-wide VCF, two were absent from both the publicly available Illumina and PacBio genome-wide VCFs, and two were genotyped as 0/0 in all family members in one publicly available genome-wide VCF but appeared de novo in the other publicly available genome-wide VCF. The remaining seven cases reflected a range of discordances between datasets: one variant was inherited in the publicly available PacBio genome-wide VCF, one was inherited in the publicly available PacBio genome-wide VCF but absent from the publicly available Illumina genome-wide VCF, one was inherited in the publicly available Illumina genome-wide VCF but appeared de novo in the publicly available PacBio genome-wide VCF, one was missing from the publicly available Illumina genome-wide VCF, one had a missing child genotype (./.) in the publicly available PacBio genome-wide VCF, and one appeared de novo because of a complex allele-matching issue compounded by LowGQ in the publicly available genome-wide VCF. Overall, these discrepancies are driven primarily by unavailability of high-quality information to call DNVs in the intersection of the publicly available genome-wide Illumina and PacBio VCFs. None of the ones missed by HAT-FLEX/SNOW were high-quality DNVs at the intersection of Illumina and PacBio data. In addition, HAT-FLEX/SNOW identified 4 DNVs that were not reported in the previous publication. All four appeared high confidence upon visual inspection of the reads. Two of these differences may be explained by the use of a different Y chromosome reference in the previous study [22]. To compare phasing performance on the 54 shared DNVs, I found that SNOW assigned parent-of-origin for all but one variant; the single exception failed phasing because of SNOW phaser’s genotype quality requirement for phase-by-transmission variants. By contrast, the previous publication phased only 46 of these variants, and the parent-of-origin assignments were identical for all variants phased by both SNOW and the previous publication [22]. Overall, SNOW phased 7 additional variants relative to the previous publication (Supplementary TableS10). In total HAT-FLEX/SNOW contained 57 DNVs as two of the DNVs were collapsed to one DNV usingsnow consolidate(Supplementary TableS11). HAT-FLEX and SNOW demonstrated robust accuracy on high-quality benchmark WGS data, recovering the great majority of evaluable published DNVs, identifying additional high-confidence events not reported previously, and achieving fully concordant but more complete parent-of-origin phasing.

In this study, I investigated DNVs in ASD, with a particular focus on differences by sex and diagnostic status. ASD shows a sex bias, with males affected more frequently than females. Previous investigations of DNVs related to sex bias in ASD have often included individuals with developmental disorders more broadly, whereas this current study focuses on the primary ASD phenotype. Using data from three ASD family cohorts, I generated uniform high-quality DNV callsets for individuals with and without ASD. These callsets enabled a range of complementary analyses, including: 1) testing enrichment across variant and gene-class categories; 2) identifying genes reaching exome-wide significance in males with ASD, females with ASD, males without ASD, and females without ASD; 3) directly comparing DNV enrichment between males and females across multiple comparison groups; 4) estimating rates of LOF and missense DNV alleles, 5) examining burden of LOF and missense DNVs, and 6) evaluating the enrichment of noncoding variants. Importantly, I performed analyses that were aware of sex chromosomes including PARs.

The foundation of this study is in the generation of a high-quality DNV callset. This was done by developing a sex-aware DNV caller (HAT-FLEX) and another tool for further optimizing DNV calls (SNOW). Rather than filtering DNVs using genomic annotations (e.g., repeat regions), I used intensive assessment of each candidate DNV, through SNOW, to produce a robust, high confidence callset. This opens up new regions of the genome to investigation in the context of ASD. After generating the high-confidence DNV callset, I tested enrichment across predefined gene sets stratified by DNV class. As expected, synonymous DNVs showed no significant gene-set enrichments. Consistent with prior observations, I observed enrichment of missense and LOF DNVs in individuals with ASD. Additional enrichments were detected in females, including gene sets on the non-PAR regions of the X chromosome and genes with sex-biased expression in the brain. Several gene sets are relatively small (e.g., hormone receptor genes); although they showed large enrichment, they did not remain significant after multiple-testing correction. These signals may warrant followup in larger cohorts.

Analyses of gene-specific enrichments by sex and diagnostic category, were insightful. Few genes met enrichment in any category in individualswithoutASD as one would expect. Several genes were enriched in individuals with ASD and by performing sex-stratified analyses I could pinpoint genes that were enriched only in one sex for the DNV class being tested. These genes included genes previously implicated in ASD and some newly implicated genes. Those specific to males includedFOXP1, SMAD6, AUTS2, CCDC168, PIEZO1, EML6, ZNF84, IGSF23, OTOG, NHSL1, ADNP,andFREM3and to females includedTAOK1, MECP2,andDDX3X. BothMECP2andDDX3Xare well-established genes with known enrichment in females. Some genes of interest from these lists include the newly implicatedPIEZO1, which is a mechanosensitive ion channel and is important in the brain [43,44] andTAOK1, which is involved in a neurodevelopmental disorder [45] wherein it has recently been reported that endocrinopathies are also common [46]. One intriguing gene in the comparison of males with ASD to females with ASD was the geneGABBR2with enrichment of DNVs in females. While not exome-wide significant, this is a gene that may be of interest in future studies as it is the only one that was not an X chromosome gene that was close to significance. The neurodevelopmental disorder associated withGABBR2variants has been documented to resemble Rett syndrome [47] (typically caused by variants in the X-linked geneMECP2). Additionally,GABBR2variants have been reported in association with Rett-like phenotypes in individuals with classical Rett syndrome caused byMECP2variants [48].

The probit analyses provide an additional way to interpret my findings within a latent-liability framework. In this setting, the results suggest that the observed sex difference is unlikely to be explained simply by differences in mutation rate. Even after normalizing de novo burden using observed-minus-expected counts, including sex-specific expectations for X-linked variants, damaging variation remained associated with affection status, indicating that mutational opportunity alone does not fully account for the pattern we observed. The absence of a similar association for synonymous variants further supports the specificity of this signal and makes a broad technical artifact or general ascertainment effect less likely, although diagnostic bias cannot be completely excluded on the basis of genetic data alone. At the same time, the substantially lower baseline for females in the probit model, together with damaging-burden effects that were comparable to or even stronger than those in males, is most consistent with a female protective effect interpretation. On the liability scale, this implies that females may need to accumulate a greater overall burden before crossing the threshold for diagnosis, offering a coherent explanation for the sex disparity seen in our cohort.

In the final part of this study, I focused on promoters as they have previously been implicated in bulk as important for ASD [49,50]. Herein, I queried each promoter for significant excess of DNVs and identified theWDR74promoter as significant specifically in males with ASD. By inspection of the promoter in the UCSC Genome Browser [38], I found that theRNU2-2noncoding RNA gene was located within this promoter and nearly all the DNVs were within it. This led me to hypothesize that the signal may be from this gene, and I found thatRNU2-2was also genome-wide significant for excess DNVs.RNU2-2DNVs are present in 0.2% of males with ASD. I further found that DNVs occur in females with ASD as well and the overall rate is also at 0.2% of individuals with ASD. There were two males without ASD that also had a DNV leading to an overall rate of 0.1% in unaffected males. Assessment of females without ASD identified no DNVs in this region. Overall, 0.05% of individuals without ASD have DNVs in this noncoding gene. These results indicate thatRNU2-2may be an important gene related to ASD in addition to the severe neurodevelopmental disorder for which it is already associated with in the literature [51]. Further investigation of DNVs in the exonic regions of protein-coding and noncoding genes identifiesRNU2-2as the top gene for enrichment of DNVs in ASD. More broadly spliceosomal RNA variants have been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders and represent an emerging class of noncoding risk signals. Among the spliceosomal RNA genes linked to neurodevelopmental disorders areRNU4-2[52],RNU2-2[51],RNU4ATAC[53], andRNU5B-1[54]. In the present study, onlyRNU2-2showed significant enrichment for DNVs.RNU2-2encodes a U2 snRNA expressed in the cortex early in development in timepoints relevant to autism (e.g., the 18–20 post-conception week window and the 1- to 4-year-old postnatal ages windows) (Supplementary FigureS18). Disruption of splicing in the developing cortex could preferentially affect long, multi-exonic genes by altering exon usage. Given that ASD-associated genes are often long and contain numerous exons [55], this mechanism may increase transcriptional variability and contribute to phenotypic heterogeneity in ASD.

Overall, this study establishes sex as an important modifier of the contribution of DNVs to ASD risk and provides further support for a female protective effect, with females appearing to require a greater burden of damaging variation to reach comparable ASD liability. The findings also suggest that ASD risk is shaped, in part, by sex-specific patterns at the gene level, indicating that important signals may be obscured when males with ASD and females with ASD are analyzed together. In addition to these coding-sequence differences, the identification ofRNU2-2within theWDR74promoter implicates noncoding variation in ASD susceptibility and broadens the spectrum of ASD-associated variation. These results are important to the field because they support a more complete model of ASD genetic architecture that incorporates both sex-dependent effects and contributions from noncoding sequence. Moreover, HAT-FLEX and SNOW increase the broader utility of this work by providing a sex-aware, high-confidence framework for DNV discovery and evaluation that can support future family-based sequencing studies. Collectively, this study deepens our understanding of the genetic basis of ASD and its pronounced sex bias, while offering insights that may inform and support broader research across the genomics community.
