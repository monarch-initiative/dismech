# Progressive Myoclonic Epilepsies - A Pragmatic Review.
**Authors:** Cherian A, Divya KP
**Journal:** Neurol India (2026)
**DOI:** [10.4103/neurol-india.Neurol-India-D-25-00075](https://doi.org/10.4103/neurol-india.Neurol-India-D-25-00075)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Progressive Myoclonus Epilepsy (PME) is a rare and complex group of inherited neurodegenerative disorders characterized by progressively worsening myoclonus, cognitive impairment, tonic-clonic seizures and ataxia. The clinical features and genetic underpinnings of PME are diverse, with approximately 80% of individuals now able to receive a molecular diagnosis. This review outlines the clinical phenotypes, genotypes, and management strategies for PME. Literature search for publications on PME in the preceding 20 years, with emphasis for the past one decade, performed using Medline, JSTOR (journal storage) and PubMed databases.

DISCUSSION: In PME progression of symptoms can vary widely among patients, with some experiencing rapid deterioration while others may have a slower rate of decline. Lafora Disease, characterized by the presence of Lafora bodies in tissues, Unverricht-Lundborg Disease (EPM1), caused by mutations in the CSTB gene, Myoclonic Epilepsy with Ragged-Red Fibers (MERRF), a mitochondrial disorder and Neuronal Ceroid Lipofuscinoses (NCL) make up the major chunk of PME syndromes. In the workup of PME, certain clinical and electroencephalogram (EEG) findings can help differentiate the specific etiologies. Valproic acid, perampanel, phenobarbitone and zonisamide are frequently prescribed as a treatment for various seizure types associated with PME. They are effective for managing both myoclonic and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. However, patients often have a progressive course and may find their myoclonus resistant to treatment.

CONCLUSION: This review explores the clinical features and genetic factors associated with the more prevalent as well as recently described forms of PME for effective clinical evaluation, and suggests management strategies for this challenging condition.

Progressive myoclonus epilepsy (PME) encompasses a group of rare epilepsy syndromes characterized by a combination of myoclonus, cognitive impairment, generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCS), and ataxia.[1]These conditions typically emerge in childhood or adolescence and lead to significant functional impairment as symptoms worsen over time. Cognitive decline and neuropsychiatric issues may also be present, although their severity can vary widely among individuals. The key clinical features include:

(a) Myoclonus: Myoclonus is the defining feature of PME. It manifests as involuntary muscle jerks that can be triggered by various stimuli, such as light, sound, touch, or specific movements. This type of myoclonus is often referred to as stimulus-sensitive or reflex myoclonus and can be multifocal.[1]The action myoclonus in PME is typically multifocal, more often distal, and stimulus-sensitive. Patients frequently find this symptom more debilitating than GTCS [Video 1].(b) Tonic-clonic seizures: In addition to myoclonus, individuals with PME may experience different seizure types, including tonic-clonic seizures. The relationship between myoclonic jerks and seizures is distinct in PME; while myoclonus is prevalent, it does not always correlate with tonic-clonic seizure activity.(c) Ataxia: Ataxia, or impaired coordination due to cerebellar involvement, is another common symptom that can develop alongside seizures and myoclonus.(d) Cognitive and neuropsychiatric manifestations: Cognitive decline is variable among patients, with some experiencing significant challenges over time. Additionally, neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and psychosis are frequently reported in this population.[1]

In the initial stages, the most common differentials of PME are familial adult myoclonus epilepsy (FAME) and resistant juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME).[2]

PME is a chronic, progressive disorder. Most patients experience worsening myoclonus, cognitive decline, and neurological disability over time. Disease progression is variable and can vary significantly among individuals and specific PME syndromes. Sodium valproate is typically the first drug of choice due to its broad-spectrum efficacy, and clonazepam, zonisamide, phenobarbitone, and perampanel are commonly used as add-on antiseizure medications (ASM). Some patients remain relatively stable and autonomous for many years and are mistakenly diagnosed as JME, while others may become severely disabled, wheelchair-bound, or dependent for basic activities of daily living within a decade or less. Death is often due to complications such as respiratory infections or aspiration pneumonia.

PME is associated with numerous genetic mutations, with nearly 50 known causes identified so far.[3]The most common form worldwide is Unverricht–Lundborg disease (ULD), particularly noted for its higher prevalence in Finland (approximately 1.9 per 100,000).[4]Other significant causes include as follows: Lafora disease (LD) (with a prevalence of 1 to 9 per million), NCLs, and myoclonic epilepsy with ragged-red fibers (MERRF). The distribution of these genetic forms can vary geographically due to factors such as consanguinity and local genetic founder effects. With the greater availability of genetic testing, a definitive diagnosis can now be ascertained in 80% of PME patients.[5]

A large single-center study from South India had 147 patients with PME, of which NCL (44.2%) was the most common, followed by LD (36.7%), ULD (5.4%), MERRF (6.8%), and Tay–Sach’s disease (6.8%). These patients were diagnosed based on histopathological diagnosis. The study showed a male preponderance (M: F = 1.8:1) with a mean age of onset of illness of 10.7 ± 8.2 years, with a consanguineous parentage in 61.8% of the cohort. Most of the patients presented with a triad of cognitive decline, myoclonus, and neurological deficits.[6]Most of the data on PME syndromes in India are from case reports and case series. The availability of genetic testing has aided in early confirmation of diagnosis in PME syndromes.[7]

Unverricht–Lundborg disease (ULD), also referred to as progressive myoclonic epilepsy type 1 (EPM1), is an autosomal recessive neurological disorder primarily linked to mutations in theCSTBgene, which encodes cystatin B. The majority of ULD cases arise from homozygous or compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in theCSTBgene located in 21q22.3. A total of 90% of affected individuals worldwide have an unstable expansion of a 12-nucleotide dodecamer repeat (5′-CCCCGCCCCGCG-3′) within this gene. Individuals with ULD typically exhibit at least 30 copies of this repeat sequence, with some alleles containing between 30 and 80 repeats. In contrast, normal alleles usually possess only two or three repeats. The length of the CSTB expansion has a modulating effect on the age of disease onset, myoclonus severity, and neurophysiological markers.[8]While missense pathogenic variants inCSTBdo occur, they represent a smaller fraction of cases. Those who are compound heterozygotes with one missense variant and one repeat expansion often display more severe disease characteristics. Cystatin B acts as an inhibitor for cathepsins B, H, and L, which are involved in various cellular functions. When CSTB is deficient, it can lead to significant neuronal excitability and impaired functioning of cortical networks.[8]

ULD is characterized by symptoms such as myoclonus, seizures, and progressive neurological deterioration. Symptoms generally manifest during late childhood to adolescence, with the peak onset around the early second decade. Medication-refractory reflex myoclonus, especially in the form of action myoclonus, poses a considerable challenge and is often a major contributor to functional disability. GTCS is the next most frequently observed seizure type, which usually responds well to ASM. GTCS typically occur during sleep or upon awakening. EEG may also show photoparoxysmal response (PPR).[9]Ataxia progresses as the disease advances. Cognitive abilities are largely maintained, although there can be subtle deficits in processing speed and executive function.[10]Therefore, ULD may be mistaken for JME in the initial phases of the disease. Psychiatric issues such as depression and suicidal tendencies are often seen in ULD patients, and they tend to have a shortened life span, with a median age at death of 53.9 years.[11]

The application of oligonucleotide therapeutics, particularly locked nucleic acid (LNA) antisense oligonucleotides, is an innovative strategy for gene modulation aimed at correcting splicing abnormalities. This approach specifically targets cryptic splice sites, such as a hidden 5′ splice site in intron 1, to restore the normal splicing process.[12]

LD is a rare autosomal recessive disorder primarily characterized by severe myoclonic epilepsy and progressive neurodegeneration. It results from loss-of-function mutations in two key genes:EPM2A, which encodes the enzyme laforin, andNHLRC1(also referred to asEPM2B), which encodes Malin. Recent research has identified a third gene,PRDM8(Pr Domain-Containing Protein 8), associated with early-onset forms of this disorder.[13]Among the genes implicated in LD,EPM2Ais notable for harboring the most common mutation, R241X, which occurs in about 17% of patients. Additionally, large deletions in this gene account for approximately 10–15% of cases.[13]InEPM2Bgene, the most frequently observed mutations are the missense mutation P69A and the frameshift mutation G158fs16. LD has a high geographic prevalence in certain regions, including Mediterranean countries such as Spain, Italy, and France, Northern Africa, Southern India, and the Middle East Asia.[13]

Mutations inEPM2Alead to the absence or dysfunction of laforin, disrupting normal glycogen metabolism. This dysfunction results in the accumulation of abnormally branched glycogen, forming insoluble aggregates known as Lafora bodies. Mutations inNHLRC1also affect glycogen metabolism but are often associated with a milder clinical course and a longer life expectancy, particularly with the missense variant D146N. Laforin and Malin work together to regulate the synthesis and breakdown of glycogen.[14]Laforin is a phosphatase enzyme that removes phosphate groups from glycogen, while Malin, a ubiquitin E3 ligase, is involved in tagging proteins for degradation through ubiquitination. Their cooperative function is essential for preventing the excessive branching of glycogen, which is crucial for maintaining its soluble form. A deficiency in either protein leads to the accumulation of hyperphosphorylated glycogen, resulting in the formation of Lafora bodies that disrupt cellular functions across various tissues, including the brain, liver, and muscles.[15]

The clinical onset of LD is between 8 and 19 years of age, with an average onset of around 14 to 16 years. Focal seizures of occipital origin often represent the initial signs of the disease, leading to visual disturbances such as transient blindness or hallucinations. This is followed by myoclonic seizures and GTCS; absences and atonic drops can also occur [Video 2]. The disease is associated with a rapid progression of dementia, ataxia, apraxia, visual impairment, and various neuropsychiatric issues, along with status epilepticus. Median survival is 10 years following symptom onset. Cases that begin after the age of 18 years tend to progress more slowly and have a longer duration.[16]

Focal occipital spikes are very characteristic of the disease. EEG often shows long bursts of generalized spikes and polyspikes, associated with myoclonic jerks enhanced by low-frequency photic stimulation [Figure 2].[16]

NCLs are characterized by the accumulation of lipopigments in lysosomes. This buildup leads to progressive neurological deterioration, primarily affecting children, during infancy or early childhood. The clinical manifestations of NCL include seizures, visual impairment, cognitive decline, and motor function deterioration, often resulting in severe disability and premature death in late adolescence or early adulthood.[17]The genetic factors underlying NCL involve mutations in genes responsible for lysosomal enzymes or related proteins. A total of 13 distinct genes contribute to NCL [Table 2]. Most NCLs are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner except for CLN4.[17]

Genes associated with human neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis

Each variant is designated by a ceroid lipofuscinosis, neuronal (CLN) number (e.g. CLN1 through CLN14), corresponding to specific genetic mutations that disrupt lysosomal function. Notable forms include CLN2 disease caused by mutations in theTPP1gene. This variant is associated with significant vision loss and language delays. It is unique among NCL for having a disease-modifying treatment available.[18]CLN3mutations are responsible for a significant portion of juvenile Batten disease, whereasDNAJC5(CLN4) is associated with adult-onset forms of the disease.[19]Adult-onset presentation of NCL is termed Kufs disease (KD) and has two types: Type A (PME phenotype) and type B (dementia with motor disturbances) have been described. CLN6 disease can present as type A or type B KD, and the latter subtype can show excellent response to levodopa.[19]Progressive visual impairment is common and may be the initial symptom observed in infantile and juvenile NCL. KD presents differently, typically preserving vision while still resulting in cognitive decline.[19]

EEG shows focal occipital IEDs and PPR at low frequency, especially in CLN2 and CLN6. MRI shows cerebral and cerebellar atrophy due to neuronal loss, along with T2/FLAIR hyperintense signals in the posterior white matter. Skin biopsy shows granular lipopigments in CLN1 and CLN10, curvilinear profiles in CLN2, fingerprint profiles in CLN3, and a combination of these in other forms.[20]

The US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency have authorized the use of cerliponase alfa (marketed as Brineura), an enzyme replacement therapy specifically designed for NCL2. This treatment is administered directly into the cerebrospinal fluid through an intracerebroventricular route, using an infusion pump enabling targeted intervention for this neurodegenerative condition linked to a deficiency in the enzyme tripeptidyl peptidase 1 (TPP1). Cerliponase alfa is a treatment that can potentially slow disease progression.[21]

PME linked toKCTD7mutations is a rare genetic disorder, reported from a variety of ethnic backgrounds with a high degree of consanguinity that typically manifests in early childhood.

This condition arises from homozygous mutations in the KCTD7 gene, which is located on chromosome 7q11.21.[22]KCTD7 protein plays a vital role in regulating potassium channels, essential for maintaining proper neuronal excitability. Mutations in this gene disrupt these functions, leading to increased neuronal excitability and seizures. Onset generally occurs between 5 months and 3 years of age, often following a period of normal or slightly delayed psychomotor development. Features include severe seizures primarily manifesting as myoclonus and GTCS, cognitive decline, and movement disorders (ataxia, tremor, dystonia, and chorea). Other seizure types include generalized clonic, tonic, atonic, and atypical absences.[23]Frequent multifocal and generalized spike-wave patterns, especially in the posterior regions, are the abnormal EEG findings. The epilepsy associated withKCTD7mutations is often resistant to treatment.[24]

MERRF is a mitochondrial disorder primarily caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), the most common being the m.8344A>G variant in theMT-TKgene, which encodes tRNA for lysine. This particular variant is found in 80% to 90% of patients diagnosed with the condition. The mutation disrupts the synthesis of mitochondrial proteins, leading to energy production deficiencies that predominantly affect high-energy-demand tissues, such as the brain and muscles. MERRF has a heterogeneous phenotype similar to other mitochondrial disorders.[25]The main clinical features include myoclonus, GTCS, myopathy, ataxia, sensori-neural hearing loss, optic atrophy, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive decline, and short stature. Less frequently, cardiomyopathy, diabetes mellitus, retinitis pigmentosa, progressive external ophthalmoplegia, and multiple lipomas are often located on the neck or trunk.[25]MERRF typically begins in childhood, although cases with adult onset have been reported. The disorder is inherited maternally since mtDNA is passed down from mother to child. The variability in clinical expression and penetrance can make diagnosis challenging. MERRF shares phenotypic characteristics with other mitochondrial disorders, particularly mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS), though MELAS is often characterized by acute neurological episodes.

Fabbriet al.[27]have investigated the potential therapeutic applications of the bacterial protein cytotoxic necrotizing factor 1 (CNF1) in the context of mitochondrial diseases. CNF1, which originates fromEscherichia coli, is recognized for its ability to activate Rho GTPases—molecules that are vital for numerous cellular functions, including the ability to boost mitochondrial ATP production in cells, promote healthier mitochondrial shapes, which are often altered in mitochondrial diseases, and improve respiratory chain complex function.

Myoclonus epilepsy and ataxia due to potassium channel mutation (MEAK) is a specific form of PME linked to ade novoheterozygous pathogenic variant in theKCNC1gene, particularly the R320H variant. This genetic alteration results in a dominant-negative loss-of-function effect on Kv3.1 potassium channels, which are crucial for regulating neuronal excitability, especially in GABAergic interneurons within the central nervous system (CNS). The R320H variant disrupts the normal function of these channels, leading to the disinhibition of excitatory pathways. This disruption is the key factor in the development of the symptoms associated with MEAK, including myoclonus and ataxia. While most cases are sporadic due to de novo mutations, some families exhibit autosomal dominant inheritance.[28]

Patients have a normal early development and symptoms of MEAK typically begin between the ages of 6 and 14 years, with myoclonus being the most significant clinical feature. This myoclonus often intensifies during adolescence and can severely affect mobility. Although GTCS may occur occasionally, cognitive abilities generally remain intact throughout the progression of the disorder. EEG may show generalized epileptiform activity and occasional photosensitivity, and brain imaging can reveal cerebellar atrophy. Patients with MEAK usually do not face early mortality, though they may encounter considerable challenges related to mobility and overall quality of life as their symptoms evolve. The initial evolution of MEAK closely mimics ULD; however, MEAK usually has a more severe course. Treatment primarily focuses on managing symptoms with ASMs such as valproate.[29]

Sialidosis is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in theNEU1gene, which leads to a deficiency in the enzyme N-acetyl neuraminidase, leading to abnormal accumulation of sialic acid-rich substances within lysosomes, particularly affecting the CNS and other organs. Sialidosis is classified into two main types based on age of onset, clinical features, and progression.[30]

In type I sialidosis (Cherry-Red Spot Myoclonus Syndrome/normomorphic), symptoms generally appear between the ages of 10 and 20 years, as they have some residual sialidase activity. Clinical features include myoclonus, ataxia, seizures, and visual impairment. Perampanel improves the myoclonus in sialidosis.[31]Type I is typically less severe and diagnosed later in life compared to type II. Patients often retain normal cognitive function and life expectancy, although mobility may decline over time, potentially requiring wheelchair assistance.[32]

Symptoms in type II (dysmorphic) occur often earlier than in type I, often as congenital or infantile forms. Type II has more severe symptoms such as hepatosplenomegaly, skeletal abnormalities (dysostosis multiplex), and cognitive impairments. Patients may also exhibit distinctive coarse facial features and other systemic complications and have a prognosis worse than type I, with many individuals facing significant developmental challenges. A defining characteristic of both types is the presence of a cherry-red spot on fundoscopy, indicating storage material accumulation in retinal ganglion cells, leading to progressive vision loss.[32]

Familial encephalopathy with neuroserpin inclusion bodies (FENIB) is an uncommon progressive genetic disorder manifesting with dementia and myoclonic epilepsy. This is an autosomal dominant condition due to mutations in theSERPINI1gene, located on chromosome 3q26, which encodes neuroserpin, a serine protease inhibitor vital for maintaining neuronal function and health. Neuroserpin not only inhibits proteases but also plays a role in cognitive functions such as learning and memory by regulating glutamatergic neurotransmission at NMDA receptors. Dysfunction in this pathway may contribute to some aspects of the epileptic symptoms seen in FENIB.[33]

TheSERPINI1mutations cause the production of an unstable form of neuroserpin that aggregates within neurons, resulting in the formation of structures known as Collins bodies. The presence of these inclusions disrupts normal cellular processes, leading to neuronal death and thereby clinical symptoms. This accumulation leads to gain-of-function neuronal dysfunction due to the buildup of these polymers within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of neurons. Furthermore, the loss of neuroserpin’s normal inhibitory function may exacerbate the dysregulation of proteases it typically controls, contributing to additional neuronal damage and increasing the risk of epilepsy.[34]

The severity of the condition is closely linked to specific mutations, with certain variants leading to earlier onset and more pronounced clinical features, including a higher frequency of seizures. Symptoms can begin as early as adolescence or later in life, often appearing in a person’s 40s or 50s. Early signs may include attention deficit, repetitive thought patterns, and changes in personality. As the disease progresses, cognitive impairment becomes more severe, affecting judgment and memory, followed by myoclonic seizures.[35]

Spinal muscular atrophy with progressive myoclonic epilepsy (SMA-PME) is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder primarily affecting children. It is characterized by a combination of lower motor neuron disease and PME, which arises due to mutations in theASAH1gene.[36]This gene encodes an enzyme known as acid ceramidase, crucial for lipid metabolism, particularly in the breakdown of ceramides within lysosomes. Mutations in theASAH1gene lead to decreased activity of acid ceramidase, resulting in an accumulation of ceramides within cells. This buildup is thought to contribute to neuronal damage and the clinical features observed in SMA-PME.[37]

The symptoms of SMA-PME manifest between the ages of 1 and 6 years. Initially, affected children may experience muscle weakness and atrophy, particularly in the legs. As the condition advances, weakness can extend to the arms and other areas, leading to significant mobility issues. In severe cases, respiratory muscles can also be involved. As muscle weakness progresses, patients often develop seizures characterized by myoclonic jerks and GTCS. These seizures are frequently resistant to conventional ASMs. EEG in SMA-PME typically shows epileptiform discharges triggered by hyperventilation. Brain MRI is most often normal or displays mild cortical atrophy. While cognitive deficits are usually mild, they can accompany other neurological symptoms. Some patients may also experience hearing loss due to damage to the auditory nerve. Diagnosis typically involves genetic testing to identifyASAH1mutations and assess acid ceramidase activity.[38]

Action myoclonus–renal failure syndrome (AMRF) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by PME and renal dysfunction, resulting from mutations in theSCARB2gene (Scavenger Receptor Class B, Member 2), which encodes the lysosomal integral membrane protein known as LIMP-2.[39]It was first identified in 1986 and has since been recognized worldwide. Symptoms typically emerge in late adolescence or early adulthood, often beginning with tremors in the fingers and hands that worsen during fine motor tasks. Patients experience myoclonic jerks triggered by voluntary movements, which can escalate to involuntary jerks even at rest. GTCS, ataxia, cerebellar dysarthria, and auditory impairments are seen.[40]Cognitive abilities usually remain intact until the later stages of the disease. Renal symptoms often present as proteinuria, which can progress to nephrotic syndrome and ultimately lead to end-stage renal failure. Some patients may not exhibit any renal symptoms, complicating the diagnostic process.[41]

Mutations in theSCARB2gene result in dysfunctional LIMP-2 protein, which disrupts lysosomal function and endosomal transport processes. This impairment is believed to contribute to both the neurological and renal symptoms associated with AMRF. Overproduction of LIMP-2 has been linked to enlarged endosomes and lysosomes, further indicating its vital role in maintaining the endolysosomal system. Diagnosis of AMRF is confirmed through genetic testing that identifies biallelic loss-of-function variants in theSCARB2gene. EEG typically reveals generalized epileptiform activity along with a progressive slowing of background brain activity over time. Brain imaging shows diffuse cerebral atrophy, but it often appears normal otherwise.[42]

The prognosis for individuals with AMRF is generally poor, with life expectancy typically ranging from 7 to 15 years following the onset of symptoms. While renal treatments such as dialysis or transplantation may enhance quality of life, neurological symptoms tend to progress despite these interventions.[41]

Progressive myoclonic epilepsy 6 (EPM6), previously denoted as “North Sea progressive myoclonus epilepsy,” is a rare autosomal recessive neurological disorder linked to mutations in theGOSR2gene, the most common variant being G144W. TheGOSR2gene plays a crucial role in vesicular transport within the Golgi apparatus. EPM6 typically presents in early childhood and progresses through a series of characteristic symptoms.[43]The disorder usually begins with ataxia by 2 years of age, and later patients develop action myoclonus and various types of seizures (GTCS, absences, and atonic). Cognitive abilities generally remain intact during earlier stages. By the second decade, many individuals lose the ability to walk independently. Other additional clinical features include areflexia, scoliosis, and elevated creatine kinase levels, which are unrelated to seizure-related muscle activity. EEG typically shows generalized spike-and-slow-wave discharges, predominantly in the posterior regions with photosensitivity. Nerve conduction studies show a mild, predominantly axonal peripheral neuropathy. MRI brain scans appear normal, although some patients may exhibit generalized atrophy of cerebral and cerebellar structures.[44]

Dentatorubral-pallidoluysian atrophy is a progressive neurologic disorder due to heterozygous pathogenic Cytosine, Adenine, Guanine (CAG) trinucleotide expansion in ATN1 gene. The age of onset is variable and ranges from infancy to late adulthood and is characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia, myoclonus, seizures, dementia, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and chorea.[45]Gaucher disease, a lysosomal storage disorder due to deficient glucocerebrosidase enzyme, can also present as PME. The other emergent genes that are attributed to PME phenotype includeIRF2BPL, DHDDS, ALG10, NUS1, CHD2, andTBC1D24.[1]

In the past, histopathology and ultrastructural examination of tissues like brain, skin, muscle, and liver were the mainstay of the diagnosis of PME syndrome. LD could be diagnosed by the presence of polyglucosan bodies called Lafora bodies in the apocrine glands of axillary skin biopsy, while MERRF was diagnosed based on the classical ragged-red fibers in muscle biopsy.[6]Electron microscopy aided ultrastructural examination of skin and peripheral blood lymphocytes showed granular bodies in infantile NCL, curvilinear bodies in late infantile NCL, fingerprint bodies in juvenile NCL, and a combination of inclusions in varied forms in adult-onset NCL.[6]Diagnosis of ULD was mostly a diagnosis of exclusion, with the clinical picture and when other types of PME were ruled out by appropriate histopathological methods.[7]However, with the wide availability of genetic testing, tissue (skin/muscle) biopsy is now primarily reserved for patients with nondiagnostic genetic testing or when genetic testing shows variants of uncertain significance.

Genetic testing has become the preferred initial diagnostic approach for PMEs as it has a high diagnostic success rate.[46]However, conditions like Unverricht–Lundborg disease (ULD) and Dentatorubral–pallidoluysian atrophy (DRPLA) often involve repeat expansions that are not effectively identified by standard sequencing methods. They require specialized techniques such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or Southern blotting. Also, standard gene panels and exome sequencing usually do not include mitochondrial DNA, potentially leading to missed diagnoses of MERRF. Many PMEs are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, suggesting that finding one pathogenic variant does not confirm a diagnosis.

Valproic acid is often the first-line ASM in treating PME due to its high effectiveness on myoclonus, seizures, and photosensitivity, except in MERRF. The first add-on treatments to be considered are levetiracetam and benzodiazepines (clonazepam and clobazam). Perampanel, phenobarbitone, and zonisamide are other frequently prescribed ASMs. Sodium channel blockers (phenytoin, carbamazepine, and oxcarbazepine) and GABAergic drugs (gabapentin, pregabalin, and vigabatrin) are to be avoided, as these drugs may exacerbate myoclonus and myoclonic seizures.[47]However, patients often have a progressive course and may find their myoclonus resistant to treatment. The ketogenic diet has been tried but was futile.[48]

Enzyme replacement therapy is already available for Gaucher’s disease and CLN2 and may be an option for targeting enzyme deficiencies, as in sialidosis and SMA-PME. Gene therapy and precision medicine are the future for PME, which could alter the natural course of the illness.[48]
