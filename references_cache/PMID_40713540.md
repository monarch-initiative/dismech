# Extremely rare case report of CEBALID syndrome presenting as congenital arthrogryposis.
**Authors:** Dirani FA, Kahil G, Jaber R, Slim Z, El Hajj R, Chokr I
**Journal:** BMC Pediatr (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12887-025-05949-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-025-05949-2)

## Content

BACKGROUND: CEBALID syndrome (Craniofacial defects, dysmorphic Ears, Brain Abnormalities, Language delay, and Intellectual Disability) is caused by C-terminal truncated transcriptional activator MN1. This novel syndrome characterized by neurodevelopmental delay, craniofacial anomalies, and partial rhombencephalosynapsis was recently identified in 2020. Since then, less than 30 cases have been reported worldwide. None of the reported cases described an association of arthrogryposis with CEBALID syndrome. Below we present the first reported case in Lebanon with an associated arthrogryposis at birth. CASE PRESENTATION: A case of a late preterm newborn girl admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for multiple joint contractures and facial dysmorphology. In addition, the patient had poor extra-uterine cardiopulmonary adaptation that required mechanical ventilation, and feeding difficulties were noted with hypotonicity. The maternal history revealed two prior abortions. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was done for the newborn and revealed polymicrogyri, agenesis of the genu of the corpus callosum and rhombencephalosynapsis. Whole exome sequencing confirmed the diagnosis of CEBALID syndrome which was the underlying cause of the complex of malformations, with novel presentation as arthrogryposis. Genetic counselling was recommended for the parents for future pregnancies and supportive medical care was given to the patient. CONCLUSION: Multiple congenital anomalies, along with repetitive abortions that were present in the maternal history, suggested the presence of genetic background. Multi-disciplinary approach is very important in cases with complex malformations and whole exome sequencing helped establish the diagnosis. Rarity of the case led to absence of clear guidelines in management which made supportive treatment the best approach available.

The MN1 gene, primarily known for its roles in palate formation in mammals and osteoblast function, is also considered a proto-oncogene, mainly in familial meningioma. In 2020, Mak et al. identified a group of de novo C-terminal truncating pathogenic variants in MN1 gene in 22 patients sharing similar craniofacial features, brain malformations, and neurodevelopmental delay, leading to CEBALID syndrome [1]. Requiring a multidisciplinary approach due to its complex presentations, this syndrome often overlaps with conditions such as Toriello-Carey syndrome, given the resemblance in clinical features, and the involvement of the MN1 gene, despite differing genetic mechanisms, as Toriello-Carey syndrome is associated with MN1 microdeletions [1].

Arthrogryposis, seen in several genetic disorders, typically manifests as multiple non-progressive joint contractures present at birth [2]. While common in other syndromes, this case presents the first reported association between CEBALID syndrome and arthrogryposis, as well as the first described CEBALID case in Lebanon.

A late preterm (35th week) female neonate, born via elective Cesarean section, was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) due to fetal-to-neonatal cardiorespiratory maladaptation, requiring resuscitation and intubation. She also had several congenital malformations that required thorough investigations. The newborn weighed 2.3 kg at birth, with an APGAR score of 4 at 1 min and 7 at 5 min.

Maternal history reveals a 26-year-old, previously healthy female who was well followed up during her current pregnancy at a local gynecology and obstetrics clinic. She was G4P2A2L1, with four prior gestations, two resulted in spontaneous abortion during the first trimester (second and third pregnancies). Her first gestation resulted in a healthy female offspring with no prenatal or postnatal complications. Also, the mother reported being a non-smoker and non-drinker and was not on any medications during or prior to pregnancy, except for pregnancy vitamin supplement. Prenatal detailed ultrasound done at 34th week of gestation showed polymicrogyri with simple cysts in the third ventricle of the brain, as well as bilateral hyperextension of the lower limbs with right hand in continuous extension and left hand in continuous flexion, suggesting the possibility of arthrogryposis. Moreover, signs of polyhydramnios were also noted. The newborn was the result of a non-consanguineous union and the parents reported no family history of congenital malformations.

The patient was immediately intubated after birth due to apnea and bradycardia despite stimulation and bag mask ventilation. Cord blood gas analysis showed a pH of 7.3, indicating mild metabolic acidosis.

Upon admission to the NICU, the patient was placed in a warm incubator and supported by pressure control mechanical ventilation. Oxygen saturation improved, and caffeine citrate was initiated to improve her respiratory drive.

The physical examination revealed narrow anterior and posterior fontanelles, bitemporal narrowing of the head, hypertelorism, folded pinna with low setting of the ears, a high arched palate, mild narrowing of the chest, hypotonicity, semi-clenched fists, bilateral hyperflexion of the hips, and bilateral hyperextension of the knees, which were not reducible by manual efforts (Fig. 1). Initial investigations included a full panel of laboratory tests and imaging studies in order to evaluate the extent of the congenital malformations. Few days later, whole exome sequencing identified a heterozygous variant of uncertain significance (VUS) c.2287G > A, p.(Val763Met), identified in the MN1 gene. Pathogenic variants in this gene are associated with autosomal dominant CEBALID syndrome. However, it was unclear whether this variant is inherited or de novo, hence parental testing was recommended.

Before discharge the family was educated on NGT feeding and genetic counseling was recommended before any future pregnancy.

Approximately 3.5% of liveborn singleton infants have major congenital malformations with congenital heart defects being the most commonly encountered followed by malformations of the genitals, limbs, urinary tract and others [3]. With the introduction of the whole exome sequencing technique, new genetic variants are being identified and their relation to neonatal phenotypes is being suggested [1]. CEBALID syndrome is one of the newly described syndromes. It was found that diverse craniofacial features were associated with C-terminal truncation of MN1 gene, such as hypertelorism, midface hypoplasia, dysplastic ears, high arched palate, down slanting palpebral fissure, plagiocephaly, brachycephaly, turricephaly, dolicocephaly, and bitemporal narrowing [1]. Also, possible neurodevelopmental findings are hearing loss, speech delay, intellectual disability, motor delays, feeding difficulties and hypotonia [1].

On the other hand, brain imaging may reveal a wide range of malformations including rhombencephalosynapsis (RES), cerebellar dysplasia (other than RES), perisylvian polymicrogyri, persistent trigeminal artery, ventriculomegaly, thick rostral corpus callosum and other findings [1].

In our case, the newborn had multiple facial and motor presentations and a variety of imaging findings – including bitemporal narrowing, high arched palate, hypotonia, polymicrogyri and arthrogryposis – that were not suggestive of a specific syndrome for the neonatologist at birth.

Arthrogryposis may be present in as much as 400 different conditions [2]. This term is used to describe a finding of multiple (more than two) congenital joint contractures involving at least two body areas [2]. Several theories have been proposed to explain the condition, and the most widely accepted was the occurrence fetal dyskinesia early during the fetal life that led to abnormal stretching of muscles and decreased compliance of joints. Arthrogryposis is divided into three main categories: classic arthrogryposis or amyoplasia where the central nervous system function is normal but the muscles were replaced by fatty and fibrous tissue and this is the most common type, distal arthrogryposis which is inherited in autosomal dominant manner and involves mainly the distal joints, and syndromic arthrogryposis which is the least common [2].

Several syndromes, including Toriello-Carey syndrome and Marden-Walker syndrome, present with arthrogryposis alongside facial malformations and central nervous system (CNS) abnormalities, making differential diagnosis challenging [2]. In this case, the striking presence of facial and motor abnormalities, associated with arthrogryposis, in light of inconclusive clinical findings, mediated the use of whole exome sequencing, considering its critical role in identifying rare conditions, particularly in newborns with multiple congenital anomalies. The results showed MN1 C-terminal truncation, which confirmed the diagnosis of the novel CEBALID syndrome.

Furthermore, the identification of pathogenic variants of the MN1 gene in CEBALID patients suggests a potential link between MN1 variants and isolated cases of RES. Investigating MN1 variants in these cases could provide valuable insights into the genetic mechanism underlying this extremely rare malformation.

This unprecedented association of CEBALID syndrome with congenital arthrogryposis expands the phenotypic spectrum of this rare disorder. This finding serves as a foundation for further studies that may establish a clear relation between MCTT and fetal dyskinesia as a cause of arthrogryposis. While there are no established guidelines for the management of cases with CEBALID syndrome, a professional multidisciplinary approach is essential in addressing the diverse clinical manifestations. Genetic counseling and long-term follow-up are crucial in managing novel presentations like this, ensuring optimal care for future pregnancies.
