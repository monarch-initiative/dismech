Splicing Factor 3b Subunit 1 (SF3B1), a core component of the spliceosome, undergoes dynamic phosphorylation and dephosphorylation during the splicing cycle to regulate pre-mRNA splicing. Twenty-eight threonine/proline repeats are phosphorylated by CDK11 during spliceosome activation and remain phosphorylated in the catalytically active spliceosomes. The function of phosphorylated SF3B1 (P-SF3B1), and the identity of spliceosomes stalled by CDK11 inhibition remain unclear. Using quantitative proteomics of chromatin-associated spliceosomes, we identify a previously uncharacterized intermediate complex BOTS964, arrested by CDK11 inhibitor OTS964, that incorporates the nineteen-related (NTR) but not nineteen (NTC) complex. iCLIP-seq revealed that P-SF3B1 engages with the U6 snRNA internal stem-loop (ISL), suggesting a potential role in stabilizing the RNA catalytic core. We further demonstrate that P-SF3B1 is recognized by forkhead-associated (FHA) domain of SNIP1, which promotes recruitment of retention and splicing (RES) complex during spliceosome activation. Acute SNIP1 depletion disrupts RES incorporation, causes widespread splicing defects, and promotes hyperphosphorylation of SF3B1 by CDK11. Mutations in SNIP1 FHA domain, including the neurodevelopmental disorder-associated E366G variant, impair P-SF3B1 binding, pre-mRNA splicing, and cell viability. Together, these findings uncover the phosphorylation-dependent CDK11/P-SF3B1/SNIP1 signaling axis that is critical for pre-mRNA splicing and cellular proliferation and provide a mechanistic insight into its dysregulation in disease.

Transcription of eukaryotic genes produces RNA precursors called pre-mRNAs, which contain non-coding introns. Introns are removed by spliceosome in the process of pre-mRNA splicing. The spliceosome is a dynamic RNA-protein complex that assembles on intron sequences through a series of steps involving the sequential recruitment and release of five small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particles (snRNPs): U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6, along with numerous non-snRNP proteins. This process is accompanied by significant structural rearrangements. As a result, a series of intermediate spliceosome complexes are formed, including E, A, B, Bact, B*, C, C*, P, and ILS. Pre-mRNA splicing consists of four key steps: spliceosome assembly (E → B), catalytic activation (B → B*), two-step catalysis of splicing (B* → P), and spliceosome disassembly (P → ILS)1,2,3,4,5,6. While the roles of kinases and their substrates in facilitating transitions between intermediates are essential, they remain poorly understood7,8,9.

Activation of the spliceosome occurs as it transitions from the B to the Bactcomplex, leading to the formation of the catalytically active U2/U6 snRNA structure. This activation progresses via two intermediates, pre-Bact1and pre-Bact2, and requires the release and recruitment of about 20 and 25 proteins, respectively10,11,12. A key event in this activation is the unwinding of the U4/U6 snRNA duplex, followed by the release of the U4 snRNP and B-specific proteins, and the recruitment of various protein complexes such as the NTC (Nineteen), NTR (Nineteen-related), RES (retention and splicing), and IBC (intron-binding)12,13,14,15,16. In the activated spliceosome (Bact), the newly formed U2/U6 snRNA catalytic center is stabilized by various proteins, including components of the NTR and NTC complexes12,17,18,19. The catalytic center remains static until the ILS complex4. In higher eukaryotes, this activation also requires the action of CDK11, which phosphorylates SF3B1, a core splicing factor and component of the U2 snRNP. The inhibition of CDK11 with OTS964 traps the spliceosome in a B-like intermediate, in which U4 snRNA and B-specific proteins remain associated, and RES and IBC complexes unbound8,20. The precise composition of the stalled spliceosome however, remains unknown.

Human SF3B1 consists of an N-terminal intrinsically disordered region (IDR) and a C-terminal twenty HEAT-repeats domain21. These regions serve distinct and essential roles in the spliceosome lifecycle and are targeted by small molecules that disrupt spliceosome assembly and have antiproliferative effects8,22,23,24,25,26. While the functions of the HEAT-repeats are relatively well-understood21, the roles of the IDR, due to its structural flexibility, which prevents its visualization in cryo-EM structures, remains largely unclear20. The IDR acts as a dynamic interaction hub for proteins and RNA8,27,28,29,30, containing a cluster of 28 threonine/proline (Thr/Pro) CDK-consensus sites that overlap with several U2AF homology ligand motifs (ULM)s20,27,28,31,32. These motifs serve as binding sites for splicing factors (such as U2AF65, PUF60, TAT-SF1, RBM39, and SPF45 (RBM17)) when IDR is unphosphorylated in the A intermediate31,33,34,35,36. Upon the formation of the Bactcomplex, the IDR becomes fully phosphorylated, a state that persists until the second catalytic reaction (C complex)8,20,27, when SF3B1 is dephosphorylated by the PP1 phosphatase37,38. RNA immunoprecipitation coupled to qPCR (RIP-qPCR) showed that phosphorylated SF3B1 (P-SF3B1) associates with U6 snRNA8. However, it remains unclear whether this interaction is direct and where at U6 snRNA the P-SF3B1 binds. Additionally, interacting proteins (readers) of P-SF3B1 and their functions in the activated and catalytically active spliceosomes (B* → C) also remain elusive.

SNIP1, a component of the trimeric RES complex (which also includes RBMX2 and BUD13)39,40, is integrated into the spliceosome during activation and is essential for the assembly of Bactin both yeast and humans12,13,40,41. The knockdown of SNIP1 and other RES subunits results in retention of mostly short and GC-rich introns in human cells42. A similar phenotype is observed in zebrafish models with loss-of-function mutations in RES components43. SNIP1 contains a conserved forkhead-associated (FHA) domain, a putative P-Thr-binding module44,45, with unknown function. A recent study suggested that it is required for the interaction with RNPS1, possibly affecting the post-transcriptional splicing of detained introns46. A biallelic mutation (E366G) inSNIP1, located near the FHA domain, has been associated with a complex neurodevelopmental disorder in 35 individuals from the Amish community47,48, although the underlying mechanistic defect caused by the mutation is unknown. Other aberrations of SNIP1 have been linked to neurogenesis and neurodegeneration, both in human and zebrafish43,46,48,49. SNIP1 has also been implicated in transcription regulation and oncogenesis50,51,52,53,54,55, highlighting its clinical relevance.

In this study, we demonstrate that the CDK11 inhibitor OTS964 traps the spliceosome in a novel intermediate, with the NTR complex integrated but the NTC complex not associated. We show that P-SF3B1 localizes to U6 snRNA´s internal stem-loop (ISL), potentially contributing to the formation and stabilization of the U2/U6 snRNA catalytic center in the activated spliceosomes. Additionally, we identify the FHA domain of SNIP1 as a “reader” of P-SF3B1, guiding the recruitment and assembly of the RES complex during spliceosome activation, and show that mutations in the FHA domain, including the neurodevelopmental disorder-linked E366G variant, impair this interaction, resulting in defects in splicing and cellular proliferation. Overall, our findings define the roles of CDK11 kinase activity and the phosphorylated form of SF3B1 during spliceosome activation and in the catalytically active spliceosome.

Previously, we showed that CDK11 inhibition by OTS964 blocks spliceosome during B to Bacttransition, in a B-like intermediate8. To define a CDK11-dependent rate-limiting step in spliceosome activation, we decided to determine the precise protein composition of spliceosomes blocked by OTS964 in cells. We took advantage of the fact that most pre-mRNA splicing occurs co-transcriptionally and that spliceosomes are tightly associated with chromatin during this process8,27,56,57,58. Splicing inhibitors cause the accumulation of stalled spliceosome intermediates on chromatin, enabling their isolation and characterization directly from living cells8,59. We treated HCT116 cells with either DMSO (control) or 50 nM OTS964, isolated chromatin fractions, and digested them with micrococcal nuclease (MNase)8,59,60. The released spliceosome complexes were immunoprecipitated with SF3B1 antibody, and the composition of SF3B1-associated complexes was determined by mass spectrometry (Fig.1a). Since SF3B1 is a core component of the spliceosome (Fig.1b), the analyses of SF3B1-associated proteins reflect the composition of a spliceosome intermediate.

In DMSO-treated cells, ~30% of SF3B1-associated proteins were classified as RNA splicing factors (GO biological process: RNA splicing) (Supplementary Data1, sheet “IP SF3B1 (Chromatin) all data”). CDK11 inhibition significantly altered the association of 78 splicing factors with SF3B1, with 52 proteins decreased and 26 increased in abundance (−0.5 > log2FC > 0.5, Padj <0.05) (Fig.1c, and Supplementary Data1, sheet “IP SF3B1 (Chromatin) all data”). Since our previous work identified a critical role for CDK11 in the B to Bacttransition8, we followed changes in snRNP and non-snRNP complexes involved in this transition12. As expected, the levels of U2 snRNP and SF3B1 remained unchanged after OTS964 treatment (Fig.1d, and Supplementary Data1, sheet “IP SF3B1 (Chromatin) splicing”). In contrast, the components of B intermediate-associated complexes, namely U5 and U4/U6 snRNPs and B-specific proteins, increased in abundance (Fig.1d, and Supplementary Data1, sheet “IP SF3B1 (Chromatin) splicing”), consistent with their accumulation upstream of a blocked transition during spliceosome activation8. Protein complexes that are normally recruited during spliceosome activation, such as RES, NTC and IBC, were significantly reduced, with the notable exception of NTR, which remained unchanged, suggesting that it was already integrated into the spliceosome. This result suggests that OTS964 arrests spliceosome progression during activation in a previously uncharacterized intermediate.

To validate this result by a method that omits the immunoprecipitation step, we isolated soluble and chromatin fractions from cells and quantified protein levels as proxies for the presence of their respective spliceosome complexes8,59(Supplementary Fig.1a). After CDK11 inhibition, the chromatin abundance of proteins associated with E (U2AF65), and A (RBM17) intermediates as well as the U4/U6 snRNP (NHP2L1) complex remained equal. Meanwhile, the levels of U5 snRNP (EFTUD2, PRPF4, TXNL4A) and B-specific proteins (SMU1, IK, WBP4 and MFAP1) increased, and the amount of NTR (SNW1, RBM22, BUD31) complex remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig.1a). In contrast, proteins from RES (SNIP1, BUD13, RBMX2), NTC (CDC5L, PRPF19), IBC (AQR) and exon-junction (EJC) (RBM8A) complexes exhibited decreased chromatin association (Supplementary Fig.1a). The optimal fractionation of chromatin and effect of treatment with OTS964 were verified by histone H2A and SF3B1 phosphorylation, respectively (Supplementary Fig.1a).

This result suggests that OTS964 arrests spliceosome progression during activation in an intermediate with integrated NTR complex, and NTC complex still not associated. We refer to this intermediate as BOTS964.

The N-terminal region of SF3B1 is a large IDR containing multiple phosphorylatable Thr/Pro repeats8,20. As for most other IDRs of core spliceosome factors that are not resolved in cryo-EM structures, the functional roles of the P-SF3B1 in the activated and catalytically active spliceosomes remain unknown. Here we applied UV crosslinking and an immunoprecipitation (iCLIP), a method that identifiesdirectprotein-RNA interactions at single nucleotide resolution61,62to investigate the interaction of P-SF3B1 with U6 snRNA8, a component of catalytically active U2/U6 snRNA structure63,64,65,66.

P-SF3B1 was immunoprecipitated from HCT116 cells, treated or untreated with OTS964. To ensure the specificity of the P-SF3B1 iCLIP binding profiles, we used two phospho-specific antibodies recognizing either P-Thr211 or P-Thr235 of SF3B18. Because iCLIP replicates generated with the P-Thr211 and P-Thr235 antibodies correlated well, we combined the replicates for each antibody and used them for further genome-wide analyses (Supplementary Fig.2a).

To determine the contacts between P-SF3B1 and U6 snRNA, we analyzed crosslinking patterns of P-Thr211 and P-Thr235 SF3B1 iCLIPs within U6 snRNA. Both datasets revealed a distinct peak around nucleotide 66 in the ISL of U6 snRNA (Fig.2a, b), suggesting an association of P-SF3B1 with this catalytically important RNA element of U6 snRNA formed in the activated spliceosome12(Fig.2c). Previously published SF3B1 iCLIP data8showed a detectable peak at the same position in the U6 ISL, which was insensitive to OTS964 treatment (Fig.2d). In contrast, OTS964 treatment reduced contacts between P-SF3B1 and the ISL U6 two- to three-fold relative to total SF3B1 at this specific position (Fig.2a, b, d), providing further evidence for the specificity of P-SF3B1 association with this U6 snRNA element.

As expected, neither P-SF3B1 nor total SF3B1 crosslinked to U1 snRNA which binds to 5´ splice sites during early stages of spliceosome assembly (Supplementary Fig.2b). By contrast, U2 snRNA, another component of the catalytically active U2/U6 snRNA spliceosome structure, was bound predominantly by total SF3B1 and to lesser extent by P-SF3B1 (Supplementary Fig.2c)8.

To identify proteins that preferentially associate with P-SF3B1 in the spliceosome, we performed a pull-down assay followed by mass spectrometry (Fig.3a). We expressed and purified the GST-tagged N-terminus of SF3B1 (residues 1-463) fromE. coli, and phosphorylated it in vitro using flag-tagged CDK11 purified from HEK293 cells. A non-phosphorylated version of the protein was used as a control. GST-SF3B1 proteins were immobilized on glutathione resin and incubated with soluble nuclear lysate from HCT116 cells. To minimize unspecific interactions with pre-assembled spliceosome complexes, we removed the chromatin fraction and used soluble nuclear lysate treated with RNase A to further promote the release of proteins from various RNA-dependent subcomplexes of the spliceosome. After stringent washes, SF3B1-interacting proteins were identified by mass spectrometry (Fig.3a).

A comparison of proteins enriched (−1 > log2FC > 1, Padj <0.05) with phosphorylated or unphosphorylated SF3B1 identified 8 and 26 interactors, respectively (Fig.3b, and Supplementary Data2). As expected, known ULM-binding proteins U2AF65 and PUF60 were detected, and RBM39 and RBM1731,33,34,36were also enriched on unphosphorylated SF3B1, albeit below the statistical significance threshold (Fig.3b, Supplementary Data2). These proteins are associated with the A spliceosome intermediate in which SF3B1 remains unphosphorylated (Fig.1b)20, validating the assay´s biological relevance. Among proteins preferentially associated with P-SF3B1 was identified NIPP1, a PP1 adaptor protein that interacts with P-SF3B1 via its FHA domain67,68and SNIP1, another FHA domain-containing protein and component of the RES complex39,40,41(Fig.3b). The presence of CDK11 and components of its complex, CCNL1/L2 and SAP30BP69,70, is a consequence of P-SF3B1 sample preparation (Fig.3a, b). To validate these interactions, we performed GST pull-down assays using (P)-SF3B1 and analyzed bound proteins by western blotting. U2AF65 and RBM17 preferentially bound unphosphorylated SF3B1 (Supplementary Fig.3a) and SNIP1 preferred the phosphorylated one (Fig.3c). Notably, CDK11 remained associated with P-SF3B1 even after multiple washes, in line with the proteomics results (Fig.3c). Since SNIP1 contains a P-Thr binding FHA domain and is recruited to the spliceosome during its activation (B to Bact), we focused on its functional characterization.

To investigate the role of SNIP1 in pre-mRNA splicing and spliceosome assembly, we tagged the endogenousSNIP1with an inducible-degron (dTAG) system71. dTAG facilitates the acute and often near-complete depletion of a protein of interest, allowing for the examination of its primary function in dynamic processes such as splicing8,72,73. We introduced a cassette encoding a hemagglutinin (HA) tag and the FKBP12F36Vdegron into both alleles of theSNIP1gene by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing in HCT116 cells (Supplementary Fig.4a). Accurate and homozygous integration into genomic DNA was verified by sequencing (Supplementary Fig.4b). Comparative western blot analysis of cell lysates from HCT116 and HCT116 dTAG SNIP1 cells confirmed the absence of the wild-type and the appropriate size of the tagged SNIP1 in the edited cell line (Supplementary Fig.4c). Upon addition of the small molecule inducer dTAG-v1, SNIP1 was depleted specifically in the dTAG-tagged cell line, with > 90% degradation observed within 2 h of the treatment (Supplementary Fig.4c).

To assess how acute SNIP1 depletion impacts pre-mRNA splicing, we performed total RNA-seq on nuclear extracts from HCT116 dTAG SNIP1 cells treated with either 500 nM dTAG-v1 or the DMSO control for 4 h. Visual inspection of individual genes showed widespread intron retention in transcripts from cells treated with dTAG-v1 (Fig.4a, Supplementary Fig.4 d). For global splicing analyses, we only considered the 6222 major isoforms of protein-coding genes with a prevalence higher than 70%, no overlap with other genes, and with read counts per kilobase (RPK) higher than 50, as previously described8,73. The splicing ratio was calculated separately for 5´SS and 3´SS by dividing the number of spliced reads by the total number of spliced and unspliced reads crossing the respective SS. We observed significant (Wilcoxon signed rank test; p < 2.2.10-16) accumulations of unspliced reads when all the introns were considered (Fig.4b) and also when first, middle, and last introns were analyzed separately (Supplementary Fig.4e). To rigorously compare the effects of CDK11 inhibition and SNIP1 loss on splicing we compared fold changes of intron ratios between OTS964- and DMSO-treated HCT116 cells from our previous study8and between dTAG-induced SNIP1 depletion and DMSO control in dTAG SNIP1 HCT116 cells across 12727 protein-coding genes. Intron ratios per gene were calculated as the ratio between the total number of intronic and exonic reads for each gene as described previously8. Only genes for which intron ratios varied by less than two-fold between replicates in each condition were included. This analysis revealed a strong correlation between CDK11 inhibition and SNIP1 depletion (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.79) providing evidence that CDK11 and SNIP1 affect splicing in a similar manner (Fig.4c).

Highly similar splicing defects, observed both globally (Fig.4b, c, Supplementary Fig.4e) and in individual transcripts (Fig.4a, Supplementary Fig.4 d) after SNIP1 depletion and CDK11 inhibition8, strongly suggest that both proteins act in the same step of spliceosome regulation. To determine the composition of a spliceosome, intermediate after acute SNIP1 depletion and compare it to BOTS964, we isolated chromatin fractions as in Supplementary Fig.1a. Immunoblotting analyses of chromatin-bound spliceosome complexes after acute SNIP1 depletion revealed reduced levels of RES components (BUD13 and RBMX2), indicating the failure to recruit the RES complex during the transition from B to Bact(Fig.4d). Notably, while some subunits of spliceosome complexes normally recruited during its activation (e.g., NTR, NTC, IBC) were still incorporated (e.g., SNW1, RBM22, PRPF19, AQR), the others were decreased (e.g., BCAS2, CDC5L, CWC22). Components recruited at or after the Bactstage, such as those from the EJC, C and C*12complexes, were reduced or absent (Fig.4d). The optimal fractionation of chromatin and SNIP1 depletion were verified by using histone H4 and SNIP1 antibodies, respectively (Fig.4d).

In summary, our results demonstrate that SNIP1 is essential for the proper recruitment of the RES complex, and its depletion results in the accumulation of a mis-assembled, likely dead-end spliceosome intermediate leading to widespread splicing defects.

While analyzing the composition of chromatin-associated spliceosomes after either CDK11 inhibition or SNIP1 depletion, we observed an increase in U5 snRNP and B-specific proteins in both conditions (Supplementary Fig.1aand Fig.4d). We interpret this accumulation as a consequence of spliceosome stalling at the B to Bacttransition. However, these perturbations had the opposite effects on SF3B1 phosphorylation: while CDK11 inhibition diminished phosphorylation8(Supplementary Fig.1a), SNIP1 depletion caused a marked increase (Fig.4d, and Supplementary Fig.4c). We interpret this increase as a rapid compensatory response to the absence of SNIP1, indicating that SF3B1 phosphorylation is a prerequisite for proper SNIP1 recruitment. An alternative explanation for the SF3B1 hyperphosphorylation is a “spliceosome cycle arrest” with SF3B1 dephosphorylation by phosphatases being blocked while CDK11 activity remains high. Altogether, these results are consistent with CDK11 acting as a primary kinase for SF3B1 phosphorylation8, and suggest that P-SF3B1 is required for the efficient integration of SNIP1 and the RES complex to the spliceosome during its activation. To directly determine whether this hyperphosphorylation is CDK11-dependent, we first depleted SNIP1 to induce SF3B1 phosphorylation and subsequently treated cells with OTS964 (Fig.5a–c, and Supplementary Fig.5a). Inhibition of CDK11 for 30 min fully abrogated the phosphorylation of SF3B1 as well as phosphorylation of Thr806 in SPT5, a recently identified CDK11 substrate74. In contrast, phosphorylation of the RNA Polymerase II (RNAPII) C-terminal domain (CTD) on serine 2, 5 and 7 (Ser2, Ser5 and Ser7) were reduced gradually and less specifically, as expected upon constitutive splicing inhibition8,73(Fig.5c, and Supplementary Fig.5a).

Given that previous studies have implicated transcriptional CDKs (e.g., CDK7, CDK9, CDK12/13) in SF3B1 phosphorylation75,76,77,78,79, we compared the effects of their inhibition with OTS964. Treatment with the CDK9 inhibitor NVP-280, CDK12/13 inhibitor THZ53181or CDK7 inhibitor SY-35175for 30 min did not reduce P-SF3B1 levels (Fig.5a, c, and Supplementary Fig.5a). Only a modest decrease in SF3B1 phosphorylation was observed after 1 h of CDK9 and CDK7 inhibition, whereas longer treatments (≥ 2 h) led to a substantial reduction in P-SF3B1 levels for all inhibitors (Fig.5c, and Supplementary Fig.5a). The decrease in SF3B1 phosphorylation observed after longer inhibition (≥ 1 h) can be attributed to roles of these kinases in transcription, a process upstream of splicing8,20,72, and, in the case of CDK7 to its recently described role in CDK11 activation75,82,83. Effective inhibition of CDK7, CDK9 and CDK12 was confirmed by the expected changes in RNAPII CTD and SPT5 phosphorylation. Specifically, phosphorylation of Ser5 and Ser7 in the CTD was reduced upon CDK7 inhibition84,85,86; phosphorylation of Thr806 in SPT5 and also in the CTD of RNAPII were decreased after CDK9 inhibition80,87,88, and modest changes in phosphorylation of Ser2 and Ser7 were detected upon longer (≥ 1 h) CDK12/13 inhibition78,81,89,90,91,92,93,94,95(Fig.5c, and Supplementary Fig.5a). In summary, our data demonstrate that SF3B1 hyperphosphorylation induced by SNIP1 depletion is specifically mediated by CDK11.

Given that SNIP1 harbors a candidate P-Thr-binding FHA domain at its C-terminus, we tested whether this domain is necessary for interaction with P-SF3B1 (Fig.6a). We used in vitro binding assays (Fig.3a) and incubated GST-tagged (P)-SF3B1 (1-463) with his-tagged wild-type or FHA-deletion mutant (del FHA) SNIP1 proteins expressed inE. coli(Fig.6a). As expected, wild-type SNIP1 associated with P-SF3B1, while deletion of the FHA domain abolished this binding (Supplementary Fig.6a), confirming the essential role of the FHA domain in the interaction.

FHA domains typically consist of eleven ß-strands (ß1- ß11) interspaced with six loop regions (L1-L6), and recognize a single P-Thr via residues on apical surfaces of the loops44,45. We used AlphaFold 3 (AF3)96to model the interaction between the SNIP1 FHA domain (amino acids 259-371) (Fig.6b) and P-SF3B1 (amino acids 142-436). The model predicted that the SNIP1 FHA domain interacts with two P-Thr residues in P-SF3B1, P-Thr358 and P-Thr369. The P-Thr358 is in close proximity to SNIP1 K301 in loop L3 and P-Thr369 interacts with SNIP1 K265 in loop L1 and R365 in loop L6 (Fig.6c).

To determine the relevance of these amino acid residues for the interaction, we generated SNIP1 mutants K265G, K301G and R365G and tested their binding to P-SF3B1 in an in vitro binding assay (Supplementary Fig.6b). Both K265G and K301G mutations abolished the binding, and R365G mutation reduced binding by ~3-fold (Supplementary Fig.6b). SF3B1 contains 28 Thr/Pro repeats (Supplementary Fig.6c) that can be phosphorylated8,20, so we next asked whether P-Thr358 and P-Thr369 are essential for SNIP1 binding or whether they can be substituted by the remaining P-Thr. Therefore, we mutated these two amino acids to alanine (Thr358A and Thr369A) in the GST-SF3B1 and tested their binding to wild-type SNIP1 in an in vitro binding assay. Since the binding of SNIP1 to the wild-type and mutated (Thr358A and Thr369A) SF3B1 proteins remained equal, this indicates that the other P-Thr residues in SF3B1 can compensate for the loss of P-Thr358 and P-Thr369 (Supplementary Fig.6 d). These findings suggest that the SNIP1 FHA domain recognizes multiple P-Thr residues in SF3B1, with flexibility in which sites are utilized.

Since cryo-EM structures of the activated spliceosome (Bact) containing P-SF3B1 and RES complex were published13,16we wanted to determine whether association of P-SF3B1 with FHA domain of SNIP1 can be visualized in these structures. Close examination of the Bactcomplexes (PDB: 8I0R16and PDB: 5Z5713) identified a contact point between SF3B1 and SNIP1 and non-interpreted density in the loops of the FHA domain of SNIP1 (Fig.6d). An AF3 model of SNIP1 FHA in complex with SF3B1-derived phospho-peptide motif (P)ThrPro[X]n(P)ThrPro from the repetitive sequence of 28 Thr/Pro (Supplementary Fig.6c) demonstrated that the predicted motif containing two P-Thr fits exactly to the region of the unassigned cryo-EM density (CCC = 0.85 at 4 Å map resolution for the AF3 prediction of SNIP1 FHA domain in complex with the SF3B1 phospho-peptide) (Fig.6e). This observation independently validates our in vitro mutational analysis of SNIP1-loop mutants (K265G, K301G and R365G) and supports the findings that the FHA domain of SNIP1 binds two P-Thr in P-SF3B1 to guide the recruitment and/or anchoring of the RES complex during the spliceosome activation.

A homozygous SNIP1 mutation (E366G) found in ~ 35 individuals from the Amish population has been identified as the genetic cause of a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS)47,48. However, the molecular basis of this mutation´s effect remains unclear. As E366 is adjacent to R365 in loop L6 of the FHA domain, a region critical for P-Thr recognition, we hypothesized that the E366G mutant could impair SNIP1 binding to P-SF3B1. To test this, we compared the interactions of wild-type, E366G and R365G SNIP1 proteins with P-SF3B1 in in vitro binding assays (Supplementary Fig.6e). The E366G mutant exhibited an ~3-fold reduced binding, similar to the R365G mutation (Supplementary Fig.6e). Close inspection of our SNIP1-P-SF3B1 interaction model also indicates that E366 is in close proximity to the binding interface and its mutation to glycine (E366G) induces most likely local conformational changes in the FHA domain leading to suboptimal P-SF3B1 binding (Fig.6e). Altogether, these findings strongly suggest that impaired SNIP1-P-SF3B1 interaction is the likely molecular defect underlying NEDHCS.

To determine whether mutations in the SNIP1 FHA domain affect incorporation of SNIP1 into the activated spliceosome, we expressed flag-tagged SNIP1 (F-SNIP1) WT, single mutants K265G, K301G, R365G, E366G, and the double mutant K265G/K301G in cells and compared their association with components of the activated spliceosome (Fig.7a, b, and Supplementary Fig.7a). Immunoprecipitation of endogenous BUD13, a component of the RES complex, revealed interactions with P-SF3B1 and CWC22, but not with U1-70K and U2AF65, which associate with the spliceosome at stages preceding activation17(Fig.7a, b, and Supplementary Fig.7a). These results indicate full integration of BUD13 into the activated spliceosome. Notably, BUD13 immunoprecipitated WT F-SNIP1 more efficiently than the F-SNIP1 mutants, indicating a reduced ability of the mutants to associate with the activated spliceosome (Fig.7a, b, and Supplementary Fig.7a). Because substitution with glycine (G) may disrupt the FHA domain conformation, we additionally generated alanine (A) substitutions (K265A, K301A, E365A, and K265A/K301A). These variants also showed reduced associations with the spliceosome in BUD13 immunoprecipitates compared with WT F-SNIP1 (Supplementary Fig.7a), confirming the importance of residues K265, K301, and E365 for mediating interaction with P-SF3B1 in cells. Consistent with these findings, immunoprecipitation of F-SNIP1 E366G, K265G, and double mutant K265G/K301G revealed moderate (for E366G) and severe (for K265G and K265G/K301G) reductions in association with components of the activated spliceosome (P-SF3B1, CWC22, RBM22 and BUD13) (Supplementary Fig.7b, c).

To assess the physiological importance of these FHA domain residues for cell viability and pre-mRNA splicing, we stably integratedF-SNIP1constructs encoding WT or mutant SNIP1 into the FRT/TO integration site of the HCT116 dTAG SNIP1 cell line. Immunoblot analysis showed that F-SNIP1 K265G, E366G and double K265G/K301G mutants were expressed at levels comparable to WT F-SNIP1 (Supplementary Fig.7 d, e); therefore, subsequent analyses were performed in these cell lines.

Cells were treated with dTAG-v1 or DMSO, and cell viability was assessed either indirectly, by measuring metabolic activity of mitochondrial dehydrogenases by WST assay (Fig.7c), or directly by crystal violet staining (Fig.7d). In the absence of ectopic F-SNIP1 expression, cells were not viable, consistent with the essential role of SNIP1 in constitutive splicing. Expression of WT F-SNIP1 fully rescued both metabolic activity and cellular proliferation. In contrast, the K265G and the double K265G/K301G mutants severely impaired metabolic activity and cell viability, whereas the patient-derived E366G mutation caused only a moderate reduction of both (Fig.7c, d).

We next examined whether these metabolic and viability phenotypes reflected defects in pre-mRNA splicing (Fig.7e, f, and Supplementary Fig.7 f). Splicing of endogenousRIOK3,ARRDC1, andCCNL1transcripts was analyzed by RT-PCR following dTAG-v1 treatment. Accumulation of unspliced transcripts was evident within 2 h of SNIP1 depletion in HCT116 dTAG-SNIP1 cells (Fig.7e, f). This splicing defect was partially rescued by expression of WT F-SNIP1, confirming that the phenotype was specific to SNIP1 depletion (Fig.7e, f). In contrast, expression of F-SNIP1 K265G, E366G, or the double K265G/K301G mutant failed to rescue, or rescued less efficiently, the splicing defects compared with WT F-SNIP1 (Fig.7e, f). Notably, the double K265G/K301G mutant consistently showed a more severe defect than the patient-derived E366G mutation (Fig.7e). These differences were further supported by quantitative PCR analysis of splicing of long introns in theCCNL1andSFSWAPgenes (Supplementary Fig.7 f), indicating that the observed splicing defects are general and not dependent on intron length. Taken together, these results demonstrate that FHA domain residues mediating interaction with P-SF3B1 are essential for efficient pre-mRNA splicing and cell viability.

In this study, we identified a spliceosome intermediate in cells treated with the CDK11 inhibitor OTS964. This complex, which we refer to as BOTS964, exhibits full association of the NTR complex, but the NTC complex is not yet incorporated. This finding reveals a stage of spliceosome activation, which is dependent on CDK11 kinase activity. We also characterized the molecular functions of P-SF3B1 in the activated/catalytically active spliceosome. Specifically, P-SF3B1 contacts the ISL of U6 snRNA, which may help to stabilize the U2/U6 snRNA catalytic core. Additionally, P-SF3B1 associates with the FHA domain of SNIP1, suggesting that this interaction plays a key role in integrating the RES complex into the spliceosome during activation. Furthermore, SNIP1 mutation E366G, found in neurodegenerative disorder patients, reduces association with P-SF3B1, revealing a mechanistic defect caused by the mutation. We found that the rapid depletion of SNIP1 induces widespread splicing defects, resembling the effects of CDK11 inhibition with OTS964. Moreover, mutations in P-SF3B1–binding residues within the SNIP1 FHA domain, including neurodevelopmental disorder-associated mutation E366G, caused defects in pre-mRNA splicing and cell proliferation. Collectively, these results underscore the critical roles of P-SF3B1 and its interacting factors in pre-mRNA splicing and disease onset. A model summarizing the findings is presented in Fig.8.

OTS964 stalls chromatin-associated spliceosome assembly in cells at a specific stage of spliceosome activation. BOTS964lacks the NTC complex, which aligns with the presence of U4 snRNA in the blocked spliceosome8. The release of U4 snRNA from the U6 snRNA by BRR2 helicase is a known prerequisite for NTC binding18,19. Additionally, U4/U6 proteins and U5 PRP6 protein, which are mutually exclusive with NTC binding12, are still present in BOTS964(Supplementary Data1). It is worth noting that BOTS964differs from intermediates previously reported during spliceosome activation12,97,98,99, in whichbothNTC and NTR complexes are either bound12,97or absent98,99. The presence of only NTR in BOTS964suggests that NTR and NTC are recruited sequentially during spliceosome activation. Our data also indicate that while NTC integration requires CDK11 activity and likely also P-SF3B1, NTR does not. It is worth noting that in contrast to NTC, the NTR proteins do not form a preassembled complex12. While NTR component BUD31 is already present in the B complex15, additional subunits RBM22 and SKIP were only found in pre-Bact1, an intermediate that also has fully integrated NTC12. Thus, OTS964 blocks spliceosome progression at an intermediate step prior to pre-Bact1. Notably, recent cryo-EM work from the Shi lab identified six intermediate states during spliceosome activation using a different splicing inhibitor,N-palmitoyl-L-leucine (NPL). Three of these intermediates, termed pre-Bact, Bact-I and Bact-II, contain the complete NTR complex and three components of the NTC complex (CDC5L, CWC15 and PLRG1), whereas additional NTC components (PRP19, BCAS2) are not yet integrated16. Additional three intermediates (Bact-III, Bact-IV, post-Bact) have complete NTR and NTC complexes incorporated. Our biochemical analyses therefore indicate that OTS964 arrests spliceosome assembly at a stage preceding the intermediates identified in this work.

The roles of hyperphosphorylated SF3B1 IDR in the catalytically active spliceosomes (Bactto C)8,27,28has been challenging to elucidate. Our findings suggest that P-SF3B1 engages with the upper loop of the U6-ISL element, likely contributing to the stabilization of the RNA structures required for splicing catalysis. The catalytically-active U2/U6 structure is formed during spliceosome activation on the central PRP8 scaffold with the assistance of activation-associated proteins such as CDC5L, PLRG1, RBM22, SKIP, and WBP11(NPW38BP)12,18,19,100,101,102. Since SF3B1 phosphorylation occurs in tandem with the formation of a catalytically active U2/U6 RNA structure (i.e., from B to Bact)8,12, P-SF3B1 may also help to stabilize this structure during the activation process. Although SF3B1 IDR lacks an RNA-binding domain, another study indicated that P-SF3B1 can associate with RNA, suggesting that in cells even a negatively charged phosphorylated form of the protein can contribute to the interaction with negatively charged RNA29. Because P-SF3B1 is only a small proportion (10-15%) of total SF3B1 in cells8,20,27, we used two different SF3B1 phospho-specific antibodiesandOTS964 treatments to control for the specificity of iCLIP binding profiles on U6 snRNA. Still, we cannot completely exclude the scenario, that U6 binding peak originates from co-immunoprecipitation of other spliceosome component(s). As OTS964 treatment results not only in SF3B1 dephosphorylation, but also in stalling of spliceosome in BOTS964intermediate, it is very likely that conformational change of the spliceosome itself also contributes to the observed decrease of P-SF3B1 binding to the ISL in U6 snRNA and we cannot distinguish between these two scenarios. It is also important to note that the decrease in P-SF3B1 binding to U6 ISL following OTS964 treatment represents only an estimate, as iCLIP is inherently a semiquantitative method. Taken together, our data indicate that the phosphorylation of SF3B1 and the formation of the RNA catalytic structure in the Bactcomplex jointly confer specificity of SF3B1 for ISL binding.

The identification of the SNIP1 FHA domain as a reader of P-SF3B1 provides insight into how phosphorylation mediates the recruitment, assembly and stabilization of the RES complex during the activation of the spliceosome. We show that the acute depletion of SNIP1 prevents the recruitment of BUD13 and RBMX2 to the spliceosome, leading to a misassembled spliceosome and a widespread splicing defect. Specific residues in the FHA domain (K265, R365 and K301) are crucial for binding to P-SF3B1. Consistent with their functional importance, individual mutation of these residues to either glycine or alanine reduced the association of SNIP1 with the activated spliceosome, and the K265G single mutant as well as the K265G/K301G double mutant caused defects in pre-mRNA splicing and cell proliferation. Due to technical limitations (the lack of cell lines with sufficient expression of the mutants), we were unable to individually assess the roles of K301 and R365 in splicing and proliferation. It is worth noting that the methylation of K301 has been implicated in the progression of triple-negative breast cancer53. However, whether this is due to methylation or change in substrate binding in K301 mutants remains an open question103. SNIP1 is also upregulated in various cancers and associated with cell transformation52,55. As our results show that SNIP1 and CDK11 act together in spliceosome activation and CDK11 is overexpressed in many cancers8,104,105,106,107,108, the CDK11/P-SF3B1/SNIP1 pathway may be a novel oncogenic driver of splicing-dependent cancers109, warranting further investigation. Notably, several earlier studies, primarily based on siRNA-mediated depletion, protein overexpression, and reporter assays, have suggested roles for SNIP1 in the regulation of cell growth and proliferation through multiple mechanisms50,51,52,53,54,55,110,111,112,113. These include interactions of the SNIP1 N-terminus with Smad4, a modulator of TGF-β signaling51,112, and with RelA/p65, a transcription factor in the NF-κB pathway110, as well as P-Thr-independent interaction of its C-terminal FHA domain with c-Myc, a protooncogene and transcriptional driver of cellular proliferation52,53,55. Whether these pathways and mechanisms are linked to the regulation of cellular proliferation via the CDK11–phosphorylated SF3B1–SNIP1 pathway remains to be determined and warrants further investigation. Our work shows that single-residue mutations within the SNIP1 FHA domain that mediate association with phosphorylated SF3B1 are sufficient to disrupt optimal splicing and cellular proliferation.

Finally, we found that the E366G mutation in the SNIP1 FHA domain, which is linked to a neurodevelopmental disorder in the Amish, reduces binding to P-SF3B1 and to the activated spliceosome. This mutation impairs splicing and cellular proliferation, albeit to a lesser extent than other SNIP1 FHA domain mutations that we identified as mediators of direct contacts with phosphorylated SF3B1. SNIP1 and SF3B1 are important players in neurogenesis43,46,49,114and defects in other spliceosome activation proteins, such as PQBP1115, are known to cause neurological disorders. This highlights the importance of the SNIP1/P-SF3B1 interaction identified here, and the process of spliceosome activation, for proper splicing regulation and the onset of disease.

Our findings, based on the analyses of mutants, also provide insight into the specific properties of the SNIP1 FHA domain. Unlike most FHA domains that recognize single P-Thr residues45,116, the SNIP1 FHA domain likely requires two P-Thrs in P-SF3B1 for binding. This conclusion is further supported by modeling of a phosphorylated SF3B1 peptide into previously uninterpreted density in published cryo-EM structures of the activated spliceosome (Bact)13,16. This dual requirement could be important for the precise regulation of splicing by balancing the binding between various P-Thr/Pro repeats in SF3B1.

It is worth noting that in yeast SF3B1 lacks an IDR, and CDK11 is absent20, however a SNIP1 yeast homolog, Pml1p, contains an FHA domain117. Since no phosphopeptides binding Pml1p have been identified40,118, and mutation in the FHA did not impair splicing117, this suggests a divergent roles of SNIP1 FHA domains in yeast versus higher eukaryotes.

In summary, our study reveals that P-SF3B1 plays crucial roles in the activated/catalytically active spliceosomes, and that the CDK11/P-SF3B1/SNIP1 regulatory axis is essential for both normal and disease-related splicing. These findings provide a framework for future basic research and potential therapeutic interventions targeting splicing defects associated with various diseases.

The primary transcript isoforms were determined following established protocols73. RNA-sequencing data was processed using the NF-Core pipeline127(Nextflow v23.04.1) from the NF-Core consortium (v3.14, available athttps://github.com/nf-core/rnaseq; archived athttps://zenodo.org/records/10471647). To identify the predominant isoform for each gene, sequencing reads were mapped with Salmon128(v1.10.1) against curated UCSC RefSeq annotations (GRCh38, downloaded July 2024). The isoform exhibiting the highest mean transcript-per-million (TPM) value across all samples was designated as the major isoform, provided it accounted for at least 70% of the gene’s isoform expression. Isoforms associated with overlapping genes and those located on chromosomes X, Y, and M were excluded.

Splicing ratios were computed via an exon-centric splice junction analysis58. Only exons from major isoforms with an initial exon exceeding 100 base pairs were considered, while isoforms annotated as single-exon were excluded. A window spanning ±4 base pairs around each splice junction (2 bp in the exon and 2 bp in the intron) was defined to assess both spliced and unspliced reads using the findOverlaps function from the GenomicRanges129R package (v1.56). Reads overlapping at least 3 bp of this window were classified as unspliced, whereas reads overlapping at least 2 bp were counted as total reads (spliced and unspliced). Spliced reads were determined by subtracting the unspliced count from the total. The splicing ratio was calculated as the proportion of spliced reads relative to the total, spliced and unspliced reads73.

iCLIP sequencing data, including total, P-Thr211- and P-Thr235 SF3B1 samples, were analyzed on the flow.bio platform (https://app.flow.bio/) using the nf-core/clipseq130pipeline (v1.0;https://github.com/nf-core/clipseq/tree/1.0.0). Initial processing involved removing experimental barcodes (iCount version 2.0.0, de-multiplex functionhttps://github.com/tomazc/iCount), followed by read alignment with STAR131against the human reference genome (hg38, GRCh38.p13 primary assembly, GENCODE version 39), allowing up to two mismatches. Unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were applied to identify and eliminate PCR duplicates. Cross-linking events were determined by identifying the nucleotide immediately preceding each uniquely mapped sequencing read.

Genome-wide coverage tracks for combined iCLIP replicates were generated using deepTools132, normalized by sequencing depth and total read count, and binned per nucleotide. cDNA counts at individual iCLIP cross-link sites were normalized to the total number of reads in each experimental library and scaled to counts per million (CPM). Metagene density plots were generated from normalized coverage data across all RNUs. To account for variations in region lengths between main and pseudogene RNUs, the mode distance was used to scale the signal for each RNU class as follows: RNU1 (116 genes, 163 nt), RNU2 (39 genes, 190 nt) and RNU6 (505 genes, 106 nt).
