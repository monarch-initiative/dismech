# Molecular pathogenesis and gene therapy-based intervention of GTPBP3-related mitochondrial disease.
**Authors:** Zhang Y, Yao SY, Li J, Yu T, Liu H, Zhu N, Peng GX, Zheng WQ, Ma CR, Wang ED, Song C, Zhou XL
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-71750-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-71750-z)

## Content

Mitochondrial transfer RNA (mt-tRNA) modification determines organelle translation and function. GTPBP3 and MTO1 catalyze 5-taurinomethyluridine (τm5U) modification at wobble uridine of five mt-tRNAs. τm5U hypomodification causes mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, but the underlying pathogenesis and intervention strategy due to GTPBP3 mutations are lacking. In this study, we identify two genetic variants (c.689 A > C (p.Q230P) and c.1120 A > G (p.N374D)) of GTPBP3 in a Chinese proband with metabolic disorders and multisystem dysfunction. Mechanistically, Q230P and N374D mutations induce protein multimerization/aggregation, protease degradation, decreased GTPase activity, and tRNA modification to varying degrees, affecting mitochondrial translation, respiration, dynamics, and function. Homozygous N374D mutations in mice cause embryonic lethality; homozygous E230P or compound heterozygous E230P/N374D knock-in mice develop cardiac and muscular dysfunction due to altered mitochondrial translation. Mitochondrial dysfunction and pathology are efficiently reversed by virus-mediated GTPBP3 expression in cells and animals. This study provides valuable insights into the etiology of and promising intervention strategies for GTPBP3-related diseases.

Mitochondria perform various fundamental cellular functions including energy biogenesis through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Mitochondria harbor their own DNA (mtDNA). Human mtDNA contains two ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and 22 transfer RNA (tRNAs) genes that are required for the translation of 13 mtDNA-encoded proteins. These proteins are essential for the assembly and function of respiratory chain complexes I (CI), III (CIII), IV (CIV), and ATP synthase (CV)1–3.

The high efficiency and fidelity of mitochondrial translation determine both the quality and quantity of mtDNA-encoded proteins2,4. All nucleic acids involved in mitochondrial translation, including rRNAs, tRNAs, and mRNAs, are transcribed from mtDNA. All protein constituents, including tRNA-modifying enzymes, are encoded by nuclear DNA (nDNA), synthesized in the cytoplasm and then imported into organelles1–3. In contrast to more than 400 cytoplasmic tRNAs, only 22 mitochondrial tRNAs (mt-tRNAs) are used for human mitochondrial translation3,5.

Human mt-tRNAs undergo 18 types of post-transcriptional modifications that are required for proper tRNA architecture and function6. The wobble position (position 34) was the most extensively modified. Two mt-tRNAs (tRNALeu(UUR) and tRNATrp) contain 5-taurinomethyluridine (τm5U) and three mt-tRNAs (tRNAGlu, tRNAGln and tRNALys) predominantly harbor 5-taurinomethyl-2-thiouridine (τm5s2U) at U345–8. These 5-methyluridine derivatives promote decoding efficiency at cognate codons, and simultaneously restrict the tRNA decoding capacity at near-cognate codons in two codon boxes5,9,10. τm5U and τm5s2U are equivalent modifications to 5-carboxymethylaminomethyluridine (cmnm5U) and its 2-thio derivative, 5-carboxymethylaminomethyl-2-thiouridine (cmnm5s2U), found in bacterial tRNAs and yeast mt-tRNAs11. These homologous modifications are synthesized by MnmE/MSS1/GTPBP3 and MnmG/MTO1 protein families, respectively, suggesting pivotal and conserved functions across species7,12–16. In human mitochondria, nDNA-encoded GTPBP3 (hGTPBP3) and MTO1 (hMTO1) form a complex and use taurine, 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate (CH2-THF), GTP, and mt-tRNAs as substrates and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), potassium (K+) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) as cofactors to generate τm5U15. According to the mechanism of cmnm5U biogenesis, GTPBP3 binds to CH2-THF and exhibits K+-dependent GTPase activity, which is essential for tRNA modification17–19. MTO1 binds FAD and NADH and is hypothesized to contribute to tRNA binding and catalysis of τm5U formation15,20. Deficiency in τm5U modification has been tightly connected with various disorders5,21,22. MTO1 deletion in zebrafish, Caenorhabditis elegans, and other mammalian and mouse models leads to tRNA hypomodification, impairs mitochondrial translation, causing OXPHOS defects and cardiomyopathy23–27. hMTO1 genetic mutations are associated with various human pathologies including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, lactic acidosis, optic neuropathy, and cognitive disability24,28,29. Concerning mt-tRNAs, pathogenic mechanisms associated with the loss of the τm5U modification and the deficiency in codon decoding have been identified, such as the m.3243 A > G mutation in tRNALeu(UUR), causing mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS). The m.8344 A > G mutation in tRNALys has been shown to cause myoclonic epilepsy with shredded red fibers (MERRF)9,10. As far as GTPBP3 is concerned, depletion of GTPBP3 in mammalian cells or in a non-mammalian model (zebrafish) leads to CI deficiency, mitochondrial dysfunction, and abnormal cardiac development15,30.

To date, 35 hGTPBP3 genetic variants were identified in 30 pedigrees from eight countries, leading to Combined Oxidative Phosphorylation Deficiency 23 (OMIM #608536, COXPD23), an early-onset autosomal recessive disease characterized by hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and encephalopathy (Table S1)31–42. A previous study from our laboratory elucidated the molecular mechanism of specific pathogenic hGTPBP3 mutations in terms of protein abundance, mitochondrial targeting, interaction with hMTO1, and GTPase activity in vitro and in vivo19. However, the downstream effects of hGTPBP3 mutations on tRNA modification, mitochondrial structure, and function remain unknown. Moreover, pathogenic studies based on mammalian animal models of hGTPBP3 deficiency are currently lacking. Therefore, an effective intervention strategy to improve the phenotype of patients with hGTPBP3 mutations is required to mitigate disease symptoms.

In this study, we identified two variants of hGTPBP3: NM_032620.4 c.689 A > C (p.Q230P) and c.1120 A > G (p.N374D) in a COXPD23 patient who presented with the main clinical manifestations of dyspnea, convulsions, decreased muscle tone, myocardial damage, encephalomyopathy, hyperlactatemia, and metabolic acidosis. Interestingly, the N374D mutation has not been previously described clinically, whereas the Q230P mutation has been frequently reported (Table S1). We showed that the Q230P and N374D mutations cause the multimerization/aggregation and degradation of hGTPBP3 via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Moreover, these two mutations severely disrupted or nearly abolished enzyme activity. Alteration or loss of τm5U modification affected mitochondrial translation, respiration, and dynamics. We constructed homozygous E230P/E230P and heterozygous E230P/N374D knock-in mice, confirming that both mutations are detrimental to animal mitochondrial translation, function, mouse growth, and lifespan. Interestingly, virus-mediated exogenous expression of Gtpbp3 almost completely reversed all mitochondrial defects in cells, and in cardiac and muscular mitochondrial dysfunction in mice, representing a valuable and promising clinical intervention strategy for hGTPBP3-related mitochondrial diseases.

The patient (Table S2) was a 13-month-old girl who was referred to the Department of Critical Care Medicine of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (CHCMU) with clinical symptoms of fever, dyspnea, cyanosis, convulsions, consciousness disturbance, and decreased muscle tone after acute upper respiratory infections. The patient was G3P1 (Gravida 3, Para 1) and delivered normally at 37+5d-week gestation with a birth weight of 3300 g. The first and second fetuses previously conceived by the mother at different times were aborted owing to social factors (Fig. 1A). The patient’s parents were physically healthy and nonconsanguineous. The patient developed normally, but exhibited lethargy, poor responsiveness, and a pale face. Computed tomography (CT) showed pneumonia, right lung upper lobe consolidation, and atelectasis (Fig. S1A). The liver structure was normal but slightly swollen. Sonography revealed a normal pancreas, spleen, and kidney. Cardiac color ultrasound showed thickening of interventricular septum and posterior wall of the left ventricle (Fig. S1B). Electrocardiography (ECG) revealed ST-segment and T wave changes. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed bilateral enlargement of the frontotemporal extracerebral spaces, slight enlargement of the bilateral ventricles, increased volume of the bilateral thalamus, and symmetrical high signal in T2WI sequences. DWI sequences showed high signal in the bilateral thalamus, which indicated a diffusion limitation. The right pontine cerebellar call region was enlarged (Fig. S1C–F). Electroencephalogram (EEG) showed mixed activity of 2–4 HzδΘ increased during waking period. The lactate levels were remarkably high. The arterial blood gas analysis suggested decompensated metabolic acidosis (Table S3). Tandem mass spectrometry showed that the level of C3DC + C4OH, the ratio of C3DC + C4OH to C4, C8, C10, and C16 were significantly increased (Table S3). Subsequent determination of urine organic acids showed that various indicators increased significantly, suggesting an energy metabolism disorder (Table S3). To understand the genetic basis, whole-exome sequencing and mitochondrial genome testing were performed. The patient (proband) carried two missense alleles of hGTPBP3: c.689 A > C (p.Q230P) inherited from the father and c.1120 A > G (p.N374D) inherited from the mother (Fig. 1B). Notably, the c.689 A > C (p.Q230P) variant has been reported several times in recent independent studies, particularly in Chinese populations (Table S1)32–35,41,42, suggesting a high possibility of pathogenicity. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) variant-interpretation guidelines43, this variant is likely pathogenic (PM1 + PM2_Supporting+PM3_Strong+PP4). However, the c.1120 A > G (p.N374D) variant has not been previously identified and represents a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) (PM2_Supporting+PM3 + PP3 + PP4). Although classified as a VUS, our sequence and structural analyses suggested that it likely alters the function of the protein as a pivotal residue in GTP substrate accommodation, which has been experimentally verified (vide infra).

The hGTPBP3 precursor contains 492 amino acids, with the M1-C20 peptide as the mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS)19. The structure of the mature hGTPBP3 (T21-K492) has not been experimentally determined. AlphaFold predicted that hGTPBP3 contains three domains (Fig. S2A)44, with a model confidence score per residue of >90 in a large majority of proteins. The predicted structure of hGTPBP3 (AF-Q969Y2-F1) closely resembled that of bacterial MnmE proteins18,45. The N-terminal domain (N domain, T21-L153) binds 5,10-CH2-THF (an essential substrate for τm5U modification) and mediates the dimerization of hGTPBP318. The G domain (G249-P426) catalyzes K+-dependent GTP hydrolysis via dimerization, which is essential for τm5U modification17,18,46. The helical domain (HD) (N154-S248 and P427-K492) is separated by the inserted G domain18, possibly mediating GTPBP3-MTO1 interaction, but its precise function in τm5U modification remains unknown (Fig. S2A).

Primary sequence alignment was performed to analyze the two mutated sites. Q230 is not a conserved amino acid residue of the GTPBP3 family. For example, E230 was detected in the mouse Gtpbp3 (Fig. S2B). Spatially, Q230 is located in the fourth α-helix of the HD domain (Fig. S2A). According to the predicted structure of hGTPBP3, the amino group of the side chain of Q230 interacted directly with the carbonyl group of the main chain of L445 (Fig. S2C). However, in the predicted structure of mouse Gtpbp3 (AF-Q923K4-F1), the side chain of E230 pointed toward the solvent and had no contact with L445 (Fig. S2C). N374 is a conserved residue in the G domain (Fig. S2A, S2B). Notably, crystal structures showed that side chain amino and carbonyl groups in all N374 counterparts in other species, such as N335 in E. coli MnmE (EcMnmE) (PDB: 2GJ8)46, N348 in Chlorobium tepidum MnmE (CtMnmE) (PDB: 3GEE) and N335 in Nostoc MnmE (PDB: 3GEH)45, directly participate in recognition of GDP or GDP-AlFx (a GTP analog), with the assistance of the main chain amino group of other residues (A359 in EcMnmE, A376 in CtMnmE, and A362 in Nostoc MnmE) (Fig. S2D). We modeled the structure of hGTPBP3 bound to GMPPCP (a GTP analog), revealing that N374 was directly involved in GTP recognition in the same manner, with the help of the amino group of the main chain of C398 (Fig. S2D). Based on these analyses, N374 appears to be an essential residue for GTP interactions during GTP hydrolysis, which is consistent with its high evolutionary conservation. Therefore, the N374D mutation likely impairs the GTP-binding capacity and subsequently compromises the GTPase activity of hGTPBP3.

To investigate the GTPase activities of the two hGTPBP3 mutants, we expressed genes encoding the mature forms of the wild-type (WT) and the two hGTPBP3 mutants in E. coli. After initial purification by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography, subsequent analysis by gel filtration revealed that hGTPBP3 was primarily eluted as a dimer (approximately 12.7 mL on a Superdex 200 column), with a small peak corresponding to another high-molecular-weight form (8.82 mL). In contrast, the hGTPBP3-Q230P and –N374D mutants were predominantly eluted at a high molecular weight peak, with an elution volume of 8.6 mL (corresponding to a molecular weight >440 kDa based on standard protein elution volumes). Minor peak eluting (16.6 mL) was also obtained with either hGTPBP3-Q230P or-N374D, which may be the monomeric forms of the proteins (Fig. 1C). Further static light scattering (SLS) analyses confirmed that WT hGTPBP3 was predominantly dimeric, whereas hGTPBP3-Q230P or -N374D were predominantly multimers (Table S4). Using small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) analysis, we were able to fit the dimer model of Thermotoga maritima MnmE (TmMnmE) (PDB:1XZP)18 to the hGTPBP3 solution structure. The scattering signals from both mutants were too dispersed to fit the dimeric structure of TmMnmE, suggesting a multimeric state of both mutants (Fig. S3A).

Following these initial observations suggesting multimerization of hGTPBP3 mutants in solution, FLAG-tagged hGTPBP3 (hGTPBP3-FLAG) and the two mutants were expressed in human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells. The cells were lysed and centrifuged (13,800 g) to collect the supernatant and pellet. The pellets were treated with 8 M urea for solubilization to observe potential protein aggregation. Western blot analysis revealed that more amount of two mutants were present as high-molecular-weight multimers in the supernatant (soluble but not dissociated under gel denaturing conditions), as well as insoluble aggregates in the pellet. Accordingly, the intensity of the bands located around 55 kDa (molecular mass of hGTPBP3 = 53 kDa) was weaker for the two mutants than for the cell line containing the WT hGTPBP3 (the two bands represent the precursor and mature forms of hGTPBP3) (Fig. 1D).

The observed multimeric state suggests a structural alteration in hGTPBP3-Q230P and -N374D. We digested purified hGTPBP3 and its two mutants with proteinase K in vitro and found that both mutants degraded more rapidly than the WT enzyme. Particularly, hGTPBP3-N374D almost completely disappeared after one minute of digestion (Fig. S3B). Using dynamic light scattering (DLS), we found that the particles of the two mutants were larger than those of WT hGTPBP3, indicated by the autocorrelation function, which decays more slowly for larger particles (Fig. S3C). Circular dichroism (CD) analysis showed that the secondary structures of both mutants were significantly altered, with a decrease in α-helices and an increase in random coil regions (Fig. S3D, Table S5). Further determination of the melting temperature (Tm) revealed that both mutants exhibited lower Tm values than the WT hGTPBP3 (Table S5).

After individual expression of WT and mutant hGTPBP3 in HEK293T cells and inhibition of global protein synthesis by treatment with cycloheximide (CHX), we observed that the levels of the two mutants decreased rapidly (within two hours of treatment), whereas the level of WT hGTPBP3 remained unchanged even after 10 h of treatment (Fig. 1E, F). We also used MG132 to block the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway and found that the levels of both mutants significantly increased in the presence of the inhibitor (Fig. 1G). hGTPBP3 and its two mutants were co-expressed with HA-tagged ubiquitin (Ub-HA). Co-immunoprecipitation analyses showed that hGTPBP3-Q230P and -N374D were more ubiquitinated than hGTPBP3 with a higher molecular weight (>250 kDa) (Fig. 1H). Besides degradation in cytoplasm, we also explored the possibility of turnover of hGTPBP3 mutants by mitochondrial proteases. Degradation of mutant MTU1, the mitochondrial 2-thiouridine (s2U)-modifying enzyme, by caseinolytic peptidase (CLPP) has been observed47. There are three main proteases in the mitochondrial matrix, including CLPP (composed of CLPP and CLPX subunits), LONP1 and AFG3L248. We cloned all these genes into the eukaryotic expression plasmids with a specific tag at the C-terminus. We co-expressed Myc-tagged hGTPBP3, Q230P or N374D with CLPP-FLAG, CLPX-HA or CLPP-FLAG plus CLPX-HA in HEK293T cells. No reduction in protein level of hGTPBP3 or two mutants was observed (Fig. S4A). Similarly, when simultaneously expressing Myc-tagged hGTPBP3 or two mutants with FLAG-tagged LONP1 or AFG3L2, hGTPBP3 or two mutants were not more degraded (Fig. S4B). We further overexpressed CLPP subunits, LONP1 or AFG3L2 in wild-type, Q230P and N374D knockin cells (see results below) and found that none stimulated degradation of endogenous hGTPBP3 and its two mutants (Fig. S4C, D). These results demonstrate that the two hGTPBP3 mutants are not degraded in the mitochondrial matrix by the mitochondrial proteases.

hGTPBP3 forms a complex with hMTO1 for τm5U15,19. We further studied the effect of the mutations on hGTPBP3-hMTO1 interaction. The steady-state abundance of overexpressed hMTO1 in the whole cell lysate (WCL) was substantially lower when either mutant was coexpressed (Fig. S5A, B). Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays showed that interaction between hMTO1 and hGTPBP3 was enhanced by the mutations, in particular N374D (Fig. S5A, B). Considering the tendency to multimerization of the two hGTPBP3 mutants, we suggest that hMTO1 was possibly entangled in the hGTPBP3 multimers; thus, a seemingly enhanced interaction between hMTO1 and hGTPBP3 mutants and a reduced level of hMTO1 in the WCL were observed.

The in vitro GTPase activities of WT hGTPBP3 and the two mutants were then compared by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using [α−32P]GTP19. The GTP hydrolysis efficiency (Kcat/Km) of both mutants dropped significantly to 43.3% and 3.3% for the Q230P and N374D mutants, respectively, with higher Km (approximately 7-fold for N374D) and lower Kcat values (Table S6). We compared the heat sensitivity of hGTPBP3 and the Q230P mutant, which has residual activity, and found that the Q230P mutant was more susceptible to heat stress than hGTPBP3, confirming a local conformational change (Fig. S6A, B). However, activity of N374D mutant was below the detection limit; and thus a signal sufficient to evaluate thermal inactivation could not be obtained. Microscale thermophoresis (MST) determination showed that the equilibrium binding parameter (Kd) of hGTPBP3-N374D for GppNHp (a stable analogue of GTP) was significantly increased to 40.43 ± 2.99 μM versus 12.44 ± 2.73 and 10.42 ± 1.71 μM for hGTPBP3-Q230P and WT hGTPBP3, respectively (Fig. S6C), also suggesting that the Q230P mutation in the HD domain does not influence GTP binding. Thus, the kinetic data and Kd were consistent with the spatial location of N374 in the GTP binding site (Fig. S2D); the N374D mutation effectively disrupted GTP binding and led to a change in GTPase activity.

These results suggest that the two mutations alter the conformation of hGTPBP3, which likely leads to the multimerization and aggregation of the protein and its degradation by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. In addition, these two mutations alter GTPase activity to varying degrees.

To investigate the cellular effects of the two mutations in hGTPBP3, we constructed two HEK293T knock-in cell lines expressing hGTPBP3-Q230P and hGTPBP3-N374D using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (Fig. S7A). These were designated as Q230P and N374D cell lines, respectively. Consistent with the observed ectopic expression (Figs. 1D, S7), the level of hGTPBP3-Q230P in Q230P cells was substantially lower than that of native hGTPBP3 in WT cells. Moreover, hGTPBP3-N374D was barely detected in N374D cells, and the level of endogenous hMTO1 in both edited cell lines was dramatically decreased compared to that in WT cells (Fig. S7B), suggesting a stabilizing effect of hGTPBP3 on hMTO1. The lower mitochondrial content of hGTPBP3-Q230P and -N374D was also confirmed by immunofluorescence (IF) assays, as indicated by the weaker green fluorescence signals in both mutant cell lines (Fig. S7C). IF analyses of endogenous hGTPBP3 in the two mutant cell lines (Fig. S7C) or overexpression of hGTPBP3-Q230P and -N374D in HEK293T cells (Fig. S7D) showed that the Q230P and N374D mutations did not influence mitochondrial targeting. Interestingly, the culture medium of N374D cells turned yellow, in contrast to that of WT or Q230P cells at the same conditions, indicating elevated lactate production and acidification in N374D cells (Fig. S7E). Cell growth was significantly lower than that of WT HEK293T cells in both mutant cell lines, particularly in the N374D cell line (Fig. S7F).

Similar levels of the five τm5(s2)U34-containing hmtRNAs were observed in wild-type and mutant cell lines by northern blot (Fig. 2B,C), suggesting that the two pathogenic mutations do not influence mitochondrial transcription. Similarly, we found that the copy number of mtDNA in both mutant cell lines was comparable to that in WT cells, suggesting that the two mutations did not influence mitochondrial DNA replication (Fig. S9C). However, when mitochondrial transcription was blocked using ethidium bromide (EB), the amount of hmtRNALeu(UUR) in both mutant cell lines decreased progressively and significantly (with a half-life approximately 20 h), whereas hmtRNALeu(UUR) in WT cells remained relatively stable. In contrast, the amount of hmtRNALys in the three cell lines remained comparable at the same time points (Fig. S9D, E). These observations suggest that the τm5U34-hypomodified hmtRNALeu(UUR) is more prone to degradation. No alteration in steady-state of hmtRNALeu(UUR) in the mutant cells (Fig. 2B, C) implies that the reduced half-life of hmtRNALeu(UUR) (~20 h) is insufficient to cause a significant decrease in its abundance with an active and successive mtDNA transcription. Northern blotting performed under acidic conditions, which allowed the visualization of charged and uncharged tRNA, showed that the aminoacylation levels of hmtRNALeu(UUR), hmtRNAGlu, hmtRNAGln and hmtRNATrp were unchanged in both mutant cell lines. Aminoacylation of hmtRNALys was mildly but significantly decreased in the N374D cell line, but not in the Q230P cell line (Fig. 2D, F), suggesting that the charging of hmtRNALys by mitochondrial lysyl-tRNA synthetase was slightly impaired when taurine modification was missing.

In summary, we constructed Q230P and N374D cell lines and globally demonstrated that the Q230P and N374D mutations led to a defect in τm5U biogenesis in vivo with different effects on the tRNAs that carry it.

These reductions in OXPHOS subunits encoded by mtDNA and nDNA resulted in a clear decrease in the CI of Q230P and N374D cell lines, as shown by blue native PAGE (BN-PAGE) (Fig. 3C). There was no observable change in the abundance of other OXPHOS complexes. The CI and CIV complexes were significantly less active in the two mutant cell lines (Fig. 3D), consistent with reduced amounts of MT-ND3, MT-ND6, and MT-CO1 (Fig. 3A, B). The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), detected by fluorescence microscopy and a tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester perchlorate (TMRE) probe, was also lower in both mutant cell lines (Fig. 3E, F). The level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in both mutant cell lines was significantly higher than in WT cells, as measured by flow cytometry using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe (Fig. 3G). Cellular ROS frequently cause post-translational protein modifications. In agreement, higher levels of global protein carbonylation were observed in the two mutant cell lines, particularly in N374D cells (Fig. 3H).

We compared the mitochondrial metabolic activities of the three cell lines. The overall oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in the two mutant cell lines, particularly in N374D cells, was significantly lower (Fig. 3I), including lower levels of basal respiration, maximal respiration, ATP production, and proton leakage (Fig. S10A). Determination of glycolytic activity by the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) showed that the level of glycolysis in the Q230P cell line was comparable to that in WT cells, whereas that in the N374D cell line was considerably higher (Fig. 3J, Fig, S10B). In detail, the overall glycolysis activity, the maximum ECAR rate (glycolytic capacity), glycolytic reserve and other sources of extracellular acidification not attributed to glycolysis (non-glycolytic acidification) were significantly higher only in N374D, but not Q230P cell line (Fig. 3J, Fig. S10B). These data indicates that the glycolytic activity of N374D cells increased, to hypothetically compensate for the energy deficit due to more severely impaired aerobic respiration, which is consistent with the change in color of the medium during cell culture (Fig. S7E).

Taken together, these data show that the Q230P and N374D mutations lead to alterations in mitochondrial translation, respiration, and metabolism.

Mitochondrial dysfunction frequently leads to an altered cell fate. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that neither the Q230P nor N374D mutations triggered cell apoptosis (Fig. S10C). Similarly, neither the Q230P nor N374D mutations induced cell cycle variation (Fig. S10D).

Quantification of TEM images revealed that the ratio of abnormal mitochondria (designated as those with enlarged volume, destroyed cristae, or morphology) significantly increased in the two mutant cell lines (Fig. 4C). In response to mitochondrial damage, the mutant cells appeared to produce more mitochondria to remedy mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 4D). Given the structural and functional abnormalities of the mitochondria observed in the two mutant cell lines, we investigated whether mitophagy was activated. We first isolated mitochondria from the three cell lines and detected the abundance of proteins representative of mitophagy using western blotting. Strikingly, levels of proteins including p62, LC3-II, (poly)Ubiquitin, MFN2 and Parkin, which functioned as “adaptor molecules” in mitophagy50, were significantly higher in both mutant cell lines (Fig. 4E). The increase in MFN2 was consistent with the enlargement of the mitochondrial surface observed in both mutant cell lines (Fig. 4B). When Bafilomycin A1 was added to inhibit autophagic flux, the amounts of these “adaptor molecules” increased significantly in all three cell lines (Fig. 4F), suggesting that mitophagic flux was not impaired in the two mutant cell lines under physiological conditions. To confirm the activation of mitophagy in the two mutant cell lines, we studied the cellular localization of LC3 and p62 in the three cell lines using IF. In both mutant cell lines, the p62 and LC3 signals were brighter, indicating higher protein levels (Fig. 4E) in the form of obvious puncta (Fig. 4G). Some p62 or LC3 signals colocalized with the mitochondria, whereas others accumulated around the mitochondrial surface in both mutant cell lines. In contrast, the co-localization of p62 or LC3 with mitochondria in WT cells was less evident (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, we determined whether the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt)51 was activated in the two mutant cell lines. Real-time quantitative PCR analyses showed that the mRNA levels of representative UPRmt-relevant genes51 were significantly lower in Q230P and/or N374D cells (Fig. S10E). Moreover, the abundance of mitochondrial proteases, which are typical players in the UPRmt52, including LONP1, AFG3L2, and YME1L1, was comparable between the WT and the two mutant cell lines (Fig. S10F). These results suggest that damaged mitochondria were not rescued by the UPRmt but were cleared by mitophagy.

A deficit in energy production is linked to alterations in cell signaling pathways. Strikingly, phosphorylation of S6K and S6 increased significantly, whereas phosphorylation of AKT decreased slightly in the two mutant cell lines; however, the protein level of mTOR was comparable in all three cell lines (Fig. S10G). These results show that the mTOR signaling pathway was more active in the two mutant cell lines, especially in N374D cells. Notably, when the three cell lines were treated with rapamycin to inhibit the mTOR pathway, more p62 and LC3 puncta were co-localized with or adjacent to the mitochondria in the two mutant cell lines, as shown by IF (Fig. S10H), suggesting that inhibition of the mTOR pathway further alters mitochondrial quality and accelerates the clearance of damaged mitochondria in the two mutant cells. Notably, mTOR activation and rapamycin-stimulated mitophagy were more pronounced in N374D cells (Fig. S10G, H).

Overall, these results show that taurine modification deficiency changes mitochondrial morphology and activates the mitophagy pathway to eliminate dysfunctional mitochondria, with the activation of the mTOR pathway as a possible compensatory effect, in the human cell line.

We first isolated hmtRNALeu(UUR) from the HEK293T, N374D, N374D + WT, and N374D + N259A cell lines. UPLC-MS/MS analyses showed that τm5U34 biogenesis in hmtRNALeu(UUR) from the N374D + WT cell line was almost completely restored, while τm5U34 modification was undetectable in N374D + N259A cells (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Data 1). Overexpression of hGTPBP3 in N374D cells effectively stabilized hmtRNALeu(UUR) (Fig. 5C, D), and the aminoacylation level of hmtRNALys was increased by the overexpression of hGTPBP3, but not by the hGTPBP3-N259A mutation (Fig. 5E, F).

Western blot analysis showed that the abundance of mtDNA-encoded proteins, such as MT-ND3, MT-ND6, MT-CO1, and nDNA-encoded NDUFS1, in the N374D + WT cell line was significantly enhanced to a level comparable to that in HEK293T cells. However, the abundance of these proteins remained unchanged in the presence of hGTPBP3-N259A overexpressed protein (Fig. 5G). The CI assembly in the N374D + WT cell line, but not in the N374D + N259A cell line, was significantly improved, as shown by BN-PAGE, and was close to that of the HEK293T cell line (Fig. 5H). The activity of both CI and CIV in the N374D + WT cell line, but not in the N374D + N259A cell line, increased to levels comparable to those observed in the HEK293T cell line (Fig. 5I). OCR analyses confirmed that mitochondrial respiratory activity recovered significantly to a level comparable to that in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5J). Global protein carbonylation levels were also reduced in N374D cells overexpressing WT hGTPBP3, suggesting that oxidative stress was eliminated in N374D + WT cells. This effect was not observed when hGTPBP3-N259A was overexpressed (Fig. S11A). Therefore, it is not surprising that N374D + WT cells exhibited higher cell growth than N374D cells, despite having a slightly lower growth rate than WT cells (Fig. S11B).

TEM analyses showed that after overexpression of WT hGTPBP3, both the increase in mitochondrial volume and the increase in mitochondrial number per cell in N374D cells were significantly reduced to levels comparable to those in HEK293T cells (Fig. S11C–F). Moreover, the percentage of abnormal mitochondria was significantly reduced in N374D + WT cells, although it was still slightly higher than in HEK293T cells (Fig. S11F). No improvements in mitochondrial structure/morphology were observed in N374D + N259A cells (Fig. S11C–F).

Taken together, almost all mitochondrial dysfunctions observed in the N374D cell line were effectively corrected by stably expressing WT hGTPBP3, but not catalysis-deficient hGTPBP3.

E230 and N374 in mouse Gtpbp3 correspond to Q230 and N374 in hGTPBP3, respectively (Fig. S2B). We constructed mouse Gtpbp3 E230P and N374D, respectively. After gene expression in NIH/3T3 cell line, western blot analysis showed that either mutation influenced protein level (Fig. S12A). This result is in contrast to the destabilizing effect of human mutations on hGTPBP3 level (Figs. 1D, S5, S7B) but consistent with the data from mutant mice with E230P and N374D mutations (see results below), suggesting that Gtpbp3 mutants were not rapidly degraded in mouse cells. Considering the amino acid difference at position 230 between hGTPBP3 and mouse Gtpbp3, we purified wild-type Gtpbp3 and Gtpbp3-E230P mutant. GTPase kinetics determination showed that E230P mutation reduced the catalytic efficiency of GTP hydrolysis of mouse Gtpbp3 to approximately 38% (Fig. S12B), aligning with the residual ~43% GTPase activity of hGTPBP3-Q230P (Table S6).

To further investigate the pathogenic features of hGTPBP3 Q230P and N374D mutations, we generated two heterozygous whole-body Gtpbp3 knock-in mice, Gtpbp3-E230P (-/+) and Gtpbp3-N374D (-/+), via CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing (Fig. S13A). Through heterozygous self-mating and cross-mating, we successfully obtained homozygous Gtpbp3-E230P (designated as E/E) and compound heterozygous Gtpbp3-E230P/N374D (designated as E/N) mice; however, homozygous Gtpbp3-N374D (designated as N/N) mice could not be obtained (Fig. S13B). These results suggested that the homozygous N374D mutation at the conserved GTP-binding site in Gtpbp3 is lethal for embryonic development. The genotype of the E/N mice was similar to that of the patient. E/N and E/E mice were used as models to understand the effects of these two mutations at the animal level.

We first studied the growth of WT and mutant mice from the same litter and found that at 12 weeks of age, the body lengths of adult E/E and E/N mice were comparable to that of WT mice (Fig. S13B). In parallel with progressive weight loss, the lifespan of both mutant mice was significantly reduced. The median survival of E/E mice was 36 weeks, whereas that of E/N mice was 25 weeks (Fig. 6B). The heart is particularly sensitive to mitochondrial dysfunctions. Western blot analysis showed that the abundance of Gtpbp3 in mouse heart was significantly higher than that in other tissues (Fig. S13C). Cardiomyopathy is one of the most apparent phenotypes in our case and in patients reported elsewhere31,32,34,35,39–41, so we examined heart function in both mouse models. First, we found that the cardiac volume of E/E and E/N mice was significantly larger than that of WT mice (Fig. 6C) and that the heart-to-body weight ratio (HW/BW) was also higher in the two mutant mice (Fig. 6D). Masson staining of the hearts from 14-week-old mice showed that E/E and E/N mice developed cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis (Fig. 6E). Quantification of the relative ratio of the cardiac fibrotic area to the cardiac area revealed that the percentage of fibrotic area was approximately 2.01% in E/E mice and 2.77% in E/N mice, compared with approximately 0.09% in WT mice (Fig. 6F). Additionally, the primary echocardiographic assessment of left ventricular (LV) function revealed abnormal cardiac function in adult mutant mice (Fig. 6G). Cardiac function was continuously monitored every two weeks between weeks 6 and 12 using LV longitudinal echocardiography (LVLE). The results showed that the LV ejection fraction (LVEF), LV fractional shortening (LVFS), and cardiac output parameters were significantly lower in both mutant mice than in WT mice (Fig. 6H–J), whereas the LV end-systolic diameter (LVESD) and LV end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) were significantly higher in E/E and E/N mice than in WT mice, parallel to the age (Fig. 6K, L) at almost all ages. In particular, all parameters of the two mutant mice showed significant differences from those of the WT animals at the age of six weeks (Fig. 6H–L). However, growth (measured as body weight) (Fig. 6A) and lifespan (Fig. 6B) were not affected at this age. These data suggest that heart dysfunction precedes the disruptive effects on mouse growth and survival. Notably, all parameters tested showed that cardiac dysfunction in E/N mice was much more severe than that in E/E mice, consistent with the absolute conservation of N374 (Fig. S2B) and its pivotal role in GTP binding during catalysis (Fig. S6C, Table S6).

In addition, a metabolic cage experiment was performed to investigate whether the Gtpbp3-E230P and -N374D mutations influence global metabolism. O2 consumption and CO2 production in E/E mice were only slightly lower, whereas those in E/N mice were significantly lower than those in WT mice (Fig. S13D, E). Moreover, heat production in E/E mice was slightly attenuated, but that in E/N mice was less than 50% of that in WT mice (Fig. S13F). These results suggest that the Gtpbp3-E230P and -N374D mutations alter metabolism in mice. Given that muscle is one of the most important heat-producing organs in the body, Gtpbp3 expression was high in muscle (Fig. S13C) and that muscle tone was decreased in the patient, we further determined the development and function of mouse skeletal muscle. We first assessed muscle exercise performance in live mice at 10 weeks of age using the rotarod test, which showed that the falling speed in E/E and E/N mice was significantly decreased (Fig. S13G). The grip strength of E/E and E/N mice was also lower than that of WT mice (Fig. S13H).

Collectively, these data from mouse models reveal that Gtpbp3-E230P and -N374D mutations lead to cardiac and muscle dysfunction and considerably shorten the lifespan of mice.

Based on the cardiac and muscle dysfunctions observed in E/E and E/N mice, we investigated whether E/E and E/N mice had defects in mitochondrial translation and function in these two organs.

The steady-state levels of Mt-Nd3 (CI subunit), Mt-Co1 (CIV subunit), and/or Mt-Nd6 (CI subunit) were significantly lower in both organs of mutant mice (Fig. S14A). The levels of Ndufs1 (CI subunit) encoded by nDNA were also lower in both organs of the two mutant mice, whereas other OXPHOS components encoded by nDNA, including Sdhb (CII subunit), Uqcrc2 (CIII subunit), Cox4 (CIV subunit), and Atp5a1 (CV subunit), were comparable in both organs of the three mouse lines (Fig. S14B). Surprisingly, Gtpbp3 levels were comparable in the hearts and muscles of WT and mutant mice (Fig. S14B). These data conflict with the results obtained from human cells, where the hGTPBP3-Q230P and -N374 mutations resulted in a reduction in the amount of overexpressed hGTPBP3 in HEK293T cells (Fig. 1D, S5) or in the reduction of endogenous hGTPBP3 in knock-in cells (Fig. S7B), but align with the comparable levels of overexpressed Gtpbp3 and its two mutants (Fig. S12A). The reason for this striking difference in the E230P mutant is probably that E230 does not interact with L445 in mouse Gtpbp3 (Fig. S2C). However, given the absolute conservation of N374 in the MnmE/GTPBP3 protein family (Fig. S2B) and the reduced amount of the hGTPBP3-N374D mutant (Figs. 1D, S5, S7B), it is unclear why the Gtpbp3-N374D mutant was stable in mutant mice. Level of Mto1 was not changed among WT and mutant mice (Fig. S14C), again suggesting the stabilizing effect of Gtpbp3 on Mto1. Given the activated mTOR and mitophagy activities in hGTPBP3 mutant cell lines (Fig. 4E–G, S10G), we further determined the levels of key players of these two pathways among WT and mutant mice and found comparable levels of phosphorylated S6 and 4EBP1 (Fig. S14C). These results suggest that neither pathway was activated in the mutant mice and distinct responses to taurine modification deficiency between cell and animal levels.

BN-PAGE analyses showed that the steady-state level of CI was significantly lower, whereas the other three complexes were not altered in the hearts and muscles of the two mutant mice (Fig. S14D). Moreover, the levels of the supercomplex (SC) of cardiac and muscular mitochondrial OXPHOS were dramatically lower in both mutant mice (Fig. S14D). Consequently, CI and CIV activities were significantly lower in E/E and E/N mice (Fig. S14E).

The area of the cardiac mitochondria was enlarged, and almost all cardiac mitochondria exhibited disrupted cristae to different degrees in the two mutant mice (Fig. S14F, G). The percentage of abnormal cardiac mitochondria was 25.14% in E/E mice and 44.36% in E/N mice compared to 4.16% in WT mice (Fig. S14H).

Regarding the morphology of muscle mitochondria, the alteration of muscular mitochondria was much more severe than that of cardiac mitochondria. For example, the cristae of muscle mitochondria in E/N mice were almost completely disrupted (Fig. S14I). At the same time, the surface area of muscle mitochondria in E/E and E/N mice decreased dramatically (Fig. S14J), as did the significantly higher percentage of abnormal muscle mitochondria, 44.00% and 84.57% in E/E and E/N mice, respectively, compared to 8.89% in WT mice (Fig. S14K).

Overall, these results suggest that the Gtpbp3-E230P and -N374D mutations significantly altered mitochondrial translation in the heart and muscle, resulting in reduced steady-state and OXPHOS activities and altered mitochondrial morphology.

We found that the enlarged cardiac morphology of E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice was completely reversed to a size comparable to that of WT mice (Fig. 7B). The ratio of heart weight to body weight decreased in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice (Fig. 7C). Masson’s staining showed that cardiac fibrosis was attenuated in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice (Fig. 7D). Further quantification revealed that the fibrotic area in the hearts of E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice was 0.18% (0.15% in hearts of WT mice; 2.42% in hearts of E/N + AAV9-Ctr mice) (Fig. 7E).

Echocardiographic analyses were performed monthly for 3 months (2–4 months after viral infection) to monitor cardiac function. Primary echocardiography showed that the cardiac function of E/N mice was restored one month after AAV9-Gtpbp3 injection (Fig. 7F). Regular analyses revealed that AAV9-Gtpbp3 completely restored cardiac function in E/N mice to levels comparable to those of WT mice within one month after injection, and that the effect lasted for at least 2 months (Fig. 7G–K). All five parameters, including LVEF, LVFS, cardiac output, LVEDD, and LVESD, returned to normal levels in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice. However, in E/N + AAV9-Ctr mice, cardiac function became abnormal before 2 months of age and progressively decreased or increased during the detection period, as observed in native E/N mice (Fig. 7G–K).

Since the rAAV9 virus can infect both the heart and muscle, we wanted to understand whether the expression of Gtpbp3 by the rAAV9 virus could improve mitochondrial defects in the muscle. Western blot analysis showed that Gtpbp3 levels were indeed abundant in both the heart and muscle tissues of E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice, but not in E/N + AAV9-Ctr mice, suggesting that the AAV9-Gtpbp3 virus effectively infects heart and muscle tissues and that the CMV promoter efficiently drives the expression of Gtpbp3 (Fig. 7L, Fig. S15A). To detect τm5U modification, we enriched mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) from muscle of WT, E/N + AAV9-Ctr, and E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice. Liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS) for tRNA fragment analysis showed that τm5U in mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) was nearly completely rescued in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice; however, that in E/N + AAV9-Ctr was missing (Fig. S16). These results demonstrate efficient restoration of τm5U modification upon Gtpbp3 expression.

The abundance of Mt-Nd3 or Ndufs1 in the heart or muscle of E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice increased to a level comparable to that in WT mice at three months (infection for two months). A similar elevation in the level of Ndufs3 was observed in both tissues of the infected mice (Fig. 7L, Fig. S15A). Consistently, the steady-state levels of cardiac mitochondrial CI and SC in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice were significantly elevated, albeit to a lesser extent than in WT mice (Fig. 7M). Accordingly, cardiac mitochondrial CI and CIV activities were effectively rescued in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice (Fig. 7N). Similarly, the steady-state abundance of CI and SC, and the activities of OXPHOS CI and CV in muscle mitochondria were also significantly rescued in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice (Fig. S15B, C). However, the activity of CIV only increased from 27.88 to 74.45% compared to that in WT mice (Fig. S15C).

TEM analyses revealed that both the disrupted cristae and enlarged cardiac mitochondrial surface area were effectively repaired in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 (Fig. 7O, P). Simultaneously, the percentage of abnormal cardiac mitochondria in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice significantly decreased from 52.40% in E/N + AAV9-Ctr mice to 22.80%, which was close to 13.82% in WT mice (Fig. 7Q). In the muscle, we also observed effective amelioration of disrupted cristae and an increase in mitochondrial area in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice (Fig. S15D, E). Furthermore, the level of abnormal muscle mitochondria in E/N + AAV9-Gtpbp3 mice decreased to 31.64% compared to 81.50% in E/N + AAV9-Ctr mice, although it was still significantly higher than 8% in WT mice (Fig. S15F).

Overall, these results show that Gtpbp3 overexpression via adenovirus efficiently abrogated cardiomyopathy and cardiac fibrosis, and improved cardiac and muscle mitochondrial translation, respiratory activity, and morphology.

In this study, we identified two genetic variants of the hGTPBP3 locus. The patient exhibited clinical manifestations typical of mitochondrial disease, and genetic screening expanded the known spectrum of pathogenic mutations in hGTPBP3. In vitro and in vivo analyses suggested that the Q230P and N374D mutants are prone to multimerization and aggregation. Q230 interacts with L445 in hGTPBP3. Alteration of the hydrophilic residue Q230 to a rigid proline likely disrupts this interaction and modifies the local protein conformation. A recent study reported the cryo-EM structure of bacterial MnmE and MnmG, which showed that the helical domain of MnmE mediates interaction between MnmE and MnmG subunits55. Considering that Q230 is in the helical domain, Q230P mutation possibly induces a structural rearrangement in the local helix domain and thus influences affinity between hGTPBP3 and hMTO1. We observed a seemingly enhanced interaction between hMTO1 and hGTPBP3 mutants in Co-IP, which was possibly derived from hijacking of hMTO1 during multimerization of hGTPBP3 mutants. The detailed effects of Q230P mutation on affinity between hGTPBP3 and hMTO1 awaits further explorations. The residue N374, buried in the G domain, is essential for GTP binding. The N374D mutation reduces GTP-binding affinity and potentially leaves a more vacant active site. However, the dimerization of the G domain occurs only after GTP binding17,45. The N374D mutation likely influences the correct dimerization of the G domain. Accordingly, the N374D mutation almost completely abolished GTP hydrolysis activity, whereas the Q230P mutation, by influencing the local structure, led to an intermediate reduction in GTPase activity. The net result of these mutations could be the loss of GTPase activity as well as structural instability, leading to the formation of multimers, aggregation, ubiquitination, and degradation.

In addition to the reduced abundance of the two hGTPBP3 mutants, we observed impaired (for the Q230P mutant) or almost abolished (for the N374D mutant) GTPase activity in vitro and tRNA modification efficiency in cells. Damage to mitochondrial translation, respiration, metabolism, and morphology in human cells is proportional to the impairment of GTPase activity. All deleterious effects caused by the Q230P mutant were intermediate, whereas those caused by the N374D mutant were more severe. In knock-in mice, the protein levels of the E230P and N374D mutants were similar to those of Gtpbp3 in WT mice. However, N374D homozygous mice were embryonically lethal, whereas E230P homozygous and E230P/N374D heterozygous mice were viable, confirming a more serious effect of the N374D mutation. These analyses support the idea that impaired tRNA modification activity is the main cause of mitochondrial dysfunction in both cells and animals. In this regard, despite the identification of an unknown E3 ligase that mediates the ubiquitination of hGTPBP319, it appears to be insufficient to relieve patient manifestations by inhibiting hGTPBP3 proteolysis, particularly if the tRNA modification activity is significantly reduced.

The deficiency of taurine modification in the two mutant cell lines selectively influenced the aminoacylation and stability of hmtRNAs. Aminoacylation of hmtRNALys and the stability of hmtRNALeu(UUR) decreased in N374D cells. Taurine modification may locally remodel the tRNA structure and influence the recognition of hmtRNALys or hmtRNALeu(UUR) by mitochondrial lysyl-tRNA synthetase or unknown tRNA nuclease(s). In vitro translation assays have shown that the τm5U modification of hmtRNALeu(UUR) improves the decoding efficiency of the UUG Leu codon9. Similarly, the τm5s2U modification of hmtRNALys is essential for the translation of AAA and AAG Lys codons10. In MELAS patients with m.3243 A > G, MT-ND6 appears to be the most affected protein due to the high number of UUG codons5,9. Here, we show that a global reduction (or almost complete loss) of the τm5(s2)U modification in mt-tRNAs, due to hGTPBP3 mutations impairs not only the translation of MT-ND6, but also of MT-ND3, MT-CO1 and MT-ATP6. However, the translation of MT-ND1, MT-ND2, MT-ND4, MT-ND5, MT-CYB, MT-CO2, and MT-CO3 was largely unaltered. These results suggest that the loss of τm5(s2)U34 has a targeted effect on the translation of specific mt-mRNAs. We analyzed the Leu(UUR), Trp, Lys, Glu, and Gln codon usage in 13 mtDNA-encoded proteins (Fig. S17). The frequencies of Leu(UUR) and Glu codons in MT-ND3 and MT-ND6 were strikingly higher than those in other mtDNA-encoded proteins. Therefore, it is plausible that taurine modification deficiency affects the translation of MT-ND3 and MT-ND6 owing to inefficient codon-anticodon pairing. MT-CO1 and MT-ATP6 mRNAs did not show high usage of the Leu (UUR), Trp, Lys, Glu, and Gln codons. Conversely, other mt-mRNAs have a relatively high content of codons that require τm5(s2)U-tRNAs to be decoded. For example, MT-CO2 and MT-CO3 mRNAs have a high usage of Glu, Gln, or Trp codons. However, the protein abundance did not change in the mutant cell lines. These analyses suggest that codon usage is not the only factor that determines the sensitivity of mt-mRNA translation to τm5(s2)U hypomodification. As one of the functions of the τm5U modification is to restrict decoding capacity in two-codon boxes3,5, it is plausible that τm5U defects also alter translation fidelity. For instance, loss of τm5U in tRNALeu(UUR) may make UAA anticodon to recognize UUU Phe codons in error, thus leading to Phe-to-Leu misincorporation (mis-reading of UUC Phe codons by unmodified UAA anticodon has been excluded using an in vitro translation assay)9. The translation errors of Cys-to-Trp, His-to-Gln, Asn-to-Lys, and Asp-to-Gln would result from a similar situation. Further research is required to determine whether these different possible amino acid replacements are present in mtDNA-encoded proteins, including those with constant levels.

We observed that the damaged mitochondria in the two mutant cell lines were cleared by mitophagy, and that mitochondrial dysfunction did not activate apoptosis or the UPRmt pathway. Furthermore, we observed that mutant cells activated the mTOR pathway. We propose that a higher level of mTOR activity enhances the translation of mitochondrial proteins encoded by the nucleus to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, thus serving as a compensatory mechanism to counteract alterations in mitochondrial structure and function56. Activation of mTOR has also been reported in mitochondrial diseases resulting from either mtDNA or nDNA mutations57,58. Alternatively, it has been revealed that active mTOR increases transcription of the transcription factor TonEBP/NFAT5, which directly binds the TonE site of taurine transporter (TauT) promoter to enhance taurine uptake activity59–61. It is possible that the activated mTOR pathway is to increase cellular taurine availability to compensate the reduced tRNA taurine modification activity in GTPBP3 mutant cells. The precise molecular mechanism underlying the enhanced mTOR activity and its subsequent consequences in GTPBP3 mutant cells awaits further exploration. However, the mTOR activity was not elevated in the N374D mutant mice, suggesting different responses to τm5U deficiency between cell and animal levels.

Several non-mammalian and mammalian animal models of MTO1 depletion have been described23–27. GTPBP3-deficient animal models have only been described in C. elegans and zebrafish26,30. Deletion of the GTPBP3 homolog (MTCU-1) in C. elegans has mild effects on OXPHOS function, but significantly decreases membrane potential and fertility. The depletion of GTPBP3 in zebrafish impairs mitochondrial translation and alters heart morphology and function. To the best of our knowledge, E/E and E/N mice represent the first mammalian animal models with GTPBP3-functional deficiency. These mice mimic patient characteristics, such as cardiac hypertrophy and skeletal muscle weakness. Both MTO1 and GTPBP3 dysfunction in vertebrates is associated with mitochondrial and cardiac dysfunction28,31, highlighting the critical importance of taurine modification for mRNA translation in cardiomyocytes and interpreting the common manifestations of cardiomyopathy in human patients with hGTPBP3 or hMTO1 genetic variants28,31. Consistently, we found that cardiac and muscle mitochondrial translation was impaired in the E/E and E/N mice.

Mitochondrial diseases are clinically heterogeneous disorders for which there are very few therapeutic treatment strategies62,63. Current options focus on symptom management, such as increasing mitochondrial mass, maintaining redox homeostasis, or regulating metabolic pathways. However, methods aimed at improving biochemical defects caused by a particular genetic variant are limited64. Regarding taurine modification deficiency, oral administration of taurine appears to improve stroke-like episodes in MELAS patients65,66; however, elevation of τm5U level in mutant tRNALeu(UUR) is not observed when culturing MELAS patient’s myoblasts carrying the m.3243 A > G mutation in the presence of 10 mM taurine67. It has recently been reported that MTO1 overexpression in patient-derived myoblasts can restore the τm5(s2)U modification in mutant tRNALeu(UUR) or tRNALys and, therefore, represents a promising clinical choice for MELAS or MERRF patients67. In this study, we demonstrate that virus-mediated overexpression of WT Gtpbp3 almost completely restores τm5(s2)U modification, mitochondrial translation, OXPHOS assembly, and respiratory activities at both cellular and animal levels. Adeno-associated viruses are clinically demonstrated strategies with safety, high efficacy, and excellent targeting53. AAV9-Gtpbp3 gene therapy can be maintained for several months, offering clear advantages over traditional symptomatic treatments. These results suggest that gene therapy using WT GTPBP3 expression is an effective and promising strategy for the intervention or treatment of hGTPBP3-related mitochondrial diseases, especially to ameliorate symptoms in the heart and muscle. However, whether AAV-based gene therapy is able to rescue phenotypes in other tissues/organs, such as brain, is not experimentally studied in the present work and need further explorations. In this regard, Q230P and N374D knock-in cells, as well as E/E and E/N mice, are excellent cellular and animal models for future studies.

In conclusion, we identified two pathogenic mutations in hGTPBP3. Both mutations caused local structural alterations, protein multimerization in solution, pellet aggregation, and ubiquitin-mediated degradation in cells. Moreover, both mutations, particularly N374D, significantly impaired tRNA modification activity and mitochondrial translation of MT-ND6, MT-ND3, MT-CO1, and MT-ATP6, resulting in profound mitochondrial structural and functional defects. The homozygous N374D mutation is lethal for embryonic development, whereas homozygous E/E or heterozygous E/N mice develop phenotypes that reproduce certain clinical manifestations of the patient, such as cardiomyopathy and muscle weakness. Using cellular and animal models, we demonstrated that virus-mediated exogenous expression of Gtpbp3 almost completely reversed mitochondrial, heart, and muscular dysfunction, opening new perspectives on an intervention strategy for GTPBP3-related mitochondrial diseases (Fig. 8). When evaluating 35 pathogenic mutations identified in hGTPBP3 gene, we suggest that the reduced tRNA modification activity is a common underlying mechanism for onset of diseases. However, the causative factors for the decreased activity may be diverse. For example, we have shown that R3L mutation renders failure in mitochondrial localization of hGTPBP3; moreover, A162P mutation and G312-V319 deletion have no effect on protein stability and organelle localization, but induce an impaired interaction with hMTO1 in the mitochondrial matrix19. Total RNAs were incubated with specific biotinylated DNA probes (Table S8) at 85 °C for 5 min for denaturation; then the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h for annealing. Streptavidin agarose beads were added to the mixture for 1 h at RT to bind the biotinylated DNA probe. Then the agarose beads were centrifuged and washed with PBST buffer for three times. Lastly, agarose beads were extracted using TRIzol reagent, and the specific tRNAs were precipitated in 75% ethanol containing 0.1 M NaAc (pH 5.2) at −80 °C overnight. 500 ng purified specific tRNAs were digested with 1 U benzonase, 1 U phosphodiesterase I, and 1 U bacterial alkaline phosphatase in a 30 μl of solution including 50 mM NH4OAc (pH 5.2) at 37 °C overnight. After complete hydrolysis, 10 μl of products were applied to UPLC-MS/MS for τm5U detection.

Enriched tRNA (1 µg in 6 µl SMART Digest Buffer) was digested with magnetic bead-immobilized RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 60 min at 37 °C with shaking at 300 rpm. Then 5 µl of digest was analyzed by LC‑ESI‑MS using a Vanquish Flex UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Separation was performed on a DNA Pac RP column (100 × 2.1 mm, 4 µm) at 300 µl min−1 with a gradient of solvent A (water containing 2% HFIP and 0.1% tetraethylammonium) and solvent B (methanol): 1% B (0–3 min), 1–30% B (3–18 min), 30–90% B (18–18.1 min), 90% B (18.1–21 min), 90–1% B (21–21.1 min), and 1% B (21.1–25 min). MS data were acquired in negative ion mode (m/z 350–3000, resolution 60,000) with AGC 100% and maximum injection time 100 ms. ESI settings were: spray voltage 3.4 kV, sheath gas 40 Arb, auxiliary gas 10 Arb, ion transfer tube 320 °C, vaporizer 350 °C, RF lens 70. The high energy collision dissociation (HCD) fragmentation used data-independent mode with stepped collision energies of 21, 23, 25, and 28 eV. Data were processed with Xcalibur and Biopharma Finder (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative modification levels were quantified by comparing XIC intensities of modified fragments, based on similar ionization efficiencies for identical sequences in negative ion mode.

To detect steady-state tRNA abundance, 4 μg of total RNAs were loaded on 10% polyacrylamide-8 M urea gel in Tris-borate EDTA (TBE) buffer under 200 V for 1 h at RT. To determine aminoacylation level of mitochondrial tRNAs, 6 μg of total RNAs were resolved in 0.1 M NaAc (pH 5.2) and loaded on an acidic (0.1 M NaAc, pH 5.2) 10% polyacrylamide-8 M urea gel at 4 °C under 18 W for 22 h, to separate charged and uncharged tRNAs. Total RNAs resolved in 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 10.0) at 37 °C for 1 h were loaded as the deaminoacylation control. After electrophoresis, RNAs were transferred onto a positively charged nylon membrane at 4 °C under 250 mA for 45 min. The RNAs and nylon membrane were crosslinked by HB-1000 hybridizer (3000 × 100 J/cm2) (UVP, CA, USA). The membrane was prehybridized with prehybridization solution (4 × SSC buffer, 20 mM Na2HPO4, 7% SDS, 1.5 × Denhardt solution, 20 mg/l salmon sperm DNA) at 55 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrane was hybridized with digoxin-labeled probes (resolved in a prehybridization solution) (Table S8) for specific tRNAs and 5S rRNA at 55 °C overnight. The membrane was then washed with washing buffer (0.1 M maleic acid, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.3% Tween-20, pH 7.5) for 5 min and then blocked with 1× blocking buffer (0.1 M maleic acid, 0.15 M NaCl, 1% blocking reagent) for 30 min at RT. Next, the membrane was incubated with antibody buffer (1× blocking reagent, 1:10,000 anti-digoxigenin-AP) for 1 h and washed twice with washing buffer for every 15 min. Lastly, the membranes were treated with CDP-Star and imaged using an Amersham Imager 680 system (GE, CA, USA). The gray value analysis was performed by Multi-Gauge Version 3.0 software.

APM gel was used to detect tRNAs which contains sulfur molecular modification70. APM gel contains three layers of 10% polyacrylamide-8 M urea gel, with the middle one containing 100 μg/mL APM to retard tRNAs with s2U modification. Four micrograms of total RNAs were loaded on APM gel in TBE buffer under 200 V for 45 min until the Xylene Cyanol dye indicator enter into bottom layer. The APM gel was immersed in 0.2 M β-ME for 1 h to terminate retardation and then washed with H2O. The following procedures were the same as described in the northern blot section.

Cells were cultured with DMEM medium containing 250 ng/mL EB for 0, 6, 12, and 24 h before harvest. Total RNAs were extracted from cells by using TRIzol reagent following manufacturer instructions. The results were performed gray value analysis by Multi-Gauge Version 3.0 software and the degeneration rate was plotted and quantified by using GraphPad 8.0.1 software.

Cells were treated with MitoTracker in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 for 20 min. DMEM was then removed and cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (dissolved in PBS) for 30 min at RT. The fixed cells were washed three times with PBS for every 5 min at RT, blocked in blocking buffer (0.1% Triton-X100 and 5% BSA in PBS) for 2 h and then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. 1 × 105 cells were seeded into the 6-well cell plate and cultured in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Cell number of each well was counted at 24 h time-intervals. The cell numbers were plotted using GraphPad 8.0.1 software.

HEK293T cells were washed three times with pre-cold PBS, resuspended in 4 mL pre-cold mitochondrial isolation buffer (30 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 225 mM mannitol, 75 mM sucrose, 1 mM PMSF), using an electrical homogenizer (T10 Basic ULTRA-TURRAX® (IKA)) for 15 min at 4 °C to rupture the cell membranes. The pellet (mitochondria) was collected and stored at −80 °C for further use. Cardiac and muscular mitochondria were isolated by Tissue Mitochondria Isolation Kit (C3606, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

The supernatant was added 50% glycerol (solubilization buffer: 50% glycerol = 8:1, v/v) and 5% G-250 (digitonin: G-250 = 8:1, m/m). The samples were stored at −80 °C. Thirty micrograms of total mitochondrial proteins were separated on a 3–12% Bis–Tris Native PAGE gel at 4 °C overnight and then the gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue.

Mitochondria were isolated from cells and tissues as above described. The quantification of mitochondria was determined by Bradford assay. A Seahorse Bioscience XF-24 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to measure the level of OCR and ECAR. Cells were seeded onto Seahorse XF24 polystyrene tissue culture plates at a density of 2.5 × 104 per well and adhered overnight. The cells were washed twice with 1 mL pre-warmed assay medium (DMEM medium with 1.85 g/l NaCl, 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine, pH 7.4). After washing, every well was added 500 μl assay medium and the 24-well culture plate was incubated at 37 °C without CO2 for 30 min. Subsequently, 1 μM oligomycin, FCCP, rotenone and antimycin A were added to the drug delivery ports according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The process of ECAR assay was similar to that of OCR. Differently, only 2 mM glutamine was added in the ECAR assay medium. The following inhibitors were added to the drag delivery ports: 10 mM glucose, 1 μM oligomycin, and 1 μM 2-DG (2-deoxy-D-glucose). After seahorse measurement, each cell well was incubated with propidium iodide and Hoechst and the primary results were normalized by cell counting which obtained by using EnSight (Perkin Elmer).

Cells and tissues (heart and muscle) were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4 °C and washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer for three times. The fixed samples were post-fixed with 2% osmium tetroxide at RT (30 min for cell sample, 1 h for tissue sample) and washed twice with distilled H2O. The samples were dehydrated in a series of ethanol with progressively increasing concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), cleared in acetone, and infiltrated with a 100% Epon812 resin. The Gtpbp3 gene heterozygous knockin mice (Gtpbp3-E230P (-/+) and Gtpbp3-N374D (-/+)) were constructed using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology (Cyagen Biosciences, Suzhou, China). Briefly, a donor vector containing the desired mutation, Cas9 protein, and the guide RNA (Table S8) were injected into fertilized eggs of C57BL/6 J mice. The embryos were then transferred to recipient female mice to obtain F0 generation mice. F0 founders were bred with WT mice to obtain F1 generation mice carrying the mutant allele. The animal housing environment was an SPF-grade animal facility, maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with an ambient temperature of 20–26 °C and relative humidity of 30–70%. The animal feed was sourced from Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-Engineering Co., Ltd. (Catalog Number: 1010010). The littermate was genotyped at P10 and cage-divided at P21. E/E and E/N mice were obtained by the self-mating of Gtpbp3-E230P (-/+) and cross-mating between Gtpbp3-E230P (-/+) and Gtpbp3-N374D (-/+). The specific genotyping primers are listed in Table S8.

Mouse metabolism was detected by Oxymax/CLAMS system (Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH, USA). O2 consumption, CO2 exhalation and heat production of WT, E/E and E/N mice were automatically and continuously monitored for 3 days with male mice of 12 w of the age.

The Rota-rod test was performed according to the speed of the rotating rod acceleration (speed-up from 4 to 40 rpm within 5 min) and latency to fall. The rotation speed of the rod when the mice fallen (speed at fall) was calculated. The maximum test speed was 40 rpm and the maximum duration time was 5 min.

Grip strength test assesses the muscular endurance and motor ability of the mice by measuring the grip of their forelimbs. Mice were placed on the grid, maintained at trunk level, and only their forepaws were allowed to attach to the grid before making any measurements. The tail of the mouse was gently pulled back, mouse was hold at the top of the grid and trunk horizontal, and then the maximum grip value was recorded. Each mouse was repeated twice more to obtain three forelimb grip strength measurements. The maximum mouse grip strength before grip release was recorded.

Mouse heart was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h at RT, then dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution overnight. The heart was immersed into O.C.T compound (Sakura, Torrance, CA, USA) and quick-frozen by liquid nitrogen. Then the samples were sliced at 10 µm and stained with Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (G1340, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The histological images were captured by a AXIO Zoom.V16 microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and the results were analyzed by Fiji software.

Left ventricular systolic function of mutant mice was determined using a digital ultrasound system Vevo 3100LT (Fujifilm Visual Sonics, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an inhalational anesthesia system, a 30-MHz mouse ultrasound probe and a thermostatic table. The heartbeat number, end-diastolic and end-systolic dimensions of the mouse heart could be directly measured by the M-mode ultrasound of this system; based on these indexes, left ventricular ejection fraction, fractional shortening and cardiac output were obtained.
