# Diagnostic Yield of Whole-Genome Sequencing in Patients With Kidney Failure of Undetermined Etiology at Age 50 Years or Younger.
**Authors:** Granhøj J, Aagaard MM, Pedersen KV, Lildballe DL, Ørskov B, Tougaard BG, Pausgaard RH, Brusgaard K, Svenningsen P, Birn H, Rasmussen M
**Journal:** Kidney Int Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ekir.2025.09.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ekir.2025.09.001)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: The cause of kidney failure (KF) often remains undetermined despite extensive diagnostic efforts. Given that genetic kidney diseases are underdiagnosed, we conducted a cross-sectional study to investigate the prevalence in patients with KF of undetermined etiology (uKF) using multifaceted genetic screening.

METHODS: One hundred twenty-four patients with uKF by age ≤ 50 years previously underwent single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-array and MUC1 analysis. Of these, 114 remained without a genetic diagnosis and underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS) in 3 consecutive stages as follows: (i) proband-based gene panel analysis; (ii) family-based analysis; and (iii) cohort-based reanalysis, including mitochondrial genome screening.

RESULTS: The median age at uKF was 37.5 years, with 92% having adult-onset uKF. WGS identified genetic causes in 26 of 114 (23%). Including all initial 124 families, the combined diagnostic yield was 36 of 124 (29%), with 3 diagnoses from SNP-array, 7 from MUC1 analysis, and 26 from WGS. Six genes (COL4A3/A4/A5, MUC1, NPHP1, and INF2) accounted for nearly 70% of diagnoses. Except for pathogenic MUC1 variants, all were detectable by WGS. A family history of KF was the only predictor of positive genetic screening; however, 18 of 99 patients (18%) without such family history still had a genetic kidney disease. In addition, we propose biallelic variants in complex I assembly factor gene TMEM126B as a cause of adult-onset kidney disease.

CONCLUSION: Using WGS, we identified a genetic cause in 23% of patients with uKF at age 50 or younger, resulting in a combined diagnostic rate of 29%. Most pathogenic variants were detectable by WGS.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) arises from a variety of underlying conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, and glomerulonephritis. Identifying the primary cause of CKD is particularly challenging in advanced stages, when the diagnostic phenotypes are masked by kidney atrophy, secondary hypertension, and nonspecific, chronic kidney histology, irrespective of the underlying etiology. Consequently, hypertensive kidney disease or CKD of unknown etiology (uCKD) are reported in ≥20% of patients with incident KF in European and US registries, 1 , 2 , 3 posing a significant barrier to optimizing patient care through precision nephrology strategies. Genetic screening provides a cost-saving diagnostic approach 4 that is independent of CKD stage, helping to reduce unnecessary diagnostic procedures and guide treatment decisions. In addition, a genetic diagnosis can assist familial kidney donor selection and inform disease prognostication. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Therefore, the 2024 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes CKD guideline now recommends implementing genetic screening into standard clinical practice. 10

Monogenic causes are responsible for approximately 10% of all CKD cases, 5 with a higher prevalence observed in younger patients with KF, and genetic kidney diseases are associated with a higher risk of progression to KF. 11 Most studies have employed targeted gene panels or whole-exome sequencing (WES), 12 screening either preselected kidney disease genes or all protein-coding regions. In previous studies, diagnostic yields range from 11% to 47% in patients with CKD of unknown or undetermined etiology, 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 with uCKD being an independent predictor of positive genetic screening outcomes. 5 The term “undetermined etiology” encompasses not only uCKD but also cases of any patients lacking a specific clinical or histopathological diagnosis by conventional kidney diagnostics. 13 , 17

Unlike WES, WGS covers nearly all exonic, intronic, and intergenic DNA regions, making WGS superior in detection capability and gradually replacing WES as the first-tier genetic screening method. 20 In nephrology, WGS has mainly been applied in unsolved case studies 21 , 22 or in specific kidney disease groups. 23 , 24 To date, only 1 larger Australian study has used WGS to screen patients with uKF, identifying a genetic cause in 25% of cases. 25 In this present study, we investigated the diagnostic yield of WGS in an unselective cohort diagnosed with uKF at age ≤ 50 years.

The recruitment of the uKF cohort has been described previously. 26 , 27 Briefly, patients with suspected uKF were recruited between February 2020 and May 2023, primarily from the Danish Nephrology Registry 28 (Figure 1). All participants underwent an initial, comprehensive review of medical records to exclude definite causes of kidney disease (e.g. diabetic kidney disease). This involved collection of data on clinical kidney diagnosis, any kidney imaging or biopsy results, and whether hypertension was documented at the initial time of kidney disease diagnosis. We defined KF as estimated glomerular filtration rate < 15 ml/min or the requirement of kidney replacement therapy for ≥ 3 months. 29 A family history of CKD and family history of KF were defined as a reported case of any-stage CKD or KF present in ≥1 first or second degree relative, respectively. Consistent with previous studies, 13 , 17 uKF was defined as the absence of any specific clinical, histopathological, or structural kidney disease diagnosis. Cases with nonspecific abnormal kidney morphology (e.g., hypodysplasia) or histology (e.g., focal segmental glomerulosclerosis), or hypertensive kidney disease were also categorized as uKF.

The inclusion criteria for genetic screening were as follows: (i) onset of uKF ≤ 50 years, or (ii) CKD of undetermined etiology and a family history of KF at age ≤ 50 years, and (iii) age ≥ 18 years at inclusion. We excluded patients with known genetic kidney disease previously established by molecular genetic screening in a clinical setting.

We investigated the prevalence of undiagnosed genetic kidney diseases in a cross-sectional cohort with uKF at age 50 years, using a sequential diagnostic approach. Patients progressed to the next diagnostic step if the previous step did not identify a genetic cause (Figure 1). The diagnostic outcomes and detailed methodological descriptions of the SNP-array 27 and MUC1 screening 26 have previously been published in separate studies. Next, we performed proband-based WGS restricted to 540 priority genes ± 20 base pairs from exon-intron boundaries. Family-based WGS without gene-specific filtering was offered to probands with negative WGS screening if they had developed KF by age 35 years or younger, or if they had additional affected relatives. Finally, WGS data from all unsolved probands and relatives underwent cohort-based reanalysis, including the following: (i) an updated priority gene list, (ii) screening of rare variants in families with autosomal dominant inheritance, and (iii) mitochondrial genome screening. The study was approved by the Danish National Committee on Health Research Ethics (1906020) and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. All patients received genetic counselling and provided written informed consent. Patients were reoffered genetic counselling when actionable diagnostic or incidental genetic findings were identified.

Whole-genome libraries were constructed from genomic DNA (xGen DNA Lib Prep EZ UNI and xGen Deceleration module, Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA) and sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) with paired-end 2∗150 base pairs and dual indexing. The data were analyzed using an in-house bioinformatics pipeline (For additional details, see Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Figure S1, and Supplementary Tables S1-S5). Variants were classified according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines 30 with updated recommendations. Diagnostic variants were defined as likely pathogenic (C4) or pathogenic (C5) variants in genes explaining the patient’s kidney phenotype. Similar to Blasco et al., 18 genetic results were determined as either positive if a diagnostic variant was found, inconclusive if a clinically relevant variant of unknown significance (VUS) was found, and negative if no relevant genetic variants were identified.

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Numeric variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. Diagnostic yield was calculated as the percentage of families with diagnostic variants. To compare clinical characteristics of patients with and without a positive genetic screening (i.e., detection of a diagnostic variant), we performed univariate and multivariate logistic regression with the independent variables sex, age of KF, family history of CKD, family history of KF, previous kidney biopsy, and hypertension at initial kidney disease diagnosis. A 2-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA 18 (Statacorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Overall, 172 patients were included in the study for eligibility assessment based on medical records; 45 were excluded initially based on eligibility, and 3 patients were later excluded because of failure to provide a blood sample (Figure 1). Thus, 124 patients underwent initial screening with SNP-array and MUC1 genotyping performed in parallel batches. In 2 cases, a genetic diagnosis was obtained from one analysis, prompting the cancellation of the other. Thus, SNP-array and MUC1 screening were each completed in 123 of 124 probands, identifying a causative genetic diagnosis in 3 patients 27 and 7 patients, 26 respectively (Figure 2). The baseline characteristics of the whole uKF cohort and the 114 probands who underwent WGS screening are summarized in Table 1. The median age of KF was 37.5 years (interquartile range: 28–45), with 92% having adult-onset KF. Advanced kidney disease at presentation was common, as indicated by 46% having hypertension recorded at diagnosis and only 41% undergoing a native kidney biopsy. In addition, 16% of the probands reported a family history of KF.

Using our WGS approach (Figure 2), we achieved a positive diagnostic outcome in 26 of 114 probands (23%) (Table 2, Supplementary Tables S6–S8). In the initial proband-based analysis, diagnostic variants were detected in 23 of 114 patients (20%), distributed over 14 genes and with variants in COL4A3/A4/A5 accounting for 10 of 25 diagnostic variants (40%) (Supplementary Table S6). The previously reported patient F441 harboured a 2q13 (NPHP1) deletion along with a novel missense VUS (NM_000272.5:c.1309T>G, (p.Trp437Gly)). 27 Modeling of the NPHP1 protein indicated that Trp437 is highly conserved and structurally important (Supplementary Figure S2), and the variant has been observed in other patients with kidney disease, leading to its reclassification as likely pathogenic. Seven patients had inconclusive genetic findings (Supplementary Table S9) as follows: 5 patients carried variants of unknown significance in genes consistent with the kidney phenotypes, and 2 patients (F96 and F268) had likely pathogenic variants in genes not clearly explaining their kidney phenotypes. F96, F268, and F583 were eligible for family-based segregation analysis, whereas no further investigations were initiated in the remaining 4 probands with VUS findings. The remaining 84 probands had negative proband-based WGS result.

Family-based WGS analysis was performed in 17 families after recruiting additional 34 family members (7 affected and 27 were unaffected; Supplementary Table S10). A genetic cause of kidney disease was identified in 2 of 17 family analyses (12%); we confirmed that the BICC1 variant in F268 was inherited from an affected mother. In F159, biallelic variants in complex I assembly factor gene TMEM126B were discovered in 2 affected siblings through family-based analysis, implicating TMEM126B as a possible autosomal recessive cause of kidney disease. In addition, we obtained a candidate VUS outcome in F583; because the PKHD1 variants initially detected in the proband-based WGS analysis were reidentified in a sister with a similar kidney phenotype and liver involvement consistent with PKHD1-associated autosomal recessive kidney disease, whereas the unaffected mother was a heterozygous carrier of the c.6122-3T>A variant. This strongly indicates the PKHD1 variants as the underlying cause of KF in F583; however, they remain VUS findings because of lack of functional evidence demonstrating aberrant splicing by the c.6122-3T>A variant. Furthermore, the novel JAG1 missense variant identified in F96 was found in an unaffected parent and reexamination of the proband’s phenotype did not fit with Alagille syndrome, leading to reclassification of the JAG1 variant to a VUS. No diagnostic or candidate variants were detected in the remaining families.

Finally, we performed cohort-based reanalysis of WGS data from the remaining unsolved patients and relatives, comprising 116 individuals from 87 families. We identified a previously reported likely pathogenic intronic COL4A4 variant (NM_000092.5:c.4334-23A>G) 31 , 32 that was initially missed because of ± 20 base-pair filtering around intron-exon boundaries. Subsequent segregation analysis showed that the variant was inherited from a deceased mother with KF. We detected no pathogenic mitochondrial variants, and the genome-wide reanalysis yielded no additional diagnostic variants or candidate VUS findings. In summary, the WGS screening was positive in 26 of 114 (23%), inconclusive in 6 of 114 (5%), and negative in 82 of 114 (72%).

By combining the diagnostic yields of SNP-array, 27 MUC1, 26 and WGS screening, we achieved a genetic diagnosis in 36 of the initial 124 families (29%) (Table 3). The diagnostic variants encompassed 18 different genes (Figure 3; Supplementary Table S11), with 6 genes (COL4A3/A4/A5, MUC1, NPHP1, and INF2) accounting for almost 70% of the diagnoses. Variants in the remaining 12 genes were only identified in a single family. Most diagnostic variants were located in exons, with missense and frameshift variants being most common (Table 3). The clinical characteristics of patients with and without a positive genetic screening are summarized in Table 4. Unadjusted logistic regression showed that family history of CKD and KF were significantly associated with a higher probability of positive genetic screening. However, in multivariate analysis, only family history of KF remained significant (Table 5). Of note, a genetic diagnosis was still achieved in 18 of 99 probands without a family history of KF (18%). When classifying MUC1-positive probands as “negative,” we found no statistically significant clinical predictors in the multivariate analysis (Supplementary Table S12).

We identified biallelic TMEM126B variants in 2 siblings with suspected hypertensive kidney disease (Figure 4). The nuclear gene TMEM126B (OMIM∗ 615533) encodes a component of the mitochondrial complex I assembly (MCIA) complex 33 and is required for complex I assembly in vitro. 33 , 34 To date, only 11 patients with biallelic TMEM126B variants have been reported; all showing complex I deficiency confirmed by thorough functional characterization 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 (Supplementary Table S13). The hallmark features of TMEM126B-associated complex I deficiency include exercise intolerance, muscle weakness, fatigue, and hyperlactatemia, whereas multisystemic features of mitochondrial disease are less common. Chronic KF and tubular acidosis have been reported in 1 affected child 36 ; however, the role of kidney disease in the TMEM126B-associated complex I deficiency remains unclear. Our female proband (II-1, Figure 4) had childhood-onset exercise intolerance that reportedly improved after kidney transplantation at age 48 years. She was diagnosed with hypertension at age 29 years and had severe preeclampsia with subsequent decline in kidney function. A kidney biopsy was never performed. The proband’s brother (II-3) also had childhood-onset exercise intolerance and progressive fatigue from early adulthood. He was diagnosed with early-onset hypertension at age 23 and underwent kidney biopsy at age 39 years because of unexplained loss of kidney function, showing 20% sclerotic glomeruli, interstitial fibrosis, and moderate hyaline arteriosclerosis consistent with hypertensive kidney disease. The kidney biopsy material was insufficient for electron microscopy to visualize mitochondrial morphology. Following family-based WGS, we performed Sanger sequencing for the 2 identified variants in the unaffected mother (I-2) and in another affected sister (II-2) with a similar phenotype of exercise intolerance and fatigue. The mother was a heterozygous carrier of the c.397G>A (p.Asp133Asn) variant, confirming that the 2 variants were in trans. The proband’s sister carried both variants, explaining her phenotype. Notably, she had hypertension and slightly decreased estimated glomerular filtration rate corresponding to CKD stage 2 at the age of 50 years. In this cohort of patients with mainly adult-onset uKF, we identified causative variants in 26 of 114 probands (23%) through multistep WGS analysis, achieving a combined diagnostic yield of 29% among the initial 124 cases of uKF.

Our diagnostic yield from WGS aligns with that reported in similar cohorts with uKF, including the 100,000 Genomes pilot report (30%, 6/20) 24 and a national Australian cohort with uKF at age 50 years (25%, 25/100). 25 Therefore, though WGS is an effective diagnostic screening method in patients with uKF, the diagnostic yields are comparable to those achieved by targeted gene panels 13 , 14 , 18 , 19 or WES. 7 , 15 , 16 This likely reflects the fact that most diagnostic variants are exonic and clustering in the same key genes, with variants in COL4A3/COL4A4/COL4A5 being most frequently detected. We detected one intronic COL4A4 variant, which would likely be missed by WES, typically restricted to ± 20 base-pairs around exon-intron boundaries. Similarly, using WGS Mallawaarachchi et al. 25 detected only 1 mitochondrial variant, whereas the remaining diagnostic variants were exonic in known kidney disease genes. Although WGS outperforms targeted gene panels and WES in detection capacities because of the genome-wide coverage, including noncoding and the mitochondrial genome, such “special” variants may represent a minority of all diagnostic variants. In comparison, MUC1 screening is more important in our study and accounts for 7 of 36 genetic diagnoses (19%), although the prevalence of MUC1-associated kidney disease may be higher in Denmark compared with other countries. 26

Another advantage of WGS and WES compared with targeted gene panels is the ability to identify novel candidate genes, particularly through family-based analyses. In this study, we found biallelic known pathogenic variants in the complex I assembly factor TMEM126B in 3 siblings with hypertensive kidney disease, accompanied by the hallmark features of exercise intolerance, fatigue, and hyperlactatemia. Few cases with TMEM126B-associated complex I deficiency have been reported, and the kidney phenotypes remain poorly characterized. Mitochondrial diseases often present a variety of kidney phenotypes, 39 and we observed variable kidney disease severity within the affected family members in this study. We propose TMEM126B to be considered as a candidate kidney disease gene, although detailed functional characterization of the underlying mechanisms is warranted.

Previous studies have consistently identified family history of CKD 4 , 5 , 7 , 11 , 15 , 17 , 25 , 32 and uCKD 5 as independent predictors of positive genetic screening using targeted gene panels and WES. Our cohort was selected based on undetermined etiology of KF, which corresponds to uCKD. Only family history of KF had a significantly higher odds ratio in our multivariate regression analysis, indicating that family history of any-stage CKD was not a significant predictor once adjusted for family history of KF. However, a genetic kidney disease was identified in 18% of patients without family history of KF, highlighting the value of genetic screening in early-onset uKF regardless of family history.

The strength of this study is the comprehensive genetic screening of a single uKF cohort, using both SNP-array and MUC1 genotyping followed by multistep WGS analyses. This approach enables direct comparison of the incremental diagnostic yields in each analysis step. Chromosomal microarrays are preferably replaced by copy number variation (CNV) screening using WES or WGS data, allowing simultaneous detection of both CNVs and sequence variants. In accordance, we recently demonstrated that our WGS pipeline effectively reidentified pathogenic CNVs initially detected by SNP-array genotyping while also being able to uncover alternative genetic explanations. 27 Our findings indicate that WGS yields diagnostic rates similar to previously reported using targeted gene panels and WES platforms, while being able to detect additional variants missed by these methods.

Our study is limited by a relatively small cohort with mainly retrospective clinical data, which makes it unfeasible to assess the clinical impact of the genetic screening. The cohort size was partly limited, because we focused exclusively on patients with unexplained KF, excluding those with already established genetic kidney diseases identified in a clinical setting. Consequently, our reported prevalence of genetic kidney diseases in patients with uKF may be underestimated. Our selective inclusion of patients with uKF at age ≤ 50 years likely enrich the cohort for individuals with more severe or monogenic forms of kidney disease. 11 This may have contributed to a higher diagnostic yield than would be expected if patients with earlier CKD stages or a higher age limit had been included. Furthermore, novel long-read sequencing technologies enable almost-complete genomic sequencing, including regions unmappable by current short-read sequencing technologies. The diagnostic yield of long-read sequencing platforms remains to be systematically investigated in cohorts with kidney disease, and could potentially uncover genes such as MUC1 that remain hidden using the current methods. In addition, this study focuses on rare, large-effect variants causing monogenic kidney disease. Thus, more common, intermediate-effect variants in, for example, UMOD 40 or similar genes may contribute to kidney disease at a population level but remain overlooked in our case-by-case analysis.

To conclude, using WGS we identified a genetic cause in 23% of families with uKF at age ≤ 50 years demonstrating its diagnostic value in this population. Our findings highlight the utility of genomic approaches, particularly WGS, in evaluating early-onset KF and supports its integration into the diagnostic workflow of unexplained kidney disease.
