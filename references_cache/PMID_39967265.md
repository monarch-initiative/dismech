# Polyamine metabolism is dysregulated in COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease.
**Authors:** Marquez J, Viviano S, Beckman E, Thies J, Friedland-Little J, Lam CT, Deniz E, Shelkowitz E
**Journal:** HGG Adv (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xhgg.2025.100418](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xhgg.2025.100418)

## Content

Most of the chemical energy that organisms rely on to support cellular function is generated through oxidative phosphorylation, a metabolic pathway in which electron donors NADH and FADH are oxidized through a series of successive steps to generate adenosine triphosphate. These redox reactions are orchestrated by a series of five protein complexes that sit within the mitochondrial membrane. Deficiency of cytochrome c oxidase, the fourth of these complexes, is a recognized cause of mitochondrial disease. COXFA4 encodes one of the protein subunits of cytochrome c oxidase, and variants in COXFA4 have recently been reported in individuals with a range of symptoms. These symptoms can include feeding difficulties, poor growth, cardiomyopathy, Leigh or Leigh-like disease, and neurodevelopmental delay, although these symptoms vary widely between individuals. However, a mechanistic understanding of the connection between COXFA4 loss and these varied disease manifestations is lacking. Using animal modeling in Xenopus, we explored the ramifications of coxfa4 loss of function on the early developing heart. We then conducted a hypothesis naive analysis of cellular gene expression in the context of COXFA4 deletion and discovered a downstream deficiency in the ornithine decarboxylase pathway. Small-molecule-based modulation of the ornithine decarboxylase pathway in our model modified the extent of disease, including improvement of cardiac function. Our findings point to a mechanism by which COXFA4 dysfunction leads to tissue-specific disease.

Cytochrome c oxidase subunit FA4 (COXFA4, previously known as NDUFA4) encodes a subunit of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase complex also known as complex IV. This gene is well conserved across vertebrates (Figure S1). Complex IV acts as a catalyst in the process of establishing a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane.1,2 This in turn drives adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis.3,4 COXFA4 was previously thought to be a subunit of complex I, another respiratory chain enzyme, but recent studies have shown that it is exclusively associated with complex IV and has a crucial role in its assembly and function.5,6,7,8 All eukaryotic organisms including plants and animals require the ATP generated through the respiratory chain of mitochondria. In humans, some cell types such as myocytes contain hundreds or thousands of mitochondria, while others such as red blood cells are devoid of mitochondria. The impacts of mitochondrial dysfunction thus result in variable repercussions on different cell types.9,10 Resultant diseases are clinically heterogeneous and can involve any organ or tissue, although they characteristically involve multiple organ systems.10,11 While some syndromic presentations of mitochondrial disease manifest with recognizable patterns of organ dysfunction, these classical presentations are less common in children.10,11

Mitochondrial proteins are dually encoded by genes of both mitochondrial and nuclear genomes. Variants in genes encoding mitochondrial proteins of both provenances have overlapping associations with disease. Causative variants leading to mitochondrial disease typically affect one of a few pathways crucial for integrity or function: oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) enzyme activity and assembly, mitochondrial DNA maintenance, cofactor biosynthesis, mitochondrial membrane remodeling, pyruvate dehydrogenase, and B vitamin metabolism.12,13

Variants in COXFA4 have been implicated as a cause of complex IV dysfunction that results in a mitochondrial disease (OMIM: 603833). Individuals with previously published putatively pathogenic variants in COXFA4 experienced cardiomyopathy, developmental delays, progressive neurodegeneration, muscle weakness, and lactic acidosis.14,15,16,17 Here, we review previously reported cases of COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease and describe an additional family with 2 affected individuals and a third, presumably affected child, who passed away in infancy of cardiomyopathy (Figure 1A; Table 1). This work further supports the previously described phenotypic spectrum of COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease and strengthens its association with early-onset cardiomyopathy. The mechanism by which COXFA4 deficiency leads to disease is not fully understood. To this end, we developed a vertebrate model of COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease. In a high-throughput Xenopus tropicalis model, coxfa4 loss of function led to multiple phenotypes that recapitulated affected individuals’ disease. To understand potential developmental pathways that are disrupted and may contribute to these phenotypes, we analyzed a COXFA4 knockout induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) line in a hypothesis naive approach to identify disrupted signaling. Through this approach, we identified deficits in the ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) pathway.

ODC has been characterized as the rate-limiting enzyme in the biosynthesis of polyamines.18,19 High levels of embryonic ODC activity have been well documented in a variety of organisms,20,21 yet its significance in this context remains poorly understood, although it appears to be correlated with cell-type differentiation in organ-specific contexts.22 One particularly interesting facet of the ODC pathway is the rapid turnover rate of ODC protein, which has been observed to exhibit a half-life of 10–20 min.18 This implies a dynamic ability to respond to stimuli. In fact, ODC concentration is typically low in quiescent cells and increased dramatically in response to growth stimuli.23,24 Here, we demonstrate that depletion of coxfa4 leads to complex IV dysfunction and ODC pathway dysregulation. Modulation of ODC signaling appears to modify this phenotypic spectrum and raises the possibility that downstream polyamine supplementation may improve cardiac disease.

Imaging and photographs of individuals were obtained from review of their medical charts, with explicit permission provided by their legal guardians following informed consent.

Xenopus tropicalis were raised, housed, and cared for in accordance with established protocols that were approved by the Yale Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol no. 11035). We induced ovulation and collected embryos by in vitro fertilization as previously described.25 Embryos were raised in 1/9 modified Ringer’s solution (MR) (0.1 M NaCl, 1.8 mM KCl, 2.0 mM CaCl2, 1.0 mM MgCl2, and 5.0 mM HEPES-NaOH) + gentamicin. Staging of Xenopus tadpoles was performed as previously described.26,27
Xenopus tadpoles were raised at 25°C except for those observed for survival assay, which were raised at 22°C.

Using standard protocols, we injected 1-cell Xenopus embryos with 400 pg single-guide RNA along with 1.6 ng Cas9 to target exon 1 of coxfa4 based on reference genomic sequence NC_030683.2 designed to generate F0 mosaic knockout embryos (negative strand target sequence 5′-AGTGCGGAACATGATGGCGGCGG-3′) or the same mixture along with 100 pg COXFA4 mRNA generated from clone FLH130970.01X or 100 pg COXFA4L2 mRNA generated from clone ccsbBroadEn_03751 in a pDEST backbone using the T7 mMessage machine kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The same amount of mRNA was used for overexpression experiments. These mRNAs encode the human forms of COXFA4 and COXFA4L2. Guide RNA was designed based on sequence information accessed through Xenbase28 and CRISPRScan.29

We amplified the sequence adjacent to the CRISPR cut site using polymerase chain reaction with Phusion HighFidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB). Resultant products were Sanger sequenced and analyzed via Synthego browser-based platform (ice.synthego.com). This approach allows for detecting differing sizes of CRISPR edits in pooled embryo samples consisting of 20 embryos. We complemented this by assessing edits in individual embryos by Sanger sequencing to confirm editing on individual embryo samples.

Stage 45 Xenopus embryos were immobilized and imaged using a Thorlabs Telesto 1,325-nm spectral domain-optical coherence tomography (OCT) system. The cardiac 2-dimensional cross-sectional images were obtained as part of a continuous scan. The maximum and minimum distances between the outer bounds of opposing ventricular walls were identified and measured using the integrated Thorlabs software. The values for fractional shortening are the average of 3 cardiac contraction cycles.

Stage 45 embryos were fixed in 100% ethanol for 48 h at room temperature and then washed briefly in acid alcohol (1.2% HCl in 70% EtOH). A 0.25% Alcian blue solution in acid alcohol was used to stain the embryos for 48 h at room temperature. Specimens were then washed in acid alcohol several times, rehydrated into H2O, and bleached for 2 h in 1.2% hydrogen peroxide under a bright light. They were then washed several times in 2% KOH and left rocking overnight in 10% glycerol in 2% KOH. Samples were processed through 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% glycerol in 2% KOH. The cartilage area was measured in intact embryos following Alcian blue staining by measuring the area of each cartilage region (Meckel, ceratohyal, and branchial).

Assessment of tadpole motility was carried out as previously described.30 Briefly, stage 45 tadpoles were individually placed into wells of a standard 48-well culture dish. Prior to imaging, individual wells were aligned for video capture, and tadpoles were allowed to reach a resting state for at least 5 min. Tadpoles were then gently prodded using a capillary pipette tip. Video of tadpole movement was captured over 30 s after this stimulation, and average motion was determined by converting marked tracking points to vectors and measuring the sum of these vectors over the 30-s recorded time.

Preparation of mitochondrial fractions was completed as previously described.16,31 Stage 45 Xenopus tissue (dissected brain, heart, muscle, kidney, and liver; Figure S5) was manually homogenized in homogenizing buffer (320 mM sucrose with 1 mM EDTA and 10 mM Tris-HCl at a pH of 7.4) based on previously reported methods. Homogenates were centrifuged at 600 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Supernatants were stored on ice and the pellets resuspended in homogenizing buffer, homogenized, and centrifuged at 600 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Supernatants were mixed and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The mitochondrial enriched protein pellets were subsequently used for all biochemical assays. Protein concentrations were determined by Bradford assay. Complex IV and complex I in gel activity assays were completed in 3%–12% polyacrylamide blue native gels loaded with 50 μg of mitochondrial enriched protein. Complex IV and complex I labeling were achieved via incubation with Coomassie blue and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrachloride, respectively.

A previously described dataset of bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) expression of control and COXFA4 CRISPR-derived knockout iPSCs that were generated in triplicate was utilized to assay for transcript level expression in these cells.32 This dataset was accessed via the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE182376.33,34

Pathway analysis was carried out using Reactome (https://reactome.org/). The input was all genes with decreased transcripts detected in COXFA4 CRISPR-derived knockout iPSCs compared to control iPSCs or all genes with decreased transcripts detected in COX6A2 CRISPR-derived knockout cardiomyocytes compared to control cardiomyocytes.

Stage 45 embryos were collected and rinsed in DNase-RNase free water. Pools of 20 embryos were homogenized in TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a pestle. Total RNA was extracted based on the TRIzol manufacturer protocol. Insoluble materials were removed after homogenization by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C before adding chloroform. Reverse transcription (RT) was performed using SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System for RT-PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In vitro transcription reactions were performed using 1 μg total RNA and a mixture of 500 ng of anchored Oligo(dT)20 primers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 150 ng of random pentadecamer primers (Sigma-Aldrich). Primers (Table S1) were either designed using Primer3Plus for azin1 and oaz135 or previously described for odc1.36 PCR reactions included reverse transcriptase product, SYBR Green qPCR master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and gene-specific primers (Sigma-Aldrich).

Stage 45 embryos were collected, and pools of 20 embryos were homogenized in polyamine assay buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) with a pestle. We utilized a selective enzyme assay (Sigma-Aldrich) in which a reaction that utilizes polyamines as a substrate to generate hydrogen peroxide is subsequently detected with a fluorometric probe (λex = 535 nm/λem = 587 nm).

Sterile 1,000× polyamine supplement (Tocris) was diluted to 1× in 1/9 MR + gentamicin prior to the incubation of embryos in this medium (0.00005 μg/mL putrescine, 0.000048 μg/mL spermine, and 0.000025 μg/mL spermidine). Allicin (Waverlex) was dissolved in 1/9 MR + gentamicin to a final concentration of 25 μg/mL prior to the incubation of embryos in this medium.

Statistical significance of fractional shortening, relative craniofacial cartilage area, relative complex IV and I activity, ODC component expression in Xenopus, and polyamine abundance was tested using two-tailed t tests (p < 0.05 was deemed significant). The statistical significance of changes in iPSC RNA-seq expression was assessed via Benjamini-Hochberg p value correction for false discovery rate (adjusted p < 0.05 was deemed significant). Overrepresentation pathway analysis determines whether certain Reactome pathways are overrepresented (enriched) in the submitted data. This test produces a probability score, which is corrected for false discovery rate using the Benjamani-Hochberg method (adjusted p < 0.05 was deemed significant).

Individual IV-6 initially presented neonatally with cardiomyopathy, microcephaly, and faltering growth (Figures 1A, 1B, and 1D). He has remained small for age and at last evaluation had a height Z score of −3.2, a weight Z score of −4.9, and a head circumference Z score of −3.5. His older sibling (IV-3) was noted to have developmental delays and faltering growth (Figures 1A and 1B). She has similarly remained small for age and at last evaluation had a height Z score of −2.6, a weight Z score of −3.1, and a head circumference Z score of −2.6. In addition, on magnetic resonance imaging she was found to have a thin corpus callosum and scattered T2-weighted hyperintensities near the gray-white matter junction (Figure 1C). Both siblings present as immature for age and hyperactive. Both attend a mainstream school, and neither has had formal neuropsychological testing. Their neurologic exams are unremarkable. Both have persistent lactic acidosis in the range of 3–7 mmol/L. Their parents are consanguineous (Figure 1A). Based on these findings, these siblings underwent exome sequencing. Clinical exome sequencing was carried out using standard methods and with variant classification completed following criteria established by the American College of Medical Genetics.37 Exome sequencing revealed a homozygous deletion of 7p21.3 (10933348–10941418, GRCh38) in both siblings that includes the entirety of the coding region of COXFA4 and no other protein-coding genes. Their mother was heterozygous for this deletion (III-2). Another sibling who died due to cardiomyopathy at 3 weeks of life did not undergo genetic testing (IV-2). No other variants were detected that would be predicted to lead to cardiomyopathy. In particular, regions of homozygosity shared between the siblings who underwent testing did not harbor biallelic pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants that would constitute a recessive cause of these siblings’ clinical presentation. A recent study described another individual with a similar full gene deletion of COXFA4, encompassed by distinct breakpoints. This individual presented at age 2 years with developmental regression, lactic acidosis, cystic leukoencephalopathy and a low body mass index.17 The siblings described in our study have mild developmental delays that overlap with previously described individuals with biallelic COXFA4 loss of function variants; however, to date, neither individual has manifested clinical or radiographic signs of Leigh syndrome, which has been described in other reported cases of COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease.

While the deletion described for the family reported in this study could in theory affect the expression of other genes not contained within the deletion, we hypothesized that cardiomyopathy and microcephaly may be a manifestation of COXFA4 loss of function. Cardiomyopathy and increased ventricular wall thickness have been reported in 2 previous individuals with COXFA4 loss-of-function variants, although microcephaly has not been noted specifically.14,15,16,17 To test this hypothesis, we sought to model COXFA4 loss of function in a vertebrate system. X. tropicalis is an ideal model for these efforts as it is a high-throughput rapidly developing diploid model that is amenable to non-invasive high-resolution live imaging through OCT. Xenopus hearts are also morphologically more similar to human hearts when compared to other aquatic models. They have a septated atrium and cardiac trabeculations. Additionally, Xenopus has well characterized and predictable patterns of craniofacial cartilage development that can be assayed to detect changes in size and morphology. These facets allow for excellent modeling of human heart and craniofacial development. Recent work has made use of these advantages in studying mitochondrial function in congenital disease.38

We thus sought to test whether heart and craniofacial features in Xenopus coxfa4 crispants injected at the 1-cell stage recapitulated affected individuals’ phenotypes (Figure 2A). To accomplish this, we assessed the editing that could be achieved through CRISPR-based coxfa4 loss of function. In pooled embryo samples, we were able to detect a variety of edits that were predominantly predicted to cause frameshift mutations in coxfa4 through PCR-based inference of CRISPR edits analysis39 in F0 crispants (Figures S2A and S2B). As this approach does not readily allow for detection of specific editing beyond base number, we further confirmed that the predominant variant would not lead to expression of coxfa4 using a nearby start site by Sanger sequencing of individual tadpoles (Figure S2C). We proceeded to analyze cardiac contractility in this model to detect dysfunction that could be related to the phenotypes seen in individuals with COXFA4 variants. Remarkably, using OCT imaging, crispant embryos displayed decreased cardiac contractility compared to controls (Figure 2B; Videos S1 and S2). We detected a change in the ventricular shortening fraction from 0.25 (n = 66, SD = 0.05) in control embryos to 0.20 (n = 111, SD = 0.07) in coxfa4 crispants. Next, we examined craniofacial morphology. Using Alcian blue, which stains the cartilage elements of the tadpole head, we detected deficits in the developing craniofacial skeleton. The mandibular, hyoid, and branchial cartilage elements were impacted, with a total area of 114,048 μm2 (n = 15, SD = 3,362 μm2) in control embryos compared to 91,815 μm2 (n = 15, SD = 4,474 μm2) in coxfa4 crispants (Figures 2A, 2C, and 2D). These phenotypes were readily rescued via co-injection with COXFA4 mRNA encoding the human version of COXFA4 (Figure 2).

Given the previously proposed contributions of COXFA4 to complex IV activity, we sought to assay this in various Xenopus tissues to determine whether variable organ-level manifestations of disease could stem from differing utilization of the COXFA4 subunits among different tissues. To prioritize tested tissues, we analyzed RNA-seq and proteomic data to determine tissue types that may display varying coxfa4 expression.40,41,42 Mouse and human cardiomyocytes are particularly rich in Coxfa4/COXFA4 expression (Figures S4A and S4B). We therefore compared complex IV activity between control and coxfa4 crispant tissues (brain, muscle, liver, and heart). While brain and heart tissue demonstrated statistically significant decreased in-gel complex IV activity in coxfa4 crispants, muscle and liver tissue did not demonstrate statistically significant in-gel complex IV activity in coxfa4 crispants (Figure 2D). In comparison, complex I showed only a modest variable decrease or even in rare cases increased in-gel activity within assayed tissues upon coxfa4 crispants (Figure 2D).

To better understand the early developmental effects of COXFA4 dysfunction, we took a hypothesis naive approach to analyze the global landscape of expression changes in COXFA4 knockout cells. We used a recently generated bulk RNA-seq dataset from control and COXFA4 knockout iPSCs.32 This allowed us to evaluate the effects of COXFA4 deletion in undifferentiated cells prior to assaying functional changes on a tissue-specific level. Using pathway analysis,43 we uncovered several disrupted signaling pathways with overrepresented constituents among decreased transcripts in this dataset (Figures 3A and 3B). We were particularly struck by the decrease in nearly all components of the ODC pathway, and we sought to investigate this pathway in the context of our coxfa4 crispant Xenopus model. To accomplish this, we first assessed ODC component expression in our Xenopus model through RT-qPCR. We observed a similar decrease in mRNA of all assayed components (Figure 3C). Of note, there was a larger decrease in integral components of the ODC pathway (odc1 and azin1) compared to inhibitory ODC pathway component oaz1. Consistent with this, we observed a decrease in global polyamine levels in coxfa4 crispant embryos (Figure 3D). Overexpression of COXFA4, however, was not sufficient to promote an increase in ODC signaling components or total polyamine levels (Figure S3). Finally, several proteins similar to COXFA4 have been described to carry out overlapping functions in distinct tissues.45,46,47,48 Indeed, often, isoforms may share basic functions, but ultimately they have distinct roles due to variable expression in different tissues. We aimed to evaluate whether one of these similar proteins might be able to replace the function of COXFA4 in deficient tissues. To assess the viability of this approach, we first analyzed predicted structural similarity as predicted through AlphaFold.49 We then proceeded to evaluate expression patterns that might support overlapping developmental roles. We hypothesized that a protein like COXFA4 that is not usually as highly expressed in the tissues where COXFA4 is abundant may be able to replace a COXFA4 deficiency in a new cellular context. We therefore queried whether such tissue-specific expression was borne out in temporally resolved mouse development.50 The predicted structures of COXFA4 and COXFA4L2 (previously known as NDUFA4L2) are strikingly similar, and expression in murine tissues differs between Coxfa4 and Coxfa4l2 (Figure 5E). It appeared in distinct cells such as hepatocytes and cardiomyocytes that expression of Coxfa4 and Coxfa4l2 varied in a manner that could be consistent with overlapping functions being carried out in distinct tissues at distinct developmental time points (Figures 6A and 6B). Based on this observation, we attempted to drive expression of COXFA4L2 broadly in Xenopus through injection of mRNA encoding the human version of COXFA4L2 with the aim of replacing coxfa4 with COXFA4L2 in tissues where it may not typically be highly expressed. With a growing number of cases of COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease, the importance of this complex IV subunit for human health has become increasingly clear (Table 1). Despite the recognized role of complex IV in mitochondrial biology and organismal energetics, our understanding of the effects of COXFA4 deficiency on complex IV activity was lacking. In fact, given the ubiquitous need for complex IV activity across cell types, one might expect that COXFA4 dysfunction would lead to early embryonic lethality rather than Leigh-like syndrome and progressive neurodegeneration. However, other diseases without embryonic lethality have been linked to variants in genes encoding the components of complex IV.51,52,53 Biallelic full gene deletions, however, are rare and provide a unique opportunity to understand the ramifications of phenotypes in the absence of crucial proteins such as mitochondrial subunits. Indeed, phenotypes of affected individuals hint at a cardiac and nervous system reliance on COXFA4.

We sought to identify a mechanistic explanation for the phenotypes of cardiomyopathy and microcephaly noted in individuals with COXFA4 loss of function variants. We recapitulated both craniofacial and cardiac phenotypes of individuals with COXFA4 variants in our Xenopus model (Figure 2). Although changes in expression patterns within COXFA4 knockout iPSCs could have been unrelated to the broader effects of COXFA4 loss at an organismal level, we observed remarkable parallels between our Xenopus model and expression patterns observed in an iPSC COXFA4 knockout RNA-seq dataset (Figure 3). In both cases, multiple components of the ODC pathway were diminished due to a loss of COXFA4. We detected a striking reduction in ODC1, which given the dynamic and critical role for ODC1 in this pathway, would likely have profound effects on tissues reliant on this pathway during development. In fact, variants in ODC1 have been described in association with neurodevelopmental disorders and brain development more broadly.54,55

How COXFA4 loss could lead to tissue-specific disease was far from obvious. While there were no clear expression-level changes detected in COXFA4 knockout iPSCs that would suggest a tissue-specific program that might be affected (Figure 3), our direct investigation of complex IV activity in specific Xenopus tissues revealed an interesting tropism for reduced activity in tissues also affected in individuals with COXFA4 loss-of-function variants (Figure 2). While it remains unclear why some tissues (cardiac and brain) appear to be more reliant on COXFA4 for complex IV activity and others (liver and skeletal muscle) less so, these findings suggest a specialized tissue or even cell-type-specific role for the COXFA4 subunit. There is some evidence that complex IV utilizes different subunits to carry out its function in diverse processes such as during spermatogenesis48 or in inflammatory states.56 This is further suggested by the temporal variability in cell-type-specific expression of subunit transcripts (Figures 5E, 6A, and 6B). In fact, this observation helps us better understand why multiple individuals who had complex IV testing carried out on fibroblasts did not have a noted complex IV deficiency, while the individual with complex IV testing carried out on muscle tissue did have results consistent with decreased complex IV activity (Table 1).

Our observation that that ODC1 expression is decreased in the setting of COXFA4 loss informed experiments carried out in our Xenopus model to test how modulating the ODC pathway might modify observed phenotypes. We hypothesized that repleting downstream polyamine might improve defects noted in cardiac morphology (Figure 4). Strikingly, supplementation of polyamines significantly ameliorated cardiac dysfunction in coxfa4 crispants (Figure 5A). Importantly, this seems to be independent of complex IV activity, since there was no detectable restoration of complex IV activity with polyamine supplementation (Figure 5B). From these results, we propose a model where the cardiomyopathy observed in individuals with COXFA4 homozygous loss of function variants is in part due to a disruption in the ODC pathway. However, how complex IV activity is linked to ODC signaling is unclear. While this modulation of ODC signaling may lie downstream of complex IV function, we would not discard the possibility of COXFA4 carrying out an additional function either within or beyond the mitochondrion. Although some of our results could be consistent with COXFA4 driving increased gene expression or polyamine stimulation as a method of regulating ODC signaling overexpression of COXFA4 was insufficient to increase ODC component expression or overall polyamine levels, which makes this model less likely. While no clear evidence of an alternative function for COXFA4 has been found, studies of complex IV structure have suggested this because the binding site of COXFA4 lies at the interface of complex IV and the mitochondrial intermembrane space.7 In addition, COXFA4 appears to readily dissociate from complex IV in the presence of stronger acidic salt solutions.7 These observations could suggest the ability of COXFA4 to access additional intracellular compartments based on the composition of its subcellular compartment.

Although individuals with loss-of-function variants in COXFA4 have variable neurological features, our Xenopus model did not have clearly identifiable neurological dysfunction such as atypical movements or seizure propensity, as has been identified in Xenopus models of other genetic diseases.30,57,58 This may well reflect what is observed in individuals with loss-of-function variants in COXFA4. Neurological challenges such as learning differences and movement disorders appear to affect these individuals more prominently as they age. Such long-term effects may not be well captured in our early developmental Xenopus model. However, interestingly, complex IV activity is already detectably lower in the brain even at these early time points, which may suggest that a propensity for neurological disease is already established early in life prior to symptom onset (Figure 2).

Additionally, the affected individuals we describe seemed to have a more severe degree of microcephaly than other previously reported individuals with loss-of-function variants in COXFA4. We speculate that this could be due to the nature of the full gene deletion of COXFA4 leading to more severe manifestations of at least a subset of observed phenotypes as compared to the splice-site variants underlying previously described cases of COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease. Our Xenopus loss-of-function model was well suited to capture this phenotype as the resulting CRISPR editing could mimic this full loss through a frameshift in the first exon.

Although our analysis of COXFA4 knockout iPSC RNA-seq data prompted our exploration of ODC as a candidate pathway for targeting resultant phenotypes, many other changes are readily observed in the expression of possibly important components of COXFA4-related disease pathogenesis. Many of these, however, are in less-well-defined genes that may merit further study. For example, several zinc-finger protein-encoding transcripts were particularly downregulated and may have far-reaching effects on downstream signaling, as has been observed in other diseases.59,60 Additionally, while the pathway of separation of sister chromatids was also enriched in our analysis, this pathway is predominantly populated by genes required for the effective separation of dividing cells in growing tissues. Given the importance of the ODC pathway in cell growth and differentiation,23,24 we believe this pathway may lie downstream of decreased ODC signaling.

Our results in analyzing COXFA4 knockout iPSC RNA-seq and modeling COXFA4-related disease in Xenopus provide important insights into the mechanism for disease observed in affected individuals. While we observed that COXFA4 loss of function has a somewhat unsurprising connection to complex IV activity, we observe that this connection is strikingly tissue dependent (Figure 2). Crucially, we identified a downstream pathway that may be supplemented to modify disease. While modulation of this pathway does not seem to ameliorate complex IV activity (Figure 5C), it does seem to improve survival and cardiac function (Figures 5A and 5B). This has immediate implications for affected individuals, as polyamine supplementation may be easily achieved to effectively rescue the ODC pathway without known risks intrinsic to polyamine addition to the diet. Polyamine supplementation for individuals with biallelic COXFA4 loss-of-function variants could represent a reasonable empiric approach to prevent or improve cardiac outcomes. Indeed, spermidine, one of the polyamines, has previously been shown to yield cardioprotective effects in mice.61 This approach parallels similar efforts toward treatment in Snyder-Robinson and Bachmann-Bupp syndromes due to spermidine synthetase deficiency and ODC overactivity, respectively,62,63 although the precise roles of polyamine levels in these diseases are a topic of ongoing study. Intriguingly, OXPHOS also appears to be dysregulated in Snyder-Robinson mouse models.64

Based on our Xenopus model, it does appear that early initiation of polyamine supplementation would be necessary to observe the cardiac benefits of this approach (Figure 5A), although how early this would be necessary in individuals with COXFA4-related disease to exert a disease-modifying effect requires further investigation. An alternative therapeutic approach might involve stimulating the expression of COXFA4L2, as this seemed capable of functionally replacing COXFA4 (Figure 5A) to some extent in our Xenopus model, although this would seem a more challenging therapy to implement compared to polyamine supplementation that may be possible through dietary supplementation or modifications. While we are hopeful that this strategy of polyamine supplementation may prove efficacious in treating other related disease processes, on analysis of other models of dysfunctional complex IV such as a COX6A2 knockout cardiomyocytes,65 a similar downregulation of ODC signaling is not apparent even when we apply our same analysis (Figure S7). Thus, the utility of polyamine supplementation may be more specific to COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease.

In summary, our work indicates that COXFA4 plays a tissue-dependent role in complex IV activity. In our model, coxfa4 loss was associated with ODC dysfunction. While additional work will be necessary to fully characterize COXFA4-related disease pathogenesis, our results highlight the utility of Xenopus as a robust and high-throughput model for investigating the basis for COXFA4-related disease. In addition, we have been able to leverage this model to identify a plausible approach to the treatment of the cardiac manifestations of COXFA4-related disease that are a cause of morbidity and mortality for individuals with COXFA4-related mitochondrial disease.
