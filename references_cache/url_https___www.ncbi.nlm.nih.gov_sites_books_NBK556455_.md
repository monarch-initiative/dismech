            <div class="main-content lit-style" itemscope="itemscope" itemtype="http://schema.org/CreativeWork"><div class="meta-content fm-sec"><h1 id="_NBK556455_"><span class="title" itemprop="name"><i>WT1</i> Disorder</span></h1><p class="contrib-group"><span itemprop="author">Beata S Lipska-Zi&#x00119;tkiewicz</span>, MD, PhD.</p><a data-jig="ncbitoggler" href="#__NBK556455_ai__" style="border:0;text-decoration:none">Author Information and Affiliations</a><div style="display:none" class="ui-widget" id="__NBK556455_ai__"><div class="contrib half_rhythm"><span itemprop="author">Beata S Lipska-Zi&#x00119;tkiewicz</span>, MD, PhD<div class="affiliation small">Centre for Rare Diseases<br />Clinical Genetics Unit<br />Department of Biology and Genetics<br />Medical University of Gda&#x00144;sk<br />Gda&#x00144;sk, Poland<div><span class="email-label">Email: </span><a href="mailto:dev@null" data-email="lp.ude.demug@akspil.b" class="oemail">lp.ude.demug@akspil.b</a></div></div></div></div><p class="small">Initial Posting: <span itemprop="datePublished">April 30, 2020</span>; Last Update: <span itemprop="dateModified">May 15, 2025</span>.</p><p><em>Estimated reading time: 32 minutes</em></p></div><div class="jig-ncbiinpagenav body-content whole_rhythm" data-jigconfig="allHeadingLevels: ['h2'],smoothScroll: false" itemprop="text"><div id="wt1-dis.Summary" itemprop="description"><h2 id="_wt1-dis_Summary_">Summary</h2><div><h4 class="inline">Clinical characteristics.</h4><p><i>WT1</i> disorder is characterized by <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/congenital/">congenital</a>/infantile or childhood onset of steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS), a progressive glomerulopathy that does not respond to standard steroid therapy. Additional common findings can include disorders of testicular development (with or without abnormalities of the external genitalia and/or m&#x000fc;llerian structures) and Wilms tumor. Less common findings are congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), gonadoblastoma, and 46,XX gonadal dysgenesis. In adulthood, most individuals are affected by early gonadal insufficiency of variable severity with potential impact on puberty and fertility. While various combinations of renal and other findings associated with a <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> were designated as certain syndromes in the past (the most common being Denys-Drash and Frasier syndromes), those designations are now recognized to be part of a phenotypic continuum and are no longer clinically helpful.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of <i>WT1</i> disorder is established in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with suggestive clinical findings and a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in <i>WT1</i> identified by <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> SRNS: avoid immunosuppressants; consider renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibition. Disorders of testicular development and 46,XX gonadal dysgenesis: management is often by a multidisciplinary team (clinical geneticist, endocrinologist, urologist, and psychologist). Treat Wilms tumor with standard oncology protocols and, when applicable, nephron-sparing surgery. Treat CAKUT per standard care. Prevent whenever possible gonadoblastoma by prophylactic gonadectomy in those with a disorder of testicular development. Diaphragmatic hernia repair prior to the start of peritoneal dialysis.</p><p><i>Surveillance</i>: Monitor for first appearance of the following: proteinuria every six months until age ten years, yearly thereafter; Wilms tumor every three months until age seven years; early gonadal insufficiency yearly after puberty. For ongoing issues with disorders of testicular development and 46,XX gonadal dysgenesis, monitor per treating multidisciplinary team, and for CAKUT, monitor per treating nephrologist and/or urologist.</p><p><i>Agents/circumstances to avoid:</i> Avoid treating glomerulopathy with immunosuppressants, as they are not effective and potentially toxic.</p><p><i>Evaluation of relatives at risk:</i> It is appropriate to clarify the genetic status of apparently asymptomatic older and younger at-risk relatives of an individual with <i>WT1</i> disorder in order to identify as early as possible those who would benefit from prompt initiation of treatment and surveillance for glomerulopathy and Wilms tumor.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Genetic counseling.</h4><p><i>WT1</i> disorder is inherited in an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> manner. Most individuals diagnosed with <i>WT1</i> disorder have the disorder as the result of an apparent <i>de novo WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>; in rare instances, a parent of an individual with <i>WT1</i> disorder is <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for the pathogenic variant identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>. If a parent of the proband is affected and/or is known to have the <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variant identified in the proband, the risk to the sibs of inheriting the pathogenic variant is 50%. If the <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variant identified in the proband cannot be detected in the leukocyte DNA of either parent, the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/recurrence-risk/">recurrence risk</a> to sibs is slightly greater than that of the general population because of the possibility of parental <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gonadal-mosaicism/">gonadal mosaicism</a>. Once the <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variant has been identified in an affected family member, prenatal and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> are possible.</p></div></div><div id="wt1-dis.Diagnosis"><h2 id="_wt1-dis_Diagnosis_">Diagnosis</h2><p>Formal diagnostic criteria for <i>WT1</i> disorder have not been established.</p><p>Note: This chapter on <i>WT1</i> disorder excludes WAGR syndrome (<i>W</i>ilms tumor-<i>a</i>niridia-<i>g</i>enital anomalies-<i>r</i>ange of developmental delays), caused by a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/contiguous-gene-deletion/">contiguous gene deletion</a> of <i>PAX6</i> and <i>WT1</i> (see <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/wagr/">WAGR Spectrum Disorder</a>).</p><div id="wt1-dis.Suggestive_Findings"><h3>Suggestive Findings</h3><p><i>WT1</i> disorder <b>should be suspected</b> in an individual with the following clinical, imaging, and supportive laboratory findings.</p><div id="wt1-dis.Clinical_Findings"><h4>Clinical Findings</h4><p><b>Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome.</b> A progressive glomerulopathy that does not respond to standard steroid therapy; Additional common findings can include disorders of testicular development (with or without abnormalities of the external genitalia and/or m&#x000fc;llerian structures) and Wilms tumor. Less common findings are congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), gonadoblastoma, and 46,XX gonadal dysgenesis (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK556455/table/wt1-dis.T.wt1_disorder_frequency_of_sele/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobwt1disTwt1disorderfrequencyofsele">Table 2</a>). In adulthood, most individuals are affected by early gonadal insufficiency of variable severity with potential impact on puberty and fertility. </p></div></dd></dl></div></div></div><p><b>Progressive glomerulopathy.</b> Persistent proteinuria is the most common initial finding of the glomerulopathy in <i>WT1</i> disorder. While the degree of proteinuria may fluctuate at the onset of renal involvement, it becomes progressively worse over time. The severity of the proteinuria varies among affected individuals, even within the same family. Note: Individuals with end-stage kidney failure may be anuric, and thus will not have proteinuria.</p><p>Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) &#x02013; proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, edema, and hyperlipidemia that does not respond to standard steroid therapy &#x02013; is the characteristic kidney finding in <i>WT1</i> disorder. SRNS can precede Wilms tumor by as much as four years, present at the time of Wilms tumor diagnosis, or develop after Wilms tumor (as much as 10 years after completion of the oncology treatment) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lehnhardt.2015.825">Lehnhardt et al 2015</a>].</p><p>SRNS results in irreversible and progressive decline of kidney function and inevitably leads to end-stage kidney failure. Congenital nephrotic syndrome (nephrotic syndrome that presents in the first 3 months of life) is more rapidly progressive, resulting in end stage kidney failure within weeks to months [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.boyer.2021.277">Boyer et al 2021</a>].</p><p>Typical findings of the glomerulopathy on kidney biopsy are diffuse mesangial sclerosis reported primarily in children younger than age two years and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis in older individuals, although other diagnoses, including membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, have also been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.anderson.2022.2369">Anderson et al 2022</a>]. Note: Because the histologic findings do not correlate with the clinical findings and because remarkable histopathologic heterogeneity is observed even among individuals with the same <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lehnhardt.2015.825">Lehnhardt et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.trautmann.2017.3055">Trautmann et al 2017</a>], kidney biopsy is no longer considered a first-tier diagnostic measure for individuals of any age.</p><p><b>Wilms tumor.</b> Wilms tumor (nephroblastoma) is one of the most common pediatric malignant solid tumors. The estimated risk to heterozygotes who have an exonic <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> of developing Wilms tumor is one tumor per nine years at risk. Calculation of the exact <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> is hampered because a significant number of individuals with a <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variant undergo prophylactic nephrectomy at the time of kidney transplantation or placement of a peritoneal dialysis catheter.</p><p>The median age at Wilms tumor diagnosis in <i>WT1</i> disorder is significantly younger (median age: 1.3-1.6 years; range: 0-4.5 years) compared to Wilms tumor of unknown cause.</p><p>Bilateral tumors are more frequent in individuals with a truncating <i>WT1</i> variant compared to individuals with other variants (&#x0003e;50% vs &#x0003c;15%) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lehnhardt.2015.825">Lehnhardt et al 2015</a>] (see <a href="#wt1-dis.GenotypePhenotype_Correlations">Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</a>).</p><p>The survival rates for individuals with Wilms tumor caused by <i>WT1</i> disorder do not differ significantly from those of individuals with Wilms tumor of unknown cause.</p><p><b>Genital findings.</b> 46,XY individuals with <i>WT1</i> disorder typically have a disorder of testicular development that is either a disorder of sex development (DSD) or complete gonadal dysgenesis (CGD) (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK556455/table/wt1-dis.T.wt1_disorder_frequency_of_sele/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobwt1disTwt1disorderfrequencyofsele">Table 2</a>). 46,XY individuals with normal testes, normal male external genitalia, and normal fertility have been reported anecdotally.</p><p>46,XX individuals with a <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> typically have normal ovaries, normal female external genitalia, and m&#x000fc;llerian structures that are usually normal (however, on occasion bicornuate uterus has been observed [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>]) (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK556455/table/wt1-dis.T.wt1_disorder_frequency_of_sele/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobwt1disTwt1disorderfrequencyofsele">Table 2</a>). 46,XX CGD has been reported in two 46,XX individuals with a <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variant with absent ovaries [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.ahn.2017.81">Ahn et al 2017</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.roca.2019.836">Roca et al 2019</a>]. 46,XX DSD (testicular DSD or ovotesticular DSD) has been reported in seven individuals with a pathogenic variant in <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exon/">exon</a> 10 encoding the fourth zinc finger of <i>WT1</i> or within <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/splicing/">splicing</a> sites involved in <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/intron/">intron</a> 9 splicing [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.eozenou.2020.13680">Eozenou et al 2020</a>].</p><p>In adulthood, most individuals are affected by early gonadal insufficiency of variable severity with potential impact on puberty and fertility [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.carr_lecoindre.2024">Carr&#x000e9; Lecoindre et al 2024</a>]. Premature gonadal failure was observed in persons with all karyotypes and genotypes irrespective of the timing of renal insufficiency.</p><p><b>Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT).</b> CAKUT are observed in about 10% of individuals with <i>WT1</i> disorder. The most common kidney abnormalities are duplex kidney, horseshoe kidney, and kidney malrotation. The most commonly reported urinary tract anomalies are vesicoureteral reflux, ureteropelvic junction stenosis, and urogenital sinus (in a 46,XX individual in whom both the urethra and vagina open into a common channel).</p><p><b>Gonadoblastoma.</b> Individuals with 46,XY disorder of testicular development (either 46,XY DSD or 46,XY CGD) are at increased risk for germ cell tumors, particularly gonadoblastoma. The observed incidence is one gonadal tumor per 30 years at risk [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>].</p><p>Because of the lack of long-term follow-up data, exact <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> and long-term outcome are unknown. The survival rates for gonadoblastoma are excellent; however, if not treated it may result in malignant transformation of germ cells. A few instances of Sertoli tumor or other malignant testicular germ cell tumors have been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.kitsioutzeli.2012.361">Kitsiou-Tzeli et al 2012</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.van_peer.2024.3570">van Peer et al 2024</a>].</p><p><b>Other.</b> Diaphragmatic defect or herniation is a rare finding in <i>WT1</i> disorder, reported in fewer than ten infants [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.denamur.2000.1868">Denamur et al 2000</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.suri.2007.2312">Suri et al 2007</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.ahn.2017.81">Ahn et al 2017</a>].</p><p>Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) was reported in 7%-17% of individuals with <i>WT1</i> disorder following kidney transplantation [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.ahn.2017.81">Ahn et al 2017</a>]. In all children undergoing kidney transplantation, the 25-year cumulative incidence of PTLD, adjusted for the competing risk of death, is 3.6% (95% CI: 2.7-4.8). Because of small numbers and lack of standardized follow-up data, it is not yet possible to determine if the frequency of PTLD is higher for <i>WT1</i> disorder than for other children undergoing kidney transplantation.</p></div><div id="wt1-dis.GenotypePhenotype_Correlations"><h3>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</h3><p>Recent developments have allowed delineation of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genotype-phenotype-correlations/">genotype-phenotype correlations</a> for certain subgroups of <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variants (see also <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10157-024-02539-x/tables/1" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">Table 1</a> in <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.nagano.2025.1">Nagano &#x00026; Nozu [2025]</a>).</p><p><b>Truncating pathogenic variants</b> (all <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/nonsense-variant/">nonsense</a>, frameshift, or splice-site variants that are not KTS [lysine, threonine, and serine] <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/intron/">intron</a> 9 variants; see <a href="#wt1-dis.Molecular_Genetics">Molecular Genetics</a>) are associated with the following [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lehnhardt.2015.825">Lehnhardt et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.van_peer.2024.3570">van Peer et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.gl_nisson.2025.1205">Gl&#x000e9;nisson et al 2025</a>]:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Wilms tumor is often the first clinical manifestation.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Glomerulopathy. Proteinuria is typically diagnosed in the second decade of life in individuals who underwent unilateral or partial nephrectomy for Wilms tumor. The course of SRNS is slower.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Genital anomalies secondary to a 46,XY DSD affect the vast majority of phenotypic males; 46,XY CGD is unlikely.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The risk for bilateral Wilms tumor is the highest (odds ratio = 18.4).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>One in five individuals has CAKUT.</div></li></ul><p><b>Missense</b>
<b>pathogenic variants affecting nucleotides coding for amino acid residues in the DNA-binding region in exons 8 and 9</b> (see <a href="#wt1-dis.Molecular_Genetics">Molecular Genetics</a>) are associated with the following [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.nagano.2021.2114">Nagano et al 2021</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.gl_nisson.2025.1205">Gl&#x000e9;nisson et al 2025</a>]:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The risk for <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/congenital/">congenital</a> nephrotic syndrome or early-onset rapidly progressive SRNS is the highest. By age 2.5 years, 50% of affected children have end-stage kidney failure.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Of 46,XY individuals, approximately 80% have 46,XY DSD and 20% 46,XY CGD [BS Lipska-Zi&#x00119;tkiewicz, personal observation].</div></li></ul><p><b>Missense pathogenic variants</b>
<b>in exons 8 and 9 outside the DNA-binding region</b> are associated with an intermediate glomerulopathy <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> that manifests before age five years and progresses to end-stage kidney failure by about age ten years [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.nagano.2021.2114">Nagano et al 2021</a>].</p><p><b>Certain donor splice-site pathogenic variants</b>
<b>in <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/intron/">intron</a> 9</b> (see <a href="#wt1-dis.Molecular_Genetics">Molecular Genetics</a>) are associated with the following [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.chernin.2010.1655">Chernin et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lehnhardt.2015.825">Lehnhardt et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.tsuji.2021.2585">Tsuji et al 2021</a>]:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Later onset and relatively slow progression of glomerulopathy that typically leads to end-stage kidney failure in adolescence</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>46,XY CGD in the majority of (but not all) 46,XY individuals and 46,XY DSD in a few individuals</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Highest risk for gonadoblastoma in CGD/DSD individuals, with risk of Wilms tumor significantly lower (&#x02264;3%)</div></li></ul></div><div id="wt1-dis.Penetrance"><h3>Penetrance</h3><p>The <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> of <i>WT1</i> disorder is high. It is age dependent, reaching about 90% by the end of puberty.</p><p>A few asymptomatic parents <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for the same <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <i>WT1</i> variant in their affected offspring have been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.fencl.2012.121">Fencl et al 2012</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.kaneko.2015.1121">Kaneko et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.boyer.2017.50">Boyer et al 2017</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.kirino.2023.51">Kirino et al 2023</a>]. The <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> appears to depend on the sex of the affected parent, with higher penetrance associated with paternal origin of the <i>WT1</i> variant [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.kaneko.2015.1121">Kaneko et al 2015</a>]. However, current data on penetrance are limited because the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/variable-expressivity/">variable expressivity</a> of <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variants was not recognized until recently, and the asymptomatic parents of a child with a <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> were not routinely tested.</p></div><div id="wt1-dis.Nomenclature"><h3>Nomenclature</h3><p>Frasier syndrome, Denys-Drash syndrome, and Meacham syndrome were originally described as distinct disorders on the basis of clinical findings but are now understood to represent a continuum of features caused by a <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>. Given the extensive clinical overlap between these clinical diagnoses and molecular characterization of their shared genetic etiology, Frasier syndrome, Denys-Drash syndrome, and Meacham syndrome are no longer useful clinical diagnoses. However, these terms may still be used in the medical literature to refer to the following general phenotypic constellations:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Frasier syndrome.</b> SRNS, 46,XY CGD, and gonadoblastoma</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Denys-Drash syndrome.</b> SRNS with diffuse mesangial sclerosis on kidney biopsy, Wilms tumor, and 46,XY DSD</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Meacham syndrome.</b> Diaphragmatic hernia, pulmonary dysplasia, complex <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/congenital/">congenital</a> heart defects, and genitourinary abnormalities including ambiguous genitalia and gonadal dysgenesis; in most reports, the condition was lethal early in infancy prior to development of other possible manifestations of <i>WT1</i> disorder, such as SRNS or Wilms tumor. So far, none of the reported individuals with a confirmed <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> and a diaphragmatic defect had a complex congenital heart defect. A multigenic cause of this syndrome, with another as-yet-unknown <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> responsible for the more severe cardiopulmonary <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>, has been suggested [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.suri.2007.2312">Suri et al 2007</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Male pseudohermaphroditism.</b> The spectrum of clinical manifestations related to 46,XY DSD with a <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> was previously referred to using outdated terms such as male pseudohermaphroditism.</p></div><div id="wt1-dis.Prevalence"><h3>Prevalence</h3><p>The prevalence of <i>WT1</i> disorder is not known. Fewer than 500 affected individuals have been reported to date.</p><p>There are no <i>WT1</i> founder variants or biased geographic distribution in specific populations.</p></div></div><div The following section deals with genetic risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics professional</i>. &#x02014;ED.</p><div id="wt1-dis.Mode_of_Inheritance"><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p><i>WT1</i> disorder is inherited in an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> manner.</p></div><div id="wt1-dis.Risk_to_Family_Members"><h3>Risk to Family Members</h3><p>
<b>Parents of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Most individuals diagnosed with <i>WT1</i> disorder have the disorder as the result of an apparent <i>de novo WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>In rare families, a parent of an individual with <i>WT1</i> disorder is <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>. Intrafamilial clinical variability is observed in <i>WT1</i> disorder, and a heterozygous parent may be asymptomatic [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.fencl.2012.121">Fencl et al 2012</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.lipska.2014.1169">Lipska et al 2014</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.kaneko.2015.1121">Kaneko et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.boyer.2017.50">Boyer et al 2017</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.kirino.2023.51">Kirino et al 2023</a>] or have clinical manifestations of the disorder. For example:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.fencl.2012.121">Fencl et al [2012]</a> described monozygotic twins presenting with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/congenital/">congenital</a> nephrotic syndrome born to an asymptomatic father age 41 years with the same <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.guaragna.2013.371">Guaragna et al [2013]</a> described two families with vertical transmission of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isolated/">isolated</a> <i>WT1</i>-related steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome from an affected parent to an affected child.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> appears to be the only affected family member (i.e., a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/simplex/">simplex</a> case), <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> is recommended for the parents of the proband to evaluate their genetic status and inform <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/recurrence-risk/">recurrence risk</a> assessment. Note: A proband may appear to be the only affected family member because of failure to recognize the disorder in family members, reduced <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a>, early death of the parent before the onset of symptoms, or late onset of the disease in the affected parent. Therefore, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> occurrence of a <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> cannot be confirmed unless molecular genetic testing has demonstrated that neither parent is <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for the <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variant.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> is not identified in either parent and parental identity testing has confirmed biological maternity and paternity, the following possibilities should be considered:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">The <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> has a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">The <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> inherited a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> from a parent with gonadal (or somatic and gonadal) <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/mosaicism/">mosaicism</a>* [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.beltcheva.2016.290">Beltcheva et al 2016</a>]. Note: Testing of parental leukocyte DNA may not detect all instances of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/somatic-mosaicism/">somatic mosaicism</a> and will not detect a pathogenic variant that is present in the germ (gonadal) cells only.</div><div class="half_rhythm">*&#x000a0;If the parent is the individual in whom the <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> first occurred, the parent may have <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/somatic-mosaicism/">somatic mosaicism</a> for the variant and may be mildly/minimally affected or asymptomatic [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.beltcheva.2016.290">Beltcheva et al 2016</a>].</div></li></ul></li></ul><p><b>Sibs of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> The risk to the sibs of the proband depends on the clinical/genetic status of the proband's parents:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If a parent of the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> is affected and/or is known to have the <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the proband, the risk to the sibs of inheriting the <i>WT1</i> pathogenic variant is 50%. Although a sib who inherits a pathogenic variant is likely to have clinical manifestations of <i>WT1</i> disorder, the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> sib cannot be reliably predicted because both intrafamilial clinical variability and reduced <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> are observed in <i>WT1</i> disorder (see <a href="#wt1-dis.Penetrance">Penetrance</a>).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> cannot be detected in the leukocyte DNA of either parent, the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/recurrence-risk/">recurrence risk</a> to sibs is slightly greater than that of the general population because of the possibility of parental <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gonadal-mosaicism/">gonadal mosaicism</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.beltcheva.2016.290">Beltcheva et al 2016</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the parents have not been tested for the <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> but are clinically unaffected, the risk to the sibs of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> appears to be low. However, sibs of a proband with clinically unaffected parents are still presumed to be at increased risk for <i>WT1</i> disorder because of the possibility of reduced <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> parent and the possibility of parental <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gonadal-mosaicism/">gonadal mosaicism</a>.</div></li></ul><p><b>Offspring of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> Each child of an individual with <i>WT1</i> disorder has a 50% chance of inheriting the <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>. Note: Most individuals with a disorder of testicular development are infertile.</p><p><b>Other family members.</b> The risk to other family members depends on the status of the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>'s parents: if a parent has the <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, the parent's family members may be at risk.</p></div><div id="wt1-dis.Related_Genetic_Counseling_Issue"><h3>Related Genetic Counseling Issues</h3><p>See Management, <a href="#wt1-dis.Evaluation_of_Relatives_at_Risk">Evaluation of Relatives at Risk</a> for information on evaluating at-risk relatives for the purpose of early diagnosis and treatment.</p><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic risk and discussion of the availability of prenatal/<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> is before pregnancy.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is appropriate to offer <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> (including discussion of potential risks to offspring and reproductive options) to young adults who are affected or at risk.</div></li></ul></div><div id="wt1-dis.Prenatal_Testing_and_Preimplanta"><h3>Prenatal Testing and Preimplantation Genetic Testing</h3><p>Once the <i>WT1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> has been identified in an affected family member, prenatal and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> are possible.</p><p>Differences in perspective may exist among medical professionals and within families regarding the use of prenatal and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a>. Expression of <i>WT1</i> occurs for the most part in kidney progenitors and podocytes; in the urogenital ridge; and in gonadal progenitors [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.wilm.2016.23">Wilm &#x00026; Mu&#x000f1;oz-Chapuli 2016</a>]. More than 30 WT1 <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isoforms/">isoforms</a> are derived from alternative <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/splicing/">splicing</a> as well as alternative translation start sites and RNA editing; their relative ratios regulate particular processes of urogenital differentiation.</p><p>WT1 is a major <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/transcription-factor/">transcription factor</a> involved in cell differentiation and survival in the developing kidney, urinary tract, and gonads. WT1 regulates the expression of numerous target genes, including many genes encoding proteins that localize to the slit diaphragm of the glomeruli, such as nephrin and podocin. WT1 controls the polarity of podocytes, cytoskeleton arrangement, and the cell-matrix adhesion of podocytes. It has a tumor suppressor as well as an oncogenic role in tumor formation [<a class="bk_pop" href="#wt1-dis.REF.dong.2015.684">Dong et al 2015</a>].</p><p>
<b>Mechanism of disease causation</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Dominant-negative mechanism.</b>
<i>WT1</i> disorder is caused by a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/dominant-negative/">dominant-negative</a> mechanism. For example, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a>, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/nonsense-variant/">nonsense</a>, and frameshift variants that affect the zinc finger region may result in the loss, reduction, or altered specificity of WT1 <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isoforms/">isoforms</a> to bind target DNAs. The numerous WT1 isoforms, derived from alternative <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/splicing/">splicing</a>, alternative translation start sites, and RNA editing, likely have varying effects in different tissues during development. For example, two common pathogenic variants with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/intron/">intron</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/splice-site/">splice site</a> nucleotide changes (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK556455/table/wt1-dis.T.wt1_pathogenic_variants_refere/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobwt1disTwt1pathogenicvariantsrefere">Table 6</a>) alter the ratio of alternative transcripts. Physiologically, an alternative donor splice site in intron 9 of <i>WT1</i> results in the addition of three amino acid residues &#x02013; lysine (K), threonine (T), and serine (S), referred to as the KTS splice variant &#x02013; between the third and fourth zinc finger domains. Disruption of this splice site due to single-nucleotide variants at positions +4 or +5 of intron 9 alters the ratio of the WT1 isoforms with and without the KTS insert (+KTS and &#x02212;KTS, respectively). Affected individuals have significantly fewer +KTS splice variants, resulting in a decreased ratio of +KTS:&#x02212;KTS isoforms, a regulatory factor involved in proper testicular development in 46,XY individuals.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Tumor suppressor mechanism.</b> Consistent with the Knudson two-hit model of tumorigenesis and previous observations, children with a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/loss-of-function/">loss-of-function</a> <i>WT1</i> variant are at very high risk for Wilms tumor (see <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/wilms-ov/">Wilms Tumor Predisposition</a>). See also <a href="#wt1-dis.Cancer_and_Benign_Tumors">Cancer and Benign Tumors</a>.</div></li></ul><p><b><i>WT1</i>-specific laboratory technical considerations.</b> Because of high GC (guanine and cytosine) content, the sequencing of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exon/">exon</a> 1 of <i>WT1</i> is problematic. The numerous transcripts and their <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isoforms/">isoforms</a> resulting from alternative <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/splicing/">splicing</a> and translation start sites result in a single variant having many <a href="https://varnomen.hgvs.org" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">HGVS</a>-approved names depending on the reference sequence. As there is no consensus in the literature or databases as to the "canonic" protein sequence, care must be taken when interpreting numbers of residues. In this chapter, amino acid positions are given according to <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/NP_077742.3"
