# Dock8 mutations cripple B cell immunological synapses, germinal centers and long-lived antibody production.
**Authors:** Randall KL, Lambe T, Johnson AL, Treanor B, Kucharska E, Domaschenz H, Whittle B, Tze LE, Enders A, Crockford TL, Bouriez-Jones T, Alston D, Cyster JG, Lenardo MJ, Mackay F, Deenick EK, Tangye SG, Chan TD, Camidge T, Brink R, Vinuesa CG, Batista FD, Cornall RJ, Goodnow CC
**Journal:** Nat Immunol (2009)
**DOI:** [10.1038/ni.1820](https://doi.org/10.1038/ni.1820)

## Content

To identify genes and mechanisms involved in humoral immunity, we did a mouse genetic screen for mutations that do not affect the first wave of antibody to immunization but disrupt response maturation and persistence. The first two mutants identified had loss-of-function mutations in the gene encoding a previously obscure member of a family of Rho-Rac GTP-exchange factors, DOCK8. DOCK8-mutant B cells were unable to form marginal zone B cells or to persist in germinal centers and undergo affinity maturation. Dock8 mutations disrupted accumulation of the integrin ligand ICAM-1 in the B cell immunological synapse but did not alter other aspects of B cell antigen receptor signaling. Humoral immunodeficiency due to Dock8 mutation provides evidence that organization of the immunological synapse is critical for signaling the survival of B cell subsets required for long-lasting immunity.

Antigen receptor signaling coordinates the antibody response to infection or immunization, but the nature of the signals that guide the formation of high affinity memory B cells and long-lived humoral immunity remains unclear. Two classes of antibody deficiency syndromes are currently recognized as arising from defects in antigen receptor signaling: defects in early B cell development and common variable immunodeficiency 1. Defects in early B cell development result from mutations in the components of the B cell antigen receptor (BCR) and the Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) signaling cascade immediately downstream. These syndromes are readily identified by agammaglobulinemia and reduced numbers of circulating B cells. By contrast, common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) syndromes have relatively normal B cell numbers and may have qualitative or quantitative defects in immunoglobulin (Ig) production 2. CVID is the most common clinically significant primary immunodeficiency, yet over 75% of cases cannot currently be explained genetically or mechanistically 3. A small number of cases result from mutations in CD19 4 [http://www.signaling-gateway.org/molecule/query?afcsid=A000538], a co-receptor for enhancing BCR signaling that recruits PI3-kinase5 [http://www.signaling-gateway.org/molecule/query?afcsid=A001770]. Other subsets are due to mutations in TACI, which is one of the B cell receptors for the TNF-related cytokines BAFF and APRIL 6, 7, or mutations in ICOS, which serves as a costimulatory receptor for T follicular helper cells 8. Understanding the variable antibody responses and deficiencies of switched memory B cells in the CVID spectrum of disorders 9 is hindered by the limited understanding of genes and signaling mechanisms that are specifically required for maturation and longevity of the antibody response.

To identify new genes and mechanisms generating mature antibody responses, and animal models for understanding CVID and related immunodeficiencies, we describe here an immunization screen for selective defects in antibody response maturation and longevity in mouse pedigrees segregating thousands of induced single nucleotide substitutions. The first two pedigrees with humoral immune deficiency identified by this screen resulted from independent mutations in a previously obscure member of the DOCK family of Rho-Rac guanine exchange factors (GEFs; [http://www.signaling-gateway.org/molecule/query?afcsid=A002062]), Dedicator of cytokinesis 8 (DOCK8). The consequences of the mutation in B cells provide evidence that polarization of lymphocyte antigen receptors and integrins into an immune synapse is critical for signaling survival and establishment of high affinity antibody producing cells and long-lasting immunity, and yield a timely animal model for understanding human immunodeficiency.

To understand the generation of long-lived humoral immunity, we developed a genetic screen that could identify mutant mice with a failure to mount long-lived, high affinity antibody responses despite a relatively normal initial wave of antibody production. 301 pedigrees of C57BL/6 mice, each segregating thousands of N-ethyl-N-nitrosourea (ENU)-induced single nucleotide substitutions, were tested for initial and mature serum antibody after immunizing multiple siblings in each pedigree with a mixture of antigens. Two recessive mutations that disrupted antibody response maturation (Fig. 1a,b) were propagated from independent pedigrees and denoted captain morgan (cpm) and primurus (pri). During the initial antibody response, IgG antibodies against killed Bordetella pertussis bacteria and against the protein antigen chicken gamma globulin (CGG) were correctly polarized to IgG2c and IgG1, respectively, and reached indistinguishable titers 14 days after immunization in pri/pri homozygous mice, cpm/cpm animals, and wild-type siblings (Fig. 1a, top; Fig. 1b; and data not shown). Since the majority of CGG-antibody and B. pertussis antibody produced at this time comes from extrafollicular B cell clonal expansion and plasma cell formation in collaboration with TH1 and TH2 T cells in the T cell zone 10, the absence of a substantial effect of the mutations on this phase of antibody production establishes that this phase of B and T cell activation is intact. The homozygous mutations also did not have an appreciable effect on T cell-independent extrafollicular B cell responses to nitrophenyl (NP) hapten coupled to the polysaccharide antigen Ficoll (Fig. 1a). However during the mature phase of the antibody response, either after a booster immunization (44 days, Fig. 1a) or without boosting (28 or 56 days after primary immunization, Fig. 1b), IgG antibody to CGG failed to be sustained in pri/pri, cpm/cpm mice or in compound heterozygotes. The CGG immunogen carried a haptenic chemical group, azobenzenearsonate (ARS), which only elicits appreciable antibody in wild-type mice after 4 weeks (Fig. 1b) due to the need for antibody hypermutation and affinity maturation 11, 12. Antibody to the ARS hapten failed to develop in pri or cpm homozygotes or compound heterozygotes.

A comprehensive analysis of B cell subsets in blood and primary and secondary lymphoid organs revealed qualitatively normal development of circulating mature subsets, but near-absence of marginal zone B (MZB) cells (Fig. 2). Formation of immature B cells in the bone marrow and accumulation of mature follicular B cells appeared normal in mutant mice (Fig. 2a,b and data not shown), but there was profound deficiency of splenic MZB cells in cpm and pri homozygotes and cpm/pri compound heterozygotes (Fig. 2a-d, and Supplementary Fig. 1), which was cell autonomous to B cells bearing the mutation in mixed chimeras (Fig. 2e). MZB cell numbers are markedly elevated in transgenic mice over-expressing BAFF cytokine, but this failed to rescue MZB cell formation by mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). The frequency of B1 B cells in the peritoneal cavity was decreased 60% in the mutant mice (Fig. 2f) and this was cell autonomous in mixed chimeras. Analysis of splenic T cells revealed normal numbers of activated or memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells but a mean 50% decrease in the number of naïve T cells in cpm and pri homozygotes and in cpm/pri compound heterozygotes, although this trait overlapped the lower end of the normal range and was not reliable for identifying individual homozygotes (Fig. 2g). In 50:50 bone marrow chimeras with mutant T cells reconstituting in the presence of wild-type T cells, T cells bearing the mutation had a cell-autonomous competitive disadvantage and comprised only 5% of the total peripheral T cells (Fig. 2h).

MZ B cell deficiency provided a clear Mendelian recessive trait to map and identify the causative cpm and pri mutations. Both mutations mapped to the same region of chromosome 19 (Supplementary Fig. 3) and sequencing of B cell-expressed transcripts within the minimal 4.5 Mb interval identified independent mutations in Dock8 (Fig. 3a). DOCK8 is a little studied member of a family of proteins that are critical activators of the Rho family of small GTPases (G-proteins) in worms, flies and mammals 13. In other DOCK proteins, the Dock Homology Region 1 (DHR1) domain (also known as CDM-zizimin homology 1 or CZH1 domain) bindsphosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-triphosphate (PtdIns(3,4,5)P3) lipids generated byphosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI(3)K) activity to promote localized membrane binding and activation. The DHR2 (CZH2) domain binds to Rho-family G-proteins (Rho, Rac, Cdc42; [http://www.signaling-gateway.org/molecule/query?afcsid=A002062]), promoting GTP exchange and activating local cytoskeletal changes, integrin-mediated adhesion, lamellipodia formation, cell polarization, phagocytosis or cell fusion 13, 14. DOCK8 has been isolated in a yeast two-hybrid screen for binding partners of Cdc42 and found to localize at lamellipodia in fibroblasts 15, but its in vivo function was unknown.

The cpm mutation was a T to C substitution in the exon 20 splice donor sequence that caused aberrant mRNA splicing between cryptic 5′ or 3′ splice donors and the normal exon 21 splice acceptor (Fig. 3b,c). Sequencing of 74 cDNA clones from cpm/cpm splenocytes showed aberrant splicing to yield frame-shifts and premature stop codons that eliminate the DHR2 domain entirely (Fig. 3c). The pri mutation, in exon 43, was a T to C substitution changing codon 1827 from serine to proline (Fig. 3a). The mutated serine is absolutely conserved in vertebrate DOCK8 and in the DHR2 domain of DOCK6, 7 and 9 (Fig. 3d). In the structure of DOCK9 complexed with Cdc42 16 this conserved serine lies in DHR2 lobe B alpha helix 6, which forms part of the Cdc42 binding site and provides four Cdc42 contact residues (triangles in Fig. 3d). The pri substitution of Ser 1827 to Pro would be expected to break this alpha helical structure and interfere with the GEF activity of the DHR2 domain. The nature of the mutations, their recessive inheritance, and their failure to complement in crosses establish the cause of the antibody response, B and T cell defects as being due to loss of normal DOCK8 function. Dock8 mRNA was expressed ten times more abundantly in B and T lymphocytes than in other tissues (Supplementary Fig. 4). Since homozygotes for the truncating Dock8cpm allele are weaned at normal Mendelian frequency and appear normal in growth and behavior, we conclude that Dock8 has a relatively specialized role in adaptive immunity.

Since immunization of DOCK8 mutant mice elicited a normal early wave of antibody formation but the response failed to mature or be sustained, we sought to define the cellular basis for this variable immune deficiency. Following immunization with sheep red blood cells (SRBCs), wild-type mice formed large germinal centers, denoted by peanut agglutinin-positive (PNA+) IgD− B cells, whereas pri/pri and cpm/cpm animals formed only small PNA+ clusters (Fig. 4a and data not shown). Phenotypically normal CD95+ GL7+ B220+ GC B cells could be readily detected by flow cytometry in homozygous mutants after immunization but their numbers were only 15% of normal (Fig. 4b). Immunization of competitive hematopoietic chimeras, constructed by reconstituting irradiated mice with a mixture of CD45.1+ wild-type and CD45.2+
pri/pri or cpm/cpm bone marrow, revealed that B cells bearing the DOCK8 mutation contributed normally to the main subsets of B220+ and IgD+ recirculating follicular B cells but contributed poorly to the B220+ CD95+ GL7+ GC subset particularly at day 11 after immunization when the response has begun maturing (Fig. 4c). The mutations therefore acted autonomously within individual B cells to interfere selectively with their capacity to differentiate into or sustain a GC state.

The precise step where the mutation interfered with GC B cells was determined by tracing a homogeneous population of B cells specific for hen egg lysozyme (HEL) antigen after adoptive transfer into wild-type mice immunized with SRBCs conjugated to HEL (Fig. 5,6). The primurus mutation was crossed with CD45.1 SWHEL transgenic mice, bearing a rearranged HEL-specific V(D)JH element targeted into the Igh locus encoding immunoglobulin H chain combined with a HEL-specific kappa L-chain transgene 17. B cells from pri/pri CD45.1 SWHEL transgenic donors, or from wild-type CD45.1 SWHEL transgenic controls, were adoptively transferred into wild-type C57BL6 (CD45.2) recipients. The recipients were then immunized with a conjugate antigen comprising SRBCs covalently decorated with HEL protein bearing two substitutions (HEL2X). In contrast with the very high affinity of Ig receptors on SWHEL B cells for native HEL (2 × 1010 M−1), HEL2X is recognized with intermediate affinity (8 × 107 M−1) by Ig receptors on SWHEL B cells due largely to the effect of substituting Asp101 to Arg 18-20. Six amino acids in the complementarity determining region 2 (CDR2) loop of the SWHEL VH chain contact native HEL at Asp101 21, and selection of amino acid replacements in these CDR2 residues increases the affinity for D101R-substituted HEL2X or HEL3X in SWHEL GC cells 19 and unpublished data).

In HEL2X-SRBC immunized wild-type recipients, both wild-type and pri/pri HEL-specific B cells underwent comparable activation into GL7+ Fas+ cells scattered within follicles 2.5 days after immunization, and comparable rates of isotype switching to IgG1 occurred between days 3.5 and 4.5 (Fig. 5a,b and Supplementary Fig. 5). Germinal centers formed within follicles on day 4.5, when the majority of both wild-type and mutant SWHEL B cells had differentiated into Fashi GL7hi GC cells. No accumulation or GC differentiation by donor B cells occurred when recipients were immunized with SRBCs lacking HEL2X (groups marked by asterisks). Immunoglobulin class switching is a sensitive indicator of T helper cell signals 22, and requires CD40 on SWHEL B cells in immune responses to HEL2X-SRBC 23. These data reinforce those in Fig. 1 to indicate that DOCK8 is not essential for the initial phase of B cell activation and collaboration with helper T cells in vivo.

By contrast, DOCK8 deficiency more profoundly disrupted the response 4.5-10 days after immunization when wild-type SWHEL B cells undergo hypermutation and affinity maturation. Although there was no discernable difference in their location within germinal centers on days 4.5 and 5 post-immunization (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 5), accumulation of SWHEL
pri/pri GC cells at this time was one-third of wild-type controls at this time (Fig. 5b, 6b) and fell to one-fortieth of controls by day 9 despite ongoing germinal centers composed of wild-type host B and T cells (Fig. 6b). This difference was also observed upon adoptive transfer of lymph node cells from SWHEL mice (Supplementary Fig. 6), excluding the possibility that it results from absence of MZB cells in transferred pri/pri SWHEL spleen cells. IgG1-switched B cells doubled as a percentage of wild-type SWHEL B cells on day 9 compared with day 7 or 5, whereas the percentage IgG1+ failed to increase in SWHEL GC B cells bearing the pri mutation (Fig. 6b).

Nine days after HEL2X-SRBC immunization, sequencing of Igh genes from individual flow-sorted SWHEL GC B cells showed that wild-type cells had accumulated a high ratio of replacement to silent mutations (10.3) in the CDR2 residues contacting the D101R substitution in HEL2X (Fig. 6c,d and Supplementary Fig. 7). Six of the 38 cells analyzed (16%) had Y58F replacements in CDR2 that have been shown to increase affinity 24. Flow cytometric staining with nanomolar concentrations of HEL3X directly enumerated high affinity IgG+ variants for D101R-substituted HEL at this time, comprising 18% of wild-type IgG+ SWHEL B cells, 3.4% of all SWHEL B cells and 0.0082% of all spleen cells (Fig 6e). Sorted pri/pri GC cells on day 9 had accumulated the same or greater load of VH nucleotide mutations (mean of 2.3 per cell) as their wild-type counterparts (mean of 2.0), including comparable numbers of mutations in the CDR2 codons. Despite undergoing comparable hypermutation, the mutant GC cells had a low ratio of replacement to silent mutations in CDR2 (2.4) and only 2 out of 44 cells (4.5%) had Y58F replacements (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 7). Flow cytometric staining with HEL3X showed that pri/pri SWHEL B cells generated few high affinity IgG+ variants to D101R-substituted HEL even when adjusted for their poor survival (Fig. 6e). These cells represented only 3% of IgG1+ SWHEL B cells, 0.2% of the SWHEL B cells and 0.00004% of all spleen cells. DOCK8 deficiency thus acts within GC B cells to allow normal immunoglobulin gene hypermutation but diminishing survival and selection so that the formation of higher affinity IgG+ B cells was decreased by 200-fold.

A systematic analysis of the effects of DOCK8 deficiency upon established molecular pathways for GC and MZ B cell formation revealed a selective disruption of immunological synapse organization. When B cells recognize antigen on cell membranes, BCR-signaling triggers LFA-1 integrin to bind strongly to ICAM-1[http://www.signaling-gateway.org/molecule/query?afcsid=A002871] on the antigen-presenting membrane, promoting strong B cell adhesion and spreading 25. During the first 3 min of this process, BCRs become polarized in the center of the adhesion zone and cluster antigen into a central supramolecular cluster (cSMAC). LFA-1 becomes polarized into a surrounding peripheral supramolecular cluster (pSMAC) where it concentrates ICAM-1 in the antigen presenting membrane. DOCK8 mutant and wild-type B cells were isolated from MD4 transgenic mice expressing HEL-specific BCRs. The B cells were allowed to settle on planar membranes containing fluorescently-tagged HEL antigen and ICAM-1, and imaged by a combination of confocal, differential interference contrast (DIC) and interference-reflection microscopy (IRM). Both mutant and wild-type cells adhered and clustered antigen into a cSMAC, but the mutant cells exhibited an inability to cluster ICAM-1 into a pSMAC and consequently formed smaller areas of close membrane apposition (Fig. 7a).

By contrast with the disruption of B cell synapse formation, DOCK8 mutation had no effect on other BCR signaling events including anti-IgM stimulated elevation of intracellular calcium (Fig. 7b) or ERK phosphorylation (not shown), induction of activation markers CD25, CD86 and CD69 (Fig. 7c), and induction of DNA synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 8) or cell division (Fig. 7d), even under conditions of limiting BCR engagement (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Fig. 9,10). The mutation also had no discernable effect on CD40- or LPS-stimulated activation or proliferation (Fig. 7d, Supplementary Fig. 8-10 and data not shown). DOCK8 mutant B cells were also normal in forming stable conjugates during antigen presentation to specific CD4+ T cells, in a B–T cell adhesion process that depends upon the adaptor protein SAP (SLAM-associated protein) in T cells and is important for GC formation 26 (Supplementary Fig. 11). DOCK8 mutation also did not decrease in vivo homing and accumulation of B cells in lymph nodes in competitive mixed chimeras (Supplementary Fig. 12), despite the dependency of this process on signaling of LFA-1 adhesion to ICAM-1 by CCR7 and CXCR4 chemokine receptors 27. After adoptive transfer, DOCK8 mutant SWHEL B cells migrated and positioned themselves normally in follicles and germinal centers (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 5). Mutant and wild-type spleen B cells were indistinguishable by in vitro chemotaxis to the three main chemotactic factors for B cells, namely S1P, CXCL12 and CXCL13 (Supplementary Fig. 13). DOCK8 therefore functions quite specifically in B cells, being necessary for BCR signaling of integrin–ICAM-1 polarization within the immunological synapse but not for many other BCR-, chemotactic, or integrin-mediated processes.

The findings above reveal that mutations in DOCK8 cause humoral immunodeficiency that preserves T-independent antibody formation and the initial extrafollicular wave of T-dependent antibody formation, but severely cripples the longevity and affinity maturation of T-dependent antibody responses. The basis for these humoral deficits is a profound defect in germinal center B cell survival and selection during the affinity maturation phase of germinal center responses, due to an intrinsic requirement for DOCK8 within the GC B cells themselves. The GC B cell defect may be compounded by the decrease in the numbers of CD4+ helper T cells in DOCK8 mutant mice, although mixed chimera and adoptive transfer studies clearly establish that the mutation profoundly cripples the persistence and affinity maturation of mutant GC B cells even in the presence of a normal complement of helper T cells. Within B cells, DOCK8 plays a critical role in antigen recognition, being essential for recruiting ICAM-1 to the immunological synapse but unnecessary for the central events in BCR signaling to soluble ligands leading to activation and proliferation. The requirement for DOCK8 in B cell synapse formation and for longevity and affinity maturation of T-dependent antibody illuminates an important step in the establishment of humoral immunity.

The requirement for DOCK8 to organize a normally polarized B cell immunological synapse is very similar to defects in B cell pSMAC formation caused by mutations in the Rho-family G protein, Rac2 [http://www.signaling-gateway.org/molecule/query?afcsid=A002002], or in the Rac GEFs Vav1 or Vav2 28 [http://www.signaling-gateway.org/molecule/query?afcsid=A002361]. In comparison to these proteins, DOCK8 appears to have a more restricted function for BCR-integrin coordination, because follicular B cell development, migration, chemotaxis, activation and T-independent type 2 antibody responses are normal in Dock8 mutant B cells whereas pleiotropic defects in these processes are caused by mutations in Rac2 or Vav proteins. In addition to their role in BCR-integrin coordination, Rac2 and Vav proteins are critical for the initial events in BCR signaling of intracellular calcium and proliferation in response to soluble anti-IgM and for chemokine responsiveness 29-32. The specificity of DOCK8 function in BCR-integrin signaling and GC selection in B cells also contrasts with that of DOCK2, whose mutation in mice causes profound lymphopenia, disrupted lymphoid architecture, crippled B cell migration to lymph nodes and to CXCL12 and CXCL13 chemokines in vitro
33, and a generalized defect in B cell integrin activation in response to a range of stimuli 34. To understand how DOCK8 serves such a restricted role in B cells compared to Rac2, Vav or DOCK2, it will be interesting in future work to define the G-proteins and biochemical pathway regulated by DOCK8 for B cell immune synapse formation.

Mutations in CD19, a co-receptor that is phosphorylated by BCR engagement and then recruits PI(3)K 5, cause a human CVID syndrome with normal numbers of IgD+ recirculating B cells, presence of germinal centers, but low numbers of CD27+ memory B cells and low avidity antibody to rabies vaccine 4. In the mouse, CD19 mutations that eliminate the protein or inactivate its ability to recruit PI(3)K cause a humoral immunodeficiency syndrome that closely resembles that caused by DOCK8 mutation. These mice have normal numbers of recirculating IgD+ B cells, lack of MZB cells, normal calcium response and proliferation to soluble anti-IgM stimulation, relatively normal TI-2 antibody responses, and initiation of GC reactions and isotype switching but limited expansion of GCs and failure of affinity maturation or long-lived antibody 35-38. The reduction in GCs and loss of MZB cells in CD19 mutant mice can be rescued by a mutation in the PtdIns(3,4,5)P3 phosphatase, PTEN, underscoring the role of PtdIns(3,4,5)P3 lipid products of PI(3)K in these processes 39. A distinction between mutations in CD19 and DOCK8 is that CD19 deficiency greatly diminished the early step of B cell adhesion and clustering of membrane HEL antigen in the bilayer assay used here 40, whereas DOCK8 deficiency was restricted to disrupting the later step of pSMAC formation. This difference may reflect the proximal role of CD19 in recruiting not only PI(3)K but also BCR signaling molecules with general roles in the initiation of BCR signaling such as Lyn 41. The phenotypic similarities and the fact that the DHR1 domain of related DOCK proteins binds PtdIns(3,4,5)P3 lipids to recruit these GEFs to the membrane and activate Rac at sites of PI(3)K activation 13, make it logical to hypothesize that DOCK8 serves as an effector downstream of CD19 and PI(3)K to promote G-protein signaling events critical for integrin polarization at the synapse and for marginal zone and germinal center B cell survival.

The discovery here that DOCK8 mutations result in humoral immune deficiency in mice is complemented by the independent discovery of DOCK8 mutations associated with immunodeficiency in humans 42. Recurrent sinopulmonary bacterial infections typical of humoral immunodeficiency were observed in DOCK8 deficient people, in addition to cutaneous viral infections usually associated with partial T cell deficiency. Despite normal or increased gammaglobulins, these individuals exhibited a variable lack of antibody to T-dependent vaccines. The data here establish that DOCK8 deficiency indeed causes a primary humoral immunodeficiency selectively affecting the longevity and maturation of T-dependent antibody responses to protein antigens, and establish its cellular basis and its B cell intrinsic role. Although the focus of the current study was to understand the humoral defects caused by DOCK8 deficiency, an additional requirement for DOCK8 in T cells is demonstrated here and associated with the human syndrome. Future, separate studies will be needed to define the cellular basis for the decreased numbers of peripheral T cells caused by DOCK8 mutation.

The requirement for DOCK8 to form marginal zone B cells may be explained by the fact that this B cell subset is highly dependent upon both BCR-CD19-PI(3)K 35, 38, 43-45 and integrin signaling 46, 47. Coordinated signaling by BCR and integrins has also been proposed as critical for GC B cell survival and selection, since ICAM-1 is highly expressed on follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) presenting antigen in germinal centers and inhibits apoptosis of GC B cells in vitro
48. ICAM-1 fails to be induced on FDCs when the Ikbkb gene is selectively inactivated in FDCs, and this leads to small germinal centers 49. In vivo soluble antigen has the opposite effect from FDC-bound antigen on germinal center B cells by triggering apoptosis within 4 h 50, 51. ICAM-1 costimulation has also been shown to modify the outcome of TCR stimulation in ways that are distinct from CD28 costimulation, converting a death signal into a survival signal in immature thymocytes 52, 53 and being critical in formation of memory CD8+ T cells 54. It is tempting to speculate that DOCK8 immunodeficiency may result from failure of germinal center B lymphocytes and some classes of T cells to organize a normal immune synapse and thereby receive an integrin costimulus during antigen recognition that is needed to convert an antigen receptor-induced death signal into a survival signal.

DNA synthesis assays were carried out as previously described 56. For CFSE division assays, cells were labeled with 1.5μM CFSE for 5 min at 37°C and then cultured for 96 h. For chemotaxis assays, cells were washed in RPMI1640 with 0.5% fatty-acid-free BSA several times, re-sensitized for 30 min at 37°C, and then tested for transmigration across uncoated 5 mm transwell filters (Corning Costar) for 3 h to the indicated chemokines in the bottom chamber as described 57. Analysis of B cell synapse formation on lipid bilayers 28 and in vitro B:T cell conjugation assays (with peptide activated OT-II cells and LPS stimulated B cells) 26 were performed as previously described.

(B6.cpm × 129)F1 and (B6.pri × B10)F1 mice were intercrossed to generate F2 progeny, and strain specific microsatellite and SNP markers used to map the cpm and pri loci to chromosome 19. (B6.cpm × CBA)F2 and (B6.pri × CBA)F2 mice were analyzed to map to a 4.5Mb interval using the SNP markers rs3697576, rs13483577 and rs3717280. Dock8 was amplified from genomic DNA or splenocyte cDNA (primer sequences available upon request) and sequenced using ABI Big-Dye terminators and analysis on an ABI 3830 capillary sequencer. Splenocyte cDNA was amplified with primers spanning Dock8 exons 18-21, products resolved on agarose gels and TA-subcloned into Invitrogen TOPO TA Cloning® Kit (with pCR2.1 vector) and individual colonies picked were PCR amplified and sequenced to identify the splicing products produced by the cpm/cpm mutation.
