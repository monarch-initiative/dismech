# CGGV:assertion_f11594cd-9107-4a45-86af-4e464417ce50-2026-06-17T160000.000Z  GCK / hyperinsulinism due to glucokinase deficiency

**CGGV:assertion_f11594cd-9107-4a45-86af-4e464417ce50-2026-06-17T160000.000Z** - GCK / hyperinsulinism due to glucokinase deficiency (Definitive)

## Evidence summary

Activating variants in the GCK gene, which encodes glucokinase, a hexokinase expressed in pancreatic β-cells, were first reported in association with familial hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia (HH) in 1998 (Glaser et al., PMID: 9435328). These variants typically follow an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. Gain-of-function variants in GCK lead to increased glucokinase activity, resulting in a lowered glucose threshold for insulin secretion. Consequently, pancreatic β-cells inappropriately secrete insulin at low blood glucose concentrations, causing hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia (PMIDs:37725835,34680961). Heterozygous activating variants are associated with autosomal dominant hyperinsulinism and demonstrate marked phenotypic variability. Clinical presentation ranges from severe neonatal-onset hypoglycemia to milder forms presenting later in childhood or adulthood. While some individuals exhibit persistent hypoglycemia requiring treatment, others may have intermittent or relatively mild symptoms. Rarely, atypical presentations have been reported in individuals with activating variants in GCK , including mild or asymptomatic hypoglycemia, variable severity, hypoglycemia unawareness, and diagnosis later in life. Considerable variability can also be observed within families.

Per criteria outlined by the ClinGen Lumping and Splitting Working Group, we found differences in inheritance pattern as well as molecular mechanism and phenotypic variability underlying the GCK -related disease entities. Therefore, we have split curations for these disease entities. The present curation includes autosomal dominant GCK - related hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia caused by activating (GOF) variants only. Autosomal dominant forms of monogenic hyperglycemia caused by heterozygous inactivating (LOF) variants (MONDO:0015967) and permanent neonatal diabetes caused by bi-allelic inactivating variants are curated separately as semi-dominant GCK -related monogenic diabetes.

The clinical presentation of individuals with activating variants in GCK is characterized by hyperinsulinism, typically presenting as persistent or intermittent hypoglycemia during the neonatal or infantile period, although later-presenting cases have also been reported. Activating variants lower the glucose threshold for insulin secretion by increasing glucokinase activity, resulting in inappropriate insulin release at low blood glucose concentrations. Affected individuals can activate ketogenesis after prolonged fasting, a distinguishing feature from other forms of hyperinsulinism. Many affected individuals have a partial response to medical therapy, particularly diazoxide, although treatment response is variable. These variants predominantly cluster in the allosteric activator site within the small domain near the hinge region, where they stabilize the active conformation of the enzyme and increase glucose affinity. However, additional activating variants have been identified in the hinge and distal large-domain regions, demonstrating that multiple structural domains contribute to glucokinase activation through distinct mechanisms (PMIDs: 14517946, 18450771,19790256,15277402, 29425029). Classification of HI as GCK -related HI is established by identification of a pathogenic activating GCK variant. In some cases, pancreatic histology may demonstrate enlarged or hyperplastic islets, which can be seen in other HI etiologies.

Dominantly acting gain-of-function variants in GCK gene have been curated in at least 17 probands across 11 publications (PMIDs: 3468096, 9435328, 21454522, 15277402, 24890200, 37725835,18450771,19884385,11916951,17976205,31094068,) with clinical presentations consistent with GCK- related hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia. Additional evidence supporting this gene-disease association is available in the literature, but as the maximum score for genetic evidence (12 points) has been reached this has not been exhaustively curated.

The gene–disease relationship is strongly supported by gain-of-function mechanism, with evidence from functional studies demonstrating increased glucokinase activity, expression data, and animal models. The total points for the experimental evidence are 5, and the combined total points for genetic and experimental evidence together are 17.

In summary, GCK is definitively associated with autosomal dominant hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia due to activating variants. This has been repeatedly demonstrated in both the research and clinical diagnostic settings and has been upheld over time.
