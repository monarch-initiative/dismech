3-Hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase deficiency (HIBCHD) is a rare inborn error of
the valine catabolic pathway associated with Leigh-like disease. We report a
female patient who presented at the age of 5months with hypotonia, developmental
delay and cerebral atrophy on MRI. Pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency was
initially suspected and decreased activity was shown in fibroblasts. Urine
tandem mass spectrometry screening showed large increases in the cysteine
conjugate of methacrylate previously described in HIBCHD.
3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase activity in fibroblasts was below the limit of
detection of the enzymatic assay and two novel HIBCH mutations were identified
(c.[129dupA];[1033G>A]). Urine metabolite investigations also showed increases
in 3-hydroxyisobutyryl carnitine, 2,3-dihydroxy-2-methylbutyrate and several
metabolites indicating accumulation and subsequent metabolism of methacrylyl-CoA
and acryloyl-CoA. The metabolites derived from acryloyl-CoA were also increased
in patients with inborn errors of propionyl-CoA metabolism, indicating the
involvement of a secondary propionyl-CoA pathway utilising
3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase. With the exception of 3-hydroxyisobutyryl
carnitine, the metabolite abnormalities were essentially the same as those
observed in patients with ECHS1 mutations, a recently described disorder that
also affects valine metabolism. Our findings demonstrate the benefits of urine
tandem mass spectrometry screening for diagnosing HIBCH and ECHS1 defects and
that propionate metabolism may play a role in their pathogenesis. These
disorders should be considered during the differential diagnosis of Leigh
like-diseases and hypotonia.

1. Introduction
3-Hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase (HIBCH) catalyses theﬁfth step
in the valine catabolic pathway in eukaryotes, namely the conversion of
3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA to free 3-hydroxyisobutyrate [1]. This mito-
chondrial enzyme is also reactive towards 3-hydroxypropionyl-CoA,
giving it a dual role in a secondary pathway of propionate metabolism
(Fig. 1 ). 3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase de ﬁciency (HIBCHD,
OMIM 250620) was ﬁrst described in 1982 in a neonate with multiple
congenital abnormalities [2]. An important clue to the diagnosis was
the presence of two unusual amino acids, S-(2-carboxypropyl)cysteine
(SCPC) and S-(2-caboxypropyl)cysteamine (SCPCM), in the urine of
this child [3]. Their structures suggested they were conjugates of
methacrylyl-CoA, the immediate precursor of 3-hydroxyisobutyryl-
CoA, with cysteine and cysteamine respectively (Fig. 1).
Recently, short-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase deﬁciency (SCEHD) has
been described as another inborn error of metabolism affecting valine
metabolism and associated with Leigh disease [4]. Short-chain enoyl-
CoA hydratase (also known as crotonase) is encoded by ECHS1 and is
active in several pathways including valine, isoleucine and fatty acid
catabolism. Interestingly, the metabolite abnormalities described in
ECHS1 deﬁciency thus far are restricted to the valine pathway. Short-
chain enoyl-CoA hydratase is immediately upstream of HIBCH in the
metabolic pathway and increased excretion of SCPC and SCPCM,
reﬂecting the accumulation of methacrylyl-CoA, was also a feature of
patients with ECHS1 mutations.
In this report, we describe a new patient with HIBCHD identiﬁed via
urine screening for SCPC and compare and contrast the metabolite
proﬁles in HIBCH and ECHS1 defects. Several new urine metabolites
were identiﬁed, some implicating the glutathione/mercapturic pathway
in the elimination of accumulating intermediates. These new

2. Material and methods
2.1. Materials
Structures and synthesis of metabolites are described in the
Supplementary Material.
2.2. Metabolite measurements
Semi-quantitative screening of random urine samples was performed
by electrospray tandem mass spectrometry as previously described[5]
with the addition of the mass transitions for SCPC and its carnitine
ester as described in Supplementary Material Table 1. Quantitative deter-
mination of metabolites was performed by UPLC –MSMS as previously
described [4] with the addition N-acetyl-S-(2-carboxypropyl)cysteine
(NAcSCPC) and N-acetyl-S-(2-carboxyethyl)cysteine (NAcSCEC) as de-
scribed in the Supplementary Material. Standard tandem mass spectrom-
etry methods were used to measure dried blood spot acyl carnitines.
Organic acids were analysed as methoxime/trimethylsilyl derivatives by
standard GC-MS methodology.
2.3. Enzyme assays
PDHC activity was measured in cultured skinﬁbroblasts (Prof David
Thorburn, Murdoch Children ’s Research Institute) as previously de-
scribed [6]. HIBCH activity in cultured skin ﬁbroblasts was measured
as previously described [7].
3. Results
3.1. Case reports
3.1.1. HIBCHD patient
This female was from a non-consanguineous Anglo-Celtic family.
There was intrauterine growth retardation during pregnancy and the
birth weight was 2160 g following induction at 38 weeks gestation.
She presented with feeding difﬁculties. At eight weeks she was not ﬁx-
ing or following. At four months she was noted to be markedly irritable.
There were no overt seizures but her EEG showed poorly modulated
background with excessive slow activity. Her development was delayed
and she was noted to be hypotonic. Routine metabolic investigations at
that time (plasma and CSF amino acids, urine amino and organic acids,
transferrin isoforms, very long chain fatty acids) were uninformative.
At six months she was ﬁxing and following, smiling (non-social smile)
but was severely hypotonic. MRI performed at ten months showed cere-
bral atrophy with markedly delayed myelination, thinning of the corpus
callosum, and markedly increased signals in the brain stem, upper pons,
basal ganglia and thalami. These ﬁndings progressed, as shown in a
follow-up MRI at 17 months (see Supplementary Material Fig. 1). She
was thought to have pyruvate dehydrogenase de ﬁciency and this was
shown enzymatically in cultured ﬁbroblasts. However, the coding re-
gion of the E1 α gene was sequenced and was found normal (Dr Garry
Brown, Oxford, UK), raising the possibility of a secondary pyruvate de-
hydrogenase de ﬁciency. Respiratory chain complexes activities were
normal in liver and muscle. Testing for SCPC was later introduced into
the routine urine screening panel which led to the diagnosis being
made during repeat investigations at age seven years. At seven years
she was non communicative, had no head control and was unable to
sit unsupported. She died in palliative care at the age of eight years.

Two siblings with ECHS1 mutations presenting in the neonatal
period with Leigh disease were previously described[4].

3.3. Enzyme activities
PDHC activity relative to citrate synthase activity in the HIBCHD
patient's cultured skin ﬁbroblasts was 26% of controls as previously re-
ported [6]. This was initially considered indicative of PDHC de ﬁciency.
After the diagnosis of HIBCHD was made, PDHC activity was also mea-
sured in cultured ﬁbroblasts from the index HIBCHD patient [2] and a
similar level of activity (31%) was found.
HIBCH activity in cultured skinﬁbroblasts was below the limit of de-

3.4. Sequencing results
Sequencing of HIBCH revealed two novel mutations (genotype was
c.[129dupA];[1033G N A]). Parental testing showed that the mother
and father carried the c.[129dupA] and c.[1033GN A] mutations, respec-
tively. The c.[129dupA] nonsense mutation introduces a stop codon
eight amino acids downstream. The missense c.[1033G N A] mutation
predicts a p.Gly345Ser change affecting the substrate binding pocket
and is predicted to be damaging with a Polyphen2 score of 0.997.
4. Discussion
The ﬁrst three steps of the valine, leucine and isoleucine catabolic
pathways are analogous and result in the production of the unsaturated
intermediates methacrylyl-CoA, 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA and tiglyl-CoA
respectively. Whereas 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA and tiglyl-CoA are sub-
sequently metabolised as CoA esters, methacrylyl-CoA is converted to
3-hydroxyisobutyrate by the concerted action of mitochondrial short-
chain enoyl-CoA hydratase (crotonase) and HIBCH. After further
metabolism, a CoA moiety is again added to produce propionyl-CoA
(Fig. 1). The removal and subsequent addition of CoA is energetically
unfavourable and alternative, more efﬁcient pathways are theoretically
possible. It has been suggested that this unusual metabolic arrangement
serves to minimise accumulation of methacrylyl-CoA which is highly re-
active and capable of spontaneously reacting with sulphydryl groups
with subsequent disruption of many biochemical systems [8]. HIBCH
and short-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase have a much higher tissue activity
and turnover numbers compared to other enzymes in the valine cata-
bolic pathway and therefore methacrylyl-CoA will be rapidly converted
to less toxic 3-hydroxyisobutyrate[8,9].
Human deﬁciency of HIBCH appears to be quite rare. It was ﬁrst de-
scribed in 1982 [2] and seven additional patients have been described
[7,10–
14]. With the addition of the patient in this report, some clearer
conclusions can be drawn regarding the phenotype of this disorder.
HIBCHD appears to manifest primarily as a Leigh-like disease with pro-
gressive, neurometabolic degeneration characterised by hypotonia,
ataxia and episodes of encephalopathy. Imaging studies have shown
Leigh-like basal ganglia changes.
Two unusual amino acids, SCPC and SCPCM, were ﬁrst identiﬁed in
HIBCHD [2,3]. The diagnosis of HIBCHD in our patient relied on the de-
tection of increased urine SCPC. This metabolite is not usually measured
during routine amino acid analysis and can be easily overlooked with
ion exchange chromatography due to co-elution with more abundant
amino acids [7]. Indeed, routine urine amino acid analysis performed
at the age of 5 months did not reveal any signi ﬁcant abnormalities in
the HIBCHD patient. It was only with the later addition of SCPC to the
urine tandem mass spectrometry screening panel that we were able
to diagnose this patient at the age of 7 years. This diagnosis was sup-
ported by the ﬁnding of increased SCPCM (Table 1). We conducted fur-
ther targeted analyses to identify related metabolites. This led to the
discovery of increased urine levels of NAcSCPC, the carnitine ester of
SCPC, 23DH2MB and three metabolites related to acrylate, namely
SCEC, SCECM and NAcSCEC.
HIBCH also functions in a secondary pathway of propionate metabo-
lism [8]. This pathway contains acryloyl-CoA, a compound homologous
to methacrylyl-CoA. The methacrylyl and acryloyl moieties both contain
a highly reactive double bond that can react spontaneously with a vari-
ety of substrates, including sulphydryl groups. It was originally shown
that methacrylyl-CoA can react with cysteine and cysteamine to form
SCPC and SCPCM respectively [2]. However, the ﬁnding of increased
NAcSCPC, a mercapturic acid, suggests that glutathione metabolism
may be the origin of the sulphur-containing HIBCHD metabolites. Excre-
tion of mercapturic acids, produced via glutathione conjugation, is a
well-known mechanism for the elimination of many xenobiotics and
endogenous intermediates[15]. SCEC, SCECM and NAcSCEC are homol-
ogous metabolites produced from acryloyl-CoA. The same mercapturic
acids have been identiﬁed in the urine of rats treated with methyl acry-
late and methyl methacrylate [16]. The structural inter-relationships of
the methacrylyl-CoA metabolites are summarised in Supplementary
Fig. 2.
An additional feature in the HIBCHD patient was increased excretion
of 23DH2MB, detected by routine urine organic acid screening ( Fig. 3,
Table 1 ). 23DH2MB was ﬁrst identi ﬁed in trace amounts in normal
urine [17]. A metabolite with similar structure, 2-methyl-2,3-
butanediol, has also been detected in the urine of patients with propio-
nate disorders [18]. We studied the excretion of 23DH2MB, SCEC,
SCECM and NAcSCEC in subjects with propionic acidaemia and
methylmalonyl-CoA mutase de ﬁciency and found increased levels of
these metabolites, consistent with the involvement of the secondary
propionate pathway in their formation. Presumably this occurs as a re-
sult of increasedﬂux through this pathway when the normal propionate
pathway is blocked. In contrast, levels of SCPC, SCPCM and NAcSCPC,
derived from the other arm of the metabolic pathway, were normal
(Fig. 1 , Table 1 ). Moreover, levels of 23DH2MB were initially in-
creased but normalised in patients with cobalamin A and cobalamin
C disease after treatment with vitamin B12 with concomitant reduc-
tions in the propionate-related metabolites methylmalonate and
methycitrate. These observations and the occurrence of this meta-
bolite in patients with SCEHD are consistent with acryoly-CoA
metabolism being involved with the formation of 23DH2MB but
further studies are required to determine the exact mechanism of
its formation.
Increased urine levels of the carnitine ester of SCPC were also identi-
ﬁed in the HIBCHD patient. This is an unusual example of an amino acid-
carnitine conjugate. The mass spectral fragmentation pattern indicated
ester formation via the 2-carboxyl group rather than the cysteine car-
boxyl group, consistent with an initial reaction between methacrylyl-
CoA (or its glutathione conjugate) and carnitine (see Supplementary
Fig. 2). For this metabolite and SCPC, we observed a rough correlation
with 3-hydroxybutyrate levels ( Fig. 2). Increased circulating levels of
branched-chain amino acids, including valine, is a well-known compen-
satory mechanism during ketosis and the increased levels of SCPC and
its carnitine ester may reﬂect increased metabolic ﬂux through the va-
line pathway during ketosis. Alternatively, the increased levels may be

Mutations in the ECHS1 gene are another recently described cause
of Leigh-like disease [4,19,20] and accumulating metabolites in this
disorder indicate a defect in valine metabolism [4]. ECHS1 encodes
for short-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase (also known as crotonase)
which performs the preceding step in the pathway, namely the con-
version of methacrylyl-CoA to 3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA ( Fig. 1). We
previously showed that SCPC, SCPCM, SCEC, SCECM and 23DH2MB
accumulate in SCEHD. We now extend these ﬁndings to show that
NAcSCPC, the carnitine ester of SCPC and NAcSCEC are also increased
in SCEHD ( Table 1)a se x p e c t e df r o mt h ep a t h w a y(Fig. 1). These me-
tabolites are in common with HIBCHD but detection of increased 3-
hydroxyisobutyryl-carnitine provides a biochemical means of
distinguishing the two disorders (see Supplementary Fig. 3).
HIBCH and ECHS1 defects have clinical features that are sug-
gestive of a mitochondrial disorder or PDHC de ﬁciency and this
is often reinforced by increased plasma lactate and urine 3-
methylglutaconic acid. Confusingly, secondary de ﬁciencies of these
enzyme systems often occur in both disorders [4,7,10,11] . Indeed,
our HIBCHD patient had decreased PDHC activity in cultured skin ﬁ-
broblasts. It was only the later ﬁnding of increased urinary SCPC that
allowed the correct diagnosis to be made. We then showed that the
index HIBCHD patient [2] also had decreased PDHC activity in cul-
tured skin ﬁbroblasts. The reactive metabolites methacrylyl-CoA
and acryloyl-CoA that accumulate in HIBCH and ECHS1 defects can
potentially react with many sulfhydryl-containing biological mole-
cules, including cysteine residues in proteins and enzymes, and
therefore disrupt a wide range of biological systems. Our ﬁnding of
increased mercapturic acids derived from methacrylyl-CoA and
acryloyl-CoA is also relevant because it supports the hypothesis
that glutathione depletion is also involved in the pathogenesis of
these disorders [10].
Increased hydroxy-C4 carnitine in dried blood spots or plasma has
aided the diagnosis in some reported HIBCHD patients[7,10,11,13].R e t -
rospective review of our HIBCHD patient's newborn screening results
showed that the hydroxy-C4 carnitine level of 1.2μmol/L corresponded
to the 99.97 percentile of the newborn population which prompted a re-
vision of the cut-off to 1.0 μmol/L. However, we observed normal
hydroxy-C4 carnitine levels in one out of two dried blood spots
collected after the neonatal period. Furthermore, 3-hydroxybutyryl-
carnitine increases during ketosis and non-chromatographic tandem
mass spectrometry methods detect both the isobaric metabolites
3-hydroxyisobutyryl-carnitine and 3-hydroxybutyryl-carnitine as
hydroxy-C4 carnitine. Increased hydroxy-C4 carnitine is therefore not
speciﬁc for HIBCHD and the simultaneous measurement of the carnitine
ester of SCPC may be a more speci ﬁc and sensitive screening test that
will also detect SCEHD.
Early detection of HIBCH
and ECHS1 defects raises the question of
treatment. Carnitine and pantothenate have been suggested as pos-
sible treatments to enhance excretion of 3-hydroxyisobutyryl carni-
tine and ameliorate sequestration of coenzyme A in the form of 3-
hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA [7]. Another option would be cysteamine
treatment, effectively enhancing the natural mechanism for detoxi-
fying methacrylic and acrylic moieties. Cysteamine is routinely
used in the treatment of cystinosis in which its safety and ef ﬁcacy
are well established. Furthermore, cysteamine crosses the blood
brain barrier, an important attribute for treatment of neurodegener-
ative diseases such as HIBCHD and ECHS1 defects, and it has shown
positive effects in animal models of Huntington's disease and
Parkinson's disease [21]. Given the apparent role of glutathione in
the detoxi ﬁcation of accumulating metabolites, it is also worth con-
sidering treatment with N-acetylcysteine, a glutathione-sparing
drug that has shown promise in the treatment of neurological dis-
eases [22].
5. Conclusions
HIBCH and ECHS1 defects may be dif ﬁcult to recognise because
the associated biomarkers are not usually targeted during routine
metabolic testing. Furthermore, the phenotype, suggesting a mito-
chondrial energy de ﬁcit and the confounding issue of secondarily
decreased respiratory chain and/o r PDHC activities may cause mis-
diagnosis. Measurement of urine SCPC and its carnitine ester by
tandem mass spectrometry is effective for initial screening. Alter-
natively, detection of 23DH2MB by organic acid pro ﬁling may be
helpful in recognising these disorders.
