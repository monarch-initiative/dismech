# ZNFX1 Deficiency in a Child with Interstitial Pneumonitis and Peripheral Monocytosis.
**Authors:** Al-Saud B, KFSHRC Physicians & Researchers Consortium, Alshareef T, Al-Alwan M, Alazami AM
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-023-01529-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-023-01529-0)

## Content

The response and production of interferon (IFN) types I and III are crucial components of the immune system’s fight against pathogens such as viral organisms. In recent years, an increasing number of patients with susceptibility to specific viral infections were found to have genetic defects leading to type I IFN pathway deficiency. The association between TLR3 and IRF7 mutations and lethal SARS-CoV2 pneumonia is a topical example [1]. IFN type II (IFN-gamma (IFN-γ)) is an essential cytokine that plays a vital role in the control of infections caused by intracellular pathogens, in particular mycobacteria. Thus, genetic diseases of IFN-γ mediated immunity have been documented in patients with atypical mycobacteria. Recently, NFX1-type zinc finger-containing 1 (ZNFX1) mutations have been described in 28 patients from 17 families with a type I IFN inborn error of immunity [2–4]. These patients are reported to have recurrent viral and mycobacterial infections as well as monocytosis, thrombocytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) or HLH-like manifestations. Here, we describe the clinical, molecular, and immunological characteristics for a patient with a ZNFX1 mutation who presented with HLH-like disease and was found to have interstitial pneumonitis, peripheral monocytosis, renal disease, and B cell compartment abnormalities.

The case is a 2-year-old Saudi girl, a product of full-term spontaneous vaginal delivery with uneventful neonatal history. The patient was well until the age of 1 year, when she presented to the local hospital with erythematous skin rash in the upper and lower extremities and epistaxis. She was found to have abdominal distention and hepatosplenomegaly on examination. Her initial laboratory investigation showed low hemoglobin, thrombocytopenia, and monocytosis in peripheral blood. She received blood transfusion at the local hospital then transferred to our center, suspected of having an underlying hematological malignancy or HLH, for further work up and management. Her past medical history revealed that she was thriving well and had only twice presented with upper respiratory tract infection (at the ages of 2 and 10 months), which lasted for a few days and required no hospital admissions. She received all vaccinations up to 9 months of age, including live viral vaccines and the BCG vaccine as scheduled without complications.

Family history indicated that parents are first-degree cousins and have three other healthy children. There was no family history of early childhood deaths, immunodeficiency, hematological disorders, or autoimmune diseases. Patient developmental history was appropriate for her age.

HLH diagnosis was considered; however, she did not meet all the criteria: complete blood count showed low platelet counts at 71 × 109/L (reference range 200–550 × 109), ferritin 719 µg/L (reference range 13–150), triglyceride 3.79 mmol/L (normal < 1.7), and fibrinogen 4.46 g/L (reference range 1.7–3.38). Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy revealed no abnormalities. The immunology work-up was unremarkable except for elevated IgE (Table S1).

Three months later (at the age of 15 months), the child presented to the emergency room with runny nose, cough, and fever. She was found to be in respiratory distress but required no oxygen therapy. Her chest X-ray showed bilateral infiltration, and nasopharyngeal aspiration PCR came up positive for rhinoviruses (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, she underwent CT chest, which showed mediastinal lymphadenopathy and diffuse parenchymal lung disease (Fig. 1B, C). At the age of 2 years, she was admitted with respiratory symptoms and fever, and required oxygen by nasal cannula. Her chest X-ray showed bilateral peripheral interstitial infiltration, and nasopharyngeal aspiration PCR came up positive for respiratory syncytial virus. She improved over the 1-week admission with standard viral bronchiolitis management. During this admission, she manifested hypertension for which she underwent further evaluation. Her serum albumin was borderline low at 34 g/L. Her urinalysis revealed microscopic hematuria (3 +) and proteinuria (2 +). Her protein/creatinine ratio of random urine was elevated at 200 mg/mmol, and albumin/creatinine ratio was 126 mg/mmol. Her renal ultrasounds showed bilateral nephromegaly with mild increased parenchymal echogenicity of both kidneys. There was no evidence of kidney failure given her normal for age serum creatinine. Her peripheral blood smears showed less than 1% schistocytes. Direct Coombs test were negative. The lactate dehydrogenase was 551 units/L, and haptoglobin was 4.9 g/L (reference range 0.3–2). These findings were suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy/hemolytic uremic syndrome and membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), but no kidney biopsy was performed.

Whole exome sequencing revealed what was at that time a novel mutation in the ZNFX1 gene (NM_021035.2:c.3292_3302del:p.L1098Qfs*3), an 11-bp deletion which predicted a prematurely truncated protein. Since then, this patient has been briefly described as part of a cohort in another report [4]. The mutation itself appears to be a founder mutation present in the local population, based on shared autozygosity between apparently unrelated cases [4]. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease state, as the patient was homozygous for the frameshift mutation while both parents were carriers. In addition, RNA from patient blood was compared against three healthy controls and showed greatly increased expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β, in line with her clinical phenotype (Fig. 1D). There was also significantly higher expression of ZNFX1, suggesting that the transcripts were not under surveillance by the nonsense-mediated decay pathway.

To search for increased IL-1β protein, neutrophils were isolated from whole blood then stimulated with a combination of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Elevated IL-1β was most clearly observed using the patient’s unstimulated neutrophils, at levels exceeding what we detected in three healthy controls, while in the presence of LPS with ATP, the readings trended towards the upper range of our controls (Fig. 1E).

To determine the immunological effects of the mutation on the patient, peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patient and controls were stained with a panel of fluorescent antibodies for T and B cell compartmental subtypes, as well as NK and regulatory T cells (Fig. S1). Most percentages appeared within normal limits, though there was an increase in the proportion of naïve (CD19+ CD27− IgD+), and a decrease in the percentage of memory (CD19+ CD27+ IgD−) B cells, as compared with our cohort of healthy pediatric controls.

Inborn errors in the interferon pathways are a group of genetically inherited diseases that lead to variable recurrent and chronic infections. Multiple interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) have been described. ZNFX1 is an ISG double-stranded RNA sensor that restricts the replication of RNA viruses in mice [5]. In humans, impaired viral clearance and deregulation for ISGs have been shown in patients with biallelic defects in this gene [3]. Additionally, human ZNFX1 is recruited in stress granules, and is essential for monocyte homeostasis and in immunity against mycobacteria infections [2].

The clinical phenotype for ZNFX1-deficient patients is variable [2–4]. This patient’s clinical phenotype was consistent with previous reports and includes recurrent viral illness, monocytosis, thrombocytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, nephromegaly, interstitial pneumonitis, and suspected HLH. In one report, recurrent viral infections leading to severe respiratory viral illness was the predominant clinical phenotype in a cohort of 15 patients [3]. This patient had recurrent chest infections presumably related to viruses, evident by a positive nasopharyngeal aspiration PCR for rhinovirus and respiratory syncytial virus on two different occasions, and a chest CT showing diffuse airspace opacity infiltration. These findings are suggestive for viral interstitial pneumonitis (Fig. 1B). Interstitial pneumonitis has been reported previously in patient with multisystem inflammation due to ZNFX1 deficiency [3]. This patient did not undergo a lung biopsy to confirm interstitial pneumonitis, as she had no progression in her lung clinical condition and responded to the standard bronchiolitis management.

In previous reports, kidney involvement was very common. It was reported as kidney failure in multi-organ failure contest, thrombotic microangiopathy/hemolytic uremic syndrome, nephrotic syndrome, and membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN) [3]. We believe that this variability might be due to a common pathophysiological process, as these phenotypes are descriptive findings to much more complex immunological reactions. This patient has nephrotic range proteinuria, generalized edema, borderline low serum albumin, hematuria, and hypertension especially during her acute illnesses, which resolved partially when she recovered. This mixed picture of nephrotic/nephritic presentation may represent a common immunological disorder that does not respond well to immunosuppression treatment. We propose that recurrent infections and re-activation of this abnormal immune response render the kidney involvement more dominant and obvious, when it might be too late to intervene.

A previous report had phenotyped patient CD4+ and CD8+ T cell compartments and found the averages to be largely comparable to what was observed with age-matched controls [2]. B cell compartments however were not assessed. For this patient, we analyzed both B and T cell compartments, and noted that the percentage of naïve B cells was higher than any of our pediatric controls. Conversely, the percentage of memory B cells was extremely low at 2.3%. Plasmablasts were within range but at the low end of our controls. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documentation of abnormal naïve and memory B cell counts for ZNFX1 deficiency. Additional data will need to be generated from other ZNFX1 patients to clarify whether this phenomenon is unique to this patient or a consistent attribute of this disorder.
