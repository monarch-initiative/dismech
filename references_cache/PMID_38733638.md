# Liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-phosphate therapy improves global N-glycosylation in different congenital disorders of glycosylation.
**Authors:** Budhraja R, Radenkovic S, Jain A, Muffels IJJ, Ismaili MHA, Kozicz T, Pandey A, Morava E
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgme.2024.108487](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgme.2024.108487)

## Content

Phosphomannomutase 2 (PMM2) converts mannose-6-phospahate to mannose-1-phosphate; the substrate for GDP-mannose, a building block of the glycosylation biosynthetic pathway. Pathogenic variants in the PMM2 gene have been shown to be associated with protein hypoglycosylation causing PMM2-congenital disorder of glycosylation (PMM2-CDG). While mannose supplementation improves glycosylation in vitro, but not in vivo, we hypothesized that liposomal delivery of mannose-1-phosphate could increase the stability and delivery of the activated sugar to enter the targeted compartments of cells. Thus, we studied the effect of liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-P (GLM101) on global protein glycosylation and on the cellular proteome in skin fibroblasts from individuals with PMM2-CDG, as well as in individuals with two N-glycosylation defects early in the pathway, namely ALG2-CDG and ALG11-CDG. We leveraged multiplexed proteomics and N-glycoproteomics in fibroblasts derived from different individuals with various pathogenic variants in PMM2, ALG2 and ALG11 genes. Proteomics data revealed a moderate but significant change in the abundance of some of the proteins in all CDG fibroblasts upon GLM101 treatment. On the other hand, N-glycoproteomics revealed the GLM101 treatment enhanced the expression levels of several high-mannose and complex/hybrid glycopeptides from numerous cellular proteins in individuals with defects in PMM2 and ALG2 genes. Both PMM2-CDG and ALG2-CDG exhibited several-fold increase in glycopeptides bearing Man6 and higher glycans and a decrease in Man5 and smaller glycan moieties, suggesting that GLM101 helps in the formation of mature glycoforms. These changes in protein glycosylation were observed in all individuals irrespective of their genetic variants. ALG11-CDG fibroblasts also showed increase in high mannose glycopeptides upon treatment; however, the improvement was not as dramatic as the other two CDG. Overall, our findings suggest that treatment with GLM101 overcomes the genetic block in the glycosylation pathway and can be used as a potential therapy for CDG with enzymatic defects in early steps in protein N-glycosylation.

Congenital disorders of glycosylation (CDG) are rare genetic disorders that are caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in glycosylation. They can be categorized according to the type of glycosylation that is primarily affected; N-glycosylation, O-glycosylation, combined defects or glycosphingolipid and glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor synthesis defects. There are over 180 distinct CDG types, with N-glycosylation defects being the most prevalent. phosphomannomutase-2 (PMM2)-CDG, a common N-glycosylation defect, typically manifests as a severe multiorgan phenotype with cerebellar ataxia, central hypotonia, proximal muscle weakness, spasticity, and peripheral neuropathy. Liver, cardiac, and endocrinological involvement are also frequently observed [1–3]. PMM2-CDG is caused by pathogenic variants in PMM2 and result in deficiency of mannose-1-P and its downstream metabolite GDP-mannose. GDP-mannose is an important sugar donor for N-glycosylation in endoplasmic reticulum, where it is used in the formation and elongation of N-glycans. In addition, other enzymes involved in elongation of glycan chain use GDP-mannose as a substrate. Pathogenic variants in these genes can also lead to a CDG. For example, ALG2 gene, which is an alpha-1,3/1,6-mannosyltransferase that transfers second and third mannose units from GDP-mannose to Man1GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol in N-glycosylation. Pathogenic ALG2 variants lead to a severe multi-organ disease characterized by epilepsy, developmental delay, hypsarrhythmia, hepatomegaly, coagulation abnormalities, and ophthalmological manifestations [4–6]. Another gene ALG11 is an alpha-1,2-mannosyltransferase that transfer the fourth and fifth mannose units from GDP-mannose to Man3GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol resulting in the generation of Man5GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol. ALG11-CDG presents with intellectual disability, seizures, strabismus and failure to thrive [7]. Despite being severe, progressive, and at times fatal diseases, most CDG have limited treatment options available.

Currently available treatment options for CDG are mostly symptomatic. Dietary supplementation, including the administration of monosaccharides, is a widely applied therapy for CDG [8]. An example of effective sugar therapy in CDG involves galactose supplementation, which demonstrates favorable outcomes for PGM1-CDG and promising results for SLC35A2-CDG, SLC39A8-CDG, and TMEM165-CDG, although these were only assessed in small patient cohorts [9–15]. In addition, mannose supplementation represents one of the oldest monosaccharide treatments for mannose-6-phosphate isomerase (MPI)-CDG [16]. Despite promising results of mannose treatment in preclinical studies for various CDG types beyond MPI-CDG [17–19], conflicting evidence has emerged from clinical trials that suggests no discernible impact of mannose treatment in individuals beyond MPI-CDG [20–22].

Given that PMM2 catalyzes the conversion of mannose-6-P to mannose-1-P, administering mannose-1-P to PMM2-deficient individuals presents an opportunity to bypass the defective enzyme. Indeed, the addition of mannose-1-P leads to normalization of glycosylation patterns in fibroblasts from individuals with PMM2-CDG, demonstrating its therapeutic efficacy [24]. Interestingly, fibroblasts derived from individuals with other types of CDG, beyond PMM2-CDG similarly benefit from mannose-1-P treatment, although the mechanism of action is expected to be different [24]. The effectiveness of mannose-1-P treatment is believed to rely on elevating intracellular GDP-mannose concentrations, facilitating the normalization of lipid linked oligosaccharides (LLO) structures, particularly in instances where the defective enzymes retain residual activity. Notably, hydrophobic mannose-1-P treatment is beneficial in fibroblasts derived from individuals with defects in enzymes involved in the earliest steps of N-glycosylation, such as DPM1-CDG, with limited effects on protein defects further downstream, such as ALG3-CDG or ALG12-CDG [24]. Defects in early steps of N-glycosylation biosynthesis pathway probably benefit more from mannose-1-P treatment, since the heightened GDP-mannose concentrations can be utilized directly by these enzymes.

Until now, the unstable nature mannose-1-P in blood plasma has limited its clinical applicability. As an alternative, liposome encapsuled mannose-1-P has been developed, which could deliver mannose-1-P directly to cells making it directly applicable for clinical use. Currently, liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-P has been evaluated in individuals with PMM2-CDG in Phase 2 clinical trials (NCT05549219). In PMM2-deficient fibroblasts, liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-P has been shown to normalize intracellular GDP-mannose concentrations (Shirakura et al., submitted). This suggests a potential broader applicability, wherein the increased GDP-mannose concentrations may extend benefits to other types of CDG, especially for glycosylation defects early in the N-glycosylation pathway. However, the effect of liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-P on N-glycosylation in fibroblasts of PMM2-CDG and other types of CDG remains to be studied.

Here, we hypothesized that liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-P (GLM101) could enhance protein glycosylation in fibroblasts derived from individuals with PMM2-CDG as well as in individuals with two early-stage N-glycosylation defects, namely ALG2-CDG and ALG11-CDG. Our primary goal was to investigate the efficacy of GLM101 in improving the glycoproteome of individuals with CDG, particularly those expected to experience the most significant therapeutic benefits. Additionally, we aimed to gain insights into protein abundances and site-specific glycosylation patterns upon the treatment.

Deidentified residual samples were used for proteomics and glycoproteomics studies from CDG individuals participating in the CDG natural history study (institutional review board [IRB]: 19–005187; NCT04199000). Fibroblasts from all CDG individuals were collected as part of clinical care. Natural history data was collected after informed consent after institutional approval (IRB: 16–004682). Prospective data, as well as retrospective data, were available for all CDG individuals as part of standard care at the study site.

We obtained fibroblasts from three individuals with PMM2-CDG, presenting with developmental delay, hypotonia, speech delay, ataxia and variable associated features of visual and hearing loss, autistic features and antithrombin deficiency. In addition, we obtained fibroblasts from three individuals with ALG2-CDG, who had intellectual disability and variable features of hearing and visual loss and speech delay, were evaluated. Finally, we included the fibroblasts of two individuals with ALG11-CDG, who had features of mild to severe developmental delay, speech delay, infantile seizures and progressive microcephaly (Table 1).

All fibroblasts were maintained at subconfluent densities in RPMI 1640 media (ThermoFisher) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic and 10% non-essential amino acids maintained in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Routine mycoplasma testing was performed. The cells were plated at 90–95% confluency, in T75 flasks in 11 mM glucose RPMI 1640 media (ThermoFisher) with 10% FBS, 1% AA. After 24h, the medium was removed and the cells were washed with DPBS. Then, the prepared flasks were treated with no glucose RPMI 1640 medium (ThermoFisher) supplemented with 0.5mM glucose, 10% dialyzed FBS, 1% AA and either with or without 1mM GLM101. The cells were incubated for 24 h. Cells were harvested by scraping in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 2000 rpm at 4°C for 10 min. Harvested cells were lysed as described previously. Briefly, lysis buffer containing 8 M urea (in 100 mM TEAB buffer) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Scientific) was added to cell pellet. Subsequently, the cells underwent sonication using a tip sonicator set at 30% amplitude for 3 cycles of 10 seconds each. The protein levels within these collected cells were quantified through a BCA assay following the manufacturer’s guidelines (Thermo Scientific).

Equal amounts of protein lysates from all samples were first reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37°C followed by alkylated with 40 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at room temperature in the dark. Following this, the proteins were digested with trypsin (Worthington, USA) at a ratio of 1:20 w/w (protein: trypsin) and left overnight at 37°C with gentle shaking on a thermomixer. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 tips (Glygen, USA) and labeled using tandem mass tags (TMT) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

First, the TMT labeling efficiency was checked in labeled peptides and upon normalization, the samples were pooled. Pooled peptides were subsequently divided into two portions. One portion, comprising approximately 20% of the total peptides, was reconstituted in solvent A (consisting of 5 mM ammonium formate at pH 9) and subjected to fractionation using basic pH reversed-phase liquid chromatography (bRPLC) on a C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 100 mm column, Waters). This separation utilized a linear gradient of solvent B (5 mM ammonium formate at pH 9, in 90% acetonitrile) for 120 minutes on the Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system. total of ninety-six fractions were collected and subsequently concatenated into 12 fractions for the proteomics study. These concatenated 12 fractions were lyophilized and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. The remaining 80% of peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid and introduced into the Superdex peptide 10/300 column (GE Healthcare) as described previously [25]. The peptides were separated by employing an isocratic flow of 0.1% formic acid for 130 min and 24 early fractions were collected beginning 10 minutes post-injection. These fractions were subsequently combined into 12 fractions for glycoproteomics study. The fractions were lyophilized and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid for subsequent analysis via LC-MS/MS.

The LC-MS/MS analysis of fractionated samples and enriched glycopeptides followed previously established protocols with some adjustments [26–29]. Briefly, the samples were analyzed on an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to Ultimate 3000 liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Peptides/glycopeptides were first trapped on a trap column (100 mm × 2 cm, Acclaim PepMap100 Nano-Trap, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 20 μl/min using 0.1% formic acid. The peptides were subsequently separated on an analytical column (EasySpray 75 μm × 50 cm, C18 2 μm, 100 Å, (Thermo Scientific, USA) with a flow rate of 300 nl/min for 150 min using a linear gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). All experiments were conducted in DDA mode with an isolation window set at 0.7 m/z. Precursor ions were acquired at a resolution of 120,000 (at m/z 200), and fragment ions were obtained at a resolution of 30,000 (at m/z 200). Data acquisition included the use of the “lock mass” option (m/z 441.12002) for all data. The scans were acquired in top-speed method with 3 s cycle time between MS and MS/MS. Precursor fragmentation was carried out using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) method using normalized collision energy (NCE) of 34% for proteomics or stepped HCD at 15, 25, 40% for glycoproteomics. The stepped HCD provides information about both glycan and peptide moieties.

Data analysis was performed as described previously with slight modifications [26–29]. For the proteomics data, the Sequest search engine within Proteome Discoverer 2.5 was used to search against the human Uniprot protein database. For the glycoproteomics data, the publicly available software pGlyco3 [30] was utilized, incorporating an internal N-glycan database for glycans and an added human UniProt protein database for peptide sequence identification. In both proteomics and glycoproteomics analyses, two missed cleavages were allowed. In proteomics, the error tolerance was set at 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively, for precursor and fragment ions. In glycoproteomics, it was set at 10 ppm and 20 ppm, respectively, for precursor and fragment ions. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine as variable modification. The false discovery rate (FDR) was set to 1% at the peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs), peptides, proteins and glycopeptides level. In proteomics, quantitation of peptides across treated and untreated fibroblasts was done using TMT reporter ion intensities using “reporter ion quantifier” node. To quantify glycopeptides, reporter ion quantification was performed for glycoproteomics raw files within Proteome Discoverer and glycopeptide IDs obtained from pGlyco were aligned with quantitation on a scan-to-scan basis (MS/MS). All data are expressed as mean ±SD. Statistical analysis was performed using the publicly available computational platforms Perseus, MetaboAnalyst and R studio. Biological processes analysis of the significantly increased glycoproteins was performed using the DAVID web server [31]. Two sample t tests were used to compare two groups. All dot plots were made using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.

In this study, we used a deep multiplexed N-glycoproteomics approach to analyze the effect of liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-phosphate (GLM101) on fibroblasts from different CDG individuals with a defect in genes that catalyze the early steps in N-glycosylation biosynthesis (Figure 1A). We included three individuals with PMM2-CDG with three different genotypes (all compound heterozygous), three individuals with ALG2-CDG with three different genotypes (all homozygous) and two ALG11-CDG individuals with two different genotypes (both homozygous). Following with or without GLM101 treatment, cells were lysed, proteins were extracted and subjected to in-solution proteolytic digestion. The resulting peptides from each sample were then labeled with tandem mass tags (TMT). One portion of pooled labeled peptides was fractionated using bRPLC into 12 fractions for proteomics. The larger portion of pooled labeled peptides was subjected to SEC for the enrichment of glycopeptides because the glycopeptides are larger than non-glycosylated peptides. These fractions were subsequently analyzed by LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer. A schematic of the optimized workflow for multiplexed N-glycoproteomics is shown in Figure 1B. Multiplexed proteomics study of fibroblasts identified a total of 7,725 proteins with 126,076 peptides. The N-glycoproteomics study identified and quantified 4,339 individual N-glycopeptides in all 3 cell types. These peptides encompassed 300 distinct N-glycan compositions and were located on 994 glycosylation sites associated with 573 glycoproteins. Among the identified 4,339 glycopeptides, 1,058 contained high mannose glycan moieties and 3,281 contained complex/hybrid glycan moieties.

In our multiplexed proteomics study, we identified several proteins with marginal changes in their abundance in individuals with PMM2-CDG with and without GLM101 (Figure 2). The volcano plot shows the distribution of protein abundance, which was generated by calculating the average abundance of all treated individuals versus untreated individuals, demonstrating that treated individuals exhibit the distinct abundance of several proteins (Figure 2A). The most highly overexpressed protein was 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), which was increased by 3.6-fold in PMM2-CDG fibroblasts upon GLM101 treatment. Other proteins which were increased upon treatment by more than 2-fold included beta-secretase 1 (BACE1), semaphorin-7A (SEMA7A), protein APCDD1-like (APCDD1L), aflatoxin B1 aldehyde reductase member 3 (AKR7A3), squalene synthase (FDFT1) and low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR). Conversely, the top significantly downregulated proteins included translocon-associated protein subunit beta (SSR2), semaphorin-3D (SEMA3D), complement C1s subcomponent (C1S) and DnaJ homolog subfamily B member 9 (DNAJB9). The expression pattern of several of the most upregulated and downregulated proteins with more than 2-fold change in PMM2-CDG across both group of samples are shown in a heatmap (Figure 2B).

In our recent investigations, we have consistently observed a substantial decrease in protein glycosylation levels in PMM2 deficient fibroblasts [26, 32] and recently in 3D human cortical organoids derived from PMM2-CDG individuals [33]. In the current study, a substantial increased N-glycosylation pattern was observed in GLM101 treated PMM2-CDG fibroblasts, relative to untreated cells. The overall increase in high mannose and complex/hybrid glycopeptides is evident from the volcano plot shown in Figure 3A and 3B, respectively. This increase was observed in all three individuals for various glycan structures on various glycosylation sites derived from multiple glycoproteins. Among the 4,339 identified glycopeptides, significant glycosylation change (p < 0.05) was observed in 719 unique N-glycopeptides derived from 246 distinct glycoproteins. Among the significantly changing 719 glycopeptides, 348 glycopeptides derived from 153 glycoproteins contained a different degree of high mannose glycan structures (Man3 to Man9). Interestingly, 333 high mannose bearing glycopeptides were significantly upregulated and only 15 glycopeptides were significantly downregulated upon GLM101 treatment. Remarkably, a total of 281 high mannose glycopeptides were increased with more than 1.5-fold change. We also observed the increase in complex/hybrid glycopeptides containing either fucosylation or sialylation or both in PMM2-CDG after GLM101 treatment. Among the 719 glycopeptides, we observed the significantly change (p < 0.05) in 320 complex/hybrid glycopeptides derived from 137 glycoproteins. Of these, 306 glycopeptides were significantly upregulated upon treatment where a total of 162 complex/hybrid glycopeptides were increased with more than 1.5-fold change. We observed increased abundance of different glycopeptides that encompass a variety of glycan structures in all three PMM2-CDG individuals indicating that GLM101 treatment results to a broader glycosylation improvement in the cells independent of their genotype (Figure 3C).

Since the scarcity of mannose-1-P leads to reduced glycosylation, especially high mannose glycans, we specifically assessed the abundance of different high mannose glycan-bearing glycopeptides in PMM2 deficient fibroblasts upon GLM101 treatment. We observed that among the significantly increased 281 high mannose glycopeptides (>1.5-fold change), 277 glycopeptides encompassed Man6 or higher glycan compositions. On the other hand, among the downregulated 15 glycopeptides, 14 glycopeptides encompassed Man5 or lower glycan compositions. Which explain that the Mannsoe-1-P treatment largely improved the abundance of higher mannose units containing glycopeptides. A differential chord diagram is drawn to quantitatively visualize glycosylation differences in mannose-1-P treated as compared to untreated PMM2-CDG fibroblasts with different degree of high mannose (Man3 to Man9) on distinct glycosylation sites of various cellular proteins (Figure 3D).

Among the most profoundly affected glycoproteins exhibiting a global increase in glycosylation were low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1; 34 glycopeptides from 11 glycosylation sites), protein-lysine 6-oxidase (LYOX; 18 glycopeptides with from a single site), laminin subunit gamma-1 (LAMC1; 16 glycopeptides from 7 sites), lysosome-associated membrane glycoprotein 2 (LAMP2; 12 glycopeptides from 4 sites), hypoxia up-regulated protein 1 (HYOU1; 12 glycopeptides from 5 sites) and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (IGFBP3; 5 glycopeptides from a single site). Additionally, other increased glycopeptides were derived from CD109, lysosome-associated membrane glycoprotein 1 (LAMP1), thrombospondin-1 (TSP1), endoplasmin (ENPL) and fibronectin (FINC). The hypoglycosylation of majority of these proteins has already been observed in PMM2-CDG fibroblasts [34, 35]. We next performed Gene Ontology analysis of significantly increased glycoproteins to identify functionally relevant biological processes dysregulated upon GLM101 treatment. Cell adhesion, cell migration, angiogenesis and extracellular matrix organization were enriched upon GLM101 treatment in PMM2-CDG (Supplementary figure S2A). Relative abundance of some of the significantly changing glycopeptides bearing different form of glycan structures at the specific sites on some proteins is shown in Figure 3E, where smaller glycans are downregulated and mature forms of glycans are upregulated after treatment. Proteomics data demonstrated a marginal change in the protein abundance in ALG2-CDG fibroblasts upon GLM101 treatment (Supplementary figure S1A). Similar to our findings in the case of PMM2-CDG fibroblasts, the most upregulated protein in GLM101-treated ALG2-CDG cells was 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), which was increased 3.4-fold upon treatment. Other abundant proteins with >2-fold increase was acyl-CoA 6-desaturase (FADS2), squalene synthase (FDFT1), squalene monooxygenase (SQLE) and low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR). Between PMM2-CDG and ALG2-CDG, 18 of these proteins overlapped (Supplementary figure 3B). These overlapped proteins included HMGCR, BACE1, AKR7A3, FDFT1, LDLR and SQLE.

In this study, our primary objective was to comprehensively analyze the effect of liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-phosphate (GLM101) on global proteome and N-glycoproteome within fibroblasts derived from individuals with PMM2-CDG. Additionally, we tested treatment effects in fibroblasts of individuals harboring early N-glycosylation defects (ALG2-CDG and ALG11-CDG). This investigation employed a multiplexed mass spectrometry approach and encompassed a range of biallelic pathogenic variants in PMM2, ALG2 and ALG11. These CDG individuals were confirmed by whole exome sequencing and carbohydrate-deficient transferrin assay.

We first conducted proteomics analysis with the hypothesis that GLM101 might exert an influence on the abundance of various cellular proteins. Intriguingly, while the proteomics data did not exhibit significant alterations in the overall cellular proteome, it did unveil dysregulated abundance in several critical cellular proteins. Notably, among the findings, we observed consistent upregulation of certain proteins across all CDG samples, regardless of the specific gene defects. These proteins include 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), beta-secretase 1 (BACE1), and low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR). HMGCR is known to be involved in biosynthesis of cholesterol and lipid [36] and LDLR functions as a carrier of cholesterol [37]. BACE1 is responsible for the proteolytic processing of the amyloid precursor protein [38]. The increased abundance of these proteins following treatment may be attributed to the presence of liposomes in GLM101. The impact of liposomes on cholesterol metabolism in cell cultures has previously been demonstrated [39]. Interestingly, the abundance levels of commonly utilized glycosylation biomarkers, such as lysosome-associated membrane glycoprotein 2 (LAMP2) and intracellular cell adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1), remained largely unchanged following treatment.

We and others have previously reported the reduced glycosylation in cells and plasma samples of PMM2-CDG, ALG2-CDG and ALG11-CDG individuals using different mass spectrometry-based glycoproteomic and glycomic strategies [6, 26, 32, 40–42]. These studies have indicated that individuals with CDG often present with elevated levels of truncated and immature forms of glycans, alongside a notable decrease in mature glycan structures. Intriguingly, our N-glycoproteomic profiling of fibroblasts from PMM2-CDG, ALG2-CDG, and ALG11-CDG individuals upon GLM101 treatment unveiled significant improvements in glycosylation at specific sites. Many of the identified glycopeptides derived from various cellular proteins exhibited heightened levels following treatment, predominantly comprised of diverse mature glycan forms. Conversely, the diminished glycan forms observed at these sites prior to treatment encompassed smaller and truncated variants, suggesting that GLM101 enhances the efficiency of the glycosylation process. Notably, proteins such as LRP1, LAMP1, LAMP2, and LAMC1, previously reported to be hypoglycosylated in PMM2-CDG, displayed improved glycosylation upon GLM101 treatment. In a pilot study, we performed a quantitative N-glycoproteomics study, where we administered mannose-1-P loaded liposomes (GLM101) or empty liposome bodies (lacking the mannose-1-P) to PMM2-CDG fibroblasts. We observed increased abundance of several glycopeptides after GLM101 treatment implying mannose-1-P is the true cause of the enhanced glycosylation and not the liposome bodies (data available upon request).

We observed a marked increase in mature high mannose, hybrid and complex type glycan bearing glycopeptides in both PMM2-CDG and ALG2-CDG, which suggest that glycosylation can be restored in the presence of sufficient amount of mannose-1-P. Although individuals with ALG11-CDG did not show a significant increase in complex or hybrid glycans, high mannose bearing glycopeptides did show a marginal improvement, suggesting that GLM101 could be beneficial for individuals with defects in ALG11 gene. The improved site-specific glycosylation in individuals with defects in ALG2 and ALG11 genes might be due to the presence of excessive substrate (mannose-1-P) for these steps which can drive these reactions through the gene products’ residual activity.

It is important to underscore that our findings in fibroblasts from CDG individuals might not directly translate to the clinical in vivo use of GLM101. While our studies suggest potential benefits in glycosylation for tissues capable of drug uptake, our approach can only target “visceral/peripheral” tissues. GLM101 has not been demonstrated to cross the blood-brain barrier; therefore, any potential improvement in glycosylation should primarily affect secretory glycosylation abnormalities when the drug is delivered intravenously. Moreover, certain glycoforms and small glycopeptides may be able to traverse the blood-brain barrier and improve some aspects of central nervous system symptoms, similar to observations in individuals with PMM2-CDG following liver transplantation [43]. However, this aspect requires further investigation, particularly in light of the ongoing clinical trials in adolescents and adults with PMM2-CDG.

Our findings demonstrate a significant improvement in protein glycosylation at multiple glycosylation sites in fibroblasts derived from various CDG upon treatment with liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-phosphate. We have provided evidence that cell treatment with liposome-encapsulated mannose-1-phosphate can overcome PMM2 enzyme deficiency, thereby enhancing glycosylation, potentially by serving as a precursor for guanosine diphosphate (GDP)-mannose pyrophosphorylase. Additionally, the GLM101 treatment leads to a significant increase in glycosylation in ALG2-CDG and a mild increase in ALG11-CDG, suggesting its potential utility in treating these CDG.
