# Bi-allelic loss of function variant in the NRCAM gene is associated with motor-predominant axonal polyneuropathy; the second report.
**Authors:** Elahi Z, Soveyzi M, Nafissi S, Nilipour Y, Goleyjani Moghadam M, Keshavarz E, Kariminejad A, Najmabadi H, Fattahi Z
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.2131](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.2131)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The role of biallelic variants in the NRCAM gene underlying a neurodevelopmental disorder has been defined recently. The phenotype is mainly recognized by varying severity of global developmental delay/intellectual disability, hypotonia, spasticity, and peripheral neuropathy.

METHODS: Here, we describe a patient with an initial diagnosis of motor-predominant axonal polyneuropathy or a form of distal SMA. Whole-exome sequencing (WES), in parallel with WES-based CNV detection and assessment of homozygosity runs, was performed to identify this patient's possible genetic cause.

RESULTS: Whole exome sequencing revealed a homozygous variant, c.73C > T (p.Gln25*), in the NRCAM gene, while the patient manifests a mild range of phenotypes compared to NRCAM-related disorder. He presented only motor-predominant axonal polyneuropathy with no other signs of central nervous system involvement.

CONCLUSIONS: This study is the second report of an association between biallelic NRCAM gene variants and a Mendelian disorder. The obtained clinical data, together with the molecular findings in this patient, expands the clinical and molecular spectrum of NRCAM-related disorder and highlights its phenotypic complexity. Although patients with loss of function variants in this gene have previously presented severe clinical features, we show that type of the pathogenic variant does not necessarily determine the severity of this phenotype.

Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) are transmembrane proteins that mediate the binding of neighboring cells or their interaction with the extracellular matrix, preserving a critical unit for signaling. NRCAM, along with L1CAM, CHL1, and NFASC, belongs to the L1 subgroup of immunoglobin (Ig)‐CAMs that are highly expressed in the central nervous system. These proteins play essential roles in neural development by contributing to myelination, differentiation, proliferation, and migration, as well as synapsis formation, axonal growth, and guidance (Kurolap et al., 2022; Sakurai, 2012). The neuronal cell adhesion molecule, NRCAM, has overlapping functions with other L1‐IgCAMs. This protein contains an N‐terminal signal peptide, six immunoglobin (Ig)‐like domains, five fibronectin III repeats, a transmembrane domain, and a C‐terminal cytoplasmic domain (Liang et al., 2020). The Ig‐like and Fn‐III domains at the extracellular region play critical roles in mediating the interaction between cells and adhesion signaling (Kurolap et al., 2022).

Different studies suggest the NRACM role in the brain and peripheral nervous system. The Nrcam‐mutant mice showed a defect in neurite activity in cerebellar granule cells due to failed neurite outgrowth (Sakurai et al., 2001). Further studies have documented its role in the formation of synapses, the interaction of Schwann cells and axons, and the formation and maintenance of Ranvier nodes on myelinated axons, confirmed with the observation of the delayed or abnormal formation of Ranvier nodes in the peripheral nervous system of NRCAM‐double mutant mice (Amor et al., 2014; Sakurai, 2012). This protein might also be essential for the adult nervous system as its absence defects the remyelination process (Douglas et al., 2009). Furthermore, a synergistic effect with other genes (Lpin1 and Scn8a) is proposed for NRCAM, leading to a more severe phenotype in mouse models of peripheral neuropathy (Douglas et al., 2009; Morelli et al., 2017).

Visual activity can also be affected due to NRCAM deficiency, observed as poor visual acuity and cataracts in mice. In addition, hearing defects due to cochlear afferent and efferent fiber abnormalities were detected in the developmental stages of Nrcam‐mutant mice but were recovered after hearing onset (Demyanenko et al., 2011; Harley et al., 2018). In keeping with the findings from mouse models, Nrcam‐mutant zebrafish also revealed moderate alteration in white matter tracts and axonal projections and abnormally increased swimming behavior and movement in the darkness, implying an enhanced predatory response or exploratory boldness (Kurolap et al., 2022).

Previous research has linked NRCAM variants to neuropsychiatric disorders such as autism, schizophrenia, drug addiction, and also to tumor progression (Liang et al., 2020; Sakurai, 2012). Recently, NRCAM bi‐allelic variants have been introduced to cause a neurodevelopmental syndrome of varying severity, mainly represented by developmental delay, intellectual disability, hypotonia, peripheral neuropathy, and/or spasticity (#MIM:619833) (Kurolap et al., 2022). To our knowledge, no other affected patients carrying NRCAM mutations have been reported later. Considering the variable severity of symptoms in the first report, the detection of additional patients can contribute to a better genotype–phenotype delineation of the disease. In this regard, the present study reports a patient presenting motor‐predominant axonal polyneuropathy without any other developmental symptoms who carried a homozygous loss of function (LOF) variant in the NRCAM gene.

The investigated patient is a 19‐year‐old male referred to our diagnostic laboratory for whole‐exome sequencing who was diagnosed with motor‐predominant axonal polyneuropathy or a form of distal SMA at 13 years old. A complete clinical history was collected under the supervision of an expert neurologist and geneticist.

According to the standard salting‐out method, genomic DNA was isolated from the proband and his healthy parents' peripheral blood samples. The proband's DNA underwent exome enrichment using Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 (Agilent Technologies Inc, Santa Clara, CA, USA) enrichment kit. Then the captured library was sequenced on the HiSeq Illumina sequencer machine (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The mean depth of coverage for the exons of the human genome based on CCDS Release 22 was 75.68X, with 97% and 94.9% coverage at 10x and 20x, respectively.

The obtained reads were aligned to the hg19 assembly (GRCh37) of the human reference genome using Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner (0.7.17‐r1188) (Li & Durbin, 2009) and further processed adhering to the best practices of GATK pipeline (V4.1.4.1) (Van der Auwera et al., 2013). The generated VCF file was annotated using Annovar (Wang et al., 2010) and analyzed by the standard filtering pipeline, including filtration of the variants based on minor allele frequency (MAF >0.01), followed by prioritization based on the bioinformatics predictions and gene functions. The final rare variants were validated by Sanger sequencing, and co‐segregation studies were performed using the available DNA samples.

In parallel, the cohort‐mode GATK germline CNV detection pipeline (V4.1.4.1) was used to detect rare and common copy number variations (CNVs) based on sequencing depth of the patients' WES data compared to a cohort model of 333 comparable WES samples. GermlineCNVCaller algorithm was used to detect the CNVs; we then imported the generated VCF file into the AnnotSV program (Geoffroy et al., 2018) for annotating and ranking.

ROH intervals were obtained by the AutoMap algorithm (v1.2) (Quinodoz et al., 2021) using the patient's VCF file and the algorithm's default options (size of the sliding window: 0.75; a minimal number of variants detected in the ROH: 25; minimal percentage of homozygous variants in the detected ROH: 88).

The proband is a 19‐year‐old male who is the only child of consanguineous (first cousin) healthy Iranian parents (Figure 1a). Pregnancy was uncomplicated, and he was born by cesarean section at term, but shortly after birth, he experienced respiratory distress and became hospitalized in NICU for 1 day. He also experienced severe neonatal jaundice. Milestones were on time, holding the head at 4 months, sitting at 7 months, walking at 12 months, and saying the first word at 12 months. The onset of symptoms was at 13 years of age, starting with limping and frequent falls and progressing to difficulty running and climbing stairs. He had progressive weakness involving proximal and distal muscles, predominating in the lower limbs. He also has lordosis and abnormal gait with limitations in walking ability (he can walk with braces). However, facial weakness and muscle atrophy were not observed. On examination at 15 years of age; he had normal cognition, no facial dysmorphism, no behavioral issues, and no ophthalmology and hearing problems.

The proband had an elevated CPK of 722 (Reference range: 55–170 U/L). Nerve conduction studies were performed when he was 14 years old. Sensory potentials had normal amplitude but borderline conduction velocities in the median and ulnar (47 m/s). Motor studies showed reduced CMAP amplitude with normal velocities. Needle examination showed chronic neurogenic changes and spontaneous activity in leg muscles (proximal and distal). Based on the primary diagnosis of motor neuropathy or distal spinal muscular atrophy, the initial molecular analysis by real‐time PCR and MLPA for exon 7 of the SMN1 gene revealed no exon deletion in this patient.

Muscle biopsy at the age of 14 and from the left deltoid showed groups of atrophy of angular and some basophilic degenerative/regenerative fibers associated with some round hypertrophied fibers (Figure 1b,c). Marked atrophy of type 2 fibers with only focal fiber type grouping was also observed (Figure 1d). The biopsy was interpreted as neurogenic atrophy. Moreover, brain imaging (MRI) at the same age was normal (Figure 1e–g).

The electrodiagnostic study was repeated at the age of 16, and there was a significant reduction in sensory potential amplitudes and motor conduction velocities compared to the previous study, and the diagnosis of chronic motor>sensory, axonal polyneuropathy was reached.

Whole‐exome sequencing revealed a novel homozygous likely pathogenic variant in the NRCAM gene (NM_001037132.4). The prioritized SNV, c.73C > T, creates a premature translational stop signal (p.Gln25*) in the initial region of the NRCAM protein, the N‐terminal signal peptide (Figure 2a,b).

This variant is not observed in public databases such as gnomAD (Karczewski et al., 2020) and also Iranome; an ethnic‐specific database (Fattahi et al., 2019). Along the whole NRCAM gene, no homozygous LOF variant is observed in the normal population. This LOF variant is expected to result in an absent or truncated protein product that lacks almost all the protein's functional domains and can be classified as likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines (Richards et al., 2015). Furthermore, WES analysis led to the prioritization of another homozygous variant; a missense variant of uncertain significance in the TANC1 gene (NM_033394.3) defined as c.3437G > A (p.Arg1146Gln). This SNV is located on the ankyrin‐8 repeat domain of the expressed protein, and all bioinformatic predictions support its pathogenicity. Although it is not considered the primary cause of phenotype in our patient, but TANC1 function is noticeable. TANC1 is highly expressed in the brain and tibial nerves interact with scaffold and cytoskeletal proteins and signaling molecules, which supports its key roles in the positive regulation of dendritic spines and excitatory synapse (Han et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2019). Besides its roles in the nervous system, TANC1 contributes to myoblast fusion in skeletal muscle development (Avirneni‐Vadlamudi et al., 2012). We showed that both variants were inherited from the heterozygote parents (Figure 1a). However, TANC1 is not listed as an OMIM morbid gene; therefore, its contribution to the phenotype in the described patient is still unknown. Of note, WES‐based CNV analysis revealed no causal large deletion or duplication in this patient.

In the following, the consecutive homozygous genotypes or ROHs were assessed directly from the patient's WES data. As expected by his consanguineous background, 245.92 Mb of this patient's autosomal chromosomes are in homozygous regions. The homozygous NRCAM:p.Gln25* variant is located in an 8.82 Mbp ROH interval on chr7:107613379–116,435,768, encompassing 43 genes, while the missense variant in the TANC1 gene is located in a smaller (4.07 Mbp) ROH interval (Figure 2a,c). Generally, ROHs are more likely to encompass the disease‐causing variant. It is shown that ROH intervals larger than 5 Mbp are more likely to harbor causative variants for recessive Mendelian disorders, and are suggested as supporting evidence in the context of variant classification according to the ACMG guideline (Wakeling et al., 2019).

Therefore, we considered NRCAM deficiency as the cause of the observed phenotype in this patient. However, it is not clear if the missense variant in the TANC1 gene is only a benign rare polymorphism or has a synergistic or modifying effect on patient's symptoms (Douglas et al., 2009; Kurolap et al., 2022; Morelli et al., 2017).

The current study describes a patient with polyneuropathy, progressive muscle weakness in proximal and distal limbs, and lordosis who showed a biallelic defect in the NRCAM gene. The disease‐causing variant is located before the first functional Ig‐like‐1 domain of the protein and in the N‐terminal signal peptide. Although the exact function of its N‐terminal signal peptide is not described so far, all the functional domains of the protein seem to be disrupted by this LOF variant.

NRCAM protein, a member of the L1‐Ig‐CAM superfamily, plays a significant role in nervous system development, notably the peripheral nervous system (Sakurai, 2012). NRCAM is expressed temporally and spatially, in both neurons and glial cells, in the developing and mature nervous system. The expressed protein is localized in the cell membrane through its transmembrane domain, but the soluble forms of NRCAM are also produced via specialized Schwann cells, which are significantly involved in forming the myelinated nerve structures in the peripheral nervous system. Both forms of NRCAM interact with other intracellular and extracellular molecules in CNS or PNS for proper functioning (Kurolap et al., 2022; Sakurai, 2012).

In humans, the NRCAM gene is located on chromosome 7q31. Alternative splicing is a common feature of the gene (Liang et al., 2020), producing at least 67 different RefSeq transcripts, in which the identified variant in this study (p.Gln25*) is coding in 57 isoforms, including the canonical transcript (NM_001037132.2), which has the most expression in brain regions and tibial nerves according to the GTEx database (Lonsdale et al., 2013). The broad spectrum of splicing patterns in inter or intra‐domain regions may increase the diversity of interactions between NRCAM and other proteins or molecules. As documented, these changes in the splicing patterns are possibly the reason for the spectrum of features in NRCAM‐related autism disorders (Liang et al., 2020).

For the first time, the involvement of the NRCAM gene in a Mendelian disorder was determined by Kurolap et al. (2022). They described ten patients from eight families showing mild to severe spectrum of phenotypes ranging from motor neuropathy with secondary myopathic involvement without developmental delays due to homozygous missense Ser134Pro variant (individuals 8a and 8b) into the most severe forms represented by severe global developmental delay (GDD) along with the neurological and neuromuscular features due to homozygous LOF variants (individual 1 and 2) (Kurolap et al., 2022). The variant type and location, presence of multiple NRCAM isoforms in different tissues, or modifier molecules that may compensate for NRCAM deficiency in the central or peripheral nervous system can explain the spectrum of the observed phenotypes (Kurolap et al., 2022). These symptoms can be considered as a single but heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder or categorized as several separate syndromes, which can be delineated with the observation of more patients.

Although our patient carries a homozygous LOF variant, which may eventually disturb all of the functional domains, he just showed predominantly motor neuropathy with no signs of central nervous system involvement. This observation questions the correlation between the type and location of the mutation to the severity of the disease suggested by Kurolap et al. Individual 2 from their study is the only patient harboring a homozygous LOF variant (Glu111*) at the beginning of the NRCAM protein, similar to the Gln25* variant in our patient, but represented one of the most severe forms of the disorder among the other patients (Table 1) (Kurolap et al., 2022).

On the contrary, the observed phenotype in our patient is more similar to Individuals 8a and 8b, who harbor less severe homozygous missense variants and share the following clinical features with our currently described patient; peripheral neuropathy, progressive muscle weakness predominating in lower limbs, elevated CPK, normal growth parameters, cognition and hearing, no dysmorphism and behavioral issues, and final appearance of the clinical features in the second decade compared to all other patients who showed a congenital form of the disease (Table 1) (Kurolap et al., 2022). Therefore, our study shows that the type of mutation in the NRCAM gene might not necessarily determine the severity of the disease.

We suspect that the milder phenotype, despite the loss of a large portion of the protein in our patient, might be due to possible unknown modifiers/compensatory mechanisms, similar to the mice, supported by the recovery of NRCAM‐associated hearing defects in Nrcam‐mutant mice (Harley et al., 2018). Although the lack of compensatory mechanisms in humans is discussed (Kurolap et al., 2022), there are some suggestive data for its presence. For instance, in the formation of Ranvier nodes, NRCAM cooperates with gliomedin and neurofascin‐186 to mediate Na + channel clustering in heminodes, but not in the mature nodes and it seems that additional mechanisms independent of CAMs are involved in the mature nodes (Feinberg et al., 2010). This might play a role in the varying severity of symptoms in NRCAM deficiency. Hypothetically, activation of the nonsense‐mediated decay (NMD) pathway may cause the expression or higher expression of compensatory proteins with similar functions or structures (Kurolap et al., 2022).

In summary, this is the second report of an association between biallelic NRCAM gene variants and a Mendelian disorder. The observed phenotype in this patient verifies the phenotypic heterogeneity of NRCAM deficiency and highlights its phenotypic complexity.
