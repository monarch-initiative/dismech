# Transient Hyperparathyroidism and Severe Intrauterine Osteopenia Linked to Novel Homozygous TRPV6 Deletion.
**Authors:** Grigore T, Leusink Q, Latta F, van der Eerden B, de Laat P, van der Kaay D, Hoenderop J
**Journal:** Clin Endocrinol (Oxf) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cen.70134](https://doi.org/10.1111/cen.70134)

## Content

CONTEXT: Placental calcium (Ca2+) transport is essential for foetal bone mineralisation and development, as well as for Ca2+ homoeostasis. Rare mutations in transient receptor potential vanilloid (TRPV) 6 of the infant cause insufficient maternal-foetal Ca2+ transport through the placenta. Although there is a limited number of cases, phenotype analyses of these patients show skeletal deformities and hyperparathyroidism. In this study, we identified a case with a homozygous mutation in the TRPV6 channel that leads to the deletion of the Ca2+ pore.

PATIENT MEASUREMENTS: The neonate was born with skeletal abnormalities, increased umbilical cord PTH levels (75 pmol/L) and normal total Ca2+ level (2.06 mmol/L). Skeletal abnormalities improved during the first months of life.

RESULTS: 45Ca2+ uptake assays demonstrated loss-of-function, and biotinylation assays demonstrated failure of the channel to reach the cell surface.

CONCLUSIONS: These findings indicate that the TRPV6-p.Arg492* mutation affects maternal-foetal Ca2+ transport and explains the observed phenotype.

The epithelial Ca2+ channel TRPV6 is characterised by having a high selectivity for Ca2+ and having a luminal localisation in epithelial tissues that have a Ca2+‐transporting function. These features highlight TRPV6 as an important regulator for serum Ca2+ levels by facilitating intestinal absorption and partial renal reabsorption of Ca2+. TRPV6 has a broad tissular expression pattern, prominently in the intestine [1], kidney [2, 3, 4] and placenta [5], which is crucial in providing the nutritional and mineral requirements of the foetus [6] through the maternal‐foetal Ca2+ transport. The placenta‐mediated Ca2+ transport is critical for foetal bone health, and the role of TRPV6 in maternal‐foetal Ca2+ transport has been documented in both in vivo and in vitro studies [5, 7, 8, 9]. Mice lacking TRPV6 were reported to have significantly lower Ca2+ levels in serum and amniotic fluid [5]. In these fetuses, 40% less 45Ca2+ transport activity, a decrease in ash weight showing differences in tissue mineralisation and bone composition [5], altered placenta morphology, altered bone microarchitecture and growth defects in embryos [10] was observed. Additionally, a loss‐of‐function TRPV6 mutation described in zebrafish demonstrated a 65% reduction in Ca2+ content and severe bone mineral defects [11].

TRPV6 is a transmembrane (TM) multimeric protein built from 4 identical subunits [12, 13, 14, 15, 16], that each contains 6 TM domains and a Ca2+ pore with a selectivity filter between TM domains 5 and 6. TRPV6 is formed from 725 amino acids, and the 20 pore‐forming residues are located in the position 566‐585 [17]. The iris‐like Ca2+ pore occupies a central role in the protein structure and allows for Ca2+ absorption and regulation of intracellular Ca2+ levels [16, 18], considering that the TRPV6 channel is activated by low internal Ca2+ level [19].

The pathophysiology of TRPV6 has yet to be fully understood, considering the first reports of inherited human TRPV6 deficiency emerged in 2018 [20]. Patients with TRPV6 mutations (p.Ala18Ser [20]; p.Cys212Tyr [20]; p.Ile223Thr [20, 21]; p.Asp328* [20]; p.Arg425Gln [20]; p.Gly428Arg [20, 21]; p.Gly451Glu [20]; p.Arg483Trp [20]) were diagnosed with transient neonatal hyperparathyroidism and osteopenic and with skeletal deformities [20, 21]. All these cases were homozygous or compounds‐heterozygous mutation and, in most cases, the clinical symptoms of the TRPV6 variants resolved during infancy as a result of adequate nutrition and Ca2+ supply from the intestines. The only post‐mortem case reported suggests a postnatal recovery, however the unexpected death at 4 months may point toward a long‐lasting effect on the Ca2+ homoeostasis [22].

Herein we report a patient with severe skeletal but mild biochemical abnormalities due to a homozygous deletion in the TRPV6 gene. Using a combination of medical investigations and molecular techniques, we characterised the effect of the TRPV6‐p.Arg492* mutation. The functional studies we present link the genotype to the phenotype.

We studied a female patient of Moroccan descent. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents. Clinical characteristics, laboratory, radiological and genetic investigations were collected. Bone mineral content (BMC) and bone mass density (BMD) were measured with the Lunar Prodigy Advance R00279 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) DXA machine, using Encore v14.1 software. The DXA machine was calibrated according to the supplier's manual. During the DXA scans, the patient wore a disposable diaper. A vacuum cushion was not used [23]. Reference values for BMC and BMD in infants aged 6 months have been established at the Erasmus University Medical Center (unpublished data).

The comparison of native and mutant protein structures (Figure 1) and the figures were done using UCSF ChimeraX‐1.4 (https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax), and the protein structure used was 5WO7 (PDB identifier). To determine the estimated size of the TRPV6 variant ending at the 492nd residue, we used an online molecular size prediction tool (https://www.bioinformatics.org/sms/prot_mw.html).

HEK293 (human embryonic kidney 293) cells were cultivated in a 37°C and 5% (v/v) CO2 environment, in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) enriched with 10% (v/v) foetal bovine serum (BioWest, Nuaillé, France), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 µL/mL non‐essential amino acids. All experiments were done on cells under passage 20. Cells were transiently transfected 4–6 h after seeding with the respective DNA construct using a ratio of 1:2 DNA:Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

HEK293 cells were seeded on 6‐well plates and transiently transfected with TRPV6, mutant TRPV6 and mock (2 µg). 16 h after transfection, cells were reseeded in 24‐well plates coated with poly‐L‐lysine (0.1 mg/mL). After 24 h, cells were pre‐treated for 30 min with 25 µM BAPTA‐AM, washed with KHB buffer and incubated for 10 min with 45CaCl2 (1 µCi/mL; Perkin‐Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) in KHB buffer enriched with voltage‐gated Ca2+ channel inhibitors (10 µM felodipine and 10 µM verapamil). Blocking of the TRPV6‐mediated 45Ca2+ uptake was done with 10 µM ruthenium red during the 10‐min incubation. The 45CaCl2 uptake was stopped with several wash steps using ice‐cold stop buffer. Cells were lysed using 0.05% (w/v) SDS buffer and mixed with Opti‐Fluor liquid scintillation cocktail (Perkin Elmer), then scintillation was measured using Hidex 300SL.

Before transfection with the indicated constructs (2000 ng), HEK293 cells were seeded in 6‐well plates coated with poly‐L‐lysine. Cells were washed once with 2 mL ice‐cold PBS‐CM, and incubated with 0.5 mg/mL biotin (Ez‐link Sulfo‐NHS‐LC‐LC‐biotin, Pierce Biotechnology, Waltham, MA, USA) for 30 min at 4°C. Subsequently, the biotinylation reaction was stopped by washing the cells twice with quenching buffer and once with PBS. The cells were lysed in 0.5 mL Triton lysis buffer and collected using cells scrapers, then centrifuged at 16,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions, and equal amounts of protein were incubated for 2 h at 4°C with neutravidin beads (Pierce Biotechnology). The beads were then washed three times in lysis buffer.

All samples (total cell lysates or biotin beads) were denaturated with Laemmli buffer with 100 mmol/L DTT for 5 min at 95°C, then subjected to 10% (w/v) SDS‐Page and transferred to low background polyvinylidene membranes. The membranes were blocked using 5% (w/v) milk, dissolved in Tris‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween‐20 (TBS‐T) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were immunoblotted overnight at 4°C for HA‐tag or β‐actin. Subsequently, the blots were washed three times in TBS‐T, and incubated with PO‐labelled secondary antibodies. After washing three times with TBS‐T, the proteins were visualised for chemiluminescence using the SuperSignal West Femto (Thermo Fisher Scientific), using the ImageQuant LAS 4000 imaging system (GE Healthcare). Band densities were quantified using the densitometric analysis software Image J (version 2.1.0).

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and the statistical significance was determined using a one‐way analysis of variance. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The patient is a currently 17 months‐old girl, the first and only child born to consanguineous parents. An ultrasound performed at 20 weeks gestation demonstrated a bell‐shaped chest and shortened long bones. CNV analysis showed no abnormalities. Trio‐based Whole Exome Sequencing showed a homozygous, likely pathogenic variant in the TRPV6 gene (TRPV6; Chr7(GRCh37): g.142571874 G > A: NM_018646.4:c.1474 C > T p.(Arg492*)). This variant has not been previously reported in the literature, was present only once as a heterozygous variant in gnomAD, and is categorised as likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines [25]. The mother did not have gestational diabetes, thyroid function was not tested since she had no risk factors for thyroid disease. Vitamin D level was checked at 17 weeks gestation and was found to be low (33 nmol/L). She started vitamin D treatment. Normal total calcium (2.25 mmol/l) and vitamin D (91 nmol/L) levels were found at 30 weeks gestation and shortly after birth. The family history is unremarkable regarding congenital disorders or developmental delay.

The patient was born at 38 + 3 weeks gestation after a secondary caesarian section due prolonged labour, foetal distress and suspicion of maternal infection. Apgar scores were 8, 9 and 9 after respectively 1‐, 5‐ and 10‐min. Birth weight was 3075 grams (−0.14 standard deviation score).

Skeletal survey on day 4 of life showed diffuse osteopenia with multiple bilateral rib fractures and bilateral fractures of the humerus, ulna, femur and tibia with callus formation (Figure 2). The spine and pelvis demonstrated no signs of fractures. She received pain medication (acetaminophen) when she showed signs of discomfort related to the fractures during the first couple of days after birth.

Ca2+ levels remained within normal reference ranges thereafter. Vitamin D level was normal, and alkaline phosphatase level on day 3 of life was slightly elevated. Due to low‐normal saturations and tachypnea she received respiratory support with high flow oxygen therapy for 5 days and low‐flow oxygen therapy for another week. She received antibiotics because of maternal fever and respiratory distress. A blood culture remained negative and antibiotic treatment was discontinued 2 days after birth. There were no feeding difficulties, she received breast milk on demand. Vitamin D supplementation 400 IU once daily was started at day 3 of life. She was transferred to a non‐academic hospital on day 10 of life and discharged home on day 25 of life.

A cardiac ultrasound at age 1 month demonstrated a structurally normal heart with mildly elevated right arterial pressure which was interpreted as physiological at this age. A repeat ultrasound was performed at age 1 year and normal.

She is followed in our outpatient clinic. Gross motor development is slightly delayed for which she is followed by a physical therapist. From a clinical perspective, no new fractures have occurred after birth. Ca2+ levels were measured until age 4 months and remained within normal range. At 17 months, her weight was 9.9 kg (−0.72 standard deviation score), height 79.4 cm (−0.55 standard deviation score) and head circumference 47 cm (−0.14 standard deviation score). The bell‐shaped chest is still apparent, but less pronounced compared to directly after birth. There are no pulmonary concerns.

A DXA scan was performed at age 4 months, 10 months and 17 months. At age 4 months, total body BMD was 0.279 g/cm2 and total body less head (TBLH) BMC was 57.3 grams. DXA was repeated at age 10 months and demonstrated a total body BMD of 0.388 g/cm2 and BMC TBLH of 112.2 grams. The most recent DXA at age 17 months demonstrated a total body BMD of 0.437 g/cm2 and BMC TBLH of 155.3 grams.

HEK293 cells were transiently transfected and the total expression of TRPV6 and TRPV6‐p.Arg492* was compared. TRPV6‐p.Arg492* demonstrated bands around ±55 kDa (Figure 3), indicating that the protein is truncated before reaching the Ca2+ pore at transmembrane 5 and 6. The estimated molecular size of the TRPV6 variant ending at the amino acid number 492 is of 56 kilodaltons. The biotinylation experiment (Figure 3) indicated that the plasma membrane fraction of TRPV6‐p.Arg492* did not show any bands, suggesting that the mutated TRPV6‐p.Arg492* is not expressed on the cell membrane.

In this study, we report a patient whose phenotype is in all probability caused by insufficient transplacental Ca2+ transport to sustain normal foetal bone mineralisation. In the present study, the TRPV6‐p.Arg492* displayed a severe decrease in functionality, according to the results from in silico prediction tools. We were able to confirm pathogenicity using our functional 45Ca2+ uptake assay, which allows us to draw the conclusion that this patient's phenotype is caused by the observed TRPV6‐p.Arg492* deletion, demonstrating that this mutation is a loss‐of‐function variant.

The maternal‐foetal Ca2+ transport through the placenta is essential for foetal development, especially for bone formation and mineralisation and neuromuscular activity [28]. Approximately 80% of the Ca2+‐content is transferred during the last trimester from the mother to the foetus [29, 30], which hinders the early detection of possible abnormalities caused by defective Ca2+ transport. This is consistent with the previous reports [20] where no abnormalities were detected during the second trimester ultrasound. In contrast, the patient described in this report already showed skeletal abnormalities during the second trimester detailed ultrasound at 20 weeks.

Foetal blood Ca2+ values are approximately 0.3–0.5 mmol/L higher than maternal values. Normally, PTH levels are low during foetal life due to the relative hypercalcemia in utero [27, 31]. Umbilical cord PTH level was significantly elevated in our patient. Secondary hyperparathyroidism was likely due to defective transplacental Ca2+ transport in order to maintain Ca2+ levels within the normal range. Elevated PTH levels in utero result in increased mobilisation of Ca2+ from the foetal skeleton with subsequent demineralisation and multiple fractures as was demonstrated on the skeletal survey on day 4 of life. After birth, the intestines take over the responsibility for providing minerals and symptoms improved.

DXA scan was performed three times in our patient with approximate 6‐month intervals. Reference data at this young age are very scarce. Whole body BMD and BMC with Lunar DXA machine were measured in term children at age 6 and 12 months [32]. BMD increased from 0.57 ± 0.05 g/cm2 (N = 30 children) at age 6 months to 0.63 ± 0.04 g/cm2 (N = 25 children) at age 12 months. BMC increased from 167 ± 39 grams to 244 ± 46 grams, respectively. A study performed in female children at age 3 (N = 31) months and 6 (N = 27) months using the Hologic DXA machine demonstrated a TBLH BMC of 65.41 ± 8.33 grams and 82.90 ± 12.68, respectively [33]. A study [34] performed at the Erasmus University Medical Center in 135 female infants with a mean age of 6 months (range 5.9–6.1 months) demonstrated a mean total body BMD of 0.356 ± 0.025 g/cm2 and mean BMC TBLH of 90.4 ± 14.1 grams. These infants were measured on the same DXA machine as our patient. Over time, BMD and BMC values significantly improved in our patient. We expect a continued improvement and will repeat DXA scans until values have normalised.

Previously reported variants fall into three categories: variations that are located in the transmembrane (TM) helices and impair the ability of TRPV6 to reach the cell surface (TRPV6‐p.Arg425Gln and TRPV6‐p.Gly428Arg in TM2, and TRPV6‐p.Arg483Trp in TM3), variations in the fourth ankyrin repeat domain that decrease the function of TRPV6 (TRPV6‐p.Cys212Tyr and TRPV6‐p.Ile223Thr), and a variation localised in intracellular loop between TM2 and TM3, which increases the intracellular Ca2+ (TRPV6‐p.Gly451Glu). In contrast to the other variations, the variant described in our study is localised in the intracellular loop between TM4 and TM5, before the Ca2+ pore. Interestingly, Arg492 is located exactly at the CoA‐binding site and the PI(4,5)P2‐binding site, and it's very likely that any mutation in this position renders it to be a loss‐of‐function mutation and would have a negative impact on the functional activity of TRPV6 [35]. That is because both CoA and PI(4,5)P2 have important roles in the functioning of TRPV6, particularly in activating and maintaining the channel activity. However, the mutation described in this paper is a deleterious mutation, therefore the loss of function is caused by the lack of the Ca2+ pore, which is also suggested by the truncated form of TRPV6‐p.Arg492*.

The 45Ca2+ uptakes showed that the TRPV6‐p.Arg492* is not able to function normally, when compared to wild type TRPV6, and the biotinylation assays indicate that the TRPV6p.Arg492* does not reach the cell membrane surface, suggesting that the mutated TRPV6‐p.Arg492* might be recycled or retained in the endoplasmic reticulum due to its structural abnormality.

Our findings recapitulate previous findings of patients with TRPV6 mutations that were diagnosed with transient neonatal hyperparathyroidism, and osteopenic and skeletal deformities. Few studies have extensively investigated TRPV6 mutations incorporating clinical, laboratory and radiological findings. This has enabled a detailed delineation of the mutation's key phenotypic manifestations.

Furthermore, we made use of molecular techniques to shed light on the function and localisation of the TRPV6‐p.Arg492* variant. A strength of our study is that no previous study has investigated the function of a TRPV6 variant by using 45Ca2+ uptakes.

A limitation of our study is that the variant has been observed in only one patient, which makes it difficult to establish a robust consensus regarding the phenotype specifically associated with this mutation.

We thus conclude that the defective maternal‐foetal Ca2+ transport is caused by this novel TRPV6 loss of function mutation that results in foetal Ca2+ deficiency, secondary hyperparathyroidism with severe intrauterine osteopenia and multiple fractures. Postnatal improvement occurred once the placental Ca2+ transport ceased and other Ca2+ transport mechanisms became available.
