# Transient neonatal hyperparathyroidism caused by a monoallelic TRPV6 dominant negative variant.
**Authors:** Lam Shang Leen J, Suzuki Y, Koh AL, Jayagobi PA, Nishimura G, Lim WK, Jamuar SS, Vasanwala RF
**Journal:** JBMR Plus (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/jbmrpl/ziaf159](https://doi.org/10.1093/jbmrpl/ziaf159)

## Content

Transient neonatal hyperparathyroidism (TNHP) is a rare disorder characterized by severe skeletal abnormalities in newborns, resulting from insufficient maternal-fetal calcium transport across the placenta. Transient receptor potential cation channel, subfamily V, member 6 (TRPV6) plays a significant role in placental calcium transport. While biallelic variants in the TRPV6 gene have been associated with TNHP, we present a case involving a neonate of Malay descent, born to non-consanguineous parents, who exhibited severe skeletal abnormalities at birth that resolved spontaneously within 6 wk. Biochemical evaluation revealed neonatal hyperparathyroidism alongside maternal vitamin D deficiency. Whole exome sequencing identified a novel paternally inherited heterozygous missense variant in the TRPV6 gene resulting in a valine-to-glutamic acid substitution at codon 625 (p.Val625Glu). This variant is situated adjacent to the TRP box, a region where a neighboring subunit is implicated in modulating channel gating via the S4-S5 linker. Functional studies were conducted by transfecting HEK293 cells with TRPV6-DsRed plasmids encoding either the wild-type or mutant protein. Immunocytochemistry demonstrated comparable cellular distribution between the variant and wild-type proteins. Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of both proteins at the plasma membrane. However, coimmunoprecipitation, intracellular calcium imaging, and patch-clamp studies suggested that the p.Val625Glu variant exerts a dominant-negative effect by interacting with the wild-type subunit to form a heteromeric complex, thereby impairing channel function. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a monoallelic TRPV6 variant associated with TNHP, expanding the understanding of its genetic underpinnings.

Calcium is crucial for fetal bone mineralization and maternal-fetal transport of calcium increases towards term to meet fetal requirements.1 At this stage, fetal blood has a higher concentration of calcium in comparison to maternal blood thereby creating conditions that require active transcellular transport of calcium from mother to the fetus. Transient receptor potential cation channel, subfamily V, member 6 (TRPV6; OMIM #606680), which is abundantly expressed in mammalian placental tissues, plays an indispensable role in placental calcium transport as fetal bone mineralization peaks during late pregnancy.2 Transcellular transport of calcium across the placenta has 3 major components: (1) movement from maternal blood, down an electrochemical gradient, into the cytosol of the syncytiotrophoblast through epithelial calcium channels such as TRPV6, located on the maternal-facing microvillous membrane; (2) buffering by calcium binding proteins such as calbindin-D9K in the trophoblast cytosol and delivery to the fetal-facing basal membrane; and (3) efflux of calcium across the basal membrane against the electrochemical gradient mediated by the plasma membrane calcium ATPase.3 Active placental calcium transport from mother to fetus in normal pregnancy maintains a higher calcium concentration in the fetus as compared to maternal plasma,1 and defects in placental transport of calcium can be precursors for calcium deficiency syndromes and intrauterine growth restriction.

Transient neonatal hyperparathyroidism (TNHP; OMIM #618188) with skeletal abnormalities results from insufficient maternal-fetal transport of calcium across the placenta and is associated with loss-of-function variants in the gene encoding TRPV6.4 To date, all reported cases of TRPV6-related TNHP were due to biallelic variants in TRPV6.4–7 Herein, we report a case of TRPV6-related TNHP caused by a novel heterozygous missense variant in TRPV6 with a dominant negative effect, leading to skeletal abnormalities that eventually self-resolved.

N is a female infant born at 32 + 6 wk via emergency caesarean section to a 33-yr-old G3P2 Malay mother with a history of preeclampsia. Antenatal scans at 19 wk showed a narrow and irregular chest wall with normal long bones. Birth weight was 1560 g (−0.79 SDS). After birth, she had respiratory distress syndrome requiring continuous positive airway pressure support and surfactant. Examination showed a narrow chest wall indicating hypoplastic ribs but no long bone, skull, or spine deformity. A chest radiograph at day 3 of life revealed gracile osteopenic ribs (Figure 1A). Bone biochemistry showed elevated iPTH (230 pmol/L), raised alkaline phosphatase (502 u/L), low calcium (1.98 mmol/L), low phosphate 1.7 mmol/L, and vitamin D insufficiency (25(OH)D3), 11.2 ng/mL). The clinical impression was neonatal hyperparathyroidism due to calcium and vitamin D deficiency. The baby had no symptomatic hypocalcemia, managed with total parenteral nutrition and full feeds by day 13 of life. Oral vitamin D supplementation (colecalciferol) started on day 10 of life after feed intolerance resolved. Trends of bone biochemistry showed normalization of iPTH by 2 wk after full oral feeds. A follow-up X-ray after 6 wk showed rib mineralization and improved chest deformity (Figure 1B).

The mother reported muscle cramps before and during pregnancy. She did not take calcium and vitamin D regularly. Post-delivery bone biochemistry showed secondary hyperparathyroidism from severe vitamin D deficiency and likely calcium deficiency (Total calcium: 2.3 mmol/L; iPTH 17.4 pmol/L (reference range: 0.9-6.2 pmol/L) and vitamin D < 7 ng/mL (reference range: 20-100 ng/mL). There was no family history of genetic disorders related to calcium, parathyroid, and skeletal disorders. The mother did not show the manifestation of Albright hereditary osteodystrophy. The mother was started on calcium and vitamin D supplementation. The father’s medical history was not available to confirm despite speaking with family to clarify.

The profound neonatal hyperparathyroidism with bone changes suggested severe antenatal calcium deficiency. As this could not be fully explained by maternal deficiency alone, genetic testing was performed to identify inherited causes and guide management.

Written informed consent for the clinical genetic testing was obtained from the patient’s parents. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood cells. A customized gene panel including AP2S1, CASR, CDC73, CDKN1B, GNA11, MEN1, RET, and TRPV6 was performed at Fulgent Genetics (California, United States). For this specimen, 100.00% of coding regions and splicing junctions of genes listed were sequenced with at least 10x and 20x coverage, respectively, by Next Generation Sequencing (NGS). Sanger sequencing was used as a secondary method for confirmation of reported variants. The Fulgent Germline v2019.2 pipeline was used for analysis.

Subsequently, trio-based whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was also performed with DNA samples from the patient and her unaffected parents at Prevention Genetics (Wisconsin, United States) at a later stage to detect a second deep-intronic or noncoding variant. DNA fragments were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 using 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. The Prevention Genetics laboratory-developed Infinity pipeline was used to analyze genomic data. The WGS data were reanalyzed using an in-house bioinformatic pipeline. The WGS program used to detect structural variants (SV) is Parliament2.8

Structural variant and copy number variant (CNV) detection was performed on WGS data using Smoove,9 which refines calls from the LUMPY algorithm10 with automated read filtering, genotyping, and joint calling. Additional filtering with Duphold11 ensured that deletions and duplications were supported by corresponding shifts in read depth.

The reagents are manufactured in the United States of America, unless otherwise stated.

TRPV6-DsRed plasmid was generated by an In-fusion kit (Takara, Japan) by using plasmid including the non-AUG start codon of human TRPV6 cDNA. For site-directed mutagenesis, the reaction mix was as follows: 1x PrimeSTAR Max premix (Takara, Japan), 10 pmol of forward (5’-TGGCGAGAGGCCCATGAGCGGGATGA) and reverse (5’-ATGGGCCTCTCGCCAGTGAGTGTCGC) primers, and 0.1 ng of the TRPV6-DsRed plasmid. PCR reaction was as follows: 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 38 s. Samples were transformed into DH5α competent cells (Takara, Japan), then plasmids were purified by QIAprep Spin Miniprep kit (Qiagen). TRPV6 sequence was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fasmac, Japan).

Human embryonic kidney (HEK)293 cells (ECACC#12022001, K.A.C., Japan) were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (ThermoFisher) containing 10% FBS (Nichirei, Japan), penicillin/streptomycin (ThermoFisher), and GlutaMAX (ThermoFisher). Plasmid was introduced into the cells using Lipofectamine reagent (ThermoFisher).

HEK293 cells on a coverslip were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, rinsed with PBS, and blocked by the solution with 0.4% saponin, 1% bovine serum albumin, and 2% normal goat serum. Cells were then incubated with rabbit anti-Na+, K+-ATPase (1/500, Cell Signal Technology #23565) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, cells were incubated with anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa488 (1/200, Life Technologies, #A11034) for 2 h at room temperature. After washing, coverslips with cells were mounted onto a glass slide using Fluoromount (Diagnostic BioSystems). Fluorescent signals were visualized by fluorescent microscope (BX-9000, KYENCE, Japan). For live-cell imaging, HEK293 cells were directly observed by the confocal microscopy (A1R, Nikon, Japan) after overnight transfection.

Biotinylation of plasma membrane proteins was performed as described previously.4,5 HEK293 cells were treated with EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC biotin (Abcam)/DPBS at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by the incubation with 100 mM glycine/DPBS for 5 min. Then, membrane proteins were solubilized by 1x TNE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM Na3VO4 with 1% NP-40, and complete mini (Roche, Switzerland) at 4 °C. Biotinylated proteins were isolated by Dynabeads MyOne Strepavidin T1 (ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For Western blotting, isolated proteins were mixed with 1x SDS sample buffer, then electrophoresed in 7.5% TGX (tris-glycine expanded) gel (Bio-rad) at 120 V, transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Bio-rad) at 100 V for 90 min. The membrane was blocked by 3% BSA at 4 °C overnight, then incubated with mouse anti-DsRed antibody (1/500, Origene Technologies, #CF180084) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the membrane was incubated with anti-mouse IgG-horseradish peroxidase (1/5000, CST, #7076) for 2 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized by ECL prime kit (Amersham, USA) using Light Capture system (ATTO, Japan).

Plasmids were transfected into HEK293 cells and cultured overnight. Cells were lysed in 1x TNE buffer with 1% NP-40 and complete mini (Roche, Switzerland) at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 15,300 g for 15 min, the supernatant was treated with anti-myc antibody (Sigma M5546) with Dynabeads Protein G (ThermoFisher), overnight at 4 °C. The magnetic beads were washed with lysis buffer, solubilized with SDS sample buffer and Western blotting was then performed as described before. The rabbit anti-green fluorescent protein (GFP) antibody (Proteintech PABG1-100, 1/1000) was used for the primary antibody. For visualization, HRP substrate 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethyl-benzidene (BW-2010, Apro Science, Japan) was used for 2 min at room temperature.

Plasmid was introduced into HEK293 cells by above mentioned method with the pCMV-DsRed-Express plasmid (0.1 μg, Clontech). After overnight transfection, cells were treated with Fura-2-AM (Dojindo, Japan) at 5 μM concentration at 37 °C for 1 h. Intracellular Ca2+ concentration was measured in a standard bath solution;143 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM glucose, 5 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (pH 7.4). Ratiometric imaging of DsRed-positive cells was performed at 340 and 380 nm, and the emitted light was read at 510 nm (Incyt IM2, Intracellular Imaging). The ratio of 340/380 was then calculated.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed as described previously.5 Pipette solution contained as follows: 140 mM CsCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid, and 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.3). The standard bath solution contained 143 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM glucose, 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4). The divalent cation-free (DVF) solution contained 148 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 10 mM glucose, 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4). The N-methyl-d-glucamine (NMDG) solution contained: 148 mM NMDG, 5 mM KCl, 10 mM glucose, 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4 by HCl). The 30Ca solution contained 113 mM NMDG, 30 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM glucose, and 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4 by HCl). Membrane currents of the HEK293 cells were recorded using an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices) with a holding potential at −60 mV, digitized by the Digidata 1440A (Molecular Devices), and analyzed by the pCLAMP11 software (Molecular Devices).

Statistical analysis was performed with Mann-Whitney rank-sum test using SigmaPlot ver.12.0. The difference with p value of <.05 was considered significant.

A paternally inherited heterozygous missense variant was identified in TRPV6 (NM_018646.5:c.1874 T > A, p.Val625Glu). This variant is absent from population databases (gnomAD v.2.0 and v4.1.012), affects a highly conserved Valine at codon 625 (substituted by Glutamic acid), and is predicted to be deleterious by multiple in silico tool.13 Whole-genome sequencing revealed no second heterozygous deep intronic, non-coding, or SV in TRPV6. Integrative Genomics Viewer analysis confirmed the absence of structural or CNVs (Figure 2). Single nucleotide and indel analysis identified only this coding variant, while SpliceAI analysis of non-coding variants showed no cryptic splicing candidates.

The p.Val625Glu variant lies at the edge of the TRP box, where neighboring subunit can modulate channel gating function via S4-S5 linker,14–16 suggesting potential disruption of channel function through altered subunit interactions (4 subunits form 1 channel pore). We hypothesized that this variant exerts a dominant negative effect on normal TRPV6 function, warranting functional studies.

To confirm its physiological impact, intracellular localization was examined in HEK293 cells using a transient expression system and DsRed-tagged TRPV6. Immunocytochemistry showed WT TRPV6-DsRed localized both intracellularly and at the plasma membrane, regardless of the variant’s presence (Figure 3A and B). Western blotting of biotinylated surface proteins confirmed that both WT and p.Val625Glu were present at the plasma membrane (Figure 3C).

To confirm WT and p.Val625Glu interaction at the cellular level, live-cell imaging was performed using confocal microscopy under coexpression of WT-AcGFP (green) and p.Val625Glu-DsRed (red) (Figure 3D-G). In most cells, these proteins were colocalized (Figure 3G and H, p < .05, n = 393 cells). Moreover, coimmunoprecipitation further demonstrated that WT-AcGFP and p.Val625Glu-myc formed a complex (Figure 3I). Our results strongly suggest interaction between p.Val625Glu and wild type TRPV6.

For functional evidence of a dominant-negative effect, intracellular Ca2+ imaging was conducted with 2 mM extracellular Ca2+ (Figure 4A and B). WT TRPV6 expression in HEK293 cells increased intracellular Ca2+ due to higher plasma membrane permeability (Figure 4A and B). The plasma membrane permeability of WT TRPV6 was decreased when coexpressed with p.Val625Glu (p < .05, n = 6-11), but not with p.Arg510stop (p = .810), which was reported not to have a dominant-negative effect17 (Figure 4A). Arg510stop was reported to be expressed into the plasma membrane, changing only Gly660Arg but not WT activity; suggesting Arg510stop did not cause a significant nonsense-mediated RNA decay nor preventing successful delivery of the WT channels.17 The effect of p.Val625Glu was more prominent when cells were transfected with a higher ratio of its cDNA relative to WT (p < .001, n = 8-32, Figure 4B). These results suggest that p.Val625Glu has a dominant-negative effect.

To confirm the dominant-negative effect in a more direct way, membrane currents were recorded by whole-cell patch-clamp method in the HEK293 cells. WT-expressing cells exhibited hallmark TRPV6 activity: non-selective cation current under DVF conditions and Ca2+ transients upon extracellular Ca2+ loading, in line with previous reports4,5 (Figure 4C, upper  trace). However, both currents were significantly reduced when coexpressed with p.Val625Glu (p < .05, n = 4) (Figure 4C, lower  trace, Figure 4D). Scatter-plot graphs of the bar graphs are presented in Figures S1-S4.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that p.Val625Glu exerts a dominant-negative effect by interacting with WT subunits to form heteromers with impaired channel activity.

We report the first case of TNHP caused by a paternally inherited monoallelic variant in TRPV6 with a dominant-negative effect, in comparison to biallelic variants (Figure 5). TRPV6 expressed in placental tissue during pregnancy is important for calcium transport to the fetus. TNHP related to TRPV6 is by a novel mechanism that results in a reversible pathology starting during fetal life with skeletal demineralization and resolution within short period after birth.6,7

Patient N’s TNHP was detected as part of metabolic disease workup due to chest wall deformity and respiratory distress after birth. Even though her antenatal scans showed a narrow and irregular chest wall, suspicion for skeletal dysplasia or metabolic bone disease was low as the cortical long bones and spine appeared normal. The initial bone biochemical results showed a markedly raised iPTH, with borderline hypocalcemia and low vitamin D levels. Therefore, the child was suspected to have secondary hyperparathyroidism, likely due to a normal physiological response to low maternal supply of calcium and vitamin D in-utero. This appropriate physiological response also indicated an intact iPTH axis in the baby. This was further affirmed by the normalization of iPTH and calcium after birth with provision of calcium via milk feeds. Thus, the suspicion was a possible maternal factor operating in utero that caused neonatal hypocalcaemia and hyperparathyroidism.

We speculated on the following possible aetiologies for TNHP: (1) low maternal calcium/ vitamin D stores resulting in fetal deficiency, and (2) issues with transfer of calcium involving the TRPV6 transport channels in the placental tissue. The mother experienced hypocalcemia symptoms, and her dietary intake of calcium and vitamin D was not optimal, which was supported by her blood investigations. Therefore, the former hypothesis can be possible. However, there have been reports of neonates born to mothers with vitamin D deficiency showed hypocalcemia but not skeletal abnormalities.18,19 By contrast, the patient had profound skeletal manifestations of hyperparathyroidism, including severe thoracic deformity and extensive demineralization of the skeleton. Thus, other possible etiologies for TNHP were considered and genetic testing revealed a paternally inherited heterozygous missense variant in TRPV6. Functional studies confirmed the dominant-negative effect that resulted in the phenotype.

A case series showed that there were neonates born with radiographic signs of metabolic bone disease with hyperparathyroidism and their bone panel improved over time and normalized.4 Genetic studies showed biallelic mutations in TRPV6 which interfered with the TRPV6 function. The group postulated that the loss-of-function mutation in the TRPV6 variant interferes with calcium transport across the placenta leading to fetal hypocalcaemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism and bone mineralization defects. When oral milk feeds were established, the calcium homeostasis normalized due to intestinal calcium absorption via paracellular transport. Patient N’s TNHP with bone mineralization defect also improved when oral feeding was established.

Our patient was found to be heterozygous for a missense variant in TRPV6. A second variant in TRPV6 might not have been identified on WGS as our understanding of non-coding and regulatory variants in the intronic region remains nascent. However, the functional studies performed in our study had demonstrated a dominant negative effect of this missense variant towards the WT subunit. We also have demonstrated with the coimmunoprecipitation and functional studies that that the p.Val625Glu interacts with wild type TRPV6, supporting a direct mechanism. However, this does not exclude the possibility of indirect mechanisms. This may explain our patient’s disease mechanism and reduce the likelihood of the presence of a second variant in TRPV6. The presence of maternal hypocalcemia and severe vitamin D deficiency during her antenatal period may have contributed to fetal skeletal under mineralization in addition to the dominant-negative effect of the missense variant in TRPV6 affecting fetal placental transport. For example, Suzuki et al demonstrated that 1 of 2 siblings, although having the same genotype, was not diagnosed with TNHP. This suggests the maternal calcium/vitamin D store may affect the manifestation of TNHP.5 Therefore, our study demonstrated the importance of gene-environmental interaction leading to the child’s presentation.

There were study limitations. As this study showed a paternally inherited monoallelic variant, we wanted to verify the father’s past medical history. However, we were limited in obtaining and confirming the father’s clinical history due to a lack of records. Another limitation was that there were no interval images of the long bones as radiological imaging was performed for medical purposes. Finally, it was difficult to obtain patient-derived cells to check the total level of TRPV6 in this patient, as it’s mainly expressed in the placenta and minimally in skin keratinocytes.20

Despite its limitations, this study highlights that in a newborn presenting with a significant chest deformity out of proportion for bone biochemistry showing hyperparathyroidism with hypocalcemia, a differential diagnosis of TNHP should be considered and investigated accordingly with genetic testing.
