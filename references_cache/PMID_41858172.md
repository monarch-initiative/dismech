# The R203W substitution drives PACS-1 syndrome by disrupting intramolecular regulation.
**Authors:** Krzysiak TC, Byeon IL, Ponticelli R, Lucas ME, Thompson L, DeHaven C, Thomas G, Gronenborn AM
**Journal:** FEBS J (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/febs.70492](https://doi.org/10.1111/febs.70492)

## Content

The c607C>T mutation in the PACS1 gene results in an Arg203Trp substitution in the multifunctional protein PACS-1, and drives a syndrome characterized by intellectual disability, seizures, craniofacial dysmorphisms, and various characteristics of the autism spectrum. On the molecular level, this syndrome, in part, results from enhanced association of PACS-1 with the protein deacetylase HDAC6. PACS-1 uses its Furin binding region (FBR: amino acids 101-273) to directly interact with the catalytic domains of HDAC6. We present the solution structure of a chimeric PACS-1 FBR and use NMR to demonstrate that the PACS-1/HDAC6 interaction is regulated by an intramolecular mechanism involving the central unstructured region of PACS-1 folding back across the FBR and engaging in contacts with an extended, positively charged loop. The R203W substitution, located in this loop, disrupts this regulatory interaction and, in vitro, displays the ability to promote aberrant protein-protein interactions.

Phosphofurin acidic cluster sorting (PACS‐1) is a broadly expressed, multifunctional protein with roles in protein and membrane trafficking [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], HIV immune evasion [6, 7], genome stability [8], and neurobiology [9, 10, 11]. Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) suggest polymorphisms in PACS1 contribute to severe childhood obesity and bipolar disorder [12, 13]. Recently, a recurrent missense mutation in PACS1 (c607C>T) that results in an Arg203Trp (R203W) substitution has been identified as the causative agent of a severe neurological disorder known as PACS‐1 syndrome or Schuurs–Hoeijmakers syndrome [9]. Individuals carrying this mutation exhibit characteristic craniofacial dysmorphisms and several neurological abnormalities including: developmental delay, intellectual disability with varying degrees of autism, and seizures [9, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Mechanistically, this syndrome is poorly understood.

Much of the 963 amino acid PACS‐1 protein is unstructured. There are two large unstructured regions; at the N‐terminus resides the ill‐structured atrophin‐1‐related region (ARR), named for its sequence similarity with the nuclear transcriptional corepressor [18]. The second, the middle region (MR), regulates PACS‐1 protein–protein interactions and separates the protein's two structured domains: the Furin binding region (FBR) and a C‐terminal PACS domain (CTD) of unknown fold and function [19]. The FBR is the most extensively studied region of the protein given that it is essential for PACS‐1 interaction with sorting adapters such as AP‐1, AP‐3, and GGA3, as well as cellular cargo [2, 20]. PACS‐1 protein–protein interactions often occur through recognition of an acidic cluster in the interacting partner [1, 5, 21], though, increasing evidence indicates that the complete PACS‐1 recognition site on a partner is more complex [21, 22]. The pathogenicity of an amino acid substitution commonly results from substitution‐induced changes in the protein's structure or dynamics, or from alterations to important protein–protein interactions [23, 24, 25]. The PACS‐1 R203W substitution is located in the FBR, positioned in a cluster of basic amino acids (196RRKRYKNR203), which might be important for recognition of acidic patches in potential partners. Mutagenesis of this cluster has implicated a role for it in PACS‐1 interaction with CK2β and various HDACs [8, 20], but direct evidence of binding between the PACS‐1 basic cluster and a partner acidic cluster has not been reported. Nevertheless, the R203W substitution has the potential to drive pathogenesis through each common mechanism. Interestingly, charge‐altering substitutions in the PACS‐1 paralog, PACS‐2, were reported as the causative agents of another neurological disorder with phenotypes similar to those of PACS‐1 syndrome [26, 27], highlighting the importance of charge–charge interactions in PACS protein function.

HDAC6 is a class II histone deacetylase responsible for the deacetylation of α‐tubulin lysine 40 [28, 29] which, when acetylated, promotes microtubule stability [30]. Villar‐Pazos et al. found that neurons, as well as patient‐derived fibroblasts expressing PACS‐1 containing the R203W substitution, exhibited increased immunoprecipitation of PACS‐1 R203W with HDAC6, profoundly disrupted cytoskeletal architecture, and a dispersed Golgi [10]. Importantly, Villar‐Pazos et al. demonstrated that silencing HDAC6 could rescue PACS‐1 syndrome cellular phenotypes, strongly suggesting that the relationship between PACS‐1 and HDAC6 is critical for disease manifestation [10]. While both the PACS‐1 FBR and CTD contribute to PACS‐1/HDAC6 association, current evidence suggests that the FBR drives the association between the two proteins, similar to its role in cellular trafficking interactions [10].

In this manuscript, we demonstrate that the PACS‐1 FBR is capable of directly contacting the two catalytic domains of HDAC6 and that this communication is modulated by an acidic cluster immediately C‐terminus to the FBR that interacts with the basic cluster of the FBR. We also present the NMR solution structure of the FBR which reveals that the FBR exhibits a C2 fold (similar to the second domain of Ca2+‐dependent protein kinase C) with the basic cluster existing as a positively charged loop protruding away from the core β‐sandwich structure. Finally, we demonstrate that the R203W substitution disrupts PACS‐1 intramolecular regulation and has the ability to promote aberrant protein–protein interactions.

The association between PACS‐1 and HDAC6, first identified by Villar‐Pazos et al., was based on co‐immunoprecipitation experiments that do not require direct physical interaction between two proteins for identifying a functional relationship [10]. Therefore, to verify that a direct physical interaction between these two proteins exists, 1H15N HSQC spectroscopy was employed. Spectra of uniformly 15N‐labeled (15N)‐PACS1101‐273 (FBR) were recorded in the absence and presence of natural abundance (na)‐HDAC6 catalytic domain 1 (CD1) or 2 (CD2). Addition of the ~40 kDa CD1 to the FBR resulted in extensive line‐broadening of amide resonances of the FBR spectrum, with only resonances of very flexible, unstructured regions remaining (Fig. 1A). This unambiguously demonstrates that a direct physical interaction between the FBR and CD1 occurs. Addition of an equal amount of the identically sized CD2 to the FBR also resulted in broadening of FBR resonances (Fig. 1B) establishing that both catalytic domains are capable of engaging the PACS‐1 FBR. Qualitatively, more pronounced effects on the FBR 1H15N HSQC spectrum were observed in the presence of CD1 suggesting FBR/CD1 binding is tighter than that of FBR/CD2, in agreement with the report by Villar‐Pazos et al. [10].

We next aimed to characterize the effect of the R203W substitution on the PACS‐1 FBR/HDAC6 CD1/2 interaction. Unfortunately, while the R203W substituted FBR was expressed at comparable levels to the wild‐type protein, it accumulated in inclusion bodies. Attempts to refold the R203W‐substituted FBR were unsuccessful, and even extensive trials to obtain sufficient amounts of soluble, folded protein for structural studies failed. This prompted us to adopt a more targeted approach relying on the solution properties of the PACS‐1 ortholog, PACS‐2 [22]. The 1H15N HSQC spectrum of wild‐type PACS‐1 FBR exhibits reasonable quality only at low protein concentrations (30 μm), with spectral quality diminishing as protein concentration is increased, unlike the PACS‐2 FBR (Fig. S1A–D and [22]), despite the two domains containing only 34 amino acid differences (Fig. S2). We hypothesized that substitution of a few PACS‐2 amino acids into their corresponding positions in the PACS‐1 FBR might generate a chimeric FBR with more favorable biophysical properties to allow for the expression of a more soluble protein and more straightforward purification of the R203W substituted PACS‐1 FBR. Substitution of only three PACS‐1 FBR amino acids with the corresponding PACS‐2 amino acids (P244K, K249W, and Y251A) resulted in a substantially better‐behaved protein at the concentrations necessary for structure determination (Fig. S1E,F). This chimeric FBR (cFBR) exhibited interactions with the HDAC6 CD1 comparable to those of the wild‐type FBR (Fig. 1C,D). Furthermore, introduction of the R203W substitution into the cFBR (cR203W) resulted in soluble protein that also displayed comparable interaction with HDAC6 CDs akin to those of both the wild‐type and chimeric FBRs (Fig. 1E,F). However, the substitution resulted in increased protein self‐interaction as evidenced by spectral quality similar to observations with wild‐type PACS‐1 FBR (Fig. S1G,H).

Since the R203W substitution made the wild‐type PACS‐1 FBR refractory to protein purification, we postulated that the amino acid change might reduce the thermal stability of the domain. Numerous down‐field resonances, distinct from those located in the random coil region of 8.1–8.4 p.p.m., in the 1H15N HSQC spectra of PACS FBRs, indicated that these domains contain mostly β‐sheet secondary structure. We, therefore, performed circular dichroism measurements at 220 nm to monitor thermal unfolding [31, 32]. The PACS‐1 and PACS‐2 wild‐type FBRs exhibited T
m values of ~54 °C and 57 °C, respectively (Fig. 2). As suggested by the improved quality of the 1H15N HSQC spectrum, the cFBR, was stabilized by ~19 °C compared to the wild‐type PACS‐1 FBR, exhibiting a T
m of ~70 °C. Increased thermal stability was also observed for cR203W, which exhibited a T
m value of ~68 °C (Fig. 2). All FBRs precipitated upon thermal unfolding.

AlphaFold (AF) predictions suggest the (196RRKRYKNR203) basic cluster has a few residues at the end of a β‐strand but mostly occupies a large loop on one side of the FBR [33, 34]. Introduction of a large hydrophobic side chain into an exposed loop such as this, potentially, can generate new intermolecular protein–protein interactions. Such induced intermolecular protein associations may account for the observed decrease in the cR203W 1H15N HSQC spectrum quality at higher protein concentration. To verify the computational predictions and because no experimentally derived structures for any part of the PACS proteins are available to date, we determined the solution structure of the PACS‐1 cFBR using NMR spectroscopy.

Overall, assignment of more than 95% of all backbone and side chain resonances of the cFBR, comprising residues 101 through 273, and the cFBR294, comprising residues 101 through 294, was achieved. Importantly, the additional amino acids in the cFBR294 contain critical autoregulatory components of the MR [20]. NMR structure calculations were performed using XPLOR‐NIH [35] with 4689 NOE‐derived distance constraints and 279 dihedral angle constraints based on HN, N, Cα, and Cβ chemical shifts using TALOS‐N [36]. The lowest energy 40‐conformer ensemble (Fig. 3A) satisfies all experimental constraints and displays excellent covalent geometry with atomic root‐mean‐square differences (r.m.s.d.) of 0.57 ± 0.05 and 1.07 ± 0.06 Å for the backbone and all heavy atoms, respectively, relative to their mean positions (Table 1). The largest r.m.s.d. values (> 3 Å) were observed for the unstructured C‐terminus (residues 260–273) and the first loop (residues 110–113) due to a low number of experimental constraints (Fig. 3A).

The experimental NMR structure of the cFBR is very similar to the AF‐predicted fold [33, 34], with a backbone (N, Cα and C′) atomic r.m.s.d. value of 1.25 Å for the confidently modeled regions (Fig. 3B). However, differences are observed in the positively charged loop containing R203 and in the loop connecting the last two β‐strands around V241 (Figs 3B,C and S3A–C). The presence of conformational differences between the experimental NMR and AF models are confirmed unambiguously by NOESY data (Fig. S3D). For example, medium sized NOE peaks are observed between the N202 Hβ and I205Hδ resonances (these atoms are separated by 3.7 Å in the NMR structure), the V243 and S236 HN resonances (these atoms are separated by 3.1 Å in the NMR structure), and the V243 and N237 HN resonances (these atoms are separated by 3.6 Å in the NMR structure). No such NOEs are expected from the AF‐predicted structure since much longer inter‐proton distances (7.1–10.6 Å) are seen (Table S1). These differences arise primarily from local conformational differences in the loops. Note the R203 sidechain in both the NMR and AF models (Fig. 3B) is pointing away from the FBR structural domain (> 8 Å) without exhibiting any close contacts.

In contrast to both the cFBR and cFBR294, whose 1H15N HSQC spectra exhibited excellent quality without signs of heterogeneity (compared in Fig. 5), spectra of the corresponding R203W substituted domains, cR203W and cR203W294, respectively, display heterogeneity as evidenced by resonance doubling. For several resonances, one set of peaks possesses narrow line‐widths at frequency positions close to those of the unsubstituted sequence protein. A second set exhibits larger line‐widths and is located at different spectral frequencies (Fig. 4A). Such doubling of resonances suggests the presence of a second protein species in cR203W samples most likely caused by oligomerization. Therefore, 1H15N HSQC spectra of these protein constructs were recorded at multiple protein concentrations (Fig. 4B). Peak doubling persisted throughout the series, and the intensity of the broader line species increased with increasing protein concentration (Fig. 4B). TROSY‐type 3D HNCACB and HN(CO)CACB spectra recorded for a 2H13C15N cR203W294 sample were used to obtain backbone and Cβ assignments for the doubled peaks and confirmed that the two sets of resonances were associated with the same amino acid in the protein sequence. Since several doubled resonance sets are available in the spectra, it was possible to measure the intensities (peak volumes) of well‐resolved pairs and calculate the disassociation constant for oligomerization. The K
d values were 134 ± 43 and 110 ± 37 μm for cR203W and cR203W294 respectively.

To ascertain the oligomerization state of the R203W substituted FBRs, tandem size exclusion multi‐angle light scattering (SEC‐MALS) experiments were performed. The expected monomeric molecular mass for cFBR294 and cR203W294 is ~23 kDa. The SEC‐MALS extracted average molecular mass for cFBR294 at an injection concentration of ~140 μm was 25 kDa indicating that the unsubstituted FBR exists as a monomer. However, the average extracted mass for cR203W294 was 36 kDa with the scattering points sloping downwards across the peak from 42 to 32 kDa indicating that the substituted variant exists in a monomer/dimer equilibrium (Fig. 4C).

The dimerization interface, deduced from the amino acid identity of the doubled resonances, comprises four clusters along the linear amino acids sequence (Fig. 4D). These four regions reside on one face of the NMR structure where the basic loop and strand β1 are located. We generated a model for the cR203W294 dimer by docking two NMR structures onto each other using HADDOCK (Fig. 4E) [38, 39]. Interestingly, two resonances, L267 and S271, that are located in the random‐coil C‐terminal tail exhibit doubled peaks and are located on the opposite side of the FBR, away from the dimerization interface. This suggests that this random coil region, comprised of the MR, transiently contacts the dimerization interface.

Scott et al. reported that an acidic cluster in the MR, which can be phosphorylated by CK2 and is located just C‐terminus to the FBR, can interact with the FBR, and by doing so, regulate the interaction between PACS‐1 and various partner proteins [19]. The binding site(s) on the FBR for this regulatory acidic cluster has not been identified, but a likely candidate may be the (196RRKRYKNR203) basic cluster that contains the R203W substitution. Our above dimerization data imply that regions C‐terminus to the FBR might contact this part of the protein. To identify which regions in the PACS‐1 FBR interact with the MR, we compared the 1H15N HSQC spectra of the cFBR, which contains the basic cluster, and the cFBR294, which contains the basic cluster as well as the MR acidic cluster (Fig. 5). Chemical shift differences (CSDs) are observed for resonances of amino acids comprising the R203W induced dimerization interface, both in/near the basic loop (R197, Y200, K201, N202, R203, I205, L206) as well as surrounding components (T106 (β1), W107, V142 (β3), L177, Q178), and in a region from R152‐R155 that is outside of the dimerization interface but adjacent to the basic loop in our NMR structure. In addition, CSDs are observed for resonances associated with the C‐terminus (H260, K264, S265, L267) immediately proximal to the addition of the MR acidic cluster (Fig. 5B). Essentially, the regulatory component of the MR transiently contacts the FBR, ‘laying’ across one face of the domain to reach the basic cluster on the opposite side of the domain (Fig. 5A inset). To examine whether the electrostatic interactions between the FBR basic cluster and the MR acidic cluster are sufficient to inhibit the interaction of the PACS‐1 FBR with a partner, 1H15N HSQC spectra of 15N‐cFBR294 were collected in the presence and absence of na‐HDAC6 CD1. The cFBR294 1H15N HSQC spectrum remained unchanged in the presence of HDAC6 CD1 (Fig. 6A), different from what was observed for the FBR and cFBR spectra in the presence of CD1, which exhibited a large degree of spectral broadening (compare Fig. 6A with Fig. 1A,C). Together, these data suggest that electrostatic interactions between the MR acidic cluster and the FBR basic cluster facilitate the MR folding back across the FBR, thereby regulating PACS‐1 protein–protein interactions, supporting the data of Scott et al. [19].

To confirm that electrostatics play an important role in PACS‐1 protein–protein interactions, glutamic acids located within the MR acidic cluster, 279EEEEE283, were replaced by alanines. Variants, EEAEE, EAAAE, and AAAAA, were generated and assessed by NMR for any structural impact (Figs 6B,C and S4) and for their ability to interact with HDAC6 CD1 (Fig. 6D–F). 1H15N HSQC spectra of 15N‐alanine substituted variants were collected and compared to the spectrum of wild‐type cFBR294 (Fig. S4). This analysis identified three key findings. First, CSDs between the wild‐type cFBR294 and the alanine variants are restricted to FBR resonances that were responsive to the presence of the wild‐type MR (Compare Figs 5B and 6B), demonstrating that the alanine substitutions do not create new contacts between the MR and the FBR. Second, increasing the number of alanine substitutions causes resonance positions to shift toward the position of those observed in the absence of the MR (Fig. S4). Finally, when the cFBR 1H15N HSQC spectrum is compared with that of the AAAAA cFBR294 (Figs 6C and S4D), CSDs between resonances of amino acids in the basic region are substantially reduced, while those associated with other parts of the FBR, specifically T106 and V142‐ L154, remain (Fig. 6C), indicating that the 279EEEEE283 patch cannot represent the sole determinant in the FBR/MR interaction. This aligns with reports that charge reversal substitutions E209K [26] and E211K [27] in PACS‐2, which are located outside of the acidic cluster in a CK2‐phosphorylatable region, result in enhanced interaction between the PACS‐1 paralog and some of its partners. Thus, other negative charges in the cFBR294 located in the corresponding region such as E288, E290, D293, and D294 might also contribute to the electrostatic interaction or may compensate for the loss of the 279EEEEE283 patch.

We also assessed whether any of the alanine substitution variants restored interaction with HDAC6 CD 1. 1H15N HSQC spectra of 15N EEAEE, EAAAE, and AAAAA‐substituted cFBR294 were recorded in the absence and presence of na‐HDAC6 CD1. Resonance broadening was observed in all variants' spectra (Fig. 6D–F) indicating that removal of negative charges from the acidic cluster partially restored the ability of the cFBR294 to interact with HDAC6 CD1, but did not prevent the MR from binding the FBR basic cluster (Fig. 6D–F). Interestingly, the affinity of the alanine substituted cFBR294s for CD1, as judged by the number or resonances experiencing chemical shift broadening, did not linearly correlate with the number of substitutions. The AAAAA substituted cFBR294 1H15N HSQC spectrum exhibited the largest number of broadened resonances (40), while the EEAEE and EAAAE‐substituted cFBR294 proteins exhibited comparable numbers (29 and 28 resonances, respectively; Fig. 6D–F). Together these data suggest that any change in the FBR/MR interaction may lead to aberrant PACS‐1/partner interaction.

The experimental NMR PACS‐1 structure (Fig. 3A) shows that the R203W substitution resides in the middle of a protruding loop that contains the basic cluster residues. Therefore, this substitution may disrupt the regulatory electrostatic interactions as well as induce hydrophobic protein–protein interactions exemplified by the observed dimerization of cR203W (Fig. 4). To examine this, 1H15N HSQC spectra of 15N‐cR203W294 were collected in the absence and presence of na‐HDAC6 CD1. Indeed, the observed resonance broadening indicates that the R203W substitution allowed the cFBR294 to interact with CD1 (Fig. 6G).

Charge reversal substitutions of glutamic acids neighboring the MR acidic cluster in PACS‐2 enhance interactions with partner proteins [26]. Therefore, we evaluated whether a charge reversal in the MR is sufficient to disrupt the intramolecular regulation of PACS proteins. The corresponding PACS‐2 E209K substitution, E288K, was introduced into the PACS‐1 cFBR294, and 1H15N HSQC spectra of 15N protein were collected in the absence and presence of na‐HDAC6 CD1. Although we observed some line broadening for a few resonances, the E288K substitution did not restore the PACS‐1/HDAC6 interaction to the same degree as any of the alanine substitutions in the basic cluster or the R203W syndrome‐causing substitution (Fig. 6H).

Unlike the 88% amino acid identity between the PACS‐1 and PACS‐2 FBRs, the MR, proximal to the FBR, exhibits more divergence and only possesses 58% identity. The PACS‐2 acidic cluster is notably disrupted by a tyrosine (PACS‐1 279EEEEE283: PACS‐2 200EEEYE207), which may result in other negative charges in the PACS‐2 MR having a greater contribution to the electrostatic interactions between the FBR and MR (Fig. 7A). To evaluate this possibility, 1H15N HSQC spectra of 15N‐PACS‐222‐213, the equivalent of the PACS1 cFBR294, with and without the E209K substitution were recorded in the absence and presence of the na‐SIRT1 N‐terminus (SIRT11‐233). PACS‐2 recognizes the SIRT1 N‐terminus through a bipartite binding site [22, 40]. Contrary to the PACS‐1/HDAC6 interaction, the presence of the PACS‐2 MR did not completely abrogate interaction with SIRT1 (Fig. 7B, C). However, the E209K variant exhibited more extensive resonance broadening indicating that it was less efficient at blocking the interaction (Fig. 7D). Since NMR studies necessitate relatively high concentrations of components, we posited that more pronounced effects may be observable under more dilute conditions. To this end, we supplemented our NMR studies with analytical gel filtration experiments. The SIRT1 N‐terminus eluted earlier from the column than PACS‐2 due to its unstructured nature despite comparable molecular masses (25 and 22 kDa respectively). Whether injected alone or in the presence of the SIRT1 N‐terminus, wild‐type PACS‐222‐213 eluted at the same position with only a minor decrease in peak intensity. However, the PACS‐222‐213E209K peak intensity decreased by 50% in the presence of SIRT1 with an intensity increase in the earlier eluting peak, where the complex also elutes (Fig. 7E). These data indicate that the negatively charged amino acids neighboring the MR acidic cluster also contribute to PACS protein intramolecular interaction.

The genetics behind disease manifestation are complex and poorly understood. This is supported by numerous GWAS that have examined thousands of genes to identify those strongly linked with specific diseases [13, 42, 43]. However, pinpointing causal variants remains challenging due to unknown mechanisms of action, linkage disequilibrium and small patient sample sizes [44, 45]. About 300 individuals with PACS‐1 syndrome have been identified with a few thousand predicted to exist worldwide [46]. The enzyme, HDAC6, has been identified as an effector for the pathogenesis associated with the PACS‐1 R203W substitution [10]. Its hyperactivity correlates with increased association with PACS‐1 R203W and causes significant disruption of the cytoskeleton and Golgi apparatus in neural cells, leading to several neural deficits in vivo [10]. Here, we provide direct physical evidence for the increased binding between the PACS‐1 R203W variant and HDAC6. The MR of PACS‐1 has been reported to modulate protein–protein interactions by interacting with the FBR, thereby preventing this domain from engaging in such interactions [19]. We structurally investigated this notion by determining the solution structure of the FBR (Fig. 3) and demonstrated that the N‐terminus of the MR interacts with one face of the FBR including the large positively charged loop on the opposite side of the domain (Fig. 5). The interaction of the MR acidic cluster with the basic loop was sufficient to block the interaction between the PACS‐1 FBR and HDAC6 CD1 (Fig. 6). These regulatory electrostatic contacts can be disrupted by removal of charged residues in either the FBR basic cluster or the MR acidic cluster. With respect to PACS‐1 syndrome, we specifically demonstrated that the R203W substitution in PACS‐1 permits the PACS‐1/HDAC6 interaction in the presence of the regulatory MR acidic cluster (Fig. 5). These findings suggest that PACS‐1 syndrome, which involves increased PACS‐1/HDAC6 interaction, results from a loss of an intramolecular autoregulatory interaction within the PACS‐1 protein.

Despite severe cellular consequences resulting from cytoskeletal disruption and Golgi dispersal, a surprising aspect of PACS‐1 syndrome is that the clinical features vary [9, 15, 16, 17, 47, 48, 49, 50], strongly suggesting that the PACS‐1/HDAC6 interaction is not the sole contributor to disease manifestation. PACS‐1, by virtue of its activity as a cellular cargo sorting protein [1], interacts with numerous partners [51], suggesting that its dysregulation might result in a multitude of effects. Our NMR analysis of cR203W revealed a substitution‐induced dimerization of the PACS‐1 FBR. The observed dimerization constant (K
d ~ 150 μm) reflects weak association, and a high ‘local effective concentration’ would be required if oligomerization were a pathogenic feature of the R203W substitution. Irrespective, the observed dimerization reveals that this pathogenic substitution allows PACS‐1 to interact with non‐canonical partners. Therefore, considering the diversity of the human genome, it is reasonable to suggest that the spectrum of clinical features experienced by PACS‐1 syndrome afflicted individuals, while primarily a manifestation of the PACS‐1/HDAC6 interaction, is a result of multiple PACS‐1/wild‐type partner and PACS‐1/R203W‐induced partner interactions specific to an individual's genetic makeup.

Our work regarding the R203W substitution in PACS‐1 is not only relevant for PACS‐1 syndrome. The human genome contains two PACS genes encoding PACS‐1 and PACS‐2. Mutations in both genes are associated with neurodevelopmental disorders exhibiting several overlapping symptoms. While the PACS‐1 pathogenic substitution is located in the basic loop of the FBR, the PACS‐2 pathogenic substitutions, E209K and E211K, are located just beyond the MR acidic cluster (200EEEYE204) in a region undergoing phosphorylation [26, 27]. We hypothesized that these additional charged residues also contribute to changes in intramolecular interactions for two reasons. In the original report by Olson et al. [26], enhanced immunoprecipitation was observed between the E209K‐substituted PACS‐2 and three partner proteins. Also, we observed that removing all negative charges from the PACS‐1 MR acidic cluster did not prevent the MR/FBR interaction (Figs 6 and S2). Indeed, in the PACS‐2 context, the E209K containing protein interacted more tightly with one of its partners, SIRT1. In contrast, the introduction of the equivalent change, E288K, into PACS‐1 did not affect the PACS‐1/HDAC6 interaction, as evidenced by our data for the alanine substitutions in the acidic cluster. Whether this is associated with the presence of a tyrosine in place of one of the glutamates in the PACS‐2 acidic cluster needs further investigation. Irrespectively, both PACS‐1 and PACS‐2 disease‐associated genetic changes result in PACS proteins in which the intramolecular interaction between the FBR basic loop and the acidic residues in the N‐terminus of the MR are perturbed.

While this manuscript demonstrates that the negatively charged amino acids in the MR, proximal to the FBR, are critical to PACS protein intramolecular regulation, phosphorylation of the serines in this region has previously been shown to play a critical role in PACS/partner interaction [19, 52]. Phosphorylation of S278 in PACS‐1, located immediately prior to the acidic cluster, enhances the PACS‐1/Nef interaction [19], while phosphorylation of S207, S208, and S213 in PACS‐2, located immediately C‐terminal to the acidic cluster, is critical for PACS‐2 association with the PKD2 (polycystic kidney disease 2) ion channel and their combined localization to mitochondria‐associated ER membranes (MAMs) [52]. Based on the findings in this manuscript, one would predict that serine phosphorylation in the MR, proximal to the acidic cluster, would repress PACS protein–protein interactions since the additional negative charge(s) would enhance electrostatic interactions with the FBR basic cluster. However, this is not the case. Therefore, PACS proteins likely rely on a third party that specifically recognizes the phosphoserine while being indifferent to the negative charge of an acidic cluster, such as an FHA (Forkhead‐Associated) domain [53], WW (tryptophan‐tryptophan) domain [54], or a BRCT (BRCA1 C‐terminus) domain [55]. Targeting the phosphoserine followed by subsequent interaction with the acidic cluster could act as a mechanism to free the basic loop and initiate PACS/partner interaction.

Finally, our work suggests a mechanism by which PACS proteins sample the proteome and discern their diverse partners. In multiple instances, PACS proteins recognize a bipartite binding site on their partner that is composed of an acidic cluster and hydrophobic residues in an α‐helix [21, 22] with the α‐helical amino acids comprising the higher affinity site [22]. In this manuscript, we demonstrated that the loop containing most of the basic cluster needs to be freed from intramolecular interaction with the MR to engage a partner, such as HDAC6. Our NMR structure identified this loop as 199
RYKNRTIL206. In the wild‐type sequence, positive charge essentially extends over the length of the loop apart from the last three amino acids, making electrostatics the primary mechanism for this loop to engage a partner. However, in the R203W substitution (199
RYKNWTIL206), the charges on the surface are reduced, with hydrophobic side chains becoming available for interaction and forming the dimerization interface (Fig. 4E). We propose that the basic loop probes for acidic clusters, sampling prospective partners, with a separate part of the FBR governing selectivity. Notably, the solution structure indicates that partner selectivity is not determined by a single region of the FBR. Mapping residues important for the PACS‐1/GGA3 interaction 249KIY251 [20] and the PACS‐1/Nef interaction 181HFLKRDANKLQIMLQ195 [21] onto our structure indicates that these regions lie on opposite sides of the β‐core.

Our structural analysis of the PACS‐1 FBR reveals that PACS‐1 protein–protein interactions are regulated through electrostatic interactions between a basic loop in the FBR and an acidic and post‐translationally modified part of the MR. Substitutions in these regions, such as the PACS‐1 syndrome causing R203W substitution, disrupt the electrostatic‐based intramolecular regulation and drive aberrant protein–protein interactions (Fig. 7F).
