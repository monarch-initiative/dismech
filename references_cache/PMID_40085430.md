# Variants in ATP6V0C are associated with Dravet-like developmental and epileptic encephalopathy.
**Authors:** Rong M, Marques PT, Ali QZ, Morcos R, Chandran I, Qaiser F, Møller RS, Bayat A, Rubboli G, Gardella E, Reuter MS, Sands TT, Scheffer IE, Schneider A, Poduri A, Wirrell E, Nabbout R, Sullivan J, Valente K, Auvin S, Knupp KG, Brunklaus A, Aledo-Serrano Á, Andrade DM
**Journal:** Epilepsia (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/epi.18346](https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.18346)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Dravet syndrome (DS) is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. Diagnosis is clinical, but ~90% of patients have pathogenic variants in SCN1A. ATP6V0C has recently been proposed as a novel candidate gene for epilepsy, with or without developmental delay. Here we describe two adult patients with a clinical diagnosis of DS associated with ATP6V0C variants.

METHODS: Patients with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies were evaluated by physicians who are experts in DS, and their clinical diagnosis was correlated with genetic findings. A subgroup of those patients with DS but without known genetic causes were evaluated through gene panels, whole exome sequencing, and chromosome microarray. Phenotype was determined by pediatric and adult chart reviews, interviews, and physical examinations.

RESULTS: Of 753 patients with DS, two unrelated individuals with classic features of DS during childhood and adulthood were identified with heterozygous de novo missense variants in ATP6V0C (c.319G > C, p.(Gly107Arg) and c.284C > T, p.(Ala95Val), respectively). Both variants were absent in normal populations and computational prediction algorithms suggested deleterious effects on protein structure and/or function. No disease-causing variants in other genes previously associated with DS were found.

SIGNIFICANCE: Here we describe two adult patients with Dravet-like syndrome and pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in ATP6V0C. We propose that abnormal ATP6V0C function can, at the severe end of the clinical spectrum, be associated with Dravet-like phenotype. This is relevant, as these patients would not qualify for disease-modifying antisense nucleotide or gene therapies targeting SCN1A.


Key points
Pathogenic variants in ATP6V0C can lead to a Dravet‐like phenotype.The clinical manifestations in adulthood are also in keeping with the features seen in adults with classic Dravet syndrome.It is important to clarify the ATP6V0C etiology in Dravet‐like cases, as these patients may not qualify for certain precision therapies.

Dravet Syndrome (DS) (OMIM#607208) is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) characterized by refractory seizures, motor dysfunction, and intellectual disability (ID).
1
,
2
,
3
 DS is caused by pathogenic SCN1A variants in up to 90% of patients.
4
,
5




ATP6V0C is a gene that encodes a protein involved in proton gradient generation and pH regulation.
6
 Recently, ATP6V0C variants have been associated with early onset epilepsy with or without developmental delay (OMIM#108745).
7
 Only four adults (i.e., over 18 years of age) with ATP6V0C variants have been described in the literature thus far.
8



Here we report two adult patients with a DS‐like phenotype and de novo pathogenic and likely pathogenic (P/LP) ATP6V0C variants. We propose that variants in ATP6V0C are associated with SCN1A‐negative DS‐like DEE, thus extending the adult phenotype of patients with ATP6V0C‐related disorders.

Specialized genetic epilepsies and/or Dravet syndrome clinics were surveyed. Patients with DS phenotype and no genetic cause were further evaluated with multigene panels, genome or exome sequencing, and chromosomal microarray (CMA).

The two patients reported below had their ATP6V0C variants identified through trio exome sequencing using Agilent SureSelect Clinical Research Exome V1 enrichment followed by 2 × 100 bp paired end sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq6000 platform to an average depth of coverage of at least 100×. Genome alignment (Build GRCh38/hg38) and small sequence variant and copy number variant analysis was performed with the Illumina Dragen Enrichment analysis platform. Copy number variants were detected using CytoScan HD SNP Array (Affymetrix). Variants were interpreted according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.
9



Informed consent was obtained from patients' parents. Ethics approval was granted by the University Health Network Research Ethics Board. Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available by request from any qualified investigator.

Together, 753 patients with a phenotype compatible with DS3 were identified. Of those 753 patients with DS, 25 (3.4%) did not have a known SCN1A P/LP variant, nor did they have variants in genes associated with Dravet‐like syndromes such as PCHD19. Two unrelated patients who are SCN1A‐negative were found to carry P/LP variants in the ATP6V0C gene and are described in detail below.

Patient 1 (P1) is male born to non‐consanguineous parents, following normal pregnancy and delivery. The first seizure was prolonged febrile bilateral tonic‐clonic (BTC) at 11 months. Afebrile seizures (myoclonic, focal with impaired awareness [later associated with falls], focal tonic, and atypical absence) started after 14 months and persisted until the present day. He had several episodes of status epilepticus (often initially associated with fever) requiring induced coma. Seizures triggered by hyperthermia were less common after the age of 20 years.

Currently, 31‐year‐old P1 has four types of seizures: focal motor seizures with impaired awareness showing eye deviation to the left, accompanied by automatisms; series of myoclonic jerks that could evolve into BTC seizures; BTC without preceding myoclonic jerks and tonic seizures. All seizures last ~1 min or less and happen during sleep, once or twice a week (except for daily myoclonic jerks). He has tried several antiseizure medications (ASMs), including carbamazepine and phenytoin until the age of 4 years. His seizure frequency improved after switching from those two drugs to valproate. Electroencephalography (EEG) recordings and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are described in Table 1.

P1 had normal early development and independent walking at 11 months. Developmental delay was documented at age 3 years, although it was present before that. After the age of 4 years, his development plateaued. No regression was observed.

At age 31 years, he had autism spectrum disorder and severe ID, speaking only 1‐ to 2‐word sentences. He had behavioral problems starting from childhood, with aggression requiring antipsychotic drugs. Truncal hypotonia was documented at age 15 years in addition to crouch gait with everted feet. By age 16 years, he had hesitancy when going up and down the stairs and difficulties getting into vehicles. He was diagnosed with parkinsonism, which may be in part caused by antipsychotics.

Trio exome sequencing at age 29 years showed heterozygous de novo missense variant [c.319G > C, p.(Gly107Arg)] in ATP6V0C. The variant has not been reported previously, is absent in normal populations, and located at the trans‐membrane region 3 (TMR‐3). Prediction tools including REVEL score and CADD score suggest that the variant has deleterious effects on protein structure and/or function. No variants in SCN1A or other genes associated with DS and any other disease were identified. CMA also ruled out copy number variants.

Patient 2 (P2) is a 27‐year‐old man born to non‐consanguineous parents. Pregnancy and delivery were normal. At age 4 months, he had his first prolonged febrile BTC seizure and continued to have febrile BTC seizures throughout his life. P2 presented unilateral clonic (hemiconvulsions) seizures lasting 1–2 min sometimes followed by post‐ictal paresis in his first year of life. Afebrile seizures started at age 3 years. P2 had clusters of BTC seizures 2–3 days a week, with a frequency of 7–30 seizures per cluster, sometimes leading to status epilepticus (SE). Other seizure types recorded over the years included myoclonic, focal motor onset with impaired awareness (later associated with falls), focal tonic, and atypical absence seizures. In adulthood, P2 has focal tonic seizures during sleep, occurring once per month. Currently he is taking valproate, levetiracetam, and lamotrigine. A trial of zonisamide led to worsening seizure frequency.

He had normal early development, and independent walking was observed at age 15 months. P2 also had a slowing in developmental milestones and plateaued at the age of 4 years, with language and fine motor skills being most affected. No regression was noted.

At age 27 years, P2 had autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and severe ID. P2 also had behavioral problems starting from childhood, including aggression requiring antipsychotic drugs. Later in life he developed parkinsonism.

MRI during childhood and adulthood were normal. Trio exome sequencing at age 27 years identified a heterozygous de novo pathogenic missense variant [c.284C > T, p.(Ala95Val)] in ATP6V0C. This variant is absent in the general population and is located at trans‐membrane region 3 (TMR‐3). Prediction tools including REVEL score and CADD score suggest the variant has deleterious effects on protein structure and/or function. No variants in SCN1A or other genes associated with DS, or other diseases, were identified. CMA also ruled out copy number variants.

In both patients, neither exome sequencing nor CMA identified other pathogenic variants. P1 had a novel likely pathogenic ATP6V0C variant. The pathogenic variant seen in P2 (p.Ala95Val) has been reported previously in a patient with seizure onset at 5 months of age and a similar course: febrile, then afebrile BTCs, absences, myoclonic, and nocturnal tonic seizures in adulthood.
8
 That patient had normal MRI, background slowing and multifocal epileptiform activity, and moderate ID at age 25 years.

Another patient with DS was shown previously to have a de novo frameshift variant in ATP6V0C and variants in NKAIN3 and SLC8A1.
15
 At the time, ATP6V0C was not known to cause human disease and the variant could not be linked to DS. To date, pathogenic variants in NKAIN3 and SLC8A1 have not been associated with epilepsy and ID, suggesting that the patient's symptoms were indeed associated with ATP6V0C.
15
 Like our patients, that 8‐year‐old male had febrile seizures beginning before 12 months (BTCs) and developed focal, myoclonic, and tonic seizures. However, he later developed atonic seizures, which is different from patients reported here and uncommon in DS.

Variants in ATP6V0C have been linked to epilepsy, often associated with developmental/intellectual delay in pediatric patients.

The exact mechanism by which abnormal ATP6V0C function leads to epilepsy and DEE phenotype is unclear. Recent studies suggest that ATP6V0C is highly intolerant to missense variants. Knockdown models of ATP6V0C show decreased V‐ATPase activity in yeast, and impaired motor function, growth, and lifespan in worms with increased duration of seizure‐like behavior.
8

As a retrospective study, the two adult patients were not examined by the authors when they were children. Chart description of signs and symptoms were in keeping with what is observed in typical cases of DS. Variants were interpreted according to established guidelines.
9
 However, functional studies of the consequences on a protein level were not performed. In addition, not all patients who are SCN1A negative had a complete genomic investigation; as such, the incidence of patients with ATP6V0C P/LP variants leading to a DS‐like phenotype cannot be estimated.

The identification of P/LP ATP6V0C variants in two unrelated adult patients with typical DS‐like phenotype expands the clinical spectrum of ATP6V0C‐associated epilepsy to include DS‐like DEE. This finding is important particularly as those patients would not qualify for Dravet syndrome disease‐modifying antisense nucleotide or gene therapies targeting SCN1A.
