# MITOCHONDRIAL SKELETAL DISORDERS PROVIDE INSIGHT INTO THE EFFECT OF MITOCHONDRIAL PROTEOSTATIC STRESS ON STEROIDOGENESIS

## Content

Mitochondria are best known for their role in energy production, and impairments in this essential function are generally thought to cause mitochondrial disease, which typically affects organs and tissues with high energy demand. However, increasing evidence shows that impairments to other critical mitochondrial functions such as lipid metabolism, and steroidogenesis can also contribute to mitochondrial diseases. In this thesis, we identified a group of mitochondrial diseases where mitochondrial protein homeostasis is disrupted, which we termed mitochondrial skeletal disorders, as the patients exhibited phenotypes such as skeletal dysplasia, short stature, and cataracts. In addition, further investigation identified a potential mechanism by which altered mitochondrial protein homeostasis and increased steroidogenesis may contribute to these patient phenotypes. First, we identified novel pathogenic variants in phosphatidylserine decarboxylase (PISD) gene from patients with short stature, neurodevelopmental issues, and cataracts. We demonstrated that the variants impair function and stability of PISD and are likely causative of their disease. We also identified evidence for impaired activity of inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) proteases, suggesting dysfunctions in mitochondrial protein homeostasis. To further investigate the mechanism by which impaired mitochondrial proteostasis may lead to these phenotypes, we tested the hypothesis that impaired mitochondrial proteostasis can lead to overproduction of the stress hormone cortisol, as this could potentially explain the patients’ phenotypes described above. Using both pharmacological and genetic stresses to impair mitochondrial proteostasis, we showed impaired mitochondrial protein import activates STARD1, a mitochondrial protein crucial in steroidogenesis. We then showed that increased cortisol production is STARD1 dependent and follows a biphasic response to mitochondrial stress. Overall, these findings expand our current understanding of mitochondrial disease and link mitochondrial quality control and steroidogenesis as potential disease mechanisms.

Abstract 
Mitochondria are best known for their role in energy production, and impairments in 
this essential function are generally thought to cause mitochondrial disease, which typically 
affects organs and tissues with high energy demand. However, increasing evidence shows 
that impairments to other critical mitochondrial functions such as lipid metabolism, and 
steroidogenesis can also contribute to mitochondrial diseases. In this thesis, we identified a 
group of mitochondrial diseases where mitochondrial protein homeostasis is disrupted, which 
we termed mitochondrial skeletal disorders, as the patients exhibited phenotypes such as 
skeletal dysplasia, short stature, and cataracts. In addition, further investigation identified a 
potential mechanism by which altered mitochondrial protein homeostasis and increased 
steroidogenesis may contribute to these patient phenotypes.  
First, we identified novel pathogenic variants in phosphatidylserine decarboxylase 
(PISD) gene from patients with short stature, neurodevelopmental issues, and cataracts. We 
demonstrated that the variants impair function and stability of PISD and are likely causative 
of their disease. We also identified evidence for impaired activity of inner mitochondrial 
membrane (IMM) proteases, suggesting dysfunctions in mitochondrial protein homeostasis. 
To further investigate the mechanism by which impaired mitochondrial proteostasis may lead 
to these phenotypes, we tested the hypothesis that impaired mitochondrial proteostasis can 
lead to overproduction of the stress hormone cortisol, as this could potentially explain the 
patients’ phenotypes described above. Using both pharmacological and genetic stresses to 
impair mitochondrial proteostasis, we showed impaired mitochondrial protein import activates 
STARD1, a mitochondrial protein crucial in steroidogenesis. We then showed that increased 
cortisol production is STARD1 dependent and follows a biphasic response to mitochondrial 
stress. Overall, these findings expand our current understanding of mitochondrial disease 
and link mitochondrial quality control and steroidogenesis as potential disease mechanisms.

Chapter 1  Introduction 
Mitochondrial diseases  
Mitochondrial disease is a heterogeneous collection of disorders resulting from 
primary mitochondrial dysfunction due to mutations in either the nuclear genome or the 
mitochondrial genome (mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)) (1,2). Historically, mitochondrial 
diseases are associated with defects in oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production, with 
clinical manifestations that tend to be progressive, multisystem, and often affect energy-
demanding tissues such as the brain, nerve, and muscle. Moreover, due to the well-
recognized role of mitochondria in energy production, it has long been the case that many or 
most mitochondrial disorders are conceived of as primary disorders of cellular energy 
production. However, the advent of next generation sequencing has revolutionized how we 
interpret the characteristics of a mitochondrial disease and has led to the identification of 
many novel pathogenic variants in mitochondrial proteins (2–4), many of which do not 
necessarily lead to classical clinical phenotypes of mitochondrial disease (3–6). For example, 
disorders of the bones and joints have recently been characterized as one of the uncommon 
phenotypes of patients presenting with mitochondrial disease (7). Thus, our understanding of 
mitochondrial dysfunction in human disease continues to evolve. Here, we define 
mitochondrial disease as (i) genetic diseases in which the affected gene product primarily 
functions in the mitochondrion, and (ii) excludes disorders primarily belonging to another 
well-defined category of inborn error (e.g., fatty-acid oxidation, urea cycle, organic acids, and 
amino acids catabolism). 
Although abnormal cellular bioenergetics are certainly evident in many mitochondrial 
diseases, energy deficiency itself cannot fully account for the marked heterogeneity of 
mitochondrial disease phenotypes (5). Notably, mitochondria have many other important 

2 
 
 
cellular roles, impairment of which can also contribute to disease. Some of these other 
mitochondrial functions include mediating apoptosis, calcium signaling, regulating innate 
immunity, and participation in a variety of metabolic pathways such as steroidogenesis and 
nucleotide synthesis. Exactly how impairment of these other mitochondrial functions impacts 
human pathology and contributes to mitochondrial disease remains largely unresolved. 
To begin to answer this question, this thesis will look at mitochondrial dysfunction in 
the context of impaired mitochondrial protein homeostasis. 
 
Mitochondrial protein homeostasis 
Part of the phenotypic complexity that characterizes mitochondrial disorders arises 
from the mitochondrion’s proteomic complexity. While the mtDNA encodes thirteen proteins 
that are translated by a dedicated mitochondrial translation machinery, the remaining ~1500 
nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins are translated in the cytosol and imported into 
mitochondria. Nuclear mitochondrial protein expression and import must be coordinated with 
mtDNA gene expression, in particular for macromolecular complexes such as mitochondrial 
ribosomes and oxidative phosphorylation subunits (i.e., complexes I, III, IV, and V), which 
are comprised of subunits encoded by both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. In addition 
to import and translation, proper folding and protein degradation contribute to maintaining 
mitochondrial protein homeostasis. As such, mitochondria have a set of chaperones to 
ensure proper folding, as well as a dedicated set of proteases that can degrade damaged or 
unwanted protein (8). Notably, many of these chaperones and proteases are also involved in 
mitochondrial protein import. 
Impaired mitochondrial protein homeostasis can lead to activation of the so-called 
mitochondrial unfolded protein response (mtUPR). Though first described in mammalian 
cells (9,10), the mtUPR was characterized initially in C. elegans (11). Upon the accumulation 

3 
 
 
of unfolded proteins in the matrix, protein import is reduced. Subsequently, the activating 
transcription factor associated with stress-1 (ATFS-1), which is normally imported into 
mitochondria for degradation, can go to the nucleus and upregulate a spate of genes, 
including various mitochondrial chaperones and proteases, to deal with the initial stress. An 
analogous situation appears conserved in mammalian cells, though it is still not fully 
understood, the transcriptional response appears to be part of a larger cellular integrated 
stress response (ISR) that responds to a variety of cellular and mitochondrial stresses (12). 
In mammalian cells, ATF5, a transcription factor that is a functional ortholog of C. elegans 
ATFS-1, is also reported to regulate the expression of mitochondrial protein homeostasis 
machinery (13). Notably, a fraction of ATF5 is reported to localize to mitochondria, 
suggesting a similar organelle partitioning mechanism as ATFS-1 in C. elegans. Additionally, 
other transcription factors such as ATF4 and CHOP are also implicated in mediating the 
mammalian mtUPR/ISR (8,9). With respect to disease, the mtUPR/ISR is activated under 
conditions of cardiac stress (10), and pharmacological induction of the mtUPR/ISR requires 
ATF5 and is cardioprotective (11). Nonetheless, how the mtUPR/ISR is activated by and 
impacts mitochondrial diseases remains to be fully elucidated. 
Activating transcription factor 5 (ATF5) is an example of a sensing mechanism using 
mitochondrial protein import. ATF5 contains both a mitochondrial targeting sequence and 
nucleus targeting sequence. Normally ATF5 is localized to mitochondria, but when protein 
import stalls, ATF5 can localize to nucleus and activate genes that is responsible for mtUPR 
(13). Similarly, the PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin pathway, which is 
implicated in Parkinson disease (PD), also depends on mitochondrial import for activation. 
Mitochondrial stress sensor PINK1 normally gets degraded after partially import to 
mitochondria. Under mitochondrial proteostatic stress, PINK1 accumulates on the OMM due 
to failure of import. The accumulation of PINK1 activates its kinase activity to phosphorylate 
Parkin, which targets mitochondria for mitophagy (14,15).It appears that maintaining a

Mitochondrial protein import  
Several interrelated mitochondrial import pathways target proteins to the outer 
mitochondrial membrane (OMM), the intermembrane space (IMS), the inner mitochondrial 
membrane (IMM), and the matrix (16) (Fig 1.2). Generally, mitochondrial protein import is 
initiated via the translocase of outer membrane (TOM) complex. From the TOM complex, 
proteins can be inserted into the OMM, with single-spanning and multi-spanning OMM 
proteins entering through the MIM (mitochondrial import machinery) complex, and β-barrel 
proteins assembled via the sorting and assembly machinery (SAM) complex. Proteins 
destined for the IMS can be refolded via a disulfide relay system in the IMS known as the 
mitochondrial import and assembly (MIA) pathway that mediates proper folding of cysteine-
rich proteins. Meanwhile, following transfer through the TOM complex, insertion into the IMM 
and the matrix occurs via the translocase of inner membrane (TIM) complexes. The TIM23 
complex recognizes proteins with a mitochondrial targeting sequence and can import 
proteins to the matrix, IMM, or IMS. Finally, the TIM22 complex imports multi-spanning 
proteins into the IMM. 
More than half of the nuclear encoded mitochondrial proteins are targeted to 
mitochondria via an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) composed of an 
amphipathic alpha-helix which contains both polar and non-polar amino acid residues.  This 
feature allows in MTS to interact with both polar group-heads of the lipids and the non-polar 
tail of the lipids in mitochondrial membranes. Mitochondria have a set of proteases that 
mediate the cleavage and removal of the MTS, further linking mitochondrial proteases and 
import. These proteases are important for maintaining protein homeostasis and mediating 
mitochondrial protein import. In the matrix, the mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP) 
performs the initial cleavage of the MTS (17). The MPP is composed of two protein subunits. 
The non-catalytic MPPα subunit, encoded by PMPCA, is involved in substrate recognition 
and binding. Meanwhile, the MPPβ subunit, encoded by PMPCB, comprises the catalytic 

7 
 
 
subunit. Critically, MPP cleavage can result in unstable intermediate proteins due to 
destabilizing N-terminal amino acids according to the N-end rule which induce ubiquitylation 
and proteasomal degradation of the protein (18). To counteract this instability issue, 
mitochondria harbor two additional distinct proteases that can mediate additional processing 
events. The first protease is the MIP (mitochondrial intermediate presequence) protease, 
which is encoded by MIPEP. MIP, also known as Oct1, removes an additional octapeptide 
after MPP cleavage. The second enzyme is an X-pro-aminopeptidase, encoded by X-Prolyl 
Aminopeptidase 3 (XPNPEP3), which removes a single N-terminal amino acid. 
Approximately ~25% of the imported mitochondrial proteins that are cleaved by MPP 
undergo a secondary cleavage. Notably, cleavage events mediated by both MIPEP and 
XPNPEP3 are implicated in the activation of CLPP, a subunit of the mitochondrial CLPXP 
protease that contributes to proper mitochondrial protein homeostasis (19,20). In the IMM, 
the inner membrane peptidase (IMP) complex cleaves the MTS from proteins that are 
partially inserted into the IMM, mediating their release into the IMS. The IMP complex is 
made up of two proteolytically active subunits, IMP1 and IMP2, encoded by IMMP1L and 
IMMP2L, respectively (21). Next, we will discuss how pathogenic variants in genes important 
for mitochondrial proteostasis have similar pathogenic features that suggest a common 
mechanistic underpinning.  
Mitochondrial skeletal disorders  
The recognition of skeletal features in patients with pathogenic mutations in Lon 
Peptidase 1, Mitochondrial (LONP1) and Heat Shock Protein Family A (Hsp70) Member 9 
(HSPA9) led to an initial grouping of these syndromes as ‘mitochondrial 
chaperonopathies’(22–24), as these genes encode a protease and chaperone, respectively. 
Subsequently, Apoptosis Inducing Factor Mitochondria Associated 1 (AIFM1) was also 
suggested as a mitochondrial chaperonopathy gene, as mutations in this gene, encoding a 

8 
 
 
protein implicated in OxPhos complex assembly, had similar phenotypes (25,26). However, 
as discussed below, pathogenic variants causing skeletal phenotypes have also been found 
in other mitochondrial proteins that do not encode chaperones (Table 1.1). Moreover, there 
are pathogenic variants in other mitochondrial chaperones and proteases that do not cause 
overt skeletal phenotypes (reviewed in Table 1.2). Thus, the term mitochondrial 
chaperonopathy does not fit for some of these skeletal disorders. Instead, we refer to these 
disorders here as ‘mitochondrial skeletal disorders’ since the unique and unifying feature of 
these groups of diseases are the skeletal dysplasia phenotype. Here, we will review key 
genes that are implicated in mitochondrial skeletal disorders to give insights on the 
dysfunction.

LONP1 and CODAS (Cerebral, Ocular, Dental, Auricular, and Skeletal 
Syndrome) 
LONP1 encodes a multi-functional member of the AAA+ superfamily of ATPases, 
which is localized to the mitochondrial matrix where it has protease, chaperone, and mtDNA-
binding activities (27,28). In addition to maintaining general protein homeostasis, LONP1 
regulates mtDNA stability and expression via processing of Transcription Factor A, 
Mitochondrial (TFAM) (22) and Mitochondrial Transcription Termination Factor 3 (MTERF3) 
(29). Notably, LONP1 physically associates with MPP, potentially accounting for the role 
LONP1 plays in processing newly imported mitochondrial proteins (29). Although 
constitutively expressed, LONP1 is upregulated during acute cellular stresses including heat 
shock and oxidative damage, and is a key mediator of the mtUPR/ISR (29). 
Cerebral, Ocular, Dental, Auricular, and Skeletal Syndrome (CODAS) was initially 
described in 1991 as an autosomal recessive disorder encompassing a distinctive set of 
phenotypes including spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, short stature, dental anomalies, 
cataracts, hearing loss, developmental delay, and variable dysmorphism. (30). However, 
LONP1 was only identified as a causative gene in 2015 (22,23). The identification of a 
mitochondrial gene was unexpected for a developmental dysmorphology disorder, as severe 
skeletal phenotypes (i.e., spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia) had not been recognized previously 
in mitochondrial diseases. As with many mitochondrial diseases, the phenotype of patients 
with LONP1 pathogenic variants varies widely. Since the initial identification of LONP1 as the 
causative gene for CODAS, reports have emerged with additional LONP1 variants 
associated with an expanding set of clinical phenotypes (31–35). These presentations vary 
from milder presentations that include only cataracts with minimal extraocular findings (33), 
to an atypical presentation more similar to Marinesco-Sjogren syndrome (31), and even 
classic mitochondrial disease phenotypes associated with mtDNA depletion (32,34,35). 
While LONP1 pathogenic variants are generally recessive, a heterozygous gain-of-function 

12 
 
 
variant exhibiting excessive proteolytic activity and loss of chaperone function was recently 
proposed to be the cause of a neonatal-onset disorder characterized by epilepsy, 
pachygyria, diffuse progressive white matter atrophy, global developmental delay, and death 
in infancy (36). 
This phenotypic variability raises an interesting question about how LONP1 
dysfunction leads to disease. Described LONP1 variants causing CODAS in humans are 
generally substitutions or small, in-frame deletions in the conserved ATP-binding and 
proteolytic domains of LONP1 (31,35). Moreover, LONP1 knockout mice exhibit embryonic 
lethality (27). Thus, it is likely that this protein is essential, which suggests that patients with 
LONP1 variants must retain some LONP1 activity. Therefore, it is possible that the severity 
of phenotypes associated with LONP1 variants may be linked to the severity of the 
dysfunction imparted by the sequence changes and/or impairments in substrate recognition 
caused by the various LONP1 variants. Further supporting this notion is the observation that 
the R721G and P676S variants causing CODAS do not affect TFAM degradation in an in 
vitro assay, but do affect the degradation of another LONP1 target, STARD1 (22). Moreover, 
basal mitochondrial respiration in patient fibroblasts with the R721G variant causing classic 
CODAS is largely unaffected, with only a slight decrease in spare respiratory capacity, 
suggesting that a major defect in oxidative phosphorylation may not be the driving force of 
pathogenicity for CODAS. Meanwhile, the Y565H variant linked to mtDNA depletion reduces 
the ability of LONP1 to bind and degrade TFAM (32), a master regulator of mtDNA. This 
difference in substrate recognition may explain the mtDNA depletion and more severe 
subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction in oxidative phosphorylation.

HSPA9 and EVEN-PLUS (Epiphyseal, Vertebral, and Ocular Changes Plus 
Associated Findings of Severe Microtia, Nasal Hypoplasia, and Other 
Malformations) 
HSPA9 encodes heat shock protein A9 (HSPA9/mtHsp70/Mortalin/Grp75), a 
chaperone that functions in folding mitochondrial matrix proteins, but has also been 
attributed to peroxisomal regulation (37), amongst other cellular localizations and functions 
(38). HSPA9 interacts with hydrophobic portions of unfolded proteins to prevent protein 
aggregation, and associates with the TIM23 import machinery to assist in mitochondrial 
import into the matrix. (39,40). Notably, HSPA9 interacts with LONP1 and is important for 
mediating its proteolytic activity (24,39), and is also upregulated by the mtUPR/ISR (41). 
Recessive compound heterozygous or homozygous variants in HSPA9 cause EVEN-
PLUS syndrome, which is characterized by skeletal phenotypes resembling CODAS, with 
additional findings (24). The two HSPA9 variants initially linked to EVEN-PLUS syndrome are 
found in the nucleotide binding domain of HSPA9. Biochemically, both variants reduce ATP 
hydrolysis required for chaperone activity of the protein and decrease the stability of the 
protein (42). More recently, an additional EVEN-PLUS patient with novel variants in HSPA9 
has been identified, adding to the clinical variability of the disorder (43). Meanwhile, variants 
in HSPA9 have also been associated with sideroblastic anemia from recessive (or pseudo-
dominant) mutations (44) and are potentially associated with PD (45). 
 
AIFM1 and SEMD-HL (Spondyloepimetaphyseal Dysplasia, X-linked, with 
Hypomyelinating Leukodystrophy) 
Apoptosis-inducing factor (AIFM1) encodes a protein first identified for its role in 
promoting apoptosis when released from the mitochondrial IMS (46). However, AIFM1 also 
has other distinct functions in the mitochondria. For example, AIFM1 has a FAD-dependent 

14 
 
 
NADH-oxidase activity that functions in the assembly of mitochondrial complexes I and III 
(25,26,47). In addition, AIFM1 contains an HSP70 binding domain (48). Similar to HSPA9 
and LONP1, AIFM1 is also involved in protein import, as it interacts functionally with GFER 
and affects the stability of MIA40 (49–51), two key proteins that mediate protein import to 
IMS via the MIA pathway. 
Recently, SEMD-HL was reported as another mitochondrial disorder linking skeletal 
phenotypes with mitochondrial dysfunction resembling CODAS syndrome and EVEN-PLUS 
(52,53). SEMD-HL is characterized by spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia, midface 
hypoplasia, and hypomyelinating leukodystrophy with progressive neurodegeneration of the 
central and peripheral nervous system. The X-linked recessive AIFM1 variant causing 
SEMD-HL occurs in the middle of AIFM1′s FAD-binding domain (53). 
In addition to SEMD-HL, pathogenic AIFM1 variants have been associated with other 
syndromes (54). These diseases include: Cowchock syndrome (X-linked Charcot–Marie–
Tooth disease, CMTX4) characterized by axonal sensorimotor neuropathy, deafness, and 
cognitive impairment (25,55,56), X-linked recessive sensorineural hearing loss with sensory 
polyneuropathy (57) (also described in milder form as X-linked dominant (58)), cerebellar 
ataxia (59,60), and mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, ranging from mild (61,62) to severe 
phenotypes (26,63–66). These different syndromes associated with distinct variants are 
reminiscent of the situation with LONP1 and HSPA9, suggesting that the different variants 
either have different severity or affect different functions/substrates of AIFM1 (25). 
 
PAM16 and SMDMDM (Spondylometaphyseal Dysplasia, Megarbane–Dagher–
Melki Type) 
PAM16 (presequence translocase-associated motor 16), also known as MAGMAS 
(mitochondria-associated granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor-signaling), is a 

15 
 
 
critical mediator of mitochondrial protein import into the mitochondrial matrix via the TIM23 
complex (67,68). PAM16 functions in a complex with the J-type domains of DNAJ homologs, 
which stimulate the ATPase activity of HSPA9 (69,70). Thus, in addition to a clear functional 
link to HSPA9, PAM16 is primarily involved in mitochondrial protein import. 
The Megarbane–Dagher–Melki type of spondylometaphyseal dysplasia (SMDMDM) 
was first described in two siblings with chondrodysplasia, short stature, developmental delay, 
and cardiomyopathy who died before the age of two (71). Later, a second family with similar 
phenotypes was described (72), and a homozygous missense variant in PAM16 (N76D) was 
identified in both families (73). More recently, a slightly milder phenotype of SMDMDM was 
also reported in another patient harboring a nearby variant in PAM16 (Q74P) (74). 
 
IARS2 and CAGSSS (Cataracts, Growth Hormone Deficiency, Sensory 
Neuropathy, Sensorineural Hearing Loss, Skeletal Dysplasia Syndrome) 
IARS2 encodes the mitochondrial isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase, an enzyme that 
attaches the isoleucyl amino acid to its cognate tRNA for translation of mitochondrial-
encoded proteins. The first report of CAGSSS, an autosomal recessive syndrome with 
cataracts, short-stature secondary to growth hormone deficiency, hearing loss, peripheral 
neuropathy, and skeletal dysplasia, was described in three patients with variants in IARS2 
(75). As skeletal dysplasias had not previously been described in association with 
mitochondrial dysfunction, the initial description of skeletal features was questioned as 
possibly secondary, or due to a concurrent disorder (76). However, as discussed above with 
CODAS and EVEN-PLUS, and the growing list of mitochondrial diseases presented in this 
review, skeletal features are gaining traction as a feature of mitochondrial disease. 
Moreover, it is now clear that pathogenic variants in IARS2 are indeed causative and directly 
linked to the skeletal dysplasia phenotype, as multiple examples of homozygous and 

16 
 
 
compound heterozygous missense mutations have been described (77–81). Additional 
features of note described in these patients include neurodevelopmental delay and seizures. 
Interestingly, the majority of reported mutations in IARS2 are either intronic or missense. 
Hence, it is likely that IARS2 is essential for human functioning and complete loss of its 
function is lethal. Somewhat surprisingly, no obvious defects in mitochondrial translation 
were evident in patient fibroblasts harboring IARS2 variants (75). 
Many of the conditions discussed above are caused by pathogenic variants in genes 
directly involved in mitochondrial protein homeostasis, including mitochondrial chaperones, 
proteases, or protein import factors. However, it is less obvious how mitochondrial tRNA 
aminoacylation (IARS2) might also link to mitochondrial protein homeostasis or mitochondrial 
import. Understanding how these processes may be connected could provide insight into the 
mechanistic underpinnings of the skeletal phenotypes in mitochondrial disease patients.  
 
PISD – A novel mitochondrial skeletal disorder? 
Recently we discovered novel compound heterozygous variants in the PISD gene via 
exome sequencing. The patients carrying these PISD variants exhibit congenital cataracts, 
short stature, skeletal dysplasia and white matter changes reminiscent of the mitochondrial 
skeletal disorders described above. The PISD gene encodes an enzyme that localizes to the 
IMM, where it converts phosphatidylserine to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) a critical lipid 
(For more details on phospholipid synthesis see Fig 1.3) that is important for mitochondrial 
function (82–85). Complete loss of PISD is embryonic lethal in mice, highlighting the 
importance of mitochondrial PE(82). In cellular models, severe depletion or complete loss of 
phosphatidylserine decarboxylase (PISD) results in decreased mitochondrial oxidative 
phosphorylation and fragmentation of the mitochondrial network (82,83). While PISD is not a 
protease or chaperone that directly involves in mitochondrial protein homeostasis, altered 

17 
 
 
IMM lipids composition (namely lack of PE) can affect many crucial proteins embedded and 
associated with IMM. PE is an important structural lipid due to its cone-like shape with a 
small headgroup. This shape helps lipid packing in highly curved membrane regions in the 
IMM. Notably, many mitochondrial-specific proteases such as OMA1 (86) and PARL (87) are 
bound to the IMM and can potentially be affected by changes in IMM composition. In Ch. 3, 
we report the first example of patients with novel pathogenic variants in PISD. We also 
investigated effects of PISD on mitochondrial protein homeostasis by examining disruption of 
mitochondrial protease functions and its consequences.

A common mechanistic basis for key features of mitochondrial 
skeletal disorders 
Given the core features of short stature, skeletal dysplasia, and cataracts in 
mitochondrial skeletal disorders, which can all be linked to impaired mitochondrial 
proteostasis, we wondered if there could be a common underlying mechanism that might 
explain these pathologies. In this regard, it is notable that elevated levels of the steroid 
hormone cortisol can cause short stature, skeletal dysplasia, and cataracts. For example, 
glucocorticoid therapy is a common cause of faltering growth in children (88), while Cushing 
syndrome, which is caused by excessive glucocorticoids, can also cause growth retardation 
and skeletal dysplasia similar to mitochondrial skeletal disorder patients (89,90). Additional 
common side effects of glucocorticoids include cataracts (91) and cardiomyopathy (92), 
which we also observe in some of the patients described above. Moreover, mitochondria are 
critical regulators of steroidogenesis. Thus, we hypothesize that elevated cortisol can 
potentially explain the phenotype we see in these patients. Next, we will explore how 
mitochondrial dysfunction might lead to elevated cortisol production.

Steroidogenesis and cortisol 
Hormones are chemical messengers that are secreted to regulate other parts of the 
body. Steroid hormones are a group of lipid hormones with variety of functions such as 
regulating metabolism, inflammation, immune functions, salt and water balance, and 
development of sexual characteristics. They can pass through cell membrane and bind with 
receptors inside of cells to exert their effects. These hormones include glucocorticoids 
(cortisol, corticosterone), mineralocorticoids (aldosterone), estrogens (estradiol), progestins 
(progesterone), androgens (testosterone, dihydro-testosterone) and calciferols (1,25-
dihydroxy vitamin D [1,25OH2D]) (93). They are made from cholesterol via complex 
biosynthetic pathways in specialized cells (Fig 1.4).  
In the scope of this study, we are interested in cortisol, a type of glucocorticoid that is 
produced by specialized cells in zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex. (94) Timely

Steroidogenic Acute Regulatory Protein (STARD1) is important for steroidogenesis, 
as pathogenic variants in STARD1 that decrease its functions cause a potentially fatal 
disease known as congenital lipoid adrenal hyperplasia (98). adrenocorticotropic hormone 
(ACTH) and Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) stimulate STARD1 expression by 
increasing transcription and translation of STARD1 and via an activating phosphorylation at 
Ser195. STARD1 is synthesized as a 37 kDa protein with an N-terminal mitochondrial 


23 
 
 
targeting sequence. It is then cleaved to 30 kDa after mitochondrial import to the matrix. 
STARD1 is active on the OMM where it can interact with other OMM proteins including 18 
kDa translocator protein (TSPO), the voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC-1),TSPO-
associated protein 7 (PAP7, ACBD3), ATPase family AAA domain-containing protein 3A 
(ATAD3A) and protein kinase A regulatory subunit 1a (PKAR1A) (93,99,100). Together they 
form a complex that is crucial to import of cholesterol and the conversion of cholesterol to 
pregnenolone (100). In cells with active steroidogenesis STARD1 is only in the active form 
for 3-5 min before imported into the matrix (101). Early in vitro expression of STARD1 
showed that continued synthesis and import of STARD1 is important for its activity in 
steroidogenesis. STARD1 is active on the OMM and is inactivated by import into the matrix 
(101–103). Therefore, any disruption or delay in import of mitochondrial proteins can allow 
STARD1 to remain on the OMM and be active for a long period of time.  
 
Proposing a Model of mitochondrial skeletal disorders 
Given mitochondria play critical role in steroidogenesis, especially for cortisol, we 
would like to propose that mitochondrial function can impact cortisol production in 
mitochondrial diseases. Mitochondria are the site where steroidogenesis begins, as the 
precursor for all steroid hormones by mitochondrial enzyme P450ssc is in mitochondria 
(104). Importantly, the rate limiting step of steroidogenesis is the import of cholesterol into 
the mitochondria by STARD1, which is regulated by protein import to the mitochondria. Here, 
we posit a model whereby impairments in mitochondrial proteostasis reduces mitochondrial 
protein import of STARD1. Stalled STARD1 stays active and increasing cholesterol import, 
which will drive steroidogenesis and elevated cortisol levels that could contribute to the 
mitochondrial skeletal disorders. In Chapter 4, we will explore this model in more detail by 

24 
 
 
examining effect on STARD1, cholesterol import and downstream cortisol production in 
cellular model.

Chapter 2  Materials and Methods 
Patient selection and clinical investigations  
The two patients of Caucasian/European ancestry were ascertained through the 
clinical practice of Dr Innes at the Alberta Children's Hospital. Consent for participation and 
for the publication of patient photos from both patients and their mother was obtained as part 
of a study approved by the Conjoint Ethics Review Board of the University of Calgary. 
Consent for experiments conformed to the principles of the WMA Declaration of Helsinki and 
the Department for Health and Human Services Belmont Report. Investigations such as 
basic genetic and metabolic blood work, skeletal surveys, and MRI scans on both patients 
had been obtained during the course of their routine clinical care. As they were undiagnosed 
after conventional investigations, they were enrolled in a whole-exome sequencing–based 
research study to identify the cause of their presumed genetic rare disease.  
 
Molecular genetics studies  
Clinical analysis by Clinical Genetic Research group (Alberta Children's Hospital 
Research Institute) involved genomic DNA (gDNA) extracted from peripheral blood provided 
by the patients and their unaffected mother using an Autopure LS system and Gentra 
Puregene blood kit (QIAGEN). The Agilent SureSelect V5 All Exon kit (Agilent) and Illumina 
HiSeq 2500 were used for target enrichment and sequencing, respectively. Read alignment 
to the hg19 reference genome, as well as variant calling and annotation, were completed as 
described in previously published Care4Rare Consortium projects (Beaulieu et al, 2014). 
 
Because of the rarity of the sisters’ phenotype, annotated variants with an ExAC 
minor allele frequency less than 1% and seen in less than five previous Care4Rare samples 

26 
 
 
were considered. An autosomal recessive mode of inheritance was assumed and included 
analysis of rare homozygous variants shared by both sisters and carried by the mother, or, 
rare bi-allelic variants shared by the sisters, of which only one of the variants was carried by 
the mother. Resulting rare variants, and their respective genes were visualized using Alamut 
Visual version 2.9.0 (Interactive Biosoftware). Conservation, in silico pathogenicity prediction 
scores (Polyphen, SIFT and MutationTaster), and splice prediction scores (SpliceSiteFinder-
like, MaxEntScan, NNSPLICE, GeneSplicer and Human Splicing Finder) were taken in 
account. Sanger sequencing, using the ABI BigDye Terminator Cycle Sequencing kit v1.1 
(Life Technologies) on a 3130xl genetic analyzer (Life Technologies), confirmed the 
candidate variants. 
 
cDNA sequencing and analysis  
For clinical analysis by Clinical Genetic Research group (Alberta Children's Hospital 
Research Institute) led by Dr Innes, the PAXgene Blood RNA kit (PreAnalytiX) was used to 
extract RNA from peripheral blood samples provided by patient II-2 and their mother. cDNA 
was synthesized (SuperScript III; Invitrogen) from the extracted RNA. PCR amplification 
(HotStar Taq Plus; QIAGEN) across exon junctions of cDNA was performed using primers 5′-
GGCGAAATGGTTGCACTTC-3′ (forward) and 5′-GCAGGTCCCGGTCAAAG-3′ (reverse). 
Patient cDNA was Sanger-sequenced using the 5′- GGCGAAATGGTTGCACTTC-3′ 
(forward) and 5′-GCAGGTCCCGGTCAAAG-3’ (reverse) primers as well as a sequencing

Chapter 3  PISD 
PISD is a mitochondrial disease gene causing skeletal dysplasia, 
cataracts, and white matter changes. 
Abstract 
Exome sequencing of two sisters with congenital cataracts, short stature, and white 
matter changes identified compound heterozygous variants in the PISD gene, encoding the 
phosphatidylserine decarboxylase enzyme that converts phosphatidylserine to 
phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) in the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM). Decreased 
conversion of phosphatidylserine to PE in patient fibroblasts is consistent with impaired 
phosphatidylserine decarboxylase (PISD) enzyme activity. Meanwhile, as evidence for 
mitochondrial dysfunction, patient fibroblasts exhibited more fragmented mitochondrial 
networks, enlarged lysosomes, decreased maximal oxygen consumption rates, and 
increased sensitivity to 2-deoxyglucose. Moreover, treatment with lyso-PE, which can 
replenish the mitochondrial pool of PE, and genetic complementation restored mitochondrial 
and lysosome morphology in patient fibroblasts. Functional characterization of the PISD 
variants demonstrates that the maternal variant causes an alternative splice product. 
Meanwhile, the paternal variant impairs autocatalytic self-processing of the PISD protein 
required for its activity. Finally, evidence for impaired activity of mitochondrial IMM proteases 
suggests an explanation as to why the phenotypes of these PISD patients resemble recently 
described “mitochondrial chaperonopathies.” Collectively, these findings demonstrate that 
PISD is a novel mitochondrial disease gene.

Introduction 
Mitochondria are double-membrane–bound organelles, which in addition to 
generating most of a cell's energy via oxidative phosphorylation, have important roles in 
regulating many other cellular processes (e.g., apoptosis, immune response, and numerous 
metabolic pathways (110). Although mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in a 
growing list of human diseases, more severe forms of mitochondrial dysfunction cause a 
group of rare disorders known as mitochondrial diseases, estimated at ∼1 in 4,300 in adults 
(111). Classic mitochondrial disease is caused by impaired energy production, and often 
manifests in tissues with high energy demands, such as heart, muscle, brain, and eyes. 
However, diagnosing mitochondrial disease is difficult because of the clinical and genetic 
heterogeneity of this group of disorders. 
More recently, an atypical class of mitochondrial diseases has been described where 
impaired mitochondrial protein homeostasis appears to be the underlying cause of 
mitochondrial dysfunction (24). These “mitochondrial chaperonopathies” are characterized by 
atypical skeletal phenotypes and craniofacial features that are not commonly seen in classic 
mitochondrial disease, as well as cataracts and central nervous system involvement, which 
are sometimes found in mitochondrial disease. To date, only three genes (LONP1, HSPA9, 
and AIFM1) encoding mitochondrial proteins have been linked to mitochondrial 
chaperonopathies (22–24,52). The molecular mechanisms through which impaired 
mitochondrial protein homeostasis led to skeletal abnormalities, rather than more traditional 
mitochondrial disease phenotypes, remains unknown. 
Maintenance of mitochondrial protein homeostasis is, thus, a key aspect regulating 
mitochondrial function, and its impairment leads to disease. Notably, many mitochondrial-
specific proteases are bound to the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) (112). Thus, it is 
not surprising that the IMM lipid composition is an important regulator of mitochondrial 
function (85). The PISD gene encodes a mitochondrial-localized enzyme that converts 

43 
 
 
phosphatidylserine (PS) to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) in the IMM (113–116). PE, which 
comprises ∼15–25% of cellular membranes, is an important lipid that provides membrane 
curvature (117). Although complete loss of PISD is embryonic lethal in mice, highlighting the 
importance of mitochondrial PE, heterozygous mice do not have any overt phenotypes (82). 
In cellular models, severe depletion or complete loss of phosphatidylserine decarboxylase 
(PISD) results in decreased mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and fragmentation of the 
mitochondrial network (82,83). Notably, an autocatalytic processing event that generates two 
subunits (α and β) is required to form a functional PISD enzyme (118). 
In the present study, we report the first example of patients with pathogenic variants 
in PISD, who presented with congenital cataracts, short stature, mid-face hypoplasia, 
hypomyelination, ataxia, and intellectual disability. These phenotypes are reminiscent of the 
skeletal abnormalities described for pathogenic variants in LONP1 (cerebral, ocular, dental, 
auricular, skeletal syndrome [CODAS] syndrome), HSPA9 (epiphyseal, vertebral, ear, nose, 
plus associated findings [EVEN-PLUS] syndrome), and AIFM1 (spondyloepimetaphyseal 
dysplasia with mental retardation [SEMD-MR]), rather than classic mitochondrial disease. 
Our findings show that mitochondrial protein homeostasis is impaired in fibroblasts from 
patients with PISD variants. As such, we suggest that PISD be included in the list genes 
associated with impaired mitochondrial protein homeostasis.

Fibroblast characterization 
Given that there were no previous reports of individuals with pathogenic variants in 
PISD, combined with the unusual clinical presentation for a mitochondrial disease, we sought 
to perform additional functional analysis of patient fibroblast cells previously obtained from 
individual II-I to determine whether mitochondrial and/or PISD function were impaired. To 
begin, we measured the ability of both patient- and age-matched control fibroblast cells to 
convert PS to PE. We observed an approximately 50% decrease in PE synthesis from PS in 
the patient fibroblast cells, consistent with impaired PISD activity (Fig 3.2A). 
Next, we sought to investigate the effect that PISD deficiency might have on 
mitochondrial function. We observed marked differences between control and PISD patient 
fibroblasts using a Seahorse metabolic flux analyzer. Whereas basal respiration was 
elevated in PISD fibroblast cells, maximal respiration was decreased. In fact, PISD 
fibroblasts had no reserve capacity (Fig 3.2B). These findings clearly demonstrate alterations 
in the mitochondrial activity of PISD fibroblast cells. In an attempt to gain further mechanistic 
insight, we investigated additional parameters of mitochondrial function. As complex IV 
activity is reported to be decreased in CHO cells with severe knockdown of PISD expression, 
we examined the expression of several oxidative phosphorylation proteins (Fig 3.2C) and 
more specifically activity of complex IV (Fig 3.2D). The fact that complex IV activity was 
unaffected in patient fibroblasts likely reflects a cell-type difference or milder deficiency in 
PISD activity compared with previous studies (83). Next, we examined mtDNA copy number, 
mitochondrial biogenesis, and mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 3.2E–G), but again 
found no significant differences.

fragmentation phenotype compared with control fibroblasts (Fig 3.3C and D).

morphology in the patient fibroblasts is due to low levels of PE in the cells, and that the

Genetic complementation 
To confirm that impairments in PISD were the underlying cause of mitochondrial 
dysfunction in patient fibroblasts, we performed a genetic complementation by transfecting 
fibroblast cells with WT-PISD (Fig 3.5A). As transfection efficiency of the fibroblast cells was 
low (∼5%), we focused on examining the previously described mitochondrial and lysosome 
morphology phenotypes in cells where we could confirm transfection by 
immunofluorescence. During the analysis, we noted that the cells expressing highest levels 
of PISD all exhibited fragmented mitochondrial networks, even in control fibroblasts. As this 
likely reflects an overexpression artifact, cells expressing high levels were excluded from 
further morphological analysis. Notably, we found that patient fibroblasts transfected with 
WT-PISD were indistinguishable from control fibroblasts with respect to both mitochondrial 
and lysosome morphology phenotypes (Fig 3.5B and C), indicating that similar to lyso-PE 
supplementation, expression of WT-PISD rescued the mitochondrial dysfunction.

Functional characterization of pathogenic PISD variants 
Additional functional analyses were performed to provide evidence that the two PISD 
variants in our patients were pathogenic. First, we examined the effect of the c.697+5G>A 
variant on splicing of the PISD mRNA. This variant located in intron 5, five base pairs after 
the end of exon 5, is predicted to weaken the endogenous splice donor site at the end of 
exon 5, with a cryptic splice donor site located 76 base pairs upstream predicted to occur 
instead. The use of the upstream cryptic splice site would result in the skipping of the end of 
exon 5, with a 1-base pair frameshift for 51 amino acids and ending in a premature stop 
codon in exon 7. To investigate PISD splicing products, we generated cDNA from control 
and patient fibroblast RNA. PCR amplification across the region containing the splice sites 
allowed us to visualize an alternative splicing product in patient samples as a minor PCR

Next, we characterized the c.830G>A [p.Arg277Gln] variant, which was predicted to 
be pathogenic. To begin, we used the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, an established and 
robust model for mitochondrial disease (126). Previously, a protein sequence alignment of 
phosphatidylserine decarboxylase (PSD) enzymes from humans to bacteria was used to 
identify evolutionarily conserved residues that are essential for the autocatalytic proteolysis 
of yeast phosphatidylserine decarboxylase 1 (Psd1p) (119). Interestingly, R358 in yeast 
Psd1p, corresponding to human R277, occurs two residues downstream of one of the four 
missense mutations present in a temperature sensitive PSD1 allele (Figs 3.1D and  3.8A) 
(120). Furthermore, it is also in the vicinity of His345, a component of the conserved catalytic 
triad that is essential for Psd1p autocatalytic proteolysis (119). As such, we hypothesized 
that the R358Q mutation may disrupt Psd1p autocatalytic proteolysis via a mechanism that is 
enhanced at elevated temperatures. Importantly, both the expression (Fig 3.8B) and 
autocatalytic processing (Fig 3.8C) of the R358Q mutant was similar to WT at 30°C. 
Consistent with our hypothesis, autocatalytic proteolysis of the R358Q mutant was 
significantly impaired at 37°C (Fig 3.8C). In contrast, self-processing of WT Psd1p into 
separate α and β subunits occurred at both tested temperatures. Although autocatalytic 
proteolysis was impaired for the R358Q mutant at elevated temperature, it was not 
completely ablated. Consistent with the persistence of some functional enzyme, the R358Q 
mutant enabled growth of the psd1Δpsd2Δ strain at 30°C and 37°C in the absence of 
ethanolamine (Fig 3.8D). These results establish the R358Q mutant as a novel temperature-
sensitive PSD1 allele with severely impaired, but not completely ablated, autocatalytic 
proteolysis at super-physiological temperatures.

PISD and alterations to mitochondrial protein homeostasis 
Finally, given that the phenotypic characteristics of our PISD patients resembled 
those of mitochondrial chaperonopathies, we investigated whether or not mitochondrial 
proteases as well as mitochondrial protein homeostasis were impaired in PISD patient 
fibroblast cells treated with 2DG, where we saw dramatic changes in mitochondrial 
morphology. Given the importance of PE in the IMM, we started with OMA1, a zinc 
metalloprotease that is activated upon mitochondrial stress (128). We found that the levels of 
OMA1 protein expression were severely decreased in PISD patient fibroblast cells (Fig 
3.11A). To determine whether this depletion was sufficient to alter protein homeostasis, we 
examined the cleavage PGAM5, which is processed from a long to a short form by several 
membrane-bound IMM proteases, such as OMA1 (86) and PARL (87). In particular, we 
found decreased levels of the short form of the PGAM5 protein in patient fibroblast cells, 
suggestive of decreased IMM protease activity. To look for more global effects on 
mitochondrial IMM proteases, we also examined other established targets of mitochondrial 
IMM proteases. We saw changes to the levels of OPA1, an IMM protein that regulates 
mitochondrial fusion, and which is regulated by multiple proteases (129), including OMA1 
(130,131). In addition, we saw decreased levels of the mature form of MRPL32, a 
mitochondrial ribosomal protein subunit that is processed by the IMM m-AAA protease (132). 
Finally, we found that treating PISD patient fibroblasts with lyso-PE rescued the changes in 
OMA1, PGAM5, OPA1, and MRPL32 proteins. Importantly, quantitative RT-PCR revealed 
that changes in mRNA levels cannot explain all the differences in protein stability between 
control and patient fibroblasts, nor upon rescue by lyso-PE (Fig 3.12A), indicating that global 
changes in these protein levels are post-translational in nature. 
We also examined the effects of overexpressing wild-type and mutant PISD on 
mitochondrial protein homeostasis in HEK cells (Fig 3.11B). Notably, overexpressing WT-
PISD alone significantly reduced the levels of OMA1 and MRPL32 but did not have any 

64 
 
 
major effects on PGAM5 or OPA1. Meanwhile, overexpressing the R277Q and S378A PISD 
mutants also decreased levels of OMA1 and MRPL32 proteins, although to a less extent 
than WT-PISD. Again, quantitative RT-PCR showed that differences in mRNA expression 
cannot account for all the changes in protein expression in cells overexpressing the various 
PISD constructs (Fig 3.12B). Altogether, these data are consistent with the notion that 
altering levels of PE in the IMM, either up or down, impacts mitochondrial proteases and 
alters mitochondrial protein homeostasis.

Discussion 
Consistent with impaired PISD activity, our characterization of PISD patient 
fibroblasts revealed impaired phospholipid metabolism, altered mitochondrial respiration, and 


68 
 
 
fragmentation of the mitochondrial network, which was exacerbated by 2DG. The fact that 
supplementation of patient fibroblasts with lyso-PE and genetic complementation with WT-
PISD rescued mitochondrial fragmentation in 2DG-treated patient cells strongly supports a 
mitochondrial PE deficiency due to decreased PISD activity in these cells. Furthermore, 
functional characterization of the PISD patient variants shows the presence of an alternative 
splicing event of the maternally encoded transcript and reduced autocatalytic proteolysis of 
the paternally encoded protein, demonstrating that these polymorphisms reduce PISD 
activity and are pathogenic. 
An initial quandary when PISD was identified as the only candidate gene was why 
the patient phenotype did not resemble classic mitochondrial disease. To this end, the fact 
that we did not observe any significant differences for several other mitochondrial 
parameters, suggests that the underlying mitochondrial dysfunction is different from what is 
typically found in classic mitochondrial diseases. Importantly, during the revision of this 
manuscript, an article was published describing the C266Y pathogenic variant in PISD in 
patients with similar phenotypes (127), further supporting PISD as the responsible gene. The 
similarity of skeletal phenotypes in PISD patients with recently described skeletal 
abnormalities in mitochondrial chaperonopathies was notable. To this end, our data 
suggesting that IMM proteases and mitochondrial protein homeostasis are impaired in PISD 
patient fibroblasts may explain the similar patient phenotypes. 
We considered two possible mechanisms by which pathogenic variants in PISD 
might impair mitochondrial protein homeostasis. The first possibility was that accumulation of 
the unprocessed PISD protein reduced the ability of mitochondrial proteases to maintain 
normal mitochondrial proteostasis. To this point, it is likely that unprocessed human PISD is 
degraded by mitochondrial proteases, similar to the situation in yeast, where the 
unprocessed autocatalytic mutant Psd1 protein is degraded by the OMA1 protease(119). In

transfection is consistent with an inability of mitochondrial proteases to efficiently remove the 
accumulated 45-kD precursor. However, given that transcript levels were increased by 
several orders of magnitude in HEK cells, we are hesitant to draw conclusions on whether 
normal levels of the mutant PISD protein would be sufficient to impair mitochondrial protein 
homeostasis and lead to pathology. Moreover, given that the unaffected father presumably 
carries a single copy of R277Q, it is unlikely that the presence of the mutant protein alone, at 
physiological levels, is sufficient to induce pathology. However, as DNA from the father was 
unavailable, we could not completely exclude the unlikely possibility of gonadal mosaicism. 
The second, more likely possibility is that the global reduction of PISD activity and 
subsequent decrease in PE levels contributes indirectly to impaired mitochondrial protein 
homeostasis. The fact that depletion of PISD leads to mitochondrial fragmentation and 
decreased oxidative phosphorylation (82,83) demonstrates that depletion of mitochondrial 
PE is sufficient to cause cellular defects. To this end, several mitochondrial proteases are 
associated with, or are integral to, the IMM (e.g., HTRA2/OMI, iAAA, mAAA, OMA1, and 
PARL), and their functions can be regulated by membrane lipid domains (112). As such, 
these proteases are likely sensitive to alterations in membrane lipid composition such as 
decreased levels of PE. 
The relationship among mitochondrial proteases is complex, as they often cleave 
and activate each other(128), and it is not possible to directly measure protease activity. 
Nonetheless, our Western analysis of mitochondrial proteins in PISD patient fibroblasts 
provide strong evidence for decreased mitochondrial protease activity and subsequent 
impairments in mitochondrial protein homeostasis. In particular, we observed a marked 
decrease in the protein levels of the protease OMA1, but not its mRNA levels. This decrease 
in OMA1 is likely due to destabilization of the protein itself because of altered lipid 
composition and/or increased degradation of OMA1 by other mitochondrial proteases that 
cleave OMA1. Notably, oma1−/− mice are viable, and although they have an obesity 

70 
 
 
phenotype and defective thermogenesis (133), it does not match the phenotypes of our PISD 
patients. This dissimilarity in phenotypes suggests that loss of OMA1 alone is not sufficient to 
induce the pathology seen in our PISD patients, where there must be additional contributing 
factors. Certainly, our data point to the fact that multiple IMM proteases are affected in PISD 
fibroblasts, as we also see differences in OPA1, PGAM5, and MRPL32 protein profiles, 
which cannot all be explained by changes to mRNA. Importantly, the rescue of these protein 
profiles in patient fibroblasts supplemented with lyso-PE demonstrates that the altered 
mitochondrial protein homeostasis is due to depleted PE levels. Finally, the fact that we also 
see changes to mitochondrial protein homeostasis when PISD is overexpressed in HEK 
cells, albeit with a different profile than the changes in PISD fibroblasts, demonstrates that 
higher levels of PE can also be detrimental. This finding is also supported by the 
mitochondrial fragmentation we observed in fibroblast cells that highly expressed WT-PISD. 
The different mitochondrial protein profiles in patient fibroblasts deficient in PE and HEK cells 
overexpressing PISD likely reflects that the various IMM proteases respond differently to 
either an excess or deficiency of PE levels. Altogether, our data support the notion that IMM 
proteases are sensitive to changes in PE levels. 
Another open question pertains to the loss of PISD activity required for pathogenesis. 
The fact that both parents were unaffected, combined with the fact PISD +/− mice appear 
normal (82),suggests that the threshold of PISD depletion required for pathogenicity lies 
somewhere between 50 and 100%. In this light, it is likely that the PISD patients still produce 
some functional PISD protein, as PISD knockout mice are embryonic lethal (82). Although 
we cannot currently assign the relative contribution of each patient allele to the remaining 
PISD function in patients, it is notable that the R358Q mutation in yeast Psd1 retains some 
activity. Similarly, we also see low-level autocatalytic processing of the recently described 
C266Y variant. Finally, we note that overexpression of the R277Q and S378A mutants in 
HEK cells led to a milder depletion of OMA1 and MRPL32 compared with control, consistent 

71 
 
 
with reduced activity. Moreover, it is also possible that a portion of the maternal allele is 
spliced normally, or that the amount of alternative splicing varies in different tissue types. 
Collectively, our data demonstrate that pathogenic variants in PISD lead to 
mitochondrial dysfunction that likely leads to the broad phenotypic spectrum in mitochondrial 
phospholipid metabolism-based diseases (85).

Critical role for STARD1 in upregulating steroidogenesis in response 
to impaired mitochondrial proteostatic stress. 
Abstract  
While mitochondria play a critical role in mediating steroidogenesis, it is not clear 
how impaired mitochondria function impacts this important process. Because patients with 
mitochondrial skeletal disorders share phenotypes of short stature, cataracts, and skeletal 
dysplasia, which can be caused by elevated cortisol, we hypothesized impaired 
mitochondrial protein homeostasis could lead to these specific phenotypes by increasing 
cortisol expression. To test this hypothesis, we explored the role of STARD1, which 
regulates the rate limiting step of steroidogenesis, cholesterol import into mitochondria. We 
found impaired mitochondrial protein import activates STARD1 and increased cholesterol 
import to the IMM. We demonstrated the increased cholesterol import is STARD1 
dependent. We also showed that increased cortisol production follows a biphasic response 
to mitochondrial stress, where cortisol production increases under mild stress but decreases 
under high stress. Based on these findings, we propose a model where mitochondrial protein 
homeostasis alters steroidogenesis through import of STARD1. These findings have 
important implications on defining the molecular underpinnings of skeletal abnormalities in 
mitochondrial skeletal disorders, a novel class of mitochondrial disease. Findings from this 
study could also be applied to more common diseases that exhibit impair mitochondrial 
proteostasis, such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer's disease, and diabetes, where 
elevated cortisol is also reported.

Introduction  
Mitochondrial disease collectively refers to a complex and heterogeneous class of 
disorders in which regular mitochondria functions are disrupted (111). Mitochondrial disease 
is often syndromic in nature with classic phenotypes such as myopathy, ataxia, and 
neurodegeneration in tissues with high energy demands due to decreased ATP generation. 
However, emerging evidence suggests that other types of mitochondrial dysfunction can lead 
to non-classic mitochondrial disease pathology. For example, mutations in mitochondrial 
chaperones and proteases impair mitochondrial proteostasis and cause mitochondrial 
skeletal disorders, characterized by short stature, cataracts and skeletal abnormalities 
(22,42,53). Identifying the cause of these skeletal abnormalities will help us expand and 
define this novel class of mitochondrial disease to better understand the underlying 
molecular mechanism. 
In addition to energy production, mitochondria have other important functions such 
as metabolism, immunity, and steroidogenesis (111,112). Over 1500 proteins are required 
for mitochondria to properly maintain their many functions (2,8,16). The majority of these 
proteins are encoded by nuclear DNA and are imported into the mitochondria (16). 
Mitochondrial proteins translated as precursors in the cytosol and are sorted by the 
translocase of the TOM complex and transported to the sub-compartments of mitochondria: 
OMM, IMM, intermembrane space and matrix (Fig. 1.1) The majority of mitochondrial 
precursors for IMM or the matrix contain a cleavable mitochondrial targeting presequence, 
which is cleaved by the MPP to form the final form of the protein. Mitochondrial protein 
quality control ensures damaged and misfolded proteins are promptly removed so that they 
do not interfere with mitochondrial function(16,17). The proteasome degrades proteins on the 
OMM, while turn over of proteins in the IMS, IMM or the matrix rely on mitochondrial 
proteases. Mitochondrial quality control also involves packaging damaged proteins into 

74 
 
 
mitochondrial-derived vesicles (MDV) or even removal of entire mitochondria in a process 
known as mitochondrial autophagy (mitophagy) (134,135).  
Mitochondrial protein import complexes and mitochondrial proteases are critical 
sensors for mitochondrial protein homeostasis and their impairment triggers quality control 
mechanisms. One example of how import and proteases regulate mitophagy involves PTEN-
induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1), which is implicated in early onset Parkinson’s disease 
(PD). PINK1 is a mitochondrially localized kinase that normally gets partially imported into 
the mitochondria and is cleaved by MPP and PINK1/PGAM5-associated rhomboid-like 
protease (PARL) (134) for degradation. When mitochondrial protein import is disrupted, 
PINK1 is stabilized and activated on the OMM where it recruits and phosphorylates Parkin, a 
cytosolic E3 ubiquitin ligase. Parkin, activated in this process, ubiquitinates OMM proteins 
targeting mitochondria for mitophagy. The UPRmt, with ATFS-1 in C. elegans (and possibly 
ATF5A humans (11,12), is a similar example of sensing impaired mitochondrial protein 
import. Thus, mitochondrial protein import serves an important role in retrograde signalling 
indicating mitochondrial health.  
Given the role of mitochondria in steroidogenesis, we want to investigate if impaired 
mitochondrial protein homeostasis in mitochondrial skeletal disorders affects 
steroidogenesis, which may impact the entire organism. Steroidogenesis is the production of 
steroid hormones in specialized cells in the body through a multi step enzymatic process. 
(Fig 1.2). Steroid hormones include glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, estrogens, 
progestins, androgens and calciferols, all made to regulate important processes in the 
organism (94). For the scope of this thesis, we will only be looking at the glucocorticoid 
steroid cortisol. Cortisol is a hormone that is released during stress with variety of functions 
such as regulate metabolism, inflammation, and immune functions. Cortisol is produced by 
specialized cells in the zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex (94).  

75 
 
 
The production of cortisol starts and ends in mitochondria. As mentioned before, the 
rate limiting step of steroidogenesis is import of cholesterol into mitochondria mediated by 
STARD1 (14,136,137). It is notable that STARD1, is itself regulated by mitochondrial protein 
import. Bose et. al. (2002) demonstrated that the localization of STARD1 is critical to its 
function by demonstrating that localizing STARD1 to the OMM activated cholesterol import, 
while targeting STARD1 to IMS or matrix had no effect on cholesterol import. Importantly, the 
active form of STARD1 is the full-length 37 kDa protein localized to the outer mitochondrial 
membrane, while the imported mitochondrial matrix localized STARD1 is cleaved to a 30 
kDa form that is inactive (14). The cholesterol import activity of STARD1 is thus controlled by 
its import into mitochondria, a process that is slowed when mitochondrial proteostasis is 
impaired. Notably, previous work showed that impaired mitochondrial protein import leads to 
accumulation of unprocessed STARD1 (37 kDa) and increased cholesterol import using an 
artificial model in non-steroidogenic HEK cells (104,138). 
A novel link between mitochondrial protein homeostasis and steroidogenesis is 
suggested by the fact that increased levels of the steroid cortisol can cause growth 
deficiency and skeletal abnormalities resembling patients with mitochondrial skeletal 
disorders (139), and the fact that mitochondria are the site of the rate-limiting step in 
steroidogenesis mediated by STARD1. The regulation of STARD1 by mitochondrial import 
suggest a mechanism in which cholesterol import can be impacted by mitochondrial protein 
homeostasis. In this thesis, building on the previous works, we investigated how 
mitochondrial protein homeostasis can affect STARD1 processing, cholesterol import and 
cortisol production using both pharmacological and genetic stressors in steroidogenic cell 
lines. Establishing this novel mechanism for regulations of steroidogenesis can help 
potentially explain some symptoms of mitochondrial skeletal disorders and help understand 
the complex presentations of mitochondrial disease.

Discussion  
Our results show that drugs impairing mitochondrial proteostasis delayed STARD1 
import, and increased import of cholesterol to the IMM, but had a biphasic effect on cortisol 
production, with low stress increasing cortisol and high stress decreasing cortisol. 
Mechanistically, we showed that STARD1 is necessary for the increased cholesterol import 
in response to proteostatic stress. Based on these findings, we propose a mechanism where 
mitochondrial proteostatic stress can impact steroidogenesis by influencing the mitochondrial 
import of STARD1. Our findings are intriguing as they suggest a novel retrograde 
mitochondrial stress response pathway involving steroidogenesis that have potential impact 
on the entire organism.  


95 
 
 
 
Bi-phasic response of cortisol production to mitochondrial proteostatic stress. 
Our findings are consistent with previous work (14) suggesting that STARD1 activity 
is regulated by mitochondrial stress. The early work used a form of STARD1 targeted to the 
OMM to show that this form is active for cholesterol import (14,136,137) and showed that 
slowing down protein import could increase mitochondrial cholesterol import (14). Previous 
work showed that protease inhibitors could slow STARD1 import and increase mitochondrial 
cholesterol import in a non-steroidogenic line (138,141).  We expanded this finding by 
showing that protease inhibitor CDDO and O-Phen, protein import blocker auranofin and 
Complex I inhibitor rotenone also slow STARD1 import into mitochondria. Focusing on 
proteostatic stress via CDDO and O-Phen, we then looked at the downstream impact on 
mitochondrial cholesterol import and cortisol production in active steroidogenic cell lines. 
We found that impaired mitochondrial proteostasis delays STARD1 import (Fig 4.1-
4.3), resulting in more of the active form 37 kDa form of STARD1 in HEK cells. As expected, 
we also found that both CDDO and O-Phen could increase mitochondrial cholesterol import 
in steroidogenic cells. However, when we looked at cortisol levels in steroidogenic cells, we 
saw decreased cortisol levels, which was the opposite of our prediction. We reasoned this 
unexpected decrease might be because the drugs were causing too much mitochondrial 
dysfunction, as higher concentration of CDDO can inhibit mitochondrial metabolism and 
OxPhos which can shut down steroidogenesis (154–157). Additionally, disruption in OxPhos 
is also detrimental to ER function and mitochondria-ER contacts (93,154,158,159), which is 
also relevant as ER function and overall cell health are also crucial to ensured continued 
steroidogenesis This notion was confirmed by showing that high levels of CDDO reduce 
steroidogenesis even when using 22-OH-cholesterol that bypasses cholesterol import. We 
then found that lower CDDO treatment resulted in an increase in cortisol (Fig 4.9-4.10), 
confirming the biphasic response of cortisol production.  

96 
 
 
The finding that low stress increases cortisol led us to think about diverse ways that 
cells might respond to varying levels of mitochondrial proteostatic stress. For example, in 
UPRmt, a mild proteostatic stress can be resolved by increase transcription of protease and 
chaperones. On the other hand, removal of entire mitochondria by mitophagy would be 
required for high level stress (11). However, UPRmt responses are limited on a cellular level, 
whereas cortisol can potentially signal organism wide. There are many potential benefits for 
cells to signal to the entire organism in condition of stress. In the case of mild proteostatic 
impairment, increased energy metabolism mediated by cortisol can help combat any 
potential defects caused by mitochondrial impairment. Increased metabolism and an 
activated immune system mediated by cortisol can also help regulate the stress in an 
organism wide fashion. Our finding suggest that cortisol is a messenger for cells to signal 
mitochondrial proteostatic stress.  
 
Response of steroidogenesis to mitochondrial proteostatic stress is STARD1 
dependent. 
We have shown that increased cholesterol import in response to low proteostatic 
stress is STARD1 dependent, as STARD1 KO cells did not show elevated pregnenolone 
production. Moreover, increased cortisol in response to low proteostatic stress is also 
dependent on STARD1 rather than downstream steps in steroidogenesis, as there was no 
further stimulation in the presence of 22-OH-cholesterol, which bypasses the need for 
STARD1. Therefore, the response of elevated cholesterol import when treated with CDDO is 
acting through STARD1 to increase steroidogenesis. Our finding also supports that STARD1 
as a mitochondrial proteostatic stress sensor regulated by mitochondrial import.

Using genetic proteostatic stress 
Lastly, to move away from pharmacological treatments, which can have off-target 
effects, we wanted to look at a genetic model of proteostatic stress. We looked at how 
expressing an unfolded protein in mitochondrial matrix might affect steroidogenesis in H295R 
cells. The initial results were promising and indicating that an acute proteostatic stress 
induced by OTC or ΔOTC overexpression might alter steroidogenesis. However, at the time 
of these initial experiments, we did not have protocols in place to measure expression of 
OTC/ΔOTC. When we tried to repeat these experiments using stably transduced cells 
expressing OTC/ΔOTC, where we could monitor expression via mCherry expression, we did 
not see any consistent changes in pregnenolone or cortisol production between cell line 
expressing OTC and ΔOTC. We anticipate this inconsistency is the result of the lengthy 
selection process with viral vectors expression of OTC/ΔOTC. During the selection and 
culturing process, the population of cells may have shifted and expression levels of 
OTC/ΔOTC have changed from the original cell line (Fig 4.12-4.14). From a heterogenous 
cell like such as H295R, this would result in different baseline of cell population in each 
different cell line, and this baseline would diverge even further as the time goes on or after 
freeze/thaw.  At the same time, the surviving cells expressing OTC/ΔOTC may have adapted 
to this chronic stress, which may also be why the earlier results are different from the later 
results. Expressing OTC/ΔOTC via inducible promoters such as Tet-On Systems for 
doxycycline-inducible expression or cumate-inducible expression, may allow the flexibility to 
study more acute responses of the cell to mitochondrial proteostatic stress in the future.  
 
Limitation of steroidogenic cell lines.  
To study steroidogenesis, we used three steroidogenic cell lines, MA10, H295R, and 
HAC15. MA10 cells are derived from a mouse Leydig cell tumor(160), and are widely used to 

98 
 
 
study steroidogenesis. However, it does not produce cortisol due to its origin from testis. In 
this study, we used it mainly for the purpose of studying the dependence of cholesterol 
import on STARD1 since we were able to obtain a STARD1 KO line. The H295R cell line 
was isolated from 48 years-old female patient with adrenal carcinoma (148), is one of the 
most widely used cell lines to study steroidogenesis, and can produce cortisol. However, as 
it is not clonal, population shifting occurs during passaging and this instability of H295R cell 
lines limited the use of this line for any genetic manipulation, which requires selection. As 
discussed before, the selected cells can behave differently from the original population which 
made experiments that genetically alter the cells exceedingly difficult to interpret. Thus, for 
future genetic studies (e.g., genetic stress with OTC/ΔOTC), we propose using the HAC15 
cell line, which is a subclone of H295R derived from a single cell (161). Compared to H295R, 
HAC15 is more stable for genetic manipulations such as viral transduction and produced 
more stable results. We have also shown that HAC15 produces much higher levels of 
pregnenolone and cortisol, which will allow easier detection of steroids for less sensitive 
methods such as mass spectrometry.  
Another drawback of the steroidogenic cell lines used in our study is that we were not 
able to detect the 37 kDa STARD1 and had to resort to studying processing of STARD1 with 
an over-expression model in HEK cells. The 37 kDa form of STARD1 has a short half-life of 
less than 5 min (101), therefore the levels of 37 kDa STARD1 are undetectable with the 
antibodies available to us. We have also not seen any published work in H295R or HAC15 
cell lines that reported the 37 kDa STARD1 suggesting that detecting this band is very 
difficult to detect using existing antibodies. This issue can potentially be mediated by 
sourcing a more sensitive antibody for STARD1. Alternatively, genetically tagging STARD1 
with an epitope tag could potentially avoid the issue of antibody sensitivity and allow for 
better detection of 37 kDa form of STARD1.  

99 
 
 
To test the role of STARD1 in the proteostasis induced-steroidogenesis, we were 
able to source STARD1 KO MA10 cells as another steroidogenic cell line, . However, since 
MA10 cells are not from the adrenal gland they do not produce cortisol. Additionally, MA10 
cells are not of human origin, which differs from H295R and HAC15. We assumed that the 
regulation of STARD1 is similar in these cells compare to H295R and HAC15. Ideally, we 
would use the same cell line for consistency without making the assumptions that 
steroidogenesis and STARD1 processing works in similar ways in the cells used. However, 
finding similar trends across different lines supports the generalizability of the model. 
 
Applying the appropriate mitochondrial stress to study steroidogenesis.  
We have shown that steroidogenesis is highly sensitive to the magnitude of 
mitochondrial stress. In future studies, it will also be important to think about the exact type of 
mitochondrial stress to induce. In this thesis, we mostly focused on inhibitors of mitochondrial 
proteases to induce a mitochondrial stress response in context with the impaired proteases 
(e.g., LONP1) that cause mitochondrial skeletal disorders. We also showed that the 
mitochondrial import inhibitor auranofin and the mitochondrial complex I inhibitor rotenone 
can also slow down import of STARD1. Both aspects can be further investigated in HAC15 
cells to see if cortisol production is also affected.  
To overcome limitations of using drugs to trigger proteostatic stress, we choose to 
express an unfolded protein (ΔOTC) in the matrix. ΔOTC is a robust trigger for mtUPR that is 
well characterized. However, as discussed before, regulation of STARD1 depends on 
mitochondrial protein import. Using ΔOTC as a trigger for mitochondrial proteostatic stress 
may not the be the most direct way to slow down import. Since mitochondrial UPR is part of 
the integrated stress response pathway, which affects other parts of the cell such as the ER 
and lysosome. Using ΔOTC will complicate the cell’s stress response and give additional 
variables that may impact studying steroidogenesis. Also, prolonged expression to ΔOTC 

100 
 
 
can activate the mitophagy pathway, thus removing the mitochondria that we want to study 
(12,162,163). A more direct approach to slowing down import of protein to mitochondria may 
be through the use of mitochondria-targeted dihydrofolate reductase (mtDHFR), a 
mitochondrial protein clogger (164,165). DHFR is normally a cytosolic protein that binds its 
ligand folate. The strong binding prevents the DHFR from unfolding during import to 
mitochondria, thus clogging the mitochondria protein import system. This would allow us to 
study the direct effect of mitochondrial protein import on the regulation of steroidogenesis. 
Since we showed that STARD1 is critical to mitochondrial stress induced steroidogenesis 
and inhibiting mitochondrial protein import can also lead to delayed processing of STARD1, it 
will be interesting to see how directly jamming protein import to mitochondria can affect 
steroidogenesis.  
This study serves as an exploratory study that establishes a novel link between 
impaired mitochondrial proteostasis and alterations in steroidogenesis. We only looked at the 
production of cortisol, as it best fits our contexts of mitochondrial skeletal disorders. 
However, the pathway we stablished would also be applied to other steroid hormones such 
as aldosterone and sex hormones. These hormones all have significant impact on health of 
patients and having a better understanding of how they are impacted by mitochondrial 
protein homeostasis can improve the patient’s quality of life.  
Here, we have identified impaired mitochondrial proteostasis as a novel factor that 
alters steroidogenesis, which has several important implications for human health. For 
example, there are diseases such as Alzheimer disease (AD) and PD that also exhibit 
impaired mitochondrial protein homeostasis as well as elevated cortisol in patients 
(166,167) . We have already shown that rotenone, which is a drug used to generate models 
of PD, can slow down import of STARD1 (Fig 4.3). Even though this thesis mostly focused 
on the context of rare diseases, the findings may be relevant to these more common 
diseases. Importantly, since steroids pathways are well studied and easily targetable by 

101 
 
 
hormone blockers, it is an actionable pathway to target clinically. A better understanding of 
how mitochondrial stress can affect steroidogenesis could lead to novel treatments for 
people with AD and PD.

Chapter 5  Discussion  
The presentation of mitochondrial disease is highly variable because different 
functions of mitochondria are impaired in different diseases. To begin understanding this 
variability, we need to look beyond the simplified notion of “mitochondria as the powerhouse 
of the cell” and think of mitochondria as dynamic multifunctional organelles with complex 
interactions within the cell and the entire organism. Part of the variability in mitochondrial 
disease presentation may arise from the different types of mitochondrial dysfunctions caused 
by variants in mitochondrial proteins. For example, LONP1 is most known for its protease 
function to degrade damaged proteins. Additionally it also serves as a chaperone and a 
regulator of mtDNA (28,142). Variants affecting LONP1’s mtDNA regulation have very 
different symptoms compare to CODAS where the protease activity of LONP1 is impaired 
(22,23,31,34). In this thesis we focused on how mitochondrial lipid homeostasis, 
mitochondria protein homeostasis and mitochondrial steroidogenesis contribute to 
mitochondrial disease. We hypothesized impaired mitochondrial protein homeostasis could 
lead to specific phenotypes of short stature, cataracts, and skeletal dysplasia that we see in 
mitochondrial skeletal disorders by increasing cortisol expression. We identified patients with 
variants in PISD that present with short stature, cataracts and skeletal dysplasia and 
identified PISD as a novel mitochondrial disease gene. During the characterization of the 
patient fibroblasts, we found that the alteration of lipid composition affects mitochondrial 
proteases and protein homeostasis. We then tested how mitochondrial proteostasis affects 
steroidogenesis and found that delayed import of STARD1 increased import of cholesterol to 
the IMM. Interesting, we showed the production of cortisol follows a biphasic response where 
cortisol increases at low proteostatic stress but decrease in high stress. Our work establishes 
a novel retrograde signaling pathway through which impaired mitochondrial proteostasis 

103 
 
 
alters steroidogenesis. This pathway has the potential to explain the mitochondrial skeletal 
disorder phenotypes.

Mitochondrial skeletal disorders and mitochondrial protein homeostasis.  
The list of mitochondrial disease genes linked to skeletal abnormalities, which we 
term mitochondrial skeletal disorders, affects several mitochondrial functions, including 
chaperones, proteases, protein import, IMM lipid metabolism, and tRNA maturation. To link 
these mitochondrial functions, we propose a model whereby impaired mitochondrial protein 
homeostasis leads to reduced mitochondrial protein import, and that impaired import may be 
the key underlying dysfunction (Fig 5.1). In these cases, the proteostatic stress could be 
caused by reduced chaperone capacity, decreased protease activity, or impaired 
mitochondrial translation. 
Although one could imagine a situation where impaired protein import could also 
cause a proteostatic stress (e.g., partially imported proteins), there is not yet any direct 
evidence to support this notion. Nonetheless, there are several reasons to think that impaired 
protein import may be necessary and sufficient to mediate the proposed pathway. First, the 
proteases and chaperones covered here can be linked directly to protein import. Second, 
though we discussed how altered IMM lipid composition can affect mitochondrial proteases, 
it is also possible to envision how this aspect of mitochondrial function could also have a 
direct effect on IMM import. Third, as mitochondrial-encoded proteins are integral membrane 
proteins that are co-translationally inserted into the IMM, it is reasonable to assume that 
accumulation of ‘irregular’ proteins would also put a stress on the IMM. Finally, there is 
accumulating evidence for examples of retrograde signaling that are mediated by impaired 
mitochondrial protein import, suggesting that functional protein import may act as a rheostat 
for mitochondrial function. 
The most notable example of this type of retrograde signaling which listens to 
mitochondrial protein import is the mtUPR/ISR (12). Another well-studied example is the PD 
protein PINK1, which promotes mitochondrial autophagy when mitochondrial import is 
blocked (163), and which can be activated by the accumulation of unfolded proteins in the 

105 
 
 
matrix(162). In addition, there is a growing appreciation for the interrelationship between 
mitochondrial and cytosolic protein homeostasis and the importance of mitochondrial protein 
import (8).  
There is a reciprocal relationship between mitochondrial proteostasis and 
mitochondrial protein import. After being translated in the cytosol, nuclear-encoded 
mitochondrial proteins are imported into mitochondria in an unfolded state where they 
become substrates for mitochondrial proteases and chaperones that aid in protein import. 
Meanwhile, impaired mitochondrial protein homeostasis can cause reduced protein import, 
which is thought to be a key step in activating the UPR/ISR that upregulates several 
mitochondrial proteases and chaperones. In the context of discussing the role of 
mitochondrial import in mitochondrial skeletal disorders, it is also worth noting that 
pathogenic variants in other proteins directly involved in mitochondrial protein import have 
been described (168).  
Together, these examples highlight the importance of mitochondrial protein import, 
as several retrograde pathways can be activated when this process is impaired. While it is 
expected that such protein import retrograde pathways might be activated in mitochondrial 
skeletal disorders, this has yet to be confirmed. Moreover, whether constitutive activation of 
these pathways would protect or aggravate the pathology of these disorders also remains 
unknown. 
 
PISD – A novel mitochondrial skeletal disorder. 
In addition to our initial report of pathogenic variants in PISD (Chapter 3), other 
groups have since identified novel pathogenic variants in PISD in patients with a variety of 
skeletal phenotypes (127,169,170). Together, these independent reports provide compelling 
evidence that pathogenic variants in PISD cause disease, though there are some differences 
in the patients described. We described two sisters with short stature, mid-face hypoplasia, 

106 
 
 
congenital cataracts, and white matter changes with compound heterozygous variants (one 
missense, Arg277Gln, and one splice variant) (169). Meanwhile, Girisha et al. described 
unrelated patients with spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia and a c.797G>A/p.(Cys266Tyr) 
homozygous missense variant in PISD (127). Finally, Peter et al. described patients 
harboring a splicing variant at the 3′ end of the PISD gene with early-onset retinal 
degeneration, sensorineural hearing loss, microcephaly, intellectual disability, and skeletal 
dysplasia with scoliosis and short stature resembling Liberfarb syndrome (170). Compared to 
other reported variants in PISD, this cohort of patients present with similar short stature and 
skeletal phenotypes, but also with more serve symptoms and the disease is more 
progressive than the previous two reports.  
With respect to the mechanism of disease, we reported that these PISD substitutions 
impair an autocatalytic processing event required to generate the catalytic site of the PISD 
enzyme, and the Arg277Gln variant was shown to impair the activity of IMM proteases (169). 
Since the C-terminus of PISD is crucial for it is enzymatic activity (10,82), the truncation 
caused by the splicing variant described by Peter et al. could have severe loss of function. It 
is reasonable to speculate that the more severe phenotypes in patients’ symptoms 
resembling Liberfarb syndrome are a result of greater deficiency of PISD activity. However, 
more functional study of the variant should be performed. In all cases, it is expected that 
patients maintain some residual levels of PISD activity, as complete loss of PISD is 
embryonic lethal in mice (82), and thus far no clinical patient has been reported with biallelic 
truncating variants. Thus, the disease phenotypes appear to be caused by a significant 
reduction in the amount of active PISD enzyme leading to reduced mitochondrial PE levels.  
 
Mitochondrial lipid homeostasis and mitochondrial protein homeostasis.  
Lipids are critical for the proper function of mitochondria, which are comprised of two 
distinct membranes. The OMM mediates interactions between mitochondria, the cytosol, and 

107 
 
 
other organelles. The IMM consists of several functionally specialized domains, including 
invaginations (cristae), which house the electron transport chain complexes, and maintain 
the electrochemical gradient required to generate ATP. To maintain these functions, the IMM 
is rich in two key lipids that support membrane curvature, phosphatidylethanolamine, and the 
mitochondrial-specific lipid cardiolipin.  
We are categorizing PISD as a mitochondrial skeletal disorder gene based on the 
clear mitochondrial role of PISD and the unusual skeletal phenotypes. However, rather than 
lipid defects, many of the other mitochondrial skeletal disorders discussed here are caused 
by pathogenic variants in mitochondrial chaperones, proteases, or protein import factors, 
which have directly impact on mitochondrial protein homeostasis. This discrepancy raises 
the possibility that IMM lipid homeostasis might link to mitochondrial protein homeostasis or 
mitochondrial import. In this regard, there are several important links between IMM lipids and 
mitochondrial proteostasis worth noting. Many mitochondrial proteases are embedded within 
the IMM (112,128), where they are positioned to sense changes in lipid composition. 
Importantly, recent work has shown that alterations to the IMM composition of 
phosphatidylethanolamine affects these proteases and impacts mitochondrial proteins 
(10,171). For example, both decreased PISD activity and overexpression of PISD reduce the 
expression of the IMM protease OMA1 (10), while PISD depletion also impacts the IMM 
protease YME1L (171). Given that the mitochondrial protein import machinery also resides 
within the mitochondrial membranes, it is likely that mitochondrial protein import is also 
affected by changes in lipid composition (172). In this regard, the recent identification of the 
AGK as a component of the TIM22 import complex (173,174), and the role of DNAJC19 in 
mitochondrial protein import (175), are intriguing. Both AGK and DNAJC19, even though first 
regarded as mitochondrial lipid related proteins, also been identified to associate with 
mitochondrial protein import as chaperones, which is an important part of mitochondrial 
protein homeostasis (174,176,177) (with similar mitochondrial disease gene affecting import 

108 
 
 
reviewed in Table 5.1). Meanwhile, cardiolipin is an important mediator of the interaction 
between TIMM50 and the TIM23 complex (178). This further supports the connection of 
mitochondrial lipid composition and mitochondrial protein import. Next, we will look at other 
mitochondrial diseases involving lipid homeostasis and compare and relate them to 
mitochondrial skeletal disorders.  
In addition to PISD, there are a few other disease genes linked to mitochondrial lipid 
homeostasis (Table 5.2). These genes include TAZ, AGK, and DNAJC19, mutations in which 
cause Barth syndrome (179,180), Senger syndrome (181,182), and DCMA (dilated 
cardiomyopathy with ataxia) syndrome (183), respectively. TAZ encodes a cardiolipin 
transacylase required cardiolipin remodeling (85). AGK encodes acylglycerol kinase, an 
enzyme that produces phosphatidic and lysophosphatidic acids in the IMM. Meanwhile, 
DNAJC19 encodes a protein implicated in regulating cardiolipin remodeling (176). Notably, 
both DNAJC19 and AGK proteins are further described to play roles in mitochondrial protein 
import(173–175,184).  
Compared to PISD patients, this group of patients’ symptoms are more cardiac and 
muscular in nature but still share some common symptoms such as short stature and 
skeletal and developmental issues (Table 5.2). The difference between the symptoms can 
possibly be linked to difference role of PE and cardiolipin in the IMM. While both lipids are 
curvature inducing lipids, which are important for membrane properties, cardiolipin is tightly 
associated with mitochondrial OxPhos complexes I, III, IV and V and is crucial for their 
functions (185). Cardiolipin has a more direct affect on energy production, while the impact 
on OxPhos from lack of PE in PISD patients were relatively mild(169). Therefore, patients 
with Barth syndrome and DCMA generally have more cardiac and muscular presentations 
compares to PISD patients, which could be due to additional OxPhos defects. This disparity 
further illustrates that differential impairment of mitochondrial functions can lead to vastly 
different phenotypic presentations.

Links between skeletal deregulation and mitochondrial dysfunction. 
In the context of the skeletal dysplasia described in mitochondrial skeletal disorders, 
while it is clear that impaired mitochondrial protein homeostasis can cause mitochondrial 
disease, it remains unclear the exact molecular mechanism by which impaired mitochondrial 
protein homeostasis negatively impacts skeletal development. The expanding role of 
mitochondrial function in bone, aside from the function of mitochondria in energy production, 
is not as well understood, as bone is physically more difficult to access compared to tissues 
like skeletal muscle, has a lower mitochondrial density, and bone tissue pathology does not 
target examination of mitochondrial structural abnormalities. Interestingly, tetracycline, which 
has for decades been the standard to look for bone turnover, specifically targets 
mitochondria in bone and impairs their function (186), and these effects can be manifested in 
metabolic bone diseases like osteogenesis imperfecta (187). Notably, estrogen and 
androgen receptors play critical roles in bone health (188), providing a potential mechanism 
through which alterations to steroidogenesis could impact bone development. Meanwhile, 
mitochondrial dysfunction in the PolG mouse model of aging is recognized to impair 
osteogenesis, suggesting a direct effect of mitochondrial function on osteoclasts and 
osteoblasts (189). Finally, while the skeletal impairments in mitochondrial skeletal disorders 
are rather severe, it is also worth noting that poor bone health is recognized generally in 
primary mitochondrial disease (190). Another key link, which we will discuss next is that 
elevated cortisol, which we show can be a result of specific mitochondrial dysfunction, can 
impair skeletal development. 
Mitochondrial dysfunction and steroidogenesis. 
Notably, endocrine manifestations have been previously described in mitochondrial 
disease (191). However, this link has typically been in the context of more severe 

112 
 
 
phenotypes with impaired oxidative phosphorylation. Many mitochondrial disease genes 
(mtDNA mutation and deletion, NDUFAF5, POLG1, C10orf2) lead to decrease in steroids 
production which can lead to delayed sexual development or adrenal insufficiency (94,191). 
Given the severe mitochondrial impairment and interrelatedness of many mitochondrial 
functions, it is likely that multiple mitochondrial functions are impaired in many mitochondrial 
diseases. Thus, it has not been possible to ascribe these endocrine abnormalities to a 
specific function.  
In some of the mitochondrial skeletal disorders we described in Chapter 1 of this 
thesis there are symptoms suggesting that the production of steroid hormones is impacted. 
For example, the ovarian failure in Perrault syndrome and the genital abnormalities (e.g., 
cryptorchidism/hypospadia) associated with variants in HSPA9 and DNAJC19 could be due 
to altered levels of sex hormones. In addition, elevated levels of glucocorticoids could explain 
several of the key features of mitochondrial skeletal disorders (e.g., short stature/growth 
retardation, cataracts, and cardiomyopathy), which are shared with other mitochondrial 
diseases linked to protein import. For example, glucocorticoid therapy is a common cause of 
faltering growth in children (88), while Cushing syndrome, which is caused by excessive 
glucocorticoids, can also cause growth retardation (89,90). Additional common side effects of 
glucocorticoids include cataracts (91) and cardiomyopathy (92), similar to the symptoms 
observed in Barth syndrome and DCMA (Table 5.2) 
STARD1 and a possible molecular mechanism of skeletal disorders. 
The results described in Chapter 4 of this thesis showed that impaired mitochondrial 
proteostasis increases STARD1 activity by delaying its mitochondrial import, ultimately 
stimulating pregnenolone production. We demonstrated that this increased cholesterol 
import, and steroidogenesis is STARD1 dependent. This finding further established the 
importance of maintaining proper mitochondrial import and its impact on mitochondrial 

113 
 
 
health. Similar to other mitochondrial protein homeostasis sensor proteins such as ATF5 and 
PINK1, we showed STARD1 is a novel sensor of mitochondrial proteostatic stress. STARD1 
activates a novel retrograde signalling pathway mediated by steroidogenesis under 
mitochondrial stress. This pathway allows small groups of cells to signal and potentially 
respond to mitochondrial proteostatic stress on an organism level. Though only small groups 
of cells express STARD1 and are steroidogenic, the steroids released by these cells can 
diffuse freely across membranes to impact entire organism. We only studied this pathway in 
the context of stress hormone cortisol producing cells, but the same response could also 
potentially increase production of aldosterone and sex hormones, and cause issues of 
electrolyte balance and in sexual development.  
Since elevated cortisol can potentially explain some of the features of mitochondrial 
skeletal disorders, it is likely mitochondrial skeletal disorder patients would have elevated 
levels of cortisol. However, as cortisol levels can change drastically depending on the 
condition of patients and how cortisol is measured, this can make interpreting cortisol 
measurements difficult. Having only established this novel STARD1-mediated pathway in 
cultured steroidogenic cells, it will be interesting in the future to monitor cortisol levels in 
patients with mitochondrial skeletal disorders to see if they are increased.  
A biphasic model of steroidogenesis and mitochondrial dysfunction. 
What is interesting about mitochondrial proteostatic stress induced steroidogenesis is 
the biphasic relationship between the level of proteostatic stress and cortisol production. Low 
proteostatic stress stimulates cortisol production, while high proteostatic stress inhibits 
cortisol production. We attributed this biphasic response to the balance between protein 
(STARD1) import and OxPhos. Both protein import and steroidogenesis require functional 
OxPhos. We showed that disrupted OxPhos direct can result in accumulation of active 
STARD1, which increases cholesterol import to the IMM. However, disruption of OxPhos 

114 
 
 
also shuts down later steps in steroidogenesis. For production of cortisol to increase, the 
level of proteostatic stress need to be at a mild level where the protein import is stalled but 
the overall OxPhos is not affected.  
Translating this biphasic response of steroidogenesis into mitochondrial disease, it 
means that mitochondrial disease where mitochondrial respiration is not severely affected 
may have different presentations compared to more classical mitochondrial disease. 
Alternatively, severe OxPhos defects can mask other presentations of mitochondrial disease. 
For mitochondrial skeletal disorders, patients generally have milder defects in OxPhos, and 
their symptoms can involve organs with less energy demands such as bones. One intriguing 
example is variants of LONP1, where some LONP1 variants cause CODAS syndrome that 
presents with short stature, cataracts, and skeletal dysplasia. One of the LONP1 variants 
that causes classical CODAS (R721G) is in the proteolytic domain and impacts the protease 
function of LONP1, but this variant only slightly affects mitochondrial respiration, suggesting 
that protease function rather than OxPhos is causing the symptoms of CODAS. Meanwhile, 
the Y565H variant of LONP1 reduces the ability of LONP1 to bind and degrade TFAM (32), a 
master regulator of mtDNA and is linked to mtDNA depletion. This variant resulted severe 
mitochondrial dysfunction in OxPhos. Patient symptoms in this second case do not resemble 
CODAS and are more similar to classical mitochondrial disease with severe respiratory 
distress and acidosis. In the case of LONP1 causing more classical mitochondrial disease, 
even though there is still mitochondrial proteostatic stress, the patients do not present with 
symptoms related with elevated cortisol, likely as their disrupted mitochondrial respiration 
shuts down steroidogenesis. This demonstrated that different type of mitochondrial 
dysfunction can have vastly different disease presentations and patient outcomes and further 
illustrated the diversity of mitochondrial diseases.

Challenges of studying rare disease. 
As rare disease is hard to come by, opportunities to characterize genes and obtain 
patient samples are limited. One of the limitations to our approach of studying mitochondrial 
skeletal disorders is the fact that only a handful of patients have been described for many of 
the diseases discussed. The rarity of these disorders makes it difficult to get a complete 
clinical picture and some of the detailed pathophysiological mechanisms still need to be 
more clearly understood. Rare disease research also suffers from lack of appropriate cellular 
and animal models that is available to researchers. We did not have any animal models 
available to study our patient variant for PISD, which would allow us to look at the 
phenotypes of the animal compared to our patients. While studying patient fibroblast cells 
give us an opportunity to characterize the mitochondrial dysfunctions in the cell, we can gain 
more insight on how our patients’ PISD variants affect the entire organism with an animal 
model that carries the same PISD variants.  
With next-generation sequencing approaches quickly gaining traction and becoming 
more available for the diagnosis of these rare genetic disorders, it is likely that additional 
patients will be found that harbor new variants in both known and novel disease genes and 
extend their phenotypic spectra. These discoveries will undoubtedly contribute to a better 
understanding of mitochondrial functions and how their impairment leads to disease.  
 
Applying knowledge from rare disease more broadly.  
Ultimately, studying rare diseases gives us an opportunity to understand biology on a 
fundamental level. Understanding the underlying dysfunction in rare diseases allows us to 
apply this knowledge to other human diseases. It will be interesting to see how the insight 
gained from the mitochondrial skeletal disorders we describe here could potentially be 
informative to other disorders linked to mitochondrial dysfunction. For example, mitochondrial 

117 
 
 
quality control and protein import are already implicated in neurodegenerative diseases such 
as PD and AD (192,193). PINK1, autosomal-recessive PD gene, serves an important sensor 
for mitochondrial protein homeostasis, as it accumulates on OMM and activates upon failure 
of mitochondrial import, which leads to recruitment of Parkin, another autosomal-recessive 
PD gene(162,163). The cause that delays PINK1 import can potentially lead to stalling of 
STARD1 import, which in turn elevates cortisol. Notably, stress and inflammation, which are 
functions regulated by cortisol, are risk factors in PD(166). In AD patients, the plaque forming 
amyloid beta peptides accumulate in the mitochondria and its precursor amyloid precursor 
protein forms complexes with the TOM and TIM complexes blocking mitochondrial protein 
import (193). The accumulation of these peptides activates the PINK-Parkin pathway as in 
PD patients and can also stall the import of STARD1 and increase cortisol production. Both 
PD and AD patients are reported to have elevated cortisol levels(166,167), which can lead to 
worsening symptoms. Thus, the novel relationship between mitochondrial protein 
homeostasis and steroidogenesis we have described could further improve our 
understanding of neurodegenerative diseases and improve the quality of life for patients.  
 
Conclusion 
In this thesis, we have identified PISD as a novel mitochondrial disease gene and 
showed that mitochondrial protein homeostasis is impaired in patient cells. We have also 
identified a novel STARD1 mediated pathway that alters steroidogenesis in response to 
mitochondrial proteostasis stress. Identifying new rare mitochondrial diseases with 
uncommon phenotypes and linking these to different mitochondrial functions provides 
valuable insight on how mitochondrial function is linked to human disease. Moreover, this 
furthers our understanding of mitochondrial biology and potentially links our findings to more 
common diseases.
