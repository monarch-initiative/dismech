Dysfunctional sodium channel kinetics as a novel epilepsy mechanism in 
chromosome 15q11-q13 duplication syndrome.

Elamin M(1), Lemtiri-Chlieh F(1), Robinson TM(1), Levine ES(1).

Author information:
(1)Department of Neuroscience, School of Medicine, University of Connecticut, 
Farmington, Connecticut, USA.

OBJECTIVE: Duplication of the maternal chromosome 15q11.2-q13.1 region causes 
Dup15q syndrome, a highly penetrant neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by 
severe autism and refractory seizures. Although UBE3A, the gene encoding the 
ubiquitin ligase E3A, is thought to be the main driver of disease phenotypes, 
the cellular and molecular mechanisms that contribute to the development of the 
syndrome are yet to be determined. We previously established the necessity of 
UBE3A overexpression for the development of cellular phenotypes in human Dup15q 
neurons, including increased action potential firing and increased inward 
current density, which prompted us to further investigate sodium channel 
kinetics.
METHODS: We used a Dup15q patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell line 
that was CRISPR-edited to remove the supernumerary chromosome and create an 
isogenic control line. We performed whole cell patch clamp electrophysiology on 
Dup15q and corrected control neurons at two time points of in vitro development.
RESULTS: Compared to corrected neurons, Dup15q neurons showed increased sodium 
current density and a depolarizing shift in steady-state inactivation. Moreover, 
onset of slow inactivation was delayed, and a faster recovery from both fast and 
slow inactivation processes was observed in Dup15q neurons. A fraction of sodium 
current in Dup15q neurons (~15%) appeared to be resistant to slow inactivation. 
Not unexpectedly, a higher fraction of persistent sodium current was also 
observed in Dup15q neurons. These phenotypes were modulated by the 
anticonvulsant drug rufinamide.
SIGNIFICANCE: Sodium channels play a crucial role in the generation of action 
potentials, and sodium channelopathies have been uncovered in multiple forms of 
epilepsy. For the first time, our work identifies in Dup15q neurons 
dysfunctional inactivation kinetics, which have been previously linked to 
multiple forms of epilepsy. Our work can also guide therapeutic approaches to 
epileptic seizures in Dup15q patients and emphasize the role of drugs that 
modulate inactivation kinetics, such as rufinamide.

Chromosome 15q11-q13 duplication syndrome (Dup15q) is a neurodevelopmental syndrome caused by maternal duplications of this region of chromosome 15. The most severe form results from a supernumerary maternal chromosome that carries two copies of the 11.2-13.1 region (1, 2). Affected children exhibit severe epileptic seizures, a profound autism phenotype, motor delays, hypotonia, and intellectual disability, among other symptoms (3).

The comorbidity of epilepsy is well-documented and well-studied in both syndromic and non-syndromic forms of autism (4-8). In Dup15q syndrome, epilepsy is one of the most common and most debilitating symptoms. Over 63% of Dup15q patients experience severe forms of seizures, which are often refractory to treatment and might progress to a Lennox-Gastaut syndrome phenotype, a severe form of epilepsy that presents early in life with multiple seizure types (7). EEG findings in Dup15q include focal spikes, multifocal spikes, generalized spikes, diffuse fast rhythms, and hypsarrhythmia (7, 9-11). Moreover, this genetic abnormality carries an increased risk of Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy (SUDEP) (12). Thus, it is crucial to understand the precise molecular mechanisms that drive the development of this syndrome.

Creating a Dup15q animal model that recapitulates the genetic and phenotypic complexity of the syndrome has proven to be a challenging task. Surprisingly, duplication of the maternal syntenic mouse region resulted in no behavioral phenotypes (13). Several mouse models have also been generated with increased UBE3A expression, as UBE3A in neurons is expressed only from the maternal allele. These mice showed only mild autism-like phenotypes (e.g. decreased sociability) or mild seizure phenotypes (e.g. altered evoked seizure threshold) (14-17). Hence, the use of patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) lines, which carry the exact genetic makeup of the affected individuals, can provide valuable insights into the pathophysiology of this neurodevelopmental syndrome. Indeed, comparing iPSC-derived neurons from multiple Dup15q patients to neurons derived from unaffected individuals uncovered hyperexcitability phenotypes that include increased excitatory synaptic current frequency, decreased inhibitory synaptic current frequency, and increased action potential firing (18). In a subsequent study, we employed a novel CRISPR-based technique to remove the extra isodicentric chromosome in a Dup15q iPSC line to create an isogenic control line, thereby avoiding genetic background variability in cells lines from unrelated individuals. Using whole-cell patch clamp electrophysiology, we compared Dup15q neurons to their isogenic corrected counterparts, and we confirmed the hyperexcitability phenotypes including increased action potential firing frequency. Moreover, we uncovered an increase in voltage-gated inward current (19), which prompted us to further investigate the gating kinetics of voltage-gated sodium channels in the present study.

Sodium channels undergo three conformational states: resting, activated, and inactivated (20, 21). When channels are in the resting state, they are closed and ready to respond to depolarizing voltages. A depolarization in membrane voltage that reaches threshold opens the channels, which allows sodium ions to rush into the cells; thereby causing the upstroke phase of the action potential (20, 22). Prolonged depolarization inactivates the channels, a state where sodium conductance is blocked and hyperpolarization of the membrane voltage is required to remove this block (23-25). Abnormal changes in sodium channel gating mechanisms, such as hyperpolarization of the voltage-dependent activation curve, depolarization of the inactivation curve, and an increase in persistent current (the fraction of sodium current that fails to inactivate with prolonged depolarizations) have been shown to increase neuronal excitability and have been linked to various epilepsy syndromes (26).

Neurons derived from Dravet syndrome patients, for example, show increased sodium current density and neuronal hyperexcitability (27). Another study reported that iPSC-derived neurons from both a Dravet syndrome patient and a patient with mild febrile seizures had increased sodium current density, in addition to a depolarizing shift in the voltage-dependence of inactivation, increased persistent current, and an increase in evoked and spontaneous neuronal firing (28). Mammalian expression of an SCN1A mutation found in a family with generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus (GEFS+) revealed defects in the voltage- and time-dependence of fast inactivation, as well as enhanced recovery from inactivation, supporting a link between sodium channel gating kinetics and the development of neuronal hyperexcitability (29).

In the present study, we use the Dup15q iPSC-derived line and its isogenic corrected counterpart to identify for the first time dysfunctional inactivation kinetics in voltage-gated sodium channels. These deficits, along with the intrinsic hyperexcitability phenotypes, were reversed by application of rufinamide, a second-generation antiepileptic drug that modulates the inactivation state of sodium channels.

Stem cell colonies were cultured and maintained on mouse embryonic fibroblasts treated with mitomycin C (Millipore, MA) and human embryonic stem cell medium (hESC; replaced daily; DMEM-F12 (Life Technologies, CA), 20% knockout serum replacer, 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids, 1 mM L-glutamine, 10 ng/ml basic fibroblast growth factor, and 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol). Stem cell colonies were mechanically passaged once per week.

Stem cells were differentiated into neurons using a previously-established protocol (19). In brief, dual-SMAD inhibition was carried out for 10 days using 500 ng/mL of noggin (R&D Systems, Minneapolis MN) and 2 μM SB431542 (Stemgent, Beltsville MD) in N2/B27 neural induction media containing neurobasal media, 1% N2 supplement, 2% B27 supplement, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% insulin-transferrin selenium (Life Technologies). For days 11-28, noggin and SB431542 were omitted from the media and multiple steps were carried out including passaging neuronal rosettes, dissociating cells, and freezing neural precursor cells (NPCs). NPCs were subsequently thawed for experiments and plated on polyornithine/laminin-coated glass coverslips (density 20,000 cells/coverslip). Two independent neuronal differentiation rounds were carried out for each cell line.

Patch clamp experiments were conducted at 8 weeks and at 21-23 weeks of in vitro development. For voltage-clamp experiments to isolate sodium currents, pipettes were filled with a solution containing 130 mM CsCl, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM phosphocreatine, 4 mM Na2-ATP, 0.3 mM Na2-GTP, 1 mM CaCl, and 10 mM EGTA, and the pH was adjusted to 7.2 using CsOH. The bath solution contained 120 mM NaCl, 15 mM glucose, 2 mM MgCl2-6H2O, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM BaCl2, 20 mM TEA-Cl, 0.2 mM CdCl2, and the pH was adjusted to 7.35 with CsOH. Electrophysiology protocols used to evoke sodium currents and action potential firing are described in Table 1. Current-clamp experiments were performed using established protocols (19). All recordings were performed at room temperature (22-24°C).

Data were analyzed using Student’s t-test or repeated measures ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons tests. Data are presented as mean +/− standard error of the mean. Prism software (GraphPad, San Diego CA) was used for all statistical analyses. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 and is represented by an asterisk.

To investigate sodium channel kinetics, we used a patient-derived Dup15q iPSC line and a CRISPR-corrected isogenic line that has the extra chromosome totally removed (Corr.Control), as characterized in our previous study (19). We used a dual-SMAD inhibition protocol (Supplemental Fig. 1A) to differentiate iPSCs into functional neurons. This protocol yields ~80% glutamatergic TBR1-positive neurons and a smaller fraction (~20%) of GABAergic GAD65-positive neurons (18). Representative images of neurons stained with the neuronal marker MAP2 are depicted in Fig. 1A.

We performed whole-cell voltage clamp recordings and isolated sodium currents using a cesium-based internal solution and an external bath solution containing barium, TEA, and cadmium to block potassium and calcium currents. As expected, Dup15q neurons had a significantly increased sodium current density at 23 weeks of in vitro development (Fig. 1B; 1C). Cell capacitance was not different between the two groups (Supplemental Fig. 1B). The increase in current density was mediated by TTX-sensitive sodium channels, as application of 100 nM TTX completely inhibited the current in both groups (Supplemental Fig. 1C). Moreover, a larger persistent current was observed in Dup15q neurons at 23 weeks (Fig. 1E). The increase in sodium current density was also observed at an earlier point of development (8 weeks; Supplemental Fig. 2A), but no difference in persistent current was observed at this time point (Supplemental Fig. 2B). Voltage dependence of sodium channel activation was not different between Dup15q and Corr.Control neurons (Fig. 1D). Boltzmann fitted data also yielded similar voltages for half-maximal channel activation (Corr.Control V1/2 = −31.34 ± 0.56, n = 22; Dup15q V1/2 = −32.44 ± 0.49 mV, n = 14).

Following activation, sodium channels can undergo fast inactivation (within a few milliseconds) and slow inactivation (~100 ms to several seconds) (30-32). A depolarizing shift in voltage dependence of steady state inactivation was observed in Dup15q neurons at 23 weeks of in vitro development (Fig. 2A).

Boltzmann-fitted data yielded a significantly more depolarized voltage of half-maximal inactivation in Dup15q cells (Corr.Control V1/2 = −42.61 ± 0.45; Dup15q V1/2 = −39.92 ± 0.21 mV), consistent with an increase in channel availability in Dup15q neurons. A smaller statistically significant difference was observed at 8 weeks of development (Supplemental Fig. 2C). We also examined the time kinetics of sodium channel inactivation, and we found no difference in the decay time constant tau between Dup15q and Corr.Control neurons (Fig. 2B).

Next, we looked at the time of onset of slow inactivation, and found a delayed entry into the slow inactivated state in Dup15q neurons (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, a fraction of sodium current (~%15) in Dup15q cells appeared to be resistant to slow inactivation after 20 seconds of depolarization, while Corr.Control neurons completely inactivated by that time point (Fig. 2C). This dysfunction in slow inactivation was not present at 8 weeks of in vitro development (Supplemental Fig. 2D).

We also investigated sodium channel recovery from fast and slow types of inactivation. Recovery was tested via either a 10 millisecond (fast inactivation) or a 1 second (slow inactivation) conditioning pulse at 0 mV and a second test pulse (0 mV/ 25 ms) following various recovery times from 10 to 1000 ms. Dup15q neurons showed rapid recovery from both types of inactivation at 23 weeks in vitro (Fig. 2D; 2E). Recovery from fast inactivation was not different at 8 weeks of in vitro development (Supplemental Fig. 2E).

We performed whole-cell current clamp experiments to examine intrinsic excitability and action potential characteristics of Dup15q and Corr.Control neurons. In response to a series of 500 ms depolarizing current injections, Dup15q neurons showed an increase in the maximum action potential firing frequency at 22 weeks of in vitro development (Fig. 3A, Supplemental Fig. 1F), although no statistically significant increase was observed at 8 weeks (Supplemental Fig. 2F). This increased excitability was not due to increased input resistance because Dup15q neurons had decreased input resistance compared to Corr.Control neurons (Supplemental Fig. 1E). Both the increased excitability and decreased input resistance were also reported in our previous work (19). The latency to the first action potential was not different between Dup15q and Corr. Control neurons (Supplemental Fig. 1D).

The upstroke of the action potential is largely mediated by the influx of sodium ions (21, 22, 33). Dup15q neurons exhibited an increased action potential amplitude (Fig. 3B), most likely due to the increase in inward current density observed in these cells (Fig. 1C). A hyperpolarized action potential threshold and a reduced action potential width were also observed in Dup15q neurons (Fig. 3B). Next, we examined the ability of Corr.Control and Dup15q neurons to fire repetitive bursts. Firing evoked via 200 ms depolarizing current steps with 1 second interpulse interval showed increased firing frequency in Dup15q neurons and revealed that both groups were capable of firing multiple bursts of action potentials with no difference in frequency between the first (baseline) and the last pulse (Fig. 3C).

Because differences between Dup15q and Corr.Control neurons with regard to sodium channel recovery from inactivation were most evident with short recovery times (10 ms), we shortened the interpulse interval to 10 ms to investigate the ability to fire repeated bursts of action potentials. A significant difference in burst modulation was uncovered, as Dup15q neurons showed enhanced repetitive firing rate compared to Corr.Control neurons, which exhibited a more prominent reduction in frequency with repetitive bursts (Fig. 3D).

Voltage-gated sodium channels play an important role in neuronal excitation, and sodium channel pore blockers, such as phenytoin, lamotrigine, and carbamazepine are among the most commonly used drugs in the treatment of epilepsy (34). Here, we conducted voltage clamp experiments at 21 weeks of in vitro development to investigate if treatment with rufinamide, a second-generation antiepileptic drug that works via enhancing the inactivated state of voltage-gated sodium channels and slowing recovery from inactivation (35), can ameliorate the observed aberrations in Dup15q neurons.

We recorded baseline kinetics of Dup15q cells in the presence of 0.1% DMSO, then 100 μM rufinamide (dissolved in DMSO) was acutely applied to the cells. This concentration of rufinamide has been previously shown to be clinically relevant and effective in modulating sodium channel kinetics (36). Compared to baseline, a significant hyperpolarization of the steady-state inactivation curve was observed (Fig. 4A). Boltzmann-fitted data yielded a significant ~ 6-mV hyperpolarizing shift by rufinamide in the voltage of half-maximal inactivation (Dup15q + DMSO V1/2 = −36.6 ± 0.2225; Dup15q + RUF V1/2 = −42.5 ± 0.523 mV; n = 7 cells), bringing it to a level similar to the control level (Corr.Control V1/2 = −40.7 ± 0.7138 ; n = 9; Fig. 2A). Voltage-dependence of activation was not different after treatment with rufinamide (Supplemental Fig. 3A), but a small decrease in maximum current density was observed (Fig. 4B). Sodium channels in Dup15q neurons showed delayed entry into the slow inactivated state and an enhanced recovery from both fast and slow inactivation states (Fig. 2). Application of 100 μM rufinamide accelerated sodium channel entry into the slow inactivated state (Fig. 4C) and delayed recovery from both fast and slow inactivation states (Fig. 4D-E). Inactivation properties after treatment with rufinamide appear to be similar to those of Corr.Control neurons, which are provided in Fig. 4 for illustrative purposes. Acute treatment with rufinamide did not, however, alter the persistent current (Supplemental Fig. 3B) or the voltage-dependence of slow inactivation (Supplemental Fig. 3C).

Dup15q neurons also showed increased action potential firing frequency and increased action potential amplitude (Fig. 3A, 3B). To investigate the effect of rufinamide on these phenotypes, we performed current-clamp experiments at 22 weeks of in vitro development. Application of 100 μM rufinamide decreased action potential firing frequency, decreased action potential amplitude, and modulated burst firing frequency with a 10 ms interpulse interval, bringing these properties to a level similar to control level (Fig. 4F-G). Rufinamide, however, did not influence action potential width or threshold (Supplemental Fig. 3D; 3E), nor did it modulate bursting frequency when the interpulse interval was increased to 1 second (Supplemental Fig. 3F). The input resistance and the latency to the first action potential were also not changed by the application of rufinamide (Supplemental Fig. 3G; 3H).

Isodicentric maternal duplications in the q11.2-q13.1 region of chromosome 15 cause Dup15q, a neurodevelopmental syndrome characterized by profound autism and severe epilepsy among other symptoms. The extra chromosomal material in Dup15q syndrome carries many functional genes, including UBE3A, a maternally-expressed gene that encodes a ubiquitin ligase, and one that is thought to drive Dup15q syndrome. Our previous work confirmed that UBE3A is necessary for the development of Dup15q cellular phenotypes (19); nevertheless, the downstream targets and the mechanisms that link UBE3A to neuronal hyperexcitability are still an open question in the field.

In the present study, we report higher sodium current density in Dup15q neurons compared to isogenic corrected control neurons. Although activation kinetics were not different between the two groups, we identified novel dysfunctional inactivation kinetics in Dup15q neurons. Both fast and slow inactivation kinetics were affected, processes that negatively regulate sodium conductance and modulate the electrical activity of neurons (30).

Sodium channel inactivation reduces peak sodium current, and if these mechanisms are dysfunctional, an increase in peak current is to be expected (23-25). Therefore, the inactivation defects observed in Dup15q cells might contribute to the increased peak current density and the increased action potential amplitude observed in these cells. Moreover, it has been proposed that a positive shift in the voltage dependence of fast inactivation (similar to that observed Dup15q neurons) and enhanced recovery from inactivation can cause neuronal hyperexcitability and spontaneous seizures (26, 28, 29, 37), further supporting a possible role of the observed inactivation defects in mediating Dup15q seizure pathology.

Dup15q neurons also show increased maximal evoked firing frequency (Fig. 3A). Although many factors can contribute to this increase in intrinsic excitability, it has been suggested that increased sodium channel availability via incomplete inactivation following spikes can facilitate rapid, repetitive firing in neurons (38). In addition, increased persistent current (also observed in Dup15q neurons) has been strongly linked to epileptic seizures and neuronal hyperexcitability (39). Hence, the observed inactivation deficits (which increase current availability) and the observed increase in persistent current might also contribute to the higher firing rate in Dup15q neurons.

Rufinamide is a second-generation antiepileptic drug with a well-documented efficacy in both animal studies and human clinical trials (35, 40-42). Although it is sometimes described as “broad-spectrum”, this drug has a unique structure among antiepileptic drugs and its primary mechanism of action involves modulation of the inactivated state of sodium channels (42). Indeed, in Dup15q neurons, rufinamide hyperpolarized the voltage-dependence of steady-state inactivation, accelerated entry into slow inactivation, and delayed recovery from fast and slow inactivation types (Fig. 4). Furthermore, rufinamide successfully reduced action potential firing frequency and repetitive burst firing ability (Fig 4C; 4D). This supports a role of this drug in the management of treatment-resistant seizures such as Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (43), a syndrome that often manifests in Dup15q patients (7). In fact, the largest study that investigated seizure phenotypes and treatments in Dup15q syndrome reported low efficacy for GABAergic medications such as vigabatrin and other benzodiazepines in inhibiting seizures in Dup15q individuals. Moreover, the highest seizure response rate was reported in patients who were treated with rufinamide (7). This further supports the hypothesis that sodium channel inactivation deficits in Dup15q neurons may contribute to the severe seizure phenotype associated with the syndrome.

Overexpression of UBE3A is necessary for the development of hyperexcitability in Dup15q neurons (19), but the mechanisms leading to this hyperexcitability are yet to be determined. The sodium channel inactivation deficits uncovered in this study highlight a potential link between UBE3A and sodium channels. Posttranslational regulation of voltage-gated sodium channels has been previously reported for specific sodium channel subtypes. For example, the E3 ubiquitin ligases Nedd4 and Nedd4-2 have been shown to readily ubiquitinate Nav1.2 (44), a sodium channel subtype that has been linked to autism and various genetic epilepsy syndromes (45, 46). Such a change in subtype expression might significantly alter inactivation kinetics, as different sodium channel subtypes possess different activation and inactivation gating properties (47). Thus, a direct effect of UBE3A on sodium channels via ubiquitination of a specific subtype might be a potential mechanism leading to altered sodium channel kinetics. An alternative indirect mechanistic hypothesis might involve ubiquitination of a sodium channel modulator. For example, UBE3A-mediated regulation of PP2A activity via ubiquitination of PTPA can modulate CAMKII level, a known modulator of sodium channels in excitatory neurons that has been shown to enhance persistent sodium current when activated, and whose activity has been shown to be inhibited in Angelman syndrome (a syndrome linked to UBE3A deletion) (48-51). Further studies are needed to examine the potential link between UBE3A, its downstream effectors, and sodium channels.

Dup15q is a neurodevelopmental syndrome strongly associated with autism and intractable seizures. This study identifies – for the first time – dysfunctional sodium channel inactivation kinetics in Dup15q patient-derived neurons, a phenotype that has been previously linked to epileptic seizures. This dysfunction may underly many of the observed Dup15 cellular phenotypes, such as the increased sodium current density, increased persistent current, increased action potential amplitude, and increased action potential firing rate. Our data highlights a role for antiepileptic drugs that target sodium channel inactivation processes, such as rufinamide.

## Table 1. Electrophysiology protocols.

| Activation | Activation of sodium channels was evoked using 50 ms steps from −80 to +40 mV in 5 mV increments. Peak current (I Na ) was normalized to capacitance to obtain maximum current density. Cell capacitance was obtained from the C-slow capacitance compensation function (Patchmaster, HEKA Electronik) by applying short 5 mV hyperpolarizing square-wave pulses and measuring the leak-subtracted capacitive transient currents. Sodium conductance (G Na ) was calculated using the equation G Na = I Na / (E − E Na ). For each cell, conductance values were divided over the maximum conductance to obtain the normalized fractional conductance (G/Gmax). |
| Persistent current | Persistent current was obtained via averaging the current amplitude over the last 10% of the activation test pulse (from 45 to 50 ms) and the maximum value for each cell across all voltage steps was used for comparison. |
| Steady-state inactivation | Voltage-dependence of steady-state inactivation was assessed using 500 ms steps from −120 to 0 mV in 5 mV increments, followed directly by a depolarization step to +10 mV for 20 ms. For each cell, the current value in each voltage step was divided by the maximum current value to obtain (I/I max ). |
| Inactivation time constant (tau) | Current decay rate at different voltages was measured with 50 ms depolarizing steps from −80 to +40 mV. The peak of the current was determined and the decay phase from the peak was fitted to a single exponential function to obtain the decay tau. |
| Slow inactivation | Onset of slow inactivation was examined with 0 mV voltage pulses between 10 and 20,000 ms, followed by a 5 ms recovery to −120 mV, and a 0 mV, 25 ms test pulse. Voltage-dependence of slow inactivation was examined via 10 mV, 10 second depolarizing steps from −120 mV to +10 mV, followed by a 200 ms recovery pulse at −120 mV and a 50 ms test pulse at 0 mV. For each cell, the current value in each voltage step was divided by the maximum current value to obtain (I/I max ). |
| Recovery from fast inactivation | Recovery from fast inactivation was examined with 10 ms, 0 mV conditioning pulses and recovery pulses of −120 mV (between 10 and 1000 ms), followed by a 25-ms test pulse at 0 mV. |
| Recovery from slow inactivation | Recovery from slow inactivation was examined after a 1 second, 0 mV conditioning pulse, followed by recovery pulses of −120 mV (between 10 and 1000 ms) and a 25 ms test pulse at 0 mV. |
| Action potential firing | Current was injected to hold the cells at ~−70 mV, and action potentials were evoked via 500 ms current steps from −10 to +80 pA in 5 pA increments. Action potential amplitude, threshold, width, and latency were measured using the first action potential from the minimum current injection that elicited a train of at least 3 action potentials. The current injection step that elicited the maximum number of action potentials was used to compare the firing frequency. As shown in the frequency vs. current plot in Supplemental Fig. 1F , this protocol was sufficient to reach a steady-state maximum firing rate in Dup15q and Corr.Control neurons. |
| Input resistance | Input resistance was calculated from a 5 pA subthreshold current injection by dividing the applied current by the resultant change in steady-state voltage. |
| Burst modulation | Burst modulation was examined via a series of four 40 pA/ 200 ms pulses with interpulse intervals of either 10 or 1000 ms. These pulses were repeated in separate traces with 60, 80, and 100 pA injections to confirm that maximum firing was achieved in each cell. The trace with the maximum number of action potentials was used to calculate the burst frequency. |
