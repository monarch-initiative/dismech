Friedreich ataxia is a rare, autosomal recessive neurogenerative disorder caused 
by mutations to the frataxin (FXN) gene resulting in loss of functional FXN 
protein. Applications of cutting-edge genetic and cellular therapies expand 
therapeutic options for patients with rare, genetic diseases including 
Friedreich ataxia. Multiple investigational techniques and strategies seek to 
permanently alter the disease course in patients with Friedreich ataxia, 
although no product has established definitive benefit. This review catalogs 
both the history and ongoing efforts of genetic and cellular therapies applied 
to Friedreich ataxia and its disease models, including therapeutic efficacy and 
adverse effects. We list the key limitations and cautions of such therapies, 
chiefly those of potential FXN overexpression toxicity, critical therapeutic 
windows, and adverse effects of these therapies applicable to any disease 
target. As gene and cellular therapy continue to diversify in design and 
strategy, Friedreich ataxia patients will likely have multiple therapeutic 
options in the future from both investigational therapies described here and 
future ones yet to be optimized.

1 Introduction
Friedreich ataxia (FRDA) is an autosomal recessive ataxia 
that typically manifests in childhood with limb ataxia, gait 
instability, and areflexia. Patients progressively decline in 
motor function and develop sensory deficits, cardiomyo-
pathy, scoliosis, and variably diabetes, optic neuropathy, 
and auditory dysfunction. It is caused by mutations in the 
frataxin (FXN) gene, predominantly by GAA triplet-repeat 
expansions in intron 1 that profoundly limit the amount 
of functional FXN protein by suppressing transcription 
[1]. About 4% of FRDA patients are compound heterozy -
gous for a GAA expanded allele and one non-traditional 
disease-causing FXN allele, consisting of point mutations 
or indels in FXN. About 1:50,000 individuals are diag-
nosed with FRDA in the USA and Europe, with a carrier 
frequency of about 1 in 100 [2 ]. Mechanistically, FXN 
facilitates iron-sulfur cluster synthesis, providing this 
vital prosthetic group for the Electron Transport Chain 
(ETC), mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, and many other 
enzymes in the cell. Without FXN, these bioenergetic pro-
cesses are impaired, and iron can accumulate in mitochon-
dria, potentially leading to increased oxidative stress [3 ]. 
FXN deficiency impacts highly metabolic tissues such as 
the heart and nervous system, the latter of which dem-
onstrates marked degeneration of the dorsal root ganglia 
(DRG), cerebellar dentate nucleus (DN), corticospinal

Various investigational molecular therapies have 
reached clinical trials to correct frataxin deficiency 
from FRDA-causing mutations. Such therapies aim to 
increase FXN protein expression through gene addition/
replacement, DNA editing, epigenetic reversal, or peptide 
replacement. Some rely on viral vectors to deliver genetic 
material for FXN replacement or editing; presently, they 
investigate multiple administration strategies to efficiently 
target affected tissues in FRDA. Cellular therapies seek 
to provide donor or autologous cells that secrete protec-
tive factors in degenerating tissues or perhaps replace 
resident FXN-deficient cells in such tissues. Such cutting-
edge therapies have gained federal approval for other rare 
and common diseases, and at present in FRDA they have 
advanced to clinical trials. This review catalogs the gene 
and cellular therapies being pursued to treat FRDA, from 
active clinical trials to mechanistic pre-clinical studies.
2  Gene Therapy
2.1  Gene Addition
Gene-targeted therapies comprise multiple interventional 
strategies aimed at correcting pathologic DNA sequence 
variants, gene expression, or splicing. The gene addition 
strategy delivers extrachromosomal DNA encoding the wild-
type sequence of a deficient or dysfunctional gene. Currently 
initiated genetic therapies for FRDA in clinical trials aim to 
deliver a copy of the FXN gene to affected tissues (Fig.  1). 
Delivery of the human FXN gene (hFXN) to FXN-deficient 
hearts in FRDA mouse models rescues both the molecular 
phenotype (mitochondrial and sarcomeric deficiencies) and 
improves cardiac function and long-term survival [6 , 7]. 
Encouragingly, functional improvement in these hearts can 
occur when only 50% of cardiomyocytes re-express FXN 
[7], suggesting that less than 100% transduction efficiency 
might achieve clinical benefit (Fig. 2). Moreover, intraperi-
toneal injection of viral vectors containing hFXN improved 
function in FXN-conditional knockout in cardiac muscle and 
neural tissue, respectively, and improved survival [8]. Viral 
delivery of hFXN can be achieved in non-human primates 
(NHPs), even at the low viral infusion titers [9]. These foun-
dational studies demonstrate the potential benefit of deliver-
ing hFXN to humans to prevent cardiac decompensation.

While delivering copies of genes is routine in cell cul -
ture and in mice, the difficulty of delivering genetic material 
to human cells complicates such approaches, particularly 
due to the low transduction rate. Hence, great emphasis 
is placed on optimizing the vector, the vehicle that deliv -
ers the gene, and particularly its capsid, the outer cover -
ing. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is the most used vector 
for clinical applications because of its relative safety and 
in vivo efficiency. Astellas Gene Therapies using single IV 
administration of the AAV8 serotype to deliver FXN to car-
diac and skeletal muscle FXN-conditional knockout mouse 
(MCK-Cre) demonstrates improved survival and preserved 
ejection fraction (Table  1) [10]. The Phase I clinical trial 
(NCT06483802) of this product was planned but ultimately 
withdrawn. Artificially modifying natural AAV serotypes 
can optimize tissue specificity and delivery efficiency, as 
seen with AAVrh.10hFXN, also called LX2006, developed 
by Lexeo Therapeutics. This technology comprises a recom-
binant AAV optimized to deliver hFXN to the heart via a 
single infusion. Interim updates on two ongoing clinical tri-
als of LX2006 (NCT05445323, NCT05302271) find that 
administration reduces left ventricular mass index (LVMI) 
and LV wall thickness on echocardiographs, reduces tro-
ponin I after at least 6 months, and increases FXN protein in 
cardiac biopsies [11]. Such results demonstrate drug activ -
ity and downstream effects, but the direct relationship of 
hypertrophy to eventual systolic dysfunction and death is not 
linear; only longer observation times can truly demonstrate 
clinical benefit [12]. Still, it represents a significant result in 
this moderate-duration study.
While LX2006 aims to manage FRDA cardiomyopathy, 
Solid Bioscience has developed SGT-212, an AAV approach 
that expresses FXN in the heart and CNS via dual routes 
of administration: MRI-guided intradentate nucleus (IDN) 
infusion and systemic IV delivery. Single administration 
of SGT-212 in parvalbumin-Cre mice driving conditional 
Fxn knockout in DRG and cerebellar neurons or striated 
muscle (MCK-Cre) improves neurobehavioral and cardiac 
function, respectively, and increases survival in both mod-
els [13]. Testing on NHPs shows their dual-route approach 
successfully expresses hFXN protein in the DN and heart 
[14]. MRI-guided neurosurgical placement of cannulas in 
the DN of cadavers demonstrates the technical feasibility 
of such an approach in humans [15]. In 2026, the FAL -

first patient [16]. However, this approach has study-specific 
limitations. Peripherally, the exact tropism of SGT212 for

In contrast, Capsida Biotherapeutics similarly seeks to 
deliver wildtype FXN to all affected tissues but with a single 
IV administration of their modified vector. Their product, 
CAP-004, is a modified viral capsid capable of penetrat-
ing the blood brain barrier (BBB) while minimizing non-
target transduction. CAP-004 increases hFXN expression 
in multiple neural tissues and left ventricle in non-human 
primates, with less transduction in non-target tissues com-
pared to AAV9 [19]. It remains to be seen if CAP-004 can 
improve symptomatic features of FRDA in animal models, 
but this preliminary data bodes well for efficacy in FXN 
re-expression and its safety profile. However, the Phase I/
IIa trial on their related product CAP-002 aiming to treat 
STXBP1-related Developmental and Epileptic Encephalopa-

participant, so the future of CAP-004 is uncertain [20]. Sep-
arately, a collaboration between Neurocrine Biosciences and 
Voyager Therapeutics aims to develop the latter’s TRACER 
AAV capsid for IV delivery of a FXN replacement gene to 
the brain. The product, NBIB-′223, has no publicly available 
data of its preclinical studies at the time of writing, but are 
presumptively in final studies for IND status. StrideBio has 
also been developing a modified capsid designed for efficient 
BBB crossing to treat FRDA, but after their acquisition by 
Ginkgo Bioworks, the future of this product is unknown. 
These gene-addition trials have the potential to prevent fur-
ther clinical decline in FRDA if their translational studies 
yield consistent, long-term benefits for participants.
There are limitations and cautions with these strate-
gies that must be considered when predicting prognosis 
and potential adverse effects of gene addition. They must 
be carefully monitored to avoid increasing FXN protein 
expression to toxic levels. hFXN overexpression in cardiac 
tissue in mice is safe up to ninefold, but toxic at 20-fold 
[21]. Special consideration of the delivered construct and 
vector must ensure efficient transduction and that FXN does 
not reach such toxic levels by optimizing the promoter and 
other such elements. However, it remains unknown how sta-
ble the delivered gene is once it enters target cells. Current 
trials have not continued long enough to assess long-term 
responses, and most animal studies do not assess transgene 
degradation after delivery. If such degradation occurs, it may 
affect the prognosis of those treated with these strategies 
and necessitate repeat administrations. However, this raises 
another limitation of viral vectors on whether subsequent 
administrations of AAV beyond the planned one-time 
administration are efficacious or safe. Immunogenicity of 
AAVs is a common concern in gene therapy, and patients 
may develop acquired immunity to the vector after the first 
administration. Those with pre-existing antibodies are com-
monly ineligible to receive this form of treatment. Hence, 
neutralizing antibodies may block the second administration 
of the same AAV or the first administration of similar AAVs 
targeting different tissues. The immune response to the vec-
tor could be managed with short-term immune suppression 
at the time the AAV is delivered, but this strategy poses 
inherent risks. Of greatest concern are the acute and delayed 
toxicities of AAV gene delivery, as the infusion of these 
viral particles leads to substantially higher circulating virus 
than typically seen during systemic viral transduction [22]. 
Excessive activation of complement and cytotoxic T-cells 
will damage tissues that can be compounded by antibodies 
against capsid proteins leading to thrombotic microangiopa-
thy [23, 24]. In addition, various degrees of hepatoxicity 
commonly manifest in primate and human studies of AAV 
delivery, ranging from transiently elevated liver enzymes 
to acute liver failure [25, 26]. Dorsal root ganglion toxicity 
also occurs, but this may be moot in FRDA as such cells are 
likely absent in FRDA patients at presentation [ 17]. These 
broad risks necessitate multidisciplinary monitoring by vari-
ous consulting services in patients receiving viral vector-
delivered gene therapy to ensure safety. Nonetheless, FXN  
gene delivery is in some ways the most developed genetic 
therapy approach available for FRDA patients, and its effi-
cacy and safety will be closely monitored to determine its 
readiness for Phase 3 trials.
2.2  Gene Editing
With the advent of CRISPR technology, the prior concep-
tualization of genetic editing to correct pathological muta-
tions in patients has become a reality. In vivo gene editing 
delivers the molecular machinery necessary to edit the chro-
mosomal DNA of target tissues within the patient. In vivo 
CRISPR editing of chromosomal DNA in humans has been 
pursued clinically in hereditary angioedema [27] and CPS1 
deficiency [28 ]. Strategies of in vivo gene editing extend 
beyond correcting mutations to wild-type sequences and 
include inactivating or dampening gene expression that 
contributes to pathology despite the related sequence being 
otherwise wild-type. Preclinical work in FRDA-derived 
patient cells demonstrates that it is possible to remove the 
entire GAA-repeat expansion using zinc-finger nucleases 
(ZFN) or CRISPR in vitro. Removal of the GAA repeats 
from the FXN gene of patient-derived induced pluripotent 
stem cells (iPSCs) increases FXN protein expression and 
restores dysregulated gene expression associated with the 
FRDA cellular phenotype [29, 30]. Similarly, removal of

methylated region in intron 1 and the GAA repeats further 
improves FXN expression and restores the neuronal pheno-

for removing the GAA repeats and other repressive elements 
in the FXN gene as a preventive therapy for GAA repeat-

repeats from iPSCs before differentiating them into neurons 
or cardiomyocytes, distinct from in vivo gene editing of tar-
get tissues that have already developed in utero in the context 
of FXN deficiency. AAV-delivered CRISPR-Cas9 with guide 
RNAs (gRNA) directing removal of GAA repeats in YG8 
and YG8sR mice demonstrates sufficient GAA removal in 
heart, liver, and skeletal muscle, but only increases FXN in 
the liver [33]. While removal of the expanded GAA repeats 
is possible in vivo, further optimization at the preclinical 
level is required to demonstrate reversal of both molecular 
and functional phenotypes in FRDA animal models. Such 
approaches would have many of the same adverse issues as 
in gene addition, but require delivery of a larger amount of 
material, thus complicating transduction of large numbers of 
cells. However, gene-editing approaches would return FXN 
regulation toward endogenous mechanisms rather than intro-
ducing an uncontrollable FXN gene, a strategy that is less 
likely to restore normal physiology.
Alternatively, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) could 
provide a gene-editing approach to benefit FRDA in both 
GAA-repeat-associated alleles and select non-GAA muta-
tions. ASO therapy delivers a short nucleotide sequence that 
binds to pathologically associated DNA elements or RNA 
molecules, degrading targeted RNAs, or promoting a spe-
cific transcript derived from alternative splicing. In FRDA, 
ASOs have been applied to both GAA-repeat-associated 
pathology and point mutations. Modified ASOs that target 
the GAA repeat increase FXN protein more than threefold 
in patient cell lines [34, 35]. Similarly, the c.165+5G>C 
splice-site mutation is amendable to ASO therapy, as ASO 
targeting this mutation corrects aberrant splicing and pro-
duces transcript synthesizing functional FXN [36]. These 
studies provide rationale for ASO therapy in clinical trials 
as a more accessible option for restoring FXN expression.
The most notable limitation for gene editing and target-
ing patient chromatin is the potential for off-target editing, 
which could adversely alter gene expression in patient tis -
sues. This off-target editing is a concern of any CRISPR-
based approach, as the non-specificity of gRNAs may direct 
Cas9 to damage multiple chromatin loci, which may be del-
eterious irrespective of whether it affects a non-coding gene. 
In FRDA, such a risk is theoretically doubled as remov -
ing the GAA repeat expansion requires two unique gRNAs. 
Off-target effects are also concerning for ASO therapy. 
Furthermore, successful in vivo editing in animals has not 
consistently restored FXN expression, a minimum require-
ment for investigating such strategies clinically. One pos-
sible solution to this issue may be to remove both the GAA 
expansion and the methylated region upstream of it [31]. At 
present, gene editing of the FXN gene by removing the GAA 
expansion requires further preclinical work to demonstrate 
consistent benefit in animal models and in terminally dif-
ferentiated affected cell types.
2.3  Epigenetic Therapy
Unlike genetic therapies, epigenetic therapies target chroma-
tin modifications and impact gene expression without modi-
fying the DNA sequence of affected genes. These therapies 
often use more traditional small-molecule pharmaceuticals 
to inhibit specific classes of enzymes that modify chromatin 
organization. Classical FRDA caused by GAA expansions 
involves repressive chromatin modifications at the FXN  
gene and its regulatory elements. These include increased 
DNA methylation at CpG islands in the FXN gene and 
decreased occupancy by acetylated histones permissive to 
expression, with a reciprocal increase in repressive histone 
marks (e.g., H3K9me3 and H3K27me3). Initial attention 
has been on histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) as a 
means increase FXN mRNA transcription. The most notable 
HDACi in FRDA was RG2833, a brain-penetrant molecule 
that increases FXN protein expression in multiple cellular 
and rodent models [37, 38]. Patients treated with RG2833 
show increased levels of FXN mRNA and increased histone 
acetylation at the gene locus [39]. Ultimately, RG2833 failed 
to demonstrate sufficient benefit to FXN expression, and its 
use is confounded by potentially problematic metabolites. 
The effects of RG2833 on epigenetic marks of the FXN gene 
show that DNA methylation persists with inhibitor treatment 
[40]. These data suggest that inhibiting HDACs alone may 
not be sufficient to reverse the repressive epigenetic changes 
at the FXN gene.
Current clinical trials utilize an epigenetic approach in 
FRDA with Design Therapeutics product DT-216P2. This 
investigational drug leverages a technology dubbed Gen-
eTAC that promotes transcription elongation of nascent FXN 
mRNA through the GAA-repeat expansion. This results in 
increased full-length FXN mRNA and protein expression 
[36]. A prior formulation of DT-216 increased FXN mRNA 
expression in skeletal muscle biopsies of FRDA participants 
[41]. This formulation was largely considered safe with the 
notable exception of injection site thrombophlebitis that 
has been addressed with DT-216P2 in the RESTORE-FA 
Phase I/II trial (NCT06874010) [42]. This application of 
GeneTAC in FRDA offers a key advantage over previous 
epigenetic therapeutics. DT-216P2 shows specificity for the

GAA-repeat expansion, likely having fewer global effects on 
chromatin while also targeting the genetic pathophysiology 
of FRDA. However, the clinical efficacy of this new technol-
ogy in FRDA remains to be seen.
Epigenetic therapies avoid the risks of genetic therapies, 
which can cause irreversible DNA damage, mutations, or 
errant gene expression. The adverse effects of epigenetic 
therapies are hopefully reversible with medication with-
drawal. However, there are multiple concerns regarding both 
the safety and efficacy of these medications. Drugs that tar-
get entire classes of epigenetic enzymes may have global 
consequences. For instance, global HDAC inhibition may 
de-repress inappropriate gene programs aside from FXN. 
DT216P2 addresses this limitation somewhat by binding 
to repeat sequences, but the off-target effects of this class 
of novel therapeutics on microsatellite repeats are not well 
understood. Nonetheless, targeting epigenetic enzymes or 
processes through pharmacologic approaches may still rep-
resent a niche of potential therapies in FRDA, as to date 
most attention has been given to HDACs and histone acety-
lation. However, there has been little clinical exploration of 
DNA or histone methylation, alone or in conjunction with 
previous therapies, as a means of FXN gene activation.
2.4  Peptide Replacement
A final approach to reversing the genetic defect is to supply 
FXN itself as a protein product. Such approaches will have 
some of the limitations of gene therapy (distribution to rele-
vant tissues, potential immunogenicity) and issues of limited 
stability. Proteins are intrinsically less stable than DNA, and 
such agents will require more frequent administration (daily 
in some cases). Still, they are simpler to develop in many 
situations. The first such form, TAT-MTS-hFXN (nomlabo-
fusp) has entered clinical trials after preclinical development 
[43]. In the Fxn-MCK mouse, a model with severe cardio-
myopathy, it extends life expectancy and corrects many of 
the structural and biochemical features of FRDA cardiomyo-
pathy [44]. Similarly, it decreases pathological features of 
neurons derived from FRDA mice. However, its CNS pen-
etration in mice is unclear, but it is still  a potential cardiac 
therapy. In human trials, it restores FXN levels to almost car-
rier levels in skin (a proxy for peripheral tissues) with daily 
injection, showing that, based on correlations with rodents, it 
likely reaches all peripheral tissues including affected heart 
and skeletal muscle. However, no published evidence shows 
BBB penetration at present, and no placebo-controlled data 
is available on CNS benefit [44]. Furthermore, re-adminis-
tration gives rise to a high rate of anaphylaxis, presumptively 
reflecting the TAT motif, confounding easy management 
in clinical situations. Still, it provides a paradigm through 
which more advanced approaches can overcome the issues 
of limited CNS penetration and immune response through 
alternative cell penetrant approaches.
3  Cellular Therapy
Cellular therapies provide an evolving field of investigational 
interventions aimed at ameliorating disease by introducing 
donor or autologous edited cells that replace diseased cells 
or secrete protective factors into affected tissues. Recent 
investigations are examining the role of hematopoietic stem 
and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and mesenchymal stem cell 
(MSC) transplants in ameliorating various diseases, from 
heart failure to neurodegeneration [45]. Transplantation 
of these cells from healthy donors or from edited autolo-
gous cells may exert protective effects by secreting soluble 
factors or by replacing select cell types, such as resident 
macrophages or microglia. In FRDA mouse models, periph-
eral delivery of wild-type HSPC in YG8R mice improves 
muscle weakness, DRG degeneration, and neural bioener -
getics, mechanistically believed to be due to donor HSPC 
engraftment in the brain and differentiation into microglia 
that transfer wild-type FXN to affected neurons [46– 48]. 
Similar phenotypic improvement is seen with wild-type 
MSC intrathecal delivery in FRDA YG8 mice, which dem-
onstrate improved motor function and DRG survival, from 
MSC migration to DRGs and secretion of neurotrophic fac-
tors [49–51]. Implantation of donor cells into affected brains 
may provide neuroprotective benefits in FRDA, as implanta-
tion of FRDA-iPSC-derived microglial progenitors into the 
cerebellum of non-FRDA mice recapitulates the neurode-
generation observed in FRDA, suggesting that replacement 
of such cells with FXN wildtype progenitor cells may be a 
therapeutic approach [52]. Interestingly, the benefit to these 
YG8 FRDA mice is present with MSCs derived from both 
wild-type and YG8 mice themselves, suggesting that secre-
tion of these neurotrophic factors does not depend on FXN 
expression; thus, clinical application of this strategy may 
not require healthy donor or even edited autologous MSCs 
[49]. Altogether, these studies suggest that MSC/HSPC 
transplantation represents a potentially effective means to 
ameliorate neurological decline in FRDA patients. Still, this 
approach may require autologous bone marrow transplanta-
tion, a procedure with significant and tangible complications 
[48]. There are no cellular therapy trials for FRDA active 
or recruiting presently, but the preclinical studies described 
here provide proof of concept for designing future trials. 
These studies are in fact the basis for Papillon Therapeu -
tics’ PPL-001, a strategy that seeks to use CRISPR-edited 
removal of GAA repeats from autologous HSPCs to restore 
FXN expression. This product has received an Orphan Drug 
Designation from the FDA, so the first cellular therapy for 
FRDA entering clinical trials is on the horizon.

A limitation of cellular therapy is immune-mediated 
rejection of donor cells. Autologous cell donation could 
circumvent immune rejection, although genetic editing of 
autologous cells may inadvertently contribute to immuno-
genicity. Furthermore, if HSPC transplants from healthy 
donors require bone marrow depletion in FRDA recipients, 
then implementation of such a protocol as a clinical trial 
would impose a risk of bone marrow failure. If the mecha-
nisms of the described cell therapies occur in patients, then 
the benefit of microglial replacement in nervous tissue is still 
questionable, as any protective effect these therapies may 
exert, whether by secretion of neurotropic factors or trans-
fer of FXN protein, may not result in functional recovery if 
neurodegeneration is too advanced by the time of diagnosis 
and management.
4  Overall Limitations
Three issues confound all attempts to replace FXN in ani-
mals. First, while immunoassays of mass spectrometry-
based approaches readily quantify FXN levels in solubilized 
tissue, details of quantification in other tissues are less well 
defined, and the relevant tissue for FXN assessment is com-
monly inaccessible [53, 54]. Muscle and cardiac tissue can 
be tested for FXN through biopsies (with different levels of 
invasiveness), but FXN cannot be assessed in living brain, 
and FXN is not a normal component of CSF. Thus, detection 
of increases in FXN in CNS must rely on downstream bio-
markers in affected tissue for quantification of FXN replace-
ment. Among soluble serum-based biomarkers, circulating 
neurofilament light (NFL) is elevated in younger FRDA 
patients, but is confounded by the age dependence of NFL; it 
is also higher in normal children for reasons that are unclear 
[55–58]. As a cytoskeletal protein, its levels conceivably 
could rise during normal axonal growth and repair, making 
interpretation of incomplete responses difficult [59]. Simi-
lar issues confound use of troponin as a marker of cardiac 
disease [60–62]. It is high consistently in FRDA in a man-
ner that is minimally predictive of eventual outcome. Other 
blood biomarkers of disease status in FRDA are presently 
incompletely explored [63–65].
Imaging biomarkers using brain MRI may provide alter-
natives to serum biomarkers, as volume loss of brainstem 
regions, cerebellar DN, inferior and superior cerebellar 
peduncles are established disease features appreciable on 
MRI [66– 68]. Additionally, cervical spine cross-sectional 
area decreases with disease progression, providing a simple 
and accessible means of examining therapeutic response at a 
broad range of institutions [69]. Volume loss of these regions 
correlates with disease onset, duration, and severity [70], thus 
these imaging modalities may provide a meaningful neurologi-
cal readout if the therapeutic interventions discussed here are 
administered early in the disease course. However, the change 
over time of most of these is slow, and in unselected cohorts 
may not be sufficient to detect change over short time periods. 
Thus, they may be most useful for cohort selection to enhance 
the sensitivity of response.
Second, what level of FXN replacement is needed for clini-
cal meaning? In unaffected tissue, the most severely affected 
patients have FXN levels around 2–5% of normal with the least 
affected patients having up to 30–40% of control [71, 72]. In 
affected tissues the level appears much lower, but the exact 
level defining clinical disease is unclear. The inactivation of 
cellular alleles through epigenetic silencing suggests that the 
correct measure is not the level of frataxin, but the number of 
cells that have been restored to a minimal sufficient level. The 
regulation of FXN levels differs somewhat between tissues, 
so extrapolating from blood levels to affected tissue does not 
clearly have meaning. In mice, animals can have as low as 2% 
of normal FXN levels without developing a phenotype, and 
cell culture models suggest that normal cellular functions can 
occur with levels less than 10% of normal [73–75]. This sug-
gests that restoration of FXN to carrier levels is unnecessary, 
but the level that is clinically meaningful in specific tissues is 
not yet well-defined.
Third, all FXN restoration methods must address the 
issue of timing the therapy to disease course. While FRDA 
is a progressive disease, many of the neurological changes 
appear before presentation. This includes loss of large DRG 
cells before presentation, and early MRI changes happen-
ing by 5 years of age (Fig.  3) [68, 76]. Restoration of FXN 
after such times may provide limited benefit, though other 
affected tissues such as cardiac and skeletal muscle may have 
fewer developmental issues. During typical clinical progres-
sion, the major loss of neurons and axons occurs in the DN 
and in the corticospinal tract, but other areas such as the 
Purkinje cells may be involved in FRDA pathophysiology 
[77–80]. In later stages of disease (and potentially earlier), 
the retina becomes a clear target [81–83]. Unfortunately, pre-
clinical experiments in animals with gene replacement and 
gene editing have generally not addressed these issues; most 
of the experiments have been performed in models in which 
FXN is knocked down after birth, thus, they model subacute 
FXN deficiency, not FRDA. Results comparing animal and 
cellular models can provide discrepant results. Future stud-
ies must employ more work in the congenital FRDA models 
rather than the subacute knockdown models to provide better 
evidence of benefit relevant to huma disease.
5  Conclusion
Advances in gene therapies have been rapid in the last dec-
ade and have considerable momentum to address genetic 
disorders such as FRDA. Gene addition strategies are the 

Advances in Gene and Cellular Therapy in Friedreich Ataxia
furthest along in clinical trials and may represent the first 
gene therapy for FRDA, while in cellular therapies, the 
indication of MSC transplantation for neuroprotection in 
general may allow for their application in FRDA indirectly. 
The goal of any of these strategies is to prevent or reverse 
neurological dysfunction by restoring FXN expression or 
the degenerative consequences of its deficiency. Special 
considerations that gene therapy must bear in mind for 
FRDA are: (1) FXN overexpression toxicity, (2) transduc-
tion efficiency in affected tissues, and (3) timing of the 
intervention during the neurodegenerative process to dem-
onstrate meaningful clinical improvements. These issues 
and others are actively discussed among FRDA experts 
and guide trial design and implementation. The current 
genetic therapies approach FRDA from diverse angles. 
Epigenetic modification and ASO increase endogenous 
FXN gene expression, which avoids FXN protein overex-
pression and chromatin modification, but these techniques 
lack tissue specificity and may affect expression of off-
target genes. Gene addition or editing require specific or 
modified vectors that facilitate tissue specificity but then 
pose the risk of toxic protein overexpression, off-target 
chromatin modification, or immunogenicity of the host 
to said vector. Furthermore, these vectors are typically 
designed to transduce only a single affected tissue, and if 
the immune response to one AAV-associated vector gen-
eralized to others, then a patient receiving AAV-delivered 
gene therapy targeting cardiomyocytes may be ineligible 
to receive a similar AAV-delivered therapy targeting neu-
rons. This possibility has left many patients to reconsider 
participating in current AAV-associated gene therapies if 
only one affected tissue can be restored. Peptide replace-
ment benefits by supplying only the target protein with-
out any direct effects on host DNA, but the requirement 
of FXN protein to be fused to potentially immunogenic 
peptides required to facilitate membrane permeability 
introduces the risk of anaphylaxis. Cellular therapies via 
autologous HSPC/MSC transplantation may avoid the 
issues with gene/peptides replacement or gene editing 
altogether by providing direct neuroprotective effects or 
even leveraging mitochondrial transfer to restore FXN 
expression in affected tissues. However, these mechanisms 
demand further investigation and ultimately validation in 
human participants. These various strategies have dem-
onstrated their efficacy in animal models, but at present it 
is too early to say whether there is clear benefit for FRDA 
patients. The possibility that FRDA may have a therapy 
that can prevent, or even reverse, disease progression is 
becoming a more concrete ideal with these advances in 
gene and cellular therapy.
