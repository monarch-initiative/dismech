BACKGROUND: Mevalonate kinase deficiency (MKD) is a rare autoinflammatory 
disease, and mevalonic aciduria (MA) is a severe phenotype of MKD. The present 
study reports the characteristics of MKD and four novel mutations in the 
mevalonate kinase (MVK) gene in a Chinese cohort.
METHOD: A retrospective study was conducted on patients diagnosed with MKD from 
July 2013 to December 2024. The clinical, immunological, and follow-up data were 
collected from electronic medical records. Next-generation sequencing and Sanger 
sequencing were performed to identify gene mutations. A literature review was 
performed on MKD patients to further investigate the associations between 
genotype and phenotype.
RESULTS: Eleven MKD patients were enrolled from a Chinese cohort of prolonged 
and recurrent fever of unknown origin. Ten patients were classified as having 
hyperimmunoglobulin D syndrome (HIDS), and one patient was classified as having 
MA. The median follow-up duration was 5 years (IQR: 1.5-6 years). Recurrent 
fever and gastrointestinal symptoms were the most common symptoms. Anemia was 
observed in 8 of the 11 patients, including one patient with severe 
hematological complications. Growth restriction (5/11 patients) and 
developmental delay (4/11 patients) were also observed. IgD levels were measured 
in ten patients, and the median IgD level was 85.23 µg/ml (IQR: 
18.74-385.19 µg/ml). Four novel mutation sites in the MVK gene were discovered: 
c.78G > A, c.463G > A, c.1076C > T and c.1088G > A. Etanercept was the effective 
biological agent tested, leading to complete or partial remission in 5 of the 6 
patients. A literature review of 20 MA patients suggested that homozygous MVK 
gene mutations are more frequently associated with MA. Moreover, MA patients 
with the homozygous A334T mutation present a milder phenotype, and those with 
the I268T homozygous mutation present a more severe phenotype.
CONCLUSIONS: This study is the largest case series of MKD pediatric patients 
from China. Four new mutation sites in MVK were identified, further expanding 
the phenotypic and genotypic spectrum of MKD and emphasizing the significance of 
MVK mutation patterns in influencing disease severity.

Mevalonate kinase deficiency (MKD) is a rare autosomal recessive autoinflammatory disease caused by mutations in the mevalonate kinase (MVK) gene located on chromosome 12q24 [1]. MKD can be classified as hyperimmunoglobulin D syndrome (HIDS), a milder form, or mevalonic aciduria (MA), a more severe form, according to distinct clinical characteristics and increased levels of mevalonic acid excreted in the urine [2].


MKD usually manifests in the first year of life and is characterized by an autoinflammatory phenotype [3]. The clinical manifestations of MKD include recurrent fever, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, arthritis, abdominal pain, and rash [4]. Generally, MKD episodes occur spontaneously or are triggered by vaccination, infection or stress. Patients with HIDS often experience bouts of recurrent fever lasting 4–6 days. Compared with HIDS, MA has an earlier age of onset and more severe symptoms, including recurrent fever, cognitive impairment, psychomotor impairment, progressive cerebellar ataxia, dysmorphic features and progressive visual impairment [5]. The long-term complications of MKD include amyloidosis, joint contractures and intestinal adhesions.

Early identification and accurate diagnosis of diseases are crucial for the prognosis of pediatric patients. However, owing to the rarity of MKD, achieving early and accurate diagnoses has been challenging. For example, in the European Fever Registry, among 114 registered MKD patients, the median age of onset was 6 months, but the median age at diagnosis was 6.5 years [6]. Similarly, van der Hilst et al. reported that among 103 patients from 18 countries, the average age at diagnosis was 9.9 years [7]. Delayed diagnosis could lead to serious complications, such as severe organ inflammation, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies for affected pediatric patients. Therefore, enhancing our understanding of the phenotypic diversity and genotype‒phenotype associations in MKD is crucial.

Since MKD was first identified in 1984 [8], more than 300 cases have been reported globally [5–7, 9–11], with HIDS being the predominant clinical phenotype. The incidence of MKD is mainly concentrated in Caucasians, especially in European countries such as the Netherlands and Italy [12]. Fewer patients have been reported in Asia, including small cohorts in Japan and India [9, 13] and a few case reports in China [14, 15]. In the present study, we collected and analyzed the clinical and genetic characteristics of 11 patients from the Children's Hospital of Fudan University, establishing the first MKD patient cohort in China. The aim of this study was to provide valuable guidance for clinical practitioners.

We enrolled MKD patients from our cohort of prolonged and recurrent fever of unknown origin (FUO) patients who were referred to our hospital between July 2013 and December 2024 [16–18]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinical features of MKD, including onset age less than 1 year, gastrointestinal symptoms, cervical lymph node enlargement, periodic fever episodes, oral ulcers, or maculopapular rash [4]; and (2) two variations in the MVK gene. Follow-up was conducted every 3 to 6 months via outpatient visits and/or telephone consultations, and annual assessments were recommended. The relevant data were retrospectively summarized in detail. Previous clinical characteristics and genetic phenotypes reported in PubMed, EMBASE, Ovid, Wanfang Data, CNKI, and the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database were reviewed. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all participating patients.

Routine blood tests and detection of inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and serum amyloid A (SAA), were performed to identify any abnormalities that could be related to MKD. Serum immunoglobulins, including IgG, IgA and IgM, were detected via nephelometry. The distribution of lymphocyte subsets was determined via flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) using. Validated antibodies, including anti-CD3 (UCHT1), anti-CD8 (RPAT8), anti-CD4 (RPA-T4), anti-CD19 (HIB19), anti-CD16 (3G8), and anti-CD56 (QA17A16) antibodies (all from BD Biosciences). IgD plasma levels were estimated via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Gas chromatography‒mass spectrometry (GC‒MS) was used to measure the levels of methylmalonic acid in the urine. Complete remission was defined as the disappearance of all clinical symptoms and the return of CRP levels and the ESR to normal values during the follow-up period. Partial remission was defined as not meeting the criteria for complete remission but exhibiting a reduction of at least 50% in the average number of fever episodes per year and/or the absence of persistent inflammatory disease during follow-up.

The QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) to extract DNA from the peripheral blood samples of patients and their parents. The quality of the DNA was evaluated with a NanoDrop ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The ratios of the absorbance values at 260 nm and 280 nm (A260/A280) were used to assess the purity of the DNA, with a ratio of approximately 1.8 indicating a high-quality sample.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was conducted with a panel encompassing all previously documented immunodeficiency genes. Patient genomic DNA fragments were ligated with adaptors, forming two paired-end DNA libraries with insert sizes of 500 bp for each sample. The DNA libraries were subsequently enriched and sequenced on the HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After sequencing, the variants were annotated via ANNOVAR and Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) software, and the potential functional impact of the identified mutations was predicted via SIFT, PolyPhen-2, and Mutation Taster.

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Numeric variables are presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR). A t test was used to determine the differences in inflammatory mediator levels between acute attacks and the remission period. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with SPSS 21.

The initial diagnosis was made at a median age of 50 months (IQR, 8–62.0 months), with a median diagnostic delay of 48 months (IQR,6.0–59.0 months). Recurrent fever episodes lasting from 2 to 14 days (mean, 5.5 days) were observed in all patients. The peak temperature of each fever episode was greater than 38 °C. During the initial onset of the disease, six patients (55%) presented with fever accompanied by respiratory symptoms such as cough, tonsillar erythema and suppuration. Four patients (36%) experienced gastrointestinal symptoms, such as vomiting, abdominal pain and diarrhea. Lymphadenopathy and musculoskeletal symptoms were also observed. During subsequent febrile episodes, dizziness and headache were frequently recorded (P1, P2, and P4).

Clinical manifestations are shown in Fig. 1. Lymphadenopathy being the most prevalent (10/11 patients). Cervical lymph node enlargement (8/10 patients) was more common than axillary or inguinal involvement. Gastrointestinal symptoms were also a prevalent feature. The main location of abdominal pain was concentrated in the lower right abdomen and around the navel. Moreover, four growth-restricted patients (P6, P8, P10 and P11) presented symptoms of diarrhea, which may have contributed to their failure to gain weight. Specifically, P8 presented diarrhea and malnutrition half a month after birth. From the age of 4, the increase in height and weight halted at an initial height of 115 cm (< 3 percentiles) and weight of 20 kg (3–10 percentiles); however, after treatment with biological agents, diarrhea improved, and slow and steady growth was observed.

Interestingly, the hematological manifestations were particularly prominent. Three patients (P3, P4, and P6) had microcytic hypochromic anemia, whereas the remaining patients had normocytic anemia. Furthermore, three patients (P3, P5, and P8) presented moderate to severe anemia, and blood transfusion therapy was administered to ameliorate anemia. Notably, P5 presented severe anemia accompanied by thrombocytopenia beginning three days after birth. The platelet count did not increase significantly after platelet transfusion and fluctuated within a range of 20–41 × 109/L, whereas the hemoglobin level ranged from 65–90 g/L. A bone marrow aspiration examination revealed active marrow hyperplasia, a decreased erythroid ratio, an increased megakaryocyte count and occasional erythrophagocytosis. Since the age of 1 month, the patient presented with fever accompanied by hepatosplenomegaly, anemia and decreased levels of platelets. As the disease progressed, other symptoms of hemophagocytic syndrome did not appear, excluding this condition. Eventually, it was concluded that the hematologic manifestations may be caused by MKD.

Additionally, 8 of the 11 patients experienced musculoskeletal symptoms. Two patients reported myalgia, which occurred primarily in the left shoulder and back. The shoulder and knee joints were the most common sites of inflammatory accumulation. Moreover, three patients reported headaches during febrile episodes, and four patients (P4, P5, P6, and P8) were diagnosed with developmental delay (Table 1). Cranial MRI revealed intracranial cysts in three patients (P1, P6, and P8) and abnormal intracranial signals in P3 and P4. Follow-up examinations revealed that the cysts in P1 and P6 had resolved. In addition, psychomotor development significantly improved in P4 and P6, whereas P5 and P8 remained delayed compared with their age-matched peers.

A total of 16 mutation sites were identified in the coding exons of the MVK gene, including 2 frameshift mutations, 13 missense mutations and 1 synonymous mutation that were confirmed; while 1 mutation site was detected in the intron region. Among the corresponding amino acid changes (Fig. 2b), four mutations are located in the GHMP kinase N domain, and three are located in the GHMP kinase C domain. All the patients harbored compound heterozygous mutations, and variants were evaluated according to the ACMG guidelines (Table 1). Four novel mutation sites were discovered, namely, c.78G > A, c.463G > A, c.1076C > T, and c.1088G > A; these mutations included three missense mutations and one synonymous mutation, and the corresponding amino acid changes were K26K, A155T, A359V and C363Y, respectively. P8 carried two previously unreported MVK variants (K26K and A155T), and he exhibited a severe MA phenotype. Additionally, three patients were observed to have mutations in K26K (P1, P8 and P11), and P8 and P11 exhibited growth restriction and severe diarrhea as prominent phenotypes. Various treatment approaches have been utilized to prevent and alleviate MKD in patients, including NSAIDs (primarily ibuprofen), corticosteroids and biologics (Fig. 3f, Table 1). Nine patients used NSAIDs and achieved partial symptom control only. Six patients were subsequently treated with corticosteroids, which resulted in a good response. In addition, seven patients required treatment with biological agents. Six patients used etanercept, five of whom demonstrated favorable outcomes, with extended intervals between fever episodes and alleviation of accompanying symptoms, and follow-up revealed complete or partial remission in these patients. The effects of tocilizumab varied; P3 responded well, whereas P2 had no response and experienced shortened intervals between fever episodes. P6, who did not respond to etanercept, achieved a good therapeutic response after switching to canakinumab, leading to complete remission.

A literature search was conducted in both domestic and international databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, Ovid, Wanfang Data, CNKI, and the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, using the keywords “mevalonate kinase deficiency (MKD)”, “hyperimmunoglobulin D syndrome (HIDS)”, and “mevalonic aciduria (MA)” for articles published from 2000 to December 2023. Studies that provided clinical and genotypic reports of relevant cases were included, whereas studies that did not focus on MKD or presented incomplete clinical data were excluded. To compare the clinical manifestations of patients from different countries, we selected a large European cohort (The cohort includes no fewer than 50 individuals) and Asian cohort for the study [6, 7, 9, 10, 13]. The clinical manifestations of MKD are summarized in Additional File 1 (Table S1). We summarized the characteristics of MA patients reported in previous studies to explore the potential relationships between severe phenotypes and genotypes. The data of 20 patients with MA were retrieved [22–31] and are summarized in Table 3. Among the 20 patients with MA, 10 patients had homozygous mutations. The most common MVK variant in MA patients was c.1000G > A; p.Ala334Thr, which was found in 7 patients, most of whom were compound heterozygous, with one patient carrying homozygous variants. Over six years of follow-up, the patient presented with a mild phenotype and remained free from any episodes of MVK while consistently following a regimen that included vitamins C, E, and CoQ. The second most common variant was c.803C > T; p.Ile268Thr. One homozygous individual presented severe symptoms, including cardiomyopathy, pulmonary hypertension, cerebellar hypoplasia, brain cysts, and hepatosplenomegaly; this patient unfortunately died at 21 days of age due to cardiac arrest.

In the present study, the clinical features, genetic phenotypes, and immunophenotypes were described for 11 pediatric patients with MKD in China, representing the first cohort reported in China. The clinical and genetic spectrum of the disease was broader than previously reported. In the present cohort, patients with MKD experienced disease episodes before the age of 1 year, with the median age of onset being 2 months earlier than the 6-month median in European cohorts [6]. Despite advancements in genetic testing, a significant delay between disease onset and diagnosis remains, highlighting the need for improving the early recognition of MKD symptoms by clinicians.

At disease onset, patients often presented with periodic fever as the main manifestation, with peak temperatures exceeding 38 °C and a duration of 2–14 days (mean, 5.5 days), similar to the 4–7 days observed in the Japanese cohort and slightly longer than the median of 4 days reported in the European cohort [7, 9], suggesting that early-onset periodic fever should raise suspicion for MKD. Furthermore, consistent with previous reports, gastrointestinal symptoms, such as diarrhea and abdominal pain, were prominent in the present cohort. Moreover, growth restriction in children with MKD was often associated with severe diarrhea.

In the present cohort, 73% of patients experienced hematologic issues such as anemia and thrombocytopenia. Hinson et al. reported two MKD patients with hematologic abnormalities characterized by normocytic hypoplastic anemia, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia and abnormal blood cell morphology [32]. Clinical signs of anemia and thrombocytopenia have been reported in a three-year-old male diagnosed with MA [22]. Moreover, macrophage activation syndrome may occur in association with MKD [33]. Bader-Meunier et al. reported three MKD patients with macrophage activation syndrome who experienced febrile episodes in the absence of infection and intoxication [10]. Simona et al. analyzed microarray datasets from MKD patients, identifying hematopoiesis-related functions and pathways as the most significantly altered, aligning with the clinical presentation of anemia [34]. Moreover, MVK gene mutations may lead to hematological abnormalities. Specifically, MVK mutations lead to RhoA inactivation through defects in prenylation [35], and Rho GTPases (RhoA, RAC, and Cdc42) play crucial regulatory roles in the development of red blood cells [36] Therefore, hematological abnormalities in MKD patients warrant further attention.

As previously reported, more than 120 mutations in MVK have been identified, which are listed on the Infevers website (http://fmf.igh.cnrs.fr/infevers/). The V377I mutation is the most common variant causing MVK genetic defects in European patients [7, 10]. In the present cohort, only two patients carried V377I (P1 and P7), which may be attributed to differences among human populations. Although K26K is a synonymous mutation, the present patients exhibited significant periodic fever and autoinflammatory phenotypes characteristic of MKD; according to the ACMG guidelines, the variant is classified as likely pathogenic (LP). Furthermore, studies have suggested that most synonymous mutations can be harmful [37]. Thus, the synonymous mutation carried by patients in the present cohort may exacerbate the MKD phenotype.

Laboratory tests and immunological evaluations are crucial for distinguishing between acute attacks and the remission period of MKD. Increased CRP, ESR, and SAA levels indicate acute attacks of the disease. Immunological parameters in MKD patients may help distinguish MKD from other inborn errors of immunity (IEI), such as cyclic neutropenia, While both conditions present with periodic fever, MKD patients typically exhibit normal to elevated neutrophil counts, whereas cyclic neutropenia is characterized by severe neutropenia during febrile episodes [38]. In addition to laboratory markers of inflammation, current laboratory diagnostic approaches for MKD include the measurement of serum IgD levels, urinary mevalonic acid levels and MVK enzyme activity. A prospective study evaluating the diagnostic value of increased serum IgD levels for HIDS has indicated that serum IgD levels are not reliable or sensitive for diagnosing MKD [39]; for example, 44% of MKD patients in the Japanese cohort and 28% of those in the European cohort did not have increased IgD levels [6, 9]. In the present cohort, plasma IgD levels were measured in 91% of patients; among these patients, 50% did not have elevated levels of IgD. In addition, the level of mevalonic acid in urine can serve as a sensitive indicator for diagnosing MKD. Although MVK enzyme activity is considered the gold standard for diagnosing MKD [40], MVK enzyme activity assays are not routinely conducted in laboratories owing to methodologic limitations. Consequently, the diagnosis of MKD relies primarily on clinical manifestations and genome sequencing.

In the early stage of the disease, NSAIDs are commonly used to control fever symptoms, providing partial and transient relief. Additionally, patients typically respond well to corticosteroid therapy. A previous study has reported that a patient with MKD experienced prolonged relief from fever after six courses of hydrocortisone treatment [41]. The present study confirmed the effectiveness of corticosteroid use; however, extended corticosteroid use is limited by adverse effects. To achieve better therapeutic outcomes, biological therapies, including interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) antagonists, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors and interleukin­6 (IL-6) receptor antagonists, should be considered. Although IL-1R antagonists, such as anakinra, are the first-line treatment for MKD, these drugs are not available in China, and only one patient in the present cohort used them. Another study has indicated that treatment can be switched to anti-TNF agents, such as etanercept, if IL-1R inhibitors are unavailable or ineffective [42]. A previous study has reported that among 27 patients with MKD who were treated with etanercept, two achieved complete relief, 16 achieved partial remission, and 11 experienced no improvement [6]. In the present cohort, etanercept was the effective biological agent tested, leading to complete or partial remission in 5 of the 6 patients. Tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor antagonist, can prevent or reduce disease episodes. A previous study has reported the successful cure of an MKD patient with tocilizumab [43]. In the present cohort, tocilizumab had varying effects; P3 responded well to tocilizumab, whereas P2 showed no response to tocilizumab. Hence, tocilizumab is not recommended for all patients owing to individual variations in drug response, and choosing the appropriate biologic agent for treatment is essential. Moreover, studies have suggested that CoQ10, vitamin C, and vitamin E supplementation may help stabilize the clinical course of mevalonic aciduria and improve neurodevelopment and overall prognosis [30, 44]. Additionally, follow-up examinations revealed that the intracranial cysts in P1 and P6 had resolved, which may be related to standardized anti-inflammatory therapy. More studies are needed to further understand the efficacy and safety of different treatment approaches in MKD patients.

The associations between the genotypes and phenotypes of MKD remain unclear. A literature review of MA patients revealed that homozygous MVK mutations are more frequently associated with MA; patients with A334T homozygous mutations present milder symptoms, and those with I268T homozygous mutations present a more severe phenotype. Hinson et al. demonstrated that base substitution from alanine to threonine at position 334 results in decreased affinity for substrates (RS)-mevalonate binding but still allows for normal movement through the mevalonate pathway [45]. This result may explain the milder clinical presentation observed in patients with the A334T mutation. Homozygosity for I268T has also been identified in severe presentations [46, 47]. A previous study has indicated that patients with p.V377I/p.I268T compound heterozygosity have AA amyloidosis significantly more often compared with other patients [31]. The I268T mutation is associated with increased pathogenicity, potentially affecting overall protein stability [48]. Early genetic testing enables timely diagnosis and intervention.

In summary, the present single-center retrospective study described the clinical and genetic characteristics of Chinese children with MKD. In the present cohort, hematological abnormalities were a manifestation of MKD, expanding the phenotypic spectrum of the disease. The present study contributes valuable knowledge to the field and will aid clinicians in better diagnosing and managing patients with MKD. However, owing to the small sample size, the present study had certain limitations. The absence of amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis in the present cohort may be attributed to the younger age of the patients and the relatively short follow-up period. Moreover, in vivo and in vitro experiments are needed to validate the functions of these gene mutations. In the future, we hope that more studies on gene mutations and their clinical correlations can contribute to predicting disease severity and exploring the efficacy of pharmacological interventions for disease control.

The present study systematically reported the clinical manifestations and treatment outcomes of the largest case series of pediatric MKD patients from China, broadening the phenotypic spectrum of MKD by emphasizing potential hematologic manifestations. Patients with homozygous mutations are more likely to present with the MA phenotype, with A334T homozygous mutations linked to milder symptoms and I268T homozygous mutations associated with a more severe clinical presentation.
