Diabetic Kidney Disease: From Pathophysiology To Treatment Perspectives.

Varda L, Vreča N, Ekart R, Bevc S, Piko N.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) presents an extensive healthcare burden globally. 
Diabetes mellitus (DM) is the most prevalent cause of CKD and end-stage kidney 
disease (ESKD), requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT). Type II DM remains 
the most prevalent subtype, as it is closely related to metabolic syndrome, 
whose prevalence is also rising with a sedentary lifestyle and Western diet. 
Therefore, understanding the pathophysiology of diabetic kidney disease (DKD), 
its early recognition and early initiation of the currently available therapy 
remains an important measure to slow the progression of the disease. 
Pathophysiology of DKD is a complex process, where all the mechanisms are still 
being elucidated on preclinical animal and in-vitro models as well as in 
clinical studies. Changes in glomerular hemodynamics, glomerular and tubular 
hypertrophy, hyperfiltration, overactivation of the 
renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), podocyte injury, inflammation, 
oxidative stress, renal hypoxia, mitochondrial injury, and epigenetic changes 
are the main mechanisms leading to albuminuria, glomerulosclerosis and fibrosis. 
In the latter years, the importance of gut microbiota in DKD has also been 
shown. Understanding the mechanisms behind DKD is important, especially for 
researching current and possibly future pharmacological treatment of DKD. While 
angiotensin convertase enzyme inhibitors (ACEi) and angiotensin receptor 
blockers (ARB) alongside nonpharmacological measures have been the pillars of 
DKD treatment and reduction of albuminuria for many decades, novel 
pharmacological agents are emerging. Starting with sodium-glucose transporter 2 
(SGLT2) inhibitors, which have been proven to affect many of the 
pathophysiological mechanisms, continuing with novel non-steroidal 
mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), which are a new anti-inflammatory 
and antifibrotic possibility, and finishing with glucagon-like peptide 1 
receptor antagonists (GLP1RA) which have recently joined the therapeutic options 
for DKD. This comprehensive review focuses on the main pathophysiological 
mechanisms of DKD and current available pharmacological and non-pharmacological 
possibilities.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) presents a significant healthcare challenge worldwide. Chronically elevated levels of blood glucose result in a metabolic disorder that is a consequence of impaired insulin secretion, its action on target tissues, or both [1]. DM can be classified into type I DM, where the destruction of insulin-producing cells of the pancreas leads to hyperglycemia and type II DM, where insufficient insulin secretion, insulin resistance, and scarce compensatory insulin response present the main pathophysiological mechanisms leading to hyperglycemia [2]. Other known forms of DM can be classified as pregnancy-related hyperglycemia or gestational diabetes, slowly evolving immune-mediated diabetes or latent autoimmune diabetes in adults, ketosis-prone-type II diabetes, which closely resembles type I diabetes, and other special types of DM such as maturity-onset diabetes of the young, drug or chemical-induced DM, endocrinopathies, infections, and genetic defects in insulin action leading to DM [1]. According to the World Health Organization, the prevalence of DM rose from just over 100 million to more than 400 million in the last 40 years [3]. With more than 90% of the cases, type II DM, often correlating with metabolic syndrome, is the most prevalent type. Obesity is strongly associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) in both type I and type II DM. It contributes to and amplifies several key pathophysiological processes characteristic of diabetic nephropathy (DN), including glomerular hyperfiltration, intraglomerular hypertension, and increased renal adiposity [4]. DM presents a significant risk factor for blindness, stroke, lower limb amputation, kidney failure, and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1].

Among the latter, CKD presents one of the biggest healthcare burdens regarding both financial aspects and patients’ quality of life [5]. According to the epidemiological update from 2022, there are estimated to be more than 840 million patients with CKD worldwide. It is expected to become the 5th most common cause of death in the next 15 years [6]. Many factors and comorbidities lead to CKD and its progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), with DM being the most prevalent (30–47%). More than 50% of patients with type I DM will develop ESRD requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT) [7]. As a microvascular complication, it is estimated that diabetes-related CKD occurs in 30% of type I DM and 40% of type II DM [8, 9]. The prevalence of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is rising in parallel to the global prevalence of DM. It is more prevalent in some racial and ethnic groups, such as Native Americans, African Americans, Mexican Americans, Polynesians, and Aboriginal Australians [3, 10]. Therefore, early recognition, diagnosis, management, monitoring of patients, and timely implementation of appropriate therapy are indispensable to prevent kidney disease progression and reduce all associated cardiovascular events. This review focuses on distinguishing between the terms DKD and DN, pathophysiological mechanisms of diabetes-related CKD, current treatment possibilities, and possible future perspectives of treating the cardio-renal-metabolic spectrum of diseases.

Typical clinical presentation of DKD is the presence of clinically significant albuminuria (Table 1) in the absence of confounding factors such as an infection, fever, strenuous physical activity, uncontrolled hypertension, heart failure, non-DKD, etc. [11]. By observing type I DM patients, it was initially believed that the DKD followed the typical pathophysiological steps, beginning with glomerular hyperfiltration, increased glomerular size, and thickening of glomerular basement membrane (GBM), followed by mesangial expansion, inflammation, glomerulosclerosis, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, which clinically presented as progressive albuminuria and subsequently decline of glomerular filtration rate (GFR), leading to ESKD [12]. However, in type II DM, the course of the disease may not go through these classical steps [13]. DKD can present without albuminuria (non-albuminuric DKD), which is more commonly seen in older patients with shorter DM duration, females, and in patients with lower glycated hemoglobin and lower blood pressure (BP). They also have less frequent diabetic retinopathy and fewer classic glomerular lesions, with pathology more often showing tubulointerstitial and vascular changes rather than glomerular injury [14]. Pathophysiologically, chronic hyperglycemia in these patients is characterized by predominant microcirculatory dysfunction, increased intrarenal arterial resistance, and a progressive decline in nephron function, occurring without substantial impairment of the glomerular filtration barrier. This pattern of injury accounts for the absence of albuminuria despite ongoing renal deterioration [15]. Typically, patients with non-albuminuric DKD have a slower course of DKD. Factors such as poor glycemic and BP control, higher albuminuria, longer DM duration, older age, male gender, smoking, dyslipidemia, obesity, and cardiovascular comorbidities are associated with a rapid progression of DKD [16].

A common feature in all DM types is chronic hyperglycemia, which leads to the release of several vasoactive mediators – nitric oxide (NO), prostaglandins, glucagon, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). The release of the latter results in dilation of the afferent glomerular arteriole. Consequently, glomerular filtration and tubular reabsorption (mainly through the upregulation of SGLT-2 cotransporters) of glucose and sodium are increased, leading to a decrease in the sodium delivery to macula densa, impairing the tubuloglomerular feedback and ultimately enhancing dilation of the afferent arteriole. Furthermore, dysregulation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) leads to a higher concentration of angiotensin II, causing efferent arteriole constriction. Without reducing hyperglycemia, afferent arteriole vascular disease and renal autoregulation can become permanent, favoring glomerular hypertension and hyperfiltration [13, 17, 18].

Chronic hyperglycemia initially causes hyperinsulinemia and, consequently, endothelial dysfunction, mainly due to the imbalanced production of vasoconstrictors and vasodilators [19]. Endothelin-1 is an important vasoconstrictor, which has been shown to be elevated in DKD patients [20]. In addition to vasoconstriction and endothelial dysfunction, the latter has been associated with podocyte injury [21], oxidative stress [22, 23], and inflammation [24]. Alongside hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia, a high-protein diet has also been shown to augment the glomerular hyperfiltration response [25, 26].

Kidney enlargement as a part of glomerular and tubular hypertrophy and mesangial expansion is characteristic of DKD and has been associated with a decline in GFR and worse kidney outcomes [27, 28]. It has been previously shown that even a short-term strict glycemic control for 3 months can reduce kidney size and slow the progression of GFR decline in patients with type I DM [25]. Growth factors, such as VEGF, IGF-1, TGF-β, epidermal growth factor, platelet-derived growth factor, and angiotensin II, are chronically released in response to unregulated hyperglycemia [29, 30]. Reduction of albuminuria and prevention of glomerular hypertrophy has been shown by inhibiting VEGF [18]. Glomerular size is mainly enlarged due to mesangial cell proliferation, hypertrophy, and increased capillary loops, which results in a larger filtration surface. The advanced end glycation products-protein kinase pathway plays a central role in the development of mesangial hypertrophy in DKD, leading to increased transcription and synthesis of several growth factors, such as TGF-β1 and IGF-1, which are associated with an increased production of extracellular matrix (collagen IV, fibronectin, laminin) [31].

On the other hand, tubular enlargement results from the proliferation and hypertrophy of tubular epithelial cells, leading to higher resorptive capacity, lowering the delivery of sodium chloride to macula densa, and impairing the tubuloglomerular feedback [18]. Whether tubular hypertrophy is a primary event or a response to vascular changes and growth factors remains a topic for discussion. In animal models, tubular hypertrophy happened before glomerular hypertrophy. Inhibition of ornithine decarboxylase, an enzyme which has an important role in tubular hypertrophy, prevented glomerulosclerosis, glomerular hypertrophy, and reabsorption of sodium in the proximal tubule [32], which could indicate that factors released from tubular cells also induce glomerular changes. Several growth factors, such as VEGF, IGF-1, TGF-β, platelet-derived growth factor, and connective tissue growth factor, have been connected with tubular hypertrophy [33]. TGF-1 induced cell proliferation, migration differentiation, and ECM turnover [34]. While the use of neutralizing antibodies against TGF-β has shown promising results in reducing mesangial matrix accumulation, long-term use did not prevent the progression of DKD, indicating a complex pathophysiology [29]. The anti-connective tissue growth factor effect has also shown promising results in animal models [35] and humans in a phase I trial where it decreased albuminuria [36].

Filtration of albumin is the result of both charge and size-selective properties of the glomerular capillary wall [37]. High accumulation of collagen type IV and reduced heparan sulphate proteoglycan, which is negatively charged, are the starting point of albuminuria emergence [17, 18]. Podocytes have a remarkable role in preventing the filtration of albumin in a healthy state. In DKD, the decreased number of podocytes due to apoptosis, podocyte detachment from the GBM, and loss of heparan sulphate directly correlate with increased urinary albumin excretion [38, 39]. Various factors contribute to the latter, such as angiotensin II, TGF-β, hyperglycemia-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation [40], lipid accumulation, and lipotoxicity [41]. Regardless of DM, hypertension, or dyslipidemia, the progression of CKD is independently associated with obesity. Accumulation of lipids can be seen in mesangial cells, proximal tubular cells, and podocytes, which are especially prone to lipotoxic injury. Lipid vacuolization, loss of cellular density, and foot process effacement in podocytes are characteristic of DKD and obesity-related nephropathy [42]. Free fatty acids bound to plasma albumin can cause glomerular injury and tubulointerstitial damage as well [43].

Angiotensin II suppresses the transcription of nephrin, a permeability-controlling protein that is a part of podocyte slit diaphragm and is low in DKD; however, due to the mechanism of this change, RAAS inhibitors can restore its transcription [18].

Activated protein C is known to have anti-inflammatory and protective effects in the kidney. In DKD, it is suppressed due to reduced expression of thrombomodulin. Thus, glomerular endothelial cell and podocyte apoptosis is enhanced [44]. However, albuminuria is not considered only the consequence of the disease but also the instigator [45] as it can independently promote and stimulate mesangial proliferation, glomerulosclerosis, and tubular injury [46, 47].

Inflammation in DKD is a process of high complexity, including several molecular pathways, which at the endpoint result in chronic inflammatory infiltrate consisting of macrophages and other immune cells, producing pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines and creating a profibrotic environment [48]. Inflammation is present at the systemic as well as renal level [49]. Both blood cells and intrinsic kidney cells, such as glomerular, tubular, mesangial, and endothelial cells, can produce cytokines and chemokines. The most important cytokines in the pathogenesis of DKD are interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-18, and TNFα [50]. IL-1 is involved in increased vascular permeability and glomerular hemodynamic changes. IL-6 is involved in initiating interstitial neutrophil infiltration, promoting renal hypertrophy, thickening of the GBM and ECM changes. IL-18 has been connected to microvascular injury in DKD. TNFα promotes apoptosis, differentiation of inflammatory cells, renal cytotoxicity, oxidative injury, and endothelial permeability [48, 51]. Chemokines, such as monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1)/chemokine C-C motif ligand 2 (CCL2), C-X3-C motif chemokine (CX3CL1), and CCL5/RANTES (C-C motif ligand 5/regulated on activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted) are important modulators of inflammatory cell recruitment, cell adhesion, and tissue damage [52]. Intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), a vascular cell adhesion protein 1, endothelial cell-selective adhesion molecule, and E-selectin are important adhesion molecules associated with the progression of albuminuria and DKD [53].

The Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription pathway regulate an inflammatory diabetic response and have been associated with the progressive growth of glomerular mesangial cells and the progression of DKD with reduced GFR [54]. The nuclear transcription factor-kappa B (NFκB) pathway is activated by several diabetic factors, such as hyperglycemia, advanced glycation end-products, mechanical stress, ROS, inflammatory cytokines, angiotensin II, and albuminuria. This pathway results in the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules [48].

Impaired redox balance is another important inflammatory mechanism, which enhances oxidative damage and tissue injury and is a consequence of either increased generation of ROS or reduced antioxidant tissue defense [55, 56]. Endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress are especially important mechanisms in type II DM [57]. They are characterized by reduced NO bioavailability. The latter is synthesized from arginine via NO synthase, function of which is impaired in the hyperglycemic milieu. Therefore, superoxide and other ROS can damage essential cellular proteins and DNA [58]. This leads to glomerulosclerosis and fibrosis via the activation of NFκB, protein kinase C, hexosamine metabolites, and glycosylation end products. Antioxidants have not been shown to halt the progression of DKD thus far [17].

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is essential for properly functioning cellular transmembrane transporters in the kidneys. Renal blood flow presents approximately 25% of cardiac output, ensuring adequate oxygen delivery to the kidneys [59]. DKD presents a state of hyperfiltration with increased oxygen requirements but limited capability of increasing its delivery [60]. Furthermore, anemia in advanced CKD can lead to a further hypoxic state [61]. Hypoxia-inducible factor-regulated genes, such as erythropoietin, are usually upregulated in a hypoxic environment. However, in DKD, the response is different, resulting in decreased erythropoiesis and anemia [62]. Endothelial dysfunction results in the activation of the profibrotic state, leading to tissue fibrosis and ischemic kidney injury, ending in the loss of peritubular capillaries and increasing kidney hypoxia [63, 64].

Mitochondrial dysfunction also plays an important role in the pathogenesis of DKD. Podocytes and proximal tubular cells are most prone to mitochondrial dysfunction [65]. Hyperglycemia leads to excessive production of ROS within mitochondria, causing oxidative stress and damage to mitochondrial DNA, proteins, and lipids. This impairs mitochondrial function, reducing energy production and disrupting cellular homeostasis. The resulting energy deficit and oxidative damage contribute to inflammation, fibrosis, and apoptosis in kidney cells, exacerbating kidney injury and promoting the progression of DKD [13, 17]. It has been shown that in the setting of DKD, as a response to hyperglycemia, mitochondrial superoxide is reduced, leading to reduced oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial ATP generation. Decreased adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase and endothelial NO synthase activity leads to inflammatory response via NFκB. Caloric restriction and physical exercise can restore adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase activity and increase mitochondrial superoxide and, therefore, oxidative phosphorylation in the kidney [66].

DNA methylation, noncoding RNAs, and histone posttranslational modifications (PTMs) are the proposed epigenetic pathophysiological mechanisms important in DKD. Previously described mechanisms, such as hyperglycemia, hypoxia, and inflammation, are known epigenetic modulators [67].

Specific DNA methyltransferases catalyze DNA methylation and are often considered “silencing” epigenetic markers. There is evidence of different DNA methylation patterns between DKD and non-DKD patients. One of the associated genes is the UNC13B gene, which mediates apoptosis in glomerular cells and could have a role in initiating DKD [68]. Overall, 187 genes have been identified to have distinctive methylation patterns between DM patients with ESKD and DKD without ESKD [69]. Several differences in DNA methylation have also been shown in tubular cells of DKD patients and controls [70].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small noncoding RNAs that are able to silence messenger RNA targets [71]. Several miRNAs have been shown to have a role in DKD pathophysiology, with miRNA-192 being the most studied. Its expression is increased in the early stages of DKD and is associated with an upregulation in several genes, such as Cola2 and Col4a1, and can promote autoregulation of TGF-β1 [72]. The latter has been shown to upregulate miR-200b/c, miR21, and miR-1207-5p in murine mesangial cells [73].

Important histone PTMs are lysine acetylation, lysine methylation, and phosphorylation and are mostly present in the exposed amino-terminal tails regulating gene expression chromatin structure [74]. The role of histone H3 lysine methylation in regulating TGF-β1-induced ECM gene expression in mesangial cells has been demonstrated in both normal and high-glucose environments [75]. PTMs also have a connection with miRNAs. MiRNA-192 induction in DKD relates to histone acetylation by p300 [76].

Human microbiota inhabiting the gut can function as an endocrine organ affecting hormones and other pathways in connection to the nervous system, metabolic diseases, microvascular diseases, and nephropathy [77]. Species Bacteroides acidifaciens, genera Ruminococcus and Rikenella, have been shown to produce short-chain fatty acids, such as butyrate, propionate, and acetate, which have been correlated with the regulation of insulin signaling, lipogenesis, and inflammation [78]. The decrease in the abundance of these bacteria can shift the immune response to pro-inflammatory. In diabetic rats with tubulointerstitial injury, the mentioned bacteria were reduced [79]. High-throughput sequencing technology enabled deep insight into the topic. A metagenome-wide association study showed that patients with type II DM contain more pathogenic and less butyrate-producing bacteria [80]. Genus Abiotrophia and family Peptococcaceae were associated with higher albuminuria [81]. Furthermore, DM patients have compromised gut barrier and higher levels of circulating lipopolysaccharides, inducing inflammation [82]. Application of lipopolysaccharides in mice models increased albuminuria [83]. Short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria can improve the integrity of the gut barrier [84, 85]. A diet rich in fiber can increase the abundance of short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria and protect them from DKD [86]. Furthermore, dysregulation of cholesterol homeostasis was mediated through gut microbiota change by oral antibiotic application or fecal microbiota transplantation from healthy individuals to diabetic rats and reduced albuminuria and tubulointerstitial injury [79].

Patients who present with CKD and DM can have true DKD (wherein CKD is a direct consequence of their diabetes status), non-DKD coincident with DM, or a combination of both [87]. Throughout the literature, DN refers explicitly to the histopathological changes observed in the kidneys of diabetic patients. DN is often classified according to glomerular, tubular, interstitial, and vascular lesions. Hallmark histological findings of DN are round, eosinophilic nodules within the mesangium known as nodular glomerulosclerosis or Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesions; diffuse mesangial sclerosis; thickening of arteriolar walls due to hyaline deposits or arteriolar hyalinosis and basement membrane thickening, which affects both glomeruli and tubules [87, 88].

A kidney biopsy is necessary to reveal the aforementioned specific histopathological features for a definitive diagnosis of DN. However, because biopsy is an invasive procedure, clinical diagnosis is typically based on persistent proteinuria, a decline in GFR, and excluding other essential kidney diseases in diabetic patients (Table 1) – in this case, the term DKD is used [11, 89]. Management and treatment of DKD and DN demands a multifaceted approach focusing primarily on glycemic control, BP regulation, and reduction of proteinuria. The therapeutic approach has evolved dramatically during the last decades, and its history ranges from everyday life changes to nonpharmacological approaches and the groundbreaking invention and use of angiotensin convertase enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) and angiotensin-receptor blockers (ARBs) [9, 90].

The history of treating DKD and DN has shown us that simple lifestyle changes and nonpharmacological measures have proven to be very effective. The primary goals were to control blood glucose levels and manage hypertension [9]. Since the mid-20th century, diet has been considered one of DN’s most essential treatment factors. It is well known that the Western diet, which is characterized by high consumption of energy-dense, highly processed foods, including excess sodium, high-fat dairy products, added sugars, saturated fats, and processed meats, leads to a faster progression of DKD [91].

An ideal diet recommended for DN and DKD patients includes significant amounts of unsaturated fats to prevent malnutrition. Since calories from protein and carbohydrate intake need to be restricted, larger quantities of vegetable oils and omega-rich fatty acids are advised while limiting saturated fatty acid consumption [91, 92]. A low-salt diet devoid of salted and pickled foods is highly recommended for DN and DKD patients as restricting sodium intake allows for better BP control [91, 93]. The recommended daily salt intake should not exceed 5 g of sodium chloride or 2 g of sodium [91, 92].

Clinical nutrition research and consensus statements underscore that weight-based calculation of caloric and protein requirements is preferable to a uniform approach in patients with DKD as it permits appropriate differentiation between obese and non-obese individuals. With respect to energy intake, the National Kidney Foundation Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) guidelines recommend a range of 25–35 kcal/kg body weight per day, while emphasizing that calculations should be anchored in weight-status goals, body composition, and overall clinical context rather than actual body weight alone, particularly in the setting of obesity. Similarly, guidelines from the American Diabetes Association and KDIGO advise that dietary protein intake be individualized and typically set at 0.8 g/kg body weight per day for patients with diabetes and non-dialysis CKD, with further adjustments based on nutritional status and body composition [94, 95]. Important factors are also smoking cessation and regular aerobic physical activity of at least 150 min weekly [96].

As our understanding of DN and DKD evolved, so did the pharmacological options available for its management. Introducing antihypertensive agents such as diuretics and β-blockers provided new ways of controlling BP. With the introduction of ACEi, new aspects of treating and managing DKD and DN came to life [97].

Elevations in BP, especially >150 mm Hg systolic, are linearly related to increased kidney disease progression and a higher incidence of cardiovascular events in patients with diabetes [98]. The most recent KDIGO guidelines for BP management in all CKD patients with or without DM from 2021 suggest that adults with high BP and CKD should have a target systolic BP of less than 120 mm Hg. Treatment with RAAS inhibitor drugs such as ACEi or ARBs is recommended in all patients with hypertension, CKD, and diabetes who also have moderately to severely increased albuminuria [99].

ACE converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II, which has pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic effects. ACE also increases sympathetic activity, tubular sodium and chloride absorption, aldosterone and ADH secretion and causes arteriolar vasoconstriction. ACE inhibition and angiotensin blockade have several protective cardiorenal effects [100].

Multiple clinical trials have established the benefits and efficacy of ACEi in slowing the rapid decrease in kidney function and lowering proteinuria levels. The CSG Captopril Trial conducted in 1993 compared captopril with placebo in patients with insulin-dependent DM, proteinuria ≥500 mg/day, and creatinine clearance <2.5 mg/dL. The main goal was to evaluate captopril’s effect on survival, symptoms, and clinical status in patients with chronic heart failure. The trial showed that captopril was not only effective in reducing the hospitalization rate in patients with heart failure but also in slowing the deterioration of kidney function, independent of BP control alone [100, 101].

The RENAAL Trial conducted in 2001 assessed the role of losartan in patients with type II DM and nephropathy. It compared randomized participants with losartan to patients with a placebo, in addition to conventional hypertension therapy, including calcium channel blockers, diuretics, alpha-blockers, and β-blockers. The study established that losartan and conventional antihypertensive treatment confer strong renal protection in patients with type II DM and nephropathy. The risk of the primary renal endpoint, a composite of a doubling of the serum creatinine concentration, ESKD, or death from any cause, was reduced by 16% with losartan [100, 102].

A systematic review and network meta-analysis conducted in 2020 by Zhang et al. [103] evaluated the effect of ACEis or ARBs on kidney disease and cardiovascular outcomes in individuals with CKD stages 3–5. The study showed that ACEis were superior to ARBs and other antihypertensive drugs and had the highest probability of being the most beneficial treatments for kidney events, cardiovascular outcomes, and all-cause mortality in non-dialysis CKD stage 3–5 patients. In the subgroup of patients with DKD, ARBs were superior to ACEis in lowering the probability of kidney events and cardiovascular events.

The IDNT (Irbesartan Diabetic Nephropathy Trial) was a randomized, double-blind trial, comparing irbesartan with a calcium channel blocker (amlodipine) and a placebo. Similarly to the REENAAL trial, this study showed that irbesartan reduced the risk of doubling serum creatinine by 33% and the risk of progression to ESRD by 23%. It also reduced proteinuria by nearly 50%, thus confirming that ARBs specifically protect the kidneys in patients with DM and kidney injury, strengthening evidence for their use in CKD management [104].

The MARVAL (Microalbuminuria Reduction with Valsartan) Trial investigated the effects of valsartan on reducing albuminuria in patients with type II DM. The study’s results highlighted that valsartan significantly reduced microalbuminuria by 44% compared to amlodipine. It showed that valsartan could mitigate kidney damage in diabetic patients independently of BP control [105].

The ONTARGET Trial compared telmisartan in monotherapy, ramipril (an ACE inhibitor) in monotherapy, and a combination of both in high-risk patients with cardiovascular disease or DM. The trial confirmed that telmisartan was as effective as ramipril in preventing kidney decline, thus confirming the efficacy of ARBs in kidney protection and discouraging combination therapy with ACE inhibitors due to a higher risk of adverse events [106]. Findings in these trials led to the recommendation of ACEi and ARBs for patients with DN and CKD and helped shape the current guidelines for kidney disease management.

Initially developed for glycemic control in type II DM, SGLT2 inhibitors also have several beneficial renal and cardiovascular effects [13, 96]. SGLT2 inhibitors reduce fasting and postprandial plasma glucose concentrations in individuals with DM. They work by lowering the renal threshold for glucose excretion, resulting in glucosuria, which decreases the mean plasma glucose concentration and ameliorates glucotoxicity. This all leads to improved β-cell function and enhanced insulin sensitivity. Glucose uptake by kidney cells is not insulin regulated and increases in proportion to the plasma glucose concentration. Glucose is diverted into non-glycolytic pathways, including the hexosamine and aldose reductase pathways, which results in the glycosylation of proteins and generation of advanced glycation end products. Reducing plasma glucose levels with SGLT2 inhibitors reverses these adverse intracellular metabolic effects [17, 107].

The widespread use of SGLT2 inhibitors has provided evidence that their effects on the RAAS are as effective as those of ACEi or ARBs. SGLT2 inhibitors induce a negative sodium and water balance, causing a mild reduction in intravascular volume. Lowering intravascular volume contributes to a 4–5 mm Hg decline in BP [17, 108]. Furthermore, by lowering hepcidin levels, they stimulate erythropoiesis independent of any effects on ferroportin. These inhibitors are thought to reduce hypoxic stress, which, over time, slows the progression of DKD [108]. SGLT2 inhibitors also increase the production of ketones, a more efficient energy source than glucose. The kidney freely absorbs and oxidizes ketones in proportion to their plasma concentration, producing more ATP and consuming fewer oxygen atoms [17]. Following the tubular hypothesis, SGLT2 inhibition attenuates proximal tubule hyper-reabsorption in the diabetic kidney, lowering diabetic glomerular hyperfiltration. An initial GFR reduction is later followed by long-term GFR preservation and a reduced risk of developing acute kidney injury [109, 110].

Studies have shown that dapagliflozin enhances insulin sensitivity in patients with DM. This improvement in insulin podocyte sensitivity could prevent podocyte injury and reduce albuminuria in DKD. The SGLT2 inhibitors’ shift from whole-body glucose utilization to fatty acid oxidation also shows the potential to decrease podocyte lipid content, promoting podocyte health and reducing albuminuria [17, 109]. The use of SGLT2 inhibitors uniformly produces a weight loss of 2–3 kg in 6–12 months after the initiation of therapy. This modest weight reduction is considered a significant contributor to the reversal of lipotoxicity and prevention of DKD. SGLT2 inhibitors cause a shift in substrate utilization from glucose to free fatty acids. This shift reduces intracellular levels of toxic lipid metabolites, reducing oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and pro-inflammatory and fibrotic processes [17].

In recent years, numerous large-scale clinical trials have substantiated the renal benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors and led to widespread drug use. The EMPAREG-OUTCOME Trial, published in 2015, was conducted on 7,020 patients and compared patients given empagliflozin with those given a placebo. This landmark trial demonstrated a significant reduction in CKD progression and albuminuria, doubling of serum creatinine, initiation of RRT, and death from renal causes. Furthermore, it provided strong evidence for a reduction in cardiovascular risk [111].

The CREDENCE Trial, conducted in 2019, evaluated renal outcomes as the primary end point in patients with albuminuric CKD. Patients were given canagliflozin compared to those with a placebo. The trial showed that canagliflozin significantly reduced the risk of ESKD, doubling of serum creatinine, and renal or cardiovascular death in patients with DM and known DKD [112].

The DAPA-CKD Trial, published in 2020, demonstrated that dapagliflozin reduced the risk of sustained decline in estimated GFR (eGFR), ESKD, and renal or cardiovascular death in patients with CKD with and without DM. Because of dapagliflozins’ proven efficacy, the independent data monitoring committee recommended stopping the trial prematurely. The study also adds to the literature by examining the effect of an SGLT2 inhibitor added to background therapy, including an ACE inhibitor or ARBs [113].

The EMPA-KIDNEY Trial was conducted to evaluate the impact of empagliflozin treatment on a diverse group of patients. Like previous trials, it reaffirmed that empagliflozin therapy reduced the risk of kidney disease progression or cardiovascular-related death among patients with CKD at risk for disease progression compared to the placebo [114].

In absolute terms, among 1,000 individuals with DKD treated with SGLT-2 inhibitors for 1 year, approximately 11 fewer will experience progression of kidney disease and 11 fewer will die from cardiovascular causes or require hospitalization for heart failure, relative to placebo. Moreover, SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy substantially postpones the onset of ESKD by several years and is associated with a corresponding extension in life expectancy [115].

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) have gained attention among therapeutic strategies to combat DKD due to their potential renal and cardiovascular benefits. Preclinical and clinical studies have shown that MRAs consistently reduce albuminuria, CKD progression, and activation of fibrotic and inflammatory pathways [116].

The mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) (encoded by NR3C2) represents an additional potential therapeutic target in CKD. By inhibiting the action of aldosterone via MR, MRAs decrease sodium reabsorption in the distal nephron, promote natriuresis, and lower BP, contributing to better outcomes in patients with DKD and DN [117].

Aldosterone induces pro-inflammatory cytokines and profibrotic factors, leading to renal inflammation and interstitial fibrosis. MRAs help suppress the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thus protecting renal tissues from further damage [116, 117]. MRAs significantly impact oxidative stress pathways by reducing the production of ROS. The latter are highly reactive molecules that include free radicals such as superoxide anion (O2-), hydroxyl radical (OH-), and non-radical species like hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The overproduction of ROS is a significant contributor to renal damage. By blocking the effects of aldosterone, MRAs inhibit the activation of NADPH oxidase, leading to a decrease in ROS generation. In addition to reducing ROS production, MRAs enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes, which are crucial for neutralizing ROS and protecting cells from oxidative stress [118].

MRAs in patients with DKD and DN also help modulate autophagy and apoptosis by influencing the pathways involved. They promote autophagy, a cellular process that removes damaged proteins and organelles, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing apoptosis in renal cells [116].

Furthermore, their vascular effects help improve endothelial function by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation in blood vessels. Enhanced endothelial function leads to better vascular health and a reduced risk of cardiovascular events, which is common in patients with DKD [117, 118].

Steroidal MRAs, such as spironolactone and eplerenone, are structurally related to aldosterone and competitively inhibit the MR. Steroidal MRAs exert their effects by binding to the MR in the kidney, heart, and vessels, preventing aldosterone-induced sodium retention and potassium excretion. Nonsteroidal MRAs, including finerenone, represent a class of selective MRAs. They differ from steroidal MRAs with non-competitive binding to MR and with greater receptor selectivity. This reduces the impact on sex hormone receptors and lowers the risk of endocrine-related side effects [119]. The 1999 »RALES (Randomized Aldactone Evaluation Study)« showed that spironolactone in addition to standard therapy substantially reduced the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality among patients with severe heart failure. Although primarily focused on heart failure, RALES also demonstrated renal benefits of spironolactone, including reductions in proteinuria and slowing of renal function decline [121]. The »FIDELIO-DKD Trial« was a double-blind randomized study in which patients with DM and albuminuria were randomly assigned either finerenone or placebo. During the 2.6-year follow-up, patients with finerenone had lower albuminuria, slower CKD progression, and a reduction in adverse cardiovascular events, compared to the placebo group [122]. The »FIGARO-DKD Trial« was a complementary trial to FIDELIO-DKD, which focused primarily on cardiovascular outcomes and reinforced the benefits of finerenone in reducing cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in DKD patients [123]. An additional pooled analysis (FIDELITY) was performed, and both of the finerenone-based studies were included. In FIDELITY, finerenone reduced the hazard of the composite kidney outcome (kidney failure, sustained ≥57% eGFR decline, or renal death) by 23% (hazard ratio 0.77, 95% CI: 0.67–0.88), with an absolute risk reduction of 1.7% at 3 years compared to placebo. The number needed to treat to prevent one such event over 3 years was 59. For ESKD, the relative risk reduction was 20% (HR 0.80, 95% CI: 0.64–0.99) [124].

Recently, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (CONFIDENCE trial) was published regarding dual therapy with empagliflozin and finerenone in patients with type II DM, eGFR between 30 and 90 mL/min/1.73 m2, and urinary albumin/creatinine ratio (UACR) between 100 and 5,000 mg/g. Patients were already receiving RAAS inhibition. After 6 months of treatment, combination therapy reduced UACR 29% more compared to finerenone alone and 32% more compared to empagliflozin alone. Altogether, AUCR decreased by 52% from the baseline. Numerically, there was a lower incidence of treatment-emergent hyperkalemia in a group with combination therapy than finerenone [125]. The latter provides promising results for the simultaneous initiation of MRA and SGLT2 inhibitors.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP1RAs), a class of incretin-based therapies, have demonstrated significant promise in managing type II DM, particularly in patients at risk for or suffering from DKD. GLP1 is a vital incretin hormone that is released postprandially and induces insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells, decreases gastric motility and emptying, β-cell apoptosis, and improves glucose metabolism [126, 127]. Therefore, initially, GLP1RA emerged as an important antidiabetic drug as a diminished effect of GLP1 is an important pathophysiological mechanism of type II DM, β-cell resistance, and obesity [128]. Later, mainly due to their effect on delayed gastric emptying and increased satiety, liraglutide and semaglutide have been approved for the treatment of obesity [129, 130]. Only recently have their favorable cardiovascular [131], renal [132], and liver outcomes [133] been reported.

The effect of GLP1RA on the kidney has mostly been tested on animal models. Favorable outcomes appear to be multifactorial. A rat model showed the infusion of GLP1 into rats improved renal plasma flow and GFR, increased diuresis and excretion of sodium, potassium, and bicarbonate. The main mechanism elucidated for the latter was the downregulation of Na+/H+ exchanger isoform 3 (NHE3) by phosphorylation in the proximal tubule [134]. The mechanism was also confirmed in obese mice with type II DM, where the researchers reported a modest BP reduction. However, the inhibition of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) did not show similar effects [135].

Furthermore, in diabetic db/db mice, GLP1 cleavage products could reduce tubulointerstitial damage and the inflammatory response by attenuating macrophage and T-cell infiltration. The expression of pro-inflammatory markers, such as TNFα and the tubular injury marker kidney injury molecule 1 (KIM1), was reduced. However, none of the GLP1 constructs reduced albuminuria and electrolyte excretion but increased glucosuria [136]. Mice with genetic deletion of GLP1R spontaneously developed nephropathy, shown as albuminuria, glomerulosclerosis, and increased fibronectin deposition in the renal cortex. Upon inducing insulin deficiency, mice developed even worse albuminuria. The results suggest the importance of the functioning of the GLP1 pathway. In the same study, a mouse model of severe insulin deficiency was treated with liraglutide and showed a reduction of albuminuria and glomerulosclerosis (which was not achieved with perindopril), reduced thickening of the GBM, and restoration of foot processes in podocytes. Liraglutide also reduced the expression of TGF-β1 and cortical fibronectin deposition. However, it could not reduce tubular injury markers, which was achieved with perindopril. Monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, which attracts inflammatory cells to the point of inflammation, was reduced, and NFκB was suppressed with liraglutide and perindopril [137]. It has also been suggested that GLP1RA reduce ROS production and their effects [138].

Based on these discoveries, clinical trials have also shown favorable effects in DKD patients. In a multicenter randomized trial (AWARD-7), insulin was compared with dulaglutide in doses of 1.5 mg and 0.75 mg weekly regarding kidney outcomes. Patients with type II DM with an HbA1c of 7.5–10.5% and CKD stages 3 and 4 receiving ACEi or ARB at the maximum tolerated dose were included. Glycemic control was similar between groups; however, the dulaglutide group presented with a reduced decline of eGRF after 52 weeks. The authors report no significant changes in UACR or adverse effects incidence [139]. A nationwide cohort study in Taiwan also compared GLP1RA and long-acting insulin and found a reduced possibility of CKD progression, ESKD requiring RRT or kidney-associated death [140]. In an exploratory analysis of the REWIND trial – a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, liraglutide was able to reduce the incidence of new-onset macroalbuminuria (defined as UACR >33.9 mg/mmol) in type II DM patients older than 50 years of age. The hazard ratio for the composite renal outcome, including a decline in eGRF >30% or chronic RRT, was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.77–0.93; p = 0.0004) for the placebo group [141]. LEADER was another double-blind, placebo-controlled trial comparing liraglutide and placebo in type II DM patients with high cardiovascular risk, showing a significantly lower incidence of the rate of the first occurrence of death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke [142]. In the analysis for secondary renal outcomes, which were defined as a composite of new-onset persistent macroalbuminuria (urinary albumin >300 mg/day or AUCR >300 mg/g), persistent doubling of the serum creatinine level, ESRD, or renal death, the study showed a kidney-favorable effect for the liraglutide arm, mainly due to reduced incidence of newly developed macroalbuminuria after a median follow-up of 3.84 years (the risks of persistent doubling of the serum creatinine level and of ESRD did not differ significantly between groups). In the subgroup analysis in patients with an eGFR of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and microalbuminuria or macroalbuminuria, the results also favored liraglutide [132].

Recently, a FLOW trial was published where type II DM patients with an eGFR of 50–75 mL per minute per 1.73 m2 and UACR of >300 and <5,000 mg/g or an eGFR of 25 to <50 mL per minute per 1.73 m2 and a UACR of >100 and <5,000 mg/g were included. Patients received the maximum labelled dose of ACEi or ARB unless they were contraindicated due to side effects. Semaglutide was compared to placebo, starting at 0.25 mg for 4 weeks, continuing with 0.5 mg for another 4 weeks, and then titrated to 1.0 mg weekly for the remainder of the observation period if possible. Semaglutide significantly reduced primary kidney outcomes by 24%, which were defined as a composite of the onset of kidney failure (dialysis, transplantation, or an eGFR of <15 mL per minute per 1.73 m2), at least a 50% reduction in the eGFR from baseline, or death from kidney-related or cardiovascular causes. Semaglutide also showed a slower decline in eGFR and a reduced risk of cardiovascular events or all-cause mortality. Permanent discontinuation in the semaglutide arm was mainly due to gastrointestinal symptoms [143]. Clinical trials encompassing kidney-related outcomes regarding treatment with GLP1RA are presented in Table 4. In patients with obesity and DKD, the use of GLP-1 RAs confers an estimated gain of 1.2–2.4 additional years of life and 4.5–5.5 additional years of kidney event-free survival compared to conventional care, based on actuarial modeling of clinical trial data in type II DM with albuminuria [144].

Therefore, GLP1RAs have shown promising cardiovascular and renal outcomes in type II DM patients. United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has recently approved semaglutide for adult patients with type II DM and CKD to reduce the risk of worsening kidney disease, kidney failure, and death due to cardiovascular disease. The drug should be considered one of the possible options for treating the cardio-renal-metabolic spectrum of diseases.

DKD is an important chronic microvascular complication of DM and the most common cause of CKD progression to ESKD. New pathophysiological mechanisms have recently been discovered, especially in preclinical animal and in vitro models. These scientific data are generating new therapeutic options. Strict glycemic control remains the most important measure. It can be fulfilled through lifestyle changes or antihyperglycemic medication [9]. In recent years, the discussion about the cardio-renal-metabolic spectrum of diseases has emerged. This proposed classification is based on new therapeutic options and epidemiological, pathophysiologic, and prognostic factors between type II DM, cardiovascular diseases, and CKD, which are remarkably similar [145]. A balanced diet with salt restriction (less than 5 g days), reduced protein intake, and sufficient hydration is recommended to prevent CKD progression and cardiovascular complications. Furthermore, regular aerobic physical activity, reduction of alcohol intake, and cessation of smoking are also recommended. Regulated BP and cholesterol levels further reduce the risk of complications [146].

For many years, the only therapeutic options for proteinuria reduction and progression of CKD were ACEi and ARBs. SGLT2 inhibitors started as antihyperglycemic drugs, which later showed favorable effects in treating heart failure with reduced and preserved ejection fraction, as well as DKD and later for CKD of any cause [113, 114, 147–149]. Steroidal MRAs have been used for several decades and have first shown promising results for the treatment of arterial hypertension. Later, they became one of the pillars of the treatment of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [119]. With concerns about several side effects of steroidal MRAs, a novel non-steroidal MRA, finerenone, became available, with an indication for the treatment of proteinuric type II DKD [124]. Finerenone was also shown to be effective in preventing heart failure, worsening events and cardiovascular death in patients with mildly reduced and preserved ejection fraction [150]. While beta-receptor blockers conclude the four pillars for heart failure management, they have not shown significant importance in managing DKD, excluding their antihypertensive effects [151]. GLP1RA is the latest therapeutic acquisition. Besides their effect on glycemic control through the incretin system, obesity, and favorable effects on many organ systems, they have demonstrated an important reduction in the progression of CKD, as well as reduced cardiovascular risk and all-cause mortality [143, 152].

An alternative option to MRA may be aldosterone synthase inhibitors. The latter aim to prevent even the nongenomic effects of aldosterone, which have been linked to inflammation, fibrosis, and cardio-renal dysfunction [153]. Phase I trial has been completed in diabetic patients with albuminuric CKD, where the reduction of aldosterone was recorded, and a possible reduction of albuminuria was suggested with treatment with BI 690517 [154]. The latter was also tested in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II trial as an add-on therapy to ACEi/ARB and empagliflozin and dose-dependently reduced albuminuria in patients with eGFR >30 mL/min and UACR of 200 to less than 5,000 mg/g and serum potassium of 4.8 mmol/L or less [155]. Currently, a clinical trial is registered, where an aldosterone synthase inhibitor lorundrostat will be tested for the treatment of hypertension in patients with albuminuric CKD as an add-on therapy to SGLT2i (NCT06150924).

Endothelin receptor antagonists have also been proposed as a possible future therapy for DKD as it plays an important role in its pathophysiology. Some positive renoprotective effects have been shown in preclinical models [156]. Atrasentan, a selective endothelin A receptor antagonist, was tested in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (SONAR). Altogether, 2,648 patients with type II DM and eGFR 25–75 mL/min per 1.73 m2 and a UAR of 300–5,000 mg/g had received maximum labelled or tolerated RAAS inhibition for at least 4 weeks. Atrasentan showed a protective kidney effect for the primary composite outcome (doubling of serum creatinine sustained for ≥30 days or ESKD [eGFR <15 mL/min per 1.73 m2 sustained for ≥90 days, chronic dialysis for ≥90 days, kidney transplantation, or death from kidney failure]). Fluid retention and anemia events were higher in the atrasentan group [157]. Aprocitentan, a dual endothelin A and B receptor antagonist, showed promising results in reducing BP in patients with resistant hypertension (PRECISION trial – multicenter, blinded, randomized, parallel-group, phase 3 study). Fluid retention was also recorded as frequent adverse event [158]. Dual endothelin and angiotensin receptor antagonist sparsentan showed an important reduction in proteinuria compared to irbesartan in the international, randomized, double-blind, active-controlled study (PROTECT trial) [159]. These were not yet tested in DKD patients. In phase III clinical trials, many endothelin antagonists produced adverse events due to fluid retention. Therefore, the dual therapy with endothelin antagonists and SGLT2i was proposed and should be evaluated in future clinical trials [156].

Another promising drug in the field of DKD is tirzepatide, a dual GLP1RA and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide agonist. It successfully reduced body weight and other cardiorenal risk factors (BP, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin) in obese in overweight patients [160]. Furthermore, in the SURPASS-4 clinical trial, it was able to decrease albuminuria and total eGFR slopes and nearly halved the risk of a pre-specified composite kidney endpoint (eGFR decline ≥40%, renal death, kidney failure, or new-onset macroalbuminuria) in participants with type II DM and high cardiovascular risk when compared with insulin glargine [161].

Treatment of CKD complications, such as renal anemia, metabolic acidosis, and mineral bone disease, is of utmost importance as it further prevents cardiovascular complications [146]. Hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor roxadustat has emerged as an alternative to erythropoietin treatment for renal anemia [162]. It has also shown potential favourable effects on oxidative stress, angiogenesis, inflammation, lipid metabolism, and fibrosis [163]. It reduced albuminuria in obese, type II DM mice [164]. Clinical trials need to confirm the proposed mechanisms in clinical practice. A clinical trial, FOXTROT, has been registered and will evaluate the effect of roxadustat on improving kidney oxygenation in patients with DN using blood oxygen level-dependent magnetic resonance imaging (NCT05810311). DKD’s pathophysiological mechanisms, such as oxidative stress, genetic and epigenetic modifications, and its connection to the gut microbiota, may lead to further advancements in treatment.

DM remains the leading cause of CKD and its progression to ESKD. Chronic hyperglycemia initiates a complex cascade of metabolic, hemodynamic, inflammatory, and fibrotic processes that progressively damage the kidneys. In the literature, the terms DKD and DN are often used interchangeably; however, it is important to distinguish between them. DKD refers to the clinical diagnosis of CKD in individuals with DM, whereas DN denotes a histopathological diagnosis confirmed by kidney biopsy. The standard of care consists of non-pharmacological interventions in conjunction with RAAS inhibition, whereas emerging therapeutic strategies include SGLT-2 inhibitors, non-steroidal MRAs, and GLP-1RAs. While further preclinical research is essential to clarify the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, ongoing and future clinical studies are expected to identify novel therapeutic strategies. In clinical practice, early screening of at-risk populations, prompt recognition of DKD, and timely initiation of appropriate therapies are critical to slowing disease progression and enhancing patients’ quality of life.
