Friedreich's Ataxia (FA) exemplifies a critical challenge for gene therapies
targeting multi-system disorders: achieving widespread therapeutic protein
repletion while minimizing vector dose and systemic exposure. No
disease-modifying therapies are approved for FA, and current gene therapy
approaches fail to address the full disease, forcing patients to choose between
cardiac protection and neurological benefit. Here, we present "Engineered
Cross-Correction," in which the therapeutic protein is bioengineered for
secretion, expanding the therapeutic footprint. We apply this approach to FA by
engineering a secretable frataxin and delivering it via a single
intra-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) injection of an adeno-associated viral (AAV)
vector equipped with a novel capsid and tissue-selective promoter. We achieved
broad protein repletion across key target tissues-heart, dorsal root ganglia,
and cerebellum-in mouse and non-human primate. In FA mouse models, we observed
rescue of cardiac and neurological phenotypes, marking the first demonstration
of dual correction with a single, minimally invasive administration. These
benefits were achieved without widespread transduction, reducing vector burden
and associated toxicity. Our findings establish a scalable platform that
contrasts with intravenous blood-brain barrier (BBB)-penetrant gene delivery and
offers a generalizable strategy for multi-system disorders. Beyond FA, this
positions Engineered Cross-Correction as a new frontier for the next generation
of gene therapies.

Friedreich’s Ataxia (FA) is the most common inherited ataxia, with an estimated prevalence of 1 case per 50,000 individuals.1,2,3,4 FA is caused by loss of expression of the frataxin gene (FXN), primarily due to GAA trinucleotide repeat expansions in the first intron.5,6,7 Complete loss of FXN protein is embryonic lethal, whereas heterozygous carriers of expanded alleles expressing ∼50%–60% of normal FXN are asymptomatic.8 In contrast, patients typically express ∼5%–30% of normal FXN, with longer repeat expansions correlating with lower expression and more aggressive disease.9,10,11,12

FXN is a ubiquitous mitochondrial protein produced as a pre-protein that localizes to the mitochondria due to an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS), which is cleaved by mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP), freeing the mature FXN peptide.13 Functionally, FXN is an accelerator of iron-sulfur cluster biogenesis.14,15 Loss of FXN activity leads to toxic accumulation of iron5,6 and mitochondrial dysfunction,6,15 which cause neuronal dysfunction and degeneration in cerebellar dentate nucleus and dorsal root ganglia (DRG), resulting in ataxia symptoms and reduced quality of life. Meanwhile, the leading cause of death among FA patients is arrhythmia due to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.6,16 Ataxia symptoms develop in childhood and are progressive with patients confined to wheelchairs by their mid-20s.17 Cardiac symptoms affect some patients and develop later, causing premature death, typically between the ages of 40 and 50 years of age.18,19

A key challenge for FA therapeutic development has been targeting both neuronal and cardiac manifestations.20,21 Omaveloxolone, the first therapeutic approved for FA, targets cellular phenotypes related to mitochondrial dysfunction downstream of FXN loss,22 slowing progression of ataxia symptoms without affecting cardiac-related symptoms.23,24 Multiple strategies targeting mitochondrial dysfunction and iron accumulation are being pursued,20,21 but FXN replacement holds the most promise. FXN protein replacement therapy (NCT04176991, NCT04519567, NCT05579691, and NCT06447025)25,26 and peripherally administered adeno-associated viral (AAV)-FXN (NCT05302271, NCT05445323)27,28,29,30,31,32,33 have advanced to clinical trials but can only address cardiac symptoms, clashing with expressed patient preferences that highlight primacy of ataxia and progression.34 Neuronal repletion of FXN by AAV rescued ataxia in a mouse model35; strategies for neuronal FXN repletion using blood-brain barrier (BBB)-penetrant AAV capsids have been in development for years, but no AAV-FXN gene therapy targeting ataxia has yet advanced to clinical trials and systemic administration of AAV increases safety risks associated with liver over-expression.29,30

To overcome these limitations and to develop an innovative strategy addressing both neuronal and cardiac dysfunction in FA, we developed Engineered Cross-Correction, a novel strategy to expand the therapeutic reach of AAV-delivered FXN via intra-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) administration. Rather than relying solely on widespread transduction, this approach uses a bioengineered frataxin variant capable of secretion from transduced “factory” cells and uptake by neighboring, non-transduced cells, enabling broad protein repletion without excessive vector burden. This approach leverages the inherent advantages of intra-CSF delivery to achieve direct access to the central nervous system (CNS) and sensory ganglia, bypassing the BBB,36 while exploiting the natural outflow of AAV particles from CSF into the bloodstream to simultaneously target peripheral tissues.37,38 By integrating a tissue-selective promoter, we further enhanced precision and reduced off-target tissue expression.

This strategy successfully rescued both neurological and cardiac phenotypes in FA mouse models and achieved clinically relevant FXN repletion in the non-human primate (NHP) heart, demonstrating its promise as a scalable and translationally viable gene therapy for FA and more broadly, as a platform for addressing multi-organ disorders requiring coordinated CNS and peripheral protein repletion.

For treatment of FA, transduction of cerebellar dentate neurons, sensory ganglia neurons, and cardiomyocytes is critical for treating all disease manifestations. AAV.GMU01 achieves this transduction pattern with intra-CSF administration39; however, to determine if systemic administration of AAV.GMU01 can enhance transduction in these target tissues, we administered AAV.GMU01 intravenous (i.v.) to NHPs (Table S1; Figure 1D) and evaluated vector genome (VG) biodistribution. For comparison, intra-cisterna magna (ICM) biodistribution data for AAV.GMU01 were obtained from our previously published study,39 which used the same transgene as in the i.v.-administration study shown here and a total dose of 2.75e13 VG. The immunohistochemistry (IHC) images in Figures 1A–1C are new generated data from animals treated in that prior work.39 In our current systemic administration study, we observed high VG distribution in the liver and heart (Figure 1E), with 10-fold higher cardiac distribution than previously reported with ICM administration,39 and reporter expression in cardiomyocytes throughout the heart (Figure 1F). In CNS, however, AAV.GMU01 penetration across the BBB was low (Figure 1E), resulting in 25- and 62-fold lower transduction in cerebellar dentate nucleus and DRG with i.v. administration relative to ICM.39 These data—25-fold superiority in cerebellum, 62-fold superiority in DRG, and 10-fold inferiority in heart—indicate that ICM administration in NHPs better balances biodistribution of AAV.GMU01 across target tissues for the treatment of FA.

NHP biodistribution better approximates distribution in patients, but rodent studies are critical for demonstrating therapeutic efficacy; however, AAV capsid biodistribution profiles and cellular tropism can differ between rodents and NHPs. Therefore, we evaluated VG biodistribution in the mouse following intra-cerebroventricular (i.c.v.), ICM, intrathecal (IT), and combined ICM+IT administration (Figures 1G; Table S2), administering equal VG doses allometrically scaled from NHPs based on brain weight.39 Across target tissues, combined ICM+IT administration reliably achieved highest distribution (Figure 1H). Transgene expression was linearly related to VG distribution (Figures 1I and S1A; p < 2e−16, adjusted R2 = 0.365, by linear modeling), and in situ hybridization (ISH) for GFP mRNA showed strong expression in cerebellar Purkinje neurons, granule cells, and deep cerebellar neurons (Figures 1J and S1B), as well as consistent expression in cardiomyocytes throughout the heart (Figure 1K). With ICM or ICM+IT administration in mouse, distribution to cerebellum exceeded transduction in NHP cerebellum with ICM administration; however, for all other target tissues, distribution was lower in mouse relative to NHP (Figure 1H).39 Although the present study was not powered to detect statistically significant differences among intra-CSF routes, we selected ICM+IT for subsequent rodent studies based on its slightly lower variability in injection quality compared with ICM alone, potentially due to the dose being split between two injections.

Although FA is caused by reduced FXN expression ubiquitously, FXN repletion is primarily required in neurons and cardiomyocytes.20,41,42 Indeed, excessive over-expression of FXN protein in liver impairs mitochondrial function.29,30 Therefore, we sought to refine our approach by selectively driving AAV-FXN expression in target tissues. To achieve this, we designed a bicistronic genome encoding two copies of human FXN (Figure 2A). To enable neuronal expression, one copy was driven by the human synapsin (hSynapsin) promoter,43,44,45,46 whereas the second copy was driven by newDesmin, a novel variant of the desmin promoter (Figure 2D),47 to enable expression in cardiomyocytes. To eliminate AAV genome complementarity, we codon-optimized only one transgene and used distinct poly-adenylation terminator sequences for each transgene. We administered AAV.GMU01 encoding bicistronic genomes to mice via ICM+IT injection (Figures 2A; Table S3) and evaluated FXN mRNA expression from each promoter. Using transgene-specific primer-probe assays, we observed a robust linear correlation between hSynapsin and newDesmin activity within individual samples (Figure 2B; p < 2e−16, adjusted R2 = 0.853, by linear modeling) with a consistent bias toward higher newDesmin expression (Figures 2B and S2A). To determine how this bias changes across tissue types, we evaluated the relative contribution of each promoter to total FXN mRNA expression across tissues and, surprisingly, observed near-complete reliance on the newDesmin promoter in brain, where hSynapsin would have been predicted to dominate (Figure S2A). Desmin is a muscle-enriched cytoskeletal protein48,49; thus the high neuronal expression was surprising. The newDesmin promoter was developed from the classic desmin promoter48,49 by replacing a repressor region with a second enhancer copy (Figure 2D).47 To determine if this modification drives an expanded expression profile, we evaluated FXN RNA expression and VG distribution following ICM+IT administration of AAV.GMU01 encoding human FXN under control of desmin or newDesmin promoters (Figure 2D and Table S4). To evaluate promoter efficiency across tissues independent of VG biodistribution, we quantified mRNA expression relative to VG levels and observed highly efficient mRNA expression in the heart (Figure 2E) with 10-fold lower efficiency in the liver and cerebellum and further decreases in the spinal cord and DRG (Figure 2E). These data demonstrate the tissue selectivity of Desmin and newDesmin promoters. To evaluate the actual expression strength of each promoter across tissues, we quantified FXN mRNA relative to an endogenous gene and observed highest expression in the liver and cerebellum (Figure 2F). Heart and DRG expression were comparable (Figure 2F). Desmin and newDesmin promoters demonstrated similar profiles overall with similar expression across heart regions (Figure 2G) and DRGs across spinal levels (Figure 2H). Heart expression at 6 weeks was reduced relative to 2 weeks (Figure 2I); however, the decrease in newDesmin activity was less than from the Desmin promoter. FXN protein expression was apparent in DRG (Figure 2J) and cerebellar neurons (Figure 2K). Altogether, these data indicate that expression in neurons is not unique to the newDesmin promoter variant; however, newDesmin provides more durable expression than the classic desmin promoter.

Next, we compared expression from newDesmin in bicistronic (Figure 2A) and monocistronic (Figure 2D) formats. Across studies, VG distribution was consistent (Figure S2B) and expression in CNS and DRG tissues was equivalent (Figures 2L and S2C); however, we observed a surprising reduction in newDesmin expression specific to heart and liver with the bicistronic genome (Figures 2L and S2C), suggesting an interaction between newDesmin and hSynapsin regulatory elements. Based on these data, we simplified our strategy selecting newDesmin to drive high levels of transgene expression in the heart, with opportunistic expression in the cerebellum and DRG.

Use of a muscle-enriched promoter increases total FXN protein repletion in heart, but the footprint of repletion would still be limited by AAV distribution. Previous estimates in mouse suggest that treating 40% of cardiomyocytes is necessary for therapeutic benefit.28 Cross-correction, which involves secretion of an AAV-expressed transgene from transduced cells and uptake of secreted transgene by non-transduced cells, can expand the therapeutic footprint for gene therapies and enable broad protein repletion.39 Therefore, we sought to engineer the FXN protein to facilitate secretion and cellular uptake.

Previous pre-clinical and clinical studies have used cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) to enable uptake of extracellular cargoes, including FXN protein.25,26,50,51 FXN protein tagged with a fragment of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) trans-activator of transcription (TAT) protein was taken into cells without disrupting mitochondrial localization.25,26,50 We used a variant of this CPP, designated TATk, specifically designed for in vivo secretion.52,53 Endogenous FXN localizes to the mitochondria due to a strong, N-terminal MTS that is necessary for mitochondrial targeting and cannot be removed.54,55 Thus, enabling FXN secretion posed a challenge. We hypothesized that incorporating an exogenous secretory signal peptide before the FXN MTS could promote secretion.56

To identify candidate signal peptides enabling secretion of full-length human FXN, we performed an in silico screen of signal peptides from 16 genes encoding natively secreted proteins, evaluating secretion likelihood using AI-enabled DeepLoc57 (Figure S3A) and the likelihood and accuracy of signal peptide cleavage (Figure S3B) using SignalP.58 We identified only two candidates suitable for in vivo testing (Figure S3C).

To increase our candidate pool, we generated optimized signal peptides by incorporating N- and C-terminal sequence motifs from the best-performing signal peptides into the native sequences. N-terminal modifications were selected to increase secretion and C-terminal modifications were selected to influence cleavage. In total, 21 of 52 evaluated engineered candidates, representing 13 distinct parental signal peptides, met criteria supporting in vivo analysis (Figure S3D). For each parent, we identified the best sequence and, from these, we selected five candidates (SP1-5), based on secretion probability and cleavage accuracy, for in vivo evaluation. We included three candidates generated through our engineering process (SP1-3) and the two native sequences originally identified (SP4-5).

We administered AAV.GMU01 encoding codon-optimized FXN modified with each signal peptide sequence and TATk CPP53 ICM+IT in mice (Table S5) and collected brain, spinal cord, and heart after 4 weeks (Figures S3E and S3F). Of note, these engineered FXN variants were included in the same study in which we evaluated bicistronic expression from hSynapsin and newDesmin promoters (Figures 2A–2C; Table S3) and also had bicistronic construction. Due to the strong bias toward newDesmin-mediated expression (Figure 2B), we focused our analysis on codon-optimized FXN expressed from the newDesmin promoter. In the cerebellum, AAV-FXN protein expression was widespread and largely overlapped with regions of AAV-FXN RNA (Figure S3G). For variants engineered with SP1, SP3, and SP5 signal peptides, we observed cells containing AAV-FXN protein without AAV-FXN RNA (Figure 3A, arrowheads), suggesting cross-correction. We did not observe such cells for SP2- and SP4-engineered variants. Our primary objective in this study was to determine the area of heart tissue with clinically relevant FXN repletion for each candidate. We evaluated FXN repletion throughout the NHP heart using IHC of six regions and observed a notable increase in FXN signal (Figures S5D, S5E, and 5B). To quantify the heart area expressing elevated FXN, we measured FXN signal in segments encompassing ∼5–7 cardiomyocytes (Figure 5C) and used a novel modification of quantile-quantile (QQ) analysis to align FXN signal distributions with distributions from tissue sections from buffer-treated animals (Figure 5D). Based on published data in the literature,8,10,11,28 we estimated that repletion of FXN protein to 60% of the heart would likely completely rescue FA symptoms, and a minimum of 30% repletion, when combined with endogenous expression, could provide durable symptom relief. We found that FXN protein was increased by at least 60% in 40%–60% of the heart and by at least 30% in >85% of the heart (Figure 5E). Furthermore, we used capillary western to evaluate FXN processing in the heart and only detected mature FXN protein, indicating mitochondrial localization and cleavage of both engineered variants (Figure 5F). These data suggest robust repletion of functional FXN protein, within clinically meaningful levels, across a large majority of heart tissue in primate.

We report the development of a novel gene therapy platform with strong potential to address the dual cardiac and neurological manifestations of FA. This was accomplished through a multi-pronged approach: (1) leveraging intra-CSF delivery to access CNS, peripheral nervous system, and peripheral tissues; (2) incorporating a tissue-selective promoter to balance expression across organs with disparate transduction efficiencies; and (3) protein engineering to enable secretion and uptake of frataxin, thereby expanding the therapeutic footprint beyond directly transduced cells. Together, these design features enabled rescue of both ataxia and cardiac phenotypes in mouse models, as well as functionally relevant FXN repletion in NHP DRG and heart.

The relationship between FXN expression and disease severity is well established.10,11,12 Even modest increases in FXN protein are predicted to improve neuronal and cardiac function and partially relieve symptoms in FA patients.10,11,12 Asymptomatic carriers of a single expanded allele maintain approximately 60% of normal FXN levels, and preclinical data suggest that restoring FXN in 50% of cardiomyocytes can be sufficient for full phenotypic correction.8,27,28 Based on these benchmarks, the observed 60% increase in FXN protein across 40%–60% of NHP heart tissue would be expected to have a significant beneficial effect on cardiac dysfunction.

The observation that robust transduction of cardiomyocytes was achieved in NHPs, with both a high percentage of cells transduced and strong per-cell expression, suggests that the heart is a tractable target for our approach and supports the potential for clinically meaningful efficacy in patients. These results, however, must be interpreted in the context of our mouse model results, where a similar magnitude of cardiac biodistribution did not translate into a large survival extension. Whereas studies using high-dose systemic AAV-FXN (1e13–5e13 vector genomes per kg body weight, translating to 6.5e14–3.25e15 VG per patient) have demonstrated significant lifespan rescue,27,28 our dosing strategy was designed to reflect a clinically feasible exposure level. Within this context, our results are comparable to the benefit observed with a minimally effective i.v. dose reported by Muñoz-Zuluaga et al.32 and support the translational potential of our intra-CSF, cross-correcting strategy to achieve therapeutic benefit while limiting systemic vector exposure. Furthermore, in rodents, AAV.GMU01 exhibits a distinct biodistribution pattern, with reduced spread throughout tissues and lower peripheral distribution in mouse than NHP with intra-CSF administration.39 Differences between AAV performance in rodent and primate has been observed for other capsids,62,63,64,65,66 and species-dependent variability poses a challenge for AAV-based gene therapy development.

Importantly, the benefits of frataxin repletion must be balanced against toxicity risks associated with over-expression, particularly in the heart and liver. Previous studies have demonstrated that FXN expression at 9-fold above endogenous in the heart and liver was well tolerated; however, levels exceeding 20-fold above normal can impair mitochondrial function.29,30 Thus, gene therapy for FA requires achieving expression within a therapeutic window of 60%–900%, and this poses a significant challenge for systemically administered gene therapies, whether CNS penetrant or not. Notably, intra-CSF administration of AAV.GMU01 reduced cardiac biodistribution by ∼90% compared to i.v. administration in NHPs,39 offering a straightforward and effective mitigation strategy. Additionally, Engineered Cross-Correction provides a built-in safety mechanism by distributing protein expression across a broader cellular population, thereby limiting peak concentrations in any single transduced cell. To address persistent liver exposure across routes, we employed a muscle-biased newDesmin promoter that reduced hepatic expression to levels comparable to those in the heart, even in the context of 100-fold higher transduction efficiency. Indeed, the combination of CSF delivery and transcriptional targeting prevented frataxin over-expression beyond 9-fold in any NHP tissue. While both Desmin and newDesmin promoters performed comparably in most contexts, the newDesmin promoter demonstrated improved durability of expression in the heart, which contributed to its selection for further development. This decision was informed by both promoter efficiency, independent of biodistribution, and absolute expression strength, reflecting the combined effects of capsid distribution and promoter activity.

To enable cross-correction, we designed a secretable FXN variant by appending an N-terminal secretory signal peptide and CPP onto the native FXN coding sequence. Retaining the MTS was essential to preserve mitochondrial localization and function.26,55 However, competition between the MTS and the secretory signal posed a challenge, necessitating a structure-guided, iterative engineering approach. By integrating high-performing elements from both native and synthetic signal peptides, we expanded our design space from two initial candidates to 23, ultimately identifying a top-performing construct (SP1) with strong activity across species. Although our current data collectively is suggestive of cross-correction, further confirmatory assays, potentially including in vitro analysis, would strengthen the mechanistic case. This protein engineering framework may be broadly applicable to other indications requiring extensive protein distribution, such as Rett syndrome and Fragile X syndrome.

Although protein engineering offers significant benefits, it also poses risks related to antigenicity and impaired protein processing and function. FA patients maintain residual expression of FXN; thus antigenicity risk for FXN itself is minimal. The signal peptide is rapidly cleaved following protein synthesis, resulting in a transient pool of engineered FXN protein that likely poses minimal antigenicity risk. Although the CPP represents the primary potential immunogenic component, similar CPPs have been used in FXN replacement therapies currently in clinical trials and in other protein therapeutics—such as TAT-fused proteins—that have completed human testing with favorable safety profiles.67,68,69,70,71 Thus, in this case, protein engineering appears to add minimal risk to the overall antigenicity profile, and in our 3-month-long NHP study, we observed no clinical safety signs. We showed that engineered FXN protein is processed to the mature form, a step restricted to mitochondria, demonstrating that engineering does not prevent mitochondrial targeting, in agreement with previous work.50 Once the mature FXN isoform is released from the MTS, we expect AAV-expressed FXN to exhibit normal activity levels as there is no longer any distinction between AAV-expressed, engineered FXN variants and endogenously expressed FXN.

In summary, we propose an alternative gene therapy paradigm—Engineered Cross-Correction—for broad therapeutic protein delivery that is both precise and scalable. In the context of FA, AAV.GMU01 delivered via intra-CSF administration achieved simultaneous targeting of cardiac and neurological tissues, aligning with patient priorities and addressing critical gaps in current therapeutic strategies. This approach has the potential to improve both quality of life and life expectancy for individuals with FA. More broadly, the combined use of targeted administration, protein engineering, and transcriptional control offers a flexible framework to overcome long-standing challenges in the field—namely, how to reconcile therapeutic breadth with manufacturing and safety constraints. Engineered Cross-Correction may thus enable the translation of promising gene therapies to a wider range of multisystem disorders that were previously considered intractable.

A minimum of two DRGs from each level were evaluated. Microscopic findings were graded as 0 for absence of lesion, 1 for minimal, 2 for mild, 3 for moderate, 4 for marked, and 5 for severe.
