# Chalazion: racial risk factors for formation, recurrence, and surgical intervention.
**Authors:** Evans J, Vo KBH, Schmitt M
**Journal:** Can J Ophthalmol (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jcjo.2021.04.023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcjo.2021.04.023)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: An association between race and formation of chalazion has yet to be objectively established. This study investigates race as a risk factor for chalazion and chalazion surgery. Understanding racial risk factors in formation of chalazion, recurrent chalazion, and chalazion requiring surgery (often with general anesthesia in children) informs decisions regarding eyelid hygiene, early topical medical therapy, and aggressiveness with oral antibiotic therapy for coexisting conditions such as blepharitis.

METHODS: Demographic data was collected for all pediatric visits to the University of Wisconsin-Madison ophthalmology department from 2012-2019. Retrospective chart review was performed for the subset with chalazion.

RESULTS: Of 28 433 minors, 584 had 1088 chalazia, a 2% overall rate. Chalazion was seen in 1.8% of non-Hispanic/Latino participants and 3.8% of Hispanic/Latino participants (p value <0.0001). Chalazion was seen in 1.7% of white participants, compared to 4.3% of American Indian or Alaska Native participants (p value <0.0001) and 4.0% of Asian participants (p value <0.0001). More than one chalazion was recorded in 31% of subjects without coexisting meibomian gland disease, blepharitis, or marginal keratitis, and in 56% (p < 0.0001) with one of these conditions. Repeated diagnoses of chalazion on separate encounters were seen in 17% without these conditions and in 33% (p < 0.0001) with one of these conditions.

CONCLUSION: Hispanic/Latino, American Indian, and Asian participants developed chalazion at a rate higher than other racial/ethnic groups, whereas patients with meibomian gland disease or blepharitis are especially at risk for developing multiple chalazia on separate encounters. No group was more likely to require surgical intervention than any other.

Chalazion is a common, benign lipogranulomatous condition of the eyelids that presents in both children and adults. 1 , 2 Although a racial predisposition for formation of chalazion has been suspected, it has never been objectively investigated. 3‒ 5 Population studies have helped established the prevalence of chalazion in populations made up of more than one race. 3‒ 9 Furthermore, the relationship between race and the need for surgical intervention or recurrence of chalazion has not been studied. The purpose of this study is to investigate any racial predisposition for the diagnosis of chalazion, as well as any predisposition toward recurrent chalazion or chalazion that requires surgery. Identifying those at higher risk of chalazion formation, recurrent chalazion, and chalazion requiring surgery (often including general anesthesia in children) can inform medical decision making. Specifically, identifying populations at higher risk of multiple surgical encounters may influence the decision to recommend more long-term eyelid hygiene, early topical medical therapy, and a lower threshold for recommending oral antibiotic therapy or longer duration of oral antibiotic therapy to treat coexisting ocular conditions such as blepharitis, which may influence the risk of developing recurrent chalazion.

Chalazion arises in a sebaceous gland, namely a meibomian gland or a gland of Zeis. 1 , 2 Diagnosis is clinical, based on typical features. Without treatment, they frequently become chronic in nature. Its primary danger is that malignancies such as sebaceous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma may mimic the appearance of chalazion.

Management of chalazion is typically with warm compresses, and where coexisting blepharitis is present, eyelid scrubs (e.g., with hypochlorous acid) are frequently added. In more acute or inflammatory cases with erythema, a topical steroid (sometimes in conjunction with a topical antibiotic) may be added, though the effectiveness of this treatment is not clear. 2 A tetracycline class oral antibiotic may be attempted, especially in cases associated with rosacea. When the chalazion is inflamed or secondarily infected, oral azithromycin may be used. 2 When medical management has failed, injectable steroids have shown to be effective in reducing lesion size by at least 80% in about 80% of those who receive 1‒2 injections. 9 Coexisting blepharitis is known to reduce the efficacy of this method of treatment. 9 Intralesional steroid injection may be a good choice when a chalazion is located near the canaliculus. 10 Incision and curettage is considered the more definitive treatment, though recurrences are still known to occur afterward. 1 , 9

Approval for this Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act‒compliant study was obtained through the Institutional Review Board of University of Wisconsin, Madison, WI, and all research adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Pediatric population statistics were obtained via the RedCap electronic data capture tools (Nashville, Tenn.) hosted by the University of Wisconsin, which revealed the racial and ethnic proportions of all pediatric patients who visited the University of Wisconsin ophthalmology and optometry departments between 2012‒2019 with or without a diagnosis of chalazion. Patients older than the age of 18 at the time of diagnosis were excluded. Further, retrospective chart review was performed for the subset of patients with chalazion.

Demographic data as well as the number of chalazia in each location were recorded, as were the treatments required in order to achieve resolution of a chalazion, including eyelid hygiene, medical, and surgical treatments. To ensure uniformity in data, a single reviewer (K.B.V.) performed all chart reviews. Race and ethnicity were both collected, as defined in the United States census.

Eyelid hygiene consisted of warm compresses and eyelid scrubs. When medical treatment was required, an antibiotic and steroid combination was prescribed. When surgical treatment was required, this consisted of incision and curettage under general anesthesia with accompanying expression of meibomian glands.

Statistical analysis included χ2 analysis in a family-wise approach with a Bonferroni-corrected statistical significance threshold of 0.00156.

The population investigated by RedCap consisted of 28433 persons. Of these, 584 patients with 1088 chalazia underwent retrospective chart review. Table 1 reflects the demographic distribution of chalazion in the full population of 28433 persons and any significant associations with race or ethnicity. All other data and tables are based on data from the subset of 584 persons with chalazion. Overall, 2% of the pediatric population presenting to University of Wisconsin clinics in this period had chalazion.

Of those 584 children who presented with chalazion, 63% were female, 54% had blepharitis, 56% had meibomian gland dysfunction, and 4% had staphylococcal marginal keratitis. In this cohort, 255 subjects presented with their first chalazion between ages 0 and 5, which was more frequent compared to 156 subjects aged 6–11 (p value 0.000001) and compared to 173 subjects aged 12‒18 (p value 0.00007). Multiple chalazion were present in 38% of children, and surprisingly only 4% of these were affected unilaterally, with the other 34% having chalazion on each side of the face at some point in time, while 18% of children with chalazion developed at least 3 chalazia. Children with multiple chalazia had a mean of 3.3 chalazia. Repeated encounters for unique new chalazion were present in 22% of patients, with 8% having at least 3 separate encounters with new chalazia. Children with repeated encounters for unique new chalazia presented a mean of 2.7 times. Among all 1088 chalazia, 39.4% required surgical treatment, and no race was identified as higher risk for requiring surgical management (p = 0.727). Although a weak correlation between blepharitis and increased risk of requiring surgery was found (p ∼ 0.08), no statistically significant associations between meibomian gland dysfunction, staph marginal disease, or blepharitis and surgical intervention were discovered.

Analysis of racial and ethnic association with surgical intervention showed no statistically significant differences. Hispanic/Latino participants required surgery at a rate of 36%, whereas non-Hispanic/Latino participants required surgery at a rate of 32% (p value 0.4386). Similarly, surgery was required at a rate of 32% in white participants and, compared to white participants, was required at a rate of 35% in American Indian or Alaskan Native participants (p value 0.9387), 32% in Asian participants (p value 1), 35% in Black or African American participants (p value 0.7741), and 40% (2 of 5 participants) in Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander participants.

Analysis of the racial and ethnic association with formation of multiple chalazion as a minor showed no statistically significant differences. Multiple chalazia formed at a rate of 42% in Hispanic/Latino participants and 37% in non-Hispanic/Latino participants (p value 0.3641). Similarly, multiple chalazia formed in 38% in white participants and, compared to white participants, formed at a rate of 30% in American Indian or Alaskan Native participants (p value 0.6025), 39% in Asian participants (p value 0.9652), 38% in Black or African American participants (p value 1), and 20% (1 of 5 subjects) in Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander participants.

Similarly, there were no significant differences in the number of minors who developed new chalazion on separate occasions between racial or ethnic groups. Presentation with new chalazion on multiple occasions occurred at a rate of 28% in Hispanic/Latino participants and 20% in non-Hispanic/Latino participants (p value 0.09618). New chalazia on multiple occasions formed at a rate of 22% in white participants and, compared to white participants, formed at a rate of 25% in American Indian or Alaskan Native participants (p value 0.9803), 25% in Asian participants (p value 0.6918), 12% in Black or African American participants (p value 0.1129), and 20% (1 of 5 subjects) in Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander participants.

Table 2 highlights each ocular comorbidity and whether each was significantly associated with a higher rate of developing multiple chalazia. When considering surgical treatment, perhaps more important to know than whether a child may develop multiple chalazia is whether chalazia develop on multiple occasions. These children are then at risk for having multiple trips to the operating room requiring general anesthesia. Thus Table 3 is similar to Table 2 but focuses on those children whose additional chalazia developed on separate encounters.

No ocular disease was significantly associated with a higher rate of surgery. Among subjects with meibomian gland disease, 46% required surgery, whereas among those without meibomian gland disease, 30% required surgery (p value 0.001927), which was not significant after Bonferroni whereas in those without blepharitis, 30% required surgery (p value 0.5629). In those with staphylococcal marginal hypersensitivity, 5 subjects required surgery while 3 did not. When meibomian gland disease, blepharitis, and staphylococcal marginal hypersensitivity were pooled, 40% of patients with at least one of these conditions required surgery whereas 29% without any of these conditions required surgery (p value 0.02219), which was not significant after Bonferroni correction.

Two percent of all pediatric patients presenting to the ophthalmology and optometry clinics had chalazion. The age at which patients presented with a first chalazion is displayed in Figure 1. Overall, a significantly larger percentage of patients presenting with a first episode of chalazion were in the age range from 2‒5 years old. It should be noted that ophthalmology and optometry are specialty clinics, and chalazion is a diagnosis that is sometimes cared for by a primary care provider. It is possible the age distribution is somewhat delayed from the true population distribution and that the whole first 5 years of life are an especially common time for chalazion to manifest.

Race and ethnicity showed some important associations with chalazion. When all the population data were considered, participants who were Hispanic/Latino developed chalazion at a higher rate than non-Hispanic/Latino participants. In addition, participants who were American Indian and Asian developed chalazion at a higher rate than white subjects. Only 5 participants of native Hawaiian or Pacific Island descent were included, and although 4 of these 5 had chalazion—which is a very high rate of chalazion in this group—the low number of participants prevents any meaningful or statistical conclusions. This area may merit further study to see if a strong association is in fact present in a larger cohort. Interestingly, race and ethnicity showed no significant differences in the rates of multiple chalazion formation or chalazion formation on multiple occasions.

Ocular comorbidities also showed some important associations with chalazion. Meibomian gland disease, blepharitis, or any one of the three tested ocular comorbidities was associated with a higher chance of developing multiple chalazion and with a higher chance of developing chalazion on multiple occasions.

Race, ethnicity, and ocular disease seem to play a part in the development one or more chalazia, yet not in which chalazia require surgical intervention. There were no significant associations with chalazia that required surgical intervention in terms of race, ethnicity, or ocular comorbidities. Some associations were suggested but not statistically significant.

This study has limitations. The most significant limitation is the generalizability of the study to other populations. This study assumes that no particular race was more likely than any other to present to the ophthalmology and optometry clinics with the diagnosis of a chalazion, as opposed to being managed either at home or by a primary care physician. However, it is possible this was not the case. Although the data in this study was previously unavailable in any form and therefore is valuable, large multicentre studies would be required to address this limitation. Other study limitations are primarily related to the retrospective nature of the study. For example, good data regarding patient compliance with eyelid hygiene or medical treatments were not available because this factor was not routinely recorded in patient encounters. The study assumes that parents and children, prior to electing for surgery, would have reasonably attempted to exhaust the medical means prescribed to them and that providers would inquire as to the compliance and effectiveness of the medical treatments prescribed prior to resorting to surgical treatment. However, it is possible that was not true in all cases. There were no statistically significant associations with surgical intervention identified despite some groups being more likely to develop multiple chalazia than others. Since nearly all patients were recommended eyelid hygiene and most also had medical treatment, it is possible that once started, continued eyelid hygiene habits helped to prevent future episodes of chalazion from progressing to the point that surgical intervention was required. This cannot be determined from this study because a group randomized to receive no eyelid hygiene was not included. Thus, given the associations that ocular disease had with multiple chalazion formation, it is likely prudent to continue to recommend eyelid hygiene as not only a means to treat and/or prevent chalazion formation, but also to possibly prevent the need for future chalazion surgery.

This study provides a better understanding of chalazion risk factors for formation, repeat presentation, and resistance to medical treatment requiring surgical intervention. Hispanic/ Latino, American Indian, and Asian participants develop chalazion at a rate higher than other racial/ethnic groups, whereas patients with meibomian gland disease or blepharitis are especially at risk for developing multiple chalazia on separate encounters. Age at presentation with first chalazion was more frequent in young children aged 0‒5 than in older age-groups. Overall, multiple chalazion were present in 38% of children with chalazion, whereas only 18% of children developed 3 or more total chalazia. Children with multiple chalazia had a mean of 3.3 chalazia, which presented over a mean of 2.7 encounters. Repeat encounters for unique new chalazia were seen in 22% of children with chalazion, with 8% having at least 3 encounters for unique new chalazia. Surgical treatment was required for 39.4% of chalazia. Although no group was more likely to require surgical intervention than any other, pediatric patients with meibomian gland disease or blepharitis may benefit from long-term use of eyelid hygiene to prevent the need for multiple trips to the operating room for chalazion treatment.
