# A novel dominant-negative variant of IRF8 in a mother and son: Clinical, phenotypic and biological characteristics.
**Authors:** Ham H, Isham CR, Ristagno EH, Correia C, Ennis SM, Kandasamy RK, Garapati K, Zhang C, Kohlhagen MC, Sadighi Akha E, Rodriguez-Quevedo MF, Schultz DF, Chen B, Boyce TG, Gregory SW, Kohorst MA, Dasari S, Murray DL, Halling KC, Kipp BR, Kumánovics A, Li H, Pandey A, Billadeau DD, Sadighi Akha AA
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaci.2024.11.041](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2024.11.041)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The few reported patients with pathogenic IRF8 variants have manifested 2 distinct phenotypes: (1) an autosomal recessive severe immunodeficiency with significant neutrophilia and absence of or significant decrease in monocytes and dendritic cells and (2) a dominant-negative form with only a decrease in conventional type 2 dendritic cells (cDC2s) and susceptibility to mycobacterial disease.

OBJECTIVES: Genetic testing of a child with persistent EBV viremia identified a novel IRF8 variant: c.1279dupT (p.∗427Leuext∗42). The variant was also found in his mother, who was subsequently diagnosed with a human papillomavirus-positive tumor. We sought to examine the pathogenicity of the identified IRF8 variant and its phenotypic and functional characteristics.

METHODS: Immunophenotypic and functional flow cytometry, natural killer cell cytotoxicity, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry, T-cell receptor Vβ spectratyping, Sanger sequencing, RNA-sequencing, Olink proteomics, immunoblotting, molecular cloning, dual-luciferase reporter assay, immunofluorescence microscopy, and image analysis.

RESULTS: The 42 amino acid C-terminal extension of the mutant IRF8 (∼4 kDa heavier than wild type) impaired IRF8 nuclear localization in a dominant-negative manner and inhibited IRF1/IRF8-mediated transcriptional activities. Both patients had a decrease in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) and in cDC1s, a mild neutrophilia and a mild monocytosis. Their existing pDCs had impaired IFN-α production. On TLR engagement, the production of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12 by their monocytes and of IL-12 by their myeloid DCs were within normal limits. Natural killer cell development and cytolytic activity were essentially normal. RNA-sequencing and proteomic approaches bolstered the phenotypic and functional findings.

CONCLUSIONS: This study defines the pathogenic nature of the c.1279dupT (p.∗427Leuext∗42) IRF8 variant, determines its dominant-negative mechanism of action, and broadens the existing phenotype of human IRF8 immunodeficiency.

The interferon regulatory factor (IRF) family of transcription factors has 9 members in mammals and is involved in regulating gene expression in response to interferons1. All IRFs contain an amino-terminal DNA-binding domain (DBD) with a helix-loop-helix structure that recognizes the consensus DNA sequence element (A/GNGAAANNGAAACT), known as interferon sequence response element (ISRE)2. IRFs also contain IRF-association domain(s) (IADs) at the carboxy-terminal end that allow them to form homo- and hetero-dimers, and to interact with co-factors3. As a group, IRFs play an important role in immune cell development and in regulating interferon type I production and response. IRF8 was first identified for its ability to bind the interferon responsive sequences present in several IFN-inducible genes and designated as interferon consensus sequence binding protein (ICSBP)4, 5. Due to its structural similarity to other IRF family members, it has been officially renamed IRF86.

Studies in animal models have provided a detailed account of IRF8’s mechanisms of action. The Irf8−/− mice show increased susceptibility to viral infections, decreased production of IL-12p40 and IFN-γ, and systemic expansion of neutrophils followed by fatal blast crisis7. Additionally, they have reduced numbers of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) and CD8α+ myeloid dendritic cells, but not CD11b+ dendritic cells8, 9. The BXH-2 mice are homozygous for the Irf8R294C mutation in the IAD region of the gene, which affects IRF8’s dimerization. They retain their monocytes, macrophages and pDCs, but show a chronic myeloid leukemia-like syndrome, are unable to produce IL-12 and IFN-γ, and are susceptible to infection10. IRF8 promotes monocyte/dendritic cell differentiation while impeding the development of neutrophils: It induces monocyte differentiation directly, through its interaction with PU.1, and indirectly by inducing the transcription factor KLF411. It suppresses neutrophil differentiation through physically interacting with the transcription factor C/EBPα and hindering its binding to chromatin in monocyte-DC progenitors and common monocyte progenitors12. IRF8 is required for pDC development and function13, 14, is a terminal selector of the conventional type I dendritic cell (cDC1) lineage14 and maintains cDC1’s terminal differentiation through its interaction with BATF315. In addition, IRF8 is essential for the expression of IL-12p3516, IL-12p4017, 18 and IL-1819. The defect in IL-12p40 production and the consequent lack of Th1 polarization leads to higher susceptibility to intracellular pathogens in Irf8−/− and BXH-2 mice20–22

There are a handful of reports on pathogenic variants in human IRF8. They include five cases with an autosomal recessive inheritance and two unrelated individuals with a de novo dominant-negative variant. Table 1 lists the main reported clinical manifestations (Table 1A) and biological characteristics (Table 1B) of these variants. Two distinct phenotypes can be discerned in these patients: an autosomal recessive severe immunodeficiency with significant impairment of the myeloid lineage; and a dominant-negative form with only a decrease in cDC2 dendritic cells and susceptibility to mycobacterial disease.

The four functionally proven cases with autosomal recessive complete IRF8 deficiency include a patient homozygous for the missense variant p.K108E23, 24; another with the compound heterozygous variants p.R83C and p.R291Q (orthologue of R294C in BXH-2 mice)25; a third one with the homozygous truncating variant p.R111*26; and a fourth one with the compound heterozygous variants c.55del and p.R111*27. All of these patients displayed marked impairment in the myeloid lineage, including significant neutrophilia, the absence/severe decrease of both CD14+ and CD16+ monocytes and of dendritic cells, both plasmacytoid and myeloid/conventional23–27. They presented with a variety of infections; and adverse reactions to live bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)23, 24, 27, and measles, mumps and rubella (MMR)25 vaccines.

The p.K108E mutation leads to impaired nuclear localization, loss of transcriptional activity for IRF8 targets including IL12B and NOS2, and decreased protein stability24. Like the p.K108E mutant, p.R83C shows defective nuclear localization, while it is suggested that p.R291Q will not be able to interact effectively with other transcription factors through IAD25. Neither c.55del nor p.R111* were transcriptionally active27.

A fifth reported autosomal recessive case, who was compound heterozygous for variants p.A201V and p.P224L in the IAD region of IRF8, showed a defect in NK cell development and function, including a significant decrease in NK cell counts in the blood, an increased CD56bright/CD56dim ratio and a lack of NK cell cytolytic activity to K562 tumor cells28. It is noteworthy that the patients with the p.K108E and p.R83C/p.R291Q mutations have a similar defect in NK cell development as this case23–25, 28. Neither the p.A201V nor the p.P224L variant had any effect on protein levels, nuclear targeting, or trans-activation of IRF8 targets28; therefore this case has been categorized as functionally non-proven27.

A de novo dominant-negative mutation p.T80A has been reported in two patients23. The mutation significantly decreases binding to, and transactivation of, the IRF8 target genes IL12B and NOS2. These patients had normal pDC and CD141+ mDC numbers but a marked decrease in the CD11c+CD1c+ mDC subset (cDC2), and an increased susceptibility to mycobacterial infection23, which is attributed to a decrease in IL-12 production because of cDC2 deficiency23, and a decrease in IFN-γ production in response to various stimuli29.

Here we describe a mother and son with a novel autosomal dominant mutation in IRF8 and delineate its clinical, phenotypic and biological characteristics.

A detailed account of the Materials and Methods used in this study is included in the supplementary material.

The highlights of the patients’ clinical picture have been mentioned in Table 1A. A detailed account of their clinical history is included in the supplementary material.

Sequence analysis and deletion/duplication testing of the son using the Invitae Primary Immunodeficiency panel showed the heterozygous variant NM_002163.2:c.1279dupT (p.*427Leuext*42) in IRF8 and the heterozygous variant NM_139276.2:c.2228G>T (p.Gly743Val) in STAT3. Subsequent testing of the mother on the same panel confirmed the existence of the same heterozygous variant in IRF8. She did not have the STAT3 variant observed in her son. Neither of the mother’s biological parents had the IRF8 variant, pointing to the de novo occurrence of the IRF8 mutation in the mother.

First, cDNA obtained from T cells of healthy donors and the patients were used for Sanger sequencing of the stop codon region of IRF8. This confirmed the insertion of thymidine (‘T’) within the IRF8 stop codon sequence (‘TAA’ >> ‘TTAA’) (Figure 1A). The IRF8 variant is predicted to have an additional 126 nucleotides/42 amino acids. As the coding sequence of the DNA expression construct was derived from patient cDNA, and the extension was within coverage of the performed cDNA sequencing, the sequence of the 126 nucleotides could be validated (Figure E1A). To complement these findings, we also analyzed the mutated IRF8 sequence detected on the Invitae Primary Immunodeficiency panel with Alamut Software (SOPHiA Genetics, Boston, MA). The section including the detected loss-of-stop mutation and the predicted new stop site are displayed in Figure E1B. Secondly, analysis of the RNAseq data acquired using the patients’ PBMCs showed two IRF8 transcripts for each of the patients, wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut), with a mutant allele frequency (MAF) of 0.432 and 0.405 in the mother and son respectively (Figure 1B). Finally, IRF8 protein expression was examined by immunoblotting healthy control and patient PBMC lysates. The expected single band of IRF8 at 50 kDa was detected in both the healthy donor and patient lysates. The patient lysates also contained another band with a higher molecular weight, consistent with the expected increase in molecular weight (~4 kDa) with the addition of 42 amino acids to the protein sequence because of the mutation (Figure 1C).

Complete blood count and differentials frequently showed mild neutrophilia over a decade of monitoring, less severe than the findings in autosomal recessive cases (Figure 2A). Unlike patients with biallelic autosomal recessive disease, who display lack of monocytes or severe monocytopenia, our patients had persistent mild monocytosis (Figure 2A); their monocytes included both classical CD14+ and non-classical CD16+ subsets (Figure 2B). Both patients had a decrease in pDCs (Figure 2B; Table E1). While their myeloid dendritic cell (mDC) counts were normal (Table E1), their CD141+ dendritic cell subset (cDC1) was substantially lower than control (Figure 2B). Functional assessment of the patients’ existing pDCs showed an impairment in producing IFN-α in response to the TLR7/8 agonist R848, and to the TLR9 agonist ODN2216 (Figure 3A; TableE2). By contrast, the patients’ mDCs produced a normal level of IL-12 in response to R848 (Figure 3B; TableE2). Additionally, engaging the TLR1/2, TLR2/6, TLR4, TLR5 and TLR7/8 receptors on the patients’ monocytes led to normal production of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-12 by these cells (TableE2).

The patients’ CD3+ T cell, CD4+ T cell, CD8+ T cell, B cell and NK cell counts are shown in Figure 4A. The patients displayed a normal distribution of the major NK cell subsets, i.e. CD16++CD56+ cytotoxic NK cells and CD56++ cytokine-producing NK cells, with the former decreasing in percentage in the son over time; both had normal expression of perforin and granzyme B (Table E1).

B cell subset analysis showed a decrease in switched memory B cells in the son, but not in the mother (Figure 4B, left). This notwithstanding, they have both had increased IgG and normal IgA levels during the period of observation (Figure 4B, middle). Mass spectrometric evaluation of their serum immunoglobulins confirmed that they were polyclonal (Figure 4B, right). T cell subset phenotyping showed an increase in central memory T cells in both subjects and an increase in effector memory T cells and HLA-DR+ T cells in the son over time (Table E1). More importantly, there has been a progressive increase and substantial down-regulation of CD28 and the increased expression of HLA-DR on the son’s CD8+ T cells (Figure 4C, bottom; Table E1). The CD8+CD28− T cells are mainly CD45RA+CCR7− and can therefore be classified as TEMRA, are comprised of HLA-DR− and HLA-DR+ cells (Figure 4C, bottom), but do not have a unique PD-1 or CD57 signature (data not shown). A parallel course has not been observed in the mother (Figure 4C, top; Table E1). Relatedly, TCRVβ spectratyping has revealed a significant overall decrease in TCRVβ repertoire diversity in the son, including the presence of 4 oligoclonal families (Figure 4D, bottom). By contrast, the mother’s TCRVβ repertoire is normal (Figure 4D, top).

The immunophenotypic analyses were complemented by functional studies. NK cell cytotoxic function in the two patients was within normal limits (Figure 4E), which contrasts with the findings in a subset of patients with biallelic autosomal recessive mutations28. They both had normal proliferative responses to PHA, PWM, anti-CD3, anti-CD3 + anti-CD28, and anti-CD3 + IL-2 stimulation (TableE4). Additionally, their CD8+ T cells expressed normal levels of CD107a/b and produced normal levels of IFN-γ after stimulation with PMA and ionomycin (Figure 4F).

To examine the potential effect of the proband’s STAT3 variant, we assessed STAT3 phosphorylation in CD4+ T cells in response to IL-6, IL-10 and IL-21 in both the son and the mother, who lacks this variant, and found them to be comparable (Figure 4G). STAT3 mutations with a functional effect may interfere with STAT1 phosphorylation30. Evaluating STAT1 phosphorylation in monocytes in response to IFN-α and IFN-γ also showed a similar response to these stimulants in the two patients, incidentally ruling out the existence of neutralizing anti-IFN-α and anti-IFN-γ in either subject. These results, and the absence of other findings associated with STAT3 mutations in the son, are consistent with a likely benign nature for this variant: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/514074/, last accessed on 30 September 2024.

We also examined the effect of the IRF8 mutation on the transcriptomic and proteomic profile of the two patients. RNA-seq was performed on PBMC from the two patients and compared to an unaffected control subject. The data were analyzed at various levels of complexity. Immune cell deconvolution was performed with CIBERSORT31, using leukocyte signature matrix (LM22). This suggested an increase in activated myeloid dendritic cells, mast cells and eosinophils (Figure 5A). Using a cut-off of abs(fold-change)>2, comparison of the patients with the control sample showed a significant difference in the expression of 939 genes in one or both subjects with the control (Pearson correlation rho=0.6996; p-value <2.2e−16); see Supplementary Tables E5–E7. Of these, 351 genes are shared between the two patients (Figure 5B). The scatterplot in Figure 5C shows the genome-wide RNA-seq correlation between the two patients, and the heatmap in Figure 5D displays the genes in common between the two patients with a cut-off of abs(log2 fold-change)>2.

Close inspection of this set of transcripts reveals notable findings. For example, the mother and son both show decreased expression of CLEC4C (CD303 or BDCA-2), a specific marker of pDC32; LAMP5, a biomarker of non-activated human pDC33; and DNASE1L334, which is predominantly expressed in pDC34. The patients also show a decrease in GATA2 expression, which can affect hematopoietic cell development, including pDCs35, and is also decreased in Irf8−/− mice36. These are consistent with the observed decrease in the pDC counts in the two patients (Figure 2B) and provide a basic biological validation of the acquired data.

Pathway enrichment analysis for genes with a cut-off of abs(log2 fold-change)>2 in both patients is displayed in Figure 5E; see Supplementary Table E8 for the list of genes included in each pathway. IL1B, IFNG, IL10 and its family member IL2637, and DUSP4 (Dual Specificity Phosphatase 4), which is decreased in patients with biallelic IRF8 mutations25, are among the notable genes in the enriched cytokine signaling pathway. Finally, we assessed changes in IRF8 target genes as defined in Shin et al.38 (Figure 5F). Both subjects showed a decrease in the expression of SPIB, an essential and specific regulator of pDC development that is required for TLR7- and TLR9-mediated IFN-α production in pDCs39–42, which were defective in the existing pDCs of both patients (Figure 3A). The two patients also showed an increase in the expression of numerous members of the transmembrane (TMEM) family of proteins43.

The RNA-seq approach was complemented by Olink proteomic analysis of the plasma samples of the two patients and two control subjects. A total of 2,888 proteins were quantified. The heatmap in Figure 5G displays the difference in protein abundance values across all samples and shows that the two patient samples cluster closely with each other, away from the control samples. Aggregate differences between the patient and control samples are shown in Figure 5H as a volcano plot. A total of 98 proteins were significantly altered between the two groups (p<0.05); see supplementary Table E9 for the list of these proteins, and supplementary Table E10 for the fold changes of all the quantified proteins. Like the RNA-seq results (Figure 5B), the proteomics findings also showed that while the two patients had certain protein changes in common, they were disparate in the expression of others (Figure 5I). We then assessed the correlation between the proteins evaluated on the Olink platform and their RNA-seq counterparts (Figure 5J; supplementary Table E11). The figure highlights molecules with abs(log2 fold-change)>1.0 in both conditions. These notably include IFNG, IL10, CD28 and CLEC4C, with the increase in IFNG and IL10 further underscoring the production of IFN-γ and IL-10 by these patients, and the decrease in CLEC4C verifying a decrease in pDCs.

Since the patients’ cells showed normal expression of the mutant IRF8 protein (Figure 1C), we examined the potential effect of the mutant protein’s C-terminal elongation on IRF8 function. At steady state, IRF8 resides in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus. Its expression and nuclear localization are enhanced in response to microbial products and/or to IFN-γ16, 44. As a first step, we assessed the patient mutant (Mut) IRF8 protein’s nuclear localization. Wild-type (WT) and Mut IRF8 cDNA were isolated from healthy donor or patient T cells respectively, and prepared in mammalian expression plasmids. The WT and Mut IRF8 tagged with FLAG-YFP were separately overexpressed in 293T cells to examine their localization (Figure 6A–6C). The Mut IRF8 showed a slight increase in molecular size (Figure 6A), consistent with the mutant IRF8 detected in the patient PBMCs (Figure 1C). In cells expressing WT YFP-IRF8, the majority of YFP-IRF8 was detected within the nucleus relative to the outer cytoplasmic region (Figure 6B and 6C). By contrast, Mut IRF8 was evenly distributed between the nucleus and cytoplasm, to a similar degree as YFP-expression within a cell, indicating that C-terminal elongation impairs Mut IRF8’s normal nuclear localization.

To further validate that the 42 amino acid extension at the C-terminal end of Mut IRF8 (designated as ‘tail’ here) is responsible for its impaired nuclear localization, we examined whether presence of the ‘tail’ is sufficient to affect the nuclear localization of IRF4, an IRF family member most closely related to IRF845. 293T cells were transfected with either YFP-tagged WT IRF4 or YFP-tagged IRF4 containing the Mut IRF8 tail (Figure 6D). Whereas the majority of WT YFP-IRF4 was detected within the nucleus relative to cytoplasmic region, presence of the 42 amino acid ‘tail’ impaired nuclear localization of IRF4 like Mut IRF8 (Figure 6E and 6F), giving further credence to the notion that the C-terminal elongation present in the Mut IRF8 protein impairs normal IRF8 nuclear localization.

IRF8 family proteins exert their transcriptional activities by forming homo- or hetero-dimers with other IRF family members and/or transcription factors with their IADs1. On this basis, we hypothesized that the mutant IRF8 might also impair nuclear localization of WT IRF8 in a dominant-negative manner. As a precedent, in patients with the pathogenic variant p.T80A, the mutation was found to be dominant-negative, as it suppressed the transactivation potential of WT IRF823. To test this hypothesis, we co-expressed WT or the Mut IRF8 tagged with YFP or mCherry into the 293T cells and examined their intracellular localization (Figure 7A and 7B). Distribution of YFP-IRF8 and mCherry-IRF8 in ‘WT+WT’ or ‘Mut+Mut’ combinations showed mostly nuclear distribution of the former and evenly dispersed distribution of the latter, matching our previous experiment (Figure 6B, 6C, 7A and7B). Importantly, when Mut IRF8 was co-expressed with WT, Mut IRF8 also interfered with the normal distribution of WT IRF8, irrespective of the tag/fluorescence conditions (‘Mut+WT’ or ‘WT+Mut’). These results show that mutant IRF8 impairs the nuclear localization of WT IRF8 in a dominant-negative manner. Furthermore, we examined endogenous IRF8 distribution within healthy donor and patient cells (Figure 7C and7D). Most IRF8+ populations within the healthy donor PBMCs showed clear nuclear distribution of IRF8. By contrast, the endogenous IRF8 within the patient IRF8+ PBMCs more often showed homogeneous distribution throughout the cells, giving further substantiating our previous observations (Figure 6, 7A and 7B).

IRF8 heterodimerizes with IRF1, and based on the bound DNA consensus sequence(s), either activates23 or represses25, 27 IRF1-driven transcriptional activity. Therefore, we also tested the ability of Mut IRF8 to repress (TAPBP promoter regions) or activate (IL12B promoter regions) IRF1-mediated transcription. In concordance with previous reports23, 25, 27, in our experiments, WT IRF8 repressed IRF1-mediated transcription on the TAPBP promoter and activated it on the IL12B promoter, whereas the autosomal recessive loss-of-function IRF8 mutants (K108E and R291Q) failed to do either (Figure 7E–7G). By contrast, Mut IRF8 completely impaired IRF1-mediated transcription regardless of the target promoter region. Similarly, the previously reported dominant-negative T80A IRF8 mutant also inhibited IRF1 activity at both promoter regions.

Since the Mut IRF8 impairs nuclear localization of WT IRF8 in a dominant-negative manner (Figure 7A–7D), we examined whether it could also similarly affect the nuclear localization of IRF1 within cells. To do so, we co-expressed HA-tagged IRF1 with either YFP or YFP-tagged IRF8 (WT or mutant, including p.K108E, p.R291Q, p.T80A, and our patients’ Mut IRF8), and examined the relative distribution of IRF8 and IRF1 within the nucleus and cytoplasm (Figure E2). The different IRF8 mutants affected IRF8 localization differently: our patients’ Mut IRF8 and the K108E IRF8 mutant impaired nuclear localization as expected (Figure 6B and 6C), whereas R291Q IRF8 showed normal nuclear localization akin to WT IRF8 (Figure E2, A and B). On the other hand, the dominant-negative T80A IRF8 mutant showed strong nuclear localization and often existed as nuclear puncta. As for IRF1 localization, exogenously expressed IRF1 showed strong nuclear presence, and there was no striking change in the ‘nucleus/cytoplasm ratio’ among experimental groups (Figure E2, A and C). However, we observed a statistically significant decrease in the nuclear proportion of IRF1 in cells co-expressing the Mut IRF8 (Figure E2, A and C). Therefore, we could conclude that impaired nuclear localization of IRF1 by Mut IRF8 is at least part of the mechanism responsible for the loss of IRF1-driven transcriptional activity in the presence of this IRF8 mutant. Collectively, our results suggest that the Mut IRF8 is dominant-negative and impairs IRF1-driven transcriptional activities.

The current work has focused on a novel variant in IRF8. In silico functional predictions by two separate groups have led to different conjectures about this variant. The Invitae laboratory, which has mainly relied on Sherloc variant classification framework46 for its analysis, has classified it as a variant of unknown significance, whereas whole-genome sequencing and analysis of a sporadic primary immunodeficiency cohort47 predicted it to be pathogenic: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/583095/, last accessed on 21 November 2024. Using a multi-disciplinary approach, we provide ample evidence in support of an autosomal dominant pathogenic role for this variant. The immunophenotype of our patients has important similarities and differences with the previously published cases in the literature (see Table 1A). The published autosomal recessive cases have all displayed substantial neutrophilia and severe monocytopenia23–25, 27, while patients with the reported autosomal dominant p.T80A variant have normal neutrophil and monocyte counts. Our patients have had mild neutrophilia in most of their assessments over a decade of monitoring. Unlike the biallelic cases, who lack monocytes or have severe monocytopenia, our patients display a low grade monocytosis over the period of observation. Their monocytes show the expected STAT1 phosphorylation in response to IFN-α and IFN-γ, and engaging their TLR1/2, TLR2/6, TLR4, TLR5 and TLR7/8 receptors leads to normal production of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-12 by these cells. Like the biallelic cases, our patients show a significant decrease in pDC and cDC1 dendritic cells, whereas the patients with the p.T80A variant only have a decrease in cDC2 dendritic cells. The existing pDC show an impairment in IFN-α production after engagement by either TLR 7/8 or TLR9 agonists. By contrast, their mDC produce normal levels of IL-12 in response to TLR 7/8 engagement. While defective NK cell development and function have been documented in a subset of patients with biallelic IRF8 mutations28, our patients show an essentially normal NK cell phenotype and NK cytolytic activity; the lower NK cytotoxicity in the proband in comparison to his mother could be due to normal person to person variation and/or due to a factor unrelated to their shared IRF8 variant. We did not detect a distinctive shared abnormality in either B or T phenotype or function in the two patients.

The RNAseq and proteomic data bolster our phenotypic and functional findings. The following are among the most notable instances:

The observed decrease in the patients’ pDC numbers corresponds with a decrease in LAMP5, DNASE1L3 and GATA2 in the RNA-seq analysis, and of CLEC4C (CD303 or BDCA-2) at both the transcript and protein levels, whereas the impairment in TLR7- and TLR9-mediated IFN-α production in the patients’ existing pDCs corresponds with decreased SPIB expression, underscoring the reduced expression of multiple factors which play distinct roles in the development and/or function of pDCs48–50.

Our patients show a clear distinction with the previously reported cases in respect of cytokine production. The published cases of human IRF8 deficiency point to the complete absence of IL-12 production and reduced levels of IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-10 and TNF-α23–25, 27. By contrast, our patients’ monocytes show normal production of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-12 after TLR receptor engagement, their mDCs produce normal levels of IL-12 in response to R848, and their CD8+ T cells produce normal levels of IFN-γ after stimulation with PMA/ionomycin. Pathway enrichment analysis of the RNA-seq data shows an increase in cytokine signaling which includes IL1B, IFNG and IL10. Additionally, correlative analysis of the RNA-seq and Olink proteomic data shows an increase in IFN-γ and IL-10 in both sets of data, giving further credence to the distinction between the reported IRF8 mutations and our patients concerning cytokine production. While this contrast is at least in part due to the absence of myeloid mononuclear cells in the autosomal recessive cases23–25, 27, it might also reflect a difference in the level of IRF8 activity between those cases23–25, 27 and our patients.

The delineated biological effects of this mutation provide certain tangible therapeutic implications: The mother’s lack of response to imiquimod, an imidazoquinoline approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treating anogenital warts, superficial basal cell carcinomas and actinic keratoses, is a case in point. The compound can induce pDC to produce IFN-α by engaging the TLR7 receptor and activating a MyD88-dependent signaling pathway51, 52. The patient’s decreased pDC numbers, mirrored by the decrease in CLEC4C, LAMP5 and DNASE1L3 transcripts; the decrease in the production of IFN-α in response to R848, an imiquimod analogue, by her existing pDCs, which corresponds with the decrease in the SPIB transcript; collectively provide a compelling rationale for her lack of response to imiquimod. They also cast doubt on the efficacy and utility of any potential vaccine using TLR-7 agonists as adjuvants53, 54 in our patients.

Our findings also provide new avenues of enquiry for the future. The contrast between the reported impairment in cytokine production in the biallelic cases with our patients would require further investigation, as does the direction of change in DUSP4, a dual specificity phosphatase55, which incidentally is increased in older individuals and can decrease T cell-mediated B cell help56. The changes observed in expression of numerous members of the transmembrane (TMEM) family of proteins in the RNA-seq analysis, and of proteins interacting with TMEM family members using the Olink proteomic approach is another notable example. While the physiological function of many members of this family of proteins remains unknown, their documented role in cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, modulation of the immune response, metastasis and response to antineoplastic drugs through engaging different signaling pathways43, provides a strong impetus for investigating their contribution to the biology and clinical manifestations of IRF8 mutations.

It is important to emphasize the differences between the mode of transmission and biological effects of this mutation with the ones described so far. The biallelic cases of IRF8 deficiency include a patient homozygous for the missense variant p.K108E23, 24; another with the compound heterozygous variants p.R83C and p.R291Q25; a third one with the homozygous truncating variant p.R111*26; and a fourth one with the compound heterozygous variants c.55del and p.R111*27. In vitro assessments have categorized all these variants as loss of function, which is consistent with autosomal recessive IRF8 deficiency23, 24, 25, 27. By contrast, the reported de novo p.T80A variant acts in a dominant-negative manner23. Our patients display a novel variant that has first emerged de novo in the mother. Unlike the variants cited above, the one reported here is a loss-of-stop (nostop, readthrough, or nonstop) DNA mutation where the loss of the stop codon leads to the extension of the IRF8 protein by an estimated 42 amino acids. In principle, this type of mutation can affect both the encoded RNA and protein in multiple manners, leading to a variety of outcomes: Extension beyond the stop-codon often leads to nonstop RNA decay, but the ribosomal read-through may also interfere with the RNA stability elements localized in the 3’ UTR of the mRNA leading to diminished transcript levels. Loss-of-stop mutations can also cause protein degradation/destabilization, or alter protein subcellular localization, change the multimerization or other protein interactions resulting in a gain- or loss-function57–62. There are reported examples of translational readthrough where the protein extended beyond the canonical stop codon has a modified function63–65. In our patients, we have documented that the mutated gene has a transcript with an allele frequency of 0.432 and 0.405 in the mother and son respectively (Figure 1B), is expressed as a protein (Figure 1C), has reduced nuclear localization, and interferes with the wild-type copy’s nuclear localization (Figure 6 and Figure 7A–7D), thereby exerting a dominant-negative effect. It is not yet fully clear how the 42 amino acid extension in the mutant protein mediates these effects. In principle, either the elongation at the C-terminal end itself or the presence of a specific ‘element’ within the extension could be responsible for its effect on the patients’ IRF8 function. So far, neither conventional modeling nor AlphaFold66 has provided actionable clues in this respect (data not shown).To confirm that the C-terminal elongation of the mutant IRF8 is responsible for the impaired IRF8 localization and function, we adopted the following approaches: First we showed that adding the 42 amino acid extension to WT IRF4 would impair nuclear localization of IRF4 like that of Mut IRF8 (Figure 6D– 6F). Secondly, we showed that while WT IRF8 represses IRF1-mediated transcription on the TAPBP promoter and activates it on the IL12B promoter, our patients’ Mut IRF8, as well as the p.T80A mutant, completely impair IRF1-mediated transcription regardless of the target promoter region (Figure 7E–7G). The similar effect of our patients’ Mut IRF8 and the p.T80A mutant on IRF1-mediated transcription should not be misconstrued as an identical underlying mechanism of action for these two variants. Conceptually, it is unlikely that a mutation in the DNA binding domain (p.T80A) and a loss-of-stop mutation (our patients’ Mut IRF8) would affect the protein’s function in the same way. Experimentally, here we have shown that while our patients’ Mut IRF8 has impaired nuclear localization, the p.T80A mutant, by contrast, has strong nuclear localization (Figure E2). In this respect, it is worth emphasizing that the strong nuclear localization observed in the p.T80A mutant has also been described in the p.T95R IRF4 autosomal dominant variant67, a mutation in the DNA binding domain of an IRF protein with close homology to IRF8. Further progress in defining the mechanism of action of our patients’ IRF8 mutation, and in therapeutic targeting of IRFs45, 68, may conceivably provide the biological basis for effective precision-based interventions in the future.

The parallel assessment of our patients, who by nature share half of their genomes, has bestowed crucial advantages to this study. It has allowed us to identify the core phenotypic effects of the mutation versus those that may or may not manifest in an affected individual; has enabled us to, in aggregate, investigate the course of the illness over an extended time span; and has provided the chance to document phenotypic disparities that have plausible environmental explanations:

The significant decrease in pDCs and their impaired production of IFN-α, the decrease in cDC1 cells, a modest monocytosis, and the propensity to persistent infection with a particular virus and its sequelae comprise the core effects of this pathogenic variant and are seen in both individuals. In our opinion, the persistent EBV viremia in the son and the HPV-driven tumor in the mother justify adding this IRF8 variant to the list of disorders that incur genetic susceptibility to EBV infection69, 70 and to HPV-driven disease71. By contrast, a decrease in switched memory B cells, that has been documented in the biallelic cases, can only be seen in one of the two patients.

Certain immunophenotypic disparities between the mother and son, who live in the same household, could be due to the difference in the type of viral infection they have been afflicted with: The son, who has had a persistent long-term EBV viremia has shown a progressive loss of CD28 expression on his CD8+ T cells, characterized as TEMRA cells, with a substantial fraction being HLA-DR+. In addition, he has a less diverse TCRVβ repertoire. By contrast, the mother, whose major virus-related manifestation has been an HPV+ tumor, does not display either of these phenotypes. Additionally, while numerous reports have documented an increase in CD21low/T-Bet+ B cells in the context of viremia (see Sadighi Akha et al.72 for a detailed review), we did not observe this finding in the son (data not shown). This could plausibly be due to the role of IRF8 in the development of this B cell subset73.

The current work has chronicled the clinical manifestations of a novel IRF8 variant over an extended period of observation, confirmed its pathogenic nature by providing a detailed description of its phenotypic and functional effects through a multi-disciplinary approach, and specified the dominant-negative mechanism of its action. It has also discussed the tangible therapeutic implications of these findings and proposed important future lines of enquiry. It should be emphasized that delineating a definitive phenotypic and biological picture of this variant would require the discovery and description of additional patients affected by it. The winding road to accomplishing this goal for patients with TYK274–76 and RAG77 mutations are prime examples for underscoring this necessity.
