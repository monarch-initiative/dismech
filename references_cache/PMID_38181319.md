BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Sarcoidosis is a multisystem inflammatory
granulomatous disease. Among systemic sarcoidosis manifestations, cardiac or
nervous system involvement can result in significant morbidity and mortality. We
describe the overlapping incidence of cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) within a
neurosarcoidosis (NS) cohort and determine the frequency of other nonsarcoid
cardiac diseases in these patients.
METHODS: We performed a retrospective chart review of patients evaluated at the
University of Utah from 2010 to 2022. Patients were included if they had (1) at
least one instance of a diagnostic code for sarcoidosis in their medical
record-International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9 code 135 or ICD 10 code
D86; (2) at least one outpatient visit in the Neurology Department within the
University of Utah electronic health record with a diagnosis of definite,
probable, or possible NS based on 2018 consensus criteria; (3) at least one
outpatient visit in the Cardiology Department within the University of Utah
electronic health record; and (4) ECG available in their medical record for
review. Of 64 definite, probable, or possible patients with NS in the University
of Utah cohort, 52 met our inclusion criteria and were included in this study.
RESULTS: Of 52 patients with NS who met our inclusion criteria, 65.38% were
female, with an average age of 60.9 years (range 38-84). More than half (58%)
were obese (BMI ≥ 30). CS was diagnosed in 6 patients with NS (12%). Symptoms
suggestive of possible cardiac dysfunction included lower extremity edema (50%),
palpitations (46%), chest pain (44%), and shortness of breath (27%). ECG
abnormalities included nonspecific T-wave change (40%) and right bundle branch
block (17%). Three patients experienced ventricular tachycardia: sustained in
one patient and nonsustained in 2 patients. Cardiac MRI was performed in 17
patients (32.7%) and in 3 patients (17.6%), which revealed diffuse myocardial
enhancement suggesting CS.
DISCUSSION: In this cohort, 12% of patients with NS also had confirmed CS. In
addition, these patients had a high burden of cardiovascular disease not
directly attributed to sarcoidosis. Our data suggest that patients with NS
require comprehensive cardiac evaluation. Future studies are needed to clarify
the extent of the direct contribution of granulomatous inflammation on the
cardiovascular system from the indirect contribution of treatments such as
glucocorticoids that lead to increased risk of cardiovascular disease in
sarcoidosis.

Sarcoidosis is an immune-mediated inflammatory disease that often insidiously affects multiple organs, resulting in variable clinical symptoms and outcomes.1 The diagnosis of sarcoidosis is supported by detecting noncaseating granulomas on tissue biopsy. The incidence of sarcoidosis in the United States is 8–11 per 100,000.2 While pulmonary involvement is most frequent, cardiac and CNS involvement occurs in 20%–30% and 5%–15% of patients with sarcoidosis.3-5 Symptoms in CS vary from clinically silent CS to a range of manifestations, including heart block, ventricular arrhythmias, and heart failure.6 Ventricular arrhythmias and severe left ventricular (LV) dysfunction are associated with poor prognosis and necessitate management by experienced multidisciplinary teams to improve patient outcomes and minimize morbidity and mortality.3,7,8 Previous studies show that early CS diagnosis with relatively preserved cardiac function is associated with better outcomes with stabilization of cardiac function with appropriate treatment. By contrast, delayed diagnosis with moderately to severely decreased left ventricular function at presentation is associated with worse response to immunosuppressive therapy and higher morbidity and mortality.3

Patients with NS have a higher frequency of cardiac involvement than those without neurologic involvement (11.6% cardiac involvement in NS vs 2.8% overall; p < 0.001).9 Thus, in accordance with American Thoracic Society guidelines for all patients with extracardiac sarcoidosis, workup aimed at ruling our CS should be considered in all patients with NS, starting with obtaining a history and physical examination directed at detecting signs or symptoms of heart disease and ECG.5,10,11 In patients with suspicion of CS, the diagnosis usually requires additional investigation with dedicated cardiac imaging, such as echocardiography (ECHO), fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) with PET, and/or cardiac MRI (cMRI) with late gadolinium enhancement.12,13 These observations of the frequency and severity of CS overall and within the NS subset of patients with sarcoidosis underscore the importance of early detection of CS through screening and, when appropriate, timely cardiac resynchronization therapy device placement. In this study, we investigate the frequency of cardiac screening, cardiac symptoms, and the overall CS diagnostic process in a cohort of patients with NS at a tertiary referral center.

We performed a retrospective review of patients evaluated at the University of Utah from 2010 to 2022. Patients were included if they had (1) at least one instance of a diagnostic code for sarcoidosis in their medical record—International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9 code 135 or ICD 10 code D86; (2) at least one outpatient visit in the Neurology Department within the University of Utah electronic health record with a diagnosis of definite, probable, or possible NS based on 2018 consensus criteria; (3) at least one outpatient visit in the Cardiology Department within the University of Utah electronic health record; and (4) an ECG available in their medical record for review (Figure).14 Demographics, medical history, laboratory data, ECG, and imaging results were reviewed for each patient meeting our inclusion criteria. cMRI and PET scan results were collected from the final reports provided by the interpreting radiologist of record; ECG and ECHO results were likewise collected from the interpreting cardiologist's final, signed reports. CS was evaluated before, during, or after NS diagnostic workup.

CS diagnosis was typically determined by either histologic/biopsy confirmation in cardiac tissue or a supportive extracardiac biopsy for sarcoidosis and evidence of cardiac noninvasive diagnostic findings consistent with sarcoidosis: nonischemic cardiomyopathy not otherwise explained, heart block, cardiac PET, or cMRI with findings consistent with CS.15

The institutional review board approved the study at the University of Utah under IRB 00108537.

This is a descriptive study.

All reasonable requests for deidentified participant data will be made available on request.

Sixty-four patients met the diagnostic criteria for definite, probable, or possible NS. Of those, 52 patients had a cardiology visit and an ECG available for review and were thus included in the final cohort for analysis. Eleven (21%) of them had definite NS, 35 (67%) had probable NS, and 6 (12%) had possible NS. Table 1 summarizes the patient demographics.

Patient Characteristics and Symptoms

Thirty-four patients (65%) were women, and most patients (83%) were Caucasian. The mean age was 61 years (SD = 10.96, range 38–84, no pediatric patients). The median follow-up for these patients was 7 years. Risk factors identified for cardiac or vascular disease included diabetes (29%), dyslipidemia (38%), hypertension (42%), and smoking (5% current smoker and 21% former smoker). The mean BMI was 32 (SD = 9.55, range 14.87–59.8), and 58% had a BMI > 30. Lower extremity edema (50%) was the most common cardiac manifestation, followed by palpitations (46%), chest pain (44%), and shortness of breath (SOB) (43%). Five patients (10%) experienced syncope or presyncope. Ten patients (19%) had a history of coronary artery disease. The most common ECG findings were nonspecific T-wave change (40%), right bundle branch block (RBBB) (17%), atrioventricular node (AV) block (16%), and QT prolongation (12%). Two patients experienced sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) and one nonsustained VT (NSVT) (Table 2).

Six patients (12%) met the criteria for CS. Of the 6 patients with confirmed CS, 3 were initially diagnosed with NS an average of 4.7 years before diagnosis of CS, and 3 were diagnosed with CS and later developed NS symptoms at an average of 4.3 years later (Table 3). On initial assessment, 3 of 6 patients with CS underwent both cardiac PET and cMRI; both studies were normal in 2 patients, and one patient had a normal cardiac PET but cMRI concerning for CS. On follow-up imaging, only 1 patient with CS underwent both cardiac PET and cMRI; both of these studies were abnormal in this patient. In addition to 1 patient who could not tolerate any immunosuppressant treatment, 3 patients were diagnosed with CS/NS while on medication while the other 2 patients were diagnosed with CS/NS during the period when they had been weaned off of medication.

Whole-body PET.

cMRI was performed in 17 patients (33%), with 3 of these (17%) having findings supportive of CS. A myocardial PET scan was performed on 9 patients with NS, confirming 3 known CS and identifying one additional patient with CS. One patient had cardiac biopsy supporting CS diagnosis (Case 6, Table 3). Six patients required implantable cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) defibrillator (CRT-D) placement, and one patient required a CRT pacemaker (CRT-P). One additional patient was diagnosed with ischemic cardiomyopathy unrelated to sarcoidosis.

In this single-institution review of our NS experience, we identified a high cardiovascular disease burden in patients with NS. In our NS cohort, we observed a prevalence of CS at 12%, a figure in close alignment with findings reported in previous research.8 A subset of patients had CS diagnosis at the time NS was diagnosed while others were diagnosed with CS after their NS diagnosis. The proportion of White patients in our cohort was higher than what is typically seen in patients with sarcoidosis. This is likely a result of the fact that the non-White population in Utah is relatively small (9.7% in Utah compared with 24.2% in the United States).16

The most common cardiac features which led to a cardiac evaluation in patients with NS were lower extremity edema, palpitations, chest pain, and SOB. Previous studies of cardiac screening in patients with extrapulmonary sarcoidosis showed the most common symptom to be palpitations.17 Although ECG changes are not specific in asymptomatic CS, nonspecific ST T-wave change followed by RBBB were our cohort's most common ECG changes, followed by AV or infranodal conduction block, which is similar to existing literature.15,17 ECHO is often normal in early CS. Abnormalities seen in more advanced disease are increased myocardial wall thickness and LV dysfunction, sometimes with wall motion abnormalities.15,17,18

CS may be initially clinically silent, depending on the location and extent of myocardial inflammation and also perhaps because of misattribution of symptoms to other etiologies in patients with sarcoidosis (e.g., dyspnea attributed to pulmonary sarcoid and not queried for cardiac etiology). However, the clinical manifestations of advanced sarcoidosis include arrhythmias and heart failure, as seen in our 6 patients with CS.19 In retrospect, the 3 patients with CS who were first diagnosed with NS had an initial ECG with branch block but, on further imaging evaluation with cMRI or PET scan, were not initially diagnosed with CS. Later in their course, however, they developed heart failure symptoms, followed by myocardial involvement on cMRI/PET scan, ultimately confirming CS at an average of 4.7 years after initial NS diagnosis. Similarly, for the patients who were first diagnosed as CS and then later NS, the NS was diagnosed at an average of 4.3 years after the initial CS diagnosis. For comparison, in the published literature, PET has a sensitivity ranging from 85% to 100% and specificity between 38.5% and 90.9% while cMRI has sensitivity rates between 75% and 100% along with specificity levels ranging from 76.9% to 78% in detecting cardiac sarcoidosis; the combined use of cMRI and PET can provide a comprehensive evaluation and better recognition of cardiac involvement.20,21

An increased risk of obesity and cardiovascular disease in sarcoidosis and other autoimmune disorders is supported by the literature.22-25 In our cohort, more than half (58%) of the patients were obese (compared with 41.9% for the general population in the United States and 30.9% for Utah), and 10 patients (19%) had evidence of coronary artery disease.26 A likely contributor to metabolic syndrome in sarcoidosis is exposure to glucocorticoid therapy. Glucocorticoids are the first-line therapy for sarcoidosis, specifically NS, where doses are often high and prolonged. Chronic treatment with glucocorticoids is associated with weight gain, dyslipidemia, and hyperglycemia, which in addition to overexpression of proteins involved in high-density lipoprotein and fatty acid metabolism in patients with sarcoidosis enhances the risk of atherosclerotic events.2,27,28 Another risk factor for obesity and related metabolic syndrome is reduced mobility, which may affect patients with cardiovascular or nervous system sarcoidosis complications, including reduced exercise capacity, weakness, imbalance, and/or vision changes. To decrease the burden of long-term glucocorticoid exposure in these patients, limiting glucocorticoid exposure when possible should be a focus of study and the goal of all treatment strategies.28

This study is limited by its retrospective design within a single-institution cohort. Whether the findings can be generalized to other more ethnically diverse populations requires further study in diverse cohorts.

Twelve percent of patients with NS in our cohort developed CS, and cardiovascular disease not directly attributed to CS was also very prevalent. These data indicate that close cardiac follow-up in patients with NS is warranted to identify CS that may develop after the diagnosis of NS and to manage cardiovascular risk stemming from treatment adverse effects and comorbidities. The observed prevalence of comorbid cardiovascular conditions in this cohort highlights the need for neurologists to screen for possible comorbid cardiac conditions and to collaborate closely with primary care and cardiology to ensure comprehensive care for patients with NS. Future studies are needed to disambiguate the direct contribution of granulomatous inflammation from the indirect contribution of comorbidities such as hyperlipidemia and obesity and the effects of prolonged use of glucocorticoids on cardiac disease in patients with sarcoidosis.
