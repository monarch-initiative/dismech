Abstract
Background To evaluate the prevalence and characteristics of taurodontism in northern China by using cone-beam 
computed tomography (CBCT) and assisting the treatment.
Methods The study involved CBCT scans of 8112 teeth from 507 participants of northern China, comprising 217 
males and 290 females aged 18 to 60. Analysis was conducted using Shifman and Chanannel’s criteria to assess the 
prevalence and attributes of taurodontism, examining differences based on tooth position (maxilla and mandible) as 
well as gender (P < 0.05). Specific morphology including C-shaped canal was recorded. The curvature of the canals 
was measured using a modified Schneider method. Moreover, we reported two failure cases with taurodontism 
referred to a retreatment.
Results Taurodontism was observed in 113 participants, affecting 23.50% of the males and 21.38% of the females 
in at least one tooth (P > 0.05). The prevalence was significant higher in maxilla (P < 0.05). The highest incidence of 
taurodontism was detected in premolars, 9.86% in the maxillary first premolars, and in molars, with 3.94% in the 
maxillary first molars. Regarding canal curvature, a higher incidence of curved canal could be found in maxillary and 
mandibular molars of taurodontism (47.50-66.67%), alongside an elevated prevalence of C-shaped taurodontism in 
mandibular second molars (71.43%). After follow-up, the healing response was satisfactory in both cases.
Conclusion The study highlighted a higher incidence of taurodontism in maxilla, indicating a significant association 
between taurodontism, C-shaped characteristics, and canal curvature. Dentists are advised to carefully consider the 
presence of special morphologies during taurodontism treatment.

Background
In 1913, Keith [ 1] initially introduced the term “Tau -
rodontism” to describe the tooth with enlarged pulp 
chambers and abbreviated roots, similar of those found 
in ungulate animals or mammals. In 1978, Shifman and 
Chanannel provided a method for diagnosing taur -
odontism [ 2]. The precise mechanism of taurodontism 
remains unclear. Current research suggests that it may 
be related to alterations in the Hertwig’s epithelial root 
sheath [3, 4] and genetic factors [ 5, 6]. The prevalence of 
taurodontism varies among diverse ethnic groups, rang -
ing from less than 0.10–48.00% [ 7–10]. Neanderthals 
were reported as high-risk populations [ 11]. Studies 
have focused mostly on populations such as Eskimos and 
Native Central Americans [5, 9].
For taurodontism, the variations of the tooth anat -
omy present major challenges to thorough debride -
ment and fine obturation [ 12–14]. For example, during 
pulpal exploration, the deep pulp chamber floor in tau -
rodontism leads to rich bleeding and close canal orifices, 
making canal localization difficult, which can result in 
perforations. The above could explain the occurrence of 
numerous complications following the root canal treat -
ment of taurodontism [ 15]. Despite encountering addi -
tional challenges during treatment, the overall attention 
on taurodontism remains relatively limited. Studies on 
the Chinese population are scarce, with earlier studies 
mostly depending on panoramic radiographs rather than 
cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) [16]. A recent 
study focused on the northwestern region of China but 
reported limited information, only prevalence of taur -
odontism [17].
Considering the assistant in treatment, the recognition 
of canal morphology in taurodontism is pivotal, however 
limited research were performed. For instance, a study in 
2020 observed a high association between taurodontism 
and C-shaped root canals in Turkey [ 18]. Moreover, in 
daily practice, dentists often find severely curved or spe -
cial morphology of root canals in taurodontism. How -
ever, the correlation between specific canal morphology 
and taurodontism has not been well announced.
Therefore, in this study, we performed a statisti -
cal analysis of the maximum curvature of different root 
canals in premolars and molars of taurodontism. Besides 
the announcement of prevalence across different gen -
ders or dental positions, we aimed to explore the asso -
ciation between taurodontism and specific root canal 
morphology.
Methods
Collection of CBCT
This retrospective study was approved by Institutional 
Review Board of Beijing Stomatological Hospital, Capi -
tal Medical University (CMUSH-IRB-KJ-PJ-2024-08). 
Images of total of 507 participants aged 18–60 years who 
applied to the hospital between 2021 and 2023 were eval -
uated. All CBCT images were taken by the same machine 
(New Tom VGi, 110 kV, 4.33 mA, 10 × 10 mm, 3.6 s) for 
various reasons, including treatment planning in cases 
of implantation, orthodontic therapy or trauma, manu -
factured by experienced oral radiologists. Measured by 
NewTom’s 3D imaging software to assess CBCT images 
in the horizontal, coronal, and sagittal planes. CBCT 
images of poor quality (distorted or over/underexposed 
images) were excluded. In addition, participants with an 
inadequate number of teeth (more than one premolar 
and molar missed), fractured teeth, third molars, teeth 
that had crowns, caries, restorations or other dental 
treatment that influenced the diagnosis of taurodontism, 
teeth with incomplete apical foramen formation and 
teeth with undetectable furcation were excluded.
Analysis of the prevalence and classification of 
taurodontism
According to “Shifman and Chanannel” standard (Fig.  1), 
taurodontism can be diagnosed when the taurodontic 
index (TI) exceeded 20 and the vertical distance from the 
Cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) to the highest point at 
the bottom of the pulp chamber |BD| exceeded 2.5 mm. 
TI = |AB|/|AC| × 100, where |AB| is the vertical distance 
between the lowest point at the top of the pulp chamber 
and the highest point at the bottom of the pulp chamber, 
and |AC| is the perpendicular distance between the low -
est point at the top of the pulp chamber and the apex of 
the longest root. According to the value of the TI, tau -
rodontism can be divided into three different degrees:

20 < TI < 30 indicates hypotaurodontism, 30 ≤ TI < 40 
indicates mesotaurodontism, and 40 ≤ TI indicates hyper-
taurodontism [ 19]. A written step-by-step analysis as 
following, 1 Adjust the vertical axis to be parallel to the 
long axis of teeth beneath the sagittal and coronal planes. 
2 Determination of measurement points: (1) The low -
est point of the chamber is where the first high-density 
image disappears in the chamber; (2) The highest point 
of the chamber floor is where the high-density image is 
about to reappear in the chamber; (3) The apex of the 
longest tooth root is where the first disappearance of the 
high-density image at the apex of the longest tooth root 
occurs; (4) The CEJ is where the high-density enamel dis -
appears around the tooth. 3 Measurement of the vertical 
distance of the marked points mentioned above.
Analysis of the root canal morphology of taurodontism
The number of root canal and the specific morphol -
ogy such as C-shaped was recorded. And according to 
“ AAE Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment form and 
Guidelines” root canal curvature can be divided into 
three degrees: grade I, slight or no curvature < 10°; grade 
II, moderate curvature10°-30°; grade III, severe curva -
ture > 30°or S-shaped [ 20]. A modified Schneider [ 2, 
21–23]method was used to measure root canal curva -
ture (Fig. 2), a written step-by-step analysis as following, 
1 A 3D reconstruction software of the CBCT image was 
used to set the root canal orifice as point A and the apical 
foramen as point C. 2 A straight line was drawn along the 
root canal image from point A, and the inflection point 
was set as point B. 3 The angle of line AB and line BC was 
measured and recorded by the same examiner 3 times 
and took the average of three measurements (Note: the 
molars with only one buccal (B) canal is accounted for as 
the mesiobuccal (MB). In premolars which have both the 
MB and distalbuccal (DB) canals, calculated the curva -
ture of the larger canals.).
Statistical analysis
The position and number of taurodontism were recorded, 
the incidence rate was calculated, the classification was 
determined based on TI, and the curvature of root canal 
was accounted. Statistical analysis was performed using 
SPSS22.0. For quantitative data, we checked if the data 
followed a normal distribution. If the data was not nor -
mally distributed, we used the Kruskal-Wallis test; if 
normally distributed, we employed one-way analysis of 
variance (for more than two groups) or the t-test (for two 
groups). For categorical data, we used the chi-square test. 
The significance level was set at 0.05.
Results
Within this study, there were 217 male individuals and 
290 female participants, aged between 18 and 60 years. 
Representative images of the CBCT examinations could 
be seen in Fig.  3. For the 507 participants, 113 indi -
viduals (22.29%) were diagnosed with taurodontism. 
Furthermore, the prevalence of taurodontism among 
507 participants categorized by gender. Specifically, 
taurodontism was identified in 51 of the 217 male par -
ticipants (23.50%) and 62 of the 290 female partici -
pants (21.38%). However, no significant difference was 
observed between the genders (P > 0.05).
Table  1 reported the prevalence of taurodontism 
among 507 participants categorized by different posi -
tion and degree. Among 8112 teeth, 87 teeth (1.07%) 
were diagnosed with hypotaurodontism, 93 teeth (1.15%) 
were classified with mesotaurodontism, 36 teeth (0.44%) 
were characterized by hypertaurodontism. With respect 
to diverse tooth position, the occurrence of taurodontism 
in the maxilla markedly surpasses that in the mandible 
(P < 0.05), as no taurodontism could be found in man -
dibular premolars in this study and 0.20%, 1.38% in man -
dibular first and second molars, respectively. The overall 
prevalence of taurodontism in premolars and molars 
amounted to 2.66%.

Discussion
In 1928, Shaw [ 24]proposed to classify teeth with taur -
odontism into three categories. However, the diagnos -
tic criteria proposed at that time did not rely on specific 
quantitative measurements. In 1966, Keene [ 25] intro -
duced the first quantitative criteria, but the anatomical 
landmarks used were unstable and questionable. In 1971, 
the Blumberg [ 26] standard only described the charac -
teristics without clear grading. With the advancement of 
imaging technology, the diagnosis of taurodontism now 
mainly relies on quantitative measurement and analysis 
of distances between dental landmarks. Apart from the 
standards set forth by Shifman and Chanannel [ 19] in 
1978, some studies used the criteria proposed by Seow 
and Lai [ 27, 28] in 1989, which was based on the crown-
to-root ratio (CB/R). However, one of the selected land -
marks in this standard is the lowest point on the occlusal 
surface. With aging, the position of this point may change 
due to physiological or pathological erosion, leading to 
unstable measurements. Research [ 29] has revealed sig -
nificant instability in measurements for individuals aged 
50 and above when applying Seow and Lai standards due 
to occurrences of pulp chamber calcification and erosion. 
Therefore, our study chose to employ the widely used 
Shifman and Chanannel standards. However, whether

In our research, statistical results revealed that in 
the northern regions of China, the incidence of taur -
odontism among the population stood at 22.29%, with a 
tooth-specific occurrence of 2.66%. Previous studies pri -
marily relied on panoramic radiographs to diagnose tau -
rodontism based on |BD| values. For instance, a study by 
MacDonald and Li in 1993 reported an individual prev -
alence of 46.4% and tooth prevalence of 21.7% in China 
[16]. The variations in results can be attributed to dif -
ferences in measurement methods. This study employed 
stricter diagnostic criteria and more precise CBCT scans, 
excluding third molars with significant variations and 
limited therapeutic value. In a 2023 study in the north -
west region of China using CBCT, employing the similar 
diagnostic strategy, reported that the prevalence of tau -
rodontism among individuals was 29.14%, with a tooth 
prevalence of 7.45% [ 30]. Of which was little higher than 
it in this study.
It is believed that the occurrence of taurodontism is 
closely related to the regulation of Hertwig’s epithe -
lial root sheath (HERS) development. Delay or failure 
of HERS to invaginate into the mesenchyme can lead 
to root bifurcation migrating towards the root, subse -
quently resulting in taurodontism. Current research sug -
gests that genetic factors play a role in occurrences of 
taurodontism. EDAR (the receptor), expressed during 
epithelial root sheath development, has been identified 
as a crucial factor. Mutations in EDAR have been shown 
to significantly increase the incidence of taurodontism 
in mice, and similarly, individuals with mutations in 
EDA-A1 exhibited a high prevalence of taurodontism 
[31]. Furthermore, studies have revealed that genetic 
diseases such as enamel defects caused by mutations in 
DLX3, tricho-dento-osseous syndrome (TDO), Down 
syndrome, ectodermal dysplasia, and types I, II, and III 
osteogenesis imperfecta can also be associated with 
widespread taurodontism [ 32–39]. Unfortunately, the 
key genes and pathways underlying have not yet been 
identified. Scholars currently view taurodontism as 
a polygenic hereditary disorder controlled by a small 
number of genes, primarily affecting the development 
of the epithelial root sheath and subsequently leading to 
taurodontism.

challenges. This research innovatively measured pulp 
chamber heights, offering essential guidance for end -
odontic treatments of taurodontism to prevent complica-
tions such as perforations. Our data especially suggests, 
more attention should be paid on if the negotiate distance 
of pulp chamber exceed 3.5 mm in maxilla and 3.0 mm 
in mandible to avoid perforation. Moreover, if the initial 
opening depth is less than 2.0  mm and the root canal

maximum curvature of each root canal in taurodontism, 
establishing a novel connection between taurodontism

of curvature (47.50-66.67%). The above making it prone 
to complications such as perforations and instrument 
separation. Additionally, mechanical preparation is less 
efficient, and manual irrigation is limited. During root 
canal obturation, seating the master cone is challenging, 
and three-dimensional filling is difficult.
Considering the above issues, along with the findings 
of our study, we propose the following recommenda -
tions: 1 Preoperative CBCT for three-dimensional analy -
sis; 2 Enhanced employment of dental microscope for 
examination; 3 Emphasized chemical preparation: using 
sodium hypochlorite in the pulp chamber to control 
bleeding and improve visibility; employing continuous 
chemical preparation to enhance efficiency. Therefore, 
early identification of taurodontism for caries prevention 
is vital, making X-ray examinations crucial for identify -
ing taurodontism and detecting early-stage caries [ 42]. 
Besides pit and fissure sealing and fluoride employment, 
special attention should also be given to the poten -
tial genetic or systemic diseases that may lead to taur -
odontism, implementing personalized oral preventive 
measures to protect the teeth with taurodontism.
Even though CBCT was used in our study, however, 
present research showed that CBCT still could not fully 
address the artifacts caused by dental restorative materi -
als such as crown or composite resin and low resolution 
[43, 44], which could significantly affect image quality, 
interfere with physicians’ analysis of anatomical struc -
tures, and lead to inaccurate diagnosis and measure -
ments. However, with the assistant of AI technology, 
artifacts elimination, segmentation and reconstruction 
of tooth anatomy could be achieved [ 45, 46]. Although 
there is currently little research on the application of AI 
in measurements specific to taurodontism, scholars have 
developed AI models for identifying caries and maxillo -
facial diseases [ 47, 48]. In the future, the development of 
AI algorithm for identifying taurodontism may help the 
early detection and diagnosis from patient imaging data, 
thereby enabling personalized oral disease prevention 
and treatment strategies.
Considering the high height of pulp chamber, the cur -
vature poses greater challenges for treatment. We offered 
valuable insights for current clinical research, analyzing 
taurodontism prevalence can elevate awareness among 
dentists during diagnosis and treatment, while also serv -
ing as a reference for future extensive surveys.
Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, this study was 
regionally limited and may not fully represent the prev -
alence of taurodontism across all of China. Second, the 
curvature analysis was performed by a modified Schnei -
der method, although used in lots of studies, not as good 
as real 3D measurement. We are taking effort to make a 
3D analysis platform and hope to employed in the future.
Conclusion
In sum, the prevalence of taurodontism has no signifi -
cant alteration in genders in northern China, but a higher 
incidence of taurodontism in maxilla and a close correla -
tion were revealed between taurodontism and C-shaped, 
as well as canal curvature were reported. Dentists should 
pay close attention about the presence of special canal 
morphology in taurodontism treatment. A preoperative 
CBCT and employment of dental microscope, sufficient 
chemical preparation could be suggested in root canal 
treatment of teeth with taurodontism.
