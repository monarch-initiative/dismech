# FHOD3 deficiency disrupts sarcomere organization and activates caMKII signaling in human stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes.
**Authors:** Wei M, Hou X, Zhang S, Hu X, Chen X, Gao Z, Xu S, Shi Z, Zhu M, Lan F, Cui M
**Journal:** Stem Cell Res Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13287-026-04902-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-04902-z)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Inherited cardiomyopathy (ICM) is a genetic disorder characterized by abnormal myocardial structure and function, often progressing to heart failure. FHOD3, a member of the Formin gene family, plays a crucial role in cardiomyocyte cytoskeletal organization. Mutations in FHOD3 have been associated with various cardiomyopathies, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and left ventricular noncompaction (LVNC). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying FHOD3 deficiency-induced cardiomyopathy remain elusive.

METHODS: A FHOD3 knockout (FHOD3-/-) human embryonic stem cell (hESC) line was generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system and subsequently differentiated into cardiomyocytes (hESC-CMs). Sarcomere structure, calcium handling, mitochondrial function, and contractility were evaluated via immunofluorescence, electron microscopy, Seahorse metabolic analysis, and high-definition video analysis, respectively. Transcriptomic sequencing was performed to identify differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways.

RESULTS: FHOD3-deficient hESC-CMs exhibited marked sarcomere disorganization and degradation, impaired calcium handling and compromised mitochondrial function, ultimately leading to reduced contractility. Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant downregulation of sarcomere-related genes and calcium-handling genes, with enrichment in pathways associated with cardiomyopathy and calcium signaling. Furthermore, FHOD3 deficiency triggered the phosphorylation of CaMKII (Thr286), a key regulator of cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling, contributing to the progression of heart failure. Treatment with the myosin activator Omecamtiv mecarbil (OM) partially restored contractility without affecting calcium handling, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic strategy.

CONCLUSIONS: Our study establishes a valuable human-derived model for investigating the molecular mechanisms of FHOD3 deficiency-induced cardiomyopathy. This model allows for extensive investigation into the phenotypes caused by FHOD3 deficiency and identifies CaMKII activation as a crucial factor contributing to the HF phenotype. Additionally, this model serves as an important tool for discovering novel therapeutic agents, and we demonstrate that OM can partially improve myocardial function in FHOD3 KO hESC-CMs.

Inherited cardiomyopathy (ICM) is a group of diseases caused by genetic mutations, characterized by abnormal myocardial structure and function, and often leads to heart failure as a severe complication. The incidence of inherited cardiomyopathy in the general population is approximately 0.2% -0.5%, posing a serious threat to human life [1, 2]. Therefore, investigating the function and pathogenic mechanisms of relevant mutated genes may facilitate the provision of personalized treatment, including drug and gene therapy, for patients with inherited cardiomyopathy [3, 4].

FHOD3 protein (Formin Homology 2 Domain Containing 3) is a member of the Formin gene family, which is characterized by its Formin homology domains (FH1 and FH2). It plays a major role in cell cytoskeletal organization, particularly in actin filament assembly and microtubule stabilization. FHOD3 is highly expressed in cardiomyocytes and mediates the nucleation and polymerization of actin filaments through the FH2 domain, thereby regulating actin assembly and sarcomere organization in cardiomyocytes, playing a key role in myofibrillogenesis [5, 6]. Previous studies have shown that mutations in FHOD3 are associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and left ventricular noncompaction (LVNC) in humans [7–10]. FHOD3 mutations are estimated to account for 1–2% of genetically explained hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) in unselected cohorts and up to 4% in familial cases [7, 8, 11, 12]. Clinically, most of FHOD3-related HCM presents in adulthood with asymmetric septal hypertrophy and carries a markedly elevated arrhythmic burden. Consequently, affected individuals face a significantly higher risk of sudden cardiac death [7, 11]. Notably, homozygosity for the p.Arg637Gln variant observed in several families is associated with a much earlier and more aggressive phenotype, with several patients developing severe disease and multiple complications between the ages of 2 and 4 years [13]. In contrast, FHOD3-related DCM is still sparsely reported and lacks a direct prevalence rate in the general population. The only fully characterized case is a family in Japan with the p.Tyr1249Asn variant, showing slow-progressing adult DCM without arrhythmia [10, 14]. In addition, only one FHOD3-LVNC pedigree has been reported: an aggressive phenotype of massive trabeculation, rapid EF fall and lethal ventricular arrhythmias causing death at 25 and 49 years. The patients produces severe trabeculation, diffuse fibrosis, rapid EF decline, recurrent LV thrombi and lethal arrhythmias, with sudden death at 25 and 49 years, indicating an aggressive phenotype [9].

In mouse models, FHOD3 knockout (KO) results in embryonic lethality by embryonic day 11.5 (E11.5) due to immature premyofibrils. Although conditional knockout of Fhod3 in the adult mouse heart does not cause lethal effects or sarcomere disorders, adult Fhod3 cKO mice exhibited a mild decline in cardiac function and slight cardiomegaly [15, 16]. These studies display that Fhod3 is crucial for both cardiac development and functional maintenance. However, all the aforementioned studies were conducted in mouse models, there are intrinsic differences in cardiac physiology and Ca2+-cycling processes between mice and humans, which limit the ability of mouse models to fully recapitulate the relevant pathological phenotypes of human cardiomyopathy [17]. This highlights the need for more precise human cardiac models to investigate the molecular mechanism of FHOD3 deficiency-induced cardiomyopathy.

Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), which can be differentiated into cardiomyocytes (hPSC-CMs), provide a valuable approach for studying cardiac diseases without species limitations [18]. Thus, we generated a FHOD3 knockout (FHOD3−/−) human embryonic stem cell (hESC) line (H9) using the CRISPR/Cas9 system and differentiate it into cardiomyocytes to elucidate the mechanism of cardiomyopathy and heart failure (HF) caused by FHOD3 deficiency. Our study found that FHOD3-deficient hPSC-CMs exhibit sarcomere disorganization and degradation. Additionally, impaired calcium homeostasis exacerbates the pathogenesis of myocardial dysfunction and HF.

Human embryonic stem cell (hESC) line H9 (WA09, WiCell) was used in this study. All hESCs were cultured on feeder-free plates coated with Matrigel (Corning, USA), and maintained in PSCeasy medium (Cellapy, China) and kept at 37℃, 5% CO2 incubator. When cells reached 80–90% confluence, they were induced to differentiate into cardiomyocytes using chemically specific small molecules that modulate Wnt/β-catenin pathway [19].

An FHOD3 knockout hESC line was generated from the H9 line via Cas9-mediated frameshift mutation. Briefly, a single-stranded guide RNA (sgRNA, sequence: 5’-ACTCTGCAGCTCTCTCACAAT-3’) was designed using an online tool (https://design.synthego.com). This sgRNA sequence was then cloned into a Cas9-expressing episomal vector (PX458-T2, Addgene #113194). The constructed plasmids were electroporated into H9 cells (Lonza Bioscience, Germany). The treated cells were re-seeded into 6-well plates in a low confluence and drug screening (using puromycin) was initiated when the cells reached 30–40% confluence to obtain homozygous transfected clones. After approximately 10 days of culture, each cell clone was passaged and expanded for DNA extraction, followed by PCR and Sanger sequencing to identify the FHOD3−/− hESCs lines.

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma) for 15 minutes, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma) for 60 minutes at room temperature. The cells were then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibody (Invitrogen, USA) at 37°C for 1 hour in the dark. After washing with PBS, the cells were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Immunofluorescent images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV3000, LYMPUS, Japan).

Cardiomyocytes were treated with CardioEasy Cardiomyocyte Digestive Fluid (Cellapy, China) to form a single-cell suspension. The cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed. Next, the cells were incubated with the primary antibody for 1 h. After three additional washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with the secondary antibody for 30 min. The samples were then washed in PBS and assessed using flow cytometry (EPICS XL, Beckman). FlowJo software was used to data analysis and quantification.

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript™ reverse transcription system (Takara). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using 2 × SYBR Master Mix (Takara) on the iCycler iQ5 (Bio-Rad). The △△CT Mode was used to analyze the relative changes in gene expression. Results are expressed relative to the expression of IPO8, which served as an internal control. Primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Total RNA was extracted from 30-day-old cardiomyocytes of and purified by RNase H and DNase I. RNA-Seq analysis was conducted by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Briefly, RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit, which included poly-A selection, RNA fragmentation, reverse transcription, and PCR amplification. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were processed using FastQC for quality control and STAR for alignment to the human reference genome (mm10/hg38). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with statistical significance set at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Cardiomyocytes pellet were fixed in cold 2.5% glutaraldehyde. The pellet were then dehydrated and embedded in resin. Ultrathin Sects. (60–80 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome. Images were captured using a transmission electron microscope (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan).

A Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, Chicopee, MA, USA) was used to analyze cellular bioenergetics. Cells were seeded into a 24-well XF Seahorse cell culture plate. The XF assay medium contained 5.5 mM/L pyruvate, 4 mM/L glutamine and 1.25 mM/L glucose. The Agilent Seahorse XFe24 system was used to measure cellular respiration. Results were normalized to protein content.

Cardiomyocytes were seeded into a confocal dish and incubated with Fluo-4 (Beyotime, China) working solution (5 µmol/L) at 37 °C for 20 min. The cells were then washed three times with PBS. Fluo-4 fluorescence signals in cardiomyocytes were collected using a laser confocal microscope (Leica, TCS5 SP5, Germany) to determine the intracellular Ca2+ transients. Caffeine release experiment was performed using calcium-free Tyrodes solution (Solarbio, China) to deplete extracellular Ca2+, followed by treatment with 10 mmol/L caffeine to induce sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca2+ release. Results were analyzed using ImageJ and GraphPad Prism software.

Cardiomyocytes were seeded as a monolayer in 6-well plate. Videos of beating cardiomyocytes were captured using a Leica DMI 4000B. Videos (3–5 s in duration) were saved in the original .czi format and then converted to an uncompressed .avi format (70 frames per second [fps]). A specialized video analysis plugin(MUSCLEMOTION) was installed in ImageJ for data analysis [20].

Total proteins were extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a mixture of phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher, USA). Samples were mixed with SDS-PAGE Sample Loading Buffer. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto a polyacrylamide gel and electrophoresed and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h at 37 °C and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies and scanned using an Odyssey CLx 9140 Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR, USA). Protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. The primary and secondary antibodies used in these experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were detected using ROS Brite™ 670 (AAT Bioquest, USA) assay. Briefly, cells were seeded on the confocal plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/dish and incubated with 10 µM Brite™ 670 working solution at 37◦C for 20 min. The cells Then washed three times with PBS, and fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

The activities of electron transport chain (ETC) complexes were detected using Mitochondrial Complex Activity Assay Kit (Elabscience, China). Briefly, freshly extracted mitochondrial proteins from cells were incubated with specific substrates for each ETC complex. The change in absorbance at designated wavelengths was recorded, and enzymatic activity was calculated based on standard formulas derived from the rate of absorbance change.

Omecamtiv mecarbil (HY-14233) is purchased from Medchemexpress and dissolved in DMSO for storage. For OM rescue experiments, it is diluted to 1 µM with cell culture medium, a concentration previously shown to yield peak inotropy in hiPSC-cardiomyocyte tissues, and cells are treated for 30 min at 37 °C [21, 22].

All data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. The Student t-tests was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. A P-values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001; ns, not significant).

To establish a FHOD3 knockout cell line, we designed a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the second exon of FHOD3 gene in hESCs (H9) by using epiCRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing system. After puromycin selection, the genotypes of resistant clones were determined by Sanger sequencing. One homozygous FHOD3 KO cell line was identified, with a 16 bp deletion in one allele and a 2 bp deletion in the other, resulting in two frameshift mutations (Fig. 1A). FHOD3 KO hESCs exhibited normal clone morphology and karyotypes (Figure S1A, B). Immunofluorescence staining and qPCR analysis of the human pluripotency markers SSEA4 and OCT4 showed that FHOD3 KO did not affect the pluripotency of hESCs (Fig. 1B, C). Then we used a small-molecule-based differentiation procedure to generate hESC-CMs (Fig. 1D, Figure S1C). The deletion of FHOD3 in hESC-CMs was validated by WB analysis (Fig. 1E). Since FHOD3 is involved in myofibrillogenesis, we next evaluated the impact of FHOD3 deficiency on myocardial differentiation. Flow cytometry analysis showed that FHOD3 KO-derived cells expressed the myocardial-specific marker cardiac Troponin T (TNNT2) at a high level (80.7%), similar to wild type (WT)-derived cells, indicating that FHOD3 KO did not affect the differentiation efficiency of the cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1F). These findings suggest that FHOD3 deficiency does not significantly affect cell characteristics and cardiac differentiation.

A Previous study demonstrated that knockdown of FHOD3 by siRNA in primary neonatal rat cardiomyocytes leads to sarcomere disorders [5]. Therefore, we next examined the morphological changes of FHOD3-KO human derived cardiomyocytes. Compared with WT hESC-CMs, immunofluorescence staining of sarcomeric proteins cTNT and α-actinin revealed a significantly higher percentage of disrupted and disorganized sarcomeres in FHOD3 KO hESC-CMs (Fig. 2A), with sarcomeric proteins are distributed in a punctate pattern. To further validate sarcomere structure in FHOD3 KO hESC-CMs, we analyzed the ultrastructure of cardiomyocytes by electron microscopy, and the results showed disarranged sarcomere, consistent with the immunostaining findings (Fig. 2B). Under a light microscope, WT and KO hESC-CMs appeared to differ in cell size, so we measured the cell volume via flow cytometry. FSC (forward scatter) analysis showed that FHOD3 hESC-CMs exhibited a larger cell volume (Fig. 2C, D). In addition, phalloidin staining of the intact cytoskeleton, and confocal microscopy analysis revealed a significantly increased surface area of FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2E, F). These findings demonstrated that FHOD3 is involved in myofibrillogenesis and cytoskeleton size regulation, and that FHOD3 deficiency recapitulates the cytoarchitectural phenotypes of ICM in vitro.

Excitation–contraction coupling is the fundamental function of cardiomyocytes, and its dysfunction is a key features of ICM [23]. To assess the impact of FHOD3-KO on cardiac contractility, we evaluated the contractile force of hESC-CMs by recording high-definition videos of beating cardiomyocytes and analyzing them using the ‘MUSCLEMOTION’ software tool, as previously reported [20]. Compared with WT cardiomyocytes, FHOD3-KO cardiomyocytes showed a significantly decrease in contractility amplitude, while the relaxation time, contraction duration, and time to peak all increased significantly (Fig. 3A-C). Given the critical role of calcium homoeostasis in regulating cardiac contraction, we further detected the calcium (Ca2+) transients using Fluo-4 AM probes. The amplitude of Ca2+ transients in KO cardiomyocytes was significantly reduced, while the rising phase (time to peak) and decay time were significantly prolonged, which was consistent with the changes in contractility (Fig. 3D, E, F, I). To further investigate Ca2+ handling properties, we estimated sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca2+ load via caffeine-induced Ca2+ release. FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes exhibited smaller amplitudes, prolonged duration and slower decay time compared with WT cardiomyocytes, indicating that FHOD3 deletion impaired SR calcium storage and Ca2+ release function (Fig. 3G, H, J). Collectively, these results confirmed that FHOD3-deficient hESC-CMs develop contractile dysfunction.

To investigate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying FHOD3 deficiency-induced cardiomyopathy, we performed transcriptomic sequencing on WT and KO hESC-CMs after 30 days of differentiation. A total of 3,089 genes exhibited significantly differential expression between WT and KO cardiomyocytes, with 1,521 genes upregulated and 1,577 genes downregulated (Fig. 4A, Figure S2A). Next, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis to determine the enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). As expected, the DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways associated with cardiomyopathy, cardiac contraction, and calcium signaling (Fig. 4B, C). Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis identified 15, 2, and 15 distinct functional categories in the biological process, cell component, and molecular function, respectively (Figure S2B). Among these categories, pathways related to sarcomere structure and contractile function were among the most significantly enriched (Fig. 4D). To further validate the RNA sequencing results, we examined the molecular changes involved in sarcomere structure and calcium handling using qPCR. The results showed that expression of nearly all sarcomere-related genes was significantly downregulated, and calcium handling-related genes were also downregulated except for CACNG4 and CACNA2D4 (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these findings provide insights into the phenotypic characteristics of FHOD3 deficiency and suggest that FHOD3 is involved in regulating sarcomere organization and calcium handling.

KEGG analysis of RNA sequencing between WT and FHDO3-KO hESC-CMs also revealed enrichment of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway. Previous studies have demonstrated that sarcomere disorganization can directly induce mitochondrial dysfunction via “structure-metabolism coupling” and the “actin-mitochondrial axis”, thereby exacerbating heart failure [24, 25]. We therefore assessed mitochondrial respiratory capacity in WT and KO cardiomyocytes using Cell Mito Stress Tests (Seahorse assay). FHOD3 deficiency significantly impaired the mitochondrial basal respiration, maximal respiration and ATP production capacity to varying extents (Fig. 5A). Consistently, chemiluminescence assays also revealed a marked reduction in intracellular ATP levels in FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes compared with WT controls (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulated in FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes, indicating the mitochondrial injury (Fig. 5C). Disruption of F-actin polymerization has been shown to markedly suppresses the activities of mitochondrial electron transport (ETC) complexes I, IV and V, ultimately reducing ATP content. In our study, FHOD3 deficiency in hESC-CMs impaired the activities of ETC complexes IV and V, whereas the activity of complex I remained unaffected (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these findings suggest that FHOD3 deficiency may exacerbate HF phenotypes due to mitochondrial damage.

We next further examined the critical molecular changes in sarcomere structure and calcium signaling pathway using WB analysis. Our results showed that the levels of sarcomere structure proteins cTNT, MYH6, and α-actinin were significantly lower in FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes than in WT cardiomyocytes, which was consistent with the transcriptomic result (Fig. 6A). This downregulation suggests that loss of FHOD3 may repress gene transcription and accelerate proteolytic degradation of these structural components. Sarco/endoplasmic-reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) is a key regulator of Ca2+ homeostasis in cardiomyocytes, driving Ca2+ reuptake during diastole and thus ensuring robust Ca2+ release for subsequent contraction [26]. WB results showed that the level of SERCA protein were significantly decreased, which explained why calcium transients are impaired following FHOD3 knockout (Fig. 6B). Disequilibrium of Calcium Homeostasis in cardiomyocytes can trigger Ca2+-dependent transcriptional patterns, which induce myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis and apoptosis [27]. Calcineurin and CaMKII are two well-characterized pathways that play critical role in cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling. Our results revealed increased level of phosphorylated CaMKII (p-CamkII, Thr286) in FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes, while the levels of CaMKII and calcineurin remained unchanged (Fig. 6B). Phosphorylated CaMKII further transcriptionally activates hypertrophy, inflammation, apoptosis and fetal gene programs, ultimately leading to cardiac remodeling and HF. Therefore, we detected the CaMKII-activated genes involved in HF progression. QPCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of the cardiac remodeling markers NPPA and NPPB in FHOD3-knockout cardiomyocytes (Fig. 6C). We also observed a switch in myosin heavy chain (MHC) isoform expression from MHC-α (MYH6) to the fetal isoform MHC-β (MYH7)—a hallmark of myocardial stress and HF (Fig. 6C). Moreover, FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes exhibited elevated expression of the pro-apoptotic factors BCL-10 and p53, along with the inflammatory mediators complement factor B (CFB) and MMP9 (Fig. 6C). In summary, our findings indicate that CaMKII activation amplifies the heart failure phenotype induced by FHOD3 deficiency.

Currently, there is no curative therapy for inherited cardiomyopathy, and therapeutic strategies mainly focused on improving cardiac function and delaying cardiac remodeling [28]. Contractile dysfunction resulting from sarcomere disassembly was the primary phenotype observed in FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes. We therefore evaluated whether drugs targeting sarcomere function could compensate for the FHOD3 deficiency. Omecamtiv mecarbil (OM) is a candidate myosin activator that enhances contractile force independently of intracellular Ca²⁺ levels, and we thus tested the effects of OM on FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes after 30 days of differentiation [29]. Treatment with OM at a therapeutic dosages (1µM) markedly restored contractility in FHOD3-KO cells and shortened time-to-peak tension (Fig. 7A, B). Consistent with previous reports, OM also significantly prolonged the relaxation phase. Although total contraction duration tended to increase, this change did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 7A, B). Immunostaining results showed that OM failed to restore sarcomere alignment in FHOD3-KO cells (Figure S5A). We next evaluated the effects of OM on Ca2+ transient. As expected, no significant differences in Ca2+ transient parameters were observed before and after OM treatment (Fig. 7C, D). Interestingly, seahorse analysis revealed that OM elevated basal respiration in FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes, however, maximal respiration remained unchanged and showed a non-significant downward trend, indicating that OM imposes energetic stress without improving mitochondrial function (Figure S5B). Taken together, these results showed that OM rapidly restores contractile strength in FHOD3-deficient cardiomyocytes without altering Ca2+ transient or sarcomere architecture, and only mildly increases mitochondrial energy demand, highlighting its therapeutic potential for FHOD3-deficient cardiomyopathy.

Inherited cardiomyopathy arise primarily from pathogenic gene defects, dissecting gene function is essential for mechanistic insights and identifying therapeutic targets [30]. Human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hPSC-CMs) represent an ideal tool for studying inherited cardiomyopathies, as they combine human specificity, scalable production, and CRISPR-mediated genetic engineering to provide unrivaled models for exploring disease mechanisms and screening patient-tailored therapies [31, 32]. In this study, we established a FHOD3-KO hESC-CMs model to investigate the function of FHOD3 and the pathogenesis of FHOD3-related cardiomyopathy using a combination of CRISPR/Cas9 editing and stem cell differentiation technologies. Specifically, we demonstrated that FHOD3-KO hESC-CMs exhibit phenotypes associated with cardiomyopathy and HF. Abnormalities in sarcomere structure and calcium handling emerged as central mechanisms underlying disease pathogenesis. Notably, treatment with myosin activator Omecamtiv mecarbil rescued myocardial contractile dysfunction without altering Ca²+ handling (Graphical abstract).

Human and murine studies have linked FHOD3 mutations to both hypertrophic (HCM, accounting for 1–2% of HCM) and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM); FHOD3 loss-of-function in mice disrupts sarcomeres and leads to lethal perinatal DCM [10, 15, 16, 33]. This early embryonic lethality has limited postnatal mechanistic investigations, creating a gap in our understanding of how FHOD3 deficiency drives human cardiac disease. HESC-CMs overcome this limitation, enabling dissection of the molecular pathogenesis of FHOD3-related cardiomyopathy in a human cellular context. In our study, FHOD3-knockout (KO) hESC-CMs recapitulated several key features that align with known clinical phenotypes associated with FHOD3 variants. Specifically, the increased cell size in our KO model is indicative of hypertrophy, which is a characteristic feature of HCM. Additionally, we observed significant contractile dysfunction and mitochondrial failure in our KO model. These features are more consistent with dilated cardiomyopathy DCM. Apart from these divergent phenotypes, the model also displayed pathological signatures shared by both disease spectra: disorganized sarcomeres and impared calcium handling [34, 35]. Collectively, these data demonstrate that FHOD3 deficiency can simultaneously evoke hypertrophic and dilated phenotypes within the same human cardiomyocyte, providing a cellular explanation for the variable clinical expressivity of FHOD3-associated cardiomyopathy. Nevertheless, it is important to note that while our KO model recapitulates severe loss-of-function (LoF) phenotypes, FHOD3 patients typically carry missense or splice-site mutations, which may lead to gain-of-function (GoF) or dominant-negative effects, therefore, a simple knockout model may not fully recapitulate patient-specific phenotypes. For example, in a Japanese family with dilated cardiomyopathy, there was a rare missense mutation p.Tyr1249Asn. Functional studies showed that this mutation weakened actin SRF signaling through a dominant negative effect, leading to mild left ventricular dilation and sudden death events within the family [14]. While our KO model allows a more straightforward and definitive assessment of the essential role of FHOD3 in cardiomyocyte function and can mimic potentially severe LoF variants. In addition our KO model creates an isogenic null background for targeted knock-in of patient-specific missense or splice-site variants, enabling future dose–response studies that directly link residual protein level to cellular phenotype.

Consistent with previous rodent data, FHOD3 deletion in hESC-CMs resulted in rapid sarcomere disassembly [15]. Importantly, FHOD3 deletion did not reduce the final yield of TNNT2⁺ cardiomyocytes after 15 days of differentiation, indicating comparable differentiation efficiency between two groups. WB results further showed that the levels of critical sarcomeric proteins (cTNT, α-actinin and MYH6) were markedly reduced after FHOD3 deletion. This downregulation appears to be a secondary consequence of structural collapse: loss of FHOD3 destabilizes the sarcomeric scaffold, and surplus or misfolded component proteins are cleared via the proteasomal and autophagic pathways [6, 36]. Consequently, sarcomeric collapse triggered a cascade of contractile and Ca²+ handling abnormalities in FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes, accompanied by upregulation of HF-related genes, collectively recapitulating phenotypes of human heart failure [37].

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key driver in the progression of cardiomyopathy, and emerging evidence suggests that sarcomere disarray impairs mitochondrial function via a “structure-metabolism coupling” mechanism [38, 39]. KEGG profiling of our RNA-seq data revealed a significant down-regulation of oxidative-phosphorylation genes, mirroring the energy-starvation signature of failing hearts. Concordantly, FHOD3-null cardiomyocytes displayed impaired mitochondrial respiratory capacity and elevated ROS production. Previous studies have shown that disruption of sarcomeric architecture consistently leads to mitochondrial detachment and misalignment, resulting in a marked reduction in NADH respiratory chain function and ATP production [40]. Consistent with these findings, FHOD3 deficiency also reduced the activities of ETC complexes IV and V. Additionally, a recent study demonstrated that cTnT p.K185E mutation aberrantly activates the RAS/RAF1-p44/42 signaling axis, triggering excessive mitochondrial fission, dysfunction, and impaired cardiomyocyte contraction, ultimately leading to DCM [38]. These independent findings reinforce our observation and confirm mitochondrial dysfunction as a central driver of FHOD3-associated cardiomyopathy.

It is well established that disturbances in cellular Ca2+-handling are a critical determinant of Ca2+-dependent cardiac remodeling and HF [41, 42]. In our study, FHOD3-KO cardiomyocytes displayed abnormal Ca2+ transients, including decreased amplitude and prolonged recovery time. Furthermore, the level of SR Ca2+ uptake protein SERCA was significantly decreased. These results suggest that the dysfunction of Ca2+ transport in KO cardiomyocytes results in Ca2+ overload. Ca2+ regulates the activity of diverse downstream effectors via excitation-transcription (ET) coupling. Among these, CaMKII and Calcineurin are two well-characterized pathways with established roles in cardiac remodeling [27, 43]. Consistent with this, we found that the level of phosphorylated CaMKII (p-CaMKII) was significantly increased in FHOD3 KO cardiomyocytes compared with WT controls, whereas the protein expression levels of calcineurin and total CaMKII remained unchanged. This suggests that the CaMKII-regulated signaling pathway contributes to FHOD3 deficieny- indcuced heart failure.

In addition to CaMKII-calcium axis, our transcriptomic analysis also revealed several other significantly enriched pathways, including adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, sarcomere-centric cardiomyopathy gene sets, PI3K-Akt and cAMP signaling pathway. While these pathways have been implicated in cardiac remodeling and stress responses, their activation largely converges on CaMKII-dependent calcium dysregulation. Beyond these canonical pathways, several previously unappreciated pathways YAP/TEAD signaling, metabolic reprogramming, and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction—all statistically robust and biologically intriguing. While these pathways offer fertile ground for future exploration, they currently lack a direct mechanistic link connecting cytoskeletal damage with calcium dysregulation and the consequent heart-failure phenotype.

Omecamtiv mecarbil (OM) is a candidate myosin activator that directly enhances contractile force without the disadvantages of classic inotropic agents including increased oxygen demand, elevated heart rate, and disruption of intracellular calcium homeostasis. While OM improves contraction efficiency, it does not restore disorganized sarcomeres. Instead, it prolongs the myosin-actin cross-bridge cycle, thereby increasing force per beat without altering Ca2+ transients or sarcomere architecture [21, 22, 29]. Thus, it acts as a functional compensator rather than a curative treatment. In this study, we confirmed that OM enhanced myocardial contraction in FHOD3-KO cardiomyocytes but did not improve sarcomere architecture or Ca2+ transients. Additionally, OM modestly but significantly prolonged relaxation time in FHOD3-KO cardiomyocytes, a finding consistent with observations in human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived engineered heart tissues (EHTs) from previous study [21]. In contrast, OM has been shown to reduce relaxation time in rat-derived EHTs. Currently, there is no definitive conclusion regarding whether the changes in diastolic time affect the ability of OM to improve contractility. The impact of OM on mitochondrial energetics remains incompletely defined. In the research by Rhoden et al., they found that treatment with OM at a therapeutic dosages (1µM) did not increase ROS, whereas levels > 3µM do elevate ROS. However, they did not directly evalute mitochondrial respiration [21]. In our study, we directly assessed the effect of OM on respiration in FHOD3-KO cardiomyocytes by Seahorse analysis. OM (1 µM) raised basal OCR by about 15%, yet maximal respiration remained unchanged, indicating that OM boosts ATP demand without increasing mitochondrial capacity. This phenomenon likely reflects the mechanism of OM: by prolonging the cycle of individual myosin-actin cross-bridges, OM increases the effective turnover frequency and ATP hydrolysis rate, thereby mildly stimulating basal respiration to meet the elevated energy demand. Overall, our data indicate that OM could serve as a potential therapeutic agent to alleviate certain phenotypes caused by FHOD3 deficiency.

Our research has several limitations. First all experimental results are based on a single knockout (KO) clone. Although we controlled for guide RNA off-target sites by prediction and whole-exome sequencing, the use of a single clone may introduce clonal variability and off-target CRISPR effects, which could potentially impact our conclusions. Multiple independent clones or patient-derived hiPSC lines will be necessary to confirm the generalizability of the observed phenotypes in the future work. Second our KO hESC-CMs are conventional 2D monolayer model which retain an immature, fetal-like sarcomeric and electrophysiological signature, consequently, the impact of FHOD3 deficiency may differ in more mature or adult cardiomyocytes. Future work should use more mature cardiac models such as electromechanically stimulated 3D-EHTs to further validate our findings. Third the KO model can not completely reproduce haploinsufficiency, GoF or dominant-negative mechanisms that may arise from clinical patient-specific missense or splice-site variants. Future work will introduce representative mutations using CRISPR knock-in in wild-type hESCs and under graded expression in an isogenic KO line. This approach will generate a continuous allelic series to determine whether patient-relevant GoF or dominant-negative alleles produce phenotypes distinct from simple loss-of-function. It will also directly correlate residual FHOD3 protein levels with clinical phenotypes and distinguish expression-driven from activity-driven pathogenic mechanisms.

In conclusion, our research establishes a valuable human-derived loss-of-function model for investigating the biological functions of FHOD3 and the pathogenic mechanisms underlying FHOD3-deficiency-induced cardiomyopathy. This model enables extensive exploration of the phenotypes caused by FHOD3 deficiency and identifies CaMKII activation as a crucial factor contributing to the HF phenotype. Additionally, this model serves as a robust tool for discovering novel therapeutic agents, and we demonstrate that Omecamtiv mecarbil (OM) can partially improve myocardial function in FHOD3 KO hESC-CMs.
