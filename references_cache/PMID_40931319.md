# LONP1 Variants Are Associated With Clinically Diverse Phenotypes.
**Authors:** Young RE, Qiao L, Hernan R, Sweetser DA, Waxler JL, Scott DA, Scott TM, Lalani SR, Azamian MS, Rosenfeld JA, Bostwick B, Burrage LC, Undiagnosed Diseases Network, Rodan LH, Russell BE, Dutra-Clarke M, Kruer M, Bakhtiarim S, Darvish H, Amor DJ, Rahman S, Stals K, Bradley L, Byrne S, Tolusso LK, Wong B, Benedict L, Wallis K, Micke K, Colson C, Smol T, Southwick SV, Miller KA, Kush ML, Chorin O, Rothschild A, Wang W, Shen Y, Chung WK
**Journal:** Clin Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cge.70057](https://doi.org/10.1111/cge.70057)

## Content

LONP1 encodes a mitochondrial protease essential for protein quality control and metabolism. Variants in LONP1 are associated with a diverse and expanding spectrum of disorders, including Cerebral, Ocular, Dental, Auricular, and Skeletal anomalies syndrome (CODAS), congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH), and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD), with some individuals exhibiting features of mitochondrial encephalopathy. We report 16 novel LONP1 variants identified in 16 individuals (11 with NDD, 5 with CDH), further expanding the clinical spectrum. Structural mapping of disease-associated missense variants revealed phenotype-specific clustering, with CODAS variants enriched in the proteolytic chamber and NDD variants more broadly distributed. CODAS is caused by biallelic variants and CDH by monoallelic variants, both of which are predicted to act through loss-of-function mechanisms. Both monoallelic and biallelic variants are associated with LONP1-related NDD, suggesting complex mechanisms such as dominant-negative effects. Our findings broaden the phenotypic and genetic spectrum of LONP1-associated disorders and highlight the essential role of LONP1 in mitochondrial function and development.

LONP1 is an ATP-dependent mitochondrial protease essential for mitochondrial proteostasis, metabolism, and cellular stress responses. This highly conserved, multifunctional enzyme maintains mitochondrial homeostasis by utilizing ATP hydrolysis to degrade misfolded or oxidized proteins and by functioning as a chaperone for protein folding [1–4]. LONP1 also regulates mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number [5]. In humans, LONP1 is broadly expressed, with the highest levels in metabolically active tissues such as the heart, brain, liver, lung, and skeletal muscle [1].

The human LONP1 protein consists of 959 amino acids and contains three conserved structural domains and a mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS), which is cleaved upon mitochondrial transport leading to mature LONP1 protein [6]. The N-terminal domain facilitates substrate recognition and may mediate chaperone activity. The ATP-binding domain includes the AAA+ module, comprised of the α/β and α subdomains, and is critical for ATP binding and substrate translocation [7]. When ATP-binding activity is ablated, LONP1 cannot degrade or chaperone proteins. The HI(CC) module spans both the N-terminal and ATP-binding domains, and is comprised of the NTD5H and NTD3H subdomains. The proteolytic domain at the C terminus is responsible for substrate cleavage and degradation.

The clinical spectrum associated with LONP1 is remarkably diverse. Variants in LONP1 are associated with a wide range of diseases including cancer, neurodegeneration, heart disease, and developmental disorders [8, 9]. Biallelic LONP1 variants were first associated with autosomal recessive CODAS syndrome (Cerebral, Ocular, Dental, Auricular, and Skeletal anomalies syndrome; OMIM #600373), a rare disorder characterized by multi-system features including developmental delay, craniofacial anomalies, hearing loss, delayed tooth eruption, short stature, hip dysplasia, delayed epiphyseal ossification, vertebral clefts, and cataracts [10–17]. We previously identified LONP1 as a candidate risk gene for congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH), a condition often co-occurring with cardiac defects, skeletal anomalies, and neurodevelopmental features [18, 19]. Recently, monoallelic and biallelic LONP1 variants have also been reported in individuals with mitochondrial encephalopathy, metabolic features such as lactic acidosis, and neurodevelopmental phenotypes without pulmonary, diaphragm, or cardinal reported CODAS features, further expanding the clinical spectrum of LONP1-associated disorders [19–22].

In this study, we report 16 individuals with 16 novel variants in LONP1–11 with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) and five individuals with CDH. Our findings demonstrate that LONP1 variants are associated with at least three distinct clinical phenotypes: CODAS syndrome (biallelic variants), CDH (primarily monoallelic), and NDDs (with both monoallelic and biallelic variants), each with distinct inheritance patterns.

Twelve of the sixteen newly described individuals were identified through GeneMatcher (Cases 1–11, 14; https://genematcher.org/statistics/) [23, 24]. The remaining four cases were referred to us directly by investigators (Cases 12, 13, 15, 16). Detailed phenotypic and genotypic data were obtained through collaborative follow-up communications with submitting clinicians and researchers.

Exome sequencing (ES) was performed on all cases, except for Cases 13 and 15, in which multi-gene CDH panels with exome-wide copy number variant (CNV) detection were performed (Prevention Genetics). Exome sequencing and variant filtering were performed according to the routine procedures at each institute. Case 4 had trio whole genome sequencing (WGS) performed by Hudson Alpha using methods previously described, and research GS analysis was performed using Codified Genomics (https://www.codifiedgenomics.com) [25].

We used ANNOVAR to annotate variant population frequencies in gnomAD v4.1 and in silico prediction scores for LONP1 NM_004793.3. The combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD version 1.4), rare exome variant ensemble learner (REVEL), and AlphaMissense in silico prediction tools were used to assess missense variants [26–30]. SpliceAI was used to predict the effect of splice variants [31]. Variants were classified according to ACMG criteria (Table S1) [32].

We used ConSurf (https://consurf.tau.ac.il/consurf_index.php) to perform an evolutionary conservation analysis by aligning homologous sequences and computing a conservation score for each amino acid position [33, 34]. We submitted the human LONP1 protein sequence and selected 150 homologs from a range of vertebrate species.

We generated the lollipop plot for LONP1 (GenBank: NM_004793.3) using the R trackViewer package [35]. We referred to previously published structures and AlphaFold (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/P36776) to determine LONP1 domain and subdomain boundaries [7, 36].

To visualize the 3D LONP1 protein structure, we searched the LONP1 canonical sequence (identifier: P36776–1) in UniProt and obtained the cryo-EM structure of the human mitochondrial LONP1 monomer (encompassing the residue range 115–959) and hexamer from PDB ID 7NG5 [37]. The 3D structure was visualized using PyMOL molecular viewer (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 1.2r3pre, Schrödinger LLC).

We used AlphaCluster (https://github.com/ShenLab/AlphaCluster), which comes pre-loaded with tertiary structures from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database, to quantify 3D clustering based on physical distance and determine the statistical significance of clustering for missense variants on coding sequence of LONP1 [38]. Briefly, we loaded the LONP1 protein model and retrieved the 3D coordinates of the central carbon atom of each amino acid. We set n as number of LONP1 missense variants of interest. The observed geometric mean of the pairwise distance between each of the n LONP1 missense variants is calculated. For 1 × 109 iterations, n random variants are selected from all the possible variants in LONP1, with respect to the underlying background mutation rate; the geometric means of the pairwise distances are calculated and used to form a null distribution of geometric means. The null distribution is used to designate a p-value for the observed geometric mean of the n actual missense observed in LONP1.

We identified 11 individuals with LONP1 variants and neurodevelopmental features without CDH or classic CODAS features, and five previously reported cases [19–22]. Table 1 summarizes the major clinical features for these 11 individuals. We also identified an additional five individuals with monoallelic LONP1 variants with complex CDH associated with severe congenital abnormalities. Table 2 summarizes the major findings of these five individuals. Clinical descriptions for each individual are in the Supporting Information. ACMG criteria used for variant classification are provided in Table S1 [32].

Biallelic LONP1 variants have been reported in 29 individuals with CODAS syndrome [10–17]. Of the 29 reported individuals with CODAS syndrome, 19 are homozygous for a LONP1 variant, and 10 are compound heterozygous for two LONP1 variants. There are 17 unique variants, including 14 missense variants, identified in individuals with CODAS syndrome (Table 3). Five variants are each observed in at least three individuals. The most frequent variant c.2161C>G p.(Arg721Gly) is a founder mutation in the Pennsylvania Amish and has been identified as homozygous in eight individuals. In individuals with CODAS syndrome where inheritance is known, all LONP1 variants have been inherited from two unaffected heterozygous parents. All reported individuals with CODAS syndrome have cataracts.

Previously, our group identified 24 LONP1 variants associated with CDH in 23 individuals [18]. Here, we describe an additional five individuals for a total of 28 individuals with 24 unique LONP1 variants associated with CDH. Among these 24 variants, 19 are inherited, three are de novo, and two are of unknown inheritance, suggesting reduced or incomplete penetrance of inherited LONP1 variants associated with CDH (Table 3). In total, we have identified 11 missense, seven frameshift, three stop gain, and three splice variants in LONP1 associated with CDH, suggesting a loss-of-function mechanism. Among the 28 individuals with CDH, 27 individuals are heterozygous, and one individual has biallelic inherited LONP1 variants (p.(Pro525Leu) and p.(Arg755Gly)).

Compared to individuals with CODAS syndrome, who all have biallelic LONP1 variants, and individuals with CDH who, with one exception, have monoallelic variants, individuals with an isolated NDD phenotype carry monoallelic and biallelic variants. In total, 10/16 individuals have biallelic, either homozygous or compound heterozygous variants, and 6/16 individuals have monoallelic, heterozygous variants. Of note, two individuals with biallelic LONP1 variants have a pathogenic variant in unknown phase with a likely benign variant (Case 6: c.1773+2T (pathogenic) and c.742G>A p.(Asp248Asn) (VLB); Peter et al. c.1693T>C p.(Tyr565His) (pathogenic) and c.2197G>A p.(-Glu733Lys) (VLB)). There are 18 unique variants found in individuals with LONP1-related NDD including 16 missense variants. LONP1 variants associated with NDD were predicted to be deleterious by in silico predictions and rare in population databases (Table 3).

Two missense variants at amino acid Arg301 were identified in three unrelated individuals, p.(Arg301Trp) (de novo in two individuals) and p.(Arg301Gln) (inheritance unknown). In particular, the p.(Arg301Trp) missense variant is associated with severe lactic acidosis. Three different missense variants at amino acid Tyr565 in three unrelated individuals (p.(Tyr565Asn) p.(Tyr565Cys), p.(Tyr565His)). Missense variants at p.(Tyr565) are associated with depletion of mtDNA in skeletal muscle, reduced OXPHOS, and moderate lactic acidosis. We performed an evolutionary conservation analysis for LONP1 and found that the Tyr565 residue is highly conserved across vertebrates and Arg301 is mildly conserved (Figure S1). Individuals with Arg301 and Tyr565 missense variants exhibited more severe presentations, with three individuals deceased before the age of two.

The sixteen individuals with LONP1-related NDD range in age from 2 months to 23 years old. Features common to the majority of individuals are presented in Table 4. Neurodevelopmental features observed in individuals with LONP1 variants span a spectrum and are largely non-specific, with some individuals reported to have mild to severe biochemical features and mitochondrial encephalopathy. Although these neurodevelopmental features can overlap with those seen in CODAS syndrome, individuals with CODAS exhibit additional distinctive syndromic features, including craniofacial dysmorphisms, dental features, sensorineural hearing loss, and skeletal abnormalities that are rarely observed in individuals with isolated NDD or CDH. LONP1-related disorders encompass a broad and overlapping phenotypic spectrum. Congenital cataracts are found in all individuals with CODAS syndrome, one individual with CDH, and five individuals with NDD, suggesting that cataracts may be a common phenotype associated across multiple classes of LONP1 variants.

There is one LONP1 variant shared between individuals with CDH and CODAS at p.(Arg755Gly). The p.(Arg755Gly) variant has previously only been reported in cases of CDH, as an inherited heterozygous variant in one individual and as an inherited biallelic variant in an unrelated individual (along with p.(Pro525Leu)). Recently, an individual with the homozygous p.(Arg755Gly) variant and CODAS syndrome was reported with unaffected heterozygous parents [17]. This family previously had a child with CDH who was not genotyped.

There is one LONP1 splice variant shared between CDH and NDD at the position c.1773+2T>C; however, there are no overlapping features between individuals.

LONP1 variants in CODAS cluster in the AAA+ subdomain in the ATP-binding domain and near the boundary of the proteolytic domain (Figure 1). CDH-associated variants span all three domains (one in the MTS region, nine in the N-terminal, 16 in the ATP-binding, and two in the proteolytic domain). NDD-associated variants include four in the MTS region, seven in the N-terminal, six in the ATP-binding, and one in the proteolytic domain (Figure 1).

LONP1 forms a hexamer with ATP-binding and proteolytic domains comprising a chamber while the N-terminal domain is arranged as a trimer of dimers. LONP1 conformational changes are necessary for protease function. Mapping disease-associated variants onto the 3D hexameric structure revealed CODAS variants are clustered on the periphery of the proteolytic core, while CDH and NDD variants are more widely distributed (Figure 2A–F).

To assess phenotype specific clustering, we used AlphaCluster to evaluate the spatial distribution of pathogenic missense variants on the AlphaFold LONP1 structure (AF-Q9H9GZ), validated against the cryo-EM structure (PDB 7NG5; TM-score: 0.56). The geometric mean of the pairwise distances of LONP1 missense variants was calculated under the assumption that clustering of pathogenic missense variants is non-random and phenotype specific. CODAS and CDH missense variants showed significant clustering, whereas NDD variants were more broadly distributed (Figure 2G).

We report 16 newly identified individuals with novel variants in LONP1, expanding the clinical spectrum of LONP1-associated disorders. LONP1-related NDD shares several overlapping features with CODAS syndrome, including developmental delay, ID, hypotonia, and cataracts. Many individuals also exhibit biochemical features of mitochondrial encephalopathy, including lactic acidosis and deficient oxidative phosphorylation. In contrast, skeletal and dental anomalies are specific to CODAS and are not observed in individuals with monoallelic or biallelic variants presenting with isolated NDD and CDH. Recently, individuals with biallelic LONP1 variants and isolated epilepsy have been reported without developmental delay or cardinal CODAS features [39]. These recent reports, along with our identification of 11 additional individuals with LONP1-related NDD, further broaden the LONP1 clinical landscape.

We found that the distribution of variants across LONP1 protein domains correlates with distinct clinical phenotypes. CODAS variants cluster in the ATP-binding domain, particularly the α subdomain of the AAA+ module, and the proteolytic domain boundary. Variants in these regions impair LONP1 activity in vitro. The p.Pro676Ser variant reduced protease activity and likely disrupts the AAA+ module active site, decreasing ATP-binding and hydrolysis [13]. Similarly, p.Arg721Gly may disrupt the formation of salt bridges in the AAA+ module, destabilizing the LONP1 complex, leading to reduced homo-oligomerization, ATPase activity, and peptidase activity [13]. Both variants also exhibit substrate-specific defects, failing to degrade or chaperone MT-CO2, suggesting biallelic CODAS variants impair ATP-dependent proteolytic activity and substrate interactions.

Variants in isolated NDD cases are primarily located in the N-terminal and ATP-binding domains, particularly within the HI(CC) and AAA+ modules. Three individuals with variants at p.Tyr565 (p.Tyr565Asn, p.Tyr565Cys, p.Tyr565His) presented with congenital lactic acidosis and OXPHOS defects. The p.Tyr565His variant impairs substrate binding and proteolysis [22]. The Tyr565 residue directly interacts with substrates, and its disruption decreases substrate degradation and increases ATP consumption, indicating nonproductive ATP hydrolysis [7]. The N-terminal domain is required for hexamer assembly and enzymatic activity [40]. Variants in the HI(CC) module may act through dominant-negative effects by disrupting LONP1 assembly and enzymatic function.

CDH-associated variants are dispersed across all domains and predicted loss-of-function. Individuals with heterozygous damaging LONP1 variants show higher mortality and ECMO use than individuals with damaging variants in other genes [18, 41, 42]. Homozygous Lonp1 deletion in mice causes embryonic lethality, while lung-specific conditional inactivation impairs lung development and airway epithelial regulation via mitochondrial deficiency [18, 43, 44]. These findings suggest that LONP1 loss directly affects lung development independently of diaphragmatic hernia. The incomplete penetrance of CDH observed in LONP1 variant carriers underscores the complex genetic and environmental contributions to this phenotype. While many individuals harboring LONP1 variants exhibit severe congenital anomalies, others present with milder or no symptoms. This variability may be influenced by modifier genes, epigenetic regulation, or environmental factors during development. The shared p.Arg755Gly variant in both CODAS and CDH highlights phenotypic variability and suggests additional modifying factors influence clinical outcomes.

Molecular mechanisms vary by variant type and inheritance. CODAS and CDH variants are hypothesized to cause loss-of-function effects, while NDD variants may act through dominant-negative mechanisms. Structural mapping of pathogenic variants revealed that CODAS variants cluster at the periphery of the proteolytic core, while NDD variants are more diffusely distributed. Quantitative analysis of disease-associated missense variants revealed that CDH and CODAS variants are clustered non-randomly on the 3D protein tertiary structure, suggesting CDH and CODAS missense variants are significantly enriched in distinct regions, whereas NDD variants lack such clustering. Recurrent substitutions at Tyr565 in three individuals with severe phenotypes further support a dominant-negative mechanism in LONP1-related NDD. Given that LONP1 functions as a hexamer, spatial clustering likely reflects distinct pathogenic mechanisms. Future biochemical and structural assays will be critical to dissect how pathogenic variants perturb LONP1 function and lead to divergent clinical phenotypes.

LONP1 is a multifunctional enzyme essential for regulating mitochondrial homeostasis, primarily through degradation of damaged or misfolded proteins. Beyond its proteolytic role, LONP1 regulates mitochondrial metabolism and energetics. It modulates steroidogenesis by degrading rate-limiting proteins such as StAR, which transports cholesterol into the mitochondrial membrane [45]. LONP1 also mediates PDH activity via proteolytic turnover of PDK4 and regulation of E1α phosphorylation [21, 46]. Given LONP1’s diverse, tissue-specific roles, its disruption leads to variable downstream effects depending on the domains and pathways affected.

This study has important limitations. Clinical data were gathered across international sites with variable phenotypic detail. AlphaCluster structural analysis was limited to protein-level data and a default background mutational model, which may overestimate clustering in highly mutable regions. Additionally, in individuals with a single heterozygous LONP1 variant identified by exome sequencing, the possibility of a second undetected variant, such as a non-coding, intronic, or structural variant, cannot be excluded. This is particularly relevant given that both monoallelic and biallelic LONP1 variants have been associated with NDD.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a well-established contributor to NDDs [47, 48]. LONP1-associated disorders exhibit both distinct and overlapping features. As our understanding of LONP1 and its role in human disease deepens, the spectrum of related disorders is likely to further expand.
