# Functional neuroimaging identifies a cross-species biomarker for treatment monitoring in Angelman syndrome
**Authors:** Baroncelli L, Dadà L, Simoncelli C, Sagona G, Vecchieschi G, De Cesari C, Diversi R, Biondi F, Jafar-nejad P, Bosetti C, Prisco G, Mazziotti R, Tonazzini I, Battini R
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-9937060/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9937060/v1)

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>Angelman syndrome (AS) is a neurodevelopmental disorder for which non-invasive biomarkers suitable for longitudinal monitoring and clinical trials remain limited. Although EEG abnormalities have shown promise as treatment-responsive biomarkers, their applicability may be constrained by tolerability and developmental variability. Here, we identify visually evoked hemodynamic responses as a robust cross-species functional biomarker of AS. Using intrinsic optical signal (IOS) imaging, we show that AS mouse model exhibits enhanced visual cortical responses that persist across development and partially reflect disease severity in adulthood. These alterations are highly sensitive to therapeutic interventions: restoration of UBE3A expression normalizes visual responses and scales with both behavioral recovery and molecular rescue. To assess translational relevance, we performed functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) in individuals with AS during visual stimulation, demonstrating the feasibility of fNIRS in behaviorally challenging settings. Individuals with AS display amplified and temporally altered hemodynamic responses that are consistently detected across preprocessing strategies and remaine stable across age. Our findings demonstrate that visually evoked hemodynamic responses capture a disease-specific cortical signature of AS conserved across species. The scalability, non-invasiveness, and feasibility of fNIRS, combined with preclinical sensitivity to therapeutic interventions, position neurovascular imaging as a promising biomarker for future clinical trials in AS.</p>

Introduction 95 
Angelman syndrome (AS) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder characteri zed by severe 96 
intellectual disability, motor dysfunction, absence of speech, seizures, and behavioral 97 
abnormalities1. The disorder is caused by disruption of the maternally inherit ed UBE3A gene. 98 
In neurons, the paternal UBE3A allele is epigenetically silenced by the expression of a lon g 99 
non-coding antisense RNA ( UBE3A-ATS), leaving the maternal gene as the sole source of 100 
UBE3A protein expression 2. Consequently, genetic alterations affecting the maternal allele, 101 
including deletions or mutations, paternal uniparental diso my, or imprinting defects, result in 102 
neuronal loss of UBE3A and lead to the development of AS3. 103 
 Currently, no disease-modifying therapies are available for AS, and clinical management 104 
remains largely symptomatic4. Over the past decade, several therapeutic strategies have been 105 
explored in AS mouse models, including antisense oligonucleoti des (ASOs), gene transfer 106 
therapy, and small-molecule approaches 5–13. Notably, both gene transfer therapy and ASO-107 
based therapies targeting UBE3A-ATS to unsilence the paternal allele have recently entered 108 
clinical trials 6,14 (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT06914609; NCT07 136454; 109 
NCT07157254; NCT07181837), underscoring the urgent need f or reliable and quantitative 110 
outcome measures to monitor disease progression and assess therapeutic efficacy. 111 
Electroencephalography (EEG)-based metrics have emerged from previous studi es as 112 
promising functional biomarkers for AS. In particular, abnormally el evated delta-band 113 
rhythmicity has been consistently reported in both AS mouse models a nd patients 15,16. 114 
However, longitudinal EEG recordings in humans indicate that d elta power progressively 115 
decreases with age, suggesting that this biomarker might be most sensitive during early 116 
development and less reliable in older individuals. In addition, EEG recordings are often poorly 117 
tolerated by children with AS, limiting their feasibility in  clinical settings and longitudinal 118 
studies17. Together, these limitations highlight the need for alt ernative biomarkers that can be 119 
robustly applied across the lifespan. 120 
Here, we investigated highly translational optical imaging approaches, Intrinsic Optical 121 
Signal (IOS) imaging in mice and functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) in humans, to 122 
assess visually evoked responses as functional brain readouts in AS. Bo th neuroimaging 123 
techniques measure activity-dependent hemodynamic changes and provi de non-invasive 124 
measures of cortical function that are complementary to electrophysio logical recordings. The 125 
visual system represents a particularly suitable model for probing cort ical dysfunction in 126 
neurodevelopmental disorders18–21. Sensory processing alterations in the visual domain have 127 
been reported in AS, and neuroimaging studies in patients h ave identified structural 128 
abnormalities in the occipital cortex, including reduced grey mat ter volume and cortical 129 
thickness22. Consistently, maternal loss of Ube3a in mice disrupts synaptic plasticity in the 130 
visual cortex, impairing experience-dependent circuit maturation23,24.  131 
Building on this evidence, we evaluated visually evoked hemodyn amic responses as a 132 
candidate prognostic and treatment-responsive biomarker for AS. In t he mouse model, we 133 
observed significantly enhanced visual responses in AS animals compare d to wild-type (WT) 134 
controls. These alterations partially correlated with the severit y of motor deficits. Importantly, 135 
visual responses were sensitive to therapeutic interventions: reinst atement of UBE3A 136 
expression through either gene transfer therapy or ASO treatment normalized visual responses 137 
in AS mice, and the extent of recovery correlated with both moto r improvement and cortical 138 
UBE3A levels. Strikingly, a similar pattern of enhanced visually evo ked responses was also 139 
observed in individuals with AS, where cortical activity was assessed  using fNIRS, supporting 140 
the translational relevance of this measure across species. 141

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that visually evoked hemod ynamic responses 142 
measured using non-invasive optical imaging techniques represent a  sensitive, quantitative, 143 
and cross-species functional biomarker for monitoring disease progressio n and therapeutic 144 
efficacy in AS. 145

Results 147 
Enhanced visually evoked cortical responses persist across development in AS mice 148 
To evaluate whether visual cortical hemodynamic responses could serve as a functional 149 
biomarker of brain dysfunction in AS, we first characterized visually evoked cortical activity 150 
using IOS imaging in juvenile (postnatal day 45, P45) AS mice and their WT littermate controls. 151 
As shown in Fig. 1b, contralateral eye stimulation elicited the canonical IOS response pattern 152 
in both genotypes. However, quantitative analysis revealed th at the amplitude of visually 153 
evoked responses was significantly increased in AS mice compared to WT  controls (Fig. 1c). 154 
This alteration was comparable in male and female animals, indicating that the abnormal visual 155 
response amplitude was not sex-dependent (Fig. 1d). 156 
To determine whether these alterations persist beyond early deve lopmental stages, 157 
visually evoked IOS responses were recorded in an independent coho rt of adult mice at P90. 158 
Adult AS mice displayed a response profile similar to that observe d at P45, with significantly 159 
higher IOS amplitudes compared to WT animals in both sexes (Fig.  1e, f). Together, these 160 
findings indicate that abnormal visually evoked hemodynamic respon ses are a stable and 161 
persistent feature of the AS mouse model, supporting the robust ness of visual IOS measures 162 
as a readout of cortical processing across development. 163 
 164 
Visual cortical hemodynamic hyper-responsiveness partially reflects motor impairment 165 
in adult AS mice 166 
To determine whether altered visual cortical responses are associate d with disease severity, 167 
we examined the relationship between IOS response amplitude and behavioral performance in 168 
juvenile and adult mice 25. At both P45 and P90, AS mice exhibited the expected behavio ral 169 
impairments compared to WT littermates, including deficits in motor coordination in the rotarod 170 
test, reduced digging behavior in the marble burying assay, and decreased locomotory activity 171 
in the open field25 (Supplementary Fig. 1).  172 
In adult P90 AS mice, IOS response amplitude was inversely correlate d with rotarod 173 
performance, indicating that animals with greater visual cortical hyper-responsiveness tended 174 
to exhibit poorer motor coordination (Fig. 2a). A similar trend was observed for marble burying 175 
performance, although this association did not reach statistical  significance (Fig. 2b). No 176 
relationship was detected with open-field locomotor activity (Fig. 2c). In contrast, no significant 177 
correlations between IOS measures and behavioral performance were observed in juvenile AS 178 
mice (Supplementary Fig. 2). Together, these findings suggest th at altered visual cortical 179 
responses capture specific aspects of disease severity in adult AS mice.  Although IOS 180 
measures do not broadly predict behavioral dysfunction across all do mains, their association 181 
with motor performance supports the biological relevance of this imaging readout and indicates 182 
that it reflects functionally meaningful features of the AS phenotype. 183

Random forest can be a robust and sensitive diagnostic classifier 185 
To evaluate whether IOS imaging features alone, or in combination with behavioral measures, 186 
could reliably discriminate genotypes, we applied a Random Fo rest classifier, a machine 187 
learning approach well suited to integrate multiple variables and identify the features that best 188

contribute to classification performance (Supplementary Figure 3). T he classifier trained on 189 
combined IOS and behavioral features showed strong performance in  distinguishing WT and 190 
AS mice (n = 67). Ten-fold cross-validation confirmed robust generali zation, with accuracy 191 
ranging from 0.84 to 0.90, alongside consistently high precision, recall, and F1-score, and low 192 
variability across folds. The model achieved an AUC of 0.97, with low false positive (0.11) and 193 
false negative (0.12) rates, indicating excellent discriminative power. Statistical significance 194 
was assessed using a permutation test in which class labels were randomly shuffled (n = 1000 195 
iterations). The observed cross-validated accuracy (0.85) was markedly higher than chance, as 196 
indicated by the permutation distribution (mean = 0.5, SD = 0.07) and a significant permutation 197 
test (p = 0.001). These results confirm that model performance captu res meaningful structure 198 
in the data rather than random variation. 199 
Moreover, feature importance analysis revealed that, while behavioral variables ranked 200 
among the strongest predictors, IOS-derived measures, including peak amplitude and average 201 
response amplitude, made a substantial and independent cont ribution to classification 202 
accuracy, supporting a complementary role of neurophysiological feat ures in group 203 
discrimination. Detailed results are reported in Supplementary Table 2.  204 
This underscores the central role of imaging-based features as objective and quantitative 205 
markers that significantly enhance model performance while providin g biologically meaningful 206 
discrimination between groups. Importantly, IOS-derived measures con tribute complementary 207 
and non-redundant information to behavioral readouts, strengt hening the overall classification 208 
framework. 209

Visual IOS responses are a sensitive readout of therapeu tic efficacy in the AS mouse 211 
model 212 
To further assess the translational relevance of visually evoked IO S responses, we examined 213 
their sensitivity to therapeutic interventions previously shown to  restore UBE3A protein 214 
expression in the AS brain, including gene transfer therapy and early ASO 215 
administration5,6,11,26,27 (Fig. 3a, h). IOS recordings were performed at developmental t ime 216 
points previously identified as showing robust therapeutic eff ects for each treatment 217 
paradigm5,6. 218 
We found that perinatal gene transfer therapy fully normalize d visually evoked IOS 219 
responses in AS mice at both juvenile and adult stages (Fig. 3 b, c). Consistent with these 220 
findings, treated animals showed a significant improvement in motor coordination, with rotarod 221 
performance comparable to WT mice (Fig. 3d). Western blot analysis confirmed robust 222 
restoration of cortical UBE3A protein levels in AAV-treated AS mice, reaching over 70% of WT 223 
expression (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 4a). Importantly, IOS amplitude significantly correlated 224 
with both rotarod performance and cortical UBE3A levels (Fig. 3e, g), indicating that visual IOS 225 
measures provide a quantitative and biologically meaningful re adout of therapy-induced 226 
recovery. 227 
Similarly, administration of ASO at P1 rescued visually evoked IOS resp onses in AS 228 
mice at P35 (Fig. 3i) and significantly increased UBE3A expression l evels compared to 229 
phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-injected AS mice (Fig. 3l, Supplemen tary Fig. 4b), further 230 
demonstrating that visual cortical alterations are reversible and treatment-sensitive. Notably, 231 
IOS amplitude showed a trend toward correlation with UBE3A expression levels in ASO-treated 232 
mice, although this association did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3m). 233 
Collectively, our findings establish visually evoked IOS responses as a sensitive, robust, 234 
and treatment-responsive functional imaging biomarker of cortical d ysfunction in AS. This 235

fNIRS reveals altered hemodynamic signatures in AS individuals 239 
To evaluate the translational potential of hemodynamic responses as a functional biomarker in 240 
AS, we performed functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) during  visual stimulation in 241 
individuals with AS and neurotypical controls. The study inclu ded 59 individuals with AS (age 242 
range: 2–40 years) carrying different AS genotypes, and 45 neurot ypical controls (age range: 243 
2–48 years) (see Supplementary Table 3 for detailed demographics and genotypic information). 244 
The portability of fNIRS enabled an itinerant data collection strategy across multiple regions in 245 
Italy, implemented during events organized by the Italian AS -parent association 246 
Organizzazione Sindrome di Angelman (OR.S.A.). Within this framework, we obtained data of 247 
sufficient quality in 83% of AS participants (N= 49), support ing the feasibility of this approach 248 
in real-world settings. 249 
Because recordings were performed in a behaviorally challenging population particularly 250 
prone to movement during acquisition, we initially analyzed t he data using a previously 251 
validated standard preprocessing pipeline28,29 and subsequently implemented a more stringent 252 
approach specifically optimized for motion-artifact correction. Import antly, the main 253 
abnormalities observed in AS were consistently detected across both approaches, supporting 254 
the reproducibility of the findings. Indeed, stimulus-evoked hemodynamic responses were 255 
consistently detected in both AS participants and controls. Visual stimulation elicited increases 256 
in oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and total hemoglobin (HbT ), together with a concomitant 257 
decrease in deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) relative to baselin e, whereas no such changes 258 
were observed in the mock condition (Fig. 4,5). These findings establish that fNIRS can reliably 259 
capture cortical responses in individuals with AS. 260 
Importantly, individuals with AS exhibited a distinct and con sistently altered 261 
hemodynamic profile. Compared to controls, HbO and HbR responses were characterized by 262 
greater amplitude, broader temporal dynamics, and increased variabi lity, indicating altered 263 
neurovascular dynamics following visual stimulation (Fig. 4,5). 264 
The second, enhanced preprocessing pipeline produced cleaner sig nals with closer 265 
correspondence to the canonical hemodynamic response function (Supplementary Fig. 5a) and 266 
enabled the identification of additional abnormalities in AS response dynamics, including 267 
prolonged time-to-peak responses (Supplementary Fig. 5b), which were less evident using the 268 
standard preprocessing approach (Supplementary Fig. 6). 269 
Leveraging this improved signal fidelity, we next examined age -related effects. In 270 
neurotypical controls, HbO and HbT amplitudes showed the expected age-related attenuation, 271 
with an opposite trend for HbR (Supplementary Fig. 7a-b, 8a-b ), consistent with previous 272 
literature28,30–32. A comparable developmental profile was also observed in AS individuals when 273 
using the enhanced preprocessing pipeline (Supplementary Fig. 8). Importantly, despite these 274 
shared developmental trends, the increased response amplitude in  AS persisted across age. 275 
After normalization for age-predicted control responses, HbO values in the AS group remained 276 
significantly elevated, and directional tests of the excess-respo nse slope showed neither 277 
increase nor decrease with age (Supplementary Fig. 8c), indicatin g that this enhanced 278 
hemodynamic response represents a stable, disease-specific feature. 279 
Together, these findings establish fNIRS as a robust and scala ble translational 280 
biomarker of cortical dysfunction in AS, directly bridging preclinica l and human studies and 281 
supporting its use as an objective outcome measure in clinical trials. 282

Discussion 284 
Angelman syndrome is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder characterized  by profound 285 
cognitive and motor impairments, for which the development of re liable biomarkers remains a 286 
critical unmet need. In this context, the visual system has emerged  as a powerful model to 287 
probe cortical dysfunction in neurodevelopmental disorders, providi ng quantitative and 288 
translational readouts across species. Previous studies have demonstrated that visual evoked 289 
potentials (VEPs) capture neural alterations in both animal model s and patients with Rett 290 
syndrome and CDKL5 deficiency disorder 18–20, while emerging evidence indicates that visual 291 
neurovascular coupling can serve as a sensitive marker of cortical processin g in CDKL5 292 
deficiency20 and Creatine Transporter Deficiency21. 293 
Here, we show that visually evoked hemodynamic activity is markedly al tered in AS 294 
mice. Increased IOS responses were consistently observed across development al stages, 295 
indicating that this phenotype is stable over time and suppo rting its use as a biomarker in AS. 296 
Although AS is primarily characterized by severe motor impairments, we focused on the visual 297 
cortex because visually evoked IOS responses provide a robust and high ly translational 298 
measure of cortical function. Unlike motor cortex recordings, which re quire stereotyped motor 299 
behaviors that are difficult to standardize, visual stimulation can be precisely controlled in both 300 
mice and humans, enabling direct cross-species comparisons. The absence of sex-dependent 301 
differences further suggests that these alterations represent a co re genotype-driven 302 
feature25,33,34. Importantly, rather than reflecting a simple increase in activa tion, our data 303 
indicate a qualitatively distinct neurovascular state in AS, pointing to fundamental alterations in 304 
cortical processing.  305 
At the mechanistic level, these abnormalities likely arise from th e convergence of 306 
metabolic and circuit-level dysfunctions. IOS signals reflect activit y-dependent changes in 307 
metabolic demand and neurovascular coupling, suggesting that a lterations in cellular 308 
metabolism may directly contribute to the observed phenotype. Exten sive evidence in AS 309 
models demonstrates widespread impairments in cellular energy metabolism, particularly within 310 
the hippocampus. These include increased levels of reactive oxygen  species (ROS) in CA1 311 
pyramidal neurons, associated with impaired long-term potentiati on (LTP) and memory 312 
deficits35, as well as abnormal mitochondrial morphology, reduced oxidative  phosphorylation 313 
complex III activity36, and defects in autophagy and protein metabolism 37. Our findings extend 314 
these metabolic alterations beyond the hippocampus to cortical regions, including the visual 315 
cortex, indicating that disrupted metabolic support may broadly impair neurovascular coupling. 316 
Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress have also been reported at embryonic 317 
stages in AS mouse models38,39, raising the possibility that neurovascular deficits may emerge 318 
early during development. In parallel circuit-level abnormalitie s, particularly involving 319 
GABAergic interneurons and excitation–inhibition imbalance40, are likely to further contribute to 320 
altered hemodynamic responses in AS animals. Synaptic and network dysfunctions have been 321 
reported across multiple brain regions in AS mice, including hippocampal and cortical areas40–322 
43. Within the visual cortex, maternal loss of Ube3a impairs experience-dependent visual 323 
cortical plasticity 23,24 and disrupts processing in higher visual areas 44. These alterations are 324 
associated with neuron-type-specific synaptic alterations that pref erentially affect GABAergic 325 
interneurons, leading to an imbalance between excitation and inhibition within cortical circuits45. 326 
Reduced inhibitory input in cortical layer 2/3 increases the exci tability of pyramidal neurons, 327 
thereby elevating activity-dependent metabolic demand during sensory stimulation. This 328 
provides a plausible mechanistic connection between circuit dysfun ction and the exaggerated 329 

8 
 
hemodynamic responses observed in AS mice during IOS recordings 46,47. Within this 330 
framework, adenosine may act as a key metabolic sensor 48 linking metabolic and synaptic 331 
dysfunctions to the observed hemodynamic alterations. Consistent with this view, dysregulation 332 
of adenosine signaling has been implicated in AS patholog y. Increased density of adenosine 333 
A2A receptors has been reported in the hippocampus of AS mice, a nd pharmacological 334 
blockade of these receptors rescues motor deficits, synaptic alterations in cerebellar and striatal 335 
circuits, and hippocampal memory impairments49,50. Together, these data suggest that altered 336 
adenosine signalling may represent a mechanistic bridge between  metabolic dysfunction, 337 
circuit-level alterations, and impaired neurovascular coupling in AS. 338 
To further assess the utility of this biomarker, we examined its relationship with disease 339 
severity and its responsiveness to therapeutic interventions. Correla tions between visually 340 
evoked responses and behavioral impairments emerged in adult mice but were not detected at 341 
juvenile stages. Moreover, these associations were restricted to speci fic behavioral domains, 342 
suggesting that visually evoked responses do not provide a direct surrogate of overall 343 
behavioral performance. Rather, they appear to capture distinct aspects of circuit dysfunction 344 
that are only partially reflected by behavioral measures. This observation is consistent with the 345 
multifaceted nature of the AS phenotype, in which individu al behavioral measures reflect the 346 
integrated function of multiple neural systems. In contrast, IO S imaging provides a direct and 347 
quantitative assessment of cortical function, enabling the detection of circuit-level abnormalities 348 
that may not be fully represented by any single behavioral out come. Consistent with this 349 
interpretation, our machine-learning analysis identified imagi ng-derived features as major 350 
contributors to classification performance, supporting the notion that visually evoked responses 351 
capture disease-relevant information that is complementary to conven tional behavioral 352 
measures. 353 
 Crucially, we also found that these imaging measures are highly sensitive to therapeutic 354 
interventions. Restoration of UBE3A expression through either ge ne transfer therapy or ASO-355 
mediated paternal allele reactivation normalized visually evoked responses in transgenic mice 356 
and scaled with both behavioral recovery and molecular rescue, ind icating that hemodynamic 357 
signals directly reflect treatment efficacy. The correlation betwe en IOS normalization, motor 358 
improvement, and molecular rescue indicates that visually evoked respo nses capture 359 
functionally relevant aspects of brain recovery. Given the widespread reinstatement of UBE3A 360 
expression achieved by both gene transfer therapy and ASO treatmen t5,6, visual cortex IOS 361 
responses reflect global cortical rescue rather than local circuit-speci fic effects. These 362 
properties position neurovascular imaging as a particularly suitab le candidate biomarker for 363 
tracking target engagement and functional rescue in preclinical  therapeutic studies at both 364 
juvenile and adult stages. 365 
Building on these robust preclinical findings, we demonstrate that a comparable 366 
neurovascular signature can be reliably detected in AS individua ls using fNIRS. Despite the 367 
substantial challenges associated with neuroimaging in this p opulation, we obtained high-368 
quality recordings in the majority of participants under real-worl d conditions, representing a 369 
marked improvement over previous reports 51. These results strongly support the feasibility of 370 
fNIRS as a practical, scalable, and clinically applicable tool f or probing cortical function in AS. 371 
Importantly, the successful implementation of an itinerant acquisition strategy further highlights 372 
the adaptability of fNIRS to decentralized and real-world cl inical settings. The persistence of 373 
enhanced hemodynamic response across age indicates that this alteration represents a stable 374 
feature of AS rather than a transient developmental effect. This stability represents a critical 375 
requirement for biomarker development and qualification in clinical contexts. Our findings also 376 

9 
 
align with previous reports describing hypersensitive sensory phenotype in AS52–55, suggesting 377 
that the amplified hemodynamic response may represent a physiologi cal correlate of altered 378 
sensory processing and integration.  379 
Importantly, the core disease-associated alterations were robustly d etected across 380 
independent preprocessing strategies, demonstrating that the o bserved neurovascular 381 
phenotype is not driven by analytical choices or residual motion-re lated confounds. The 382 
implementation of a more stringent preprocessing pipeline increased signal fidelity and enabled 383 
the resolution of additional abnormalities in response dynamics, revealing subtle physiological 384 
alterations that were not fully captured by standard preprocessing approaches.  385 
Together, our findings establish visually evoked hemodynamic activit y as a robust, 386 
reproducible, and translationally relevant biomarker of cortical dysfunction associated with AS 387 
across both preclinical and human studies. This cross-species convergence  positions 388 
neurovascular imaging as a powerful translational framework of di rectly bridging mechanistic 389 
studies in animal models with clinical investigations in patients. Importantly, the scalability, non-390 
invasiveness, and demonstrated feasibility of fNIRS in real-world  settings, combined with the 391 
sensitivity of analogous measures to therapeutic rescue in the AS mouse model, support its 392 
near-term applicability as an objective biomarker for clinical trials. Its practical ease of use and 393 
adaptability further strengthen its potential for longitud inal monitoring and therapeutic 394 
assessment in AS.  395

Material and methods 397 
Animals 398 
We used mice carrying a deletion of the maternal Ube3a gene (Ube3ap+/m-)56 on a C57BL/6J 399 
background. To maintain the colony, Ube3ap-/m+ males were first crossed with WT females. 400 
Then, experimental heterozygous animals ( Ube3ap+/m-; hereafter referred to as AS) were 401 
generated by crossing Ube3ap-/m+ females with WT males. Ube3ap+/m+ WT littermates were used 402 
as control. Genotyping was performed as previously described56. Mice were housed in groups 403 
of 2-5 per cage under controlled environmental conditions (tempe rature 21±1°C, humidity 404 
60±10%) with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All experiments 405 
were conducted in compliance with the directive of the European Communities Council 63/2010 406 
and approved by the Italian Ministry of Health (authorization number 582/2021-PR). 407

Experimental design for the mouse study 409

Surgical preparation and IOS recordings were performed as previously d escribed21,58. Briefly, 426 
mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane (1-3%), and a thin layer of cyanoacrylate was applied 427 
to the exposed skull to secure a custom-made metal ring (9 mm intern al diameter) over the 428 
binocular visual cortex. IOS recordings were conducted under light isoflurane anesthesia (0.5-429 
1%) supplemented with chlorprothixene (1.5 mg/Kg, i.p.). Images were acquired using a custom 430 
microscope (Leica Microsystem) under red light illumination (630 nm) provi ded by eight 431 
individually addressable LEDs (WS2812) mounted on the objective (Leica Z6 APO coupled with 432 
a Leica PanApo 2.0X 10447170) using a custom 3D-printed conical  holder 59. Mice were 433 
secured under the objective using a ring-shaped magnet integrat ed into an Arduino-based 434 
imaging chamber. Visual stimuli were generated using MATLAB Psychto olbox and presented 435 
on a monitor positioned 15 cm from the mouse’s eye. Sinusoidal g ratings (spatial frequency 436 
0.03 cycles/degree; mean luminance 20 cd/m2; contrast 90%) were presented in the binocular 437 
visual field. The stimulus consisted of abrupt contrast reversals at 4 Hz. Images were acquired 438 
at 30 frames/s with a resolution of 512 x 512 pixels, and resp onses were averaged across at 439 
least 80 trials. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined from t he mean contralateral eye 440 
response by selecting pixels corresponding to the lowest 30% ΔR/ R values within the range 441 
between maximal and minimal intensity. IOS amplitude was quan tified as the mean signal 442 
intensity within the ROI. Because neuronal activation induce s a decrease in reflectance, IOS 443 
amplitudes are intrinsically negative. Therefore, inverted values of the mean IOS amplitude are 444 
reported for clarity, with larger values corresponding to stronger visual responses. 445

Experimental design for human study 528 
The experimental paradigm was adapted from Mazziotti et al. 28, with minor methodological 529 
refinements. Hemodynamic responses were recorded while participants viewed their preferred 530 
video under both mock and stimulation conditions. In the mock condition, participants watched 531 
the video presented on a full-field background with uniform grey luminance, serving as baseline. 532 
In the stimulation condition, a high-contrast, black-and-white radial checkerboard with square-533 
wave pattern reversal was superimposed to evoke visually induced hemodynamic activity62–64. 534 
The checkerboard stimulus (5s duration) had a spatial frequency of 0.33 cycles per degree and 535 
reversed at 4 Hz. A total of 20 trials were delivered with randomized interstimulus intervals (ISI), 536 
resulting in an overall session duration of 10 minutes. Before each recording, head 537 
circumference was measured to determine the most appropriate cap size.  The cap was then 538 
positioned on the participant’s head, and a calibration p rocedure was performed to optimize 539 
optical coupling between sources and detectors. Participants were  seated 70 cm from the 540 
display monitor, and the full experimental session lasted about 20 minutes. 541

fNIRS data processing 543 
To ensure robustness of the results, fNIRS data were processed usin g two independent 544 
preprocessing pipelines. The first pipeline (Method 1) followe d the workflow described in 545 
Mazziotti et al.28. Briefly, raw light intensity signals were converted to optical density (OD), poor-546 
quality channels were excluded, motion artifacts were corrected, a nd band-pass filtering was 547 
applied to remove physiological and instrumental noise. OD sig nals were then converted into 548 
concentration changes of oxygenated (ΔHbO) and deoxygenated h emoglobin (ΔHbR) using 549 
the modified Beer–Lambert law 28,65. To enhance data reliability and minimize the influence o f 550 
extreme observations, outliers were identified and excluded using the Interquartile Range (IQR) 551 
approach. For each variable, the first (Q1) and third (Q3) quartiles were computed, and the IQR 552 
was defined as Q3 − Q1. Observations falling below Q1 − 1.5  × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR 553 
were considered outliers and excluded from subsequent analyses. 554 
The second pipeline (Method 2) followed the same workflow bu t employed a more 555 
stringent, Python-based preprocessing strategy designed to mitigate motion artifacts, which are 556 
particularly prominent in individuals with AS. Full methodolo gical details are provided in 557 
Supplementary Materials. This pipeline was developed using MNE-Python and MNE-NIRS and 558 
incorporated motion-correction procedures combining spline interpolation and wavelet filtering 559 
adapted from Homer3 into custom Python functions 66–68. Channel quality was assessed using 560 
the Scalp Coupling Index and Peak Spectral Power, as provided by the QT-NIRS framework 561 
implemented in MNE-NIRS 69. After conversion to hemoglobin concentrations, signals were 562 
band-pass filtered and further refined using correlation-based signal improvement (CBSI)70. For 563 
both pipelines, data were epoched relative to stimulus onset  and baseline-corrected. 564 

13 
 
Hemodynamic responses were obtained by averaging trials within ea ch condition over a 565 
window spanning from 1-2 s before to 20 s after stimulus onset. 566 
To optimize signal-to-noise ratio and account for anatomical vari ability, two 567 
complementary strategies were applied. In Method 1, analyses focused on the “best channel”, 568 
defined as the channel exhibiting the maximal peak response f or each chromophore 28,29. In 569 
Method 2, channels were ranked based on HbO response amplitude , and the top 50% were 570 
retained and averaged within each participant. This approach  aimed to increase sensitivity 571 
while accounting for inter-individual variability, consistent with functionally defined channel-572 
selection approaches in fNIRS, which improve sensitivity and can b enefit from the inclusion of 573 
multiple responsive channels71. From these subject-level signals, key features, including area 574 
under the curve (AUC), peak amplitude, and time-to-peak, were e xtracted for each condition. 575 
AUC was computed using trapezoidal integration. Peak amplitude was defined as the maximum 576 
response for HbO and HbT and the minimum response for HbR, consi stent with the expected 577 
polarity of the deoxygenated signal. Time to peak (TTP) was de fined as the latency of this 578 
extremum and was computed for the stimulation condition only. C oncentration measures are 579 
reported in mM, and integrated measures in mM·s. 580 
The use of two independent preprocessing strategies allowed us to directly assess the 581 
robustness of the observed effects. While Method 1 reflects stand ard practice in the field, 582 
Method 2 provides a more conservative approach tailored to populations with increased motion 583 
and variability. The convergence of results across both pipelines strengthens confidence in the 584 
physiological validity and reproducibility of the identified hemodynamic signature. 585

Statistical analysis and random forest data modelling 587 
For animal data, statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad 8.0.2. Differences 588 
between two groups were assessed using two-tailed unpaired t-t ests or Mann–Whitney tests. 589 
Comparisons involving more than two groups were analyzed using one-way or two-way ANOVA 590 
followed by the Tukey multiple comparison test. Correlations between variables were evaluated 591 
using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients. Statistical resu lts for mouse data are 592 
summarized in Supplementary Table 1. 593 
For human data, statistical analyses were performed in a Python environment using the 594 
Pingouin statistical package, along with SciPy and Matplotlib72–74. Between-group effects were 595 
initially assessed using repeated-measures ANOVAs. To further charact erize AS-related 596 
alterations, within-subject comparisons were subsequently conducted  using post hoc tests, 597 
including Welch-corrected or paired t-tests, as appropriate. Age-related effects were assessed 598 
separately within each group using Spearman correlation. Multiple comparisons were controlled 599 
using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied within each family 600 
of tests. To account for normative developmental effects, a smoot hing spline was fitted to 601 
control data to derive an age-dependent reference curve. Indivi dual responses from the AS 602 
group were then projected onto this model to estimate age-exp ected values, and deviations 603 
from these expectations were defined as age-normalized excess response s. The excess-604 
response values were tested against zero using a one-sample directi onal test, and their 605 
association with age was further evaluated using directional tests on the regression slope. 606 
Additional details about age-related analyses are provided in the Supplementary Material. 607 
Data distributions were assessed prior to analysis, and non-normall y distributed data 608 
were rank-transformed where appropriate. Statistical significance w as set at p < 0.05. Animal 609 
data are presented as mean ± SEM. For human data, representative traces are shown as mean 610 
± SEM, while cumulative results are displayed as box plots with in terquartile ranges (IQR); 611
