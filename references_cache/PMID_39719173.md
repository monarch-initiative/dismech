# Essential role of sulfide oxidation in brain health and neurological disorders.
**Authors:** Kanemaru E, Ichinose F
**Journal:** Pharmacol Ther (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.pharmthera.2024.108787](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pharmthera.2024.108787)

## Content

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is an environmental hazard well known for its neurotoxicity. In mammalian cells, H2S is predominantly generated by transsulfuration pathway enzymes. In addition, H2S produced by gut microbiome significantly contributes to the total sulfide burden in the body. Although low levels of H2S is believed to exert various physiological functions such as neurotransmission and vasomotor control, elevated levels of H2S inhibit the activity of cytochrome c oxidase (i.e., mitochondrial complex IV), thereby impairing oxidative phosphorylation. To protect the electron transport chain from respiratory poisoning by H2S, the compound is actively oxidized to form persulfides and polysulfides by a mitochondrial resident sulfide oxidation pathway. The reaction, catalyzed by sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase (SQOR), is the initial and critical step in sulfide oxidation. The persulfide species are subsequently oxidized to sulfite, thiosulfate, and sulfate by persulfide dioxygenase (ETHE1 or SDO), thiosulfate sulfurtransferase (TST), and sulfite oxidase (SUOX). While SQOR is abundantly expressed in the colon, liver, lung, and skeletal muscle, its expression is notably low in the brains of most mammals. Consequently, the brain's limited capacity to oxidize H2S renders it particularly sensitive to the deleterious effects of H2S accumulation. Impaired sulfide oxidation can lead to fatal encephalopathy, and the overproduction of H2S has been implicated in the developmental delays observed in Down syndrome. Our recent findings indicate that the brain's limited capacity to oxidize sulfide exacerbates its sensitivity to oxygen deprivation. The beneficial effects of sulfide oxidation are likely to be mediated not only by the detoxification of H2S but also by the formation of persulfide, which exerts cytoprotective effects through multiple mechanisms. Therefore, pharmacological agents designed to scavenge H2S and/or enhance persulfide levels may offer therapeutic potential against neurological disorders characterized by impaired or insufficient sulfide oxidation or excessive H2S production.

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is a colorless gas with a characteristic rotten-egg odor (Mustafa, Gadalla, & Snyder, 2009). Initially identified as an environmental hazard, research over the past two decades has revealed the role of H2S as a signaling molecule (Abe & Kimura, 1996; Cirino, Szabo, & Papapetropoulos, 2023). In mammalian cells, H2S is primarily produced via the transsulfuration pathway. Additionally, a significant amount of sulfide is absorbed from the gut, where it is produced by microbiota, with the intraluminal H2S concentrations reaching 0.2–2.4 mM (Cirino et al., 2023). At low levels (nM to low μM), H2S modulates various physiological functions, including vasodilation, angiogenesis, inflammation regulation, oxygen sensing, and cytoprotection, and enhances mitochondrial respiration as an electron donor (Lefer, 2019; Szabó, 2007; Szabo et al., 2014). However, at high concentrations (greater than 10 μM), H2S becomes toxic to aerobic organisms by inhibiting mitochondrial complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), thereby impairing oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Szabo et al., 2014).

The mitochondrial sulfide oxidation pathway prevents mitochondrial respiratory poisoning by sulfide accumulation, oxidizing H2S to persulfides (Landry, Ballou, & Banerjee, 2021). The initial step in sulfide oxidation involves the conversion of H2S to glutathione hydropersulfide (GSSH) by sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase (SQOR) in mitochondria, donating electrons to mitochondrial complex III via coenzyme Q (Landry, Ballou, & Banerjee, 2017). Persulfides are further oxidized to sulfite, thiosulfate, and sulfate by persulfide dioxygenase (SDO or ETHE1), thiosulfate sulfurtransferase (TST), and sulfite oxidase (SUOX). Therefore, the sulfide oxidation reaction catalyzed by SQOR regulates mitochondrial bioenergetics by eliminating H2S, donating electrons to the electron transport chain, and producing persulfides (Akaike et al., 2017; Landry et al., 2021). SQOR levels are low in the brain but high in the liver, heart, lung, skeletal muscle, and colon of most mammals, including humans (Linden et al., 2012) (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000137767-SQOR/tissue). Consequently, the brain’s limited capacity to oxidize H2S renders it particularly sensitive to sulfide accumulation (Marutani et al., 2021). For example, loss of ETHE1 results in fatal H2S toxicity in ethylmalonic encephalopathy (Tiranti et al., 2009). SQOR deficiency has recently been linked to Leigh syndrome-like disease, characterized by encephalopathy and brain lesions in the basal ganglia and cortex (Friederich et al., 2020). Elevated H2S levels have also been implicated in adverse effects on brain development in Down syndrome (Panagaki, Lozano-Montes, et al., 2022; Panagaki, Randi, Augsburger, & Szabo, 2019).

In addition to sulfide accumulation, the brain is highly vulnerable to oxygen deprivation. However, the mechanism underlying the brain’s sensitivity to hypoxia is not fully understood. Recent studies have shown that in mice, rats, and naturally hypoxia-tolerant ground squirrels, the brain’s sensitivity to hypoxia inversely correlates with SQOR levels and the capacity to catabolize sulfide (Marutani et al., 2021). Silencing SQOR increased the sensitivity of the brain of ground squirrels and mice to hypoxia, whereas neuron-specific SQOR expression prevented hypoxia-induced sulfide accumulation, bioenergetic failure, and ischemic brain injury in mice (Marutani et al., 2021). Excluding SQOR from mitochondria increased sensitivity to hypoxia not only in the brain but also in the heart and liver (Marutani et al., 2021). Pharmacological scavenging of sulfide maintained mitochondrial respiration in neurons in hypoxia rendering mice resistant to brain ischemia (Marutani et al., 2021). These observations underscore the protective effects of sulfide oxidation on bioenergetics and brain integrity during acute oxygen shortage.

The beneficial effects of sulfide oxidation may be mediated not only by elimination of H2S but also by oxidation of H2S to sulfane suflur species including persulfides (RSSH) and polysulfides (RSnH) (Kimura, 2020). Early studies reported plasma H2S levels as high as 20–80 μM (Whiteman & Moore, 2009). However, advanced analytical methods have subsequently revealed that free H2S levels are actually low (6–80 nM) (Filipovic, Zivanovic, Alvarez, & Banerjee, 2018; Landry et al., 2021), with a labile pool of sulfur-containing species, including persulfides and polysulfides (Ida et al., 2014; Nagy et al., 2014). Significant levels (50–100 μM) of persulfides and polysulfides have been detected in mammalian cells, tissues, and plasma (Ida et al., 2014). Sulfane suflur in persulfides (oxidation state: 0) exhibits dual reactivity as nucleophiles and electrophiles, whereas H2S (oxidation state: −2) is a fully reduced sulfur metabolite and can only be oxidized (Kimura, 2015). This unique characteristic makes persulfides versatile reactive metabolites in biological reactions (Ono et al., 2014). The cytoprotective effects of persulfides are mediated by multiple mechanisms, including enhancing mitochondria function (Akaike et al., 2017), antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (Sawa, Motohashi, Ihara, & Akaike, 2020; Zhang et al., 2019), inhibition of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis by scavenging free radicals (Barayeu et al., 2022), and post-translational protein modifications (persulfidation) (Braunstein et al., 2020; Kimura, 2015).

This review article aims to provide up-to-date information on the role of sulfur metabolism and propose sulfide oxidation as a druggable target for treating nervous system disorders. The primary focus is on the role of sulfide oxidation in brain health and neurological diseases, given the brain’s limited capacity to catabolize sulfide and its apparent vulnerability to sulfide accumulation. Mechanisms of sulfur metabolism and its impact on neurological diseases will be reviewed, and potential therapeutic strategies identified in preclinical studies will be presented.

The steady-state H2S concentration in the mammalian brain is estimated to be between 20 and 200 pmol/mg protein (Kasamatsu et al., 2021; Vitvitsky, Kabil, & Banerjee, 2012). In mammals, H2S is generated by both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms. Enzymatically, the transsulfuration pathway, which involves enzymes such as cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS), cystathionine gamma-lyase (CSE), and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (3-MST), contributes to H2S production (Figure 1) (Paul & Snyder, 2012; Shibuya et al., 2009). CBS and CSE generates H2S from cysteine (CysSH) and homocysteine, requiring pyridoxal 5’-phosphate as a cofactor (Cirino et al., 2023; Marutani & Ichinose, 2020). 3-MST catalyzes the sulfur transfer from 3-mercaptopyruvate (produced from cysteine by cysteine aminotransferase, CAT) to an active cysteine site, resulting in pyruvate and 3-MST-bound persulfide (MST-SSH). MST-SSH then reacts with thiols or thioredoxin to produce H2S (Figure 1) (Cirino et al., 2023; Landry et al., 2021).

CBS is highly expressed in the central nervous system, with varying levels across different brain regions, including the hippocampus, cerebellum, and cerebral cortex (Abe & Kimura, 1996; Cirino et al., 2023; Marutani & Ichinose, 2020). 3-MST is also highly expressed in the brain. CBS is localized to astrocytes and 3-MST found in both neurons and glia (Enokido et al., 2005; Kimura, 2024; Shibuya et al., 2009). In contrast, CSE contributes minimally to H2S production in the central nervous system (Cirino et al., 2023; Marutani & Ichinose, 2020). CBS and CSE are primarily cytosolic, but some reports suggest they can translocate to mitochondria under hypoxic stress or conditions that increase intracellular free calcium, thereby promoting mitochondrial H2S production (Fu et al., 2012; Teng et al., 2013). 3-MST is expressed in both the cytosol and mitochondria in approximately equal amounts under baseline conditions (Cirino et al., 2023).

Non-enzymatically, H2S is supplied from the acid-labile pool and the sulfane sulfur pool (Ishigami et al., 2009; Shen, Peter, Bir, Wang, & Kevil, 2012). The acid-labile pool consists of sulfur present in iron-sulfur [Fe-S] clusters within proteins and enzymes, that play roles in electron transfer, regulation of gene expression, and enzyme activities (Ishigami et al., 2009; Johnson, Dean, Smith, & Johnson, 2005). The sulfane sulfur pool includes persulfides, polysulfides, thiosulfonates (R-S(O)-S-R’), polythionates (SnO62−), thiosulfate (S2O32−), and elemental sulfur (S8) (Shen et al., 2012). Cysteinyl-tRNA synthetases (CARSs) are the principal enzymes for synthesizing cysteine hydropersulfide (CysSSH), a major persulfide, using cysteine as a substrate (Figure 1) (Akaike et al., 2017). CARS1 and CARS2 are localized in the cytosol and mitochondria, respectively, with CARS2 playing a predominant role in CysSSH production. Mutations in CARS2, identified in an epileptic encephalopathy patient, were associated with electron transport chain impairment and mitochondrial dysfunction, underscoring the essential role of CARS2 in mitochondrial bioenergetics (Coughlin et al., 2015). CysSSH is also synthesized from cystine (CysSSCys) catalyzed by CBS and CSE (Figure 1) (Ida et al., 2014). CysSSH, in turn, contributes to high levels of GSSH (as high as 150 μM in mouse brain) and other CysSSH derivatives in cells, tissues, and plasma (Ida et al., 2014). Recently, certain gut bacteria (e.g., Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceas) were found to have a high capacity to produce persulfides, utilizing cystine as a substrate to produce CysSSH (Figure 1) (Uchiyama, Akiyama, Hase, Kumagai, & Kim, 2022). H2S is produced by the reduction of persulfides and polysulfides, which are more abundant than H2S in mammals (Ida et al., 2014).

The gut microbiome is a major exogenous source of H2S in mammals. Shen and colleagues reported that germ-free mice showed a 60% reduction in free H2S and a 50–80% reduction in persulfides (Shen et al., 2013). Various gut bacteria, including sulfate- and sulfite-reducing bacteria and Clostridia species (sensitive to metronidazole), produce H2S (Linden, 2014; Viscomi et al., 2010). Most H2S is produced from sulfur-containing amino acids, such as cysteine and methionine, in both host tissues and gut microbiome. Enterococcus, Enterobacteriaceae, and Clostridia species degrade these sulfur-containing amino acids using desulfhydrases, producing H2S in the intestine (Cirino et al., 2023). Flannigan and colleagues demonstrated that colonic cells and bacteria were unable to produce H2S in media lacking methionine and L-cysteine, indicating the necessity of these amino acids for H2S production (Flannigan, McCoy, & Wallace, 2011). Recently, it was shown that treatment with metronidazole or a sulfur-restricted diet reduced H2S levels in feces and host tissues (including brain, liver, and muscle) in a mouse model of Leigh syndrome (SqorΔN/ΔN mice) (Kanemaru et al., 2024). Reduced H2S levels were associated with the prevention of SqorΔN/ΔN mice from developing neurological symptoms and abnormal energy metabolism caused by respiratory poisoning due to sulfide accumulation, highlighting the gut microbiome as a significant source of H2S (Kanemaru et al., 2024).

There is a suggested link between H2S-producing gut bacteria and neurological diseases. For example, Deslfovibrio, a H2S-producing bacterium, is associated with increased susceptibility to Alzheimer’s disease (Guo, Zhang, Tang, Chong, & Li, 2023). Deslfovibrio is also significantly elevated in patients with Parkinson’s disease, with its abundance correlating with disease severity (Nie et al., 2023). Worms fed Deslfovibrio isolated from the feces of patients with Parkinson’s disease showed more and larger α-synuclein aggregates and higher mortality compared to worms fed Deslfovibrio from healthy individuals (Huynh, Takala, Murros, Diwedi, & Saris, 2023). Additionally, Sutterella, another H2S-producing gut bacterium, is increased in Down syndrome patients, with its abundance significantly correlated with worse neuropsychiatric test scores assessing maladaptive behaviors (Biagi et al., 2014). These findings suggest that increased H2S production by gut bacteria may contribute to the pathogenesis of these neurological diseases.

Sulfide is catabolized through the canonical sulfide oxidation pathway in mitochondria and other nonenzymatic mechanisms including oxidation by globin molecules. It has also been suggested that copper/zinc superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) in cytoplasm may catabolize sulfide.

In mitochondria, H2S undergoes sequential oxidation by a series of mitochondrial enzymes constituting the sulfide oxidation pathway. The pathway comprises SQOR, ETHE1, SUOX, and TST. The initial and committing step involves the conversion of H2S to GSSH by SQOR, a flavoenzyme anchored to the inner mitochondrial membrane (Figure 1) (Landry et al., 2017). Glutathione (GSH) is considered the physiological sulfur-acceptor, leading to the formation of GSSH (Libiad, Yadav, Vitvitsky, Martinov, & Banerjee, 2014). Subsequently, ETHE1 converts GSSH to GSH and sulfite in an oxygen-dependent reaction. Sulfite is further oxidized to sulfate by SUOX and to thiosulfate by TST (Figure 1) (Szabo et al., 2014). Thiosulfate can be reverted to H2S through catalysis by 3-MST and TST (Libiad, Sriraman, & Banerjee, 2015). Simultaneously, during the conversion of H2S to GSSH by SQOR, electrons are transferred from H2S to mitochondrial complex III via coenzyme Q, enhancing the mitochondrial membrane potential and promoting ATP production (Figure 1). The enzymatic oxidation of one H2S molecule requires the consumption of 0.75 O2 molecules (0.5 by ETHE1 and 0.25 by complex III) (Szabo et al., 2014; Wilkie, Borland, Carter, Morton, & Selman, 2021).

SQOR is highly expressed in the liver, heart, lung, skeletal muscle, and colon, but its expression is low in the brain (Linden et al., 2012). ETHE1 also shows high expression in the colon, but low levels in the brain (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000105755-ETHE1/tissu). Although SUOX and TST are expressed in various tissues, including the liver and colon, their expression in the brain is limited. (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000139531-SUOX/tissue, and https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000128311-TST/tissue).

Deficiency in sulfide oxidation enzymes leads to sulfide accumulation, contributing to disease pathogenesis. SQOR and ETHE1 deficiencies cause Leigh syndrome and ethylmalonic encephalopathy, respectively (discussed in later sections) (Friederich et al., 2020; Tiranti et al., 2009). SUOX deficiency results in neurological abnormalities, including neuronal necrosis, brain atrophy, and cognitive impairment, and often early death (Garrett, Johnson, Graf, Feigenbaum, & Rajagopalan, 1998; Kožich et al., 2022; Mellis et al., 2021; Shih et al., 1977). TST deficiency leads to high circulating sulfide, increased gluconeogenesis, hypertriglyceridemia, and fatty liver (Carter et al., 2021). TST expression levels in human adipose tissue positively correlate with insulin sensitivity and negatively with fat mass, suggesting TST activation may benefit type 2 diabetes (Morton et al., 2016).

Thiosulfate and sulfate, the end products of sulfide oxidation pathway, are excreted in urine (Wilkie et al., 2021). Serum levels of thiosulfate are around 0.6 μM, while sulfate levels are significantly higher, at approximately 300 μM (Cole & Evrovski, 1997; Landry et al., 2021). In urine, the concentrations of thiosulfate and sulfate are around 9 μM and 17 mM, respectively (Cole & Evrovski, 1996; Cole, Evrovski, & Pirone, 1995). SUOX deficiency significantly increases urinary thiosulfate levels, reported to be around 2 mM (Cole & Evrovski, 1996; Garrett et al., 1998). Elevated urinary thiosulfate levels are also observed in ethylmalonic encephalopathy patients compared to healthy controls (Kožich et al., 2022; Tiranti et al., 2009). Thiosulfate levels in blood and urine increase following acute H2S exposure, indicating its potential as a biomarker of H2S poisoning (Kage, Takekawa, Kurosaki, Imamura, & Kudo, 1997). The severity of the clinical diseases and the extent of sulfide accumulation due to genetic mutations in sulfide oxidation enzymes highlight the essential role of enzymatic sulfide oxidation in preventing systemic sulfide accumulation.

H2S is oxidized by ferric heme proteins, including hemoglobin, myoglobin, and neuroglobin. Red blood cells produce H2S but lack mitochondria and thus cannot utilize the mitochondrial sulfide oxidation pathway. Vitvitsky and colleagues discovered that ferric hemoglobin (methemoglobin) binds and oxidizes H2S to a mixture of thiosulfate and polysulfides in red blood cells (Jensen & Fago, 2018; Vitvitsky, Yadav, Kurthen, & Banerjee, 2015). Methemoglobin, formed by the auto-oxidation of ferrous hemoglobin, represents 1–3% of total hemoglobin, with concentrations significantly higher than circulating H2S levels (Vitvitsky et al., 2015). Given that red blood cells constitute about 50% of blood volume, they play a critical role in maintaining physiologically low levels of circulating H2S. Myoglobin, designed to bind oxygen to ferrous iron, similarly oxidizes H2S to thiosulfate and polysulfides under aerobic conditions (Bostelaar et al., 2016; Jensen & Fago, 2018). Neuroglobin, a globin enriched in the brain, binds H2S tightly and oxidizes it, modulating neuronal sensitivity to sulfide accumulation (Brittain, Yosaatmadja, & Henty, 2008). However, Ruetz and colleagues reported that the H2S reactivity of neuroglobin’s coordinately saturated heme is lower than that of hemoglobin and myoglobin due to the distal histidine ligand (Ruetz et al., 2017).

Copper/zinc superoxide dismutase 1 is a highly expressed, ubiquitous copper and zinc-containing protein in cytoplasm. In addition to its function as a superoxide oxidoreductase, it has been reported that SOD1 may detoxify endogenous and exogenous sulfide to sulfate (Switzer, Kasamatsu, Ihara, & Eaton, 2023). As H2S is in equilibrium with various reactive sulfur species, SOD1 seems to regulate cellular reactive sulfur species levels. The antioxidant effect of SOD1 may be partially mediated by the regulation of cellular H2S and reactive sulfur species.

Sulfide and persulfide play critical roles in mitochondrial bioenergetics. Sulfide stimulates the oxygen consumption rate through the electron transport chain via SQOR at low concentrations but inhibits OXPHOS by blocking complex IV at higher concentrations (> 10 μM) (Lagoutte et al., 2010; Szabo et al., 2014). Akaike and colleagues proposed that CysSSH produced by CARS2 in mitochondria might accept an electron from the electron transport chain to release sulfide, which is then oxidized by SQOR to produce ATP via OXPHOS (Figure 1) (Akaike et al., 2017). The relative concentrations of SQOR and complex IV are likely to be cell-type dependent. Because SQOR has a higher affinity for H2S than complex IV, the expression level of SQOR influences the threshold concentration at which sulfide switches from activating to inhibiting OXPHOS (Kumar & Banerjee, 2021).

The brain, with its high metabolic demand, relies heavily on OXPHOS for ATP production (Erecińska & Silver, 1989, 1994; Yellen, 2018). Sulfide oxidation enzymes, including SQOR, ETHE1, SUOX, and TST, are expressed at low levels in the brains of most mammals (Linden et al., 2012). This limited capacity for sulfide oxidation makes the brain particularly sensitive to sulfide accumulation, which inhibits OXPHOS (Lagoutte et al., 2010). For example, SQOR deficiency causes Leigh syndrome, characterized by encephalopathy, brain lesions, and abnormal energy metabolism due to sulfide accumulation (Friederich et al., 2020). Similarly, ETHE1 deficiency results in fatal sulfide toxicity in ethylmalonic encephalopathy (Tiranti et al., 2009). These findings highlight the brain’s vulnerability to sulfide accumulation.

SQOR activity is linked to the CoQ pool, intersecting sulfur metabolism with other metabolic pathways. Sulfide oxidation by SQOR requires CoQ, potentially perturbing major redox nodes (NAD+/NADH, FAD/FADH2, and CoQ/CoQH2) that converge at the electron transport chain (Kumar & Banerjee, 2021). Excess sulfide depletes the oxidized CoQ pool and, together with complex IV inhibition, induces a reductive shift in these redox couples, reduces the proton motive force, and decreases ATP (Figure 2) (Kumar & Banerjee, 2021). Reductive stress from excess sulfide increases aerobic glycolysis, glutamine-dependent reductive carboxylation, and lipogenesis (Hanna, Kumar, & Banerjee, 2023). SQOR knockdown cells, more sensitive to complex IV inhibition, exhibit a greater reductive shift in the NAD+/NADH ratio and upregulate aerobic glycolysis at lower sulfide concentrations (Libiad et al., 2019; Vitvitsky et al., 2021). This suggests that CoQ-dependent metabolic pathways are readily affected by reductive stress in the brain due to its low SQOR expression.

Unlike other tissues, the colon epithelium is exposed to high H2S concentrations (0.2–2.4 mM) from the gut microbiome (Deplancke et al., 2003). H2S diffuses across cellular membranes without a specialized transporter (Mathai et al., 2009), and the colon epithelium acts as a metabolic barrier, limiting H2S entry into the host (Landry et al., 2021). Although adaptive mechanisms in the colon epithelium to counter high H2S exposure are incompletely understood, colon epithelial cells possess a sulfide oxidation pathway to withstand high H2S levels (Levitt, Furne, Springfield, Suarez, & DeMaster, 1999). Sulfide oxidation enzymes are apically localized in human colonic crypts at the host-microbiome interface (Libiad et al., 2019). Notably, SQOR expression in the colon is significantly higher than in the brain. H2S at concentrations below 20 μM stimulates oxygen consumption in colon epithelial cells, but higher concentrations (> 30 μM) inhibit oxygen consumption by blocking complex IV (Libiad et al., 2019). SQOR ablation causes H2S poisoning in colon epithelial cells at concentrations as low as 5 μM, indicating that SQOR is a critical respiratory shield against H2S poisoning in the colon (Libiad et al., 2019).

In the canonical operation of the electron transport chain, O2 serves as a terminal electron acceptor and is converted to H2O at complex IV, with approximately 90% of the body’s oxygen consumption occurring in this reaction. Recently, Kumar and colleagues reported that under sulfide poisoning (complex IV is inhibited), colonocytes utilize fumarate as an alternate terminal electron acceptor to remove sulfide (Kumar et al., 2022). A new redox circuitry is established between SQOR and complex II: SQOR reduces CoQ while oxidizing H2S, and complex II in reverse mode regenerates CoQ from CoQH2 by reducing fumarate (Figure 2). Mice with targeted deletion of complex II in intestinal epithelial cells showed a significant reduction in sulfide oxidation (Kumar et al., 2022). When complex II and /or fumarate are limited, electron transfer from SQOR to O2 might represent an alternative sulfide oxidation route, generating H2O2 (Hanna et al., 2023).

When OXPHOS is inhibited, unused O2 accumulates, which can be directly toxic. Increased levels of O2 and NADH contribute to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Hirst, King, & Pryde, 2008; Kussmaul & Hirst, 2006). Jain and colleagues found that Ndufs4 knockout mice, a model of complex I deficiency and Leigh syndrome, exhibited brain hyperoxia due to decreased oxygen consumption (Jain et al., 2019). In patients with mitochondrial disease, brain oxygen consumption is decreased (Lindroos et al., 2009). Intriguingly, decreased O2 delivery to the brain, such as through chronic hypoxic exposure (11% O2), breathing carbon monoxide, or anemia, normalized elevated brain O2 levels and reversed neurological disease in Ndufs4 knockout mice, suggesting unused O2 as a likely culprit in Leigh syndrome pathology (Jain et al., 2019; Jain et al., 2016). Similarly, SqorΔN/ΔN mice, another model of Leigh syndrome caused by SQOR gene mutations, exhibited brain hyperoxia probably due to complex IV inhibition by increased H2S levels (Kanemaru et al., 2024). ETHE1 knockout mice showed significantly decreased O2 consumption rates in tissues compared to wild-type littermates (Tiranti et al., 2009). These findings suggest that oxygen toxicity in the brain under excess sulfide is due to the brain’s limited capacity to oxidize sulfide, contributing to sulfide toxicity (Figure 2).

The primary fuel source for colon epithelial cells is butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid produced by gut microbiome via fermentation of insoluble fiber remnants (den Besten et al., 2013; Hamer et al., 2008). The first step in butyrate oxidation is the oxidation of butyryl-CoA to crotonyl-CoA, catalyzed by butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase, a flavoenzyme in the mitochondrial matrix (den Besten et al., 2013). Electrons from butyrate oxidation relay to electron-transferring flavoprotein (ETF), which then reduces ETF dehydrogenase in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where electrons enter the CoQ pool (Landry et al., 2021). Since sulfide oxidation by SQOR also drives electrons into the CoQ pool, high levels of sulfide exposure can interfere with energy production for colon epithelial cells (Landry et al., 2021). For instance, in ulcerative colitis, sulfide clearance in colonocytes is defective, and butyrate oxidation is significantly decreased (Babidge, Millard, & Roediger, 1998). These results indicate that although the colon tolerates sulfide accumulation, exposure to sulfide levels above the catabolic capacity of colon epithelial cells can reduce energy production and cause cellular damage.

Due to its limited capacity to oxidize H2S, the brain is particularly vulnerable to the detrimental effects of H2S accumulation. An imbalance between sulfide synthesis and catabolism can disrupt the bioenergetic function of neurons, contributing to the onset and progression of both congenital and acquired neurological disorders (Figure 3). This disruption can lead to a cascade of adverse effects, including impaired mitochondrial function, increased oxidative stress, and altered neuronal signaling, ultimately manifesting in various neurological pathologies.

Down syndrome results from trisomy of human chromosome 21 and is the most common chromosomal disorder, occurring in approximately 1 in 700 live births. This condition presents with various clinical symptoms, including intellectual disability, craniofacial dysmorphism, and congenital heart defects, attributable to the over 200 protein-coding genes on chromosome 21 (Antonarakis et al., 2020). Among the biochemical abnormalities associated with Down syndrome, mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired cellular bioenergetics are prominent (Pecze, Randi, & Szabo, 2020). One of the genes on chromosome 21 is CBS, which is overexpressed in Down syndrome, leading to the overproduction of H2S (Szabo, 2020). Kamoun hypothesized that this excessive H2S production could cause metabolic poisoning, partly by inhibiting mitochondrial complex IV (Kamoun, 2001).

Pecze and colleagues conducted a meta-analysis of bioenergetic in Down syndrome using data from 41 published reports and associated databases. They found that Down syndrome patients exhibited suppressed mitochondrial OXPHOS and Krebs cycle functions, alongside upregulated glycolysis, mimicking cellular responses to hypoxia despite adequate oxygen supply (Pecze et al., 2020). They further demonstrated that high H2S levels in fibroblasts from Down syndrome patients suppressed mitochondrial electron transport chain activity, oxygen consumption, and ATP production, leading to poor cell proliferation, increased ROS generation, and mitochondrial DNA damage (Panagaki, Pecze, Randi, Nieminen, & Szabo, 2022; Panagaki et al., 2019). Pharmacological inhibition of CBS with aminooxyacetate or siRNA-mediated CBS silencing normalized H2S levels, restored complex IV activity, improved ATP production and mitochondrial DNA integrity, and enhanced cell proliferation (Panagaki, Pecze, et al., 2022; Panagaki et al., 2019). In a rat model of Down syndrome, electrical activity in the brain (specifically γ oscillations) was significantly decreased, along with a significantly decreased synaptic protein expression, and social recognition memory defects were observed (Panagaki, Lozano-Montes, et al., 2022). Treatment with aminooxyacetate restored the decreased synaptic protein expression, γ activity, and social recognition memory (Panagaki, Lozano-Montes, et al., 2022). Moreover, aminooxyacetate restored Krebs cycle activity in Down syndrome fibroblasts by inhibiting CBS (Panagaki, Pecze, et al., 2022). Although H2S levels were not measured in these experiments, these results suggest that the CBS-derived overproduction of H2S may be responsible for the neurological impairments of Down syndrome. A recent study also reported higher levels of 3-MST in fibroblasts from Down syndrome patients, suggesting that H2S produced by 3-MST might also contribute to mitochondrial impairments (Panagaki, Randi, & Szabo, 2020).

Derry and colleagues reported that in lymphocytes from Down syndrome patients, poly(ethylene glycol) oxidized activated charcoal, which oxidizes H2S to persulfides, increased H2S catabolism and improved impaired cell proliferation (Derry et al., 2023). In a mouse model of Down syndrome, this treatment also restored impaired osteoclast differentiation associated with low bone mass and increased fracture risk, common complications in Down syndrome (Derry et al., 2023). These findings suggest that enhancing H2S catabolism to reduce H2S accumulation could be a promising therapeutic approach for Down syndrome.

Leigh syndrome is an inherited metabolic disorder that affects the central nervous system, recognized as the most common mitochondrial disease in children (Lake, Compton, Rahman, & Thorburn, 2016; Walker, Miranda, Allred, & Mootha, 2022). It involves significant genetic diversity, with over 80 genes linked to the condition (Walker et al., 2022), most of which encode proteins essential for mitochondrial energy production (Lake et al., 2016; Walker et al., 2022). Patients generally appear healthy at birth, but soon experience psychomotor retardation and neurological regression, often triggered by fasting or infection, usually within the first year of life. Typically, children often succumb within 2–3 years (Lake et al., 2016; Rahman et al., 1996). Radiological assessment reveals bilateral, symmetrical lesions in the brainstem and basal ganglia with gliosis, which appear hyperintense signals on T2-weighted MRI. The biochemical profile shows abnormal energy metabolism, indicated by elevated lactic acid levels (Lake et al., 2016; Walker et al., 2022).

In 2020, mutations in the gene encoding SQOR were identified in three patients, who exhibited low SQOR protein levels and significantly reduced SQOR enzyme activity. They had reduced complex IV activity, despite normal complex IV protein levels (Friederich et al., 2020). The patients appeared to be healthy at birth, but developed encephalopathy and metabolic acidosis, with increased levels of lactic acid, after episodes of gastroenteritis and prolonged fasting (Friederich et al., 2020). MRI scans revealed T2-hyperintense lesions in the basal ganglia and brainstem. Two patients died at ages 4 and 8, while the third survived despite recurrent episodes of encephalopathy (Friederich et al., 2020). The authors hypothesized that impaired H2S catabolism, due to low SQOR levels, contributed to the development of Leigh disease in these patients (Friederich et al., 2020).

To explore the pathogenetic mechanisms responsible for Leigh syndrome induced by mutations in the gene encoding SQOR, we studied SqorΔN/ΔN mice, in which SQOR was excluded from mitochondria (Kanemaru et al., 2024). SqorΔN/ΔN mice recapitulated the clinical characteristics of Leigh syndrome, including brain lesions, hyperlactacidemia, progressive motor dysfunction, and shortened lifespan, mirroring the human condition (Kanemaru et al., 2024). These mice also exhibited an integrated stress response and reductive stress (Kanemaru et al., 2024), which are often linked to mitochondrial dysfunction (Derisbourg, Hartman, & Denzel, 2021; Mick et al., 2020; R. Sharma et al., 2021). Neuropathological examination showed neuronal loss and gliosis with microglia and astrocyte activation in the brain lesions, which were identified by MRI (Kanemaru et al., 2024). In addition, SqorΔN/ΔN mice suffered diffuse vascular damage with acute and sub-acute hemorrhages in the brain, possibly due to H2S accumulation, which is toxic to endothelial cells (Kanemaru et al., 2024). The pathogenesis of Leigh syndrome caused by the exclusion of SQOR from mitochondria appeared to involve the inhibition of complex IV activity by H2S accumulation from impaired H2S catabolism (Kanemaru et al., 2024). Recently, two separate groups have reported successful generation of SQOR knockout mice, which showed similar, if not identical, phenotype with SqorΔN/ΔN mice (Griffiths, Wang, Jonas, & Levy, 2024; Jia et al., 2020). Taken together, these observations indicate the essential role of mitochondrial SQOR in preventing systemic H2S buildup.

To explore possible treatment strategy for Leigh syndrome induced by SQOR mutations, we investigated effects of measures to decrease systemic sulfide load. Daily treatment with metronidazole decreased H2S levels in feces, alleviated tissue H2S accumulation, normalized complex IV activity and blood lactate levels, attenuated the reductive stress, and prolonged the survival of SqorΔN/ΔN mice (Kanemaru et al., 2024). Similarly, a sulfur-restricted diet produced comparable effects (Kanemaru et al., 2024). These results suggest that administration of metronidazole or a sulfur-restricted diet may be therapeutic options for patients with Leigh syndrome caused by SQOR mutations.

Ethylmalonic encephalopathy is an autosomal recessive and fatal disease characterized by early-onset encephalopathy, developmental delay, microangiopathy, and chronic diarrhea. It is considered a sub-type of Leigh syndrome caused by ETHE1 deficiency (Tiranti et al., 2009). Patients with ethylmalonic encephalopathy exhibit defective complex IV activity, increased levels of lactic acid, high concentrations of C4 and C5 acylcarnitines in blood, and high urinary excretion of ethylmalonic acid (Tiranti et al., 2009). The pathogenesis of ethylmalonic encephalopathy involves the inhibition of complex IV activity by H2S accumulation (Di Meo et al., 2011; Tiranti et al., 2009), leading to diffuse vascular damage, including acute and sub-acute hemorrhages in the brain and colonic mucosa (Giordano et al., 2012).

Treatment for ethylmalonic encephalopathy primarily targets modulation of H2S-producing gut bacteria using metronidazole (Lake et al., 2016; Viscomi et al., 2010). N-acetylcysteine, a precursor of glutathione, is also administered to reduce H2S levels by facilitating sulfur transfer via SQOR (Ersoy, Tiranti, & Zeviani, 2020; Lake et al., 2016; Viscomi et al., 2010). In addition, a sulfur-restricted diet was reported to improve biochemical parameters and clinical outcomes in a child with ethylmalonic encephalopathy (Boyer et al., 2018). Although these palliative therapies are beneficial, they are far from being curative for patients with ethylmalonic encephalopathy. Di Meo and colleagues reported that adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated ETHE1 gene transfer to the liver of ETHE1 knockout mouse, a mouse model of ethylmalonic encephalopathy, restored ETHE1 activity and normalized plasma thiosulfate levels, a marker of H2S accumulation, alongside clinical improvement (Di Meo et al., 2012). In 2014, based on this promising result obtained by liver-targeted gene therapy, liver transplantation was performed in a 9-month-old girl with ethylmalonic encephalopathy. The long-term follow-up, lasting over 6 years, of this liver-transplanted patient demonstrated that liver transplantation stabilized, or even improved, the neurological symptoms, normalized plasma thiosulfate levels, and reversed cerebral atrophy with no further lesions in brain MRI (Olivieri et al., 2021). These results suggest that reducing H2S production and enhancing its catabolism are effective therapeutic strategies, with liver transplantation emerging as the most effective therapy for ethylmalonic encephalopathy.

Sulfite oxidase deficiency is a rare, autosomal recessive, and often fatal metabolic disorder caused by mutations in the SUOX gene, leading to a loss of function in the SUOX enzyme (Claerhout et al., 2018). SUOX is a molybdenum cofactor- and heme- dependent enzyme localized to the intermembrane space of mitochondria (Claerhout et al., 2018). Patients with SUOX deficiency typically present with neurological abnormalities including psychomotor retardation, axial and peripheral hypotonia, seizure, and global developmental delay, usually manifesting within the first year of life (Claerhout et al., 2018). Although SUOX deficiency often results in infantile death, there are some milder and late-onset cases, where patients have survived beyond 10 years. (Claerhout et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2019). Radiologically, patients with SUOX deficiency show evidence of multicystic leukoencephalopathy and brain atrophy (Claerhout et al., 2018). Although incurable, a sulfur-restricted diet, reducing intake of sulfur-containing amino acids, such as methionine and cysteine, has been beneficial in some milder cases (Claerhout et al., 2018; Kaczmarek et al., 2022).

Biochemical hallmarks of SUOX deficiency include increased levels of sulfur-related metabolites, such as sulfite, thiosulfate, S-sulfocysteine, and taurine in plasma and urine, with reduced levels of cysteine and homocysteine in plasma (Kaczmarek et al., 2022; Kožich et al., 2022). S-sulfocysteine is implicated in fatal neurodegeneration, likely due to its structural similarity to glutamate, leading to NMDA receptor-mediated hyperexcitation (Kaczmarek et al., 2022). Recently, research by Mellis and colleagues demonstrated that SUOX knockout in HEK293 cells causes accumulation of sulfite, H2S, glutathione persulfide, and cysteine persulfide, alongside elevated SQOR and decreased ETHE1 levels (Mellis et al., 2021). It is crucial to determine whether sulfide toxicity contributes to the pathogenesis of SUOX deficiency, which may unveil new therapeutic strategies.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a debilitating, progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the loss of cognitive function (Long & Holtzman, 2019). AD affects approximately 22% of individuals aged 50 and above, including those with prodromal and preclinical stage (Gustavsson et al., 2023). AD can be familial or sporadic, with familial cases accounting for less than 5% (Karch & Goate, 2015). The hippocampus and cortex are primarily impacted, with hallmark features including aggregation of the microtubule-associated protein Tau and amyloid-β peptides, which form neurofibrillary tangles and amyloid plaques (Lane, Hardy, & Schott, 2018; Long & Holtzman, 2019). The pathogenesis of AD involves the deposition of amyloid-β plaques and Tau neurofibrillary tangles, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, inflammation, and reduced levels of acetylcholine (Ali et al., 2022; Ashraf, Jeandriens, Parkes, & So, 2020; Bao et al., 2021; Davies & Maloney, 1976; Frost, Jacks, & Diamond, 2009; Hardy & Allsop, 1991; Reid et al., 2023; Wilson et al., 2023).

Recent studies suggest that dysregulated sulfur metabolism may play a role in AD. Elevated plasma levels of H2S and H2S metabolites have been linked to cognitive dysfunction and microvascular disease, with increased sulfide levels correlating with decreased cortical thickness (Disbrow et al., 2021; Reekes et al., 2023). Furthermore, increased intracellular H2S, driven by proinflammatory cytokines, has been shown to induce persulfidation of AKT leading to glycogen synthase kinase 3β activation, triggering Tau-phosphorylation and synaptic dysfunction (Sen, Saha, Jiang, & Sen, 2020). The meta-analysis of observational studies showed that Desulfovibrionales and Desulfovibrionaceae, which are categorized as sulfate-reducing bacteria, are increased in patients with AD (Guo et al., 2023). In addition, Deslfovibrio is linked to a higher risk of AD based on a genome-wide association study (Guo et al., 2023). These reports suggest a link between elevated sulfur levels and AD.

Conversely, other research reports decreased sulfur levels in the brains of AD patients or animal models. Giovinazzo and colleagues reported that the levels of persulfidated glycogen synthase kinase 3β were significantly decreased in the brains of AD patients, leading to hyperphosphorylation of Tau and formation of neurofibrillary tangles (Giovinazzo et al., 2021). Morrison and colleagues reported that S-adenosyl-L-methionine, a CBS activator, was significantly reduced and homocysteine accumulated in the brains of patients with AD, suggesting that the CBS activity is reduced in the brains of AD (Morrison, Smith, & Kish, 1996). Recently, Kinno and colleagues reported that the total polysulfide content and the levels of polysulfidated proteins, but not H2S, were significantly decreased in the brain of a mouse model of AD (Kinno, Kasamatsu, Akaike, & Ihara, 2023).

Preclinical studies demonstrate the potential protective effects of H2S, such as inhibiting amyloid-β aggregation, reducing oxidative stress, and increasing acetylcholine levels in the Caenorhabditis elegans expressing human amyloid-β (Ali et al., 2022). H2S donors and related compounds have shown promise in reducing inflammation, preventing pathological Tau phosphorylation, and improving cognitive function in various models (Fan et al., 2013; Giovinazzo et al., 2021; Rao et al., 2022; Sestito et al., 2019; Xi et al., 2023).

Taken together, while sulfur metabolism dysregulation is evident in AD, there is no consensus on whether sulfur levels are elevated or reduced. This discrepancy may result from variations in disease severity, stage, and measurement methods across different studies.

The brain is highly sensitive to hypoxia, presumably due to its substantial metabolic demand and reliance on OXPHOS (Erecińska & Silver, 1989, 1994; Kalogeris, Baines, Krenz, & Korthuis, 2012; Kristián, 2004). When brain tissue PO2 falls to a critical level of 6–8 mmHg, cellular ATP concentration drops precipitously, leading to catastrophic energy failure (Erecinska & Silver, 2001). Complex IV, however, has a high affinity for O2 with a Km of 0.03–0.3 mmHg, significantly lower than the critical brain tissue PO2 level (Erecinska & Silver, 2001). This indicates that there is more than sufficient O2 in the brain tissue for complex IV to maintain electron flow in the electron transport chain at the critical brain tissue PO2 level, suggesting other factors contribute to OXPHOS inhibition under hypoxic conditions.

In hypoxic tissues, oxygen deficiency is accompanied by increased sulfide production (Morikawa et al., 2012), which inhibits OXPHOS and leads to neuronal death (Marutani et al., 2021). Upregulation of SQOR induced by sulfide-pre-conditioning enables mice to survive longer under 5% O2 by preventing sulfide accumulation, suggesting that enhancing sulfide catabolism by SQOR confers resistance to lethal hypoxia (Marutani et al., 2021). In contrast, SqorΔN/ΔN mice, which lack functional SQOR, show higher sensitivity to hypoxia, with a shorter survival under 5.5% O2 compared to wild-type mice (Marutani et al., 2021). Although breathing 5.5% O2 for 3 minutes increases levels of sulfide and persulfide and the ratio of NADH/NAD+ in the brains of both wild-type and SqorΔN/ΔN mice, sulfide levels and the ratio of NADH/NAD+ are higher in the brains of SqorΔN/ΔN mice than in wild-type mice (Marutani et al., 2021). In the brains of hypoxia-tolerant 13-lined ground squirrels, which possess 100-fold greater SQOR levels in the brain than mice and rats, sulfide does not accumulate under 5% O2, and there is resistance to hypoxia-induced impairment of OXPHOS (Marutani et al., 2021). SQOR knockdown restores the ability of hypoxia to increase sulfide levels and the ratio of NADH/NAD+ in the brains of 13-lined ground squirrels, highlighting the role of SQOR in the hypoxia tolerance of 13-lined ground squirrels (Marutani et al., 2021). AAV-mediated neuron-specific SQOR overexpression in the brain of male mice prevents hypoxia-induced increase in the levels of sulfide and persulfide and the ratios of NADH/NAD+ and lactate/pyruvate (Marutani et al., 2021). These results suggest that sulfide accumulation contributes to the inhibition of OXPHOS under hypoxic conditions in the brain, and the sensitivity of the brain to hypoxia is inversely related to the levels of SQOR.

Enhanced sulfide catabolism by SQOR offers protection against brain injury from ischemia and reperfusion. AAV-mediated SQOR overexpression improves neurological function and survival following bilateral carotid artery occlusion and reperfusion, reducing neuronal death in the hippocampal CA1 and CA3 regions and the cerebral cortex (Marutani et al., 2021).

The mechanism by which SQOR-mediated sulfide catabolism boosts ATP production is unclear, but it may primarily prevent sulfide accumulation rather than directly contributing electrons to the electron transport chain. This is because sulfide oxidation as an electron source is energetically unfavorable, especially during O2 shortage, requiring significantly more oxygen for equivalent electron transfer compared to NADH or succinate oxidation (Szabo et al., 2014). Pharmacological sulfide scavengers, like hydroxocobalamin, prevent sulfide accumulation and maintain the ratio of NADH/NAD+ in the brain during global cerebral ischemia (Marutani et al., 2021). Treatment with the specific sulfide scavenger SS20 after middle cerebral artery occlusion significantly decreases infarct size and improves neurological function, suggesting that the preventing sulfide accumulation maintains energy homeostasis and neuronal survival during acute O2 deprivation.

Among toxic gases, H2S is the second leading cause of gas-induced death after carbon monoxide (Guidotti, 2010; Mowry, Spyker, Brooks, Zimmerman, & Schauben, 2016). High level exposure to H2S can be life-threatening, leading to rapid unconsciousness, coma, shock, and respiratory failure. Survivors of acute sulfide poisoning often suffer from persistent neurologic deficits and neurodegeneration (Ng et al., 2019; Reiffenstein, Hulbert, & Roth, 1992). As H2S is an environmental hazard in sewers, sulfur springs, and certain industries, and can be used in terrorist attacks and suicide, effective antidotes for sulfide poisoning have been explored (Anderson, 2016; Kamijo et al., 2013; Ng et al., 2019; Reiffenstein et al., 1992). Various materials, such as metallic oxide, alkanolamines, oxidizing chemicals, metal carboxylates/chelates, aldehydes, and triazines, have been used as sulfide scavengers. However, these industrial sulfide scavengers are unsuitable for biological systems due to toxicity. Clinically used antidotes like hydroxocobalamin, cobinamide, and methylene blue are not specific to H2S as they also scavenge nitric oxide, carbon monoxide, and ROS (Brenner et al., 2014; Haouzi, Sonobe, & Judenherc-Haouzi, 2020; Jiang et al., 2016; V. S. Sharma, Pilz, Boss, & Magde, 2003).

In collaboration with the Xian laboratory, we developed novel H2S-specific scavengers, identifying sulfonyl azide-based compounds as promising candidates through data-driven analysis and extensive in vitro and in vivo screening (Yang et al., 2019). One such compound, SS20, showed high reactivity and selectivity to H2S with low cellular toxicity (Yang et al., 2019). SS20 reacts with H2S and produces sulfane sulfur species (Figure 4A). In mice exposed to 790 ppm of H2S, SS20 markedly prolonged survival (Miyazaki et al., 2021). In addition, administration of SS20 reduced plasma H2S levels, shortened time to the return of spontaneous circulation and breathing, improved survival and neurological function, and attenuated brain injury in mice resuscitated from cardiopulmonary arrest induced by sulfide intoxication (Miyazaki et al., 2021). Furthermore, as discussed above, SS20 markedly decreased infarct size and improved neurological function in mice subjected to transient middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion (Marutani et al., 2021). These results suggest that SS20 is an effective sulfide scavenger for both sulfide poisoning and conditions related to endogenous sulfide accumulation.

Most sulfide scavengers operate through stoichiometric reactions with H2S, which can lead to cytotoxicity. To mitigate the potential cytotoxicity, we investigated selenium-based molecules as catalytic scavengers to simultaneously remove H2S and ROS (Ni et al., 2024). During H2S-induced acute intoxication, significant ROS production, such as H2O2, contributes to cellular damage (Eghbal, Pennefather, & O’Brien, 2004). Structural-activity relationship studies identified a promising compound, S6, which catalyzes the reaction between H2S and ROS, removing both from biological systems (Ni et al., 2024). The chemical structure of S6 and the proposed mechanism of how S6 scavenges H2S and ROS are shown in Figure 4B and 4C. The S6 (diselenide: RSeSeR) can react with H2S to produce selenylsulfide (RSeSH) and selenol (RSeH). The RSeSH can further react with H2S to produce H2S2 and RSeH. The RSeH can be oxidized back to diselenide using O2. In the presence of H2S2, diselenide can be rapidly oxidized to RSeOH and RSeO2H, which also can react with H2S to produce RSeSH, consequently leading to the regeneration of diselenide.

Both SS20 and S6 mimic the SQOR-mediated H2S catabolism by producing persulfides, converting H2S to sulfane sulfur species, including elemental sulfur (S8) through H2S2 as an intermediate (Figure 4A and 4C) (Miyazaki et al., 2021; Ni et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2019). Although S8 typically shows low reactivity in biological systems, recent studies suggest S8 could function via persulfidation under certain conditions (Bolton & Pluth, 2022). Serving as an endogenous reserve pool for sulfide and persulfide, controlled exchange reactions between H2S and S8 may be crucial for redox signaling. Recently, Cui and colleagues developed a unique SQOR mimetics, 2H-thiopyran-2-thione sulfine, which specifically converts H2S to HSOH, and then to H2S2 in the presence of excess H2S (Cui et al., 2024). Cell-based studies revealed that 2H-thiopyran-2-thione sulfine effectively increased intracellular sulfane sulfur levels and compensated for aspects of SQOR deficiency (Cui et al., 2024). These novel SQOR mimetics and sulfide scavengers may have potential therapeutic applications in neurological diseases associated with sulfide accumulation by reducing sulfide levels and producing persulfides.

Human SQOR is the first reported enzyme with a catalytically active redox cofactor possessing a cysteine trisulfide configuration. The trisulfide configuration enhances the rate of initial nucleophilic addition of sulfide by approximately 105-fold over a disulfide, granting SQOR remarkable substrate promiscuity (Landry et al., 2021; Landry et al., 2020). Besides sulfide, various nucleophiles, including sulfite, glutathione (GSH), methanethiol, and CoA, can add to the resting trisulfide of SQOR (Landry et al., 2021). Glutathione trisulfide (GSSSG), which has a similar trisulfide configuration as SQOR (Figure 5), is an endogenous trisulfide and maintains dynamic equilibrium with various reactive sulfur species including GSH, GSSH, glutathione hydropolysulfides, and other glutathione polysulfides (Bianco et al., 2019; Ida et al., 2014). As GSSSG can react with H2S, similar to SQOR, and potentially exhibit physiological effects as a persulfide donor, we investigated its cytoprotective effects on neurological disorders.

Approximately 2 to 12% of patients undergoing thoracoabdominal aortic surgery suffer the devastating complication of paraplegia, with more than 80% of cases being delayed (Etz et al., 2008; Riambau et al., 2017; Ullery et al., 2011; Ziganshin & Elefteriades, 2014). This delay offers a window of opportunity for preventive intervention, though no pharmacologic treatment has proven effective thus far. We assessed the neuroprotective effects and pharmacokinetics of intranasal administration of GSSSG in a mouse model of spinal cord ischemia (Kanemaru et al., 2023). Post-reperfusion intranasal administration of GSSSG, but not GSH or glutathione disulfide (GSSG), prevented extensive neuronal loss in the ventral horns of the lumbar spinal cord and rescued mice from delayed paraplegia after spinal cord ischemia (Kanemaru et al., 2023). The beneficial effects of GSSSG were associated with inhibition of the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines and inhibition of activation of microglia and caspase-3. Intranasal administration of 34S-labeled GSSSG lead to a marked increase in several 34S-labeled sulfane sulfur species in the lumbar spinal cord, particularly 34S-labeled GSSH (Kanemaru et al., 2023). These observations suggest that sulfane sulfur can be readily delivered to the central nervous system with intranasal administration of GSSSG, preventing delayed neurodegeneration and mitigating delayed paraplegia.

Our prior research showed that chronic, intermittent H2S inhalation protects mice from neurodegeneration and movement disorders in the 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced mouse model of Parkinson’s disease (Kida et al., 2011). The exact mechanisms of neuroprotection from H2S inhalation remained incompletely understood. As mentioned above, upregulated SQOR from sulfide-preconditioning significantly improved survival of mice under severe hypoxia, suggesting a crucial role of sulfide oxidation by SQOR in cerebral energy homeostasis (Marutani et al., 2021). To investigate the effects of enhanced sulfide oxidation on the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease, we examined the effects of chronic, intermittent H2S inhalation on the MPTP-induced mouse model of Parkinson’s disease (Nagashima et al., 2022). SQOR upregulation by sulfide-preconditioning prevented MPTP-induced degeneration of dopaminergic neurons and movement disorders (Nagashima et al., 2022). In addition, neuron-specific AAV-mediated SQOR expression also inhibited MPTP-induced loss of dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatal region (Nagashima et al., 2022). The protective effects of SQOR upregulation were associated with increased sulfane sulfur levels in the nigrostriatal region (Nagashima et al., 2022). Furthermore, the intranasal administration of GSSSG attenuated MPTP-induced neurodegeneration (Nagashima et al., 2022). These findings suggest that enhancing sulfane sulfur levels through SQOR or persulfide donor compounds may be therapeutic for Parkinson’s disease.

Peripheral neuropathy is a dose-limiting side effect of chemotherapy with paclitaxel, affecting 30–70% of patients (Seretny et al., 2014). Oxidative mitochondrial damage in peripheral sensory neurons is implicated in the pathogenesis (Duggett et al., 2016; Flatters & Bennett, 2006; McCormick, Lowes, Colvin, Torsney, & Galley, 2016; Xiao et al., 2011). We studied the neuroprotective effects of GSSSG in a mouse model of paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy (Ezaka et al., 2022). Oral administration of GSSSG alleviated paclitaxel-induced mechanical allodynia, upregulated antioxidant protein genes in lumbar dorsal root ganglia, prevented unmyelinated axon loss, and inhibited mitochondria degeneration in the sciatic nerve and cultured primary lumbar dorsal root ganglia neurons (Ezaka et al., 2022). A marked increase in several 34S-labeled sulfane sulfur species was detected in the lumbar dorsal root ganglia two hours after oral administration of 34S-labeled GSSSG (Ezaka et al., 2022). These results suggest that elevating sulfane sulfur species in the lumbar dorsal root ganglia via GSSSG administration could be a strategy to treat or prevent paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy.

Inspired by the discovery of H2S as a signaling molecule in the brain by Kimura and colleagues, the biological roles of sulfide have been intensively investigated over the past quarter century. Despite significant progress in understanding the pathophysiological effects of sulfide-based signaling and the development of numerous chemical tools and several pharma startups, sulfide-based drug therapy has not yet materialized. The translation of sulfide-based therapy to clinical practice has been stymied by the very low therapeutic index of H2S donors (Derwall et al., 2010; Elrod et al., 2007; Marutani et al., 2012) and an incomplete understanding of sulfur metabolism.

The relative underappreciation of sulfide catabolism compared to endogenous sulfide synthesis is surprising, given the well-established role of H2S as a potent inhibitor of mitochondrial respiration and its involvement in congenital metabolic diseases caused by mutations in sulfide oxidation enzymes. This review highlights this knowledge gap and summarizes recent clinical and preclinical research progress.

Sulfide oxidation catalyzed by SQOR acts as a protective shield against respiratory poisoning by H2S. Neurodegeneration induced by impaired sulfide oxidation in patients and animal models underscores the essential role of SQOR and sulfide oxidation pathway in protecting brain health. As H2S is continuously produced by gut microbiome, symbiosis with bacteria is impossible without an intact sulfide oxidation capacity in the host.

The beneficial effects of sulfide catabolism are likely to be mediated not only by the elimination of H2S but also by the oxidation of H2S to persulfide, which exerts cytoprotective effects. Novel SQOR mimetics that specifically scavenge H2S and/or augment persulfide levels are poised to elucidate the precise role of sulfide catabolism, and may prove to be therapeutic for congenital metabolic disorders as well as common neurodegenerative diseases.
