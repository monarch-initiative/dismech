# Glycerol-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase 1 Deficiency and Steatotic Liver Disease in Children: Our Cases and Review of Literature.
**Authors:** Agrawal A, Srivastava A, Moirangthem A, Singh SM, Kodethoor D, Vadlapudi SS, Sarma MS, Poddar U
**Journal:** J Clin Exp Hepatol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jceh.2024.102484](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jceh.2024.102484)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1) deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder causing hypertriglyceridemia, hepatomegaly, fatty liver, and hepatic fibrosis in infancy. It is an under-recognized cause of pediatric steatotic liver disease (SLD) with only 36 cases reported worldwide.

Majority had hepatomegaly (94.4%), 22.2% each had splenomegaly and growth failure. Hypertriglyceridemia (97.2%) was nearly universal, 100% had fatty liver, and hypoglycaemia (11.2%) was uncommon. Abstract

Introduction

Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1) deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder causing hypertriglyceridemia, hepatomegaly, fatty liver, and hepatic fibrosis in infancy. It is an under-recognized cause of pediatric steatotic liver disease (SLD) with only 36 cases reported worldwide.

Method

We analyzed the clinical profile of our five cases diagnosed by exome sequencing (ES) and reviewed the published cases till December 2023 using PubMed search.

Results

Five unrelated boys (6–24months) presented with hepatomegaly, hypertriglyceridemia, transaminase elevation, and fatty liver. ES revealed compound heterozygous mutations in two and homozygous mutation in three including two novel mutations (c.917T >C, c.905C > G). All received supportive therapy, and fenofibrate was successfully used in one case for progressive hypertriglyceridemia. Globally, 36 cases (age at diagnosis: 6 [1–164 months]) have been reported. Majority had hepatomegaly (94.4%), 22.2% each had splenomegaly and growth failure. Hypertriglyceridemia (97.2%) was nearly universal, 100% had fatty liver, and hypoglycaemia (11.2%) was uncommon. Liver biopsy (n = 18) demonstrated steatosis in all, fibrosis in 94.4%, and cirrhosis in 22.2%. During follow-up (11–376 months), hepatomegaly resolved in 35.2%, triglycerides, and transaminases normalized in 29.1% and 31.5%, respectively. No pancreatitis, cardiac events, or liver decompensation was reported.

Conclusion

GPD1 is an uncommon cause of SLD, raised transaminases and hypertriglyceridemia in young children. Diagnosis is confirmed by genetic testing. Supportive therapy, parental counseling about the disease nature and close follow-up is recommended.

Keywords: hypertriglyceridemia, GPD1, fatty liver

Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1) deficiency was first identified by Basal-Vanagaite et al. in 2012.1 It is also known as transient infantile hypertriglyceridemia (TIH) and is perhaps underdiagnosed as only 36 cases are described in the literature until 2023. It is a rare autosomal recessive disorder, encoded by GPD1 gene on chromosome 12q12-q13. GPD1 plays a crucial role in both carbohydrate and lipid metabolism.

We report five unrelated children from North India and review 36 published cases, highlighting the phenotypic disease spectrum and novel genetic mutations in two cases, expanding the emerging spectrum of GPD1 deficiency.

METHOD

Clinical profile and outcome of our 5 cases diagnosed by exome sequencing (ES) were analyzed. The published cases until 2023 were reviewed using a PubMed search.

Patient Consent

The parents of all five children have given their written consent for the anonymous publication of this case series.

Case Series Description

Case 1

A six-month-old male (term delivery, no perinatal complications), first child of non-consanguineous healthy parents, presented with organomegaly from 2 months of age. Examination revealed weight of 6.3 kg (3rd centile), length of 66 cm (3–50th centile), soft hepatomegaly (13 cm, span 15 cm), and splenomegaly (3 cm). The laboratory investigations (Table 1) showed hyperuricemia (11.2 mg/dL), hypertriglyceridemia (989 mg/dL), raised transaminases (SGOT-239u/L, SGPT-124u/L), cholesterol (256 mg/dL) and grade II fatty liver on abdominal ultrasonography (USG). The parents had a normal lipid profile. Liver biopsy showed extensive (∼90%) steatosis, predominantly macro-vesicular and some micro-vesicular, mild pericellular fibrosis, focal porto-portal bridging fibrosis without cirrhosis. The ES revealed two heterozygous variations in GPD1 (ENST00000301149.8): c.392_393del (p.Leu131Hisfs∗27) and c.500G>A (p.Gly167Asp). Sanger sequencing in the parents confirmed that these variants were in trans in the patient. The c.500G>A variant has been previously reported in homozygous state in a patient with TIH. As per the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG-AMP) criteria, the c.392_393del variant is classified as “likely pathogenic” and the c.500G>A as “variant of uncertain significance (VUS).”

Case 2

A 12-month-old adopted boy, born small for gestational age (birth weight 1.6 kg), presented with progressive organomegaly from 1.5 months of age. His weight and length were 8.8 kg (50th centile) and 79 cm (50–97th centile), respectively. Examination revealed soft hepatomegaly (7 cm palpable) with splenomegaly (3 cm). The laboratory investigation showed hyperuricemia (7.9 mg/dL), hypertriglyceridemia (752 mg/dL), and grade I fatty liver on USG. ES showed a homozygous missense variant c.917T>C (p.Leu306Pro) in GPD1 gene (ENST00000301149.8). This is a novel variant predicted to be deleterious by various in-silico tools and classified as VUS.

Case 3

An 18-month-old male, first child of third-degree consanguineous healthy parents, presented with organomegaly and voracious appetite from 7 months of age. He had failure to thrive (weight-7.29 kg, length 70 cm [both <3rd centile]). Examination revealed doll-like facies, soft hepatomegaly (6 cm palpable) without splenomegaly. Investigation showed normal uric acid (6.8 mg/dL), hypertriglyceridemia (346 mg/dL), raised transaminases (SGOT-96u/L, SGPT-47u/L), and grade II fatty liver on imaging. Liver biopsy showed diffuse macro-vesicular steatosis. ES from a commercial lab failed to prioritize any relevant variant. ES data showed adequate coverage of all glycogen storage disease (GSD)-related genes. On re-analysis at our center, two heterozygous variants in GPD1 were identified. A missense variant c.361G>A (p.Gly121Arg) was inherited from the mother and was classified as VUS. This variant has been reported in clinvar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/702598/) as VUS (4 submissions) and as likely benign (1 submission). The second variant c.500G>A is the same as detected in case 1 and was absent in the mother. The father's sample was unavailable for analysis.

Case 4

A 2-year-old-boy, the 2nd child of 3rd degree consanguineous healthy parents, presented with organomegaly for 1 year. He weighed 11 kg (50–97th centile) and measured 80.5 cm (50–97th centile). Examination revealed soft hepatomegaly (11 cm) with splenomegaly (4.5 cm). Investigations showed hypertriglyceridemia, raised transaminases, and grade I fatty liver (Table 1). Liver biopsy showed moderate steatosis and periportal fibrosis. ES showed a homozygous missense variant c.905C>G (p.Thr302Arg) in GPD1 gene (ENST00000301149.8). This is a novel variant predicted to be deleterious by various in-silico tools and classified as VUS.

Case 5

A 1-year-old-boy, born of 2nd degree consanguineous marriage presented with organomegaly for last 7 months. He was on thyroxine (50mcg) OD for hypothyroidism. Examination showed weight of 9.5 kg (50–97th centile), length of 78 cm (50–97th centile), flat facial features, soft hepatomegaly (6 cm), and splenomegaly (5 cm). Investigations showed hypertriglyceridemia, raised transaminases, and grade III fatty liver (Table 1). ES showed a homozygous missense variant c.500G>A (p.Gly167Asp) in GPD1 gene (ENST00000301149.8).

In all the cases, hemogram, renal functions, blood sugar, synthetic liver function, echocardiogram, and ophthalmological evaluation were normal (Table1). None had jaundice, anemia, excessive irritability, seizures, hypotonia or specific food aversions.

A working diagnosis of GSD type 1 was considered in light of early presentation with hepatosplenomegaly, hypertriglyceridemia, hyperuricemia, raised transaminases, and fatty liver. However, except for case number 3, the other 4 cases had no hypoglycemia or chubby facies and had splenomegaly, which were odd for GSD-1. Pending genetic analysis for a definitive diagnosis, children were empirically advised frequent feeding and night time corn starch supplementation to avoid hypoglycemia.

FOLLOW-UP AND MANAGEMENT

All subjects were started on a low-fat, medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) rich diet and multivitamin supplementation. Case 1 had progressive hypertriglyceridemia over 6-months and was started on allopurinol (30 mg twice in a day) and fenofibrate (50 mg once in a day) at 11 months in consultation with pediatric endocrinology, resulting in uric acid normalization and significant reduction in triglycerides, cholesterol, and transaminases (Table 1). Cases 2 and 3 showed a spontaneous triglyceride reduction by 50%. Case 4 showed mild reduction in triglycerides but transaminases remained elevated. Organomegaly persisted in all patients.

Genetic analysis revealed variants in the GPD1 gene in our five cases. Some were previously reported, while others were novel (c.917T>C, c.905C>G). The GPD1 disease-causing variations comprise of loss-of-function and missense variants. Majority of the reported variants are scattered towards the C-terminal of the protein.1,3 Only a few recurrent variants are observed e.g. c.361-1G>C, which is a founder variant in the Israeli-Arab population. Three of our patients had the c.500G>A variant, which was reported previously in another North Indian family.10 This may possibly be a founder variant in our population.

Differentials for infants with hepatomegaly, fatty liver, and hypertriglyceridemia include GSD-1, hereditary fructose intolerance, cholesterol ester storage disorder, citrin deficiency, TIH, and fatty acid oxidation defects (Table 3). In our cohort, GSD-related genes were all sequenced at adequate quality and analysis did not reveal any significant small variations or large exonic deletions in these genes. Therefore, we have attributed disease causality to the GPD1 variants described in this report despite these being classified as VUS in two cases.

GPD1 plays a crucial role in lipid biosynthesis by converting dihydroxy acetone phosphate (DHAP) to glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P). In these patients, excess DHAP might lead to fatty liver via acyl-DHAP pathway. Hypertriglyceridemia may result from reduced conversion of G3P to DHAP, increasing the hepatic G3P for triglyceride synthesis.14 In TIH, hypertriglyceridemia is transient and tends to improve with age, exact reason for which remains unclear.

Liver biopsy and genetic testing aids in diagnosing TIH and distinguishing it from other conditions. In GPD1 deficiency, biopsy commonly shows steatosis and fibrosis, with cirrhosis in ∼20%. Matarazzo et al. reported a boy with two sequential biopsies at 3 and 15 years of age, respectively. The first showed incomplete cirrhosis, inflammation, and steatosis and the second showed diffuse nodular transformation of liver and extensive steatosis, suggesting slow disease progression. None of the reported patients had decompensated liver disease. The natural history is unclear, but prognosis appears favorable even with early liver fibrosis. None had coronary heart disease or pancreatitis, even in the patient followed up to 31 years of age. In follow-up (11–376 months duration), triglycerides normalized in 29.1% (7/24), transaminases in 31.5% (6/19), and hepatomegaly in 35.2% (6/17) of the reported cases. However, it is challenging to draw conclusions regarding the risk of liver disease in adulthood or over long-term. Additionally, this also poses challenges in classifying GPD1 variants as per the standard ACMG-AMP guidelines. Several potentially disease-causing variants are likely to be present in population databases like gnomAD even in the homozygous state, like the c.500G>A and c.361 G>A variants detected in cases 1 and 3, respectively. Some of the polymorphisms may also have an effect only in conjunction with another more deleterious variant.

Among 36 cases, 86.1% were homozygous and 13.9% were compound heterozygous. The most common mutations were c.361-1G>C and c.895G>A. Parents were asymptomatic except for one obese mother with normal USG but elevated triglycerides. Parental lipid profile was normal in four cases and unknown in the remaining.

Currently, there are no dietary guidelines for GPD1 deficiency. A low fat (<25%–30% calories from fat, <7% saturated fat, <1% trans-fat) and MCT rich diet (∼70% of total fat) may help reduce the triglyceride levels. Children with triglyceride (TG) levels of ≥500 mg/dL are at risk of secondary pancreatitis. Although there are no medications approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for hypertriglyceridemia in children, fibric acid derivatives (fibrates) can be considered in consultation with an endocrinologist.15 We successfully used fenofibrate in one case for hypertriglyceridemia (2002 mg/dL). Similarly, Matarazzo et al. used fenofibrate in a 15-year-old with persistent hypertriglyceridemia (1017 mg/dL) and showed TG normalization over 15-month follow-up without side-effects. The fibroscan values before and after 1 year of therapy were unchanged at 11.5 and 10.6 kPa, respectively. Fibrates have been shown to reduce serum TG levels by 40–60% in 9 patients with genetically confirmed APO-CIII deficiency. Fibrate therapy was started at a mean age of 9.5 years and given for 39 months. However, it was discontinued in three patients (2-rhabdomyolysis, 1-cholelithiasis).16 Considering the limited experience of fibrates in children and concerns of adverse effects (raised liver enzyme, muscle enzymes, gall stone, and renal impairment), fenofibrate should be used cautiously under close supervision and regular monitoring.

Counseling the parents regarding the limited information about the natural history of the disease and ensuring long-term follow-up especially in terms of complications related to liver disease, hypertriglyceridemia, and disease outcome is most important. We recommend 3–4 monthly outpatient department follow-up with monitoring of growth, liver and spleen size, liver function test, international normalized ratio, and lipid profile. Imaging (USG, transient elastography) may be done every 6–12 months, but protocols should be tailored as per patient's clinical status as the data on disease progression are limited. Avoiding other liver injuries by vaccination against hepatitis A/B and caution with use of hepatotoxic drugs/CAM is advisable.

In summary, we present five cases of GPD1 deficiency (two novel homozygous mutation). The condition is likely under-recognized due to several clinical and genotype interpretation challenges. The clinical presentation closely mimics other inborn errors of metabolism like GSD-1. It can also be mislabeled as metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) due to presence of hepatomegaly, fatty liver, and hypertriglyceridemia. However, the key distinguishing features include early presentation in the first 2 years and absence of obesity in majority of the reported cases. One should always suspect inborn errors of metabolism including GPD1 deficiency in younger patients presenting with SLD. Fenofibrate is a potential therapeutic option for progressive hypertriglyceridemia. Regular follow-up is essential to manage and understand the disease progression.
