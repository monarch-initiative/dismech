# Denosumab for the Treatment of Aggressive Recurrent Central Giant Cell Granuloma of the Maxilla in a Patient with Osteoglophonic Dysplasia.
**Authors:** Mugunam K, Ramli R
**Journal:** Tohoku J Exp Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1620/tjem.2025.J124](https://doi.org/10.1620/tjem.2025.J124)

## Content

Osteoglophonic dysplasia (OD) is a rare skeletal disorder marked by craniosynostosis, craniofacial anomalies, and abnormal bone development, primarily caused by FGFR gene mutations. We present the case of a 26-year-old Chinese female, who has been followed since age 11 for recurrent central giant cell granulomas (CGCG) and non-ossifying fibroma affecting her jaws and lower limbs. While her craniofacial and limb features have been described in two earlier publications, this report provides a detailed account of the aggressive and recurrent maxillary CGCG that resulted in significant clinical morbidity. Despite treatment with Alendronate targeting femoral lesions, the patient sustained a pathological fracture, resulting in long-term wheelchair dependency. Additionally, the maxillary lesion persisted and extended into the ethmoid sinus, causing recurrent epistaxis and anemia that required multiple surgical interventions. A multidisciplinary team initiated treatment with Denosumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) and directly targets osteoclast-like giant cells. Compared to bisphosphonates like Alendronate, which reduce bone resorption indirectly, Denosumab acts more selectively and showed superior clinical response in this case. The therapeutic switch resulted in marked tumor regression, effectively reducing surgical burden and improving quality of life. This case highlights the complexity of managing OD-associated CGCGs and the potential of Denosumab as a more effective therapeutic option than Alendronate. However, ongoing surveillance for adverse effects such as medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, hypocalcemia, and atypical fractures is essential.

Abstract

craniofacial anomalies, and abnormal bone development, primarily caused by FGFR gene 
mutations. We present the case of a 26 -year-old Chinese female with OD, who has been 
followed since age 11 for recurrent central giant cell granulomas (CGCG) and non-ossifying 
fibroma affecting her jaws and lower limbs. While her craniofacial and limb features have been 
described in two earlier publications, this report provides a detailed account of the aggressive 
and recurrent maxillary CGCG that resulted in significant clinical morbidity. Despite treatment 
with Alendronate targeting femoral lesions, the patient sustained a pathological fracture, 
resulting in long-term wheelchair dependency. Additionally, the maxillary lesion persisted and 
extended into the ethmoid sinus, causing recurrent epistaxis and anemia that required multiple 
surgical interventions. A multidisciplinary team initiated treatment with Denosumab, a 
monoclonal antibody that inhibits receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) and 
directly targets osteoclast -like giant cells. Co mpared to bisphosphonates like A lendronate,

Introduction 
Osteoglophonic dysplasia (OD), was first reported by Sir Thomas Farbank in a 
compendium of skeletal deformities in 1951 (Farbank 1951). The condition is very rare and  
further delineated by Beighton in the Journal of Medical Genetics in 1989  (Beighton 1989). 
The cases of OD are most commonly sporadic and have an autosomal pattern of inheritance 
(Shankar et al. 2010).The quality of life and life expectan cy of these individuals are highly 
dependent on the extent of their skeletal and craniofacial deformities  owing to the gene 
mutation of the fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFR) (Kuthiroly et al. 2017). 
The FGFR genes are associated with different anomalies, the FGFR1  and FGFR2 
mutations are mostly observed with syndromic craniosynostosis while the FGFR3  is linked 
with achondroplasia, hypochondroplasia and thanatophoric dysplasia. OD displays a spectrum 
of these mutations  (Kuthiroly et al. 2017). FGFR1-4 are important genes for skeletal 
development, a germline mutation from th ese genes would lead to severe bone disease from 
disruptions in the Ras  mitogen activated protein kinase (RAS/MAPK) signaling pathway 
components (Kuthiroly et al. 2017) . Research indicates that FGFR1  mutations are frequently 
present in giant cell lesions of the jaw, particularly in central giant cell granulomas (CGCG)  
(Gomes et al. 2018). Hence, FGFR1  mutations, along with other genetic changes, are pivotal 
in the pathogenesis of giant cell tumors (Gomes et al. 2018). 
CGCG of the jaws have histologically similarities to osteoclast-rich lesions such as non-
ossifying fibroma of long bones, chondroblastoma, and associated with syndromes like Noonan 
syndrome, cherubism, c ardio-fascio-cutaneous syndrome, Jaffe -Campanacci syndrome and 
oculoectodermal syndrome ( Gomes et al. 2020) . Clinically, CGCG, when aggressive, may 
cause severe complications such as  airway obstruction, frequent hemorrhagic events and 
involvement of vital structures (Tarsitano et al. 2015).  
This case has been previously reported twice, in 2010 and 2021. The initial report in 2010 
detailed the patient's clinical features, blood investigations, and genetic analysis (Sow et al., 
2010). The subsequent report in 2021 focused on lesions of the right femur that rendered the 
patient wheelchair-bound (Kumar et al. 2021). In the present report, we aim to highlight the 
progressive and persistent complications of maxillary CGCG observed over a 14 -year period 
since the initial publication.

Case Presentation 
A 26-year-old female of Chinese ethnicity has been on follow up with the Department 
of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia Medical Centre from the 
age of seven  years old. At birth she was diagnosed with craniosynostosis spectrum and had 
feeding difficulties leading to failure to thrive. It was also reported she had stridor due to the 
choanal stenosis and recurrent chest infections. When she was three years old , a computed 
tomography (CT) scan of the skull was performed and revealed fusion of the metopic and 
lateral coronal sutures . F ortunately, there was no evidence of hydrocephalus. C raniofacial 
advancement and suture release was advised, however, was not agreeable.  
Molecular genetic testing of FGFR1 gene analysis revealed heterozygous mutation of 
c.1141C>T identified in exon 10, resulting in cysteine replacement with arginine 
(Cys381Arg) on the FGFR1 transmembrane domain (Sow et al. 2010). Given that her parents 
and siblings are normal, this patient can be classified as having sporadic OD. The 
concomitant clinical features include short stature, craniofacial dysplasia, hypertelorism, 
anteverted nares, maxillary hypoplasia, depressed nasal bridge, anodontia, multiple impacted 
teeth, and multiple lytic lobular lesions on the jaws.  
Between the ages of seven to twelve years old, she had multiple admissions to the hospital 
due to right facial swelling and cellulitis. Multiple biopsies and surgical excisions were 
performed during this period due to recurrent CGCG of the maxilla and aneurysmal bone cyst 
of the left mandible. The diagnosis of CGCG was first made when she was eleven years old. 
To monitor the progress of the tumor and the extension, serial CT scans were 
performed and revealed no gross reduction in size of the expansile, lytic and sclerotic lesions 
of the maxilla. The mandibular expansile lytic lesion remained dormant and no further 
increase in size from the age of 17.  When she was 14 years old, three doses intravenous 
Pamidronate was initiated as means to halt the remodelling process of the maxillary CGCG. 
This treatment was based on clinical evidence in the management of central giant cell tumor 
(Cheng et al. 2004). The tumor, however, did not respond to the pharmacotherapy and she 
was continuously under follow up and treated symptomatically. 
Apart from the CGCG  of the maxilla and aneurysmal bone cyst of the mandible, her 
bilateral lower limbs also had similar lesions. At the age of 14 years, following an initial sign 
of bilateral hip swelling, plain x -rays and CT scan revealed multilocular expansile lesions in

conservatively. However, six years after the fracture, at the age of 23,  she developed another

expansile multilocular lesions. She was wheelchair bound at this point. The orthopaedic team 
then started her on a bisphosphonate, i.e. Alendronate, at the age of 23 years, along with 
physiotherapy, in an effort to reduce the pain and control the advancing osteoclastic resorption. 
The use of Alendronate was able to control the lesions of her right femur. This was evidenced 
from increase in cortication of the lesion of her femur, controlled pain and able to mobilise on 
foot around the house  (Kumar et al. 2021).  Alendronate was selected due to its oral 
administration route and its potentially greater effectiveness compared to pamidronate in 
reducing specific biochemical markers, such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and the urine 
deoxypyridinoline (DPD)/creatinine ratio (Walsh et al. 2004). 
In relation to the jaw bones, the CGCG of her maxilla continued to expand resulting in 
osteolytic bony involvement and haemorrhage leading to frequent episodes of epistaxis which 
commonly resolved on its own. Figure 1 shows diffuse swelling of her left cheek with slight 
narrowing of her left nostril and loss of the nasolabial fold. This problem started to become 
prominent when she was 21 years old. Series of surgical excision were performed to remove 
the recurrent lesions. The difference encountered during the surgical excision in this period 
compared to when she was in her teens was that the tumour had become very vascular, causing 
profound haemorrhage during surgery, necessitating a transfusion with packed cells.  
The epistaxis continued to recur after some time following surgery and when she was 
24-year old, there was an episode of prolonged epistaxis which she had to seek treatment at the 
emergency department. We admitted her into our ward and started her on oral tranexamic acid, 
650 mg three times a day. The bleeding was successfully arrested.  Prior to this episode, she 
reported of a detachment of a ‘ball-like’ mass from the back of her nose into her mouth while 
sleeping, and a short period of bleeding was experienced. A CT scan was performed and  
revealed the growth of the tumour from  the initial region involving the nasal cavity and hard 
palate had progressed and involved the nasal vestibules up to the ethmoid air cells  (Figure 2). 
She underwent endoscopic inferior turbinate mass biopsy and hemostasis with  the 
otorhinolaryngology team. They reported that the left nostril was completely obliterated by the 
mass. Given the extent and obliteration of the maxillary soft tissue lesion, a maxillectomy was 
suggested for complete eradication of the problem. However, we debated that performing a

The CGCG in this case was aggressive in nature and required an intervention to 
constraint its extension. A multi-disciplinary discussion was carried out between the oral and 
maxillofacial surgeons and orthopaedic surgeons to find means i) to control the growth of the 
maxillary tumor, ii) to avoid a debilitating surgery to her face  and iii) to resolve epistaxis. A 
consensus was made to change the pharmacotherapy of bi sphosphates, i.e. Alendronate  to 
monoclonal antibody, i.e. Denosumab, 60mg subcutaneously for every six monthly. The main 
rationale was based on the pathobiological features of central giant cell granuloma (CGCG), 
which include the presence of multinucleat ed osteoclast-like giant cells expressing receptor 
activator of nuclear factor kappa-B (RANK) and mononuclear stromal cells expressing RANK 
ligand (RANKL) (Xu et al. 2013). Denosumab exerts its effect by inhibiting RANKL, thereby 
disrupting this signaling pathway (Thomas et al. 2010). 
A CT scan taken in  January 2023 was used as the baseline and a surveillance scan 
performed after two doses of Denosumab in December 2023  was used to compare the 
effectiveness of the treatment. The CGCG of the maxilla was significantly reduced in size and 
extent (Table 1, Figures 2-4). This approach saved the patient from a major surgery that would 
have likely to cause a reduced quality of life. In addition, the lesions on her lower limb were 
also under control. At present, she is still on close monitoring for maxillary lesions as well as 
for any complications related to Denosumab. The patient has not reported any adverse effects 
associated with Denosumab therapy. In addition to monitoring for potential side effects, annual 
computed tomography (CT) scans are performed to evaluate the lesion's size and extent of 
involvement.

Discussion 
OD involves a genetic mutation of the FGFR gene. FGFR1 to 4 are expressed in 
osteoblasts and chondrocytes and they affect proliferation and differentiation of cells in the 
regulation of bone generation (Gomes et al. 2018). FGFR1 is also expressed in osteoclasts and 
along with the FGFR2, they regulate osteoclast genesis. The mutation of FGFR1 thus prevents 
the eruption of both deciduous and permanent teeth (Zou et al. 2022), which is shown in this 
case. These mutations, in combination with mutations in genes  like Kirsten ras oncogene 
homolog ( KRAS) and transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 4 
(TRPV4), play a significant role in the development of these tumors by activating signal ling 
pathways such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase ( MAPK) pathway. This activation 
affects cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation, contributing to the neoplastic 
characteristics observed in giant cell tumo urs (Gomes et al. 2020). Studies revealed crosstalk 
between FGFR signaling and the MAPK and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B 
(PI3K/Akt) pathways, highlighting complex networks that regulate cell behavior and fate  (Li 
et al. 2015; Szymczyk et al. 2022).  
Central giant cell granuloma (CGCG) is a rare benign intraosseous lytic lesion of the 
jaws that has a predilection towards women before the age of 30 years old (de Lange et al. 
2007). Clinical presentations of CGCG include slow growing mass in the jaw bones causing 
tooth displacement with no predilection to maxilla or mandible  (Kaban et al. 2002).  The 
radiographic findings often show expansile osteolytic lesions, with dispersed trabecular pattern, 
cortical thinning and multilocular appearance (Kaffe et al. 1996).  
 CGCG has histopathological features that are highly identical to those of peripheral 
giant cell tumors and non- ossifying fibromas (NOF) of long bones (Franklin et al. 1979; 
Waldron 1953). CGCG is similar to peripheral giant cell tumours with histomorphological 
features of multinucleated giant cells, whirling fibrous stroma, leakage of haemorrhagic areas 
with hemosiderin (Franklin et al. 1979; Waldron 1953) . In CGCG, there is formation of an 
osteoid matrix, while the origin of peripheral giant cell granuloma is from soft tissues, such as 
the gingiva or alveolar mucosa. (Katsikeris  et al. 1988; Slootweg 1989). Non-ossifying 
fibromas are also a form of giant cell tumour which is distinctively found in the lower 
extremities. They present histologically with fibrous stroma in a whirling pattern and 
multinucleated giant cells with haemorrhagic areas.  However, in NOF, the foam cells, which 
are a type of macrophage found on blood vessels that ingest low -density lipoproteins, are

CGCG. A follow-up report of the cases would be highly valuable to observe the onset and

 The nature of growth of CGCG  can be classified as aggressive or non- aggressive 
(Gigliotti et al. 2015). Kaban et al. described the distinctive features of the aggressive variant, 
i.e. size greater than 5cm, rapid growth, cortical bone perforation and recurrence after curettage 
(Kaban et al. 2002). I n this variant , pharmacotherapy as an adjunct would possibly benefit 
when the tumour is extensive and surgery alone will be mutilating to the patient (Kaban et al. 
2002). Nonetheless, there is no conclusive evidence to support the use of non-surgical therapies 
in the management of CGCG. A recent systematic review by Camarini et al (2022) described 
multiple modalities available for the treatment of CGCG which include intra -lesional 
triamcinolone, calcitonin, imatinib, Denosumab and subcutaneous interferon. They found that 
pharmacotherapy halted the progression of CGCG  to a certain extent , however, 40% of the 
patients still had to go for a  surgical therapy eventually. Benefits of pharmacotherapy in the 
management of aggressive CGCG are also highlighted by other studies (de Lange et al. 2007; 
Gigliotti et al. 2015; Tarsitano et al. 2015). 
 In our case, despite the beneficial effect of Alendronate for her lower limb 
lesions, the CGCG lesion in the maxilla continued to recur, contrary to the findings of many 
studies (Chien et al. 2015; Tse et al. 2008). This could be attributed to the mechanism of action 
of bisphosphonates in bone  (Drake et al. 2008). Bisphosphonates attaches to hydroxyapatite 
binding sites on bony surfaces and impairs osteoclasts from forming a ruffled border to adhere 
to bony surface  (Martins et al. 2015) . CGCG, on the other hand, is an intrabony neoplasm 
composed of round mononuclear cells in a whirl-like stroma with characteristic osteoclast-like 
giant cell clusters. The mononuclear stromal cells are found to express receptor activation of 
nuclear factor kappa-β ligand (RANK ligand), which is a mediator for osteoclastic activation

and the presence of osteoprotegerin (OPG) showed a likely negative feedback mechanism in 
the lesions (Liu  et al. 2003).  RANKL-RANK interaction is essential for osteoclast 
differentiation and activity, which contributes to bone resorption and tumor expansion in 
FGFR1-mutated central giant cell granuloma (CGCG).  Hence, the use of a monoclonal 
antibody, i.e. Denosumab, primarily acts by binding to RANKL, thereby inhibiting the 
activation of the RANK/RANKL/OPG signaling pathway and blocking osteoclast -mediated 
bone resorption (Erdogan et al. 2016; Roitman et al. 2017; Ud Din et al. 2020; Aulestia-Viera 
et al. 2022; Lu et al. 2023).  Since FGFR1 also signals through pathways such as MAPK, the 
inhibition of RANKL by Denosumab could potentially disrupt shared or interacting 
downstream signals, thereby affecting the proliferation and migration of tumor cells driven by 
FGFR1 mutations  (Lu et al. 2023). RANKL inhibition by denosumab could interrupt a 
feedback loop where FGFR1 activation promotes the expression of its target (RANKL), 
contributing to tumor growth and bone disruption (Mohammad & Akhund 2024). RANKL 
blockade with denosumab has been shown to reverse this tolerogenic profile, enhancing anti -
tumor immunity dependent on adaptive immune system activity (Pilard et al. 2025). 
Following successful treatment with Denosumab for giant cell tumors of the femur s, 
many clinicians postulated similar results for CGCGs of the jaws (Bredell et al. 2018; Mariz et 
al. 2021; Pogrel et al, 2021). Its first use in a CGCG of the jaws were described in 2014 (Naidu 
et al. 2014; Schreuder et al. 2014).  
The effect of Denosumab on CGCG and other giant cell tumours have brought a 
positive outcome, though there are some vital distinctive histopathological changes that should 
be highlighted.  The histological changes include different degrees of ossification, proliferation 
of mononuclear cells, fibrosis, and depletion of giant cells (Roitman et al. 2017; Wojcik et al. 
2016). These may lead to misdiagnosis of other benign and malignant bone tumours such as 
fibrous dysplasia, osteosarcoma and osteoblastoma (Roitman et al. 2017; Wojcik et al. 2016). 
However, the absence of extensive necrosis, dysplastic cells, atypical mitotic entities,  and 
invasive pattern would assist in achieving the true diagnosis  (Roitman et al. 2017; Wojcik et 
al. 2016).  
This patient is under our close monitoring for the side effects of Denosumab as well 
as recurrence of CGCG. The pertinent side effects of Denosumab includes arthralgia, 
headache, nausea, back pain, fatigue, and pain in the extremities (Pittman et al. 2017). Apart 
from these, reported toxicities of Denosumab especially for those on long term therapy, are

medication-induced osteonecrosis of the jaw, hypocalcemia and atypical stress fractures 
(Pittman et al. 2017). Our primary clinical focus, as elucidated by Gaston et al. (2016), 
revolves around the optimal duration of treatment and the enduring consequences thereof. A 
significant apprehension persists regarding the heightened risk of tumor recurrence following 
Denosumab cessation as shown by a Norwegian study (Boye et al.2017). 
This case is challenging because we are dealing with a recurrent CGCG, which is 
caused by a genetic mutation. At the same time, we need to be careful about possible 
toxicities although our dose of Denosumab is low. If we have to stop using Denosumab, we 
might have to remove the tumour all over again if the patient shows symptoms like bleeding 
or swelling. 
 
Conclusion 
Central giant cell granuloma in this case is distinct due to its association with a mutation in the 
FGFR1 gene. The tumor exhibited aggressive behavior and frequent recurrence, necessitating 
repeated excisions. Administration of D enosumab effectively reduced the tumor size and 
immediately diminished the episodes of epistaxis. Vigilant management of potential toxicities 
is essential to ensure the patient can receive sustained treatment.
