Abstract
Objective To investigate the clinical characteristics, related risk factors and outcomes of thyroid immune-related 
adverse events (irAEs) in patients with malignant solid tumor treated with programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) or 
programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors in a large tertiary Chinese center.
Methods We retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 1151 patients with malignant solid tumors who received 
PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors treatment and underwent thyroid function evaluation in a large 3 A hospital of Beijing from 
September 2019 to December 2023. According to the thyroid status after receiving PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors treatment, 
patients were divided into normal thyroid group and thyroid irAEs group. The clinical characteristics, including age, 
gender, tumor type, previous anti-cancer treatment history and thyroid function status were evaluated. After the 
occurrence of thyroid irAEs, thyroid function evaluation, onset time, and survival outcomes were analyzed. Risk factors 
that may contribute to the thyroid irAEs were further explored by logistic regression.
Results Out of 1151 patients treated with a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor, 257 (22.3%) developed new thyroid irAEs, with 
the vast majority (98.0%) being hypothyroidism (193/257, 75.1%) and Grade1-2 (252/257, 98.0%), including 252 
Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (98.0%), 3 subacute thyroiditis (1.17%) and 2 Graves’ disease (0.78%). There was a significant 
difference in the number of treatment cycles of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors between the two groups (P = 0.001). In 
multivariate analysis, gastrointestinal cancer, radiotherapy history, targeted therapy history, positive TgAb and TPOAb 
at baseline were associated with thyroid irAEs caused by PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. Absence of thyroid irAEs predicted 
increased mortality overall (HR = 2.935, P = 0.024) and particularly in gastrointestinal cancers (HR = 9.453, P = 0.007), 
despite comparable crude mortality (5.84% vs. 5.82%, P = 0.228). No association was observed in lung/other tumors.

Introduction
In the past decade, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICPis) 
have become a new class of effective regimen for the 
treatment of certain cancers, especially for refractory and 
recurrent malignant tumors. ICPis include programmed 
death-1 (PD-1) inhibitors, programmed death ligand-1 
(PD-L1) inhibitors and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associ -
ated protein-4 (CTLA4) inhibitors, which elicit the anti-
tumor effect in a multifactorial fashion, such as blocking 
immunosuppressive molecules, reactivating effector T 
lymphocytes and specifically killing tumor cells. While 
the activated immune cells by these drugs kill tumor 
cells, they may attack normal cells, cause immune-related 
adverse events (irAEs), and lead to functional deficiency 
of the affected organs. Endocrine adverse reactions are 
relatively prevalent irAEs linked with ICPis, primarily 
affecting the thyroid, pituitary, pancreatic and adrenal 
gland [1].
The most common endocrine adverse reaction is thy -
roid dysfunction. According to Management of immune-
related adverse events in patients treated with immune 
checkpoint inhibitors therapy: American society of clini -
cal oncology clinical practice guideline [ 2], if the thyroid 
dysfunction is grade 3–4, patients should hold ICPis until 
symptoms resolve to baseline with appropriate supple -
mentation. Therefore, thyroid dysfunction during tumor 
treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors is an 
important factor affecting tumor treatment strategies. At 
present, it is still unclear the possible risk factors contrib-
uting to thyroid damage induced by immune checkpoint 
inhibitors therapy used in the Chinese population, as well 
as the exact degree.
We retrospectively analyzed the degree and type of thy-
roid irAEs in patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibi -
tors in our Medical Center from September 2019 to 
December 2023. Most importantly, we analyzed the sur -
vival outcomes and risk factors contributing to the thy -
roid damage in this large Chinese group.
Materials and methods
Study design and population
A retrospective cohort study of thyroid irAEs in cancer 
patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (includ -
ing pembrolizumab, sintilimab, nivolumab, toripalimab, 
duralumab, tislelizumab and atezolizumab) between Sep-
tember, 2019 and December, 2023 in a large 3  A hospi -
tal of Beijing was conducted. We reviewed the medical 
records and evaluated patient characteristics, includ -
ing age, sex, cancer type, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy, 
treatment cycles, previous surgical treatment, chemo -
therapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy and thyroid func-
tion of consecutive patients. Patients who received at 
least one dose of the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor and under -
went regular measurement of thyroid function follow -
ing the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor dose while with normal 
thyroid function at baseline and further thyroid function 
evaluation after administration of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors 
were included in the study.
Patients with a history of thyroid dysfuction (defined 
as abnormal thyroid function at baseline or currently 
receiving treatment for thyroid disease at baseline) 
were excluded. This study was approved by the Human 
Research Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General 
Hospital (No. S2022-190-01). Clinical trial number: not 
applicable.
Data collection
The medical records of all patients were reviewed indi -
vidually by using a standardized data collection template. 
We collected the following variables: demographic char -
acteristics (gender and age), type of cancer (lung cancer, 
gastrointestinal cancer and other tumors), TNM staging, 
history of treatment (prior surgical treatment, radio -
therapy, chemotherapy and targeted therapy), immu -
notherapy agents (PD-1 inhibitor and PD-L1 inhibitor), 
treatment cycles and laboratory test results before PD-1/
PD-L1 inhibitors treatment [free triiodothyronine (FT 3), 
free thyroxine (FT4), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), 
thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb), and thyroid peroxidase 
antibody (TPOAb)].
Thyroid function screening and classification of thyroid 
IrAEs
Thyroid function screening was performed before 
(<3 months) and during treatment with PD-1/PD-L1 
inhibitors. The reference ranges in our laboratory are 
0.35∼5.50 mIU/L for TSH, 10.42 ∼24.32 pmol/L for 
FT4 and 2.76 ∼6.3 pmol/L for FT 3 in adults. Results of 
Thyroid function recovery occurred in 36.2% of patients, and treatment interruption due to thyroid irAEs was rare 
(3.5%).
Conclusion In our group, 22.3% patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors developed thyroid irAEs. The main 
subtype of thyroid irAEs was hypothyroidism (75.1%). Patients with gastrointestinal cancer, previous radiotherapy, 
history of targeted therapy, and baseline TgAb or TPOAb positivity may increase the risk of thyroid irAEs. Thyroid irAEs 
was associated with a trend for a survival benefit in patients with gastrointestinal cancer.

TgAb (reference range < 60 IU/mL), TPOAb (reference 
range<60 IU/mL) and TSH receptor antibody (TRAb) 
(reference range<1.75 U/L) were also collected when 
tested.
Thyroid dysfunction was characterized as:
(1) Thyrotoxicosis, overt was defined as TSH<0.35 
mIU/L while FT4>24.32 pmol/L and/or FT3>6.3 
pmol/L, subclinical was defined as TSH<0.35 mIU/L 
while FT4 and FT3 in the normal range;
(2) Hypothyroidism, overt was defined as TSH>5.5 
mIU/L while FT4 < 10.42 pmol/L and/or FT3 < 2.76 
pmol/L, subclinical was defined as TSH>5.5 mIU/L 
while FT4 and FT3 in the normal range;
(3) Thyroiditis (Hashimoto disease) with euthyroidism 
was defined as FT4, FT3 and TSH in the normal 
range while TPOAb>60 IU/mL and/or TgAb>60 IU/
mL.
Types of thyroid irAEs was characterized as:
(1) Hashimoto disease was defined as TPOAb>60 IU/
mL and/or TgAb>60 IU/mL.
(2) Graves’ disease was considered when thyrotoxicosis 
was diagnosed with TRAb positive and thyroid 
ultrasound showed diffuse goiter.
(3) Subacute thyroiditis was defined as the occurrence 
of fever, anterior neck pain, fatigue, a rapid 
increase in erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), 
an elevation in C-reactive protein (CRP), changes 
in thyroid function with thyrotoxicosis followed 
by hypothyroidism. Thyroid gland tenderness and 
small diffuse goiter may be detected in the physical 
examination, and the thyroid ultrasound indicated a 
patchy hypoechoic area.
Criteria of common terminology criteria for adverse events 
(version 5.0)
All patients evaluated had accurate clinical records of 
the irAEs with a description of their severity and their 
treatment. Toxicity was assessed according to Common 
Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, ver -
sion 5.0). The severity of thyroid injury was divided into 
5 grades. Grade 1: Asymptomatic; clinical or diagnostic 
observations only; intervention not indicated; Grade 2: 
Symptomatic; thyroid replacement indicated; limiting 
instrumental activities of daily living; Grade 3: Severe 
symptoms; limiting self-care activities of daily living; hos-
pitalization indicated; Grade 4: Life-threatening conse -
quences; urgent intervention indicated; Grade 5: Death.
Follow-up and survival outcomes
All-cause mortality at two-year was the primary outcome 
of this study. The survival status was obtained through 
telephone contact with patients or their relatives or 
through outpatient care. The follow-up period began at 
the initiation of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment, until 2 
years after the start of follow-up or until the occurrence 
of the primary endpoint.
Statistics
Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS ver -
sion 26. Continuous variables were expressed as median 
(interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables were 
reported as frequencies (percentages). Group compari -
sons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test, 
Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Kruskal-Wallis test, Fisher’s 
exact test, or Chi-square test, as appropriate. A logistic 
regression nomogram was developed based on multivari -
ate logistic regression analysis, and the results of the mul-
tivariable logistic regression are presented as odds ratios 
(OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Survival analysis 
was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and dif -
ferences between groups were assessed with the log-rank 
test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression 
analysis was used to evaluate the association between the 
primary exposure and survival outcomes, with results 
reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95%CI. All tests were 
two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statisti -
cally significant.
Results
Study population
From September 2019 and December 2023, a total 
of 1151 patients who met the research criteria were 
screened in the study, of whom 257 patients (22.3%) 
developed thyroid irAEs following PD-1/PD-L1 inhibi -
tors treatment. The detailed demographics and clini -
cal characteristics of the 1151 patients were shown in 
Table 1. The media age was 63 (IQR 55, 69) years and 
856 cases (74.4%) were male. In terms of primary tumor 
types, there were 388 cases (33.7%) of lung cancer, 534 of 
gastrointestinal cancer (46.4%, including stomach can -
cer, esophageal cancer, colon cancer, etc.), and 229 of 
other malignant tumors (19.9%) ( P = 0.01, Table 1). Oth-
ers tumor types included laryngeal cancer, nasopharyn -
geal cancer, gingival cancer, renal cancer, cervical cancer, 
intracranial hemangiopericytoma, breast cancer, etc. 
Some patients had been treated with surgical treatment 
(432, 37.5%), chemotherapy (1069, 92.9%), and radiother-
apy (353, 30.7%) before receiving PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. 
Some patients (373, 32.4%) received targeted therapy 
including small molecule and monoclonal antibody tar -
geted therapies. Altogether, 92.6% of the patients received 
PD-1 inhibitors (pembrolizumab 33.3%, sintilimab 28.3%, 
nivolumab 14.5%, toripalimab 13.6% and tislelizumab 
2.9%), and 7.3% received PD-L1 inhibitors (durvalumab 
6.3% and atezolizumab 1.0%) (P = 0.02, Table 1).

Incidence and time to onset of thyroid IrAEs
Out of 1151 cancer patients treated with the PD-1/
PD-L1 inhibitor, 22.3% patients (257/1151) developed 
thyroid irAEs. The highest incident of thyroid irAEs 
occurred with sintilimab (98/326, 30.1%), followed by 
nivolumab (37/167, 22.2%), pembrolizumab (75/383, 
19.6%), toripalimab (31/157, 19.7%), durvalumab (13/73, 
17.8%), atezolizumab (1/12, 8.3%) and tislelizumab (2/33, 
6.1%) ( P < 0.01). However, there was no statistically sig -
nificant between PD-1 inhibitors and PD-L1 inhibitors 
(P = 0.178).
The time to onset of thyroid irAEs after PD-1/PD-L1 
inhibitors treatment was variable and the median time to 
onset was 116 (IQR 83, 224) days in overt hypothyroid -
ism, 144 (IQR 53, 227) days in subclinical hypothyroid -
ism, 50 (IQR 24.8, 78) days in overt thyrotoxicosis and 56 
(IQR 25, 81) days in subclinical thyrotoxicosis, respec -
tively (Table 2). Time to thyroid dysfunction was signifi -
cantly shorter for patients with thyrotoxicosis than those 
with hypothyroidism (median 55 vs. 138 days, P < 0.001).
Evolution and outcome of thyroid function
Multiple types of thyroid irAEs were observed initially. 
The vast majority of thyroid irAEs was caused by Hashi -
moto’s disease (252/257, 98.0%), with a few cases of thy -
roiditis (3/257, 1.17%) and Graves’ disease (2/257, 0.78%).

Among the patients with Hashimoto’s disease, their thy -

After the median days of 140 days, the thyroid func -

During treatment, 3.5% (9/257) of patients experienced 
treatment interruption due to thyroid irAEs. Among 
these, only 1 patient discontinued treatment specifically 
because of thyroid irAEs, while the remaining 8 patients 
stopped treatment due to other reasons, such as poor 
physical condition, infections, or switching to alternative 
treatment.

Graves’ disease and 3 patients with subacute thyroiditis 
in our group.

Severity of thyroid IrAEs

Evaluation Criteria for Time Events of Common Adverse

inhibitors treatment, and all patients were asymptom -
atic and did not require medical intervention. 43 patients

and received treatment with methimazole or levothyrox -
ine sodium. Their thyroid function returned to normal 
afterwords without suspension of the PD-1/PD-L1 inhib -
itor treatment. Five patients experienced grade 3 and 
need to suspend the treatment of PD1/PD-L1 inhibitors.

The mortality rate was 5.84% ( n = 15) in thyroid irAE

Discussion
In recent years, with the increasing utilization of PD-1/
PD-L1 inhibitors in the treatment of malignant tumors, 
irAEs have been concerned. Endocrine organs are com -
mon target organs for irAEs, and thyroid dysfunction is a 
frequent endocrine toxic reaction caused by PD-1/PD-L1 
inhibitors in the endocrine system [4].
The incidence of thyroid irAEs remained debating in 
diverse studies. In our study, the incidence of thyroid 
irAEs was 22.3% of thyroid irAEs. In a meta-analysis 
comprising 38 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the 
incidence of ICPis-related hypothyroidism was estimated 
to be 6.6% in all patients on ICPis therapy [ 5], and the 
incidence of thyroid irAEs was even higher in some RCTs 
[6].
In the thyroid irAEs caused by PD1/PD-L1 inhibitor, 
we found that the incidences of PD-1 inhibitors (pem -
brolizumab, sintilimab, nivolumab, toripalimab and 
tislelizumab), were as high as 30.1 ~ 6.1%, substantially 
higher than that of PD-L1 inhibitors (atezolizumab and 
durvalumab) (8.3% and 17.8%, respectively). According 
to the literature, the prevalence of thyroid dysfunction 
depends on the type of ICPis used [ 7, 8]. The incidence 
of thyroid dysfunction was 8.0 ~ 9.0% in patients treated

In our analysis, the vast majority of patients had thyroid 
irAEs of grade 1–2 and did not require discontinuation 
of medication, while a small percentage had grade 3 and 
required discontinuation of PD1/PD-L1. The study dem -
onstrated that among patients who developed immune 
thyroid dysfunction during the administration of ICPis, 
there was a low incidence of grade 2 or higher related 
adverse reactions, few required levothyroxine replace -
ment therapy [ 9], and usually there was no need to sus -
pend immunotherapy [10]. Therefore, it can be presumed 
that thyroid immune-related adverse events following 
PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors treatment in clinical practice are 
not severe and are manageable with regular monitoring 
of thyroid function.
Notably, with the power of our large dataset we were 
able to clearly delineate subsets of thyroid irAEs and 
describe new clinical and biochemical functional evolu -
tion. Our findings suggested that most thyroid irAEs was 
hypothyroidism, which was consistent with others [ 11]. 
Typically, thyroid dysfunction in PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors 
therapy has a bi- or tri-phasic course with brief and mild 
or asymptomatic thyrotoxicosis followed by an euthyroid 
state, which can further progress to hypothyroidism as 
frequently observed [ 12–14]. Such a clinical pattern is a 
result of the initial release of thyroid hormones due to 
destructive thyroiditis and a subsequent damage leading 
to hypothyroidism [13]. The conversion of thyrotoxicosis 
to hypothyroidism supported that destructive thyroiditis 
was the underlying process in ICPis related thyroid dys -
function [15, 16].
Interestingly, our clinical observation has revealed 
2 Graves’ disease patients and 3 subacute thyroid -
itis patients. Immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated 
Graves’ disease was rare but has been reported, where 
8.22% (6/73) of patients with Graves’ disease had posi -
tive TRAb or thyroid stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI) 
[17]. In our study, TRAb was positive in 3 Graves’ disease 
patients with consistent overt thyrotoxicosis. These cases 
demonstrated that thyroiditis may co-exist with Graves’ 
disease in patients with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors-related 
thyroid dysfunction. Subacute thyroiditis was another 
thyroid irAEs in our study which has not been reported 
in others. Due to the severe clinical symptoms and higher 
grades of this type of thyroid damage, which have a sig -
nificant impact on the patient’s life, PD1/PD-L1 inhibi -
tor often needed to be discontinued. Moreover, patients 
of this type in our study experienced permanent hypo -
thyroidism afterwards, which was quite different from

The pathogenesis of thyroid dysfunction caused by 
PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors is still unclear, but it can be con -
firmed that Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is the main cause of 
thyroid damage, as evidenced by the significant increase 
in autoantibodies in our study. The mechanism of thy -
roid dysfunction caused by PD-1 inhibitors may involve 
T cells and natural killer cell, monocyte-mediated path -
ways and so on [ 18]. Blocking of the PD-1 receptor with 
ICPis leads to a proliferation of T cells, possibly including 
autoreactive B cells, which attack the thyroid gland [ 19]. 
Patient with pembrolizumab-induced acute thyroiditis 
exhibited unique phenotypic differences from autoim -
mune disease patients, which included a decrease in 
immature NK cells (CD56 brCD16-) as well as a decrease 
in CD14 +HLA-DRlo/neg immunosuppressive monocytes 
[18]. In addition, Torimoto et al. found a marked increase 
in the proportion of Tfh cells 2 weeks after nivolumab, 
followed by a decrease at 4 months after treatment. PD-1 
inhibitors treatment led to suppression of PD-1/PD-L1 
inhibitory signals in Tfh cells, which may have enhanced 
Tfh cell proliferation and contributed to development of 
hypothyroidism [ 20]. The cause of subacute thyroiditis 
with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors is currently unknown and 
we speculate that it may be related to the susceptibility of 
patients to viral infections when their immune system is 
disturbed.
Regarding to the risk factors contributing to thyroid 
irAEs in PD1/PD-L1 inhibitors therapy, different studies 
revealed different results. Our results showed that diges -
tive tract cancer increased the risk of thyroid dysfunction 
by around 70%. One study also described the relationship 
between gastrointestinal cancer and thyroid hormone 
levels [ 21]. Furthermore, they found the serum T 3, FT 3, 
T4 and FT 4 in the patients with the malignant digestive 
tract tumors at stage III and IV were significantly lower 
than those in the patients at I and II, indicating an obvi -
ous downward trend with the aggravation of the disease 
[21].
In addition, our study showed the patients who received 
radiotherapy had higher incidence of thyroid dysfunc -
tion, though the radiotherapy did not focus on the neck 
directly; And multivariate Logistic regression analysis 
showed the history of radiation was a risk factor to PD-1/
PD-L1 inhibitors related thyroid dysfunction. Although 
the mechanism of radiation-induced hypothyroidism is 
not fully understood, the mechanisms of thyroidal disor -
ders might be concerned with vascular damage, capsular 
fibrosis, iodine insufficiency due to inadequate feeding, 
and direct parenchymal damage [22].
Targeted treatment was another risk factor for thyroid 
irAEs in our study. The most likely mechanism of small-
molecular vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 
(VEGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)-induced 
hypothyroidism is that VEGFR TKIs disrupt the blood 
supply network of the thyroid gland, resulting in reduced 
blood supply to the thyroid, leading to thyroiditis, which 
further leads to transient thyrotoxicosis and subsequent 
persistent hypothyroidism [23]. Other recently described 
mechanisms for sunitinib-induced hypothyroidism 
include impairment of thyroid iodine uptake [ 24] and 
inhibition of thyroid peroxidase [25].
Whether the presence of TgAb and/or TPOAb are 
important risk factors for thyroid function abnormali -
ties caused by PD-1 inhibitors still needs to be verified by 
a large number of studies. Anti-thyroid antibodies were 
present in some non-small cell lung cancer patients with 
thyroid dysfunction treated with pembrolizumab [ 26]. 
Elevated TgAb and/or TPOAb levels were associated 
with an increased risk of thyroid dysfunction compared 
to baseline levels prior to treatment [ 27, 28]. However, 
some scholars believed that thyroid dysfunction induced 
by PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors was independent of thyroid 
autoantibodies, and speculated that it may be related 
to other autoantibodies [ 14]. In our study, TgAb and/or 
TPOAb became positive after the treatment of PD-1/
PD-L1 inhibitors in some patients, but were negative 
in some other patients with thyroid dysfunction. These 
observations suggest that thyroid autoantibodies may 
predispose the risk of developing thyroiditis caused by 
PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in some patients.
Although we are concerned about and analyzing PD1/
PD-L1 inhibitors related thyroid irAEs, there are some 
reports showing that patients with endocrine gland dam -
age during PD1/PD-L1 treatment of tumors have a better 
prognosis [26, 29, 30]. In other words, irAEs were found 
to be related to the efficacy of ICPis and patients with 
irAEs often had better prognosis. No significant mortality 
difference was observed between groups (absolute differ -
ence 0.02%, P = 0.228), though the slight numerical imbal-
ance may warrant further investigation in larger cohorts. 
Importantly, while thyroid irAEs showed no overall sur -
vival association in univariate analysis, the absence of 
thyroid irAEs was identified as an independent predictor 
of increased mortality risk, particularly in gastrointesti -
nal cancers. The significant survival advantage observed 
in patients developing thyroid irAEs suggests these 
immune-related events may serve as clinical indicators 
of enhanced immunotherapy response. This protective 
association likely stems from thyroid irAEs representing 
systemic immune activation, consistent with established 
correlations between endocrine irAEs and treatment effi -
cacy [30].
From our analysis, it showed that monitoring of thy -
roid function is necessary in cancer patients undergoing 
PD-1/PD-L1 treatment. On the one hand, early detec -
tion of abnormal thyroid function can lead to effective

treatment to thyroid disfunction, and on the other hand, 
the effectiveness of PD-1/PD-L1 treatment can be pre -
dicted based on the occurrence of thyroid irAEs. Since 
thyroid disease is usually asymptomatic at the early stage, 
and may only be detected by laboratory examination as 
subclinical thyroid dysfunction and the clinical mani -
festations of hypothyroidism patients are chills, fatigue, 
lethargy, memory loss, loss of appetite, etc., which is 
lack specific symptoms and signs and often atypical and 
can be easily confused with late-stage tumors or chemo -
therapy reactions. Therefore, regular evaluation of thy -
roid function is crucial to detect early signs of thyroid 
dysfunction. Additionally, it should be noted that if FT 4 
is reduced while TSH is not high enough, the possibility 
of central hypothyroidism should be considered and the 
patient should be evaluated for immunotherapy-induced 
hypophysitis.
As a retrospective analysis, our study has its inherent 
obstacles. In addition, our research is a single-center ret -
rospective study although it is a large tertiary center, and 
the sample size of patients with each malignant tumor is 
very limited. Therefore, there may be some deficiencies 
in the comprehensive evaluation of thyroid function, and 
further research is needed.
In conclusion, in our group, the incidence of thy -
roid irAEs related to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors treatment 
was 22.3%, with the main types being hypothyroidism, 
and most is mild to moderate (grade 1–2), and only a 
few require interruption or discontinuation of the PD1/
PD-L1 treatment. Multiple risk factors including gas -
trointestinal cancer, previous radiotherapy, history of 
targeted therapy, baseline TgAb and/or TPOAb positiv -
ity may contribute to the thyroid irAEs following PD-1/
PD-L1 inhibitors therapy in our group. Patients with thy-
roid irAEs in gastrointestinal cancer, had a longer median 
overall survival compared with patients without thyroid 
irAEs, indicating thyroid irAEs resulting from PD-1/
PD-L1 inhibitors in some kind of cancers may be a use -
ful marker for a better prognosis. Close monitoring is 
required before and during the treatment of PD-1/PD-L1 
inhibitors.
