46,XX testicular/ovotesticular disorders of sexual development (T/OT DSD) are infrequent congenital conditions characterized by the presence of functional ovarian and testicular or only testicular parenchyma.

The aim of the study was to retrospectively describe clinical, hormonal, and genetic characteristics of 29 patients with 46,XX T/OT DSD (2000–2023), focusing on gonadal function, hormonal production, and long-term follow-up.

Most patients (n= 25, 86.2%) presented with atypical genitalia that suggested DSD. Median age at first assessment was 0.38 years. Sex assignment was male in 21 patients without reports of discordant gender identity. Sex assignment was recommended before expert evaluation and without adequate studies in 64% of those patients with atypical genitalia (16/25). The median external masculinization score was 8 (range 4–12). During mini-puberty, LH, testosterone, AMH, and the LH/FSH ratio were above the female reference range and no different from the normal male reference range. Spontaneous puberty was observed in one female and 10 male-assigned subjects. Among the latter, pubertal virilization occurred with signs of hypergonadotropic hypogonadism and gynecomastia. Molecular studies identified the underlying mechanism in 7 patients:SRYgene was identified in two,WT1gene variations were detected in three others, and 2 syndromic patients harbored complex chromosomal rearrangements.

Our findings underscore the clinical and biochemical variability in 46,XX T/OT DSD. Expert evaluation and accurate diagnostic work-up are essential prior to sex assignment and to prevent misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatments. Mini-puberty was characterized by a masculinized pattern of gonadotropin secretion. The potential for functional male pubertal development should be taken into account when making sex assignment decisions.

Keywords:46,XX; Disorder of sexual development; Ovotesticular disorders of sexual development; Testicular disorders of sexual development

Disorders/differences of sexual development (DSD) encompass congenital conditions where chromosomal, gonadal, and/or anatomical sex development is atypical. Among these, 46,XX DSD include two main categories: disorders of gonadal/ovarian development and disorders secondary to extragonadal androgen excess. Within the former, 46,XX testicular/ovotesticular DSD (46,XX T/OT DSD) represents a highly heterogeneous and infrequent disorder characterized by the presence of functional testicular tissue, alone or coexistent with functional ovarian tissue. The clinical phenotype presents with a variable degree of genital androgenization, presence of Müllerian remnants, and development of Wolffian derivatives [1,2].

In 46,XX newborns, the presence of testicular tissue can be assumed by the detection of increased AMH levels; however, there are no reliable biochemical markers of the presence of ovarian tissue [3,4]. Consequently, histological evaluation of the gonads is critical to characterize gonadal differentiation, although the small size and heterogeneity of gonadal tissue may challenge the representativeness of biopsy samples [5]. This information is relevant for sex assignment and might also allow the understanding of developmental aspects and anticipate potential for adult hormonal production and fertility as ovarian parenchyma usually remains better preserved, while testicular tissue reveals dysgenetic features and progressive fibrotic changes, as found in earlier studies [5,6]. Histological evaluation of gonadal parenchyma is also crucial to identify germ cell malignancies [5,7,8]. There is limited information regarding the risk for the development of germ cell malignancies in patients with a complete absence of Y-chromosome genetic material. In this context, we previously documented cases of early invasive and pre-invasive germ cell lesions in patients withSRY-negative 46,XX T/OT DSD, suggesting a moderate risk [2,5]. This finding contrasts with earlier reports; however, the mechanisms underlying this discrepancy observed in our cohort remain unidentified [5].

Over the past decades, several molecular mechanisms have been implicated in the development of 46,XX T/OT DSD. These includeSRYtranslocations (usually on the X chromosome), chromosomal rearrangements that result in dysregulation ofSRY-related gene expression (e.g.,SOX9, SOX3, SOX10), loss-of-function variants in genes involved in ovarian development (e.g.,RSPO1, WNT4, NR2F2), a specific amino acid change in the SF1 protein (p.Arg92Trp variant), and, more recently, variants located in the fourth zinc finger of theWT1gene [2,9–21]. The recognition of shared molecular mechanisms involved in the development of both testicular and ovotesticular 46,XX DSD suggests that these conditions might represent different manifestations along a spectrum of a single underlying disorder [22].

The clinical and biochemical heterogeneity observed among patients with 46,XX T/OT DSD presents significant challenges in establishing general recommendations for sex assignment at birth [23,24]. An individualized approach that includes a thorough assessment by an experienced interdisciplinary team is critical to guide clinical decisions and optimize patient care. Long-term follow-up studies are essential to better understand long-term clinical outcomes, including gonadal function, hormonal production, and gender identity development [25].

In this context, our study aimed to describe the clinical presentation, gonadal characterization, and long-term follow-up of a cohort of 29 patients with 46,XX T/OT DSD evaluated at a single institution. Special interest is placed on gonadal function and hormonal production during mini-puberty and puberty, with a particular focus on male-assigned individuals.

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study by reviewing the medical records of patients with 46,XX testicular/ovotesticular DSD from the cohort of DSD patients followed at Hospital de Pediatría “Prof. Dr. Juan P. Garrahan” between January 2000 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria were 46,XX karyotype and evidence of functional testicular tissue based on at least one of the following parameters: serum AMH levels above the female reference range, testosterone increase after hCG stimulation in the male reference range, or gonadal histological evaluation. All patients were examined by trained pediatric endocrinologists. External masculinization score (EMS, range: 0–12) was used to quantify the degree of virilization [26].

Serum hormone levels were measured as follows: LH, FSH, testosterone, and estradiol using automated chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA, Abbott – ARCHITECT i4000), and AMH through 2-site ELISA (Beckman Coulter Gen II). The assays were performed according to the standard operating procedures recommended by the manufacturer. Detection limits and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV%) were as follows: for LH and FSH, 0.06 mIU/mL and 4.4–2.9; for testosterone, 0.023 ng/mL (0.08 nmol/L) and 3.6–6.3; for estradiol, 13.5 pg/mL and 1.4–6.4, and 9.5; and for AMH, 0.6 pmol/L and 7.7, respectively.

For the hCG stimulation test, two intramuscular doses of 2,500 IU hCG were administered on days 0 and 3 and the response was evaluated on day 7. Testosterone increase above 1.1 ng/mL after hCG stimulation was considered a normal testicular response [27].

For the purposes of this study, pubertal onset was defined by the appearance of secondary sexual characteristics (e.g., breast development and/or genital changes such as penile growth or testicular volume >3 mL) with subsequent documented clinical progression, and biochemical evidence of an active hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis [28]. Serum samples from 23 male and 22 female healthy infants during mini-puberty were used for the reference values (own unpublished data). For determinations outside mini-puberty, published normal reference values for age, sex, and Tanner stage were used [29–31].

SRYgene detection was performed using PCR and/or Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA, SALSA Probemix P185-C2, Tecnolab, Buenos Aires, Argentina) in DNA from lymphocytes from peripheral blood samples and/or DNA extracted from gonadal tissue when available. To identify deletions and duplications in known genes related to DSD, MLPA analysis was conducted in 17 patients. In syndromic patients, the presence of copy number variations (CNVs) was further studied using a whole genome CGH microarray platform (Agilent Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) containing 180,000 oligonucleotide probes for ∼900 clinically relevant regions of the human genome known to play a role in genetic disease including genes for syndromic conditions associated with altered gonadal differentiation and genitourinary tract development. This design enables the detection of gains and losses with the resolution of 10–25 kb at the targeted gene loci and 50–150 kb for the remaining genomic regions. Array CGH analysis was performed using reference DNA from normal female individuals.

Missense and nonsense genetic variants as well as small deletions and insertions in a group of candidate genes (NR0B1, RSPO1, WNT4, FOXL2, WT1) were assessed by Sanger sequencing in 16 patients. In 9 patients, whole exome sequencing (WES) using the Agilent Sure Select XT Human All Exon v5 kit was performed. Bioinformatic filtering pipeline and genomic analysis tools were applied to evaluate suspected pathogenic variants in genes implicated in female-to-male sex reversal, development of the bipotential gonad, ovarian differentiation, gonadal dysgenesis, and steroidogenesis.

Gonadal tissue samples were evaluated using the methodology as described in our previous publication [5]. All available samples were reviewed by members of the study team with expertise in gonadal pathology. Among theSRY-negative patients, 16 were already evaluated and published in our previous study (P1–3, 5–14, 16, 17, and 19). In the present study, gonadal samples from 4 additional patients (P24–27) were analyzed following the same protocol. The mean age at biopsy/gonadectomy in these cases was 1.9 years (range 0.17–3.9 years). We also include data from histological reports for 4 additional patients (P4, 15, 20, and 22); however, tissue samples for these cases were not available for direct review (mean age at gonadal biopsy/gonadectomy 2 years, range 0.5–3.8 years). Gonadal tissue was not available for 5 patients (P18, 21, 23, 28, and 29), and no histological reports could be retrieved for them.

Statistical analysis was performed using InfoStat 2020 [32]. Descriptive analyses were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with ranges, while categorical variables were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to test the normality of sample data and the Wilcoxon or Kruskal-Wallis test were used for comparisons. Apvalue of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Among the cohort of 209 46,XX DSD patients followed at the “Prof. Dr. Juan P. Garrahan” Pediatric Hospital (January 2000–December 2023), we identified 29 T/OT DSD. The median age at first assessment in our hospital was 0.38 years, ranging from birth to 16.3 years of age. Most patients presented with atypical genitalia at birth suggestive of DSD (25 patients, 86.2%) and these patients were younger at the time of the initial evaluation (mean age 0.25 vs. 11.25 years, p 0.036). Among the remaining 4 cases, 1 patient was referred due to distal hypospadias (P18,Table 1), while the other three presented with typical male genitalia (P21, P23, P29;Table 1). In two of the latter cases, the diagnosis was suspected during adolescence due to short stature, small testicular volume, and gynecomastia.

Among the patients with atypical genitalia, sex assignment was recommended in 16 cases (64%) prior to expert evaluation and without adequate studies. In two of these cases, congenital adrenal hyperplasia was incorrectly assumed, and hydrocortisone treatment was administered. Multidisciplinary team evaluation at our institution was requested in 14 patients regarding sex assignment, resulting in a recommendation to rectify the sex designation at birth in 3 cases after a median of 22 days. Final sex assignment in the entire group was male in 21 patients (72.4%) and female in 8. No cases of discordant gender identity were reported during follow-up (mean age at last assessment 12.9 years, range 0.1–21.7). Of note, 14 patients were older than 12 years at the time of the last evaluation.

EMS was available for 26 cases, with a median score of 8 (range: 4–12). The median EMS was lower in those assigned females compared to those assigned males (5.75 vs. 8.50,p0.0048). At the initial evaluation, gonadal position was labioscrotal in 22 gonads (39%), inguinal in 15 (26%), intra-abdominal in 20 (35%), and only one gonad remained unfound.

Müllerian derivatives were identified in 18 patients. In 14 of these, the structures were visualized by ultrasound, and in the remaining 4, they were identified through laparoscopic evaluation. Most structures corresponded to a uterus. In patients P8 and P12, the structures were characterized as a hemi-uterus. Patients P5, P14, and P19 presented two separated hemi-uteri. In patient P17, the structure was rudimentary, and histological analysis confirmed it as a Müllerian derivative.

Data on pubertal development were available for 16 patients. Four female-assigned patients required hormonal induction of puberty due to early gonadectomy (P2,8,14,15;Table 1). In P2 and 15, the presence of intermingled ovarian and testicular tissue, without clear margins, made it impossible to remove the testicular tissue while preserving the ovarian tissue in situ. In P8 and 14, the presence of germ cell malignancies precluded any fertility preservation efforts. One male-assigned patient with testicular DSD started pubertal induction with testosterone replacement at the age of 12.5 years due to hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, in the context of prior surgical descent of bilateral high intra-abdominal gonads (P19;Table 1). Among the 11 patients who developed spontaneous puberty, the only one that was assigned female had one ovary preserved in situ following laparoscopic exploration with bilateral biopsy and removal of the left ovotesticular gonad (P4;Table 1). This patient started breast development at 11.9 years, experienced menarche at 13.9 years, and reported regular menses. Although clinical and biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism were observed during pubertal development, the persistence of testicular tissue was not demonstrated (laparoscopic exploration and biopsy at 18 years of age).

The 10 remaining patients who experienced spontaneous puberty were raised as males. Data regarding age of pubertal onset were available only in four (Table 3). All of these patients achieved adequate clinical virilization, together with moderate-to-severe gynecomastia during puberty. Final testicular volume was small in most pubertal patients (Table 3). In P1, a right gonadectomy was performed before puberty after gonadoblastoma was found on biopsy. Left gonadectomy was recommended by the medical team for patient P6 due to the presence of painful hemorrhagic cysts. The patient and their parents agreed with the recommendation, and the procedure was performed at 16.8 years of age. Three additional patients underwent bilateral gonadectomy during or after puberty. The indications were parental decision in a patient with severe intellectual disability who presented with cyclic pain and bilateral scrotal enlargement with hemorrhagic cysts (P3, 13 years); bilateral torsion (P16, 17 years); and patient preference related to cyclic gonadal pain and gynecomastia (P17, 17.8 years). In the latter case, mature germ cells were identified exclusively in the ovarian tissue, and fertility preservation options were declined by the patient.

Hormonal assessments during spontaneous puberty were available for 10 male-raised patients. In most cases, gonadotropin levels were increased, while testosterone levels were within the male pubertal range (Table 3). Few paired early and late pubertal samples were available for comparison and no differences were found in basal serum gonadotropins, testosterone, and estradiol levels. Unfortunately, pre- and post-gonadectomy hormonal data were not available in pubertal patients (P3, P16, and P17).

The results of gonadal histological analysis were previously published [5]. Briefly, we reported bilateral ovotestes as the most frequent histological finding, without histological alterations found in the ovarian parenchyma, while signs of dysgenesis were seen in all cases of testicular parenchyma. In 4 patients, the first sample obtained from gonadal biopsy failed to identify ovarian tissue that was later demonstrated during pubertal evaluation. We detected early prepubertal, pre-invasive and invasive malignancies in this cohort (5 patients had undifferentiated gonadal tissue, 5 patients had gonadoblastoma, and 1 patient had dysgerminoma). Eight new samples have been added to the present report, with no malignancies detected in any of them. Data from gonadal biopsy or gonadectomy were available for 24 patients (a total of 45 gonads). Most gonads were classified as ovotestes due to the coexistence of ovarian and testicular tissue (30/45, 67%).

Genetic studies allowed the identification of a molecular mechanism in 7 patients.WT1gene variations affecting the fourth zinc finger were detected in 3 patients (P14, P19, P20), and complex chromosomal rearrangements detected in 2 syndromic patients (P3, P16) were previously reported [2,5]. The presence of a translocatedSRYgene was identified in 2 patients (P18, P29).

In this study, we report our experience in the assessment and follow-up of 29 patients with 46,XX testicular/ovotesticular disorders of gonadal development. Our findings illustrate the heterogeneity and complexity of the disorder, that emphasize the need for a multidisciplinary approach in the management of these patients.

Most of the patients in our cohort presented with atypical genitalia at birth, a finding that may reflect the bias inherent in a pediatric hospital setting as detection was later in those without atypical features in the neonatal period. In some patients with atypical genitalia, a precise diagnosis was delayed, and sex assignment was recommended before adequate evaluation, leading not only to inaccurate diagnoses but also to inappropriate interventions. The misidentification of 2 patients as having congenital adrenal hyperplasia resulted in unnecessary hydrocortisone treatments and highlighted the importance of involving experienced teams to guide decision-making and avoid potentially harmful interventions. Although none of the patients in our cohort experienced discordant gender identity, long-term monitoring and psychological support remain essential to address any future concerns.

In 46,XX OT DSD, preservation of ovarian tissue separated from the testicular component has been proposed to avoid virilization in patients with female identity, allow female puberty, and preserve fertility potential [6,33–35]. However, there is limited information on testicular preservation and pubertal gonadal function in male-raised patients [33,36]. Our follow-up data on pubertal development support that preserving testicular tissue in situ in patients with 46,XX OT DSD also provides an opportunity for spontaneous hormone production and pubertal development in male-assigned patients. While spontaneous masculinization was observed, clinical signs of hypergonadotropic hypogonadism and estrogenization, likely due to the presence of ovarian tissue, were also frequently noted. Previous reports proved that ovarian resection with preservation of the testicular tissue was followed by an improvement in testosterone levels with an increase in gonadotropin levels above the reference range [37]. Unfortunately, data to compare hormonal levels before and after gonadectomy were not available in our pubertal patients. In pubertal and post-pubertal male-assigned patients, the presence of ovarian tissue also leads to considerable morbidity, such as gonadal torsion, hemorrhagic cysts, and pain, that guided the decision of gonadectomy. These aspects must be anticipated and discussed with patients and families by the multidisciplinary team.

Biochemical data support the value of AMH to confirm the presence of functional testicular tissue in prepubertal patients. The lack of reliable biochemical markers for ovarian tissue remains challenging and emphasizes the critical role of histological examination. The postnatal surge of gonadotropins and sex steroids during mini-puberty is a sexually dimorphic physiological phenomenon [38,39]. The mechanism of this sex difference is not completely understood. Animal studies suggest that fetal or perinatal exposure to androgenic steroids exert organizational actions on the GnRH neuronal network and the control of LH secretion [40]. Similarly, data from infants with congenital adrenal hyperplasia and androgen insensitivity syndrome have provided valuable insights into the role of in-utero androgen exposure in the regulation of gonadotropin secretion in humans. These findings suggest that differences in the prenatal hormonal environment may directly influence the development and function of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis [41,42]. In our cohort of 46,XX patients with an ovotesticular/testicular disorder of gonadal development, the hormonal profile during mini-puberty showed a masculinized pattern of gonadotropin secretion with LH serum levels and the LH/FSH ratio above the female reference range and within the normal male reference range. This finding supports the concept that prenatal and early postnatal androgen exposure is involved in the programming of LH secretion independently of chromosomal sex.

FSH levels in our cohort were higher than those observed in typical male infants. Although AMH concentrations remained within the male reference range, they tended to be lower than those of male controls, without reaching statistical significance. Similarly, Lambert et al. reported neonatal AMH levels that fell between normal female and male references, in twelve 46,XXSRY-negative OT DSD [3]. In addition, while the LH/FSH ratio did not differ significantly from that of typical males, it showed a trend toward intermediate values between male and female reference ranges. Together these findings may reflect impaired gonadal function. As we previously reported, histological analysis of the testicular parenchyma revealed features of dysgenesis, which may account for the relative reduction in AMH production and the compensatory increase in FSH levels [5].

Gonadal histological evaluation remains a cornerstone for accurate diagnosis. In our cohort, there was a high prevalence of ovotestes (86.1%), and we identified early prepubertal pre-invasive and invasive malignancies, as discussed in our previous publication [5]. Regarding fertility, as previously reported, in the limited number of pubertal gonadal samples available, ovarian tissue appeared to be better preserved [5,43]. Only one female-assigned patient retained ovarian tissue in situ, which was sufficient to support complete female pubertal development. Unfortunately, no information is available regarding her adult life or long-term fertility outcomes. In pubertal testicular tissue, germ cells were either absent or very scarce, without complete spermatogenesis [5]. Chen et al. reported azoospermia in all 128 adult males with 46,XX disorders of gonadal development [43]. It has been proposed that azoospermia would be secondary to the absence of the Y chromosome that is required for the expression of genetic factors with a critical role in spermatogenesis. To date, no successful pregnancy has been reported in couples where the male partner has 46,XX disorder of gonadal development. Preservation of female gametes in male-assigned patients poses additional challenges, regarding patient acceptance and social implications.

Molecular studies identified the underlying mechanism in 7 of our patients (24.1%). Similarly, low rates of molecular diagnostic yield have been reported in other large cohorts of 46,XX patients with disorders of gonadal development [3,44]. Over the past decades, epigenetic regulation has been shown to be involved in human gonadal development [45,46]. Innovative studies that enable the identification of these mechanisms will be essential to elucidate their role in the etiology of 46,XX disorders of gonadal development.

This study has the limitation of a relatively small cohort size and the inherent heterogeneity of the condition. Nevertheless, our experience caring for 29 individuals with 46,XX testicular/ovotesticular disorders of gonadal development offers valuable insight into the real-world challenges faced by the diagnostic and clinical complexity of this rare condition. The wide phenotypic variability, frequent delays in diagnosis, and risk of inappropriate early interventions highlight the critical need for timely assessment by specialized multidisciplinary teams. Our data emphasize the value of hormonal and histological evaluations in guiding clinical decisions and long-term management. Fertility potential remains limited, and molecular diagnoses are still elusive in most cases, calling for further research into underlying genetic and epigenetic mechanisms. Ongoing follow-up is essential to support physical health, psychosocial development, and informed decision-making throughout life.
