# Whole-Body Muscle MRI in Non-5q Spinal Muscular Atrophy: Patterns, Genotype Prediction, and Diagnostic Implications.
**Authors:** Berling E, Reyngoudt H, Le Guillou A, Noury JB, Cintas P, Cadot B, Ackermann-Bonan I, Marty B, Fromes Y, Fernandez-Eulate G, Masingue M, Béhin A, Bassez G, Rouyer A, Lefeuvre C, Laforet P, Nicolas G, Konyukh M, Quadrio I, Carlier RY, Stojkovic T
**Journal:** Neurol Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1212/NXG.0000000000200433](https://doi.org/10.1212/NXG.0000000000200433)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a group of genetically heterogeneous motor neuron disorders characterized by progressive, predominantly proximal weakness. Non-5q SMA refers to forms occurring without SMN1 pathogenic variants or deletions. More than half of non-5q SMA cases remain genetically unresolved, and muscle MRI may reveal characteristic patterns supporting diagnosis.

METHODS: We retrospectively analyzed multicenter data from adults presenting with chronic, progressive, motor-predominant weakness, beginning proximally in the upper or lower limbs, with exclusive or predominant motor involvement on nerve conduction studies and neurogenic changes on EMG. Whole-body MRI was qualitatively graded (Mercuri scale, 42 paired muscles) and quantitatively analyzed in the lower limbs.

RESULTS: Among 28 patients (median age 46 [26-50] years), 23 (82.1%) carried pathogenic variants in 13 genes, most commonly VWA1 (n = 5), TRPV4 (n = 4), and VRK1 and DYNC1H1 (n = 3 each). Distinct and reproducible MRI patterns were identified in VRK1-related, DYNC1H1-related, and VWA1-related cases. In VRK1-related SMA, the pattern combined upper limb and iliopsoas sparing with severe gluteal involvement, diffuse thigh involvement, and relative preservation of the posterior tibialis and medial gastrocnemius despite early calf involvement. DYNC1H1-related cases were characterized by sparing of the gluteus maximus, isolated adductor magnus involvement, predominant anterior thigh involvement with marked long head of biceps femoris involvement, and severe triceps surae involvement. In VWA1-related SMA, the pattern included selective gluteus maximus and quadriceps involvement with iliopsoas, gracilis, and sartorius preservation, and predominant peroneal involvement with relative extensor digitorum longus sparing. In TRPV4-related SMA, patterns of muscle involvement were heterogeneous, but the sternocleidomastoid was severely affected in 2 of 4 patients, whereas this muscle remained unaffected in all other cohort members.

DISCUSSION: Whole-body muscle MRI appears to be a valuable diagnostic tool for non-5q SMA. Gene-specific imaging signatures, particularly for VRK1, DYNC1H1, and VWA1, can guide targeted genetic testing and support interpretation of variants of uncertain significance. Sternocleidomastoid involvement may represent a specific marker of TRPV4-related SMA. However, confirmation in larger cohorts is needed, although this is constrained by the rarity of these disorders.

Hereditary motor neuropathies (HMN) are a clinically, electrophysiologically, and genetically heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by motor neuron weakness with minimal sensory involvement, distinguishing them from Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease and hereditary sensory neuropathies.1 Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) refers to HMN with predominant proximal weakness. Although most cases are linked to SMN1 deletions, non-5q SMA encompasses diverse phenotypes associated with variants in multiple genes, including BICD2, DYNC1H1, and TRPV4.1-4 Despite advances in sequencing, many patients lack a definitive diagnosis, and variants of uncertain significance (VUS) remain challenging to interpret.4

In genetic muscle diseases, muscle MRI supports diagnosis through pattern recognition, guides genetic testing, and may reduce the need for biopsy.5-14 Quantitative approaches further enhance sensitivity.15 In HMN, MRI reveals selective muscle involvement, and whole-body MRI (WB-MRI) extends detection to regions such as tongue and upper limbs.16,17

We aimed to characterize WB-MRI patterns in non-5q SMA to improve diagnostic stratification and VUS interpretation.

Patients were evaluated and followed at the Departments of Neurology of Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital, Raymond-Poincaré Hospital, and the University Hospitals of Toulouse and Brest in France all of which are part of the French network of neuromuscular centers (FILNEMUS).

We enrolled adults with a confirmed diagnosis of SMA who were followed at tertiary referral centers specializing in peripheral nerve disorders. The diagnosis of SMA was based on a chronic, progressive motor-predominant weakness, typically starting proximally in either upper or lower limbs, with or without distal involvement. Electrophysiologic studies demonstrated an exclusive or predominant motor involvement, with neurogenic patterns on needle EMG in clinically affected muscles.

All patients tested negative for SMN1 gene deletion or pathogenic variants. Patients were stratified into 2 cohorts according to whether an associated gene had been identified. All genetic findings were identified using NGS approaches (either targeted gene panels or whole-exome/genome sequencing) on a clinical basis. Identified variants were classified according to ACMG/AMP criteria (eTable 1) as pathogenic or likely pathogenic. No VUS were retained in the cohort. For individuals without an identified causal variant, the coding regions and exon-intron boundaries (±30 bp) of the following genes were analyzed, in accordance with the National French Consensus Gene List for the Diagnosis of Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease and Related Disorders (eTable 2).18

WB-MRI was performed using 1.5 T or 3 T clinical MRI systems (GE or Siemens). T1-weighted and fat-suppressed T2-weighted sequences were acquired with axial and coronal stacks covering the whole body. T1-weighted sequences (echo time [TE] = 9.8 ms, repetition time [TR] = 450–800 ms) were acquired in 6 mm slices with in-plane resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 mm2. Fat-suppressed T2-weighted images (TE = 45 ms, TR = 3,500 ms) were acquired in 6 mm slices with in-plane resolution of 1.1 × 1.1 mm2. On GE platforms, IDEAL-T2 fat images were used as substitutes for standard Siemens T1w sequences. Muscle involvement was assessed using the Mercuri visual grading scale for the degree of muscle-fat replacement on T1w images, scored from 1 (normal) to 4 (end-stage disease): 1 = normal, 2 = fatty replacement <30%, 3 = 30–60%, and 4 = >60%.19 On fat-suppressed T2 images (IDEAL-T2 water), muscle edema was defined as areas of increased signal within preserved structure. Forty-two paired muscles, 4 limb muscle groups, and the tongue were evaluated by 2 experienced raters (Y.F. or R.Y.C. and E.B.). Heat maps were generated in Microsoft Excel.

In addition, in one of the centers, Pitié-Salpêtrière, quantitative fat fraction (FF) mapping was performed on 21 patients using a chemical-shift–based (Dixon-type) 3D gradient-echo sequence (3 echoes, TE = 2.75, 3.95, 5.15 ms, TR = 10 ms, flip angle 3°, resolution 1 × 1 × 5 mm3, 64 slices). To achieve proton density weighting in the chemical-shift–encoding qMRI sequence (Dixon-type), the acquisition parameters were selected with a short TR and a low-flip angle. This combination ensures that we have little or no T1 weighting in the images and that no correction for T1 is therefore necessary. Data were obtained in both thighs and legs using an 18-channel body radiofrequency (RF) coil in combination with a 32-channel posterior RF coil. For the thighs, the center slice was placed at the origin of the biceps femoris short head muscle. For the legs, the center slice was located at the origin of the popliteus muscle. Segmentation was performed using the ITK-SNAP software tool. Muscle regions of interest (ROIs) were manually drawn in 11 muscles of the thighs (adductor longus, adductor magnus, biceps femoris long head, gracilis, rectus femoris, sartorius, semimembranosus, semitendinosus, vastus intermedius, vastus lateralis, and vastus medialis) and 7 muscles of the legs (extensor digitorum, gastrocnemius lateralis, gastrocnemius medialis, peroneus longus, soleus, tibialis anterior, and tibialis posterior). The procedure is to segment the 5 central slices on the 2D multislice multiecho (MSME) images, which are spaced in an equidistant manner, used for quantitative T2 mapping. The slices of the 3D Dixon sequence, corresponding to these 5 slices on MSME, are segmented. FF values presented here correspond to the mean value across these 5 slices (weighted for the size of the ROI/segment). The central slice was based on anatomical landmarks: for thighs, the last slice where the short head of the biceps femoris was visible; for legs, the last slice where the popliteus muscle was visible. FF maps were reconstructed based on the graph cut algorithm using a custom-developed script, as described in earlier published work.20

We systematically screened the literature for reports describing gene-specific patterns of muscle involvement on MRI. For each gene identified in our cohort, we searched PubMed using the keywords “MRI,” “imaging,” or “magnetic resonance” combined with the gene name. Retrieved articles were reviewed, and those reporting muscle MRI findings in cohorts of patients with HMN were included. For each gene, we recorded whether a characteristic pattern of involvement had been described.

For each published pattern, muscles were classified as involved (Mercuri score [MS] = 3–4), spared (MS = 1–2), or indeterminate when data were contradictory or not available (e.g., no shoulder girdle data as only lower limbs were reported). We then compared these patterns with the MRI findings of our cohort to determine the number of discordant muscles (NDM; i.e., the number of muscles involved when expected to be spared, and vice versa). The NDM score was calculated separately for each side; thus, bilateral discordance of a given muscle (e.g., left and right triceps) contributed 2 points to the total score. A lower NDM indicated greater concordance between a patient's MRI and the published pattern. This corresponded to the calculation of Hamming distance.21

The same approach was applied to quantitative MRI data, considering a muscle as moderately to strongly involved when the FF exceeded 30%.

Data are summarized as median values with corresponding interquartile ranges.

Quantitative variables were compared with the Welch t test, and categorical variables were compared with the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. For all tests, p values < 0.05 were considered to indicate significant differences. All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

A total of 28 patients were included, with a slight male predominance (64.3%). A confirmed genetic diagnosis was established in 23 patients (82.1%). The most frequently implicated genes were VWA1 (n = 5), TRPV4 (n = 4), and VRK1 and DYNC1H1 (n = 3 each). The remaining genes—BICD2, BSCL2, CHCHD10, HEXB, MORC2, PLEKHG5, SETX, and VCP—were each found in a single patient. Genetic variants identified are presented in eTable 2.

We compared patients with (n = 23) and patients without an identified associated gene (n = 5). In the overall cohort, 57.1% of patients had a proximo-distal weakness pattern, 14.3% had a purely proximal presentation, and 28.6% had a scapuloperoneal pattern; among patients without an identified causal gene, the distribution was 60%, 20%, and 20%, respectively. The distribution of weakness patterns did not differ between patients with and those without an identified associated gene (p > 0.99). The median age at clinical evaluation for the cohort was 46 [26–50] years, with comparable values across groups (46.4 [34–49] vs 49.6 [21–61] years, p = 0.85). Disease onset occurred at a median age of 5.5 [2–22] years, and childhood onset was reported in 64.3% overall similarly distributed between groups (p > 0.99). The median non-5q SMA diagnostic delay was 21.0 [8–26] years, with similar durations across groups (22.8 [11–27] vs 8.1 [7–15] years, p = 0.60).

Clinical severity was variable. Across all patients, the median medical research council sum score was 46 [39–51], and no significant difference was observed between genetically diagnosed and undiagnosed patients (46.4 [39–49] vs 46.5 [40–51], p = 0.27). Functional disability was moderate overall, with a median modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 3.0 [2–3]. Among the cohort, 7 patients required a cane to walk (4/7 with a genetic diagnosis) and 3 used a wheelchair (2/3 with a genetic diagnosis). Sensory abnormalities were present in 11.1% of patients overall, exclusively in genetically diagnosed patients (13.0% vs none). Cranial or bulbar involvement was present in 3 genetically diagnosed cases: bilateral ptosis in 1 patient with a HEXB pathogenic variant (age 49), dysphonia due to vocal cord paralysis in a BSCL2-related case (age 21), and facial weakness in a BICD2-related case (age 48). Bladder dysfunction was rare (7.4%) and evenly distributed between groups. No patients reported dysphagia, and ataxia was infrequent, occurring only in a patient with a VWA1 pathogenic variant.

Deep tendon reflexes were absent in all limbs in 39.3% of patients and only in the lower limbs in 34.8%, with comparable rates across both groups (p > 0.99). These reflexes were retained in 25.0% of cases overall, with brisk responses observed in 1 patient (VRK1-related). Respiratory involvement was reported in 17.9% of the entire cohort, with similar frequencies in genetically diagnosed (17.4%) and undiagnosed (20.0%) patients, p > 0.99. Noninvasive ventilation was required in a BSCL2-related case, 2 TRPV4-related cases, and a genetically undiagnosed patient. Among the 14 patients with available slow vital capacity (SVC) data, the median SVC was 100.5 [70.5–112]. No cardiac manifestations were observed.

Neurophysiologic studies showed reduced motor amplitude with normal motor conduction velocity (MCV) values (median nerve MCV values systematically >40 m/s). Sensory conduction abnormalities, consistent with a length-dependent sensory neuropathy, were present in 17.9% of patients overall, with comparable distribution between groups (17.4% vs 20.0%, p > 0.99). These patients included a 51-year-old woman with a VWA1 pathogenic variant, a 64-year-old woman with no genetic diagnosis, a 21-year-old woman with a BSCL2 pathogenic variant, a 38-year-old man with a PLEKHG5 pathogenic variant, and a 34-year-old man with a TRPV4 pathogenic variant. In all the patients with sensory conduction abnormalities, sensory involvement was mild because sensory nerve action potentials in all limbs remained detectable.

We report a cohort of patients with non-5q SMA who underwent WB-MRI, allowing us to characterize diagnostic muscle involvement patterns. This cohort included 28 patients, 23 of whom had genetically confirmed diagnoses. The most frequently implicated genes were VWA1, TRPV4, DYNC1H1, and VRK1. While TRPV4 and DYNC1H1 are among the most commonly reported causes of non-5q SMA, VRK1 and VWA1 are rarely described; conversely, BICD2 was less represented in our series than in a previously published large cohort.4 This discrepancy is likely related to the inclusion criterion of whole-body MRI, which in clinical practice is often performed in diagnostically challenging cases where no pathogenic variant has been identified in the more frequently implicated genes.

Muscle MRI is widely used in inherited myopathies to guide genetic testing, with many gene-specific involvement patterns described.6-9,32 By contrast, the literature on MRI patterns in SMA is scarce. In our cohort, MRI revealed highly heterogeneous patterns, with severely affected muscles adjacent to spared ones, regardless of disease severity. In some patients, involvement was also observed in extra-limb musculature, such as the tongue and sternocleidomastoid muscles. These findings underscore the value of systematically studying the distribution of muscle involvement in SMA, as has been performed in hereditary myopathies.6-9,32

Prior small case series have proposed gene-associated muscle involvement patterns, but their specificity has not been systematically evaluated.17,23-28,33 We compared the concordance of our patients' MRI findings with previously reported patterns, using the Mercuri score. This analysis demonstrated highly specific and thus diagnostically useful patterns for DYNC1H1 and BICD2. Conversely, although patients with BSCL2, MORC2, or VRK1 variants showed concordant involvement, similar patterns were also observed in patients with other gene pathogenic variants, limiting their diagnostic value. The VCP-associated pattern previously described was not well reflected in the single VCP patient in our cohort. Similarly, the TRPV4 pattern showed poor concordance with the imaging findings in all 4 TRPV4 patients. This marked variability in muscle involvement among TRPV4-related cases has been previously observed, highlighting the limited diagnostic utility of lower-limb MRI alone.31 Two of the 4 TRPV4-related cases showed severe sternocleidomastoid involvement, a feature not seen in other cohort members regardless of motor disability. This pattern, not previously reported because earlier MRI studies focused solely on the lower limbs, may serve as a supportive diagnostic clue for SMA due to TRPV4 pathogenic variants, although it is not consistently observed. Assessing both the sensitivity and specificity of MRI patterns is important for their use as diagnostic tools in clinical practice. A larger number of patients would be required to confirm these findings, although assembling larger cohorts remains challenging given the rarity of these disorders.

In addition to the Mercuri score, more studies in the neuromuscular field include quantitative MRI to assess muscle-fat replacement quantitatively as FF.34-37 In our cohort, we compared the results of the quantitative analysis with a Mercuri-based qualitative scoring system. The 2 methods showed a high degree of concordance, suggesting that automated FF assessment provides reliable measurements of muscle involvement. This approach may help reduce the need for time-consuming manual segmentation, offering a more efficient alternative for the application of these muscle involvement patterns in clinical practice. This approach may, in the future, improve efficiency and reproducibility for radiologists while maintaining diagnostic accuracy in identifying gene-specific patterns. In addition, the availability of quantitative data provides a valuable opportunity for longitudinal patient follow-up, especially in CMT subtypes for which muscle MRI has demonstrated excellent sensitivity to disease progression.38,39 Incorporating such measurements may therefore strengthen future natural history studies and improve monitoring in both clinical and trial settings.

Finally, fat-suppressed T2-weighted hyperintensities were occasionally observed, without guiding toward a specific gene involvement. The presence of hyperintensities has previously been reported as frequent across different genes in a large HMN cohort, and most likely reflects active denervation.40

When examining genes present in multiple patients within our cohort (VRK1, DYNC1H1, VWA1, and TRPV4), recurrent involvement patterns were identified. Notably, selective sparing of the sartorius and gracilis muscles at the thigh level was seen in 7 patients, which included all 5 individuals with VWA1 pathogenic variants. This selective sparing, classically reported in numerous hereditary myopathies (dystrophinopathies, laminopathies, calpainopathies, dysferlinopathy, sarcoglycanopathies, Fukutin-related protein diseases, and anoctaminopathy) is notable in SMA.7,8,11,15 In our cohort, VWA1 was the only gene associated with a neuromyopathy involving both primary myopathic and neuropathic mechanisms.28 It is therefore possible that the preservation of the sartorius and gracilis muscles in SMA indicates a pathophysiologic mechanism involving direct muscle effects and may provide information useful for interpreting VUS.

Additional recurrent patterns were also identified, including selective sparing of the short head of the biceps femoris with long head involvement, orienting toward DYNC1H1 or BICD2 pathogenic variants, and selective sparing of the extensor digitorum longus despite tibialis anterior and fibularis involvement, suggestive of DYNC1H1, BICD2, or BSCL2. Among additional guiding features, involvement of the levator scapulae suggested a HEXB pathogenic variant, whereas sternocleidomastoid involvement suggested a TRPV4 pathogenic variant.

The selective pattern of muscle involvement (i.e., increased muscle-fat replacement) associated with DYNC1H1 and BICD2 pathogenic variants provides a valuable reference for identifying additional genes implicated in related pathways. Recognizing these characteristic imaging patterns can guide clinicians to consider variants in DYNC1H1 or BICD2 that may be missed by targeted sequencing panels or to explore other genes within the same molecular network. This is supported by the functional relationship between the 2 genes: BICD2 encodes an adaptor protein that interacts with the dynein heavy chain, the product of DYNC1H1. The dynein complex is a multiprotein motor apparatus essential for intracellular cargo transport.41 Thus, investigating candidate genes encoding components of this complex—such as dynamitin (DCTN2), dynactin (DCTN1), dynein light chain (DYNLL1), or dynein intermediate chain (DYNC1I1)—as well as other dynein adaptors, may help elucidate the genetic basis of overlapping clinical phenotypes. This approach is particularly relevant for patients in whom genome sequencing fails to identify variants in known disease-associated genes. Consequently, muscle imaging patterns in HMN may serve as a phenotypic signature pointing to novel genes that share common pathophysiologic mechanisms with DYNC1H1 and BICD2.

Our study has several limitations. First, previously published cohorts describing gene-specific muscle MRI patterns have generally involved very small groups of patients, which inherently limits the robustness and reproducibility of the proposed patterns. This issue is further compounded by the fact that most of the earlier studies relied predominantly on imaging restricted to the lower limbs, thereby reducing the number of muscles available for pattern analysis and potentially overlooking disease-relevant involvement in dorsal or upper-limb muscles. Similarly, although our cohort included patients from different families and carrying distinct pathogenic variants, the rarity of these disorders means that our sample size remains small, and the patterns we describe should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, these neuropathies are slowly progressive diseases, and our participants were not all examined at the same age or disease stage. This heterogeneity may affect the apparent severity and distribution of muscle involvement. Although we focused our analysis on adult patients to limit this bias, 2 participants had MRIs performed during childhood, and 1 at age 64. Age and disease stage represent important limitations for pattern detection, highlighting the value of performing MRIs in patients with moderately progressed muscle involvement.

In conclusion, whole-body muscle MRI appears to be a useful tool to guide genetic diagnosis in non-5q SMA. By providing results comparable with manual scoring while removing the burden of visual assessment, qMRI-based FF mapping paves the way for the practical application of muscle involvement patterns in routine clinical care of HMN. Gene-specific patterns were identified, particularly for VRK1, DYNC1H1, and VWA1, and should prompt careful evaluation of these genes and aid geneticists in the interpretation of VUS. Selective sparing of the gracilis and sartorius muscles may indicate involvement of genes at the nerve-muscle interface, which are also linked to hereditary myopathies. Sternocleidomastoid involvement may serve as a supportive MRI clue for diagnosing TRPV4-related SMA, highlighting the importance of performing whole-body imaging. These findings should be interpreted with caution because sample sizes for each genetic subtype were small. Confirmation will require larger cohorts, although this remains limited by the rarity of these disorders.
