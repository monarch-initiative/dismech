# Application of microsurgical surgery in patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis unsuited for endovascular treatment: a single-center retrospective study.

To investigate the clinical efficacy and safety of microsurgical surgery in 
patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis unsuitable for endovascular 
treatment. A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 34 
patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis who underwent microsurgical 
surgery at the Department of Cerebrovascular Surgery, Shengli Oilfield Central 
Hospital, Dongying, Shandong, from March 2020 to April 2023. Preoperative 
imaging confirmation of proximal vertebral artery stenosis or occlusion was 
obtained using cervical CT angiography (CTA), CT perfusion imaging (CTP), and 
magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). Postoperative imaging examinations were 
utilized to evaluate blood flow patency, and clinical symptoms and complications 
during hospitalization and follow-up were documented. Postoperative imaging 
examinations in the 34 patients (Males: 79.4%; Mean age: 66.7 ± 9.6 years) 
revealed patent vertebral and supplying arteries. No new instances of transient 
ischemic attacks (TIAs) or other cerebrovascular events were observed during 
hospitalization, and clinical symptoms were improved. The mean follow-up 
duration was 10 months (range: 6-39 months). One patient died from septic shock 
due to abdominal infection, and one patient exhibited moderate ipsilateral 
vertebral artery stenosis on a follow-up CTA at 6 months postoperatively. The 
Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores decreased for 30 patients after surgery 
compared to preoperative scores, with all postoperative mRS scores being less 
than 1. The difference between preoperative and postoperative mRS scores was 
statistically significant (P < 0.01). Six patients experienced temporary 
postoperative complications, which resolved after active treatment, and one 
patient developed permanent Horner's syndrome without affecting the quality of 
life. Microsurgical surgery for patients with proximal vertebral artery 
stenosis, when endovascular treatment is unsuitable, demonstrates good clinical 
efficacy and a low incidence of complications, offering a viable surgical 
treatment option. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes will be 
instrumental in validating its clinical application value.

Ischemic stroke is a prevalent neurological disorder characterized by high rates of disability and mortality, with posterior circulation ischemia accounting for approximately 20-30% of ischemic strokes. Proximal vertebral artery stenosis is the most common site of narrowing leading to posterior circulation ischemia [1, 2]. Patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis may not exhibit significant neurological symptoms; however, it is noteworthy that the risk of posterior circulation ischemia significantly increases within 5 years in patients with symptomatic vertebral artery stenosis [2, 3].

Patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis require intensive medical therapy, including antiplatelet and lipid-lowering treatments [2]. Previous studies have indicated that in cases where medical therapy is ineffective, the risk of posterior circulation ischemia significantly increases in patients with vertebral artery stenosis [4]. Guidelines suggest that for patients with recurrent symptoms of vertebrobasilar artery and 50–99% extracranial vertebral artery stenosis, revascularization treatment may be considered in addition to intensive medical therapy [2].

In recent years, endovascular treatment, characterized by its simplicity, speed, and performance under local anesthesia, has become the primary modality for treating proximal vertebral artery stenosis. However, not all patients can successfully undergo endovascular treatment. In cases of severe vertebral artery stenosis or near occlusion, the luminal diameter of the vertebral artery is narrow, making it difficult to identify the origin of the vertebral artery during endovascular treatment, and the microguidewire has difficulty passing through the occluded segment. Forced implementation may result in plaque shedding and vascular injury [5]. Moreover, when vertebral artery underdevelopment is present, the artery is more prone to tortuosity or elongation of the arterial wall. When the tortuous angle of the proximal vertebral artery is too large, the difficulty of endovascular stent implantation significantly increases. The 2021 AHA guidelines state that the role of vertebral artery stents in symptomatic patients has not been established [6]. Additionally, the research by Markus and others, as well as the 2023 clinical practice guidelines for atherosclerotic carotid and vertebral artery disease from the European Society for Vascular Surgery, recommend against routine stent implantation for patients with transient ischemic attacks or strokes and vertebral artery stenosis of 50–99% [2, 7].

For patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis, particularly those who are unsuitable for endovascular treatment, microsurgical revascularization becomes a treatment of choice. The surgery encompasses two main methods: (1) Simple vertebral endarterectomy (VEA) or vertebral endarterectomy accompanied by partial endarterectomy of the subclavian artery. (2) Vascular bypass grafting, such as vertebral artery to common carotid artery, vertebral artery to subclavian artery, and vertebral artery to thyroid stem transposition [8–10]. In this study, we analyzed and summarized the microsurgical revascularization performed at our center for patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis who were unsuitable for endovascular treatment. The feasibility, safety, and clinical efficacy of this technique were assessed by analyzing the comparison of preoperative and postoperative imaging data, changes in clinical symptoms of patients after surgery, the incidence of postoperative complications, and the recovery status.

We retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 34 patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis unsuitable for endovascular treatment who underwent microsurgical surgery in the Department of Head and Neck Vascular Surgery at Shengli Oilfield Central Hospital in Dongying City, Shandong Province, from March 2020 to April 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Severe stenosis of the unilateral proximal vertebral artery, moderate stenosis of the unilateral proximal vertebral artery with contralateral vertebral artery hypoplasia or occlusion, moderate stenosis of the unilateral proximal vertebral artery accompanied by a history of recurrent posterior circulation ischemia; (2) Patients who had previously undergone endovascular intervention and failed or were unable to undergo endovascular intervention (including but not limited to allergies to contrast agents or inability to tolerate contrast agents due to renal failure); (3)Restenosis after stent placement in the proximal vertebral artery (verified by more than 50% reduction in vascular lumen diameter post-stent); (4) Patients who, due to other diseases (such as long-term recurrent peptic ulcers) or the need for other open surgeries, could not accept long-term dual antiplatelet aggregation medication after endovascular treatment; (5) Patients who refused endovascular treatment of the proximal vertebral artery.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with poor cardiac or pulmonary function who could not tolerate general anesthesia surgery; (2) Patients with poor development of the vascular wall muscle fibers or with large artery inflammation; (3) Patients who refused surgical treatment.

All patients underwent preoperative imaging to confirm the presence of proximal vertebral artery stenosis or occlusion, utilizing either head and neck CT angiography (CTA) combined with CT perfusion imaging (CTP), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), or digital subtraction angiography (DSA). In this study, the term “proximal vertebral artery” refers to the segment of the vertebral artery that originates from the subclavian artery and extends to the transverse foramen of the C6 vertebra. The degree of stenosis was classified based on the confirmed reduction in vascular lumen diameter: a 50–70% reduction was considered moderate stenosis, and a reduction of 70% or greater was classified as severe stenosis.

Clinical data were collected from patients, including gender, age, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia), clinical manifestations at admission, smoking history (> 20 cigarettes per day), characteristics of imaging data, and methods of microsurgical revascularization. The modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score was used to assess the condition of patients at admission, detailed in Table 2.

Postoperatively, the healing of the surgical incision, the condition of the surgical drainage, and the assessment of vocal cord function, swallowing function, and other postoperative complications were observed. Complications that improved with treatment within six months after surgery are referred to as transient complications, while those that did not improve are termed permanent complications. A CTA examination was performed within one week after surgery to assess the patency of the blood vessels. Before discharge, attention was paid to whether the patient had any new transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) or other cerebrovascular accidents (CVAs), and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score was reassessed by the operating surgeon. The modified Rankin Scale (mRS) is an efficacy assessment indicator for the level of functional disability, which can be followed up by telephone. A score of 0 indicates no symptoms at all, a score of 1 indicates symptoms but no significant disability, and a score of 2 or more is based on the severity of disability affecting normal life.

Patients were followed up clinically and radiologically for at least six months. Initially, monthly telephone follow-ups were conducted to understand the recovery of postoperative complications and the presence of new cerebrovascular-related symptoms, with the operating surgeon scoring the patient on the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Patients were advised to return to our outpatient clinic for clinical examination and radiological tests at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months post-discharge. Radiological assessments primarily focused on the origin of the vertebral artery and the anastomotic site of the bypass surgery, as well as the blood flow in the vessels, to determine if there was any restenosis.

The current microsurgical revascularization procedures for the proximal vertebral artery primarily consist of endarterectomy for a single vessel and bypass grafting involving multiple vessels. There are two main approaches for exposure of the proximal vertebral artery: the supraclavicular transverse incision approach and the anterior cervical longitudinal incision approach. The supraclavicular transverse incision approach provides excellent exposure of the origin of the vertebral artery but is relatively limited and requires transection of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. In contrast, the anterior cervical longitudinal incision approach does not require muscle transection, and the incision can be rapidly extended to involve more distal segments of the vertebral artery.

A longitudinal incision is made along the anterior edge of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, extending from the posterior region of the mandible to the head of the clavicle. The platysma and superficial fascia are incised down to the anterior belly of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and the external jugular vein is ligated and divided along the lateral edge of the muscle. The carotid sheath is then dissected inferiorly, and by retracting the muscle laterally and the superior belly of the omohyoid muscle, an adequate operative field is obtained. The carotid sheath and its contents are carefully mobilized along the lateral edge of the internal jugular vein, with attention to protecting the vagus nerve and the sympathetic chain posterior and lateral to the carotid sheath. The pretracheal fat pad is retracted laterally along its medial edge, and to prevent intraoperative bleeding, the superficial vascular structures within the fat pad are meticulously dissected and ligated. After retracting the fat pad, the anterior scalene muscle is exposed, and the phrenic nerve on the anterior surface of the muscle is carefully protected. The vertebral artery is located at the center of the angle formed by the longus colli muscle and the anterior scalene muscle. The vertebral artery is mobilized to its origin from the subclavian artery, fully exposed, and the thyrocervical trunk crossing anterior to the vertebral artery is retracted and protected, as shown in Fig. 1.

During the dissection to locate the vertebral artery, the thyrocervical trunk, which arises from the subclavian artery, is first encountered, with its proximal dissection leading towards the subclavian artery. By following the right subclavian artery from proximal to distal in an antegrade manner, the vertebral artery can be distinguished from the thyrocervical trunk by the lack of branches in the proximal vertebral artery. In up to 90% of cases [11], the vertebral artery enters the transverse foramen at the C6 level, and the C6 transverse process can also be palpated to guide the localization of the vessel.

The endarterectomy procedure for the left vertebral artery is essentially the same as for the right side. However, for patients undergoing left vertebral artery endarterectomy, careful exploration of the fat pad deep within the internal jugular vein at the angle where it meets the subclavian vein is necessary to identify and ligate the thoracic duct to prevent postoperative lymphatic leakage. Additionally, the left recurrent laryngeal nerve is longer than the right side and should be carefully considered during dissection. If the stenosis involves the part of the subclavian artery at the origin of the vertebral artery or if the patient has concomitant subclavian steal syndrome, the plaque of the subclavian artery can be excised concurrently with the vertebral artery endarterectomy.

After temporarily occluding the distal vertebral artery intraoperatively, the artery is ligated and obliquely transected at its origin from the subclavian artery, creating a beveled end. The proximal and distal right common carotid artery is occluded, and a punch is used on the posterior wall of the common carotid artery to create a smooth, flush elliptical anastomotic opening based on the condition of the transected vertebral artery end. The posterior wall of the common carotid artery is then rotated outward and continuously sutured to the transected end of the vertebral artery using vascular sutures.

The anastomotic opening should be comprehensively considered based on preoperative imaging data and the intraoperative position of the vertebral artery end to ensure a suitable, smooth, and flush elliptical shape, which can reduce the incidence of postoperative thrombosis and anastomotic stenosis. Patients undergoing bypass grafting often have some degree of stenosis and plaque in the carotid artery. For those with preoperative confirmation of stenosis ≥ 70%, or with stenosis < 70% but with a history of ipsilateral stroke or transient cerebral ischemia, we typically perform carotid endarterectomy first and then schedule the bypass grafting at a later date. If the endarterectomy site on the carotid artery is high, care should be taken to protect the hypoglossal nerve, which crosses from the external to the internal carotid artery and the external carotid artery. For other patients, the bypass grafting treatment is selected at a location on the common carotid artery with a better endarterectomy outcome.

Preoperatively, patients are administered oral aspirin (100 mg) and clopidogrel (75 mg) for at least 3 days, once daily, with careful observation of gum bleeding and urine color. Antiplatelet medications are avoided within 24 h postoperatively to prevent oozing. After 24 h postoperatively, aspirin (100 mg) and atorvastatin (20 mg) are prescribed for long-term oral administration, once daily. For patients with a history of peptic ulcer disease, ulcer healing is confirmed preoperatively before proceeding with surgical treatment, and aspirin is administered in combination with a proton pump inhibitor orally.

Within one week postoperatively, head and neck CTA imaging in the 34 patients indicated patency of the vertebral artery and feeding arteries, with no stenosis at the anastomotic sites. No new episodes of TIA or cerebrovascular accidents occurred during the hospital stay, and there were no wound infections or other systemic diseases. Thirty patients had a postoperative mRS score of less than 1, and 4 patients had a postoperative mRS score of 2.

Follow-up results: The average follow-up time was 10 months (range: 6–39 months). None of the 34 patients had recurrent TIA or other cerebrovascular accidents. One patient died from septic shock due to abdominal infection; one patient who had stent removal for restenosis of the left vertebral artery showed moderate stenosis of the left vertebral artery on head and neck CTA at the 6-month follow-up visit. As of the last follow-up, the postoperative restenosis rate was 2.9%, with 30 patients having an mRS score of 0. Four patients had residual sequelae from cerebral infarction before hospitalization, with preoperative and postoperative mRS scores of 2, but during the follow-up period, these 4 patients did not experience any further TIA or other CVA.

Vertebral artery stenosis is a condition characterized by the compression and narrowing of the vertebral artery lumen, which is often asymptomatic [2, 12]; symptomatic vertebral artery stenosis may manifest as dizziness (47%), unilateral limb weakness (41%), dysarthria (31%), headache (28%), and nausea or vomiting (27%) [13, 14]. The V1 segment of the vertebral artery at its origin is more prone to stenosis compared to other segments [15–17].

Evidence suggests that simultaneous surgery on the vertebral and carotid arteries may lead to increased mortality and stroke rates within 30 days postoperatively (8-33%), and the occurrence of transient or permanent Horner’s syndrome (2-21%), thus concurrent carotid and vertebral artery surgery is not recommended for patients with combined carotid and vertebral artery disease [2]. This study posits that for patients requiring the removal of carotid plaques simultaneously, the basic condition of the patient should be combined with an assessment of the surgical timing and tolerance. Preoperative imaging examinations should be completed to accurately measure the degree and length of carotid stenosis, and the anastomotic site should be preselected. After a thorough evaluation of the surgical risk by anesthesiologists and other relevant department physicians, the surgery should commence. During the surgical process, it is recommended to fully dissect and expose the vertebral artery first, perform the carotid endarterectomy, and after the intraoperative plaque removal and confirmation of unobstructed carotid blood flow, proceed with the bypass surgery based on the preselected anastomotic site.

Horner’s syndrome is a clinical syndrome resulting from the interruption of any part of the ocular sympathetic efferent pathway, characterized by ptosis, miosis, and anhidrosis on the ipsilateral side of the face. Surgical injury to the cervical sympathetic trunk may involve thermal or anatomical damage. Thermal injury is often due to the heat generated by energy devices such as electric or ultrasonic scalpels, leading to injury of the cervical sympathetic trunk. Mild thermal injuries are generally reversible due to the integrity of the nerve. Anatomical damage usually involves the transection of the preganglionic fibers of the cervical sympathetic nerve. The local nerve ganglion may be palpated similarly to lymph nodes, and inexperienced operators may mistakenly excise the nerve ganglion along with lymph nodes. Therefore, when palpating enlarged lymph nodes posterior to the carotid artery, it is important to distinguish them from nerve ganglia, especially when the lymph nodes are adherent to surrounding tissues.

In the event of a postoperative surgical site hematoma, attention should be paid to whether the patient’s trachea has shifted, and whether there are symptoms such as difficulty swallowing or respiratory distress, as asphyxiation is the greatest risk associated with wound hematoma. If the patient presents with symptoms of asphyxiation or stridor, consider the possibility of rupture at the site of vascular anastomosis. Perform tracheal intubation, promptly open the incision, remove the blood clot, and achieve hemostasis. If the patient has no symptoms other than the surgical site hematoma, consider the possibility of inadequate hemostasis during surgery; if the swelling continues to increase, it may also be necessary to reopen the incision for hemostasis. Layer-by-layer dissection during surgery and timely separation and ligation of blood vessels are crucial to prevent postoperative bleeding, especially the superficial vascular structures within the fat pad, which should not be overlooked.

The incidence of cranial nerve injury following carotid endarterectomy is as high as 8-10% [18], but there are no study reports on its occurrence in the treatment of vertebral artery stenosis. Intraoperative injury to the vagus or recurrent laryngeal nerve may lead to unilateral vocal cord paralysis. Damage to the unilateral hypoglossal nerve may cause difficulties in speaking, chewing, and swallowing, while bilateral damage can lead to upper airway obstruction. During surgery, traction on the nerves should be minimized, and once symptoms of nerve injury appear, timely administration of neurotrophic drug therapy should be initiated. In most cases, symptoms will gradually alleviate if the nerves were not damaged during surgery. However, it should be noted that unilateral hypoglossal nerve paralysis is a contraindication for contralateral endarterectomy, and the subsequent treatment should be deferred until recovery on that side is achieved.

Hoarseness, if present postoperatively, should prompt consideration of whether the surgical field involved the superior laryngeal nerve or the recurrent laryngeal nerve. However, the most common cause is laryngeal edema, which typically resolves as the swelling subsides, leading to the disappearance of the patient’s symptoms.

Chylous leaks are more prevalent in patients undergoing surgery on the left vertebral artery. During surgery, careful management of the thoracic duct is essential. A significant leakage of chyle can lead to electrolyte imbalances, hypoproteinemia, infection, and even bleeding. It may also result in chylothorax or chyloperitoneum, causing respiratory distress and thoracoabdominal infections, with severe cases potentially life-threatening. Patients with milder symptoms are treated with a high-protein, low-fat diet and local compression bandaging. If the patient has a large volume of chyle drainage or shows no significant improvement after three days of compression therapy, surgical exploration may be considered.

In this study, 14 patients with proximal vertebral artery stenosis had previously undergone failed interventions due to vascular reasons, and 4 patients experienced in-stent restenosis following stent therapy. The vertebral artery has more developed elastin and smooth muscle compared to the coronary artery, which can make stent placement more challenging. Additionally, the origin of the vertebral artery is more flexible, increasing the inflammatory response to stent placement and potentially leading to in-stent restenosis [19]. A meta-analysis of non-randomized studies reported no difference in technical success and procedural complications between drug-eluting stents (DES) and bare-metal stents (BMS), but patients treated with BMS had a higher incidence of recurrent symptoms and postoperative reintervention compared to those treated with DES [20]. A multicenter study involving 420 patients who underwent endovascular stent placement reported an average in-stent restenosis rate of 26% at 12 months [21]. In a single-center series, the stent fracture rate was 5%, 15%, and 30% at one, three, and five years, respectively, with most being asymptomatic [21, 22]. Compared to endovascular treatment, microsurgical treatment has a relatively lower rate of restenosis.

In these 34 cases, 94% of the patients received active antiplatelet therapy and/or anticoagulant therapy. Some patients had previously undergone endovascular stent treatment, but their clinical symptoms did not show significant improvement. For all patients who underwent microsurgical treatment, we confirmed that their symptoms were alleviated or improved postoperatively, and successful revascularization was achieved. The modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores before and after surgery showed statistical significance with a paired t-test P < 0.01, as seen in Fig. 4. Moreover, compared with other studies as shown in Table 5, vertebral artery endarterectomy alone is associated with relatively fewer complications than surgical treatment involving more than two vessels. Compared to arterial transposition surgery, when the supplying vessel has severe stenosis or unstable atherosclerotic plaques, performing vertebral artery endarterectomy alone does not involve dealing with other vessels, nor does it require a second surgery, resulting in less trauma and preserving the original physiological and anatomical structure of the vertebral artery [15]. Under permissible conditions, vertebral artery endarterectomy alone may be a better choice in the microsurgical treatment of proximal vertebral artery stenosis.

Overall, the prognosis and follow-up results suggest that microsurgical revascularization surgery is highly feasible, with good safety and clinical outcomes in the treatment of proximal vertebral artery stenosis.

For proximal vertebral artery stenosis, microsurgical revascularization is feasible with a low incidence of long-term complications and restenosis, especially for those not suitable for endovascular treatment. Our results support the use of microsurgical techniques for revascularization. Proficiency in the anatomy of the surgical site, mature vascular anastomosis techniques, and comprehensive clinical assessments during the perioperative period are key to the successful treatment of proximal vertebral artery stenosis with microsurgery. This study represents only a preliminary exploration from a single center with a small sample size and inherent limitations. Further validation is needed through large-scale, multicenter studies.
