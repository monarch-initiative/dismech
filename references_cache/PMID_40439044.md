# Elevated Plasma Neurofilament Light Chain Levels in Children with Infantile-Onset Ascending Hereditary Spastic Paralysis.
**Authors:** Zubair U, Battaglia N, Alecu JE, Tam A, Rong J, Quiroz V, Yang K, Kim HM, Warren K, Mannix R, Della Vecchia S, Santorelli FM, Ebrahimi-Fakhari D
**Journal:** Mov Disord Clin Pract (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mdc3.70157](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.70157)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Infantile-onset ascending hereditary spastic paralysis (IAHSP), caused by bi-allelic variants in ALS2, is an ultra-rare, neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive ascending spasticity and weakness with bulbar dysfunction.

OBJECTIVES: To investigate baseline plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels in children with IAHSP patients compared to age-matched controls.

METHODS: Five patients (age range: 6-11 years) with genetically confirmed IAHSP and 74 healthy controls were assessed. Plasma NfL was measured using a single-molecule array. Statistical analyses included non-parametric tests, age-matched comparisons, and effect size estimation.

RESULTS: Children with IAHSP had modestly but significantly elevated plasma NfL levels compared to controls (median: 17.5 vs. 4.78 pg/mL, p = 0.004). High NfL levels persisted across varying levels of clinical severity.

CONCLUSIONS: Plasma NfL levels are elevated in IAHSP, suggesting axonal degeneration. These pilot findings highlight NfL as a potential marker for disease activity, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts and longitudinal studies.

Keywords:ALS2 gene, axonal degeneration, biomarker, infantile‐onset ascending hereditary spastic paralysis, neurofilament light chain

Infantile‐onset ascending hereditary spastic paralysis (IAHSP, OMIM # 607225) is an ultra‐rare neurodegenerative disorder reported in fewer than 100 individuals worldwide.1,2,3Characterized by progressive ascending spasticity of the lower and upper extremities and bulbar dysfunction with dysarthria/anarthria and dysphagia, IAHSP leads to significant morbidity and early mortality.2,4IAHSP is caused by biallelic mutations in theALS2gene, which encodes alsin, a guanine‐nucleotide exchange factor critical for Rab5‐dependent endosomal trafficking and neuronal survival.5,6Truncating variants often disrupt the VPS9 domain and lead to severe, early‐onset presentations, whereas missense or in‐frame deletions generally correlate with milder phenotypes.7,8Variants inALS2are also implicated in juvenile‐onset amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (JALS, OMIM # 205100) and juvenile primary lateral sclerosis (JPLS, OMIM # 606353).9,10There are currently no validated biomarkers or disease‐modifying therapies forALS2‐related disorders.

Neurofilament light chain (NfL), a neuronal cytoskeletal protein, is a well‐established biomarker of axonal injury, released into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood following neuronal damage. Elevated NfL levels are documented in diverse neurological disorders, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis,11spinal muscular atrophy,12and some hereditary spastic paraplegias.13,14Given the pathophysiological overlap between IAHSP and these conditions, it is plausible that NfL may also be elevated in IAHSP. This study aims to assess plasma NfL levels in children with IAHSP compared to age‐matched healthy controls, providing preliminary data on the utility of NfL as a biomarker for this ultra‐rare disease.

Through the Registry and Natural History Study for Early‐Onset Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia (NCT04712812), we included five children with genetically confirmedALS2‐related IAHSP. The control group comprised 74 age‐matched healthy children without neurological disorders, recruited from the Boston Children's Hospital PrecisionLink Biobank. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Boston Children's Hospital (IRB P00033016), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Venous blood samples were collected in PST tubes (BD Biosciences, Cat #367962), and plasma was separated and stored at −80°C. Plasma NfL levels were quantified using the single‐molecule array (Simoa) HD‐X analyzer (Quanterix, Billerica, MA) with NF‐Light Advantage kits. Each sample was analyzed in duplicate. Quality control measures included evaluating intra‐assay variability, with median intra‐assay coefficients of variation (CV) of 3.13% (IQR: 2.88%) for patient samples and 6.61% (IQR: 9.35%) for controls.

Plasma NfL data were visualized using violin and box plots to illustrate distributions and individual data points. Due to the right‐skewed distribution, data are presented on a log scale for visualization while statistical comparisons used non‐parametric methods on the original scale. An age‐matched subgroup analysis was performed, restricting controls to those aged 6.3–13.3 years, matching the IAHSP patient age range. Non‐parametric Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests compared NfL levels between groups. Cliff's Delta quantified the magnitude of differences, with bootstrapping (10,000 replications) providing 95% confidence intervals. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1).

The IAHSP cohort included five patients (1 male, 4 females) with a median age of 9.2 years (interquartile range (IQR): 5.9 years). The control group comprised 74 healthy individuals (45 males, 29 females) with a mean age of 5.3 years (IQR: 6.3 years). Among the five patients, comprehensive functional assessments revealed a clear severity gradient correlating with variant type. Those with truncating variants disrupting the VPS9 domain demonstrated the most severe phenotypes exemplified by patient 1 (c.4878_4881dup / c.359_361del, Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) level 5) and patient 3 (homozygous c.3520A>T, GMFCS level 4). In contrast, patients with missense variants or in‐frame deletions showed milder manifestations. Patients 4 and 5, both carrying missense variants, had a relatively milder spasticity (GMFCS level 2). These clinical observations support earlier reports of genotype–phenotype correlations inALS2‐related conditions.7,8,10

Overall, IAHSP patients demonstrated significantly elevated plasma NfL levels compared to controls (median: 17.5 pg/mL [IQR: 11.1–20.6 pg/mL] vs. 4.78 pg/mL [IQR: 3.29–7.17 pg/mL]; Wilcoxon rank‐sum test,p= 0. 004, Fig.1A). The effect size, measured by Cliff's Delta (0. 776, 95% CI: 0.459 to 1.000), indicated a meaningful group difference. An age‐matched subgroup analysis, including 13 control individuals within the 6.3–13.3‐year range, confirmed elevated NfL levels in IAHSP patients. The age‐matched control group exhibited a median NfL level of 4.95 pg/mL (IQR: 3.4–12.2 pg/mL), further supporting the significant difference (p= 0.019) (Fig.1B).

Recognizing the small sample size, a post‐hoc simulation‐based power analysis was conducted, indicating that future studies would require at least eight participants per group to achieve 80% power. However, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and the potential for small samples to overestimate true effects.

This study provides the first evidence of modestly but significantly elevated plasma NfL levels in IAHSP patients compared to controls, aligning with the known pathophysiology ofALS2‐related disorders characterized by upper motor neuron degeneration and axonal dysfunction.3,8,15

Notably, elevated NfL levels were observed across different levels of clinical severity, from patients with a relatively milder (patients 4 and 5) to those with more severe presentations (patients 1 and 3). These findings underscore the potential of NfL as a biomarker for disease activity and progression along the spectrum of IAHSP.

These findings must be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. Although robust methodologies for biomarker analysis were applied, the small sample size limits the study's generalizability and statistical power, increasing the risk of Type II errors. While the confidence intervals for Cliff's Delta are relatively wide—particularly in the age‐matched analysis, reflecting statistical uncertainty with limited samples—the consistently positive lower bounds reinforce the robustness of the observed group differences. Bootstrap‐based confidence intervals, while suggestive of a strong effect, also warrant cautious interpretation. The ultra‐rare nature of IAHSP imposes constraints on cohort size, underscoring the need for multicenter collaborations and the establishment of patient registries to address this challenge. Additionally, the imbalance in group sizes between IAHSP patients and controls, despite statistical adjustments, may introduce bias. Finally, uncontrolled confounding factors, such as sex, comorbidities, medication use, and genetic heterogeneity, limit the interpretability of these results.

From a broader perspective, this pilot study contributes to the growing recognition of NfL as a biomarker for neurodegeneration across diverse neurological conditions.16This highlights the importance of biomarker research in ultra‐rare diseases, which can advance understanding of disease mechanisms and support the development of targeted therapies.

In conclusion, these findings provide preliminary support for NfL as a biomarker in IAHSP. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to validate its clinical utility, explore its prognostic potential, and assess its role in monitoring therapeutic interventions.
