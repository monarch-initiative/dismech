# Low Dose Tocilizumab for Mitigation of Cytokine Release Syndrome With T-Cell Engaging Bispecific Antibodies.
**Authors:** Hamadeh IS, Merz M, Derkach A, Modelevsky L, Lesokhin AM, Mailankody S, Hultcrantz ML, Tan CR, Hassoun H, Shah UA, Maclachlan K, Rajeeve S, Hashmi H, Maura F, Firestone RS, Wang AX, Amin A, Patel D, Scordo M, Shah GL, Landau HJ, Giralt S, Usmani SZ, Korde N
**Journal:** Clin Lymphoma Myeloma Leuk (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.clml.2026.03.016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clml.2026.03.016)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is one of the most common adverse effects of T-cell engaging bispecific antibodies (T-BsAbs) during the step-up dosing (SUD) phase of treatment. Previous studies demonstrated that a single prophylactic tocilizumab (Toci) dose of 8 mg/kg mitigated the incidence CRS events. Herein, we evaluated the efficacy of a low Toci dose (Toci-lo) of 4 mg/kg for CRS prophylaxis during the SUD phase.

METHODS: The study included relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma patients, treated with T-BsAbs and received prophylactic Toci-lo (cohort 1) administered prior to the first SUD only. A historical patient cohort that did not receive Toci prophylaxis was identified (cohort 2) for comparison. The CRS rates were compared between the 2 cohorts using the Fisher's exact/Chi-square test. Univariate/multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to estimate the odds of CRS events with Toci-lo prophylaxis.

RESULTS: A total of ninety patients were included in the final analysis (56 in cohort 1 and 34 in cohort 2). CRS rate was significantly lower in cohort 1 (21%, 95% CI:10.7%-37.8%) compared to cohort 2 (55%, 95% CI: 38.0%-70.8%; P = .009). Grade 2 CRS rates were 2% and 21% in cohorts 1 and 2, respectively (P = .0041). Based on multivariate logistic regression, Toci-low was associated with a significant reduction in CRS rates (OR: 0.2, 95% CI: 0.06-0.65; adjusted P = .01).

CONCLUSION: Based on our findings, prophylactic Toci-lo proved to be effective in reducing CRS events during the SUD phase of treatment with T-BsAbs. The implementation of this prophylactic strategy in clinical practice might minimize healthcare expenditures.

T-cell engaging bispecific antibodies (T-BsAbs, such as teclistamab, elranatamab, talquetamab and linvoseltamab) have become the cornerstone of therapy for heavily pretreated relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) ( 1– 5 ). The favorable patient outcomes reported in the respective clinical trials for each of these T-BsAbs have provided the impetus to further investigate these agents in combination with different classes of therapeutics for early disease relapses and in the frontline settings for newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) patients ( 6– 8 ). Hence, it is reasonable to infer that the number of patients who will be exposed to or treated with T-BsAbs during the course of their disease is projected to increase.

T-BsAbs have a unique adverse effect profile which varies depending on the phase of treatment. This could be attributed to their unique mechanism of action which hinges on T-cell activation and redirection ( 9 ). Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) are the major adverse effects anticipated to occur during the step-up dosing (SUD) phase of treatment. Compared to other T-cell redirecting therapies (T-CRT) such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cells, CRS events occurring with T-BsAbs tend to be mild in severity ( 10– 13 ). Given their high prevalence with rates ranging from 46–80%, CRS events remain the primary barrier or challenge that limits patients’ access to these agents to academic or large cancer centers where the infrastructure ensures their safe administration.

Several approaches have been explored to mitigate the incidence of CRS and thereby facilitate the operationalization of SUD in the outpatient settings. Because of the central role that IL-6 plays in the pathogenesis of CRS, one of the most extensively investigated preventative modalities is the upfront administration of a single dose of tocilizumab (Toci, a monoclonal antibody that blocks IL-6 receptors) prior to the first SUD of a T-BsAb ( 14 ). This approach resulted in a significant decline in the occurrence of CRS events during the SUD phase of treatment from 57–80% to 8–29% ( 15– 19 ). Such compelling results prompted the National Cancer Comprehensive Network (NCCN) to recommend Toci prophylaxis in their recent guidelines for treating RRMM ( 20 ).

For CRS prevention and treatment, the recommended dose of Toci used in clinical practice is 8 mg/kg. Nonetheless, the FDA-approved drug label for Toci bears other indications where this agent can be used at lower doses (4 mg/kg) such as in rheumatoid arthritis ( 21 ). Furthermore, there is some evidence in the literature indicating that a flat Toci dose of 400 mg was not only as efficacious as the standard dose in treating cytokine storm during COVID-19 infections but also associated with lower rates of secondary viral and fungal infections ( 22 , 23 ).

Because of the paucity of data in the MM setting, the primary objective of this study was to assess the efficacy of a low Toci dose (Toci-lo, 4 mg/kg) in preventing the incidence of CRS among RRMM patients treated with T-BsAbs. Of note, the average cost of the standard Toci 8 mg/kg dose is $4,462; if Toci-lo proved to be effective, the adoption of this strategy for CRS prophylaxis might be cost-saving by lowering drug expenses by about 50% as well as help avoid or curb Toci shortages.

This is a single center retrospective-prospective study seeking to evaluate the efficacy of Toci-lo in preventing CRS.

Adult patients with RRMM who were started on any of the commercial T-BsAbs by their treating physicians due to disease progression were deemed eligible to be included in this study if they completed the step-up dosing (SUD, cohort 1) phase for each respective agent. The study was approved by the institutional review board and performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Belmont report.

Per institutional standards of clinical practice, patients were hospitalized for the initiation of the SUD phase of treatment. A single prophylactic dose of Toci of 4 mg/kg (based on actual body weight) was administered intravenously about an hour prior to the first SUD only of any of the commercial T-BsAbs. Pre-medications (acetaminophen 650 mg, dexamethasone 16 mg and diphenhydramine 25 mg) were also given about 30 minutes to 1 hour before each of the SUDs and first full dose. Afterwards, all these pre-medications were discontinued when treatment was transitioned to outpatient settings.

During hospitalization for initiating the SUD phase, antimicrobial prophylaxis against varicella zoster virus (VZV), Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) and hepatitis B virus (HBV, if deemed necessary) was either initiated or resumed per institutional guidelines. All patients were closely monitored for any signs and symptoms of CRS as well as ICANS/neurotoxicity; in the event patients developed CRS or ICANS, the criteria set forth by the American Society of Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) were used for grading. Management of these events was in accordance with institutional guidelines for CRS and/or ICANS ( 25 ).

For statistical analysis, a historic cohort of patients (cohort 2) who received commercial T-BsAbs during the period of November 2022 until 12/2024 (prior to the implementation of Toci prophylaxis) without Toci prophylaxis were identified using the institutional plasma cell disorders database. This cohort served as the comparator arm to evaluate the efficacy of Toci-lo (4 mg/kg) for CRS prevention.

Baseline characteristics were described as either median (range) or frequency. The Chi-square/Fisher exact test (with 1 degree of freedom) was used to compare differences in the rates of CRS between the two cohorts. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to estimate the odds of CRS with Toci-lo prophylaxis (after adjusting for number of prior lines of therapy, prior exposure to TCRT, ALC, high risk cytogenetics). Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to compare median time to CRS following each SUD among patients who developed this complication in cohort 1. For the cost-saving analysis, an average adult weight of 70 kg was used to compare the difference in price between the 4 mg/kg and 8 mg/kg Toci doses when used for CRS prevention. IBM SPSS (version 26) was used for statistical analyses.

A total of 90 patients were included in the final analysis (cohort 1: 56 patients and cohort 2: 34) as illustrated in Figure 1. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the two cohorts except for the number of prior lines of therapy and the presence of extramedullary disease (EMD, Table 1); 25% (n=14) and 32% (n=11) of the patients had prior exposure to T-CRT (CAR T-cells and/or T-BsAbs) in cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. The time interval between prior T-CRT and current T-BsAb initiation was not significantly different between cohort 1 (median: 12.7 months, range: 0.5–31.4 months) and cohort 2 (median: 6.9 months, range: 0.7–65.0 months, p=0.231).

As shown in Figure 1, the majority of the patients (in either cohort) received teclistamab for treating RRMM (61% in cohort 1 and 59% in cohort 2).

The overall CRS rate in cohort 1 was 23.2% (n=16), and it was significantly lower than that in cohort 2 (59%, n=20; p=0.001) as shown in Figure 2. There was also a significant difference in the incidence of grade 2 CRS events between cohorts 1 (2%, n=1) and 2 (21%, n=7, p=0.004). Following patient stratification based on individual T-BsAb, the decline in CRS events was significant among the subgroup of patients who received teclistamab in cohort 1 (10.7%, n=6) compared to the corresponding subgroup (teclistamab subgroup) in cohort 2 (35%, n=12, p=0.003, Figure 3a). Most of the CRS events occurred after the administration of SUDs 1 (38%) and 2 (38%) in cohort 1 whereas in cohort 2, 60% of all CRS events occurred after SUD1 (Figure 3b).

In univariate analysis, prophylaxis with Toci-lo was associated with a significant reduction in CRS rates in cohort 1 compared to cohort 2 with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.21 (95% CI: 0.08–0.52; p=0.005, Table 2). The same trend was also observed in the subgroup of patients who were treated with teclistamab in cohorts 1 and 2 (OR: 0.13, 95% CI: 0.03–0.43; p=0.005).

After adjusting for other covariates (Table 2), the protective effect of Toci-lo against CRS events with T-BsAbs remained significant with an OR of 0.21 (95% CI: 0.07–0.60, p<0.0001). None of the other covariates included in the multivariate logistic regression model predicted the incidence of CRS events.

In the subgroup of patients in cohort 1 who developed CRS, the median time to the onset of this event following the administration of any SUD was 27 hours (range: 7.5 −48 hours). Breakdown of the median time of CRS onset by SUD showed that it was significantly shorter after the first SUD (median: 17 hours, range: 7.5–23.0 hours; p=0.002) compared to the other SUDs (Figure 4). Comparing the median time to CRS onset between the two study cohorts (cohorts 1 and 2) was not feasible since data regarding CRS onset was not available for cohort 2.

Recurrent CRS was noted in 1 patient in cohort 1 compared to 6 patients in cohort 2; all patients received Toci at 8 mg/kg for management. The incidence of neurotoxicity/ICANS events was low in both cohorts with a rate of 4% in cohort 1 (n=2) and 6% cohort 2 (n=2). One patient in either cohort developed grade 2 neurotoxicity/ICANS which resolved within 48 hours following management per institutional guidelines.

Despite the widespread use of T-BsAbs for RRMM, CRS events which are mostly confined to the SUD phase of treatment, are considered the main obstacle impeding the expanded or broadened patient access to these agents particularly in community settings.

Findings from this single center study suggested that a single prophylactic Toci dose of 4 mg/kg administered before the first SUD led to a marked reduction in the incidence as well as severity of CRS. Patients who received teclistamab benefited the most from Toci-lo prophylaxis. For talquetamab, the CRS rate in cohort 1 was numerically lower compared to cohort 2; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance presumably because of the smaller number of patients who were treated with this T-BsAb. This also might explain the significantly low CRS rates in the subgroup of patients who received elranatamab (about 3%) particularly in cohort 2 (the historic cohort that did not receive Toci-lo prophylaxis) given the fact that the incidence rate of this event in the pivotal MagnetisMM-3 clinical trial was 56.3% ( 2 ). Regardless, our results underscore the clinical relevance of this strategy with Toci-lo as it pertains to improving patients’ tolerability of these agents. Additionally, multivariate logistic regression analysis identified Toci-lo prophylaxis as the only independent protective factor against CRS during the SUD phase of treatment with BsAbs typified by approximately an 80% reduction in the overall incidence of these events (OR=0.21, 95% CI: 0.07–0.60). None of the other factors that were added to the logistic regression model (multiple lines of therapy, exposure to T-CRT, ALC and presence of EMD) had a significant role in the incidence of CRS. Of note, the percentage of malignant plasma cells was not accounted for because bone marrow biopsy results were not available for most of the patients at the time of disease progression. In the clinical trials that led to the approval of T-BsAbs, the median time to CRS onset was about 2 days (range: 1–29 days) ( 1 , 3 ). The nature of our study design prohibited the direct comparison of the time to CRS onset between the two patient cohorts (cohorts 1 and 2); however, our findings were consistent with previously published evidence; thereby suggesting the use of prophylactic Toci-lo did not delay CRS onset.

To our knowledge, this is the first study set out to evaluate the efficacy of Toci-lo for the prevention of CRS in patients treated with T-BsAbs (during SUD phase) for RRMM. Most importantly, our results were in line with those from prior studies which helped establish the efficacy of the standard Toci dose of 8 mg/kg for CRS prevention. Van de Donk reported on the subgroup of patients enrolled in the MajesTEC-1 clinical trial (n=24) who received a single intravenous dose of Toci 8/kg within four hours of treatment initiation ( 17 ). With this preventative approach, only 29% of the patients developed CRS compared to 73% as noted in the original MajesTEC-1 clinical trial. Hence, the institution of Toci prophylaxis as a CRS mitigation strategy translated into a relative risk reduction of 65%. In the studies by Kowalski et al and Torpe et al, CRS events occurred at a lower rate than what we noted herein where only10.1% (95% CI: 5.3–17.0) and 8% of the patients, respectively developed this complication with prophylactic Toci at the 8 mg/kg dose prior to the first SUD of the T-BsAbs. ( 15 , 19 ). This discrepancy in the reported CRS rates could be ascribed to several underlying factors such as the heterogeneity in the patient population included as well as the T-BsAbs used to treat RRMM. To illustrate, most of the patients (98%) in the study by Torpe et al were exposed to T-cell redirecting therapies (T-CRTs) prior to teclistamab compared to only 14% in our study. Hamadeh et al showed that patients who received teclistamab following T-CRTs experienced significantly lower rates of CRS compared to those who were T-CRT naïve ( 24 ). This might provide a plausible explanation for the lower CRS rates observed in the study by Torpe et al since the median time interval from previous T-CRT exposure among patients in cohort 1 in our study was about a year (12.7 months), which might be sufficient to restore T-cell function. It is also worth mentioning that the incidence and severity of CRS events tend to vary with each of the approved T-BsAbs where teclistamab and talquetamab were associated with the highest rates. ( 1 , 3 ) Sixty-one percent of the patients in our study received teclistamab which was approximately double the percentage of patients in the study by Kowalski et al. Furthermore, only 10% of our patients were treated with elranatamab whereas about one third of the patients in the study by Kowalski et al received this agent. None of our patients were treated with linvoseltamab because it was not on the institution’s formulary when the study was underway. This lack of uniformity in the use of each of the approved T-BsAbs might have contributed to the difference in the CRS rates observed in the study by Kowalski et al and ours.

Given the short duration or follow up period of the study (which was limited to the SUD phase of treatment), the impact of Toci-lo on response to T-BsAbs as well as the incidence of infections could not be ascertained. This was one of the main limitations of our study. Nonetheless, previous studies demonstrated that the Toci 8 mg/kg dose neither compromised patients’ response to T-BsAbs nor portended an increased risk of infections ( 15 , 16 , 18 ). These safety signals coupled with the efficacy of Toci prompted the NCCN to consider incorporating it into the pre-medication regimen.

Although Toci (8 mg/kg) is not considered a universal standard of care for CRS prophylaxis with T-BsAbs, many institutions are currently adopting this practice. The increase or surge in Toci demand would inevitably lead to drug shortages since Toci is widely utilized for other approved indications (including rheumatoid arthritis) and endorsed by several consensus guidelines as the drug of choice for treating CRS with not only T-BsAbs but also CAR T-cells therapy ( 25 ). Hence, our findings have two important ramifications; the utilization of Toci-lo (4 mg/kg) instead of the standard dose of 8 mg/kg for CRS prophylaxis in clinical practice will help curtail Toci stockouts by allowing more patients to be treated with the same available drug stock. In addition, prophylactic Toci-lo might be a cost-saving strategy that confers at least a two-fold decrease in drug cost from about $4,462 to $2,231. This would potentially translate into a reduction in healthcare expenditures as summarized in Table 3.

In conclusion, our results indicated that a single prophylactic dose of Toci 4 mg/kg proved to be an effective approach for reducing CRS incidence during the SUD phase of treatment with T-BsAbs. The implementation of the Toci-lo protocol in practice could not only improve patients’ tolerability of T-BsAbs but also promote cost-saving.
