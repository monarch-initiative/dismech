# Case Report: Compound Heterozygous SCNN1B Mutations Causing Pseudohypoaldosteronism Type 1B2 in Neonatal Twins.
**Authors:** Wang Z, Long L, Bi H
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.70173](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.70173)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Pseudohypoaldosteronism type I (PHA1) is a rare genetic disorder characterized by renal or systemic resistance to aldosterone, resulting in hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis. The autosomal recessive systemic form (PHA1B), caused by mutations in SCNN1A, SCNN1B, or SCNN1G, is particularly severe and typically manifests in the neonatal period. Among these, SCNN1B-related cases occur less frequently than SCNN1A-related cases.

CASE PRESENTATION: We report a pair of dizygotic preterm twins (male and female) born at 35 + 5 weeks of gestation who presented at 8 days of age with poor feeding, weight loss, and severe jaundice. Both developed life-threatening hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and metabolic acidosis, and these conditions were unresponsive to initial management including hydrocortisone. Laboratory findings showed markedly elevated plasma renin and aldosterone levels, with no evidence of congenital adrenal hyperplasia or other metabolic disorders. Whole-exome sequencing revealed compound heterozygous mutations in the SCNN1B gene: a splice-site variant (c.585+2T>C) inherited from the mother and a missense variant (c.1544T>C, p.Ile515Thr) from the father. Both mutations are novel and were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Diagnosis of systemic PHA1B2 was established. Treatment with oral sodium chloride, sodium citrate, and potassium-binding resins led to gradual correction of electrolyte imbalance and clinical stabilization.

CONCLUSION: These are the first reported Chinese cases of neonatal-onset PHA1B due to novel compound heterozygous SCNN1B mutations. This report broadens the mutational spectrum of SCNN1B and underscores the importance of early genetic testing in refractory neonatal electrolyte disturbances.

Pseudohypoaldosteronism type I (PHA1) is a rare genetic disorder characterized by renal or systemic resistance to the action of aldosterone, which results in impaired sodium reabsorption, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and metabolic acidosis in affected individuals (Amin et al. 2013; Hanukoglu and Hanukoglu 2016). The condition is broadly categorized into two subtypes: the autosomal dominant renal‐limited form (PHA1A), which is caused by mutations in the NR3C2 gene encoding the mineralocorticoid receptor (Geller et al. 1998); and the more severe autosomal recessive systemic form (PHA1B), which results from loss‐of‐function mutations in the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) subunit genes—SCNN1A, SCNN1B, or SCNN1G. The first reports describing ENaC gene mutations were published by Chang et al. (1996) and Strautnieks et al. (1996).

PHA1B affects multiple organ systems including the kidneys, lungs, sweat glands, and colon, and often presents in the neonatal period with life‐threatening electrolyte imbalances (Alzahrani et al. 2021).

Among systemic PHA1 (PHA1B) cases, mutations in SCNN1A are most frequently reported (Blobner et al. 2022). Therefore, the identification of novel compound heterozygous SCNN1B variants contributes to the expanding variant spectrum associated with this gene. Variants in SCNN1B leading to systemic PHA1 occur less frequently than SCNN1A‐related mutations, whereas SCNN1G mutations remain truly rare (Huneif et al. 2022; Nobel et al. 2016). Early diagnosis of this condition remains challenging due to clinical overlap with other neonatal endocrine or metabolic disorders, such as congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), especially in the context of nonspecific symptoms like feeding difficulty, poor weight gain, and persistent jaundice (Rosli et al. 2021).

Herein, we report a pair of dizygotic neonatal twins diagnosed with systemic PHA1B due to compound heterozygous mutations in the SCNN1B gene, both of which are previously unreported. We detail the clinical presentation, biochemical abnormalities, genetic findings, and treatment course for these two cases. In addition, we provide a comprehensive literature review to contextualize these cases within the broader spectrum of SCNN1B‐associated PHA1B. Although all three ENaC subunit genes (SCNN1A, SCNN1B, SCNN1G) can lead to systemic PHA1B, limited data suggest possible phenotypic variability. Although individual case reports have described variable pulmonary involvement among ENaC subunit mutations, a recent comprehensive review indicates that no distinct genotype–phenotype correlation has been established between specific ENaC variants and respiratory manifestations (Chantzaras et al. 2025).

An 8‐day‐old male infant was admitted on January 9, 2024, with “jaundice for 8 days and poor feeding with weight loss for 3 days.” He was the first of twins, delivered via cesarean section at 35 + 5 weeks gestation, weighing 3000 g, with Apgar scores of 10 at 1 and 5 min. Shortly after birth, he was hospitalized for “neonatal swallowing syndrome and hyperbilirubinemia,” for which he received phototherapy and parenteral nutrition. He was discharged after 5 days on 30 mL feeds every 3 h, weighing 2630 g. However, worsened jaundice and poor feeding required readmission.

The patient had no history of fever, seizures, cyanosis, or respiratory/gastrointestinal symptoms. He had received vitamin K1 as well as hepatitis B and Bacille Calmette‐Guérin vaccines. The results of newborn metabolic and hearing screenings were normal. Maternal gestational diabetes was well controlled. His father was healthy, with no relevant family history. His twin sister is described in Case 2.

On examination, he was conscious but weakly responsive, weighing 2350 g, with a temperature of 36.7°C, heart rate of 120 beats/min, respiratory rate of 42 breaths/min, and blood pressure of 63/35 mmHg. He had a weak cry, marked jaundice, dry inelastic skin, and no dysmorphic features. The anterior fontanel was soft and flat. Heart sounds were normal; lungs had coarse breath sounds without rales. The abdomen was soft with normal bowel sounds. Genitalia appeared normal; reflexes were present.

Laboratory tests revealed: hyperkalemia (K+ 9.3 mmol/L; reference: 3.5–5.5 mmol/L), hyponatremia (Na+ 119.5 mmol/L; reference: 135–145 mmol/L), total bilirubin of 356 μmol/L (reference: < 205 μmol/L), alanine transaminase (ALT) of 8 U/L (reference: < 50 U/L), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) of 12.5 mmol/L (reference: 1.8–6.5 mmol/L), creatinine of 73.5 μmol/L (reference: 20–60 μmol/L), and creatine kinase MB (CKMB) of 42 U/L (reference: 0–25 U/L). Arterial blood gas showed metabolic acidosis with pH 7.32 (reference: 7.35–7.45), base excess −16.2 mmol/L (reference: −2 to +2 mmol/L), HCO3
− 15.3 mmol/L (reference: 22–26 mmol/L), and PCO2 19.2 mmHg (reference: 35–45 mmHg). The results of routine blood, urine, and stool tests were unremarkable. The results of metabolic screenings (tandem mass spectrometry [MS], urine gas chromatography [GC]‐MS, serum ammonia) were normal. Hormonal tests—including cortisol, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), 17α‐hydroxyprogesterone, testosterone, androstenedione, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA‐S), progesterone, angiotensin II, free thyroxine (FT4), and thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH)—were normal. However, plasma levels of renin (795 pg/mL) and aldosterone (> 4000 pg/mL) were markedly elevated. The patients' karyotype was 46,XY.

Ultrasound imaging of the kidneys, adrenals, and genitalia produced normal findings. Electrocardiography (ECG) revealed atrial tachycardia, intraventricular conduction delay, QT prolongation, and first‐degree atrioventricular (AV) block, which were considered secondary to severe electrolyte imbalances.

Initial hydrocortisone treatment for suspected CAH was ineffective. Based on the normal cortisol/ACTH levels and elevated aldosterone, along with hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and acidosis in both twins, whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was performed. It revealed compound heterozygous mutations in SCNN1B, confirming pseudohypoaldosteronism type IB2 (PHAIB2). Hydrocortisone was discontinued.

Treatment included oral 10% NaCl initiated at 10 mL every 3 h and subsequently adjusted, with the dose later reduced to 2.5 mL every 3 h as maintenance, together with sodium citrate in feeds, breastfeeding, potassium‐binding resin, and insulin. The patient's electrolyte levels normalized by day 8. He was discharged on day 26 on sodium citrate and sodium polystyrene sulfonate. A clinical timeline is shown in Table 1.

A female neonate, born via cesarean section at 35 + 5 weeks gestation with a birth weight of 2500 g, was admitted at 8 days old for persistent jaundice and 3 days of poor feeding and weight loss. She had no history of perinatal asphyxia, fever, seizures, or respiratory/gastrointestinal symptoms. The family noted decreased intake and activity.

On examination, she was conscious but weakly responsive, with a temperature of 36.7°C, heart rate of 122 beats/min, respiratory rate of 43 breaths/min, blood pressure of 65/35 mmHg, and weight of 2100 g. She had generalized jaundice, dry poorly elastic skin, and no dysmorphic features. Cardiopulmonary, abdominal, and neurologic exam findings were unremarkable except for mild hypotonia. Primitive reflexes were present.

Laboratory tests revealed severe hyperkalemia (K+ 10.0 mmol/L), hyponatremia (Na+ 122.6 mmol/L), metabolic acidosis (BE −11.2, HCO3
− 16.9 mmol/L, pH 7.28), and elevated total bilirubin (342 μmol/L). ALT, BUN, creatinine, and CKMB levels were mildly abnormal. Tandem MS, urine GC–MS, and serum ammonia results were normal. Endocrine testing was largely normal, except for markedly elevated levels of plasma renin (4771.7 pg/mL) and aldosterone (> 4000 pg/mL). The patient's karyotype was 46,XX. ECG showed sinus tachycardia and complete right bundle branch block. In both cases, the ECG abnormalities resolved following correction of hyperkalemia and hyponatremia, supporting their transient and reversible nature associated with PHA1B.

Sweat chloride testing was not performed, as the diagnosis was confirmed genetically and sweat testing is not routinely reliable in preterm or clinically unstable neonates.

With parental consent, WES was performed on the proband and his twin sister. Peripheral blood was collected, and genomic DNA was extracted. Exonic regions were enriched using the IDT Human Exome Enrichment Kit and sequenced on the BGI platform. Bioinformatic analysis and pathogenicity prediction were performed using TGex, dbNSFP, SIFT, and PolyPhen‐2. Variant interpretation was based on clinical phenotype, ClinVar, dbSNP, and relevant literature. Familial segregation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 1).

Karyotyping (G‐banding) and WES were performed concurrently during hospitalization using peripheral blood samples. Both patients were confirmed to have normal karyotypes (46,XY for Case 1 and 46,XX for Case 2) before WES results became available. WES results were returned between hospitalization days 25–31 for Case 1 and days 26–32 for Case 2. Both twins were found to carry the same compound heterozygous mutations in the SCNN1B gene (NM_000336.3): a splice site variant c.585+2T>C inherited from the mother and a missense mutation c.1544T>C (p.Ile515Thr) inherited from the father. In this report, we describe dizygotic preterm twins with systemic PHA1B caused by novel compound heterozygous variants in SCNN1B. Their early‐onset hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, markedly elevated aldosterone and renin levels, and poor response to hydrocortisone were consistent with ENaC dysfunction. Genetic confirmation of the SCNN1B variants enabled timely diagnosis and appropriate sodium supplementation, which led to stabilization of electrolyte levels and clinical improvement.


SCNN1B mutations account for an important subset of systemic PHA1B cases, and published reports have expanded the clinical spectrum associated with ENaC dysfunction. However, recent comprehensive reviews emphasize that no definitive genotype–phenotype correlations have been established for respiratory, cardiac, or gastrointestinal manifestations among ENaC subunit variants. In our study, both siblings were identified as compound heterozygotes for SCNN1B variants, one maternally inherited splice‐site variant and one paternally inherited missense variant, highlighting the importance of molecular testing in confirming systemic PHA1B. These findings reinforce the value of early genetic diagnosis in guiding sodium supplementation, anticipating disease severity, and improving clinical outcomes.

Importantly, both parents were clinically healthy, with normal blood pressure, electrolyte profiles, and no history of hypertension or features of Liddle syndrome, which have been associated with autosomal dominant SCNN1B variants. This absence of parental phenotype, together with the segregation analysis, strongly supports a recessive mode of inheritance in our cases.

The SCNN1B gene is located on chromosome 16p12.2 (Figure 2) and spans 13 exons over 79,030 bp. Figure 3 presents the transcriptomic (RNA‐level) expression pattern of SCNN1B across human tissues; however, RNA abundance does not necessarily reflect protein expression. High‐resolution immunohistochemical studies have demonstrated that ENaC subunits are predominantly localized in the kidney, airway epithelium, salivary glands, and distal colon (Enuka et al. 2012; Hanukoglu et al. 2017). Mutations in SCNN1B lead to inactivation of sodium channels and systemic impairment of sodium reabsorption in multiple organs. This results in distal nephron dysfunction with impaired hydrogen and potassium excretion and excessive sodium loss through sweat, saliva, and urine, thereby contributing to the systemic phenotype of PHA1B.

Two recently reported cases expanded the known mutation spectrum of SCNN1B. One Turkish case described a homozygous frameshift variant c.1234dup (p.Glu412Glyfs*39), leading to death at 10 months (Singin et al. 2024). Another Chinese case reported a compound heterozygous mutation comprising a novel splice‐site variant c.1152+2T>G and a known frameshift variant c.1245_1246insC (p.Asn416fs*35) (Yang et al. 2024). In contrast, our patients carried two previously unreported variants: c.585+2T>C (splice‐site, maternal) and c.1544T>C (missense, paternal). These findings broaden the SCNN1B variant landscape and support early genetic testing for accurate diagnosis.

A literature search of Chinese databases (CNKI, Wanfang, VIP) was conducted from database inception through June 11, 2025, using the keywords “SCNN1B” or “pseudohypoaldosteronism,” and yielded 13 reports of neonatal PHA. Of these, only one case involved a mutation in SCNN1B. That patient developed poor responsiveness, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, metabolic acidosis, and dehydration within the first week of life. The initial treatment with volume expansion, bicarbonate, and hydrocortisone led to transient improvement, but the condition soon relapsed. PHA was diagnosed based on clinical and genetic findings, and the patient responded to oral sodium polystyrene sulfonate and sodium citrate. During follow‐up, the child experienced recurrent eczema, shock, metabolic acidosis, electrolyte imbalance, and pulmonary infections. Genetic testing identified compound heterozygous mutations in SCNN1B: c.118C>T and c.776+1G>A (Yang et al. 2019).

A search of the PubMed database using the terms “Pseudohypoaldosteronism” and “SCNN1B” and a query of the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database (#620125) yielded 15 articles describing 20 patients with SCNN1B‐related systemic PHA1. These included 5 cases from Israel (Edelheit et al. 2005, 2010; Hanukoglu et al. 2008), 5 from Turkey (Cayir et al. 2019; Dogan et al. 2012; Karacan Küçükali et al. 2021; Seyhanli et al. 2020; Singin et al. 2024), 3 from the United Kingdom (Gopal‐Kothandapani et al. 2019; Saxena et al. 2002), 2 from Spain (Pugh 2022), and one each from Saudi Arabia (Alzahrani et al. 2021), the United States (Nobel et al. 2016), India (Joshi et al. 2022), China (Liu et al. 2022), and Sudan (Abdalla et al. 2021). All cases presented during the neonatal period, with a median onset age of 7.5 days (Gopal‐Kothandapani et al. 2019). Clinical manifestations included vomiting, feeding difficulties, weight loss, and recurrent disturbances in acid–base and electrolyte balances (i.e., hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis). The oldest genetically confirmed case was 32 years old. Two patients died from severe electrolyte disturbances at 6 months (Cayir et al. 2019) and 6.5 years (Saxena et al. 2002), respectively. The two novel mutations (c.585+2T>C and c.1544T>C) identified in the present study have not been previously reported.

According to reports in the OMIM, SCNN1B‐related PHA1B typically presents in the neonatal period and may involve multiple systems, including the kidneys, lungs, skin, and gastrointestinal tract. In addition to electrolyte disturbances, common symptoms include recurrent respiratory infections, eczema, suppurative hidradenitis, and growth retardation. Severe cases can progress to cardiac arrest and death.

Beyond the novelty of the variants, the clinical importance of this report lies in the severe and refractory presentation in both twins, the clear biochemical phenotype, and the comprehensive genetic analysis including parental segregation. Together, these findings provide strong evidence linking the identified SCNN1B variants to systemic PHA1B and contribute to the expanding variant spectrum and phenotypic understanding of this rare disease.

Early clinical and biochemical features of PHA often resemble CAH, leading to misdiagnosis. CAH, caused by steroid synthesis defects (e.g., 17α‐hydroxylase deficiency), presents with cortisol deficiency and elevated steroid precursors. While both conditions may involve hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and dehydration, CAH typically features low aldosterone and renin levels and responds to glucocorticoids—unlike PHA.

Management of PHA focuses on sodium supplementation, potassium‐lowering agents, and correction of metabolic acidosis. In our cases, both infants received oral 10% sodium chloride, sodium citrate in milk, breastfeeding, potassium‐binding resins, and insulin. Electrolyte levels in both patients normalized by hospitalization day 8. They were discharged with continued sodium citrate and sodium polystyrene sulfonate, and follow‐up showed stable electrolyte levels without recurrent acidosis.

PHA1A is renal‐limited and often improves with age, while PHA1B is systemic, more severe, and may persist into adulthood. Both patients remain under close follow‐up. As of the most recent outpatient visit at corrected age 6 months, both infants achieved stable electrolyte levels on ongoing treatment with oral 10% NaCl (2.5 mL Q3H), sodium citrate (0.2 g Q3H), and sodium polystyrene sulfonate (0.1–0.15 g Q3H). However, their weight gain by corrected age 6 months (6.2 kg and 6.0 kg, respectively) was below the expected range for age, which may be related to the underlying disease and its impact on gastrointestinal function. Caregivers reported good adherence to the prescribed regimen.

With advances in genetic diagnostics, high‐throughput sequencing has become vital for identifying and managing rare diseases. This report emphasizes the value of early genetic testing and targeted treatment in improving outcomes in PHA.
