# Two Cases of CLIPPERS-like Syndrome Sharing a Hypomorphic UNC13D Variant.
**Authors:** Sagawa H, Hirata K, Katayama S, Shibata H, Toyofuku E, Kaneko S, Katata Y, Takezawa Y, Uematsu M, Onishi I, Yamazaki Y, Hattori T, Yamamoto M, Shimizu M, Imai K, Yasumi T, Morio T, Yokota T, Sasahara Y, Kanegane H
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-026-01988-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-026-01988-1)

## Content

Chronic lymphocytic inflammation with pontine perivascular enhancement responsive to steroids (CLIPPERS) syndrome is a central nervous system (CNS) inflammatory disease characterized by contrast-enhanced MRI findings of salt-and-pepper-like lesions predominantly affecting the brainstem and cerebellum. We report two patients with CLIPPERS-like brain MRI findings who carried the same missense UNC13D variant in one allele along with deleterious variants in the opposite allele. Patient 1, a 23-year-old female, presented at 15 years of age with neurological symptoms and an MRI showing spontaneously resolving, contrast-enhancing lesions in the cerebellum. At age 16, the patient experienced an episode with systemic manifestations followed by recurrent CNS lesions that responded to steroid therapy. At age 22, the patient developed punctate to nodular contrast-enhancing lesions in the brainstem, cerebellum, and cerebrum, findings consistent with CLIPPERS. Patient 2, an 18-year-old female, presented at age 11 with ataxia and dysarthria, and an MRI showing multiple contrast-enhancing lesions in the cerebellum and brainstem, consistent with CLIPPERS MRI findings. Cerebellar biopsy revealed perivascular CD4+ T-lymphocyte infiltration, and the patient responded to steroid therapy, leading to an initial diagnosis of CLIPPERS. These patients were suspected of having inborn errors of immunity and were identified to have compound heterozygous UNC13D variants along with downregulated Munc13-4 protein. Both patients underwent allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation, with patient 1 remaining neurologically stable for two years post-transplantation, while patient 2 experienced a post-transplant relapse requiring steroid therapy. These cases highlight that biallelic variants in the UNC13D gene may cause CNS-predominant inflammation that mimics CLIPPERS.

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by excessive immune activation and cytokine production, resulting in multi-organ dysfunction [1]. Familial HLH (FHL), caused by genetic defects in lymphocyte cytotoxicity, typically presents in early childhood; however, an increasing number of late-onset cases have been reported in recent years [2]. Chronic lymphocytic inflammation with pontine perivascular enhancement responsive to steroids (CLIPPERS) is an inflammatory disorder of the central nervous system (CNS), first described by Pittock et al. in 2010 [3]. It is characterized by punctate and curvilinear gadolinium enhancement in the brainstem and cerebellum, particularly the pons, on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and by a clinical response to corticosteroids [3]. Although its pathogenesis remains poorly understood, recent reports have identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in FHL-associated genes in children presenting with isolated CNS inflammation mimicking CLIPPERS [4–6] as well as in adults with CLIPPERS-like presentations [7, 8]. This raises the possibility that a subset of CLIPPERS cases may represent a CNS-predominant form of FHL.

While the overlap between late-onset FHL and CLIPPERS is increasingly recognized, the clinical course and treatment outcomes for these cases, especially the role of allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT), remain underexplored. Given that HCT is the only curative treatment for FHL [9], understanding its effectiveness in this specific patient population is crucial to guiding clinical decisions and improving prognosis.

Here, we report two patients, one adolescent and one adult, carrying biallelic UNC13D variants who presented with CLIPPERS-like MRI findings. By characterizing this distinct clinical subset and describing their post-HCT outcomes, we aim to broaden the understanding of the clinical and genetic spectrum of late-onset FHL and its potential overlap with CLIPPERS. Given the risk of progressive neurological deterioration and the curative potential of HCT, our findings suggest the importance of early recognition and timely treatment of patients with CLIPPERS-like presentations harbouring biallelic variants in FHL-associated genes.

Genetic analysis was performed using a next-generation sequencing panel targeting approximately 400 genes associated with known inborn errors of immunity (IEIs), including FHL-related genes (PRF1, UNC13D, STX11, STXBP2, FAAP24, SLC7A7, LYST, RAB27A, AP3B1, AP3D1, SH2D1A, and XIAP). Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples, and the library preparation and sequencing were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Genetic analyses were performed at the Kazusa DNA Research Institute (https://www.kazusa.or.jp/en/).

Degranulation capacity was evaluated by measuring surface CD107a expression on natural killer (NK) cells as described [10]. For NK cell degranulation, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were cultured with or without K562 target cells for 2 h and were then stained with monoclonal antibodies against CD3, CD8, CD16, CD56 (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA), and CD107a (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). The cells were analyzed by flow cytometry, and the percentage of CD107a+ cells within the CD3−CD56+ NK cell populations was determined. Munc13-4 expression in patient-derived platelets was evaluated as previously described [11, 12]. Functional assays were performed according to standard protocols, and the results were compared with those of healthy controls.

A 23-year-old female initially presented with dysarthria and vertigo at the age of 15 years (Table 1). Brain MRI revealed cerebellar enlargement, diffuse fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities predominantly involving the cerebellum, and contrast-enhancing lesions along the cerebellar sulci, which resolved spontaneously (Fig. S1). At 16 years of age, the patient developed fever, cytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, and multiple infiltrative pulmonary lesions. Bone marrow, lung, and liver biopsies were obtained. Bone marrow biopsy revealed a normocellular marrow with small clusters of CD3+CD4+ lymphocytes. Lung biopsy revealed diffusely proliferating atypical lymphoid-like cells containing large nuclei around the blood vessels, destroying the alveolar walls. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated that the infiltrating lymphocytes were positive for CD3 and CD4, whereas a smaller population exhibited CD8 positivity (Fig. S2). Liver biopsy revealed proliferation of atypical lymphocytes, with a predominance of CD4+ cells over CD8+ cells. Flow cytometry and chromosomal analyses revealed no abnormalities. Although lymphoma was not evident, the patient received CHOP chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisolone) based on a tentative lymphoma diagnosis. This stabilized her condition for three years. At 19 years of age, the patient experienced diplopia, headache, and dizziness, with new contrast-enhancing lesions detected in the cerebellum and brainstem (Fig. S1). The symptoms improved spontaneously. At 21 years of age, the patient developed impaired consciousness and visual acuity, with new FLAIR hyperintense lesions in the cerebellum, brainstem, and occipital lobe (Fig. S1). Methylprednisolone (mPSL) pulse therapy (1000 mg/d intravenous drip for 3 d) improved the symptoms and lesions, and oral prednisolone (PSL) 30 mg/d was subsequently initiated. At 22 years of age and after 14 months of treatment, the patient’s PSL dose was tapered to 8 mg/d. This led to worsening dizziness, and an MRI revealed numerous punctate-to-nodular, contrast-enhancing lesions in the brainstem (primarily pons), cerebellum, and cerebrum characteristic of CLIPPERS (Fig. 1a, b). Treatment with mPSL pulse therapy improved the symptoms, and oral PSL was subsequently increased to 30 mg/d. The refractory clinical course of the patient prompted us to search for IEIs as the underlying cause. Flow cytometry analysis of peripheral blood revealed mildly decreased degranulation of NK cells (Fig. 2a) and genetic analysis identified compound heterozygous UNC13D variants (c.2588G > A, p.G863D and c.3229_3235del, p.R1077Sfs*48). After confirming downregulation of Munc13-4 protein in platelets (Fig. 2b), the patient was diagnosed with FHL type 3 (FHL3). At 23 years of age, serum CXCL9 levels were 2,366 pg/mL (Fig. S3), suggesting an ongoing cytokine storm. To prevent the recurrence of CNS lesions and systemic symptoms, allogeneic HCT from a human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-matched sibling donor was performed following an intrathecal therapy with methotrexate and PSL. An 18-year-old female first experienced ataxia, dysarthria, intention tremor, nystagmus, and diplopia at the age of 11 years (Table 1). An MRI revealed multiple T2-weighted hyperintense lesions, predominantly in the cerebellum. The patient was initially diagnosed with acute disseminated encephalomyelitis and treated with mPSL pulse therapy and intravenous immunoglobulin. Although these treatments were effective, a reduction in the PSL dose caused symptom relapses. Four months after symptom onset, the patient was transferred to our hospital. A subsequent brain MRI revealed numerous granular to nodular contrast-enhancing lesions, mainly in the cerebellum and brainstem (Fig. 1c, d). Given the radiological findings, a provisional diagnosis of CLIPPERS was made. Next, a brain biopsy of the left cerebellar hemisphere lesion was performed. Histopathological analysis confirmed a perivascular infiltration of inflammatory lymphocytes, predominantly CD4+ T lymphocytes, within the parenchyma (Fig. 3). These findings were consistent with the clinical and radiological presentation, leading to a clinically definitive diagnosis of CLIPPERS. Therefore, we treated the patient with several immunosuppressive therapies, including cyclophosphamide pulse therapy, methotrexate, azathioprine, rituximab, and tacrolimus; however, all therapies showed limited efficacy. At 14 years of age, the patient developed motor paralysis and sensory impairment in both lower limbs due to spinal cord lesions (Fig. S4). At 16 years of age, the patient developed a low-grade fever and leg edema. Laboratory blood test revealed anemia, hypoalbuminemia, and elevated C-reactive protein levels. Bone marrow aspiration revealed activated macrophages and hemophagocytosis. The brain and spinal cord MRI confirmed the absence of new lesions. Late-onset FHL was suspected, and the genetic analysis identified compound heterozygous UNC13D variants (c.2588G>A, p.G863D and c.118-307 G>A, a deep intronic variant). After confirming mildly decreased NK degranulation (Fig. 2a) and downregulated Munc13-4 protein in platelets (Fig. 2b), the patient was diagnosed with FHL3. At 17 years of age, serum CXCL9 levels were 347 pg/mL even under stable conditions (Fig. S3). Therefore, to prevent new CNS lesions from developing, allogeneic HCT from an unrelated HLA-matched donor was performed. The conditioning regimen comprised fludarabine, targeted doses of busulfan, and alemtuzumab. Although the patient was receiving immunosuppressive therapy with PSL, azathioprine, and tacrolimus, diplopia, spastic paralysis of the lower limbs, and right-sided sensory disturbances persisted during HCT. PSL and azathioprine were discontinued at the start of conditioning chemotherapy, while tacrolimus was continued as prophylaxis for graft-versus-host disease. The patient was discharged 100 d after HCT with no new CNS lesions. The white blood cell counts at 100 d post-HCT was 2800/μL, including 27% lymphocytes (756/μL), and T cells remained low at 9.3% (70/μL). Post-HCT chimerism assessed using a variant-specific droplet digital polymerase chain reaction revealed almost 100% donor chimerism in the whole blood but only approximately 70% in the peripheral blood T cells (Table S1). Tacrolimus was gradually reduced and discontinued 155 d after HCT, and the donor chimerism of the peripheral blood T cells reached 75%. A few days after discontinuing tacrolimus, the patient developed left ptosis, right upper limb paralysis, and dysarthria. MRI revealed numerous nodular contrast-enhancing lesions in the cerebellum, brainstem, cerebrum, left oculomotor nerve, and left trigeminal nerve (Fig. S4). Pleocytosis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was observed. At this time, the donor chimerism of the peripheral blood T cells was 83.6%. However, we were unable to analyze the chimerism of CSF lymphocytes. Based on the clinical and laboratory findings, this condition was determined to be a recurring FHL, and not GVHD; therefore, we administered mPSL at a dose of 2 mg/kg/d for 2 weeks. Subsequently, the patient’s symptoms and MRI findings improved, and the mPSL dose was reduced and switched to 1 mg/kg/d of PSL and gradually tapered. By day 220, the donor chimerism of the peripheral blood T cells had increased to nearly 100%. The current study identified biallelic UNC13D variants in two patients with CLIPPERS-like syndrome, which may broaden the recognized clinical and genetic spectrum of FHL3, a disorder typically presenting during infancy or early childhood [2]. Both patients carried the same missense UNC13D variant on one allele and a deleterious variant on the other. Patient 1 initially presented with systemic inflammation, but following chemotherapy-induced remission, inflammation recurred in the CNS. The predominantly CNS-restricted inflammation observed in our patients suggests that UNC13D variants can cause a late-onset FHL with primary CNS involvement, regardless of the presence or absence of overt systemic hyperinflammation.

Although the pathogenesis of CLIPPERS remains unclear, several clinical and pathological findings suggest a complex relationship with FHL, indicating that the two conditions may share both distinct and overlapping pathogenic mechanisms. First, neuropathological analyses of CLIPPERS have demonstrated perivascular and parenchymal infiltration predominantly by CD4⁺ T cells, with a smaller presence of CD8⁺ T cells [13, 14]. In contrast, FHL is typically characterized by CD8⁺ T cell–dominant immune activation [15]. While both conditions exhibit T cell infiltration, these findings indicate immunophenotypic differences. Interestingly, in our FHL case (patient 2), brain biopsy revealed predominantly CD4+ T cell infiltration, deviating from the typical FHL profile and displaying features consistent with CLIPPERS [16]. Similar CD4+-dominant profiles have been reported in other FHL cases with CLIPPERS-like MRI findings [7, 8]. This raises the possibility that FHL may manifest with heterogeneous immunopathological profiles, particularly in CNS-restricted forms. Second, the relapsing-remitting course and steroid responsiveness observed in CLIPPERS mirror those of FHL, in which inflammation flares during immune-activation triggers and abates with immunosuppression, but inevitably relapses in the absence of a definitive treatment [17, 18]. Third, the reported association of CLIPPERS with systemic lymphoma in some cases [19] resembles the increased risk of lymphoproliferative disorders seen in patients with FHL [20, 21]. Collectively, these observations suggest that a subset of CLIPPERS cases may represent a CNS-predominant form of FHL, in which genetically impaired cytotoxic function of T cells and NK cells leads to chronic immune dysregulation and neuroinflammation.

Notably, recent reports have identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in FHL-associated genes in both children and adults presenting with isolated CNS inflammation mimicking CLIPPERS. In our literature review of reported FHL cases presenting with CNS lesions mimicking CLIPPERS, we identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in UNC13D and PRF1 [4–8], including the same UNC13D variant (c.2588G > A, p.G863D) observed in our patients (Table 2). Although several cases with CLIPPERS-like presentations and underlying FHL have undergone HCT in these prior reports [4–6], evidence supporting its efficacy remains limited in this context. Our report adds to the emerging literature by describing two cases with CLIPPERS-like features who harbored biallelic UNC13D variants and underwent HCT, and by detailing their clinical course and outcomes. A previously reported patient with a homozygous PRF1 variant (c.272 C > T, p.A91V) initially presented with macrophage activation syndrome and subsequently developed CLIPPERS-like CNS lesions [8], similar to patient 1. Most genetically confirmed FHL3 cases that carried a missense variant in one or both UNC13D alleles followed atypical or late-onset clinical courses, suggesting that the majority of them are hypomorphic [22–26]. Likewise, the UNC13D missense variant p.G863D, identified in our patients, is likely hypomorphic because it has been reported only in atypical or late-onset FHL3 cases [4, 21, 22, 27, 28]. Consistent with this, Munc13-4 expression was relatively preserved in the platelets of our patients compared to early-onset cases (Fig. 2b). The partial preservation of cytotoxic function may account for the late-onset, CNS-predominant presentation observed in our cases, in contrast to the early-onset systemic FHL typically associated with complete loss-of-function variants [29]. However, even partial impairment of cytotoxicity may be sufficient for disrupting immune homeostasis and could predispose affected individuals to CNS inflammation in adulthood.

Our cases exhibited MRI findings that were not entirely typical of CLIPPERS [3]. Patient 1 initially presented with cerebellar enlargement, diffuse FLAIR hyperintensities centered in the cerebellum, and enhancing lesions along the cerebellar sulci, which differed from the typical CLIPPERS pattern. Patient 2 exhibited a gadolinium-enhancing lesion measuring greater than 3 mm in diameter. These atypical features suggest that deviations from the classic CLIPPERS-like MRI pattern may indicate underlying pathogenic FHL gene variants, as supported by previous reports. For example, Taieb et al. [7] reported that among the four CLIPPERS patients in their cohort who carried HLH-related gene mutations, three exhibited atypical findings, including systemic non-Hodgkin lymphoma in one patient and confluent gadolinium-enhancing lesions on MRI in all three. Such findings support the importance of comprehensive evaluation, including genetic testing, in patients with CLIPPERS-like syndromes, particularly when atypical radiological features are present.

Early identification of the underlying genetic etiology will enable prompt initiation of targeted therapies such as HCT, which remains the only curative treatment for FHL [9]. Both patients in our report underwent HCT, with generally favorable outcomes. However, Patient 2 experienced a relapse of neurological symptoms after HCT, likely due to immunosuppression tapering in the context of donor-dominant mixed chimerism. This case highlights the complexity of post-HCT immune dynamics in the CNS and the need for close monitoring. It also suggests that early diagnosis and timely HCT may be critical to prevent irreversible neurological damage. Nevertheless, as our observations are based on two cases, further studies will be required to better define the role of HCT in FHL with CLIPPERS-like presentation.

In addition to patients with FHL presenting with late-onset CLIPPERS-like presentation, recent studies have stressed the need to consider genetic disorders that mimic acquired neuroinflammatory conditions in adults [30]. For example, retinal vasculopathy with cerebral leukoencephalopathy and systemic manifestations, caused by pathogenic TREX1 variants, can present with multifocal white matter lesions and contrast-enhancing pseudo-tumoral lesions, which may be mistaken for tumefactive demyelination or brain tumors [31]. CTLA4 haploinsufficiency, caused by pathogenic CTLA4 variants, can present with large, recurrent inflammatory lesions affecting the brain and spinal cord [32]. Other conditions, such as Alexander disease and X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy, manifest neuroinflammatory features in adulthood [33, 34].

In conclusion, our cases further support the emerging association between CLIPPERS-like syndrome and underlying FHL. We propose that adolescent and adult patients with CLIPPERS, especially those with atypical radiological evolution or systemic complications, be considered for a comprehensive evaluation for FHL. Our findings also underscore the relevance of considering FHL in the differential diagnosis of late-onset CNS inflammation, even in the absence of systemic hyperinflammation. Early genetic diagnosis and appropriate therapeutic intervention, including HCT, may help improve patient outcomes.
