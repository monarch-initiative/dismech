# Genetic and Phenotypic Features of the Five Known Polyaminopathies: A Critical Narrative Review.
**Authors:** VanSickle EA, Sarasua SM, Lowe T, Farrell CL, Boccuto L, Schwartz C, Pegg AE, Peron A, Faundes V, Ganapathi M, Chung WK, Ziegler A, Hofstede F, Prouteau C, Steindl K, Olson C, Devinsky O, Mastracci TL, Casero RA Jr, Stewart TM, Gilmour S, Koerner T, Kutler MJ, Rajasekaran S, Michael J, Bachmann AS, Bupp CP
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmga.70029](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmga.70029)

## Content

Polyaminopathies are a recently described family of rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorders. Polyaminopathies disrupt the biosynthesis of the primary polyamines: putrescine, spermidine, and spermine. Snyder-Robinson syndrome results from hemizygous loss-of-function variants in the spermine synthase (SMS) gene, resulting in decreased or complete loss of spermine synthase enzyme activity. Bachmann-Bupp syndrome results from heterozygous gain-of-function variants in the ornithine decarboxylase 1 (ODC1) gene, resulting in increased ornithine decarboxylase enzyme activity. Faundes-Banka syndrome results from heterozygous loss-of-function variants in the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 5A (EIF5A) gene, impairing eIF5A protein function. DHPS (deoxyhypusine synthase) deficiency is an autosomal recessive disease and results from bi-allelic hypomorphic variants in the deoxyhypusine synthase (DHPS) gene, which results in reduced deoxyhypusine synthase enzyme activity. Finally, DOHH (deoxyhypusine hydroxylase) disorder is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by bi-allelic loss-of-function variants in the deoxyhypusine hydroxylase (DOHH) gene, which causes decreased deoxyhypusine hydroxylase enzyme activity. Snyder-Robinson syndrome was first described in 1969, while the other four syndromes have only been identified in the past 7 years. A comprehensive phenotypic and genotypic description of these five syndromes is needed. We review the clinical and genetic features of these five polyaminopathies to create an inclusive clinical resource. A systematic keyword search strategy was used to identify all published cases in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases. The five known syndromes associated with the polyamine pathway share many similar clinical phenotypes, and yet patients with each syndrome present with distinctive syndromic features. This review will serve as a valuable resource for clinicians diagnosing and caring for patients with these rare polyaminopathies.

Polyamines are aliphatic molecules that can be found in all living organisms. In humans, the primary polyamines are putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, which are involved in key cellular functions, including DNA replication, RNA transcription, protein translation, post-translational modification, and regulation of ion channels, thereby impacting cellular processes such as proliferation, embryogenesis, organogenesis, and signal transduction. Due to their roles in these essential cellular processes, changes in polyamine homeostasis and metabolism are thought to be involved in the manifestation and development of human diseases (Minois et al., 2011; Moinard et al., 2005; Pegg et al., 1982; Pegg et al., 2009; Pegg et al., 2016; Ramani et al., 2014; Wallace et al., 2003; Wu et al., 2024; Xuan et al., 2023).

Polyaminopathies are a family of rare genetic syndromes that are caused by variants in genes in the polyamine and related pathway, which lead to changes in the activity of enzymes that function in polyamine metabolism or hypusine biosynthesis. The first polyaminopathy to be described was an X-linked disorder called Snyder-Robinson Syndrome (SRS), which was clinically reported in 1969 (Snyder & Robinson, 1969). The underlying genetic cause of variants in the spermine synthase (SMS) gene was later described by Cason et al. (2003). In 2018, the second polyaminopathy was discovered: Bachmann-Bupp Syndrome (BABS) (Bupp et al., 2018), which is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by de novo gain of function variants in the ornithine decarboxylase 1 (ODC1) gene. Currently, BABS is the only polyaminopathy with a viable treatment option available, which is an irreversible ODC inhibitor called alpha-difluoromethylornithine (DFMO or Eflornithine) (Rajasekaran et al., 2021).

One specific and required function for the polyamine spermidine is the in the synthesis of deoxyhypusine, a modified amino acid necessary for the synthesis of the mature, active eukaryotic translation initiation factor 5A (EIF5A) (Jeelani G. and Nozaki T, 2021). So, it is not surprising that soon after the discovery of the underlying cause of BABS, additional polyaminopathies caused by variants in the enzymes necessary for eIF5A synthesis were identified. The first was Deoxyhypusine Synthase deficiency or DHPS deficiency, caused by hypomorphic variants in the deoxyhypusine synthase (DHPS) gene, which is the first enzyme in the hypusine biosynthesis pathway (Ganapathi et al., 2019). In 2021, Faundes-Banka Syndrome (FABAS), which is caused by variants in the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 5A (EIF5A) gene, was described (Faundes et al., 2021). Lastly, Deoxyhypusine Hydroxylase disorder, which is caused by variants in the deoxyhypusine hydroxylase (DOHH) gene, which is the second enzyme in the hypusine biosynthesis pathway, was described in 2022 (Ziegler et al., 2022). These three conditions are linked to polyamines because spermidine is needed for the activity of eIF5A which is a protein that plays an essential role in protein synthesis. It is needed for efficient production of proteins that contain certain sequences such as adjacent multiple proline molecules. The activity of eIF5A is dependent on a post-translational modification in which a lysine residue is converted into hypusine (N6-(4-amino-2-hydroxybutyl)lysine). This conversion requires spermidine and two enzymes, DHPS and DOHH (Park et al. 2018). DHPS transfers the aminobutyl moiety of spermidine to the lysine residue forming deoxyhypusine and DOHH then hydroxylates this residue to form hypusine.

While the phenotypes across these five syndromes display similarities, the natural histories of these syndromes are still largely unknown, which provides a challenge for clinicians caring for patients with these rare diseases. Therefore, a critical review of the literature to identify similarities and differences in the clinical phenotypes and molecular genetics across and between all reported cases of these polyaminopathies is both timely and warranted for this newly identified family of disorders. This review will help further characterize these syndromes and provide needed guidance as more patients are diagnosed. To date, just over 60 patients have been reported in the literature across all five of these syndromes. The small numbers of currently diagnosed patients suggest that these syndromes are rare in nature. However, patients continue to be diagnosed and identified with variants in the causative genes due to improvements in genetic testing. This review will serve as a resource for clinicians who see patients with rare disorders, for healthcare providers who diagnose patients with these conditions, and for translational researchers and scientists who may find variants in the associated genes or have interest in the mechanisms of the pathway. It is also hoped that this will be a helpful resource for patients, families, and caregivers of those diagnosed with a polyaminopathy.

We employed a rigorous and reproducible key word search strategy that was developed with the help of a reference librarian. These keywords were searched against three databases: PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, and Scopus. MeSH terms were not found to be useful in this search, as they have not been indexed for MEDLINE due to the recent nature of the development of this field. The search strategy utilized for this critical narrative review can be found in Table 1, which describes the concept, databases, keywords with Boolean operators, and number of articles identified. Due to the rarity and recent description of these syndromes, abstracts of novel case presentations were also included in this review.

The search strategy described in Table 1 identified a total of 330 articles with 135 duplicate articles identified. Articles were then screened by title and abstract, which excluded 123 additional reporting cases of patients with disorders other than the five described in this review. The remaining 72 articles were assessed for eligibility and 31 were excluded on the basis of not reporting patient cases: of these, 16 were animal model studies and 15 were in vitro studies. The remaining 41 articles were included in the full narrative review (Fig. 1). This review was assessed using the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) (Baethge et al., 2019).

Of the 41 papers identified for full review, 28 papers reported SRS (38 patients identified), eight papers concerned BABS (12 unique patients identified), one paper described seven patients with FABAS, two papers covered five patients with DOHH disorder, and one paper described five patients with DHPS deficiency (Supplemental Table 1). While most of the publications reviewed described clinical case reports of patients diagnosed with a polyaminopathy, some of the studies uncovered other clinical or molecular genetic insights into this family of rare disorders. While all papers were written in English, they were written and published by authors across nine different countries, with the majority of the publications coming from institutions in the United States of America.

The 38 patients with SRS that have been described in the literature were reported across 20 manuscripts or abstracts. From manuscripts published between 1969–2024, this body of literature thoroughly describes the phenotypes, natural history, and known variants of the first known polyaminopathy. In 2010, Hu et al. reported a novel (p.Y328C) variant in SMS; however, no clinical data were reported, and this variant was later described with a clinical case report (Zhang et al., 2013). Five novel variants, c.424C>T (p.L142F), c.661–13A>C, c.104T>G (p.M35R), c.335C>T (p.P112L), and c.550T>C (p.I150T), were also reported in the literature, however, no clinical data were described in association with these variants (Al-Mubarak et al., 2017; Civit et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2011). The 12 patients with BABS reported in the literature to date were described in five papers or abstracts. Four manuscripts were robust in the reporting of their clinical and molecular genetic data. The most recently described patient was from an online conference abstract, so limited clinical data was published in this format. All 17 patients described in the literature with DHPS deficiency, FABAS, and DOHH disorder were reported in the manuscripts that originally described each syndrome. However, the summaries of the clinical case reports and molecular genetics findings were thorough, with comprehensive descriptions of what is known about each syndrome to date (Table 1).

Across all patients reported with a polyaminopathy in the literature, developmental delay was present in 60/62 (96.8%) of all patients, and each syndrome demonstrated a spectrum of severity from mild to severe. Another common phenotype described across all five polyaminopathies was the presence of dysmorphic features, which were present in 58/62 (93.5%) of patients with a polyaminopathy. Hypotonia also represents one of the most frequently described clinical manifestations, with 44/62 (71%) of patients presenting with this phenotype. Finally, 28/62 (45.2%) of patients described in the literature had nonspecific brain MRI anomalies identified (Table 1).

SRS is a rare, X-linked disorder that is caused by variants in the SMS gene (Fig. 2). SRS was initially reported by Snyder and Robinson in 1969 (Snyder & Robinson, 1969), but the genetic cause of SRS was not determined until 2003 (Cason et al., 2003). While there are approximately 100 affected individuals known worldwide spanning eight countries (authors’ personal knowledge), 33 clinical cases have been reported, with 5 other novel variants reported without clinical data. One variant, c.335C>T (p.P112L), was reported in 3 separate families (Bownass et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2016; Valera Ribera et al., 2021). SRS is typically diagnosed through molecular genetic testing with the identification of a hemizygous hypomorphic SMS variant. A methylation assay called EpiSign used to identify unique epigenetic signatures can also be used to aid in the diagnosis of a variant of uncertain significance in SMS (Sadikovic et al., 2021). SRS is a result of the loss or largely reduced activity of the spermine synthase (SMS) enzyme, which then leads to lower levels of spermine and increased levels of spermidine. As patients with SRS demonstrate reduced SMS enzyme activity and an increased spermidine/ spermine ratio, a diagnosis can also be confirmed through SMS assay and polyamine analysis, which is available as a research test only (Schwartz et al., 2011; Schwartz et al., 2013).

SRS is characterized by developmental delay, intellectual disability, speech delays, seizures, asthenic build, facial dysmorphism, and bone anomalies including osteoporosis and kyphoscoliosis (Arena, 1996; Schwartz, 2013). Of the 33 clinical cases reported in the literature, the most common phenotypes identified were developmental delay, seizures, hypotonia, dysmorphic features, and bone anomalies. Other commonly reported manifestations included ophthalmological abnormalities such as myopia and nonspecific brain MRI findings, including thin corpus callosum and ventricular dilation (Albert et al., 2015; Abela et al., 2016; Arena et al., 1996; Becerra-Solano et al., 2009; Bownass et al., 2017; Cremer et al., 2020; de Alencastro et al., 2008; Dontaine et al., 2021; Kesler et al., 2009; Larcher et al., 2020; Leung et al., 2024; Marhabaie et al., 2021; Mouskou et al., 2021; Peron et al., 2013; Qazi et al., 2020; Snyder et al., 1969; Starks et al., 2018; Steiner et al., 2017; Valera Ribera et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2013) (Supplemental Table 1). Other less-commonly reported manifestations include pulmonary complications in 8 patients, gastrointestinal issues in 7 patients (Albert et al., 2015; Dontaine et al., 2021; Larcher et al., 2020; Peron et al., 2013l; Steiner et al., 2017) and renal complications in 6 patients (Albert et al., 2015; Dontaine et al., 2021; Larcher et al., 2020; Marhabaie et al., 2021).

BABS is a rare, autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder that is caused by de novo heterozygous pathogenic variants in ODC1 (Fig. 2). These gain-of-function variants lead to premature C-terminal truncations in the ODC protein, which produces cellular accumulation of enzymatically active ODC protein and its product, putrescine. One variant, c.1242–2A>G (p.IVS11–2A>G), has been reported in 3 individuals (Michael et al., 2023; VanSickle et al., 2021). Patients with BABS have been diagnosed through molecular genetic testing, but elevated polyamine pathway metabolites, primarily N-acetyl-putrescine, identified on metabolic profiling may also support a diagnosis. Additionally, increased ODC enzyme activity measured via a radiolabeled 14C-ornithine assay and increased putrescine measured by reverse phase (RP)-HPLC, may also support a diagnosis (Bachmann et al., 2024; Bupp et al., 2022; Schultz et al., 2019). While there are 18 cases of BABS known worldwide (authors’ personal knowledge), spanning eight countries, 12 clinical cases have been reported in the literature (Bupp et al., 2018; Halleb et al., 2023; Michael et al., 2023; Rodan et al., 2018; VanSickle et al., 2021).

BABS is characterized by developmental delay, hypotonia, varying degrees of non-congenital alopecia, nonspecific brain MRI abnormalities, and dysmorphic features (Bachmann, 2024; Bupp, 2022). Of the 12 clinical cases reviewed in the literature, the most commonly reported phenotypes were developmental delay, pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios, hypotonia, dysmorphic features, macrocephaly, skin differences, alopecia, and nonspecific brain MRI findings. Other commonly reported phenotypes were behavioral problems and skin abnormalities including follicular cysts (Bupp, 2018; Halleb, 2023; Michael, 2023; Rodan, 2018; VanSickle, 2021) (Supplemental Table 1).

BABS is also the first polyaminopathy with a viable treatment option available. In 2019, the first patient reported with BABS was treated with a repurposed drug, eflornithine or DFMO (Rajasekaran et al., 2021). DFMO is an inhibitor of ODC, which converts ornithine to putrescine. DFMO binds to ODC irreversibly and permanently inactivates the enzyme (LoGiudice et al., 2018; Metcalf et al., 1978). The FDA first approved eflornithine (Ornidyl ®) in 1990 for the treatment of West African Sleeping Sickness (trypanosomiasis) through intravenous administration. Then, in 2000, topical eflornithine (Vaniqua ®) was approved by the FDA to treat hirsutism. Finally, in December 2023, the FDA approved oral eflornithine (Iwilfin ®) as a maintenance therapy for pediatric and adult patients with high-risk neuroblastoma (PubChem Compound Summary for CID 3009, Eflornithine) (Duke et al., 2024). DFMO dosing for BABS was modeled after pediatric neuroblastoma dosing strategies (Saulnier Sholler et al., 2015), where patients begin at a dose of 500 mg/m2/BID for 3 months, increase to 750 mg/m2/BID for another 3 months, and then finally increase to 1000 mg/m2/BID indefinitely (Rajasekaran et al., 2021). The first patient with BABS treated with DFMO showed significant hair growth, improvement in muscle tone, hypotonia, development, and neurological symptoms, as well as resolution of recurrent follicular cysts on the patient’s back, neck, and posterior scalp (Afrin, 2023; Rajasekaran, 2021). Currently, there are 4 patients in the United States with BABS being treated with DFMO under FDA-Approved Single Patient Investigational New Drug Protocols and 1 patient in the European Union being treated on an off-label basis.

DHPS deficiency is a rare, autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder that is caused by de novo biallelic, pathogenic variants in DHPS (Fig. 2). These biallelic hypomorphic variants result in reduced, but not completely absent, DHPS enzyme activity. This in turn limits the hypusination of eIF5A in the cytoplasm and indirectly increases the acetylation of eIF5A in the nucleus (Padgett et al., 2023). Using spermidine as a cofactor, hypusine is produced by the post-translational modification of one specific lysine in eIF5A1 or eIF5A2; the hypusination of eIF5A1 or eIF5A2 activates the mRNA translation function of these proteins. Hypusine biosynthesis is a two-step process: DHPS catalyzes the first enzymatic step to produce the deoxyhypusine intermediate, which is subsequently hydroxylated by the second enzyme, DOHH thereby producing hypusine and thus the hypusinated form of eIF5A (eIF5AHYP) (Park et al., 2018). The first five identified individuals with DHPS deficiency were described by Ganapathi et al. in 2019. Patients with DHPS deficiency are also typically diagnosed through genetic testing. All reported patients with DHPS deficiency share the p.N173S variant (Ganapathi et al., 2019). Of the five clinical cases reviewed in the literature, the most frequently reported clinical phenotypes were developmental delay, prenatal findings, epilepsy, hypotonia, dysmorphic features, and skin differences. Other reported phenotypes were behavioral differences, immunological issues, and microcephaly (Ganapathi, 2019; Park, 2022).

Faundes-Banka Syndrome (FABAS) is a rare, autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder that is caused by de novo heterozygous EIF5A variants (Fig. 2). The 7 reported variants are novel, but cluster in the functional sites and domains of eIF5A. These variants have been shown to decreased interaction between eIF5A and the ribosome resulting in reduced synthesis of proteins containing polyproline. (Faundes et al., 2021). FABAS was first described by Faundes et al. in 2021, who provided clinical case reports of seven patients. Molecular genetic testing to identify variants in EIF5A can be used to diagnose patients with FABAS. The most repeated clinical phenotypes of FABAS that have been reported in the literature include developmental delay, dysmorphic features, specifically micrognathia, microcephaly, and ophthalmological differences (Faundes, 2021; Park, 2022).

DOHH disorder or deoxyhypusine hydroxylase disorder is a rare, autosomal recessive disorder that is caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in DOHH (Fig. 2). DOHH is an enzyme that catalyzes the second enzymatic step in the process of hypusine biosynthesis, thereby completing the hypusination of eIF5A. Additionally, variants in DOHH lead to reduced DOHH enzyme activity. DOHH disorder is diagnosed through genetic testing. DOHH disorder was first described by Ziegler et al. in 2022, with the clinical description of the first five patients identified with this diagnosis. One variant, p.E219Nfs*54, was reported in 3 individuals between 2 separate families (Ziegler et al., 2022). The most common phenotypes reported in these five patients with DOHH disorder were developmental delay, hypotonia, dysmorphic features, cardiac abnormalities, microcephaly, and ophthalmological differences, mainly nystagmus or strabismus. Other reported phenotypes included epilepsy and recurrent infections (Ziegler, 2022; Park, 2022). (Supplemental Table 1)

This review compiled phenotypic and genetic profiles across five polyaminopathies. This information is based on 62 patients across 41 publications or abstracts presenting clinical case reports of patients with polyaminopathies, standalone novel variants in genes associated with a polyaminopathy diagnosis, or investigation of other clinical or molecular genetic insights into this family of rare disorders. Developmental delay, dysmorphic features, hypotonia, and nonspecific brain MRI anomalies were the phenotypes most observed across all 5 polyaminopathies. While other phenotypes, such as epilepsy or bone abnormalities, were present in many patients, these were primarily seen in patients with two polyaminopathies, namely SRS and DHPS deficiency, and were not distributed as evenly across all syndromes as the four phenotypes described above. SRS is the only X-linked polyaminopathy, while BABS and FABAS are both inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, and DHPS deficiency and DOHH disorder are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner.

Along with identifying common phenotypes observed across all five polyaminopathies, distinctive features of each syndrome are also identified in this comprehensive review. The most striking phenotypes described in patients with SRS include bone anomalies, primarily osteoporosis and kyphoscoliosis, with findings reported in 31/33 (94%) of patients. Bone anomalies were not reported in patients with BABS or DHPS deficiency and were only reported in one patient each with FABAS and DOHH disorder. A recent summary of bone manifestations in 40 patients with SRS whose clinical data were reported from the Global SRS Natural History Study confirmed these findings and reported that most patients also experienced at least one fracture, which typically occurred in the lower extremities (Koerner et al., 2024). Patients with SRS also present with distinct facial dysmorphic features, including facial asymmetry, long oval face, thin upper lip and full lower lip, and palate abnormalities.

All 12 patients with BABS described in the literature presented with varying forms of non-congenital alopecia. Some patients were born with a full head of hair, which fell out soon after birth and never regrew, while other patients presented with thin or absent eyebrows and/or eyelashes. The majority of patients (9/12, 75%) also presented with macrocephaly, which would be considered a distinctive feature of BABS, as this was not reported in any patients with DHPS deficiency, FABAS, or DOHH disorder, and was only reported in 3/33 (9%) of patients with SRS.

Epilepsy was reported in all five patients with DHPS deficiency. Epilepsy has also been reported in lesser frequencies in patients with SRS (21/33, 63.6%), BABS (1/12, 8.3%), and DOHH disorder (3/5, 60%). When considering the functional relationship between DOHH and DHPS enzymes, this is an especially important similarity to note between these two syndromes. Therefore, while epilepsy is currently a defining feature of DHPS deficiency, it will be key to observe the presence of this phenotype in patients with this diagnosis in the future. Skin features such as easy bruising, sensitivity, eczema, and erythema were also reported in 4/5 (80%) of patients with DHPS deficiency. It is worth noting that DHPS deficiency is the only polyaminopathy where patients did not have abnormal brain MRI findings.

A distinctive FABAS phenotype observed in 5/7 (71.4%) of patients was facial dysmorphism, defined by broad eyebrows and micrognathia. Other commonly reported phenotypes in patients with FABAS overlapped closely with phenotypes reported in patients with DOHH deficiency and, thus, cannot be defined as distinctive of the syndrome.

Cardiac abnormalities are a distinctive phenotype of DOHH disorder, with this being reported in 4/5 (80%) of patients. The most common cardiac abnormalities described in these patients include atrial septal defect (ASD) and atrioventricular (AV) block. Ophthalmological problems, mainly nystagmus or strabismus, and brain MRI anomalies were also reported in all patients with DOHH disorder.

Results for this review were limited to articles written in English and the search was limited to 3 databases. However, given the specificity and overall limited number of cases of patients with polyaminopathies combined with the fact that 4 of the five syndromes have only been described in the past 5 years, this critical narrative review approach serves as the most efficient and accurate method of compiling, analyzing, and summarizing all clinical data published to date in the scope of polyaminopathy research.

Understanding the molecular mechanisms, biochemical implications and the full spectrum of phenotypes of rare diseases is essential for improving diagnostic rates, accuracy of diagnosis, and identification of treatments. Cases of patients with polyaminopathies continue to be identified and published and the field of polyaminopathy research is rapidly expanding. The five known polyaminopathies share many overlapping features, including developmental delay, dysmorphic features, hypotonia, and nonspecific brain MRI findings. Bone features and dysmorphic facial features, primarily facial asymmetry and thin upper lip with full lower lip are distinctive of SRS, whereas alopecia and macrocephaly are unique to BABS. Skin differences, epilepsy, and absence of brain MRI findings are distinguishing features of DHPS deficiency. Phenotypes that are distinctive of FABAS include dysmorphic facial features, primarily micrognathia. Finally, brain MRI findings, ophthalmological differences, and cardiology differences are distinctive of DOHH disorder.

With 4 of the 5 known polyaminopathies being described in just the past 5 years, this review is incredibly timely for rare disease clinicians and researchers. As patients with rare disease often present with phenotypes that are on a spectrum, the compilation of published clinical cases that this review provides will aid clinicians in the diagnosis and medical management of patients with polyaminopathies. This review uncovers patterns in the overlapping phenotypes and molecular mechanisms of these syndromes, which will serve as a resource for future research as we continue to learn more about this growing family of rare diseases.
