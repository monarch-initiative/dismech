# Aortoiliac and superior mesenteric artery narrowing and calcification in Singleton Merten syndrome.
**Authors:** Arshad H, Fishman EK
**Journal:** Radiol Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.radcr.2025.11.037](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.radcr.2025.11.037)

## Content

Singleton Merten Syndrome (SMS) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by IFIH1 or DDX58 mutations, characterized by chronic inflammation leading to vascular calcifications, valvular disease, musculoskeletal abnormalities, dental dysplasia, skin findings like psoriasis and glaucoma. We report an 8-year-old boy with a DDX58 mutation associated SMS who presented with difficulty walking and ankle pain, found to have extensive aortoiliac and mesenteric artery narrowing. Advanced imaging with computed tomography (CT) and cinematic rendering provided detailed vascular mapping, underscoring the importance of radiologic evaluation in detecting and evaluating the extent of vascular involvement in SMS.

Singleton Merten Syndrome (SMS) was first described by Singleton and Merten in 1973 in 2 children as a combination of aortic calcification, bilateral glaucoma, dental dysplasia, and skeletal changes including osteoporosis and enlarged medullary cavities [ 1 ]. It is a rare autosomal dominant disorder which can be divided into 2 genetic subtypes. SMS type 1 is associated with a mutation in the IFIH1 protein gene and SMS type 2 is associated with mutation in DDX58 gene [ [2] , [3] , [4] , [5] , [6] , [7] ]. The underlying pathophysiology involves mutation-driven chronic inflammation and elevated cytokine activity, resulting in multisystem involvement [ 6 ]. Patients commonly develop aortic stenosis and insufficiency, with widespread vascular calcifications affecting the abdominal aorta, iliac arteries, and coronary arteries, and represent the primary cause of sudden death in this condition [ 8 , 9 ].

We present the case of an 8-year-old boy with a DDX58 mutation SMS who developed difficulty walking and ankle pain. Imaging revealed aortoiliac and superior mesenteric artery thickening with calcifications. While previous reports have largely focused on the genetic and molecular pathways of SMS, this case emphasizes the radiological perspective, demonstrating the characteristic vascular involvement through computed tomography angiography (CTA) and advanced cinematic rendering (CR).

An 8-year-old boy with known Singleton Merten syndrome (SMS) presented with increased difficulty walking and ankle pain. His onset of medical symptomatology associated with SMS started around age 18 months. He had corneal dystrophy with cataracts starting early in his life, leading to multiple corneal transplants and cataract surgeries. He also reported photosensitivity, progressive acro-osteolysis of all distal phalangeal segments, reduced joint mobility and hand contractures, and dental anomalies with unerupted and misaligned teeth. Physical findings included small stature, poor weight gain, nail dystrophy, acquired ankle and knee deformity, hyperpigmented skin lesions, atopic dermatitis, hypoplasia of fingers, low muscle mass and tone, joint laxity, and scoliosis. His exome sequences were positive for variations in KRT6A, consistent with Pachyonychia congenita and a mutation of DDX58, a potential cause of SMS.

His difficulty in ambulation and inward deviation of ankles started 2–3 years ago. His parents also noted flattening of the feet. He started walking with a shuffling gait and reported weakness in his legs, joint abnormalities and progressive ankle pain with activity. He was recommended to use orthotics, that helped him to ambulate slightly better. However, symptoms worsened in the past few months requiring clinical evaluation.

On cardiac exam, a systolic ejection murmur was noted at the apex and back. Distal pulses were symmetrical but reduced in lower extremities. Previously, 16–23 mm Hg gradient were identified between upper and lower extremity blood pressures. With known association of SMS with vascular and cardiac thickening and calcifications, computed tomography angiography (CTA) and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was performed. TTE noted no coarctation of aorta and normal flow in abdominal aorta. Mild tricuspid and moderate mitral valve regurgitation was noted.

On CTA (Fig. 1), wall irregularity, narrowing and calcification were noted in the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Marked narrowing of aorta was seen near the bifurcation and bilateral common iliac arteries had small caliber. SMA was also constricted, with poststenotic ectasia. JAK-inhibitor therapy (Ruxolitinib) was started to control the inflammation. He was kept under long-term follow up to evaluate for improvement. Later, he was noted to have some clinical improvement with weight gain, decreased musculoskeletal pain, slowed decline of vision, and increased exercise tolerance. However, the joint abnormalities and vascular narrowing remain unchanged on follow-up imaging. Due to moderate clinical improvement, ruxolitinib was continued and he was kept under long-term surveillance with yearly CTA for vascular assessment. Singleton Merten syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder, first described in 1973, affecting multiple body systems including musculoskeletal, dental, skin and cardiovascular. It is classified as a congenital type 1 interferonopathy [ 1 , 10 ]. The cardinal symptoms of SMS include skeletal abnormalities such as osteopenia/osteoporosis, several dental anomalies including delayed or failed dental eruption, extensive bone resorption, glaucoma, vascular and valvular calcifications, and skin manifestations like psoriasis [ 11 , 12 ]. Later, in 1976, Gay and Kuhn expanded on the phenotypic spectrum of SMS reporting 4 patients with additional symptoms of muscle weakness as well as psoriatic skin lesions [ 13 ]. Uncommon features reported in SMS include resistant hypertension of unknown origin, rickets, femoral head necrosis, and strabismus [ 14 , 15 ].

Chronic inflammation due to gene mutations is the main factor leading to widespread vascular and valvular calcifications. It was initially thought that SMS was related to IFIH1 mutation only (typical SMS type 1), however, GOF mutation in DDX58 genes have also been described, which was considered "atypical" SMS type 2 [ 11 ]. Mutations in DDX58 were initially considered as "atypical" due to lack of classic dental anomalies, tendon rupture, and muscle weakness. However, recently, severe and diverse phenotypic presentations of this mutation have been seen [ 3 , 6 ]. This can be seen in our patient who had delayed dental eruption and progressive muscle weakness. Aortic and valvular calcification was also seen in 5 out of 7 DDX58-mutation SMS patients [ 6 ]. Symptomatic valvular heart disease is treated by surgical intervention including open cardiac or minimally invasive valve replacement [ 16 ].

Both type of mutations leads to dysregulation of the immune response leading to chronic inflammation. The Melanoma Differentiation-Associated protein 5 (MDA5) and retinoic acid-inducible gene 1 (RIG-I) proteins encoded by these genes are key mediators of the interferon cascade that normally initiate antiviral defense. Their overactivation leads to excessive interferon signaling, resulting in systemic inflammation and multiorgan involvement [ 12 , [17] , [18] , [19] ]. IFIH1 and DDX58 gene mutations are associated with a range of autoimmune diseases including Aicardi-Goutières syndrome, which can present as an overlapping phenotype [ [20] , [21] , [22] ]. Other associated autoimmune conditions include diabetes, psoriasis, autoimmune thyroid disease, and systemic lupus erythematosus [ 14 ].

Cardiovascular features reported in SMS include aortic valve and mitral valve stenosis and calcifications, aortic medial necrosis, calcifications of coronary artery, descending aorta, carotid arteries, abdominal aorta, subclavian arteries and iliac arteries [ 6 , 8 , 9 , [23] , [24] , [25] ]. Consequently, patients often develop left ventricular hypertrophy, arrhythmias, and progressive valvular dysfunction [ 26 ]. Consequences of progressive vascular calcification is the leading cause of death in such patients [ 8 ]. These calcifications are unusual due to early onset (often in childhood or puberty), as seen in our patient. They commonly localize in the ascending aorta with possible involvement of coronary ostia, and rapid progression to the aortic arch. In severe cases, calcification can be so extensive forming large bars of calcium [ 9 ]. Ozyuksel et al. [ 24 ] reported a young girl with SMS who initially underwent aortic root enlargement and valve replacement for stenosis but later developed progressive supra-aortic stenosis that required urgent surgical intervention. In our patient, imaging demonstrated aorto-iliac wall thickening and calcification with significant luminal narrowing (Fig. 1).

Our patient had the GOF DDX58 mutation with clinical symptoms of SMS including progressive destruction of distal phalanges, nail dystrophy, hyperpigmented skin lesions, reduced joint mobility and hand contractures, muscle weakness, unerupted and misaligned teeth, corneal dystrophy and aortoiliac narrowing. His declining corneal function led to multiple failed corneal transplants, which can be attributed to dysfunctional RIG-1 receptors in the cornea. Juvenile open-angle glaucoma, diagnosed between ages 3–18 years and present in ∼90% of SMS patients with a DDX58 variant, is hypothesized to arise from IFN-β–mediated autophagy in trabecular meshwork cells, leading to elevated intraocular pressure [ 27 , 28 ].

Recent studies have emphasized targeting the dysregulated JAK-STAT pathway in SMS, as IFN-mediated apoptosis and necrosis disrupt osteoblastogenesis and contribute to skeletal dysplasia. These insights have led to the use of JAK inhibitors and anti-IFN therapies as potential treatment strategies [ 10 , 14 ]. Clinical evidence shows that tofacitinib leads to rapid resolution of psoriatic skin lesions within a month, while ruxolitinib, another JAK-STAT inhibitor, has been associated with improved osteoblast number, reduced inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and pMLKL expression, and restoration of bone mineralization [ 10 , 29 ]. Notably, Boser et al. reported marked clinical improvements including disappearance of psoriatic lesions within days, enhanced muscle strength, significant weight gain, and reduction of the interferon quantity in blood, following ruxolitinib treatment [ 21 ]. Our patient similarly demonstrated substantial benefit with ruxolitinib, supporting the therapeutic potential of JAK inhibition in SMS. However, its impact on inflammation-driven vascular calcification remains an important area for further study.

Singleton Merten syndrome is a rare genetic interferonopathy characterized by early-onset, extensive vascular calcifications driven by chronic inflammation. This case underscores the value of advanced imaging in defining the vascular anatomy, with CTA, MIP, and advanced CR providing detailed visualization of aorto-iliac and mesenteric arterial narrowing and calcifications. Such radiologic evaluation is essential for accurate vascular mapping, facilitating timely recognition of disease progression and guiding management decisions. Early detection of vascular involvement may prompt surgical intervention where feasible. The potential of emerging anti-inflammatory and targeted therapies, such as JAK inhibitors, has shown promise in addressing skeletal and skin abnormalities in SMS; however, their role in preventing or alleviating vascular calcification remains to be underexplored. Raising awareness of these imaging findings and their clinical implications is essential for improving outcomes in SMS, where morbidity and mortality remain strongly linked to progressive cardiovascular disease.
