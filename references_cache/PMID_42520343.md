# Multi-generational mitochondrial complex V deficiency due to the recurrent ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) pathogenic variant: A novel family and a review of the literature.
**Authors:** Williams A, Mizerik E, Chang HC, Difalco CR, Liu N, Bacino C, Bertuch AA, Rosenfeld JA, Undiagnosed Diseases Network, Lee BH, Elsea SH, Murali CN, Scaglia F
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgme.2026.110207](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgme.2026.110207)

## Content

ATP5F1A encodes part of the catalytic core of mitochondrial complex V, which is responsible for the majority of ATP production. Mitochondrial complex V deficiency, nuclear type 4A (MC5DN4A; MIM#620358) is due to monoallelic pathogenic variants in ATP5F1A. MC5DN4A is a neonatal-onset disorder with features including growth faltering, developmental delay, epilepsy, and a biochemical phenotype indicative of urea cycle dysfunction. Interestingly, patients who have MC5DN4A due to the recurrent pathogenic ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant appear to demonstrate clinical resolution before 18 months of age. All reported cases of MC5DN4A due to the ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant have been de novo. Here we present a mother and her three children with MC5DN4A harboring the ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant. The oldest child has growth faltering, seizures, autism spectrum disorder, global developmental delay, urea cycle dysfunction, and elevated plasma lactate without full clinical resolution. The two younger children have similar biochemical findings with a more severe clinical phenotype including congenital heart disease, growth faltering, sideroblastic anemia, and hypogammaglobulinemia, however both children are showing signs of spontaneous clinical resolution. Additionally, both younger children have a deletion of the entire coding sequence of NDUFA12, which may be contributing to the more severe presentation. The mother is reportedly asymptomatic. In summary, we present the first known instance of transgenerational transmission of the pathogenic ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant. The presence of congenital heart disease, sideroblastic anemia, and hypogammaglobulinemia in two cases may indicate a phenotypic expansion of MC5DN4A or may suggest a modifying effect of the NDUFA12 deletion on the phenotype.

Mitochondrial complex V or mitochondrial ATP synthase is the principal producer of cellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [ 1 ]. During physiologic conditions, complex V function is coupled to the redox reactions of mitochondrial complexes I-IV, which compose the electron transport chain (ETC) [ 2 ]. The ETC pumps protons from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient [ 2 ]. These protons then fall down their electrochemical gradient through complex V into the mitochondrial matrix, generating ATP [ 2 ]. Complex V consists of two main domains: F1 and Fo, with Fo forming the proton pore and F1 forming the ATPase [ 3 ]. The Fo proton pore consists of repeating c subunits, with accessory a, e, f, g, j, k, and 8 (also known as A6L) subunits [ 4 ]. F1 is a hetero-multimer made up of α, β, γ, δ, and ε subunits in a 3:3:1:1:1 stoichiometric ratio, respectively. A peripheral stalk supports the F1 and Fo domains and consists of the b, d, F6, and the oligomycin sensitivity-conferring protein (OSCP) subunits [ 4 ]. Given its importance in cellular energetics, complex V deficiency causes a diverse range of phenotypes depending on the severity of the deficiency [ 3 ]. The most common complex V deficiency is due to pathogenic variants or deletions in the mitochondrial-encoded ATP6 (MT-ATP6) and ATP8 (MT-ATP8) genes [ 5 , 6 ]. The phenotypes described in patients with ATP6 or ATP8 variants range from isolated ataxia; neuropathy, ataxia, and retinitis pigmentosa (NARP); maternally inherited Leigh syndrome (MILS); and mitochondrial myopathy, lactic acidosis and sideroblastic anemia (MLASA) [ 5– 7 ]. Complex V deficiency may also be due to biallelic variants in nuclear-encoded genes that are involved in complex V biogenesis such as ATPAF2 (formerly known as ATP12) and TMEM70 [ 8 , 9 ]. Complex V deficiency commonly has the biochemical finding of hyperlactatemia, and 3-methylglutaconic aciduria has been described in complex V deficiency due to nuclear-encoded genes [ 10 ]. Interestingly, by 2010 only one nuclear gene that directly encodes for a complex V subunit, ATP5F1E, had been described to cause autosomal recessive complex V deficiency [ 11 ]. However, over the past 15 years, four other nuclear genes (ATP5F1A, ATP5PO, ATP5F1D, and ATP5MK) encoding various complex V subunits have been shown to cause complex V deficiency when biallelic pathogenic variants are present [ 11– 17 ]. ATP5F1A encodes part of the alpha subunit of the catalytic core of complex V [ 16 ]. Multiple disease phenotypes have been linked to this gene. Combined oxidative phosphorylation deficiency 22 (MIM #616045) and mitochondrial complex V deficiency, nuclear type 4B, encephalopathic type (MIM #615228) are due to biallelic pathogenic variants in ATP5F1A, and result in a fatal neonatal mitochondrial encephalopathy which have been replicated in a murine model [ 13 , 18 ]. In contrast, mitochondrial complex V deficiency nuclear type 4A (MC5DN4A; MIM #620358) is due to monoallelic pathogenic variants in ATP5F1A. ATP5F1A is one of only two genes currently associated with autosomal dominant complex V deficiency, with the other being ATP5F1B, which encodes the beta subunit of the catalytic core of complex V [ 14 , 19– 21 ]. Of note, ATP5F1A and ATP5F1B directly interact on the protein level to form the catalytic core of complex V, and pathogenic variants in both genes cause an overlapping phenotype including movement disorders, growth faltering (GF), and developmental delay (DD). Specifically, MC5DN4A due to pathogenic monoallelic variants in ATP5F1A is a neonatal-onset mitochondrial disorder with variable features including GF, DD, intellectual disability (ID), movement disorders, epilepsy, hyperlactatemia with metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia, and depletion of urea cycle intermediates in plasma (citrulline, arginine and ornithine). Intriguingly, individuals with MC5DN4A due to the pathogenic ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant appear to demonstrate spontaneous clinical resolution before 18 months [ 4 , 14 , 16 ]. The reason for this is currently unclear. The ATP5F1A c.545G>A (p.Arg182Gln), c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe), c.992C>T (p.Pro331Leu), c.326T>C (p.Leu109Ser), c.1404del (p.Glu469Serfs*3), and c.1252G>A (p.Gly418Arg) variants have been previously reported to be pathogenic leading to MC5DN4A that does not resolve [ 4 , 14 , 16 , 21 ]. All variants previously reported in the literature except the c.1252G>A (p.Gly418Arg) variant are apparently de novo [ 4 , 14 , 16 , 21 ]. While Fielder et al, 2025 demonstrated that a dominant negative effect underlies the pathogenesis for the c.545G>A (p.Arg182Gln), c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe), c.992C>T (p.Pro331Leu), and c.326T>C (p.Leu109Ser) variants, it is unclear whether the p.(Arg207His) variant causes disease through haploinsufficiency or a similar dominant negative effect and whether this mediates the spontaneous resolution phenotype [ 4 , 14 , 16 ]. Here we present a two-generation case of three siblings and their mother with MC5DN4A due to the pathogenic ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant, making this the first reported occurrence of its transgenerational inheritance.

Case 1 is a 10-year-old male with a history of GF, clubfeet, infantile spasms, global DD, and autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Birth and pregnancy history were unremarkable. He was born full term, and Apgar scores were 9 at 1 minute and 9 and 5 minutes. At 4 months of age there was initial concern for GF (weight: Z= −5.41, length: Z= −5.42) with hypotonia, and he was admitted to the hospital. He required a gastrostomy tube for one month during this admission. Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) was normal. Initial plasma amino acid analysis (PAA) showed low arginine (22 μmol/L, normal (n) 42-132 μmol/L) and citrulline (1 μmol/L, n 2-41 μmol/L). Repeat PAA one week later showed persistence of low arginine (17 mmol/L, n 42-132 mmol/L) and citrulline (1 mmol/L, n 2-41 mmol/L) (Table 1). Serum ammonia and pyruvic acid were normal. Urine organic acid analysis (UOA) was non-diagnostic. Free carnitine was drawn at 5 months of life and was low at 10 μmol/L (n 28-50 μmol/L). Initial venous lactate was 3.4 μmol/L (n 0.2-2 μmol/L). Follow up venous lactate was 3.4 μmol/L (n 0.2-2 μmol/L) at 5 months of age. He then presented at eight months of age with concern for infantile spasms. Initial therapy with adrenocorticotropic hormone was ineffective, and he required dual therapy with zonisamide and clobazam. Trio exome sequencing (ES) was performed and was non-diagnostic at that time (Table 1). Follow up PAA showed low arginine (26 μmol/L, n 42-132) with normal citrulline. Orotic acid analysis was normal. Acylcarnitine panel (ACP) was normal with normalization of free carnitine without supplementation. Seizures completely resolved at 16 months of age, and he was weaned off anti-seizure medications. He was first diagnosed with global DD at a year of age; he did not walk or talk until two years of age. His delayed walking was thought to be due to the casting of his clubfeet and not due to his underlying mitochondrial disorder. Due to persistent speech delay and behavioral issues, he was diagnosed with ASD around 4 years of age. With therapies and special education classes his developmental issues have greatly improved with almost normal speech for age. However, he still carries a diagnosis of ASD and struggles with bouts of aggression. Currently, growth remains delayed (height: Z = −2.01, weight: Z = −2.27) while hypotonia has improved. At the age of 9, duo genome sequencing (GS) was sent by his pediatrician which identified a maternally inherited ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant, consistent with a diagnosis of MC5DN4A. At most recent genetics visit at age 10 PAA and carnitine panel was repeated. Free carnitine remained normalized at 35 μmol/L (n 25-54 μmol/L). PAA revealed persistently low urea cycle intermediates with low citrulline (5 μmol/L, n 9-52 μmol/L), low arginine (37 μmol/L, n 38-122 μmol/L), and low ornithine (31 μmol/L, n 33-103 μmol/L). L-citrulline supplementation was started at 250 mg/kg/day.

Case 2 is the 4-year-old younger brother of Case 1, with a history of hypoglycemia, patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), GF, sideroblastic anemia (SA), and hypogammaglobulinemia. The pregnancy was complicated by intra-uterine growth restriction (IUGR) and suspected coarctation of the aorta. Following delivery, Apgar scores were 8 at 1 minute and 9 at 5 minutes. At birth he was small for gestational age (SGA, weight: Z= −2.66, length: Z= −2.58). He was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for further care. No coarctation was detected on postnatal echocardiogram, however he was found to have prominent left ventricular trabeculations not meeting criteria for non-compaction, large PDA, and moderately depressed left ventricular function. He had persistent hypoglycemia over the first few weeks of life, requiring a maximum glucose infusion rate (GIR) of up to 12.1 mg/kg/min. CMA was sent on day of life 2, which identified a copy number loss within chromosome band 12q22 spanning approximately 0.228 Mb, including the NDUFA12 gene (arr[GRCh37] 12q22(95364858_95593294)x1). The CMA also identified an intragenic gain within chromosome band 6q12 spanning approximately 0.047 Mb including one non-coding exon and the first two coding exons of the EYS gene (arr[GRCh37] 6q12(66174543_66221417)x3). Arterial lactate at birth was 3.8 μmol/L (n 0.2-1.7 μmol/L). Ammonia was 82 μmol/L (n 47-80 μmol/L) on day of life 10 and 87 μmol/L (n 47-80 μmol/L) on day of life 12. Arterial lactate peaked at 11.4 μmol/L (n 0.2-1.7 μmol/L) on day of life 32 and generally remained between 3-5 μmol/L over his first year of life. GDF-15 was sent and was >6,000 pg/mL (reference value <1,150 pg/mL), suggesting mitochondrial dysfunction. Additionally, lactic acid, succinic acid and fumaric acid were increased on UOA, further suggesting possible mitochondrial dysfunction. Untargeted metabolomics analysis done at two weeks of age demonstrated significant deficiency of arginine (Z= −5.3), urea (Z= −6.5), ornithine (Z= −8.2), and citrulline (Z= −9.5) with significant elevation of orotate (Z= 5.0), lactate (Z= 2.3), alanine (Z= 5.4), and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutarate (Z= 4.6). This was overall suggestive of mitochondrial dysfunction along with specific dysfunction of the urea cycle. PAA done at the same time showed marked glutamine elevation (1486 μmol/L, n 238-842 μmol/L) along with low arginine (5 μmol/L, n 42-132 μmol/L), consistent with urea cycle dysfunction. Free carnitine at 3 weeks of life on ACP was low at 11 μmol/L (n 28-56 μmol/L). He additionally had persistently elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis requiring high doses of sodium citrate supplementation. Trio GS was sent at 2 months of age and returned with the known NDUFA12 deletion, which was determined to be paternally inherited, but was otherwise non-diagnostic. Due to high suspicion for mitochondrial disorder and non-diagnostic genetic testing, he was enrolled in the Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN) at 13 months of age. During his original NICU stay he was found to have intermittently low hemoglobin counts with low reticulocyte counts and no evidence of hemolysis. Additionally, he was noted to have persistently low absolute neutrophil counts with a low of 620 cells/μL (n 2,500-6,000 cells/μL). For this reason, he was evaluated by hematology and immunology and received packed red blood cell (pRBC) transfusions every 6 weeks in addition to 100 mg of vitamin B6 daily. He had a bone marrow biopsy at 15 months of age, which was consistent with a diagnosis of mitochondrial cytopathy and sideroblastic anemia. He was diagnosed with hypogammaglobulinemia due to low B-cell count and has a history of frequent infections, although this has reportedly improved with intravenous immunoglobulin infusions (IVIG). While he continues to receive IVIG and oral vitamin B6 therapy, he is currently no longer pRBC transfusion dependent. He additionally carries a diagnosis of GF due to weight consistently below the 10th percentile. At last visit with genetics, he remains small but both weight and height are on the curve (weight: Z= −1.35, height: Z= −0.72). In terms of his developmental history, he has met almost all milestones, and he does not carry a diagnosis of developmental delay or ASD. Reanalysis of his GS through the UDN after Case 3’s GS was reported revealed the ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant. At most recent genetics visit at age 4 PAA and carnitine panel was repeated. Free carnitine remains low at 21 μmol/L (n 25-54 μmol/L). PAA revealed persistently low urea cycle intermediates with low citrulline (3 μmol/L, n 9-52 μmol/L) and low arginine (21 μmol/L, n 38-122 μmol/L). He was started on L-citrulline at 250 mg/kg/day and L-carnitine at 50 mg/kg/day.

Case 3 is the 23-month-old younger sister of Cases 1 and 2 with a history of complex CHD, GF, hypogammaglobulinemia, SA, and developmental delay. She was born term by caesarean section for known double outlet right ventricle (DORV). Upon delivery she was started on prostaglandin-E for the ductal dependent lesion. At birth she was SGA (weight: Z= −2.53, length: Z= −2.18). Her DORV has normally related great arteries and was classified as a Tetralogy of Fallot type physiology, with subaortic ventricular septal defect (VSD), secundum atrial septal defect (ASD), and severe pulmonary stenosis. She received a PDA stent at 1 month of age. CMA was sent upon delivery and identified the same changes identified in Case 2, including a copy number loss within chromosome band 12q22 spanning approximately 0.228 Mb, including the NDUFA12 gene, and an intragenic gain within chromosome band 6q12 spanning approximately 0.047 Mb including one non-coding exon and the first two coding exons of the EYS gene. On day 4 of life, the patient experienced hypoglycemic episodes requiring a GIR of up to 7.5 mg/kg/min. Additionally, plasma lactate was slightly elevated at 2 mmol/L (n 0.2-1.7 mmol/L), and serum ammonia was normal. Lactate rose again to 2.6 mmol/L (n 0.2-1.7 mmol/L) on day of life 9 and again on day of life 11 to 2.8 mmol/L (n 0.2-1.7 mmol/L). Finally, on day of life 24 lactate rose from 6.2 mmol/L to 11.5 mmol/L. She also developed severe metabolic acidosis with a bicarbonate less than 5 μmol/L (n 20-28 μmol/L). PAA at 1 month of age showed low arginine (19.07 μmol/L, n 35-140 μmol/L), low citrulline (<2.50 μmol/L, n 9-40 μmol/L), and low ornithine (16.33 μmol/L, n 30-228 μmol/L) (Table 1). Rapid trio GS identified a maternally inherited pathogenic variant in ATP5F1A, c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) (Table 1). L-citrulline supplementation was started at a dose of 385mg (~170 mg/kg/day) three times daily per urea cycle disorder guidelines [ 22 ]. After starting L-citrulline supplementation, repeat PAA demonstrated correction of low citrulline, arginine, and ornithine. By 5 months of age, her lactate levels had essentially normalized. Electron transport chain (ETC) analysis of complex I-complex IV was performed on cultured skin fibroblasts at 6 months of age, and enzymatic activities were normal. Similar to her older brother (Case 2), she has been diagnosed with sideroblastic anemia as well as hypogammaglobulinemia, for which she receives IVIG and vitamin B6. Additionally, she was pRBC transfusion dependent until 19 months of age. Most recently, gross motor delay and feeding difficulties were noted, although she has had her central line removed and now uses formula with some solid foods. She still cannot walk unassisted. Growth continues to be delayed (height: Z= −2.84, weight: Z= −2.43). PAA at most recent genetics visit at 23 months revealed persistently low citrulline (1 μmol/L, n 4-50 μmol/L), so her dose of L-citrulline was increased to 250 mg/kg/day.

Case 4 is the 29-year-old mother of Cases 1-3. She does not recall any childhood medical history. She is currently asymptomatic, without any dietary aversion to protein, and has normal growth and development as an adult. No biochemical analyses have been conducted in the mother due to financial limitations. Her diagnosis was revealed on Case 3’s trio GS showing the ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant.

Complex V deficiency due to monoallelic variants in ATP5F1A (mitochondrial complex V deficiency, nuclear type 4A or MC5DN4A) is a unique disorder of mitochondrial metabolism as it is one of only two currently identified autosomal dominant complex V deficiencies, and a subset of patients (specifically those with the c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant) demonstrate spontaneous clinical resolution in early childhood [ 4 , 14 , 16 , 19- 21 ]. Here we review the literature and discuss our family with 4 heterozygous carriers of p.(Arg207His) to share novel insights into this enigmatic disorder.

Lines et al, 2021 first reported three probands with a heterozygous de novo c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant in ATP5F1A causing a complex V deficiency (later termed MC5DN4A) [ 14 ]. In this paper, the authors described a phenotype of neonatal onset GF and feeding difficulties, with two of the probands developing anemia and chronic diarrhea, and one proband demonstrating intermittent hyperammonemia associated with encephalopathy [ 14 ]. All cases had some initial developmental delays with symptom resolution by 18 months of age [ 14 ]. Functional studies in cultured fibroblasts demonstrated decreased oxygen consumption rate (OCR), complex V activity, and complex V expression, however resting mitochondrial inner membrane potential remained normal in probands versus controls [ 14 ]. Finally, the authors showed that the Arg207 residue sits at the interface between the α and β-subunits, and at mitochondrial matrix pH (7.8) the p.(Arg207His) variant may strengthen the interaction between the α and β-subunits leading to increased affinity [ 14 ]. In 2022, Zech et al published data from three probands with complex V deficiency due to heterozygous ATP5F1A variants: one of the probands also had a de novo c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant, while the two others had novel de novo variants; c.545G>A (p.Arg182Gln) and c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe) [ 16 ]. Similarly to the data from Lines et al, 2021, the Zech et al, 2022 group demonstrated decreased complex V activity in cultured fibroblasts from the proband with the ATP5F1A c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant, as well as a similar clinical course to previously reported cases with developmental delays and GF with clinical resolution within the first two years of life [ 16 ].

Evaluation of each of our cases’ clinical and biochemical phenotypes is consistent with previous descriptions of probands with MC5DN4A due to the c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant (summarized in Table 1) [ 14 , 16 ]. Three of the current cases demonstrated onset in infancy and GF, along with all previously reported cases in the literature. Developmental delays, with some persisting late into childhood, were identified in Cases 1 and 3. Case 1 also had seizures, although these resolved in the first two years of life; one other case in the literature was reported to have had seizures [ 16 ].

While anemia of unknown type was previously described in two other cases, Cases 2 and 3 are the first to have bone marrow studies characterizing a sideroblastic anemia and neutropenia consistent with a mitochondrial cytopathy. Sideroblastic anemia is the accumulation of ring sideroblasts in the bone marrow due to disrupted heme biosynthesis and has been previously reported in mitochondrial disorders such as Pearson syndrome and MLASA [ 7 , 23- 25 ]. MLASA may be due to deficiencies of nuclear-encoded genes such as PUS1 and YARS2, however, Burrage et al, 2014 described MLASA due to a heteroplasmic pathogenic MT-ATP6 variant, demonstrating that complex V deficiency may cause SA [ 7 , 26 , 27 ]. Heme synthesis begins in the mitochondria with the condensation of glycine and succinyl-CoA by δ-aminolevulinate synthase [ 25 , 28 ]. The mechanism of sideroblastic anemia in mitochondrial disorders is likely multifaceted and may be due to factors such as decreased bioavailability of succinyl-CoA and faulty iron utilization/sensing [ 23 , 25 , 29 ]. We suggest that sideroblastic anemia may represent phenotypic expansion of MC5DN4A. Hypogammaglobulinemia was another hematologic phenotype reported in two of our cases. Immune dysregulation is a known phenotype in mitochondrial disorders, ranging from cytopenias (such as the neutropenia in Cases 2 and 3) to interferonopathies [ 30- 33 ]. Specifically, hypogammaglobinemia has been reported in POLG- and FBXL4-related mitochondrial disorders [ 31 , 33 ]. We suggest the presence of hypogammaglobulinemia in Cases 2 and 3 may represent phenotypic expansion of MC5DN4A.

Additionally, two of our cases had CHD, with Case 2 having a patent ductus arteriosus and Case 3 having complex CHD with DORV. There is an emerging body of evidence on the association between mitochondrial dysfunction and CHD, however specific genotype-phenotype correlations are not well described [ 34 , 35 ]. Interestingly, biallelic variants in TMEM70 are associated with congenital heart disease in the Romani population in Central Europe, demonstrating that complex V deficiency may result in cardiac phenotypes [ 8 , 36 ]. Additionally, Fielder et al, 2025 published six cases of severe MC5DN4A due to non-c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variants, with two of the probands demonstrating CHD such as mildly dilated aortic sinus of Valsalva and atrial septal defect [ 4 ]. We further suggest that CHD may represent phenotypic expansion of MC5DN4A. However, we acknowledge that cytogenetic deletions may also cause CHD and cannot rule out the Complex V deletion itself causing the CHD phenotype, as this deletion was present in both Case 2 and 3, and proving phenotypic expansion will be difficult without phenotyping more patients with MC5DN4A.

While phenotypic expansion is an intriguing possibility, it is important to note that Cases 2 and 3 carry a paternally inherited copy number loss within chromosome band 12q22 spanning approximately 0.228 Mb, including the NDUFA12 gene. Biallelic pathogenic variants in this gene are associated with mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 23 (OMIM# 618244), inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. These data indicate that Cases 2 and 3 are carriers for this disorder. However, one further question we have regarding this deletion is whether this deletion could act as a modifier on the ATP5F1A variant given that the ETC complexes form supercomplexes on the inner mitochondrial membrane [ 37- 39 ]. If this deletion is acting as a modifier, this is unlikely to be due to a direct physical interaction as Complex V is thought to sit at the tips of the mitochondrial cristae whereas the ETC supercomplexes are thought to sit along the cristae away from the tips [ 3 , 37- 39 ]. While admittedly unlikely, a modifier effect may explain some of the phenotypic differences between the cases such as the hematologic/immunologic and cardiac phenotypes in Cases 2 and 3.

Symptom resolution was described in the four previously reported cases of MC5DN4A due to the recurrent c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant by the second year of life. Importantly, Case 4, which is the mother of Cases 1-3, is apparently asymptomatic now in her late 20s. She does not recall any symptoms as a child or hearing her parents discuss any symptoms. However, we cannot rule out that she was symptomatic as an infant with subsequent spontaneous resolution of symptoms. Interestingly, Cases 1-3 have a mix of persistent neurologic, hematologic/immunologic, and biochemical phenotypes. To our knowledge these are the first cases with the c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant to not have spontaneous clinical resolution within the first two years of life.

Biochemically, seven out of eight known cases had elevated plasma lactate, which was episodic. Additionally, of cases that had plasma amino acid analysis, all demonstrated low levels of at least one urea cycle intermediate such as ornithine, arginine, or citrulline. As expected with urea cycle dysfunction, glutamine was elevated in three cases. Hyperammonemia was present in four patients. These biochemical findings are in agreement with Lines et al, 2021’s suggestion that MC5DN4A may represent a population which may be misdiagnosed as ‘mutation-negative’ ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency [ 14 ]. Our team started Case 3 on L-citrulline supplementation with improvement of urea cycle intermediate levels on follow up PAAs in concordance with urea cycle disorder guidelines, which was suggested and used by Lines et al in their probands [ 14 , 22 ]. However, on most recent PAA she demonstrated low citrulline, requiring an increase in her L-citrulline dosing. Additionally, as Cases 1 and 2 both demonstrated low citrulline on recent PAA, L-citrulline supplementation was started after a recent clinic visit. Repeat PAAs after treatment initiation have not been completed yet. The mechanism of urea cycle dysfunction in MC5DN4A is currently unclear. As the first three (proximal) reactions of the urea cycle occur within the mitochondria, it is possible that global mitochondrial dysfunction could result in decreased activity of proximal urea cycle reactions. It is possible that there could be interference with the activity of the mitochondrial ornithine transporter, which could impair flux in the urea cycle. Additionally, hyperammonemia is seen in other Complex V deficiencies, like TMEM70-deficiency and ATP6-deficiency, with a proposed mechanism due to a decrease in available ATP for ATP-dependent enzymes in the urea cycle such as carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I and argininosuccinate synthetase [ 40- 42 ]. Five cases demonstrated elevated alanine, consistent with lactic acidosis and elevated total body nitrogen [ 43 , 44 ]. Case 2 also had a GDF-15 above 6,000 pg/mL, consistent with a diagnosis of a primary mitochondrial disorder, while two other cases in the literature also demonstrated elevated GDF-15 [ 45 ]. Interestingly, Case 1 and Case 2 both had very low free carnitine on ACP analysis, which has not been reported but may be another biochemical parameter to monitor. Both levels were drawn well after maternal carnitine effect is expected to wear off [ 46 ]. Carnitine deficiency is a known effect of mitochondrial disorders due to secondary depletion from increased use or degradation, and carnitine levels may need to be monitored in MC5DN4A [ 47 , 48 ]. On recent carnitine profile, free carnitine had normalized in Case 1 but remains low in Case 2. We have started Case 2 on L-carnitine supplementation after his most recent clinic visit. Interestingly, Cases 1-3 demonstrated an overall milder biochemical phenotype than the cases reported by Lines et al, 2021 and Zech et al, 2022, but an overall more severe clinical phenotype [ 14 , 16 ]. At this time, it is difficult to know if this is a clinically significant finding or within the realm of normal variation.

Recently, the mutational landscape of MC5DN4A has been expanded with novel insights into the disorder. Zech et al, 2022 showed the two probands with novel c.545G>A (p.Arg182Gln) and c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe) pathogenic variants in ATP5F1A did not show clinical resolution and were reported to have a persistent neurodevelopmental phenotype [ 16 ]. Fielder et al, 2025 published data from six more probands with MC5DN4A due to de novo monoallelic variants in ATP5F1A, including recurrences of the c.545G>A (p.Arg182Gln) and c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe) variants reported by Zech et al, 2022 and the novel c.992C>T (p.Pro331Leu) and c.326T>C (p.Leu109Ser) variants [ 4 ]. All probands had persistent neurodevelopmental issues without resolution [ 4 ]. The authors demonstrated that a dominant negative effect underlies pathogenesis for the c.545G>A (p.Arg182Gln), c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe), c.992C>T (p.Pro331Leu), and c.326T>C (p.Leu109Ser) variants [ 4 ]. Additionally, in contrast to the published functional data for the for the c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant from Lines et al, 2021, the c.545G>A (p.Arg182Gln), c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe), and c.326T>C (p.Leu109Ser) variants showed increased OCR and decreased resting mitochondrial inner membrane potential [ 4 , 14 ]. These recent data underscoring the dominant-negative mechanism of disease for the ATP5F1A c.545G>A (p.Arg182Gln), c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe), c.992C>T (p.Pro331Leu), and c.326T>C (p.Leu109Ser) variants beg the question of whether the c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant causes disease by the same mechanism. As discussed above, Lines et al, 2021 demonstrated that the c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant sits at the α and β-subunit interface, which may strengthen subunit affinity leading to a dominant-negative effect [ 14 ]. Other pathogenic variants reported to cause dominant-negative effects also lie at the α and β-subunit interface, and ATP5F1A itself is predicted to be tolerant to loss of function (pLI=0.29; o/e = 0.3–0.58)) [ 49 ]. However, no clear mechanism exists to explain why there is a difference in OCR and resting mitochondrial inner membrane potential between cases with the c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant and the c.545G>A (p.-Arg182Gln), c.1037C>T (p.Ser346Phe), c.992C>T (p.Pro331Leu), and c.326T>C (p.Leu109Ser) variants, or why most cases with the c.620G>A (p.Arg207His) variant demonstrate spontaneous clinical improvement over the first two years of life. Further work is required to elucidate answers to these questions.

In conclusion, here we present the first reported family with transgenerational inheritance of the ATP5F1A p.(Arg207His) variant associated with the MC5DN4A phenotype. Additionally, we present data suggesting a phenotypic expansion of MC5DN4A.
