# Arterial Tortuosity Syndrome: A Case Report and Literature Review

Arterial tortuosity syndrome (ATS) is a rare genetic syndrome characterized by elongation, tortuosity, stenosis, and aneurysm formation of major arteries. A one year ten months old boy presented with a history of repeated chest infections. Cardiovascular examination was normal except for slightly increased heart rate (130 beats per minute). Echocardiography revealed situs solitus, levocardia, D-loop, and atrioventricular and ventricular-arterial concordance. Both right and left pulmonary arteries appeared mildly narrowed in caliber, measuring 7.1 and 9 mm, respectively. CT angiography was advised for detailed evaluation of branch pulmonary arteries. CT showed early branching of the main pulmonary trunk with narrowed caliber branch pulmonary arteries forming an inverted V sign on axial maximum intensity projection images and an V sign on coronal volume rendering technique images. All the three arch branches showed tortuous courses after their origin. The thoraco-abdominal aorta also appeared tortuous in course that extended beyond the normal expected course into adjacent anatomic areas. There was no evidence of any aortic aneurysm or dissection. In view of these typical imaging findings, the diagnosis of ATS was made. This case report highlights the role of radiological imaging to ascertain the diagnosis and detect the complications of ATS.

Arterial tortuosity syndrome (ATS) is a connective tissue disorder caused by a mutation in the SLC2A10 gene. Its exact incidence and prevalence is unknown because affected individuals may go undiagnosed or misdiagnosed. Onset is usually in infancy or early childhood. Approximately 100 cases have been reported in the medical literature. Usually, there is involvement of large and medium sized arteries that include the aorta, pulmonary arteries and arch branches, but in rare cases involvement of smaller arteries has also been seen [1]. Affected vessels become twisted, forming loops and kinks. These tortuous vessels are prone to aneurysm formation, dissection, and ischemic events. Cardiovascular complications include hypertension, stroke, and infarcts. Respiratory complications may arise due to vascular compression of airways and include difficult breathing and repeated chest infections. Early diagnosis of this syndrome is of paramount importance to avoid life threatening complications. This case report provides an overview of the typical imaging features of ATS to aid radiologists in making this uncommon diagnosis.

A one year ten months old boy was referred to our institution with a history of repeated chest infections. According to the family history, he was the first child of healthy consanguineous parents. Physical examination revealed that the patient's weight (14.3 kg) and height (91 cm) were appropriate for his age. His skin appeared slightly hyperelastic. Cardiovascular examination was normal except for a slightly increased heart rate (130 beats per minute). His chest appeared clear on auscultation. All the routine blood investigations were normal. Echocardiography revealed situs solitus, levocardia, D-loop, and atrioventricular and ventricular-arterial concordance. The foramen ovale was patent. Trace tricuspid regurgitation was noted with a pressure gradient of 50 mm Hg. Laminar flow was seen in the left and right ventricular outflow tracts. A confluent branching pattern of the pulmonary artery was seen. Both right and left pulmonary arteries appeared mildly narrowed in caliber and measured 7.1 and 9 mm, respectively. Mild flow acceleration was seen in branch pulmonary arteries (maximum pressure gradient was 17 mm Hg). The aortic root appeared normal. Ventricular functions were within normal limits. CT angiography was advised for detailed evaluation of branch pulmonary arteries. CT angiography showed early branching of the main pulmonary artery with narrowed caliber branch pulmonary arteries forming an inverted V sign (Fig. 1A) on axial maximum intensity projection and a V sign on coronal volume rendering technique images (Fig. 1B). All the three arch branches showed a tortuous course after origin (Fig. 2A and B). The thoraco-abdominal aorta also appeared tortuous in course and extended beyond the normal expected course into adjacent anatomic areas (Fig. 2C and D). In view of the typical imaging findings, a diagnosis of ATS was made. As there was no life-threatening emergency, no active intervention was done. The patient is in good condition and is on follow up. Many genetic syndromes like Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, aneurysms osteoarthritis syndrome, autosomal recessive cutis laxa type 1, and Menkes disease have arterial tortuosity as one of their features and are good differentials for this case. However, Marfan syndrome has typical marfanoid features and vascular changes that are evident mainly as aortic root dilatation and aortic aneurysm. Loeys-Dietz syndrome is characterized by the triad of bifid uvula, hypertelorism, and arterial tortuosity or aneurysm. Aneurysms osteoarthritis syndrome and autosomal recessive cutis laxa type 1 have osteoarthritis and lax skin as predominant features, respectively. Menkes disease is characterized by hypotonia and kinky hairs. None of these morphological features were present in our case except mild skin hyperelasticity. Beside this, the involvement of pulmonary arteries, especially their narrowing is specific to ATS. ATS is a rare autosomal recessive connective tissue disorder related to SLC2A10 mutation (chromosome 20q13). It is characterized by disruption of elastic fibers in the medial layer of the arterial wall which leads to various ranges of manifestations that involve the cutaneous, skeletal, gastrointestinal, and cardiovascular systems. It was first described in 1967 [1]. It affects both sexes equally and presents in childhood depending upon the severity of involvement of various systems and the presence of complications.

The typical cutaneous manifestation is skin laxity. Although it is a clinical feature, it can occasionally be seen on plain radiographs as well. Chest radiographs demonstrate linear shadows that overlie the lung fields. Skeletal manifestations include chest wall deformity, hyper mobile joints, pectus excavatum, pectus carinatum, arachnodactyly, and kyphoscoliosis. Gastrointestinal manifestations encompass esophageal elongation, reflux, various hernias (hiatal, Morgagni, Bochdalek, and inguinal) and duodeno-jejunal diverticulae. There is involvement of the diaphragm as well, that leads to diaphragmatic humps and eventration.

Among the cardiovascular malformations, vascular tortuosity is the most common finding. It can be localized or generalized, and may affect small sized (subungual capillaries and retinal arteries) and middle sized (coronary, cerebrovascular or iliac arteries) arteries or the aorta. Tortuosity is defined as an S- or C-shaped elongation or undulation along the course of the vessel. Kinking is defined as acute angulation in the vessel course. This can vary from mild (angle ≥60°) to moderate (angle between 30° and 60°) and severe (angle <30°). Looping is an exaggerated S-shaped curve and coiling represents a circle formation [2,3,4].

There are three patterns of tortuosity described by Bullitt et al. [5] Type I includes high-amplitude and low-frequency tortuosity which corresponds to C- or S-shaped curves. Type II includes medium-amplitude and medium-frequency curves. Type III is characterized by low-amplitude–high-frequency tortuosity that usually appears as tight coils.

There are various radiological signs described in the literature with respect to arterial tortuosity. These include aortic elongation sign, meandering vessel sign, cluster of vessels sign, and V and inverted V signs. Aortic elongation sign is the prolongation of the aortic knuckle seen on chest radiographs in young adults. A meandering vessel is a tortuous vessel which extends beyond the normal expected course into adjacent anatomic areas. Excessive tortuosity is seen as a clustering of vessels on axial contrast enhanced CT (CECT) images which is termed as the cluster of vessels sign. Early bifurcation of the pulmonary artery with small caliber main branches appears as a V shape on coronal and an inverted V shape on axial CECT images.

The pulmonary arteries are especially prone to narrowing. Involvement of the neck and intracranial vessels is often seen. Intracranial branches of the internal cerebral artery can show multiple aneurysms. Kinking is seen in visceral arteries (the celiac and superior mesentery arteries) as well. Excessive tortuosity of the femoral artery can pose difficulties for femoral access. Similarly, there could be difficulties with venous access, vessel rupture, vascular occlusion, pseudo-aneurysm formation, and contrast extravasations.

Patient presentation can vary depending upon organ of involvement. A dysmorphic face, skin and joint laxity, and cardiovascular manifestations are common presentations. Atypical presentations like tracheobronchial compression have also been reported [6].

There are many genetic syndromes associated with arterial tortuosity. The list includes Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, aneurysms osteoarthritis syndrome, autosomal recessive cutis laxa type 1 and Menkes disease. Less likely disorders include autosomal dominant cutis laxa due to elastin mutations and X-linked periventricular nodular heterotopias—related to FLNA mutations [7]. Arterial tortuosity is also seen in non-syndromic forms of thoracic aortic aneurysm caused by PRKG1 mutations and in Turner syndrome. The typical phenotypic appearances, molecular studies, ultra-structural analysis of skin, gene analyses, analysis of the collagens produced in cultured fibroblasts, and quantification of the plasma levels of copper and ceruloplasmin may help to differentiate between these different disease forms.

The role of radiological imaging in ATS is to ascertain diagnosis and detect complications. Follow-up imaging is necessary in these patients to avoid life threatening complications. Radiographs are helpful in identifying bony deformities (pectus excavatum, pectus carinatum, vertebral defects), diaphragmatic eventration, arachnodactyly, and kyphoscoliosis, although subtle things like skin folds that are apparent as fine lines can be seen.

Ultrasound can demonstrate tortuosity of neck and peripheral vessels. Echocardiography is good for cardiac evaluation. CT angiography or MR angiography are primary diagnostic tools. Regular clinical and radiological follow up of the patients is advised to avoid life threatening complications. This should include electrocardiograms, CECT thorax abdomen, and MRI brain with angiography sequences as per the clinical scenario.

Conclusion

ATS is a rare connective tissue disorder characterized by tortuosity, dilation, aneurysm, and stenosis of large and medium sized arteries. The role of radiological imaging is to make a timely diagnosis and detect complications, if any. Radiologists must be familiar with the typical imaging features of ATS for early and confident diagnosis.
