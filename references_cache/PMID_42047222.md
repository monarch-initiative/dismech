# Adults With Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency Have Sustained Improvements in Clinical Outcomes With up to 5 Years of Olipudase Alfa Enzyme Replacement Therapy: ASCEND Trial Final Results.
**Authors:** Wasserstein MP, Hollak CE, Barbato A, Gallagher RC, Giugliani R, Guelbert NB, Hennermann JB, Ikezoe T, Lachmann R, Lidove O, Mabe P, Mengel E, Scarpa M, Senates E, Tchan M, Thurberg BL, Villarrubia J, Armstrong NM, Bhushan S, Kim Y, Kumar M
**Journal:** J Inherit Metab Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jimd.70192](https://doi.org/10.1002/jimd.70192)

## Content

Acid sphingomyelinase deficiency (ASMD) is a rare debilitating lysosomal storage disease resulting in multisystemic disease manifestations, significant disease burden, and early mortality for some individuals. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with olipudase alfa (Xenpozyme) is the first disease-specific treatment indicated for noncentral nervous system manifestations of ASMD in children and adults. During the 1-year primary analysis of the ASCEND placebo-controlled trial in 36 adults with ASMD, olipudase alfa treatment reduced sphingomyelin storage and was associated with clinically significant improvements relative to placebo in multiple endpoints. An open-label extension of the ASCEND trial followed 35 of 36 adults during olipudase alfa treatment for up to 5 years. Mean time on olipudase alfa was 4.2 ± 1.0 years; mean compliance was 90% ± 13%. During long-term olipudase alfa treatment, percent predicted diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) increased (mean 50.1% ± 10.8% at baseline vs. 66.5% ± 13.3% at final assessment; mean change from baseline of 35.9% ± 27.5% (p < 0.0001). Mean baseline spleen volume of 11.5 ± 4.6 multiples of normal (MN) decreased to 4.8 ± 2.1 MN at final assessment, mean change from baseline -57.5% ± 10.1% (p < 0.0001) and mean baseline liver volume (1.5 ± 0.4 MN) decreased to 0.95 ± 0.23 MN at final assessment, (mean change from baseline -36.8% ± 11.5%, p < 0.0001). Plasma lyso-sphingomyelin levels decreased by 72% from baseline to final assessment. Overall, improvements in clinical parameters occurred regardless of baseline severity. No new safety issues emerged during the trial extension and 98% of treatment emergent adverse events were mild/moderate. Improvements in visceral ASMD disease with olipudase alfa treatment will significantly impact the disease burden for those with this progressive multiorgan disorder.

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with olipudase alfa (Xenpozyme) is the first disease‐specific treatment indicated for noncentral nervous system (CNS) manifestations of acid sphingomyelinase deficiency (ASMD) in children and adults. Pathogenic mutations in the SMPD1 gene result in deficient activity of acid sphingomyelinase (ASM), an enzyme whose primary substrate is sphingomyelin. In ASMD, sphingomyelin accumulates in a variety of cells including macrophages, hepatocytes, and, in the most severe form, neurons [1, 2]. This results in clinical manifestations that include splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, interstitial lung disease (ILD), hepatopathy including liver dysfunction and progressive fibrosis, thrombocytopenia, increased bleeding, dyslipidemia, growth deficits and delayed puberty, osteoporosis/osteopenia, and, in the most severe subtype, neurodegeneration [3, 4]. The disease burden in chronic forms of ASMD is significant [5, 6, 7, 8, 9] and early mortality occurs in both children and adults depending on symptom severity (median survival age 21 years and 57 years, respectively) [8, 9, 10, 11].

Three subtypes of ASMD based on age of onset, rate of progression, and extent of neurologic involvement delineate the ASMD phenotypic spectrum. ASMD Type A (OMIM257200) describes the infantile neurovisceral phenotype (historically known as Niemann‐Pick disease Type A [NPD A]) that is the most severe phenotype characterized by rapidly progressive visceral and neurologic disease and death within the first 3 years of life [3]. ASMD Type B [chronic visceral ASMD; NPD B; ASMD (OMIM607616)] follows a protracted, multisystemic course with onset in childhood, minimal, or absent neurodegeneration [9]. Intermediate ASMD Type A/B, (chronic neurovisceral ASMD; NPD A/B) shares similarities with ASMD Type B but has earlier onset and slowly progressing or static neurological symptoms. ASMD Types A/B and B, referred to jointly as chronic ASMD, are associated with significant morbidity and disease burden [5, 6, 7, 8, 9] as well as early mortality primarily due to respiratory or liver failure [9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

ERT with olipudase alfa reduces sphingomyelin accumulation and improves multisystemic clinical manifestations in children and adults with chronic ASMD [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Olipudase alfa is well tolerated [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20] using a within‐patient dose escalation regimen designed to gradually debulk tissue sphingomyelin [21, 22]. During the 1‐year primary analysis of the ASCEND placebo‐controlled trial in 36 adults with ASMD, olipudase alfa treatment reduced sphingomyelin storage and was associated with clinically significant improvements relative to placebo in percent predicted DLCO, hepatosplenomegaly, plasma transaminase concentrations, and atherogenic plasma lipid profile [18]. An open‐label extension of the ASCEND trial followed 35 of 36 adults during olipudase alfa treatment. This paper reports the safety and clinical outcomes data for this adult population who received long‐term olipudase alfa infusions for up to 5 years.

Thirty‐five of 36 adults from Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Chile, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Spain, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States enrolled in the placebo‐controlled primary analysis 1‐year period of the ASCEND trial (NCT02004691; EudraCT 2015–000371–26) [18] continued in the open‐label extension phase. Eligible adults with ASMD were required to have DLCO ≤ 70% of predicted normal value and spleen volume ≥ 6 multiples of normal (MN). ASMD Types B and A/B were not differentiated. Exclusion criteria were platelet counts < 60 × 109/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) > 250 IU/L, or total bilirubin > 1.5 mg/dL at screening (except for patients with Gilbert syndrome). Institutional Review Boards at study sites approved the protocol and participants provided written consent.

Olipudase alfa was intravenously infused once every 2 weeks with a target maintenance dose of 3 mg/kg. During the primary analysis period, those randomly assigned to the olipudase alfa group continued in the open‐label extension at the olipudase alfa maintenance dose. Adults who crossed over to olipudase alfa from placebo underwent an olipudase alfa dose escalation scheme over approximately 14 weeks, as previously described [18]. Olipudase alfa infusions were administered either at clinical sites or at home. Home infusion was initiated due to the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Liver and spleen volumes were determined from abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evaluated centrally by a medical imaging core laboratory, blinded to participant and time point, and expressed as MN, where normal liver and spleen volumes were assumed to be 2.5% and 0.2% of body weight, respectively [23]. Severe and moderate organomegaly were defined as > 2.5 and > 1.25 to ≤ 2.5MN, respectively, for hepatomegaly and > 15 and > 5 to ≤ 15MN, respectively, for splenomegaly [23].

Transaminase plasma concentrations were measured with an upper limit of normal (ULN) of 36 IU/L for ALT and 34 IU/L for AST. Platelet counts were monitored, and thrombocytopenia was defined as a platelet count threshold of < 150 × 109/L. Fasting plasma lipid profiles assessed dyslipidemia over time.

Percent predicted DLCO adjusted for hemoglobin and barometric pressure was calculated using standardized formulas [24, 25]. Percent predicted DLCO values > 80% were considered normal, > 60%–80% mild impairment, 40%–60% moderate impairment, and < 40% severe impairment [26, 27]. Additional pulmonary function tests included forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), and total lung capacity (TLC) (normal ranges 80%–120% of the predicted value for age, sex, and height). ILD scores for ground glass opacification (GGO) were determined from high resolution computed tomography (HRCT) based on grading of 3 = severe with 51%–100% lung volume affected, 2 = moderate, 1 = mild, 0 = none [28].

Disease biomarkers included lyso‐sphingomyelin (ULN 10 μg/L), the deacylated form of sphingomyelin, and plasma chitotriosidase activity (ULN 120 μmol/L/h), a biomarker of storage macrophages. Lyso‐sphingomyelin levels were measured by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS), and chitotriosidase activity as previously described [18]. Chitotriosidase activity was normalized based on CHIT1 genotype classification, where values were doubled for individuals heterozygous for the 24 bp dup CHIT1 mutation with one functional allele (eight individuals, 22%), while those with a homozygous mutation/no functional allele were not included (five individuals, 14%).

Bone marrow burden (BMB) was determined from lumbar spine and bilateral femur MRIs using semiquantitative image analysis of the degree of bone marrow infiltration by lipid‐loaded cells [29]. The BMB scoring system used a categorical score out of a total of 16 (8 points for the spine and 8 for femur) with higher scores indicating more severe disease. Dual energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA) was used for bone mineral density (BMD) measurements including total density and T‐ and Z‐score measurements (comparisons to healthy adults and age‐matched controls, respectively) [30, 31]. Images for BMB and BMD assessments were evaluated centrally by a medical imaging core laboratory blinded to participant and time point. C‐telopeptide, a biomarker for bone resorption, and bone‐specific alkaline phosphatase (associated with macrophage proliferation and a marker of bone formation) were measured in serum.

Safety and tolerability assessments included physical examinations, cardiac evaluations, using standard 12‐lead ECGs and standard 2‐dimensional and M‐mode Doppler echocardiography, standard clinical laboratory testing, safety biomarker plasma levels (C‐reactive protein, ceramide, troponin I, iron, ferritin, calcitonin, and Interleukin‐8), and reporting of adverse events including infusion‐associated reactions (IARs). IARs indicate an inflammatory response characterized by symptoms, that is, pyrexia/nausea/vomiting/fatigue/pain and associated with increases in pro‐inflammatory laboratory values, that is, high sensitivity C‐reactive protein or ferritin. Immunogenicity was assessed using a validated enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay to screen for anti‐drug antibodies (ADA) and to determine titers if present. As previously described [18], IgG anti‐drug antibody‐positive samples were assayed for neutralizing activity.

Following the placebo‐controlled 52‐week primary analysis period, 35 adults continued in the open‐label trial extension, including 17 of 18 who crossed over to olipudase alfa from placebo and all 18 adults from the original olipudase alfa group (Figure 1). Twenty‐nine adults completed the trial. The six discontinuations included three withdrawn consent or participant decision, one pregnancy, one adverse event of rash considered treatment‐related, and one discontinuation due to COVID‐19 travel restrictions. Mean cumulative olipudase alfa exposure was 4.2 ± 1.0 years.

Patient disposition.

Baseline characteristics for the placebo‐controlled portion of the study have been previously described [18]. Characteristics for the combined population reflecting baseline values for all individuals immediately before receiving olipudase alfa are summarized in Table S1.

Thirty‐five of 36 adults maintained the 3 mg/kg target dose of olipudase alfa, with one participant maintaining a maximum dose of 2 mg/kg due to multiple missed infusions resulting from adverse events unrelated to study drug. Mean compliance with treatment was 90.2% ± 12.9% and ranged from 34% to 100%. Twenty‐one participants missed one or more assessments due to travel restrictions, laboratory closures, or infections related to COVID‐19. The mean ± SD treatment compliance during the extension period for those not impacted and those impacted by the COVID‐19 pandemic was 94.4% ± 6.1% and 87.8% ± 15.2%, respectively. Home infusion was initiated in 18 participants due to the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Fourteen participants had treatment interruptions consisting of three or more consecutive missed infusions, with a mean length of the first treatment interruption of 3.8 ± 2.6 months. Eleven had one treatment interruption and three had more than 1. A swimmer plot showing the time frame of treatment interruptions and dose reduction is shown in Figure S1. In this small sample size, 7–25 consecutive missed infusions resulted in clinically meaningful, worsening impacts on pulmonary function or organ volume for six participants with contributing factors that included nonconsecutive missed infusions, reductions in dose, and/or respiratory infection. Summaries of these six cases are provided in Supporting Information.

Lyso‐sphingomyelin level was 437 ± 199 μg/L at baseline (40× the ULN of 10 μg/L) and decreased to 119 ± 115 μg/L at the final assessment representing a −72% change from baseline (nominal p < 0.0001) (n = 32, mean time on olipudase alfa 4.4 ± 0.6 years). As previously reported, decreases occurred by 6 months and were maintained or further improved at 2 years of treatment [18, 32].

Plasma chitotriosidase activity normalized based on the participant's CHIT1 genotype was 981 ± 548 μmol/L/h at baseline, (6× the ULN of 120 μmol/L/h) and decreased to 196 ± 172 μmol/L/h at the final assessment representing a −77% change from baseline (nominal p < 0.0001) (n = 27, mean time on olipudase alfa 4.5 ± 0.6 years).

Treatment emergent adverse events occurring during the first 2 years of treatment have been described in detail previously [18, 32], and no new safety issues arose in the remaining treatment period. Overall, all participants experienced at least one treatment emergent adverse event, the majority of which were mild or moderate in severity (1070/1088, 98%). Olipudase alfa was well tolerated in participants receiving infusions either in the home or in the clinical setting. Overall, there were 113 treatment emergent adverse events in 15 of 18 participants receiving at‐home infusion (83.3%) compared to 708 in 35 participants (100%) receiving infusion at site. The majority of events were mild to moderate in both settings. Severe events were reported in 6 (17.1%) participants while receiving infusions at site and 1 (5.6%) participant while receiving infusions at home (COVID‐19 infection). Among treatment emergent adverse events, 219 (20%) were considered potentially related to olipudase alfa treatment. Among 23 serious events reported for 13 participants, one serious event of extrasystoles in an individual with previously documented cardiomyopathy was considered possibly treatment‐related and was previously reported [18, 32]. None of the other serious events were considered related to study treatment.

As reported during the primary safety period [19], the most common treatment emergent adverse events included urticaria, nausea, and headache, most of which were mild IARs, nasopharyngitis, and upper respiratory tract infection (Table 3). There were no clinically significant adverse changes in vital signs, neurological examinations, hematology, or cardiac safety parameters. No cases of acute phase reactions or cytokine release syndrome were reported during the study.

There were two permanent discontinuations: one for pregnancy (the individual had an uneventful pregnancy and delivered a healthy baby) and one for an adverse event of mild intensity reported as rash after 156 weeks of olipudase alfa treatment. The event was considered an IAR, and treatment included IV methylprednisolone. After discontinuation, the rash resolved on the same day. No deaths were reported during the study.

Fifteen of 35 individuals (42.9%) remained ADA negative during olipudase alfa treatment. Among 16 (45.7%) with treatment emergent ADA, no individual developed neutralizing anti‐drug IgG antibodies that interfered with ASM enzyme cellular uptake during olipudase alfa treatment.

Olipudase alfa represents the first disease‐modifying treatment for the nonCNS manifestations of ASMD in children and adults. The primary analysis period of the ASCEND clinical trial assessed olipudase alfa versus placebo in adult patients with chronic ASMD for 1 year, and showed that olipudase alfa cleared tissue sphingomyelin levels, reduced plasma levels of lyso‐sphingomyelin, and improved multiple ASMD clinical manifestations [18]. Individuals who crossed over from the placebo group and received 1 year of olipudase alfa treatment in the extension study had improvements in liver, spleen, and lung endpoints comparable to those who received olipudase alfa for 1 year during the primary analysis period, while olipudase alfa treatment for 2 years showed sustained or continued improvement in clinical outcomes [32]. The present final analysis of participants in the ASCEND trial receiving up to 5 years of olipudase alfa treatment shows durable benefits in key clinical parameters for adults with ASMD. Overall, even with variability of individual responses, improvements occurred across clinical domains irrespective of baseline severity. Clinical improvements in disease manifestations following olipudase alfa treatment were consistent with those previously reported in adults and children with chronic ASMD [15, 20]. Long‐term safety data showed that treatment with olipudase alfa was well tolerated, and no new safety concerns emerged. Importantly, prolonged treatment interruptions can potentially impact clinical outcomes, particularly lung diffusing capacity and organ volumes, and it is therefore important to maintain treatment regimen. Home infusion with olipudase alfa, which was initiated during the COVID‐19 pandemic, was well tolerated and appears to be an option for individuals with ASMD.

As shown by natural history study data for untreated adults with ASMD, multiorgan manifestations may remain stable [33] or worsen over time [6, 8], necessitating individual assessment of patients and their eligibility for treatment. Lung disease and respiratory infections, and hepatopathy including cirrhosis and liver failure may worsen with age among those with chronic ASMD [4] and are significant contributors to mortality [8, 13]. Olipudase alfa addressed multiple clinical manifestations impacting the burden of disease in individuals with chronic ASMD. With respect to lung involvement, olipudase alfa infusions for up to 5 years were associated with continued improvement in lung diffusing capacity and stability of lung volumetric tests. In some cases, improvements in lung diffusing capacity were subsequently attenuated and apparently impacted by multiple missed olipudase alfa infusions or dose reduction. Olipudase alfa improvements in lung function tests were accompanied by resolution of “ground glass” opacities among individuals with scores > 0 at baseline, indicating elimination of cellular infiltrates [34]. Considering the effects on the liver, olipudase alfa treatment resulted in almost complete clearance of sphingomyelin storage from the liver after 2 years concomitantly with normalization of liver volume and transaminase levels [32]. Importantly, spleen volumes normalized following olipudase alfa treatment. Platelet counts improved with olipudase alfa treatment indicating correction of secondary hypersplenism that contributes to thrombocytopenia [35]. Dyslipidemia, which can worsen over time in individuals with chronic ASMD [4, 8], improved with continued olipudase alfa treatment, particularly for levels of anti‐atherogenic lipids, consistent with results reported previously [36].

Skeletal complications are common in chronic ASMD and adults with ASMD show osteopenia or osteoporosis at one or more sites and decreased BMD compared to a control population [37]. In a previous study in five adults with ASMD, improvements in BMD particularly in the spine were noted in some individuals receiving olipudase alfa over 5 years [16, 20]. In adults in the present study, spine and femur Z‐ and T‐scores improved or remained stable with olipudase alfa treatment, with some individual scores improving from low BMD or osteopenia to normal values.

Mean BMB score was moderate at baseline, similar to scores reported in Type 1 Gaucher disease (GD1). Olipudase alfa reduced the mean BMB score, potentially reflecting decreased storage of sphingomyelin. In a previous study of five adults with ASMD, individuals with the highest BMB scores had changes in bone marrow in the femur and spine after treatment with olipudase alfa for 30 months [16]. In the present study, there was variability in BMB score improvement. A recent retrospective analysis looking at BMB scores in GD1 found no change with treatment [38]. Since various conditions other than the underlying LSD can impact the fat signal during BMB scans, consistent longitudinal analyses are required for accurate interpretation [39].

The individuals with ASMD included in the ASCEND study represent a global population of affected individuals spanning ages from younger to older adults. A limitation of the study is lack of demonstration of impact on quality of life (QOL). In the placebo‐controlled 1 year trial period, improvements in QOL and PRO measures were not different in the placebo and olipudase alfa‐treated groups [18], and it has been suggested that adults with ASMD may adjust to the limitations of their chronic disease [33]. A recent study of the natural disease course of chronic visceral ASMD in adults with relatively mild disease found that patient‐reported QOL did not differ significantly from that of a healthy population, although scores were impacted by severity of pulmonary involvement [33]. While the individuals with ASMD included in the ASCEND study do not reflect the entire population with ASMD, they may be similar to the adult patients discussed above. In a pediatric cohort with chronic ASMD receiving olipudase alfa for at least 12 months, physical, emotional, and mental well‐being of affected children and caregivers was improved by olipudase alfa treatment [40].

In summary, this long‐term extension study of olipudase alfa for up to 5 years in adults with ASMD demonstrates that olipudase alfa infusions remain well tolerated and effective. Many of the clinical features of ASMD that normalized after 2 years of treatment were stable or showed additional improvements with continued treatment. The improvements in visceral ASMD disease with olipudase alfa treatment will significantly impact the disease burden for those with this progressive multiorgan disorder.
