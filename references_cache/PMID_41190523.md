# Persistent Leukoencephalopathy Following H1N1 Infection Associated With a Novel MYRF Variant (p.Gly735Asp).
**Authors:** Hu J, Huang L, Zhu W, Peng M, Gong Q, Qian J
**Journal:** Ann Clin Transl Neurol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/acn3.70242](https://doi.org/10.1002/acn3.70242)

## Content

Mutations in myelin regulatory factor (MYRF) are linked to demyelinating disorders. We report a 38-year-old male who developed acute symmetric leukoencephalopathy mimicking a stroke following an influenza A virus infection. While clinical symptoms markedly improved with corticosteroids, MRI revealed persistent white matter lesions, contrasting with the known phenotype of MYRF-related mild encephalopathy with reversible myelin vacuolization (MMERV). Genetic analysis identified a novel MYRF variant (c.2204G>A, p.Gly735Asp) in its C-terminal domain, with familial cosegregation confirming autosomal dominant inheritance. This case demonstrates that viral infections can trigger severe MYRF-related pathology and establishes persistent leukoencephalopathy as a novel clinical phenotype within the MYRF disease spectrum.

Myelin regulatory factor (MYRF) is a key transcription factor for myelination in the central nervous system (CNS) and oligodendrocyte differentiation [1, 2]. Its C‐terminal domain mediates endoplasmic reticulum localization and self‐cleavage, releasing the N‐terminal fragment into the nucleus to activate myelin‐related genes [3, 4, 5]. Currently, mutations in MYRF can not only cause developmental malformations in the eyes, heart, and urogenital system but may also lead to MYRF‐related mild encephalopathy with reversible myelin vacuolization (MMERV) [5, 6, 7, 8], often triggered by infections, manifesting as transient leukoencephalopathy and neurological deficits. MRI typically shows bilateral corpus callosum and white matter lesions. The clinical reversibility likely reflects developmental CNS plasticity, particularly in early childhood, where partial myelin repair remains possible [6, 9].

The role of viral infections as triggers for CNS pathology is well established, often by unmasking an underlying genetic susceptibility [10, 11]. This is particularly critical in the context of myelin integrity, which relies on the lifelong, precise regulation by factors like MYRF. In individuals with a pre‐existing, subclinical genetic vulnerability such as MYRF haploinsufficiency, the robust systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation following a viral infection (e.g., cytokine storms seen in COVID‐19 or influenza) can act as a “second hit” [8]. This may disrupt the blood–brain barrier, promote neuroinflammation, and overwhelm the compromised myelin maintenance or repair capacity, thereby precipitating acute neurological disease [10, 12]. This study, therefore, provides compelling evidence for this “viral trigger” hypothesis in MYRF‐related pathogenesis and proposes that persistent white matter lesions may represent a novel clinical phenotype in carriers of specific MYRF mutations.

A 38‐year‐old male was admitted with a 10‐day history of fever (peak 39.8°C), cough, and dyspnea. Physical examination revealed tachycardia (126 bpm), tachypnea (29 bpm), hypertension (162/120 mmHg), and a positive nucleic acid test for influenza A virus. Laboratory findings included NT‐proBNP 16,088 pg/mL and troponin 0.096 ng/mL, indicating cardiac overload; CRP 42.1 mg/L, IL‐6 96 ng/L, and neutrophilia (82.6%) suggesting systemic inflammation. Imaging showed bilateral pneumonia and cardiomegaly with pulmonary hypertension. Oseltamivir, moxifloxacin, antihypertensives, and cardiac support improved respiratory and circulatory function.

On the fifth day of hospitalization, the patient suddenly developed right upper limb weakness (muscle strength 4/5), dysarthria, and cognitive decline. Head CT revealed hyperdense lesions in the basal ganglia and cerebellum (Figure 1A[a,b]); MRI showed symmetrically restricted diffusion in the bilateral centrum semiovale (DWI hyperintensity, ADC hypointensity), leading to a preliminary diagnosis of stroke, and treatment with aspirin and statins was initiated (Figure 1A[c–h]). After remote consultation, the patient was transferred to our hospital. Physical examination revealed right upper limb muscle weakness (4/5), retrograde amnesia, recent memory impairment, and decreased calculation ability (mRS = 2; MMSE could not be completed due to motor dysfunction). Laboratory tests showed leukocytosis (19.45 × 109/L), elevated neutrophils (92%), decreased NT‐proBNP (885.3 pg/mL), and elevated parathyroid hormone (10.06 pmol/L, normal range 1.59–7.24 pmol/L). MRI displayed hyperintense signals in the bilateral cerebellum, basal ganglia, thalamus, and periventricular white matter (Fazekas = 1), with restricted diffusion in the centrum semiovale and enhancing lesions. MRA, MRV, and SWI were all normal (Figure 1B[a–d]). Next‐generation sequencing of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was negative (including H1N1), and CSF analysis was normal (pressure 95 mmH2O, white blood cells 6 × 106/L, protein 318 mg/L).

After excluding metabolic, vascular, and neoplastic causes, immune‐mediated encephalitis was suspected, and the patient received methylprednisolone pulse therapy (500 mg/day). After 7 days, cognition and muscle strength recovered (MRC 5/5), but fine motor skills remained impaired (9‐hole peg test 26 s, normal < 18 s). Whole‐exome sequencing revealed a novel MYRF variant (NM_001127392.2: c.2204G>A, p.Gly735Asp). Genetic testing revealed that his asymptomatic mother (I‐2, age 65), sister (II‐2, age 42), and niece (III‐1, age 12) also carried the p.Gly735Asp variant. The proband's father (I‐1, age 68) was confirmed to be wild‐type. The sister and niece are currently in good health with no reported neurological symptoms or history of significant infections. A full neurological examination and brain MRI were not performed on them. This familial cosegregation confirms an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern (Figure 2A,C).

In this study, we describe a novel MYRF variant (p.Gly735Asp) associated with a unique phenotype of virus‐triggered, nonreversible leukoencephalopathy. This finding is significant as it expands the known clinical spectrum of MYRF‐related disorders. Pathogenic MYRF variants are typically clustered in two critical domains: the N‐terminal DNA‐binding domain (DBD), where mutations impair transcriptional activity, and the C‐terminal intramolecular chaperone auto‐processing (ICA) domain, where they can disrupt autoproteolytic cleavage [2, 4]. The p.Gly735Asp variant is located in a relatively variable genetic region (e.g., near natural variants like VAR_081886, VAR_081887, and VAR_038907), some of which have been associated with cardio‐urogenital syndrome (CUGS), characterized by genital anomalies and congenital heart defects [13]. CUGS may arise from pathogenic MYRF variants [14], with certain mutations linked to cardiac disease [3], and recent exome sequencing identifying MYRF as a cause [15]. However, in our case, the variant's position in the C‐terminal domain likely preferentially affects adult myelin maintenance rather than developmental anomalies [6], distinguishing it from the pathogenesis of CUGS. The pathogenicity of the p.Gly735Asp variant is strongly supported by a confluence of evidence. Its novelty is established by its absence from large population databases (e.g., gnomAD), indicating it is not a common benign polymorphism. Critically, the variant resides within the highly conserved intramolecular chaperone auto‐processing (ICA)/peptidase S74 domain, a region essential for the protein's activation. The nonconservative amino acid substitution—replacing a small, neutral glycine with a larger, charged aspartic acid—is predicted by computational simulations to disrupt the local protein structure. Compelling genetic evidence for its pathogenic role is provided by its perfect cosegregation with the clinical or subclinical phenotype across three generations of the family. Finally, the resulting leukoencephalopathy phenotype is highly consistent with the protein's known biological function in myelin regulation, providing strong biological plausibility. Crucially, the few previously reported MYRF mutations linked to CNS disease, such as the p.Gln403Arg variant in the DBD, cause a distinct syndrome known as MYRF‐related mild encephalopathy with reversible myelin vacuolization (MMERV), characterized by transient, fully reversible white matter lesions following infection [6, 7, 8]. Our case, with its ICA domain mutation and persistent white matter damage, presents a stark contrast and supports a “two‐hit” model, where a viral infection acts as a physiological stressor that unmasks an underlying, and in this case more severe, genetic susceptibility, with the variant's effects manifesting through genetic vulnerability plus viral trigger [8], thereby clarifying its distinction from CUGS to avoid confusion.

The radiological findings in our patient present both similarities and critical differences when compared to classic, reversible MMERV cases. Similar to MMERV, our patient's acute MRI showed symmetric, bilateral white matter lesions with restricted diffusion, suggesting cytotoxic edema [6, 7]. However, the distribution of lesions diverged significantly. While MMERV typically involves the splenium of the corpus callosum and deep cerebral white matter, our patient's lesions were most prominent in the bilateral centrum semiovale, thalamus, and basal ganglia, with minimal callosal involvement. Most importantly, follow‐up imaging revealed the irreversible nature of these lesions, which evolved into chronic microstructural damage (as indicated by increased ADC signal), a stark contrast to the complete radiological resolution that defines MMERV. This persistent damage strongly supports our conclusion that this case represents a more severe, nonreversible phenotype on the MYRF‐related disease spectrum. We conducted a detailed comparison of published MYRF‐related encephalopathy cases with the present case (Table 1).

An additional finding of note was the presence of intracranial calcifications in the basal ganglia and cerebellum, which correlated with the patient's mildly elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels. Hyperparathyroidism is a recognized cause of dysregulated calcium‐phosphate metabolism leading to ectopic calcification, and such findings are often associated with conditions like Fahr's disease. While this metabolic abnormality likely explains the chronic calcifications seen on CT, it is considered a distinct comorbidity and likely independent of the acute H1N1‐triggered demyelinating event. Acknowledging this concurrent pathology is crucial, as it highlights the clinical complexity and the need to differentiate chronic, stable findings from the acute, dynamic lesions central to the patient's neurological decline.

The CSF analysis in this case was notably normal, showing no viral‐genetic material, pleocytosis, elevated protein, or increased intracranial pressure on the day neurological symptoms emerged, which might suggest the absence of direct CNS inflammation. We acknowledge this observation as potentially puzzling; however, it aligns with the known phenotype of MYRF‐related disorders, such as MMERV, where CSF findings are often mild or normal, without overt inflammatory evidence [6]. This may reflect the viral infection acting as a “second hit” to unmask underlying genetic susceptibility rather than causing direct CNS infection [11]. In our patient, symptoms likely arose from virus‐induced systemic inflammation (e.g., cytokine storm) exacerbating the MYRF variant's impact on myelin maintenance, rather than direct viral invasion of the CNS [8].

The core of our patient's pathology lies in the irreversible nature of the white matter lesions. We propose this critical phenotypic difference stems from the specific location of the p.Gly735Asp variant. This mutation is situated within the C‐terminal intramolecular chaperone auto‐processing (ICA) domain, which is essential for MYRF's proteolytic self‐cleavage. This cleavage is a prerequisite for the N‐terminal fragment to form a functional homo‐trimer, translocate to the nucleus, and activate the transcription of myelin genes [2, 4].

We hypothesize that the p.Gly735Asp variant may specifically impair MYRF's maintenance function rather than its role in developmental myelination. This hypothesis is strongly supported by studies in adult mice, where inducible ablation of Myrf in mature oligodendrocytes leads to a rapid downregulation of myelin gene expression followed by severe, progressive demyelination, demonstrating its critical ongoing role in myelin maintenance [9]. In an adult patient like ours, whose developmental myelination is complete, MYRF's primary role shifts to the lifelong maintenance and repair of myelin sheaths [3, 9]. The severe systemic inflammation from the influenza infection likely acted as a profound “stress test,” overwhelming a system with a pre‐existing, compromised maintenance capacity. This led not to the transient cytotoxic edema seen in MMERV [6], but to irreversible myelin damage or oligodendrocyte loss, resulting in the observed persistent lesions.

The familial analysis provides further crucial insights. The asymptomatic mother also carried the variant. Her brain MRI showed bilateral periventricular white matter hyperintensities (Fazekas = 2), a pattern resembling cerebral small vessel disease. However, given her lack of relevant vascular risk factors, these findings are more likely a chronic, subclinical manifestation of the MYRF variant itself. This intrafamilial phenotypic variability is a hallmark of MYRF‐related disorders, which exhibit a wide spectrum ranging from severe, often lethal, congenital anomalies (cardiac‐urogenital‐diaphragm‐lung syndrome) [16] to the recurrent but reversible encephalopathy of MMERV [6], and the subclinical presentation seen in our patient's mother. Such incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity may be attributed to a combination of factors, including age‐related vulnerability, the nature and severity of environmental triggers (e.g., different viral infections), the influence of genetic modifiers, or the specific functional consequence of the mutation (e.g., haploinsufficiency vs. dominant‐negative effects) [2]. Following genetic counseling, the asymptomatic carriers were advised of the potential risk of developing neurological symptoms, particularly in the context of severe febrile illnesses, and were encouraged to seek prompt medical evaluation should such symptoms arise. Regular neurological follow‐up has been recommended.

A limitation of this study is the absence of systematic neurological and imaging examinations for all asymptomatic carriers, which would be invaluable for a more complete assessment of the variant's subclinical penetrance and expressivity.

Drawing a parallel to Sydenham's chorea, where anti‐streptococcal antibodies mistakenly target neuronal antigens, molecular mimicry stands as a plausible alternative mechanism in our patient [17]. This involves a blend of immune complex hypersensitivity and molecular mimicry [18], with shared epitopes between streptococcus and host leading to autoimmunity [19]. Unfortunately, through ProteinBlast analysis (Appendix S1), we found no homologous sequences after comparing the NA/HA proteins of nine influenza viruses with the C‐terminal domain of MYRF (containing the p.Gly735Asp site). Future studies could investigate T‐cell or antibody reactivity to both viral and myelin antigens in affected patients to explore this possibility further.

In conclusion, this case identifies a novel MYRF variant linked to a new phenotype of persistent leukoencephalopathy triggered by a viral infection. It highlights the critical role of MYRF's C‐terminal domain in long‐term myelin maintenance and demonstrates how environmental stressors can precipitate severe disease in genetically predisposed individuals. Clinicians should consider MYRF mutations in the differential diagnosis of adult‐onset, infection‐triggered leukoencephalopathies, particularly when imaging findings are persistent or only partially reversible, distinguishing them from the classic, fully reversible MMERV presentation [6].

We report a novel MYRF C‐terminal domain variant (c.2204G>A, p.Gly735Asp) associated with persistent, post‐H1N1 leukoencephalopathy. This phenotype of irreversible white matter damage stands in stark contrast to the transient lesions of classic MYRF‐related mild encephalopathy with reversible myelin vacuolization (MMERV). These findings illustrate a critical viral‐genetic interaction and establish persistent, infection‐triggered leukoencephalopathy as a novel and severe clinical entity within the MYRF disease spectrum.
