# Identification of Novel LOXHD1 Variants in Chinese Patients with Non-Syndromic Hearing Loss.
**Authors:** Zhang K, Xin X, Huang S, Hou B, Xu X, Qu P, Han X, Zhang H
**Journal:** J Otol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.26599/JOTO.2026.9540055](https://doi.org/10.26599/JOTO.2026.9540055)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: Although LOXHD1 mutations have been increasingly implicated in late-onset hereditary hearing loss, the genetic spectrum and underlying molecular mechanisms in the Chinese population remain inadequately characterized. This study aims to delineate the genetic landscape of LOXHD1 in Chinese families with non-syndromic hearing loss and to determine the pathogenic significance of the identified variants.

METHOD: In this study, we conducted targeted whole-exome sequencing along with Sanger sequencing on 157 probands with non-syndromic hearing loss, as well as their family members. We identified novel variants in the LOXHD1 gene. Additionally, we employed bioinformatic analyses to investigate the potential molecular functions of these variants.

RESULTS: In this study, potential causal variants were identified in 95 (60.51%) affected individuals and variants in SLC26A4 and GJB2 were most frequent in this cohort. we identified ten different variants in the LOXHD1 gene from five patients in their families. Among them, five novel LOXHD1 gene variants linked to hearing loss were recognized: c.3734G>A, c.4952T>A, c.4486C>T, c.6375G>C, and c.5002del. The pathogenicity of these variants was evaluated through multiple bioinformatics analyses. Notably, two patients with severe bilateral sensorineural hearing loss associated with LOXHD1 mutations showed significant improvement after cochlear implantation, attaining receptive and expressive language skills appropriate for their chronological age.

CONCLUSIONS: We have identified five novel variants of the LOXHD1 gene in Chinese family affected by nonsyndromic hearing loss. These findings enrich the current understanding of LOXHD1-related pathogenic variants and offer important implications for molecular diagnosis, genetic counseling, and prenatal screening in hereditary hearing loss.

Hearing loss (HL) represents the most prevalent sensory impairment in humans and can significantly compromise an individual’s quality of life (Fink, 2021). Globally, an estimated 466 million people are currently affected by auditory dysfunction, and this figure is projected to increase to approximately 900 million by the year 2050 (Li et al., 2021). Hearing loss is the fourth most common cause of disability in the world, and it places a heavy financial burden on individuals, families, and society. About 50–60% of hearing loss is caused by genetic factors, and more than 200 genes have been found to contribute to hearing loss (Cheng et al., 2025). The use of next-generation sequencing has accelerated the identification of pathogenic/likely-pathogenic variants in hearing loss susceptibility genes, leading to improved detection and early treatment of childhood hearing loss (Imizcoz et al., 2023).The LOXHD1 gene in humans is located on chromosome 18q12-q21 andencodes the lipoxygenase homology domain-containing protein 1 (Morlet et al., 2021). The LOXHD1 protein plays a crucial role in extracellular matrix assembly and tissue development. LOXHD1 encodes a highly conserved protein localized in stereocilia, characterized by 15 polycystin-1/lipoxygenase/alpha-toxin (PLAT) domains that are implicated in membrane-associated interactions (Aleem et al., 2008). This gene is expressed in various tissues, particularly in the cochlea and retina (Trouillet et al., 2021). The protein product of LOXHD1 is essential for maintaining the functionality of hair cells in the cochlea (Trouillet et al., 2021). LOXHD1 has been associated with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (DFNB77), a rare form especially in the East Asian population (Grillet et al., 2009). Although mutations in LOXHD1 have been identified, they are relatively infrequent, with approximately 60 variants reported in nonsyndromic hearing loss (NSHL) cases over the past decade (Shen et al., 2019; Morlet et al., 2021). Further research is necessary to understand the relationship between LOXHD1 variants and hearing impairment.

In recent years, the use of high-throughput DNA sequencing technology in clinical settings has led to the identification of more genetic variants associated with congenital or early childhood hearing loss. Over the past 5 years, we conducted a study involving 157 individuals with sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL). Whole-exome sequencing (WES) combined with bioinformatic analysis identified five previously unreported LOXHD1 variants in four individuals affected by severe-to-profound SNHL. This study aimed to contribute evidence for early diagnosis and intervention.

A cohort of 157 probands with non-syndromic sensorineural hearing loss, along with 292 family members for segregation analysis, was recruited by Qilu Hospital of Shandong University from 2019 to 2023 for WES and subsequent validation. The diagnosis was made based on clinical expression and adjunctive tests. Detailed medical and family histories were collected and a series of tests was performed such as audiological tests, high-resolution CT and MRI scans. Inclusion criteria included: (1) confirmed diagnosis of sensorineural hearing loss by audiological testing; (2) no known syndromic features or systemic diseases associated with hearing loss; (3) willingness to undergo genetic testing and provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of ototoxic drug exposure; (2) clear environmental causes such as severe noise exposure, trauma, or infection; (3) presence of syndromic hearing loss.

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples collected from affected individuals and their family members according to the manufacturer’s instructions for high-quality DNA isolation (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). WES was conducted by Mygenostics Medical Laboratory Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and the detailed procedures have been described previously (Fu et al., 2022). All putative variants were interpreted based on the guidelines of the ACMG and subsequently confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

The gene mutation followed the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS). The subject’s clinical information, population (1000 Genomes, ESP6500, ExAC, and gnomAD), and disease (OMIM, ClinVar and HGMD) databases were referred to for variant annotation and screening. The pathogenicity of variants was evaluated following the ACMG guidelines. Hearing loss associated mutations were validated by Sanger sequencing. SeqMan software (Version Ultra, US) was used for LOXHD1 gene sequence alignment and analysis.

Missense mutations were predicted by SIFT (http://sift.jcvi.org/) Polyphen2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/) and REVEL (https://sites.google.com/site/revelgenomics/), splicing sites by SpliceAI (https://spliceailookup.broadinstitute.org/) and dbscSNV (http://database.liulab.science/dbNSFP). The HomoloGene system of NCBI (https:// www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/) and uniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) was used to determine the amino acid conservation of novel variants in different species. LOXHD1 sequences, including human and other species, were aligned by CLUSTALX (http://www.clustal.org/). Wild-type and mutated LOXHD1 protein were modeled by SWISS MODEL (https://www.swissmodel.expasy.org/) and Pymol software (Version2.6, DeLano Scientific LLC, US) and further generated and rendered for the 3D structures of LOXHD1 protein. Protein hydrophobicity detection using ProtScale program (https://web.expasy.org/protscale/).

Over the past five years, WES was performed on 157 individuals diagnosed with SNHL. Likely pathogenic variants were identified in 95 of these cases, accounting for 60.51% of the cohort. About 75 were male and 82 were female (ratio 1:1.09). Among the 157 affected individuals, SLC26A4 variants were identified in 29 cases (18.47%), representing the most commonly implicated gene in the cohort. The GJB2 gene was the second most frequently affected, with variants detected in 15.28% of individuals. 5.09% of individuals were found to have LOXHD1 gene mutations. In addition, 41 additional mutated genes were detected in our cohort, including MYO15A (5.73%), MYO7A (5.09%), OTOF (3.82%), TMC1 (3.18%), PCDH15 (2.54%), MYH14 (2.54%), among others. In this study, ten distinct LOXHD1 variants were identified in five probands and their respective families through exome sequencing, with all variants subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing. Among the ten variants, six were non-truncating and four were truncating. Nine of these variants were located within PLAT domains, while one was a splice-site variant. Among these, five variants—c.3734G>A, c.4952T>A, c.4486C>T, c.6375G>C, and c.5002del—had not been previously reported or documented in HGMD (Figure 1 and Table 2). The hearing impairments in these four families were severe to profound SNHL. Case 1 passed the newborn hearing screening; however, delayed-onset hearing loss became apparent at age 2. An ABR test conducted at age 3 confirmed bilateral severe SNHL (80 dBnHL), and bilateral hearing aids were prescribed thereafter. However, by the age of 10, the hearing aids no longer provided sufficient benefit, and she subsequently underwent cochlear implantation.

Approximately 60% of patients exhibited delayed-onset hearing loss, and 80% experienced disease onset before the age of 5. None of the patients showed signs of vestibular or visual impairment. High-resolution CT and MRI of the temporal bone in probands of four families revealed no significant abnormalities in the middle or inner ear. Additionally, the proband had no associated symptoms, including vestibular disorders (such as dizziness or vertigo), visual disturbances (including blurred or distorted vision and eye pain), developmental delay, or intellectual disabilities.

To assess the evolutionary conservation of the five amino acid residues affected by mutations in the patients, LOXHD1 protein sequences from ten different species were obtained from NCBI and UniProt. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using CLUSTALX (https://www.clustal.org/). As illustrated in Figure 2, all four altered residues—including Gly1245, Leu1496, Leu1651, and Lys2125—are highly conserved across species, suggesting their potential functional significance.

Furthermore, the potential functional impact of these variants was assessed using several widely adopted in silico prediction tools. All identified missense variants were predicted to be deleterious or likely damaging by both SIFT and REVEL. Additionally, pathogenicity was assessed according to the ACMG guidelines (Table 3).

To investigate the potential impact of the missense variants on the structure and function of the LOXHD1 protein, 3D models of four regions corresponding to exons 24, 29, 32, and 40 of the wild-type protein were generated using SWISS-MODEL. Next, the mutant structures were then generated with PyMOL and visually analyzed in Figure 3. All five missense variants within these regions are predicted to influence the local structural environment of LOXHD1. Notably, the p.G1245D, p.K2125N, and p.L1651H variants were found to disrupt hydrogen bond formation. Although the p.L1496F variant did not alter hydrogen bonding, the replacement of charged residues may have significant physicochemical consequences. The ProtScale program analyzes the hydrophobicity of mutated amino acids in proteins. The p.G1245D and p.L1651H variations showed the most significant changes in amino acid hydrophobicity, and the results are shown in Figure 4.

In this study, we conducted exome sequencing analysis on 157 individuals with hearing loss. We found that pathogenic variants in SLC26A4 and GJB2 were the most frequently identified, which was consistent with previous reports (Babanejad et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2023). Additionally, we identified five novel variants in the LOXHD1 gene (Table 1), expanding the mutational spectrum of LOXHD1-related non-syndromic autosomal recessive hearing loss.

LOXHD1 encodes a crucial protein composed of 15 PLAT domains. These domains are important for facilitating essential protein interactions necessary for maintaining normal hair cell function (Grillet et al., 2009). Pathogenic variants in LOXHD1 can lead to a spectrum of NSHL phenotypes, including both progressive and non-progressive congenital hearing impairments (Edvardson et al., 2011). While individual PLAT domains are identified in various proteins, LOXHD1 is uniquely composed entirely of these domains (Bateman and Sandford, 1999). This protein is found in mature mechanosensory hair cells and is located along the stereociliary membrane. PLAT domains, originally recognized in polycystin-1, are evolutionarily conserved across multiple eukaryotic proteins, including lipoxygenases, Rab6-interacting protein 1, and lipoprotein lipase (Recacha et al., 2009; Trouillet et al., 2021; Dinkins et al., 2022). The presence of these domains in bacterial α-toxins and Plasmodium falciparum gametocyte proteins suggests that they are an ancient structural motif (Naylor et al., 1998; Delrieu et al., 2002). LOXHD1 stands out as the only known protein composed exclusively of PLAT domains. LOXHD1’s orthologs are highly conserved within Chordates, whose mechanosensory cells characterized by protrusions enriched in both F-actin and microtubules. In contrast, these orthologs are absent in Ecdysozoa, whose mechanosensory systems are solely based on microtubules. This distribution implies that LOXHD1 may have co-evolved with F-actin-based mechanosensory organelles (Grillet et al., 2009; Dinkins et al., 2022). Notably, during cochlear hair cell development, LOXHD1 expression becomes prominent as the kinocilium is lost, suggesting that the protein may play a role in stabilizing the stereociliary bundle in the absence of the kinocilium. This change may affect the normal conformation of the LOXHD1 protein molecule, leading to loss of activity and function, and ultimately hearing loss. In vestibular hair cells that retain their kinocilium, the expression of LOXHD1 is significantly lower, and these cells are not affected by LOXHD1 mutations (Grillet et al., 2009). In our study, no episodes of vestibular dysfunction, such as dizziness or vertigo, were reported by the affected individuals.

LOXHD1 is a gene crucial for maintaining auditory function, and mutations in LOXHD1 have been rarely reported. Since Grillet (Grillet et al., 2009) first cloned the LOXHD1 gene in 2009, a total of 100 pathogenic variants have been identified (HGMD, https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php, Feb 2, 2026.). However, the specific pathogenic mechanism is still unclear. In our study, all cases showed congenital hearing loss that was in accordance with previous reports (Bai et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2022). Out of the 10 variants identified, six were non-truncating variants and 4 were truncating variants. Nine of these variants were located within the PLAT domains, and one was a splice variant within an interval. All six point mutations were missense mutations.

In our study, we discovered five novel variants in the LOXHD1 gene in patients with hearing loss. We employed high-throughput sequencing to identify these variants in 157 affected individuals, and subsequently confirmed the variants in their family members by Sanger sequencing. We carried out comprehensive analyses including examining the family history, using whole exome sequencing, confirming co-segregation, determining the rarity of the variants in control populations, and performing bioinformatics predictions. Our findings suggest that the five variants we identified in the LOXHD1 gene may be causative factors. One of the mutations, p.R1668E fsTer27, is a frameshift mutation that leads to the substitution of arginine by glutamate and the premature termination of protein translation due to the introduction of a stop codon. This mutation not only disrupts the C-terminal structural domain of the LOXHD1 protein but may also impact the folding and stability of the entire protein. The p.L1651H and p.L1496F variants may have changed the hydrophobicity and spatial conformation of local regions of the LOXHD1 protein, potentially affecting its stability and function in the cell membrane. The p.G1245D and p.K2125N variants result in a change in protein charge, which may affect the interaction of the LOXHD1 protein with other charged molecules, leading to local conformational changes that further influence the protein’s function. Analysis of the protein structure model suggests that all five mutation sites may be responsible for hearing loss. Our bioinformatics analysis indicates that these sites are highly conserved in multiple species, highlighting their importance.

Lastly, genomic sequencing is beneficial for patients with hearing loss because it helps in preoperative assessment and predicting postoperative effects for clinical and cochlear implant purposes. For instance, patients with LOXHD1 mutations could potentially see improvements in auditory and speech function after cochlear implantation (Seligman et al., 2022). Because DFNB77 is rare, there are fewer reports on outcomes after cochlear implantation. In our study, two affected children underwent unilateral CI surgery and both showed satisfactory recovery. Patient 4 received a unilateral cochlear implant at 10 months of age, while patient 1 received one at 10 years of age. A speech language evaluation 12 months after the cochlear implants demonstrated age-appropriate receptive and expressive language skills. Audiologic testing indicated a normal PTA and a speech reception thresholds (SRT) of 31 dB HL. There are still many unknown mutations in the LOXHD1 gene that need to be explored, and the reasons for different hearing loss phenotypes caused by gene mutations still require further in-depth exploration.

In summary, we performed next-generation sequencing on 157 individuals affected by hearing loss and their family members. We identified five novel variants in the LOXHD1 gene across four unrelated families, specifically c.3734G>A, c.4952T>A, c.4486C>T, c.6375G>C, and c.5002del. These results substantially broaden the mutational spectrum associated with LOXHD1-related non-syndromic hearing loss. Two patients with profound bilateral SNHL caused by LOXHD1 mutations showed significant benefit from cochlear implantation and achieved age-appropriate receptive and expressive language development.
