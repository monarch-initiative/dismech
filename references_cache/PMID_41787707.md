# Unraveling PEX6: insights into very-long-chain fatty acid levels and peroxisome biogenesis disorders in pediatric populations.
**Authors:** Ahangari N, Shirvan BB, Ashrafzadeh F, Karimiani EG, Hashemi N, Imannezhad S, Kalat HL, Ebrahimzadeh F, Akhondian J, Toosi MB
**Journal:** Ann Pediatr Endocrinol Metab (2026)
**DOI:** [10.6065/apem.2550134.067](https://doi.org/10.6065/apem.2550134.067)

## Content

Peroxisome biogenesis disorders (PBDs) are a genetically heterogeneous group of metabolic diseases caused by impaired peroxisome assembly and function. PBDs exhibit striking clinical variability, ranging from lethal neonatal forms (e.g., Zellweger spectrum disorders) to milder childhood-onset presentations such as rhizomelic chondrodysplasia punctata. While elevated levels of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) remain a key diagnostic feature, the existence of unusual cases with normal plasma VLCFA levels highlight the limitations of relying solely on this biochemical marker for diagnosis. Genetic variations in PEX6, an important peroxisome biogenesis factor, contribute significantly to this phenotypic diversity, with missense variants often associated with less severe disease compared to truncating mutations. Recent studies further implicate dysregulated pexophagy-a targeted autophagic degradation of peroxisomes-in the underlying disease mechanisms. This review underscores the necessity for a multifaceted diagnostic approach that, thorough clinical assessment, detailed biochemical evaluation, and advanced molecular genetic testing, seeks to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient care, particularly in pediatric populations. Advancements in identifying novel biomarkers and targeted therapies offer promise for tailored interventions, underscoring the importance of precision medicine in optimizing outcomes for pediatric PBD patients.

· Peroxisome biogenesis disorders include diverse genetic diseases with a broad clinical spectrum, presenting from severe neonatal forms to milder forms in childhood.

· Relying solely on very long-chain fatty acid assays may result in missing unusual cases, as normal biochemical profiles are present in some affected patients with different PEX6 variants.

· Integrated clinical, biochemical, and genetic testing, along with new studies on pexophagia and targeted therapies, support a precision medicine approach to improve diagnosis and care.

Peroxisome biogenesis disorders (PBDs) represent a heterogeneous group of inherited metabolic diseases characterized by impaired assembly of peroxisome and subsequent dysfunction [1,2]. These disorders present with a wide spectrum of clinical severity, ranging from the most severe form, Zellweger syndrome (ZS), to milder presentations such as neonatal adrenoleukodystrophy (NALD) and infantile Refsum disease and to the mildest form, chondrodysplasia punctata [1,3]. A hallmark of many PBDs is the accumulation of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) in body fluids and tissues due to impaired peroxisomal β-oxidation [4]. However, atypical cases exhibiting normal VLCFA levels despite clear evidence of PBD have been reported [5,6], challenging the reliance on VLCFA levels as a sole diagnostic criterion. The PEX6 gene, encoding peroxisome assembly factor 2, is pivotal in peroxisome biogenesis and is implicated in a significant subset of PBD cases [7-9]. The relationship between PEX6 mutations and VLCFA status is complex, as some individuals with PEX6-related PBDs may present with normal or only mildly elevated VLCFA levels, complicating both opinion and prognostication [6].

This review aims to clarify the relationship between PEX6 gene mutations and VLCFA accumulation in PBDs, critically examining the molecular mechanisms, phenotypic variability, and individual challenges, with a focus on pediatric donations. By addressing cases where VLCFA situations are not elevated despite the presence of pathogenic PEX6 variants, we seek to upgrade the individual approach and to ameliorate clinical protocols for affected children.

Peroxisome assembly factor 2, encoded by the PEX6 gene, is a member of the AAA (“ATPases Associated with diverse cellular Activities”) protein family that plays a pivotal role in peroxisome biogenesis [7,10]. PEX6, together with PEX1, assembles into a heterohexameric double-ring AAA–ATPase complex that is essential for peroxisome biogenesis and the maintenance of peroxisome homeostasis [10-12]. This complex plays a central role in the intricate cycle of peroxisomal protein import, specifically by mediating the retrotranslocation of PEX5, a critical peroxisomal protein import receptor [11-13], from the peroxisome membrane back to the cytosol. This retrotranslocation process is essential for PEX5 recycling and the subsequent import of peroxisomal matrix proteins. Disruptions to this cycle, caused by mutations in PEX6, lead to defective peroxisomal protein import, accumulating ubiquitinated PEX5 on the peroxisomal membrane [13,14]. This accumulation acts as a signal for pexophagy, the selective autophagic degradation of peroxisomes [10,13], further contributing to the peroxisome deficiency observed in PBDs. The AAA–ATPase complex's role extends beyond protein import; it is also implicated in peroxisome quality control [13] and the regulation of pexophagy [6,13]. The intricate interplay between PEX1 and PEX6 is highlighted by studies demonstrating that a PEX1 mutation can partially compensate for the defects caused by a PEX6 mutation in Arabidopsis [11], suggesting functional overlap and complex regulatory mechanisms. Furthermore, PEX6's influence extends beyond peroxisomes; research in yeast suggests a role in mitochondrial function and potentially even in the aging process [15], indicating that PEX6 might have more wide-ranging cellular roles than previously appreciated.

Mutations in PEX6 are linked to a broad spectrum of PBD phenotypes [1,7-9]. While many mutations result in severe PBDs, such as ZS, marked by the complete absence of functional peroxisomes [7,11], others can manifest as milder phenotypes like NALD or Infantile Refsum disease [1,8,16]. This significant clinical heterogeneity arises not only from the affected gene but also from the specific nature and location of the mutation within PEX6 [1,9,16]. Missense mutations, which may retain residual PEX6 protein function, often lead to milder clinical presentations [2,7,8], whereas nonsense mutations causing premature truncation of the PEX6 protein typically result in more severe disease [7]. Numerous studies have identified novel PEX6 pathogenic sequence variants [9,17,18], enriching our understanding of the genotypephenotype correlation in PBDs which are summarized as Tables 1 and 2, respectively. A striking example is the report of a patient presenting with both Heimler syndrome and infantile Refsum disease, linked to 2 novel homozygous nonsynonymous mutations in PEX6 [9], further illustrating the extensive clinical variability associated with this gene. The use of Drosophila models for PBDs, including those with PEX6 mutations [1,19], provides a powerful tool for dissecting these complex genotype-phenotype relationships, enabling a systematic investigation of the effects of specific mutations on various phenotypic parameters [1].

Peroxisomes are critically involved in the β-oxidation of VLCFAs [16,24]. Consequently, defects in peroxisomal function, as seen in PBDs, often lead to an accumulation of VLCFAs in plasma and tissues [3,25,26], representing a critical diagnostic marker for many PBDs. This accumulation typically correlates with disease severity [25,26]. However, the existence of patients presenting with normal VLCFA levels despite clear clinical and genetic evidence of PBD challenges the reliance on VLCFA analysis as the sole diagnostic approach [5].

Some patients with PEX6 mutations and classic clinical features of ZSD demonstrate increased plasma VLCFA levels, while others—especially those with milder phenotypes or certain genetic backgrounds—may present with normal or only mildly elevated VLCFA concentrations [27,28]. Recently, a 10-year-old girl with neurodevelopmental delay, facial dysmorphism, and hearing loss was found to be affected with Zellweger spectrum disorder through whole-exome sequencing. Specifically, she was homozygous for a likely pathogenic variant in PEX6 (c.1992G>C, p.Glu664Asp). Laboratory findings showed normal plasma VLCFA levels. This case highlights that pathogenic or likely pathogenic PEX6 mutations can occur with no biochemical abnormalities expected in the patients, suggesting that VLCFA screening alone may fail to detect some PEX6-related PBDs [29].

Although elevated VLCFA levels are a hallmark of PBDs, recent studies highlight that normal VLCFA profiles do not preclude a PBD diagnosis, particularly in early-onset or atypical cases [24]. Lüsebrink et al. [30] described a clinically severe patient with confirmed PBD whose initial biochemical assessments—including VLCFA analysis—were within reference ranges, delaying diagnosis. This underscores the importance of longitudinal monitoring and the application of complementary diagnostic modalities, especially in high-suspicion clinical scenarios. Additional reports have documented PEX6 variants in patients exhibiting broad phenotypic variability and normal or fluctuating biochemical markers [30,31], reinforcing the complexity of genotypephenotype correlations in PBD [32].

These atypical cases highlight the limitations of using VLCFA levels as a universal diagnostic criterion and underscore the importance of adopting a comprehensive diagnostic strategy incorporating detailed clinical evaluation, a broader range of biochemical tests, and genetic analysis [26,33,34]. The study of these atypical presentations is crucial for unraveling the complex pathophysiology of PBDs and for the identification of novel biomarkers that can overcome the limitations of VLCFA analysis. The development of advanced lipidomic techniques and the exploration of alternative biomarkers, such as C26:0-lysophosphatidylcholine and C26:0-carnitine, are crucial for improving diagnostic accuracy and broadening our understanding of PBDs [26,34].

Recent research has shed light on the significant role of pexophagy—referring to the selective autophagic degradation of peroxisomes—in the pathogenesis of PBDs. As shown in Fig. 1, The peroxisomal AAA–ATPase complex, encompassing PEX1, PEX6, and PEX26, is essential for suppressing pexophagy [14,35]. Disruptions in this complex, commonly observed in PBDs, lead to increased pexophagy, resulting in the accelerated degradation of peroxisomes and contributing substantially to the disease phenotype [13,14]. This understanding has shifted the focus from a primary defect in peroxisome assembly to a dysregulation of peroxisome homeostasis driven by pexophagy [13]. Studies involving autophagy inhibition have reported varying degrees of success in rescuing peroxisome function in PBD models [14,36], suggesting that targeting pexophagy might offer therapeutic potential. However, the results are not consistent across all PBD subtypes or mutations [36], highlighting the need for further investigation into the precise role of pexophagy and its intricate interplay with other cellular processes in the diverse clinical manifestations of PBDs. The complex relationship between PEX6, the AAA–ATPase complex, and pexophagy underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of PBD’s pathogenesis.

PBDs present major challenges in pediatrics due to their broad spectrum of clinical manifestations and profound impact on development [16,24,37,38]. Early and accurate diagnosis is paramount for the timely initiation of appropriate supportive care [37-39]. This care encompasses the management of neurological symptoms, such as hypotonia, seizures, developmental delays, and intellectual disabilities [16,24,38], as well as hepatic dysfunction, adrenal insufficiency, and other systemic complications [24,38]. The clinical course of PBDs is highly variable, ranging from neonatal lethality to milder forms presenting later in childhood, demanding individualized treatment plans tailored to the specific clinical presentation and genetic background of each patient [39].

Genetic counseling is essential for families affected by PBDs, considering the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and the availability of prenatal diagnostic testing [43,44]. Despite the availability of indefinable effective therapeutic strategies for most PBDs cases, novel therapeutic investigations, such as those focusing on targeting pexophagy [10,13] or correcting specific metabolic defects [45,46], have reported promising outcomes. Furthermore, correcting underlying metabolic defects remains a key therapeutic focus. Pharmacological induction of peroxisome proliferation or the use of drugs such as sodium 4-phenylbutyrate have been reported to improve peroxisomal β-oxidation and normalize VLCFA and plasmalogen levels in fibroblast models, with some patients experiencing milder disease [47]. Additionally, next-generation sequencing-guided personalized interventions and gene-based therapies are aiming to correct the causative biochemical and specific genetic abnormalities in individual patients [48]. The efficiency of these therapies may vary according to the specific gene mutations and related clinical phenotype [36,46], highlighting the role of a personalized medicine approach to the management of PBDs.

Diagnosis of PBDs typically involves a multifaceted approach integrating clinical evaluation, biochemical testing (including VLCFA analysis), and genetic testing [16,39,44,49]. While plasma VLCFA analysis remains a cornerstone of diagnosis [25,26], its limitations are evident in cases with normal VLCFA levels [5,34]. The development and application of alternative biomarkers, such as C26:0-lysophosphatidylcholine [34] and C26:0-carnitine [34], hold promise for improving diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. Next-generation sequencing has revolutionized genetic testing, enabling rapid and comprehensive analysis of multiple PEX genes [18,33], facilitating accurate diagnosis even in cases with atypical or subtle presentations. Advanced lipidomic techniques [26] and targeted metabolomics approaches [50] are further enhancing our capacity to identify disease-specific lipid profiles and metabolic abnormalities. The use of sophisticated imaging techniques, such as superresolution microscopy [14], provides valuable insights into peroxisome morphology and protein localization in PBDs. Furthermore, the development of humanized cell lines with targeted PEX gene knockouts using CRISPR/Cas9 technology [14,51] offers a powerful tool for studying the functions of individual peroxins and their contributions to peroxisome biogenesis and function. This integration of advanced technologies promises to refine the accuracy and efficiency of PBD diagnosis significantly and to accelerate the identification of novel therapeutic targets [52]. The use of such sophisticated approaches will be crucial in moving beyond VLCFA analysis as a primary diagnostic tool, addressing the challenges posed by atypical cases with normal VLCFA levels, and improving the overall management of PBDs in pediatric populations.

This review underscores the critical role of PEX6 in peroxisome biogenesis and its complex relationship with the diverse clinical spectrum of PBDs affecting children. The variability in clinical phenotypes, including the existence of atypical cases with normal VLCFA levels, emphasizes the intricate nature of these disorders. The identification of pexophagy as a crucial factor in PBD pathogenesis has significantly advanced our understanding of disease mechanisms. While effective curative therapies remain limited, ongoing research into novel biomarkers, pexophagy regulation, and advanced therapeutic strategies offers considerable hope for improving outcomes in children affected by PBDs. Future research must delve deeper into the complex interplay between PEX genes, peroxisome homeostasis, and metabolic abnormalities, focusing on identifying precise therapeutic targets and developing effective treatments for this group of challenging pediatric metabolic disorders. The continued use and development of advanced technologies, including next-generation sequencing, advanced lipidomic and metabolomic techniques, super-resolution microscopy, and CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing, will be essential for achieving these goals and ultimately transforming the lives of children and their families affected by PBDs. The focus should be on developing personalized therapeutic approaches that address the specific genetic and clinical characteristics of each patient, leading to improved diagnostic accuracy; targeted therapies; and, ultimately, better outcomes for children suffering from these complex disorders.
