# Familial Hyperkalemic Hypertension.
**Authors:** Cornelius RJ, Maeoka Y, Shinde U, McCormick JA
**Journal:** Compr Physiol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/cphy.c240004](https://doi.org/10.1002/cphy.c240004)

## Content

The rare disease Familial Hyperkalemic Hypertension (FHHt) is caused by mutations in the genes encoding Cullin 3 (CUL3), Kelch-Like 3 (KLHL3), and two members of the With-No-Lysine [K] (WNK) kinase family, WNK1 and WNK4. In the kidney, these mutations ultimately cause hyperactivation of NCC along the renal distal convoluted tubule. Hypertension results from increased NaCl retention, and hyperkalemia by impaired K + secretion by downstream nephron segments. CUL3 and KLHL3 are now known to form a ubiquitin ligase complex that promotes proteasomal degradation of WNK kinases, which activate downstream kinases that phosphorylate and thus activate NCC. For CUL3, potent effects on the vasculature that contribute to the more severe hypertensive phenotype have also been identified. Here we outline the in vitro and in vivo studies that led to the discovery of the molecular pathways regulating NCC and vascular tone, and how FHHt-causing mutations disrupt these pathways. Potential mechanisms for variability in disease severity related to differential effects of each mutation on the kidney and vasculature are described, and other possible effects of the mutant proteins beyond the kidney and vasculature are explored. © 2024 American Physiological Society. Compr Physiol 14:5839-5874, 2024.

Familial Hyperkalemic Hypertension (FHHt) (also known as Gordon syndrome or
Pseudohypoaldosteronism II) is a rare disease that in addition to hypertension and
hyperkalemia, features hyperchloremia, hypercalciuria, hyporeninemia, and metabolic
acidosis. The primary defect for most forms of FHHt is increased activity of the
renal thiazide-sensitive NaCl cotransporter (NCC) expressed along the distal
convoluted tubule(1) (Figure 1), since administration of
thiazide diuretics correct the disease. While FHHt is rare, affecting only several
hundred patients globally, study of the causative mutations has greatly expanded our
understanding of NCC regulation and its relationship to K+ homeostasis.
FHHt is caused by mutations in the genes encoding Cullin 3 (CUL3) and Kelch-Like 3
(KLHL3), components of a ubiquitin ligase complex that mediates ubiquitination of
protein substrates(2, 3). FHHt is also caused by mutations in genes encoding
two members of the With-No-Lysine [K] (WNK) kinases, WNK1 and WNK4 that promote
phosphorylation-dependent NCC activation(4).
WNK kinases are targets for CUL3-KLHL3-mediated ubiquitination. Mutations in the
coding regions of CUL3, KLHL3, or WNK4 decrease WNK4 ubiquitination leading to WNK4
accumulation. While KLHL3 and WNK4 mutations affect assembly of the
WNK4-ubiquination complex, CUL3 mutations have complicated effects whose in vivo
significance are not completely understood. WNK1 mutations are intronic, and lead to
ectopic WNK1 expression along the DCT. The net effect of FHHt-causing mutations is
to increase WNK kinase abundance along the DCT (Figure 2). This in turn increases
phosphorylation of the downstream kinases STE20/SPS1-related proline/alanine-rich
kinase (SPAK) and oxidative stress responsive kinase-1 (OSR1) that phosphorylate and
thus stimulate activity of NCC (Figure
2). In addition to promoting NCC hyperactivation, CUL3
mutations also lead to significant effects on the vasculature that promote
vasoconstriction and impair vasodilation, contributing to the most severe form of
the disease (Figure
2). The study of these effects has provided novel insight
into the roles of CUL3 in both vascular smooth muscle (Figure 2) and the endothelium. It is unclear
whether KLHL3 or WNK mutations affect vascular
tone, with evidence both for and against this possibility.

Here, our overall goal is to summarize current knowledge of the molecular and
pathophysiological mechanisms underlying FHHt. We will provide an overview of FHHt
and the heterogeneity in disease severity depending on the causative mutation. While
these mutations have guided our understanding of the normal physiological roles of
CUL3, KLHL3, and WNK kinases in the kidney and vasculature, we will first describe
the normal physiological pathways as revealed by both in vitro and knockout mouse
models. We will then discuss in vitro studies that have revealed how FHHt-causing
mutations disrupt these pathways, and the evidence supporting these disease
mechanisms gleaned from mouse models of FHHt. While NCC has been suggested to be the
major dysregulated ion transporting pathway in kidney, potential contributions of
other ion channels and transporters to the FHHt phenotype will be discussed.
Heterogeneity in disease severity based on the causative mutation will be
considered, and finally we will speculate on other potential pathophysiological
effects caused by mutant CUL3, KLHL3, and WNK kinases in the kidney and vasculature
and beyond.

In 1964, Paver and Pauline described a 15-year-old male patient bearing
the now classic clinical features of FHHt(5). This subject participated in several studies over the following
years that permitted detailed phenotypic characterization(6, 7). In
addition to high blood pressure (180/120 mmHg) and hyperkalemia (8.2 mmol/L), he
was reported to display metabolic acidosis, low renin activity but normal
aldosterone levels, and normal glomerular filtration rate. After several years
of attempts at control, administration of chlorothiazide and the potassium
chelator Resonium A (sodium polystyrene sulfonate) effectively corrected his
high blood pressure and hyperkalemia(6).
The authors of these studies suggested a genetic basis for the subject’s
symptoms, but no living relatives presented with the same disorder.
Subsequently, Gordon and colleagues reported a 10-year-old female subject
presenting with a very similar phenotype. Both dietary sodium restriction and
thiazide diuretics corrected the hypertension, hyperkalemia, and metabolic
acidosis in this patient. While FHHt was initially named Gordon syndrome,
Schambelan and colleagues later suggested the term
“pseudohypoaldosteronism II” (PHAII) to reflect the presence of
renal hyperkalemia in the absence of aldosterone deficiency (as might be seen
with Addison disease) that persists even with supraphysiologic doses of
mineralocorticoids(8). The type II
designation distinguishes the hypertension-associated PHAII from the classic
PHAI featuring renal salt-wasting and hypotension caused by mutations in the
genes encoding either the mineralocorticoid receptor, or subunits of the
epithelial Na+ channel (ENaC). Over the past decade, the term FHHt
has become the preferred and more widely used term since it more explicitly
describes the disease presentation.

Since it was originally described in the 1960s, several hundred
individuals with FHHt have been identified. One notable aspect of FHHt is
significant variability in the age at which is diagnosed. This is tied to the
specific causative gene mutation which will be discussed in this context in more
detail below (see “Disease severity correlates with causative gene
defect”). A recent in-depth analysis of 153
cases (84 probands and 69 relatives) identified FHHt in individuals ranging from
<1 year of age to over 70 years of age(9). This and another study from the Lifton group(2) also revealed a wide variability in serum
[K+], [HCO3−],
[Cl−] (hyperchloremia is another symptom of the disease)
and systolic and diastolic blood pressures. Some individuals present with a
milder form of FHHt, with serum [K+] at the high end of the normal
range (3.5–5.0 mmol/L) or normal blood pressure (Table 1). Notably,
hyperkalemia may be discovered early in life, with hypertension developing
later(2, 9). Irrespective of causative mutation, FHHt is
highly sensitive to treatment with thiazide diuretics, which corrects all
symptoms(6, 7, 10).
Furthermore, the responsiveness of FHHt to thiazides positively correlates with
disease severity(9). Since FHHt is highly
responsive to hydrochlorothiazide treatment, significant effort has been placed
on studying the renal manifestations and electrolyte imbalances caused by the
disease. Molecular cloning approaches by Gamba and colleagues led to the
identification of the gene encoding the thiazide-sensitive NaCl cotransporter
(NCC), SLC12A3(11). NCC
is specifically expressed along the distal convoluted tubule of the nephron
((12) and
Figure 1). It was first
thought that activating SLC12A3 mutations cause FHHt given its
profound sensitivity to thiazide diuretics. This idea was strengthened since the
disease is almost the mirror-image of Gitelman syndrome, which is caused by
loss-of-function mutations in NCC and presents with hypokalemia, hypercalciuria,
hyporeninemia, and metabolic alkalosis (a key difference being hypomagnesemia in
Gitelman syndrome, but normomagnesemia in FHHt(9)). However, no FHHt-causing mutations in SLC12A3
have been identified so far.

A seminal breakthrough in our understanding of the mechanisms underlying
FHHt was the identification in 2001 by the Lifton group of causative mutations
in two related genes(4). First, using a
positional cloning approach, one kindred was shown to have a large (41
kilobases) deletion within intron 1 of the WNK1 gene, located
at chromosome 12p (Figure
3). PCR analysis of another kindred previously shown to
link to the same chromosomal region revealed a 21 kilobase overlapping deletion
within the same intron. Genomic sequence and EST database analysis showed that
the WNK1 paralog WNK4, is located on a region
of chromosome 17 previously linked to FHHt. Using this information to examine
several kindreds, three missense mutations within a conserved 10 residue acidic
motif of WNK4 (Figure
3), and one mutation close to a WNK4 carboxyl-terminal
coiled-coil motif, were identified. For both WNK1 and WNK4, the mutations follow
an autosomal dominant mode of inheritance. These genes encode With-No-Lysine [K]
kinases, serine-threonine kinases named due to the presence of a cysteine
residue in the position in subdomain II occupied by the catalytic lysine residue
in other protein kinases(13, 14). Instead, a lysine in subdomain I
provides the catalytic site. This finding suggested a role for phosphorylation
in activation of NCC in FHHt (see “WNK kinases and NCC activation” and
“Identification of SPAK/OSR1 as
activators of NCC”). In addition,
WNK1 mutations within a region homologous to the WNK4
acidic motif were recently identified(15). These WNK1 mutations lead to a phenotype
resembling Spitzer-Weinstein syndrome, a Type 4 renal tubular acidosis that
presents with early onset thiazide-sensitive hyperkalemia and tubular acidosis,
without hypertension(16). Thus, these
WNK1 mutations appear to elicit clinical manifestations of
Type 4 renal tubular acidosis rather than FHHt.

Mutations in WNK1 and WNK4 only
accounted for a small fraction (22%) of the 153 FHHt cases reported in a recent
study(9). The majority of cases are de
novo or present in kindreds with few affected individuals, limiting positional
cloning approaches to identify causative mutations. In 2012, the Lifton group
studied a cohort of 52 FHHt kindreds including 126 affected individuals, of
which only 7 of the kindreds bore WNK1 or WNK4
mutations (a combined 37% of individual cases)(2). Taking advantage of newly developed whole exome sequencing
approaches, they identified several mutations in Kelch-like 3
(KLHL3) in individuals without WNK
mutations. Sequencing all remaining FHHt probands led to the identification of
24 KLHL3 mutations, including nonsense and missense mutations
(Figure 3).
Furthermore, both dominant and recessive modes of inheritance were identified.
Contemporaneously, the Jeunemaitre group identified two missense
KLHL3 mutations by linkage analysis of two families with 5
and 14 affected members. Extending this identification of KLHL3
mutations to 43 additional FHHt patients, 11 additional KLHL3
mutations were found, displaying dominant or recessive modes of
inheritance(3) These mutations occur
in several different regions necessary for KLHL3 function (see
“WNK1 and WNK4 are
substrates of the CUL3/KLHL3 CRL”).
KLHL3 mutations account for 50–60% of FHHt-causing
mutations, and of these, about 25% are recessive and 75% are dominant(2, 9).
KLHL3 interacts with the scaffold protein Cullin 3 (CUL3) to form an E3
ubiquitin ligase complex (which targets WNK kinases for ubiquitination as
discussed extensively below). Therefore, the Lifton group sequenced the
CUL3 gene for all remaining kindreds without mutations in
KLHL3, WNK1, or WNK4. CUL3 mutations clustering to intronic or
exonic sites involved in splicing of exon 9 were identified, many of which were
de novo (Figure
3). In vitro these mutations led to inappropriate
splicing of exon 9 from the CUL3 mRNA, which is translated to produce a mutant
CUL3 protein lacking amino acids 403–459 (Δ403–459, or
CUL3-Δ9 to reflect the exon deleted). More recently, a novel
CUL3 mutation leading to deletion of four amino acids
within exon 10 (CUL3Δ474−477) was identified in a
single patient(17) (Figure 3). About 20% of patients
with FHHt carry a CUL3 mutation(2, 9). The identification of
CUL3 and KLHL3 mutations led to the
hypothesis that these mutations impaired ubiquitination and hence proteasomal
degradation of NCC(3), but this is now
known not to be the case. Work from many groups over the past two decades have
now demonstrated that WNK kinases, CUL3, and KLHL3 are components of a broader
NCC regulatory pathway (see “Regulation of the thiazide-sensitive sodium chloride
cotransporter”). While the majority of
FHHt-causing mutations have been identified, it should be noted that mutations
have not yet been identified for four kindreds studied by the Lifton group(2).

Boyden and colleagues observed that the severity of FHHt manifestations
depends on the underlying mutated gene(2).
Notably, CUL3 mutations, which were all CUL3-Δ9
generating in this report, were associated with a high rate of growth impairment
or failure to thrive. The average age of diagnosis was 9 ± 6 years old,
with 94% of patients being diagnosed as hypertensive before the age of 18.
Average serum [K+] was extremely high (7.5 ± 0.9 mmol/L),
accompanied with a profound metabolic acidosis (serum
HCO3− 15.5 ± 2.0 mmol/L). For mutations
in KLHL3, WNK1, or WNK4, age
of diagnosis was on average in the second or third decade of life, and the
incidence of childhood hypertension was much lower (only 10–17% diagnosed
≤ age 18 for each gene). Hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis were also
less severe, and the authors noted an overall pattern of disease severity as
CUL3>KLHL3
recessive>KLHL3
dominant>WNK4>WNK1 (Table 1). A more recent detailed analysis
of 153 patients confirmed this pattern, but the average age of work-up of
KLHL3 recessive mutations was younger (9 years old),
similar to what they observed for CUL3 (CUL3-Δ9)
mutations (7 years old)(9). Similar to the
findings of Boyden and colleagues, diagnoses for patients with
KLHL3 dominant, WNK4, or
WNK1 mutations were on average made in the second or third
decades of life. WNK1 acidic motif mutations, which as noted
above cause Type 4 renal tubular acidosis (without hypertension), were included
in this analysis and ranked below WNK1 intron deletions
(Table 1).
Finally, with the caveat that only a single patient has been identified(17), the severity of FHHt with the
CUL3Δ474−477 mutation appears to be milder compared
with CUL3-Δ9 mutations (serum values (mmol/L) [K+] 5.9,
[HCO3−] 19.0, and [Cl−]
108.0), and more similar to KLHL3 dominant cases. Potential
mechanisms for this mutation-disease severity correlation will be discussed
below.

The study of genes mutated in FHHt, Gitelman syndrome, and related diseases
that display altered serum [K+] have provided a new understanding of the
molecular mechanisms through which NCC activity is modulated, and how this
contributes to normal K+ homeostasis. We will briefly outline these
normal physiological pathways before describing the functional effects of mutations
that disrupt them and lead to FHHt in a later section.

While NCC expression is the sine qua non of the DCT, in mouse, rat, and
human, the segment has been subdivided into “early” or DCT1 and
“late” or DCT2 based on differential expression of several genes.
In mice for example, along the DCT Umod, encoding uromodulin or
Tamm-Horsfall protein is only expressed along DCT1, while Calb1
is only expressed along DCT2(18). From a
functional viewpoint, magnesium transport genes are more abundant along DCT1
whereas calcium, electrogenic sodium, and potassium transport genes are more
abundant along DCT2. Thus, the DCT2 expresses not only NCC, but also subunits of
the Epithelial Na+ Channel (ENaC) and the Renal Outer Medullary
K+ channel (ROMK). Co-localization of ENaC and ROMK in segments
downstream of DCT1, the DCT2, the connecting segment (CNT), and the cortical
collecting duct (CCD), is important for physiological regulation of
K+ homeostasis. In these segments, ENaC-mediated Na+
reabsorption generates the electrical driving force for ROMK-mediated
K+ secretion into the tubule lumen. This arrangement of the
K+-secreting machinery led to the hypothesis that increased NCC
activity and the subsequent increase in Na+ reabsorption lowers the
delivery to the downstream segments that require Na+ entry through
ENaC to drive K+ secretion (Figure 4). This results in lower
K+ secretion and thus hyperkalemia. Consistent with this, an
FHHt-like phenotype featuring higher plasma [K+] was reported in a
mouse model in which NCC hyperactivation was limited to the DCT1
((19) and see
“SPAK hyperactivation in
FHHt”), despite the lack of pathways for
significant K+ reabsorption or secretion along this segment. Note
that NCC hyperactivation alone is sufficient for this effect. An important
finding in this model was that the DCT1 displays hypertrophy, further increasing
Na+-reabsorptive capacity, while the CNT atrophies, lowering the
capacity for Na+ reabsorption and K+ secretion,
exacerbating effects on K+ secretion through ROMK. The molecular
mechanisms underlying DCT1 hypertrophy are unknown but may be related to
enhanced NaCl entry(20, 21). Conversely, in NCC knockout mice (a model of
Gitelman syndrome) or in mice or rats administered thiazide diuretics
chronically, loss or reduced Na+ entry through NCC increases
Na+ delivery to downstream segments. This, coupled with increased
ENaC apical abundance along CNT/CCD due to aldosterone secretion resulting from
mild volume depletion, results in greater Na+ entry through ENaC.
This provides a greater drive for K+ secretion through ROMK, and thus
hypokalemia develops. Flow-mediated activation of BK/maxi-K channels along the
CCD due to reduced NCC activity might also contribute to K+ wasting.
The possible roles of more direct effects of FHHt-causing mutations on ENaC and
ROMK in vivo to promote hyperkalemia are discussed below (See
“Mouse models of
FHHt”).

Important insight into how NCC might be overactive in FHHt came from the
identification of causative mutations in WNK1(4). This finding suggested a potential role for
phosphorylation, but it took several years to confirm this since WNK kinases do
not directly phosphorylate NCC. Studies from two groups examining
phosphorylation-dependent stimulation of the
Na+-K+−2Cl− cotransporter 1
(NKCC1) identified two serine-threonine kinases, STE20/SPS1-related
proline/alanine-rich kinase (SPAK) and oxidative stress responsive kinase-1
(OSR1) that interact with, phosphorylate, and stimulate activity of NKCC1(22, 23). It was then shown that both SPAK and OSR1 interact with, and
are activated by, WNK kinases(24, 25). The final piece of the puzzle was
resolved in 2008 when Richardson and colleagues demonstrated that SPAK and OSR1
directly bind to and phosphorylate NCC at several residues along its cytoplasmic
amino-terminal tail(26). This
phosphorylation of NCC is essential for transporter activity, since mutation of
some or all the sites ablates NCC activity(26, 27). Studies in knockout
mice have since shown that in vivo, SPAK is the main activator of NCC(28–30) and the main activator of SPAK along the DCT is WNK4(31). Thus, the NCC activation pathway is
often referred to as the WNK4-SPAK-NCC pathway. More recent studies have
revealed that WNK-mediated activation of SPAK occurs in membraneless
compartments that concentrate biological molecules generically called molecular
condensates. More specifically with respect to WNK-SPAK signaling these are
termed “WNK bodies”(32).
These most notably form to activate NCC under conditions that promote
hypokalemia in order to maintain K+ homeostasis (see
“The distal convoluted tubule,
NCC activity, and K+ homeostasis”). In FHHt,
large WNK bodies appear to form, though confirmation that they are true WNK
bodies rather than simple aggregates of WNK kinases and SPAK is lacking.

Two major WNK kinase regulatory mechanisms relevant to SPAK-mediated NCC
activation have been identified. The first is inhibition through WNK kinase
Cl− binding, and the second (described in the next
section) is proteasomal degradation of WNK kinases following ubiquitination. The
first mechanism hinges on the important discovery by Goldsmith and colleagues
that Cl− can directly bind to WNK kinases, and that this
inhibits WNK kinase autophosphorylation-dependent activation(33). This regulatory mechanism does not appear to be
relevant to the etiology of FHHt, but rather plays a critical role in regulation
of NCC activity in response to changes in serum [K+] in non-disease
states (see
Fig 5
and (34) for a recent
in-depth review). Briefly, as serum [K+] decreases,
K+ exits DCT cells via the basolateral K+ channel
Kir4.1/5.1. This leads to hyperpolarization of the basolateral membrane favoring
efflux of Cl− via the CLC-KB channel, lowering intracellular
[Cl−]. Thus, less Cl− is available to
bind to WNK kinases, leading to their autophosphorylation and activation, SPAK
activation, and ultimately NCC activation. This lowers Na+ delivery
to the downstream segments where Na+ entry through ENaC drives
K+ secretion through ROMK, providing a mechanism to raise serum
[K+]. Conversely, when serum [K+] is higher, there is
less drive for Cl− exit from DCT cells, which promotes WNK
kinase inhibition and lower NCC activity, increasing downstream Na+
delivery, which then promotes K+ secretion to lower serum
[K+]. Loss-of-function mutations in KCNJ10, encoding Kir4.1,
cause EAST (Epilepsy, Ataxia, Sensorineural deafness, Tubulopathy) or SeSAME
(Seizures, Sensorineural deafness, Ataxia, Mental retardation, and Electrolyte
imbalance) syndrome, which features a tubulopathy similar to Gitelman syndrome.
In this disease, intracellular Cl− cannot change in response
to alterations in serum [K+], leading to constitutive WNK kinase
inhibition and thus lower NCC activity. The net effect is uncontrolled urinary
K+ wasting due to increased Na+ delivery to ENaC.
To-date, no mutations have been identified that prevent binding of
Cl− to WNK kinases. Such mutations would be predicted to
hyperactivate NCC, leading to an FHHt phenotype.

Further complexity regarding WNK function arises from the generation of
variant forms of WNK1 from the WNK1 gene through alternative
promoter usage(35, 36). These forms include the full-length form that
possesses kinase activity, often referred to as L-WNK1 (long WNK1). In contrast,
an alternative promoter leads to transcription of a form of WNK1 with the first
4 exons replaced by an alternative exon, leading to deletion of the kinase
domain. This kinase-inactive form is particularly enriched along the distal
nephron and is therefore referred to as kidney-specific WNK1 (KS-WNK1). The
functional effects of KS-WNK1 and how this relates to FHHt will be described in
detail (see “The role of
KS-WNK1 in FHHt”). Alternative splicing of the
L-WNK1 transcript (primarily exons 11 and 12) also occurs without affecting the
L-WNK1 kinase domain (35, 36) For simplicity, we will refer to the active
L-WNK1 as WNK1, and KS-WNK1 is specifically used to indicate the kinase inactive
form.

The ubiquitin proteasome system regulates WNK kinase signaling and thus
NCC activity through cullin-RING ligases (CRLs), the largest group of E3
ubiquitin ligases. CRLs consist of several components that interact to form an
active E3 ubiquitin ligase. These components are a cullin scaffold protein, a
RING subunit that transfers the ubiquitin moiety to substrates, a substrate
receptor that determines the specific substrate(s) that will be ubiquitinated,
and an adaptor protein that links the cullin to the substrate receptor
(see
Figure 6
which uses CUL3-KLHL3-WNK4 as an example). CRLs are modular, and
adaptor and substrate receptors can be exchanged to confer specificity to either
one substrate, or to a small group of substrates. Eight cullins have been
identified in humans: cullins 1, 2, 3, 4A, 4B, 5, 7, and 9/PARC(37). Most relevant to FHHt is Cullin 3 (CUL3) since
mutations in the CUL3 gene cause the most severe form of the
disease. Uniquely among the cullins, adaptor and substrate receptor functions
for CUL3-containing CRLs are performed by a single protein. These adaptors
interact with CUL3 through their Broad complex, Tramtrack, Bric-a-brac (BTB)
domain. Over 200 BTB-containing proteins that interact with CUL3 have been
identified to-date, permitting CUL3-containing CRLs to ubiquitinate a vast array
of substrates. As outlined above, mutations in the CUL3 substrate adaptor KLHL3
were identified as FHHt-causing. Mutations have not been identified in other
cullins or in other CUL3 adaptors, suggesting the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL specifically
plays a central role in modulating NCC activity.

Soon after the identification of FHHt-causing mutations in CUL3 and
KLHL3, attention turned to identifying the target of the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL by in
vitro methods. Immunoprecipitation experiments in HEK293 cells showed no
interactions between KLHL3 and SPAK or NCC(38). Mass spectrometry and immunoprecipitation studies showed
interactions between KLHL3 and WNK1, WNK2, WNK3, and WNK4(38–41)
In vitro ubiquitination studies showed that the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL promoted
polyubiquitination of WNK1 and WNK4(38–41), lowering their
abundances. Knockdown of CUL3 in HeLa cells using siRNA increased WNK1, through
a post-transcriptional effect(38).
Together, these data from several independent groups showed that WNK kinases are
targets of the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL. The overall consensus is that FHHt-causing
mutations cause dysregulation of WNK kinase ubiquitination and proteasomal
degradation, leading to increased SPAK activation and ultimately NCC
hyperactivation.

Available published evidence suggests that WNK kinase ubiquitination may
be a physiologically regulated process. Shibata and colleagues showed that KLHL3
is phosphorylated at several sites, including S433 which is mutated in several
patients with FHHt(42). This site was
shown to be phosphorylated directly by protein kinase C, and phosphorylation was
induced by angiotensin II, a hormone released in response to sodium restriction
or volume depletion (Figure
6). Importantly, compared with WT KLHL3, phosphomimetic
substitution (S433E) of KLHL3 impaired WNK4 binding to KLHL3 and WNK4
degradation. This effect was recapitulated by angiotensin II administration in
vitro, and in mice, even in the context of a high salt diet(42). These data suggest angiotensin II directly
induces KLHL3 phosphorylation. A subsequent study by the same group suggests
that this regulatory mechanism may also be important for activation of NCC in
response to a decrease in serum [K+] (see
“The distal
convoluted tubule, NCC activity, and K+
homeostasis”)(43). Dietary K+ restriction was shown to
promote KLHL3 phosphorylation as S433 through protein kinase C and lower total
KLHL3 abundance (Figure
6), preventing WNK4 degradation and increasing SPAK/OSR1
activity and NCC phosphorylation(43). The
mechanism by which dietary K+ restriction (and hence hypokalemia)
stimulates PKC activity has not been determined. Phosphorylation of KLHL3 at
S433 has also been reported to be stimulated by PKA and Akt, suggesting
potential effects to increase WNK4 abundance in response to vasopressin and
insulin(44). Reductions in KLHL3
abundance and thus WNK kinase degradation may also play a role in NCC activation
and inappropriate Na+ retention in diabetes(45, 46).
Activity of cullins including CUL3 may also be physiologically regulated to
maintain K+ homeostasis as demonstrated by recent studies. Altering
dietary K+ intake may affect the abundance and activation status of
several cullins including CUL3 in mouse DCT(47, 48). CRLs are activated
by covalent attachment of the small (8KDa) ubiquitin-like protein neuronal
precursor cell-expressed developmentally downregulated protein 8 (NEDD8) to the
cullin scaffold, a process called “neddylation”(49) (discussed in detail below “Impaired deneddylation”). Low dietary
K+ decreased the degree of neddylation of cullins 1, 3, 4, and 5,
which negatively correlated with abundances of total and phosphorylated NCC.
Thus, when serum [K+] is lower, cullins are inhibited to prevent WNK4
ubiquitination and degradation, increasing NCC activity. This in turn reduces
Na+ delivery to downstream K+-secreting segments,
decreasing K+ secretion to normalize serum [K+]. When
serum [K+] is raised, the opposite occurs to inhibit NCC and promote
K+ excretion. The signaling mechanisms linking changes in serum
[K+] to alterations in cullin neddylation are unknown. The
significance of changes in activity of cullins other than CUL3 are also unclear
since only CUL3 has been reported to regulate the WNK4-SPAK-NCC pathway.

Since the identification of mutations in WNK kinases and CUL3 as causing
FHHt, most studies have focused on how the WNK kinase and CUL3-KLHL3 CRL regulates
renal electrolyte handling and blood pressure. However, the presence of hypertension
in the disease also prompted interest in non-renal functions of these proteins in
the cardiovascular system, revealing important roles for WNK kinases and CUL3 in
vascular function. We will describe some of the key findings here and discuss how
these might relate to FHHt in a later section.

Several studies have revealed an important role for WNK kinase signaling
in development of the cardiovascular system. Delaloy and colleagues generated a
transgenic mouse line expressing a β-galactosidase reporter from a
bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) modified to only assess expression of the
kinase active form of WNK1 and not KS-WNK1(50). β-galactosidase activity revealed strong WNK1 expression
in the heart and vasculature starting early in embryogenesis and persisting into
adulthood. Endothelial-specific disruption of WNK1 in mice impaired heart
development and angiogenesis of both arteries and veins, and no homozygous
knockouts survived to birth(51). The WNK
kinase inhibitor WNK463 was reported to inhibit sprouting of endothelial cells
from aortic slices as well as blood vessel extension in ex vivo cultured
embryos(52). The phenotypes of both
global and endothelial OSR1 knockout mice showed almost identical results(53). Furthermore, endothelial-specific
expression of a constitutively active OSR1 mutant rescued global WNK1 knockout
mice, suggesting OSR1, rather than SPAK, is the downstream mediator of WNK1 in
heart and vasculature development. A study in human umbilical vein endothelial
cells suggested that OSR1 is required for chemotaxis and invasion of endothelial
cells, while SPAK is more important for endothelial cell proliferation(54). Upstream factors that may interact
with WNK and OSR1 signaling during angiogenesis include TGF-β and
VEGF(52, 55). Studies using xenotransplantation of human
hepatoma cells into zebrafish suggest that the effects of WNK1 to stimulate
angiogenesis may contribute to tumor growth and metastasis(56). However, there have been no reports of worse
outcomes for cancer in FHHt patients.

The SPAK/OSR1 target NKCC1 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of
hypertension, since loop diuretics such as furosemide or bumetanide that inhibit
the transporter lower intracellular [Cl−], hyperpolarize
vascular smooth muscle cells, and lower contractile responses to several stimuli
including phenylephrine and angiotensin II(57). Bumetanide lowered blood pressure in rats even when the renal
arteries were clamped suggesting effects on the other target of loop diuretics,
Na+-K+−2Cl− cotransporter 2
(NKCC2), did not contribute(58).
Furthermore, bumetanide did not lower blood pressure in NKCC1 knockout mice.
This raises the possibility that dysregulation of WNK-SPAK/OSR1 signaling in the
vasculature contributes to elevated blood pressure in FHHt by hyperactivating
NKCC1 (Figure 7).
In support of this hypothesis, both WNK1 heterozygote(59) and SPAK knockout(60) mice display lower abundance of phosphorylated NKCC1 in aorta
and impaired aortic contractility responses to phenylephrine. WNK1 heterozygotes
also showed lower pressure-induced myogenic responses in mesenteric
arteries(60). In wild type mice, a
high salt diet lowered phosphorylation of SPAK/OSR1 and NKCC1 in aorta, whereas
acute or chronic angiotensin administration increased both(61). These effects were absent in WNK3 knockout mice,
which also displayed a lower pressor response to angiotensin II infusion.

A role for CUL3 in regulating vascular tone (Figure 7) first came from a mouse model
expressing a dominant-negative mutant of the peroxisome proliferator activated
receptor γ (PPARγ-P467L) specifically in vascular smooth muscle
cells(62). The equivalent mutation in
humans (P467L) causes early-onset hypertension in humans. In aorta from these
mice, abundances of CUL3 and activated neddylated CUL3 were lower than in WT
mice. As a consequence, abundance and activity of the CUL3 substrate RhoA was
increased. RhoA and its downstream effector Rho kinase (ROCK) increase vascular
tone by mediating phosphorylation of myosin phosphatase target subunit 1 (MYPT1)
(Figure 7).
Pharmacological inhibition of cullin neddylation with MLN4924 enhanced ex vivo
aortic contractile responses to several agonists, an effect blocked by ROCK
inhibition. Therefore, CUL3 dysregulation in this model promotes hypertension by
stimulating RhoA/ROCK activity. A subsequent in vitro study identified BACURD1
(KCTD13) as the CUL3 adaptor protein that interacts with RhoA to promote
CUL3-mediated ubiquitination(63).

CUL3-mediated ubiquitination also affects vascular smooth muscle tone
through effects on vasodilation (Figure 7). The nitric oxide (NO)-soluble guanylyl
cyclase (sGC)-cyclic GMP (cGMP) pathway plays an important role in vasodilation
by activating cGMP-dependent protein kinase 1 (PKG1) activity in vascular smooth
muscle cells, which subsequently leads to lower intracellular Ca2+
levels. Phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE5) hydrolyzes cGMP to inactive 5′-GMP,
counteracting its vasodilatory effect. Pharmacological inhibition of PDE5
suggested that increased PDE5 activity caused impaired vasodilation in
PPARγ-P467L mice(64), and it was
shown that PDE5 is a substrate of a CUL3-RhoBTB1 ubiquitin ligase complex(64, 65). Mice overexpressing RhoBTB1 specifically in vascular smooth
muscle cells were protected against hypertension caused by both expression of
PPARγ-P467L and chronic infusion of angiotensin II. Disruption of CUL3
specifically in vascular smooth muscle causes severe hypertension, but there are
some discrepant effects that may reflect effects of CUL3 loss on multiple
cellular pathways(66). While abundance of
RhoA was increased in aorta from these mice, PDE5 levels were unchanged.
However, expression of sGC, cGMP production, and vasodilatory responses to cGMP
analogues were all reduced.

One study has made a direct connection between CUL3 and WNK kinases in
causing vasoconstriction through effects on vascular smooth muscle in response
to angiotensin II(67). Mouse aorta and
vascular smooth muscle cells were found to express KLHL2 rather than KLHL3.
Overexpression or knockdown of KLHL2 in cultured mouse vascular smooth muscle
cells showed that WNK3 is a target of a CUL3-KLH2 ubiquitin ligase complex.
Angiotensin II treatment of mouse vascular smooth muscle cells lowered KLHL2
abundance within minutes, increasing WNK3, SPAK phosphorylation, and NKCC1
phosphorylation. In vivo, dietary sodium restriction and acute infusion of
angiotensin II decreased KLHL2 and increased WNK3 levels in mouse aorta.

Similar to WNK kinases, there is evidence that CUL3 is involved in
angiogenesis, in particular through effects on endothelial cells(68). Several CUL3-BTB protein complexes have been
identified that can affect vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-Notch
pathway crosstalk, gene expression, and endothelial barrier formation. While
endothelial cell-specific CUL3 knockout mice have not yet been reported, the
Sigmund group characterized mice expressing CUL3-Δ9 specifically in
endothelial cells(69). These mice did not
display a significant difference in blood pressure compared with WT mice.
However, impaired vasodilatory responses to acetylcholine and sodium
nitroprusside in carotid and basilar arteries suggest a role for CUL3 in
endothelial function in vivo. The findings from these mice will be discussed in
more detail below (see “Vascular effects of FHHt-causing CUL3
mutations”).

In vitro analysis of FHHt-causing mutations has uncovered a novel NCC
regulatory pathway. Examination of the first mutations discovered in WNK kinases led
to the discovery of a WNK-SPAK/OSR1 phosphorylation cascade that activates NCC.
These WNK kinase mutations ultimately cause a gain-of-function due to increased WNK
kinase abundance. Analysis of subsequently identified mutations in
CUL3 and KLHL3 unveiled increasingly more
complex mechanisms leading to the disease phenotype. These FHHt-causing mutations
led to the discovery of a pathway involving degradation of primarily WNK4 and
revealed roles for impaired CUL3 deneddylation and increased CUL3-adaptor
interaction. Here we will summarize these in vitro studies, before discussing in
vivo models that support these proposed mechanisms.

Initial characterization of mutations in WNK1 suggested
an increase in WNK kinase abundance may contribute to WNK1-mediated FHHt. In
2001, Wilson and colleagues identified several intronic mutations in WNK1 in
multiple FHHt kindreds. These mutations caused genetic deletion of large regions
of intron 1 (21 or 41 kilobases). Comparisons of transcripts levels in
leukocytes attained from affected individuals showed a five-fold increase in
WNK1 mRNA expression compared to unaffected controls(4). Thus, these intronic mutations caused an increase
in mRNA, and were presumed to increase WNK1 protein abundance
(Figure 8)
and ultimately NCC activity. More recently, WNK1 mutations that
cause Type 4 renal tubular acidosis (without hypertension) were identified in
the acidic domain(15), similar to those
first identified in WNK4 as causing FHHt, as described
below.

In contrast to WNK1 mutations, the majority of WNK4 mutations are exonic
and located in a region encoding the acidic motif that contains a run of
negatively-charged residues(4). Although
we now know, based on mouse models that WNK4 is a critical activator of NCC in
vivo (see “WNK4 models of
FHHt”), some early in vitro experiments suggested
an inhibitory effect on NCC. Initial in vitro studies suggested that WNK4
inhibited NCC activation basally, and that some FHHt WNK4 mutations prevented
this inhibition. Studies in Xenopus laevis oocytes(70–72) demonstrated that the disease-causing WNK4 mutants Q562E and
D564H prevented the ability of WT WNK4 to reduce NCC expression in the plasma
membrane. These data suggested a loss of function mutation, but other WNK4
mutants, D561A and D564A, had a similar effect to WT WNK4 in Xenopus
laevis oocytes(72) and
Marine-Darby canine kidney cells(73) and
were unable to prevent the effect of WT WNK4 on NCC. These contradictory results
were likely caused by limitations of the in vitro system used, particularly the
Cl− content of incubation solutions. It is now well
established that WNK kinases are directly inhibited by direct binding of
Cl−(33), with WNK4
particularly sensitive to this inhibition(74, 75). Thus, under
conditions when intracellular [Cl−] is high, WNK4 is
inhibited, leading to a dominant-negative effect of WNK4 on NCC phosphorylation
and activation. With regard to modulation of NCC surface abundance by WNK4 under
high [Cl−] conditions, lower NCC phosphorylation has been
reported to promote clathrin-mediated endocytosis of NCC(76). The effects of modulation of intracellular
[Cl−] on NCC activation in vivo is described in more
detail above (see “WNK kinases
and NCC activation”).

The common factor in FHHt regardless of the causative mutation is
greater WNK kinase expression, which ultimately leads to higher NCC activity via
increased phosphorylation. Direct phosphorylation of NCC was not via WNK
kinases, but rather by two intermediary kinases SPAK and OSR1. SPAK and OSR1
were first discovered because of their ability to interact with and directly
phosphorylate the Na+-K+−2Cl–
cotransporter 1 (NKCC1)(22, 77), leading to activation of the
transporter. WNK1 and WNK4 were later shown to phosphorylate and activate SPAK
and OSR1. Immunoprecipitation studies by Vitari and colleagues found that both
WNK1 and WNK4 interacted with SPAK and OSR1, and phosphorylation of these
kinases increased their activity(25). The
Shibuya group showed that SPAK and OSR1 directly phosphorylate NCC, NKCC1, and
NKCC2(24). The importance of this
SPAK/OSR1 mediated phosphorylation of the amino-terminus of NCC for NCC activity
was demonstrated in Xenopus laevis oocytes(24). Mutation of two threonines and one serine (T53,
T58, and S71, rat numbering) prevented activation of NCC in response to
depletion of intracellular Cl−. A later analysis of the
WNK1-SPAK/OSR1-NCC phosphorylation cascade by the Alessi group showed that WNK1
activation by hypotonic low-Cl− activated NCC, and this effect
was dependent on NCC interaction and phosphorylation by SPAK/OSR1(26). The importance of NCC phosphorylation
in NCC activation was demonstrated by the observation that mice overexpressing
NCC from a BAC transgene are phenotypically normal at baseline(78), but display an exaggerated increase in blood
pressure in after administration of the phosphatase inhibitor calcineurin,
administration of which can lead to FHHt-like side effects in humans(79). Thus, a linear pathway from WNK
kinases to SPAK/OSR1 followed by SPAK/OSR1-mediated phosphorylation and
activation of NCC was established.

Identification of KLHL3(2, 3) and CUL3(2) mutations led to multiple studies demonstrating that CRLs
ubiquitinate WNK kinases, targeting them for proteasomal degradation. KLHL3
belongs to the family of BTB-domain containing KELCH substrate adaptors that
interact with the scaffold CUL3 in conjunction with the RING subunit to form a
CRL(80). BTB-KELCH proteins consist
of an N-terminal BTB/POZ domain and a C-terminal kelch domain containing five to
six KELCH motifs that form a beta-propeller structure(80). In between the BTB and KELCH domains is a BACK
domain. BTB-containing KELCH proteins interact with CUL3 via the BTB domain and
bind substrates through the KELCH propeller. Both recessive and dominant
KLHL3 FHHt-causing mutations are located in both the KELCH
propeller and BTB domains(2, 9). Disruption of the propeller due to
mutations in the KELCH motifs suggests a mechanism involving loss of interaction
between the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL and WNK kinases (Figure 8). For mutations in the BTB and
BACK domains, a plausible mechanism is impaired interaction between KLHL3 and
CUL3. Therefore, for all KLHL3 mutations, assembly of the CUL3-KLHL3-WNK kinase
complex is impaired, which would lead to WNK kinase accumulation, increased
SPAK/OSR1 activation, and inappropriate NCC activation.

These possibilities have been tested experimentally by several groups.
Using HEK293T cells Wakabayashi and colleagues found that KLHL3
co-immunoprecipitated with WNK4, but not with SPAK, OSR1, or NCC(41). KLHL3-dependent ubiquitination of WNK4 required
CUL3. FHHt-causing mutations in the KLHL3 KELCH motif (R528H) and WNK4 (E562K,
D564A, and Q585E) impaired the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of WNK4 shown by
expression of WT KLHL3 and WT WNK4. Confirmation that the KLHL3-WNK4 interaction
was direct was demonstrated using fluorescent correlation spectroscopy using a
TAMRA-labeled WNK4 peptide (Figure
8). TAMRA-labeled WNK4 with acidic motif mutations did
not directly bind to KLHL3. The Lifton group reported similar findings in COS-7
cells, demonstrating interactions between KLHL3 and WNK1 or WNK4(40) by co-immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry.
Dominant FHHt-causing mutations in KLHL3 (R528H) impaired ubiquitination and
mutations in both KLHL3 (R528H) and WNK4 (E559K, Q562E) prevented degradation of
WNK4 due to impaired binding. This study also demonstrated that KLHL3 mutations
enhance WNK4 inhibition of ROMK membrane expression, suggesting a potential role
in impaired K+ secretion in KLHL3-mediated FHHt (see
“WNK4
models of FHHt”).

Other studies have performed more extensive analyses beyond the KELCH
motif. A study using Xenopus oocytes confirmed the results of studies using
mammalian cells and provided insight into effects of mutations in the BTB and
BACK domains(81). Five different KLHL3
mutations impaired WNK4 degradation, A77E (BTB), C164F (BACK), Q309R, L387P,
R528C (all in KELCH domains) to different degrees. Overexpression of WT KLHL3
strongly promoted WNK4 degradation, while the BTB domain mutant had almost no
effect. The BACK and Kelch domain mutants displayed some ability to promote WNK4
degradation, but weaker than that of WT KLHL3. In HEK293 cells, KLHL3 R528H and
S432N (Kelch) impaired pulldown of WNK1, WNK2, and WNK3(38). Of 15 KLHL3 mutations, 13 impaired binding to
either CUL3 or WNK1. Three BTB domain mutants (A77E, M78V, and E85A) and a
mutant located in the BACK domain (C164F) reduced interaction with CUL3 but not
WNK1. Eight KELCH domain mutants (Q309R, R384Q, L387P, S410L, S432N, R528H,
R528C and N529K) impaired binding to WNK1 but not CUL3. Two WNK4 mutants (E562K
and Q565 E) and three WNK1 mutants (E633K, D635A, Q636E) displayed impaired
binding to KLHL3. The smallest fragment of WNK1 able to pull-down KLHL3 was a
479–667 residue fragment located on the C-terminal side of the kinase
domain containing the auto-inhibitory and a coiled-coil domain. Mori and
colleagues(82) studied four different
KLHL3 mutations in HEK293T cells, E85A (BTB), C164F (BACK), S410L and R528H
(KELCH). They uncovered a unique mechanism for one KLHL3 mutant. Cycloheximide
chase experiments showed a rapid reduction in abundance of S410L KELCH compared
to WT and KLHL3 R528H. This indicated that the mechanism for the increase in WNK
abundance was reduced stability of the KLHL3 S410L mutant protein. They also
performed fluorescent correlation spectroscopy with TAMRA-labeled WNK4 and found
that only KELCH mutants had decreased binding to WNK4. This “double
hit” of lower protein stability and lower binding affinity may explain
the more severe clinical symptoms of patients with the KLHL3 S410L
mutation(3). Other mechanistically
interesting mutations are KLHL3 S433G and S433N(9). As outlined above S433 undergoes phosphorylation in response to
angiotensin II and dietary K+ restriction (see
“Physiological significance of
CUL3-KLHL3 mediated WNK kinase ubiquitination”).
This impairs binding of WNK4 to KLHL3, preventing interaction with the WNK4
acidic motif(82) and hence WNK4
ubiquitination ultimately activating NCC. FHHt-causing mutations S433G and S443N
likely recapitulate the effect of bona fide phosphorylation of S433 or the
phosphomimetic S433E mutation exerting significant structural changes that
prevent WNK4 binding to the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL. Further supporting this mechanism
for these mutants, the calcineurin inhibitor tacrolimus, which can cause an
FHHt-like phenotype in mice and humans by increasing NCC activity though the
WNK-SPAK pathway(79), also increases
KLHL3 S433 phosphorylation(83).

A more recent in vitro study explored the structural effects of several
FHHt-causing KLHL3 mutations on KLHL3 KELCH-WNK4 acidic motif interactions,
focusing on differential effects of mutations on the KELCH domain WNK4 binding
surface versus those buried within the KECLH domain(84). Overall, mutations on the surface had a greater
effect on disrupting interactions with KLHL3, as reflected by higher WNK4
protein abundance when WNK4 was co-injected with mutant KLHL3 versus WT KLHL3
into Xenopus laevis oocytes. Several buried mutations had a
similar effect to WT KLHL3, with no effect on WNK4 abundance. Molecular dynamics
simulations identified two main effects of binding surface mutations 1) altered
electrostatic binding potential of the acidic motif binding site and 2)
disruption of KELCH-acidic motif hydrogen bonds. Buried mutations were modeled
to have milder or no effects on the KELCH-acidic motif interaction, suggesting
some mutations exert additional effects on the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL. For example,
interactions with CUL3 might be affected, or there may be a change in the
overall architecture of the CRL, impairing ubiquitin transfer from the RING
ligase to WNK4.

Recent work identified novel missense mutations in the
WNK1 gene located in a region homologous to the WNK4 acidic
motif(15). Affected individuals
present with hyperkalemia and hyperchloremia at early onset, but without
elevated blood pressure, so this disorder is Type 4 renal tubular acidosis
rather than FHHt. Immunoprecipitation studies in HEK293T cells showed a
differential effect of these mutations on interactions with KLHL3 depending on
whether the mutation was in WNK1 (WNK1 A634T) or KS-WNK1 (KS-WNK1 A227T). In
Xenopus laevis oocytes, both WT WNK1 and WT KS-WNK1
interacted with KLHL3, but this interaction was about 35% lower for WNK1 A634T
and completely abolished for KS-WNK1 A227T. Co-expression studies in oocytes
suggested that KS-WNK1 is the primary target for CUL3-KLHL3 CRL-mediated
degradation. Similar results were obtained for the WNK1 D635N and KS-WNK1 D228N
mutants using HEK293T cells. Notably, KS-WNK1 rather than WNK1 was shown to be
the preferred target for the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL, with KS-WNK1 displaying relatively
higher interaction and ubiquitination compared with WNK1. WNK1 D635N and KS-WNK1
D228N mutants both displayed reduced binding to KLHL3 and impaired
ubiquitination. These data suggest that dysregulation of CUL3-KLHL3-mediated
KS-WNK1 ubiquitination leading to KS-WNK1 accumulation contributes to the Type 4
renal tubular acidosis seen in these patients, and this may also be of
significance for FHHt due to mutations in CUL3 or KLHL3. The phenotype of mice
generated to test the effects of WNK1 acidic motifs in vivo and how this may
contribute to FHHt are described below (see
“The role of
KS-WNK1 in FHHt”).

In vitro studies have revealed that the 57 amino acid deletion present
in CUL3-Δ9 leads to multiple effects that lead to abnormal CUL3 CRL
function. CUL3Δ474−477 also causes some of these
effects, despite the deletion of significantly fewer residues. This has made
understanding the in vivo mechanisms by which CUL3 mutations
cause FHHt more complicated than for KLHL3 and WNK mutations. A shared
characteristic of both CUL3 mutants is reduced interaction with a large protein
complex called the (Constitutive photomorphogenesis 9) COP9 signalosome, which
plays a critical role in mediating activity of not only CUL3 CRL, but activity
of all CRLs. Interactions with other regulators of the CUL3 CRL are also
impaired, although whether this is exclusively due to the effects of reduced
COP9 signalosome interaction or structural effects has not been determined. Of
note, structural modeling suggests that CUL3-Δ9 is more flexible than WT
CUL3(85). Whether the loss of 4 amino
acids in CUL3Δ474−477 results in significant structural
changes seems less likely but has not formally been determined. There may be
some minor differences in the mechanisms in the vasculature and kidney, but
ultimately CUL3 mutants lead to accumulation of CUL3-CRL substrates. The key
functional defects in the CRL that have been identified are (1) impaired cycling of CUL3 CRL assembly and
disassembly due to loss of interaction with the COP9 signalosome, (2) trapping of substrate adaptors, (3) CUL3-Δ9 autoubiquitination and
degradation, (4) impaired mutant CUL3
homo- and WT heterodimer formation, and (5) inappropriate adaptor degradation (Figure 8). In vivo it is
likely that a combination of these functional defects contributes to the
inability to ubiquitinate substrates. Evidence for each except impaired dimer
formation has been demonstrated from various mouse models, as will be described
below. Inappropriate adaptor degradation may be more relevant to renal effects
but whether it occurs in vivo remains controversial due to conflicting
findings.

CUL3 CRL complexes must cycle between inactive and active states to
function normally (Figure
9)(39, 85–87). This cycling allows recruitment of adaptors
and substrates to form the active CRL for substrate ubiquitination, followed
by dissociation of the complex, releasing the adaptor to permit formation of
a new CRL with another adaptor. In the inactive state, CUL3 is bound to the
RING ligase Ring-Box 1 (RBX1) and interacts with Cullin-associated
NEDD8-dissociated protein 1 (CAND1). Dissociation of CAND1 permits
recruitment of adaptor proteins and substrates and activation of the CRL
complex. Critical to the activation step is covalent attachment of a 9 KDa
ubiquitin-like protein, neuronal precursor cell-expressed developmentally
downregulated 8 (NEDD8) to CUL3, a process termed
“neddylation”. Neddylation induces a conformational change in
CUL3 that is propagated through the CRL, leading to substrate
ubiquitination. To cycle the CRL back to the inactive state, NEDD8 is
removed from CUL3, a process termed “deneddylation” that is
mediated by the COP9 signalosome.

The first indication that disruption of cycling between the inactive
and active states was disrupted in CUL3-Δ9 CRLs was demonstrated by
McCormick and colleagues, who found that CUL3-Δ9 immunoprecipitated
from transfected HEK293 cells was more neddylated than WT CUL3(39). It has since been shown by several
groups that CUL3-Δ9 displays significantly reduced interactions with
several components of the COP9 signalosome (CSN) including the subunit that
directly catalyzes deneddylation, CSN5 (also called JAB1)(17, 85–87), as well as
CSN1(17), CSN4(86), and CSN8(85). The absence of amino acids 403–459 leads to loss of
a 4-helix bundle immediately upstream of an alpha/beta fold (residues
461–581) that directly interacts with JAB1; the structural change
induced in CUL3-Δ9 is sufficient to disrupt JAB1 binding to this
region(87).
CUL3Δ474−477 has deletion of four residues in
the alpha/beta fold. It also displays reduced interactions with CSN1 and
CSN5, though to a lesser degree than for CUL3-Δ9(17). The result of loss of interaction with the
COP9 signalosome impairs deneddylation, locking both CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 in a
“hyperneddylated” state.

Hyperneddylation impairs dissociation of the CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 CRLs, impairing interaction with
CAND1 and hence assembly of a new CRL; this further disrupts CRL
cycling(17, 85, 86).
Inhibition of neddylation with the NEDD8-activating enzyme (NAE) inhibitor
MLN4924 did not restore the CUL3-Δ9-CAND1 interaction, suggesting a
structural defect contributes to the loss of CAND1 interaction(86). This conclusion is supported by
the observation that CUL3Δ474−477 displays only
partial loss of interaction with CAND1 whereas for CUL3-Δ9 the
interaction with CAND1 is almost completely ablated(17, 86).
This impairment of CRL cycling results in greater binding between
CUL3-Δ9 and many of its adaptors, as was demonstrated for KLHL3(39, 63, 85), relevant to
kidney, and for RhoBTB1 and Bacurd1(63), relevant to the vasculature. More extensive unbiased mass
spectrometry screens have revealed enhanced affinity for CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 with over 40 other adaptors(17, 86). Stable Isotope Labeling by Amino acids in Cell culture
(SILAC) coupled with pulse-chase and mass spectrometry in HEK293 cells
confirmed impaired exchange of substrate adaptors(86). CUL3Δ474−477
binding to adaptors appears to be even greater than that of
CUL3-Δ9(17). Therefore,
since CAND1 binding is relatively unimpaired for
CUL3Δ474−477, loss of CAND1 interaction may
play less a significant role in adaptor trapping. Trapping of adaptors is
likely to have consequences with respect to normal CUL3 CRL function within
cells. First, it will sequester adaptors making them unavailable for the
assembly of CRLs with WT CUL3. If a particular adaptor has a greater
affinity than another CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477, this may impact ubiquitination of
certain substrates due to greater trapping of the adaptor for those
substrates. However, this effect will be confounded by the presence of
relatively low levels of CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 due to autoubiquitination
(see “Autoubiquitination” below). Of note,
CUL3-mediated FHHt is only caused by dominant mutations, suggesting
CUL3-Δ9 and CUL3Δ474−477 are not able to
ubiquitinate substrates normally. This has been experimentally demonstrated
in mice, since similar to
Cul3−/−
mice,(88)
Cul3Δ9/Δ9
mice are non-viable(85, 89), and kidney-specific expression of
CUL3-Δ9 on a kidney-specific
Cul3−/−
background does not rescue the renal injury and polyuria phenotype (see
“CUL3 models of
FHHt” for more detail on kidney
phenotypes). Thus, CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 trap adaptors in CRLs with
impaired ubiquitination activity towards substrates, leading to substrate
accumulation.

While CUL3-Δ9 and CUL3Δ474−477
display reduced interaction with the COP9 signalosome leading to
hyperneddylation, interactions with other CRL regulators have been reported
to be enhanced. Both mutants display enhanced interactions with
glomulin(17, 86), which acts as an inhibitor of CRL activity
by blocking the E2-binding site of the RBX RING domain independently of
neddylation status(90). Enhanced
interaction with glomulin may be the direct cause of absence of normal
function for the CUL3-Δ9 CRL suggested by the non-viability of
Cul3Δ9/Δ9
mice. CUL3-Δ9 also displayed neddylation-dependent increased
interaction with the deubiquitinating enzyme USP25(86). Greater recruitment of USP25 to the CRL may
also contribute to loss of activity of CUL3-Δ9 CRLs by
deubiquitinating bound substrates. Indeed, compared with WT CUL3,
CUL3-Δ9 displays greater interaction with WNK4, yet is unable to
promote its degradation(39).

A key finding regarding the effects of CUL3-Δ9 on CRL
function was the observation by Schumacher and colleagues that it displays
autoubiquitination(85).
Autoubiquitination by the CRL may serve as a regulatory mechanism to promote
proteasomal degradation of CRL components, and in some E3 ubiquitin ligases
may serve as an activating mechanism(91). Using an in vitro ubiquitination assay, it was shown that
WT CUL3 autoubiquitinated a single lysine residue, whereas CUL3-Δ9
displayed autoubiquitination at multiple lysines. Consistent with targeting
it for proteasomal degradation, CUL3-Δ9 abundance is extremely low in
all mouse models expressing CUL3-Δ9 (see “CUL3 models of
FHHt”)(85, 89, 92–94). The structural changes resulting from the deletion of 57
amino acids were proposed to increase accessibility of lysines to this
aberrant autoubiquitination. However, CUL3Δ474−477,
which might be predicted to undergo minor structural changes compared with
CUL3-Δ9, also displays autoubiquitination at multiple sites(17).

The active CUL3 CRL is a dimer composed of two CUL3-adaptor protein
complexes that ubiquitinates one substrate molecule.(95, 96)
The possibility that CUL3-Δ9 displays disrupts CRL function by
interfering with dimerization was first explored by the Sigmund group
through immunoprecipitation studies in HEK293 cells(63). After pull-down, CUL3-Δ9 homodimers
could only be detected if the cells were treated with the proteasomal
inhibitor MG132 or the neddylation inhibitor MLN4924. CUL3-Δ9 formed
heterodimers with WT CUL3, but ability of each protein to pull the other
down was weaker than for WT-WT pull-downs, suggesting CUL3-Δ9
interacts relatively weakly with WT CUL3. A more direct and quantitative
assessment of interactions using bioluminescence resonance energy transfer
proximity assay showed that WT/Δ9 and Δ9/Δ9 dimers
tended to display lower binding affinity than WT/WT dimers, though the
differences were not statistically significant.(86) A previous study reported that preventing
neddylation of WT CUL3 by mutating the neddylated lysine (K712) does not
affect WT/WT dimerization(95),
suggesting that CUL3-Δ9 hyperneddylation interferes with its ability
to form dimers. This has not been formally tested by mutating this lysine in
CUL3-Δ9. CUL3Δ474−477 heterodimerized with
WT CUL3, but the relative strength of interaction compared with WT/WT dimers
was not determined, and CUL3Δ474−477
homodimerization was not assessed(17). These findings suggest that CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 may sequester WT CUL3 into
dysfunctional CRLs. However, given the low abundance of both mutants due to
autoubiquitination coupled with their weaker affinity for WT CUL3, it is
unclear how significantly this would affect formation of normally
functioning WT/WT homodimers.

In yeast, it has been reported that BTB adaptor proteins are
themselves substrates for CUL3 CRLs(97) Counteracting this effect, the COP9 signalosome has been
reported to cooperate with deubiquitinating enzymes to maintain stability of
labile CUL3 substrate adaptors(98).
Therefore, inappropriate degradation of substrate adaptors by CUL3-Δ9
and CUL3Δ474−477 might contribute to FHHt by
further reducing the available pool of adaptors to form not only mutant
CRLs, but also WT CUL3 CRLs. It is important to note that while the mutant
CUL3 CRL lacks normal function, CUL3-Δ9 can form a functional CRL
that displays higher ubiquitination activity as
demonstrated by ubiquitin-release assays in which CUL3-Δ9 hydrolyzed
E2~UB more efficiently than WT CUL3(85). This paradoxical ability of CUL3-Δ9 CRLs to display
higher ubiquitination activity despite loss-of-function towards non-adaptor
substrates is critical for proposed effects on inappropriate ubiquitination
and degradation of CUL3 adaptors. Consistent with the ability of CUL3 CRLS
to mediate adaptor degradation, the Ellison group demonstrated that in
transfected HEK293 cells, KLHL3 abundance was lower in cells overexpressing
WT CUL3, and this was related to higher WNK4 abundance.(39) Importantly, CUL3-Δ9 overexpression
led to a dramatic reduction in KLHL3 abundance, with a greater effect to
increase WNK4 abundance. These data are congruent with the finding that
CUL3-Δ9 promotes a greater degree of KLHL3 ubiquitination than WT
CUL3(85). Since deneddylation of
CUL3 by the COP9 signalosome contributes to stabilization of adaptors in
yeast(98), CUL3-Δ9
hyperneddylation may directly contribute to the ability of CUL3-Δ9 to
promote KLHL3 degradation. For example, hyperneddylation of CUL3 induced by
JAB1 siRNA knockdown in HEK293 cells promotes KLHL3 degradation(87). In contrast, two other in vitro
studies have suggested that CUL3-Δ9 has no effect on KLHL3
abundance(17, 86), nor does
CUL3Δ474−477(17). However, data from some mouse models suggest that both WT
CUL3 and CUL3-Δ9 promote KLHL3 degradation in vivo (see
“CUL3
models of FHHt”).

The ability of CUL3-Δ9 to aberrantly promote adaptor
degradation is unlikely to occur for all adaptors. For example, Ibeawuchi
and colleagues found that while RhoBTB1 was a substrate for WT CUL3,
CUL3-Δ9 displayed an impaired ability to promote RhoBTB1 degradation,
while neither WT CUL3 of CUL3-Δ9 affected Bacurd1 abundance(63). Abundance of KEAP1 was also shown
to be unaffected by CUL3-Δ9 both in vitro(87) and in vivo(99). Similar to their effects on KLHL3, both WT CUL3 and
CUL3-Δ9 promoted degradation of KLHL2 in vitro(100). However, while abundance of KLHL3 was lower
in a CUL3-Δ9 knockin mouse model, abundances of KLHL2, as well as
another adaptor, KLHL16, were unchanged compared with WT mice(89). This demonstrates the importance
of confirming in vitro findings obtained using animal models.

Several mouse models have been generated to determine the mechanisms through
which mutations in CUL3, WNK1,
WNK4, or KLHL3, cause FHHt. Many of these have
focused on effects in the kidney, and all have confirmed inappropriate activation of
NCC. Several have explored effects on other segments that may contribute to the
development of hypertension and electrolyte imbalance. Effects in the vasculature
have also been explored and are likely to contribute significantly to the
development of hypertension. In this section we will summarize these models, also
comparing them to phenotypes observed in knockout mice for each gene. The
characteristics of mice modeling WNK1-, WNK4-, or KLHL3-mediated FHHt are summarized
in Table 2, while those for CUL3-mediated
FHHt are summarized in Table 3. Some
potential contributing mechanisms, particularly in the vasculature, have not been
fully explored in these models.

Soon after FHHt-causing WNK1 mutations were
identified, characterization of mice with reduced WNK1 expression were
reported(101). These mice were
generated using a gene-trap approach, and the targeting vector disrupted the
first intron of the mouse Wnk1 gene. No live homozygous
mice were produced, and it was found that no homozygous embryos survived
past embryonic day 13, most likely due to effects on vascular development
(see “WNK kinases and
vascular function” above). Heterozygous mice,
were viable and compared with WT littermates, displayed a 49% reduction in
Wnk1 mRNA and lower blood pressure, but no electrolyte
abnormalities were found(101). Later
characterization of the same mouse strain found no difference in blood
pressure in heterozygotes compared with controls, even when dietary sodium
was restricted(60). These data
suggested that dominant WNK1 mutations do not cause FHHt
because of loss-of-function.

To determine how deletion of intron 1 of WNK1 leads
to FHHt, Delaloy and colleagues generated transgenic mice in which a BAC was
used to drive expression of the mouse Wnk1 gene, with a
β-galactosidase cassette included(102). Incorporation of loxP sites at the start and end of intron
1 permitted global deletion of this intron by crossing with a strain
expressing Cre recombinase ubiquitously. Activity of β-galactosidase
acted as a surrogate for the pattern and level of tissue expression of
Wnk1 in the two mouse strains. Deletion of intron 1 led
to ectopic β-galactosidase in several tissues including heart and
aorta. Within the kidney, there was not only higher β-galactosidase
activity; endogenous WNK1 exon 1 mRNA expression was also
higher, especially along the DCT. In vitro functional analysis identified
regulatory elements that likely impair interactions between the regulatory
elements of WNK1 and KS-WNK1. The same group then generated mice with global
deletion of intron 1 of Wnk1 (WNK1+/FHHt mice).
WNK1+/FHHt mice displayed an FHHt phenotype(103), with hyperkalemia and hyperchloremic
acidosis, and higher blood pressure that was lowered by hydrochlorothiazide
administration. Also consistent with hyperactivation of NCC, Western
blotting showed that WNK1+/FHHt mice displayed higher total and
phosphorylated NCC, and signal for phosphorylated SPAK was higher along DCT.
No effects on expression of ENaC subunits or the natriuretic and
anti-kaliuretic responses to amiloride were observed, suggesting effects on
ENaC do not contribute significantly to WNK1-mediated FHHt. However,
consistent with earlier in vitro studies showing inhibition of ROMK activity
by WNK1 due to increased endocytosis(104–106), ROMK
shifted from the apical membrane to a less intense and more diffuse
cytoplasmic distribution, specifically along DCT2 and CNT. Therefore, lower
ROMK may contribute to hyperkalemia in WNK1-mediated FHHt. Consistent with
the data described above showing dysregulated WNK1
expression with deletion of intron 1(102), quantitative RT-PCR analysis of microdissected tubules
from WNK1+/FHHt mice showed higher WNK1 mRNA expression
specifically along DCT and CNT, but there were no differences in KS-WNK1
expression in any segments(103).
Unexpectedly, since WNK4 appears to be the major regulator of NCC in vivo
((31) and see
“WNK4
models of FHHt”), when
WNK1+/FHHt mice were interbred with
WNK4−/− mice the resulting
WNK1+/FHHt-WNK4−/− mice still
displayed increase phosphorylated NCC and an FHHt phenotype(107). These data suggest that the inappropriately
higher WNK1 expression activates NCC independently of WNK4 in FHHt. In a
similar study, the contribution of SPAK to WNK1-mediated FHHt was determined
by interbreeding WNK1+/FHHt
mice with SPAKT243A/T243A mice, in which a threonine
phosphorylated by WNK kinases is mutated to alanine(108). This mutation limits WNK-mediated
activation of SPAK, a protein kinase that directly phosphorylates NCC.
SPAKT243A/T243A mice were previously reported to display
lower abundance of phosphorylated NCC, reduced blood pressure, but normal
plasma [K+](109). Double
mutant WNK1+/FHHt-SPAKT243A/T243A mice displayed a
phenotype in-between that of WNK1+/FHHt and
SPAKT243A/T243A mice, with normal blood pressure,
normokalemia, but mild metabolic hypochloremic acidosis. Abundances of total
and phosphorylated NCC were lower than in control mice, but significantly
higher than in SPAKT243A/T243A mice, suggesting that OSR1, which
also contributes to NCC phosphorylation in vivo(110), may be activated by WNK1+/FHHt
in the double mutant mice.

Dysregulation of KS-WNK1 is unlikely to play a role in WNK1-mediated
FHHt since its abundance was unchanged along the DCT in
WNK1+/FHHt mice(103). The exact physiological role of
KS-WNK1 along DCT remains uncertain. Early in vitro experiments suggested it
is a dominant-negative inhibitor of WNK1(111), which might be expected to inhibit NCC activity. An
inhibitory role for KS-WNK1 was supported by lower abundances of total and
phosphorylated NCC in mice overexpressing KS-WNK1(112), but higher abundances of both in
KS-WNK1−/− mice(112, 113). More recent data from Xenopus oocytes suggest
it is a potent activator of NCC(114)
that is required for the assembly of WNK signaling complexes called
“WNK bodies” in hypokalemic states to activate WNK signaling
and hence NCC(115, 116). It has been proposed that increased NCC
phosphorylation observed in KS-WNK1−/− mice may be
a secondary effect to counter K+ loss (see
“WNK kinases and NCC
activation”) since these mice are
normokalemic(117). Rather,
KS-WNK1 serves to permit rapid activation of NCC in response to extreme
alterations in K+ intake. This is supported by the observation
that on a K+-deficient diet the relative activation of NCC
(phosphorylated NCC/total NCC) is 50% lower in
KS-WNK1−/− mice than in controls, and NCC
inactivation is delayed in KS-WNK1−/− mice(117).

A recent study provides more direct in vivo evidence that KS-WNK1
may contribute to activation of NCC. As outlined above (see
“Identification of
FHHt-causing mutations”), patients with novel
mutations in the WNK1 gene, expressing both WNK1 and
KS-WNK1 from different promoters, were found to present with Type 4 renal
tubular acidosis featuring thiazide-sensitive hyperkalemic hyperchloremic
acidosis, but normotension(15). These
mutations are located within a region homologous to the acidic motif of
WNK4, and several homologous disease-causing mutations
were identified for each kinase (WNK1: D635 and Q636, WNK4: D564 and Q565).
In vitro studies (see above “WNK1 acidic motif mutations (Type 4 renal tubular
acidosis)”) suggested that these
WNK1 mutations affected KS-WNK1 but not WNK1, impairing
KS-WNK1 binding to KLHL3, and subsequent ubiquitination and protein
abundance. Attempts to generate mice bearing a D635E mutation homologous to
the human mutation were unsuccessful, but a mouse model with deletion of
E631 was generated(15). In vitro
analysis showed that this mutant, delE631 displayed the same properties in
vitro as D635E. Wnk1+/delE631
mice phenocopied the patient presentation and were normotensive even when
placed on a high salt diet. Phosphorylation of SPAK and NCC were higher than
in WT mice and immunofluorescence revealed large “WNK bodies”
(see “KLHL3 models of
FHHt” for discussion of WNK bodies versus
protein aggregates in FHHt) along DCT. There were no alterations ENaC
activity. In WT mice, ROMK abundance increased following amiloride
administration, but this response was absent in
Wnk1+/delE631 mice,
suggesting defective ROMK function. While an increase in phosphorylated
NKCC2 was also reported, this likely reflects cross-reactivity of the
antibody used with phosphorylated NCC, a known issue with this antibody when
studying mice on the C57BL/6 background(118, 119). In all forms
of FHHt, electrolyte abnormalities precede hypertension, suggesting
hypertension reflects more severe signaling defects. It is notable that the
equivalent mutations in WNK4 cause the full FHHt phenotype.
This suggests that defective KS-WNK1 is not sufficient to reach the
threshold of NCC activation needed, or it does not cause effects on the
vasculature required, to cause hypertension. Consistent with this, mice
homozygous for delE631 displayed higher blood pressure on a normal diet.

Early in vitro studies in Xenopus oocytes suggested
that WNK4 is an inhibitor of WNK1, and thus NCC activity(71, 72). A
transgenic mouse model in which WNK4 was overexpressed from a BAC had a
Gitelman syndrome-like phenotype (i.e. lower NCC activity), supporting this
hypothesis(120). However, since
Cl− directly binds to and inhibits WNK kinases(33), the in vitro data could reflect
experimental conditions that lead to a dominant-negative effect of WNK4.
Indeed, WNK4 is more sensitive to intracellular [Cl−] than
other WNK kinase(75). A more recent
study reported that two independently generated WNK4 transgenic mouse lines
with 2 or 30 copies of a WNK4 BAC display an FHHt phenotype with the high
copy number mice displaying a more severe phenotype. These mice also
displayed a dose-dependent effect of copy number on WNK4 abundance,
phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1, and phosphorylated NCC(41). The discrepancy between these different WNK4
transgenic lines is unclear, but the first study only reported 50% higher
WNK4 mRNA abundance without data for protein abundance(120). Later analysis of the same mice suggested
higher WNK4 protein abundance, but this was demonstrated by qualitative
immunofluorescence(40).
Furthermore, disruption of WNK4 in mice leads to a phenotype closely
resembling Gitelman syndrome with normal blood pressure that was lowered by
a low salt diet, higher plasma renin activity, lower plasma [K+]
and [Mg2+], and reduced phosphorylation of NCC(31, 121).
Abundance of phosphorylated SPAK was modestly lower(31, 121),
while WNK1 abundance was higher, but NCC phosphorylation was almost
completely ablated. The effects of insulin, angiotensin II, and dietary
K+ restriction to stimulate NCC phosphorylation were absent
in WNK4−/− mice. Together, the in vivo data largely
support the hypothesis that WNK4 plays a critical role in activation of NCC
in vivo.

The FHHt phenotypes and levels of WNK4 observed in mouse models
bearing WNK4 mutations are also consistent with a role for
WNK4 in NCC activation. The Lifton group generated transgenic mice
expressing WNK4 with a mutation in the acidic motif (Q562E) from a BAC, and
found an FHHt phenotype, with hyperkalemia exacerbated by a high
K+ diet(120).
Metabolic acidosis and hypercalciuria were also present. Interbreeding with
NCC knockout mice lowered blood pressure and plasma [K+] to WT
levels, suggesting activation of NCC is a primary mechanism of FHHt caused
by Q562E. Immunofluorescence revealed DCT hypertrophy that was also
corrected by interbreeding with NCC knockout mice. Notably, NCC
hyperactivation is often associated with DCT hypertrophy as seen in CA-SPAK
mice (19) or in WNK1+/FHHt
mice(103), whereas
pharmacological inhibition (122) or
genetic ablation(123) of NCC in mice
lowers DCT mass. Wnk4 mRNA abundance was 2.2-fold higher in
these mice than in WT mice, and in a later study, qualitative
immunofluorescence suggested higher WNK4 abundance(40). No differences were observed in ROMK protein
abundance or distribution even when Q562E mice were placed on a 5%
K+ diet. However, a later study in which ENaC and ROMK
activities were measured in microdissected DCT2/CNT suggest there may be
direct effects on ENaC-dependent K+ secretion in Q562E mice(124). Another study using Q562E mice
reported a significantly blunted anti-kaliuretic response to amiloride,
despite no difference in natriuretic response(125). A global knockin mouse bearing the acidic
motif mutation D561A was generated by the Uchida group(126). These mice displayed a complete FHHt
phenotype that was thiazide-reversible. While WNK4 abundance was reported to
be normal, this likely reflected an issue with the antibody used. Subsequent
analysis with a new antibody validated in WNK4−/−
mice demonstrated significantly higher WNK4 abundance in
Wnk4D561A/+ mice and
Wnk4D561A/D561A, with the
highest abundance in the mice with two mutated alleles(41). ROMK protein abundance and localization to
the apical membrane did not differ from that of controls in
Wnk4D561A/+ mice(126). Effects on other channels
relevant to hyperkalemia were likely secondary effects. Western blotting
showed that abundances of α-ENaC and β-ENaC subunits were
higher while that of γ-ENaC was lower, and microperfusion of isolated
collecting ducts suggested higher ENaC activity. Abundance of the
flow-induced maxi-K (also called BK) K+ channel was higher in
Wnk4D561A/+ mice. However,
effects of D561A on both ENaC and maxi-K was absent in
Wnk4D561A/+ mice
administered hydrochlorothiazide for 5 days suggesting they are secondary to
NCC activation in this model. Additional evidence suggested that
hyperkalemia in this model is a direct result of higher NCC activity rather
than direct effects on ENaC and/or ROMK. Injection of 0.9% saline increased
the Fractional Excretion of K+ (FEK) in WT mice but lowered it in
Wnk4D561A/+ mice, whereas
FEK was increased in both WT and
Wnk4D561A/+ mice
following injection of Na2SO4, which is not
transported by NCC. These data suggest higher NCC reabsorption reduces
distal Na+ delivery, leading to hyperkalemia. The requirement for
SPAK/OSR1 activation in WNK4D561A-mediated FHHt was demonstrated
by interbreeding with knockin mice carrying mutations that prevent
phosphorylation of SPAK (T243A) or OSR1 (T185A) by WNK kinases(127). Similar to findings described
above for WNK1+/FHHt-SPAKT243A/T243A mice(108), the FHHt phenotype was only
partially corrected in mice with two copies of the SPAK mutation
(Wnk4D561A/+/SPAKT243A/T243A
mice). In contrast, abundances of total and phosphorylated NCC were lower
than those in WT mice in WNK1+/FHHt-SPAKT243A/T243A
mice, but not in
Wnk4D561A/+/SPAKT243A/T243A.
This is consistent with WNK4 being the major activator of NCC in vivo.
Notably, a single copy of each SPAK/OSR1 mutation
(Wnk4D561A/+/SPAKT243A/OSR1T185A
mice) corrected the FHHt phenotype, and in
Wnk4D561A/+/SPAKT243A/T243A/OSR1T185A
mice the correction was exaggerated. These data suggest a role for OSR1 in
WNK4-dependent NCC activation. They also suggest relatively minor roles for
ENaC and ROMK in WNK4D561A-mediated FHHt, since both channels
been reported to be regulated by WNK kinases through kinase-independent
mechanisms not involving SPAK/OSR1.

In patients(128) or
mice(120) with the WNK Q562E
mutation, higher salt intake provoked greater hypercalciuria. Hypercalciuria
in FHHt has been attributed to reduced Na+/Ca2+ uptake
along the proximal tubule because of reduced proximal tubule Na+
reabsorption as a response to lower ECF volume and thus blood pressure(128). Inhibition of Ca2+
reabsorption along the distal tubule may also contribute. Along the DCT2/CNT
apical Ca2+ entry is mediated through transient receptor
potential cation channel subfamily V member 5 (TRPV5). An in vitro study
using the Xenopus oocyte expression system reported that WT
WNK4 or FHHt-causing WNK4 mutants stimulated TRPV5 activity similarly(129). However, while NCC inhibited the
stimulatory effect of both WT and mutant WNK4, the effect on mutant WNK4 was
greater. Therefore, in FHHt caused by WNK4 mutations, the higher NCC
activity may cause hypercalciuria by exerting a greater inhibitory effect on
TRPV5. Higher entry of apical Na+ through NCC has also been
proposed to promote basolateral Na+/Ca2+ exchange,
raising intracellular Ca2+ and reducing the drive for apical
entry of Ca2+(130). A
subsequent study using
Wnk4D561A/+ mice sought to
determine the contributing factors (131). In
Wnk4D561A/+ mice,
glomerular filtration of Ca2+, and serum levels of parathyroid
hormone and 1,25(OH)2D3 (important regulators of
Ca2+ homeostasis) were similar to those of control mice(131). Administration of the NKCC2
blocker furosemide revealed that
Wnk4D561A/+ mice
displayed a blunted increase in urinary Ca2+ excretion compared
to control mice, suggesting lower paracellular Ca2+ reabsorption
along the TAL contributes to hypercalciuria in this model. Protein abundance
of TRPV5 was not different in
Wnk4D561A/+ mice.
However, abundances of the related Ca2+ channel TRPV6 and the
calcium binding protein calbindin D28K, both expressed along the DCT2/CNT
and CCD(132, 133), were higher. These data suggest a
compensatory response to promote Ca2+ reabsorption downstream of
lower TAL reabsorption. However, TRPV5 activity or effects on proximal
tubule Ca2+ reabsorption were not directly determined, so further
studies are needed to fully establish the mechanisms involved. Furthermore,
the mechanisms of hypercalciuria in FHHt caused by WNK1,
CUL3, or KLHL3 have note been
explored.

The importance of [Cl−] binding in determining
WNK4 activity was demonstrated in knockin mice with mutations in two
residues essential for WNK4-Cl− interactions
(Wnk4L319F/L321F
mice)(134). In contrast to the
two WNK4 FHHt knockin mice generated to-date
(Wnk4D561A/+
and Wnk4Q562E/+ mice), abundance of WNK4 was not
affected by these mutations. Consistent with an inhibitory effect of
Cl− binding, abundances of phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1,
phosphorylated NCC, and total NCC, and natriuresis following acute thiazide
administration were higher compared with WT mice.
Wnk4L319F/L321F mice
displayed an FHHt phenotype. No differences in ROMK abundance or apical
abundance were observed, but ENaC activity was lower, as determined by the
acute natriuretic response to amiloride. This may be a direct inhibitory
effect on ENaC(135, 136) or may have been secondary to volume
expansion caused by higher NCC-mediated NaCl reabsorption and would be
expected to contribute to elevated plasma [K+]. While no effect
on NKCC2 phosphorylation was reported, the site examined (S130) is
phosphorylated by SPAK/OSR1 independent mechanisms including PKA and
AMPK(137, 138). Under physiological conditions higher
plasma [K+] leads to lower K+ efflux along the DCT
through Kir4.1/5.1 and thus higher intracellular [Cl−]
(see “WNK kinases and
NCC activation”). Despite this, WNK4 activity
is sustained in FHHt, presumably WNK4 abundance is too high to be saturated
by the available Cl−.

Further evidence that inappropriate activation of SPAK, which is
activated by WNK-mediated phosphorylation, is central to the development of
FHHt was provided using an elegant mouse model generated by the Welling
(19). In this model a
constitutively active phosphomimetic SPAK mutant
(SpakT243E/S383D, referred to as CA-SPAK) is
expressed specifically along the DCT1 following Cre-mediated recombination
(using parvalbumin-Cre). CA-SPAK displayed higher abundances of total and
phosphorylated NCC, with expansion of DCT1, and an FHHt phenotype;
administration of hydrochlorothiazide lowered blood pressure to those seen
in WT mice rapidly, by 12 hours. One key finding from study of these mice
was that additional mechanisms may contribute to hyperkalemia beyond
increased delivery of Na+ to downstream K+-secreting
segments (CNT and CCD). While hydrochlorothiazide also increased urinary
K+ excretion and corrected hyperkalemia in CA-SPAK mice, this
took significantly longer than normalization of blood pressure (3 days
versus 12 hours). Morphological analysis revealed that CNT mass was lower in
CA-SPAK mice compared with WT mice, which likely lowers capacity for
K+-secretion, exacerbating hyperkalemia. Indeed, the lower
CNT mass was associated with a lower blunted response to amiloride, and
lower ROMK abundances and apical localization. Similar to
Wnk4D561A/+ mice,
abundances of maxi-K subunits were higher in CA-SPAK mice, likely to
counteract elevated plasma [K+]. A critical finding was that
after 3 days of hydrochlorothiazide administration, ROMK abundance and CNT
mass in CA-SPAK mice were similar to those of WT mice. These data suggest
that in CA-SPAK mice, inhibition of downstream K+ secretion and
thus hyperkalemia, is completely driven by higher NCC activity along DCT1.
The underlying mechanisms leading to this “remodeling” in the
DCT1 and downstream K+-secreting segments are unclear, but along
DCT1 may be related to higher NaCl reabsorption itself; loss of CNT mass
might be driven by lower Na+ delivery and entry or by paracrine
factors from the DCT1. One limitation of this model is that CA-SPAK mice are
SPAK−/− in all cell types other than DCT1 which
may confound some of the effects of hydrochlorothiazide. Note that no
activating SPAK (encoded by STK39) mutations like CA-SPAK
have been identified in humans, which could reflect the extremely low
probability of an activating mutation occurring at a single codon, or
lethality of global constitutive SPAK activity.

Hyperkalemia can be a direct cause of metabolic acidosis primarily
through effects on renal ammonia metabolism and excretion(139), but whether metabolic acidosis is directly
caused by mutations in WNK1, WNK4,
CUL3, or KLHL3 or is secondary to
hyperkalemia in FHHt is controversial. The Eladari group examined Q562E
mice, expressing the WNK4 Q562E mutation from a transgene, and proposed that
Q562E exerts a direct effect that causes metabolic acidosis(125). The mechanism hinges on activity of the
Cl−/HCO3− exchanger
pendrin in β-intercalated cells along the cortical collecting duct.
Pendrin has been proposed to work in conjunction with the
Na+-dependent
Cl−/2HCO3− exchanger
(NDCBE) to drive thiazide-sensitive NaCl reabsorption(140). Microperfusion of CCDs from Q562E mice but
not WT mice displayed thiazide-sensitive NaCl reabsorption(125). A low K+ diet did not correct
metabolic acidosis in Q562E mice despite correction of hyperkalemia,
suggesting Q562E WNK4 directly affects plasma
HCO3−. Acid loading exacerbated acidosis in
Q562E mice, but urine pH decreased and ammonium excretion increased,
suggesting that proton secretion and ammonium metabolism and transport are
normal in Q562E mice. Similar effects were seen in
WNK1+/FHHt mice
suggesting a common mechanism leads to metabolic acidosis in both models.
Pendrin activity was higher in Q562E mice, and deletion of pendrin by
interbreeding Q562E mice with global pendrin knockout mice
(Q562E/pendrin−/−) corrected both hyperkalemia
and metabolic acidosis. The anti-kaliuretic response to amiloride was higher
in Q562E/pendrin−/− mice than in Q562E suggesting
hyperkalemia is corrected in these mice by increased ENaC-dependent
K+ secretion. Lower HCO3−
secretion in Q562E/pendrin−/− mice suggests that in
Q562E mice, metabolic acidosis is due to excessive
HCO3− secretion along the CCD. However, in
a cohort of 18 human patients with FHHt caused by the WNK4 Q565E mutant,
renal ammonium excretion was not lower than in unaffected subjects,
suggesting a proximal tubule origin(141).

CA-SPAK mice have provided an alternative mechanism underlying
hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis in FHHt(142). As outlined in “SPAK
hyperactivation in FHHt”, lower CNT mass may contribute to
hyperkalemia in this mode. Hydrochlorothiazide administration and dietary
K+ restriction, both of which corrected hyperkalemia, also
corrected metabolic acidosis in CA-SPAK mice. Renal ammonia excretion and
metabolism play a central role in acid-base homeostasis, with ammonia
excretion serving as a primary means of H+ excretion. Compared
with WT mice, CA-SPAK mice displayed lower expression of the
ammonia-generating enzymes phosphate-dependent glutaminase and
phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase and overexpression of the
ammonia-recycling enzyme glutamine synthetase along the proximal tubule.
CA-SPAK mice also displayed lower abundance of the ammonia transporter
Rhesus glycoprotein Rhcg along the outer medullary collecting duct. These
defects led to lower urinary ammonia excretion. Importantly, the effects of
CA-SPAK on expression of genes involved in ammonia metabolism and secretion
were corrected by both hydrochlorothiazide administration, which also
increased urinary ammonia excretion. Thus, metabolic acidosis in FHHt occurs
due to secondary effects of hyperkalemia on ammonia metabolism and/or
secretion along the proximal tubule and collecting duct.

The findings from CA-SPAK mice suggest that isolated activation of
NCC is sufficient to (1) cause hyperkalemia by remodeling the CNT and
altering Na+ delivery to the CNT, and (2) cause metabolic
acidosis via a secondary effect of hyperkalemia to alter ammonium metabolism
and excretion. In contrast, hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis in Q562E
mice (and perhaps WNK1+/FHHt
mice that were not as extensively studied in (125)) suggest a mechanism involving direct
effects on the CNT and CCD. One difference between CA-SPAK mice and Q562E
mice is that CNT mass is more dramatically reduced in CA-SPAK mice. This
suggests CNT remodeling does not contribute to hyperkalemia in Q562E mice.
However, CNT mass is significantly lower in Q562E mice, and this may
contribute to lower ENaC and ROMK in DCT2/CNT this model (124). Furthermore, it was not determined whether
deletion of pendrin altered CNT mass in
Q562E/pendrin−/− mice. On the other hand, the
CA-SPAK mouse circumvents the higher abundance of WNK kinases observed in
bona fide models of FHHt caused by mutations in Wnk1,
Wnk4, Cul3, or
Klhl3.

Several in vitro studies suggest effects of both WT and mutant WNK
kinases on ENaC, ROMK, and maxi-K channels. Both WT WNK4 and WNK4 Q562E
enhance endocytosis of ROMK through a mechanism independent of kinase
activity(143). WNK1 also
promotes ROMK endocytosis in conjunction with the clathrin adaptor molecule
autosomal recessive hypercholesterolemia (ARH)(143), but the requirement for WNK1 kinase
activity was not determined. Reduced apical ROMK would lower K+
secretion, contributing to hyperkalemia. Effects of WNK1 and WNK4 on ENaC
and BK/maxi-K channels demonstrated in vitro may be less relevant to
hyperkalemia since they would be expected to promote K+
secretion. WT WNK4 was shown to inhibit ENaC activity(135) by promoting its internalization(144), whereas WNK4 Q562E lacks this
effect(135). WNK1 also promotes
activation of ENaC independently of kinase activity(145) by acting as a scaffold that increases
SGK1-dependent promotion of insertion of ENaC into the apical membrane(146). Thus, WNK kinase mutations that
increase WNK1 or WNK4 expression would be predicted to stimulate ENaC,
increasing the drive for K+ secretion through ROMK. Similarly,
WNK1 increased BK/maxi-K channel activity in vitro, and intercalated
cell-specific WNK1 knockout mice displayed reduced BK/maxi-K knockout
channel activity and higher plasma [K+] following a high
K+ diet(147). Many of
these effects mediated by WNK1 or WNK4 are kinase-independent and would be
absent from CA-SPAK mice since they would not occur through increased SPAK
activation. Therefore, the precise mechanisms underlying both hyperkalemia
and metabolic acidosis in FHHt have not been completely resolved.

To recap the major findings of the mouse models described thus far,
mutations in WNK kinases increase NCC phosphorylation, and thus activity, by
increasing phosphorylation of SPAK and OSR1, which both directly
phosphorylate NCC. The FHHt phenotype is absent with loss of NCC activity
(NCC−/− background or thiazide administration).
Furthermore, the FHHt phenotype is absent when SPAK/OSR1 cannot be
activated. These observations show that hyperactivation of the
WNK-SPAK/OSR1-NCC pathway is necessary and sufficient to cause FHHt. The
molecular mechanisms involve over-expression of WNK1 along the DCT due to
loss of intron-mediated suppression, and increased WNK4 abundance along DCT
due to loss of interaction with KLHL3 and subsequent ubiquitination.
However, other mechanisms may exist for WNK4 since not all mutations have
been tested in vivo. Overall, while in vitro suggest effects of WNK1 and
WNK4 on on ENaC, ROMK, and BK/maxi-K channels, some of these might promote
loss of K+, and in vivo data from several models suggest effects
on these channels are largely secondary to increased NCC activity. Rather,
increased delivery of Na+ to, and reduced mass of, downstream
K+-secreting segments cause hyperkalemia. The development of
metabolic acidosis is also likely to be secondary, due to effects of
hyperkalemia on ammonia metabolism and secretion. However, studies using
WNK4 Q562E and WNK1+/FHHt mice
suggest that there might be more direct effects on CNT and CCD that promote
excessive K+ and HCO3− secretion.
Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms involved.

While over 50 FHHt-causing mutations in KLHL3 have
been identified, only two relevant mouse models have been generated to date.
KLHL3-mediated FHHt is caused by both dominant and recessive mutations that
can occur in the BTB domain that interacts with CUL3, the BACK domain, which
may participate in formation of the full CUL3-KLHL3-RING E3 ubiquitin
ligase, or in the kelch domains that form the interface for interactions
with substrates to recruit them for ubiquitination(2, 9).
While the functional effects of each mutation have not been extensively
tested, they are largely likely to impair assembly or disassembly of the E3
ubiquitin ligase. As outlined above (see “Physiological significance of CUL3-KLHL3
mediated WNK kinase ubiquitination”),
phosphorylation at S433 appears to be physiologically regulated, and
prevents WNK4 binding under conditions where NCC activation is required
(volume depletion and hypokalemia). However, no phosphomimetic mutations
predicted to increase WNK4 abundance have been identified in FHHt patients.
This suggests the FHHt-causing mutations S433N and S433G exert similar but
distinct structural effects to impair WNK4 binding.

The Uchida group generated knockin mice carrying a dominant
FHHt-causing mutation in R528 (R528H), located in the fifth KELCH
domain(148). Both mice
heterozygous (KLHL3R528/+) and homozygous
(KLHL3R528/R528) for this mutation (KLHL3R528/+)
were characterized. KLHL3R528/+ and KLHL3R528/R528
mice displayed the full FHHt phenotype, but the phenotype was not
exacerbated by the presence of two mutant alleles. Protein abundances, but
not mRNA abundances, of WNK1 and WNK4 in kidney were higher compared with
controls in both genotypes. Abundances of phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1, and
phosphorylated and total NCC were also higher. Of note, while WNK1, WNK4,
and phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1 were significantly higher in
KLHL3R528/R528 mice compared with KLHL3R528/+
mice, this did not translate to a larger effect on abundances of total and
phosphorylated NCC, suggesting a ceiling for NCC hyperactivation in
R528H-mediated FHHt. A KLHL3R528/+ mouse independently generated
by the Gamba group largely confirmed these findings(149). KLHL3R528/+ mice also displayed
higher abundances of the cleaved forms of α- and γ-ENaC, but
there were no changes in ROMK abundance, despite the finding that unlike WT
KLHL3, R528H KLHL3 did not promote internalization of ROMK from the plasma
membrane in COS-7 cells(40). However,
in R528 knockin mice generated by the Gamba group, abundances of both
uncleaved and cleaved forms of α-ENaC were lower in
KLHL3R528/+ and in KLHL3R528/R528 mice (149). Thus, as with WNK kinase models
of FHHt, effects on NCC are likely the primary cause of KLHL3-mediated
FHHt.

To gain a better understanding of how KLHL3 mutations affect
WNK-SPAK/OSR1 signaling, the Uchida group interbred their
KLHL3R528/+ mice with WNK4−/− mice
to generate WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/+ and
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/R528 mice(150). For both genotypes, abundances
of phosphorylated and total NCC were dramatically lower than those in
control mice, and similar to those seen in WNK4−/−
mice. This was despite abundances of WNK1 and phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1 being
higher in both WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/+ and
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/R528 mice compared
with WT mice. However, in both strains, abundances of WNK1 and
phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1 were lower than when WNK4 was not disrupted. Thus,
WNK4 is required for the development of FHHt in KLHL3R528/+ mice.
WNK1 abundance was higher in
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/+ mice than in
KLHL3R528/+ mice but WNK4 disruption did not further increase
WNK1 abundance in WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/R528
mice. These data contrast with what is observed when WNK4 is disrupted in
WNK1+/FHHt mice(107),
in which WNK1 is increased by deletion of intron 1.
WNK1+/FHHt-WNK4−/−
mice retain the FHHt phenotype, with higher abundance of NCC
phosphorylation. One possibility is differential effects of the R528 on WNK1
and KS-WNK1. As outlined above, KS-WNK1 is important for the formation of
WNK bodies required for SPAK/OSR1 activation (see
“The
role of KS-WNK1 in FHHt”),
and both dramatically higher or lower KS-WNK1 levels might interfere with
WNK body formation. Indeed, immunofluorescence revealed larger
p-SPAK-containing puncta in kidney sections from KLHL3R528/+ mice
than in sections from
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/+ mice, though
this may reflect the dramatic difference in pSPAK abundance between the two.
KS-WNK1 abundance was not assessed in
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/+ or in
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/R528 mice. The
Gamba group proposed a plausible explanation for the discrepant findings in
WNK1+/FHHt-WNK4−/−
(retained FHHt phenotype) versus
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/R528 mice (loss of
FHHt phenotype)(149). WNK1 is likely
not normally expressed at high levels along DCT since
WNK4−/− mice display a Gitelman syndrome-like
phenotype(31, 121), and no active WNK (detected using a pan
anti-WNK phospho T-loop antibody) is expressed in
WNK4−/− WNK bodies(116). In
WNK1+/FHHt-WNK4−/−
mice, WNK1 can override the loss of WNK4 since WNK1 is now expressed where
it is normally absent, and WNK1 is more active even if expressed at lower
abundance since it is relatively Cl−-insensitive. Higher
renal WNK1 abundance in
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/+ and
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/R528 mice could be
attributed to segments other than DCT. Therefore, higher WNK1 is not able to
maintain NCC phosphorylation in these models. Further evidence that WNK1
abundance must be higher in non-DCT segments comes from the observation that
NCC phosphorylation was almost absent despite abundances of phosphorylated
SPAK and OSR1 in WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/+ and
WNK4−/−/KLHL3R528/R528 mice being
higher than those in WT mice(150).
CA-SPAK mice, with isolated activation of SPAK along DCT1, independent of
WNK4-mediated phosphorylation, display higher NCC phosphorylation and an
FHHt phenotype(19). The Gamba group
provided some additional important insights into KLHL3-mediated FHHt using
their R528 model. KS-WNK1 protein abundance was significantly higher in
KLHL3R528/R528 mice(149) confirming an earlier study showing that KS-WNK1 is an
important target of CUL3-KLHL3-mediated ubiquitination(15). Furthermore, similar to
WNK1+/FHHt-WNK4−/−
mice, KLHL3R528/R528/KS-WNK1−/− mice
still retain the FHHt phenotype(149). This last finding is difficult to reconcile with the emerging
idea that KS-WNK1 is critical for the assembly of WNK bodies required WNK1/4
mediated SPAK/OSR1 phosphorylation. The Gamba group did not determine
whether WNK4 bodies are still present in
KLHL3R528/R528/KS-WNK1−/− mice.
However, even in the absence of WNK bodies, WNK4 may be sufficiently high to
bypass the requirement for these structures to activate SPAK/OSR1.
Furthermore, it has not been determined whether the large cytosolic puncta
containing WNK4 and phosphorylated SPAK in DCT of FHHt models are true WNK
bodies, or simply aggregates formed due to accumulation of large amounts of
WNK4.

Mechanistic understanding of how recessive mutations in
KLHL3 cause FHHt is much simpler than those for
dominant mutations since many lead to frameshifts, premature stop codons,
and mis-splicing, all of which prevent expression of a functional KLHL3
protein. Mutations in coding regions are also likely to cause complete loss
of KLHL3 interactions with CUL3 of WNK4. Thus, while no mouse models
specifically expressing recessive mutations have been generated, the
phenotype of constitutive global KLHL3−/− mice
generated by the Uchida group(151)
likely recapitulates the majority of recessive mutations. Intriguingly,
deletion of KLHL3 increases abundances of WNK1 and WNK4 exclusively in the
kidney but not in other tissues, which displayed significantly higher
abundances of both. These data suggest that other unidentified factors
contribute to CUL3/KLHL3-mediated WNK kinase degradation in kidney.
KLHL3−/− mice displayed the metabolic defects
of FHHt (hyperkalemia, hyperchloremia, and metabolic acidosis), but blood
pressure was only significantly higher than that of WT mice after 3 days on
a high salt diet. Abundances of phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1, and total and
phosphorylated NCC were dramatically elevated. An independently generated
inducible renal tubule-specific KLHL3−/− mouse
model showed accumulation of WNK4 and phosphorylated SPAK in large puncta
along DCT1 and DCT2/CNT, but not along TAL(94). Furthermore, phosphorylation of NKCC2 or responsiveness to
furosemide did not differ from controls in this inducible model. Abundances
of WNK kinases, phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1, and total and phosphorylated NCC
in KLHL3−/− mice were elevated to levels higher
than those in KLHL3R528/+ mouse kidney(151). This is consistent with the observation
that in human subjects, recessive KLHL3 mutations cause a
more severe FHHt phenotype than dominant KLHL3
mutations(2, 9). Effects on ENaC or ROMK have not been
examined in KLHL3−/− mice but may contribute to the
phenotype given expression of KLHL3 in calbindin-positive DCT2/CNT
cells(94).

The precise in vivo mechanisms underlying FHHt mediated by mutations
in CUL3 have been more challenging to elucidate. As
outlined above (see “CUL3
mutations exert multiple effects on CRL
function”), CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 exert multiple effects on the CRL
including (1) mutant CUL3 hyperneddylation due to loss of interaction with
JAB1 that impairs cycling of CRL assembly and disassembly, (2) increased
binding to multiple CUL3 adaptors, leading to adaptor trapping (3)
autoubiquitination and subsequent degradation of the mutant CUL3, (4)
impaired dimerization with itself and with WT CUL3, and (5) inappropriate
degradation of substrate adaptors including KLHL3. All of these effects
would be predicted to impair the availability and/or ability of CUL3-KLHL3
complexes to bind and ubiquitinate WNK4, but which are relevant in vivo
remains controversial.

The first mouse model that explored the function of CUL3 in kidney
was an inducible renal tubule-specific CUL3 knockout(39). It was hypothesized that loss of CUL3 would
phenocopy FHHt due to loss of CUL3-KLHL3-mediated WNK4 ubiquitination. While
these mice had higher levels of WNK1, WNK4, and phosphorylated NCC, they did
not display an FHHt phenotype due to additional effects of CUL3 deletion
along the entire renal tubule. These effects include extensive renal injury
that may result from dysregulation of the cell cycle(152), and polyuria due to loss of the
vasopressin-regulated water channel aquaporin 2(153), expressed along the collecting duct. These
data suggest that FHHt is not due to complete loss of CUL3 activity in the
kidney, consistent with the embryonic lethal phenotype of constitutive
global CUL3−/− mice (88).

The Kurz group used a knock-in approach to generate the first mouse
model expressing CUL3-Δ9 by deleting the entirety of exon 9(85). CUL3WT/Δ9 mice
were studied since no CUL3Δ9/Δ9 progeny were
produced, indicative of embryonic lethality like global
CUL3−/− mice. CUL3WT/Δ9 mice
recapitulated the FHHt phenotype, with hyperkalemia, hyperchloremia, and
metabolic acidosis, and higher blood pressure compared with WT mice. Higher
plasma [Mg2+] was also reported, but this is not typically
observed in FHHt, and a study in CA-SPAK mice suggest that expression of the
Mg2+ channel TRPM6 that is responsible for Mg2+
reabsorption along the DCT is unchanged despite DCT hypertrophy(154). As would be expected, abundances
of WNK4, phosphorylated SPAK, and total and phosphorylated NCC were all
higher in CUL3WT/Δ9 mice(85). WNK4 and SPAK localized to puncta in
CUL3WT/Δ9 mice. Effects on ENaC, ROMK, or other
channels/transporters were not assessed. This study also provided evidence
that CUL3-Δ9 autoubiquitination occurs in vivo, since total CUL3
abundance in kidney from CUL3WT/Δ9 mice was about half
that of WT mice. No difference in KLHL3 abundance was observed between
CUL3WT/Δ9 mice and WT mice, suggesting that
inappropriate KLHL3 degradation does not contribute to FHHt. Instead, it was
hypothesized that haploinsufficiency of functional CUL3 due to
CUL3-Δ9 autoubiquitination and proteasomal degradation causes FHHt.
However, efforts by the Uchida group to generate mice expressing
CUL3-Δ9 by making splice-site mutations rather than deleting exon 9
itself suggested that CUL3 haploinsufficiency does not cause FHHt, and other
effects of CUL3-Δ9 protein are required(155). Sequence alignment of the human and mouse
CUL3 genes showed that it was highly conserved, and two FHHt-causing splice
site mutations within intron 8, c.1207−1G>A (G(−1)A)
and c.1207−6T>G, were selected for targeted mutagenesis.
However, the generated mutant mice did not display an FHHt phenotype, with
unaltered protein abundances of all components of the WNK-SPAK/OSR1-NCC
signaling pathway. These data suggest that CUL3-Δ9 protein was not
generated. Mis-splicing of exon 9 may not have occurred in the mouse model
since the DNA sequence of the 3’ end of intron 8 is not completely
conserved between mouse and human. However, CUL3 protein abundance was about
25% lower in kidneys from these mice compared with WT mice. These data
suggest haploinsufficiency does not cause FHHt, though one caveat is that a
25% reduction in CUL3 may not have been sufficient to allow WNK4
accumulation and thus increased NCC phosphorylation. More direct evidence in
support of this came from characterization of mice with inducible Cre
recombinase-mediated inactivation of one CUL3 allele along the renal
tubule(93). Tubule-specific
CUL3+/− mice were phenotypically indistinguishable
from uninduced control CUL3+/fl mice, with no FHHt phenotype and
no changes in protein abundances of WNK4, and total or phosphorylated
NCC.

CUL3+/− mice that expressed CUL3-Δ9
following Cre-mediated activation of a transgene were also generated(93). Consistent with the findings of
the Uchida group, CUL3+/− mice had 50% of the control CUL3
levels in the kidney and had no apparent FHHt phenotype. In contrast,
CUL3+/−/Δ9 mice displayed an FHHt-like
phenotype (higher blood pressure and higher plasma [K+] that was
corrected by administration of hydrochlorothiazide) with WNK4 accumulation,
and higher abundances of total and phosphorylated NCC. While the data were
more variable, abundances of WNK4 and phosphorylated NCC were also higher in
CUL3+/+/Δ9, consistent with CUL3-Δ9 possessing
additional effects beyond autodegradation. Similar to studies in FHHt models
with altered WNK/SPAK signaling (see
“Summary
of FHHt models affecting WNK-SPAK/OSR1
signaling”),
CUL3+/−/Δ9 mice displayed a lower natriuretic
response to amiloride, though as in those models, this may reflect a
secondary effect on ENaC. This study also suggested that CUL3-Δ9
expression leads to higher phosphorylation of NKCC2, and this was proposed
to explain the more severe phenotype seen with this mutation compared with
mutations in KLHL3, WNK1, and
WNK4. However, more recent studies have shown that the
anti-phosphorylated NKCC2 antibody used in this study cross-reacts with
phosphorylated NCC in mice on the C57BL/6 background (the background of
CUL3+/−/Δ9 mice)(118, 119). Reassessment of abundance of phosphorylated NKCC2 using a
newly generated antibody specific for the C57BL/6 background revealed no
difference in abundance of phosphorylated NKCC2 between WT and
CUL3+/−/Δ9 mice(118). There was also no difference in KLHL3
abundance between WT and CUL3+/−/Δ9 mice. Another
knock-in model in which CUL3-Δ9 was ubiquitously expressed by
Cre-recombinase-mediated deletion of exon 9 (pgk-CUL3-Δ9 mice)(92) also recapitulated FHHt, with
greater abundances of WNK, phosphorylated SPAK/OSR1, and total and
phosphorylated NCC in kidney. Consistent with autoubiquitination, this model
also confirmed CUL3-Δ9. Abundance of KLHL3 in kidney was also not
different from controls.

The data described thus far suggest that CUL3-Δ9
autoubiquitination leads to lower CUL3-Δ9 abundance in vivo, but the
resulting CUL3 haploinsufficiency is not sufficient to cause FHHt (since
CUL3+/− mice do not display FHHt). They also suggest
that inappropriate degradation of KLHL3 does not contribute to the FHHt
phenotype. However, other studies support a role for KLHL3 degradation.
These discrepant findings may reflect lack of specificity for the anti-KLHL3
antibodies used in the non-supportive studies. In initial immunolocalization
studies, the Ellison group reported that KLHL3 expression is high along
TAL(39), as did the Kurz group
using a different anti-KLHL3 antibody that was only validated in transfected
cells(85). In pgk-CUL3-Δ9
mice, the antibody used detected signal above the 75kDa marker, at around
90kDa(92), yet the predicted
molecular weight of KLHL3 is 65kDa. The Uchida group validated an anti-KLHL3
for Western blotting using kidney from constitutive
KLHL3−/− mice, detecting a band between 50kDa
and 75kDa, consistent with the predicted molecular weight of 65KDa(151). Specificity of this antibody for
Western blotting and immunolocalization in kidney was further confirmed in
an independently generated renal tubule-specific
Klhl3−/− mouse model(94). Using this antibody, no
significant KLHL3 expression was found in TAL (NKCC2 co-localization), but
high expression was observed along the DCT (NCC co-localization)(94). Thus, it is likely that the
antibody used in non-supportive studies were not completely specific for
KLHL3. Signal detected along TAL by immunolocalization using antibodies with
uncertain specificity could be KLHL2, which is highly expressed in the renal
medulla(156).

Data generated using this anti-KLHL3 antibody validated on kidney
from Klhl3−/− mice indeed support
a contribution of inappropriate KLHL3 degradation to WNK4 accumulation,
higher NCC phosphorylation, and the development FHHt. Similar to the Kurz
group(85), the Uchida group used
a knock-in approach to delete the entire exon 9
(CUL3wt/Δex9 mice)(89). Consistent with the Kurz findings, these mice displayed
lower total CUL3, with the band corresponding to CUL3-Δ9 much less
abundant than that of WT CUL3. These CUL3wt/Δex9 mice also
displayed an FHHt phenotype. However, in contrast to the Kurz group
findings, using the
Klhl3−/−-validated antibody KLHL3
abundance was about 35% of that seen in WT mice, while in brain it was about
20% of WT levels(89). Reanalysis of
renal tubule-specific CUL3+/−/Δ9 mice using this
validated antibody found that KLHL3 was about 50% lower than in
controls(94). In both of these
studies, there were no differences in KLHL3 mRNA, suggesting that an effect
on protein degradation is likely, consistent with higher ubiquitination of
KLHL3 in the presence of CUL3-Δ9 in vitro(39). In further support of CUL3-mediated
degradation of KLHL3, KLHL3 abundance was strikingly elevated in inducible
tubule-specific CUL3−/− mice, and simultaneous
induction of CUL3-Δ9 (CUL3−/−/Δ9
mice) prevented accumulation of KLHL3(94). A consistent finding in these studies of
CUL3wt/Δex9 and CUL3+/−/Δ9
mice is that abundances of both CUL3 and KLHL3 are about half of those in WT
kidney. This led the McCormick group to hypothesize that both effects, lower
total CUL3 and KLHL3, contribute to hyperactivation of NCC in FHHt. To test
this hypothesis inducible renal-specific
CUL3+/−/KLHL3+/− mice, without
expression of CUL3-Δ9, were generated(94). These mice with lower abundances of CUL3 and KLHL3 of WT
mice largely recapitulated the FHHt phenotype. Abundances of WNK4, total
NCC, and phosphorylated NCC were higher than in WT mice, and
immunofluorescence showed aggregates containing phosphorylated SPAK. Plasma
[K+] was significantly higher, but a high salt/low
K+ diet was needed to maximally activate NCC and increase
higher blood pressure to a greater degree than in controls. This likely
reflects the observation that KLHL3 abundance was only about 30% lower in
CUL3+/−/KLHL3+/− mice due to
incomplete induction, yet this relatively small effect was sufficient to
increase WNK4 abundance and NCC phosphorylation. Therefore, in the context
of CUL3-Δ9 expression and the resulting CUL3 haploinsufficiency, even
a relatively small reduction in KLHL3 abundance would be sufficient to cause
FHHt.

Many CUL3 substrate adaptors are expressed in kidney at varying
levels, in some cases at lower levels than KLHL3(157), and many of these have been shown to bind
more tightly to CUL3-Δ9(17,
86). Therefore, more widespread
effects of CUL3-Δ9 along the renal tubule might be expected if other
CRLs were significantly affected given the severe phenotype of
tubule-specific CUL3−/− mice. However, the renal
effects of CUL3-Δ9 are highly specific to the DCT. There are likely
to be other effects of CUL3-Δ9 in kidney, but these are likely
relatively minor compared to the effects on NCC activation which is readily
observed by the manifestation of FHHt. For example, in
CUL3+/−/Δ9 mice mild effects on the function of
KEAP1-containing CRLs were observed resulting in increased activity of
NRF2(99), but without obvious
phenotypic consequences. This effect was unrelated to effects on KEAP1
abundance, which is unaffected by CUL3-Δ9(89, 99).
Furthermore, abundances of two other CUL3 substrate adaptors (KLHL2 and
KLHL16) in kidney and brain were unaffected by CUL3-Δ9 in a study
that showed lower KLHL3 abundance in both tissues(89). Abundances and function of other substrate
adaptors may also be affected by CUL3-Δ9 kidney, but this remains to
be determined. Overall, the in vivo data from the Uchida and McCormick
groups suggest that the combined reduction in CUL3 and KLHL3 caused by
CUL3-Δ9 contributes to FHHt. This is a plausible mechanism to explain
why CUL3-Δ9 exerts a highly specific effect on the DCT. This
mechanism does not exclude a contribution of KLHL3 adaptor trapping but
given the low abundance of CUL3-Δ9 due to autoubiquitination, this is
likely a less important factor. KLHL3 trapping may be more relevant for
CUL3Δ474−477, which was reported to have no
effect on KLHL3 abundance in dermal cells isolated from the proband patient,
or in transfected cells(17). Notably,
this patient had a significantly milder renal phenotype (lower serum
[K+] and [Cl−], and higher
[HCO3−]) than the majority of patients with
CUL3-Δ9 mutations(158).
Therefore, effects on NCC activity may be less severe with the
CUL3Δ474−477 mutation due to unchanged KLHL3
abundance, but further studies both in vitro and in vivo are needed to test
this hypothesis.

The Ellison group first reported that CUL3-Δ9 displays
“hyperneddylation” compared with WT CUL3(39). Later studies from several groups revealed
that both CUL3-Δ9 and CUL3Δ474−477 display
reduced ability to interact with JAB1 (also called CSN5), the catalytic
subunit of the COP9 signalosome that mediates deneddylation of CUL3(17, 85–87). Impaired
deneddylation of mutant CUL3, which is required for CRL adaptor exchange, is
likely to play a central role in CUL3-mediated FHHt (see
Figure 8
and “Impaired
deneddylation”). Disruption of JAB1 along the
renal tubule largely mimics the effects of CUL3 disruption. Thus, inducible
tubule-specific JAB1−/− mice display activation of
the WNK-SPAK pathway, but do not display an FHHt phenotype due to additional
effects along the tubule(159). While
long-term disruption of JAB1 leads to relative NCC hyperactivation (as
demonstrated by higher phosphorylated NCC: total NCC ratio), total NCC is
lower than in control mice due to DCT injury(159, 160). As seen with tubule-specific CUL3−/−
mice(39), tubule-specific
JAB1−/− mice also develop severe renal fibrosis
and polyuria due to effects outside of the DCT(159, 160). Short-term JAB1 disruption showed that prior to the
development of significant DCT or more global renal injury, there were
abundances of WT CUL3 and KLHL3 and increased abundances of WNK4 and
phosphorylated NCC. These data suggest that CUL3 hyperneddylation alone
disrupts CRL function sufficiently to cause inappropriate KLHL3 and CUL3
degradation, leading to WNK4-SPAK-NCC pathway activation. However, these
data are difficult to reconcile with the finding that
CUL3Δ474−477 does not promote KLHL3 degradation
in vitro(17). One possibility is the
degree of impaired deneddylation is important in determining overall CRL
dysfunction. While the ability of CUL3Δ474−477 to
interact JAB1 is reduced, it still binds JAB1 to a greater degree than does
CUL3-Δ9(17).

KLHL3R528 and KLHL3−/− mouse
models primarily lead to accumulation of WNK4, leading to greater
phosphorylation of SPAK and hence NCC. KLHL3−/−
likely mimic KLHL3 recessive mutations with complete loss
of KLHL3 function. KLHL3R528 displays loss of interaction with
WNK4, and thus recapitulates mutations in the KLHL3-WNK4 binding surface.
Mouse models with mutations in the BTB or BACK domains have not yet been
generated, but it would reasonable to assume that these would also lead to
diminished CRL assembly and/or activity. Mouse models expressing
CUL3-Δ9 suggest that this mutant promotes its own degradation, but
this is not sufficient to elicit clinical manifestations of FHHt. There is
evidence that CUL3-Δ9 promotes degradation of KLHL3, and in
combination with lower total CUL3, this is a significant disease mechanism.
While this model is not universally accepted, discrepant findings may
reflect use of non-specific KLHL3 antibodies. Other factors including
trapping of adaptors in dysfunctional complexes and impaired dimerization
with WT CUL3 are likely to contribute too, but these mechanisms are
challenging to test in vivo. The dysfunctional CRLs arising from
CUL3-Δ9 expression led to WNK4 accumulation and ultimately
inappropriate NCC activation. A CUL3Δ474−477 mouse
model has not yet been generated but based on in vitro data and the milder
patient phenotype, effects on WNK4 and NCC phosphorylation are likely to be
milder. This might be due to a lack of effect of
CUL3Δ474−477 on KLHL3 abundance. For both KLHL3
and CUL3 models, the primary mechanism of hyperkalemia and metabolic
acidosis is likely to be due to NCC hyperactivation, with effects on ENaC
and ROMK being secondary. Increased NKCC2 phosphorylation does not appear to
contribute to CUL3-Δ9-mediated FHHt. Finally, findings from
tubule-specific JAB1−/− mice suggest that impaired
CUL3-Δ9 deneddylation likely plays a critical role in the ability of
both CUL3-Δ9 and CUL3Δ474−477 to promote
inappropriate activation of NCC.

The contributions of vascular effects of FHHt-causing mutations to the
development of hypertension are largely unexplored, except for
CUL3 mutations. Therefore, here we will focus on FHHt
models expressing CUL3-Δ9. The Sigmund group generated transgenic mice in
which CUL3-Δ9 was inducibly expressed in vascular smooth muscle cells
following Cre-mediated recombination of a transcriptional inhibitor sequence
(S-CUL3Δ9 mice)(161).
S-CUL3Δ9 mice displayed only a modest increase in systolic blood pressure
during the inactive phase, but a strong hypertensive effect was elicited by
infusion of angiotensin II at a rate that did not increase blood pressure in
controls. Consistent with impaired arterial compliance and greater arterial
stiffness, which are often associated with isolated systolic hypertension(162), pulse wave velocity was higher in
S-CUL3Δ9 mice and arterial compliance was lower following administration
of a subpressor dose of angiotensin II. A similar defect in pulse wave velocity
was observed in global constitutive CUL3WT/Δ9 knockin
mice(85). To determine mechanisms
leading to dysregulation of blood pressure, studies in isolated vessels were
performed. Compared with uninduced controls, basilar arteries from
S-CUL3Δ9 mice displayed a severe impairment in vasodilatory responses to
several treatments including acetylcholine and angiotensin 1–7 (which
stimulate endothelium-mediated vasodilation), low-dose KCl, and cromakalim, an
ATP-sensitive K+-channel activator. However, the vasodilatory
response to the Ca2+ channel blocker nifedipine was normal,
suggesting a specific functional defect. Similarly, responses to some
vasoconstricting agents (angiotensin II, phenylephrine, endothelin-1) were
enhanced, while the responses to others (5-hydroxytryptamine and the thromboxane
A2 mimetic U46619) were not. Consistent with these findings in isolated vessels,
angiotensin II and phenylephrine caused a greater increase in systolic blood
pressure in CUL3WT/Δ9 mice than in WT mice(85). Further suggesting vascular dysfunction rather
than an inherent structural defect due to CUL3-Δ9 expression, there were
no difference in external vessel diameter, lumen diameter, or wall thickness. A
similar impairment in acetylcholine-mediated relaxation of the aorta was also
observed. Consistent with autoubiquitination and similar to what has been
observed in kidney(85, 92, 93)
CUL3-Δ9 protein abundance was barely detectable in aorta(161). Abundance of the CUL3 substrate RhoA was higher
in aorta from S-CUL3Δ9 mice and S-CUL3Δ9 aorta displayed a greater
increase in RhoA-GTP in response to 5-hydroxytryptamine. Pathways downstream of
RhoA were also activated, with greater phosphorylation of the Rho kinase (ROCK)
substrate myosin phosphatase target subunit 1 (MYPT1). The activation of this
pathway in S-CUL3Δ9 mice is consistent with the phenotype observed in
PPARγ-P467L mice, which identified PPARγ as an upstream regulator
of WT CUL3(62). The ROCK inhibitor
Y-27632 restored acetylcholine-mediated relaxation in both aorta and basilar
arteries. Thus, impaired vasodilation in S-CUL3Δ9 mice results from
elevated ROCK activity due to higher RhoA activity.

Similar findings were reported in mice with constitutive vascular smooth
muscle-specific deletion of exon 9 from one endogenous Cul3
allele (Cul3+/VSMCΔ9 or
SM22-Cul3Δ9 mice)(92). SM22-Cul3Δ9 mice displayed higher
systolic and diastolic blood pressure than WT mice, in contrast to the isolated
systolic hypertension observed in S-CUL3Δ9 mice(161). This difference may reflect more robust
deletion of exon 9 in SM22-Cul3Δ9 mice, due to
constitutive expression of CUL3-Δ9 throughout life and the use of a
non-inducible Cre recombinase transgenic line. Also in line with the findings of
the Sigmund group, the vasodilatory response to acetylcholine was impaired in
SM22-Cul3Δ9 mice aorta, through a mechanism
involving accumulation of RhoA and higher ROCK activity, and subsequent
phosphorylation of myosin phosphatase target subunit 1.

The Sigmund group used a strategy similar to that used to generate
S-CUL3Δ9 mice to drive CUL3-Δ9 expression only in endothelial
cells (E-Cul3Δ9 mice)(69) and
identified additional vascular defects that likely contribute to FHHt. In
contrast to S-CUL3Δ9 mice that displayed isolated systolic hypertension
during the inactive phase(161),
E-Cul3Δ9 mice systolic and diastolic blood pressure trended higher than
controls during the active phase. A high salt diet provoked a significantly
greater increase in blood pressure compared with controls. Renal arteries from
female but not male E-Cul3Δ9 mice displayed impaired vasodilation in
response to acetylcholine, which may contribute to salt-sensitive hypertension
in female E-Cul3Δ9 mice. Carotid arteries from E-Cul3Δ9 mice were
isolated and found to display impaired endothelium-dependent vasodilation in
response to acetylcholine, an effect abrogated by inhibition of nitric oxide
synthase (NO synthase) with L-NAME. A similar defect in acetylcholine-induced
vasodilation was seen in basilar arteries, though this was only evident in males
suggesting there may be some sex differences in the effects of CUL3-Δ9 on
the vasculature. Carotid responses to several vasoconstrictive agents including
KCl, 5-hydroxytryptamine, and endothelin-1 were normal, suggesting smooth muscle
function was unaffected. CUL3-Δ9 was undetectable in primary aortic
endothelial cells cultured from E-Cul3Δ9 mice. While total abundance of
eNOS was not different compared with controls, E-Cul3Δ9 cells displayed
lower phosphorylation of eNOS at serine 1117. Inhibition of the CUL3-KLHL15 CRL
target protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A)(163) with okadaic acid increased eNOS phosphorylation in E-Cul3Δ9
cells, suggesting the vasodilation defect is due to reduced endothelial NO
generation.

Together these data suggest a significant role for vascular dysfunction
in the hypertensive phenotype in CUL3-Δ9-mediated FHHt, with defects in
the function of vascular smooth muscle and endothelium both contributing. It has
not been demonstrated whether CUL3Δ474−477 causes
vascular effects, though this is likely since it displays increased binding to
Bacurd1 (KCTD13) (17), the substrate
adaptor for RhoA. Notable with regard to vascular dysfunction in CUL3-mediated
FHHt is the enhanced interaction between both CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 and the CRL inhibitor glomulin(17, 86). Mutations in the GLMN gene that enodes it
cause vascular lesions In humans, and in mouse glomulin is highly expressed in
large blood vessels, and smaller arteries and veins(164). Thus, additional vascular defects may exist
that contribute to the hypertensive phenotype.

No studies have directly determined whether mouse models with mutations
in KLHL3, WNK1, or WNK4 lead
to vascular defects. While the Kurz group reported expression of KLHL3 in mouse
and human aorta by Western blot and immunohistochemistry(85), the Uchida group did not detect KLHL3 protein in
mouse aorta or mouse isolated vascular smooth muscle cells, using an antibody
validated in kidney from KLHL3−/− mice and RT-PCR(67). Single-cell transcriptomics of aorta
also fails to detect KLHL3 mRNA in mouse aorta(165). Therefore, it is unlikely that KLHL3 is expressed in aorta,
though it may be expressed in smaller resistance vessels more relevant to blood
pressure control so potential effects of KLHL3 in the vasculature cannot be
excluded.

As described in detail in “WNK kinases and vascular
function”, there is evidence that WNK kinases
modulate vascular tone most likely through effects on NKCC1, with increased
NKCC1 phosphorylation and activity potentiating responses to vasoconstrictors.
WNK3 has been implicated in the modulation of vascular tone by promoting NKCC1
activation in response to angiotensin II(61). Therefore, it is possible that higher WNK1 or WNK4 abundance in
the vasculature contribute to hypertension in FHHt through effects on NKCC1,
though this has not been demonstrated. Indeed, there was a trend towards
lower mRNA expression of WNK1 in aorta from
WNK1+/FHHt
mice, which may reflect a response to compensate for increased
intravascular volume due to higher NCC activity in the kidney.

Abundance of the CUL3 adaptor KLHL2 was reported to decrease in mouse
vascular smooth muscle cells derived from aorta in response to angiotensin II,
leading to WNK3 accumulation(67). WNK4
has also been reported to be a target of KLHL2 both in vitro(100) and in vivo(156), but WNK4 protein and mRNA were not detected in mouse
aorta(67). However, as for KLHL3,
expression in resistance vessels cannot be excluded, especially in light of
effects of siRNA knockdown of WNK4 in mesenteric arteries(166). Thus, it is possible that loss of interaction
with KLHL2 and/or KLHL3 lead to accumulation of mutant WNK4 in the vasculature,
leading to higher NKCC1 activation and enhanced vasoconstriction. These data
also raise the possibility that in addition to effects on RhoA and NO,
CUL3-Δ9 and CUL3Δ474−477 may lead to excessive
WNK-mediated NKCC1 activation in the vasculature due to formation of abnormal
CRLs with KLHL2 and/or KLHL3. However, NKCC1 phosphorylation was not different
to that of controls in SM22-Cul3Δ9 mice, despite
enhanced abundance and phosphorylation of SPAK(92).

WNK4 has also been shown to play a role in modulating responses to
vasoconstrictors by inhibiting Ca2+ influx through the
Ca2+ channel TRPC3. TRPC3 has been reported to be expressed at
higher levels in the vasculature of hypertensive rats(167) and humans(168, 169). Knockdown of WNK4
by siRNA in mesenteric arteries increased vasoconstriction in
response to phenylephrine(166). In
vitro, knockdown of WNK4 stimulated TRPC3 activity, while WT WNK4 overexpression
strongly inhibited it. The mechanism of TRPC3 inhibition by WNK4 was independent
of kinase activity and involved a reduction in surface abundance of TRPC3. In
contrast, WNK4 Q562E did not inhibit TRPC3 activity or reduce its surface
abundance. This might sustain TRPC3 activity under circumstances when it might
be inhibited, promoting a permissive milieu for enhanced vasoconstriction. This
mechanism is plausible but has not been examined in mice expressing WNK4 Q562E.
It is conceivable that mutations in CUL3 and/or
KLHL3 that increase WNK4 abundance could exert a similar
effect by increasing WNK4 abundance. Mutations in WNK1 that
increase WNK1 abundance might also lead to a similar effect. Whether WNK1
inhibits TRPC3 has not been determined, but it does activate rather than inhibit
the related channel TRPC6(170).

Two studies examining large cohorts of FHHT patients found that the disease
severity follows the order
CUL3-Δ9>KLHL3
recessive>KLHL3
dominant>WNK4>WNK1(2, 9).
This pattern is likely related to the functional effects of each mutation, and the
inheritance pattern of each type of mutation provides additional insight.
CUL3 mutations are all dominant and the CUL3-Δ9 protein
displays an unusual combination of gain-of-function (higher ubiquitination activity)
and loss-of-function (towards non-adaptor substrates) that can exert effects on many
substrate adaptors, as well as significant dominant-negative effects on WT CUL3
CRLs. However, despite CUL3 being ubiquitously expressed, the overall phenotypic
effects of CUL3-Δ9 expression reported are relatively limited, with data from
both patients and mouse models supporting effects primarily on the vasculature and
kidney. Failure to thrive and growth impairment have been described for children
with CUL3-Δ9-mediated FHHt(2, 9, 171).
Lower body weight and length was also reported in CUL3WT/Δ9
knockin mice that express CUL3-Δ9 globally and constitutively(85). Growth impairment was also seen in children with
mutations in other FHHt-causing genes except for WNK4(9). However, in one case report normalization of
growth occurred after administration of thiazides(171), suggesting growth impairment is secondary to the electrolyte
and/or blood pressure abnormalities. Therefore, whether the effect on growth is
direct or indirect is unclear. The single individual identified bearing the de novo
CUL3Δ474−477 mutation appears to display milder
hyperkalemia, hyperchloremia, and metabolic acidosis compared with patients with the
CUL3-Δ9 mutation but presents with hypertension(17). Speculatively, the milder effect of
CUL3Δ474−477 on electrolytes may reflect less
activation of NCC than occurs with CUL3-Δ9, perhaps due to an inability to
promote KLHL3 degradation. CUL3Δ474−477 would still exert
vascular effects, which in vitro studies suggest do not involve adaptor degradation
but rather adaptor trapping(63), leading to
significant hypertension. Overall, CUL3Δ474−477 appears to
be less dysfunctional than CUL3-Δ9 perhaps due to deletion of only 4 residues
resulting in limited structural changes (see “CUL3 mutations exert multiple effects on CRL
function”). The CUL3Δ474−477
patient also displayed failure to thrive and skeletomuscular developmental
abnormalities, though it is not clear whether these were a direct effect of CUL3 CRL
dysfunction, or secondary to the electrolyte abnormalities and hypertension during
childhood, maternal factors (medications administered during pregnancy and a prior
history of difficult pregnancies) or medications (including growth hormone and oral
glucocorticoids) administered to the patient prior to diagnosis of FHHt(17). More thorough analysis of mice and
patients with FHHt-mediated by CUL3Δ474−477 and
CUL3-Δ9 may identify additional phenotypes specifically from effects of
FHHt-causing CUL3 (see “Other
potential manifestations of FHHt”). The dominant
rather than recessive nature of FHHt-causing CUL3 mutations is
consistent with the embryonic lethality observed with complete loss of CUL3 CRL
function present in CUL3−/− mice(88)and presumably
CUL3Δ9/Δ9(85,
155). The more severe effects of these
mutations are also likely to impair reproductive fitness, as evident by the high
occurrence of de novo mutations, limited parental transmission, and
small pedigrees(2, 9).

There are several explanations for the milder forms of FHHt resulting from
KLHL3 and WNK kinase mutations. These proteins are likely to
have more limited physiological roles compared with CUL3, which can form hundreds of
different CRLs targeting many substrates for ubiquitination. CUL3-KLHL3 CRLs will
target only a small subset of all CUL3 CRL targets, and in turn WNK kinases likely
constitute a limited subset of CUL3-KLHL3 targets. Next, a more limited expression
pattern may limit effects on the vasculature, since there are data suggesting that
KLHL3 and WNK4 are not expressed in the vasculature (see “Vascular defects in KLHL3-, WNK1-, and WNK4-mediated
FHHt”). A complete loss of KLHL3 function may occur
with most recessive KLHL3 mutations, though individual mutants may
retain some ability to form an active CUL3-KLHL3 CRL. This more severe disruption to
CUL3-KLHL3 CRL activity, is likely to result in the greatest accumulation of WNK4
and KS-WNK1, leading to maximal activation of NCC and the most severe effects after
CUL3 mutations. For dominant KLHL3 and
WNK4 mutations, the unaffected WT alleles will still permit
assembly of CUL3-KLHL3-WNK4 CRL complexes, so the increase in WNK4 abundance will be
relatively less. Dominant WNK4 mutations may lead to a milder
effect than dominant KLHL3 mutations since capacity for
ubiquitination of WT WNK4 will be greater. Furthermore, a recent study identified an
additional 25 amino acid motif in the carboxyl-terminus of WNK4 (residues
1051–1075) that mediates interactions between WNK4 and KLHL3. This motif is
not conserved in human WNK1 (J.A. McCormick, unpublished observation). The motif
contains several acidic residues required for KLHL3 interaction and it may permit
some degree of interaction with KLHL3 and thus CUL3-KLHL3-mediated ubiquitination of
FHHt-causing WNK4 acidic motif mutants(172).
This may contribute to the milder phenotype caused by WNK4
mutations compared with KLHL3 mutations. In contrast, dominant
KLHL3 mutations will lower the overall capacity to ubiquitinate
both WNK4 and KS-WNK1, but to a lesser degree than recessive mutations with 2
affected alleles. For WNK1 intronic mutations that lead to ectopic
WNK1 expression normally functioning CUL3-KLHL3 CRLs are present that will help
counter the increased WNK1 abundance, although KS-WNK1 has been proposed to be the
preferred target for the CUL3-KLHL3 CRL(15).
The mild effect of WNK1 intronic and acidic motif mutations suggest
much more limited effects on the vasculature compared with CUL3
mutations.

The primary focus of the clinical effects of mutations in
WNK1, WNK4, KLHL3, and
CUL3 has been the classical features of FHHt, electrolyte
imbalances and hypertension. As outlined above, until the discovery of mutations in
CUL3, the contribution of extra-renal effects on the development of hypertension
were underappreciated due to correction of the electrolyte imbalances and
hypertension by the NCC-inhibiting thiazide diuretics, despite evidence for effects
of thiazides on peripheral vascular resistance(173). Potential effects of KLHL3 and WNK kinase
mutations on the vasculature have already been considered (see
“Vascular defects in KLHL3-, WNK1-,
and WNK4-mediated FHHt”). Beyond the kidney and
vasculature other phenotypes may exist given the ubiquitous expression of CUL3(174) and widespread expression of KLHL3(151), WNK4(175), and WNK1(36). Not all
potential phenotypes can be considered in this review, so a few select examples of
studies exploring the roles of CUL3, KLHL3, and WNK kinases in other organs will be
briefly described. A more expansive discussion of additional manifestations of
CUL3-mediated FHHt is provided by Sharma and Chatrathi(176), who suggest that it might be more appropriate to
refer to CUL3-mediated FHHt as “FHHt with developmental
dysregulation”. This would better reflect the many non-renal and
non-cardiovascular clinical presentations described, including the commonly seen
growth impairment.

Mice with disruption of CUL3 in cardiomyocytes die shortly after birth, and
their hearts displayed widespread cardiomyocyte vascularization and protein
aggregation(177). Proteomic analysis
identified many upregulated proteins that participate in oxidation–reduction
and other metabolic processes. Notably, there was higher abundance of NAD(P)H
dehydrogenase [quinone] 1 (NQO1), which plays a key role in the response to
oxidative stress and is a target of the CUL3-KEAP1 transcription factor Nuclear
factor-E2-related factor 2 (NRF2). KLHL3 has been identified as the CUL3-KLHL3
adaptor for cardiac myosin binding protein, and mutations and variants in the
MYBPC3 gene encoding it are commonly detected in patients with
cardiomyopathies or heart failure(178).
Pharmacological inhibition of WNK1 in a rat model of pulmonary hypertension improved
right ventricular function by improving mitochondrial function(179). These studies raise the possibility that
FHHt-causing mutations exert effects on cardiac development and function that may
contribute to the hypertensive phenotype or cause other cardiovascular defects.

Within the kidney, the effects of disrupting CUL3, KLHL3, WNK1, and WNK4
have been described in detail (see “Mouse models of FHHt”), with those for
KLHL3 and the WNK kinases having clear effects on NCC activity, with minor likely
secondary effects on other segments. In contrast, disruption of CUL3 along the renal
tubule leads to a severe phenotype(39) with
progressive renal injury leading to fibrosis, polyuria due to loss of AQP2
expression(153), and lower blood
pressure when salt restricted. Similar effects were found when cullin deneddylation
was ablated in renal tubule JAB1−/− mice(159), confirming the importance of
neddylation/deneddylation cycling in normal CUL3 CRL function. There is some
evidence that both CUL3-Δ9 and CUL3Δ474−477 could
have additional effects on renal function. For example, the abundance of the NRF2
target NQO1 was higher in CUL3-Δ9 mice than in WT mice(99). Cyclin E, another CUL3 target, was increased in
fibroblasts derived from the patient with the CUL3Δ474−477
mutation, and this was associated with higher cell proliferation(17), though cyclin E was not higher in CUL3-Δ9
mice(99). These findings raise the
possibility that there may be additional unidentified renal complications in FHHt
due to either type of CUL3 mutation that may predispose to injury or impaired
response to oxidative stress. It is notable that the clearest phenotype resulting
from CUL3-Δ9 or CUL3Δ474−477 expression is
inappropriate NCC activation, especially since many of the adaptors to which they
more tightly bind are expressed along the entire tubule and not just the DCT. While
we have proposed that CUL3-Δ9-mediated KLHL3 degradation, observed both in
vitro and in vivo, provides a plausible mechanism for this DCT-specific effect, this
mechanism is not universally accepted(176).

Since CUL3, KLHL3, and WNK kinases are also expressed in the brain,
FHHt-causing mutations may also have effects in the central nervous system. FHHt
caused by WNK4 Q565E mutation was recently reported to be associated with a higher
incidence of epileptic seizures (5.5% versus 0.7% in the general population)(141). It was speculated that hyperkalemia
directly leads to seizures in FHHt through an additional effect of WNK4 Q565E to
disrupt the blood brain barrier, increasing the [K+] of the cerebral
spinal fluid above the normal range (2.7–3.9 mmol/L)(141). Lower expression of KLHL3 was associated with
subarachnoid hemorrhage due to aneurysm rupture and confirmed by quantitative
PCR(180). There is also extensive
literature regarding a role for CUL3 in several neurological disorders including
autism spectrum disorder, developmental delay, and intellectual disability,
discussed extensively in a recent review by Lin and colleagues(181). Many de novo mutations in the
CUL3 gene have been identified in patients, though none leading
to generation of CUL3-Δ9 or CUL3Δ474–477.
Constitutive and brain-region-specific
Cul3+/− mice display
anxiety-like behaviors, social deficits, and cognitive impairment that is mitigated
when Cul3 is disrupted in adulthood, suggesting a developmental
effect(181). One mechanism highly
relevant to CUL3 mutations involves effects on the neuronal
cytoskeleton and neurogenesis due to higher levels of RhoA(182). RhoA abundance is higher in the vasculature of
CUL3-Δ9 mice (see “Vascular effects of FHHt-causing CUL3 mutations”),
so it is probable that it is also higher in the brain. However, there is limited
information regarding neurological or neurodevelopmental defects in patients with
CUL3 mutations. Two out of 19 patients with CUL3-Δ9
mutations were reported as displaying developmental and behavioral disorder or
retardation(9), while a case report
described autism spectrum disorder in one patient(183) and some other case reports noted intellectual impairment(184). Similarly, the single
CUL3Δ474−477 patient also displayed intellectual
impairment(17). Therefore, it is possible
that subtle intellectual and cognitive deficits exist in other patients.

The discovery that mutations in WNK1,
WNK4, CUL3, or KLHL3 cause FHHt
has ultimately led to the elucidation of a complex regulatory pathway for NCC along
the distal convoluted tubule. In normal physiology, this pathway plays a critical
role in the maintenance of K+ homeostasis by determining Na+
delivery to downstream K+-secreting segments, and perhaps remodeling of
these segments too. Other important contributing discoveries to the physiological
pathway include the identification of cation cotransporter phosphorylation by SPAK,
and mutations in the basolateral K+ channel subunit Kir4.1 (encoded by
KCNJ10) causing EAST/SeSAME syndrome, a renal tubulopathy that
mirrors FHHt. In this scheme, WNK4 plays a central role, phosphorylating SPAK/OSR1
that in turn phosphorylate and activate NCC. Abundance of WNK4 is in turn determined
by CUL3-KLHL3-mediated ubiquitination, which is impaired by KLHL3 phosphorylation by
PKC in response to dietary K+ restriction. Accumulation of WNK4 following
KLHL3 phosphorylation hyperactivates NCC, limiting downstream K+
secretion to maintain K+ homeostasis. Changes in K+ exit
through Kir4.1/5.1 at the basolateral membrane of the DCT in response to changes in
plasma [K+] alter efflux of Cl− via the basolateral
CLC-KB channel, affecting intracellular [Cl−]. When plasma
[K+] decreases, Cl− exit increases, and this leads
to activation of WNK4 which is inhibited by direct binding of Cl−.
The resulting NCC activation again indirectly limits downstream K+
secretion to reduce K+ losses. While WNK kinases have been shown to
modulate other ion transporting pathways including NKCC2, ENaC, and ROMK in vitro,
these effects appear to be less important for the disease etiology in vivo. In the
vasculature, the discovery of CUL3 mutations led to studies showing
that CUL3 modulates vascular tone, while WNK1 has been shown to play roles in
vascular development and modulation of vascular tone through effects on NKCC1.
Numerous mouse models have revealed that mutations in CUL3, KLHL3,
or WNK kinases disrupt these physiological pathways in the kidney and vasculature
leading to uncontrolled NCC activity and defective vasoconstriction and vasodilation
in both vascular smooth muscle and endothelial cells.

The mechanisms by which the causative mutations cause FHHt have been largely
determined for KLHL3 and WNK kinases. Impaired formation of CUL3-KLHL3-WNK CRLs
reduce WNK ubiquitination (WNK4) or ectopic WNK expression (WNK1) lead to
hyperactivation of NCC and possibly some effects in the vasculature. The effects of
CUL3 mutations are more complex, with multiple functional
defects in CUL3-Δ9 and CUL3Δ474−477 resulting from
impaired deneddylation and for CUL3-Δ9 large changes in structural
flexibility. In the vasculature, enhanced binding to substrate adaptors,
autoubiquitination, and impaired dimerization are all likely to play a role. In the
kidney, additional effects to degrade KLHL3 may contribute, explaining the
DCT-specific effects of the mutations since KLHL3 is highly expressed along this
segment of the nephron. However, this mechanism is controversial and additional
studies are needed; differences between CUL3-Δ9 and
CUL3Δ474−477 may resolves this issue. Regardless of the
precise mechanisms, the overall mechanism is the same as for KLHL3
and WNK4 mutations, with reduced WNK4 ubiquitination leading to
WNK4 accumulation due to impaired CUL3-KLHL3 CRL function. Notably, CUL3-Δ9
exerts a strong effect in the vasculature since in this tissue it affects pathways
other than cation cotransporters. This likely explains why CUL3
mutations cause the most severe form of FHHt.

To-date, the study of FHHt both in humans and mice has focused on effects on
the vasculature and kidney. Given the widespread expression of the genes that cause
FHHt when mutated, it is likely that there are direct effects in other tissue that
lead to additional phenotypes. The brain is a particularly promising organ for study
since there is evidence from several mouse models suggesting important roles for
CUL3, KLHL3, and WNK kinases in this organ. Studying the effects of FHHt mutations
in other organs may also lead to the discovery of novel signaling pathways
regulating normal physiological function, as occurred in the kidney and vasculature.
The study of FHHt represents a textbook example of the importance of the study of
rare diseases, and how this can lead to a better understanding of normal
physiology.
