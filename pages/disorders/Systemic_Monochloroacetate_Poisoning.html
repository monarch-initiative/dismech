Systemic Monochloroacetate Poisoning

Environmental MONDO:0019545 Pathograph 36 Show in embeddings browser

Systemic monochloroacetate poisoning is a rare, life-threatening intoxication caused by dermal, oral, or inhalational exposure to monochloroacetic acid (chloroacetic acid; MCAA), a strong corrosive haloacetic acid used widely as a chemical-synthesis intermediate. The agent is doubly dangerous: it produces a corrosive chemical burn at the contact site and, once absorbed, acts as a systemic metabolic poison. Dermal contact is the most important route because the acid penetrates skin rapidly and extensively, so contamination of a modest body-surface area can deliver a lethal systemic dose. A characteristic latent interval of roughly one to three and a half hours separates a seemingly survivable burn from systemic collapse. Intracellularly, monochloroacetate is a reactive sulfhydryl-alkylating electrophile that inactivates thiol-dependent enzymes of energy metabolism; the best-characterised target is glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), whose inactivation blocks glycolysis and gluconeogenesis and produces severe hypoglycemia together with lactate and pyruvate accumulation and a profound lactic/metabolic acidosis. The resulting bioenergetic failure injures the organs most dependent on aerobic metabolism — the heart (myocardial depression and shock), the central nervous system (disorientation, convulsions, coma and cerebral edema), the kidneys (acute injury), skeletal muscle (rhabdomyolysis), the lungs (acute lung injury) and the liver. There is no widely licensed specific antidote; management is early decontamination, correction of acidosis, hypoglycemia and shock, organ support including renal replacement, and surgical burn care.

Systemic absorption of monochloroacetic acid
Monochloroacetic acid is rapidly absorbed after dermal contact, ingestion, or inhalation and distributes systemically. Dermal absorption is the most important route: percutaneous uptake across apparently intact skin is rapid and extensive, and a plasma concentration measurable hours after a splash confirms transdermal loading.

  Systemic monochloroacetate poisoning is a rare, life-threatening intoxication
  caused by dermal, oral, or inhalational exposure to monochloroacetic acid
  (chloroacetic acid; MCAA), a strong corrosive haloacetic acid used widely as a
  chemical-synthesis intermediate. The agent is doubly dangerous: it produces a
  corrosive chemical burn at the contact site and, once absorbed, acts as a
  systemic metabolic poison. Dermal contact is the most important route because
  the acid penetrates skin rapidly and extensively, so contamination of a modest
  body-surface area can deliver a lethal systemic dose. A characteristic latent
  interval of roughly one to three and a half hours separates a seemingly
  survivable burn from systemic collapse. Intracellularly, monochloroacetate is a reactive
  sulfhydryl-alkylating electrophile that inactivates thiol-dependent enzymes of
  energy metabolism; the best-characterised target is glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate
  dehydrogenase (GAPDH), whose inactivation blocks glycolysis and gluconeogenesis
  and produces severe hypoglycemia together with lactate and pyruvate
  accumulation and a profound lactic/metabolic acidosis. The resulting
  bioenergetic failure injures the organs most dependent on aerobic metabolism —
  the heart (myocardial depression and shock), the central nervous system
  (disorientation, convulsions, coma and cerebral edema), the kidneys (acute
  injury), skeletal muscle (rhabdomyolysis), the lungs (acute lung injury) and
  the liver. There is no widely licensed specific antidote; management is early
  decontamination, correction of acidosis, hypoglycemia and shock, organ support
  including renal replacement, and surgical burn care.

0. Orientation (read this first)

There is no causal gene. "Systemic Monochloroacetate Poisoning" (MONDO:0019545) is an acute chemical toxidrome, not a Mendelian disease — the "causal gene(s): not recorded" and "OMIM: not recorded" lines in the template are correct and should stay empty. The etiologic agent is monochloroacetic acid (MCAA; chloroacetic acid; the conjugate base is monochloroacetate / chloroacetate), a small corrosive haloacetic acid used industrially. 1. Disease Information

Overview. Systemic monochloroacetate poisoning is acute, often fatal, multi-organ intoxication following absorption of monochloroacetic acid — most characteristically through the skin after an industrial splash, but also by ingestion or inhalation. The agent is doubly dangerous: it is corrosive (a chemical burn at the contact site) and a systemic metabolic poison (it shuts down cellular energy production). The hallmark is that a seemingly survivable skin burn is followed, 1–3.5 hours later, by lethal systemic collapse — severe metabolic (lactic) acidosis, hypoglycemia, cardiac failure, coma, and death within hours to a few days.

2. Etiology

Primary cause (environmental/chemical). Exposure to monochloroacetic acid or its sodium salt. Not genetic, not infectious. The ECETOC human-intoxication review tallies 41 systemic poisonings from dermal exposure, 26 of them fatal, and notes that in 5 of the 6 best-documented fatalities ≥10% of body surface area was contaminated.

Risk factors — environmental/occupational (the only ones that apply): - Occupational handling is the dominant setting. The EU CSTEE opinion states exposure is "mainly occupational, during production, synthesis, and paint-remover formulation or use." Industrial uses: synthesis of carboxymethylcellulose, glycine, thioglycolic acid, herbicides (e.g., 2,4-D), dyes, and pharmaceuticals; also wart/callus removers and tissue fixatives. - Large contaminated skin area. The single most important determinant of a fatal outcome. Thresholds reported vary by source — a supplier TDS says fatal when >10% BSA; LOINC chemical info says >6%; the Tao 2020 case discussion says "Contamination of more than 5% of the skin can cause poisoning and even death." (These numbers are secondary/consensus statements, not derived from a controlled study — carry them with that caveat.) - High concentration of the solution and delayed/inadequate decontamination. - Route: dermal ≫ oral/inhalation for reported systemic cases, though a fatal oral case is documented (Rogers 1995).

Genetic risk/protective factors: none established. No susceptibility loci, modifier genes, or protective alleles are known — appropriate to leave blank.

Protective factors (environmental): prompt copious decontamination (water, then dilute sodium bicarbonate), limiting contaminated BSA, PPE. Gene-environment interactions: none described.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical death)

  1. Concentrated MCAA contacts skin (or is ingested) → local corrosive injury: first-to-second, sometimes third-degree chemical burn. (Human; demonstrated.)
  2. Once a large area (~≥5–10% BSA) is contaminated, MCA is rapidly absorbed through intact skin and distributed systemically, with systemic signs appearing 1–3.5 h later. (Human case/review inference; plasma level at 4 h in Kulling 1992 confirms transdermal uptake.)
  3. Intracellularly, MCA — a reactive, sulfhydryl-alkylating haloacetate — inactivates thiol-dependent metabolic enzymes. The specific target is disputed:
  4. GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase): Sakai et al. 2005 (PMID 15720133), isolated perfused rat liver, found MCA inactivates GAPDH (to ~19% of control) and that aconitase was not inhibited, overturning the earlier assumption. (In vitro/ex vivo, animal.)
  5. Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (α-KGDH): rat heart mitochondria studies report inhibition, via oxalate and/or direct SH-alkylation. (Animal/in vitro.)
  6. Aconitase: historically proposed ("MCA enters the TCA cycle and inhibits aconitase") but refuted by Sakai 2005 — note the disagreement explicitly in the entry rather than asserting aconitase.
  7. Enzyme blockade throttles glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and gluconeogenesis →
  8. branch A: impaired aerobic ATP production in high-energy-demand tissues (heart, CNS, skeletal muscle) → cell injury;
  9. branch B: GAPDH/gluconeogenesis block → severe hypoglycemia;
  10. branch C: pyruvate cannot enter the TCA cycle → lactate accumulation → profound lactic/metabolic acidosis (Dartsch et al. 2000 show ↑lactate + ↑pyruvate and ultrastructural/cytoskeletal damage in cultured kidney and liver epithelial cells — in vitro, animal/cell).
  11. Multi-organ failure: cardiac failure/arrhythmia, CNS depression → coma/seizures → cerebral edema, rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, acute lung injury/pulmonary edema (Kato & Dote 2006, subcutaneous rat model: lethal acute lung injury + hypoglycemia — animal), hepatic injury, hyperthermia.
  12. Death within hours to ~8 days — in Kulling 1992 the patient died on day 8 of brain herniation after refractory acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, renal failure, and cerebral edema; other cases die within hours of cardiac arrest or refractory shock/acidosis. The ECETOC review and Toshina/Dote 2004 both note the exact cause of death remains poorly understood.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: high once systemic poisoning develops — 26/41 (≈63%) of documented dermal systemic cases were fatal (ECETOC). Survival is the exception and is tied to small contaminated area + rapid aggressive care (Tao 2020 survived a 10% third-degree burn with CVVH, bicarbonate, steroids, grafting).
  • Prognostic factors: % BSA contaminated (dominant), concentration, speed of decontamination, depth/refractoriness of acidosis and hypoglycemia, onset of coma/cerebral edema, and access to extracorporeal support/antidote.
  • Complications: refractory lactic acidosis, cerebral edema/herniation, cardiac arrest, AKI (rhabdomyolysis), acute lung injury, aspiration pneumonia, extensive burn wounds.
  • Recovery: survivors can normalize systemic labs (Tao 2020 follow-up normal) with residual burn-wound morbidity. No validated QoL instrument data exist for this entity.

12. Treatment

No proven specific antidote is licensed in most countries; management is decontamination + aggressive supportive care, with dichloroacetate as the candidate antidote.

  • Immediate decontamination (primary): remove clothing, copious water irrigation, then dilute sodium bicarbonate lavage of skin/eyes (Tao 2020 used 4% NaHCO₃; the Chinese chloroacetyl-chloride case used 5%). NCIT: Decontamination / supportive-care terms.
  • Correct acidosis: IV sodium bicarbonate; NCIT treatment = Pharmacotherapy (NCIT:C15986) + therapeutic_agent sodium bicarbonate (CHEBI). Correct hypoglycemia: IV glucose — and animal data suggest glucose is partly protective (10% glucose infusion gave ~79% 10-h survival in a rat lethal-dose model; octreotide + oral glucose improved 14-day survival).
  • Candidate antidote — dichloroacetate (DCA): the most consistently effective agent in rodents ("dichloroacetate, and to a lesser degree phenobarbitone… reduced mortality and decreased lactate accumulation in blood and CSF of rats and mice"). Approved in Sweden as an antidote for life-threatening MCAA poisoning; not FDA-approved in the US. An informal regimen (50 mg/kg IV over 10 min, repeat at 2 h, dose doubled if hemodialyzed) circulates but is not a validated clinical guideline — do not treat it as one. (Evidence: animal + reference documents; human efficacy not established.)
  • Ineffective antidotes: glycerol monoacetate and ethanol were tested and did not work — worth recording as a negative to prevent re-nomination.
  • Extracorporeal support: CVVH / hemodialysis (Tao 2020) — both organ support and possible enhanced elimination. NCIT: hemofiltration/hemodialysis procedure terms.
  • Organ/supportive: mechanical ventilation, vasopressors, seizure control, cerebral-edema management; burn care — debridement, negative-pressure wound therapy, skin grafting (NCIT: Surgical Procedure NCIT:C15329; skin graft term); corticosteroids were used in Tao 2020 (methylprednisolone pulse) though not evidence-based for the systemic toxin itself.
  • Experimental/trials: no registered clinical trials (sporadic poisoning); evidence is bench/animal + case reports.
  • Pharmacogenomics / targeted / cell / gene / RNA therapy: not applicable.

13. Prevention

  • Primary (the real lever): occupational controls — engineering containment, closed handling, chemical-resistant PPE, splash protection, immediate-access emergency showers/eyewash, worker training on the delayed systemic risk (a "minor" burn can kill). EU/IPCS hazard communication; ICSC 1449 stresses that specific antidotal treatment should be available on site.
  • Secondary: rapid on-site decontamination protocols; low threshold for hospital observation of any sizeable MCAA skin contact through the pre-systemic window.
  • Tertiary: aggressive ICU support to prevent progression to multi-organ failure.
  • Environmental (separate issue): drinking-water disinfection-by-product limits (WHO) — chronic, not acute.
  • Not applicable: immunization, genetic/carrier screening, PGD, genetic counseling.
