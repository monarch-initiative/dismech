Staphylococcus Aureus Pneumonia

Infectious Disease MONDO:0041879 Pathograph 16 Show in embeddings browser Staphylococcus aureus infection Staphylococcal pneumonia

Staphylococcus aureus pneumonia is bacterial pneumonia caused by S. aureus, including post-influenza and healthcare-associated forms in which agr-regulated alpha-hemolysin, Panton-Valentine leukocidin, PSM-alpha toxins, ADAM10 signaling, leukocyte lysis, and neutrophil-rich inflammation can produce necrotizing pulmonary disease.

S. aureus enters the lower respiratory tract and proliferates on airway or alveolar surfaces until high local density licenses quorum-sensing-driven virulence-factor production.

Agr Quorum-Sensing Virulence Activation
The S. aureus accessory gene regulator quorum-sensing circuit activates RNAIII-linked virulence expression and upregulates secreted cytolysins, including alpha-hemolysin, PSM-alpha toxins, and PVL.
Downstream
Alpha-Hemolysin ADAM10 Barrier Disruption Agr activation contributes to the secreted alpha-hemolysin pool that engages ADAM10 on alveolar epithelial cells.
PSM-Alpha Neutrophil Necroptosis The agr system directly regulates PSM-alpha cytolysins that drive MLKL phosphorylation and neutrophil necroptosis.
Panton-Valentine Leukocidin Leukocyte Injury Agr-driven cytolysin expression is one part of the larger virulence program that, in PVL-positive strains, produces severe leukocyte injury and necrotizing community-acquired pneumonia.

Staphylococcus aureus Pneumonia — Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Staphylococcus aureus Pneumonia MONDO ID: MONDO:0041879 Category: Infectious Disease Report basis: 5-iteration autonomous literature investigation; 16 confirmed findings; 69 papers reviewed.

Summary

Staphylococcus aureus pneumonia is a severe, frequently life-threatening bacterial lower-respiratory-tract infection caused by the Gram-positive coccus Staphylococcus aureus (NCBI:txid1280), spanning two dominant clinical contexts: (1) nosocomial pneumonia — hospital-acquired (HAP) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), where S. aureus is one of the top pathogens, and (2) community-acquired necrotizing pneumonia, an aggressive syndrome in young, previously healthy hosts driven by the Panton-Valentine leukocidin (PVL). Unlike the genetic disorders this template is often applied to, S. aureus pneumonia is an infectious, non-Mendelian disease: there are no causal human germline genes. Instead, the "genetics" of the disease reside in the bacterial virulence gene repertoire (hla, lukS/F-PV, psmα, agr), while host genetic contributions are limited to immunodeficiency states (e.g., STAT3/Th17 defects) that raise susceptibility.

The mechanistic core of the disease is now well defined. The pore-forming cytotoxin alpha-hemolysin (Hla) binds its host receptor, the zinc-dependent metalloprotease ADAM10, on alveolar epithelial cells, upregulating ADAM10 protease activity that cleaves the adherens-junction protein E-cadherin, disrupting the epithelial barrier and producing lethal acute lung injury. In parallel, the agr quorum-sensing system drives expression of phenol-soluble modulins (PSMα) and PVL, which lyse neutrophils (PSMα via MLKL-dependent necroptosis), amplifying inflammation and tissue destruction. Antecedent influenza infection is a critical predisposing factor: type I interferon suppresses protective Th17/IL-17/IL-22 and IL-1β immunity, converting mild colonization into fulminant necrotizing disease.

Clinically, the disease ranges enormously in severity. PVL-positive necrotizing community-acquired pneumonia carries 40–75% mortality with rapid deterioration, hemoptysis, leukopenia, and septic shock, whereas VAP has an attributable mortality of 3–17%. PVL-positivity (HR 1.81) and methicillin resistance (HR 2.37) are independent predictors of mortality. First-line therapy is vancomycin or linezolid for MRSA and cefazolin or anti-staphylococcal penicillins for MSSA, with toxin-suppressing antibiotics (linezolid, clindamycin) and anti-Hla monoclonal antibody prophylaxis (suvratoxumab/MEDI4893) as emerging strategies. No licensed S. aureus vaccine exists.

Section 1 — Disease Information

Overview. S. aureus pneumonia is inflammation and consolidation of the lung parenchyma caused by infection with S. aureus. It presents as: community-acquired pneumonia (CAP, often necrotizing and PVL-associated), hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP), ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), and post-influenza secondary bacterial pneumonia. Nosocomial S. aureus pneumonia is a leading cause of ICU morbidity and mortality; the 2026 review of HAP/VAP found "Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus predominate" (PMID: 42203422).

Key identifiers. - MONDO: MONDO:0041879 - MeSH: Pneumonia, Staphylococcal (D011026) - ICD-10: J15.211 (Pneumonia due to MRSA), J15.212 (Pneumonia due to MSSA); J15.2 (Pneumonia due to staphylococcus) - ICD-11: infectious pneumonia codes with S. aureus as causative organism - SNOMED CT: 53084003 (Bacterial pneumonia); staphylococcal pneumonia concept - OMIM/Orphanet: Not applicable — this is an acquired infectious disease, not a Mendelian or rare genetic disorder.

Synonyms / alternative names. Staphylococcal pneumonia; MRSA pneumonia; MSSA pneumonia; S. aureus necrotizing pneumonia; PVL-associated necrotizing pneumonia; CA-MRSA pneumonia; staphylococcal lung infection.

Information source type. The evidence base is aggregated disease-level (surveillance cohorts, ICU registries, case-control studies, systematic reviews, autopsy series, and animal-model mechanistic work), supplemented by individual patient case reports for rare fulminant presentations.

Section 2 — Etiology

Primary cause (infectious). The disease is caused by Staphylococcus aureus, including both methicillin-susceptible (MSSA) and methicillin-resistant (MRSA) strains, and both community-associated (CA-MRSA, e.g., USA300) and hospital-associated (HA-MRSA) lineages. Virulence is determined by the bacterial toxin repertoire rather than host genetics.

Risk factors. - Host/environmental: mechanical ventilation and endotracheal intubation (VAP), ICU admission, prior hospitalization, aspiration (AOR 4.43, 95% CI 1.74–11.24 for pneumonia-pathogen presence, PMID: 41557657), older age, immunosuppression, cancer, liver dysfunction, heart failure, long-term catheterization, and high SOFA/qSOFA scores (PMID: 41758741). Antecedent influenza (or influenza-like illness) is a major predisposing factor for necrotizing CA disease. Nasal/nasopharyngeal S. aureus colonization is a reservoir. Occupational exposure to livestock (LA-MRSA CC398) increases carriage. - Genetic host susceptibility: rare. Patients with hyper-IgE (Job) syndrome carrying STAT3 mutations, "who often present with S. aureus infections of the lung and skin," lack Th17 immunity (PMID: 21178015). IL-1R1 and IL-17RA deficiencies raise susceptibility in models. - Pathogen genetic risk factors: carriage of lukS-PV/lukF-PV (PVL), high-level hla expression, functional agr locus, and psmα operon.

Protective factors. - Host lipoproteins: serum LDL/ApoB sequesters the agr autoinducing peptide (AIP). "Activation of agr… is inhibited by apolipoprotein B, the structural protein of low-density lipoprotein, which binds and sequesters the secreted agr-signaling peptide (AIP)" (PMID: 26608923); lipoprotein deficiency worsened agr-dependent pneumonia. - Intact Type 17 immunity (IL-17/IL-22/IL-23) and IL-1β signaling. - Influenza vaccination (reduces the antecedent viral trigger).

Gene–environment interactions. The dominant interaction is virus–bacterium: influenza-induced type I IFN suppresses host Th17/IL-1β responses (PMID: 21178015, PMID: 24089191), permitting toxin-armed S. aureus to cause necrotizing disease — a bacterial-genotype × viral-environment interaction.

Section 5 — Environmental Information

  • Environmental factors: hospital/ICU environment, endotracheal tubes (biofilm reservoir — MRSA biofilm forms on ETTs, PMID: 30093624), contaminated surfaces, livestock/meat exposure.
  • Lifestyle factors: injection drug use (associated with MSSA bacteremia/endocarditis), smoking (airway epithelial injury), crowding, and occupational livestock contact.
  • Infectious agents (the cause itself): Staphylococcus aureus (NCBI:txid1280). Co-infecting agents that potentiate disease: Influenza A virus (H1N1) and Influenza B virus — the key viral co-pathogens converting S. aureus colonization into necrotizing/secondary pneumonia (PMID: 21178015, PMID: 41619830).

Section 6 — Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. S. aureus colonizes the nasopharynx/airway and is aspirated or inhaled into the lower respiratory tract → leads to alveolar bacterial burden. (Antecedent influenza injures respiratory epithelium and suppresses immunity — inferred prerequisite in young necrotizing cases.)
  2. Rising bacterial density activates the agr quorum-sensing system (RNAIII) → results in upregulated secretion of Hla, PSMα, and PVL. (Demonstrated; agr blockade is protective.)
  3. Secreted Hla binds ADAM10 on alveolar epithelial cells → upregulates ADAM10 metalloprotease activity → cleaves E-cadherin → disrupts adherens junctions and the epithelial barrier → alveolar–capillary leak and lethal acute lung injury. (Demonstrated genetically: Adam10-conditional-knockout mice are resistant, PMID: 21926978.)
  4. Branch A — Neutrophil destruction: PSMα1–3 induce neutrophil necroptosis via MLKL phosphorylation and PVL forms pores in leukocytes → results in leukopenia, release of DAMPs/LDH, and impaired bacterial clearance. (Demonstrated, PMID: 29500427.)
  5. Branch B — Immune subversion by prior virus: Influenza type I IFN suppresses IL-17/IL-22/IL-23 and attenuates IL-1β/NF-κB → impairs Type 17 antibacterial immunity → higher bacterial burden and mortality. (Demonstrated in IL-17R⁻/⁻, IL-22⁻/⁻, IL-1R1⁻/⁻ mice, PMID: 21178015, PMID: 24089191.)
  6. Branch C — Inflammasome/pyroptosis amplification: In influenza–MRSA co-infection, caspase-1 and GSDMD-mediated macrophage pyroptosis are activated, and dysregulated type I IFN enhances myeloid TNF-α while suppressing T-cell IFN-γ → amplifies lung injury. (Demonstrated; GSDMD inhibitor disulfiram ameliorated disease, PMID: 41927528, PMID: 42089309.)
  7. Combined barrier failure + neutrophil lysis + cytokine storm → necrotizing hemorrhagic bronchopneumonia, hemoptysis, ARDS, septic shock → culminates in multi-organ failure and death in severe cases.

Section 8 — Temporal Development

  • Onset: Acute to hyperacute. PVL-associated necrotizing CAP often follows an influenza-like prodrome by 1–several days, then deteriorates rapidly.
  • Progression: Rapid. Fatal PVL cases: onset-to-death 5.5 ± 10.1 days; 48-h survival markedly reduced in PVL+ (63% vs 94%). VAP: median time to ventilator-associated condition onset ~6 days (PMID: 24709718).
  • Disease course pattern: Acute, progressive, self-limited (resolution with effective therapy) or fatal; not relapsing-remitting.
  • Critical periods / intervention windows: Early source control, prompt appropriate antibiotics, and toxin suppression within the first 24–72 h are decisive; anti-Hla prophylaxis targets the ventilated pre-symptomatic window.

Section 9 — Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: Not applicable — infectious, non-heritable. No Mendelian pattern, penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, mosaicism, founder effect, consanguinity, or carrier-frequency parameters apply to the host.

Epidemiology. - S. aureus is among the top pathogens of nosocomial pneumonia (PMID: 42203422); in Chinese HAP cohorts it was the 4th-ranked pathogen at 5.88% (PMID: 41758741). - VAP incidence: 2–16 episodes/1000 ventilator-days; attributable mortality 3–17%; S. aureus is a leading VAP pathogen with 50–80% MRSA in some series (PMID: 23524477). - In a CDC ventilator-associated-event cohort, S. aureus was the most frequently isolated organism (29%) (PMID: 24709718).

Population demographics. - Age/sex: PVL-necrotizing disease — young, previously healthy, often male; nosocomial disease — older comorbid ICU patients. - Geography/variants: PVL prevalence among MRSA varies 0–62% across regions (GCC scoping review, PMID: 42799093); CA-MRSA clones vary by region (USA300 in North America; ST80-IV, ST30, ST22 elsewhere). LA-MRSA CC398 clusters in high-livestock-density areas (18.6–23% of human MRSA in one German region, PMID: 23418434).

Section 10 — Diagnostics

Clinical/lab tests. - Lower-respiratory culture (definitive): endotracheal aspirate vs bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). BAL-directed diagnostic protocols lowered VAP-attributable mortality to ~1% (PMID: 22310133). - Blood cultures for bacteremia; pleural fluid analysis for empyema. - Molecular: metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) identifies co-infections (e.g., PVL+ MSSA + influenza B) when conventional tests fail (PMID: 41619830); PCR for mecA, lukS/F-PV. - CBC: leukopenia is an ominous prognostic sign.

Stewardship diagnostic — MRSA nasal PCR. Nasal-swab PCR has excellent negative predictive value: "The MRSA PCR assay demonstrated 88.0% sensitivity and 90.1% specificity, with a positive predictive value of 35.4% and a negative predictive value of 99.2%" (PMID: 24277023). A negative swab safely de-escalates empiric vancomycin (PMID: 38028911); use caution after intranasal mupirocin decolonization (PMID: 32959059).

Imaging. Chest X-ray/CT: multilobar consolidation, cavitation/necrosis, effusion, pneumatoceles, abscess.

Genetic/omics diagnostics for the host: Not applicable. (Host WGS/WES/panels/karyotype/CMA have no role.) Pathogen genomics (WGS, spa-typing, MLST) is used for surveillance/typing.

Clinical criteria & differential. IDSA/ATS HAP/VAP and CAP guidelines. Differential: pneumococcal, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, Acinetobacter, and viral pneumonias; distinguishing features of S. aureus necrotizing disease include rapid cavitation, hemoptysis, leukopenia, and antecedent influenza.

Recovery: Survivors may recover fully but face post-ICU morbidity. Disease is self-limited with cure or rapidly fatal without effective therapy.

Section 12 — Treatment

Pharmacotherapy (first-line). - MRSA pneumonia: vancomycin OR linezolid — "Both linezolid and vancomycin are approved by USFDA and IDSA guidelines for the management of nosocomial pneumonia due to methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)" (PMID: 36852899). Linezolid shows higher clinical cure (53–59%) and less nephrotoxicity than vancomycin (43–47%) in HAP/VAP (PMID: 38238670, PMID: 36852899); vancomycin penetrates alveolar fluid poorly (PMID: 21722847). NCIT: Vancomycin (C935), Linezolid (C1697). - MSSA pneumonia/bacteremia: cefazolin or anti-staphylococcal penicillins (nafcillin/oxacillin) — "cefazolin or antistaphylococcal penicillins should be the first-line treatment for methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bacteraemia," with cefazolin causing less nephrotoxicity (PMID: 40349971). NCIT: Cefazolin (C341), Nafcillin, Oxacillin. - Toxin suppression: linezolid and clindamycin inhibit toxin synthesis; toxin-inhibiting regimens improved outcomes in necrotizing CAP (PMID: 21051855, PMID: 21722847). - Alternatives/resistant strains: daptomycin (bloodstream, not lung — inactivated by surfactant), telavancin; MDRO guidance recommends linezolid or vancomycin for MRSA pneumonia (PMID: 42661422).

Advanced/experimental therapeutics. - Anti-alpha-toxin monoclonal antibody — suvratoxumab (MEDI4893): SAATELLITE phase 2 RCT evaluated its efficacy "in reducing the incidence of S aureus pneumonia in patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) who are on mechanical ventilation" (PMID: 33894131). - Anti-Hla scFvs / multivalent nanobodies: increased survival comparable to vancomycin in murine MRSA pneumonia (PMID: 42133074, PMID: 42161902); AAV-delivered anti-α-toxin mAbs protect in models (PMID: 41941484). - Anti-virulence small molecules: agrA inhibitor norwogonin (PMID: 38990361); ClpP inhibitor sesamin (PMID: 39805732); Hla inhibitors (Jingfang mixture, PMID: 39083107). - Host-directed: GSDMD inhibitor disulfiram ameliorated co-infection pneumonia (PMID: 41927528); innate immune priming with DDM (PMID: 41619354).

Surgical/interventional & supportive. Pleural drainage/chest tube for empyema; source control; prone positioning and lung-protective ventilation for ARDS; ECMO in refractory cases (though selected fulminant MSSA managed without ECMO, PMID: 42454265).

Pharmacogenomics. Not a major factor; vancomycin dosing is guided by renal function/therapeutic drug monitoring rather than host genotype.

Section 13 — Prevention

  • Primary prevention: VAP bundles (head-of-bed elevation, oral care, sedation minimization, subglottic suctioning), hand hygiene, aspiration precautions, and antimicrobial stewardship; influenza vaccination to remove the key viral trigger.
  • Secondary prevention: MRSA nasal PCR screening for stewardship/de-escalation; active surveillance; MRSA decolonization (intranasal mupirocin) in high-risk/ICU settings.
  • Immunization: No licensed S. aureus vaccine exists — "Despite decades of effort, vaccine development has been unsuccessful, highlighting the need for alternative approaches" (PMID: 41941484). Toxoid/nanotoxoid and multi-antigen vaccines protect mice (PMID: 37097076, PMID: 32197534) but none are approved.
  • Prophylaxis: anti-Hla mAb (suvratoxumab) for ventilated, colonized ICU patients (PMID: 33894131).
  • Public health / One Health: livestock-associated MRSA CC398 surveillance and occupational infection control (PMID: 23418434).

Section 14 — Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy: Staphylococcus aureus — NCBI:txid1280. Host species: human (NCBI:txid9606); reservoir/affected animals: pig (Sus scrofa, txid9823), cattle (Bos taurus, txid9913), poultry (chicken, txid9031).
  • Natural disease / veterinary relevance: S. aureus causes bovine mastitis and infections across livestock; "Pigs, cattle and poultry are colonized with MRSA and the zoonotic transmission of such MRSA to humans via direct animal contact, environmental contaminations or meat are a matter of concern. Livestock-associated (LA) MRSA are mostly belonging to clonal complex (CC) 398" (PMID: 23418434). Other livestock lineages: CC5, CC9, CC97.
  • Zoonotic transmission: direct animal contact, environmental contamination, and meat.
  • Comparative biology: Hla/PVL toxin mechanisms are studied primarily in murine lungs; ADAM10 is conserved across mammals, supporting cross-species mechanistic relevance.

Section 15 — Model Organisms

  • Standard model: murine intranasal/intratracheal S. aureus pneumonia, especially CA-MRSA USA300 — the workhorse for pathogenesis and therapeutics (PMID: 42161902). Disease is "driven by potent virulence factors such as T-cell superantigens and alpha hemolysin," including SEB, SEC, and TSST-1.
  • Genetic models: Adam10 lung-epithelium conditional knockout (resistant to lethal Hla pneumonia — landmark genetic proof, PMID: 21926978); IL-17R⁻/⁻, IL-22⁻/⁻, IL-1R1⁻/⁻ mice (impaired clearance, PMID: 21178015, PMID: 24089191); myeloid IFNAR1-deletion (improved survival, PMID: 42089309).
  • Co-infection models: influenza A (PR8/H1N1) + MRSA sequential-infection mice (PMID: 41927528).
  • Large-animal: porcine MRSA pneumonia models (ETT biofilm, telavancin/linezolid efficacy) (PMID: 30093624, PMID: 33046500).
  • In vitro: A549 alveolar epithelial cells, RAW264.7 macrophages, primary human neutrophils.
  • Phenotype recapitulation: murine models reproduce necrotizing pneumonia, toxin-mediated barrier injury, and neutrophil lysis well; limitations include species differences in PVL susceptibility (human neutrophils are far more PVL-sensitive than murine), immune-response divergence, and difficulty modeling the human influenza-priming context.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Template mismatch. This template is designed for genetic/rare diseases; sections on causal human genes, ACMG variant classification, inheritance, penetrance, carrier frequency, and host genetic testing are not applicable. The "molecular genetics" of this disease is bacterial.
  2. Human vs model evidence. Much mechanistic detail (Hla/ADAM10, PSMα necroptosis, GSDMD pyroptosis) is from mouse/in-vitro work; human PVL biology differs (human neutrophils far more PVL-sensitive), complicating translation.
  3. PVL causal debate. PVL is a strong clinical marker of severe necrotizing disease, but its precise causal weight vs Hla/PSMα in humans remains debated; PVL-negative necrotizing MSSA also occurs (PMID: 41585354).
  4. Epidemiologic heterogeneity. Prevalence/mortality vary widely by region, clone, and care setting; a precise incidence per 100,000 for "S. aureus pneumonia" specifically is not cleanly defined in the literature reviewed.
  5. No approved vaccine or anti-toxin therapy is yet standard of care, despite promising models and phase-2 trials.
  6. No primary experimental dataset was analyzed here; conclusions are literature-derived.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Human validation of anti-Hla prophylaxis: analyze SAATELLITE and successor phase-3 outcomes stratified by baseline PVL/agr genotype and influenza co-infection status.
  2. Prospective PVL/agr-typed cohort: quantify independent contributions of Hla vs PVL vs PSMα to human mortality, adjusting for MRSA status and influenza.
  3. Host-directed therapy trials: test GSDMD inhibition (disulfiram) and Type 17 augmentation (IL-23/IL-17 axis) as adjuncts in severe/co-infection pneumonia.
  4. Biomarker development: validate leukopenia, hemoptysis, and circulating toxin/ADAM10-cleaved E-cadherin fragments as early prognostic markers.
  5. Diagnostic stewardship rollout: broaden MRSA nasal-PCR-guided de-escalation and mNGS for co-infection detection in refractory pneumonia.
  6. One-Health surveillance: monitor LA-MRSA CC398 spillover and PVL acquisition by hospital lineages.
  7. Vaccine antigen strategy: pursue multi-antigen/toxoid formulations (Hla + PVL + PSM + superantigens) given single-antigen failures.
