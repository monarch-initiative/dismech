SLC25A46-Related Hereditary Motor And Sensory Neuropathy

Mendelian MONDO:0014671 Pathograph 36 Show in embeddings browser hereditary neurological disease peripheral nervous system disease mitochondrial disease

HMSN6B is an autosomal recessive axonal Charcot-Marie-Tooth neuropathy combined with optic atrophy, caused by biallelic variants in SLC25A46. The gene encodes a highly derived member of the SLC25 mitochondrial carrier family that is unusual in two ways: it sits in the outer mitochondrial membrane rather than the inner one, and it has no identified transported substrate. It is instead the closest human relative of yeast Ugo1, an adaptor of the mitochondrial fusion machinery, and it works as a structural organiser of mitochondrial membrane dynamics. The protein sits at the membrane sites where mitochondria divide and merge, in discrete foci at branch points and tube tips alongside DRP1 and OPA1, and it forms complexes with the fusion GTPases MFN1, MFN2 and OPA1, with the MICOS cristae-organising complex through MIC60, and with the ER membrane protein complex at ER-mitochondria contacts. Losing it therefore breaks three things at once: the fission-fusion balance, so the network becomes hyperfused and hyperfilamentous; cristae architecture, which shortens the folds that house the respiratory complexes; and non-vesicular lipid transfer, which shifts mitochondrial phospholipid composition. The downstream cost is paid by neurons that must deliver and position mitochondria along very long processes. Enlarged, incompletely divided organelles are transported and distributed abnormally, and the cells that fail are the predicted ones: retinal ganglion cells and their optic nerve axons, long peripheral motor and sensory axons, and cerebellar Purkinje cells. Severity tracks how much functional protein a genotype leaves, and it does so through two distinct molecular routes rather than one. Most pathogenic missense variants destabilise the protein, which is then ubiquitylated by MULAN and MARCH5 and stripped from the outer membrane by p97 and the proteasome; the degree of destabilisation tracks disease severity, protein stability itself correlating inversely with it. Milder variants leave protein abundance largely intact and instead disturb a specific interaction, such as the one with MIC60. That graded relationship is why one gene spans a spectrum whose ends are usually named as different diseases: near-complete loss presents as lethal congenital pontocerebellar hypoplasia (PCH1E) or Leigh syndrome, while partially functional alleles give the childhood-onset optic atrophy and axonal neuropathy that this entry covers, and the mildest reported case is an apparently isolated optic neuropathy with only subclinical neuropathy on electrophysiology. This entry is scoped to the neuropathy-with-optic-atrophy rung; PCH1E is curated as a subtype of Pontocerebellar_Hypoplasia. No disease-modifying treatment exists and management is supportive. The one encouraging therapeutic result is preclinical: systemic AAV-PHP.B delivery of Slc25a46 to knockout mice rescued premature death, improved nerve conduction and mobility, and normalised mitochondrial morphology in both central and peripheral nervous tissue. A natural-history study was registered to collect the longitudinal data the disease lacks; its current recruitment status has not been re-checked against the registry.

Does SLC25A46 promote mitochondrial fission or mitochondrial fusion, and which direction of imbalance actually drives neurodegeneration in patients?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN slc25a46_fission_or_fusion
Attached to
pathophysiology#Loss of SLC25A46 at Mitochondrial Fission and Fusion Sites pathophysiology#Dysregulated MFN1/MFN2 and OPA1 Oligomerization pathophysiology#Aberrant Mitochondrial Network Morphology
This entry's causal chain runs through loss of fission and a shift toward MFN1/MFN2-mediated hyperfusion, which is what patient fibroblasts and pathogenic missense variants show. But the primary literature does not agree on the protein's direction of action, and the disagreement is explicit rather than apparent. The founding study concluded that SLC25A46 acts pro-fission and found no interaction with MFN2 or OPA1, only with mitofilin. Subsequent work reported direct interactions with both fusion GTPases and mapped the interfaces. Morphology does not settle it either: knockdown and pathogenic variants give hyperfusion, whereas a complete knockout cell line gives fragmentation, and the C. elegans orthologue behaves as a fusion factor whose phenotype is suppressed by loss of the fission factor drp-1 and rescued by overexpressing the mitofusin orthologue. A resource that asserts a single direction without recording the disagreement would overstate what is known, and the direction matters because it determines whether a therapeutic strategy should push the balance toward fission or toward fusion.

Why do retinal ganglion cells, long peripheral axons and Purkinje cells degenerate when SLC25A46 is lost, while the ubiquitously expressed protein leaves most tissues clinically unaffected?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN slc25a46_tissue_selectivity
Attached to
pathophysiology#Respiratory Chain Deficiency and Bioenergetic Failure pathophysiology#Impaired Mitochondrial Transport and Distribution in Neuronal Processes
The protein is expressed broadly and its interactome spans OXPHOS complexes, adenine nucleotide translocases, MICOS subunits and ER contact-site components in cerebellum, cerebrum, heart and thymus alike. Yet the disease is almost entirely neurological, and within the nervous system it picks out three cell populations. Transcript enrichment in retinal ganglion cells is part of the answer but cannot be the whole of it, since Purkinje cells and long peripheral axons are hit without a comparable enrichment claim. The plausible explanation is that these cells have the longest or most trafficking-dependent processes and the least metabolic reserve, so they are the first to fail when organelle delivery and cristae integrity degrade together. That remains a hypothesis: no study has compared the threshold for degeneration across cell types in the same system. The mouse models also report a lymphoid phenotype and neuromuscular junction abnormalities that have no recognised clinical counterpart in patients, which sharpens rather than resolves the question.

Biallelic SLC25A46 Loss of Function
Homozygous or compound heterozygous SLC25A46 variants remove or cripple a highly derived SLC25-family protein of the outer mitochondrial membrane. The protein has no identified transported substrate and is the closest human relative of yeast Ugo1, an adaptor of the fusion machinery, so the lesion is a loss of structural organisation of mitochondrial membrane dynamics rather than loss of a transport activity.

5

Conditions with similar clinical presentations that must be differentiated from SLC25A46-Related Hereditary Motor And Sensory Neuropathy:

MFN2-related Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 2A
Overlapping Features The closest mimic, and not a coincidence: MFN2 is one of the fusion GTPases SLC25A46 partners with. MFN2 causes axonal CMT2 and a subset of patients also develop optic atrophy, giving the same two-feature combination. The separators are inheritance, dominant for MFN2 against recessive here, and the gene.
Distinguishing Features
  • Dominant inheritance in most MFN2 disease, against obligate biallelic SLC25A46 variants
  • Molecular testing is the definitive separator; the clinical overlap is genuine

description: >-
  HMSN6B is an autosomal recessive axonal Charcot-Marie-Tooth neuropathy combined
  with optic atrophy, caused by biallelic variants in SLC25A46. The gene encodes a
  highly derived member of the SLC25 mitochondrial carrier family that is unusual
  in two ways: it sits in the outer mitochondrial membrane rather than the inner
  one, and it has no identified transported substrate. It is instead the closest
  human relative of yeast Ugo1, an adaptor of the mitochondrial fusion machinery,
  and it works as a structural organiser of mitochondrial membrane dynamics.


  The protein sits at the membrane sites where mitochondria divide and merge, in
  discrete foci at branch points and tube tips alongside DRP1 and OPA1, and it
  forms complexes with the fusion GTPases MFN1, MFN2 and OPA1, with the MICOS
  cristae-organising complex through MIC60, and with the ER membrane protein
  complex at ER-mitochondria contacts. Losing it therefore breaks three things at
  once: the fission-fusion balance, so the network becomes hyperfused and
  hyperfilamentous; cristae architecture, which shortens the folds that house the
  respiratory complexes; and non-vesicular lipid transfer, which shifts
  mitochondrial phospholipid composition. The downstream cost is paid by neurons
  that must deliver and position mitochondria along very long processes. Enlarged,
  incompletely divided organelles are transported and distributed abnormally, and
  the cells that fail are the predicted ones: retinal ganglion cells and their
  optic nerve axons, long peripheral motor and sensory axons, and cerebellar
  Purkinje cells.


  Severity tracks how much functional protein a genotype leaves, and it does so
  through two distinct molecular routes rather than one. Most pathogenic missense
  variants destabilise the protein, which is then ubiquitylated by MULAN and
  MARCH5 and stripped from the outer membrane by p97 and the proteasome; the
  degree of destabilisation tracks disease severity, protein stability itself
  correlating inversely with it.
  Milder variants leave protein abundance largely intact and instead disturb a
  specific interaction, such as the one with MIC60. That graded relationship is
  why one gene spans a spectrum whose ends are usually named as different
  diseases: near-complete loss presents as lethal congenital pontocerebellar
  hypoplasia (PCH1E) or Leigh syndrome, while partially functional alleles give
  the childhood-onset optic atrophy and axonal neuropathy that this entry covers,
  and the mildest reported case is an apparently isolated optic neuropathy with
  only subclinical neuropathy on electrophysiology. This entry is scoped to the
  neuropathy-with-optic-atrophy rung; PCH1E is curated as a subtype of
  Pontocerebellar_Hypoplasia.


  No disease-modifying treatment exists and management is supportive. The one
  encouraging therapeutic result is preclinical: systemic AAV-PHP.B delivery of
  Slc25a46 to knockout mice rescued premature death, improved nerve conduction and
  mobility, and normalised mitochondrial morphology in both central and peripheral
  nervous tissue. A natural-history study was registered to collect the longitudinal
  data the disease lacks; its current recruitment status has not been re-checked
  against the registry.

  Entry name. The MONDO label for the bound term is the positional
  "neuropathy, hereditary motor and sensory, type 6B". The gene-related name used
  here is established as a MONDO EXACT synonym of the same term, "SLC25A46

  entry's pathograph. The "type 6" suffix is dropped because HMSN type 6 is the
  parent class, which also contains the MFN2-related type 6A and the PDXK-related
  type VIc, so carrying it would make the name broader than the entry. All prior
  names are kept in synonyms.


  Scope. SLC25A46 causes a graded allelic spectrum, and this entry is the
  neuropathy-with-optic-atrophy rung of it, which is what MONDO:0014671 and
  OMIM:616505 denote. The severe ends have their own terms and their own homes:
  pontocerebellar hypoplasia type 1E is curated as a has_subtypes row on
  Pontocerebellar_Hypoplasia under MONDO:0030260, and SLC25A46-related Leigh
  syndrome is a separate ClinGen gene-disease pair rated Limited. Both are recorded
  in differential_diagnoses rather than restated here. The ataxia-predominant and
  progressive-myoclonic-ataxia presentations are not split out, because the
  published families are too few to separate them from this rung on anything other
  than which feature happened to dominate at presentation.


  GeneReviews. There is no SLC25A46-specific chapter and no chapter titled for this
  disease; a PubMed search of genereviews[book] for SLC25A46 in title or abstract
  returns nothing, and a search for the gene symbol returns nothing. The
  Charcot-Marie-Tooth Hereditary Neuropathy Overview is tagged in references as the
  disease-family baseline, but its PubMed abstract is only a statement of the
  chapter's purpose and contains no clinical content, so no phenotype in this entry
  rests on it. The phenotype baseline here is therefore the primary literature plus
  the ClinGen curation, which is weaker than a GeneReviews baseline and is the main
  limitation of this entry.


  Frequencies. VERY_FREQUENT is used only for the two core features that every
  series reports. Most phenotypes carry no frequency at all, because the published
  cohorts are small case reports and family series that do not tabulate findings
  systematically, and guessing a band from a handful of patients would invent
  precision. Myoclonus and spasticity are deliberately among those: the sources name
  them as part of the spectrum without quantifying them.


  Unevidenced claims. Three items carry no evidence and say so rather than reaching
  for a quote that does not support them. Optic disc pallor is the expected
  ophthalmoscopic sign of the optic atrophy, and none of the cited reports describes
  disc appearance; the foot surgery and low-vision support treatments are standard
  management of the deformity and of visual loss, and the cited literature never
  discusses either. Each records that absence in its own notes.



  Phenotypes outside the causal graph. Myoclonus and spasticity have no inbound
  causal edge, because the entry has no cortical or pyramidal pathophysiology node
  to originate one and the evidence does not support inventing one. Both are
  therefore recorded as features of the spectrum that this entry's mechanism does
  not yet explain, which is the honest state rather than a wiring omission.

  amplitudes and relatively preserved velocities. The narrower term would assert the

1. Disease information

Overview. CMT6B (HMSN6B) is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in SLC25A46. The core phenotype combines: - early-onset optic atrophy; and - axonal sensorimotor peripheral neuropathy (CMT2-like).

Many patients also have cerebellar ataxia and cerebellar atrophy. Abrams et al. (2015) reported the gene after whole-exome sequencing of patients with optic atrophy and CMT2 identified four families with recessive SLC25A46 mutations. They described it as an "optic atrophy spectrum disorder" that overlaps clinically with OPA1- and MFN2-related disease (Abrams et al., Nat Genet 2015;47:926–932, PMID:26168012 — confirmed).

The disorder sits on a severity spectrum: - Mildest: isolated or mild optic atrophy with survival into adulthood. - Classic: optic atrophy plus axonal neuropathy (HMSN6B). - Intermediate: progressive myoclonic ataxia with optic atrophy and neuropathy (Charlesworth et al., Mov Disord 2016;31:1249–1251, doi:10.1002/mds.26716). - Ataxia-predominant: autosomal recessive cerebellar ataxia without optic atrophy (Hammer et al., Neurodegener Dis 2017;17:208–212, doi:10.1159/000464445). - Severe: Leigh syndrome (Janer et al., EMBO Mol Med 2016, PMID:27390132 — confirmed). - Most severe: lethal congenital pontocerebellar hypoplasia (Wan et al., Brain 2016;139:2877, PMID:27543974 — confirmed).

  • Causal factor. The cause is genetic: biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) germline variants in SLC25A46. No environmental cause has been described.

4. Genetic and molecular information

  • Gene. SLC25A46, at 5q22.1. It encodes an atypical member of the mitochondrial carrier family (SLC25) that sits in the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM). It is a distant homolog of yeast Ugo1. Abrams et al. (2015) put it this way: "SLC25A46, like Ugo1, is a modified carrier protein that has been recruited to the outer mitochondrial membrane and interacts with the inner membrane remodeling protein mitofilin (Fcj1)."

  • p.T142I, p.R257Q, p.E335D, p.G249D;
  • p.L341P (the PCH-associated variant in Wan et al. 2016) [verify];
  • p.Trp160Ser, in North African ataxia families.

The other reported classes are frameshift and nonsense variants, a splice-site variant, and exonic deletions (an exonic deletion in a PCH family; Wan et al. 2016). No dominant-negative effect has been reported. - Modifier genes, epigenetics, chromosomal abnormalities. None reported.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. Biallelic SLC25A46 loss-of-function variants lead to absent, unstable or interaction-defective SLC25A46 protein in the outer mitochondrial membrane.
  2. Destabilizing missense variants are degraded by the ubiquitin–proteasome system (Steffen 2017 [verify]; Wan 2016; Janer 2016).
  3. Loss of SLC25A46 results in an imbalance of fission and fusion towards MFN1/2-mediated mitochondrial hyperfusion, giving elongated, hyperconnected networks.
  4. Shown in patient fibroblasts, knockout cells and zebrafish (Abrams 2015; Janer 2016).
  5. SLC25A46 interacts with MFN2 and OPA1. A 2023 Life Science Alliance study in knockout fibroblasts (testing p.T142I, p.R257Q and p.E335D) localized SLC25A46 to discrete foci involved in fission and fusion.
  6. A 2024 C. elegans preprint argues instead for a role in promoting fusion through FZO-1. That preprint is not peer-reviewed.
  7. Branch A, cristae and membrane lipids. Loss of the SLC25A46–MIC60 (MICOS) interaction leads to abnormal cristae architecture and altered mitochondrial phospholipid composition. Janer et al. (2016) report that SLC25A46 functions upstream of MICOS and is required for cristae maintenance, with changes in ER morphology suggesting a defect at ER–mitochondria contact sites.
  8. Branch B, organelle distribution. Enlarged, hyperfused mitochondria lead to impaired axonal transport and abnormal distribution in long axons. This was shown in Slc25a46-knockout mouse neurons (Li et al., Hum Mol Genet 2017;26:3776, PMID:28934388 [verify]).
  9. Defective cristae and impaired mitophagy or quality control lead to reduced oxidative phosphorylation. Reported findings include:
  10. impaired respiration;
  11. reduced respiratory-chain complex activities and a raised lactate:pyruvate ratio in some patient fibroblasts;
  12. premature senescence (Janer 2016).

The link between step 4 and the energy deficit is inferred from cell and mouse data. 6. A bioenergetic and trafficking deficit in neurons with long axons and high energy demand results in degeneration of: - retinal ganglion cells and optic nerve axons, causing optic atrophy; - peripheral motor and sensory axons, causing axonal neuropathy; - cerebellar Purkinje cells, causing ataxia and cerebellar atrophy.

All three are shown in knockout mice (Li 2017; Terzenidou et al., PLoS Genet 2017, PMC5380310, PMID:28376086 [verify]). 7. In the most destabilizing genotypes, near-complete loss during brain development leads to pontocerebellar hypoplasia and neonatal death (Wan 2016) or to Leigh syndrome (Janer 2016). The genotype–severity relationship is graded.

10. Diagnostics

  • Electrophysiology. Nerve conduction studies show reduced or absent sensory and motor amplitudes with relatively preserved conduction velocities, i.e. an axonal pattern. EMG shows chronic denervation.
  • Ophthalmology. Fundoscopy shows optic disc pallor. OCT shows thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer. Visual evoked potentials are abnormal.
  • Imaging. Brain MRI may show cerebellar atrophy, pontocerebellar hypoplasia in severe cases, or Leigh-type lesions.
  • Laboratory. Lactate and the lactate:pyruvate ratio are raised in some patients. Respiratory-chain enzymology in fibroblasts or muscle may show combined deficiency.
  • Pathology. Nerve biopsy (where performed) shows axonal loss. In fibroblasts, hyperfused mitochondrial networks support functional confirmation.
  • Genetic testing. Exome or genome sequencing, or a CMT/hereditary optic neuropathy panel that includes SLC25A46. Deletion/duplication analysis is needed because exonic deletions have been reported. Confirm biallelic phase by parental testing.
  • Differential diagnosis. MFN2-related CMT2A with optic atrophy (CMT6A), OPA1 "plus" disease, Friedreich ataxia, other mitochondrial disorders (POLG, NDUFS), Behr syndrome (OPA1, OPA3), and other forms of pontocerebellar hypoplasia (TSEN, EXOSC3).
  • Clinical criteria and screening. There are no formal criteria and no newborn screening. Cascade carrier testing and prenatal or preimplantation diagnosis are available once the familial variants are known.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Classic HMSN6B: survival into adulthood with progressive visual and motor disability.
  • PCH and Leigh forms: death in the neonatal period or infancy (Wan 2016; Janer 2016; J Pers Med 2021).
  • Prognostic factor: protein stability of the variant, i.e. destabilizing variants carry a worse outcome (Wan 2016; Abrams 2018).
  • Data gaps: no survival statistics or QoL data are available.

13. Prevention

There is no primary prevention. The available measures are: - genetic counseling, with 25% recurrence risk for carrier parents; - carrier testing of relatives; - prenatal or preimplantation genetic diagnosis; - tertiary care for the feet, falls and vision.
