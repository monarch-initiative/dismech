Neurodevelopmental Disorder With Seizures And Gingival Overgrowth

Mendelian MONDO:0859148 Pathograph 32 Show in embeddings browser Neurodevelopmental Disorder

Neurodevelopmental disorder with seizures and gingival overgrowth (NEDSGO) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in TBC1D2B, which encodes a TBC-domain RAB GTPase-activating protein. It was defined in 2020 in three unrelated individuals and has since been reported in roughly 20 affected individuals from about a dozen families. Gingival overgrowth usually starts in early childhood, and most patients develop seizures, with onset anywhere from infancy to the fourth decade. Early development may be normal or delayed. In about half of the reported patients the disease is progressive: mental deterioration with gait ataxia and slurred speech begins between about 5 and 20 years, with cerebellar and/or cerebral atrophy, movement disorders (tremor, dystonia, myoclonus), flexion contractures, visual loss and, at the severe end, loss of ambulation, respiratory failure needing ventilation and death in early adulthood. The jaw is often abnormal, with mandibular prognathia and, in a few patients, fibrous dysplasia of the mandible or maxilla diagnosed as cherubism. Low blood counts, hearing loss and facial freckling have been reported in some patients. Disease alleles are nonsense, frameshift, splice and one missense variant; patient fibroblasts show absent or reduced TBC1D2B protein. TBC1D2B is a GAP for RAB7 recruited by RAB31 and RAB22A, localises to RAB5-positive early endocytic vesicles, binds LC3/GABARAP proteins, and promotes autophagy initiation. TBC1D2B-knockout HeLa cells internalise less epidermal growth factor and die more readily under serum starvation. How these cellular defects produce the neurodegeneration, the seizures and the gingival and jaw overgrowth has not been studied in neurons, gingiva, bone or an animal model, so every edge beyond the cell-line findings is recorded as inferred. Treatment is symptomatic: antiseizure medication and gingivectomy.

Nonsense, frameshift and splice alleles abolish or sharply reduce TBC1D2B mRNA and protein in patient cells; in the one compound heterozygote with a missense allele, protein is halved. TBC1D2B is a TBC-domain RAB GTPase-activating protein, so its loss removes a RAB GAP activity, whose best-characterised substrate is RAB7.

  an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants

  respiratory failure needing ventilation and death in early adulthood. The jaw
  is often abnormal, with mandibular prognathia and, in a few patients, fibrous
  dysplasia of the mandible or maxilla diagnosed as cherubism. Low blood counts,
  hearing loss and facial freckling have been reported in some patients.

  patient fibroblasts show absent or reduced TBC1D2B protein. TBC1D2B is a GAP

  initiation. TBC1D2B-knockout

  been studied in neurons, gingiva, bone or an animal model, so every edge
  beyond the cell-line findings is recorded as inferred. Treatment is
  symptomatic: antiseizure medication and gingivectomy.

Ontology IDs were looked up through OAK/OLS during this session. Anything taken only from web pages is marked as such.

1. Disease information

Overview. NEDSGO is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in TBC1D2B. That gene encodes a TBC-domain RAB GTPase-activating protein (RABGAP). The disorder was first reported as a neurodevelopmental disorder (Harms et al., 2020). The 2024 cohort of 10 subjects redefines it as a progressive neurological disease. Its main features are seizures, gingival overgrowth that starts in early childhood, and abnormal mandible morphology (prognathia, fibrous dysplasia or cherubism). In many patients these are followed by neurodegeneration beginning between ages 10 and 20.

  • Harms et al. 2024 (PMID:38374468), verbatim: "The TBC1D2B disorder is a progressive neurological disease with gingival overgrowth and abnormal mandible morphology."

2. Etiology

  • Cause: biallelic loss of function in germline TBC1D2B, either homozygous or compound heterozygous.
  • Harms 2020 (PMID:32623794), verbatim: "We report biallelic loss-of-function variants in TBC1D2B ... as the underlying cause of cognitive impairment, seizures, and/or gingival overgrowth in three individuals from unrelated families."
  • Risk factors:
  • Consanguinity is the only documented one. It was reported in 2 of 8 families in Table 1 of PMID:38374468, including the Brazilian brothers.
  • No environmental risk factors are known.
  • Protective factors and gene-environment interactions: none reported.
  • Possible drug contribution: in subject 7 the gingival overgrowth was footnoted as "Possibly drug induced" (PMID:38374468, Table 1). This is relevant because antiseizure drugs such as phenytoin cause gingival overgrowth, which can confound this feature.

Not applicable. No environmental, lifestyle or infectious factors are known. The one caveat is that drugs, especially phenytoin, may contribute to gingival overgrowth.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. Biallelic TBC1D2B LoF variants (nonsense, frameshift, splice, or a GAP-domain missense) lead to nonsense-mediated decay and/or unstable protein, and so to absent or reduced TBC1D2B. This step is demonstrated in patient fibroblasts and leukocytes (PMID:32623794; PMID:38374468).
  2. Loss of TBC1D2B results in loss of three things:
  3. (a) RABGAP activity toward RAB7. RAB31 recruits TBC1D2B to late endosomes to inactivate RAB7. Without it, multivesicular endosome–lysosome fusion and exosome secretion are predicted to be dysregulated. This is demonstrated in cell lines, not in patients. PMID:32958903, verbatim: "RAB31 recruits GTPase-activating protein TBC1D2B to inactivate RAB7, thereby preventing the fusion of MVEs with lysosomes and enabling the secretion of ILVs as exosomes."
  4. (b) Early-endosome function. TBC1D2B colocalizes with RAB5 vesicles, and EGF internalization is reduced in TBC1D2B-knockout HeLa cells. This is demonstrated in vitro (PMID:32623794).
  5. (c) Autophagy initiation. TBC1D2B has a canonical LIR, binds LC3/GABARAP and ATG12 conjugation complexes, and forms phase-separated condensates when autophagy is induced. This is demonstrated in vitro (PMID:38226533). PMID:38226533, verbatim: "TBC1D2B contains a functional canonical LIR motif and acts at an early stage of autophagy by binding to both LC3/GABARAP and ATG12 conjugation complexes."
  6. Branch A, neurons. Defective endolysosomal trafficking and autophagy may lead to neuronal dysfunction and death. Separately, the knockout cells show more apoptosis when serum-starved (PMID:32623794). This branch is inferred: there are no neuronal data.
  7. Neuronal loss results in cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, which presents as seizures, cognitive regression, ataxia, tremor, myoclonus and dystonia, and eventually bedridden status and respiratory failure. The link is clinically correlated, not mechanistically demonstrated. PMID:38374468, verbatim: "defects in autophagy and the endolysosomal system could be associated with neuronal dysfunction and the neurodegenerative disease".
  8. Branch B, gingiva and jaw. Loss of TBC1D2B is hypothesized to dysregulate fibroblast and osteoclast behaviour, producing gingival overgrowth and fibro-osseous jaw lesions (cherubism, fibrous dysplasia). This branch is entirely hypothetical:
  9. The authors draw an analogy to SH3BP2 in endocytic regulation (PMID:38374468).
  10. PMID:39201553 proposes links to E-cadherin, β-catenin and TGF-β based only on immunohistochemistry of one specimen.
  11. Another source links TBC1D2B to suppression of E-cadherin internalization (PMID:31719531; this one was not fetched or verified).
  12. Branch C, bone marrow. The cytopenias and dyserythropoiesis have no proposed mechanism.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset:
  • gingival overgrowth in early childhood (18 months to 3 years);
  • seizures from 4 months to 32 years;
  • neurological decline from 5 to 20 years, typically 10 to 20.
  • Course: progressive in 6/10, with stepwise loss of ambulation, speech and respiratory independence.
  • Duration and outcome: lifelong; death in early adulthood in 3/10.
  • Remission: none reported.
  • Possible windows for intervention: unknown. The period before regression (5 to 20 years) would be the theoretical window.

9. Inheritance and population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive; HP:0000007 is the standard HPO term (not re-verified in this session).
  • Penetrance: apparently complete in biallelic individuals. Heterozygous parents are unaffected.
  • Expressivity: highly variable, from a mild 35-year-old to death at 21.
  • Prevalence: unknown, ultra-rare. About 11 to 12 patients have been published.
  • Ancestry: Indian, Portuguese, Chechen, Brazilian, Norwegian/Polish, Turkish, German-American, English/Irish (Table 1 of PMID:38374468), plus Thai (PMID:39201553).
  • Founder effects: none known.
  • Sex: 5 male and 5 female in the 2024 cohort.
  • Germline mosaicism and anticipation: not reported.

10. Diagnostics

  • Genetic testing: exome or genome sequencing. Every case was found by trio or singleton ES or GS, with Sanger segregation.
  • TBC1D2B is on PanelApp Australia's Genetic Epilepsy and Intellectual Disability panels (web).
  • RT-PCR or qPCR on leukocyte RNA can resolve splice variants (PMID:38374468).
  • Imaging:
  • brain MRI or CT: cerebral and/or cerebellar atrophy, ventricular dilatation;
  • orthopantomogram: multilocular radiolucent mandibular lesions (cherubism or fibrous dysplasia).
  • EEG: abnormal in 5/8.
  • Labs: complete blood count (cytopenias). One subject was enrolled in the CDA Registry (NCT02964494) because of dyserythropoiesis.
  • Ophthalmology: fundus examination, macular OCT.
  • Differential diagnosis (PMID:38374468):
  • Zimmermann-Laband syndrome / K⁺ channelopathies (KCNH1, KCNN3, KCNK4): these have hypertrichosis and hypoplastic distal phalanges, which NEDSGO lacks.
  • Ramon syndrome (OMIM 266270; ELMO2): cherubism, gingival fibromatosis, seizures, hypertrichosis, but no regression. PMID:39201553 now attributes some "Ramon" cases to TBC1D2B.
  • Isolated cherubism (SH3BP2 gain-of-function, autosomal dominant).
  • Drug-induced gingival overgrowth.
  • Screening: no newborn screening. Carrier and cascade testing in families with known variants is possible.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Mortality: 3/10 died, at 21, 26.8 and 39 years. Respiratory failure and being bedridden came before death. The oldest living patients were 31 and 35.
  • Morbidity:
  • 3/10 bedridden and needing ventilation;
  • wheelchair dependence;
  • absent speech in some.
  • Prognostic factors: none established. Genotype does not predict severity.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: genetic counselling. Recurrence risk for carrier parents is 25%. Prenatal and preimplantation testing is available once familial variants are known.
  • Secondary: early exome sequencing in children with gingival overgrowth plus seizures and/or jaw lesions.
  • Tertiary:
  • surveillance of neurology, vision, hearing, blood counts and respiratory function;
  • dental and maxillofacial follow-up, including watching for brainstem compression from maxillary fibrous dysplasia (DOI:10.1111/cge.14215);
  • avoiding gingival-overgrowth-inducing drugs where possible.
  • Vaccines and public health: not applicable.
