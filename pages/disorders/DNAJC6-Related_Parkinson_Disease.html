DNAJC6-Related Parkinson Disease

Mendelian MONDO:0014231 Pathograph 27 Show in embeddings browser Movement Disorder Autosomal Recessive Early-Onset Parkinsonism

DNAJC6-related Parkinson disease (PARK19) is an autosomal-recessive form of parkinsonism caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in DNAJC6, which encodes auxilin, the neuron-specific clathrin-uncoating co-chaperone. Auxilin works with the constitutive heat-shock protein HSC70 to disassemble the clathrin coat from newly endocytosed synaptic vesicles, so loss of its activity impairs clathrin uncoating, synaptic-vesicle endocytosis and recycling at the nerve terminal, and presynaptic dopamine handling. This places the disease on the presynaptic, endocytic arm of Parkinson pathophysiology alongside SYNJ1 (PARK20), rather than on the mitochondrial arm of PRKN/PINK1. The phenotype spans a juvenile-onset form (PARK19A) whose core features are parkinsonism (bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, postural instability) but in which developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures and other movement disorders usually come first, and a milder early-onset form (PARK19B) presenting as parkinsonism in the third to fifth decade without the additional neurologic features. The two ends are one gene and one mechanism class and are curated here as one entry, with PARK19B as a subtype.

Auxilin Loss of Function
Mechanism confidence: Established
The root lesion is biallelic loss of DNAJC6, which encodes auxilin, a neuron-selective HSP40/DnaJ co-chaperone. Through its J domain auxilin recruits HSC70 and stimulates its ATPase activity to drive ATP-dependent disassembly of the clathrin coat, so a null or deleterious allele removes the neuron's dedicated clathrin-uncoating activity. The reported juvenile allele produced abnormal transcripts and markedly reduced DNAJC6 mRNA, and a later nonsense allele (p.Cys325*) is predicted to truncate the protein, both consistent with loss of function.

Clathrin Coat Disassembly Failure
Mechanism confidence: Established
Without auxilin-driven uncoating, the clathrin coat is not stripped from coated vesicles: auxilin knockout synapses accumulate clathrin-coated vesicles and empty clathrin cages, with coat components sequestered in assembled coats. GAK, the ubiquitous homolog, only partially compensates.

1. Disease Information

Overview. DNAJC6-related Parkinson disease is an autosomal recessive monogenic parkinsonism. It is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in DNAJC6, which encodes auxilin. Auxilin is a neuron-enriched HSP40 (J-domain) co-chaperone that recruits Hsc70 to strip the clathrin coat from vesicles during synaptic vesicle endocytosis.

OMIM's description (mirror of OMIM 615528, link) defines two forms: - PARK19A: autosomal recessive parkinsonism with onset in the first or second decade. Some patients also have intellectual disability and seizures. - PARK19B: onset in the third to fifth decade, slowly progressive, resembles late-onset PD, and responds well to dopaminergic therapy.

GeneReviews says "the majority of individuals have juvenile onset" (before age 21 years). It describes the juvenile form as "rapidly progressive with loss of ambulation in mid-adolescence in the majority of individuals" (GeneReviews NBK570369, snippet level only).

2. Etiology

Cause. The disease is purely genetic: biallelic (homozygous in nearly every report) pathogenic DNAJC6 variants. Almost all known families are consanguineous. - First described by Edvardson et al. 2012 (PMID 22563501, PMC3341348). They used homozygosity mapping and exome sequencing to find a splice variant in DNAJC6 in two patients with juvenile parkinsonism. Abstract: "The mutation was associated with abnormal transcripts and marked reduced DNAJC6 mRNA level."

Genetic risk factors - Biallelic loss-of-function variants are causal (see §4). - Heterozygous carriers: no established increase in risk was found. - Common or sporadic PD: a large 2023 rare-variant burden study (7,184 cases; aminer record) was retrieved, but its snippets do not report DNAJC6 as significant. Population-level DNAJC6 risk evidence is not available. - Expression in sporadic PD (preclinical and postmortem): Darsono et al. (JCI Insight, article 194989) report that "DNAJC6 expression is significantly downregulated in postmortem substantia nigra tissues and transcriptomic datasets from patients with late-onset sporadic PD." They attribute the loss to "impaired transcription mediated by the midbrain-specific factors NURR1/FOXA2 and reduced protein stability regulated by LRRK2."

Environmental risk factors, protective factors, and gene–environment interaction (GxE) - No human data. - The only GxE hint comes from flies: Drosophila with reduced auxilin (aux) are "more sensitive to the toxin paraquat, suggesting that genetic and environmental factors intertwine" (Song et al. 2017, PMID 28147270, FlyBase FBrf0234656). Fly aux is closest to GAK and shares only 31% identity with DNAJC6. - No protective factors are known.

3. Phenotypes

Three phenotypic groups are recognized. Manfield et al. 2024 (PMID 39654465, PMC11952936) list them as: 1. Slowly progressive, levodopa-responsive parkinsonism (PARK19B). 2. Rapidly progressive parkinsonism with intellectual disability, cognitive decline, upper motor neuron signs, dystonia and epilepsy (classic PARK19A). 3. Dystonia-parkinsonism.

The letter notes "Most reported cases are of juvenile onset."

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. Biallelic DNAJC6 nonsense, splice or J-domain missense variants lead to absent or reduced auxilin protein. Nonsense-mediated decay is predicted for truncating variants, and the fall in protein is demonstrated in fibroblasts and CSF (PMIDs 22563501, 26528954, 32472658).
  2. Loss of auxilin results in failure to recruit Hsc70 to clathrin-coated vesicles, which impairs clathrin uncoating. This is demonstrated in knockout mouse synapses, which accumulate clathrin-coated vesicles and empty cages (Yim 2010). Partial GAK compensation may explain why the effect is cell-type selective; this is inferred.
  3. Impaired uncoating leads to defective clathrin-mediated synaptic vesicle endocytosis and recycling, because coat components stay trapped in assembled coats (Yim 2010; Vidyadhara 2023, PMID 36920906).
  4. Branch A, dopamine handling in the presynaptic terminal. Poor endocytosis and SV protein sorting result in:
  5. Dopamine transporter (DAT) being misrouted into axonal membrane deformities.
  6. Slower dopamine reuptake.
  7. Elevated presynaptic macroautophagy.
  8. Inefficient packaging of dopamine into vesicles.

These lead to dopamine dyshomeostasis: striatal dopamine down 14.5% at 3 months and 52% at 9 months, and DOPAC up. This is shown in mice (Vidyadhara 2023). In patients, low CSF HVA and reduced presynaptic dopaminergic proteins point the same way (Ng 2020). In LRRK2 neurons, the same auxilin-dependent endocytic defect causes oxidized dopamine to accumulate, glucocerebrosidase activity to fall and α-synuclein to rise (Nguyen & Krainc 2018, PNAS, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1717590115). That this happens in DNAJC6 patients is inferred. 5. Branch B, membrane lipids. J-domain mutations result in reduced phosphoinositides and other polyunsaturated membrane lipids. These defects are rescued by Synaptojanin-1, the product of another parkinsonism gene (fly knock-in carrying the human R927G change; Jacquemyn 2023, PMID 36739293). 6. Branch C, neurodevelopment. Defective endocytosis impairs WNT–LMX1A signaling during midbrain dopaminergic neuron development. This produces intrinsically vulnerable dopaminergic neurons and neurodevelopmental defects (CRISPR-edited human embryonic stem cell midbrain organoids; Wulansari 2021, Sci Adv 7:eabb1540, PMID 33597231 as shown in a BVS record). This branch plausibly explains the infantile developmental delay and intellectual disability in PARK19A; that link is inferred. 7. Branches A–C together lead to α-synuclein pathology (pSer129), mitochondrial and lysosomal dysfunction, and neuroinflammation (gliosis at 9 months). The result is selective loss of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons, about 40% by 9 months in knockout mice, with the VTA relatively spared (Vidyadhara 2023; Wulansari 2021). Astrocytic DNAJC6 loss adds proinflammatory and phagocytic defects (Darsono, JCI Insight). 8. Nigrostriatal denervation (absent or reduced DaT uptake in patients) results in parkinsonism and dystonia. Wider synaptic dysfunction in cortical and hippocampal neurons is thought to cause epilepsy, intellectual disability and cognitive decline; this is inferred.

Upstream vs downstream: Uncoating and endocytosis failure (steps 2–3) is upstream. Dopamine and lipid dyshomeostasis (steps 4–5) sit in the middle. α-synuclein aggregation, inflammation and neuron death (step 7) are downstream.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ: brain (nervous system). Secondary involvement of the gut has been reported (gastroparesis; Manfield 2024).
  • Primary sites (suggested UBERON labels): substantia nigra pars compacta; striatum (dorsal striatum); nigrostriatal tract. Cerebral cortex and cerebellum show atrophy in a few patients.
  • Cells: midbrain dopaminergic neurons and astrocytes.
  • Subcellular (suggested GO cellular-component labels): clathrin-coated vesicle; presynapse/synaptic vesicle; axon terminal membrane; autophagosome; lysosome; mitochondrion; cytosol.
  • Lateralization: Bilateral and generalized. No data on asymmetry were found.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset:
  • PARK19A: motor onset at 7–13 years. Developmental delay can be evident in infancy (Ng 2020), and seizures can start at 1–5 years.
  • PARK19B: onset at 21–42 years (Olgiati 2016).
  • Onset is insidious.
  • Progression:
  • PARK19A is rapidly progressive. Ambulation is lost in mid-adolescence, and patients are wheelchair-bound or bedridden 10–15 years after onset.
  • PARK19B progresses slowly.
  • The course is chronic and lifelong. No remission occurs; levodopa gives only partial or symptomatic benefit.
  • Critical periods: Organoid data suggest that a developmental window (WNT–LMX1A-dependent dopaminergic neuron specification) is affected before symptoms appear. This is inferred from in vitro work.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive (OMIM; LOVD; GenCC).
  • Penetrance: appears complete in homozygotes in the reported families. There are not enough data for a formal estimate.
  • Expressivity: highly variable, ranging from infantile developmental delay with epilepsy to adult PD resembling typical PD.
  • Consanguinity: present in most families (Palestinian, Turkish, Yemeni, Pakistani).
  • Founder effects: p.Arg256* was found in two Pakistani families from the same region (Ng 2020). This suggests a founder allele, which is inferred.
  • Anticipation and germline mosaicism: not reported.
  • Carrier frequency: not retrieved.
  • Prevalence: "Rare" (GeneReviews table). No numeric prevalence or incidence exists.
  • Geography: Mostly Middle Eastern and South Asian families, plus families of European origin (MDPI Genes review).
  • Sex ratio: no bias is evident in this small number of cases.
  • MDSGene review: pooled DNAJC6 with five other atypical-parkinsonism genes (140 patients from 73 families). Across that group, median onset was 24 years and levodopa response was 49%, compared with 93% in typical monogenic PD (Wittke et al. 2021, Mov Disord, DOI 10.1002/mds.28517, PMC9070562). DNAJC6-specific numbers were not retrieved.

10. Diagnostics

  • Genetic testing (confirms the diagnosis): identify biallelic pathogenic DNAJC6 variants. GeneReviews recommends a multigene PD or dystonia panel, or exome/genome sequencing, rather than testing DNAJC6 alone.
  • Variants have been found by WES (Edvardson; Köroğlu), a targeted PD panel (Elsayed) and WGS (Manfield).
  • Olgiati: "Screening of DNAJC6 is warranted in all patients with early-onset PD compatible with autosomal recessive inheritance."
  • Imaging:
  • DaT-SPECT: absent or reduced basal ganglia uptake (3/3 tested in Ng 2020).
  • PET: nigrostriatal abnormalities in one PARK19B family.
  • MRI: usually normal, sometimes atrophy.
  • CSF neurotransmitters: isolated low HVA with normal 5-HIAA, a pattern resembling TH deficiency. Ng et al. state that DNAJC6 disease "should be considered as a differential diagnosis for pediatric neurotransmitter disorders associated with low homovanillic acid levels."
  • Research biomarkers: auxilin is reduced in fibroblasts and CSF, and GAK is raised in CSF.
  • EEG: diffuse encephalopathy with focal epileptiform activity (Elsayed).
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • Other recessive juvenile/early-onset PD: PRKN, PINK1, DJ-1, ATP13A2, FBXO7, SYNJ1, VPS13C, PLA2G6.
  • Pediatric neurotransmitter disorders: TH deficiency, AADC deficiency, dopamine transporter deficiency syndrome (SLC6A3).
  • Dopa-responsive dystonia (GCH1).
  • Wilson disease and NBIA.
  • Distinguishing features of DNAJC6: developmental delay with epilepsy, poor or unsustained levodopa response, and isolated low CSF HVA.
  • Omics diagnostics, CMA and karyotype: no defined role, apart from CMA's potential to detect rare deletions.
  • Screening: No newborn screening exists. Cascade carrier testing is possible in families.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival: Life-expectancy and mortality data are not available. No deaths are reported in Ng 2020 (patients aged 10–28 at report).
  • Morbidity: Severe disability in PARK19A, with loss of walking in adolescence, bedridden state, intellectual disability, epilepsy and dysphagia.
  • Prognostic factors: Truncating genotypes, juvenile onset and developmental delay predict a severe, pharmacoresistant course. Adult onset and missense or splice variants predict a slower course with good levodopa response. This is an observational correlation.
  • Complications: levodopa-induced dyskinesia, psychosis or hallucinations, dystonic storms, and feeding difficulties.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not possible beyond reproductive options.
  • Genetic counseling: autosomal recessive, so each sibling of a proband has a 25% risk. Carrier testing of relatives is possible, and prenatal or preimplantation testing is possible once the familial variants are known (GeneReviews, snippet level). Counseling is especially relevant in consanguineous families.
  • Tertiary prevention: seizure control, careful levodopa dosing to limit dyskinesia and psychosis, swallowing and nutrition monitoring, and rehabilitation.
  • Vaccines, prophylaxis and public-health measures do not apply.
