Intestinal Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0001678 Pathograph 34 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Extrapulmonary tuberculosis Gastrointestinal tuberculosis

Intestinal tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the bowel wall, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and, uncommonly, by Mycobacterium bovis acquired from unpasteurized dairy products. Bacilli reach the intestine in swallowed sputum, in the blood from a pulmonary focus, or by spread from adjacent tuberculous lymph nodes or viscera, and invade the lymphoid tissue of the mucosa. The ileocecal region, rich in Peyer's patches, is the commonest site. Caseating granulomatous inflammation of the submucosa produces transverse or circumferential ulcers (ulcerative form), an inflammatory pseudotumour with wall thickening (hypertrophic form), or both (ulcerohypertrophic form), with necrotic mesenteric lymphadenitis. The illness is insidious, with chronic abdominal pain, weight loss, fever, night sweats, anaemia, diarrhoea or altered bowel habit, and sometimes a right lower quadrant mass. Complications are strictures with obstruction, perforation, fistula and bleeding. The disease closely mimics Crohn disease clinically, endoscopically and histologically; misdiagnosis and treatment with corticosteroids or anti-TNF agents can lead to dissemination and death. Diagnosis rests on ileocolonoscopy with biopsies for histology, acid-fast staining, culture and nucleic acid amplification (PCR, Xpert MTB/RIF), supported by CT and evidence of pulmonary tuberculosis; because every test is insensitive, an empirical trial of antituberculous therapy with clinical and endoscopic follow-up is often needed. Six months of standard antituberculous chemotherapy is as effective as longer courses in randomized trials, and surgery is reserved for perforation, refractory obstruction or massive bleeding.

Caseating Granulomatous Inflammation of the Intestinal Wall
Large, numerous, often confluent caseating granulomas of epithelioid macrophages and multinucleated giant cells, with a cuff of lymphocytes, form mainly in the submucosa and serosa with surrounding fibrosis; noncaseating granulomas also occur. Caseating granulomas are found in about 70-75% of biopsied or resected cases in large series. This systemic inflammatory focus underlies the fever, night sweats, weight loss and anaemia.

description: >-
  Intestinal tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the bowel wall, caused by
  Mycobacterium tuberculosis and, uncommonly, by Mycobacterium bovis acquired
  from unpasteurized dairy products. Bacilli reach the intestine in swallowed
  sputum, in the blood from a pulmonary focus, or by spread from adjacent
  tuberculous lymph nodes or viscera, and invade the lymphoid tissue of the
  mucosa. The ileocecal region, rich in Peyer's patches, is the commonest
  site. Caseating granulomatous inflammation of the submucosa produces
  transverse or circumferential ulcers (ulcerative form), an inflammatory
  pseudotumour with wall thickening (hypertrophic form), or both
  (ulcerohypertrophic form), with necrotic mesenteric lymphadenitis. The
  illness is insidious, with chronic abdominal pain, weight loss, fever,
  night sweats, anaemia, diarrhoea or altered bowel habit, and sometimes a
  right lower quadrant mass. Complications are strictures with obstruction,
  perforation, fistula and bleeding. The disease closely mimics Crohn
  disease clinically, endoscopically and histologically; misdiagnosis and
  treatment with corticosteroids or anti-TNF agents can lead to dissemination
  and death. Diagnosis rests on ileocolonoscopy with biopsies for histology,
  acid-fast staining, culture and nucleic acid amplification (PCR, Xpert
  MTB/RIF), supported by CT and evidence of pulmonary tuberculosis; because
  every test is insensitive, an empirical trial of antituberculous therapy
  with clinical and endoscopic follow-up is often needed. Six months of
  standard antituberculous chemotherapy is as effective as longer courses in
  randomized trials, and surgery is reserved for perforation, refractory
  obstruction or massive bleeding.

Intestinal Tuberculosis (MONDO:0001678): Disease Characteristics Research Report

How I scoped this report. Intestinal tuberculosis (ITB) is an infectious disease. It has no causal human gene: it is caused by bacteria of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, mainly M. tuberculosis and, less often, M. bovis. The causal-gene and OMIM fields in the template are therefore "not applicable", not merely "not recorded". I covered gastrointestinal (luminal) TB and included the overlapping literature on abdominal TB, which also covers peritoneal and mesenteric lymph-node disease, where intestine-specific data were missing.

Limits on the evidence. - Direct PubMed access was blocked in this session, so all evidence comes from web search results. - Quotations are taken from search snippets of abstracts and reviews. Check them against the primary records before entering them in a knowledge base. - I only give PMIDs that appeared in retrieved URLs or snippets. Other sources are cited by PMC ID, DOI or URL. - Following the citation rules, I give ontology terms (HPO, GO, CL, UBERON, NCIT, CHEBI) as labels only, without IDs, because I did not retrieve their identifiers in this session. The IDs need a lookup before use.

1. Disease Information

Overview. ITB is tuberculosis of the gastrointestinal tract. It most often affects the ileocecal region, but "it can involve any part of the gastrointestinal tract" (StatPearls, NBK556115).

The MeSH scope note defines it as "tuberculosis that involves any region of the GASTROINTESTINAL TRACT, mostly in the distal ILEUM and the CECUM", with M. tuberculosis as the usual pathogen (MeSH D014385).

A 2021 systematic review (Al-Zanbagi & Shariff, Saudi J Gastroenterol, PMID 34213424, PMC8555774) calls gastrointestinal TB a leading form of extrapulmonary TB that "can defy diagnosis", because its symptoms overlap with other gut diseases and diagnostic tests have limited accuracy.

2. Etiology

Causal factors (infectious). - The main cause is M. tuberculosis. M. bovis, the agent of zoonotic TB, is a secondary cause. - ITB "is caused predominantly by infection with M. tuberculosis, the same bacterium responsible for pulmonary tuberculosis" (Springer review 2025; UCL PDF). - Suggested routes of entry into the gut are swallowing infected sputum, eating contaminated food (unpasteurised milk for M. bovis), spread through the blood, and spread from neighbouring organs. My searches did not retrieve a source that ranks these routes, so treat the list as background.

Risk factors (environmental and host). - HIV co-infection. Extrapulmonary disease is about 20% of all TB but about 50% of TB in people with HIV (StatPearls extrapulmonary TB). In industrialised countries, most abdominal TB occurs in people co-infected with HIV, or in immigrants from high-prevalence regions where reactivation is the usual mechanism (StatPearls abdominal TB). In an HIV/TB co-infected cohort, extrapulmonary TB was 28% of cases, and abdominal TB was 11% of the extrapulmonary cases (PMC8555774). - Other host factors listed in a 2023 review: younger age, female sex, Asian ethnicity, immunosuppression, diabetes and peritoneal dialysis (Indian J Gastroenterol 2023, doi 10.1007/s12664-023-01343-x). - Anti-TNF and other immunosuppression, often given for IBD or for ITB misdiagnosed as Crohn's disease. In a GETAID cohort of 44 patients with IBD on anti-TNF therapy, TB developed despite negative screening before treatment and was "mostly extrapulmonary and disseminated" (HAL 01470713). - Unpasteurised milk and dairy products, for M. bovis (WHO zoonotic TB roadmap). - Residence in or migration from a TB-endemic region (StatPearls).

Genetic risk factors (susceptibility only, not causal). - ITB-specific evidence. Yu & Chen (2019, World J Gastroenterol 25:2473, doi 10.3748/wjg.v25.i20.2473, PMC6543246) studied 133 ITB patients, 128 Crohn's disease patients and 500 controls in China, genotyping SNPs in the IL-23/IL-17 axis. - They report that "rs2227473 and rs1143627 polymorphisms affect ITB susceptibility instead of CD". - They state that "the IL22 gene promoter SNP rs2227473 G allele is a risk factor for intestinal tuberculosis". - The abstract also reports an A-allele frequency difference with OR = 0.60, P = 0.030. The direction of the allele effect is ambiguous and needs checking in the full text. - The gene for rs1143627 was not confirmed from the snippets; it is commonly attributed to the IL1B promoter, but this was not verified here. - Evidence from TB in general, not ITB-specific. - NOD2: Arg587Arg was associated with TB in Han Chinese, and a meta-analysis suggests Arg702Trp may be protective (BMC Infect Dis 12:91; PMC3862802). - SLC11A1 (NRAMP1), VDR, HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQB1 were associated with TB in Chinese Kazakhs (PMC3758880). - TLR2 rs1898830 was associated with TB in Han and Tibetan populations, while TLR4 rs11536889 and TOLLIP rs3750920 were protective in Tibetans (PMC7403954). - Databases. No GWAS Catalog, ClinVar or ClinGen entries specific to ITB were retrieved.

Protective factors - Heat treatment of milk: "Heat-treatment of milk is key to reducing the risk to people" (WHO). - Control of HIV. - Screening for and treating latent TB before immunosuppression (HAL 01470713). - Genetic: NOD2 Arg702Trp and the Tibetan-population TLR4/TOLLIP variants, both from TB in general. - Yu & Chen suggest that high IL-22 expression "seems to be a protective factor for intestinal tuberculosis" (PMC6543246).

Gene–environment interactions - The best-documented interaction is iatrogenic: anti-TNF therapy (a drug blocking a host pathway) combined with latent M. tuberculosis infection leads to reactivation and disseminated extrapulmonary TB. - HIV-related CD4 depletion combined with TB exposure is the other major interaction. - I did not retrieve any formal GxE studies for ITB.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Human causal genes, pathogenic variants (ACMG classification), allele frequencies, somatic origin, chromosomal abnormalities and epigenetics: none of these apply. ITB is an acquired infection.

Host susceptibility and modifier genes: see Section 2. The candidates are IL22 (rs2227473), NOD2, SLC11A1, VDR, TLR2, TLR4 and TOLLIP. Except for the IL22 finding, the evidence comes from pulmonary or all-site TB, which leaves a gap for ITB specifically. A practically relevant point is that NOD2 is the main Crohn's disease gene, yet the ITB study found the IL-23/IL-17 SNPs distinguished ITB from Crohn's disease (PMC6543246).

Pathogen genetics - M. bovis "is naturally resistant to one of the main first-line drugs (pyrazinamide)" (WHO roadmap). - Rifampicin resistance in ITB can be detected with Xpert MTB/RIF (see Section 10).

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agents: the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, mainly M. tuberculosis and M. bovis. NCBI Taxonomy IDs were not retrieved in this session.
  • Zoonotic burden of M. bovis: WHO estimated "147,000 new cases of ZTB and 12,500 deaths" in 2016. True incidence is "likely to be underestimated" because routine labs cannot tell M. bovis from M. tuberculosis (PMC6008525).
  • Lifestyle: drinking unpasteurised or unboiled milk and eating undercooked meat, for M. bovis (WHO).
  • Other factors: crowding and poverty in endemic regions. These were not specifically retrieved for ITB.
  • Smoking and alcohol: I found no ITB-specific data. Yu & Chen adjusted their analyses for smoking and drinking but did not report them as risk factors.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Exposure. Mycobacteria reach the gut lumen through swallowed sputum from active pulmonary TB, contaminated milk (M. bovis), spread through the blood, or spread from neighbouring organs. The relative weight of each route was not quantified in retrieved sources.
  2. Uptake. Bacilli in the lumen are taken up by M cells of the follicle-associated epithelium over Peyer's patches, mostly in the ileocecal region. This step is described in reviews; the human evidence is mainly morphological, so it is partly inferred. M cells "provide a route of entry for pathogens into the mucosa and can phagocytose tubercle bacilli" (UCL review). The ileocecal predilection is attributed to abundant lymphoid tissue and stasis, which is inferred.
  3. Infection of phagocytes. Bacilli crossing the epithelium are "ingested by macrophages and dendritic cells in the subepithelial region". The bacilli survive inside these cells.
  4. Granuloma formation. Innate and adaptive cell-mediated immunity (Th1/IFN-γ, TNF-dependent, and the IL-23/IL-17/IL-22 axis, which is inferred from TB biology and the ITB genetic study) leads to granulomas "initially in the mucosa or Peyer's patches". These are confluent epithelioid granulomas with Langhans giant cells, a lymphocyte cuff and central caseous necrosis (UCL; Thieme).
  5. Spread and ulceration. Granulomas spread along the lymphatics of the bowel wall and lead to mucosal ulceration. The transverse ulcers are thought to follow the circumferential path of the intestinal lymphatics; this is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.
  6. Lymphatic spread. Spread to mesenteric lymph nodes leads to caseating lymphadenitis, which may form masses or calcify.
  7. Three branches of tissue response (Springer 2025):
  8. Ulcerative form, the most common: transverse ulcers lead to bleeding, perforation or fistulas.
  9. Hypertrophic form: fibroblastic and inflammatory thickening leads to an ileocecal mass and pseudotumour.
  10. Fibrostenotic form: healing with fibrosis leads to strictures and bowel obstruction.
  11. Clinical expression.
  12. Local disease leads to pain, diarrhoea, obstruction, a mass, bleeding and peritonitis.
  13. Systemic cytokine release (TNF and others) leads to fever, weight loss, anaemia and raised acute-phase reactants. This link is inferred.
  14. Immunosuppression as a modifier. Anti-TNF therapy or HIV impairs granuloma containment, which results in disseminated disease (PMC6942708; HAL 01470713).

Supporting detail - Upstream vs downstream. Uptake by M cells and macrophages is upstream. Granuloma formation and caseation are the core mid-chain step. Fibrosis, stricture, perforation and systemic wasting are downstream. - Suggested GO process labels: phagocytosis; granuloma formation; inflammatory response; response to bacterium; interferon-gamma-mediated signaling pathway; tumor necrosis factor-mediated signaling pathway; interleukin-17-mediated signaling; necrotic cell death; tissue remodeling / fibrosis. - Suggested CL cell labels: microfold cell of epithelium of small intestine (M cell); macrophage; epithelioid cell; Langhans giant cell / multinucleate giant cell; dendritic cell; CD4-positive T cell (Th1, Th17); fibroblast. - Immunohistochemistry: IL-22R1 expression was higher in ITB tissue than in controls (PMC6543246). - Not retrieved for ITB: transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, single-cell, spatial and CRISPR data.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organs:
  • Distal ileum and caecum (ileocecal region), the most common site (MeSH).
  • Ileum in 43% and ileocecal valve in 35% of a surgical series (BMC Gastroenterol 5:21).
  • Less often the jejunum, colon, anorectum, duodenum, stomach and oesophagus.
  • Secondary involvement: mesenteric lymph nodes, peritoneum (ascites), and coexisting pulmonary TB. Chest CT raised the sensitivity of ITB diagnosis from 26.2% to 56.9% (Intest Res 20(2), KoreaMed).
  • Body systems: digestive and immune/lymphatic, and respiratory when lung disease coexists.
  • Tissue level: mucosa, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue and Peyer's patches, submucosa, and serosa. Lesions are seen "mainly in the submucosal and serosal layers".
  • Subcellular level (suggested GO cellular-component labels): phagosome; lysosome; phagolysosome. These are inferred from general TB biology.
  • Suggested UBERON labels: ileum; cecum; ileocecal valve; Peyer's patch; jejunum; colon; mesenteric lymph node; peritoneum.
  • Laterality: not applicable. In the abdomen, lesions are predominantly right-sided (ileocecal).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: mainly young to middle-aged adults; children are also affected (PMC3211607). Onset is insidious, usually with weeks to months of symptoms. Some patients present acutely with obstruction or perforation.
  • Stages: there is no formal staging. The sequence is mucosal and lymphoid infection, then granuloma and ulceration, then hypertrophic or fibrostenotic healing, then complications.
  • Course: progressive without treatment. Treatment produces remission; a 2025 review cites recurrence in 13–20% of cases even with treatment (Springer 2025). This figure was not verified against primary data.
  • Critical windows:
  • Diagnosis before complications develop.
  • Screening before anti-TNF therapy, with repeat screening for ongoing exposure (HAL 01470713).
  • The empirical ATT trial window in uncertain ITB vs Crohn's disease, which may delay a Crohn's diagnosis (Kedia et al., WJG 2019;25:418, wjgnet).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: not applicable. ITB is not Mendelian; host susceptibility is multifactorial. Penetrance, anticipation, founder effects and carrier frequency do not apply.
  • Global TB context:
  • WHO estimated about 10.8 million incident TB cases in 2023 (range 10.1–11.7 million), or 134 per 100,000 (WHO GTB Report 2024).
  • The 2025 report states that incidence fell in 2024 (WHO GTB 2025).
  • Extrapulmonary TB is about 20% of all TB (StatPearls).
  • I did not retrieve a reliable global incidence for ITB itself.
  • Geography: endemic in South and Southeast Asia, Africa and East Asia, which is also where most of the clinical series come from (India, China, Korea, Malaysia, Tanzania, Nigeria). In high-income countries it mainly affects migrants and people with HIV (StatPearls).
  • Sex and age: a 2023 review lists female sex and younger age as risk factors for abdominal TB (doi 10.1007/s12664-023-01343-x). I did not retrieve a precise sex ratio.

10. Diagnostics

Microbiology and molecular tests on biopsies - Xpert MTB/RIF. Ding et al., Lab Med 2024;55(2):238, reported a pooled sensitivity of 48% and specificity of 99%: "limited diagnostic sensitivity … but with high specificity" (Lab Med). - TB PCR. A meta-analysis found PCR "promising and highly specific" for separating ITB from Crohn's disease (Saudi J Gastroenterol 2017, PMC5329974). - Culture and histology. Sending more colonoscopic biopsies for culture raises the yield, and culture and histology are complementary (Mehta et al., PMID 31111030).

Histopathology - Large, confluent caseating granulomas are the hallmark, but caseation has low sensitivity. - Confluent granulomas and ulcers lined by epithelioid histiocytes were proposed as more sensitive discriminators from Crohn's disease (PLoS One 2014 meta-analysis, PMC4191941).

Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA) - Chen et al. 2013 (WJG 19:8133) pooled a sensitivity of 0.74 and specificity of 0.87, with LR+ 5.98 (wjgnet). - Ng et al. (J Gastroenterol Hepatol 29:1664) found T-SPOT.TB more sensitive than QuantiFERON (CUHK archive). - In an endemic setting, IGRA sensitivity was only 40.68% and specificity 75.51% (Sachdeva et al., Intest Res 2023, DOAJ).

Imaging - CT and CT enterography features have been pooled in a meta-analysis (Intest Res, KoreaMed). - Chest CT for coexisting lung TB raises diagnostic sensitivity (KoreaMed). - "Imaging techniques coupled with fine needle biopsies are useful aids" (PMID 19623062).

Clinical criteria and algorithms - Logan's modified criteria make "response to anti tubercular therapy … a key factor in establishing the diagnosis of ITB". - Kedia et al. 2019 give an algorithm for following a patient on an ATT trial (WJG 25:418). - A combined laboratory, imaging and endoscopy model has been developed for separating Crohn's disease from ITB (PMC8276438).

Differential diagnosis - Crohn's disease. This is the key differential, because the two "have overlapping clinical, radiological, endoscopic and histological characteristics" (PMID 17539977; also PMID 19240705). Male sex, bloody stools, perianal disease and extraintestinal manifestations favour Crohn's disease (PMC5551982). - Others: malignancy (lymphoma, caecal carcinoma), amoebiasis, Yersinia infection, and other granulomatous diseases.

Genetic testing: not applicable to diagnosis.

Screening: IGRA or tuberculin testing for latent TB before biologic therapy; no population screening for ITB exists.

11. Outcome/Prognosis

Mortality - A prospective Malaysian cohort of 52 gastrointestinal TB patients reported 79% treatment completion, 21% adverse events, 13% mortality and no treatment failures (BMC Infect Dis, doi 10.1186/s12879-020-04983-y). - Across surgical series the range is wide, from 0% to about 45%. One South Asian series of 25 patients reported 8% mortality from sepsis. - Untreated mortality may exceed 40% (Springer 2025).

Prognostic factors - Late presentation with perforation or peritonitis. - Emergency surgery. - In children: older age, small-bowel involvement and surgery are "independently associated with higher mortality" (PMC3211607). - In an African series, moving away from early laparotomy cut morbidity from 30% to 11% and mortality from 12% to 0%.

Complications: obstruction, perforation, fistula, bleeding, stricture, malabsorption, and disseminated TB (especially after misdiagnosis as Crohn's disease treated with immunosuppression, which "can be fatal": PMC6942708).

Recovery: most patients respond to ATT; the recurrence rate is in Section 8.

Quality-of-life and disability data: not retrieved.

12. Treatment

Pharmacotherapy - Standard first-line ATT: isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol for a 2-month intensive phase, then isoniazid and rifampicin. Suggested NCIT label: "Antitubercular Agent". Suggested CHEBI labels: isoniazid; rifampicin; pyrazinamide; ethambutol. - M. bovis is intrinsically resistant to pyrazinamide (WHO). - Duration (Cochrane, Jullien et al. 2016, PMID 27801499): "Most current guidelines recommend treating … abdominal TB … for six months". In the pooled RCTs, which were all from Asia and excluded HIV, relapse occurred in 2 of 140 patients on 6 months versus 0 of 129 on 9 months. Deaths all occurred in the first 4 months (Cochrane). - Makharia multicentre RCT: 6 versus 9 months of intermittent directly observed therapy (NCT01124929, ClinicalTrials.gov). - Empirical treatment: a 1998 review recommends empirical ATT when ITB is suspected, even when histology, smear and culture are negative (PMID 9625110). - Adverse effects: hepatotoxicity is the main risk, including with an empirical trial (PMC11642180). - Pharmacogenomics: NAT2 acetylator status and isoniazid toxicity were not retrieved in this session.

Surgery (NCIT labels: "Intestinal Resection"; "Strictureplasty"; "Laparotomy") - Indicated for diagnostic doubt, suspected neoplasm, perforation or obstruction (PMID 9625110). - Laparotomy should be reserved for complications or a diagnosis that remains unclear (African series, above).

Supportive care: nutritional support and endoscopic dilation of strictures; no ITB-specific trials were retrieved.

Biologics in co-existing IBD: after TB, restarting anti-TNF therapy "seems safe" (HAL 01470713). Guidance on whether to continue anti-TNF therapy during TB differs between the BTS and the ACR (PMC3063288).

Not applicable: gene, cell or RNA therapies, and targeted therapies.

Experimental: apart from NCT01124929, I did not retrieve ITB-specific trials.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • Control of pulmonary TB, which is the source of swallowed bacilli.
  • Pasteurisation of milk and control of bovine TB, under the 2017 WHO/FAO/WOAH/Union zoonotic TB roadmap and its ten priorities (WHO).
  • HIV control.
  • Immunisation: BCG. I did not retrieve ITB-specific efficacy data.
  • Secondary prevention: screening for and treating latent TB before anti-TNF or immunosuppressive therapy, with repeat screening when exposure continues.
  • Tertiary prevention: early ATT to prevent strictures and perforation, and avoiding immunosuppression until ITB has been excluded in endemic areas (PMID 17539977).
  • Genetic counselling: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Cattle (bovine TB, M. bovis) are the main animal reservoir. The disease is zoonotic and spreads mainly through unpasteurised milk (WHO).
  • M. bovis and M. orygis have been studied in oral-route mouse models (see Section 15).
  • Intestinal involvement in natural bovine TB, wildlife reservoirs and OMIA entries were not retrieved in detail.
  • Orthologous genes: not applicable, since there is no causal gene.

15. Model Organisms

  • Oral-infection mouse model: streptomycin pretreatment before oral gavage "significantly increased organ colony forming units". This was applied to M. bovis and M. orygis (McGill thesis; Duffy et al., Gut Pathog 2023, doi 10.1186/s13099-023-00573-w). Whether this model reproduces human-type intestinal granulomas was not confirmed in retrieved snippets.
  • General TB models, not intestine-specific (PMC6984742; PMC4871865):
  • Guinea pigs are highly susceptible and need only a few bacilli to become infected.
  • Rabbits have granulomas resembling human histopathology, but few commercial reagents exist.
  • Mice are the most common model but lack some human immune features.
  • Zebrafish with M. marinum form macrophage aggregates and granulomas and do not need BSL-3 containment.
  • Limitations: "no animal model perfectly recapitulates the human TB syndrome". No well-validated model reproduces ileocecal transverse ulcers or fibrostenosis.
  • Genetic models: knockout mice for IFN-γ, TNF and similar genes are widely used for TB in general, but were not retrieved here for ITB.
