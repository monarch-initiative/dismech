Spinal Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0043836 Pathograph 33 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Skeletal tuberculosis

Spinal tuberculosis (Pott disease, tuberculous spondylitis) is tuberculous infection of the vertebral column. It accounts for about half of skeletal tuberculosis and is its most destructive form. It is always secondary: bacilli reach the spine through the bloodstream from a pulmonary or other primary focus and lodge in the subchondral bone of the anterior vertebral body on either side of a disc (the paradiscal pattern). A slow, paucibacillary caseating granulomatous osteomyelitis follows. The infection spreads beneath the anterior longitudinal ligament to neighbouring levels, often leaving the disc relatively preserved early on, and forms large "cold" paravertebral, psoas and epidural abscesses with little inflammation. Progressive destruction collapses the vertebral bodies and wedges them anteriorly, giving kyphosis and the angular gibbus. The thoracic and lumbar spine are affected most; cervical and craniovertebral junction disease is less common and presents with neck pain, restricted movement, retropharyngeal abscess with dysphagia, and atlantoaxial instability. In children kyphosis can keep progressing with growth after cure. Back pain is the dominant symptom; fever and constitutional symptoms are less common than in pyogenic spondylodiscitis, and diagnosis is typically delayed by months. Neurological deficit (Pott paraplegia) affects roughly half of patients. It arises either early, from compression by abscess, granulation tissue, sequestra or instability during active disease, or late, from stretching of the cord over a healed internal gibbus years or decades later. MRI is the imaging modality of choice and separates tuberculous from pyogenic infection on features such as thin-walled paraspinal abscesses and subligamentous spread over several levels. The diagnosis is confirmed by CT-guided biopsy with histology, mycobacterial culture and Xpert MTB/RIF. Antituberculous chemotherapy is the mainstay, given for 6 to 12 months or longer; paradoxical worsening early in treatment is recognised and should not be mistaken for failure. Surgery is reserved for neurological deficit, instability, severe deformity, large abscesses or failure of medical treatment.

Hematogenous Seeding of the Vertebral Body
Bacilli lodge in the subchondral bone of the vertebral endplates on either side of a disc, supplied by the paradiscal arteries, and usually in the anterior part of the vertebral body. The adult disc is avascular and is reached secondarily. Batson's paravertebral venous plexus may contribute to the thoracic predilection, multilevel spread and occasional skip lesions. Other patterns are central (vertebral body), posterior (posterior elements) and non-osseous abscess.

Cold Abscess Formation
Caseous material forms an abscess with little local inflammation (a cold abscess) that starts at the infective focus and tracks along fascial and neurovascular planes: as a paravertebral swelling in the thoracic spine, along the psoas to the groin or thigh from the lumbar spine, into the retropharyngeal space from the cervical spine and craniovertebral junction, and into the epidural space. Abscesses are thin- and smooth-walled on MRI.

Progressive Kyphotic Deformity
Collapse of one vertebra gives a knuckle, of two a gibbus and of more a rounded kyphosis. About 3 to 5% of patients treated with chemotherapy alone reach 60 degrees or more and need surgery. In children, kyphosis can progress during active disease and again after cure as the spine grows; radiological "spine at risk" instability signs predict a late increase, and the most severe form, buckling collapse beyond 120 degrees, is linked to infection before age seven, thoracolumbar disease and loss of more than two vertebral bodies. Severe kyphosis in adults usually stems from childhood disease.

Spinal Cord Compression in Active Disease
Early-onset Pott paraplegia. During active infection the cord or cauda equina is compressed by abscess, granulation tissue, sequestra or instability. It carries a better prognosis than late-onset paraplegia and can reverse with prompt treatment; mild weakness, a low paraplegia score and normal evoked potentials predict full recovery with conservative treatment.

The imaging modality of choice: it detects the earliest changes, shows the extent of bone and soft-tissue disease, abscesses and neural compression, and helps follow the response to treatment.   bacilli reach the spine through the bloodstream from a pulmonary or other
  primary focus and lodge in the subchondral bone of the anterior vertebral
  body on either side of a disc (the paradiscal pattern). A slow, paucibacillary
  caseating granulomatous osteomyelitis follows. The infection spreads
  beneath the anterior longitudinal ligament to neighbouring levels, often
  leaving the disc relatively preserved early on, and forms large "cold"
  paravertebral, psoas and epidural abscesses with little inflammation.
  Progressive destruction collapses the vertebral bodies and wedges them
  anteriorly, giving kyphosis and the angular gibbus. The thoracic and lumbar
  spine are affected most; cervical and craniovertebral junction disease is
  less common and presents with neck pain, restricted movement,
  retropharyngeal abscess with dysphagia, and atlantoaxial instability. In
  children kyphosis can keep progressing with growth after cure. Back pain is the dominant symptom; fever and
  constitutional symptoms are less common than in pyogenic spondylodiscitis,
  and diagnosis is typically delayed by months. Neurological deficit (Pott
  paraplegia) affects roughly half of patients. It arises either early, from
  compression by abscess, granulation tissue, sequestra or instability during
  active disease, or late, from stretching of the cord over a healed internal
  gibbus years or decades later. MRI is the imaging modality of choice and
  separates tuberculous from pyogenic infection on features such as thin-walled
  paraspinal abscesses and subligamentous spread over several levels. The
  diagnosis is confirmed by CT-guided biopsy with histology, mycobacterial
  culture and Xpert MTB/RIF. Antituberculous chemotherapy is the mainstay,
  given for 6 to 12 months or longer; paradoxical worsening early in
  treatment is recognised and should not be mistaken for failure. Surgery is reserved for
  neurological deficit, instability, severe deformity, large abscesses or
  failure of medical treatment.

  description: >-
    The cornerstone of treatment. The standard regimen is an intensive phase
    of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (or streptomycin)

    in the reviews totals 9 months; WHO guidance is also quoted as 6 to 9

    18. The Medical Research Council trials found 6- and 9-month

1. Disease Information

Overview. Spinal TB is "a destructive form of tuberculosis" that "accounts for approximately half of all cases of musculoskeletal tuberculosis" (Garg & Somvanshi 2011, PMID 22118251). "The spinal column is involved in less than 1% of all cases of tuberculosis (TB)." It is "a very dangerous type of skeletal TB as it can be associated with neurologic deficit" (Rasouli et al. 2012, PMID 23275816).

Data provenance. The evidence comes from aggregated disease-level literature: reviews, cohort series, randomised trials and meta-analyses. None of it is individual EHR data.

2. Etiology

Causal factors

  • Infection by the M. tuberculosis complex. In one genotyped series from south-central China, "Of the 56, cases, 53 involved M. tuberculosis and 3 involved M. bovis" (Liu et al. 2020, PMID 32076625).
  • Iatrogenic M. bovis BCG. Vertebral osteomyelitis can follow intravesical BCG therapy for bladder cancer (Mackel et al. 2016, PMID 27158574).
  • Spread from a primary focus. MeSH describes the disease as "usually occurring as a complication of tuberculosis of the lungs." A 2025 series found that parenchymal spinal TB "is more frequently associated with haematogenous pulmonary dissemination" (Shan et al. 2025, PMID 40211169).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain. Steps 1–2 and 5–8 rest on the retrieved sources. Step 3 is inferred from general TB biology and was not retrieved here, and step 4 is supported only by early single-cell data.

  1. Inhaled M. tuberculosis (or another complex member) establishes a primary focus, usually pulmonary. This leads to haematogenous dissemination. Supported by the MeSH scope note and PMID 40211169.
  2. Bacilli seed the vertebral body, which results in vertebral osteomyelitis. Biopsy "from the affected site in the center of the vertebral body" is the diagnostic standard (PMID 22118251).
  3. Infection of macrophages leads to granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis. Inferred; the step was not retrieved for the spine specifically.
  4. Local immune programmes form in the lesion.
  5. Single-cell RNA-seq of lesion tissue "yielded 67,274 high-quality cells and identified nine major cell types." Spinal TB and brucellar spondylitis "exhibit distinct local immunopathological programs," including foamy macrophages (Liu et al. 2026, PMID 42698543).
  6. T-cell circuits "converge on a common osteo-immune pathway" (Yang et al. 2026, PMID 42239541).
  7. Matrix degradation by host proteases (MMP-1 "plays a curial role in extracellular matrix degradation during the progression of tuberculosis", PMID 28129430), together with osteo-immune bone resorption, results in endplate and vertebral body destruction.
  8. Destruction progresses along two branches:
  9. (6a) Vertebral collapse leads to angular kyphosis (gibbus) (PMIDs 42477203, 38632071).
  10. (6b) Caseous material spreads beneath the ligaments and into soft tissue, which results in paravertebral, psoas or epidural cold abscesses (PMID 42488243).
  11. Abscess, sequestrum or kyphotic deformity compresses the cord, which leads to neurological deficit (Pott's paraplegia). MRI compression stage predicts recovery after surgery (OR 2.87). Even so, "severe MRI compression does not necessarily indicate irreversible spinal cord injury" (PMID 42431540).
  12. Modifier branch: HIV-1 co-infection is associated with "reciprocal relationships" between Mtb and HIV-1 abundance in spinal tissue (PMIDs 41232750, PPR1035753).

Not retrieved: proteomics, metabolomics, spatial transcriptomics and CRISPR screen data specific to spinal TB.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

UBERON labels are given; IDs were not retrieved.

  • Primary site: vertebral column and vertebral body, with secondary involvement of the intervertebral disc. Marked disc involvement favours pyogenic infection, OR 0.209 for TB (PMID 42477203).
  • Distribution by region:
  • Reviews often cite the thoracic or thoracolumbar spine as most common.
  • Large Chinese series find the lumbar spine most common: 47.30% lumbar vs 40.95% thoracic in a Guizhou series of 597 patients, and 43.53% vs 32.55% in Guangxi (via https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8683179, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7019620).

  • Spinal cord, through compressive myelopathy.
  • Spinal meninges, in the meningeal form (PMID 40211169).
  • Psoas muscle (abscess).
  • Lung (concurrent pulmonary TB).
  • Body systems: musculoskeletal, nervous and respiratory.
  • Tissue and cell level: bone tissue, bone marrow and epidural soft tissue; macrophages, foam cells and T cells (PMID 42698543).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: occurs from childhood to old age. A paediatric series had a mean age of 8.5 years (PMID 33327790). An East China series of 893 patients had a median age of 56 (PMID 40890663). Onset is insidious or chronic, and diagnostic delay is common (PMID 32154388; PMC9955044).
  • Progression:
  • Untreated disease progresses from vertebral osteomyelitis to collapse, abscess, deformity and neurological deficit.
  • MRI compression stages have been proposed for thoracic disease (PMID 42431540).
  • Remission: treatment-induced. With treatment, "clinical results… are generally quite good with adequate and prompt care" (PMID 37304201).
  • Recurrence: occurs, including after surgery and with emerging drug resistance in HIV infection (PMID 38162320; PMID 37083590).
  • Critical period: early diagnosis. "A delay in diagnosis is the factor that most contributes to a worse prognosis" (PMC9955044).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Frequency:
  • Less than 1% of all TB (PMID 23275816).
  • About half of musculoskeletal TB (PMID 22118251; Jia 2024, PMID 39033332: "spinal tuberculosis accounting for approximately 50%").
  • Patient.info gives about 15% of extrapulmonary TB (https://patient.info/doctor/spinal-tuberculosis).
  • Population incidence and prevalence per 100,000 were not retrieved.
  • Inheritance: not Mendelian. Risk is multifactorial, with host susceptibility loci (§2).
  • Not applicable: penetrance, anticipation, mosaicism, founder effects and carrier frequency.
  • Sex ratio: male:female 1.4:1 (PMID 40890663).
  • Geography: endemic in high-TB-burden regions. The retrieved cohorts come from China, India, South Africa and Pakistan.

10. Diagnostics

Gold standard. "Neuroimaging-guided needle biopsy from the affected site in the center of the vertebral body is the gold standard technique for early histopathological diagnosis" (PMID 22118251).

Imaging. - "MRI is most common imaging modality used," and "PET CT is recently evaluated for response assessment with promising results" (Kubihal 2022, PMID 34956831). - MRI "is more sensitive imaging technique than x-ray and more specific than computed tomography" (PMID 22118251). - Telling tuberculous from pyogenic spondylitis on MRI: - Signal-intensity ratio scores reached AUC 0.888 and 0.870. A cut-off of EAS ≥0.400 gave 93.1% sensitivity and 85.3% specificity (PMID 42550235). - Vertebral collapse (OR 3.713) favours TB (PMID 42477203). - Features that differ significantly across pyogenic, tuberculous, brucellar and fungal spondylitis include skip lesions, intraosseous abscess and the extent of destruction (PMID 41081173). - Machine-learning differentiation: - Five-centre multimodal model for TB, pyogenic and brucellar spondylitis (PMID 42819251). - Radiomics vs spinal metastasis, AUC 0.971 (PMID 43033258). - Nomogram vs pyogenic infection, AUC 0.770 (PMID 42025441).

11. Outcome/Prognosis

  • Mortality and survival: spinal-TB-specific figures were not retrieved.
  • Outcome on treatment: generally good (PMID 37304201). In the Madras MRC trial, 10-year favourable status was 90% after radical surgery, 94% after 6 months of ambulant chemotherapy and 99% after 9 months (PMID 10872368; figures from a summary of the record).
  • Neurological recovery:
  • In Sharma et al. 2016, 128 of 200 patients with deficit (64%) recovered completely on the AIS scale. The level of vertebral involvement, AIS grade at presentation, and bladder or bowel involvement affected recovery (https://www.nature.com/articles/sc201685).
  • Children can recover from long-standing deficits lasting 6–24 months (PMID 33327790).
  • Complications:
  • Kyphotic deformity.
  • Permanent paraplegia.
  • Recurrence.
  • Drug resistance (PMIDs 38162320, 40890663).
  • Prognostic factors:
  • Diagnostic delay (PMC9955044).
  • AIS grade and duration of deficit.
  • MRI compression stage (PMID 42431540).
  • HIV status.
  • Drug resistance.

12. Treatment

NCIT labels are given; IDs were not retrieved.

Pharmacotherapy. "Combination multidrug antitubercular therapy is the basis of treatment" (PMID 37304201). Standard regimens use isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol. The Madras trial used isoniazid plus rifampicin for 6 or 9 months (PMID 10422019).

  • Duration of treatment:
  • A meta-analysis found 6 months of antitubercular therapy healed 375/414 patients (90.58%). "Overall, the healed status of spinal TB was equivalent in patients in both groups" (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.92–1.04) (Aryal 2022, PMID 34784702).
  • In surgically treated Chinese patients, "short-course chemotherapy could be equivalent to the standard chemotherapy in terms of efficacy" (PMID 33781290).
  • Guidance is largely "extrapolated from trials on pulmonary disease," and there have been few good-quality studies since the BMRC trials of the 1970s (PMID 32154388).
  • Landmark trials (British Medical Research Council Working Party on Tuberculosis of the Spine):
  • Korea, 265 patients followed for 3 years (PMID 8444944).
  • 15-year assessment from Korea and Hong Kong (PMID 9619936).
  • 5-year assessment of 6-, 9- and 18-month regimens (PMID 10422019).
  • Ambulant chemotherapy vs radical surgery in Madras (PMID 10872368).
  • Drug-resistant disease: managed with regimens guided by resistance testing (review PMID 32904104).
  • Pharmacogenomics: no spinal-TB-specific data retrieved.

Surgery. - Indications: "Patients who come with significant kyphosis or neurological impairments are the only ones who require surgical care" (PMID 37304201). - Procedures: - Debridement and decompression. - Bone grafting. A network meta-analysis of 517 patients favoured autograft for safety (PMID 37202780). - Instrumented fusion. - Posterior revision and kyphosis correction (PMIDs 37083590, 38632071). - Oblique lateral interbody fusion (OLIF) vs open surgery in lumbar disease: shorter operative time and less blood loss in a 617-patient systematic review (preprint PPR1243934). - Gardner-Wells traction for paediatric kyphosis (PMID 39631124).

Supportive and rehabilitative care. Physical and rehabilitation medicine, nutrition, and vitamin D supplementation (case series PMID 39033700).

Advanced therapeutics. Gene, cell, RNA, targeted and checkpoint therapies are not applicable. No spinal-TB NCT trials were retrieved.

Suggested NCIT labels: antitubercular agent; isoniazid; rifampin; pyrazinamide; ethambutol; spinal fusion; surgical debridement; laminectomy/decompression; bone graft; physical therapy.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: control of pulmonary TB transmission, and BCG vaccination in endemic settings. No spinal-specific vaccine efficacy data were retrieved.
  • Secondary prevention: early detection of infection and molecular diagnosis. "Novel molecular diagnostic methods are effective and timely choices" (PMID 36826156).
  • Tertiary prevention: complete antitubercular therapy, timely decompression, deformity correction, and surveillance for relapse and resistance.
  • Genetic counselling: not applicable.
  • Prophylaxis: treatment of latent TB infection in high-risk groups, such as people with HIV. Spinal-specific data were not retrieved.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Lesions consistent with tuberculous spondylitis have been found at abattoir inspection of domestic pigs and in hunted wild swine. They have been proposed as a model for human Pott disease (Amato et al. 2019, PMID 29846908).
  • Zoonotic potential: human M. bovis spinal TB occurs (3/56 cases, PMID 32076625), which implies an animal reservoir.
  • OMIA, VBO and ortholog data are not applicable or were not retrieved.

15. Model Organisms

  • Rabbit, the principal model.
  • Review: "New Zealand rabbits, possessing a comparable anatomical structure to humans" (Jia 2024, PMID 39033332).
  • Model establishment (Geng 2015, PMID 25325713).
  • Direct infusion of M. tuberculosis H37Rv into the disc space through a posterolateral approach (Yue 2022, PMID 35896778).
  • Lumbar vertebral model: "M. tuberculosis was cultured in 67% of the abscesses" (Liu 2015, PMID 25420665).
  • What the models reproduce: local vertebral destruction and abscess formation.
  • Limitations: these are mainly direct local inoculation models, not haematogenous spread from a pulmonary focus. They bypass the natural disease course and are used for surgical, biomaterial and drug-delivery research.
  • Swine: a possible natural model (PMID 29846908).
  • Not retrieved: spinal-TB-specific mouse, zebrafish or organoid models, and model database entries (MGI and others).
