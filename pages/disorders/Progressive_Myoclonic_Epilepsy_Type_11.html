Progressive Myoclonic Epilepsy Type 11

Mendelian MONDO:0030034 Pathograph 32 Show in embeddings browser Progressive Myoclonus Epilepsy

Progressive myoclonic epilepsy type 11 (EPM11) is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental and epileptic disorder caused by heterozygous, almost always de novo, truncating variants in the last exon (exon 17) of SEMA6B, which encodes the class 6 transmembrane semaphorin 6B. Because these variants lie in the region where a premature stop codon escapes nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, the mutant transcript is stable and is predicted to yield a truncated protein that keeps its extracellular sema domain and transmembrane segment but lacks the intracellular domain through which class 6 semaphorins signal as receptors. Heterozygous truncating variants earlier in the gene occur in the general population, so simple haploinsufficiency is not the accepted mechanism; whether the truncated protein acts as a dominant negative, gains a toxic function, or adds to a partial loss of SEMA6B signalling is unresolved. Development is mildly delayed from infancy and seizures usually begin between about 1 and 6 years. In the classic presentation, action and cortical myoclonus, generalized tonic-clonic, atonic and absence seizures, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal signs and cognitive and motor regression follow, with loss of independent walking and speech in the second decade. The same class of variants also produces developmental and epileptic encephalopathy without a recognized progressive myoclonic course and, less often, intellectual disability without epilepsy. This entry treats these presentations as one SEMA6B-related disease spectrum rather than as separate disorders, because they share the genotype and no genotype-phenotype correlation separates them.

Inheritance

1
Autosomal Dominant (predominantly de novo) HP:0000006
A single heterozygous SEMA6B variant is sufficient to cause disease. Nearly all reported variants arose de novo, so recurrence within a family is unusual. Two exceptions are reported: a family with an adult-onset course and a missense variant shared by two affected siblings, and a family in which a splice-site variant outside the last exon segregated with epilepsy with marked incomplete penetrance. Penetrance of the classic exon 17 truncations cannot be judged because they are almost never inherited.

Attached to

The discovery study inferred a dominant-negative or gain-of-function mechanism from three facts: the variants escape decay, upstream truncating variants are tolerated, and zebrafish last-exon crispants were more severely affected than start-codon crispants. Later papers state the mechanism in different directions: one review calls it a toxic gain of function, while the neuronal study calls the tested variant loss-of-function and found that knockdown of wild-type Sema6b reproduced the spine defect. The truncated protein is overexpressed and mislocalized in transfected cells, which fits either retention of wild-type partners or a new activity. None of the experiments tested the variant at physiological heterozygous dosage in a mammalian neuron, and the zebrafish crispants were mosaic, so the field has a consistent observation (position matters) without a demonstrated mechanism. This is why functional_impact_category is UNKNOWN.

Pathophysiology

8
SEMA6B Last-Exon Truncating Variant Escaping NMD
Disease-causing variants are heterozygous nonsense and frameshift variants clustered in exon 17, the last exon of SEMA6B, where a premature stop codon does not trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. In lymphoblastoid cells from an affected individual the mutant transcript was present at a level comparable to the wild-type transcript. Truncating variants upstream of this region, which would be degraded, are seen in the general population, and SEMA6B is tolerant of loss-of-function variation; the pathogenic variant class is therefore defined by its position, not by truncation as such.

Disrupted SEMA6B-Plexin Signalling
SEMA6B binds plexin-A2 and acts as a receptor whose intracellular domain carries reverse signalling into the cell; in chick commissural neurons a SEMA6B lacking that domain cannot substitute for the full-length protein. Disease variants alter SEMA6B binding to plexin-A2 in co-immunoprecipitation experiments. Whether the truncated protein blocks signalling by the wild-type protein, signals aberrantly, or simply fails to signal is not established, so the node records altered signalling without a direction.

Abnormal Neural Circuit Development
SEMA6B is expressed broadly in the developing and mature brain, most strongly in cortical excitatory neurons and cerebellar Purkinje cells. In zebrafish, mosaic truncation of the last coding exon of both sema6b orthologs caused small heads, a smaller optic tectum and defective neuronal development, whereas truncation near the start codon caused only a mild phenotype. The developmental timing of the human disease, with delay before seizure onset, fits a disorder of circuit formation, but which circuits are miswired in patients has not been shown.

Cortical Hyperexcitability
Patients show the neurophysiological signs of a hyperexcitable sensorimotor cortex typical of the progressive myoclonus epilepsies: giant or enlarged somatosensory evoked potentials, an enhanced long-latency C reflex, and myoclonic jerks preceded by a cortical potential on jerk-locked back averaging. Zebrafish carrying last-exon truncations of the sema6b orthologs were more sensitive to the convulsant pentylenetetrazole. How altered SEMA6B signalling produces this hyperexcitability, for example whether inhibitory interneurons are involved, is unknown.

Diagnosis

1
Molecular Genetic Testing
Diagnosis rests on a heterozygous pathogenic SEMA6B variant, usually a de novo truncating variant in exon 17, found by exome sequencing or a multigene epilepsy panel. Because SEMA6B truncating variants outside the decay-escape region are tolerated, the position of a truncating variant is central to its interpretation. Supportive findings are giant or enlarged somatosensory evoked potentials, an enhanced C reflex and cortical myoclonus on polygraphy; brain MRI is usually normal.
Differential diagnosis includes the other progressive myoclonus epilepsies with developmental delay and the developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, notably Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and epilepsy with myoclonic-atonic seizures, which several SEMA6B patients were first diagnosed with. One reported patient's presentation was initially taken for Angelman syndrome.

    Do last-exon SEMA6B truncations cause disease by a dominant-negative
    effect on wild-type SEMA6B, by a new toxic function of the truncated

  attaches_to:

  - pathophysiology#Stable Truncated SEMA6B Lacking the Intracellular Domain
  - pathophysiology#Disrupted SEMA6B-Plexin Signalling
  rationale: >-
    The discovery study inferred a dominant-negative or gain-of-function
    mechanism from three facts: the variants escape decay, upstream truncating
    variants are tolerated, and zebrafish last-exon crispants were more severely
    affected than start-codon crispants. Later papers state the mechanism in
    different directions: one review calls it a toxic gain of function, while
    the neuronal study calls the tested variant loss-of-function and found that
    knockdown of wild-type Sema6b reproduced the spine defect. The truncated
    protein is overexpressed and mislocalized in transfected cells, which fits
    either retention of wild-type partners or a new activity. None of the
    experiments tested the variant at physiological heterozygous dosage in a
    mammalian neuron, and the zebrafish crispants were mosaic, so the field
    has a consistent observation (position matters) without a demonstrated
    mechanism. This is why functional_impact_category is UNKNOWN.

Progressive Myoclonic Epilepsy Type 11 (EPM11): A Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Progressive Myoclonic Epilepsy Type 11 (EPM11) MONDO ID: MONDO:0030034 · OMIM: #618876 · Causal gene: SEMA6B (HGNC:7357) Category: Mendelian (monogenic), autosomal dominant

Summary

Progressive Myoclonic Epilepsy Type 11 (EPM11) is an ultra-rare, autosomal-dominant progressive myoclonic epilepsy / developmental-and-epileptic-encephalopathy caused by heterozygous, usually de novo, truncating variants clustered in the last exon (exon 17) of SEMA6B (semaphorin-6B). The defining molecular feature is that these variants fall within the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD)-insensitive region of the transcript. Rather than being degraded, the mutant mRNA is translated into a stable truncated protein lacking the intracellular domain, which acts through a toxic gain-of-function / dominant-negative mechanism. This distinguishes EPM11 from simple haploinsufficiency disorders and explains why the de novo truncating variant class is so tightly disease-associated (PMID: 32169168; PMID: 36719163).

Mechanistically, SEMA6B is a transmembrane class-6 semaphorin that functions as an axon-guidance receptor binding floorplate-derived PlexinA2 (and, in hippocampal mossy fibers, PlexinA4). The intracellular domain lost in disease truncations is essential for its receptor-signaling function. Disruption of SEMA6B signaling impairs axon guidance, dendritic-spine maturation, and synaptogenesis across cortical, hippocampal, and cerebellar circuits—including GABAergic interneurons—producing an excitation/inhibition imbalance that drives cortical hyperexcitability, seizures, and myoclonus, alongside developmental regression, intellectual disability, and ataxia (PMID: 25209245; PMID: 35604360; PMID: 20484647).

Clinically, EPM11 typically presents with developmental regression around age 2 after relatively normal early development, followed by polymorphic and often refractory seizures, progressive myoclonus, ataxia, spasticity, loss of independent ambulation, and severe cognitive impairment. The known worldwide caseload is tiny—approximately 36 reported patients—spanning a phenotypic spectrum from classic PME to non-syndromic intellectual disability. There is no disease-modifying or curative therapy; management is symptomatic anti-seizure/anti-myoclonic polytherapy with supportive care, and prognosis is poor with lifelong, progressive disability (PMID: 39419291; PMID: 30999254).

1. Disease Information

Overview. EPM11 is a monogenic progressive myoclonic epilepsy (PME) belonging to the group of developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. It is characterized by infantile/early-childhood onset, developmental regression, progressive myoclonus, polymorphic and frequently pharmacoresistant seizures, cerebellar and pyramidal/extrapyramidal signs, and severe cognitive impairment.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. EPM11 is a purely monogenic disorder caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in SEMA6B. The predominant and best-validated class is truncating (frameshift/nonsense) variants clustered in the last exon (exon 17), which escape NMD and produce a toxic truncated protein (PMID: 32169168; PMID: 36719163).

Genetic risk factors. The causal variants are themselves the disease determinant. Most arise de novo; variants are absent from population databases such as gnomAD, consistent with pathogenicity. No susceptibility loci or modifier genes have been established.

Environmental risk factors. None implicated. No toxic, infectious, lifestyle, occupational, dietary, radiation, or age/sex-related environmental risk factors are known to contribute. EPM11 is not triggered by environmental exposure.

Protective factors. No genetic or environmental protective factors or modifier alleles are known. The only outcome-modifying factor identified is effective seizure control (see Prognosis).

Gene–environment interactions. None known; as a Mendelian disorder with high penetrance of the classic truncating variants, no GxE interactions have been characterized.

Phenotype characteristics. Onset of developmental regression occurs around 2 years of age after relatively normal early development; various seizure types emerge between 18 months and 6 years and are often refractory. Over the first and second decades, pyramidal, extrapyramidal, and cerebellar signs accumulate. Cognitive impairment is severe (few words or non-verbal). The phenotype is progressive and does not correlate with variant type or location (PMID: 32169168; PMID: 36719163; PMID: 35604360).

Phenotypic spectrum. Cordovado et al. (2022) showed the spectrum extends beyond classic PME: "until now, SEMA6B variants have mainly been reported in patients with progressive myoclonic epilepsy, our study indicates that the clinical spectrum is wider and also includes non-syndromic ID without epilepsy or myoclonus" (PMID: 35604360). The 2025 systematic review (n=36) subdivided presentations into PME (50%, 18/36), developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (36%, 13/36), neurodevelopmental disorder (11%, 4/36), and epilepsy (3%, 1/36) (PMID: 39419291).

Quality of life impact. Severe. Progressive loss of ambulation, non-verbal status, refractory seizures, and myoclonus produce profound dependence in daily functioning and lifelong disability. EPM11 can clinically mimic Angelman syndrome (PMID: 42125992).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene. SEMA6B (semaphorin-6B), HGNC:7357, located on chromosome 15q21.1, encoding a transmembrane class-6 semaphorin. UniProt: Q9H3T3.

Pathogenic variants. - Predominant class: Truncating (frameshift and nonsense) variants clustered in the last exon (exon 17), which fall in the NMD-insensitive region. These escape nonsense-mediated decay and produce a stable protein lacking the intracellular domain (PMID: 32169168; PMID: 36719163). - Rarer classes: Missense variants (e.g., c.432C>G p.Cys144Trp, reported in an adult-onset case) and non-last-exon splice-site variants (e.g., c.1680-2A>G, NM_032108.4) producing diverse aberrant transcripts with incomplete penetrance (PMID: 42696975).

Variant classification (ACMG/AMP). Recurrent de novo exon-17 truncating variants are classified pathogenic/likely pathogenic, supported by genome-wide statistical enrichment in developmental and epileptic encephalopathy trios and absence from population databases.

Allele frequency. The causal de novo variants are absent from gnomAD and other population databases, consistent with pathogenicity.

Somatic vs germline. Variants are germline (constitutional), usually de novo; rare inherited (autosomal-dominant transmitted) cases exist.

Functional consequences. Gain of function / dominant-negative. Castellotti et al. state: "The exon 17 is mainly involved, with truncating mutations causing the production of aberrant proteins with toxic gain of function" (PMID: 36719163). The truncated protein lacks the intracellular domain required for receptor signaling and mislocalizes subcellularly, reducing dendritic spine maturation (PMID: 35604360).

Modifier genes / epigenetics / chromosomal abnormalities. None established. No DNA methylation or histone-modification changes have been characterized for EPM11, and no large-scale chromosomal rearrangements are implicated (though a 12p13.32 deletion appears in the broader PMA differential, unrelated to SEMA6B).

5. Environmental Information

Not applicable. EPM11 is a monogenic disorder with no identified environmental, lifestyle, toxic, occupational, radiation, or infectious contributing factors. No infectious agents trigger or cause the disease.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. A de novo (usually) truncating variant in the last exon (exon 17) of SEMA6B arises → leads to a premature stop codon in the NMD-insensitive region.
  2. Because the variant lies in the NMD(-) region, the mutant mRNA escapes nonsense-mediated decay → results in a stable truncated SEMA6B protein lacking the intracellular domain (inferred from NMD rules + observed protein; demonstrated in overexpression systems).
  3. The truncated protein exerts a dominant-negative / toxic gain-of-function effect on SEMA6B signaling (and mislocalizes subcellularly) → results in disrupted SEMA6B–PlexinA2 (and PlexinA4) receptor signaling.
  4. Disrupted semaphorin–plexin signaling → leads to impaired axon guidance, reduced dendritic-spine density/maturation, and defective synaptogenesis.
  5. Faulty wiring across cortical, hippocampal (mossy fiber), and cerebellar circuits, including GABAergic interneurons → results in abnormal neural connectivity. The mechanism branches here:
  6. Branch 6a (epileptogenesis): reduced inhibition / excitation–inhibition imbalance → cortical hyperexcitability → myoclonus, polymorphic and refractory seizures.
  7. Branch 6b (neurodevelopmental): abnormal connectivity and ongoing circuit dysfunction → developmental regression, severe intellectual disability, ataxia, and progressive pyramidal/extrapyramidal signs.
  8. Progressive accumulation of circuit dysfunction over the first two decades → leads to loss of ambulation, non-verbal status, and lifelong disability.

(Steps 1–3 and the gain-of-function nature are directly supported; the GABAergic/E-I-imbalance link in branch 6a is partly inferred from expression data and general PME pathophysiology.)

Supporting detail

  • Molecular pathways: Semaphorin–plexin axon-guidance signaling. SEMA6B binds PlexinA2 at the midline and PlexinA4 in the hippocampus. "we demonstrate that Sema6B binds to floorplate-derived plexin A2 (PlxnA2) for navigation at the midline" (PMID: 25209245).
  • Protein dysfunction: Loss of the intracellular domain abolishes receptor signaling: "Truncated Sema6B lacking the intracellular domain was unable to rescue the loss-of-function phenotype, confirming a receptor function of Sema6B" (PMID: 25209245). Patient variants mislocalize and reduce spine density: "The overexpression of some variants leads to a subcellular mislocalization of SEMA6B protein in HEK293T cells and to a reduced spine density owing to loss of mature spines in neuronal cultures" (PMID: 35604360).
  • Cellular processes: Dendritic spine formation/maturation (GO:0060996, GO:0060997), synaptogenesis, neuronal migration/wiring. Cell types: cortical excitatory neurons (CL:0000679), GABAergic inhibitory interneurons (CL:0000617), cerebellar Purkinje cells (CL:0000121), hippocampal pyramidal neurons.
  • Subcellular compartments: Plasma membrane (transmembrane receptor), dendritic spine, axonal growth cone; disease-associated mislocalization.
  • Metabolic / immune involvement: None characterized; EPM11 is not a metabolic or immune-mediated PME.
  • Omics profiling: No disease-specific transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, or lipidomic signatures have been published; mechanistic data derive from targeted functional assays.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

Organ / system level. Primary involvement is the central nervous system (UBERON:0001017). Key regions: cerebral cortex (UBERON:0000956), cerebellum (UBERON:0002037, Purkinje cells), hippocampus (UBERON:0002421, mossy fiber projections). Body system: nervous system (UBERON:0001016). No primary non-neurological organ involvement; secondary effects (e.g., immobility-related complications) follow from progressive disability.

Tissue / cell level. Nervous tissue. Affected cell populations: cortical excitatory neurons (CL:0000679), GABAergic interneurons (CL:0000617), cerebellar Purkinje cells (CL:0000121), hippocampal neurons. SEMA6B is highly expressed across these neuronal populations.

Subcellular level. Plasma membrane / transmembrane receptor localization; dendritic spines; axonal growth cones. GO cellular components: GO:0005886 (plasma membrane), GO:0043197 (dendritic spine).

Localization / lateralization. Diffuse, bilateral CNS involvement. EEG shows focal, multifocal, and diffuse abnormalities (see Diagnostics); brain MRI is often normal or non-specific.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Developmental regression around age 2 after relatively normal early development; insidious/subacute onset. Seizures emerge between 18 months and 6 years. A rare adult-onset missense case exists.
  • Progression: Chronic and progressive. Over the first and second decades, pyramidal, extrapyramidal, and cerebellar signs worsen (spasticity, loss of ambulation, myoclonus, tremor, ataxia). Seizures frequently refractory (~50% pharmacoresistant). Course is lifelong.
  • Stages: Early (regression + seizure onset) → intermediate (progressive myoclonus, motor decline) → advanced (loss of ambulation, non-verbal, severe disability).
  • Patterns: No spontaneous remission; seizures episodic but underlying neurodegeneration is relentlessly progressive. The critical window for intervention is early seizure control, which is associated with improved neurodevelopmental outcomes (PMID: 42696975).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology: Ultra-rare. Only ~36 patients reported worldwide (2025 systematic review); no prevalence/incidence estimates exist (PMID: 39419291).
  • Inheritance: Autosomal dominant, mostly de novo. ClinGen gene–disease validity Definitive. Rare inherited (AD-transmitted) families exist.
  • Penetrance: High/complete for classic exon-17 truncating variants; incomplete penetrance documented for a splice-site variant family (c.1680-2A>G) — "a novel heterozygous donor splice-site variant NM_032108.4 (SEMA6B): c.1680-2A>G segregating with autosomal dominant EPM1 in a family exhibiting marked incomplete penetrance" (PMID: 42696975).
  • Expressivity: Variable (PME to non-syndromic ID), not correlated with variant type/location.
  • Anticipation / mosaicism / founder effects / consanguinity: No genetic anticipation (not a repeat-expansion disorder); germline mosaicism theoretically possible but not systematically documented; no founder effect or geographic clustering. The original 4 patients were ethnically diverse (2 Japanese, 1 Israeli, 1 Malaysian).
  • Carrier frequency: Not applicable for a mostly de novo, dominant disorder.
  • Sex ratio / age distribution: No sex predilection reported; affected individuals are predominantly children and young adults.

10. Diagnostics

Diagnostic approach — genetic. Diagnosis relies on molecular genetic testing: exome (WES) or genome sequencing (WGS), or a multigene epilepsy/PME panel detecting the heterozygous SEMA6B truncating variant (typically exon 17, usually de novo). There is no specific biochemical biomarker, and brain MRI is often normal or non-specific (PMID: 32169168).

Clinical criteria & differential diagnosis. Diagnosis integrates the PME clinical syndrome with a confirmatory SEMA6B variant. Differential diagnosis includes other PMEs (Unverricht-Lundborg disease/EPM1, Lafora disease, MERRF, neuronal ceroid lipofuscinoses, sialidosis, North Sea PME/GOSR2) and Angelman syndrome, which EPM11 can mimic (PMID: 42125992). Progressive myoclonus ataxia (PMA) overlaps on a PMA-PME spectrum (PMID: 38844245).

Screening. No population newborn or carrier screening exists (ultra-rare, mostly de novo). Cascade testing is only relevant in the rare inherited families.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Prognosis is poor. EPM11 is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder (OMIM #618876). Over the first and second decades, affected individuals develop worsening pyramidal, extrapyramidal, and cerebellar signs—spasticity, loss of independent ambulation, myoclonus, tremor, ataxia—and severe cognitive impairment, becoming non-verbal (PMID: 32169168). Seizures are frequently refractory/pharmacoresistant (~half of 36 patients) (PMID: 39419291).

  • Survival/mortality: Specific survival and mortality statistics are not established given extreme rarity.
  • Morbidity/disability: Severe, progressive, lifelong. Quality of life severely impaired.
  • Recovery potential: None; the disorder does not remit and disability accumulates.
  • Prognostic factors: The principal modifiable factor is seizure control — "effective anti-seizure medication was associated with improved neurodevelopmental outcomes" (PMID: 42696975).
  • Prognostic biomarkers: None validated. Cortical hyperexcitability (giant SEPs / EMG-EEG correlate of myoclonus) may have prognostic value within the broader PMA-PME spectrum (PMID: 38844245).

12. Treatment

No disease-modifying or curative therapy exists. Management is symptomatic, centered on anti-seizure medications (ASMs), as for progressive myoclonus epilepsies generally (PMID: 32169168).

Lasek-Bal et al. describe the standard polytherapy strategy in PME: "ULD patients require anti-epileptic polytherapy, mostly benefitting from managing GTCS and myoclonus with valproic acid and clonazepam treatment. Patients may benefit from add-on therapy with levetiracetam or topiramate" (PMID: 30999254).

Drugs to avoid. Sodium-channel blockers (phenytoin, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, vigabatrin) can worsen myoclonus and are generally avoided in PME.

Emerging / cross-PME insights. In related PME models, GABA-ergic agents (sodium barbital, clonazepam, and the neurosteroid ganaxolone) suppressed seizures more effectively than valproate/levetiracetam in a GOSR2 North Sea PME Drosophila model, highlighting a potential GABA-targeted direction (PMID: 38781737). Combination levetiracetam + clonazepam is effective for myoclonus in MERRF (PMID: 30334528). These are extrapolations, not EPM11-specific trials.

Advanced/experimental therapeutics. No gene therapy, RNA-based therapy, cell therapy, or targeted molecular therapy exists or is in trials for EPM11. The toxic gain-of-function mechanism in principle makes allele-selective knockdown (ASO/siRNA) a theoretically rational—but currently unexplored—strategy.

Supportive/rehabilitative care. Physical, occupational, and speech therapy; management of spasticity, nutrition, and mobility; multidisciplinary neurological care.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Not possible for de novo disease. In rare inherited families, genetic counseling and reproductive options (preimplantation genetic testing, prenatal diagnosis) apply.
  • Secondary prevention: Early molecular diagnosis and early effective seizure control, which may improve neurodevelopmental outcomes (PMID: 42696975).
  • Tertiary prevention: Avoiding myoclonus-aggravating ASMs; managing complications of progressive disability.
  • Counseling: Genetic counseling is central—recurrence risk is low for confirmed de novo cases (with residual germline mosaicism risk) but up to 50% in autosomal-dominant transmitted families with variable/incomplete penetrance.
  • Immunization / public health / environmental interventions: Not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy / orthologs: SEMA6B is conserved across vertebrates. Mouse Sema6b (NCBI Gene) and chick Sema6B orthologs have been functionally characterized.
  • Natural disease: No naturally occurring EPM11-equivalent disease has been described in companion animals or wildlife (no OMIA entry for a SEMA6B epilepsy phenotype).
  • Comparative biology: The axon-guidance function of SEMA6B is evolutionarily conserved—defined in mouse (hippocampal mossy fibers, PlexinA4-dependent) and chick (commissural axons, PlexinA2-dependent) (PMID: 20484647; PMID: 25209245).
  • Transmission / zoonotic potential: Not applicable (genetic, non-communicable).

Convergence vs. challenge. The genetic and functional lines of evidence converge strongly on the NMD-escape/gain-of-function model. The main internal tension is that the available animal models are loss-of-function knockouts, which demonstrate the gene's normal axon-guidance role but do not directly prove the toxic gain-of-function human mechanism—this is inferred from NMD biology, protein-domain rescue failure, and variant overexpression data.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Tiny evidence base (~36 patients). No epidemiological estimates (prevalence/incidence), no survival/mortality statistics, and limited natural-history data.
  2. Mechanism inferred, not fully demonstrated in vivo. No animal model expresses the human truncated gain-of-function protein; the dominant-negative mechanism is supported by overexpression and domain-deletion rescue data, not by an endogenous knock-in.
  3. GABAergic/E-I imbalance link is partly inferred. The specific contribution of SEMA6B dysfunction in GABAergic interneurons to human epileptogenesis has not been directly measured in patient tissue.
  4. No omics signatures. No disease-specific transcriptomic, proteomic, or metabolomic profiles exist for EPM11.
  5. No disease-specific therapy or trial. All treatment data are extrapolated from PME broadly; no NCT-registered EPM11 trial exists.
  6. Genotype–phenotype correlation is weak/absent, complicating prognostic counseling, and incomplete penetrance in non-last-exon variants adds uncertainty.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Generate a knock-in mouse (or human iPSC-neuron/organoid) carrying a recurrent exon-17 truncating SEMA6B variant to directly test the dominant-negative/gain-of-function model and establish a translational model that recapitulates the human disease (not just loss of function).
  2. Characterize E/I balance and GABAergic interneuron wiring in patient-derived iPSC cortical organoids with electrophysiology/MEA to validate the cortical-hyperexcitability branch.
  3. Test allele-selective knockdown (ASO/siRNA) of the mutant truncated transcript as a mechanistically rational, disease-modifying strategy for a gain-of-function disorder.
