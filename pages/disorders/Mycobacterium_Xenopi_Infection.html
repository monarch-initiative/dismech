Mycobacterium Xenopi Infection

Infectious Disease MONDO:0017832 Pathograph 11 Show in embeddings browser Mycobacterial infectious disease

Mycobacterium xenopi infection is a nontuberculous mycobacterial infection in which inhaled environmental M. xenopi survives in pulmonary macrophage compartments, drives granulomatous airway and parenchymal inflammation, and can produce chronic cavitary lung disease.

Environmental Factors

2
Structural lung disease and prior tuberculosis damage
COPD, bronchiectasis, prior tuberculosis sequelae, and other structural lung lesions predispose airways to pulmonary NTM disease by weakening local clearance and giving inhaled M. xenopi an opportunity to persist.
Show evidence (1 reference)
PMID:34702233 SUPPORT Human Clinical
"Respiratory comorbidities were found in 68% of cases, tuberculosis sequelae in 31.4% of patients, and immunosuppression in 16.8% of cases."
Reports structural respiratory comorbidity and prior tuberculosis sequelae as common host contexts in a multicenter pulmonary-NTM cohort in which M. xenopi caused 20% of cases.
Mechanism Target:
PREDISPOSES Pulmonary Macrophage Persistence — Damaged airways impair local clearance and make the downstream macrophage-rich pulmonary niche permissive for persistent M. xenopi infection.
Immunosuppression
Acquired or iatrogenic immune impairment predisposes to NTM pulmonary disease by weakening the cell-mediated macrophage activation needed to restrict environmental mycobacteria.

Diagnosis

1
Composite NTM pulmonary disease criteria
Diagnosis requires compatible pulmonary symptoms and imaging plus repeated isolation of the same NTM species or otherwise strong microbiologic and histopathologic support, because environmental NTM can contaminate respiratory specimens without causing progressive lung disease.
Show evidence (2 references)
PMID:32797222 SUPPORT Other
"The current guideline also recommends use of these criteria to classify patients as having NTM pulmonary disease (Table 2)."
Establishes that the contemporary ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA guideline retains the composite clinical, radiographic and microbiologic NTM-PD criteria.
PMID:32797222 SUPPORT Other
">1 positive sputum culture is recommended for diagnostic purposes, and the same NTM species (or subspecies in the case of M. abscessus) should be isolated in ≥2 sputum cultures."
Supports requiring repeated same-species sputum isolation when diagnosing pulmonary disease from respiratory cultures.

Progression

1
Chronic pulmonary M. xenopi disease
M. xenopi pulmonary disease is chronic nontuberculous mycobacterial lung disease with high mortality among NTM species; available comparisons suggest that host comorbidity contributes substantially to this adverse prognosis.
Show evidence (1 reference)
PMID:32421543 SUPPORT Human Clinical
"Mycobacterium xenopi is associated with the highest mortality among pulmonary nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections, but whether this is due to the infection or other factors is unclear."
Frames the prognosis of M. xenopi pulmonary disease and the uncertainty about how much of the high mortality is pathogen-specific versus driven by comorbid host lung disease.

Transmission

Environmental Aerosol Inhalation
Environmental mycobacteria are acquired from water-associated aerosols into susceptible airways rather than by sustained person-to-person spread.

Identifies potable and hot-water plumbing as an environmental M. xenopi source that can contaminate respiratory and other clinical specimens.

Mycobacterium xenopi Infection — Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Mycobacterium xenopi Infection MONDO ID: MONDO:0017832 Category: Infectious Disease (nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease) NCBI Taxonomy: Mycobacterium xenopi txid1789 MeSH: D009165 (Mycobacterium Infections, Nontuberculous) ICD-10: A31.0 (pulmonary mycobacterial infection) · ICD-11: 1B21.0 OMIM: Not applicable (environmentally acquired infection, not a heritable Mendelian disease)

Summary

Mycobacterium xenopi infection is a chronic nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) disease caused by a slow-growing, thermophilic environmental bacillus (optimal growth ~42–45 °C) that permanently colonizes warm and hot potable-water systems and their biofilms. Humans acquire it by inhalation, aspiration, or inoculation from environmental water — not from person to person. Its practical ubiquity in hot-water plumbing makes it both a genuine pathogen and a frequent contaminant, so a single positive culture often reflects colonization rather than disease. It is among the most common NTM pulmonary pathogens in Europe (UK, France, Belgium, Central/Eastern Europe) and Canada (Ontario), typically ranking 2nd–3rd after the Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), and in some series (e.g., Serbia, 34.8%) the single most common species.

The disease predominantly affects older adults with pre-existing structural lung disease — COPD, bronchiectasis, prior tuberculosis — or impaired cell-mediated immunity. It presents insidiously as chronic pulmonary disease in one of two main radiologic forms (nodular-bronchiectatic ~54% or fibrocavitary ~19%), with chronic cough, sputum, dyspnea, fatigue, weight loss and night sweats, and substantially impaired health-related quality of life. In profound immunosuppression (notably advanced HIV/AIDS with CD4 <40/µL), M. xenopi can cause extrapulmonary and disseminated infection. Notably, M. xenopi carries the highest mortality among the pulmonary NTM species, although much of this excess reflects the frailty and comorbidity of the affected host rather than the intrinsic virulence of the organism.

Diagnosis follows the composite ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA (2020) criteria requiring concordant clinical, radiographic, and microbiologic evidence, with species identification by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and gene sequencing (rpoB, hsp65, 16S rDNA, 16S–23S ITS). Guideline treatment is a prolonged multidrug regimen of rifampicin + ethambutol + a macrolide (azithromycin preferred) and/or moxifloxacin, given for ≥12 months after sputum culture conversion, with parenteral amikacin added for cavitary/extensive or macrolide-resistant disease. A rifamycin-containing regimen is specifically protective for M. xenopi mortality. This is an environmentally acquired, non-heritable infection: there are no causal human genes, no Mendelian inheritance, penetrance, or carrier frequency. Human genetics contributes only polygenic susceptibility through the IL-12/IFN-γ axis, CFTR, SLC11A1 (NRAMP1), VDR and HLA, plus acquired neutralizing anti-IFN-γ autoantibodies.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — M. xenopi is a slow-growing, thermophilic environmental NTM causing chronic pulmonary disease, prevalent in Europe and Canada

M. xenopi is a slowly growing, scotochromogenic nontuberculous mycobacterium whose optimal growth temperature of approximately 42–45 °C explains its persistent colonization of warm- and hot-water systems. This thermophilia is central to both its ecology and its diagnostic ambiguity. As stated in a mycobacteriosis review, "M. xenopi permanently colonises most warm water systems, and its practical ubiquity makes difficult the interpretation of ambiguous findings on imaging" (PMID: 37871989).

Geographically, M. xenopi is a predominantly European and Canadian pathogen. A review of NTM epidemiology notes that "in regions within Europe, M kansasii, M xenopi, and M malmoense are more common" (PMID: 25676516). Quantitatively, in a French multicenter cohort of 477 patients, "The three most common NTM species were Mycobacterium avium complex (60%), M. xenopi (20%) and M. kansasii (5.7%)" (PMID: 34702233), making M. xenopi the 2nd most common cause of NTM pulmonary disease in France. Other series echo this: Geneva (12% of slow-growing NTM), Denmark (11%), and Serbia (34.8%, the most common species locally).

Finding 2 — M. xenopi pulmonary disease carries the highest mortality among NTM pulmonary infections

Multiple lines of evidence establish that M. xenopi pulmonary disease is associated with the highest mortality of any pulmonary NTM species, while emphasizing that causality is confounded by host factors. A retrospective matched cohort study stated directly: "Mycobacterium xenopi is associated with the highest mortality among pulmonary nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections, but whether this is due to the infection or other factors is unclear" (PMID: 32421543).

A population-based cohort from Ontario, Canada (9,681 NTM-PD; 10,936 NTM-PI) confirmed that "NTM-PD was associated with higher death rates for all species combined, Mycobacterium avium complex, and M. xenopi" (PMID: 28221106). In the French cohort where M. xenopi comprised 20% of cases, "The 2-year mortality was 14.4%" (PMID: 34702233); in that analysis, treatment was protective (HR ≈ 0.51) whereas age (HR ≈ 1.02) and M. abscessus were adverse. In the matched M. xenopi vs MAC comparison, the higher Charlson comorbidity index in the M. xenopi group (3.6 vs 3.2) points to host frailty as a major driver of the excess mortality rather than organism virulence alone.

Finding 3 — Host risk factors: structural lung disease, immunosuppression, older age, low BMI, male sex/smoking

M. xenopi pulmonary disease occurs predominantly in patients with pre-existing lung damage and impaired host defenses. In the French cohort, "Respiratory comorbidities were found in 68% of cases, tuberculosis sequelae in 31.4% of patients, and immunosuppression in 16.8% of cases" (PMID: 34702233). Radiologic forms in this and related cohorts were nodulobronchiectatic (~54%) and cavitary (~19%). A broad epidemiologic review notes that "Host factors important to the current epidemiology of NTM pulmonary disease include thoracic skeletal abnormalities, rheumatoid arthritis, and use of immunomodulatory drugs. Clustering of disease within families suggests a heritable genetic predisposition to disease susceptibility" (PMID: 25676516).

Importantly, pathogen genotype interacts with host susceptibility. In Ontario, "sequence type (ST) 5 was dominant in Ontario among 15 distinct STs and caused PD in people even without underlying lung disease, whereas disease due to non-ST5 was found almost exclusively in patients with underlying lung disease" (PMID: 29349535). Smoking and COPD are frequent (Serbia: smokers 55.6%, COPD 43.5%).

Finding 4 — Guideline treatment: rifampicin + ethambutol + macrolide and/or moxifloxacin for ≥12 months after culture conversion

The 2020 ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA guideline explicitly covers M. xenopi: "This guideline focuses on pulmonary disease in adults (without cystic fibrosis or human immunodeficiency virus infection) caused by the most common NTM pathogens such as Mycobacterium avium complex, Mycobacterium kansasii, and Mycobacterium xenopi" (PMID: 32797222, PMID: 32628747). The recommended regimen is described as: "The combination of rifampicin, ethambutol and macrolide (and/or moxifloxacin) is recommended for at least 12 months after conve[rsion]" (PMID: 33202438). Parenteral amikacin or streptomycin is added for cavitary or extensive nodular-bronchiectatic or macrolide-resistant disease, and adjuvant surgical resection is considered in selected patients (PMID: 32791550).

Treatment outcomes, from the matched cohort: "successful treatment was more common with Mx-PD (40.5% versus 16.7%, p = 0.02) owing to superior culture conversion (70.8% versus 33.3%, p = 0.0001)" (PMID: 32421543). High rates of in-vitro drug resistance are reported (84.6% resistant to ≥1 drug in a Polish series, PMID: 21334944), but the correlation between in-vitro susceptibility and clinical outcome remains poorly defined for M. xenopi.

Finding 5 — Clinical phenotype: chronic respiratory symptoms with systemic features and marked QoL impairment

M. xenopi/NTM pulmonary disease presents insidiously with chronic respiratory and systemic symptoms. A concept-elicitation study for a novel NTM symptom scale reported: "The concept elicitation interviews identified key symptoms of cough (100%), shortness of breath (98%), fatigue (95%), sputum production (90%), throat clearing (80%), mental fogging (75%), sensitivity to cold (62%), night sweats (55%), poor appetite (53%), chills/feverishness (50%/43%), weight loss (48%), and chest congestion (45%)" (PMID: 42070174). A Danish cohort (11% M. xenopi) found: "Symptoms included cough (62%), expectoration (51%), dyspnea (43%), and systemic symptoms (42%), with 36% experiencing symptoms for over 6 months" (PMID: 40139333).

Quality of life is significantly impaired: "The mean physical and mental HRQoL scores were 46.82 ± 6.33 and 43.95 ± 11.42, respectively, both significantly lower than the population norm of 50" (PMID: 42563732).

Finding 6 — Disseminated/extrapulmonary disease in severely immunocompromised hosts (notably advanced HIV/AIDS)

Although pulmonary disease predominates, M. xenopi causes extrapulmonary and disseminated infection in profound immunosuppression. In an AIDS series of uncommon pulmonary NTM: "Sixty-six percent of all patients with AIDS were infected by Mycobacterium xenopi. Chest radiographs of AIDS patients showed a tendency for predominance of interstitial infiltrate and rarity of fibronodular disease" (PMID: 9674460). The degree of immunodeficiency governs risk: "Antiretroviral therapy (ART) and toxoplasmosis prophylaxis reduced the risk of NTM disease whereas CD4 cells <40/microl at time of the first AIDS defining illness led to a 2.5 fold higher risk" (PMID: 11076783).

M. xenopi falls into an "intermediate" clinical-relevance category: "Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), M. xenopi, and M. abscessus form an intermediate category between these two extremes" (PMID: 23460010). Extrapulmonary sites reported include spondylodiscitis/vertebral osteomyelitis, genitourinary tract (urine isolates, PMID: 10624381), skin/soft tissue, and lymph nodes.

Finding 7 — Genetic and immunologic host susceptibility: IL-12/IFN-γ axis, CFTR, SLC11A1, VDR, HLA, and anti-IFN-γ autoantibodies

Susceptibility to NTM lung disease, including M. xenopi, is linked to genetically determined defects of cell-mediated immunity. A review of susceptibility describes "patients with genetically determined defects of cell-mediated immunity, including abnormalities of the interleukin-12/interferon-gamma axis, certain human leukocyte antigen alleles, cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator mutations, and polymorphisms of solute carrier 11A1 (or natural resistance-associated macrophage protein 1) and the vitamin D receptor" (PMID: 18515557). Acquired susceptibility includes "inhibitory antibodies directed against interferon-gamma, post-menopausal waning of endogenous oestrogen levels, coeliac disease" (PMID: 18515557).

Model-organism genetics corroborate the role of SLC11A1/NRAMP1: in mice, "susceptibility or resistance to infection caused by M. avium largely depended upon the Slc11a1 genotype" (PMID: 17664269). The Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) genes involved include IL12B, IL12RB1, IFNGR1, IFNGR2, and STAT1.

Suggested GO terms: interferon-gamma-mediated signaling pathway (GO:0060333), defense response to Gram-positive bacterium (GO:0050830), phagocytosis (GO:0006909), macroautophagy (GO:0016236).

Finding 8 — Prognostic factors: rifamycin protective in M. xenopi; cavitation, low BMI, age, comorbidity, smear positivity worsen outcome

A systematic review/meta-analysis of 41 studies (10,452 NTM-LD patients) quantified outcomes: "The overall all-cause mortality rate was 20% (95% CI 17-24%)" (PMID: 37147519), with clinical progression 46% and radiographic progression 43%. Crucially, a rifamycin regimen was protective specifically in M. xenopi: "increasing body mass index (BMI), hemoptysis, and treatment with rifamycin regimen (in M. xenopi) were significantly associated with decreased all-cause mortality in multivariable analysis" (PMID: 37147519).

Adverse prognostic factors were numerous: "Older age, male sex, history of TB, diabetes, chronic heart disease, malignancy, systemic immunosuppression, chronic liver disease, presence of cavity, consolidative radiologic features, acid-fast bacillus (AFB) smear positivity, hypoalbuminemia, anemia, increasing platelet count, high CRP, and high ESR were significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality" (PMID: 37147519).

Finding 9 — Diagnosis: composite ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA criteria; identification by MALDI-TOF and gene sequencing

Diagnosis follows the standardized ATS/IDSA (2007) and ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA (2020) criteria requiring all of: (1) pulmonary symptoms; (2) nodular or cavitary opacities on chest radiograph, or HRCT showing multifocal bronchiectasis with multiple small nodules; and (3) microbiologic confirmation (≥2 positive sputum cultures for the same species, OR ≥1 positive bronchial wash/lavage, OR compatible biopsy with granulomatous inflammation plus positive culture), while excluding other diagnoses. A review notes "Diagnostic evaluation follows the established criteria for all nontuberculous mycobacteria, but with certain qualifications given species-specific and regional differences in pathogenicity" (PMID: 37890917).

Because M. xenopi colonizes water and has intermediate/low clinical relevance, a single isolate frequently reflects colonization: "11 species, including M. fortuitum (32.4%), M. xenopi (20.0%), and M. gordonae (22.9%), showed clinical relevance below 50%" (PMID: 39306905) — underscoring the need for repeat sampling. Species identification uses MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry: "Species identification was conducted by MALDI-TOF MS" (PMID: 40351313), supplemented by gene sequencing (rpoB, hsp65, 16S rDNA, 16S–23S ITS). Differential diagnosis: pulmonary TB, other NTM, lung cancer, chronic aspergillosis, COPD/bronchiectasis exacerbation.

Finding 10 — Epidemiology: rising NTM-PD incidence worldwide; M. xenopi is male-predominant, European/Canadian, and not inherited

NTM pulmonary disease incidence is rising globally. US Medicare data (≥65 y, 2010–2019) reported "an average annual incidence of 20.1 per 100,000 population. NTM PD incidence was overall highest in the South and among women, Asian individuals, and persons aged ≥ 80 years" (PMID: 39358723). In Korea, "The incidence rates of NTM-PD annually increased by 14% (95% confidence interval, 10% to 19%) from 1.2 in 2006 to 4.8 in 2016" (PMID: 32550717). US pathogenic NTM prevalence rose 4.5%/year (2009–2015, PMID: 32818422), and Japanese NTM-associated mortality rose from 0.63 to 1.93/100,000/year 1997–2016 (PMID: 32556251).

Unlike female-predominant MAC, M. xenopi typically shows male predominance: "M. avium and M. intracellulare were more often isolated from women, while M. kansasii, M. gordonae and M. xenopi predominated in men" (PMID: 30594996). This is an environmentally acquired infection — NOT a heritable Mendelian condition: there are no causal human genes, no inheritance pattern, penetrance, founder effect, or carrier frequency. Host genetic factors are polygenic susceptibility modifiers only.

Finding 11 — Environmental reservoir and prevention: potable/hot-water contamination, pseudo-outbreaks, water/endoscope decontamination

M. xenopi is acquired from environmental water, colonizing potable and hospital hot-water systems and biofilms. Two classic hospital investigations illustrate the reservoir and its pitfalls. A nosocomial pseudo-outbreak (no true disease) was traced to tap water: "M xenopi was isolated from tap water in 20 of 24 patient rooms tested, the endoscopy suite, and the central hot water mixing tank" (PMID: 8132983). Bronchoscopy-associated pseudoinfections showed that "specimens obtained by bronchoscopy were more likely to yield M. xenopi than were specimens obtained by other routes (relative risk, 9.7; 95% confidence intervals, 3.2, 29.6)" (PMID: 8025757).

Prevention/control (no vaccine exists) centers on infection control: "Adequate disinfection of contaminated bronchoscopes and careful collection of specimens to avoid contamination with contaminated water are essential" (PMID: 8025757). This includes hot-water-system management (temperature control, disinfection), sterile-water rinsing of endoscopes, and, for patients, reduced aerosol exposure. In HIV, ART/immune restoration prevents dissemination.

Section-by-Section Report

1. Disease Information

M. xenopi infection is a chronic infectious disease caused by the slow-growing, thermophilic environmental NTM Mycobacterium xenopi. It most commonly manifests as chronic pulmonary disease and, rarely, disseminated/extrapulmonary disease in the severely immunocompromised. Identifiers: MONDO:0017832; MeSH D009165 (Mycobacterium Infections, Nontuberculous); NCBI Taxonomy txid1789; ICD-10 A31.0; ICD-11 1B21.0; no OMIM entry (not genetic). Synonyms: M. xenopi pulmonary disease, Mxe-PD/Mx-PD, xenopi mycobacteriosis, atypical/environmental/nontuberculous mycobacteriosis due to M. xenopi. Information is derived from aggregated disease-level resources (cohort studies, registries, reviews) rather than individual EHR.

2. Etiology

Causal factor: infection by Mycobacterium xenopi (NCBI:txid1789), acquired from environmental water. Risk factors — environmental/host: structural lung disease (COPD, bronchiectasis, prior TB sequelae 31.4%), immunosuppression (16.8%), older age, low BMI, male sex, smoking, thoracic skeletal abnormalities, rheumatoid arthritis, immunomodulatory drug use, advanced HIV (CD4 <40/µL → 2.5-fold risk). Genetic risk (susceptibility modifiers only): IL-12/IFN-γ axis genes (IL12B, IL12RB1, IFNGR1/2, STAT1), CFTR, SLC11A1/NRAMP1, VDR, HLA alleles; familial clustering suggests heritable predisposition. Protective factors: ART and higher BMI; rifamycin-containing treatment reduces mortality. Gene–environment interaction: disease requires the combination of environmental exposure and host defect; pathogen genotype (ST5 vs non-ST5) further modulates whether underlying lung disease is required.

3. Phenotypes

See Finding 5. Predominant phenotypes are chronic cough (62–100%), sputum production (51–90%), dyspnea (43–98%), fatigue (95%), weight loss (48%), night sweats (55%), fever/chills (43–50%), and hemoptysis in fibrocavitary disease. Onset: adult/late-onset, insidious/chronic. Severity: variable (milder in nodular-bronchiectatic, more severe in fibrocavitary). Progression: chronic, often progressive. QoL: significantly below population norm (physical 46.82, mental 43.95 vs 50). HPO terms listed in Finding 5.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Not applicable as a genetic disease — no causal human genes, pathogenic variants, chromosomal abnormalities, or modifier genes in the Mendelian sense. Human host-susceptibility loci (polygenic) are described in Finding 7: IL12B, IL12RB1, IFNGR1, IFNGR2, STAT1, CFTR, SLC11A1, VDR, HLA. The relevant "genome" for pathogenesis is bacterial: M. xenopi strains are typed by IS1395 RFLP and sequence typing (15 STs; ST5 dominant in Ontario). No germline/somatic human variant classification applies.

5. Environmental Information

The dominant etiologic factor is environmental: M. xenopi colonizes tap water, hospital hot-water tanks, showerheads, and biofilms; it resists chlorination and thrives at 42–45 °C. Infectious agent: Mycobacterium xenopi (slow-growing scotochromogen). It has also been isolated from fish-pond waters among diverse NTM (PMID: 41107288). Lifestyle: smoking is a frequent co-factor. Routes of acquisition are inhalation of aerosols, aspiration, and inoculation. No person-to-person transmission.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain:

  1. Environmental exposure to M. xenopi in aerosolized/aspirated warm water leads to deposition of bacilli in the distal airways.
  2. In a host with impaired mucociliary clearance (bronchiectasis/COPD) or defective cell-mediated immunity, the bacilli evade clearance and are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages (CL:0000583 alveolar macrophage).
  3. Defects in the IL-12/IFN-γ axis (inferred from NTM susceptibility genetics) result in inadequate macrophage activation and failure to restrict intracellular mycobacterial growth (GO:0060333 IFN-γ signaling; GO:0006909 phagocytosis; GO:0016236 macroautophagy).
  4. Persistent intracellular infection leads to chronic granulomatous inflammation with epithelioid histiocytes and lymphocytes in the lung parenchyma and airways.
  5. Chronic inflammation results in progressive tissue damage — bronchial wall destruction (bronchiectasis), nodule formation, and, in the fibrocavitary form, caseous necrosis and cavitation.
  6. Structural airway damage leads to the clinical phenotype (chronic cough, sputum, dyspnea, hemoptysis) and impaired gas exchange.
  7. Branch (immunocompromised host, e.g., CD4 <40/µL): failure of containment results in hematogenous dissemination to extrapulmonary sites (bone, GU tract, skin, lymph nodes).

Upstream mechanisms are host immune defect + environmental exposure; downstream are granulomatous inflammation, fibrosis/cavitation, and airway remodeling. Cell types: alveolar macrophages (CL:0000583), T lymphocytes (CL:0000084), epithelioid histiocytes, bronchial epithelial cells (CL:0002328). Processes: granulomatous inflammation, chronic inflammation, IFN-γ signaling, phagocytosis, autophagy. Detailed molecular pathway data specific to M. xenopi (transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics) are not available in the literature reviewed; the chain above is largely inferred from general mycobacterial immunology.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

Primary organ: lung (UBERON:0002048). Structures: bronchi/bronchioles (bronchiectasis, UBERON:0002185), lung parenchyma (nodules, cavities). Body system: respiratory (UBERON:0001004). Secondary/disseminated: vertebral column/intervertebral disc (spondylodiscitis, UBERON:0001130), kidney/urinary tract (UBERON:0002113), skin/soft tissue (UBERON:0002097), lymph nodes (UBERON:0000029). Cells: alveolar macrophage (CL:0000583), bronchial epithelial cell (CL:0002328), T cell (CL:0000084). Subcellular: phagosome/phagolysosome (GO:0045335), where mycobacteria reside. Lateralization: often bilateral in nodular-bronchiectatic disease; cavitary disease frequently upper-lobe predominant.

8. Temporal Development

Onset: adult/late-onset, insidious and chronic; symptoms present >6 months in ~36% at diagnosis. Progression: slow but progressive; two radiologic trajectories (nodular-bronchiectatic, generally milder; fibrocavitary, more aggressive). Bronchiectasis and nodules tend to persist even with microbiologic cure, whereas cavities/consolidation/bronchiolitis regress with successful treatment (PMID: 39088042). Course: chronic, often lifelong; relapse/recurrence common. Remission: treatment-induced culture conversion in ~70% of M. xenopi patients when treated. Critical period: early treatment initiation is advised for smear-positive or cavitary disease.

9. Inheritance and Population

See Finding 10. NTM-PD incidence is rising globally (US ~20.1/100,000 in ≥65 y; Korea 14%/year increase). M. xenopi is male-predominant, European/Canadian in distribution, and not inherited (no inheritance pattern, penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, mosaicism, founder effect, consanguinity role, or carrier frequency apply). Host susceptibility is polygenic/multifactorial. Age distribution skews older (peak mortality ages 80–84 in Japan).

10. Diagnostics

See Finding 9. Microbiology: AFB smear, culture (solid + liquid; slow-growing, ~28–68 days), MALDI-TOF MS, gene sequencing (rpoB, hsp65, 16S rDNA, ITS). Imaging: chest radiograph and HRCT (nodular bronchiectasis vs fibrocavitary patterns; CT scoring for treatment response). Criteria: ATS/ERS/ESCMID/IDSA composite (clinical + radiographic + ≥2 concordant cultures). Drug susceptibility testing per CLSI recommended, though clinical correlation is uncertain. Biomarkers: anti-GPL-core IgA is specific for MAC/M. abscessus, not M. xenopi. Differential diagnosis: TB, lung cancer, chronic pulmonary aspergillosis, other NTM. Genetic/omics diagnostics are not applicable for diagnosis of this infection.

11. Outcome/Prognosis

See Findings 2 and 8. Pooled NTM-LD all-cause mortality ~20% (95% CI 17–24%); M. xenopi has the highest mortality among pulmonary NTM, largely comorbidity-driven; French 2-year mortality 14.4%. Treatment success ~40.5% for M. xenopi (culture conversion ~70.8%). Prognostic factors tabulated in Finding 8; rifamycin regimen is specifically protective. Complications: respiratory failure, recurrent infection, hemoptysis, progression of bronchiectasis, dissemination in immunocompromised.

12. Treatment

See Finding 4. Core regimen: rifampicin + ethambutol + macrolide (azithromycin) and/or moxifloxacin, ≥12 months post-conversion. Intensification: parenteral amikacin/streptomycin for cavitary/extensive/macrolide-resistant disease; inhaled amikacin (ALIS) for refractory disease. Surgery: adjuvant resection for localized/refractory disease (recurrence risk substantial). Supportive: airway clearance, pulmonary rehabilitation, nutrition, management of comorbidities. Refractory disease: individualized intensification (PMID: 41733158). No gene/cell/RNA/targeted/immunotherapies apply. NCIT terms in Finding 4.

13. Prevention

See Finding 11. No vaccine. Primary prevention: water-system management (temperature/disinfection), endoscope decontamination with sterile-water rinse, aerosol-exposure reduction, avoiding tap-water contact with specimens/instruments. Secondary prevention: early detection in high-risk patients (COPD, bronchiectasis, immunosuppressed). Tertiary prevention: airway clearance, comorbidity management, ART in HIV to prevent dissemination. No primary chemoprophylaxis is defined specifically for M. xenopi (azithromycin prophylaxis applies to disseminated MAC in advanced HIV).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

M. xenopi was originally isolated from the toad Xenopus laevis (hence the name). It is an environmental organism found in fish-pond waters (PMID: 41107288) among diverse NTM. Naturally occurring clinical disease in companion animals/wildlife is not well documented in the reviewed literature. Zoonotic potential: not a recognized zoonosis; acquisition is from shared environmental water reservoirs rather than animal-to-human transmission. Orthologous host-defense genes (Slc11a1/Nramp1) in mice control susceptibility to related NTM (M. avium).

15. Model Organisms

No dedicated M. xenopi animal model is established in the reviewed literature. Relevant models for NTM susceptibility: mouse strains differing at Slc11a1 (Nramp1) demonstrate near-monogenic control of susceptibility to environmental mycobacteria (M. avium) (PMID: 17664269); I/St vs A/Sn mouse comparisons dissect TB vs M. avium susceptibility. Applications: studying host genetic control of macrophage mycobacterial restriction and the IL-12/IFN-γ axis. Limitation: these models use M. avium/M. tuberculosis, not M. xenopi; slow growth and thermophilia of M. xenopi complicate murine modeling. Resources: MGI (Slc11a1).

The unifying interpretation is a two-hit model: ubiquitous environmental exposure (necessary but not sufficient) combined with a permissive host (structural and/or immunologic defect). Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Evidence quality: Most treatment and outcome data derive from retrospective, observational cohorts; there are few randomized controlled trials specific to M. xenopi, and guideline recommendations are largely low-certainty (GRADE).
  • In-vitro/in-vivo disconnect: The correlation between drug susceptibility testing and clinical outcome is poorly defined for M. xenopi, complicating regimen selection.
  • Colonization vs disease: The low clinical relevance of single isolates (~20%) means epidemiologic figures conflate colonization and true disease.
  • Molecular pathophysiology: No M. xenopi-specific transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, or single-cell datasets were identified; the mechanistic chain is largely inferred from generic NTM/mycobacterial immunology.
  • Model organisms: No dedicated M. xenopi animal model exists; susceptibility genetics are extrapolated from M. avium/M. tuberculosis mouse models.
  • Host genetics: Susceptibility loci (IL-12/IFN-γ, CFTR, SLC11A1, VDR, HLA) are associations from broader NTM literature, not M. xenopi-specific GWAS.
