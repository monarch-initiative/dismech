Combined Oxidative Phosphorylation Deficiency 19

Mendelian MONDO:0014269 Pathograph 27 Show in embeddings browser Combined Oxidative Phosphorylation Deficiency Mitochondrial iron-sulfur cluster assembly disorder

Combined oxidative phosphorylation deficiency 19 (COXPD19) is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive mitochondrial disease caused by biallelic missense variants in LYRM4, which encodes ISD11, the small LYR-motif subunit that binds and stabilizes the cysteine desulfurase NFS1 in the core mitochondrial iron-sulfur cluster (ISC) assembly complex. Weakened ISD11-NFS1 binding destabilizes NFS1, cutting the sulfur supply for de novo cluster assembly, so the defect sits at the earliest step of the ISC pathway and reaches a broad range of Fe-S client proteins. Patients show combined deficiency of respiratory chain complexes I, II and III in muscle and liver, with loss of the aconitases and ferrochelatase, and present in the newborn period or early childhood with lactic acidosis and respiratory failure. Three patients in two families are reported, and the course ranges from death as a neonate to full recovery between infection-triggered crises.

Does limited cysteine availability in the newborn period explain why one cousin died as a neonate and the other, with the same genotype, is healthy as an adult, and would a sulfur donor therefore help?
EMERGING HYPOTHESIS OPEN coxpd19_cysteine_availability_hypothesis
Attached to
pathophysiology#Reduced NFS1 Cysteine Desulfurase Activity pathophysiology#Homozygous LYRM4 Missense Variant
The founding report proposes that residual NFS1 activity is substrate-limited, so that the cysteine supply sets how much cluster assembly survives. Cysteine is conditionally essential in the newborn because cystathionase activity is low, which would make the neonatal period the point of greatest vulnerability and could account for two relatives with one genotype and opposite outcomes. The proposal is the authors' interpretation; no cysteine or N-acetylcysteine has been given to a patient and no plasma cysteine measurement from these patients is published. It matters because it is the only therapeutic idea this disease has, and because the same argument does not transfer to the late-acting MMDS genes, whose step is not sulfur-limited. The hypothesis also has a published objection: the third patient's crises began at two years and recurred, which a limitation confined to the newborn period does not explain, and her own authors say so.

Why is complex IV, which contains no iron-sulfur cluster, deficient in the muscle of two of the three patients?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN coxpd19_complex_iv_deficiency
Attached to
pathophysiology#Respiratory Chain Complex I, II and III Deficiency phenotypes#Decreased activity of mitochondrial complex IV
Complex IV has copper and heme centres and no Fe-S cluster, so its deficiency cannot be read directly off the assembly defect. Three routes are plausible and none has been tested in this disease: loss of complex III destabilizing the III-IV supercomplex; the ferrochelatase deficiency limiting heme a availability; and general mitochondrial damage from the iron and reactive-oxygen accumulation the cellular model shows. Yeast work on a late assembly factor reports a role in III/IV supercomplexes, which is suggestive but a different gene and organism. The answer decides whether complex IV deficiency should be read as part of this disease's core biochemistry or as a secondary marker of tissue damage.

Why are cultured fibroblasts largely spared when muscle and liver are severely deficient, and what does that mean for diagnosis?
OPEN QUESTION OPEN coxpd19_tissue_sparing_of_fibroblasts
Attached to
pathophysiology#Respiratory Chain Complex I, II and III Deficiency
The founding report found fibroblast respiratory chain activities largely normal while the same patient's muscle and liver were severely deficient, so a normal fibroblast study does not exclude this diagnosis. Two explanations compete: a real tissue difference in demand for Fe-S flux, with oxidative tissues having the lower reserve, or an artefact of culture in high glucose, where glycolysis makes respiratory capacity dispensable and a defect need not show. The second is testable by forcing oxidative metabolism in galactose, and distinguishing them matters for which tissue a diagnostic laboratory should assay.
The fibroblast enzyme values and the MyoD myotube experiment are reported in the full text of PMID:23814038, which is cached as an abstract only, so the quantitative claim is recorded here as the open question rather than as evidence.

- discussion_id: coxpd19_cysteine_availability_hypothesis
  prompt: >-
    Does limited cysteine availability in the newborn period explain why one cousin died
    as a neonate and the other, with the same genotype, is healthy as an adult, and would
    a sulfur donor therefore help?
  kind: EMERGING_HYPOTHESIS
  status: OPEN
  attaches_to:
  - pathophysiology#Reduced NFS1 Cysteine Desulfurase Activity
  - pathophysiology#Homozygous LYRM4 Missense Variant
  rationale: >-
    The founding report proposes that residual NFS1 activity is substrate-limited, so
    that the cysteine supply sets how much cluster assembly survives. Cysteine is
    conditionally essential in the newborn because cystathionase activity is low, which
    would make the neonatal period the point of greatest vulnerability and could account
    for two relatives with one genotype and opposite outcomes. The proposal is the
    authors' interpretation; no cysteine or N-acetylcysteine has been given to a patient
    and no plasma cysteine measurement from these patients is published. It matters
    because it is the only therapeutic idea this disease has, and because the same
    argument does not transfer to the late-acting MMDS genes, whose step is not
    sulfur-limited. The hypothesis also has a published objection: the third patient's
    crises began at two years and recurred, which a limitation confined to the newborn
    period does not explain, and her own authors say so.

Combined Oxidative Phosphorylation Deficiency 19 (COXPD19), caused by LYRM4: research report

Scope: This report covers the disease with MONDO:0014269 and OMIM:615595, caused by biallelic variants in LYRM4 (LYR motif-containing 4, also called ISD11; HGNC:21365; gene MIM 613311). 1. Disease information

Overview. COXPD19 is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive mitochondrial disease. Loss of function of ISD11 (encoded by LYRM4), a key part of the mitochondrial iron–sulfur (Fe–S) cluster assembly machinery, causes deficiency of the Fe–S-containing respiratory chain complexes I, II and III. It also affects other mitochondrial and cytosolic Fe–S enzymes such as the aconitases and ferrochelatase. It presents in the neonatal period or early childhood with lactic acidosis and respiratory failure. The outcome ranges from death in infancy to full recovery. - Lim et al., Hum Mol Genet 2013, PMID 23814038: "Here, we report a homozygous mutation in LYRM4 in two patients with combined OXPHOS deficiency." - MONDO definition, via MedGen 816385: "Any combined oxidative phosphorylation deficiency in which the cause of the disease is a mutation in the LYRM4 gene." (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/medgen/816385)

2. Etiology

  • Causal factor: Biallelic (homozygous) loss-of-function missense variants in LYRM4. They impair the NFS1–ISD11 cysteine desulfurase complex, which leads to defective Fe–S cluster biogenesis (PMIDs 23814038, 26342079).
  • Genetic risk factors: Consanguinity. The first family was "an extended consanguineous family" of Lebanese/Syrian ancestry, with first-cousin parents and at least two consanguineous loops (PMID 23814038). The third patient's parents were non-consanguineous Portuguese heterozygous carriers (PMID 31497476).
  • Environmental triggers. None is known as a cause, but intercurrent infection and catabolic stress trigger decompensation. The Coelho patient's first cardiorespiratory arrest followed a viral respiratory infection (RSV in secretions, HHV-6 in CSF). Her second followed four days of fever, cough and poor intake (PMID 31497476). Group B Streptococcus was cultured from the neonatal gastric aspirate of Lim patient P2 (PMID 23814038).
  • Protective factors: None established. Lim et al. propose that adequate cysteine supply may let residual NFS1 activity support viability: "NFS1 has a residual l-cysteine desulfurase activity which is sufficient to retain the viability of the patient" (PMID 23814038). This is a hypothesis and has not been tested.
  • Gene–environment interaction: Infection or fever acting on reduced Fe–S/OXPHOS capacity precipitates crises (PMID 31497476). This is a clinical observation; the mechanism is inferred.
  • Other associations (not COXPD19): Common LYRM4-region variants have been linked to ulcerative colitis susceptibility: "Additional novel UC susceptibility genes were LYRM4 and CDKAL1" (PMID 19068216). LYRM4 promoter polymorphisms have been linked to cognitive deficit in schizophrenia (PMID 21968932). Neither association is shown to bear on COXPD19.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. Homozygous LYRM4 missense variant (p.R68L or p.Y31C) results in a destabilized or poorly interacting ISD11 protein. ISD11 was undetectable in patient muscle and liver (demonstrated for R68L; PMID 23814038). Alanine mutations at ISD11 helix-3 residues including Arg68 "leads to its compromised interaction with NFS1" (PMID 26342079). For Y31C the effect is inferred.
  2. ISD11 loss leads to reduced NFS1 L-cysteine desulfurase activity. The ISD11–ACP pair has "regulatory rather than catalytic roles" (PMID 29097656), and without it NFS1 aggregates or is unstable (yeast, PMID 16341090; human cells, PMID 26342079). With R68L, desulfurase activity was grossly reduced, reaching about 13% of wild type with ISCU and frataxin added (in vitro, PMID 23814038).
  3. Reduced persulfide supply results in impaired de novo [2Fe-2S] assembly on the ISCU scaffold within the NFS1–ISD11–ACP–ISCU–frataxin core complex (structural and biochemical work: PMIDs 27519411, 28634302, 29097656, 26016389). This early-step block affects all downstream Fe–S proteins.
  4. The Fe–S protein deficiency branches into:
  5. 4a. Loss of Fe–S subunits of complex I (NDUFS/NDUFB8), complex II (SDHB) and complex III (Rieske UQCRFS1). These lead to combined CI/CII/CIII deficiency in muscle and liver (patient tissue, PMID 23814038). Complex IV is reduced secondarily in some tissues (P2 muscle 11%; P3 muscle 31%); the mechanism is inferred, possibly supercomplex or heme effects.
  6. 4b. Loss of mitochondrial aconitase (ACO2), which leads to a TCA-cycle block (patient tissue; knockdown cells, PMID 19454487).
  7. 4c. Loss of cytosolic aconitase/IRP1 activity and activation of IRP1 RNA binding, which lead to disordered cellular iron homeostasis. ISD11 knockdown caused "abnormal punctate ferric iron accumulations" (in vitro, PMID 19454487). Mitochondrial iron and ROS accumulated with R68L (PMID 26342079). Patient liver showed mildly increased iron (PMID 23814038).
  8. 4d. Loss of ferrochelatase, an Fe–S enzyme, which was undetectable in P2 muscle and liver; any effect on heme synthesis is inferred.
  9. 4e. Possible lipoate-dependent enzyme effects (PDH, 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase) were not measured. PDH was normal in fibroblasts.
  10. The OXPHOS and TCA block results in a shift to glycolysis, raised NADH/NAD⁺ and lactic acidosis, with high lactate in blood, CSF and urine and on brain MRS. Ketosis and 3-MGA (P3) are downstream markers of mitochondrial dysfunction; their mechanism here is inferred.
  11. Energy failure in high-demand tissues (liver, skeletal and respiratory muscle, brain) leads to hepatopathy and steatosis, hypotonia, respiratory insufficiency and stridor, and, under infection or catabolic stress, acute decompensation (cardiorespiratory arrest, P3). The tissue link is inferred from the biochemistry.
  12. Tissue specificity: Fibroblasts were largely spared (CI–CIV mostly 45–181% of control; MyoD myotubes did not unmask a defect). Muscle and liver were severely affected. This explains why fibroblast testing can miss the diagnosis (PMID 23814038).

Mechanism details - Pathways: Mitochondrial Fe–S cluster assembly (ISC pathway); respiratory electron transport; TCA cycle; cellular iron homeostasis (IRP/IRE). ACP binding ties ISD11 to mitochondrial fatty-acid synthesis: Cory 2017 describes "a lock-and-key model in which LYR proteins associate with acyl-ACP as a mechanism for fatty acid biosynthesis" (PMID 28634302). - Protein structure: "a pair of ISD11 subunits form the dimeric core of the SDA complex" and "the 4'-phosphopantetheine-conjugated acyl-group of ACP occupies the hydrophobic core of ISD11" (PMID 28634302). The N-terminal 10 residues and the LYR motif are needed for mitochondrial targeting and a stable active complex with NFS1 (PMID 28271877). - Other roles of the NFS1–ISD11 complex: Sulfur donation for tRNA thiolation (Trypanosoma, PMID 20442400) and for molybdenum cofactor synthesis via MOCS3 (PMID 18650437). Whether these are disturbed in patients is not known. - Immune involvement: None described. - Omics, single-cell and CRISPR data: None specific to COXPD19. - Suggested GO biological process labels: iron-sulfur cluster assembly; cysteine desulfurase activity (molecular function); mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I assembly; cellular iron ion homeostasis; tRNA thio-modification. - Suggested CL labels: hepatocyte; skeletal muscle fiber (myocyte); neuron.

  • Primary organs: Skeletal muscle and liver, both with severe biochemical deficiency. Respiratory involvement (respiratory musculature and airway, with stridor and ventilatory failure) and brain (CSF and MRS lactate, EEG changes) are also seen.
  • Heart: Clinically spared in the reported patients (normal echo and ECG in P2 and P3).
  • Body systems: Metabolic, respiratory, hepatic/digestive, neuromuscular, nervous.
  • Tissue-level findings: P2 autopsy liver showed micro- and macrovesicular steatosis, nodulation, reduced glycogen and mildly increased iron. Electron microscopy of muscle and liver showed "swollen, pleomorphic mitochondria with sparse cristae" (PMID 23814038).

  • Suggested UBERON labels: skeletal muscle tissue; liver; brain; cerebrospinal fluid; larynx/airway.
  • Lateralization: Not applicable; the disease is systemic.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: Neonatal (day 1 to 8 weeks: P1, P2) or early childhood (2 years: P3). Onset is acute, as a metabolic or respiratory crisis.
  • Course:
  • Episodic crises triggered by infection, with full recovery between episodes (P1, P3).

  • Remission: Spontaneous clinical recovery in two of three patients. P1 was healthy at age 20.
  • Critical periods: The neonatal and infant period and intercurrent infections; this is the window for supportive and metabolic intervention.
  • Staging: None defined.

  • Inheritance: Autosomal recessive (homozygous patients, carrier parents; PMIDs 23814038, 31497476).
  • Prevalence and incidence: Unknown. Three patients have been published; P3 was described as "the third case of LYRM4 deficiency worldwide" (PMID 31497476). No later patient reports were found in PubMed through November 2026.
  • Penetrance and expressivity: Penetrance among homozygotes appears complete for a biochemical phenotype. Expressivity is highly variable, even within one family with the same genotype.

  • Founder effects: None established. One variant per family: R68L in a Lebanese/Syrian family, Y31C in a Portuguese family.

  • Carrier frequency: Unknown; both variants were absent from population databases.
  • Sex: 1 male (P1) and 2 females (P2, P3); too few to judge.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Mortality: 1 of 3 died, at 12 weeks (P2). Survival rates and life expectancy cannot be estimated.
  • Long-term outcome in survivors:
  • P1 had normal neurodevelopment and was healthy at 20 years.
  • P3 was normal at 4 years, with no neurological involvement "so far."
  • Complications:
  • Recurrent lactic acidosis crises.
  • Respiratory failure needing ventilation; P3's first episode needed 5 days of mechanical ventilation.
  • Aspiration; hepatic steatosis; cardiorespiratory arrest.
  • Prognostic factors: None validated. In the reported cases, deeper tissue deficiency (P2: CI–CIII ≤11% in muscle and liver, versus P1's milder liver values) went with death. This is an observation from n=2, not a validated predictor.
  • Quality-of-life measures: No data.

12. Treatment

  • No disease-specific or approved therapy exists, and no clinical trials were found.
  • Supportive and acute care:
  • Ventilatory support and correction of acidosis.
  • IV glucose-containing fluids during catabolic states. Coelho advises caution and notes IV 5% dextrose in saline (PMID 31497476).
  • Antibiotics for infection.
  • Nutrition and anti-reflux care.
  • Avoiding fasting.
  • Cofactors: Oral L-carnitine was given to P3 for low free carnitine and later stopped. No benefit beyond correcting the carnitine level was reported.
  • Proposed but untested: Sulfur-donor supplementation, "providing a sulfur-donor such as cysteine or N-acetyl cysteine to patients" (PMID 23814038). Coelho also raised it but did not give it (PMID 31497476). There are no efficacy data.
  • Gene, cell and RNA therapy; pharmacogenomics: None reported.
  • Suggested NCIT intervention labels: mechanical ventilation; intravenous fluid therapy (dextrose); levocarnitine; acetylcysteine (investigational/hypothetical); supportive care.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: Genetic counseling for families, carrier testing of relatives, and prenatal or preimplantation diagnosis once the familial variants are known. Recurrence risk for carrier couples is 25% per pregnancy (standard autosomal recessive).
  • Secondary: Early metabolic evaluation of infants with unexplained lactic acidosis and stridor or respiratory distress, especially in consanguineous families.
  • Tertiary:
  • Emergency "sick-day" protocols with early IV dextrose during infection or fever, and avoiding catabolism (PMID 31497476).
  • Prompt treatment of infections.
  • Routine immunization, inferred from the infection-triggered crises; no disease-specific vaccine data.
  • Public health and environmental measures: Not applicable.

15. Model organisms

  • Yeast (S. cerevisiae):
  • Isd11 is essential. Its loss blocks Fe–S assembly on Isu, and Nfs1 aggregates without Isd11 (Adam 2006, PMID 16341090; Wiedemann 2006, EMBO J, PMC1356349).
  • In the patient-variant model, isd11Δ cells carrying R71L (the yeast equivalent of R68L) showed reduced fitness on SC, SGal, SAce and YPD media (P<0.02); "the mutant protein only partially rescued the growth defect" (PMID 23814038).
  • This model recapitulates the respiratory and Fe–S defect.
  • Human cell lines:
  • ISD11 RNAi inactivated both aconitases, activated IRP1 and caused iron deposits (PMID 19454487).
  • The R68L mutant in human cells raised mitochondrial iron and ROS and lowered respiratory complex activity (PMID 26342079).
  • E. coli: The iscS-complementation and recombinant-complex system gave about 30% growth recovery with R68L versus about 85% with wild type (PMID 23814038). The NFS1–ISD11–ACP complex co-purifies from E. coli (PMID 28233492).
  • Trypanosoma brucei: Isd11 is essential for cytosolic and mitochondrial tRNA thiolation (PMID 20442400).
  • Drosophila: Fly Nfs1/Isd11/IscU core complex reconstituted biochemically (PMID 29491838). I found no in vivo Isd11 knockdown phenotype.
  • Mouse: No Lyrm4 knockout or knock-in phenotype was retrievable; IMPC and MGI queries returned nothing. This is a gap, and an in vivo mammalian model has not been described in the retrieved literature.
  • Limitations: None of the models reproduces the tissue specificity (muscle and liver affected, fibroblasts spared) or the variable, crisis-driven clinical course.
