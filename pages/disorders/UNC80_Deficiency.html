UNC80 Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0014777 Pathograph 34 Show in embeddings browser Neurodevelopmental Disorder

UNC80 deficiency (IHPRF2; OMIM:616801) is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in UNC80, which encodes an auxiliary subunit of the NALCN sodium-leak channelosome (NALCN-FAM155A-UNC79-UNC80). Affected children present from birth with neonatal hypotonia, failure to thrive and severe intellectual disability; most never speak and never walk independently. Feeding difficulties, strabismus, chronic constipation, sleep disturbance, dyskinetic limb movements, an increased tendency to infections, postnatal growth deficiency and microcephaly are common, and a recognisable facial gestalt (thin or tented upper lip, triangular face) is described. Early death is frequent: five of twelve individuals in the largest reported cohort died between 10 months and 10 years. Mechanistically the disorder is a channelopathy of resting membrane potential rather than of action-potential firing. UNC80 and UNC79 form a pincer-shaped cytosolic heterodimer that tethers to the intracellular loops of the pore-forming NALCN subunit, and this tethering does two separable things: it drives NALCN to the cell surface and to dendrites, and it relieves NALCN's own auto-inhibition. Biallelic UNC80 loss therefore destabilises and mislocalises the channelosome and reduces the voltage-independent sodium leak current that sets the neuronal resting membrane potential, which is measurable as markedly decreased NALCN currents for a patient missense allele. How that single electrophysiological lesion produces the craniofacial gestalt and the growth failure is not established and is curated here as an explicit knowledge gap rather than as a causal chain. Curation scope: IHPRF2 is kept as a standalone entry rather than lumped with IHPRF1 (NALCN), even though the two genes encode subunits of the same channelosome, because the two disorders are separately delineated clinical entities with a documented phenotypic difference - breathing abnormalities were recorded in 5/16 IHPRF1-affected individuals and 0/12 IHPRF2-affected individuals in the one cohort that assessed both against each other. See the entry notes and the lump/split discussion below.

Discussions and Knowledge Gaps

4
Do UNC80 missense and C-terminal truncating alleles act through the same lesion, given that one reduces the NALCN leak current while the other increases it?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN allele_class_mechanism_divergence
Attached to
pathophysiology#Reduced NALCN Sodium Leak Current pathophysiology#Loss of NALCN Surface and Dendritic Targeting
The entry's pivotal node is a reduced sodium leak current, and the only direct measurement of a patient allele supports it: the p.Pro1700Ser missense variant markedly decreased NALCN current in HEK293T cells. But the knock-in mouse carrying a patient-type C-terminal truncation has a leak current significantly larger than wild type in cultured neurons, while dying neonatally and confining UNC80 to the soma. If that result holds for the human truncating alleles, which are the majority of reported variants, then for most patients the operative lesion is compartmental mislocalisation of the channel rather than a loss of whole-cell conductance, and the pathograph's trunk is mis-stated for them. The two measurements are also not comparable: one is a heterologous cell line and the other is mouse primary neurons.

Why do both UNC80 mouse models die within a day of birth from apnea, when affected children survive for years and the largest cohort recorded no breathing abnormality at all?
HUMAN MODEL MISMATCH OPEN mouse_lethality_versus_human_survival
Attached to
phenotypes#Central sleep apnea pathophysiology#Dysregulated Neuronal Resting Membrane Potential
Both the null and the patient-type knock-in mouse have severe neonatal apnea and neither survives past P1, so the available mammalian models represent a lethal respiratory phenotype. In humans the picture is close to the opposite: breathing abnormalities were documented in 5 of 16 IHPRF1 individuals and 0 of 12 IHPRF2 individuals in the one cohort that compared them, and the apnea that is reported in UNC80 disease comes from a single sibling pair. Either the mouse overstates the respiratory consequence of UNC80 loss, or human apnea is underascertained because sleep studies are not routine in this disorder; the cohort authors recommended sleep studies for NALCN variants specifically. This matters for surveillance: whether polysomnography belongs in routine UNC80 follow-up turns on the answer.
Proposed experiments
Prospective polysomnography in a UNC80 cohort
prospective_polysomnography_unc80
Perform sleep studies prospectively on a molecularly confirmed UNC80 cohort rather than relying on clinically prompted investigation, to establish whether central apnea is genuinely rare in humans or is simply not looked for.

The facial gestalt is in the disease name and is used clinically for recognition, yet no mechanism connects it to the channelopathy. Summary

Infantile hypotonia with psychomotor retardation and characteristic facies 2 (IHPRF2) is a rare autosomal-recessive neurodevelopmental disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in UNC80, the gene encoding an essential auxiliary subunit of the NALCN sodium-leak channel complex (NALCN–FAM155A–UNC79–UNC80). Despite the serial "2" in its name, the defining molecular lesion of this disorder is UNC80 dysfunction; the related disorder IHPRF1 (OMIM 615419) is caused by NALCN itself, and the two should not be conflated. IHPRF2 was first delineated by Stray-Pedersen et al. in 2016 (PMID: 26708751), who described four individuals from three unrelated families with homozygous missense or compound-heterozygous truncating UNC80 mutations presenting with persistent hypotonia, encephalopathy, growth failure, and severe intellectual disability.

Mechanistically, the UNC80 protein is a large intracellular scaffold that, together with UNC79, tethers to and activates the pore-forming NALCN channel. NALCN conducts a voltage-independent sodium (Na⁺) leak current that sets the neuronal resting membrane potential and governs basal excitability. Cryo-EM structures show that UNC79–UNC80 form a piler-shaped heterodimer whose interactions both ensure proper NALCN cell-surface localization and relieve NALCN auto-inhibition. Biallelic loss of functional UNC80 destabilizes and mislocalizes the channelosome, reducing the Na⁺ leak current and thereby dysregulating resting membrane potential and neuronal excitability. The downstream clinical result is neonatal hypotonia, severe global developmental delay/intellectual disability, absent speech, feeding difficulties, postnatal growth retardation, a characteristic facial gestalt, and variable epilepsy and central sleep apnea.

There is currently no curative or disease-specific therapy. Management is entirely supportive and multidisciplinary (physical, occupational, and speech therapy; nutritional support including assisted feeding; seizure management; respiratory monitoring). Diagnosis relies on exome or genome sequencing, with chromosomal microarray needed to detect intragenic copy-number variants that can serve as one of the two causative alleles. This report consolidates six confirmed findings, 16 reviewed papers, and verified cross-references into a complete disease knowledge-base entry.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — IHPRF2 is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in UNC80 (autosomal recessive)

IHPRF2 (MONDO:0014777) results from biallelic (homozygous or compound-heterozygous) loss-of-function variants in UNC80, inherited in an autosomal-recessive pattern. The disorder was first described by Stray-Pedersen et al. (2016), who reported four individuals from three unrelated families carrying either homozygous missense or compound-heterozygous truncating UNC80 mutations. As the authors stated: "We report four individuals from three unrelated families who have homozygous missense or compound heterozygous truncating mutations in UNC80 and persistent hypotonia, encephalopathy, growth failure, and severe intellectual disability" (PMID: 26708751).

Critically, the same study provided functional validation: "HEK293T cells transfected with an expression plasmid containing the c.5098C>T (p.Pro1700Ser) UNC80 mutation found in one individual showed markedly decreased NALCN channel currents" — direct proof that a disease UNC80 variant reduces NALCN current, i.e., a loss-of-function mechanism.

Finding 2 — UNC80 is an auxiliary subunit of the NALCN sodium-leak channelosome; the mechanism runs from loss of Na⁺ leak current to neuronal hypoexcitability

UNC80 is a large auxiliary protein of the NALCN channelosome (NALCN–FAM155A–UNC79–UNC80), which conducts a voltage-independent Na⁺ leak current that sets resting membrane potential and neuronal excitability. As summarized by Bramswig et al. (2018): "NALCN is a conserved cation channel, which conducts a permanent sodium leak current and regulates resting membrane potential and neuronal excitability" (PMID: 30167850).

The cryo-EM structure of the complex (Kang & Chen 2022) resolves how UNC80 contributes: UNC79 and UNC80 form a piler-shaped heterodimer tethered to the NALCN intracellular side via two essential interactions. One interaction ensures proper NALCN cell-surface localization; the other activates the channel — "The other interaction relieves the self-inhibition of NALCN by pulling the auto-inhibitory CTD Interacting Helix (CIH) out of its binding site" (PMID: 35550517).

Wie et al. (2020) demonstrated the functional consequence of patient-type truncations: the UNC80 C-terminus interacts with UNC79 and overcomes a soma-retention signal to direct the complex to dendrites. "UNC80 lacking this domain, as found in human patients, still supports whole-cell NALCN currents but lacks dendritic localization" (PMID: 32620897). Thus, even truncations that preserve some whole-cell current disrupt the proper subcellular distribution of the channel — a disease-relevant mechanism beyond simple current loss. The same study notes that UNC80 knockout mice are neonatal lethal, underscoring the essential nature of this subunit.

Finding 3 — Clinical phenotype: neonatal hypotonia, severe global developmental delay/ID, absent speech, feeding difficulties, growth retardation, characteristic facies, with variable epilepsy and central sleep apnea

The IHPRF2 phenotype centers on a constellation of neonatal/infantile neurological features. Core features established by Stray-Pedersen (2016) are persistent hypotonia, encephalopathy, growth failure, and severe intellectual disability. He et al. (2018) reported that both of their patients had developmental delay/intellectual disability and hypotonia, with variable additional features (epilepsy, hyperactivity, postnatal growth retardation, self-injurious behavior). They defined the facial gestalt: "Common facial dysmorphisms include a thin upper lip, a tented upper lip, a triangular face, strabismus and microcephaly" (PMID: 29572195). They also noted diagnostic overlap: "To some extent, the manifestations of IHPRF2 mimic those of Angelman syndrome (AS)-like syndromes."

Benvenuto 2026 provided a detailed modern phenotype description in two affected siblings: "both presenting with neonatal hypotonia, severe global developmental delay, major feeding difficulties with the need for assisted nutrition, inability to speak and walk autonomously, epilepsy, central sleep apnea, and facial dysmorphism" (PMID: 40801661).

Finding 4 — Cross-species conservation: UNC80 is an essential, conserved NALCN channelosome subunit; Unc80-null mice are neonatal lethal

The NALCN–UNC79–UNC80 module is deeply conserved. In Drosophila, loss-of-function of unc80 (CG18437) or unc79 produces circadian locomotor defects indistinguishable from mutants of narrow abdomen (na, the NALCN ortholog): "These mutants display severe defects in circadian locomotor rhythmicity that are indistinguishable from na mutant phenotypes" (Lear et al. 2013, PMID: 24223770). The same study established the interdependent stability of the three subunits: "loss of na, unc79, or unc80 gene function leads to decreased expression of all three proteins, with minimal effect on transcript levels" — a post-transcriptional co-dependence that explains why loss of any one subunit cripples the whole complex.

In mammals, UNC80 knockout mice are neonatal lethal (PMID: 32620897), and invertebrate/mammalian models implicate the channelosome in locomotor behavior, anesthetic sensitivity, and respiratory rhythms (Cochet-Bissuel 2014, PMID: 24904279).

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function variants in UNC80 (missense, nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, or intragenic CNV on both alleles) lead to absent or non-functional UNC80 protein. (Demonstrated: multiple families; functional assay for p.Pro1700Ser.)
  2. Loss of functional UNC80 results in destabilization of the NALCN channelosome, because UNC79/UNC80/NALCN are mutually dependent for protein stability. (Demonstrated in Drosophila; inferred in human.)
  3. Loss of UNC80's two tethering interactions with NALCN leads to (a) impaired NALCN cell-surface/dendritic localization and (b) failure to relieve NALCN auto-inhibition (CIH not extracted). (Demonstrated by cryo-EM and cell biology.)
  4. These defects result in a reduced voltage-independent Na⁺ leak current through NALCN. (Demonstrated: decreased NALCN currents in HEK293T with patient variant.)
  5. Reduced Na⁺ leak current leads to a more hyperpolarized/dysregulated neuronal resting membrane potential and altered basal excitability across central neurons. (Inferred from channel physiology.)
  6. Dysregulated excitability in brainstem, cortical, and motor circuits results in the clinical phenotype, branching into:
  7. → Motor circuits / muscle tone regulation → neonatal hypotonia, motor delay, inability to walk.
  8. → Cognitive/language networks → severe intellectual disability, absent speech.
  9. → Brainstem respiratory rhythm generators → central sleep apnea.
  10. → Cortical networks → epilepsy (variable).
  11. → Hypothalamic/growth and feeding circuits → feeding difficulties, growth retardation.
  12. → Craniofacial development (mechanism less defined) → characteristic facies, microcephaly. (Largely inferred; developmental contribution of NALCN signaling not fully established.)

Section-by-Section Knowledge-Base Content

1. Disease Information

A rare autosomal-recessive neurodevelopmental/neuromuscular disorder of infancy. Identifiers: MONDO:0014777, OMIM:616801, Orphanet ORPHA:700333, GARD 0018458, MedGen/UMLS C4225203. No specific ICD-10 code; grouped under congenital hypotonia / neurodevelopmental disorders (ICD-10 ~P94.2 / ICD-11 structural analogs). Synonyms: IHPRF2; UNC80 hypotonia with psychomotor retardation and characteristic facies. Information is derived from aggregated disease-level resources (OMIM/Orphanet) plus a small number of individual-patient case reports (<30 reported patients worldwide).

2. Etiology

Primary cause: genetic — biallelic loss-of-function variants in UNC80. No environmental, infectious, or toxic cause. Genetic risk factors: consanguinity markedly raises the risk of homozygous UNC80 variants; being a heterozygous carrier plus a reproductive partner who is also a carrier. Uniparental disomy of chromosome 2 is a rare route to homozygosity (PMID: 34594366). No established environmental risk or protective factors. No known modifier genes or protective alleles. Gene–environment interactions: none established.

3. Phenotypes

See the HPO table in Finding 3. Phenotype types span clinical signs (hypotonia, dysmorphism), developmental/behavioral (ID, absent speech, hyperactivity, self-injury), and functional (feeding difficulty, apnea, seizures). Onset is neonatal/infantile; severity is generally severe; course is chronic and largely static-to-slowly-evolving (not neurodegenerative in the classic sense). Quality-of-life impact is profound: most patients are non-verbal, non-ambulatory, and dependent for daily care, frequently requiring assisted nutrition.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene UNC80 (HGNC:26582; Ensembl ENSG00000144406; OMIM 612636; chr2q34). Variant types reported: missense, nonsense, frameshift (small indels), canonical splice-site, and multi-exon intragenic deletions (CNVs). ClinVar: ~285 pathogenic / 87 likely pathogenic / ~2,644 VUS. All pathogenic alleles are germline; biallelic. Functional consequence: loss of function (reduced NALCN current; mislocalization). gnomAD constraint (Finding 5) shows heterozygous LoF tolerated (pLI≈0), missense-constrained (Z=5.39). No proven modifier genes or disease-specific epigenetic changes.

5. Environmental Information

Not applicable — IHPRF2 is a monogenic Mendelian disorder with no known environmental, lifestyle, or infectious contributors.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

See the ordered causal chain and diagram above. Core defect: reduced NALCN-mediated Na⁺ leak conductance due to loss of the UNC80 scaffold. Molecular "pathway" is ion-channel/membrane-potential regulation rather than a classical signaling cascade. No metabolic, immune, or fibrotic mechanisms are implicated.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

Primary: central nervous system (brain — cortex, brainstem, cerebellum; UBERON:0000955). Secondary: skeletal muscle (hypotonia, secondary), craniofacial structures (dysmorphism), GI/feeding apparatus (functional). Bilateral/diffuse. Subcellular: plasma membrane, dendrites, neuronal cell body (GO:0005886, GO:0030425, GO:0043025). Cell types: central neurons including pacemaker/rhythm-generating neurons (CL:0000540).

8. Temporal Development

Onset: congenital/neonatal (hypotonia present from birth). Pattern: chronic, lifelong. Course: severe static-to-slowly-evolving encephalopathy; developmental milestones severely delayed or never achieved. No remission; epilepsy and apnea may fluctuate. Critical period for intervention is early-childhood supportive/rehabilitative care.

9. Inheritance and Population

Autosomal recessive. Penetrance appears complete for biallelic LoF; expressivity is variable (epilepsy, apnea, growth, behavior differ between patients). No anticipation (not a repeat-expansion disorder). Consanguinity is an important contributor. Carrier frequency is low but not precisely defined; heterozygous LoF alleles are present in gnomAD at low frequency. No strong founder effect established. Prevalence: ultra-rare (<1/1,000,000; fewer than ~30 published patients). Sex ratio ~1:1 (autosomal). Reported across diverse populations (European, Chinese, Thai, Palestinian-Emirati, Italian).

10. Diagnostics

Primary diagnostic modality: molecular genetic testing — trio whole-exome sequencing (WES) or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) is the mainstay (multiple cases diagnosed by WES). Chromosomal microarray (CMA) is essential to detect intragenic CNVs that form one of the two alleles (PMID: 40801661). Gene panels for intellectual disability/hypotonia/neurodevelopmental disorders typically include UNC80. Supportive: brain MRI (variable/nonspecific findings), EEG (if seizures), metabolic workup to exclude mimics. No specific biomarker exists. Differential diagnosis: IHPRF1 (NALCN), PURA-related NDD (PMID: 33229923), Angelman syndrome and AS-like disorders (PMID: 29572195), Prader-Willi syndrome, and other congenital hypotonia/ID syndromes.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Severe lifelong morbidity: most patients are non-ambulatory and non-verbal with profound intellectual disability and dependence for care. Complications include feeding failure (requiring gastrostomy/assisted nutrition), aspiration, epilepsy, and central sleep apnea with attendant respiratory risk. Life expectancy data are limited given rarity; early mortality risk exists (consistent with neonatal lethality in mouse KO), but survival into childhood/adulthood with supportive care is reported. Prognostic factors likely include presence/severity of apnea, seizures, and feeding failure. No validated prognostic biomarkers.

12. Treatment

No curative or disease-specific therapy. Management is entirely supportive and multidisciplinary (NCIT concepts: Supportive Care, Physical Therapy, Occupational Therapy, Speech Therapy, Nutritional Support, Anticonvulsant Therapy): - Rehabilitation: physical, occupational, and speech/communication therapy. - Nutrition: feeding support, nasogastric/gastrostomy tube as needed. - Seizures: standard antiseizure medications tailored to seizure type. - Respiratory: monitoring and management of central sleep apnea. - Supportive/symptomatic care for behavior, orthopedics, and vision (strabismus).

No approved pharmacotherapy, gene therapy, RNA-based, or cell therapy exists; none are in clinical trials specific to IHPRF2. Pharmacogenomic considerations are generic. Personalized/precision approaches are conceptual only.

13. Prevention

No primary prevention (genetic disorder). Secondary prevention via genetic counseling and carrier/cascade testing in families and consanguineous couples; prenatal diagnosis and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M) are feasible once the familial biallelic variants are known. Recurrence risk for carrier couples is 25% per pregnancy. Tertiary prevention = management of complications (apnea monitoring, seizure control, nutrition, aspiration prevention). Not amenable to immunization or public-health environmental intervention.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

UNC80 orthologs are conserved and functionally essential: Drosophila unc80 (CG18437), C. elegans unc-80, and mouse Unc80 (Mus musculus). Loss phenocopies NALCN loss across species (PMID: 24223770). No naturally occurring companion-animal/wildlife disease is cataloged in OMIA for UNC80 at present. No zoonotic potential (non-infectious). Evolutionary conservation of the NALCN–UNC79–UNC80 module is strong.

15. Model Organisms

  • Mouse (Mus musculus): Unc80 knockout — neonatal lethal (PMID: 32620897); recapitulates the essentiality of the channelosome but limits adult-phenotype study; conditional/hypomorphic models would be needed to model viable human disease.
  • Drosophila melanogaster: unc80 LoF — circadian locomotor rhythm defects identical to na (NALCN) mutants; demonstrates conserved function and subunit co-stability (PMID: 24223770).
  • C. elegans: unc-80 mutants — classical uncoordinated locomotion; historical basis of the gene name.
  • In vitro: HEK293T heterologous expression of NALCN with UNC79/UNC80/FAM155A — used to show reduced NALCN current for patient variant p.Pro1700Ser (PMID: 26708751) and dendritic-localization assays in neurons (PMID: 32620897).

Model limitations: neonatal lethality of the mouse KO prevents modeling the chronic human course; invertebrate models capture channel physiology/behavior but not facial/cognitive features. iPSC-derived neuron/organoid models are a logical but not-yet-reported avenue.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Small sample size. Fewer than ~30 patients are published; prevalence, penetrance, expressivity, and natural-history/survival estimates are correspondingly imprecise.
  2. Genotype–phenotype correlation is unresolved. It is unclear whether missense vs truncating vs CNV alleles, or residual-function alleles, predict severity (e.g., presence of apnea or epilepsy).
  3. Facial/craniofacial and growth mechanisms are inferred. How a Na⁺-leak channelopathy produces the characteristic facies, microcephaly, and growth retardation is not mechanistically demonstrated.
  4. No viable mammalian disease model. Unc80-null mice are neonatal lethal; no conditional/hypomorphic or humanized model recapitulating the chronic human phenotype has been reported.
  5. Large VUS burden. ~2,644 UNC80 VUS in ClinVar complicate interpretation of novel variants; functional assays are not routine.
  6. No therapeutics. There are no disease-modifying treatments, biomarkers, or clinical trials specific to IHPRF2.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Build a patient registry / natural-history study to quantify prevalence, survival, and the frequency and age-dependence of epilepsy, central sleep apnea, and feeding failure.
  2. Develop conditional/hypomorphic or humanized Unc80 mouse models and patient-iPSC-derived neurons/organoids to model the chronic disease and test interventions.
  3. Systematic genotype–phenotype analysis across all reported variants (including CNVs) to test whether allele type predicts apnea, epilepsy, or motor outcome.
  4. High-throughput functional assays (e.g., automated NALCN-current and dendritic-localization readouts) to reclassify UNC80 VUS per ACMG/AMP criteria.
  5. Mechanistic studies of craniofacial and growth phenotypes to determine whether they arise from the same channelopathy or a developmental role of the channelosome.
  6. Explore pharmacological modulation of NALCN/channelosome (e.g., agents that enhance residual leak conductance) as a conceptual therapeutic strategy, and routinely include CMA in diagnostic workups to capture intragenic deletions.
