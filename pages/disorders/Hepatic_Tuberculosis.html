Hepatic Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0005787 Pathograph 37 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Extrapulmonary Tuberculosis Abdominal Tuberculosis

Hepatic tuberculosis is infection of the liver by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It takes two main forms. In the miliary form, about four in five reported cases, bacilli from a pulmonary focus reach the liver through the hepatic artery during disseminated disease and seed it diffusely with small tubercles. In the local form, bacilli reach the liver mainly through the portal vein from a gastrointestinal focus and produce larger nodules near the portal triads, which can coalesce into tuberculomas (pseudotumours) or liquefy into tuberculous liver abscesses. A tubular or biliary form, in which granulomas or enlarged porta hepatis lymph nodes involve the bile ducts, causes strictures and obstructive jaundice. In every form the liver responds with epithelioid granulomas, typically caseating. Presentation is nonspecific: hepatomegaly, fever, abdominal pain, weight loss and an infiltrative liver test pattern with a disproportionately raised alkaline phosphatase. Jaundice suggests biliary or nodal involvement. Imaging (ultrasound, and contrast-enhanced CT as the most sensitive test) shows micronodules, hepatomegaly, solitary or few low-density nodules with calcification, abscesses or hilar strictures, and local disease is often mistaken for a liver tumour. Diagnosis rests on liver biopsy with histology, acid-fast staining, mycobacterial culture and PCR. Treatment is standard multidrug antituberculous chemotherapy, with drainage or resection for some abscesses and tuberculomas and biliary decompression for obstructive jaundice. Isoniazid, rifampicin and pyrazinamide are themselves hepatotoxic, so drug-induced liver injury must be watched for in a liver already involved by disease.

Miliary (diffuse) hepatic tuberculosis
FREQUENT
Hepatic involvement in disseminated tuberculosis, about four in five reported cases. Bacilli from a pulmonary focus reach the liver through the hepatic artery and seed the lobules with tubercles of 0.6 to 2 mm. Respiratory symptoms are more common, CT shows dispersed low-density micronodules or hepatomegaly alone, and liver biopsy is less helpful than in local disease. Massive miliary seeding can cause acute liver failure.

Local hepatic tuberculosis (tuberculoma, pseudotumour or abscess)
OCCASIONAL
Isolated or predominant liver disease, about one in five reported cases, usually without active pulmonary disease. Bacilli reach the liver mainly through the portal vein from a gastrointestinal focus and form tubercles larger than 2 mm near the portal triads, which coalesce into tuberculomas or liquefy into abscesses. CT shows one or a few large low-density nodules with calcification and peripheral enhancement, the lesion is often taken for a liver tumour, liver biopsy is more helpful, and abscess drainage or resection is considered in addition to chemotherapy. Tuberculous abscesses respond less well to chemotherapy.

Tubular (biliary) hepatic tuberculosis
Involvement of the bile ducts, by tuberculous cholangitis and pericholangitis, by granulomas rupturing into the ducts, or more often by enlarged porta hepatis lymph nodes compressing them. It presents with obstructive jaundice and hilar or common hepatic duct strictures that mimic cholangiocarcinoma or pancreatic cancer, and it needs biliary decompression by stent or percutaneous drainage as well as chemotherapy. In one prospective series of 38 patients with hepatobiliary tuberculosis, 15 had biliary disease, 12 of them from lymph node masses.

Hepatic Epithelioid Granuloma Formation
Granulomas of epithelioid cells, with or without Langhans multinucleated giant cells, surrounded by lymphocytes form in the liver, most often in the periportal areas. They are typically 1 to 2 mm and are found in 80 to 100% of liver biopsies. In advanced HIV infection they may be absent or poorly formed and lack necrosis. Diffuse granulomatous infiltration enlarges the liver and produces an infiltrative liver test pattern.

Caseous Necrosis and Coalescence into Tuberculomas
Caseating granulomas coalesce into tuberculomas, nodules of 1 to 4 cm and occasionally up to 12 cm, which become encapsulated and calcify. The liver lacks cavities to drain necrotic material, and drugs penetrate the necrotic centre poorly, which may slow healing. Healing of many tubercles by scarring can leave portal fibrosis or pseudocirrhosis.

Isoniazid, rifampicin and pyrazinamide are hepatotoxic, and drug-induced liver injury is the commonest serious adverse effect of standard therapy, reported in 2 to 28% of treated tuberculosis patients. description: >-
  Hepatic tuberculosis is infection of the liver by Mycobacterium
  tuberculosis. It takes two main forms. In the miliary form, about four in
  five reported cases, bacilli from a pulmonary focus reach the liver through
  the hepatic artery during disseminated disease and seed it diffusely with
  small tubercles. In the local form, bacilli reach the liver mainly through
  the portal vein from a gastrointestinal focus and produce larger nodules
  near the portal triads, which can coalesce into tuberculomas (pseudotumours)
  or liquefy into tuberculous liver abscesses. A tubular or biliary form,
  in which granulomas or enlarged porta hepatis lymph nodes involve the bile
  ducts, causes strictures and obstructive jaundice. In every form the liver
  responds with epithelioid granulomas, typically caseating. Presentation is
  nonspecific: hepatomegaly, fever, abdominal pain, weight loss and an
  infiltrative liver test pattern with a disproportionately raised alkaline
  phosphatase. Jaundice suggests biliary or nodal involvement. Imaging
  (ultrasound, and contrast-enhanced CT as the most sensitive test) shows
  micronodules, hepatomegaly, solitary or few low-density nodules with
  calcification, abscesses or hilar strictures, and local disease is often
  mistaken for a liver tumour. Diagnosis rests on liver biopsy with
  histology, acid-fast staining, mycobacterial culture and PCR. Treatment is
  standard multidrug antituberculous chemotherapy, with drainage or resection
  for some abscesses and tuberculomas and biliary decompression for
  obstructive jaundice. Isoniazid, rifampicin and pyrazinamide are
  themselves hepatotoxic, so drug-induced liver injury must be watched for in
  a liver already involved by disease.

- name: Standard multidrug antituberculous chemotherapy
  description: >-
    Four-drug therapy with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol

    isoniazid, rifampicin and pyrazinamide are hepatotoxic, pre-existing

    separate from hepatic tuberculosis itself or from immune reconstitution
    in HIV. ALT should be monitored in patients with liver disease or HIV,

    three times the upper limit of normal with symptoms or jaundice, or five

1. Disease Information

Overview. Mondo defines hepatic TB as "infection of the liver with species of mycobacterium" (most often M. tuberculosis), "characterized by localized small tuberculous miliary lesions or tumor-like mass (tuberculoma)" with abnormal liver function tests (Mondo record via SAIL concept library).

There are two forms: - Miliary (disseminated): part of generalized haematogenous TB. This is about 79% of reported cases. - Local (isolated): about 21% of reported cases. It includes nodular tuberculoma or abscess and the tubular/ductal (cholangitic) form.

Rolleston and McNee first described this miliary/local split in 1905 (Hickey et al. 2015, PMID 25943103, PMC4425874). Hepatic TB accounts for under 1% of all TB infections and is "likely underreported" (PMID 25943103).

2. Etiology

Cause. The cause is infection of the liver by M. tuberculosis. Most cases arise from haematogenous dissemination: "Approximately 79% of all reported hepatic TB cases were due to systemic dissemination of bacilli" (PMID 25943103). Local disease is thought to follow portal-venous spread from a gut focus. This is a mechanistic inference repeated in reviews (Chaudhary 2014, PMID 24976240, PMC4073015).

Risk factors (human clinical evidence) - HIV and severe immunosuppression: - Hepatic involvement occurred in 17.4% of HIV-infected vs 4.3% of HIV-uninfected people with disseminated TB (summarised in PMID 25943103). - A South African series of 20 HIV-positive adults with hepatic TB had a median CD4 count of 47 cells/µL. In that series "hepatic TB occurred in patients with severe immunosuppression" (Gounder 2017, PMID 28148232). - "Hepatic TB has become more prevalent, likely as a result of the… HIV/AIDS epidemic" (PMID 25943103). - Solid-organ transplantation: - The prevalence of active TB in liver-transplant recipients was 1.3% (Holty 2009, PMID 19642133). - Hepatic TB after renal transplant has ended in fatal multi-organ failure (Wu 2013, PMID 24179582). - Isolated hepatic TB has been reported in a transplanted liver (PMID 15522111). - Population-level drivers of TB in general (WHO Global TB Report 2024, 2023 data): estimated attributable incident TB cases were undernutrition 0.96 M, alcohol use disorders 0.75 M, smoking 0.70 M, HIV 0.61 M and diabetes 0.38 M (WHO GTB 2024). These figures apply to TB overall, not specifically to hepatic TB. - Co-existing liver disease: found in 37% of patients in one prospective series (Maharaj 1987, PMID 3659266).

Host genetic susceptibility (TB in general; nothing hepatic-specific was found) - Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD): "caused by inborn errors of IFN-γ immunity" (Rosain/Bustamante 2018, PMID 30264912). The genes involved include IL12RB1, IL12B, IFNGR1/2, STAT1, TYK2, ISG15 and SPPL2A, among others. - TYK2 P1104A homozygosity: - This common allele "found in about 1/600 Europeans" impairs IL-23-dependent IFN-γ immunity and is enriched in TB patients (Boisson-Dupuis 2018, PMID 30578352). - In the UK Biobank, 6 of 620 TB patients (1%) were homozygous vs 0.2% of controls, an odds ratio of about 5.0 (95% CI 1.96–10.31) (Kerner 2019, PMID 31068474). - Penetrance is incomplete. - VDR FokI polymorphism: a meta-analysis reported an association overall and in Asian subgroups, but not in the allele-contrast model (Yadav 2021, PMID 33901685). Evidence is modest.

Protective factors: - Treating HIV with antiretroviral therapy (ART) to restore immunity. - Avoiding immunosuppression where possible. - TB preventive therapy (see §13).

No hepatic-specific protective variants have been reported. No BCG meta-analysis for hepatic TB was retrieved; the only hit was an irrelevant COVID-19 preprint.

Gene–environment interactions: Host IFN-γ/IL-12/IL-23 pathway defects matter only when there is exposure to mycobacteria. NAT2 acetylator genotype interacts with isoniazid to cause drug-induced liver injury (see §12). No hepatic-TB-specific interaction data were found.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Inhaled M. tuberculosis infects alveolar macrophages. This leads to a primary pulmonary focus and bacteraemia (established TB biology; not retrieved in this session for the liver specifically).
  2. Branch A (miliary, about 79%): haematogenous dissemination through the hepatic artery leads to bacilli reaching the liver sinusoids (PMID 25943103). Branch B (local): portal-venous seeding from a gut or abdominal focus leads to focal disease. This route is inferred in reviews (PMID 24976240).
  3. Kupffer cell capture (demonstrated in mice with BCG): "Kupffer cells directly capture blood-borne bacteria and subsequently nucleate formation of a nascent granuloma" (Egen 2008, PMID 18261937).
  4. Growth is restricted to professional phagocytes. This results in a low bacillary burden: "Among sites of extrapulmonary growth of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the liver is the least infected," which the authors attribute to "complete restriction of mycobacterial growth to liver macrophages" (Seiler 2001, PMID 11705978, mouse). This may explain why acid-fast bacilli (AFB) are rarely seen in human liver tissue, though that link is an inference.
  5. Recruited monocytes and macrophages, together with effector T cells (IL-12/IL-23 → IFN-γ axis), lead to an organised epithelioid granuloma with Langhans giant cells.
  6. TNF signalling maintains the granuloma structure (PMID 18261937).
  7. Inborn errors of this axis predispose to disseminated disease (PMID 30264912; PMID 30578352).
  8. Innate macrophage–mycobacterium interactions alone can initiate granulomas (zebrafish/M. marinum, Davis 2002, PMID 12479816).
  9. Central caseous necrosis develops, and granulomas coalesce. This results in macronodules (tuberculoma, 1–4 cm) or tuberculous abscess.
  10. In the ductal form, caseous material erodes into bile ducts, causing cholangitis and stricture. This step is inferred from imaging and case reports (PMID 26499189; PMID 3421231).
  11. The consequences then branch:
  12. Diffuse granulomatous infiltration leads to hepatomegaly and a cholestatic enzyme pattern (disproportionately high ALP).
  13. Biliary or hilar node compression leads to obstructive jaundice.
  14. Healed lesions lead to calcification.
  15. Rarely, portal vein thrombosis leads to portal hypertension (PMID 16026440).
  16. Severe immunosuppression (HIV) results in poorly formed granulomas, greater dissemination and high mortality (PMID 28148232; this mechanism is inferred).

Checklist summary - Pathways: IL-12/IFN-γ; IL-23/TYK2; TNF signalling. - Suggested GO process labels: granuloma formation; macrophage activation; response to interferon-gamma; tumor necrosis factor-mediated signaling pathway; phagocytosis. - Suggested CL labels: Kupffer cell; macrophage; epithelioid cell; multinucleate giant cell (Langhans); T cell (CD4-positive, alpha-beta); monocyte; hepatocyte (bystander). - Tissue damage: caseous necrosis, fibrosis, fatty change and portal fibrosis (PMID 7744492). - Omics: no hepatic-TB transcriptomic, proteomic, single-cell or CRISPR-screen data were found.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: liver (UBERON label "liver"). Within the liver: the hepatic sinusoids and parenchyma (Yu 2004 found the parenchymal type in 10 of 12 cases, PMID 15162540), and the biliary tree ("intrahepatic bile duct").
  • Other sites: hilar or porta hepatis lymph nodes, spleen, peritoneum (ascites), lung (concomitant disease in about 66–75%), and portal vein.
  • Systems: digestive (hepatobiliary), respiratory, immune.
  • Subcellular level: bacilli sit in macrophage phagosomes. Suggested GO cellular component label: "phagocytic vesicle".
  • Laterality: not applicable. Distribution is diffuse (miliary) or focal (often the right lobe in case reports; not quantified).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: adults; subacute or insidious. Diagnosis was suspected clinically in only 47% of cases (Essop 1984).
  • Course: progressive if untreated. Lesions resolve with therapy: "Complete resolution of liver lesions on treatment with 4-drug anti-tuberculosis drug chemotherapy" (Desai 2006, PMID 16531964). There was no recurrence over 48–120 months in 4 of 5 patients (PMID 24179582).
  • Staging: there is no formal staging system and "no standard classification system" (Kakkar 2015, PMID 26499189). Morphologic patterns are miliary, macronodular/tuberculoma, abscess, serohepatic and tubular/cholangitic.
  • Remission: treatment-induced only. Spontaneous remission was not documented.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: not applicable, because the disease is infectious. Rare host-susceptibility conditions in the MSMD group are mostly autosomal recessive or dominant with incomplete penetrance (PMID 30264912).
  • Frequency within TB:
  • Hepatic TB was found in 1.2% (96/8,342) of active-TB inpatients in a South African hospital, 1978–1984 (Essop 1984, Q J Med 53:465; summarised in PMID 25943103).
  • Liver TB histology was found in 37.3% of HIV/TB deaths in a Nigerian autopsy series (PMID 25943103).
  • True incidence "remains unknown".
  • Background TB burden (WHO 2024, 2023 data):
  • 10.8 million incident cases (95% UI 10.1–11.7 M), or 134 per 100,000.
  • 1.25 million deaths.
  • 6.1% of cases in people living with HIV.
  • 30 countries carry 87% of the burden (WHO GTB 2024; CIDRAP).
  • WHO notes the 2024 estimates have been superseded by the 2025 report.
  • Geography: cases are reported from endemic regions, including South Africa, India, the Philippines and East Asia (PMID 25943103).
  • Sex ratio and age distribution: not available in the retrieved abstracts.

10. Diagnostics

Laboratory: - The pattern is cholestatic: ALP and GGT are raised more than transaminases. A disproportionately raised ALP suggests infiltration (PMID 24976240). - ESR is raised; albumin may be low.

Histopathology (the most specific test): - Caseating granulomas: median sensitivity 68% (PMID 25943103); 51% in Maharaj 1987; 83% in Essop 1984. - Epithelioid granulomas: 80–100% (PMID 24976240). - Liver biopsy was "the most useful aid to correct diagnosis" (Essop 1984).

Microbiology: - AFB smear: median sensitivity 25% (0–59). Maharaj 1987 found AFB in 59%. - Liver-tissue culture: 30% (6/20) in HIV-positive patients (PMID 28148232). - PCR on liver tissue: median sensitivity 86% (30–100) (PMID 25943103). PCR was positive in 5 of 5 nodular cases (Huang 2003, PMID 14600128). - Metagenomic NGS: shown in a single case (PMID 29759945).

Imaging: - Abnormality rates: ultrasound 76%, CT 88%, chest radiograph 42.5% (medians, PMID 25943103). - CT is the most sensitive modality. Lesions appear as hypodense nodules with rim enhancement and a "cluster" sign (PMID 8419431). - Biopsy combined with nucleic acid testing is the most specific approach (PMID 25943103). - Image-guided fine-needle aspiration is "the best diagnostic method" (PMID 24179582). - FDG-PET can show a hepatic "superscan" (PMID 24347039). - Local hepatic TB often mimics cancer, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and cholangiocarcinoma (PMID 33031307; 26236700; 34687434). All 5 nodular cases in Huang 2003 were first thought to be malignant.

Differential diagnosis: - Sarcoidosis and other granulomatous hepatitis (drug-induced, fungal, brucellosis, Q fever, PBC). - HCC, cholangiocarcinoma or Klatskin tumour, and metastases. - Pyogenic or amoebic abscess. - Lymphoma.

Genetic and omics testing: not applicable to diagnosis. Consider MSMD gene panel or exome testing for unusually severe or familial disseminated mycobacterial disease (PMID 30264912).

Screening: no hepatic-specific screening exists. Standard TB/LTBI screening is used, for example in transplant candidates.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality:
  • HIV-uninfected patients: case fatality 12–42% (PMID 25943103).
  • Untreated patients: 95% (35/37) died in one series.
  • HIV-positive patients: 30% (6/20) died within one year, and "the 12-month mortality remained high" (PMID 28148232).
  • Liver-transplant recipients with TB: short-term mortality 31% (PMID 19642133).
  • Prognostic factors: HIV and immunosuppression, delayed diagnosis, and regimens without rifampicin (Essop: 0% mortality with rifampicin vs 17–50% without; PMID 24976240). Miliary and local forms have similar presentations (PMID 7744492).
  • Complications: obstructive jaundice, portal hypertension or portal vein thrombosis, abscess rupture, multi-organ failure, and drug-induced liver injury.
  • Recovery: good with therapy in drug-susceptible disease. Standard 4-drug therapy for 6–12 months gave "positive outcomes" in nearly all drug-susceptible patients (PMID 25943103).
  • Quality of life and disability data: none found.

12. Treatment

First-line drugs (suggested NCIT labels: Isoniazid, Rifampin, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol; ChEBI labels likewise): - Intensive phase: isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol. Typical doses are 5, 10, 30 and 20 mg/kg/day for 2 months. - Continuation phase: isoniazid and rifampicin, totalling 6–12 months (PMID 24976240; PMID 25943103).

Short regimen: - WHO conditionally recommends 2HPMZ/2HPM (rifapentine, isoniazid, moxifloxacin, pyrazinamide) for people aged 12 and over. The recommendation "also applies to extrapulmonary TB – except TB of the central nervous system, bone or joint" (WHO Module 4). - The supporting trial is Dorman 2021, NEJM, PMID 33951360: "Rifapentine with moxifloxacin was noninferior to the control." - The operational handbook may exclude disseminated or abdominal TB, so check the handbook before applying this regimen to hepatic TB.

Interventional: - Abscesses: percutaneous or EUS-guided drainage (Itoi 2011, PMID 21535224). - Obstructive jaundice: biliary decompression (endoscopic stenting). - Uncertain masses: surgical resection or laparotomy. Laparotomy was needed in 19 of 23 nodular cases historically (Oliva 1990, PMID 2157746).

Adverse effects: drug-induced liver injury is the main concern and is especially hard to separate from hepatic TB itself. The ATS statement gives a monitoring and rechallenge framework (Saukkonen 2006, PMID 17021358).

Pharmacogenomics: - NAT2 slow acetylators have higher isoniazid hepatotoxicity risk (PMID 30509962). - A CPIC NAT2–isoniazid guideline was still in development as of 2024–2025 (CPIC minutes Dec 2024).

HIV co-infection: give ART alongside TB therapy and watch for IRIS. No hepatic-specific IRIS figures were verified.

Gene, cell and RNA therapies: not applicable. Recombinant IFN-γ is used in some MSMD patients, but this is not specific to hepatic TB and was not retrieved here.

Hepatic-specific clinical trials: none identified.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • BCG vaccination. A hepatic-specific efficacy meta-analysis was not retrieved.
  • Infection control.
  • Addressing undernutrition, alcohol, smoking, diabetes and HIV (WHO 2024).
  • Secondary prevention:
  • LTBI testing and preventive therapy in high-risk groups: people living with HIV, transplant candidates, and people starting TNF inhibitors.
  • A high index of suspicion in endemic areas for hepatomegaly, fever or raised liver enzymes (PMID 25943103).
  • Tertiary prevention:
  • Prompt rifampicin-containing therapy.
  • Liver-function monitoring during treatment (PMID 17021358).
  • Biliary decompression where needed.
  • Genetic counselling: relevant only for MSMD families.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Cattle: M. bovis (bovine TB) can involve the liver in cattle and is zoonotic. The retrieved literature covered liver fluke co-infection as a herd-risk factor rather than hepatic lesions: liver fluke "were not improved" bTB risk models (Byrne 2019, PMID 31012527).
  • Orthologs: TYK2 and IL12RB1 orthologs exist in mouse and zebrafish. NCBI Gene IDs were not retrieved.
  • OMIA: no entry applies, because the disease is not Mendelian.
