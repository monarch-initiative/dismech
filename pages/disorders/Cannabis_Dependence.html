Cannabis Dependence

Psychiatric MONDO:0005689 Pathograph 12 Show in embeddings browser

A substance use disorder in which repeated cannabis use becomes compulsive and persists despite harm, with tolerance and a reproducible withdrawal syndrome on discontinuation. It is the most prevalent illicit-drug use disorder in populations where cannabis is widely used: 2.5 per cent of US adults met DSM-5 criteria in the preceding twelve months and 6.3 per cent over their lifetime, yet only about one in eight people with a lifetime diagnosis has ever had treatment. The reinforcing agent is delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, a partial agonist at cannabinoid CB1 and CB2 receptors. Its dopamine-releasing action in the nucleus accumbens shell is well established in animals and requires both CB1 and mu1 opioid receptors in the ventral tegmentum, but the corresponding effect in humans is small and has been inconsistently detected, and chronic users show blunted rather than heightened striatal dopamine reactivity. Chronic use also downregulates cortical CB1 receptors, reversibly and in proportion to years of smoking. No pharmacotherapy is established. Psychosocial treatment, principally cognitive-behavioural and motivational enhancement therapy, is the standard of care and reduces use at early follow-up, but roughly three quarters of treated patients are not abstinent at final follow-up and no intervention has been consistently effective beyond nine months.

Withdrawal-driven negative reinforcement maintains cannabis dependence

On this account the disorder is maintained less by the acute dopamine effect, which in humans is small and inconsistently detected, than by the withdrawal syndrome and the blunted dopamine reactivity that accompany chronic use: the person continues in order to avoid a reliably aversive state rather than to obtain a large reward. The evidence assembled here is consistent with it and does not establish it. What would distinguish it is a prospective test of whether withdrawal severity predicts relapse when prior use is held constant, which the retrospective sample here cannot provide.

The animal evidence is unambiguous: delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol raises accumbens shell dopamine by microdialysis, and the effect is abolished by a CB1 antagonist and by a mu1 antagonist in the ventral tegmentum. The human evidence is not. The largest pooled analysis found a small reduction in raclopride binding confined to limbic striatum, with nothing elsewhere in the striatum, and introduces itself by noting that earlier human imaging had been inconsistent. Against that, chronic users show blunted dopamine reactivity to a challenge, which is a reduced rather than an increased signal and is the finding that correlates with addiction severity. The two are not strictly in conflict, since one is an acute drug effect and the other a chronic adaptation, and the entry models them as separate nodes. What is unresolved is weighting: whether the acute dopamine step deserves the place the addiction-cycle module gives it in this disease, or whether cannabis dependence is better described as running mainly through the chronic and withdrawal arms. The entry marks the accumbens node provisional for that reason rather than asserting it at the strength of the animal data.

Partial Agonism of Cannabinoid CB1 Receptors by Delta-9-Tetrahydrocannabinol
Delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, the main psychotropic constituent of cannabis, is a partial agonist at the CB1 receptor. It is also a partial agonist at CB2, so this entry does not treat the pharmacology as CB1-exclusive; CB1 is the receptor the downstream nodes are about because it is the one whose antagonist blocks the dopamine effect below. Partial agonism matters for the rest of the chain: the response an exogenous partial agonist produces depends on how much receptor is present and on the endocannabinoid tone it competes with, which is the link to the downregulation node.

Cannabis Withdrawal Syndrome
A withdrawal syndrome reliably follows discontinuation of chronic heavy cannabis use, with onset and time course resembling other substance withdrawal syndromes. Its symptoms are predominantly emotional and behavioural; appetite change, weight loss and physical discomfort are also frequently reported. In a sample of 384 adult lifetime cannabis smokers reporting retrospectively on their most difficult unaided quit attempt, 40.9 per cent met the DSM-5 threshold, and meeting it was associated with the duration and frequency of prior use. Cognitive-behavioural therapy and motivational enhancement therapy, alone or in combination, are the standard of care and the only treatments for this disorder with multi-trial evidence of benefit. The 2016 Cochrane review of 23 randomised trials in 4,045 participants found that people receiving a psychosocial intervention were more likely to be abstinent at early follow-up, on low-quality evidence, and that higher-intensity delivery, more than four sessions over longer than a month, produced more consistent improvement. The limits belong next to the effect. description: >-
  A substance use disorder in which repeated cannabis use becomes compulsive and
  persists despite harm, with tolerance and a reproducible withdrawal syndrome on
  discontinuation. It is the most prevalent illicit-drug use disorder in
  populations where cannabis is widely used: 2.5 per cent of US adults met DSM-5
  criteria in the preceding twelve months and 6.3 per cent over their lifetime,
  yet only about one in eight people with a lifetime diagnosis has ever had
  treatment.

  The reinforcing agent is delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, a partial agonist at
  cannabinoid CB1 and CB2 receptors. Its dopamine-releasing action in the nucleus
  accumbens shell is well established in animals and requires both CB1 and mu1
  opioid receptors in the ventral tegmentum, but the corresponding effect in
  humans is small and has been inconsistently detected, and chronic users show
  blunted rather than heightened striatal dopamine reactivity. Chronic use also
  downregulates cortical CB1 receptors, reversibly and in proportion to years of
  smoking.

  No pharmacotherapy is established. Psychosocial treatment, principally
  cognitive-behavioural and motivational enhancement therapy, is the standard of
  care and reduces use at early follow-up, but roughly three quarters of treated
  patients are not abstinent at final follow-up and no intervention has been
  consistently effective beyond nine months.

  the asymmetry is deliberate rather than an omission: no ICD-10-CM code for

  ICD-10-CM build this repository does not commit.

    The animal evidence is unambiguous: delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol raises
    accumbens shell dopamine by microdialysis, and the effect is abolished by a

    evidence is not. The largest pooled analysis found a small reduction in
    raclopride binding confined to limbic striatum, with nothing elsewhere in the
    striatum, and introduces itself by noting that earlier human imaging had been
    inconsistent. Against that, chronic users show blunted dopamine reactivity to
    a challenge, which is a reduced rather than an increased signal and is the
    finding that correlates with addiction severity.

    dependence is better described as running mainly through the chronic and
    withdrawal arms. The entry marks the accumbens node provisional for that
    reason rather than asserting it at the strength of the animal data.
