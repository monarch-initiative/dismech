Congenital Glutamine Synthetase Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0012393 Pathograph 40 Show in embeddings browser Inborn error of metabolism Inborn error of amino acid metabolism Disorder of glutamine metabolism

Congenital glutamine synthetase deficiency is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive inborn error of amino acid synthesis caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in GLUL. Glutamine synthetase condenses glutamate and ammonia into glutamine and is the only route by which the body makes glutamine, so the defect has two biochemical faces at once: systemic glutamine deficiency, with glutamine largely absent from serum, urine and cerebrospinal fluid in the most severe cases, and chronic moderate hyperammonemia, because the high-affinity perivenous hepatic and astrocytic ammonia-clearing step is lost. The clinical picture is a neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy. The two index infants, homozygous for p.Arg324Cys and p.Arg341Cys, had prenatally evident brain malformation (abnormal or near-absent gyration, white matter immaturity, periventricular cysts), multiorgan failure and death at two days and four weeks of age. A patient homozygous for p.Arg324Ser, which retains more residual activity, survived to six years with refractory seizures, profound developmental delay, cerebral atrophy and hypomyelination. A homozygous whole-gene deletion diagnosed prenatally marks the most severe end, and a homozygous p.Arg41Cys patient with near-normal plasma glutamine marks the mildest. Necrolytic erythema with pellagra-like histology, during periods of very low glutamine, points to a secondary NAD+ deficiency, which has been measured in patient cells. The leading mechanism is glutamine starvation: of the fetal brain during development, and postnatally of the astrocyte-neuron glutamine-glutamate-GABA cycle. Hyperammonemia is a contributing, not a sufficient, explanation, since mice lacking hepatic glutamine synthetase are hyperammonemic without brain malformation. Enteral glutamine supplementation normalized plasma glutamine, raised brain glutamine and improved the EEG in one patient without worsening hyperammonemia; there is no curative therapy.

Deficient Endogenous Glutamine Synthesis
Mechanism confidence: Established
Without functional glutamine synthetase the body cannot condense glutamate with ammonia. Dietary glutamine does not make up the shortfall, because in healthy people it is consumed by enterocytes on absorption and does not reach the systemic pool. The same reaction is the high-affinity ammonia sink, so one lesion removes both a product and a detoxification step.

Impaired High-Affinity Ammonia Clearance
Mechanism confidence: Established
Ammonia escaping the periportal urea cycle is normally captured by glutamine synthetase in perivenous (centrilobular) hepatocytes, and ammonia reaching the brain is captured by astrocytic glutamine synthetase. Glutamine synthetase has a far lower Km for ammonia than the urea-cycle enzymes, so it is the high-affinity, low-capacity half of ammonia detoxification; its loss produces chronic moderate rather than catastrophic hyperammonemia.

Glutamine Starvation of the Developing Brain
Mechanism confidence: Provisional
Brain malformation is visible before birth in the severe genotypes: enlarged ventricles, delayed or near-absent gyration, immature white matter and periventricular cysts. Glutamine is needed for proliferation of fetal cells and for neural differentiation, and GS is expressed in early fetal life. In mice, Glul knockout is lethal at embryonic day 3.5, and loss restricted to astrocytes is lethal three days after birth, so the human disease is viable only through residual activity or maternal and placental supply.

Mechanism confidence: Provisional
Astrocytic glutamine synthetase converts glutamate taken up from synapses into glutamine, which neurons use to remake glutamate and GABA. With the enzyme impaired, brain glutamine is very low and the neurotransmitter pools cannot be replenished; glutamate is not raised in plasma or CSF and was slightly low on spectroscopy, so excitotoxic glutamate excess is not the documented mechanism. Cortex-specific Glul knockout mice show low glutamate, glutamine and GABA, astrogliosis and then spontaneous seizures.

  Congenital glutamine synthetase deficiency is an ultra-rare autosomal
  recessive inborn error of amino acid synthesis caused by biallelic
  loss-of-function variants in GLUL. Glutamine synthetase condenses glutamate
  and ammonia into glutamine and is the only route by which the body makes
  glutamine, so the defect has two biochemical faces at once: systemic
  glutamine deficiency, with glutamine largely absent from serum, urine and
  cerebrospinal fluid in the most severe cases, and chronic moderate
  hyperammonemia, because the high-affinity perivenous hepatic and astrocytic
  ammonia-clearing step is lost.

  The clinical picture is a neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy. The two
  index infants, homozygous for p.Arg324Cys and p.Arg341Cys, had prenatally
  evident brain malformation (abnormal or near-absent gyration, white matter
  immaturity, periventricular cysts), multiorgan failure and death at two days
  and four weeks of age. A patient homozygous for p.Arg324Ser, which retains
  more residual activity, survived to six years with refractory seizures,
  profound developmental delay, cerebral atrophy and hypomyelination. A
  homozygous whole-gene deletion diagnosed prenatally marks the most severe
  end, and a homozygous p.Arg41Cys patient with near-normal plasma glutamine
  marks the mildest. Necrolytic erythema with pellagra-like histology, during
  periods of very low glutamine, points to a secondary NAD+ deficiency, which
  has been measured in patient cells.

  The leading mechanism is glutamine starvation: of the fetal brain during
  development, and postnatally of the astrocyte-neuron
  glutamine-glutamate-GABA cycle. Hyperammonemia is a contributing, not a
  sufficient, explanation, since mice lacking hepatic glutamine synthetase are
  hyperammonemic without brain malformation. Enteral glutamine supplementation
  normalized plasma glutamine, raised brain glutamine and improved the EEG in
  one patient without worsening hyperammonemia; there is no curative therapy.

1. Disease information

Overview. This is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in GLUL. GLUL encodes glutamine synthetase (GS; EC 6.3.1.2), the only enzyme that makes glutamine de novo from glutamate and ammonia. Without it, glutamine is almost entirely absent from plasma, urine and CSF. Ammonia detoxification is also impaired, causing chronic hyperammonemia.

The core picture is a neonatal epileptic encephalopathy with prenatal-onset brain malformation, mainly abnormal gyration, white-matter disease and atrophy. Some infants also have necrolytic migratory erythema, enteropathy and multi-organ failure. The 2005 index cases died in the neonatal period [PMID:16267323]. Later patients show that early death is not inevitable [PMID:21353613; PMID:22830360; PMID:30440076].

2. Etiology

  • Causal factor. Biallelic germline GLUL variants, either missense variants at catalytic residues or a whole-gene deletion. Almost all reported families are consanguineous [PMID:27775558].
  • Genetic risk factors. The only one is parental consanguinity. No susceptibility loci, GWAS signals or modifier genes have been reported.
  • Environmental modifiers (case-level, not causal):
  • Valproate: precipitated hyperammonemic encephalopathy in two compound-heterozygous siblings [PMID:33150193].
  • Chronic hyperammonemia: proposed to cause tyrosine nitration of residual GS, which further lowers its activity. This is a hypothesis to explain the falling glutamine in patient 3 [PMID:27775558].
  • Protective factors. None are known. Residual enzyme activity, for example p.Arg324Ser compared with p.Arg324Cys, correlates with longer survival [PMID:26836257].
  • Gene–environment interaction. Not studied. Exogenous glutamine partly bypasses the defect (§12).

5. Environmental information

There are no environmental, lifestyle or infectious causes. The only reported precipitant is valproate, which worsens hyperammonemia [PMID:33150193]. An unrelated finding is that the pesticide chlorfenapyr destabilizes GLUL protein in liver [PMID:42570757]. That is an acquired GS deficiency, not this disease, and should not be cited as disease evidence.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Biallelic GLUL loss-of-function variants (missense at the catalytic site or deletion) lead to loss of GS catalytic activity. The ATP/glutamate binding step of the reaction is impaired [PMID:16267323; PMID:26836257]. Demonstrated in vitro and in silico.
  2. Absent GS activity results in failure of de novo glutamine synthesis (glutamate + NH₄⁺ + ATP → glutamine), causing systemic glutamine depletion in plasma, urine and CSF [PMID:16267323]. Demonstrated in patients.
  3. 2a. Glutamine deficiency in the placenta and fetus, combined with absent GS in fetal brain, leads to abnormal cortical development (agyria, delayed gyration), white-matter disease and cysts. Inferred: the mechanism linking glutamine supply to gyration has not been shown. Astrocyte-specific Glul knockout mice are born without malformations, which argues that systemic or placental glutamine supply matters [PMID:20140959].
  4. Loss of the GS ammonia-fixing step results in chronic, usually moderate hyperammonemia. This ammonia-fixation step is the perivenous hepatocyte "scavenger" function [PMID:27775558; liver-KO mouse PMID:25870278]. Demonstrated.
  5. Loss of astrocyte glutamine production leads to disruption of the glutamate–glutamine–GABA cycle and reduced substrate for neuronal glutamate and GABA. This results in an excitation/inhibition imbalance and epileptic encephalopathy. Partly inferred in humans. Cortical-deletion mice develop spontaneous seizures and neurodegeneration [PMID:30053506]. In astrocyte-KO mice, cortical glutamine falls about 14-fold and the pups die on postnatal day 3 [PMID:20140959].
  6. Glutamine depletion leads to secondary NAD⁺ deficiency, because glutamine is the amide donor for NAD synthetase. Lower NAD⁺ is proposed to add to cellular energy failure [PMID:25896882]. Demonstrated in patient cells; the clinical contribution is inferred.
  7. Systemic glutamine starvation of rapidly dividing and glutamine-dependent tissues (enterocytes, keratinocytes, immune cells) leads to enteropathy, necrolytic migratory erythema and multi-organ failure [PMID:16763901]. Inferred.
  8. Chronic hyperammonemia may cause tyrosine nitration that further inactivates residual GS, a proposed feed-forward loop [PMID:27775558]. Hypothesis.

Branch structure. Step 2 feeds steps 2a, 4, 5 and 6. Step 3 runs in parallel and feeds step 7, which loops back to step 1.

7. Anatomical structures affected

  • Primary organs: brain, including the cerebral cortex, cerebellum and periventricular white matter (UBERON:0000955, UBERON:0000956, UBERON:0002037; verify). Liver is affected as the site of the ammonia-scavenging defect (UBERON:0002107).
  • Secondary organs: skin (UBERON:0002097), intestine (UBERON:0000160), and multiple organs in the neonatal-lethal cases.
  • Cells: astrocytes, perivenous hepatocytes, oligodendrocytes (inferred from hypomyelination), enterocytes, keratinocytes.
  • Subcellular: cytosol, the GS location (GO:0005829); mitochondria are relevant through ammonia and NAD⁺ effects.
  • Laterality: bilateral and symmetric, systemic.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: prenatal. Polyhydramnios, ventriculomegaly and gyral abnormalities are seen on fetal ultrasound or MRI. Seizures begin at birth or in the first two weeks.
  • Course: chronic, progressive or static encephalopathy. Death ranges from day 2 to 6 years.
  • Remission: none spontaneous. Glutamine supplementation improves alertness and EEG but not structural brain disease [PMID:22830360].
  • Critical window: the brain malformations arise in utero. Any intervention that could prevent them, such as maternal or fetal glutamine supply, would have to be prenatal. This is untested.

9. Inheritance and population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive (HP:0000007). Carrier parents are unaffected, which suggests complete penetrance for biallelic loss of function.
  • Expressivity: variable, correlating with residual activity. Neonatal-lethal cases had p.Arg324Cys or p.Arg341Cys. The 6-year survivor had p.Arg324Ser, and the sibling pair had a milder epilepsy phenotype.
  • Prevalence: fewer than 10 individuals reported worldwide. Use prevalence_class: BELOW_1_IN_1000000 or measure_type: CASES_IN_LITERATURE, citing PMID:27775558, which states the disorder had been reported "in only three unrelated patients since its first description in 2005."
  • Populations: Turkish (two families), Sudanese, Turkish (Pt 4, Kocaeli), Canadian sibship and Canadian fetus. There is no founder effect and the sex ratio is balanced.
  • Anticipation and mosaicism: not applicable.

10. Diagnostics

  • First-line biochemistry: plasma amino acids. Near-absent glutamine with hyperammonemia is pathognomonic, because almost every other hyperammonemic disorder (urea-cycle defects) raises glutamine. Confirm with CSF and urine amino acids and plasma ammonia.
  • LOINC leads: Glutamine [Moles/volume] in Serum or Plasma; Glutamine in CSF; Ammonia in Plasma. Seed these with just loinc-seed-cache and do not type the labels.
  • Brain MRS: reduced glutamine peak.
  • Enzyme assay: GS activity in lymphocytes or fibroblasts; research use only.
  • NAD⁺ in leukocytes as a secondary marker [PMID:25896882].
  • Imaging: fetal and neonatal MRI showing agyria/pachygyria, delayed gyration, hypomyelination, subependymal cysts and atrophy.
  • EEG: multifocal epileptiform activity or burst suppression.
  • Genetics: WES or WGS (Pt 4 was diagnosed by WES), epilepsy or lissencephaly gene panels, and CNV analysis or CMA. CNV analysis is essential because a whole-gene deletion has been reported [PMID:32888207]. Prenatal diagnosis by CMA or exome is feasible.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • Urea-cycle disorders and organic acidemias: hyperammonemia but high glutamine.
  • Other lissencephalies (LIS1/PAFAH1B1, DCX, tubulinopathies).
  • Other neonatal epileptic encephalopathies.
  • Glucagonoma or zinc deficiency (other causes of necrolytic erythema).
  • DEE116 (dominant GLUL): normal glutamine.
  • Screening: not on any newborn-screening panel. MS/MS newborn screening could in principle detect low glutamine, but this has not been validated. Carrier testing is appropriate in affected families.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Mortality: two of the four classic patients died in the neonatal period (days 2 and 28) of multi-organ failure. One died at 6 years of respiratory decompensation [PMID:16267323; PMID:27775558]. The 2011 report concluded that GS deficiency causes severe neurological disease but is not always early-lethal [PMID:21353613].
  • Morbidity: profound developmental disability, refractory epilepsy, feeding dependence (gastrostomy) and chronic hyperammonemia.
  • Prognostic factors: residual enzyme activity as determined by genotype [PMID:26836257]; the severity of the brain malformation.
  • Recovery: structural brain abnormalities did not improve on treatment.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: genetic counseling (NCIT:C15240) for consanguineous carrier couples; prenatal diagnosis or PGT once the familial variants are known; cascade carrier testing.

14. Other species and natural disease

No naturally occurring GS deficiency has been found in companion or farm animals; OMIA should be checked. GS is among the most conserved enzymes, present in bacteria (GlnA), plants and animals. Mouse Glul is MGI:95739. Drosophila carries two GS genes; Gs1 mutants are lethal [Caggese 1992, PMID:1363402].
