Hemolytic Anemia Due to Adenylate Kinase Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0012967 Pathograph 35 Show in embeddings browser Congenital Nonspherocytic Hemolytic Anemia Red Cell Enzymopathy

A very rare autosomal recessive red-cell enzymopathy caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in AK1, which encodes the cytosolic adenylate kinase that interconverts adenine nucleotides (AMP + ATP = 2 ADP). Affected individuals have reduced or undetectable erythrocyte adenylate kinase activity and a chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia of variable severity, usually presenting with neonatal jaundice and splenomegaly. A minority, mostly those with absent enzyme activity, also have intellectual disability or psychomotor impairment. How the enzyme defect shortens red-cell survival is not established, and whether adenylate kinase deficiency is sufficient on its own to cause hemolysis has been questioned since the first biochemical studies.

Is loss of AK1 activity sufficient to cause hemolytic anemia, or is a second red-cell defect required?
CONTROVERSY OPEN controversy_ak1_deficiency_sufficient_for_hemolysis
Attached to

The genetics favor sufficiency: thirteen independent AK1 alleles in unrelated recessive families, each functionally damaging where tested. Against it stand a sibling with profound deficiency and no hemolysis, preserved adenine nucleotide turnover in enzyme-null red cells, no relation between residual activity and severity, and worse anemia when G6PD deficiency or thalassemia is co-inherited. The older biochemical studies had no genotypes, and the genotyped reports did not look for a second lesion, so the two bodies of evidence have never been tested against each other. The answer determines whether an AK1 genotype alone predicts disease in a sibling or fetus.

Pathophysiology

8
AK1 Nucleotide-Binding Site Variants
Missense and single-residue deletion alleles that alter residues lining the AMP and ATP sites of AK1. AK1 is a 194-residue monomer with a central CORE domain, an NMP-binding domain (residues 38-67) and a LID domain (residues 131-141) that close over the two nucleotides during phosphoryl transfer. In the crystal structure of human AK1 with the bisubstrate analog Ap5A (PDB 1Z83, chain A; distances computed for this entry), Gly40 (3.5 A), Gly64 (4.4 A), Arg97 (3.5 A), Gln101 (3.1 A), Arg128 (3.4 A), Arg138 (3.2 A) and Asp140 (4.0 A) all lie within 4.5 A of the ligand, and Gln24 sits 5.2 A away on the helix that follows the phosphate-binding loop. The recombinant Gly40Arg, Gly64Arg, Arg128Trp and Asp140del enzymes retain about one ten-thousandth of wild-type catalytic efficiency, and Arg97Trp loses about 89% of catalytic efficiency with weakened binding of all three nucleotides. These are loss-of-function alleles; affected individuals are homozygous or compound heterozygous and carriers are healthy, so there is no evidence of a dominant-negative or gain-of-function effect.

AK1 Core Destabilizing Variants
Missense alleles at buried residues of the CORE domain, away from the nucleotide sites, that are expected to act by destabilizing the fold and lowering the amount of enzyme in the red cell. Tyr164 is fully buried (relative side-chain accessibility 0.01 in PDB 1Z83, 11.8 A from Ap5A) and packs against Phe12, Leu115, Val160 and Ile161; it is invariant from Escherichia coli adenylate kinase to human AK1. Recombinant Tyr164Cys is grossly unstable, and the homozygous patient had no detectable erythrocyte enzyme protein on electrophoresis. The c.518T>G allele, which changes the buried strand residue Val173 to glycine on the canonical transcript, is placed in this class on structural grounds only. A mature red cell cannot replace an unstable protein, so instability becomes absence of activity as the cell ages.

AK1 Truncating Variants
Nonsense and frameshift alleles that remove or replace the C-terminal part of the enzyme. Arg107Ter and the frameshifts Val47SerfsTer45 (reported as delG138) and Met75AsnfsTer19 stop translation before the LID domain and before the nucleotide-site residues Arg128, Arg132 and Gly177, so any product lacks a complete active site; these transcripts are also expected to be subject to nonsense-mediated decay. Arg107 itself is a surface residue (relative accessibility 0.59, 14.7 A from Ap5A) that is not conserved in chicken or mouse AK1, so the nonsense change acts through truncation and not through loss of that side chain. Lys155ArgfsTer39 is different: the new stop falls at codon 193, one codon before the native stop, so the transcript should escape decay and encode a full-length protein whose last forty residues, including the C-terminal helix and the ATP-site residue Gly177, are replaced by unrelated sequence. Loss of AK1 Adenylate Kinase Activity
AK1 is the major cytosolic adenylate kinase. It catalyzes the reversible, magnesium-dependent transfer of the terminal phosphate of ATP to AMP (AMP + ATP = 2 ADP), which is the only route by which a cell returns AMP to the ADP and ATP pool without degrading it, and it sets the AMP signal that reports cellular energy state. In affected individuals erythrocyte adenylate kinase activity is undetectable or reduced to between roughly a fifth and a half of normal, and heterozygous parents have about half-normal activity. Erythrocyte activity is the feature the disease is defined by. Other cell types keep part of their adenylate kinase activity through the genetically independent isozyme AK2, which is not thought to be present in erythrocytes in more than trace amounts.

Impaired Erythrocyte Adenine Nucleotide Interconversion
Mechanism confidence: Provisional
The mature erythrocyte has no mitochondria and cannot synthesize purines de novo, so its adenine nucleotide pool depends on glycolytic ATP production, adenine salvage, and adenylate kinase to return AMP to ADP. In enzyme-deficient red cells the transfer of phosphate between ATP and ADP continues at a small fraction of the normal rate (about 1/2,000 of normal activity in one study), and labeled adenine still enters ADP and ATP at about half the expected rate. The residual flux has been attributed to trace AK2, to guanylate kinase accepting AMP, or to an AMP:GTP phosphotransferase. Steady-state ATP is normal or raised in most patients studied, which reflects the young red-cell population, so a simple fall in ATP does not explain the hemolysis; reported abnormalities are instead raised 2,3-diphosphoglycerate and, in one patient, reduced AMP.

Neuronal Phosphotransfer Deficit
Mechanism confidence: Hypothetical
A proposed consequence of AK1 loss in the brain, where AK1 is among the most highly expressed adenylate kinases: reduced adenylate kinase phosphotransfer and AMP signaling in neurons during development. It is offered as the basis of the intellectual disability and psychomotor impairment seen in a minority of patients. The supporting observation is only that neurological features cluster in patients with absent or very low erythrocyte activity (homozygous Arg107Ter, Arg97Trp, and compound heterozygotes carrying Arg138His); patients with equally absent activity and normal development are also on record, and severe neonatal hyperbilirubinemia is a competing explanation that the case reports do not exclude.

A deficiency in red blood cell adenylate kinase 1 (AK-1) is linked to congenital non-spherocytic hemolytic anemia as well as delays in both mental and psychomotor development. This deficiency is an extremely rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder, with 13 highly pathogenic variants of the AK-1 gene documented globally through genetic testing. Thalassemia, a disorder of hemoglobin production, is a common monogenic inherited disease in southern China. Severe forms of thalassemia result in defective hemoglobin synthesis, leading to hemolytic anemia due to the breakdown of red blood cells. A molecular investigation was carried out on a proband with severe hemolytic anemia from a family in Guangxi, China, to identify the underlying cause of the anemia and associated clinical manifestations, offering valuable information for family planning and prenatal counseling. Hematological, biochemical, and thalassemia-specific genetic testing were performed on the proband and family members in relation to chronic hemolytic anemia. Whole-exome sequencing was utilized to detect genetic variants, followed by an analysis of their pathogenic potential. Confirmation was conducted using Sanger sequencing. Prenatal diagnosis was carried out by analyzing amniotic fluid during the mother’s second pregnancy. The proband, who presented with severe hemolytic anemia, was diagnosed with the thalassemia genotype− SEA/αα compound βCD41–42/βN. A homozygous mutation, c.464delA (p.Lys155Arg fs*39), was identified in exon 6 of the AK-1 gene.
