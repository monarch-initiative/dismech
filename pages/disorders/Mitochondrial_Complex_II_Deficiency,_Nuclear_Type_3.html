Mitochondrial Complex II Deficiency, Nuclear Type 3

Mendelian MONDO:0030937 Pathograph 21 Show in embeddings browser Mitochondrial Disease

Mitochondrial complex II deficiency, nuclear type 3 (MC2DN3) is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial respiratory-chain disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in SDHD, which encodes one of the two small hydrophobic subunits that anchor the catalytic SDHA/SDHB dimer of succinate dehydrogenase (complex II) to the inner mitochondrial membrane. Recessive SDHD variants reduce steady-state levels of SDH subunits and impair assembly of complex II, leaving an isolated complex II deficiency that disrupts both succinate oxidation in the tricarboxylic acid cycle and electron transfer to ubiquinone. The reported phenotype is a variable multisystem mitochondrial disease: early-onset progressive encephalomyopathy with developmental delay or regression, severe to profound intellectual disability, hypotonia, seizures, nystagmus, optic atrophy, progressive microcephaly and raised lactate with urinary excretion of Krebs-cycle intermediates, and/or cardiomyopathy ranging from fatal prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular non-compaction to childhood dilated cardiomyopathy. Only a handful of families have been described. The disorder is distinct from the autosomal dominant, paternally transmitted SDHD-related hereditary paraganglioma-pheochromocytoma syndrome (PGL1), which is caused by monoallelic germline SDHD loss with somatic loss of the second allele in tumours; none of the recessive complex II deficiency alleles reported so far has been linked to tumourigenesis.

Mitochondrial Complex II Deficiency, Nuclear Type 3 (MC2DN3): Comprehensive Disease Report

Disease: Mitochondrial Complex II Deficiency, Nuclear Type 3 MONDO: MONDO:0030937 | OMIM: #619167 | Category: Mendelian (autosomal recessive) Causal gene: SDHD (11q23.1)

Summary

Mitochondrial Complex II Deficiency, Nuclear Type 3 (MC2DN3) is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder caused by biallelic (compound heterozygous or homozygous) loss-of-function variants in the nuclear gene SDHD, which encodes the small cytochrome-b membrane-anchor subunit (CybS) of succinate dehydrogenase — respiratory chain complex II (succinate:ubiquinone oxidoreductase). The first and defining case was reported by Jackson and colleagues in 2014, who described a child with early progressive encephalomyopathy harboring compound heterozygous SDHD variants (p.E69K and p.*164Lext*3), with native-PAGE/western blotting demonstrating impaired complex II assembly and cell-line complementation confirming pathogenicity (PMID: 24367056).

Mechanistically, loss of SDHD impairs assembly and anchoring of complex II in the inner mitochondrial membrane, simultaneously disrupting two coupled metabolic pathways: the TCA-cycle oxidation of succinate to fumarate and the transfer of electrons into the respiratory chain via ubiquinone. The result is an energy-deficiency state with lactic acidosis and succinate accumulation that preferentially injures high-energy-demand tissues — the central nervous system (basal ganglia, brainstem, cerebral/cerebellar white matter), heart, and skeletal muscle. Clinically this manifests most commonly as Leigh syndrome, leukoencephalopathy/leukodystrophy, epileptic encephalopathy, and cardiomyopathy. There is no cure; management is supportive and largely off-label (riboflavin, coenzyme Q10, L-carnitine). Prognosis is highly variable, with cardiomyopathy predicting poor outcome and riboflavin-responsiveness predicting better outcome.

A biologically important feature of this gene is its dual disease role: whereas biallelic recessive SDHD variants cause this mitochondrial disease, monoallelic germline SDHD variants cause hereditary paraganglioma/pheochromocytoma with a striking parent-of-origin effect (tumors form almost exclusively after paternal transmission, accompanied by somatic loss of the maternal chromosome 11). This report synthesizes 13 confirmed findings across 33 reviewed papers into a comprehensive, section-by-section knowledge-base entry.

Section 1. Disease Information

Overview. MC2DN3 is a Mendelian inborn error of mitochondrial energy metabolism belonging to the group of isolated mitochondrial complex II (succinate dehydrogenase, SDH) deficiencies. Isolated complex II deficiency is itself one of the rarest respiratory-chain disorders, accounting for approximately 2% of all mitochondrial respiratory chain deficiency diagnoses (PMID: 22972948). MC2DN3 is the specific nuclear type caused by SDHD mutations.

Section 2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. MC2DN3 is caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in SDHD, inherited in an autosomal recessive manner (Finding F001). The MONDO/OLS description states it is "autosomal recessive caused by pathogenic variants in the SDHD gene." Jackson et al. 2014 reported the first patient with isolated complex II deficiency due to compound heterozygous SDHD mutations: "This report describes the first case of isolated complex II deficiency due to recessive SDHD germline mutations" and "The patient presented with early progressive encephalomyopathy due to compound heterozygous p.E69 K and p.*164Lext*3 SDHD mutations" (PMID: 24367056).

Genetic risk factors. The only established causal factor is biallelic SDHD loss of function. Consanguinity increases risk of homozygous recessive disease (as for all AR disorders). Being a compound heterozygote or homozygote for pathogenic SDHD alleles is deterministic for disease rather than probabilistic.

Environmental risk / modifier factors. No specific environmental cause is known. As with other mitochondrial encephalomyopathies, metabolic stressors — intercurrent viral illness, fasting, and febrile episodes — can precipitate decompensation and developmental regression. A recent SDH-deficiency case presented with encephalopathy and developmental regression following viral illnesses (PMID: 37064335).

Protective factors. The best-documented modifiable protective influence is riboflavin (vitamin B2) supplementation, the FAD precursor, which can partially restore complex II activity (see Section 12, Finding F002). No protective germline alleles are established.

Gene–environment interactions. The dominant GxE axis is the interaction between the underlying enzyme deficiency and catabolic stress: patients with limited residual complex II activity decompensate during illness/fasting, whereas riboflavin availability modulates residual holoenzyme activity.

Section 3. Phenotypes

The clinical spectrum of SDHD/complex II deficiency is dominated by neurological involvement, with cardiac and muscular involvement in a substantial subset (Finding F003). Jain-Ghai et al.'s review (n=36) found "Neurological involvement is most commonly observed and brain imaging commonly shows leukoencephalopathy, Leigh syndrome, or cerebellar atrophy" (PMID: 23322652), and the broader SDH-deficiency spectrum includes "several very different presentations including Leigh syndrome, epileptic encephalopathy, and cardiomyopathy" (PMID: 37064335).

ClinVar variant landscape. ClinVar (queried Oct 2026) lists 979 SDHD variants: 276 pathogenic, 94 likely pathogenic, 619 VUS. Technical caveat — pseudogenes. SDHD has processed pseudogenes on chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 7 and 18, which can complicate NGS/Sanger analysis and require careful mapping.

Modifier genes / epigenetics / chromosomal abnormalities. No specific modifier genes are established for the recessive disease. The clinically relevant chromosomal/epigenetic phenomenon for SDHD is the parent-of-origin imprinting effect in the paraganglioma context (Section 9), not in recessive MC2DN3. No recurrent large structural rearrangements cause MC2DN3.

Section 5. Environmental Information

MC2DN3 is a monogenic disorder; environmental factors are triggers/modifiers rather than causes. No toxins, infectious agents, or occupational exposures are established as etiologic. The practically important environmental modifiers are catabolic stressors (intercurrent infection, fasting, fever) that precipitate metabolic decompensation, and nutritional cofactor availability (riboflavin/FAD). No infectious agent causes or triggers the disease in a specific sense, although viral illnesses have temporally preceded regression episodes (PMID: 37064335).

Section 6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function variants in SDHD (e.g., p.E69K / p.*164Lext*3) → reduced or dysfunctional CybS (cytochrome-b small) subunit protein. (Demonstrated — Jackson 2014.)
  2. Loss of functional SDHD leads to failure of the SDHC–SDHD membrane anchor to form → impaired assembly of the complex II holoenzyme in the inner mitochondrial membrane. (Demonstrated by native-PAGE/western: "Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and western blotting demonstrated an impaired complex II assembly," PMID: 24367056.)
  3. Impaired complex II assembly results in loss of succinate:ubiquinone oxidoreductase activity, which simultaneously blocks two coupled pathways:
  4. Branch A (TCA cycle): failure to oxidize succinate → fumarate, causing succinate accumulation (detectable as an MR-spectroscopy succinate peak and elevated succinate/fumarate ratios).
  5. Branch B (OXPHOS): failure to transfer electrons from FADH₂ via Fe-S clusters to ubiquinone → ubiquinol, reducing electron flux into the respiratory chain.
  6. Reduced electron entry leads to diminished oxidative phosphorylation and reduced ATP output, with compensatory anaerobic glycolysis → lactic acidosis (HP:0002151). (Demonstrated/inferred from biochemistry.)
  7. Energy deficit + succinate accumulation results in cellular dysfunction preferentially in high-energy-demand, post-mitotic tissues — neurons/glia (CNS), cardiomyocytes, and myofibers. (Inferred from tissue tropism.)
  8. Tissue-level injury manifests as Leigh syndrome / basal-ganglia and brainstem necrosis, leukoencephalopathy/leukodystrophy, cardiomyopathy, and myopathy (the clinical phenotype). (Demonstrated clinically.)

Molecular/biochemical detail (Finding F006)

Complex II is a ~140 kDa tetramer: catalytic SDHA (FAD flavoprotein) oxidizes succinate→fumarate; SDHB (three Fe-S clusters) relays electrons to the membrane anchor formed by SDHC + SDHD, which bind heme b and form the ubiquinone-reduction site, reducing ubiquinone→ubiquinol. SDHD/QPs3 was identified as one of the Q-binding subunits: "identified QPs3 as one of the putative Q-binding proteins in this reductase" (PMID: 9211943). The soluble catalytic domain is bound to a membrane-spanning domain, a highly conserved architecture (PMID: 36171656).

Pathways/ontology suggestions: TCA cycle (KEGG hsa00020), oxidative phosphorylation (KEGG hsa00190), Reactome "Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase)". GO biological process: GO:0006099 (tricarboxylic acid cycle), GO:0022904 (respiratory electron transport chain), GO:0006119 (oxidative phosphorylation). CHEBI entities: succinate (CHEBI:30031), fumarate (CHEBI:29806), ubiquinone (CHEBI:16389), FAD (CHEBI:16238), heme b (CHEBI:26355), lactate (CHEBI:24996).

Cellular/tissue damage mechanisms. Bioenergetic failure and oxidative stress drive neuronal/glial injury (spongiform change, demyelination in white matter, symmetric necrosis of basal ganglia/brainstem in Leigh syndrome) and cardiomyocyte dysfunction. Succinate accumulation may also exert signaling effects (e.g., HIF stabilization, GPR91/SUCNR1 signaling) known from the tumor context, though these are better characterized for monoallelic/tumor biology than for recessive disease.

Section 8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: typically congenital/neonatal to early childhood; onset pattern is often insidious or subacute, with acute decompensations superimposed by intercurrent illness.
  • Progression: generally progressive, frequently with episodic regression. In the broader complex II-deficiency spectrum, course ranges from fatal in infancy to mild adult-onset disease (Finding F012): "For some, the condition was fatal in infancy, others had multisystem involvement and some had onset in adulthood with mild symptoms and normal cognition" (PMID: 23322652).
  • Disease course pattern: chronic, lifelong, progressive, with relapsing decompensations.
  • Remission: no spontaneous cure; partial, treatment-associated stabilization/improvement reported with riboflavin (Section 12).
  • Critical periods: infancy/early childhood is the window of greatest vulnerability and the key opportunity for cofactor intervention and avoidance of catabolic stress.

Section 9. Inheritance and Population

Epidemiology (Finding F008). Mitochondrial disease collectively has a minimum prevalence >1 in 5,000 adults: "Mitochondrial disease is one of the most common groups of genetic diseases with a minimum prevalence of greater than 1 in 5000 in adults" (PMID: 26315846). Isolated complex II deficiency accounts for ~2% of respiratory-chain deficiency diagnoses (PMID: 22972948). MC2DN3 specifically is ultra-rare — only a handful of patients reported since 2014 — so no formal prevalence/incidence estimate exists.

Inheritance (Finding F001 / F004). Autosomal recessive; disease requires biallelic SDHD loss of function. Penetrance of biallelic LOF is presumed high/complete; expressivity is variable even within the complex-II-deficiency spectrum. No genetic anticipation (not a repeat-expansion disorder). Consanguinity increases risk of homozygous disease.

Dual gene role / imprinting (Finding F004). A defining molecular feature of SDHD is its dual disease role. Monoallelic germline SDHD variants cause hereditary paraganglioma/pheochromocytoma (PGL1) with a parent-of-origin effect: "In SDHD (11q23)-linked families, the disease phenotype is expressed only upon paternal transmission of the mutation, consistent with maternal imprinting" (PMID: 15064708). Tumor formation requires somatic loss of maternal chromosome 11: "loss of maternal chromosome 11p15 was demonstrated in 85% of SDHD" paragangliomas (PMID: 28099933). This dominant, imprinted tumor mechanism is mechanistically distinct from the recessive, biallelic mechanism of MC2DN3. Clinically, this means family counseling for an MC2DN3 proband must also consider tumor risk in heterozygous carriers (especially with paternal transmission).

Population demographics. No ethnic predilection or founder effect is established for the recessive MC2DN3 alleles. Sex ratio is expected ~1:1 (autosomal). Age distribution of affected individuals is skewed to infancy/early childhood.

Section 10. Diagnostics (Finding F008)

Biochemical / enzymatic: - Respiratory chain enzyme assay (muscle or fibroblasts) showing isolated low complex II (SDH) activity with normal other complexes. - SDH histochemistry on muscle (reduced staining). - Elevated blood and CSF lactate (HP:0002151).

Imaging: - Brain MRI: Leigh-pattern bilateral symmetric basal ganglia/brainstem lesions, or leukoencephalopathy/leukodystrophy, or cerebellar atrophy. - MR spectroscopy: may show an abnormal succinate peak (characteristic of SDH deficiency).

Protein/assembly: - BN-PAGE / native-PAGE and western blot demonstrating impaired complex II assembly and reduced steady-state SDH subunit levels (PMID: 24367056, PMID: 22972948).

Molecular (confirmatory): - WES/WGS or targeted SDHx gene panel (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, SDHAF1, SDHAF2). Jackson et al. explicitly recommend: "We therefore recommend screening for all SDH genes in isolated complex II deficiencies" (PMID: 24367056). Pseudogene awareness is required for SDHD. - Functional validation by cell-line/yeast complementation for VUS resolution.

Differential diagnosis: other isolated complex II deficiencies (SDHA/SDHB/SDHC/SDHAF1-related — MC2DN1/2/4), other Leigh syndrome genetic causes (e.g., complex I defects such as NDUFS4), and other leukodystrophies/mitochondrial encephalomyopathies. SDHD type is distinguished by biallelic SDHD genotype and the biochemical signature of isolated complex II deficiency.

Screening. No newborn screening exists for MC2DN3. Carrier and cascade testing is feasible once a familial genotype is known; genetic counseling must address the dual tumor/mitochondrial-disease risk of SDHD.

Section 11. Outcome / Prognosis (Finding F012)

Prognosis is highly variable. In the complex-II-deficiency spectrum, outcomes range from fatal infantile disease to mild adult-onset forms: "For some, the condition was fatal in infancy, others had multisystem involvement and some had onset in adulthood with mild symptoms and normal cognition" (PMID: 23322652).

Prognostic factors: - Cardiomyopathy is associated with high mortality (adverse prognostic marker). - Riboflavin-responsiveness (particularly in early-onset leukoencephalopathy) predicts stabilization or improvement (favorable; Finding F002, Section 12). - Within a family, "the phenotype and enzyme activities are comparable within a family" (PMID: 23322652), but across patients there is no reliable correlation between residual muscle complex II activity and clinical severity.

Disease course / complications: progressive neurodegeneration with risk of brainstem/respiratory failure (Leigh syndrome), seizures, feeding difficulties/failure to thrive, cardiac failure, and recurrent metabolic decompensation. No validated disease-specific QoL instruments exist.

There is no curative therapy. "There are currently no effective clinical treatments for mitochondrial disease" (PMID: 38883711); "There is no cure for complex II deficiency, though some reported patients showed clinical improvement following riboflavin therapy" (PMID: 37064335).

Section 13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not possible for the genetic disease; genetic counseling and reproductive options (prenatal diagnosis, preimplantation genetic testing) once a familial SDHD genotype is known. Counseling must disclose the dual risk — recessive mitochondrial disease in biallelic carriers, and paraganglioma/pheochromocytoma risk in heterozygous carriers (parent-of-origin dependent).
  • Secondary prevention: early biochemical/genetic diagnosis to enable a riboflavin trial and avoidance of metabolic stressors; cascade carrier testing.
  • Tertiary prevention: aggressive management of intercurrent illness, cardiac surveillance, seizure control, and nutritional support to prevent decompensation and complications.
  • Immunization: routine childhood vaccination is advisable to reduce illness-triggered decompensations (general mitochondrial-disease practice); no disease-specific vaccine.
  • Carrier surveillance: heterozygous relatives should be counseled regarding paraganglioma/pheochromocytoma surveillance per SDHx tumor-syndrome guidelines, especially for paternally inherited alleles.

Section 15. Model Organisms (Finding F007)

Mouse (Mus musculus, Taxon 10090) — the principal mammalian model. Piruat et al. 2004 generated the first mammalian ETC-subunit knockout. Key findings: - Homozygous Sdhd(−/−) is embryonic lethal: "Homozygous SDHD(-/-) animals die at early embryonic stages" (PMID: 15572694). - Heterozygous Sdhd(+/−): "Heterozygous SDHD(+/-) mice show a general, noncompensated deficiency of succinate dehydrogenase activity without alterations in body weight or major physiological dysfunction" (PMID: 15572694), but with constitutive carotid-body glomus-cell activation (hypertrophy/hyperplasia).

Compound-heterozygous Sdhb/Sdhc/Sdhd knockout mice (Baysal et al. 2022) show reduced SDH protein, elevated succinate and succinate/fumarate and succinate/oxoglutarate ratios, and complex-II-dependent effects on red cell distribution width and healthy lifespan under chronic hypoxia (PMID: 35881479).

Model characteristics / limitations. Because full Sdhd knockout is embryonic lethal, the complete recessive human disease is not directly recapitulated by a constitutive null; conditional/tissue-specific or hypomorphic models are needed to model the human encephalomyopathy. Heterozygous mice better model the carrier/tumor-predisposition biology (carotid body activation) than the recessive mitochondrial disease. Other respiratory-chain Leigh models (e.g., Ndufs4-KO mouse) and multi-complex-deficient zebrafish are used as surrogate platforms for therapeutic testing (PMID: 39930437, PMID: 40786033).

Resources: MGI (mouse Sdhd), IMPC/IMSR for alleles; patient-derived fibroblasts and yeast (SDH4) complementation systems for in vitro functional work.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

MC2DN3 is a clean example of a single-subunit assembly failure propagating to a bioenergetic and metabolic double-lesion. Because SDHD is the membrane anchor and part of the ubiquinone-binding site, its loss does not merely reduce one enzymatic step — it collapses the structural scaffold needed to assemble the holoenzyme, so that both the TCA-cycle succinate-oxidation arm and the respiratory-chain electron-entry arm fail together. This explains two cardinal biochemical signatures observed clinically: lactic acidosis (OXPHOS failure, anaerobic glycolysis) and a succinate peak on MRS (TCA blockade). The downstream clinical picture is governed by tissue OXPHOS dependence, hence the predilection for CNS, heart, and muscle, and the characteristic bilateral symmetric basal-ganglia/brainstem lesions of Leigh syndrome.

The upstream event is always the biallelic SDHD lesion; downstream are assembly failure → dual metabolic blockade → energy deficit/succinate accumulation → tissue-specific neurodegeneration and cardiomyopathy. The one branch point worth emphasizing for a knowledge base is the genotype-dosage branch of SDHD biology: biallelic → recessive mitochondrial disease (this entity); monoallelic + somatic loss of the wild-type/maternal allele → paraganglioma. The same enzyme deficiency therefore underlies two opposite clinical worlds (degenerative vs neoplastic), unified by succinate accumulation and its pseudohypoxic/signaling consequences.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Ultra-rarity. MC2DN3 rests on a very small number of reported biallelic-SDHD patients; much of the phenotype/prognosis detail is extrapolated from the broader isolated complex II / SDHx deficiency literature rather than from SDHD-type-specific cohorts.
  2. No epidemiology. No formal prevalence, incidence, carrier frequency, sex ratio, or geographic data exist for the SDHD type specifically.
  3. Variant interpretation complexity. Most ClinVar SDHD pathogenic alleles are reported in the monoallelic tumor context; recessive-disease pathogenicity assignment and the confounding SDHD pseudogenes complicate molecular diagnosis.
  4. No faithful mammalian model of the recessive human disease — constitutive Sdhd null is embryonic lethal; conditional/hypomorphic CNS- and heart-specific models are lacking.
  5. No controlled therapeutic evidence. Riboflavin and cofactor benefits derive from small case series and in vitro data; no randomized trials exist for complex II deficiency.
  6. Mechanistic signaling downstream of succinate (pseudohypoxia, GPR91/SUCNR1) is better characterized in tumor biology than in the degenerative recessive disease.
