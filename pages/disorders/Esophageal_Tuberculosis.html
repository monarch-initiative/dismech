Esophageal Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0004189 Pathograph 34 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Extrapulmonary tuberculosis Gastrointestinal tuberculosis

Esophageal tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the esophagus and one of the rarest forms of extrapulmonary and gastrointestinal tuberculosis. Most cases are secondary: caseating tuberculous lymph nodes of the mediastinum, usually the subcarinal group, compress the mid-esophagus and then rupture into its wall, and less often the esophagus is reached by direct extension from the lung or a tuberculous spine. Primary disease, with no tuberculous focus outside the esophagus, is rare and is attributed to swallowed bacilli. Dysphagia is the main symptom, with retrosternal pain, odynophagia and constitutional symptoms such as anorexia, weight loss and fever. Endoscopy usually shows a mid-esophageal ulcer, an extrinsic bulge with or without an ulcer at its summit, or a pseudotumour, and the lesion is often mistaken for esophageal carcinoma. Transmural disease can form tracheoesophageal, bronchoesophageal or esophagomediastinal fistulas, strictures and traction diverticula, and erosion into the aorta can cause fatal hematemesis. Diagnosis rests on endoscopic biopsy or endoscopic ultrasound-guided aspiration of the adjacent nodes showing granulomas or acid-fast bacilli, supported by PCR, culture and chest CT showing mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Standard antituberculous chemotherapy cures nearly all patients, including most with fistulas; endoscopic closure, stenting and surgery are reserved for complications.

Secondary esophageal tuberculosis
Esophageal tuberculosis arising from tuberculosis in adjacent structures, above all mediastinal lymph nodes and less often the lung or spine. It is the common form, and chest CT or endoscopic ultrasound shows the adjacent nodal or pulmonary disease.

Primary esophageal tuberculosis
Esophageal tuberculosis with no tuberculous focus elsewhere in the body. It is rare: one Beijing chest hospital found four patients among about 33,000 tuberculosis inpatients over ten years. Chest imaging is normal or shows only the esophageal lesion, which can make the disease harder to suspect.

Caseating tuberculous lymphadenitis of the mediastinal nodes, most often the subcarinal and paraesophageal groups, is the usual source of esophageal disease. The nodes are enlarged, matted and heterogeneous with hypoechoic, necrotic centres, and lie against the mid-esophagus.

How I scoped this report: Esophageal tuberculosis (ETB) is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. No causal human gene is recorded, and none exists. This matches the template ("Causal gene(s): not recorded"). The genetic sections therefore cover host susceptibility to TB in general, labelled as not specific to the esophagus.

The literature on ETB itself is small: case reports, case series and three reviews. Wherever a claim comes from general TB research rather than ETB research, I say so. I give ontology terms (HPO, GO, CL, UBERON, NCIT, CHEBI) as labels only. I did not retrieve their numeric IDs in this session, so they need to be mapped before use. 1. Disease Information

Overview. ETB is tuberculosis of the esophagus. The Disease Ontology defines its counterpart (DOID:7332) as "A gastrointestinal tuberculosis that involves formation of tuberculous ulcers located_in esophagus" (MGI/DO).

It is one of the rarest forms of extrapulmonary TB: - "Esophageal TB accounts for less than 0.2% of total TB cases" and about 2.8% of GI TB cases (Chaudhary et al. 2022, PMID 36742928, PMC9895252). - "Esophageal tuberculosis is rare, constituting about 0.3% of gastrointestinal tuberculosis" (Jain SS et al. 2013, PMID 24255751).

Most cases are secondary, spreading from nearby mediastinal lymph nodes or the lungs: "Secondary esophageal tuberculosis is much more common than primary TB. The most common source of secondary esophageal involvement is tuberculous mediastinal lymphadenitis" (PMID 36742928).

2. Etiology

Cause. The cause is infection with M. tuberculosis complex (M. tuberculosis in essentially all reported cases). M. bovis causes zoonotic TB, but I found no ETB case attributed to it.

There are three routes of spread (PMID 36742928; Ye et al. 2022, PMID 36498584): 1. Spread from contiguous structures. This is the most common route, usually from mediastinal and subcarinal nodes, less often from lung, larynx or the spine (Pott's disease). "In most cases, ET is caused by the rupture of infected mediastinal lymph nodes to the esophagus" (PMID 36498584). Devarbhavi et al. found that "Esophageal tuberculosis was always associated with spread from adjacent structures" (PMID 12665778). 2. Hematogenous or miliary spread. This is rare. 3. Primary infection. "Primary ET occurs when patients swallow sputum or food contaminated with tubercle bacilli", but it "very rarely occurs" (PMID 36498584).

Risk factors. None of these were studied in ETB cohorts. They are inferred from TB in general. - TB endemicity. Most series come from India, China and Pakistan. In Indian patients with persistent dysphagia, ETB made up "0.5% of all patients with persistent dysphagia" (Jain SK et al. 2002, PMID 11866263). - Male sex: "male to female ratio of 3.2:1" (PMID 36742928). However, the 311-patient systematic review found 50.5% were male (Birda et al. 2022, PMID 34482490). - HIV. In ETB, "less than 1% of cases of esophageal TB have been found to be HIV positive" (PMID 36742928). Case reports describe ETB in AIDS (Endoscopy 2006). In the four-patient series of Ye et al., "None of them had AIDS ... or other diseases that led to being immunocompromised" (PMID 36498584). - TNF-α blockade (general TB risk): "There were 70 reported cases of tuberculosis after treatment with infliximab, for a median of 12 weeks" (Keane et al. 2001, PMID 11596589).

Genetic risk factors (general TB, not ETB-specific): - TYK2 P1104A homozygosity: "may account for ∼1% of TB cases in Europeans". Frequency was "6/620, 1%" in TB patients versus "228/114,473, 0.2%" in controls (Kerner et al. 2019, PMID 31068474). Homozygosity impairs IL-23-dependent IFN-γ immunity, with an odds ratio of about 89 in endemic-area cohorts (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2018, PMID 30578352). - Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD). "299 unique mutations in 21 genes" have been reported. "61% ... had mutations of IL12RB1 (41%) or IFNGR1 (20%)" (Khavandegar et al. 2024, PMID 38341181). - In general, "human genetic factors contribute significantly to this interindividual variability" (Abel et al. 2014, PMID 24821915).

Protective factors. - BCG vaccination protects mainly against severe childhood forms. A systematic review of 18 trials found that absence of prior infection "is associated with higher efficacy of BCG" (Mangtani et al. 2014, PMID 24336911). Protection against infection "is inconsistent across different groups" (Pelzer et al. 2025, PMID 39709975). - No protective variants have been identified for ETB.

Gene–environment interaction. None has been studied for ETB. In general TB, defects in the IL-12/IL-23–IFN-γ axis interact with the level of exposure (PMID 30578352).

3. Phenotypes

Pooled frequencies across 311 patients from 22 studies (Birda et al. 2022, PMID 34482490): "Dysphagia (72.3%), odynophagia (22.4%) and chest pain (31.3%) were predominant symptoms." Chaudhary et al. reported that "Progressive dysphagia is the most common presenting symptom reported in more than 90% of patients" and that constitutional symptoms are "present in up to 65% of cases". They called anaemia "the most consistent physical finding" (PMID 36742928).

  • Infectious agent: Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. I did not retrieve the NCBI Taxonomy ID. Transmission is by airborne droplets. Secondary ETB arises from established thoracic infection, and swallowing infected sputum or food is proposed as the route for primary ETB (PMID 36498584).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (most common, secondary route):

  1. Inhaled M. tuberculosis infects alveolar macrophages and spreads by lymphatics to hilar, mediastinal and subcarinal lymph nodes. This step is established general TB biology.
  2. Within the nodes, ESX-1/RD1-dependent recruitment of macrophages leads to granuloma formation and expansion. Infected macrophages leave the granuloma and seed new ones (PMID 19135887, zebrafish model; for humans this is inferred).
  3. Granulomas fail to eradicate the bacteria (PMID 22517424). The result is caseous necrosis and matted, enlarged nodes, typically subcarinal (EUS: "enlarged lymph nodes adjacent to esophageal wall lesions in 18 cases" of 32; Puri et al., PMID 21777339).
  4. Enlarged nodes cause extrinsic compression of the mid-esophagus. Clinically this is a submucosal bulge with dysphagia.
  5. Caseating nodes infiltrate and rupture into the esophageal wall, which results in transmural granulomatous inflammation and mucosal ulceration (PMID 36498584; Baijal et al. via Semantic Scholar).
  6. The process then branches:
  7. 6a. Penetration into the airway or mediastinum leads to a tracheo- or broncho-esophageal fistula or esophagomediastinal sinus, which leads to coughing on swallowing and aspiration pneumonia (PMID 12665778).
  8. 6b. Erosion into the aorta leads to an aortoesophageal fistula and massive hematemesis (PMID 36498584).
  9. 6c. Healing by fibrosis, or traction by fibrotic nodes, leads to stricture or traction diverticulum (PMID 36742928).
  10. 6d. Systemic cytokine release (inferred) leads to fever, weight loss and anaemia.

Alternative initiating routes. Primary ETB begins with swallowed bacilli seeding the mucosa, then mucosal ulceration and granuloma. Hematogenous/miliary spread is rare. In both cases the chain continues from step 5.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary site: Esophagus, predominantly the middle third (UBERON label: "middle third of esophagus"; I did not retrieve the ID). "Mid-oesophagus was the commonest site of involvement (88%)" (PMID 34482490). Lower-third involvement is rare. Chaudhary et al. state that the upper third has not been reported (PMID 36742928). Abid et al., however, described "deep and large proximal esophageal ulcers" (PMID 12739552), so this point is disputed.
  • Secondary sites: mediastinal and subcarinal lymph nodes (76.5% on CT), lungs, trachea and bronchi (fistula), aorta (fistula), pleura, spine.
  • Body systems: digestive, respiratory, lymphatic/immune.
  • Tissue level: stratified squamous mucosa, submucosa, muscularis and adventitia. On EUS the lesion is "heterogeneous and hypoechoic ... with interrupted adventitia" (Yang et al. 2025, PMID 41244781).
  • Subcellular level: the bacteria live inside macrophage phagosomes (GO cellular component label: "phagocytic vesicle").
  • Laterality: Not applicable; this is a midline organ.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Mostly adults (mean 39 years; most series report means of 31 to 51). Onset is insidious or subacute.
  • Stages: No formal staging. EUS describes three nodal phases, from Phase I "homogeneous and hypoechoic" to later "heterogeneous, predominantly hypoechoic mass with local hyperechoic foci or strands, indistinct adventitia, and occasionally partial matting" (PMID 36498584). Endoscopic morphologies run roughly in order: bulge, then ulcer, then fistula or stricture.
  • Course: Without treatment it progresses, with complications such as fistula, bleeding or dissemination. With treatment, remission follows: "His dysphagia subsided within two weeks of starting therapy" (Paudel et al. 2021, PMID 34367833). Fistulas can close on medical therapy alone, as in "A follow-up endoscopy after two months revealed that the fistula was closed" (Salad et al. 2022, PMID 35298984). Endoscopic healing is usually documented by about 1 year (PMID 36498584).
  • Critical window: Diagnosing it before unnecessary esophagectomy, which can happen when ETB is mistaken for carcinoma (PMID 16252203).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Not applicable (acquired infection). Penetrance, anticipation, founder effects and carrier frequency are not applicable.
  • Epidemiology:
  • About 0.15–0.2% of TB cases or TB-related deaths, and 0.07–3% of GI TB (PMIDs 36742928, 36498584).
  • 0.30% prevalence in one Chinese surgical cohort (Ni et al. 2013, PMID 23991308).
  • 0.5–0.55% of patients with persistent dysphagia in endemic India (PMID 11866263).
  • Context: WHO estimated about 10.7 million people fell ill with TB in 2024, and 16% of notified cases were extrapulmonary (WHO Global TB Report 2025).
  • No population incidence rate exists for ETB itself.
  • Geography: Mostly TB-endemic South and East Asia, Africa and Latin America. One South African review found only 11 patients over 18 years (PMC2399217).
  • Sex ratio: Reported anywhere from about 1:1 (50.5% male, PMID 34482490) to 3.2:1 male (PMID 36742928).
  • Age distribution: 15 to 85 years.

10. Diagnostics

Endoscopy. Findings include "ulcers (59.9%), submucosal bulge (31.7%), extrinsic compression (24.8%) and pseudotumour (5.8%)" (PMID 34482490). The typical lesion is a "mid-esophageal linear ulceration with irregular infiltrated margins and grayish membranous necrotic base". Fistulas may show a "double-barreled appearance" (PMID 36498584).

Biopsy and histopathology. - Granuloma was found in 72.3% and AFB in 32.5% (PMID 34482490). - Mucosal biopsy sensitivity was 22% to 60.8% (PMID 36742928). - Ziehl–Neelsen staining is "positive in <25%". Culture has "80% and 98%" sensitivity and specificity but takes 6 to 8 weeks (PMID 36498584). - PCR on paraffin-embedded biopsies can establish the diagnosis (Fujiwara et al. 2003, PMID 12560925). - Cytology was "a very useful diagnostic parameter" (PMID 11866263).

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and EUS-guided sampling. - Puri et al. found ulcers in 56.25%, extrinsic bulge in 62.5%, and a definitive diagnosis in 27/32 (PMID 21777339). - Tang et al. reported an EUS-established diagnosis in 33/35 (94.3%) (PMID 28575247). - EUS helps when repeated biopsies fail: "All 9 patients failed to be diagnosed by at least two repeated biopsies" (Zhu et al. 2020, PMID 32859167). - EUS-guided fine-needle biopsy was diagnostic in 4 of 6 (PMID 41244781). EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration plus T-SPOT worked in a single case (PMID 38311428).

Imaging. - CT shows "mediastinal lymphadenopathy (76.5%) and oesophageal thickening (52.1%)" (PMID 34482490). Necrotic nodes are suggestive. - Chest radiograph is abnormal in 65% (PMID 12752007). - Barium swallow shows strictures, fistulas or traction diverticula.

Molecular tests. For extrapulmonary TB, Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert Ultra "may be helpful", but "Sensitivity varies across different extrapulmonary specimens" (Kohli et al. Cochrane 2021, PMID 33448348). Neither has been validated specifically on esophageal tissue.

Immunological tests. IGRA/T-SPOT and TST are supportive only.

Genetic testing. Not indicated, except for MSMD work-up when mycobacterial disease is unusually severe or recurrent (PMID 38341181).

Differential diagnosis: - Esophageal carcinoma ("Esophageal TB can mimic esophageal carcinoma on computed tomography scan", PMID 31572563; can lead to "unnecessary surgical therapy such as esophagectomy", PMID 16252203) - Crohn's disease of the esophagus (PMID 31572563) - CMV, HSV, candida and HIV-related idiopathic ulcers - Sarcoidosis - Lymphoma - Submucosal tumours (leiomyoma, GIST) - Mediastinal malignancy with fistula

Diagnostic criteria: There are no formal criteria. Diagnosis rests on histology (caseating granuloma) and/or microbiology (AFB, culture, PCR), with response to anti-TB therapy as supportive evidence.

Screening: Not applicable to ETB. Latent TB screening before TNF inhibitors is recommended (PMID 11596589).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival:
  • "Response to antitubercular therapy was excellent; 97.7% patients recovered and 2.3% patients died" (PMID 34482490).
  • Devarbhavi et al.: 9 of 10 patients recovered on drugs alone, and 1 died of disseminated disease (PMID 12665778).
  • South African series: 9 of 11 had good outcomes and 2 died (PMC2399217).
  • Chaudhary et al.: 1 of 4 local cases died of septicaemia within 6 days of starting therapy (PMID 36742928).
  • Ye et al.: "The prognosis is generally good if patients are diagnosed properly and receive anti-tubercular treatment promptly" (PMID 36498584).
  • Complications: hematemesis (8), stricture (7), traction diverticulum (4), esophagobronchial fistula (4), esophagoarterial/aortic fistula (4), esophagopleural fistula (2) (PMID 36742928, Table 2). Others include aspiration pneumonia, perforation and dissemination.
  • Poor prognostic factors (inferred from case series):
  • Disseminated or miliary disease
  • Aortoesophageal fistula
  • Delayed diagnosis
  • HIV co-infection: one cited series of 6 HIV-positive men with TB fistulas found that none improved on anti-TB therapy alone (cited secondarily via Redalyc; primary source not retrieved).
  • Prognostic biomarkers: None established.
  • Quality-of-life measures: No data.

12. Treatment

Drug therapy (NCIT labels: Antitubercular Agent; Isoniazid; Rifampin; Pyrazinamide; Ethambutol). - Standard regimen: 2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (HRZE), then 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin (HR). This is the general ATS/CDC/IDSA approach (Sotgiu et al. 2016, PMID 27587550; no abstract retrieved). - Duration: Ye et al. describe a 9-month course (2 months HRZE + 7 months HR) (PMID 36498584). Chaudhary et al. suggest 6 months for isolated disease and 12 months for disseminated disease, but that figure comes from an unverified part of the full text. Paediatric cases were treated for 6 months (PMID 24255751). - Shorter regimen: A 4-month rifapentine–moxifloxacin regimen was "noninferior to the standard 6-month regimen" for pulmonary TB (Dorman et al. 2021, PMID 33951360). It has not been studied in ETB. - Pharmacogenomics: NAT2 acetylator status affects isoniazid toxicity. This was not retrieved this session and is not ETB-specific.

Endoscopic and interventional treatment: - Esophageal stenting (11.4% of patients, PMID 34482490) - Over-the-scope clip closure of fistulas (success 33–77%) and argon plasma coagulation for bleeding (PMID 36498584) - Dilation of strictures

Surgery (NCIT labels: Esophagectomy; Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery). Surgery was needed in 14.5% (PMID 34482490). It is "indicated for failed medical therapy and complications" (PMID 36742928). VATS "is useful in improving rate of confirmative diagnosis, and relieving symptom of dysphagia" (PMID 23991308). The historic first report was esophagectomy for primary ETB (Fahmy et al. 1969, PMID 5821628).

Supportive care: nutritional support (enteral feeding when dysphagia or fistula is severe), proton-pump inhibitors, and treatment of aspiration pneumonia. These are general practice; no ETB-specific trials were retrieved.

Gene, cell, RNA, targeted or immunotherapies: Not applicable. No ETB clinical trials were identified.

Adverse events: Standard first-line TB drug toxicities apply (hepatotoxicity, optic neuritis with ethambutol). These are general knowledge, not retrieved this session.

Treatment algorithm: 1. Suspicion: mid-esophageal ulcer or bulge, mediastinal nodes, endemic exposure. 2. Endoscopic biopsy for histology, AFB, culture and PCR/Xpert. 3. If non-diagnostic, EUS-guided FNA or FNB. 4. Anti-TB therapy. 5. Endoscopic or surgical management for persistent fistula, bleeding or stricture.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • BCG vaccination; its protection against pulmonary disease varies (PMIDs 24336911, 39709975).
  • Airborne infection control and contact tracing.
  • Secondary prevention:
  • Treat latent TB infection, especially before TNF-inhibitor therapy (PMID 11596589).
  • Prompt treatment of mediastinal TB lymphadenitis, which removes the main source of secondary ETB (inferred).
  • Tertiary prevention: Early diagnosis prevents fistulas and unnecessary esophagectomy (PMIDs 16252203, 36742928). Endoscopic follow-up confirms healing.
  • Genetic counselling: Only relevant for MSMD families.
  • Public health: The WHO End TB programmes apply. Screening for ETB itself is not indicated.
