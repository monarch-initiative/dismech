Factor XIII B Subunit Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0013190 Pathograph 15 Show in embeddings browser Bleeding Disorder Coagulation Disorder Rare Bleeding Disorder

Congenital deficiency of the non-catalytic B subunit of coagulation factor XIII, caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in F13B. Plasma factor XIII circulates as the heterotetramer FXIII-A2B2: the catalytic A subunit is made by cells of haematopoietic origin and the B subunit by hepatocytes, and the two meet only in plasma. FXIII-B carries and protects FXIII-A, so when the B subunit is missing the A subunit is cleared from the circulation quickly and a secondary FXIII-A deficiency follows even though F13A1 is intact. FXIII-B also binds fibrinogen and speeds thrombin activation of FXIII-A on the clot, so its loss slows fibrin cross-linking by a second route. The clinical picture is a milder version of A-subunit disease. Plasma FXIII activity is low but usually not absent, and bleeding tends to be provoked or late-presenting rather than the umbilical-stump and intracranial haemorrhage of infancy that defines severe A-subunit deficiency. Routine screening tests (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, thrombin time) are normal, so diagnosis requires a FXIII activity assay followed by separate A and B subunit antigen measurement and F13B sequencing. The distinction matters for treatment: recombinant FXIII-A2 cannot replace the missing carrier and is not appropriate, whereas plasma-derived concentrate supplies both subunits. Severe B-subunit deficiency is much rarer than A-subunit deficiency; heterozygous F13B variants are commoner and cause mild or no bleeding.

Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance HP:0000007
Severe deficiency requires two F13B pathogenic alleles, homozygous or compound heterozygous. Heterozygous F13B variants are found more often than biallelic ones and are associated with mild FXIII deficiency; the reviews describe such heterozygotes as generally asymptomatic. Whether their bleeding is specifically provoked by trauma or surgery is stated for heterozygous FXIII deficiency in general rather than for F13B heterozygotes, so it is not asserted here.

Pathophysiology

9
Biallelic F13B Loss-of-Function Variants
Homozygous or compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in F13B, which encodes the B subunit of factor XIII, a protein built from ten sushi domains held together by disulphide bonds. Reported variants include premature-termination alleles (the AAC insertion at Tyr-80 that is a founder allele in Italian families, and a splice-boundary deletion that is a founder allele in Japanese families), frameshifts, and missense variants that remove or sit next to a structural disulphide bond. Most of them reduce the amount of FXIII-B that leaves the cell rather than producing a secreted but inactive protein.

Differential Diagnoses

2

Conditions with similar clinical presentations that must be differentiated from Factor XIII B Subunit Deficiency:

Factor XIII A subunit deficiency
Overlapping Features The far commoner form, about 95% of congenital FXIII deficiency, caused by biallelic F13A1 variants. It is separated by measuring both subunit antigens, and the two patterns are not mirror images: in A-subunit deficiency FXIII-A antigen is absent while FXIII-B antigen is normal, whereas here FXIII-B antigen is absent and FXIII-A antigen is secondarily reduced rather than normal. The distinction decides treatment, because recombinant FXIII-A2 works for A-subunit disease and not for this one. Curated as Factor_XIII_A_Subunit_Deficiency.
Acquired factor XIII deficiency
Overlapping Features Reduced FXIII activity from autoantibodies, or from consumption or reduced synthesis in critical illness, surgery or malignancy. Distinguished by age and tempo of onset, by a mixing study and inhibitor titration, and by F13A1/F13B sequencing being normal. Worth keeping in view in this disease specifically, because the one well-described patient developed anti-FXIII-B alloantibodies after replacement and so carried an acquired inhibitor on top of a congenital deficiency.

description: >-
  Congenital deficiency of the non-catalytic B subunit of coagulation factor
  XIII, caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in F13B. Plasma factor XIII
  circulates as the heterotetramer FXIII-A2B2: the catalytic A subunit is made
  by cells of haematopoietic origin and the B subunit by hepatocytes, and the two
  meet only in plasma. FXIII-B carries and protects FXIII-A, so when the B
  subunit is missing the A subunit is cleared from the circulation quickly and a
  secondary FXIII-A deficiency follows even though F13A1 is intact. FXIII-B also
  binds fibrinogen and speeds thrombin activation of FXIII-A on the clot, so its
  loss slows fibrin cross-linking by a second route.

  The clinical picture is a milder version of A-subunit disease. Plasma FXIII
  activity is low but usually not absent, and bleeding tends to be provoked or
  late-presenting rather than the umbilical-stump and intracranial haemorrhage
  of infancy that defines severe A-subunit deficiency. Routine screening tests
  (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, thrombin time) are
  normal, so diagnosis requires a FXIII activity assay followed by separate A and
  B subunit antigen measurement and F13B sequencing. The distinction matters for
  treatment: recombinant FXIII-A2 cannot replace the missing carrier and is not
  appropriate, whereas plasma-derived concentrate supplies both subunits. Severe
  B-subunit deficiency is much rarer than A-subunit deficiency; heterozygous F13B
  variants are commoner and cause mild or no bleeding.

1. Disease Information

Factor XIII B Subunit Deficiency is a congenital rare bleeding disorder (RBD) within the broader entity of congenital Factor XIII deficiency (FXIIID). FXIII is the final enzyme of the coagulation cascade: a Ca²⁺-dependent transglutaminase that covalently cross-links fibrin to stabilize the clot. Congenital FXIII deficiency arises from biallelic variants in either of two genes — F13A1 (catalytic A subunit) or F13B (carrier B subunit). The B-subunit form is defined by defects in F13B.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. The disease is caused by biallelic germline loss-of-function variants in F13B (chromosome 1q31.3). It is inherited in a strictly autosomal recessive manner; both alleles must be defective to produce clinically significant deficiency (PMID: 37314674). There is no infectious or primary environmental cause of the congenital form. (A separate, non-inherited acquired FXIII deficiency exists — autoimmune or consumptive — but that is a distinct entity from this Mendelian B-subunit disease.)

Genetic risk factors. The disease-causing variants in F13B are themselves the causal risk factors; reported classes include missense (many causing secretion/folding defects — e.g., disruption of structural disulfides of sushi domains), nonsense, splice-site, and frameshift variants (PMID: 31013569; PMID: 26247044). Consanguinity is the dominant risk factor for being born affected, since it greatly increases the chance of homozygosity for a rare recessive allele. In pediatric congenital FXIII cohorts, consanguinity rates reach ~75% (PMID: 37314674).

"Consanguinity was present in 15 (75%)" — PMID: 37314674

Environmental / modifier risk factors. No environmental exposures cause the congenital disease. Bleeding events are precipitated by trauma, surgery, childbirth, and the neonatal umbilical separation. Heterozygous carriers are usually asymptomatic but can bleed after a hemostatic challenge (surgery, trauma, delivery).

Protective factors. No specific protective genetic alleles are documented for this ultra-rare disorder. The principal "protective" circumstance is residual FXIII-A activity: because B-subunit loss only accelerates A-subunit clearance rather than abolishing A-subunit production, B-subunit deficiency tends to be milder than severe A-subunit deficiency (PMID: 26247044; PMID: 18224415).

"Defects in the F13B gene (Factor XIIIB subunit) occur more frequently in mild FXIII deficiency patients than in severe FXIII deficiency" — PMID: 26247044

Gene–environment interactions. The relevant interaction is genotype × hemostatic challenge: the underlying genetic deficiency is silent under routine screening and manifests when the coagulation system is stressed (neonatal umbilical healing, surgery, trauma, pregnancy/decidual bleeding).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene: F13B (HGNC:3534; chromosome 1q31.3; UniProt P05160). It encodes the plasma FXIII B subunit, a secreted glycoprotein of 10 repetitive sushi (CCP) domains, each stabilized by two internal disulfide bonds (PMID: 20880254). The B subunit is synthesized in hepatocytes and secreted into plasma.

"FXIII-B is a glycoprotein consisting of 10 repetitive sushi domains each held together by two internal disulfide bonds" — PMID: 20880254

Variant classification and type: Reported F13B variants span missense, nonsense, splice-site, and frameshift classes; many missense variants cause secretion or folding defects, and disruption of the structural disulfide bonds of the sushi domains has functional consequences for a rare bleeding disorder (PMID: 31013569; PMID: 26247044). Pathogenic variants act by loss of function (reduced/absent carrier subunit). The first sushi domain is important for A–B complex formation. Origin is germline; there is no somatic component. Per ACMG/AMP, null (nonsense/frameshift/splice) variants in this loss-of-function gene are typically classified pathogenic/likely pathogenic; many missense variants remain VUS pending functional (secretion) data.

Functional consequence: Loss of the B subunit does not abolish A-subunit synthesis but removes its carrier/stabilizer, so FXIII-A is cleared faster and plasma FXIII activity drops secondarily — a loss-of-function, carrier-deficiency mechanism confirmed by the knockout mouse (Section 6, PMID: 18224415).

Modifier genes: The principal modifier of plasma FXIII level in B-subunit deficiency is the status of the A-subunit pool itself (how quickly uncarried A is cleared). No independent modifier loci are well established for this subtype.

Epigenetic / chromosomal abnormalities: None are implicated. This is a single-gene point/indel mutation disorder; no recurrent aneuploidy, translocation, or methylation signature is associated.

Allele frequencies: Pathogenic F13B alleles are individually very rare in gnomAD; population carrier frequency of FXIII deficiency alleles is elevated in consanguineous populations (see Section 9). Precise per-variant gnomAD frequencies were not quantified in this investigation.

5. Environmental Information

The congenital disease has no environmental, lifestyle, or infectious cause — it is purely genetic. Environmental factors act only as triggers of bleeding events in an already-deficient individual: trauma, surgery, and childbirth. For completeness, an entirely separate acquired FXIII deficiency (not this Mendelian entity) can be caused by autoimmune inhibitors (e.g., SLE-associated) or consumption (e.g., major surgery, dilutional coagulopathy); those are distinct from B-subunit deficiency and are mentioned only to aid differential diagnosis. No toxins, radiation, pollutants, dietary factors, or pathogens cause the inherited B-subunit disease.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function variants in F13B (missense/secretion-defect, nonsense, splice, or frameshift) lead to absent or severely reduced secretion of the FXIII B (carrier) subunit from hepatocytes.
  2. Loss of circulating B subunit results in failure to chaperone and protect the catalytic FXIII-A subunit in plasma (the B subunit normally prevents fast clearance of FXIII-A and binds it to fibrinogen γ′-chain).
  3. Unprotected FXIII-A leads to accelerated clearance of FXIII-A from the circulation, so plasma FXIII activity falls secondarily (partial, not complete, loss — inferred from the milder phenotype and directly demonstrated in the B-subunit knockout mouse, which shows markedly reduced but detectable cross-linking).
  4. Low plasma FXIII activity results in deficient Ca²⁺-dependent transglutaminase action, i.e., reduced covalent cross-linking of fibrin γ- and α-chains and reduced cross-linking of α₂-antiplasmin to fibrin.
  5. Deficient fibrin cross-linking leads to mechanically weak clots; deficient α₂-antiplasmin cross-linking leads to loss of the principal antifibrinolytic mechanism → premature clot lysis.
  6. Branch A (hemostasis): unstable, fibrinolysis-prone clots result in delayed/recurrent bleeding, soft-tissue and intracranial hemorrhage.
  7. Branch B (wound healing): failure to cross-link the provisional wound matrix results in impaired leukocyte interaction and poor wound healing / impaired mucosal repair.
  8. Branch C (angiogenesis): loss of FXIIIa action on endothelial VEGFR-2 / αvβ3 integrin results in failure to downregulate antiangiogenic thrombospondin-1 → impaired angiogenesis/neovascularization (inferred contribution to healing/pregnancy defects).
  9. Branch D (reproduction): loss of FXIII's role in placental attachment results in recurrent miscarriage and antenatal hemorrhage.

Supporting detail

Molecular / biochemical defect. Activated FXIII (FXIIIa) is a Ca²⁺-dependent transglutaminase (EC 2.3.2.13) that forms ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine isopeptide bonds. Its substrates include fibrin and α₂-antiplasmin:

"These include antifibrinolytic proteins, with cross-linking of α2-antiplasmin to fibrin, and potentially fibrinogen, being the principal mechanism(s) whereby plasmin-mediated clot degradation is minimised" — PMID: 25652913

Angiogenesis. FXIIIa engages endothelial signaling:

"FXIII also acts on endothelial cell VEGFR-2 and αvβ3 integrin, which ultimately leads to downregulation of the antiangiogenic protein thrombospondin-1, promoting angiogenesis and neovascularisation" — PMID: 25652913

Placentation. FXIII is required for placental attachment, explaining the high miscarriage rate:

"factor XIII is involved in placental attachment" — PMID: 32360976

Tissue repair. In an experimental colitis model, FXIII-deficient mice failed to resolve mucosal damage, and exogenous FXIII-A improved healing — confirming FXIII's role in mucosal/tissue repair (PMID: 26098308).

Upstream vs downstream. Upstream: the F13B mutation and loss of carrier subunit. Midstream: accelerated FXIII-A clearance and low plasma FXIII activity. Downstream: deficient cross-linking → unstable clots, impaired healing, impaired placentation → clinical bleeding and pregnancy loss.

Cell types / processes involved: hepatocytes (CL:0000182) synthesize the B subunit; the A subunit's major plasma pool derives from cells of megakaryocyte/platelet lineage (CL:0000556 megakaryocyte; CL:0000233 platelet) per lineage-tracing studies (PMID: 26312861); endothelial cells (CL:0000115) are the target of FXIIIa's angiogenic action.

Suggested ontology terms: GO:0007596 (blood coagulation), GO:0018149 (peptide cross-linking), GO:0042730 (fibrinolysis), GO:0001525 (angiogenesis), GO:0042060 (wound healing), GO:0003810 (protein-glutamine γ-glutamyltransferase/transglutaminase activity); CHEBI:29108 (calcium(2+)).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary site: the blood / plasma / circulatory system (UBERON:0000178 blood; UBERON:0001969 blood plasma) — the compartment where the FXIII-A₂B₂ complex operates.
  • Organs affected by bleeding: brain (UBERON:0000955) — intracranial hemorrhage is the most feared complication; skin and subcutaneous soft tissue (UBERON:0002097) — ecchymoses, hematomas; mucosa (oral/nasal); umbilical stump in the neonate; spleen (rare spontaneous rupture reported); uterus/placenta (UBERON:0000995 / UBERON:0001987) — miscarriage, decidual bleeding, postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Body systems: hematologic/cardiovascular (primary), nervous system (secondary via ICH), reproductive system (secondary via miscarriage/PPH), integumentary (bruising/wound healing).
  • Tissue/cell level: hepatocytes (synthesis of B subunit), endothelial cells, platelets/megakaryocytes (A-subunit pool), leukocytes (wound matrix interaction).
  • Subcellular: the mature protein acts extracellularly (secreted; GO:0005576 extracellular region / GO:0005615 extracellular space); synthesis involves the hepatocyte ER/secretory pathway (relevant to missense secretion-defect variants).
  • Lateralization: not applicable — bleeding is systemic and site-dependent on challenge, not lateralized.

8. Temporal Development

Onset: congenital — the deficiency is present from birth. Classic first manifestation is neonatal delayed umbilical stump bleeding. In milder B-subunit disease, presentation may be later (post-traumatic or post-surgical bleeding). Cohort median symptom onset ~6 months; median diagnosis ~1 year (PMID: 37314674).

Course: chronic, lifelong, with an episodic bleeding pattern (events provoked by trauma, surgery, delivery; spontaneous intracranial bleeding is the dangerous exception). Severity is generally stable over life (set by genotype), not progressive.

Critical periods / windows of opportunity: the neonatal period (umbilical bleeding, early ICH risk) and pregnancy (miscarriage, decidual and postpartum bleeding) are windows of heightened vulnerability where prophylaxis is especially important. The long FXIII half-life (~7–14 days) provides a wide therapeutic window allowing infrequent (e.g., monthly) replacement dosing.

Remission: no spontaneous remission of the genetic deficiency; bleeding is controlled (treatment-induced "functional remission") by replacement therapy.

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: autosomal recessive; biallelic F13B loss-of-function required. Heterozygotes have mild deficiency (activity ~20–60%) and are usually asymptomatic but may bleed after hemostatic challenge (PMID: 37832789).

"heterozygosity at FXIII genetic loci results in mild FXIII deficiency, characterized by circulating FXIII activity levels ranging from 20% to 60%" — PMID: 37832789

Penetrance / expressivity: biallelic severe disease is highly penetrant for bleeding risk; expressivity is variable (B-subunit form milder than severe A-subunit form). No genetic anticipation (not a repeat-expansion disorder).

Founder effects / consanguinity: consanguinity drives prevalence in Iran (Sistan & Baluchestan Province has the highest global incidence), Tunisia, Pakistan, and Sudan. Demographics: affected individuals cluster in consanguineous populations (Middle East, South Asia, North Africa). Sex ratio in congenital FXIII cohorts is roughly balanced (male:female ~1.2–1.5:1), though women carry additional reproductive morbidity.

10. Diagnostics

Why it is missed: routine coagulation screening is normal. PT, aPTT, and fibrinogen do not detect FXIII deficiency because FXIII acts after fibrin polymerization. This causes frequent underdiagnosis and decades-long diagnostic delay (recurrent ICH despite normal screens is a recurring theme in case reports).

"Routine coagulation screening tests are normal and diagnosis relies on quantitative FXIII activity assays with or without antigenic phenotyping" — PMID: 41583548

ISTH/SSC diagnostic algorithm (PMID: 24286209):

  1. Quantitative FXIII activity assay (ammonia-release / amine-incorporation) — markedly reduced.
  2. Immunological antigen assays measuring total FXIII, FXIII-A antigen, FXIII-B antigen, and the A₂B₂ complex.
  3. Inhibitor testing (cross-mixing / 5-step mixing) to exclude acquired autoimmune deficiency.
  4. Plasma-derived FXIII dosing / recovery test, with molecular (DNA sequencing) confirmation of F13B.

Subunit assignment (the key step for this subtype): - FXIII-A deficiency: absent FXIII-A antigen, normal FXIII-B antigen. - FXIII-B deficiency: low/absent FXIII-B antigen with secondarily reduced FXIII-A.

"Four immunological assays detected essentially no FXIII protein, FXIII-A antigen, and A2 B2 antigen, but normal FXIII-B antigen" — PMID: 24286209 (illustrates the mirror-image logic that identifies the subunit at fault)

Genetic testing: single-gene F13B sequencing (or a rare-bleeding-disorder / FXIII gene panel) confirms the diagnosis and enables carrier and prenatal testing. WES/WGS can be used when the phenotype is unclear. Legacy test: the urea / acetic-acid clot solubility test is an insensitive legacy screen (only abnormal at very low activity) but is still used where activity assays are unavailable.

Differential diagnosis: von Willebrand disease, platelet function disorders, other rare factor deficiencies, and acquired FXIII deficiency (autoimmune/consumptive) — all distinguished by specific FXIII activity, antigen phenotyping, inhibitor testing, and F13B sequencing.

Screening: cascade (family) genetic screening and carrier testing in affected consanguineous families; prenatal/antenatal diagnosis available where the familial mutation is known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Dominant risk — intracranial hemorrhage (ICH): ICH is the most severe and life-threatening manifestation and the leading cause of death in severe congenital FXIII deficiency, affecting up to ~25–35% of severe patients untreated. In a Tunisian cohort of 7 severe patients, 3/7 had intracranial bleeding and 1 died of ICH (PMID: 28704210).

"Intracranial hemorrhage represents its most severe and life-threatening manifestation" — PMID: 42230344

"One patient was died because of intracranial hemorrhage" — PMID: 28704210

With prophylaxis: regular FXIII replacement reduces the annualized bleeding rate to essentially zero, converting a high-mortality disease into a manageable chronic condition with near-normal life expectancy.

"Prophylaxis was effective with an annualized bleeding rate of zero" — PMID: 28581691

Subtype note: B-subunit deficiency tends to be clinically milder than severe A-subunit deficiency because residual A-subunit activity persists.

Morbidity: recurrent bleeding, poor wound healing, reproductive loss (miscarriage, postpartum hemorrhage). Prognostic factors: baseline FXIII activity, adherence to prophylaxis, and prior ICH.

12. Treatment

Principle: lifelong FXIII replacement prophylaxis, feasible because FXIII has a long half-life (~7–14 days), allowing infrequent (often monthly) dosing.

Genotype-guided product selection — the critical point for B-subunit deficiency:

Product Composition Corrects FXIII-A deficiency? Corrects FXIII-B deficiency?
Plasma-derived FXIII concentrate (Fibrogammin/Corifact) A₂B₂ Yes Yes — required
Recombinant FXIII-A₂ (catridecacog; NovoThirteen/Tretten) A subunit only Yes No
Cryoprecipitate / FFP Contains FXIII Yes (older option) Yes (older option)

"recombinant FXIII‑A2 is appropriate for F13A1 defects but not patients with F13B variants" — PMID: 41583548

The rationale: recombinant A₂ lacks the carrier B subunit, so in B-subunit deficiency the infused A would itself be rapidly cleared; only the A₂B₂ plasma-derived concentrate supplies the missing carrier. Plasma-derived FXIII is therefore the mainstay for B-subunit deficiency. Its safety profile is favorable — in FXIII-knockout mice it corrected clot kinetics/strength and was not associated with thrombus formation up to 350 IU/kg (PMID: 27069637).

Dosing thresholds (pregnancy): maintain plasma FXIII activity ≥2–3%, ideally >10% to prevent decidual bleeding and miscarriage, escalating from ~250 IU/week early to ~500 IU/week after ~23 weeks, targeting >20–30% for labor (PMID: 17371605).

"the level of plasma A-subunit of factor XIII antigen (XIIIA-Ag) or factor XIII activity (XIII-act) must be at least 2%-3%, and, if possible, higher than 10% to prevent decidual bleeding and miscarriage during the pregnancy" — PMID: 17371605

"factor XIIIA concentrate is better than fresh frozen plasma or cryoprecipitate for replacement therapy" — PMID: 17371605

Acute bleeds/surgery: replacement before procedures and to treat active bleeding; perioperative FXIII concentrate enables safe major surgery. Suggested NCIT intervention terms: coagulation factor replacement therapy; "Factor XIII Concentrate"; prophylactic therapy.

Advanced therapeutics: no gene, cell, or RNA therapies are approved for this disorder; management is replacement-based. Genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis are offered for family planning. Thrombotic risk of replacement is low but non-zero and individualized dosing (avoiding full normalization) is advised in high-risk contexts (PMID: 42527937).

13. Prevention

Because the disease is congenital and genetic, "prevention" operates at two levels:

  • Primary (preventing affected births): genetic counseling, carrier/cascade screening, and prenatal diagnosis in affected consanguineous families where the F13B mutation is known; preimplantation genetic diagnosis is theoretically applicable.
  • Secondary/tertiary (preventing complications in affected individuals): lifelong prophylactic FXIII replacement to prevent spontaneous ICH and other bleeds; peri-procedural prophylaxis before surgery/delivery; continuous FXIII replacement throughout pregnancy to prevent miscarriage and postpartum hemorrhage.

"our data confirmed the high incidence of miscarriage in women with factor XIII deficiency. Good outcome of pregnancies required prophylaxis" — PMID: 32360976

No vaccine or environmental/public-health intervention is relevant. Patient education and medical-alert documentation help avoid diagnostic anchoring (e.g., misdiagnosis as von Willebrand disease).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Model species: Mus musculus (NCBI Taxon 10090) — engineered knockouts exist (Section 15). Orthologous gene: mouse F13b.
  • Naturally occurring disease: No well-characterized spontaneous FXIII-B deficiency is catalogued in companion animals or wildlife in the reviewed literature; the disorder is primarily studied in humans and engineered mice. (OMIA was not confirmed to list a natural animal FXIII-B deficiency in this investigation.)
  • Evolutionary conservation: the FXIII-A₂B₂ system and the carrier/stabilizer function of the B subunit are conserved in mouse, where knockout recapitulates the human carrier mechanism — supporting conservation of the disease mechanism.
  • Zoonotic potential: none — this is a non-transmissible inherited disorder.

15. Model Organisms

Principal model — FXIII-B knockout mouse (Souri et al., PMID: 18224415): targeted deletion of mouse F13b (and separately F13a).

Phenotype recapitulation: Homozygous F13b⁻/⁻ mice are viable and fertile but have prolonged bleeding times and markedly reduced (not absent) plasma fibrin cross-linking and thrombin-dependent amine incorporation — milder than F13a⁻/⁻ mice, in which cross-linking is undetectable. Subunit gene expression is regulated independently.

"homozygous mice of either FXIII-A- or FXIII-B-deficiency did have prolonged bleeding times" — PMID: 18224415

"fibrin-crosslinking in plasma were undetectable in the FXIII-A-deficient mice and markedly reduced in the FXIII-B-deficient mice" — PMID: 18224415

Direct mechanistic validation (the carrier mechanism): injecting recombinant human FXIII-B into F13b⁻/⁻ mice raised plasma FXIII-A levels, fibrin cross-linking, and amine-incorporation activity — demonstrating that the B subunit maintains/stabilizes circulating FXIII-A.

"rFXIII-B was injected into FXIII-B-deficient mice, FXIII-A levels, fibrin crosslinking, and amine-incorporation activities increased in their plasma, indicating that FXIII-B assisted the maintenance of FXIII-A levels in the circulation" — PMID: 18224415

Related models / applications: FXIII-A knockout mice reveal tissue-repair functions — e.g., impaired resolution of mucosal damage in DSS colitis, rescued by recombinant FXIII-A (PMID: 26098308) — and a floxed-F13a1 mouse mapped the cellular source of plasma FXIII-A to the megakaryocyte/platelet lineage (PMID: 26312861). Model limitation: the F13b⁻/⁻ mouse is milder than the human severe phenotype and does not reproduce the full spectrum of human obstetric complications. Resources: MGI for mouse F13b/F13a1 alleles.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Subtype-specific data scarcity. Only ~11 congenital FXIII-B deficiency cases were reported historically (PMID: 23407795). Much phenotype/epidemiology data is extrapolated from the far more common A-subunit form; true B-subtype frequencies, penetrance, and natural history remain imprecise.
  2. No dedicated B-subunit QoL or natural-history studies. QoL and disability metrics are inferred from the general FXIII literature.
  3. Allele-frequency precision. Individual pathogenic F13B allele frequencies in gnomAD were not quantified in this investigation.
  4. Mechanistic branches partly inferred. The angiogenesis (thrombospondin-1) and placentation contributions to the B-subtype phenotype are supported mechanistically but not specifically demonstrated in B-subunit-deficient humans.
  5. Model limitations. The F13b⁻/⁻ mouse is milder than severe human disease and does not fully model human obstetric complications.
  6. No OMIA-confirmed natural animal disease was identified for the B subtype.
