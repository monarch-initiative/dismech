FOXP2-Related Speech and Language Disorder

Mendelian MONDO:0011184 Pathograph 23 Show in embeddings browser specific language disorder hereditary neurological disease

FOXP2-related speech and language disorder (FOXP2-SLD; speech-language disorder type 1, SPCH1) is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder caused by heterozygous disruption of FOXP2, a forkhead-box transcription factor, through intragenic missense or truncating variants, whole- or partial-gene deletions at 7q31, translocations through the gene, or loss of the paternal FOXP2 allele in maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7. It was the first monogenic cause identified for a developmental speech disorder, through the R553H forkhead-domain substitution segregating in the three-generation KE family. The core phenotype is childhood apraxia of speech (developmental verbal dyspraxia), a disorder of planning and programming the sequential orofacial movements of speech, accompanied by orofacial (oromotor) dyspraxia, often dysarthria, receptive and expressive language impairment, and reading and spelling difficulties, with nonverbal cognition relatively preserved. Affected KE-family members show bilateral grey matter changes in the caudate nucleus, cerebellum and inferior frontal gyrus and underactivation of Broca's area and the putamen, matching the corticostriatal and olivocerebellar sites where FOXP2 is expressed during brain development. Mice heterozygous for the equivalent Foxp2 R552H substitution have abnormal striatal and cerebellar synaptic plasticity and impaired motor-skill learning. Scope: this entry covers childhood apraxia of speech caused by FOXP2 disruption only, which is how MONDO:0011184 is defined (MONDO asserts FOXP2 as its causal gene). Childhood apraxia of speech as a clinical diagnosis is genetically heterogeneous, and apraxia of speech arising from other genes or copy-number variants is treated here as a differential diagnosis rather than as part of this entry.

Inheritance

1
Autosomal dominant inheritance HP:0000006
FOXP2-SLD is autosomal dominant. About half of diagnosed individuals carry a de novo variant and the remainder inherited it from an affected or variably affected parent, as in the KE family where the disorder segregates over three generations. When the lesion is loss of the paternal allele (paternal 7q31 deletion or maternal uniparental disomy 7), the absence of paternal FOXP2 rather than the dominant-transmission pattern explains the phenotype.
Autosomal dominant inheritance Expressivity: VARIABLE
Parent-of-origin effect: Loss of the paternal FOXP2 copy (paternal deletion or maternal UPD7) causes the disorder, and one study reported the maternal allele to be relatively underexpressed. A parent-of-origin effect is not established: a mother and son with a maternally transmitted deletion were both affected, and a later allele-specific expression study (PMID:23033221, which has no abstract to quote) reported that FOXP2 is not imprinted.

Pathophysiology

7
Loss of the Paternal FOXP2 Allele
Mechanism confidence: Established
One route to FOXP2 deficiency is absence of the paternal copy, through a paternally derived 7q31 deletion or translocation, or through maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7 (which also causes Silver-Russell syndrome). Losing the paternal allele lowers FOXP2 dosage; whether the maternal allele is also relatively underexpressed (imprinting) is disputed, since one study found maternal underexpression and a later one found FOXP2 is not imprinted (PMID:23033221, no abstract).

Dysregulation of FOXP2 Neural Target Genes
Mechanism confidence: Provisional
FOXP2 binds promoters in neural chromatin and either represses or activates the genes it occupies; its targets include genes acting in synaptic plasticity, neurodevelopment, neurotransmission and axon guidance. FOXP2 binds and strongly down-regulates CNTNAP2, a neurexin-family gene expressed in developing cortex. Which targets mediate the speech phenotype is not established, so no edge is drawn from this node to the circuit-level nodes.

Abnormal Corticostriatal and Olivocerebellar Circuit Development
Mechanism confidence: Established
FOXP2 is expressed during brain development in the cortical plate, basal ganglia, thalamus, inferior olives and cerebellum. Affected KE-family members have bilateral grey matter reductions in the caudate nucleus, cerebellum (notably lobule VIIa Crus I) and inferior frontal gyrus, with increased grey matter in the planum temporale, and underactivate Broca's area, its right homolog and the putamen during language tasks. Caudate and Crus I volumes correlate with oral praxis and nonword repetition performance, linking these motor-related circuits to the speech phenotype.

Speech Motor Planning and Programming Deficit
Mechanism confidence: Established
The core deficit is in planning or programming the sequential orofacial movements of speech: production, sequencing, timing and stress of sounds and the sequencing of sounds into syllables and syllables into words. It extends to nonspeech orofacial praxis and interferes secondarily with phonology, grammar and literacy.

  FOXP2-related speech and language disorder (FOXP2-SLD; speech-language
  disorder type 1, SPCH1) is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder
  caused by heterozygous disruption of FOXP2, a forkhead-box transcription
  factor, through intragenic missense or truncating variants, whole- or
  partial-gene deletions at 7q31, translocations through the gene, or loss of
  the paternal FOXP2 allele in maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7. It
  was the first monogenic cause identified for a developmental speech disorder,
  through the R553H forkhead-domain substitution segregating in the
  three-generation KE family. The core phenotype is childhood apraxia of speech
  (developmental verbal dyspraxia), a disorder of planning and programming the
  sequential orofacial movements of speech, accompanied by orofacial (oromotor)
  dyspraxia, often dysarthria, receptive and expressive language impairment,
  and reading and spelling difficulties, with nonverbal cognition relatively
  preserved. Affected KE-family members show bilateral grey matter changes in
  the caudate nucleus, cerebellum and inferior frontal gyrus and underactivation
  of Broca's area and the putamen, matching the corticostriatal and
  olivocerebellar sites where FOXP2 is expressed during brain development. Mice
  heterozygous for the equivalent Foxp2 R552H substitution have abnormal
  striatal and cerebellar synaptic plasticity and impaired motor-skill learning.

  disruption only, which is how MONDO:0011184 is defined (MONDO asserts FOXP2 as
  its causal gene). Childhood apraxia of speech as a clinical diagnosis is
  genetically heterogeneous, and apraxia of speech arising from other genes or
  copy-number variants is treated here as a differential diagnosis rather than
  as part of this entry.

FOXP2-Related Speech and Language Disorder (FOXP2-SLD; SPCH1): research report

Causal gene: FOXP2 (forkhead box P2), chromosome 7q31.1. The OMIM gene entry is 605317; the phenotype entry is OMIM:602081, "Speech-language disorder 1; SPCH1". The disorder is caused by heterozygous loss of function of FOXP2. This can be a sequence variant, a deletion of all or part of the gene, a structural rearrangement, or, at the 7q31 level, maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7. Every claim below concerns this gene and MONDO ID.

1. Disease information

Overview. FOXP2-SLD is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder. Its core feature is childhood apraxia of speech (CAS), a disorder of planning and programming speech movements. It usually occurs with dysarthria, impaired expressive (and often receptive) language, literacy difficulties, and impaired orofacial praxis. Nonverbal cognition is usually relatively spared.

The GeneReviews chapter (Morgan, Fisher, Scheffer & Hildebrand; posted 2016-06-23, updated 2023-01-26; PMID:27336128, NBK368474) defines the disorder as caused by heterozygous FOXP2 pathogenic variants, including whole- or partial-gene deletions. It names CAS as the core feature, notes autistic features in some individuals, and states that there is no cure and that care is supportive.

2. Etiology

Causal factors (genetic). - The KE family. In this three-generation family, about half the members have a severe speech disorder. It was first described by Hurst et al. 1990 (PMID:2332125): "in which 16 members have a severe…"; "Intelligence and hearing are normal." The locus (SPCH1) was later mapped to 7q31. - Gene discovery. Lai et al. 2001 (PMID:11586359) identified FOXP2: - "a severe speech and language disorder is transmitted as an autosomal-dominant monogenic trait" - The gene "is directly disrupted by the translocation breakpoint in CS", an unrelated child with a t(5;7) translocation. - The KE family carries the missense variant p.Arg553His (R553H) in the forkhead DNA-binding domain. - First truncating variant. MacDermot et al. 2005 (PMID:15877281) screened probands with verbal dyspraxia and found "a heterozygous nonsense mutation that yields a dramatically truncated protein product" (p.Arg328Ter), segregating with speech difficulties. - Other mechanisms: - 7q31 deletions that include FOXP2: Zeesman et al. 2006 (PMID:16470794): "Her deletion is on the paternally inherited chromosome and includes the FOXP2 gene". Morison et al. 2025 (PMID:40714749): "Deletion size ranged from 6.8 to 15.2 Mb." - Maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7, or paternal deletion. Feuk et al. 2006 (PMID:17033973): "Of these individuals with DVD, all 12 for whom parental DNA was available showed absence of a paternal copy of FOXP2." - Small intragenic deletions: Turner et al. 2013 (PMID:23918746).

Genetic risk factors. In a monogenic sense, the risk factor is a pathogenic FOXP2 variant. Common FOXP2 variation does not reliably predict language phenotypes (Hoogman et al. 2014, PMID:25013396, on brain structure). Rare missense variants are often non-functional: Estruch et al. 2016 (PMID:27933109) tested several and "only one showed functional effects". The authors concluded that some variation "is unlikely to be a highly penetrant cause of speech/language disorder". This matters for variant interpretation.

Environmental risk factors. None documented. I found no evidence of environmental causation.

Protective factors. No genetic or environmental protective factors are documented. Early, intensive speech-language therapy improves outcomes but is treatment, not protection.

Gene–environment interactions. None documented.

Severity and progression. Severity runs from mild to severe. The course is stable or slowly improving with therapy; the disorder is not neurodegenerative.

4. Genetic and molecular information

Gene. FOXP2, a FOX-family (P subfamily) transcription factor. It has a polyglutamine tract, a zinc finger, a leucine zipper (which mediates homo- and heterodimerisation with FOXP1 and FOXP4), and a C-terminal forkhead DNA-binding domain.

Variant classes - Missense in the forkhead domain. R553H (KE family) and others: Reuter et al. 2017 (PMID:27572252): "two novel missense mutations within the forkhead domain". R553H is the residue equivalent to FOXP1 R514H, which causes a different disorder (Sollis et al. 2017, PMID:28741757). - Nonsense and frameshift. For example R328X (PMID:15877281). Reuter et al. 2017 reported "four different truncating mutations". - Deletions. Intragenic (PMID:23918746) or multi-megabase 7q31 deletions (PMID:40714749, PMID:16470794). - Translocations disrupting the gene (PMID:11586359). - Maternal UPD7 (PMID:17033973).

Classification. Truncating variants and deletions are typically classed pathogenic or likely pathogenic. Missense variants need functional assessment, because many rare variants are neutral (PMID:27933109). ClinGen gene-disease validity: I did not find a CGGV record during this search. Check cache/ and the ClinGen export before recording gene_disease_validity, and never assign a tier yourself.

Allele frequency. FOXP2 is depleted of loss-of-function variants in gnomAD (high pLI/LOEUF constraint). I did not retrieve exact metrics here; pathogenic variants are absent or ultra-rare.

Origin. Germline. Variants are either de novo or inherited from an affected parent: "Mutations occurred de novo in four families and were inherited from an affected parent in the other four." (PMID:27572252)

Functional consequence. Loss of function, i.e. haploinsufficiency. Vernes et al. 2006 (PMID:16984964): - R553H "severely affects FOXP2 function, chiefly by disrupting nuclear localization and DNA-binding properties." - R328X "yields an unstable, predominantly cytoplasmic product that lacks transactivation capacity." - Q17L "did not have any detectable functional effect in the present study."

A dominant-negative effect of R553H through dimerisation with wild-type protein has been proposed but not established. Haploinsufficiency is the consensus model, supported by deletions and UPD causing a similar phenotype.

Imprinting. Imprinting has been raised because of the paternal-absence finding (PMID:17033973: "we show the maternally inherited FOXP2 to be comparatively underexpressed"). However, "The speech gene FOXP2 is not imprinted." (Thomas et al. 2012, PMID:23033221). Maternally inherited point variants also cause the disorder, so treat parent-of-origin effects as unresolved.

Modifier genes. None established.

Epigenetics. FOXP2 is post-translationally SUMOylated: "FOXP2 is SUMOylated at a single major site" (PMID:26867680). No disease-associated methylation signature has been reported.

Chromosomal abnormalities. 7q31 deletions (6.8–15.2 Mb), t(5;7)(q22;q31.2), and maternal UPD7, which overlaps with Silver-Russell syndrome.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. A heterozygous FOXP2 pathogenic variant (truncating, deletion, or forkhead-domain missense) leads to loss of functional FOXP2 protein. Truncated proteins are unstable or cytoplasmic; R553H fails to bind DNA and mislocalises (PMID:16984964, in vitro). The functional effect is haploinsufficiency.
  2. Reduced FOXP2 dosage results in altered transcriptional regulation of neural target genes. FOXP2 "has dual functionality, acting to either repress or activate gene expression" (PMID:17999362). It "binds to and dramatically down-regulates CNTNAP2" (PMID:18987363). Fetal brain targets regulate neurite outgrowth (PMID:21765815). These are in vitro and mouse-tissue ChIP data; the target dysregulation in human patient neurons is inferred.
  3. Altered target-gene networks lead to abnormal development and function of cortico-striatal and cortico-cerebellar circuits. Foxp2 "modulates neuronal network formation" (PMID:21765815), and FOXP2 interacts with cortical transcription factors (PMID:29365100).
  4. 3a, striatum. In R552H heterozygous mice (the murine equivalent of R553H): "abnormal synaptic plasticity in striatal and cerebellar neural circuits" (PMID:18328704); "abnormally high ongoing striatal activity" (PMID:21876543).
  5. 3b, cerebellum. "Homozygous R552H mice show severe reductions in cerebellar growth" (PMID:18328704). In humans, Crus I grey matter is reduced and hypoactive (PMID:30460543).
  6. 3c, cortex and dorsal language stream. Children show "white matter reductions in the arcuate fasciculus (dorsal language…" (PMID:30796815).
  7. Circuit dysfunction results in structural and functional brain anomalies in affected humans. KE family MRI shows bilateral changes, including a smaller caudate, and "Several of these regions were abnormal bilaterally" (PMID:11872605). fMRI shows "significant underactivation … in Broca's area" (PMID:14555953).
  8. Impaired cortico-basal ganglia-cerebellar sensorimotor learning leads to deficient acquisition and sequencing of orofacial motor programs. The mouse data show motor-skill learning deficits (PMID:18328704). In songbirds, Area X knockdown impairs vocal imitation: "normal auditory-guided vocal motor learning requires FoxP2" (PMID:18052609). Applying this to human speech is a cross-species inference.
  9. This manifests as CAS, oral dyspraxia and dysarthria. Downstream of the speech-motor deficit, and possibly through partly separate cortical pathways, come language and literacy impairment and phonological working-memory deficits (PMID:7846081, PMID:29174050).
  10. Branch (inferred). Effects of FOXP2 in other organs (lung, gut, bone, islets, studied in mice: PMID:27341756, 25527076, 26021489) do not produce a recognised clinical phenotype in heterozygous humans.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: congenital and developmental. Delays in babbling and first words are often noted in infancy; CAS becomes apparent at 2–4 years. Onset is insidious.
  • Course: lifelong and stable to slowly improving. It is not progressive. Speech impairment persists into adulthood (PMID:36328423).
  • Critical period: early childhood, when speech-motor learning is most plastic and intensive therapy is likely most effective. This is an inference from general CAS practice; I found no FOXP2-specific trial.
  • Remission: none. Function improves with therapy.

9. Inheritance and population

  • Inheritance: autosomal dominant (HP:0000006). Variants are de novo or inherited.
  • Penetrance: high or complete for clear loss-of-function variants (KE family; PMID:27572252).
  • Expressivity: variable, ranging from mild speech disorder to minimal verbal output, with variable cognition and autism features (PMID:36328423).
  • Anticipation: none. Germline mosaicism: possible in principle; I found no specific data. Founder effects, carrier frequency, consanguinity: not applicable or not documented.
  • Prevalence: unknown. FOXP2-SLD is rare. Genetic diagnoses are found in roughly a third of CAS cases ("a remarkable one in three children have a genetic variant that explains their CAS", Morgan et al. 2024, PMID:38366112), and FOXP2 is one of many genes involved.   • Sex ratio: roughly equal (14 of 28 male in PMID:36328423). Geography: cases are reported worldwide; I found no population enrichment.

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical: a speech-language pathology assessment for CAS (inconsistent errors, disrupted transitions between sounds and syllables, inappropriate prosody), oral-motor assessment, and language and literacy testing. Neuropsychological testing shows that nonverbal IQ is usually relatively preserved.
  • Imaging: clinical MRI is usually unremarkable for an individual. In the framing of Morgan & Webster 2018 (PMID:30294994), "clinical MRI scans fail to reveal overt neural anomalies in individual cases with CAS". Group-level research MRI shows caudate, cerebellar and arcuate abnormalities (PMID:11872605, 30460543, 30796815, 27734906).
  • Laboratory and biomarkers: none.
  • Genetic testing: a positive genetic diagnosis is the basis for confirming the disorder.
  • Exome or genome sequencing with CNV calling is recommended for CAS, because the yield is about 1 in 3 (PMID:38366112). Morgan & Webster 2018: "a genetic aetiology should increasingly be pursued" (PMID:30294994).
  • Chromosomal microarray detects 7q31 deletions.
  • Methylation or SNP-based testing for maternal UPD7 if the clinical picture overlaps with Silver-Russell syndrome.
  • Single-gene or panel testing (CAS and speech panels).
  • Karyotype, FISH or long-read sequencing to detect translocations.
  • Testing for mitochondrial DNA and repeat expansions is not indicated.
  • Differential diagnosis: other monogenic CAS genes, including GRIN2A, SETBP1, CHD3, SETD1A, WDR5, KAT6A, ZFHX4, TNRC6B, MKL2 (PMID:29463886), FOXP1 syndrome (PMID:28741757), ELP4/16p11.2 deletion, DDX3X, BRPF1, KANSL1. Also idiopathic CAS, dysarthria from other neurological causes, hearing loss, and autism spectrum disorder.
  • Screening: no population screening. Testing is offered to relatives (cascade testing) and prenatally when the familial variant is known.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Survival: normal life expectancy. There is no disease-specific mortality.
  • Morbidity: lifelong communication disability, educational difficulties, possible reliance on AAC, and psychiatric comorbidity (anxiety 19%, depression 22%; PMID:36328423).
  • Prognostic factors: larger 7q31 deletions are associated with more severe and broader phenotypes, including ID and minimal verbal ability (PMID:40714749). Intragenic loss-of-function variants and missense variants show overlapping severity. I found no prognostic biomarkers.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: not applicable.
  • Secondary: genetic diagnosis early in CAS enables early, intensive therapy.
  • Tertiary: monitor and treat feeding, motor and mental-health comorbidities, and support literacy.
  • Counselling: each child of an affected person has a 50% risk. After a de novo variant, recurrence risk is low but above zero because of possible germline mosaicism. Prenatal or preimplantation testing is possible when the familial variant is known (GeneReviews).
