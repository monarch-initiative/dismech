COG8-congenital disorder of glycosylation

Mendelian MONDO:0012635 Pathograph 25 Show in embeddings browser congenital disorder of glycosylation type II defect in conserved oligomeric Golgi complex inborn error of metabolism

COG8-congenital disorder of glycosylation (COG8-CDG, CDG-IIh) is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive type II congenital disorder of glycosylation caused by biallelic truncating variants in COG8, which encodes a lobe B subunit of the hetero-octameric conserved oligomeric Golgi (COG) tethering complex. Loss of the COG8 C-terminus disrupts the Cog1-Cog8 interaction that bridges the two lobes, so Cog1 is lost alongside Cog8 and the intact complex cannot assemble; only smaller subcomplexes remain. The destabilised complex can no longer tether retrograde intra-Golgi and endosome-to-Golgi transport vesicles or support assembly of the Golgi SNARE complexes that drive their fusion, so Golgi glycosyltransferases and their recycling machinery are mislocalised. The biochemical consequence is a combined N- and O-glycosylation defect dominated by hyposialylation, seen clinically as a type II serum transferrin isoelectric focusing pattern. Reported patients have a multisystem, predominantly neurological phenotype - psychomotor retardation, hypotonia, failure to thrive, seizures, microcephaly, cerebellar atrophy and elevated liver enzymes - with the antenatally ascertained case additionally showing increased nuchal translucency, Dandy-Walker malformation and arthrogryposis. Severity varies considerably between the handful of published patients, from severe psychomotor retardation with seizures to a mild sialylation deficiency. Only four patients had been reported at the time of the most recent case description, and all reported variants lie in or affect exon 5.

COG8-CDG produces a type II CDG biochemical pattern. Severity of the sialylation deficiency differs between reported patients.

Reduced Proteoglycan Glycosaminoglycan Modification
Beyond N- and O-glycosylation, glycosaminoglycan modification of proteoglycans is reduced when any COG subunit is missing. COG8 is one of the two lobe-bridging subunits for which the reduction is least marked, and COG8-deficient cells retain normal glycosaminoglycan chain lengths on secreted proteoglycans, unlike cells lacking COG2-COG7. This is a knock-out cell-line result; proteoglycan synthesis has not been reported in COG8-CDG patient material.

name: COG8-congenital disorder of glycosylation
creation_date: "2026-09-03T00:00:00Z"
category: Mendelian
description: >-
  COG8-congenital disorder of glycosylation (COG8-CDG, CDG-IIh) is an
  ultra-rare autosomal recessive type II congenital disorder of glycosylation
  caused by biallelic truncating variants in COG8, which encodes a lobe B
  subunit of the hetero-octameric conserved oligomeric Golgi (COG) tethering
  complex. Loss of the COG8 C-terminus disrupts the Cog1-Cog8 interaction that
  bridges the two lobes, so Cog1 is lost alongside Cog8 and the intact complex
  cannot assemble; only smaller subcomplexes remain. The destabilised complex
  can no longer tether retrograde intra-Golgi and endosome-to-Golgi transport
  vesicles or support assembly of the Golgi SNARE complexes that drive their
  fusion, so Golgi glycosyltransferases and their recycling machinery are
  mislocalised. The biochemical consequence is a combined N- and O-glycosylation
  defect dominated by hyposialylation, seen clinically as a type II serum
  transferrin isoelectric focusing pattern. Reported patients have a
  multisystem, predominantly neurological phenotype - psychomotor retardation,
  hypotonia, failure to thrive, seizures, microcephaly, cerebellar atrophy and
  elevated liver enzymes - with the antenatally ascertained case additionally
  showing increased nuchal translucency, Dandy-Walker malformation and
  arthrogryposis. Severity varies considerably between the handful of published
  patients, from severe psychomotor retardation with seizures to a mild
  sialylation deficiency. Only four patients had been reported at the time of
  the most recent case description, and all reported variants lie in or affect
  exon 5.
