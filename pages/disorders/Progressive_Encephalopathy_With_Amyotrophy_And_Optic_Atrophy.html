Progressive Encephalopathy With Amyotrophy And Optic Atrophy

Mendelian MONDO:0014968 Pathograph 28 Show in embeddings browser hereditary disease Neurodegenerative Disease

Autosomal recessive early-onset neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic hypomorphic variants in TBCE, encoding tubulin-folding cofactor E, the chaperone that carries alpha-tubulin and, with TBCD, ARL2 and TBCC, assembles the alpha/beta-tubulin heterodimer. Patient fibroblasts contain less TBCE, less polymerized alpha-tubulin and abnormal mitotic spindles. In the pmn mouse, which carries a destabilizing Tbce missense allele, loss of Golgi-associated TBCE fragments the Golgi apparatus, interrupts the supply of tubulin to the axon and produces a dying-back motor axonopathy. Infants are normal at birth and develop hypotonia and developmental delay in the first year, then regress, with progressive spastic-ataxic tetraparesis, distal amyotrophy with foot drop from a distal spinal muscular atrophy-like motor neuronopathy, optic atrophy, moderate to severe intellectual disability and severe dysarthria or absent speech. Brain MRI shows cerebellar atrophy, a thin corpus callosum and white matter changes, with iron deposits in older patients. The first seven reported patients came from Southern Italy and carry the recurrent p.Ile155Asn allele. Unlike the allelic hypoparathyroidism-retardation-dysmorphism (Sanjad-Sakati) syndrome, growth at birth, facial features and calcium-phosphate metabolism are normal. Management is supportive. OMIM:617207.

Biallelic Hypomorphic TBCE Variants
Homozygous or compound heterozygous germline TBCE variants combining the recurrent missense allele p.Ile155Asn with itself or with a frameshift allele. The missense protein is unstable rather than absent, and the disease differs from the developmental Sanjad-Sakati syndrome caused by other TBCE alleles, which the discovery authors interpret as hypomorphic variants driving neurodegeneration.

Impaired Alpha/Beta-Tubulin Heterodimer Assembly
Newly made alpha- and beta-tubulin pass from the chaperonin to the tubulin-specific cofactors: TBCE holds alpha-tubulin and TBCD beta-tubulin in a complex with ARL2 until TBCC triggers release of the native heterodimer. With less TBCE, the supply of assembled heterodimers that feeds microtubule growth is limited.

Golgi Fragmentation in Motor Neurons
TBCE is a peripheral membrane protein that accumulates at the Golgi apparatus. In pmn mice it disappears from the Golgi of motor neurons, and loss of the ARF1-TBCE cross-talk that couples COPI vesicle formation to tubulin polymerization fragments the Golgi and its microtubules. Not yet examined in patient neurons.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — PEAMO is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in TBCE

Sferra et al. (2016) established PEAMO as a TBCE-related disorder by identifying biallelic TBCE mutations in six affected subjects from four unrelated families from the same geographical area in Southern Italy, with the recurrent c.464T>A (p.Ile155Asn) variant present in the homozygous or compound-heterozygous state. The authors wrote that the disorder is "caused by biallelic TBCE mutations, with the c.464T>A (p.Ile155Asn) change occurring at the heterozygous/homozygous state in six affected subjects from four unrelated families originated from the same geographical area in Southern Italy" (PMID: 27666369). The shared geographic origin and recurrent allele indicate a founder effect in the Southern Italian population.

TBCE maps to chromosome 1q42.3 and encodes tubulin-specific chaperone E, one of five tubulin-folding cofactors essential for α/β-tubulin heterodimer formation and microtubule polymerization. The mechanism is loss of function: Western blot of patient fibroblasts showed a "reduced amount of TBCE, suggestive of rapid degradation of the mutant protein" (PMID: 27666369). Thus the pathogenic variants destabilize TBCE, reducing its steady-state level and compromising its chaperone activity.

  • Ontology terms: gene TBCE (HGNC:11582); MONDO:0014968; variant NM_003193.5:c.464T>A, p.(Ile155Asn); inheritance — autosomal recessive.

Finding 2 — PEAMO is the minority (~5%) neurodegenerative pole of a broad TBCE spectrum

Sartorelli et al. (2026) assembled the largest TBCE cohort to date—335 mono-/biallelic patients (13 newly recruited plus 322 from systematic review)—and quantified the relative frequency of each clinical form. "Sanjad-Sakati syndrome was the most frequent form (85%), while the neurodegenerative phenotype accounted for a minority (5%) of cases" (PMID: 41833177). PEAMO therefore represents a small fraction of TBCE disease, which is dominated by the endocrine-dysmorphic HRD/Sanjad-Sakati presentation.

The neurodegenerative spectrum itself is clinically heterogeneous, ranging from classic PEAMO—"progressive spastic-ataxic tetraparesis, optic atrophy and distal motor axonal neuropathy, consistent with already named PEAMO"—to milder complex spastic paraparesis. Neuroimaging frequently showed "progressive thinning of the corpus callosum, cerebro-cerebellar atrophy" (PMID: 41833177).

Finding 3 — Core clinical phenotype: early-onset progressive encephalopathy, distal amyotrophy, and optic atrophy

The defining phenotype is captured in the title and text of Sferra et al. (2016): "TBCE Mutations Cause Early-Onset Progressive Encephalopathy with Distal Spinal Muscular Atrophy," describing "an early-onset and progressive neurodegenerative encephalopathy with distal spinal muscular atrophy resembling the phenotype of pmn/pmn mice" (PMID: 27666369). Combined with Sartorelli et al. (2026), the core triad is:

  1. Progressive encephalopathy / spastic-ataxic tetraparesis (central nervous system degeneration)
  2. Distal spinal muscular atrophy / amyotrophy (distal motor axonal neuropathy, lower motor neuron)
  3. Optic atrophy (anterior visual pathway degeneration)

Onset is in infancy/early childhood with a progressive course, and neuroimaging shows progressive corpus-callosum thinning and cerebro-cerebellar atrophy (PMID: 41833177).

Finding 4 — Mechanism: impaired microtubule polymerization plus ARF1/TBCE-dependent Golgi fragmentation drive a dying-back axonopathy

The mechanistic chain is anchored in mouse and cellular models. In the pmn mouse (Tbce missense), "Electron microscopy of the sciatic and phrenic nerves of affected mice showed a reduced number of microtubules, probably due to defective stabilization" (PMID: 12389029), directly linking TBCE loss to axonal microtubule depletion. Schäfer et al. (2017) showed that "the pmn mutation decreases the level of TBCE, impedes microtubule polymerization in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons and causes progressive loss of microtubules" with defective microtubule-based axonal transport (PMID: 27488538).

Beyond the cytoskeleton, TBCE has a cis-Golgi function. Bellouze et al. (2014) found that "Loss of TBCE in mutant pmn and TBCE-depleted motor neuron cultures causes defects in Golgi-derived microtubules, as expected, but surprisingly also reduced levels of COPI subunits, decreased recruitment of tethering factors p115/GM130 and impaired Golgi SNARE-mediated vesicle fusion"; the small GTPase ARF1 recruits TBCE to the Golgi and rescues fragmentation (PMID: 24951541). The degeneration follows a distal-to-proximal (dying-back) pattern: "The disease starts at the motor endplates, and then leads to axonal loss and finally to apoptosis of the corresponding cell bodies" (PMID: 12446740).

Finding 5 — The pmn mouse is a faithful, fully penetrant model and a gene-rescue proof-of-concept

Martin et al. (2002) identified the causative Tbce missense mutation (p.Trp524Gly) in the pmn mouse, which develops progressive caudio-cranial motor axon degeneration from ~2 weeks of age and dies at 4–6 weeks. "The mutation is fully penetrant, and expressivity does not depend on the genetic background" (PMID: 12389029). Critically, "Transgenic complementation with a wildtype Tbce cDNA restored a normal phenotype in mutant mice" (PMID: 12389029), providing direct causal proof and a foundation for gene-replacement strategies. At the cellular level, "Isolated motoneurons from pmn mutant mice exhibit shorter axons and axonal swelling with irregularly structured beta-tubulin and tau immunoreactivity" (PMID: 12446740). The pmn mouse also displays a sensory neuropathy with microtubule loss, broadening its relevance to axonal neurodegeneration (PMID: 27488538).

Finding 6 — Allelic disorders and differential diagnosis: PEAMO vs. HRD/Sanjad-Sakati and KCS1

Parvari et al. (2002) first linked TBCE to hypoparathyroidism-retardation-dysmorphism (HRD / Sanjad-Sakati, OMIM 241410) and autosomal-recessive Kenny-Caffey syndrome type 1 (KCS1, OMIM 244460), noting that "The gene TBCE encodes one of several chaperone proteins required for the proper folding of alpha-tubulin subunits and the formation of alpha-beta-tubulin heterodimers" (PMID: 12389028). The Middle Eastern founder mutation is a 12-bp deletion: in 21 Sanjad-Sakati patients from Kuwait, "All affected persons had homozygous deletion of 12 bp (155-166del) in exon 3 of the TBCE gene. All of the parents were heterozygous carriers of this mutation" (PMID: 19554981). A systematic review of 205 Kenny-Caffey patients noted that "Symptoms more prevalent in KCS1 included intellectual disability" (PMID: 36916904).

The key diagnostic distinction: PEAMO is a progressive neurodegenerative tubulinopathy without the congenital hypoparathyroidism, intrauterine growth retardation, or facial dysmorphism that define HRD/KCS1. A given TBCE genotype tends to determine which pole of the spectrum is expressed.

Finding 7 — The TBCE neurodegenerative spectrum extends beyond classic PEAMO, with a genotype-phenotype gradient and an out-of-frame rescue mechanism

Sparber et al. (2025) described 7 patients with a novel, milder TBCE phenotype (amyotrophy, testicular failure, mild intellectual disability ± short stature). "All patients were homozygous or compound-heterozygous for the NM_003193.5:c.100 + 1G > A variant, which causes a splicing alteration and early frameshift. However, we found that the mild phenotype arises due to translation from an alternative open reading frame, producing a partially functional protein" (PMID: 39657131). Patient fibroblasts showed reduced Golgi compactness but normal microtubule dynamics—suggesting a threshold effect in which residual TBCE function attenuates the phenotype. This dovetails with the full spectrum reported by Sartorelli et al. (2026), ranging from classic PEAMO to "milder complex spastic paraparesis" (PMID: 41833177). Together these establish a genotype–phenotype severity gradient governed by residual TBCE activity.

Finding 8 — TBCE domain architecture (CAP-Gly, LRR, UBL) mediates both tubulin heterodimer assembly and dissociation/degradation

Serna et al. (2015) solved the structure of the α-tubulin–TBCE–TBCB (αEB) complex, revealing TBCE's three functional domains: cytoskeleton-associated protein glycine-rich (CAP-Gly), leucine-rich repeat (LRR), and ubiquitin-like (UBL). Heterodimer dissociation occurs through "a steric interaction between β-tubulin and the TBCE cytoskeleton-associated protein glycine-rich (CAP-Gly) and leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domains", and "The protruding arrangement of chaperone ubiquitin-like (UBL) domains in the αEB complex suggests that there is a direct interaction of this complex with the proteasome, thus mediating α-tubulin degradation" (PMID: 25908846). TBCE is thus bifunctional—building heterodimers and controlling their disassembly/turnover—placing it at the heart of tubulin proteostasis. Loss of this activity disturbs "subcellular organelles that require microtubules for membrane trafficking, such as the Golgi and late endosomal compartments" (PMID: 12389028).

10. Diagnostics

  • Genetic testing (definitive): Molecular confirmation of biallelic TBCE variants via whole-exome/genome sequencing, neurodegeneration/neuropathy/hereditary spastic paraplegia gene panels, or targeted TBCE testing; carrier testing of parents confirms recessive segregation (PMID: 27666369; PMID: 19554981).
  • Neuroimaging: Brain MRI showing progressive corpus-callosum thinning and cerebro-cerebellar atrophy (PMID: 41833177).
  • Electrophysiology: Nerve conduction studies / EMG demonstrating a distal motor axonal neuropathy; ophthalmologic exam / OCT / VEP for optic atrophy.
  • Functional/laboratory: Reduced TBCE on Western blot of patient fibroblasts supports loss of function in a research context (PMID: 27666369). Note: unlike HRD/KCS1, PEAMO does not feature hypocalcemia/hypoparathyroidism.
  • Differential diagnosis: HRD/Sanjad-Sakati (congenital hypoparathyroidism, IUGR, dysmorphism — PMID: 12389028); Kenny-Caffey syndrome (KCS1 = TBCE; KCS2 = FAM111A); other childhood spastic-ataxias, hereditary motor-sensory neuropathies, and optic atrophy syndromes. Absence of endocrine/dysmorphic features distinguishes PEAMO.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Prognosis is poor for classic PEAMO given progressive encephalopathy, motor disability, and visual loss; milder TBCE phenotypes have a more protracted course (PMID: 39657131). Formal survival statistics are not available for this ultra-rare disease. The pmn mouse is lethal at 4–6 weeks, reflecting the severity of complete motor-neuronopathy in the model but not directly translatable to human life expectancy (PMID: 12389029). Prognostic factor: degree of residual TBCE function (genotype) is the principal determinant of severity and progression rate.

12. Treatment

  • Disease-modifying therapy: None currently available; management is supportive/symptomatic (physical, occupational, and speech therapy; management of spasticity; visual/low-vision support; nutritional and respiratory support as needed; orthopedic management of neurogenic deformities).
  • Proof-of-concept for future therapy: Transgenic wild-type Tbce cDNA fully rescued the pmn mouse phenotype, establishing gene replacement as a rational strategy (PMID: 12389029). The natural rescue by alternative-ORF translation in milder patients suggests that even partial restoration of TBCE function could be clinically meaningful (PMID: 39657131).

  • Primary prevention: Not applicable (monogenic). Genetic counseling for at-risk/consanguineous couples; carrier testing in founder populations (Southern Italian p.Ile155Asn; Middle Eastern 155-166del for allelic disorders).
  • Reproductive options: Prenatal diagnosis and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M) for couples with a known TBCE genotype.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

PEAMO is best understood as a tubulin-proteostasis disorder with tissue-selective (neuronal) vulnerability. TBCE is a bifunctional chaperone: through its CAP-Gly, LRR and UBL domains it both assembles the α/β-tubulin heterodimer and drives its dissociation and proteasomal turnover (PMID: 25908846). When biallelic loss-of-function variants destabilize TBCE, two coupled failures ensue: (i) a cytoskeletal failure—impaired microtubule polymerization and progressive microtubule loss with defective axonal transport—and (ii) a secretory failure—Golgi fragmentation from loss of Golgi-derived microtubules, COPI depletion, tether dispersal, and impaired SNARE-mediated vesicle fusion, which is normally countered by ARF1-dependent recruitment of TBCE to the Golgi. Long, metabolically demanding neurons—motor neurons and retinal ganglion cells—are exquisitely dependent on both microtubule-based transport and Golgi secretory output, explaining why the phenotype is dominated by distal motor amyotrophy, encephalopathy, and optic atrophy. The degeneration proceeds in a dying-back pattern, from motor endplate to axon to cell body, culminating in neuronal apoptosis.

The genotype–phenotype gradient is the unifying explanatory variable across the entire TBCE spectrum: the amount of residual TBCE function determines whether a patient develops severe PEAMO, milder complex spastic paraparesis, or—in the allelic context—the endocrine-dysmorphic HRD/KCS1 phenotypes. The alternative-ORF rescue described by Sparber et al. is a striking natural demonstration that partial restoration of TBCE converts a nominally null allele into a milder disease (PMID: 39657131).

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Epidemiology: No validated prevalence or incidence figures for PEAMO specifically; it is ultra-rare (~5% of an already rare TBCE disease).
  • Natural history: Survival, life expectancy, and standardized disability/QoL outcomes are not quantified; most human evidence comes from small series.
  • Optic-atrophy mechanism: Retinal ganglion cell involvement is inferred from the clinical phenotype rather than directly demonstrated in a PEAMO model; the pmn mouse best models the motor component.
  • Genotype–phenotype mapping: Beyond a handful of recurrent alleles, the relationship between specific variants, residual TBCE activity, and PEAMO vs. HRD/KCS1 outcome is incompletely resolved.
  • Therapeutics: No human disease-modifying therapy exists; the gene-rescue data are murine. Optimal therapeutic window, CNS delivery, and dosing for any future gene/protein therapy are unknown.
  • Modifiers/epigenetics: No modifier genes or epigenetic contributors identified.
