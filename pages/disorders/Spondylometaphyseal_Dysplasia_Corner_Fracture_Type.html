Spondylometaphyseal Dysplasia Corner Fracture Type

Mendelian MONDO:0008479 Pathograph 25 Show in embeddings browser Skeletal Dysplasia

Spondylometaphyseal dysplasia with "corner fractures", also called the Sutcliffe type (SMD-CF; OMIM #184255), is an ultra-rare skeletal dysplasia whose defining radiographic sign is a flake-like, triangular, or curvilinear ossification centre at the edge of an irregular metaphysis that mimics a healing fracture but is not one. Most molecularly solved cases carry heterozygous, usually de novo, missense variants in FN1, encoding fibronectin — the master organiser of extracellular matrix that promotes assembly of collagens and fibrillin-1 and is secreted by osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and mesenchymal cells. The pathogenic alleles cluster in the N-terminal 70 kDa assembly region and overwhelmingly substitute cysteine residues, breaking disulfide bonds in the type-I assembly domains; the mutant protein is retained intracellularly and secreted poorly or not at all, so the mechanism is a secretion defect rather than a defect of matrix binding. Severe scoliosis and developmental coxa vara distinguish the FN1 cases from classical Sutcliffe-type SMD, and generalised skeletal fragility with low-trauma femoral fracture has been reported once. FN1 does not account for every case: some are linked to COL2A1 — though not the original Sutcliffe family — which is why MONDO places this concept under both spondylometaphyseal dysplasia and the type 2 collagenopathies.

Autosomal Dominant HP:0000006
FN1-related SMD-CF is autosomal dominant. The founding series and several subsequent reports were de novo, which for some years made the disorder look effectively sporadic; a 2026 report of a multigenerational family with the variant segregating through four affected members shows that transmitted disease also occurs, and that affected adults reach reproductive age. Both origins therefore have to be considered when counselling a newly diagnosed proband.

Does the retained mutant fibronectin act by simple haploinsufficiency, or does it interfere with the wild-type protein or with the secretory pathway itself?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN smdcf_dominant_negative_versus_haploinsufficiency
Attached to
pathophysiology#Deficient Extracellular Matrix Assembly in Cartilage and Bone
Partly answered since this entry was first written, and worth stating precisely how far. A 2024 patient-derived iPSC study shows the mechanism has two arms, not one: extracellular fibronectin is reduced — in patient plasma as well as in culture — and the retained mutant protein simultaneously accumulates with ER chaperones in uncleared cytosolic vesicles, raising cellular stress markers and distorting mitochondrial structure. That intracellular arm is a gain of toxic consequence and cannot be produced by a null allele, which argues against simple haploinsufficiency; the allelic spectrum, almost uniformly cysteine substitutions in one small region, points the same way. What remains untested is the decisive comparison: no study has put a truncating and a missense FN1 allele side by side in the same cell background. Until that is done the relative contribution of the two arms is unknown, and it is exactly what decides whether therapy should aim to raise wild-type output or to clear the retained mutant. Note that the rescue result — exogenous fibronectin or TGF-beta-1 restoring condensation — works through the extracellular arm and does not settle the question.

The mutant fibronectin accumulates inside the cell instead of reaching the extracellular space. Immunofluorescence shows increased intracellular retention of the mutant proteins. Downstream
Vesicular Accumulation and Cellular Stress in Mesenchymal Stem Cells The retained protein is not inert. In patient-derived mesenchymal stem cells it accumulates with the endoplasmic reticulum chaperones BIP and HSP47 inside ribosome-covered cytosolic vesicles budding from the ER, most of which are never cleared.
Deficient Extracellular Matrix Assembly in Cartilage and Bone Fibronectin that never reaches the extracellular space cannot nucleate matrix assembly. Because fibronectin organises the deposition of collagens and fibrillin-1, the consequence is not the loss of one matrix component but a failure of the assembly step on which several depend. The extracellular deficit is systemic as well as local: circulating fibronectin is reduced in patient plasma.

Undegraded fibronectin-and-chaperone vesicles build up in the cytosol of patient-derived mesenchymal stem cells, a subset acquiring lysosomal markers. Cellular stress markers rise and mitochondrial structure is altered, and bulk RNA sequencing shows a transcriptomic profile specific to the patient cells. This is the second arm of the mechanism, distinct from simple extracellular deficiency: the mutant protein does harm inside the cell as well as failing to act outside it, which is the strongest argument yet that the disorder is not plain haploinsufficiency.

Impaired Mesenchymal Condensation and Chondrogenesis
Mesenchymal condensation is the first committed step of skeletogenesis, and it fails in FN1-mutant cells. The proximate reason appears to be a shortfall of TGF-beta-1, which mutant cells express at significantly lower levels and which condensation requires. The finding is reversible in vitro: supplying either exogenous fibronectin or TGF-beta-1 restores condensation and promotes chondrogenesis, which makes this the first identified point of pharmacological leverage in the disorder.

Abnormal Metaphyseal Ossification
Irregularly shaped metaphyses carrying flake-like, triangular, or curvilinear ossification centres at their edges. The appearance simulates a healing corner fracture, which is the source of the disorder's name and of its main diagnostic hazard: a child with these radiographs has repeatedly been investigated for non-accidental injury. The changes involve the distal tibia, the ulnar aspect of the distal radius, and the proximal humerus and femur, and in at least one patient the distal femoral changes healed with skeletal maturation.

Radiographic Recognition and the Non-Accidental Injury Pitfall
The diagnosis begins with recognising that the metaphyseal "corner fractures" are ossification abnormalities and not injuries. The classic metaphyseal lesion of physical abuse has the same distribution — distal tibia, distal radius, proximal humerus and femur — so a skeletal survey in an infant can be read either way, and the presence of vertebral change, coxa vara, and scoliosis is what should redirect towards a dysplasia.

  prompt: >-
    Does the retained mutant fibronectin act by simple haploinsufficiency, or does it
    interfere with the wild-type protein or with the secretory pathway itself?
  rationale: >-
    Partly answered since this entry was first written, and worth stating precisely
    how far. A 2024 patient-derived iPSC study shows the mechanism has two arms, not
    one: extracellular fibronectin is reduced — in patient plasma as well as in
    culture — and the retained mutant protein simultaneously accumulates with ER
    chaperones in uncleared cytosolic vesicles, raising cellular stress markers and
    distorting mitochondrial structure. That intracellular arm is a gain of toxic
    consequence and cannot be produced by a null allele, which argues against simple
    haploinsufficiency; the allelic spectrum, almost uniformly cysteine substitutions
    in one small region, points the same way. What remains untested is the decisive
    comparison: no study has put a truncating and a missense FN1 allele side by side
    in the same cell background. Until that is done the relative contribution of the
    two arms is unknown, and it is exactly what decides whether therapy should aim to
    raise wild-type output or to clear the retained mutant. Note that the rescue
    result — exogenous fibronectin or TGF-beta-1 restoring condensation — works
    through the extracellular arm and does not settle the question.
