Splenic Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0005967 Pathograph 28 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Extrapulmonary tuberculosis Abdominal tuberculosis

Splenic tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the spleen. This entry covers isolated (solitary) splenic tuberculosis and disease in which the spleen is the predominant site. Mycobacterium tuberculosis reaches the spleen by blood-borne spread, which is the usual route in HIV infection, or by contiguous spread from abdominal lymph nodes, bowel or pancreas. M. bovis acquired from unpasteurized dairy products also causes splenic abscesses. Caseating epithelioid granulomas form in the red and white pulp and appear as micronodular lesions (under 10 mm), macronodular lesions or tuberculomas, calcified foci, or caseous necrosis that coalesces into single or multiple tuberculous abscesses. Fever, often a prolonged fever of unknown origin, splenomegaly, left upper quadrant or diffuse abdominal pain, weight loss and anorexia are the usual features, and laboratory findings include anemia, neutropenia and pancytopenia. Ultrasound and CT show single or multiple hypoechoic, hypodense lesions or abscesses. Because no imaging appearance is specific and lymphoma is the main mimic, diagnosis rests on fine-needle aspiration, core biopsy or splenectomy specimens with histopathology, acid-fast staining, nucleic acid testing and culture. Splenic rupture with hemoperitoneum, hypersplenism and severe neutropenia are reported complications. Standard antituberculous chemotherapy is the first-line treatment; large abscesses are drained, and splenectomy is kept for diagnostic failure, refractory disease, abscess or rupture.

Micronodular splenic tuberculosis
Multiple small lesions under 10 mm across, as hypoechoic nodules on ultrasound or hypodense foci on CT, sometimes coalescing into microabscesses. In people with HIV, ultrasound-detected splenic microabscesses are caused by tuberculosis often enough to justify starting treatment before culture results.

Caseous and Coagulative Necrosis of Splenic Lesions
Caseous necrosis, and in the spleen frequent coagulative necrosis, destroys the centre of the lesions. The pathology series attributes the higher rate of coagulative necrosis than in the lung to the spleen's segmental arterial supply and lack of drainage. Large necrotic nodules give the central necrosis seen on CT.

  recorded for that reason and have no causal edge here. Persistent fever,
  anorexia, weight loss, fatigue, night sweats, abdominal distension,
  hepatomegaly and a raised ESR also have no causal edge, because no cited
  source says which step produces them. Evidence base. The

1. Disease Information

Overview. Splenic TB is a rare form of extrapulmonary TB that usually results from blood-borne spread (Liu P et al., Front Med 2025, PMID 41098981). - Splenic involvement is usually part of miliary TB. Among organs involved in miliary disease, the spleen ranks third after lung and liver. - Isolated (solitary) splenic TB is uncommon. It is seen mostly in immunosuppressed people, especially those with HIV, but it is also reported in immunocompetent people (Lin SF et al., World J Surg Oncol 2016, PMID 27250119; Ranjan R et al., Cureus 2024, PMID 38919240). - Most of the evidence comes from case reports. A 2018 review notes that "a consensus statement based on the available case reports is lacking" (Pol Przegl Chir 2018;90(5):49-51, PMID 30426945).

2. Etiology

Causal factor (infectious): Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. The spleen is seeded mostly by blood-borne spread from a primary focus, usually in the lung (PMID 41098981). M. bovis also causes spleen lesions in animals (Section 14).

Risk factors (human clinical evidence)

  • HIV and advanced immunosuppression (strongest risk factor).
  • Splenic involvement "is more common in patients with HIV and in disseminated disease" (paraphrase of the Dixit R et al. abstract, Indian J Tuberc 2010;57:25-30, PMID 20420041; IMSEAR).
  • "Up to 70% of the patients with tuberculosis and HIV infection develop extrapulmonary disease" (Salazar A, Enferm Infecc Microbiol Clin 1994, PMID 8011721).
  • Multifocal splenic TB abscesses are described in HIV-infected drug users (Soriano V, AIDS 1991, PMID 1892601) and in AIDS (Reichel C, Z Gastroenterol 1996, PMID 8794544).
  • Splenic TB has been reported with newly diagnosed advanced HIV (Tiri B, IDCases 2016, PMID 27635384).
  • TNF-α blockade.
  • Keane et al. reported 70 TB cases after infliximab, after a median of 12 weeks of treatment. Forty had extrapulmonary disease, 17 of them disseminated (NEJM 2001;345:1098, PMID 11596589; PMID taken from the MEDLINE accession).
  • Hepatic and splenic TB after infliximab for ulcerative colitis has been reported (Chen S, BMC Gastroenterol 2026, PMID 42464147).
  • Other immunosuppression. Immunodeficiency is described as "an important risk factor for splenic tuberculosis" in case literature (PMC5806411). Diabetes and other comorbidities are mentioned in case series but were not quantified in what I retrieved.
  • Geography: Living in or coming from a high-burden TB setting. Most series come from India and sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Age: Childhood. Splenic TB is described in children, including immunocompetent children (PMC6758994; PMC11788734). Abdominal TB in children is reviewed in Raj V, Pediatr Surg Int 2025 (PMID 40533676).

Genetic risk factors (host; inferred to apply to disseminated mycobacterial disease generally, not shown specifically for the spleen) - Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) involves inborn errors of IFN-γ immunity. - Genes include IFNGR1, IFNGR2, IL12B, IL12RB1, IL12RB2, IL23R, STAT1, JAK1, IRF8, SPPL2A, ISG15, TYK2, RORC, CYBB and NEMO (IKBKG). - Homozygosity for TYK2 P1104A is a common-variant cause of TB with "incomplete, and probably low" penetrance (Bustamante J, PMID 32025907, PMC7275902). - Further overviews: Errami & Bousfiha 2023, PMC10676692; PMID 40656276. - No splenic-TB-specific GWAS hits, ClinVar entries or ClinGen curations were found.

Protective factors - BCG vaccination protects strongly against disseminated (miliary) childhood TB, the form that usually involves the spleen. - Mangtani et al. (Clin Infect Dis 2014): RR 0.1 (95% CI 0.01–0.77) for meningeal or miliary TB in infants. - Trunz et al. (Lancet 2006): one miliary case prevented per 9,314 vaccinations (UCL/LSHTM summaries). PMIDs for both were not retrieved. - Antiretroviral therapy and immune restoration: inferred to be protective; no splenic-specific data retrieved. - TB preventive treatment before anti-TNF therapy: Keane 2001 recommends screening (PMID 11596589). - Genetic protective alleles: none identified.

Gene–environment interaction: Host IFN-γ/IL-12 pathway defects (genetic) or HIV/TNF blockade (acquired) combine with exposure to M. tuberculosis (environmental) to allow blood-borne spread. This is inferred from the MSMD and anti-TNF literature, not demonstrated for the spleen.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

  • Causal human genes: None (infectious disease). Not applicable: pathogenic variants, ACMG classification, allele frequency, somatic vs germline origin, chromosomal abnormalities, and disease-specific epigenetic data.
  • Host susceptibility genes (modifiers): The MSMD genes listed in Section 2.
  • Loss of function in the IL-12/IL-23–IFN-γ axis, mostly autosomal recessive (PMID 32025907).
  • TYK2 P1104A is a missense, partial loss-of-function variant with impaired IL-23 signalling (PMID 32025907).
  • IL12RB1 (~44%) and IFNGR1 (~17%) are the most frequently reported MSMD defects (PMC10676692).
  • Pathogen genetics: Drug-resistance mutations (for example, the rpoB rifampicin-resistance region detected by Xpert MTB/RIF) are relevant to treatment. An Xpert-positive splenic aspirate has been reported (PMC7920217). No splenic-specific pathogen lineage data were retrieved.

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agent: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (NCBI Taxon to verify). M. bovis also causes spleen lesions (zoonotic).
  • Co-infection with M. fortuitum in splenic abscesses has been reported in HIV (Soto-Arquíñigo L 2017, PMID 29177396).
  • Lifestyle and environment: Injecting drug use with HIV (PMID 1892601), crowding, and living in an endemic area. Smoking, alcohol and malnutrition are general TB risk factors not specifically quantified for splenic TB in retrieved sources.
  • Iatrogenic: Anti-TNF biologics (PMIDs 11596589, 42464147).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Inhaling M. tuberculosis droplet nuclei leads to infection of alveolar macrophages (CL: alveolar macrophage) and a primary focus in the lung.
  2. Bacilli spreading through lymph and blood (early primary infection, or reactivation) results in bacteraemic seeding of reticuloendothelial organs. The spleen is commonly seeded in miliary TB because of its blood-filtering red pulp and resident macrophages. This anatomical rationale is inferred from the spleen's role; that the spleen ranks after lung and liver is shown in case literature (PMID 41098981).
  3. In mice, spread from lung to spleen and liver generally occurs 11–14 days after aerosol infection. Its rate depends on host genetics (C57BL/6 vs C3H) (PMC128141; model-organism evidence).
  4. Splenic macrophages taking up bacilli lead to innate inflammatory signalling, then recruitment of T cells. IFN-γ (Th1) activation of macrophages, together with TNF-α, drives granuloma formation. "TNF-α and IFN-γ are particularly important in promoting the formation and function of the granuloma" (Frontiers Immunol 2016).
  5. Branch A, effective immunity: Contained epithelioid granulomas result in small micronodules that may heal with calcification (calcified splenic foci, PMID 17642503).
  6. Branch B, defective immunity (HIV CD4 depletion, TNF blockade, MSMD gene defects): weak granulomas lead to uncontrolled bacillary growth, many microabscesses and miliary lesions. In HIV, 88.3% of splenic microabscesses seen on ultrasound were TB (PMID 31456550).
  7. Branch C, dysregulated hyperinflammation: Excess TNF and strong T-cell immunity lead to macrophage necrosis and release of bacilli outside the cells (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, PMID 25377142). This results in caseous necrosis, which merges into macronodules or a tuberculous ("cold") abscess (PMID 17642503; PMC5566777).
  8. Tissue destruction and splenic enlargement lead to splenomegaly and LUQ pain. Rarely, the capsule gives way and the spleen ruptures, causing haemoperitoneum (PMID 38919240). Systemic cytokines (TNF, IL-6; inferred) cause fever, weight loss and fatigue.

  • Immune system: cell-mediated immunity is central; its failure (HIV) is linked to disseminated and splenic disease.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: spleen (UBERON label: spleen), including red pulp and white pulp.
  • Secondary organs:
  • liver (hepatosplenic TB), abdominal lymph nodes, lung, peritoneum;
  • bone marrow in disseminated disease (inferred).
  • Body systems: hematopoietic/lymphoid (immune) system, digestive and abdominal cavity, respiratory.
  • Tissue and cells: lymphoid tissue; granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis; macrophages, epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells, T and B lymphocytes.
  • Subcellular (GO Cellular Component labels): phagosome; phagolysosome / lysosome (bacilli persist in the phagosome); cytoplasm of macrophages.
  • Lateralization: Left upper quadrant (the spleen is a single left-sided organ). Lesions within the spleen are solitary or multiple and diffuse.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Subacute or insidious, with weeks to months of fever and weight loss. Occasionally acute, from abscess rupture (PMID 38919240). Occurs in children and adults.
  • Morphological stages (imaging and pathology):
  • miliary micronodules (<2 cm) → macronodules or confluent lesions → abscess → healing with calcification (PMID 17642503; Radiopaedia);
  • on MRI, lesions tend to be "small and multiple" and "most often occur with splenomegaly" (De Backer AI et al., AJR 2006, PMID 16554586).
  • Course: Progressive and potentially fatal without treatment. Treatment usually leads to remission; lesions shrink on ultrasound follow-up, which Sharma et al. propose for monitoring response (PMID 17642503).
  • Critical windows:
  • early in anti-TNF therapy (median 12 weeks; PMID 11596589);
  • advanced HIV (low CD4).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Not applicable (acquired infection). MSMD predisposition is mostly autosomal recessive (Section 4).
  • Epidemiology:
  • Population-level splenic TB incidence or prevalence is not available.
  • WHO 2025 report: 16% of TB notifications in 2024 were extrapulmonary (WHO GTB 2025).
  • Splenic TB was found in 4–12% of patients who underwent diagnostic splenectomy in one cited series (secondary citation in PMC4888408).
  • In HIV-positive patients, ultrasound showed splenic lesions in 21.0% (95% CI 10.6–33.8) across 5 studies with 774 patients (Schafer JM et al., S Afr Med J 2019;109:570-6, PMID 31456550).
  • Geography: High TB-burden regions (South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa). Major series come from India (PMIDs 17642503, 20420041).
  • Sex ratio and age distribution: No robust data retrieved. A 2026 systematic review (Chaudhary P, J Glob Infect Dis, PMID 41958487) covers epidemiology, but its full text or abstract could not be retrieved.

10. Diagnostics

Imaging

  • Ultrasound: In the Sharma series of 23 patients, the findings in descending order were "single or multiple hypoechoic focal lesions, splenic abscess, calcifications (on CT) and isolated splenomegaly". In 5 of 6 patients whose only ultrasound finding was splenomegaly, CT showed lesions. The authors call ultrasound affordable, non-invasive and useful for following treatment response (PMID 17642503; IJMR).
  • CT: Multiple well-defined, hypodense round lesions (Zapata Naranjo, Cureus 2025, PMID 40291232). Imaging features of splenic infections are largely non-specific (Abdom Radiol 2021, PMC8160561).
  • MRI: Small, multiple lesions, usually with splenomegaly (PMID 16554586).
  • FDG-PET/CT: Isolated splenic TB has been detected only on FDG-PET (PMC6159193). PET does not distinguish TB from lymphoma.
  • FASH protocol: Focused Assessment with Sonography for HIV-associated TB looks for splenic microabscesses (ACEP). In HIV, 88.3% (95% CI 72.3–97.9) of splenic microabscesses on ultrasound were TB, and the authors suggest this is "probably sufficient indication to initiate TB treatment prior to obtaining culture data" (PMID 31456550).

Microbiology and pathology

  • Sampling: Image-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA), core biopsy, or splenectomy specimen. FNA is safer, while core biopsy is more accurate (PMC5566777; PMC3948145).
  • Tests on the sample: AFB smear (Ziehl–Neelsen), mycobacterial culture, Xpert MTB/RIF. AFB smear and Xpert were positive on a splenic aspirate in one series (PMC7920217).
  • Histology: caseating epithelioid granulomas with Langhans giant cells.
  • Post-splenectomy diagnosis: "the definitive diagnosis is essentially formulated on the post-splenectomy specimen" in many reports (PMID 30426945).

Ancillary tests: HIV testing; IGRA or tuberculin skin test (supportive only); chest imaging; sputum testing for a concurrent pulmonary focus; urine LF-LAM in advanced HIV (general TB practice; not retrieved specifically for splenic TB).

Genetic testing: Not used for diagnosis. Consider an MSMD gene panel or exome sequencing (IL12RB1, IFNGR1 and others) only in children with disseminated or recurrent mycobacterial disease without an acquired cause (inferred from PMID 32025907).

Omics-based diagnostics: None validated for splenic TB.

Differential diagnosis: - Lymphoma: the most important mimic, with nodules plus lymphadenopathy (PMID 41098981). - Pyogenic splenic abscess: shorter illness, bacterial cultures. - Splenic infarct: wedge-shaped. - Fungal microabscesses (candidiasis) in neutropenia. - Others: sarcoidosis, metastases, histoplasmosis (calcifications), brucellosis, melioidosis, and splenic involvement by Bartonella or nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) in HIV (PMC5566777).

Screening: No population screening. In people with HIV, point-of-care ultrasound (FASH) is used as targeted case-finding.

11. Outcome/Prognosis

  • Mortality: No splenic-TB-specific survival statistics were retrieved. Prognosis with appropriate anti-TB therapy is generally good in case reports. Mortality is driven mostly by co-existing disseminated or HIV-associated disease.
  • Complications: abscess formation, splenic rupture with haemoperitoneum (PMID 38919240), and spread of disseminated disease. After splenectomy there is a lifelong risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (general surgical knowledge; not retrieved here).
  • Prognostic factors (inferred from the case literature): HIV status, CD4 count, disseminated vs isolated disease, drug resistance, and how fast diagnosis is made.
  • Quality of life and disability: No data.

12. Treatment

Pharmacotherapy (NCIT labels to verify: Isoniazid, Rifampin, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol)

  • Regimen: Standard 6-month 2HRZ(E)/4HR, that is, 2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, then 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin. WHO applies it to extrapulmonary TB except CNS and bone/joint TB, which get longer regimens (WHO TB KSP node 1933; WHO Module 4; NBK613096).
  • Duration in practice: Many case reports used 6–12 months; no trials specific to splenic TB exist.
  • Drug-resistant TB: follow WHO drug-resistant TB regimens.
  • HIV co-infection: ART coordinated with TB therapy; watch rifampicin drug interactions.
  • Mechanisms of action:
  • isoniazid: inhibits mycolic acid synthesis via InhA after KatG activation;
  • rifampicin: inhibits RNA polymerase (rpoB);
  • pyrazinamide: acts against semi-dormant bacilli;
  • ethambutol: inhibits arabinosyltransferase.
  • Pharmacogenomics: NAT2 acetylator status affects isoniazid exposure and hepatotoxicity (not retrieved in this session; verify in PharmGKB/CPIC).

Surgical and interventional (NCIT labels: Splenectomy; Percutaneous Drainage) - Splenectomy plus anti-TB therapy is curative and was historically common. Current opinion reserves it for rupture, failed medical therapy or diagnostic uncertainty (PMC4888408; PMID 38919240). - Percutaneous (pigtail) drainage of tuberculous abscesses works as an adjunct; reported success rates for splenic abscess drainage in general are 67–100% (PMC7920217).

Supportive care: nutrition, analgesia. After splenectomy: vaccination against encapsulated organisms (general practice).

Advanced therapeutics (gene, cell, RNA, targeted, immunotherapy): not applicable. No splenic-TB clinical trials (NCT IDs) were found.

Adverse events: hepatotoxicity (H, R, Z), peripheral neuropathy (H), optic neuritis (E), drug interactions (R). These are general knowledge; FAERS was not queried.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • BCG in infancy, which strongly protects against miliary and disseminated childhood TB (Mangtani 2014 RR 0.1; Trunz 2006; UCL, LSHTM);
  • infection control and contact tracing;
  • ART for people with HIV.
  • Prophylaxis: screen for latent TB infection and give TB preventive treatment before anti-TNF therapy (PMID 11596589), and for people with HIV (WHO guidance; not separately retrieved).
  • Secondary prevention: FASH ultrasound in HIV-positive patients with suspected TB (PMID 31456550); early tissue diagnosis.
  • Tertiary prevention:
  • complete the full anti-TB course;
  • follow-up imaging;
  • after splenectomy, vaccines (pneumococcal, meningococcal, Hib) and antibiotic awareness (general practice).
  • Genetic counseling: only for families with MSMD.
  • Public health: WHO End TB strategy; in animals, control of M. bovis in livestock and pasteurisation of milk.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Species affected:
  • Cattle and wildlife with M. bovis. In white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus) in Michigan, gross TB lesions were found in liver, spleen, rumen and mammary gland, sites "apparently not previously reported in this species" (J Wildl Dis 2001;37:608).
  • Spleen-specific frequency data for cattle were not retrieved.
  • Zoonotic potential: M. bovis is transmissible to hunters, processors and others (Cornell CWHL). Lesions caused by M. avium subsp. hominissuis can mimic M. bovis in deer (CDC EID 2020).
  • Breeds (VBO), orthologous genes, OMIA: Not applicable (non-Mendelian).
  • Comparative biology: Caseous granulomas are conserved across mammals. Mice (except C3HeB/FeJ) usually do not form caseous necrosis.

15. Model Organisms

  • Mouse aerosol infection model (M. tuberculosis H37Rv/Erdman):
  • Spread to the spleen at roughly 11–14 days, with spleen CFU as a standard readout of dissemination (PMC128141).
  • C57BL/6 (relatively resistant) vs C3H/DBA/2 (susceptible); host genetics affect the rate of dissemination.
  • C3HeB/FeJ ("Kramnik") mice form hypoxic, caseous granulomas that resemble human necrotic lesions, but lung CFU varies widely (PMC4457037; Frontiers 2024).
  • BCG protection measured in the spleen varies by strain (PMC7160682).
  • Genetic models: Knockouts of Ifng, Ifngr1, Tnf and Il12b in mice model susceptibility to disseminated mycobacterial disease (general knowledge; not retrieved in this session, verify in MGI).
  • Zebrafish–M. marinum: Reveals the mechanism of granuloma formation and TNF-driven necrosis (Pagán & Ramakrishnan, PMID 25377142).
  • Limitations: No model specifically reproduces isolated splenic TB or splenic abscess. Mouse spleen involvement is part of systemic spread, not a model of the focal human disease.
  • Resources: MGI, ZFIN, IMSR (not queried directly).
