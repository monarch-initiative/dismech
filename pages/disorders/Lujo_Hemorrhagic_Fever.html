Lujo Hemorrhagic Fever

Infectious MONDO:0017872 Pathograph 14 Show in embeddings browser viral infectious disease viral hemorrhagic fever

Lujo hemorrhagic fever is a severe arenaviral viral hemorrhagic fever caused by Lujo virus, a mammarenavirus first recognized in a nosocomial Southern African outbreak. Disease starts with an abrupt febrile systemic phase, commonly followed during the first week by pharyngitis, chest pain, rash, minor bleeding, and cervicofacial swelling; fatal cases can progress in the second week to disseminated intravascular coagulation, shock, multi-organ failure, and late neurologic signs. LUJV entry is unusual among arenaviruses: the viral glycoprotein uses NRP2 at the cell surface and CD63 to support low-pH endosomal fusion, and productive virion release requires LUJV Z late-domain function plus site-1-protease cleavage of the glycoprotein precursor.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — LHF is caused by Lujo virus, a divergent Old World arenavirus; there is no human causal gene

Lujo virus is the etiologic agent of LHF. It is an enveloped, bisegmented negative-sense/ambisense RNA virus of the family Arenaviridae, genus Mammarenavirus. Full-genome analysis during its discovery showed LUJV to be "unique and branching off the ancestral node of the Old World arenaviruses," with a G1 glycoprotein sequence "highly diverse and almost equidistant from that of other Old World and New World arenaviruses" (PMID: 19478873). This places LUJV outside both classical arenavirus serogroups. The name "Lujo" is a portmanteau of Lusaka and Johannesburg, the two cities linked by the 2008 outbreak. Because the disease is infectious, the "causal gene" fields (OMIM, HGNC, causal gene) are not applicable; the disease-level identifier is MONDO:0017872.

Evidence quote: "Full genome analyses of LUJV showed it to be unique and branching off the ancestral node of the Old World arenaviruses. The virus G1 glycoprotein sequence was highly diverse and almost equidistant from that of other Old World and New World arenaviruses" (PMID: 19478873).

Finding 2 — LUJV uses NRP2 as entry receptor and CD63 as intracellular fusion cofactor, distinct from all other arenaviruses

A genome-wide haploid genetic screen identified the transmembrane protein neuropilin-2 (NRP2) and the tetraspanin CD63 as the host factors required for LUJV glycoprotein (GP)-mediated infection. LUJV GP1 binds the N-terminal (a1/a2) domain of NRP2, while CD63 stimulates pH-activated LUJV GP-mediated membrane fusion in the late endosome (PMID: 29120745). This is mechanistically distinct from Old World arenaviruses (which use α-dystroglycan) and New World arenaviruses (which use transferrin receptor 1); pseudotype studies confirmed LUJV entry is independent of both α-dystroglycan and hTfR1, and depends instead on an unidentified receptor later shown to be NRP2, with additional dependence on Niemann-Pick C1 (NPC1) and cholesterol transport (PMID: 24741091). Structural work defined the receptor-binding mode by X-ray crystallography of the GP1–NRP2 complex (PMID: 30150732), and cryo-EM of the complete trimeric spike revealed a single NRP2 molecule bound at the apex via an unusual arginine-methionine interaction (PMID: 39169025). The host-range determinant is a phenylalanine at position 143 in the human CD63 large extracellular loop, which is required for efficient membrane fusion and infection (PMID: 35164564).

Evidence quotes: "A genome-wide haploid genetic screen identified the transmembrane protein neuropilin 2 (NRP2) and tetraspanin CD63 as factors for LUJV GP-mediated infection. LUJV GP binds the N-terminal domain of NRP2, while CD63 stimulates pH-activated LUJV GP-mediated membrane fusion" (PMID: 29120745); "a phenylalanine at position 143 in human CD63 was the key residue for efficient membrane fusion and VSVΔG*-LUJV/GP infection" (PMID: 35164564).

Finding 3 — Clinical course: febrile prodrome progressing to shock, DIC, and multi-organ failure

In the 2008 outbreak, illness "typically began with the abrupt onset of fever, malaise, headache, and myalgias followed successively by sore throat, chest pain, gastrointestinal symptoms, rash, minor hemorrhage, subconjunctival injection, and neck and facial swelling over the first week of illness. No major hemorrhage was noted." Neurological signs appeared late. "Shock and multi-organ system failure, often with evidence of disseminated intravascular coagulopathy, ensued in the second week, with death in four of the five cases." The single survivor received early ribavirin plus aggressive supportive care (PMID: 25393244). Laboratory features closely mirror Lassa fever — fever, thrombocytopenia, and transaminitis — and the clinical syndrome is "remarkably similar to Lassa hemorrhagic fever," with fever, headache, vomiting, diarrhea, arthralgia, myalgia, and signs of hemorrhagic syndrome (PMID: 29733163).

Finding 4 — Host immune response, not viral burden, drives LHF severity

Two independent animal models converge on the conclusion that host immune dysregulation determines outcome. In cynomolgus macaques, LUJV caused mild non-lethal illness, and transcriptomics of PBMCs revealed a 72-hour delay in the induction of inflammatory, antiviral, and cell-death gene programs compared with lethal Lassa infection (PMID: 25520505). In strain 13/N guinea pigs, survival correlated with the immune response rather than viral titer: despite similarly high LUJV titers across lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, and serum, young females survived with low inflammatory cytokines, whereas adults and young males died with cytokine storms featuring elevated IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, and CXCL10 (PMID: 41118368). This reveals both a central pathogenic mechanism (immunopathology) and age/sex disease modifiers.

Evidence quotes: "survival outcomes strongly correlated with immune responses rather than viral burden. Adult animals and young males developed more severe clinical signs, heightened hematological and biochemical abnormalities, and pronounced cytokine storms, particularly elevated IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, and CXCL10 levels" (PMID: 41118368); "We detected a 72-h delay in the induction of host responses to infection during LUJV infection compared to that of the animals infected with LASV... particularly apparent among groups of genes promoting cell death" (PMID: 25520505).

Finding 5 — Animal models: macaque (mild), guinea pig and STAT-1 KO mouse (partial lethal disease)

No single model fully reproduces fatal human LHF, but complementary systems exist. Cynomolgus macaques develop mild, non-lethal illness (PMID: 25520505). Strain 13/N guinea pigs are "a promising model for LUJV pathogenesis," recapitulating hematologic/biochemical abnormalities and age/sex-dependent lethality (PMID: 41118368). STAT-1-deficient mice develop a partially lethal disease in which "several key hematological, clinical chemistry, and histologic findings common to LHF disease are recapitulated" (PMID: 42075721). Standard inbred mice and hamsters are relatively resistant because their CD63 lacks the human F143 residue required for efficient entry (PMID: 35164564). Pseudotyped VSV bearing LUJV GP enables BSL-2 study of entry (PMID: 24741091; PMID: 22396728).

Finding 6 — Epidemiology: a single 2008 outbreak, 80% CFR, reservoir unknown, no approved countermeasures

"Lujo virus is a novel Old World arenavirus identified in Southern Africa in 2008 as the cause of a viral hemorrhagic fever (VHF) characterized by nosocomial transmission with a high case fatality rate of 80% (4/5 cases)" (PMID: 27593704). The index patient from Lusaka, Zambia, was air-evacuated to Johannesburg, with secondary transmission to four healthcare workers. LUJV remains the only strain detected to date (PMID: 24096270), and "LUJV remains poorly understood, with no approved treatments, vaccines, or known reservoir" (PMID: 41118368). An arenaviral rodent reservoir is presumed but unidentified. Rapid RT-PCR assays were developed for molecular diagnosis (PMID: 24096270). LUJV is a BSL-4 / WHO risk-group-4 pathogen.

Finding 7 — GPC matured by site-1 protease at an RKLM motif; Z-protein drives ESCRT-independent budding

LUJV's glycoprotein precursor GPC is proteolytically cleaved by cellular site-1 protease (S1P/SKI-1, gene MBTPS1) at an RKLM motif, and the S1P inhibitor PF-429242 reduced LUJV production. The matrix protein Z late (L) domains are required for efficient virus-like-particle release, but the virus buds independently of the canonical ESCRT factors Tsg101, ALIX/AIP1, and Vps4A/B (PMID: 26719243). Both the S1P maturation step and the Z-driven budding step represent druggable nodes.

Evidence quotes: "LUJV GPC is cleaved by site 1 protease (S1P) at the RKLM motif, and treatment with the S1P inhibitor PF-429242 reduced LUJV production" and "The L domains in Z are required for efficient virus-like particle release, but Tsg101, ALIX/AIP1, and Vps4A/B are unnecessary for budding" (PMID: 26719243).

Finding 8 — Multiple experimental anti-LUJV strategies; only ribavirin used clinically

Using VSV-LUJV/GP pseudotype entry screens, cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1) antagonists rimonabant, AM251, and AM281 were identified as robust inhibitors of LUJV entry (PMID: 37633192). FDA-approved drug-library screens identified additional entry inhibitors (PMID: 34925303). A recombinant reporter-LUJV high-throughput platform identified six compounds with robust anti-LUJV activity, including agents with prior pan-arenavirus activity (PMID: 34203149). The broad-spectrum lectin griffithsin blocks LUJV glycoprotein-mediated entry (PMID: 42037406). Clinically, only ribavirin (with supportive care) has been used, in the single survivor (PMID: 25393244). All targeted agents remain preclinical/investigational; none is approved for LHF.

Evidence quote: "Cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1) antagonists, such as rimonabant, AM251 and AM281, have been identified as robust inhibitors of LUJV entry" (PMID: 37633192).

Finding 9 — Tissue injury follows shared VHF mechanisms; CD63 is LUJV's intracellular fusion cofactor analogous to LAMP1 for Lassa

Across arenaviral and filoviral hemorrhagic fevers, pathogenesis involves "deficiency of hepatic synthesis of coagulation factors owing to hepatocellular necrosis, cytokine storm, increased permeability by vascular endothelial growth factor, complement activation, and disseminated intravascular coagulation" (PMID: 23121052). In a primate Lassa model, shock arose from "biochemical dysfunctions of platelets and endothelial cells," with loss of endothelial prostacyclin production and impaired platelet aggregation despite normal platelet counts (PMID: 3543155). LUJV parallels Lassa mechanistically: both are Old World arenaviruses whose GPC first engages a cell-surface receptor, then switches to an intracellular factor for low-pH fusion. Lassa switches from α-dystroglycan to LAMP1 (PMID: 29295909), whereas LUJV uses NRP2 then the tetraspanin CD63 (F143) (PMID: 29120745; PMID: 35164564).

Evidence quotes: "The pathogenic mechanisms... include deficiency of hepatic synthesis of coagulation factors owing to hepatocellular necrosis, cytokine storm, increased permeability by vascular endothelial growth factor, complement activation, and disseminated intravascular coagulation" (PMID: 23121052); "A unique feature of LASV entry is a pH-dependent switch from a primary cell surface receptor (α-dystroglycan) to an endosomal receptor, lysosomal-associated membrane protein (Lamp1)" (PMID: 29295909).

Finding 10 — NP exoribonuclease likely underlies delayed innate response; MOPEVAC is a candidate vaccine platform

Arenaviral nucleoproteins carry a conserved DEDDH exoribonuclease (RNase) domain that suppresses type I interferon; mutating the catalytic residues in Pichinde virus NP abolishes IFN suppression, causing high IFN induction and failure to replicate in IFN-competent cells and guinea pigs (PMID: 25878103). By homology, this conserved mechanism provides a molecular basis for the ~72-hour delay in innate/antiviral gene induction in LUJV-infected macaques (PMID: 25520505) and for the worsened disease in STAT-1-deficient mice (PMID: 42075721). For prevention, a recombinant hyperattenuated Mopeia virus platform (MOPEVAC) expressing heterologous arenaviral glycoproteins has been developed as a pan-arenavirus vaccine strategy (PMID: 29593043).

Evidence quote: "Previous structural and functional analyses of arenavirus nucleoproteins (NPs) revealed a conserved DEDDH exoribonuclease (RNase) domain that is important for type I interferon (IFN) suppression" (PMID: 25878103).

1. Disease Information

Lujo hemorrhagic fever is a severe, acute viral hemorrhagic fever caused by Lujo virus, an Old World mammarenavirus. The disease is defined at the disease level (not patient-EHR level); essentially all clinical knowledge derives from the aggregated five-case 2008 outbreak and subsequent experimental virology. - Mondo: MONDO:0017872 - ICD-10: A96.8 (other arenaviral hemorrhagic fevers); ICD-11: arenaviral hemorrhagic fever grouping (1D60.Y) - OMIM: Not applicable (infectious disease, no Mendelian locus) - Orphanet: Listed among rare arenaviral/viral hemorrhagic fevers - MeSH: "Hemorrhagic Fevers, Viral"; "Arenaviridae Infections" - Synonyms: Lujo viral hemorrhagic fever; Zambian hemorrhagic fever - Pathogen taxonomy (NCBI Taxon): Lujo mammarenavirus (taxid 649188); family Arenaviridae; genus Mammarenavirus

2. Etiology

Primary cause: infection with Lujo virus — an infectious, not genetic, etiology (PMID: 19478873). Risk factors are exposure-based: close/nosocomial contact with an infected patient or body fluids. Four of the five known cases were healthcare workers infected caring for the index patient (PMID: 27593704). No host genetic susceptibility or protective loci have been identified in humans. Experimental models reveal age and sex as disease modifiers (young females survived; adults and young males died in guinea pigs) (PMID: 41118368). Host STAT1/type I IFN signaling is protective, as STAT1-deficient mice develop lethal disease (PMID: 42075721). No gene–environment interaction data in humans exist (single small outbreak).

5. Environmental Information

The sole environmental/infectious factor is exposure to LUJV. The natural reservoir is unknown but presumed to be a rodent by analogy to other arenaviruses (PMID: 41118368). Documented human-to-human spread was nosocomial, via contact with blood/body fluids (PMID: 27593704). Infectious agent: Lujo mammarenavirus (NCBI taxid 649188).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating infection → clinical manifestation):

  1. LUJV is introduced via mucosal/percutaneous exposure to infected body fluids, leading to infection of permissive cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, endothelium inferred by analogy to Lassa).
  2. LUJV GP1 binds the N-terminal domain of cell-surface NRP2, resulting in receptor-mediated endocytosis (PMID: 29120745; PMID: 30150732).
  3. In the acidifying late endosome, engagement of CD63 (F143) triggers pH-activated GP2-mediated membrane fusion (a receptor "switch" analogous to Lassa α-DG→LAMP1), leading to release of the ribonucleoprotein into the cytoplasm (PMID: 35164564; PMID: 29295909). (GPC was first primed by host S1P cleavage at RKLM during biogenesis — PMID: 26719243.)
  4. Viral replication proceeds; the NP DEDDH exoribonuclease degrades immunostimulatory RNA, resulting in suppression of type I interferon and a ~72-hour delay in innate antiviral/inflammatory gene induction (inferred for LUJV from conserved arenaviral biology + macaque transcriptomics) (PMID: 25878103; PMID: 25520505).
  5. BRANCH A (controlled response → survival): an appropriately timed, measured immune response limits immunopathology → mild/non-lethal disease (macaques; young-female guinea pigs) (PMID: 25520505; PMID: 41118368).
  6. BRANCH B (dysregulated response → death): delayed-then-explosive cytokine storm (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, CXCL10) leads to systemic immunopathology (PMID: 41118368).
  7. Cytokines + VEGF-mediated endothelial permeability and endothelial/platelet biochemical dysfunction (loss of prostacyclin, impaired aggregation) result in capillary leak, facial/neck edema, and effusions (PMID: 23121052; PMID: 3543155).
  8. Hepatocellular injury/necrosis leads to deficient synthesis of coagulation factors; combined with consumptive coagulopathy, this results in DIC (PMID: 23121052).
  9. Capillary leak + DIC + vasoplegia lead to distributive/hypovolemic shock and multi-organ failure, the terminal events in week 2 (PMID: 25393244).

Upstream vs downstream: viral entry/replication and IFN suppression are upstream; cytokine storm, endothelial/platelet dysfunction, and DIC are downstream effectors of tissue injury. 8. Temporal Development

Onset: acute, adult-onset, with abrupt fever after an incubation of roughly 1–2 weeks (arenaviral range). Progression: week 1 = febrile prodrome and multisystem symptoms; week 2 = shock, DIC, multi-organ failure, death. Rate: rapid. Course: monophasic, self-limited in survivors (recovery) or fatal. In the one survivor, early ribavirin and intensive support were associated with recovery (PMID: 25393244). The critical window for intervention appears to be early, before the week-2 cytokine-storm/DIC cascade.

9. Inheritance and Population

Epidemiology: a single recognized outbreak (2008), 5 cases, 80% CFR (4/5) (PMID: 27593704). Prevalence/incidence are effectively zero outside this outbreak; true endemic burden is unknown due to under-surveillance in the region (PMID: 25416679). Inheritance: not applicable (infectious). Geographic distribution: Southern Africa — epidemiologically linked to Zambia (Lusaka) and South Africa (Johannesburg). Sex/age: too few human cases for a ratio; experimental models show male sex and adult/older age associated with worse outcomes (PMID: 41118368).

10. Diagnostics

Laboratory: thrombocytopenia, transaminitis, coagulopathy (Lassa-like) (PMID: 29733163). Molecular diagnosis of choice: real-time RT-PCR assays specific for Lujo viral RNA, developed and validated against the sole isolate (PMID: 24096270). Virus isolation and serology require BSL-4. BSL-2 surrogate systems (VSV-LUJV/GP pseudotypes) support research/entry assays (PMID: 24741091; PMID: 22396728). Differential diagnosis: Lassa fever (most important — clinically near-identical), Ebola/Marburg, Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, Rift Valley fever, severe malaria, leptospirosis, typhoid, and other septic/DIC syndromes. Genetic/omics diagnostics: not applicable for host diagnosis. No newborn/carrier screening applies.

11. Outcome/Prognosis

Mortality: 80% CFR in the only outbreak. Prognostic factors: early antiviral therapy and intensive supportive care (favorable — one survivor); magnitude/timing of the inflammatory cytokine response is the principal determinant of outcome, with cytokine storm predicting death (PMID: 41118368; PMID: 25520505). Complications: DIC, hemorrhage, shock, hepatic and multi-organ failure. Recovery: survivors can recover; long-term sequelae are undocumented given the tiny cohort. Candidate prognostic biomarkers: IL-1β, IL-6, CXCL10 (from models).

No approved antiviral or vaccine exists (PMID: 41118368). Management is early ribavirin plus aggressive supportive care (fluid resuscitation, correction of coagulopathy, organ support), as used in the single survivor (PMID: 25393244) — NCIT: Ribavirin (C790); Supportive Care (C15277). 13. Prevention

Primary prevention rests on strict infection control (barrier nursing, isolation, PPE, safe handling of body fluids), given nosocomial spread (PMID: 27593704; PMID: 29733163). Timely molecular diagnosis enables patient isolation to prevent onward transmission (PMID: 24096270). Immunization: no licensed vaccine; the MOPEVAC hyperattenuated-Mopeia platform expressing heterologous glycoproteins is a candidate pan-arenavirus vaccine (PMID: 29593043). Reservoir control cannot yet be targeted because the reservoir is unidentified.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

Pathogen: Lujo mammarenavirus (NCBI taxid 649188). Presumed reservoir: an unidentified rodent (zoonotic, by analogy to other arenaviruses) — zoonotic potential is high but the specific host and spillover route are unknown (PMID: 41118368; PMID: 25416679). No naturally occurring companion-animal or livestock disease has been reported. Experimental cross-species susceptibility is governed by the host CD63 F143 residue (PMID: 35164564).

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Single outbreak, n=5. All human clinical knowledge rests on five cases; incidence, prevalence, full phenotype spectrum, sex ratio, incubation period, and long-term sequelae are essentially unknown.
  2. Reservoir unidentified. No animal host or spillover route is known, precluding reservoir-targeted prevention and ecological risk assessment.
  3. No fully lethal animal model. Macaques are mild; guinea pig and STAT-1 KO models are only partially lethal; wild-type rodents resist infection due to non-human CD63.
  4. Mechanistic inference by homology. The NP-exonuclease IFN-suppression step and some VHF tissue-injury mechanisms (VEGF permeability, DIC) are inferred from related arenaviruses rather than demonstrated directly for LUJV.
  5. No approved countermeasures. All targeted therapeutics and the MOPEVAC vaccine are preclinical; ribavirin efficacy is anecdotal (one survivor who also received intensive supportive care).
  6. Genomic diversity unknown. Diagnostics are validated against a single isolate; antigenic/genetic variation across any future strains is uncharacterized.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Reservoir surveillance in Zambia/Southern Africa — trap and screen rodents by RT-PCR/serology to identify the natural host and define spillover ecology.
  2. Develop a robustly lethal small-animal model — e.g., humanized-CD63 (F143 knock-in) mice or further-characterized guinea pig strains — to enable rigorous efficacy testing.
  3. Test leading entry inhibitors in vivo — prioritize CB1 antagonists, griffithsin, and the S1P inhibitor PF-429242 in the guinea pig/STAT-1 KO models, alone and with ribavirin.
  4. Directly validate the NP-exonuclease IFN-suppression mechanism in LUJV by catalytic-residue mutagenesis, testing attenuation and immunogenicity (toward a rationally attenuated vaccine).
  5. Advance MOPEVAC-LUJV (Mopeia platform expressing LUJV GPC) through immunogenicity and challenge studies.
  6. Define immunopathology biomarkers (IL-1β, IL-6, CXCL10) as prognostic/therapeutic-response markers and evaluate host-directed immunomodulation to interrupt the cytokine-storm→DIC→shock cascade.
  7. Broaden and field-deploy diagnostics — expand RT-PCR design to anticipate genetic variants and distinguish LUJV from Lassa in endemic settings.
