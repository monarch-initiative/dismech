Combined Factor V and Factor VIII Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0018175 Pathograph 34 Show in embeddings browser Rare bleeding disorder Inherited blood coagulation disorder

An autosomal recessive bleeding disorder in which plasma coagulation factor V and factor VIII are reduced together, usually to 5-30% of normal. It is not caused by variants in F5 or F8. Instead, biallelic loss-of-function variants in LMAN1 (ERGIC-53) or MCFD2 disrupt a two-subunit, calcium-dependent cargo receptor that carries both factors from the endoplasmic reticulum to the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment, so less of each is secreted. LMAN1 is a hexameric transmembrane mannose-binding lectin and MCFD2 a small soluble EF-hand protein. Missense variants in either gene act mainly by disrupting formation of the LMAN1-MCFD2 complex, and the two genetic types are clinically indistinguishable, so they are curated as subtypes of one entry with one pathograph. Patients with MCFD2 variants have on average somewhat lower factor levels than patients with LMAN1 variants, with wide overlap. Bleeding is mild to moderate: excessive bleeding after trauma, surgery, dental extraction, circumcision or childbirth is the most common presentation, with epistaxis, gingival bleeding, bruising and menorrhagia as the common spontaneous symptoms. Hemarthrosis, gastrointestinal bleeding, hematuria and intracranial hemorrhage are uncommon. Screening shows prolongation of both the prothrombin time and the activated partial thromboplastin time. There is no factor V concentrate, so treatment combines fresh frozen plasma (the only source of factor V) with factor VIII concentrate or desmopressin, plus antifibrinolytics, mostly on demand or around procedures.

LMAN1-MCFD2 Cargo Receptor Complex Disruption
LMAN1 and MCFD2 form a calcium-dependent cargo receptor that cycles between the endoplasmic reticulum and the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment. The LMAN1 carbohydrate recognition domain holds separable binding sites for MCFD2 and for high-mannose glycans on factor V and factor VIII, and the MCFD2 EF-hand domains bind both LMAN1 and the cargo. Loss of either subunit, or any missense variant that prevents the two from associating, removes a functional receptor.

Impaired ER-to-Golgi Export of Factor V and Factor VIII
Without the cargo receptor, newly made factor V (in hepatocytes) and factor VIII (in endothelial cells) leave the endoplasmic reticulum inefficiently, so less of each is secreted. Export is reduced rather than abolished, which is why plasma levels fall to roughly 5-30% of normal rather than to zero.

Reduced Plasma Factor V and Factor VIII
Plasma factor V and factor VIII activities fall together, usually to 5-30% of normal (typically 10-20%). Mean levels are lower in MCFD2-related than in LMAN1-related disease. Bleeding severity correlates poorly with factor levels, and two simultaneous deficiencies do not bleed more than a single factor V or factor VIII deficiency of similar degree.

  An autosomal recessive bleeding disorder in which plasma coagulation factor V
  and factor VIII are reduced together, usually to 5-30% of normal. It is not
  caused by variants in F5 or F8. Instead, biallelic loss-of-function variants
  in LMAN1 (ERGIC-53) or MCFD2 disrupt a two-subunit, calcium-dependent cargo

  disrupting formation of the LMAN1-MCFD2 complex, and the two genetic types are
  clinically indistinguishable, so they are curated as subtypes of one entry
  with one pathograph. Patients with MCFD2 variants have on average somewhat
  lower factor levels than patients with LMAN1 variants, with wide overlap.

  dental extraction, circumcision or childbirth is the most common

  thromboplastin time.

1. Disease Information

F5F8D is a rare autosomal-recessive coagulopathy characterized by the simultaneous, coordinate reduction of two clotting factors — FV and FVIII — that are otherwise genetically and structurally normal. The defect lies in a shared intracellular transport receptor, making F5F8D a paradigm of a "secretory-pathway cargo-receptor disease" rather than a classic single-factor deficiency.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. F5F8D is caused by biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) loss-of-function mutations in LMAN1 or MCFD2. Positional cloning first mapped the locus to chromosome 18q21 and identified ERGIC-53/LMAN1 as the causal gene, with two mutations "accounting for all affected individuals in nine families studied" (PMID: 9546392). A second gene, MCFD2 on 2p21, accounts for LMAN1-negative families.

  • ~70% of families carry LMAN1 mutations; ~15–30% carry MCFD2 mutations.
  • Essentially all LMAN1 alleles are null: in a survey of 35 families, 13 distinct mutations comprised "3 splice site mutations, 6 insertions and deletions resulting in translational frameshifts, 3 nonsense codons, and elimination of the translation initiation codon" (PMID: 10090934).
  • Inheritance is autosomal recessive; heterozygous carriers are asymptomatic.

Genetic risk factors: The only established risk factor is inheritance of two pathogenic LMAN1 or MCFD2 alleles. Consanguinity sharply increases risk in endogamous populations. Founder alleles (see §9) elevate carrier frequency regionally.

Environmental risk factors: None established as causal. Because the phenotype is provoked, environmental/situational triggers of bleeding (surgery, trauma, dental procedures, childbirth, antiplatelet/anticoagulant drugs) convert a latent defect into a clinical event, but they do not cause the disease.

Protective factors: No genetic or environmental protective modifiers are established. Mechanistically, residual LMAN1 expression acts in a dose-dependent protective manner — hypomorphic alleles retaining partial function predict milder phenotypes (mouse data, §6).

Gene–environment interactions: Not formally characterized. The practical interaction is between the genetic secretion defect and hemostatic challenges (surgery, parturition), which determine when bleeding manifests.

5. Environmental Information

F5F8D is a purely monogenic disorder with no environmental, lifestyle, or infectious etiology. No toxins, radiation, occupational exposures, dietary factors, or pathogens cause or trigger the disease. Environmental factors only matter as bleeding precipitants (surgery, trauma, parturition) and through drugs that impair hemostasis (antiplatelets, anticoagulants, NSAIDs), which should be used cautiously. Infectious agents: not applicable.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function mutation in LMAN1 or MCFD2 → results in absence or dysfunction of the corresponding protein.
  2. Loss of functional LMAN1 and/or MCFD2 → leads to failure to assemble a functional LMAN1–MCFD2 cargo-receptor complex in the ER.
  3. Absent cargo receptor → results in failure to capture newly synthesized FV and FVIII in the ER and package them into COPII-coated vesicles for ER-to-Golgi transport.
  4. Impaired ER exit → leads to reduced secretion of FV and FVIII into plasma (and reduced platelet FV).
  5. Reduced circulating FV and FVIII (~5–20% of normal) → results in impaired generation of both the tenase (FVIIIa-dependent) and prothrombinase (FVa-dependent) complexes.
  6. Impaired thrombin generation on both the intrinsic/common-pathway axes → leads to prolonged aPTT (FVIII) and prolonged PT (FV), i.e., the signature dual coagulation-screen abnormality.
  7. Deficient hemostasis → manifests as mild-to-moderate mucocutaneous and procedure/trauma-provoked bleeding.

(Branch: whether the lesion is in LMAN1 or MCFD2 converges on the same complex failure; MCFD2 defects tend to lower factor levels more. Inferred step: the exact quantitative link between residual LMAN1 and human phenotype severity is extrapolated from mouse dose-response data.)

Molecular and cellular detail

The cargo-receptor complex. LMAN1 (ERGIC-53) is a hexameric type-1 transmembrane mannose-specific lectin that cycles between the ER and the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC). Its luminal carbohydrate-recognition domain (CRD) contains separable binding sites: one for its partner MCFD2 (on the N-terminal β-sheet) and one for mannose/cargo. "The CRD of LMAN1 contains distinct, separable binding sites for both its partner protein (MCFD2) and the cargo proteins (FV/FVIII)," and LMAN1 oligomerization is necessary for receptor function (PMID: 20817851). Structural work pinpointed His178 and Gly251/252 as critical cargo-binding residues (PMID: 23709226).

MCFD2 is the FV/FVIII-specific adaptor. MCFD2 is a soluble EF-hand (calcium-binding) protein. Crystallography shows a 1:1 ERGIC-53-CRD : MCFD2 complex, and "the interaction is independent of sugar-binding of ERGIC-53 and involves most of the missense mutation sites of MCFD2 so far reported in F5F8D" (PMID: 20142513). Critically, MCFD2 is not required for transport of the receptor's other cargoes: "ERGIC-53 can bind cargo glycoproteins in an MCFD2-independent fashion and … MCFD2 is a recruitment factor for blood coagulation factors V and VIII" (PMID: 17010120). This is why F5F8D patients lack lysosomal or other cargo phenotypes — only the FV/FVIII arm is affected. MCFD2 binds a 10-amino-acid segment of the FVIII B-domain (PMID: 32170195).

Division of labor. Cell-line studies indicate that MCFD2 performs cargo binding/transport while LMAN1 acts as a shuttle carrier of MCFD2: overexpression of MCFD2 alone rescues FV/FVIII secretion in LMAN1-deficient cells, and N-glycan binding by LMAN1 is not strictly essential for transport (PMID: 36490287).

pH/Ca²⁺-triggered cargo cycle. Cargo capture in the ER is Ca²⁺-dependent at neutral pH; a conserved CRD histidine acts as a pH/Ca²⁺ sensor so that mild acidification and lower Ca²⁺ in the ERGIC trigger glycoprotein release: "Acidification of cells inhibited the association of ERGIC-53 with the known cargo … and dissociation of this glycoprotein in the ERGIC was impaired by organelle neutralization" (PMID: 14718532).

Broader cargo repertoire. The LMAN1–MCFD2 receptor also exports cathepsin C and cathepsin Z (PMID: 15849265; PMID: 17010120), alpha-1-antitrypsin (PMID: 18283111), and Mac-2BP/LGALS3BP (PMID: 23550150). Because MCFD2 is dedicated to FV/FVIII, humans with MCFD2 mutations lose only FV/FVIII transport; the other cargoes are handled MCFD2-independently.

Cell types and tissues. FVIII is synthesized primarily by endothelial cells, not hepatocytes — conditional-knockout mouse studies "demonstrate that endothelial cells are the primary biosynthetic source of murine FVIII and that hepatocytes make no significant contribution to the plasma FVIII pool" (PMID: 24719406). FV is produced by hepatocytes and megakaryocytes (platelet FV).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ/system level: The hematologic/coagulation system and the cardiovascular (blood/vasculature) system are primarily affected. There is no primary organ pathology; bleeding manifests at mucosal surfaces, skin, joints (rarely), and the reproductive tract.
  • Primary biosynthetic sites: Liver (hepatocytes) for FV; vascular endothelium for FVIII (PMID: 24719406); megakaryocytes/platelets carry a FV pool.
  • Tissue/cell level: hepatocytes (CL:0000182), endothelial cells (CL:0000115), megakaryocytes (CL:0000556).
  • Subcellular level: The molecular lesion resides in the early secretory pathway — endoplasmic reticulum (GO:0005783), ER-Golgi intermediate compartment/ERGIC (GO:0005793), and COPII vesicles (GO:0030134).
  • Localization of bleeding: Mucocutaneous surfaces (nasal mucosa, oral/gingival mucosa, skin), gastrointestinal and intracranial sites (rare), and the uterus/reproductive tract (menorrhagia, postpartum). Bleeding is systemic/multifocal, not lateralized.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Congenital — the secretion defect is present from birth. Clinical recognition is often delayed to childhood or adulthood because bleeding is mild; many cases surface at the first hemostatic challenge (circumcision, dental extraction, surgery, menarche, childbirth) or incidentally on preoperative screening.
  • Onset pattern: Insidious/chronic baseline with episodic, provoked bleeding events.
  • Progression: Stable and non-progressive. Factor levels remain constant over life.
  • Course: Lifelong (chronic), with excellent prognosis. No disease "stages."
  • Remission: Not applicable — the genetic defect is permanent; bleeding is managed, not cured. Replacement therapy produces transient correction.
  • Critical periods: Hemostatic challenges — surgery, dental procedures, trauma, menstruation, and especially pregnancy/delivery — are the windows requiring intervention. In pregnancy the physiologic FVIII rise is blunted because secretion (not synthesis) is limiting, so the defect persists and postpartum-hemorrhage risk remains (PMID: 19298378).

9. Inheritance and Population

Epidemiology: Estimated prevalence ~1 in 1,000,000 in the general population; F5F8D constitutes roughly 3% of rare coagulation disorders. "The prevalence of this disease in the general population is estimated to be 1:1,000,000 and is significantly higher in regions where consanguineous marriages are permitted, such as the Mideast and South Asia" (PMID: 39209292).

Inheritance: Autosomal recessive. Heterozygotes are asymptomatic. Equal sex incidence (though women are more often symptomatic due to menstrual/reproductive bleeding).

Penetrance/expressivity: High penetrance of the biochemical phenotype (low FV/FVIII) in biallelic carriers; variable expressivity of bleeding severity. No genetic anticipation (not a repeat-expansion disorder). Germline mosaicism not a recognized feature.

Founder effects and consanguinity: Strong. Documented founder alleles include an LMAN1 intron-9 donor-splice mutation prevalent among Tunisian Djerba Jews (carrier allele frequency ~0.011) and an exon-1 G insertion among Middle Eastern/Iraqi Jews: "a thymidine (T) to cytosine (C) transition in intron 9 at a donor splice site among Tunisian families. For each mutation, haplotype analysis revealed a founder effect" (PMID: 15166951). High prevalence is reported in consanguineous cohorts in Iran (PMID: 27894217; PMID: 23632381) and Pakistan (PMID: 28388959).

Population demographics: Enriched in Mediterranean, Middle-Eastern, and South-Asian populations where endogamy is common. Geographic distribution of variants tracks founder haplotypes. Sex ratio ~1:1.

10. Diagnostics

Diagnostic algorithm: 1. Screening coagulation tests: simultaneously prolonged PT and aPTT, both fully corrected by 1:1 mixing (indicates factor deficiency, not an inhibitor). 2. Specific factor assays: concordant reduction of FV:C and FVIII:C to ~5–20%, with normal levels of other coagulation factors and normal von Willebrand factor antigen/activity. This pattern distinguishes F5F8D from isolated hemophilia A (normal PT), isolated FV deficiency (normal FVIII), and type 2N/3 von Willebrand disease. Illustrative case: "a combined deficiency of factors V and VIII at 12.4% … and 9.1%, respectively, whereas other coagulation factor levels remained within the normal range, including the antigenic activity of von Willebrand" (PMID: 40296097). 3. Molecular confirmation: targeted LMAN1/MCFD2 sequencing, a rare-bleeding-disorder gene panel, or whole-exome sequencing (which identified a novel homozygous LMAN1 c.604C>T, PMID: 38659545). Dosage/copy-number analysis is needed because a heterozygous large MCFD2 deletion can masquerade as a homozygous substitution (PMID: 38764219).

Biomarkers: FV:C and FVIII:C are the defining biomarkers; reduced platelet FV supports the diagnosis. vWF is normal (key discriminator).

Clinical/bleeding assessment: Standardized bleeding-assessment tools (ISTH-BAT; EN-RBD bleeding score) quantify symptom burden, with bleeding score inversely correlated with factor activity (PMID: 27855295).

Differential diagnosis: hemophilia A (isolated FVIII, normal PT); isolated FV deficiency (normal FVIII); combined vitamin-K-dependent factor deficiency; von Willebrand disease types 2N/3; rare digenic coincidental F5 + F8 mutations (PMID: 41040772).

Screening: Carrier and cascade testing within affected consanguineous families; no population newborn screening exists given rarity and mild phenotype. Imaging/electrophysiology/biopsy are not diagnostic for this disorder.

LOINC concepts: PT (5902-2), aPTT (3173-2/14979-9), Factor V activity (3192-2), Factor VIII activity (3209-4), von Willebrand factor antigen (5088-1).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Prognosis is excellent with normal life expectancy. F5F8D is a milder rare bleeding disorder than FXIII, FX, or FVII deficiencies: "patients with factor XIII, factor X and factor VII more commonly presented severe life-threatening bleeding, while patients with combined factor V and factor VIII presented a milder phenotype" (PMID: 27894217).

  • Mortality: Low; disease-specific mortality is uncommon with appropriate peri-procedural and obstetric management.
  • Morbidity: Mucocutaneous bleeding, menorrhagia, and procedure/trauma-provoked bleeding; hemarthrosis and life-threatening CNS/GI bleeds are uncommon. A 37-case single-center India series documents the clinical spectrum (PMID: 20575023).
  • Disease course: Stable, lifelong, non-progressive. Many patients remain asymptomatic until a hemostatic challenge (PMID: 38912355; PMID: 38659545).
  • Prognostic factors: Baseline FV/FVIII activity broadly tracks bleeding tendency (MCFD2 genotype → lower levels), but factor levels correlate inconsistently with bleeding outcomes, particularly in pregnancy (PMID: 41789952), so clinical history and bleeding scores remain essential.

12. Treatment

Core principle: replace BOTH factors. Because no clotting-factor gene is defective but two factor proteins are low, therapy must raise both FV and FVIII. "Treatment of bleeding episodes requires a source of both FV and FVIII; replacement of FV is achieved through the use of fresh frozen plasma, and that of FVIII by desmopressin or specific FVIII concentrates, plasma-derived or recombinant FVIII products" (PMID: 19141160).

Pregnancy/peripartum: A documented F5F8D pregnancy (FV 29%, FVIII 21%; aPTT 78s) was managed with a protocol-based multidisciplinary plan: 2000 IU FVIII at onset of labor raising FVIII to 36% and FV to 38%, then 12-hourly tapering doses keeping FVIII >50% for 5 days — delivery with no bleeding: "An infusion of 2000IU of FVIII was implemented over 5 minutes; soon thereafter, PT was 17 seconds, aPTT patient/control ratio had decreased to 1.9 and FV and FVIII reached 38% and 36% respectively" (PMID: 24161289). Pharmacogenomics / gene / cell / targeted therapies: None approved or required; F5F8D is managed with replacement and antifibrinolytics. (Of note, the LMAN1–MCFD2 axis is being explored in the opposite direction as an anticoagulant target — GalNAc-siRNA knockdown of LMAN1/MCFD2 lowers FVIII and prolongs aPTT in mice, PMID: 39222205 — which conceptually validates the pathway but is not a treatment for the deficiency.)

Treatment strategy: On-demand replacement for bleeds/procedures; prophylaxis is rarely needed given the mild phenotype; antifibrinolytics first-line for menorrhagia and mucosal bleeding.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Not possible (monogenic, congenital). Genetic counseling and carrier/cascade screening in affected families — especially in consanguineous communities and founder populations — reduce recurrence; prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing is available where a familial mutation is known.
  • Secondary prevention: Early diagnosis via recognition of the dual PT/aPTT pattern on preoperative screening; confirm before elective surgery to plan hemostatic cover.
  • Tertiary prevention (preventing bleeding complications): Pre-procedure factor replacement, peripartum protocols, antifibrinolytics, avoidance of antiplatelet/anticoagulant drugs and intramuscular injections, routine dental care, and iron supplementation for menorrhagia-related anemia.
  • Counseling: Autosomal-recessive recurrence risk (25% for carrier couples); emphasize in consanguineous families. NSGC/ACMG genetic-counseling frameworks apply.
  • Immunization / public-health / environmental interventions: Not applicable (non-infectious, non-environmental).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy / orthologs: LMAN1 and MCFD2 are conserved in mammals. Mouse orthologs Lman1 and Mcfd2 have been functionally studied (see §15).
  • Natural disease in other species: No well-characterized spontaneous F5F8D-equivalent is established in companion animals or wildlife in the reviewed literature; the disease is primarily described in humans and in engineered mouse models.
  • Comparative biology: The LMAN1–MCFD2 cargo-receptor mechanism is evolutionarily conserved, making mouse models directly informative. A notable species difference: in mice the ER-exit defect is partial (FV/FVIII ~50% vs ~5–20% in humans), implying additional redundancy.
  • Zoonotic potential: None (non-infectious).

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

F5F8D is best understood as a trafficking disease masquerading as a coagulation-factor deficiency. The single unifying lesion — loss of the LMAN1–MCFD2 cargo receptor — explains every clinical observation: (1) why two unrelated factors fall together (they share a transporter); (2) why the factors are structurally normal (synthesis is intact, export is not); (3) why levels sit near 5–20% rather than zero (bulk-flow and residual secretion provide a floor, and residual-LMAN1 dose modulates it); (4) why the phenotype is mild and non-progressive (partial, stable secretion defect); and (5) why MCFD2 defects tend to be slightly more severe (MCFD2 is the dedicated FV/FVIII adaptor, so its loss more directly cripples their recruitment while sparing other cargoes). The MCFD2-is-a-recruitment-factor finding is the linchpin that reconciles the broad cargo repertoire of LMAN1 (cathepsins, α1-antitrypsin, Mac-2BP) with the narrow, FV/FVIII-only clinical picture of F5F8D.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Rarity-limited epidemiology. Prevalence (~1:1,000,000) is an estimate; true incidence, age distribution, and population-specific carrier frequencies outside founder groups are poorly quantified.
  2. Genotype–phenotype resolution is coarse. Although MCFD2 mutations trend toward lower factor levels, individual bleeding severity correlates imperfectly with factor activity, especially in pregnancy — predictive models are lacking.
  3. Incomplete cargo census. Mouse data (perinatal lethality, A1AT accumulation) imply additional unidentified LMAN1 cargoes whose loss could contribute to subtle, under-recognized human phenotypes.
  4. Model mismatch. Mouse knockouts do not reproduce the human factor-level depth, limiting their use for severity modeling.
  5. No disease-specific therapeutics. FV replacement still depends on FFP; no recombinant FV product and no gene/cell therapy exist for F5F8D.
  6. QoL data are sparse. Patient-reported outcomes, particularly the menstrual/reproductive burden in women, are under-studied.
  7. Digenic cases. Rare coincidental F5 + F8 defects can phenocopy F5F8D and complicate molecular diagnosis.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Variant functional classification pipeline: Use the established LMAN1/MCFD2-deficient cell lines (PMID: 36490287) as a standardized rescue assay to reclassify VUS and resolve digenic vs monogenic cases.
  2. Copy-number-aware molecular testing: Adopt routine dosage/CNV analysis of MCFD2 and LMAN1 to avoid misclassifying heterozygous large deletions as homozygous substitutions (PMID: 38764219).
  3. Natural-history registry: Pool founder-population cohorts (Tunisian, Iranian, Pakistani, Indian, Saudi) into a harmonized registry linking genotype, factor levels, ISTH-BAT/EN-RBD scores, and bleeding outcomes to build a severity-prediction model.
  4. Cargo discovery: Proteomic profiling of LMAN1/MCFD2-null cells and conditional mouse tissues to complete the cargo census and test whether additional cargoes produce subclinical human phenotypes.
  5. Therapeutic development: Evaluate recombinant FV products and the "passport-sequence" secretion-enhancement concept (PMID: 32170195) as routes to a dedicated FV replacement, reducing FFP dependence.
  6. Reproductive-health protocols: Prospectively validate individualized peripartum dual-factor protocols (PMID: 24161289) and quantify menorrhagia-related QoL and anemia burden in affected women.
  7. Carrier screening in endogamous communities: Offer targeted founder-allele carrier and cascade screening with genetic counseling to reduce recurrence.
