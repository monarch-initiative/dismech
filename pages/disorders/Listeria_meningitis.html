Listeria meningitis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0006836 Pathograph 24 Show in embeddings browser Bacterial meningitis Listeriosis

Listeria meningitis is foodborne or maternal-neonatal bacterial meningitis caused by invasive Listeria monocytogenes, in which a facultative intracellular organism crosses intestinal, placental, or postnatal entry barriers, survives in phagocytes, disseminates in blood, invades the central nervous system, escapes phagosomes through listeriolysin O, spreads between host cells through ActA- and efferocytosis-linked mechanisms, and produces suppurative meningoencephalitis with characteristic brainstem involvement in a subset of patients.

Impaired Host Cell-Mediated Immunity
Invasive listeriosis is largely confined to hosts whose cell-mediated immunity is compromised by age, pregnancy, immunosuppressive therapy, or malignancy; intact T-cell immunity normally contains the organism before it disseminates.

Hydrocephalus Ventricular involvement and inflammatory obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid flow produce contrast-enhancing ventricles or hydrocephalus in a minority of cases.

Headache Inflammation of the meninges and their sensory innervation produces headache.
Vomiting Meningeal irritation and raised intracranial pressure from the suppurative process provoke vomiting.
Reduced consciousness Parenchymal involvement of the brain by the suppurative process impairs the level of consciousness.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — Listeria is an "atypical" meningitis pathogen concentrated in the elderly, diabetic, and immunosuppressed

L. monocytogenes is a minority cause of community-acquired bacterial meningitis overall, but it is strongly over-represented in specific vulnerable populations, reflecting the requirement for intact cell-mediated immunity to control an intracellular pathogen.

In a Dutch nationwide prospective cohort, diabetes conferred a 2.2-fold higher risk of acquiring bacterial meningitis and was an independent risk factor for death (adjusted OR 1.98, 95% CI 1.13–3.48, P=0.017); Listeria caused 6% of meningitis episodes in diabetic patients (PMID: 27845429). Among patients ≥80 years, atypical pathogens including L. monocytogenes occurred substantially more often than in younger adults (49/149 [33%] vs 362/1991 [18%]; p<0.001) (PMID: 35352336). A Barcelona cohort likewise found Listeria more frequent among diabetics (18% vs 10%, p=0.033) (PMID: 32312231). Additional risk-group associations include alcoholism (Listeria caused 8% of alcoholic meningitis, with 25% mortality; PMID: 28065808), immunosuppressive medication (chiefly corticosteroids; PMID: 27613024), and systemic lupus erythematosus (PMID: 20213126).

"the risk of acquiring bacterial meningitis was 2.2-fold higher for diabetes patients" — PMID: 27845429

Interpretation: The disease is defined by host immune status. This has direct clinical consequence — any adult ≥50, or any immunocompromised or diabetic patient, presenting with meningitis should receive empirical ampicillin to cover Listeria in addition to a cephalosporin/vancomycin regimen.

Finding 2 — The ST6/CC6 genotype independently predicts unfavorable outcome

Outcome is partly determined by the bacterial genotype, not only the host. In 92 adults with L. monocytogenes meningitis pooled from two Dutch nationwide cohorts, the rate of unfavorable outcome rose from 27% (1998–2002) to 61% (2006–2012, P=0.002). Multivariate regression identified infection with sequence type 6 (ST6) as the sole independent predictor of unfavorable outcome (OR 3.77, 95% CI 1.07–13.33), and ST6 became more prevalent over time (4% vs 29%, P=0.009) (PMID: 23592828).

"Multivariate regression analysis identified infection with L. monocytogenes ST6 as the sole predictor of unfavorable outcome (odds ratio, 3.77; 95% confidence interval, 1.07-13.33)" — PMID: 23592828

This links to the genomic epidemiology of hypervirulent clonal complex 1 (CC1) and related lineages (PMID: 38165396) and is reinforced by animal-model data (Finding 6) showing genotype-dependent virulence.

Finding 3 — Neurolisteriosis presents chiefly as meningoencephalitis, with characteristic brainstem involvement

In the French MONALISA national prospective cohort (818 cases: 107 maternal-neonatal, 427 bacteraemia, 252 neurolisteriosis), neurolisteriosis presented as meningoencephalitis in 212/252 (84%) and brainstem (rhombencephalitis) involvement in 42/252 (17%). Three-month mortality was higher for pure bacteraemia than neurolisteriosis (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.41–0.69, p<0.0001). Among pregnant women, 26/107 (24%) experienced fetal loss — notably, never after 29 weeks' gestation (PMID: 28139432).

"Neurolisteriosis presented as meningoencephalitis in 212 (84%) of 252 patients; brainstem involvement was only reported in 42 (17%) of 252 patients" — PMID: 28139432

Historically, in an epidemic Swiss series, 6 of 8 adults with parenchymal CNS infection had brainstem involvement, and a preceding influenza-like prodrome was common (PMID: 3918344). The predilection for the brainstem distinguishes Listeria clinically from other bacterial meningitides.

Finding 4 — CNS invasion is mediated by internalins (InlA/InlB → E-cadherin/Met) with LLO/ActA driving intracellular survival and spread

The molecular pathogenesis is well characterized. Internalins InlA and InlB interact with host receptors E-cadherin and MET (mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor) to facilitate brain invasion, and L. monocytogenes activates NF-κB in brain endothelium (PMID: 30117097). Once internalized, listeriolysin O (LLO) mediates phagosomal escape into the cytosol, and ActA drives actin-based motility and cell-to-cell spread. In the zebrafish larval model, bacteria escaped the macrophage phagosome in an LLO-dependent manner, an event critical for virulence (PMID: 19546195); LLO additionally promotes protrusion formation and spread by exploiting efferocytosis (PMID: 24739967).

"Internalins (InlA and InlB) of Listeria monocytogenes interact with its cellular receptors E-cadherin and mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET) to facilitate invading the brain" — PMID: 30117097

"bacteria could escape the macrophage phagosome in a listeriolysin-dependent manner and accessed the cytosol; this event was critical for bacterial virulence" — PMID: 19546195

Secretion of these virulence factors depends on the Sec/SecDF translocation system (PMID: 24056100) and is governed by the master transcriptional regulator PrfA (PMID: 26055558). Passive immunization with anti-ActA and anti-LLO antibodies is protective in mice, confirming these factors as functional virulence determinants (PMID: 28004800).

Finding 5 — Ampicillin/amoxicillin (± gentamicin) is first-line; cephalosporins fail; TMP-SMX is the key alternative

L. monocytogenes is intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins, the single most important therapeutic fact about this disease. Guidelines specify amoxicillin (+gentamicin) for Listeria and continue amoxicillin alone for 14 days in confirmed Listeria meningitis; cefotaxime is reserved for E. coli precisely because Listeria is not covered by cephalosporins (PMID: 29433694). In an infant-rat meningoencephalitis model, amoxicillin plus gentamicin was the most active regimen in brain tissue (PMID: 10390217). TMP-SMX has bactericidal intracellular activity and excellent CNS penetration and has cured refractory listeriosis unresponsive to amoxicillin/gentamicin (PMID: 10959905).

"In case of meningitis due to GBS or L. monocytogenes antibiotherapy is continued for 14 days with amoxicillin alone" — PMID: 29433694

"TMP/SMX has bactericidal extracellular and intracellular activity against Listeria and excellent central nervous system penetration, and thus may be effective for the treatment of refractory listeriosis" — PMID: 10959905

Regarding adjunctive dexamethasone: while corticosteroids reduce mortality and hearing loss in bacterial meningitis overall (largely driven by pneumococcus; PMID: 17253505, PMID: 23152589), current guidance is to stop dexamethasone once Listeria is confirmed (PMID: 36472590, PMID: 42601017).

Finding 6 — Animal models recapitulate the disease and reveal genotype and species specificity

A validated C57BL/6 intracisternal L. monocytogenes meningitis mouse model demonstrated genotype-dependent severity: ST1-infected mice had a more severe inflammatory response and brain damage than ST6-infected mice (PMID: 30193592). Because the InlA:E-cadherin interaction is species-specific (human vs murine E-cadherin differ at a critical residue), transgenic mice expressing human E-cadherin were generated, in which the essential role of InlA in crossing the intestinal barrier was established (PMID: 15060982). The zebrafish larval model reproduces LLO-dependent phagosomal escape and ActA-dependent spread with real-time imaging (PMID: 19546195).

"ST1-infected mice had a more severe inflammatory response and brain damage as compared to ST6-infected mice" — PMID: 30193592

Finding 7 — Invasive listeriosis is rare but severe, concentrated in the elderly and pregnant

US CDC surveillance (2009–2011, 1,651 cases) reported an average annual incidence of 0.29 cases/100,000, a case-fatality rate of 21%, with 58% of cases in adults ≥65 years and 14% pregnancy-associated. Incidence was markedly higher among adults ≥65 (1.3/100,000; relative rate 4.4) and pregnant women (3.0/100,000; RR 10.1); ≥74% of non-pregnant patients <65 had an immunocompromising condition (PMID: 23739339). A global 80-year meta-analysis found Listeria meningitis had the highest case-fatality (27%, 95% CI 24–31%) of the common causes, exceeding pneumococcus (24%) and far exceeding meningococcus (9%) (PMID: 39093565).

"The average annual incidence was 0.29 cases per 100,000 population. Compared with the overall population, incidence was markedly higher among adults aged ≥65 years (1.3; relative rate [RR]: 4.4) and pregnant women (3.0; RR: 10.1)" — PMID: 23739339

Finding 8 — Neuroimaging shows rhombencephalitis, abscesses, and hydrocephalus with prognostic weight

In the MONALISA prospective imaging study (71 neurolisteriosis patients, mean age 64), 85% had encephalitis signs and imaging was abnormal in 87%: meningeal enhancement (35%), abscess/nodules (14%), hemorrhages (15%), hydrocephalus/contrast-enhancing ventricles (10%). Three-month survival was lower with hydrocephalus (14% vs 73%, P=0.005) and with parenchymal lesions (54% vs 92%, P=0.004) (PMID: 29796652). A Granada cohort found rhombencephalitis in 10/120 (8.3%), with MRI showing T2-FLAIR hyperintensity (100%), pons/medulla/cerebellum involvement, and cranial-nerve enhancement (70%); rhombencephalitis increased in-hospital mortality (PMID: 37303100). The classic MRI appearance of Listeria rhombencephalitis has been described since the 1990s (PMID: 8826752).

"The most frequent MRI findings in patients with confirmed rhombencephalitis were T2-FLAIR hyperintensity (100%)... while the most frequent anatomical involvement were pons, medulla oblongata, and cerebellum" — PMID: 37303100

Finding 9 — Neurolisteriosis is a shared zoonotic syndrome ("circling disease") in ruminants

Bovine listeriosis mirrors the human disease: 11/12 cases presented as neurolisteriosis with suppurative meningoencephalitis and brainstem microabscesses, and isolates were serotypes 4b and 1/2a (shared with humans) carrying the inlA virulence gene (PMID: 40192846). In goats with encephalitic listeriosis, circling (or a history of circling) was associated with survival (OR 6.24), and males were 14× more likely to be non-survivors (PMID: 37084183). Ruminant isolates display zoonotic characteristics, causing similar clinical signs in humans including abortion and encephalitis (PMID: 42667904).

"its ruminant isolates display zoonotic characteristics, causing similar clinical signs in humans, including abortion and encephalitis" — PMID: 42667904

Finding 10 — Neonatal listeriosis is bimodal: early-onset (in-utero sepsis) vs late-onset (meningitis)

In a Swedish series (1958–1974, 110 cases), of 37 liveborn neonates, 22 had "early disease" (mostly granulomatosis infantiseptica) acquired in utero from the mother, and 8 had "late disease" with meningoencephalitis from often nosocomial postnatal sources; maternal cases included 8 abortions and 3 stillbirths, with serotypes 1 and 4b predominating (PMID: 419368). Modern reviews confirm foodborne maternal acquisition (deli meats, unprocessed cheese) placing neonates at high morbidity risk (PMID: 40887071).

"Out of 37 neonates born alive, 22 had 'early disease', mostly granulomatosis infantiseptica, and 8 had 'late disease' with meningoencephalitis" — PMID: 419368

Section-by-Section Disease Characterization

1. Disease Information

Listeria meningitis is an acute bacterial infection of the meninges (and frequently the brain parenchyma, hence meningoencephalitis) caused by Listeria monocytogenes. The data derive from aggregated disease-level resources — national prospective cohorts (Dutch MeningItis cohorts, French MONALISA), CDC surveillance, and case series — rather than a single-gene EHR phenotype; this is an infectious rather than a Mendelian entity.

2. Etiology

Primary cause: infection with L. monocytogenes, a foodborne facultative intracellular Gram-positive bacillus (Findings 1, 4, 5). The route is ingestion of contaminated food (deli meats, soft/unpasteurized cheeses, unprocessed dairy, produce) → intestinal translocation → bacteremia → CNS/placental seeding.

Environmental/host risk factors: age ≥65 (RR 4.4), pregnancy (RR 10.1), neonatal status, diabetes mellitus (2.2× meningitis risk), immunosuppressive medication (chiefly corticosteroids), alcoholism, malignancy, SLE, and other conditions impairing cell-mediated immunity (Findings 1, 7; PMIDs 27845429, 35352336, 32312231, 28065808, 27613024, 20213126, 23739339).

Genetic risk factors (host): No Mendelian causal genes. Susceptibility is driven by acquired/iatrogenic immune compromise. No established GWAS locus specific to Listeria meningitis was found — a knowledge gap — though defects in cell-mediated immunity (e.g., TNF-α blockade, corticosteroids) are mechanistically implicated.

Pathogen genetic factors: virulence and outcome vary by bacterial genotype — hypervirulent clonal complexes CC1, CC4, CC6; ST6 independently predicts poor outcome (Finding 2; PMID 23592828). Key virulence genes: inlA, inlB, hly (LLO), actA, plcA/plcB, regulated by prfA.

Protective factors: intact cell-mediated (T-cell) immunity is the principal protective factor. Behaviorally, avoidance of high-risk foods reduces exposure. No genetic protective variant is established.

Not applicable for host germline genetics — Listeria meningitis is infectious, with no causal human gene, pathogenic variant, ACMG classification, or inheritance pattern. The relevant "genetics" is the bacterial genome: virulence loci inlA/inlB (internalins → E-cadherin/Met), hly (listeriolysin O), actA (actin-based motility), plcA/plcB (phospholipases), master regulator prfA. Genotype (ST/CC) modifies outcome (Finding 2). Host HGNC genes engaged as receptors: CDH1 (E-cadherin) and MET.

5. Environmental Information

Infectious agent: Listeria monocytogenes (NCBI txid1639); serotypes 4b, 1/2a, 1/2b are most clinically important. Environmental reservoir: soil, water, decaying vegetation; contaminates food-processing environments via biofilm formation (PMID: 38287241). Lifestyle/dietary factors: consumption of deli meats, pâté, soft cheeses, unpasteurized dairy, and refrigerated ready-to-eat foods (Listeria grows at refrigeration temperatures). Alcohol use is a host risk factor (PMID: 28065808).

8. Temporal Development

Onset is acute to subacute (days), often after an influenza-like prodrome. Age of onset is bimodal at the population level: neonatal (early-onset in-utero sepsis vs late-onset meningitis; Finding 10) and elderly/immunocompromised adult. Course without treatment is rapidly progressive to coma and death; with treatment, disease is self-limited over a 2–3 week antibiotic course (14 days minimum for meningitis, longer for rhombencephalitis/abscess). Complications (hydrocephalus, parenchymal lesions) mark a worse trajectory (Finding 8). The critical intervention window is prompt empirical ampicillin — delay worsens outcome.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Not applicable (infectious).
  • Epidemiology: US incidence ≈0.29/100,000/year overall; 1.3/100,000 in ≥65 (RR 4.4); 3.0/100,000 in pregnancy (RR 10.1) (Finding 7). 58% of cases in ≥65; 14% pregnancy-associated.
  • Sex: Several series show female predominance driven by pregnancy-associated cases (e.g., 69.8% female in a Karachi cohort, PMID: 42018538; 77% female in Qatar, PMID: 40978187); outside pregnancy, elderly males predominate.
  • Geographic distribution: worldwide; outbreaks tied to contaminated food products; winter seasonal peaks reported in some series (PMID: 3918344).

10. Diagnostics

  • Definitive: CSF and/or blood culture of L. monocytogenes (Gram-positive rods; may be sparse). Gram-stain sensitivity is limited.
  • CSF profile: pleocytosis (may be modest/mononuclear, especially in immunosuppressed — <1000/mm³ in ~46% of immunosuppressed; PMID: 27613024), elevated protein, low glucose.
  • Molecular: PCR / multiplex meningitis-encephalitis panels; whole-genome sequencing for genotyping/surveillance (Findings 2, 10; PMID 40887071).
  • Imaging: MRI is key — T2-FLAIR hyperintensity of pons/medulla/cerebellum, meningeal enhancement, abscesses, hydrocephalus, hemorrhage (Finding 8; PMIDs 29796652, 37303100, 8826752).
  • Differential diagnosis: other bacterial meningitides (pneumococcus, meningococcus), viral encephalitis (esp. HSV rhombencephalitis), tuberculous meningitis, brain abscess, and — in SLE patients — lupus flare (which Listeria can mimic; PMID: 20213126).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Case-fatality: ~20–30% (US surveillance 21%; global meta-analysis 27%, the highest of common causes) (Finding 7; PMIDs 23739339, 39093565).
  • Prognostic factors: older age; bacterial genotype ST6 (OR 3.77; Finding 2); imaging findings — hydrocephalus (survival 14% vs 73%) and parenchymal lesions (54% vs 92%) (Finding 8); coma/altered consciousness; intracranial hemorrhage (associated with 63% mortality; PMID: 23028898).
  • Sequelae: hearing loss, persistent neurological deficits, cognitive/depressive symptoms among survivors (PMID: 31927117).
  • Maternal-fetal: high fetal loss (24%), stillbirth, preterm delivery; mothers often recover fully.

13. Prevention

  • Primary (no vaccine exists): dietary/behavioral counseling of high-risk groups — pregnant women, elderly, immunocompromised — to avoid deli meats, pâté, soft/unpasteurized cheeses, unpasteurized milk, and to reheat ready-to-eat foods; refrigeration hygiene (PMID: 40887071, PMID: 42018538).

  • Secondary/tertiary: prompt empirical ampicillin in at-risk meningitis patients; neurocritical monitoring to prevent complications.
  • No genetic screening, immunization, or chemoprophylaxis is applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

Listeria neurolisteriosis is a well-recognized zoonotic and veterinary disease. In ruminants (cattle, sheep, goats) it causes "circling disease" — suppurative meningoencephalitis with brainstem microabscesses that pathologically parallel human rhombencephalitis (Finding 9; PMIDs 40192846, 37084183, 42667904). Shared serotypes (4b, 1/2a) and virulence genes (inlA) underscore cross-species conservation of mechanism. Species affected: Bos taurus (txid9913), Capra hircus (txid9925), Ovis aries (txid9940). Orthologous host receptor genes (CDH1, MET) are conserved, though InlA:E-cadherin binding is species-specific (Finding 6). Ovine-derived isolates infect trigeminal ganglion neurons via InlA/InlB/LLO (PMID: 42121802), a proposed route of neuroinvasion in ruminants.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The unifying theme is that Listeria meningitis is a disease of the intersection between an intracellular pathogen's molecular toolkit and a host who cannot deploy cell-mediated immunity. Three axes explain nearly all clinical observations:

  1. Host immune axis (Findings 1, 7) — defines who gets the disease: neonates, elderly, pregnant, diabetic, immunosuppressed. This drives the epidemiology (RR 4.4–10.1) and the demand for empirical ampicillin in at-risk meningitis.

  2. Pathogen invasion/spread axis (Findings 4, 6) — defines how the disease happens: InlA/InlB→E-cadherin/Met for barrier crossing (gut, placenta, BBB), LLO for phagosomal escape, ActA for intracellular spread. This intracellular, cell-to-cell lifestyle explains why humoral immunity and cephalosporins fail while cell-penetrant agents (ampicillin, TMP-SMX) succeed.

  3. Pathogen genotype axis (Findings 2, 6) — defines how badly: hypervirulent ST6/CC lineages independently worsen outcome, corroborated by genotype-dependent murine brain damage.

These converge on a brainstem-predominant meningoencephalitis (Findings 3, 8) whose imaging signature (T2-FLAIR pons/medulla/cerebellum, hydrocephalus) is both diagnostic and prognostic, and which is faithfully mirrored in ruminant "circling disease" (Finding 9), confirming a deeply conserved zoonotic syndrome.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Host genetic susceptibility is undefined. No GWAS locus or Mendelian modifier for Listeria meningitis specifically was identified; susceptibility is framed entirely through acquired immune compromise. Section 4 (host genetics) and much of Section 9 (inheritance) are not applicable to this infectious disease.
  • BBB-crossing route is partially inferred. Whether Listeria crosses the BBB primarily transcellularly, paracellularly, or via infected leukocytes ("Trojan horse") is not fully resolved in the reviewed evidence.
  • Sex-ratio data are confounded by the large pregnancy-associated fraction; true non-pregnant sex ratios are less well quantified.
  • Metabolomics/proteomics/single-cell/spatial profiling of human Listeria meningitis was not found — these omics domains remain gaps.
  • Prognostic value of ST6 derives largely from Dutch cohorts; generalizability across geographies needs confirmation.
