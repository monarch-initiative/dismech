Streptococcal meningitis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0001316 Pathograph 19 Show in embeddings browser Bacterial meningitis

Streptococcal meningitis is bacterial meningitis caused by invasive Streptococcus species. Pneumococci, neonatal group B Streptococcus, and zoonotic Streptococcus suis reach the bloodstream from respiratory, genital, or swine-associated exposure sites, cross the blood-brain or blood-CSF barrier, replicate in the cerebrospinal fluid, and trigger acute neutrophilic meningeal inflammation with fever, headache, nuchal rigidity, and potentially permanent auditory or neurologic sequelae.

Diagnosis

1
CSF analysis and culture
Lumbar puncture for CSF analysis (neutrophilic pleocytosis, low glucose, raised protein) with Gram stain and culture is the defining diagnostic act; a normal CSF cell count does not exclude bacterial meningitis.

Streptococcal Meningitis (MONDO:0001316): A Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Category: Infectious Disease (bacterial CNS infection) | Evidence base: 13 confirmed findings, 58 papers reviewed | Date: 2026-09-30

Summary

Streptococcal meningitis is an acute, life-threatening bacterial inflammation of the leptomeninges and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) caused predominantly by three Streptococcus species: Streptococcus pneumoniae (the pneumococcus, the leading cause across all ages and the most lethal), Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus, GBS, the leading neonatal cause), and the zoonotic Streptococcus suis (associated with pig/pork exposure). It is a multifactorial, acquired infectious disease with no Mendelian genetic cause; host susceptibility is instead governed by comorbidity burden, immune status, age extremes, and anatomical portals of entry. Pneumococcal meningitis carries the highest mortality of the common bacterial meningitides (~20–30% in adults), while GBS dominates neonatal disease and S. suis produces a characteristic early, permanent sensorineural hearing loss.

The pathophysiology follows a well-defined causal chain: mucosal, vertical, or zoonotic colonization → capsule- and adhesin-mediated bacteremia with immune evasion → blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration driven by pneumolysin, astroglial connexin-43 (aCx43), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2/-9) → unchecked subarachnoid bacterial replication in the immune-privileged CSF → TLR2/4–NF-κB–NLRP3 innate immune activation with a neutrophilic pleocytosis → downstream cerebral edema, vasculitis/infarction, hydrocephalus, cochlear injury, and hippocampal/cortical apoptosis producing fever, meningism, altered consciousness, seizures, and permanent neurological/auditory sequelae. Adjunctive dexamethasone, which dampens the downstream inflammatory cascade, is the only proven adjunctive therapy and significantly reduces hearing loss and severe neurological sequelae.

Management is an emergency: empiric ceftriaxone plus vancomycin (adding ampicillin where Listeria is a concern) started within one hour, together with dexamethasone. Prevention rests on pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) — which have reduced pneumococcal meningitis by 48–74% in young children but driven non-vaccine serotype replacement — and on maternal GBS screening with intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP). This report synthesizes 13 confirmed findings from 58 papers across the 15 requested domains.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — S. pneumoniae is the leading cause of streptococcal (and overall bacterial) meningitis mortality

Streptococcus pneumoniae is a Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic diplococcus and the single most important cause of both streptococcal and overall community-acquired bacterial meningitis mortality. Across all pneumococcal disease it is responsible for an estimated 1.6 million deaths annually (PMID: 42160795: "Streptococcus pneumoniae is a Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic bacterium and a leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia, meningitis, bacteremia, and otitis media, responsible for an estimated 1.6 million deaths annually."). The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 analysis identified it as the leading pathogen for child deaths and DALY loss in G20 countries, accounting for approximately 66,400 child deaths in 2023 (PMID: 42752455: "Streptococcus pneumoniae was the leading pathogen contributing to death and DALY loss among children in G20 countries, accounting for approximately 66,400 deaths").

Streptococcal meningitis is therefore not a single-organism entity but a spectrum defined by three species with distinct epidemiologies: S. pneumoniae (all ages), S. agalactiae / GBS (neonates), and the zoonotic S. suis (pig/pork-exposed adults). This species-based framing organizes the remainder of the report.

Finding 2 — Pneumolysin drives BBB crossing and astrocyte injury via connexin-43

A central mechanistic advance is the identification of the pore-forming toxin pneumolysin (Ply) as a key virulence factor for CNS invasion, and its dependence on the host astrocyte gap-junction protein connexin-43 (aCx43). In murine and in vitro models, pneumolysin requires aCx43 to permeabilize host cells; aCx43-deficient mice showed milder symptoms and severely reduced brain bacterial counts (PMID: 33370401: "we show that the astroglial connexin 43 (aCx43), a major gap junctional component expressed in astrocytes, plays a predominant role during PN meningitis"). Pneumococcal infection induces aCx43-dependent destruction of the GFAP astrocyte network, astrocyte nuclear shrinkage, loss of BBB integrity, and bacterial translocation across endothelial vessels.

Downstream, matrix metalloproteinases MMP-2 and MMP-9 degrade the basal lamina and tight junctions of the neurovascular unit. In a Wistar-rat pneumococcal meningitis model, adjuvant inhibition of MMP-2/-9 prevented BBB breakdown in the hippocampus and rescued cognitive impairment (PMID: 24419461: "Adjuvant treatments with inhibitors of MMP-2 or MMP-2/9 prevented BBB breakdown in the hippocampus"). These toxin–host interactions are the molecular fulcrum of the disease and are candidate adjunctive-therapy targets.

Ontology suggestions: GO:0050830 (defense response to Gram-positive bacterium), GO:0016264 (gap junction assembly), GO:0006909 (phagocytosis); CL:0000127 (astrocyte); UBERON:0000956 (cerebral cortex).

Finding 3 — Adjunctive dexamethasone reduces hearing loss and neurological sequelae

Adjunctive corticosteroid therapy targets the downstream neuroinflammatory step of the causal chain. A meta-analysis of 15 randomized controlled trials (2,409 children) found that adjunctive dexamethasone significantly reduced hearing loss (OR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.53–0.89, p = 0.004) and severe neurological sequelae (OR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.37–0.95, p = 0.03), without a statistically significant reduction in mortality (OR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.67–1.10, p = 0.23) (PMID: 29188363: "patients who received dexamethasone have significantly lower risks in incidence of hearing loss (OR = 0.68, 95%CI 0.53-0.89, P = 0.004) and severe neurological sequelae (OR = 0.59, 95%CI 0.37-0.95, P = 0.03), but the follow-up mortality is hardly effected").

The ESCMID guideline states that dexamethasone is the only proven adjunctive treatment and should be started together with antibiotics, with empiric antibiotics administered within one hour (PMID: 28478238: "Dexamethasone is the only proven adjunctive treatment and should be started together with the antibiotics."). The benefit is most robust in pneumococcal meningitis in high-income settings.

NCIT suggestion: NCIT:C459 (Dexamethasone).

Finding 4 — GBS neonatal meningitis is driven by the hypervirulent serotype III / ST-17 (CC17) clone carrying hvgA

Neonatal streptococcal meningitis is dominated by Streptococcus agalactiae (GBS), and within GBS by a single hypervirulent lineage. Serotype III / clonal complex 17 (CC17) carries the hvgA (hypervirulent GBS adhesin) virulence gene, which is largely restricted to this lineage (PMID: 32060499: "Presence of the hvgA virulence gene was primarily restricted to serotype III/CC17 isolates"). ST-17 strains are disproportionately associated with meningitis and late-onset disease (PMID: 30833941: "ST-17 strains were more likely to cause meningitis and late-onset disease than other strains.").

Maternal capsular antibody is protective: the risk of invasive GBS disease falls below 10% at maternal anti-capsular IgG concentrations ≥6 µg/mL (serotype Ia) and ≥3 µg/mL (serotype III) (PMID: 26478200: "the risk of invasive GBS disease was less than 10% with maternal antibody concentrations ≥ 6 μg/mL and ≥ 3 μg/mL for serotypes Ia and III, respectively"). This correlate of protection is the immunological rationale for maternal GBS vaccines under development.

Finding 5 — PCVs reduced pneumococcal meningitis but drove non-vaccine serotype replacement

Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines have substantially reduced meningitis incidence, but their serotype-specific action creates ecological pressure for replacement. The incidence of bacterial meningitis decreased following pneumococcal and meningococcal conjugate vaccine introduction (PMID: 28478238: "The incidence of bacterial meningitis has decreased following pneumococcal and meningococcal conjugate vaccine introduction"). However, in a post-PCV13 Taiwanese pneumococcal meningitis cohort (2009–2025), the majority of isolates were non-vaccine serotypes (35B, 23A, 15B) (PMID: 42202671: "Majority of the isolates are NVTs (35B, 23 A, and 15B)."). In that cohort most isolates were resistant to penicillin and ceftriaxone but susceptible to vancomycin; in-hospital mortality was 18%, and most survivors had complications (empyema, infarcts).

Serotype replacement is driven by negative frequency-dependent selection (NFDS) on the accessory genome, meaning vaccines shift intraspecific competition rather than eliminating the species — a dynamic that mandates ongoing genomic surveillance.

Finding 6 — Clinical phenotype and prognosis of adult community-acquired pneumococcal meningitis

The landmark Dutch nationwide prospective cohort (n = 696 episodes) defines the adult clinical picture. S. pneumoniae caused 51% and N. meningitidis 37% of cases. The classic triad of fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status was present in only 44% of episodes, but 95% had at least two of four cardinal features (headache, fever, neck stiffness, altered mental status) (PMID: 15509818: "The classic triad of fever, neck stiffness, and a change in mental status was present in only 44 percent of episodes; however, 95 percent had at least two of the four symptoms of headache, fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status."). On admission, 14% were comatose and 33% had focal neurologic deficits.

Overall mortality was 21%, but pneumococcal mortality was markedly higher than meningococcal (30% vs 7%, p < 0.001) (PMID: 15509818: "The mortality rate was higher among patients with pneumococcal meningitis than among those with meningococcal meningitis (30 percent vs. 7 percent, P<0.001)."); unfavorable outcome occurred in 34%. A particularly severe presentation is Austrian syndrome — the triad of pneumococcal pneumonia, endocarditis, and meningitis (PMID: 28266699: "the combination of endocarditis, pneumonia, and meningitis... The clinical entity described is also called Austrian syndrome.").

Finding 7 — Host comorbidity and immune status are the dominant risk factors

Because streptococcal meningitis is an acquired infection, susceptibility is driven by host factors rather than genes. A population-based cohort of 2,059,645 adults ≥50 years in Catalonia showed pneumococcal disease incidence rising steeply with comorbidity burden — from 42.1 to 761.2 per 100,000 person-years across 0 to ≥5 comorbidities (an ~18-fold gradient). The highest-risk conditions were prior IPD/pneumonia, followed by haematological neoplasia (475.0), HIV infection (423.7; multivariable HR ~5.1), renal disease (384.9), chronic respiratory disease (314.7), liver disease (232.5), heart disease (221.4), alcoholism (204.8), solid cancer (186.2), and diabetes (159.6) (PMID: 37291502: "Maximum IRs emerged among persons with history of previous IPD or all-cause pneumonia, followed by haematological neoplasia (475.0), HIV-infection (423.7), renal disease (384.9), chronic respiratory disease (314.7), liver disease (232.5), heart disease (221.4), alcoholism (204.8), solid cancer (186.2) and diabetes (159.6)").

Classic host risk factors include functional or anatomical asplenia, alcoholism, and immunocompromise including HIV (PMID: 16163421: "functional or anatomical asplenia, alcoholism), in immunocompromised hosts, including HIV infection"). Anatomical portals such as CSF leak (chronic BBB breach), and age >65 or <5 years, further amplify risk. These groups define the vaccine-eligible high-risk population.

Finding 8 — Diagnosis rests on CSF analysis with molecular backup

Diagnosis is anchored on lumbar puncture and CSF analysis. Probable bacterial meningitis is defined by CSF leukocytosis (>100 cells/mm³, neutrophil-predominant), turbidity, decreased glucose (<40 mg/dL), and raised protein (>100 mg/dL) (PMID: 39230218: "A probable case of bacterial meningitis was determined by leukocytosis (>100 cells/mm 3), turbidity, decreased glucose (<40 mg/dl), and raised protein levels (>100 mg/dl)"). The CSF:serum glucose ratio is typically low, and Gram stain of pneumococcus reveals Gram-positive diplococci. The CSF/serum albumin ratio (Qalb) indexes BBB integrity (PMID: 29286010: "Cerebrospinal fluid/serum albumin ratio is one of the most informative parameters for blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity in cases of central nervous system (CNS) infectious diseases").

A key caveat is that the absence of pleocytosis does not reliably exclude bacterial meningitis, especially early (<24 h) or in neonates (PMID: 33591075: "the absence of pleocytosis does not exclude bacterial meningitis reliably and should be interpreted in the context of the duration of illness"). Blood cultures are positive in ~28% of neonates and ~68% of older children. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) resolves culture-negative and atypical cases, and multiplex PCR panels (e.g., FilmArray) speed pathogen identification.

Finding 9 — Global epidemiology: 259,000 deaths and 2.54M cases in 2023

GBD 2023 estimated 259,000 (95% UI 202,000–335,000) global meningitis deaths and 2.54 million (2.20–2.93M) incident cases in 2023, with children under 5 accounting for more than a third of deaths (86,600) (PMID: 41911930: "In 2023, there were 259 000 (95% uncertainty interval 202 000-335 000) global deaths and 2·54 million (2·20-2·93) incident cases of meningitis. Children younger than 5 years accounted for more than a third of deaths (86 600 [53 300-149 000])."). S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, and non-polio enteroviruses were the leading causes; the African meningitis belt bears a disproportionate burden with sporadic pneumococcal outbreaks.

The PSERENADE project quantified vaccine impact: six years after PCV10/PCV13 introduction, pneumococcal meningitis incidence declined 48–74% in children <5 years, 35–62% in 5–17 years, and 0–36% in adults ≥18 years, although non-vaccine-type replacement persisted (PMID: 39864526: "Six years after PCV10/PCV13 introduction, pneumococcal meningitis declined 48-74% across products and PCV7 impact strata for children <5 y, 35-62% for 5-17 y and 0-36% for ≥ 18 y"). (Note: this snippet is flagged as a normalization/mismatch and should be reverified against the source prior to database ingestion.)

Finding 10 — Prevention combines conjugate vaccines, maternal GBS screening + IAP, and chemoprophylaxis

Prevention is species-specific. For GBS, any prevention strategy — universal antenatal culture screening at 36–37 weeks or intrapartum PCR, plus intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) with penicillin — reduces early-onset GBS (EOGBS), all-cause early-onset sepsis, and EOGBS mortality versus no strategy, with universal screening more effective than risk-based (PMID: 42060466: "Any prevention strategy reduces the incidence of EOGBS, all-cause EOS and EOGBS-related mortality compared to no strategy."). Under rapid intrapartum PCR screening, EOGBS sepsis incidence was 0.46 per 1,000 live births (PMID: 42036407: "The incidence of early onset GBS sepsis was 0,46 per 1,000 live births").

For pneumococcus, childhood PCV (PCV7→PCV13/15/20/21) immunization and PPSV23/PCV for high-risk adults constitute primary prevention. In the African meningitis belt, modeling suggests reactive PCV vaccination during outbreaks could avert 10–20% of outbreak cases (PMID: 38797628: "implementing reactive vaccination in previous pneumococcal meningitis outbreaks could have averted up to 10-20 % of outbreak cases").

Finding 11 — Streptococcus suis is a zoonotic cause with characteristic early, permanent sensorineural hearing loss

Streptococcus suis (serotype 2) is an emerging zoonosis in Asia in which meningitis is the most common human manifestation, characteristically associated with hearing loss and cochleovestibular signs (PMID: 21779197: "The most common disease manifestation is meningitis, which is often associated with hearing loss and cochleovestibular signs. S. suis infection in humans mainly occurs among risk groups that have frequent exposure to pigs or raw pork."). Infection concentrates in occupational pork/carcass handlers and heavy alcohol users.

In a 40-patient northern Thailand series, 75% were men with heavy drinking, 62.5% had consumed raw pork or pig's blood; presentations were sepsis (30), meningitis (19), and endocarditis (10), with overall mortality 20%. Among meningitis survivors, 73% had persistent sensorineural hearing loss and 50% vestibular impairment (PMID: 19275779: "73 per cent of the surviving meningitis cases had persistent sensorineural hearing loss and 50 per cent demonstrated vestibular impairment"). The deafness develops early and remains permanent despite penicillin or third-generation cephalosporin cure (PMID: 18672627: "The clinical feature of the meningitis is characteristic with severe sensorineural hearing loss, developing early during the course of meningitis").

Finding 12 — Experimental animal models recapitulate CSF inflammation, hippocampal apoptosis, and hearing loss

Validated animal models underpin the mechanistic understanding. The infant-rat intracisternal S. pneumoniae model reproduces CSF pleocytosis, TNF-α, MMP-9, and hippocampal apoptosis; the TLR2 agonist Pam3CysSK4 induced pleocytosis, TNF-α, and MMP-9 in CSF (PMID: 19012971: "Pam(3)CysSK(4) induced pleocytosis, TNF-alpha and MMP-9 in CSF"). The adult Wistar-rat cisterna magna model reproduces BBB breakdown and cognitive/memory impairment reversible by MMP-2/9 inhibition (PMID: 24419461: "the animals that received adjuvant treatment with the inhibitor of MMP-2/9 demonstrated that animals habituated to the open-field task faster and enhanced memory").

The murine intravenous PN challenge model demonstrates aCx43-dependent astrocyte damage and brain invasion (Finding 2). Additional mechanistic nodes include BDNF, whose selective loss exacerbates brain injury by enhancing neuroinflammation, and historical rabbit models that defined subarachnoid bacterial multiplication and PMN-mediated CNS injury. These models recapitulate the human features of neutrophilic pleocytosis, neuronal apoptosis, and hearing loss with high fidelity, though they compress the disease timescale and cannot fully model human comorbidity.

Finding 13 — Integrated causal model: colonization → bacteremia → BBB crossing → neuroinflammation → CNS injury

Synthesizing all findings, streptococcal meningitis follows a six-stage causal cascade (detailed in the Mechanistic Model below). The therapeutic validity of this model is confirmed at its downstream node: adjunctive dexamethasone, which targets the neuroinflammatory step, reduces hearing loss (OR 0.68) and severe sequelae (OR 0.59) (PMID: 29188363), while the BBB-crossing step is anchored by the aCx43/pneumolysin axis (PMID: 33370401).

1. Disease Information

Overview: Streptococcal meningitis is an acute purulent (pyogenic) inflammation of the meninges and subarachnoid CSF caused by Streptococcus species. It is a subset of acute bacterial meningitis, a neurological emergency requiring treatment within one hour.

2. Etiology

Causal factors: The disease is infectious and multifactorial, with no Mendelian genetic cause. Primary etiologic agents are S. pneumoniae (all ages), S. agalactiae/GBS (neonates), and S. suis (zoonotic).

Genetic risk factors: No high-penetrance causal variants. Susceptibility relates to innate immunity (complement deficiencies, asplenia-associated immune defects, TLR pathway variants) and is a secondary modifier — not causal in the Mendelian sense. Anatomical predispositions (CSF leak, inner-ear malformation, cochlear implants) increase recurrent meningitis risk.

Environmental/host risk factors (Finding 7): Age (<5 or >65 years), comorbidity burden (up to ~18-fold gradient), HIV (HR ~5.1), haematological malignancy, asplenia, alcoholism, diabetes, chronic renal/respiratory/liver/heart disease, and CSF leak. For S. suis, occupational pig/pork exposure and heavy alcohol use.

Protective factors: Maternal anti-capsular IgG (≥6 µg/mL Ia, ≥3 µg/mL III) protects neonates from GBS (Finding 4); PCV/PPSV vaccination and prior natural immunity protect against pneumococcus.

Gene–environment interactions: Host immunodeficiency (genetic or acquired, e.g., HIV, asplenia) interacts with pathogen exposure and capsular serotype to determine invasive disease risk.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary: leptomeninges (UBERON:0002361 meninges; UBERON:0007616 leptomeninges), subarachnoid space (UBERON:0002357), CSF (UBERON:0001359).
  • Secondary: cerebral cortex (UBERON:0000956), hippocampus (UBERON:0002421), cochlea/inner ear (UBERON:0001844) → hearing loss, cerebral vasculature → infarction, ventricles → hydrocephalus.
  • Body systems: central nervous system (primary); cardiovascular (Austrian syndrome endocarditis); respiratory (pneumococcal pneumonia portal).
  • Cell populations: astrocytes (CL:0000127), neutrophils (CL:0000775), meningeal/endothelial cells, hippocampal neurons.
  • Lateralization: typically bilateral/diffuse meningeal involvement; focal infarcts may be unilateral.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Acute (hours to 1–2 days). Neonatal GBS: early-onset (<7 days) or late-onset (7–89 days, associated with ST-17). Pneumococcal: any age, acute.
  • Progression: Rapid; untreated disease progresses to coma and death within days. Fulminant Austrian syndrome.
  • Course: Monophasic/self-limited with treatment, but recurrent meningitis occurs with anatomical defects (CSF leak, inner-ear malformation).
  • Critical period: The first hour ("door-to-antibiotic time") is decisive; early dexamethasone (with the first antibiotic dose) defines the therapeutic window for reducing sequelae.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology (Finding 9): 2.54M global meningitis cases and 259,000 deaths in 2023; children <5 account for >1/3 of deaths. African meningitis belt disproportionately affected.
  • Inheritance: Not heritable (acquired infection). Multifactorial host susceptibility.
  • Sex ratio: Male predominance in S. suis (75% men) and many pneumococcal cohorts.
  • Age distribution: Bimodal — neonates (GBS) and older adults/immunocompromised (pneumococcus).
  • Geographic: S. suis endemic in Asia; pneumococcal outbreaks in the African meningitis belt; serotype distribution varies by region and vaccine era.

10. Diagnostics (Finding 8)

  • CSF analysis (gold standard): neutrophilic pleocytosis (>100 cells/mm³), low glucose (<40 mg/dL, low CSF:serum ratio), high protein (>100 mg/dL), turbidity.
  • Microbiology: Gram stain (Gram-positive diplococci for pneumococcus), CSF and blood culture.
  • Molecular: multiplex PCR panels (FilmArray), metagenomic NGS for culture-negative/atypical cases.
  • Biomarkers: CSF/serum albumin ratio (Qalb) for BBB integrity; serum procalcitonin/CRP.
  • Imaging: CT/MRI to assess complications (infarction, hydrocephalus, edema, abscess) and before LP where raised ICP is suspected.
  • Differential diagnosis: viral (enteroviral) meningitis, tuberculous meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis, very low glucose), cryptococcal meningitis, other bacterial meningitides (meningococcal, Listeria, H. influenzae).
  • Caveat: normal cell count does not exclude early bacterial meningitis.

11. Outcome/Prognosis (Findings 6, 11)

  • Mortality: Adult pneumococcal ~30% (vs meningococcal 7%); overall bacterial meningitis ~21%; S. suis ~20%; post-PCV13 pneumococcal cohort in-hospital 18%.
  • Morbidity: Unfavorable outcome in ~34% of adult episodes; most survivors of severe cases have complications.
  • Sequelae: permanent sensorineural hearing loss (majority of S. suis survivors), cognitive impairment, epilepsy, focal deficits, hydrocephalus.
  • Prognostic factors: age, comorbidity, impaired consciousness on admission, serotype/virulence, antibiotic resistance, delay to treatment.

12. Treatment

  • Empiric antibiotics (within 1 hour): third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone/cefotaxime) + vancomycin (for resistant pneumococcus); add ampicillin where Listeria is possible (neonates, elderly, immunocompromised). Penicillin G for susceptible GBS/S. suis.
  • Adjunctive dexamethasone (Finding 3): with or before the first antibiotic dose; reduces hearing loss (OR 0.68) and severe sequelae (OR 0.59). NCIT:C459.
  • Resistance consideration: post-PCV13 non-vaccine serotypes frequently penicillin/ceftriaxone-resistant but vancomycin-susceptible (Finding 5).
  • Supportive care: ICU management of raised ICP, seizures, hydrocephalus (CSF diversion), fluid/electrolyte management.
  • Rehabilitation: audiology/cochlear implantation for deafness; cognitive and physical rehabilitation.

NCIT term suggestions: NCIT:C459 (Dexamethasone), NCIT:C1746 (Ceftriaxone), NCIT:C1194 (Vancomycin), NCIT:C263 (Ampicillin), NCIT:C61780 (Penicillin G).

13. Prevention (Finding 10)

  • Primary — pneumococcal: PCV7→PCV13/15/20/21 childhood immunization; PPSV23/higher-valency PCV for high-risk adults. Reactive PCV in outbreaks could avert 10–20% of belt cases.
  • Primary — GBS: universal antenatal culture screening (36–37 wk) or intrapartum PCR + IAP (penicillin); reduces EOGBS, sepsis, and mortality; EOGBS incidence ~0.46/1,000 live births under PCR screening. Maternal GBS vaccines under development (capsular antibody correlate, Finding 4).
  • Primary — S. suis: occupational precautions, avoiding raw pork consumption.
  • Secondary/tertiary: rapid diagnosis and treatment; chemoprophylaxis for close contacts (context-dependent); management of CSF leaks and anatomical defects.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Zoonosis: S. suis is the paradigmatic zoonotic streptococcal meningitis, transmitted from pigs (Sus scrofa, NCBI taxid 9823) via occupational contact or raw pork (Finding 11).
  • GBS naturally colonizes and causes disease in cattle (bovine mastitis) and fish.
  • Cross-species susceptibility: S. suis affects both swine and humans; comparative pathology shows conserved meningeal invasion and hearing loss mechanisms.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The disease is best understood as a two-phase cascade: an upstream, pathogen-driven invasion phase (colonization → bacteremia → BBB crossing → CSF replication) and a downstream, host-driven injury phase (innate immune activation → neutrophilic inflammation → CNS tissue damage). This division has direct therapeutic logic:

Cascade step Dominant driver Therapeutic lever Evidence
1. Colonization Pathogen + host portal Vaccines, IAP, hygiene Findings 4, 5, 10
2. Bacteremia Capsule/adhesin evasion; host immunodeficiency Vaccines, host optimization Finding 7
3. BBB crossing Pneumolysin + aCx43 + MMP-2/9 (Experimental) MMP inhibitors, anti-Ply Finding 2
4. CSF replication Immune-privileged niche Bactericidal antibiotics Finding 6
5. Neuroinflammation TLR–NF-κB–NLRP3 cytokines Dexamethasone Finding 3
6. CNS injury Edema, vasculitis, apoptosis Supportive care, dexamethasone Findings 3, 6

The aCx43/pneumolysin axis (step 3) and the cytokine/MMP cascade (steps 5–6) are the two nodes with the strongest mechanistic and therapeutic evidence. Dexamethasone's demonstrated benefit at steps 5–6 validates the model, while MMP-inhibitor rescue in rats nominates step 3 as the next translational target.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No primary dataset was analyzed — this report synthesizes literature; all quantitative claims derive from cited studies rather than de novo analysis.
  2. Geographic bias: Much clinical prognostic data come from high-income cohorts (Netherlands, Taiwan); African meningitis-belt and low-resource outcomes are less granular.
  3. Host genetics under-characterized: The contribution of human innate-immunity variants (complement, TLR, Toll pathway) to susceptibility is not quantified here.
  4. Mechanistic translation gap: The aCx43/pneumolysin and MMP axes are validated in animals but lack human adjunctive-therapy trials.
  5. Serotype dynamics are moving targets: Post-PCV serotype replacement and antibiotic resistance evolve continuously, limiting the shelf-life of epidemiological specifics.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions
