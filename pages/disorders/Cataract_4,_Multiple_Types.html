Cataract 4, Multiple Types

Mendelian MONDO:0007281 Pathograph 27 Show in embeddings browser Early-onset non-syndromic cataract

Autosomal dominant, non-syndromic early-onset cataract caused by heterozygous variants in CRYGD, which encodes gamma-D crystallin, one of the most abundant monomeric structural proteins of the lens fiber cell cytoplasm. Most families have a congenital, bilateral cataract that is present at birth, but the first reported allele (R14C) causes a progressive cataract that appears in the first years of life and evolves slowly through childhood. The disease is named for its morphological diversity: coralliform, aculeiform, cerulean, crystalline, nuclear, lamellar and fasciculiform cataracts have all been reported, and the recurrent P23T allele alone has produced most of these morphologies, sometimes within one family. Biophysical work on recombinant mutant proteins established an unusual mechanism for several alleles: the mutant protein keeps a native fold and normal stability but has a sharply reduced solubility, so it aggregates (R14C through disulfide-linked oligomers, P23T through a condensed phase with inverted temperature dependence, E107A as light-scattering particles) or crystallizes in the lens (R36S and R58H). Other alleles, such as W43R and C-terminal truncations, instead destabilize the fold and expose hydrophobic surfaces that drive aggregation. Both routes converge on light scattering and lens opacity, causing visual impairment that, when the cataract is dense, is complicated by nystagmus and amblyopia. Management is surgical lens extraction; there is no disease-specific therapy.

Pathophysiology

6
CRYGD Pathogenic Variant
Heterozygous missense, nonsense and frameshift variants in CRYGD. Most pathogenic missense alleles change surface residues (R14C, P23T, R36S, R58H, E107A); others change structurally critical residues (W43R) or truncate the C-terminal Greek key motifs (W156X, Y134X, and frameshifts). The biophysical studies below attribute the cataract to properties gained by the mutant protein. One allele points the other way: in transfected cells R36P lowers the total and soluble amount of gamma-D crystallin without increasing the insoluble fraction, so a reduced soluble pool may contribute for some alleles; this has not been tested in lens.

Reduced Gamma-D Crystallin Solubility With Preserved Native Fold
For R14C, P23T, R36S, R58H and E107A the recombinant mutant protein has essentially the secondary structure, tertiary structure and conformational stability of wild type. What changes is its intermolecular behavior: an extra exposed cysteine (R14C), a solubility that falls as temperature rises (P23T), or loss of the arginine side chain whose absence allows crystal packing (R36S) or removes an intermolecular ion pair (R58H). These alleles showed that unfolding is not required for a crystallin to cause cataract.

Gamma-D Crystallin Destabilization and Hydrophobic Exposure
A second group of alleles does perturb the protein. W43R (W42R in processed-protein numbering) lowers stability, and in vitro the W42R mutant aggregates through a non-native internal disulfide that locks a misfolded, aggregation-prone intermediate. C-terminal truncations (W156X, Y151X, T160RfsX8) disrupt Greek key motif 4, destabilize the protein and expose hydrophobic side chains to solvent. In cultured cells heat, UV and oxidative stress increase aggregation of truncated mutants, and co-expressed alpha-crystallins reduce it.

Platform: Surgery
Lens extraction is the standard management of visually significant CRYGD cataract, performed from early infancy for dense congenital opacities and in childhood or adolescence for the progressive R14C cataract. In paediatric cataract generally it is lens aspiration with or without intraocular lens implantation, followed by amblyopia therapy, and for dense bilateral congenital cataract the timing of surgery in the first weeks of life governs visual outcome. The timing and technique evidence comes from cohorts that were not genotyped. Complications after paediatric cataract surgery include secondary membranes over the visual axis requiring re-operation and glaucoma, both more frequent after surgery in the first four weeks of life. No CRYGD-specific therapy exists.

CRYGD-Related Congenital Cataract 4 (CTRCT4): Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: CRYGD-Related Congenital Cataract 4 MONDO ID: MONDO:0007281 · OMIM: 115700 · Category: Mendelian (autosomal dominant) Gene: CRYGD (γD-crystallin), chromosome 2q33–q35

Summary

CRYGD-related congenital cataract 4 (CTRCT4) is an autosomal dominant, bilateral, congenital (present-at-birth) lens opacity caused by heterozygous mutations in CRYGD, the gene encoding the lens structural protein γD-crystallin. γD-crystallin is one of the most abundant, long-lived, and essentially non-renewing proteins of the eye lens fiber cells; its extremely high solubility and stability are prerequisites for lifelong lens transparency and high refractive index. Pathogenic CRYGD variants degrade one or more of these properties—solubility, thermodynamic stability, or resistance to oxidation—converting a transparent protein solution into light-scattering deposits and producing a visible cataract. The clinical course is typically non-progressive and non-syndromic, and the disease is not life-threatening; morbidity derives entirely from visual deprivation and the risk of irreversible amblyopia if the opacity is not removed during a narrow developmental critical window.

A central scientific insight from this investigation is that a single gene, and even a single recurrent allele, produces cataract through multiple convergent biophysical mechanisms rather than one. At least three distinct routes to light scattering have been documented: (1) ordered crystallization of a less-soluble mutant protein (R58H "aculeiform" cataract; R36S), (2) native-like amorphous aggregation / liquid–liquid phase separation in which the protein keeps its fold but condenses out of solution with inverted ("retrograde") solubility (P23T/P24T—the dominant hotspot allele), and (3) oxidation/disulfide-driven or truncation-driven misfolding (W42R; the C-terminal Greek-key truncations Y134X, Y151X, T160RfsX8). Across these routes the α-crystallin small heat-shock chaperone acts as a protective buffer, and its capacity to hold mutant client protein in a soluble state is a key modifier of whether and when opacity appears. Strikingly, a single genotype—P23T/P24T—can present as cerulean, coralliform, or lamellar cataract, demonstrating marked intrafamilial and interfamilial phenotypic heterogeneity that is not explained by the primary mutation alone.

The clinical management implications are clear and well supported. There is no approved disease-specific pharmacotherapy; the established treatment is early surgical lens extraction (lens aspiration ± intraocular lens implantation) followed by aggressive refractive correction and amblyopia therapy. Outcome is dominated by timing: in dense bilateral congenital cataract a ~14-week critical period governs visual-acuity prognosis. Experimental aggregation-dissolving small molecules (lanosterol, 25-hydroxycholesterol/VP1-001) and antioxidant/chaperone strategies show that crystallin aggregation is, in principle, reversible, but these remain pre-clinical. CRYGD is consistently among the top congenital-cataract genes in large sequencing cohorts, and congenital cataract is one of the most common and highest-yield inherited eye disorders for molecular diagnosis, making NGS panel/exome testing the diagnostic standard.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — CRYGD mutations cause autosomal dominant congenital cataract with diverse lens-opacity morphologies

Linkage studies repeatedly map autosomal dominant congenital cataract (ADCC) to the γ-crystallin gene cluster at chromosome 2q33–q35, with CRYGD as the causal gene. Nandrot et al. obtained a LOD score of 7.19 (θ = 0) at marker D2S2208 near the CRYG cluster for a cerulean cataract pedigree, identifying a heterozygous CRYGD exon-2 C>A change producing the P23T substitution: "This mutation resulted in a proline to threonine substitution at amino acid 23 of the protein in the first of the four Greek key motifs that characterise this protein" (PMID: 12676897).

The same P23T allele independently causes coralliform cataract (LOD 3.5 at D2S325): "This mutation resulted in a substitution of threonine for proline at amino acid 23 (P23T) of the protein" (PMID: 14767902). One genotype therefore produces several distinct opacity morphologies (cerulean, coralliform, lamellar)—a hallmark of variable expressivity in this disease. The allelic spectrum also includes nonsense alleles: the Y134X truncation causes cataract with microcornea—"one mutation (Y134X) was detected in crystallin gamma-D (CRYGD)" (PMID: 17724170).

Ontology: HGNC CRYGD; phenotype HP:0000519 (congenital cataract), HP:0000568 (microcornea); MONDO:0007281.

Finding 2 — Two distinct biophysical mechanisms: crystallization vs. native-like amorphous aggregation

Not all CRYGD cataracts form by the same physical process. R58H γD-crystallin is roughly an order of magnitude less soluble than wild-type and literally crystallizes within the lens, producing needle-like ("aculeiform") opacities. High-resolution X-ray structures (WT at 1.25 Å, R58H at 1.15 Å) show the mutant loses a direct intermolecular ion pair at residue 58: the cataract "is caused by crystallization of the mutant protein, which is an order of magnitude less soluble than the wild-type" (PMID: 12729747). R36S is an analogous surface-charge crystallization mutation.

By contrast, P23T forms amorphous-looking aggregates at physiological pH that, by solid-state NMR, retain a native-like fold: "the aggregated protein retains a native-like conformation, with no evidence for large-scale misfolding" (PMID: 28474685). The underlying defect is a lowered and inverted (retrograde) solubility traced to the substitution site itself: "This contact, at the P23T substitution site, relates to a genetic cataract and reveals at a molecular level the origin of the lowered and retrograde solubility of the protein" (PMID: 31422822). The P23T condensate behavior measured in purified protein matches that of protein expressed in cell-free and mammalian-cell systems (PMID: 28401204), connecting in-vitro phase diagrams to the cellular disease state.

Finding 3 — A third mechanism: oxidation/disulfide-driven and truncation-induced misfolding, buffered by α-crystallin

For the congenital W42R mutant, formation of a non-native internal disulfide bond (Cys32–Cys41) is the decisive event: "formation of an internal disulfide was necessary and sufficient for aggregation under physiological conditions" (PMID: 27417136). This locks a partially unfolded, aggregation-prone intermediate that propagates via domain-swap-like intermolecular contacts.

C-terminal truncating mutations in Greek-key motif 4 (Y151X, T160RfsX8; also Y134X) destabilize γD-crystallin and cause insoluble aggregates: "Both truncation mutations induced protein misfolding and resulted in the formation of insoluble aggregates when overexpressed in HLE B3 and HEK 293T cells" (PMID: 39084439). Critically, the same study shows a protective modifier: "co-expression of α-crystallins facilitated the proper folding of truncated mutants and mitigated protein aggregation" (PMID: 39084439). A further quantitative mechanism is reduced soluble protein, as with R36P: "The total protein, especially the soluble fraction, was significantly reduced in the R36P mutant" (PMID: 39563676).

Finding 4 — CRYGD is among the most common congenital-cataract genes, with a recurrent coralliform hotspot

In a large Chinese NGS cohort, PAX6, GJA8, and CRYGD together accounted for 33.06% of molecularly diagnosed cases: these genes were "commonly detected in PAX6, GJA8 and CRYGD, and the three genes covered 33.06% of cases" (PMID: 37337769). For coralliform cataract specifically, a recurrent CRYGD c.70C>A (p.P24T)—equivalent to classic P23T under alternative numbering—dominated: "A recurrent c.70 C > A (p. P24T) mutation in CRYGD was identified in 10 families (83.3%) with congenital cataract" (PMID: 37480084). The phenotype was uniform and congenital: "all affected individuals presented with bilateral non-progressive coralliform cataracts since birth, with the best-corrected Snellen visual acuities ranging from 20/200 to 20/25" (PMID: 37480084). CRYGD variants recur across Chinese and North Indian ADCC pedigrees and can co-occur with nystagmus (PMID: 35222542).

Finding 5 — Congenital cataract is a leading treatable cause of childhood visual impairment; surgery, not pharmacology, is the treatment

Crystallin genes are the leading cause of bilateral pediatric cataract: causative variants were found in 18/22 (81.8%) Israeli/Palestinian families, crystallin genes predominating (PMID: 38874774). Definitive treatment is surgical: "Surgical treatment, mainly lens aspiration with or without intraocular lens implantation, is effective, but visual prognosis depends on early intervention and adherence to amblyopia therapy" (PMID: 42216740). Post-surgical complications include secondary glaucoma, visual-axis opacification, and postoperative high myopia; risk factors for high myopia include initial postoperative refraction <+0.75 D, a higher IOL/axial-length ratio, and shorter preoperative axial length (PMID: 42288323).

Finding 6 — Prognosis is governed by a narrow surgical critical period (~14 weeks)

In dense bilateral congenital cataract, a bilinear model with a critical age of 14 weeks fit visual-acuity outcomes better than a linear model (χ² = 14.7; p < 0.0006): "A bilinear model with a critical age of 14 weeks fit the data better than a linear model" (PMID: 19084444). Within that window, "During weeks 0-14, mean visual acuity decreased by 1 line with each 3 weeks' delay in surgery" (PMID: 19084444); after 14 weeks acuity plateaued near 20/80 regardless of further delay. Surgery after 4 weeks of age associated with more strabismus/nystagmus, while surgery within the first 4 weeks associated with more secondary membrane formation and glaucoma.

Finding 7 — High molecular diagnostic yield supports NGS-based diagnosis

Congenital cataract (CC) has the highest diagnostic yield among congenital eye malformations: "Diagnostic rates were highest in CC (75%), bilateral cases (46%), complex ocular phenotypes (78%), patients with extraocular manifestations (55%) and positive family history (70%)" (PMID: 41155148). Multigene panels (186 eye genes) outperformed limited panels and added yield beyond exome. Prenatal detection pathways exist—fetal cataracts seen on morphology ultrasound can prompt exome sequencing after infectious workup and microarray (PMID: 37578328). In a 40-year Danish national registry, congenital cataract was the 2nd most frequent inherited eye diagnosis (1,220 cases), and a confirmed genetic etiology was established in 60.9% of families (PMID: 42272130).

Finding 8 — Cross-species conservation and model systems

Crystallin cataracts are evolutionarily conserved. Canine hereditary cataract has been studied with γ-crystallin candidate genes: "The crystallin genes may be involved in the pathogenesis of canine CAT as shown in humans and mice" (PMID: 17327821), and naturally occurring congenital hereditary cataract with microphthalmia in miniature schnauzers is autosomal recessive (PMID: 1869417). A transgenic zebrafish expressing a destabilized human γD-crystallin mutant reproduces lens aggregation/opacity (PMID: 30475834); crystallin knock-in mice (αA-R49C, αB-R120G) develop nuclear/congenital cataract (PMID: 18056999). The lens is an ideal model tissue because "Crystallins are long-lived proteins that do not turnover throughout life" (PMID: 29338044).

Finding 9 — Experimental non-surgical therapeutics

Crystallin aggregation is, in principle, reversible: "lanosterol, and more recently 5-cholesten-3b,25-diol (VP1-001), have been reported to combat the aggregation of crystallins in vivo and ex vivo" (PMID: 36484206). Lanosterol reversed the effects of a pathogenic crystallin variant in a cell model: "The addition of lanosterol reversed the negative effects of the G91del mutation under external stress" (PMID: 34419537), and reviews conclude "protein aggregation is not an endpoint and could be reversed with specific small-molecule drugs" (PMID: 33313297). These remain experimental; surgery is the only established treatment.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. A heterozygous germline CRYGD mutation (missense, nonsense, or frameshift) is present from conception → leads to expression of a structurally altered γD-crystallin in lens fiber cells.
  2. The mutation degrades one or more protective biophysical properties of γD-crystallin—net surface charge/solubility, thermodynamic fold stability, or resistance to oxidation → results in a protein that is metastable under the extreme crowding of the lens (~400 mg/mL).
  3. The destabilized protein follows one of three branching routes:
  4. Branch A (crystallization): R58H/R36S lose stabilizing intermolecular contacts → lead to ordered crystal nucleation → aculeiform/needle opacities (PMID: 12729747).
  5. Branch B (native-like condensation): P23T/P24T acquire lowered, retrograde solubility → lead to liquid–liquid phase separation / amorphous condensation with the fold retained → cerulean/coralliform/lamellar opacities (PMID: 28474685, PMID: 31422822).
  6. Branch C (misfolding): W42R forms a non-native Cys32–Cys41 disulfide, and C-terminal truncations destabilize Greek-key motif 4 → lead to trapped unfolding intermediates and insoluble aggregates (PMID: 27417136, PMID: 39084439).
  7. The α-crystallin chaperone buffer attempts to hold mutant client in a soluble state → modulates the threshold and timing of visible opacity; when its capacity is exceeded, aggregation proceeds (PMID: 39084439). (Branch-dependent; most directly demonstrated for truncation mutants.)
  8. Aggregated/crystallized protein creates refractive-index inhomogeneities on the scale of visible light → results in light scattering and lens opacity (cataract).
  9. Because crystallins do not turnover, the deposit is permanent and present at birth → results in congenital, typically non-progressive, bilateral cataract (PMID: 29338044).
  10. The opacity blocks formation of a sharp retinal image during the critical period of visual-cortex development → leads to deprivation amblyopia unless surgically corrected within ~14 weeks (PMID: 19084444).

Section-by-Section Reference Content

1. Disease Information

CTRCT4 is a non-syndromic, autosomal dominant congenital cataract caused by CRYGD mutations. Identifiers: OMIM 115700 (CTRCT4); MONDO:0007281; gene CRYGD (HGNC:2411; NCBI Gene 1421; chromosome 2q33–q35). Synonyms: cataract 4, multiple types; crystallin gamma D-related cataract; punctate/coralliform/cerulean/aculeiform congenital cataract (allele-dependent). Information is derived from aggregated disease-level resources and family-based studies (linkage, Sanger, NGS cohorts), not EHR.

2. Etiology

Primary cause: heterozygous germline CRYGD coding mutations (genetic; not environmental or infectious). Genetic risk: causal variants P23T/P24T (recurrent hotspot), R58H, R36S, R36P, W42R, Y134X, Y151X, T160RfsX8. Modifier gene: α-crystallin (CRYAA/CRYAB) chaperone capacity (PMID: 39084439). Environmental risk: not established as causal; oxidative/UV/heat stress aggravate aggregation of already-mutant protein in cell models (PMID: 39084439). Protective / gene–environment: antioxidant capacity and chaperone reserve are inferred protective buffers; no validated protective allele reported. Consanguinity is not required (dominant).

3. Phenotypes

Core phenotype: bilateral congenital cataract (HP:0000519), typically non-progressive since birth, with best-corrected acuity ranging 20/200–20/25 for coralliform cases (PMID: 37480084). Morphologies are allele-/family-variable: cerulean (blue-dot), coralliform, lamellar, aculeiform (needle-like, R58H), nuclear, punctate. Associated features in specific alleles: microcornea with Y134X (HP:0000568) and nystagmus in one CRYGD family (HP:0000639) (PMID: 35222542). Onset: congenital/neonatal. Severity: variable. QoL impact: untreated → childhood visual impairment/amblyopia; treated early → good functional vision.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Causal gene: CRYGD. Variant types: missense (P23T/P24T, R58H, R36S, R36P, W42R), nonsense (Y134X, Y151X), frameshift (T160RfsX8). Classification: recurrent, segregating, pathogenic/likely pathogenic by ACMG criteria (segregation, absence in controls, functional data). Allele frequency: pathogenic alleles are rare/absent in gnomAD. Origin: germline. Functional consequence: gain-of-aggregation / dominant-negative reduction of soluble crystallin rather than haploinsufficiency; R36P reduces the soluble fraction (PMID: 39563676). Modifier: α-crystallin. Epigenetics / chromosomal abnormalities: none implicated for CTRCT4; karyotype and microarray are typically normal in these families (PMID: 42111499).

5. Environmental Information

No environmental agent causes CTRCT4; it is monogenic. Oxidative stress, UV, and heat are aggravating cofactors that lower the aggregation threshold of mutant γD-crystallin in vitro/in cellulo (PMID: 39084439). No infectious agent is involved (distinguishing it from congenital rubella cataract, which is excluded during prenatal/neonatal workup).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

See the ordered causal chain and mechanistic model above. In brief: CRYGD mutation → destabilized γD-crystallin → crystallization (R58H/R36S) or native-like condensation (P23T) or oxidative/truncation misfolding (W42R/truncations) → α-crystallin buffering exceeded → light-scattering deposits → congenital opacity → deprivation amblyopia if untreated. Protein-level dysfunction (loss of solubility/stability, non-native disulfide) is the core defect; no classical signaling pathway, enzyme deficiency, or immune process is causal.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

Primary organ: eye lens (UBERON:0000965), bilateral. Cells: lens fiber cells (CL:0000149), lens epithelial cells (CL:0002224). Subcellular: cytosol (GO:0005829). Secondary/iatrogenic: cornea (microcornea with Y134X), and post-surgical anterior-segment sequelae (glaucoma, visual-axis opacification). Lateralization: bilateral (often symmetric).

8. Temporal Development

Onset: congenital (present at birth). Pattern: non-progressive/stable in most CRYGD cases. Course: chronic/lifelong deposit (crystallins do not turnover). Critical period: ~first 14 weeks of life for surgical intervention to preserve vision (PMID: 19084444); surgery <4 weeks raises glaucoma/secondary-membrane risk, >4 weeks raises strabismus/nystagmus risk.

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: autosomal dominant. Penetrance: high (often complete within families), with variable expressivity (one allele → multiple morphologies). Mapping evidence: LOD 7.19 at the CRYG cluster (PMID: 12676897). Recurrent allele / apparent hotspot: P23T/P24T, found in 83.3% of coralliform families (PMID: 37480084). Epidemiology context: congenital cataract is the 2nd most frequent inherited eye disorder in a national registry (PMID: 42272130); ~8.3–25% of congenital cataract is inherited (PMID: 39994538). Reported across Chinese, North Indian, and European/Middle-Eastern cohorts; no strong sex bias expected for an autosomal dominant lens disorder.

10. Diagnostics

Clinical: slit-lamp/retroillumination phenotyping of bilateral opacity morphology; red-reflex screening in neonates. Genetic: NGS multigene panels or exome are first-line; congenital cataract yields 75% diagnostic rate, highest among congenital eye malformations (PMID: 41155148). Single-gene CRYGD testing confirms a known family variant. Karyotype/CMA are typically normal (PMID: 42111499). Prenatal: fetal cataract on ultrasound → exome after infectious/microarray workup (PMID: 37578328). Differential diagnosis: other crystallin (CRYAA/CRYAB/CRYBB/CRYGC/CRYGS), connexin (GJA3/GJA8), MIP, PAX6, MAF cataracts; metabolic/syndromic and congenital-infection cataracts must be excluded.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Mortality: none—non-lethal. Morbidity: entirely visual; risk of irreversible amblyopia, strabismus, nystagmus if untreated. Prognostic factors: age at surgery (dominant factor; ~14-week window), amblyopia-therapy adherence, bilaterality, and post-surgical refractive management (PMID: 19084444, PMID: 42216740). With timely surgery and amblyopia therapy, good functional vision is achievable; complications include secondary glaucoma, visual-axis opacification, and postoperative high myopia (PMID: 42288323).

12. Treatment

Established: surgical lens aspiration ± IOL implantation (NCIT: Cataract Surgery / Lens Extraction), with refractive correction and amblyopia therapy (PMID: 42216740). Capsular-bag fixation is generally preferred over ciliary-sulcus fixation in children (PMID: 42678450). No approved disease-specific drug. Experimental/pre-clinical: aggregation-dissolving sterols lanosterol and 25-hydroxycholesterol/VP1-001 (PMID: 36484206, PMID: 34419537); antioxidants (N-acetylcarnosine, N-acetylcysteine amide); peptide and chaperone-based strategies (PMID: 42657226). Pharmacogenomics: not applicable.

13. Prevention

Primary prevention: none (monogenic, congenital). Secondary: newborn red-reflex screening for early detection enabling surgery within the critical window. Genetic: cascade testing of at-risk relatives, genetic counseling (50% transmission risk per child for an affected heterozygote), prenatal/preimplantation testing where a familial variant is known. Tertiary: prevention of amblyopia (occlusion therapy) and management of post-surgical glaucoma/myopia.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

γ-crystallin cataracts are conserved. Dog: γ-crystallin candidate genes investigated in dachshund/Entlebucher mountain dog (PMID: 17327821, PMID: 16879363); autosomal recessive congenital hereditary cataract with microphthalmia in miniature schnauzer (PMID: 1869417). Orthologs: mouse Crygd (NCBI Gene 14959); zebrafish crystallins. Comparative mechanism: crystallins maintain lens transparency from fish to mammals, with conserved solubility/stability requirements.

15. Model Organisms

Zebrafish: transgenic lens expression of destabilized human γD-crystallin reproduces aggregation/opacity and dissects chaperone–client failure (PMID: 30475834). Mouse: crystallin knock-in models (αA-R49C, αB-R120G) develop nuclear/congenital cataract with increased insoluble crystallin and fiber-cell apoptosis (PMID: 18056999). Cellular: HLE-B3 and HEK293T overexpression systems recapitulate mutant aggregation and α-crystallin rescue (PMID: 39084439). Limitation: no dedicated CRYGD-P23T knock-in mouse was identified; most mechanistic data are in vitro/heterologous or from other crystallin genes.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • No CRYGD-specific mammalian knock-in model was identified; most mechanistic evidence comes from purified-protein biophysics, heterologous cell overexpression, zebrafish, or other crystallin genes (αA, αB, βA3/A1, βB2). Cross-gene inference should be treated cautiously.
  • Chaperone-buffer modifier evidence is strongest for truncation mutants (PMID: 39084439); its quantitative role for P23T and crystallization alleles in vivo is inferred, not directly demonstrated.
  • Genotype–phenotype determinants remain unexplained: why one P23T allele produces cerulean vs. coralliform vs. lamellar opacity across families is unknown (modifier genes, stochastic nucleation, and local protein concentration are candidates but untested).
  • Precise CTRCT4 prevalence is not separately quantified; figures are for congenital cataract broadly. CRYGD-attributable incidence per 100,000 is not available.
  • Experimental therapeutics (lanosterol, VP1-001, peptides) lack human trial validation and allele-specific efficacy data for CRYGD variants.
  • Penetrance/expressivity estimates are family-level qualitative, not population-calibrated.
