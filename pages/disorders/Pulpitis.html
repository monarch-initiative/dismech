Pulpitis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0006937 Pathograph 28 Show in embeddings browser Dental Pulp Disorder Bacterial Infectious Disease Inflammatory Disease

Pulpitis is inflammation of the dental pulp. It is usually caused by bacteria of a deep carious lesion whose products, and then the bacteria themselves, reach the pulp through dentinal tubules; crown fracture and operative dental procedures are less common causes. Odontoblasts at the pulp-dentine border sense bacterial products and start an innate inflammatory response, and pulpal sensory nerves add neurogenic inflammation and pain. Clinically it is graded as reversible pulpitis, where the pulp heals once the cause is removed, or irreversible pulpitis, symptomatic or asymptomatic, where it cannot. Untreated irreversible pulpitis progresses to pulp necrosis, infection of the root canal and apical periodontitis. MONDO places the term under both dental pulp disorder and bacterial infectious disease; the bacterial cause is a polymicrobial caries community rather than one pathogen, and the dental caries itself is curated separately (Dental_Caries).

Asymptomatic Irreversible Pulpitis (AIP)
A vital pulp inflamed beyond healing, usually beneath deep caries or a carious exposure, without clinical symptoms. Inflammatory markers are raised in the pulp although the tooth is painless, and the overlying caries microbiota differs from that of symptomatic teeth.

Cariogenic Biofilm Penetration of Dentine
A deep carious lesion advances through dentine towards the pulp. Bacterial products diffuse down the dentinal tubules ahead of the bacteria, and pulpal inflammation starts before the pulp is exposed, increasing as the lesion deepens. Caries is the commonest cause of pulpitis (see Dental_Caries).

Pulpal Neurogenic Inflammation
Pulpal sensory nerve fibres, which carry pain, also release substance P, CGRP, neurokinin A and other neuropeptides. These raise intrapulpal pressure and vascular permeability and amplify the inflammatory response; their levels are raised in human pulps with irreversible pulpitis.

Pulpal Nociceptor TRP Channel Sensitization
Inflammatory mediators, including TNF-alpha from immune cells, sensitize and upregulate TRPV1 and TRPA1 channels on pulpal sensory afferents, and pulpitis raises endogenous TRP agonists. Sensitized afferents respond to heat and chemical stimuli more strongly, which amplifies pulpal pain. Most of the mechanistic evidence comes from animal and cell models.

Bacteria enter the pulp tissue itself, after carious or traumatic exposure. This provokes a severe inflammatory reaction with localized pulpal abscesses, and marks the irreversible stage: bacteria in the pulp are found in irreversible pulpitis and not in reversibly inflamed pulps.

description: >-
  Pulpitis is inflammation of the dental pulp. It is usually caused by bacteria of a deep
  carious lesion whose products, and then the bacteria themselves, reach the pulp through
  dentinal tubules; crown fracture and operative dental procedures are less common causes.

  response, and pulpal sensory nerves add neurogenic inflammation and pain. Clinically it
  is graded as reversible pulpitis, where the pulp heals once the cause is removed, or irreversible

  places the term under both dental pulp disorder and bacterial infectious disease; the bacterial
  cause is a polymicrobial caries community rather than one pathogen, and the dental caries
  itself is curated separately (Dental_Caries).

1. Disease information

Overview. MeSH defines pulpitis as inflammation of the dental pulp, "usually from bacterial infection tied to caries or tooth fracture." Chemical irritants, heat and hyperemic changes can also cause it (MeSH D011671).

Clinically it runs on a continuum: - Reversible pulpitis: the pulp can heal once the irritant is removed. - Irreversible pulpitis: without intervention the pulp will progress to necrosis (Cleveland Clinic; MSD Manual).

2. Etiology

Causal factors - Infectious: Bacteria from dental caries are the main cause. Bacterial products diffuse through dentinal tubules before the pulp is exposed (MeSH; Rôças et al. 2016, PMC4852894). - Traumatic: Crown fractures that expose the pulp. - Iatrogenic: Cavity preparation and heat generation. - Chemical: Irritants, including restorative materials. - Thermal or hyperemic: As listed in MeSH.

Risk factors - Deep and extremely deep caries. The ESE defines deep caries as reaching the inner quarter of dentine, and extremely deep caries as penetrating the full thickness of dentine (ESE position statement, Duncan et al. 2019, Int Endod J 52:923–934, doi:10.1111/iej.13080). - Dental trauma. - Type 2 diabetes. In a 77-patient cross-sectional study, pulpal MMP-9 and TNF-α were "significantly higher (p<0.005) in irreversible pulpits than reversible pulpits and also in Type-2 diabetics than non-diabetics" (Eur Endod J 2023, DOAJ). This is an association with inflammatory burden, not established causation. - Genetic: I found no GWAS or validated susceptibility locus for pulpitis itself. - Reviews say "genetic polymorphisms in disease relevant genes have suggested that individual predisposition may contribute to susceptibility to pulpal and periapical diseases" (Front Physiol 2020, PMC7010935). - The best-documented variant, IL1B rs1143643, is linked to periapical lesion development in people with untreated deep caries (PMC5682944). That is a downstream endpoint, not pulpitis. - Another review covers SNPs in endodontic pathology (PMC11669393).

Protective factors - Caries control: fluoride, diet, oral hygiene. - Early management of caries with selective removal to avoid pulp exposure (ESE 2019). - Reparative (tertiary) dentin formation. - No protective genetic variants were found.

Gene–environment interactions: Not established. One plausible model is that cytokine-gene variants change the host's inflammatory response to the same caries-bacterial challenge; this is inferred from apical periodontitis data (PMC5682944). CTD lookup was not done.

  • Causal genes, pathogenic variants, ACMG classification, allele frequencies, chromosomal abnormalities: Not applicable. The disease is acquired, with no germline causal variants, and no ClinVar, gnomAD or DECIPHER entries are relevant.
  • Susceptibility and modifier candidates (low evidence): IL1B, including rs1143643 and -511 C/T, mainly for apical periodontitis (PMC5682944; Cureus 2024 review). TLR2 and TLR4 data are expression data only; TLR2 and Dectin-1 expression were correlated in irreversibly inflamed pulp (BMC Oral Health 2022, doi:10.1186/s12903-022-02621-4).

5. Environmental information

  • Infectious agents. The microbiome of deep caries in teeth with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (10 teeth, next-generation sequencing) contained 14 phyla and 101 genera (Rôças et al., PLoS ONE 2016, PMC4852894):
  • The main genera were Lactobacillus (42.3% of sequences), Olsenella (13.7%), Pseudoramibacter (10.7%) and Streptococcus (5.5%).
  • Half the samples were Lactobacillus-dominated and half had very little Lactobacillus.
  • The authors call the detected taxa "candidate pathogens for irreversible pulpitis and possibly the pioneers in pulp invasion."
  • A 2015 J Endod checkerboard study by the same group found taxa "significantly associated with symptoms" (UFRN repository).
  • In reversible pulpitis, symptomatic teeth had more Lactobacillus spp., Capnocytophaga sputigena and Leptotrichia buccalis (Clin Oral Investig, doi:10.1007/s00784-020-03624-7).

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Causal chain (some steps are inferred from in vitro or animal data, as noted)

  1. Cariogenic bacteria demineralize enamel and dentin. This leads to bacterial antigens (LPS, lipoteichoic acid) diffusing through dentinal tubules (PMC4852894).
  2. Branch: Trauma or iatrogenic exposure gives bacteria direct access to the pulp.
  3. Odontoblasts at the pulp–dentin border sense these antigens through TLR2 (Gram-positive) and TLR4 (Gram-negative). TLR2 is "upregulated in odontoblasts beneath caries lesions" (Japanese Dental Science Review 2018, PMC6094490; PMC4619960).
  4. TLR signaling results in NF-κB and p38 MAPK activation. This leads to IL-6, chemokines and other mediators (Farges et al., cited in Front Physiol 2013, PMC3823031). Evidence: in vitro and ex vivo.
  5. In parallel, Aδ and C sensory fibers release substance P and CGRP within about 24 hours (Front Dent Med 2025). Levels of CGRP, SP and neurokinin A are significantly higher in inflamed human pulp (PMID 16640639). This leads to vasodilation, more vascular permeability and recruitment of immune cells (neurogenic inflammation).
  6. Neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells and T cells are recruited (review: PMID 38959180). This results in release of proteases such as MMP-9 and of TNF-α (DOAJ 2023).
  7. Inflammatory mediators sensitize TRPV1 and TRPA1 on pulpal afferents, and the inflammation produces endogenous TRP agonists (IJMS 2025, PMC12785836). Upregulation of TTX-resistant sodium channels is proposed. Together these lead to hyperalgesia, spontaneous and lingering pain, and failure of local anesthesia (PMC4450520). The anesthesia mechanism is only partly established.
  8. Edema occurs inside the rigid, low-compliance pulp chamber. The resulting circulatory compromise is the classic explanation for spreading necrosis, but I did not retrieve a primary source this session, so treat it as inferred. Ferroptosis of pulp cells is also implicated (Int J Oral Sci 2025, PMC12521540).
  9. Branch A: If the irritant is removed, inflammation resolves and odontoblast-like cells form reparative dentin (reversible pulpitis).
  10. Branch B: If it persists, the condition progresses to pulp necrosis (K04.1), then infection of the root canal and apical periodontitis.

Molecular profiling - Single-cell RNA-seq, pulpitis: Three pulpitis and three healthy pulps gave 40,231 cells in 12 clusters. Ferroptosis-gene changes appeared in almost every cluster, and fibroblasts, which dominate healthy pulp, "significantly decreased in pulpitis" (thymosin α1 study, PMC12521540). - Single-cell RNA-seq, carious pulp: An atlas of carious versus healthy pulp found changes in immune-cell, mesenchymal stem cell and fibroblast clusters (PMID 37532703). - Single-cell pilot: 6,810 cells showed altered immune-cell composition in deep caries but not in enamel caries (Front Dent Med 2022).

7. Anatomical structures

  • Organ: Tooth; primarily the dental pulp, a connective tissue rich in nerves and blood vessels.
  • Secondary: Periapical tissues and alveolar bone, if the condition progresses to apical periodontitis. Body system: digestive/oral.
  • Cells: Odontoblasts, pulp fibroblasts, stem cells, immune cells and trigeminal afferents. Trigeminal ganglion TRPV1 changes have been shown in mouse models (PMID 21712529).
  • Subcellular: Plasma membrane (TLRs, TRP channels; GO:0005886); secreted mediators in the extracellular space.
  • Suggested UBERON (verify): dental pulp (UBERON:0001754), dentine (UBERON:0001752), tooth (UBERON:0001091), trigeminal ganglion (UBERON:0001675).
  • Lateralization: Usually a single tooth, unilateral. Referred pain can make the tooth hard to localize.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: Acute (symptomatic) or insidious (asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis). It occurs at any age once teeth have erupted.
  • Stages:
  • AAE: reversible, then irreversible (symptomatic or asymptomatic), then necrosis (AAE).
  • Wolters: initial, mild, moderate, severe.
  • A revised Wolters model reached 100% sensitivity and specificity for separating mild from severe pulpitis, and 83% sensitivity with 85.7% specificity for moderate pulpitis. Interrater agreement was κ = 0.593 for Wolters versus 0.888 for AAE (IEJ 2025, doi:10.1111/iej.14254; PMC12254530).
  • Course: Progressive without treatment. Reversible pulpitis can go into remission once the irritant is removed. The window for vital pulp therapy is before necrosis.
  • Revision under way: A joint AAE/ESE committee is revising the terminology (AAE/ESE 2025; 2026 stakeholder summary).

9. Inheritance and population

  • Inheritance: Not inherited; multifactorial and acquired. Penetrance, anticipation, founder effects and carrier frequency do not apply.
  • Epidemiology: I found no population prevalence or GBD-specific figure. Clinic-level data:
  • Irreversible pulpitis was the most common diagnosis (30.4%, 299 cases) among pulpal and periapical diagnoses in a Brazilian emergency clinic (RGO 2018).
  • It was 77% in an Iranian referral sample of 100 patients with pain (BMC Oral Health 2018).
  • "Nearly 90%" of emergency dental patients have pulpal or periapical symptoms (BDJ).
  • Demographics: These follow the burden of untreated caries. No reliable sex ratio was retrieved.

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical criteria:
  • AAE 2009: diagnosis rests on history, thermal and electric sensibility testing, percussion and radiographs. Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis is defined as "a clinical diagnosis based on subjective and objective findings indicating that the vital inflamed pulp is incapable of healing" (AAE).
  • The Wolters 2017 and ESE 2019 classifications are alternatives.
  • Histologic correlation: In 95 consecutively collected teeth, clinical "normal pulp or reversible pulpitis" matched histology in 57 of 59 (96.6%), and irreversible pulpitis matched in 27 of 32 (84.4%) (Ricucci et al., J Endod 2014;40:1932–9, PMID 25312886).
  • Imaging: Periapical or bitewing radiographs show caries depth and rule out apical periodontitis. CBCT is an adjunct.
  • Biomarkers (investigational):
  • Pulpal-blood MMP-9 "could be a useful ancillary diagnostic tool" (Mente 2016, J Endod).
  • MMP-9 normalized to total protein predicted pulp-capping survival in an RCT (PMC8935528).
  • MMP-2 and MMP-9 are raised in irreversible pulpitis of primary molars (BMC Oral Health 2024).
  • Ongoing trials: NCT06960603, NCT06514053, NCT03807674. There are no validated cut-offs.
  • Genetic testing, karyotyping, CMA, newborn or carrier screening: Not applicable.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • Dentin hypersensitivity (short, sharp pain with exposed dentin).
  • Cracked tooth.
  • Pulp necrosis and apical periodontitis (no response to sensibility tests, pain on percussion, periapical radiolucency).
  • Periodontal abscess.
  • Maxillary sinusitis.
  • Trigeminal neuralgia and other non-odontogenic pain.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Mortality: Pulpitis is essentially non-lethal. Mortality data are not applicable and none were found.
  • Morbidity: Severe pain, emergency visits and loss of the tooth after extraction.
  • Complications: Pulp necrosis, then apical periodontitis, abscess and, rarely, spreading odontogenic infection. These come from MeSH and the ICD hierarchy; I retrieved no cohort data on frequency.
  • Recovery: Reversible pulpitis resolves after the cause is removed. Irreversible pulpitis responds well to treatment:
  • Pulpotomy pooled success was 86% (95% CI 76–92%; I² = 81.9%), with root development stage and biomaterial as predictors (Ather et al., Sci Rep 2022, PMC9669040).
  • Calcium silicate vital pulp therapy reached 81–90% radiographic success (PMC8228104).
  • Prognostic factors: Severity grade, since severe Wolters pulpitis did worse than mild (Careddu & Duncan 2021); diabetes; and MMP-9 levels.

12. Treatment

Pharmacotherapy - NSAID premedication before the inferior alveolar nerve block: - All NSAIDs versus placebo: RR 1.92 (1.55–2.38). - Ibuprofen above 400 mg: RR 1.85 (1.39–2.45). - Ibuprofen at or below 400 mg: RR 1.78 (0.90–3.55, not significant). - Sources: meta-analyses at pubmed.gov 29709297, 27834417 and 30295006. I could not tell from snippets which estimate comes from which PMID. - Anesthetic strategy: Articaine, supplemental buccal infiltration or intraosseous injection, and larger volumes (JOMA review). - Analgesics: Ibuprofen and acetaminophen. - Antibiotics are not indicated. The Cochrane review included one trial of 40 patients (penicillin versus placebo) and found that antibiotics "do not appear to significantly reduce toothache"; certainty was low (Agnihotry et al. 2019, CD004969.pub5; PMC6542501). - Pharmacogenomics: None relevant.

Procedures - Indirect pulp capping and selective caries removal or stepwise excavation to avoid exposure. A hydraulic calcium silicate or glass ionomer is placed over deep dentine (ESE 2019). - Direct pulp capping; partial or full pulpotomy with MTA, Biodentine or calcium-enriched mixture (CEM) cement. - Pulpectomy and root canal treatment. - Extraction.

Key trials of pulpotomy versus root canal treatment - NCT03956199 (PMID 39325552): equivalent pain and QoL outcomes. - Taha et al., J Endod 2023 (PMID 37080387 as cited; not verified). - Systematic review PMID 37188923: "comparable clinical and patient outcomes, but insufficient evidence." - PROVE individual-participant meta-analysis protocol (PMC10722670). - Registered trials: NCT00748280 (multicenter, Iran), NCT06002698, NCT05726357, NCT07375576, NCT07046533.

Experimental (preclinical) - Thymosin α1, which acts against ferroptosis (PMC12521540). - HDAC and DNMT inhibitors for pulp repair (PMC6090030). - Gene, cell, RNA and immunotherapies: none in clinical use.

Treatment algorithm (ESE and AAE based): assess caries depth and symptoms, then choose by diagnosis: - Reversible or mild pulpitis: selective caries removal and indirect capping. - Exposure in a vital pulp: capping or partial pulpotomy, then full pulpotomy if needed. - Severe pulpitis or bleeding that cannot be controlled: pulpectomy and root canal treatment.

Suggested NCIT terms (verify): Root Canal Therapy; Pulpotomy; Tooth Extraction; Ibuprofen (C561); Local Anesthesia.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: Caries prevention (fluoride, sealants, sugar reduction, oral hygiene) and mouthguards against trauma. There is no vaccine. I did not retrieve dedicated guideline sources this session.
  • Secondary: Regular dental examination, plus radiographs to catch deep caries before the pulp is exposed.
  • Tertiary: Timely vital pulp therapy to prevent necrosis and apical periodontitis.
  • Not applicable: Genetic counseling and screening.
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis: Not supported (Cochrane).

14. Other species and natural disease

  • Dogs (Canis lupus familiaris, NCBI Taxon 9615) and cats (Felis catus, 9685):
  • Pulpitis mostly follows complicated crown fractures. In dogs and cats "most cases of pulpitis are diagnosed as irreversible" (MSD Vet Manual).
  • The maxillary fourth premolar is a frequent site.
  • Reported tooth-fracture prevalence in dogs is 20–27% (PMC9872002).
  • In military working dogs, prevalence of traumatic dental injury was 43.6% (J Vet Dent).
  • Enamel–dentin–pulp fracture was the most common injury (49.6%) in a dog and cat series (PMID 26197685, as retrieved).
  • Other: Not zoonotic, no breed predisposition was identified, and there is no OMIA entry.
