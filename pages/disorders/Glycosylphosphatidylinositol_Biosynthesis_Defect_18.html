Glycosylphosphatidylinositol Biosynthesis Defect 18

Mendelian MONDO:0029140 Pathograph 56 Show in embeddings browser Disorder of GPI Anchor Biosynthesis Congenital Disorder of Glycosylation Inborn Error of Metabolism Epilepsy

Glycosylphosphatidylinositol biosynthesis defect 18 (GPIBD18, also developmental and epileptic encephalopathy 95) is an autosomal recessive inherited GPI deficiency caused by biallelic variants in PIGS. PIGS is one of the five subunits of the GPI transamidase complex (PIGK, GPAA1, PIGS, PIGT, PIGU), which in the endoplasmic reticulum replaces the C-terminal GPI attachment signal of precursor proteins with the preassembled GPI anchor; loss of any one subunit abolishes the activity. Patient granulocytes, lymphocytes and amniocytes show reduced cell-surface GPI-anchored proteins, with CD16 and FLAER the most sensitive markers. The disorder is a severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy: profound global developmental delay with absent speech, hypotonia, intractable epilepsy beginning in the first year of life, microcephaly, cerebellar and cerebral (frontotemporal predominant) atrophy, visual and hearing impairment and coarse facial features, with early death from neurological or respiratory complications in several patients. At the severe end, two fetuses had multiple joint contractures consistent with fetal akinesia. Seizures improved with pyridoxine in a few patients. Serum alkaline phosphatase is usually normal. About fourteen individuals from ten families have been published in four reports.

Canonical GPI Transamidase Deficiency Model
canonical_gpi_transamidase_deficiency_model CANONICAL
Biallelic loss-of-function PIGS variants lower GPI transamidase activity, so fewer proteins receive a GPI anchor and the surface density of the GPI-anchored protein class falls. Because that class is important in neural development, the result is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with brain atrophy. Supported by reduced GPI-anchored proteins on patient blood cells and amniocytes and by incomplete rescue with a patient allele in PIGS-knockout cells.

Biallelic PIGS Loss-of-Function Variants
Germline biallelic PIGS variants. The published alleles are missense, nonsense, in-frame indel or duplication, and splice variants (including a canonical splice-donor allele and exonic and intronic alleles shown by RNA studies to cause exon skipping). In the founding family the p.Trp36* allele lowered PIGS mRNA and protein, and downstream methionines produced truncated isoforms with some residual activity. PIGS is not the catalytic subunit, so the activity lost is that of the whole transamidase complex.

Impaired GPI Anchor Attachment to Precursor Proteins
The GPI transamidase in the endoplasmic reticulum cleaves the C-terminal GPI attachment signal of precursor proteins, forms a carbonyl intermediate with the substrate, and transfers the preassembled GPI to the new C terminus. PIGK carries the catalytic triad; PIGS and PIGT are required for formation of the carbonyl intermediate. Without PIGS the anchor is still synthesized but is not transferred to protein; in Drosophila, loss of PIG-S destabilizes PIG-K.

Reduced Cell-Surface GPI-Anchored Proteins
Precursor proteins that are not anchored do not reach the cell surface as GPI-anchored proteins. Patient granulocytes showed reduced CD16 in all tested individuals and reduced FLAER; CD55 and CD59 were reduced in some patients and normal in others. Amniocytes from an affected fetus showed reduced signal for every GPI-anchored marker tested, the lowest being CD24 at 85% below control. In PIGS-knockout HEK293 cells the founding p.Trp36* allele only partly restored surface GPI-anchored proteins. With over 150 GPI-anchored proteins, no single one has been shown to account for any clinical feature.

Impaired Neural GPI-Anchored Protein Function
Many GPI-anchored proteins act in synaptic function, central nervous system development and plasticity. Their reduction is the proposed route to the developmental encephalopathy, but no study has attributed any feature of PIGS deficiency to a particular GPI-anchored protein.

- hypothesis_group_id: canonical_gpi_transamidase_deficiency_model
  hypothesis_label: Canonical GPI Transamidase Deficiency Model
  status: CANONICAL
  description: >-
    Biallelic loss-of-function PIGS variants lower GPI transamidase activity,
    so fewer proteins receive a GPI anchor and the surface density of the
    GPI-anchored protein class falls. Because that class is important in
    neural development, the result is a developmental and epileptic
    encephalopathy with brain atrophy. Supported by reduced GPI-anchored
    proteins on patient blood cells and amniocytes and by incomplete rescue
    with a patient allele in PIGS-knockout cells.

Status of the evidence. The clinical literature is small: four primary case papers covering about 14 individuals (12 born, 2 fetuses) from about 11 families (PMIDs 30269814, 32612635, 33410539, 37035392). 1. Disease information

Overview. GPIBD18 is an autosomal recessive inherited GPI-anchor deficiency (IGD), a subgroup of the congenital disorders of glycosylation. It is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in PIGS, which encodes a subunit of the GPI transamidase (GPI-T). The clinical range runs from fetal akinesia, at the lethal end, to an early-infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE). Other features include hypotonia, microcephaly, coarse facies, visual and hearing impairment, and brain atrophy. The seizures sometimes respond to pyridoxine.

"We present a study of six individuals from three unrelated families in which WES or WGS identified bi-allelic phosphatidylinositol glycan class S (PIGS) biosynthesis mutations. Phenotypes included severe global developmental delay, seizures (partly responding to pyridoxine), hypotonia, weakness, ataxia, and dysmorphic facial features." (PMID:30269814, Nguyen et al., Am J Hum Genet 2018)

2. Etiology

  • Cause: biallelic germline PIGS variants. These reduce GPI-T activity, and less GPI-anchored protein (GPI-AP) reaches the cell surface.
  • Genetic risk factors:
  • Parental consanguinity or shared ancestry. In 4 of 5 Efthymiou families the parents were consanguineous and the variants homozygous (PMID:33410539).
  • One Mexican family came from "the same small rural community" (PMID:30269814).
  • East Asian recurrent allele c.1141_1164dup (p.Asp381_Val388dup). It has been found in three unrelated East Asian probands and is "seen in gnomAD in a heterozygous carrier state in five individuals of East Asian ethnicity" (PMID:33410539, 32612635, 37035392).
  • Environmental risk, protective factors, G×E: none reported. Fever and infection make seizures worse ("aggravated by fevers or infections", PMID:32612635), but that is a trigger for seizures, not a cause of the disease.
  • Modifier genes: none reported.

6. Mechanism / pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Biallelic PIGS LoF or hypomorphic variants lead to less PIGS mRNA or protein, or to a dysfunctional protein. This is shown in patient LCLs (qPCR, western blot) and by HEK293 rescue (PMID:30269814).
  2. Loss of PIGS leads to a defective GPI transamidase complex in the ER membrane. The complex has five subunits, PIGK, GPAA1, PIGT, PIGS and PIGU (PMID:35165458, 12802054).
  3. Within the complex, loss of PIGS destabilizes PIGK, the catalytic subunit: "loss of PIG-S reduced the stability of PIG-K and left the other subunits unaffected" (PMID:30914202; Drosophila/cell model).
  4. PIGK carries the "C206-H164-N58 triad that is critical for the transamination reaction" (PMID:35165458).
  5. The defective transamidase cannot form the carbonyl intermediate, so GPI is not transferred to proproteins. The fully built GPI lipid accumulates but is not attached:

    "PIG-S and PIG-T knockout cells were defective in transfer of GPI to proteins, particularly in formation of the carbonyl intermediates." (PMID:11483512; mouse F9 cells, IN_VITRO)

  6. Failed GPI attachment leads to fewer GPI-APs on the cell surface: CD16 and FLAER on granulocytes, FLAER on lymphocytes, CD14 on monocytes, and CD24/CD55/CD59/CD73 on amniocytes (PMID:30269814, 33410539). Proproteins that cannot be anchored are presumably degraded by ER quality control. That step is inferred from the general IGD biology, not shown for PIGS specifically.
  7. Neural branch (inferred): missing neural GPI-APs (synaptic, adhesion and axon-guidance proteins) lead to abnormal cortical and pontocerebellar development, atrophy and network hyperexcitability. Clinically this appears as DEE, microcephaly, frontotemporal and pontine atrophy, and developmental delay. No PIGS-specific neural experiment exists; the authors infer this from the IGD class. Efthymiou: "There are more than 150 human GPI‐APs, which often play a role in synaptic function, development of the central nervous system (CNS), and plasticity."
  8. Vitamin B6 branch (hypothesis): less GPI-anchored tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP) means less dephosphorylation of PLP to pyridoxal and less pyridoxal transport into the brain. The result would be a CNS pyridoxal-5′-phosphate shortfall and reduced GABA synthesis, lowering the seizure threshold. This is consistent with the pyridoxine response, but CSF PLP has never been measured in PIGS patients ("we could not measure the cerebrospinal fluid pyridoxal‐5‐phosphate levels", PMID:33410539). The link to folate, through the GPI-anchored folate receptor FOLR1, is equally speculative.
  9. Developmental/musculoskeletal branch: severe GPI-AP deficiency in the fetus leads to fetal akinesia and contractures, and to cystic hygroma and renal cysts (PMID:30269814). The mechanism is not characterized.
  10. Terminal branch: severe hypotonia, bulbar dysfunction and feeding difficulty lead to aspiration and recurrent respiratory infection, and these drive early death (PMID:32612635, 33410539).

7. Anatomical structures affected

  • Primary organ: brain. Frontal and anterior temporal lobes, pons, cerebellar vermis, corpus callosum and white matter (PMID:33410539). Candidate UBERON terms: cerebral cortex, pons, cerebellar vermis, corpus callosum (unverified).
  • Secondary organs:
  • eye and visual cortex (cortical blindness, nystagmus);
  • inner ear;
  • skeleton (hands: brachytelephalangy);
  • gingiva;
  • kidney (glomerular cysts, malformation);
  • heart (VSD in one case);
  • joints (contractures);
  • respiratory system, secondarily.
  • Subcellular: ER membrane, where the transamidase sits, and the plasma-membrane outer leaflet, where GPI-APs are lost.
  • Lateralization: brain changes are bilateral and symmetric.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: prenatal (fetal akinesia, thickened nuchal fold noted in the Li 2023 case) through early infancy. Median seizure onset is under 3 months.
  • Course: chronic, lifelong and progressive. Seizure types change over time (focal, then myoclonic or spasms), and atrophy progresses on MRI. There is no remission without treatment. A partial or full seizure response to pyridoxine has been reported in 3–4 cases.
  • Critical period: early infancy, when an early pyridoxine trial is recommended.

9. Inheritance and population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive, HP:0000007 (unverified; it does not appear in the cache lookups above). Penetrance appears complete for biallelic pathogenic genotypes. Expressivity varies from fetal lethal to survival into childhood; the oldest reported individuals were about 7.5–9 years old.
  • Prevalence: unknown, about 14 reported cases. Use CASES_IN_LITERATURE; no numeric estimate exists. IGDs as a whole may account for about 0.15% of developmental disorders: "IGDs alone might account for 0.15% of all developmental disorders" (PMID:30269814, citing the DDD study).
  • Populations: European (twins), Mexican, Middle Eastern / South Asian (consanguineous families in the Efthymiou cohort, mainly Iran and Pakistan per the authors' recruitment, though country is not stated per family in the abstract), and Chinese (recurrent dup allele). There is no reported sex bias, though most reported cases are male.
  • Not reported: anticipation, germline mosaicism, carrier-frequency data.

10. Diagnostics

  • Genetic testing: trio exome or genome sequencing is the route to diagnosis in every reported case. PIGS is on DEE, IGD and CDG panels (Genomics England PanelApp green). RNA studies (minigene assay, RT-PCR) are needed to confirm splice variants (PMID:33410539, 37035392).
  • Flow cytometry (key functional biomarker): reduced CD16 and FLAER on granulocytes, with CD55/CD59 variably reduced.

    "Individual 6 had significantly lower levels of CD16 and fluorescent aerolysin (FLAER) in granulocytes, the most sensitive markers of inherited GPI deficiency" (PMID:33410539)

  • Supportive or exclusion labs:
  • serum alkaline phosphatase, usually normal, which separates PIGS from PIGV/PIGO/PGAP3 deficiency;
  • normal transferrin isoelectric focusing;
  • no 2-oxoglutaric aciduria.

Nguyen et al.: "Laboratory findings that can help distinguish PIGS-associated disorders … include flow-cytometry analysis with reduced CD16 expression … a lack of elevated serum tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase … and normal serum transferrin isoelectric focusing" (PMID:30269814). - EEG: hypsarrhythmia or pseudohypsarrhythmia, burst suppression, multifocal discharges. The EEG may be normal at onset (PMID:37035392). - MRI: frontotemporal-predominant atrophy, pontine hypoplasia, vermian atrophy, delayed myelination. - Differential diagnosis: - other IGDs, especially PIGT, GPAA1, PIGK and PIGU (the transamidase-complex disorders); - DOORS syndrome (TBC1D24); - other CDGs; - peroxisomal disorders and mucopolysaccharidoses; - other pyridoxine-dependent epilepsies (ALDH7A1, PNPO); - West syndrome of other cause. - Screening: no newborn screening. 11. Outcome / prognosis

  • Mortality is high. In the Efthymiou cohort, 4 of 6 died ("Four individuals died due to neurologic complications"), several from recurrent respiratory infection. The Zhang patient died at 15 months, "due to asphyxia during an epileptic seizure" (PMID:32612635). Both fetal-akinesia pregnancies were terminated.
  • Morbidity: profound intellectual disability, no speech, non-ambulatory in most cases, cortical visual impairment and deafness.
  • Prognostic factors (tentative): a combined splice/missense or truncating genotype with very low GPI-AP levels points toward fetal lethality. A pyridoxine response is associated with better seizure control and development.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: genetic counseling, carrier testing of relatives, and prenatal or preimplantation genetic diagnosis for known familial variants. These are especially relevant in consanguineous families.
  • Secondary prevention: early exome sequencing in infantile DEE, then an early pyridoxine trial.
  • Tertiary prevention:
  • seizure control;
  • aspiration prevention (feeding assessment, gastrostomy where needed);
  • prompt treatment of respiratory infections, the main cause of death;
  • vision and hearing screening.
  • There are no vaccines, no public-health measures and no prophylactic drugs.

14. Other species / natural disease

  • Orthologs: mouse Pigs (MGI:2687325) and S. cerevisiae GPI17. Ohishi et al.: "Saccharomyces cerevisiae … Gpi17p (YDR434W) … orthologues of … PIG-S" (PMID:11483512). There is also an Arabidopsis AtPIG-S (PMID:32811442, pollen-tube growth) and a Drosophila ortholog (PMID:30914202, 33496978).
  • Naturally occurring disease: none found in OMIA. No veterinary relevance and no zoonotic potential.
