Bowen-Conradi Syndrome

Mendelian MONDO:0008879 Pathograph 15 Show in embeddings browser Ribosomopathy Multiple congenital anomalies/dysmorphic syndrome-intellectual disability

Bowen-Conradi syndrome is a lethal autosomal recessive multiple congenital anomaly syndrome with severe prenatal and postnatal growth failure, microcephaly, a prominent nose, micrognathia, joint contractures and rocker-bottom feet, profound psychomotor delay and death in infancy, at an average age of about 13 months. Almost all cases are in the Hutterite population of the Canadian Prairies and the northern Great Plains of the United States, where the birth prevalence is about 1 in 355 and all affected children are homozygous for one founder missense variant in EMG1, p.Asp86Gly. EMG1 (also called NEP1) is a nucleolar SPOUT-class methyltransferase of the small subunit processome that methylates a pseudouridine of 18S rRNA to begin forming the hypermodified m1acp3-pseudouridine and that is needed, as an assembly factor, for 18S rRNA maturation and 40S ribosomal subunit biogenesis. The disease is therefore a ribosomopathy. The Asp86Gly substitution leaves catalytic activity intact but makes the protein unstable, prone to aggregate and poorly recruited to the nucleolus, so EMG1 protein is severely reduced in patient cells. The result is delayed 18S rRNA processing and a proliferation defect, with G2/M delay and a mitotic defect, in rapidly dividing cells, which is thought to underlie the global growth failure and the failure of brain growth.

Reduced Nucleolar Recruitment of EMG1
EMG1 is imported into the nucleus by importins, which also act as chaperones, and is recruited to nucleoli as part of a subcomplex with NOP14, NOC4L and UTP14A. The mutant protein is released from the import complex, aggregates in the nucleus and reaches the nucleolus less efficiently, so less EMG1 is available to the small subunit processome. One study in patient fibroblasts did not detect a change in nucleolar localization, so the extent of this step in patient cells is uncertain.

Delayed 18S rRNA Maturation and Small Ribosomal Subunit Biogenesis
EMG1 is a small subunit processome factor required for 18S rRNA maturation and 40S subunit production, in addition to catalysing N1-methylation of the pseudouridine at the m1acp3-pseudouridine site of 18S rRNA. In yeast the assembly-factor role and the methyltransferase role are separable. In patient lymphoblasts and fibroblasts, 18S rRNA processing is slightly but consistently delayed, although steady-state 40S levels and global protein synthesis are not detectably reduced in these cells.

name: Bowen-Conradi Syndrome
creation_date: "2026-10-29T03:40:00Z"
description: >-
  Bowen-Conradi syndrome is a lethal autosomal recessive multiple congenital
  anomaly syndrome with severe prenatal and postnatal growth failure,
  microcephaly, a prominent nose, micrognathia, joint contractures and
  rocker-bottom feet, profound psychomotor delay and death in infancy, at an
  average age of about 13 months. Almost all cases are in the Hutterite
  population of the Canadian Prairies and the northern Great Plains of the
  United States, where the birth prevalence is about 1 in 355 and all affected
  children are homozygous for one founder missense variant in EMG1, p.Asp86Gly.
  EMG1 (also called NEP1) is a nucleolar SPOUT-class methyltransferase of the
  small subunit processome that methylates a pseudouridine of 18S rRNA to begin
  forming the hypermodified m1acp3-pseudouridine and that is needed, as an
  assembly factor, for 18S rRNA maturation and 40S ribosomal subunit
  biogenesis. The disease is therefore a ribosomopathy. The Asp86Gly
  substitution leaves catalytic activity intact but makes the protein unstable,
  prone to aggregate and poorly recruited to the nucleolus, so EMG1 protein is
  severely reduced in patient cells. The result is delayed 18S rRNA processing
  and a proliferation defect, with G2/M delay and a mitotic defect, in rapidly
  dividing cells, which is thought to underlie the global growth failure and
  the failure of brain growth.

  No GeneReviews chapter exists for Bowen-Conradi syndrome; the phenotype
  baseline is the Hutterite clinical series and the Orphanet record.

  Every molecularly confirmed patient is a Hutterite homozygous for EMG1
  p.Asp86Gly, and no other disease-causing EMG1 variant has been published.
  Before the gene was known, a few non-Hutterite infants (including one from
  India) were reported as Bowen-Conradi syndrome on clinical grounds alone;
  none of these was tested for EMG1, and the phenotype overlaps trisomy 18 and
  cerebro-oculo-facial-skeletal syndrome, so whether the disease occurs outside
  the Hutterite population is not established. In the Hutterites themselves, a
  sibling of a clinically diagnosed child was found to have the same
  phenotype without the EMG1 variant, so clinical diagnosis is not reliable
  even within the founder population.

  One mechanistic study describes Bowen-Conradi syndrome as including bone
  marrow failure, and reduced cell proliferation has been reported in patient
  bone marrow at autopsy, but cytopenias are not part of the clinical
  descriptions used here, so bone marrow failure is not recorded as a
  phenotype.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — BCS is caused by a homozygous EMG1 founder mutation (p.Asp86Gly)

BCS maps to chromosome 12p13.3 and is caused by the EMG1 variant p.Asp86Gly, originally reported as NM_006331.6:c.400A>G and in later literature/ClinVar as NM_006331.8:c.257A>G. The mutation segregated with disease across Hutterite pedigrees and was absent from 414 non-Hutterite alleles, consistent with a population-specific founder allele. It alters a highly conserved aspartate residue; a structural model predicts loss of a stabilizing D86–R84 salt bridge.

"We identified variant NM_006331.6:c.400A-->G, p.D86G in the 18S ribosome assembly protein EMG1 as the probable cause of BCS. This mutation segregated with disease, was not found in 414 non-Hutterite alleles, and altered a highly conserved aspartic acid (D) residue." — PMID: 19463982

The founder effect is striking: nearly all reported cases occur among Hutterites, with birth prevalence ~1/355 (PMID: 19463982) and a carrier frequency of ~1 in 10 — "making BCS one of the most commonly inherited genetic diseases in any human group studied to date" (PMID: 22856010).

Finding 2 — EMG1/Nep1 is a SPOUT-class pseudouridine-N1-methyltransferase with a dual role

EMG1 (yeast Nep1) is a nucleolar SPOUT-class RNA methyltransferase that catalyzes N1-methylation of a pseudouridine (Ψ1191 in yeast 18S rRNA), contributing to the hypermodified base m1acp3Ψ.

"Nep1 only methylates RNAs containing a pseudouridine at a position corresponding to a previously identified hypermodified N1-methyl-N3-(3-amino-3-carboxypropyl) pseudouridine (m1acp3-Psi) in eukaryotic 18S rRNAs." — PMID: 20047967

Critically, EMG1 has a dual function: an enzymatic methyltransferase activity and a separable, essential role as a pre-ribosome assembly factor required for small-subunit rRNA maturation and Rps19 incorporation.

"This strongly suggests a dual Nep1 function, as Ψ1191-methyltransferase and ribosome assembly factor." — PMID: 20972225

The disease mechanism is tied to the assembly role, not catalysis: "Interestingly, the Nep1 methyltransferase activity is not affected upon introduction of the BCS mutation." — PMID: 20972225. Structural work confirms Nep1 recognizes rRNA via base-specific interactions and that catalytic activity may be dispensable while RNA binding/assembly is critical (PMID: 21087996).

Finding 3 — D86G destabilizes EMG1 causing aggregation, degradation, and failed nucleolar recruitment

The pathogenic mechanism is protein instability, not loss of transcription. BCS patient fibroblasts show normal EMG1 mRNA but dramatically reduced EMG1 protein:

"In BCS patient fibroblasts, EMG1 mRNA levels did not differ from those of normal cells, but EMG1 protein was dramatically reduced in comparison to that of normal controls." — PMID: 19463982

The D86G substitution disrupts the D86–R84 salt bridge, increases EMG1 self-interaction/dimerization, and promotes aggregation. Mutant EMG1 is still imported into the nucleus but accumulates in nuclear foci and is degraded:

"The BCS mutation in EMG1 leads to reduced nucleolar localisation, accumulation of EMG1D86G in nuclear foci and its proteasome-dependent degradation." — PMID: 27798105

This reduced nucleolar pool, acting within the NOP14–NOC4L–UTP14A subcomplex, impairs small-subunit biogenesis.

Finding 4 — EMG1 deficiency delays 18S rRNA processing and causes G2/M arrest and reduced proliferation

Downstream of EMG1 depletion, patient cells show a consistent delay in 18S rRNA processing and accumulate at the G2/M boundary, with cell-type-specific proliferation effects (lymphoblasts strongly affected, fibroblasts less so):

"In BCS patient lymphoblasts, cells accumulated in G2/M, resulting in reduced proliferation rates; however, patient fibroblasts showed normal proliferation. The rate of 18S rRNA processing was consistently delayed in patient cells" — PMID: 26676230

A D86G knock-in mouse revealed a mitotic-progression defect underlying growth arrest:

"In fibroblasts derived from the BCS mouse embryos, we detected a high proportion of binucleated cells, indicating that a mitotic defect underlies the growth arrest in BCS." — PMID: 25708872

"These embryos exhibited dramatically reduced cell proliferation, which we also detected in autopsy brain tissue and bone marrow of BCS patients" — PMID: 25708872

This explains the tissue selectivity: high-turnover tissues (embryo, brain, marrow) are preferentially compromised.

Finding 5 — Clinical phenotype: severe growth failure, microcephaly, trisomy-18-like gestalt, infant death

BCS is a lethal autosomal-recessive condition characterized by severely impaired prenatal and postnatal growth, profound psychomotor retardation, and early death:

"Bowen-Conradi syndrome (BCS) is a ribosomopathy characterized by severe developmental delay and growth failure that typically leads to death by one year of age." — PMID: 25708872

"Bowen-Conradi syndrome (BCS) is a lethal autosomal recessive condition having significant clinical overlap with trisomy 18." — PMID: 22856010

Bone marrow failure is among the recognized features:

"Bowen-Conradi syndrome (BCS) is a severe genetic disorder that is characterised by various developmental abnormalities, bone marrow failure and early infant death." — PMID: 27798105

The original clinical delineation (Hunter et al., 1979) listed the defining features:

"the major features of the syndrome include porportionate intrauterine growth retardation, microcephaly, micrognathia, a prominent nose, rocker-bottom feet, joint limitation, and failure to thrive, with death within the first year of life" — PMID: 484596

Finding 6 — Mouse models: Emg1 is essential for embryogenesis; D86G recapitulates growth failure and neural tube defects

Complete loss of Emg1 is early-embryonic lethal and p53-independent:

"Loss of EMG1 function in mice arrested embryonic development prior to the blastocyst stage." — PMID: 20858271

"loss of p53, which has been shown to rescue some phenotypes resulting from defects in ribosome biogenesis, failed to rescue the Emg1-/- pre-implantation lethality" — PMID: 20858271

The D86G knock-in recapitulates the human disease:

"Embryos homozygous for the mutation in Emg1 were small for gestational age with neural tube defects, and died between embryonic days 8.5 and 12.5." — PMID: 25708872

A 2025 study adds a developmental-signaling dimension — EMG1 cooperates with GRHL3 in β-catenin/Wnt-dependent surface-ectoderm differentiation:

"Conditional knockout mutation of Emg1 in the GRHL3-positive surface ectoderm causes neural tube defects at the level of the spinal cord, i.e. spina bifida." — PMID: 40761126

Finding 7 — Natural history and locus mapping

A formal clinical/genetic study ascertained 39 cases and estimated birth prevalence at 1/355 (PMID: 12838567). A genome-wide scan localized the gene to a 3.5 cM (1.9 Mbp) segment of 12p13.3 and established genetic distinctness from COFS:

"the BCS locus was mapped to a 3.5 cM segment (1.9 Mbp) in chromosome region 12p13.3 bounded by F8VWF and D12S397" — PMID: 15578624

"This study also demonstrates that BCS and cerebro-oculo-facial-skeletal syndrome (COFS) are genetically distinct." — PMID: 15578624

Finding 8 — Variant annotation locked down (ClinVar, gnomAD)

ClinVar classifies NM_006331.8(EMG1):c.257A>G (p.Asp86Gly) as Pathogenic for BCS (VCV000000890; dbSNP rs74435397; UniProt VAR_062480; OMIM allelic variant 611531.0001; GRCh38 NC_000012.12:g.6974427A>G). In gnomAD v4 the allele frequency is ~6.6×10⁻⁶ (1/152,190) and it is absent from all major continental ancestry groups queried — reflecting the Hutterite population's near-absence from reference databases. Two other EMG1 variants are listed for this disease: c.214A>G (p.Ile72Val, Uncertain significance) and c.126dup (p.Leu43fs, Benign).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation):

  1. Homozygous EMG1 c.257A>G (p.Asp86Gly) mutation results in an amino-acid substitution at a conserved residue (demonstrated).
  2. D86G disrupts a predicted D86–R84 salt bridge, which leads to protein destabilization, increased self-interaction/dimerization, and aggregation (structural model + in vitro; partly inferred).
  3. Destabilized EMG1 is imported to the nucleus but leads to accumulation in nuclear foci and proteasome-dependent degradation (demonstrated, PMID: 27798105).
  4. Degradation results in a dramatically reduced steady-state EMG1 protein pool despite normal mRNA (demonstrated, PMID: 19463982).
  5. Reduced nucleolar EMG1 impairs small-subunit (SSU) processome function / 40S ribosome assembly and Rps19 incorporation (demonstrated in yeast/human).
  6. This leads to delayed 18S rRNA processing (demonstrated, PMID: 26676230).
  7. Delayed SSU biogenesis results in G2/M cell-cycle arrest and a mitotic-progression defect (binucleation) (demonstrated, PMID: 25708872).
  8. These lead to dramatically reduced cell proliferation, preferentially in high-turnover tissues — embryo, developing brain, bone marrow (demonstrated).
  9. Branch A (growth/CNS): reduced proliferation causes IUGR, microcephaly, psychomotor delay.
  10. Branch B (hematologic): reduced marrow proliferation causes bone marrow failure.
  11. Branch C (morphogenesis, mouse): EMG1–GRHL3/β-catenin–Wnt axis disruption causes neural tube defects/spina bifida (model organism; relevance to human facial/skeletal features inferred).
  12. Combined tissue failure results in the lethal congenital phenotype and death within the first year.

Molecular pathways: ribosome biogenesis / SSU processome; rRNA pseudouridine-N1-methylation; (mouse) Wnt/β-catenin surface-ectoderm differentiation. Cellular processes: cell-cycle (G2/M) regulation, mitosis, proliferation; nucleologenesis. Notably, the phenotype is p53-independent (PMID: 20858271), distinguishing it from some other ribosomopathies. Protein dysfunction: loss of function via misfolding/aggregation and accelerated degradation (not loss of catalysis). 8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: congenital/prenatal (IUGR detectable in utero).
  • Onset pattern: chronic/insidious from conception; not acute.
  • Progression: progressive postnatally with failure to thrive; no remission.
  • Course/duration: uniformly lethal, typically within the first year of life.
  • Critical period: embryogenesis and early infancy (windows of highest proliferative demand). No therapeutic window has been established.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive; homozygous founder allele.
  • Penetrance: complete (fully penetrant, lethal).
  • Expressivity: relatively consistent/severe.
  • Epidemiology: birth prevalence ~1/355 live births in Hutterites (PMID: 12838567, PMID: 19463982); carrier frequency ~1/10 in Hutterites (PMID: 22856010).
  • Founder effect / geography: Hutterite populations of North America; rare possible non-Hutterite cases reported (PMID: 15578624, PMID: 12002150).
  • Consanguinity: endogamous Hutterite community structure underlies the high homozygosity.
  • Sex ratio: no sex bias (autosomal); not applicable.
  • Anticipation / mosaicism: not applicable (non-repeat, Mendelian).

10. Diagnostics

  • Genetic testing (definitive): targeted single-gene/variant testing for EMG1 c.257A>G is the diagnostic gold standard; clinical molecular testing became available ~2015. The first molecularly confirmed case was reported by PMID: 25799636. WES/WGS and gene panels for ribosomopathies also detect it.
  • Importance of molecular confirmation: clinically indistinguishable Hutterite phenocopies exist — an infant clinically diagnosed as BCS was found to have a normal EMG1 sequence (PMID: 25906497), underscoring that molecular testing is essential.
  • Clinical/laboratory clues: IUGR, microcephaly, characteristic facies; possible cytopenias (bone marrow failure). Functional assays (18S rRNA processing delay) exist in research settings only.
  • Imaging: prenatal ultrasound (IUGR, microcephaly); no specific radiologic signature.
  • Differential diagnosis: trisomy 18 (karyotype/CMA to exclude), COFS, CHARGE syndrome; distinguished by EMG1 molecular testing.
  • Screening: carrier screening and cascade testing in Hutterite families; prenatal diagnosis.

The central insight is that BCS is a protein-stability ribosomopathy: the mutation does not eliminate EMG1's enzymatic function but destroys the protein, starving the nucleolus of a factor needed to build the small ribosomal subunit. Because the bottleneck is proliferation (via delayed 40S biogenesis and mitotic failure), the tissues that suffer are those dividing fastest during development — a unifying explanation for the growth failure, microcephaly, and marrow failure. The p53-independence sets BCS apart from classical "nucleolar-stress/p53" ribosomopathies and suggests the mitotic/proliferation block is the proximate driver.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Narrow genetic spectrum. Essentially all confirmed human cases carry the single founder allele; the phenotypic consequences of other EMG1 variants (e.g., p.Ile72Val VUS) are unknown. Genotype–phenotype breadth cannot be assessed.
  2. Tissue-selectivity mechanism incompletely resolved. Why some proliferating cell types (lymphoblasts) are more sensitive than others (fibroblasts) is not fully explained; redundancy or compensatory buffering is hypothesized but undefined.
  3. Bone marrow failure under-characterized in humans — reported as a feature but not systematically quantified across the cohort.
  4. Human relevance of the Wnt–GRHL3/NTD branch rests on mouse data; it is not established whether this pathway contributes to the human facial/skeletal gestalt or whether human BCS fetuses have NTDs at comparable frequency.
  5. No natural animal disease / no longitudinal human omics. There are no transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic patient datasets analyzed here; molecular profiling is limited to targeted assays.
  6. Mouse–human severity mismatch. The knock-in is embryonic-lethal whereas humans survive to infancy, limiting direct translation of therapeutic windows.
  7. No therapeutics. Absence of any disease-modifying treatment or trial means prognostic and treatment sections rely on supportive-care principles rather than disease-specific evidence.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Protein-stabilization screen. Small-molecule or pharmacological-chaperone screens to rescue EMG1-D86G from proteasomal degradation (e.g., proteasome modulation, chemical chaperones) in patient fibroblasts — a tractable target since mRNA and catalysis are intact.
  2. Patient-derived iPSC/organoid models. Generate iPSC-derived neural progenitors and hematopoietic progenitors to model the proliferation defect in the most-affected human lineages and test rescue strategies.
  3. Single-cell transcriptomics of D86G knock-in mouse embryos and patient-derived progenitors to map cell-type-specific vulnerability and identify downstream effectors of the G2/M block.
  4. Gene/mRNA replacement feasibility. Assess AAV- or LNP-mRNA-based EMG1 restoration in the knock-in mouse, given that the defect is loss-of-function and dose-sensitive.
  5. Define the Wnt–GRHL3 axis in humans. Re-examine BCS autopsy material for neural tube and surface-ectoderm defects to test the translational relevance of PMID: 40761126.
  6. Expand carrier screening in Hutterite colonies and systematically report any non-Hutterite EMG1 variants to clarify the disease's genetic spectrum and population reach.
  7. p53-independence follow-up. Dissect the p53-independent cell-cycle checkpoint driving G2/M arrest to identify alternative, druggable nodes.
