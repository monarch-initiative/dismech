Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 4

Mendelian MONDO:0010847 Pathograph 26 Show in embeddings browser Autosomal Dominant Cerebellar Ataxia Type I

Spinocerebellar ataxia type 4 (SCA4) is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disease caused by a heterozygous, uninterrupted GGC repeat expansion in the last coding exon (exon 10) of ZFHX3, which encodes an expanded polyglycine tract near the C-terminus of the transcription factor ZFHX3 (ATBF1). The locus was mapped to 16q22.1 in a Utah kindred of Swedish ancestry in 1996, but the expansion sits in an extremely GC-rich region and was found only in 2023-2024 by long-read sequencing. All reported pathogenic alleles share one ancient Swedish founder haplotype. The disease combines cerebellar ataxia with an invariable sensory neuropathy, slow saccades, brainstem signs, upper and lower motor neuron involvement and autonomic failure, of which orthostatic hypotension is the most disabling. Onset ranges from adolescence to the seventh decade; longer repeats give earlier and more severe disease, and anticipation is common. Autopsies show Purkinje cell loss, widespread brainstem and spinal neuronal loss, and p62- and ubiquitin-positive neuronal inclusions in the central and enteric nervous systems. Patient cells have more ZFHX3 protein, polyglycine aggregates and abnormal mTOR and autophagy markers that normalize when ZFHX3 is knocked down. Treatment is supportive.

ZFHX3 Exonic GGC Repeat Expansion
Mechanism confidence: Established
A heterozygous GGC repeat expansion in exon 10 of ZFHX3. Normal alleles carry about 21 units interrupted by GGT and AGT codons; expanded alleles (roughly 40 to 57 units in reported families) are pure GGC. The expansion is in frame and encodes an elongated polyglycine tract in the C-terminal part of a roughly 400 kDa homeodomain-zinc finger transcription factor. It is not detectable by short-read exome sequencing.

Polyglycine-Expanded ZFHX3 Accumulation and Aggregation
Mechanism confidence: Provisional
Steady-state ZFHX3 protein is increased in patient fibroblasts and iPSCs, and patient iPSCs contain large cytoplasmic polyglycine aggregates that co-label with ubiquitin. In autopsy tissue, neurons carry p62- and ubiquitin-positive intranuclear (and in one Swedish case also intracytoplasmic) inclusions in the pons, medulla and elsewhere in the brain, and in myenteric plexus neurons. The inclusions do not react with the polyglutamine antibody 1C2. Whether aggregation, the excess protein or a nuclear loss of function drives neuronal death is not established.
