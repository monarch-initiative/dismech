Congenital Syphilis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0005714 Pathograph 53 Show in embeddings browser Syphilis Congenital infection

Congenital syphilis is infection of the fetus or newborn with Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum acquired from an infected mother, almost always across the placenta during maternal spirochetemia. Transmission can happen at any stage of maternal infection and in any trimester, and its risk is highest when the mother has primary or secondary syphilis. Untreated maternal syphilis causes early fetal loss, stillbirth, hydrops, preterm birth, and neonatal death. Live-born infants are often asymptomatic at birth. Early congenital syphilis (presenting before age 2 years) resembles disseminated secondary syphilis, with rash, rhinitis (snuffles), hepatosplenomegaly with hepatitis and cholestasis, anemia and thrombocytopenia, and osteochondritis and periostitis of the long bones that cause pseudoparalysis. Late congenital syphilis (age 2 years or older) comprises interstitial keratitis, eighth nerve deafness, and Hutchinson teeth (the Hutchinson triad), together with stigmata of disturbed development such as saddle nose, saber shins, and palatal perforation. The disease is prevented by syphilis screening early in pregnancy, repeated in the third trimester and at delivery where risk is high, and by treating the mother with a stage-appropriate benzathine penicillin G regimen at least 30 days before delivery. Infected or at-risk newborns are classified by the CDC risk scenarios and treated with a 10-day course of aqueous crystalline or procaine penicillin G, or a single dose of benzathine penicillin G when infection is less likely.

Maternal Treponemal Spirochetemia
An untreated or inadequately treated pregnant woman with syphilis has treponemes in her bloodstream. The risk that the fetus is infected tracks maternal treponemal burden, which is highest in primary and secondary syphilis and is marked by a high nontreponemal titer. This is the trigger node of the pathograph.

Transplacental Treponemal Invasion
T. pallidum attaches to and crosses the placenta. The organism lacks toxins but binds fibronectin and laminin, which helps it attach to endothelium and leave blood vessels. The adhesin Tp0751 (pallilysin) binds endothelial cells through the 67-kDa laminin receptor (LamR) and is implicated in crossing vascular, placental and blood-brain barriers, and a surface lipoprotein (Tp0954) mediates attachment to placental cell lines. Both were shown in cultured cells, not in human placenta. Treponemes are visible in the placenta in many cases, with increased numbers of Hofbauer cells, the placental macrophages.

Fetal Anemia
Disseminated infection causes fetal anemia, seen on ultrasound as raised middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity, with erythroblastosis more common in stillborn than live-born infected infants. Severe anemia leads to ascites and nonimmune hydrops, recorded as a separate downstream phenotype; hydrops in turn marks a higher risk of fetal treatment failure and of neonatal death.

Developmental Disruption of Bone and Dentition
Infection during development leaves permanent deformities. Failure of the nasal bones to develop produces saddle nose. Infection of the developing tooth buds produces notched, semilunar Hutchinson incisors and mulberry molars. Saber shin is modelled from the periostitis node and palatal perforation from the gummatous necrosis node, because both are destructive or remodelling processes rather than failures of development.

Gummatous Tissue Necrosis
Late congenital syphilis is the congenital counterpart of tertiary syphilis, and can produce gummas, destructive granulomatous lesions with chronic central necrosis, in mucosa, skin, bone, and viscera. A gumma of the palate necroses and perforates it. This is tissue destruction during persisting late infection, distinct from the developmental failures that produce saddle nose and Hutchinson teeth.

Treponemal Peptidoglycan Cross-Linking (Beta-Lactam Target)
T. pallidum maintains its peptidoglycan cell wall by penicillin-binding protein cross-linking, the target of penicillin G. Penicillin G is the only agent known to treat fetal infection and prevent congenital syphilis, and it is the basis of every maternal and neonatal regimen.

Prevention and detection depend on serologic screening of every pregnant woman at the first prenatal visit, repeated at 28 weeks and at delivery for women in high-prevalence communities, women with HIV, and those at increased risk. Where prenatal care is poor, a stat RPR card test and same-visit treatment are recommended when pregnancy is confirmed.

CDC neonatal risk scenario classification
A neonate born to a mother with reactive nontreponemal and treponemal tests is classified into one of four CDC scenarios, from the neonate's examination, a comparison of neonatal and maternal nontreponemal titers by the same test, and the adequacy and timing of maternal treatment. Scenario 1, proven or highly probable: an abnormal examination consistent with congenital syphilis, a neonatal titer at least fourfold the maternal titer, or a positive darkfield, PCR, or silver stain of placenta, cord, lesions, or body fluids. Scenario 2, possible: a normal examination and a titer no more than fourfold the maternal titer, with a mother untreated, inadequately or undocumentedly treated, treated with a nonpenicillin regimen, or treated less than 30 days before delivery. Scenario 3, less likely: as scenario 2 but with appropriate maternal treatment begun at least 30 days before delivery and no evidence of reinfection or relapse. Scenario 4, unlikely: adequate maternal treatment before pregnancy and a low, stable maternal titer throughout. The absence of a fourfold titer difference does not exclude congenital syphilis. Any neonate at risk should be tested for HIV.

1. Disease Information

Overview. Congenital syphilis (CS) is a multisystem infectious disease of the fetus and newborn resulting from transplacental (and occasionally perinatal contact) transmission of Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum from an infected pregnant person. Outcomes span a wide spectrum: spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, hydrops fetalis, prematurity/low birth weight, symptomatic neonatal disease, or an asymptomatic infected infant. A 2023 illustrative review notes that affected newborns show "low birth weight, rash, hepatosplenomegaly, osteolytic bone lesions, pseudoparalysis, central nervous system infection, and long-term disabilities" (Congenital Syphilis—An Illustrative Review, Children 2023, PMID 37628309). More than half of infected infants are asymptomatic at birth (UW National STD Curriculum).

Key identifiers (verified against the NIH GARD record and source ontologies): - MONDO: MONDO:0005714 (NIH GARD) - ICD-10: A50 (parent), with A50.0 early symptomatic, A50.1 early latent, A50.3 late ocular, A50.4 juvenile neurosyphilis, A50.5 other late symptomatic, A50.6 late latent, A50.7 late unspecified, A50.9 unspecified (gesund.bund.de ICD A50) - ICD-9: 090 / 090.9 - MeSH: D013590 ("Syphilis, Congenital") (NLM MeSH) - Orphanet: ORPHA:499009 (GARD) (a second Orphanet code, 232035, appears in one cross-reference but I could not independently verify it) - Disease Ontology: DOID:9856 (MGI/DO) - MedGen: 52622; GARD: 22036 - OMIM: Not applicable (infectious, non-Mendelian disease)

Synonyms / alternative names: mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) of syphilis; vertical transmission of syphilis; prenatal/intrauterine syphilis; syphilis congenita; "lues congenita"; early congenital syphilis (<2 years) and late congenital syphilis (≥2 years) are the two major clinical subdivisions.

2. Etiology

Primary cause (infectious). The disease is caused by the bacterium Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum, a motile, spiral spirochete that cannot be cultured conventionally and has a reduced ~1.14-Mb genome with 1,041 predicted ORFs and few biosynthetic pathways (an obligate host-dependent pathogen) (Fraser et al. 1998, PMID 9665876). Transmission to the fetus is almost always transplacental/hematogenous; direct contact with an infectious genital lesion during delivery is a minor additional route. CHEBI/ontology note: the bacterium itself is the etiologic agent (NCBI Taxonomy Treponema pallidum).

Risk factors (for a pregnant person's infant developing CS): - Maternal disease stage/recency: Risk of fetal infection is 50–70% in early (primary/secondary) maternal syphilis, falling to ~15% when maternal infection was acquired >1 year before pregnancy (review via PMC9045944). - Maternal nontreponemal titer: Each twofold rise in maternal RPR titer increased transmission risk (Suzhou, China 2011–2014, PMC6421965). - Late, scant, or absent prenatal care; late maternal diagnosis (diagnosis after 36 weeks) (Suzhou MMWR, PMC6421965; Chicago study, PMID 35493123). - Substance use and psychiatric illness: In a Chicago hospital cohort, psychiatric illness and noninjection substance use each conferred >5-fold increased odds of an infant with CS, and cases "initiated treatment nearly 3 months later in pregnancy" (PMID 35493123); drug use in pregnancy was associated with 3.32-fold risk of no prenatal care (PMC6421965). - Inadequate/untimely maternal treatment: In CDC's 2022 analysis, timely testing and adequate treatment gaps accounted for the large majority of CS cases (Vital Signs MMWR 2023, PMC10684351). - Missed by behavioral screening: "nearly half of patients did not report any risk factors," so behavior-based screening misses many infections (review PMC9045944).

Genetic host risk factors: Not applicable.

Protective factors. The dominant protective factor is timely maternal penicillin treatment, which reduces CS by ~97%, stillbirth by ~82%, and neonatal mortality by ~80% (review PMC9045944). Early antenatal screening and treatment before ~20–28 weeks' gestation are key. Genetic protective factors: Not applicable.

Gene–environment interactions: Not applicable in the host-genetic sense. The relevant analog is host immune status × pathogen burden/stage (e.g., HIV co-infection alters placental inflammation — see §6/§7).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Host genetics: Not applicable — no causal genes, pathogenic variants, modifier genes, host epigenetics, chromosomal abnormalities, allele frequencies, or inheritance. CS is an acquired infection, not a heritable disorder.

Pathogen genetics (the relevant molecular content): - T. pallidum subsp. pallidum has a compact ~1,138,006-bp circular genome, 1,041 predicted ORFs, limited catabolic/biosynthetic capability, and a family of 12 candidate membrane (Tpr) proteins proposed as virulence factors (Fraser et al. 1998, PMID 9665876). - Virulence/dissemination genes: tp0751 encodes pallilysin, a zinc-dependent metalloprotease and laminin/fibrinogen-degrading adhesin that binds the endothelial 67-kDa laminin receptor (LamR) and is implicated in vascular dissemination and (as a placental adhesin candidate, Tp0954) transplacental crossing (mSphere 2020, PMC7113585). - Antigenic variation: tprK (Tp0897) undergoes nonreciprocal gene conversion across seven variable regions (V1–V7) from silent donor cassettes, driving immune evasion and persistence (Biological basis for syphilis, Clin Microbiol Rev 2006, PMID 16418521).

5. Environmental Information

  • Environmental factors: No toxins/radiation/pollution etiology. The operative "environment" is the in-utero / maternal-infection environment and access-to-care environment (prenatal care availability, screening programs).
  • Lifestyle factors (maternal): Substance use (noninjection and injection drug use), limited prenatal engagement, and untreated maternal sexually transmitted infection are the dominant modifiable factors (PMID 35493123; PMC6421965).
  • Infectious agent: Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum (NCBI Taxonomy; spirochete). HIV co-infection is a frequent and clinically important comorbidity that modifies placental histopathology (fewer inflammatory lesions, complicating diagnosis) (2023 placental review, informahealthcare).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (initiating infection → clinical manifestation)

  1. Maternal infection with T. pallidum establishes spirochetemia → spirochetes circulate in maternal blood, leading to access to the uteroplacental interface (most efficient during early/high-burden maternal disease). (demonstrated — correlates with maternal stage and titer; PMC9045944)
  2. Spirochetes adhere to and degrade the placental/vascular basement membrane — pallilysin (Tp0751) binds laminin and the endothelial 67-kDa laminin receptor and proteolyzes laminin/fibrinogen, resulting in endothelial attachment and intravasation/extravasation across vascular barriers (mSphere 2020, PMC7113585). (mechanistically demonstrated in vitro and in rabbit dissemination models; its exact role at the human placenta is inferred)
  3. Transplacental transmission delivers organisms into the fetal circulation → leads to hematogenous fetal dissemination to liver, bone, skin, mucosa, CNS, and other organs (pathogenesis of maternal-fetal syphilis, PMID 11438902).
  4. Organisms colonize the placenta and umbilical cord, causing chronic villitis, acute chorioamnionitis, villous immaturity (placenta) and necrotizing funisitis with perivascular spirochete invasion of the umbilical arteries (cord) (215-placenta review; umbilical cord study, PMID 7628852). (demonstrated: spirochetes found in 89% of cords)
  5. Fetal endarteritis and perivascular inflammatory infiltrate (plasma cells, lymphocytes) → results in tissue ischemia, fibrosis, and organ injury (the histologic hallmark of treponemal disease across tissues) (PMID 11438902).
  6. Immune evasion sustains chronic infection — the organism's sparse outer-membrane protein content renders it "virtually invisible to the immune system," and TprK antigenic variation selects new variants under immune pressure, leading to persistence and ongoing multi-organ seeding rather than clearance (PMID 16418521).
  7. Branch points by severity/timing: heavy early-pregnancy infection → fetal loss/stillbirth or hydrops fetalis (severe, poor prognosis); moderate infection → symptomatic early CS (hepatosplenomegaly, rash, osteochondritis); low burden/late maternal infection → asymptomatic infected neonate who, if untreated, develops late stigmata (Hutchinson triad, saber shins) over years (UW; Front Pediatr 2023).

  • Molecular pathways / protein dysfunction: Pallilysin (Tp0751) metalloprotease activity degrades extracellular matrix laminin and fibrinogen; LamR receptor engagement mediates endothelial adhesion (PMC7113585).
  • Immune involvement: Obligate chronic inflammation with plasma-cell–rich perivascular infiltrates and endarteritis; immune evasion via outer-membrane protein paucity and TprK variation (PMID 16418521). HIV co-infection dampens placental inflammatory lesions (2023 placental review).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Congenital (infection in utero). Clinical onset: early CS manifests from birth to <2 years (often first 3 months, frequently first weeks); late CS emerges ≥2 years, with stigmata appearing through childhood/adolescence (UW).
  • Onset pattern: May be apparent at birth (acute, even fulminant/hydrops) or insidious/latent in the >50% asymptomatic at birth.
  • Stages/course: fetal (loss/stillbirth/hydrops) → early CS → latency → late CS. Untreated, the disease is chronic and progressive toward irreversible stigmata; treated early, it is largely curable with prevention of late sequelae.
  • Critical window for intervention: Maternal detection and penicillin treatment before ~20–28 weeks' gestation prevents most fetal damage; treatment at any point in pregnancy reduces risk substantially (review PMC9045944).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance / penetrance / consanguinity / carrier frequency: Not applicable (acquired infection, not heritable).
  • Epidemiology:
  • Global (WHO, 2022): ~700,000 estimated CS cases, case rate 523 per 100,000 live births; ~390,000 adverse birth outcomes from maternal syphilis (≈150,000 early fetal deaths/stillbirths; 70,000 neonatal deaths; 55,000 preterm/low-birth-weight; 115,000 infants with clinical CS) (WHO STI strategic information). Untreated maternal syphilis yields adverse outcomes in 50–80% of cases (WHO syphilis fact sheet).
  • United States (CDC, 2023): 3,882 CS cases, national rate 105.8 per 100,000 live births (+3.0% vs 2022), including 279 CS stillbirths and infant deaths — the most cases since 1992 (CDC STI Surveillance 2023). This is a >10-fold rise over a decade (334 cases in 2012 → 3,761 in 2022) (Vital Signs MMWR, PMC10684351). 2024 provisional data show a 12th consecutive annual increase (~4,000 cases) (CDC 2024 provisional).
  • Transmission from untreated maternal syphilis (meta-analysis): 21% excess fetal loss/stillbirth, 9.3% excess neonatal death, 5.8% excess prematurity/LBW; 15% of infants had clinical CS (Gomez et al., Bull WHO 2013, DOI 10.2471/BLT.12.107623).
  • Geographic distribution: Highest burden in low- and middle-income regions (WHO African and other regions) and, within the US, concentrated where prenatal screening/treatment access is limited.
  • Sex ratio: Approximately equal (no strong sex predilection reported).
  • Age distribution: By definition congenital/pediatric; late stigmata manifest through adolescence.

10. Diagnostics

The diagnostic challenge: maternal IgG crosses the placenta, so an infant's nontreponemal reactivity may reflect passive transfer; interpretation relies on comparing infant and maternal titers using the same test/lab (CDC congenital syphilis guidelines).

  • Maternal serology drives suspicion; all infants of reactive mothers get a quantitative nontreponemal test (RPR or VDRL) on infant serum (not cord blood, which can be contaminated). An infant titer ≥4× (two dilutions higher) than maternal (e.g., maternal 1:8, infant ≥1:32) indicates confirmed/highly probable CS (CDC; Merck Manual).
  • Direct detection: darkfield microscopy, direct fluorescent antibody, PCR, or special/silver stains of lesions, body fluids, placenta, or umbilical cord confirm the diagnosis (CDC). Placental immunohistochemistry (sensitivity ~74%) outperforms qPCR (~26%) when serology is negative (2023 placental review).
  • CSF evaluation (VDRL, cell count, protein): reactive CSF-VDRL, or (nontraumatic tap) CSF WBC >15 cells/mm³ or protein >120 mg/dL in the first 30 days, support neurosyphilis (state CDC-derived guidance).
  • Adjunct tests: CBC with platelets, LFTs, long-bone radiographs (metaphyseal lucencies, periostitis), ophthalmologic and auditory evaluation, neuroimaging as indicated.
  • Diagnostic/surveillance criteria: CDC/CSTE surveillance case definition (confirmed vs probable) and the 2021 CDC STI Treatment Guidelines diagnostic framework (Workowski et al., MMWR Recomm Rep 2021, PMC8344968).
  • Genetic/omics/liquid-biopsy host testing: Not applicable.
  • Screening: universal antenatal syphilis screening (at first prenatal visit; many jurisdictions repeat at ~28 weeks and at delivery in high-prevalence areas); rapid dual HIV/syphilis point-of-care tests improve coverage (Cuba case study, PMC5486952). Differential diagnosis: other congenital (TORCH) infections — toxoplasmosis, rubella, CMV, herpes, parvovirus B19, Zika — plus neonatal sepsis, hemolytic disease, and non-accidental injury (for pseudoparalysis/bone changes).

Suggested LOINC/ontology anchors: RPR (LOINC 20507-0-class), VDRL CSF (LOINC 5292-8-class). SNOMED CT: congenital syphilis (disorder).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: Globally, maternal syphilis caused ~150,000 stillbirths/early fetal deaths and ~70,000 neonatal deaths in 2022 (WHO). US 2023: 279 CS stillbirths/infant deaths (CDC 2023). Hydrops fetalis carries a very poor prognosis (Front Pediatr 2023).
  • With treatment: Early penicillin treatment of the infant is curative for active infection and prevents progression to late stigmata; prognosis is excellent when treatment is timely.
  • Morbidity/disability (untreated or late-treated): irreversible sensorineural deafness, corneal scarring/visual loss (interstitial keratitis), dental/facial/skeletal deformity, neurodevelopmental impairment, seizures — producing lifelong disability (PMC2819963).
  • Complications: CNS neurosyphilis, hepatic dysfunction, hematologic cytopenias, nephrotic syndrome, pneumonia alba (severe pulmonary involvement).
  • Prognostic factors: maternal stage/titer, timing of maternal treatment, presence of hydrops, CNS involvement, and birth weight/prematurity. Validated prognostic biomarker panels specific to CS: not available.

12. Treatment

First-line (definitive): Penicillin G — the only recommended agent; no proven alternative for the infant, so penicillin-allergic infants are desensitized (CDC congenital syphilis; Workowski 2021, PMC8344968). Suggested NCIT: Penicillin G (NCIT C61656-class), Benzathine Penicillin G.

Neonatal regimens (CDC 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines): - Confirmed or highly probable CS: aqueous crystalline penicillin G 50,000 units/kg IV per dose — every 12 h for the first 7 days of life, then every 8 h, totaling 100,000–150,000 units/kg/day for 10 days; or procaine penicillin G 50,000 units/kg IM once daily × 10 days (CDC). - Possible CS: same 10-day penicillin course; a single IM benzathine penicillin G 50,000 units/kg dose is an option only when full evaluation is normal and follow-up is assured. - Less likely / CS unlikely: single benzathine penicillin G 50,000 units/kg IM. - Infants/children >1 month: aqueous penicillin G 50,000 units/kg IV every 4–6 h (200,000–300,000 units/kg/day) × 10 days.

Maternal treatment (prevents CS): stage-appropriate benzathine penicillin G during pregnancy; reduces CS by ~97% (review PMC9045944). The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction may occur after treatment.

Supportive care: management of hydrops, cytopenias, hepatic dysfunction; audiologic and ophthalmologic follow-up; developmental services for established late sequelae.

Advanced therapeutics (gene/cell/RNA/immuno/targeted therapy), pharmacogenomics, surgery, experimental trials: Not applicable / not indicated — this is a penicillin-curable bacterial infection; no gene or cell therapies apply.

Treatment strategy / algorithm: The CDC 2021 four-scenario algorithm (confirmed/highly probable, possible, less likely, unlikely) stratifies infant evaluation and treatment based on maternal treatment adequacy and infant exam/titers (CDC). Note the CDC guideline carries an erratum — verify dosing against the current official document.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: prevent/treat maternal syphilis before or early in pregnancy; universal antenatal screening; partner treatment; sexual-health education. Timely maternal penicillin reduces CS ~97% (PMC9045944).
  • Secondary prevention (screening/early detection): routine antenatal serologic screening (first visit; repeat at ~28 weeks and delivery in high-prevalence settings); rapid dual HIV/syphilis point-of-care testing to expand coverage (PMC5486952). CDC's analysis shows most US CS cases stem from missed screening/treatment opportunities, making screening the central lever (Vital Signs MMWR, PMC10684351).
  • Tertiary prevention: complete infant penicillin course and serologic/CSF follow-up to prevent late stigmata; audiology/ophthalmology monitoring.
  • Immunization: No vaccine exists; experimental T. pallidum vaccine candidates (e.g., Tp0751/pallilysin) show partial protection in rabbits but none is clinical (PMC7113585).
  • Public health / program: WHO/PAHO dual (and triple, with hepatitis B) elimination of mother-to-child transmission (EMTCT) validation framework; Cuba was first validated (2015) for HIV+syphilis EMTCT, followed by Thailand, Belarus, Moldova, Caribbean territories, Dominica, and the Maldives (first triple elimination, 2025) (WHO Triple Elimination Initiative).
  • Counseling: reproductive-health and partner-notification counseling (not genetic counseling — not applicable).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy of pathogen: Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum (NCBI Taxonomy). Related treponematoses are caused by T. pallidum subsp. pertenue (yaws) and subsp. endemicum (bejel), and T. carateum (pinta).
  • Natural host: Humans are the only natural host/reservoir for T. pallidum subsp. pallidum; there is no established natural congenital syphilis reservoir in companion animals or wildlife. (Note: nonhuman primates and rabbits can be experimentally infected — see §15.)
  • Veterinary / naturally occurring animal disease: Not applicable for subsp. pallidum. OMIA has no human-equivalent congenital syphilis entry for domestic species.
  • Comparative biology / zoonosis: No zoonotic transmission; human-restricted pathogen. (Treponeme infections exist in nonhuman primates in the wild but are distinct from human venereal/congenital syphilis.)

15. Model Organisms

  • Rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus): the principal experimental model for T. pallidum; the organism is propagated by intratesticular inoculation in New Zealand White rabbits. A rabbit model of congenital syphilis (isolated uterine horn / in-utero exposure) reproduces many features of human CS and was used to study transplacental crossing; prior in-utero treponeme exposure markedly increased subsequent lesion severity (pathogenesis review, PMID 11438902; rabbit placental/congenital studies via PMC search set). Rabbits are also the system for Tp0751/pallilysin dissemination and vaccine studies (PMC7113585).
  • Guinea pig: an established alternative congenital-infection model; a 1992 guinea pig model offered new ways to study asymptomatic infection (pathogenesis review, PMID 11438902).
  • Model type: mammalian in vivo models (rabbit, guinea pig, nonhuman primate); no genetic knockout/transgenic host models are used because disease depends on the pathogen, not host genes. Recently, in vitro continuous culture of T. pallidum in cottontail rabbit epithelial (Sf1Ep) cell co-culture has been achieved, enabling molecular study.
  • Phenotype recapitulation: the rabbit congenital model reproduces placental/fetal lesions resembling human CS; limitation — no model fully captures the human maternal–placental–fetal immunologic environment, and the historical lack of an adequate animal model has hindered study of congenital infection (PMID 11438902).
  • Databases/resources: NCBI Taxonomy (T. pallidum); no MGI/ZFIN/FlyBase host-gene models apply.
