Neurodevelopmental Disorder With Cerebellar Atrophy And Motor Dysfunction

Mendelian MONDO:0859152 Pathograph 41 Show in embeddings browser Hereditary cerebellar ataxia Mendelian neurodevelopmental disorder Spliceosomopathy

Neurodevelopmental disorder with cerebellar atrophy and motor dysfunction (NEDCAM, OMIM:619333) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in GEMIN5, the snRNA-binding component of the survival motor neuron (SMN) complex. Affected individuals present in the first two years of life with motor-predominant developmental delay, central hypotonia and cerebellar ataxia, and brain MRI shows cerebellar atrophy in essentially every reported patient. Cognitive and speech delay, appendicular spasticity with brisk reflexes, tremor, and less often epilepsy (including early-infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with infantile spasms), nystagmus, strabismus and cataract complete the picture. Severity spans a severe infantile form with an SMA-like picture and early death, a late infantile developmental delay and ataxia syndrome, and a juvenile or adult-onset spastic ataxia; most patients have a static course, but some progress. Pathogenic variants reduce GEMIN5 protein levels, lower the levels of other SMN complex proteins, impair snRNP assembly and perturb pre-mRNA splicing in patient iPSC-derived neurons; variants in the dimerization domain also abolish GEMIN5 binding to ribosomes, a second, translational function of the protein whose contribution to disease is not yet established. NEDCAM is distinct from spinal muscular atrophy, which is caused by loss of SMN itself: its transcriptomic signature in patient neurons barely overlaps that of SMA neurons, and cerebellar involvement is its defining feature.

  Neurodevelopmental disorder with cerebellar atrophy and motor dysfunction
  (NEDCAM, OMIM:619333) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic
  loss-of-function variants in GEMIN5, the snRNA-binding component of the
  survival motor neuron (SMN) complex. Affected individuals present in the first
  two years of life with motor-predominant developmental delay, central
  hypotonia and cerebellar ataxia, and brain MRI shows cerebellar atrophy in
  essentially every reported patient. Cognitive and speech delay, appendicular
  spasticity with brisk reflexes, tremor, and less often epilepsy (including
  early-infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with infantile
  spasms), nystagmus, strabismus and cataract complete the picture. Severity
  spans a severe infantile form with an SMA-like picture and early death, a late
  infantile developmental delay and ataxia syndrome, and a juvenile or
  adult-onset spastic ataxia; most patients have a static course, but some
  progress. Pathogenic variants reduce GEMIN5 protein levels, lower the levels
  of other SMN complex proteins, impair snRNP assembly and perturb pre-mRNA
  splicing in patient iPSC-derived neurons; variants in the dimerization domain
  also abolish GEMIN5 binding to ribosomes, a second, translational function of
  the protein whose contribution to disease is not yet established. NEDCAM is
  distinct from spinal muscular atrophy, which is caused by loss of SMN itself:
  its transcriptomic signature in patient neurons barely overlaps that of SMA
  neurons, and cerebellar involvement is its defining feature.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — NEDCAM is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in GEMIN5

The foundational discovery establishing NEDCAM as a Mendelian entity came from Kour et al. (2021), who identified 30 affected individuals from 22 unrelated families carrying biallelic GEMIN5 variants. As the authors state verbatim: "Here, we have identified 30 affected individuals from 22 unrelated families presenting with developmental delay, hypotonia, and cerebellar ataxia harboring biallelic variants in the GEMIN5 gene" (PMID: 33963192). This single study established the gene, the autosomal-recessive inheritance pattern, and the core clinical phenotype.

The disease mechanism is loss of function. The same study demonstrated that "Mutations in GEMIN5 perturb the subcellular distribution, stability, and expression of GEMIN5 protein and its interacting partners in patient iPSC-derived neurons, suggesting a potential loss-of-function mechanism" (PMID: 33963192). GEMIN5 maps to chromosome 5q33.2 (transcript NM_015465), and the disorder is recorded as OMIM #619333.

Finding 2 — GEMIN5 is a multidomain RNA-binding protein with distinct functional modules

GEMIN5's domain architecture is central to understanding how different variants cause disease. The protein comprises three principal functional regions:

Region Domain Function
N-terminus WD40 repeats Recognition and binding of small nuclear (sn)RNAs; initiates snRNP assembly
Middle Extended TPR-like dimerization domain Self-assembles into a canoe-shaped dimer; hub for protein–protein interactions
C-terminus Non-canonical RNA-binding site (RBS1) Translational regulation; binds SMN Tudor domain

Moreno-Morcillo et al. (2020) solved the crystal structure of the dimerization domain: "we solved the crystal structure of an extended tetratricopeptide (TPR)-like domain in human Gemin5 that self-assembles into a previously unknown canoe-shaped dimer" (PMID: 31799608). Reviews describe the N-terminal module's role: "The protein is responsible for the recognition of small nuclear (sn)RNAs through its N-terminal region, and therefore for snRNP assembly" (PMID: 40176294). Beyond snRNP assembly, GEMIN5 independently down-regulates global translation while its C-terminal half promotes translation of its own mRNA and partitions mRNAs into polysomes (PMID: 39819844).

Finding 3 — The phenotypic spectrum spans from NEDCAM to epileptic encephalopathy and late-onset neurodegeneration

While the core NEDCAM phenotype is developmental/cognitive delay, infantile hypotonia, cerebellar ataxia, and cerebellar atrophy/hypoplasia, the spectrum is notably broad. Saida et al. (2021) independently confirmed that "biallelic loss-of-function variants in GEMIN5 cause cerebellar atrophy/hypoplasia" (PMID: 34569062).

Zhang et al. (2024) expanded the spectrum to the severe epileptic end, reporting that "the association between biallelic GEMIN5 variants and early-infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (EIDEEs) has not been reported" until their study, which also explored correlations between epilepsy and the sub-regional location of variants within the gene (PMID: 38773790). At the opposite temporal extreme, an advanced/aged presentation has been documented in a 79-year-old woman with progressive ataxia, spastic paraparesis, contractures, bulbar dysfunction, dementia, and striking frontotemporal atrophy in addition to cerebellar atrophy (PMID: 42800983), indicating a potentially progressive neurodegenerative trajectory in some individuals.

Finding 4 — SMN upregulation is a leading candidate therapeutic strategy

The most actionable mechanistic insight is that SMN acts as a genetic modifier of GEMIN5 disease. Fortuna et al. (2023) reported: "we identified SMN as a genetic suppressor of GEMIN5-mediated neurodegeneration in vivo" (PMID: 37369805). Critically for drug repurposing, they showed that "an increase in SMN expression by either SMN gene therapy replacement or the antisense oligonucleotide (ASO), Nusinersen, significantly upregulated the endogenous levels of GEMIN5" (PMID: 37369805). SMN binds the C-terminus of GEMIN5 via its Tudor domain, and SMN upregulation ameliorated defective snRNP biogenesis and splicing defects in mammalian cells and in mutant GEMIN5-derived iPSC neurons. Because nusinersen and SMN gene replacement (onasemnogene abeparvovec) are already FDA-approved for spinal muscular atrophy, this represents a biologically-grounded repurposing opportunity.

Finding 5 — Model organisms recapitulate GEMIN5 loss

Multiple cross-species models reproduce disease features, strengthening causal inference:

  • Drosophila: Knockdown of rigor mortis, the fly homolog of GEMIN5, reproduces the motor phenotype — "knock down of rigor mortis, the fly homolog of human GEMIN5, leads to developmental defects, motor dysfunction, and a reduced lifespan" (PMID: 33963192).
  • Zebrafish: Models for null variants p.(Arg733Thrfs*6) and p.(Ala1305Leufs*14) "exhibited complete lethality at 2 weeks and recapitulated a distinct dysplastic phenotype" (PMID: 34569062).
  • Human iPSC-derived neurons from patients serve as a patient-specific in vitro model.

A dedicated review summarizes the current animal models of NEDCAM (PMID: 38526274).

Finding 6 — Pathogenic variants reduce protein stability and disrupt dimerization and snRNP assembly

The molecular consequence of pathogenic variants is reduced functional GEMIN5. Saida et al. (2021) demonstrated by Western blot in patient lymphoblastoid cells that "Western blotting analysis using lymphoblastoid cell lines derived from both affected individuals showed significantly reduced levels of GEMIN5 protein" (PMID: 34569062). Francisco-Velilla et al. (2022) extended this, showing functional and structural deficiencies of disease-associated variants, with dimerization-defective variants exhibiting decreased protein levels and reduced ribosome association (PMID: 35393353).

Full Report by Section

1. Disease Information

NEDCAM is a rare autosomal-recessive neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by the triad of developmental delay, cerebellar atrophy, and motor dysfunction. Key identifiers: MONDO:0859152; OMIM #619333; DOID:0070443; MedGen/UMLS C5543427. No dedicated Orphanet, ICD-10/ICD-11, or MeSH entry was confirmed in this investigation (it would fall under broad categories such as ICD-11 LD90 "developmental anomalies" or hereditary ataxia groupings). Synonyms: NEDCAM; GEMIN5 disorder; GEMIN5-related neurodevelopmental disorder. The information is derived from aggregated disease-level resources and published patient cohorts (OMIM, HPO, primary literature) rather than individual EHR data; the phenotype frequencies originate from the pooled discovery cohort of 30 patients.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. NEDCAM is a monogenic Mendelian disorder caused exclusively by biallelic (autosomal-recessive) loss-of-function variants in GEMIN5 (PMID: 33963192). There is no environmental, infectious, or multifactorial contribution to disease causation.

  • Genetic risk factors: The causal variants themselves (see Section 4). No independent susceptibility loci or GWAS signals apply to this monogenic disorder.
  • Modifier genes: SMN1/SMN2 (SMN) is a confirmed modifier — SMN dosage suppresses GEMIN5-mediated neurodegeneration (PMID: 37369805). Sub-regional variant location within GEMIN5 may modify the likelihood of epilepsy (PMID: 38773790).
  • Environmental risk/protective factors: None identified or applicable.
  • Gene–environment interactions: None reported.
  • Consanguinity increases risk of homozygous variants and is a relevant demographic factor in affected families.

3. Phenotypes

The phenotype is dominated by neurological/neurodevelopmental features (see Finding 8 table for frequencies). All are clinical signs/neurodevelopmental manifestations rather than laboratory abnormalities (no consistent biochemical marker exists). Key features:

  • Motor/global developmental delay (HP:0001270, HP:0001263) — ~100%, infantile onset, moderate-to-severe.
  • Cerebellar atrophy/hypoplasia (HP:0001272) — ~100%, an imaging hallmark.
  • Delayed speech/language (HP:0000750) — ~96%.
  • Hypotonia (HP:0001252), often infantile, ~90%.
  • Cerebellar ataxia (HP:0001251) — ~70%.
  • Hyperreflexia (HP:0001348, ~60%) and, less often, areflexia (HP:0001284, ~16%), indicating variable upper/lower motor involvement.
  • Inability to walk (HP:0002540) — ~30%.
  • Seizures/epileptic encephalopathy at the severe end of the spectrum (PMID: 38773790).
  • Advanced/late features: spastic paraparesis, contractures, bulbar dysfunction, dementia, frontotemporal atrophy (PMID: 42800983).

Age of onset: predominantly infantile (70%) to congenital (24%). Severity: variable, moderate to severe. Progression: mostly stable developmental delay, but ~21% show a progressive course, and late presentations suggest neurodegeneration. Quality of life: substantial impact — motor and cognitive disability, speech impairment, and (in a subset) non-ambulation profoundly affect daily functioning and independence.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene: GEMIN5 (HGNC:20043; NCBI 25929; Ensembl ENSG00000082516; UniProt Q8TEQ6; gene OMIM 607005; 5q33.2; transcript NM_015465). Inheritance: autosomal recessive (biallelic; compound heterozygous or homozygous). Variant classes: frameshift, nonsense, missense, splice, and in-frame indels (see Finding 6 table). Functional consequence: loss of function via reduced protein stability/abundance and impaired dimerization, snRNP assembly, and translational regulation (PMID: 34569062; PMID: 35393353). ClinVar: 411 submissions (92 pathogenic, 28 likely pathogenic). Allele frequency: individual pathogenic variants are rare in gnomAD, consistent with a rare recessive disorder. Somatic vs germline: germline. Modifier genes: SMN. Epigenetic/chromosomal abnormalities:* none specifically implicated.

5. Environmental Information

Not applicable. NEDCAM is a purely genetic Mendelian disorder with no documented environmental, lifestyle, toxic, or infectious contributors.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation):

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function variant in GEMIN5 → leads to reduced GEMIN5 protein stability, abundance, and altered subcellular distribution (PMID: 33963192; PMID: 34569062).
  2. Reduced/dysfunctional GEMIN5 → impairs dimerization (TPR domain) and snRNA recognition (WD40 domain) (PMID: 31799608; PMID: 35393353).
  3. Impaired snRNA delivery to the SMN complex → results in defective spliceosomal snRNP biogenesis → causes downstream splicing defects (inferred to perturb neuronal transcriptomes).
  4. Branch A: Loss of GEMIN5 translational-regulatory function → dysregulated (de-repressed) global translation and altered polysome partitioning of mRNAs (PMID: 39819844).
  5. Combined snRNP and translational dysregulation → leads to impaired development/maintenance of neurons, with the cerebellum particularly vulnerable (inferred from the cerebellar-atrophy phenotype).
  6. Cerebellar (and in severe/late cases, cortical/frontotemporal) neurodegeneration/hypoplasia → produces the clinical phenotype: developmental delay, hypotonia, ataxia, motor dysfunction, and — at the severe end — epileptic encephalopathy (PMID: 38773790).

Modifier branch: Increasing SMN → upregulates endogenous GEMIN5 and rescues snRNP/splicing deficits → suppresses neurodegeneration (PMID: 37369805).

8. Temporal Development

Onset: predominantly infantile (70%) and congenital (24%), rarely childhood. Onset pattern: chronic/insidious developmental. Progression: developmental delay is often relatively stable, but ~21% display a progressive course; late-onset/advanced cases show frank progressive neurodegeneration (ataxia, spasticity, dementia, cortical atrophy) (PMID: 42800983). Course: chronic, lifelong. Critical period: early infancy is the key window for developmental and potential therapeutic intervention.

9. Inheritance and Population

Inheritance: autosomal recessive (biallelic), HP:0000007. Penetrance: essentially complete for the core phenotype. Expressivity: highly variable (NEDCAM core → EIDEE → late neurodegeneration). Epidemiology: no formal prevalence/incidence has been established; the disorder is ultra-rare, with ~30+ reported families as of the discovery cohort. Consanguinity contributes homozygous cases. Founder effects/carrier frequency: not specifically established; individual pathogenic alleles are rare in gnomAD. Sex ratio: no strong sex bias reported (recessive). Geographic distribution: worldwide, drawn from 22 unrelated families across multiple populations.

10. Diagnostics

Genetic testing is the diagnostic cornerstone. Diagnosis is established by identifying biallelic pathogenic GEMIN5 variants via whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing (WES/WGS), or multigene neurodevelopmental/ataxia panels that include GEMIN5. Single-gene testing or targeted variant confirmation is appropriate once a variant is identified; segregation/parental testing confirms biallelic (trans) configuration. Imaging: brain MRI is key, revealing cerebellar atrophy/hypoplasia (and frontotemporal atrophy in advanced cases) — a strong diagnostic clue (PMID: 34569062; PMID: 42800983). EEG is indicated when seizures are present (EIDEE subset). Variant interpretation follows ACMG/AMP guidelines with ClinVar/ClinGen reference. Differential diagnosis: other recessive congenital/childhood cerebellar ataxias and hypoplasias (e.g., pontocerebellar hypoplasias, CACNA1A-related disorders, other SMN-complex/snRNP disorders), distinguished by the specific GEMIN5 genotype and the characteristic motor-predominant delay with cerebellar atrophy. No specific biochemical biomarker exists; Western blot showing reduced GEMIN5 is a research-level confirmation (PMID: 34569062).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Prognosis is chronic and lifelong with significant neurodevelopmental disability. Core morbidity includes motor dysfunction, cognitive impairment, speech impairment, and non-ambulation in a subset (~30%). A minority show progressive deterioration; the elderly case demonstrates survival into the 8th decade but with accumulating neurodegenerative burden (PMID: 42800983). The EIDEE subset carries the poorest prognosis owing to severe epilepsy. No formal survival statistics exist. Prognostic factors likely include variant type/location (epilepsy association) and residual GEMIN5 function. Quality of life is substantially reduced; no validated disease-specific QoL instrument has been reported.

12. Treatment

No disease-specific or curative therapy currently exists; management is supportive. Supportive/rehabilitative care includes physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy for motor and communication deficits; anti-seizure medications for the epileptic subset; and nutritional/feeding support for bulbar dysfunction. Leading experimental strategy — SMN upregulation: boosting SMN via SMN gene-replacement therapy or the antisense oligonucleotide nusinersen raises endogenous GEMIN5 and rescues molecular deficits in cells and iPSC neurons (PMID: 37369805); both modalities are already approved for spinal muscular atrophy, making this a plausible repurposing candidate (NCIT suggestion: nusinersen; gene therapy). No pharmacogenomic, immunotherapeutic, or surgical interventions apply. No completed clinical trials specific to NEDCAM are reported.

13. Prevention

Prevention is genetic and reproductive, not population/behavioral. Options include genetic counseling for affected families (25% recurrence risk per pregnancy for carrier couples), carrier/cascade screening of relatives, prenatal diagnosis, and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M) once the familial variants are known. There is no primary/secondary public-health prevention, immunization, or lifestyle modification applicable to this monogenic disorder. Consanguinity counseling is relevant in affected populations.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

No naturally occurring companion-animal or wildlife NEDCAM disease is documented in OMIA. Orthologous/model genes: Drosophila melanogaster rigor mortis (the fly GEMIN5 homolog) and zebrafish gemin5 are functional orthologs used in modeling (see Section 15). GEMIN5/SMN-complex function is evolutionarily conserved across metazoans, underpinning the validity of invertebrate and fish models. No zoonotic or cross-species transmission applies (non-infectious genetic disorder).

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

NEDCAM is best understood as a snRNP-biogenesis and translational-regulation deficiency disorder. A single molecular lesion — reduced functional GEMIN5 — propagates along two convergent branches (defective snRNP assembly and dysregulated translation) to impair the development and maintenance of neurons, with the cerebellum being the most vulnerable structure. The breadth of the phenotypic spectrum (NEDCAM core ↔ EIDEE ↔ late frontotemporal/cerebellar neurodegeneration) is plausibly explained by variant-specific residual function and sub-regional variant location, modulated by SMN dosage. The SMN–GEMIN5 modifier relationship is the single most translationally important finding: it provides both a mechanistic bridge to the well-characterized SMA biology and a ready-made pharmacological toolkit (nusinersen, SMN gene replacement) for therapeutic testing.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Small cohort basis. Essentially all phenotype frequencies derive from a single ~30-patient discovery cohort; broader, independent natural-history data are lacking.
  2. No epidemiological data. Prevalence, incidence, carrier frequency, and founder effects are unquantified.
  3. Genotype–phenotype correlation is preliminary. The variant sub-region/epilepsy correlation needs validation in larger series.
  4. Therapeutic evidence is preclinical. SMN-upregulation rescue has been shown only in cells, iPSC neurons, and model organisms — no human clinical trial data exist.
  5. Mechanistic inference. The specific cerebellar vulnerability and the relative contributions of the snRNP vs translational branches to clinical disease remain partly inferred rather than directly demonstrated.
  6. No validated biomarker or disease-specific QoL instrument exists.
  7. Model limitations. Zebrafish nulls are lethal; no faithful mammalian (mouse) model of the chronic human phenotype was identified in this investigation.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Establish a conditional/hypomorphic mouse model of Gemin5 to recapitulate chronic cerebellar atrophy and enable preclinical therapeutic testing (circumventing zebrafish lethality).
  2. Preclinical trial of nusinersen and SMN gene replacement in GEMIN5-deficient iPSC-neuron and animal models, with quantitative snRNP/splicing and behavioral endpoints — the highest-priority translational step (PMID: 37369805).
  3. Systematic genotype–phenotype study across an expanded international cohort to validate the variant sub-region ↔ epilepsy/severity correlation (PMID: 38773790).
  4. Longitudinal natural-history registry to define progression rates, the frequency of late neurodegeneration, and survival.
  5. Cerebellar-specific transcriptomic/splicing profiling (patient iPSC-derived cerebellar organoids) to directly test which downstream splicing/translation targets drive neuronal loss and to nominate biomarkers.
  6. Structure-guided variant functional assays across the WD40, TPR, and C-terminal domains to build a quantitative map of residual function for ACMG variant classification.
