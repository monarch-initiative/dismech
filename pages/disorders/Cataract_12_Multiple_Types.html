Cataract 12 Multiple Types

Mendelian MONDO:0012701 Pathograph 19 Show in embeddings browser Early-onset non-syndromic cataract

Non-syndromic cataract caused by pathogenic variants in BFSP2, which encodes phakinin (CP49), one of the two lens-specific intermediate filament proteins that co-assemble with BFSP1 (filensin) into the beaded filament of lens fiber cells. Most families show autosomal dominant inheritance of heterozygous missense or in-frame deletion alleles, most of them in the central rod domain; the recurrent in-frame deletion p.Glu233del has been found in several unrelated families in the United States and China. Morphology varies between and within families: congenital nuclear, sutural (often Y-sutural), pulverulent, spokelike cortical, lamellar, posterior polar radial or total cataract, and a juvenile-onset progressive form in which the lens is clear at birth. Myopia co-segregated with Y-sutural cataract in one family. One consanguineous family has a likely null allele causing autosomal recessive diffuse cortical cataract with unaffected heterozygous carriers. Mice lacking CP49, either by targeted deletion or through the natural Bfsp2 deletion carried by 129 and FVB/N strains, form no beaded filaments and lose lens optical quality progressively with age.

Pathophysiology

5
Heterozygous Dominant BFSP2 Variants
Dominant alleles cluster in the alpha-helical central rod domain, which mediates coiled-coil dimerization: R287W, the recurrent in-frame E233del and R339H; G112E lies near the head-rod boundary. E233 and R287 are conserved across species and among intermediate filament proteins, and deleting E233 is predicted to shift the heptad repeat phase. Because a likely null allele causes no cataract in heterozygous carriers, the dominant alleles are thought to act by a mechanism other than haploinsufficiency; no functional study of a human BFSP2 allele was found.

Loss of Fiber Cell Architecture and Long-Range Order
In CP49-null lenses fiber cells differentiate and elongate normally, then lose their shape, membrane organization and long-range stacking as they mature. The lenses are also smaller and less stiff than wild type. The Y-shaped sutural opacity of E233del families is thought to reflect a change in the internal cellular organization of the lens.

Cataract 12, Multiple Types (CTRCT12; MONDO:0012701; OMIM 611597) — Comprehensive Disease Report

Causal gene: BFSP2 (phakinin / CP49; HGNC:1041; NCBI Gene 8419; UniProt Q13515; gene OMIM 603212), encoding a lens-fiber-specific beaded-filament intermediate-filament protein.

Summary

Cataract 12, Multiple Types (CTRCT12) is a Mendelian, non-syndromic, bilateral inherited cataract caused by variants in BFSP2, the gene encoding phakinin (also called CP49). Phakinin is a lens-fiber-specific intermediate-filament (IF) protein that, together with filensin (BFSP1), assembles the beaded filament, a cytoskeletal structure unique to vertebrate lens fiber cells. The "Multiple Types" in the disease name reflects true phenotypic heterogeneity: across reported pedigrees the cataracts range in morphology (nuclear/pulverulent, sutural, lamellar/zonular, cortical) and in age of onset (congenital through juvenile-onset in the second–third decades), and are frequently progressive. The disorder is confined to the eye lens — there is no systemic or extra-ocular involvement.

Most CTRCT12 is autosomal dominant. Two founding dominant alleles were independently reported in 2000 — an in-frame deletion ΔE233 (exon 3) and a missense R287W (exon 4, central rod domain) — and both map to BFSP2 at chromosome 3q21–q22 (now 3q22.1). Additional dominant alleles include G112E (pulverulent + sutural), R339H (congenital lamellar), and recurrent ΔE233 (progressive congenital). A rare homozygous null allele causes a milder autosomal recessive diffuse cortical cataract, and — critically — heterozygous carriers of the null are unaffected. This asymmetry (dominant point mutations cause disease, while loss of one allele does not) is the strongest line of evidence that the dominant mutations act through a dominant-negative / toxic gain-of-function mechanism rather than haploinsufficiency: mutant phakinin is incorporated into and poisons beaded-filament assembly, increasing lens light scatter.

There is no gene-specific or pharmacologic therapy. Management follows general pediatric/inherited cataract practice: surgical lens extraction (lens aspiration ± IOL) for visually significant opacity, combined with optical correction and amblyopia rehabilitation. Because many BFSP2 opacities are mild and slowly progressive, surgery is frequently deferred until the opacity becomes visually significant. With timely treatment the visual prognosis is good. The disease models are mammalian/mouse: targeted CP49 knockouts and a naturally occurring ~6-kb Bfsp2 deletion present in common inbred strains (129, FVB/N). These reproduce beaded-filament loss and degraded lens optical quality, though frank cataract in knockouts is mild — consistent with the human genetics that frank dominant disease requires a poisoning mutant protein, not mere absence.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — BFSP2 is the causal gene; two founding dominant alleles at 3q21–q22 (F001)

CTRCT12 is caused by mutations in BFSP2, encoding phakinin/CP49. The gene was established as the cause by two independent 2000 reports of autosomal dominant cataract mapping to chromosome 3q21–q22:

  • ΔE233 — an in-frame deletion in exon 3, in family ADCC-3 (Jakobs et al. 2000). This was explicitly the first inherited cataract shown to be caused by a mutation in a cytoskeletal protein: "we found that a deletion mutation, DeltaE233, is associated with cataracts in this family. This is the first report of an inherited cataract that is caused by a mutation in a cytoskeletal protein" PMID: 10739768.
  • R287W — a nonconservative missense substitution in exon 4 affecting a conserved arginine in the central rod (coiled-coil) domain, causing a juvenile-onset progressive cataract: "We predicted that this led to a nonconservative R287W substitution in exon 4 that cosegregated with cataracts. This mutation alters an evolutionarily conserved arginine residue in the central rod domain of the intermediate filament" PMID: 10729115.

Subsequently reported dominant alleles broaden the mutational spectrum: G112E PMID: 24654948, R339H (G1091A, exon 5) PMID: 18958306, and recurrent ΔE233 in a progressive Chinese family mapping to 3q22.1 with LOD 3.99 PMID: 17982427. In every pedigree the variant cosegregated with disease and was absent from 100–200 control chromosomes.

Finding 2 — Dominant mechanism is dominant-negative, not haploinsufficiency (F002)

Aldahmesh et al. (2011) identified a novel homozygous, likely null BFSP2 mutation causing autosomal recessive diffuse cortical cataract in a consanguineous family, with no phenotype in heterozygous carriers: "a novel BFSP2 mutation causes autosomal recessive diffuse cortical cataract with scattered lens opacities" and "The likely null nature of both mutations and lack of manifestation in heterozygotes strongly argues for a mechanism other than loss of function in the previously reported dominant mutations in these two genes" PMID: 21836522.

The logic is decisive: because losing one allele is asymptomatic, the heterozygous dominant alleles (ΔE233, R287W, etc.) cannot be causing disease by simply halving protein dose. Instead, mutant phakinin is produced and disrupts normal beaded-filament assembly — a dominant-negative/toxic gain-of-function effect. The structural rationale supports this: the key residues lie in or near the conserved central rod (coiled-coil) domain required for coassembly with filensin, so a mutant subunit can be incorporated into and poison the heteropolymer.

Finding 3 — Beaded filaments are required for lens optical quality (mouse knockouts) (F003)

Two independent CP49/Bfsp2 knockout mouse lines establish the function of the beaded filament:

  • Targeted CP49 deletion (Alizadeh et al. 2002) abolished beaded filaments and sharply reduced filensin protein (filensin mRNA unchanged), producing a progressive, age-worsening loss of optical clarity: "The absence of CP49 causes a subtle loss of optical clarity in the ocular lens, a loss that worsens with age" PMID: 12454043.
  • An independent CP49 knockout (Sandilands et al. 2003) found disrupted beaded filaments with deteriorated light-scatter/optical properties but no frank cataract: "no cataract was observed despite the fact that the beaded filaments of the lens fibre cells had been disrupted. Light scatter and the lens optical properties had, however, deteriorated in the CP49 knockout lenses" PMID: 12573667.

The milder knockout phenotype (optical degradation without frank cataract) reinforces Finding 2: absence of phakinin is comparatively mild, whereas human dominant disease requires a poisoning mutant protein. Beaded filaments also interact with Aquaporin 0 (AQP0), contribute to lens biomechanics, and are required for cold-cataract formation PMID: 34087204; PMID: 32913386. An important experimental confounder is that common inbred strains 129 and FVB/N carry a natural ~6-kb Bfsp2 deletion and lack beaded-filament proteins PMID: 17065509.

Finding 4 — Gene/protein annotation: phakinin/CP49, a tailless lens-specific IF protein at 3q22.1 (F004)

Authoritative identifiers: Gene symbol BFSP2; NCBI Gene 8419; HGNC:1041; gene OMIM 603212 (phenotype CTRCT12 OMIM 611597); Ensembl ENSG00000170819; UniProt Q13515; cytogenetic location 3q22.1 (GRCh38 chr3:133,400,056–133,475,222, + strand); protein-coding. Aliases: CP47, CP49, CTRCT12, LIFL-L, PHAKOSIN.

Phakinin and filensin are two lens-specific IF-like proteins expressed only after fiber-cell differentiation begins; they coassemble into the beaded filament. CP49 is structurally distinctive — completely tailless — while filensin has a short rod domain and a very large C-terminal tail; neither forms filaments alone: "Whilst CP49 is completely tailless, filensin has a rather short rod domain and extremely large C-terminal tail domain. In vitro, CP49 and filensin do not form IFs on their own" PMID: 15777782.

Finding 5 — Epidemiology: a rare cause within pediatric cataract (~2–3/10,000 births), predominantly AD (F005)

A population-based Australian study estimated pediatric cataract incidence at 2.2 per 10,000 births and found autosomal dominant inheritance predominant among familial cases: "421 patients with paediatric cataract were identified, which gives an estimated incidence of 2.2 per 10,000 births" and "The mode of inheritance was autosomal dominant in 30 families, X linked in four, autosomal recessive in two, and uncertain in three" PMID: 12084750. Contemporary cohorts achieve a molecular diagnosis in ~80% of bilateral pediatric cataract, with crystallin genes most common and BFSP2 a minor contributor PMID: 38874774. CTRCT12 has no separate prevalence estimate; it is one of ≥26 inherited cataract genes PMID: 28526010. Reported BFSP2 pedigrees are predominantly Chinese, with European (German ADCC-3), US, and Saudi (recessive) families.

Finding 6 — Treatment is non-gene-specific surgery + rehabilitation (F006)

No pharmacologic or gene-targeted therapy exists; management follows general pediatric cataract guidelines. "lens opacity obscuring the red reflex in preverbal children and visual acuity of less than 20/40 is an absolute indication for lens aspiration" PMID: 31317088. Surgery is recommended early for visually significant opacity (~6 weeks for unilateral, 6–8 weeks for bilateral) to prevent amblyopia; lens aspiration with posterior capsulotomy/anterior vitrectomy is used in children <6 years, with IOL implantation generally considered in patients older than 1 year. The principal under-2-years decision is IOL vs primary aphakia: "Two options currently considered for children under 2 years of age with bilateral congenital cataracts are: (i) intraocular lens (IOL) implantation; or (ii) leaving a child with primary aphakia" PMID: 36107778. Because many BFSP2 opacities are mild/progressive (juvenile-onset), surgery may be deferred until the opacity is visually significant.

Finding 7 — Conservation and model organisms (F007)

BFSP2 is evolutionarily conserved (HomoloGene 20791): mouse Bfsp2 (NCBI 107993, MGI:1333828, chr 9 F1), rat (501046), dog (608938), cow (281645), chicken (396141), zebrafish bfsp2 (494090), Xenopus (100486639), rhesus (717551), chimp (460708). The principal disease models are mouse: targeted CP49 knockouts PMID: 12454043; PMID: 12573667, the natural ~6-kb deletion in strains 129/FVB/N: "FVB/N mice obtained from all four US distributors were shown to harbor a 6-kb deletion of the CP49 gene identical with that previously reported in mouse strain 129" PMID: 17065509 — which "will effectively generate a CP49 knockout" PMID: 15777782 — and the filensin (Bfsp1) knockout, whose lenses also lose CP49 beaded filaments.

Finding 8 — Phenotype spectrum: variable bilateral opacities, congenital to juvenile, often progressive (F008)

Across BFSP2 pedigrees, morphology and onset are heterogeneous — hence "multiple types": embryonal-nuclear pulverulent with sutural opacities (G112E) — "All affected individuals presented pulverulent opacities in the embryonal nucleus and sutures" PMID: 24654948; juvenile-onset progressive with a clear lens at birth developing opacity in the 2nd–3rd decades (R287W) — "Juvenile-onset cataracts are distinguished from congenital cataracts by the initial clarity of the lens at birth and the gradual development of lens opacity in the second and third decades of life" PMID: 10729115; progressive congenital (ΔE233) PMID: 17982427; congenital lamellar (R339H) PMID: 18958306; and recessive diffuse cortical cataract in the null state PMID: 21836522. Opacities are bilateral and non-syndromic.

Primary cause — genetic. Germline pathogenic variants in BFSP2. Genetic risk factors. The causal variant itself is the determinant; CTRCT12 is monogenic with high penetrance. No established common susceptibility loci or modifier genes are specific to CTRCT12.

Environmental risk factors. Not applicable to the Mendelian form. General (age-related/secondary) cataract risk factors — UV exposure, smoking, diabetes, corticosteroids — are not causes of CTRCT12 and should not be conflated with it.

Protective factors. No genetic or environmental protective factors are established for CTRCT12.

Gene–environment interactions. None demonstrated for CTRCT12. (Mouse work shows beaded filaments are required for cold-cataract formation — a temperature×genotype interaction in model systems — but this is not an established human environmental modifier [PMID: 32913386].)

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (dominant form):

  1. A dominant BFSP2 mutation (e.g., ΔE233 or R287W in/near the conserved central rod domain) leads to production of a structurally abnormal phakinin subunit.
  2. The mutant subunit is incorporated into the phakinin–filensin heteropolymer and disrupts normal beaded-filament assembly (dominant-negative) — inferred from the structural rod-domain location plus the genetic evidence that heterozygous null is asymptomatic [PMID: 21836522].
  3. Disrupted/destabilised beaded filaments result in loss of the fiber-cell IF cytoskeleton and secondary loss/destabilisation of filensin (demonstrated in KO: filensin protein falls though mRNA is unchanged) [PMID: 12454043].
  4. Cytoskeletal disorganisation leads to altered fiber-cell architecture, membrane organisation, and perturbed interaction with AQP0, degrading the lens refractive-index gradient and biomechanics [PMID: 34087204].
  5. These changes result in increased intracellular light scatter [PMID: 12573667].
  6. Increased light scatter manifests as a clinically visible lens opacity (cataract), which in several alleles progresses with age [PMID: 12454043; 17982427].

Branch (recessive null): Homozygous loss of phakinin leads to absence of beaded filaments and a milder, diffuse cortical opacity; heterozygotes are unaffected [PMID: 21836522].

  • Upstream: mutation → mutant protein → filament poisoning. Downstream: light scatter → opacity → visual impairment.
  • Cell types / processes involved: lens fiber cells (CL:0000148, lens fiber cell); intermediate filament cytoskeleton organization (GO:0045104); beaded filament assembly; lens development in camera-type eye (GO:0002088); visual perception (GO:0007601).
  • Subcellular compartment: cytoplasmic intermediate filament / cytoskeleton (GO:0005882, intermediate filament; GO:0045111, intermediate filament cytoskeleton); plasma-membrane–associated organisation.
  • Molecular profiling: no transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic signature specific to human CTRCT12; mouse KO shows selective loss of filensin protein with preserved mRNA [PMID: 12454043].

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ: eye lens (UBERON:0000965). No secondary organ involvement; body system = visual/sensory.
  • Tissue/cell: lens fiber cells (CL:0000148); the opacity is intrinsic to differentiated fiber cells where beaded filaments are expressed.
  • Subcellular: intermediate filament cytoskeleton (GO:0045111); fiber-cell plasma membrane region.
  • Localization: bilateral; opacity site varies by allele (nuclear/embryonal nucleus, sutures, lamellar/zonular layer, cortex).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: congenital in many dominant alleles (ΔE233, R339H, G112E); juvenile-onset (clear lens at birth, opacity developing in the 2nd–3rd decades) for R287W [PMID: 10729115].
  • Onset pattern: insidious/chronic.
  • Progression: frequently progressive and age-worsening (ΔE233 [PMID: 17982427]; KO optical clarity worsens with age [PMID: 12454043]); some stable.
  • Course: chronic, lifelong; opacity does not spontaneously remit — "remission" is treatment-induced (surgical removal). Critical period: early childhood is the critical window for preventing deprivation amblyopia in congenital/early cases.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology: no CTRCT12-specific prevalence; nested within pediatric cataract incidence of ~2.2 per 10,000 births [PMID: 12084750]; BFSP2 is a minor contributor relative to crystallin genes [PMID: 38874774].
  • Inheritance: predominantly autosomal dominant (HP:0000006); a rare autosomal recessive null form (HP:0000007) [PMID: 21836522].
  • Penetrance: high/complete within reported pedigrees; expressivity variable (morphology and onset differ even for the same allele across families).
  • Anticipation / germline mosaicism: not reported.
  • Founder effects / consanguinity: recessive null reported in a consanguineous family [PMID: 21836522]; many dominant pedigrees are Chinese, but no established founder allele.
  • Carrier frequency: low; private alleles.
  • Demographics: reported families predominantly Chinese, plus European, US, and Saudi. Sex ratio ~1:1 (autosomal). Age distribution: presents in infancy/childhood through early adulthood.

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical examination: slit-lamp biomicroscopy is the primary diagnostic (documents morphology, laterality, progression); red-reflex testing for screening in preverbal children [PMID: 31317088]. Visual acuity assessment.
  • Biomarkers / labs / imaging: no biochemical biomarker; imaging not required for diagnosis (B-scan ultrasound if fundus view obscured).
  • Genetic testing: confirmatory. Recommended approach — gene panels for congenital/inherited cataract (including crystallins, connexins, BFSP1/BFSP2, MIP, MAF, HSF4, PITX3) or whole-exome sequencing, achieving ~80% diagnostic yield [PMID: 38874774]. Single-gene BFSP2 testing is appropriate when phenotype/pedigree points to it. WGS, CMA, karyotyping, FISH, mtDNA, and repeat-expansion testing are generally not indicated for isolated CTRCT12.
  • Omics-based diagnostics: not used clinically.
  • Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis: diagnosis by bilateral lens opacity + confirmatory BFSP2 variant. Differentials: other inherited cataract genes (crystallins CRYAA/CRYAB/CRYB/CRYG, connexins GJA3/GJA8, MIP, MAF, HSF4), syndromic cataracts, and metabolic/secondary cataract.
  • Screening: cascade testing of at-risk relatives once the familial variant is known; red-reflex screening in neonates/infants.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival/mortality: CTRCT12 is not life-limiting; no excess mortality. Normal life expectancy.
  • Morbidity/function: the principal morbidity is visual — reduced acuity and, if untreated in the critical period, deprivation amblyopia. With timely surgery and optical/amblyopia management, visual prognosis is generally good.
  • Complications: largely treatment-related — visual axis opacification, glaucoma, retinal detachment, and amblyopia are the main concerns in pediatric cataract surgery [PMID: 16855998; 36107778].
  • Recovery potential: excellent functional recovery with surgery for visually significant opacity; mild/stable opacities may never require intervention.
  • Prognostic factors: opacity density/location, laterality, age at detection and treatment (earlier treatment → lower amblyopia risk). No molecular prognostic biomarker.

12. Treatment

  • Pharmacotherapy: none disease-specific; no gene-targeted drug.
  • Advanced therapeutics (gene/cell/RNA): none in clinical use for CTRCT12; purely theoretical given dominant-negative mechanism (allele-silencing approaches would be conceptually rational but are not developed).
  • Surgical/interventional (standard of care): lens aspiration/extraction for visually significant opacity. "lens opacity obscuring the red reflex in preverbal children and visual acuity of less than 20/40 is an absolute indication for lens aspiration" [PMID: 31317088]. Technique in young children: lens aspiration with posterior capsulotomy/anterior vitrectomy; IOL implantation generally considered after age 1. NCIT suggestions: NCIT:C157672 (Cataract Surgery), NCIT:C15277 (Surgery), NCIT:C62314 (Intraocular Lens).
  • Optical/supportive & rehabilitative: aphakic correction (contact lens/aphakic glasses) vs IOL in under-2s [PMID: 36107778]; amblyopia therapy (patching), refractive correction.
  • Experimental: no CTRCT12-specific trials.
  • Treatment strategy: timing-driven (early surgery for dense bilateral/unilateral congenital opacity to prevent amblyopia; defer for mild/progressive juvenile opacity until visually significant). Personalized element = genotype confirms diagnosis and informs family planning, not drug choice.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not possible for a germline Mendelian disorder; genetic counseling and reproductive options (prenatal testing, preimplantation genetic diagnosis) can prevent transmission once the familial variant is known.
  • Secondary prevention: neonatal/infant red-reflex screening for early detection; prompt referral.
  • Tertiary prevention: timely surgery + amblyopia management to prevent irreversible visual deprivation; post-operative monitoring for glaucoma and visual-axis opacification.
  • Immunization / public health / environmental / prophylaxis: not applicable.
  • Counseling: autosomal dominant → 50% transmission risk; cascade testing of relatives.

15. Model Organisms

  • Model type: mammalian (mouse) is primary.
  • Specific systems & genetic models:
  • Targeted CP49/Bfsp2 knockout (Alizadeh 2002) — beaded filaments abolished, filensin protein reduced, progressive optical-clarity loss [PMID: 12454043].
  • Independent CP49 knockout (Sandilands 2003) — disrupted beaded filaments, deteriorated light scatter, no frank cataract [PMID: 12573667].
  • Natural ~6-kb Bfsp2 deletion in inbred strains 129 and FVB/N — effectively a CP49 knockout and an important confounder in lens studies [PMID: 17065509; 15777782].
  • Filensin (Bfsp1) knockout — also loses CP49 beaded filaments; shows cold-cataract and AQP0-related distortion changes [PMID: 32913386; 34087204].
  • Phenotype recapitulation: models reproduce beaded-filament loss and degraded lens optical quality, capturing the cytoskeletal mechanism. Limitation: knockouts are loss-of-function and produce a milder phenotype than the human dominant-negative disease, so they model the recessive/null end better than the dominant alleles; no knock-in of a human dominant allele (e.g., R287W, ΔE233) was identified in this investigation.
  • Resources: MGI (Bfsp2 MGI:1333828), IMPC/IMSR for mouse lines.

The central interpretive insight unifying the genetics and the mouse models is the dominant-negative hypothesis. Human heterozygous null carriers are unaffected and homozygous null causes only a mild recessive cortical cataract [PMID: 21836522]; mouse CP49 knockouts likewise show degraded optics but mild/absent frank cataract [PMID: 12573667]. Yet heterozygous point mutations/in-frame deletions cause dominant, sometimes congenital, cataract. The only coherent reconciliation is that the dominant mutant protein is expressed, enters the beaded-filament heteropolymer, and actively poisons its assembly — a toxic gain-of-function that is more damaging than simple absence. The rod-domain localisation of R287W (and proximity of ΔE233) to the coiled-coil assembly interface provides the structural rationale.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No direct biochemical proof of dominant-negative assembly in human disease. The mechanism is strongly inferred from genetics (het null asymptomatic) plus structural reasoning, but in vitro coassembly assays of each dominant mutant with filensin were not reviewed here.
  2. No knock-in animal model of a human dominant allele. Available mouse models are loss-of-function (knockout/natural deletion), which model the recessive/null end better than the dominant disease — a gap for therapeutic and mechanistic studies.
  3. No CTRCT12-specific epidemiology. Prevalence/incidence are only inferable from the broader pediatric cataract literature; BFSP2 is a minor contributor.
  4. Genotype–phenotype correlation is incomplete. Why specific alleles produce nuclear vs lamellar vs cortical morphology is not mechanistically resolved; expressivity is variable even within a reported allele.
  5. Population/ethnic distribution is ascertainment-biased toward Chinese pedigrees; true global distribution and any founder effects are unknown.
  6. No omics signature (transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic) exists for human CTRCT12.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. In vitro coassembly assays of each dominant mutant phakinin (ΔE233, R287W, G112E, R339H) with wild-type filensin to directly test and quantify the dominant-negative effect on filament formation.
  2. Knock-in mouse (or lens organoid/iPSC) models of a human dominant allele (e.g., R287W) to recapitulate the dominant phenotype and provide a therapeutic test bed.
  3. Structural modeling/cryo-EM of mutant-containing beaded filaments to map how rod-domain substitutions disrupt the coiled-coil assembly interface.
  4. Allele-selective silencing proof-of-concept (ASO/siRNA/CRISPR) targeting the mutant allele in patient-derived cells, exploiting the benign heterozygous-null state.
  5. Genotype–phenotype registry aggregating all BFSP2 families to correlate allele with morphology, onset, and progression, and to estimate penetrance/expressivity rigorously.
  6. Targeted gnomAD/population screening of BFSP2 to estimate carrier frequency of null and dominant alleles and probe founder effects.
