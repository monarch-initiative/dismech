Gonococcal Arthritis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0041903 Pathograph 27 Show in embeddings browser Bacterial Infection

Gonococcal arthritis is the articular manifestation of disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI), the bacteremic spread of Neisseria gonorrhoeae from a usually asymptomatic mucosal focus. It presents as one of two overlapping syndromes: an arthritis-dermatitis syndrome - the classic triad of migratory polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis, and pustular dermatitis, often with sterile synovial fluid - or a purulent mono- or oligoarticular septic arthritis with a positive synovial fluid. Dissemination is driven by serum-resistant gonococcal strains, historically the PorB1A (Por1A) porin serovar, which bind the host complement regulator factor H and evade complement killing, and is favoured by host terminal complement deficiency, C5-inhibitor (eculizumab) therapy, female sex, menstruation, and pregnancy. First-line treatment is intravenous or intramuscular ceftriaxone with joint drainage for purulent effusions; residual articular impairment is common despite therapy.

Mucosal Gonococcal Colonization
Neisseria gonorrhoeae colonizes a urogenital, pharyngeal, or rectal mucosal surface, often without local symptoms, establishing the reservoir from which bloodstream invasion occurs. Many disseminating strains cause minimal genital inflammation, so DGI is frequently diagnosed without a recognized preceding genital infection.

Gonococcal Serum Resistance and Complement Evasion
Disseminating gonococci, historically of the Por1A (PorB1A) porin serovar, bind the host alternative-pathway complement regulator factor H at a surface-exposed region of the porin, markedly reducing complement deposition and membrane-attack-complex killing. This stable serum resistance, independent of lipooligosaccharide sialylation, is the precondition for survival in blood.

Terminal Complement Pathway Deficiency and MAC Failure
Congenital deficiency of a terminal complement component (C5-C9), or pharmacologic blockade of C5 by eculizumab, removes the membrane attack complex that is the principal bactericidal defence against Neisseria. A host lacking MAC can be disseminated even by strains that are not strongly serum-resistant, which is why terminal complement deficiency and C5 inhibitors are recognized DGI risk factors. Terminal complement inhibitor therapy (eculizumab)
Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks C5 and prevents membrane attack complex formation, is an iatrogenic phenocopy of terminal complement deficiency and markedly raises the risk of disseminated, often blood-culture positive, gonococcal infection. Patients on eculizumab should be counselled about this risk and screened for sexually transmitted infections; if antibiotic prophylaxis is used, gonococcal resistance trends should guide the choice.

1. Disease Information

Overview. Gonococcal arthritis is the joint manifestation of disseminated gonococcal infection — bacteremic spread of N. gonorrhoeae from a usually asymptomatic mucosal (urogenital, pharyngeal, or rectal) site. DGI classically presents as one of two overlapping syndromes: (1) an arthritis–dermatitis syndrome (the triad of tenosynovitis, dermatitis/skin lesions, and migratory polyarthralgia, typically with negative joint-fluid cultures but occasionally positive blood cultures), and (2) a localized purulent/septic arthritis (usually mono- or oligoarticular, with positive synovial-fluid cultures and usually negative blood cultures) (StatPearls, NBK470439; PMID 16297736). The two are often described as a continuum rather than discrete entities.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — infectious. The obligate cause is infection with Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NCBI Taxonomy ID 485). DGI follows hematogenous dissemination from a mucosal focus, which is frequently asymptomatic — a key reason DGI is often diagnosed without a recognized preceding genital infection (PMC2213355; StatPearls).

Bacterial (pathogen-side) determinants. Dissemination is strongly associated with gonococcal strains showing stable serum (complement) resistance, historically typed as Por1A (PorB1A) porin serovars, which are also often penicillin-sensitive, AHU auxotrophs (PMID 11157049; PMC2213355). "Disseminated gonococcal infection often occurs without local genital inflammation, and it is mostly caused by strains that resist normal human serum without relying on sialylation... Por1A strains are the ones most often linked to disseminated disease" (PMID 11157049).

Host risk factors — genetic: - Terminal complement component deficiencies (C5, C6, C7, C8, and C9; and properdin for the alternative pathway). These impair formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), which is the principal defense against Neisseria, and markedly raise risk of disseminated/recurrent neisserial disease (Terminal complement pathway deficiency overview; PMC8793329). Candidate susceptibility genes: C5, C6, C7, C8A/C8B/C8G, C9, CFP (properdin, X-linked). - Deficiencies "raise susceptibility mainly to Neisseria species, while heterozygous carriers are generally not affected" (PMC4869260).

Host risk factors — environmental / demographic: - Female sex and menstruation/pregnancy/postpartum (hormonal and complement changes increase dissemination risk) (CID 2019 case series, PMID 30418536). - Complement-inhibitor therapy: eculizumab (and by mechanism, other terminal C5 inhibitors) — an iatrogenic phenocopy of terminal complement deficiency (see §5, §12). - Multiple sex partners / high community gonorrhea prevalence; injection drug and methamphetamine use and housing instability in recent US clusters (MMWR Alaska 2025; MMWR Michigan 2019, PMID 32214085); untreated mucosal gonorrhea.

Protective factors. Intact terminal complement pathway and prompt treatment of mucosal gonorrhea. No validated protective genetic variant or dietary factor is established for DGI; I found no specific literature, so this is reported as not established.

Gene–environment interactions. The clearest interaction is between host MAC capability and bacterial serum resistance: dissemination requires either a serum-resistant strain (bacterial genotype) or a host with impaired MAC (complement-deficient or on a C5 inhibitor). Terminal C5 blockade converts even relatively serum-sensitive strains into disseminators, as shown by the eculizumab case series (PMID 30418536).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Human causal genes: none. Gonococcal arthritis is infectious; there is no human pathogenic variant that causes it.

Host susceptibility genes (not causal): Terminal complement components — C5, C6, C7, C8A, C8B, C8G, C9, and CFP (properdin, X-linked). Loss-of-function variants cause MAC deficiency and predispose to disseminated neisserial infection; carriers (heterozygotes) are generally unaffected (PMC4869260). Inheritance of these deficiencies is mostly autosomal recessive (properdin X-linked). These are susceptibility loci, not DGI-causing mutations; full ClinVar/gnomAD allele-frequency annotation would be at the complement-deficiency disease level, not under MONDO:0041903.

Bacterial virulence genetics (the operative "molecular" story): - porB allele porB1A (Por1A) — porin that binds host complement regulator factor H at surface loop 5, conferring stable serum resistance and dissemination (JCI/J Immunol references, PMID 18292569). "Factor H binding was localized to surface loop 5 of Por1A, and peptides from that loop inhibited binding" (insight.jci.org references). - LOS sialylation (via lst sialyltransferase using host CMP-NANA) — a separate, unstable route to serum resistance that recruits factor H; many stable disseminating strains lack the LOS acceptor and do not rely on it (PMC3536161; PMC2213355). - NspA contributes to serum resistance when LOS is truncated; lipid A phosphoethanolamine modulates C4BP/factor H binding (PMC6346132; PMC3536161).

Epigenetics / chromosomal abnormalities: Not applicable to this infectious disease (no human epigenetic or cytogenetic lesion). Bacterial antigenic/phase variation (pilin, Opa) is a gonococcal genome feature but not a human chromosomal change.

5. Environmental Information

Infectious agent. Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NCBI Taxon 485), a Gram-negative, oxidase-positive diplococcus; humans are the only natural host. Transmission is sexual (urogenital, anorectal, pharyngeal) and vertical (perinatal) — DGI arises when an organism from such a site enters the bloodstream.

Environmental/iatrogenic factors. Terminal complement inhibitors (eculizumab; mechanistically ravulizumab and other anti-C5 agents) are a documented environmental risk: the FDA safety-report series found 9 gonorrhea cases on eculizumab, 8/9 (89%) disseminated, 8 hospitalized, 7 with positive blood cultures (PMID 30418536). No DGI-specific ravulizumab case counts were retrieved.

Lifestyle factors. High-risk sexual behavior and untreated mucosal gonorrhea; methamphetamine/injection drug use and housing instability featured in recent US outbreak clusters (MMWR Alaska 2025; MMWR Michigan 2019). No chemical-toxin (CTD-type) etiology applies.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating event → clinical manifestation):

  1. N. gonorrhoeae colonizes a mucosal surface (urogenital/pharyngeal/rectal), often asymptomatically → establishes a reservoir for invasion. (demonstrated)
  2. A strain with stable serum resistance (Por1A phenotype) binds host factor H via PorB loop 5 and/or sialylates LOS → evades alternative-pathway complement and MAC killing in blood. This leads to survival in serum (PMID 18292569; PMC2213355). (demonstrated in vitro)
  3. Serum-resistant organisms enter the bloodstream → bacteremia (gonococcemia). Branch point: host MAC deficiency or C5-inhibitor therapy permits dissemination even by less serum-resistant strains (PMID 30418536). (inferred from epidemiology + mechanism)
  4. Circulating gonococci and their products (LOS/endotoxin, peptidoglycan) deposit in synovium, tenosynovium, and dermal vessels and trigger innate immune activation (complement C5a, TLR4/LOS signaling, cytokine release) → local inflammation. (partly inferred)
  5. Branch A — arthritis–dermatitis syndrome: immune-complex/complement-mediated and embolic small-vessel injury results in migratory polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis, and pustular/hemorrhagic skin lesions; synovial fluid is often sterile while blood cultures may be positive. (mechanism partly inferred; phenotype demonstrated)
  6. Branch B — purulent/septic arthritis: viable organisms seed and proliferate within a joint → neutrophilic purulent arthritis with positive synovial-fluid culture; blood cultures usually negative. (demonstrated)
  7. Sustained intra-articular infection/inflammation → cartilage and joint damage; rare hematogenous seeding of endocardium or meninges → endocarditis/meningitis. (demonstrated clinically)

Molecular/cellular detail: Key pathways are complement (alternative and terminal/MAC) regulation and evasion (factor H, C4BP recruitment); innate sensing of LOS via TLR4/MD-2; neutrophil recruitment and degranulation driving purulent arthritis. Suggested GO terms: complement activation, alternative pathway (GO:0006957); regulation of complement activation (GO:0030449); inflammatory response (GO:0006954); neutrophil chemotaxis (GO:0030593). Suggested CL terms: neutrophil (CL:0000775), synovial fibroblast (CL:0000210), macrophage (CL:0000235), endothelial cell (CL:0000115). Suggested CHEBI: N-acetylneuraminic acid/sialic acid (CHEBI:28853) for LOS sialylation.

Omics/single-cell/CRISPR: No DGI-specific transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, single-cell, or functional-genomics screens were retrieved — reported as not available at the disease level.

  • Primary organs/systems: Joints (synovium) and tendon sheaths — musculoskeletal system; skin (dermis/epidermis); bloodstream (bacteremia). UBERON suggestions: synovial joint (UBERON:0002217), synovial membrane (UBERON:0002018), tendon sheath (UBERON:0007844), skin (UBERON:0002097), blood (UBERON:0000178).
  • Secondary/complication organs: heart valves/endocardium (UBERON:0002165) in endocarditis; meninges (UBERON:0002360) in meningitis; liver capsule/peritoneum (perihepatitis).
  • Joint distribution: typically knees, wrists, ankles, and small hand joints; often asymmetric, migratory (polyarthralgia) or mono-/oligoarticular (purulent form); lateralization is variable (HP: often asymmetric).
  • Tissue/cell level: synovial lining cells, dermal vascular endothelium, infiltrating neutrophils (CL:0000775) and macrophages (CL:0000235).
  • Subcellular: gonococci are typically extracellular/intraphagosomal; no human organelle lesion. GO cellular component of note: external side of plasma membrane / cell surface (complement regulator binding).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Adult-onset, acute to subacute, in young sexually active people (not congenital/pediatric except perinatal gonorrhea, which is a different presentation). Symptoms in the eculizumab setting appeared ~days after exposure/triggering (one report ~8 days) (PMC8216817).
  • Progression/course: Usually monophasic and self-limited to treatment-responsive; the arthritis–dermatitis phase can evolve into localized purulent arthritis. With appropriate antibiotics, systemic features resolve quickly; untreated or delayed purulent arthritis can progress to joint destruction.
  • Duration: Acute illness over days–weeks; ~60% have residual/persistent articular symptoms at discharge in one series (PMID 35590115). It is not a chronic lifelong disease once treated, though recurrent DGI occurs in complement-deficient or complement-blocked hosts.
  • Critical window: Early antibiotic therapy and prompt joint drainage are the intervention window preventing permanent joint damage (AFP/CCJM review).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology: DGI historically develops in ~0.5–3% of gonococcal infections (AFP 1992 review). It is a complication of gonorrhea, which is extremely common (hundreds of thousands to millions of US infections annually), so DGI incidence tracks gonorrhea incidence and local serovar distribution. No stable population prevalence per 100,000 is well defined; it is uncommon but rising in recent US clusters.
  • Recent surveillance signal: Alaska reported 35 DGI cases in 2023–2024 vs. 3 in 2022 (median age 36 years, IQR 15; ~49–51% male) (MMWR 75(27) 2025); Southwest Michigan reported a 2019 cluster of 13 confirmed + 3 probable cases, several in people experiencing homelessness (MMWR 69(12) 2020, PMID 32214085).
  • Inheritance: Not a heritable disease. Relevant host susceptibility (terminal complement deficiency) is mostly autosomal recessive (properdin X-linked); penetrance for neisserial disease is incomplete; carrier frequency and founder effects pertain to the complement-deficiency disorders, not to gonococcal arthritis itself.
  • Sex ratio: Classically female-predominant (menstruation, pregnancy, and asymptomatic carriage favoring dissemination), though recent clusters show near-equal or male-leaning sex distribution (MMWR Alaska).
  • Age: Predominantly young to middle-aged sexually active adults.

10. Diagnostics

  • Cultures / microbiology:
  • Blood cultures positive in <~1/3 of cases, more often in the arthritis–dermatitis form; reported positivity ranges ~4–70% across series (StatPearls).
  • Synovial fluid culture/NAAT: more often positive in the purulent arthritis form; synovial fluid is typically inflammatory (elevated WBC, neutrophil-predominant).
  • Mucosal screening (urogenital, pharyngeal, rectal) NAAT is essential because the primary site is often asymptomatic and more frequently positive than blood/joint (CDC STI app, DGI page).
  • Skin lesion Gram stain/culture may show organisms.
  • Lab markers: elevated inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR), leukocytosis — nonspecific. LOINC-type gonococcal NAAT and culture results apply.
  • Imaging: joint ultrasound/MRI/radiography to assess effusion, tenosynovitis, and joint damage (supportive, nonspecific).
  • Host workup: In recurrent or unexplained disseminated neisserial infection, test complement function (CH50/AH50, then individual terminal components) — low CH50 with normal C3/C4 suggests terminal pathway deficiency (Terminal complement pathway deficiency overview); review medication list for C5 inhibitors.
  • Diagnostic criteria / differential: Clinical diagnosis supported by compatible syndrome + positive gonococcal testing at any site. Differentials: other septic (staph/strep) arthritis, reactive arthritis, crystal arthropathy (gout/pseudogout), Lyme and other bacterial arthritides, meningococcemia (disseminated N. meningitidis), and rheumatologic disease. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing on any cultured isolate is important given resistance trends.
  • Genetic/omics testing: Not used to diagnose the arthritis itself. Host complement-gene sequencing is reserved for suspected inherited terminal-complement deficiency. Omics-based diagnostics: not applicable.
  • Screening: Screen and treat mucosal gonorrhea; screen sexual partners; consider complement evaluation after an unexplained or recurrent episode.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Generally favorable with prompt treatment. Systemic illness and arthritis–dermatitis features usually resolve with antibiotics; mortality is low in uncomplicated DGI.
  • Morbidity: Persistent articular symptoms in ~60% at discharge in one series (PMID 35590115); untreated/delayed purulent arthritis can cause permanent joint damage (CCJM/AFP review).
  • Severe complications (high morbidity/mortality): endocarditis and meningitis are rare but historically carry substantial risk; recent Alaska surveillance documented severe outcomes including gonococcal endocarditis and at least one fatal case (MMWR Alaska 2025; Alaska endocarditis abstract).
  • Prognostic factors: promptness of therapy, presence of purulent arthritis vs. arthritis–dermatitis, endocardial/CNS involvement, antimicrobial resistance of the isolate, and host complement status/immunosuppression.

12. Treatment

  • First-line antibiotic (DGI/arthritis): Ceftriaxone 1 g IM/IV every 24 hours (CDC 2021 STI guidelines list ceftriaxone 1 g q24h, with a 1–2 g IV q24h option for more severe disease such as endocarditis/meningitis), continued for ≥7 days (longer for endocarditis/meningitis), with transition guided by susceptibility and clinical response (CDC STI app DGI page; CDC gonorrhea treatment guidelines). Add doxycycline 100 mg BID ×7 days if chlamydia not excluded. NCIT suggestions: Ceftriaxone (NCIT:C47704), Doxycycline (NCIT:C736).
  • Note on dosing: DGI uses a higher/longer ceftriaxone regimen than uncomplicated gonorrhea (which is a single 500 mg IM dose per the 2020 update) (MMWR mm6950a6, 2020). Do not conflate the two.
  • Source control: drainage/arthrocentesis of purulent joints (repeat aspiration or arthroscopic lavage) as needed. NCIT: Joint Aspiration/Arthrocentesis.
  • Resistance considerations: empiric ceftriaxone reflects current gonococcal resistance to penicillins, tetracyclines, and fluoroquinolones; susceptibility testing of isolates is advised.
  • Host-directed / special populations: In patients on eculizumab or other terminal C5 inhibitors, clinicians are advised to counsel about gonococcal risk and consider antimicrobial prophylaxis per local resistance patterns, in addition to meningococcal vaccination (PMID 30418536; Risk of DGI with terminal complement blockade, PMID 33560079).
  • Pharmacogenomics / gene / cell / RNA / targeted / immunotherapy: Not applicable to this bacterial infection (beta-lactam allergy history guides alternatives). No DGI-specific experimental trial agents were retrieved.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: prevent and promptly treat mucosal gonorrhea — condom use, partner reduction, routine STI screening of at-risk populations; expedited partner therapy / partner notification to interrupt transmission (CDC STI guidelines).
  • Secondary: screening asymptomatic high-risk individuals (and screening/treating partners) to prevent progression to DGI; outbreak investigation and partner-network tracing (as done in Alaska/Michigan clusters) (MMWR Michigan; MMWR Alaska).
  • Tertiary: timely antibiotics + joint drainage to prevent joint destruction, endocarditis, and meningitis.
  • Special-population prophylaxis: For patients starting complement C5 inhibitors, counsel on gonococcal risk and consider antibacterial prophylaxis per local resistance data (PMID 30418536).
  • Immunization: No licensed gonococcus vaccine; some evidence that serogroup B meningococcal (4CMenB/OMV) vaccines may offer partial cross-protection against gonorrhea is under study, but this is not an established DGI-prevention measure (not confirmed from retrieved sources here). Meningococcal vaccination is separately required for complement-inhibitor recipients.
  • Counseling: genetic counseling applies only to inherited terminal-complement deficiency, not to the infection itself.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

N. gonorrhoeae is a human-restricted pathogen (NCBI Taxon 485); there is no natural animal reservoir or naturally occurring gonococcal arthritis in other species. Host restriction arises partly from the organism's specificity for human factors — e.g., human factor H binds gonococci species-specifically, and human CEACAMs/transferrin are required (PMID 18292569). There is no OMIA veterinary counterpart, no zoonotic transmission, and no cross-species susceptibility of clinical relevance.

15. Model Organisms

  • Primary model: the 17β-estradiol-treated female mouse model of N. gonorrhoeae genital tract infection — "the only small-animal model of gonococcal genital tract infection," requiring estradiol (and antibiotics to suppress competing flora) to maintain an estrus-like, colonizable state (Front Microbiol 2011 review; PMC3129519).
  • Humanized models: transgenic mice expressing human CEACAMs (supporting Opa-mediated attachment) and supplementation/transgenesis with human factor H / complement factors to overcome host restriction (Front Microbiol 2011; PMID 18292569).
  • Limitations / phenotype recapitulation: These models reproduce mucosal (genital) colonization and local infection, not the disseminated/arthritic phenotype; murine complement and receptor differences limit modeling of human serum resistance and dissemination. No established animal model specifically recapitulates gonococcal arthritis/DGI was retrieved — reported as not available.
  • In vitro: normal-human-serum bactericidal assays with isogenic Por1A/Por1B strains and factor-H-binding assays are the main experimental systems for the dissemination/serum-resistance mechanism (PMC2213355; PMID 18292569).
