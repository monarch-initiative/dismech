Overhydrated Hereditary Stomatocytosis

Mendelian MONDO:0008493 Pathograph 33 Show in embeddings browser familial hemolytic anemia hereditary stomatocytosis

Overhydrated hereditary stomatocytosis (OHSt), also called hereditary hydrocytosis, is a rare autosomal dominant congenital hemolytic anemia caused by heterozygous missense substitutions in RHAG, the gene encoding the Rh-associated glycoprotein. It is the most leaky member of the cation-leak hereditary stomatocytosis family, a group of dominantly inherited anemias in which the red cell membrane abnormally conducts the monovalent cations sodium and potassium and so loses control of its own volume. In OHSt the mutant RhAG is converted from an ammonia-transporting channel into a non-selective cation pore: sodium enters faster than potassium leaves, the cell gains cations and water, and the swollen, overhydrated erythrocyte takes the cup-shaped (stomatocyte) morphology that names the disease. The hydrated cells have a high mean corpuscular volume, a low mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration, increased osmotic fragility and reduced deformability, and are removed prematurely in the spleen, producing chronic extravascular haemolysis with reticulocytosis, unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, jaundice, splenomegaly and pigment gallstones. Severity ranges from compensated haemolysis to transfusion-dependent anaemia. Two features dominate management. Iron overload tends to develop even without transfusion, and splenectomy carries a severe, sometimes fatal, risk of thromboembolic disease and should be avoided. The near-absence of the membrane protein stomatin from OHSt red cells, historically the finding that named the protein, is a secondary consequence of the disorder rather than its cause: the primary lesion is the RhAG mutation.

RhAG Nonselective Cation Pore Formation
RhAG belongs to the ammonium-transporter family, whose members form pore-like structures. Molecular modelling on the homologous Nitrosomonas europaea Rh50 protein indicates that the OHSt substitutions widen the central channel and convert it into a non-selective cation pore, a qualitative gain of a conductance the wild-type protein does not normally provide.

Through the mutant pore the red cell membrane leaks monovalent cations. Secondary Erythrocyte Stomatin Deficiency
OHSt red cells are near-completely deficient in the 32 kDa membrane protein stomatin (band 7.2b), the finding after which the protein was originally named. No mutation in the stomatin gene is found; the protein is mis-trafficked and lost on erythroid maturation. This is a secondary correlate of the primary RhAG defect, a dead-end branch that does not drive the haemolysis.

description: >-
  Overhydrated hereditary stomatocytosis (OHSt), also called hereditary
  hydrocytosis, is a rare autosomal dominant congenital hemolytic anemia caused
  by heterozygous missense substitutions in RHAG, the gene encoding the
  Rh-associated glycoprotein. It is the most leaky member of the cation-leak
  hereditary stomatocytosis family, a group of dominantly inherited anemias in
  which the red cell membrane abnormally conducts the monovalent cations sodium
  and potassium and so loses control of its own volume. In OHSt the mutant RhAG
  is converted from an ammonia-transporting channel into a non-selective cation
  pore: sodium enters faster than potassium leaves, the cell gains cations and
  water, and the swollen, overhydrated erythrocyte takes the cup-shaped
  (stomatocyte) morphology that names the disease. The hydrated cells have a
  high mean corpuscular volume, a low mean corpuscular haemoglobin
  concentration, increased osmotic fragility and reduced deformability, and are
  removed prematurely in the spleen, producing chronic extravascular haemolysis
  with reticulocytosis, unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, jaundice,
  splenomegaly and pigment gallstones. Severity ranges from compensated
  haemolysis to transfusion-dependent anaemia.

  transfusion, and splenectomy carries a severe, sometimes fatal, risk of
  thromboembolic disease and should be avoided. The near-absence of the membrane
  protein stomatin from OHSt red cells, historically the finding that named the
  protein, is a secondary consequence of the disorder rather than its cause: the
  primary lesion is the RhAG mutation.

1. Disease information

  • OHSt is a rare, autosomal dominant congenital hemolytic anemia. It belongs to the "cation-leak" hereditary stomatocytoses, together with dehydrated stomatocytosis (xerocytosis, mainly PIEZO1) and the other leak forms. (Frontiers 2018)
  • Red cells are swollen (high MCV, low MCHC). A summary of the pathogenesis says: "The influx of Na+ exceeds the loss of K+ causing water influx and resulting in swollen erythrocytes, hemolysis, and stomatocyte formation." (Frontiers 2018)
  • Identifiers. The MONDO ID you gave (MONDO:0008493) was not checked. OMIM, Orphanet, ICD-10/11 and MeSH identifiers are not verified either; look them up directly. KEGG has an entry (H01979), found by search: KEGG.
  • Synonyms: overhydrated stomatocytosis; hydrocytosis; OHSt; hereditary stomatocytosis with overhydration. Related labels you will meet in the same sources are "Rh-null disease, regulator type" and "Rh deficiency syndrome". These are separate recessive conditions, not synonyms.
  • Data level: the evidence is aggregated disease-level resources and case series. It is not EHR-derived.

2. Etiology

  • Cause: heterozygous missense variants in RHAG, which encodes the Rh-associated glycoprotein. Search results describe overhydrated stomatocytosis as "mainly caused by RHAG gene mutations, and milder forms have been linked to SLC4A1 variants". (J Blood Med 2019, PMC6613601)
  • Landmark paper: Bruce LJ et al., Blood 2009, PMID 18931342, "The monovalent cation leak in overhydrated stomatocytic red blood cells results from amino acid substitutions in the Rh-associated glycoprotein." (abstract mirror)
  • Search results report that "DNA analysis revealed that the OHSt patients have 1 of 2 heterozygous mutations (t182g, t194c) in RHAG that lead to substitutions of 2 highly conserved amino acids (Ile61Arg, Phe65Ser)."
  • They also report that expressing the mutant proteins in Xenopus oocytes gave a cation leak about six times greater than wild type.
  • Further variants: later reports describe additional RHAG missense variants (for example p.Phe65Ser and others) [unverified]. Please search ClinVar and the literature for the current list.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain 1. A heterozygous missense variant in RHAG (Ile61Arg or Phe65Ser) alters a conserved transmembrane residue. This is demonstrated. (Bruce 2009) 2. The altered Rh-associated glycoprotein, which is part of the Rh/ankyrin-R/band 3 complex, forms or opens a pore-like cation pathway. This is demonstrated by Xenopus oocyte expression (about 6-fold leak) and supported by structural modelling. 3. The pathway lets monovalent cations (Na⁺ and K⁺) cross the red cell membrane. 4. Na⁺ influx exceeds K⁺ loss, so net cation gain draws water into the cell. This is demonstrated. (Frontiers 2018) 5. Compensatory Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity rises. It consumes ATP and glycolytic capacity, and a 2011 metabolomic study of four patients reported "glycolysis is exhausted", with ADP, pyruvate, lactate and malate accumulating. (Haematologica 2011) That study also suggests transporter abnormalities or unrecognised oxidative stress. This step is inferred from small-sample data. 6. The cells swell, take a cup shape (stomatocytes) and have reduced deformability. Why the cup shape forms is unknown. 7. The spleen removes the abnormal cells, which leads to extravascular hemolysis. This is inferred. 8. Chronic hemolysis causes anemia, jaundice, splenomegaly and pigment gallstones. Iron overload can follow [unverified].
