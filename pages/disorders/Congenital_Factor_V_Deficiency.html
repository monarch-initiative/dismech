Congenital Factor V Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0009210 Pathograph 34 Show in embeddings browser Rare bleeding disorder Inherited blood coagulation disorder

An autosomal recessive bleeding disorder caused by biallelic variants in F5, the gene encoding coagulation factor V (proaccelerin or labile factor). It is among the rarest of the inherited bleeding disorders, at roughly one per million. Factor V is the non-enzymatic cofactor of the prothrombinase complex: once activated to factor Va it assembles with the protease factor Xa on an anionic phospholipid surface and accelerates the conversion of prothrombin to thrombin by several orders of magnitude. A defect in factor V therefore sits in the common pathway, downstream of both the tissue-factor (extrinsic) and contact-activated (intrinsic) arms, so both the prothrombin time and the activated partial thromboplastin time are prolonged while the thrombin time stays normal. That screening picture, followed by a low factor V activity assay, is what establishes the diagnosis. The disorder's most distinctive feature is how poorly residual factor V activity predicts bleeding. Patients with undetectable plasma factor V often bleed far less than expected, and the leading explanation is that about a fifth of the body's factor V is stored in platelet alpha-granules rather than in plasma. This platelet-derived pool, released locally at a wound, together with the physiologically low tissue factor pathway inhibitor levels seen in factor-V-deficient plasma, supports enough thrombin generation to rescue many patients from fatal haemorrhage. The clinical phenotype is usually mucocutaneous bleeding - epistaxis, gingival bleeding, easy bruising, menorrhagia and postoperative bleeding - but a minority, especially neonates with the lowest levels, suffer life-threatening intracranial haemorrhage. Two deficiency types are described by analogy with other factor deficiencies - type I (cross-reactive-material-negative, antigen and activity reduced together) and type II (cross-reactive-material-positive, a dysfunctional molecule present at near-normal antigen). They converge on the same prothrombinase cofactor defect and are not curated here as separate diseases. Three things are deliberately hedged. First, there is no licensed factor-V-specific concentrate; treatment still rests on fresh frozen plasma, with platelet transfusion, antifibrinolytics and recombinant activated factor VII as adjuncts, and liver transplantation as a definitive cure in the most severe cases. Second, the genotype-phenotype correlation is weak and no node claims otherwise. Third, acquired factor V deficiency (autoantibody-mediated) and combined factor V and factor VIII deficiency (LMAN1/MCFD2, a trafficking defect) are different entities and are recorded only as differential diagnoses.

Impaired Prothrombinase Cofactor Function
The non-redundant step. Activated factor V (factor Va) is the non-enzymatic cofactor of the prothrombinase complex, assembling with factor Xa on an anionic phospholipid surface and accelerating prothrombin cleavage far beyond what factor Xa achieves alone. Because both the extrinsic and intrinsic arms of the cascade converge on this common-pathway step, neither can compensate for the defect.

Residual Platelet Factor V
About a fifth to a quarter of the body's factor V is stored in platelet alpha-granules, acquired by megakaryocyte endocytosis of plasma factor V rather than synthesised in the platelet. In patients with undetectable plasma factor V, a measurable platelet-derived factor V/Va pool can persist and be released locally at a wound, where it is the predominant cofactor for thrombin generation at the platelet surface. This pool, not any residual plasma factor V, is the leading explanation for the mild phenotype of many severely deficient patients.

Factor-V-deficient plasma carries physiologically low levels of tissue factor pathway inhibitor, possibly because factor V affects TFPI stability or clearance in vivo. The reduced TFPI lowers the factor V threshold needed for minimal thrombin generation and so works alongside residual platelet factor V to protect deficient patients from severe bleeding.

description: >-
  An autosomal recessive bleeding disorder caused by biallelic variants in F5,
  the gene encoding coagulation factor V (proaccelerin or labile factor). It is
  among the rarest of the inherited bleeding disorders, at roughly one per
  million.

  Factor V is the non-enzymatic cofactor of the prothrombinase complex: once
  activated to factor Va it assembles with the protease factor Xa on an anionic
  phospholipid surface and accelerates the conversion of prothrombin to thrombin
  by several orders of magnitude. A defect in factor V therefore sits in the
  common pathway, downstream of both the tissue-factor (extrinsic) and
  contact-activated (intrinsic) arms, so both the prothrombin time and the
  activated partial thromboplastin time are prolonged while the thrombin time
  stays normal. That screening picture, followed by a low factor V activity
  assay, is what establishes the diagnosis.

  The disorder's most distinctive feature is how poorly residual factor V
  activity predicts bleeding. Patients with undetectable plasma factor V often
  bleed far less than expected, and the leading explanation is that about a fifth
  of the body's factor V is stored in platelet alpha-granules rather than in
  plasma. This platelet-derived pool, released locally at a wound, together with
  the physiologically low tissue factor pathway inhibitor levels seen in
  factor-V-deficient plasma, supports enough thrombin generation to rescue many
  patients from fatal haemorrhage. The clinical phenotype is usually
  mucocutaneous bleeding - epistaxis, gingival bleeding, easy bruising,
  menorrhagia and postoperative bleeding - but a minority, especially neonates
  with the lowest levels, suffer life-threatening intracranial haemorrhage.

  Two deficiency types are described by analogy with other factor deficiencies -
  type I (cross-reactive-material-negative, antigen and activity reduced
  together) and type II (cross-reactive-material-positive, a dysfunctional
  molecule present at near-normal antigen). They converge on the same
  prothrombinase cofactor defect and are not curated here as separate diseases.

  Three things are deliberately hedged. First, there is no licensed
  factor-V-specific concentrate; treatment still rests on fresh frozen plasma,
  with platelet transfusion, antifibrinolytics and recombinant activated factor
  VII as adjuncts, and liver transplantation as a definitive cure in the most
  severe cases. Second, the genotype-phenotype correlation is weak and no node
  claims otherwise. Third, acquired factor V deficiency (autoantibody-mediated)
  and combined factor V and factor VIII deficiency (LMAN1/MCFD2, a trafficking
  defect) are different entities and are recorded only as differential diagnoses.

1. Disease information

  • Congenital factor V (FV) deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive bleeding disorder. It is caused by a deficiency of coagulation factor V (proaccelerin, labile factor). [sourced: NHF/HFA overview]
  • Synonyms: Owren's disease, parahemophilia, proaccelerin deficiency, labile factor deficiency. [sourced: same page]
  • Identifiers:
  • MONDO:0009210, given in the template.
  • HGNC:3542 appears in a GenCC submission linking F5 to MONDO:0009210. [sourced: GenCC]
  • The repo uses the lowercase form hgnc:3542.
  • OMIM 227400 [background, unverified].
  • Orphanet number: not found.
  • ICD-10 D68.2 (hereditary deficiency of other clotting factors) [background, unverified].
  • Do not confuse it with FV Leiden (thrombophilia), combined FV/FVIII deficiency (LMAN1/MCFD2), or acquired FV inhibitors. Combined FV/FVIII deficiency is a separate disease. [background]
  • The data are aggregated disease-level resources and case series, not EHR-derived.

2. Etiology

  • Causal factor: biallelic loss-of-function variants in F5. [sourced: Asselta & Peyvandi review, 2009] The review reports that 56 mutations had been published in severe or moderately severe FV deficiency. More than two thirds were null mutations, and the rest were mostly missense.
  • Heterozygous carriers have about half-normal FV and are usually asymptomatic. [sourced]
  • Risk factors:
  • Consanguinity raises the chance of homozygosity. [background]
  • No environmental risk factors are known.
  • Protective factors:
  • Platelet-derived FV can partly preserve hemostasis even when plasma FV is near zero. [sourced: Cornell VIVO record, "Platelets and platelet-derived factor Va confer hemostatic competence in complete factor V deficiency"]
  • Concurrent thrombophilic variants (e.g. FV Leiden in trans) can attenuate the bleeding phenotype. [background, unverified]
  • Gene–environment interactions: none established.

5. Environmental information

None are known. There are no environmental, lifestyle, or infectious causes of the congenital form. Acquired FV deficiency (inhibitors, liver disease, DIC) is a separate entity.

6. Mechanism / pathophysiology

Causal chain: 1. Biallelic F5 loss-of-function variants lead to absent or reduced synthesis or secretion of FV. [sourced] 2. FV is made mainly in the liver (hepatocytes) and circulates as plasma FV. About 20% is stored in platelet α-granules. [sourced via search summary; hepatic synthesis is background] 3. Without FV there is no cofactor Va for factor Xa in the prothrombinase complex. Prothrombin conversion to thrombin on activated platelet surfaces is then impaired. [background] 4. Reduced thrombin generation leads to defective fibrin formation, and the PT and aPTT are prolonged. [background] 5. The result is mucosal and postoperative bleeding. The phenotype is variable because platelet FV/Va can partly compensate. [sourced: Cornell VIVO record] 6. Branch: in the most severe genotypes, with absent plasma and platelet FV, intracranial hemorrhage occurs, including perinatally. [sourced: Haematologica]

7. Anatomical structures affected

  • Systemic hemostasis, with the liver as the site of synthesis. Mucosae (nose, gut, uterus), brain, lung, and joints are the bleeding sites. [sourced / background]
  • Subcellular: platelet alpha granule. Laterality: not applicable.

8. Temporal development

  • Onset is congenital. The phenotype may present in the neonatal period, in childhood, or in adulthood.
  • The course is lifelong, non-progressive, and episodic. Intracranial hemorrhage is the main early-life risk. [sourced]
  • No remission patterns are described.

10. Diagnostics

  • Prolonged PT and aPTT with a normal thrombin time. A mixing study corrects. [background]
  • FV activity assay, which is diagnostic. FV antigen can distinguish type I (quantitative) from type II (qualitative) deficiency. [background]
  • Heterozygotes have about 50% FV. [sourced]
  • Genetic testing: F5 sequencing, which is large and requires Sanger sequencing, a panel, or exome sequencing. [background]
  • Differential: combined FV/FVIII deficiency (LMAN1/MCFD2), acquired FV inhibitor, liver disease, vitamin K deficiency, DIC. [background]
  • Screening: none population-wide. Cascade testing of relatives is relevant.

11. Outcome / prognosis

  • Bleeding severity is variable. Most patients have mucosal bleeding that is managed with treatment. Severe cases can involve life-threatening intracranial, pulmonary, or GI hemorrhage. [sourced]
  • Survival and life-expectancy data: not retrieved.
  • Prognostic factors: FV activity level and genotype, with platelet FV as a modifier. [sourced / background]

12. Treatment

  • There is no FV-specific concentrate. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) is the mainstay. [sourced: Rare Coagulation Disorders; NHF/HFA overview]
  • Dosing: one review sets a target of FV above 20% for procedures and acute bleeding, with daily FFP infusions of 15–20 mL/kg typically enough. [sourced via search summary; verify at the source]
  • Platelet transfusion is added in severe or refractory bleeding. Platelet FV supports hemostasis after plasma infusion. [sourced: Cornell VIVO record]
  • Antifibrinolytics (aminocaproic acid or tranexamic acid) are used for mucosal bleeding. rFVIIa and prothrombin complex concentrate have been used in refractory cases. [sourced via search summary]
  • Experimental: a 2025 Thieme article, "From Fresh Frozen Plasma to First-in-human: Bringing Coagulation Factor V Deficiency into Therapeutic Trials", covers development of FV therapeutics, but I did not read it. [sourced: Thieme] I found no NCT identifiers.

13. Prevention

  • No primary prevention.
  • Genetic counseling and cascade testing apply. Prenatal diagnosis is possible in families with a known variant. [background]
  • Prophylactic FFP before surgery or procedures is used as secondary and tertiary prevention. Peripartum and neonatal management planning is needed in severe cases. [background]
  • Avoid antiplatelet drugs. [background, unverified]
