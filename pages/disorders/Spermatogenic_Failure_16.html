Spermatogenic Failure 16

Mendelian MONDO:0014961 Pathograph 10 Show in embeddings browser Spermatogenic Failure Genetic Disease

Spermatogenic failure 16 (SPGF16) is autosomal recessive male infertility caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in SUN5, a testis-specific SUN domain protein of the sperm nuclear envelope. Affected men are otherwise healthy and present with acephalic spermatozoa syndrome: the ejaculate contains mostly headless flagella, a minority of tail-less heads, and a few intact spermatozoa with an abnormal head-tail junction. SUN5 sits in the posterior nuclear envelope at the implantation fossa, where it is thought to form a LINC (linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton) bridge that holds the head-tail coupling apparatus against the nucleus. Without it, the coupling apparatus separates from the nucleus during spermatid elongation and the break lies between the nucleus and the proximal centriole. Nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, missense and whole-gene deletion alleles are reported, and SUN5 is the most frequently mutated gene in acephalic spermatozoa syndrome, with a founder splice-site duplication common in men of North African origin. Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) with the detached heads has mostly given good fertilization and pregnancy rates.

Pathophysiology

4
Loss of SUN5 Protein
Nonsense, frameshift and splice-site alleles truncate SUN5 or remove its transcript, a 5-kb deletion-insertion removes exon 8, and a large deletion removes the whole gene. Several missense variants in the SUN domain alter folding and localization, and patient sperm carrying them also lack the protein. SUN5 is a testis-specific inner nuclear membrane protein that, in mature sperm, sits at the implantation fossa where the tail attaches to the head.

Disrupted SUN5 Nuclear Envelope Anchoring Complex
At the posterior nuclear envelope SUN5 binds partners on both sides of the envelope: lamin B1 inside the nucleus, and the KASH protein Nesprin3, the centriole-associated septin SEPTIN12, ODF1 and the linker CENTLEIN outside it. Cryo-electron tomography of mammalian sperm shows a regular hexagonal protein lattice in the nuclear envelope at the base of the head, proposed to be formed by these LINC complexes. Losing SUN5 dissolves this complex and Nesprin3 loses its posterior localization. The partner data come mainly from Sun5 knockout mice and transfected cells, with altered Nesprin3 and ODF1 staining confirmed in patient sperm.

Defective Sperm Head-Tail Coupling
The head-tail coupling apparatus assembles normally early in spermiogenesis, but without SUN5 it detaches with the basal plate from the implantation fossa as the spermatid elongates. The centrosome and coupling apparatus drift away from the nucleus, and head and tail separate once the manchette disappears. The break lies between the nucleus and the proximal centriole, so the headless tails keep both centrioles and the heads lack an implantation fossa and basal plate.

Spermatogenic Failure 16 (SPGF16) — Comprehensive Disease Report

Disease: Spermatogenic Failure 16 (SPGF16) · Causal gene: SUN5 (SPAG4L / TSARG4) OMIM: 617187 · MONDO: MONDO:0014961 · HGNC: HGNC:16252 · Category: Mendelian (autosomal recessive)

Summary

Spermatogenic Failure 16 (SPGF16) is a non-syndromic, autosomal-recessive form of male infertility caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in SUN5 (also named SPAG4L or TSARG4), a testis-specific inner-nuclear-membrane LINC-complex protein. SPGF16 is the molecular-genetic designation for the most common genetic subtype of acephalic spermatozoa syndrome (ASS) — a severe teratozoospermia in which the ejaculate is dominated by headless ("decapitated") sperm tails, detached heads, and sperm whose heads and tails are joined by a fragile, abnormal junction. SUN5 mutations are reported in roughly 33–47% of ASS cases, making it the single most frequently identified cause of the syndrome.

Mechanistically, SUN5 is a SUN-domain protein that localizes to the base of the sperm head (the head–tail coupling apparatus, HTCA). It physically bridges the sperm nucleus to the proximal centriole/flagellum: its nucleoplasmic side binds the nuclear-membrane protein LaminB1, while its other side binds the cytoskeletal GTPase Septin12 and cooperates with CENTLEIN and PMFBP1. In situ cryo-electron tomography shows SUN5 forms an extensive hexagonal protein lattice in the nuclear envelope at this junction. When SUN5 is absent, the Septin12-associated proximal centriole separates from the nucleus, the head–tail junction fails, and sperm heads detach from their tails during spermiogenesis or ejaculation — producing the acephalic phenotype. Sun5-knockout mice recapitulate the human disease, confirming a conserved, autosomal-recessive mechanism.

Clinically, SPGF16 presents as isolated primary male infertility. Affected men have normal virilization, normal testicular volume, and normal endocrine function; there are no systemic or syndromic features. Diagnosis rests on semen analysis and sperm morphology (H&E staining, transmission electron microscopy showing head–tail junction defects) confirmed by SUN5 sequencing (WES/Sanger) and absent SUN5 protein on immunofluorescence/Western blot. Because the defect is purely structural and the detached heads frequently contain intact genetic material, intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) is an effective treatment, with reported fertilization, embryo, and pregnancy rates that are favorable (one cohort: 81.5% fertilization, 81.8% good-quality embryos, 100% pregnancy among SUN5 patients). This report synthesizes 8 confirmed findings across 26 reviewed papers into a complete, section-by-section knowledge-base entry.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. SPGF16 is caused by biallelic (homozygous or compound-heterozygous) loss-of-function variants in SUN5. The landmark discovery study performed "whole-exome sequencing in two unrelated infertile men and subsequent variant filtering identified one homozygous (c.824C>T [p.Thr275Met]) and one compound heterozygous (c.1006C>T [p.Arg356Cys] and c.485T>A [p.Met162Lys]) SUN5 (also named TSARG4) variants" (PMID: 27640305). SUN5 is the most frequently identified ASS cause: "Mutations in the SUN5 gene have been identified most frequently (33-47%) in ASS cases" (PMID: 38870534).

Genetic risk factors. The only established risk factor is inheritance of two pathogenic SUN5 alleles. Consanguinity increases the risk of homozygosity — multiple reported cases arise from consanguineous or first-cousin unions (e.g., two infertile brothers of first-cousin parents, PMID: 40977202). No susceptibility loci or validated modifier genes have been established for SPGF16 specifically.

Environmental risk factors. None established. SPGF16 is a monogenic structural sperm defect; it is not attributable to toxins, lifestyle, occupational exposure, age, or infection. These classic male-infertility risk factors are not implicated in this Mendelian subtype.

Protective factors. None identified. Heterozygous carriers are fertile (recessive inheritance); a single wild-type allele is "protective" in the Mendelian sense, but there are no described protective variants or environmental protective factors.

Gene–environment interactions. No gene–environment interaction has been documented for SPGF16. The phenotype is driven by the genotype essentially deterministically (high penetrance in biallelic LoF).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Biallelic LoF mutation in SUN5 → leads to loss or destabilization of the testis-specific SUN-domain protein SUN5 in developing male germ cells (demonstrated: absent SUN5 on patient sperm immunostaining).
  2. Absence of SUN5 at the sperm-head base → results in failure to assemble the nuclear-envelope hexagonal lattice and head–tail coupling apparatus (HTCA) during spermiogenesis (demonstrated by cryo-ET; PMID: 41855266).
  3. Loss of the LaminB1–SUN5–Septin12 bridge → leads to separation of the Septin12-associated proximal centriole from the nucleus (demonstrated in Sun5-KO mice; PMID: 38870534).
  4. Breakage of the head-to-tail junction → results in mechanical uncoupling of the sperm head from the flagellum.
  5. During spermiogenesis/epididymal transit/ejaculation → the fragile or absent junction causes the head and tail to detach → acephalic (headless) spermatozoa and detached heads in the ejaculate.
  6. Headless/malformed sperm cannot fertilize an oocyte naturally → results in primary male infertility (the clinical manifestation of SPGF16).

Branch (downstream network): SUN5 acts within a multi-protein HTCA scaffold. The adaptor CENTLEIN links SUN5 to PMFBP1; in its absence "the absence of Centlein sets SUN5 and PMFBP1 apart, the former close to the sperm head and the latter in the decapitated tail" (PMID: 34389728). Other HTCA partners — CCDC113, CCDC188, CEP250, TSGA10, SPATA20, SPATC1L, BRDT, and ZMYND15 (which interacts directly with SUN5) — cause overlapping ASS phenotypes when mutated, placing SUN5 at the hub of head–tail anchoring.

Mechanistic detail

  • Molecular pathways / complex: LINC complex (SUN–KASH), nuclear-envelope–cytoskeleton coupling. SUN5 "connected the nucleus by interacting with LaminB1 and connected the proximal centriole by interacting with Septin12. The binding between SUN5 and Septin12 promoted their aggregation together in the sperm neck. The disruption of the LaminB1/SUN5/Septin12 complex by Sun5 deficiency caused separation of the Septin12-proximal centriole from the nucleus, leading to the breakage of the head-to-tail junction" (PMID: 38870534).
  • Cellular processes: Spermiogenesis — nuclear-envelope remodeling, implantation-fossa/basal-plate formation, centriole–nucleus tethering. SUN5 dynamically relocates during germ-cell development: "In pachytene spermatocytes, it is located in a membranous compartment… In round spermatids, it progresses to the Golgi and the NE before to be located to the tail/head junction in epididymal sperm… Sun5 is not, as initially reported, facing the acrosome but is in fact excluded from this zone" (PMID: 25775128).
  • Protein dysfunction: Loss of function of a SUN-domain transmembrane protein; truncating and destabilizing missense alleles eliminate the functional anchor. Structural data: "sperm-specific SUN5 localizes to the base of the head in human, mouse, and boar sperm. By applying in situ cryoelectron tomography, we find an extensive hexagonal lattice in the nuclear envelope in this region" (PMID: 41855266).
  • Tissue damage mechanism: Mechanical failure (not inflammatory/ischemic) — the head–tail junction physically fractures.
  • Immune / metabolic involvement: None.

  • Organ level (primary): Testis (UBERON:0000473) — specifically the seminiferous tubules (UBERON:0001343) where spermiogenesis occurs; and the spermatozoon itself (UBERON:0000408 / CL:0000019). Secondary: none — no other organ is affected.
  • Body system: Male reproductive system (UBERON:0000079) only.
  • Tissue / cell level: Germinal epithelium of the seminiferous tubule; affected cells are spermatids and spermatozoa (CL:0000018 spermatid; CL:0000019 sperm). Somatic Sertoli/Leydig cells and the endocrine axis are unaffected (normal testosterone/testicular volume).
  • Subcellular level: The lesion is at the sperm head–tail coupling apparatus / neck, at the interface of the nuclear envelope / inner nuclear membrane (GO:0005637), the implantation fossa (basal plate) of the nucleus, and the proximal centriole.   • Localization: Site = base of the sperm head / sperm neck; systemic to essentially all sperm (not a lateralized anatomical defect). UBERON: sperm (UBERON:0000408); testis (UBERON:0000473).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Congenital at the molecular/cellular level (the defect is present from first spermatogenesis at puberty); clinically recognized in adulthood when infertility is investigated. Onset pattern is chronic/insidious (silent until reproduction attempted).
  • Progression: Stable, non-progressive. There are no disease stages; the sperm defect is constant across the reproductive lifespan. Disease course is a chronic, lifelong structural infertility with no spontaneous remission.
  • Critical period: The vulnerable window is spermiogenesis (round-to-elongating spermatid stage) when the HTCA is built. There is no developmental window for correction; the therapeutic "window" is at assisted reproduction (ICSI), which bypasses the defect.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Autosomal recessive. Biallelic pathogenic variants are required; heterozygous carriers are fertile and asymptomatic. Consistent with gnomAD LoF-tolerance (pLI ≈ 0).
  • Penetrance: Effectively complete for the sperm phenotype in biallelic LoF individuals.
  • Expressivity: Relatively consistent (acephalic spermatozoa), though the proportion of headless vs. fragile-junction vs. malformed sperm varies between individuals.
  • Epidemiology: ASS overall is rare (no precise prevalence/incidence figures; considered a rare form of severe teratozoospermia). Within ASS, SUN5 accounts for 33–47% of cases (PMID: 38870534). No cases-per-100,000 estimate is available.
  • Consanguinity / founder effects: Consanguinity elevates homozygosity risk; several reported families are consanguineous. No classical founder haplotype is established, though the recurrent c.381del frameshift is the most common allele in gnomAD.
  • Carrier frequency: Individual pathogenic alleles are rare (AF ≤ ~4×10⁻⁵); aggregate carrier frequency is low but non-trivial given allelic heterogeneity.
  • Population demographics: Most reported cohorts are Han Chinese, reflecting study ascertainment rather than proven ethnic predilection; cases also reported from Iran and elsewhere. Sex: affects males only (a reproductive phenotype); the genotype can be carried by either sex. Age distribution: reproductive-age men.

10. Diagnostics

Clinical / laboratory workup: - Semen analysis + sperm morphology (H&E staining): predominance of headless tails, detached heads, and malformed sperm; "a large number of immature sperm… they had the ability to move" (PMID: 34393249). - Transmission electron microscopy (TEM): demonstrates head–tail junction/HTCA defects and abnormal neck structures. - Hormonal panel: FSH, LH, testosterone — typically normal, distinguishing from hypogonadotropic/primary testicular failure. - Physical exam / scrotal ultrasound: normal testicular volume, no varicocele/obstruction. - Immunofluorescence / Western blot: absent SUN5 in spermatozoa confirms the molecular defect.

Genetic testing: - Single-gene / panel testing of SUN5 is first-line given its dominant contribution; targeted Sanger confirmation of candidate variants. - Whole-exome sequencing (WES) is the workhorse for discovery and for cases negative on SUN5 (to assess PMFBP1, TSGA10, CEP250, CCDC188, CCDC113, SPATA20, SPATC1L, BRDT, ZMYND15). - CMA / CNV analysis is warranted because large deletions encompassing SUN5 occur (PMID: 34159570). - Karyotyping/FISH/mtDNA/repeat-expansion testing: not specifically indicated for SPGF16 (karyotype normal in typical cases; useful to exclude other azoospermia causes).

Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis: Diagnosis = morphological ASS + biallelic SUN5. Differential includes other ASS genes (PMFBP1, TSGA10, CEP250, CCDC188, CCDC113, etc.), globozoospermia (DPY19L2 — round-headed, acrosome defect), MMAF, and obstructive/non-obstructive azoospermia. SUN5 is distinguished by its specific role at the head–tail junction (not the acrosome; PMID: 25775128) and its favorable ICSI prognosis.

Screening: No population newborn/carrier screening exists. Cascade carrier testing of relatives and partner carrier testing are reasonable once a proband's variants are known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival / mortality / morbidity: SPGF16 has no effect on survival, life expectancy, or physical health. The only "morbidity" is infertility and its psychosocial burden.
  • Disease course: Stable, lifelong infertility; no complications in the conventional medical sense.
  • Recovery / treatment response: Natural fertility is essentially absent, but ICSI yields favorable reproductive outcomes (see Treatment). In a SUN5 cohort: "The rates of fertilization, good-quality embryos and pregnancy for five patients with SUN5 mutations following ICSI were 81.5%, 81.8% and 100%, respectively" (PMID: 29329387).
  • Prognostic factors: The genetic cause predicts ICSI prognosis — SUN5 and PMFBP1/TSGA10 generally carry favorable ICSI outcomes, whereas some genes (e.g., SPATA20 loss of function, proximal-centriole-deficient TSGA10, certain CEP250/CCDC188 cases) may give poorer outcomes. Notably, not all SUN5 ICSI attempts succeed — two ICSI cycles failed to achieve pregnancy in one c.879dupC case (PMID: 40977202).

12. Treatment

There is no pharmacological cure; management is reproductive, centered on ICSI.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Not possible (congenital genetic). Genetic counseling for consanguineous couples and families with affected relatives is the principal preventive measure.
  • Secondary prevention / screening: Carrier screening of partners and cascade testing of relatives once a proband's SUN5 genotype is defined; preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) and prenatal testing are options for couples at recurrence risk.
  • Tertiary prevention: ICSI to achieve pregnancy; counseling that affected/carrier status may be transmitted depending on partner genotype.
  • Immunization / behavioral / public-health / environmental / prophylaxis measures: Not applicable — no environmental or infectious component.
  • Counseling (ontology): NCIT:C15417 Genetic Counseling.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy / orthologs: Human SUN5 has a mouse ortholog Sun5 (NCBI Gene 76407; MGI:1923657; Ensembl ENSMUSG00000027480). SUN5 protein localizes to the sperm-head base in human, mouse, and boar (pig) (PMID: 41855266), indicating evolutionary conservation of its function across mammals.
  • Natural disease in other species: No naturally occurring SUN5 ASS has been catalogued in OMIA for companion animals/livestock; the boar data are experimental/structural, and mouse data are from engineered knockouts.
  • Comparative biology: The mechanism (head–tail anchoring via a SUN-domain LINC protein) is conserved; mouse knockouts faithfully reproduce the human phenotype (see §15). One caveat: for some other ASS genes mouse and human phenotypes diverge (e.g., CEP250 knock-in mice show azoospermia rather than classic ASS; PMID: 39726222), but SUN5 is concordant across species.
  • Transmission / zoonosis: Not applicable.

15. Model Organisms

  • Model type: Mammalian — mouse (Mus musculus) is the principal model.
  • Genetic models: Sun5-knockout mice (constitutive null). At least two independent lines:
  • Shang et al., 2017, eLife — "Essential role for SUN5 in anchoring sperm head to the tail" (PMID: 28945193).
  • Zhang et al., 2024 — "we generated Sun5 knockout mice, which presented the phenotype of ASS," used to map the LaminB1–SUN5–Septin12 bridge (PMID: 38870534).
  • Phenotype recapitulation: High — Sun5-null males reproduce human acephalic spermatozoa syndrome (sperm heads detached from tails), validating the causal mechanism.
  • Limitations: Mice do not model the ICSI-specific clinical outcome variability seen in humans; most lines are constitutive nulls rather than patient-variant knock-ins, so allele-specific (missense) effects are less explored.
  • Applications: Dissecting HTCA assembly, SUN5 interaction partners (LaminB1, Septin12, CENTLEIN, PMFBP1), and nuclear-envelope lattice architecture.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Ascertainment bias: Most cohorts are Han Chinese and small; true population prevalence, ethnic distribution, and carrier frequencies are not precisely quantified.
  2. ICSI outcome heterogeneity: Reported rates are favorable but based on few patients; occasional failures (e.g., PMID: 40977202) indicate incomplete predictability. Prognostic biomarkers for ICSI success are undefined.
  3. Genotype–phenotype correlations: Whether specific missense vs. truncating alleles predict ICSI prognosis or residual sperm quality is not resolved.
  4. Mechanistic completeness: The full stoichiometry/architecture of the SUN5–LaminB1–Septin12–CENTLEIN–PMFBP1 complex and the exact developmental timing of junction failure are still being mapped.
  5. No standardized QoL or natural-history data specific to SPGF16.
  6. Modifier genes/epigenetics: None established; unexplored.
