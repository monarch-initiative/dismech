Sycosis Barbae

Infectious Disease MONDO:1050000 Pathograph 10 Show in embeddings browser Folliculitis

Sycosis barbae is the deeper staphylococcal form of folliculitis barbae in which Staphylococcus aureus infection of shaved beard-region hair follicles extends beyond superficial follicular pustules into perifollicular nodules, furuncles, or coalescent carbuncles.

Sycosis Barbae — Comprehensive Disease Characteristics Report

Disease: Sycosis Barbae (staphylococcal beard folliculitis) MONDO ID: MONDO:1050000 (as provided) Category: Infectious Disease Evidence base: 11 confirmed findings, 46 papers reviewed, 5 investigation iterations Report type: Literature-based disease knowledge-base entry. No primary datasets were provided; all content is derived from aggregated, disease-level resources (PubMed primary literature, ontologies, clinical references), not from individual patient/EHR data.

Summary

Sycosis barbae is a chronic, deep-seated bacterial (staphylococcal) folliculitis and perifolliculitis of the bearded skin of adult men. It is caused by Staphylococcus aureus infecting terminal beard-hair follicles that have been rendered susceptible by shaving-induced follicular microtrauma. The disease lies on a severity continuum: it begins as a superficial folliculitis barbae (Bockhart impetigo), and if untreated deepens into sycosis barbae, then can progress to furuncles (boils) and coalescing carbuncles with occasional systemic upset. A chronic variant, lupoid sycosis, produces scarring (cicatricial) alopecia and permanent cosmetic disfigurement of the beard (PMID: 25226722).

Mechanistically, the disease follows a well-defined causal chain drawn largely from murine S. aureus skin-infection models plus human genetics: shaving microtrauma and follicular S. aureus colonization → bacterial breach of the follicular epithelium → secreted toxins (phenol-soluble modulins, α-hemolysin) trigger innate immune sensing → MyD88/EGR1-dependent recruitment of neutrophils, amplified by T-cell-derived IL-17A and IL-22 → neutrophils wall bacteria into a perifollicular abscess (furuncle) → α-hemolysin-mediated dermonecrosis causes tissue damage. Failure of clearance—because of host neutrophil/immune defects or persistent nasal carriage—produces the chronic, relapsing course (PMID: 34873293; PMID: 26351278; PMID: 32946878; PMID: 25681348).

Sycosis barbae is an acquired infection with no Mendelian/genetic basis and no available vaccine. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supplemented by Gram stain and bacterial culture, with KOH/fungal culture and dermatophyte PCR used to exclude the principal infectious mimic, tinea barbae, and clinical judgment to exclude the non-infectious mimic pseudofolliculitis barbae and the viral mimic herpetic sycosis. Treatment combines anti-staphylococcal antibiotics (topical for limited disease, systemic for deep/extensive disease), incision and drainage of fluctuant lesions, S. aureus decolonization, and shaving modification. Prognosis is benign with respect to mortality but prone to chronicity and relapse while shaving and nasal carriage persist.

Key Findings

F001 — Sycosis barbae is a deep-seated staphylococcal folliculitis of the beard

Sycosis barbae is defined as a chronic, deep, perifollicular bacterial infection of the bearded region, representing the deep progression of a superficial folliculitis barbae (Bockhart impetigo). The most common etiologic agent is Staphylococcus aureus, and the condition is precipitated and aggravated by shaving. Untreated superficial folliculitis can deepen into sycosis barbae, and coalescing perifollicular nodules (furuncles) can progress to carbuncles with systemic upset.

"It most frequently presents as a superficial folliculitis, with fine pustules appearing at the opening of hair follicles in the beard area, often associated with shaving; known as Bockhart impetigo and usually due to infection caused by Staphylococcus aureus. If untreated, the infection and inflammation can progress, leading to a more deeply seated infection known as sycosis barbae." — PMID: 25226722

This establishes the core definition, the causative organism, the severity continuum, and the shaving trigger that organize the rest of this report.

F002 — Tinea barbae is the principal infectious mimic

Deep beard-area infections that resemble sycosis barbae or a carbuncle can also be caused by dermatophytes (tinea barbae), most often zoophilic Trichophyton species (e.g., T. mentagrophytes, T. verrucosum) acquired through animal/livestock contact, and occasionally anthropophilic species (T. rubrum, T. tonsurans—the latter transmissible via contaminated barbershop tools). Clues favoring tinea barbae over bacterial sycosis include close animal contact, lack of response to antibiotics, deep boggy nodules/pustules with adherent crusts, and regional lymphadenopathy. An intense inflammatory reaction can form a kerion. Diagnosis requires mycological culture, histology, and dermatophyte PCR; treatment is systemic antifungals (terbinafine/itraconazole) ± corticosteroids, and surgical incision is contraindicated.

"Particularly in the context of a history of close animal contact or a lack of response to antibiotic treatment, a diagnosis of tinea barbae should be considered and investigated." — PMID: 25226722

"Often this leads to the initial suspected diagnosis of a bacterial folliculitis barbae or impetigo contagiosa." — PMID: 31001659

F003 — Pseudofolliculitis barbae is the main non-infectious differential and has a genetic basis

Pseudofolliculitis barbae (PFB) is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the beard caused by shaved curly hairs re-penetrating the skin (extrafollicular and transfollicular penetration), producing a foreign-body inflammatory reaction with papules, pustules, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and sometimes keloidal scarring. It is far more common in men of African and Asian ancestry. Unlike infectious sycosis barbae, PFB is mechanical/foreign-body rather than infectious. A single-nucleotide substitution (A12T) in the hair-follicle companion-layer keratin gene K6hf (KRT75) confers roughly six-fold increased risk of PFB.

"Individuals with the single nucleotide substitution in the hair follicle companion layer specific keratin gene (K6hf) have a six fold increased chance of developing PFB." — PMID: 31354326

"Pseudofolliculitis barbae (PFB) is an inflammatory condition of the beard area, with a high prevalence in men of subequatorial African ancestry" — PMID: 27212468

F004 — S. aureus nasal carriage and host immune factors drive recurrent/chronic disease

S. aureus is the dominant pathogen of pilosebaceous-unit infections (folliculitis, furunculosis, carbunculosis). Its invasiveness depends on numerous cellular and extracellular virulence factors whose expression varies with growth phase, environment, and infection site; host susceptibility reflects innate and acquired immune responses to colonization. Recurrent/chronic disease is associated with S. aureus reservoirs (notably the anterior nares) and with host defects—for example, documented recurrent furunculosis with defective neutrophil phagocytic (ingestion-phase) activity, in whom early antibiotics and intensified hygiene reduced abscess formation.

"S. aureus produces a great variety of cellular and extracellular factors responsible for its invasiveness and ability to cause pathological lesions. Their expression depends on the growth phase, environmental factors, and location of the infection." — PMID: 30995649

"All laboratory tests were normal except for a decrease of the neutrophil phagocytic ingestion phase." — PMID: 11269901

F005 — Management combines antibiotics with decolonization and shaving hygiene

First-line therapy for bacterial sycosis barbae targets S. aureus: topical antibiotics (e.g., mupirocin, fusidic acid) for limited disease and systemic anti-staphylococcal antibiotics for deeper/extensive disease, plus incision and drainage of fluctuant furuncles/carbuncles. Because colonization is an early, critical step and more than half of patients with staphylococcal skin and soft tissue infection (SSTI) experience recurrence (often clustering in households), recurrence prevention emphasizes decolonization: intranasal mupirocin plus antiseptic washes (chlorhexidine or dilute bleach baths) for the patient and household members, hygiene optimization, and avoiding shared personal items. Beard-specific measures include temporarily stopping shaving and not sharing razors. Importantly, decolonization reliably reduces colonization but has variable effect on recurrence, with sustained/periodic regimens showing the most benefit.

"A recommended decolonization regimen includes application of intranasal mupirocin and antiseptic body washes with chlorhexidine or dilute bleach water baths." — PMID: 30859379

"More than half of patients experiencing SSTI will have at least one recurrent infection. These infections frequently cluster in households." — PMID: 30859379

"Despite successful decolonization, the rate of SSTI recurrence did not decrease." — PMID: 27820584

F006 — Mechanistic causal chain: toxins → innate sensing → neutrophilic follicular abscess

The pathophysiology follows a defined causal chain (largely from mouse SSTI models plus human genetics), described in full in the Mechanistic Model section below. Key experimentally supported steps: phenol-soluble modulins (PSMs) directly recruit leukocytes via an EGR1-dependent "high-alert" mechanism; MyD88-dependent sensing by dermal macrophages escalates and terminates neutrophil recruitment; T-cell-derived IL-17A and IL-22 drive neutrophil infiltration and antimicrobial peptide production; neutrophils wall bacteria into a discrete abscess; and α-hemolysin causes dermonecrosis, with antibody neutralization plus early neutrophil recruitment limiting necrosis.

"this early recruitment was dependent on the transcription factor EGR1 and contributed to the prevention of infection" — PMID: 34873293

"Neutralization of either IL-17A or IL-22 reduced T cell and neutrophil infiltration and host defense peptide elaboration in lesions. These events corresponded with increased abscess severity, MRSA viability, and CFU density in skin." — PMID: 26351278

"Protection was characterized by robust neutrophilic inflammation and compartmentalization of bacteria into discrete abscesses" — PMID: 32946878

"MyD88-dependent sensing of S. aureus by dermal macrophages (Mϕ) contributes to both timely escalation and termination of PML-mediated inflammation" — PMID: 25681348

F007 — Comparative disease: staphylococcal superficial bacterial folliculitis in animals

An analogous staphylococcal folliculitis occurs naturally in other species. In dogs, superficial bacterial folliculitis (SBF) is the most common bacterial skin disease and is caused principally by Staphylococcus pseudintermedius (NCBI Taxonomy txid283734), with histology showing neutrophilic follicular/intracorneal pustules containing intracellular cocci within neutrophils—mirroring the neutrophilic follicular inflammation of human sycosis barbae. Methicillin-resistant S. pseudintermedius (MRSP) and MRSA are increasingly prevalent, driving reliance on culture-guided systemic antibiotics plus topical antiseptics (dilute sodium hypochlorite/bleach, chlorhexidine, benzoyl peroxide)—the same topical antiseptic principles used in human decolonization.

"Superficial bacterial folliculitis (SBF) is more common in the dog than other mammalian species." — PMID: 24345778

"Cytology from the margins of collarettes identified neutrophils, extracellular and intracellular cocci within neutrophils" — PMID: 27426474

"methicillin resistant Staphylococcus pseudintermedius (MRSP) and Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) are becoming more prevalent in canine SBF cases" — PMID: 24345778

F011 — Herpetic sycosis (HSV) is a viral mimic requiring antivirals

A third important differential of beard folliculitis is herpetic sycosis—a follicular infection of the beard caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV-1 or HSV-2), a variant of herpetic folliculitis. It presents with acutely developing grouped papulovesicles/pustules, pain, crusting, and reactive cervical lymphadenopathy, and characteristically fails to respond to antibacterial and antifungal therapy. It occurs in immunocompromised and immunocompetent hosts and may be primary or recurrent/relapsing. Routine bacterial, mycological, and Tzanck tests may be negative; diagnosis is confirmed by Tzanck cytology, direct immunofluorescence, viral culture, or (most sensitively) HSV PCR. Treatment is systemic antivirals (aciclovir/valaciclovir), with rapid resolution.

"We describe an otherwise healthy young adult male with extensive herpetic sycosis of the beard area, which is a variant of herpetic folliculitis." — PMID: 11896419

"The possibility of a herpetic sycosis of the beard must be considered in the case of non-response to antibiotic or antifungal treatment." — PMID: 15118392

"Bacteriological, mycological and Tzanck tests from the lesions were negative... nested PCR technique (nPCR) demonstrated the presence of herpes simplex virus type 2" — PMID: 15564209

2. Etiology

Causal factors: Sycosis barbae is a multifactorial acquired infection. The necessary agent is Staphylococcus aureus (infectious; NCBI Taxonomy txid1280) acting on beard follicles primed by a mechanical trigger (shaving-induced follicular microtrauma). There is no Mendelian/causal gene; it is not heritable.

Risk factors (environmental/host): male sex with post-pubertal terminal beard hair; regular shaving (especially close/against-the-grain shaving, shared or contaminated razors); S. aureus nasal/skin carriage (anterior nares reservoir); coexisting pseudofolliculitis barbae or atopic dermatitis; immunosuppression/diabetes and neutrophil-function defects predisposing to recurrent staphylococcal disease (PMID: 11269901); warm humid climate and poor hygiene; barbershop exposure.

Genetic risk factors: none for bacterial sycosis barbae. (The related mechanical mimic PFB carries the KRT75/K6hf A12T variant conferring ~6-fold risk — PMID: 31354326.)

Protective factors (environmental/behavioral): growing a beard / cessation of shaving; single-use clean razors; pre-shave hydration and post-shave moisturization; good hygiene; S. aureus decolonization. No genetic protective variants are described.

Gene-environment interaction: No specific GxE is established for bacterial sycosis barbae. The closely related mechanical mimic PFB shows a clear GxE—a KRT75 (K6hf) A12T variant (curly-hair/follicle-curvature phenotype) combined with the environmental exposure of shaving produces ~6-fold risk; secondary S. aureus infection of PFB lesions links the two conditions (PMID: 31354326).

Not applicable as a genetic disease. Sycosis barbae has no causal gene, no pathogenic germline/somatic variants, no modifier genes, no disease-defining epigenetic changes, and no chromosomal abnormalities. It is an acquired bacterial infection. The relevant "molecular information" is microbial: S. aureus virulence factors (phenol-soluble modulins, α-hemolysin/Hla, and numerous growth-phase- and site-dependent factors) drive pathology (PMID: 30995649; PMID: 34873293; PMID: 32946878). For completeness, the related mimic PFB has a host keratin variant, KRT75 (K6hf) A12T (PMID: 31354326).

5. Environmental Information

  • Environmental/mechanical factors: shaving (follicular microtrauma), shared/contaminated razors and barbershop tools, warm humid climate.
  • Lifestyle factors: shaving practices and personal hygiene; barbershop attendance.
  • Infectious agents: Staphylococcus aureus (NCBI Taxonomy txid1280) is the primary agent. Mimics that must be distinguished are dermatophytes (tinea barbae: Trichophyton mentagrophytes, T. verrucosum, T. rubrum, T. tonsurans) and herpes simplex virus (herpetic sycosis) (PMID: 25226722; PMID: 11896419).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (steps largely demonstrated in murine SSTI models plus human genetics; inferred steps flagged):

  1. Shaving produces follicular microtrauma and S. aureus colonizes the beard follicle → leads to bacterial breach of the follicular epithelium into the dermis (inferred for the beard specifically, extrapolated from SSTI biology).
  2. Secreted phenol-soluble modulins (PSMs) directly recruit circulating leukocytes via an EGR1-dependent "high-alert" mechanism → results in early neutrophil influx (PMID: 34873293).
  3. MyD88-dependent sensing of S. aureus by resident dermal macrophages → escalates and later terminates polymorphonuclear leukocyte (neutrophil) recruitment (PMID: 25681348).
  4. T-cell-derived IL-17A and IL-22 → drive neutrophil infiltration and antimicrobial host-defense-peptide production; their neutralization increases abscess severity and bacterial burden (PMID: 26351278).
  5. Neutrophils wall off bacteria into a discrete perifollicular abscess (furuncle) (PMID: 32946878).
  6. Pore-forming toxin α-hemolysin (Hla) → causes dermonecrosis and tissue damage; antibody neutralization of Hla plus early neutrophil recruitment limits necrosis and enhances clearance (PMID: 32946878).
  7. Branch: coalescence of abscesses → yields a carbuncle; failure of clearance (host neutrophil/immune defects, persistent nasal carriage) → produces chronic/recurrent disease and, in lupoid sycosis, scarring alopecia (PMID: 11269901; PMID: 30995649).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ level: skin (integumentary system only). Primary site is bearded skin of the face and neck; secondary involvement is limited to regional lymphadenopathy or, rarely, systemic upset with carbuncle.
  • Tissue/cell level: pilosebaceous unit / hair follicle of terminal beard hairs; epithelial (follicular epithelium, epidermis) and dermal connective tissue; neutrophils and macrophages dominate the infiltrate.
  • UBERON terms: hair follicle (UBERON:0002073), skin of face (UBERON:0008203), skin (UBERON:0002097), dermis (UBERON:0002067), epidermis (UBERON:0001003).
  • CL terms: neutrophil (CL:0000775), macrophage (CL:0000235), keratinocyte (CL:0000312).
  • Subcellular: no disease-specific compartment; the microbial pore-forming toxin acts at the host plasma membrane (GO:0005886).
  • Localization/lateralization: typically bilateral, over upper lip, chin, cheeks, and mandibular/submental neck.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: post-pubertal adult men; onset is typically subacute to chronic and insidious, superimposed on ongoing shaving.
  • Progression: superficial folliculitis barbae → deep sycosis barbae → furuncle → carbuncle; the chronic lupoid variant is slowly progressive with scarring. Course is episodic/relapsing while shaving and S. aureus carriage persist.
  • Duration: self-limited superficial episodes can resolve, but the deep/chronic form tends to be chronic and relapsing.
  • Remission: treatment-induced remission is achievable with antibiotics + decolonization + shaving cessation; relapse is common if shaving resumes and carriage persists (PMID: 27820584; PMID: 30859379).
  • Critical intervention window: early antibiotic treatment of superficial folliculitis to prevent deepening into sycosis/furuncle/carbuncle (PMID: 25226722).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: none — acquired infection, not heritable; no OMIM entry.
  • Epidemiology: no formal incidence/prevalence figures are available (not a reportable disease; data derive from clinical/textbook description rather than registries).
  • Demographics: overwhelmingly post-pubertal adult men with terminal beard hair who shave; essentially absent in prepubertal children and in women (who lack a terminal beard). Sex ratio strongly male-predominant.
  • Geographic distribution: worldwide; no defined endemic areas for the bacterial form (unlike zoophilic tinea barbae, which clusters among people with animal/livestock contact).

10. Diagnostics

Diagnosis is primarily clinical (beard-area follicular pustules/papules in an adult man who shaves). Confirmatory/differentiating tests:

  1. Bacterial swab with Gram stain and culture + susceptibility of pustule contents to confirm S. aureus and detect MRSA (LOINC: aerobic wound culture, Gram stain).
  2. KOH wet mount, fungal culture, and dermatophyte PCR of plucked hairs to exclude tinea barbae — mandatory when there is animal contact or antibiotic non-response (PMID: 25226722; PMID: 31001659).
  3. Skin biopsy/histopathology in atypical or chronic scarring cases: perifollicular/follicular neutrophilic (suppurative) inflammation acutely; mixed granulomatous inflammation with fibrosis/scarring in lupoid sycosis.
  4. HSV testing (Tzanck cytology, direct immunofluorescence, viral culture, or HSV PCR) when lesions are acute/grouped/vesicular and fail antibiotics and antifungals — to identify herpetic sycosis (PMID: 11896419; PMID: 15118392; PMID: 15564209).
  5. Dermoscopy and anterior-nares carriage swabs in recurrent disease.

Differential diagnosis: tinea barbae (fungal), pseudofolliculitis barbae (mechanical), herpetic sycosis (viral), acne vulgaris, folliculitis decalvans, and other pustular dermatoses.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Prognosis is generally good/benign: acute bacterial sycosis barbae is curable with appropriate antibiotics and hygiene and carries no mortality. However, it is prone to chronicity and relapse while shaving continues and S. aureus carriage persists. The chronic deep lupoid sycosis variant can cause permanent cicatricial (scarring) alopecia and cosmetic disfigurement, with attendant psychosocial/quality-of-life impact. Complications: furuncle, carbuncle, abscess, cellulitis, regional lymphadenitis, and rarely systemic/bacteremic spread. Recurrence is common: more than half of patients with staphylococcal SSTI have at least one recurrence (PMID: 30859379).

13. Prevention

  • Primary: behavioral — shaving modification (or beard growth), single-use clean razors, avoiding shared razors, good hygiene, and reducing S. aureus carriage.
  • Secondary: early antibiotic treatment of superficial folliculitis to prevent progression to deep sycosis/furuncle/carbuncle.
  • Tertiary: decolonization and shaving cessation to prevent recurrence and scarring.
  • Immunization: NOT AVAILABLE — no licensed S. aureus vaccine exists; multiple candidates have failed clinical endpoints, and natural colonization/infection does not elicit protective immunity because of extensive immune-evasion factors: "S. aureus colonization and invasive disease are not associated with the development of protective immune responses, which is attributable to a large spectrum of immune evasion factors." (PMID: 27526714).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

Human sycosis barbae affects Homo sapiens (NCBI Taxonomy txid9606); its agent is S. aureus (txid1280). A close natural analog is canine superficial bacterial folliculitis (SBF), the most common bacterial skin disease of dogs, caused principally by Staphylococcus pseudintermedius (txid283734), with neutrophilic follicular pustules containing intracellular cocci—histologically parallel to human staphylococcal folliculitis. Shared antimicrobial-resistance dynamics (MRSP, MRSA) and shared topical antiseptic management (dilute bleach, chlorhexidine, benzoyl peroxide) further underscore the comparative relevance (PMID: 24345778; PMID: 27426474). Zoonotic note: the tinea barbae mimic is often zoophilic (livestock-acquired), whereas bacterial sycosis barbae itself is not classically zoonotic.

15. Model Organisms

There is no dedicated genetic model of "sycosis barbae." The pathophysiology is studied in murine S. aureus skin and soft-tissue infection models, including LysM-EGFP reporter mice for noninvasive neutrophil imaging and MyD88-deficient strains for innate-immunity dissection (PMID: 30882782; PMID: 25681348; PMID: 26351278; PMID: 32946878). Recapitulation: these models faithfully reproduce neutrophilic abscess formation, toxin-mediated dermonecrosis, and cytokine (IL-17/IL-22) control of bacterial burden. Limitations: mice are more resistant to several human-adapted S. aureus virulence factors, and no model reproduces the beard-follicle-specific, shaving-triggered anatomy of human sycosis barbae. The dog (SBF) serves as a naturally occurring large-animal comparator.

The unifying interpretation is that sycosis barbae is a host-defense outcome: neutrophilic walling-off is simultaneously the protective response and the histologic lesion (the abscess). Disease severity is set by the balance between toxin output (PSMs, Hla) and the speed/adequacy of the IL-17/IL-22 → neutrophil axis. This explains why the three cornerstones of therapy—killing the organism (antibiotics), removing the reservoir (decolonization), and removing the trigger (shaving modification)—map directly onto the causal chain, and why host immune defects and persistent nasal carriage predict the chronic/relapsing phenotype.
