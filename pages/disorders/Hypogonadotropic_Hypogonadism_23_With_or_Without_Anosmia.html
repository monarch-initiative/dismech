Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism 23 With or Without Anosmia

Mendelian MONDO:0009223 Pathograph 23 Show in embeddings browser Congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism Leydig cell hypoplasia

An autosomal recessive form of hypogonadism caused by biallelic inactivating variants in LHB, the gene for the hormone-specific beta subunit of luteinizing hormone (LH). Because only the LH beta chain is affected, the defect is selective: the pituitary still makes FSH, and the common alpha subunit shared with FSH, TSH and hCG is intact. Reported variants act at different steps (failed heterodimerization with the alpha subunit, intracellular retention of the hormone, aberrant splicing, premature termination), but the common end point is the absence of biologically active LH in the circulation. Some patients have immunoreactive LH that is bioinactive, so an assay result in the normal range does not exclude the diagnosis. The consequences depend on sex. Males are born with normal male genitalia and descended testes, because fetal Leydig cells are driven by placental hCG rather than by pituitary LH, and present in adolescence or adulthood with absent or delayed puberty, low testosterone, small testes with immature Leydig cells, eunuchoid proportions and azoospermia. Females go through spontaneous breast development and menarche, because early follicle growth and enough estradiol for puberty are FSH-dependent, and then present with oligomenorrhea or secondary amenorrhea and infertility from anovulation. FSH is normal or raised in both sexes. The condition is very rare: a 2023 systematic review counted 11 published males and 3 females. LH is the missing signal and its target cells are intact, so the disorder responds to hCG, which acts on the same receptor. hCG restores testosterone and can induce spermatogenesis in men, and LH followed by hCG has achieved ovulation and a live birth in a woman.

Pathophysiology

5
Biallelic LHB Inactivating Variants
LHB encodes the beta subunit that gives LH its receptor specificity. Pathogenic variants are biallelic and of several classes: missense changes in the cystine knot (p.Gly36Asp, p.Cys46Arg), in-frame deletions (p.Lys40del, a four-leucine deletion in the signal peptide), splice-site variants in intron 2 (IVS2+1G>C, IVS2+1G>T), a nonsense variant (p.Trp28*) and a frameshift. All abolish production of bioactive LH, which is why the impact is recorded as loss of function regardless of the step affected.

Leydig Cell Hypoplasia and Testosterone Deficiency
Without LH stimulation after birth, testicular Leydig cells stay undifferentiated and immature, testosterone synthesis is low, and the testes remain small. Prenatal male development is normal because placental hCG drives fetal Leydig cells through the same receptor. The Leydig cells keep their responsiveness to LH, which is why hCG restores testosterone production.

Impaired Spermatogenesis
Germ cell maturation fails for lack of intratesticular testosterone, giving azoospermia in most genotyped men until they are treated. The degree depends on residual LH activity: a man with a hypomorphic LHB allele whose LH kept low partial activity had complete, quantitatively normal spermatogenesis despite absent virilization, because a few mature Leydig cells made enough intratesticular testosterone. This is the pattern of the eponymous "fertile eunuch". In Lhb-null mice spermatogenesis stops at the round spermatid.

description: >-
  An autosomal recessive form of hypogonadism caused by biallelic inactivating variants in LHB,
  the gene for the hormone-specific beta subunit of luteinizing hormone (LH). Because only the LH
  beta chain is affected, the defect is selective: the pituitary still makes FSH, and the common
  alpha subunit shared with FSH, TSH and hCG is intact. Reported variants act at different steps
  (failed heterodimerization with the alpha subunit, intracellular retention of the hormone,
  aberrant splicing, premature termination), but the common end point is the absence of
  biologically active LH in the circulation. Some patients have immunoreactive LH that is
  bioinactive, so an assay result in the normal range does not exclude the diagnosis.

  The consequences depend on sex. Males are born with normal male genitalia and descended
  testes, because fetal Leydig cells are driven by placental hCG rather than by pituitary LH,
  and present in adolescence or adulthood with absent or delayed puberty, low testosterone,
  small testes with immature Leydig cells, eunuchoid proportions and azoospermia. Females go
  through spontaneous breast development and menarche, because early follicle growth and enough
  estradiol for puberty are FSH-dependent, and then present with oligomenorrhea or secondary
  amenorrhea and infertility from anovulation. FSH is normal or raised in both sexes.

  hCG, which acts on the same receptor. hCG restores testosterone and can induce spermatogenesis

Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism 23 With or Without Anosmia (HH23) — Comprehensive Disease Report

Disease: Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism 23 With or Without Anosmia MONDO ID: MONDO:0009223 · OMIM: #228300 · Category: Mendelian (autosomal recessive) Causal gene: LHB (luteinizing hormone subunit beta, 19q13.33)

Summary

Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism 23 With or Without Anosmia (HH23) is, despite its name, an autosomal-recessive isolated luteinizing-hormone (LH) deficiency caused by biallelic loss-of-function or bioinactivating mutations in the LHB gene encoding the β-subunit of luteinizing hormone. The disease corresponds historically to "Pasqualini syndrome" and the "fertile eunuch" phenotype. The core lesion is the absence of a functional bioactive LH heterodimer, which leaves the intact LH/choriogonadotropin receptor (LHCGR) unstimulated and silences the downstream cAMP/PKA/StAR steroidogenic program in gonadal (Leydig and theca/granulosa) cells. GnRH secretion, FSH, the LH receptor, and the gonads themselves are intrinsically normal — the defect is confined to the LH ligand.

A critical clarification arising from this investigation: anosmia is NOT an intrinsic feature of HH23. The "With or Without Anosmia" phrasing is a naming artifact inherited from the OMIM/MONDO hypogonadotropic-hypogonadism numbered series (which groups Kallmann-syndrome loci alongside isolated gonadotropin-subunit defects). HH23/LHB deficiency is a normosmic, non-Kallmann condition. The olfactory system is unaffected because the pathology lies in gonadotrope hormone production, not in embryonic GnRH-neuron/olfactory-placode migration.

The phenotype is sex-dimorphic and highly treatable. Males present with severe disease — absent or incomplete puberty, delayed virilization, small testes, Leydig-cell hypoplasia, and azoospermia or markedly impaired spermatogenesis, with undetectable/low LH but normal FSH and low testosterone. Females show a milder picture — normal female external genitalia, spontaneous breast and pubic-hair development, normal or late menarche, followed by secondary oligo-/amenorrhea and anovulatory infertility. Because the receptor and gonad are intact, hCG (which binds the same LHCGR) restores steroidogenesis, spermatogenesis, ovulation and fertility, and in females pulsatile GnRH or LH/hCG ovulation induction has produced healthy term pregnancies. The biochemical hallmark — low LH with normal FSH and preserved hCG responsiveness — distinguishes HH23 both from classic GnRH-deficient CHH (where both gonadotropins are low) and from LHCGR inactivation (where LH is high and the patient is hormone-resistant).

Key Findings

Finding 1 — HH23 is caused by biallelic inactivating LHB mutations (isolated LH deficiency, not Kallmann syndrome)

HH23 (MONDO:0009223 / OMIM #228300) results from biallelic (homozygous or compound-heterozygous) loss-of-function or bioinactivating variants in LHB, the gene encoding the hormone-specific β-subunit of luteinizing hormone on 19q13.33. This is an isolated LH deficiency, mechanistically and clinically distinct from Kallmann syndrome (which is caused by genes governing GnRH-neuron/olfactory migration such as ANOS1/KAL1, FGFR1, PROKR2, PROK2, FGF8, SOX10). The "with or without anosmia" descriptor is a series-naming artifact; olfaction is intrinsically preserved.

Finding 2 — hCG (and pulsatile GnRH/LH in females) restores gonadal function and fertility

Because HH23 is a ligand-replaceable deficiency with an intact receptor and gonad, treatment is LH replacement. hCG binds and activates the same LHCGR, driving steroidogenesis and gametogenesis.

  • Male, Trp28X: after 40 days of hCG, a few spermatozoa appeared; by 6 months, normal seminal parameters (PMID: 29476300).
  • Male brothers, Lys40del: hCG induced pubertal development, rising testosterone, and spermatogenesis (PMID: 27656125).
  • Female, IVS2+1G>C: ovulation induction with recombinant LH then urinary hCG achieved follicle growth, ovulation, and a healthy term pregnancy (PMID: 34535998).
  • Female, compound het F84L+G122S: pulsatile GnRH promoted puberty and ovulation (PMID: 39786527).

Males (severe): pubertal delay/absent virilization, small testes, azoospermia or variable spermatogenesis, undetectable/low LH with normal FSH, low testosterone, and absent or few fetal Leydig cells (Leydig-cell hypoplasia) ([PMIDs: 27656125, 19890128, 29476300]).

Females (milder): normal female external genitalia; spontaneous thelarche and pubarche; normal or late menarche followed by secondary oligo-/amenorrhea and anovulatory infertility; estradiol and progesterone reach follicular but not ovulatory/luteal levels; low LH with normal/slightly raised FSH; ovaries normal or enlarged with cysts ([PMIDs: 19129711, 17761593]).

The sex dimorphism is explained developmentally: in females, fetal ovarian development and early puberty are largely FSH- and placental-hCG-dependent, so early pubertal milestones proceed; LH is required specifically for the ovulatory surge and corpus-luteum/progesterone function, so the deficit manifests as anovulation after menarche. In males, fetal Leydig-cell testosterone production (driving virilization of the external genitalia and later pubertal maturation) depends on LH/hCG, so loss causes more profound and earlier manifestations.

Finding 4 — Mechanism: LHB loss-of-function abrogates bioactive LH, silencing the LHCGR–cAMP/PKA–StAR steroidogenic cascade

LH is an αβ heterodimer: a shared α-subunit (encoded by CGA) non-covalently associated with the hormone-specific LHB β-subunit. Different LHB mutation classes disrupt the hormone in distinct ways:

  • Missense/in-frame deletion (e.g., Lys40del): the mutant subunit is retained intracellularly (diffuse cytoplasmic distribution) and is not secreted, even though it can still heterodimerize with the α-subunit and, in vitro, activate the receptor. Lys40 lies in a conserved trafficking determinant shared across the hCG/FSH/TSH β-subunits (PMID: 27656125).
  • Splice/nonsense (IVS2+1G>C, Trp28X): abolish LH synthesis or secretion outright ([PMIDs: 17761593, 29476300]).
  • Bioinactive missense (Gln54Arg): a hormone that is immunoreactive but biologically inactive — it cannot productively engage/activate LHCGR (PMID: 1727547).

Downstream, LH/hCG act through LHCGR, a Gs-coupled GPCR, activating cAMP → PKA → CREB, which induces StAR (steroidogenic acute regulatory protein) and steroidogenic enzymes (CYP11A1, HSD3B) to drive testosterone/progesterone synthesis ([PMIDs: 33112765, 38621656]). Loss of bioactive LH therefore silences this cascade at its most upstream (ligand) node.

"the mutant LH is retained intracellularly and showed diffuse cytoplasmic distribution. The mutated LHB heterodimerizes with the common alpha-subunit and can activate its receptor." — PMID: 27656125

Mouse models recapitulate the disease: Lhb-knockout mice show hypogonadism with reduced androgen-dependent testicular gene programs (e.g., Slc7a11), and female Lhb-KO mice are close phenocopies of human LHB mutations ([PMIDs: 35398053, 19129711]).

Diagnostic pitfall — bioinactive LH: The Gln54Arg hormone is immunoreactive but biologically inactive. A standard immunoassay may therefore report "detectable/normal" LH that is in fact non-functional. In a patient with hypogonadism and a normal-appearing LH immunoassay, discordance between immunoreactivity and bioactivity (or molecular testing) is required to avoid missing the diagnosis (PMID: 1727547).

Finding 6 — Minimal residual intratesticular testosterone suffices for complete spermatogenesis (molecular basis of the "fertile eunuch")

A man with a hypomorphic LHB mutation had undetectable serum LH and low serum testosterone, yet complete, quantitatively normal spermatogenesis. The mutant LH retained low partial in-vitro activity, sufficient to drive steroidogenic-enzyme expression in a few mature Leydig cells that produced only ~20.2 ng/g intratesticular testosterone — enough for normal spermatogenesis despite absent virilization (PMID: 19890128). This dissociation of poor virilization from preserved fertility is the molecular basis of the historical "fertile eunuch"/Pasqualini phenotype (e.g., Trp8Arg+Ile15Thr; PMID: 14709845) and challenges the dogma about the high intratesticular-testosterone threshold required for spermatogenesis (PMID: 34884539).

"the residual luteinizing hormone activity, resulting in the expression of steroidogenic enzymes in few mature Leydig cells producing small amounts of intratesticular testosterone (20.2 ng per gram), was sufficient for complete and quantitatively normal spermatogenesis" — PMID: 19890128

"Normal virilization and improved semen parameters were achieved after hCG supplementation." — PMID: 14709845

Finding 7 — Historical landmark: Weiss 1992 established LH-beta deficiency as a distinct cause of hypogonadism

The first human LHB mutation was reported by Weiss et al., N Engl J Med 1992;326(3):179–183 ("Hypogonadism caused by a single amino acid substitution in the beta subunit of luteinizing hormone"; PMID: 1727547). They described a homozygous Gln54Arg missense variant in a man with delayed puberty/hypogonadism. The mutant LH was immunoreactive but biologically inactive — the first molecular proof that an intrinsic LH-beta defect (rather than GnRH deficiency or pituitary failure) can cause hypogonadism. Bibliographic details were verified via NCBI E-utilities.

Anatomical Structures, Temporal Course, and Epidemiology

Anatomy / organ level (UBERON/CL): Endocrine axis — pituitary gonadotrope (CL:0000173) as the site of the production defect; primary target organs are the testis (UBERON:0000473) Leydig/interstitial cells (CL:0000178) and the ovary (UBERON:0000992) theca (CL:0000503) and granulosa cells (CL:0000501). Subcellular compartments: endoplasmic reticulum (trafficking-retained mutants), extracellular space (secreted hormone), and mitochondrial/ER steroidogenic machinery (StAR/CYP11A1). Secondary manifestations: reduced bone mineralization and body-composition changes expected with chronic sex-steroid deficiency (general hypogonadism complications; not disease-specific data).

Temporal development: Congenital/germline genetic basis with onset recognized at the expected pubertal window — males present with absent/incomplete puberty and small testes; females present at or after the normal menarcheal age with secondary amenorrhea. Course is chronic and lifelong at the level of the endogenous deficiency but is not progressive; it is fully controllable with replacement therapy. The pubertal window is the critical period for diagnosis and for virilization/breast development; fertility can be induced later with hCG/LH or pulsatile GnRH.

Epidemiology: Inactivating LHB mutations are "exceptionally rare" (PMID: 27656125) — fewer than ~20 unrelated families reported globally; no reliable prevalence/incidence estimate exists (ultra-rare). Inheritance is autosomal recessive; most reported probands are homozygous, several from consanguineous unions, implying a contributing role for consanguinity and founder/private alleles. Penetrance of biochemical hypogonadism appears high in biallelic carriers, with variable expressivity (severe azoospermia through fertile-eunuch depending on residual allele activity). Heterozygous carriers are clinically unaffected. No genetic anticipation (not a repeat-expansion disorder). Both sexes affected; males are more severely affected.

Diagnostics (Synthesis)

  • Hormonal profile: low/undetectable LH, normal FSH, low sex steroids (testosterone in males; failure to reach ovulatory estradiol/luteal progesterone in females). This FSH-normal, LH-low pattern is the biochemical signature.
  • Caution: some bioinactive mutants (Gln54Arg) are immunoreactive, so immunoassay LH may appear normal — bioassay discordance or molecular testing resolves this (PMID: 1727547).
  • hCG stimulation test: a rise in testosterone/steroids confirms an intact receptor/gonad and supports LHB (vs LHCGR) etiology (PMID: 19129711).
  • Genetic testing: single-gene LHB sequencing, or CHH/hypogonadism gene panels / whole-exome sequencing. Given ultra-rarity and phenotype overlap, panel/WES is practical; confirm biallelic status and segregation.
  • Imaging: small testes on exam/ultrasound (males); normal or cystic/enlarged ovaries (females). Normal olfaction on formal smell testing distinguishes from Kallmann syndrome; normal MRI of olfactory bulbs expected (not routinely required).

Treatment, Prognosis, and Prevention

Treatment. HH23 is among the most treatable hypogonadotropic disorders because the receptor and gonad are intact: - Virilization / sex-steroid replacement: testosterone (males), estrogen/progestin (females) for secondary sexual characteristics and bone health when fertility is not the immediate goal. - Fertility induction: hCG (acts on LHCGR) ± FSH in males to initiate/restore spermatogenesis ([PMIDs: 29476300, 27656125, 14709845]); recombinant LH / hCG ovulation induction or pulsatile GnRH in females ([PMIDs: 34535998, 39786527]). - Documented outcomes include normalization of seminal parameters within months and a healthy term pregnancy — excellent prognosis for both sexual maturation and fertility with treatment.

Prognosis. Life expectancy is normal; morbidity relates to untreated hypogonadism (infertility, reduced bone density, psychosocial impact of delayed puberty) and is largely reversible with therapy. No disease-specific mortality.

Prevention / counseling. No primary prevention (germline Mendelian disorder). Genetic counseling for autosomal-recessive recurrence risk (25% in carrier × carrier couples), cascade carrier testing of relatives, and reproductive options (prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing) are appropriate given identified familial variants. Carrier frequency is presumably very low (ultra-rare alleles; check gnomAD for specific variants).

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Ultra-rarity. The entire human evidence base is a handful of case reports/small families; no epidemiological prevalence, no natural-history cohort, and no quantitative penetrance/expressivity estimates exist. Frequency statements are qualitative.
  2. Name/ontology artifact. The OMIM/MONDO series name ("with or without anosmia") implies an olfactory feature that is not a genuine part of LHB deficiency; downstream knowledge bases may mis-associate HH23 with Kallmann phenotypes. This report explicitly flags anosmia as non-intrinsic.
  3. Mechanistic inference. The cAMP/PKA/StAR silencing step (chain step 4) is inferred from LHCGR receptor biology and model systems rather than demonstrated directly in LHB-deficient human gonadal tissue.
  4. Modifier genes, epigenetics, and QoL are unstudied for this specific disorder; no disease-specific quality-of-life, modifier-allele, or methylation data are available.
  5. Genotype–phenotype granularity. The allele-activity-to-phenotype relationship (severe vs fertile-eunuch) is supported by individual cases but lacks a systematic functional allelic series.

Proposed Follow-up Actions

  1. Curate an international LHB-mutation registry to aggregate the scattered case reports and derive penetrance, expressivity, and genotype–phenotype (allele residual activity vs virilization/fertility) relationships.
  2. Standardized functional assays (secretion/trafficking + LHCGR activation) for each reported and novel LHB variant to build a quantitative allelic series predicting fertile-eunuch vs severe phenotypes.
