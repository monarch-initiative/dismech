ROHHAD Syndrome

MONDO:0017408 Pathograph 26 Show in embeddings browser endocrine system disorder

Rapid-onset obesity with hypothalamic dysfunction, hypoventilation and autonomic dysregulation (ROHHAD) is an ultra-rare syndrome that begins in previously healthy children. Its usual sequence is rapid weight gain in early childhood, then hypothalamic dysfunction, then autonomic dysregulation and alveolar hypoventilation, which can end in cardiorespiratory arrest. A neural crest tumour, most often a ganglioneuroma, was reported in 56 per cent of the patients of one systematic review, and the syndrome is then written ROHHAD(NET), ROHHADNET or ROHHAD-NET. The cause is unknown. No disease-defining gene has been found. Autoantibodies to the protein ZSCAN1 have been found in patients with and without a tumour, but the search for an autoimmune basis has not given a definitive answer. No diagnostic biomarker is established, so the diagnosis is clinical, and with no cure the care is supportive: assisted ventilation and hormone replacement.

An immune response against the nervous system, in some patients set off by a neural crest tumour, causes the syndrome
paraneoplastic_autoimmune EMERGING
Evidence balance 3 support 1 refute
The syndrome is proposed to be immune-mediated, and paraneoplastic where a neural crest tumour is present. In favour: ZSCAN1 autoantibodies in patients with and without a tumour, intrathecal oligoclonal bands, improvement after immune ablation in a few children, and the failure of sequencing studies to find a gene. Against a settled answer: the antibodies are not shown to be pathogenic, immunotherapy has helped some patients and not others, and a 2023 collaborative review concluded that the search for an autoimmune basis had not given a definitive answer (see the discussions). The two edges that belong to this hypothesis run from Neural Crest Tumour to Anti-Neural Autoimmunity and from there to Hypothalamic Dysfunction.

A single inherited or de novo gene defect causes the syndrome
monogenic_germline ALTERNATIVE
Evidence balance 4 support 7 refute
The syndrome was thought to be a genetic disease caused by de novo mutations, because many of its features recall genetic disorders such as congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, and a recurrence in siblings was read as pointing to a single gene. The search has been negative so far. Candidate genes for hypothalamic, autonomic and respiratory control were sequenced without result; exome sequencing of seven trios, of tumours and of a discordant monozygotic twin pair found no gene with de novo variants in two patients and no coding difference between the twins; and whole genome sequencing in two later studies found no shared variant. The authors of the latest study say that their findings do not exclude a genetic cause. No edge of this entry belongs to the hypothesis, because no gene or locus is known to draw one from.

Neural Crest Tumour
A neural crest tumour was reported in 56 per cent of the patients in a systematic review of 43 individual patient descriptions, and 70 per cent of those tumours were diagnosed within two years of the first weight gain; the other 44 per cent had no tumour reported. Ganglioneuroma is the commonest type, and ganglioneuroblastoma also occurs. An earlier series of 13 patients put the proportion as high as 40 per cent and described the tumours as usually differentiated and without a significant effect on prognosis. Whether the tumour starts the syndrome or only accompanies it is not settled. One hypothesis holds that, where there is a tumour, it sets off an immune response against the nervous system (see the edge to Anti-Neural Autoimmunity).

Anti-Neural Autoimmunity
Autoantibodies to the zinc finger protein ZSCAN1 were validated in 7 of 9 children in a study of paraneoplastic ROHHAD and in none of 125 controls. A second group then found them in patients without a tumour: of 6 with paired serum and cerebrospinal fluid, 5 (4 children and 1 adult) had the antibodies in cerebrospinal fluid only and 1 in both, while 7 further patients with serum only and 199 controls were negative. A third group reported serum positivity in over 85 per cent of its patients. A Japanese nationwide survey, which tested for anti-ZSCAN1 and anti-Nax antibodies, found antibodies in 70 per cent of 30 cases; its abstract does not divide the figure between the two. Other signs of an immune process come from one or two patients each: intrathecal oligoclonal bands, antibodies against hypothalamus and pituitary, and inflammatory cerebrospinal fluid with focal inflammation of the hypothalamus and periaqueductal grey matter on imaging. None of this shows that the antibodies cause the disease (see the discussions).

Hypothalamic Dysfunction
The rapid weight gain is regarded as the first sign of hypothalamic dysfunction, and hormonal and water-balance abnormalities follow. In a series of six children, hyperprolactinaemia was found in all six, complete growth hormone deficiency and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism in four each, abnormal thyroid tests in five of five, and a raised serum sodium without diabetes insipidus in all. What damages the hypothalamus is not known.

Impaired Central Control of Breathing
Hypoventilation is a defining feature. Awake ventilatory challenge testing in seven patients found a diminished tidal-volume and inspiratory-drive response to hypoxic hypercapnia and no behavioural awareness of the compromise, while most other measures resembled those of published controls, which the authors read as a mild chemosensory deficit. In a series of six children followed with polysomnography, four had obstructive sleep apnoea at first screening, all those followed later had nocturnal hypoventilation, and three showed irregular breathing while awake.

Autonomic Dysregulation
Symptoms of autonomic dysregulation began at a median of 3.6 years in the series that named the syndrome, and a systematic review puts dysautonomia at a median of 4.95 years. A series of 10 children found it in all, with strabismus the commonest sign. Reported signs also include disordered temperature control, abnormal pupils, bradycardia that needed a pacemaker, a raised pain threshold and gut dysmotility. Its cause is as unknown as that of the hypothalamic dysfunction, and no edge joins the two nodes.
