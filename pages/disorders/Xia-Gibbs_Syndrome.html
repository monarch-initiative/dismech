Xia-Gibbs Syndrome

Mendelian MONDO:0014358 Pathograph 20 Show in embeddings browser Syndromic Intellectual Disability Mendelian Neurodevelopmental Disorder

Xia-Gibbs syndrome (XGS) is a rare autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in AHDC1 (1p36.11), which encodes the AT-hook DNA-binding motif containing protein 1 (Gibbin). Most variants are de novo nonsense or frameshift changes predicted to truncate the protein; de novo missense variants clustering near the second AT-hook and a REV3L-homologous region, and rare mosaic cases, have also been reported. Affected individuals present in infancy with hypotonia and delayed motor milestones, followed by speech delay (often severely limited or absent speech) and intellectual disability. Variable features include obstructive sleep apnea, structural brain anomalies (hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, posterior fossa or retrocerebellar cysts), seizures, ataxia and other movement disorders, autism spectrum features, strabismus, scoliosis, short stature, laryngomalacia and nonspecific mild facial dysmorphism. AHDC1 is a nuclear protein of largely undefined function; how its truncation produces the neurodevelopmental phenotype is not known.

ClinGen records haploinsufficiency as the disease mechanism, but the single coding exon predicts escape from nonsense-mediated decay (confirmed in one patient's sample), missense variants cluster in conserved domains, truncated proteins mislocalize in a position-dependent way, and a deletion of one allele did not lower total AHDC1 expression in blood. Pathophysiology

5
AHDC1 Truncating Variant
A heterozygous, usually de novo, nonsense or frameshift variant in the single coding exon of AHDC1. Because the variant lies in the last (only) coding exon, the mutant transcript is predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay and could encode a truncated protein lacking the AT-hook DNA-binding motifs and conserved regions downstream of the truncation. Escape from nonsense-mediated decay has been confirmed in one patient's sample. Whether the variant acts through loss of function or a dominant-negative effect has not been established.

Abnormal Nuclear Localization of Truncated AHDC1

Wild-type AHDC1 is nuclear. Truncated forms modelled on patient alleles show either a diffuse distribution (short N-terminal fragments) or aggregation in the nucleolus (mid-protein truncations). Dysregulated Gibbin-Dependent Mesodermal Gene Expression
Mechanism confidence: Provisional
Gibbin binds enhancers and promoters together with many zinc-finger transcription factors and methyl-CpG-binding proteins. Its loss increases DNA methylation at GATA3-dependent mesodermal genes, impairs dermal maturation in skin organoids and, in chimeric mouse mutants, produces craniofacial, abdominal wall and epidermal patterning defects. The study proposes that some XGS features arise from abnormal mesoderm maturation; how far this explains the neurodevelopmental phenotype has not been tested.

1. Disease Information

Overview. XGS is a neurodevelopmental disorder. Its main features are: - hypotonia in infancy; - global developmental delay, with motor milestones especially late; - severely limited or absent expressive speech; - moderate-to-severe intellectual disability; - structural brain anomalies, most often a thin corpus callosum; - mild, nonspecific facial dysmorphism.

Variable additional features include obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), movement disorders (ataxia, tremor, bradykinesia), seizures, short stature, scoliosis, strabismus, autism spectrum disorder and laryngomalacia.

  • GeneReviews (Murdock et al., PMID:34902026) [cache]: "The main features of Xia-Gibbs syndrome (XGS), present in a majority of affected individuals, include delayed motor milestones, speech delay with severely limited or absent speech, moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment, hypotonia, structural brain anomalies, and nonspecific dysmorphic features."
  • The discovery paper (Xia et al. 2014, PMID:24791903) [cache]: "we identified AHDC1 de novo truncating mutations that most likely cause this genetic syndrome."

2. Etiology

Primary cause: heterozygous, usually de novo, pathogenic variants in AHDC1: - Protein-truncating variants (most cases): nonsense and frameshift variants in the single coding exon. - Missense variants: a minority, clustered in conserved domains (PMID:34950897). - Deletions: whole-gene or partial AHDC1 deletions and 1p36.11 contiguous deletions (PMID:36054313, PMID:39359946, PMID:30152016). - Structural rearrangement: one de novo balanced translocation with a breakpoint in AHDC1 intron 1, in a boy with a bicuspid aortic valve, aortic coarctation and PDA (Quintero-Rivera et al. 2015, PMID:25574029). The paper's title centres on MATR3; secondary sources attribute the AHDC1 breakpoint to it. Verify before citing.

Genetic risk factors - Causal: AHDC1 is the only established gene. - Modifier genes: none established. - Co-occurring diagnoses: a second Mendelian diagnosis can deepen the phenotype. A Turkish cohort of 5 patients had one with a concurrent CDK13 diagnosis that explained a more severe presentation (PMID:42136190, 2026). - Obstructive sleep apnea: one study reported rare AHDC1 variants in patients with OSA (PMID:31737670, Biomed Res Int 2019). This is not established as a susceptibility locus.

Environmental risk factors: none known. Parental age has not been shown to matter.

Protective factors: none known, genetic or environmental.

Gene–environment interactions: none described.

Parental mosaicism: - Maternal germline mosaicism caused recurrence: a prenatal phenotype of hypoplastic corpus callosum recurred in two pregnancies (PMID:31812316, Eur J Obstet Gynecol Reprod Biol 2020). - Somatic mosaicism in a proband (PMID:34229113 [cache]): "we present a case of Xia-Gibbs syndrome caused by mosaicism for an AHDC1 variant." - "Double mosaicism": two adjacent de novo AHDC1 variants lying in trans on the same parental haplotype, which implies a post-zygotic origin (PMID:41738116, 2026).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Gene structure: - AHDC1, chr1p36.11; NCBI Gene ID for human AHDC1 not checked (look up). - The whole open reading frame is in one coding exon. Pathogenic truncations therefore fall in the last coding exon and are expected to escape nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). - PMID:39835152 [cache]: "Notably, only the sixth exon of the AHDC1 gene encodes protein, while the rest are non-coding regions."

Protein: AHDC1/"Gibbin" is a 1,603-aa nuclear protein with AT-hook DNA-binding motifs. Its functional domains are poorly defined (PMID:39835152).

Chromosomal abnormalities: 1p36.11 interstitial deletions and duplications that include AHDC1 (PMID:30615951, PMID:28841002, PMID:36054313). Distal 1p36 deletion syndrome and Radio-Tartaglia syndrome (SPEN) are the differential diagnoses for this region.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Heterozygous de novo AHDC1 variant (initiating lesion)
  2. Truncating variants in the single coding exon are the commonest; missense variants and deletions also occur.
  3. This leads to either reduced functional Gibbin (haploinsufficiency) or production of NMD-escaping truncated Gibbin. (Demonstrated: NMD escape, PMID:41653504; deletions causal, PMID:39359946.)
  4. The mechanism branches here:
  5. 2a. Truncated Gibbin mislocalises (diffuse nuclear distribution, or nucleolar aggregation). This results in loss of normal chromatin-associated function, and possibly interference with the wild-type allele. (The localisation is demonstrated in vitro by overexpression, PMID:33644933. Dominant-negative action is inferred, not shown.)
  6. 2b. Reduced Gibbin dosage (deletions) results in the same downstream loss of function. (Inferred; one deletion showed compensated expression, PMID:36054313.)
  7. Loss of Gibbin at enhancers and promoters, where it interacts with zinc-finger transcription factors and methyl-CpG-binding proteins, leads to aberrant gene-specific DNA hypermethylation and disrupted promoter–enhancer contacts.
  8. PMID:35585237 [cache]: "The loss of Gibbin causes an increase in DNA methylation at GATA3-dependent mesodermal genes, resulting in a loss of signalling between developing dermal and epidermal cell types."
  9. (Demonstrated in hESC/organoid and mouse; neural tissue not yet studied.)
  10. Dysregulated transcription of lineage-specification genes leads to abnormal mesoderm maturation and defective signalling between mesoderm and ectoderm. This results in patterning defects affecting craniofacial structure, the epidermis and the abdominal wall.
  11. These correspond to the dysmorphism, aplasia cutis and craniosynostosis seen in patients.
  12. PMID:35585237 [cache]: "In vivo chimeric CRISPR mouse mutants reveal a spectrum of Gibbin-dependent developmental patterning defects affecting craniofacial structure, abdominal wall closure and epidermal stratification that mirror patient phenotypes."
  13. Chromatin and transcriptional dysregulation in the developing brain is proposed to lead to abnormal neurodevelopment: corpus callosum hypoplasia, posterior fossa anomalies and delayed myelination. These in turn result in developmental delay, ID, absent speech, autism, seizures and later ataxia or tremor.
  14. (Largely inferred. PMID:39835152 proposes an analogy with MECP2 and ADNP. No neural-specific mechanistic study exists.)
  15. Branch — airway, tone and growth:
  16. Hypotonia together with upper-airway anomalies (laryngomalacia) leads to OSA. (Clinically inferred.)
  17. Partial GH deficiency in some patients leads to short stature (PMID:30729726).
  18. Branch — metabolism:
  19. In mice, Ahdc1 deficiency leads to reduced energy expenditure, then adiposity and obesity with hyperleptinemia, insulin resistance and fatty liver (PMID:37819197 [cache]).
  20. This may relate to the hyperphagia reported in humans (PMID:40501103). (The link is inferred.)

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: congenital. Corpus callosum hypoplasia can be detected prenatally (PMID:31812316). Hypotonia and feeding problems appear in the neonatal or infantile period.
  • Course: a static neurodevelopmental disorder overall, with features emerging over time:
  • movement disorders (ataxia, tremor, bradykinesia) in childhood or adolescence;
  • scoliosis at 10–21 years;
  • seizures at a median of 4 years;
  • regression in a subset (PMID:40501103).
  • Duration: lifelong. Adults have been described (PMID:30622101; the age range widened in Jiang 2018).
  • Critical periods:
  • early childhood, for speech, communication and AAC therapy;
  • childhood, for sleep-apnea screening;
  • adolescence, for scoliosis and movement-disorder surveillance.
  • Remission: none.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: autosomal dominant (HP:0000006); almost all cases de novo.
  • Somatic mosaicism: documented in probands (PMID:34229113, PMID:41738116), so HP:0001442 is a candidate. Apply the somatic-mosaicism skill tiers before binding it, because parental germline mosaicism (PMID:31812316) must not be bound.
  • Penetrance: presumed complete for truncating variants. Expressivity: highly variable, including between identical twins (PMID:35636160).
  • Anticipation: none. Founder effects: none. Consanguinity: not relevant. Carrier frequency: not applicable.
  • Prevalence: unknown. There are about 390 or more reported cases worldwide (2025), across many countries: USA, Europe, China, Japan, Brazil, India, Turkey, Iran and the DR Congo. A suitable prevalence_class is NOT_YET_DOCUMENTED, or RARE with measure_type: CASES_IN_LITERATURE.
  • Sex ratio:
  • Roughly balanced: 9 female and 11 male in Jiang 2018.
  • Males may be more severely affected (Jiang 2018), but this is not consistently replicated.
  • Ethnicity: the registry was 90% White (ascertainment bias). Cohorts are now published from multiple ancestries.

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical criteria: none. Diagnosis is genetic, because the phenotype is nonspecific.
  • Jiang 2018 [cache]: "making DNA testing and the establishment of molecular diagnosis the primary diagnostic tool for XGS."
  • GeneReviews [cache]: "The diagnosis of XGS is established in a proband with suggestive clinical findings and a heterozygous pathogenic variant in AHDC1 that is predicted to lead to a truncated protein, identified by molecular genetic testing"
  • Genetic testing:
  • First tier: exome or genome sequencing (trio preferred), or an ID/autism/neurodevelopmental gene panel that includes AHDC1.
  • Chromosomal microarray detects 1p36.11 deletions. Genome sequencing detects small intragenic or 5′ deletions (PMID:39359946), and long-read sequencing resolves complex cases (PMID:36054313, PMID:41738116).
  • Low-level mosaicism may need deep sequencing.
  • Karyotype and FISH have little role, except for balanced rearrangements.
  • Imaging: brain MRI (thin corpus callosum, posterior fossa cysts, ventricular anomalies, delayed myelination).
  • Other tests:
  • Polysomnography for OSA. Suggested NCIT term: verify "Polysomnography" before binding.
  • EEG.
  • Spine radiographs.
  • Ophthalmology and audiology.
  • GH stimulation testing when growth velocity is poor.
  • Biomarkers: none. No methylation episignature has been published.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • 1p36 deletion syndrome
  • Angelman syndrome (absent speech, ataxia, seizures)
  • Pitt-Hopkins syndrome
  • Prader-Willi syndrome (hypotonia, hyperphagia, OSA)
  • Other chromatin-regulator neurodevelopmental disorders: ADNP, MECP2, PRR12 (PMID:38798311), SPEN/Radio-Tartaglia (DOI:10.26815/acn.2024.00717)
  • RNU4-2-related neurodevelopmental disorder (PMID:38821540)
  • Mitochondrial and connective-tissue disorders, considered in the CHOP series
  • Screening: no newborn or carrier screening. Prenatal or preimplantation testing is possible once the familial variant is known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival: no mortality data. Survival into adulthood is documented (PMID:30622101), and life expectancy is not known to be reduced.
  • Morbidity:
  • lifelong ID and limited speech;
  • 80% achieve independent walking (median 2.5 y);
  • 90% follow simple commands.
  • Main complications: OSA (45%; some need CPAP), seizures (about 30%), scoliosis (20%, often surgical), movement disorders, behavioural and psychiatric problems.
  • Prognostic factors:
  • Male sex: possibly more severe.
  • N-terminal truncation: associated with seizures and scoliosis.
  • Age and variant type: predict phenotypic class (PMID:39648204).
  • Recovery: not applicable. Function improves with therapy, but the core deficits persist.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: not possible, because the variants are de novo.
  • Recurrence risk: low, but higher than the population risk because of parental germline mosaicism (PMID:31812316). Prenatal or preimplantation testing can be offered once the variant is known.
  • Tertiary prevention (surveillance, per GeneReviews):
  • growth and nutrition at every visit;
  • seizures and movement disorders;
  • scoliosis checks until skeletal maturity;
  • annual vision and hearing;
  • sleep and respiratory assessment;
  • GH levels if growth velocity is poor.
  • Vaccines, prophylaxis, public health: not applicable.
