Cataract 1 Multiple Types

Mendelian MONDO:0007285 Pathograph 35 Show in embeddings browser Early-onset non-syndromic cataract

Inherited, usually congenital, bilateral cataract caused by germline variants in GJA8 on 1q21, which encodes connexin 50 (Cx50), a gap junction protein of lens epithelial and fiber cells. Most families carry a heterozygous missense variant and show autosomal dominant transmission; the first allele, P88S, was found in the eight-generation British family in which zonular pulverulent cataract (the CZP1 locus) had been the first inherited disease linked to a human autosome. Cataract morphology varies between and within families (zonular or nuclear pulverulent, lamellar pulverulent, Y-sutural "balloon-like", "jellyfish-like", triangular nuclear, total), and many families also have microcornea, sometimes with myopia. A few missense alleles (G46E, D51N, G94R) are associated with microphthalmia, sclerocornea, coloboma, congenital aphakia or acorea together with cataract. Rare biallelic frameshift alleles cause recessive cataract, with mild or no lens opacity in heterozygous carriers. Mutant Cx50 fails to form functional channels, is retained intracellularly, or inhibits co-expressed wild-type Cx50 and Cx46; in mouse models the resulting loss of fiber cell coupling disrupts the lens internal circulation, so calcium accumulates and precipitates where the opacities form. Loss of Cx50 also curbs postnatal lens epithelial proliferation, which accounts for the small lens and eye in mice. Management is lens extraction with optical correction and amblyopia therapy; there is no genotype-directed treatment.

GJA8 Variant
Germline GJA8 variants alter connexin 50. Most are heterozygous missense substitutions, many in the first extracellular and transmembrane domains (P88S, P88Q, E48K, V44E, W45S, G46V, D47N, D51N, V79L); the recessive alleles are frameshifts that replace the C-terminal part of the protein. Different alleles act through different routes: absent channel function, failure to reach the plasma membrane, dominant inhibition of wild-type lens connexins, altered gating, or, for G46V, excess hemichannel activity.

Mutant Cx50 subunits (P88S, P88Q, S50P, G22R, the c.670insA frameshift) accumulate in cytoplasmic membranes or the endoplasmic reticulum instead of forming gap junction plaques. In mouse lenses carrying S50P or G22R, retained protein is accompanied by a modest unfolded protein response.

  Inherited, usually congenital, bilateral cataract caused by germline variants
  in GJA8 on 1q21, which encodes connexin 50 (Cx50), a gap junction protein of
  lens epithelial and fiber cells. Most families carry a heterozygous missense
  variant and show autosomal dominant transmission; the first allele, P88S, was
  found in the eight-generation British family in which zonular pulverulent
  cataract (the CZP1 locus) had been the first inherited disease linked to a
  human autosome. Cataract morphology varies between and within families
  (zonular or nuclear pulverulent, lamellar pulverulent, Y-sutural

  families also have microcornea, sometimes with myopia. A few missense
  alleles (G46E, D51N, G94R) are associated with microphthalmia, sclerocornea,
  coloboma, congenital aphakia or acorea together with cataract. Rare biallelic
  frameshift alleles cause recessive cataract, with mild or no lens opacity in
  heterozygous carriers. Mutant Cx50 fails to form functional channels, is
  retained intracellularly, or inhibits co-expressed wild-type Cx50 and Cx46;

  opacities form. Loss of Cx50 also curbs postnatal lens epithelial
  proliferation, which accounts for the small lens and eye in mice. Management
  is lens extraction with optical correction and amblyopia therapy; there is
  no genotype-directed treatment.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — GJA8/Cx50 is the causal gene; dominant missense hotspots in EL1/M1 dominate the spectrum

CTRCT1 is caused by heterozygous variants in GJA8 (HGNC:4302), the great majority being dominant missense substitutions clustering in the first extracellular loop (EL1) and first transmembrane domain (M1) of connexin-50. The founding evidence is the classic linkage study of a family with autosomal-dominant zonular pulverulent cataract: the CZP1 locus mapped to 1q21 with a maximum LOD score Zmax = 5.22 at D1S2669, and a C→T transition in codon 88 (P88S) cosegregated with disease — "the underlying mutation is present in GJA8, the gene for connexin50" and "a C-->T transition in codon 88, which introduced a novel MnlI restriction-enzyme site that also cosegregated with the cataract" (PMID: 9497259). This was the first inherited disease linked to a defined human autosome.

A modern multicentre cohort reinforced and broadened this: 15 new families carried 14 different heterozygous GJA8 variants — 12 missense plus two 1q21 microdeletions — "We identified 15 new families carrying 14 different heterozygous GJA8 variants (12 missense variants and two 1q21 microdeletions)" (PMID: 40301690). Recurrent hotspot residues reported across studies include G22S, T39R, V44M, W45S, G46R/E, D47N, R76C, P88L/S, G94E/R, H98R, P189S, R198W, S258F. The D47N substitution is a documented hot-spot, confirmed segregating in a six-generation Chinese family (PMID: 28526010); P88L is a newer dominant allele (PMID: 35531093).

Ontology anchors: gene HGNC:4302 (GJA8); disease MONDO:0007285; HPO HP:0000518 (Cataract), HP:0010920 (Zonular cataract), HP:0007971 (Congenital cataract).

Finding 2 — Cx50 mouse knockouts recapitulate the human phenotype (microphthalmia + pulverulent cataract)

Loss of Cx50 in mice reproduces the defining human features. Targeted ablation of connexin-50 produced "Cx50-null mice exhibited microphthalmia and nuclear cataracts," and quantitatively "At postnatal day 14 (P14), Cx50-knockout eyes weighed 32% less than controls, whereas lens mass was reduced by 46%" (PMID: 9813099). Independent Gja8 disruption likewise caused microphthalmia with small lenses, nuclear cataract, and delayed fiber-cell denucleation (PMID: 11782410). Critically, knocking Cx46 into the Cx50 locus rescued differentiation and cataract but not growth — "intrinsic properties of Cx50 were required for cellular growth, whereas nonspecific restoration of communication by Cx46 maintained differentiation" (PMID: 11786642). This separates two Cx50 jobs: a generic coupling function (shareable with Cx46) and a Cx50-specific growth function that explains microcornea/microphthalmia.

Finding 3 — Pathogenic missense variants converge on loss of functional channels, via heterogeneous routes

Functional dissection shows multiple molecular routes to a common endpoint. Comparing three alleles, "All mutants failed to form functional hemichannels and gap junction channels," yet they reached that state differently: "the Cx50R76C mutant and Cx50S258F mutant exhibited trafficking defects resulting from delayed degradation and accelerated degradation," while V44M trafficked normally; all three increased autophagic activity (PMID: 40158616). The A69T allele "inhibited GJA8 hemichannel activity, reduced cell tolerance to oxidative stress," altered protein distribution, and inhibited cell growth (PMID: 33218330). The mouse R205G allele (homologous to human R198W) directly demonstrates dominance: "Cx50-R205G mutant proteins block channel function of gap junctions composed of wild-type Cx50," and it also alters wild-type Cx46 gating (PMID: 23300808). A structural example, D67E in EL1, disrupts the Asp67–Arg185 ionic interaction and rigidifies EL1, impairing gating (PMID: 42827312).

Finding 4 — Phenotypic spectrum and genotype–phenotype correlations

GJA8 disease is not monolithic. The Ragge multicentre study found phenotypes "ranged from isolated cataracts to a combination of microphthalmia and cataract with/without sclerocornea," and specifically "confirm an important genotype-phenotype correlation between sclerocornea and the p.(Gly94Arg) variant" (PMID: 40301690). GJA8 is also "the first gene associated with familial acorea-microphthalmia-cataract syndrome" (alleles G46E, D51N) (PMID: 38052906). Severe microphthalmia with sclerocornea has been tied to a novel GJA8 variant (PMID: 26694549); microcornea frequently co-occurs (e.g., the W45S "jellyfish-like" cataract, PMID: 18334946; T39R and G46R, PMID: 21686328). Intra- and inter-familial variability is documented.

Finding 5 — Management: early surgery and amblyopia therapy; no gene-specific treatment

There is no molecularly targeted therapy. A visually significant opacity is a surgical indication: "lens opacity obscuring the red reflex in preverbal children and visual acuity of less than 20/40 is an absolute indication for lens aspiration," ideally ~6 weeks (unilateral) to 6–8 weeks (bilateral), with IOL considered after age 1 (PMID: 31317088). For bilateral congenital cataract under age 2, the two options are "intraocular lens (IOL) implantation; or (ii) leaving a child with primary aphakia (no lens in the eye), necessitating the need for contact lenses or aphakic glasses" (PMID: 36107778). Visual prognosis "depends on early intervention and adherence to amblyopia therapy" (PMID: 42216740).

Finding 6 — Dominant-negative, not haploinsufficiency (the core mechanistic verdict)

gnomAD constraint metrics for GJA8 show it is not depleted of LoF or missense variants: pLI ≈ 0.0001, LoF observed/expected ≈ 13/15.3 = 0.85 (90% CI 0.55–1.35), missense o/e ≈ 1.10 (slight excess, mis_z ≈ −0.97). Thus simply halving gene dosage is well tolerated at the population level, inconsistent with a haploinsufficiency model for dominant disease. In ClinVar (n≈317 GJA8 variants), 51 of 60 (85%) non-conflicting pathogenic/likely-pathogenic alleles are missense. The decisive biological contrast: heterozygous Cx50-null mice have "relatively normal eyes and lenses" (PMID: 11782410), whereas dominant human alleles act by poisoning wild-type channels — "Cx50-R205G mutant proteins block channel function of gap junctions composed of wild-type Cx50" (PMID: 23300808).

Finding 7 — The No2 (Cx50D47A) mouse: genotype-matched proof of poison-subunit depletion

The No2 line carries Cx50D47A, paralleling the recurrent human D47 hotspot (incl. D47N). Both heterozygous and homozygous mice had cataracts and smaller lenses, and the mutant dominantly depleted Cx50: "Levels of Cx50 were severely reduced in mutant animals as compared with those in wild-type mice (<20% in heterozygotes and ≤3% in homozygotes)" — far below the 50% expected from a null allele. Partner proteins Cx46 and aquaporin-0 (AQP0/MIP) were also decreased, and "The lenses of Cx50D47A mice showed persistence of nuclear remnants in deep regions of the lens," with elevated H3 histone and mitochondrial Tom20 and reduced γ- (and in homozygotes β-) crystallins (PMID: 24204043). The general connexin principle underlying such dominance was shown for a conserved TM3 threonine mutation (Cx43T154A/Cx26T135A): "a single point mutation that eliminates functionality without interfering with gap junction formation," i.e., an assembled but non-conductive channel (PMID: 16407179).

Finding 8 — Cross-species conservation; the "natural disease" is a mouse story

GJA8 is conserved across vertebrates with clear orthologs (HomoloGene group 3857): human NCBI Gene 2703, mouse Gja8 14616, rat 29601, dog 483156, cattle 524042, chicken 395846, zebrafish gja8a 114403. Disease-relevant residues are conserved — "The Gja8(R205G) mutation occurs at the same conserved residue as the human GJA8(R198W) mutation" (PMID: 23300808). Naturally occurring GJA8/Cx50 cataract is essentially a laboratory-mouse phenomenon (engineered and ENU/spontaneous alleles: No2, Nm2249/R205G, knockouts); a targeted search found no established naturally occurring GJA8 cataract in companion animals or wildlife (canine hereditary cataract is more typically linked to other genes such as HSF4).

Section-by-Section Dossier

1. Disease Information

  • Overview: CTRCT1 is a Mendelian, mostly autosomal-dominant congenital/developmental cataract (bilateral lens opacification present at or shortly after birth), frequently accompanied by microcornea/microphthalmia and, in some alleles, anterior-segment dysgenesis. Caused by GJA8/Cx50.
  • Identifiers: OMIM 116200; MONDO :0007285; Orphanet "early-onset/congenital cataract" groupings; MeSH Cataract (D002386); ICD-10 Q12.0 (congenital cataract); ICD-11 congenital cataract (LD2A.0x). Gene: HGNC:4302, NCBI Gene 2703, OMIM 600897, UniProt P48165 (CXA8_HUMAN).
  • Synonyms: CTRCT1; cataract 1, multiple types, with or without microcornea; GJA8 cataract; cataract caused by mutation in GJA8; zonular pulverulent cataract-1 (CZP1).
  • Data source type: Aggregated disease-level resources (OMIM, ClinVar, gnomAD) plus pedigree/case-level clinical-genetics literature; not EHR-derived.

2. Etiology

  • Causal factor: Genetic — heterozygous pathogenic GJA8 variants (dominant missense predominate; rare biallelic/compound-heterozygous and 1q21 microdeletion cases exist). Not infectious or environmental in the Mendelian form.
  • Genetic risk architecture: Monogenic causal variants; recurrent hotspots (D47N, P88S/L, R198W, G94R, etc.). No established common-variant susceptibility loci or disease-specific modifier genes identified for GJA8 cataract.
  • Environmental/protective factors: Not applicable to the Mendelian etiology; no diet/lifestyle factor modifies the primary genetic cause. (Age-related cataract is a separate, multifactorial entity.)
  • Gene–environment interactions: None established. In vitro, the A69T allele reduces tolerance to oxidative stress (PMID: 33218330) — a plausible but undemonstrated-in-vivo modifier axis.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Causal gene: GJA8 (HGNC:4302, NCBI 2703, OMIM 600897), 1q21.1; protein connexin-50 (UniProt P48165), a four-transmembrane connexin forming hexameric hemichannels (connexons) that dock into intercellular gap-junction channels.
  • Variant classes: Predominantly missense (ClinVar ~254/317; 85% of P/LP alleles missense); also frameshift (~13), stop-gained (~5), and 1q21 microdeletions (PMID: 40301690). ACMG/AMP: many hotspot alleles Pathogenic/Likely Pathogenic; ~177 VUS in ClinVar.
  • Allele frequency: Pathogenic alleles are private/rare; the gene overall is LoF/missense-tolerant in gnomAD (pLI≈0.0001; missense o/e≈1.10).
  • Origin: Germline (dominant, often inherited; de novo cases reported).
  • Functional consequence: Dominant-negative loss of channel function (poison subunit) for most dominant alleles; assembled-but-non-conductive or trafficking-defective channels; co-depletion of partner connexin Cx46 and AQP0 (PMID: 24204043, PMID: 23300808, PMID: 40158616).
  • Modifier genes/epigenetics/chromosomal abnormalities: No specific DNA-methylation or histone modifications implicated. 1q21 microdeletions encompassing GJA8 are documented structural causes; no recurrent aneuploidy/translocation signature.

5. Environmental Information

Not applicable to the Mendelian etiology. No toxins, radiation, infectious agents, or lifestyle factors are established causes of GJA8-CTRCT1. (Congenital infections such as rubella cause phenocopy cataracts but are a distinct differential diagnosis, not CTRCT1.)

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation):

  1. A heterozygous GJA8 missense variant (e.g., D47N/D47A, R198W/R205G, P88S, V44M, R76C, S258F) leads to a mutant Cx50 subunit that still co-oligomerizes with wild-type Cx50/Cx46 (inferred for most alleles from in vitro and mouse data).
  2. The mutant subunit results in non-conductive or mistrafficked connexons ("poison subunit"), and in some alleles promotes degradation/autophagy of the connexin pool — "All mutants failed to form functional hemichannels and gap junction channels" (PMID: 40158616).
  3. This leads to dominant depletion of total Cx50 below the 50% expected from a null (<20% in No2 heterozygotes) and co-depletion of Cx46 and AQP0 (PMID: 24204043) — branching the lesion beyond the mutant protein itself.
  4. Loss of fiber-cell gap-junction coupling results in collapse of the lens internal microcirculation (ion/metabolite/antioxidant flux to the avascular lens core), plus loss of a Cx50-specific growth function (PMID: 11786642).
  5. These lead to impaired lens-fiber differentiation and failed fiber-cell denucleation ("persistence of nuclear remnants in deep regions of the lens") with crystallin dysregulation (reduced γ-/β-crystallins) and retained organelles (PMID: 24204043).
  6. Light scattering from retained nuclei/organelles and disordered crystallins results in lens opacity (cataract); the growth deficit results in reduced lens/eye size (microcornea/microphthalmia) (PMID: 9813099).
  7. Congenital bilateral opacity leads to form-deprivation amblyopia if not surgically cleared within the critical visual-development window.

Upstream vs downstream: Upstream = mutant Cx50 subunit + poison-subunit channel loss; midstream = connexin/AQP0 depletion and microcirculatory failure; downstream = differentiation/denucleation failure, crystallin disorder, opacity, microphthalmia; terminal = amblyopia.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ: Eye (UBERON:0000970), specifically the crystalline lens (UBERON:0000965); secondary involvement of cornea (UBERON:0000964) (microcornea/sclerocornea) and the whole globe (microphthalmia). Body system: visual/sensory system.
  • Tissue/cell: Lens fiber cells (CL:0011004) and lens epithelial cells (CL:0002224); epithelial lineage. Corneal stroma in sclerocornea.
  • Subcellular: Plasma-membrane gap junctions/connexin complexes (GO:0005921/0005922); retained nuclei and mitochondria in deep fibers (failed organelle degradation).
  • Localization/laterality: Bilateral (variable asymmetry), with central/zonular/nuclear distributions within the lens.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Congenital/neonatal; cataract present at birth or detected in early infancy.
  • Progression: Opacity can be stable or slowly progressive; the developmental growth deficit (microcornea/microphthalmia) is fixed congenitally. Disease is chronic/lifelong absent surgery.
  • Critical period: The pre-operative window for preventing deprivation amblyopia is narrow — bilateral surgery recommended by ~6–8 weeks of age (PMID: 31317088). No spontaneous remission.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Autosomal dominant (predominant); rare autosomal-recessive/compound-heterozygous and microdeletion cases.
  • Penetrance/expressivity: High penetrance with variable expressivity (intra- and inter-familial variability in morphology and associated anterior-segment features) (PMID: 40301690).
  • Mechanism of dominance: Dominant-negative (see Findings 6–7), not haploinsufficiency.
  • Epidemiology: Congenital cataract overall affects roughly 1–6 per 10,000 births; GJA8 is a recurrent contributor among genetically solved familial/bilateral pediatric cataract (connexin genes among the most common after crystallins). No founder effect established; both sexes affected (autosomal). Hotspot alleles (D47N, P88S) recur independently across populations (notably multiple Chinese pedigrees).
  • Anticipation/mosaicism/consanguinity: No anticipation (not a repeat-expansion disorder). Germline mosaicism not specifically characterized; consanguinity relevant only to rare biallelic cases.

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical: Slit-lamp biomicroscopy and red-reflex testing identify and classify the opacity; B-scan ultrasound and biometry assess posterior segment and axial length (microphthalmia). Morphology (zonular pulverulent, nuclear, jellyfish-like, membranous) raises suspicion of a connexin etiology.
  • Genetic testing: Molecular diagnosis is by NGS — targeted congenital-cataract gene panels (crystallins, connexins GJA3/GJA8, MIP, BFSP, transcription factors) or whole-exome sequencing; single-gene GJA8 Sanger confirmation with segregation. NGS panels solve ~70–82% of familial/bilateral pediatric cataract (PMID: 26694549, PMID: 38874774). WES also captures digenic/second-gene contributions (PMID: 24281366, PMID: 36916241). Chromosomal microarray detects 1q21 microdeletions.
  • Differential diagnosis: Other genetic congenital cataracts (crystallins CRYAA/CRYBB2/CRYGC/CRYGD, GJA3/Cx46, MIP/AQP0, HSF4, PAX6, MAF, BFSP), syndromic forms (NHS/Nance-Horan, BCOR), and acquired phenocopies (congenital rubella, galactosemia, steroid/trauma).
  • Screening: Universal newborn red-reflex screening (secondary prevention); cascade genetic testing of at-risk relatives once a familial variant is known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival/mortality: Not life-limiting; normal life expectancy. The burden is sensory/visual.
  • Morbidity/function: Principal risk is irreversible amblyopia and lifelong visual impairment if surgery is delayed; well-timed surgery plus amblyopia therapy yields good functional outcomes in many children (PMID: 42216740).
  • Complications: Post-surgical — posterior capsule opacification, aphakic/secondary glaucoma, refractive challenges of a growing eye, strabismus; pre-surgical — deprivation amblyopia. Microphthalmic eyes carry a more guarded prognosis.
  • Prognostic factors: Timing of surgery (within the critical period), adherence to amblyopia therapy, laterality, and severity of associated microphthalmia/anterior-segment anomaly.

12. Treatment

  • Definitive: Surgical lens aspiration/lensectomy (vitrector-assisted with posterior capsulotomy ± anterior vitrectomy in young children), with IOL implantation (hydrophobic acrylic PCIOL, typically >1 year) or primary aphakia corrected by contact lenses/aphakic spectacles (PMID: 31317088, PMID: 36107778).   • Rehabilitative: Amblyopia therapy (occlusion/patching) and optical correction are essential adjuncts.
