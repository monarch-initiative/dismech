Renal Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0005938 Pathograph 39 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Urogenital tuberculosis

Renal tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the kidney and the commonest form of genitourinary tuberculosis. Mycobacterium tuberculosis reaches both kidneys through the bloodstream from a primary pulmonary infection and forms small cortical granulomas that usually heal and stay latent, often for decades. When a focus reactivates, usually in one kidney, caseating granulomas erode the renal papillae and discharge bacilli into the urine. Papillary necrosis and caseous cavitation follow, and the infection descends the collecting system to the renal pelvis, ureter and bladder. Fibrosis causes ureteric strictures with hydronephrosis and a contracted, low-capacity bladder whose reflux can damage the other kidney. Untreated disease ends in a calcified, non-functioning kidney ("putty kidney" or autonephrectomy) and can cause chronic kidney disease. The disease is insidious. Sterile pyuria, hematuria and lower urinary tract symptoms are the usual clues, and diagnosis is often late. Urine mycobacterial culture and Xpert MTB/RIF confirm it, and CT urography shows its extent. Treatment is standard six-month antituberculous chemotherapy, with early ureteric stenting or nephrostomy for obstruction, reconstructive surgery for strictures and contracted bladder, and nephrectomy for a non-functioning kidney.

Fibrotic Urinary Tract Stricture
Circular fibrosis narrows the infundibula, renal pelvis or ureter, most often the distal ureter, and longitudinal fibrosis shortens the ureter. Strictures are usually unilateral, can be multiple, and can worsen during antituberculous treatment as fibrosis progresses. Urinary tract stenosis is the main cause of loss of renal function in renal tuberculosis.

Renal Tuberculosis (MONDO:0005938): Disease Characteristics Research Report

How I read the request: Renal tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease, not a Mendelian one. Its cause is infection with the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex: mainly M. tuberculosis, sometimes M. bovis. No single causal gene is recorded for it, and none exists. Host genes only change how susceptible a person is. Most primary literature treats renal TB as the main part of "genitourinary" or "urogenital" TB (GUTB/UGTB), so I used those sources where none were kidney-specific.

Limits on the evidence: - PubMed and PMC full-text pages could not be fetched in this session (cookie and CAPTCHA blocks, and shell network access was denied). Most facts below come from abstracts and excerpts returned by web search. - I only marked text as a direct quote where it came back as quoted source text. Where only a PMC ID or DOI was retrieved, I give that instead of a PMID. - Ontology terms (HPO, GO, CL, UBERON, CHEBI, NCIT) are given as labels only. 2. Etiology

Causal agent. - Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, mainly M. tuberculosis; M. bovis is a zoonotic cause. - The usual route is reactivation of bacilli that reached the kidney through the blood during the first lung infection (PMID 24578959). Genitourinary involvement "almost always follows a pulmonary infection" (Transl Androl Urol 2017, PMID 28540230). - A separate iatrogenic route exists: granulomatous disease after intravesical BCG (M. bovis BCG) for bladder cancer. This is in the general literature, but I did not retrieve a source for it this session.

Risk factors (human clinical evidence). - Chronic kidney disease, dialysis, and kidney transplant: - A 2023 meta-analysis (Clin Infect Dis 77:1194–1200, doi 10.1093/cid/ciad364; PMC10573716) found a pooled adjusted HR of 1.57 (95% CI 1.22–2.03) for TB in CKD without kidney failure. Risk was highest in stages 4–5 (IRR 3.63, 2.25–5.86). - An earlier meta-analysis (PMID 26614191) reported a dialysis rate ratio of 3.62 (1.79–7.33) and raised risk after kidney transplant. - A Korean cohort found kidney failure carried a 4.39-fold TB risk. - These are risks for TB overall, not renal TB specifically. - HIV / AIDS: "The AIDS epidemic caused unexpected worldwide levels of tuberculosis, even in developed countries" (PMID 18836557). - Age and sex: Most common in men in their 40s and 50s (PMC11687435). - Living in or migrating from a high-burden area, and a past history of pulmonary TB (PMID 31548730). - Diabetes and immunosuppressive drugs are established TB risk factors in general. I did not retrieve renal-specific data for them.

Genetic risk factors (susceptibility only, not causal). - Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) comes from inborn errors of IFN-γ immunity. It involves at least 19–20 genes, including IFNGR1 (AR or AD) and IL12RB1 (AR), and penetrance is incomplete (Bustamante et al., PMC4357480; update PMC6438774; overview PMID 40656276). - TYK2 P1104A homozygosity: "TYK2 P1104A impairs cellular responses to IL-23, but not to IFN-α, IL-10, or even IL-12." Homozygotes are "found in about 1/600 Europeans and between 1/1000 and 1/10,000 individuals in regions other than East Asia" (Boisson-Dupuis et al., Sci Immunol 2018, PMID 30578352). In UK Biobank, homozygotes were 6/620 TB patients (1%) versus 0.2% of controls, OR about 5. - Polymorphisms tested for extrapulmonary TB: - IL1B, VDR FokI, and TLR2 variants were associated with EPTB in a small pilot (24 EPTB cases; PMC2837863). - IFNG +874 T/A (rs2430561) looked protective for pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB in a 42-study meta-analysis (PMC5584145). - TLR2 Arg753Gln was associated with pulmonary TB but not EPTB (PMC6348792). - No GWAS signal specific to renal TB was found.

Protective factors. - Genetic: possibly the IFNG +874 alleles above (PMC5584145). - Environmental: avoiding unpasteurized milk (lowers M. bovis risk; WHO Zoonotic TB Roadmap, https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241513043), treating latent infection, controlling HIV, and finishing treatment of pulmonary TB.

Gene–environment interaction. Disease needs exposure plus a host immune defect. The MSMD and TYK2 genotypes only matter after mycobacterial exposure, and penetrance is incomplete (PMC4357480; PMID 30578352). No GxE studies specific to renal TB were found.

  • Infectious agents: M. tuberculosis complex (M. tuberculosis, M. bovis; NCBI Taxonomy IDs not retrieved this session).
  • Zoonotic route:

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. The M. tuberculosis complex is inhaled (or M. bovis is swallowed), which leads to a primary infection, usually in the lung (PMID 28540230).
  2. Bacilli spread through the blood during that primary infection, which seeds the kidney. "After hematogenous spread from the pulmonary focus, there is colonization of the renal parenchyma, with initially bilateral, cortical, glomerular, and pericapillary renal lesions" (urogenital TB review retrieved via search, PMC11687435/PMC2556487).
  3. Cell-mediated immunity (IL-12/IL-23 to IFN-γ to macrophage activation) contains the seeded bacilli in small cortical granulomas, which results in clinical latency.
  4. Latency averages about 22 years (range 1–46 years). Elsewhere it is given as 5–40 years, with GUTB in 2–20% of people with pulmonary TB.
  5. Autopsy series: GUTB in 3.1% of 5,424 autopsies, 98% bilateral, 85% with lung lesions.
  6. Sources: search-retrieved review excerpts; PMC11687435.
  7. (Branch, inferred from genetic data:) Defects in this axis (IFNGR1, IL12RB1, TYK2 P1104A) or acquired immunosuppression (HIV, CKD/dialysis, transplant drugs) lead to failed containment (PMC4357480; PMID 30578352; doi 10.1093/cid/ciad364).
  8. Waning immunity lets the bacilli reactivate, typically at one focus. That focus grows, so clinical disease is usually unilateral even though seeding was bilateral: "infection progresses from a single focus, affecting one kidney and sparing the other."
  9. Granulomas grow and caseate in the medulla and papillae, which results in papillary necrosis (tuberculous papillitis) and rupture of caseous material into the collecting system. This is Kulchavenya stage 2 (PMC4340670).
  10. Bacilli and debris then pass into the urine, which leads to:
  11. (a) urine positive for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) or NAAT with "sterile" pyuria and hematuria, and
  12. (b) spread down to the ureter, bladder, and, in men, the prostate and epididymis (PMID 36482950 for retrograde genital spread).
  13. Chronic granulomatous inflammation heals by fibrosis, which results in strictures of the infundibula, pelviureteric junction, and ureterovesical junction. This causes "phantom calyx", hydrocalycosis, and hydronephrosis. Ureteral stenosis accounts for "up to 93.7%" of renal exclusion (PMC2684360; RSNA RadioGraphics 2021, doi 10.1148/rg.2021200154).
  14. Cavities form and obstruction persists, which leads to cavernous and then polycavernous disease (stages 3–4). With dystrophic calcification this becomes a non-functioning "putty kidney" or autonephrectomy (PMID 35812261; PMC4340670).
  15. Bladder involvement leads to fibrosis, which results in a small contracted bladder, vesicoureteral reflux, and pressure on the upper tract (PMC11687435).
  16. Clinical outcome: lower urinary tract symptoms, flank pain, hematuria, hypertension, and loss of function in one kidney or, if bilateral, CKD or ESKD.
    • Parallel branch: Diffuse granulomatous tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN) without urinary-tract destruction leads directly to acute or chronic kidney failure. In one series, 17/25 biopsied TB patients with significant renal disease had chronic granulomatous TIN, and it "presents late with advanced disease" (PMC6977373/related series; PMC9999700).
    • Secondary AA amyloidosis is a further branch from chronic inflammation. This is from general literature; I did not retrieve a source this session.

Mechanism detail

  • Molecular pathways: IL-12/IL-23 to JAK/TYK2 to STAT4 to IFN-γ (TYK2 P1104A selectively breaks IL-23-driven IFN-γ; PMID 30578352). Also TLR2 recognition and VDR-dependent antimicrobial responses (PMC2837863).
  • Cellular processes: Granuloma formation, caseous necrosis, macrophage activation, fibrosis. Suggested GO labels: granuloma formation, response to bacterium / defense response to Gram-positive bacterium, interferon-gamma-mediated signaling pathway, macrophage activation, inflammatory response, fibroblast proliferation.
  • Cell types (CL labels): macrophage; epithelioid cell; multinucleated giant cell (Langhans); CD4-positive alpha-beta T cell; renal tubular epithelial cell; glomerular endothelial cell; urothelial cell; fibroblast.
  • Upstream vs downstream: Steps 1–4 (infection, seeding, immune containment and its failure) are upstream. Steps 5–10 (necrosis, fibrosis, obstruction, kidney loss) are downstream tissue-damage mechanisms.
  • Omics, single-cell, and spatial data: None specific to renal TB were found. Granuloma omics work in TB is lung-based.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: Kidney: cortex at seeding, then medulla, papillae, calyces, and renal pelvis (UBERON labels: kidney, renal cortex, renal medulla, renal papilla, renal pelvis, major/minor calyx).
  • Secondary organs:
  • ureter (strictures, often at the ureterovesical junction), urinary bladder (contracted bladder);
  • in men, the prostate, seminal vesicle, epididymis, and vas deferens (PMID 36482950);
  • the lung (concurrent or prior disease; 85% at autopsy).
  • Body systems: Urinary, reproductive, immune, and respiratory.
  • Tissue level: Renal tubulointerstitium, glomeruli (initial seeding), urothelium, fibrous connective tissue.
  • Subcellular level: Bacilli live inside the macrophage phagosome or phagolysosome (GO CC labels: phagocytic vesicle, lysosome). This is general TB biology; I did not retrieve a renal-specific source.
  • Laterality: Seeding is bilateral (98% at autopsy). Clinical, destructive disease is usually unilateral (search-retrieved review excerpts). Bilateral disease puts kidney function at risk.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Adults, mostly in their 40s and 50s (PMC11687435). Insidious and chronic, after a latency averaging about 22 years.
  • Stages (Kulchavenya; PMC4340670):
  • "Level 1 – nondestructive form, TB of parenchyma"
  • "Level 2 – small destructive form, TB papillitis"
  • "Level 3 – destructive form with one or two caverns (cavernous kidney TB)"
  • "Level 4 – widespread destructive form with more than two caverns (polycavernous kidney TB)"
  • Progression: Slow, but strictures can form or worsen during treatment as lesions heal. This is why early stenting or nephrostomy is advised (PMC2684360).
  • Remission: Treatment-induced. Stages 1–2 are curable with drugs. "Cavernous KTB… recovery without surgery is rare. Polycavernous KTB… recovery with anti-TB drugs only is impossible, surgery is necessary, basically nephrectomy" (PMC4340670).
  • Critical window: Diagnosis before cavities and strictures form (stages 1–2) preserves the kidney.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Not inherited. Susceptibility can be monogenic (MSMD: AR or AD, incomplete penetrance) or multifactorial (PMC4357480).
  • Global TB burden:
  • 10.8 million incident TB cases in 2023; 8.2 million notified.
  • About 16% of notified cases were extrapulmonary (the denominator should be checked) (WHO Global TB Report 2024, https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240101531; https://www.who.int/teams/global-programme-on-tuberculosis-and-lung-health/tb-reports/global-tuberculosis-report-2024/tb-diagnosis-and-treatment/2-1-case-notifications).
  • 8.3 million notifications in 2024 (WHO 2025 report).
  • Renal and urogenital share: UGTB is 30–40% of EPTB in reviews (PMC11687435; PMID 18836557). Its share is much lower in some low-burden series.
  • No direct prevalence or incidence per 100,000 for renal TB was retrieved.
  • Geography: Endemic regions (Africa, South and Southeast Asia) and migrants from them (PMID 34827337, which also notes that GUTB is under-recognized in Europe).
  • Sex ratio: Male predominance (PMC11687435).
  • Pediatric cases are rare because of the long latency.

Microbiology (reference standard). "Specific diagnosis rests on culturing M. tuberculosis from a suitable clinical sample or identifying its DNA" (PMID 31548730). Standard practice is 3–5 early-morning urine samples for AFB smear and mycobacterial culture (liquid or solid).

Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) on urine:

Routine lab tests. Urinalysis (sterile pyuria, hematuria, proteinuria), serum creatinine and eGFR. IGRA or tuberculin skin test supports infection but does not distinguish latent from active disease.

Imaging: - MDCT urography has replaced IV urography. "Multidetector computed tomography can reproduce images comparable with intravenous excretory urography" (Mthalane et al., PMID 31754491). - Key findings: papillary necrosis, infundibular stenosis, "phantom calyx," hydrocalycosis, cortical scarring, calcification (CT is the most sensitive), thickened ureter or pelvis wall, "putty kidney" (PMID 35812261; RadioGraphics doi 10.1148/rg.2021200154). - Individual findings are not specific, but "presence of multiple imaging findings in a single case is pointer to this disease" (Sankhe & Joshi, Curr Radiol Rep 2014). - Also: chest imaging, and DMSA/renography to estimate split kidney function.

Biopsy and histopathology. - Caseating epithelioid granulomas with Langhans giant cells, AFB on Ziehl–Neelsen stain, tissue PCR. - Granulomatous interstitial nephritis is found in 0.5–0.95% of native kidney biopsies. TB causes just over half of cases in an Indian series and none in a Glasgow series (PMC9999700).

Genetic testing. Not applicable for diagnosis. Consider MSMD gene panels or exomes (IFNGR1, IL12RB1, TYK2, and others) only for disseminated, recurrent, or BCG/environmental-mycobacterial disease in young patients (PMC4357480).

Omics diagnostics. None validated for renal TB.

Differential diagnosis. - Other causes of sterile pyuria: partly treated UTI, interstitial nephritis, urolithiasis, Chlamydia/Ureaplasma. - Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis. - Papillary necrosis from analgesics, diabetes, or sickle cell disease. - Renal cell carcinoma or urothelial carcinoma. - Renal sarcoidosis (granulomatous TIN; caseation favors TB) (PMC9999700). - Medullary sponge kidney; post-BCG granulomatous disease.

Screening. There is no population screening. Screen high-risk groups for latent TB: before dialysis or transplant, people with CKD stages 4–5, people with HIV (doi 10.1093/cid/ciad364).

11. Outcome/Prognosis

  • Mortality: Low when treated. No 5- or 10-year survival figures specific to renal TB were retrieved.
  • Morbidity:
  • Kidney loss (non-functioning kidney, autonephrectomy).
  • Ureteral stricture; contracted bladder; hypertension; infertility (genital involvement).
  • CKD or ESKD: in granulomatous-TIN series, two of five TB patients reached ESKD and the rest had a mean eGFR of 28 mL/min at 1 year (PMC9999700/related UK series).
  • Historical shift: In 1945 the nephrectomy rate was 97% (Nesbit, 260 patients). In 1970, Bloom et al. managed all 25 non-functioning kidneys conservatively (PMC2684360).
  • Prognostic factors:
  • Stage at diagnosis (Kulchavenya 1–2 versus 3–4).
  • Bilateral disease.
  • Length of stricture: balloon dilation does best with strictures under 1.5 cm, non-ischemic, and with adequate kidney function (PMC2684360).
  • Baseline eGFR; HIV and immunosuppression; drug resistance.
  • Prognostic biomarkers: None established.
  • Quality-of-life instruments: None reported.

12. Treatment

Drug therapy (drug-susceptible disease). - Standard 2HRZE/4HR: isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for 2 months, then isoniazid and rifampicin for 4 months. GUTB is treated for 6–9 months (PMID 31548730; ATS/CDC/IDSA guideline, Nahid et al., Clin Infect Dis 2016, doi 10.1093/cid/ciw376; WHO Module 4, https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240048126). - Drug-resistant TB needs 12–24 months of more toxic drugs with close monitoring (PMID 31548730). WHO's newer short regimens for drug-resistant TB are not covered by the sources retrieved here. - The WHO 4-month HPMZ regimen (rifapentine and moxifloxacin) covers pulmonary TB in people aged 12 and over, based on Study 31. Its use in extrapulmonary or renal TB is not established. - M. bovis is naturally pyrazinamide-resistant, so treat for 9 months without pyrazinamide (WHO Zoonotic TB Roadmap; duration from general practice). - Dose adjustment in reduced kidney function: Ethambutol and pyrazinamide are cleared by the kidney, so their dosing interval is extended in CKD and on dialysis. This is a guideline-level fact that I did not re-verify this session. - Corticosteroids to prevent ureteral stricture are sometimes used, but the evidence is weak; no source was retrieved.

Pharmacogenomics. NAT2 acetylator status affects isoniazid exposure and liver toxicity. This is known biology, but I did not retrieve a source this session.

Surgery and interventions (PMC2684360; PMID 23730329). - Stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy for obstruction: "Stents or nephrostomy tubes decrease the likelihood of renal loss." - Endoscopic balloon dilation of strictures. - Ureteral reimplantation, Boari flap, ileal ureter substitution, and pyelo- or ureterocalyceal reconstruction. - Augmentation cystoplasty for a contracted bladder. - Nephrectomy or partial nephrectomy for a non-functioning kidney with pain or hypertension, deferred until several weeks of drugs. Laparoscopic nephrectomy is "safe, effective and less invasive."

Advanced therapies (gene, cell, RNA, targeted, immunotherapy). Not applicable. No renal-TB trials (NCT numbers) were identified.

NCIT-style intervention labels: Isoniazid; Rifampin; Pyrazinamide; Ethambutol; Rifapentine; Moxifloxacin; Nephrectomy; Ureteral Stent Placement; Percutaneous Nephrostomy; Ureteral Reimplantation; Augmentation Cystoplasty.

Treatment algorithm. 1. Microbiological confirmation (urine culture plus Xpert, which also screens for rifampicin resistance). 2. Drug susceptibility testing. 3. Standard regimen. 4. Imaging at baseline and during early treatment to catch new strictures. 5. Early drainage for obstruction. 6. Delayed reconstruction or nephrectomy for stage 3–4 disease.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • TB infection control; HIV control; pasteurizing milk and controlling bovine TB (WHO Roadmap).
  • BCG vaccination protects mainly children from disseminated TB. Its effect on adult renal TB is not established.
  • Secondary prevention:
  • Screening for and treating latent TB in CKD stages 4–5, before dialysis or transplant, and in people with HIV (doi 10.1093/cid/ciad364; PMID 26614191).
  • Raising suspicion for sterile pyuria or recurrent UTI that fails standard antibiotics in endemic settings (PMID 31754491).
  • Tertiary prevention: Early stenting and imaging follow-up to stop strictures from destroying the kidney (PMC2684360); following kidney function over time.
  • Genetic counseling: Only for families with MSMD or TYK2 genotypes (PMC4357480).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Mycobacterium bovis in cattle is the main animal reservoir. Zoonotic transmission to humans is mainly through food (WHO Roadmap).
  • I retrieved no kidney- or urogenital-specific veterinary data, and none from OMIA (not applicable, because the disease is not Mendelian).
  • Comparative note: The rabbit model produces kidney lesions "very closely related to those found in chronic human kidney tuberculosis" (Jpn J Urol 1932, https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpnjurol1928/21/10/21_10_506/_article/-char/en).

15. Model Organisms

  • Rabbit, direct kidney inoculation: "A fine needle puncture of the cortical layer of the parenchyma of the lower pole of the kidney is performed under ultrasound control to the rabbit" with M. tuberculosis H37Rv (patent RU2776130C1, https://patents.google.com/patent/RU2776130C1/en). This is an induced model meant to imitate the clinical course.
  • Rabbit, intravenous: Ear-vein inoculation produces miliary disease plus chronic-type kidney lesions (1932 study above).
  • Guinea pig: Recapitulates human-like (lung) granuloma pathology (PMC10556493). Its kidney lesions are not characterized in the sources retrieved.
  • Mice:
  • Standard mice form poor human-like granulomas. C3HeB/FeJ mice form necrotic lesions, but these are lung-focused (PMC3631743 and related).
  • Humanized BLT mice use the kidney capsule only for engrafting human tissue, not as an infection model.
  • No validated mouse model of renal TB was found.
  • Genetic models: Ifngr1-, Il12rb1-, and Tyk2-deficient mice exist for studying TB susceptibility in general. I did not retrieve renal-specific data for them.
  • Limitations:
  • No model captures decades of latency, unilateral reactivation, or stricture fibrosis.
  • Direct inoculation skips the blood-borne seeding step.
  • Resources: MGI disease page DOID:9733 (https://www.informatics.jax.org/disease/DOID:9733).

Evidence gaps

  1. There are no prevalence or incidence figures specific to renal TB. Data are pooled at the GUTB or EPTB level.
  2. There are no quality-of-life (EQ-5D/SF-36) studies.
  3. There are no omics, single-cell, or spatial studies of human renal TB tissue.
  4. There is no validated small-animal renal TB model.
  5. Evidence for corticosteroids against strictures, and for short-course regimens in renal TB, is lacking.
