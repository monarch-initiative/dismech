Pleural Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0005922 Pathograph 24 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Extrapulmonary tuberculosis Pleurisy

Pleural tuberculosis (tuberculous pleuritis, tuberculous pleural effusion) is infection of the pleura by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and is one of the commonest forms of extrapulmonary tuberculosis. In its common form a small number of bacilli and their antigens reach the pleural space, usually from a ruptured subpleural caseous focus, 6-12 weeks after primary infection or on reactivation. Previously sensitised Th1 lymphocytes home to the pleura and mount a compartmentalised, interferon-gamma-dominated delayed-type hypersensitivity response. Cytokine-driven capillary leak and lymphatic obstruction by pleural granulomas produce a unilateral, straw-coloured, protein-rich, lymphocyte-predominant exudate. The effective Th1 response keeps the bacillary load low, so smears are almost always negative and fluid culture yields are modest, while pleural fluid adenosine deaminase and interferon-gamma are high. Patients are typically young adults with an acute illness of fever, cough and pleuritic chest pain. The effusion usually resolves spontaneously within months, but most untreated patients later develop active pulmonary or extrapulmonary tuberculosis. Diagnosis rests on caseating granulomas or mycobacteria in pleural tissue or fluid, or in high-prevalence settings on a lymphocytic exudate with a high adenosine deaminase level. Treatment is the standard regimen for pulmonary tuberculosis; therapeutic thoracentesis relieves dyspnoea, adjunctive corticosteroids are not routinely recommended, and residual pleural thickening is common.

Pleural Th1 Delayed-Type Hypersensitivity Response
Memory Th1 cells sensitised at the initial infection home selectively to the pleura and produce more interferon-gamma than their blood counterparts, compartmentalising the response. Interferon-gamma activates pleural macrophages, which release adenosine deaminase (the ADA2 isoenzyme). In sensitised guinea pigs, lymphocyte depletion prevents the effusion, so the lymphocytic influx is required for it.

  Pleural tuberculosis (tuberculous pleuritis, tuberculous pleural effusion) is
  infection of the pleura by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and is one of the
  commonest forms of extrapulmonary tuberculosis. In its common form a small
  number of bacilli and their antigens reach the pleural space, usually from a
  ruptured subpleural caseous focus, 6-12 weeks after primary infection or on
  reactivation. Previously sensitised Th1 lymphocytes home to the pleura and
  mount a compartmentalised, interferon-gamma-dominated delayed-type
  hypersensitivity response. Cytokine-driven capillary leak and lymphatic
  obstruction by pleural granulomas produce a unilateral, straw-coloured,
  protein-rich, lymphocyte-predominant exudate. The effective Th1 response keeps
  the bacillary load low, so smears are almost always negative and fluid
  culture yields are modest, while pleural fluid adenosine deaminase and
  interferon-gamma are high. Patients are typically young adults with an acute
  illness of fever, cough and pleuritic chest pain. The effusion usually
  resolves spontaneously within months, but most untreated patients later
  develop active pulmonary or extrapulmonary tuberculosis. Diagnosis rests on
  caseating granulomas or mycobacteria in pleural tissue or fluid, or in
  high-prevalence settings on a lymphocytic exudate with a high adenosine
  deaminase level. Treatment is the standard regimen for pulmonary tuberculosis;
  therapeutic thoracentesis relieves dyspnoea, adjunctive corticosteroids are
  not routinely recommended, and residual pleural thickening is common.

Record notes

Scope. This entry covers the common paucibacillary, lymphocytic tuberculous pleural effusion (tuberculous pleuritis). Frank tuberculous empyema (MONDO:0005999, a MONDO child of this term), with purulent, multibacillary fluid and a thick pleural rind, is curated separately in Tuberculous_Empyema.yaml and appears here only as a pointer subtype and as the failed-containment complication. Tuberculosis.yaml carries a single Tuberculous Pleuritis node within the general tuberculosis pathograph; this entry is the full curation of that site. Lipid effusions (pseudochylothorax, chylothorax) mentioned in the cited review are not modelled. Evidence base. The mechanism rests on narrative reviews and on guinea pig and human pleural-fluid studies summarised in them; the clinical picture on retrospective or small prospective hospital series from Spain, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Taiwan, Brazil (children), Uganda and Zimbabwe; diagnostic accuracy on pooled meta-analyses whose included studies were all at high risk of bias; treatment on one Cochrane review of six corticosteroid trials and one randomised drainage trial. 1. Disease Information

Overview. Pleural TB is a form of extrapulmonary TB. M. tuberculosis infects the pleural space and causes a usually unilateral, lymphocyte-predominant, exudative pleural effusion. - The older idea was that the effusion was purely a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction. That view "has given way to the idea that it stems from direct infection of the pleural space" (Vorster et al. 2015, PMID 26150911; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4466424). - The gold-standard diagnosis still depends on detecting M. tuberculosis in pleural fluid or a pleural biopsy (PMID 26150911). - Because the effusion is paucibacillary, diagnosis is hard, and pleural biopsy is needed when fluid studies are indeterminate (PMID 34536698, Respir Med 2021).

2. Etiology

Causal factor (infectious). The cause is Mycobacterium tuberculosis (NCBITaxon:1773), or less commonly another member of the MTB complex such as M. bovis (NCBITaxon:1765).

Two disease forms - Primary pleurisy. This follows recent primary infection, often in young people in high-burden settings. - Reactivation pleurisy. This occurs in older adults in low-burden settings. - HIV changes the picture. In sub-Saharan Africa, TB pleurisy "ha[s] become associated with older age, a chronic onset, and an increased mycobacterial load" (Zimbabwe cohort, PMID 9484963).

Risk factors - HIV co-infection. - In Uganda, 80% of 142 adults with TB pleurisy were HIV-positive, and they had more severe and longer illness (PMID 11495266). - In Harare, 85% of 90 patients were HIV-positive, and a CD4 count below 200 was linked to a positive pleural smear (37% vs 0%) (PMID 9484963). - Age and sex. - In US surveillance for 1993–2003, pleural TB was more frequent than pulmonary TB among people aged 65 or older (30.4% vs 23.3%) (PMID 17426219). - The male-to-female ratio is about 2:1 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4958858). - TB exposure. Contact with a TB case is a key clue in children (Brazilian pediatric series, https://scielo.br/j/jped/a/9cQPGk7GL4pz4M3WFPZ3Trs/). - Host genetics (susceptibility only, weak evidence). - One Taiwanese study hypothesized that TLR2 polymorphisms predispose pulmonary-TB patients to pleural involvement (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2824655). - IL10 promoter variants (−1082G/A in Caucasians, −592C/A in Asians) are associated with TB generally, not specifically with pleurisy (meta-analysis in the same search results). - No pleurisy-specific GWAS or ClinVar entries were found.

Protective factors. Nothing pleurisy-specific was retrieved. General TB control measures (BCG vaccination, treating latent infection, HIV antiretroviral therapy) are plausible protective factors, but I did not retrieve pleural-specific evidence for them.

Gene–environment interaction. No data were retrieved. The interaction of host immune genotype with HIV status is a plausible but unstudied area.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Primary infection or reactivation. Inhaled M. tuberculosis is taken up by alveolar macrophages and forms a subpleural focus. This leads to a caseous subpleural lesion that ruptures into the pleural space, or to haematogenous or lymphatic seeding. Rupture is the classical model; direct infection of the pleura is now favoured (PMID 26150911).
  2. Mycobacterial antigens enter the pleural space. This results in antigen uptake by pleural macrophages and mesothelial cells, and production of chemokines and cytokines. Mesothelial cells present antigen and promote Th9 differentiation, and anaphylatoxins drive cytokine secretion by mesothelial cells and CD16⁺ monocytes (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8291687; in vitro and human samples).
  3. Recruitment of effector T cells. Chemokines lead to compartmentalised accumulation of CD4⁺ Th1 and Th17 cells in the pleura. In single-cell RNA sequencing, CD4 T cells were about 43% of pleural mononuclear cells and "favored a Th1 and Th17 response" (Yang et al. 2023, PMID 37435066). Parietal pleura shows more CD3, CD4, CCR4 and Th17 cells than nonspecific pleuritis (https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0031710).
  4. Delayed-type hypersensitivity / Th1 effector response. IFN-γ, TNF and IL-27 release leads to macrophage activation, raised ADA (from lymphocyte and monocyte activation, an inferred link) and raised pleural IFN-γ. This is the basis of the diagnostic biomarkers (§10).
  5. Inflammation increases pleural capillary permeability and blocks lymphatic drainage. This results in a protein-rich exudative effusion, which is the clinical manifestation. The mechanism is classically described and is inferred here; no primary experiment was retrieved.
  6. Granuloma formation. TNF/TNFR signalling leads to containment. In mice lacking TNF or TNFR1/2, BCG pleurisy was "uncontrolled and progressive" and caused disseminated, fatal infection (Chavez-Galan et al. 2016, PMID 27456129; mouse).
  7. Branch A, resolution. Most effusions resolve with treatment, or even spontaneously. Branch B, fibrosis. Fibrin deposition, with higher pleural LDH, lysozyme, TNF-α and IFN-γ and lower glucose and pH, leads to residual pleural thickening (koreamed; PMC3051910; associations only). Branch C, failed containment. This leads to tuberculous empyema or later pulmonary or extrapulmonary TB.
  8. Immune regulation and exhaustion. Effector Tregs are expanded via mesothelial adhesion molecules (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3777994), NK cells become exhausted, and TNF/XAF1 pathways drive T-cell apoptosis (PMID 37435066). These are likely modulators of bacillary persistence (inferred).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: acute or subacute in most patients; chronic in HIV-associated disease in Africa (PMID 9484963).
  • Age: occurs from childhood (mean age 10.9 years in the Brazilian paediatric series) to old age (in the US, over-represented at age 65 or older, PMID 17426219).
  • Natural history: effusions may resolve spontaneously, but later active TB is common if untreated. Roper & Waring (Am Rev Tuberc 1955; DOI 10.1164/artpd.1955.71.5.616) is the classic source. I could not retrieve the frequently quoted ~65% figure, so it is unverified.
  • Treated course:
  • About 47% of patients had residual pleural thickening after 6 months of chemotherapy (Korean 1999 study).
  • About 53% had slow but complete resorption (Korean 2001 study).
  • No relapse occurred over a mean 41 months among 115 patients treated with isoniazid plus rifampicin for 6 months (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1021356; Thorax 1994).
  • Critical window: early drainage and treatment may limit loculation and thickening, but the evidence is inconclusive (koreamed).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: not applicable. The disease is infectious, and host susceptibility is multifactorial.
  • US epidemiology:
  • 1993–2003: 7,549 pleural cases; the annual share of all TB was stable at a median of 3.6% (range 3.3–4.0%) (PMID 17426219).
  • 2024: pleural disease was 24% of extrapulmonary TB, second after lymphatic disease at 28% (CDC, https://www.cdc.gov/tb-surveillance-report-2024/data/site-of-disease.html).
  • Global: the share of patients with TPE who are HIV-positive varies widely, from about 85% (Zimbabwe) to about 10% (Spain) (PMC4958858). No GBD-specific incidence for the pleural form was retrieved.
  • Sex ratio: about 2:1 male to female (PMC4958858).
  • Founder effects, penetrance and carrier frequency: not applicable.

Microbiology - Pleural fluid culture yield is 63% with liquid media, and sputum culture yield is 48%. Combined, they reach 79% (382-patient study, search results). - Induced sputum is culture-positive in 52% of patients even without parenchymal disease (Conde et al. 2003, as cited in PMC4958858). - Liquid culture outperforms solid culture (PMID 11495266).

Molecular tests - Xpert MTB/RIF has pooled sensitivity of 30% and specificity of 99% (Huo & Peng, in the search results). Against a composite reference it detects 22.7% of patients, and against culture 51.4% (Sehgal; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6019837). - Xpert Ultra has sensitivity of 52% and specificity of 98% against a composite reference (Medicine 2022). Head to head, it is more sensitive than Xpert (PLoS ONE 2022, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9273090/). - Xpert and Ultra are useful to rule in TB, but not to rule it out.

Biopsy - Closed needle biopsy has sensitivity of 66% for histology, 48% for culture and 79% combined. Thoracoscopy reaches 100%, 76% and 100% (ERJ 2003, https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/22/4/589). - In a 2018 randomized trial, ultrasound-guided biopsy had 82% sensitivity versus 90% for thoracoscopy (p=0.30) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6192452). - The typical histological finding is caseating granuloma.

Clinical criteria and differential diagnosis - A working definition is a lymphocytic exudate with ADA ≥35 U/L after other causes are excluded (Porcel group, Arch Bronconeumol 2019). - The differential diagnosis includes malignant effusion (low ADA, cytology positive), lymphoma (which can also raise ADA), parapneumonic effusion/empyema (neutrophilic), and rheumatoid effusion.

Genetic testing: not applicable to the host. Pathogen drug-resistance testing (rpoB with Xpert) applies.

Screening: none specific beyond TB contact tracing and IGRA or tuberculin skin testing.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: no disease-specific mortality data were retrieved. HIV-associated disease is more severe (PMID 11495266).
  • Recovery: treatment is highly effective. There were no relapses in 115 patients at about 41 months (PMC1021356).
  • Complications:
  • Residual pleural thickening in 47–81%, with ≥10 mm in about 20–32% (koreamed; PMC3051910)
  • Loculation (17.5% in children)
  • Empyema
  • Later pulmonary TB if untreated (historical data; figure unverified)
  • Prognostic factors for residual thickening: positive AFB and granuloma on biopsy, higher LDH, IFN-γ, TNF-α and lysozyme, and lower glucose and pH. The findings are inconsistent across studies (koreamed; PMC3051910).

Antituberculous therapy - Standard regimen: 6 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (2HRZE/4HR). Suggested NCIT and ChEBI codes:

  • Older evidence shows isoniazid plus rifampicin for 6 months is effective (PMC1021356). Isoniazid plus rifampicin was as effective as adding pyrazinamide, with fewer adverse effects (2019 report via TB Online).
  • Current guidelines add ethambutol because of isoniazid resistance (NCT01560078 background).

Corticosteroids (adjunctive prednisolone) - In the Cochrane review (6 trials, 590 participants), residual effusion was lower with steroids: - At 8 weeks: RR 0.54 (0.37–0.78) - At 24 weeks: RR 0.35 (0.18–0.66) - The evidence was low certainty, there was no clear long-term lung-function benefit, and steroids caused more adverse events leading to discontinuation. - Kaposi's sarcoma occurred in 6/99 steroid recipients versus 0/98 controls in the HIV-positive trial. - The authors note the evidence raises "doubt as to whether such treatment is worthwhile" (PMID 28290161). They are not routinely recommended.

Procedures - Therapeutic thoracentesis or pigtail drainage relieves symptoms. Its effect on residual thickening is inconclusive (koreamed). - Decortication is used for symptomatic fibrothorax or empyema. - Intrapleural fibrinolytics: no trial data were retrieved.

Gaps - Pharmacogenomics: not retrieved. NAT2 acetylator status is a general isoniazid issue and was not verified here. - Experimental: NCT01560078 (AIIMS, thrice-weekly DOTS; outcome is ultrasound resolution at 6 months). It is listed as completed, but no results were found. - Gene, cell and RNA therapies: not applicable.

13. Prevention

  • Primary:
  • BCG vaccination
  • Infection control and contact tracing
  • Treatment of latent TB infection
  • Antiretroviral therapy for HIV (inferred from the HIV association; not retrieved as pleural-specific evidence)
  • Secondary: early diagnosis with ADA and culture, and sputum culture even when no infiltrate is present (PMC4958858).
  • Tertiary: a full course of therapy to prevent later pulmonary TB, and drainage to limit loculation and fibrothorax.
  • Genetic counselling: not applicable.
