Female_Infertility_Due_To_Zona_Pellucida_Defect

Mendelian MONDO:0014342 Pathograph 22 Show in embeddings browser Female Infertility Oocyte Maturation Defect

Female infertility due to zona pellucida defect (also designated oocyte maturation defect 1, OOMD1; OMIM:615774) is a Mendelian disorder of female gamete development and extracellular matrix assembly caused by pathogenic variants in ZP1, which encodes zona pellucida glycoprotein 1, the intermolecular disulfide-forming cross-linker of human zona pellucida (ZP) filaments. Affected women have normal female karyotypes, normal secondary sexual development, normal menstrual cycles, and normal ovarian reserve and hormone profiles, yet present with primary female infertility revealed during assisted reproductive technology (ART) cycles. Loss-of-function or dominant-negative ZP1 variants impair ZP1 homodimerization, intracellular transport, secretion, or heteromeric assembly with ZP2, ZP3, and ZP4, resulting in a clinical spectrum ranging from retrieval of viable metaphase II oocytes with a thin or completely absent zona pellucida that fail conventional in vitro fertilization (IVF), to intra-follicular cumulus-oocyte complex disorganization, fragile or degenerated atretic oocytes, and genuine empty follicle syndrome (GEFS) in which no oocytes are recovered despite normal follicular growth and steroidogenesis. While conventional IVF fails because the absent or defective ZP cannot support normal sperm-egg interaction, intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) of viable ZP-free metaphase II oocytes can bypass the zona pellucida barrier and achieve blastocyst development and live birth in a subset of patients.

ZP1 Loss-of-Function and Cytoplasmic Co-Sequestration of Zona Pellucida Glycoproteins
Pathogenic truncating, splice-site, or missense variants in ZP1 lead to premature stop codons, reduced protein stability, or misfolded ZP1 polypeptides that fail to undergo normal post-translational trafficking and cleavage-coupled secretion in growing oocytes. Instead of being secreted into the extracellular perivitelline space, mutant ZP1 accumulates inside the oocyte cytoplasm and endoplasmic reticulum and aberrantly binds and sequesters wild-type ZP1, ZP2, ZP3, and ZP4 intracellularly, preventing their transport to the oocyte surface.

Failure of ZP1-N1 Disulfide Cross-Linking and Zona Pellucida Extracellular Matrix Assembly
In healthy human oocytes, secreted ZP2, ZP3, and ZP4 polymerize into long filaments that are covalently cross-linked into a stable three-dimensional extracellular matrix via intermolecular disulfide bonds (mediated by conserved Cys66 in chicken ZP1 and the corresponding Cys77 in the N-terminal ZP-N1 domain of human ZP1) forming ZP1-N1 homodimers, while human ZP4-N1 remains monomeric and cannot substitute for ZP1 cross-linking. Pathogenic ZP1 variants either eliminate secreted ZP1, disrupt ZP1-N1 homodimerization (such as p.Trp83Arg and p.Arg109His), alter the trefoil or ZP domains, or abolish heteromeric interaction with ZP2 and ZP3, resulting in a markedly thinned, fragile, or completely absent zona pellucida matrix.

Impaired Cumulus-Oocyte Complex Organization and Granulosa-Oocyte Crosstalk
During ovarian folliculogenesis, the zona pellucida provides the structural scaffold that stabilizes transzonal projections and gap-junctional communication between surrounding cumulus granulosa cells and the growing primary oocyte. In ZP1-deficient follicles, preantral follicles initially form a thin ZP composed of ZP2, ZP3, and ZP4 without ZP1; however, as follicles expand into the antral stage and antral cavity fluid accumulates, the uncross-linked ZP separates from surrounding granulosa cells, cumulus-oocyte complex (COC) architecture collapses, and bidirectional nutrient and paracrine signaling between cumulus granulosa cells and the oocyte is disrupted.

Oocyte Mitochondrial Dysfunction, Fragility, and Intra-Follicular Degeneration
Deprived of a protective zona pellucida scaffold and cumulus-oocyte metabolic coupling, ZP1-deficient oocytes develop transcriptomic and ultrastructural hallmarks of cellular stress, including downregulation of antioxidant defenses (such as SOD2), oxidative stress, extensive mitochondrial swelling at germinal vesicle and metaphase II stages, increased autophagy, and extreme mechanical vulnerability to shear stress. Consequently, oocytes either degenerate within antral follicles prior to ovulation or rupture during transvaginal follicular aspiration, resulting in retrieval of shriveled, dark degenerated oocytes or genuine empty follicle syndrome (GEFS).

1. Disease Information

Overview. The disorder is an autosomal recessive form of primary female infertility, with a smaller autosomal dominant arm. It is caused mainly by pathogenic variants in ZP1, which encodes zona pellucida glycoprotein 1. ZP1 cross-links the filaments of the zona pellucida (ZP), the extracellular coat around the oocyte.

Affected women otherwise look reproductively normal: normal karyotype, menarche, menstrual cycles, hormone levels, ovarian reserve and tubal/uterine anatomy. The defect only shows up during ART cycles. Retrieval then yields one of three things: - no oocytes at all (genuine empty follicle syndrome, GEFS); - shrivelled, degenerated oocytes; - mature MII oocytes with no ZP, or a very thin one, which fail conventional IVF.

"Here we describe a form of infertility with an autosomal recessive mode of inheritance, characterized by abnormal eggs that lack a zona pellucida. We identified a homozygous frameshift mutation in ZP1 in six family members." — Huang et al., NEJM 2014, PMID:24670168 (abstract quote)

2. Etiology

Primary cause. Germline pathogenic ZP1 variants. Inheritance is mostly biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous), with a minority of heterozygous variants that appear to act in a dominant-negative way.

Genetic risk factors: - Consanguinity. The index family was a consanguineous Chinese pedigree (PMID:24670168), and the North African founder families are also consanguineous (PMID:42060237). - Founder variant ZP1 c.1097G>A p.(Arg366Gln) in North African populations (PMID:35460069, PMID:42060237).

"The recurrent ZP1 c.1097G > A p.(Arg366Gln) variant contributes to EFS in consanguineous North African families." — PMID:42060237 (abstract quote) - Paternally transmitted heterozygous variants with sex-limited expression, for example ZP1 p.Tyr262Cys (PMID:38966008). Carrier fathers are fertile.

Environmental risk factors: none known. The disorder is fully genetic. Maternal age and stimulation protocol affect ART outcome but do not cause the zona defect.

Protective factors / gene–environment interactions: none described.

Modifiers. Phenotype severity (EFS vs ZP-free MII oocytes) depends on variant type and location. Truncating and splice variants lean toward EFS or degeneration. Some missense variants (for example in the ZP-N1 domain) allow retrieval of ZP-free MII oocytes (PMID:36476320; PMID:42060237). No modifier genes have been identified.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene: ZP1 (hgnc:13187; 12 exons; 638-aa type I membrane glycoprotein). Its domains run from N- to C-terminus: signal peptide, ZP-N1 (the cross-linking domain), proline-rich linker, trefoil (P-type) domain, bipartite ZP module (ZP-N and ZP-C), consensus furin cleavage site, transmembrane domain and cytoplasmic tail.

Variant spectrum.

"A total of 47 ZP1 mutations have been reported in 19 pieces of literature … Mutations are distributed in all 12 exons of ZP1 gene … including 13 nonsense mutations, 16 missense mutations, seven splicing mutations and 11 frameshift mutations" — PMID:36476320

Origin and function. All variants are germline. The functional consequence is loss of function: truncation, absent secretion, or loss of ZP1-N1 dimerization. Some heterozygous missense and frameshift alleles are proposed to act dominant-negatively, by trapping wild-type ZP2, ZP3 and ZP4 inside the cell (PMID:24670168; PMID:33272616; PMID:32573113). Strictly, heterozygous causality rests on a few families and in-vitro data, so treat it as moderate evidence.

Related ZP genes (differential/allelic group): - ZP3, heterozygous p.Ala134Thr. Autosomal dominant EFS with paternal transmission (Chen et al., AJHG 2017, PMID:28886344 [verify]). A ZP2 loss-of-function variant has been modelled in humans and rats (PMC10244809). - ZP4. Heterozygous loss-of-function variants were found in 3 of 26 women with thin, irregular ZP (about 11%); ICSI pregnancies were achieved (summarized in PMID:36476320). - Combined cohorts:

Epigenetics and chromosomal abnormalities: none reported. Karyotype is normal (46,XX).

5. Environmental Information

Not applicable. No toxin, lifestyle or infectious contribution is known. Note that acquired, iatrogenic "false" EFS (for example from a faulty hCG trigger) is a non-genetic phenocopy and has to be excluded clinically (see §10).

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. Germline ZP1 variant: truncating, splice, or missense in ZP-N1, trefoil or ZP module.
  2. This leads to loss of secreted ZP1. Mutant ZP1 is misfolded or truncated and is retained in the oocyte cytoplasm / ER instead of being secreted (shown in HeLa, CHO and 293T cells, and in rat oocytes).

    "secretion of hZP1Mut is completely abolished" — PMID:31300655 (in vitro)

  3. Branch A (mainly truncating and dominant-negative alleles). Retained mutant ZP1 binds and co-sequesters wild-type ZP3 and ZP4, and possibly ZP2, inside the cell, so they cannot be exported.

    "the aberrant ZP1 results in the sequestration of ZP3 in the cytoplasm, thereby preventing the formation of the zona pellucida around the oocyte." — PMID:24670168 (in vitro)

Branch B (ZP-N1 missense alleles). ZP1 is secreted but cannot form the disulfide-bonded ZP1-N1 homodimer, so ZP2/ZP3/ZP4 filaments are not cross-linked. Human ZP4 cannot substitute.

"W83R … significantly reduces its ability to form disulphide-bonded homodimers … the mutation leads to lack of the ZP by hindering human ZP filament cross-linking." — PMID:31300655 (in vitro, structural) 4. Both branches result in failed ZP matrix assembly or maintenance. The ZP is absent, very thin, or fragile around growing oocytes. Human ovarian histology shows a thin, ZP1-lacking ZP in preantral follicles (PMID:31734689; one case). 5. Branch point at the antral transition. - 5a. If the matrix is lost or unstable in antral follicles, cumulus–oocyte complex (COC) organization and transzonal oocyte–granulosa communication fail. [Partly inferred] Human histology supports the COC defect (PMID:31734689). The mechanism through communication loss is hypothesized (PMID:36476320). In macaques, ZP1 loss causes oxidative stress, SOD2 downregulation, mitochondrial swelling and autophagy (PMID:40923306; model organism). - 5a → 6a. These changes lead to oocyte degeneration in the follicle, or to fragile oocytes that rupture during aspiration. Clinically this is GEFS or degenerated oocytes. - 5b. If oocytes survive to MII, they lack the ZP sperm-binding matrix. ZP2-N terminus binding, the ZP-induced acrosome reaction, and the zona block to polyspermy are all missing. - 5b → 6b. This leads to fertilization failure in conventional IVF. ICSI bypasses it (§12). 6. Clinical endpoint: primary female infertility (obligate).

Upstream vs downstream. Steps 1–3 are molecular and oocyte-intrinsic. Step 4 is extracellular matrix. Steps 5–6 are follicular and gamete-level.

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Organ: ovary, UBERON:0000992 (primary; the only organ affected). No secondary organ involvement. Body system: female reproductive.
  • Tissue: ovarian follicle (look up the UBERON ID), cumulus oophorus, and the zona pellucida ECM.
  • Cells: oocyte, primary oocyte, granulosa/cumulus cells.
  • Subcellular: oocyte ER/secretory pathway (retention) and the extracellular egg coat.
  • Laterality: bilateral (all oocytes affected).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: the molecular defect is present from oocyte growth, which is fetal/prepubertal for primordial follicles and pubertal onward for growth. It is clinically silent until adulthood, when infertility is investigated or ART is attempted.
  • Course: stable and lifelong. No stages and no remission.
  • Critical window: the preantral→antral follicle transition, when the ZP must be assembled and maintained (PMID:31734689).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive (HP:0000007) is the main mode. A minority is autosomal dominant (HP:0000006) with sex-limited expression: male carriers, homozygous or heterozygous, are fertile.

    "The ZP1 variants were inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern; the ZP2 and ZP3 variants were inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern." — PMID:33272616

  • Penetrance: complete in homozygous females, as far as reported. Penetrance of heterozygous alleles is uncertain.
  • Expressivity: variable (EFS vs degeneration vs ZP-free MII), correlated with genotype.
  • Founder effect: p.Arg366Gln in North Africa (Algeria/Tunisia/Morocco [country breakdown to verify]).
  • Consanguinity: a major contributor in reported families.
  • Prevalence: unknown in the general population; ultra-rare (ORPHA lists no estimate [verify]). In enriched cohorts:
  • ZP gene variants in 18/35 (51.4%) of Chinese GEFS/degeneration patients (PMID:33272616);
  • homozygous ZP1 p.Arg366Gln in 5/27 (18.5%) of North African oocyte-maturation-defect patients (PMID:35460069);
  • a 2025 BJOG retrospective study of 12 Chinese women with EFS or degenerated oocytes found rare ZP variants [PMID to verify].
  • Underlying context: GEFS itself is reported in roughly 0.016–0.07% of IVF cycles [verify].
  • Sex ratio: female-only phenotype.

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical and laboratory work-up:
  • Normal FSH, LH, E2 and AMH; normal antral follicle count; normal follicular growth on ultrasound during stimulation.
  • At retrieval: no oocytes, degenerated oocytes, or ZP-free/thin-ZP oocytes, seen under light microscopy (polarized-light ZP imaging can quantify thickness).
  • Exclude "false EFS" by confirming adequate serum hCG or LH on trigger day.
  • Histology (research only): ZP1 immunostaining of ovarian tissue shows a thin ZP lacking ZP1 (PMID:31734689).
  • Genetic testing (definitive). Use whole-exome sequencing, or a female-infertility / oocyte-maturation-defect panel covering ZP1, ZP2, ZP3, ZP4, TUBB8, PATL2, WEE2, PANX1, BTG4, TLE6, NLRP family genes and others.

    "In this cohort of North African OMD patients, whole exome sequencing permitted to diagnose 44% of the patients studied and to identify a new frequent ZP1 variant." — PMID:35460069

Targeted testing for p.Arg366Gln is reasonable in North African patients. Microarray, karyotype, FISH, mtDNA and repeat testing are not relevant beyond confirming 46,XX. - Diagnostic ICSI. A small test ICSI can reveal the embryo-development potential of ZP-free oocytes (PMID:32847637). - NCIT: Genetic Testing NCIT:C15709. LOINC has no disease-specific assay. - Differential diagnosis: - OOMD3 (ZP3; AD) - OOMD6 (ZP2; thin ZP with sperm-binding failure) - ZP4 thin zona - OOMD2 (TUBB8; MI arrest with an intact ZP; PMID:26789871) - PATL2 (GV arrest; PMID:35460069 contrasts it with ZP1) - WEE2 (fertilization failure, intact ZP) - iatrogenic false EFS - diminished ovarian reserve / POI (abnormal AMH and FSH) - Screening: none at population level. Cascade testing of sisters is reasonable. Carrier screening is possible in founder populations but not established.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Survival, life expectancy and mortality are not affected. There is no systemic morbidity.
  • The outcome is infertility with autologous oocytes. Prognosis depends on genotype and phenotype:

    "Three patients with ZP1 mutations, two with ZP2 mutations, and three with ZP4 mutations had successful pregnancies through Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) from ZFO or ZTO." — PMID:36476320

The same review built a pregnancy-prediction model (AUC 0.898) from mutation type and location [verify the figure in the full text]. - Poor-prognosis predictors: biallelic truncating ZP1 variants, EFS, and fully degenerated oocytes. Patients in this group usually need oocyte donation. - Complications: repeated futile stimulation cycles, OHSS risk with re-stimulation, and psychological burden.

There is no disease-modifying therapy. Management is reproductive.

13. Prevention

  • Primary: none. The disease is genetic.
  • Secondary: genotype-guided ART planning. Women with prior EFS, degenerated oocytes or ZP-free oocytes should be offered ZP-gene testing before repeat cycles. They should go straight to ICSI rather than IVF, or to donation when the genotype predicts EFS (PMID:32573113; PMID:36476320; 2025 BJOG study).
  • Counseling: cascade testing of female siblings, especially in consanguineous or North African families. PGT for future generations is theoretically possible, but no one has reported it and there is little rationale because the phenotype is only infertility.
  • Vaccination / public health: not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • OMIA: "Infertility, ZP1-related" in Macaca fascicularis (NCBITaxon:9541), OMIA 003005-9541. This entry records the engineered model (PMID:40923306), not natural disease.

  • Comparative biology: ZP gene composition differs across species, and this explains the phenotypes seen in models:
  • humans, macaques and rats have four ZP proteins (ZP1–4);
  • in mice, Zp4 is a pseudogene (three proteins);
  • birds and fish have different ZP repertoires.
