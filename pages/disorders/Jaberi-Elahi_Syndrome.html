Jaberi-Elahi Syndrome

Mendelian MONDO:0060711 Pathograph 31 Show in embeddings browser Mendelian Neurodevelopmental Disorder Genetic Disease

Jaberi-Elahi syndrome (JABELS) is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in GTPBP2, a translational GTPase that binds the ribosome rescue protein Pelota and is thought to release ribosomes that pause when the tRNA for a codon is scarce. Most affected children come from consanguineous families. Motor and cognitive impairment begin in infancy and range from walking with an ataxic gait to absent motor control and speech, usually with microcephaly that can begin before birth, hypotonia, early seizures, spasticity, choreoathetoid movements, retinal dysfunction with visual impairment, cerebellar atrophy and, in some families, agenesis of the corpus callosum. Ectodermal features (sparse scalp hair, eyebrows and eyelashes, abnormal teeth), scoliosis and limb contractures are common. The ectodermal features and a peripheral neuropathy are most prominent in older individuals, and brain iron accumulation, found only in the first family, may appear only after childhood. Deficiency of the paralog GTPBP1 causes an identical syndrome. In mice, Gtpbp2 loss causes codon-specific ribosome stalling and neuron death only together with a strain-specific mutation in a neuronal tRNA gene, and no stalling was found in patient fibroblasts.

Pathophysiology

8
Loss of GTPBP2 Function
Biallelic nonsense, frameshift and canonical splice-site variants remove or truncate GTPBP2; the founding splice variant deletes exon 9 and yields a protein lacking its conserved C-terminal domains. A few missense variants have also been reported. GTPBP2 is a cytoplasmic translational GTPase of the family that includes eEF1A, eRF3 and Hbs1, and binds the ribosome rescue protein Pelota. Its biochemical activity is not settled: purified GTPBP2 binds GTP weakly, and aminoacyl-tRNA stimulates the binding, but unlike its paralog GTPBP1 it has no elongation activity and does not stimulate exosomal mRNA decay.

Unresolved Ribosome Pausing
GTPBP2 is thought to rescue ribosomes that pause when the tRNA for a codon is scarce. In Gtpbp2 mutant mice on the C57BL/6J background, which carries a processing mutation in the neuron-specific arginine tRNA gene n-Tr20, elongating ribosomes stall at AGA codons in the brain. Whether pausing occurs in human neurons is not known; it was not seen by ribosome profiling of fibroblasts from GTPBP2-deficient patients.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — JABELS is an autosomal-recessive neurodevelopmental/neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic GTPBP2 loss-of-function (F001)

The disorder was first reported by Jaberi et al. (2016) in a consanguineous Iranian family with three affected siblings. Using linkage analysis plus exome sequencing, they identified a homozygous splice-site mutation in GTPBP2 that deletes exon 9 and produces a truncated protein lacking conserved C-terminal domains. The authors state: "A splice site mutation in GTPBP2 that encodes GTP-binding protein 2 was identified in the patients and considered possible cause of their disease" and note that "Iron accumulation in the brain of the patients was a notable phenotypic feature" (PMID: 26675814).

Independent confirmation came from Bertoli-Avella et al. (2018), who reported three unrelated families with homozygous nonsense variants—c.430C>T p.(Arg144*), c.1219C>T p.(Gln407*), and c.1408C>T p.(Arg470*)—establishing pathogenicity beyond the index family: "whole-exome sequencing identified the homozygous non-sense variants c.430[C>T];[C>T] p.(Arg144*), c.1219[C>T];[C>T] p.(Gln407*) and c.1408[C>T];[C>T] p.(Arg470*) in GTPBP2" (PMID: 29449720). Together these establish a recessive loss-of-function mechanism. Identifiers: OMIM #617988; MONDO:0060711; gene GTPBP2 (6p21.1); synonym JABELS.

Finding 3 — Mechanism: GTPBP2 is a ribosome-rescue factor; its loss causes codon-specific ribosome stalling and neurodegeneration (F003)

The pivotal mechanistic work came from Ishimura et al. (2014), who showed in mice that loss of GTPBP2—a binding partner of the ribosome-recycling protein Pelota—combined with mutation of a CNS-specific tRNA gene (n-Tr20) causes ribosome stalling and widespread neurodegeneration: "loss of GTPBP2, a novel binding partner of the ribosome recycling protein Pelota, in mice with a mutation in a tRNA gene that is specifically expressed in the central nervous system causes ribosome stalling and widespread neurodegeneration" (PMID: 25061210).

This paradigm was reinforced by Terrey et al. (2020) studying the paralog GTPBP1: "Loss of a tRNA gene leads to ribosomal pausing that is resolved by the translational GTPase GTPBP2, and in its absence causes neuron death" (PMID: 33186095), indicating that GTPBP1 and GTPBP2 act non-redundantly in the same ribosome-rescue pathway. Salpietro et al. (2024) added that human disease variants are loss-of-function with ribosome-profiling abnormalities, and that Drosophila knockdown of the ortholog CG2017 recapitulates the defects (PMID: 38118446).

GO terms: GO:0072344 rescue of stalled ribosome; GO:0006412 translation; GO:0003924 GTPase activity. CL terms: CL:0000540 neuron; CL:0000210 photoreceptor cell (retinal degeneration).

Finding 4 — GTPBP1 and GTPBP2 produce an identical syndrome; both are translational GTPases related to eEF1A/eRF3/Hbs1 (F004)

Salpietro et al. (2024) described 20 individuals from 16 families with biallelic GTPBP1 or GTPBP2 variants sharing "microcephaly, profound neurodevelopmental impairment, pathognomonic craniofacial features, and ectodermal defects", together with abnormal vision/hearing, progressive spasticity, choreoathetoid movements, refractory epilepsy, and brain atrophy; all variants were loss-of-function (PMID: 38118446).

Zinoviev et al. (2018) classified these proteins biochemically: "GTP-binding protein 1 (GTPBP1) and GTPBP2 comprise a divergent group of translational GTPases with obscure functions, which are most closely related to eEF1A, eRF3, and Hbs1" (PMID: 30108131). GTPBP1 has eEF1A-like aminoacyl-tRNA delivery activity and stimulates exosomal mRNA degradation, implicating both paralogs in mRNA surveillance and ribosome-associated quality control (RQC).

Finding 5 — Downstream mechanism: AGA-codon stalling activates the neuroprotective GCN2–eIF2α–ATF4 integrated stress response (F005)

Ishimura et al. (2016) dissected the downstream signaling in Gtpbp2(nmf205) mice: "neuronal elongation complexes are stalled at AGA codons due to deficiencies in a tRNA(Arg)UCU tRNA and GTPBP2, a mammalian ribosome rescue factor. Increased levels of phosphorylation of eIF2α (Ser51) were detected prior to neurodegeneration" (PMID: 27085088). Transcriptomics showed ATF4 activation—the hallmark of the integrated stress response (ISR). Critically, the authors found that "this pathway was activated by the eIF2α kinase, GCN2, in an apparent deacylated tRNA-independent fashion. Further we found that the ISR attenuates neurodegeneration" (PMID: 27085088).

This is a mechanistically important nuance: the ISR is a protective, compensatory response, not the proximate cause of neuron death. Neurodegeneration therefore reflects the failure/insufficiency of ribosome rescue and proteostasis, with the ISR buffering—but not preventing—the injury. GO terms: GO:0140469 GCN2-mediated ISR signaling; GO:0034198 cellular response to amino acid starvation.

Finding 6 — Model organisms and epidemiology (F006)

Mouse: The Gtpbp2(nmf205) mouse on the B6J background (which carries the n-Tr20 tRNA^Arg mutation) develops progressive neurodegeneration, retinal degeneration, and ribosome stalling (PMID: 25061210; PMID: 27085088). Invertebrate: Drosophila knockdown of the ortholog CG2017 recapitulates defects: "we also investigated the effect of reducing expression of CG2017, an ortholog of human GTPBP1/2, in the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster" (PMID: 38118446).

Epidemiology: JABELS is ultra-rare, with ~28 individuals reported as of 2024 (PMID: 38852771). GTPBP2 is listed among autosomal-recessive NBIA (neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation) genes with a very low combined lifetime risk. Kolarova et al. (2022) estimated: "The combined estimated lifetime risk of all 13 investigated NBIA disorders is 0.88 (95% confidence interval 0.70-1.10) per 100,000 based on the global gnomAD dataset", with GTPBP2 individually far below the top contributors (PLA2G6/PANK2/COASY) (PMID: 35180557).

Finding 7 — Expanded phenotypic spectrum: severe congenital microcephaly, early seizures, white-matter and cardiac anomalies (F007)

Soğukpınar et al. (2026) reported a 4-month-old female of consanguineous parents with a novel homozygous frameshift GTPBP2 variant: "presenting with severe microcephaly, developmental delay, hypotonia, and craniofacial features. Additional findings included left-sided pes equinovarus, secundum atrial septal defect, and periventricular white matter abnormalities on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). No overt ectodermal abnormalities were observed" (PMID: 42553919). This broadens the spectrum to include cardiac (secundum ASD) and limb (pes equinovarus) anomalies and highlights that ectodermal features are non-obligate.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2018) similarly emphasized family-specific expressivity: "Notable differences include structural brain abnormalities (e.g., agenesis of the corpus callosum, exclusive to our patients), and evidence for brain iron accumulation (exclusive to the previously described family)" (PMID: 29449720). HPO terms: HP:0001631 Atrial septal defect; HP:0001762 Talipes equinovarus; HP:0002500 Abnormal cerebral white matter morphology; HP:0000252 Microcephaly.

Finding 8 — GTPBP2 protein architecture (F008)

Per UniProt Q9BX10, human GTPBP2 is a 602-amino-acid protein containing a tr-type (translational) G domain (≈residues 170–398) with GTP-binding/P-loop motifs (binding sites ~179–186, 260–264, 316–319) and an N-terminal disordered region (16–64). Functionally it is involved in the rescue of ribosome stalling due to the presence of non-functional tRNA and has very low GTP-binding activity; it interacts with PELO (Pelota). The weak/context-dependent GTP binding is supported by Zinoviev et al.: "Although GTPBP2's binding to GTP was stimulated by Phe-tRNA" (PMID: 30108131). Tissue expression is highest in thymus, spleen, and testis, with lower expression in brain, lung, kidney, and ovary. Disease-causing truncating variants (p.Arg144*, p.Gln407*, p.Arg470*, exon-9 splice deletion) remove or disrupt the G domain and/or C-terminal effector region. GO cellular component: GO:0005737 cytoplasm; GO:0022626 cytosolic ribosome (associated).

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. Biallelic loss-of-function variant in GTPBP2 (nonsense, frameshift, or splice) → absent or truncated, non-functional GTPBP2 protein lacking the tr-type G domain and/or C-terminal effector region (demonstrated; PMID 26675814, 29449720, 42553919).
  2. Loss of functional GTPBP2 → failure of PELO-dependent rescue of stalled/paused ribosomes (ribosome-associated quality control / no-go decay) (demonstrated in mouse; PMID 25061210).
  3. Impaired rescue, when combined with limiting tissue-specific tRNA supply (e.g., CNS-enriched tRNA^Arg_UCU), → codon-specific ribosome stalling at cognate codons (AGA/Arg) and accumulation of stalled elongation complexes (demonstrated in mouse; PMID 27085088).
  4. Ribosome stalling → GCN2 (eIF2α kinase) activation → eIF2α-Ser51 phosphorylation → ATF4-driven integrated stress response (ISR) (demonstrated; PMID 27085088). Branch: the ISR is neuroprotective and attenuates degeneration, so it is a compensatory side-arm, not the injurious main path.
  5. Persistent unresolved stalling, aberrant/truncated nascent peptides, and proteostatic stress (inferred for human disease from model data) → progressive neuronal and retinal cell death (demonstrated in mouse; PMID 25061210, 27085088).
  6. Neurodegeneration + abnormal neurodevelopment → clinical phenotype: motor delay, intellectual disability, speech disorder, hypotonia, spasticity, choreoathetosis, epilepsy, visual impairment, microcephaly, brain atrophy (demonstrated in humans; PMID 38852771, 38118446, 29449720).
  7. Parallel/variable branches (family-specific, mechanism inferred): brain iron accumulation (NBIA-spectrum), ectodermal defects (sparse hair), skeletal/cardiac/limb anomalies—reflecting broader roles of translational quality control in development.

Section-by-Section Knowledge Base Content

1. Disease Information

JABELS is an autosomal-recessive neurodevelopmental/neurodegenerative syndrome. Identifiers: OMIM #617988; MONDO:0060711; gene GTPBP2 (HGNC). A robust ICD-10/ICD-11, MeSH, and dedicated Orphanet code are not well established for this ultra-rare entity (information not well-indexed). Synonyms: JABELS; Jaberi-Elahi syndrome; GTPBP2-related neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA-spectrum). Information is derived from aggregated, disease-level case reports and small family series (not EHR-scale cohorts).

2. Etiology

Causal factor: purely genetic—biallelic (homozygous or compound-heterozygous) loss-of-function variants in GTPBP2. Genetic risk factor: consanguinity (most reported families are consanguineous, enriching for homozygous recessive alleles). Inheritance: autosomal recessive. Penetrance: appears complete in biallelic LoF carriers. 10. Diagnostics

Genetic testing is the diagnostic cornerstone: whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing (often with homozygosity mapping in consanguineous pedigrees) identifying biallelic GTPBP2 LoF variants; single-gene/panel testing (NBIA or intellectual-disability panels including GTPBP2) is an alternative. Imaging: brain MRI shows atrophy, white-matter abnormalities, possible corpus callosum agenesis, and T2*/SWI signal consistent with iron accumulation in a subset. Supportive studies: EEG for epilepsy; ophthalmologic/retinal evaluation; echocardiography if cardiac anomaly suspected. No specific biochemical biomarker exists. Differential diagnosis: other NBIA disorders (PKAN/PANK2, PLAN/PLA2G6, MPAN/C19orf12, BPAN/WDR45), GTPBP1-related disorder (phenotypically identical), and other recessive neurodevelopmental/microcephaly syndromes.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

Prognosis is poor: severe, lifelong intellectual and motor disability with progressive neurologic decline. Formal survival/mortality statistics are unavailable given the small cohort, but refractory epilepsy and severe neurodisability imply increased morbidity and likely reduced life expectancy. No recovery potential; management is palliative/supportive.

12. Treatment

No disease-specific or disease-modifying therapy exists. Management is supportive and multidisciplinary: anti-seizure medications for epilepsy; physical/occupational/speech therapy; management of spasticity; nutritional support; ophthalmologic and orthopedic/cardiac care as needed. The protective role of the GCN2/ISR arm (PMID 27085088) suggests ISR modulation as a theoretical future target, and tRNA-supplementation strategies (cf. tRNA overexpression rescuing related tRNA/translation disorders, PMID 34516840) are conceptual avenues—none clinically available. Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Very small human cohort (~28 patients). Frequency estimates, natural-history staging, survival, and genotype–phenotype correlations are statistically fragile.
  • Model caveat: the pivotal mouse neurodegeneration phenotype required a co-occurring CNS-tRNA modifier (n-Tr20). Whether analogous tissue-specific tRNA limitations drive the human phenotype is unproven; it is an attractive but inferred bridge.
  • Mechanism of secondary features (brain iron accumulation, ectodermal/sparse hair, skeletal/cardiac/limb anomalies) is not mechanistically explained and is family-variable.
  • No validated biomarker for diagnosis, severity, or progression monitoring.
  • No human therapeutic data; ISR-modulating or tRNA-based strategies are purely conceptual.
  • Ontology/registry indexing (ICD-11, MeSH, Orphanet code) for JABELS is incomplete.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Establish an international patient registry to pool genotype–phenotype data, natural history, and survival from the dispersed case reports.
  2. Patient-derived models: generate iPSC-derived neurons and cerebral organoids from GTPBP2-null patients to test whether codon-specific stalling and ISR activation occur in human cells without an engineered tRNA modifier.
  3. Ribosome profiling in human GTPBP2-deficient neurons to map stall sites and compare with the mouse AGA-codon signature.
  4. Test ISR modulators (e.g., ISRIB, GCN2 agonists/antagonists) in patient neurons/organoids and the Gtpbp2 mouse to determine whether enhancing the protective ISR or clearing stalled ribosomes is beneficial.
  5. tRNA-supplementation / AAV gene-replacement proof-of-concept in the Gtpbp2 mouse, leveraging the analogy to tRNA rescue of tRNA-synthetase neuropathy (PMID 34516840).
  6. Mechanistic study of brain iron accumulation in GTPBP2 deficiency to clarify its link (if any) to impaired translational quality control and to position JABELS within the NBIA spectrum.
  7. Formal ClinVar/ClinGen curation and ACMG classification of all reported GTPBP2 variants, plus submission of an Orphanet/ICD-11 entry to improve indexing.
