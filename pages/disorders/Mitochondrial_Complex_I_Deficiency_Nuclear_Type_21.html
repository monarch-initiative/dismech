Mitochondrial Complex I Deficiency Nuclear Type 21

Mendelian MONDO:0032625 Pathograph 25 Show in embeddings browser Mitochondrial Complex I Deficiency

MC1DN21 is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder caused by biallelic variants in NUBPL, which encodes an iron-sulfur cluster assembly factor for respiratory chain complex I (the yeast homologue is Ind1). The lesion is therefore not a missing structural subunit but a failure to deliver iron-sulfur clusters into the peripheral arm early in the assembly pathway, so complex I is not built in normal amounts rather than being built and then working badly. Two features distinguish it from the other numbered nuclear complex I deficiencies. **The MRI pattern is diagnostic rather than merely suggestive.** Patients were originally assembled *from* their imaging: a group with an unclassified white matter disorder sharing a distinct pattern - predominant abnormalities of the cerebellar cortex, deep cerebral white matter and corpus callosum - was selected by MRI pattern recognition, and exome sequencing then found NUBPL mutations in all of them. The authors concluded the pattern is consistent and recognisable enough to obviate other tests, including muscle or fibroblast respiratory chain assays. That is an unusually strong claim for a mitochondrial disease, where imaging is normally supportive at best. **The common allele is a branch-site variant, and its architecture matters.** c.815-27T>C sits in a branch site rather than a splice donor or acceptor, and it is present in about 1.2% of European haplotypes - far too common to be pathogenic on its own. In the first patients it was always found in cis with c.166G>A (p.Gly56Arg), but later families carry it without that second change, so the two are separable. Its pathogenicity was established functionally rather than by frequency argument: reconstructing the resulting altered C-terminus in the yeast Ind1 protein left mRNA stable but reduced protein levels, and that instability was enough to cause a strong fall in complex I activity. A second clinical presentation - generalized dystonia with bilateral striatal necrosis and cerebellar atrophy - was described in a UK kindred and is recorded here alongside the leukoencephalopathy picture. The relationship between the two is explicitly unresolved in the literature and is curated as an open question rather than smoothed into a single phenotype.

Pathophysiology

6
NUBPL Loss of Function
Biallelic NUBPL variants reduce functional NUBPL protein. The recurrent allele is the branch-site variant c.815-27T>C, present in all reported families and in roughly 1.2% of European haplotypes, which produces a frameshifted, altered C-terminus. Modelling that altered C-terminus in the yeast homologue Ind1 showed the mechanism is protein instability rather than transcript loss: mRNA was unaffected while protein level fell. Not every missense allele is equally damaging - p.Leu104Pro impaired complex I function in the yeast model while p.Val182Ala did not.

Impaired Iron-Sulfur Cluster Delivery to the Complex I Peripheral Arm
NUBPL is an iron-sulfur cluster assembly factor for complex I. Analysis of how mutant NUBPL affects assembly of the peripheral arm placed its role early in the complex I assembly pathway, at the stage where iron-sulfur clusters are inserted. The peripheral arm is the electron-transferring limb of the complex, so a cluster-insertion failure there prevents the holoenzyme being completed rather than degrading an assembled enzyme's performance.

Mitochondrial Complex I Deficiency
Complex I is the entry point of the respiratory chain, transferring electrons from NADH to ubiquinone while pumping protons. With the holoenzyme depleted, oxidative phosphorylation capacity falls. In patient fibroblasts the measurable consequence is significantly reduced spare respiratory capacity - the reserve a cell can call on above baseline demand - which is why tissues with high and fluctuating energy demand are affected first.

description: >-
  MC1DN21 is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder caused by biallelic variants in
  NUBPL, which encodes an iron-sulfur cluster assembly factor for respiratory chain complex I
  (the yeast homologue is Ind1). The lesion is therefore not a missing structural subunit but a
  failure to deliver iron-sulfur clusters into the peripheral arm early in the assembly
  pathway, so complex I is not built in normal amounts rather than being built and then
  working badly.

  Two features distinguish it from the other numbered nuclear complex I deficiencies.

  **The MRI pattern is diagnostic rather than merely suggestive.** Patients were originally
  assembled *from* their imaging: a group with an unclassified white matter disorder sharing a
  distinct pattern - predominant abnormalities of the cerebellar cortex, deep cerebral white
  matter and corpus callosum - was selected by MRI pattern recognition, and exome sequencing
  then found NUBPL mutations in all of them. The authors concluded the pattern is consistent
  and recognisable enough to obviate other tests, including muscle or fibroblast respiratory
  chain assays. That is an unusually strong claim for a mitochondrial disease, where imaging
  is normally supportive at best.

  **The common allele is a branch-site variant, and its architecture matters.** c.815-27T>C
  sits in a branch site rather than a splice donor or acceptor, and it is present in about 1.2%
  of European haplotypes - far too common to be pathogenic on its own. In the first patients it
  was always found in cis with c.166G>A (p.Gly56Arg), but later families carry it without that
  second change, so the two are separable. Its pathogenicity was established functionally
  rather than by frequency argument: reconstructing the resulting altered C-terminus in the
  yeast Ind1 protein left mRNA stable but reduced protein levels, and that instability was
  enough to cause a strong fall in complex I activity.

  A second clinical presentation - generalized dystonia with bilateral striatal necrosis and
  cerebellar atrophy - was described in a UK kindred and is recorded here alongside the
  leukoencephalopathy picture. The relationship between the two is explicitly unresolved in the
  literature and is curated as an open question rather than smoothed into a single phenotype.
