Pseudohypoaldosteronism Type 2E

Mendelian MONDO:0013782 Pathograph 24 Show in embeddings browser Pseudohypoaldosteronism type 2 Autosomal dominant disease

Pseudohypoaldosteronism type 2E is the CUL3-related form of familial hyperkalaemic hypertension (FHHt, also called pseudohypoaldosteronism type 2 or Gordon syndrome), and the most severe of its four genetic subtypes. Heterozygous and frequently de novo CUL3 variants clustered around the exon 9 splice sites all converge on skipping of exon 9, producing an in-frame deletion of residues 403 to 459 from cullin-3 (CUL3-delta9). Cullin-3 is the scaffold of a cullin-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase that, bound to the substrate adaptor KLHL3, ubiquitinates WNK1 and WNK4 for proteasomal degradation in the distal convoluted tubule. CUL3-delta9 cannot ubiquitinate WNK kinases efficiently and also depletes KLHL3, so WNK kinases accumulate, activate the downstream kinases SPAK and OSR1, and these phosphorylate and activate the thiazide-sensitive Na-Cl cotransporter NCC. The resulting salt retention raises blood pressure while reducing potassium and hydrogen ion secretion, giving hypertension with hyperkalaemia, hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis and suppressed renin despite a normal glomerular filtration rate. Cullin-3 also turns over RhoA and phosphodiesterase 5 in vascular smooth muscle, and a knock-in mouse model shows arterial pulse waveform changes, so a vascular contribution to the hypertension is recognised alongside the renal one. Children typically present earlier and more severely than in the WNK and KLHL3 subtypes, sometimes with short stature, and low-dose thiazide diuretics correct both the electrolyte disturbance and the blood pressure.

Is CUL3-delta9 hyperneddylated and over-activated, or under-neddylated and under-activated, and does the answer change what the dominant mechanism in patients is?
CONTROVERSY OPEN cul3_delta9_neddylation_direction
Attached to
pathophysiology#Loss of CUL3-delta9 Interaction with Cullin Regulators pathophysiology#CUL3-delta9 Ubiquitin Ligase Dysfunction
Two cell-based studies report opposite directions for the same modification on the same mutant protein. McCormick et al. report that CUL3-delta9 is more heavily neddylated and more activated than wild-type, with the disease arising because that over-active ligase destroys its own adaptor KLHL3. Ibeawuchi et al. report that neddylation of the same mutant and its binding to RBX1 are both significantly impaired, with the disease arising because the mutant sequesters adaptors and poisons wild-type dimers. Cornelius et al. set out to resolve it, name hyperneddylation as one of several postulated mechanisms, and conclude for a protein that is itself ligase-defective while driving autophagic KLHL3 degradation. The two accounts are not merely different emphases: they disagree on the sign of the measured modification. Both are compatible with the clinical phenotype because both end in failed WNK degradation, which is why the disagreement has persisted without being decisive for diagnosis or treatment. It matters for anything that would target the pathway pharmacologically, since a neddylation inhibitor would be expected to act in opposite directions under the two models.

What accounts for the part of the hyperkalaemia in PHA2E that NCC activation does not explain, given that reduced ROMK expression was tested in the knock-in mouse and not found?
KNOWLEDGE GAP OPEN pha2e_hyperkalemia_origin
Attached to
phenotypes#Hyperkalemia pathophysiology#Increased Distal Nephron Salt Reabsorption
The hypertension in this disease follows cleanly from NCC activation, but the hyperkalaemia does not. Two sources state that the electrolyte abnormalities are only partly driven by NCC. The obvious candidate was reduced expression of the distal secretory potassium channel ROMK, and that was the explicit target of a dedicated study: CUL3-delta9 did fail to prevent WNK4 suppression of ROMK currents in oocytes and did not support ROMK surface expression in collecting duct cells, yet in the knock-in mouse no change in ROMK expression could be shown by western blotting or confocal microscopy. So the in vitro result did not transfer to the animal, and the residual mechanism is unidentified. This is a gap rather than a controversy because no competing positive account has been offered.

CUL3 Exon 9 Skipping
Heterozygous CUL3 variants of several different types, intronic, canonical splice donor, splice acceptor and synonymous exonic, all cause skipping of exon 9 during splicing of the CUL3 transcript. The transcript stays in frame, so a stable protein lacking residues 403 to 459 is produced rather than a null allele.

Loss of CUL3-delta9 Interaction with Cullin Regulators
CUL3-delta9 loses its interaction with the COP9 signalosome, which deneddylates cullins, and with CAND1, which exchanges substrate adaptors. Cullin-RING ligase activity depends on cycling between neddylated and deneddylated states rather than on neddylation alone, so losing both regulators dysregulates the cycle. The direction of the resulting neddylation change is disputed in the literature and is recorded as an open question rather than asserted here.

KLHL3 Depletion
KLHL3, the substrate adaptor that presents WNK kinases to cullin-3, falls in abundance in the presence of CUL3-delta9. Degradation runs through both the proteasome and autophagy. In knock-in mice the fall is specific to KLHL3: other KLHL family proteins such as KLHL2 are unchanged. Because the adaptor is what recruits the substrate, losing it blocks WNK degradation even by the remaining wild-type cullin-3.

Increased Distal Nephron Salt Reabsorption
Overactive NCC reabsorbs sodium and chloride in the distal convoluted tubule. Because sodium delivered more distally is the driving force for potassium and hydrogen ion secretion in the collecting duct, taking it up earlier both expands volume and reduces the gradient available for potassium and acid excretion. This single step therefore produces the hypertension and the electrolyte picture together, and it is salt-dependent: dietary salt restriction alone improves it.

  hyperkalaemic hypertension (FHHt, also called pseudohypoaldosteronism type 2
  or Gordon syndrome), and the most severe of its four genetic subtypes.

1. Disease Information

PHA2E is a rare inherited monogenic form of low-renin hypertension within the FHHt/Gordon syndrome/PHAII spectrum. It is defined molecularly by its causal gene, CUL3 (cullin-3), which distinguishes it from the other PHAII subtypes caused by WNK1 (PHA2C), WNK4 (PHA2B), and KLHL3 (PHA2D).

Information source: The disease-level characterization here derives principally from aggregated disease-level resources (OMIM, published case series, and mechanistic studies) combined with individual patient case reports. PHA2E is sufficiently rare that no large patient-level EHR registries exist specifically for the CUL3 subtype; evidence comes from case reports, small pedigree studies, and mechanistic mouse/cell models.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — genetic. PHA2E is caused by heterozygous, dominant, predominantly de novo mutations in CUL3. Boyden et al. (2012) used exome sequencing across 41 unrelated PHAII families to identify CUL3 (and KLHL3) as causal genes: "KLHL3 mutations are either recessive or dominant, whereas CUL3 mutations are dominant and predominantly de novo" (PMID: 22266938). A key convergent feature is that diverse CUL3 mutations — spanning splice-donor and splice-acceptor sites flanking exon 9 — all produce the same outcome: "Diverse CUL3 mutations all result in skipping of exon 9, producing an in-frame deletion" (PMID: 22266938).

Genetic risk factors. The causal variants are the CUL3 exon-9 splice-site mutations themselves. There are no known common susceptibility loci or modifier alleles established specifically for PHA2E; the disorder is monogenic with high penetrance.

Environmental risk factors. Dietary sodium (salt) load worsens the hypertensive phenotype because the underlying defect is excessive renal NaCl reabsorption; dietary potassium load exacerbates hyperkalemia. These are modifiers of expression rather than causes.

Protective factors. Dietary sodium restriction is a protective/ameliorating environmental factor, reducing the substrate for NCC-driven NaCl retention. No genetic protective variants are established.

Gene-environment interactions. The core interaction is between the CUL3 genotype (which fixes NCC in a hyperactive state) and dietary salt and potassium intake, which determine the magnitude of hypertension and hyperkalemia. Co-occurrence of a second channelopathy variant can modify phenotype — a notable case reported a girl carrying both SCNN1A (PHAI) and CUL3 (PHAII) mutations, producing a complex mixed salt-handling phenotype (PMID: 36303414).

3. Phenotypes

The core phenotype is a biochemical and hemodynamic constellation. Bruel et al. (2016) summarize the typical picture: "The typical clinical picture of Gordon syndrome includes, apart from arterial hypertension and dyskaliemia, hyperchloremia metabolic acidosis, hypercalciuria, a low rate of renin, and most frequently, a normal or high rate of aldosterone. Dental abnormalities and growth retardation can also be associated" (PMID: 27369102).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

Causal gene: CUL3 (cullin-3), HGNC:2553, NCBI Gene 8452, located at chromosome 2q36.2. OMIM gene entry 603136; disease entry 614496.

Pathogenic variants. The pathognomonic lesion is skipping of exon 9, caused by heterozygous mutations at the intron 8 / exon 9 splice-acceptor site and the exon 9 / intron 9 splice-donor site, as well as some exonic variants. All converge on an in-frame deletion of 57 amino acids (residues 403-459): CUL3Δ403-459 (PMID: 22266938; PMID: 27378813).

  • Variant classification (ACMG/AMP): Pathogenic (established, recurrent, functionally validated disease-causing variants).
  • Variant type/class: Predominantly splice-site variants producing an in-frame exon-skip; functionally equivalent to an internal deletion.
  • Allele frequency: Effectively absent from population databases (gnomAD) — these are private, predominantly de novo pathogenic variants, consistent with a severe, early-onset dominant disorder.
  • Somatic vs germline: Germline; a large fraction arise de novo in the proband.
  • Functional consequence: A combination of loss of function (impaired WNK ubiquitylation) and abnormal gain/altered function (auto-ubiquitylation, loss of regulatory interactions). Schumacher et al. (2015) showed: "the PHA2E mutant, CUL3(Δ403-459), is severely compromised in its ability to ubiquitylate WNKs, possibly due to altered structural flexibility. Instead, CUL3(Δ403-459) auto-ubiquitylates and loses interaction with two important Cullin regulators: the COP9-signalosome and CAND1" (PMID: 26286618). The dual gain/loss nature is emphasized in PMID: 29897280.

Why CUL3 is more severe. The same study notes: "These findings may explain the severity of the FHHt phenotype caused by CUL3 mutations compared to those reported in KLHL3 or WNK kinases" (PMID: 26286618). Loss of COP9-signalosome (CSN) binding is central — the catalytically active CSN subunit JAB1 fails to associate with the deleted CUL3 4-helix bundle domain (PMID: 29897280), and disrupted CSN binding has been confirmed as a major contributor to the FHHt phenotype in a renal CSN-deficient mouse (PMID: 30301860; see also PMID: 31813255).

Modifier genes: Within the pathway, the levels/activity of WNK1, WNK4, KLHL3, SPAK, OSR1, and NCC (SLC12A3) modulate the net phenotype. No formal modifier loci are established for PHA2E.

Epigenetic information: No disease-specific DNA methylation or histone modification signatures have been established for PHA2E. (CUL3 itself regulates protein stability of many substrates via ubiquitination/neddylation — a post-translational rather than epigenetic mechanism.)

Chromosomal abnormalities: None characteristic; PHA2E is a single-gene splice-defect disorder, not a copy-number or structural disorder.

5. Environmental Information

PHA2E is a genetic disorder; environmental factors modulate expression rather than cause disease.

  • Environmental factors: No toxic, radiation, or occupational exposures are implicated as causes.
  • Lifestyle factors: Dietary sodium (salt) intake worsens hypertension; dietary potassium intake worsens hyperkalemia. Salt restriction is beneficial.
  • Infectious agents: None. PHA2E is not an infectious or post-infectious disease.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain

  1. A heterozygous de novo CUL3 splice-site mutation disrupts splicing → leads to skipping of exon 9 and an in-frame deletion (CUL3Δ403-459). (PMID: 22266938)
  2. CUL3Δ403-459 protein results in a structurally altered cullin-RING E3 ligase that loses binding to the COP9 signalosome (CSN) and CAND1, and instead auto-ubiquitylates. (PMID: 26286618; PMID: 29897280)
  3. The defective CUL3-KLHL3 ligase fails to ubiquitinate WNK1/WNK4 kinases → proteasomal degradation of WNKs is abrogated → leads to cytoplasmic accumulation of WNK kinases. (PMID: 29361671)
  4. Accumulated WNK kinases phosphorylate and activate SPAK and OSR1 (overactivation). (PMID: 29361671)
  5. Active SPAK/OSR1 phosphorylate and activate the thiazide-sensitive Na-Cl cotransporter (NCC) in the distal convoluted tubule. (PMID: 29361671; PMID: 39699086) 6a. Renal branch: NCC hyperactivation → increased NaCl reabsorption → volume expansion → hypertension; reduced distal Na⁺ delivery impairs K⁺ and H⁺ secretion → hyperkalemia and hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis. (PMID: 39699086) 6b. Vascular branch (demonstrated in mouse, inferred in human): The same WNK-OSR1/SPAK-SLC12a cascade is active in vascular smooth muscle, where it regulates arterial tone — contributing a vascular component to hypertension evidenced by altered arterial pulse waveform in the CUL3 knock-in mouse. (PMID: 26286618; PMID: 26152401)

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: Kidney (UBERON:0002113), specifically the renal distal convoluted tubule (DCT) (UBERON:0001292). Cornelius et al.: "In the kidney, these mutations ultimately cause hyperactivation of NCC along the renal distal convoluted tubule. Hypertension results from increased NaCl retention, and hyperkalemia by impaired K[+ secretion]" (PMID: 39699086).
  • Secondary / body systems: Cardiovascular system (hypertension, vascular smooth muscle involvement; UBERON:0004535), endocrine/renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis (suppressed renin), skeletal/dental (growth retardation, dental abnormalities).
  • Tissue and cell level: Renal tubular epithelium — DCT epithelial cells (CL:1000768) where KLHL3, CUL3, WNK kinases, and NCC are co-expressed (PMID: 22266938); vascular smooth muscle cells (CL:0000359) where the WNK-OSR1/SPAK-SLC12a cascade regulates arterial tone (PMID: 26152401). Downstream potassium handling involves the connecting tubule/collecting duct (ENaC/ROMK) secondarily — notably ROMK expression remained unaltered in the CUL3Δ403-459 mouse, indicating hyperkalemia arises chiefly from reduced distal Na⁺ delivery rather than ROMK loss (PMID: 27378813).
  • Subcellular level: Cytoplasm (WNK kinase accumulation), plasma/apical membrane (NCC), and the Cul3-RING ubiquitin ligase complex (GO:0031463). GO cellular components: GO:0005737 (cytoplasm), GO:0016020 (membrane), GO:0031463 (Cul3-RING ligase complex).
  • Localization / lateralization: Bilateral renal involvement; systemic (not lateralized) hypertension.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Congenital genetic basis; clinical onset frequently in childhood for CUL3/PHA2E (the most severe subtype), though biochemical abnormalities may be detected at various ages. Onset pattern is insidious/chronic. Representative case: 7-year-old girl (PMID: 27369102); a 9-year follow-up of early-onset PHAII has been reported (PMID: 41380172).
  • Progression: Chronic, lifelong, non-remitting without treatment, but not typically a destructive/degenerative course. Hypertension shows a positive relationship with age, as in the general population (PMID: 11903313).
  • Progression rate / course: Stable-chronic; electrolyte abnormalities and hypertension persist until treated.
  • Patterns: No spontaneous remission; treatment-induced normalization is rapid and dramatic with thiazides. Critical period: Childhood is a window where untreated acidosis and hypertension impair growth and development — early diagnosis and intervention are key (PMID: 41380172).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Epidemiology: FHHt/Gordon syndrome is a very rare cause of hypertension. Among 5,100 patients referred to a tertiary hypertension unit, 7.3% had hyperkalemia and FHHt was a rare diagnosis within that group (PMID: 39229746). More than 90 single cases/families of FHHt have been reported historically, with marked phenotypic heterogeneity (PMID: 11903313). CUL3 (PHA2E) is one of the rarer and most severe subtypes. Precise prevalence/incidence figures for the CUL3 subtype specifically are not established.
  • Inheritance pattern: Autosomal dominant, predominantly de novo for CUL3 (PMID: 22266938). CUL3 and KLHL3 variants segregated in 63% of previously unexplained FHHt pedigrees (PMID: 24266877).
  • Penetrance: High (effectively complete for the biochemical phenotype in reported pedigrees).
  • Expressivity: Variable — severity of biochemical abnormalities varies among individuals and families (PMID: 11903313).
  • Genetic anticipation: Not applicable (not a repeat-expansion disorder).
  • Germline mosaicism / founder effects / consanguinity / carrier frequency: Given the predominantly de novo nature of CUL3 variants, founder effects and consanguinity are not typical features (unlike recessive KLHL3). No meaningful carrier frequency applies to a de novo dominant disorder.
  • Population demographics: No ethnic or geographic predilection established for PHA2E. Sex ratio: Autosomal — expected ~1:1 male:female. Age distribution: Childhood onset is characteristic of the CUL3 subtype.

Biochemical diagnosis. PHA2E is suspected on the triad of hyperkalemia, hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, and hypertension with suppressed plasma renin and normal GFR, with normal or high aldosterone and hypercalciuria (PMID: 27369102; PMID: 6384650).

Genetic testing (confirmatory). Diagnosis is confirmed by molecular testing of the four FHHt genes — WNK1, WNK4, KLHL3, CUL3 — via next-generation sequencing (gene panel or exome), with attention to splice-site variants causing CUL3 exon-9 skipping. Network-medicine-informed diagnostic panels (e.g., PHA-X incorporating CNV detection and ACMG/AMP curation) have been proposed (PMID: 41569463). Genetic testing is indispensable for precise subtyping, which directs etiology-specific management and prognostication (PMID: 41982973).

Differential diagnosis. PHAII must be distinguished from conditions producing hypotension: PHA type I (salt-wasting, hypotension, elevated aldosterone), type 4 RTA/hypoaldosteronism (aldosterone deficiency, low BP), and other causes of hyperkalemia (renal failure, drugs). The discriminating feature is the direction of blood pressure: "Hyperabsorption of chloride in the distal nephron leads to pseudohypoaldosteronism type II, which is linked with hypertension, whereas blood pressure in the other mentioned disorders is decreased" (PMID: 6384650). A spectacular therapeutic response to thiazide also serves as a diagnostic pointer (PMID: 27369102).

Screening. No newborn or population screening program exists; cascade genetic testing of first-degree relatives is appropriate once a familial variant is identified, though de novo status is common in CUL3.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

PHA2E is a chronic, lifelong but highly treatable condition with an excellent prognosis when diagnosed and treated. Thiazide diuretics produce dramatic ("spectacular") normalization of blood pressure and kalemia (PMID: 27369102).

  • Survival/mortality: Not a directly lethal disorder when treated; life expectancy is expected to be near-normal with therapy. Severe untreated hyperkalemia can cause life-threatening cardiac arrhythmia.
  • Morbidity: Untreated or late-diagnosed disease causes morbidity — "persistent metabolic acidosis and hypertension may lead to significant growth and developmental impairments in pediatric patients" (PMID: 41380172). Chronic hypertension carries the usual long-term cardiovascular/renal end-organ risks if uncontrolled.
  • Prognostic factors: Timeliness of diagnosis and treatment adherence are the dominant determinants. "A timely diagnosis of FHH is important for effective management of hypertension, electrolyte imbalance correction with tailored treatment, and genetic counseling" (PMID: 39229746).
  • Recovery potential: The biochemical and hemodynamic abnormalities are fully reversible with thiazide therapy; genotype-specific (NCC-targeted) treatment is highly effective.

12. Treatment

First-line pharmacotherapy — thiazide diuretics. Thiazides directly inhibit the hyperactivated NCC, reversing hypertension, hyperkalemia, and acidosis — often with dramatic effect. Boyden et al.: "Disease features are reversed by thiazide diuretics, which inhibit the Na-Cl cotransporter in the distal nephron of the kidney; KLHL3 and CUL3 are expressed in this location" (PMID: 22266938). Glover et al. confirm FHHt "is effectively treated by thiazide diuretics and/or dietary salt restriction" (PMID: 24266877). NCIT: thiazide diuretic (e.g., hydrochlorothiazide, NCIT:C29181); chlorthalidone.

Dietary / supportive measures. Sodium (salt) restriction reduces substrate for NaCl retention; potassium restriction and, if needed, potassium-binding resins address hyperkalemia. Klaus: "sodium restriction and chloruretic diuretics (thiazide) in type II of pseudohypoaldosteronism" (PMID: 6384650).

Treatment strategy / personalized medicine. PHA2E is a model of genotype-guided therapy: identifying a CUL3/KLHL3-WNK pathway defect directs clinicians specifically to NCC-blocking thiazides rather than empirical antihypertensives. The CUL3/KLHL3–WNK–SPAK/OSR1 pathway is itself being pursued as a target for novel antihypertensive agents, with the appeal that "its inhibition may not only reduce renal sodium reabsorption along multiple segments but may also reduce vascular tone" (PMID: 27076645).

Advanced/experimental therapeutics: No gene therapy, cell therapy, or RNA-based therapy is clinically available or in trials specifically for PHA2E; the pathway (WNK/SPAK inhibitors) is at the preclinical/translational stage.

Treatment outcomes and adverse events: Response is typically excellent and rapid. Thiazide adverse effects to monitor include hypokalemia (useful here, as it counters the hyperkalemia), hyponatremia, hyperuricemia, and volume depletion; doses are titrated to normalize potassium and blood pressure.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: Not applicable as a genetic de novo disorder — occurrence cannot be prevented. Risk-factor modification (salt restriction) prevents symptomatic worsening.
  • Secondary prevention (early detection): Measuring blood pressure in children and recognizing the hyperkalemia/acidosis/hypertension triad enables early diagnosis; the title of PMID: 27369102 itself stresses "the importance of measuring blood pressure in children." Early thiazide treatment prevents growth/developmental complications.
  • Tertiary prevention: Ongoing thiazide therapy plus dietary salt/potassium management prevents hypertensive end-organ damage and hyperkalemic arrhythmia.
  • Genetic counseling: Important for affected families — explaining autosomal-dominant transmission (50% risk to offspring of an affected individual) and the predominantly de novo origin in CUL3. Cascade genetic testing and counseling are recommended (PMID: 39229746).
  • Immunization / public health / environmental interventions: Not applicable.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy / orthologs: CUL3 is highly conserved. Mouse Cul3 (NCBI Gene 26554; Mus musculus, NCBI Taxon 10090) is the principal experimental ortholog. The WNK-SPAK/OSR1-NCC signaling axis is conserved across mammals.
  • Natural disease in other species: No well-documented naturally occurring CUL3-exon-9 Gordon syndrome equivalent is established in companion animals or wildlife (no specific OMIA entry identified for this exact condition in the reviewed literature).
  • Comparative biology: The knock-in mouse (below) recapitulates the human disease closely, demonstrating strong evolutionary conservation of the disease mechanism.
  • Transmission / zoonosis: Not applicable — PHA2E is a non-transmissible genetic disorder.

Key Findings (with evidence)

F1 — PHA2E is caused by dominant, predominantly de novo CUL3 mutations causing exon-9 skipping

Boyden et al. (2012) identified CUL3 and KLHL3 as PHAII genes across 41 families; CUL3 mutations are dominant and predominantly de novo, and diverse mutations converge on exon-9 skipping (CUL3Δ403-459). "KLHL3 mutations are either recessive or dominant, whereas CUL3 mutations are dominant and predominantly de novo" and "Diverse CUL3 mutations all result in skipping of exon 9, producing an in-frame deletion" (PMID: 22266938).

F2 — Mechanism: CUL3-Δ9 abrogates WNK degradation, hyperactivating SPAK/OSR1-NCC

"In CUL3-∆9-mediated FHHt, proteasomal degradation of WNKs is abrogated, leading to overactivation of the WNK targets [SPAK] and oxidative stress-response kinase-1, which directly phosphorylate and activate the thiazide-sensitive Na[-Cl cotransporter]" (PMID: 29361671); NCC hyperactivation occurs along the DCT, producing hypertension and hyperkalemia (PMID: 39699086).

F3 — CUL3 causes the most severe FHHt; thiazides reverse it

"Autosomal dominant mutations in cullin-3 (Cul3) cause the most severe form of familial hyperkalemic hypertension (FHHt)" (PMID: 29361671); disease features reverse with thiazides (PMID: 22266938).

F4 — A CUL3Δ403-459 knock-in mouse recapitulates PHA2E

"Deletion of exon 9 from CUL3 ... causes a severe form of FHHt (PHA2E) that is recapitulated closely in a knock-in mouse model" (PMID: 27378813).

F5 — The mutant auto-ubiquitylates and loses CSN/CAND1 binding (explains severity)

"the PHA2E mutant, CUL3(Δ403-459), is severely compromised in its ability to ubiquitylate WNKs ... Instead, CUL3(Δ403-459) auto-ubiquitylates and loses interaction with two important Cullin regulators: the COP9-signalosome and CAND1"; "These findings may explain the severity of the FHHt phenotype caused by CUL3 mutations compared to those reported in KLHL3 or WNK kinases" (PMID: 26286618); dual gain/loss of function and JAB1/CSN disruption (PMID: 29897280; PMID: 30301860).

F6 — PHA2E hypertension has a vascular component

"These mice also show changes in the arterial pulse waveform, suggesting a vascular contribution to their hypertension not reported in previous FHHt models" (PMID: 26286618); the WNK-OSR1/SPAK-SLC12a cascade is present in VSMCs and regulates arterial tone (PMID: 26152401).

F7 — Full clinical phenotype

"The typical clinical picture ... includes ... arterial hypertension and dyskaliemia, hyperchloremia metabolic acidosis, hypercalciuria, a low rate of renin, and most frequently, a normal or high rate of aldosterone. Dental abnormalities and growth retardation can also be associated" (PMID: 27369102); growth/developmental impairment from persistent acidosis/hypertension (PMID: 41380172).

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Precise epidemiology unknown: No reliable prevalence/incidence estimate exists for the CUL3 subtype specifically; it is simply "very rare." Case counts are small.
  2. Mechanistic controversy: The relative contributions of gain-of-function (auto-ubiquitylation, altered substrate repertoire, KLHL3 degradation) versus loss-of-function (impaired WNK ubiquitylation) remain debated (PMID: 29361671; PMID: 29897280).
  3. Vascular contribution not fully quantified in humans: The vascular component is demonstrated in mice (PMID: 26286618); its magnitude in human PHA2E is inferred, not directly measured.
  4. Limited data on non-renal phenotypes: Dental abnormalities and growth retardation are reported as associations, but their frequency, mechanism, and reversibility are not well quantified.
  5. No omics datasets (transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolomic) specific to PHA2E patient tissue were available in this investigation; molecular profiling evidence is derived from mechanistic cell/mouse studies.
  6. No formal modifier genes or genotype-phenotype correlation within CUL3 variants is established beyond the shared exon-9-skipping outcome.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Human vascular phenotyping: Measure arterial stiffness/pulse-wave velocity in genetically confirmed PHA2E patients to test whether the mouse-observed vascular component is clinically relevant and whether it responds to thiazides.
  2. Natural-history registry: Establish a CUL3-specific FHHt registry to quantify age of onset, penetrance, growth/dental outcomes, and long-term cardiovascular/renal endpoints with and without early treatment.
  3. CSN/neddylation-targeted studies: Given that loss of CSN/JAB1 binding is central to CUL3 severity, test whether modulating neddylation (e.g., NEDD8-pathway tools) in the knock-in mouse corrects NCC hyperactivation — a potential precision-therapy avenue.
  4. WNK/SPAK inhibitor translation: Advance preclinical WNK-SPAK/OSR1 inhibitors in the CUL3Δ403-459 mouse, directly comparing renal and vascular blood-pressure effects against thiazide.
  5. Patient-derived omics: Perform transcriptomic/proteomic profiling of patient-derived cells or urinary exosomes (NCC abundance) to develop non-invasive biomarkers of disease activity and treatment response (PMID: 28511177).
  6. Growth-impairment mechanism: Investigate whether early acidosis correction fully rescues growth in pediatric PHA2E, clarifying the critical intervention window.
