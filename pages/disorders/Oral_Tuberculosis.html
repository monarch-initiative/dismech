Oral Tuberculosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0005887 Pathograph 27 Show in embeddings browser Tuberculosis Extrapulmonary tuberculosis Gastrointestinal tuberculosis

Oral tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the oral cavity: the tongue, gingiva, palate, lips, buccal mucosa and floor of the mouth, and the jaw bones. Mycobacterium tuberculosis reaches the mouth mostly in bacilli-laden sputum from active pulmonary tuberculosis, entering through small breaks in the mucosa (secondary oral tuberculosis), and less often by blood-borne spread or with no other detectable focus (primary oral tuberculosis). An intact oral epithelium and saliva normally resist infection, which is one reason oral disease is rare, at about 0.5-1% of tuberculosis cases. The lesion is a caseating granulomatous inflammation. The typical sign is a chronic, non-healing ulcer with irregular, undermined edges, most often on the tongue; secondary ulcers are usually painful, and primary lesions, more common in younger patients and on the gingiva, are often painless with enlarged regional lymph nodes. Nodular or proliferative masses, gingival enlargement and tuberculous osteomyelitis of the mandible or maxilla also occur. Because the lesion closely mimics squamous cell carcinoma, biopsy is required; histology shows caseating granulomas, acid-fast staining is often negative because lesions are paucibacillary, and culture, PCR or Xpert testing and chest imaging for a pulmonary focus complete the work-up. In about half of reported cases the oral lesion led to the diagnosis of previously unknown systemic tuberculosis. Standard multidrug antituberculous therapy heals the lesions; surgery is limited to biopsy and selected cases.

Secondary oral tuberculosis
Oral tuberculosis in a patient with active tuberculosis elsewhere, usually pulmonary, attributed to sputum inoculation of the mucosa or to blood-borne spread. It is the commoner form (58% of pooled cases), affects mainly middle-aged and older people, and typically presents as a painful, irregular ulcer with undermined edges, often at a site of trauma.

Primary oral tuberculosis
Oral tuberculosis with no detectable tuberculosis elsewhere (42% of pooled cases in otherwise uncompromised patients). Reviews describe it in children and younger patients, predominantly on the gingiva or buccal mucosa, as a painless ulcer or proliferative lesion with enlarged regional lymph nodes and rarely with systemic symptoms. Case series show that it also occurs in older adults, and chest imaging is normal, so the diagnosis rests on biopsy.

Breach of the Oral Mucosal Barrier
An intact oral squamous epithelium, the flow of saliva and its antibacterial properties resist tubercle bacilli, which is why oral disease is rare although bacilli are common in the oral fluids of patients with pulmonary tuberculosis. Local trauma (including from sharp or broken teeth), poor oral hygiene, chronic inflammation, leukoplakia and tooth extraction are proposed to breach this barrier.

Caseating Granulomatous Inflammation of the Oral Tissues
Granulomas with central caseous necrosis, epithelioid macrophages, Langhans-type multinucleated giant cells and lymphocytes form in the oral mucosa and submucosa. Bacilli lie within macrophages and giant cells but are few, so the lesion is paucibacillary. In immunocompromised patients, including those with AIDS, the granulomas may lack caseation.

Chronic Mucosal Ulceration with Undermined Edges
A usually solitary ulcer with irregular, undermined, indurated and ragged margins, often covered with yellowish exudate, that persists for weeks to months, enlarges slowly and does not heal with conservative treatment. It is most common on the tongue. Secondary ulcers are usually painful and often arise at sites of trauma; primary ulcers are often painless. The ulcer closely mimics squamous cell carcinoma and traumatic ulcer.

Tuberculous Osteomyelitis of the Jaw
Granulomatous infection of the mandible or, less often, the maxilla, either as a tuberculous periapical granuloma or as diffuse bone involvement. It usually follows blood-borne spread but can follow direct extension or tooth extraction, and it can occur without other tuberculosis. It occurs mainly in later disease, carries an unfavorable prognosis, has no characteristic radiological appearance and drains through extraoral fistulas, mainly on the cheek.

  Oral tuberculosis is tuberculosis of the oral cavity: the tongue, gingiva, palate,
  lips, buccal mucosa and floor of the mouth, and the jaw bones. Mycobacterium tuberculosis
  reaches the mouth mostly in bacilli-laden sputum from active pulmonary tuberculosis,
  entering through small breaks in the mucosa (secondary oral tuberculosis), and less
  often by blood-borne spread or with no other detectable focus (primary oral tuberculosis).
  An intact oral epithelium and saliva normally resist infection, which is one reason
  oral disease is rare, at about 0.5-1% of tuberculosis cases. The lesion is a caseating
  granulomatous inflammation. The typical sign is a chronic, non-healing ulcer with
  irregular, undermined edges, most often on the tongue; secondary ulcers are usually
  painful, and primary lesions, more common in younger patients and on the gingiva,
  are often painless with enlarged regional lymph nodes. Nodular or proliferative
  masses, gingival enlargement and tuberculous osteomyelitis of the mandible or maxilla
  also occur. Because the lesion closely mimics squamous cell carcinoma, biopsy is
  required; histology shows caseating granulomas, acid-fast staining is often negative
  because lesions are paucibacillary, and culture, PCR or Xpert testing and chest
  imaging for a pulmonary focus complete the work-up. In about half of reported cases
  the oral lesion led to the diagnosis of previously unknown systemic tuberculosis.
  Standard multidrug antituberculous therapy heals the lesions; surgery is limited
  to biopsy and selected cases.

Record notes

Scope and boundaries. This entry covers tuberculosis of the oral cavity proper: the oral mucosa (tongue, gingiva, hard and soft palate, lips, buccal mucosa, floor of the mouth) and the jaw bones. MONDO defines the class as tuberculosis of the mouth, tongue and salivary glands, so salivary gland tuberculosis falls within it, but the cited series report salivary disease only together with lymph node involvement and no salivary-specific node is curated here. Tuberculosis of the larynx is curated separately in Laryngeal_Tuberculosis, and cutaneous tuberculosis of the face, including lupus vulgaris, in Lupus_Vulgaris; an oral mucosal lesion is in scope here whatever its source. Tonsillar and oropharyngeal tuberculosis are outside this entry. Pulmonary and systemic tuberculosis are in Tuberculosis. Esophageal tuberculosis is a separate entity. Evidence base. The literature is case reports, small case series (the largest cited here has 42 patients) and two systematic reviews of pooled case reports (one of 301 oral and maxillofacial cases, which includes jaw and some extraoral sites). No prospective or controlled study defines the pathogenesis; the sputum-inoculation route and the protective role of the intact epithelium and saliva are stated in reviews and case discussions rather than demonstrated, and the granuloma mechanism is inferred from tuberculosis generally. The pooled systematic review restricted itself to otherwise uncompromised patients. Symptom frequencies are set only where a cited series or review gives a figure, and pooled case-report figures are biased toward unusual presentations. 1. Disease Information

Overview. Oral tuberculosis is a rare form of extrapulmonary TB affecting the oral soft tissues, the jaws and the salivary glands. - It is usually secondary to pulmonary TB. "Oral TB manifestations are uncommon and usually secondary to pulmonary changes" (Krawiecka & Szponar 2015, PMID 26366156). - It most often appears as a chronic, painful ulcer that does not heal. - Primary oral TB, with no other focus of disease, is rarer still (PMID 33456244; PMID 34932768).

2. Etiology

Cause. Infection of the oral tissues with M. tuberculosis complex. There are two routes: - Secondary oral TB: bacilli in infected sputum reach mucosa that is already broken, or the disease spreads through the blood. A 2025 South African HIV case series (University of Pretoria repository) states that oral TB "usually results from secondary bacterial inoculation of oral mucosa breached by any ulceration or minor masticatory trauma." - Primary oral TB: direct inoculation in a person with no other TB focus (PMID 33456244).

Risk factors - Active pulmonary TB. In a four-case series, every secondary case had "cavitary lesions in lungs and were sputum positive for acid fast bacilli" (Dogra et al., Indian J Otolaryngol). - Immunosuppression and HIV. In 200 people with HIV–TB coinfection, "A total of 77.5% patients coinfected with HIV-TB had shown oral manifestations" (PMID 32181218). That figure covers all oral lesions, not oral TB specifically. The authors relate oral lesions to CD4 count. - Diabetes mellitus. It was the most common comorbidity (12 of 78 patients) in an Indian cross-sectional TB cohort that included one case of primary oral TB (JIAOMR 2011). - Local factors: - mucosal trauma, poor oral hygiene, extraction sockets and leukoplakia (UP repository; PMID 26366156). The full text supporting this list was not verified. - multidrug-resistant strains, which Krawiecka & Szponar suggest may increase oral TB (PMC4565830). - Sex and age. In the systematic review, 63.7% of patients were male and the mean age was 39.6 years (PMID 35785411). - Genetic risk. Inborn errors of IFN-γ immunity (Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease, MSMD) predispose to mycobacterial disease. Some MSMD patients develop disease from M. tuberculosis itself (PMID 25453225; PMID 30264912). No genetic risk factor specific to oral TB has been reported.

Protective factors - Treating pulmonary TB promptly removes the source of infected sputum. - An intact oral epithelium, saliva and the oral flora are commonly described as making the mouth resistant to infection. This rests on a review-level statement whose full text was not verified (PMC4565830). - BCG vaccination and other primary prevention: see Section 13 [not retrieved].

Gene–environment interaction. Not documented for oral TB. In MSMD, defects in IFN-γ-dependent immunity combine with exposure to mycobacteria to cause disease, with incomplete penetrance (PMID 25453225).

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Causal genes: none. This is not a Mendelian disease, so pathogenic variants, allele frequencies, somatic versus germline origin, chromosomal abnormalities and epigenetic data specific to oral TB are not applicable or not available.
  • Host susceptibility genes, applicable to mycobacterial disease generally:
  • MSMD genes in the IL-12/IL-23–IFN-γ axis, including IL12RB1 (MIM 601604). IL-12Rβ1 deficiency is the most common MSMD form (PMID 23864330).
  • Bustamante et al. state that "The nine gene products are physiologically related, as all are involved in IFN-γ-dependent immunity" and "Most of these inborn errors do not show complete clinical penetrance" (PMID 25453225).
  • The 2014–2018 update reports disease-causing mutations "in 11 genes" underlying "21 different genetic disorders", and adds SPPL2A, RORC and JAK1 (PMID 30264912; PMID 32025907).
  • These are mainly autosomal recessive loss-of-function or autosomal dominant dominant-negative variants [mode details not retrieved here].
  • Pathogen genetics: resistance mutations bear on treatment. The rpoB rifampicin-resistance region is detected by Xpert MTB/RIF (Section 10).

5. Environmental Information

  • Infectious agent: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (and the wider complex, including M. bovis, by inference). NCBI Taxonomy IDs were not retrieved here.
  • Exposures: close contact with infectious pulmonary TB; settings with high TB burden.
  • The 2025 WHO report lists 16% of notified TB as extrapulmonary (WHO GTB 2025).
  • Lifestyle: smoking and tobacco chewing, alcohol, malnutrition and poor oral hygiene are cited in the case literature as local predisposing factors [not verified in full text].

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain 1. Viable M. tuberculosis reaches the oral mucosa, by one of two routes: - Branch A (secondary): infected sputum from pulmonary TB, or spread through blood or lymph. - Branch B (primary): direct exogenous inoculation. 2. Breaks in the epithelium (trauma, extraction, inflammation) lead to bacilli entering the submucosa. An intact epithelium is thought to resist infection; this step is inferred from the case literature (UP repository). 3. Tissue macrophages take up the bacilli, which results in intracellular bacterial persistence and recruitment of more macrophages. This is shown in the zebrafish model (PMID 24973748) and extrapolated to the oral site. 4. Macrophages aggregate and change into epithelioid cells and multinucleated Langhans giant cells, which leads to granuloma formation. "The granuloma… is the hallmark structure of tuberculosis" (PMID 22517424, paraphrased). 5. Antigen-specific T cells produce IFN-γ under IL-12 control, and TNF maintains the granuloma. Together these lead to macrophage activation and partial containment (PMC4561401 and other reviews; human genetic evidence in PMID 25453225). - Branch: when IFN-γ immunity is deficient (MSMD, HIV with CD4 depletion), containment fails and the disease is more extensive. 6. Excess TNF or strong T-cell immunity leads to macrophage necrosis, which then results in caseous necrosis and extracellular bacterial growth. "Macrophage necrosis promotes more bacterial growth than macrophage apoptosis" (PMID 22517424, figure caption). The granuloma may itself drive "the expansion and dissemination of infection" (PMID 22517424). 7. Necrosis breaking through the surface mucosa results in a chronic ulcer with undermined edges. In bone it results in tuberculous osteomyelitis of the mandible or maxilla. This step is inferred from clinical and pathological correlation (PMID 35785411). 8. Spread along lymphatics leads to cervical lymphadenitis. Clinically, the result is pain, dysphagia and a non-healing ulcer.

Mechanism checklist - Pathways: IL-12/IL-23–IFN-γ signalling; TNF signalling. - Suggested GO terms: - granuloma formation - macrophage activation - interferon-gamma-mediated signaling pathway - tumor necrosis factor-mediated signaling pathway - phagocytosis - necrotic cell death - Suggested CL terms: macrophage; epithelioid cell; multinucleated giant cell (Langhans); CD4-positive alpha-beta T cell; neutrophil. - Omics, single-cell and CRISPR data specific to oral TB: none found.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: insidious and chronic. Lesions are typically present for weeks to months before diagnosis, and they fail to heal with conservative treatment (PMID 37842328).
  • Age: mostly adults; primary oral TB occurs in children and young adults (PMC10569891).
  • Course: progressive without treatment. Remission is treatment-induced, and all four primary cases in one series resolved on antituberculous therapy (PMID 33456244).
  • Critical window: early biopsy of any non-healing ulcer avoids delayed diagnosis, which matters most where carcinoma is suspected.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: not applicable; this is an acquired infection. Penetrance, anticipation, founder effects and carrier frequency are not applicable.
  • Frequency:
  • Oral lesions occur in about 0.5–1.5% of TB patients (PMC4565830). Other sources give a range of 0.05–5%.
  • Primary oral TB is reported as under 1% of extrapulmonary cases (PMC8687499).
  • In a Brazilian TB cohort of 121 patients, 1 (0.8%) had oral ulceration (search summary).
  • Incidence per 100,000: not available for oral TB specifically.
  • Geography: mirrors the overall TB burden. Most published cases come from South Asia, Latin America and sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Sex ratio: male-predominant, 63.7% male, about 1.8:1 (PMID 35785411).
  • Age: mean 39.6 ± 19.8 years (PMID 35785411).

10. Diagnostics

Biopsy and histopathology are the cornerstone of diagnosis. - Typical findings are epithelioid granulomas with Langhans giant cells, with or without caseous necrosis. - Granulomatous inflammation was seen in 63.1% of cases (PMID 35785411).

Microbiology on tissue - Ziehl–Neelsen (acid-fast) stain: low sensitivity. It was positive in 31.25% (5/16) of an orofacial series (IJPM 2021). - Culture: slow, and often not reported in the case literature [not retrieved].

Molecular tests - Nested PCR and Xpert MTB/RIF on oral biopsies were positive in 2 and 3 caseating-granuloma samples respectively; the authors call them useful auxiliary methods (JORDI). - PCR was used in primary oral TB cases (PMID 33456244).

Immunological tests - Tuberculin skin test (Mantoux) was used in PMID 33456244. - Interferon-γ release assays (IGRA) [not retrieved].

Work-up for a primary focus - Chest radiograph or CT. - Sputum AFB smear or Xpert testing (Dogra et al.).

Differential diagnosis - squamous cell carcinoma (the most important mimic; PMID 33456244) - traumatic ulcer - aphthous ulcer - deep fungal infections such as histoplasmosis - syphilis - Crohn disease and orofacial granulomatosis - sarcoidosis - lymphoma - granulomatosis with polyangiitis - PMID 26366156 recommends that TB be considered for atypical or non-healing ulcers.

Genetic testing - Not used to diagnose oral TB. - Worth considering only for MSMD work-up in severe, recurrent or disseminated mycobacterial disease, using a gene panel or exome for IL12RB1, IFNGR1/2, STAT1 and others (PMID 38535546).

Screening: no programme exists for oral TB. Oral lesions have been proposed as a screening signal in HIV–TB coinfection (PMID 32181218).

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Mortality: "Antituberculosis therapy was used in 244 cases (100.0%) and 5.2% of patients died" (PMID 35785411). Deaths probably reflect disseminated or HIV-associated disease (inferred).
  • Recovery: oral lesions usually heal fully on standard therapy (PMID 33456244).
  • Complications:
  • jaw osteomyelitis and pathological changes in bone (PMID 35785411)
  • scarring, tissue loss and lymphadenitis
  • transmission, when the disease is secondary to pulmonary TB
  • Prognostic factors: HIV with a low CD4 count (PMID 32181218), drug resistance and delay in diagnosis. Formal prognostic biomarkers have not been described.

12. Treatment

Systemic antituberculous therapy is mandatory. "Anti-tubercular systemic therapy is required in every patient diagnosed with oral TB" (PMID 26366156). Topical treatment is adjuvant only.

Drug-susceptible TB - Standard regimen: 2 months of isoniazid + rifampicin + pyrazinamide + ethambutol, then 4 months of isoniazid + rifampicin (2HRZE/4HR). This is the standard regimen in WHO Module 4 (WHO 2022). - 4-month alternative (2HPMZ/2HPM): isoniazid + rifapentine + moxifloxacin + pyrazinamide for people aged 12 or older. WHO restricts it to pulmonary DS-TB, so its use in extrapulmonary oral TB is outside the guideline (inferred). - In Study 31/ACTG A5349 it was non-inferior, with cure in 84.5% versus 85.4% and grade ≥3 adverse events in 18.8% versus 19.3% (tbksp.who.int).

Drug-resistant TB: MDR/RR-TB regimens under WHO Module 4 [details not retrieved].

Pharmacogenomics: NAT2 acetylator status affects isoniazid toxicity [not retrieved in this session].

Supportive care - analgesia - topical anaesthetic or antiseptic rinses - nutritional support - treatment of HIV with antiretroviral therapy (ART) - surgery is limited to biopsy and debridement of necrotic bone

Adverse events: hepatotoxicity (H, R, Z); peripheral neuropathy (H); optic neuritis (E); rifampicin drug eruptions. Rifampicin eruptions on the lips were seen in an Indian cohort (JIAOMR).

Not applicable: gene, cell and RNA therapies, targeted therapy and immunotherapy. No oral-TB-specific trials were found.

Suggested NCIT intervention labels: Isoniazid; Rifampin; Pyrazinamide; Ethambutol; Rifapentine; Moxifloxacin; Biopsy; Antiretroviral therapy.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention:
  • TB infection control
  • treatment of contacts and TB preventive therapy
  • BCG vaccination, which mainly protects children against severe forms [not retrieved]
  • oral hygiene and avoiding mucosal trauma in people with TB (inferred)
  • Secondary prevention:
  • biopsy of non-healing oral ulcers
  • chest imaging when oral TB is found
  • attention to oral lesions in people with HIV (PMID 32181218)
  • Tertiary prevention:
  • completing the full course with adherence support
  • drug susceptibility testing
  • HIV care
  • Genetic counselling: relevant only to MSMD families.

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • No oral-TB-specific veterinary literature was retrieved.
  • M. bovis causes TB in cattle and is zoonotic, for example through unpasteurized milk [not retrieved; general knowledge].
  • M. marinum naturally infects fish and forms granulomas like those of human TB (PMID 24973748).
  • There is no OMIA entry; the disease is not Mendelian.
