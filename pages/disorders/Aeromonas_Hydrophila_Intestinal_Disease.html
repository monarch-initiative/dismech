Aeromonas Hydrophila Intestinal Disease

Infectious Disease MONDO:0024459 Pathograph 14 Show in embeddings browser Aeromonas Hydrophila Infectious Disease

Aeromonas hydrophila intestinal disease is an acquired bacterial gastroenteritis caused by waterborne or foodborne Aeromonas hydrophila. It can present as watery, cholera-like diarrhea with dehydration or as a dysentery-like bloody diarrhea when pore-forming toxins drive epithelial injury and inflammatory signaling.

Human Aeromonas gastroenteritis surveys recover several Aeromonas species, and stools from symptomatic and asymptomatic people can have similar Aeromonas positivity rates, so species-resolved testing and the exclusion of other pathogens are important for attributing an individual diarrheal episode to A. hydrophila.

Progression

4
Incubation and onset
Enteric symptoms typically begin within hours to a few days of ingesting contaminated water or food.
Acute self-limited gastroenteritis
Duration: up to one to two weeks
In immunocompetent hosts the diarrhoeal illness is usually self-limited, resolving over days to about two weeks, though some cases are prolonged.

Severe secretory dehydration
A minority of patients progress to profuse cholera-like watery stools with severe dehydration.

Invasive systemic disease
Rarely, infection progresses to bacteraemia and sepsis, mainly in immunocompromised or elderly hosts, carrying high mortality.

1. Disease Information

Overview. Aeromonas hydrophila intestinal disease is an acute bacterial gastroenteritis caused by an environmental, oxidase-positive, facultatively anaerobic, motile Gram-negative rod belonging to the family Aeromonadaceae. It is classed as an emerging, opportunistic, waterborne/foodborne enteric infection. The clinical presentation ranges from mild watery diarrhea to cholera-like secretory diarrhea and dysentery-like bloody diarrhea (PMID: 29205196).

Key identifiers. - MONDO: MONDO:0024459 - MeSH: Aeromonas infections / Gram-Negative Bacterial Infections; organism MeSH "Aeromonas hydrophila" - NCBI Taxonomy: Aeromonas hydrophila = txid644; genus Aeromonas = txid642 - ICD-10: A04.8 (Other specified bacterial intestinal infections) — Aeromonas enteritis has no dedicated code and is captured under other/specified bacterial intestinal infections. - OMIM / Orphanet: Not applicable — this is an acquired infectious disease with no Mendelian basis; no OMIM or Orphanet rare-disease entry.

Synonyms / alternative names. Aeromonas gastroenteritis; Aeromonas-associated diarrhea; Aeromonas enteritis; mesophilic Aeromonas infection; motile aeromonad infection (in veterinary contexts, Motile Aeromonad Septicaemia, MAS, when systemic in fish).

Information source. Content is aggregated from disease-level resources — clinical case series, case-control studies, PCR/genomic surveys, and systematic reviews/meta-analyses — rather than individual EHR-derived patient records.

2. Etiology

Primary cause — infectious. The disease is caused by ingestion of A. hydrophila (and closely related motile mesophilic aeromonads such as A. caviae, A. veronii, A. dhakensis). There is no host genetic etiology; the disease is entirely acquired. Virulence is multifactorial and strain-dependent (Findings F002, F003).

Environmental / risk factors. - Waterborne and foodborne transmission — Aeromonas is transmitted via contaminated water, fish, and chicken meat, and can grow competitively at 5 °C, making it a refrigerated-food hazard (PMID: 27051177). - Young age — isolation rates are significantly higher in diarrheal than control children (p<0.001) (PMID: 15005966); A. hydrophila was among the most frequent enteropathogens (9%) in Somali children (PMID: 3076719). - Immunocompromise / malignancy — in immunocompromised children with diarrhea (mostly acute lymphoblastic leukemia on chemotherapy), Aeromonas spp. were the most commonly detected target (14.6%), with bloody diarrhea in 98.5% (PMID: 38778271). - Advanced age / hematologic disease — severe/hemorrhagic disease reported in elderly patients with aplastic anemia/thrombocytopenia (PMID: 41903649). - Poor water and sanitation — prevalence 8.8% where water/sanitation quality score is <25% (PMID: 37217902).

Protective factors. No genetic protective variants are defined (acquired disease). Environmental/behavioural protection derives from clean water, sanitation, and food safety (adequate cooking, avoiding prolonged refrigerated storage). In aquaculture reservoirs, probiotics and phytobiotics are protective (Findings F005, F012).

Gene-environment interactions. Not applicable to the human host. The relevant axis is on the pathogen side: strains carrying more virulence genes are more lethal, and the environmental/host niche selects for hypervirulent clonal lineages (Findings F002, F007).

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Not applicable to the human host. This is an infectious disease; there are no causal host genes, pathogenic germline/somatic variants, modifier genes, host epigenetic changes, or chromosomal abnormalities. Host genetic testing is not indicated.

The relevant "genetics" is microbial virulence genetics: - aerolysin (aer/aerA) — β-pore-forming toxin, present in up to 87% of isolates (PMID: 25216211); 72.7% of fish isolates (PMID: 30989358). - cytotoxic enterotoxin (act) — 7.7–18.2% of isolates. - cytotonic enterotoxins (alt, ast) — ast 17% (PMID: 25216211). - hemolysin A (hylA), serine protease (ahp), temperature-sensitive protease (eprCAI). - Regulation: DNA adenine methyltransferase (Dam) and tRNA-modifying enzyme GidA co-regulate act expression via GATC methylation (PMID: 22391092). - Pfam / protein family: aerolysin/ETX family, Pfam PF01117 (aerolysin).

Strains carrying more virulence genes show progressively higher proteolytic, hemolytic, and cytotoxic activity and lower LD50 in zebrafish; the aer+alt+act+eprCAI+ahp+ genotype is enriched among diseased hosts and most virulent (PMID: 21219556, PMID: 22725694).

5. Environmental Information

  • Environmental reservoir: freshwater, brackish water, wastewater, aquaculture ponds, and food (fish, poultry). Wastewater treatment plants are reservoirs for antibiotic-resistance gene dissemination in Aeromonas (PMID: 40269715).
  • Psychrotrophy: growth at 5 °C makes refrigerated foods a hazard (PMID: 27051177).
  • Lifestyle factors: consumption of untreated water, raw/undercooked fish and shellfish; exposure to recreational or occupational water (e.g., paddy-field injuries associated with invasive Aeromonas disease, PMID: 42288750).
  • Infectious agents: Aeromonas hydrophila (txid644); related A. caviae, A. veronii, A. dhakensis, A. enteropelogenes. Toxin entities: aerolysin, cytotoxic enterotoxin Act.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Ordered causal chain (initiating exposure → clinical manifestation)

  1. Ingestion of A. hydrophila in contaminated water/food leads to delivery of viable, motile bacteria to the small and large intestine.
  2. Chemotaxis toward intestinal mucus (flagella-driven) results in localization at the mucosal surface; chemotaxis may be required for obligate pathogenicity (PMID: 18355183).
  3. Adhesin/pilus-mediated attachment to mucus glycoproteins (galactose/mannose receptors) leads to stable colonization; stronger adhesion correlates with greater cytotoxicity (PMID: 18924378, PMID: 1363704).
  4. Secretion of toxins (T2SS-secreted Act and aerolysin; hemolysins; proteases) results in inactive pro-toxins at the epithelial surface.
  5. Proteolytic activation (C-terminal propeptide cleavage of proaerolysin/Act) leads to active toxin monomers with increased β-sheet content (PMID: 9398250).
  6. GPI-anchored receptor binding + heptamerization results in a prepore that undergoes a concerted "swirling" collapse, inserting a transmembrane β-barrel pore (1.14–2.8 nm) (PMID: 23912165, PMID: 9317040, PMID: 34162996).
  7. Membrane permeabilization branches into two downstream arms:
  8. 7a (secretory arm): pore formation + Act signalling raises cAMP and activates COX-2/PGE2 and eicosanoid metabolism → net fluid/electrolyte secretion → watery, cholera-like diarrhea (PMID: 10768977).
  9. 7b (inflammatory/cytotoxic arm): Act activates p38, JNK, ERK1/2 MAPK cascades, induces TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, iNOS, and triggers caspase-3/8/9–dependent apoptosis in macrophages and human intestinal epithelial cells (T84, HT-29) → mucosal injury, neutrophilic inflammation → dysenteric/bloody diarrhea (PMID: 15215244).
  10. (Inferred, strain-dependent) In strains with T3SS/T6SS, effectors AexU (T3SS) and Hcp (T6SS) subvert host immune signalling and promote apoptosis/pyroptosis leading to deeper invasion and rarely bacteremia/sepsis (PMID: 24199174, PMID: 23611906).
  11. (Inferred, lineage-dependent) Epidemic ST251 clones achieve high virulence independent of classical T3SS/T6SS/lateral flagella, instead exploiting myo-inositol, sialic acid, and L-fucose utilization pathways (PMID: 26014286).

7. Anatomical Structures Affected

  • Primary organ: intestine — small intestine and colon (UBERON:0000160 intestine; UBERON:0002108 small intestine; UBERON:0001155 colon). Body system: digestive/gastrointestinal (UBERON:0001007 digestive system).
  • Secondary involvement: bloodstream (bacteremia/sepsis) in immunocompromised/elderly hosts; rarely liver (acute hepatitis with invasive Aeromonas, PMID: 41356972).
  • Tissue/cell level: intestinal epithelium (mucosa) — enterocytes (CL:0000584) and colonic epithelial cells (CL:0002253) are primary targets; macrophages (CL:0000235) and neutrophils (CL:0000775) mediate inflammation. Toxins act on GPI-anchored-protein–bearing membranes.
  • Subcellular level: plasma membrane (pore insertion; GO:0005886); mitochondria/cytoplasm (caspase-9 intrinsic apoptosis; GO:0005739); cell-surface GPI anchor (GO:0098552).
  • Localization / lateralization: diffuse mucosal involvement; not lateralized.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: acute, within hours to a few days of ingestion (not congenital, not insidious).
  • Progression: most cases self-limited over several days; a minority (severe cholera-like) progress rapidly to dehydration; dysenteric cases run an inflammatory course. No chronic-lifelong course in immunocompetent hosts.
  • Disease course pattern: acute, self-limited; can be episodic with re-exposure. Rare progression to invasive/systemic disease in vulnerable hosts.
  • Remission: usually spontaneous with supportive rehydration; antibiotics shorten severe cases.
  • Critical periods: early rehydration is the key intervention window; in severe/immunocompromised cases, early appropriate antibiotics prevent progression to sepsis.

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: Not applicable — acquired infectious disease, no heritability, penetrance, expressivity, anticipation, founder effects, consanguinity, or carrier frequency.
  • Epidemiology: Global pooled prevalence in children with diarrhea = 4.2% (95% CI 3.1–5.6%) across 31 studies / 38,663 participants (PMID: 37217902). Higher in upper-middle-income countries (5.1%), very populous countries (>100 million: 9.4%), and poor-sanitation settings (<25% score: 8.8%); a significant decreasing trend over time (P=0.0001).
  • Demographics: disproportionately affects young children, the elderly, and the immunocompromised (Finding F008). Higher isolation in diarrheal vs control children (p<0.001, PMID: 15005966).
  • Geographic distribution: worldwide; higher burden where water/sanitation is poor; endemic in tropical/subtropical aquaculture regions. Epidemic hypervirulent clones (ST251) reported from China and the southeastern USA (PMID: 26014286).
  • Seasonality: peaks in warm months.

10. Diagnostics

  • Stool culture on blood/selective agar with oxidase-positive screening is the mainstay.
  • Species-level identification requires molecular methods — biochemical and automated systems misidentify Aeromonas. In one case, MALDI-TOF gave high scores for two species and an automated system called it "A. veronii complex"; definitive ID required multilocus phylogenetic analysis of gyrB, rpoD, gyrA (PMID: 41903649).
  • PCR / molecular: multiplex PCR for virulence genes (aer/act/alt/ast), and gyrB/16S rDNA sequencing for detection and genotyping (PMID: 25216211, PMID: 22725694).
  • Biosensors: DNA hybridization electrochemical biosensors developed for A. hydrophila (PMID: 20829023).
  • Clinical criteria / differential diagnosis: acute infectious diarrhea; distinguish from Vibrio cholerae (secretory), Shigella/Campylobacter/Salmonella/EIEC (dysenteric), C. difficile (antibiotic-associated). Stool culture is important in severe or protracted illness when antimicrobial therapy is contemplated (PMID: 3683260).
  • Genetic/omics diagnostics: host genetic testing not applicable. Pathogen WGS/MLST is used for outbreak tracing and AMR profiling.
  • Screening: no human screening program; environmental/food surveillance applies.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

  • Prognosis: generally good; most cases are self-limited in immunocompetent hosts.
  • Mortality: low overall but non-zero — one death (dehydration + septicaemia) in a 50-patient series (PMID: 29205196). Invasive/bacteremic disease carries substantial mortality, especially in immunocompromised/elderly patients (PMID: 41903649, PMID: 42288750).
  • Complications: severe dehydration, dysentery, bacteremia/sepsis, rarely acute hepatitis; carbapenem treatment failure reported in critically ill patients due to PK/PD factors (PMID: 42288750).
  • Prognostic factors: host immune status (immunocompromise, malignancy, advanced age worsen outcomes); strain virulence-gene load and hypervirulent lineage; timeliness of rehydration and appropriate antibiotics; AMR profile (ESBL/integron-borne resistance worsens outcomes, PMID: 41625157).

12. Treatment

  • Supportive care (cornerstone): oral/IV rehydration and electrolyte correction; most immunocompetent cases need no antibiotics. NCIT: Fluid and Electrolyte Therapy.
  • Antimicrobials (when severe/invasive or immunocompromised):
  • Avoid ampicillin/amoxicillin and 1st-generation cephalosporins — A. hydrophila is intrinsically resistant via chromosomal β-lactamases (only ~14% amoxicillin-susceptible) (PMID: 29205196).
  • Effective agents: fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin), 3rd/4th-generation cephalosporins (ceftazidime, cefepime), carbapenems (meropenem), aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin), aztreonam, azithromycin, cotrimoxazole — gastroenteritis isolates are almost uniformly susceptible (PMID: 38783344). NCIT: Ciprofloxacin, Ceftazidime, Meropenem, Amikacin, Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole.
  • AMR caveat: emerging ESBL (CTX-M) and class-1-integron–borne resistance, including carbapenem treatment failures (PMID: 42288750, PMID: 41625157). Susceptibility testing should guide therapy.
  • Advanced/experimental therapeutics: none specific for human Aeromonas enteritis. In aquaculture, engineered probiotics secreting antimicrobial peptides are investigational (see Prevention).

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: clean water, sanitation, and food safety (adequate cooking; limiting refrigerated storage given growth at 5 °C) (PMID: 27051177, PMID: 37217902).
  • Human immunization: no licensed human vaccine.
  • Veterinary/aquaculture prevention (One Health):
  • Vaccines: whole-cell inactivated A. hydrophila vaccines give the highest protection (vs live-attenuated/recombinant); i.p. injection most effective; feed-based bivalent (S. iniae + A. hydrophila) vaccines raise IgM and improve survival (PMID: 32331001, PMID: 33857622, PMID: 32615969).
  • Probiotics: dietary Saccharomyces cerevisiae cut post-challenge mortality from 53.3% to 13.3% in African catfish (PMID: 41946979); Bacillus licheniformis, Lactobacillus brevis (+trans-cinnamic acid), and engineered Bacillus subtilis secreting piscidin-1/hepcidin improved survival (PMID: 42067537, PMID: 42014760, PMID: 42176787); Clostridium butyricum blocks colonization in vitro (PMID: 18795975).
  • Phytobiotics: dietary essential oils (basil, thyme, fenugreek) improve immunity and resistance (PMID: 42400869, PMID: 42376504).
  • Public health: integrated One Health AMR surveillance across environmental, food, and clinical compartments (PMID: 42288750).

14. Other Species / Natural Disease

  • Taxonomy of pathogen: Aeromonas hydrophila NCBI txid644; genus Aeromonas txid642.
  • Natural disease — fish: A. hydrophila is the usual etiological agent of Motile Aeromonad Septicaemia (MAS) in tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), carp (Cyprinus carpio), catfish (Clarias gariepinus), and other freshwater fish, with high mortality and major economic loss (PMID: 32331001, PMID: 30989358).
  • Other hosts / zoonotic potential: environmental organism of aquatic habitats; found in hippopotamus oral flora, of zoonotic importance in bite-wound infections (PMID: 40269996). Human infection is acquired from environmental/animal reservoirs (One Health).
  • Comparative biology: the same toxin/virulence machinery (Act, aerolysin, T3SS/T6SS) drives disease across fish and mammals, and virulence-gene load predicts lethality across host models (zebrafish, mice) — indicating conserved mechanisms.

Key Findings (with statistical evidence)

F001 — Clinical spectrum and pathogen-vs-commensal ambiguity

A. hydrophila causes acute gastroenteritis ranging from watery/cholera-like to dysenteric diarrhea, but is often a minor contributor or coloniser. In 50 patients, 26% had cholera-like rice-water stools with severe dehydration, 16% a dysentery-like picture, and one died (PMID: 29205196). Aeromonas was among the most frequent enteropathogens (9%) in Somali children (PMID: 3076719). In N. Israel, positivity was similar in symptomatic (4.24–4.81%) and asymptomatic (4.9%) stools — "similar positivity rates of Aeromonas spp. in symptomatic and asymptomatic making the distinction between true pathogen versus commensal bacteria difficult" (PMID: 39388901).

F002 — Multifactorial, dose-dependent virulence

Virulence combines aerolysin (aer), Act (act), hemolysins, cytotonic enterotoxins (alt/ast), and proteases. aer was present in 87%, act 7.7%, ast 17% of a Libyan isolate set (PMID: 25216211). Crucially, "as the present of virulence genes increased, the proteolytic, haemolytic and cytotoxic activities of extracellular products also increased" (PMID: 21219556), and 5-gene strains had lower LD50 in fish (PMID: 22725694).

F003 — Act is the core enterotoxic engine

Act is a 52-kDa aerolysin-related pore-forming toxin (pores 1.14–2.8 nm; rat ileal-loop minimal dose 200 ng; mouse IV LD50 27.5 ng) (PMID: 9317040). It "activates stress-associated p38, c-Jun NH(2)-terminal kinase (JNK), and extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2)" and induces apoptosis (PMID: 15215244), and "stimulated the production of tumor necrosis factor alpha and upregulated ... IL-1beta ... and IL-6" (PMID: 10768977).

F004 — Intrinsic β-lactam resistance shapes therapy

Isolates are uniformly susceptible to cefepime, amikacin, azithromycin, meropenem; only 14% to amoxicillin (PMID: 29205196); gastroenteritis isolates almost uniformly susceptible to amikacin, gentamicin, aztreonam, cefepime, ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, meropenem (PMID: 38783344).

F005 — Major veterinary disease (MAS); vaccines protect

"The etiological agent of this disease is usually Aeromonas hydrophila. Mortality rates due to MAS are frequently high" (PMID: 32331001); whole-cell inactivated vaccines give highest protection.

F006 — Molecular ID required for accurate speciation

Routine MALDI-TOF/automated systems misidentify Aeromonas; definitive ID needs gyrB/rpoD/gyrA MLPA (PMID: 41903649).

F007 — Hypervirulent lineages independent of classical secretion systems

For epidemic ST251 clones, "the known virulence factors, including a type III secretion system, a type VI secretion system and lateral flagella, are not required for the high virulence" — instead myo-inositol/sialic-acid/L-fucose metabolism is implicated (PMID: 26014286).

F008 — Waterborne/foodborne transmission; vulnerable hosts

"The ability of these bacteria to grow competitively at 5°C may be indicative of their potential as a public health hazard" (PMID: 27051177); enriched in diarrheal children (p<0.001, PMID: 15005966); leading enteric agent (14.6%) in immunocompromised children (PMID: 38778271).

F009 — Aerolysin: prototype β-pore-forming toxin

Secreted as proaerolysin, proteolytically activated ("a decrease in random structure and an increase in beta-sheet content" PMID: 9398250), binds GPI anchors, heptamerizes (mass 333,850 Da confirms heptamer, PMID: 8617369), and inserts via a "unique concerted swirling movement ... leading to the insertion of a transmembrane β-barrel" (PMID: 23912165).

F010 — Global prevalence 4.2%, sanitation-linked, declining

"The pooled prevalence of Aeromonas in children with diarrhoea worldwide was 4.2% (95% CI 3.1-5.6%)"; poor sanitation → 8.8%; decreasing trend over time (PMID: 37217902).

F011 — Adhesion/colonization gates cytotoxicity

"bacteria that showed a stronger adhesion to intestinal mucus were cytotoxic ... and bacteria that showed a weaker adhesion ... did not lead to alterations of monolayers" (PMID: 18924378); pili are colonization factors via galactose/mannose receptors (PMID: 1363704).

F012 — Antibiotic-alternative prevention works in aquaculture

Dietary probiotics reduced post-challenge mortality from 53.3% to 13.3% (PMID: 41946979); rising AMR "necessitat[es] the urgent need for sustainable alternative treatments" (PMID: 42573755).

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Causality unmet at population level. Similar carriage in symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals (PMID: 39388901) and no toxin–disease correlation in one series (PMID: 3584426) mean attribution specifically to A. hydrophila is often uncertain; co-infections are common.
  2. Species misidentification historically conflated A. caviae, A. veronii, A. dhakensis, and A. hydrophila, so species-specific clinical data are imprecise; A. caviae now dominates many clinical series (PMID: 38783344).
  3. Human vs fish evidence imbalance. Much mechanistic and prevention data come from fish/aquaculture and cell/animal models, not human enteric disease.
  4. No human clinical trials define antibiotic benefit for uncomplicated cases; treatment recommendations are extrapolated from susceptibility data.
  5. AMR evolution (ESBL/CTX-M, class-1 integrons, carbapenem failures) is dynamic and may outpace current guidance.
  6. No host-genetic dimension — sections 4 (host genetics) and 9 (inheritance) are inherently not applicable for an acquired infection.

Proposed Follow-up Actions

  1. Prospective, culture-independent, species-resolved cohort studies (metagenomics + strain WGS) pairing symptom status with virulence-gene content to resolve the pathogen-vs-commensal question and define an attributable fraction.
  2. Randomized controlled trials of antibiotic therapy vs rehydration alone in moderate–severe Aeromonas gastroenteritis, stratified by host immune status.
  3. Integrated One Health AMR surveillance linking environmental/wastewater, food, aquaculture, and clinical isolates to track ESBL/integron gene flow (PMID: 42288750, PMID: 40269715).
  4. Mechanistic dissection of ST251-type metabolism-linked hypervirulence (inositol/sialic acid/fucose utilization) as potential anti-virulence targets (PMID: 26014286).
  5. Anti-virulence / anti-toxin therapeutics (Act/aerolysin pore inhibitors, adhesion blockers) and continued development of aquaculture probiotic/vaccine reservoir control to reduce human exposure.
  6. Standardize molecular species identification (gyrB/rpoD/gyrA) in diagnostic labs to improve epidemiological precision.
