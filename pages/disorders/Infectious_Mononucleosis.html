Infectious Mononucleosis

Infectious Disease MONDO:0005810 Pathograph 15 Show in embeddings browser

The clinical syndrome that follows symptomatic primary infection with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV, human gammaherpesvirus 4), a gamma-herpesvirus spread by saliva. EBV enters oropharyngeal B lymphocytes through the complement receptor CR2 (CD21), drives their proliferation, and establishes lifelong latency; the illness itself is largely immunopathology, produced by a massive oligoclonal expansion of EBV-specific CD8+ T cells (the atypical "Downey" lymphocytes) that infiltrate lymphoid tissue. The typical picture is sore throat, posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, fever, fatigue and splenomegaly in an adolescent or young adult, lasting several weeks and self-limited. Subclinical hepatitis is common; splenic rupture is the feared acute complication. Rare inborn errors of immunity (notably SH2D1A, causing X-linked lymphoproliferative disease) convert primary EBV infection into severe or fatal mononucleosis with hemophagocytosis. A history of infectious mononucleosis raises the later risk of Hodgkin lymphoma and multiple sclerosis.

Heterophile antibody and EBV-specific serology
A typical clinical picture with a positive heterophile (monospot) test usually suffices, but heterophile antibodies are not specific and are often absent in young children, so EBV-specific antibody profiles are the reference for staging infection. Atypical lymphocytosis on the blood film strongly supports the diagnosis.

1. Disease Information

Overview. Infectious mononucleosis (IM) is the clinical illness that sometimes follows primary EBV infection. A review by Dunmire, Hogquist and Balfour describes it as typically "sore throat, cervical lymph node enlargement, fatigue and fever, most often in adolescents and young adults, lasting several weeks" (PMC4670567, PMID 26424648). Balfour et al. (Clin Transl Immunol 2015, PMID 25774295) add two points: - A typical picture with a positive heterophile test is usually enough to diagnose IM, although heterophile antibodies are not specific. - IM is linked to long-term consequences, especially Hodgkin lymphoma and multiple sclerosis.

According to the CDC, EBV is the most common cause of IM, but other viruses can cause it too (CDC).

2. Etiology

Cause. Primary infection with EBV, a gammaherpesvirus spread through saliva (CDC). Whether primary infection causes IM, rather than no symptoms, depends strongly on age. In a prospective cohort of Minnesota students who started out antibody-negative, 66 of 143 became infected over a median of 3 years. Of those 66, 77% developed IM, 12% had atypical symptoms and 11% had no symptoms (Balfour et al., J Infect Dis 2013, PMID 23100562).

Environmental and behavioural risk factors - Deep kissing: students who reported deep kissing, with or without intercourse, had a higher risk of infection than those who reported no kissing (PMID 23100562). - Age at first infection: infection in adolescence or early adulthood carries the highest risk. Peak incidence is at ages 15–24 (AAFP review via PMC6822211). - Socioeconomic status and race/ethnicity: in NHANES, EBV seroprevalence differed widely by race/ethnicity and income (Dowd et al., PLoS ONE 2013, PMC3661547). Better-off groups tend to acquire EBV later, which raises their risk of IM.

Genetic risk factors (host susceptibility)

HLA class I. McAulay et al. studied markers near the HLA class I region (J Clin Invest 2007;117:3042, PMC1994627). - Alleles of the microsatellites D6S510 and D6S265 were "significantly associated with development of IM". - Alleles of the SNPs rs2530388 and rs6457110 were more frequent in IM than in EBV-seronegative controls. For rs6457110, the T allele was found in 70% of IM patients versus 57% of controls (P = 0.038). - Patients carrying the associated allele "displayed higher EBV titers and milder IM symptoms".

Twin evidence. In the California Twin Program, pairwise concordance for IM was 12.1% in identical (monozygotic) pairs versus 6.1% in non-identical (dizygotic) pairs. The hazard ratio for the unaffected co-twin was 1.9 (95% CI 1.1–3.4). The authors call this "compatible with a heritable contribution" (Hwang et al., Epidemiol Infect 2012, PMC3845900).

Genome-wide association study (GWAS). Tian et al. (Nat Commun 2017, PMID 28928442) fine-mapped the HLA region across 23 infections, including mononucleosis. They report that virus-induced infections are "mainly associated with class I molecules". I did not find a GWAS specific to IM published in 2023–2024.

Inborn errors of immunity (rare). These cause severe, fulminant or fatal IM: - SH2D1A, which encodes SAP and causes XLP1 (Coffey et al., Nat Genet 1998, PMID 9771704). - XIAP, which causes XLP2. - ITK, MAGT1, CTPS1, CD27, CD70, CORO1A and RASGRP1 (Latour & Winter, Front Immunol 2018, PMC6004768).

Protective factors - HLA-A*02 is associated with lower risk of EBV-positive Hodgkin lymphoma (OR 0.70 per allele). The added Hodgkin risk after IM disappeared in people carrying HLA-A*02 (Hjalgrim et al., PNAS 2010, PMC2851961). Whether HLA-A*02 protects against IM itself was not shown directly here. - Early childhood infection: acquiring EBV in childhood, which is usually symptom-free, effectively protects against IM. This is inferred from the age dependence above.

Gene–environment interaction - Sequence of events toward Hodgkin lymphoma: host HLA-A type, then IM, then EBV-positive Hodgkin lymphoma. The authors conclude that "HLA class I-restricted EBV-specific cytotoxic T-cell responses and events in the early immune response to EBV infection in IM play critical roles" (PMC2851961). - Multiple sclerosis: IM is also a risk factor for MS (see §11). - Databases: CTD and other GxE databases were not queried.

4. Genetic/Molecular Information

Causal genes: none in the host, because IM is infectious. The causal agent is the EBV genome. The relevant viral entry proteins are gp350, gp42, gH/gL and gB (§6).

Pathogenic variants and ACMG classification: not applicable to IM as a whole. ClinVar and gnomAD apply only to the inborn-error genes. - SH2D1A lies on Xq25, has 4 exons and encodes a 128-amino-acid protein (SAP). - XLP mutations make SAP lose function: "SAP cDNAs isolated from … patients with X-linked lymphoproliferative syndrome did not bind SLAM" (search summary of Sayos et al.). - For ITK, MAGT1, CTPS1 and RASGRP1, "LOF mutations … impair T-cell activation and proliferation after TCR triggering" (Béziat & Jouanguy 2021, via PMC8721657). - These variants are germline: X-linked for SH2D1A and XIAP, autosomal recessive for most of the others.

Modifier loci: the HLA class I region (rs6457110, rs2530388, D6S510, D6S265) and HLA-A*01 / A*02 (§2). A linkage and association study found that at least 2 HLA loci affect anti-EBNA-1 antibody levels (PMC4099326).

Epigenetics and chromosomal abnormalities: I retrieved no IM-specific human data. (EBV latency programs are controlled epigenetically, but that literature was not retrieved here.)

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. Oral exposure to EBV in saliva. Transmission happens through saliva (CDC). This leads to infection of tonsillar B cells and epithelium.
  2. Entry into cells. Two branches:
  3. B cells: viral gp350 binds CD21 (CR2), gp42 binds HLA class II, and this triggers fusion by gH/gL and gB (PMC9529843).
  4. Epithelial cells: these lack CD21 and HLA class II, so gH/gL binding to αv integrins acts as the trigger instead (PMC7317989).

Results in latent infection that transforms and expands B cells, plus lytic replication. 3. Epithelium amplifies the virus. Shedding dynamics "strongly implicate the epithelium as a site where virus is amplified prior to shedding" (Hadinoto et al., PLoS Pathog 2009, PMC2708605). This leads to high viral load in saliva and blood. The incubation period is about 4–6 weeks (CDC). 4. Innate control first. NK cells provide initial control, and human NK cells limit CD8+ T-cell expansion. This was shown in humanized mice (PMC6542544), so in humans it is inferred. 5. Massive expansion of CD8+ T cells. "Selective and massive expansion of a few dominant clones of CD8+ T cells is an important feature of the primary response" (Callan et al., Nat Med 1996;2:906, Nature). The expansions are limited to CD8 cells, and some clones persist about a year (PMID 11067931). This results in the atypical lymphocytosis and inverted CD8:CD4 ratio. 6. Symptoms come from the immune response. The lymphocytosis infiltrates lymphoid organs, causing lymphadenopathy, tonsillar swelling and splenomegaly, and is accompanied by fever and fatigue. The idea that the immune response causes the symptoms is supported by HLA findings: patients with the risk allele had higher viral titres but milder symptoms and fewer lymphocytes (PMC1994627). This step is therefore inferred from correlation. - Branch, splenic infiltration: leads to splenomegaly and, rarely, rupture. - Branch, liver: leads to transaminitis. - Branch, tonsillar swelling: leads to airway obstruction. - Branch, failure of cytotoxic killing (for example SAP/SLAM or MAGT1/NKG2D defects): leads to uncontrolled B-cell proliferation, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) or fatal IM (PMC6004768). 7. Resolution. Clinical recovery follows, with lifelong latency in memory B cells. A history of IM is linked to later EBV-positive Hodgkin lymphoma and multiple sclerosis (§11).

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: acute, after about 4–6 weeks of incubation. Peak ages are 15–24 years.
  • Course: self-limited, lasting several weeks. Fatigue can persist for months.
  • Remission: spontaneous.
  • Critical periods:
  • Most splenic ruptures happen in the first 3 weeks (Bartlett et al., Injury 2016, 85 cases).
  • Military data suggest the risk lasts longer, and support delaying return to contact sport until about 31 days after symptom onset (Sylvester et al.; summarized in PMC6822211).
  • Return-to-play decisions should be individualized.
