D-Lactate Dehydrogenase Deficiency

Mendelian MONDO:0009505 Pathograph 25 Show in embeddings browser disorder of glycolysis Inborn Error of Metabolism

D-lactate dehydrogenase deficiency is an autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in LDHD. LDHD encodes the mitochondrial FAD-dependent D-lactate dehydrogenase, which oxidises D-lactate and other hydrophobic D-2-hydroxyacids to the corresponding 2-oxoacids. D-lactate is made in small amounts by the glyoxalase pathway that detoxifies methylglyoxal, and is also taken up from the diet and from gut bacteria; LDHD is the route by which it is cleared. Without the enzyme, D-lactate rises in plasma and is excreted in very large amounts in urine, together with D-2-hydroxyisovaleric and D-2-hydroxyisocaproic acids, while L-lactate stays normal. Two clinical presentations have been reported in about twenty patients. In several families the only clinical feature is hyperuricemia with juvenile- or early-onset gout, attributed to renal urate retention coupled to D-lactate excretion. In other patients the disorder was found during the work-up of developmental delay, hypotonia, ataxia or metabolic acidosis, but three of those patients carried a second diagnosis that could explain the neurological picture, so whether LDHD deficiency itself harms the nervous system is unresolved. All reported pathogenic variants reduce or abolish enzyme activity; there is no evidence for a gain-of-function or dominant-negative mechanism, and heterozygous carriers are unaffected.

Methylglyoxal-Derived and Gut Bacterial D-Lactate Load
Mechanism confidence: Established
D-lactate is not a product of mammalian glycolysis. It is formed continuously in small amounts when the cytosolic glyoxalase system detoxifies methylglyoxal, a by-product of triose phosphate metabolism, and it also enters the body from fermented foods and from carbohydrate fermentation by intestinal bacteria. This load is normal; in LDHD deficiency it is the substrate that can no longer be cleared.

Loss of Mitochondrial D-Lactate Dehydrogenase Activity
Mechanism confidence: Established
LDHD is a mitochondrial flavoenzyme of the vanillyl-alcohol-oxidase (FAD-binding type 4) family, peripherally associated with the inner membrane. With FAD, and in the mouse enzyme a manganese ion, in the active site it oxidises D-lactate to pyruvate and, with the same chemistry, other D-2-hydroxyacids that carry a small hydrophobic side chain; it does not act on L-lactate. Purified human LDHD passes the electrons directly to cytochrome c, so D-lactate oxidation enters the respiratory chain downstream of complexes I and III. Which face of the inner membrane the enzyme occupies is not settled: activity measurements in rat liver mitochondria placed it on the matrix side, whereas direct reduction of cytochrome c implies access to the intermembrane space. Its oligomeric state is also unsettled (monomer, dimer, tetramer and hexamer have each been reported). Biallelic pathogenic variants remove the dehydrogenase activity. Null alleles (a deletion of exons 1-7, a splice-donor duplication whose transcript is not detectable, and a frameshift in the last exon that replaces the C-terminal 53 residues, which include the manganese ligand Glu465, the catalytic His466 and the FAD-binding Asn502) abolish the protein or its active site. The five reported missense changes fall into two structural classes when placed on the mouse LDHD crystal structure with FAD, manganese and D-lactate bound: Arg370 and Trp374 line the substrate and flavin pocket (the Arg370 guanidinium is 2.9 angstroms from both carboxylate oxygens of D-lactate; Trp374 is 3.4 angstroms from FAD), whereas Thr463, Thr251 and Val69 are buried second-shell or core residues whose substitution destabilises the fold or the FAD site. In recombinant mouse enzyme all four tested substitutions lose about 90 percent of activity and are thermally destabilised, and the two threonine substitutions also lose FAD. The disease mechanism is therefore loss of function; nothing reported suggests a gain of function or a dominant-negative effect, and carrier parents are unaffected.

Systemic D-Lactate Accumulation
Mechanism confidence: Established
Plasma D-lactate, normally in the low micromolar range, rises to between about 0.3 and 3 mmol/L, and D-lactate is excreted in urine in very large amounts. L-lactate is unaffected, so routine lactate assays, which measure only the L isomer, miss the abnormality. The accumulation is constant, is not corrected by antibiotics, and adds an unmeasured anion to plasma.

Accumulation of Branched-Chain D-2-Hydroxyacids
Mechanism confidence: Established
D-2-hydroxyisovaleric acid and D-2-hydroxyisocaproic acid, and in some patients 2-hydroxy-3-methylvaleric acid, are raised in urine and plasma. These are the D-2-hydroxy analogues of the branched-chain oxoacids of valine, leucine and isoleucine and are oxidised by LDHD in vitro. Their origin in the body, endogenous or bacterial, is not established. They are a more specific marker of LDHD deficiency than D-lactate alone.

Renal Urate Retention Coupled to D-Lactate Excretion
Mechanism confidence: Provisional
Homozygotes excrete a smaller fraction of filtered urate than their relatives, and their serum urate is raised. The proposed mechanism is anion exchange in the proximal tubule: D-lactate in the tubular lumen and cell is exchanged for urate, so the large D-lactate flux through the kidney drives urate back into blood. URAT1 (SLC22A12) is the known apical urate-anion exchanger of the human proximal tubule and is the obvious candidate, but no study of LDHD deficiency has tested which transporter is responsible.

description: >-
  D-lactate dehydrogenase deficiency is an autosomal recessive inborn error of
  metabolism caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in LDHD. LDHD
  encodes the mitochondrial FAD-dependent D-lactate dehydrogenase, which oxidises D-lactate and other hydrophobic
  D-2-hydroxyacids to the corresponding 2-oxoacids. D-lactate is made in small
  amounts by the glyoxalase pathway that detoxifies methylglyoxal, and is also
  taken up from the diet and from gut bacteria; LDHD is the route by which it
  is cleared. Without the enzyme, D-lactate rises in plasma and is excreted in
  very large amounts in urine, together with D-2-hydroxyisovaleric and
  D-2-hydroxyisocaproic acids, while L-lactate stays normal. Two clinical
  presentations have been reported in about twenty patients. In several
  families the only clinical feature is hyperuricemia with juvenile- or
  early-onset gout, attributed to renal urate retention coupled to D-lactate
  excretion. In other patients the disorder was found during the work-up of
  developmental delay, hypotonia, ataxia or metabolic acidosis, but three of
  those patients carried a second diagnosis that could explain the neurological
  picture, so whether LDHD deficiency itself harms the nervous system is
  unresolved. All reported pathogenic variants reduce or abolish enzyme
  activity; there is no evidence for a gain-of-function or dominant-negative
  mechanism, and heterozygous carriers are unaffected.

  description: >-
    LDHD is a mitochondrial flavoenzyme of the vanillyl-alcohol-oxidase
    (FAD-binding type 4) family, peripherally associated with the inner
    membrane. With FAD, and in the mouse enzyme a manganese ion, in the active
    site it oxidises D-lactate to pyruvate and, with the same chemistry, other
    D-2-hydroxyacids that carry a small hydrophobic side chain; it does not
    act on L-lactate. Purified human LDHD passes the electrons directly to
    cytochrome c, so D-lactate oxidation enters the respiratory chain
    downstream of complexes I and III. Which face of the inner membrane the
    enzyme occupies is not settled: activity measurements in rat liver
    mitochondria placed it on the matrix side, whereas direct reduction of
    cytochrome c implies access to the intermembrane space. Its oligomeric
    state is also unsettled (monomer, dimer, tetramer and hexamer have each
    been reported). Biallelic pathogenic variants remove the dehydrogenase
    activity. Null alleles (a deletion of exons 1-7, a splice-donor
    duplication whose transcript is not detectable, and a frameshift in the
    last exon that replaces the C-terminal 53 residues, which include the
    manganese ligand Glu465, the catalytic His466 and the FAD-binding Asn502)
    abolish the protein or its active site. The five reported missense changes fall into two
    structural classes when placed on the mouse LDHD crystal structure with
    FAD, manganese and D-lactate bound: Arg370 and Trp374 line the substrate
