Glucocorticoid Resistance

Mendelian MONDO:0014421 Pathograph 20 Show in embeddings browser Adrenogenital syndrome

Primary generalized glucocorticoid resistance (Chrousos syndrome) is a rare genetic disorder of generalized, partial target-tissue insensitivity to glucocorticoids caused by inactivating variants in NR3C1, which encodes the human glucocorticoid receptor. Because peripheral tissues respond poorly to cortisol, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal negative feedback is blunted and the axis is compensatorily upregulated, raising cortisol and ACTH. The excess ACTH drives adrenal overproduction of steroids with mineralocorticoid activity (deoxycorticosterone, corticosterone) and of adrenal androgens, so affected individuals present with hypertension, hypokalemic alkalosis, and/or hyperandrogenism, together with adrenal hyperplasia, while cortisol-dependent functions are largely preserved and stigmata of Cushing's syndrome are absent. The clinical spectrum ranges from asymptomatic to severe; homozygous or compound-heterozygous defects present earlier and more severely than heterozygous ones. This entry covers the primary (NR3C1-based) Mendelian disorder, not acquired or tissue-specific glucocorticoid resistance seen in conditions such as asthma, inflammatory disease, or hematologic malignancy.

1. Disease information

Overview. Primary generalized glucocorticoid resistance (GGR) is also called Chrousos syndrome, familial or sporadic glucocorticoid resistance, or cortisol resistance. It is a rare condition with generalized, partial tissue insensitivity to glucocorticoids. The cause is usually an inactivating defect in the glucocorticoid receptor gene (NR3C1). - The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is compensatorily activated to overcome the resistance, giving raised ACTH and cortisol. - Raised ACTH also drives adrenal steroids with mineralocorticoid and androgen activity. - Patients therefore show mineralocorticoid excess and hyperandrogenism, but no Cushingoid features. - Verified: Charmandari et al. 2008 (PMID:18319312) report that the spectrum runs from asymptomatic to severe cases of hyperandrogenism, fatigue, and/or mineralocorticoid excess. - Verified: compensatory HPA-axis activation drives ACTH hypersecretion because the reduced sensitivity of peripheral tissues is offset by increased HPA activity.

2. Etiology

Causal factors. - Germline NR3C1 variants (chromosome 5q31.3) reduce the receptor's function. - Verified: the first familial defect was an abnormal receptor with decreased affinity for cortisol, in Chrousos 1982 (PMID:6282933). The father had mineralocorticoid excess with hypertension and hypokalemia. He and his son had high cortisol without Cushing's stigmata. - Verified: Vingerhoeds 1976 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 43:1128–1133) was the earlier "Spontaneous hypercortisolism without Cushing's syndrome" report. I did not find its PMID. - Verified: Hurley 1991 (PMID:1704018) found a point mutation in the hormone-binding domain, with a Val 641 mutant receptor. It reports a two- to threefold reduction in receptor-ligand binding affinity in the propositus, and threefold lower dexamethasone binding for the mutant. The D641V shorthand was not confirmed in the retrieved text. - Verified: Karl et al. 1993 described the first family with glucocorticoid receptor haploinsufficiency, a different mechanism from the affinity defect. - Genetic heterogeneity: some patients with a similar phenotype have no NR3C1 mutation (PMID:28742501). Other glucocorticoid-signaling factors are suspected, such as co-chaperones and coregulators.

Risk factors. Genetic factors are the dominant ones. Environmental risk factors are not established. Related polymorphisms (BclI, N363S, ER22/23EK) modulate sensitivity in the general population and are not causal for this disease (unverified).

Protective factors. None known.

Gene-environment interactions. Not established for the Mendelian disease. Stress and illness may unmask or worsen it, but I have no source.

4. Genetic and molecular information

Causal gene. NR3C1 (glucocorticoid receptor, GR). Alternative-splicing isoforms are GRα (the active one) and GRβ.

Variants. - The spectrum includes missense variants in the ligand-binding domain (e.g., Val641, as in Hurley 1991), nonsense and frameshift variants causing haploinsufficiency (Karl 1993), splice-site variants, and deletions. - The Endotext chapter lists the consequences as: - reduced affinity to glucocorticoids or to target DNA; - inability to transactivate glucocorticoid-responsive genes; - slowed cytoplasmic-to-nuclear translocation. - Dominant-negative effects are described for some missense variants (unverified). - Classification per ACMG/AMP, population frequencies from gnomAD, and ClinGen validity were not checked. None established as a cause. - Environmental factors, lifestyle factors, and infectious agents: none. - Exogenous glucocorticoids and illness-related stress affect the HPA axis and can confound testing. - The pharmacological dexamethasone suppression test is itself the diagnostic perturbation.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Causal chain. 1. A germline NR3C1 variant reduces functional GRα. The mechanisms are reduced ligand affinity, reduced DNA binding or transactivation, slowed nuclear translocation, or haploinsufficiency. (Demonstrated: Hurley 1991 and others.) 2. Tissues become partially insensitive to cortisol. Negative feedback at the hypothalamus and pituitary is blunted. (Demonstrated: dexamethasone-suppression resistance.) 3. The result is raised CRH and ACTH, which in turn raise adrenal output. Cortisol rises until it partially overcomes the resistance. (Charmandari 2008, PMID:18319312.) 4. The branch, driven by the raised ACTH, goes two ways: - Branch A, mineralocorticoid. Excess cortisol, deoxycorticosterone, and corticosterone act at the mineralocorticoid receptor. Hypertension, hypokalemia, and alkalosis follow. Part of this is inferred. Cortisol is normally inactivated in kidney by 11β-HSD2, so saturation of that enzyme is a proposed contributor. - Branch B, androgen. Adrenal androgens (DHEA, DHEA-S, androstenedione) rise. Hyperandrogenism follows, with acne, hirsutism, oligo-anovulation, and precocious puberty. Androgen excess may also suppress the gonadotropin axis. 5. Chronic ACTH drive causes adrenal hyperplasia. Compensation maintains cortisol-dependent functions in most tissues, so Cushingoid features are absent. (Inferred.)

10. Diagnostics

  • Laboratory. Elevated 24-hour urinary free cortisol, plasma cortisol, and ACTH, with preserved circadian rhythm. The 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test is abnormal. Elevated adrenal androgens. Hypokalemia. LOINC codes were not checked and must be sourced through just loinc-seed-cache.
  • Imaging. Adrenal CT or MRI shows hyperplasia or adrenal masses.
  • Genetic testing. Single-gene or panel testing of NR3C1. Exome and genome sequencing are alternatives. Deletion/duplication analysis is a reasonable adjunct.
  • Functional. Glucocorticoid receptor binding assays in lymphocytes or fibroblasts, and reporter assays, can show reduced function (these are what the original studies used).
  • Differential. Cushing's syndrome (distinguished by absent Cushingoid features), apparent mineralocorticoid excess, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, PCOS, primary aldosteronism, pseudo-Cushing states, and depression. Hyperandrogenism with high cortisol and no Cushingoid features is the key clue.
  • Screening. No population screening. Cascade testing of relatives is appropriate.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Life expectancy is probably normal. No survival data were found.
  • Complications are hypertension and hypokalemia, infertility, and the effects of long-standing hyperandrogenism. Adrenal incidentalomas occur.
  • Treatment-related risk matters: over-treatment with glucocorticoids can produce iatrogenic Cushing's.
  • Prognostic factors and biomarkers: not studied.

12. Treatment

  • Principle. Suppress the excess ACTH drive with a high dose of a synthetic glucocorticoid with little mineralocorticoid activity, usually dexamethasone. The dose is titrated to the hormone and blood-pressure response, and cortisol alone is not the target.
  • Pharmacotherapy.
  • Dexamethasone (CHEBI/NCIT agent). NCIT treatment term: Pharmacotherapy NCIT:C15986, with therapeutic_agent.
  • Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone) for hypertension and hypokalemia. Spironolactone also has antiandrogen activity.
  • Antiandrogens or combined oral contraceptives for hyperandrogenism in women.
  • Advanced therapeutics. None. Gene therapy and cell therapy are not applicable.
  • Surgery. Not indicated except for an adrenal mass.
  • Supportive care. Fertility counseling and management.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention is not possible. Genetic counseling and cascade testing of relatives are the main measures.
  • Prenatal diagnosis and preimplantation testing are options where the variant is known.
  • Prophylaxis: none.
  • Tertiary prevention is blood-pressure and potassium monitoring, and avoiding glucocorticoid over-treatment.
