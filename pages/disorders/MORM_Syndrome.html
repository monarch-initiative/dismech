MORM Syndrome

Mendelian MONDO:0012423 Pathograph 21 Show in embeddings browser Ciliopathy Syndromic obesity

MORM syndrome (mental retardation, truncal obesity, retinal dystrophy and micropenis) is an autosomal recessive ciliopathy caused by biallelic nonsense variants in the last exon of INPP5E that truncate the protein just upstream of its C-terminal CaaX farnesylation motif. It was first described in a large consanguineous Pakistani kindred, where a founder p.Gln627* allele segregates, and a second truncating allele (p.Gln633*) has since been reported in a Slovenian girl. The truncated phosphatase loses its PDE6D-dependent ciliary targeting rather than its catalytic activity, which distinguishes MORM syndrome mechanistically from the allelic Joubert syndrome 1, whose INPP5E variants cluster in the phosphatase domain and abolish 5-phosphatase activity. The clinical picture is static intellectual disability, early-onset truncal obesity with hyperphagia and insulin resistance, retinal dystrophy, and micropenis in males, without the molar tooth sign or renal involvement of Joubert syndrome.

Pathophysiology

7
INPP5E C-terminal Truncation Removing the CaaX Motif
Both MORM alleles are nonsense variants in the terminal exon of INPP5E that delete the last residues of the protein, including the CaaX box whose cysteine (Cys644) is farnesylated. Unlike the Joubert syndrome 1 variants, which cluster in the phosphatase domain and abolish catalysis, the MORM truncations leave the phosphatase domain intact; ClinGen's curation records that the MORM variants lose membrane localisation while retaining enzymatic activity.

Failed PDE6D-Dependent Ciliary Targeting of INPP5E
Farnesylated INPP5E is carried to the cilium by the prenyl-binding protein PDE6D, released inside the cilium by ARL3-GTP, and retained with the help of ARL13B. Without its farnesylated C terminus the truncated MORM protein cannot use this route and fails to accumulate in the ciliary membrane. The same targeting failure is produced from the carrier side by a homozygous PDE6D splice variant in Joubert syndrome. The conformance target is the module's ciliary-gating node; the MORM defect is in cargo targeting rather than in basal-body or transition-zone structure, so the fit is partial.

Ciliary INPP5E converts PI(4,5)P2 to PI4P, keeping the ciliary membrane PI4P-rich. When INPP5E is absent, PI(4,5)P2 accumulates and recruits TULP3 together with the Hedgehog-repressing receptor GPR161 into the cilium, raising ciliary cAMP.

1. Disease Information

Overview. MORM syndrome was first described in 2006 in one consanguineous family with 14 affected members. The authors called it "a novel autosomal recessive disorder" with "static moderate mental retardation, truncal obesity, a congenital non-progressive retinal dystrophy and micropenis in males" (Hampshire et al., Eur J Hum Genet 2006;14(5):543-8, PMID 16493448, DOI 10.1038/sj.ejhg.5201577; Nature, Durham repository). It overlaps clinically with Bardet–Biedl syndrome (BBS) and Cohen syndrome. Jacoby et al. 2009 (PMID 19668215) later identified a homozygous nonsense variant in INPP5E as the cause, which places MORM among the ciliopathies.

2. Etiology

  • Causal factor: The disease is genetic and autosomal recessive. It is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in INPP5E. The founding variant is c.1879C>T, p.(Gln627Ter) (Q627X; OMIM 613037.0001), homozygous in the original family (Jacoby 2009, PMID 19668215; OMIM mirror).
  • Genetic risk factors:
  • Parental consanguinity: the index family was consanguineous (PMID 16493448).
  • Possible regional founder alleles: an INPP5E nonsense variant was reported in a Pakistani ciliopathy family, and the authors suggested a regional founder mutation (Khan et al., Ann Hum Genet 2019, PMID 31173343).
  • No GWAS susceptibility loci or modifier genes are established.
  • Environmental risk factors: None known. Not applicable for a monogenic disorder.
  • Protective factors: None reported, genetic or environmental.
  • Gene–environment interactions: None documented. In principle, diet and activity could change how severe the obesity becomes, but there is no MORM-specific evidence.

4. Genetic / Molecular Information

  • Gene: INPP5E (HGNC:21474, as I recall; not retrieved this session, so verify), 9q34.3, OMIM *613037. It encodes a 72-kDa inositol polyphosphate 5-phosphatase that localizes to the primary cilium.
  • Founding variant: NM_019892 c.1879C>T, p.Gln627Ter, in the last exon. Because it sits in the last exon, it is expected to escape nonsense-mediated decay and produce a truncated protein. The truncated protein lacks the C-terminal CaaX box, whose farnesylation tethers INPP5E to membranes (OMIM 613037 mirror; PMC3946372). In vitro, the mutant protein "had impaired localization in cilia and was unable to stabilize ciliary structures" (OMIM summary of PMID 19668215).
  • Variant classes: nonsense, frameshift, and missense.
  • Joubert-associated missense variants "clustered in the phosphatase domain and impaired 5-phosphatase activity" (verbatim; Bielas et al. 2009, PMID 19668216).
  • The MORM variant instead leaves the catalytic domain intact and removes the ciliary-targeting C-terminus.
  • ClinVar (query "INPP5E[gene] AND MORM", 128 records; 9 read verbatim). Most records list the conditions "MORM syndrome; Joubert syndrome 1". Examples:
  • c.657C>A p.(Tyr219Ter): Likely pathogenic
  • c.66dup p.(Leu23fs): Likely pathogenic
  • c.177del p.(Ser59fs): Likely pathogenic
  • c.208_214dup p.(Pro72fs): Likely pathogenic (multiple submitters)
  • c.1282G>A p.(Gly428Ser): VUS
  • several other missense variants: VUS
  • Allele frequency: gnomAD data for Q627X were not retrieved. The variant is presumed very rare.
  • Origin: germline.
  • Functional consequence: loss of function (mislocalization and loss of ciliary function). No gain-of-function or dominant-negative effect has been shown.
  • Allelic disorders: Joubert syndrome 1 (OMIM 213300) and non-syndromic retinitis pigmentosa / IRD (PMIDs 34188062, 38806661).
  • Modifier genes, epigenetics, chromosomal abnormalities: none reported. Note that the separate 9q34 subtelomeric deletion syndrome is caused by EHMT1 haploinsufficiency (Kleefstra 2006, PMID 16826528). It is a different disorder and should not be confused with MORM.

5. Environmental Information

There are no known environmental, lifestyle, or infectious causes. Diet and physical activity probably affect how severe the obesity becomes, but this is inferred and has not been studied.

6. Mechanism / Pathophysiology

Causal chain (inferred steps are marked):

  1. Biallelic INPP5E loss-of-function variants (for example, Q627X removing the CaaX box) lead to failure of INPP5E to localize to, or act in, the primary cilium (PMID 19668215; ciliary-targeting signals described in eLife 2022, PMID 36063381).
  2. Loss of ciliary 5-phosphatase activity results in a build-up of the phosphoinositides PI(4,5)P2 and PI(3,4,5)P3 in the ciliary membrane (Chávez et al., Dev Cell 2015, PMID 26190144; Garcia-Gonzalo et al. 2015).
  3. Higher ciliary PI(4,5)P2 recruits TULP3 and the GPCR Gpr161 into the cilium. This raises cAMP and represses Sonic Hedgehog output (lower Gli1) (PMID 26190144).
  4. 3a (cilium-stability branch): Unopposed PI3K/PDGFRα–Akt signaling leads to destabilization of the primary cilium. Bielas: mutations "promoted premature destabilization of cilia in response to stimulation" (verbatim, PMID 19668216); Jacoby (PMID 19668215). INPP5E also interacts with AURKA (PMID 25395580).
  5. 3b (transition-zone branch): Altered phosphoinositides disrupt transition-zone function and ciliary protein trafficking (PMID 27998989; PMID 27927754).
  6. Retina: in photoreceptor connecting cilia, INPP5E loss impairs axoneme formation, disc morphogenesis, and outer-segment maintenance. This leads to photoreceptor dysfunction and degeneration, which presents as retinal dystrophy (mouse: Sharif et al. 2021, PMID 33711342, and Gupta et al. 2025, PMID 39871753; human iPSC retinal organoids: PMID 42374981). Mouse models degenerate rapidly, whereas the original MORM retinopathy was non-progressive. Why this differs is unexplained.
  7. Hypothalamus (inferred for MORM): ciliary dysfunction in MC4R-expressing neurons impairs melanocortin and satiety signaling, which leads to hyperphagia and truncal obesity. Supporting evidence: CRISPRi repression of Inpp5e in MC4R neurons was sufficient to promote obesity in mice (Diener Blake et al., Mol Metab 2026, PMID 42838451).
  8. Brain and gonadal axis (inferred): ciliary signaling defects during neurodevelopment lead to intellectual disability. Ciliary defects affecting the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, or Hh-dependent genital development, are a plausible route to micropenis. Neither has been shown directly in MORM.

Pathways: primary-cilium phosphoinositide signaling; PI3K–Akt; Sonic Hedgehog; cAMP/PKA; melanocortin (MC4R).

Other cellular roles: INPP5E also supports autophagosome–lysosome fusion in neurons (PMID 27340123) and genomic stability during mitosis (PMID 28031327). Their relevance to MORM is unknown.

8. Temporal Development

  • Onset: Congenital. The retinal dystrophy and micropenis are present early. Intellectual disability and obesity become apparent in early childhood.
  • Progression: The original family showed static intellectual disability and non-progressive retinal dystrophy (PMID 16493448), with cataract later (OMIM summary). Other INPP5E-related retinopathies are progressive (PMIDs 34211432, 41465079).
  • Duration: lifelong.
  • Remission: none.
  • Critical periods: early childhood, for vision support, educational intervention, and weight management (inferred).

9. Inheritance and Population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive (OMIM; PMID 16493448).
  • Penetrance: appears complete in the homozygous family; expressivity is variable across the INPP5E spectrum.
  • Anticipation and mosaicism: none reported.
  • Consanguinity: central to the index family.
  • Founder effects: possible in Pakistani families (PMID 31173343).
  • Prevalence: not established; fewer than 1 in 1,000,000. Orphanet-derived text describes "14 individuals" (consistent with the index family) (Qeios/Orphanet text). A few additional MORM-like cases with INPP5E variants have been published, for example a female with severe obesity and cone-rod dystrophy (PMID 34211432).
  • Geography: the index family was consanguineous and, per the OMIM summary, Pakistani.
  • Sex ratio: both sexes are affected. Micropenis is male-specific.
  • Carrier frequency: not determined.
  • Related prevalence data: in Joubert syndrome and related disorders (JSRD), INPP5E mutations were found in 17 of 483 probands (Travaglini et al. 2013, PMID 23386033).

10. Diagnostics

  • Clinical evaluation:
  • ophthalmology: fundoscopy, ERG, OCT, visual acuity (OCT and ERG findings in INPP5E retinopathy: PMID 41465079)
  • developmental and IQ assessment
  • anthropometry (BMI, waist)
  • genital exam and endocrine work-up (gonadotropins and testosterone are reasonable but not described in the literature)
  • metabolic labs: glucose/HbA1c, lipids, liver enzymes
  • brain MRI to look for the Joubert molar tooth sign, which separates MORM from Joubert syndrome
  • renal ultrasound to exclude the cystic kidney disease seen in ciliopathies (inferred)
  • Genetic testing:
  • Exome or genome sequencing, or a ciliopathy/IRD/BBS/obesity gene panel that includes INPP5E, is the practical first approach.
  • Targeted testing for c.1879C>T can be used for families.
  • Exome sequencing has proved useful in JSRD (PMID 23034536).
  • Reverse phenotyping helped reclassify an INPP5E family (de Goede et al. 2016, PMID 26748598).
  • CMA, karyotype, FISH, mtDNA, and repeat-expansion testing are not indicated except to rule out other diagnoses, such as a 9q34 deletion (EHMT1).
  • Functional assays (research): cilia length and INPP5E localization in fibroblasts (PMIDs 38806661, 29052317).
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • Bardet–Biedl syndrome: polydactyly, renal disease, progressive rod-cone dystrophy
  • Cohen syndrome: microcephaly, characteristic facies, neutropenia, mottled retina
  • Alström syndrome
  • Joubert syndrome (INPP5E, molar tooth sign)
  • Prader–Willi syndrome
  • MC4R and other monogenic obesity
  • non-syndromic INPP5E retinopathy
  • No formal diagnostic criteria exist.
  • Screening: no newborn screening. Cascade carrier testing and prenatal or preimplantation testing are possible once familial variants are known.

11. Outcome / Prognosis

There are no survival or life-expectancy data. Survival into adulthood is implied by the multigenerational pedigree and the onset of cataract in the second or third decade (OMIM summary). The main morbidities are: - lifelong intellectual disability; - visual impairment, possibly progressive in some genotypes; - obesity with metabolic complications such as insulin resistance (PMID 34211432); - cataract.

There are no prognostic biomarkers and no QoL studies.

12. Treatment

No disease-specific therapy exists. Management is supportive. All items below are general standard of care, not trial-based: - Low-vision services and cataract surgery when needed. NCIT terms: Cataract Extraction; Low Vision Rehabilitation (verify IDs). - Obesity: diet, exercise, behavioral therapy. - MC4R agonist therapy (setmelanotide) is approved for BBS-associated obesity. Its use in MORM is untested but has mechanistic rationale (PMID 42838451, the MC4R-neuron link). I did not retrieve approval sources for setmelanotide in this session. - Micropenis: endocrine evaluation and possible testosterone therapy (general practice, not MORM-specific). - Developmental and educational support, plus speech therapy. - Gene therapy: none. RPGR-type retinal gene therapy vectors exist for other IRDs (PMID 31106594), but nothing exists for INPP5E. - Clinical trials: none found that are specific to MORM or INPP5E. - Pharmacogenomics: not applicable.

13. Prevention

  • Primary prevention: genetic counseling. Recurrence risk is 25% per pregnancy for carrier parents. Carrier testing, prenatal diagnosis, or PGT are options once the familial variant is known. Counseling is especially relevant for consanguineous families.
  • Tertiary prevention:
  • early weight management to prevent diabetes and cardiometabolic complications;
  • regular ophthalmic surveillance, including for cataract;
  • early developmental intervention.
  • Vaccination, environmental, and public-health measures: not applicable.
