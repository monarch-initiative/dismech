Mitochondrial Complex I Deficiency, Nuclear Type 26

Mendelian MONDO:0032630 Pathograph 28 Show in embeddings browser Mitochondrial Complex I Deficiency Mitochondrial Disease

Mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 26 (MC1DN26) is the isolated complex I deficiency caused by biallelic variants in NDUFA9, a nuclear-encoded accessory (supernumerary) subunit of the complex I Q module. NDUFA9 sits where the hydrophilic matrix arm meets the membrane arm. Knocking it out in human cells leaves a membrane-arm subcomplex with no matrix arm attached, so the protein is needed to hold the two arms together during a late step of assembly. Patient fibroblasts show the same thing from the other side: less fully assembled complex I, Q-module subassemblies piling up, and wild-type NDUFA9 rescuing both. The disease was first described in 2012 in a single neonate with fatal Leigh syndrome. By 2025, twelve affected people from ten families were known, and the picture had widened into a progressive neurodevelopmental disorder with prominent dystonia and a Leigh syndrome pattern on brain MRI. Neurodevelopmental delay followed by motor deterioration and seizures is the usual presentation. Deterioration always starts in the first decade, dystonia was present in every patient who survived past early infancy, and the striatum (putamen and caudate) was abnormal on every MRI reviewed. The range runs from death in the first months of life to isolated generalized dystonia with survival into adulthood. Every disease-associated allele reported so far is a missense change, and three of them hit arginine 360. No disease-modifying treatment exists. Management is symptomatic, chiefly of the dystonia.

Biallelic NDUFA9 Missense Variants
Mechanism confidence: Established
The initiating lesion. Nine missense variants are known, all in the mature protein rather than the mitochondrial targeting presequence, and most lie in the last exon. Arg360 is changed in three unrelated families (to Cys, Gly and Leu) and has been proposed as a mutational hotspot. Two complementation experiments in patient fibroblasts, carrying the Arg321Pro and Arg360Cys alleles, show that wild-type NDUFA9 corrects the defect. That makes the variants causal and loss of function.

NDUFA9 Misfolding and Loss of Membrane Anchoring
Mechanism confidence: Provisional
How a single amino-acid change in an accessory subunit could weaken the enzyme. Structural modelling on the human complex I structure predicts two kinds of damage. Most variants replace a positively charged arginine or lysine. Some of these break ion pairs, which leads to misfolding. The Arg360 variants instead remove a residue that binds negatively charged membrane phospholipids, and that binding is how NDUFA9 anchors the hydrophilic domain to the membrane. Steady-state NDUFA9 protein was reduced in fibroblasts from two brothers homozygous for Arg354Gln, but was normal in a compound heterozygote (Arg360Gly/Gly267Val) who still lost assembled complex I. So reduced protein is not the only route to disease. Marked PROVISIONAL because the misfolding and phospholipid-binding consequences come from in silico modelling and have not been measured for any variant.

Destabilized Matrix-Membrane Arm Junction and Stalled Complex I Assembly
Mechanism confidence: Established
NDUFA9 joins complex I early, as one of the Q-module matrix-arm subunits (with NDUFS2 and NDUFS3) in the roughly 400 kDa intermediate that contains ND1 and the NDUFAF3/NDUFAF4 assembly factors. This is the intermediate that fails to form in MC1DN18 (NDUFAF3). Its essential job, though, comes late. Human cells lacking NDUFA9 build a membrane-arm subcomplex that has no matrix arm attached, which places NDUFA9 at the junction that holds the two arms together. Patient fibroblasts show the same failure. Fully assembled complex I is reduced and Q-module subassemblies accumulate. In the most severely affected patient, P-module subassemblies accumulate as well, and in one 2025 patient a stalled, partially assembled intermediate was seen. Re-expressing wild-type NDUFA9 corrects assembly both in knockout cells and in patient cells.

Isolated Complex I Deficiency
Mechanism confidence: Established
The biochemical endpoint. All five patients tested had complex I deficiency in fibroblasts and/or muscle, and the deficiency is isolated: the other respiratory chain complexes are normal in both amount and activity. Residual activity ranges from about 2 percent of control in the fatal neonatal case to about 20 percent in the two adults with dystonia. That fits the report that clinical severity tracks the severity of the assembly defect, although one affected brother had normal fibroblast activity (see discussions). The module's ATP-synthesis consequence is inferred from loss of complex I and was not measured in these patients.

Striatal and Brainstem Energy Failure
Mechanism confidence: Established
The Leigh syndrome lesion pattern, and the substrate of the movement disorder. Every patient imaged had abnormal basal ganglia signal, always involving the putamen and caudate.
