Malignant Melanotic Nerve Sheath Tumor

Neoplastic MONDO:0002558 Pathograph 11 Show in embeddings browser nerve sheath neoplasm tumor of cranial and spinal nerves

Malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumour (MMNST) is a rare tumour of Schwann cells that make melanin. It arises most often from spinal or paraspinal nerve roots, and also from the sympathetic chain and the gastrointestinal tract, mostly in adults; a paraspinal tumour presents as a mass with pain or with signs of nerve root or spinal cord compression. Formerly called melanotic schwannoma, it was renamed and redefined as a potentially aggressive tumour in the 2020 WHO classification of soft tissue tumours and the 2021 WHO classification of central nervous system tumours, after case series and long-term data showed frequent metastasis and recurrence, and it is treated as a distinct tumour rather than a variant of schwannoma. In a series of 40, 35% of the patients with follow-up had a local recurrence and 44% metastases, and only a mitotic rate above 2 per 10 high-power fields correlated with metastasis. Some patients have the Carney complex, an autosomal dominant syndrome usually caused by germline PRKAR1A variants, and PRKAR1A loss of function is also reported in sporadic tumours; losing this regulatory subunit leaves protein kinase A unopposed. Complete resection is the accepted treatment, but there is no standard of care.

Inheritance

Autosomal dominant predisposition (Carney complex) HP:0000006
A 2022 case report's discussion states that the majority of tumours are sporadic. Others arise in people with the Carney complex, an autosomal dominant multiple neoplasia syndrome in which, according to GeneReviews, about 70% of those diagnosed have an affected parent and about 30% a de novo pathogenic variant, and in which a psammomatous melanotic schwannoma occurs in an estimated 10%. How often the complex is present varies widely with the series: 17 of 31 patients (55%) in a 1990 series of psammomatous tumours, 2 of the 40 patients in the 2014 series with known Carney complex, and 19 of the published patients (31.1%) in a 2024 systematic review; a 2025 case report's discussion cites over 50% in some series and under 5% in others.

Pathophysiology

5
PRKAR1A Loss of Function
A lesion often reported in this tumour: loss of function of PRKAR1A, which encodes the type 1A regulatory subunit of protein kinase A. A 2023 review states that somatic PRKAR1A loss-of-function mutations occur in both sporadic and Carney complex-associated tumours. How often it is found varies: in a 2026 conference abstract of 13 patients, 8 of the 11 sequenced cases had a PRKAR1A loss-of-function mutation, without saying whether germline or somatic; in the 2014 series PRKAR1A protein was lost in 7 of 20 tumours stained; and a 2025 case report's background notes that not all tumours show PRKAR1A loss and that sporadic tumours frequently retain its expression. In the Carney complex the predisposing lesion is usually a heterozygous germline PRKAR1A variant.

Adjuvant radiotherapy
Action: adjuvant radiotherapyNCI Thesaurus (NCIT) Relation: this treatment is this clinical intervention This treatment is adjuvant radiotherapy, annotated with Radiation Therapy (NCIT:C15313). NCIT:C15313 is a clinical intervention from the NCI Thesaurus. Ontology label: Radiation Therapy NCIT:C15313
Platform: Radiotherapy
Radiotherapy after resection, conventionally fractionated in most reports, given in reported cases after subtotal or gross total resection or after positive margins, and offered for aggressive histological features or significant residual tumour; a 2023 review says radiation therapy, with or without chemotherapy, may be considered for residual disease or unresectable tumours. Its potential benefit remains unclear, there are no formal guidelines and its use is controversial, and the available data are limited and come largely from case reports and small series. Of six patients irradiated after subtotal and three after gross total resection in a systematic review of 63 patients, four developed local recurrence and four metastatic disease, as a 2026 series reports it. The findings of a 2024 systematic review of 8 institutional and 63 published patients suggest a trend favouring gross total resection alone or subtotal resection with radiation therapy over subtotal resection alone, with similar mortality regardless. Single cases that the 2026 series and a 2021 case report cite in their discussions had no evidence of recurrence 16 months after a gross total resection that was followed by 55 Gy, and none after 2 years of follow-up when 60 Gy followed positive margins, while in a 2013 case report, local recurrence and lung metastatic dissemination appeared six months after 40 Gy given after total resection; in a 2020 series of 7, three patients were offered adjuvant radiotherapy and one showed a good clinical response.

Outcome
Local recurrence and metastasis are common, how often they are found depends on the series and on how long its patients were followed, and histology predicts them poorly. In the 2014 series of 40, the 26 patients with follow-up (mean 55 months) had local recurrences in 9 (35%) and metastases in 11 (which the abstract gives as 44%), and 7 died of the disease. Of the 12 of 31 patients followed in a 2023 single-centre series (mean 4.8 years), 6 were disease-free, 2 had recurrence or metastases and 4 had died of metastases; the other 19 were second-opinion cases. In a 2005 series of 13 patients with nonpsammomatous tumours, 7 of the 11 followed for over 2 years (mean 5.9 years) had no recurrence or metastasis. In a 1999 series of 5 spinal tumours followed for 3 to 7 years, 4 behaved malignantly and 3 patients died of metastases; in its review of 57 published cases, its own 5 among them, 15% recurred and 26.3% metastasized, and 53% of those followed for more than 5 years were free of disease against 67.5% of those followed for less (the abstract adds that 20 further cases had no follow-up). A 2020 systematic review found local recurrence or metastasis in 19 of the 37 published extramedullary spinal cases with information on metastasis, and calls follow-up across its source studies highly variable. A 2024 systematic review gives pooled rates of 42% for local recurrence, 27% for distant metastasis and 26% for mortality, and at its authors' institution 5 of 8 patients developed metastasis, 6 a long-term recurrence and 5 died. A 2025 case report's patient had no recurrence on MRI at 18 months and, in its discussion, none for 24 months after the gross total resection; its abstract counts the 18 months from the gross total resection, but its case description counts them from the first, partial operation five months earlier and calls that scan the most recent. Only a mitotic rate above 2 per 10 high-power fields correlated with metastasis in the 2014 series, and a 2022 case report's discussion, citing one study in which the same threshold correlated with metastases, adds that over half the tumours that eventually metastasized did not show increased mitoses; the primary tumours of the 1999 series showed no high mitotic count, atypia or necrosis although 4 of 5 behaved malignantly; and in a 2020 series of 7 cranial and spinal tumours, one of the two recurrences its abstract reports came from a tumour without mitoses. A 2018 review notes that strict criteria of malignancy are not well developed, although a combination of worrisome histological features, among them brisk mitotic activity and necrosis, raises concern for aggressive behaviour, and a 2025 case report's discussion says that apart from increased mitotic activity no morphological feature that reliably predicts behaviour has yet been identified. Recurrences have been documented up to 20 years after diagnosis, so long-term follow-up is recommended.

Conventional schwannoma MONDO:0002546
Overlapping Features A schwannoma without this tumour's melanocytic markers, Melan-A and HMB-45. The differential is difficult: in a 2010 series only 9 of 25 tumours first diagnosed as melanotic schwannoma kept that diagnosis on review, and 9 were reclassified as conventional schwannoma. Most of the malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumours in a 2026 cohort had DNA methylation profiles compatible with or suggestive of schwannoma; two matched the methylation class of this tumour with the Heidelberg classifier, and none did with the Bethesda classifier.
Distinguishing Features
  • Melanocytic markers and GFAP: a 2023 review says this tumour tends to stain for HMB-45 and Melan-A and to be negative for GFAP, which differentiates it from conventional schwannoma; a 2012 review reports GFAP in a subset of schwannomas. The 2010 series did not keep its nine reclassified tumours as melanotic schwannoma because both Melan-A and HMB-45 were negative.
  • Architecture: the nine reclassified tumours showed frequent Antoni A and B areas, nuclear palisading, hyalinized vessels and wavy nuclei. A 2022 case report's discussion says this tumour generally lacks Antoni A and B regions and hyalinized vessels, although in the 2010 series some tumours of this kind showed nuclear palisading and one had Antoni A and B areas with Verocay bodies.
  • Pigment: all nine reclassified tumours held some focal, fine Schmorl-positive pigment, which the 2010 series suggests is autophagosome-related, such as neuromelanin or lipofuscin, rather than melanin in true melanosomes, because Melan-A and HMB-45 were negative.
  • Gene expression: compared with schwannoma, this tumour showed downregulation of Schwann cell genes such as PMP22, PMP2 and MPZ and upregulation of genes related to melanin synthesis, as the 2022 case report's discussion reports it.
  • Site and age: this tumour tends to occur on posterior spinal nerves and ganglia, while schwannomas may also involve other nerves, including cranial nerves, and it peaks a decade younger, in the same discussion.

Meningeal melanocytoma MONDO:0016746
Overlapping Features Both tumours are neural crest-derived and generally positive for S100 and melanocytic markers such as Melan-A and HMB-45, so immunohistochemistry is often of little help, and their sites overlap: melanocytoma occurs especially at the cervical and thoracic spinal levels, in the posterior cranial fossa and in Meckel's cave, and this tumour frequently on cranial nerves, spinal nerve roots and the paraspinal sympathetic chain. Of the 25 tumours first diagnosed as melanotic schwannoma in the 2010 series, 4 were reclassified as melanocytoma and 2 could not be classified with certainty as either; in the 2026 cohort, integrating histological and molecular findings changed the diagnosis of two tumours classed histologically as this tumour to melanocytoma.
Distinguishing Features
  • Growth and contents: by the criteria the 2010 series used, this tumour is circumscribed, often encapsulated, and may contain psammoma bodies or fat, and in that series 8 of its 9 tumours held psammoma bodies and 1 large empty vacuoles resembling fat. By the same criteria, melanocytoma is circumscribed but not encapsulated and made of nests or fascicles of cells with little nuclear atypia. The series' discussion adds that psammoma bodies are generally absent in melanocytic tumours, although one psammomatous melanoma, arising in an intradermal nevus, has been described.
  • S100 and basement membrane: in the 2010 series S100 was diffuse and strong in all nine tumours of this kind but only focal in the melanocytomas. Stains for basement membrane (reticulin, collagen IV and laminin) outlined individual tumour cells in 4 of the 9 and showed a biphasic pattern, around single cells in some areas and around nests in others, in 5; in the melanocytomas they outlined nests and vessels but not single cells. On biopsy material the biphasic pattern might lead to the tumour being misread as melanocytic.
  • GNAQ: a GNAQ mutation supports a melanocytic lesion. In the 2010 series, with the authors' earlier study of leptomeningeal melanocytic lesions included, GNAQ mutations were 100% specific for those lesions against this tumour, with a sensitivity of 43%, and lack of a mutation does not rule a melanocytic lesion out. The 2010 series tested exon 5 of GNAQ; a 2022 case report's discussion notes that an important study comparing this tumour with melanocytic lesions sequenced only exon 5 of GNAQ, so that hotspot mutations at codons T96, Y101 and R183, in exons 2 and 4, could not have been detected in it.
  • Reported GNAQ and GNA11 mutations in tumours diagnosed as this one: a 2015 review cites a series of 14 such tumours in which one carried a GNAQ and one a GNA11 mutation, both with DNA methylation profiles that clustered with melanocytomas, which suggests they may have been melanocytomas; and a 2017 case report found a GNA11 mutation in a mesenteric tumour of this kind with some histological features of melanocytoma (a term its introduction uses for tumours mainly of the skin or mucosal surfaces). From that finding its authors conclude that a gene defect cannot be used as the sole factor to classify the different melanotic tumours.
  • DNA methylation: the 2026 cohort confirms that meningeal melanocytic tumours and this tumour are epigenetically distinct, but its discussion adds that the analysis may be limited by high melanin content, which precludes successful profiling in a substantial proportion of cases.
  • Mitotic activity: this tumour may show low mitotic activity and mimic a benign melanocytoma. In the 2026 cohort the mitotic index of its tumours of this kind was below 0.5 per mm2 in 6, between 0.5 and 1.4 in 5 and 1.5 or more in 1. These are the cut-offs that the WHO uses, with necrosis and CNS invasion, to grade meningeal melanocytic tumours, so mitotic index alone spans all three meningeal grades.

progression:
- phase: Outcome
  description: >-
    Local recurrence and metastasis are common, how often they are found depends on the series and
    on how long its patients were followed, and histology predicts them poorly. In the 2014 series
    of 40, the 26 patients with follow-up (mean 55 months) had local recurrences in 9 (35%) and
    metastases in 11 (which the abstract gives as 44%), and 7 died of the disease. Of the 12 of
    31 patients followed in a 2023 single-centre series (mean 4.8 years), 6 were disease-free, 2 had
    recurrence or metastases and 4 had died of metastases; the other 19 were second-opinion cases.
    In a 2005 series of 13 patients with nonpsammomatous tumours, 7 of the 11 followed for over
    2 years (mean 5.9 years) had no recurrence or metastasis. In a 1999 series of 5 spinal tumours
    followed for 3 to 7 years, 4 behaved malignantly and 3 patients died of metastases; in its
    review of 57 published cases, its own 5 among them, 15% recurred and 26.3% metastasized, and
    53% of those followed for more than 5 years were free of disease against 67.5% of those followed
