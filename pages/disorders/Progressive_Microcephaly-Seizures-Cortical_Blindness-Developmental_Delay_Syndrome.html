Progressive Microcephaly-Seizures-Cortical Blindness-Developmental Delay Syndrome

Mendelian MONDO:0014714 Pathograph 25 Show in embeddings browser Mendelian neurodevelopmental disorder Inborn error of immunity

An ultra-rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in DIAPH1, which encodes the Rho-effector formin mDia1. mDia1 nucleates and processively elongates long linear actin filaments on activation by GTP-bound Rho and coordinates the microtubule cytoskeleton with them, so its loss removes a single cytoskeletal function on which cell polarity, mitotic spindle formation and microtubule-organizing-center positioning all depend. The clinical core is early-infantile progressive postnatal microcephaly, epilepsy, cortical visual impairment or blindness and severe developmental delay, frequently with occipital-predominant structural brain abnormalities. Beyond the brain, the same lesion produces a combined immune deficiency in which lymphocyte subsets and immunoglobulins are often normal while T-cell receptor excision circles and naive T-lymphocyte counts are consistently low, and patient T cells show impaired adhesion and inefficient microtubule-organizing-center repositioning to the immunologic synapse. Reduced mitochondrial respiratory-chain complex IV and B-cell lymphoma in a subset of patients are documented in the Finnish and Omani cohort. The disorder must be kept apart from the allelic dominant gain-of-function DIAPH1 entity, which causes sensorineural hearing loss with macrothrombocytopenia and no neurodevelopmental phenotype.

Why does loss of the orthologous gene leave mouse brain size grossly normal when biallelic DIAPH1 loss causes progressive microcephaly in humans, and which model can therefore be used to study the cortical phenotype?
HUMAN MODEL MISMATCH scbms_mouse_lacks_microcephaly
Attached to
pathophysiology#Impaired Neural Precursor Division and Cortical Neuron Output animal_models#Mouse
This is a mismatch of the kind where evidence exists in a model and its translational validity is the open question, not one where evidence is absent. The mouse knockout exists, has been examined histologically, and reproduces the immune phenotype; what it does not reproduce is the microcephaly, the seizures or the cortical blindness, which are the features that define the human disease and give it its name. ClinGen's own curation records this discrepancy explicitly. The consequence for curation is that the chain from the demonstrated human-neural-precursor spindle defect to the cortical phenotype rests on an inference across a gap no current mammalian model spans, which is why that node is marked provisional. Mammalian formin redundancy with mDia2 and mDia3, and species differences in cortical neurogenesis, are the candidate explanations named in the literature and neither has been tested. The Xenopus knockout reproduces growth and eye defects and the zebrafish knockout shows provoked seizure-like behaviour without malformation, but neither animal has a six-layered neocortex, so neither can model occipital-predominant cortical malformation either.

Biallelic DIAPH1 Loss of Function
Mechanism confidence: Established
Homozygous or compound heterozygous nonsense, frameshift and canonical splice-site DIAPH1 variants remove functional mDia1. ClinGen records loss of function as the mechanism of pathogenicity for this entity, in explicit contrast to the gain-of-function mechanism of the dominant DIAPH1 hearing-loss arm. A heterozygous truncating allele that removes the FH2 domain abolishes actin-elongation activity in vitro, which is the direct biochemical demonstration that truncation destroys the protein's catalytic output.

Impaired Neural Precursor Division and Cortical Neuron Output
Mechanism confidence: Provisional
mDia1 is expressed in human neuronal precursor cells during mitotic division and is required for normal spindle formation, so its loss distorts progenitor division during corticogenesis and lowers cortical neuron output. The resulting microcephaly is postnatal and progressive rather than congenital, and the structural abnormalities reported across the largest cohort are frequently occipital-predominant, which is consistent with the cortical visual impairment that accompanies them. The step from a demonstrated human-precursor spindle defect to the cortical phenotype is an inference: no human neurodevelopmental tissue or organoid study of DIAPH1 loss is cited in the sources used here, and the mouse knockout does not reproduce the microcephaly.

Defective Lymphocyte Maturation and Activation
Mechanism confidence: Established
The same adhesion and microtubule-organizing-center defect in lymphocytes produces poor lymphocyte activation and proliferation, defective B-cell maturation and a lack of naive T cells. CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of DIAPH1 in healthy-donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells reproduces the T-cell activation defect, which makes this the most directly demonstrated branch of the mechanism. In the largest cohort the gross lymphocyte subsets and serum immunoglobulins were usually normal while T-cell receptor excision circles and naive T-lymphocyte counts were consistently low, so the laboratory signature is thymic output rather than lymphopenia.

Overlapping Features Congenital cytomegalovirus and the other TORCH infections produce microcephaly, seizures and visual impairment, and are the main acquired mimic to exclude before pursuing a Mendelian diagnosis in an infant with this presentation.

discussions:
- discussion_id: scbms_mouse_lacks_microcephaly
  kind: HUMAN_MODEL_MISMATCH
  attaches_to:
  - pathophysiology#Impaired Neural Precursor Division and Cortical Neuron Output
  - animal_models#Mouse
  prompt: >-
    Why does loss of the orthologous gene leave mouse brain size grossly normal when biallelic
    DIAPH1 loss causes progressive microcephaly in humans, and which model can therefore be used
    to study the cortical phenotype?
  rationale: >-
    This is a mismatch of the kind where evidence exists in a model and its translational validity
    is the open question, not one where evidence is absent. The mouse knockout exists, has been
    examined histologically, and reproduces the immune phenotype; what it does not reproduce is
    the microcephaly, the seizures or the cortical blindness, which are the features that define
    the human disease and give it its name. ClinGen's own curation records this discrepancy
    explicitly. The consequence for curation is that the chain from the demonstrated
    human-neural-precursor spindle defect to the cortical phenotype rests on an inference across a
    gap no current mammalian model spans, which is why that node is marked provisional.
    Mammalian formin redundancy with mDia2 and mDia3, and species differences in cortical
    neurogenesis, are the candidate explanations named in the literature and neither has been
    tested. The Xenopus knockout reproduces growth and eye defects and the zebrafish knockout
    shows provoked seizure-like behaviour without malformation, but neither animal has a
    six-layered neocortex, so neither can model occipital-predominant cortical malformation
    either.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — Biallelic LOF of DIAPH1 causes SCBMS (autosomal recessive)

SCBMS (MONDO:0014714; OMIM 616632) is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in DIAPH1, which encodes the formin mDia1. Multiple independent cohorts converge on this conclusion:

  • Ercan-Sencicek et al., 2015 (PMID: 24781755) — founding study; identified a homozygous nonsense variant p.Gln778* by linkage plus whole-exome sequencing (WES) in a consanguineous family with microcephaly, severe visual impairment, intellectual disability, and short stature.
  • Kaustio et al., 2021 (PMID: 33662367) — 7 patients; 5 Finnish homozygous for the splice variant c.684+1G>A, 2 Omani homozygous for the frameshift c.2769delT / p.F923fs.
  • Esmaeilzadeh et al., 2024 (PMID: 39578953) — novel homozygous nonsense c.1285C>T / p.Gln429* plus review of 20 prior cases; recurrent variants c.2769del, c.684+1G>A, c.2332C>T account for ~72% of cases.
  • Galassi Deforie et al., 2026 (PMID: 41860019) — largest cohort, 53 individuals (33 newly reported).

"Homozygous loss of DIAPH1 results in seizures, cortical blindness, and microcephaly syndrome (SCBMS)." — PMID: 33662367

"Biallelic DIAPH1 pathogenic variants cause a neurodevelopmental syndrome occasionally associated with immunodeficiency." — PMID: 41860019

The inheritance is autosomal recessive, with nonsense, frameshift, and splice LOF variants. This contrasts sharply with the dominant gain-of-function DIAPH1 variants that cause DFNA1 / macrothrombocytopenia:

"Dominant gain-of-function DIAPH1 variants cause macrothrombocytopenia and sensorineural deafness (autosomal dominant non-syndromic hearing loss 1 (DFNA1)), while homozygous loss of DIAPH1 results in seizures, cortical blindness, and microcephaly syndrome (SCBMS)." — PMID: 38915998

Key identifiers: MONDO:0014714 · OMIM:616632 · gene DIAPH1 (HGNC:2876, chromosome 5q31.3). Synonyms: seizures, cortical blindness, microcephaly syndrome; SCBMS. Information is derived from aggregated disease-level resources (OMIM/Orphanet) and from individual-patient case series/cohorts in the primary literature.

Finding 2 — Core clinical phenotype: progressive microcephaly, epilepsy, cortical visual impairment, developmental delay, early infantile onset

The defining tetrad of SCBMS is progressive (postnatal) microcephaly, epilepsy, cortical visual impairment/blindness, and severe developmental delay/intellectual disability, with onset in early infancy.

"Clinical features included developmental delay, intellectual disability, progressive microcephaly, cortical visual impairment or blindness, epilepsy, and frequent occipital-predominant brain abnormalities." — PMID: 41860019 (n=53)

"The mean age of onset for patients in the group of 20 patients with a known age of onset was 2.3 months (SD = 1.6)." — PMID: 39578953

Nearly half of patients in the large cohort had infections (mainly respiratory, related to epilepsy/aspiration). The 2021 cohort additionally documented mitochondrial dysfunction and immunodeficiency in the same patients (PMID: 33662367). The phenotype is progressive (microcephaly worsens postnatally) and severe, with substantial quality-of-life impact: profound disability, dependence for daily activities, visual loss, and seizure burden.

Suggested HPO terms: Progressive microcephaly (HP:0000253), Seizure (HP:0001250), Cortical visual impairment (HP:0100704), Global developmental delay (HP:0001263), Intellectual disability (HP:0001249), Cerebral atrophy (HP:0002059), Cerebellar atrophy (HP:0001272), Recurrent respiratory infections (HP:0002205), Short stature (HP:0004322).

Finding 3 — Multisystem signature: combined immunodeficiency, mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA-repair defect, and B-cell lymphoma risk

DIAPH1 loss extends well beyond the brain. Kaustio et al. (PMID: 33662367) found poor lymphocyte activation/proliferation, defective B-cell maturation, absence of naïve T cells, and mitochondrial dysfunction; critically, CRISPR DIAPH1 knockout in healthy-donor PBMCs replicated the T-cell activation defect, and patient T cells showed impaired adhesion and inefficient MTOC repositioning. Three of seven patients developed B-cell lymphoma.

"the patients presented with infection susceptibility due to defective lymphocyte maturation and 3 patients developed B-cell lymphoma" — PMID: 33662367

Azizoglu et al., 2024 (PMID: 39120629) reported six patients with novel LOF variants and major T, NK, and ILC defects:

"Loss of function mutations in Diaphanous related formin 1 (DIAPH1) are associated with seizures, cortical blindness, and microcephaly syndrome (SCBMS) and are recently linked to combined immunodeficiency." — PMID: 39120629

The large 2026 cohort confirmed a consistent immunological laboratory signature even when gross subsets looked normal:

"T cell receptor excision circles and naïve T-lymphocyte counts were consistently low" — PMID: 41860019

Finding 4 — Mechanism: mDia1 is a RhoA-effector formin building linear actin and stabilizing microtubules

DIAPH1/mDia1 is a Diaphanous-related formin and a canonical downstream effector of the small GTPase RhoA. When RhoA-GTP binds mDia1, it relieves the protein's N-terminal (DID)–C-terminal (DAD) autoinhibition, activating the FH2 domain to nucleate and processively elongate long, linear, unbranched actin filaments that resist myosin-driven contraction; mDia1 also coordinates and stabilizes microtubules.

"mDia1 generates linear actin filaments in the maintenance of polarity during adhesion, migration, and division in immune cells and neuroepithelial cells, and in driving tangential migration of cortical interneurons in the rodent" — PMID: 24781755

Supporting mechanistic cell biology: - mDia1 regulates microtubule stability and dynamics as a Rho effector coordinating actin and MT cytoskeletons (PMID: 25298036). - The Memo–RhoA–mDia1 axis controls MT dynamics, adhesion-site formation, and migration (PMID: 18955552). - mDia-family formins assemble long linear filaments capable of withstanding myosin-driven contractile load; combined formin loss causes reduced cortical rigidity, excessive blebbing, and delayed cytokinesis (PMID: 42827260). - Truncation of the FH2 domain (p.R1049X) abolishes F-actin elongation activity and impairs cell–cell junction formation and mitosis (PMID: 35060117).

Because mDia1 protein is expressed in human neuronal precursor cells during mitotic division, loss impairs the mitosis, polarity, and migration of neural precursors and cortical interneurons — the cell-biological root of microcephaly and cortical malformation.

Suggested GO BP terms: actin filament polymerization (GO:0030041), actin nucleation (GO:0045010), regulation of microtubule cytoskeleton organization (GO:0070507), neuron migration (GO:0001764), mitotic cell cycle (GO:0000278), establishment of cell polarity (GO:0030010).

Finding 6 — Epidemiology and inheritance: ultra-rare, consanguinity-enriched, with a Finnish founder allele

SCBMS is ultra-rare. Only ~20 cases across 7 studies were on record by 2024 (PMID: 39578953), expanding to 53 individuals (33 new) by 2026 (PMID: 41860019). No formal prevalence/incidence figure is published; the condition sits far below 1/100,000.

Inheritance is autosomal recessive, frequently homozygous in consanguineous pedigrees (Ercan-Sencicek 2015, Esmaeilzadeh 2024). A Finnish founder splice variant drives a population cluster:

"all Finnish patients homozygous for a rare DIAPH1 splice-variant (NM_005219:c.684+1G>A) enriched in the Finnish population" — PMID: 33662367

"Twenty cases from seven studies were found after a review of the literature." — PMID: 39578953

"A total of 53 individuals with biallelic DIAPH1 variants, including 33 previously unreported patients, were studied." — PMID: 41860019

Reported populations include Middle Eastern (Omani, Saudi, Iranian, Egyptian), Finnish, and others. Recurrent variants c.2769del, c.684+1G>A, c.2332C>T account for ~72% of cases. Penetrance appears high/complete for biallelic LOF; expressivity is variable (notably in immunological severity). Consanguinity is a major risk-amplifying factor; no sex bias is reported (autosomal recessive).

Finding 7 — Diagnostics, management, and prognosis

Diagnosis is molecular. The disorder was discovered by, and is diagnosed with, whole-exome/whole-genome sequencing (often aided by linkage/homozygosity mapping in consanguineous families), confirmed by Sanger sequencing:

"exome sequencing was performed to identify the genetic basis of the clinical presentation in an Iranian 7-year-old boy. Validation of the detected variant was done by Sanger sequencing." — PMID: 39578953

Supportive workup includes brain MRI (progressive microcephaly, occipital-predominant abnormalities, cerebral/cerebellar atrophy), EEG (epilepsy), ophthalmologic/visual assessment (cortical visual impairment), and immunological evaluation (lymphocyte subsets, TRECs, naïve T cells, immunoglobulins) (PMID: 41860019, PMID: 33662367, PMID: 39120629).

Differential diagnosis includes other primary/progressive microcephaly syndromes (e.g., MCPH genes such as ASPM, WDR62, CASK-related microcephaly), congenital infections (TORCH), and other combined-immunodeficiency-with-neurodevelopmental syndromes; the occipital-predominant imaging, cortical blindness, and combined immunodeficiency with low TRECs/naïve T cells point toward DIAPH1.

No curative or disease-specific therapy exists. Management is supportive and symptomatic: antiseizure medications for epilepsy, developmental/rehabilitative therapies (PT/OT/speech), visual support, nutritional support (aspiration risk), and prophylaxis/treatment of infections. Immunological surveillance and oncologic vigilance (lymphoma) are warranted. Genetic counseling (recessive recurrence risk 25%; carrier testing; prenatal/preimplantation options) is central to prevention.

Prognosis is guarded. Severe neurodevelopmental disability, early onset (~2.3 months), respiratory infection susceptibility, and lymphoma predisposition dominate the clinical course:

"The DNA repair defect may influence susceptibility to infection, lymphoma, or treatment-related toxicity." — PMID: 41860019

Suggested NCIT intervention terms: Anticonvulsant Agent (C264), Physical Therapy (C15351), Occupational Therapy (C15214), Nutritional Support (C15229), Supportive Care (C15226).

Finding 8 — Synthesis: a unified RhoA–mDia1 cytoskeletopathy

Integrating five primary clinical studies (PMIDs 24781755, 33662367, 39120629, 39578953, 41860019) with mechanistic cell biology (PMIDs 25298036, 18955552, 42827260), every SCBMS feature traces to loss of a single cytoskeletal function.

"DIAPH1 is critical for neurodevelopment, immune regulation, and DNA repair." — PMID: 41860019

"mDia1 generates linear actin filaments in the maintenance of polarity during adhesion, migration, and division in immune cells and neuroepithelial cells..." — PMID: 24781755

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

Ordered causal chain (initiating lesion → clinical manifestation)

  1. Biallelic LOF variant in DIAPH1 (nonsense/frameshift/splice; e.g., p.Gln778*, c.684+1G>A, p.F923fs) leads to absent or non-functional mDia1 formin protein (loss of function). [demonstrated — multiple cohorts]
  2. Loss of mDia1 results in failure to relieve RhoA-GTP–dependent formin activation, hence loss of processive elongation of long, linear, unbranched actin filaments and loss of coordinated microtubule stabilization. [demonstrated in vitro — PMID 24781755, 25298036, 35060117, 42827260]
  3. Defective actin/MT architecture impairs cell polarity, mitotic spindle orientation, cytokinesis, cell adhesion/migration, and MTOC repositioning. [demonstrated — patient T cells, CRISPR PBMC; inferred for neural cells]
  4. Branch A — Neural: In neuroepithelial precursors and tangentially migrating cortical interneurons, impaired division and migration lead to reduced neuron production and faulty cortical wiring → progressive postnatal microcephaly, epilepsy, cortical visual impairment, developmental delay. Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Small denominator. Even the largest cohort is 53 patients; no validated prevalence/incidence, penetrance quantification, or natural-history survival curves exist. Phenotype frequencies are approximate.
  2. Mechanistic inference in the brain. The neural branch of the causal chain is inferred from mDia1 cell biology and rodent interneuron-migration data plus the human phenotype; direct human neural-cell (e.g., iPSC-derived organoid) demonstration of the microcephaly mechanism is limited.
  3. Model–human discordance. The mouse Diap1 KO does not reproduce microcephaly, so no fully faithful mammalian genetic model is yet available; Xenopus and zebrafish capture only subsets of features.
  4. Genotype–phenotype correlation. Whether specific variants (hypomorphic splice vs complete null) predict immune vs lymphoma vs severity remains unresolved.
  5. Immune/lymphoma quantification. Lymphoma risk is derived from a single small cohort (3/7); the true lifetime risk and whether all patients require oncologic surveillance are unknown.
  6. DNA-repair mechanism. The nature of the DNA-repair defect and its molecular link to the actin/MT cytoskeleton is asserted but mechanistically underspecified.
  7. No therapeutics. There are no disease-modifying treatments, trials, or validated biomarkers of progression. Sections on gene therapy, immunization, infectious agents, and veterinary/natural disease are not applicable / not reported for this disorder.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Patient-derived cerebral organoids / iPSC neural models to directly test the neural branch (progenitor division, spindle orientation, interneuron migration) and close the inference gap in the causal chain.
  2. Conditional / double-formin mouse models (e.g., Diaph1 with Diaph3 or neural-specific conditional KO) to overcome redundancy and recapitulate microcephaly.
  3. Genotype–phenotype registry across all 53+ patients with standardized MRI, EEG, immune panels (TRECs, naïve T, Ig), and oncologic outcomes to define variant-specific severity and lymphoma risk.
  4. Prospective immunologic and oncologic surveillance protocol (lymphocyte subsets, TRECs, imaging) given the documented combined immunodeficiency and B-cell lymphoma predisposition; evaluate whether IVIG/antimicrobial prophylaxis improves outcomes.
  5. Molecular dissection of the DNA-repair defect (e.g., γH2AX dynamics, repair-pathway assays) in patient cells to clarify the genome-stability branch and its link to the cytoskeleton.
  6. Natural-history study to establish progression rate of microcephaly, epilepsy severity, survival, and causes of mortality (infection/aspiration vs lymphoma).
  7. Carrier-screening consideration in the Finnish population (founder c.684+1G>A) and consanguineous communities, with genetic counseling and prenatal/preimplantation options for at-risk families.
