Glycogen Storage Disease Type VI

Mendelian MONDO:0009294 Pathograph 11 Show in embeddings browser Glycogen Storage Disease

Glycogen storage disease type VI (Hers disease) is an autosomal recessive disorder of hepatic glycogen metabolism caused by biallelic PYGL variants producing deficiency of liver glycogen phosphorylase, the rate-limiting enzyme of glycogenolysis. The block is narrow and its narrowness is diagnostically load-bearing: glycogen synthesis, gluconeogenesis and fatty-acid oxidation are all intact, so glycogen accumulates in hepatocytes while the fasting response remains partly compensated. Children present with hepatomegaly and poor growth, with elevated transaminases, hypertriglyceridaemia, fasting hypoglycaemia and postprandial hyperlactataemia. Because fatty-acid oxidation is preserved, the hypoglycaemia is characteristically KETOTIC - the positive finding that separates GSD VI from the non-ketotic hypoglycaemia of GSD I, where gluconeogenesis is also blocked. GSD VI cannot be distinguished from GSD IX (phosphorylase kinase deficiency) on clinical or laboratory grounds; the two are managed under a single joint guideline and separated only by enzymatic or genetic testing. The disorder is often described as benign, and this entry deliberately does not repeat that: in the largest systematic review, liver fibrosis was present in about a third of biopsied patients and early cirrhosis in about a tenth.

Hepatic Glycogen Phosphorylase Deficiency
Biallelic PYGL loss of function removes the liver isoform of glycogen phosphorylase, the rate-limiting enzyme of glycogenolysis, which cleaves the alpha-1,4 bonds of glycogen to release glucose-1-phosphate. The enzyme is the substrate enzyme of the hepatic phosphorylase system; its activating kinase, phosphorylase kinase, is a distinct protein whose deficiency causes the separate disease GSD IX.

Impaired Hepatic Glycogenolysis with Intact Synthesis and Gluconeogenesis
This node is defined as much by what remains intact as by what fails. Glycogen breakdown is blocked, but glycogen synthesis, gluconeogenesis and fatty-acid oxidation are all preserved. That combination produces the two signatures of the disease: glycogen accumulates because synthesis continues unopposed, and fasting hypoglycaemia is comparatively mild and ketotic because gluconeogenesis can be upregulated and ketogenesis is available. The negative content of this node is the whole contrast with GSD I, where glucose-6-phosphatase failure blocks gluconeogenesis too and the hypoglycaemia is severe and non-ketotic.

Hepatic Fibrosis and Cirrhosis
This is the node that contradicts the disease's reputation. Across 37 biopsies in the largest systematic review, liver fibrosis was present in 32.4% and early cirrhosis in 10.8%; a Chinese series independently found regenerative nodules in four of ten patients. Note the ascertainment caveat: these are proportions of BIOPSIED patients, not of all patients, and biopsy is not performed at random. description: >-
  Glycogen storage disease type VI (Hers disease) is an autosomal recessive
  disorder of hepatic glycogen metabolism caused by biallelic PYGL variants
  producing deficiency of liver glycogen phosphorylase, the rate-limiting enzyme
  of glycogenolysis. The block is narrow and its narrowness is diagnostically
  load-bearing: glycogen synthesis, gluconeogenesis and fatty-acid oxidation are
  all intact, so glycogen accumulates in hepatocytes while the fasting response
  remains partly compensated. Children present with hepatomegaly and poor
  growth, with elevated transaminases, hypertriglyceridaemia, fasting
  hypoglycaemia and postprandial hyperlactataemia. Because fatty-acid oxidation
  is preserved, the hypoglycaemia is characteristically KETOTIC - the positive
  finding that separates GSD VI from the non-ketotic hypoglycaemia of GSD I,
  where gluconeogenesis is also blocked. GSD VI cannot be distinguished from GSD
  IX (phosphorylase kinase deficiency) on clinical or laboratory grounds; the
  two are managed under a single joint guideline and separated only by enzymatic
  or genetic testing. The disorder is often described as benign, and this entry
  deliberately does not repeat that: in the largest systematic review, liver
  fibrosis was present in about a third of biopsied patients and early cirrhosis
  in about a tenth.
