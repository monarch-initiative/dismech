Intellectual Developmental Disorder With Autism And Dysmorphic Facies

Mendelian MONDO:0859281 Pathograph 17 Show in embeddings browser Neurodevelopmental disorder Syndromic intellectual disability

An autosomal recessive syndromic intellectual disability caused by homozygous premature termination codons in PDZD8, an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) transmembrane lipid transfer protein that tethers the ER to late endosomes/lysosomes and to mitochondria. Six affected individuals from three consanguineous families (Omani, Emirati and Afghan) have been reported, homozygous for p.(Q30*), p.(Y298*) or p.(S733*). All six have developmental delay, intellectual disability (severe in four), autistic behaviour (autism spectrum disorder diagnosed in five) and facial dysmorphism, with orbital hypertelorism, malar flattening, a myopathic or hypotonic face and an open mouth. Mild muscle weakness, epilepsy, myopia, marfanoid habitus or short stature, scoliosis and ADHD occur in some. Pdzd8 mutant mice show reduced preweaning viability, repetitive jumping, social recognition deficits, impaired long-term spatial memory and reduced hippocampal long-term potentiation, and fly knockdown impairs long-term memory. Cellular studies place PDZD8 at ER-endolysosome contacts, where its lipid transfer promotes endosome maturation, lipophagy and neurite outgrowth, and at ER-mitochondria contacts, where it couples synaptic ER calcium release to mitochondrial uptake. Management is supportive.

Pathophysiology

6
PDZD8 Biallelic Loss of Function
Both PDZD8 alleles carry a premature termination codon (p.Q30* in exon 1, p.Y298* in exon 2, p.S733* in the last exon). Any protein escaping nonsense-mediated decay would keep the ER transmembrane anchor but lack the C-terminal regions that bind protrudin, late-endosome/lysosome phospholipids and Rab7, so no functional full-length protein is expected. PDZD8's SMP domain binds and transfers glycerophospholipids and ceramides between membranes, and the protein tethers the ER to late endosomes/lysosomes and to mitochondria. These activities are the gene's established functions; they were not measured on the patient alleles.

Impaired Endolysosome Maturation
Without PDZD8-mediated lipid supply at ER-late endosome/lysosome contacts, endosome maturation fails. PDZD8- or protrudin-depleted mouse neurons form abnormal large vacuoles, and in Drosophila PDZD8 is needed for lysosome maturation and autophagic flux at these contacts.

1. Disease information

Overview. IDDADF is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive form of intellectual disability that comes with other features (a "syndromic" ID). It is caused by homozygous premature termination codons (PTCs) in PDZD8. PDZD8 is a protein embedded in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane that transfers lipids between membranes and helps hold organelles together at contact sites.

"Intellectual developmental disorder with autism and dysmorphic facies (IDDADF; OMIM #620021) is a very rare syndromic ID caused by homozygous premature termination codons (PTCs) in PDZD8, encoding PDZ domain-containing protein 8 (PDZD8)" — PMID:40016860

2. Etiology

  • Cause. Biallelic (homozygous) loss-of-function PTC variants in PDZD8. In every family the variant was found by homozygosity mapping plus exome sequencing, and it segregated with the disease.
  • Genetic risk factors.
  • Parental consanguinity: 6/6 cases, all children of first-cousin parents.
  • Ancestry: Omani, Emirati and Afghan.
  • Possible second variant in family A. Family A is also homozygous for ANKRD2 c.982C>T p.(R328W). The authors think this variant is unlikely to cause the ID, but it "may contribute to myopathy in family A" (PMID:35227461). This matters when scoring the muscle phenotype.
  • Modifier, protective, environmental and gene-environment factors. None reported.

6. Mechanism and pathophysiology

Causal chain

  1. Biallelic PTC in PDZD8. This leads to loss of full-length PDZD8, through NMD or truncation.
  2. Demonstrated in mouse: full-length protein is absent in Pdzd8^tm1b^ brain (PMID:35227461).
  3. Inferred in humans: PDZD8 protein cannot be detected in blood.
  4. Loss of PDZD8 at ER contact sites. This impairs two things:
  5. (a) Lipid transfer from the ER to late endosomes and lysosomes, and therefore endosome maturation (branch A).
  6. (b) ER–mitochondria tethering, and therefore mitochondrial Ca²⁺ uptake after synaptically triggered Ca²⁺ release from the ER (branch B).
  7. Both roles come from cell-biology work cited in the clinical papers. Their role in the human disease is inferred.
  8. Branch A. Defective endosomal homeostasis leads to abnormal vacuoles and impaired neuronal integrity.
  9. Shown in cultured neurons; not studied in patients.
  10. Branch B. Mitochondria take up less Ca²⁺, which leads to higher cytosolic Ca²⁺ in dendrites and likely impairs synaptic Ca²⁺ buffering and ATP supply.
  11. The ATP effect is the authors' hypothesis (PMID:35227461, Discussion).
  12. Synaptic dysfunction. In mouse hippocampus there is reduced saturated LTP (long-term potentiation, the strengthening of synapses with use) after 3× theta-burst stimulation, lower paired-pulse facilitation, and increased dendritic spine density.
  13. These lead to long-term memory deficits in mouse (Barnes maze) and fly (48-hour courtship memory).
  14. Source: model organisms, PMID:35227461 and PMID:40016860.
  15. Altered brain development. In mouse, total brain volume is smaller; relative corpus callosum volume is lower and relative hippocampal volume higher; the accessory olfactory bulb and primary olfactory cortex are larger; the cerebellar nuclei are smaller; and adult neurogenesis is reduced.
  16. The mouse corpus callosum change mirrors the splenium hypoplasia in patient A.IV.5.
  17. Clinical outcome. Steps 5 and 6 lead to ID, autism/ASD, and impaired social recognition. The mouse shows social-recognition and social-odor-discrimination deficits and repetitive hindlimb jumping (a stereotypy).
  18. The authors frame the disorder as a synaptopathy (a disease of synapse function).
  19. The step from the mouse to patients is inferred.
  20. Parallel systemic branch. Loss of PDZD8 affects cell metabolism. The paper notes that AMPK phosphorylates PDZD8 at T527, which is needed for glutaminolysis during low glucose. In mice this leads to higher metabolic rate, low body weight, lower plasma triglyceride in males, and smaller body size.
  21. This may relate to the short stature in family C, but the link is inferred.
  22. Stress and organelle readouts. Mouse brain shows more ER-stress and mitochondrial-fusion transcripts (Atf4, Hspa5, Mfn1/2, Opa1 were measured).
  23. Measured at the mRNA level only, which the authors list as a limitation.
  24. The white-matter lesions in family C are unexplained. PDZD8 is expressed in oligodendrocytes, but no mechanism is shown.

"In neurons, depletion of PDZD8 has been shown to impair endosomal homeostasis (13), decrease the proximity of the ER and mitochondria (14), and decrease calcium ion (Ca2+) uptake by mitochondria following synaptic transmission–induced Ca2+ release from the ER (15)." — PMID:35227461

"the cognitive impairment associated with PDZD8 disruption is therefore likely to represent a synaptopathy resulting from synaptic dysfunction" — PMID:35227461

8. Temporal development

  • Onset: congenital or infantile. Developmental delay is present in all patients, who were assessed between ages 4 and 30.
  • Course: a fixed neurodevelopmental disorder. No regression has been reported.
  • Possible progressive features (open question): scoliosis was worse at age 18 than at 13 in C.IV.1. The demyelinating lesions in C.IV.1 raise the possibility of a progressive white-matter process. With no natural-history data, this remains unanswered.
  • Critical periods: PDZD8 expression is stable from 8 weeks after conception to age 23 (BrainSpan data), which suggests a role throughout neurodevelopment (PMID:35227461).

9. Inheritance and population

  • Inheritance: autosomal recessive. Penetrance in homozygotes is 6/6 within the three families.
  • Expressivity: variable. ID ranges from moderate to severe, epilepsy is present in 3/6, and the non-core features differ between families.
  • Prevalence: unknown, with 6 published cases in 3 families. Suggested prevalence_class: CASES_IN_LITERATURE with ULTRA_RARE.
  • Population factors: no founder variant is known; each family has its own variant. All families are consanguineous, from the Arabian Peninsula and Afghanistan.
  • Sex: 4 male, 2 female.
  • Not reported: anticipation, germline mosaicism, carrier frequency.

10. Diagnostics

  • Diagnosis is genetic: exome sequencing, combined with homozygosity mapping in consanguineous families. Confirmation and segregation testing use Sanger sequencing. Exome CNV analysis (ExomeDepth) excluded deletions in family C.
  • Clinical evaluation:
  • IQ: Stanford-Binet
  • Adaptive behavior: Vineland-II, PEP-3
  • Autism: CARS2 and ADOS-2 against DSM-5 criteria
  • Brain MRI
  • EEG where seizures occur
  • Spine radiographs
  • Ophthalmologic exam
  • Biomarkers: none. PDZD8 protein is not detectable in blood.
  • Differential diagnosis:
  • Other autosomal recessive syndromic ID/ASD genes in consanguineous populations
  • Congenital myopathies (myopathic face)
  • Distal 10q deletion syndrome
  • Disorders of the PDZD8 partners: ZFYVE27/protrudin (spastic paraplegia) and RAB7A (CMT2B)
  • Screening: no newborn screening. Carrier testing and cascade testing are possible in families with a known variant.

11. Outcome and prognosis

  • Survival and life expectancy: no data. The oldest reported patient is 30 years old.
  • Pregnancy losses: family B had two in-utero deaths, but it is unknown whether they relate to PDZD8. In mice, survival to weaning was 36–48% lower than expected.
  • Morbidity: lifelong ID; severe ASD in family C; epilepsy; limited mobility (walking distance) in family C.
  • Prognostic factors: none established.
