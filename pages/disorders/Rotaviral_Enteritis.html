Rotaviral Enteritis

Infectious MONDO:0005194 Pathograph 20 Show in embeddings browser Infectious Disease Gastrointestinal Disease

Rotaviral enteritis is an acute viral gastroenteritis caused primarily by Group A rotavirus (Reoviridae), a triple-layered non-enveloped double-stranded RNA virus that infects mature enterocytes at the tips of small intestinal villi. Disease is driven by a dual mechanism combining NSP4 enterotoxin-mediated intracellular calcium mobilization, chloride secretion, and enteric nervous system activation (early secretory diarrhea) with proteasomal DGAT1 degradation, brush-border disaccharidase loss, and villus blunting (secondary malabsorptive osmotic diarrhea). Host histo-blood group antigen (HBGA) secretor status controlled by FUT2 and Lewis status controlled by FUT3 govern VP8*-mediated attachment and strain-specific susceptibility to both natural infection and live-attenuated oral rotavirus vaccines.

Host Glycan Attachment and Mature Villus Enterocyte Entry
Following luminal trypsin cleavage of the outer capsid spike protein VP4 into the distal lectin domain VP8* and membrane-penetration domain VP5*, rotavirus attaches to mature non-dividing enterocytes in the middle and tip of small intestinal villi and to enteroendocrine cells. VP8* binds host cell-surface sialoglycans and histo-blood group antigens (HBGAs) synthesized on the intestinal mucosa under the control of FUT2 (secretor status) in a P-genotype-specific manner (with predominant human P[8] and P[4] strains preferentially binding secretor HBGAs), followed by endocytic entry into the enterocyte cytoplasm.

Cytoplasmic Replication and Innate Antiviral Antagonism
Following cellular uptake, rotavirus genome replication and virion assembly take place in specialized cytoplasmic inclusions termed viroplasms, and progeny virions are released by enterocyte lysis or non-classical vesicular transport. Viral NSP1 and other rotavirus proteins inhibit signaling pathways downstream of host pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), blunting type I and type III interferon induction; in children with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), defective T-cell and antibody responses permit chronic viral replication and severe intestinal and systemic disease.

Systemic Antigenemia and Transient Viremia
During the first 5 days of acute rotavirus gastroenteritis, viral antigens (antigenemia, reported in one study in at least 90% of acutely infected children) and infectious virions (viremia) enter the bloodstream across the damaged intestinal barrier. Antigenemia correlates with higher acute gastroenteritis severity and limited extraintestinal viral dissemination, including mild rotavirus-associated hepatitis with transient elevation of serum hepatic transaminases (ALT and AST), though direct causal links between antigenemia and other extraintestinal presentations remain unestablished in human cohorts.

NSP4 Viroporin-Mediated Intracellular Calcium Mobilization
Rotavirus nonstructural protein 4 (NSP4) is the first described viral enterotoxin. Calcium-Activated Chloride Secretion
NSP4-induced elevation of cytosolic free calcium activates calcium-gated chloride channels (including ANO1/TMEM16A) on the apical membrane of intestinal epithelial cells, driving transepithelial halide and chloride ion secretion into the gut lumen and creating an osmotic gradient that draws water into the lumen independently of cyclic nucleotides (cAMP/cGMP). Because rotavirus does not infect all enterocytes, sodium-glucose cotransporter 1 (SGLT1) function remains intact across uninfected enterocytes, enabling oral rehydration therapy.

Enteric Nervous System Secretomotor Activation
Rotavirus infection and NSP4-mediated intracellular calcium elevation stimulate serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) secretion from enteroendocrine (enterochromaffin) cells in the small intestine. Paracrine 5-HT activates enteric nervous system (ENS) secretomotor and motility reflexes that amplify diarrheal fluid loss and intestinal hypermotility, and stimulates vagal afferent nerves projecting to brainstem emetic centers associated with nausea and vomiting.

Proteasomal DGAT1 Degradation and Villus Enterocyte Destruction
Rotavirus infection induces proteasome-dependent degradation of diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1) and increases phosphorylation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2alpha) in infected enterocytes. Loss of DGAT1 and global translational arrest downregulate apical brush-border enzymes (including lactase, sucrase, and maltase), disrupt enterocyte lipid droplet and membrane homeostasis, and cause cytolysis and desquamation of mature villus tip enterocytes with reactive crypt hyperplasia. Unabsorbed luminal disaccharides and solutes create an osmotic gradient that drives malabsorptive osmotic diarrhea.
